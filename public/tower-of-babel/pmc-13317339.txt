Single-cell insights into maladaptive endothelial plasticity and therapeutic targets in diabetic vascular complications
Authors: Noura S Alnuaimi, Lena Lößlein, Mira Mousa, Thanumol Abdul Khader, Ayesha Alkhaaldi, Sarah K Azzam, Amaryllis H Van Craenenbroeck, Syed Salman Ashraf, Peter Carmeliet, Habiba Alsafar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEndothelial plasticity, the capacity of endothelial cells (ECs) to reversibly alter their phenotype in response to environmental cues, typically enables adaptive vascular remodeling and tissue homeostasis. In diabetes, this plasticity becomes maladaptive, driving pathological transitions across interconnected axes: dysregulated angiogenesis with barrier destabilization, inflammatory reprogramming through immune-endothelial crosstalk, metabolic dysfunction spanning mitochondrial stress to senescence, and endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition with fibrosis. In this review, we synthesize mechanistic insights across endothelial state transitions and highlight how single-cell approaches have reframed diabetic vascular disease as a disorder of maladaptive endothelial plasticity. By integrating single-cell insights from diabetic mouse models and human patient samples, we demonstrate that restoring adaptive endothelial plasticity requires coordinated multi-dimensional intervention targeting the intersecting pathways that perpetuate pathological transitions, timed to disease stage and calibrated to vascular bed-specific context. For example, combining metabolic therapies such as GLP-1 receptor agonists or SGLT2 inhibitors with anti-inflammatory agents targeting IL-17A or IL-1β, pairing anti-VEGF treatments with inhibitors of MFAP4 or ANGPTL4 to overcome angiogenic bypass pathways, or coupling senolytics, such as UBX1325, with anti-fibrotic strategies like TGF-β or SETD7 inhibition to prevent irreversible EndoMT. We identify candidate therapeutic targets across angiogenic, inflammatory, metabolic, and fibrotic domains, and highlight critical knowledge gaps, most notably the limited characterization of human diabetic ECs, that must be addressed to translate these insights into effective clinical strategies for preventing diabetic vascular complications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12933-026-03187-z.Keywords: Endothelial plasticity, Diabetes, Single-cell RNA sequencing, Angiogenesis, Inflammation, Metabolic dysfunction, Endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition, Fibrosis

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus affects 589 million people worldwide, with prevalence projected to reach 853 million by 2050 [1]. Beyond dysregulated glucose homeostasis, diabetes drives a broad spectrum of vascular complications including both microvascular (retinopathy, nephropathy, neuropathy) and macrovascular (cardiovascular disease, stroke, peripheral artery disease) pathologies (Fig. 1) [2–4]. Despite advances in glycemic management, vascular complications persist at alarming rates [5, 6], highlighting fundamental gaps in our understanding of diabetic vascular pathophysiology. Central to this pathology is the endothelium, a single-cell layer lining all blood vessels that maintains vascular homeostasis through barrier regulation, vasomotor control, hemostasis, angiogenesis, and immune surveillance [7, 8]. The endothelium's functional versatility depends on endothelial plasticity, the reversible capacity to adopt context-dependent phenotypes that normally enables adaptive vascular remodeling [9–12].

Under physiological conditions, endothelial plasticity orchestrates coordinated vascular remodeling across development, tissue homeostasis, and regenerative responses. During development, ECs transition between arterial, venous, and lymphatic lineages through vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)-Notch and COUP-TFII signaling [13], with adaptive plasticity underlying specific processes such as heart valve formation, where endocardial ECs undergo endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EndoMT) to generate mesenchymal progenitors and endothelial-to-hematopoietic transitions to produce blood-forming stem cells [14, 15]. In adult vasculature, ECs exhibit reversible transitions between quiescent, tip, and stalk cell states [16–18] (Fig. 2). Quiescent ECs maintain tissue-specific homeostatic programs through transcription factors that regulate anti-thrombotic balance, NO-mediated vasodilation, and barrier integrity [19–21]. Glomerular ECs balance filtration with barrier selectivity through specialized fenestrations [22], retinal ECs form the tight blood-retinal barrier while coordinating oxygen delivery [23], cardiac microvascular ECs sustain high capillary density matched to myocardial demand [24], and adipose ECs modulate nutrient delivery and inflammatory tone [25]. During wound healing and tissue regeneration, ECs activate pro-angiogenic signals with new vessel formation proceeding through the tip-stalk cell paradigm wherein VEGF-sensing tip cells guide sprouting while proliferative stalk cells stabilize emerging vessels [14]. Critically, these physiological transitions, including transient inflammatory activation, angiogenic sprouting, and barrier modulation, share a defining feature: they are tightly regulated, context-dependent, and resolve once homeostasis is restored.

Diabetes fundamentally undermines this reversibility. We use the term 'maladaptive endothelial plasticity' to describe pathological EC state transitions that are distinguished from physiological plasticity by three defining features. The first is chronicity, whereby responses that are appropriate and transient in acute settings, such as inflammatory activation during wound healing or angiogenic sprouting during hypoxia, become sustained and unresolved under chronic diabetic conditions. The second is dysregulation, whereby the precise transcriptional programs controlling tip/stalk specification, barrier maintenance, and immune crosstalk lose their context-appropriate coordination and feedback control. The third is progressive hloss of reversibility, whereby ECs transition from states that retain adaptive flexibility toward fixed pathological endpoints including senescence and EndoMT. This distinction is important: the same molecular programs that enable physiological EC plasticity, including VEGF-driven angiogenesis, NF-κB-mediated inflammatory activation, and TGF-β signaling, become maladaptive when they operate outside their normal spatial and temporal boundaries in the diabetic milieu, persisting beyond the tissue context and disease phase in which they are physiologically warranted. Hyperglycemia-induced oxidative stress, advanced glycation end-products, and inflammatory signaling disrupt endothelial nitric oxide (NO) production, barrier integrity, and thrombo-inflammatory balance, progressively corrupting adaptive plasticity into maladaptive cellular reprogramming that constitutes an early and predictive hallmark of diabetic vascular complications [26–30]. Dissecting these maladaptive transitions requires tools capable of resolving endothelial heterogeneity at single-cell resolution.

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) technologies have revolutionized the understanding of endothelial plasticity by revealing that seemingly homogeneous endothelial populations comprise multiple transcriptionally distinct subtypes. Unlike bulk RNA sequencing that averages expression across thousands of cells, these approaches capture individual cell or nuclear transcriptomes, enabling detection of rare populations, intermediate states, and phenotypic transitions [31]. Computational pseudotime analysis reconstructs state transitions, ligand-receptor mapping identifies multicellular communication networks, and integration with spatial transcriptomics and chromatin accessibility profiling provides multi-dimensional views of endothelial identity regulation [32–34]. These technologies demonstrate that ECs exist as a collection of specialized, dynamically interconvertible subtypes with organ-specific transcriptomic signatures in glomerular, retinal, cardiac, and adipose vasculature [31, 35, 36].

Building on these single-cell technologies, mouse models of diabetes provide essential systems for dissecting how these normally reversible state transitions become fixed or maladaptive in metabolic disease. Models include type 1 diabetes variants such as streptozotocin (STZ)-induced [37], OVE26 (calmodulin-overexpressing) [38], non-obese diabetic (NOD) [39, 40], and Akita (Ins2 mutation) mice [41], and STZ-induced eNOS−/− (endothelial nitric oxide synthase-deficient, modelling advanced diabetic nephropathy) [42]. Type 2 diabetes (T2D) models including high-fat diet combined with STZ (HFD + STZ) [43], db/db (leptin receptor-deficient), and ob/ob (leptin-deficient) mice [44]; pre-diabetic metabolic stress models using high-fat diet (HFD) alone [45]; and complication-specific models such as diabetic ischemic limb injury [46], proliferative retinopathy (Akimba cross and oxygen-induced retinopathy [OIR]) [47, 48], and atherogenic backgrounds (LDL receptor-knockout [Ldlr⁻/⁻] mice on diabetogenic diet) [49]. These systems reveal how hyperglycemia, dyslipidemia, tissue hypoxia, and inflammatory milieus differentially perturb angiogenic activation, barrier regulation, immune interactions, and lineage transitions such as EndoMT. Collectively, they offer complementary windows into endothelial plasticity dynamics while highlighting model-specific constraints that must be considered when extrapolating to human diabetic vasculopathy (Fig. 3).

This review synthesizes emerging single-cell transcriptomic evidence that diabetes drives maladaptive endothelial plasticity through four interconnected axes: dysregulated angiogenesis with barrier destabilization, inflammatory reprogramming through immune-endothelial crosstalk, metabolic dysfunction spanning mitochondrial stress to senescence, and EndoMT and fibrosis (Fig. 2;Table 1). We integrate findings across mouse models of diabetes and the limited yet expanding single-cell datasets from human tissues, focusing on kidney, retina, heart, adipose tissue, and skin wound healing, to delineate conserved mechanisms and translational gaps. Restoring adaptive endothelial plasticity will likely require coordinated, multi-pathway interventions to overcome compensatory network rewiring. Expanded single-cell profiling of human diabetic tissues is essential for translating these mechanistic insights into clinically effective endothelial-targeted therapies.

Angiogenic drivers of endothelial cell plasticity

Loss of endothelial identity and impaired regenerative capacity

As introduced above, physiological EC plasticity depends on precise reversible transitions between tip, stalk, and quiescent states coordinated through VEGF gradient sensing and DLL4-Notch lateral inhibition, with stalk cell identity enforced through VEGFR2 downregulation and quiescent barrier function maintained through claudin-5 and ZO-1 (Fig. 2); in diabetes, this coordinated hierarchy breaks down simultaneously across all three states [16–18]. In patient-derived glomerular biopsies from early diabetic kidney disease (DKD), glomerular ECs simultaneously express angiogenic drivers while downregulating barrier-stabilizing genes and upregulating pro-fibrotic programs (Fig. 4A), creating cells that attempt vessel growth while destabilizing existing structures and promoting matrix deposition [65]. Beyond the glomerulus, single-cell transcriptomics of BTBRob/ob diabetic mouse kidneys identified peritubular capillary rarefaction, the progressive reduction of peritubular capillary density driven by EC loss, from as early as 6 weeks of disease [69] (Fig. 4A).This loss of identity extends to patient-derived proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR) fibrovascular membranes (FVMs), where tip cells express stalk markers and stalk cells express tip-associated genes, alongside widespread expression of the permeability marker PLVAP [61] (Fig. 5A), demonstrating that diabetes disrupts the precise cell-state specification normally required for coordinated angiogenesis.

In patient-derived non-healing diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs), ECs downregulate critical angiogenic machinery, including RAB17, whose restoration in patient-derived primary human dermal microvascular ECs rescues angiogenic function through MAPK/ERK activation [87], and show suppressed pro-angiogenic genes (CCND1, ENO1, HIF1α) [88]. In healthy wound tissue, dermal fibroblasts and macrophages coordinate endothelial-directed neovascularisation through dynamic VEGF and platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF) signalling, with macrophages transitioning from inflammatory to reparative states that guide endothelial sprouting and vessel stabilisation. In diabetes, this endothelial-immune-fibroblast communication is disrupted, further impairing the angiogenic response required for tissue repair [88]. In contrast, patient-derived healing DFUs demonstrate upregulation of SH3BGRL3, which promotes endothelial proliferation and migration in human umbelical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) under high.

glucose conditions in vitro [89]. Similarly, in STZ-induced diabetic ischemic mice, endothelial miR-200b inhibition partially restores vasculogenic endothelial populations by improving angiogenesis [90]. The regenerative deficit extends beyond tissue repair to endothelial progenitors themselves. In high-fat high-sucrose diet-fed T2D mice, a proliferative mouse aortic EC-1 cluster numerically expands under diabetic conditions yet show markedly reduced proliferative capacity attributable to impaired YAP-KDR signaling [81], revealing that diabetes impairs progenitor function despite adequate progenitor availability (Fig. 6A, 5B). In db/db diabetic mouse hearts, reduced Arnt and Stat5 levels further impair regeneration [75], as these factors enable hypoxia-induced VEGF signaling and proliferative programs [94] (Fig. 6B). Reduced ARNT was also reported in cardiac microvascular ECs from diabetic mice and human hearts; its loss elevates the extracellular matrix-degrading enzyme MMP3, compromising barrier integrity [95]. These findings reveal that impaired wound healing and tissue repair in diabetes reflect maladaptive angiogenic plasticity in which ECs retain activation signatures but cannot execute the coordinated transitions required for functional vessel formation. In addition to endothelial identity loss and progenitor regenerative exhaustion, diabetes further dysregulates canonical VEGF signaling while alternative angiogenic drivers emerge to sustain maladaptive vascular responses. Precision targeting will require resolving when “angiogenic rescue” versus “angiogenic suppression” is the correct therapeutic strategy..

Dysregulation of VEGF signaling and alternative angiogenic pathways in diabetes

VEGF is a central driver of pathological neovascularization in DR [96], and anti-VEGF therapy remains the standard treatment for proliferative disease and macular edema. However, incomplete response and treatment resistance limit effectiveness, prompting investigation into bypass mechanisms. Single-cell RNA-seq of PDR FVMs reveal that ECs concurrently upregulate VEGFC alongside VEGFA [65], enabling continued pathological angiogenesis even under VEGFA blockade and illustrating a broader principle that diabetic angiogenesis rarely depends on a single ligand-receptor axis [97] (Fig. 5A).

The tissue-specific role of VEGF function further complicates therapeutic targeting. In the healthy glomerulus, podocytes constitutively secrete VEGFA to maintain glomerular EC fenestration, survival, and filtration barrier integrity through a tightly regulated paracrine VEGFA-VEGFR2 signaling axis, such that both excess and deficiency lead to glomerular injury. In diabetes, hyperglycaemia-induced podocyte injury.

disrupts this paracrine crosstalk, impairing EC VEGFR2 signaling despite persistent or elevated VEGFA availability [98]. While anti-VEGF blockade targets pathological angiogenesis in the retina, intravitreal anti-VEGF therapy can worsen renal function in patients with pre-existing diabetic nephropathy, reflecting the kidney’s dependence on podocyte-derived VEGF for glomerular endothelial survival and filtration barrier maintenance [99]. In human DKD, single-cell analysis reveals disrupted podocyte-endothelial crosstalk where podocytes upregulate VEGFA expression yet glomerular endothelial cells fail to appropriately upregulate VEGFR2 despite elevated ligand [65, 98] (Fig. 4C). The dysfunction extends to db/db mice, where VEGFR2 is transcriptionally upregulated in response to podocyte-derived VEGFA but functionally impaired at the cell surface through constitutive Src-mediated internalization [100]. This convergent dysfunction across species demonstrates that when canonical VEGF-VEGFR2 signaling is disrupted, compensatory angiogenic pathways emerge to sustain pathological angiogenesis, explaining anti-VEGF resistance and necessitating identification of these alternative mechanisms.

Beyond VEGF family members, diabetic ECs activate a network of parallel angiogenic pathways that sustain pathological angiogenesis independent of VEGFR2 signaling, creating a redundant angiogenic network resistant to single-target therapies. Cytoskeletal dynamics provide one VEGF-independent mechanism through tubulin β-6 chain (TUBB6), which single-cell transcriptomic analysis of the murine OIR model identifies as enriched in tip cell and proliferative EC subclusters, with transcriptomic upregulation confirmed in human PDR retinal microvascular ECs by bulk RNA-seq re-analysis of patient-derived samples, and which drives angiogenesis via WNT3A-FZD8 signaling [59] (Fig. 5B). TUBB6 knockdown reduces endothelial proliferation, migration in vitro, and neovascular burden in murine models, positioning cytoskeletal remodeling as a therapeutic target complementing anti-VEGF approaches.

In the healthy retina, pericytes wrap nascent vessels and enforce vessel stabilisation and maturation through PDGFB-PDGFRβ and Angiopoietin-1-Tie2 signalling [101, 102], while Müller glia and astrocytes reinforce EC quiescence and blood-retinal barrier integrity through constitutive secretion of pigment epithelium-derived factor (PEDF), Angiopoietin-1, and thrombospondin-1, together establishing an anti-angiogenic perivascular microenvironment [103]. In diabetes, pericyte loss reduces ANGPT1-mediated Tie2 activation and shifts the balance toward destabilising ANGPT2 signalling, while chronic oxidative stress reprogrammes the glial scaffold toward pro-angiogenic and matrix-remodelling states, collectively inverting perivascular crosstalk with ECs from quiescence maintenance to pathological activation. Single-cell studies of human diabetic retina and choroid reveal that this reprogrammed perivascular niche drives extracellular matrix (ECM)-mediated, VEGF-independent pro-angiogenic signalling, with pericytes, astrocytes, Müller glia, and fibroblasts collectively upregulating microfibril-associated protein 4 (MFAP4) secretion [60] (Fig. 5C). MFAP4 is deposited into the ECM, where it binds endothelial integrins αVβ3/αVβ5 to activate FAK-ERK signaling, driving proliferation, motility, and barrier destabilization. MFAP4 neutralizing antibody (hAS0326) reduced neovascularization in STZ-diabetic rats and suppressed vascular leakage for three months in non-human primates, demonstrating sustained efficacy exceeding anti-VEGF agents and validating ECM-mediated angiogenesis as a druggable axis.

Metabolic stress further drives VEGF-independent angiogenesis through angiopoietin-like 4 (ANGPTL4), a secreted glycoprotein that destabilizes barriers and drives vessel growth across multiple diabetic tissues [50]. In the healthy retina, photoreceptors regulate vascular tone and endothelial quiescence through neurovascular coupling mechanisms that align oxygen delivery with metabolic demand, including tightly regulated VEGF signaling. Diabetes-driven photoreceptor metabolic stress redirects this paracrine output toward pro-angiogenic mediators that destabilise rather than maintain the retinal microvasculature [50]. Single-cell transcriptomic studies in STZ-induced diabetic rats implicate photoreceptor-endothelial Angptl4-Sdc4 signaling in retinal pathological sprouting through VE-cadherin internalization, while pleiotrophin and midkine signal via nucleolin drive migration and ECM remodeling [50] (Fig. 5C). Angptl4 blockade reduces retinal thickening, vascular leakage, and downstream Vegfa, Igf-1, and Hif1a expression in diabetic mice, while ANGPTL4 levels remain elevated in human PDR during anti-VEGF therapy [104], confirming VEGF-independent action. In the healthy myocardium, cardiomyocytes sustain coronary microvascular endothelial cell (EC) survival and capillary density through metabolically matched VEGFA secretion, while ECs reciprocally support cardiomyocytes via neuregulin-1 and NO signaling. In diabetic cardiomyopathy (DCM), hyperglycaemia and lipid overload disrupt this bidirectional crosstalk, altering cardiomyocyte paracrine signaling and promoting endothelial dysfunction and microvascular rarefaction. ANGPTL4 operates as one such mediator, though its cardiac role possibly diverges from its retinal context: in STZ-induced diabetic mice, scRNA-seq re-analysis of cardiac tissue combined with high-glucose EC-cardiomyocyte co-culture experiments identify cardiomyocyte (CM)-derived Angptl4 signaling via endothelial Cdh5/Sdc3 as a hyperglycaemia-induced EC-CM paracrine axis possibly associated with lipid-metabolic remodelling [80] (Fig. 6C), with higher circulating ANGPTL4 levels predicting cardiovascular mortality in diabetic patients [105]. Nonetheless, whether this axis suppresses adaptive angiogenesis or primarily mediates metabolic reprogramming in DCM remains experimentally unresolved. In healthy perivascular adipose tissue PVAT, resident macrophages maintain vascular homeostasis through anti-inflammatory paracrine programmes that support EC barrier integrity and vascular tone; in diabetes, dyslipidaemia and metabolic stress reprogram these macrophages toward pro-inflammatory states that secrete barrier-destabilising mediators. In HFD/STZ mice, scRNA-seq of PVAT identifies macrophage-derived Angptl4 disrupts endothelial junctions by interacting with endothelial-Cdh5 and induces apoptosis and migration [91]. Adipose-specific Angptl4 deletion reduces Icam1/Vcam1 expression and vascular inflammation [106], and recombinant cANGPTL4 disrupts endothelial junctions via binding to integrin α5β1, VE-cadherin, and claudin-5 [107]. These findings establish ANGPTL4 as a convergent mediator arising from photoreceptor, cardiomyocyte, and perivascular macrophage sources that translates metabolic stress into pathological vascular remodelling across multiple diabetic tissues, though the directionality of its angiogenic effect is context-dependent and warrants organ-specific investigation,

Beyond their role in the perivascular adipose niche, tissue-resident macrophages also directly regulate EC angiogenic behaviour during vessel formation itself. In physiological angiogenesis, tissue-resident macrophages serve as indispensable vessel guidance cells, positioning at sites of tip cell contact to physically chaperone anastomosis and facilitate vascular fusion downstream of VEGF-mediated sprouting [108], transitioning thereafter to pro-resolution roles that coordinate vessel regression and sprouting termination once perfusion is established, including phagocytic clearance of apoptotic endothelial cells to enable orderly vascular remodeling [109]. In diabetes, this tightly staged guidance function is subverted, and macrophages are reprogrammed into sustained drivers of pathological neovascularization. These immune-endothelial interactions further compound diabetic angiogenic dysregulation by providing continuous immune-derived pro-angiogenic signals that sustain pathological neovascularization. In human PDR FVMs, SPP1 + macrophages secrete chemokines (CXCL1/2/3/8, CCL2/3) and VEGF ligands that engage endothelial activation via Notch and VEGF pathways to sustain aberrant sprouting [61]. This creates a feed-forward loop where dysfunctional ECs recruit macrophages through inflammatory chemokine secretion, and infiltrating macrophages reciprocally provide pro-angiogenic signals that perpetuate endothelial maladaptation. Homeostatic microglia survey the retinal vasculature in an anti-inflammatory state that supports tissue homeostasis and vascular stability [62]. In PDR, sustained hyperglycaemic and hypoxic stress promotes microglial activation and proliferation, shifting their role from immune surveillance to the production of pro-inflammatory and pro-angiogenic mediators that amplify pathological neovascularisation. In human PDR FVMs, proliferative MKI67 + microglia further amplify this network by secreting osteopontin that engages endothelial integrin α4β1, triggering excessive proliferation and migration [62]. CDK4/6 inhibitor abemaciclib suppressed microglial proliferation in OIR mice, a model of hypoxia-driven rather than hyperglycemic retinopathy, reducing neovascular tufting and leakage by disrupting immune-driven pro-angiogenic feedback, though direct translation to the diabetic context requires investigation (Fig. 5C).

Dysregulated angiogenesis in diabetes is driven by the convergence of several principal signaling intermediates, including ANGPT2-mediated Tie2 destabilization, dysregulated HIF-1α transcriptional signaling, and loss of DLL4-Notch lateral inhibition, each of which disrupts the coordinated EC state transitions required for functional vessel formation. Single-cell transcriptomic studies have further revealed that these disruptions are compounded by activation of alternative angiogenic pathways, including ANGPTL4, MFAP4, TUBB6, and VEGFC, that sustain pathological angiogenesis independently of canonical VEGF-VEGFR2 signaling and may collectively explain the limited efficacy of anti-VEGF monotherapy. Effective intervention will therefore require coordinated targeting of endothelial dysfunction across these parallel axes rather than isolated ligand blockade. Beyond angiogenic dysregulation, diabetes induces profound inflammatory reprogramming of the endothelium, representing a second major axis of maladaptive plasticity.

Inflammatory control of endothelial plasticity in diabetic vascular disease

Progressive transition from angiogenesis to terminal inflammation

Loss of canonical endothelial identity and maladaptive angiogenic signaling in diabetes prime ECs for inflammatory activation, representing a second major axis of maladaptive endothelial plasticity closely intertwined with angiogenic dysfunction. Diabetes induces a shift from quiescent, barrier-stabilizing phenotypes to immune-activated states marked by adhesion molecule expression, chemokine and cytokine secretion, and formation of local inflammatory niches driven by metabolic stress and immune-derived cues [110]. This inflammatory transformation is amplified by concurrent loss of endothelial-intrinsic anti-inflammatory mechanisms, exemplified in diabetic kidneys by downregulation of endothelial cell-specific molecule-1 (ESM-1), a secreted proteoglycan that inhibits leukocyte transmigration [67]. In re-analysis of human and murine DKD scRNA-seq databases validated by RNAscope in STZ-induced mice and human nephrectomy specimens, Esm-1 + renal ECs represent a specialized subset enriched in vascular development genes, and their depletion reflects coupled loss of both anti-inflammatory protection and regenerative angiogenic capacity, accompanied by a transcriptional shift toward chemotaxis gene enrichment (Fig. 4B).

Single-cell profiling of db/db mouse hearts shows that chronic metabolic stress drives endothelial plasticity along a progressive trajectory from angiogenic-homeostatic to inflammatory injury states [76] (Fig. 6A). Early angiogenic clusters enriched for NO and vascular development pathways gradually transition to late clusters dominated by leukocyte adhesion, antigen presentation, and extracellular matrix remodeling. This transition is accompanied by progressive downregulation of Ets1, a master regulator of 154 endothelial genes controlling NO synthesis and vascular stability, alongside upregulation of stress-responsive transcription factors (Meis2, Foxp1, Cebpb), indicating angiogenic exhaustion and transcriptional reprogramming (Fig. 6B). Endothelial-specific Ets1 overexpression rescued vascular density, suppressed EndoMT, and improved survival in db/db mice, while Ets1 knockdown exacerbated all phenotypes, establishing Ets1 loss as a driver of diabetic cardiac endothelial deterioration. These injured ECs also upregulate Igfbp5, a marker of fibrosis (Fig. 6B).

Pericytes actively drive this shift by delivering TNFα signals that suppress angiogenic (Apln + , Nrp2 +) and proliferative (Mki67 + , Top2a +) endothelial subclusters while arteriolar cells (Cxcl12 + , Rbp7 + , Hey1 +) expand in murine diabetic hearts [77], as shown by scRNA-seq of cardiac tissue from diabetic db/db mice given 1% salt as a model of heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) (Fig. 6C). NicheNet analysis identified a dominant pericyte-to-endothelial TNFα signaling axis that is predicted to activate inflammatory transcription factors (Stat1, Irf1, Nfκbia) while suppressing cyclin D1 and inducing Vcam1 and P21, driving ECs toward growth arrest and inflammatory injury. Mechanistically, STAT1 accumulation drives cyclin D1 degradation independently of its canonical Tyr701 phosphorylation, distinguishing this pericyte-driven arrest from classical interferon-mediated STAT1 activation and indicating that upstream TNFα blockade rather than JAK inhibition is the appropriate therapeutic strategy for this axis. In vivo validation confirmed increased endothelial VCAM1 and p21 expression with disrupted VE-cadherin junctions, and human pericyte-endothelial co-cultures demonstrated that oxidative-stress-primed pericytes induce endothelial inflammation and senescence, reversed by TNFα knockdown. Type I interferon signaling represents an additional inflammatory axis, with single-nucleus profiling of HFD/STZ-induced diabetic mouse hearts revealing endothelial enrichment of Interferon Regulatory Factor 7 (Irf7) [81] that drives interferon-stimulated gene expression promoting immune cell recruitment. Whether this response is protective or pathological in diabetic heart remains unclear (Fig. 6B). IRF7-responsive endothelial populations have been identified in human heart-failure tissues and murine myocardial infarction models [111], and IRF7 plays an established role in antiviral immunity [112], yet whether these functions translate to chronic metabolic stress contexts requires investigation.

At the extreme end of this inflammatory trajectory lies pyroptosis, an inflammatory programmed cell death representing terminal loss of endothelial plasticity [82]. Re-analysis of db/db mouse hearts and DDC-fed diabetic mice identified Inosine Monophosphate Dehydrogenase 1 (Impdh1) as a key endothelial gene localized to the terminal branch of differentiation trajectories (Fig. 6A). In vivo validation in db/db mouse aortas demonstrated elevated Impdh1 with canonical pyroptotic markers (NLRP3, pro-caspase-1, GSDMD), increased IL-1β and IL-18 secretion, vascular wall thickening, and collagen deposition, all ameliorated by Impdh1 knockdown. High-glucose-treated HUVECs similarly showed increased pyroptotic markers and impaired tube formation rescued by Impdh1 silencing. These findings position pyroptosis as an irreversible inflammatory endpoint characterized by loss of adaptive endothelial plasticity and amplified tissue injury through inflammatory cytokine release and barrier breakdown, though whether this contributes to human DCM requires investigation. Beyond cell-intrinsic inflammatory trajectories, cytokine signaling and immune cell crosstalk actively drive endothelial reprogramming.

Immune-endothelial inflammatory networks driving maladaptive plasticity

Under homeostatic conditions, regulatory T cells (Tregs) contribute to vascular immune quiescence by secreting IL-10 and other anti-inflammatory mediators that suppress endothelial adhesion molecule expression and leukocyte recruitment. In diabetes, chronic hyperglycaemia and metabolic inflammation shift the adaptive immune balance toward Th17 cells, a CD4+ T helper subset defined by IL-17A secretion, promoting sustained endothelial activation and inflammatory remodeling [113]. In STZ-induced diabetic mouse retinas, Th17-derived Il-17a activates endothelial Il-17 receptors (Il17ra/Il17rc) to trigger Nf-κb and Mapk pathways, upregulating inflammatory genes (S100a8, S100a9, Il1b, Cxcl2, Lcn2, Vcam-1) while suppressing shear-responsive genes Nos3 and Pdgfb [51] (Fig. 5C). This combined impairment of vasodilation, pericyte support, and barrier integrity accelerates capillary degeneration. IL-17A deficiency protects against endothelial apoptosis and its levels correlate with PDR severity in patients [114–116]. In human DKD, spatial transcriptomic mapping reveals that venous ECs within fibrotic regions transform into immune hubs enriched for TNF-α, NF-κB, and IL-17 signaling, with VEC proportion increasing progressively from normal control to DM to DKD; these VECs spatially colocalize with fibroblasts in fibrotic areas and recruit leukocytes via CCL2-ACKR1 and CCL5-ACKR1 interactions, directly linking inflammatory endothelial reprogramming to fibrotic remodeling in the diabetic kidney [68] (Fig. 4C). The enrichment of IL-17 signaling in these fibrosis-associated VECs suggests that the same IL-17A-driven endothelial activation documented in the diabetic retina operates across microvascular beds, a hypothesis supported by the observation that IL-17A neutralization in BTBR ob/ob diabetic mice reduces albuminuria, inflammatory infiltration, and NF-κB activation [117], confirming that disrupting this immune-endothelial axis has functional benefit in the diabetic kidney.

Chen et al. [68], Lavoz et al. [117] Macrophage populations profoundly shape endothelial inflammatory states through both pro-inflammatory and vasoprotective signals, with their imbalance driving maladaptive endothelial transformation. In late stage of db/db murine diabetic heart, macrophage-derived Il-1β engages endothelial Il1r1/Il1rap signaling to progressively amplify inflammatory transcriptional programs (Cxcl1 and Vcam1), promoting leukocyte adhesion and endothelial injury [78] (Fig. 6C). In HUVECs, IL1RAP-blocking antibodies suppress IL-1β-, IL-33-, and IL-36γ-induced cytokine release (IL-6, IL-8, MCP-1), adhesion molecule expression (Icam1, Vcam1, Sele), and neutrophil recruitment while restoring barrier integrity and reducing NF-κB activation [118], with elevated plasma IL-1β correlating with prothrombotic coagulation abnormalities in diabetic patients [119]. Conversely, Pdpn + macrophages, identified by scRNA-seq as depleted in diabetic rat PVAT (HFD/STZ-induced) and confirmed via immunofluorescence in diabetic (db/db) mice and human PVAT, represent a conserved vasoprotective subset enriched for lipid-handling genes (Fabp4, Apoe, Abca1, Pltp, Pparg) and anti-inflammatory programs [91]. Their reintroduction reduces vascular inflammation and enhances vasodilatory responses by restoring NO bioavailability, a process mediated through an adipocyte-dependent paracrine chain in which Pdpn + macrophage-secreted phospholipase Pla2g2d generates the omega-3 fatty acids docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) and eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA), which signal via the lipid-sensing receptor GPR120 on ECs to rescue eNOS phosphorylation and restore NO production.

Inflammatory control of endothelial plasticity in diabetes operates through integrated cell-intrinsic trajectories and immune-endothelial networks. ECs progress from angiogenic states through inflammatory injury phenotypes characterized by barrier dysfunction, fibrotic signaling, and immune activation to terminal pyroptotic cell death. This progression is actively driven by cytokine signaling from T cells and macrophages that activate inflammatory transcriptional programs while suppressing protective pathways. Concurrent depletion of vasoprotective macrophage populations and amplification by pericyte-derived inflammatory signals create a permissive microenvironment that progressively constrains adaptive endothelial responses. Reciprocal reinforcement between metabolic dysfunction and inflammatory signaling further solidifies these states, making combined metabolic-immune-endothelial interventions essential for restoring endothelial health.

Metabolic control of endothelial plasticity

Metabolic dysfunction drives the progressive loss of endothelial plasticity in diabetes in a progression that parallels earlier angiogenic and inflammatory shifts. Initially, ECs respond to hyperglycemic stress by increasing glycolytic flux, activating lipid-handling programs, upregulating nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD⁺) biosynthesis, and engaging heat shock pathways. However, chronic metabolic stress exhausts these compensatory mechanisms, progressively constraining plasticity as cells lose metabolic flexibility and transition to terminal senescence characterized by metabolic collapse and pro-inflammatory secretory phenotype acquisition.

Mitochondrial and metabolic dysfunction promote maladaptive endothelial plasticity

While ECs rely primarily on glycolysis for ATP production, mitochondria serve critical non-bioenergetic functions including reactive oxygen species (ROS) signaling, calcium buffering, and biosynthetic pathway integration. In diabetes, mitochondrial homeostasis shifts from adaptive signaling to chronic inflammatory activation. Single-cell profiling of BTBRob/ob mouse kidneys reveals progressive downregulation of oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) machinery and Ucp2 suppression in glomerular and peritubular ECs, consistent with impaired mitochondrial ROS buffering [69](Fig. 4B). Notably, glomerular and peritubular ECs execute divergent stress programs from as early as six weeks: EIF2, a master regulator of protein synthesis that is inactivated under cellular stress, is compensatorily activated in glomerular ECs while simultaneously inactivated in peritubular ECs. Both subtypes converge on EIF2 inactivation and apoptosis by twenty weeks, indicating that EC subtypes within the same diabetic organ are not simply at different stages of the same disease trajectory but are executing fundamentally distinct adaptive responses that both ultimately fail [69]. A deleterious UCP2 haplotype (− 866A/55Val/Ins) associates with reduced expression in human kidney biopsies, increased DKD risk, and lower estimated glomerular filtration rate [120], establishing impaired mitochondrial ROS buffering as a conserved contributor of maladaptive EC plasticity that triggers inflammatory cascades.

Single-cell RNA-seq of human PDR FVMs and murine OIR retinas identified STING-high ECs expressing elevated angiogenic mediators (VEGFA, ANGPT2) and pro-inflammatory factors (VCAM1, IL1B) while losing barrier-protective junctional proteins (claudin-5, zonula occludens-1) [63]. Diabetic metabolic stress induces mitochondrial depolarization and release of mitochondrial DNA into the cytosol, activating STING-TBK1-IRF3-NF-κB signaling (Fig. 5B). This represents a critical node where metabolic stress drives transcriptional reprogramming, shifting ECs from quiescent barrier phenotypes toward inflammatory states while limiting reversibility. Importantly, this mitochondrial-STING axis is therapeutically actionable. GLP-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs) suppress mitochondrial depolarization, and STING activation via CREB, restoring endothelial barrier integrity and reducing vascular pathology in diabetic mice. In human retinal ECs, GLP-1RAs dampen STING signaling, and in a cohort of 1,626 T2D patients demonstrated reduced DR incidence [121]. These convergent mechanistic and clinical data establish the mitochondrial-STING-CREB pathway as a validated target through which metabolic stress reshapes endothelial plasticity. Beyond STING, additional mitochondrial regulators identified in single-cell studies are emerging. STZ-induced diabetic mouse retinas show endothelial enrichment of mitochondria-localized glutamic acid-rich protein (Mgarp) [52], which regulates mitochondrial distribution and dynamics. Its silencing in human retinal microvascular ECs (HRMECs) reduced proliferation, migration, and tube formation, yet Mgarp expression remains unvalidated in patient tissues, limiting assessment of its clinical relevance.

Mitochondrial ROS-induced damage feeds directly into interconnected metabolic hubs whose progressive failure determines whether ECs maintain adaptive plasticity or become locked into maladaptive states. NAD⁺ is a central metabolic cofactor regulating glycolysis, oxidative phosphorylation, and cellular signaling [122]. In ECs, NAD⁺ bioavailability regulates SIRT1, which controls angiogenic programs, mitochondrial biogenesis, and inflammatory responses [123], positioning NAD⁺ as a master regulator of endothelial plasticity. Single-cell profiling of STZ-induced diabetic retinas identified choline kinase alpha (CHKA) enrichment in angiogenic endothelial subclusters [53] (Fig. 5B). CHKA silencing in HRMECs depletes NAD⁺, reduces SIRT1 activity, and activates Notch signaling, shifting cells from maladaptive sprouting toward a more stabilized phenotype. CHKA is localized to neovascular tufts in OIR mice, enriched in human PDR fibrovascular membranes, and elevated in aqueous humor from PDR patients. Mendelian randomization supports a causal association between higher CHKA expression and increased DR risk, while pharmacological CHKA inhibition (MN58b) reduces vascular leakage and acellular capillaries. These findings nominate the CHKA-NAD⁺-SIRT1-Notch axis as a metabolically defined, druggable pathway for modulating pathological angiogenesis in DR.

Sphingolipid and sterol metabolism add further layers of tissue-specific metabolic vulnerability. Single-cell profiling of db/db mouse retinas identified protective diabetes-specific endothelial subclusters enriched for alkaline ceramidase 2 (ACER2), which converts ceramide to sphingosine-1-phosphate [54] (Fig. 5B). Progressive ceramide accumulation correlated with disease severity in vitreous humor of diabetic mice and plasma of human NPDR and PDR patients. ACER2 overexpression restores VE-cadherin and tight junction proteins, reduces vascular leakage, and synergizes with anti-VEGF therapy, highlighting sphingolipid handling as a modifiable determinant of endothelial resilience. Conversely, diabetes suppresses cholesterol biosynthesis in corneal endothelium. Single-cell profiling of human corneal ECs and bulk RNA-seq of STZ-induced diabetic mouse corneas demonstrate coordinated downregulation of cholesterol synthesis genes, with cholesterol supplementation restoring barrier integrity in vitro and reducing postoperative edema following endothelial keratoplasty in diabetic mice [64]. Diabetes simultaneously compromises protein quality control, with single-cell profiling across kidney, heart, and liver in STZ-induced diabetic mice revealing suppression of the HSF1-heat shock pathway (Hsph1, Hspa1a, Hspb1) [36] (Fig. 4B, 5B), a proteostatic disturbance that may reduce endothelial capacity to buffer metabolic stress and increase the propensity for maladaptive phenotypic reprogramming. Together, these mitochondrial, metabolic, and proteostatic perturbations act on convergent hubs that alter endothelial plasticity and worsen diabetic vasculopathy.

Metabolic trajectories from adaptive endothelial plasticity to senescence and EndoMT

ECs initially mount compensatory responses demonstrating remarkable adaptive plasticity. These cells exhibit extraordinary metabolic flexibility, shifting between glucose and fatty acid utilization to preserve the potential for reversible state transitions. In human single-cell profiling of adipose depots, microvascular ECs encode lipid transport and oxidative stress resistance genes (FABP4, RBP7, GPX3, CD36, LGALS) [92], while murine lipid-associated ECs in HFD-fed mice expand after high-fat diet feeding [93], reflecting preserved adaptive plasticity in response to lipid overload. In the diabetic heart, single-cell profiling of DDC-fed Ldlr-/- and STZ-induced diabetic mice reveals early expansion of endothelial clusters enriched for lipid-handling genes (Cd36, Lpl, Gpihbp1, and Fabp4) [83], with circulating FABP4 elevation predicting cardiovascular mortality in diabetic and heart failure patients [124] (Fig. 6A). Concurrently, early STZ-induced diabetic mouse diabetic retinas show increased glycolytic activity [51], accompanied by stress-responsive RNA-binding proteins and metallothioneins as compensatory antioxidant responses. These adaptations represent a phase of metabolic resilience, during which ECs retain flexibility and signaling responsiveness needed for reversible state transitions.

Chronic metabolic stress eventually exhausts adaptive responses, driving ECs toward maladaptive states. Late-stage EndoMT-like endothelial populations in db/db mouse hearts suppress fatty acid oxidation and lipid-handling genes alongside glycolytic dysregulation [83]. These metabolic deficits coincide with transcriptional signatures of cellular stress, confirming that exhaustion of metabolic flexibility is a defining step toward maladaptive endothelial states (Fig. 6A). PPARγ, a lipid-handling transcriptional regulator, exhibits species divergence in diabetes in which db/db mouse hearts maintain elevated endothelial PPARγ expression [75]. By contrast, circulating PPARγ is significantly lower in human patients with microvascular complications, correlating inversely with microalbuminuria and HbA1c [125]. This suggests mice may preserve PPARγ-mediated adaptive plasticity longer than humans, who undergo more rapid exhaustion and loss of adaptive flexibility. Whether this species’ divergence reflects differences in PPARγ stability, transcriptional regulation, or downstream signaling remains unclear.

When metabolic collapse becomes sufficiently severe, ECs enter a state of irreversible senescence, characterized by permanent cell cycle arrest, metabolic impairment, and acquiring senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) factors that amplify tissue dysfunction. Stage-dependent metabolic divergence is evident in DR: early STZ-induced disease exhibits glycolytic activation consistent with preserved adaptive plasticity [51], whereas re-analysis of Akimba mouse retinal scRNA-seq data revealed downregulation of glycolytic genes and enrichment of oxidative stress pathways in the EC cluster, reflecting loss of adaptive plasticity in advanced PDR [56, 58]. Chronic high-glucose exposure of human microvascular retinal ECs (HMRECs) in vitro induces premature senescence with reduced proliferation, impaired tube formation, and metabolic collapse characterized by decreased glucose transporters (GLUT1, GLUT3), and glycolytic enzyme PFKFB3, confirming senescent cells lose glycolytic capacity [56]. In HMRECs, high glucose induces canonical senescence markers including β-galactosidase activity alongside decreased SIRT1 (consequent to NAD⁺ depletion), HMGB2 (a chromatin protein regulating proliferative capacity), and phosphorylated retinoblastoma protein, with the latter indicating accumulation of growth-suppressive hypo-phosphorylated Rb that enforces permanent cell cycle arrest [55]. Concurrently, oxidative stress-induced DNA damage triggers p53 upregulation [55].

Single-cell RNA-seq of Akimba and db/db retinas highlights enrichment of p53 signaling alongside senescence genes (Cdkn2d, Fos) and SASP factors, including pro-fibrotic factors (Ctgf, Igfbp4) [56] and inflammatory mediators (Il1b, Icam, Igfbp7, Mmp9) [55]. Mechanistically, p53 induces the ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme UBE2L6, promoting proteasomal degradation of FOXO3a, an anti-senescence transcription factor whose depletion in diabetic retinas amplifies both senescence progression and SASP expression (Fig. 5B). Genetic restoration of FOXO3a suppresses these responses [55], while its protective function extends to diabetic nephropathy through Sirt6-dependent deacetylation of Smad3, thereby limiting EndoMT [126] and establishing a mechanistic link between metabolic senescence and fibrotic reprogramming across vascular beds. Senescent retinal ECs emerge as signaling hubs with highest ligand-receptor connectivity in db/db mice [55], secreting SASP factors that reinforce endothelial dysfunction, remodel extracellular matrix, and activate leukocytes and Müller glia (Fig. 5C). Simiarily, glomerular ECs of BTBR ob/ob mice exhibit significantly elevated senescence scores relative to non-glomerular cell types and relative to normoglycaemic controls at both disease onset and early disease stages, with high-senescence glomerular EC clusters enriched for oxidative phosphorylation disruption, supporting a glomerular-specific pattern of EC senescence in diabetic kidney disease(Fig. 4A) [70]. Unlike transiently dysfunctional ECs subject to clearance, senescent cells escape elimination and establish self-amplifying inflammatory networks that propagate vascular injury across cell types and tissues.

These findings establish that diabetes drives ECs through a progressive metabolic trajectory from initial adaptive compensation through lipid handling and glycolytic upregulation, to intermediate exhaustion and mitochondrial dysfunction, culminating in terminal senescence characterized by irreversible metabolic collapse and SASP-mediated tissue damage. Therapeutic interventions targeting mitochondrial-STING signaling with GLP-1RAs, upstream metabolic enzymes with CHKA inhibitors, or senescent cell clearance with senolytics may restore adaptive plasticity if applied before exhaustion becomes irreversible. However, persistent metabolic collapse creates permissive conditions for EndoMT by depleting the metabolic resources required to maintain endothelial identity. Under metabolic exhaustion, cells lose capacity to sustain barrier and angiogenic functions, rendering them susceptible to transcriptional reprogramming that suppresses endothelial genes while activating mesenchymal programs. EndoMT thus represents an alternative terminal fate where maladaptive plasticity culminates not in senescence but in complete phenotypic conversion to matrix-producing, pro-fibrotic effectors driving organ fibrosis. Identifying the temporal window during which endothelial plasticity can still be rescued represents a central challenge and opportunity for future translational work.

Endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition and fibrosis as a terminal fate of maladaptive endothelial plasticity

Metabolic exhaustion progressively constrains endothelial plasticity, redirecting adaptive stress responses toward maladaptive pro-fibrotic cell-state transitions that culminate in EndoMT. Single-cell transcriptomics reveal this transformation as a continuum beginning with partial loss of plasticity, where ECs retain canonical markers (PECAM1, CDH5, NOS3) while aberrantly expressing matrix genes and pro-fibrotic signals including TGF-β and LTBP1 [84]. As metabolic stress advances, endothelial markers decline while mesenchymal transcripts (VIM, FN1, ACTA2) become progressively dominant, reflecting near-complete phenotypic conversion. This progression is driven by TGF-β superfamily activation, reinforced by bidirectional endothelial-stromal paracrine signaling, and ultimately stabilized by epigenetic remodeling that dismantles transcriptional programs maintaining endothelial identity.

TGF-β-driven reprogramming and stromal crosstalk in maladaptive endothelial plasticity

TGF-β superfamily signaling is the principal upstream driver that redirects endothelial plasticity toward pro-fibrotic programming. It acts through LTBP1-dependent activation of latent TGF-β and additional LTBP1-independent routes that converge on Smad-mediated transcriptional reprogramming [127]. Human DKD single-cell profiling identifies glomerular ECs that co-express extracellular matrix genes such as COL4A1 together with LTBP1, TGFB2, ADAM17, and ITGB8 [65](Fig. 4A). This hybrid state demonstrates how persistent TGF-β signaling shifts endothelial plasticity from barrier maintenance toward pathological matrix deposition. Mechanistically, ITGB8 interacts with mesangial-derived COL4A1 to activate LTBP1-associated TGF-β signaling [65](Fig. 4C), while ADAM17 contributes to basement membrane thickening, with endothelial-specific deletion ameliorating injury [128]. ScRNA-seq of STZ-induced diabetic eNOS⁻/⁻ glomeruli identifies glomerular endothelial Lrg1 upregulation [42], consistent with broader evidence that Lrg1 enhances Smad signaling to promote fibrotic remodeling [129]. BMP6, a member of the TGF-β superfamily, is upregulated in glomerular ECs and signals through podocyte BMPR1A receptors, as identified by snRNA-seq profiling of the db/db model [71] (Fig. 4C). A multi-organ analysis reveals sustained endothelial BMP6-BMPR1A signaling correlates with EC-fibroblast communication, fibrosis, basement membrane thickening, and vascular remodeling in diabetic heart and kidney [130]. Together, these data indicate that sustained TGF-β superfamily activation progressively constrains endothelial plasticity and promotes pro-fibrotic reprogramming across diabetic tissues.

Across multiple tissues, neighboring cells contribute distinct pro-EndoMT signals that progressively stabilize mesenchymal programming and constrain endothelial plasticity, leading to multicellular crosstalk. Mesangial cells are pericyte-like glomerular mural cells that, under physiological conditions, maintain capillary loop integrity and regulate filtration dynamics, with their recruitment and maintenance strongly dependent on endothelial-derived PDGF-B signaling via mesangial PDGFRβ [131]. In diabetes, mesangial expansion and hyperglycaemia-driven reprogramming convert this supportive relationship into a source of pro-fibrotic paracrine signals. Single-cell analyses of db/db mouse kidneys reveal that hyperglycaemia induces mesangial secretion of SEMA3C, which binds neuropilin receptors on glomerular ECs to upregulate EndoMT markers, effects mitigated by SEMA3C siRNA-mediated knockdown or neutralizing antibodies [73](Fig. 4C). Urinary SEMA3C correlates with albuminuria in T2D patients, serving as a potential non-invasive biomarker of EndoMT and fibrosis in DKD. Similarily, Single-cell RNA-seq of OIR mouse retinas identified a pericyte subpopulation marked by Col1a1 enrichment that induces EndoMT [57] (Fig. 5C). In vitro Col1a1 silencing reduced pericyte recruitment to ECs and ameliorated TGF-β1-induced EndoMT by restoring CD31 and VE-cadherin while suppressing fibronectin and α-SMA. Consistent with these findings, COL1A1 is elevated in aqueous humor of PDR patients and colocalizes with pericyte markers in the diabetic retina.

In atherosclerotic and diabetic vessels, ECs and stromal populations create reciprocal signalling loops that drive both cell types toward fibrotic programming. VSMCs are the contractile mural cells of large and medium-calibre vessels that under physiological conditions maintain arterial integrity through laminar shear-driven EC-derived NO signalling, which suppresses VSMC proliferation and preserves their differentiated phenotype; loss of this EC-derived restraint under diabetic and atherosclerotic conditions drives VSMC phenotypic switching toward synthetic, pro-fibrotic states [132]. Cheng et al. [84] In ApoE−/− mouse aortae, EndoMT-like ECs secrete fibronectin as a candidate paracrine signal predicted to engage VSMC surface integrins, potentially promoting VSMC activation and ECM deposition [84]. Reciprocally, VSMCs upregulate Igfbp2, which may suppress IGF-1 bioavailability at the endothelial surface and engage integrin-mediated signaling, potentially reinforcing EC dysfunction [84] (Fig. 5C). The functional consequences of this bidirectional crosstalk remain to be experimentally validated.

Similar bidirectional communication occurs between ECs and fibroblasts in diabetic hearts. Cardiac fibroblasts are the principal matrix-producing cells of the myocardium, maintaining the collagen scaffold through homeostatic low-level reciprocal PDGF and TGFβ signalling with cardiac ECs that preserves tissue architecture without driving excessive matrix deposition [133]; in DCM, this reciprocal restraint is lost as hyperglycaemia reprogrammes both cell types toward mutual activation, creating a self-amplifying pro-fibrotic circuit. Single-cell RNA-seq of HFD/STZ-induced diabetic mouse hearts revealed endothelial upregulation of PDGFB and PDGFD, which activate PDGFRA + fibroblasts and trigger ITGB1-mediated matrix signaling driving fibroblast expansion [79]. Blocking this axis with imatinib, a PDGFRA inhibitor, or Itgb1 knockdown attenuated both fibroblast activation and cardiac hypertrophy [79]. Reciprocally, db/db mouse heart fibroblasts secrete PDGFB back to ECs, perpetuating vascular stress and cardiac fibrosis [75]. At the systemic level, multi-organ single-cell analysis in STZ-induced diabetic mice identified endothelial-to-fibroblast Col4a2-Sdc4 signaling coordinating matrix remodeling across kidney, heart, and liver [36] (Fig. 4C). These paracrine networks progressively lock ECs into pro-fibrotic maladaptive plasticity, yet most mechanisms have been characterized in mouse models with human validation limited to in vitro systems or biomarker correlations (urinary SEMA3C, aqueous humor COL1A1). Direct single-cell spatial profiling of human diabetic tissues is essential to validate which multicellular interactions constrain endothelial plasticity in patients and represent therapeutic targets.

Even when TGF-β signaling is suppressed and paracrine networks disrupted, ECs that have adopted pro-fibrotic phenotypes often fail to restore normal function. This irreversibility reflects epigenetic modifications that constrain plasticity through heritable chromatin changes. The lysine methyltransferase SETD7 connects hyperglycemia to fibrotic programming through its target gene IGFBP5, which serves as a transcriptional hub for fibrotic remodeling (Fig. 4B). In STZ-induced diabetic mouse kidneys, Set7 deletion suppresses NF-κB and JAK-STAT signaling, reduces mitochondrial ROS, and downregulates pro-fibrotic genes while ameliorating albuminuria [74]. Pharmacological Set7 inhibition with PFI-2 produces similar anti-fibrotic effects in cultured human glomerular ECs. Single-cell studies reveal IGFBP5 upregulation in glomerular ECs of BTBRob/ob diabetic kidneys [69] and late-stage endothelial clusters in db/db mouse hearts [76], where it drives endothelial-stromal crosstalk activating fibroblast programs [67] (Fig. 4C, 5B). Cross-species validation confirms IGFBP5 enrichment in glomerular and peritubular ECs in human kidney single-cell transcriptomic data and the Nephrocell database, and a DKD GWAS independently maps IGFBP5 to a kidney function locus [69, 134, 135], reinforcing IGFBP5 upregulation as a conserved feature of glomerular EC dysfunction across murine and human DKD.

Super-enhancer-driven noncoding RNAs provide an additional epigenetic mechanism of promoting pro-fibrotic EC plasticity. The long noncoding RNA LINC00607 is upregulated in ECs from human diabetic arteries alongside pro-inflammatory and pro-fibrotic genes [85], with chromatin profiling confirming H3K27ac and H3K4me1 enrichment at its locus indicating an active super-enhancer. Under high-glucose and TNFα stress, c-Myc directly induces LINC00607, and its knockdown reduced fibrotic genes (FN1, SERPINE1, COL4A1, TGFB2), validating its role as a fibrotic stabilizer. Critically, LINC00607 is human-specific and absent in mice, representing a fundamental limitation of mouse models for epigenetic studies. This human-specific enhancer-based regulation may explain why human diabetic vascular disease exhibits more persistent and less reversible fibrotic remodeling compared to mouse models, demonstrating the need for direct epigenomic mapping of human diabetic endothelium.

Transcriptional control of EndoMT: protective gatekeepers and stress-activated drivers

The balance between endothelial identity maintenance and EndoMT is governed by opposing transcriptional programs. In diabetes, loss of protective KLF family gatekeepers combined with upregulation of stress-activated drivers (SOX4, ATF3) tips this balance toward EndoMT. KLF2 is a shear-responsive factor that maintains anti-inflammatory programs, junctional integrity, and barrier stability, yet hyperglycemia suppresses KLF2 expression. In DDC-fed Ldlr−/− hearts, Klf2/Klf4-high endothelial cell clusters progressively depleted while EndoMT-like clusters expanded [83] (Fig. 6A). Human DCM shows similar features, with endothelial marker (CD31) co-localizing with mesenchymal marker (SM22) and altered expression of PECAM1/CDH5/COL1A1/TAGLN [136]. GLP-1 receptor agonists restore KLF program: liraglutide activates KLF2 through ERK5-dependent signaling and dulaglutide prevents p53-mediated KLF2 repression [137, 138]. Whether GLP-1 receptor agonists also restore KLF4 or upregulate KLF11 remains unknown, though their shared anti-oxidative and anti-inflammatory effects suggest potential convergence on the KLF family protective program.

Unlike KLF2, diabetic stress upregulates KLF11 as a compensatory response that directly represses EndoMT by depositing repressive histone marks at TXNIP, SNAI1, and NOTCH1 promoters [86] (Fig. 6B). Single-cell RNA-seq of DDC-fed Ldlr−/− aortas showed that endothelial-Klf11 knockout expanded EndoMT clusters with mesenchymal markers (Fn1, Vim, Acta2, Col3a1) and worsened lesion burden, while overexpression stabilized plaques [86]. KLF11 protein is elevated in aortic endothelium from hyperglycemic patients, with knockdown in cultured coronary artery ECs amplifying oxidative and mesenchymal responses to high glucose while overexpression reverses them. These findings indicate that KLF11 induction represents an endogeneous, but ultimately insufficient, counter-regulatory mechanism against EndoMT.

In contrast, stress-activated transcription factors SOX4 and ATF3 actively drive EndoMT across multiple diabetic vascular beds. Single-cell profiling of western diet-fed ApoE−/− mouse aortae revealed mesenchymal-like endothelial subsets enriched for SOX4, with trajectory analysis suggesting progressive EndoMT [84] (Fig. 6B). Pro-inflammatory and mechanical stressors (IL-1β, TGF-β, disturbed flow) upregulate SOX4, which promotes EndoMT by upregulating mesenchymal marker induction (ACTA2, VIM, FN1, PRSS23) with loss of PECAM1 and eNOS, these effects were indirectly attenuated by metformin in vitro. SOX4 knockdown attenuates EndoMT, whereas overexpression accelerates plaque burden. SOX4-positive ECs have been observed in human coronary and renal atherosclerotic plaques [84], and elevated SOX4 expression has been detected in peripheral blood monocytes of DR patients [139]. ATF3 represents a complementary stress-responsive regulator linking oxidative stress to both EndoMT and endothelial apoptosis. ATF3 is upregulated in late-stage db/db mouse heart venous ECs, where it directly activates TGF-β signaling [78, 140]. In human atherosclerotic plaques, ATF3 expression colocalizes with apoptotic ECs [141], marking terminal loss of adaptive plasticity. Through these transcriptional drivers, EndoMT converts barrier-forming, anti-thrombotic ECs into matrix-producing, pro-fibrotic mesenchymal cells that contribute to vascular rarefaction, increased stiffness, and organ fibrosis across diabetic kidney, heart, and retina [142].

EndoMT represents the terminal endpoint of maladaptive endothelial plasticity in diabetes. Metabolic exhaustion, chronic inflammation, and dysregulated angiogenic signaling create a permissive landscape in which TGF-β superfamily activation initiates mesenchymal reprogramming; paracrine signals from surrounding mesangial cells, pericytes, and vascular smooth muscle cells amplify and propogate this state. Epigenetic remodeling stabilizes mesenchymal identity, and the combined loss of protective transcriptional gatekeepers (KLF2/KLF4) combined with stress-induced drivers (SOX4/ATF3) complete the transition. This phenotypic conversion transforms barrier-forming ECs into matrix-producing effectors driving vascular rarefaction and organ fibrosis, representing the final dimension of maladaptive endothelial plasticity where cells lose both function and identity.

Therapeutic implications for targeting maladaptive endothelial plasticity

Single-cell transcriptomic insights establish that restoring adaptive endothelial plasticity requires coordinated multi-pathway intervention rather than single-pathway approaches that encounter inevitable compensatory activation. The mechanistic understanding of how diabetes drives maladaptive endothelial plasticity across dysregulated angiogenesis, inflammatory reprogramming, metabolic dysfunction, and EndoMT provides a foundation for precision therapeutic strategies addressing multiple intersecting mechanisms simultaneously. However, critical questions remain regarding therapeutic timing, combination strategies, human-specific targets, patient stratification, and organ-specific approaches.First, the therapeutic window for restoring adaptive endothelial plasticity remains undefined: at what disease stage maladaptive plasticity becomes irreversible, and whether metabolic interventions can restore plasticity in established complications or must be deployed earlier, are open questions that require biomarkers capable of distinguishing reversible from irreversible endothelial states. Second, whether multi-pathway combination therapies are superior to sequential single-pathway approaches remains untested, including which pathway combinations are synergistic versus redundant and what endothelial-specific endpoints should replace HbA1c in trial design. Third, human-specific epigenetic programs such as the super-enhancer lncRNA LINC00607, absent in mice, stabilize fibrotic programming in ways that preclinical models cannot capture, and the full extent of human-specific epigenetic regulators constraining therapeutic responses remains uncharacterized. Fourth, whether endothelial plasticity profiling through single-cell biopsy or circulating biomarkers can stratify patients by dominant maladaptive axis, enabling inflammatory-enriched profiles to receive cytokine blockade while EndoMT-dominant profiles receive anti-fibrotic strategies, remains clinically untested. Fifth, pan-vascular targets such ANGPTL4 drive dysfunction across kidney, heart, and retina, yet whether uniform systemic inhibition achieves equivalent benefit across organs with distinct barrier properties and metabolic demands requires organ-specific investigation. Addressing these questions will determine whether mechanistic insights can be translated into effective clinical interventions.

Critically, the therapeutic window for restoring adaptive EC plasticity is not uniform across disease progression. In early-to-intermediate disease states, where EC identity remains partially intact and maladaptive plasticity has not yet become epigenetically stabilized, metabolic restoration, anti-angiogenic, and anti-inflammatory strategies act on simultaneously active mechanisms and are most effective when deployed in combination rather than in sequence. As disease progresses toward senescent accumulation and irreversible mesenchymal reprogramming, senolytic and anti-fibrotic interventions become necessary to address cellular states beyond the reach of earlier-stage strategies. The therapeutic approaches discussed below are organized to reflect this two-stage framework. Early-to-intermediate stage interventions: metabolic restoration combined with anti-angiogenic and anti-inflammatory strategies

Endothelial homeostasis depends on the NO-cGMP signaling axis, where endothelial NO synthase (eNOS)-derived NO activates soluble guanylate cyclase to generate cGMP and support vasodilation, barrier integrity, and anti-inflammatory tone [172]. In diabetes, oxidative stress and eNOS uncoupling impair this pathway [173], which can be restored through upstream NO enhancement (GLP-1 receptor agonists, SGLT2 inhibitors, metformin) or downstream cGMP preservation (soluble guanylate cyclase stimulators, PDE5 inhibitors). GLP-1 receptor agonists bind the GLP-1 receptor on vascular endothelium to activate cAMP-PKA-dependent signaling, producing direct endothelial effects that are independent of their glycemic actions and include suppression of mitochondrial-STING inflammation [63], restoration of KLF2 and eNOS expression [137], and reduced DR incidence clinically [121] (Fig. 5B). H-151, a small-molecule STING inhibitor that blocks STING palmitoylation and downstream TBK1-IRF3-NF-κB signaling, suppressed pathological CNV and vascular leakage in a murine nAMD model [150], providing ocular proof-of-concept for STING inhibition as a complementary anti-angiogenic and anti-inflammatory strategy warranting evaluation in the diabetic retinal context where STING-high ECs drive maladaptive endothelial reprogramming(Fig. 5B).

Shifting from the retinal to the renal vascular bed, similar principles of metabolic endothelial protection apply but through distinct organ-specific mechanisms. SGLT2 inhibitors such as dapagliflozin block the sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 in the renal proximal tubule to reduce glucotoxicity and promote a metabolic shift toward ketone body utilization. Dapagliflozin is approved for DKD and HFrEF based on the DAPA-CKD (NCT03036150) [151] and DAPA-HF (NCT03036124) trials respectively. At the vascular level, SGLT2 inhibitors improve endothelial function in diabetic patients [174], indirectly attenuates podocyte-endothelial BMP6 signaling in preclinical models through mechanisms that remain incompletely characterized [71, 130] (Fig. 4C), and appears to restore eNOS coupling and mitochondrial redox balance [175]. Notably, snRNA-seq profiling of the db/db uninephrectomy renin-hypertension model demonstrates that no monotherapy alone fully normalizes GEC-to-podocyte BMP6 crosstalk. Complete normalization required combination of renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system blockade via an ACE inhibitor with either an SGLT2 inhibitor or rosiglitazone, a PPARγ agonist that improves insulin sensitivity by activating a nuclear receptor regulating glucose and lipid metabolism [71], underscoring that multi-pathway intervention is necessary to resolve established glomerular EC-podocyte paracrine dysregulation.

Beyond SGLT2 inhibition, additional metabolic agents target overlapping but distinct nodes of the same NO-cGMP pathway. Metformin activates AMPK-eNOS to enhance NO bioavailability [164] and has been shown to attenuate SOX4 levels and EndoMT markers in vitro [84] (Fig. 6B), though the intermediate pathways mediating this effect remain incompletely characterized, positioning metformin as an agent that addresses both metabolic dysfunction at this stage and partially restrains the epigenetic reprogramming that drives late-stage progression. In diabetic PVAT, eNOS-dependent NO production is disrupted through depletion of Pdpn + macrophages, whose secreted omega-3 fatty acids DHA and EPA normally restore eNOS phosphorylation via GPR120 signaling on ECs [91]. Clinical evidence demonstrates that omega-3 supplementation enhances endothelial NO bioavailability and improves endothelial function in patients with primary antiphospholipid syndrome [170]. Icosapent ethyl (AMR101/Vascepa), an FDA-approved ethyl ester of EPA, represents the most clinically advanced candidate. Notably, in the REDUCE-IT trial ( [171]; NCT01492361), icosapent ethyl significantly reduced major adverse cardiovascular events, supporting its repurposing potential for restoring adaptive endothelial plasticity in the diabetic adipose vascular context.

When upstream NO restoration is insufficient, downstream cGMP preservation and orthogonal metabolic targeting offer complementary strategies applicable across vascular beds where eNOS uncoupling is documented. Soluble guanylate cyclase stimulator (vericiguat) binds directly to the reduced heme moiety of soluble guanylate cyclase to sensitize the enzyme to low NO levels and augment cGMP production independent of eNOS activity [156]. Phosphodiesterase 5 (PDE5), the enzyme responsible for cGMP degradation, terminates NO-cGMP-PKG signaling by converting cGMP to its inactive form; in diabetes, where NO bioavailability is already reduced due to eNOS uncoupling, unopposed PDE5 activity further depletes the limited cGMP pool, amplifying endothelial dysfunction. Sildenafil competitively inhibits PDE5, preventing cGMP degradation and prolonging NO-cGMP-PKG signaling independently of upstream NO production, together with vericiguat acting downstream to re-establish this axis [176]. Neither agent has a direct single-cell evidence anchor in diabetic cardiac or large vessel ECs; their inclusion reflects the well-established role of eNOS uncoupling in diabetic endothelial dysfunction. Vericiguat demonstrated cardiovascular benefit in the VICTORIA trial (NCT02861534) with efficacy preserved in diabetic patients [156], supporting its repurposing potential for DCM, while sildenafil improved flow-mediated dilation and endothelial function in T2D (NCT00527995) [165], suggesting broader applicability to diabetic endothelial dysfunction by restoring healthy endothelial cell plasticity through reactivation of NO-dependent signaling.

Beyond the NO-cGMP axis, targeting endothelial metabolic reprogramming in the retina offers additional early-stage opportunities. CHKA inhibition using MN58b reduced pathological sprouting and vascular leakage in DR models by modulating NAD + levels and SIRT1-Notch signaling [53] (Fig. 5B). The oncology-derived CHKA inhibitor (RSM-932A) provides mechanistic precedent for vascular applications [143] (Fig. 5B). ACER2 upregulation restores barrier integrity through ceramide-sphingosine-1-phosphate metabolism in DR models [54], while cholesterol supplementation corrects downregulation of cholesterol biosynthesis genes to prevent postoperative corneal endothelial edema in diabetic contexts [64] (Fig. 5B).

While metabolic correction addresses the biochemical substrate of endothelial dysfunction, it does not fully resolve the aberrant angiogenic reprogramming that metabolic disturbance has already triggered, necessitating concurrent anti-angiogenic intervention. Anti-VEGF therapies, which neutralize VEGFA to suppress pathological neovascularization and vascular leakage, remain standard-of-care for DR [96], yet incomplete therapeutic response and treatment resistance necessitate identification of alternative angiogenic pathways. Single-cell analyses reveal compensatory mechanisms including VEGFC upregulation [65, 98], MFAP4-integrin signaling from pericytes and glia [60], TUBB6-driven WNT pathway activation [59], and ANGPTL4-mediated barrier destabilization that remains elevated during anti-VEGF therapy [50]. Directly targeting this VEGFC-mediated resistance, OPT-302 is a soluble fusion protein that traps and neutralizes VEGF-C and VEGF-D by mimicking the extracellular domain of VEGFR-3, preventing their binding to endothelial receptors; in combination with anti-VEGFA ranibizumab (Figs. 5A) [97]. OPT-302 demonstrated superior visual acuity gain over anti-VEGF monotherapy in neovascular age-related macular degeneration (nAMD; NCT03345082), providing clinical proof-of-concept for dual VEGF family blockade that warrants evaluation in DR where concurrent VEGFA and VEGFC upregulation similarly drives treatment resistance (Fig. 5A). Neutralizing antibodies targeting MFAP4 (hAS0326) and ANGPTL4 (MAR001) demonstrated sustained efficacy in non-human primate models [50, 60] (Fig. 5C, 6C), validating these VEGF-independent pathways as druggable targets for overcoming anti-VEGF resistance. Clinical translation of ANGPTL4 blockade is already underway, with MAR001 currently being evaluated in phase 1b/2a and phase 2b trials in adults with metabolic dysfunction and hypertriglyceridemia (NCT05896254, NCT07028749) [144].

While targeting VEGF-independent angiogenic pathways addresses compensatory resistance mechanisms, the upstream disruption of vascular stability driven by pericyte loss in the diabetic retina requires direct restoration of the ANGPT1-Tie2 quiescence axis. In the healthy retina, pericytes enforce vessel stabilization through ANGPT1-Tie2 signaling, and their loss in diabetes reduces ANGPT1-mediated Tie2 activation while shifting the balance toward destabilizing ANGPT2 signaling, collectively inverting perivascular EC crosstalk from quiescence maintenance toward pathological activation [101, 102]. Faricimab, a bispecific antibody targeting both VEGFA and ANGPT2, is FDA-approved for DR and DME, simultaneously suppressing pathological neovascularization and preventing ANGPT2-mediated Tie2 destabilization(Fig. 5A)(YOSEMITE/RHINE: NCT03622580, NCT03622593) [145]. However, in advanced DR where pericyte depletion has sufficiently reduced ANGPT1 availability, ANGPT2 neutralization alone cannot fully restore Tie2 signaling. In this context, AKB-9778 (razuprotafib) inhibits vascular endothelial protein tyrosine phosphatase (VE-PTP/PTPRB), the phosphatase responsible for dephosphorylating and inactivating Tie2, thereby sustaining Tie2 phosphorylation independently of ANGPT1 ligand availability(Fig. 5A). This ligand-independent mechanism is particularly relevant in advanced DR, where pericyte depletion reduces ANGPT1 sufficiently that ANGPT2 blockade alone cannot fully restore Tie2 signaling. Consistent with this, AKB-9778 improved retinal vascular outcomes when combined with ranibizumab in the phase 2b TIME-2b trial for DME (NCT03197870; [146]), supporting ligand-independent Tie2 activation as a clinically validated strategy for restoring vascular stability in advanced diabetic retinal disease.

Conversely, DFUs and ischemic limbs, and the diabetic hearts exhibit impaired angiogenesis requiring pro-angiogenic interventions to restore adaptive vessel growth. RAB17 restoration rescues MAPK/ERK-dependent endothelial angiogenic function in diabetic wounds [87], while SH3BGRL3 upregulation promotes endothelial proliferation and migration under high glucose conditions [89]. Inhibition of miR-200b, a microRNA that suppresses VEGFR2 and Ets-1 transcription factor expression, restores vasculogenic endothelial populations in diabetic ischemic limb models [90], with clinical correlative studies linking miR-200b expression to diabetic wound healing (NCT02581098). ScRNA-seq profiling of db/db mouse hearts and HFHSD-fed aortic ECs similarly identifies impaired regenerative capacity through reduced Arnt, Stat5, and YAP-KDR signaling [75, 81], reinforcing that cardiac and large vessel contexts share with DFUs a pro-angiogenic therapeutic requirement that stands in direct contrast to the anti-angiogenic strategies needed in the diabetic retina.

Alongside metabolic and angiogenic agents, anti-inflammatory strategies targeting immune-endothelial crosstalk complete the early-stage combination framework by disrupting the inflammatory reprogramming that amplifies both angiogenic dysregulation and metabolic exhaustion. Maladaptive inflammatory endothelial plasticity can be targeted using clinically validated immunomodulators that disrupt immune-endothelial crosstalk. IL-17A blockade can be achieved through secukinumab and ixekizumab, monoclonal antibodies that directly neutralize IL-17A, or brodalumab, a monoclonal antibody targeting the IL-17RA receptor subunit to block signaling by IL-17A, IL-17F, and related family members; all three demonstrate robust efficacy in psoriasis (NCT01961609, NCT01646177, NCT01708603) [147, 148] (Fig. 5C), suggesting potential for repurposing to target IL-17A-driven endothelial dysfunction in diabetic retina [51] and kidney [68], where vitreous IL-17A correlates with PDR severity [115, 116]. IL-1 axis blockade can be achieved through anakinra, a recombinant IL-1 receptor antagonist that competitively inhibits both IL-1α and IL-1β binding to IL-1R1 (VCU-ART3 STEMI trial NCT01950299), or canakinumab, a human monoclonal antibody that selectively neutralizes IL-1β to interrupt downstream NF-κB and MAPK inflammatory signaling (CANTOS cardiovascular outcomes trial NCT01327846) [157, 158], and may modulate macrophage-endothelial inflammatory networks in the diabetic heart [78] (Fig. 6C). Nadunolimab (CAN04) targets IL-1 receptor accessory protein (IL1RAP), the obligate co-receptor required for both IL-1α and IL-1β signaling through IL-1R1, thereby achieving broader IL-1 pathway suppression than receptor-specific agents [159], and is currently being evaluated in phase I/II solid tumor trials (NCT03267316; NCT05116891), representing a repurposing candidate for macrophage-endothelial inflammatory crosstalk in the diabetic heart, pending vascular-specific evaluation (Fig. 6C). AMG-108, a fully human monoclonal antibody that blocks IL-1R1 to prevent binding of both IL-1α and IL-1β (NCT00293826) [160], represents an additional candidate for modulating the same pathological crosstalk (Fig. 6C). TNF pathway blockade can be achieved through infliximab and adalimumab, chimeric and fully human monoclonal antibodies respectively that neutralize soluble and membrane-bound TNF, or etanercept, a dimeric fusion protein combining the TNF receptor 2 extracellular domain with the Fc region of IgG1 to act as a soluble decoy receptor; all three have been associated with reduced DR incidence in observational studies [161], suggesting potential to counter pericyte-driven endothelial inflammation in the diabetic heart [77], though prior cardiovascular trials highlight the need for careful cardiac safety monitoring [162] (Fig. 6C). Beyond direct immune-endothelial signaling, glial-mediated inflammatory activation represents an additional upstream driver of pathological EC reprogramming in the retina. Abemaciclib, a CDK4/6 inhibitor approved for hormone receptor-positive, HER2-negative breast cancer (MONARCH-2 NCT02107703; MONARCH-3 NCT02246621) where it arrests tumour cells in G1 by preventing retinoblastoma protein phosphorylation, may be repurposed to target the inflammatory-angiogenesis axis by suppressing proliferative microglia driving pathological EC activation in models of hypoxia-driven retinopathy, though direct evidence in hyperglycemic diabetic models is currently lacking, representing an important translational gap to address [62] (Fig. 5C).

Late-stage interventions: senolytic and anti-fibrotic therapies targeting irreversible EC reprogramming

Senescent EC accumulation and epigenetically stabilized EndoMT represent late-stage manifestations of maladaptive endothelial plasticity because both reflect cellular states in which the molecular programs driving dysfunction have become epigenetically reinforced, extending beyond what metabolic, anti-angiogenic, or anti-inflammatory interventions alone can resolve. At this stage, therapeutic intervention aims to clear the senescent cells sustaining the pro-inflammatory microenvironment and to halt the fibrotic stabilization that progressively narrows the window for EC identity recovery. These processes are addressed together because both converge on the same vascular microenvironment in advanced diabetic vascular disease, and targeting them in combination reflects the mechanistic complexity that defines this stage of maladaptive endothelial plasticity.

Senolytic therapies may restore adaptive endothelial plasticity by clearing senescent cells that exhibit impaired NO signaling, heightened oxidative stress, and pro-inflammatory secretory phenotypes [177]. Senescent ECs upregulate BCL-xL as a survival mechanism to resist apoptosis despite permanent cell cycle arrest; the BCL-xL inhibitor UBX1325 (foselutoclax) removes this survival advantage, selectively triggering apoptosis in senescent cells while sparing healthy ECs that do not depend on BCL-xL for survival, demonstrating safety and anatomical improvement in phase 1 trials for diabetic macular edema (NCT04537884) [149] (Fig. 5C). The dasatinib-quercetin combination exploits the observation that senescent cells depend on multiple overlapping pro-survival networks to resist apoptosis(Fig. 4C); dasatinib, a tyrosine kinase inhibitor, disables src-family kinase and receptor tyrosine kinase pro-survival signaling, while quercetin inhibits PI3K/AKT and BCL-2 family activity, with the two agents acting synergistically to selectively eliminate senescent cells that no single agent alone can clear, and together reduced senescent cell burden systemically in diabetic kidney disease patients (NCT02848131) [152, 153]. Fisetin, a flavonoid that induces apoptosis in senescent cells by inhibiting PI3K/AKT signaling and reducing BCL-2 family pro-survival activity, selectively targeting cells with the senescent metabolic profile, improved vascular function in preclinical models [169], with an early clinical trial underway to test its effects on vascular endothelial function in older adults (NCT06133634) (Fig. 6C). Collectively, these approaches suggest that selective clearance of senescent ECs could reopen a window for adaptive plasticity in metabolically stressed vascular beds.

EndoMT represents a core mechanism of vascular fibrosis driven by TGF-β superfamily signaling, reinforced through paracrine endothelial-stromal networks, and stabilized by epigenetic modifications. TGF-β pathway inhibition can be achieved through galunisertib, a TGF-β receptor I kinase inhibitor that competitively occupies the ATP-binding site of TGF-βRI to prevent ALK5-mediated phosphorylation of Smad2/3 and downstream Smad-dependent mesenchymal activation, with established safety in cancer trials that suppresses Smad-dependent mesenchymal activation (e.g. NCT02423343, NCT02688712) [154] (Fig. 4C). Single-cell transcriptomic profiling of ECs from diabetic cardiovascular tissue identifies Tgfbr2 as a marker of EndMT + EC subpopulations expanded under diabetogenic conditions in pooled heart and aortic tissue, providing SC-level rationale for ALK5 inhibition at the site of mesenchymal commitment(Fig. 6C) [83]. Alternatively, the dual integrin αvβ1/αvβ6 antagonist bexotegrast can prevent latent TGF-β activation by blocking the integrin-mediated mechanical release of active TGF-β from its latency-associated peptide complex, acting upstream of receptor engagement where galunisertib acts downstream. Currently in phase 2 development for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (NCT06097260), bexotegrast represents a repurposing candidate for diabetic vascular fibrosis where the same integrin-mediated TGF-β activation axis drives glomerular and cardiac EC mesenchymal reprogramming(Fig. 4C) [155]. Both agents may address the LTBP1/TGFB2/ITGB8 upregulation observed in diabetic kidney [65].

Paracrine endothelial-stromal networks provide additional therapeutic entry points. Imatinib, a tyrosine kinase inhibitor originally developed for oncology, disrupts the Pdgf(s)-Pdgfra fibrotic axis in diabetic hearts, where cardiac ECs upregulate Pdgfb and Pdgfd to drive fibroblast Pdgfra activation and collagen deposition; imatinib treatment in HFD/STZ and db/db diabetic mice reduces collagen accumulation and improves LV function, providing direct preclinical proof-of-concept for this repurposing strategy [79] (Fig. 6C), though cardiotoxicity warrants monitoring. Preclinical anti-PDGF-BB designed ankyrin repeat proteins (DARPins), small engineered binding proteins with antibody-like target specificity and superior tissue penetrance, neutralize endothelial-derived PDGFB upstream of receptor engagement to potentially prevent fibroblast activation and fibrotic remodeling in the diabetic heart [75, 79, 163], though no clinical translation exists yet. In the kidney, SEMA3C mediates mesangial-endothelial fibrotic signaling in DKD [73], though no specific inhibitors currently exist beyond gene silencing or indirect blockade through TGF-β or neuropilin pathways [178].

Epigenetic stabilization of mesenchymal programming occurs through SETD7-IGFBP5 signaling in diabetic kidney glomerular ECs, targetable using SETD7 inhibitor PFI-2, which occupies the substrate-binding groove of SETD7 to competitively block histone H3K4 monomethylation, thereby suppressing NF-κB and JAK-STAT signaling while attenuating pro-fibrotic gene expression (Fig. 4B); however, PFI-2 remains a tool compound with no clinical-grade equivalent currently available [74]. The human-specific super-enhancer lncRNA LINC00607, which is upregulated in human diabetic arterial endothelium, reinforces fibrotic gene expression by supporting c-Myc-dependent enhancer activity [85]. OTX015 (birabresib) is a bromodomain and extra-terminal (BET) inhibitor originally developed in oncology (NCT01713582, NCT02259114) that competitively displaces bromodomain-containing protein 4 (BRD4) from acetylated histone lysine residues at super-enhancer regions, disproportionately collapsing super-enhancer-driven transcription [166, 167]. Given that LINC00607 is a super-enhancer-derived lncRNA upregulated in diabetic human vascular ECs and drives endothelial fibrotic reprogramming, BET inhibition represents a potential epigenetic strategy to suppress this axis in human diabetic vascular fibrosis. Complementarily, Omomyc (OMO-103), originally developed in oncology (NCT04808362), is a dominant negative miniprotein that carries the basic helix-loop-helix leucine zipper domain of c-Myc with stabilizing mutations, enabling heterodimerization with MAX while preventing E-box DNA binding, thereby competitively displacing endogenous c-Myc-MAX complexes from fibrotic target gene loci [168]. The human specificity of LINC00607 reinforces the broader imperative to directly map and target human diabetic endothelial epigenomes when advancing anti-fibrotic strategies into the clinic, as preclinical models alone will not capture this layer of epigenetic stabilization.

Limitations, critical gaps, and future directions for restoring adaptive endothelial plasticity through single-cell approaches

Limitations and critical gaps

Despite major advances, fundamental limitations constrain the translation of endothelial single-cell discoveries into therapeutic strategies. Mechanistic insights derive predominantly from mouse models depicting different stages of diabetic complications without fully recapitulating human disease complexity. However, most murine models do not naturally develop advanced fibrovascular membranes characteristic of human PDR [179], limiting the ability to model late-stage angiogenic-fibrotic remodeling. In addition, species-specific divergences in molecular pathways, lack of macular structures in mice [180], use of young animals without comorbidities, and absence of comprehensive multi-organ complication modeling limit translational applicability.

Most critically, human single-cell profiling remains insufficient, with datasets deriving primarily from end-stage disease including proliferative DR fibrovascular membranes [61] and advanced nephropathy [65], while DCM remains largely unrepresented. Healthy control samples are rarely available as non-diabetic patients do not typically require the surgical procedures that enable tissue collection, creating a critical gap in defining baseline endothelial states and distinguishing early disease transitions from end-stage pathology [181]. This problem is further compounded by uneven vascular bed coverage, with critical microvascular beds such as brain, hepatic sinusoids, and skeletal muscle capillaries remaining poorly represented, thereby limiting the generalizability of maladaptive plasticity mechanisms across diabetic organ systems [182, 183]. Additionally, the scope of this review intentionally prioritizes single-cell and single-nucleus transcriptomic studies in which endothelial populations were explicitly resolved and characterized. Studies that did not analyze endothelial cells at single-cell resolution were not included unless they directly informed endothelial state transitions through defined intercellular interactions. Consequently, non-endothelial cell types are discussed primarily in the context of their direct influence on endothelial plasticity rather than as independent drivers of diabetic pathology. A further limitation is the variable depth of experimental validation across the studies reviewed. While some datasets are accompanied by comprehensive functional interrogation through in vitro mechanistic experiments and in vivo genetic or pharmacological approaches, others rely on histological confirmation of candidate markers alone, and some remain entirely descriptive with no experimental follow-up beyond the sequencing data itself. The mechanistic depth with which individual signaling intermediates and pathway dynamics can be discussed is therefore inherently variable across vascular beds and bounded by the functional evidence each study provides.

Another critical gap is lack of sex-stratified analyses in studies exploring diabetic complications at the single-cell level. Biological sex influences diabetes risk, vascular complication trajectories, and therapeutic responses through both hormonal and sex-chromosome-linked mechanisms. Estrogen increases NO bioavailability and promotes vasodilation, conferring relative vasculoprotection in premenopausal females that is progressively lost after menopause, while diabetic women paradoxically experience greater excess cardiovascular risk than diabetic men, with more severe endothelium-dependent vasodilation impairment across microvascular beds [184, 185]. Compounding this, preclinical diabetic research has historically relied predominantly on male animals, as females often exhibit delayed, attenuated, or more variable hyperglycemic phenotypes in several widely used T2D models [186]. The single-cell datasets reviewed here predominantly derive from cohorts without reporting sex as a biological variable, precluding determination of whether the maladaptive endothelial states, transcriptional drivers, and multicellular crosstalk networks described are conserved, amplified, or divergent between sexes. Future single-cell studies must explicitly stratify by sex and hormonal status to determine whether precision therapeutic strategies targeting maladaptive endothelial plasticity require sex-specific calibration.

Additionally, ECs face unique technical challenges in single-cell RNA-seq due to their embedded position within tissue matrices requiring enzymatic dissociation that can induce artifactual stress response gene expression, obscuring true disease-associated programs [187, 188]. As a minority cell type, ECs are frequently under-represented in standard protocols, and some subpopulations exhibit dissociation sensitivity, leading to selective loss and bias toward stress-resistant subtypes while missing functionally critical stressed or dying populations [189, 190]. Incomplete transcript capture leads to dropout events where expressed genes fail detection, resulting in false negatives for lowly expressed transcripts [191]. High costs and requirements for fresh tissue limit sample sizes and accessibility. Single-nucleus RNA sequencing with enrichment strategies such as ERG-based fluorescence-activated nuclear sorting can overcome dissociation-induced stress artifacts and enable profiling of frozen archived tissues while enriching for sufficient endothelial representation [192].

A fundamental limitation of current single-cell approaches is their inability to capture hemodynamic forces [193], despite shear stress being a major regulator of endothelial plasticity. Laminar shear stress promotes quiescent, anti-inflammatory endothelial phenotypes through KLF2/KLF4 activation, while disturbed flow patterns characteristic of T2D induce inflammatory and pro-atherosclerotic programs [18]. This is particularly problematic for diabetes research, where altered hemodynamics interact with metabolic stress to drive vascular complications. Additionally, single time-point snapshots cannot capture temporal dynamics of maladaptive endothelial plasticity, making longitudinal profiling essential to distinguish transient adaptive responses from stable pathological endpoints. Technical variability, batch effects [194], clustering ambiguity [195] and computational assumptions in trajectory inference and cell–cell communication analysis introduce substantial noise [196].

Current mechanistic validations further limit translational applicability by relying predominantly on HRMECs or HUVECs. These cell lines lack organ-specific specialization and do not reproduce physiological shear stress, metabolic gradients, or tissue-specific paracrine signals that define endothelial behavior in vivo [197]. HUVECs in particular represent large vessel endothelium with distinct transcriptional profiles from diabetic-relevant microvascular beds. Advanced vascularized organoid systems that recapitulate organ-specific endothelial phenotypes, physiological flow patterns, metabolic gradients, and multicellular interactions represent critical next-generation platforms for validating plasticity mechanisms and testing therapeutic interventions under conditions that more faithfully mirror human diabetic microenvironments [198].

These technical and model-level constraints extend to a deeper omics-level gap, namely the imperfect concordance between mRNA abundance and protein levels. Using SPARC, a single-cell co-profiling approach that simultaneously measures global mRNA and intracellular proteins in the same cell, Reimegård et al. demonstrated that transcript levels are poor predictors of protein abundance at the time of measurement, driven by the stochastic nature of transcription, differential mRNA and protein half-lives, and the temporal lag between transcription and translation [199]. This means that transcriptomic signatures of maladaptive endothelial states may not faithfully reflect the underlying functional proteome, and therapeutic targets nominated solely on the basis of differential gene expression require protein-level validation to confirm biological relevance. Beyond proteomics, metabolomic integration remains equally underdeveloped in diabetic endothelial research. While metabolomic studies have been conducted in diabetic tissues and biofluids, dedicated EC-specific metabolomic profiling integrated with single-cell transcriptomic data remains sparse. Since metabolic dysfunction is a central and mechanistically upstream axis of maladaptive EC plasticity, this gap is particularly consequential. Integrating metabolomics with transcriptomic and proteomic datasets through multi-omics frameworks would enable direct quantification of metabolic alterations, uncover vulnerabilities that are invisible to RNA-based approaches alone, and provide a stronger mechanistic basis for therapeutic target prioritization and patient stratification. Collectively, these limitations do not diminish the insights reviewed here but define the conditions under which they can be translated into durable therapeutic strategies for diabetic vascular disease.

Future directions

The failure of monotherapies across diabetic vascular beds reflects the interconnected nature of the four maladaptive axes, where dysregulated angiogenesis with barrier destabilization, inflammatory reprogramming through immune-endothelial crosstalk, metabolic dysfunction progressing from mitochondrial stress to senescence, and EndoMT with fibrosis converge and mutually reinforce one another, such that durable restoration of adaptive plasticity requires multi-pathway combinations timed to disease stage and calibrated to organ-specific context. In the diabetic ocular vascular bed, combining GLP-1 receptor agonists with IL-17A blockade restores mitochondrial homeostasis while disrupting Th17-driven inflammatory circuits, pairing anti-VEGFA therapy with MFAP4 or ANGPTL4 inhibition, OPT-302, or Tie2-activating agents (faricimab or razuprotafib) overcomes compensatory angiogenic resistance, while UBX1325 is added at late stage to clear senescent ECs. In the diabetic kidney, an early foundation of ACEi combined with SGLT2 inhibitors and IL-17A blockade is followed by dasatinib-quercetin senolysis combined with Bexotegrast or galunisertib to suppress TGF-β-driven fibrotic progression, with SETD7 inhibition representing an additional epigenetic layer that currently lacks a clinical-grade agent. In the diabetic heart, early-stage combintion of vericiguat with broad IL-1 axis and TNF blockade alongside ANGPTL4 neutralization is followed by transition to galunisertib or imatinib to disrupt TGF-β and PDGF-driven fibrotic remodeling. In large vessels and the aorta, metformin and sildenafil provide an early metabolic-vasoprotective foundation, while late-stage EndoMT and fibrosis require galunisertib combined with OTX015/birabresib or Omomyc, and fisetin senolysis to address aortic stiffness and the senescence-associated secretory phenotype. While Table 2 outlines single-pathway therapeutic entry points for DFUs and adipose vasculopathy informed by single-cell findings, combination frameworks for these beds remain nascent and have yet to be defined. Certain targets warrant prioritization across beds: ANGPTL4 drives pathological EC reprogramming across retinal [50], cardiac [80], and adipose endothelium [91], yet whether its inhibition achieves equivalent benefit across these contexts or requires organ-tailored dosing and combinatorial partners remains unresolved and warrants systematic investigation.

Advancing translation demands further integrated technological and methodological approaches. Spatial transcriptomics are essential to map endothelial state transitions within native tissue architecture, revealing how multicellular niches constrain or enable plasticity while avoiding dissociation artifacts [200]. Multi-omic integration combining transcriptomics with chromatin accessibility profiling will illuminate epigenetic mechanisms stabilizing pathological states [201], clarifying whether EndoMT and senescence represent reversible or irreversible endpoints of maladaptive endothelial plasticity. Comprehensive human endothelial profiling across early and intermediate disease stages using endothelial enrichment strategies, longitudinal studies capturing dynamic transitions within individual patients, and standardized experimental protocols minimizing technical variability remain critical needs.

Translating these single-cell insights into precision therapeutics requires prospective trials integrating sequential tissue profiling with clinical outcomes. Early proof-of-concept studies in diabetic kidney disease demonstrate the feasibility of this approach: the REMODEL trial (NCT04865770) employs single-nucleus and spatial RNA sequencing on paired kidney biopsies before and after 52 weeks of GLP-1RA (semaglutide) treatment to directly correlate endothelial-specific gene expression changes with multiparametric MRI assessments of tissue oxygenation, perfusion, and inflammation [202], while parallel trials investigating SGLT2 inhibitors (NCT05507892, NCT06291155) enable molecular comparison across drug classes at single-cell resolution. Extending this framework of sequential biopsies coupled with comprehensive single-cell profiling to retinopathy, neuropathy, and cardiomyopathy will be essential for identifying endothelial biomarkers predictive of treatment response and for enabling therapeutic selection guided by individual molecular phenotypes.

A critical dimension of precision medicine for diabetic vascular disease is defining not only which therapeutic targets are relevant, but also at which stage of maladaptive endothelial plasticity they remain actionable. As detailed in Sect. 6, the maladaptive trajectory represents a continuum of progressively narrowing therapeutic windows, where early-to-intermediate disease states where EC identity remains partially intact are best addressed through combination metabolic, anti-angiogenic, and anti-inflammatory strategies, while late-stage senescent accumulation and epigenetically stabilized EndoMT require senolytic and anti-fibrotic interventions targeting cellular states that have moved beyond transcriptional correction alone. Liquid biopsy approaches, which detect disease-relevant biomarkers in accessible biofluids such as blood, urine, and ocular fluids rather than requiring invasive tissue sampling, may enable non-invasive patient stratification within this framework. Biomarkers including aqueous humor CHKA [53] and MFAP4 [60], vitreous IL-17A [115, 116], circulating ANGPTL4 [104] and FABP4 [124], and urinary SEMA3C [73] and IGFBP5 [203] collectively reflect the dominant maladaptive axes across retinal, cardiac, and renal vascular beds, and may enable disease monitoring and therapeutic response assessment without requiring repeated tissue biopsy. Staging individual patients along this continuum using single-cell biopsy profiling or liquid biopsy biomarker signatures, then selecting therapeutic strategies matched to their predominant EC dysfunction state, represents a conceptually grounded but as yet clinically untested framework for precision endothelial medicine in diabetes. Clinical trial design must therefore evolve beyond HbA1c reduction toward endothelial-specific functional outcomes including barrier integrity, inflammatory markers, and vascular density measurements that directly assess restoration of adaptive endothelial plasticity.

Conclusion

Single-cell transcriptomics has fundamentally reframed diabetic vascular complications as disorders of maladaptive endothelial plasticity, revealing not isolated pathogenic pathways but an interconnected network in which dysregulated angiogenesis, inflammatory reprogramming, metabolic dysfunction, and fibrotic transformation continuously reinforce one another to progressively constrain the endothelium's adaptive capacity. Organ-specific combination therapies targeting these intersecting pathways hold promise for transforming diabetic vascular disease management from reactive suppression toward precision strategies capable of restoring the endothelium's capacity for reversible, tightly regulated state transitions. Yet critical gaps persist: human diabetic endothelium remains severely underrepresented in single-cell atlases, longitudinal profiling capturing early-to-late disease transitions is largely absent, and functional validation in physiologically relevant systems remains limited. Addressing these limitations through expanded human tissue profiling, integration of spatial and epigenomic technologies, and development of organ-specific vascularized models will be essential for translating mechanistic insights into clinical reality. Success in this endeavor will enable durable, tissue-specific vascular repair, ultimately addressing the global burden of diabetic vascular morbidity and mortality.
