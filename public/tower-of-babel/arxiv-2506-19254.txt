Abstract
. We study the Steinberg algebra with coefficients in an arbitrary field K K for the two- and three-headed snake groupoids, which are basic examples of non-Hausdorff groupoids. We are particularly interested in elements of this algebra that are no longer continuous, known as singular functions. We prove that the ideal of singular functions in the Steinberg algebra of the two-headed snake does not properly contain any non-zero ideal, regardless of the choice of the base field K K . In the case of the three-headed snake, we prove that the ideal of singular functions of the Steinberg algebra properly contains non-zero ideals if, and only if, the base field K K is a splitting field of x 2 + x + 1 x^{2}+x+1 . In particular, there are always non-zero proper subset ideals when K K has characteristic a prime p ≢ − 1 ​ ( mod ​ 3 ) p\not\equiv-1\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 3) .

1.
Introduction

Steinberg algebras were introduced in
[
9
]
as algebras with coefficients in the field

\mathbb{C}
of complex numbers. They are constructed from certain topological groupoids and have proven useful in purely algebraic settings as abstract models for inverse semigroup algebras
[
9
]
, Leavitt path algebras
[
1
]
, and Kumjian–Pask algebras
[
4
]
. Complex Steinberg algebras have also found applications in functional analysis, for example in
[
3
]
or more recently in
[
2
]
; they sit densely inside the associated groupoid C*-algebra
[
7
]
. The ability to move between algebraic and analytic frameworks has become a valuable technique for making progress in both areas.

At first, the specific choice of
\mathbb{C}
as field of coefficients did not appear to be particularly significant in the algebraic setting. However, when the groupoid is not Hausdorff, the study of the simplicity of such algebras
[
6
,
8
,
10
,
11
]
has made it clear that the choice of the field of coefficients, and more precisely its characteristic, plays a crucial role.

Given a field
K
, recall that a Steinberg algebra over
K
is defined as the
K
-linear span of characteristic functions of compact open sets. In a non-Hausdorff groupoid, we may have compact sets that are not closed, and so the associated Steinberg algebra may contain functions whose support has empty interior: such functions are called
singular functions
. From
[
6
, Theorem 3.14]
, we know that a Steinberg algebra that contains singular functions cannot be simple.

In this paper, we begin to study the relationship between the characteristic of the field
K
and the existence of proper non-zero ideals inside the ideal of singular functions of the Steinberg algebra. We consider the Steinberg algebras of the non-Hausdorff groupoids known as the
n
-headed snake
, for the cases where
n
is either
2
or
3
.

The two-headed snake
\mathcal{G}_{2S}

[
7
, Example 2.1]
is one of the most basic examples of a non-Hausdorff groupoid. For us, the body of the snake comprises the well-known Hausdorff Cantor set, which is the unit space of this groupoid. The fact that the groupoid
\mathcal{G}_{2S}
is non-Hausdorff is concentrated at one location: the two heads of the snake, denoted as
0
and
1
, which interact with each other as in
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
. The open sets of
\mathcal{G}_{2S}
are the open sets of the Cantor set together with copies of those sets with head
0
replaced by head
1
.
In this paper, we show that the singular functions of the associated Steinberg algebra
\mathcal{A}_{K}(\mathcal{G}_{2S})
are precisely those with support equal to
\{0,1\}
– that is, functions that are non-zero precisely on the heads of the snake.

In a Steinberg algebra
\mathcal{A}_{K}
, the set
\mathcal{S}_{K}
of singular functions is an ideal. We are interested in whether this ideal is
S
-simple
– that is, whether
\mathcal{S}_{K}
has non-trivial proper subsets that are also ideals of
\mathcal{A}_{K}
. We show that the ideal
\mathcal{S}_{K}(\mathcal{G}_{2S})
of singular functions in the Steinberg algebra
\mathcal{A}_{K}(\mathcal{G}_{2S})
is
S
-simple, regardless of the field
K
.

Next, we add a third head, denoted
2
, such that the three heads interact with each other as
\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}
: this is the three-headed snake groupoid
\mathcal{G}_{3S}
. Again, the fact that
\mathcal{G}_{3S}
is non-Hausdorff is confined to the heads, and we prove that the singular functions of the associated Steinberg algebra
\mathcal{A}_{K}(\mathcal{G}_{3S})
are those whose support is contained in
\{0,1,2\}
. However, with three heads, the
S
-simplicity of
\mathcal{S}_{K}(\mathcal{G}_{3S})
now depends on the choice of field
K
. Indeed, we prove that
\mathcal{S}_{K}(\mathcal{G}_{3S})
is not

-simple if, and only if,

is a

for
x^{2}+x+1
– that is, iff

contains roots of
x^{2}+x+1

In particular, this means that whenever the field

is algebraically closed (e.g. when

\mathcal{S}_{K}(\mathcal{G}_{3S})
is never

-simple.
Also, if

or if

is a prime congruent to
1

\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
is a splitting field for
x^{2}+x+1

p
.

We also prove that when
\mathcal{S}_{K}(\mathcal{G}_{3S})
is not
S
-simple, it contains precisely two proper subset ideals (resp. one proper subset ideal), associated with the roots of

is different from

(resp. is equal to

This paper focuses on the two- and three-headed snakes because the group structures of the heads –
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
and
\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}
respectively – are the most straightforward nontrivial options and provide already interesting properties. In future work, we will study arbitrary

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Ample groupoids and Steinberg algebras

This paper involves a detailed study of two particular groupoids: the two-headed snake
\mathcal{G}_{2S}
and the three-headed snake
\mathcal{G}_{3S}
, along with the Steinberg algebras associated to each.
We will work entirely in these two special cases, but provide some brief preliminaries for context. For a more detailed presentation of groupoids and Steinberg algebras, the interested reader can for example see
[
9
]
.

A
groupoid

\mathcal{G}
is a generalisation of a group where the binary operation is only partially defined. As a result, the identity, or ‘unit’, is not a single element, but a set of elements. We write
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
for the set of all units in
\mathcal{G}
. Each
\gamma\in\mathcal{G}
has an inverse
\gamma^{-1}\in\mathcal{G}
and we write
\textbf{r},\textbf{s}:\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
for the maps

\textbf{r}(\gamma)=\gamma\gamma^{-1}\quad\text{ and }\quad\textbf{s}(\gamma)=\gamma^{-1}\gamma\ ,

which we call the
range
and
source
maps respectively. Composition between two elements
\gamma
and
\eta
in
\mathcal{G}
is defined if, and only if,
\textbf{s}(\gamma)=\textbf{r}(\eta)
.

The motivating example for this paper is a bundle of groups. That is, let
X
be a nonempty set and, for each
x\in X
, let
\Gamma_{x}
be a group. Then:

\mathcal{G}=\bigsqcup_{x\in X}\Gamma_{x}

is a groupoid: a pair of elements is composable if, and only if, there exists an
x\in X
such that both elements are in
\Gamma_{x}
; then composition is defined as composition in the group
\Gamma_{x}
.

A
topological groupoid
is a groupoid equipped with a topology such that composition and inversion define continuous maps.

Steinberg algebras are constructed from certain topological groupoids that generalise the class of discrete groups. First, we say an open set
B
in a topological groupoid
\mathcal{G}
is an
open bisection
if the source and range maps restrict to homeomorphisms from
B
onto an open subset of
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
.
Then, a topological groupoid is
ample
if its topology is generated by a basis of compact open bisections. We always require the unit space of our groupoids to be Hausdorff, although the main point of our investigation here is that the groupoid itself need not be Hausdorff.

Now, let
\mathcal{G}
be an ample groupoid with Hausdorff unit space. The
Steinberg algebra
of
\mathcal{G}
over a field
K
is the
K
-algebra
\mathcal{A}_{K}(\mathcal{G})
of functions
f:\mathcal{G}\rightarrow K
such that:

f=\sum\limits_{B\in F}a_{B}1_{B}\ ,

where
F
is a finite collection of compact open bisections
B
of
\mathcal{G}
and, for all
B\in F
,
a_{B}\in K
and
1_{B}
denotes the characteristic function of
B
.

Addition and scalar multiplication in
\mathcal{A}_{K}(\mathcal{G})
are defined pointwise, and multiplication in the algebra is given by the following convolution formula:

\forall\ f,g\in\mathcal{A}_{K}(G),\ \ \ \ \ (f*g)(\gamma):=\sum\limits_{\mathbf{r}(\eta)=\mathbf{r}(\gamma)}f(\eta)g(\eta^{-1}\gamma)\ .

Note that, for compact open sets
U
and
V
, convolution of their characteristic functions reduces to
1_{U}*1_{V}=1_{UV}.

Further, if we assume
U,V\subseteq\mathcal{G}^{0}
, then the range and source maps are the identity maps; hence,
U
and
V
are necessarily open bisections. In this case, convolution of their characteristic functions becomes:

1_{U}*1_{V}=1_{U\cap V}\ .

Ideals of Singular Functions

When
\mathcal{G}

For a function

\supp(f)\coloneqq\{\gamma\in\mathcal{G}:f(\gamma)\neq 0\}.

This is sometimes referred to as the ‘open support’ when the function

\supp(f)
is not necessarily open. We say a function

\mathcal{S}_{K}(\mathcal{G}):=\{f\in\mathcal{A}_{K}(\mathcal{G}):\supp(f)\text{ has empty interior}\}.

We know from
[
6
, Proposition 3.7]
that
\mathcal{S}_{K}(\mathcal{G})
is an ideal

in
\mathcal{A}_{K}(\mathcal{G})
.
A

\mathcal{A}_{K}(\mathcal{G})
is a proper subset of
\mathcal{S}_{K}(\mathcal{G})

.

Remark 2.1
.

If
I
is an ideal of a ring

and if

is a subideal of

, then it is not true in general that

is an ideal of

\mathcal{A}_{K}(\mathcal{G})

\mathcal{G}_{2S}^{(2)}=\Delta X\cup\{(0,1),(1,0),(1,1)\}\ ,

As above, we call all of the elements in

With this structure,

is an ample groupoid with Hausdorff unit space. The entire groupoid is not Hausdorff because the elements 0 and 1 cannot be separated by open sets. Each element of the basis
\mathcal{B}_{2S}

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
K
be a field and let
f\in\mathcal{A}_{K}(\mathcal{G}_{2S})
. Then
f
may be written in the form:

f=a_{0}1_{X_{0}}+a_{1}1_{X_{1}}+\sum\limits_{B\in F_{w}}a_{B}1_{B}\ ,

where
F_{w}
is a finite collection of compact open bisections of
\mathcal{G}_{2S}
that contain neither head of the snake, and
a_{0},a_{1},a_{B}\in K
(possibly zero).

Proof.

Begin by writing
f
in the standard form:
f=\sum\limits_{B\in F}a_{B}1_{B}
, where
F
is a finite collection of compact open bisections of
\mathcal{G}_{2S}
. Without loss of generality, we can assume each
B\in F
is a cylinder set (by decomposing
B
into a disjoint union of basic open sets and writing
a_{B}1_{B}
as the corresponding sum).

Now, split
F
into three disjoint parts: the collection of those

that contain the head
0
(denoted

); those that contain the head
1
(denoted
F_{1}
); and those that contain neither head (denoted

). Then:

f=\sum\limits_{B\in F_{0}}a_{B}1_{B}+\sum\limits_{B\in F_{1}}a_{B}1_{B}+\sum\limits_{B\in F_{w}}a_{B}1_{B}\ .

We first consider
F_{0}
. Let
a_{0}:=f(0)\in K
(possibly zero) and for each

, write:

a_{B}1_{B}=a_{B}1_{X_{0}}-a_{B}1_{X_{0}\backslash B}\ ,

where

that are not in

\sum\limits_{B\in F_{0}}a_{B}1_{B}=\left(\sum\limits_{B\in F_{0}}a_{B}\right)1_{X_{0}}+\left(\sum\limits_{B\in F_{0}}-a_{B}1_{X_{0}\backslash B}\right).

containing the head

, so its coefficient

must be equal to

.

We can repeat the same process for the collection

∎

4.3.

In this subsection, we provide a full description of the singular ideals of

Recall that a singular ideal of
