Paediatric Keratoconus: An ERN-EYE Clinical Consensus Statement on Diagnosis, Treatment and Follow-Up Care
Authors: Luca Buzzonetti, Cosimo Mazzotta, Michele Iester, Mikk Pauklin, Daniel Boehringer, Reda Zemaitiene, Martino Vannucchi, Lidia Cocho, Pavel Studeny, Amélie Gavard, Cristina Monterosso, Dominique Bremond-Gignac, Manca Tekavcic Pompe, Jean-Louis Bourges, Petra Liskova

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis clinical consensus statement, developed through the European Reference Network for Rare Eye Diseases (ERN-EYE), provides recommendations for the diagnosis, monitoring and management of paediatric keratoconus. Paediatric keratoconus is a corneal ectasia that typically presents during childhood or adolescence, often advancing rapidly and significantly affecting visual development. Despite its impact, standardised paediatric-specific guidelines are lacking. A multidisciplinary panel of experts from across Europe contributed to this statement through a structured Delphi process. The consensus reviews the current understanding of the physiopathology, diagnostic criteria, risk factors and classification systems of paediatric keratoconus. It outlines strategies for early detection and monitoring, emphasises the role of patient and family education and presents current treatment approaches, with specific recommendations for paediatric care. The aim of this document is to guide ophthalmologists, paediatricians and associated professionals in delivering timely and appropriate care, improving outcomes for children with keratoconus across Europe.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40123-026-01463-5.Keywords: Cross-linking, Guidelines, Keratoconus, Keratoplasty, Paediatric keratoconus, Rare diseases

Key Summary Points

Introduction

Keratoconus (KC) is a progressive, multifactorial corneal degenerative disease characterised by changes in the collagen structure and organisation of the cornea. The main clinical findings in KC are ectasia, abnormal corneal thickness distribution, irregular astigmatism with high-order aberrations (HOAs) and corneal thinning. KC classically manifests in the second decade of life, and it may progress until the third to fourth decade of life, when it usually stabilises [1]. Corneal tomography [Scheimpflug imaging and anterior segment optical coherence tomography (OCT)] has recently increased the ability of ophthalmologists to identify corneal ectasia at a much earlier stage than is usually possible using only slit-lamp examination and keratometry measurements. While KC is still classified as a rare disease, the previously established KC prevalence of approximately 1/2,000 among the general population has been challenged, with much higher rates found in many parts of the world [2].

KC is more progressive in children than in adult patients and usually more severe at the time of diagnosis. However, the diagnosis of paediatric KC (pKC) is often delayed because of few functional complaints, especially before the age of 8 years. pKC represents one of the main indications of paediatric corneal transplantation, causing about 15–20% of all corneal transplants in children. A prompt management is therefore crucial to halt the progression of the disease [3].

Although technological advances have significantly improved chances for early diagnosis in paediatric patients affected by KC, many controversial aspects about their management remain. This clinical consensus statement (CCS) was developed by a European group of expert physicians, with the aim to facilitate equitable care by standardising the assessment of all patients with pKC, and is supported by the European Reference Network for Rare Eye Diseases (ERN-EYE), a network of European hospitals specialised in the care of rare eye diseases. Its objective is to improve and harmonise the management of paediatric patients with suspected or confirmed KC by giving specific recommendations intended to support ophthalmologists, paediatricians, as well as patients and their families.

Materials and Methods

The CCS executive group (CCSEG) was composed of 14 expert representatives from a range of professional backgrounds, including paediatric ophthalmologists and ophthalmological surgeons. They were selected on predefined criteria, including at least 10 years of experience in pKC clinical care or research. Professional details on these experts can be found in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM). Experts were based in the following European Union (EU) countries: Czech Republic, Estonia, France, Germany, Italy, Lithuania, Slovenia and Spain. The team was divided into three working groups (WGs), each of which focused on a specific topic related to pKC: genetics and pathogenesis; clinical features and diagnosis; and management and treatments.

Conflicts of interest (COI) were collected from all participants prior to the start of the process. No relevant conflicts were declared that were considered likely to affect the objectivity of the discussions, consensus process or voting outcomes. One author reported industry relationships; however, these were not considered to be directly relevant to the topics addressed in this statement and therefore did not warrant exclusion from the panel. In accordance with ERN-EYE procedures, if a conflict had arisen, the concerned expert could have either been excluded from the panel, prevented from participating to specific discussions or would have abstained from voting on the relevant items.

Physiopathology

Keratoconus is a degenerative structural disease of the cornea. It is defined typically as a bilateral progressive ectatic corneal disorder and often displays asymmetry between eyes within the same patient. Its multifactorial aetiology involves an interplay between genetic and environmental factors [5]. Progression leads to corneal shape deformation, which in turn leads to HOAs and irregular astigmatism associated with corneal thinning. This causes a deterioration of uncorrected distance visual acuity (UDVA) and best spectacle corrected distance visual acuity (DCVA) that is not easily correctable by standard means.

Despite the progress made in understanding the genetics of KC, the exact pathogenesis of the disease has yet to be elucidated. Nevertheless, several pathways have been linked to KC, such as primary dysregulation of corneal collagen matrix integrity and cell differentiation [6]. KC can be categorised as syndromic and non-syndromic, but the lack of understanding of its physiopathology makes it difficult determining whether ectasia associated with monogenic disease is true KC or if it is a separate entity with a similar phenotype. Notably, it is usual to exclude patients with monogenic diseases from KC studies in case these are later proven to be different diseases.

While KC onset is commonly associated with puberty, progressing through adolescence and stabilising around the fourth decade of life [7], instances of KC have been documented in children younger than 12 years [8, 9 ]. pKC tends to be more severe at initial diagnosis and progresses more aggressively than KC in adults, with an 88% occurrence of progression in the paediatric population [1, 10–13]. A single case aged 4 years was reported in a patient affected by Down syndrome [14].

Epidemiology

A 2020 systematic review and meta-analysis by Hashemi et al. estimated the global prevalence of KC to be approximately 138/100,000 [95% confidence interval (CI) 114–162] [15]. More recently, a 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis by Sriranganathan et al. reported a higher global prevalence estimate of 289/100,000 (95% CI 124–671; 25 studies), with marked geographic variation [16]. The lowest regional prevalence was observed in Europe (37/100,000; 95% CI 14–96; 6 studies).

The EU defines a rare disease as one that affects no more than five people per 10,000 inhabitants. The global prevalence estimates reported by Hashemi et al. [15] and Sriranganathan et al. [16] (13.8 and 28.9/10,000, respectively) exceed this threshold. In contrast, the pooled prevalence estimate for Europe (3.7/10,000) falls below the threshold; however, its 95% CI (1.4–9.6/10,000) includes values above five people per 10,000. Consequently, it remains uncertain whether KC still fulfils the epidemiological criteria for classification as a rare disease within the EU. Nevertheless, KC continues to be legally recognised as a rare disease in several EU countries, including Italy, to ensure access to essential healthcare benefits and reimbursement schemes. It is also currently classified as a rare disease by ERN-EYE.

It is important to note that there is no evidence showing that pKC is a disease separate from adult KC. Prevalence of pKC is expected to be variable, dependant on populations and definition of paediatrics, as the upper limit of paediatric age can vary according to countries. For example, some countries define paediatric care for patients under 18 years of age, while other countries choose to extend the paediatric definition to patients under 21 years of age [17].

The Intelligence Research of Sight Registry (IRIS) of the American Academy of Ophthalmology reports a pKC prevalence in children aged 0–17 years as 0.16% [18]. Similarly, a retrospective study in Lebanon reported a prevalence of 0.53% in children under 15 years of age, suggesting a frequency higher than 5/10,000 general population [19]. In an Australian multigenerational study where 20-year-olds underwent Scheimpflug imaging, 1.2% of individuals were diagnosed with KC [20]. One of the highest paediatric prevalence to date (4.79%) was reported in a Saudi Arabia study, in individuals aged 6–21 years [21]. In Sriranganathan et al., the highest pooled prevalence was seen individuals aged 20–29 years (526/100,000; 95% CI 93–2 823; 6 studies), followed by those aged 10–19 years (346/100,000; 95% CI 68–1 740; 6 studies) [16]. The lowest pooled prevalence was found in children aged 0–9 years (8.6/100,000; 95% CI 7.9–9.5; 2 studies).

The prevalence of pKC is thought to be higher in populations where consanguinity is common and in areas where the prevalence of adult KC is higher, supported by the finding that 7.5% of paediatric cases have a familial history of the disease [22, 23].

Risk Factors for Keratoconus Development

The pathogenesis of pKC remains poorly understood. Current evidence suggests that it results from a complex interaction between genetic susceptibility and environmental or behavioural factors. The principal risk factors for the development of KC identified in the literature include family history of KC, parental consanguinity, the presence of syndromic disease, hormonal imbalance, nutrition deficiencies, atopy, allergy, eye rubbing and ultraviolet (UV) exposure [19, 24]. Genetic Factors

The primary risk factor for the development of pKC is a positive family history of KC. Hashemi et al., in a recent systematic review and meta-analysis including 7,158,241 participants from 15 countries, give an odds ratio of 6.42 for those with a family history of KC, showing a significant influence of genetics [15]. While it is generally reported that only 10% of patients may have family members also affected, genetics are an important risk factor for KC development and progression [15]. If the family history shows that several members have been affected, then a regular clinical screening may be indicated for children aged > 12 years. Screening in children aged < 12 years old is generally not indicated, as it may result in false positives.

There are no significant data to provide statistical probability that a dizygotic twin of a patient with pKC will also develop the disease, as dizygotic twins are no more genetically related than a pair of siblings and present a similar risk as any other family sibling. However, the individual is expected to be at a greater risk of developing disease compared to the general population due to the likely shared genetic and environmental risk factors, which warrants an increased frequency of surveillance. The risk for a monozygotic twin of a patient with pKC to manifest the disease is higher than that of a dizygotic or sibling pair, although the severity of disease may differ between the two twins. This is supported by a higher concordance of severity in monozygotic twins than dizygotic twins [29].

The largest genome-wide association study (GWAS) performed to date explained only 12.5% of KC heritability in a population of European ancestry [6]. Larger GWAS and analyses of variants in familial cases may help to enhance effectiveness of genetic testing for predicting susceptibility to KC, although multi-ethnic cohorts should be also included in such studies to be relevant for individuals across different populations .

The most common genetic condition associated with KC is Down syndrome [30]. Approximately 50 monogenic conditions have been reported as a comorbidity of KC, the most frequently reported being Ehlers–Danlos syndrome, brittle cornea syndrome and inherited retinal diseases [12, 31–35].

Finally, the associations between gender and KC development present considerable variability, with prevalence rates ranging from two- to fivefold higher in males to a higher prevalence in females. Hashemi et al. [15] found KC prevalence rates to be 1% higher in men than in women, indicating a non-significant difference. However, none of these factors can currently be used to develop a polygenic risk score or any other type of predictive model.

Clinical and Environmental Risk Factors

Eye-Rubbing Behaviours

Eye rubbing is a well-known and significant contributor to KC. Regardless of the underlying cause of the rubbing, be it allergies, dry eye, asthenopia, or psychological factors, engaging in this behaviour increases the likelihood of developing KC. According to Hashemi et al. [15], patients who rub their eyes are threefold more likely to develop KC. The exact mechanism by which eye rubbing initiates and/or induces KC development and progression is unknown, although one hypothesis is that the stress from vigorous and prolonged eye rubbing causes mechanical fatigue of the cornea [36]. Beyond mechanical stress, chronic habit of abnormal rubbing may be linked to higher release of cytokines and inflammatory mediators such as metalloproteinases, inducing progression [28, 37]. Gritz and McDonnell [38] reported that microtrauma of susceptible subjects may damage the epithelium, leading to cytokine release, myofibroblast differentiation, a change in biomechanical forces and thinning of corneal tissue.

Atopy

A large proportion of patients with KC (65–78%) have a history of allergic conjunctivitis [39, 40]. Among the most common allergic conjunctivitis in children, rare forms present more severe prognosis, which include vernal keratoconjunctivitis (VKC) and atopic keratoconjunctivitis. VKC is a chronic, bilateral and seasonal inflammatory ocular disease that mainly affects boys between 3 and 15 years of age. While it usually resolves spontaneously over time, in some cases VKC can become recurrent, leading to long-term visual complications, such as KC and limbal stem cell deficiency. The prevalence of KC among patients with VKC has been reported to be as high as 26.8%, while abnormal corneal topographic findings may be present in up to 71% of VKC cases [41]. Due to this increased risk, children with VKC should undergo regular ophthalmological follow-up to allow early detection of KC. Early identification and management of both VKC and KC are important to limit disease progression and to preserve visual function and quality of life [41, 42].

Although some large studies have shown a significant association (ranging from 11% to 30%) between atopy and KC [36, 43], other studies have failed to document a clear correlation in atopy frequency between cases and controls. This inconsistency might be linked to variations in the definition of atopy, which can include conditions such as eczema, rhinitis, asthma and skin rash. Hashemi et al. report odd ratios of 1.42 for allergy, 1.94 for asthma and 2.95 for eczema [15]. Furthermore, it is clear that patients with an atopic disease (i.e. allergic conjunctivitis) can be more prone to initiate eye-rubbing behaviour, a known significant risk factor for development and progression of KC [36]. Therefore, atopy could be considered to be an indirect contributor to KC [15], and early treatment of allergic eye conditions and refraining from eye rubbing could prevent new cases of the disease [5, 13, 28].

Other Factors

There is no evidence that the factors that increase the risk of myopia, such as prolonged indoor or near-sighted distance activities, are linked to the risk of developing KC. Sunlight overexposure and alkaline pH may be linked with higher geographic prevalence of KC [21, 44], while the geographic composition of ocular microbiome may also play an important role [45, 46].

Keratoconus Progression

Paediatric KC progression is influenced by eye rubbing, early-onset KC and familial history where genetic factors prevail [47]. Although eye rubbing represents a significant risk factor, the variance within the literature suggests an incomplete understanding of the impact of eye rubbing on aspects such as disease progression. In a recent retrospective study, Mazharian et al. [48] observed less variability in all progression criteria among patients who discontinued risky behaviours (eye rubbing and sleeping with any kind of pressure on the eye), compared to natural KC progression as defined by Ferdi et al. [11] in a large meta-analysis and by the control group from the 2014 Wittig-Silva et al. study [49].

In addition to eye rubbing, a younger age of onset of KC tends to be associated with rapid progression and more advanced stage of disease at diagnosis [50]. Léoni-Mesplié et al. [51] reported that, out of a cohort of 216 patients, 27.8% of patients aged < 15 years were diagnosed at stage 4 KC (according to the Amsler–Krumeich classification), compared to only 7.8% of patients aged > 27 years. Chatzis and Hafezi [10] observed KC progression in 88% of children within 1 year of diagnosis, advocating for early cross-linking (CXL) treatment in these paediatric eyes.

Diagnosis

Screening

Keratoconus should be suspected when a young person complains of progressive near-sightedness and/or distortion or fast refraction changes. Early signs often involve refractive errors, particularly a progressive shift in the astigmatism axis. In the paediatric population, KC is usually in a moderate to advanced stage when first diagnosed. Hence, corneal tomography should be systematically performed in children with recent-onset corneal astigmatism [52] and, according to the edition 2 of Global Consensus on Keratoconus and ectatic diseases, in children aged between 6 and 18 years who present with astigmatism greater than 2 Dioptres (D) in subjective refraction [2].

On the other hand, an against-the-rule myopic astigmatism that quickly progresses over months deserves further investigation with corneal tomography, as it may be an early sign of KC.

In conclusion, data currently available are not sufficient to conclude with certainty, but a progressive, against-the-rule myopic astigmatism, especially in the case of anisometropia, could be an alert factor for the development of KC and would warrant further investigation, at least with corneal tomography. However, it is important to remember that in case of high ametropia or irregular astigmatism, mainly in non-cooperative patients, early KC could be detected by retinoscopy, which has been shown to be a very sensitive and reliable test [53].

A pKC diagnosis should be suspected when a child’s astigmatism becomes progressive, particularly if it is against-the-rule astigmatism or oblique incidence astigmatism and also changes its axis. A differential diagnosis can be performed using pinhole when best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) does not reach 1.0, as BCVA will improve in the case of pKC. Early in the course of the disease, BCVA may be normal even in symptomatic patients. KC should also be suspected when a young person has high, irregular myopic astigmatism with a scissoring reflex on retinoscopy. Retinoscopy findings, such as the scissoring reflex and the oil droplet sign, are sensitive and reliable tests for pKC detection, even at early stages. Retinoscopy is also the only test able to detect pKC in non-cooperative patients [53]. Should KC screening programmes be implemented, retinoscopy could be a safe and quick method to identify KC suspicion in presence of early refractive error.

In advanced KC, the corneal protrusion may cause angulation of the lower lid on downgaze, referred to as the Munson sign. This is a nonspecific sign and usually the diagnosis of the disease is made long before the Munson sign is evident.

Specific contexts should also alert the clinician to a possible pKC diagnosis. In an allergic context and particularly in atopic keratoconjunctivitis, it is essential to assess patients early as severe ocular allergies, if persistent, can contribute to the progression of KC in children. Examinations may be challenging before the age of 10 years. For cases with a family history of KC, early examinations are crucial and are recommended around 8–13 years of age. OCT and videotopography of the anterior and posterior cornea using a Scheimpflug photography camera are advised. The onset of astigmatism should initiate follow-ups, even if the astigmatism appears regular, with yearly check-ups thereafter.

Clinical Diagnosis

The diagnosis of KC depends first on the clinical suspicion of the condition and then on careful evaluation, employing various available diagnostic and imaging tools. Corneal imaging devices include tomographic platforms (Scheimpflug photography, OCT) and functional biomechanical devices (imaging an air impulse on the cornea) [54].

Slit lamp examination remains a major diagnostic tool, particularly for late diagnosis. An eccentrically located ectatic protrusion of the cornea is noted. While the apex is usually inferior to an imaginary horizontal line drawn through the pupillary axis, it can be located in the central cornea.

Corneal tomography and epithelial thickness mapping are currently the most effective diagnostic tools for detecting KC across its various evolutionary stages [2]. Critically, while traditional corneal topography relies on Placido disk-based systems to analyse anterior curvature, modern corneal tomography utilises Scheimpflug or OCT imaging to generate comprehensive elevation and pachymetric maps of both the anterior and posterior corneal surfaces [55]. In patients with KC, posterior surface elevation changes typically precede anterior curvature alterations. When utilising a standard best-fit sphere (BFS) reference, a maximal posterior elevation displacement exceeding + 15 μm to + 20 μm is generally considered to be suspicious or indicative of early ectasia, whereas values in the posterior float are highly sensitive but require careful interpretation alongside pachymetric progression indices. In addition, the corneal pachymetry map provides a detailed spatial overview of corneal thickness, characteristically demonstrating localised thinning at the apex of the protrusion [55].

As a complement to morphological data, functional biomechanical devices assess the corneal response to deformation induced by a calibrated air pulse. The Ocular Response Analyzer (ORA; Reichert Technologies, Depew, NY, USA) measures corneal hysteresis based on central corneal reflex changes, whereas the Corvis ST system (OCULUS Optikgeräte GmbH, Wetzlar, Germany) integrates ultra-high-speed Scheimpflug technology to dynamically monitor the entire horizontal corneal profile during deformation and recovery, providing robust metrics for early KC detection [55].

Clinical diagnosis of moderate to advanced KC is relatively easy on slit-lamp examination. However, diagnosing subclinical and early KC, especially in eyes with good visual acuity (VA), becomes challenging [52]. KC assessment, whether for diagnosis or progression evaluation, requires several imaging tools, including a keratometer, corneal topographer, corneal tomographer, ultrasound pachymeter, epithelium thickness map [56] and biomechanics.

Differences between subclinical and clinical KC can be subtle and require tomographic imaging for accurate detection. Both forms will present posterior corneal ectasia and/or pachymetric progression abnormalities, which are generally considered to be the first indicators of ectatic disease. However, changes on the anterior corneal surface, which are more commonly associated with visual symptoms and patient’s complaints, will only be present in clinical KC. Examination of the posterior corneal surface can often reveal pathology that may not be detected if relying on anterior analysis alone [57].

Some dedicated software, as the Belin ABCD staging and classification system and the ABCD Progression Display, could represent a grading and monitoring system for KC assessment.

Signs and Symptoms

The clinical presentation of KC varies significantly depending on the stage of disease progression. In the early stages, patient-reported symptoms mimic common refractive errors, then an increased irregular astigmatism generally occurs that cannot be fully corrected with spectacles. Patients frequently report monocular polyopia (seeing multiple ghost images), micro-diplopia, significant glare, halos around light sources and marked photophobia. As the ectasia advances, severe distortion of the visual field and a rapid change in the refractive prescription become characteristic.

On clinical examination via slit-lamp biomicroscopy, several classic pathognomonic signs emerge as the disease progresses. In moderate cases, an iron line known as Fleischer’s ring can often be observed in the basal epithelium surrounding the base of the cone. Vogt’s striae (deep stromal, vertical stress lines) appear parallel to the meridian of the cone due to mechanical stretching, temporarily disappearing upon digital pressure on the globe. Fine subepithelial fibrillar networks or anterior stromal scars may also develop at the apex [7]. In advanced stages, mechanical stretching leads to Rizzuti’s sign (a sharp conical reflection on the nasal cornea when light is shone from the temporal side) and Munson’s sign (a V-shaped protrusion of the lower eyelid during downgaze) [7]. Acute corneal hydrops, characterised by a sudden rupture of Descemet’s membrane, represents a severe complication resulting in acute stromal edema, pain, and a sudden drop in VA [58].

Current Placido disc-based corneal topographers are devices that use photographs of Placido disc reflections off the anterior corneal surface and provide quantitative information by generating curvature maps using computational technology. A small curvature radius with a correspondent high dioptric value has to be considered to be a suspicious diagnostic sign for pKC. Placido disc-based corneal topography can provide indices to quantify corneal surface irregularities, with many showing good sensitivity for KC detection. However, since these systems are based on curvature analysis, they do not reflect the true corneal elevation profile and can lead to misinterpretations [59].

Subclinical KC is associated with characteristic changes in epithelial and stromal thickness. Compared with normal corneas, subclinical keratoconic eyes generally demonstrate focal inferotemporal epithelial thinning associated with relative thickening of the surrounding epithelium, whereas normal epithelial thickness maps typically show maximal thickness centrally with gradual superior thinning. Similar regional thinning patterns may also be observed on pachymetric and stromal thickness maps, although epithelial changes tend to appear more pronounced. Pattern deviation analyses can further enhance the visibility of these localised abnormalities [56].

Corneal thinning is another early feature of ectatic disease. In KC, the thinnest corneal point is usually located near the cone apex in a paracentral position, so progressive thinning may result in greater steepening of the posterior corneal surface compared with the anterior surface. Because epithelial remodelling can partially compensate for anterior surface irregularities through local epithelial thinning over the cone and compensatory thickening around it, posterior corneal elevation abnormalities may sometimes represent one of the earliest detectable signs of KC. Curvature or power maps show an asymmetric bow tie corresponding to irregular astigmatism and sometimes a skewed steepest axis. In advanced cases, one of the bows of the bow tie may be absent [60, 61].

In advanced KC, the topography map shows increased steepening of both anterior and posterior corneal surfaces, with the highest point being at the apex of the cone. The cone is commonly positioned either inferiorly or paracentral in either the nasal or temporal inferior part of the cornea. Morphologically, the cone is classified based on size and shape into nipple, oval, and globus. Most cones have an oval shape with a diameter of 5–6 mm, usually involving one or two quadrants, and are commonly displaced inferotemporally. Nipple-shaped and globus cones are much less common. Nipple-shaped cones are smaller (≤ 5 mm in diameter) and are usually round, central or paracentral and commonly located inferonasally. The globus cone is extensive (> 6 mm) and involves up to three-quarters of the corneal surface [62].

Paediatric Keratoconus Classification

The time course of the development of KC signs and symptoms and their relationship to disease severity vary widely, making classification of KC severity challenging. Biomechanical changes in the cornea lead to changes in morphology and consecutively to visual deterioration with an increase of irregular astigmatism, HOAs and a decrease in corneal pachymetry.

KC is usually classified into two main categories, primarily based on corneal morphology (including cone pattern, position and shape) and topographic/tomographic characteristics (involving maps of the anterior and posterior corneal surfaces, as well as corneal and cone thickness) [63]. The severity of the disease is commonly assessed by considering HOAs, VA and astigmatism. Signs such as scars, Vogt’s striae and Fleischer’s ring are also taken into account [52, 63].

Using these criteria, the Amsler–Krumeich classification for KC diagnosis is widely used, but was recognised by the 2015 Global Consensus on Keratoconus and Ectatic Diseases as inadequate and outdated [64]. Indeed, tomographic analysis revealed the limitations of the Amsler–Krumeich classification, which is based on central corneal thickness (often deviating by nearly 100 µm from the actual thinnest point of the cone) [65] and does not consider the posterior cornea or early or subclinical stages of the disease [66]. The 2015 Global Consensus also recognised the limitations of the commonly used 1-D change in maximum keratometry (Kmax) and suggested a more comprehensive approach [64]. Consequently, several authors have recently attempted to establish a comprehensive system for classifying the disease, using multiple imaging techniques:

The Belin ABCD staging and classification is an anatomical-based classification system, introduced partly in response to the shortcomings outlined in the Global Consensus on Keratoconus and Ectatic Diseases [64], and is now the basis for the new severity system recommended by the second edition of the Global Consensus on Keratoconus and Ectatic Diseases [2]. The ABCD classification uses four parameters: “A” represents the anterior radius of curvature taken from a 3.0-mm optical zone centred on the thinnest point; “B” (for back) is the posterior radius of curvature from a 3.0-mm zone centred on the thinnest point; “C” is minimal corneal thickness; and “D” is best spectacle distance visual acuity. The first three parameters are machine generated (objective), whereas the “D” value (vision) is subjective and operator entered. Each parameter is independently staged from 0 to 4. The four ABCD parameters are displayed three ways: graphically, with the actual radius of curvature and pachymetry values and in a five-step staging, ranging from 0 to 5. Of the newer proposed classification/staging systems, the ABCD seems to have the widest acceptance, yet keratometry-based systems remain the most commonly used in articles and presentations, despite their significant limitations and lack of clinical utility [57]. Some authors have reported that for patients with pKC, the ABCD system showed superior clinical performance compared with previous historical systems to measure progression [72].

To facilitate monitoring, the Belin ABCD Progression Display was introduced to graphically display ABCD parameters and provide confidence intervals for changes. The display also includes gates for young patients with early disease and post-CXL data [57, 66]. Recent studies have confirmed the clinical benefits of the ABCD Progression Display, which helps clinicians to monitor and diagnose progressive disease earlier than the previous 1-D change in Kmax systems [73]. Furthermore, compared to historical systems, the ABCD system has demonstrated superior clinical performance in measuring progression in patients with pKC [72].

Genetic Testing

Genetic research in KC aims to develop a panel of genetic factors that could be used to determine the polygenic risk score for an individual with a significant level of confidence. Genetic testing for KC would be also helpful for individuals wanting laser refractive surgery. Currently, however, genetic testing for KC is not sufficiently accurate to predict the likelihood of disease development and is not ready to be implemented in a diagnostic setting. It is therefore not worth performing genetic testing unless an associated monogenic disease is suspected.

In pKC, lower thinnest corneal thickness (TCT), higher average central corneal keratometry (Km) and higher maximum central posterior elevation (MCPE) on corneal tomography seem to be risk factors for faster rates of progressive corneal thinning. Eyes with TCT < 450 μm, Km > 50 D and MCPE > 50 μm at presentation have been reported to demonstrate the highest rates of progressive corneal thinning in one study [78]. This was confirmed by a recent study in which eyes that progressed had more advanced disease at presentation with higher anterior curvature (Kmax 55.4 ± 6.3 vs. 52.2 ± 5.4 D; p < 0.01), posterior elevation (108.2 ± 40.9 vs. 86.3 ± 35.6 µm; p < 0.01) and lower central pachymetry measurements (442.1 ± 56.7 vs. 454.4 ± 47.5 µm; p = 0.01) [79]. A meta-analysis including 11,529 eyes from paediatric and adult studies showed that patients younger than 17 years and those with Kmax steeper than 55 D at presentation have a significantly greater risk to progress by at least 1.5 D Kmax in 12 months. In addition, Middle Eastern patients were reported to experience more progression [11]. Anterior chamber volume and the KC Match Index seem to play a major role in the progression of pKC, but not in KC in adults [80].

Therapeutic Education

It is of primary importance to explain to patients and caregivers that KC is a progressive disorder. While the rate of progression and its impact on vision may vary from person to person, it should be emphasised that regular eye exams are crucial to monitor its progression and tailor treatments accordingly. It should also be clear to the patient and their family that the condition will require a regular monitoring up to the age of 35–40 years, as well as regular ophthalmic follow-up throughout life.

Understanding the condition and the impact of aggravating factors, such as eye rubbing and family history is important, including early detection of KC in family members. Patients and caregivers should be educated about the importance of avoiding eye rubbing and ensuring prompt treatment of allergic eye disease, as these factors are associated with disease progression. Timely treatment of allergies can reduce progression risks.

KC management consists of three major components: (1) prevention, (2) progression management and (3) visual rehabilitation. Physicians should address these components simultaneously as much as possible. However, significant KC progression or high risk to lose BCVA may prioritise KC progression over other components of management [90]. Of note, as CXL improves refractive, optical aberrations and keratometric indices as long-term outcomes [91], CXL VA outcomes play a significant role in the overall rehabilitation process.

Finally, future therapeutic options, such as transplantation of bioengineered tissue, cell therapy, lysyl oxidase upregulation and extracellular vesicles to reorganise the corneal matrix, are now being reported [92]. These options are still under evaluation and are not integrated to the routine management of KC.

Visual Acuity

Contact lenses can be used to improve the VA of both children and adults according to KC staging [93, 94], and these are usually the first option if glasses compliance is poor.

Intrastromal rings as well as simultaneous customised excimer laser and CXL represent good options to correct (at least partially) the refractive error, but mainly in adult patients. Moreover, CXL wound healing may reshape corneas and remodel anterior curvatures which, in some patients, also improves VA. However, in more advanced stages of the disease, keratoplasty represents the only option to provide a BCVA recovery [95–98].

Management of Associated Conditions

In the case of KC and allergic conjunctivitis, all authors agree that topical or systemic treatment with antihistamines and artificial tears is fundamental [36]. Controlling ocular allergy and eye rubbing is the first step in the treatment of KC. Early treatment of allergic eye conditions and refraining from eye rubbing could also prevent new cases of the disease [5, 13]. Assessment and correction of vitamin D deficiency may also be considered as an adjunctive therapy in patients with documented vitamin D insufficiency. Emerging evidence suggests that vitamin D supplementation could influence KC progression through the modulation of inflammation, inhibition of collagen degradation and promotion of proteoglycan synthesis [99]. However, while vitamin D supplementation can be considered to be an important supportive strategy, it should not be considered a substitute for corneal cross-linking, which remains the standard of care for progressive KC according to the Second Global Consensus on Keratoconus and Ectatic Diseases [2].

Currently available topical agents are effective in managing acute episodes of allergic ocular inflammation, while corticosteroids can control both acute exacerbations and chronic disease. Unfortunately, their long-term use is restricted by the risk of important adverse effects, making disease recurrence and chronic management particularly challenging. As a result, immunomodulatory agents such as tacrolimus and cyclosporine are frequently used as steroid-sparing alternatives [100–104].

Eye rubbing is a proven factor that can trigger the onset and progression of KC. Apart from allergy, it is a repetitive motor stereotypy. Eye rubbing is frequently observed in children with autism spectrum disorders and in individuals who may be at risk for developing KC. Behavioural interventions are likely to provide positive results in an eye rubbing habit reversal in children with autism spectrum disorders [105].

Lifestyle modifications, particularly avoiding eye rubbing and ensuring optimal control of allergy and ocular surface inflammation, are important components of KC management. However, while these measures may help reduce the risk of progression, they are not considered sufficient to halt progressive disease and should not replace corneal cross-linking, which is clearly recognised as the standard of care for progressive KC by the Second Global Consensus on Keratoconus and Ectatic Diseases [2].

Cross-Linking

Cross-linking is a photodynamic therapy that increases corneal stiffening. It stabilises and delays the progression of both primary and secondary/iatrogenic ectasia through a photo-polymerisation of the collagen and proteoglycans that are constituents of the stroma. It also decreases enzymatic digestion of collagen by enzymes and metalloproteinases. The CXL mechanism of action uses a combination of UV light and riboflavin isotonic solution, with concentrations ranging from 0.1% up to 0.25%, depending on the protocol used. All UVA emitters used for CXL are set at a specific wavelength (370 nm), which is one of the UV absorption peaks of riboflavin. The procedure is generally performed under topical anaesthesia, and is mainly indicated for the early stages of KC (stages 1 and 2, at corneal thickness > 400 µm); CXL starting at stage 3 (corneal thickness 400 µm) is generally sub-optimal.

Since the first publication of a prospective, nonrandomised clinical pilot study evaluating CXL for patients with KC [106], numerous studies have been conducted to evaluate the procedure's effectiveness, although randomised controlled trials (RCTs) are still lacking. To date, only one study has been published in the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews [107]. The authors of this study summarised many studies that evaluated CXL as an effective and safe treatment for preventing progression of KC and found that the relative risk of progression can be reduced by 80–90% over a 12-month period [107]. Factors such as high preoperative K values, female gender, eye rubbing and allergic conjunctivitis have been associated with the risk of progression, but the exact reasons are unknown [108, 109].

Several different protocols have been established for CXL, and these can be classified into two main categories. The first category includes protocols using the Epithelium-off (Epi-off) technique; the second includes protocols using Epithelium-on (Epi-on) techniques.

Epithelium-Off Protocols

The epithelium represents a physical barrier for both riboflavin, which is a hydrosoluble and high-molecular-weight substance, and UVA as the epithelium absorbs around 30% of the treatment energy dose (also referred to as fluence). Epi-off CXL therefore involves removing the corneal epithelium, generally with a blunt metal spatula, before applying the riboflavin solution and UV irradiation.

Standard Epithelium-Off Cross-Linking

Several long-term studies have proven that the standard Epi-off CXL (S-CXL) technique, also known as the Dresden protocol, is a safe and effective treatment in pKC. Using this technique, the rate of KC stabilisation after 10 years was over 95% with a retreatment rate of 4.2%, generally performed in younger and eye-rubbing allergic patients who were under 15 years of age at the time of enrolment in the treatment protocol. In this population, CXL re-treatment was performed after an average of 36 months, which resulted in stopping KC progression and proving that S-CXL can be repeated if necessary, without adverse events. At the 15-year follow-up time point, S-CXL proved to be a safe treatment for progressive KC in paediatric patients, with an anterior curvature progression rate of up to 7.9% beyond the 10-year follow-up [74, 110].

However, while the evidence based on long-term published studies shows the S-CXL protocol delivers good results in terms of KC stabilisation, it also shows a relatively higher complication rate (haze and infectious keratitis). This protocol is also very time consuming, as it includes 20 min of riboflavin soaking, followed by 30 min of UV exposure, bringing the duration of the procedure to at least 1 h per patient, which can create issues with patient compliance [111].

Accelerated Cross-Linking

To help prevent the issues associated with the standard Epi-off protocol, accelerated CXL (A-CXL) protocols have reduced UVA exposure time by increasing the power of UVA irradiation [112].

The A-CXL protocol, using a fluence 9 mW/5.4 J/cm2 for 20 min, allows surgeons to perform the treatment in 20 min instead of 1 h. The procedure is well tolerated, with achievement of long-term stability of ectasia and reduced side effects. Currently, the 9 mW/5.4 J/cm2 A-CXL protocol is the most performed protocol, replacing the Dresden protocol in young adults outside the USA [it is not approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA)]. It is also the preferred procedure for paediatric patients [113], as it allows for better patient compliance with comparable efficacy. Although no formal consensus was reached regarding a single preferred protocol, the Second Global Consensus on Keratoconus and Ectatic Diseases identified this protocol (9 mW/5.4 J/cm2 for 20 min) as the most widely adopted approach in paediatric patients and young adults with a minimum corneal thickness exceeding 400 µm [2].

The “M nomogram” proposed by Mazzotta et al. include a wide range of Epi-off A-CXL, age-independent protocols that have been developed to be used for ecatic corneas of all thicknesses, including thin corneas [98]. It is a pachymetry-based, accelerated and individualised CXL approach, in which the depth of the demarcation line (DL) is customised according to the baseline minimum corneal thickness by maintaining the constant fluence standardised in the Dresden protocol (5.4 J/cm2) and adapting both the UVA power and the exposure time interval by using continuous and pulsed light:

Amongst the M nomogram recommendations for young adult and paediatric patients, the accelerated 9 mW/5.4J/cm2 crosslinking protocol is the most used Epi-off protocol, showing equivalents results in terms of efficacy and safety compared with the 3 mW/5.4J/cm2 original Dresden protocol [2, 113–116].

In conclusion, the 9 mW “M nomogram” is the only evidence-based, long-term follow-up standardised A-CXL treatment recommended for thin corneas (thickness < 400 µm), but also for corneas of all thickness. Adjusting the power and exposure time confers the same dose and strength to the stroma, without decreasing the fluence, meaning that the A-CXL can be individualised for corneas included in a range between 200 and 400 μm. This is particularly useful for combined treatments and the standardised treatment of thin ectatic corneas. Paediatric patients with KC with faster progression could be treated at diagnosis without awaiting for further disease progression [10, 74].

Epithelium-On Protocols

Higher fluence transepithelial CXL (or Epi-on CXL) has recently emerged as a strategy to improve safety and reduce postoperative complications and discomfort in young adult patients through the use of iontophoresis and without iontophoresis riboflavin loading [98, 117, 118].

Techniques

Riboflavin loading is achieved through either iontophoresis (electrically assisted loading through intact epithelium) or the use of chemically-enhanced riboflavin solutions. These solutions can contain benzalkonium chloride (BAK), trometamol (amino-alcohol) or ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA). Addition of surface anaesthetics drops can sometimes be used to partially alter the epithelial permeability to riboflavin. Swiping the epithelial surface with a sponge can also be helpful to assist with loading as this removes the mucine layer of the epithelium before the chemically-enhanced riboflavin solution is applied.

Riboflavin concentrations achieved using iontophoresis have been found to be half of those achieved with the Epi-off method; chemically-enhanced concentrations were one third of those achieved in Epi-off passive diffusion. However, as the optimal concentration of riboflavin is not known, an increased concentration may not correlate with better outcomes, particularly as other parameters, such as fluence, also need to be considered. Nevertheless, Epi-on protocols have achieved clinically significant results, even at lower riboflavin concentrations, as those can apparently be offset by using increased riboflavin concentrations (usually 0.15–0.25% in Epi-on protocols vs 0.1% in Epi-off protocols) and by increasing the fluence and adding pulsed light.

From a clinical and practical perspective, developing a safer and faster transepithelial CXL approach with an efficacy comparable to that of Epi-off CXL protocol, minimal adverse events and rapid visual recovery remains difficult. Current data suggest that adults aged > 26 years can be treated in both eyes, possibly on the same day, to reduce the burden of time and organisation for both the patient and surgeon. However, young adults aged > 21 should only be treated in the eye with less advanced keratoectasia. This treatment protocol can also be repeated if the progression of the ectasia restarts during the follow-up, thus retaining the advantages of Epi-on procedures, namely a virtual absence of infection risk and uncontrolled corneal scarring.

Epi-on corneal CXL offers several practical advantages, including reduced postoperative pain and discomfort, faster visual recovery and a lower risk of complications such as infectious keratitis or stromal haze. Preserving the epithelium also limits ocular surface damage, allowing patients to resume normal activities more quickly. In addition, Epi-on procedures can be performed in an outpatient setting without the need for an operating theatre, thereby improving clinical workflow and reducing organisational burden, particularly when simultaneous bilateral treatment is considered.

Use of Epithelium-On Protocols in Paediatric Patients

Regarding paediatric patients, a study including 26 eyes (26 patients) treated by Epi-on CXL showed that five eyes (10 patients) of patients aged < 18 years (19% of the population; 50% of paediatric patients) had a clinically significant deterioration in all parameters, leading to the conclusion that, at the present time, Epi-on CXL should be avoided in the paediatric population if possible because of the high failure rates (nearly 50% in the series) [119]. However, early evidence from other studies may allow surgeons to extend the indications for the photodynamic variable fluence (from 7 to 10 J/cm2) A-CXL protocol to increasingly younger patients, potentially to treat developmental ectasias even in paediatric patients [86, 120].

However, in patients aged < 21 years affected by progressive KC, the gold standard of CXL treatment currently remains an accelerated continuous light 9 mW/5.4 J/cm2 Epi-off CXL. Closer follow-up and a lower threshold for CXL should be adopted in patients younger than 17 years and steeper corneas (Kmax > 55 D) [11, 121]. KC is prevalent in the intellectually disabled population, and as this group is rarely suitable for corneal transplantation, CXL is an effective intervention to prevent progression to advanced KC in this already socially restricted group [122, 123].

Use of Cross-Linking in Paediatric Keratoconus Management

In conclusion, CXL is safe and well tolerated and may prevent KC progression. The procedure stabilises, and in some cases improves visual and corneal parameters in paediatric and young adult patients with KC, and, in line with the recommendations of the Second Global Consensus on Keratoconus and Ectatic Diseases, is recommended in paediatric patients at diagnosis of KC, even in absence of progression [2, 13, 115, 116, 124–140].

When considering KC progression after CXL in children, with an increase in some corneal parameters [Kmax, Kmean or steep keratometry (Ksteep) ≥ 1.0 D], the risk of progression is reported to be between 4% and 35% [124, 131, 141–147]. Age at presentation was found to be the only factor predicting KC progression [148]. The fellow eye needs to be carefully monitored, but only 25% of the patients will require CXL in the fellow eye [149], and treatment strategies should be tailored according to the needs and risk factors of each patient. While Epi-off CXL remains the preferred option for the most affected or rapidly progressive eye, Epi-on A-CXL with high-fluence (10 J) pulsed-light transepithelial may be considered in fellow eyes considered to be at lower risk of progression [120].

According to the Second Global Consensus on Keratoconus and Ectatic Diseases, the transepithelial method accounts for approximately 30–35% of CXL procedures. However, compared to other CXL methods it has been shown to be less effective in halting disease progression in children [2, 128, 131, 150].

Cross-Linking Indications

Due to structural differences between the corneas of paediatric and adult patients, KC is considered to be more aggressive in children than in adults. It remains controversial whether therapeutic intervention in pKC should differ from that in adult KC [13].

A number of relevant RCTs have shown that CXL prevents KC progression to a statistically significant extent, based on the measurement of topographic parameters. In the largest RCT carried out to date (follow-up of 100 eyes for 3 years), the maximal corneal refractive power increased by 1.75 ± 0.38 D in the control group and decreased by − 1.03 ± 0.19 D in the CXL group (p < 0.001) [151]. Epi-off CXL is therefore the treatment that should be discussed as soon as there is evidence of disease progression [1, 110].

CXL treatment and corneal transplantation appear to be as effective in patients with VKC as in patients without VKC. However, postoperative complications are more frequent in patients with VKC and these patients require close monitoring, tight control of local inflammation and prompt awareness of restraining/eliminating eye rubbing [41, 152–154].

Strong evidence suggests that corneal CXL is a safe procedure capable of stopping or slowing down the progression of KC, avoiding visual loss associated with progression and delaying or preventing the need for corneal transplantation. The progressive and chronic nature of the disease validates the option for prompt CXL upon KC diagnosis in a paediatric patient [155]. Nevertheless, it does not seem to be necessary to perform CXL in all children presenting with possible KC. The ABCD progression display along with the Belin-Ambrósio Enhanced Ectasia Total Deviation (BAD) ‘D’ value seem to be effective parameters to detect progression in children with KC [156].

Cross-Linking Contraindications

Cross-linking should not be considered as a treatment option in case of cornea opacity. Epi-off can be safely performed in thin corneas (< 400 µm) with M nomogram or sub400 protocol [98, 157]. Moreover, CXL contraindications have been reported for pregnant or breastfeeding patients and patients with prior herpetic infections, active ocular inflammation, corneal scars, autoimmune disorders and a history of poor epithelial wound healing [86, 158].

Safety and Complications of Cross-Linking

The most common postoperative finding is mild transient corneal haze, which typically resolves within 3–6 months without affecting visual outcomes [159]. Persistent stromal haze with corneal scarring and stromal hyperflattening resulting in reduced VA is uncommon, occurring in fewer than 3% of cases [108]. Sterile corneal infiltrates have also been reported (1.4–7.8%) and are thought to represent an inflammatory response rather than infection [160].

Infectious keratitis is a rare but potentially sight-threatening complication. Although riboflavin–UVA treatment itself has intrinsic antimicrobial properties, most reported infections are associated with postoperative factors, including bandage contact lens wear, poor hygiene, inadequate compliance with postoperative treatment or follow-up and water exposure [161]. The most frequently isolated organisms include Staphylococcus epidermidis, Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Escherichia coli, while Acanthamoeba and herpetic keratitis have been reported only rarely [162]. Optimising ocular surface health before treatment, including management of blepharitis, VKX, ocular allergy and eye rubbing, and appropriate postoperative care, such as moxifloxacin eye drops, may help minimise these complications [163].

In conclusion, Epi-off CXL is considered to be a safe and effective procedure, with treatment failure reported in fewer than 3% of cases [108]. Long-term follow-up studies in paediatric patients have demonstrated sustained efficacy, with retreatment rates of approximately 4.2% after 10 years and < 8% after 14 years [74, 110]. Epi-on CXL protocols may further reduce the risk of postoperative complications while maintaining long-term efficacy, although additional long-term comparative studies are still needed [120].

Recent reports have highlighted the potential role of topical losartan, a transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) pathway inhibitor, in the management of corneal haze and stromal fibrosis following epi-off CXL. By targeting myofibroblast-mediated scarring, topical losartan may improve corneal transparency and support corneal healing. Preliminary evidence, including a paediatric case report following post-CXL infectious keratitis, suggests a favourable safety profile and potential benefit in reducing fibrotic remodelling. However, evidence remains limited, and larger controlled studies are needed to define its efficacy, optimal treatment regimen and long-term outcomes, particularly in paediatric patients [164, 165].

Repeatability of Cross-Linking in Disease Progression

Although CXL has become the preferred treatment for patients with progressive corneal ectasia, there are patients in whom the condition seems to worsen even after undergoing CXL, necessitating re-treatment. The Second Global Consensus on Keratoconus and Ectatic Diseases states that repeat CXL can be performed safely in paediatric patients, and that in eyes initially treated with an Epi-on protocol, a repeat Epi-off CXL procedure may be considered after 6–12 months if progression persists [2].

In general, an increase of > 1 D in the Kmax value in association with possible BCVA deterioration after the first 6 months post-intervention can be considered to be an indication of progression. In such cases, repeating the CXL procedure to stabilise the cornea further is possible, unless contraindications are present [166]. However, before deciding to repeat the treatment, it is important to rule out possible causes of pseudoprogression, as they may be mimicking a proper disease progression [147]. For example, in the first few months after CXL, corneal thickness will decrease due to the reorganisation of collagen fibres during corneal remodelling [167], which may lead to false-positive results regarding KC progression following treatment. Haze formation may also occasionally contribute to a change in VA [168]. However, both conditions usually resolve—although not always—with time. Schuerch et al. studied the long-term efficacy of CXL in children with KC, noting the possible progression of KC in five of 33 eyes during the follow-up period after CXL [147]. These authors subsequently determined that pseudoprogression had occurred in three (60%) of these eyes, attributed to changes in the epithelium due to VKC in two eyes and measurement error in one eye. They also noted that in these patients, the progression was transient, and the deterioration of corneal topography was completely reversible [147]. As a result, any determination of true progression and the decision for re-treatment should be made after the initial 6-month period and be based on a combination of clinical and imaging criteria, using tomographic and topographic evaluations whenever possible. Repeated and consistent measurements ensure accuracy and reliability in assessing progression [166].

Nevertheless, given the rarity of progression after CXL, the published literature on this topic is inconsistent. Many case reports and small case series of repeated CXL procedures have shown good outcome with few exceptions [109, 119, 169–174 ], but to date no RCTs have focused on the safety and efficacy of repeated CXL. Currently available information mainly comes from individual case reports and small case series [166], although these studies show positive results in terms of reduction or stability of Kmax values and improvement or stability of VA.

In the vast majority of publications, repeated CXL procedures were performed according to the standard protocol (Epi-off, 3 mW/cm2, 30 min). The two most recent retrospective studies, each with a small number of eyes (10 and 11, respectively), investigated the safety and efficacy of repeated CXL procedures [125, 174]. In one study, the mean age of patients was 23.4 ± 6.2 (range 14–34) years, and in the other study, the mean age was 20.2 ± 6.0 (range 10.8–26.5) years. Repeated CXL procedures were performed at different time points, ranging from 6 to 80 months after the initial CXL procedure. Both studies used an accelerated repeat Epi-off CXL procedure. The results of both studies were similar, showing disease stabilisation and safety of the repeated procedure [125, 174]. Mazzotta et al. published long-term results (10-year follow-up) on paediatric patients who have been initially treated with the Siena protocol (30 min, 3 mW/cm2, Epi-off CXL). Their study identified four eyes (6.3%) of two children (4.2%) with disease progression 3 years after CXL treatment. These eyes were treated with the same protocol, and Kmax values were stabilised in both patients 12 months after treatment [74]. Accelerated Epi-off for repeat CXL is the most indicated procedure since re-progression or instability after the first treatment generally occurs in paediatric allergic eye-rubbing patients with positive familial history of KC or other inflammatory ocular surface diseases.

While making formal recommendations remains difficult, as large-scale studies evaluating safety and efficacy remain few, preliminary data show that in case of KC progression, repeated standard Epi-off protocol can be considered safe and helps to stabilise the disease. Still, further studies are needed to determine whether patients should be treated, which repeat method should be used (conventional or accelerated) and when repeat treatment should occur.

Cross-Linking-Associated Surgical Options

Different refractive approaches can be associated or combined—simultaneously or sequentially—with the CXL technique [Crosslinking Plus (CXL-Plus)] [175].

The goal is to improve the spectacle-corrected DCVA in patients who are intolerant to rigid gas permeable (RGP) contact lenses by reducing HOAs (vertical coma and trefoil aberrations, spherical aberration) and transforming them into low-order aberrations that can be easily corrected with spectacles or soft contact lenses. Modern ray-tracing guided (RTG) or total corneal wavefront guided (WFG) trans-epithelial photorefractive (t-PRK) or phototherapeutic (t-PTK) keratectomy are the preferred procedure, with the aim to increase optical performance while sparing tissue with very limited stromal ablations [98, 176].

According to the Second Global Consensus on Keratoconus and Ectatic Disease, invasive visual rehabilitation must be limited to selected patients who are intolerant to RGP contact lenses, and may be combined with perioperative CXL. Previous CXL treatment is not a contraindication to CXL-Plus treatment when needed and can be associated with repeat CXL [2]. Trans-epithelial RTG and total corneal WFG PRK are the preferred techniques. As mitomycin C application following CXL significantly increases corneal haze, its use in combined CXL-Plus procedures is not recommended [177].

Keratoplasty

Keratoconus used to be the first reason for keratoplasty in the past [178]. Currently, however, it accounts for only 12% of indications in the EU, ranking in fourth place after the Fuchs corneal endothelial dystrophy, regrafting and pseudophakic bullous keratopathy [179]. KC is still one of the most common indications for paediatric keratoplasty and its main goal is improvement of VA [180–183].

Indications

Corneal surgery is indicated in patients with contact lens intolerance or in patients with no benefits from wearing contact lens, as the complication rate of the surgery is even greater in children. Nevertheless, keratoplasty remains indicated in KC when no other option is of any help to the patient, or when the natural course of KC could prevent the best therapeutic option from being implemented. Visual comfort in both eyes and quality of life are the major factors when keratoplasty is considered as an option, specifically with KC still progressing on the recipient’s corneal ring, which might lead to giant astigmatism a decade after the surgical procedure [184–186].

Keratoplasty can still be considered a legitimate option in advanced KC [187]. Corneal transplantation is overall indicated when opacities are impairing corneal transparency within the central area in the visual axis, when KC ectasia compromises contact or scleral lens tolerance and intracorneal ring segments (ICRS) and when the risk of hydrops seems imminent [188].

Safety and Complications of Keratoplasty

Despite generally good visual outcomes, keratoplasty in children is associated with a higher risk of graft failure than in adults [183, 189–191], possibly partly related to the more active immune response in paediatric patients, with reported graft rejection rates ranging from 12% to 43.4% [192, 193]. Graft survival improves with increasing patient age, and children older than 5 years have significantly better outcomes than younger patients [190, 194, 195]. Additional factors associated with poorer prognosis include limited postoperative compliance, inadequate parental cooperation and ocular trauma [196, 197]. Although both penetrating keratoplasty (PK) and deep anterior lamellar keratoplasty (DALK) provide good visual rehabilitation, DALK appears to offer the greatest advantage by reducing the risk of graft failure and endothelial rejection while achieving transplant survival comparable to that of PK [41, 195, 196, 198–200].

Penetrating Keratoplasty

Initially, PK was the most commonly performed technique to address the corneal ectasia of KC. Although excellent levels of VA can be reached with PK [201], the technique weakens corneal biomechanics and exposes the cornea to allograft rejection, a main cause of graft failure in the long-term outcomes.

Deep Anterior Lamellar Keratoplasty

For these two main reasons (weakening of corneal biomechanics and increased possibility of allograft rejection), the deep anterior lamellar keratoplasty (DALK) procedure is now preferred over PK as a routine standard of care. Endothelial allo-immune rejection is not possible with DALK, and long-term results are improved compared to PK, although the surgical technique in DALK is one of the most intricate in corneal surgery. Any significant intraoperative rupture of the Descemet membrane might compromise endothelial layer persistence and require that the technique be converted into a standard PK. In this regard, DALK could benefit from a better standardisation [202–205]. Several DALK techniques have been described, but the big-bubble technique is the more common procedure, also in paediatric patients [1].

Despite the advantages of DALK, specific situations still require PK [206]. For example, PK could be indicated in KC associated with central (pre-)descemetic opacity, in the presence of compromised endothelial cell function or to treat the exceptional corneal perforation mostly seen in the context of karyotype anomaly. The late occurrence of giant astigmatism after keratoplasty for KC (post-keratoplasty ectasia) may correspond to a slow corneal melting at the site of mechanical trephination, a slipping translation between both sides of the keratoplasty and/or the natural course of the KC disease itself, progressing within the recipient’s corneal ring [207, 208]. A possible option to prevent this might be to strengthen the recipient’s corneal ring with CXL ahead of the grafting procedure [209–211].

As KC can be more aggressive in children, paediatric PK can be indicated in end-stage pKC, but should be approached with caution, as prognosis can be variable [212]. It should be reserved for cases with advanced pathology or dysfunction, as rejection and failure rates greatly supersede the rates of adult PK [115]. In addition, some patients may need contact lenses after keratoplasty, which will require discussion prior to PK in cases of contact lens intolerance. DALK remains an effective therapeutic modality for the management of advanced KC in children [213].

Other Transplantation Techniques

Other transplantation techniques also exist, such as Bowman layer transplantation, small stromal lenticule, corneal allogenic ring segments or bioengineered tissue transplant, but these have not yet been incorporated into the standard of care for KC surgical management [214–219].

Intracorneal Ring Segments (Synthetic or Allogenic)

Intracorneal ring segments are synthetic polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) rings capable of reshaping the cornea, thus reducing anterior corneal curvature, astigmatism and asymmetry in keratoconic corneas. Rings can be implanted before CXL (recommended) or after CXL in patients with intolerance to RGP contact lenses and poor spectacle-corrected DCVA. These implants require a minimum corneal thickness of 450 µm in the site of the implant, usually located 5 or 6 mm from the corneal centre.

The ICRS can be inserted in stromal tunnels, created manually or by a femtosecond laser stromal dissection, by preforming an entry cut, followed by tunnel dissection at about 70–80% of the corneal pachymetry. In the last step, the segments are inserted into each channel using special forceps. Femtosecond lasers are the fastest option, and provide greater control of the depth, width and centring of the tunnel as well as an increased accuracy, thereby achieving better patient compliance.

In 2000, Colin et al. reported the first results of ICRS technology in KC. These authors found that INTACS technology could reduce the corneal steepening and astigmatism associated with KC, and proposed this technology as an additive surgical procedure for KC management [220]. ICRS implantation, alone or in combination with CXL, has been reported to induce visual and topographic improvement in patients with KC [39, 141, 221], but more published works describe the more frequent occurrence of complications (KC progression, segment extrusion and segment migration) in children [141, 221, 222].

Complications may occur intraoperatively (corneal perforation, incomplete tunnel dissection, vacuum loss) and postoperatively (migration, extrusion, infections, melting, unsuccessful visual results). Explantation due to functional failure is the main reason for ICRS removal, which leads to either a worsening of VA, more often in mild cases of KC, or to the need for a keratoplasty to further improve the VA in cases of very advanced disease. Spontaneous extrusion of the ring represents the main cause of anatomical failure and has happened in implantations performed in advanced cases of KC [223, 224].

Allogenic Intracorneal Ring Segments

Allogenic intracorneal ring segments of corneal tissue (AIRS) have become recently available as alternatives to synthetic PMMA rings. These consist of ring segments derived from allogenic human donor corneal tissue that can be combined simultaneously or sequentially with a CXL procedure in patients with KC intolerant to RGP contact lenses and who need a conservative improvement of spectacle-corrected DCVA. The AIRS technique consists in introducing customised semi-circular segments of allogenic tissue into preformed intra-corneal channels created manually by a femtosecond laser in the mid-peripheral stroma in order to reshape the corneal surface, improving its symmetry. The insertion is done under topical anaesthesia after femtosecond laser or manual channels preparation in the recipient’s eye [225].

AIRS combines the morphological and biomechanical benefits of ICRS, while avoiding the major complications reported with synthetic ICRS, thereby maintaining the advantage of an additive treatment with reshaping ability, improving the refractive power of the cornea also in a customised fashion according to corneal tomographic data. AIRS, being allogenic and less rigid, may be implanted into thinner corneas, making it suitable for more advanced cases. Despite its less invasive technique, limitations exist due to poorly predictable refractive outcomes, as a higher integration into the host corneal stroma is a must for allogenic corneal tissue [225, 226].

Currently, ring segment-shaped corneal allografts, implanted in intrastromal tunnels created using a femtosecond laser, are also available. The implantation of corneal allograft ring segments is a viable alternative treatment for KC with good safety and visual results [227].

Transepithelial Excimer Laser Central Corneal Remodelling or Regularisation

Selective topography-guided sequential and simultaneous excimer laser transepithelial photorefractive keratectomy (t-PRK) or transepithelial phototherapeutic keratectomy (t-PTK) combined with conventional and accelerated CXL (M nomogram) is another procedure used to improve the spectacle-corrected DCVA of patients with KC, in selected cases. These treatments aim to improve patients’ UDVA and best spectacle-corrected DCVA, reducing HOAs. Another purpose of central corneal remodelling combined with CXL is the improvement of contact lenses refitting on a more regular reshaped surface. The simultaneous technique (laser plus CXL in a same-day session) seems to overcome the drawbacks of the initial two-step CXL-PRK procedure, as laser ablation does not interfere with already cross-linked corneal tissue. However, the execution of the excimer laser, at least 6–12 months after CXL, yields functional results comparable to those of the sequential Laser/CXL treatment [228–230].

The goal of these treatments is therefore to minimise the need for corneal transplant by improving the quality of vision and life of patients with KC and offer new therapeutic hope for patients with contact lens intolerance or inadequate fitting, as these cases represent the best indication of CXL-combined refractive empowerment procedures, such as t-PRK or t-PTK. Furthermore, they do not prevent performing a DALK or PK in case of failure, insufficient VA, ectasia instability or patient dissatisfaction.

Follow-up data at 10 years reported gains in UDVA and BCVA in the absence of adverse events despite a small amount of tissue ablation. The micro-photo-ablative remodelling of the central corneal profile is generally planned by using modern ray-tracing and topo-guided excimer laser software. This remodelling helps to optimise the optical zones, minimise tissue consumption to a maximum 55 μm of corneal stroma and takes into account the contribution of the posterior corneal surface, thus avoiding overcorrections, preserving tissue consumption and regaining regular corneal shape and improving UDVA and BCVA [98].

Phakic Intraocular Lenses

Phakic intraocular lenses (pIOLs) can also be indicated in the visual rehabilitation of stable or CXL-stabilised patients with KC, usually in those suffering from high refractive errors with significant levels of anisometropia. pIOLs are an essential tool in the visual rehabilitation of patients with KC, if KC is stable. pIOL implantation is a reversible procedure, so pIOL exchange can be an option in the presence of an unexpected refractive change as unlike pseudophakic IOLs, accommodation is preserved [2, 231]. It is important to understand that pIOLs correct low-order aberrations (spherical and cylindrical refractive errors), but not HOAs, which are usually present in large amounts in patients with high levels of coma and coma-like aberrations and degrade the VA and quality of vision [232]. However, HOAs can be managed prior to pIOL implantation by ICRS, Excimer laser remodelling or both, in combination with CXL. Corneal regularisation and reduction of HOAs is recommended before pIOL implantation in order to improve the accuracy of biometric calculation and the final VA [233].

Phakic intraocular lens Implant Criteria

Phakic intraocular lenses may be considered when there is a stable KC with demonstrated refractive stability, as a progressive cone will affect the visual and refractive stability in the long term. In that case, a CXL procedure may be performed in advance.

As stated previously, pIOLs do not rehabilitate corneal irregularity and HOAs. In cases of unilateral highly asymmetric central KC with high myopia (anisometropia), pIOLs may be implanted if the patient shows an acceptable DCVA (> 0.5, decimal scale), with this level of vision being subjectively considered to be “acceptable” by the patient. If not, corneal reshaping procedures such as PRK-CXL or ICRS may precede pIOL implantation in order to reduce RGP dependency and enhance the visual outcome expected with the pIOL [2].

To meet pIOL anatomical requirements, the usual anatomical recommendations, according to the preferred pIOL model, need to be addressed before implantation (minimal anterior chamber depth and angle aperture, lens rise, endothelial cell density, etc.) [234].

Since the cornea and the crystalline lens remain unchanged with pIOL implantation, IOL power calculation is based on the subjective refraction preoperatively and the specific IOL calculation nomograms provided by the different pIOL manufacturers. To date, no specific pIOL calculation nomograms have been developed for KC to optimise the resulting vault (space between the posterior surface of the pIOL and the anterior lens capsule) in the case of posterior chamber pIOLs (PC-pIOL). Anterior chamber pIOLs (AC-pIOL) are not limited by a variable vault, as they are fixed to the anterior surface of the iris [235].

pIOL Models

AC-pIOLs are currently available in two formats, according to the amount of targeted spherical equivalent. The first format is a non-foldable PMMA lens, which requires a 5.5-mm incision, ideally through a scleral tunnel. The second format is a foldable polysiloxane lens with PMMA haptics, requiring a 3.2-mm corneal/limbal incision. Both types are fixed to the anterior surface of the iris by using a PMMA claw at the two haptics. Due to this mechanism of implantation, there is no risk of postoperative pIOL rotation, but a superior peripheral iridotomy (PI) is still needed to avoid the risk of pupillary block. Current models can correct up to 23.5 D of myopia and 7.5 D of astigmatism.

PC-pIOLs are currently available in different models, with most of the scientific evidence regarding the use of PC-pIOL in KC focusing on the implantable collamer lens (ICL). The ICL is a plate-haptic foldable collamer IOL implanted at the ciliary sulcus, between the iris and the crystalline lens, through a 3.2-mm corneal/limbal incision. The latest ICL models incorporate a tiny hole in the middle of the lens to prevent aqueous flow blockage, thereby eliminating the need for further placement of a PI. Its dioptre range can correct up to 18 D of myopia and 6 D of astigmatism [234].

New PC-pIOL models (none of them FDA-approved) have recently become available, such as the intraocular posterior chamber lens (IPCL) and Eyecryl Phakic IOL (Biotech Vision Care, Ahmedabad, India). They are hydrophilic acrylic IOLs and are supposed to offer a broader dioptre range than the ICL. If considered, they should be viewed with caution, as scientific evidence in peer-reviewed journals regarding their use is scarce. PC-pIOLs are usually preferred by the majority of surgeons over the iris claw AC-pIOLs as the former are easier to implant; nonetheless, they also present a risk of postoperative IOL rotation that does not exist with AC-pIOLs as long as they are correctly implanted.

In addition, the vault should be carefully optimised when using PC-pIOLs, as an excessive vault can compromise the irido-cornea angles, while an insufficient vault may imply a risk for secondary cataract. Vault prediction in KC eyes may be negatively influenced by a potential overestimation of the ACD if it is measured considering the apex of the cone. This could occasionally generate the selection of a larger diameter IOL than needed, and, in turn, a higher vault than expected. Although no specific calculation nomograms have been suggested for KC, careful measurement of the ACD is advised during the IOL selection for such patients [236].

Conclusion

Paediatric keratoconus is a corneal disorder that can significantly impair VA if left untreated. A range of therapeutic options is available to manage the condition based on the degree of evolution. Corneal CXL has demonstrated safety and efficacy in halting disease progression in children and adolescents. The use of contact lenses or ICRS can improve VA, while DALK appears to be a good surgical option. Nevertheless, lifestyle modifications should complement clinical management. Measures such as protecting the eyes from UV exposure and actively discouraging eye-rubbing behaviours are essential components of a comprehensive care strategy.

Finally, the need for international collaboration is critical. Initiatives such as the Keratoconus International Consortium (KIC), which promotes data sharing and the creation of common registries, offer promising pathways to deepen our understanding of pKC and optimise patient outcomes [237].
