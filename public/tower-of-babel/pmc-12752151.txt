Evaluation of the total antioxidant capacity and cytotoxicity of single shade universal resin composites
Authors: Betul Erdal, Gokhan Karadag
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundResin composites used must be biocompatible, considering their interaction with living tissues. The aim of this study was to compare the total antioxidant capacity and cytotoxicity induced by single shade universal resin composites on human gingival fibroblasts.MethodsIn this study, the cytotoxic effects of 7 different commercially available single shade universal resin composites on human gingival fibroblast cells were evaluated by MTT analysis on days 1 and days 7. In addition, the total antioxidant capacity of the cells was determined via the FRAP assay. The data obtained in the study were statistically analysed via the Kruskal-Wallis H and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (p < 0.05).ResultsIn the day-1 cytotoxicity assay, Charisma Diamond One was found to be the most cytotoxic of the resin composites tested and control group. Considering the day 7 results, all the resin composites caused a significant decrease in cell viability compared with that of control group. When the total antioxidant capacity of the cells incubated with the resin composites was compared, the cells incubated with the Charisma Diamond One presented the highest values, whereas the Omnichroma group presented significantly lower values than did the other resin composites tested.ConclusionConsidering the in vitro results and the materials’ low cytotoxic effects on HGF-1 cells, Essentia Universal and Omnichroma demonstrated lower cytotoxic effects in this in vitro model, suggesting a potentially favorable biological profile for restorations positioned close to the gingiva. However, further in vivo studies are necessary to confirm clinical safety.

Background

In restorative dentistry, resin composites are frequently used for aesthetic and functional rehabilitation. These materials must not only meet patients’ increasing aesthetic expectations but also restore the structural integrity of the tooth, demonstrate mechanical durability, and be biocompatible [1].

The layering technique has been used for many years to mimic the optical properties of natural teeth. In this technique, composite resins with different opacities and shades are used in successive layers to achieve aesthetic outcomes resembling natural tooth structure [2]. However, this method requires advanced technical skills by the clinician, prolongs the clinical application time, and has limitations due to its dependence on subjective factors in shade selection. To eliminate these disadvantages, single shade universal resin composites (SSURC) have been developed in recent years. These materials eliminate the need for shade selection by adapting to the shade of the surrounding tooth structure and enable restorations that are compatible with the tooth [3]. The clinical success of these products is based on an optical phenomenon described in the literature as the ‘blending effect.’ This effect refers to the ability of the dental material to optically reflect the shade of the surrounding natural hard tooth tissues [4].

Researchers have conducted numerous studies to evaluate the optical and mechanical properties of these newly introduced SSURCs [5–8]. However, biocompatibility is also critical to the long-term success of these restorative materials, which are in constant contact with oral tissues [9]. The biocompatibility of resin composites is primarily compromised by the release of residual monomers and other chemical components that leak into the surrounding tissue as a result of incomplete polymerization [1]. The primary mechanism of monomer-induced cytotoxicity is disruption of cellular redox balance. Leached monomers create oxidative stress in cells, which in turn develop an antioxidant defense mechanism. The balance between these two phenomena is called the redox balance; a disruption of this balance in favor of oxidative stress leads to cell death [10–12].

Regarding chemical composition, the hydrophobic monomer Bis-GMA has been reported to be associated with higher toxicity, partly due to its nature as a derivative of Bisphenol A (BPA) and its tendency for higher release rates compared to other monomers [10, 13, 14]. Similarly, other common monomers like TEGDMA have been shown to exert concentration-dependent cytotoxic effects by activating various cellular pathways, including apoptosis [11, 15, 16]. Consequently, the consideration of biocompatibility is of crucial clinical importance, particularly for restorations placed in close proximity to the gingival or pulpal tissues [9].

While the available literature on the cytotoxicity of SSURCs on gingival fibroblasts is limited, existing studies provide valuable data. Beltrami et al. evaluated the cytotoxicity of eight different composites, including Omnichroma, on human gingival fibroblast (HGF-1) cells and found that while Omnichroma exhibited the lowest toxicity at 48 h, a significant decrease in cell viability was observed at 72 h [17]. Ilie et al., in a study involving Omnichroma and other single-color composites, observed no significant differences between the materials but noted a significant change in cell viability at different time points [18]. Furthermore, a study by Mahmoud et al. similarly noted a significant decrease in the viability of cells treated with Omnichroma at 72 h [19]. While these findings suggest that SSURC cytotoxicity is both material- and time-dependent and generally exhibits a cumulative effect over time, a comprehensive study evaluating this cytotoxicity in conjunction with Total Antioxidant Capacity (TAC), a key indicator of cellular defense for this selection of available single-color composites, is currently lacking. To address this gap, the aim of the current study was to compare the cytotoxicity and TAC levels of seven different commercially available SSURC on HGF-1 cells.

The null hypotheses are as follows:

H01: There was no difference in cytotoxicity between the composites used.

H01: There was no difference in cytotoxicity between the composites used.

H02: The cytotoxicity of the composites did not change over time.

H02: The cytotoxicity of the composites did not change over time.

H03: The composites used do not lead to different total antioxidant capacities.

A priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7) to determine the adequate sample size for this study. Based on previous comparable research in dental resin composite cytotoxicity [9, 17], an effect size f = 1.78 was calculated, which represents a large effect size according to Cohen’s conventions. With an alpha level of 0.05 and power of 0.95 for detecting significant differences among eight experimental groups, the minimum required total sample size was calculated as 32 (4 per group). However, to account for potential technical variations in cell culture experiments and to ensure robust statistical power for post-hoc analyses, the sample size was set to n = 6 per group (total N = 48).

Preparation of samples

For this study, a metal mold with a diameter of 7 mm and a depth of 2 mm was produced. The composites were placed in the metal molds using a sterile spatula and the overflowing material was removed. The samples were polymerized for 20 s on both surfaces with an LED light device (Elipar™ S10; 3 M ESPE, St. Paul, MN, USA) with a light power of 1200 mW/cm2. A total of 18 samples were prepared from each SSURC, 12 of which were for cytotoxicity evaluation (6 for day-1, 6 for day-7) and 6 for TAC measurement.

Aluminium oxide coated polishing discs (Sof-Lex™ Finishing and Polishing Disc Set; 3 M ESPE, St. Paul, MN, USA) and diamond coated polishing wheels (Sof-Lex™ Diamond Polishing System; 3 M ESPE, St. Paul, MN, USA) were used for polishing the composite disc samples according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The prepared composite disc samples were then sterilised in an autoclave at 121 °C, 1 atmosphere pressure, and in steam for 20 min, in accordance with the manufacturers’ instructions.

Evaluation of the cytotoxic effects of the samples

HGF-1 cells (CRL-2014, ATCC, USA) were used for the cell culture studies. The cells were cultured in fibroblast basal medium supplemented with fibroblast growth kit components in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator at 37 °C. When the cells proliferated 80%, the cells were transferred to 12-well plates (3 × 104 cell/well) and incubated overnight in a CO2 incubator.

The cytotoxicity analysis was performed using an indirect method (leachables testing), which is commonly reported in the literature for dental materials [20]. A 2-compartment system was created by placing cell culture inserts in 12-well plates (Fig. 1). The HGF-1 cells were placed in the lower chamber (1.5 ml cell medium), and the test materials were placed in the upper chamber (0.5 ml cell medium). This setup allowed the diffusion of leachable components (monomers, degradation products) from the composite discs into the cell culture medium without direct physical contact with the cells.

Two-compartment (indirect contact) system

The exposure time points for cytotoxicity evaluation were set at Day 1 (24 h) and Day 7. This selection was based on the need to assess both initial leachables release (Day 1) and longer-term cumulative toxicity (Day 7), consistent with established protocols and studies evaluating monomer release over time [14]. After the respective incubation periods (1 and 7 days), the medium was removed, 1 mg/ml MTT solution was added to the cells, and the resulting absorbance was read at 570 nm. As a negative control, cells were incubated with fresh culture medium only (100% viability) (Merck Protocol Guide). A positive control was not included, as the primary objective of this study was the relative comparison of cytotoxicity among the tested composites against the baseline (Control), rather than determining the absolute cytotoxic threshold, consistent with the standard protocol in similar dental material toxicity studies.

Evaluation of the total antioxidant capacity of the cell samples

HGF-1 cells were cultured in 12-well plates at a concentration of 3 × 104. The 2-compartment indirect system was used as in the cytotoxicity test. The 2-chamber system was used as in the cytotoxicity test, but cells were exposed to the materials for 72 h (37 °C, 5% CO2). The 72-hour exposure time was selected to assess the cellular oxidative stress response during an acute and critical phase of material exposure; a timeframe commonly used for in vitro toxicity and oxidative marker evaluation in dental material studies [21].

Following the exposure, the cells were collected by centrifugation at 2500 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C and washed 2 times with cold PBS. To obtain cell lysates and release antioxidants, the cells were first sonicated and then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min. The total antioxidant content in the cell supernatants was determined via a T-AOC kit (FRAP assay) and the total protein content of the cells from each well was determined for normalization, allowing the final results to be expressed as concentration per unit of protein.

Statistical analysis

The data obtained in this study were analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS Inc., Version 21, Chicago, IL, USA) package program. Because the data did not show a normal distribution according to the Shapiro-Wilk test, the Kruskal-Wallis H test was used for comparisons between groups (different SSURCs). When the Kruskal-Wallis H test results were statistically significant, the Dunn’s post hoc test with Bonferroni correction was applied for paired comparisons to determine the source of the difference. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used for comparisons within groups (evaluation at different time periods), and a p-value of less than 0.05 was considered to indicate significant difference.

On Day 1, cell viability differed significantly among the groups (p = 0.0001). The cell viability rate for CDO (60.87%) was statistically significantly lower than all groups. A lack of statistically significant differences was observed when the ZC, CMU, EU, and VAU (95.50%, 89.23%, 95.04%, 94.28%, respectively) groups were compared with the control group (98.30%) (p > 0.05). The viability level of cells cultured with XF and OC (85.37%, 85.88%, respectively) group composites was significantly lower than the Control group, but significantly higher than CDO (p < 0.05).

On Day 7, all composite groups exhibited a statistically significant decrease in cell viability compared to the Control group (p < 0.05). While the cell viability values of the OC and EU (81.06%, 92.91%, respectively) groups did not demonstrate a significant difference when compared to each other (p > 0.05), they were significantly higher than the other tested composites (p < 0.05).

Subsequent examination of the alterations in cell viability among the experimental groups revealed that the CDO, EU, and OC groups exhibited no substantial variance (p = 0.917, p = 0.345, p = 0.601, respectively). However, the cell viability values of the ZC, CMU, VAU, and XF groups demonstrated a significant decline (p = 0.028, p = 0.028, p = 0.028, p = 0.046, respectively).

To provide clinical context, viability results were categorized according to cytotoxicity criteria. According to the ISO 10993-5 standard (cell viability ≤ 70% defines cytotoxicity), cell viability in the groups exposed to CDO (Days 1 and 7) and ZC (Day 7 only) was classified as cytotoxic [22].

When evaluated using a defined classification scale used by Ahrari et al. (e.g., non-cytotoxic: ≥90%, mild: 60–90%, moderate: 30–59%, severe cytotoxicity: <%30), the results showed a time-dependent change: on Day 1, ZC, EU, and VAU exhibited no cytotoxicity, while CDO, CMU, OC, and XF were classified as mild cytotoxic. By Day 7, only the EU group was classified as non-cytotoxic, while all other compound groups were classified as mild cytotoxic [23].

Discussion

Materials that interact with living tissues can cause cytotoxicity by exerting an oxidative effect on cells. Since resin composites are in contact with living tissues in the oral environment, the toxicity of the materials is important for the health of the gingiva and surrounding tissues and the success of the treatment. This study compared the cytotoxicity and TAC of different SSURCs (OC, VAU, CMU, XF, ZC, CDO, EU) on gingival fibroblasts.

According to the first day MTT results, the viability level of the cells exposed to CDO was significantly lower than all the composites tested; the viability of the cells incubated with ZC, CMU and EU was significantly higher than the XF group. According to the seventh day MTT results, the cytotoxicity of CDO was significantly higher than CMU, EU, VAU, OC and XF; the cytotoxicity of EU and OC was significantly lower than the other composites tested. As a consequence of these results, our first null hypothesis ‘There is no difference in cytotoxicity between the composites used’ was rejected. Comparing the MTT values at both time points, CDO, EU, OC had no significant effect on cell viability, whereas there was a significant decrease in the viability level of the other groups. Based on these results, the second null hypothesis ‘The cytotoxicity of composites does not change over time’ was partially rejected. The third null hypothesis ‘The composites used do not result in different total antioxidant capacities’ was investigated. When the total antioxidant capacities were compared, the TAC of the cells exposed to CDO was significantly higher than the other groups tested, while the TAC of the cells incubated with OC was significantly lower than the other groups tested. The third null hypothesis was therefore rejected.

The structure and properties of the monomers contained in the resin composites also have an important role in the cytotoxic effects of the materials. studies have shown that the release of Bis-GMA from resin composites is higher than that of other monomers tested [24, 25]. According to the results of our study, the viability rate of OC, which does not contain BisGMA in the monomer content, was significantly higher than CMU, XF, and ZC containing BisGMA in the results of cell viability analysis at day 7.

Many researchers have stated that the cytotoxicity of resin composites is mainly related to the chemical composition of the material and the degree of conversion, expressed as the conversion of monomers to polymers. As a result of incomplete polymerisation, methacrylate and dimethacrylate monomers, which are called residual monomers and known to be toxic, are released into the environment. In parallel, it is known that the degree of conversion is directly proportional to biocompatibility [10, 26–28].

Yılmaz Atalı et al. compared the physical and mechanical properties of SSURC and found that there was no statistically significant difference between the degree of conversion of OC, ZC and EU. In the same study, it was reported that there was no difference between the degree of conversion of CDO, EU, ZC and VAU [8]. Alharbi et al. compared four composites including OC, CDO and VAU and found that there was a significant difference between the composites in terms of the degree of conversion. VAU has the highest degree of conversion, followed by OC and then CDO [29]. According to the results of 24 h MTT test in our study, the viability values of the cells incubated with VAU, OC and CDO were determined to be 94.28, 85.88, 60.87, respectively. While there was no statistically significant difference in the viability values of cells exposed to ZC, CMU, EU, VAU and OC, CDO caused significantly lower cell viability than the other composites. It can be said that these results from our study are in parallel with the studies investigating the degree of transformation in the literature.

Researchers have reached a consensus that BisGMA is the most toxic monomer, with the order of toxicity being BisGMA >UDMA >TEGDMA >HEMA [24, 25]. The OC we used in our study is a resin composite that does not contain BisGMA as a monomer. According to the Day 7 cell viability analysis, the viability rate induced by OC was significantly higher than that of CMU, XF, and ZC, which contain BisGMA.

Al-Hiyasat et al. investigated the cytotoxicity of conventional and flowable resin composites and reported that the chemical structure and monomer ratio of the composite have a significant impact on cytotoxicity, with cytotoxicity increasing as the organic matrix ratio increases [30]. In this context, the cell viability of the composites evaluated in our study, EU and OC [81% filler ratio, 79 − 68% (w/v), respectively], was significantly higher than that of CMU and ZC [78 − 40% (w/v), 75 − 53% (w/v), respectively], based on Day 7 cell viability analysis. Furthermore, the cell viability of cells exposed to VAU [82 − 72% (w/v)] was significantly higher than that of cells exposed to CDO [81 − 64% (w/v)] based on Day 1 MTT analysis.

Kavuncu et al. investigated the cytotoxicity of three different composites, Estelite Quick Sigma, Admira Fusion, and Charisma Topaz, on HGF-1 and HPDL using MTT analysis over a 1-day and 1-week period [9]. Charisma Topaz contains the newly developed TCD monomer as a monomer and does not contain BisGMA derivative monomers, which are known to be the most toxic monomers [20, 21]. In our study, CDO from Kulzer also contains the same monomer. Kavuncu et al. found Charisma Topaz to be the most toxic material in both cell groups, and its toxicity increased significantly over time [9]. In our study, CDO was significantly the most toxic material based on 24-hour results. Looking at the 1-week results, although it yielded the lowest average cell viability, there was no significant difference with ZC. When looking at the change over time, there was no significant change in our study. Although composites containing BisGMA were found in both studies, CDO was the most toxic composite.

The increased TAC levels, particularly in the CDO group, suggest a cellular defensive response against the cytotoxic components released by the composite. This response involves the increased synthesis of endogenous antioxidants such as glutathione and the upregulation of detoxifying enzymes, which act to neutralize Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) [11]. The high TAC in CDO, despite high cytotoxicity, may indicate that the cellular damage (cytotoxicity) is triggering a maximal, yet insufficient, antioxidant defense.

In the literature, there is a limited number of studies evaluating TAC formed by composites. Çelik et al. investigated the cytotoxic and oxidative effects of different restorative materials (amalgam, glass ionomer cement, compomer, and composite) on HGF-1. As a result of the study, it was found that all materials caused a significant decrease in cell viability compared to the control group, and these restorative materials significantly increased the TAC level in the cells after 7 days of incubation [31]. This is consistent with the fact that CDO, which caused a significant decrease in cell viability compared to the control group, resulted in the highest TAC level in our study.

Gociu et al. reported that monomers released from dental materials increased ROS in cells [32]. Increased ROS can cause oxidative stress by causing an imbalance between oxidants and antioxidants in the cell, resulting in cell death [33]. In our study, it was observed that the cells incubated with CDO produced the highest TAC and its cytotoxic effect was the highest among the groups tested.

Protecting HGF-1 is of critical clinical importance, as these cells play a vital role in maintaining the integrity of the periodontium and repairing oral soft tissues. Cytotoxicity to HGF-1 can lead to chronic inflammation, gingival recession, delayed healing, and ultimately compromise the longevity of the restoration and overall periodontal health [9].

This in vitro study, despite providing valuable data on the biocompatibility of single-shade composites, has several inherent limitations that must be acknowledged. Firstly, we utilized the established HGF-1 cell line to simulate the clinical environment, but this immortalized line may not fully replicate the complex physiological responses of primary human gingival fibroblasts or the in vivo defense mechanisms. Secondly, the study employed an indirect contact method using a two-compartment system, which allows the diffusion of leachables but does not entirely mimic the constant flow of saliva, tissue fluid, temperature fluctuations, and masticatory forces present in the oral cavity. Furthermore, the assessment of biocompatibility was primarily based on cell viability and total antioxidant capacity. The inclusion of qualitative analyses, such as microscopic evaluation of cell morphology, and the quantification of specific inflammatory markers would have provided a more comprehensive understanding of the cellular response to these materials. The absence of this data constitutes a limitation of the current study’s scope.

Additionally, the sterilization protocol presents a methodological limitation. Although autoclaving is a manufacturer-recommended technique and ensures sterility, exposure to heat and humidity (121 °C, 1 atm) may have inadvertently caused some postpolymerization in the resin samples, which may have reduced the residual monomer content and thus reduced the observed cytotoxicity. Lastly, the cytotoxicity was evaluated only at Day 1 and Day 7, and TAC at a single 72-hour time point. Given that composite restorations have a significantly longer retention time in the mouth, this short observation periods limit the assessment of long-term biological effects and degradation byproducts. Therefore, future research incorporating longer time periods, different cell lines, and in vivo models is warranted to fully elucidate the long-term clinical safety of these materials.

Conclusions

This in vitro study investigated the cytotoxic profiles and cellular antioxidant responses induced by seven different SSURC on HGF-1 cells. Within the limitations of this study, significant differences were observed in both the cytotoxic effects and the TAC levels among the tested groups. Specifically, EU and OC demonstrated the least cytotoxic effect, while CDO and ZC were found to be the most cytotoxic. These findings underscore the critical importance of material selection in restorative procedures near soft tissues, as the biocompatibility directly influences gingival health and the longevity of the restoration. Considering the lower cytotoxic impact observed in this in vitro model, EU and OC may be preferred for restorations positioned close to the gingiva. However, given the nature of in vitro methodologies and potential differences from the complex oral environment, further studies evaluating longer-term effects and incorporating in vivo models are necessary to fully validate the clinical safety and long-term performance of these materials.
