Comparison of four unidimensional pain scales and women’s preferences during the first stage of labor: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Zehra Çerçer, Ferhat Aslan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo compare four unidimensional pain assessment scales during the first stage of labor by evaluating agreement among the scales and women’s preferences for pain assessment tools in a clinical setting.MethodsA cross-sectional comparative study was conducted between August 2023 and June 2024 with 135 women who experienced spontaneous vaginal births at a state hospital in Türkiye. Four commonly used unidimensional pain scales—the Visual Analog Scale (VAS), Facial Pain Scale (FPS), Numerical Rating Scale (NRS), and Verbal Descriptive Scale (VDS)—were compared in terms of inter-scale agreement during the latent and active phases of labor. Participants were also asked to indicate their preferred pain scale.ResultsThe FPS was the most preferred scale, chosen by 41.5% of the women. All four pain scales showed strong, positive, and statistically significant correlations during both the latent and active phases of labor (p < 0.001). The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) for pain scores among the scales was 0.941 (F = 17.063, p < 0.001) during the latent phase and 0.932 (F = 14.679, p < 0.001) during the active phase.ConclusionsThe Facial Pain Scale demonstrated the highest preference and reliability among women during labor, whereas the Visual Analog Scale was rated lower in both preference and consistency. These findings highlight the importance of using appropriate and patient-centered tools in labor pain management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12884-026-09811-w.Keywords: Labor pain, Pain measurement, Pain scales, Patient preference, Women

Introduction

Labor pain, which is known to be one of the most severe forms of pain, is a physiological and acute type of pain [1, 2]. The expression of labor pain is an experience that needs to be considered in the context of not only its physiological dimensions but also its cultural, emotional, and individual aspects [1–3]. The management of labor pain is critical to the identification of physiopathological events that may develop during labor and the maintenance of maternal/fetal health [1, 4]. The measurement of the pain levels of women during labor and the administration of analgesia by healthcare professionals inform their decisions about the type and timing of the interventions they will make [5]. For this reason, optimal pain assessment during labor is highly important for pain management [1, 6].

The first stage of labor begins with the onset of regular uterine contractions and continues until the cervix reaches full dilation. This stage is divided into two phases: the latent phase and the active phase. The latent phase is the period extending from the onset of labor to the transition into the active phase, during which cervical dilation progresses slowly and uterine contractions are generally mild to moderate in intensity and infrequent. During this phase, cervical effacement, softening, and gradual dilation occur, and the duration of this process varies considerably among individuals, potentially lasting several hours [7]. The active phase, in contrast, is characterized by a marked acceleration in the rate of cervical dilation, with contractions becoming stronger, more frequent, and progressively more intense, leading to a more rapid progression of labor [8]. These two phases constitute the fundamental physiological basis for changes in the intensity and perception of labor pain and are critical for the clinical monitoring of the labor process [3].

Measurement instruments are used in pain assessments to identify the severity and quality of pain experienced by individuals more objectively [9–11]. Pain intensity should be measured using tools that are easy to apply and preferred by patients; patient preferences should be taken into account to establish a common language [12]. In clinical practice, unidimensional measurement instruments designed to identify the intensity of pain are frequently used. The most prevalently employed unidimensional pain measurement instruments are the Visual Analog Scale (VAS), the Faces Pain Scale (FPS), the Verbal Descriptor Scale (VDS), and the Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) [9, 13, 14]. It is seen that unidimensional pain scales are also used in general in the assessment of pain intensity during labor and in obstetrics practice [5, 15, 16].

While unidimensional pain scales use pictures, statements, and numeric ratings, their reliability and compatibility with each other are also important [11, 12]. Reliable pain scales enable practitioners to accurately assess pain management and the effectiveness of interventions. Evaluating the reliability of different scales is important for consistent and valid pain measurement and helps to obtain standardized data [17]. Although previous studies have used various pain assessment instruments, studies specifically focusing on different unidimensional pain scales during the first stage of labor are limited [9, 18, 19].

In previous studies, labor pain has been evaluated from various perspectives. For example, Deng and colleagues examined labor pain in terms of parity and differences in pain intensity between consecutive vaginal deliveries [5]. Pratici and colleagues, on the other hand, investigated the agreement between measurements obtained from commonly used pain scales such as the VAS and NRS and women’s subjective pain assessments [16]. A comprehensive review addressing the assessment of labor pain has shown that the VAS and NRS are the most widely used tools and that the need for standardization in pain assessment practices persists [20].

Although unidimensional pain scales are widely used in various clinical settings, their performance and reliability during labor are not entirely clear due to the unique physiological and subjective characteristics of labor pain. In this context, the present study addresses a significant gap in the literature by comparing four unidimensional pain assessment scales in women during the first stage of labor, with a particular focus on inter-scale agreement and women’s preferences regarding these scales. This study was conducted to compare four unidimensional pain assessment scales during the first stage of labor by evaluating agreement among the scales and women’s preferences for pain assessment tools in a clinical setting.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional comparative study was conducted to evaluate inter-scale agreement and women’s preferences regarding four commonly used unidimensional pain scales during the first stage of labor and adhered to the STROBE checklist [see Additional file 1] [21]. Conducted in the labor unit of a state hospital between August 2023 and June 2024, the study included 135 women presenting for spontaneous vaginal delivery. Participants were recruited consecutively among those meeting the inclusion criteria and providing informed consent. Eligible participants were Turkish-speaking women aged 18–35, with singleton pregnancies at 38–40 weeks, no psychiatric disorders or communication barriers, and no high-risk obstetric conditions. Women who received epidural anesthesia/analgesia, underwent cesarean delivery, developed dystocia, or withdrew were excluded.

The sample size for this study was determined based on the sample size approach for reliability studies proposed by Walter et al. (1998), using a power analysis based on the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) [22]. The analysis assumed an expected ICC of 0.80 with an acceptable precision of ± 0.10, a minimum acceptable reliability coefficient (ρ₀) of 0.70, a significance level of α = 0.05 (two-tailed), and a statistical power of 80% (1 − β = 0.80). There were four repeated measurements (k = 4) for each participant. The analysis revealed that the minimum sample size was 86 participants. The study was completed with 135 participants, and this number was deemed sufficient since it met the planned minimum sample size.

Data collection instruments

Data were collected using the Personal Information Form, Visual Analog Scale (VAS), Facial Pain Scale (FPS), Numerical Rating Scale (NRS), and Verbal Descriptive Scale (VDS).

Personal information form

The form was developed by the researchers based on relevant literature and included items on women’s characteristics [2, 5]. In addition to a single item designed to determine the pain scale participants preferred for assessing labor pain, the form included four variables—age, education level, number of previous births (parity), and whether the current pregnancy was planned—and consisted of a total of five items.

Visual analog scale

The VAS is a 10 cm line anchored by “0” (no pain) and “10” (worst pain); the pain score corresponds to the distance in centimeters from “0” to the participant’s mark. Its validity and reliability have been proven, and it is widely used in clinical settings [11, 18]. The advantages of VAS are its simplicity, minimal equipment requirements, and repeatability. Its disadvantages are that it requires visual and abstract thinking, is impractical in emergencies, and is less practical than verbal expressions [11, 12, 23].

Faces pain scale

FPS is a 6-level scale that assesses pain levels using facial expressions. The participant selects the facial expression that best reflects the pain they are feeling, and a numerical value is assigned to this expression [12, 23, 24]. Due to its non-verbal structure, it is preferred for use in patients with different communication/language characteristics, in children, and in adults with limited mental capacity [12, 25]. In line with the literature, in this study, the facial expressions used to score the scale were assigned the respective scores of 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, and 10, where facial expressions indicating more pain corresponded to higher scores [12, 23].

Numeric rating scale

NRS is a unidimensional scale consisting of 11 numbers between 0 and 10 designed to measure the severity of pain. The respondent is asked to rate their experience from “0” (no pain at all) to “10” (most severe pain). It is frequently used in the measurement of pain due to its convenience of use and scoring [12, 23].

Verbal descriptor scale

The VDS measures pain severity through verbal descriptors. In this study, scores were assigned as follows: no pain = 0, mild = 2, moderate = 4, severe = 6, very severe = 8, and unbearable = 10, based on existing literature [12, 23]. VDS is preferred, especially in elderly patients, due to its simplicity. However, its disadvantage is that it requires literacy and may present difficulties in selecting the appropriate expression [24].

Data collection

Clinical setting and routine obstetric practice

The study was conducted in a clinical setting where no standardized pharmacological analgesia protocol or structured non-pharmacological pain management program was routinely implemented. Women did not receive any study-specific pain relief intervention, and labor progressed according to routine clinical care. Oxytocin was not routinely administered and was used only when clinically indicated for labor induction or augmentation based on physician decision.

Assessment of labor stages

Digital vaginal examinations were performed as part of routine obstetric care to assess labor progression. Upon admission to the delivery unit, an initial digital vaginal examination was conducted to determine the stage of labor. Women were admitted to the delivery room when cervical dilation was ≥ 3 cm. The latent phase was defined as cervical dilation of 0–4 cm, while the active phase was defined as cervical dilation of ≥ 5 cm. During the latent phase, examinations were performed at longer intervals based on clinical indication, whereas during the active phase, they were typically repeated every 2–4 h or more frequently depending on clinical necessity.

Study procedure and participant recruitment

Women who met the inclusion criteria were admitted to the delivery unit and informed about the purpose and procedures of the study. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. Participants were consecutively recruited according to eligibility criteria.

Pain assessment and scale administration

Pain intensity was assessed during both the latent and active phases of labor. Participants were asked to rate their current contraction-related pain using four unidimensional pain scales during the same contraction. The scales were presented as paper-based forms in a non-fixed order to minimize potential order and learning effects. Participants completed each scale within a few seconds, according to their ability to express their perceived pain intensity. No clinical intervention or additional procedure was performed between scale administrations. After completion of each scale, the form was removed from view before the next scale was presented to reduce response bias. Following completion of the latent phase assessments, progression to the active phase was confirmed via digital cervical examination, after which the same assessment procedure was repeated. Immediately after completing all active phase assessments, participants were asked to identify the pain scale they found most suitable and comfortable for clinical use. Further details of the data collection process are provided in File 2 [see Additional file 2].

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 22. Descriptive statistics included frequency, percentage, mean, and standard deviation. Normality was confirmed by skewness and kurtosis coefficients [26]. Fisher’s exact test examined associations between sociodemographic and obstetric variables and scale preferences. Concurrent validity among the four unidimensional pain scales (VAS, FPS, NRS, and VDS) was examined using Pearson correlation analysis to assess the degree of association between scales. These analyses were performed separately for the latent and active phases of labor. Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) were used as the primary measure of agreement among the scales and were calculated independently for each labor phase. Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) were calculated using a two-way random effects model with a consistency definition (Type C). To assess agreement among the four pain scales, average-measures ICC values were reported. Although single-measures ICC values were also calculated, average-measures ICC values were considered the primary indicator of overall agreement between scales. Since this study was not a scale development or psychometric validation study, content validity and construct validity analyses were not performed. Due to the dynamic and rapidly changing nature of labor pain, test–retest reliability was not methodologically appropriate. Therefore, reliability assessment was limited to ICC analysis, which reflects agreement between different scales. Analyses were conducted with a 95% confidence interval and significance set at p < 0.05.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Gaziantep Islam Science and Technology University Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Decision No: 234.25.11, Date: 30.05.2023) and written permission for the Provincial Directorate of Health responsible for the hospital where the study would be carried out (Decision No: 118, Date: 14.07.2023) were obtained to conduct the study. Participants were informed about the study, and written informed consent was obtained. This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Discussion

Unidimensional measurement instruments are used more frequently as they are fast and convenient in practice [20]. The most frequently used unidimensional measurement instruments in pain assessment during labor are FPS, VDS, NRS, and VAS [20, 27, 28]. Effective pain assessment is key to improving maternal comfort and birth outcomes. This study uniquely compares four unidimensional pain scales in early labor, offering practical guidance for selecting reliable, patient-preferred tools in obstetric care.

Our results demonstrated a high agreement among the scales and showed that women most preferred the FPS and the least frequently preferred one was VAS. This finding highlights the importance of clinicians using pain assessment tools that are not chosen arbitrarily but are both reliable and aligned with patient preferences. To the best of our knowledge, no studies examining women’s preferences for unidimensional pain scales during labor have been encountered in the literature; however, research in other patient groups generally indicates a preference for the FPS [29–31], while some studies have also shown the preference of other unidimensional scales [12, 32, 33]. Labor is a process in which severe and long-lasting pain is experienced by its nature [34]. In our study, women’s preference for the FPS likely reflects its ability to quickly and practically convey pain through facial expressions. Variations across studies may result from cultural factors influencing pain perception.

No statistically significant differences were found in scale preferences based on sociodemographic or obstetric factors. The literature generally supports these findings, showing that individuals with higher education levels prefer numerical and visual scales, while those with lower education levels prefer FPS [30–32]. The lower preference for VAS and higher preference for FPS may reflect the greater cognitive demands of VAS and NRS, with participants favoring scales requiring minimal mental effort. Additionally, FPS was consistently preferred across childbirth order and pregnancy planning groups, likely due to its more intuitive visual format.

This study provides comparative data on four pain scales used during the latent and active phases of labor, all demonstrating good agreement. Strong and significant correlations were observed among the pain scales used in this study. These findings are generally in line with previous research reporting moderate to high correlations between different unidimensional pain scales across various clinical populations, particularly in non-obstetric groups [18, 19, 23, 32, 35, 36]. In obstetric populations, the limited available data also suggests comparable patterns, indicating that pain scales tend to show good but not perfect agreement when assessing severe labor pain [16]. It should be noted that the Pearson correlation analyses were included solely to provide a descriptive comparison of the relationships among the pain scales and should not be interpreted as evidence of construct validity. Further studies are needed to confirm the reproducibility of these findings in larger obstetric samples.

ICC values showed excellent agreement among the four scales, confirming that VAS, NRS, VDS, and FPS reliably assess pain severity in the first labor stage. To our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate their reliability specifically for labor pain. For this reason, it was not possible to directly discuss these results along with other studies. Labor pain is described as an acute form of physiological pain [37]. Based on this information, the results of this study were compared to those in previous studies examining the reliability of pain scales in the assessment of acute pain, and similarly high ICC values were observed [23, 36, 38]. In a systematic review, it was reported that VAS, the Verbal Rating Scale (VRS), and NRS were reliable for use in clinical practice [11]. In another systematic review, Hjermstad et al. (2011) reported that using any scale among NRS, VRS, and VAS was effective in measuring pain [39].

During the latent phase, mean pain scores were 3.39 (VAS), 3.52 (FPS), 4.28 (NRS), and 3.3 (VDS). In the active phase, scores increased to 8.77 (VAS), 8.82 (FPS), 9.05 (NRS), and 8.91 (VDS). A test-retest reliability analysis was not considered appropriate for the study, considering that the severity of labor pain naturally increases as the labor progresses [37, 40]. In this study, there were also increases in the pain levels of the participants from the latent phase to the active phase of labor. There are several studies demonstrating the relationship between the severity of labor pain and the progression of labor [37–41]. The first stage of labor is more stable, while the frequency, duration, and magnitude of uterine contractions increase in the second stage, and the pain becomes sharper [37, 40]. In this context, the increase in pain scores over time observed in our study was an expected result.

A review study showed that VAS was the most frequently used unidimensional scale in the assessment of labor pain in the literature [20]. In our study, the scores of the participants on VAS were lower than their scores on the other scales for the measurements made in both the latent and active phases. Gürkan et al. (2020) determined VAS to be the scale with the highest error rate among all scales examined in their study [12]. VAS is a scale requiring the respondent to mark a point on a line corresponding to pain levels varying from no pain at all to the most severe pain [11, 18]. Thus, it requires more abstract thinking in the definition of pain, which could explain the results of this study.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the consecutive administration of four different pain scales to the same participant may have introduced an order effect, meaning that responses to one scale could have influenced responses to subsequent scales. Although the order of scale administration was varied to minimize this effect, repeated exposure to multiple pain scales may still have introduced a degree of recall bias that could have influenced participants’ responses. This potential carryover effect should be considered when interpreting the results. The study’s sample is specific and homogeneous, limiting the generalizability of the results to women with different pregnancy and delivery conditions. Future research should application and include different obstetric groups.

Conclusion

In this study, the FPS was the most preferred scale for expressing pain during the first stage of labor, while the VAS was the least preferred. The four pain assessment scales demonstrated excellent agreement, as indicated by high intraclass correlation coefficients, during both the latent and active phases of labor. Given its lower preference among women, the routine use of the VAS in labor practice may warrant reconsideration. As childbirth care providers, nurses, midwives, and physicians can contribute to a more patient-centered approach to labor pain assessment by selecting pain assessment scales that demonstrate good reliability and are preferred by patients. Future studies comparing unidimensional pain scales with multidimensional childbirth-specific assessment tools may further improve labor pain assessment.
