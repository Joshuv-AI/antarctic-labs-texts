Levels of acylated and deacylated ghrelin are elevated in unmedicated patients with major depressive disorder and obesity: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Michał Lis, Tymoteusz Miłuch, Renata Krupa, Aleksandra Langowska, Maciej Majdowski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Major depressive disorder (MDD) frequently co-occurs with obesity, suggesting shared neuroendocrine mechanisms. Ghrelin, a hormone involved in appetite regulation, stress response, and reward processing, has been proposed as a biological link between metabolic and mood disorders. However, previous studies have reported inconsistent findings, and most assessed only total ghrelin rather than its biologically distinct fractions. This cross-sectional study examined circulating concentrations of acylated and deacylated ghrelin in unmedicated (≥ 12 months) patients with MDD and comorbid obesity compared with non-depressed controls with obesity, matched by sex, age, and BMI. Sixty-two adults were included (31 with MDD and 31 controls), and MDD diagnosis was confirmed using the MINI-International Neuropsychiatric Interview. Fasting plasma ghrelin fractions were measured using ELISA under standardised preanalytical conditions. Participants with MDD exhibited significantly higher acylated (p < 0.001) and deacylated (p = 0.007) ghrelin concentrations than controls. In multivariable general linear models adjusted for sex, age, and BMI, MDD remained an independent predictor of both acylated (F(1,56) = 17.17, p < 0.001) and deacylated (F(1,56) = 6.93, p = 0.011) ghrelin, with a stronger effect observed for the biologically active, acylated form. The acyl-to-deacyl ghrelin ratio did not differ significantly between groups, suggesting increased overall ghrelin secretion rather than a selective shift in acylation. The persistence of ghrelin elevation within a metabolic state physiologically associated with ghrelin suppression supports the involvement of the ghrelin system in MDD pathophysiology.

Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is one of the leading causes of disability worldwide, hence it constitutes a major public health burden. The prevalence of obesity has increased profoundly over recent decades. These two conditions frequently co-occur, which each condition increasing the risk of developing the other. To explain this association, it has been proposed that MDD and obesity share common pathophysiological pathways including dysregulation of hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, low-grade inflammation, metabolic disturbances including insulin-resistance, and socioeconomical factors1. The potential involvement of appetite-regulating hormones, such as leptin and ghrelin, has also been proposed in recent research2,3.

Ghrelin, a 28-amino acid peptide hormone is secreted by neuroendocrine cells located in the stomach. Its levels rise during fasting periods and drop after meal consumption. Ghrelin crosses blood brain barrier and acts in the arcuate nucleus of the hypothalamus. By activating orexigenic (NPY/AgRP) and inhibiting anorexigenic (POMC/CART) neurons, it promotes appetite and energy conservation4. Furthermore, ghrelin stimulates dopaminergic neurons in the ventral tegmental area (VTA), influencing reward processing and motivational behaviour5.

Ghrelin circulates in two principal isoforms (acylated and deacylated) that differ in their biochemistry and receptor pharmacology. The acylated form binds to the growth hormone secretagogue receptor (GHSR), whereas the deacylated fraction may act via partially distinct pathways, suggesting potential isoform-specific roles in neuroendocrine regulation. Deacylated ghrelin predominates in circulation and exerts context-dependent, GHSR1a-independent, or low-potency effects6. Recent data suggest its involvement in controlling food intake, glucose metabolism, gastric movement, cell survival and neuroprotection7. In the context of metabolic and neuroendocrine regulation, these distinct signalling profiles (GHSR-dependent and GHSR independent) carry substantial implications. While deacylated can improve insulin sensitivity and metabolic profile independently of GHSR8, acylated ghrelin acts as a strong orexigenic signal and HPA axis modulator via GHSR1a activation4. Moreover it has been suggested, that while acylated ghrelin promotes neurogenesis in the hippocamp, its deacylated isoform might inhibit these processes9. Given these distinct biological roles, it is crucial to separately measure both fractions of ghrelin, as quantification of total ghrelin only might obscure isoform-specific alterations relevant to depressive pathophysiology.

Gathered preclinical evidence suggests that beyond its metabolic role, ghrelin is also involved in mood regulation. In rodent models, chronic stress increases circulating ghrelin levels, and ghrelin receptor deficiency exacerbates stress-induced depressive-like behaviour. Moreover, central or peripheral ghrelin administration has been shown to exhibit antidepressant-like effects. Animals treated with ghrelin spent more time in open arm and were more active in forced swimming test, indicating anti-depressive and anxiolytic properties of ghrelin10. These findings have led to the hypothesis that stress-induced elevation of ghrelin may represent a compensatory neuroendocrine adaptation aimed at counterbalancing depressive symptoms11.

Clinical studies investigating ghrelin in patients with MDD have yielded inconsistent results. While some authors reported higher circulating ghrelin in individuals with MDD12–17, others demonstrated its reduced18,19 or unchanged20–24 concentrations when compared to non-depressed controls. Some of the authors assessed only depressive symptoms with no formal diagnosis: ghrelin correlated positively25, negatively26 or not correlated at all27. However, interpretation of these findings is difficult due to substantial heterogeneity in methodological approaches (Table 1). Psychometric tools, medication status, metabolic profiles, preanalytical sample handling and method of analysis varied significantly across the studies. As ghrelin is highly unstable ex vivo, the preanalytical handling plays a crucial role to adequately assess its concentrations28. In majority of the studies only total ghrelin concentration was measured14,16,17,19,22–24,26,27,29. Given that acylated and deacylated ghrelin act via different receptors and possibly exert distinct biological effects, it could have had a substantial impact on study outcomes and may partly explain the inconsistent findings across the literature.

Obesity is a major factor influencing ghrelin secretion. Circulating ghrelin is typically reduced in individuals with obesity when compared to healthy controls30. Both MDD and obesity are characterised by dysregulation of shared neuroendocrine pathways such as HPA axis hyperactivation and alterations in metabolic pathways including ghrelin and leptin signalling31,32. Given the strong metabolic influence of obesity on ghrelin, failure to adequately control for this confounder may blur the potential association between ghrelin and MDD. Hence, examining patients with comparable metabolic status may provide clearer insight into the role of ghrelin in depression.

The aim of the present study was to evaluate circulating acylated and deacylated ghrelin concentrations in drug-free patients with MDD comorbid by obesity when compared to non-depressed controls with obesity. By controlling for obesity, a major metabolic confounder, and assessing both forms of ghrelin under standardized conditions, we aimed to clarify whether alterations in ghrelin secretion are associated with MDD. We additionally explored whether the proportional balance between acylated and deacylated ghrelin differs in MDD, which could suggest altered ghrelin activation dynamics.

Methods

The study protocol was approved by Lazarski University Bioethical Committee.

Study population

The study was carried out at Czerniakowski Hospital in Warsaw on 31 patients with major depressive disorder and comorbid obesity and 31 controls. Participants were recruited from the outpatient clinic of metabolic diseases, where they presented for the treatment of obesity (three individuals in control group were recruited from the hospital staff). Participants were recruited using a two-step procedure: first, all participants were screened for depressive symptoms using the Polish adaptation of Patients Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) and agreed to participate in the study. PHQ-9 is a screening tool for depression symptoms validated in Polish population33,34. A score equal to or greater than 12 points indicates high probability of depression. Second, all candidates (regardless of PHQ-9 result) underwent a confirmatory structured psychiatric interview using the MINI-International Neuropsychiatric Interview (M.I.N.I.) to establish or rule out a diagnosis of MDD.

Adult patients with obesity were included in the MDD group if they screened positive on the PHQ-9 and met M.I.N.I. diagnostic criteria for MDD. Controls were recruited from the same outpatient clinic if MDD was excluded by both PHQ-9 score (< 12 points) and M.I.N.I. assessment, and had no active endocrine, metabolic, or psychiatric conditions beyond obesity and were not receiving pharmacological treatment at the time of enrolment. Obesity was defined as body mass index (BMI) greater than 30 kg/m2.

The same exclusion criteria were applied to both MDD and control group: (1) use of antidepressants, antipsychotics, or anxiolytics in the 12 months preceding enrolment, (2) diagnosis of any other psychiatric disorder, (3) history of substance abuse, (4) diagnosis of neurological disease, (5) hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis dysfunction, (6) thyroid diseases, (7) current pharmacological treatment of obesity, (8) bariatric surgery in the past.

Laboratory assay

Sample of patients’ blood were collected after overnight fast between 7.30 and 8.30 am. (patients were instructed to restrain from eating and advised to drink nothing but clear water for at least 10 h prior to procedure). Immediately after, a protease inhibitor (AEBSF, Hydrochloride manufactured by Millipore, catalogue number: D57383) was added, and samples were prepared in pre-cooled centrifuge and then stored at −80 Celsius degrees. Acylated ghrelin was measured using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (Bertin Bioreagent, catalogue number: BR-A05306.96). Deacylated ghrelin was also measured using ELISA kit (Bertin Bioreagent, catalogue number: BR-A05319.96). Each plasma sample was analysed twice, and the mean of two measurements was used for statistical analysis. The mean intra-assay coefficient of variance (CV) was 1.89% (range: 0.79% − 2.24%) for acylated ghrelin and 2.73% (range: 1.03% − 4.42%) for deacylated ghrelin. The inter-assay CV for acylated ghrelin was 6.43%, and 8.59% for deacylated ghrelin.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) (IBM, version 29). Variables were checked for normality using Shapiro-Wilk test, while the homogeneity of variances was verified with Levene’s test. Descriptive statistics are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed data, and as median and interquartile range (IQR) for non-parametric data. Student’s t-test and Mann-Whitney U test were used to evaluate the differences between subjects and controls. Values of acylated and deacylated ghrelin were log-transformed to meet the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance, allowing the use of parametric tests.

Additionally, multivariable general linear models were constructed to examine whether the association between MDD and circulating ghrelin concentrations remained significant after adjustment for potential confounders. Log-transformed acylated and deacylated ghrelin concentrations as well as log-transformed acyl-to-deacyl ghrelin ratio were entered as dependent variables in separate models. Independent variables included MDD diagnosis and sex (entered as fixed factors), while age and BMI were included as covariates based on prior evidence for their influence on ghrelin secretion; BMI was retained to account for residual within-group variability even though groups were matched by obesity status. To assess potential sex-specific effects, an interaction term (MDD*sex) was included in the model. Effect sizes were expressed as partial eta squared (η²p). Estimated marginal means with standard errors were calculated to present adjusted group differences, and pairwise comparisons were adjusted using Bonferroni correction.

Moreover, within MDD group, correlations between PHQ-9 scores and log-transformed ghrelin concentrations were calculated using Pearson’s correlation analysis to examine whether symptoms severity experienced by diagnosed with MDD corresponded to ghrelin concentration.

A significance level of two-tailed α = 0.05 was adopted for all tests. Post-hoc power analyses were conducted using G*Power (version 3.1.9.6) to determine the achieved statistical power for selected effects, with Cohen’s f calculated from partial eta squared values obtained from the general linear models.

Results

A total of 62 participants were included in the study. 31 participants (20 women and 11 men) were enrolled in the MDD group, and 31 (20 women and 11 men) in the control group. The groups did not differ in terms of age, sex and BMI (Table 2). Values of both log-transformed acylated (t = 3.53, p < 0.001) and deacylated (t = 2.54, p = 0.007) ghrelin concentrations were higher among participants with MDD when compared to controls (Table 2).

In multivariable general linear models adjusted for sex, age and BMI, MDD diagnosis remained a significant independent predictor of log-transformed acylated ghrelin concentrations (F(1,56) = 17.17, p < 0.001, η²p = 0.235), representing a large effect size by Cohen’s conventions. Sex was also independently associated with acylated ghrelin (F = 16.52, p < 0.001, η²p = 0.228), whereas age (F = 0.04, p = 0.838, η²p = 0.001) and BMI (F = 0.01, p = 0.921, η²p < 0.001) were not significant predictors. No significant MDD*sex interaction was observed (F = 1.17, p = 0.284), however, given the limited number of male participants (n = 22), the study was insufficiently powered to formally exclude MDD*sex moderation.

Similarly, MDD diagnosis independently predicted log-transformed deacylated ghrelin levels (F(1,56) = 6.93, p = 0.011, η²p = 0.110), corresponding to a medium effect size. Sex was also significantly associated with deacylated ghrelin (F = 4.34, p = 0.042, η²p = 0.072), while age (F = 0.04, p = 0.852, η²p = 0.001), BMI (F = 0.19, p = 0.665, η²p = 0.003), and the MDD*sex interaction (F = 0.42, p = 0.518, η²p = 0.008) were not significant.

For the acyl-to-deacyl ghrelin ratio, MDD diagnosis showed only a trend-level association (F = 3.35, p = 0.072, η²p = 0.057), representing a small-to-medium effect size by Cohen’s conventions. While this finding cannot be interpreted as a statistically significant group difference, the observed effect size suggests a potentially biologically meaningful shift in the relative proportion of active to inactive ghrelin. A post-hoc power analysis indicated that the study achieved only 47.9% power to detect an effect of this magnitude (Cohen’s f = 0.246, α = 0.05, n = 62), which suggests the trend-level result may reflect insufficient statistical power rather than a true absence of effect. Replication in larger samples is therefore warranted. Sex remained a significant predictor (F = 5.06, p = 0.028, η²p = 0.083), while BMI (F = 0.77, p = 0.770, η²p = 0.002) and the MDD*sex interaction (F = 0.26, p = 0.661, η²p = 0.006) were not significant (Table 3).

Within MDD group, neither acylated (Pearson’s r = −0.126, p = 0.244), nor deacylated (Pearson’s r = 0.049, p = 0.792) ghrelin correlated with symptoms severity expressed as PHQ-9 score. Discussion

The present study demonstrated that ghrelin levels (both acylated and deacylated) are higher among individuals with obesity and MDD when compared to non-depressed controls with obesity matched by sex, age and BMI. This is consistent with previously published research reporting higher circulating ghrelin among patients with MDD12–17. The observed association was stronger for the biologically active, acylated form of ghrelin. To the best of our knowledge, it is one of the few studies assessing both acylated and deacylated circulating ghrelin in antidepressant-free patients with MDD and obesity. Our results suggest that higher ghrelin is associated with major depression in patients with comorbid obesity.

In an exploratory analysis, neither acylated nor deacylated ghrelin correlated with symptoms severity expressed as PHQ-9 score in MDD group. This finding may reflect a dissociation between circulating biomarker levels and symptom severity that has been described for other neuroendocrine markers in MDD35. The restricted variance in PHQ-9 scores within a diagnostically homogeneous sample (31 patients with diagnosed MDD) may have attenuated the correlation coefficient. Whether ghrelin tracks symptom severity in individuals with comorbid MDD and obesity therefore remains an open question that cannot be adequately addressed by the present data, and requires dedicated, adequately powered investigation in future studies.

Interpretation of existing literature on the subject is difficult due to substantial heterogeneity in methodological approaches across the studies (Table 1). Both psychometric and analytical methods varied significantly between the studies. Researchers investigated either individuals with diagnosed depression or focused solely on depressive symptoms presence and/or their severity. If diagnosis was made, it was based either on psychiatric evaluation (with accordance to DSM IV or 5) criteria or was additionally supported by structured clinical interview (such as M.I.N.I.). Symptoms severity was assessed either with clinician-rated tools (e.g. HAM-D) or by self-report questionnaires (e.g. BDI, PHQ-9) (Table 1). As depressive symptoms are a component of many psychiatric disorders other than MDD, their measurement without a confirmed diagnosis does not necessarily reflect MDD pathophysiology and restricts the interpretation of the reported results in depression research.

Ghrelin is a relatively small peptide (it consists of 28 amino acids) vulnerable to enzymatic degradation and high temperature. Moreover, it is present in small concentration in the bloodstream, therefore many of its molecules may be lost during preanalytical procedures. Acylated ghrelin is particularly sensitive to these factors making precise measures difficult. To mitigate this issue, it was proposed to handle samples in low temperature (e.g. on ice), with acidification and protease inhibition28,36. ELISA tests were found more sensitive and specific, hence recommended for ghrelin measurements37. Taken together, analytical methods are another major source of heterogeneity in published research on ghrelin in depression.

In this study, we minimized key sources of heterogeneity. MDD diagnosis was confirmed by structured clinical interview (M.I.N.I.), and only antidepressant-free patients were recruited. Patients’ blood was collected after overnight fasting period (at least 10 h), protease inhibitor was added to ensure ghrelin’s stability. Samples were immediately centrifuged in pre-cooled conditions and stored at −80 °C prior to analysis. Importantly, both acylated and deacylated ghrelin were measured and each sample analysed in duplicate, then mean value used for statistics. These methodological strengths enhance the validity of the presented findings.

The effect size was stronger for acylated ghrelin, the biologically active isoform which align with the isoform-specific framework outlined in the Introduction. The acyl-to-deacyl ghrelin ratio did not differ significantly between groups after adjustment for sex, age and BMI (p = 0.072, η²p = 0.057), suggesting that although absolute concentrations of both ghrelin fractions were elevated in MDD, the proportional balance between acylated and deacylated forms remained relatively preserved. However, the post-hoc power analysis indicated that the study was insufficiently powered to detect an effect of this magnitude (observed power 0.479), and replication in larger samples is warranted.

Ghrelin is increasingly recognised as a stress-responsive hormone, with preclinical evidence demonstrating that exposure to various stressors (e.g. chronic unpredictable stress, social defeat, and restraint) elevates circulating ghrelin levels in rodents38,39. The ghrelin signalling system appears to interact bidirectionally with the HPA axis: ghrelin stimulates ACTH release (thereby activating the stress axis), while functional GHSR signalling is required to prevent unrestrained glucocorticoid release. It suggests a modulatory role in the negative feedback regulation of the stress response10,38,39. Importantly, mice lacking endogenous ghrelin or functional GHSR demonstrated exaggerated HPA axis responses to acute stressors and exhibited depressive-like behaviour, which highlight the possible protective and adaptive role of intact ghrelin-dependent signalling under stress conditions40. In patients with MDD, ghrelin has been shown to correlate positively with corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) levels, with CRH fully mediating the relationship between ghrelin and depressive symptom severity as measured by HAM-D scores17. This positions ghrelin as a potential neuroendocrine factor linking chronic stress exposure and the development of depression. In this context, the elevated acylated ghrelin concentrations observed in our MDD group may reflect a compensatory neuroendocrine response to chronic stress-related HPA dysregulation rather than a primary pathogenic mechanism. However, given the cross-sectional design of the present study, causal inferences cannot be drawn, and longitudinal studies are needed to clarify whether increased ghrelin represents an adaptive response, a contributor to MDD development, or both.

Although women exhibited higher baseline ghrelin levels than men, no significant MDD*sex interaction was observed. Sex differences in ghrelin physiology have been described both in healthy and psychiatric populations, with ghrelin being higher among women41,42, a pattern consistent with our findings. However, the interpretation of the absent interaction requires considerable caution: with only 22 male participants across both groups, a post-hoc power analysis indicated that the study achieved only 19.8% power to detect an interaction effect of the observed magnitude (Cohen’s f = 0.143, η²p = 0.020, α = 0.05, n = 62). The absence of a significant MDD*sex interaction should therefore not be interpreted as evidence that the association is equivalent across sexes. Future studies with larger and more balanced sex distributions are needed to adequately examine whether sex moderates MDD-related ghrelin elevation.

Individuals with obesity have lower circulating ghrelin than healthy controls30. Despite the expectation of reduced ghrelin levels in obesity, participants with MDD and obesity exhibited significantly higher circulating ghrelin than controls (also with obesity). The persistence of significantly higher ghrelin concentrations in the MDD group, within a metabolic state physiologically associated with ghrelin suppression, is a noteworthy observation. However, whether this pattern reflects an MDD-specific signal, a consequence of shared neuroendocrine dysregulation underlying MDD and obesity, or an artefact of unmeasured confounders cannot be determined from the present data. Notably, the fact that ghrelin was significantly elevated specifically in participants with MDD, despite both groups having comparable obesity status, suggests that the observed difference is more likely attributable to MDD diagnosis than to adiposity per se.

Another aspect which needs further discussion is influence of antidepressive treatment on ghrelin secretion. It is particularly relevant, as previous research have shown that psychotropic drugs alter ghrelin levels. The selective serotonin-reuptake inhibitors, venlafaxine and mirtazapine were associated with decreased ghrelin concentrations compared to pretreatment levels14. The same normalization effect (higher pre-treatment and lower post-treatment ghrelin) was observed following electroconvulsive therapy12,14. Furthermore, ghrelin concentration decreased in treatment responders while remaining high in non-responders13. These findings highlight the importance of controlling for medication status, as the present study does, in order to obtain clearer results unconfounded by pharmacological effects.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the relatively small sample size, although comparable to many previous studies on ghrelin in depression, limits statistical power and generalizability of the findings. Most notably, the study achieved only 19.8% power to detect the observed MDD*sex interaction effect, and 47.9% power to detect the trend-level acyl-to-deacyl ratio difference, indicating that these analyses were critically underpowered. Second, as all participants had obesity, the results cannot be directly extrapolated to individuals with normal weight or overweight. This was, however, an intentional design choice aimed at controlling for the substantial metabolic influence of obesity on ghrelin secretion. Third, information on menstrual cycle phase was not collected, which is a relevant omission given that 64.5% of the sample were women and sex was a significant independent predictor of ghrelin concentrations. Fourth, the cross-sectional design precludes any causal inference, and it remains unclear whether elevated ghrelin precedes depressive episodes, arises as a consequence of chronic stress exposure, or is mediated by unmeasured confounder. Fifth, although all patients had been antidepressant-free for at least 12 months, this criterion does not exclude a history of prior pharmacological treatment, which may have produced lasting effects on the ghrelin signalling system. Sixth, ghrelin was assessed using single fasting measurements, which may not fully capture the dynamic regulation of ghrelin secretion – Paslakis et al. suggested that functional test using standardised glucose load may be more suitable for assessment of between-group ghrelin concentration differences22. Finally, potential medication use among controls recruited from the outpatient clinic (including antihypertensives, statins, or metformin) was not systematically recorded and cannot be excluded as a source of possible confounding on circulating ghrelin levels.

In conclusion, both acylated and deacylated ghrelin levels are elevated in patients with comorbid MDD and obesity compared to non-depressed controls with comorbid obesity, with stronger effect observed for biologically active, acylated isoform of ghrelin. The acyl-to-deacyl ghrelin ratio showed only a trend-level difference, however, it needs to be replicated in larger sample as the analysis was underpowered. Neither acylated nor deacylated ghrelin fraction correlated with symptom severity within the MDD group, suggesting that the observed elevation may reflect a diagnosis-associated rather than severity-dependent phenomenon, though this interpretation is preliminary given the limited sample and exploratory nature of that analysis. The persistence of ghrelin elevation within patients with obesity, a metabolic state physiologically associated with ghrelin suppression, strengthens the case for MDD-specific ghrelin system dysregulation in this comorbid population. These findings may provide a preliminary basis for considering ghrelin as a candidate biomarker in individuals with comorbid MDD and obesity, though this remains speculative and requires validation in larger, longitudinal studies. Future research should examine whether ghrelin dysregulation precedes, accompanies, or resolves with depressive episodes, and whether it moderates treatment response in this metabolically complex population.
