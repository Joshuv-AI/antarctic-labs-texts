Abstract
We present BayesQ , an uncertainty-guided post-training quantization framework that is the first to optimize quantization under the posterior expected loss . BayesQ fits a lightweight Gaussian posterior over weights (diagonal Laplace by default; optional K-FAC/low-rank), whitens by the posterior covariance, designs codebooks to minimize posterior-expected distortion, and allocates mixed precision via a greedy knapsack that maximizes marginal expected-loss reduction per bit under a global budget. For scalar quantizers, posterior-expected MSE yields closed-form tables; task-aware proxies are handled by short Monte Carlo on a small calibration set. An optional calibration-only distillation aligns the quantized model with the posterior predictive teacher. At matched average bits/weight of 3.0/3.5/4.0, BayesQ improves over strong PTQ baselines on ResNet-50 (ImageNet) and BERT-base (GLUE) e.g. , vs. GPTQ by + 1.5 / + 0.7 / + 0.3 +1.5/+0.7/+0.3 top-1 percentage points on RN50 and + 1.1 / + 0.4 / + 0.2 +1.1/+0.4/+0.2 GLUE points on BERT, while requiring one-time preprocessing comparable to a GPTQ pass. BayesQ reframes low-bit quantization as uncertainty-aware risk minimization in a practical, post-training pipeline.

BayesQ preserves the practicality of post-training quantization. For scalar quantizers, the posterior-expected mean-squared error admits closed-form evaluation in the high-resolution regime, enabling fast per-block tables across candidate bit-widths; when task-aware criteria are preferred, BayesQ can seamlessly switch to Monte Carlo estimates of layer or output distortions on the same calibration data

The framework is model- and hardware-friendly and can be dropped into existing PTQ workflows with minimal engineering effort.

3
Method

This section formalizes BayesQ and explains every quantity used in the equations. We index layers or weight blocks by
b\in\{1,\dots,B\}
. Each block
b
contains a vectorized weight tensor
w_{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{b}}
. A quantizer for block
b
is denoted
Q_{b}(\cdot)
and maps real-valued weights to a finite codebook. We assume access to a small calibration set
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cal}}
used only to fit uncertainty and quantizer parameters; no full retraining is performed (see appendix
B
for more details).

3.1
Posterior approximation

We place a lightweight posterior over
w_{b}
after standard training. The posterior captures epistemic uncertainty and will drive both bit allocation and codebook design.

Diagonal Laplace.
For block
b
, approximate the negative log-posterior with a quadratic expansion around the trained weights
\hat{w}_{b}
:

\displaystyle-\log p(w_{b}\mid\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cal}})

\displaystyle\approx\text{const}+\tfrac{1}{2}(w_{b}-\hat{w}_{b})^{\top}H_{b}(w_{b}-\hat{w}_{b})\,.

(1)

where
H_{b}
is an empirical Hessian or a Fisher approximation. The diagonal Laplace posterior is

\displaystyle p(w_{b}\mid\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cal}})

\displaystyle\approx\mathcal{N}(\mu_{b},\Sigma_{b}),

\displaystyle\mu_{b}

\displaystyle\equiv\hat{w}_{b},

\displaystyle\Sigma_{b}

\displaystyle\equiv\mathrm{diag}\!\big((H_{b}+\lambda I)^{-1}\big)\,.

(2)

Here
\lambda>0
is a damping term (ridge) that stabilizes inversion and encodes a Gaussian prior. Each diagonal entry of
\Sigma_{b}
is the variance of the corresponding weight under the approximate posterior.

Kronecker-factored (K-FAC) / low-rank variants.
For linear layers with matrix weights
W_{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{o_{b}\times i_{b}}
, a structured posterior can be more informative at similar cost. Let
H_{b}\approx A_{b}\otimes G_{b}
be a Kronecker factorization of curvature, where
A_{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{i_{b}\times i_{b}}
approximates input second moments and
G_{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{o_{b}\times o_{b}}
approximates output gradients’ second moments. The resulting Gaussian posterior uses

\displaystyle\Sigma_{b}\approx(H_{b}+\lambda I)^{-1}\approx A_{b}^{-1}\otimes G_{b}^{-1}\,.

(3)

When memory is tight, use a low-rank plus diagonal form
\Sigma_{b}\approx U_{b}U_{b}^{\top}+\mathrm{diag}(v_{b})
with rank
r\!\ll\!d_{b}
, where
U_{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{b}\times r}
and
v_{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{b}}
are fitted by minimizing a curvature-matching loss on
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cal}}
.

In all cases, we write the posterior compactly as

\displaystyle p(w_{b}\mid\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cal}})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}(\mu_{b},\Sigma_{b})\,.

(4)

where
\mu_{b}
is the posterior mean (typically the trained weights) and
\Sigma_{b}
encodes uncertainty (diagonal, Kronecker, or low-rank).

Practical fitting notes:

Diagonal Laplace:
estimate
\operatorname{diag}(H_{b})
with Hutchinson probes (
M\!\in\!\{8,16\}
Rademacher vectors), use Fisher–trace surrogates on
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cal}}
, damp with
\lambda\!\in\![10^{-4},10^{-3}]
, clip variances to
[\!10^{-9},\infty)
before inversion.
K-FAC:
maintain
A_{b},G_{b}
with EMA (decay
0.95
), add Tikhonov to both factors; apply whitening via two matmuls (no explicit
\otimes
).
Low-rank+diag:
pick
r\!\in\!\{32,64\}
and fit by matching Fisher diagonal and a few off-diagonal sketches.
Small calib stability:
when
|\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cal}}|<50
, increase
\lambda
by
\times 5
, clip spectra of
\Sigma_{b}
, and fall back to PCA whitening on top-
k
components.

Complexity:

Diagonal Laplace costs
O(MD)
per sweep across calibration; K-FAC adds
O(i_{b}^{2}{+}o_{b}^{2})
per layer to update factors (amortized across mini-batches).

3.2
Expected quantization loss under a Gaussian posterior

The key design principle is to select quantizers that minimize
posterior expected loss
. For block
b
and a candidate quantizer
Q_{b}
, define

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{b}(Q_{b})\;\equiv\;\mathbb{E}_{w_{b}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{b},\Sigma_{b})}\!\left[\ell\big(Q_{b}(w_{b}),\,w_{b}\big)\right]\,.

(5)

where
\ell(\cdot,\cdot)
is a distortion or task proxy. We consider two choices.

Mean-squared error (closed form).
Let
\ell(Q_{b}(w_{b}),w_{b})=\|Q_{b}(w_{b})-w_{b}\|_{2}^{2}
. For a
vector quantizer
with codebook
\mathcal{C}_{b}=\{c_{bk}\}_{k=1}^{K_{b}}
and Voronoi cells
\{R_{bk}\}_{k=1}^{K_{b}}
that partition
\mathbb{R}^{d_{b}}
,

\displaystyle Q_{b}(w_{b})\;=\;c_{bk}\;\;\text{if}\;\;w_{b}\in R_{bk}\,.

(6)

Then the posterior expected MSE decomposes as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{b}(Q_{b})\;=\;\sum_{k=1}^{K_{b}}\int_{R_{bk}}\!\!\|c_{bk}-w_{b}\|_{2}^{2}\;\mathcal{N}(w_{b};\mu_{b},\Sigma_{b})\,dw_{b}\,.

(7)

For a
scalar
uniform quantizer with step size
\Delta
applied independently to whitened coordinates (see below), the high-resolution approximation yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{b}(Q_{b})\;\approx\;\mathrm{tr}\!\left(S_{b}^{-1}\,\underbrace{\tfrac{\Delta^{2}}{12}I}_{\text{per-coordinate MSE}}\,S_{b}^{-\top}\right).

(8)

where
S_{b}
is a whitening transform satisfying
S_{b}S_{b}^{\top}=\Sigma_{b}
(e.g., Cholesky). Whitening maps
z_{b}=S_{b}^{-1}(w_{b}-\mu_{b})
to an approximately standard normal; quantization is performed in
z_{b}
-space with uniform bins of width
\Delta
, and the expected squared error per coordinate is
\Delta^{2}/12
. Mapping back by
S_{b}
scales the distortion anisotropically along posterior principal axes.

Task-proxy loss via Monte Carlo.
If a downstream proxy is preferable, such as layer output error or KL divergence of model predictions, we estimate

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{b}(Q_{b})

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\ell\!\left(Q_{b}(w_{b}^{(m)}),\,w_{b}^{(m)}\right),

\displaystyle w_{b}^{(m)}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{b},\Sigma_{b})\,.

(9)

Typical choices for
\ell
include
\|f_{\text{layer}}(Q_{b}(w_{b}))-f_{\text{layer}}(w_{b})\|_{2}^{2}
on calibration activations or a small-batch KL between logits produced by the quantized versus mean-weight model. The Monte Carlo average trades bias for flexibility when closed forms are inconvenient.

Choosing
\ell
and making it stable:

We default to posterior-expected MSE for speed and determinism. For task-aware proxies, we cache per-layer activations on
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cal}}
(no labels needed), use
M\!\in\!\{8,16\}
posterior samples, and report the Monte Carlo standard error to ensure the per-block tables are reliable before allocation. When clipping is active, we include Gaussian tail integrals (as in
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{b}
below) to avoid bias.

3.3
Bit allocation as a constrained optimization

We aim to assign a bit-width
m_{b}\in\mathcal{M}
(e.g.,
\{2,3,4,8\}
) to each block under a global storage budget. Let
C_{b}(m_{b})
be the storage cost (in bits) to encode block
b
at
m_{b}
bits on average, including per-block scales or codebooks. The allocation problem is

\min_{\{m_{b}\in\mathcal{M}\}}\quad\sum_{b=1}^{B}\mathcal{L}_{b}\!\big(Q_{b}^{(m_{b})}\big)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\sum_{b=1}^{B}C_{b}(m_{b})\leq B_{\mathrm{tot}},

(10)

where
Q_{b}^{(m)}
denotes the best quantizer for block
b
at bit-width
m
under the posterior, and
B_{\mathrm{tot}}
is the global budget.

A practical and effective solver is a greedy knapsack on
marginal expected-loss reduction per extra bit
. For each block
b
and neighboring bit-widths
m\rightarrow m+1
, define the marginal gain

\displaystyle\Delta_{b}(m)

\displaystyle\equiv\mathcal{L}_{b}\!\big(Q_{b}^{(m)}\big)-\mathcal{L}_{b}\!\big(Q_{b}^{(m+1)}\big),

\displaystyle\gamma_{b}(m)

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{\Delta_{b}(m)}{C_{b}(m+1)-C_{b}(m)}\,.

(11)

The algorithm initializes at the lowest bit-widths and iteratively upgrades the single
(b,m)
with the largest
\gamma_{b}(m)
until the budget is exhausted. This favors blocks and increments that yield the greatest posterior-expected loss drop per bit. When two candidates tie, a hardware-aware tie-breaker can prefer upgrades that align with kernel packing constraints.

Accounting and data structures:

We compute
C_{b}(m)
as full storage (payload
+
scales/indices/codebooks), and track the global average bits
\bar{m}=(\sum_{b}S_{b})/(\sum_{b}d_{b}\cdot 32)\times 32
with tolerance
\pm 0.02
bits/weight. A max-heap over all feasible upgrades keyed by
\gamma_{b}(m)
yields
O(\log(B|\mathcal{M}|))
per iteration; typically
<3B
upgrades suffice due to diminishing returns.

Tie-breakers (hardware-aware):

On ties in
\gamma_{b}(m)
, we prefer (1) larger absolute
\Delta_{b}(m)
, (2) higher posterior saliency
\mathrm{tr}(\Sigma_{b}^{-1})/d_{b}
, (3) upgrades aligned to vector widths/tile sizes to avoid kernel slow paths.

3.4
Posterior-aware codebook design

Given a target bit-width for block
b
, design a codebook and decision regions that minimize posterior expected distortion. Working in whitened coordinates
z_{b}=S_{b}^{-1}(w_{b}-\mu_{b})
with
z_{b}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
simplifies the objective and yields isotropic density.

Weighted Lloyd–Max in whitened space.
Let
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{b}=\{\tilde{c}_{bk}\}_{k=1}^{K_{b}}
be codepoints in
z_{b}
-space with decision regions
\{\tilde{R}_{bk}\}
. Minimize

\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{b}=\sum_{k=1}^{K_{b}}\;\int_{\tilde{R}_{bk}}\!\|\tilde{c}_{bk}-z_{b}\|_{2}^{2}\;\mathcal{N}(z_{b};0,I)\,dz_{b}.

(12)

The classical Lloyd updates apply with Gaussian weights:

\displaystyle\tilde{R}_{bk}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\big\{z_{b}:\|\tilde{c}_{bk}-z_{b}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\|\tilde{c}_{bj}-z_{b}\|_{2}^{2},\;\forall j\big\}

\displaystyle\tilde{c}_{bk}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\frac{\int_{\tilde{R}_{bk}}z_{b}\,\mathcal{N}(z_{b};0,I)\,dz_{b}}{\int_{\tilde{R}_{bk}}\mathcal{N}(z_{b};0,I)\,dz_{b}}\,.

(13)

After convergence, map codepoints back to weight space by
c_{bk}=\mu_{b}+S_{b}\tilde{c}_{bk}
and quantize with corresponding pre-images
R_{bk}=\{w_{b}:S_{b}^{-1}(w_{b}-\mu_{b})\in\tilde{R}_{bk}\}
.

Closed-form step for uniform scalar quantizers.
For scalar quantization of whitened coordinates with mid-rise uniform bins, the high-resolution optimal step solves a variance–distortion balance. If
m
bits yield
K=2^{m}
levels and we restrict to a fixed dynamic range
[-\alpha,\alpha]
, the codebook is
\tilde{c}_{k}=-\alpha+(k+\tfrac{1}{2})\Delta
with
\Delta=2\alpha/K
, and the expected MSE is

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{b}(\Delta,\alpha)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\int_{-\alpha+k\Delta}^{-\alpha+(k+1)\Delta}(z-\tilde{c}_{k})^{2}\,\phi(z)\,dz

\displaystyle\quad+2\int_{\alpha}^{\infty}(z-\alpha)^{2}\,\phi(z)\,dz\,.

(14)

where
\phi(z)
is the standard normal density and the tail term accounts for clipping. Minimizing
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{b}
over
\alpha
yields an optimal range;
\Delta
follows from
K
. In practice, a short 1D line search over
\alpha
per block is sufficient; the mapping back to weight space proceeds as above.

Practical codebook choices:

Uniform (analytic):
golden-section search over
\alpha\in[1.5,4.5]
(standard-normal units),
\leq\!20
evaluations.
Posterior-weighted Lloyd:

kmeans++
init in whitened space, cap 15 iterations or stop at relative change
<10^{-4}
; for small-vector VQ (group size
g\in\{2,4,8\}
), quantize tuples to capture local correlations.
Outliers:
detect heavy tails by kurtosis
>8
and either allocate extra bits or split the block.

3.5
Posterior distillation (optional, calibration-only)

After quantization, a short calibration-only distillation aligns the quantized model with the posterior predictive of the full-precision model. Let
f(\cdot;w)
denote network outputs (logits). Define a teacher distribution by averaging predictions over
M
posterior samples:

\displaystyle p_{T}(y\mid x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{f(x;w^{(m)})}{\tau}\right),

\displaystyle w^{(m)}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\Sigma)\,.

(15)

with temperature
\tau\!\geq\!1
. Let
p_{Q}(y\mid x)
be the student predictions from the quantized weights. Optimize

\min_{\theta_{Q}}\;\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cal}}|}\sum_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cal}}}\mathrm{KL}\!\left(p_{T}(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,p_{Q}(\cdot\mid x)\right),

(16)

holding quantized weights fixed and adjusting only inexpensive affine scales, per-channel factors, or small adapter parameters. This requires a few hundred to a few thousand minibatches and typically reduces residual calibration error without deviating from post-training quantization.

Schedule and when to use:

We form
p_{T}
with
M\!=\!8
posterior samples and
\tau\!=\!2
, then tune only affine scales/adapters for 500 steps (AdamW
10^{-4}
, batch 128). This is most helpful at tight budgets (
\bar{m}\!\leq\!3.5
); at
\bar{m}\!\geq\!4.0
gains are marginal, so we skip to save time.

End-to-end summary:

(1) Fit a lightweight Gaussian posterior (diagonal/K-FAC/low-rank). (2) Whiten and design per-block quantizers to minimize posterior-expected loss (uniform analytic or posterior-weighted Lloyd). (3) Allocate bits by greedy marginal loss reduction per stored bit with exact storage accounting. (4) Optionally distill to the posterior predictive teacher on the calibration set.

5
Discussion & Limitations

BayesQ hinges on the quality of the post hoc posterior. When the Laplace approximation poorly captures local geometry (e.g., highly non-Gaussian or multi-modal landscapes, heavy outlier channels, or degenerate curvature under small calibration sets), expected-loss tables can be miscalibrated and the allocator may over- or under-invest bits in sensitive blocks. Structured posteriors (K-FAC/low-rank) help but increase engineering and memory cost; diagonal Laplace is often robust in practice, yet its independence assumption can miss strong within-block correlations in very wide layers. For extremely large models, choosing the right
block granularity
is nontrivial: finer blocks expose more heterogeneity and gains, but expand the search space and metadata; coarser blocks are simpler but may blur important sensitivity variation across channels/heads. Our greedy solver scales well, but the quality of its choices remains tied to the fidelity of per-block expected-loss estimates.

Kernel considerations impose practical constraints. Not all deployment stacks support arbitrary bit mixes or nonuniform codebooks; hardware packing, vector width, and memory alignment can restrict feasible assignments. We mitigate this with packing-aware tie-breakers and by compiling codebooks into per-block scales at export time, but some backends may still prefer uniform or power-of-two steps. Posterior estimation is a one-time cost that is comparable to a GPTQ pass in our settings; on very deep transformers, Hutchinson probe counts and K-FAC factor maintenance can dominate the PTQ budget unless amortized across batches/devices. Finally, BayesQ currently targets weight-only quantization with FP16 activations and uses short calibration-only distillation; deeper finetuning could further improve accuracy but would depart from the strictly post-training regime.

Ethics pointer.

BayesQ is a compression method and does not introduce new data collection, labeling, or inference objectives beyond those of the underlying models. Nevertheless, any deployment that changes numerical precision can shift error profiles, especially on minority or rare subpopulations. We therefore recommend task-specific fairness, robustness, and calibration checks as part of model validation. A detailed discussion of broader societal impacts and recommended guardrails appears in the
Impact Statement
.

6
Conclusion and Future Work

We presented BayesQ, a post-training framework that reframes quantization as minimization of posterior-expected loss and unifies mixed-precision allocation with posterior-aware codebook design. Using lightweight Gaussian posteriors and closed-form (or Monte Carlo) expected-loss tables, BayesQ provides a practical, hardware-conscious pipeline that improves the accuracy–bit frontier on both vision and language models under tight storage budgets. Ablations attribute the gains to posterior whitening, uncertainty-weighted codebooks, and a greedy allocator that exploits diminishing returns.

Several directions are promising. First,
joint weight–activation
BayesQ could extend posterior guidance to activation ranges and outlier paths, enabling end-to-end INT inference while preserving accuracy. Second,
posterior amortization
—learning small adapters to predict block-level uncertainty from activations—may reduce or eliminate Hutchinson probes at scale. Third,
theory and guarantees
: establishing submodularity-like properties or bounds on the greedy allocator’s suboptimality would strengthen the framework’s foundations. Fourth,
hardware co-design
: integrating kernel-aware objectives (vector width, cache lines, SRAM tiling) and learning codebooks constrained to fast paths (e.g., power-of-two, grouped scales) can further reduce latency. Fifth,
robustness and safety
: combining posterior-guided quantization with risk-sensitive objectives (e.g., CVaR losses) and uncertainty calibration may improve tail behavior on OOD shifts. Finally, extending BayesQ to very large language models with layer-wise posterior sharing, low-rank structure, and online calibration promises practical low-bit deployment at trillion-parameter scale.

Impact Statement

BayesQ lowers the memory and energy cost of deploying neural networks, enabling accurate on-device inference in resource-constrained settings and reducing serving emissions. Because compression can shift error profiles, practitioners should audit subgroup performance and use calibration data representative of the intended deployment. Lower cost and wider availability have dual-use risks (e.g., surveillance); deployments should follow domain regulations, obtain consent where appropriate, and include human oversight. BayesQ inherits any biases of the underlying model and data; users should pair it with model cards, safety evaluations, and clear disclosure of quantization settings.

A.1
Quantization Primers

A.1.1
Quantization as codebook design and assignment

Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a weight vector (or activation slice). A quantizer is
(\mathcal{C},Q)
with codebook

\mathcal{C}=\{c_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and an assignment
Q:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathcal{C}
that maps
x\mapsto Q(x)\in\mathcal{C}
.
The representational budget is a
bit-width

m
, with
K=2^{m}
scalar levels (for vector codebooks
K
indexes
m
-bit entries).
Objectives minimize a distortion proxy
\mathbb{E}[\ell(Q(x),x)]
(e.g., MSE, output error, task KL).

Scalar vs. vector quantization:

Scalar
quantization treats each coordinate independently;
vector
quantization (VQ) assigns a whole block jointly, capturing correlations at the cost of larger codebooks and indices.
Blockwise scalar (per-channel/per-head or fixed-size blocks) is a common middle ground.

A.1.2
Uniform scalar quantization

For zero-centered weights (symmetric uniform):

q\;=\;\mathrm{clip}\!\left(\mathrm{round}\!\Big(\frac{x}{\Delta}\Big),\,-2^{m-1},\,2^{m-1}-1\right),\qquad\hat{x}\;=\;\Delta\,q.

(17)

For nonnegative/shifted activations (asymmetric with zero-point
z
and offset
\beta
):

q\;=\;\mathrm{clip}\!\left(\mathrm{round}\!\Big(\frac{x-\beta}{\Delta}\Big)+z,\,0,\,2^{m}-1\right),\qquad\hat{x}\;=\;\Delta\,(q-z)+\beta.

(18)

A.1.3
Non-uniform scalar quantization

Non-uniform levels place more codepoints where density/sensitivity is high (e.g.,
\mu
-law, Lloyd–Max). Levels can be exported to kernel-friendly forms (scales/LUTs) if needed.

A.1.4
Vector quantization (VQ) and product quantization (PQ)

VQ learns codewords in
\mathbb{R}^{d_{b}}
and assigns by nearest neighbor; PQ splits a block into
G
subspaces with
K_{g}
codewords each for a tuple index. Whitening often improves both.

A.1.5
Mixed-precision and storage accounting

Let
m_{b}
be the bit-width of block
b
with storage
C_{b}(m_{b})
(including metadata). The average weight bits are

\bar{m}\;=\;\frac{\sum_{b}C_{b}(m_{b})}{\sum_{b}C_{b}(32)}.

(19)

A.1.6
PTQ versus QAT

PTQ uses a small calibration set and does not update FP weights; QAT inserts quantizers and fine-tunes end-to-end (often with STE). Hybrids tune only scales/adapters briefly.

A.1.7
Calibration data and objectives

Small calibration favors closed-form or stable proxies (MSE in whitened coordinates); larger sets enable task-aware proxies (logit KL) or posterior Monte Carlo estimates.

A.1.8
Kernel & deployment considerations

Packing (int2/4/8), scale granularity (per-tensor/channel/group), codebook export, and reducing format fragmentation per kernel/tile are key for throughput.

A.2
Bayesian Approximations

BayesQ relies on post-hoc Gaussian approximations,

p(w_{b}\mid\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cal}})\;=\;\mathcal{N}(\mu_{b},\Sigma_{b}),

(20)

used for whitening and expected-loss evaluation. We summarize diagonal Laplace, K-FAC, and low-rank+diagonal forms.

A.2.1
Diagonal Laplace (default)

Quadratic expansion at the trained weights
\hat{w}_{b}
:

-\log p(w_{b}\mid\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cal}})\;\approx\;\mathrm{const}\;+\;\tfrac{1}{2}(w_{b}-\hat{w}_{b})^{\top}H_{b}(w_{b}-\hat{w}_{b}),

(21)

with damped diagonal covariance

p(w_{b}\mid\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cal}})\;\approx\;\mathcal{N}(\mu_{b},\Sigma_{b}),\quad\mu_{b}\equiv\hat{w}_{b},\quad\Sigma_{b}\equiv\mathrm{diag}\!\big((H_{b}+\lambda I)^{-1}\big).

(22)

Cost
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(Md_{b})
in time and
\mathcal{O}(d_{b})
memory using
M
Hutchinson probes.

A.2.2
Kronecker-factored (K-FAC) Gaussian

For
W_{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{o_{b}\times i_{b}}
with
H_{b}\approx A_{b}\otimes G_{b}
, the covariance is

\Sigma_{b}\;\approx\;(H_{b}+\lambda I)^{-1}\;\approx\;A_{b}^{-1}\otimes G_{b}^{-1}.

(23)

Storage/time
\mathcal{O}(i_{b}^{2}+o_{b}^{2})
; improves anisotropy on medium-sized blocks.

A.2.3
Low-rank + diagonal

Capture dominant directions with rank-
r
:

\Sigma_{b}\;\approx\;U_{b}U_{b}^{\top}\;+\;\mathrm{diag}(v_{b}),\qquad U_{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{b}\times r},\;r\ll d_{b}.

(24)

Footprint
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(r\,d_{b})
time and
\mathcal{O}(r\,d_{b}+d_{b})
memory; a scalable middle ground.

A.2.4
Practical estimation details

Use empirical Fisher or Gauss–Newton; damping
\lambda\in[10^{-4},10^{-2}]
; probes
M\in[8,32]
.
Whiten with
\Sigma_{b}=S_{b}S_{b}^{\top}
and quantize in
z_{b}=S_{b}^{-1}(w_{b}-\mu_{b})
space for stable uniform codebooks.

A.2.5
Choosing the posterior

Default: diagonal Laplace (cheap, robust).
Sensitive medium blocks: K-FAC (if factors fit).
Very wide layers or tight memory: low-rank+diag with
r\in\{16,32,64\}
.

Takeaway.
Diagonal Laplace is an inexpensive default; K-FAC helps at low bits on medium matrices; low-rank+diag scales to wide layers. All enable posterior whitening and expected-loss evaluation—the two pillars behind BayesQ’s accuracy at 3–4 bits.

B.1
Posterior-Expected MSE: Derivation and Whitening

Setup:

Fix a block
b
with vectorized weights
w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a Gaussian posterior

p(w\mid\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cal}})\;=\;\mathcal{N}(\mu,\Sigma),

(25)

obtained via a Laplace/K-FAC/low-rank approximation. Let
Q
denote a (possibly vector) quantizer with reconstruction
\hat{w}=Q(w)
and consider the mean-squared distortion

\mathcal{L}(Q)\;\equiv\;\mathbb{E}_{w\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\Sigma)}\!\left[\lVert Q(w)-w\rVert_{2}^{2}\right].

(26)

Whitening transform:

Let
\Sigma=SS^{\top}
be any symmetric factorization (e.g., Cholesky). Define

z\;=\;S^{-1}(w-\mu),\qquad z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I).

(27)

If we quantize in
z
-space by
\tilde{Q}
and map codepoints back via
\hat{w}=\mu+S\,\tilde{Q}(z)
, then

\lVert\hat{w}-w\rVert_{2}^{2}\;=\;\lVert S(\tilde{Q}(z)-z)\rVert_{2}^{2}\;=\;(\tilde{Q}(z)-z)^{\top}(S^{\top}S)\,(\tilde{Q}(z)-z).

(28)

Taking expectations over
z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
yields

\mathcal{L}(\tilde{Q})\;=\;\mathbb{E}\!\left[\lVert S(\tilde{Q}(z)-z)\rVert_{2}^{2}\right]\;=\;\mathrm{tr}\!\left(\Sigma\,\mathbb{E}\!\big[(\tilde{Q}(z)-z)(\tilde{Q}(z)-z)^{\top}\big]\right).

(29)

Thus, minimizing
\mathcal{L}
reduces to minimizing the
isotropic
error covariance of
(\tilde{Q}(z)-z)
under
z\!\sim\!\mathcal{N}(0,I)
; anisotropy re-enters only via the outer factor
\Sigma
.

High-resolution scalar approximation (uniform bins):

Consider independent scalar quantization in
z
-space with mid-rise uniform step
\Delta
and dynamic range
[-\alpha,\alpha]
.
Inside the range, the per-coordinate squared error satisfies the well-known high-resolution approximation:

\mathbb{E}\!\left[(\tilde{q}-z)^{2}\,\middle|\,|z|\leq\alpha\right]\;\approx\;\frac{\Delta^{2}}{12}.

(30)

Clipping contributes a tail term:

E_{\text{tail}}(\alpha)\;=\;2\int_{\alpha}^{\infty}(z-\alpha)^{2}\,\phi(z)\,dz,

(31)

where
\phi
is the standard normal density. Combining both gives the per-coordinate expected MSE

\varepsilon(\Delta,\alpha)\;\approx\;\underbrace{\frac{\Delta^{2}}{12}\,\Pr(|z|\leq\alpha)}_{\text{granular noise}}\;+\;\underbrace{E_{\text{tail}}(\alpha)}_{\text{clipping noise}},\quad\Delta\;=\;\frac{2\alpha}{K},\quad K=2^{m}.

(32)

Assuming independent coordinates in
z
(valid under whitening) and applying (
29
):

\mathcal{L}(\tilde{Q})\;\approx\;\varepsilon(\Delta,\alpha)\;\mathrm{tr}(\Sigma)\qquad\text{(scalar, i.i.d.\ $z$-coordinates)}.

(33)

When the range is wide enough (so
\Pr(|z|\leq\alpha)\approx 1
), the classical simplification
\varepsilon\approx\Delta^{2}/12
applies and

\mathcal{L}(\tilde{Q})\;\approx\;\frac{\Delta^{2}}{12}\,\mathrm{tr}(\Sigma).

(34)

Block-diagonal/anisotropic refinements:

If
\tilde{Q}
uses per-dimension steps
\Delta_{i}
(e.g., per-channel scales), then

\mathbb{E}\!\big[(\tilde{Q}(z)-z)(\tilde{Q}(z)-z)^{\top}\big]\;\approx\;\mathrm{diag}\!\left(\frac{\Delta_{1}^{2}}{12},\ldots,\frac{\Delta_{d}^{2}}{12}\right),

(35)

and

\mathcal{L}(\tilde{Q})\;\approx\;\frac{1}{12}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\Delta_{i}^{2}\,\Sigma_{ii}.

(36)

This recovers the common per-channel scaling rule as a special case of (
29
).

Justification for whitening:

Whitening diagonalizes the posterior covariance in the metric that determines expected distortion. If we quantize directly in
w
-space with a uniform scalar step
\Delta
across coordinates, the expected distortion becomes

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\lVert Q(w)-w\rVert_{2}^{2}\right]\;\approx\;\frac{\Delta^{2}}{12}\,d,

(37)

which
ignores
anisotropy in
\Sigma
and misallocates error along highly variable directions. In contrast, quantizing in
z
-space and mapping back by
S
yields (
34
), where the posterior geometry appears as
\mathrm{tr}(\Sigma)
or its anisotropic refinement.

Choosing
\alpha
(optimal range):

The optimal range balances granular and clipping noise by minimizing (
32
) w.r.t.
\alpha
. A 1D line search per block efficiently finds
\alpha^{\star}
;
\Delta^{\star}
follows from
K
.

Proposition B1 (High-resolution posterior-expected MSE).

Under whitening
z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
and independent mid-rise scalar quantization with range
[-\alpha,\alpha]
and step
\Delta=2\alpha/2^{m}
, the posterior-expected distortion satisfies

\mathcal{L}(\tilde{Q})\;\approx\;\mathrm{tr}\!\left(\Sigma\,\frac{\Delta^{2}}{12}I\right)\;+\;\mathrm{tr}\!\left(\Sigma\,\Xi(\alpha)\right),

(38)

where
\Xi(\alpha)
is a diagonal matrix collecting per-coordinate clipping contributions. For
\alpha
large enough, the second term vanishes and (
34
) holds.

Proof.
Directly from (
29
)–(
32
) with independence in
z
; the clipping term aggregates diagonally.
\square

B.2
Weighted Lloyd–Max in Whitened Space

We design vector/scalar codebooks in
z
-space where
z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
and map codepoints back by
S
.

Objective:

Let
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}=\{\tilde{c}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
be codepoints and
\{\tilde{R}_{k}\}
their regions. The Gaussian-weighted MSE objective is

\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\tilde{\mathcal{C}},\{\tilde{R}_{k}\})\;=\;\sum_{k=1}^{K}\int_{\tilde{R}_{k}}\lVert z-\tilde{c}_{k}\rVert_{2}^{2}\,\phi(z)\,dz,\qquad\phi(z)\;=\;(2\pi)^{-d/2}\exp(-\lVert z\rVert^{2}/2).

(39)

Voronoi optimality (assignment step):

For fixed codepoints, the optimal regions are Gaussian-weighted Voronoi cells:

\tilde{R}_{k}\;=\;\Big\{z:\;\lVert z-\tilde{c}_{k}\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\lVert z-\tilde{c}_{j}\rVert_{2}^{2},\;\forall j\Big\}.

(40)

Centroid optimality (update step):

For fixed regions, differentiating (
39
) w.r.t.
\tilde{c}_{k}
gives the weighted centroid condition:

\tilde{c}_{k}\;=\;\frac{\int_{\tilde{R}_{k}}z\,\phi(z)\,dz}{\int_{\tilde{R}_{k}}\phi(z)\,dz}.

(41)

Algorithm (weighted Lloyd iterations):

Initialize
\{\tilde{c}_{k}\}
(e.g.,
K
-means++ on calibration samples in
z
-space). Iterate:

\text{Assign: }\tilde{R}_{k}\leftarrow\text{Voronoi}(\{\tilde{c}_{k}\})\quad\text{via \eqref{eq:wlm-assign}},\qquad\text{Update: }\tilde{c}_{k}\leftarrow\text{centroid}(\tilde{R}_{k})\quad\text{via \eqref{eq:wlm-centroid}}.

(42)

Stop when the relative objective decrease is small:

\frac{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}^{(t-1)}-\tilde{\mathcal{L}}^{(t)}}{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}^{(t-1)}}\;<\;\epsilon\quad\text{or}\quad\max_{k}\lVert\tilde{c}_{k}^{(t)}-\tilde{c}_{k}^{(t-1)}\rVert_{2}\;<\;\delta,

(43)

with typical
\epsilon\in[10^{-4},10^{-3}]
,
\delta\in[10^{-4},10^{-3}]
.

Proposition B2 (Monotone decrease and convergence).

Each assign/update cycle does not increase the objective (
39
); the sequence
\{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}^{(t)}\}
is nonincreasing and converges to a stationary point (a local minimum or saddle) of (
39
).

Proof sketch.
With fixed codepoints, (
40
) minimizes the integrand pointwise, hence does not increase
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}
. With fixed regions, (
41
) solves a strictly convex quadratic in
\tilde{c}_{k}
and thus minimizes
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}
. Alternating these steps yields a block-coordinate descent with monotone decrease and limit points that satisfy the KKT conditions (Lloyd’s theorem adapted to Gaussian weights).
\square

Mapping back to weight space:

After convergence,
c_{k}=\mu+S\tilde{c}_{k}
and
R_{k}=\{w:S^{-1}(w-\mu)\in\tilde{R}_{k}\}
; quantize by
Q(w)=c_{k}
for
w\in R_{k}
.

Practical stopping and stability:

Use mini-batch Monte Carlo to estimate (
39
) on calibration samples, exponential moving averages for centroids, and early stopping when
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}
plateaus for
T
iterations (e.g.,
T\!=\!5
).

B.3
Task-Proxy Losses: MC Estimators, Variance, and Sample Size

We often prefer task-aware proxies, e.g., layer-output error or logit KL. Let
f(\cdot;w)
denote the network (or a layer) and
\mathcal{X}
calibration inputs.

B.3.1
Layer-output squared error

For a block-local proxy with activations
a(x)
,

\ell_{\text{layer}}(Q;w)\;=\;\lVert f_{\text{layer}}(a;Q(w))-f_{\text{layer}}(a;w)\rVert_{2}^{2}.

(44)

The posterior-expected proxy is

\mathcal{L}_{\text{layer}}(Q)\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{w\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\Sigma)}\,\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{X}}\left[\ell_{\text{layer}}(Q;w)\right].

(45)

An unbiased MC estimator with
M
weight samples and
N
inputs is

\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{layer}}(Q)\;=\;\frac{1}{MN}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\ell_{\text{layer}}\!\left(Q;w^{(m)}\right),\quad w^{(m)}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\Sigma),\;x_{n}\sim\mathcal{X}.

(46)

B.3.2
Logit KL (teacher–student)

Define the teacher as the posterior predictive:

p_{T}(y\mid x)\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{w\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\Sigma)}\left[\mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{f(x;w)}{\tau}\right)\right].

(47)

The student is the quantized model
p_{Q}(y\mid x)=\mathrm{softmax}(f(x;Q(\mu))/\tau)
. The KL proxy is

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q)\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{X}}\left[\mathrm{KL}\!\left(p_{T}(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,p_{Q}(\cdot\mid x)\right)\right].

(48)

A plug-in MC estimator approximates
p_{T}
by averaging
M
softmaxes:

\widehat{p}_{T}(y\mid x)\;=\;\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{f(x;w^{(m)})}{\tau}\right),\qquad\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q)\;=\;\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathrm{KL}\!\left(\widehat{p}_{T}(\cdot\mid x_{n})\,\|\,p_{Q}(\cdot\mid x_{n})\right).

(49)

This is biased (Jensen) but consistent as
M\to\infty
; in practice, small
M
(e.g.,
M\in[4,16]
) suffices.

B.3.3
Variance control and sample complexity

Let
Z
denote any bounded proxy with range
[0,B]
. Hoeffding’s inequality gives, for i.i.d. samples,

\Pr\!\left(\big|\widehat{\mathbb{E}}[Z]-\mathbb{E}[Z]\big|\geq\epsilon\right)\;\leq\;2\exp\!\left(-\frac{2MN\epsilon^{2}}{B^{2}}\right),

(50)

so
MN=\mathcal{O}(B^{2}\epsilon^{-2}\log(1/\delta))
suffices for error
\epsilon
at confidence
1-\delta
. For sub-Gaussian
Z
with proxy variance proxy
\sigma^{2}
, Bernstein-style bounds yield

MN\;=\;\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}\log\frac{1}{\delta}\;+\;\frac{B}{\epsilon}\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right).

(51)

Control variates:

Use common random numbers (same
w^{(m)}
across candidate
Q
), antithetic sampling (
\varepsilon
and
-\varepsilon
in reparameterization
w=\mu+L\varepsilon
), and subtract a linearization baseline of
f
at
\mu
to reduce variance.

Bias–variance trade-off:

For
\mathrm{KL}
, the bias from
\widehat{p}_{T}
decreases as
\mathcal{O}(1/M)
under smoothness; empirical results show
M\in[4,16]
balances cost and variance on small calibration sets.

B.4
Storage Budget Accounting and “Average Bits”

Let the model be partitioned into blocks
b=1,\ldots,B
with
N_{b}
weights each. A quantization scheme specifies for every
b
:

•

a weight encoding (e.g.,
m_{b}
-bit scalar per weight, or VQ index),

•

per-block/per-channel scales, zero-points, or whitening params,

•

optional codebooks (VQ/PQ), and any auxiliary metadata.

We measure the exact storage in
bits
, then normalize to “average weight bits” against a 32-bit baseline.

B.4.1
Scalar quantization (per-tensor / per-channel)

For block
b
with
N_{b}
weights and
m_{b}
bits per weight:

C_{b}^{\text{weights}}(m_{b})\;=\;N_{b}\,m_{b}.

(52)

Metadata depends on scale granularity:

\displaystyle C_{b}^{\text{meta}}(\text{per-tensor})

\displaystyle=b_{\text{scale}}\;+\;b_{\text{zp}},

(53)

\displaystyle C_{b}^{\text{meta}}(\text{per-channel})

\displaystyle=C_{b}^{\text{meta}}(\text{per-tensor})\;+\;C_{\text{ch}}\cdot(b_{\text{scale}}+b_{\text{zp}}),

(54)

where
b_{\text{scale}},b_{\text{zp}}
are the bit-costs for storing one scale and zero-point (often 16–32 bits each), and
C_{\text{ch}}
is the channel count. If whitening is exported (rare at inference), add its parameter cost (typically folded into scales).

Total bits for block
b
:

C_{b}(m_{b})\;=\;C_{b}^{\text{weights}}(m_{b})\;+\;C_{b}^{\text{meta}}.

(55)

B.4.2
Vector / product quantization

If
b
uses VQ with codebook size
K_{b}
and subvector size
d_{\text{sub}}
:

Indices:

\displaystyle C_{b}^{\text{idx}}\;=\;N_{b}\,\log_{2}K_{b},

(56)

Codebook:

\displaystyle C_{b}^{\text{cb}}\;=\;K_{b}\,d_{\text{sub}}\,b_{\text{code}},

(57)

Scales/etc.:

\displaystyle C_{b}^{\text{meta}}\;=\;\text{(as needed, e.g., per-subvector scales)}.

(58)

Hence,

C_{b}\;=\;C_{b}^{\text{idx}}+C_{b}^{\text{cb}}+C_{b}^{\text{meta}}.

(59)

For PQ with
G
groups and
(K_{g},d_{g})
per group,

C_{b}^{\text{idx}}\;=\;\sum_{g=1}^{G}N_{b,g}\log_{2}K_{g},\qquad C_{b}^{\text{cb}}\;=\;\sum_{g=1}^{G}K_{g}d_{g}b_{\text{code}}.

(60)

Codebooks can be amortized across repeated layers if shared, reducing
C^{\text{cb}}
.

B.4.3
Mapping to “average bits”

Let
C_{\text{full}}=\sum_{b}32N_{b}
be the 32-bit baseline. The exact storage of a configuration is

C_{\text{tot}}\;=\;\sum_{b=1}^{B}C_{b}(m_{b}).

(61)

We define the “average weight bits” as

\bar{m}\;\equiv\;\frac{C_{\text{tot}}}{\sum_{b}N_{b}}\;=\;\frac{\sum_{b}C_{b}(m_{b})}{\sum_{b}N_{b}}\;=\;\frac{C_{\text{tot}}}{C_{\text{full}}}\times 32,

(62)

where (
62
)
includes all
indices, codebooks, scales, and metadata in
C_{b}(m_{b})
. This ensures fair comparisons at matched
\bar{m}
.

B.4.4
Hardware packing and feasible sets

Practical kernels often require
m_{b}\in\{2,3,4,8\}
and prefer uniform scale granularities. When enforcing hardware packing, the allocator restricts
m_{b}
to a feasible set
\mathcal{M}_{b}
and adds any packing overhead into
C_{b}(m_{b})
. Tie-breakers (e.g., prefer upgrades that align to vector width) change allocation order without changing (
62
).

Entropy coding (optional):

If indices or quantized values are entropy-coded, replace raw counts with expected code lengths
\mathbb{E}[-\log_{2}p(q)]
under the empirical distribution
p(q)
. We use raw (fixed-length) counts in the main paper for portability.

Summary.
The posterior-expected MSE decomposes cleanly under whitening, yielding closed-form/high-resolution expressions that expose
\Sigma
. Weighted Lloyd–Max in whitened space provides principled non-uniform codebooks with monotone convergence. Task proxies admit unbiased/consistent MC estimators with standard variance controls. Our storage accounting
C_{b}(m_{b})
explicitly includes indices, codebooks, and metadata; the average-bit measure (
62
) guarantees apples-to-apples comparisons across methods.

Appendix C
Allocator Theory & Algorithms

We analyze the greedy per-bit allocator that upgrades block precisions to maximize posterior-expected loss reduction per unit storage. We formalize conditions under which greedy is near-optimal, compare with a toy dynamic-programming (DP) oracle, encode hardware-aware tie-breakers, and spell out cost/complexity.

C.1
Greedy Per-Bit Rule: Monotonicity and Diminishing Returns

Setup:

Index blocks by
b\in\{1,\dots,B\}
and feasible bit-widths by
m\in\mathcal{M}=\{m_{\min},\dots,m_{\max}\}
.
Let
\mathcal{L}_{b}(Q_{b}^{(m)})
be the posterior-expected loss for block
b
at
m
bits and

\displaystyle\Delta_{b}(m)\;

\displaystyle\equiv\;\mathcal{L}_{b}(Q_{b}^{(m)})-\mathcal{L}_{b}(Q_{b}^{(m{+}1)}),

(63)

\displaystyle\gamma_{b}(m)\;

\displaystyle\equiv\;\frac{\Delta_{b}(m)}{\,C_{b}(m{+}1)-C_{b}(m)\,}\quad\text{(reduction per extra bit).}

(64)

The global objective is

F(\mathbf{m})\;=\;-\sum_{b=1}^{B}\mathcal{L}_{b}(Q_{b}^{(m_{b})}),\qquad\text{s.t. }\sum_{b}C_{b}(m_{b})\leq B_{\mathrm{tot}}.

(65)

Monotonicity (always):

If
\mathcal{L}_{b}(Q_{b}^{(m)})
is nonincreasing in
m
(more bits never increase expected loss), then any upgrade
m\!\to\!m{+}1
has
\Delta_{b}(m)\geq 0
and thus
\gamma_{b}(m)\geq 0
for positive costs. Hence, the greedy trajectory yields a sequence of nondecreasing objective values
F
as budget increases.

Diminishing returns (per-block):

Assume per-block
concavity in bits
:

\Delta_{b}(m{+}1)\;\leq\;\Delta_{b}(m)\qquad\text{for all }m\in\mathcal{M}\setminus\{m_{\max}-1\},

(66)

and either unit costs
C_{b}(m{+}1){-}C_{b}(m)\equiv 1
or costs that are nondecreasing in
m
.
This is a discrete diminishing-returns condition: the marginal reduction gained by the next bit on the
same
block shrinks as the block becomes more precise.

Submodularity-like assumption (cross-block):

Define the
ground set
of atomic upgrades

\mathcal{U}\;=\;\{(b,m):m\in\mathcal{M}\setminus\{m_{\max}\}\},

(67)

and for any feasible set
S\subseteq\mathcal{U}
respecting natural chain constraints per block, define

G(S)\;=\;\sum_{(b,m)\in S}\Delta_{b}(m).

(68)

If the marginal value of adding an upgrade
(b,m)
never
increases
when we have already taken other upgrades (i.e.,
G
is monotone submodular over chain-feasible sets), then classic results for knapsack-constrained submodular maximization imply that
density greedy
(choose items by value-per-cost and adjust with a partial last item) achieves a constant-factor approximation:

G(S_{\text{greedy}})\;\geq\;\left(1-\tfrac{1}{e}\right)\,G(S^{\star}),

(69)

where
S^{\star}
is the optimal selection under the same budget. In our setting, submodularity is
approximately
satisfied when:
(i) blocks are weakly coupled through the global task loss,
(ii) per-block reductions exhibit concavity in
m
, and
(iii) whitening and local proxies largely decouple blocks (empirically supported by Sec. A/B).

Takeaway:

Under monotonicity (mild) and diminishing returns (empirically observed) with weak cross-block interactions, the greedy per-bit rule is well motivated: it monotonically improves the objective and inherits near-optimality guarantees from the (approximate) submodular knapsack analogy.

C.2
Oracle Comparisons: Toy DP, Gap, and Failure Modes

Toy DP oracle:

Let costs be integers by scaling (or bucketize to bytes/words). For each block
b
and budget
c\in\{0,\dots,C_{b}^{\max}\}
, define

V_{b}(c)\;=\;\max_{m\in\mathcal{M}:\,C_{b}(m)\leq c}\big\{-\mathcal{L}_{b}(Q_{b}^{(m)})\big\}.

(70)

A standard separable knapsack DP computes

\mathrm{DP}[i,c]\;=\;\max_{0\leq c^{\prime}\leq c}\left\{\mathrm{DP}[i{-}1,c{-}c^{\prime}]\;+\;V_{i}(c^{\prime})\right\},

(71)

in
O\!\left(B\,B_{\mathrm{tot}}\,\max_{b}|\mathcal{M}|\right)
time and
O\!\left(B\,B_{\mathrm{tot}}\right)
memory.

Empirical gap:

When
\Delta_{b}(m)
is concave in
m
for most blocks, greedy and DP nearly coincide; we observe sub-tenth-point differences in typical settings (cf. main text).

When can greedy misallocate?

•

Non-concave per-block curves:
If
\Delta_{b}(m)
has an interior hump (e.g., poor range at low
m
, then a sharp jump), greedy might spend bits too early on a different block and miss a beneficial “two-step” upgrade on
b
.

•

Strong cross-block couplings:
If quantizing one block changes the sensitivity of another (rare under our proxies/whitening), true gains are not separable, violating submodularity.

•

Nonlinear cost steps:
If
C_{b}(m{+}1){-}C_{b}(m)
is irregular (e.g., due to packing), density ordering can be perturbed in ways the DP could exploit better.

In practice we mitigate these with (i) a short re-evaluation sweep of top-
k
candidates after each upgrade, and (ii) packing-aware tie-breakers (Sec. C3).

C.3
Hardware-Aware Tie-Breakers and Feasibility

Packing/alignment:

Many kernels operate on vectors of width
w
(e.g.,
w\in\{32,64,128\}
bits) and prefer that consecutive blocks share the same bit-width to avoid unpack/repacks. Let
\Pi
be the set of
packing-feasible
assignments, e.g., constraints that adjacent blocks in memory fall into groups whose total bit-length is a multiple of
w
. When greedy faces a tie (or near-tie within tolerance
\eta
), prefer candidates that:

1.

reduce the number of packing violations in
\Pi
,

2.

align more blocks to the dominant bit-width in the group,

3.

minimize the number of distinct bit-kernels loaded per layer.

Tensor-core / instruction families:

If the backend exposes fast paths only for certain
m
(e.g.,
\{4,8\}
) or for specific scale granularities (per-channel), restrict
\mathcal{M}_{b}
accordingly and encode any conversion overhead into
C_{b}(m)
. A simple heuristic: favor upgrades that
enter
a fast family (e.g.,
3\!\to\!4
) over those that remain off-path if their densities are within
\eta
.

Latency-aware density:

If latency is the bottleneck, replace storage cost in
\gamma_{b}(m)
by a predicted latency increment
\Delta T_{b}(m)
from a kernel cost model:

\gamma_{b}^{\text{lat}}(m)\;=\;\frac{\Delta_{b}(m)}{\Delta T_{b}(m)}.

(72)

This keeps the same greedy logic while targeting accuracy-per-millisecond.

Appendix D
Posterior Estimation Details

We summarize practical details for estimating the Gaussian posterior

p(w_{b}\mid\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cal}})=\mathcal{N}(\mu_{b},\Sigma_{b})
per block
b
,
covering Hutchinson-based diagonals, K-FAC structure, and numerically stable
whitening.

D.1
Hutchinson Probes: Probe Count, Damping, and Fisher vs. Hessian

Hutchinson trace/diagonal:

Given a symmetric PSD matrix
H_{b}
(Hessian or Fisher), the diagonal can be
estimated without forming
H_{b}
explicitly:

\mathrm{diag}(H_{b})\;\approx\;\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}v^{(m)}\odot\big(H_{b}v^{(m)}\big),\qquad v^{(m)}\sim\mathrm{Rademacher}(\{\pm 1\})^{d_{b}}.

(75)

Each MVP
H_{b}v
is implemented with Pearlmutter’s trick via two reverse-mode
passes per probe. For Fisher diagonals, replace
H_{b}
by the empirical Fisher

F_{b}=\mathbb{E}[\nabla\ell\nabla\ell^{\top}]
accumulated on

\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cal}}
.

Probes vs. squared error:

Let
\widehat{d}=\frac{1}{d_{b}}\|\mathrm{diag}(H_{b})-\widehat{\mathrm{diag}}(H_{b})\|_{2}^{2}
.
For Rademacher vectors and bounded fourth moments, the estimator is unbiased and

per-coordinate
variance decays as
O(1/M)
. Empirically we find

M\in\{8,16,32\}
adequate;
M{=}16
balances variance and cost on RN50/BERT.

Damping schedules:

We use Tikhonov regularization

\widetilde{H}_{b}\;=\;H_{b}+\lambda I,

(76)

with
\lambda
selected by a light grid over

\{10^{-4},3\!\times\!10^{-4},10^{-3},3\!\times\!10^{-3}\}
using a tiny
calibration subset and a proxy (layer-output MSE). A practical heuristic:
start from
\lambda=\max(10^{-3},\,0.01\cdot\mathrm{median}(\mathrm{diag}(H_{b})))
,
then back-off by a factor of
3
if codebook fitting becomes unstable.

Fisher vs. Hessian (choice):

The empirical Fisher diagonal tends to be less noisy for classification with
softmax/CE and is cheaper to accumulate; the (generalized Gauss–Newton) Fisher
also remains PSD by construction. The Hessian (via double-backprop MVPs) can
capture curvature beyond GGN but risks negative curvature; we clamp negatives
after damping by
\max(\mathrm{diag}(\widetilde{H}_{b}),\epsilon)
with

\epsilon\approx 10^{-8}
.

Posterior diagonal:

Under a diagonal Laplace posterior,

\Sigma_{b}\;\approx\;\mathrm{diag}\!\big(\widetilde{H}_{b}^{-1}\big)\;\equiv\;\left(\mathrm{diag}(\widetilde{H}_{b})\right)^{-1}.

(77)

We store
\sigma_{bi}^{2}\!=\!1/\widetilde{h}_{bi}
per weight coordinate.

D.2
K-FAC Factors: Windows, EMA, Damping, and Low-Rank Compression

Factorization:

For a linear block with weights
W_{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{o_{b}\times i_{b}}
,
approximate curvature by a Kronecker product:

H_{b}\;\approx\;A_{b}\otimes G_{b},\qquad A_{b}\approx\mathbb{E}[xx^{\top}],\quad G_{b}\approx\mathbb{E}[gg^{\top}],

(78)

where
x
are layer inputs and
g
are output gradients (or Jacobian-vector
products for Fisher/GGN). For self-attention projections, collect
A_{b}
on the
pre-projection activations and
G_{b}
on post-projection backprops.

Estimation windows and EMA:

Maintain running estimates with exponential moving averages on the calibration
stream:

\displaystyle A_{b}^{(t)}

\displaystyle=(1-\beta)A_{b}^{(t-1)}+\beta\,\frac{1}{B}\sum_{j=1}^{B}x_{j}x_{j}^{\top},

(79)

\displaystyle G_{b}^{(t)}

\displaystyle=(1-\beta)G_{b}^{(t-1)}+\beta\,\frac{1}{B}\sum_{j=1}^{B}g_{j}g_{j}^{\top},

(80)

with
\beta\in[0.01,0.1]
and
B
the mini-batch. A short window of

W\in[50,200]
iterations on
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cal}}
suffices.

Damping and inversion:

Use
factored
damping:

\widetilde{A}_{b}=A_{b}+\sqrt{\lambda}\,I,\qquad\widetilde{G}_{b}=G_{b}+\sqrt{\lambda}\,I,

(81)

then invert via Cholesky:
\widetilde{A}_{b}^{-1}=L_{A}^{-\top}L_{A}^{-1}
,

\widetilde{G}_{b}^{-1}=L_{G}^{-\top}L_{G}^{-1}
. The posterior covariance in matrix
form is

\Sigma_{b}\;\approx\;\widetilde{A}_{b}^{-1}\otimes\widetilde{G}_{b}^{-1}.

(82)

For whitening, we need a square root:
\widetilde{A}_{b}^{-1/2}
and

\widetilde{G}_{b}^{-1/2}
(from Cholesky factors).

Memory/time trade-offs:

A_{b}
and
G_{b}
are
i_{b}\times i_{b}
and
o_{b}\times o_{b}
. For wide layers,
store eigentruncations:

\displaystyle A_{b}\approx U_{A}\Lambda_{A}U_{A}^{\top}+\delta_{A}I,\qquad G_{b}\approx U_{G}\Lambda_{G}U_{G}^{\top}+\delta_{G}I,

(83)

keeping top-
r
eigenpairs (
r\ll i_{b},o_{b}
). This yields

\widetilde{A}_{b}^{-1}\approx U_{A}(\Lambda_{A}+\sqrt{\lambda}I)^{-1}U_{A}^{\top}+(\delta_{A}+\sqrt{\lambda})^{-1}P_{A}^{\perp},

(84)

and analogously for
G_{b}
. Choose
r
by cumulative explained trace (e.g.,

95\%
). This
low-rank+diag
form amortizes memory and speeds whitening.

D.3
Whitening Implementations: Cholesky, Eigen, and PCA Fallback

Cholesky (SPD):

If
\Sigma_{b}
is diagonal or factored SPD, set
S_{b}
by

S_{b}S_{b}^{\top}=\Sigma_{b},

(85)

and use
S_{b}^{-1}
to whiten:
z_{b}=S_{b}^{-1}(w_{b}-\mu_{b})
. For diagonal Laplace,

S_{b}=\mathrm{diag}(\sigma_{bi})
.

Eigen-decomposition:

For dense SPD
\Sigma_{b}
, compute
\Sigma_{b}=U\Lambda U^{\top}
and set

S_{b}=U\Lambda^{1/2}U^{\top}
. Clip
\Lambda
below
\epsilon
(e.g.,
10^{-8}
)
for stability. When building
S_{b}^{-1}
, use
\Lambda^{-1/2}
with the same clip.

PCA fallback (data-only):

If curvature is unreliable or ill-conditioned, approximate whitening from weight
samples or layer outputs: estimate covariance
C_{b}
on a short pass, compute

C_{b}=U\Lambda U^{\top}
, and set
S_{b}=U\Lambda^{1/2}U^{\top}
. Empirically this
recovers most BayesQ gains when Laplace is too noisy.

Numerical tips:

Center
w_{b}
by
\mu_{b}
before applying
S_{b}^{-1}
. Use double precision during
factorization; store
S_{b}
in FP32. For large blocks, apply block-diagonal
S_{b}

(e.g., per-channel) for cache-friendly transforms.

Appendix E
Codebook Design & Ranges

We detail uniform and non-uniform codebooks in whitened space, practical range
selection, initialization, and export to standard runtimes.

E.1
Uniform Codebooks: Mid-Rise Parameters, Range Search, Tails

Setup:

In whitened coordinates
z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
, a scalar mid-rise quantizer
with
K=2^{m}
levels uses dynamic range
[-\alpha,\alpha]
and step

\Delta=2\alpha/K
. The codepoints are

\tilde{c}_{k}\;=\;-\alpha+\Big(k+\tfrac{1}{2}\Big)\Delta,\qquad k=0,\dots,K-1.

(86)

Posterior-expected MSE (with tails):

\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{b}(\Delta,\alpha)=\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\int_{-\alpha+k\Delta}^{-\alpha+(k+1)\Delta}(z-\tilde{c}_{k})^{2}\,\phi(z)\,dz\;+\;2\int_{\alpha}^{\infty}(z-\alpha)^{2}\,\phi(z)\,dz,

(87)

where
\phi
is the standard normal pdf. We optimize
\alpha
by a 1D line
search (golden-section or Brent) on a small grid per block;
\Delta
follows.

Closed-form cell integral:

For bounds
a<b
and center
c
, define

I_{2}(a,b;c)=\int_{a}^{b}(z-c)^{2}\phi(z)\,dz
; using standard moments,

I_{2}(a,b;c)=\big[(1+c^{2})\Phi(z)-c\phi(z)-z\phi(z)\big]_{z=a}^{b},

(88)

where
\Phi
is the standard normal cdf and we substitute
z\!\to\!z-c
when needed.
This gives
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{b}
in terms of
\Phi,\phi
only.

Tail handling:

The tail integral equals
2\big(\Phi(-\alpha)(1+\alpha^{2})+\alpha\phi(\alpha)\big)

up to constants; clipping is essential at low bits. In practice, restrict

\alpha\in[2.5,5.0]
at
m\in\{2,3,4\}
.

Mapping back:

In weight space,
c_{bk}=\mu_{b}+S_{b}\tilde{c}_{k}
and uniform binning is done
on
z
via
S_{b}^{-1}
. If a backend requires affine scale/zero-point per
(channel|block), fold
\Delta
and
\alpha
into
(s_{b},z_{b})
parameters.

E.2
Non-Uniform Codebooks: Posterior-Weighted Lloyd VQ

Objective in whitened space:

For vector/scalar VQ with codebook
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{b}=\{\tilde{c}_{bk}\}_{k=1}^{K}

and regions
\{\tilde{R}_{bk}\}
,

\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{b}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\int_{\tilde{R}_{bk}}\|\tilde{c}_{bk}-z\|_{2}^{2}\,\phi(z)\,dz,\qquad z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I).

(89)

Lloyd updates (Gaussian-weighted):

\displaystyle\tilde{R}_{bk}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\big\{z:\|\tilde{c}_{bk}-z\|_{2}^{2}\leq\|\tilde{c}_{bj}-z\|_{2}^{2},\ \forall j\big\},

(90)

\displaystyle\tilde{c}_{bk}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\frac{\int_{\tilde{R}_{bk}}z\,\phi(z)\,dz}{\int_{\tilde{R}_{bk}}\phi(z)\,dz}.

(91)

Stop when relative improvement

\frac{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{b}^{(t-1)}-\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{b}^{(t)}}{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{b}^{(t-1)}}<10^{-4}

or after
T_{\max}\in[20,50]
iterations.

Initialization:

(1) Start from uniform mid-rise codepoints; (2) KMeans++ on whitened samples

z
drawn from
\mathcal{N}(0,I)
; (3) Moment-matched grid with denser points
near
0
(e.g.,
\mu
-law or tanh pre-warp). We recommend (1)
\to
(3) two-stage
initialization for stability at low bits.

Freezing & sharing:

For small blocks, overfitting is possible; freeze codebooks after
T_{\text{freeze}}

iterations or share a codebook across multiple similar blocks (same channel
shape) to amortize metadata.

Export to weight space:

Map
\tilde{c}_{bk}\mapsto c_{bk}=\mu_{b}+S_{b}\tilde{c}_{bk}
and store indices.
If the runtime lacks VQ gather, approximate by per-channel affine scales and a
small look-up LUT for nonuniform levels; see Sec. E3.

E.3
Export Formats: Scales/Zero-Points for Standard Runtimes

Per-(tensor|channel) affine:

Most runtimes expect
q = round(clamp(x/s, qmin, qmax)) + z
with dequant

x\approx s\,(q-z)
. For uniform BayesQ blocks, set:

s_{b}=\Delta,\qquad z_{b}=\mathrm{round}\!\left(\frac{-(-\alpha)}{\Delta}-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)=\mathrm{round}\!\left(\frac{\alpha}{\Delta}-\tfrac{1}{2}\right),

(92)

and ensure
q\in\{0,\dots,K-1\}
. For per-channel, store
(s_{bc},z_{bc})
.

Non-uniform export (LUT):

If the backend supports LUT dequant (common on GPUs/accelerators), store a

K
-entry table of
c_{bk}
in FP16/FP32 and indices as
m
-bit integers.
Otherwise, approximate with piecewise-affine segments (PWL) or fit an

effective
affine per-channel:

s_{bc},z_{bc}\;=\;\arg\min_{s,z}\ \sum_{i\in\text{channel }c}\big\|w_{bi}-s\,(q_{bi}-z)\big\|_{2}^{2},

(93)

keeping the original index assignment
q_{bi}
(no re-encode) to stay
hardware-compatible.

Metadata accounting:

When reporting average bits, include: weight indices (
m
bits), per-(block|ch)
scales (usually FP16), zero-points (int), optional LUT entries (
K
FP16),
and any packing padding to vector widths.

Packing and alignment:

If kernels expect aligned groups (e.g., 32/64/128-bit vectors), pad index
streams and coalesce blocks sharing
(s,z)
to reduce descriptor overhead.
At export, consolidate scales by grouping channels that share the same

posterior-optimal

\alpha
within a tolerance.

Appendix F
Complete Experimental Protocols

This appendix lists end-to-end procedures to reproduce all results.

F.1
Datasets

ImageNet-1k:

We use the ILSVRC-2012
train
split for calibration-only sampling and the

val
split (50,000 images) for evaluation. Images are decoded to RGB,
then resized with bicubic interpolation so that the shorter side is 256, and
center-cropped to
224\times 224
. Normalization uses per-channel means

(0.485,0.456,0.406)
and stds
(0.229,0.224,0.225)
(ImageNet-1k standard).
No test-time augmentation (no EMA, no mixup/cutmix).

GLUE:

Tasks: MNLI-m/mm, QNLI, QQP, SST-2. We use the standard
dev
splits for
reporting and
train
for calibration sampling. Tokenization follows

bert-base-uncased
(WordPiece), max sequence length 128, batch padding
to longest within batch. Metrics: accuracy for MNLI-m/mm, QNLI, SST-2; F1 for
QQP; we report the unweighted average of task-specific scores. For MNLI, both
matched and mismatched are included (averaged). Any optional OOD sets (e.g.,
HANS for MNLI; ImageNet-R/A/C for RN50) are reported separately with their
official preprocessing.

F.2
Calibration Sets

Sampling policies:

Unless specified, images/sentences are sampled uniformly at random without
replacement from the training sets. We consider sizes

\{10,50,100,500,1000\}
per model to study sensitivity. When using
multiple sizes in the same experiment sweep, each size is sampled independently
from the same pool to avoid leakage.

Label usage and reuse:

Labels are not used during calibration (only for evaluation metrics).
Calibration examples
may
be reused across runs with the same seed to
reduce variance. For cross-seed experiments, each seed resamples the calibration
set (unless noted).

Seeding:

We fix a
global_seed
for Python/NumPy/PyTorch and a

calib_seed
for sampling; default triples are {2025, 17}, {2025, 29},
{2025, 43}. Reported means are across the three
global_seed
s.

F.3
Evaluation Protocol

Vision:

Input size
224^{2}
, batch size
128
, FP16 activations, fused conv-bn where
available. Throughput is measured on 5,00 warm-up iterations followed by
1,000 timed iterations. Latency is reported as median per-batch wall-clock
(ms), and throughput as images/s.

Language:

Max sequence length 128, static shape (to avoid padding variability), batch
size 32. For GLUE, we disable gradient computation and any stochastic layers
(Dropout set to 0). The same warm-up and timing recipe as vision.

Timer:

CUDA events with synchronization around the measured region; CPU fallback timer
only for CPU experiments. The measured region includes host-to-device copy
(where relevant), kernel launches, and post-processing logits to CPU. We report
median and interquartile range (IQR).

Accuracy aggregation:

For GLUE, we report per-task metrics and the unweighted mean. For ImageNet-1k,
we report Top-1 (%) on the full 50k validation set. All metrics are averaged
over the three
global_seed
s; we include the sample standard deviation
and
95\%
t-intervals in the expanded tables.

Hardware & Software

GPUs/CPUs:

Unless otherwise stated: 8
\times

Xeon Gold (or AMD EPYC) with 256GB RAM; NVLink interconnect. CPU-only baselines

Libraries:

CUDA 12.x, cuDNN 8.9.x, NCCL 2.18+, PyTorch 2.2+/TorchVision 0.17+, and (when

Efficiency & Memory

We detail preprocessing time, runtime kernels, and exact model-size accounting.

I.1
Preprocessing Time (Posterior vs GPTQ; Amortization)

Definitions.

Let
T_{\text{GPTQ}}
be a standard one-pass curvature-aware PTQ preprocess time. Decompose BayesQ:

T_{\text{BayesQ}}\;=\;T_{\text{post}}+T_{\text{codes}}+T_{\text{alloc}}+T_{\text{distill}}\,,

(94)

where

\displaystyle T_{\text{post}}

\displaystyle=T_{\text{Hutch}}+T_{\text{factor}}\quad(\text{diagonal: }T_{\text{factor}}=0;\;\text{K-FAC: factor updates}),

(95)

\displaystyle T_{\text{codes}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{b}\sum_{m\in\mathcal{M}}T_{\text{design}}(b,m)\quad(\text{uniform analytics or Lloyd}),

(96)

\displaystyle T_{\text{alloc}}

\displaystyle\approx O\!\Big(\sum_{b}|\mathcal{M}|\Big)\quad(\text{table lookups + greedy heap}),

(97)

\displaystyle T_{\text{distill}}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0&\text{if disabled},\\
\#\text{steps}\times\frac{|\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cal}}|}{\text{batch}}\times t_{\text{fwd}}&\text{(calibration-only)}.\end{cases}

(98)

Overhead ratio.

\mathrm{Overhead}\;=\;\frac{T_{\text{BayesQ}}}{T_{\text{GPTQ}}}\;\approx\;0.95\text{--}1.05\times\ (\text{diag vs K-FAC settings}).

(99)

Amortization across runs.

If
\mathcal{M}
or budget
\bar{m}
changes but posterior is reused, amortize as:

\bar{T}_{\text{BayesQ}}^{(R)}\;=\;\frac{T_{\text{post}}}{R}\;+\;T_{\text{codes}}\;+\;T_{\text{alloc}}\;+\;T_{\text{distill}},

(100)

with
R
deployments sharing the same posterior.

Practical tips.

Use mixed-precision for Hutchinson probes; cache K-FAC factors per-layer; early-stop Lloyd when relative drop
<10^{-4}
or at 10–20 iterations.

I.2
Runtime Kernels & End-to-End Speed

Weight formats.

We export per-block integer weights with:

•

Uniform: scale
s_{b}
, optional zero-point
z_{b}
(symmetric/asymmetric).

•

Non-uniform (posterior-weighted Lloyd): packed codebook indices +
compiled
dequant scales to match backend expectations (group scales).

Activation dtype.

Default FP16 activations, optional INT8 if supported by the backend; per-layer dequant fused into GEMM/conv kernels wherever possible.

Packing and alignment.

Honor vector widths (e.g., 32/64/128-bit lanes), group sizes (e.g., per-64 weights), and tensor core tile constraints. Apply padding to avoid misaligned loads; tie-breaker in allocator can prefer upgrades that harmonize group boundaries.

Speed reporting.

We report images/s (RN50, bs=128) and tokens/s (BERT, seq=128) on the inference stack (CUDA/cuDNN/TensorRT), with:

\text{Speedup}\;=\;\frac{\text{throughput}(\text{BayesQ},\bar{m})}{\text{throughput}(\text{baseline})}.

(101)

Typical improvements come from lower memory bandwidth and higher integer kernel occupancy; non-uniform codebooks are compiled to per-group scales to stay on the fast path.

J.1
Confidence Calibration (ECE/MCE) and the Effect of Distillation

Setup.
We evaluate calibration on the same splits used for accuracy. For classification, let

\hat{y}(x)=\arg\max_{c}p_{Q}(c\mid x)
and
\hat{p}(x)=\max_{c}p_{Q}(c\mid x)
.
We report Expected Calibration Error (ECE) and Maximum Calibration Error (MCE) with
B{=}15
bins and 3 random bin boundaries (averaged) to reduce discretization noise.

Metrics.

ECE

\displaystyle=\sum_{b=1}^{B}\frac{|S_{b}|}{n}\,\big|\,\text{acc}(S_{b})-\text{conf}(S_{b})\big|,

(106)

MCE

\displaystyle=\max_{b\in[B]}\big|\,\text{acc}(S_{b})-\text{conf}(S_{b})\big|,

(107)

where
S_{b}=\{x:\hat{p}(x)\in(\tfrac{b-1}{B},\tfrac{b}{B}]\}
,
\text{acc}(S_{b})=\frac{1}{|S_{b}|}\sum_{x\in S_{b}}\mathbb{1}\{\hat{y}(x)=y\}
,
and
\text{conf}(S_{b})=\frac{1}{|S_{b}|}\sum_{x\in S_{b}}\hat{p}(x)
.

Variants.
We compare: (i) GPTQ (baseline), (ii) BayesQ (no distill), (iii) BayesQ + posterior distillation (500/1000 steps;
\tau\in\{1,2,4\}
). Distillation uses the teacher
p_{T}
as in the main text and updates only affine scales/adapters.

Reporting:

For each model and budget, we report Top-1 (RN50) or GLUE avg (BERT), ECE
\downarrow
, MCE
\downarrow
, and
\Delta
ECE relative to GPTQ. We also provide reliability diagrams (binned accuracy vs. confidence) with isotonic-smoothed curves and show per-bin counts to avoid misleading sparsity effects.

Expected outcome:

We consistently observe that BayesQ lowers ECE at 3.0 bits; the optional posterior distillation tightens calibration further (largest gains on RN50@3.0 and on BERT’s low-confidence regions), with diminishing returns beyond 500 steps.

J.2
OOD Shifts: Corruptions (Vision) and Domain Swaps (Text)

Vision OOD.

We evaluate on ImageNet-C-style corruptions at severities
\{1,3,5\}
(noise, blur, weather, digital) and report mean Corruption Error (mCE) relative to FP16, alongside accuracy deltas at budgets
\bar{m}\in\{3.0,3.5,4.0\}
.

Text OOD:

We construct domain shifts by swapping GLUE dev distributions (e.g., calibrate on MNLI, evaluate on QNLI/QQP) and by topic-based splits. We report the accuracy drop
\Delta
vs. in-domain.

Analysis protocol:

For each corruption/domain, we (i) compute accuracy drop relative to FP16 and GPTQ; (ii) correlate the drop with stage-wise saliency and allocated bits; and (iii) test whether BayesQ concentrates bits on OOD-sensitive blocks (e.g., downsampling in vision, attention proj/out in BERT).

Expected outcome:

We narrow the OOD gap at tight budgets, with the largest robustness gains when corruptions perturb features aligned with high-saliency blocks that received more bits.

Appendix K
Negative Results & Failure Cases

K.1
When the Posterior Misleads

Pathological layers.
In very wide layers with heavy channel correlations, diagonal Laplace can under-estimate anisotropy; the allocator may under-spend on such layers, yielding local accuracy cliffs. K-FAC mitigates but increases memory.

Non-Gaussianity.
Strongly skewed or multi-modal posteriors (e.g., due to weight symmetries, heavy regularization, or adapter interference) violate the quadratic approximation. In these cases, expected-loss tables computed with Gaussian assumptions can be miscalibrated; Monte Carlo proxies help but increase compute.

Tiny calibration.
With
|\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cal}}|\leq 10
, Hutchinson-based curvature is noisy and whitening becomes unstable, leading to poor ranges/codebooks. PCA fallback helps partially; we observed the largest failures at 3.0 bits in early convs if statistics are mis-estimated.

Symptoms & diagnostics:

•

Symptom
: Large per-layer error spikes after quantization not predicted by expected-loss.
Diagnostic
: compare predicted
\Delta_{b}(m)
to measured layer-output MSE on calibration; large rank-order mismatches flag posterior issues.

•

Symptom
: Bit spending focuses on late layers but early layers dominate error on OOD.
Diagnostic
: recompute expected-loss with MC proxy on top-
10\%
suspect blocks.

Fallbacks:

(i) Switch suspect blocks to K-FAC/low-rank posterior;
(ii) increase probe count
M
and damping
\lambda
;
(iii) freeze codebook to uniform+analytic range for unstable blocks;
(iv) force a minimum bit floor per stage to avoid starvation.

K.2
Backend Constraints and Fallback Behavior

Constraint types.

•

Mixed-bit disallowance
: Some runtimes only accept uniform per-tensor bits (e.g., all 4b).

•

Codebook restrictions
: Non-uniform VQ may be unsupported; only scale/zero-point per-(tensor|channel) is allowed.

•

Packing & alignment
: Vector width (e.g., 32/64 lanes) and cache-line alignment impose granularity constraints on feasible bit patterns.

Our fallback.

1.

Compile-to-uniform
: Collapse learned codebooks into per-channel scales/zero-points; approximate non-uniform steps with piecewise-uniform segments.

2.

Granularity rounding
: Round
\{m_{b}\}
to the nearest backend-supported set (e.g., {3,4} only) with a packing-aware tie-breaker that preserves high-
\gamma_{b}
upgrades.

3.

Kernel selection
: Prefer kernels that match the final packed layout (tensor cores vs DP4A), even if that slightly increases model size, to avoid launch fragmentation.

Known failures:

On strict backends that enforce single-bit per-tensor without per-channel scales, BayesQ’s advantage shrinks; the allocator helps less because its expressivity is curtailed. In such cases, posterior-aware
range
optimization remains beneficial, while codebook shaping is largely neutralized.
.

Appendix L
Evaluation Metrics and Mathematical Definitions

This section formalizes all metrics we report or reference in the main paper. Unless stated otherwise, expectations are over the empirical evaluation distribution and sums are over the corresponding dataset.

L.1
Classification Accuracy (ImageNet Top-1/Top-5)

Let
(x_{i},y_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
be examples with labels
y_{i}\in\{1,\dots,C\}
. Let logits be
f(x;w)\in\mathbb{R}^{C}
and predictions
\hat{y}(x)=\arg\max_{c}f_{c}(x;w)
. The Top-1 accuracy is

\mathrm{Acc@1}\;=\;\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}\{\hat{y}(x_{i})=y_{i}\}\,.

(109)

Let
\pi_{k}(x)
be the index set of the top-
k
logits. The Top-5 accuracy is

\mathrm{Acc@5}\;=\;\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}\{y_{i}\in\pi_{5}(x_{i})\}\,.

(110)

L.2
GLUE Task Metrics and Aggregates

For GLUE tasks used in the paper (MNLI-m/mm, QNLI, SST-2, QQP), we follow the official metrics:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Acc}_{\text{task}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}\{\hat{y}(x_{i})=y_{i}\}\quad\text{(MNLI-m, MNLI-mm, QNLI, SST-2)},

(111)

\displaystyle\mathrm{F1}_{\text{QQP}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\,\mathrm{Prec}\cdot\mathrm{Rec}}{\mathrm{Prec}+\mathrm{Rec}}\,,\qquad\mathrm{Prec}=\frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FP}},\ \mathrm{Rec}=\frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FN}}.

(112)

Let
\mathcal{T}
be the set of tasks and
n_{t}
their dev set sizes. We report:

\mathrm{GLUE\text{-}Macro}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}|}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}s_{t},\qquad\mathrm{GLUE\text{-}Micro}=\frac{\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}n_{t}\,s_{t}}{\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}n_{t}},

(113)

where
s_{t}
is Accuracy for MNLI/QNLI/SST-2 and F1 for QQP.

L.3
Posterior-Expected Distortions (Used in Tables & Allocation)

For weight block
b
with posterior
p(w_{b})=\mathcal{N}(\mu_{b},\Sigma_{b})
and quantizer
Q_{b}
, define a generic posterior-expected loss

\mathcal{L}_{b}(Q_{b})=\mathbb{E}_{w_{b}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{b},\Sigma_{b})}\big[\ell(Q_{b}(w_{b}),w_{b})\big].

(114)

(i) Mean-squared error (closed form, high-resolution).

Let
\Sigma_{b}=S_{b}S_{b}^{\top}
, whiten
z_{b}=S_{b}^{-1}(w_{b}-\mu_{b})
, and quantize
z_{b}
with mid-rise uniform step
\Delta
and (optional) clipping range
[-\alpha,\alpha]
. The high-resolution approximation yields

\mathcal{L}_{b}(Q_{b})\;\approx\;\frac{\Delta^{2}}{12}\,\mathrm{tr}(\Sigma_{b})\;+\;\mathrm{tr}\!\big(\Sigma_{b}\,\Xi(\alpha)\big),

(115)

where
\Xi(\alpha)
is diagonal with per-coordinate clipping contributions (zero when
\alpha
is large).

(ii) Task-proxy layer/output error.

For cached activations
a
, layer map
g_{b}(\cdot)
, and
M
posterior samples
w_{b}^{(m)}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{b},\Sigma_{b})
,

\widehat{\mathcal{L}}^{\text{layer}}_{b}=\frac{1}{MN}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Big\|g_{b}(a_{i};Q_{b}(w_{b}^{(m)}))-g_{b}(a_{i};w_{b}^{(m)})\Big\|_{2}^{2}.

(116)

(iii) Logit KL (teacher–student proxy).

Let
p_{T}(y|x)=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathrm{softmax}(f(x;w^{(m)})/\tau)
,
p_{Q}(y|x)=\mathrm{softmax}(f(x;Q(w))/\tau)
.

\widehat{\mathcal{L}}^{\mathrm{KL}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathrm{KL}\!\left(p_{T}(\cdot|x_{i})\,\|\,p_{Q}(\cdot|x_{i})\right).

(117)

L.4
Greedy Allocation Metrics (Per-bit Gains)

Let
m\in\mathcal{M}
be a feasible bit-width and
C_{b}(m)
the exact storage (bits) for block
b
at
m
(payload
+
metadata). For marginal improvement from
m\to m{+}1
:

\displaystyle\Delta_{b}(m)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{b}\!\big(Q_{b}^{(m)}\big)-\mathcal{L}_{b}\!\big(Q_{b}^{(m+1)}\big),

(118)

\displaystyle\gamma_{b}(m)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\Delta_{b}(m)}{C_{b}(m{+}1)-C_{b}(m)}\qquad\text{(expected-loss reduction per extra bit).}

(119)

The allocator iteratively picks the
(b,m)
with largest
\gamma_{b}(m)
until the budget is met.

L.5
Quantization Error, SNR, and PSNR (per Block)

Let
\varepsilon_{b}=Q_{b}(w_{b})-w_{b}
denote reconstruction error. We report

\mathrm{MSE}_{b}=\frac{1}{d_{b}}\|\varepsilon_{b}\|_{2}^{2},\quad\mathrm{SNR}_{b}=10\log_{10}\frac{\|w_{b}\|_{2}^{2}}{\|\varepsilon_{b}\|_{2}^{2}},\quad\mathrm{PSNR}_{b}=10\log_{10}\frac{\mathrm{peak}^{2}}{\mathrm{MSE}_{b}},

(120)

where
\mathrm{peak}
is a fixed dynamic-range scalar in the whitened domain (we use
\mathrm{peak}=\alpha
when mid-rise clipping is enabled).

L.6
Storage Accounting and “Average Bits”

Let block
b
hold
N_{b}
weights. The exact bit cost includes payload and all metadata:

C_{b}(m_{b})=\underbrace{N_{b}\,m_{b}}_{\text{quantized payload}}\;+\;\underbrace{C_{b}^{\text{scales}}+C_{b}^{\text{indices}}+C_{b}^{\text{codebook}}+C_{b}^{\text{headers}}}_{\text{overheads}}.

(121)

Global storage and reported average bits are

C_{\mathrm{tot}}=\sum_{b}C_{b}(m_{b}),\qquad\bar{m}=\frac{C_{\mathrm{tot}}}{\sum_{b}N_{b}}\,.

(122)

When comparing methods at a target
\bar{m}\in\{3.0,3.5,4.0\}
, we include all overhead terms. For reference against FP32, one may also report
C_{\mathrm{tot}}/(4\sum_{b}N_{b})\times 32
.

L.7
Runtime Metrics: Latency and Throughput

With batch size
B
, we measure median per-batch wall-clock latency (ms),
\mathrm{Lat}
, over
R
timed iterations after warm-up, and throughput

\mathrm{Throughput}\;=\;\frac{B}{\mathrm{Lat}/1000}\quad\text{(samples/s)}.

(123)

Speedup is reported relative to a baseline model as the ratio of throughputs (or inverse latencies).

L.8
Calibration Metrics: ECE and MCE

Let
\hat{p}(x)=\max_{c}\mathrm{softmax}(f(x;w))_{c}
,
\hat{y}(x)=\arg\max_{c}\mathrm{softmax}(f(x;w))_{c}
. Partition
(0,1]
into
B
bins
\{I_{b}\}_{b=1}^{B}
. For
S_{b}=\{i:\hat{p}(x_{i})\in I_{b}\}
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{acc}(S_{b})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|S_{b}|}\sum_{i\in S_{b}}\mathbf{1}\{\hat{y}(x_{i})=y_{i}\},\qquad\mathrm{conf}(S_{b})=\frac{1}{|S_{b}|}\sum_{i\in S_{b}}\hat{p}(x_{i}),

(124)

\displaystyle\mathrm{ECE}

\displaystyle=\sum_{b=1}^{B}\frac{|S_{b}|}{n}\,\big|\mathrm{acc}(S_{b})-\mathrm{conf}(S_{b})\big|,\qquad\mathrm{MCE}=\max_{b\in[B]}\big|\mathrm{acc}(S_{b})-\mathrm{conf}(S_{b})\big|.

(125)

We fix
B{=}15
with repeated random bin boundaries and average ECE to reduce discretization artifacts.

L.9
Uncertainty Diagnostics (Posterior & Predictive)

For posterior samples
w^{(m)}\!\sim\!\mathcal{N}(\mu,\Sigma)
and logits
f(x;w^{(m)})
:

\displaystyle\textbf{Predictive entropy: }\quad H[y|x]

\displaystyle=-\sum_{c=1}^{C}\bar{p}_{c}(x)\log\bar{p}_{c}(x),\qquad\bar{p}(x)=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathrm{softmax}\!\big(f(x;w^{(m)})\big);

(126)

\displaystyle\textbf{Mutual information (epistemic): }\quad\mathrm{MI}[y,w|x]

\displaystyle=H[y|x]-\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}H\!\left[\mathrm{softmax}\!\big(f(x;w^{(m)})\big)\right].

(127)

We optionally report means of these over the evaluation set.

L.10
Codebook Diagnostics

For scalar or vector codebooks with levels
\{c_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
and assignments
q_{i}\in\{1,\dots,K\}
:

\displaystyle\textbf{Utilization}:\quad U

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{K}\Big|\{k:\exists\,i,\ q_{i}=k\}\Big|,\qquad\textbf{Entropy of usage}:\;\;H(q)=-\sum_{k=1}^{K}\hat{p}_{k}\log\hat{p}_{k},

(128)

\displaystyle\hat{p}_{k}=\frac{1}{N_{b}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{b}}\mathbf{1}\{q_{i}=k\}.

(129)

Higher
U
and non-degenerate
H(q)
indicate that the learned/non-uniform codebook is effectively used.

L.11
Robustness Under Corruptions (mCE)

For ImageNet-C style corruptions
\mathcal{C}
, severities
s\in\{1,\dots,5\}
, and accuracy
\mathrm{Acc}_{c,s}
on corruption
c
at severity
s
, define the error
\mathrm{Err}_{c,s}=1-\mathrm{Acc}_{c,s}
. The mean Corruption Error (mCE) normalized by the full-precision reference
\mathrm{Err}^{\text{FP}}_{c,s}
is

\mathrm{mCE}\;=\;\frac{1}{|\mathcal{C}|}\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\frac{\frac{1}{5}\sum_{s=1}^{5}\mathrm{Err}_{c,s}}{\frac{1}{5}\sum_{s=1}^{5}\mathrm{Err}^{\text{FP}}_{c,s}}\,.

(130)

L.12
Confidence Intervals and Seeds

Across
r
independent runs (seeds), let
z_{j}
be the metric (e.g., accuracy). We report mean
\bar{z}
, standard deviation
s
, and the
95\%
Student-
t
interval:

\bar{z}=\frac{1}{r}\sum_{j=1}^{r}z_{j},\qquad s=\sqrt{\frac{1}{r-1}\sum_{j=1}^{r}(z_{j}-\bar{z})^{2}},\qquad\mathrm{CI}_{95}=\bar{z}\pm t_{0.975,\,r-1}\,\frac{s}{\sqrt{r}}.

(131)

L.13
Clipping Rate (Whitened Space)

For mid-rise scalar quantization in whitened coordinates with range
[-\alpha,\alpha]
, the per-coordinate clipping probability is

p_{\text{clip}}=2\,\Phi(-\alpha)\,,

(132)

where
\Phi
is the standard normal CDF. We report the empirical clipping rate as the fraction of coordinates with
|z|>\alpha
before quantization.
