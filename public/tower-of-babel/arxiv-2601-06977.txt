Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers for nonlinear constrained convex problems and applications to distributed resource allocation and constrained machine learning
Zhengjie Xiong, Yangyang Xu ∗

Abstract
We study a class of structured convex optimization problems, which have a two-block separable objective and nonlinear functional constraints as well as affine constraints that couple the two block variables. Such problems naturally arise from distributed resource allocation and constrained machine learning. To achieve high communication efficiency for the distributed applications, we propose a nonlinear alternating direction method of multipliers (NL-ADMM) that preserves the classical splitting structure while accommodating general convex functional constraints. Unlike existing ADMM variants for nonconvex constrained problems, the proposed method does not require smoothness of the objective functions or differentiability of the constraint mapping, by leveraging convexity of the considered problem. We establish global convergence and an ergodic 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1 / k ) \mathcal{O}(1/k) convergence rate of NL-ADMM by assuming the existence of a KKT solution. The results extend those of ADMM for linearly constrained convex problems. Numerical experiments are conducted on two representative distributed tasks. The results on numerous instances demonstrate that NL-ADMM can achieve (in many cases) 100x higher communication efficiency than the classic augmented Lagrangian method and nearly 2x higher than the Douglas-Rachford operator splitting method, making the new method well suited for large-scale distributed learning systems.



Keywords
nonlinear functional constraint, nonlinear ADMM, distributed resource allocation, constrained machine learning

Introduction

Large-scale structured optimization problems often arise in distributed environments with separable objectives and globally coupled constraints. On solving such problems, local computation can be carried out efficiently at individual workers, while the enforcement of global feasibility typically requires repeated communication and synchronization, causing communication cost to dominate overall performance and making communication efficiency a central algorithmic consideration. Motivated by these considerations, we study a structured convex optimization problem in the form of

(1.1)

\min_{{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}},{\mathbf{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}}}f({\mathbf{x}})+g({\mathbf{y}}),\quad\mbox{ s.t. }{\mathbf{h}}({\mathbf{x}})\leq B{\mathbf{y}},A{\mathbf{x}}+C{\mathbf{y}}={\mathbf{d}},

where
f
and
g
are proper closed convex but possibly non-differentiable functions
,
{\mathbf{h}}=[h_{1};\ldots;h_{m_{1}}]:\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}\to\mathbb{R}^{m_{1}}
is a nonlinear map with each component function being convex
but not necessarily differentiable,
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{2}\times n_{1}},B\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{1}\times n_{2}},C\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{2}\times n_{2}}
are given matrices and
{\mathbf{d}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{2}}
is a given vector.

Nonlinear Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers (NL-ADMM) for (

A classical approach for solving (
1.1
) is the augmented Lagrangian method (ALM)
[
30
]
. In distributed settings, applying ALM to (
1.1
) typically requires solving each augmented Lagrangian subproblem by an iterative method, which in turn involves repeated aggregation of global objective and constraint information across workers. As a consequence, multiple rounds of communication between the server and workers are often needed within each outer iteration, leading to high communication cost, especially for the distributed formulations (
1.2
) and (
1.4

To address this issue, we propose a nonlinear alternating direction method of multipliers (NL-ADMM) for (
1.1
). By splitting the augmented Lagrangian and updating primal variables in a blockwise fashion, NL-ADMM avoids repeatedly solving globally coupled subproblems. When applied to the distributed formulations (
1.3
) and (
1.8
), NL-ADMM requires only one communication round per iteration, resulting in significantly improved communication efficiency, as demonstrated in Section
4

1
summarizes NL-ADMM. We allow separate penalty parameters for the nonlinear and linear constraints, as well as optional over-relaxation in the dual updates. Though an operator splitting method can be applied to (
1.3
) and (
1.8
) and potentially achieve higher communication efficiency than the ALM, we observe that NL-ADMM performs significantly superior over the well-known Douglas–Rachford Splitting (DRS) method
[
11
]

1.3
Contributions

Our contributions are three-fold and summarized below.

•

First, we develop a nonlinear alternating direction method of multipliers (NL-ADMM) for a class of two-block structured convex optimization problems with nonlinear functional constraints. Unlike existing ADMM variants that are tailored to linear constraints or rely on smoothness or differentiability assumptions, the proposed framework accommodates general convex, possibly nonsmooth, nonlinear constraints while preserving the classical ADMM splitting structure. To the best of our knowledge, this work constitutes the first ADMM-type framework that systematically handles convex optimization problems with nonlinear functional constraints without resorting to linearization or smoothing techniques.

•

Second, under a standard KKT existence assumption, we establish global convergence of NL-ADMM and further derive an ergodic
\mathcal{O}(1/k)
convergence rate. The analysis is carried out under minimal regularity conditions: it relies solely on convexity and does not require smoothness of the objective functions, differentiability or Lipschitz continuity of the nonlinear constraint mapping, nor rank conditions on the associated linear operators. These results extend classical ADMM convergence theory beyond the linear-constraint setting.

•

Finally, we demonstrate the practical effectiveness of NL-ADMM on representative distributed resource allocation and constrained machine learning problems. The numerical results show that NL-ADMM consistently exhibits more favorable convergence behavior than the classical augmented Lagrangian method and the Douglas–Rachford Splitting method, particularly in terms of communication efficiency across a range of distributed problem settings.

Related Work

The ADMM, first introduced by Glowinski and Marrocco
[
16

of the form

\min_{{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}}f({\mathbf{x}})+g({\mathbf{y}})\quad\mbox{ s.t. }A{\mathbf{x}}+C{\mathbf{y}}={\mathbf{d}},

where
f
and
g
are closed convex functions. Classical convergence of ADMM for two-block structured convex optimization dates back to the operator-splitting work of Gabay and Mercier
[
14
]
and the monotone-operator analysis of Eckstein and Bertsekas
[
11
]
, and an ergodic rate of
\mathcal{O}(1/k)
is established by Monteiro & Svaiter in
[
27
]
and He & Yuan in
[
19
]
, where

k
denotes the iteration number. A comprehensive overview of the ADMM and its applications can be found in
[
2
]
. Building on these foundations, a wide range of extensions have been developed
for different structured problems
.

2.1
Linear constrained problems

Under linear constraints, we consider two main extensions of the classical two-block ADMM framework: multiblock formulations and problems with nonconvex objectives. Building on two-block convergence results, multiblock extensions have been extensively studied, and it is now well understood that direct extensions may diverge even in convex settings, as shown by Chen
et al.

[
4
]
. One approach to restoring convergence is to modify the algorithm itself: a prediction–correction variant of three-block ADMM is proposed in
[
20
]
, analyzed through a variational inequality reformulation, and shown to enjoy global convergence with an ergodic
\mathcal{O}(1/k)
convergence rate. A complementary line of work studies conditions under which the original three-block ADMM remains convergent; in particular, when one block is smooth and strongly convex with its condition number lying in a prescribed interval, global convergence holds for any penalty parameter
[
25
]
. A similar result is shown in
[
8
, Theorem 2.1]
as a special case of a three-operator splitting method.

Three-block results naturally extend to the multiblock setting
[
37
,
15
,
9
,
24
,
6
,
18
,
26
,
38
]
, where a key distinction lies between Gauss–Seidel–type schemes, which update blocks sequentially using the latest iterates, and Jacobi-type schemes, which update all blocks in parallel using only previous-iteration information. A unified symmetric Gauss–Seidel (sGS)–based proximal ADMM framework is developed in
[
6
]
, integrating majorization techniques, indefinite proximal terms, inexact updates, and sGS decompositions, and establishing global convergence. In contrast, a parallel Jacobi-type multiblock ADMM is studied in
[
9
]
, where convergence is guaranteed either under near-orthogonality and full column-rank assumptions on the constraint matrices or through blockwise proximal regularization, yielding an
\mathcal{O}(1/k)
convergence rate. A hybrid Gauss-Seidel and Jacobi update scheme is presented in
[
37
]
and also achieves the
\mathcal{O}(1/k)
convergence rate.

In addition, there is a substantial body of work on ADMM for nonconvex optimization with linear constraints
[
1
,
33
,
22
,
35
,
23
,
32
]
. Rather than attempting a comprehensive review, we briefly mention a few representative results. Hong, Luo, and Razaviyayn
[
22
]
study multi-block ADMM for a family of affinely constrained nonconvex optimization problems, with a focus on

[

]
extend the analysis of
[

Taken together, the above works establish a mature theory for ADMM under linear constraints, encompassing both multiblock formulations and nonconvex objective settings. When there are nonlinear functional constraints, this existing ADMM theory does not apply.

2.2
Nonlinear constrained problems

In contrast to the well-developed theory for ADMM under linear constraints, the literature on nonlinear variants remains relatively limited. Existing works focus on nonconvex problems with nonlinear equality constraints, and we are not aware of any work on ADMM for convex problems involving nonlinear functional constraints. Cohen, Hallak, and Teboulle
[
7
]
propose a dynamic linearized ADMM for problems with nonlinear equalities
F({\mathbf{x}})=G{\mathbf{y}}
, assuming smooth objective components with Lipschitz properties, a constraint mapping
F
with a locally Lipschitz Jacobian, and a full row rank matrix
G
. Under these assumptions and a bounded adaptive sequence of proximal parameters, they establish convergence to critical points and, under a KL condition, global convergence of the entire sequence. Zhu, Zhao, and Zhang
[
40

develop a first-order primal–dual augmented-Lagrangian scheme for nonconvex problems with nonlinear equalities
\phi({\mathbf{x}})+B{\mathbf{y}}=\mathbf{0}
, assuming smooth objective terms with Lipschitz gradients, a constraint mapping
\phi
that is Lipschitz continuous, and a full row rank matrix
B
. Their method linearizes the augmented terms at each iteration and, under these assumptions, achieves an
\epsilon
-stationary point within
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-2})
iterations, with global and linear convergence ensured by an additional error-bound condition.

More recent works follow similar lines. Sun and Sun
[
31
]
analyze a scaled dual-descent ADMM for
problems with
smooth nonlinear equality constraints
{\mathbf{h}}({\mathbf{x}})=\mathbf{0}
, requiring
{\mathbf{h}}
to be continuously differentiable with Lipschitz
continuous
and uniformly bounded Jacobian, and the smooth objective to possess a Lipschitz
continuous
gradient; under these conditions, they establish iteration complexity results to produce an
\epsilon
-stationary point. Hien and Papadimitriou
[
21

propose a multiblock majorization-based ADMM for
problems with
nonlinear constraints
\phi({\mathbf{x}})+B{\mathbf{y}}=\mathbf{0}
, under assumptions including block-separable subdifferentials, Lipschitz continuity of the gradient of the differentiable term in the objective, and full row rankness of
B

\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-2})
complexity, and global convergence under a KL property. El Bourkhissi and Necoara
[
12

study a closely related problem with nonlinear equality constraints of the form
F({\mathbf{x}})+G{\mathbf{y}}=\mathbf{0}
, where the nonlinearity involves a single primal block
{\mathbf{x}}
rather than the multiblock setting considered in
[
21

\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-2})

Although a nonlinear inequality constraint can be converted into an equality one by introducing a nonnegative slack variable, the above-mentioned convergence results do not apply to our considered problem (

However, in our considered problem (

{\mathbf{h}}

3
Convergence Analysis

In this section, we analyze the convergence of Algorithm
1
and establish its convergence rate. We make the following standard assumption.

Assumption 1

(Existence of a KKT point)
.

There exists a point
({\mathbf{x}}^{*},{\mathbf{y}}^{*},{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{*},{\mathbf{u}}_{2}^{*})
such that the following KKT conditions hold:

(3.1)

\displaystyle\mathbf{0}\in\partial f({\mathbf{x}}^{*})+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\beta_{1}\partial{\mathbf{h}}({\mathbf{x}}^{*})^{\top}{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{*}}+\beta_{2}A^{\top}{\mathbf{u}}_{2}^{*},

\displaystyle\mathbf{0}\in\partial g({\mathbf{y}}^{*})-\beta_{1}B^{\top}{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{*}+\beta_{2}C^{\top}{\mathbf{u}}_{2}^{*},

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\mathbf{h}}({\mathbf{x}}^{*})\leq B{\mathbf{y}}^{*},\ A{\mathbf{x}}^{*}+C{\mathbf{y}}^{*}={\mathbf{d}},\ {\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{*}\geq\mathbf{0},

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{*}\odot({\mathbf{h}}({\mathbf{x}}^{*})-B{\mathbf{y}}^{*})=\mathbf{0}.

In the above assumption,
\beta_{1}{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{*}
and
\beta_{2}{\mathbf{u}}_{2}^{*}
are the Lagrangian multipliers corresponding to the inequality constraint
{\mathbf{h}}({\mathbf{x}})\leq B{\mathbf{y}}
and the equality constraint
A{\mathbf{x}}+C{\mathbf{y}}={\mathbf{d}}
, where
\beta_{1}
and
\beta_{2}
are the same as those in Algorithm
1
. This is for the simplicity of our analysis, as we will see that the scaled dual iterates
\beta_{1}{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{k}
and
\beta_{2}{\mathbf{u}}_{2}^{k}
converge to the optimal dual solution.

For ease of notation, we let

(3.2a)

Theorem 3.3

.

1

For
{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}^{k+1}\geq\mathbf{0}

3.3

(3.16)

\displaystyle f({\mathbf{x}}^{k+1})+g({\mathbf{y}}^{k+1})-\bigl(f({\mathbf{x}})+g({\mathbf{y}})\bigr)+\beta_{1}\big\langle{\mathbf{h}}({\mathbf{x}}^{k+1})-B{\mathbf{y}}^{k+1},{\mathbf{u}}_{1}\big\rangle+\beta_{2}\big\langle A{\mathbf{x}}^{k+1}+C{\mathbf{y}}^{k+1}-{\mathbf{d}},{\mathbf{u}}_{2}\big\rangle

\displaystyle+\tfrac{\beta_{1}(2-\gamma_{1})}{2\gamma_{1}^{2}}\lVert{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{k+1}-{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{k}\rVert^{2}+\tfrac{\beta_{2}(2-\gamma_{2})}{2\gamma_{2}^{2}}\lVert{\mathbf{u}}_{2}^{k+1}-{\mathbf{u}}_{2}^{k}\rVert^{2}+V^{k+1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}({\mathbf{u}}_{1},{\mathbf{u}}_{2},{\mathbf{y}})}

\displaystyle V^{k}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}({\mathbf{u}}_{1},{\mathbf{u}}_{2},{\mathbf{y}})}+\tfrac{\beta_{1}}{2}\bigl(1-\tfrac{1}{\gamma_{1}}\bigr)^{2}\lVert{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{k}-{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{k-1}\rVert^{2}+\tfrac{\beta_{2}}{2}\bigl(1-\tfrac{1}{\gamma_{2}}\bigr)^{2}\lVert{\mathbf{u}}_{2}^{k}-{\mathbf{u}}_{2}^{k-1}\rVert^{2}.

({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}},{\mathbf{u}}_{1},{\mathbf{u}}_{2})=({\mathbf{x}}^{*},{\mathbf{y}}^{*},{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{*},{\mathbf{u}}_{2}^{*})

(3.17)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\Bigl(f({\mathbf{x}}^{k+1})+g({\mathbf{y}}^{k+1})-\bigl(f({\mathbf{x}}^{*})+g({\mathbf{y}}^{*})\bigr)+\beta_{1}\big\langle{\mathbf{h}}({\mathbf{x}}^{k+1})-B{\mathbf{y}}^{k+1},{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{*}\big\rangle+\beta_{2}\big\langle A{\mathbf{x}}^{k+1}+C{\mathbf{y}}^{k+1}-{\mathbf{d}},{\mathbf{u}}_{2}^{*}\big\rangle\Bigr)

\displaystyle V^{1}({\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{*},{\mathbf{u}}_{2}^{*},{\mathbf{y}}^{*})+\tfrac{\beta_{1}}{2}\bigl(1-\tfrac{1}{\gamma_{1}}\bigr)^{2}\lVert{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{1}-{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{0}\rVert^{2}+\tfrac{\beta_{2}}{2}\bigl(1-\tfrac{1}{\gamma_{2}}\bigr)^{2}\lVert{\mathbf{u}}_{2}^{1}-{\mathbf{u}}_{2}^{0}\rVert^{2}.

Since

\displaystyle f({\mathbf{x}}^{k+1})+\iota_{+}({\mathbf{s}}^{k+1})+g({\mathbf{y}}^{k+1})-\bigl(f({\mathbf{x}})+\iota_{+}({\mathbf{s}})+g({\mathbf{y}})\bigr)

\displaystyle V^{k}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}({\mathbf{u}}_{1},{\mathbf{u}}_{2},{\mathbf{y}})}+\tfrac{\beta_{1}}{2}\bigl(1-\tfrac{1}{\gamma_{1}}\bigr)^{2}\lVert{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{k}-{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{k-1}\rVert^{2}+\tfrac{\beta_{2}}{2}\bigl(1-\tfrac{1}{\gamma_{2}}\bigr)^{2}\lVert{\mathbf{u}}_{2}^{k}-{\mathbf{u}}_{2}^{k-1}\rVert^{2}.

Letting

Theorem 3.4

(Convergence Rate)
.

1

Q_{j}^{(f)}=R_{j}^{(f)\top}R_{j}^{(f)}+\mu_{f}I,\quad Q_{j}^{(h)}=R_{j}^{(h)\top}R_{j}^{(h)}+\mu_{h}I,

with
R_{j}^{(f)}
and
R_{j}^{(h)}
drawn entry-wise from a standard normal distribution and normalized by their spectral norms, i.e.,
R_{j}^{(\cdot)}\leftarrow R_{j}^{(\cdot)}/\lVert R_{j}^{(\cdot)}\rVert_{2}
. The vectors

and

are also generated entry-wise from a standard normal distribution. The constant
c_{j}
is chosen as a negative random scalar to ensure Slater’s condition at the origin, and
\mu_{f}=10^{-2}
and
\mu_{h}=10^{-4}
. In addition, we set
l=-5
and
u=5
for the box constraint.

We evaluate the numerical performance of the proposed nonlinear ADMM
by comparing

1.2
). Given a penalty parameter
\beta_{\mathrm{ALM}}>0
and an initial multiplier

u^{0}\geq 0
, the ALM iterates are defined as

(4.1a)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{x}}^{k+1}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{{\mathbf{x}}}\;\sum_{j=1}^{p}f_{j}({\mathbf{x}}_{j})+\frac{\beta_{\mathrm{ALM}}}{2}\left\|\left[\sum_{j=1}^{p}h_{j}({\mathbf{x}}_{j})+u^{k}\right]_{+}\right\|^{2},

(4.1b)

\displaystyle u^{k+1}

\displaystyle=\left[u^{k}+\sum_{j=1}^{p}h_{j}({\mathbf{x}}^{k+1}_{j})\right]_{+}.

The DRS method is applied to (

{\mathbf{w}}^{0}

\beta_{\mathrm{DRS}}>0

\displaystyle{\mathbf{u}}^{k}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{p}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}{\mathbf{w}}^{k},

(4.2b)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{q}}^{k}

\displaystyle=2{\mathbf{u}}^{k}-{\mathbf{w}}^{k},

(4.2c)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{x}}_{j}^{k}

\displaystyle\in\argmin_{{\mathbf{x}}_{j}}\;f_{j}({\mathbf{x}}_{j})+\tfrac{\beta_{\mathrm{DRS}}}{2}\bigl(\,[h_{j}({\mathbf{x}}_{j})+{\mathbf{q}}_{j}^{k}]_{+}\bigr)^{2},\quad j=1,\dots,p,

(4.2d)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{t}}^{k}

\displaystyle=[{\mathbf{q}}^{k}+{\mathbf{h}}({\mathbf{x}}^{k})]_{+},

5
Concluding Remarks

Motivated by large-scale resource allocation and constrained learning tasks, we developed a nonlinear alternating direction method of multipliers (NL-ADMM) for two-block convex optimization problems with nonlinear inequality and affine equality constraints. Under a standard KKT existence assumption, we established global convergence of NL-ADMM and derived an ergodic
\mathcal{O}(1/k)
convergence rate in both feasibility violation and objective optimality, thereby extending classical ADMM theory beyond linear constraint settings.

From a practical perspective, NL-ADMM is particularly well suited for distributed environments in which communication cost is a primary concern. Numerical experiments on representative distributed resource allocation and constrained machine learning problems show that NL-ADMM consistently achieves higher communication efficiency than the classical augmented Lagrangian method and Douglas–Rachford Splitting method across a range of problem instances.

To the best of our knowledge, this work represents the first systematic study of ADMM-type methods for convex optimization problems with general nonlinear functional constraints. Several directions merit further investigation, including stochastic (sub)gradient variants for data-intensive constrained learning and decentralized implementations for resource allocation over networked agents. Overall, this work positions NL-ADMM as a communication-efficient framework for large-scale distributed constrained optimization and provides a foundation for future stochastic and decentralized extensions.

Appendix A
Derivation of DRS update in Section
4

We consider the following problem:

(A.1)

\min_{{\mathbf{x}}_{0},{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}}\sum_{j=1}^{p}f_{j}({\mathbf{x}}_{j})+\iota_{\mathcal{Y}}({\mathbf{y}}),\quad\mbox{ s.t. }{\mathbf{h}}({\mathbf{x}})\leq{\mathbf{y}},\quad M_{0}{\mathbf{x}}_{0}+M_{1}{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{0},

where
\mathcal{Y}=\{{\mathbf{y}}:\sum_{j=1}^{p}{\mathbf{y}}_{j}=0\}
.
When both
M_{0}
and
M_{1}
are zero matrices, problem (
A.1
) reduces to the resource allocation problem (
1.3
).
When

M_{0}=-(\mathbf{1}\otimes I)
, and
M_{1}=I
,
it reduces to the consensus formulation (
1.8
). Thus we only need to derive the DRS update for problem (
A.1
) to recover the updates in (
4.2
) and (
4.3
).

Introducing dual variables
{\mathbf{u}}\geq 0
and
{\mathbf{v}}
, we define the Lagrangian function of (
A.1
) as follows

\mathcal{L}({\mathbf{x}}_{0},{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}},{\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}}):=\sum_{j=1}^{p}f_{j}({\mathbf{x}}_{j})+\iota_{\mathcal{Y}}({\mathbf{y}})+\langle{\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{h}}({\mathbf{x}})-{\mathbf{y}}\rangle+\langle{\mathbf{v}},M_{0}{\mathbf{x}}_{0}+M_{1}{\mathbf{x}}\rangle.

Then the Lagrangian dual problem is

\max_{{\mathbf{u}}\geq 0,{\mathbf{v}}}\inf_{{\mathbf{x}}_{0},{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}}\mathcal{L}({\mathbf{x}}_{0},{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}},{\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}}).

Note that for the
{\mathbf{y}}
-dependent part in the above formulation, we have

\inf_{{\mathbf{y}}}\iota_{\mathcal{Y}}({\mathbf{y}})-\langle{\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{y}}\rangle=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }{\mathbf{u}}\in\text{span}\{\mathbf{1}\},\\
-\infty,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Also, for the
{\mathbf{x}}_{0}
-dependent part, it holds

\inf_{{\mathbf{x}}_{0}}\langle{\mathbf{v}},M_{0}{\mathbf{x}}_{0}\rangle=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }M_{0}^{\top}{\mathbf{v}}=\mathbf{0},\\
-\infty,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Based on these observations, we define the following two functions:

\displaystyle d_{g}({\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}})

\displaystyle:=\iota_{\mathcal{U}}({\mathbf{u}})+\iota_{\mathcal{V}}({\mathbf{v}}),\ \text{with }\mathcal{U}:={\{{\mathbf{u}}:{\mathbf{u}}=\alpha\mathbf{1},\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\}}\text{ and }\mathcal{V}:=\{{\mathbf{v}}:M_{0}^{\top}{\mathbf{v}}=\mathbf{0}\},

\displaystyle d_{f}({\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}})

\displaystyle:=-\inf_{{\mathbf{x}}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{p}f_{j}({\mathbf{x}}_{j})+\langle{\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{h}}({\mathbf{x}})\rangle+\langle{\mathbf{v}},M_{1}{\mathbf{x}}\rangle\right)+\iota_{+}({\mathbf{u}}).

Therefore, the Lagrangian dual problem becomes

\max_{{\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}}}-d_{f}({\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}})-d_{g}({\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}}),

which is equivalent to the following zero-inclusion problem

(A.2)

\mathbf{0}\in\partial d_{f}({\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}})+\partial d_{g}({\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}}).

Then for a fixed
\eta\in(0,1]
, the DRS iteration takes the form

(A.3a)

\displaystyle({\mathbf{u}}^{k},{\mathbf{v}}^{k})

\displaystyle={\mathbf{J}}_{\beta\partial d_{g}}({\mathbf{w}}^{k}),

(A.3b)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{w}}^{k+1}

\displaystyle=(1-\eta){\mathbf{w}}^{k}+\eta(2{\mathbf{J}}_{\beta\partial d_{f}}-I)\left(2({\mathbf{u}}^{k},{\mathbf{v}}^{k})-({\mathbf{w}}_{{\mathbf{u}}}^{k},{\mathbf{w}}_{{\mathbf{v}}}^{k})\right),

where
{\mathbf{w}}^{k}=({\mathbf{w}}_{{\mathbf{u}}}^{k},{\mathbf{w}}_{{\mathbf{v}}}^{k})
and the resolvent operators are defined as

\mathbf{J}_{\beta\partial d_{g}}:=(I+\beta\partial d_{g})^{-1},\quad\mathbf{J}_{\beta\partial d_{f}}:=(I+\beta\partial d_{f})^{-1}.

Below, we first find the closed-form expression for (
A.3a
). By definition of
\mathbf{J}_{\beta\partial d_{g}}
, we have

({\mathbf{u}}^{k},{\mathbf{v}}^{k})=\argmin_{{\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}}}\tfrac{1}{2\beta}\lVert{\mathbf{u}}-{\mathbf{w}}_{{\mathbf{u}}}^{k}\rVert^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2\beta}\lVert{\mathbf{v}}-{\mathbf{w}}_{{\mathbf{v}}}^{k}\rVert^{2}+d_{g}({\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}}).

Then by the definition of
d_{g}
, the closed-form solution follows

(A.4)

{\mathbf{u}}^{k}=\tfrac{1}{p}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}{\mathbf{w}}_{{\mathbf{u}}}^{k},\quad{\mathbf{v}}^{k}=\Pi_{\mathcal{V}}({\mathbf{w}}_{{\mathbf{v}}}^{k}).

Second, we find the closed-form expression for (
A.3b
). For convenience, we define

{\mathbf{q}}^{k}=({\mathbf{q}}_{{\mathbf{u}}}^{k},{\mathbf{q}}_{{\mathbf{v}}}^{k}):=2({\mathbf{u}}^{k},{\mathbf{v}}^{k})-({\mathbf{w}}_{{\mathbf{u}}}^{k},{\mathbf{w}}_{{\mathbf{v}}}^{k}),\quad{\mathbf{t}}^{k}=({\mathbf{t}}_{{\mathbf{u}}}^{k},{\mathbf{t}}_{{\mathbf{v}}}^{k}):={\mathbf{J}}_{\beta\partial d_{f}}({\mathbf{q}}_{{\mathbf{u}}}^{k},{\mathbf{q}}_{{\mathbf{v}}}^{k}).

By the definition of
\mathbf{J}_{\beta\partial d_{f}}
and
d_{f}({\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}})
, we have

({\mathbf{t}}_{{\mathbf{u}}}^{k},{\mathbf{t}}_{{\mathbf{v}}}^{k})=\argmin_{{\mathbf{t}}_{{\mathbf{u}}}\geq\mathbf{0},{\mathbf{t}}_{{\mathbf{v}}}}\tfrac{1}{2\beta}\lVert{\mathbf{t}}_{{\mathbf{u}}}-{\mathbf{q}}_{{\mathbf{u}}}^{k}\rVert^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2\beta}\lVert{\mathbf{t}}_{{\mathbf{v}}}-{\mathbf{q}}_{{\mathbf{v}}}^{k}\rVert^{2}-\inf_{{\mathbf{x}}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{p}f_{j}({\mathbf{x}}_{j})+\langle{\mathbf{t}}_{{\mathbf{u}}},{\mathbf{h}}({\mathbf{x}})\rangle+\langle{\mathbf{t}}_{{\mathbf{v}}},M_{1}{\mathbf{x}}\rangle\right).

Note that

(A.5)

\displaystyle\min_{{\mathbf{t}}_{{\mathbf{u}}}\geq\mathbf{0},{\mathbf{t}}_{{\mathbf{v}}}}\tfrac{1}{2\beta}\lVert{\mathbf{t}}_{{\mathbf{u}}}-{\mathbf{q}}_{{\mathbf{u}}}^{k}\rVert^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2\beta}\lVert{\mathbf{t}}_{{\mathbf{v}}}-{\mathbf{q}}_{{\mathbf{v}}}^{k}\rVert^{2}-\inf_{{\mathbf{x}}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{p}f_{j}({\mathbf{x}}_{j})+\langle{\mathbf{t}}_{{\mathbf{u}}},{\mathbf{h}}({\mathbf{x}})\rangle+\langle{\mathbf{t}}_{{\mathbf{v}}},M_{1}{\mathbf{x}}\rangle\right)

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\min_{{\mathbf{t}}_{{\mathbf{u}}}\geq\mathbf{0},{\mathbf{t}}_{{\mathbf{v}}}}\sup_{{\mathbf{x}}}\left(-\sum_{j=1}^{p}f_{j}({\mathbf{x}}_{j})-\langle{\mathbf{t}}_{{\mathbf{u}}},{\mathbf{h}}({\mathbf{x}})\rangle-\langle{\mathbf{t}}_{{\mathbf{v}}},M_{1}{\mathbf{x}}\rangle\right)+\tfrac{1}{2\beta}\lVert{\mathbf{t}}_{{\mathbf{u}}}-{\mathbf{q}}_{{\mathbf{u}}}^{k}\rVert^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2\beta}\lVert{\mathbf{t}}_{{\mathbf{v}}}-{\mathbf{q}}_{{\mathbf{v}}}^{k}\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\sup_{{\mathbf{x}}}-\sum_{j=1}^{p}f_{j}({\mathbf{x}}_{j})+\min_{{\mathbf{t}}_{{\mathbf{u}}}\geq\mathbf{0},{\mathbf{t}}_{{\mathbf{v}}}}\Big(-\langle{\mathbf{t}}_{{\mathbf{u}}},{\mathbf{h}}({\mathbf{x}})\rangle-\langle{\mathbf{t}}_{{\mathbf{v}}},M_{1}{\mathbf{x}}\rangle+\tfrac{1}{2\beta}\lVert{\mathbf{t}}_{{\mathbf{u}}}-{\mathbf{q}}_{{\mathbf{u}}}^{k}\rVert^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2\beta}\lVert{\mathbf{t}}_{{\mathbf{v}}}-{\mathbf{q}}_{{\mathbf{v}}}^{k}\rVert^{2}\Big).

Then we derive

\displaystyle({\mathbf{t}}_{{\mathbf{u}}}^{*},{\mathbf{t}}_{{\mathbf{v}}}^{*})

\displaystyle=\argmin_{{\mathbf{t}}_{{\mathbf{u}}}\geq 0,{\mathbf{t}}_{{\mathbf{v}}}}-\langle{\mathbf{t}}_{{\mathbf{u}}},{\mathbf{h}}({\mathbf{x}})\rangle-\langle{\mathbf{t}}_{{\mathbf{v}}},M_{1}{\mathbf{x}}\rangle+\tfrac{1}{2\beta}\lVert{\mathbf{t}}_{{\mathbf{u}}}-{\mathbf{q}}_{{\mathbf{u}}}^{k}\rVert^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2\beta}\lVert{\mathbf{t}}_{{\mathbf{v}}}-{\mathbf{q}}_{{\mathbf{v}}}^{k}\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=\left([{\mathbf{q}}_{{\mathbf{u}}}^{k}+\beta{\mathbf{h}}({\mathbf{x}})]_{+},{\mathbf{q}}_{{\mathbf{v}}}^{k}+\beta M_{1}{\mathbf{x}}\right),

for which notice that the solution
({\mathbf{t}}_{{\mathbf{u}}}^{*},{\mathbf{t}}_{{\mathbf{v}}}^{*})
depends on
{\mathbf{x}}
.
Plug the formulation of
({\mathbf{t}}_{{\mathbf{u}}}^{*},{\mathbf{t}}_{{\mathbf{v}}}^{*})
back into the last line of (
A.5
) and simplify terms. We then obtain the following problem and denote
{\mathbf{x}}^{k}
as the optimal solution:

(A.6)

{\mathbf{x}}^{k}=\argmin_{{\mathbf{x}}}\sum_{j=1}^{p}f_{j}({\mathbf{x}}_{j})-\tfrac{1}{2\beta}\lVert[-{\mathbf{q}}_{{\mathbf{u}}}^{k}-\beta{\mathbf{h}}({\mathbf{x}})]_{+}\rVert^{2}+\tfrac{\beta}{2}\lVert{\mathbf{h}}({\mathbf{x}})\rVert^{2}+\langle{\mathbf{q}}_{{\mathbf{u}}}^{k},{\mathbf{h}}({\mathbf{x}})\rangle+\tfrac{\beta}{2}\lVert M_{1}{\mathbf{x}}\rVert^{2}+\langle{\mathbf{q}}_{{\mathbf{v}}}^{k},M_{1}{\mathbf{x}}\rangle.

With the computation of
{\mathbf{x}}^{k}
, we then let

(A.7)

({\mathbf{t}}_{{\mathbf{u}}}^{k},{\mathbf{t}}_{{\mathbf{v}}}^{k})=\left([{\mathbf{q}}_{{\mathbf{u}}}^{k}+\beta{\mathbf{h}}({\mathbf{x}}^{k})]_{+},{\mathbf{q}}_{{\mathbf{v}}}^{k}+\beta M_{1}{\mathbf{x}}^{k}\right).

Therefore, (
A.3b
) becomes

\displaystyle{\mathbf{w}}^{k+1}

\displaystyle=(1-\eta){\mathbf{w}}^{k}+\eta(2{\mathbf{J}}_{\beta\partial d_{f}}-I)\big(2({\mathbf{u}}^{k},{\mathbf{v}}^{k})-({\mathbf{w}}_{{\mathbf{u}}}^{k},{\mathbf{w}}_{{\mathbf{v}}}^{k})\big)

\displaystyle=(1-\eta){\mathbf{w}}^{k}+\eta(2{\mathbf{t}}^{k}-{\mathbf{q}}^{k})

\displaystyle=(1-\eta){\mathbf{w}}^{k}+\eta\Big(2{\mathbf{t}}^{k}-\left(2({\mathbf{u}}^{k},{\mathbf{v}}^{k})-{\mathbf{w}}^{k}\right)\Big)

\displaystyle={\mathbf{w}}^{k}+2\eta\left({\mathbf{t}}^{k}-({\mathbf{u}}^{k},{\mathbf{v}}^{k})\right).

Recovering the updates in (

) and (

)

When

and

{\mathbf{v}}

Hence, for problem (
1.3

(A.8a)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{u}}^{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}{\mathbf{w}}^{k},

(A.8b)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{q}}^{k}

\displaystyle=2{\mathbf{u}}^{k}-{\mathbf{w}}^{k},
