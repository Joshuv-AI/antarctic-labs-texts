Maternal obesogenic diet exposure regulates the offspring gut liver axis and fibroinflammatory liver disease
Authors: Naresh Naik Ramavath, Oğuz Özler, Holly Hinrichs, Francisco R Victorino, Vung Lian, Monica Young, Tarin M Bigley, Michael D Thompson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMaternal obesogenic diet exposure (MODE) promotes fibroinflammatory liver disease in offspring via vertical transfer of an altered microbiome. The mechanism for how an altered offspring microbiome increases susceptibility to liver disease is not clear. A critical early life event termed the ‘weaning reaction’ is dependent on the early microbiome and when altered, results in worse pathologic inflammation. MODE attenuates the weaning reaction promoting worse liver disease in mice in an early microbiome-dependent manner. Using our MODE model and cross-fostering approaches we assessed the effect of MODE on neonatal gut-liver axis development. MODE shifts the bile acid (BA) profile, expression of intestinal barrier genes, and establishment of gut immune cell populations in the offspring. Among the BA changes, UDCA is decreased in MODE offspring and supplementation with UDCA at 2 weeks of age reestablishes the weaning reaction and gut immune cell development. MODE offspring exhibit worse hepatic inflammation and fibrosis during dextran sodium sulfate induced colitis. These findings identify that MODE alters early gut immune programming events in offspring intestine with modifications in BA metabolism. Supplementing MODE offspring with secondary BAs may be an approach to restore the weaning reaction and immune cell populations. Subject terms: Liver fibrosis, Obesity

Introduction

Maternal obesity/obesogenic diet exposure is a predisposing factor for development of steatotic liver disease (SLD) in offspring (reviewed in ref. 1). Birth cohorts have identified maternal pre-pregnancy BMI ≥ 30 as an independent predictor of offspring SLD2,3. Animal models of maternal high fat diet exposure have been used to represent maternal obesity and corroborate these findings4–6. We have shown in mice that offspring exposed to maternal high fat, fructose, cholesterol (HFFC) diet during pregnancy develop more severe hepatic inflammation and fibrosis than maternal chow fed (CON) offspring weaned to the HFFC diet7,8. We have also shown that maternal obesogenic diet exposed (MODE) offspring are more susceptible to cholestatic liver disease with increased fibrosis and ductular reaction9. In general, MODE programs worse fibroinflammatory liver disease in animal models.

A potential mechanism for transmission of phenotypes to offspring is via shifts in the offspring microbiome. Studies in rodents, non-human primates, and humans all show that MODE alters the offspring microbiome10–12. These shifts in the microbiome are causally involved in altering bile acid homeostasis and development of steatotic liver disease12,13. It is critical to understand the mechanisms that connect MODE-induced shifts in the microbiome and development of fibroinflammatory liver disease in offspring. The early microbiome plays an indispensable role in programming the intestinal immune system, an event termed the ‘weaning reaction’14. Altering the weaning reaction results in increased susceptibility to chronic inflammatory disease and infections in adult mice14,15.

We have shown that following MODE, the weaning reaction is attenuated in an early microbiome dependent manner8. Furthermore, MODE-induced susceptibility to worse inflammation and fibrosis is similarly dependent on these early microbiome alterations8.

In the present study, we evaluate the impact of MODE on the perinatal offspring gut liver axis including changes in the weaning reaction, gut immune cell populations, and bile acid metabolism. We also evaluate the role of early microbiome changes on susceptibility of the adult offspring to hepatic injury in the setting of colitis. Our findings provide new evidence supporting the critical role of MODE induced changes in the microbiome on programming the gut-liver axis in the offspring with implications for later liver disease progression.

Results

MODE increases perinatal hepatocyte proliferation and inflammation in offspring

To assess the effect of MODE on the perinatal liver, we used a previously described mouse model of MODE with HFFC diet feeding for 6 weeks prior to mating which results in a significant increase in weight gain compared to controls (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Food intake by weight was less in HFFC females but average caloric intake was similar (Supplementary Fig. 1B, C). Liver was collected from dams at the time of weaning of offspring with both liver weight and liver weight/body weight ratio significantly increased (Supplementary Fig. 1D). Histological evaluation of HFFC dam liver showed evidence of hepatic steatosis on H&E staining and inflammation on Mac-2 staining (Supplementary Fig. 1E, F). Gonadal adipose from HFFC dams also show evidence of adipocyte hypertrophy (Supplementary Fig. 1G). Offspring were evaluated between 5 and 30 days of age and no difference in offspring body weight, liver weight, or liver weight to body weight ratio was observed (Fig. 1A–C). Hepatocyte proliferation was assessed by Ki-67 staining and showed increased hepatocyte proliferation at 15, 20, and 30 days of age in HFFC offspring (Fig. 1D, E). An increase in CD45+ and Mac-2 positive staining was present at 15 and 20 days of age in HFFC offspring (Fig. 1F, G). These findings support the finding that hepatocyte proliferation and hepatic inflammation in the perinatal period is affected by MODE.

MODE induced increase in perinatal hepatocyte proliferation and inflammation dependent on foster dam diet

To assess whether events in the weaning period and potential effects of early microbiome development are driving increased hepatocyte proliferation and inflammation, offspring were cross fostered within twenty-four hours of birth with a dam from the opposite group. Fifteen-day old CON offspring fostered by an HFFC dam (CON→HFFC) exhibited increased ki-67 positive hepatocytes compared to HFFC offspring fostered by a CON dam (HFFC→CON) (Fig. 2A). Mac-2 staining was increased in CON→HFFC offspring at 15 and 20 days old. These data demonstrate that hepatic inflammation and proliferation in offspring is dependent on the diet of the dam during the perinatal period.

MODE affects perinatal bile acid but not short chain fatty acids and branch chain amino acid levels in offspring

The early microbiome is essential for establishment of the intestinal immune system16. Specifically, the presence of the early microbiome establishes the weaning reaction and development of gut regulatory T cells14. Given the connection between the shifted microbiome and attenuation of the weaning reaction, it is possible that a microbial metabolite is critical for the weaning reaction. Three candidates include short chain fatty acids, branched chain amino acids and bile acids. We assessed total levels of all three in cecal contents of 3-week-old offspring and found that only total bile acids were increased in HFFC offspring (Fig. 3A). There were no differences in total or individual branched chain amino acids or short chain fatty acids (Fig. 3A and Supplementary Fig. 2). The average total bile acids in the liver were higher in HFFC offspring but the difference was not statistically significant (Fig. 3B). These findings support that MODE induced changes in BAs may be a candidate driver for attenuation of the weaning reaction in HFFC offspring.

MODE alters levels of specific bile acids in offspring

To determine if MODE shifts the abundance of specific bile acids in offspring, we measured the abundance of individual bile acids in cecal contents and liver of 3-week-old offspring. The abundance of tauromuricholic acid (tMCA), glycomuricholic acid (gMCA), and glycocholic acid (gCA) were all increased in the cecal contents of HFFC offspring (Fig. 4A). A trend towards an increase in taurocholic acid (tCA) was observed in HFFC offspring cecal contents (Fig. 4A, p = 0.0660). Unconjugated MCA was decreased in HFFC offspring (Fig. 4A). Measurement of the ratio of total conjugated to unconjugated bile acid trended towards an increase in HFFC offspring (Fig. 4A, p = 0.0784). The abundance of the secondary bile acid ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA) was significantly decreased in HFFC offspring, while deoxycholic acid (DCA) was unchanged (Fig. 4A).

Assessment of the abundance of liver bile acids showed a similar decrease in unconjugated MCA and a trend towards an increased in tMCA (p = 0.0773) (Fig. 4B). No difference in CA species was observed in liver. The ratio of conjugated to unconjugated bile acids was increased in HFFC offspring liver. UDCA was decreased in HFFC offspring liver (Fig. 4B). The average abundance of hepatic DCA was less in HFFC offspring but the difference was not statistically significant (Fig. 4B). Thus, maternal HFFC diet alters specific bile acid levels in offspring.

MODE induced shifts in bile acid levels dependent on foster dam diet

To determine if maternal diet in the neonatal period drives the changes in individual bile acid abundance observed in HFFC offspring, we measured the abundance of individual bile acid in cross fostered offspring. In cecal contents, the ratio of conjugated to unconjugated bile acids was increased in CON→HFFC offspring (Fig. 5A). The abundance of UDCA was not different between cross-fostered groups, but the abundance of DCA was decreased in CON→HFFC offspring (Fig. 5A). In liver, the ratio of conjugated to unconjugated bile acids was also increased in CON→HFFC offspring (Fig. 5B). The abundance of hepatic UDCA was significantly decreased in CON→HFFC offspring with a trend towards a decrease in DCA (p = 0.0574) (Fig. 5B). The changes observed in CON offspring fostered by an HFFC dam mirror the changes observed in HFFC offspring (increased conjugated bile acids, decreased secondary bile acids). These findings support the conclusion that maternal diet during the weaning period drives shifts in the abundance of offspring bile acids.

UDCA supplementation in weaning period reestablishes weaning reaction in MODE offspring

We previously reported that MODE affected the perinatal offspring microbiome and intestinal weaning reaction8. One of the bile acids decreased in the cecal contents of HFFC offspring was UDCA making it a potential candidate for regulation of the weaning reaction. To assess the role of UDCA in the weaning reaction in HFFC offspring, we gavaged two-week-old HFFC offspring with UDCA (70 µg/g body weight) daily for 5 days and measured the expression of Tnfa and Ifng in ileum. At 3 weeks of age, HFFC offspring have decreased expression of Tnfa and Ifng compared to control (Fig. 6A). Supplementation with UDCA in HFFC offspring increased the expression of Tnfa and Ifng at 3 weeks consistent with reestablishment of the weaning reaction (Fig. 6A). Given the connection between the weaning reaction and development of gut immune cell populations14, we performed flow cytometry on cells isolated from the intraepithelial and lamina propria compartments of ileum in CON, HFFC, and HFFC + UDCA offspring (Fig.6, Supplementary Fig. 3). HFFC offspring exhibited decreased numbers of CD45+ cells in both the intraepithelial and lamina propria compartment (Fig. 6B). Supplementation with UDCA in HFFC offspring increased the number of total Cd45+ intraepithelial and lamina propria cells (Fig. 6B). Evaluating specific lymphocyte populations, intraepithelial CD8 and γδ T cells were significantly decreased in HFFC offspring, while CD4, CD8, CD19, and γδ T cells were all decreased in lamina propria of HFFC offspring (Fig. 6C, D). UDCA supplementation had no effect on intraepithelial lymphocyte populations but did significantly increase CD4, CD8, CD19, and γδ T cells in the lamina propria of HFFC offspring (Fig. 6C, D). HFFC offspring also exhibited decreased numbers of CD4+Foxp3+ regulatory T cells in both the IE and LP compartments (Fig. 6E). UDCA supplementation significantly increased intraepithelial Tregs (Fig. 6E). Intraepithelial and lamina propria activated CD8 T cells are decreased in HFFC offspring and UDCA supplementation increased this population in HFFC offspring lamina propria (Fig. 6F). Intraepithelial and lamina propria activated CD T cells are decreased in HFFC offspring and UDCA supplementation exhibited a trend towards an increase this population in HFFC offspring lamina propria (Supplemetary Fig. 4). These findings support that UDCA supplementation reestablishes the weaning reaction in HFFC offspring including increasing specific lymphocyte populations in both the epithelium and lamina propria.

Another critical component of the interaction between the intestine and microbiome is the intestinal barrier including the intestinal mucus layer and the epithelial barrier. The intestinal barrier genes, Tjp1, Tjp2, and Ocln, are decreased in HFFC offspring (Fig. 6G). Ocln and Tjp1 expression are increased by UDCA supplementation, while Tjp2 expression is further decreased by UDCA (Fig. 6G). There is also an increase in Jama in UDCA supplemented offspring (Fig. 6G). Expression of Muc2 decreased in the ileum of HFFC offspring compared CON (Fig. 6H). Supplementation with UDCA increased Muc2 expression in HFFC offspring ileum (Fig. 6H). UDCA in the neonatal period has varying effects on factors associated with the intestinal barrier in HFFC offspring. Taken together, our data suggests that maternal HFFC disrupts the weaning reaction in offspring, which can be rescued by UDCA.

MODE increases hepatic inflammation and fibrosis during development of colitis

We previously reported that MODE offspring develop enhanced ductular reaction and fibrosis consistent with cholestatic liver disease when fed 3, 5-diethoxycarbonyl-1, 4-dihydrocollidine (DDC) diet9. Development of worse cholestatic liver disease was due to an altered microbiome in the offspring as transfer of the microbiome to antibiotic treated mice replicated the phenotype9. While these studies confirm that MODE is a risk factor for worse cholestatic liver disease in the offspring, the pathophysiology of DDC diet induced liver disease does not involve intestinal inflammation as typically seen in primary sclerosing cholangitis associated with inflammatory bowel disease. To evaluate whether MODE induced changes in the gut liver axis are associated with worse liver disease in the setting of intestinal inflammation, we induced colitis in CON and HFFC by giving 2.5% dextran sodium sulfate (DSS) in water (Fig. 7A). Two cycles of 7 days of DSS water were performed with a 7-day period of regular water in between each cycle. Mice in both groups lost weight with each cycle but there was no difference in weight at any time measured between the two groups (Fig. 7B). Liver weight and liver weight to body weight ratio also did not differ between the groups (Fig. 7B). Disease activity index (DAI) was monitored weekly during the experiment to assess for degree of colitis and no difference was observed between the two groups (Fig. 7C). Despite no difference in DAI or colon length, expression of Tnfa in colon was increased in HFFC offspring compared to CON offspring (Fig. 7D). Serum levels of AST and alkaline phosphatase trended higher in HFFC offspring compared to chow offspring (p = 0.0728, Fig. 7E). Histological analysis was performed to evaluate for evidence of liver disease in offspring exposed to DSS. As previously reported, there was no evidence of inflammation on Mac-2 and CD45 staining in chow offspring (Fig. 7F–H). MODE offspring exhibited clusters of Mac-2 and CD45 positive cells in the periportal and midzonal areas (Fig. 7F). Quantitation of Mac-2 and CD45 staining showed a significant increase in MODE offspring (Fig. 7G, H). Gene expression of Mcp1 and Vcam1 were increase in MODE offspring (Fig. 7K).

Picrosirius red (PSR) staining was performed to evaluate fibrosis in the liver of offspring. PSR staining was increased in MODE offspring periportal areas compared to chow offspring (Fig. 7F, I). Gene expression of Col1a1 and Tgfb were also increased in MODE offspring (Fig. 7L). Ductular reaction also occurs in the setting of periportal injury, so we measured CK19 staining and the number of CK19 positive ducts in each group. CK-19 staining and the number of positive ducts was increased in MODE offspring compared to chow offspring (Fig. 7F, J).

To evaluate hepatocyte proliferation, Ki-67 staining was performed on liver of offspring from both groups exposed to DSS. Ki-67 staining of hepatocytes increased in HFFC offspring compared to CON offspring (Fig. 7M). TUNEL staining was performed to evaluate apoptosis and was increased in HFFC offspring compared to CON offspring (Fig. 7M).

We induced DSS colitis in cross-fostered offspring to assess the role of the weaning period. Offspring fostered by an HFFC dam had increased PSR staining compared to HFFC offspring fostered by a CON dam (Fig. 7N). To assess the role of sustained changes in the microbiome, we preformed cecal microbiome transplantation from CON and HFFC offspring to antibiotic treated c57/Bl6 mice and induced DSS colitis in the recipients. Mice receiving CMT from HFFC offspring exhibited increased PSR staining compared to recipients of CMT from CON offspring (Fig. 7O). These findings support that early and sustained changes in the offspring microbiome can contribute to worse hepatic fibrosis in the setting of colitis.

Discussion

Perinatal environmental exposures, including maternal diet, have an effect on the developing offspring with lasting detrimental effects observed into adulthood. Offspring primarily acquire their pioneering microbiome at birth and this continues to develop throughout the neonatal period, thus a variety of factors that can impact the maternal microbiome could have an immediate and long-term impact on the offspring’s health17. Numerous studies have identified that MODE shifts the offspring microbiome development with consequences for the offspring. We have shown that MODE induced changes in the early microbiome attenuate the weaning reaction which is a critical programming event for protection from inflammatory conditions later in life8. We expand on this with the following key findings from our current study: (1) MODE alters neonatal bile acid homeostasis (increase in conjugated bile acids and decrease in secondary bile acids) and hepatic inflammation in a manner dependent on the maternal diet during the lactational period; (2) UDCA supplementation in the neonatal period reestablishes the weaning reaction and increases important gut immune cell populations in HFFC offspring; and (3) MODE impairments in the gut-liver axis are associated with susceptibility to liver disease in the setting of DSS-induced colitis.

A critical path for generational transmission of developmentally programmed phenotypes is via vertical transfer of an altered microbiome or modification of the offspring microbiome via maternal exposures in the neonatal weaning period. These shifts in the early microbiome could drive phenotypes in the offspring via a variety of mechanisms including shifts in offspring immune development. In this study, we identify that MODE prevents development of critical gut immune cell populations including Tregs. Lack of neonatal Treg development has already been linked to susceptibility to colitis, colon cancer, and allergic disease14. Depletion of Tregs in the liver leads to worse fibroinflammatory liver disease18. Our work shows that absence of intestinal Treg development is associated with disruption of the gut liver axis and susceptibility to periportal liver disease during colitis development. We have also previously shown worse cholestatic liver disease in adult MODE offspring in a diet-induced cholestasis model9.

One way the microbiome may contribute to alterations in the gut liver axis is through shifts in the levels of specific gut microbial metabolites. These metabolites can include SCFAs, BCAAs, and bile acids. While we did not see a difference in stool SCFAs or BCAAs in our model, there was a consistent pattern of bile acid shifts in the preweaning offspring including an increase in conjugated primary bile acids and a decrease in secondary bile acids. Microbes in the gut express several different enzymes that drive metabolism of bile acids in the gut. Bile salt hydrolases (BSH), of which there are numerous different variations, are the primary enzyme that deconjugates bile acids in the gut by removing taurine or glycine conjugates. The increase in conjugated species in HFFC offspring we observe indicates a reduction in BSH activity. Of note, BSH activity is known to increase at the time of weaning in mice supporting a developmentally critical role for this metabolic event19. Conversion of primary to secondary bile acids is also driven by enzymes expressed by gut microbes and peaks at the time of weaning in mice19. The presence of reduced secondary bile acids in our model suggests a reduction in activity of the enzymes involved in production of secondary bile acids in HFFC offspring as well.

Secondary bile acids are a known regulator of Treg development and could be an essential mediator of programming this cell population in the weaning period20,21. We assessed whether the reduction in UDCA in HFFC offspring may be mechanistically involved by supplementing two-week-old HFFC offspring with UDCA. UDCA influenced several aspects of the gut liver axis in HFFC offspring, most notably reestablishing the weaning reaction and increasing intestinal Treg numbers. UDCA also increased expression of Muc2 indicating a role in intestinal mucus production and shifted expression of several gut barrier genes. Prior studies have shown that MODE increases offspring intestinal barrier permeability and our studies suggest that UDCA may resolve some of these shifts. Further studies will need to evaluate whether there is a measurable change in gut barrier permeability. It will also be critical to evaluate whether early closure of the gut barrier is beneficial as increased gut barrier permeability in the weaning period may have beneficial effects on the offspring22.

The neonatal liver in rodents undergoes a period marked by hepatocyte proliferation as the organ transitions from its fetal hematopoietic function to its adult hepatic functions. We show here that the period of hepatocyte proliferation is enhanced by MODE and is associated with an increase in hepatic inflammation. Discuss MODE and early liver injury/proliferation. A potential concern with increased inflammation in the setting of hepatocyte proliferation is the acquisition of genotoxic stress that may establish susceptibility to development of hepatocellular carcinoma. A recent study identified a connection between MODE and development of liver cancer in mice23. This susceptibility to tumor development is likely related to an altered offspring microbiome as co-housing prevented the increase in tumor burden in MODE offspring.

Our studies have several limitations. Our design focused on early life microbiome changes (3 weeks of age) and did not fully evaluate the effect of sustained changes in the microbiome on the gut liver axis. However, multiple studies have shown that MODE drives sustained microbiome changes in adult offspring10,11,13. We have also shown that transfer of microbiome from adult offspring to antibiotic treated mice induces shifts in bile acid metabolism and increases fructose induced hepatic steatosis13. It will be important to define the role of sustained changes in the microbiome in our MODE model and ultimately whether prevention of both early life and sustained changes can prevent developmentally programmed phenotypes. This study also does not specifically identify specific microbes that may be playing a role in driving BA metabolism changes at 3 weeks of age. It is also possible that some of the changes in offspring gut BAs are due to differences in breast milk bile acids24. Future work will need to asses the affect on maternal diet on breast milk BA composition. Another limitation of this study is that this study measured the most well described bile acids, but recent work has significantly expanded the number of lower abundance of bile acids that may have physiologically relevant functions25. We also in this study specifically focused on supplementation with UDCA, but we also observe reductions in DCA. It will be important to expand the analysis of the effects of specific bile acids on the weaning reaction and gut immune development to a variety of secondary bile acids.

In summary, MODE alters the perinatal gut liver axis including changes in neonatal bile acid homeostasis and establishment of gut immune cell populations. MODE also induces signs of hepatic injury in the neonatal liver including inflammation and fibrosis. These findings establish a link between MODE-induced shifts in the offspring microbiome and periportal injury which is associated with worsening of this injury in the setting of colitis. The lactation period appears to be the critical window as UDCA supplementation in this time was able to resolve some of the associated gut deficits. Future work will be important to translate these findings to human studies and begin to develop approaches to intervene in early life.

Methods

Sex as a biological variable

Our study examined male and female animals, and similar findings are reported for both sexes. The one exception is that only male mice were used in the DSS colitis model as noted.

Mouse model, husbandry, and animal care considerations

All procedures in this study were approved by the Animal Studies Committee at Washington University School of Medicine and conformed to National Institutes of Health guidelines and reporting consistent with ARRIVE guidelines. Four-week-old female C57Bl/6 J mice were fed an obesogenic diet (HFFC diet) or a control diet (Table 1). Weights of female mice were monitored weekly prior to mating. A subset of dam tissues were collected at the time of weaning including liver and adipose tissue. Tissue and cecal contents were collected from offspring at necropsy at 2, 3, 4, and 5 weeks of age. A subset of offspring was weaned to regular chow prior to colitis induction. The numbers of offspring and number of different litters represented are noted in each figure legend with each dot representing a single animal. For these studies, sample size was based on having at least five litters represented in each group. Body and liver weights were measured at necropsy. For all breeding, potential dams were staged to identify the most likely interval for successful mating. The sire was placed in the cage for only 24 h to limit co-housing effects. Litters of <4 offspring were excluded. Animals were not randomized to groups. All studies were performed in the same animal housing facility, handled by the same individuals, and tissues collected at the same time of day. Animals were regularly monitored by animal facility staff and lab members for signs of distress and humanely euthanized if necessary.

Maternal diets

Cross-fostering of mice

On day of life 1, offspring from CON dams were cross-fostered with an HFFC dam and offspring from HFFC dams were cross-fostered with a CON dam8. Cross-fostering was done synchronously with donor and recipient dams giving birth on the same day. The litter to be fostered was placed in a clean cage and mixed with nestlets from the recipient dam’s cage. The pups were then placed in the recipient dam’s cage and mixed with bedding before returning the recipient dam to the cage. Cages were monitored closely for the next 72 h to identify evidence of rejection by the recipient dam. Tissues from cross-fostered offspring were collected at 2, 3, and 4 weeks of age.

UDCA supplementation

Starting at two weeks of age, pups were orally gavaged with 70 µg/g body weight of ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA, Sigma, St. Louis, MO) daily for 5 days. After the final gavage, mice were fasted for 4 h prior to tissue collection. Body weight and overall appearance were monitored daily. To determine the effect of UDCA administration, serum, liver, ileum, colon, and cecal contents were collected at sacrifice.

Lymphocyte isolation from small intestines

Intraepithelial lymphocytes (IELs) and lamina propria lymphocytes (LPLs) were collected following a modified STARS protocol26. In brief, the small intestine (SI) was excised from mice, Peyer’s patches were removed, and SIs were cut open in longitudinal direction and cut into 5 mm pieces. SI fragments were placed into 20 milliliter (mL) IEL dissociation buffer (DPBS/10% FCS/15 mM HEPES/2.5 mM EDTA) and incubated for 20 min (20 m) at room temperature (RT). IEL dissociation tubes were shaken vigorously at 10 m and 20 m. IEL dissociation buffer was transferred through 70 micrometer (µm) filter into new 50 mL IEL collection tube. Tissue chunks on 70 µm filter were transferred back into original 50 mL IEL dissociation tube and 20 mL of IEL buffer was added. Tissue was incubated for 20 min, vigorously agitated at 10 and 20 minat RT then filtered through 70 µm into previously used IEL collection tube. IEL collections tube was centrifuged, supernatant was removed and tissue transferred into 15 mL tube by pipetting up and down with 10 mL of DPBS + 10% FBS then placed on ice. All remaining tissue from IEL dissociation tubes and on the 70 µm filter were transferred into 15 mL tube containing 10 ml of room temperature LPL digest buffer (RPMI-1640 + Collagenase at 1 milligram (mg) per mL). LPL digest tubes were placed in a 37 °C incubator for 1 h and vigorously shaken at 30 m, 45 m, and 60 m until the tissue became completely dissociated. LPL digest tubes were then spun at 1500 rpm for 5 m, supernatant removed, and cells were washed with 10 mL of R10 (RPMI + 10% FBS). IEL collection tubes and LPL digest tubes were then spun at 1500 rpm for 5 min then resuspended in 40% Percoll in R10. Tubes were spun at RT 850 g for 20 m with lowest brake setting (not zero). After spin, supernatants were poured out and cell pellets resuspended in 1 ml of R10 for cell counts prior to FACS analysis.

Flow cytometry and immune cell analysis

Cells were stained with viability dye followed by Fc Block with 2.4G2. For surface staining, T cells were identified using CD3 then, TCRγδ+ for gamma delta T cells, CD4 or CD8 alpha beta T cells, and B cells were identified by CD19. Intracellular staining utilized fixation and permeabilization Foxp3/Transcription Factor kit. Antibodies and reagents from Thermofisher: anti-CD3ε (145-2C11), anti-γδTCR (GL3) anti-CD19 (1D3), anti-CD45 (30-F11), anti-CD69 (H1.2F3), Fixable Viability Dye eFluor e506 (65-0866-18), anti-FoxP3 (FJK-16s), anti-IL-17A (eBio17B7), Transcription Factor Staining kit (00-5521), Biolegend: anti-CXCR3 (CXCR3-173). Samples were analyzed using Cytek Aurora Spectral Analyzer. Flow cytometry files were analyzed using FlowJo v10 software (Tree Star).

Short chain fatty acid analysis

Short chain fatty acids were measured by Microbiome Insights (Richmond, British Columbia)13. Material was resuspended in MilliQ-grade H2O, and homogenized using MP Bio FastPrep, for 1 min at 4.0 m/s. 5 M HCl was added to acidify fecal suspensions to a final pH of 2.0. Acidified fecal suspensions were incubated and centrifuged at 10,000 RPM to separate the supernatant. Fecal supernatants were spiked with 2-Ethylbutyric acid for a final concentration of 1 mM.

Extracted SCFA supernatants were stored in 2-ml GC vials, with glass inserts. SCFA were detected using gas chromatography (Thermo Trace 1310), coupled to a flame ionization detector. Data are presented as concentrations relative to tissue sample weight.

Branched chain amino acid analysis

The mouse cecal contents were homogenized in water (20 mL/g cecal content). The Leu, Ile, Val were extracted from 50 µL of homogenate with protein precipitation in the presence of d3-Leu, 13C6, 15N-Ile, and d8-Val as internal standards. A quality control sample was prepared by pooling portion of study samples and injected every 5 study samples to monitor the instrument performance. The samples were analyzed with a Shimadzu 20AD HPLC system coupled to an AB Sciex 4000QTRAP mass spectrometer operated in positive multiple reaction monitoring mode. Data processing was conducted with Analyst 1.6.3. The data is reported as the peak area ratios of the analytes relative to the corresponding internal standards. Total BCAA’s are represented as a summation of the peak areas of each individual BCAA.

Bile acid analysis

Individual intrahepatic BA species were measured using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) and tandem mass spectrometry by the metabolomics core at Washington University School of Medicine as we have described13,27. The liver and cecal content samples were homogenized in water (4 mL water/g liver; 4 mL water/g cecal content). A protein precipitation procedure was used to extract bile acids from 0.05 mL of homogenate in the presence of deuterated internal standards (d4-TMCA, d4-CA, d4-GCDCA, d4-GCA, d4-CDCA, d4-DCA, d4-LCA, d4-UDCA, d4-GUDCA, d4-GDCA, d4-GLCA, d4-TUDCA, d4-TLDCA, d4-TCA, d4-TCDCA, d6-TDCA). The extracts were separated by column-switching high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) on a Security Guard Gemini C18 (4 × 3 mm) and ACE Excel Super C18 column (3 μm, 50 × 4.6 mm). Bile acids and their internal standards were detected by an Applied Biosystems Sciex 4000QTRAP tandem mass spectrometer (MS/MS) equipped with an electrospray ion source in the negative ion mode and multiple-reaction monitoring (MRM) detection. The relative quantification of bile acids was provided, and the data were reported as the peak area ratios of the analytes to the corresponding internal standards and normalized to tissue sample weight. The quantities of each BA type are presented as a relative abundance of all bile acids within the sample. Conjugated and unconjugated bile acid peak areas were summed to calculate a conjugated to unconjugated bile acids ratio.

Histology, stains, and immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Tissues fixed in 10% formalin were embedded in paraffin, and 5-μm sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin, picrosirius red (PSR) and for TUNEL (terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling). For immunohistochemistry, sections were rehydrated by passing through xylene, graded alcohol, and distilled water and then stained with select primary antibodies (Mac2 Invitrogen cat#14-5301-85 1:250, CD45 Abcam ab10558 1:150, CK19 Abcam ab15463 1:50, Ki67 Abcam ab15580 1:200) and the Vectastain Elite ABC kit (Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA) protocol9. Antigen retrieval was performed by heating for 15 min in citrate buffer in a pressure cooker, after which we inactivated tissue peroxide, and incubated them with primary antibody (~20 h, 4 °C). Sections were then washed and incubated with biotin-conjugated secondary antibody (30 min) (Vector Laboratories), washed and incubated with ABC reagent, and washed and incubated with 3,3′-diaminobenzidine or alkaline phosphatase reagent. Sections were counterstained with hematoxylin and cover slipped using Cytoseal XYL (Richard Allen Scientific, Kalamazoo, MI). CD45 IHC, Mac-2 IHC, and Ki-67 IHC were quantified using ImageJ to calculate the percent area of the section that was positive. Three representative images were taken (with 10× objective) for each mouse with one image coming from each of three different liver lobes. For PSR, ImageJ was also used to calculate the percent area with 9 representative images of 20x objective. For CK-19, the number of positive ducts were counted per field (10× objective centered on 1 periportal area) with three fields counted across three liver lobes for each animal. For TUNEL, the number of positive cells were counted per field (20x objective). For all quantitative graphs for staining, each data point represents a single mouse.

Microbiome depletion and cecal microbiome transplantation

Male C57Bl6 mice were purchased from Jackson laboratories (Cat#000664) at 6 weeks old and acclimated to our mouse facility for 1 week before antibiotic treatment (to deplete the resident gut bacterial microbiome)9. The ABX mix contained 300 ml of sterile water to which vancomycin (150 mg, Chem-Impex, Wood Dale, IL), neomycin (300 mg, Sigma, St. Louis, MO), and artificial sweetener (2 g, Equal, New Providence, NJ) were added. The antibiotic water was provided for 60 h followed by 12 h of water containing polyethylene glycol. Cecal contents were collected from 6-week-old male CON and HFFC litters and placed in PBS diluted with glycerol (adjusted to 16%) to create a slurry at 0.1 g/ml concentration and stored (−80 ◦C) until use. The slurry mix was thawed on wet ice, homogenized with a glass bulb, passed through a 100-μm nylon cell strainer, centrifuged for 3000 rpm, 30 s, and vortexed to create a homogeneous mix 30 min prior to oral gavage of the recipient mice. Microbiome depleted mice were gavaged with stool slurry from CON or HFFC offspring five times over 11 days.

Quantitative PCR analysis

Total hepatic or ileum RNA was extracted and cDNA prepared using an ABI high-capacity cDNA reverse transcription kit with one µg of total RNA8. Real-time quantitative PCR used cDNAs from at least six animals per group and was performed in duplicate on an ABI 7500 sequence detection system using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA) and appropriate primer pairs which were ordered from Integrated DNA technologies (Table 2). Relative mRNA abundance is expressed as fold change to CON group after normalization to GAPDH.

Induction of colitis

Male offspring were weaned to a chow diet and at the age of 8 weeks, were exposed to 2.5% Dextran Sodium Sulphate (DSS) solution in drinking water to induce colitis. Offspring were exposed to two 7-day cycles of DSS with 7 days of regular water in between (Fig. 1). Disease activity (DAI) index for colonic disease severity was monitored each week. DAI was calculated as the combined scores of weight loss, blood in the stool, and stool consistency with a maximum score of 12. Weight loss (0 point = No weight loss or weight gain, 1 point = 1–5% weight loss, 2 points = 5–10% weight loss, 3 points = 11–18% weight loss, 4 points = >18% weight loss); stool consistency (0 point = normal and well formed, 1 points = soft but still formed, 2 points = soft, 3 points = very soft, wet, 4 points = watery diarrhea); and blood in stool (0 point = normal color stool, 1 points = hemoccult negative, 2 points = no visible blood but hemoccult positive, 3 points = visible blood, 4 points = rectal bleeding).

After 21 days, mice were euthanized, the length of colon was measured, and blood and tissues were collected for histopathologic and molecular analysis. Body and liver weights were measured for all offspring at the time of tissue collection.

Statistics and reproducibility

Two-way ANOVA with post-hoc comparison or unpaired Student’s t tests with Welch’s correction were used when appropriate using GraphPad prism software and noted in the figure legend. Data are presented as means (SD or SEM) with two-tailed p < 0.05 representing significance and two-tailed p > 0.05 and <0.10 representing a trend. Outliers were identified using the ROUT method in Prism with the coefficient Q set at 1%. All authors had access to the data and have reviewed and approved the final manuscript.
