Quaternary Carbons from Amines and Acids
Authors: Ruheng Zhao, Shengchun Wang, Pingsen Shi, Sandip Kumar Das, Sijing Meng, Aiwen Lei, Tim Cernak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Carbon–carbon bonds are the most frequently encountered bonds in drugs, natural products, agrichemicals, and perfumes. This makes reactions that can access C–C bonds, particularly those that access sterically complex architectures, of high impact to multiple industries and applications. Common C–C bond-forming reactions such as the Suzuki coupling or Diels–Alder cycloaddition have revolutionized synthesis, yet the building blocks required to perform these reactions, such as boronic acids or dienes, are only available in modest diversity from commercial suppliers compared to other common building blocks. A reaction that makes densely functionalized C–C bonds from broadly available starting materials would facilitate deeper access into chemical space. Here, we demonstrate an sp3–sp3 C–C coupling method that uses amines and carboxylic acids, which are among the most broadly available building blocks in vendor catalogs. Our method achieves the coupling of tertiary carbon centers to access dense carbon architectures including from complex pharmaceutical and natural product substrates. Preactivation of both the amine and acid functional groups enables an iron catalyst to unite the carbon-based building blocks under reducing conditions, which were identified using high-throughput experimentation. Extensive electron paramagnetic resonance measurements highlight the intermediacy of radicals and the spin state transformation of iron during the reaction. Our amine-acid coupling establishes a method for navigating chemical space that complements the current toolbox of carbon–carbon bond-forming reactions.

Amines and carboxylic acids are among the most broadly available building blocks from commercial and natural sources. − The reaction most often used to unite amines and acidsthe amide couplingis used extensively to create pharmaceutical products, , for instance, atorvastatin, indinavir, imatinib, and semaglutide all contain amide bonds. Reactions that could repurpose amines and acids to create bond types other than amides would enable exploration of pockets of chemical space that remain inaccessible in the current synthetic chemistry toolbox. To understand how new reaction methods impact chemical space access, we have been mapping − the conceivable amine-acid coupling space and revealed that there are nearly 56 million possible ways to unite a simple amine and a simple acid (Figure A), even though very few such reactions have been so far achieved experimentally. , The value of developing an amine-acid coupling toolkit can be seen in analyses of commercially available building block catalogs, where there is little overlap of structural motifs among feedstocks bearing common functional reactive handles like amines, acids, halides, or boronates (Figure B). To develop an amine-acid coupling toolkit, recent efforts from our group and others have explored new ways of uniting amines and acids by forging esters, , ethers, amines, rearranged aldehydes and carbon–carbon bonds − as complements to the classic amide coupling. Our results demonstrate that a toolbox of diverse amine-acid coupling reactions can serve as a new lever in modulating the properties and functions of molecules. ,,,, In the current study, we developed mild reaction conditions that enable a robust C–C coupling of amines and acids to produce quaternary carbon centers based on an iron-catalyzed radical–radical open-shell substitution mechanism (Figure C). Our earlier reports on amine-acid C–C couplings (Figure D) detailed nickel-catalyzed methods for both alkyl–alkyl , and alkyl–aryl couplings, but in the former sense, sterically congested substrates exhibited low yields. Subsequently, we used active transfer learning as an artificial intelligence tool to optimize reaction conditions toward sterically congested couplings from alpha secondary amines and secondary acids; however, even under these conditions, quaternary carbon centers were unsuccessful.

Radical-based coupling paradigms offer a complementary solution, because steric hindrance can be tolerated in open-shell manifolds. In particular, bimolecular homolytic substitution (SH2) has emerged as a powerful conceptual platform for constructing congested C­(sp3)–C­(sp3) bonds: a carbon-centered radical engages a metal–alkyl intermediate to forge a new C–C bond in a step that is formally analogous to SN2 but proceeding through radical substitution. Recent contributions from Sanford, MacMillan, − Baran, Shenvi, − Zhang, − and Koh have demonstrated that nickel, cobalt, and iron catalysts can mediate SH2 couplings across a wide range of radical progenitors, enabling unions that would be difficult to achieve through traditional polar logic. To further our interest in amine-acid coupling methods that forge congested C–C bonds, we considered the SH2 reaction manifold using activated amines and acids as radical precursors.

We envisioned that creation of quaternary carbon centers via a SH2 C­(sp3)–C­(sp3) bond-forming reaction could be achieved via reductive cross-coupling of tertiary carboxylic acids activated as redox-active esters (RAEs) and primary alkyl amine-derived pyridinium salts. ,− In this design, both coupling partners serve as efficient radical precursors, as supported by recent studies. − The metalloporphyrin catalyst functions as a radical sorting center, ,,, selectively binding the primary alkyl radical via thermodynamic and kinetic preferences. The resulting weak metal–alkyl bond is then poised for bimolecular substitution by a nucleophilic tertiary radical, generating the desired quaternary C–C bond.

Based on the proposed mechanistic framework, we initiated a high-throughput experimentation (HTE) , campaign to optimize the reaction conditions. HTE was employed as a powerful platform to navigate a myriad possibile catalyst and additive combinations. RAE 1, derived from Cbz-4-methylpiperidine-4-carboxylic acid, and pyridinium salt 2, derived from benzylamine, were selected as model substrates. A 96-reaction array was designed using the phactor software, featuring 12 transition-metal catalysts (iron, ,,,, nickel, − ,, and cobalt ,,, ) known to mediate SH2 processes, four hydrosilanes or metal-based reductants, and conditions with or without added base (Figure , entry 1). Among the catalysts evaluated, iron porphyrin Cat-1 was identified as the most effective and was selected for further optimization. Metal reductants like solid manganese outperformed hydrosilanes, likely due to their higher redox potential, or involvement in distinct mechanistic pathways based on heterogeneity. ,, The presence of base also proved to be critical for efficient conversion, potentially by facilitating substrate activation and enhancing catalyst turnover. The initial "hit" conditions included iron porphyrin Cat-1 as catalyst, K2CO3 as base, and Mn as the reductant in acetonitrile (MeCN) at 60 °C, and the desired product 3 was obtained in 9% 1H NMR yield upon scale-up to 0.2 mmol. A comprehensive screening of cosolvents was subsequently conducted to tune the redox potentials of the substrates and the stability of the corresponding radicals, thereby optimizing reaction kinetics. In this 24-reaction screen, the performance of various bases, particularly their solubility, was also evaluated across different solvents (Figure , entry 2). This effort led to the identification of a binary solvent system composed of 1,2-dichloroethane (DCE) and acetonitrile. Subsequent exploration of additives revealed that NEt3·HCl and water significantly improved the reaction performance by suppressing formation of a homocoupled dibenzyl byproduct (Figure , entry 3). Further refinement of the temperature, reagent stoichiometry, and additive loadings ultimately lead to the optimal conditions of 7 mol % Cat-1, 25 mol % NEt3·HCl, and 2 equiv of water in DCE/MeCN (7:3) at 50 °C, under which the coupling product 3 was obtained in 70% isolated yield (Figure , entry 4).

With the optimized conditions in hand, we explored the substrate scope of the reaction (Figure ). A range of benzylic pyridinium salts successfully coupled with 1, particularly substrates bearing electron-deficient or moderately electron-rich substituents (4–11). A reaction conducted on a preparative scale delivered 3 in 59% yield (see the Supporting Information for details). In contrast, strongly electron-rich benzylic pyridinium salts afforded compromised yields, and primary pyridinium salts proved unreactive even after extensive reoptimization. This limitation is attributed to radical formation from primary pyridinium salts that is incompatible with overall reaction kinetics. Various alkyl and cyclic tertiary carboxylic acids also serve as compatible coupling partners (12–17). Tertiary benzylic radicals (12, 14, 16, 17), known to be challenging due to their relatively low nucleophilicity, also performed efficiently under the coupling conditions. For instance, 14 was produced in 61% yield from 2, and the RAE derived from a fragment of the cough suppressant drug fedrilate. Notably, α-oxy esters (15, 18, and 19), where radicals are generated adjacent to phenoxy groups, engaged efficiently in the coupling, providing access to sterically hindered ether scaffolds.

Given the prevalence of quaternary centers in many bioactive compounds, we next examined the applicability of this method in late-stage diversification. This catalytic system was successfully applied to multiple druglike and natural product derivatives, including bezafibrate (18), fenofibric acid (19), gemfibrozil (20), (−)-cis-myrtanylamine derivative (21), a tribromsalan derivative (22), and dehydroabietic acid (23). The reaction displayed broad functional group tolerance, accommodating ethers (7, 14, 15, 18–20), aryl halides (fluorides 8, chlorides 5, 19, and bromides 22), amides (18, 21, 22), ketones (19), esters (22), and tert-butyl carbamates (8, 12, 13). Pre-existing stereocenters within the structures (21, 23) were retained under mild reaction conditions in settings where stereochemical erosion was possible based on a SET-mediated radical mechanism, as in the formation of 23 in 60% yield, which we isolated as a single diastereomer likely due to the rigid and congested carbon backbone. ,

In certain substrate combinations, substantial homocoupling byproducts were observed alongside the desired C–C coupling products, particularly for complex molecules bearing α-oxy ester substituents (15, 19). The increased homocoupled byproduct formation likely arises from a kinetic mismatch between radical intermediates. To address this issue, the reaction temperature and RAE stoichiometry were optimized for specific substrates. Lowering the temperature and adjusting equivalents of RAE improved radical matching and increased yields in these cases.

Next, to understand the elementary steps in the reaction process, we conducted extensive EPR experiments (Figure ). First, spin-trapping experiments were performed to determine the radical generation step. Due to its unique resolution for different types of alkyl radicals, the 2-methyl-2-nitrosopropane (MNP, 24) dimer was used as the spin-trapping reagent. As shown in Figure A and B, the simulated EPR spectra for MNP trapping of a benzyl radical (I–III, a N = 15.4 G, a H = 8.5 G, a H = 8.5 G) and a primary radical (IV, a N = 15.6 G, a H = 9.6 G, a H = 9.6 G) matched the observed spectra, indicating that Mn and NEt3·HCl alone could generate benzyl radicals from 2, 25, 26, and 27 under the reaction conditions.

For compounds 28–30, we did not observe spin-trapped benzylic or primary radicals under these conditions by EPR, observing instead only the products of MNP decomposition (a N = 15.4 G). , This is consistent with our reactivity data, which showed only trace formation of products from 31 and no product formation from 29 or 30 while 2 to 3 gave high yields of coupled products. Additionally, the generation of primary C-centered radicals from 2 (a N = 15.4 G, a H = 8.5 G, a H = 8.5 G) was also observed during the reaction with 1 under standard conditions (Figure C). These results reveal that radicals can be generated by Mn reduction but are affected by substrate structure.

Moreover, to provide complementary evidence and to detect the radical intermediates derived from RAEs, spin-trapping experiments were conducted using 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline N-oxide (DMPO). The formation of carbon-centered radicals from 2 was clearly observed as 34 (a N = 14.5 G, a H = 20.9 G) with DMPO as the spin trap under Cat-1 catalysis (IX, Figure D). For RAE 1, a composite signal was detected (X, Figure D). Deconvolution analysis revealed contributions from a DMPO-trapped carbon-centered radical 35 (a N = 13.5 G, a H = 22.9 G), a nitrogen-containing species 36 (a N = 14.5 G, a H = 19.5 G, a N = 1.9 G), and an oxidized DMPO signal (a N = 13.6 G). We believe the N-centered species (36) may arise from the imide nitrogen of the RAE’s decomposition product. Taken together, these results demonstrate that both primary and tertiary carbon-centered radicals can be generated under Fe- and Mn-promoted reductive conditions.

Next, EPR experiments at liquid nitrogen temperature were conducted to investigate the interaction between the iron catalyst and pyridinium salt 2 (Figure E). A sample of pristine Fe­(TPP)Cl exhibited prominent EPR signals at g ≈ 6 (broad) and g ≈ 4.3 (sharp) (XI, Figure E), characteristic of high-spin Fe­(III) (S = 5/2). The broad feature at g ≈ 6 arises from the Kramers doublet under predominantly axial zero-field splitting (E/D ≈ 0), while the sharp signal at g ≈ 4.3 corresponds to the rhombic limit (E/D ≈ 1/3). Upon introduction of Mn and 1, the EPR spectra exhibited a decrease of the high-spin Fe­(III) signals at g = 4.3, accompanied by the appearance of a broad, featureless resonance centered at g ≈ 2 (XII, Figure E). These spectral changes are consistent with a spin-state transition from high-spin (S = 5/2) to low-spin (S = 1/2) Fe­(III), indicative of a modification in the axial coordination environment of the iron center. Moreover, the EPR spectra for the reaction of Cat-1 with Mn, 1, and 2, revealed the disappearance of the low-spin Fe­(III) signal (g ≈ 2) and the restoration of the high-spin Fe­(III) signal at g ≈ 4.3 (XIII, Figure E). The preferential consumption of the low-spin Fe­(III) species during the catalytic reaction suggests that the low-spin Fe­(III) species is more readily engaged in the reaction, potentially serving as the catalytically competent intermediate. Overall, all EPR results support our hypothesis that radicals can be formed by single-electron reduction of the activated amine and activated acid precursors, which then react with the iron-center to achieve the C­(sp3)–C­(sp3) cross-coupling by a SH2 mechanism.

We propose the catalytic cycle for reductive quaternary C­(sp3)–C­(sp3) bond formation as follows: under the Mn reductive system, RAE 1 and pyridinium salt 2 undergo single-electron reduction to generate a primary C-centered radical and a tertiary radical, respectively. The iron catalyst is expected to react preferentially, via radical sorting, with the primary radical to form an alkyl–iron complex, which subsequently undergoes SH2 attack by a tertiary radical to furnish the desired product (see Figure B). Based on these EPR findings and recent advances in amine-acid activation, we recognized that this catalytic system could also be applied beyond coupling at the benzylic position of amines. This would vastly expand the chemical space accessible by amine-acid C–C coupling. Because the reduction of primary pyridinium salts is known to be challenging and typically requires conditions distinct from those used for benzylic fluoropyridinium salts, , we shifted the activation to use primary RAEs and tertiary pyridinium salts, taking advantage of the similar redox potentials of electron-poor benzylic pyridinium salts relative to tertiary pyridinium salts (Figure ). Under these conditions, the primary carboxylic-acid-containing molecule oxaprozin (37) was successfully diversified with the fluoropyridinium salt of 38, affording a quaternary cyclopropane center (39).

We also realized that the similar redox potentials of primary and secondary RAEs relative to tertiary RAEs might allow this methodology to extend beyond primary–tertiary coupling, enabling primary–primary (42) and primary–secondary (44) amine-acid couplings. Remarkably, a tertiary–tertiary coupling to form 47, with two vicinal quaternary centers, was also achieved under alternate catalytic conditions. Through extensive studies, we revealed distinct cobalt metallophotoredox catalyzed conditions for this challenging coupling based on a CoBr2 and 3CzClIPN system under blue light irradiation. While the yield of 47 was only 15%, this initial result highlights the distinct reactivity profiles and complementary capabilities of each metal (Ni, Fe, and Co) across different substrate combinations, pointing to further opportunities to expand the amine-acid C–C coupling toolbox.

In summary, we have developed a mild, feedstock-based strategy for constructing densely functionalized C­(sp3)–C­(sp3) bonds bearing quaternary carbon centers via SH2-enabled cross-coupling of pyridinium-activated alkyl amines and carboxylic acid-derived RAEs. The protocol enables access to sterically congested architectures and demonstrates a diverse substrate scope, excellent functional group tolerance, and utility in the late-stage diversification of bioactive molecules. The mechanistic studies elucidate substrate-dependent reactivity and reveal opportunities to move into new pockets of chemical space. Overall, this work advances the amine-acid coupling paradigm and adds a valuable approach to the synthetic toolbox for constructing three-dimensional carbon frameworks.
