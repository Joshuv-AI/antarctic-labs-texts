Introduction

Advancements in surgical approach, implant design, and intraoperative technologies have aimed to optimize total hip arthroplasty (THA) outcomes and minimize complications [1–6]. Robotic-arm assisted THA, particularly when performed with a direct anterior approach (DAA), has gained popularity by facilitating enhanced acetabular component positioning and reliable restoration of hip biomechanics, with potential for earlier functional recovery [2, 7–10].

Among innovations in femoral implants, short metaphyseal-filling stems have been developed to preserve proximal bone stock and minimize stress shielding compared with conventional femoral stems [11]. These designs have demonstrated stable fixation with excellent survivorship and improvement in functional scores across studies up to 10 years of mean follow-up [12–14]. Collared stem designs further aim to reduce subsidence by distributing load across the calcar, limiting early migration [15]. However, outcomes can vary markedly between different stem designs, and implant-specific data remains essential to assess safety and efficacy [16].

The Insignia stem (Stryker, Mahwah, New Jersey) is a short, collared, and triple-tapered metaphyseal-filling design. In an early prospective analysis of 123 patients, Rainey et al. reported consistent implant positioning and improvement in patient-reported outcome measures, with no periprosthetic fractures, periprosthetic joint infections, or dislocations [17]. While these results are encouraging, a follow-up period of six months precludes conclusions on longer-term implant survivorship, clinical outcomes, and complication rates.

The present study extends follow-up of the initial cohort to a minimum of two years to evaluate (a) functional outcomes (i.e., Hip Dysfunction and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score for Joint Replacement [HOOS-JR]), (b) implant-related complications (all-cause revision, periprosthetic joint infection, periprosthetic fracture, and dislocation), and (c) survivorship. Building upon early findings, this investigation provides implant-specific evidence on the two-year survivorship of this short metaphyseal-filling collared femoral stem in robotic-arm assisted DAA THA.

Methods

Study design and patient selection

This prospective cohort study received Institutional Review Board approval. Between April 26, 2022, and May 9, 2023, a single fellowship-trained arthroplasty surgeon performed primary THAs through a direct anterior approach (DAA) with robotic-arm assistance. All cases used an Insignia metaphyseal-filling collared femoral stem and Trident II acetabular component (Stryker, Mahwah, New Jersey). Preoperative computed tomography (CT) scans were obtained for three-dimensional planning with the Mako Total Hip application (MAKO Surgical Corp. [Stryker], Fort Lauderdale, Florida). The Mako system guided femoral neck cut height, acetabular reaming, component placement, and intraoperative version assessment. All acetabular components were press-fit with line-to-line reaming, and all patients were allowed to weight-bear as tolerated from postoperative day zero.

Demographics

The follow-up cohort included 123 patients who underwent THA. An additional three patients from the original cohort were excluded because no data were captured beyond the prior study’s six-month endpoint (they had died of unknown causes not related to the prosthesis). Patients were 71% women and had a mean age of 69.2 years (range 37–90) and a mean body mass index (BMI) of 28.2 (range 18.1–49.1). Mean follow-up was 2.6 years (range 2.0–3.4) among the 107 patients (89%) who had at least two years of follow-up. Femoral morphology was classified as Dorr B in 72.9% of cases, Dorr A in 18.8%, and Dorr C in 8.3%. Baseline demographics are summarized in Table 1.

Outcome measures and data analyses

Demographic variables, including sex, age, laterality, BMI, ASA score, and Dorr femur type, were compiled using descriptive statistics. Functional outcomes were assessed using the Hip Dysfunction and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score for Joint Replacement (HOOS-JR) at final follow-up and compared to baseline scores using Student’s t-tests. Implant-related complications of interest included all-cause revision, periprosthetic joint infection, periprosthetic fracture, and dislocation. The rates of outcomes were calculated using the 107 patients who had at least two years of follow-up.

Results

Patients demonstrated significant and sustained improvement in functional outcomes at a minimum of two years postoperatively. The HOOS-JR score increased from a mean of 54.5 ± 13.9 preoperatively to 94.4 ± 10.3 at final follow-up (P < 0.001). Discussion

This study extends the follow-up period of a short, collared, triple-tapered metaphyseal-filling stem from six months to a mean of 2.6 years (range 2.0–3.4), demonstrating durable fixation, sustained functional improvement, and low complication rates [17]. The HOOS-JR scores remained significantly improved relative to baseline, confirming that six-month functional gains translate to maintained patient function at two years. Rates of both all-cause revision and periprosthetic joint infection remained low at 1.9 and 2.8%, respectively, without any periprosthetic fractures or dislocations. Implant survivorship was excellent at 98.1%. These findings indicate continued clinical efficacy and stability of this stem beyond the early postoperative period to a minimum of two years.

Preclinical and radiographic analyses have established a consistent design-to-performance rationale for this implant. Modeling with the Stryker Orthopaedic Modeling and Analytics (SOMA) computed tomography database demonstrated optimized metaphyseal engagement across variable femoral morphologies [18]. Bench testing by Carlson et al. verified the initial stability of the triple-tapered geometry [19]. An early radiographic analysis of 59 hips by Rainey et al. followed patients up to six weeks postoperatively [20]. The authors confirmed excellent collar seating and proximal fill in both the coronal and sagittal planes, supporting effective load transfer and metaphyseal fixation. In the initial six-month clinical series of 123 robotic-arm assisted DAA THAs, Rainey et al. reinforced these findings, demonstrating accurate version assessment, stable alignment, and absence of implant-related complications [17]. The present two-year results confirm that these early advantages persist, with continued improvement in patient-reported outcomes and low mechanical complication rates.

Broader registry and multicenter analyses further support these outcomes. Dunleavy et al. reported a 1.4% reoperation or readmission rate and periprosthetic fracture and infection rates below 0.5% across 443 robotic-arm assisted posterior THAs with a mean follow-up of 8.2 months [21]. In an American Joint Replacement Registry analysis of 92,025 patients, Neitzke et al. observed a two-year all-cause revision rate of 1.3% for this stem, significantly lower than for non-cemented collarless and cemented stems (all P < 0.001) [22]. Regarding two-year periprosthetic fracture, a rate of approximately 0.2% was observed, mirroring the current cohort’s excellent outcomes [22]. Collectively, the above studies demonstrate reliable fixation and low mechanical complication rates across study designs, surgical approaches, and datasets. The collared metaphyseal-filling configuration of this stem likely contributes to this performance by promoting proximal load transfer and minimizing subsidence, while the triple-tapered geometry enhances stability across variable femoral shapes.

There are several potential limitations to this study. It represents the experience of a single fellowship-trained arthroplasty surgeon highly experienced in robotic-arm assisted DAA THA, which may limit generalizability to other surgical approaches or surgeons who have other levels of familiarity with robotics. Although the data were collected prospectively, analyses were retrospective without randomization or blinding. Future multicenter studies may incorporate surgeon variability and randomized comparisons to better define generalizability and survivorship.

This short, collared, metaphyseal-filling femoral stem demonstrated sustained functional benefit, durable fixation, and low complication rates at a minimum two-year follow-up. When used with robotic-arm assisted DAA THA, it provides reliable positioning and metaphyseal stability, supporting its continued use as a safe and effective bone-preserving option in cementless THA.
