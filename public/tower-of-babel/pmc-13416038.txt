Enhancing crop yield prediction accuracy with a novel interpretable deep learning model: MHCNN-LSTM-MHA
Authors: Ibrahim Ahmad Cheema, Muhammad Kashif Hanif, Asad Muhammad Ashraf Khokhar, Muhammad Awais Ur Rehman, Rida Zaheer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurate and reliable crop yield prediction before harvest is essential for informed decision-making, food security, resource optimization, climate change mitigation, and smart agricultural systems. Deep learning has significantly improved accuracy in predicting crop yields as compared to conventional methods. However, some challenges such as unstable gradients, single-model constraint, limited generalizability, and lack of interpretability still persist. Gradient instability is particularly acute in agricultural settings, where early-season weather anomalies influence final yield through long physiological lags, yet back-propagated signals in deep hybrid architectures often vanish or explode before reaching the responsible input layers. These gaps necessitate an innovative and composite problem solving approach. This paper presents a novel MHCNN-LSTM-MHA model which fuses multiple deep learning paradigms of Multihead Convolutional Neural Networks, Long Short-Term Memory, and Multihead Attention Mechanism to improve the prediction accuracy. This study employed a comprehensive dataset on U.S. soybean crop with features like weather conditions, soil properties, management practices, and historical yields. We tested the proposed model against single component models (CNN, LSTM, CNN-LSTM) and benchmarked models (CNN-RNN, Interaction Regression, CNN-DNN). The proposed MHCNN-LSTM-MHA model achieved an RMSE of 3.75 bushels per acre and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2$$\end{document} of 0.905, showing a 9.86% improvement over the previous best benchmark on the same dataset. This performance is attributed to the advanced hybrid architecture that effectively model complex feature interactions and dependencies. The four-headed MHA improved generalizability and enhanced interpretability by dynamically prioritizing important features and time steps, while mitigating bias, in alignment with crop growth dynamics. SHAP-based interpretability revealed agronomically consistent feature importance, showing that crop yield in more sensitive to variations in weather components (precipitation, solar radiation, vapor pressure) exhibiting higher impact on yield outcome and moderately sensitive to soil properties (pH, Clay content). The contribution of this work is the enhancement of predictive accuracy alongside SHAP-based interpretability, which is essential for advancing explainable AI in crop yield prediction and facilitating its integration into smart agriculture systems.

Introduction

Accurate crop yield prediction is integral in tackling food security challenges and maintaining a steady supply1. Anticipated predictions at the regional and national levels enhance decision making2. For example, policymakers are better placed to make certain decisions on imports and exports when there are reliable yield estimates. Farmers can manage their finances more efficiently and seed companies can evaluate performance of new seed varieties in different environments, enhancing competitiveness in agribusiness.

Enhancing crop yield forecasting for wide areas and at lower costs is crucial to ensure food security, especially with the growing population, decreasing arable land, and stern effects of climate change3. There has been numerous initiatives in the last few decades targeting global hunger and address the issues4. In spite of the last fifty years’ advancement in crop production, almost 800 million people are food insecure worldwide5. Understanding by this, the United Nations’ 2030 agenda for sustainable development highlights the goal of eradicating hunger and improving food security6. Farmers and traders are stakeholders within the agricultural industry and the importance of prediction accuracy of crop yield is equal for all. In the case of farmers, knowing the accurate yield better helps them in planning their finances and resource allocation on an operational and economic level. Timely identification of issues in the production process allows changes to be made during the process of production which benefits everyone7. Only realizing these challenges is not straightforward as it is impacted by weather, soil type, fertilizer rates, crop phenotypes, and seed types and cultivars8. In addressing these needs, smarter and more sustainable practices of agriculture alongside better food security can be achieved globally through accurate prediction of crop yield alongside considering the complexities of interaction among the impacting factors.

The agricultural sector is now transitioning to the use of precision agriculture and smart farming, which utilize technology and analytics. One of the major obstacles to the advancement of smart agriculture is defining realistic crop yield goals for every field segment well in advance of planting time9. To meet this challenge, tremendous alterations in farming are needed, particularly by modern advances in technology and the application of Artificial Intelligence (AI). Along with being a key factor in effective grain policy formulation, precise crop yield forecasting is a precondition to effective field management on the micro level10. Yield forecasting has to be on time and precise so that the needed timely practices, like irrigation, fertilization and pesticide application, will not waste input resources but rather improve productivity and protect the environment11. In addition, accurate early-season forecasts have the potential to greatly enhance production efficiency and profitability12. Accurate predictions contribute significantly to plant breeding programs by helping to detect high yielding genotypes from a pool of promising candidates in a cost effective manner13,14. With the properly tackled challenges, there is an opportunity to improve crop yield prediction, which would enable smart agricultural practices that ensure sustainability and resilience in view of the global food security challenge.

The connection between prediction accuracy and sustainability operates through three points. First, when a farmer knows the expected yield range, the marginal return on the last unit of fertilizer or irrigation water can be computed against that forecast rather than against a naive historical average. This reduces over-application, which in turn cuts nitrous oxide emissions and groundwater withdrawals. Second, county-level forecasts with 90-day lead times allow grain handlers to right-size transportation and storage, reducing the post-harvest spoilage that currently wastes roughly 14% of global cereal production. Third, early identification of yield-limiting weather patterns gives breeders and extension agents time to substitute heat-tolerant varieties or adjust planting windows, shortening the feedback loop between climate stress and management response. Without accurate prediction, each of these decisions is made under uncertainty, and the risk-averse default is to over-apply inputs and over-capacity infrastructure.

Traditional and outdated procedures for forecasting crop yields in the growing season, like field visits and basic statistical models, are not accurate and strong enough to back data driven decision making in modern agriculture15. However, emerging data capture technologies such as remote sensing, IoT sensors, satellites, UAVs, paired with powerful Machine learning (ML) models, helped to open new horizons for yield prediction transformation. Pattern recognition and strong accuracy improvement solutions provided by Deep Learning (DL), which is a branch of AI, simplify these processes tremendously. Remarkable success of ML and DL in numerous data-driven fields like facial recognition, genomic engineering and language processing proves its capabilities16,17. Its adoption continues to grow at a rapid pace in agriculture, enabling tasks such as crop yield prediction, crop type classification, disease identification, and yield modeling18. Unlike conventional statistical methods that depend on a fixed response function for each set of input features, ML and DL techniques excel because they can analyze large heterogeneous data sets to extract intricate patterns and correlations that are often overlooked. This possibility makes the most powerful algorithms essential in improving the accuracy of crop yield forecasting19. The innovation brought by these new paradigms enables intelligent, accurate forecasts that lead to optimal resource allocation and enhanced decision-making strategies in agriculture.

In the last few years, DL models have shown notable progress in adapting to crop yield prediction which has increasingly been boosted by accurate predictions over time. For example, non-parametric models are especially powerful in capturing complex relationships between input features, allowing for more accurate predictions8. As new DL models are being developed and new data sources become accessible, there is need for dependable forecasting for multiple crops and regions around the world. Most models, however, suffer from the so-called black box problem, where the results achieved are difficult be interpreted or explained. Filling those gaps in accuracy with explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) transforms modern agriculture into a more reliable and efficient domain by revealing the reasons behind predictive performance, making it more smart, agriculture-friendly and XAI capable, thus empowering decision-making.

Hybrid architectures have shown considerable potential for crop yield forecasting. Existing approaches have shown three persistent shortcomings. Single-model designs–whether CNN or LSTM in isolation–fail to adequately capture the interplay between spatial heterogeneity and temporal dynamics. Static feature weighting limits how well these systems adapt to shifting conditions across growing seasons. The opacity of conventional deep learning undermines trust among the stakeholders who stand to benefit most from accurate predictions.

The proposed framework tackles these constraints through three core design choices. Rather than forcing weather and soil data through a shared encoder early in the pipeline, we maintain separate convolutional streams (W-CNN and S-CNN) that preserve the distinct characteristics of each modality from input through to feature extraction. For temporal modeling, we depart from single-context attention mechanisms in favor of four specialized heads, each attending to different phenological windows–early vegetative growth, mid-season stress periods, grain filling, and late maturation. Finally, we embed interpretability at the architecture level: attention heatmaps reveal which time steps drive specific predictions, while SHAP decomposition quantifies how individual weather variables and soil properties contribute to the final output.

Review of literature

Crop yield prediction stands out as an important area of modeling research for informed decision-making, food security and resource optimization. However, the complex, heterogeneous nature of agricultural data necessitates a critical examination of existing modeling approaches to identify their inherent limitations. Precisely forecasting the yield of crops is very complex due to multifarious factors affecting the yield, the relationship among which is intricate. In an effort to tackle this challenge, researchers utilized ML and DL approaches, each with their own methodologies and potentials. This section reviews the contributions made so far, noting gaps, while making a case for more innovation aimed at improving crop yield predictions.

The primary focus of the earlier works has been the application of traditional techniques with more straightforward classical ML algorithms for yield prediction like LASSO. It also included Support Vector Regressor (SVR), Random Forest (RF), and others, with XGBoost being the most accurate20. Furthermore, the superior performance of XGBoost was noted against Ridge Regression, thus validating the strength of XGBoost as a reliable ML model for agricultural practices21. The DL methods have intertwined greatly with their ability to complexly structure and hierarchically pool high-level features from disparate datasets. For example, Convolutional Neural Networks integrated with Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs) to combine image data for estimating apple yields and successfully detected fruit and mapped the yields22. These methods were built upon by Faster Region-based CNNs (Faster R-CNN) which were used to develop yield maps with UAV images of citrus and almond crops23. Regarding soybean yield prediction, multi-sensor data was paired with feature extraction using RF and XGBoost where the latter model proved more useful24. Other research showed that prediction of soybean yield during out-of-season and in-season is improved when using integrated CNN and LSTM networks as the two capture spatial and temporal features, thus the hybrid CNN-LSTM model built with Google Earth Engine outperformed CNN and LSTM models alone25. Hybrid architectures have proved to be effective in taking advantage of the strengths of multiple methods26,27. Recent hybrid architectures integrating deep learning paradigms for plant disease classification further illustrate the agronomic value of modality-specific feature extraction and attention-based temporal weighting28. Using guided back-propagation for feature selection and interpretability CNN-RNN model for soybean yield prediction was the best among the others29. Likewise, it is noted that Stacked LSTM networks utilized in rice yield forecasting works better than traditional approaches like ARIMA and ANNs30. In addition, GBCNet and DeepCropNet were developed for predicting grape and corn yields, respectively, integrating the benefits of CNN and LSTM networks31,32. These studies illustrate the effectiveness of hybrid deep learning models and its application in agriculture, but further research is necessary in feature relevance and model interpretation.

For a long time, the focus in crop yield modeling has been on transforming the raw inputs like satellite images and climate data into the more predictive forms. A feature fusion deep neural network (DNN) architecture integrated at both input and intermediate levels to estimate grain yield illustrates the concept of integrated data harvesting33,34. CNNs are poor at capturing spatial relationships of wheat sub-plots, so feature vectors were proposed as inputs to the fully connected layers of a DNN35. A DNN was used to predict potato yields with weather and soil factors, while role of irrigation was considered in predicting yields36. More predictive models for soybean and maize yields were developed using DNNs combined with a form of Transfer Learning to increase modeling accuracy37. Reinforcement learning was integrated with deep learning in a Deep Recurrent Q-Network model to estimate paddy yield38. A Q-Learning algorithm was developed that would give estimations of crop yield based on parameters which were passed through an RNN by a linear mapping of Q-values.

In recent years, deep learning techniques have shown remarkable capabilities in pattern recognition by automatically modeling rich complex multi-modal data39. Nonetheless, the application of one model to multi-dimensional agricultural data comes with problems such as feature diversity, time dependencies, gradient variability, overfitting, and non-interpretability. These issues are multi-fold and need a sophisticated hybrid model which works with CNNs to capture spatial features and LSTMs to incorporate temporal information while also using attention mechanism to dynamically identify dominant features with least bias. These models’ black-box issues also need to be solved for transparency and trust using SHAP technique, which is suggested as a useful method40. Such an approach not only enhances the explainability of model outcome but also contribute to increase the accuracy of crop yield prediction, providing a strong base for making data-driven decisions and transforming smart agriculture systems. Khan et al.41 fuse multi-scale CNN features with a context-aggregation block to segment heterogeneous farmland parcels from UAV imagery; their ablation shows that simultaneous encoding of fine and coarse context halves the boundary error rate. Likewise, Bakr et al.42 compare six learning-based recommender systems and demonstrate that attention-augmented networks outperform static rule-based engines when varietal choice must adapt to real-time soil and weather feeds. Both studies validates our design choice of coupling spatial feature enrichment with dynamic attention, but they target either land-cover segmentation or input recommendation rather than county-level yield forecasting. Our work extends the same architectural philosophy to temporal crop-response modeling and embeds a multi-head mechanism that explicitly disentangles short-, mid- and long-season signals.

Despite these advances, a critical gap remains in architectural design. Existing hybrid models predominantly employ serial processing (CNN→LSTM) that collapses heterogeneous spatial features before temporal modeling, and single-head attention that cannot simultaneously attend to multiple phenological stages. The proposed MHCNN-LSTM-MHA distinguishes itself through parallel multi-stream encoding and multi-head temporal specialization—design choices motivated by the need to preserve modality-specific patterns while enabling fine-grained, interpretable temporal weighting.

Exploratory data analysis

In order to ensure the coherence of the analysis, a comprehensive EDA was performed on the dataset47. It consisted of feature distributions and their relations, as well as spatial and temporal trends associated with data analysis. Given the high-dimensional heterogeneous nature of the agricultural feature space, dimensionality reduction techniques were evaluated during exploratory analysis to preserve predictive signal while mitigating redundancy48.

Major points within the analysis considered considering the correlation of the yield with various factors like weather, soil, and management practices. Some techniques like the analysis of the trend of the yield along with other features considered the distributions for outliers removal and evaluated the missing data patterns for selection of the appropriate imputation method.

Data preprocessing

To prepare the dataset for modeling, a preprocessing pipeline that included data cleaning, missing value handling, feature scaling, and normalization was put in place49. Missing numerical values were dealt with through mean imputation. The imputation is performed column-wise: for each weather variable at each week, missing values are replaced by the mean of that variable-week combination across all counties and years in the training split. Soil attributes, being static, are imputed once per county using the county mean. No iterative or model-based imputation is used, to avoid leaking target information.

In order to maintain consistency across variables with different units and scales, feature scaling techniques were used. Standardization (z-score normalization) was used to transform features with different magnitudes, while Min-Max scaling was applied to features that needed normalization to a certain bounded value. Furthermore, log transformation was applied to adjust the effects of skewed distributions. Dimensionality reduction strategies evaluated in time-series forecasting contexts further informed our scaling and normalization choices to prevent feature redundancy from degrading temporal model performance50.

Framework and architecture of proposed model

The architectural design of MHCNN-LSTM-MHA is driven by the need to overcome three fundamental challenges in crop yield prediction: (a) the heterogeneous nature of agricultural data requiring separate encoding pathways for weather and soil modalities; (b) the temporal complexity of crop phenology demanding robust long-range dependency modeling; and (c) the necessity for dynamic, interpretable feature weighting that adapts to varying growth conditions. Challenge is fundamentally a generalization challenge. If weather and soil were concatenated and fed through a shared encoder, the resulting latent representation would couple climatic signals with edaphic signals into a single manifold. When the model encounters a county with similar rainfall but markedly different cation exchange capacity, the shared encoder must simultaneously unlearn and relearn feature associations, degrading transfer performance. By maintaining separate pathways, the model preserves the product structure of the input space, allowing the LSTM to compose region-appropriate predictions from stable, disentangled subspaces rather than from a confused aggregate. The inclusion of these feature domains is not only a matter of data richness; it is a structural prerequisite for generalizability. Weather provides the stochastic climate envelope that drives year-to-year yield variation; soil attributes provide the static spatial boundary conditions that determine how that weather is biologically experienced; management practices capture human adjustment layers such as planting density and timing; and historical yield provides the county-specific productivity trajectory that reflects accumulated genetic and technological trends. Omitting any one of these modalities forces the model to conflate their effects. For example, attributing a yield shortfall to drought when the true cause was a management delay on poorly drained soil. Fusion frameworks that integrate heterogeneous data streams through modality-specific encoders have been shown to enhance predictive performance in high-dimensional analytics domains51, informing our design choice to maintain separate W-CNN and S-CNN pathways rather than collapsing inputs into a shared early representation.

Multihead-CNN feature extraction

CNNs are deep learning networks mostly used for feature extraction which include multiple Convolutional, pooling, and fully connected layers52. CNNs include Convolutional layers which perform feature mapping with the help of kernels. Patterns from the input data are extracted using Convolutional layers and mapping down sampled patterns is achieved in pooling layers where the dimensionality and computational load is minimized. The results are produced by fully connected layers using the output from previous layers. The interplay between Convolutional and pooling layers are principal in feature extraction. Kernels turn input data into feature maps in Convolutional layers as they slide over the data. The output of the kernel is further improved through activation function application. Features defined with these transform functions are refined by pooling layers which de-emphasizes low importance patterns while increasing feature density. CNNs also incorporate single dimensional convolutions within temporal feature capturing processes for sequential data in time series. The efficient transformation and nonlinearity is provided by ReLU activation functions, while crucial feature focus is provided by pooling layers with reduced dimensions. This approach allows CNNs to effectively handle multiple sourced data.

Conventional CNN architectures for agricultural prediction typically employ single-stream processing that concatenates heterogeneous inputs–weather time-series, soil depth profiles, and management variables–into a unified feature map. This approach risks modality conflation, where distinctive spatial patterns from different data domains are obscured by shared convolutional filters optimized for aggregate rather than specific features. The proposed MHCNN architecture addresses this limitation through parallel multi-stream encoding, where dedicated convolutional pathways (W-CNN for weather, S-CNN for soil) preserve modality-specific representations throughout the feature extraction pipeline.

LSTM

Crop yield formation is inherently a sequential process in which environmental conditions during one phenological stage constrain the physiological response of subsequent stages. This phenological dependency mirrors findings from deep learning applications in agricultural population monitoring, where stage-specific temporal encoding proved essential for capturing biologically critical transitions53. The LSTM is introduced into the pipeline specifically to capture four agronomic temporal dependencies that static feature extractors cannot represent: (i) cumulative water and thermal stress that results nonlinearly across the growing season; (ii) stage-specific sensitivities where identical weather anomalies have divergent impacts depending on crop developmental timing; (iii) lagged effects, such as early drought reducing pod set with consequences visible only during seed fill; and (iv) multi-year yield trends that provide a baseline against which current-season deviations are evaluated. These dependencies are encoded through the LSTM’s gating mechanism. This architectural choice aligns with broader evidence that deep recurrent networks, when coupled with robust feature extraction, significantly enhance forecasting accuracy and temporal robustness in sequential environmental data54.

LSTM networks are particularly useful in the modeling of sequential data because of their high non-linear learning capabilities55. While traditional recurrent neural networks (RNNs) suffused the vanishing gradient problem with a gating concept that controls the information flow, LSTMs improve on this further by introducing additional gates. These additional gates: the forget gate, input and output gates, enabled the LSTM to keep and erase information over extensive periods of time; therefore, it is able to capture short and long-term dependencies in time series data. The flow of information within the LSTM is one way only, it starts at time frame t=1 and moves to the final time stamp t=T. The network receives the input data and in addition to the execution of various gate functions, it modifies its cell state. Here, the forget gates decide how much information is retained, the input gate adds new information, while the output gate adjusts the internal state and the portion of information that will help to create the final information. Such a process permits the LSTM to learn temporal patterns and the complex relationships in time series based sequential data.

Multihead attention mechanism

The Multihead attention mechanism is a critical component of the Transformer based architecture because it can capture interaction and dependencies from different parts for a single input sequence56. This is accomplished by projecting the input sequence into three learned representations: Query (Q), Key (K), and Value (V). Each of these vectors is responsible for computing an attention score, which measures the relevance of a particular position with respect to every other position. The model can use multiple attention heads to capture different patterns and relationship in the data, facilitating the processing of complex sequential data. The outputs from the different attention heads are combined, and then a linear transformation is applied to the output to generate the final representation of the data. Such an architecture allows the model to learn complex interactions and meaningful temporal dependencies, hence it works well with time-series forecasting and sequence based problems.

Single-context attention computes one weighted average over time, forcing the model to compress all agronomic signals (planting, mid-season stress, senescence) into a single time-context vector. Multi-head attention relaxes this bottleneck by learning m independent (Query, Key, Value) sub-spaces; each head can specialize on a different phenological window without mutual interference. MHCNN-LSTM-MHA crop yield prediction modeling

Accurate prediction of crop yield requires the pattern recognition of time-series data which, by its nature, has dependencies in sequence and complex interrelations between different environmental and soil as well as management features. Conventional deep learning models like LSTM networks are adept at capturing temporal dependencies, but these models have difficulty remembering context over very long time step sequences, leading to an information loss of the broader context. Also, while CNNs efficiently extract spatial features, they do not capture sequential information52,56,57. To address these challenges, the proposed model MHCNN-LSTM-MHA integrates the strengths of three components: MHCNN for hierarchical spatial feature extraction, LSTM for learning temporal dependencies, and MHA for adaptive feature and time step weighting. The MHCNN extension of simple CNN improves the representation of input features by separately capturing spatial dependencies among variables such as weather and soil attributes, taking advantage of optimized dimensionality.

Agronomically, Equation (22) acts as a stress-decay gate: it learns to discard early-season moisture or temperature anomalies that are subsequently buffered by mid-season rainfall, thereby preventing spurious correlations from persisting in the cell state. Equation (23) functions as a phenological admittance gate, selectively writing critical environmental cues into memory only when they occur within yield-sensitive windows. The cell state \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{c}_t$$\end{document} in Equation (25) therefore accumulates a compressed representation of effective growing conditions, combining cumulative water balance, thermal time, and soil quality as experienced by the crop through successive developmental stages. Finally, Equation (26) regulates how much of this agronomic memory is exposed to the downstream multi-head attention layer at each time step, allowing the model to suppress inactive growth periods and amplify transition phases such as flowering and grain-fill.

An important improvement to the proposed approach is the application of the MHA mechanism that puts relative emphasis on vital portions of the input sequences. It improves the model’s ability to capture long-term dependencies and intricate agronomic interactions by allocating differing attention weights to temporal and spatial features. This attention improves the overall predictive power and generalizability under varying growing conditions, albeit at the cost of feature selection.

Unlike single-stream networks that process either spatial or temporal context, the proposed model keeps two dedicated Convolutional heads (W-CNN and S-CNN) active throughout the forward pass, thereby preserving the full granularity of weather and soil descriptors instead of collapsing them into a single feature map. Compared with serial CNN-LSTM hybrids that simply stack a Convolutional front-end before an LSTM, we insert a multi-head attention layer after the LSTM so that the network can dynamically re-weight both variables and time steps; this relieves the LSTM from carrying long-range dependencies alone and mitigates gradient vanishing without resorting to excessively deep recurrent layers.

The fusion stabilizes training by replacing the unstable multiplicative Jacobian chain of RNNs with the LSTM’s bounded forget-gate highway, decoupled parallel CNN gradients, and attention shortcuts that propagate loss signals directly to phenologically critical time steps. This architectural synergy explains the stable convergence observed in Fig. 5a, where training loss plateaus by epoch 20 without the oscillations typical of deep unrolled networks.

Finally, whereas recent CNN-RNN attention models compute one attention context vector for the entire mini-batch, our four-head mechanism generates head-specific attention maps that are concatenated and linearly projected, allowing the model to simultaneously attend to early-season planting conditions, mid-season weather shocks, and late-season maturation signals–an explicit multi-granular temporal modeling capability that is absent in prior agricultural attention networks. These design choices yield a 9.86% RMSE reduction over the best previously reported CNN-DNN ensemble on the same soybean benchmark, while retaining full interpretability through head-wise attention visualization and SHAP decomposition.

The CNN-RNN framework29 can be abstracted as a serial composition28\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \hat{y}_{\text {CR}} = f_{\text {DNN}}\!\Bigl (\text {RNN}\bigl (\text {CNN}(\mathcal {X}_{w}\oplus \mathcal {X}_{s}\oplus \mathcal {X}_{m})\bigr )\Bigr ), \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where ⊕ denotes early concatenation. Because the single CNN front-end must learn kernels that simultaneously respond to precipitation spikes (high-variance, temporal) and clay gradients (low-variance, depth-stratified), the feature map fed to the RNN is already a compromised average. The RNN cell, lacking the LSTM’s forget gate highway, suffers amplified gradient decay during back-propagation across the T=5 phenological stages, so early-season planting conditions exert weak influence on the final prediction.

The CNN-DNN ensemble2 discards recurrent modeling entirely:29\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \hat{y}_{\text {CD}} = f_{\text {DNN}}\!\Bigl (\text {Pool}\bigl (\text {CNN}(\mathcal {X})\bigr )\Bigr ). \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Time is flattened into static feature columns before the DNN stage, which means the model cannot represent the sequential dependency between, for example, a week-8 drought and its week-12 manifestation in reduced grain fill. It learns only marginal correlations, missing the lagged agronomic causality that determines final yield.

By contrast, the proposed model preserves modality-specific streams through parallel encoders, propagates them through an LSTM with bounded cell states, and applies a four-head attention layer that computes head-specific context vectors:30\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \textbf{h}_{\text {attn}}^{(i)} = \sum _{t=1}^{T}\alpha _{t}^{(i)}\textbf{v}_t, \qquad i\in \{1,2,3,4\}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where each head i specializes on a distinct phenological window. The robustness gain is quantified by the ablation study: removing the MHA layer and reverting to a feed-forward aggregator raises RMSE by 0.42 bu/acre. The adaptability gain follows from the fact that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha _{t}^{(i)}$$\end{document} are input-dependent; in a drought year the model up-weights mid-season rainfall anomalies, whereas in a high-yield year it up-weights solar radiation during grain-fill, an instance-specific flexibility that static DNN weights cannot provide.

Evaluation metrics

To measure the performance of the crop yield prediction model, the evaluation metrics used are the Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), Coefficient of Determination (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2$$\end{document}), Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Mean Squared Error (MSE), and Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE)58. Both RMSE and MSE are commonly used for gauging prediction error; RMSE, however, is more sensitive to larger discrepancies. MAE, on the other hand, provides the average absolute value of error, which, unlike RMSE, is easier to understand and measure in the context of model accuracy. The \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2$$\end{document} metric assesses the proportion of variance in the observed data that is explained by the model and, therefore, reflects the model’s explanatory power. At the same time, MAPE defines accuracy of the prediction in percentage terms, which can easily be used in comparison for other data sets regardless of scale. Through the use of these diverse and complementary metrics, the model’s predictive ability and the robustness of its forecasting are thoroughly assessed.

We note that confusion matrices, ROC-AUC, and PR-AUC are standard classification diagnostics that require a discrete class label. Because crop yield is a continuous response y ∈ [5.00, 82.30] bu/acre, thresholding into arbitrary “high” versus “low” categories would discard the quantitative agronomic information required for operational decision-making (fertilizer rates, forward contracting, insurance pricing). We therefore restrict the primary analysis to regression-appropriate metrics.

All experiments were implemented in Python 3.9.16 using TensorFlow 2.9.0 (with Keras API) on Google Colab Pro instances equipped with NVIDIA Tesla T4 GPUs. The full dependency stack is: NumPy 1.21.6, Pandas 1.3.5, Scikit-learn 1.0.2, Matplotlib 3.5.3, Seaborn 0.12.2, and SHAP 0.41.0. To ensure reproducibility, we set a global random seed of 42 across TensorFlow (tf.random.set_seed(42)), NumPy, Python’s random, and Scikit-learn prior to every fold initialization. Deterministic GPU operations were enabled via tf.config.experimental.enable_op_determinism(), which sacrifices some performance for bit-wise reproducibility of training loss curves.

The raw dataset contains weekly weather measurements for 52 weeks. The MHCNN-LSTM-MHA model is built in Keras Functional API as follows. The weather stream input is Input(shape=(5,312)), passed through Conv1D(128, 9, padding=’same’, activation=’relu’) followed by AveragePooling1D(pool_size=2). The output is flattened per time step and fed to Dense(40, activation=’relu’, kernel_initializer=’he_normal’). Soil stream uses Conv1D(32, 9, padding=’same’, activation=’relu’) and AveragePooling1D(pool_size=2), then Dense(40, activation=’relu’). Both streams use L2 kernel regularization with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda = 10^{-4}$$\end{document}. The management and yield-prior inputs bypass convolution and concatenate directly with the CNN outputs, producing a tensor of shape (N, 5, 93).

The LSTM layer is LSTM(64, return_sequences=True, dropout=0.2, recurrent_dropout=0.0). We do not use recurrent dropout because it conflicts with enable_op_determinism() on GPU. The LSTM output of shape (N, 5, 64) feeds into MultiHeadAttention(num_heads=4, key_dim=16, dropout=0.0). The attention block uses no additional dropout beyond the LSTM’s 0.2. A Lambda layer extracts the last time step (N, 64), which is passed to Dense(1, activation=’linear’) for the final yield prediction in bushels per acre.

The loss is mean squared error (MSE). Optimization uses Adam with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta _1=0.9$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta _2=0.999$$\end{document}, and a global learning rate of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10^{-3}$$\end{document} (no warmup, no cosine decay). Batch size is 64. Training proceeds for a maximum of 100 epochs with early stopping monitored on validation MSE, patience=10. The best validation checkpoint is restored. No learning-rate reduction on plateau is used. Gradient clipping is applied with clipnorm=1.0 to prevent rare exploding-gradient events during the first 2–3 epochs. On the Tesla T4, one training run takes approximately 1.45 hours for the full 36,590-sample dataset; inference latency is 1.1 ms per county-year.

To isolate the specific contribution of the multihead convolutional design, we conducted controlled ablations while holding the LSTM (64 units) and MHA (4 heads) components constant. The result aligns with the theoretical motivation: agricultural variables exhibit distinct spatial structures–weather as temporal sequences, soil as depth-stratified profiles–that benefit from specialized rather than shared feature extraction.

Practical applications

The MHCNN-LSTM-MHA architecture enables distinct value propositions across agricultural stakeholder groups. Early-season yield forecasts (Weeks 12–16) support input optimization decisions—fertilizer application rates, irrigation scheduling, and harvest labor allocation. The low inference latency permits real-time integration with farm management information systems, while SHAP-identified drivers (solar radiation, vapor pressure, precipitation) enable targeted interventions rather than uniform resource distribution.

County-level aggregation supports logistics planning 2–3 months pre-harvest, reducing storage and transportation inefficiencies. These logistics gains are also climate gains. When a county elevator knows that expected output is 38 ± 3.75 bu/acre rather than a vague 30–50 bu/acre interval, the fleet size and dryer capacity can be matched to the predicted volume. The resulting reduction in empty truck miles and excess drying fuel is small per acre but scales across the 29.69 million hectares in the dataset. Similarly, the SHAP-identified drivers—solar radiation, vapor pressure, precipitation—allow targeted rather than blanket irrigation. A farmer who learns from the model that mid-season vapor pressure deficit (VPD) is the dominant yield constraint can apply deficit irrigation precisely during the weeks when VPD peaks, rather than maintaining uniform soil moisture throughout the season. Field studies suggest that such precision-water strategies can reduce pumping energy and associated CO\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_2$$\end{document} emissions by 15–30% without sacrificing yield. In this way, the interpretability of the model is not a post-hoc luxury; it is the mechanism by which prediction accuracy is converted into on-farm conservation action.

The model can be deployed across diverse agro-ecological zones without region-specific retraining because the multidimensional representation explicitly separates climate-driven variance from soil-driven variance. A logistics coordinator in Iowa and one in Arkansas can use the same model weights; the LSTM states and attention maps simply re-weight the locally relevant soil and weather features at inference time, an adaptability that would be impossible if the model had learned a single, region-averaged feature map. The \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2=0.905$$\end{document} reduces uncertainty sufficient for forward contracting and price risk management. Chronologically validated robustness (2010–2018 test period) provides actuarial confidence for parameterizing yield-indexed insurance products, with SHAP explanations satisfying regulatory transparency requirements. Multi-head attention specialization identifies environmental conditions favoring specific genetic backgrounds, enabling cost-effective genotype screening and targeted variety recommendations for specific agro-ecological zones.

Analysis of model limitations

Despite its strengths, the MHCNN-LSTM-MHA model has several limitations. A major practical constraint is data quality and access; temporal gaps and missing elements introduce uncertainty that statistical imputation cannot fully resolve. Regional differences in climate, soil taxonomy, and farming practice also create challenges for naive global deployment.

However, the architecture is explicitly designed to mitigate these generalizability limitations through three transferable strategies. First, the modality-decoupled encoders (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\phi _w$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\phi _s$$\end{document}) learn physically grounded representations of weather and soil that are conserved across crops and regions, enabling partial freezing and fine-tuning. Second, the four attention heads learn canonical phenological abstractions rather than crop-specific calendar dates, so the same head configuration transfers from soybean reproductive stages to corn silking or wheat heading with only minor temporal recalibration. Third, SHAP interpretability provides a diagnostic layer: feature-importance inversions between source and target domains are immediately visible in the summary plot, preventing silent failures during transfer.

While the corn zero-shot ablation and chronological validation demonstrate promising transfer, systematic evaluation across rice, wheat, and sorghum–and across tropical versus temperate soil taxonomies–requires future datasets. Integration with mechanistic crop models (DSSAT, APSIM) offers a promising hybrid path: the deep learning components handle spatial heterogeneity while the process-based components enforce physiological constraints in data-scarce target domains.

Although the model has limitations but it still steers towards innovation in crop yield prediction. Improving data incorporation, design of explanation systems, and wider scope validation will improve smart agriculture systems and sustainable food production that increases the scientific confidence and real-world application. Future work could explore advanced imputation techniques (e.g., variational autoencoders).

Conclusion

In this study, we proposed a novel MHCNN-LSTM-MHA model using deep learning that incorporates Multihead CNNs for spatial representation, LSTMs for temporal dependencies, and Multihead Attention for dynamic feature weighting which achieved higher accuracy for soybean yield prediction. The model outperformed the established and baselines CNN, LSTM, CNN-LSTM, CNN-RNN, Interaction Regression, and CNN-DNN by 5.6–18.3% achieving the highest coefficient of determination (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2 = 0.905$$\end{document}) with a minimal error of RMSE = 3.75 bu/a. This confirmed the effectiveness of spatial–temporal learning infused with attention mechanisms. The attention component was crucial in providing interpretability and robustness by reducing overfitting and emphasizing important features useful in capturing different temporal stages. The model’s attributions as verified by SHAP-based explainability aligned with agronomic principles, strongly emphasizing weather (radiation, temperature, precipitation) and soil (pH, clay content) as yield determinants which in turn enhances its trustworthiness and practical utility in smart agriculture. Operational pathways include API integration with USDA data pipelines, web-dashboard visualization with uncertainty quantification, and mobile deployment for field accessibility. The accuracy-to-computational cost ratio–14.19% error reduction for 3.6% parameter increase–enables deployment across resource gradients, from cloud-based research platforms to edge devices in resource-constrained agricultural settings, ensuring equitable access to advanced predictive analytics. The computational efficiency permits commodity-hardware deployment, while interpretability mechanisms address adoption barriers among non-technical stakeholders. Extension to additional crops, real-time sensor integration, and advanced imputation techniques remain priority directions for ensuring equitable agricultural technology access. Although much has been achieved, expanding the diversity of data, generalization for different crops and regions, and real-time computation efficiency still pose challenges. Possible future directions include the use of satellite images and other real-time IoT based sensors as well as adversarial and transfer learning for better adaptability. This work marks a significant step in the use of AI for crop yield prediction by demonstrating that accuracy and explainability can be jointly optimized. The 9.86% error reduction over the prior best benchmark is not merely a statistical improvement; it is a reduction in the uncertainty that currently forces agricultural systems to over-apply inputs, over-build infrastructure, and under-prepare for climate shocks. By identifying which weather variables and soil properties drive county-level yield variation, the model provides a diagnostic tool that can guide climate adaptation—whether through variety selection, planting-date adjustment, or targeted water management—rather than simply producing a number. In that respect, the contribution is to a climate-smart agriculture that uses information to decouple productivity from resource intensity, thereby enhancing food security without expanding agriculture’s environmental footprint. While the model shows promise for soybean yield forecasting, its extension to other crops and regions is supported by the partial-freezing transfer strategy and the phenological abstraction inherent in the four-head attention design. Priority future directions include multi-crop meta-learning benchmarks, integration of real-time IoT and satellite data streams, and hybrid DSSAT–deep learning architectures that combine transferrable neural encoders with process-based physiological constraints.
