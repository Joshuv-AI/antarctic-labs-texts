Efficacy and toxicity of low-doses versus standard-dose enzalutamide in advanced prostate cancer: a real-world study with implications for cancer prevention/interception
Authors: Martino Oliva, Monica Boitano, Alessio Carbone, Irene Maria Briata, Tania Buttiron Webber, Davide Corradengo, Carlo Introini, Matteo Puntoni, Marco Ennas, Federico Germinale, Nicoletta Provinciali, Matteo Clavarezza, Alberto Gozza, Carlotta Defferrari, Mauro D’Amico, Howard L Parnes, Andrea DeCensi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEnzalutamide at standard doses (160 mg/day) is effective in advanced prostate cancer (PCa) but is associated with considerable adverse events (AEs) and high financial costs. Preliminary reports suggest that low/intermediate doses (≤80 mg/day) retain efficacy while reducing toxicity. This study evaluates the efficacy and safety of low/intermediate dose vs standard-dose enzalutamide in a real-world cohort.MethodsThis retrospective observational study included 140 assessable patients treated with enzalutamide for metastatic castration resistant (80%) or sensitive (20%) PCa. Patients were categorized by cumulative average daily dose relative to the standard dose (160 mg/day): low (≤50%, n = 11), intermediate (>50% and ≤80%, n = 16), and high (>80%, n = 113). The primary endpoint was the 12-month progression-free survival (PFS) by restricted mean time. Secondary endpoints included PSA response (decline ≥50% at 3 months), overall survival (OS) at 36 months and worsening of AEs of interest (fatigue, neurological disorders, hypertension).ResultsThere were no significant differences in PFS at 12 months on high vs intermediate dose (10.4 vs 11.4 mo, p= 0.09) and high vs low dose (10.4 vs 9.2 mo, p = 0.37), nor was there on PSA response (70% vs 75% vs 60% on high, intermediate, low doses, respectively). Intermediate or low doses showed no evidence of adverse effects on OS at 36 months. The rate of fatigue worsening on high vs intermediate vs low dose was 61.1%, 63.0% and 27.3% (p = 0.03 for high vs low).ConclusionsLow/intermediate doses of enzalutamide show comparable efficacy than high dose while improving tolerability, indicating the need for further search of its optimal dose. Moreover, our findings prompt a study of the optimal dose in advanced disease and in the prevention/interception setting in patients with low/intermediate risk PCa under active surveillance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-15396-y.Keywords: Adverse events, Dose optimization, Dose-Response relationship, Elderly patients, Enzalutamide, Fatigue, Low-Dose therapy, Progression-Free survival, Prostatic neoplasms, Real-World evidence

Introduction

Prostate cancer (PCa) is the most frequent cancer in men in the United States, with 299.000 new cases estimated in 2024, and the second leading cause of cancer-related death [1]. Since 2014, the PCa incidence rate has risen by 3% per year, mostly driven by 4–5% per year increases for regional-stage and distant-stage diagnoses that began as early as 2011 [2]. In Italy, there were more than 41.000 diagnosed cases in 2023 and 8.200 deaths, accounting for nearly one-fifth of all cancers detected in men, and over 560.000 prevalent cases of men living with the disease [3].

Enzalutamide is a new generation oral androgen-receptor selective inhibitor (ARSi) that induces tumor regression and growth suppression in patients with metastatic prostate cancer. Several randomized studies have demonstrated the efficacy of enzalutamide in treating prostate cancer [4–8]. Initially approved by the FDA in 2012 for patients with metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer who had previously received docetaxel [9], its indications were updated in October 2020 to include patients with castration-resistant prostate cancer [10] experiencing biochemical relapse, or with metastatic castration-sensitive prostate cancer [11, 12]. However, the standard recommended dosage of 160 mg/day has multiple side effects and dose reductions are often required [13]. Moreover, enzalutamide proved to be effective in a phase III trial in patients under active surveillance (AS), but toxicity was considerable [14].

In a phase I study, enzalutamide (MDV3100) was administered at doses ranging from 30 to 600 mg/day. There was a linear increase in steady state serum concentration with dose, but antitumor effects were observed at all doses, including 40–60 mg/day. The severity of treatment-related adverse effects, including fatigue and neurological disorders, was associated with drug dose [15]. Importantly, the FDA has recently launched the Optimus project with the goal of optimizing drug dose across oncology given the high risk of clinical and financial toxicity of the majority of anticancer drugs at the registered dose [16].

Recent data suggest the efficacy of enzalutamide may be retained at lower doses with significant improvement in safety and tolerability [17]. In two preliminary observational studies in metastatic PCa, low dose enzalutamide (≤ 80 mg per day) did not show difference in overall survival (OS) and prostate-specific antigen progression-free survival (PSA PFS) compared to standard dose (160 mg per day). Interestingly, there was a trend towards a better PSA response and a significantly longer longevity among the low dose group [18, 19].

Our aim is to expand those preliminary studies by analyzing a retrospective cohort of patients with metastatic prostate cancer treated in routine clinical practice with different doses. Real-world data play a critical role in informing cancer care across diverse clinical settings and patient populations, especially when registration trials do not completely reflect clinical reality, and can generate meaningful clinical insights to inform practice and policy [20]. Beyond clinical outcomes, the financial toxicity associated with enzalutamide is a critical consideration, given its high cost, long-term use in prostate cancer management and pricing-dosing relationship [21–23].

Materials and methods

This is a single-center, retrospective observational study. Eligible patients were adults (≥ 18 years) with a histologically confirmed diagnosis of metastatic PCa who started enzalutamide treatment between August 1, 2014, and December 31, 2023, and provided informed consent for data collection and analysis. Patients were excluded if they had significant cardiovascular conditions, such as recent cerebrovascular events, myocardial infarction, or unstable angina, as well as congestive heart failure of at least NYHA grade II. Other exclusion criteria included the presence of severe concomitant diseases that could interfere with treatment and life expectancy shorter than 3 months. Clinical and treatment data were retrospectively extracted from hospital medical records. The study was conducted in accordance with ethical guidelines and was approved by the local territorial ethics committee. All patients signed an informed consent.

All patients received concomitant androgen deprivation therapy with LHRH agonists in addition to enzalutamide, ensuring that any toxicity related to androgen deprivation was evenly distributed across dose groups.

Patients were categorized by cumulative average daily dose relative to the standard dose (160 mg/day): low (≤ 50%, n = 11), intermediate (>50% and ≤ 80%, n = 16), and high (>80%, n = 113). Dose categorization was based on the average daily dose over the treatment duration. For example, a patient receiving 80 mg/day consistently was classified as 50% (low dose). Baseline differences among the three dose groups were assessed based on data distribution. For continuous variables, one-way ANOVA was used when assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance were met, while the Kruskal-Wallis test was applied for non-normally distributed variables. Categorical variables were compared using Pearson’s chi-square test, and in cases where expected frequencies were below 5, Fisher’s exact test was used. The primary endpoint was the 12-month progression-free survival (PFS) by restricted mean survival time (RMST) to account for violation of proportional hazards assumption often seen in observational studies. RMST is defined as the area under the survival curve up to a specific time point and can be interpreted as the average survival time or life expectancy ranging from time 0 to a specific follow-up time point, which is straightforward and clinically meaningful [24, 25]. Secondary endpoints included PSA response (decline ≥ 50% at 3 months), OS at 36 months and worsening of AEs of special interest such as fatigue, neurological disorders and hypertension. AEs were assessed at treatment initiation based on clinical documentation. Worsening was defined as an increase in CTCAE grade or clinician-reported symptom progression during the study period. PFS and OS were adjusted for ECOG performance status at baseline. The restricted mean attained age (RMAA) was also utilized to compare overall longevity among dose groups. All statistical analyses were performed using Stata SE 18.5 MP (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA).

Results

A total of 143 patients who received enzalutamide treatment during the study period were identified. Subsequently, three patients were excluded due to missing data, early death within a week after treatment initiation, or non-compliance with therapy, resulting in a final cohort of 140 assessable patients. Of these, 80% were treated for castration-resistant PCa (mCRPC) and 20% for castration-sensitive disease (mHSPC). The three dose groups were not different at baseline on PSA, body mass index (BMI), Gleason Score, age, castration resistance and previous docetaxel therapy (Table 1). Age at the start of enzalutamide therapy was ≥ 75 years in 109 cases (78%), ECOG performance status was ≥ 2 in 4 cases (2.9%). The choice of low-dose treatment at baseline was primarily driven by clinical judgment in 82% of cases (performance status (PS) > 0 or age > 80), whereas the intermediate dose group mainly consisted of patients (63%) who required dose reduction due to toxicity after starting at full dose. In the intermediate dose group, 10 out of 16 patients were initially treated with the standard 160 mg/day regimen and underwent dose reduction after a mean duration of 5 months. The main reasons for dose reduction were grade 3 fatigue (n = 5), grade 2 fatigue (n = 2), increase in transaminases (n = 1), and, in one case, physician advice. Of the 10 patients with the dose reduction after the onset of adverse event (fatigue mainly), 7 out of 10 had an improvement whereas 3 were unchanged. The median follow-up time was 35.5 months [IQR, 16.9–51.2]. There were no significant differences in PFS at 12 months on high vs. intermediate dose (10.4 vs. 11.4 mo, p = 0.09) and high vs. low dose (10.4 vs. 9.2 mo, p = 0.37, Fig. 1). There was no difference among dose groups in PSA response (70% vs. 75% vs. 60% on high, intermediate, low, respectively).

Discussion

The results of our retrospective study suggest that reduced or intermediate doses of enzalutamide (≤ 80%) maintain clinical efficacy comparable to the standard 160 mg/day dose in patients with advanced prostate cancer, with a significantly improved tolerability profile, particularly regarding fatigue, and to a lesser extent, neurological disorders and hypertension. The observed benefit in terms of toxicity reduction, without an apparent compromise in progression-free survival or PSA response, supports the hypothesis of a dose-dependent effect on adverse events, as previously suggested in early-phase studies [18, 19].

These results are also in line with early clinical data from the phase 1–2 study by Scher et al. [15], which demonstrated enzalutamide activity across all tested doses, including 60 mg/day, with PSA responses ≥ 50% in over half of the patients. The dose–response curve plateaued between 150 and 240 mg/day, reinforcing the hypothesis that higher doses may not provide incremental benefit and instead increase toxicity, particularly fatigue. This saturation effect underscores the clinical relevance of lower doses, including 60 mg/day, for which substantial PSA response and PET imaging activity has been shown [15].

These findings align with the principles promoted by the FDA’s Project Optimus, an initiative aimed at redefining the paradigm of dose selection in oncology. The project advocates for shifting away from the traditional “maximum tolerated dose” approach of chemotherapy agents in favor of identifying the optimal dose, one that achieves efficacy while minimizing toxicity (“minimal effective dose”). The importance of patient-centered dosing strategies in oncology has also been emphasized, reflecting the need to focus on individualized dosing that considers patient well-being and quality of life [26]. This perspective is particularly relevant for treatments like enzalutamide, where dose reductions are often necessary in clinical practice to manage side effects such as fatigue. Incorporating real-world data into clinical decision-making can provide valuable insights into how patients tolerate and respond to different dosing regimens, ultimately leading to more personalized and effective cancer care for a high-cost drug often used in chronic settings [16].

Ongoing clinical trials are also exploring ARSi dose optimization. For example, the ANZadapt phase II randomized study is comparing patient-specific adaptive versus continuous abiraterone or enzalutamide therapy in metastatic CRPC (ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT05393791) [27].

Our findings are consistent with two prior observational studies by Vinh-Hung et al. [18, 19]. In their initial study, elderly patients (≥ 75 years) with metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer who received enzalutamide at ≤ 80 mg/day had comparable PSA response rates (67% vs. 45%) and similar progression-free survival (11.2 vs. 11.9 months) to those receiving standard doses, despite being clinically more frail and having more comorbidities [18]. In a subsequent analysis with a larger sample of 111 patients, the authors not only confirmed comparable survival outcomes but also reported a significant increase in clinical longevity—measured as restricted mean attained age in the low dose group (89.1 vs. 83.8 years, Δ = 5.3 years, p = 0.003). Interestingly, this longevity benefit was also seen across age subgroups, both under and over 75 years [19].

In line with these observations, our study showed no significant differences in PFS or PSA response between dose groups, yet we observed a slightly higher 36-month survival rate in the intermediate dose group (RMST + 5.1 months vs. standard, p = 0.03), and a significantly lower rate of worsening fatigue in the low dose group (27.3% vs. 61.1%, p = 0.03). These findings provide a foundation for future research aimed at identifying the optimal dose that balances efficacy, tolerability, and financial sustainability in advanced disease.

The predominance of patients with ECOG 0–1 in our cohort reflects national clinical practice and reimbursement criteria, which generally restrict the use of enzalutamide to patients with preserved performance status. This explains why even elderly and heavily pretreated patients had good baseline functional status.

In this context, our results may also have important implications for prevention and interception strategies in patients under AS for low- or intermediate-risk prostate cancer. Indeed, the ENACT randomized clinical trial evaluated the effect of enzalutamide 160 mg/day monotherapy versus AS alone in patients with localized low- or intermediate-risk prostate cancer. The study showed that enzalutamide significantly reduced the risk of pathological or therapeutic progression by 46% (HR, 0.54; 95% CI, 0.33–0.89; p = 0.02), increased the likelihood of negative biopsy at 1 year (OR, 3.5; 95% CI, 1.76–6.92; p < 0.01), and delayed PSA progression by 6 months. However, these benefits came at the cost of substantial toxicity: over 55% of patients experienced fatigue, and 37% developed gynecomastia, with 12% requiring dose reductions due to adverse events [14]. Given the chronic nature of AS and the excellent prognosis of these patients, the toxicity profile of full-dose enzalutamide limits its uptake in this setting. New-generation ARSi such as darolutamide, which has demonstrated survival benefits with a favorable safety profile and lower rates of fatigue and neurological adverse events [28, 29], may represent valuable alternatives in the future. Nevertheless, optimizing enzalutamide dosing—particularly in view of its forthcoming generic availability—remains relevant to ensure broader accessibility and cost-effectiveness.

In light of our findings that lower doses of enzalutamide retain clinical activity while significantly reducing fatigue and neurological toxicity, it is plausible to hypothesize that a reduced-dose approach could offer a more tolerable and sustainable chemoprevention strategy in the AS population. A clinical trial of low-dose enzalutamide in patients on AS is being planned.

Limitations

This study has several limitations inherent to its retrospective design. The non-randomized dose assignment introduces a potential for selection bias, although baseline characteristics were generally balanced across groups. The small sample sizes in the low- (8%) and intermediate-dose (12%) arms, along with the single-center setting, may limit the generalizability of our findings. Although patients in the low- and intermediate-dose groups had undergone fewer prior lines of systemic therapy, there were no significant differences across groups in prior docetaxel exposure or the proportion of patients with castration-resistant disease, which reduces the likelihood of major confounding by these factors. This is consistent with a recent systematic review, which reported that although reduced enzalutamide doses may preserve efficacy and improve tolerability, available studies remain heterogeneous, retrospective, and statistically underpowered [30]. Finally, we were unable to fully account for unmeasured confounders such as comorbidities.

Conclusions

In our real-life observational cohort, low- and intermediate-dose enzalutamide showed no evidence of reduced efficacy compared with full-dose therapy, while being associated with improved tolerability, particularly in terms of fatigue. Given the retrospective design, the small sample sizes of the reduced-dose groups, and the single-center setting, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Nevertheless, they support the rationale for prospective studies aimed at defining the optimal enzalutamide dose in advanced prostate cancer, as well as exploring the feasibility of reduced-dose strategies in the prevention/interception setting for patients under active surveillance.
