Pediatric necrotizing pneumonia caused by mixed infection with influenza A virus and Panton-Valentine Leucocidin-producing Staphylococcus aureus: case report and literature review
Authors: Yuanyuan Chen, Yuqi Wang, Xiaofen Tao, Mingming Zhou, Yuanjian Sheng, Dehua Yang, Yingshuo Wang, Yunlian Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMixed infection with influenza A virus (IAV) and Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) is a serious concern in children. S. aureus, particularly Panton-Valentine Leucocidin (PVL)-producing strains, is a known cause of severe, necrotizing complications. We reported three cases of severe necrotizing pneumonia caused by PVL-positive S. aureus and IAV infection from March to April in 2023.Case presentationThree previously healthy children presented with acute onset of high fever and dyspnea following IAV infection. Microbiological workup confirmed mixed infection with PVL-positive S. aureus (two methicillin-resistant, one methicillin-sensitive). Radiographic findings included necrotizing pneumonia, atelectasis and pleural effusion in all patients. All three developed necrotizing laryngotracheobronchitis, and one case was complicated by plastic bronchitis. All required intensive care unit admission, with two needing mechanical ventilation. The mainstay of successful management involved a combination of oseltamivir, linezolid, glucocorticoids, therapeutic bronchoscopy, and closed thoracic drainage.ConclusionThis study firstly documented successful treatment of three cases of severe necrotizing laryngotracheobronchitis and necrotizing pneumonia caused by PVL-positive S. aureus and IAV infection. Clinicians should raise awareness of this mixed infection in children with sudden clinical worsening after influenza to provide timely and effective treatment to decrease mortality.

Introduction

Community acquired pneumonia (CAP) is one of the most common causes of hospital admission for children worldwide, particularly among those under five years of age. Most of them usually stay in the hospital for about a week and discharge without complications, while a few severe cases have longer duration of clinical manifestations and length of stay, develop pulmonary and extra-pulmonary complications, and require transfer to intensive care unit (ICU). Historically, Streptococcus pneumoniae has been the predominant bacterial pathogen identified in childhood CAP [1]. However, the epidemiological landscape has significantly shifted following the widespread implementation of conjugate pneumococcal vaccines (PCV) into national immunization programs over the past two decades. This has led to a marked decline in the incidence of vaccine-type pneumococcal pneumonia and empyema, as evidenced by contemporary surveillance studies [2, 3]. Consequently, there is an increased relative importance of other bacterial pathogens, such as Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) and non-vaccine serotype Streptococcus pneumoniae, in complicated pediatric pneumonia cases. S. aureus pneumonia often has relation with influenza A virus (IAV) infection. This association has been observed in epidemiological studies, including a report by Hirani & Macfarlane where a significant proportion of S. aureus pneumonia cases was linked to influenza [4]. Children developing pneumonia caused by S. aureus and IAV are at high risk of rapid clinical deterioration and mortality without prompt, targeted treatment. This severe outcome may result from a destructive synergism between the viruses and the bacteria. The mortality for severe pneumonia caused by influenza and methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) in children admitted to the ICU with respiratory failure has been reported as high as 40% [5]. Panton-Valentine Leukocidin (PVL) is a cytotoxin secreted by S. aureus. The mortality of hemorrhagic necrotizing pneumonia (NP) caused by PVL producing S. aureus has been reported to be 75% [6].

This case report firstly documented successful management of necrotizing laryngotracheobronchitis and NP caused by PVL-positive S. aureus and IAV infection. All patients, despite requiring intensive care, survived with a good prognosis after a combination of antiviral and anti-staphylococcal therapy, glucocorticoids, and aggressive respiratory support. This case series highlights the importance of early recognition and comprehensive management of this serious mixed infection.

Report of cases

Case 1

This 9-year-old girl was healthy before. She had 4 days of fever and 2 days of cough. In the local hospital, she was diagnosed with IAV infection by a throat swab. The white blood count and C-reactive protein concentration were 2.7 × 109/L and 104.13 mg/L. Despite treatment with cefuroxime, piperacillin, azithromycin (targeting the high prevalence of Mycoplasma pneumoniae in Chinese children), and oseltamivir for 2 days, the patient’s condition progressed, with the development of a sore throat, hoarseness, and blood-stained sputum (Fig. 1). On admission of our hospital, her oxygen saturation level was normal with 2 L/min of oxygen via nasal cannula and respiratory rates 32 times per minute. Moist rales were heard over the left lung filed and decreased breath sounds were found over the right lung field. Then she was admitted into ICU. The white blood count was 13.09 × 109/L. The C-reactive protein and procalcitonin concentration were 28.64 mg/L (normal range < 8 mg/L) and 9.730 ng/mL (normal range < 0.05 ng/mL) respectively. The erythrocyte sedimentation rate was 50.43 mm/h (normal range < 20 mm/h). Chest CT scans (Fig. 2) showed NP, local bronchiectasis, right hydropneumothorax, right pulmonary atelectasis and subcutaneous emphysema on the right axilla and chest wall. The following day, a flexible bronchoscopy revealed necrotizing laryngotracheobronchitis characterized by diffuse mucosal ulceration, scattered whitish pseudomembranes, and copious purulent secretions. Bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) was performed using warmed, sterile isotonic saline and BAL fluid culture for MRSA was positive. It was sensitive to vancomycin, linezolid, quinolones, rifampicin, and tetracyclines and its virulence genes (PVL and α-hemolysin) were detected. Thoracocentesis and chest tube insertion were performed with the effective drainage of air and yellow and turbid pleural fluid. Analysis of pleural fluid was performed and results were shown in Table 1. Blood and pleural fluid cultures yielded no growth of bacteria, fungi, or mycobacteria. On day 9, the patient’s condition was stable and transferred to the general ward from ICU. Guided by the bronchoscopic findings of necrotizing tracheobronchitis, indicating a likely S. aureus infection given the clinical context of influenza, the child was treated with a targeted regimen of linezolid and oseltamivir based on our institutional experience and guidelines. This therapeutic choice was subsequently confirmed and continued based on the BAL findings, which identified MRSA and detected the PVL gene. The patient’s condition improved with this therapy, supplemented by methylprednisolone (initiated at 2 mg/kg q12h early, concurrently with the first dose of linezolid and subsequently tapered over a period of one month), thoracic drainage, and flexible bronchoscopy for 3 times, leading to defervescence on day 13 and resolution of cough. The chest drainage tube and nasal cannula oxygen inhalation were removed on day 16 and day 19 respectively. The patient was discharged from hospital on day 22. Case 2

This 9-year-old boy was well in the past. He had fever for 3 days and shortness of breath for 2 days before admission. In the local hospital, the chest CT revealed mucus plugs in the trachea, and the bronchus of the right middle lobe and two inferior lobes, segmental atelectasis, and bilateral pleural effusion. In the emergency room of our hospital, his oxygen saturation level was 90% with mask oxygen inhalation. The pulse rate 150 beats/min and the respiratory rate 52 times per minute. Moist rales were heard in both lung fields. Antigen rapid test for detecting respiratory pathogens revealed that IAV was positive. The white blood count was 6.34 × 109/L. The C-reactive protein and procalcitonin concentration were 290.97 mg/L (normal range < 8 mg/L) and 13.29 ng/mL (normal range < 0.05 ng/mL) respectively. The erythrocyte sedimentation rate was 59.20 mm/h (normal range < 20 mm/h). On day 2, the patient’s dyspnea progressed despite mask oxygen inhalation, and marked hoarseness developed. The patient was subsequently transferred to the ICU. Endotracheal intubation was used to maintain the airway and a flexible bronchoscopy was performed to remove extensive necrotic debris, and plastic sputum plugs with biopsy forceps and a retrieval basket (Fig. 1.). BAL was performed using warmed, sterile isotonic saline. Sputum and BAL fluid culture for methicillin-sensitive S. aureus (MSSA) was positive. The virulence genes (PVL and α-hemolysin) were detected. Repeated chest CT scans (Fig. 2.) showed NP, bilateral pleural effusion, right massive pneumothorax, and subcutaneous emphysema on the right chest wall. Multiple thoracocentesis was performed with the effective drainage of yellow and turbid pleural fluid and a chest tube was placed for drainage of air for 8 days. Analysis of pleural fluid was performed and results were shown in Table 1. Blood and pleural fluid cultures yielded no growth of bacteria, fungi, or mycobacteria. As the patient’s respiratory function improved, he was extubated and weaned from the ventilator on day 9. On day 12, the patient’s condition was stable and transferred to the general ward from ICU. Initial empirical therapy included oseltamivir for influenza and vancomycin for broad Gram-positive coverage. Once the clinical condition improved and etiological evidence was obtained, treatment was switched to the targeted antibiotic linezolid on day 13. The regimen also incorporated methylprednisolone (initiated at 2 mg/kg q12h on day 2 and subsequently tapered over a period of one month), alongside closed thoracic drainage and five therapeutic flexible bronchoscopies. Following this combined approach, the child’s fever subsided on day 17 with marked improvement in cough. And he discharged without chest pain and dyspnea on day 26. A chest radiograph taken at follow-up clinic visits at 1 month showed pleural thickening and improved lung inflammation.

Case 3

A previously healthy 8-year-old boy had fever and cough for 2 days, and repeated hemoptysis for half of the day. He was referred to a local hospital. Cefoperazone sodium and sulbactam sodium, azithromycin (given the high prevalence of Mycoplasma pneumoniae among children in China), and human immunoglobulin was prescribed. However repeated hemoptysis remained difficult to control and oxygen saturation level was below 90% with mask oxygen inhalation after 1 day of antibiotic treatment. He was intubated and transferred to our hospital for further diagnosis and treatment. On admission, the oxygen saturation level was normal with respiratory support. Moist rales were heard in both lung fields. Nucleic acid extracted from throat swab was positive for IAV by real-time polymerase chain reaction. The white blood count was 0.59 × 109/L. The C-reactive protein and procalcitonin concentration were 79.81 mg/L (normal range < 8 mg/L) and 100 ng/mL respectively (normal range < 0.05 ng/mL). The patient was admitted into ICU with mechanical ventilation. Massive blood gushed out from the endotracheal tube and the blood pressure dropped to 74/46mmHg. After shock had been corrected, a flexible bronchoscopy was performed and identified necrotizing laryngotracheobronchitis characterized by diffuse mucosal ulceration, scattered pseudomembranes (Fig. 1.). BAL was performed using warmed, sterile isotonic saline and MRSA was found positive in BAL fluid culture and tested positive for PVL. The next-generation sequencing analysis from sputum and blood both implicated S. aureus as infection. We suspected a co-infection with IAV and MRSA on admission, given the simultaneous detection of both pathogens at the very first BAL, the profound leukopenia suggestive of PVL toxin activity from the outset, and the absence of a clear biphasic clinical course. Chest CT scans (Fig. 2.) revealed NP with atelectasis and pulmonary hemorrhage. Right massive pneumothorax and bilateral pleural effusion were detected. Multiple closed thoracic drainage procedures were performed from day 10. On day 38, the chest drainage tube was removed. As the patient’s respiratory function improved, he was extubated and weaned from the ventilator on day 17, and his oxygen saturation level with mask oxygen inhalation was above 95%. On day 23, the patient suddenly developed recurrent convulsions and persisted for several hours. Thus, he was intubated again. A rapid bedside glucose test was performed and ruled out hypoglycemia. Basic metabolic panels were monitored and did not reveal significant hyponatremia or other gross electrolyte imbalances that could account for the prolonged convulsions. Lumbar puncture and cerebrospinal fluid analysis were performed and did not reveal cranial infection (Table 1.). After 5 days of ventilatory support, the condition was stable and the ventilator was substituted with mask oxygen inhalation. On day 29, mask oxygen inhalation was removed and oxygen saturation level on air was normal. Upon transfer to our hospital, the antibiotic regimen was escalated empirically to cover potential multidrug-resistant bacteria due to clinical deterioration consisting of meropenem and vancomycin, alongside oseltamivir. After the patient’s condition stabilized and Staphylococcus aureus was identified as the causative pathogen, antibiotic therapy was de-escalated to linezolid, which provided targeted coverage based on the antibiogram. Following this comprehensive management, which also included flexible bronchoscopy for 8 times, immunoglobulin (1 g/kg/day for two consecutive days), and methylprednisolone (initiated at 2 mg/kg q12h on day 2 and subsequently tapered over a period of two months), the patient’s body temperature normalized on day 26, with resolution of cough and convulsions. The patient was transferred to the general ward after 34 days of treatment in the ICU and discharged in good condition after 43 days of treatment. The patient was followed up monthly. During follow-up period of more than half a year, the child did not develop recurrent cough, panting, dyspnea, and hemoptysis, and epilepsy can be well controlled under anti-epileptic treatment. A lung function test 1 month after discharge was normal. A chest CT performed 6 months after discharge demonstrated bilateral persistent bronchiectasis.

Discussion

The epidemiology of pediatric CAP has evolved over the past two decades, with S. aureus gaining prominence following the widespread use of PCV [7]. While respiratory syncytial virus is the most common viral pathogen associated with S. aureus infection [7]. Mixed infection with IAV and S. aureus is particularly significant. This association is notable for its potential to cause severe and rapidly progressive diseases.

The timing and nature of mixed infections appeared to differ among our cases. In Cases 1 and 2, the clinical course was biphasic, with a clear deterioration following the initial influenza-like illness, supporting the diagnosis of a secondary bacterial infection with S. aureus. In contrast, the presentation in Case 3 was markedly more fulminant from the onset. The detection of MRSA in the first BAL specimen obtained only three days after symptom onset, coupled with the presence of severe leukopenia (0.59 × 109/L) – a hallmark of PVL-mediated destruction of leukocytes – suggested a true co-infection with both IAV and PVL-positive MRSA from the very beginning. This distinction is critical, as co-infections can drive an excessively robust initial inflammatory response, potentially explaining the uniquely severe and rapid progression observed in this patient.

The link between S. aureus and severe pneumonia during the influenza epidemic has long been recognized [8–10]. In 1998, the first case of NP caused by S. aureus and influenza virus in a heathy young man was reported [11]. The first case of pediatric NP caused by IAV and PVL-positive MRSA was reported in 2005. It was complicated with necrotizing tracheobronchitis revealed by autopsy [12]. Later, two cases have reported successful managements of pediatric NP due to IAV and PVL-producing S. aureus and a recent case described successful treatment for pleural empyema and pediatric NP caused by IAV and MRSA [13–15]. This is the first documented successful management of necrotizing laryngotracheobronchitis and NP caused by IAV and PVL-positive MRSA in children. Despite this clinical success, the detailed mechanisms underlying such severe mixed infections remain incompletely understood.

The interaction between influenza virus and bacteria may play a crucial role in the pathogenesis of severe pneumonia. First, virus infection favors bacteria binding of lung cells by uncovering the ligands and prevent bacteria clearance by impairing immune system [16]. Second, certain bacteria could enhance influenza virus pathogenicity by activating virus hemagglutinin [10]. Third, virus could induce a strong proinflammatory reaction and upregulate chemokines to increases neutrophil accumulation [17]. Beyond the viral-bacterial interaction, the virulence of S. aureus itself is a critical determinant of disease severity. PVL and methicillin resistance are the two critical independent factors associated with severe S. aureus pneumonia [18]. A preceding influenza infection or influenzalike symptoms, leukopenia, airway bleeding, and a high mortality rate have characterized PVL-positive S. aureus NP as a specific disease entity [6, 11, 19], with the first two features being recognized predictors of mortality [20]. Despite the presence of these severe symptoms in our patients, all three cases had a favorable outcome with aggressive managment.

Although the identified S. aureus strains in our study were PVL-positive, it is important to note that large-scale epidemiological studies report a relatively low prevalence of PVL genes among clinical isolates [7]. The main functions of PVL include leukocyte destruction and tissue necrosis [21]. In 1999, PVL was first recognized as a possible virulence factor associated with necrotic hemorrhagic pneumonia [22]. Several cases of fatal PVL S. aureus hemorrhagic pneumonia in children have been reported [23, 24]. Autopsy studies of such cases have revealed hemorrhagic necrosis of lung, tracheal mucosa, and alveolar septa [25]. Besides PVL, numerous virulence factors of S. aureus, like α-hemolysin, toxic shock syndrome toxin-1, enterotoxins serotypes B and enterotoxin serotypes C may contribute to severe NP and lead to hypotension, shock, and even multiple organ failure [24, 26].

The origin of the MRSA strains is another important consideration. In Case 3, the rapid, severe community-onset presentation and the presence of the PVL gene are highly suggestive of a community-acquired methicillin-resistant S. aureus (CA-MRSA) strain. The patient had no recent healthcare exposure, and CA-MRSA, though historically less common in CAP in our country, has been increasingly observed in pediatric CAP in recent years [27]. In contrast, the MRSA isolates in Cases 1, which were identified later during the hospitalization, may be more consistent with healthcare-associated MRSA strains, a common finding in our institutional setting.

All three patients had necrotizing laryngotracheobronchitis which can lead to acute airway obstruction and respiratory arrest through exfoliated tracheal pseudomembrane and necrotic mucus. The first documented case and another recent case of pediatric necrotizing laryngotracheobronchitis caused by co-infection of IAV and MRSA were reported to be fatal [28, 29]. In addition to antibiotics targeting pathogens, early performance of flexible bronchoscopy and application of glucocorticoids is necessary for this complication [30]. Dense mucous casts were found in the second patient by flexible bronchoscopy. A series of case reports of plastic bronchitis associated with IAV were reported but the pathogenesis is still unclear [31, 32]. Immune responses to components of the influenza virus may be involved. Removal of casts and the administration of antiviral drugs have been recommended for the treatment of plastic bronchitis [31, 32]. Pleural empyema was found in the third patient. It had resolved gradually with thoracocentesis and chest tube insertion. No thoracoscopy was performed. Regarding the recurrent convulsions involved in the third patient, we considered it as an infection-triggered, immune-mediated encephalopathy resulting from a confluence of factors, rather than a single cause. The synergistic effect of the dual infection triggered an exaggerated systemic inflammatory response, which can disrupt the blood-brain barrier and increase neuronal excitability.

To date, there are no specific treatment guidelines for severe pneumonia infected by S. aureus and IAV. In this study, all children received empiric oseltamivir in the early stage of the disease. The presence of NP, hemoptysis, pleural effusion, or signs of sepsis in a patient with influenza should prompt consideration of empirical coverage against MRSA with vancomycin or linezolid prior to microbiological confirmation. Guidance on the management of PVL-positive S. aureus infections in England has been widely cited. This guidance suggests that immunoglobulin and toxin-inhibiting antibiotics with good lung penetration (two of linezolid, clindamycin or rifampicin) should be applied [33]. In practical, we would choose to use a combination of antibiotics to cover both gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria for critically ill patients before acquiring the result of bacteria culture and antibiotic resistance patterns. The three patients all received linezolid and/or vancomycin with another antibiotic such as meropenem, or cefoperazone sodium and sulbactam sodium. Except the first patient, immunoglobulin was prescribed for other patients. It has been reported that PVL can be inhibited by serum antibodies [17]. The use of immunoglobulin may inactivate PVL and provide therapeutic benefits.

This study reported successful treatment of three cases of necrotizing laryngotracheobronchitis and NP caused by S. aureus and IAV infection. In general, these patients had pretty serious condition and rapid development. Raising awareness of this severe respiratory disorder and providing timely and effective treatment is really essential to decrease mortality.
