Association of week-2 Contrast-Enhanced Ultrasound Perfusion Changes with Response to Lenvatinib Plus PD-1 Inhibitors in Unresectable Hepatocellular Carcinoma: A Retrospective Exploratory Study
Authors: Hang Su, Yang Gao, Song Zhang, Pei-Hao Wen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo explore whether early contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS)-derived tumor perfusion changes were associated with subsequent response to Lenvatinib plus PD-1 inhibitors in unresectable hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC).MethodsThis study included 74 patients with unresectable HCC treated with Lenvatinib plus PD-1 inhibitors in routine clinical practice retrospectively. Baseline and early on-treatment CEUS examinations at approximately 2 weeks after treatment initiation were analyzed to quantify tumor perfusion parameters. Objective response rate (ORR), disease control rate (DCR), duration of response (DoR), progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) were study endpoints. Associations between CEUS findings and treatment outcomes were analyzed.ResultsORR of Lenvatinib plus PD-1 inhibitors among 74 patients with HCC was 33.8% (95% CI: 23.2–45.7%) and DCR was 73.0% (95% CI: 61.4–82.6%). Median DoR among the 25 responders was 8.7 months (95% CI: 7.39–10.01 months). After a median follow-up duration of 21.1 months, median PFS was 6.9 months (95% CI: 4.33–9.47 months) and median OS was 21.3 months (95% CI: 14.70–27.90 months). CEUS perfusion analysis showed that baseline tumor enhancement did not significantly differ between eventual responders and non-responders. In contrast, week-2 CEUS-derived perfusion changes, particularly percentage reduction in AUC, were associated with subsequent radiologic response. Using an exploratory cutoff of ≥35% AUC reduction, patients above this threshold showed longer PFS and OS than those below the threshold. These associations remained exploratory because the threshold was derived within a limited retrospective cohort. Treatment was generally well tolerated, no unexpected adverse events occurred, and there were no treatment-related deaths in this cohort.ConclusionIn this single-center retrospective exploratory cohort, week-2 CEUS-derived AUC reduction was associated with subsequent radiological response and survival outcomes in patients with unresectable HCC receiving Lenvatinib plus PD-1 inhibitors. These findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating and require prospective validation before CEUS-based perfusion changes can be used for clinical decision-making.

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the sixth most common malignancy and the third leading cause of cancer-related death worldwide.1 It was estimated that liver cancer accounted for 367,657 new cases and 316,544 deaths in China based on the GLOBOCAN 2022.2 In recent years, Lenvatinib was noninferior to sorafenib in the Phase III REFLECT trial, and was approved as an alternative first-line therapy for unresectable HCC.3 Additionally, the combination of the PD-L1 inhibitor atezolizumab with bevacizumab demonstrated significantly improved survival and objective response rate (ORR) over sorafenib in the IMbrave150 trial,4 establishing immunotherapy-based combination therapy as a new standard of care in front-line treatment of unresectable HCC. Therefore, there was a strong rationale to combine ICIs with antiangiogenic therapy to synergistically improve outcomes. Numerous studies regarding combination of Lenvatinib with ICIs showed encouraging antitumor activity and manageable safety.5 Although the phase III LEAP-002 trial did not meet its prespecified statistical thresholds for overall survival (OS) or progression-free survival (PFS) with Lenvatinib plus pembrolizumab versus Lenvatinib alone,6 Lenvatinib combined with PD-1 inhibitors continues to be used in selected patients in Chinese real-world practice because of regional treatment patterns, drug accessibility and reimbursement considerations. Accordingly, identifying early on-treatment markers of response in patients receiving this regimen remains clinically relevant, while the generalizability of findings to other contemporary first-line regimens requires caution. HCC is biologically heterogeneous, HBV-related, HCV-related, alcohol-related, and metabolic dysfunction-associated HCC may differ in inflammatory background, immune microenvironment, molecular alterations, and patterns of response to systemic therapy.7 Because immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) act within the tumor-immune microenvironment, etiological heterogeneity may influence treatment sensitivity and should be considered when interpreting biomarker studies in HCC. However, no reliable biomarkers are established in HCC for immunotherapy or TKI therapy currently.8 PD-L1 expression and tumor mutational burden are of limited utility in HCC, and blood markers (AFP, etc.) are suboptimal.9

Imaging biomarkers are an area of active research. Recent radiomic analyses of baseline CT scans suggested that intra-tumoral heterogeneity patterns might predict immunotherapy response in HCC.10 However, radiomic approaches required sophisticated image analysis and validation, and they typically assessed static pre-treatment images. Early on-treatment imaging changes, which may serve as proximal indicators of drug effect, was not been fully leveraged. Contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS) offered a unique, real-time method for imaging tumor perfusion and could fill this gap. CEUS enables real-time assessment of tumor microvascular perfusion and can be repeated without ionizing radiation. Quantitative time-intensity curve analysis provides dynamic perfusion parameters that may capture early pharmacodynamic effects of antiangiogenic therapy before conventional size changes become apparent on CT or MRI.11 Recent advances in CEUS software and methodology renewed interest in using quantitative CEUS as a biomarker. If changes in tumor vascularity induced by therapy could be captured by CEUS, it might enable very early assessment of drug efficacy without relying on delayed anatomical changes on CT/MRI.12 For instance, patients with HCC who exhibited marked tumor necrosis on CT after Lenvatinib often showed an early decrease in tumor blood flow, detecting this via CEUS might provide an earlier indication of response.13 The PD-1/PD-L1 axis is a key immune-checkpoint pathway involved in HCC immune escape. Binding of PD-1 on activated T cells to PD-L1 expressed by tumor cells or immune cells can suppress antitumor T-cell activity, thereby contributing to immune tolerance and treatment resistance. PD-1 blockade aims to restore antitumor immune activity, but its efficacy may be limited by an immunosuppressive and poorly perfused tumor microenvironment. Antiangiogenic therapy may interact with ICIs by modulating abnormal tumor vasculature, reducing VEGF-mediated immunosuppression, and improving immune-cell trafficking into the tumor microenvironment.14 Therefore, in patients receiving Lenvatinib plus PD-1 inhibitors, early changes in tumor perfusion detected by CEUS may represent a pharmacodynamic imaging signal related mainly to the vascular-modulating effect of Lenvatinib with potential downstream relevance to immune-mediated tumor control.

Early exploratory studies suggested that CEUS could predict treatment response in HCC. In a pilot study of 20 HCC patients receiving Lenvatinib monotherapy, Kuorda et al found that changes in CEUS perfusion parameters within the first week of therapy correlated strongly with later tumor response at 8 weeks.15 Responders had a significant reduction in arterial phase TIC slope and AUC (Area Under Curve) and a delay in time-to-peak, compared to non-responders, with the percentage change in AUC achieving an ROC AUC of 0.94 for predicting response. Similarly, a Japanese group reported that a simplified CEUS quantification method using perfluorobutane microbubbles could identify responders to Lenvatinib: 9 of 13 patients who showed >20% reduction in tumor vascularity on CEUS at 1–2 weeks went on to have objective responses by 8–12 weeks, whereas those without early flow reduction did not respond. These findings were compelling but limited to Lenvatinib alone. However, whether similar early CEUS-derived perfusion changes may identify responders in patients treated with Lenvatinib plus PD-1 inhibitors remains insufficiently characterized. This is clinically relevant because the addition of immunotherapy may modify the relationship between early vascular changes and later radiologic response. Against the current clinical background, identifying early on-treatment biomarkers of response for this commonly used regimen remains a meaningful unmet clinical need.

Therefore, this single-center retrospective exploratory study aimed to evaluate whether early on-treatment quantitative CEUS perfusion changes might serve as an imaging correlate of subsequent treatment response in patients with unresectable HCC receiving Lenvatinib plus PD-1 inhibitors.

Methods

Study Design and Eligibility Criteria

This was a single-center, retrospective cohort study conducted at the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University. Consecutive patients with unresectable HCC who received Lenvatinib plus a PD-1 inhibitor in routine practice from January 2019 to May 2026 were screened at the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University. Main inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≥18 years; (2) confirmed diagnosis of HCC based on histology or non-invasive criteria; (3) unresectable disease not eligible for curative resection or ablation (due to tumor extent, location or patient condition); (4) at least one measurable target lesion on imaging per RECIST 1.1; (5) eastern cooperative oncology group (ECOG) performance status 0–2; (6) patients were treated with Lenvatinib plus PD-1 inhibitor for at least one cycle in clinical practice; (7) availability of baseline CEUS within 1 week before treatment initiation and availability of early on-treatment CEUS at approximately 2 weeks after treatment initiation. Key exclusion criteria included: (1) missing baseline or week-2 CEUS data or inadequate CEUS image quality for quantitative analysis; (2) receipt of locoregional therapy before the first response assessment, patients with prior locoregional therapy were eligible if the interval between prior local treatment and initiation of combination therapy was at least one month; (3) concomitant malignancy or another life-threatening disease; (4) contraindications to ultrasound contrast agents (eg, severe cardiac shunt or allergy); (5) insufficient medical records to evaluate patients’ baseline characteristics and outcomes. No information that could identify individual participants was presented in this manuscript.

Treatment Protocol

All patients received oral Lenvatinib once daily at a starting dose of 8 mg or 12 mg based on body weight (8 mg for weight <60 kg, 12 mg for ≥60 kg), following standard dosing recommendations. Dose adjustments or interruptions were made as necessary for toxicity management, according to manufacturer guidelines. In combination, patients were treated with one of three PD-1 inhibitors that were approved and available in China during the study period: tislelizumab, sintilimab, or pembrolizumab, all three PD-1 inhibitors were administered intravenously at a dose of 200 mg every 3 weeks. The choice of PD-1 inhibitors was at the treating physician’s discretion, considering factors such as drug availability, patient comorbidities, and insurance coverage. Treatment was continued until radiologic disease progression, unacceptable toxicity, or patient withdrawal. Supportive care, including management of adverse events and hepatic function support, was provided according to best clinical practice.

Sample Size Calculation

Because this was a single-center retrospective exploratory study, all consecutive eligible patients treated during the study period were included. A biomarker-based sample size consideration was performed using PASS version 15.0 with the “Tests for One ROC Curve” procedure. Week-2 CEUS-derived AUC reduction was treated as a continuous biomarker, and RECIST version 1.1 responder status was used as the reference classification. Based on a two-sided test with an α=0.05, 80% power, a null AUROC of 0.70, an expected AUROC of 0.88, a non-responder-to-responder ratio of 2:1, a full false-positive-rate range of 0.00 to 1.00, and a standard deviation ratio of 1.00 between the negative and positive groups, the required sample size was estimated to be 69 evaluable patients, including 23 responders and 46 non-responders. This study included 74 evaluable patients, with 25 RECIST responders and 49 non-responders, exceeding the required sample size estimated for this exploratory ROC-based biomarker analysis. Nevertheless, because this sample size consideration was performed in the context of a retrospective single-center study and the ROC-derived threshold was evaluated within the same cohort, the biomarker analyses were interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive validation.

CEUS Examination

All 74 patients underwent CEUS at two prespecified time points: within 1 week before treatment initiation (baseline) and approximately 2 weeks (10–14 days) after treatment initiation (early on-treatment). CEUS was performed by experienced radiologist-sonographers using ultrasound systems equipped with contrast-specific imaging modes (GE LOGIQ E9 or Philips EPIQ) and a low-frequency convex transducer (1–5 MHz). A 2.4mL bolus of sulfur hexafluoride microbubble contrast agent (SonoVue®, Bracco) was administered intravenously, followed by a 5–10mL saline flush. A low mechanical index (0.08–0.10) was maintained, and dynamic CEUS cine loops were recorded for at least 3 minutes. One intrahepatic target lesion was selected per patient, preferentially the largest measurable lesion that was technically accessible by ultrasound and corresponded to a RECIST 1.1 target lesion whenever feasible. The same lesion was analyzed at baseline and week 2.

For quantitative time-intensity curve (TIC) analysis, only cine loops with complete arterial-phase capture, stable visualization of the target lesion, and adequate acoustic quality were included. The most stable respiratory segment was selected for analysis. Minor in-plane motion was corrected by frame-by-frame repositioning of the region of interest (ROI), whereas clips with substantial out-of-plane motion or inability to maintain a consistent ROI were excluded. A circular tumor ROI of approximately 5 mm was placed within a representative enhancing portion of the lesion while avoiding gross necrosis, large vessels, acoustic shadowing, and obvious artifacts. A similarly sized reference ROI was placed in adjacent non-tumoral liver parenchyma at a comparable imaging depth for background/reference enhancement assessment.

TICs were generated using the built-in CEUS quantification software and exported to MATLAB (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA) for standardized post-processing. The prespecified quantitative parameters were peak intensity (PI), wash-in slope, time to peak (TTP), and area under the curve (AUC). PI was defined as the maximum enhancement amplitude, wash-in slope as the steepest ascending portion of the TIC, and TTP as the interval from contrast arrival to maximum enhancement. AUC was calculated over the prespecified 30-s interval beginning at contrast arrival. A schematic illustration of the TIC-derived quantitative CEUS parameters was provided in Figure 2. Two radiologists experienced in liver CEUS and blinded to treatment response and survival outcomes independently performed the quantitative measurements in a randomly selected subset of 10 patients. Interobserver reliability was assessed using a two-way random-effects, single-measure, absolute-agreement intraclass correlation coefficient [ICC(2,1)]. Efficacy Evaluation and Study Endpoints

Tumor response was assessed radiologically at baseline and at regular follow-up intervals using both conventional cross-sectional imaging and CEUS. Triphasic contrast-enhanced MRI or CT scans were obtained at baseline and every 6 weeks (±1 week) during treatment for formal response evaluation. Response was primarily determined according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors version 1.1 (RECIST 1.1) based on CT/MRI measurements. Tumor responses were categorized as complete response (CR), partial response (PR), stable disease (SD), or progressive disease (PD) according to RECIST 1.1 definitions. ORR was defined as the proportion of patients achieving CR or PR. Disease control rate (DCR) was defined as the proportion achieving CR, PR, or SD. Because CEUS captures perfusion-related changes and viable enhancing tumor is clinically relevant in HCC response assessment, modified RECIST (mRECIST) was also applied as a supportive response criterion. mRECIST response was determined based on the enhancing viable portion of target lesions on contrast-enhanced CT or MRI. ORR and DCR were calculated according to both RECIST 1.1 and mRECIST.

Duration of response (DoR) was calculated from the time response criteria (CR/PR) were first met until disease progression or death in those who achieved a response. Progression-free survival (PFS) was defined as the time from combination therapy initiation to radiologic progression or death (whichever occurred first). OS was defined as time from therapy start to death from any cause.16 Patients without an event were censored at last follow-up. For survival analyses, the data cutoff date in this study was June 25, 2026.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (range) as appropriate, and categorical variables are presented as number (percentage). Between-group comparisons used the student t test or Mann–Whitney U-test for continuous variables and the chi-square test or Fisher exact test for categorical variables. Associations between week-2 CEUS perfusion changes and objective response were examined using exploratory logistic regression models. Because the number of responders remained limited even in the expanded 74-patient cohort, multivariable logistic regression was restricted to a small number of clinically relevant covariates. Model results were interpreted as exploratory associations rather than definitive evidence of independent predictive value. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed to evaluate the discriminative ability of quantitative CEUS variables for identifying RECIST 1.1 responders. The area under the ROC curve (AUROC) was calculated. For week-2 percentage reduction in AUC, the exploratory cutoff was selected by maximizing the Youden index (J=sensitivity+specificity-1) across candidate cutoff values, using RECIST 1.1 responder status as the reference outcome. This procedure identified an AUC reduction of approximately 35% as the cutoff providing the optimal balance between sensitivity and specificity in the present cohort. Because both cutoff derivation and performance evaluation were performed in the same retrospective cohort, the resulting AUROC and cutoff-specific operating characteristics were considered apparent performance estimates and potentially optimistic. Accordingly, the ≥35% AUC-reduction threshold was regarded as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than as a validated clinical cutoff and required independent prospective validation. Because treatment selection was non-randomized and three different PD-1 inhibitors were used, exploratory analyses were performed according to PD-1 inhibitor type. RECIST 1.1 response rates were compared among PD-1 inhibitor groups, and PFS and OS were compared using Kaplan-Meier estimates and Log rank test. The distribution of the exploratory week-2 AUC-reduction threshold (≥35% vs <35%) was also compared among PD-1 inhibitor groups. In addition, sensitivity Cox models including PD-1 inhibitor type were used to examine whether the association between week-2 AUC reduction and survival was materially altered after accounting for PD-1 inhibitor selection. Given the limited subgroup sizes and non-randomized treatment allocation, these analyses were considered exploratory and were not intended to establish comparative efficacy among individual PD-1 inhibitors. Patient-level antiviral treatment exposure and longitudinal HBV DNA or virological suppression data were not systematically available and therefore could not be reliably incorporated into subgroup or adjusted analyses.

Time-to-event outcomes were analyzed using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared using the Log rank test. Because week-2 CEUS AUC reduction was assessed after treatment initiation, survival analyses stratified by week-2 CEUS status were additionally performed using a landmark approach. The landmark time point was defined as the date of the week-2 CEUS assessment. Patients who experienced progression or death before the landmark time point were excluded from the corresponding landmark analysis. PFS and OS were recalculated from the landmark time point to reduce guarantee-time bias. Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs for PFS and OS, as applicable. Given the modest sample size and limited number of events, multivariable Cox models were restricted to a limited number of prespecified clinically relevant covariates to reduce model instability. Variables were not entered solely on the basis of univariate statistical significance. Proportional hazards assumptions were assessed using graphical methods and Schoenfeld residuals where applicable. All Cox models were interpreted as exploratory. Etiology was recorded as a baseline variable. Because most patients had HBV-related HCC and the number of HCV-related or non-viral cases was limited, formal etiology-specific modeling was not performed. Instead, etiological heterogeneity was addressed descriptively and discussed as an important limitation.

All tests were two-sided, and P<0.05 were considered statistically significant. Because of the retrospective design, modest sample size and exploratory nature of the analyses, no adjustment was made for multiple testing. Survival associations based on a week-2 on-treatment biomarker should therefore be interpreted as hypothesis-generating. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Therapeutic Response

All 74 patients were evaluable for response at the first radiologic assessment. By RECIST 1.1 criteria, 2 patients (2.7%) achieved confirmed CR, and 23 patients (31.1%) achieved PR, yielding an ORR of 33.8% (95% CI: 23.2–45.7%). An additional 29 patients (39.2%) had SD as best response, resulting in a DCR of 73.0% (95% CI: 61.4–82.6%). 20 patients (27.0%) were PD at first evaluation. Using mRECIST as a supportive HCC-specific response criterion, 3 patients (4.1%) achieved CR and 26 patients (35.1%) achieved PR, yielding a mRECIST ORR of 39.2% (95% CI: 28.0–51.2%). An additional 33 patients (44.6%) had SD, resulting in a DCR of 83.8% (95% CI: 73.4–91.3%).

As shown in Figure 3, some patients derived radiologic benefit from Lenvatinib plus PD-1 inhibitor therapy with evident target-lesion shrinkage. The waterfall plot of maximal tumor reduction indicated that 44 out of 74 patients (59.5%) experienced some decrease in target lesion size, whereas 30 (40.5%) had tumor growth as the best response. Survival Outcomes

At the date of data cut-off (June 25, 2026), the median follow-up duration for survival analysis was 21.1 months (range: 0.8–46.1 months). Overall Kaplan–Meier curves for PFS and OS were provided in Figure S1, the median PFS for the entire cohort of was 6.9 months (95% CI: 4.33–9.47 months). The 12-month PFS rate was 31.5%, and 24-month PFS rate was 11.9%. The median OS was 21.3 months (95% CI: 14.70–27.90 months). The 24-month OS rate was 43.3% and 36-month OS rate was 22.1%.

Subgroup survival analysis indicated trends in line with known prognostic factors. Patients who achieved CR/PR had a significantly longer PFS (median: 14.8 vs 6.8 months, P<0.001) and OS (median: 28.8 vs 14.8 months, P<0.001) compared to those without response, highlighting the clinical benefit of tumor response. Patients with ECOG performance status of 1–2 had shorter OS (median: 16.8 vs 22.8 months, P=0.013) than those with 0 score. Patients with extrahepatic metastases had shorter PFS (median: 4.9 vs 10.7 months, P=0.005) and OS (median: 13.3 vs 25.0 months, P=0.008) than those without metastases. And patients with portal vein tumor thrombosis were associated a trend for inferior PFS (median: 6.7 vs 9.0 months, P=0.08) and OS (median: 18.7 vs 24.7 months, P=0.06) than those without portal vein tumor thrombosis, although no statistically significant difference was observed. Exploratory analyses according to PD-1 inhibitor type showed no statistically significant differences in RECIST 1.1 ORR, DCR, PFS, OS or the proportion of patients with week-2 AUC reduction ≥35%, detailed results were shown in Supplementary Table S1. Given the limited subgroup sizes and non-randomized treatment selection, these comparisons were considered descriptive and should not be interpreted as evidence of equivalence among the three PD-1 inhibitors. Patients who required Lenvatinib dose reductions did not show inferior outcomes.

CEUS Parameters and Correlation with Efficacy

A central aim of this study was to determine whether early changes in tumor perfusion on CEUS might predict treatment response and long-term outcomes. We therefore analyzed CEUS-derived TIC parameters at baseline and at the week-2 follow-up, comparing the changes between responders (CR+PR) and non-responders (SD+PD). As shown in Table 2, baseline CEUS perfusion parameters did not significantly differ between eventual responders and non-responders. Baseline PI was 26.5 ± 7.6 AU in responders versus 25.3 ± 8.8 AU in non-responders (P=0.558). Baseline wash-in slope was 2.17 ± 0.60 AU/s versus 2.05 ± 0.55 AU/s (P=0.392), baseline TTP was 28.1 ± 3.7 s versus 29.7 ± 3.5 s (P=0.075), and baseline AUC was 577 ± 72 AU·s versus 584 ± 82 AU·s (P=0.713), respectively. To assess measurement reproducibility, interobserver reliability was evaluated in a randomly selected subset of 10 patients. The ICCs for PI, wash-in slope, TTP and AUC were 0.92, 0.85, 0.87, and 0.91, respectively, supporting high within-center interobserver agreement for quantitative TIC measurements.

In contrast, the week-2 CEUS examination revealed clear divergence in early perfusion changes between the two groups. Responders demonstrated a greater decrease in PI than non-responders (−8.7 ± 2.3 AU vs +1.2 ± 0.5 AU; P<0.001). The week-2 change in wash-in slope was −22.21% ± 3.48% in responders versus +3.12% ± 0.43% in non-responders (P<0.001). TTP increased by 2.52 ± 0.30 s in responders, compared with 0.30 ± 0.24 s in non-responders (P<0.001). The percentage change in tumor AUC also differed markedly between the two groups, with responders showing a greater AUC reduction than non-responders (−43.27% ± 3.15% vs −2.38% ± 16.14%; P<0.001).

Among all 74 patients, new or enlarged intratumoral non-enhancing areas suggestive of early perfusion defects were observed in 19 of 25 responders (76.0%) but in only 7 of 49 non-responders (14.3%; P<0.001). These findings indicate that treatment benefit was associated with an early decline in tumor perfusion rather than with baseline vascularity alone.

Predictive Performance and Prognostic Associations

Among the quantitative CEUS variables, percentage change in AUC showed the best apparent discriminative performance for identifying RECIST responders with an ROC AUC of 0.925 (95% CI: 0.815–0.988). An exploratory threshold of at least 35% AUC reduction provided 92% sensitivity, 82% specificity, a positive predictive value of 72%, and a negative predictive value of 95%. In exploratory multivariable logistic regression, AUC reduction remained associated with objective response [odds ratio (OR)=1.33 per 5% increase in AUC reduction, P=0.003], whereas wash-in slope change did not retain independent significance after accounting for AUC reduction. The presence of new qualitative perfusion defects at week 2 was also strongly associated with objective response (OR=19.00; 95% CI: 5.69–63.51; P<0.001).

Safety Profile

The safety profile was consistent with the known toxicities of Lenvatinib plus PD-1 inhibitors. The most common treatment-related adverse events were hypertension (51.4%), hand-foot skin reaction (35.1%), proteinuria (33.8%), fatigue (32.4%), and transaminitis (27.0%). Grade 3 adverse events included hypertension (9.5%), transaminitis (6.8%), and diarrhea (2.7%). Immune-related adverse events occurred in 5 patients (6.8%). No grade 4 or 5 treatment-related adverse events and no treatment-related deaths were observed. 21 patients (28.4%) required Lenvatinib dose reduction, and 6 patients (8.1%) discontinued combination therapy because of adverse events.

Discussion

In this single-center retrospective exploratory study, baseline CEUS perfusion characteristics did not distinguish responders from non-responders, whereas early week-2 perfusion changes, particularly AUC reduction, were associated with subsequent radiologic response and survival outcomes. These findings suggest that serial quantitative CEUS may provide an early pharmacodynamic imaging signal during Lenvatinib plus PD-1 inhibitor therapy. However, the results remain hypothesis-generating because of the retrospective design, limited sample size, and absence of external validation.

The observed ORR of 33.8%, median PFS of 6.9 months, and median OS of 21.3 months were generally consistent with previously reported non-randomized and early-phase studies of Lenvatinib plus PD-1 blockade.17,18 Nevertheless, the clinical context of this regimen requires careful interpretation. The phase III LEAP-002 trial did not meet its prespecified statistical thresholds for improved OS or PFS with Lenvatinib plus pembrolizumab versus Lenvatinib alone.6 Therefore, our findings should not be interpreted as supporting universal use of this combination as a standard first-line regimen. In our institution, Lenvatinib plus PD-1 inhibitors has been used in selected patients according to regional drug availability, reimbursement, patient characteristics, contraindications to anti-VEGF antibody therapy, and physician judgment. Accordingly, the generalizability of the present CEUS findings to other contemporary systemic regimens, particularly atezolizumab plus bevacizumab or durvalumab plus tremelimumab, remains uncertain.19 The supportive mRECIST analysis yielded numerically higher response rates than RECIST 1.1, whereas RECIST 1.1 was retained as the primary response criterion to provide a conservative and conventional response framework.

One of the most important messages from our data was that baseline tumor vascularity alone was not informative. This was clinically plausible. Hyper-vascular enhancement was a hallmark of HCC, and although the degree of arterial enhancement varied across tumors, baseline perfusion largely reflected tumor phenotype at diagnosis rather than treatment sensitivity.20 In contrast, the change in perfusion after exposure to therapy captured the functional interaction between the drug and the tumor microvasculature.21 This distinction was important because it supported the conceptual positioning of CEUS not primarily as a pretreatment predictive biomarker, but more precisely as an early on-treatment response-monitoring biomarker.22 Such a biomarker might be especially relevant in HCC, where antiangiogenic therapy could induce devascularization and necrosis before substantial anatomic shrinkage became apparent on conventional cross-sectional imaging.

The biological interpretation of early CEUS perfusion changes should be considered within the broader interaction between tumor vasculature and antitumor immunity. Lenvatinib is a multikinase inhibitor targeting VEGFR1–3, FGFR1–4, PDGFRα, RET, and KIT, and its antitumor activity may involve both vascular and non-vascular mechanisms.15 Abnormal tumor vasculature can contribute to hypoxia, impaired immune-cell trafficking and VEGF-mediated immunosuppression. Antiangiogenic therapy may remodel or partially normalize this abnormal vasculature, reduce immunosuppressive signaling, and facilitate the recruitment and activity of effector immune cells, thereby potentially creating a more permissive tumor microenvironment for PD-1 blockade. Consistent with this concept, translational analyses in HCC have shown that Lenvatinib treatment may enrich T-cell- and interferon-related immune signatures while reducing signatures associated with immunoinhibitory infiltrates.23 Nevertheless, our study did not directly assess vascular normalization, immune-cell infiltration, PD-L1 expression, or other immune biomarkers. Therefore, the observed CEUS perfusion decline should be interpreted as an early pharmacodynamic vascular/perfusion signal rather than as direct evidence of immune activation or immune-mediated tumor response.

Our findings were consistent with prior CEUS studies in patients with HCC treated with Lenvatinib monotherapy, in which early reductions in TIC-derived perfusion parameters were associated with subsequent radiologic response.13 More recent studies have extended the application of CEUS to HCC treated with combined antiangiogenic and immune-checkpoint blockade. Liang et al evaluated 62 patients with unresectable HCC receiving anti-VEGF agents plus anti-PD-1 antibodies and developed pretreatment multimodal ultrasound prediction models integrating conventional US, color Doppler flow imaging, CEUS, and clinical characteristics.24 Their CEUS-based and full models demonstrated favorable discrimination for subsequent treatment response, supporting the potential value of ultrasound-derived vascular features in this treatment setting. More recently, Zhu et al prospectively evaluated 67 patients with 95 HCC lesions following lenvatinib plus anti-PD-1 antibody downstaging therapy and showed that CEUS achieved sensitivity and overall diagnostic performance comparable to contrast-enhanced MRI for detecting residual viable tumor.25 These studies provide important evidence supporting CEUS assessment in antiangiogenic plus PD-1-based combination therapy. However, their objectives and imaging time points differed from those of the present study: Liang et al primarily evaluated pretreatment ultrasound features, whereas Zhu et al focused on post-treatment detection of residual viable tumor after downstaging therapy. In contrast, our study examined paired baseline and week-2 quantitative CEUS perfusion changes and specifically evaluated whether an early change in TIC-derived perfusion parameters could serve as an on-treatment pharmacodynamic imaging signal associated with subsequent radiologic response and survival outcomes. Thus, the present findings should be viewed as complementary to, rather than duplicative of, these emerging studies.23

Among the TIC-derived parameters, percentage change in AUC showed the strongest apparent discrimination for RECIST response. Because AUC integrates both the magnitude and duration of enhancement, it may provide a more comprehensive summary of tumor perfusion than a single time-point measurement.26–28 However, the ROC AUC of 0.925 and the exploratory ≥35% cutoff require cautious interpretation because the threshold was selected and evaluated within the same retrospective cohort. Consequently, the sensitivity, specificity, and predictive values represent apparent performance estimates and may be optimistic. The observed associations of the ≥35% threshold with PFS and OS support further investigation of AUC reduction as an early pharmacodynamic marker, but this cutoff should not be considered clinically actionable until it is prospectively evaluated as a prespecified threshold in an independent cohort. In a disease such as unresectable HCC, where deterioration of liver function can rapidly narrow subsequent treatment options, reducing the time spent on ineffective therapy was clinically meaningful.29 Conversely, in a patient with no meaningful CEUS perfusion change, worsening symptoms, or deteriorating laboratory markers, the absence of an early vascular signal can support closer surveillance, earlier cross-sectional imaging.30

From a clinical perspective, the present findings support the potential role of CEUS as an adjunctive early monitoring tool rather than as a stand-alone decision-making test. A marked decline in CEUS-derived tumor perfusion at week 2 may increase confidence that the tumor is undergoing an early pharmacodynamic response, whereas the absence of such a change may identify patients who require closer clinical and radiologic follow-up. However, the present retrospective data are insufficient to support CEUS-guided treatment switching, dose modification, or treatment intensification. Such strategies should only be evaluated in prospective studies with predefined decision rules and appropriate validation. Additionally, the etiological heterogeneity of HCC should also be considered when interpreting our findings. Although most patients in this cohort had HBV-related HCC, the study also included patients with HCV-related or non-viral disease. These etiological subtypes might differ in immune microenvironment, molecular features, and sensitivity to immune checkpoint blockade. Because of the modest sample size and the limited number of non-HBV cases, the present study was not powered to develop etiology-specific CEUS thresholds or to determine whether the predictive value of AUC reduction differs by etiology. Future studies with larger and etiologically diverse cohorts are needed to validate whether early CEUS perfusion changes have consistent predictive value across HBV-related, HCV-related, and non-viral HCC.

Several limitations should be considered. First, this was a single-center retrospective study with a modest sample size and potential selection bias, particularly because serial CEUS required technically evaluable lesions. The findings are therefore hypothesis-generating and require external validation. Second, only one intrahepatic target lesion was assessed per patient, and inter-lesional heterogeneity and extrahepatic disease behavior may therefore not have been fully captured. Third, the exploratory 35% AUC-reduction threshold was selected and evaluated within the same cohort, introducing risks of overfitting and optimism bias. Its operating characteristics should therefore be regarded as apparent performance estimates until prospectively validated in an independent cohort. Although a week-2 landmark analysis was performed to reduce guarantee-time bias, the survival analyses remained exploratory. Fourth, treatment and biological heterogeneity remained potential sources of residual confounding. Three different PD-1 inhibitors were used. Although exploratory subgroup and sensitivity analyses did not materially alter the main findings, the study was not powered to establish equivalence among these agents. Antiviral treatment exposure and longitudinal HBV-DNA suppression were also not systematically available. Fifth, immune biomarkers were not measured, precluding direct assessment of the biological relationship between CEUS perfusion changes and immune activation.31 Finally, the interobserver reliability assessment included only 10 randomly selected patients and two radiologists from a single center. Although within-center agreement was high, cross-center reproducibility across different ultrasound systems, acquisition protocols, ROI strategies, and post-processing platforms remains unestablished. Multicenter studies using harmonized CEUS acquisition and TIC-analysis protocols are required to establish the reproducibility and generalizability of the quantitative measurements across institutions.

Taken together, these findings support the potential role of CEUS as an adjunctive early monitoring tool in future studies. However, the present retrospective data are insufficient to support CEUS-guided treatment modification. Any CEUS-guided management strategy should be tested prospectively using predefined decision rules and independent validation cohorts.

Conclusion

In this single-center retrospective exploratory study, week-2 CEUS-derived AUC reduction was associated with subsequent RECIST response and survival outcomes in patients with unresectable HCC receiving Lenvatinib plus PD-1 inhibitors. However, because the ROC-derived threshold was evaluated within the same cohort and no external validation was available, these findings should be considered hypothesis-generating. Prospective validation is required before CEUS-derived AUC reduction can be used for clinical decision-making.
