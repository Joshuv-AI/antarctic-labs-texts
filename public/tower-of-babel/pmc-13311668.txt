Isolation and characterization of human multilineage-differentiating stress-enduring cells for use in tissue engineering: a systematic review
Authors: Ingrid Garzón, Miguel Ángel Martín-Piedra, Luis Martínez-Girón, Miguel Alaminos, James J Yoo, Anthony Atala
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHuman multilineage-differentiating stress-enduring (MUSE) cells represent a promising cell population for tissue engineering and translational medicine owing to their intrinsic pluripotency and stress resistance. MUSE cells express pluripotency markers such as SSEA-3, Nanog, Sox2, and Oct3/4, while maintaining their immunomodulatory capabilities. This systematic review (PROSPERO CRD42024532621) analyzed studies on human MUSE cell isolation, characterization, and translational potential through searches of the PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science databases in April 2024. The TIDieR and SYRCLE checklists were used to assess the risk of bias. Our findings identified 34 studies that followed the PRISMA guidelines for assessing different tissue sources, isolation techniques, and characterization methods. MUSE cells are primarily obtained from bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells, and fluorescence-activated cell sorting is the predominant isolation method. Characterization was mainly performed using SSEA-3 immunodetection (positivity ranged from <1% to 6.30%) and was linked to the expression of pluripotency and mesenchymal markers. These findings highlight the relevance of MUSE cells in regenerative medicine as a distinct pluripotent subpopulation within adult mesenchymal tissues. In addition, SSEA-3-based isolation approaches complemented by functional assays has emerged as a key methodological approach for the reliable identification and characterization of MUSE cells. However, isolation efficiency varies depending on the cell source and method. Standardized protocols are, therefore, needed to improve reproducibility and facilitate the translational development of MUSE cell-based regenerative therapies.Keywords: MUSE cells, pluripotent stem cells, tissue engineering, regenerative medicine, cell- and tissue-based therapy

This systematic review underscores the potential of human multilineage-differentiating stress-enduring (MUSE) cells as a nontumorigenic, pluripotent alternative for regenerative medicine and tissue engineering. By synthesizing current isolation and characterization methodologies, the study identifies SSEA-3-based identification as a primary standard while highlighting the critical need for protocol standardization to ensure reproducibility. These findings provide a foundational framework to facilitate the clinical translation and therapeutic application of MUSE cells in advanced tissue repair.

Introduction

Regenerative medicine is an interdisciplinary field focused on the repair, regeneration, and replacement of damaged tissues and organs. Tissue engineering (TE) has become a key research area, offering approaches for functional tissue reconstruction,1 including oral and palatal mucosa, cornea, and urethra.1–3 TE integrates three key components: cells, biomaterials, and molecular signals, with this study focusing on cells as a critical component of TE.

Stem cells play essential roles in proliferation, differentiation, and migration to injury sites.4 Although embryonic stem cells (ESC), induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSC), and mesenchymal stem cells (MSC), showed strong potential in TE, their clinical use is limited,4 by ethical concerns, tumorigenicity risk, and technical challenges.

Other nontumorigenic pluripotent stem cells, such as very small embryonic-like stem cells (VSELs), have been proposed as alternatives to classical sources.5 VSELs are extremely small, express pluripotency markers, and show a high nucleus-to-cytoplasm ratio, suggesting a primitive developmental status. They typically remain quiescent in adult tissues, exhibit partial embryonic epigenetic signatures, and do not form teratomas, suggesting a safer therapeutic profile.6 Although studies report multilineage differentiation and tissue repair, their existence and full pluripotency remain debated.7 Further study of human multilineage-differentiating stress-enduring (MUSE) cells may clarify similarities among these populations and support standardized criteria for defining nontumorigenic pluripotent stem cells in regenerative medicine.

MUSE cells were first described in 2010 by Dr. Mari Dezawa at Tohoku University, Japan. They possess properties that support advanced therapeutic applications,8 including resistance to cellular stress, maintaining the viability under conditions such as hypoxia or enzymatic stress. MUSE cells express pluripotency markers such as stage-specific embryonic-antigen-3 (SSEA-3), Nanog, Sox2, and Oct3/4, indicating regenerative potential without tumorigenicity, partly attributed to low telomerase activity.9 Some of these markers are shared with embryonic stem cells.10

Physiologically, MUSE cells can migrate to injury sites guided by signals such as sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P) commonly released from damaged tissues,11 with increased circulating levels reported after acute injury.12 They also express nonclassical human leukocyte antigen (HLA) class I molecule, HLA-G, conferring immunomodulatory properties and enabling potential allogeneic use without strict HLA matching.13 These characteristics support their relevance for optimizing isolation and characterization protocols and their application in TE and regenerative therapy.

MUSE cells isolated from different tissues exhibit variability in their properties, differentiation propensity, and proportions, highlighting the importance of identifying optimal and minimally invasive sources for standardized isolation and clinical application in TE.14

Despite growing reports, isolation and characterization protocols remain unstandardized, making comparisons across studies difficult. This underscores the need for a comprehensive review of current methodologies and the best practices. Accordingly, this study systematically reviews the literature on methods for isolating and characterizing normal human MUSE cells to clarify the main approaches used for obtaining them and their potential applicability in advanced therapies and TE.

Methods

Search strategy

This systematic review followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines,15 with the checklist provided in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2. This review was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (CRD42024532621, April 23, 2024; amended November 2025). Articles were searched in PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science Core Collection databases without time or additional restrictions. The search strategy (Supplementary File 1) was conducted on April 30, 2024.

Eligibility criteria

PICOT strategy: Previously published reports on the isolation and characterization of human MUSE cells from different tissues were qualitatively and quantitatively analyzed. Eligibility criteria applied in the order of priority, included: (i) original research and protocols, (ii) description of a human MUSE cell isolation protocol, and (iii) characterization or identification of the SSEA-3 marker. Exclusion criteria included reviews, book chapters, non-English articles, absence of an isolation protocol, and studies involving animal-derived MUSE cells.

Selection of studies

Two researchers independently reviewed potentially relevant abstracts and full-text articles, with discrepancies resolved by a third researcher. Duplicates were removed before screening. Initial evaluation based on titles and abstracts was conducted independently by three reviewers using the Rayyan online platform (https://rayyan.qcri.org).

Risk of bias assessment

A systematic assessment of 34 articles was independently conducted by two researchers, achieving >90% inter-rater agreement. Discrepancies were resolved with a third researcher using the Template for Intervention Description and Replication (TIDieR) checklist, which evaluates 12 elements essential for transparent intervention reporting.16 Preclinical studies involving animals were additionally assessed using the SYRCLE RoB tool.17

Item 5, identifying the person performing each intervention, was consistently omitted as it reflects common academic writing practices and poses little risk to reproducibility. Items 9 and 10, addressing tailoring and modifications, were challenging to assess because such details were not always relevant. Overall, adherence to the remaining elements indicated high-quality reporting and low reproducibility risk.

Data analysis

The selected articles were independently analyzed by the three authors. Extracted data included the source cell population, methods used to obtain it, and the MUSE cell isolation technique. When available, characterization methods, numbers of isolated MUSE cells and reported applications, particularly in TE, were also recorded. Studies reporting numerical results were further analyzed using mean proportion calculations.

Results

Based on the selection criteria, 61 articles were retrieved from Scopus, 51 from PubMed, and 47 from Web of Science, with eight additional records from other sources, totaling 167 articles (Figure 1). After removing 76 duplicates, 91 articles remained. Three authors screened titles and abstracts excluding 31 articles that did not isolate MUSE cells and 15 that were not original research, leaving 45 articles. One article could not be retrieved and 44 articles assessed for eligibility. Five were excluded for not describing MUSE isolation, two for not being in English, and two for not being peer-reviewed (see Supplementary Table 3). Characterization and in vitro differentiation of MUSE cells

Of the 35 articles, 22 reported characterization of isolated MUSE cells, with 16 assessing the percentage of SSEA-3 positive cells (Table 3). Other markers include Sox2, Nanog, and Oct3/4 (12 articles) while mesenchymal markers CD105 (11 articles), CD90, and CD73 (4 articles) were also used. Additional approaches included M-cluster formation and alkaline phosphatase activity each reported in six studies.

Immunofluorescence was the most common characterization technique (10 articles), followed by immunohistochemistry (8), RT-PCR (5), cytometry (5), and histochemistry (1; Table 3).

Several studies reported in vitro differentiation of MUSE cells into derivatives of the three germ layers. Differentiation toward mesodermal (adipogenic, osteogenic, myogenic, chondrogenic, and cardiac), endodermal (hepatic and insulin producing), and ectodermal (neural, neuroglial, and melanocytic) lineages was described in 13 articles using specific induction conditions, while one study reported differentiation into human primordial germ cell-like cells (Table 3). Eight studies did not assess differentiation and focused only on phenotypic characterization.

Therapeutic applications of MUSE cells

Most of the included articles (34 of 35) were experimental studies, with one describing a procedural protocol. Twenty one articles investigated therapeutic applications of MUSE cells across different tissues (Table 4) including skin diseases (five studies), nervous system disorders (4), cardiovascular diseases (5), digestive system conditions (3), cartilage repair (2), and single studies addressing endocrine and immune system disorders. Thirteen of the twenty one articles directly compared MUSE cells with their corresponding non-MUSE cells (Table 4). Six studies (46.15%) reported higher regenerative capacity in MUSE cells, attributed to enhanced homing to damaged tissues. Four studies (33.33%) highlighted their direct participation in tissue regeneration rather than in paracrine effects. Additionally, two studies (16.67%) reported stronger immunomodulatory properties compared to MSCs, including higher TGF-β1 secretion and suppression of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IFN-γ.

Discussion

Advanced therapies, TE, and cell therapy are gaining clinical interest due to the limitations of conventional approaches, with positive outcomes reported in tissues such as oral and palatal mucosa, cornea, urethra, and skin.1–3 Consequently, identifying optimal cell sources for regenerative therapies has become a major research area.

Human MUSE cells are considered a potential alternative cell source because of their pluripotency,50 and role in tissue homeostasis, repair, and regeneration. They can differentiate into all three germ layers, self-renew, express pluripotency markers, lack tumorigenicity, tolerate cellular stress, and exhibit DNA repair capability.50 However, further work on isolation and characterization is needed to clarify full potential for advanced therapeutic applications.

Most studies on MUSE isolation have obtained these cells from cultured cell lineages rather than tissue biopsies, likely due to greater cellular homogeneity, easier accessibility and fewer ethical concerns. This suggests the field is still developing, and further studies may increasingly report isolation from primary biopsies, supporting potential autologous applications.12 Additional studies have reported isolation of biopsy-based MUSE cells for applications in neural differentiation,18 skin regeneration,51 and immunomodulation.25 Consistent with our systematic review, most studies obtained MUSE cells from adipose tissue,25,52 while some used human skin.18 These tissues are commonly used due to their accessibility and high isolation efficiency.

Consistent with these results, BM-MSC lineages are more widely used than other sources, as MUSE cells were first obtained from BM-MSC.50 Biopsies for MUSE isolation are also mainly derived from BM, suggesting that BM-based procedures are relatively optimized and may serve as a reference compared with other tissues.40

Isolation methods can be classified into two categories: cell sorting (FACS or MACS), most commonly using SSEA-3 and cellular stress methods, which are less frequently applied. Stress tolerance is a unique characteristic of MUSE cells and enables their selection using various stressors. Common isolation approaches include long-term exposure to proteolytic enzymes, serum starvation, reactive oxygen species, low temperature, and hypoxia,27,50 although these methods remain unvalidated. In this study, 7 of 34 selected articles used stress-based isolation, with 5 (71.43%) employing long-term proteolytic enzyme exposure (LTT) and 2 (28.57%) using low temperature or hypoxia. Future studies are needed to compare these methods and validate their efficiency in MUSE cell research. Differences were observed in cell sources—stress-based methods are more commonly used for tissue biopsies, whereas cell sorting techniques were mainly applied to cultured cell lineages. Stress methods may be more suitable for biopsies because they select stress-enduring cells, such as MUSE cells, within heterogenous tissues. In contrast, the lower heterogeneity of cell lineages may reduce the efficiency of stress-based methods, making cell sorting approaches more appropriate.20,40

High variability was observed in readouts from isolated MUSE cells. Most studies assessed (i) survival and apoptosis resistance under stress, (ii) colony formation and self-renewal capacity, (iii) maintenance of proliferative and metabolic activity following stress recovery, and (iv) functional persistence in hostile environments such as inflamed skin or injured neural tissue.21,23,48

Another key finding was the proportion of SSEA-3 positive cells isolated from samples. The highest reported proportion was 6.3%, indicating that MUSE cells are present at low levels in adult tissues.40 This scarcity, along with their migratory behavior,50 may contribute to challenges in standardizing isolation and characterization methods and supports the need for further systematic reviews. Higher proportions were generally reported with cell sorting than with stress induced methods, possibly due to greater homogeneity of cultured cell lineages. BM-MSC showed the highest incidence of isolated MUSE cells, although yields decreased when derived from bone marrow biopsies, suggesting BM-MSCs may be an optimal source among the studies reviewed.20,26 Current research increasingly focuses on isolating MUSE cells from less invasive sources such as the umbilical cord. Although our review identified only one study reporting isolation from umbilical cord biopsies, further research is expected due to their accessibility, minimal patient morbidity, broader differentiation capacity, and high sensitivity to S1P.

Regarding characterization, SSEA-3 is the primary marker expressed by these cells and was analyzed in 33 of the 34 selected articles. Its positive expression should be considered a major criterion in MUSE characterization protocols, as it has been associated with higher differentiation potential.30,46,53 Future systematic reviews may benefit from including SSEA-3 as a specific search term, along with FACS/MACS as established characterization methods. Additional features such as M-cluster formation or stemness marker expression, may further support MUSE cell characterization.

MUSE cells can generate embryo-like clusters (M-clusters) on nonadherent surfaces, supporting their undifferentiated stage and pluripotency. Several studies reported M-cluster formation by SSEA-3 positive cells8,31,33,41,45,46 and 19 of 34 reviewed studies presented related images, suggesting this feature should be included in characterization protocols.31

In addition to SSEA-3, MUSE cells express pluripotency markers such as Sox2, Nanog, and Oct3/4. Their expression, along with M-cluster formation confirms pluripotent potential can be influenced by culture conditions, as switching to adherent conditions may reduce cluster formation and stemness marker expression.14 The regenerative potential attributed to adult tissue stem cells may partly reflect the presence of MUSE cells in these tissues.

Although MUSE cell pluripotency is widely acknowledged, functional validation through in vitro differentiation remains limited. Some studies report differentiation into the three germ layers, many focus only on phenotypic characterization restricting comparisons across studies.20,31,45 Even, phagocytosis of cell-apoptotic fragments has been recently reported to induce in vitro differentiation.47 Evaluation of specialized pathways suggests that their full developmental plasticity remains underexplored highlighting the need for standardized differentiation assays to support their translational potential.

Minor markers such as CD105 or alkaline phosphatase activity, have also been described for MUSE characterization,31 although supporting evidence remains limited. Further studies are needed to determine the proportion of MUSE cells expressing these markers and their specificity. This review found that marker detection was most often performed using immunofluorescence rather than RT-PCR or flow cytometry, often reported only as representative images without quantitative data. Quantitative assessment would help define a more reliable phenotype and support standardized identification protocols. Overall, the limited isolation and application of MUSE cells contribute to the lack of standardized, efficient criteria for their characterization.

MUSE cells have been proposed as therapeutic candidates due to their regenerative and immunomodulatory effects. Intravenous (IV) infusion is the most documented administration route, particularly in clinical trials for spinal cord injury, with reported doses of 2.1–2.7 × 105 cells/kg.54 Alternative routes, such as subcutaneous injection, have been explored preclinically55 but are not clinically standardized. In animal studies, IV administration is also common, typically without HLA matching nor immunosuppression.55 However, no consensus exists on therapeutic dosing, with reported doses being 2 × 105;23 1 × 106;56 2.1-2.7 × 106 54 to 1.5 × 107.55 Standardization of administration, dosage, and characterization protocols remains limited, highlighting the need for further systematic reviews and meta-analyses.

Standardized characterization, administration, and dosing may improve clinical effectiveness. Evidence supports the therapeutic potential of MUSE cells, including applications in dermatology for chronic wounds such as diabetes ulcers or atopic dermatitis.23 and in TE through keratinocyte and melanocyte differentiation.9,21,23,43

Preclinical studies suggest MUSE cells may benefit neurological diseases. Their implantation in ischemic conditions such as stroke, perinatal hypoxia, and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis has been associated with improved neuronal integrity, motor function, and reduced microglial activation, although clinical confirmation is needed.10,41,44,48 MUSE cells have also been explored for cardiovascular conditions, including lower limb ischemia,28 aneurysm,29 and cardiac regeneration after acute heart injury.27,35 Furthermore, their regenerative potential has been investigated for hepatocytes,40,45 enterocytes,22 beta-pancreatic cells,24 and chondrocytes.34,42

Several studies in our review directly compared MUSE cells with MSC, suggesting higher regenerative capacity, enhanced homing to damaged tissues, and direct participation in tissue regeneration.27–29,40 MUSE cells also show enhanced immunomodulatory properties, including higher TGF-β1 secretion and suppression of pro-inflammatory cytokines.25 In contrast, MSC benefits are often attributed mainly to transient paracrine effects57 rather than direct tissue replacement. MUSE cells exhibit a S1P-receptor-2-mediated homing mechanism and can spontaneously differentiate into functional cells, such as cardiomyocytes, hepatocytes, or neurons, potentially replacing lost tissue. Their inherent HLA-G expression also supports possible allogeneic use without immunosuppression.58 Although further comparative studies are needed, current evidence suggests MUSE cells retain MSC benefits while offering potential advantages, warranting further investigation of their translational potential.

These findings highlight the therapeutic potential of MUSE cells in tissue regeneration. However, further research is needed to clarify their translational use as advanced therapeutic medicinal products, particularly for regenerative applications in the oral and palatal mucosa, cornea, and skin, including cell therapy and TE.

Limitations

MUSE cells are relatively recently described, and the current evidence remains limited, making robust conclusions challenging. Research experience with these cells is still developing, and many studies originate from the groups that first described them, highlighting the need for broader investigation. Additionally, lack of standardized isolation and characterization protocols and limited inter-laboratory reproducibility remain important limitations, particularly for clinical translation. Regarding the search strategy, specific terms were initially used to identify relevant studies; however, future reviews could search terms, such as SSEA-3, to capture additional related research.

Conclusions

This systematic review highlights human MUSE cells as a promising cell source for translational medicine and TE applications in tissues such oral and palatal mucosa, cornea, and skin. The findings show high heterogeneity and limited consensus on isolation and characterization methods, indicating the need for further studies to clarify their translational potential. Most studies used culture-derived rather than biopsy derived MUSE cells, and SSEA-3 remains the only universally applied characterization marker, suggesting an early research stage. Controlled studies and standardized protocols are essential to improve reproducibility and support clinical translation of MUSE cells.
