Longitudinal coupling of obsessive-compulsive symptoms with depressive and anxiety symptoms: A cross-domain latent growth curve analysis
Authors: Minglee Yong, Angela Fang, Carolyn A. McCarty
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Obsessive-compulsive (OC) symptoms frequently co-occur with depressive and anxiety symptoms, yet the covariation of these symptoms over time is understudied. In this study, 2364 young adults (18–30 years) from a public university in Singapore were followed across five waves over 2.5 years. Latent growth curve models were estimated for OC, depressive, and anxiety symptoms, and parallel-process models examined cross-domain coupling, with regression models testing the unique influence of growth factors on OC symptom change. Results showed significant longitudinal coupling, primarily as co-improvement with steeper declines in depressive/anxiety symptoms associated with steeper declines in OC symptoms. A small subset of the sample (n = 21) showed co-worsening of OC and depressive symptoms. Higher baseline depressive and anxiety symptoms predicted slower OC improvement, highlighting the adverse impact of co-occurring internalizing symptoms. Only depressive trajectory uniquely predicted OC change beyond anxiety symptoms, suggesting that depressive symptoms may be a key influence in OC symptom progression. OC symptoms showed the greatest stability over time relative to depressive and anxiety symptoms. Overall, linked changes support shared etiological processes and underscore the role of comorbidity in maintaining OC symptoms, while also suggesting potential benefits of targeting transdiagnostic mechanisms or depressive symptoms in interventions. Longitudinal research tracking subclinical OC symptoms in community samples can further inform early identification and intervention strategies for OCD.

Obsessive-compulsive (OC) symptoms commonly co-occur with depressive and anxiety symptoms in both clinical and community populations [1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7]. Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a frequently reported comorbidity among patients diagnosed with OCD, with lifetime prevalence estimates ranging from 56.5% to 65.9% based on large trans-national datasets and when assessed by SCID DSM-IV structured interviews [8]. Lifetime anxiety disorder comorbidity is also common, occurring in about 46.1–53.0% of patients with OCD [8]. Co-occurring symptoms are linked to greater OCD severity and functional impairment. Depression comorbidity is associated with more severe OCD and typically follows OCD onset [9], [10]; anxiety comorbidity also marks a more severe phenotype but often precedes or coincides with OCD [1], [4], [5]. One way to clarify these comorbidities and their differences is to move beyond static prevalence estimates and clinical characterization to examine how depressive and anxiety symptoms covary with OC symptoms over time to reveal distinct dynamic processes.

Several longitudinal studies have examined the predictive associations between OC and depressive symptoms (single-directional effects), but findings about the temporal relationships are mixed. In a large clinical study of 324 OCD patients, Belli et al. [11] reported that OC symptoms predicted depressive symptoms over a six-year period but not vice versa. Similarly, Tibi et al. [12] found that baseline OC symptoms predicted depressive symptoms two years later in another clinical sample, although this effect did not persist at the four-year follow-up. By contrast, Rickelt et al. [13] observed the opposite direction in another OCD sample where baseline depression predicted OC severity one year later, with no significant OC to depressive symptom effects. Discrepancies could be due to methodological differences such as multi-method versus single-method assessment, clinician-rated versus self-rated measures (e.g., Y-BOCS clinician version at baseline versus self-rated at follow-up) and differing follow-up intervals, which could affect reliability and attenuate cross lagged effects. In these clinical studies, comorbidity between OCD and depression is often attributed to illness severity and its consequences. The burden of severe OC symptoms can foster helplessness, hopelessness, failure, and meaninglessness in life, termed as OCD delayed demoralization, which heightens vulnerability to depressive symptoms [14], [15]. Severe OCD is also associated with functional impairment at work, in family life, and socially, straining relationships and eroding social support, thereby increasing depression risk [16]. When depression emerges after OCD onset, it may reflect the secondary effects of OCD that accumulate over time [4], [5].

Only one community-based study, Bolhuis et al. [7], examined temporal links between OC and depressive symptoms. Using a sample of adolescent twins and their siblings, this study was the first to demonstrate a bi-directional association. Based on genetic analyses, they further showed that this co-occurrence was driven largely by shared genetic factors that accounted for 52–65% of the association, rather than environmental overlap, helping to explain the aggregation of OC and depressive symptoms in families.

Shared psychological vulnerabilities can also explain co-occurring symptoms, particularly bi-directional associations. Transdiagnostic cognitive factors, such as intolerance of uncertainty, perfectionism, threat overestimation, and inflated sense of responsibility, are linked to OC, depressive, and anxiety symptoms in various ways. These belief systems contribute to maladaptive appraisals that perpetuate and maintain the symptoms. For example, intolerance of uncertainty predicts broader emotional psychopathology (GAD, OCD, and MDD) [17], [18]. Fear of the unknown can fuel anxiety and worry, compulsions for reducing uncertainty related to catastrophizing events, as well as negative thoughts of the future and world. Perfectionistic concerns (e.g., fear of mistakes, rigid standards) show moderate positive correlations with depressive, anxiety, and OC symptoms in both clinical and non-clinical samples [19]. Threat overestimation and inflated responsibility are maladaptive cognitions especially implicated in OC and anxiety symptoms [20]. At the symptomatic level, repetitive negative thinking patterns bridge these domains, manifesting as rumination in depression, uncontrollable worry in anxiety, and persistent and intrusive thoughts in OCD [21]. Together, these overlapping and transdiagnostic processes likely underlie much of the observed co-occurrence.

Bidirectional associations between two symptom domains can also reflect reciprocal influences that perpetuate both, without implying which emerges first; rather, it points to potential co-development over time. Evidence shows that depressive symptoms could exacerbate OC symptoms [8]. Core depressive features such as fatigue, low motivation, and poor concentration may diminish insight and reduce resistance to compulsions, while negative self-esteem, worthlessness, and excessive guilt may amplify obsessive self-blame and pathological doubt [13]. Multiple mechanisms may underlie these links but research is still limited by a small number of longitudinal studies. Notably, clinical cohorts have more often shown the OC to depressive symptoms relationship [11], [12], whereas a community twin study detected bidirectional relations, including depressive to OC symptom effects, possibly facilitated by a wider distribution of symptom severity [7].

In terms of stability, OCD is typically chronic, whereas depression is more often episodic [22]. In a five-year study of adults with OCD, frequencies of obsessions and compulsions changed minimally from baseline to the second and fifth year [23]. In another study, 61% of individuals continued to meet OCD diagnostic criteria five years later [24]. Persistence in clinical OCD samples is likely amplified by greater severity and comorbidity [25]. By contrast, depressive symptoms show lower stability. In two adolescent community samples, between 29–65% with elevated baseline scores remained elevated one year later [26]. For anxiety disorders, a six-year clinical study showed that the proportion of patients retaining their diagnoses were 38%, 33%, and 28% at 2-, 4-, and 6-year follow-ups, respectively [27]. Overall, OC symptoms display the highest longitudinal stability, compared to depressive and anxiety symptoms.

Goals of the present study

Few longitudinal studies move beyond cross-lagged prediction to characterize how within-person changes in symptoms unfold together over time, and anxiety is often omitted despite its frequent co-occurrence with OC symptoms. Community-based studies are also scarce, limiting our understanding about trajectories of subclinical symptom development and thus, our efforts at developing early identification and intervention strategies for preventing OCD [28]. Retrospective research in OCD samples suggests that many patients report long history of subclinical OC symptoms alongside other non-specific symptoms, such as anxiety and depression, before more severe presentations result in diagnoses [29]. To our knowledge, no studies have simultaneously tested the unique and joint effects of depressive and anxiety symptoms on OC trajectories. This study addresses these gaps by modeling the longitudinal coupling or de-coupling among OC, depressive, and anxiety symptoms in a community sample of Singaporean undergraduates, focusing on covariation among growth factors and cross-domain rates of change.

OCD appears to have a higher prevalence in Singapore, with estimates exceeding 3% compared to rates reported in other countries [30], [31]. Demographic risk factors associated with higher odds of an OCD diagnosis include being female, aged between 18 and 34 years, and Malay ethnicity (relative to Chinese ethnicity). Among mental disorders in Singapore, OCD showed the second highest treatment gap with 84% of those meeting diagnostic criteria in the past year not receiving treatment [31]. The median delay in seeking treatment was estimated to be nine years. Despite experiencing reduced quality of life and suffering significant socio-occupational impairment, there is a tendency among individuals with OCD in Singapore to normalize their symptoms and perceive them as part of their personality traits. Additional reasons for the treatment gap include low recognition of OCD, stigma and self-reliance attitudes, concerns about cost, and limited awareness of available services [31].

We hypothesized the following. First, depressive and anxiety symptoms would co-vary with OC symptoms over time (i.e., show parallel trajectories); second, higher levels of depressive and anxiety symptoms would prospectively predict increases in OC symptoms; third, the prospective effect of depressive symptoms on OC symptoms would be stronger than that of anxiety; fourth, OC symptom trajectories would exhibit greater temporal stability than depressive or anxiety symptoms, even in non-clinical samples.

Method

Participants

Participants were 2364 undergraduate students between the ages of 18 and 30 years old from a public university in Singapore. This was a representative sample of about 10% of the university undergraduate population drawn from various colleges and schools. Because English is the language of instruction in Singapore, all students were proficient in English. Overall, 57% identified as female (sex data missing for 13). Ethnic composition was 85% were Chinese, 3% were Malay, 7% were Indian, and 4% Other. At the first wave of data collection, students were from all four years of study – 580 in Year 1, 682 in Year 2, 585 in Year 3, and 504 in Year 4. Self-reported monthly household income was 31% < SGD 4000; 47% SGD 4000–9999; and 22% ≥ SGD 10,000. Compared to the median household income of SGD10,099 estimated by the Singapore Department of Statistics [32], students probably underreported their family household income. By Wave 3 and 4, approximately 21–22% were no longer undergraduate students. By Wave 5, that figure increased to 41%. These participants reported having entered the workforce, seeking employment, pursuing further studies, or engaging in other activities. The sample sizes for Wave 1–5 were 2364, 1911, 1673, 1431, and 1315, respectively. All 2364 participants were invited in subsequent waves even if they missed participating in previous waves.

Procedure

Participants were recruited via emails sent by the different colleges and schools advertising the longitudinal study. Eligible students received a unique link to an online information sheet and consent form. After their consent, they were directed to the online survey and upon completion, they received SGD10. Wave 1 data were collected between September–December 2022. Follow-up invitations were emailed for Wave 2 (March–May 2023), Wave 3 (September–November 2023), Wave 4 (March–May 2024), and Wave 5 (March–May 2025). The study was approved by Nanyang Technological University’s Institutional Review Board.

Measures

Schedule of Compulsions, Obsessions, and Pathological Impulses (SCOPI)

The SCOPI is a multidimensional screening measure of core symptoms of OCD with a well-validated factor structure and strong convergent validity with Obsessive-Compulsive Inventory-Revised (OCI-R) [33] and Yale-Brown Obsessive Compulsive Scale (Y-BOCS) [34]. Compared with other OCD screening measures, it offers broader content coverage and stronger test-retest reliability. In this study, a 34-item subset assessed symptoms of Obsessive Checking (e.g., Even when I do something very carefully, I worry that it is not quite right), Obsessive Cleanliness (e.g., I often worry that the food I am eating may be contaminated with germs), and Compulsive Rituals (e.g., I often follow the same, fixed order in performing everyday tasks). Items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly disagree to 5 = Strongly agree) and summed to yield a total score, with higher scores indicating greater OC symptom severity across these domains (Range: 34–170). Internal consistency was estimated to be.92–.94 across three waves.

Patient Health Questionnaire-8 (PHQ-8)

The Patient Health Questionnaire–8 (PHQ-8) assessed depressive symptoms via eight items (excluding the suicidality item in the PHQ-9) rated 0–3 over the past two weeks (0 = Not at all, 1 = Several days, 2 = More than half the days, 3 = Nearly every day). Example items include “Little interest or pleasure in doing things” and “Feeling tired or having little energy.” Items were summed, with higher scores indicating greater depressive symptom severity (Range: 0–24). The PHQ-8 shows strong reliability and validity (e.g., [35], [36]). In our sample, internal consistency was good across five waves (α =.85–.88). Using the standard ≥ 10 cutoff [37], [38], [39], an average of 28.5% of participants screened above threshold across the five waves.

Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7)

The GAD-7 measures seven symptoms of anxiety. Participants rated their experience of those symptoms “in the past two weeks” on a four-point scale (0 = Not at all, 1 = Several days, 2 = More than half the days, and 3 = Nearly every day). Example items include “Feeling nervous, anxious, or on edge” and “worrying too much about different things.” A higher total score indicates greater anxiety symptom severity (Range: 0–21). This scale has demonstrated good reliability and validity across many studies [40], [41]. In this sample, internal consistency was estimated to be between.90–.92 across the waves. Based on the cut-off score of 10 [37], [38], [42], an average of 25.6% of participants screened above threshold for anxiety across five waves.

Analyses

Latent growth curve models (LGCMs) were estimated in Mplus Version 8.11 to characterize individual growth trajectories of OC, depressive, and anxiety symptoms. OC symptom trajectories were based on the total scores of the SCOPI scale measured at Waves 1, 3, and 5, while those for depressive and anxiety symptoms were based on the total scores of PHQ-8 and GAD-7 measured at Waves 1–5.

In the first stage, we fit single-group linear LGCMs for each symptom domain and multiple-group models by sex. Sex differences were evaluated by comparing the multiple-group models to their single-group counterparts using the Satorra–Bentler scaled χ² difference test [43]. For each single-domain model, we examined the unstandardized intercept and slope to determine baseline levels and change over time, respectively. We also examined the correlation between these factors [44]. Standardized slope estimates were then used to compare trajectory magnitudes among the three domains.

In the second stage, we fit dual-domain latent growth models in two steps. First, we estimated models with freely covarying latent factors across domains (Models 2a and 3a). Next, we added directional paths to predict the OC slope (Models 2b and 3b). For example, in Model 2b the OC slope was regressed on the OC intercept and on both the intercept and slope of depressive symptoms (Fig. 1). These simultaneous regressions estimate the unique contribution of each predictor while adjusting for overlap among the latent factors. In the third stage, we estimated tri-domain latent growth models with all three symptom domains simultaneously. We first fit a model with freely covarying latent factors (Model 4a) and then added directional paths to predict the OC slope (Model 4b). Specifically, in Model 4b the OC slope was regressed on the OC intercept and on the intercept and slope factors of both depressive and anxiety symptoms.

Evaluation of model fit was based on root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), comparative fit index (CFI), Tucker-Lewis index (TLI) and standardized root mean residual (SRMR). The model was considered to attain a good fit to the data when RMSEA ≤ .06, CFI or TLI ≥ .95, or SRMR ≤ .08 [45], [46], [47]. Though reported, the significance of the Chi Square test was not used as a basis for model fit, as it is sensitive to large sample size. The robust standard errors (MLR) estimator was used in the models as it made adjustment for nonnormality which is expected for all the symptomatic measures.

Across the thirteen variables, the proportion of missingness was 26%, which is reasonable given the five-wave study. Missingness analyses comparing respondents with non-respondents at each follow-up wave (Waves 2–5) on Wave 1 outcome measures showed significant differences on depressive symptoms in Wave 2 (non-respondents scored higher) and OC symptoms in Waves 3 and 5 (non-respondents scored lower). To evaluate the robustness of results due to this nonrandom missingness, we conducted sensitivity analyses that incorporated sex and household income as auxiliary variables for predicting missingness. Parameter estimates from these models closely matched those from the primary single-domain and multiple-domain models. Hence, the observed predictors of missingness (Wave 1 depressive and OC symptoms which were already part of the models and auxiliaries) increased the plausibility of Missing at Random (MAR) conditional on these variables, supporting the use of Full Information Maximum Likelihood (FIML) in Mplus for handling incomplete data and deriving unbiased estimates under MAR.

Single-domain models

As shown in Table 2, the slope factors for all the symptoms were significantly negative, indicating declines in OC, depressive, and anxiety symptoms over time, with significant variances about the average trajectories. Standardized slopes showed the slowest decline for OC (Standardized slope = −.13; most stable), followed by anxiety (Standardized slope = −.40) and depressive symptoms (Standardized slope = −.51). Baseline severity was generally inversely related to change: higher intercepts predicted steeper declines. This relation was strongest for depressive (r = -.43) and anxiety (r = -.41) symptoms and weaker for OC (r = -.16), suggesting that initial OC severity was only modestly associated with subsequent change.

Multiple-domain models

Table 3 summarizes correlations among latent factors across models. OC slopes were positively correlated with depressive and anxiety slopes, indicating linked change: faster improvement (more negative slopes) in OC was associated with faster improvement in depressive and anxiety symptoms, with stronger coupling for OC–depressive than OC–anxiety pairs.

In the dual-domain regression models (Models 2b and 3b in Table 4), OC slopes were regressed on the intercept and slope factors of either depressive or anxiety symptoms. The significant positive coefficients from the intercept to OC slope (depressive symptoms: b = 0.18, SE = 0.06, p < .01; anxiety: b = 0.19, SE = 0.06, p < .01) indicate that higher baseline depressive and anxiety levels predicted slower OC decline (i.e., less negative OC slopes), indicating adverse baseline effects on the OC trajectory. Depressive and anxiety slopes also predicted OC slopes, reinforcing co-development. Depressive symptoms explained ∼31% (p < .05) of the variance in OC slope, whereas anxiety explained ∼13% (p = .06), suggesting a comparatively stronger influence of depressive symptoms.

The three-domain regression model (Model 4b), which controlled for shared variance across all three domains, confirmed the unique role of the rate of change of depressive symptoms – the depressive slope remained a significant predictor of the OC slope (b = 2.96, SE = 1.30, p < .05). Thus, greater improvement in depressive symptoms uniquely predicted greater improvement in OC symptoms, over and above anxiety and baseline OC levels.

Post-hoc analyses

To further probe co-development between OC and depressive symptoms (given the unique effect of only the depressive slope), we examined patterns of depressive symptoms for different subsets of OC symptom change. Significant variance around the mean OC slope indicated heterogeneity which was clarified using growth mixture modeling. A three-class solution appeared to be most appropriate: it had a lower BIC and was significantly improved from the two-class model (p < .05) (Supplementary Table 3). Based on individual’s most likely latent class membership, the first class comprised the majority of 2301 (97.41%) students showing no significant change of OC symptoms over time (Mean intercept = 104.26, SE = 0.52, p < .01; Slope = −0.23, SE = 0.19, p > .05); the second class included 40 students (1.69%) showing improvement (Mean intercept = 106.01, SE = 5.98, p < .01; Slope = −11.91, SE = 3.32, p > .01); and the third class, 21 students (0.89%) showing worsening of symptoms (Mean intercept = 71.76, SE = 5.60, p < .01; Slope = 10.95, SE = 4.34, p < .05). Two students were not assigned to any class due to missing data.

Random-intercept linear mixed models were used to estimate depressive symptom trajectories for the three OC classes. For the stable OC majority group, their depressive trajectory showed a small decline (b = −0.31, SE = 0.03, p < .01). For the improving OC group, depressive symptoms showed a larger significant decline (b = −1.25, SE = 0.23, p < .01). In contrast, the worsening OC group showed a significant increase in depressive symptoms over time (b = 0.92, SE = 0.37, p < .05). Aligned with the main analyses, these results indicate a pattern of co-improvement and co-worsening between OC and depressive symptoms, with the majority class exhibiting stable OC symptoms accompanied by a modest decline in depressive symptoms.

Discussion

In this 2.5-year study, we examined how trajectories of depressive and anxiety symptoms relate to OC symptom trajectory. As hypothesized, we found significantly positive slope-slope coupling between OC and depressive symptoms, and between OC and anxiety symptoms, indicating rates of change moving in the same direction. Because mean slopes were negative across three domains in this community sample, coupling manifested primarily as co-improvement (steeper declines in depression/anxiety accompanied by steeper declines in OC symptoms). Conversely, a smaller subgroup exhibiting worsening OC symptoms also showed worsening depressive symptoms, indicating co-worsening within that segment. These results are consistent with clinical studies showing a high degree of comorbidity in OCD. Even at subclinical levels, parallel trajectories and linked changes provide strong evidence for the co-occurrence of symptoms over time, revealing a dynamic picture of comorbidity as opposed to a static picture offered by prevalence estimates.

Co-development of symptoms offers supportive evidence for shared, transdiagnostic vulnerabilities across symptom domains. In the young adult sample, co-improvement of OC symptoms with depressive and anxiety symptoms could be associated with age-related maturation and gains in stress management capacity during university, helping to mitigate their vulnerability across domains. These parallel processes reflect overlapping etiological pathways and support the classification of OC, depressive, and anxiety symptoms within the category of internalizing or emotional problems [18]. Longitudinal coupling also helps explain the greater burden of comorbidity. When symptoms rise together, total symptom load increases and individuals with co-occurring conditions show more severe symptoms and greater functional impairment [12], [49].

Despite overall declines in symptoms, the study detected adverse cross-domain effects consistent with the second hypothesis. Higher baseline depressive and anxiety symptoms predicted slower improvement in OC symptoms. This pattern suggests that co-occurring symptoms impede remission and may help maintain OC problems. Finding evidence of negative effects in a community sample underscores the clinical significance of co-occurring symptoms and suggests that the negative impact may be even more pronounced in individuals with more severe clinical presentations. These results are consistent with Bolhuis et al. [7], which reported prospective effects of depressive symptoms on OC symptoms in a community sample. Depressive symptoms can diminish motivation and executive control and heighten rumination, which can, in turn trigger anxiety-relieving avoidance behaviors that mirror OC compulsions [50]. Perfectionistic concerns and negative self-esteem associated with depressive symptoms can also increase self-doubt and self-blame, perpetuating repetitive negative thinking [51]. Similarly, increases in anxiety symptoms may increase threat appraisal and distress, promoting repetitive mental acts or behaviors to regulate affect [4], [5]. Together, these processes indicate that depressive and anxiety symptoms can potentially contribute to the persistence of OC symptoms.

OC symptoms did not predict depressive symptom trajectory in this study, contrasting with Bolhuis et al. [7], who reported bidirectionality. Further data analysis tested a model with baseline OC symptoms predicting the depressive symptom trajectory; but the model failed to converge, making the OC-depressive symptoms predictive association unlikely. One possible reason is lower base rate and lower severity of OC symptoms in a community sample, which may limit power to detect prospective effects.

Consistent with the third hypothesis, the results show that OC-depressive symptom coupling was stronger than OC-anxiety coupling, which is evident by the higher slope-slope correlation. Additionally, only the depressive trajectory uniquely predicted OC trajectory after adjusting for baseline levels and trajectories of co-occurring symptoms, explaining a greater share of variance in the OC slope. The higher rates of comorbidity between OCD and anxiety disorders reflect greater overlap and commonalities between OC and anxiety symptoms [3], [8]; while depressive symptoms may be the more salient and independent driver of OC symptom trajectory. Substantial conceptual and empirical overlap between OC and anxiety symptoms complicates isolation of their unique effects. The co-occurrence of OC with anxiety symptoms has been differentiated based on the “anxious-misery” factor, which includes depressive and generalized anxiety symptoms, and the “fear” factor, which encompasses panic and phobic symptoms [52]. OC symptoms related to the anxious-misery factor include “higher order” compulsive behaviors aimed at distress reduction, while those associated with the fear factor include “lower order” repetitive and stereotypical compulsions common in OC related conditions, such as body-focused repetitive behaviors, that tend to focus on correcting not-just-right experiences [52]. In this study, anxiety was assessed using the GAD-7, which primarily reflects anxious-misery and is aligned with depressive symptoms, and did not show independent effects on OC symptom trajectory beyond those attributable to depressive symptoms.

Consistent with the fourth hypothesis, OC symptoms were more stable over time than depressive and anxiety symptoms. On average, students showed smaller declines in OC symptoms, and larger reductions in depressive and anxiety symptoms. Moreover, the OC trajectory was less sensitive to baseline levels, whereas initial symptoms severity exerted a stronger influence on depressive and anxiety symptom trajectories. When students were categorized into three OC trajectory classes, the largest group showed a stable OC trend. Together, these patterns align with prior work suggesting that OC symptoms reflect greater relative stability than mood or generalized anxiety symptoms [8], showing a tendency to follow a chronic and unremitting course [23], [24].

Limitations and Strengths

The findings of this study should be interpreted in light of several limitations. First, this community-based sample largely captured subclinical self-reported symptom levels rather than formal OCD, depression, or anxiety diagnoses. Based on screening cutoffs, average prevalence across waves was 28.5% (PHQ-8 ≥ 10) and 25.6% (GAD-7 ≥ 10), exceeding 12-month estimates for international college students (18.8% and 16.7%; [53]). The SCOPI lacks a clinically validated cutoff, so we could not estimate elevated OC prevalence. Given relatively low OC elevated symptom base rates (based on OCD prevalence of 1–3% in adults, [3]), it is likely lower than those for depressive or anxiety symptoms. While a community sample constrains clinical generalizability, it can reveal longitudinal associations at subclinical levels, which could be indicative of emerging problems [7]. Second, OCD is a highly heterogeneous disorder, and our OC assessment emphasized broad dimensions, limiting insights into specific OCD subtypes. Third, anxiety was measured with a general instrument, preventing differentiation between anxiety related to OCD and anxiety as a distinct or additive construct.

Despite these limitations, the study has several notable strengths. It employed a 2.5 year-longitudinal design and included a large sample size of more than 2000 participants, enabling the modeling of individual symptom trajectories with accuracy. The focus on young adults – a developmental stage marked by elevated vulnerability to stress and mental health challenges [53], was particularly appropriate for examining the emergence and persistence of co-occurring symptoms. Furthermore, by analyzing anxiety and depressive symptoms within the same statistical model, the study accounted for their longitudinal interrelations, thereby strengthening the validity of the findings. As average trends could mask heterogeneity in latent growth curve models, additional post-hoc analysis was able to identify three distinct OC trajectory subgroups and their corresponding depressive symptom trajectories.

Conclusions

Co-occurring symptoms often signal greater clinical complexity and severity, yet the dynamic interplay between different symptom domains are rarely examined. This is the first longitudinal study that provides a demonstration of the coupling between OC symptoms and co-occurring symptoms, especially depressive symptoms, showing parallel within-person change over time. Findings from this study are consistent with others showing negative effects of comorbid conditions on OCD [4], [5], [10] and the higher stability of OC symptoms [23], suggesting that mechanisms operating at subclinical levels may resemble those in clinical presentations. Because OC symptoms have a low base rate in community samples [3], detecting sequential comorbidity resulting from the burden of coping with OC symptoms (i.e., OC to later depression or anxiety) may be more difficult.

Clinically, interventions that target one set of symptoms or shared vulnerability factors may improve comorbid conditions [54]. Given that depressive symptoms showed unique and adverse impact on OC symptom change, preventing or reducing depressive symptoms may confer benefits for OC symptoms, particularly at subclinical levels. More broadly, approaches that address transdiagnostic processes may be well suited for co-occurring subclinical difficulties. For example, proactively fostering approach-oriented (rather than avoidance-oriented) coping strategies and promoting positive self-evaluations may help prevent the risk of more severe psychopathology among young adults. Similarly, lifestyle-oriented strategies related to stress management, emotion regulation, healthy sleep and activity routines, and strengthening social support may also be appropriate prevention targets for OCD in community settings [28].

Future OCD research can be expanded to include more investigation with community samples to clarify how subclinical OC symptoms progress to full-blown OCD. Although OC symptoms are common in the general population, mechanisms underlying risk for clinical OCD are unclear [3], [28]. We recommend a dimensional approach to this area of study, as many individuals who are later diagnosed, report years of subthreshold symptoms beginning in adolescence and long delays (a decade or more) before seeking treatment, such as the case in Singapore and other parts of the world [55]. Our study has important implications for the early identification of OC symptoms and prevention strategies for altering the course of this debilitating disorder. To support such efforts, more longitudinal, community-based research using dimensional measures is needed.
