Expression and clinical significance of peripheral blood NK cell surface co-signaling molecules in active pulmonary tuberculosis patients
Authors: Jing Tong, Shanshan Li, Can Guo, Qingfeng Sun, Hongxuan Yan, Chunyan Chang, Ruichao Liu, Yu Pang, Mengqiu Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe aim of this study is to elucidate the expression of co-signaling molecules on NK cells following tuberculosis (TB) infection.MethodsWe analyzed peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from healthy controls (HC), individuals with latent tuberculosis infection (LTBI), and patients with active pulmonary tuberculosis (PTB). Using full-spectrum multi-parametric flow cytometry, we assessed the distribution of co-signaling molecules on the surface of NK cells, both without and with stimulation with tuberculosis-specific antigens ESAT6 and CFP10. 7 inhibitory receptors, and 6 co-stimulatory receptors were analyzed.ResultsThe proportion of NK cells was significantly higher in the LTBI individuals compared to HC (P = 0.0199) and TB patients (P = 0.0027). In the absence of tuberculosis-specific antigen stimulation, the expression of TIGIT and TIM-3 were higher in the LTBI group compared to the TB group (P = 0.0128, P = 0.0340). The expression of CD160 in the TB group was significantly lower than in the HC and LTBI individuals (P = 0.0042, P = 0.0124). Upon stimulation with tuberculosis-specific antigens, the expression of OX40 was markedly elevated in LTBI and TB compared to HC (LTBI vs. HC, P = 0.0031; TB vs. HC, P = 0.0493), and the expression of CRTAM in the TB group was obviously higher than in HC (P = 0.0192).ConclusionExpression of co-signaling molecules on NK cells, including TIGIT, TIM-3, CD160, OX40, and CRTAM, was altered following TB infection. Our findings may provide potential targets for host-directed immunotherapy and novel vaccine development for TB.Clinical trialNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-12132-6.

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) is a serious and persistent infectious disease caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb). The World Health Organization (WHO) estimated that approximately one-quarter of the global population is affected by latent tuberculosis infection (LTBI), with 5–10% of these individuals progressing to active TB. In 2021, TB resulted in approximately 10.6 million new cases and 1.6 million deaths, highlighting its status as a significant global public health concern and substantial threat to human health [1].

Tuberculosis is closely related to the immune system [2]. Upon Mtb invasion, the antigen is recognized, triggering the host’s immune system to eliminate the pathogen. In TB, although adaptive immunity plays a dominant role in TB, characterized by cellular immune responses mediated by CD4+ and CD8+ T cells [3, 4], the innate immune cells serve as the first line of defense and will produce a rapid response to the invading Mtb through mechanisms such as phagocytosis, apoptosis, autophagy, and inflammasome activation, thereby contributing to non-specific immune defense [5]. Lots of studies have indicated that among the innate immune cells, natural killer (NK) cells are crucial for the early clearance of Mtb in patients with tuberculosis [6]. NK cells exert their function by non-specifically binding to the Mtb cell wall and recognizing antigen-presenting cells that have been infected with Mtb. Upon recognition, NK cells clear mycobacteria and Mtb-infected host cells through multiple mechanisms, including cytotoxicity mediated by perforin, granulysin, and granzymes; apoptosis induced through death receptors; production of cytokines; and the generation of reactive oxygen and nitrogen species, among other pathways, both directly and indirectly [7]. Furthermore, some NK cells display similarity with CD8+ T cells with the production of effector cytokines and adapt to environmental modifications, dubbed memory or memory-like NK cells [8–10]. Memory-like NK cells are also found in TB patients and could rapidly respond to clear Mtb upon re-encounter with TB-specific antigens with an increased production of interferon-γ (IFN-γ) [11, 12].

Co-stimulatory and inhibitory receptors on the surface of NK cells play an essential role in the immune response, including NK cell activation, a moderate inflammatory response, termination of the immune response, and even immune depletion observed in chronic infections [13]. In the presence of co-stimulatory receptors, NK cells activate, proliferate, differentiate, and perform immune functions. The balance of activating and inhibitory receptors determines the functional response of NK cells [14]. Inhibitory receptors suppress and promptly terminate the immune response to prevent an overly strong reaction while achieving clearance of pathogens [14, 15]. In chronic infectious or tumor diseases, where pathogens or tumor cells persist as antigens for a long period, a sustained immune response may lead to NK cell exhaustion. Consequently, alterations in the expression of activating and inhibitory receptors on the surface of the immune cells can occur. For instance, inhibitory receptors such as cytotoxic T-lymphocyte antigen 4 (CTLA-4, also known as CD152) and programmed death-1 (PD-1, also known as CD279) tend to be up-regulated [15]. Because of these changes, the use of immune checkpoint inhibitors in oncology has become an effective host-directed immunotherapeutic approach. Although some studies suggest that immune checkpoint blockade may be efficacious for infectious diseases [16], the development of immune checkpoint-targeted therapies and vaccines for tuberculosis remains a major challenge [15].

NK cell modifications are a promising immunotherapeutic strategy against tumors and infectious diseases. Studies on co-signaling molecules on the surface of NK cells showed increased expression of receptors such as TIM-3 [17, 18] and TIGIT [19] on NK cells in active tuberculosis and showed low cytokine secretion, degranulation, and cytotoxic potential. The blockade of them significantly enhanced the function of NK cells. It is reasonable to believe that NK cell surface co-signaling molecules provide new perspectives for the treatment of tuberculosis. However, most of the relevant studies have focused on individual receptors, so in this study, we evaluated the proportion of NK cells and the expression of co-signaling molecules on the NK cell surface using peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from a diverse population with TB infection with or without tuberculosis-specific antigen stimulation, aiming to find novel targets for TB immunotherapy.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

This study was ethically approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Chest Hospital of Capital Medical University (Ethics Approval No. YJS-2023-03). All participants gave informed consent.

Study participants and inclusion criteria

64 participants were enrolled in this part of the study, including 22 Healthy Controls (HC), 20 LTBI, and 22 patients with primary active PTB. All the enrolled individuals were from the staff and wards of Beijing Chest Hospital and were tested for interferon-γ release assay (IGRA) and chest computed tomography (CT). According to the results, HC were those who were negative for IGRAs, had a normal chest CT, and had no clinical symptoms; LTBI were those who were positive for IGRAs [20], had a normal chest CT, and did not have any clinical symptoms; and PTB were those who were positive for IGRAs, had an active tuberculosis lesion characterized by a chest CT, and had a positive sputum smear or sputum culture. 6–8 ml of peripheral blood in sodium heparin tubes from all participants; for PTB patients, blood collection was done before anti-tuberculosis treatment or not longer than 2 weeks of anti-tuberculosis treatment. The following criteria were used for exclusion: age other than 18–65 years; previous tuberculosis; human immunodeficiency virus-positive; diabetes mellitus; severe hepatic and renal dysfunction; cardiovascular disease; autoimmune disease; pregnancy; and those who did not consent to enrollment.

Antigens and antibodies

ESAT6/CFP10 peptides with 15 amino acids in length and overlapping by 11 amino acids [21] were procured from LDEBIO (Guangzhou, China). All the flow cytometry antibodies and the dosage of each antibody used are described in Supplementary Tables 2–3.

PBMCs isolation, culture, and in vitro stimulation

Collected blood samples were diluted 1:1 with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). PBMCs were isolated by density gradient centrifugation and suspended in RPMI-1640 medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum, 2 mM glutamine, 100 IU/mL penicillin, and 100 mg/mL streptomycin. Resuspended cells were seeded in 24-well cell culture plates and distributed at a final concentration of 1 × 106 cells/well in 500 µL. The stimulated group was incubated with the ESAT6/CFP10 peptide pool at 20 µg/mL for 20 h at 37 °C, 5% CO2. Brefeldin A (10 µg/mL, Biolegend) was added 6 hours before the end of incubation to avoid cytokine release into the medium [21].

Cell staining

Cells were collected in 5 ml Falcon round-bottomed polystyrene tubes (12 × 75 mm), washed, and stained for 10 min with a 1/1000 dilution of Zombie NIR (Biolegend) Working Solution. The cells were then incubated for 10 min by mixing with Human TruStain FcX™ (5 µL, Biolegend) and subsequently incubated for 25 min with the surface antibody mixture (Supplementary Tables 2 and 3), and resuspended for 20 min with a 500 µL Fixation & Permeabilization solution (BD) and then stained with intracellular antibodies (Brilliant Violet 480™ anti-human IFN-γ) for 25 min. Finally, the stained cells were washed twice and resuspended with wash buffer, then analysis with the NL-CLC flow cytometry instrument (Cytek).

Flow cytometry analysis

Stained cells were collected and analyzed using a flow cytometry instrument at the Bacterial Immunology Department of Beijing Chest Hospital, Capital Medical University. Data were acquired using the SpectroFlo CLC (Cytek) software (Supplementary Table 1), with 100,000 events recorded per sample. Supplementary Material Fig. 1 shows the titration staining index results for each antibody. Supplementary Tables 4–5 shows the FMO control scheme, and Supplementary Fig. 3 shows the FMO flow pattern schematic. The multi-parameter flow cytometry data were then analyzed. In all panels, cells were first gated based on forward and side scatter plots to determine the extent of lymphocytes. This was followed by the selection of single cells, and then live cells were selected using side scatter with Zombie NIR. Supplementary materials Figure S2 presents the gating strategies for identifying different cell subsets on each panel. Data from cell populations were recorded as cell percent and exported as a csv file for statistical analysis [22].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses and graphical representations were performed using SPSS 21 for Windows and GraphPad Prism version 9.00 (GraphPad Software; CA, USA). Data from three groups were analyzed using Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Quantitative data were tested for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. If the data were normally distributed, one-way analysis of variance was used; otherwise, nonparametric tests were employed. Multiple comparisons between groups were performed using the Tukey multiple comparison method to identify specific pairs of groups with significant differences. Correlation analysis was performed using SPSS 21, with Pearson correlation coefficients calculated and charts plotted. P value ≤ 0.05 were considered significant.

The IGRA was negative in the HC and positive in the LTBI and TB, which met the inclusion criteria for our population. Distribution of NK cells in different populations

The phenotype of NK cells was defined as CD3−CD56+CD16+ (Fig. 1A), and analysis of the results showed that the percentage of NK cells was higher in the LTBI group, which differed significantly from both the HC group (P = 0.0199) and the PTB group (P = 0.0027) (Fig. 1B), making it a possible marker to differentiate between latent and active TB infection.

Distribution of co-inhibitory signaling molecules on the surface of NK cells in the resting state

We analyzed the expression levels of seven inhibitory receptors on the surface of NK + cells from TB patients, LTBI individuals, and HC donors by flow cytometry without in vitro stimulation with TB antigens (Fig. 2). The results revealed that the expression of PD-1, CTLA-4, LAG-3, and BTLA on NK cells showed no statistical significance in the three groups. While the expression of TIM-3 and TIGIT were higher in the LTBI group than in the TB group (P = 0.0340, P = 0.0128), there was no difference compared with the HC group. We also found that the expression of NK cell CD160 was lower in the TB group than in HC (P = 0.0042) and LTBI (P = 0.0124) groups, and there was no abnormality in the HC group compared with the LTBI group. Pearson correlation analysis showed that the expression of CD160 in the TB was significantly positively correlated with NK cells (r = 0.650, P = 0.002) (Fig. 5A).

Distribution of co-inhibitory signaling molecules on the surface of TB-specific NK cells

After ESAT6/CFP10 in vitro stimulation, we observed that the expression of seven inhibitory receptors on the NK cells followed the same trend as in the unstimulated (Fig. 4). PD-1, CTLA-4, LAG-3, and BTLA remained statistically insignificant in the three populations. The expression of TIM-3 and TIGIT in the LTBI group was higher than that in the TB group (P = 0.0267, P = 0.0114), with no abnormality in comparison with the HC. The expression of CD160 in NK cells was lower in the TB group than in both the HC (P = 0.0029) and LTBI (P = 0.0037), and there was no abnormality in the HC and LTBI compared with the HC.

Distribution of co-stimulatory signaling molecules on the surface of TB-specific NK cells

Different from inhibitory receptors, after ESAT6/CFP10 stimulation, co-stimulatory receptor OX40 on NK cells was significantly higher in TB patients than in HC (P = 0.0493) and LTBI (P = 0.0031) populations. The expression level of OX40 was negatively correlated with the percentage of NK cells (r = -0.489, P = 0.029) (Fig. 5B). The expression of CRTAM was higher than that of HC (P = 0.0192) in the TB but did not differ from that of LTBI. The remaining receptors, GITR, ICOS, CD40L, and 4-1BB, have no differences in HC, LTBI, and TB (Fig. 6).

Discussion

The current situation of TB remains severe. Identifying new biomarkers to distinguish LTBI from active TB, developing novel immunotherapeutic approaches, and advancing vaccine research are of great significance in the effort to end TB. Notably, research on co-signaling molecules may serve as a new and effective strategy to achieve this goal. The presence of sufficient numbers of functionally competent NK cells is essential for controlling (Mtb) infection. These cells facilitate early bacterial clearance, prevent the transition from latent infection to active disease, and augment the effectiveness of TB therapy.

Initially, we analyzed NK cells from three cohorts and found that certain NK cell subsets may serve as potential biomarkers. Although a decreasing trend in NK cells was observed in TB patients compared to HC, the difference was not statistically significant. Although the sample size of the study is relatively small, the findings are consistent with those of previous studies [22]. Compared with HC and active TB patients, the LTBI population had a higher NK cell count. As one of the essential members of the body’s first line of defense against infection, NK cells clear Mtb after they invade the body, and for the more resistant population, the NK cells have a high proportion and strong ability to clear Mtb, whereby the host is only in a state of latent TB infection and does not develop active TB disease. Therefore, we speculate that NK cells may have predictive value for latent tuberculosis infection diagnosis and can also be used to assess the body’s immune function.

We analyzed NK cell surface co-signaling molecules, including seven inhibitory receptors (also known as immune checkpoints) and six co-stimulatory receptors. The expression of inhibitory receptors PD-1, CTLA-4, LAG-3, TIM-3, and BTLA did not differ among the three populations in the presence or absence of tuberculosis-specific antigenic stimulation. While the expression of TIGIT in the LTBI group was higher than that in the TB group in the absence of antigenic stimulation, there was no abnormality compared with the HC group, and the same result was obtained after antigenic stimulation. Combined with the previous findings [13], we can infer that NK cell expression is up-regulated after Mtb infection and then declined in patients with active TB, and that the NK cell phenotype changes continuously during TB infection and pathogenesis, which can be used as a basis for future NK cell-mediated immunity-related studies.

Additionally, it was found that the expression of CD160 in NK cells was lower in the TB group than in the HC and LTBI groups in both the resting state and after stimulation. No significance difference was observed between the HC group and LTBI groups. Unlike the expression of CD160 in T cells as an inhibitory receptor, CD160 is defined as an activating receptor for NK cells. Regarding natural immunity, CD160 activation can induce NK cell cytotoxicity and the secretion of cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), and interleukin-6 (IL-6) [23]. In patients with active tuberculosis, CD160 expression on peripheral blood NK cells is significantly reduced, leading to impaired IFN-γ production and compromised NK cell immune function [24]. Thus, CD160 may represent a promising target for TB diagnosis and host-directed immunotherapy.

For co-stimulated receptors, without TB-specific antigen stimulation, NK cell expression of GITR, ICOS, CD40L, 4-1BB, OX40, and CRTAM showed no difference in HC, LTBI, and TB when analyzed by flow cytometry. After ESAT6/CFP10 stimulation, the expression of GITR, ICOS, CD40L, and 4-1BB remained indistinguishable in all three groups. However, OX40 in TB was significantly higher than that in HC and LTBI. And the expression of CRTAM in the TB was higher than that of HC, but not different from that of LTBI.

OX40 and CRTAM are both co-stimulatory receptors, in which OX40 is mainly expressed by activated T cells, so a majority of studies on OX40 have focused on adaptive immunity, focusing on immunotherapy for cancer [25]. In tuberculosis, Snelgrove [26] demonstrated that OX40 interacts with its ligand, OX40L, to enhance Th1 and CD8⁺ T-cell responses to Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG) when NK cell function is preserved. In murine models, simultaneous administration of OX40L fusion proteins and BCG, along with NK cell transfer, significantly reduced the lung bacterial load, providing protective immunity. OX40 has also been identified as a marker of newly activated CD4⁺ T cells at the site of infection, and short-term OX40-targeted immunotherapy increased CD4⁺ T-cell numbers and promoted their differentiation into parenchymal protective cells. Notably, OX40 agonist immunotherapy reduced bacterial loads in mouse lungs and extended survival, highlighting its potential as a target for TB immunotherapy [27].

Beyond T cells, OX40 is also expressed on NK cells, natural killer T (NKT) cells, and neutrophils. In our study, OX40 expression on NK cells in the TB group increased significantly after stimulation with TB-specific antigens and was negatively correlated with NK cell proportion. These findings suggest that OX40 signaling contributes not only to adaptive immune regulation but may also play an underexplored role in innate immune responses [28]. However, research on the function of OX40 in TB-related innate immunity remains limited and warrants further investigation.

CRTAM/CD355 protein is an immunoglobulin superfamily transmembrane protein receptor expressed on activated human NK cells, CD8 + T cells, and CD4 + T cell subsets [29]. It is involved in cell adhesion, polarity, and proliferation, lymphocyte function, and promotes CD8+ T cell adhesion and retention within lymph nodes. CRTAM interacts with the ligand Necl-2 to promote NK cell cytotoxicity and IFN-γ secretion from CD8+ T cells in vitro, as well as NK cell-mediated rejection of Necl-2-expressing tumors in vivo [30]; however, CRTAM is still very understudied in tuberculosis.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample sizes across groups were relatively small and unequal, which may limit the statistical power and affect the robustness of the findings. Second, the TB group comprised only patients with confirmed, non-drug-resistant disease, while individuals with negative cultures or drug-resistant tuberculosis were excluded. Including these populations in subsequent studies will allow for stratified analyzes to better reflect clinical heterogeneity. Finally, this study did not investigate the functional roles or underlying mechanisms of the differentially expressed receptors identified. Future work should incorporate functional assays and mechanistic studies to elucidate how these receptors contribute to TB pathogenesis and immunity.

In conclusion, by analyzing the expression of NK cells and their surface receptors, we found that the proportion of NK cells and the expression of TIGIT, CD160, OX40, CRTAM, and other receptors in different people infected with tuberculosis changed significantly, and this study not only can provide a new direction for the differentiation of latent tuberculosis infection and active tuberculosis to seek potential biomarkers but also provide a new theoretical basis for the development of immunotherapy against the host of tuberculosis and vaccine development.
