Finding Some Impossibility of Flat-Folding of Given Origami Crease Pattern by Graphical Representation
Chihiro Nakajima

Abstract
Flat-foldability problem of origami is the problem to determine whether a given crease pattern drawn on a piece of paper is possible to fold without any penetration or intrusion of a polygon into any connections among them. It is known from the results of Bern and Hayes and following studies that determining whether a origami diagram which constitute of polygons in general shapes can be flat-folded is an NP-hard problem. In this manuscript, on determining the flat foldability of unsigned crease patterns that satisfy the necessary conditions imposed by the Kawasaki-Justin theorem for all interior vertices, we introduce a graph representation based on an arrangement of polygons whose contours are consist of creases, allowing overlapping of the polygons. On the graphical representation, a method is proposed to efficiently detect the conditions for flat-folding inability by using the properties of the cycle basis. We also demonstrate the above method using an example of a fold that is already known to be impossible to flat-fold.



1
Introduction

Origami is a kind of artwork, folding a piece of paper and make an object.
When we once fold the paper and then unfold it, we will find that the paper is divided into several polygons.
Keeping this nature in mind, suppose dividing an entire shape of a piece of paper, into many polygons.
Here, suppose that we start from the dividing-lines already drawn on the piece of paper.
Then, we make creases along the dividing lines and fold them.
The polygons may be stacked below or above each other and the paper may be folded into a flat shape.
If the lines on the paper were created with actually folding it, then the paper would be trivially folded flat.
However, if the dividing lines were just drawn without any consideration for foldability, it is not obvious that whether it is possible to arrange up all of the polygons without penetrating any of the creases.
The flat-foldability problem asks a question from this viewpoint.
It is to determine whether a given pattern of dividing lines, an example is shown in Fig.1(a), is possible to fold without any penetration or intrusion of a polygon into any connections among them.
In other words, whether it is possible to arrange all polygons given from divisions without penetrating any connections between them, with all shared creases preserved.

Research into the problem of determining whether a given general crease pattern can be folded flat, when the creases have or have not been assigned crease directions (mountain fold or valley fold) began with the paper of Bern and Hayes
[
2
]
, in which the proof of the NP-hardness of the problem is given.
The crossover gadget included in the reduction in the paper contains an error and is after corrected by Akitaya et al
[
1
]
.
One of the necessary conditions for a crease pattern to fold into flat shape is provided by the Kawasaki-Justin theorem
[
7
,
5
]
.
This theorem concerns the angles between each of the interior vertices in the given crease pattern.
A given crease pattern is locally flat-foldable around each vertex if the conditions of the Kawasaki-Justin theorem are satisfied around all interior vertices in the crease pattern.
However, even if we actually do this around all interior vertices, it does not immediately mean that the crease pattern is flat-foldable globally.
In addition, there is also a theorem that restricts the combination of mountain or valley folds on creases as a necessary condition for the flat-folding, which is known as Big-Little-Big angle theorem
[
4
]
.

This paper presents a theorem that gives a sufficient condition for the impossibility of flat-folding for crease patterns which satisfies Kawasaki-Justin necessary condition for every internal vertices, in other words a sufficient condition of impossibility for the instances which satisfy a necessary condition for possibility.
In this paper it is shown that in some crease patterns, no suitable stack of polygon can exist due to inconsistencies among constraints imposed on each local stacking and that such inconsistencies can be detected in polynomial time.
We provide a graphical representation of each local constraint and the local orders-in-stacking each involved in.
Section 2 introduces an overlap diagram and an overlap graph.
In this introduction it is explained how to obtain a graphical representation of local constraint in which each involved a subset of polygons.
In section 3, the theorems and their proofs are given that show there are cases that the impossibility of flat-folding are lead from the properties of cycles contained in the overlap graph.
In addition, they are given with lemmas and definitions containing the notions of local order-in-stacking, intermediation and associated properties of them, switching and conserving, which are helpful for the proofs of the theorems.
For the application to detect the impossibility, the examples on a crease pattern referred from
[
3
]
are demonstrated in parallel with each definition, lemma, or theorem.
In Section 4, we provide a discussion of exceptional crease patterns that affect the interpretability into overlap graphs, at least in the straightforward way, and another constraint that is not addressed by overlap graphs that, however, gives an impossibility of flat-folding.

Definition
\thetheorem

Overlap diagram :
A diagram obtained by folding all creases in a given crease pattern is called an overlap diagram.
This is a planar arrangement obtained by connecting the polygons resulting from the devision along the creases, with keeping each sharing of the creases that are their contours, while ignoring the intrusions potentially caused by stacking of the polygons.
It should be noted that the contours of an entire paper are not included in the creases.
The procedure of generating an overlap diagram is as followings.
First the the set of the polygons are divided into two subsets by bicoloring and the shape of the polygons belonging to one of the subsets are inverted, those in another subset are conserved.
Then, the polygons are arranged with keeping those connections consisted by the shared creases.

We then state as a lemma that any crease pattern that satisfies the necessary condition for locally flat folding at all interior vertices, as required by the Kawasaki-Justin theorem, is 2-colorable. See the appendix of this manuscript for the proof.

Next, we introduce the notion of local orders of stacking to consider the realization of locating each polygon into each layer of stacking, in other words into their vertical arrangement.
Whenever there is shared region between creases and polygons in an overlap diagram, it potentially causes self-intrusion.
A feasible stacking order of polygons, or leaves or flaps, must satisfy all constraints imposed by their unintrudability.
To take this into account, we introduce a graph representation corresponding to an overlap diagram.

On each overlapping of the polygons in the overlap diagram, each local order-in-stacking is assigned.

Cycles in Overlap Graph and Impossibility of Flat-Folding

In this section we explain that some global impossibility of folding of the given crease pattern is found by focusing on cycles in the overlap graph.
Then the notion of intermediation is introduced.
It, is in fact a node itself however, is defined by the two edges in a given cycle connected to the node.
The intermediation is used to distinguish a property that relates a path in a cycle to a subset of polygons.
While an edge restricts three polygons that directly stack each other, an intermediation restricts polygons that do not always stack directly on the edges connecting to them, as explained in Lemma
3.2
later.

Definition 3.1

When a node belongs to a cycle of the overlap graph, it shall be called an intermediation.
Note that even if the node itself is the same, when it is considered in different cycles respectively, it is regarded as each different intermediation.

Reminding that each edge in an overlap graph represent a constraint among three polygons involved, we find that the two polygons are shared between the two edges connected to either side of an intermediation.
One is as an unintrudable one and the other is as one of unsandwitchable ones.
The roles of the two polygons may be the same or may be switched on the each edges.

4
Detection of Impissibility by Cycles in Overlap Graph : Applicability and Exception

The criteria based on the cycles in the overlap graphs allow us to determine various crease patterns.
However, it should be noted that even if ever no cycles with an odd number of switching intermediations are found in the overlap graph, it does not immediately mean that a globally flat fold is possible for the crease pattern.
As the previous studies shows
[
2
,
1
]
, the general problem of testing flat-foldability is NP-hard.
There are two kinds of constraints that are not taken into account in the procedure described in this manuscript;
one is a constraint on two different creases which are in parallel and in partially coincident in a given overlap diagram, where two creases share a line segment,
and the other is a constraint that prohibits cyclic ordering when three polygons simultaenously share a region in overlap diagram
[
9
,
8
]
.
The first one imposes a constraint on the order-in-stacking of four polygons connected to each of two creases.
In this case it is not prohibited for a pair polygons of one crease to be simultanenously sandwiched between the two of the other crease.
Such a pair of creases is not addressed within the framework based on Definition
2.3
, at least without an extension.
If to be treated, this constraint requires an extension of Definition
2.3
to include a hyperedge which connects four nodes in the graph.
The second one is a different kind constraint from the unintrudability of polygons into creases. Even if the overlap graph obtained by the method of this manuscript does not contain an inconsistent cycle, any overlap order may ultimately be prohibited by the constraint prohibiting cyclic ordering.

However, it should be emphasized that once there is a local inconsistency in any cycle in the overlap graph for any overlap diagram, it immediately means that the crease patterns is impossible to flat-fold and it will not be overturned even by the unconsidered factors referred above.
Therefore, the detection of inconsistent cycles of the overlap graph can be said to provide a sufficient condition for the entire crease pattern to be impossible to falt-fold.

5
Summary and Conclusions

In this manuscript the combination of unintrudabilities of a polygon into a crease cosisted of two polygons is focused and a sufficient condition for the impossibility of flat-folding is proven.
First each unintrudability condition is redescribed as the graphical representation which is in this paper called overlap graph.
Next on the graph it is shown that the relationship among the local orders-in-stacking between the polygons involved in unintrudability constraints are imposed.
Then it is proven that there are graphs with a cycle such that no combination of local orders-in-stacking can satisfy in total the required relationship on it.

The algorithmic ability to detect the impossibility of flat-folding also provides one step deeper scope in discussing the computational complexity of the flat-foldability determination.
In general the computational complexity class is determined by the worst-case complexity of instances in the problem.
Then, if the instances which are impossible to satisfy the given conditions are detected in polynomial time, they cannot be the worst-case ones, in the sense that they are easy to determine to be impossible.
From the results of this paper, we can conclude that the worst-case hardness of the flat-foldability problem does not come from the combinations of unintrudability conditions, but from avoiding the cyclic order of stacking of three polygons or choosing stacking for inclusionary fold pairs.
Thus, the studies and the methods in this paper are considered to be useful for separating the easy crease patterns from those with the worst-case computational complexity, and for identifying the properties where the hardnesss comes from.

Appendix

Lemma 5.1
.

The arrangement of polygons in the crease patterns that satisfy for all interior vertices the necessary conditions imposed by the Kawasaki-Justin theorem is bicolorable.

Proof 5.2
.

First, one of the sufficient conditions for a given graph to be bipartite is that it has no odd cycles.
Required by Kawasaki-Justin theorem, for any interior vertex in a locally flat foldable crease pattern the number of angles that converge around it shall be even.
When every crease in a given crease pattern is regarded as an edge in a graph and the crease pattern itself is considered as an entire graph, the above requirement from Kawasaki-Justin theorem neceessary to be locally flat-foldable is equivalent to that every cycle around each vertex on the plane-dual graph of the crease pattern shall be even.
In a planar graph, the set of cycles that surround every vertex is a cycle base. Therefore it is resulted that every cycle in the graph is even.
Then, the planar dual of the given crease pattern is bipartite. It means that the crease pattern is bilcolorable.

Please note that the correspondence between the crease patterns and graphs used in this proof is different from the overlap graphs in the main content of the paper.
