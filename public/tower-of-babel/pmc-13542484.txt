Stomatal cytokinesis defective2-dependent endocytic vesicular trafficking of abscisic acid
Authors: Qian Lu, Yuanyue Shen, Aide Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryAbscisic acid (ABA), a stressful phytohormone, is transported and signaled by clathrin-mediated endocytosis (CME) and clathrin-coated vesicles (CCVs). However, whether ABA promotes its cellular intake by CME and CCVs remains unknown. Here, this issue is first clarified by CME-mediated exogenous biotinylated ABA (bioABA) to localize in CCVs using three fluorescent macromolecules. BioABA has the physiological activity of ABA and may simulate the typical physiological effects of ABA during Arabidopsis seed germination and root growth. A binding analysis confirmed that both ABA and bioABA could specifically interact with SCD2 (stomatal cytokinesis defective2), key to CME and CCVs. As an application of bioABA and FM4-64 on AtSCD2-GFP transgenic Arabidopsis roots, exogenous bioABA was visually localized in the CME-mediated CCVs, which was promoted by SCD2 and ABA. Altogether, this study integrates ABA into SCD2-mediated CME and CCVs, providing a perspective understanding of ABA trafficking and signaling in plant growth and adaptation.Keywords: SCD2, bioABA, endocytosis

Introduction

Abscisic acid (ABA), as a classical sesquiterpene stress hormone, is regarded to exist in cyanobacteria to higher plants, even humans,1,2 in which ABA biosynthesis has evolved into plant- and fungal-specific forms separately through indirect C40 and direct C15 pathways.3,4,5 As a landmark in the evolution of life, ABA facilitates the development of algae into land plants, allowing higher plants better survival and growth as well as propagation by fine-tuning of stomatal apertures, seed dormancy and germination, root development, nutrient uptake, and fruit ripening.6,7,8 Thus, since the discovery of ABA in higher plants more than 50 years ago, it has been extensively studied, with more than 19,000 articles published in PubMed (January 2026). These reports not only uncover the molecular mechanisms underlying ABA function, metabolism, transport, and signaling9,10,11,12,13,14,15 but also now highlight a role of ABA in clathrin-mediated endocytosis (CME) and clathrin-coated vesicles (CCVs).16,17,18,19 However, whether ABA promotes its cellular intake by CME and CCVs remains unknown.

In response to various physiological functions, multiple ABA receptors and core signaling have been reported in the model plant Arabidopsis, including the osmotic stress-related soluble ABA receptor signaling (PYR/PYL/RCAR-PP2C-SnRK2-AREB/ABF)20 and chloroplast ABA receptor ABAR signaling (namely, magnesium-protoporphyrin IX chelatase H subunit-WRKY).21 The prerequisite for ABA action is the transport of ABA from biosynthesis sites to action sites, including ABA importers (NRT1/PTR family protein AIT1, ATP-binding cassette protein ABCG40/ABCG22) and ABA exporters (DTX family member DTX50 and ABCG25).22 Interestingly, a nitrate transporter, NRT1.1B, is now reported to serve as a cytomembrane ABA receptor in rice,23 and this dual receptor for both nitrate and ABA enables plants to fine-tune a growth-fitness balance.8 In general, the sites of ABA biosynthesis are around vascular tissues in inflorescence, stems, leaves, and roots.24 Mostly, ABA is synthesized in roots and transported to leaves or directly synthesized in leaf vascular tissues, finally trafficking to guard cells for stomatal closure in response to drought.25 In addition, ABA could be synthesized in fleshy fruit pulps, peels, and seeds during fruit maturity and ripening.26 Altogether, the transport of ABA from the site of biosynthesis to the site of action is critical for ABA physiological responses; thus, exploration of cellular ABA intake besides transporters is an attractive field of research.

It is interesting to note that Arabidopsis stomatal cytokinesis defective1 (SCD1) encodes a Rab guanine nucleotide exchange factor (RabGEF) responsible for membrane trafficking and functions in cytokinesis and cell expansion.27 Also, SCD1 acts in dependence on another distinct coiled-coil protein, SCD2, and they function together in CME and CCVs, which could be imaged by a lipophilic dye FM4-64 signal.28 In detail, the peripheral membrane protein SCD2 participates in the clathrin-mediated endomembrane trafficking at the plasma membrane (PM), a process critical to plant growth and development by regulating cytokinesis and cell expansion; thus, the scd2 mutant appears dwarfed.28 In addition, the clathrin-mediated recycling of cytomembrane proteins is regulated by SCD1 and SCD2 for subsequent endocytosis of membrane receptors,28,29 which are involved in ABA signaling transduction by a receptor PYRABACTIN RESISTANCE 1 (PYR1) and PYR1-like 4 (PYL4) degradation pathway,16,30,31,32,33,34,35 just like a previous report that ABA could trigger the endocytosis of KAT1 (K+ channel), a key target channel of ABA signaling for stomatal aperture.36 Furthermore, the N- and C-terminal regions of Arabidopsis SCD2 separately interact with both PYR1/ABA INSENSITIVE 1 (ABI1) on the PM, and SCD2 functions genetically upstream of ABI1; specifically, the knockout of SCD2 upregulates a 60-fold increase in transcripts of ABCG40 in Arabidopsis.17 This suggests an association of SCD2 with ABA homeostasis. Also, the strawberry FaRRP1, a homolog of Arabidopsis SCD2, also participates in ABA trafficking and signaling by CCVs during fruit ripening.19 Altogether, SCD2/RRP1 involves ABA in vesicle trafficking, but visual evidence of ABA in CCVs is still lacking.

For this purpose, the biotinylated ABA (bioABA), a probe to visualize the action site of ABA,37,38 is synthesized in the present study. Then, we not only confirmed a specific connection of SCD2 with ABA but also used a combination of the fluorescence molecule FM4-64 relative to CCVs and transgenic Arabidopsis carried with the SCD2-GFP (green fluorescent protein). As noted, these labeling molecules separately appear as blue, red, and green fluorescence in root cells, allowing us to visualize their subcellular co-localization of ABA in CCVs under confocal laser microscopy scanning (CLMS). The first finding of bioABA in CCVs highlights ABA trafficking besides transporters, contributing to ABA hierarchical regulation and plant rapid adaptation.

Results

Chemical synthesis of bioABA with biological activity

To clearly observe the ABA transportation process within cells, we have carried out fluorescently labeled ABA using chemical synthesis techniques according to a previous report.38 Our synthetic carbonyl (-C=O)-coupled bioABA retained the free -COOH and -OH of ABA (Figure 1A; Figure S1), largely maintaining ABA physiological activity sites.19 To confirm this, we investigated the binding activity of bioABA with the E. coli-expressed strawberry FaPYL2, an ABA receptor,19 using isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) analysis. The results showed that the bioABA could bind FaPYL2, like ABA, in comparison to the control (Figure S2). Furthermore, to identify whether bioABA has the same physiological roles as ABA during plant growth and development, we studied a systematic analysis of its effects on the germination of Arabidopsis thaliana seeds, cotyledon greening, and primary root growth by exogenous application of bioABA. The results demonstrated that, in contrast to the Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium without hormone application, bioABA treatment significantly decreased seed germination and cotyledon greening rates (Figures 1B–1D), as well as shortened the length of the primary root (Figures 1E and 1F). A further comparison with the addition of ABA demonstrated that ABA had a more substantial inhibitory effect on the Arabidopsis phenotype (Figures 1B–1F). The results indicate that bioABA maintains essential biological capabilities of ABA.

SCD2 binds specifically to ABA and bioABA

Previous studies have demonstrated that FaSCD2 binds to ABA19; however, the specific binding region responsible for this interaction has not yet been identified. To confirm SCD2 is specific to ABA trafficking, we divided the strawberry SCD2 protein into two segments, amino acids 1–300 and 301–572, named separately as SCD21−300 and SCD2301−572, and each was cloned into the MBP vector for protein purification and ITC assay (Figure S3; Table S1). Our results indicated that the SCD2301−572 could significantly bind to ABA with a Kd of 85.47 μM, while the SCD21−300 had no significant binding signal with ABA (Figures 2A, 2B, and 2E), demonstrating that the SCD2 binds to ABA at its C-terminal half. In this region, we then mutated the lysine residue 380 (Lys-380) into glycine (Table S1) based on the binding sites in the AlphaFold prediction,19 finding that the SCD2K380G protein had no ABA binding activity (Figures 2C and 2E). Additionally, the protein SCD2 could not bind to the other plant hormones, including indole-3-acetic acid (IAA; Figures S4A and S4B) and gibberellin (GA3; Figures S4C and S4D). Altogether, the SCD2 protein can highly and specifically bind to ABA, laying the biological basis for the SCD2-mediated cytological process specific to the vesicular trafficking of ABA.

In addition, as a fluorescent tracer probe to visualize intracellular ABA transport, the binding of bioABA to the SCD2 is to be confirmed. To this end, we further purified SCD2 protein into the ITC sample pool and used bioABA for titration analysis. The results showed that the affinity of the SCD2 with bioABA appeared as a Kd of 103.73 μM on the basis of establishing a stable and direct physical connection experiment (Figures 2F and 2G). Compared to the natural ABA, the binding affinity of bioABA to SCD2 is slightly weakened (Figures 2D and 2F). This is maybe as a result of bioABA having certain spatial steric hindrance, consequently diminishing the interaction strength between the ligand and the protein binding pocket in comparison to ABA.

Manipulating the expression of SCD2 can regulate transcripts of ABA- and vesicle transport-related genes

To verify whether ABA is involved in the vesicle transport process mediated by SCD2, we not only created transgenic plants overexpressing (OE) AtSCD2 with a GFP tag (AtSCD2-GFP-1 and AtSCD2-GFP-2) but also created atscd2 mutants using transfer DNA insertion and CRISPR-Cas9 gene-editing techniques (atscd2-1 and atscd2-2).17 Our results showed that the size of the OE lines is not statistically different from that of the wild-type plant (WT), but it has the feature of leaves yellowing, indicating that AtSCD2 overexpression causes plants to undergo senescence earlier. The atscd2 mutant appeared considerably dwarfed in comparison with the WT (Figure 3A), in line with the previous report.28 The RT-qPCR analysis indicated that the transcripts of AtSCD2 had a significant increase in the OE lines and a marked decrease in the mutant in comparison to the WT (Figure 3B; Table S1). To further explore the function of SCD2, we selected representative OE (AtSCD2-GFP-1) and mutant (atscd2-2) lines to determine the content of ABA. ABA contents of AtSCD2-GFP-1 increased, but atscd2-2 dropped considerably (Figure 3C). The relative expression levels of ABA signaling-related genes (PYL2, PYL8, ABI1, SnRK2.6, AREB1, and RAB18)39 and vesicle-related genes (CLC2, CLC3, CHC1, CHC2, AP2, AP3, and SCD1)28 in the WT, AtSCD2-GFP-1, and atscd2-2 were investigated using RT-qPCR. These genes tended to be up-regulated in AtSCD2-GFP-1 and down-regulated in atscd2-2 (Figures 3D and 3E; Table S1). The results indicate that these transgenic plants may be used for further investigation of the relationship between SCD2-mediated CCV and ABA.

SCD2 induces vesicle accumulation in an ABA-dependent manner and co-localizes with bioABA in CCVs

Vesicle transport, the primary trafficking mechanism for substance transport within eukaryotic cells, is involved in the regulation of signaling molecules and membrane proteins throughout the cell.40 Previous research has demonstrated that SCD2, a critical factor regulating vesicle trafficking, is involved in the assembly and molecular transport processes of CCVs.28 To determine whether SCD2 plays a role in ABA transport, WT, AtSCD2-GFP-1 and atscd2-2 were treated with ABA, and the internalization process of the cells was observed using CLMS and FM4-64, an endocytic tracker for the PM internalization and endosomal trafficking. Our results showed that in the WT, the red fluorescence of FM4-64 is distributed equally. Following ABA treatment, the fluorescence intensity markedly increased, indicating that ABA, as a signaling molecule, could effectively trigger the endocytosis of the PM and the subsequent endosome-mediated vesicular transport (Figures 4A and 4B). In the AtSCD2-GFP-1 lines, the fluorescence intensity was higher than that of the WT. After ABA treatment, the red fluorescence dots aggregated and increased in quantity compared to the WT, indicating that the increase in AtSCD2 expression significantly enhanced the endocytosis process of the cell membrane induced by ABA (Figures 4A and 4B). In the atscd2-2 mutant, whatever the ABA treatment, FM4-64 fluorescence showed no significant change and was lower than that of the WT, indicating that the knockout of AtSCD2 completely inhibited the promoting effect of ABA on intracellular endocytosis and vesicle transport (Figures 4A and 4B). These findings demonstrate that the ABA-induced pathway of intracellular membrane endocytosis and endosome trafficking is dependent on AtSCD2.

To further verify the interaction between SCD2 and ABA, co-localization detection was performed using bioABA combined with fluorescence labeling technology. To enable the observation of the location of SCD2, we selected the AtSCD2-GFP-1 as the experimental material and treated it with bioABA. Furthermore, the negative control was not given any bioABA treatment. FM4-64 is a lipophilic dye that has been reported for monitoring cellular internalization processes.28 The results showed that in the root cells of the AtSCD2-GFP-1, the SCD2-GFP protein, the vesicles and endocytic structures labeled with FM4-64, and after being treated with bioABA and labeled with avidin (Alexa Fluor 405-conjugated),38 respectively, showed green, red, and blue fluorescence within the cells (Figure 4C). It is worth noting that in the fluorescence superimposition analysis, we not only observed the interference orange fluorescence formed by the superimposition of green fluorescence (SCD2) and red fluorescence (FM4-64), suggesting that SCD2 is co-localized with the vesicles, but also detected the interference white fluorescence formed by the superimposition of green, red, and blue (bioABA) fluorescence, indicating that bioABA, SCD2, and the vesicles labeled with FM4-64 all have a co-localization relationship (Figure 4C).

Additionally, to quantify the co-localization results, the fluorescence intensity within the cells was analyzed. The results showed that in the control group without bioABA, the peak intensities of green (SCD2) and red (FM4-64) fluorescence coincided at the same position (Figure 4D), further confirming that SCD2 is localized on CCVs; in the group with bioABA, the peaks and troughs of blue (bioABA), green (SCD2), and red (FM4-64) fluorescence were approximately in the same position, further indicating that bioABA can specifically be localized on the CCVs where SCD2 is located (Figure 4E). Meanwhile, the bright-field observation results showed that there was an overlapping area between the vesicle structure and the PM, and the PM exhibited a slight depression (Figure 4C), suggesting that the vesicle might be in the process of fusing or endocytosing with the PM. This provided morphological evidence for the vesicle-mediated ABA transport mediated by SCD2.

Notably, in the atscd2-2 mutant, blue fluorescence was only enhanced in the cell wall upon exogenous application of bioABA, and almost no blue fluorescence was observed in the vesicle; especially, there was no co-localization between blue and the red signal of the vesicles tagged with FM4-64 (Figure 4F). Moreover, the quantitative analysis of fluorescence intensity curves further confirmed that the curve trends of the blue (bioABA) and the red (FM4-64) appeared no overlapping between their fluorescence peaks and troughs (Figures 4G and 4H). These results not only indirectly prove the SCD2-mediated co-localization of bioABA with endocytic vesicles but also confirm the critical role of SCD2 in the internalization of bioABA through the CME pathway. Given that SCD2 is involved in ABA core signaling,16,17,19 our data strongly suggest that the extracellular ABA-triggered, SCD2-mediated endocytic trafficking of ABA may contribute to integrating cellular ABA homeostasis and signaling.

Discussion

The CME-generated CCVs may transport extracellular and PM-bound compounds into cells, at least including membrane systems, solutions, and inside macromolecules and micromolecules; thus, this group transport form contributes to cellular hierarchical regulation. In the present study, this group transport is first integrated into the SCD2-mediated endocytic vesicular trafficking of ABA, beneficial to the rapid establishment of a balance between plant growth and fitness.

SCD2-mediated endocytic vesicular trafficking is responsible for cellular division and enlargement essential to plant growth and fruit development

No doubt, cellular endocytosis may recruit membrane lipids, transmembrane proteins, and their associated extracellular ligands, including clathrin-dependent endocytosis and clathrin-independent endocytosis.41 Notably, unlike in mammals or yeast, plant endocytosis occurs independently of actin40; thus, CME largely occurs in plants.42 For example, Arabidopsis CCV formation is required for clathrin CHC and CLC as well as combined adapter proteins (APs), especially the CME-related proteins are characteristic to coiled-coil domains.42,43,44,45

Notably, SCD2 is the first reported to serve as a coiled-coil protein, which combined with SCD1, are essential to Arabidopsis cytokinesis and cell expansion.28 In the present study, we find that ABA contents in the SCD2-OE plant significantly increased while those in the knockdown mutations significantly decreased (Figure 3). This points to a link between SCD2 and ABA homeostasis underlying complex regulatory mechanisms. First, the SCD2 protein may selectively bind and transport ABA by endocytosis (Figures 2 and 4). This is consistent with previous reports that the expression of ABA importer ABCG40 is drastically upregulated in the atscd2 mutant, as a result of a rational ABA-homeostasis compensation mechanism.17,28 Second, the increased SCD2 expression caused yellowing of leaves and earlier seedlings senescence, along with the higher accumulation of ABA in the OE plant; vice versa, leading to the atscd2 mutant being quite dwarfed along with the decreased ABA contents (Figure 3), in line with the previous report.28 Similarly, a strawberry FaRRP1 homolog, homologous to Arabidopsis SCD2, participates in the fruit ripening related to ABA homeostasis.17 Altogether, the SCD2/RRP1-mediated CME and CCVs precisely regulate ABA uptake, accumulation, and homeostasis in plants, influencing plant growth and fruit development.

ABA promotes the SCD2-mediated endocytic vesicular trafficking of ABA essential to a transition from plant growth to adaptation as well as fruit ripening

Given that ABA is key to regulating plant stress responses and fruit ripening,17,46,47 once ABA specifically binds to the SCD2/RRP1 (Figure 2), the group transport is triggered by the interaction, which, for example, accelerates strawberry fruit ripening by integrating two core signaling pathways linked to “ABA-PYL2-ABI-SnRK2.6” and “ABA-ABAR-RIPK1-SnRK2.6.”19,48,49 To a large extent, visual imaging of ABA in CCVs provides substantial evidence for the endocytic vesicular trafficking of ABA in fine-tuning plant growth and fitness.

Indeed, by using bioABA and fluorescence-labeled avidin, ABA is first visually imaged on the PM of Vitis vinifera stomatal guard cells37; this process is now demonstrated to be mediated by the SCD2-mediated endocytic vesicular trafficking (Figures 1, 2, 3, and 4), which allows PM-integrated macromolecule transport. We also note that it was previously reported that ABA complexes linked by acyl hydrazone were prone to hydrolysis inside plants, this more or less affects plant phenotypes by ABA release.50 To this end, we analyzed the released ABA in the ABA- and bioABA-treated Arabidopsis seedlings and detected a tiny level of free ABA (about 0.3 μM) in the bioABA-treated plant compared to the ABA-treated seedlings by liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS; Figure S5). According to the previous report, 0.3 μM ABA may trigger the sensitive phenotype of Arabidopsis seeds, while 0.5 μM ABA can significantly inhibit seed germination.51 In this study, the 10 μM bioABA-treated plant detected only about 0.3 μM free ABA (Figure S5); reasonably, the 1 μM bioABA-treated seedling may not produce enough free ABA to fulfill a physiological function (Figure 1). Thus, we speculate that the bioABA-treated phenotype might be a result from the role of bioABA itself rather than its released ABA. This is consistent with not only the more structural stability of the amide-labelled ABA than the acyl hydrazone-labeled ABA50 but also the affinity of bioABA to SCD2 (Figure 2). Altogether, bioABA may be used to reflect the subcellular location of ABA.

It is interesting to note that, although bioABA could simulate some aspects of ABA’s physiological effects (Figure 1), it is distinct from natural ABA in the classical PYL-ABA-PP2C core signaling, since the 4′-carbonyl group of ABA is required for the canonical core signaling pathway. That is, the location interacts with PP2C’s conserved tryptophan “lock” (Trp-lock) by a water-mediated hydrogen bond network, stabilizing the PYL-ABA-PP2C trimeric complex and activating the downstream ABA signal pathway.52 However, the bioABA probe is hinged to the biotin group at the 4′-carbonyl position of ABA (Figure 1); this directly breaks the hydrogen bond network necessary for PYL activation. Thereby, unlike the classic PYL receptor-mediated ABA recognition mechanism, SCD2, as a core protein of the CME process, promotes the formation of CCV after binding to ABA/bioABA, enabling extracellular ABA to enter cells through the endocytosis pathway for intracellular ABA homeostasis. Altogether, the function of SCD2 may not require the 4′-carbonyl group of ABA, however, it is necessary for the ABA-PYL recognition; thus, the SCD2-mediated ABA trafficking pathway is distinct from the ABA-PYL-PP2C core signaling pathway. To a large extent, the two pathways spatially cooperate to maintain ABA homeostasis and signaling for the rapid regulation of plant growth and adaptation.

A previous study has demonstrated that, to stabilize CME in the Arabidopsis PM, SCD2 and SCD1 have to collaborate together, which colocalizes with the clathrin light chain at putative sites of endocytosis at the PM.28 Interestingly, the clathrin light chain also regulates clathrin-mediated trafficking of auxin signaling in Arabidopsis.44 The present study finds that SCD2 selectively binds to ABA at the C-terminal Lys-380 location, which meets with ABA to reach the CCVs. To a large extent, we consider that SCD1 may be responsible for the molecular switch function of vesicle transport, whereas SCD2 is responsible for ABA-specific function. In contrast to ABA-carrier pathways, we consider that the SCD2-mediated ABA-endocytosis pathway may integrate more biological molecules and events, ultimately facilitating cellular hierarchical regulation. That is, the scd2 not only reduces ABA contents and downregulates ABA signaling genes (Figure 3) but also affects the expression of jasmonic acid-, salicylic acid-, and cytokinin-related signaling components.17 To this end, we consider that a balance between plant growth and adaptation is rapidly established by the ABA-SCD2 integration of ABA trafficking and signaling through endocytic vesicular group transport.

Additionally, from the view of evolution, the unique SCD2 protein may be traced back to algae approximately 1,000 million years ago (∼1,000 mya) by a phylogenetic analysis (Figure S6), providing a clue for studying an evolutionary mechanism of SCD2 with ABA from lower plants to higher plants.

Overall, our current findings provide substantial evidence for the SCD2-dependent endocytic vesicular trafficking of ABA. This will open a new door for fine-tuning plant growth and fitness as well as fruit yield and quality by the SCD2-integrated ABA trafficking and signaling; this process is outlined in a proposed model on the basis of our current results and available data (Figure 5). At low ABA levels under favorable environments, the SCD2-CME and CCV-mediated group transport are responsible for plant growth by regulating the cytokinesis and expansion of cells.28 In response to environmental stresses or fruit maturity, high levels of ABA trigger the formation of CME complexes, which will integrate the ABA signal by combining ABA with SCD2 and ABAR/PYL2. On one side, the interplay of ABAR with RIPK1 promotes the kinase to phosphorylate FaTCP7, releasing its transcriptional inhibition of sugar transporter FaSTP13 and FaSPT expression, finally accelerating strawberry fruit sugar accumulation.53 On the other side, high ABA also promotes the interaction of PYR1/PYL2 with PP2C (ABI1/ABI2), releasing the inhibited activity of SnRK2.6, which phosphorylates KAT1 to promote stomal closure in Arabidopsis.36 Meanwhile, ABA inhibits SnRK2.6/OST1 expression; the decreased activity of SnRK2.6 relieves its inhibition of the binding of basic helix-loop-helix 3 (bHLH3) to the UFGT promoter, finally facilitating strawberry fruit anthocyanin synthesis.54 Altogether, the two ABA signaling pathways cooperate to balance plant growth and fitness as well as fruit ripening by the mutual phosphorylation between RIPK1 and SnRK2.6, which are integrated into endocytic vesicular trafficking.54 This study clarifies the fundamental role of ABA in the vesicle transport, broadens the functional research limits of plant CME, and establishes a theoretical and technological framework for improving plant resilience and controlling fruit ripening.

Suggested model for the ABA-triggered own uptake driven by the SCD2-mediated endocytic vesicular transport

At low ABA, the SCD2-CME and CCV-mediated endocytic vesicular trafficking regulates cytokinesis and cell expansion to support plant growth. At high ABA, ABA binds SCD2 to trigger its endocytic vesicular trafficking, which initiates ABA signaling by the interaction of ABAR/PYL2 with SCD2. On one hand, the ABA-ABAR-RIPK1-TCP7-STP13/SPT signaling accelerates strawberry fruit sugar accumulation. On the other hand, the “ABA-PYR/PYL-PP2C-SnRK2.6-KAT1” promotes stomal closure in Arabidopsis, and the “ABA-PYR/PYL-PP2C-SnRK2.6-bHLH3-UFGT” facilitates strawberry fruit anthocyanin accumulation. Moreover, the two ABA signaling pathways cooperate a balance between plant growth and fitness as well as fruit ripening by the mutual phosphorylation between RIPK1 and SnRK2.6, which are integrated to endocytic vesicular trafficking. ABAR, abscisic acid receptor; RIPK1, red-initial protein kinase 1; TCP7, teosinte branchen 1, cycloidea, and pcf; STP13, sugar transport protein 13; STP, sugar phosphate/phosphate translocator; PYR, pyrabactin resistance 1; PP2C, protein phosphatase 2C; KAT1, K+ channel protein; bHLH3, basic helix-loop-helix 3; UFGT, UDP-glucose: flavonoid 3-O-glucosyltransferase.

Limitations of the study

In this study, we utilized three types of fluorescence to provide evidence for the ABA-induced self-uptake driven by the SCD2-mediated endocytic vesicular transport. However, the dynamic process of endocytosis was not captured. Real-time dynamic observation of SCD2-mediated CME and CCV trafficking of ABA remains to be resolved with the available research conditions.

Experimental model and study participant details

Plant materials and growth conditions

The Columbia wild-type (Col; WT) Arabidopsis thaliana, AtSCD2 overexpression lines (AtSCD2-GFP-1; AtSCD2-GFP-2), and atscd2 mutant lines (atscd2-1; atscd2-2) are all maintained as part of the laboratory’s collection. The AtSCD2 overexpression vector used in this investigation was created by introducing the AtSCD2 coding sequence into the pCAMBIA-1300-GFP vector, resulting in effective expression and fluorescence detection. The atscd2 mutant was created utilizing two different methods. atscd2-1 (SALK_063106) is a T-DNA insertion line, whereas atscd2-2 was created via CRISPR-Cas9-mediated gene editing, which introduced a targeted deletion of 68 bp in the AtSCD2 coding region, causing AtSCD2 function loss.17 atscd2-1 and atscd2-2 correspond respectively to the rrp1-1 and rrp1-2 materials in Hou and Shen.17 The seedlings underwent surface sterilization with 75% ethanol and sodium hypochlorite, succeeded by a stratification phase of 2 to 3 days. Subsequently, the seeds were planted in MS solid medium and cultured vertically for 14 days under regulated circumstances. The culture conditions were sustained at a temperature of 23°C with a photoperiod of 16 hours of illumination and 8 hours of darkness.

Method details

Seed germination and cotyledon greening rate assay

Arabidopsis thaliana WT seedlings had their surface cleaned. Three different types of culture mediums were used to equally plant the disinfected seeds: MS medium, MS medium with 1 μM ABA, and MS medium containing 1 μM bioABA. Three separate biological replicates were established. After seeding, it was stored in a refrigerator at 4°C for cold stratification treatment. Two days later, it was moved to a light incubator for cultivation. The light incubator’s parameters are as follows: temperature 23°C, light intensity 60 μmol·m−2·s−1. Starting with the transfer of the seeds to the light incubator, the number of germinated seeds and greening cotyledons were counted every 24 hours for a total of 7 days. Seed germination is determined by the emergence of the seed radicle through the seed coat, and cotyledon color change is determined by their full expansion and the appearance of green. Seed germination rate = (number of germinated seeds/total number of sowed seeds) ∗ 100%. Leaf greening rate = (Number of seeds with green cotyledon/Total number of sowed seeds) ∗ 100%.

Calculating the length of the primary root

Choose WT Arabidopsis that were grown vertically on MS medium for 4 days and had consistent primary root lengths. Transfer the qualified seedlings to MS medium, including 15 μM ABA in the MS medium and 15 μM bioABA in the MS medium, and mark the original root length of the seedlings. After 4 days of vertical culture, the net growth length of the primary root was measured using a ruler.

Determination of ABA and bioABA content

Select the WT, AtSCD2-GFP-1, and atscd2-2 lines that grew vertically on the MS medium for 14 days, then freeze and grind them quickly with liquid nitrogen. We weighed 1 g of the product and determined the ABA content using the method given by Hou and Shen.17 Three independent biological replicates were established.

Select the WT lines cultivated for 14 days, and immerse them in 1/2 MS liquid medium containing 0 μM, 10 μM ABA, and 10 μM bioABA for treatment, respectively. Samples were collected from the entire plants at three different time points of 0 h, 12 h, and 24 h, and were quickly frozen for later use. The concentration of ABA and bioABA was subsequently determined using a liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry system by Nanjing Hanguang Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Nanjing, China). The 0 μM and 10 μM ABA groups served as the control.

RNA extraction and gene expression analysis

Total RNA extraction was performed following the protocol described by previous researchers.53 cDNA synthesis was conducted in accordance with the methodology outlined in the reverse transcription kit instructions (SparkJade, Shandong, China). Standard PCR reactions were conducted in accordance with the instructions supplied with the TransStart Top Green qPCR SuperMix (Transgen, Beijing, China). All primer sequences are listed in Table S1.

Protein expression and purification

The FaPYL2 CDS was inserted into the pET28a vector; the SCD21-300, SCD2301-572, SCD2K380G, and SCD2 CDS were inserted into the pMAL-C5X vector. After that, the plasmid was converted into competent Escherichia coli BL21 (DE3) cells. Induction was performed with 1 mM IPTG at 23°C for 12 hours. The protein purification procedure was followed as reported in Li et al.55

Isothermal titration calorimetric assay

According to Li and Shen,19 the protein was concentrated and the buffer replaced. To preserve the experiment’s univariate character, all solution circumstances were kept constant. Dilute the protein and small molecules to an appropriate concentration and conduct titration tests on the protein and small molecules using the ITC200 device (MicroCal, Northampton, MA, USA). The titration conditions followed the methodology given by Li and Shen.19 Origin matched to the curve.

Subcellular-level co-localization analysis

Following 14 days of growth, WT, AtSCD2-GFP, and atscd2 Arabidopsis seedlings were subjected to 1 μM ABA treatment for 48 hours. Subsequently, the seedlings were subjected to staining with 5 μM FM4-64 for a duration of 10 minutes. After staining, surplus dye was removed, and root tissues were prepared for observation under a laser confocal microscope to assess cellular localization and membrane integrity. The co-localization experiment of bioABA and AtSCD2 was performed following the approach established in prior research.38 The fluorescence intensity was determined using ImageJ software.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All data are presented as means of 3 independent biological replicates. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), correlation analysis, and independent sample t-tests were performed using SPSS (version 23.0, International Business), and the Waller–Duncan K‑ratio t‑test was used to analyze the significance of differences among treatments. The Origin software package (2021) was used to create bar charts p values of (∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01) indicated statistical significance.
