Real‐World Use of Emicizumab in Patients With Acquired Haemophilia A: An Interim Safety Analysis of a Large‐Scale Post‑Marketing Surveillance Study
Authors: Midori Shima, Katsuyuki Fukutake, Yoshiyuki Ogawa, Yuta Kamei, Chiaki Sugita, Akinori Yuri, Ryota Kobayashi, Akiko Ioka, Tadashi Matsushita
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionAcquired haemophilia A (AHA) is a rare autoimmune disorder where the development of autoantibodies to factor (F)VIII neutralise its function, leading to bleeding. Emicizumab has been approved for treating AHA in Japan.AimThis post‐marketing study was performed to primarily examine the use and safety of emicizumab, and indirectly assess effectiveness, for AHA patients in clinical practice in Japan.MethodsThis post‐marketing surveillance study is being conducted in patients with AHA who receive emicizumab and immunosuppressive therapy (IST). For each patient, the observation period is from the first day of emicizumab to 4 weeks after the last administration, up to 24 months. The primary endpoint is adverse events (AEs).ResultsThe first 51 patients who completed the survey were included in this interim analysis; 34 (66.7%) were male, and the median (range) age was 76.0 (33–91) years. Twenty‐five (49.0%) patients experienced 63 AEs (45 serious). One thromboembolism occurred: cerebral infarction, unrelated to emicizumab. Nine (17.6%) patients died, due to infection (n = 4), haemorrhage (n = 3) and ‘other’ (n = 2), with no causal relationship with emicizumab in any case. Excluding the Week 1 emicizumab loading dose period, 8 (15.6%) patients received recombinant FVIIa while on emicizumab. All patients received IST from Day 1 (median initial prednisolone dose: 0.93 mg/kg). Median FVIII activity (one‐stage assay) reached ∼60% in the patients who completed emicizumab treatment, and continued increasing after completion.ConclusionsThis post‐marketing study confirmed that emicizumab is well tolerated in patients with AHA, with no unexpected safety concerns. The benefit–risk profile of emicizumab remains favourable.

Introduction

Acquired haemophilia A (AHA) is a rare autoimmune disorder where autoantibodies to coagulation factor (F) VIII neutralise its function, leading to bleeding [1, 2]. The incidence of AHA is approximately 1.5 cases per million people per year [1], mainly affecting older adults (> 60 years) but also a small proportion of pregnant/post‐partum women [2, 3]. While the cause of AHA is idiopathic in approximately half of cases [2], contributing factors include autoimmune disease, malignancy, and treatment with certain drugs, for example, antibiotics or interferons [1, 2, 4]. The mortality rate is approximately 7%–38% [4, 5, 6, 7, 8], increasing with age [2, 6]. AHA treatment focusses on bleed management with bypassing agents (BPAs) [3, 9, 10] and elimination of the FVIII inhibitors with immunosuppressive therapy (IST) [3, 11].

Emicizumab, a recombinant humanised bispecific monoclonal antibody, bridges activated FIX and FX to facilitate FX activation, demonstrating cofactor activity that substitutes for activated FVIII [12]. The long‐term safety and efficacy of emicizumab for people with congenital HA, where endogenous FVIII is deficient, have been demonstrated via clinical trials and real‐world evidence [13]. As the structure of emicizumab is distinct from that of FVIII, it can function in the presence of FVIII inhibitors [14, 15]. In Japan, emicizumab was approved in 2018 for prophylactic treatment of congenital HA with/without FVIII inhibitors [16]. Despite reports of emicizumab use for AHA, the regimens used were inconsistent and not necessarily tailored to the disease pathology. Therefore, AGEHA, a prospective multicentre, open‐label Phase 3 study, was conducted to evaluate the benefit–risk profile of emicizumab for AHA, [17] leading to its approval in Japan in June 2022 [16]. Based on AGEHA, a 1‐week loading regimen was developed using pharmacokinetic simulations, with 6 mg/kg emicizumab administered subcutaneously on Day 1, followed by 3 mg/kg on Day 2, and 1.5 mg/kg weekly from Day 8. AGEHA enrolled both patients who had started IST and those ineligible for IST [18]. This population is considered to reflect clinical practice, but due to the rarity of AHA, the sample was small. Data from a larger sample in a real‐world clinical setting is warranted [17].

This post‐marketing surveillance study was a requirement for regulatory approval. The study was performed primarily to evaluate the appropriate use and safety of emicizumab and, indirectly, to assess effectiveness for AHA patients in real‐world clinical practice in Japan.

Methods

Study Design

This ongoing observational post‐marketing surveillance study (UMIN000048156) is being conducted in patients with AHA receiving emicizumab between 1 August 2022 and 31 July 2025. To avoid selection bias, eligible patients at each site were enrolled consecutively. Patients had an AHA diagnosis and received emicizumab at one of 37 participating medical institutions. Treatment with IST was not mandatory for study inclusion. As the study was designed to investigate thromboembolic event (TE) occurrence, assuming an incidence of 8.3% [18] and to achieve a 95% confidence interval of < 10%, 140 patients were required; considering potential dropouts, target enrolment was 150 patients. This interim analysis was planned to identify early safety signals and was scheduled for when 50 study report forms had been collected; owing to concurrent data collection, 51 cases were ultimately included in the analysis. For each patient, the observation period began on the day of first emicizumab administration and ended 4 weeks after the last administration, up to 24 months. Completion of emicizumab treatment was determined by the treating physician, with reference to guidance distributed to healthcare professionals, which cites the termination criteria from the AGEHA study (FVIII activity > 50 IU/dL) [17]. Discontinuation of emicizumab treatment was defined as stopping emicizumab due to death, adverse events (AEs) or hospital transfer. This study was conducted as a legally mandated activity; therefore, in accordance with the ‘Ethical Guidelines for Medical and Biological Research Involving Human Subjects’, patient consent was not obtained. This paper has been prepared to fulfil the obligation to provide information on proper emicizumab use, as stipulated by the Pharmaceutical and Medical Devices Act.

Endpoints

The primary endpoint was AEs, including causal relationship with emicizumab. Causality was attributed by the sponsor based on the treating physician's assessment, employing a policy where no downgrading of causality by the sponsor was anticipated. No criteria for assigning causality were defined in the study protocol. If a causal relationship with emicizumab could not be ruled out, the event was classed as an adverse drug reaction (ADR). Secondary endpoints included FVIII activity; FVIII inhibitor titre; and use of IST, haemostatic therapy and transfusions. FVIII activity was measured using a one‐stage assay (OSA) with ex vivo emicizumab neutralisation [19]. FVIII inhibitor titre was measured using a Bethesda assay with ex vivo emicizumab neutralisation [19]. Although this study had no efficacy endpoints, prevention of new bleeds after emicizumab initiation was evaluated based on administration of haemostatic agents. Use during the Week 1 loading period, when effective emicizumab plasma concentrations were assumed not to have been reached, was interpreted as management of the initial AHA‐related bleed rather than prevention of new bleeds, and so was excluded.

Data Collection and Analyses

A centralised registration system was used, whereby treating physicians completed a registration form and faxed it to the registration centre. Analyses were descriptive only. SAS version 9.4 was used for data analyses.

Results

Patient Characteristics

Overall, a total of 167 patients were enrolled at the time of this interim analysis (data cut‐off 16 July, 2024). The first 51 patients for whom the survey was completed were included (Table 1). Thirty‐four (66.7%) patients were male, and the median (range) age was 76.0 (33–91) years. Nineteen (37.3%) patients had an underlying condition at AHA diagnosis, most commonly neoplastic disease (15.7%) or autoimmune disease (11.8%); one patient was diagnosed with AHA post‐partum (Supplementary Results; Case 1).

Emicizumab Administration Status

There was a median (range) of 6 (1–56) days from AHA diagnosis to emicizumab initiation. The approved emicizumab dosage regimen for AHA is 6 mg/kg for loading, followed by 3 mg/kg on Day 2, and thereafter 1.5 mg/kg subcutaneously once weekly. The starting dose (Day 1) was 6 mg/kg for 49 (96.1%) patients (Table S1). The median (range) duration of emicizumab treatment was 43.0 (2–405) days. At cut‐off, 37 (72.5%) patients had completed treatment, 13 (25.5%) had discontinued treatment and 1 (2.0%) continued on emicizumab. Of the 37 patients who completed treatment, one subsequently died from interstitial lung disease. Reasons for treatment discontinuation were the following: AE of aspiration pneumonia, resulting in death (n = 1); hospital transfer (n = 5; 1 subsequently died due to cholangiocarcinoma); missed visit (n = 1); death (n = 5); and refusal of treatment (due to haemorrhagic shock and poor general condition; n = 1, resulting in death due to haemorrhage).

Immunosuppressive Therapy (IST)

All 51 patients received at least one type of IST. The median (range) time from AHA diagnosis to IST start was 0.0 (0–9) days (n = 32 with data available) (Table S2). Prednisolone (PSL) monotherapy was the most common IST, used in 29 (56.9%) patients throughout the observation period. Among the 48 patients with newly diagnosed AHA, first‐line IST was PSL monotherapy for 45 cases and PSL/cyclophosphamide (CPA) for one case. For patients who received PSL in combination with other IST, the median (range) time from PSL initiation to the addition of another therapy was 45.0 (0–205) days (n = 16 with data available). Median PSL dose was 0.93 mg/kg (mean [SD]: 0.79 [0.32]) prior to emicizumab initiation, 0.87 mg/kg (mean [SD]: 0.76 [0.30]) at emicizumab initiation, and 0.48 mg/kg (mean [SD]: 0.55 [0.26]) at emicizumab completion. Rituximab (RTX), which is used off‐label for AHA in Japan, was administered in combination with PSL to seven patients and with PSL plus CPA in one patient. The baseline characteristics and effectiveness and safety outcomes for these eight patients are provided in Table S3.

FVIII Activity and FVIII Inhibitor Titre

For the patients who completed emicizumab treatment, median FVIII activity (OSA) was 1.0 IU/dL (mean [standard deviation (SD)]: 2.4 [3.1]; n = 25) before emicizumab initiation and 59.1 IU/dL (mean [SD]: 63.3 [21.0]; n = 37) at completion. After emicizumab treatment completion, median FVIII activity continued to increase (Figure 1A; Table S4). FVIII activity was monitored for 4 weeks after emicizumab treatment completion and, in 25/37 (67.6%) cases, did not exceed the upper limit of the reference range (∼150 IU/dL).

Before emicizumab initiation, median FVIII inhibitor titre was 34.1 BU/mL (mean [SD]: 56.2 [72.6]; n = 25), while at completion, it was 0.9 BU/mL (mean [SD]: 2.7 [8.3]; n = 35). Median inhibitor titre then remained at < 1 BU/mL (Figure 1B; Table S4).

Mean FVIII activity was slightly higher in the group of participants who received PSL monotherapy throughout the study compared with those who received an additional IST agent (mean [SD]: 3.3 [3.9] vs. 1.3 [0.9] IU/dL); however, the medians were the same, at 1.0 IU/dL (Table S5). Both mean and median FVIII inhibitor titres were lower in the PSL monotherapy group compared with the PSL combination therapy group (mean [SD]: 27.8 [22.9] vs. 99.1 [98.7] BU/mL; median 21.4 vs. 83.8 BU/mL) (Table S5).

Safety

Overall, 25 (49.0%) patients experienced a total of 63 AEs; 45 were serious (Table 2). One TE occurred: a cerebral infarction, reported by the physician to be associated with haemorrhagic shock and unrelated to emicizumab (Supplementary Results; Case 2). No thrombotic microangiopathies were reported. Ten patients experienced a total of 16 AEs of infection; 13 were serious. Two ADRs were reported, both serious. One was acute pyelonephritis, resulting in emicizumab discontinuation (Supplementary Results; Case 3). The second was haemorrhoids, occurring in conjunction with haemorrhoidal haemorrhage; due to insufficient information, a causal relationship with emicizumab could not be ruled out (Supplementary Results; Case 4).

Nine (17.6%) patients died during the observation period; a causal relationship with emicizumab was ruled out in all cases. Eleven AEs leading to death were reported, including infection (n = 4; Table S6), haemorrhage (n = 3; Table S7) and one case each of interstitial lung disease (recurrent lung cancer) and cholangiocarcinoma with liver metastases (2 AEs in 1 patient). Haemorrhage‐related deaths included upper gastrointestinal bleeding (Supplementary Results; Case 2), bladder haemorrhage (treated with 8 doses of recombinant activated FVII [rFVIIa] and red blood cell [RBC] transfusion), and gastric ulcer bleeding (treated with 1 dose of rFVIIa and RBC transfusion). Surgical haemostasis was not attempted in any case. All three patients received PSL monotherapy, but FVIII activity did not recover, and they died within 2 days of bleeding onset, during which time almost no rFVIIa was administered.

Haemostatic Therapy and Blood Transfusions Before and After Initiation of Emicizumab

Before emicizumab initiation, 16 (31.4%) patients were not receiving haemostatic treatment or blood transfusions. Of the 35 (68.6%) who were receiving treatment, rFVIIa was used for 20 (39.2% of the total population), activated prothrombin complex concentrate (aPCC) for 4 (7.8%) and FVIIa/FX preparation for 4 (7.8%); 16 (31.4%) patients received blood transfusions (not associated with a surgical procedure), 1 (2.0%) patient received FXIII, and 1 (2.0%) received tranexamic acid monotherapy.

The number of patients who received BPAs decreased from 26 (51.0%) before emicizumab initiation to 21 (41.2%; all rFVIIa) after (Figure 2). Thirteen patients received rFVIIa in Week 1 (Figure 3A), which is the emicizumab loading‐dose period, when effective blood concentrations have not yet been reached. Excluding these 13 patients, 30 (78.9%) of the remaining 38 patients did not use rFVIIa at any time during the study, indicating no treated bleeds in these cases.

The number of patients receiving rFVIIa decreased over time after emicizumab initiation (Figure 2); however, in Weeks 5 and 7, the number of rFVIIa administrations increased in some cases (Figure 3B). At Week 5, two patients each received 14 doses of rFVIIa; neither had any rFVIIa during Weeks 1–4. For one, rFVIIa was administered due to haemorrhoid haemorrhage (Supplementary Results; Case 4); for the other, rFVIIa was administered due to melena. At Week 7, one patient had received 80 doses of rFVIIa (33 at Week 1, 6 at Week 2, 41 at Week 7). This patient had complications of FXIII deficiency and had undergone necrotic skin debridement following surgery for compartment syndrome of the lower leg at Week 6 (Supplementary Results; Case 5).

A total of 301 doses of rFVIIa were given following emicizumab initiation. More than half (181) were administered to four patients for bleed treatment (Supplementary Results). For three of these patients, FVIII activity was < 5.0 IU/dL at the time of all administrations of rFVIIa; the rFVIIa was administered 52 and 26 times for gastrointestinal haemorrhages in two patients and 23 times for a retroperitoneal haemorrhage (Case 2). For the fourth patient (Case 5), FVIII activity was < 5.0 IU/dL when rFVIIa was administered at Weeks 1 and, 2 but was high (88.8 IU/dL) at Week 7.

Among the 37 patients who completed emicizumab treatment, none received rFVIIa after the final dose of emicizumab to the end of the 4‐week observation period.

Discussion

This post‐marketing surveillance was performed to collect information on the use and safety of emicizumab in patients with AHA in real‐world clinical practice in Japan. At this interim analysis, there were no unexpected safety concerns for 51 patients with AHA treated with emicizumab, and the consistently low incidence of bleeding supports the potential role of emicizumab in effectively preventing AHA‐related bleeds.

A key strength of this survey is its high enrolment rate. Based on an incidence of 1.5 AHA cases per million people per year [1], which would result in an estimated 500 cases during the registration period of this study, and assuming around 70% of patients with AHA in Japan are treated with emicizumab, the 167 patients enrolled overall would correspond to a registration rate of over 40%. The characteristics of the patients in this interim analysis are consistent with the known clinical features of the general AHA population, including median age of 76.0 years and presence of underlying conditions such as neoplastic disease (15.7%) and autoimmune disease (11.8%), suggesting that the findings are generalizable to clinical practice across the country. There was a higher proportion of male patients (67%) than has been seen in previous studies, including those where patients were treated with emicizumab; however, there was notable variation across these prior reports, ranging from 43% in a surveillance study from the United Kingdom to 63% in the ORIHIME observational study in Japan, so it is difficult to draw any conclusions about whether AHA occurs more frequently in males or females, or equally in both [1, 2, 7, 17, 20, 21].

The median time between AHA diagnosis and emicizumab initiation was 6 days. One potential reason for this delay may have been that physicians understood that emicizumab had no haemostatic effect on acute bleeding and, with the intention of mitigating the risk of thromboembolism, they may have exercised caution regarding the administration of emicizumab while still using BPAs to control the initial acute bleed. A further potential reason is that, with AHA being a rare condition, it may have taken time to consult an AHA specialist to determine the best treatment plan or to transfer the patient to a specialised facility. Enrolment in this PMS study started in August 2022, which is soon after approval of emicizumab for AHA (June 2022); therefore, physicians treating the early cases may have been administering emicizumab for the first time and may have been cautious about prescribing it.

This post‐marketing study implemented the emicizumab dosing regimen approved in Japan (6 mg/kg on Day 1, 3 mg/kg on Day 2, and 1.5 mg/kg once weekly from day 8 onward). Although Japan is currently the only country where emicizumab is approved for AHA, further evidence from outside of Japan for the safety and effectiveness of this dosing regimen comes from the GTH‐AHA‐EMI study, where the same approach was used [20]. Although the use of IST varied between the two studies, taken together with the results of AGEHA [18], there is accruing evidence supporting the effectiveness and safety of this accelerated loading regimen.

No thrombotic microangiopathies or TEs related to emicizumab were reported, indicating a favourable safety profile. There were nine deaths (17.6% of patients), but none considered related to emicizumab. The rates of deaths in the EACH2 and GTH‐AH01 AHA studies, where no emicizumab was used, were 27.9% and 23.3%, respectively [2, 7]. The mortality rate in the present study was higher than that in a US cohort study (8.0%), which also reported real‐world data on emicizumab use in AHA [22]. This difference may be because AHA often results in deaths during early onset, with many patients in this study initiating emicizumab soon after diagnosis (median 6 days). This interim analysis was conducted on 51 cases where study forms were collected early, possibly resulting in greater accumulation of fatal cases. Mortality rates calculated at the final analysis, with data included for all enrolled patients, will provide clearer representation.

All 51 patients received at least one type of IST. The median initial dose of PSL administered was comparable to the 1 mg/kg/day recommended in AHA clinical practice guidelines [3, 10], at 0.93 mg/kg/day, suggesting that the guidance is being followed in Japan. Furthermore, the group of patients who received other IST agents in addition to PSL had a higher FVIII inhibitor titre at baseline, indicating that additional agents were administered in the necessary cases.

In the AGEHA study, the effectiveness of emicizumab was evaluated in people with AHA who were unable to receive standard IST due to high infection risk or were refractory to IST, and IST was tailored to individual patient status [18]. These tailored strategies included potentially deferring IST initiation, use of low‐dose IST, or omitting IST altogether. In this interim analysis of the post‐marketing study, all patients received IST. While the current dataset does not permit a comparative evaluation of alternative IST regimens or a robust assessment of the impact of earlier versus later tapering on infection risk, the findings from the AGEHA study suggest that prevention of bleeding with emicizumab may enable consideration of diverse, individualised IST approaches, such as delaying initiation or dose reduction, adapted to each patient's clinical circumstances. The relationship between IST intensity and infection risk will be examined in the final analysis of this post‐marketing study, where patients who received steroid‐sparing or low‐dose IST may be included.

For the patients who completed emicizumab treatment, median FVIII activity (OSA) reached ∼60% at completion. Although a comparison with a non‐IST group was not possible in this observational study, findings suggest that the combination of emicizumab and IST may enable recovery of FVIII levels in many patients; further clarity may be provided by the final analysis and future studies.

In this interim analysis, all patients received IST, with approximately half treated with PSL monotherapy; off‐label RTX was used in eight patients. The Japanese clinical practice guidelines for AHA recommend PSL monotherapy or combination therapy with PSL/CPA as first‐line IST. In contrast, RTX is not approved for AHA in Japan and is considered a second‐line treatment (off‐label use) for cases such as those resistant to PSL monotherapy or PSL/CPA combination therapy. In this context, the selection of IST regimens observed in this study is thought to reflect real‐world clinical practice in line with the Japanese guidelines, and suggests that RTX is unlikely to be administered as a first‐line therapy due to its off‐label status. Based on the baseline characteristics and AHA‐remission data of the eight patients who received concomitant RTX, these patients were considered refractory to PSL monotherapy. Infection AEs were reported in two of the eight patients (herpes zoster and Escherichia coli bacteraemia), but both were reported to have recovered or improved. Although the use of RTX as IST in combination with emicizumab has also been reported in a US multicentre cohort study [22], the evidence is insufficient to evaluate its efficacy and safety. Therefore, we believe that further careful consideration is warranted.

As RTX was only used for eight (15.7%) patients in this analysis, the IST regimens used by the majority of patients were similar to those administered in studies performed prior to widespread use of RTX in AHA. When comparing outcomes with these previous studies, none of which employed emicizumab, overall mortality [2, 7] and infection‐related mortality [1, 7, 8] were lower in the present study. This may reflect improved bleed control associated with emicizumab use lowering the rate of haemorrhage‐related deaths, or it may have enabled early withdrawal from intensive IST and/or implementation of diverse IST approaches tailored to individual patient circumstances, such as deferral or dose reduction, which may reduce the risk of infection‐related deaths. However, direct comparisons require caution given differences in patient characteristics, study design and observation periods.

Excluding the 13 patients who received rFVIIa during Week 1 (loading‐dose period), 30 of the remaining 38 patients did not receive rFVIIa while on emicizumab, indicating no treated bleeds in almost 80% of patients. This value is similar to AGEHA, where 9/12 (75.0%) patients had no treated bleeds during emicizumab treatment, and the GTH‐AHA‐EMI study, where 33/47 (70%) patients had no bleeding events (treated or untreated) [18, 20].

The real‐world data obtained here provide insight into the timing of emicizumab initiation relative to AHA diagnosis in clinical practice. The administration of rFVIIa for 13 patients in Week 1 suggests that AHA‐related bleeding had not completely stopped prior to initiating emicizumab. While initiating emicizumab may help to achieve haemostasis [18, 20, 23], there are additional considerations in cases where bleeding is severe. Starting emicizumab before achieving complete haemostasis in such cases may limit treatment options if additional haemostatic intervention becomes necessary, as guidelines indicate that aPCC should not be used alongside emicizumab unless it is therapeutically unavoidable [16]. This may result in use of large amounts of rFVIIa. Indeed, four of the 13 patients received > 20 doses of rFVIIa. Two of these patients had gastrointestinal bleeding, similar to a case in AGEHA, where 168 doses of rFVIIa were administered over 32 days [17]. These data suggest that the timing of emicizumab initiation should be carefully considered for patients with severe bleeding, particularly in the gastrointestinal tract.

Although not specified in the study protocol, emicizumab treatment was administered based on clinical symptoms and determination of FVIII activity. Emicizumab prescribing information states that the results of a coagulation test (including FVIII activity) and the patient's condition should be taken into account when considering emicizumab treatment termination [16]. Furthermore, guidance distributed to healthcare professionals following marketing approval provides the emicizumab completion criteria adopted in AGEHA (FVIII activity > 50 IU/dL) as a reference [16, 18]. For all 37 patients who completed treatment, FVIII activity levels were referred to when determining that emicizumab should be stopped. Median FVIII activity at treatment completion for these 37 patients was 59.1 IU/dL, indicating that the guidance for terminating treatment was adhered to, with no safety concerns identified in this interim analysis. No patients received rFVIIa after emicizumab completion, with no new treated bleeds observed. In countries other than Japan, various FVIII activity levels have been used as the cut‐off for terminating emicizumab treatment [20, 24, 25]; there have been no reports of TEs associated with hypercoagulability using the 50 IU/dL cut‐off.

One study limitation is the lack of a comparative control group. Further, as many (72.5%) patients had completed emicizumab treatment by the time the study forms were collected, the population may have included a high proportion with mild AHA who achieved early remission. The number of patients receiving long‐term emicizumab, for example, those with refractory AHA, was limited in this interim analysis; experience with emicizumab for all AHA patients will be reported at final analysis. Since bleed site and severity at AHA onset were not collected, it is not possible to evaluate baseline bleeding. For the 37 patients who completed emicizumab treatment, follow‐up was only 4 weeks after the final dose; therefore, the subsequent course of AHA, including FVIII activity, FVIII inhibitor titre, and BPA usage, was not evaluated.

Conclusions

This post‐marketing study confirmed the safety profile of emicizumab by evaluating real‐world clinical experience in a large cohort, employing the completion criteria used in AGEHA. Following approval of emicizumab for AHA, safety measures were implemented in Japan, including regular monitoring of FVIII and FVIII inhibitor levels and e‐learning courses for prescribers. This study demonstrates that these measures provide a reliable framework for emicizumab use for AHA in clinical practice. Following IST, FVIII inhibitors disappeared; nevertheless, infection incidence remained low, suggesting a contribution to patients' long‐term prognosis and quality of life. While the study was not designed to evaluate efficacy, the low incidence of bleeding supports the potential role of emicizumab in effectively preventing bleeds, reinforcing its positive benefit–risk profile at this interim analysis. The final analysis will provide further details on long‐term treatment and outcomes for AHA patients receiving emicizumab.
