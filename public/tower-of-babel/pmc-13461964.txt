Machine learning‐driven investigation on liquid–liquid phase separation‐related prognostic signature in diffuse large B‐cell lymphoma
Authors: Zhen‐Zhong Zhou, Jia‐Chen Lu, Zhao Wang, Rong‐Hui Chen, Hai‐Long Li, Wan‐Ting Chen, Yu‐Ning Deng, Hui‐Juan Zhang, Yu Wang, Xuan‐Ye Zhang, Wei‐Juan Huang, Xiao‐Peng Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryDiffuse large B‐cell lymphoma (DLBCL) is the most common aggressive non‐Hodgkin lymphoma and is characterized by substantial heterogeneity. This study aimed to develop a liquid–liquid phase separation (LLPS)‐related prognostic model to improve risk stratification. Transcriptomic and clinical data from four cohorts (n = 768) were analysed. Multiple machine learning algorithms were applied to identify prognostic LLPS‐related genes (LRGs) and construct a 6‐LRG model. Model performance was assessed using survival analysis, time‐dependent receiver operating characteristic curves and multivariable modelling. Additional analyses were conducted to explore potential biological and microenvironmental differences between risk groups. The 6‐LRG model stratified patients into groups with significantly different overall survival across datasets, with 1‐year area under curve (AUCs) ranging from 0.661 to 0.820, 3‐year AUCs from 0.683 to 0.779 and 5‐year AUCs from 0.711 to 0.807. The 6‐LRG model remained independent of established clinical variables and improved risk prediction when integrated into a nomogram. Distinct biological and immune characteristics were observed between groups. The 6‐LRG model may provide additional prognostic information in DLBCL and generates hypotheses regarding underlying biological mechanisms. However, prospective validation in larger populations is essential before any implementation.Keywords: diffuse large B‐cell lymphoma, immune environment, liquid–liquid phase separation, prognostic biomarker, prognostic model

INTRODUCTION

Among all non‐Hodgkin lymphomas (NHLs), diffuse large B‐cell lymphoma (DLBCL) possesses the highest incidence rate. 1 The Rituximab, Cyclophosphamide, Doxorubicin, Vincristine, and Prednisone (R‐CHOP) regimen remains the standard first‐line therapy and has achieved durable remission in a substantial proportion of DLBCL patients. 2 Nevertheless, due to molecular and clinical heterogeneity, treatment outcomes vary among DLBCL patients. 3 , 4

Liquid–liquid phase separation (LLPS) has been increasingly recognized as an important organizing mechanism in cell biology, 5 , 6 enabling biomolecules to form droplet‐like structures that facilitate interactions involved in RNA transcription, post‐translational modification and DNA damage response. 7 , 8 , 9 LLPS has been linked to cancer progression, 10 , 11 , 12 metastasis, microenvironment remodelling and drug resistance. 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 For instance, in DLBCL, Zinc Fingers and Homeoboxes 2 (ZHX2) activates Solute Carrier Family 3 Member 2 (SLC3A2) via LLPS to enhance ferroptosis resistance. 18

Despite interest in LLPS‐related genes (LRGs), their prognostic relevance in DLBCL is unclear. LRG signatures have shown value in solid tumours, 19 , 20 but their role in DLBCL has not been systematically investigated. Here, we established a 6‐LRG model to estimate the overall survival (OS) and biological differences of DLBCL patients.

METHODS

Data acquisition

We analysed five retrospective datasets, including three independent Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) dataset, 21 , 22 , 23 one GEO dataset used for gene discovery 24 and one in‐house dataset from Sun Yat‐sen University Cancer Center. The four cohorts with available survival data included 768 cases with corresponding clinical information (OS, age, stage, lactate dehydrogenase [LDH] ratio, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status, extra‐nodal involvement and cell of origin [COO] profiles). The specific role of each dataset is documented in Supplementary Table S1. The patient inclusion criteria were as follows: a pathological diagnosis of DLBCL and overall survival >1 month.

A total of 3611 LLPS‐related genes were retrieved from the DrLLPS database 25 (http://llps.biocuckoo.cn/). A single‐cell ribonucleic acid (RNA) sequencing dataset was retrieved from the heiData database (https://heidata.uni‐heidelberg.de/). 26

Samples and RNA microarray profiling

A total of 61 treatment‐naïve DLBCL formalin‐fixed, paraffin‐embedded (FFPE) samples obtained at initial diagnosis were included. All cases were histologically confirmed, and clinical and follow‐up data were retrieved from Sun Yat‐sen University Cancer Center. Patients' COO profiles were identified by the Hans algorithm. 27 This retrospective study used de‐identified, leftover diagnostic specimens and was approved by the institutional ethics committee (Institutional Review Board [IRB] exemption: G2025‐010‐01).

Total RNA was extracted from FFPE sections, and gene expression profiling was performed using the Affymetrix Human Genome U133 Plus 2.0 Array. Raw CEL files were processed using the Robust Multi‐array Average (RMA) algorithm for background correction, log2 transformation and quantile normalization.

Screening the prognostic LLPS‐related genes

Univariable Cox regression (p < 0.001) was first performed, followed by 10 distinct machine‐learning survival strategies—least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO), ridge, elastic net, stepwise Cox (forward, backward and both), CoxBoost, plsRcox, SuperPC, survival support vector machine, gradient boosting machine and random survival forest (RSF)—to screen for candidate LRGs. These base algorithms were assembled into stepwise modelling pipelines, typically pairing feature selection with model construction (e.g. LASSO + Cox, RSF + CoxBoost), with some algorithms also applied standalone. After excluding non‐convergent or invalid combinations, 117 distinct algorithmic pipelines were evaluated in the training cohort. Only the model exhibiting the highest mean C‐index across all validation cohorts was selected. Since neither cross‐validation nor an equivalent internal validation strategy was performed, the reported performance represents an optimistic upper bound achievable within these datasets. External validation in three independent cohorts serves as a safeguard against overfitting.

Risk scoring model construction

Coefficients for each LRG were derived by the Cox proportional hazards model. The optimal cut‐off was determined by maximally selected rank statistics using the surv_cutpoint function from the ‘survminer’ package, which evaluates all possible cut‐points and selects the value that maximizes the standardized log‐rank statistic between groups. The same cut‐off was applied to all validation cohorts to examine the model's effectiveness. Subjects with a risk score above the cut‐off value were assigned to the high‐risk group, whereas those with a risk score below the cut‐off were assigned to the low‐risk group.

Evaluation of the value of the risk scoring model

Kaplan–Meier (KM) analysis and 1‐ to 5‐year receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were applied to assess the performance of the 6‐LRG model, using the R packages ‘survival’ and ‘timeROC’. To validate its generalizability, the GSE10846, GSE53786 and Sun Yat‐sen University Cancer Center (SYSUCC) cohorts were used for validation.

Development of nomogram signature

To enhance clinical applicability, a nomogram comprising the 6‐LRG model and clinical indicators was developed. The prognostic independent markers were identified through the sequential univariable and multivariable Cox regression analyses. Furthermore, the nomogram was validated in all validation cohorts.

Exploratory biological analyses

To investigate the potential biological mechanisms underlying this risk stratification, we performed functional enrichment, microenvironment assessment, single‐cell transcriptomic inference, mutational profiles, drug sensitivity prediction and molecular docking. Detailed methodologies and parameter settings for the above analyses are provided in Supplementary Methods.

RESULTS

Identifying the prognostic LLPS‐related genes

Figure 1 outlines the overall analytical workflow. GSE56315 (55 DLBCL tissues and 33 normal lymphoid tissues) was adopted to discover differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between normal and DLBCL samples. A total of 2313 LLPS‐related DEGs were determined (Figure 2A,B). The RSF + StepCox [both] combinations exhibited the highest mean C‐index across all validations (Figure 2C); therefore, six LRGs (Dynamin 1 Like [DNM1L], Peptidylprolyl Isomerase F [PPIF], Proline, Glutamate And Leucine Rich Protein 1 [PELP1], Tropomodulin 2 [TMOD2], Nuclear Receptor Subfamily 3 Group C Member 1 [NR3C1] and Chromosome 1 Open Reading Frame 198 [C1orf198]) were chosen to construct the risk model.

Constructing the 6‐LRGs prognostic model

The risk score was calculated using a Cox proportional hazards model based on six LRGs, with coefficients shown in Figure 2D: Risk score = (−0.4963741 × expression level of DNM1L) + (0.6652714 × expression level of PPIF) + (0.4887417 × expression level of PELP1) + (−0.3526994 × expression level of NR3C1) + (−0.1424594 × expression level of TMOD2) + (−0.8219123 × expression level of C1orf198).

Evaluation of the 6‐LRGs prognostic model

KM analysis and time‐dependent ROC curves were used to assess the predictive power of the 6‐LRG model. Patients were stratified into low‐ and high‐risk groups based on a fixed cut‐off of −5.497 across all cohorts. Figure 2E,F shows the KM and ROC curves of the training cohort. For the training cohort, significant survival differences (p < 0.0001) were observed between risk groups. Time‐dependent ROC analysis demonstrated that the 6‐LRG signature achieved area under curve (AUCs) of 0.745 (95% Confidence Interval [CI]: 0.662–0.828), 0.779 (95% CI: 0.711–0.847) and 0.807 (95% CI: 0.739–0.874) at 1, 3 and 5 years respectively. Similarly, in the GSE53786 cohort, risk groups exhibited markedly different OS (p = 0.0004). The corresponding AUC values at 1, 3 and 5 years were 0.719 (95% CI: 0.591–0.848), 0.683 (95% CI: 0.555–0.810) and 0.748 (95% CI: 0.621–0.876) respectively (Figure 2G,H). With regard to the GSE10846 dataset, significant survival separation was also observed between groups (p < 0.0001), with AUCs of 0.661 (95% CI: 0.588–0.734), 0.707 (95% CI: 0.645–0.769) and 0.711 (95% CI: 0.644–0.778) at 1, 3 and 5 years respectively (Figure 2I,J). Consistently, in the SYSUCC cohort, the high‐risk group had worse OS (p = 0.012), and the AUCs at 1, 3 and 5 years were 0.820 (95% CI: 0.686–0.954), 0.741 (95% CI: 0.600–0.881) and 0.773 (95% CI: 0.618–0.928) respectively (Figure 2K,L). The distribution of risk scores and OS status is shown in Figure S1.

Construction and validation of nomogram model

Univariable and multivariable Cox analyses identified age, stage and risk score as independent prognostic factors (Table 1). Based on these variables, a nomogram was generated to predict 1‐ to 5‐year survival probabilities (Figure 3A). Time‐dependent ROC analyses demonstrated stable predictive performance superior to that of the 6‐LRG model across all cohorts, achieving AUCs ranging from 0.704 to 0.857 (95% CI, 0.581–0.939) at 1, 3 and 5 years (Figure 3B–E).

DISCUSSION

Although patients with DLBCL achieved favourable cure rates with current therapies such as R‐CHOP, a subset still exhibit refractory disease. 28 , 29 Conventional clinical indicators, like International Prognostic Index (IPI) and National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN)‐IPI, show limited prognostic value in the R‐CHOP era. However, their prognostic ability can be enhanced through the integration of molecular biomarkers. 30 , 31 , 32 LLPS, an emerging biological mechanism, has been increasingly implicated in malignant behaviours across multiple tumours. Understanding the role of LLPS in DLBCL may facilitate more precise risk stratification and provide insights into related research and drug development.

Utilizing multiple algorithms, 6‐LRGs—DNM1L, PPIF, PELP1, TMOD2, NR3C1 and C1orf198—were identified as potential prognostic markers. DNM1L plays a critical role in mitochondrial fission and regulates vesicle dynamics, 33 , 34 , 35 while PPIF/cyclophilin D regulates mitochondrial membrane permeability and protein folding through cis–trans isomerization. 36 , 37 PELP1 interacts with RNA and SC35, contributing to RNA splicing and ribosome assembly, which are essential for tumour progression. 38 , 39 TMOD2 regulates actin filament dynamics and membrane cytoskeleton structure. It has also been linked to colorectal cancer progression, although its role in lymphoma remains poorly understood. 40 NR3C1 encodes the glucocorticoid receptor, a well‐established regulator in leukaemia and lymphoma that has been demonstrated to induce super‐enhancer activation via LLPS, promoting drug resistance. 41 , 42 , 43 However, the biological function of C1orf198 in cancer remains largely unknown.

The combination of time‐dependent ROC and KM analyses indicates that the 6‐LRG model and nomogram demonstrate promising prognostic predictive ability and stability. The 6‐LRG risk score remained an independent prognostic factor in multivariate Cox analysis after adjustment for selected clinical variables and showed favourable predictive performance compared with individual clinical variables. The integration of the risk score into a nomogram further improved the risk stratification ability. Although direct head‐to‐head comparison with classical indicators such as the International Prognostic Index (IPI), revised IPI or NCCN‐IPI was not feasible due to incomplete IPI component data, our 6‐LRG risk score provided incremental prognostic information beyond the IPI constituents noted above. This finding is particularly noteworthy given that numerous studies have indicated that IPI and NCCN‐IPI scores exhibit limited prognostic predictive power in certain cohorts, and their integration with molecular biomarkers and multi‐omics data may offer enhanced prognostic accuracy. 30 , 44 , 45 , 46

If validated in prospective, larger, multi‐centre cohorts, the 6‐LRG risk model could potentially be adapted for targeted quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT‐PCR) or NanoString panels, which are compatible with both FFPE and fresh biopsy samples and are substantially less expensive than next‐generation sequencing. Therefore, the 6‐LRG model has the potential to complement existing risk stratification and may help generate hypotheses for pharmacological targets.

The biological mechanisms and potential therapeutic implications underlying the risk stratification are explored in Supplementary Discussion.

It must be acknowledged that several limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings. All datasets were retrospective, lacking prospective validation, and potential variability across populations may affect generalizability. Model selection based on validation cohort C‐index values aimed to reduce overfitting but may limit independence, warranting further external validation. The inclusion criteria minimized non‐DLBCL mortality confounding but may introduce immortal time bias. In addition, the lack of treatment annotation in public datasets may obscure clinically relevant heterogeneity, which could influence outcomes. Furthermore, the biological mechanisms by which LRGs regulate the tumour microenvironment and disease progression remain to be elucidated. Therefore, the 6‐LRG model should be considered exploratory and requires prospective validation in larger, well‐characterized cohorts before clinical translation.

CONCLUSION

In summary, this study presents a 6‐LRG prognostic model that effectively stratifies overall survival in retrospective DLBCL cohorts and provides hypothesis‐generating insights into underlying biological mechanisms. However, this model requires validation in larger prospective cohorts before considering any clinical implementation.
