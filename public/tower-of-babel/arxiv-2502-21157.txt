An Eulerian formulation for dissipative materials using Lie derivatives and GENERIC
Alexander Mielke

Abstract
We recall the systematic formulation of Eulerian mechanics in terms of Lie derivatives along the vector field of the material points. Using the abstract properties of Lie derivatives we show that the transport via Lie derivatives generates in a natural way a Poisson structure on the chosen phase space. The evolution equations for thermo-viscoelastic-viscoplastic materials in the Eulerian setting is formulated in the abstract framework of GENERIC (General Equations for Non-Equilibrium Reversible Irreversible Coupling). The equations may not be new, but the systematic splitting between reversible Hamiltonian and dissipative effects allows us to see the equations in a new light that is especially useful for future generalizing of the system, e.g. for adding new effects like reactive species.

Introduction

In Section

which is indeed given by a suitable Lie derivative, see Lemma

3.1
.

In addition to the momentum
{\bm{\pi}}=\rho{\bm{v}}
(with

\rho=\rho_{\mathrm{ref}}/\det{\bm{F}}
) and
{\bm{F}}
, we use the plastic distorting

{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}}\in\mathrm{GL}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
and a scalar thermodynamical field variable
\tau
for
form the state variable

. The variable
\tau
can be
chosen quite general, e.g. as density of internal energy, enthalpy, or
entropy or as the temperature.

In Section
3.3
we discuss how the total energy
{\mathcal{E}}(q)
and
the total entropy
{\mathcal{S}}(q)
as main objects in the GENERIC framework generate
thermodynamical driving forces that generate the evolution equations

\partial_{t}q={\mathbb{J}}(q){\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(q)+\partial_{\zeta}{\mathcal{R}}^{*}\big(q,{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{S}}(q)\big),

which include the balance laws of mechanics as well as the constitutive
relations. Here

is the Poisson operator, and
{\mathcal{R}}^{*}
is the
dual dissipation potential, see Section

. All the material
properties for the dissipative mechanisms are contained in
{\mathcal{R}}^{*}
and the
mapping
\xi\mapsto{\bm{j}}:=\partial_{\xi}{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(q,\xi)
is called the abstract kinetic
relation between the vector
\xi
of all thermodynamic driving forces and the
vector
{\bm{j}}
of all corresponding fluxes.

The main theoretical result of this paper is Theorem
3.3
that
shows that there is a straightforward way for the construction of a
Poisson operator
q\mapsto{\mathbb{J}}(q)
for Eulerian mechanics based on the general
properties for Lie derivatives. It is usually difficult to show that
{\mathbb{J}}

satisfies the so-called Jacobi identity (see (
3.1
)), but
exploiting the calculation rules for general Lie derivatives (in
particular the commutator rule (
2.3
)) they can be
established without too much efforts.

In Section
3
we then follow the full program of the GENERIC
framework and derive our Eulerian model for thermo-visco-elastoplasticity
using the multiplicative split
{\bm{F}}={\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{e}}{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}}
. One of the advantages of this
framework is that we are able to derive the reversible Hamiltonian part and the
irreversible dissipative parts independently, namely

\partial_{t}q={\bm{V}}_{\mathrm{Ham}}(q)+{\bm{V}}_{\mathrm{diss}}(q)\ \text{ with }{\bm{V}}_{\mathrm{Ham}}(q):={\mathbb{J}}(q){\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(q)\text{ and }{\bm{V}}_{\mathrm{diss}}(q):=\partial_{\zeta}{\mathcal{R}}^{*}\big(q,{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{S}}(q)\big).

This allows us to study the different physical effects separately, thus
providing a better overview on the physical principles.

Our final system can be written in terms of the variables
q=({\bm{\pi}},{\mathsf{w}})

where
{\bm{\pi}}=\rho{\bm{v}}
is the momentum and
{\mathsf{w}}=({\bm{F}},{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}},\tau)
, and it
takes the form

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{\bm{\pi}}

\displaystyle=-\mathfrak{L}_{\bm{v}}^{\mathrm{mo}}{\bm{\pi}}+\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\big({\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mathrm{Cauchy}}({\mathsf{w}})+{\mathbb{D}}_{\mathrm{visc}}({\mathsf{w}}){\bm{D}}({\bm{v}})\big),

(1.1a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{\bm{F}}

\displaystyle=-\mathfrak{L}_{\bm{v}}^{\mathrm{ve}}{\bm{F}},

(1.1b)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}}

\displaystyle=-\mathfrak{L}_{\bm{v}}^{\mathrm{in}}{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}}+{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}}{\bm{L}}_{\mathrm{vi.pl}}({\mathsf{w}}),

(1.1c)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\tau

\displaystyle=j^{S}_{\mathrm{Ham}}({\mathsf{w}})+j^{E}_{\mathrm{diss}}({\mathsf{w}})-\tfrac{\displaystyle 1}{\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}E({\mathsf{w}})}\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\!\Big({\mathbb{K}}_{\mathrm{heat}}({\mathsf{q}})\nabla\tfrac{\displaystyle 1}{\displaystyle\Theta({\mathsf{w}})}\Big).

(1.1d)

The first equation is the momentum balance including the Cauchy stress
tensor and the visco-elastic term with strain-rate tensor

{\bm{D}}({\bm{v}})=\frac{1}{2}\big(\nabla{\bm{v}}+(\nabla{\bm{v}})^{*}\big)
. The second equation
states that the deformation gradient
{\bm{F}}
is simply transported by a
(suitable) Lie derivative, and the third equation contains the visco-plastic
flow rule encoded by
{\bm{L}}_{\mathrm{vi.pl}}
. The last equation is a scalar

\tau
is chosen
as the density of the internal energy, (ii) to the heat equation for the choice

\tau=\theta=
temperature, or (iii) to the entropy (im)balance if we choose

\tau
as entropy density. We refer to Section
4.3
for the
explanation of all symbols.

At this stage we want to highlight that the
first three equations clearly show the relevance of
the Lie derivative
\mathfrak{L}_{\bm{v}}
, while in the fourth equation it will only
appear, if one chooses
\tau
as an intensive or an extensive variable.
Here we have added the superscripts “mo”, “ve”, “in” for “momentum”,
“vector”, and “internal variable” to the Lie
derivative
\mathfrak{L}_{\bm{v}}
to indicate that the Lie derivatives depend on the
tensorial nature of the variables
{\bm{\pi}}
,
{\bm{F}}
, and
{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}}
, respectively.

Section
5
concludes the paper by a discussion of the
developed theory, in particular
on the relevance of the proper usage of Lie derivatives and the GENERIC
framework.

2
Lie derivatives

The theory of Lie derivatives is a well-known tool in differential
geometry as well as in
Eulerian fluid and solid mechanics. It is relevant when tensors (functions,
vectors, forces, densities, etc.) are defined over a manifold and there is a
vector field
{\bm{v}}
on this manifold, see e.g.
[
Yan55
]
,

[
Cha83
, Cha. 1]
,

[
MaH94
, Sec. 1.6]
, and
[
MaR99
, Sec. 4.3]
. See also

[
MaH94
, footnote 26]
for historical remarks concerning the
first applications of Lie derivatives in
continuum mechanics.

2.1
Differential geometric approach

In our case the manifold is the Eulerian space
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
or a subset
{\mathsf{S}}

thereof, called the body in Eulerian description. The tangent and cotangent
spaces are
{\mathrm{T}}_{\mathsf{x}}{\mathsf{S}}={\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
{\mathrm{T}}_{\mathsf{x}}^{*}{\mathsf{S}}={\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{*}
, respectively.
A vector field
{\bm{v}}
on
{\mathsf{S}}
means that
{\bm{v}}({\mathsf{x}})\in{\mathrm{T}}_{\mathsf{x}}{\mathsf{S}}
for all

{\mathsf{x}}\in{\mathsf{S}}
, and a
co-vector field
{\bm{\alpha}}
satisfies
{\bm{\alpha}}({\mathsf{x}})\in{\mathrm{T}}^{*}_{\mathsf{x}}{\mathsf{S}}
. In the
differential geometric setting, a tensor

{\bm{A}}
of type
(i_{\circ},j_{\circ})
on
{\mathsf{S}}
, written as
{\bm{A}}\in\mathfrak{T}_{j_{\circ}}^{i_{\circ}}({\mathsf{S}})
, means that
{\bm{A}}({\mathsf{x}})
is a multi-linear mapping
from
({\mathrm{T}}_{\mathsf{x}}{\mathsf{S}})^{i_{\circ}}\times({\mathrm{T}}^{*}_{\mathsf{x}}{\mathsf{S}})^{j_{\circ}}
, i.e. linear in
each of the
i_{\circ}{+}j_{\circ}
arguments. By the dual pairing

({\bm{v}},{\bm{\alpha}})\mapsto\langle{\bm{\alpha}},{\bm{v}}\rangle_{\mathsf{S}}:x\mapsto\langle{\bm{\alpha}}({\mathsf{x}}),{\bm{v}}({\mathsf{x}})\rangle_{{\mathrm{T}}_{\mathsf{x}}{\mathsf{S}}}

we can identify
{\bm{v}}
with a tensor in
\mathfrak{T}^{0}_{1}({\mathsf{S}})
and
{\bm{\alpha}}
with
a tensor in
\mathfrak{T}^{1}_{0}({\mathsf{S}})
. The anti-symmetric tensors in

\mathfrak{T}^{0}_{j_{\circ}}({\mathsf{S}})
are the differentials forms denoted by the set

\Lambda_{j_{0}}({\mathsf{S}})
.

For a general vector field
{\bm{v}}
and a tensor field
{\bm{A}}
, the Lie derivative
is defined by taking the derivative of
{\bm{A}}
along the flow of

{\bm{v}}
. Throughout this section we assume that all objects like
{\bm{v}}
and

{\bm{A}}
are independent of time. First observe, that
{\bm{v}}
defines a flow map

s\mapsto{\bm{\Psi}}(s,\cdot):{\mathsf{S}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
such that the following ODE is satisfied:

\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}s}{\bm{\Psi}}(s,{\mathsf{x}})={\bm{v}}({\bm{\Psi}}(s,{\mathsf{x}}))\text{ for }s\in{]{-}\delta,\delta[},\hskip 20.00003pt{\bm{\Psi}}(0,{\mathsf{x}})={\mathsf{x}}.

The Lie derivative of
{\bm{A}}
along
{\bm{v}}
is now defined via

\mathfrak{L}_{\bm{v}}{\bm{A}}=\big(\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}s}{\bm{\Psi}}(s,\cdot)^{*}{\bm{A}}\big)\Big|_{s=0},

(2.1)

where the pull-back
{\bm{\Phi}}^{*}{\bm{A}}
of
{\bm{A}}
by
{\bm{\Phi}}
is defined via

{\bm{\Phi}}^{*}{\bm{A}}(x)\big[{\bm{v}}_{1},..,{\bm{v}}_{i_{\circ}},{\bm{\alpha}}_{1},..,{\bm{\alpha}}_{j_{\circ}}\big]={\bm{A}}({\bm{\Phi}}(x))\big[{\mathrm{D}}{\bm{\Phi}}{\bm{v}}_{1},..,{\mathrm{D}}{\bm{\Phi}}{\bm{v}}_{i_{\circ}};\big({\mathrm{D}}{\bm{\Phi}}\big)^{-*}{\bm{\alpha}}_{1},..,\big({\mathrm{D}}{\bm{\Phi}}\big)^{-*}{\bm{\alpha}}_{j_{\circ}}\big],

where
{\mathrm{D}}{\bm{\Phi}}
is evaluated at
x\in{\mathcal{B}}
, see Appendix

A
. In particular,
\mathfrak{L}_{\bm{v}}
is
again a tensor of the same order as
{\bm{A}}
.

Because we have
{\bm{\Psi}}(s,{\mathsf{x}})={\mathsf{x}}+s{\bm{v}}({\mathsf{x}})+O(s^{2})

we find
{\mathrm{D}}{\bm{\Psi}}(s,{\mathsf{x}})={\bm{I}}+s\nabla{\bm{v}}({\mathsf{x}})+O(s^{2})
and conclude

\displaystyle\mathfrak{L}_{\bm{v}}{\bm{A}}\big[{\bm{v}}_{1},..,{\bm{v}}_{i_{\circ}};{\bm{\alpha}}_{1},..,{\bm{\alpha}}_{j_{\circ}}\big]

\displaystyle=\big({\bm{v}}{\bm{\cdot}}\nabla{\bm{A}}\big)\big[{\bm{v}}_{1},...,{\bm{v}}_{i_{\circ}};{\bm{\alpha}}_{1},...,{\bm{\alpha}}_{j_{\circ}}\big]

(2.2)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{i_{\circ}}{\bm{A}}\big[{\bm{v}}_{1},...,(\nabla{\bm{v}}){\bm{v}}_{i},...,{\bm{v}}_{i_{\circ}};{\bm{\alpha}}_{1},...,{\bm{\alpha}}_{j_{\circ}}\big]

\displaystyle-\sum_{j=1}^{j_{\circ}}{\bm{A}}\big[{\bm{v}}_{1},...,{\bm{v}}_{i_{\circ}};{\bm{\alpha}}_{1},...,\big(\nabla{\bm{v}})^{*}{\bm{\alpha}}_{j},...,{\bm{\alpha}}_{j_{\circ}}\big].

One of the fundamental properties of Lie derivatives is the commutator relation

\mathfrak{L}_{\bm{v}}\big(\mathfrak{L}_{\bm{w}}{\bm{T}}\big)-\mathfrak{L}_{\bm{w}}\big(\mathfrak{L}_{\bm{v}}{\bm{T}}\big)=\mathfrak{L}_{[\hskip-0.90417pt[{\bm{v}},{\bm{w}}]\hskip-0.90417pt]}{\bm{T}},

(2.3)

where the commutator between vector fields is given by

[\hskip-1.29167pt[{\bm{v}},{\bm{w}}]\hskip-1.29167pt]:=(\nabla{\bm{w}}){\bm{v}}-(\nabla{\bm{v}}){\bm{w}}=({\bm{v}}{\bm{\cdot}}\nabla){\bm{w}}-({\bm{w}}{\bm{\cdot}}\nabla){\bm{v}}=\mathfrak{L}_{\bm{v}}{\bm{w}}=-\mathfrak{L}_{\bm{w}}{\bm{v}}.

It is easily checked that we have the Jacobi identity for vector fields, viz.

\big[\!\big[{\bm{v}}_{1},[\hskip-1.29167pt[{\bm{v}}_{2},{\bm{v}}_{3}]\hskip-1.29167pt]\big]\!\big]+\big[\!\big[{\bm{v}}_{2},[\hskip-1.29167pt[{\bm{v}}_{3},{\bm{v}}_{1}]\hskip-1.29167pt]\big]\!\big]+\big[\!\big[{\bm{v}}_{3},[\hskip-1.29167pt[{\bm{v}}_{1},{\bm{v}}_{2}]\hskip-1.29167pt]\big]\!\big]=0\ \text{ for all }{\bm{v}}_{1},{\bm{v}}_{2},{\bm{v}}_{3}\in\mathfrak{T}^{1}({\mathsf{S}}).

(2.4)

The identities (
2.3
) and (
2.4
) will be
extremely useful for showing that
the the operator
{\mathbb{J}}
to be constructed satisfies the Jacobi identity, and
hence is a Poisson structure. It is not easy to find explicit statements of
(
2.3
) in the literature,
but it is an easy consequence of its validity for functions, vectors, and
co-vectors (
1
-forms) and of the well-known derivation rule

\mathfrak{L}_{\bm{v}}({\bm{T}}{\otimes}{\bm{S}})=(\mathfrak{L}_{\bm{v}}{\bm{T}}){\otimes}{\bm{S}}+{\bm{T}}{\otimes}(\mathfrak{L}_{\bm{v}}{\bm{S}})
for tensor products by doing induction over the rank
of the tensors (see e.g. condition “(DO1)” in
[
AbM78
, Ch. 2]
).

Here the tensor product of
{\bm{A}}\in\mathfrak{T}^{i_{\circ}}_{j_{\circ}}({\mathsf{S}})
and

{\bm{A}}\in\mathfrak{T}^{n_{\circ}}_{m_{\circ}}({\mathsf{S}})
is the tensor
{\bm{A}}{\otimes}{\bm{B}}\in\mathfrak{T}^{i_{\circ}+n_{\circ}}_{j_{\circ}+m_{\circ}}({\mathsf{S}})
defined via

\displaystyle{\bm{A}}{\otimes}{\bm{B}}[{\bm{v}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{v}}_{i_{\circ}+n_{\circ}};{\bm{\alpha}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{\alpha}}_{j_{\circ}+m_{\circ}}]

\displaystyle:={\bm{A}}[{\bm{v}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{v}}_{i_{\circ}};{\bm{\alpha}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{\alpha}}_{j_{\circ}}]

\displaystyle\cdot{\bm{B}}[{\bm{v}}_{i_{\circ}+1},\ldots,{\bm{v}}_{i_{\circ}+n_{\circ}};{\bm{\alpha}}_{j_{\circ}+1},\ldots,{\bm{\alpha}}_{j_{\circ}+m_{\circ}}].

Inner products can be defined by tensor products and subsequent contraction of
indices. For
{\bm{A}}\in\mathfrak{T}^{i_{\circ}}_{j_{\circ}}({\mathsf{S}})
and
n,m\in{\mathbb{N}}
with

1\leq n\leq i_{\circ}
and
1\leq m\leq j_{\circ}
we can contract the
n
th
vector slot with the
m
co-vector slot to obtain
\mathsf{C}^{n}_{m}{\bm{A}}\in\mathfrak{T}^{i_{\circ}-1}_{j_{\circ}-1}({\mathsf{S}})
defined via

\displaystyle\mathsf{C}^{n}_{m}{\bm{A}}

\displaystyle\big[{\bm{v}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{v}}_{i_{\circ}-1};{\bm{\alpha}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{\alpha}}_{j_{\circ}-1}\big]

\displaystyle:=\sum_{k=1}^{d}{\bm{A}}\big[{\bm{v}}_{1},..,{\bm{v}}_{k-1},{\bm{e}}_{k},{\bm{v}}_{k},..,{\bm{v}}_{i_{\circ}-1};{\bm{\alpha}}_{1},..,{\bm{\alpha}}_{m-1},{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{k},{\bm{\alpha}}_{m},\ldots,{\bm{\alpha}}_{j_{\circ}-1}\big],

where
\{{\bm{e}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{e}}_{d}\}
is an arbitrary basis in
{\mathrm{T}}_{\mathsf{x}}{\mathsf{S}}
and

{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{k}\in{\mathrm{T}}_{\mathsf{x}}^{*}{\mathsf{S}}
satisfy
\langle{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{k},{\bm{e}}_{l}\rangle=\delta_{k,l}
(the Kronecker symbol) for
k,l\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
.

2.2
Differential forms

The special inner product with a vector field
{\bm{w}}
is denoted by
{\bm{i}}_{w}:\mathfrak{T}^{i_{\circ}+1}_{j_{\circ}}({\mathsf{S}})\to\mathfrak{T}^{i_{\circ}}_{j_{\circ}}({\mathsf{S}})
and is
given by

{\bm{i}}_{\bm{w}}{\bm{A}}\big[{\bm{v}}_{1},..,{\bm{v}}_{i_{\circ}};{\bm{\alpha}}_{1},..,{\bm{\alpha}}_{j_{\circ}}\big]={\bm{A}}\big[{\bm{w}},{\bm{v}}_{1},..,{\bm{v}}_{i_{\circ}};{\bm{\alpha}}_{1},..,{\bm{\alpha}}_{j_{\circ}}\big],

i.e. by simple insertion into the first vector slot. Identifying
{\bm{w}}
with
the tensor
{\bm{B}}[{\bm{\alpha}}]=\langle{\bm{\alpha}},{\bm{w}}\rangle
we have the relation

{\bm{i}}_{\bm{w}}{\bm{A}}=\mathsf{C}^{1}_{1}\big({\bm{A}}{\otimes}{\bm{B}}\big)
. It is not difficult to
see that Lie derivatives commute with contraction, hence we also have the
product rule for inner products:

\mathfrak{L}_{\bm{v}}\mathsf{C}^{n}_{m}{\bm{A}}=C^{n}_{m}\mathfrak{L}_{\bm{v}}{\bm{A}}\hskip 10.00002pt\mathfrak{L}_{\bm{v}}\big({\bm{i}}_{\bm{w}}{\bm{A}})={\bm{i}}_{\bm{w}}(\mathfrak{L}_{\bm{v}}{\bm{A}})+{\bm{i}}_{\mathfrak{L}_{\bm{v}}{\bm{w}}}{\bm{A}}.

An important subclass of tensor is given by the differential forms that
appear as
k
-forms for
k\in\{0,\ldots,d\}
. They are given as tensors

{\bm{\beta}}\in\Lambda_{m}({\bm{S}})\subset\mathfrak{T}^{0}_{k}({\mathsf{S}})
, where
\Lambda_{m}

denotes the subset of tensors that are anti-symmetric, i.e. interchanging any
two arguments changes the result by a factor
(-1)
. One can define the
differential
{\bm{d}}:\Lambda_{k}({\bm{S}})\to\Lambda_{k+1}({\mathsf{S}})
and has

{\bm{i}}_{\bm{w}}:\Lambda_{k+1}({\bm{S}})\to\Lambda_{k}({\bm{S}})
. For differential forms

{\bm{\beta}}
one has the identities

\mathfrak{L}_{\bm{v}}{\bm{\beta}}={\bm{i}}_{\bm{v}}{\bm{d}}{\bm{\beta}}+{\bm{d}}({\bm{i}}_{\bm{v}}{\bm{\beta}})\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\mathfrak{L}_{\bm{v}}({\bm{d}}{\bm{\beta}})={\bm{d}}\big(\mathfrak{L}_{\bm{v}}{\bm{\beta}}\big),

where the first one is called “Cartan’s magic formula” (also known as
Cartan’s first structural equation) and the second follows easily
from the first when using
{\bm{d}}{\bm{d}}{\bm{\gamma}}=0
for all differential forms

{\bm{\gamma}}
.

The wedge product
\wedge
maps
\Lambda_{k}({\mathsf{S}})\times\Lambda_{l}({\mathsf{S}})
into

\Lambda_{k+l}({\mathsf{S}})
by
{\bm{\beta}}\wedge{\bm{\gamma}}=\mathrm{anti}({\bm{\beta}}{\otimes}{\bm{\gamma}})
and satisfies

\mathfrak{L}_{\bm{v}}({\bm{\beta}}\wedge{\bm{\gamma}})=\big(\mathfrak{L}_{\bm{v}}{\bm{\beta}}\big)\wedge{\bm{\gamma}}+{\bm{\beta}}\wedge\big(\mathfrak{L}_{\bm{v}}{\bm{\gamma}}\big).

2.3
Tensors with values in a linear space

The above theory can easily generalized to the case that the tensors
{\bm{A}}(x)
do
not map into
{\mathbb{R}}
but into a general linear space
{\bm{U}}_{\mathsf{x}}
, i.e.

{\bm{A}}(x):({\mathrm{T}}_{\mathsf{x}}{\mathsf{S}})^{i_{\circ}}\times({\mathrm{T}}_{\mathsf{x}}^{*}{\mathsf{S}})^{j_{\circ}}\to{\bm{U}}_{\mathsf{x}}

is still multi-linear in
({\bm{v}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{v}}_{i_{\circ}};{\bm{\alpha}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{\alpha}}_{j_{\circ}})
. We will then speak of

{\bm{U}}
-valued tensors and remark that
{\bm{U}}_{\mathsf{x}}
has to be independent of

{\mathrm{T}}_{\mathsf{x}}{\mathsf{S}}
and
{\mathrm{T}}_{\mathsf{x}}^{*}{\mathsf{S}}
, which means that the vector-field
{\bm{v}}
is
not moving the points in
{\bm{U}}_{\mathsf{x}}
.

2.4
Lie derivatives of vectors, operators, and volume forms

A proper usage of differential geometric concepts in continuum mechanics
is not standard, but we refer to
[
Seg23
]
for a careful and detailed
approach in this direction. Traditionally, it is more common to use vector,
co-vector and matrices, called tensors there. To distinguish these notions we
use the name “operator” for matrices acting as linear mappings between linear
spaces.

Even for scalar-valued functions the Lie derivatives is not simple, because
functions in continuum mechanics occur in different forms. First one has

intensive
and
extensive
functions, which have the differential
geometric interpretation as
0
-forms (i.e.
f\in\Lambda_{0}({\mathsf{S}})
) and
volume or
d
-form (i.e.
{\bm{\rho}}\in\Lambda_{d}({\mathsf{S}})
), respectively. In the
latter case identify a function
\rho
with the volume form

{\bm{\rho}}[{\bm{v}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{v}}_{d}]=\rho\,\det\big({\bm{v}}_{1}|..|{\bm{v}}_{d}\big)
, and we obtain

\mathfrak{L}_{\bm{v}}f={\bm{v}}{\cdot}\nabla f\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\mathfrak{L}_{\bm{v}}\rho=\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits(\rho{\bm{v}}).

(2.5)

In continuum mechanics the typical extensive variables are mass density
\rho
,
internal-energy density
e
, entropy density
s
, or any one-homogeneous
function of those. The typical intensive variables are concentrations,
temperature
\theta={\mathrm{d}}e/{\mathrm{d}}s
, velocity, pressure, or chemical
potentials. For general functions, not falling into the classes of intensive or
extensive functions, the Lie derivative has to be obtained by representing it
via intensive and extensive variables and then applying the chain rule and the
appropriate Lie derivatives.

A vector field
{\bm{w}}
and a co-vector field
{\bm{\alpha}}
can be
identified with tensors
{\bm{A}}_{\bm{w}}\in\mathfrak{T}^{0}_{1}({\mathsf{S}})
and
{\bm{B}}_{\bm{\beta}}\in\mathfrak{T}^{1}_{0}({\mathsf{S}})
, respectively, by

{\bm{A}}_{\bm{w}}[{\bm{\alpha}}]=\langle{\bm{\alpha}},{\bm{w}}\rangle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt{\bm{B}}_{\bm{\beta}}[{\bm{v}}]=\langle{\bm{\beta}},{\bm{v}}\rangle.

Using this, we can derive the form of the Lie derivatives from the tensor
Lie derivatives, namely

\mathfrak{L}_{\bm{v}}{\bm{w}}={\bm{v}}{\bm{\cdot}}\nabla{\bm{w}}-(\nabla{\bm{v}}){\bm{w}}=[\hskip-1.29167pt[{\bm{v}},{\bm{w}}]\hskip-1.29167pt]\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\mathfrak{L}_{\bm{v}}{\bm{\beta}}={\bm{v}}{\bm{\cdot}}\nabla{\bm{\beta}}+(\nabla{\bm{v}})^{*}{\bm{\beta}}.

(2.6)

Moreover, in continuum mechanics the momentum vector
{\bm{\pi}}=\rho I_{\mathrm{R}}{\bm{v}}
(where

I_{\mathrm{R}}:{\mathrm{T}}_{\mathsf{x}}{\mathsf{S}}\to{\mathrm{T}}_{\mathsf{x}}^{*}{\mathsf{S}})
is the Riesz isomorphism) is a
co-vector but its correct interpretation is as a
(d{-}1)
-form (by the Hodge
star operator mapping
\Lambda_{k}({\mathsf{S}})
into
\Lambda_{d-k}({\mathsf{S}})
), namely

{\bm{\pi}}=\rho I_{\mathrm{R}}{\bm{v}}={\bm{i}}_{\bm{v}}{\bm{\rho}}.

(2.7a)

This leads to the Lie derivative

\mathfrak{L}_{\bm{v}}{\bm{\pi}}=\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits({\bm{\pi}}{\otimes}{\bm{v}})+(\nabla{\bm{v}})^{*}{\bm{\pi}},

(2.7b)

see
[
MiR25
, Prop. 4.2]
.
A “vector” may also be considered as a tensor with values in the linear

{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
which is not connected to
{\mathrm{T}}_{\mathsf{x}}{\mathsf{S}}
. Such a case is occurs in

[
BN∗24
, Eqn. (2.3)]
, where the magnetization vector
{\bm{M}}(t,{\mathsf{x}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
has the Lie derivative
\mathfrak{L}^{\mathrm{mag}}_{\bm{v}}{\bm{M}}={\bm{v}}{\bm{\cdot}}\nabla M+(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits{\bm{v}}){\bm{M}}=\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\big({\bm{M}}{\otimes}{\bm{v}}\big)
. The proper tensorial
interpretation of “flux fields” is reported in

[
Seg23
, p. 71]
:
“A flux field, such as the heat flux
field, should be regarded as a 2-form in the three-dimensional body rather than as a
vector field. As a 2-form, a heat flux field may be considered independently of the
configuration of the body in space.”

We have four types of operators, namely

{\mathbb{B}}:{\mathrm{T}}_{\mathsf{x}}{\mathsf{S}}\to{\mathrm{T}}_{\mathsf{x}}{\mathsf{S}},\hskip 10.00002pt{\mathbb{C}}:{\mathrm{T}}_{\mathsf{x}}{\mathsf{S}}\to{\mathrm{T}}_{\mathsf{x}}^{*}{\mathsf{S}},\hskip 10.00002pt{\mathbb{D}}:{\mathrm{T}}_{\mathsf{x}}^{*}{\mathsf{S}}\to{\mathrm{T}}_{\mathsf{x}}^{*}{\mathsf{S}},\hskip 10.00002pt{\mathbb{E}}:{\mathrm{T}}_{\mathsf{x}}^{*}{\mathsf{S}}\to{\mathrm{T}}_{\mathsf{x}}{\mathsf{S}}.

They can be identified with tensors
{\bm{B}}\in\mathfrak{T}_{1}^{1}
,
{\bm{C}}\in\mathfrak{T}^{2}_{0}
,

{\bm{D}}\in\mathfrak{T}_{1}^{1}
, and
{\bm{E}}\in\mathfrak{T}_{2}^{0}
via

{\bm{B}}[{\bm{v}},{\bm{\alpha}}]=\langle{\bm{\alpha}},{\mathbb{B}}{\bm{v}}\rangle,\hskip 10.00002pt{\bm{C}}[{\bm{v}}_{1},{\bm{v}}_{2}]=\langle{\mathbb{C}}{\bm{v}}_{1},{\bm{v}}_{2}\rangle,\hskip 10.00002pt{\bm{D}}[{\bm{v}},{\bm{\alpha}}]=\langle{\mathbb{D}}{\bm{\alpha}},{\bm{v}}\rangle,\hskip 10.00002pt{\bm{E}}[{\bm{\alpha}}_{1},{\bm{\alpha}}_{2}]=\langle{\bm{\alpha}}_{1},{\mathbb{E}}{\bm{\alpha}}_{2}\rangle.

Thus, the corresponding Lie derivatives can be calculated by those for
{\bm{B}}

to
{\bm{E}}
and we obtain

\displaystyle\mathfrak{L}_{\bm{v}}{\mathbb{B}}={\bm{v}}{\bm{\cdot}}\nabla{\mathbb{B}}+{\mathbb{B}}(\nabla{\bm{v}})-(\nabla{\bm{v}}){\mathbb{B}},

\displaystyle\mathfrak{L}_{\bm{v}}{\mathbb{C}}={\bm{v}}{\bm{\cdot}}\nabla{\mathbb{C}}+{\mathbb{C}}(\nabla{\bm{v}})+(\nabla{\bm{v}})^{*}{\mathbb{C}},

(2.8)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{L}_{\bm{v}}{\mathbb{D}}={\bm{v}}{\bm{\cdot}}\nabla{\mathbb{C}}-{\mathbb{D}}(\nabla{\bm{v}})^{*}+(\nabla{\bm{v}})^{*}{\mathbb{D}},

\displaystyle\mathfrak{L}_{\bm{v}}{\mathbb{E}}={\bm{v}}{\bm{\cdot}}\nabla{\mathbb{E}}-{\mathbb{E}}(\nabla{\bm{v}})^{*}-(\nabla{\bm{v}}){\mathbb{E}}.

Note that only
{\mathbb{C}}
and
{\mathbb{E}}
can be symmetric tensors and that in this
case the two signs of the second and third terms are the same and one times we
have
\nabla{\bm{v}}
and the other time
(\nabla{\bm{v}})^{*}
. See also

[
MaH94
, Ch. 1 Box 6.1]
.

As an example consider the Euclidean tensor
{\bm{C}}_{\mathrm{Euc}}\in\mathfrak{T}^{2}_{0}
with

{\bm{C}}_{\mathrm{Euc}}[{\bm{v}}_{1},{\bm{v}}_{2}]=({\bm{v}}_{1}|{\bm{v}}_{2})_{\mathrm{Euc}}
(the Euclidean
scalar product). The associated operator

{\mathbb{C}}_{\mathrm{Euc}}={\mathbb{I}}_{\mathrm{Riesz}}:{\mathrm{T}}_{\mathsf{x}}{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\to{\mathrm{T}}^{*}_{\mathsf{x}}{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
is the Riesz isomorphism. The Lie derivative provides twice the
classical strain-rate tensor

\mathfrak{L}_{\bm{v}}{\mathbb{C}}_{\mathrm{Euc}}=2\,{\bm{D}}({\bm{v}})\hskip 10.00002pt\text{with }{\bm{D}}({\bm{v}}):=\frac{1}{2}\big({\mathbb{I}}_{\mathrm{Riesz}}\nabla{\bm{v}}+(\nabla{\bm{v}})^{*}{\mathbb{I}}_{\mathrm{Riesz}}\big)\in\mathrm{Lin_{sym}}\big({\mathbb{R}}^{3};({\mathbb{R}}^{3})^{*}\big).

(2.9)

There are various objective stress rates which arise from the fact that there
are various stress tensor which have different tensorial properties.
Similar to the construction of the momentum as a
(d{-}1)
form
(
2.7
) one also has to consider the Cauchy stress tensors

{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mathrm{Cauchy}}
as an extensive version, and its Lie derivative is the so-called
Truesdell stress rate

\mathfrak{L}_{\bm{v}}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mathrm{Cauchy}}={\bm{v}}{\bm{\cdot}}\nabla{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mathrm{Cauchy}}+(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits{\bm{v}}){\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mathrm{Cauchy}}-{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mathrm{Cauchy}}(\nabla{\bm{v}})^{*}-(\nabla{\bm{v}}){\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mathrm{Cauchy}}.

However, the Kirchhoff stress tensor

{\bm{T}}_{\mathrm{Kir}}=\det{\bm{F}}\,{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mathrm{Cauchy}}=\frac{\rho_{\mathrm{ref}}}{\rho}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mathrm{Cauchy}}

(see Section
3.2
for the last identity) has “intensive”
properties, because it is the quotient of two extensive objects. Thus,

{\bm{T}}_{\mathrm{Kir}}
has to be interpreted as element of
\mathfrak{T}^{0}_{2}
like

{\bm{E}}
in (
2.8
), and its Lie derivative is the upper convected
or Oldroyd stress rate

\mathfrak{L}_{\bm{v}}{\bm{T}}_{\mathrm{Kir}}={\bm{v}}{\bm{\cdot}}\nabla{\bm{T}}_{\mathrm{Kir}}-{\bm{T}}_{\mathrm{Kir}}(\nabla{\bm{v}})^{*}-(\nabla{\bm{v}}){\bm{T}}_{\mathrm{Kir}}.

Here, we do not dwell on this subject any further, but refer to

[
MaH94
, Ch. 1 Box 6.1]
for this and to
[
Fia08
]
for a
way to write the Zaremba-Jaumann derivative as a Lie derivative.

3
GENERIC structures and Eulerian mechanics

The acronym GENERIC stands for

General Equation for Non-Equilibrium
Reversible-Irreversible Coupling

and was introduced in

[
GrÖ97
]
, but the class of models appears metriplectic systems
already in
[
Mor84
,
Mor86
]
, cf. the survey

[
Mor09
]
. Over the last decade, the
GENERIC
framework has
proved to be a versatile modeling tool for various complex coupled models for
fluids and solids, see e.g.
[
LJCV08
,
Mie11
,
HüS12
,
DPZ13
,
PKG18
,
BeS19
,
Las21
,
PT∗22
,
ZPT23
,
MiR25
]
and the references therein.

3.1
Setup of GENERIC

We consider states
q
in a state space
{\bm{Q}}
which is either a flat space or a
smooth manifold. A GENERIC system is a quintuple

({\bm{Q}},{\mathcal{E}},{\mathcal{S}},{\mathbb{J}},{\mathbb{K}})
,
where the energy
{\mathcal{E}}
and the entropy
{\mathcal{S}}
are differentiable functions
on
{\bm{Q}}
with differentials
{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(q)
and
{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{S}}(q)
lie

{\mathrm{T}}_{q}{\bm{Q}}
. The geometric structures are the Poisson operator
{\mathbb{J}}
for
Hamiltonian dynamics and a (dual) dissipation potential
{\mathcal{R}}^{*}
, where

{\mathbb{J}}(q)
maps
{\mathrm{T}}^{*}{\bm{Q}}
to

{\mathrm{T}}{\bm{Q}}
and
{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(q,\cdot):{\mathrm{T}}^{*}_{q}Q\to[0,\infty]
is a convex
functional with
{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(q,0)=0
. In many cases
{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(q,\cdot)
is
a quadratic functional given in terms of a symmetric, positive
semi-definite operator
{\mathbb{K}}(q)
, namely

{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(q,\#xi)=\frac{1}{2}\langle\xi,{\mathbb{K}}(q)\xi\rangle
. Such operators

{\mathbb{K}}(q)
are often called “Onsager operators” because of Onsager’s
fundamental reciprocal relations in
[
Ons31
]
, earning him the Nobel
Prize in Chemistry in 1968.

The evolution equation then takes the form

\frac{\partial{q}}{\partial t}={\mathbb{J}}(q){\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(q)+\partial_{\xi}{\mathcal{R}}^{*}\big(q,{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{S}}(q)\big),

where
\partial_{\xi}{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(q,\cdot)
denotes the convex subdifferential of

{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(q,\cdot)
, which is possibly set-valued (e.g. in plasticity).

The Poisson operator is defined by being skew-symmetric and satisfying the
Jacobi identity, i.e.

\big\langle\zeta_{1},{\mathrm{D}}{\mathbb{J}}(q)[{\mathbb{J}}(q)\zeta_{2}]\zeta_{3}\big\rangle+\text{cycl.\,perm.}\equiv 0\ \ \text{ for all }\ \zeta_{1},\zeta_{2},\zeta_{3}\in{\mathrm{T}}^{*}_{q}{\bm{Q}}.

(3.1)

The main condition for GENERIC are the so-called
non-interaction
conditions
, namely

\text{(a) }\ {\mathbb{J}}(q){\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{S}}(q)\equiv 0\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and\hskip 20.00003pt(b) }\ {\mathcal{R}}^{*}(q,\lambda{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(q))\equiv 0\ \text{ for all
}\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}.

(3.2)

By convexity, the latter condition implies
{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(q,\zeta{+}\lambda{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(q))={\mathcal{R}}^{*}(q,\zeta)
for all
(q,\zeta)\in{\mathrm{T}}^{*}{\bm{Q}}
and
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}
, and as a consequence we have
\big\langle{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(q)),\partial_{\xi}{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(q,\xi)\big\rangle=0
for all
\xi
.

Using the chain rule, a simple consequence of Condition
(
3.2
.b) (and
{\mathbb{J}}=-{\mathbb{J}}^{*}
) is the conservation of energy
along solutions, i.e.
\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}{\mathcal{E}}(q(t))=0
. Condition
(
3.2
.a) (together with convexity of
{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(q,\cdot)
)
implies entropy increase:

\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}{\mathcal{S}}(q(t))=\big\langle{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{S}}(q),\partial_{\xi}{\mathcal{R}}^{*}\big(q,{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{S}}(q)\big)\big\rangle

\geq 0
, i.e. the
second law of thermodynamics is automatically satisfied for GENERIC
systems.

However, we emphasize that (
3.2
) is much stronger that energy
conservation and entropy increase. We refer to
[
Ött05
]
and

[
Mie11
, Sec. 2.2]
for further properties of GENERIC systems, in
particular concerning additional conservation laws and the maximum entropy
principle providing thermal equilibrium states when maximizing
{\mathcal{S}}(q)

subject to the conserved quantities. We also refer to
[
MG∗00
]
for a
discussion on the differences between GENERIC and other thermodynamical
approaches.

An efficient way of constructing GENERIC systems is using the “special form” of
GENERIC systems as described in
[
Ött05
, Sec. 2.3.2]
(following

[
Edw98
]
) and
[
Mie11
, Sec. 2.4+4.3]
.
Having a suitable “simpler” Poisson structure
{\mathbb{J}}_{\mathrm{simple}}
and a
“simpler” dual dissipation potential
{\mathcal{R}}^{*}_{\mathrm{simple}}
, one
can define more complex structures via

{\mathbb{J}}(q)=M_{\mathcal{S}}(q){\mathbb{J}}_{\mathrm{simple}}(\Phi(q))M_{\mathcal{S}}(q)^{*}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(q,\xi)={\mathcal{R}}^{*}_{\mathrm{simple}}\big(q,M_{\mathcal{E}}(q)^{*}\xi\big)

(3.3a)

where the following additional conditions have to be met:

\displaystyle M_{\mathcal{S}}(q)^{*}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{S}}(q)=f_{*},\hskip 10.00002pt{\mathbb{J}}_{\mathrm{simple}}(q)f_{*}=0,\hskip 10.00002ptM_{\mathcal{S}}(q)=\big({\mathrm{D}}\Phi(q)\big)^{-1},

(3.3b)

\displaystyle M_{\mathcal{E}}(q)^{*}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(q)=g_{*},\hskip 10.00002pt{\mathcal{R}}^{*}_{\mathrm{simple}}(q,\lambda g_{*})=0\text{ for all }\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}},

(3.3c)

where
f_{*}
and
g_{*}
are fixed vectors.

The typical application occurs in continuum systems where
q=(w,\tau)
and

\tau
is a scalar thermodynamical variable such as temperature
\theta
, internal
energy density
u
, or the entropy density
s
. Then, we set

\Phi(w,\tau)=(w,S(w,\tau))^{\top}
and obtain

M_{\mathcal{S}}(w,\tau)=\left(\begin{array}[]{@{}cc@{}}I&0\\
\partial_{w}S(w,\tau)&\partial_{\tau}S(w,\tau)\end{array}\right)^{-1}=\left(\begin{array}[]{@{}cc@{}}I&0\\
-\frac{\langle\partial_{w}S(w,\tau),\Box\rangle}{\partial_{\tau}S(w,\tau)}&\frac{1}{\partial_{\tau}S(w,\tau)}\end{array}\right).

Here and below the symbol “
\Box
” indicates at which position the
corresponding argument from matrix multiplication has to placed. In an
example this means

\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}a\,\nabla\Box&\mathrm{e}^{\Box\tau}V\\
\Box\nabla\phi&M\,\nabla\Box\end{array}\right)\binom{\xi}{\eta}=\binom{a\nabla\xi+\ \ \mathrm{e}^{\eta\tau}V\ \ }{\xi\nabla\phi+M\,\nabla\eta}.

With the similar construction for
{\mathcal{E}}
we obtain

\displaystyle M_{\mathcal{S}}(w,\tau)^{*}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc@{}}I&-\frac{\Box}{\partial_{\tau}S(w,\tau)}\,\partial_{w}S(w,\tau)\\
0&\frac{1}{\partial_{\tau}S(w,\tau)}\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{@{}cc@{}}I&0\\
0&1\end{array}\right)+\binom{-\partial_{w}S}{1{-}\partial_{\tau}S}{\otimes}\binom{0}{\frac{1}{\partial_{\tau}S}}\text{ and }

\displaystyle M_{\mathcal{E}}(w,\tau)^{*}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc@{}}I&-\frac{\Box}{\partial_{\tau}E(w,\tau)}\,\partial_{w}E(w,\tau)\\
0&\frac{1}{\partial_{\tau}E(w,\tau)}\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{@{}cc@{}}I&0\\
0&1\end{array}\right)+\binom{-\partial_{w}E}{1{-}\partial_{\tau}E}{\otimes}\binom{0}{\frac{1}{\partial_{\tau}E}}\!.

Clearly, we have (
3.3b
) with
f_{*}=g_{*}=(0,1)^{\top}
. The
remaining relation (
3.3c
) will follow from the classical
balance equations via
\nabla 1\equiv 0
.

Looking at
M_{\mathcal{S}}
the best choice for
\tau
is the entropy density
\tau=s
such that
S(w,s)=s
, because this gives
M_{\mathcal{S}}(w,s)^{*}={\mathbb{I}}
. However, this
choice is not optimal for
M_{\mathcal{E}}
. In engineering often
\tau=\theta
is
chosen, which makes both matrices
M_{\mathcal{S}}
and
M_{\mathcal{E}}
nontrivial.

3.2
Eulerian thermo-elastoplasticity and its kinematics

In Lagrangian mechanics a body is described on a reference domain
{\mathrm{M}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with material points
{\mathrm{x}}\in{\mathrm{M}}
. The time-dependent
deformation is denoted by
{\mathsf{x}}={\bm{y}}(t,{\mathrm{x}})
, where
{\mathsf{x}}\in{\mathsf{S}}
is the
spatial coordinate. Denoting the inverse mapping (also called return mapping)
by
{\mathrm{x}}={\mathrm{Y}}(t,{\mathsf{x}})

the Eulerian velocity
{\bm{v}}
and the Eulerian deformation gradient
{\bm{F}}
are
given by

{\bm{v}}(t,{\mathsf{x}})=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}{\bm{y}}\big(t,{\mathrm{Y}}(t,{\mathsf{x}})\big)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt{\bm{F}}(t,{\mathsf{x}})=\nabla_{\!{\mathrm{x}}}{\bm{y}}\big(t,{\mathrm{Y}}(t,{\mathsf{x}})\big).

Note that
{\bm{v}}
is a vector field on
{\mathsf{S}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, whereas
{\bm{F}}(t,x)

is a two-point tensor mapping
{\mathrm{T}}_{{\mathrm{Y}}(t,{\mathsf{x}})}{\mathrm{M}}
to
{\mathrm{T}}_{\mathsf{x}}{\mathsf{S}}
. Thus,
we have to understand
{\bm{F}}
as a
{\bm{U}}
-valued 1-form on
{\mathcal{S}}
, where

{\bm{U}}_{\mathsf{x}}={\mathrm{T}}_{{\mathrm{Y}}(t,{\mathsf{x}})}{\mathrm{M}}
. The important kinematic relation is that

{\bm{F}}
is directly transported by its Lie derivative. The same happens to the
Eulerian density
\rho(t,{\mathsf{x}})=\rho_{\mathrm{ref}}/\det\big({\bm{F}}(t,{\mathsf{x}})\big)
,
where the referential density
\rho_{\mathrm{ref}}
is assumed to be constant.

These result are well-known, and we state them to highlight their Lie
derivative aspect.

Lemma 3.1
(Kinematic relation)

Considering
{\bm{v}}
,
{\bm{F}}
, and
\rho
as above we have the relations (see
(
2.5
) and (
2.6
))

\partial_{t}\rho=-\mathfrak{L}_{\bm{v}}\rho=-\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits(\rho{\bm{v}})\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\partial_{t}{\bm{F}}=-\mathfrak{L}_{\bm{v}}{\bm{F}}=-{\bm{v}}{\bm{\cdot}}\nabla{\bm{F}}+(\nabla{\bm{v}}){\bm{F}}.

In finite-strain elastoplasticity, the deformation tensor
{\bm{F}}
is
multiplicatively decomposed by the so-called Kröner-Lee-Liu form
(cf.
[
Krö60
,
LeL67
]
)

{\bm{F}}(t,{\mathsf{x}})={\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{e}}(t,{\mathsf{x}})\!\;{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}}(t,{\mathsf{x}}),

(3.4)

where
{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}}(t,{\mathsf{x}})
maps
{\mathrm{T}}_{{\mathrm{Y}}(t,{\mathsf{x}})}{\mathcal{B}}
into itself, so that the
Lie derivatives are

\mathfrak{L}_{\bm{v}}{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{e}}=-{\bm{v}}{\bm{\cdot}}\nabla{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{e}}+(\nabla{\bm{v}}){\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{e}}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\mathfrak{L}_{\bm{v}}{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}}=-{\bm{v}}{\bm{\cdot}}\nabla{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}}.

Inserting these relations into the kinematic relation for
\partial_{t}{\bm{F}}
we obtain
an additive kinematic relation between the elastic and the plastic strain rates
in the form

{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{e}}^{-1}\big(\partial_{t}{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{e}}{+}\mathfrak{L}_{\bm{v}}{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{e}}\big)+\big(\partial_{t}{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}}{+}\mathfrak{L}_{\bm{v}}{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}}\big){\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}}^{-1}=0,

(3.5)

which is the counterpart to the often-used split in the Lagrangian setting, see
e.g.
[
Lee69
,
BeV94
,
GuA05
]
.

We will describe our full model for thermo-elastoplasticity by the state vector

q=\big({\bm{\pi}},{\bm{F}},{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}},\tau)^{\top},

where
{\bm{\pi}}=\rho{\bm{v}}
is the momentum and
\tau
is a scalar-valued
thermodynamical field variable such as the internal energy, enthalpy, entropy,
or temperature. At this stage the structure remains more transparent, when stay
more general, see
[
Mie11
,
BeS19
]
.

The class of models
considered here can also be described by a smaller set of variables, namely

({\bm{\pi}},{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{e}},\tau)
, see
[
MiR25
]
, where then (
3.5
) is
rewritten as
\partial_{t}{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{e}}=-\mathfrak{L}_{\bm{v}}{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{e}}-{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{e}}{\bm{L}}_{\mathrm{p}}
and
{\bm{L}}_{\mathrm{p}}
is
modeled accordingly. We hope that the present formulation is clearer, as it
treats
{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}}
as an integral part of the state of the system.

3.3
Energy, entropy and driving forces

The total energy is given as a the sum of the kinetic energy and the stored
energy in the form

{\mathcal{E}}({\bm{\pi}},{\bm{F}},{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}},\tau)=\int_{\mathcal{S}}\Big(\frac{\det{\bm{F}}}{2\rho_{\mathrm{ref}}}|{\bm{\pi}}|^{2}+E({\bm{F}},{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}},\tau)\Big)\;\!\mathrm{d}x.

(3.6)

Here we have written
E
as a general function of
{\bm{F}}
,
{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}}
, and
\tau
,
however, often it is assumed that
E
depends on
{\bm{F}}
only through
{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{e}}={\bm{F}}{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}}^{-1}
which is a consequence of the Kröner-Lee-Liu decomposition
(
3.4
). A typical choice would be

E({\bm{F}},{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}},\tau)=W({\bm{F}}{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}}^{-1},\tau)+H({\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}},\tau)
, where the latter term
can be used to describe (kinematic) hardening effects. At this stage the
structure remains clearer, if we stay with the general function
E({\bm{F}},{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}},\tau)
, but later on, we will specify to the case
E({\bm{F}},{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}},\tau)=\widetilde{E}({\bm{F}}{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}}^{-1},{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}},\tau)
.

Similarly, the entropy is given in the form

{\mathcal{S}}({\bm{F}},{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}},\tau)=\int_{\mathsf{S}}S({\bm{F}},{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}},\tau)\;\!\mathrm{d}{\mathsf{x}},

(3.7)

where no dependence on the momentum
{\bm{\pi}}
is present because of Galilean
invariance. Here
S
is assume to be strictly increasing in
\tau
, and the
temperature is defined via

\theta=\Theta({\bm{F}},{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}},\tau):=\frac{\partial_{\tau}E({\bm{F}},{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}},\tau)}{\partial_{\tau}S({\bm{F}},{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}},\tau)}\hskip 10.00002pt\Longleftrightarrow\hskip 10.00002pt\tau=\widehat{\tau}({\bm{F}},{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}},\theta).

Note that
E
and
S
are an densities with respect to the spatial domain

{\mathsf{S}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
and that they must be frame indifferent, i.e.
E({\bm{Q}}{\bm{F}},{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}},\tau)=E({\bm{F}},{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}},\tau)
and

S({\bm{Q}}{\bm{F}},{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}},\tau)=S({\bm{F}},{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}},\tau)
for all
{\bm{Q}}\in\mathrm{SO}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
,
which implies that
\partial_{{\bm{F}}}E({\bm{F}},{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}},\tau){\bm{F}}^{*}
and

\partial_{{\bm{F}}}S({\bm{F}},{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}},\tau){\bm{F}}^{*}
are symmetric. Here
{\bm{F}}^{*}
is the
adjoint operator to
{\bm{F}}:{\mathrm{T}}_{\mathrm{x}}{\mathrm{M}}\to{\mathrm{T}}_{\mathsf{x}}{\mathsf{S}}
with

{\mathsf{x}}={\bm{y}}(t,{\mathrm{x}})
, i.e.
{\bm{F}}^{*}:{\mathrm{T}}^{*}_{\mathsf{x}}{\mathsf{S}}\to{\mathrm{T}}^{*}_{\mathrm{x}}{\mathrm{M}}
, and we
will also use
{\bm{F}}^{-*}=({\bm{F}}^{*})^{-1}=({\bm{F}}^{-1})^{*}:{\mathrm{T}}^{*}_{\mathrm{x}}{\mathrm{M}}\to{\mathrm{T}}^{*}_{\mathsf{x}}{\mathsf{S}}
.

As GENERIC structures suggest, the main driving forces for the reversible
(Hamiltonian) dynamics are
{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(q)
and those for the dissipative
dynamics are
{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{S}}(q)
:

{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(q)=\left(\begin{array}[]{@{}c@{}}{\bm{v}}\\
\partial_{{\bm{F}}}E(..)+\frac{\rho|{\bm{v}}|^{2}}{2}\,{\bm{F}}^{-*}\\[3.00003pt]
\partial_{{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}}}E(..)\\[1.99997pt]
\partial_{\tau}E(..)\end{array}\right)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{S}}(q)=\left(\begin{array}[]{@{}c@{}}0\\
\partial_{{\bm{F}}}S(..)\\[3.00003pt]
\partial_{{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}}}S(..)\\[1.99997pt]
\partial_{\tau}S(..)\end{array}\right)\!.

However, in light of the
special GENERIC structure introduced at the end of Section
3.1
,
it will be more natural to look at the combined generalized driving forces

M_{\mathcal{S}}(q){\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(q)
and
M_{\mathcal{E}}(q){\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{S}}(q)
.

Proposition 3.2

With
e_{\tau}=(0,0,0,1)^{\top}
we have the relations

M_{\mathcal{S}}(q)^{*}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(q)=\left(\begin{array}[]{@{}c@{}}{\bm{v}}\\[1.99997pt]
\!{\bm{\Sigma}}^{F}_{\mathrm{e}}+\frac{\rho|{\bm{v}}|^{2}}{2}\,{\bm{F}}^{-*}\\[1.99997pt]
{\bm{\Sigma}}^{F}_{\mathrm{p}}\\[1.99997pt]
\Theta\end{array}\right)\!\text{ and }M_{\mathcal{E}}(q)^{*}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{S}}(q)=\tfrac{\displaystyle-1}{\displaystyle\Theta(q)}M_{\mathcal{E}}(q){\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{S}}(q)+\tfrac{\displaystyle 1{+}\Theta}{\displaystyle\Theta}e_{\tau},

(3.8)

where
{\bm{\Sigma}}^{F}_{\mathrm{e}}=\partial_{{\bm{F}}}E(..){-}\Theta(..)\partial_{{\bm{F}}}S(..)
and

{\bm{\Sigma}}^{F}_{\mathrm{p}}=\partial_{{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}}}E(..){-}\Theta(..)\partial_{{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}}}S(..)
.

Here
{\bm{\Sigma}}^{F}_{\mathrm{e}}
and
{\bm{\Sigma}}^{F}_{\mathrm{p}}
are the derivatives of the free energy

F(q)=E(q){-}\Theta(q)S(q)
at fixed temperature with respect to
{\bm{F}}
and

{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}}
, respectively. Inverting the relation
\theta=\Theta(w,\tau)
into

\tau=\widehat{\tau}(w,\theta)
and setting
\Psi(w,\theta):=F\big(w,\widehat{\tau}(w,\theta)\big)
, this means

{\bm{\Sigma}}^{F}_{\mathrm{e}}=\partial_{{\bm{F}}}\Psi({\bm{F}},{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}},\theta)\big|_{\theta=\Theta({\bm{F}},{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}},\tau)}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt{\bm{\Sigma}}^{F}_{\mathrm{p}}=\partial_{{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}}}\Psi({\bm{F}},{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}},\theta)\big|_{\theta=\Theta({\bm{F}},{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}},\tau)}.

Proof.
The transformation matrices in Section

3.1
read

M_{\mathcal{S}}(q)^{*}=I-\tfrac{1}{\partial_{\tau}S}\big({\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{S}}(q){-}e_{\tau}\big){\otimes}e_{\tau}\text{ and }M_{\mathcal{E}}(q)^{*}=I-\tfrac{1}{\partial_{\tau}E}\big({\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(q){-}e_{\tau}\big){\otimes}e_{\tau}.

(3.9)

Thus, the generalized driving forces take the form

M_{\mathcal{S}}(q)^{*}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(q)={\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(q)-\Theta{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{S}}(q)+\Theta e_{\tau}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002ptM_{\mathcal{E}}(q)^{*}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{S}}(q)={\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{S}}(q)-\frac{1}{\Theta}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(q)+\tfrac{\displaystyle 1}{\displaystyle\Theta}\;\!e_{\tau},

which means that the two driving forces, except for the last scalar component,
are the same up to constant
-\Theta(..)
. Hence, the second statement in
(
3.8
) is shown.

The first relation in (
3.8
) follows simply by using
(
3.9
), the formulas for
{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}
and
{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{S}}
, and the
definition of
\Theta
.

The last statement about the free energy is established in

[
Mie11
, Eqn. (2.13)]
.

In fact, for the dissipative driving forces, it will be more useful to
generalize
M_{\mathcal{E}}(q)^{*}
to the better adapted operator
N_{\mathcal{E}}
. We
emphasize that the conditions (
3.3b
) for

M_{\mathcal{S}}
are much more restrictive, because the third condition asks
M_{\mathcal{S}}

to be the inverse of a derivative. In contrast, the condition
(
3.3c
) for
M_{\mathcal{E}}
are simpler, e.g.

would be allowed (but not really useful).
In the following we will use the operator

N_{\mathcal{E}}(q)^{*}=\left(\begin{array}[]{@{}cccc@{}}{\bm{D}}(\Box)&0&0&\tfrac{\displaystyle-\Box}{\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}E}\,{\bm{D}}({\bm{v}})\\[6.00006pt]

{\bm{\xi}}\\
{\bm{\eta}}\\
\kappa\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{@{}c@{}}\!{\bm{D}}({\bm{w}}){-}\frac{\kappa}{\partial_{\tau}E}\,{\bm{D}}({\bm{v}})\\[6.00006pt]
{\bm{\eta}}-\tfrac{\displaystyle\kappa}{\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}E}\,\partial_{{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}}}E\\[6.00006pt]

(3.10)

By construction, we have
N_{\mathcal{E}}(q)^{*}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(u)=(0,0,1)^{\top}
and find
the adapted driving forces

{\bm{\eta}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{@{}c@{}}{\bm{\eta}}_{\mathrm{m}}\\
{\bm{\eta}}_{\mathrm{p}}\\
\eta_{\mathrm{t}}\end{array}\right)=N_{\mathcal{E}}(q)^{*}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{S}}(q)=\left(\begin{array}[]{@{}c@{}}-\tfrac{\displaystyle 1}{\displaystyle\Theta}\,{\bm{D}}({\bm{v}})\\[3.99994pt]
\tfrac{\displaystyle 1}{\displaystyle\Theta}\big(\partial_{{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}}}S-\Theta\partial_{{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}}}E\big)\\[3.99994pt]
1/\Theta\end{array}\right)=\frac{1}{\Theta}\left(\begin{array}[]{@{}c@{}}-{\bm{D}}({\bm{v}})\\[3.99994pt]
-{\bm{\Sigma}}^{F}_{\mathrm{p}}\\

(3.11)

The major reason for choosing

is that the arising driving forces
are Galilean invariant, in contrast to
M_{\mathcal{E}}(q)^{*}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{S}}(q)
which
involves
{\bm{v}}
and
\rho|{\bm{v}}|^{2}
. Thus, we will have more flexibility in
making physically reasonable choices for
{\mathcal{R}}^{*}_{\mathrm{simple}}
. Note also
that
N_{\mathcal{E}}(q)^{*}
is not quadratic and hence not invertible (in contrast to

M_{\mathcal{S}}(q)^{*}
). This reflects the fact that the equation of
{\bm{F}}
is purely
kinematic and thus will not include any dissipative effects.

3.4
The Poisson structure for the Hamiltonian part

The main result of paper states that skew-symmetric operators
{\mathbb{J}}
build with
Lie derivatives automatically satisfy the Jacobi identity and hence qualify as
Poisson operators for Eulerian continuum mechanics.

Theorem 3.3
(Jacobi identity via Lie derivatives)

We consider the case that the state
{\bm{z}}
is given by
{\bm{z}}=({\bm{\pi}},{\bm{X}})
where

{\bm{\pi}}
is the momentum (considered as a
(d{-}1)
form as in
(
2.7
)) and a
collection of variables comprised into a tuple
{\bm{X}}
. We define the
state-dependent skew-symmetric operator
{\mathbb{J}}({\bm{\pi}},{\bm{X}})
via

{\mathbb{J}}({\bm{\pi}},{\bm{X}})\binom{{\bm{v}}}{{\bm{\xi}}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}-\mathfrak{L}_{\Box}{\bm{\pi}}&{\mathbb{B}}({\bm{X}})\Box\\
-\mathfrak{L}_{\Box}{\bm{X}}&0\end{array}\right)\binom{{\bm{v}}}{{\bm{\xi}}}=\binom{-\mathfrak{L}_{\bm{v}}{\bm{\pi}}+{\mathbb{B}}({\bm{X}}){\bm{\xi}}}{-\mathfrak{L}_{\bm{v}}{\bm{X}}},

where
{\mathbb{B}}({\bm{X}})
is defined by skew-symmetry, namely
\langle{\bm{v}},{\mathbb{B}}({\bm{X}}){\bm{\xi}}\rangle=\langle{\bm{\xi}},\mathfrak{L}_{\bm{v}}{\bm{X}}\rangle
.

Then,
{\mathbb{J}}
satisfies the Jacobi identity (
3.1
), and hence
provides a Poisson structure.

Proof.
The proof relies in the rules for Lie derivatives, in particular the commutator
rule (
2.3
), and the simple observation that the mapping

({\bm{\pi}},{\bm{X}})\mapsto{\mathbb{J}}({\bm{\pi}},{\bm{X}})
is linear. A special case of our result was already obtained
in
[
MiR25
, Prop. A.1]
, however the present proof is significantly
shorter and still self-contained.

We consider on of the three terms in the Jacobi identity and simplify it
systematically using
{\mathrm{D}}{\mathbb{J}}({\bm{\pi}},{\bm{X}})[{\bm{\pi}}_{2},{\bm{X}}_{2}]={\mathbb{J}}({\bm{\pi}}_{2},{\bm{X}}_{2})

(because of linearity), where

({\bm{\pi}}_{2},{\bm{X}}_{2})^{\top}={\mathbb{J}}({\bm{\pi}},{\bm{X}})({\bm{v}}_{2},{\bm{\xi}}_{2})^{\top}
. We obtain

\displaystyle\mathsf{T}_{1,2,3}

\displaystyle:=\big\langle\tbinom{{\bm{v}}_{1}}{{\bm{\xi}}_{1}},{\mathrm{D}}{\mathbb{J}}({\bm{\pi}},{\bm{X}})[{\bm{\pi}}_{2},{\bm{X}}_{2}]\tbinom{{\bm{v}}_{3}}{{\bm{\xi}}_{3}}\big\rangle=\langle{\bm{v}}_{1},-\mathfrak{L}_{{\bm{v}}_{3}}{\bm{\pi}}_{2}{+}{\mathbb{B}}({\bm{X}}_{2}){\bm{\xi}}_{3}\rangle-\langle{\bm{\xi}}_{1},\mathfrak{L}_{{\bm{v}}_{3}}{\bm{X}}_{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\mathfrak{L}_{{\bm{v}}_{3}}{\bm{v}}_{1},{\bm{\pi}}_{2}\rangle+\langle{\bm{\xi}}_{3},\mathfrak{L}_{{\bm{v}}_{1}}{\bm{X}}_{2}\rangle-\langle{\bm{\xi}}_{1},\mathfrak{L}_{{\bm{v}}_{3}}{\bm{X}}_{2}\rangle

Inserting the definitions of
{\bm{\pi}}_{2}=-\mathfrak{L}_{{\bm{v}}_{2}}{\bm{\pi}}+{\mathbb{B}}({\bm{X}}){\bm{\xi}}_{2}

and

{\bm{X}}_{2}=-\mathfrak{L}_{{\bm{v}}_{2}}{\bm{X}}
and using the definition of
{\mathbb{B}}({\bm{X}})
we obtain

\displaystyle\mathsf{T}_{1,2,3}

\displaystyle=-\langle[\hskip-1.29167pt[{\bm{v}}_{3},{\bm{v}}_{1}]\hskip-1.29167pt],\mathfrak{L}_{{\bm{v}}_{2}}{\bm{\pi}}\rangle+\langle{\bm{\xi}}_{2},\mathfrak{L}_{[\hskip-0.90417pt[{\bm{v}}_{3},{\bm{v}}_{1}]\hskip-0.90417pt]}{\bm{X}}\rangle-\langle{\bm{\xi}}_{3},\mathfrak{L}_{{\bm{v}}_{1}}\mathfrak{L}_{{\bm{v}}_{2}}{\bm{X}}\rangle+\langle{\bm{\xi}}_{1},\mathfrak{L}_{{\bm{v}}_{3}}\mathfrak{L}_{{\bm{v}}_{2}}{\bm{X}}\rangle.

The first term can be rewritten as

\langle\mathfrak{L}_{{\bm{v}}_{2}}[\hskip-1.29167pt[{\bm{v}}_{3},{\bm{v}}_{1}]\hskip-1.29167pt],{\bm{\pi}}\rangle=\big\langle\big[\!\big[{\bm{v}}_{2},[\hskip-1.29167pt[{\bm{v}}_{3},{\bm{v}}_{1}]\hskip-1.29167pt]\big]\!\big],{\bm{\pi}}\big\rangle
. Hence adding the
corresponding cyclic
permutations, we can exploit the Jacobi identity for vector fields in
(
2.4
) and find that the contribution of the terms linear in

{\bm{\pi}}
in
\mathsf{C}:=\mathsf{T}_{1,2,3}+\mathsf{T}_{2,3,1}+\mathsf{T}_{3,1,2}

cancel each other.

Similarly, we can look at all terms in
\mathsf{C}
involving
{\bm{\xi}}_{1}
and find

\langle{\bm{\xi}}_{1},\mathfrak{L}_{{\bm{v}}_{3}}\mathfrak{L}_{{\bm{v}}_{2}}{\bm{X}}\rangle-\langle{\bm{\xi}}_{1},\mathfrak{L}_{{\bm{v}}_{2}}\mathfrak{L}_{{\bm{v}}_{3}}{\bm{X}}\rangle+\langle{\bm{\xi}}_{1},\mathfrak{L}_{[\hskip-0.90417pt[{\bm{v}}_{2},{\bm{v}}_{3}]\hskip-0.90417pt]}{\bm{X}}\rangle=0

by using the commutator rule (
2.3
). The same holds for the
terms involving
{\bm{\xi}}_{2}
and
{\bm{\xi}}_{3}
, and hence the Jacobi identity for

{\mathbb{J}}
is established.

In the sense of the special form of GENERIC we define
{\mathbb{J}}_{\mathrm{simple}}
by
using the special choice
\tau=s
, namely the entropy density. The reason is
that we know that the Hamiltonian dynamics does not change the entropy. Hence,
we know that the evolution should be the simple transport along the Eulerian
vector field
{\bm{v}}
as a extensive variable. Note also that
{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}}
is an
intensive variable that is
{\bm{U}}
-valued with
{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}}(t,x)\in U_{x}=\mathrm{GL}({\mathrm{T}}_{x}{\mathrm{M}}))
.
Denoting the Lie derivatives for

{\bm{\pi}}
,
{\bm{F}}
,
{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}}
, and
s
by
\mathfrak{L}_{\bm{v}}^{\mathrm{mo}}
,

\mathfrak{L}^{\mathrm{ve}}_{\bm{v}}
,
\mathfrak{L}_{\bm{v}}^{\mathrm{in}}
, and
\mathfrak{L}_{\bm{v}}^{\mathrm{ex}}
,
respectively, we set

{\mathbb{J}}_{\mathrm{simple}}({\bm{\pi}},{\bm{F}},{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}},s)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}-\mathfrak{L}_{\Box}^{\mathrm{mo}}{\bm{\pi}}&{\mathbb{B}}^{\mathrm{ve}}({\bm{F}})\Box&{\mathbb{B}}^{\mathrm{in}}({\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}})\Box&{\mathbb{B}}^{\mathrm{ex}}(s)\Box\\
-\mathfrak{L}^{\mathrm{ve}}_{\Box}{\bm{F}}&0&0&0\\
-\mathfrak{L}_{\Box}^{\mathrm{in}}{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}}&0&0&0\\
-\mathfrak{L}_{\Box}^{\mathrm{ex}}s&0&0&0\end{array}\right)

where the operator in the first row are obtained by skew symmetry from the
first column:

{\mathbb{B}}^{\mathrm{ve}}({\bm{F}}){\bm{\Xi}}_{\mathrm{e}}=\nabla{\bm{F}}{\bm{:}}{\bm{\Xi}}_{\mathrm{e}}+\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\!\big({\bm{\Xi}}_{\mathrm{e}}{\bm{F}}^{*}\big),\hskip 10.00002pt{\mathbb{B}}^{\mathrm{in}}({\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}}){\bm{\Xi}}_{\mathrm{p}}=\nabla{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}}{\bm{:}}{\bm{\Xi}}_{\mathrm{p}},\hskip 10.00002pt{\mathbb{B}}^{\mathrm{ex}}(s)\xi=-s\nabla\xi.

The full Poisson structure
{\mathbb{J}}({\bm{\pi}},{\bm{F}},{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}},\tau)
is now obtain by using
the transformation
s=S({\bm{F}},{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}},\tau)
inducing the transformation operator

M_{\mathcal{S}}
resulting in

{\mathbb{J}}({\bm{\pi}},{\bm{F}},{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}},\tau)=M_{\mathcal{S}}({\bm{\pi}},{\bm{F}},{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}},\tau){\mathbb{J}}_{\mathrm{simple}}\big({\bm{\pi}},{\bm{F}},{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}},S({\bm{F}},{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}},\tau)\big)M_{\mathcal{S}}({\bm{\pi}},{\bm{F}},{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}},\tau)^{*}.

From Theorem
3.3
we know that
{\mathbb{J}}_{\mathrm{simple}}
is a Poisson
structure, hence the transformation shows that
{\mathbb{J}}
is again a Poisson
structure.

4
The Eulerian form of thermo-visco-elastoplasticity

We now collect the evolutionary equations for thermo-visco-elastoplasticity at
finite strain from the GENERIC form. Abbreviating
q=({\bm{\pi}},{\bm{F}},{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}},\tau)
we
have

\displaystyle\partial_{t}q={\bm{V}}_{\mathrm{Ham}}(q)+{\bm{V}}_{\mathrm{diss}}(q)\hskip 10.00002pt

\displaystyle\text{with}\hskip 10.00002pt{\bm{V}}_{\mathrm{Ham}}(q)={\mathbb{J}}(q){\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(q)\ \text{ and }\ {\bm{V}}_{\mathrm{diss}}(q)=N_{\mathcal{E}}(q)\partial_{\xi}{\mathcal{R}}^{*}_{\mathrm{simple}}\big(q,N_{\mathcal{E}}(q)^{*}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{S}}(q)\big).

One of the advantages of the GENERIC framework is that we can derive the
Hamiltonian part and the dissipative part completely independently, thus
separating the two quite different physical phenomena.

4.1
The Hamiltonian part of the model

We now discuss the terms arising from of the Hamiltonian part of the dynamics,
namely
\frac{\partial{q}}{\partial t}={\bm{V}}_{\text{Ham}}(q)={\mathbb{J}}(q){\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(q)
. Using the form of

{\mathcal{E}}
and the definition of
{\mathbb{J}}

we obtain

{\bm{V}}_{\text{Ham}}(q)={\mathbb{J}}(q){\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(q)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}-\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\big(\rho{\bm{v}}{\otimes}{\bm{v}}\big)+\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\text{Cauchy}}\\[2.5pt]
-{\bm{v}}{\bm{\cdot}}\nabla{\bm{F}}+(\nabla{\bm{v}})\,{\bm{F}}\\[2.5pt]
-{\bm{v}}{\bm{\cdot}}\nabla{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}}\\[0.50003pt]
\!-{\bm{v}}{\bm{\cdot}}\nabla\tau-\tfrac{\displaystyle S(..)\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits{\bm{v}}+\partial_{\bm{F}}S(..){\bm{F}}^{*}{\bm{:}}{\bm{D}}({\bm{v}})}{\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}S(..)}\end{array}\right)\!,

(4.1)

where the Cauchy stress tensor

is defined via the
free-energy density

:

{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\text{Cauchy}}={\bm{\Sigma}}^{F}_{\mathrm{e}}{\bm{F}}^{*}+\Psi\,{\mathbb{I}}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{with }\ {\bm{\Sigma}}^{F}_{\mathrm{e}}=\partial_{\bm{F}}E({\bm{F}},{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}},\tau)-\Theta({\bm{F}},{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}},\tau)\;\!\partial_{\bm{F}}S({\bm{F}},{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}},\tau).

A simplified version of (

in
[
HüT08
, Sec. 3]
, where
{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}}
is neglected and

is
chosen.

The rest of this subsection will explain the form of the four components of

in detail.
For this it is important to keep the adapted reversible driving forces

M_{\mathcal{S}}(q)^{*}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(q)
from (
3.8

component remains
{\bm{v}}
. This vector will be applied to

\!{\bm{\Sigma}}^{F}_{\mathrm{e}}{+}\tfrac{\rho|{\bm{v}}|^{2}}{2}{\bm{F}}^{-*}\\[3.00003pt]
{\bm{\Sigma}}^{F}_{\mathrm{p}}\\
\Theta\end{array}\right)

Note that the first matrix

does not change
the first three components; hence, the second and third

are simply given by
-\mathfrak{L}^{\mathrm{vec}}_{\bm{v}}{\bm{F}}
and

For the fourth component we can take advantage from
pl{\bm{v}}S\equiv 0
; hence
it is a linear combination of the three Lie derivatives, namely

\frac{1}{\partial_{\tau}S}\Big(\partial_{\bm{F}}S{\bm{:}}\mathfrak{L}^{\mathrm{ve}}_{\bm{v}}{\bm{F}}+\partial_{{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}}}S{\bm{:}}\mathfrak{L}_{\bm{v}}^{\mathrm{in}}{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}}-\mathfrak{L}_{\bm{v}}^{\mathrm{ex}}S\Big)=-{\bm{v}}{\bm{\cdot}}\nabla\tau-\frac{S(..)\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits{\bm{v}}+\partial_{\bm{F}}S(..){\bm{F}}^{*}{\bm{:}}{\bm{D}}({\bm{v}})}{\partial_{\tau}S(..)}.

For the last identity, we use that the terms involving
{\bm{v}}{\bm{\cdot}}\nabla{\bm{F}}

and

arising from

and

Thus, it remains to establish the simple representation of the first component

4.2
The dissipative part of Eulerian thermo-elastoplasticity

The modeling of the dissipative effects is considerably simpler than that of
the reversible Hamiltonian part, because the their is much more freedom in
choosing dissipation potentials than in choosing Poisson structures. One of the
main advantages of GENERIC is indeed the very structured modeling of
dissipative processes allowing for general couplings. Using the subdifferential
of the dual dissipation potential will automatically satisfy the so-called
Onsager symmetries. In the quadratic case
{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(q,\xi)=\frac{1}{2}\langle\xi,{\mathbb{A}}(q)\xi\rangle
this is seen by the fact that
\partial_{\xi}{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(q,\xi)={\mathbb{K}}(q)\xi
with
{\mathbb{K}}(q)=\frac{1}{2}({\mathbb{A}}(q){+}{\mathbb{A}}(q)\big)={\mathbb{K}}(q)^{*}
, which
is Onsager’s reciprocal relation. Note that
{\mathbb{K}}(q)={\mathrm{D}}^{2}{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(q,0)
is the
symmetric Hessian of
{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(q,\cdot)
.

Using the special form of GENERIC as described in (
3.3
)
we can construct suitable nonlinear dissipation potentials
{\mathcal{R}}^{*}

(or linear Onsager operators
{\mathbb{K}}
) by collecting the building
blocks of the dissipative effects and combining them
with a nontrivial operator
N_{\mathcal{E}}
in the form

{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(q,{\bm{\zeta}})={\mathcal{R}}^{*}_{\mathrm{simple}}(q,N_{\mathcal{E}}(q)^{*}{\bm{\zeta}})\hskip 10.00002pt\text{or}\hskip 10.00002pt{\mathbb{K}}(q)=N_{\mathcal{E}}(q){\mathbb{K}}_{\mathrm{simple}}(q)N_{\mathcal{E}}(q)^{*}.

This strategy is propagated in
[
Ött05
, Sec. 2.3.2]
and follows

[
Edw98
]
.

In our model we can have three dissipative processes:

(A) viscoelastic dissipation induced by
{\bm{D}}({\bm{v}})=\frac{1}{2}\big(\nabla{\bm{v}}{+}(\nabla{\bm{v}})^{\top}\big)
,

(B) plastic dissipation induced by
\partial_{t}{\bm{F}}_{\mathrm{p}}
,

(C) heat flow induced by
\nabla(1/\theta)
.

Thus, we can construct a suitable dual dissipation potential in the additive form

{\mathcal{R}}^{*}_{\mathrm{simple}}={\mathcal{R}}^{*}_{\mathrm{A}}+{\mathcal{R}}^{*}_{\mathrm{B}}+{\mathcal{R}}^{*}_{\mathrm{C}}.

However, we emphasize that this simplistic assumption is by far not
necessary. Of course, it is possible to construct much more general
thermodynamically consistent models where there is a strong interaction
of the different dissipation mechanics, but to keep simplicity and clarity we
will restrict our approach to the case of a simple block structure.

The advantage of using an operator
N_{\mathcal{E}}
is three-fold. First, it is used
to guarantee the second non-interaction condition by asking

N_{\mathcal{E}}(q)^{*}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(q)=e_{\tau}=(0,...,0,1)^{\top}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt{\mathcal{R}}^{*}_{\mathrm{simple}}\big(q,\lambda\;\!e_{\tau})^{\top}\big)=0\ \text{ for all
}\ \lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}.

(4.2)

Here
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}
stand for the constant (reciprocal of the)
temperature
1/\theta
, which does not generate any dissipation.

The second advantage is the fundamental observation in

[
Mie11
, Sec. 4.3]
that the dissipative
(a.k.a. irreversible) driving forces are now given by the generalized driving
forces

{\bm{\eta}}=N_{\mathcal{E}}(q)^{*}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{S}}(q)\,,

which contains important thermodynamical information by identifying the
correct combinations of
{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{S}}
and
{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}
governing dissipative
processes and satisfying Galilean invariance.

Finally, the operator
N_{\mathcal{E}}
acting from the left on

\partial{\mathcal{R}}^{*}_{\mathrm{simple}}
adjusts the dissipative terms in such a way that
energy conservation holds for

5
Discussion and outlook

In this paper we have brought together the theory of Lie derivatives and the
GENERIC framework to provide the right modeling tools for Eulerian mechanics of
solid materials.

The GENERIC framework takes care that the model is
thermodynamically consistent in the sense that energy is conserved (first law
of thermodynamics) and that the entropy is non-decreasing (second law of
thermodynamics).
However, the GENERIC framework enforces an additional structural condition on
the reversible/conservative part and on the irreversible/dissipative part of
the system. The reversible part has to be strictly Hamiltonian which
is more restrictive than being energy and entropy preserving. For this it would
be sufficient to have the skew-symmetry
{\mathbb{J}}(q)^{*}=-{\mathbb{J}}(q)
and the
non-interaction condition
{\mathbb{J}}(q){\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{S}}(q)\equiv 0
, but GENERIC
imposes the Jacobi identity (
3.1
). The irreversible part is
restricted by enforcing that the kinetic relations for the dissipative
processes derive from the (sub)-differential of a dissipation potential, which
automatically enforces Onsager’s symmetry relations.

Thus, GENERIC systems form a subclass of all thermodynamically consistent
systems. The additional structural conditions may be restrictive but appear to
be valid for many continuum models. In particular, in situations where complex
coupled models are to be developed, the framework provides easy ways for the
derivation of good coupling terms between different variables; see e.g.
[
KM∗19
, Sec. 3.4+4.6]
, where a reaction-diffusion system for charge
carriers is coupled to a quantum mechanical system. We expect that the GENERIC
framework will also be very useful in solid mechanics when in addition to
deformation and temperature also internal variables such as a plastic
distortion, phase indicators, dislocation densities, or magnetization have to
be modeled, see e.g.
[
GK∗16
,
BN∗24
]
.

This approach was used already very successfully in

[
Mie11
,
HüS12
,
GK∗16
]
for models based on the Lagrangian
description, where a referential body
{\mathrm{M}}
is used for describing all the
fields. While the Eulerian perspective working in the spatial domain

{\mathsf{S}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is commonly used in fluid mechanics, see
e.g.
[
GrÖ97
,
Ött05
,
ZPT23
]
, the combination of
GENERIC, Eulerian description, and solid mechanics was brought together only
recently in
[
MiR25
]
. One of the reasons for using the Eulerian
description for solids is the growing mathematical interest in geophysical
flows in the upper mantle of our planet Earth, see e.g.
[
RoT21
,
Rou23
,
MRS23
]
. Depending on the time scale under
consideration the solid rock behaves visco-elastically or visco-plastically,
but there are many other variables to be taken into account, like concentrations
of different chemicals, water contents, temperature, or aging variables, see
e.g.
[
GeY07
,
HGV18
,
SB∗21
]
.

The Eulerian description of solids is intrinsically linked to the notion of Lie
derivatives, because the variables are transported with the velocity
{\bm{v}}
of
the points in the moving and deforming body. This observation from

[
MiR25
]
served as a motivation to provide a short
and self-contained introduction to Lie derivatives with a focus on the
applications in continuum thermodynamics in the present paper.
As an major outcome we found Theorem
3.3
, which
states that in the Eulerian setting the Jacobi
identity
3.1
for Poisson operators
{\mathbb{J}}
is intimately
related to the commutator relation (
2.3
) for Lie
derivatives. The result is now more general and the proof is shorter and more
direct as for
[
MiR25
, Thm. 4.1]
.
