Surgical outcomes of dermabrasion followed by split-thickness skin grafting for post-burn leukoderma: a two-center observational study
Authors: Abdulkadir Calavul
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPost-burn leukoderma is a challenging late sequela of deep dermal injury, characterized by persistent hypopigmentation and impaired scar quality. Surgical reconstruction using dermabrasion followed by split-thickness skin grafting (STSG) is commonly employed; however, systematically collected outcome data using validated scar assessment tools for postoperative scar quality evaluation remain limited.MethodsThis two-center, observational (ambispective) study included 36 patients with stable post-burn leukoderma who underwent superficial dermabrasion followed by STSG at Afrin State Hospital and Şanlıurfa Metrolife Hospital. Perioperative clinical data from procedures performed between May 2020 and July 2024 were collected retrospectively. Following ethics approval in May 2025, prospective scar evaluations were conducted between May and July 2025 using the Patient and Observer Scar Assessment Scale (POSAS). Associations between POSAS scores and demographic or clinical variables were analyzed using non-parametric statistical methods.ResultsPatient- and observer-rated POSAS scores demonstrated a very strong correlation (ρ = 0.924, p < .001), indicating excellent concordance between patient-reported and clinician-assessed scar quality. Age, sex, and burn size showed no significant associations with POSAS outcomes (all p > .05). In contrast, postoperative complications were strongly associated with significantly higher (worse) POSAS scores in both patient and observer assessments (p < .001), with large effect sizes (r ≥ .68).ConclusionsDermabrasion followed by STSG was associated with acceptable postoperative scar outcomes in patients with stable post-burn leukoderma, with strong agreement between patient and clinician evaluations of scar quality. The findings highlight complication-free healing as a key determinant of more favorable scar outcomes. Because POSAS evaluates scar quality rather than pigmentation directly, further controlled studies incorporating objective pigment-specific or colorimetric assessments are warranted to better define treatment-related repigmentation outcomes.

Introduction

Leukoderma, also called achromoderma, is characterised by localised areas of white or hypopigmented skin due to partial or complete loss of epidermal melanin pigment [1–4]. Although leukoderma itself is not a diagnosis, it represents a clinical manifestation of various underlying aetiologies. Among these, post-burn leukoderma occurs as an important sequela of deep dermal or full-thickness burns, in which the destruction of melanocytes and subsequent scarring causes irreversible pigment loss [3, 5]. The terms “leucoderma” and “hypopigmentation”, often used interchangeably in the context of burn scars, actually refer to different levels of pigment loss. Leukoderma is characterised by a complete or almost complete loss of epidermal melanin pigment, whereas hypopigmentation describes a partial reduction in the amount of melanin [3, 6, 7].

The pathogenesis of post-burn leucoderma is not fully understood. It probably involves a multifactorial mechanism involving direct damage to melanocytes, impaired melanin transfer, and fibrotic scar tissue formation that inhibits melanocyte migration and repopulation [3, 8]. This pigmentary disorder is not only a cosmetic problem but also a source of profound psychosocial distress, especially when it affects highly visible areas such as the face and hands [6, 9]. Affected individuals often suffer from depression, anxiety and reduced self-esteem, which are exacerbated by social stigma and perceived disfigurement [10–12].

The epidemiology of post-burn hypopigmentation is not well defined, but clinical reports suggest that it is particularly common in deep burns and among individuals with darker skin types [3, 5]. In paediatric populations, the incidence of post-burn hypopigmentation has been reported to be as high as 25% [13]. Furthermore, this condition is more common and more pronounced in patients of African and Asian descent due to the apparent contrast in skin colour [6].

Although medical treatments such as topical corticosteroids, calcineurin inhibitors, PUVA and NB-UVB phototherapy have been investigated, their efficacy in established leukoderma is inconsistent and often unsatisfactory [8, 12]. As a result, surgical interventions have received more attention. Among these, split-thickness skin grafting (STSG) has been a cornerstone technique offering promising results in terms of long-term repigmentation [4, 14]. Dermabrasion, when applied prior to grafting, improves graft adherence and melanocyte survival by removing the fibrotic barrier and improving the vascularity of the recipient bed [4, 15]. This combined approach offers a synergistic therapeutic potential by addressing both structural and cellular deficiencies [4].

Despite growing clinical interest, high-quality comparative evidence on dermabrasion followed by STSG for post-burn leukoderma remains limited. Most reports comprise small, single-arm series with short follow-up and heterogeneous techniques, and standardized, validated outcome measures such as the Patient and Observer Scar Assessment Scale (POSAS) are used infrequently [16, 17]. Moreover, systematic long-term evaluations and objective documentation—such as colorimetry or structured image-based assessments—are rarely reported, constraining cross-study synthesis and external validity [18, 19]. Accordingly, this two-center ambispective descriptive study characterizes postoperative scar quality after dermabrasion plus STSG in stable post-burn leukoderma. Using dual-perspective POSAS assessments, it provides structured scar ratings from both patients and observers, evaluates patient–observer concordance, and examines the relationship between clinical variables—particularly postoperative complications—and scar assessment scores. By detailing the operative protocol and reporting effect sizes alongside nonparametric analyses, the study adds two-center clinical data within a standardized scar assessment framework. It also provides outcome information that may inform the design of future controlled studies in post-burn leukoderma reconstruction.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This two-center, descriptive cross-sectional (ambispective) study combined retrospective chart abstraction with prospective, clinic-based assessment. It was conducted at Afrin State Hospital and Şanlıurfa Metrolife Hospital. Index procedures performed between 26 May 2020 and 20 July 2024 (superficial dermabrasion followed by split-thickness skin grafting, STSG) were identified from surgical and follow-up records, and clinical/demographic variables were extracted retrospectively. After ethics approval from the Eskişehir City Hospital Ethics Committee (Approval No: ESH/BAEK 2025/152, 22 May 2025), eligible patients were contacted by telephone and invited for an in-clinic follow-up visit between 26 May 2025 and 20 July 2025. At this visit, scar outcomes were evaluated using the POSAS, with patient self-ratings and an observer rating performed by a clinician in a standardized manner. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians prior to the prospective phase.

Study population and sample selection

This two-center, descriptive cross-sectional (ambispective) study enrolled consecutive eligible patients aged 8–20 years who underwent superficial dermabrasion followed by STSG for stable post-burn leukoderma. Index procedures performed between 26 May 2020 and 20 July 2024 were identified from surgical logs and follow-up files, and core perioperative variables were abstracted retrospectively. Thereafter, with assent/consent from patients and/or guardians, prospective POSAS assessments were collected between 26 May and 20 July 2025. Eligibility required a clinically confirmed diagnosis of post-burn leukoderma with stability for ≥ 6 months prior to surgery, receipt of dermabrasion + STSG as the primary reconstructive procedure within the study window, age 8–20 years at surgery, and availability of essential perioperative data with ≥ 6 months postoperative follow-up at the time of POSAS evaluation. Patients were excluded if records for key variables were insufficient or POSAS data could not be obtained despite contact attempts; if there was active local infection at the donor/recipient site before surgery or an ongoing systemic infection at POSAS assessment; if uncontrolled systemic conditions likely to confound healing were present (e.g., poorly controlled diabetes or chronic systemic immunosuppression); or if hypopigmentation was non-burn in origin or the index surgery involved procedures other than dermabrasion + STSG (e.g., cellular grafting). Postoperative complications were not grounds for exclusion and were instead recorded as analytic variables to examine their association with scar outcomes.

Surgical technique

All procedures consisted of superficial dermabrasion followed by application of a thin STSG. Dermabrasion was performed with a motorized dermabrader using a diamond fraise (≈ 60–120 grit). Where applicable, sterile silicon-carbide paper 180–220 grit was used to achieve uniform pinpoint bleeding and a pliable, well-vascularized bed while avoiding deep reticular dermis. Hemostasis was achieved with gentle compression and bipolar cautery as needed. STSGs were harvested from the anterolateral thigh or lower abdomen using a dermatome set to 0.012–0.015 inch (≈ 0.30–0.38 mm). Grafts were trimmed to fit and oriented without tension. Grafts were used unmeshed in cosmetically sensitive or small areas. Meshing at 1:1.5 was applied when contour, exudate control, or surface area required. Fixation used interrupted fine monofilament sutures and, when appropriate, skin staples. Quilting sutures were added to limit shear. Donor sites received non-adherent dressings. The first dressing change occurred on postoperative day 3–5. Protected mobilization began thereafter. Range of motion increased progressively as tolerated, and sutures or staples were removed at approximately 10–14 days. Perioperative antibiotic prophylaxis and analgesia followed institutional protocols. All procedures were performed in an operating theatre under general anesthesia or sedation according to lesion extent and patient tolerance, using standard sterile technique.

Follow-up and outcome assessment

Patients were followed for a minimum of six months postoperatively. Clinical outcomes were assessed using the POSAS [20], which captures both patient- and observer-rated evaluations of scar quality. Postoperative complications—including graft loss, hypertrophic scarring, graft-related pigmentary mismatch, and infections—were systematically documented. In addition to quantitative assessments, clinical photographs were taken at standardized time points (preoperative, intraoperative, and 6-month postoperative) under consistent lighting and positioning conditions. Photographs were used to qualitatively demonstrate postoperative appearance, such as skin color compatibility and texture normalization. Identifying features were anonymized in all images, and written informed consent was obtained from all patients or their legal guardians for the use of clinical photographs in publication.

The observer-rated POSAS was completed by the operating plastic surgeon after brief standardized training and calibration with sample cases. Ratings were performed at the follow-up visit under consistent room lighting and a fixed viewing distance, after patients had completed their self-ratings. The observer was not blinded to surgical details or postoperative course, which may introduce assessment bias; to partially standardize judgments, a written checklist and reference anchors were used for each POSAS item. Formal inter- or intra-rater reliability testing was not conducted and is acknowledged as a limitation.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), with a significance threshold of α = 0.05. Descriptive statistics were calculated for demographic and clinical variables, including means, standard deviations, frequencies, and percentages. Data normality was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test due to the limited sample size (n < 50). Since POSAS scores deviated from a normal distribution, non-parametric methods were applied. Spearman’s rank-order correlation was used to explore associations with POSAS scores. Mann–Whitney U tests were employed to compare POSAS outcomes by binary variables such as sex and complication status. Kruskal–Wallis H tests examined POSAS differences across burn-size categories, which were derived from wound area calculations in cm² and grouped as small (≤ 50 cm²), medium (51–100 cm²), and large (> 100 cm²).

Results

The correlation analysis revealed a very strong and statistically significant association between patient-reported and clinician-rated scar quality scores on the POSAS (r = .924, p < .001), demonstrating excellent concordance between subjective patient experiences and professional assessments of scar appearance. In contrast, patient age showed no meaningful correlation with either the POSAS-Patient (r = .126, p = .462) or POSAS-Observer (r = .028, p = .873) scores, suggesting that perceptions of scar severity and aesthetic outcomes were not influenced by age in this cohort. These findings further support the reliability of POSAS as a dual-perspective assessment tool across different age groups (Table 1).

Discussion

The strong correlation between patient and observer POSAS scores indicates convergent appraisal of scar quality in routine practice. This agrees with recent work on POSAS 3.0 that refined observer items through an international Delphi process and strengthened content validity for generic and linear scars [21]. Robustness of POSAS as an outcome measure has also been supported by psychometric studies using modern analyses. These studies show reliable structure and coherent measurement across the patient and observer perspectives [22]. Clinical interpretation improves further with new minimal clinically important difference estimates for POSAS 2.0 in burn populations, which suggest that small score changes can be meaningful to patients [23].

Age and sex showed no association with POSAS ratings in this cohort. Similar neutrality has been observed when cohorts were restricted to localized lesions and managed under standardized aftercare protocols [24]. Larger registries sometimes detect demographic or size-related signals, yet these often reflect overall burn severity rather than intrinsic age or sex effects. Burn-size category also did not influence POSAS scores in this cohort. This is plausible, as the lesions were stable and treated with targeted resurfacing, which may blunt the broader gradients observed in extensive or deep injuries over prolonged follow-up [25]. Objective assessment literature reminds us that perceived severity can drift over months. This supports a focus on standardized timing and complementary tools when comparing groups of different clinical backgrounds [26, 27].

Complications emerged as the dominant determinant of poorer ratings with large effect sizes. This pattern has a direct mechanistic basis in graft biology. Hematoma, seroma, shear, and early infection disrupt adherence, delay inosculation, and can impair melanocyte survival and transfer [28–30]. Recent syntheses highlight modifiable risk factors and perioperative strategies that improve graft take. Negative-pressure wound therapy and related dressings reduce fluid collections and shear, which translates into fewer failures [31]. Clinical series continue to show that early wound problems correlate with partial loss and worse patient-reported outcomes [32]. The strength of association we observed therefore fits the pathophysiology of grafted wounds.

Dermabrasion followed by grafting remains a rational option for stable post-burn leukoderma because it prepares a receptive bed and supports reconstructive resurfacing with thin grafts. Prospective studies in post-burn leucoderma report meaningful repigmentation after superficial dermabrasion combined with epidermal graft techniques [33]. Earlier series describe durable color match with dermabrasion and thin split-thickness grafts, including extremity sites [14, 34]. A focused review of post-burn leukoderma surgery also supports these pathways as pragmatic choices when resources are limited [35]. Contemporary consensus statements in vitiligo surgery emphasize disease stability, meticulous recipient-site preparation, and structured postoperative care. These principles align with our protocol and likely contributed to more favorable postoperative scar assessments in the absence of complications [36, 37].

Alternative cell-based options merit consideration in selected settings. Non-cultured epidermal cell suspensions show benefit in controlled studies and can be paired with targeted phototherapy to consolidate repigmentation [38]. Large retrospective cohorts of melanocyte-keratinocyte transplantation report durable responses in stable disease, although expertise and cost limit access in many regions [39]. Recent dermatology reports describe effective and durable repigmentation with autologous cell suspensions in adults, which expands the armamentarium where infrastructure exists [40]. These data justify a tiered approach in which dermabrasion plus split-thickness grafting remains a dependable reconstructive option, while cell-based techniques are reserved for complex sites or prior failures [35].

Clinical implications are clear. Teams should treat complication avoidance as a primary priority, since postoperative scar quality and graft stability are closely linked to early stability. Hemostasis, dead-space control, immobilization, and bolster fixation reduce shear and fluid collections that threaten graft take [29]. Risk factors identified in recent reviews can be addressed through careful bed preparation and standardized dressings [28, 41]. Postoperative adjuvants informed by consensus guidance—such as topical calcineurin inhibitors or excimer-based phototherapy—may support postoperative integration and appearance once stability is confirmed [36, 37]. Scar modulation can be integrated after wound maturation. Laser consensus statements and systematic reviews report improvements in vascularity, pliability, pigmentation, and thickness across traumatic and burn scars, albeit with heterogeneity [42, 43]. Evidence for pressure garments is mixed, suggesting shared decision-making and attention to patient tolerance rather than a blanket mandate [44, 45].

Methodological constraints warrant caution: the ambispective, observational, cross-sectional analyses preclude causal inference; the modest, two-center sample (n = 36) limits generalizability and reduces subgroup power; and small expected counts necessitated nonparametric tests that, while robust, are less efficient. Although POSAS is validated and responsive, it reflects subjective appraisal; the observer rating was completed by the operating surgeon without blinding, and inter-/intra-rater reliability was not assessed, introducing potential measurement bias. Future studies should triangulate POSAS with objective color metrics (e.g., colorimetry, standardized photogrammetry/3D imaging) to stabilize comparisons across centers and time and pre-specify MCID thresholds to guide sample-size planning and interpret clinical meaning. Prospective multicenter designs with harmonized complication definitions, longer follow-up, and blinded independent raters are needed to establish comparative effectiveness; pragmatic trials that link perioperative complication-prevention bundles to postoperative appearance, scar quality, and graft durability would clarify real-world impact beyond lesion stability.
