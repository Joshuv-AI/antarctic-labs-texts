Hecke’s inverse cotangent numbers
Kurt Girstmair

Abstract
In connection with Eisenstein series for the principal congruence subgroup Γ ⁡ ( n ) \Gamma(n) , Hecke introduced certain numbers, of which he said that they are rational and cumbersome to calculate. We show, however, that these numbers are (essentially) generators of the n n th cyclotomic field or its maximal real subfield. They arise from the well investigated cotangent numbers by matrix inversion, which is why we call them inverse cotangent numbers . We describe them as linear combinations of roots of unity with rational coefficients in a fairly closed form, provided that n n is square-free. We also exhibit a formula for these numbers in terms of generalized Bernoulli numbers and Gauss sums.



1. Introduction

Let
n\geq 3,r\geq 1
be positive integers. Let
j\in\mathbb{Z}
be prime to
n
.
In his paper
[
9
]
Hecke introduced the numbers

d^{(r)}_{j}=\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{1}{m\in\Z}{m\equiv j\,{\rm mod }\,n}}\frac{\mu(|m|)}{m^{r}},

(1)

where
\mu
denotes the Möbius function.
In the case
r=1
the summation of the series has to be understood in the sense of

\lim_{k\to\infty}\sum_{|m|\leq k}.

(2)

Hecke said that the numbers
d^{(r)}_{j}
turn out to be rational (“ergeben sich als rationale Zahlen”).
However, he doubtlessly knew that a certain power of
\pi
is involved in
d^{(r)}_{j}
, which, by the analogy of
1/\zeta(r)
, can only be
\pi^{-r}
. Indeed, put

\widehat{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{j}=\frac{-i^{r}\pi^{r}}{(r-1)!\,n^{r}}\,d^{(r)}_{j}.

(3)

Then this number generates the
n
th cycotomic field (over
\mathbb{Q}
) if
r
is odd, and its maximal real subfield if
r
is even (see Proposition
2
).
In particular,
\widehat{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}
is rational only in few cases like
n\in\{3,4,6\}
,
r
even.
The notation
\widehat{\rm{ct}}
will turn out to be reasonable.

Hecke further said that the computation of

d^{(r)}_{j}
is fairly laborious if
n
is not a prime, and will not be discussed in his paper (“Ihre Berechnung ist, wenn nicht gerade
N
eine Primzahl ist,
ziemlich umständlich, und soll hier nicht weiter erörtert werden” (Hecke’s
N
corresponds to our
n
)).
We will see, however, that even in the case of a small prime number
n
and a small parameter
r
(like
n=11
,
r=4
) the result is fairly complex (see Section 4).

Let us briefly sketch Hecke’s context. He studies the vector space of Eisenstein series of weight
r
,
r\in\mathbb{Z}
,
r\geq 1
, for the principal congruence subgroup

\Gamma(n)=\{A\in\mbox{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z});A\equiv I\mbox{ mod }n\}
, where
I
denotes the unit matrix. Since the cases
r=1,2
are more complicated, we assume
r\geq 3
for our sketch.
Hecke’s primitive Eisenstein series
G(z,r,\underline{a})
,

\underline{a}=(a_{1},a_{2})
a pair of coprime integers, have an easily computable Fourier expansion, but they do not form a
\mathbb{C}
-basis of the vector space in question. Therefore, he defines

G^{*}(z,r,\underline{a})=\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{1}{1\le j\le n/2}{(j,n)=1}}d^{(r)}_{j^{*}}G(z,r,j\underline{a}),

where
j^{*}
is an inverse of
j
mod
n
,
and obtains such a basis, provided that the pairs
\underline{a}
are suitably chosen with respect to cusps.

Since the numbers
\widehat{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{j}
are in the
n
th-cyclotomic field
\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{n})
,
\zeta_{n}=e^{2\pi i/n}
, a computation of these number means a representation as a rational linear combination of powers of
\zeta_{n}
.
We obtain this, in a fairly explicit form, only if
\zeta_{n}
generates a
normal basis
of
\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{n})
. This, however, is true if, an only if,
n
is
square-free
(see
[
13
]
).
“Fairly explicit” means up to the inversion of an easily computable rational matrix involving values of Bernoulli polynomials (see Theorem
1
and the examples of Section 4).

The numbers
\widehat{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{j}
are closely connected with the
cotangent numbers

{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{j}
,
defined by

{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{j}=i^{r}\cot^{(r-1)}(\pi j/n),\kern 5.0pt(j,n)=1,

(4)

where
\cot^{(r-1)}
stands for the
(r-1)
th derivative of the cotangent function.
So we have
\cot^{(0)}=\cot
,
\cot^{(1)}=-\cot^{2}-1
, and so on.
Since the numbers
\widehat{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{j}
arise from the numbers
{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{j}
by matrix inversion,
they will be called
inverse cotangent numbers
(see (
6
)).

Cotangent numbers have been studied in the case
r=1
in connection with
formulas for the
relative class number
of
\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{n})
, see, for instance,
[
2
, formula (3.1)]
,
[
6
, formulas (3), (4)]
,
[
3
]
,
[
14
]
,
[
11
]
, for
r\geq 2
also

[
5
]
and
[
6
]
. See also the final remark of this paper.

As a consequence of our study, we exhibit the values of series like

\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{1}{m>0,}{(m,n)=1}}\mu(m)\frac{\sin(2\pi m^{*}/n)}{m^{r}},\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{1}{m>0,}{(m,n)=1}}\mu(m)\frac{\cos(2\pi m^{*}/n)}{m^{r}},

where
n
is square-free,
r
odd in the first,
r
even in the second case (
m^{*}
has been defined above). These series have rational values up to the factor
\pi^{-r}
, see Proposition
5
.

Finally, we determine the eigenvalues of the matrices used for the definition of the numbers
\widehat{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{j}
, see Section 6. This gives another formula for the numbers
\widehat{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{j}
in terms of generalized Bernoulli numbers
and Gauss sums, see Theorem
2
.

2. Inverse cotangent numbers

Let
n,r
be as above.
The partial fraction decomposition of the cotangent function yields, for
j\in\mathbb{Z}
,
(j,n)=1
,

\cot^{(r-1)}(\pi j/n)=(-1)^{r-1}(r-1)!\left(\frac{n}{\pi}\right)^{r}\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{1}{m\in\Z}{m\equiv j\,{\rm mod }\,n}}\frac{1}{m^{r}}.

In the case
r=1
the series has to be understood in the sense of (
2
).

Proposition 1

Let
n
and
r
be as above.
Let
{\cal R}=\{j;1\leq j\leq n/2,(j,n)=1\}.
For a number
j\in\mathbb{Z}
,
(j,n)=1
, let
j^{*}
denote the inverse of
j
mod
n
, i.e.,

jj^{*}\equiv 1\mod n
,
1\leq j^{*}\leq n
.
If
{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{j}
and
\widehat{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{j}
are defined by
(
1
), (
3
), and (
4
), then

\sum_{l\in{\cal R}}{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{sl^{*}}\,\widehat{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{lt^{*}}=\delta_{s,t},

(5)

for all
s,t\in{\cal R}
. Here
\delta
is the Kronecker delta.

Proof.
The case
r=1
of conditional convergence is somewhat subtle. It was settled in
[
7
]
. In the case
r\geq 2
the series for
{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{j}
,
\widehat{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{k}
are absolutely
convergent and can, thus, be multiplied without problems. We obtain that the sum in question equals

\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{1}{m\in\Z}{m\equiv st^*\,{\rm mod }\,n}}\frac{1}{m^{r}}\sum_{l\in{\cal R}}\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{1}{j\in\Z,j\DIV m}{j\equiv lt^*\,{\rm mod }\,n}}\mu(|j|).

For a fixed number
m
,
m\equiv st^{*}\mod n
, the inner double sum can be written

\sum_{l\in{\cal R}}\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{1}{j\DIV m,j>0}{j\equiv lt^*\,\rm{mod}\,n}}\mu(j)+\sum_{l\in{\cal R}}\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{1}{j\DIV m,j>0}{j\equiv-lt^*\,\rm{mod}\,n}}\mu(j).

This is the same as

\sum_{j\,|\,m,j>0}\mu(j)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1,&\hbox{ if $m=\pm 1$;}\\
0,&\hbox{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

However, the case
m=-1
would imply
s\equiv-t
mod
n
, which is impossible for
s,t\in{\cal R}
. Therefore, only
m=1
and
s\equiv t
mod
n
contribute to the whole.

\Box

In view of (
5
), we introduce the matrices

{\rm{Ct}}^{(r)}=({\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{jk^{*}})_{j,k\in{\cal R}},\kern 5.0pt\widehat{\rm{Ct}}^{(r)}=(\widehat{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{jk^{*}})_{j,k\in{\cal R}},

the rows and columns of these matrices having the natural order of
{\cal R}
.
Then Proposition
1
can be phrased as

\widehat{\rm{Ct}}^{(r)}=({\rm{Ct}}^{(r)})^{-1}.

(6)

This justifies the name
inverse cotangent numbers
given to the numbers
\widehat{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{j}
in Section 1.

We further note that the numbers
{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{j}
, and, by (
6
), also the numbers
\widehat{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{j}
,
(j,n)=1
, lie in
\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{n})
.
Indeed,
\mbox{ct}^{(1)}_{j}=i\cot(\pi j/n)=(1+\zeta_{n}^{j})/(1-\zeta_{n}^{j})
, and
{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{j}
is a polynomial in
i\cot(\pi j/n)
with rational coefficients.

We also need the action of the Galois group of
\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{n})
over
\mathbb{Q}
on the numbers
{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{j}
,
(j,n)=1
.
Let
\sigma_{k}
,
(k,n)=1
, be the Galois automorphism of
\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{n})
defined by
\zeta_{n}\mapsto\zeta_{n}^{k}
.
Then

\sigma_{k}({\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{j})={\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{kj},\kern 5.0pt(j,n)=1,

(7)

The action of
\sigma_{k}
on the numbers
\widehat{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{j}
,
j\in{\cal R}
, can be found by (
6
), namely, by the identity

\sigma_{k}(\widehat{\rm{Ct}}^{(r)})\cdot\sigma_{k}({\rm{Ct}}^{(r)})=\sigma_{k}(\widehat{\rm{Ct}}^{(r)})\cdot({\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{jkl^{*}})_{j,l\in{\cal R}}=I,

where
I=(\delta_{j,l})_{j,l\in{\cal R}}
is the unit matrix. We obtain

\sigma_{k}(\widehat{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{j})=\widehat{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{jk^{*}}.

(8)

The numbers
{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{j}
,
j\in{\cal R}
, are
\mathbb{Q}
-linearly independent. Indeed, a nontrivial relation

\sum_{j\in{\cal R}}b_{j}{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{j}=0,\kern 5.0ptb_{j}\in\mathbb{Q},

would, by Galois action, entail the relations

\sum_{j\in{\cal R}}b_{j}{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{jk^{*}}=0,\kern 5.0ptk\in{\cal R}.

Accordingly, the rows of the matrix
{\rm{Ct}}^{(r)}
would be linearly dependent (over
\mathbb{C}
), which is impossible,
since the matrix
{\rm{Ct}}^{(r)}
is invertible. In the same way, the numbers
\widehat{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{j}
,
j\in{\cal R}
, are
\mathbb{Q}
-linearly independent.

Further, we observe that
\sigma_{-1}{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{j}
=
(-1)^{r}{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{j}
,
j\in{\cal R}
. In view of (
6
), this implies

\sigma_{-1}(\widehat{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{j})=(-1)^{r}\widehat{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{j}
. Hence the numbers
\widehat{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}
are real if
r
is even, and purely imaginary if
r
is odd.

Proposition 2

We adopt the above notation.
Then for each
j
with
(j,n)=1
,

\mathbb{Q}(\widehat{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{j})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{n}),&\hbox{if $r$ is odd;}\\
\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{n}+\zeta_{n}^{-1}),&\hbox{if $r$ is even.}\end{array}\right.

Proof.

We know that the numbers
\widehat{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{j}
,
j\in{\cal R}
, span a
\mathbb{Q}
-vector space of dimension
|{\cal R}|=\varphi(n)/2
.
If
r
is odd, this vector space is not a subfield of
\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{n})
because all of its numbers are purely imaginary. Since the field

\mathbb{Q}(\widehat{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{j})
is normal over
\mathbb{Q}
, it contains this subspace, and, accordingly, has a
\mathbb{Q}
-dimension
>\varphi(n)/2
.
Therefore, this field coincides with
\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{n})
. If
r
is even, this subspace lies in the maximal real subfield
\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{n}+\zeta_{n}^{-1})
and has
the same
\mathbb{Q}
-dimension.

\Box

Our plan for the next section is as follows. We assume that
n
is square-free and express the entries of
{\rm{Ct}}^{(r)}
as rational linear combinations of the basis vectors
\zeta_{n}^{k}
,
1\leq k\leq n
,
(k,n)=1
,
of the field
\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{n})
. This will give the respective representation of the entries of
\widehat{\rm{Ct}}^{(r)}
.

3. Generalized Bernoulli matrices

We need the following result, which holds for all
n\geq 3
and seems to be not widely known (see
[
5
, p. 381]
and the reference given there).

{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{1}=\frac{2^{r}n^{r-1}}{r}\sum_{k=1}^{n}B^{(r)}(k/n)\zeta_{n}^{-k}-\delta_{r,1},

(9)

where
B^{(r)}
denotes the
r
th
Bernoulli polynomial
. Here the cotangent number
{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{j}
is expressed as a rational linear combination of powers of
\zeta_{n}
, which are, in general, not
\mathbb{Q}
-linearly independent.

Proposition 3

If
n\geq 3
is square-free, the number
{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{1}
can be written

{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{1}=\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{1}{1\le j\le n}{(j,n)=1}}\widetilde{B}^{(r)}_{j}\zeta_{n}^{j},

(10)

where
\widetilde{B}^{(r)}_{j}
is given by

\widetilde{B}^{(r)}_{j}=\frac{(-1)^{r}2^{r}n^{r-1}}{r}\sum_{d\,|\,n}\mu(d)\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{1}{1\le k\le\NT}{(k,n)=d,\, k\equiv j\,\rm{mod}\, n/d}}B^{(r)}(k/n).

Here

\widetilde{n}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}n-1,&\hbox{if $r$ is odd;}\\
n,&\hbox{if $r$ is even.}\end{array}\right.

Proof.

Using the identity
B^{(r)}(1-x)=(-1)^{r}B^{(r)}(x)
, we may write (
9
) as

{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{1}=\frac{(-1)^{r}2^{r}n^{r-1}}{r}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}B^{(r)}(k/n)\zeta_{n}^{k}-\delta_{r,1}.

If
r=1
,
(-1)^{r}2^{r}n^{r-1}B^{(r)}(0)/r-\delta_{r,1}=0
. If
r
is odd,
r\geq 3
,
B^{(r)}(0)=0
.
Hence we have to deal with the sum

\sum_{k=1}^{\widetilde{n}}B^{(r)}(k/n)\zeta_{n}^{k}

(observe
B^{(r)}(0)=B^{(r)}(1)
if
r
is even).
This sum can be written

\sum_{d\,|\,n}\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{1}{1\le k\le\NT}{(k,n)=d}}B^{(r)}(k/n)\zeta_{n/d}^{k/d}.

(11)

Since
n
is square-free,
\zeta_{n/d}^{k/d}
is - up to the factor
\mu(d)
– the conjugate of a certain trace of
\zeta_{n}
(see equation (34) in
[
6
]
). Therefore,

\zeta_{n/d}^{k/d}=\mu(d)\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{1}{1\le j\le n}{(j,n)=1, j\equiv k\,\rm{mod}\,n/d}}\zeta_{n}^{j}.

Accordingly, the sum of (
11
) equals

\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{1}{1\le j\le n}{(j,n)=1}}\zeta^{j}\sum_{d\,|\,n}\mu(d)\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{1}{1\le k\le\NT}{(k,n)=d, k\equiv j\,\rm{mod}\, n/d}}B^{(r)}(k/n),

which gives the desired result.

\Box

We define, for
j\in\mathbb{Z}
,
(j,n)=1
,

s_{j}=\zeta_{n}^{j}-\zeta_{n}^{-j}=2i\sin(2\pi j/n)\mbox{ and }c_{j}=\zeta_{n}^{j}+\zeta_{n}^{-j}=2\cos(2\pi j/n).

Recall the definition of
{\cal R}
from the foregoing section.
Due to the identity
B^{(r)}(1-x)=(-1)^{r}B^{(r)}(x)
, we have
\widetilde{B}^{(r)}_{n-j}=(-1)^{r}\widetilde{B}^{(r)}_{j}
.
Hence (
10
) yields the following corollary.

Corollary 1

If
n\geq 3
is square-free, the number
{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{1}
can be written

{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{1}=\sum_{j\in{\cal R}}\widetilde{B}^{(r)}_{j}s_{j},\mbox{ if }r\mbox{ is odd},

(12)

and

{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{1}=\sum_{j\in{\cal R}}\widetilde{B}^{(r)}_{j}c_{j},\mbox{ if }r\mbox{ is even}.

(13)

Next we introduce certain additional matrices.
To this end we observe

\sigma_{k}(s_{j})=s_{kj},\sigma_{k}(c_{j})=c_{kj},\kern 5.0ptj\in\mathbb{Z},(j,n)=1,

where
\sigma_{k}
is the Galois automorphism of the foregoing section.
In view of (
7
), (
12
), and (
13
), we obtain

{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{k}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\sum_{j\in{\cal R}}\widetilde{B}^{(r)}_{j}s_{kj},&\hbox{if $r$ is odd;}\\
\sum_{j\in{\cal R}}\widetilde{B}^{(r)}_{j}c_{kj},&\hbox{if $r$ is even.}\end{array}\right.

This can be written

{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{k}=\sum_{j\in{\cal R}}\widetilde{B}^{(r)}_{jk^{*}}s_{j},\kern 5.0pt{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{k}=\sum_{j\in{\cal R}}\widetilde{B}^{(r)}_{jk^{*}}c_{j},

(14)

in the respective cases. Here, however, we have to
make the following
convention
: The subscript
jk^{*}
means the representative of
jk^{*}
in
\{1,\ldots,n\}
, i.e.,
the number
l
in this range, defined by
l\equiv jk^{*}
mod
n
.

Now we define the matrices

\widetilde{B}^{(r)}=(\widetilde{B}^{(r)}_{jk^{*}})_{j,k\in{\cal R}},\kern 5.0ptS=(s_{jk^{*}})_{j,k\in{\cal R}},\mbox{ and }C=(c_{jk^{*}})_{j,k\in{\cal R}}.

We call
\widetilde{B}^{(r)}
the
generalized Bernoulli matrix
.
The identities of (
14
) yield the following proposition.

Proposition 4

If
n\geq 3
is square-free, we have

{\rm{Ct}}^{(r)}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}S\cdot(\widetilde{B}^{(r)})^{T},&\hbox{if $r$ is odd;}\\
C\cdot(\widetilde{B}^{(r)})^{T},&\hbox{if $r$ is even,}\end{array}\right.,

where
(\kern 5.0pt)^{T}
denotes the transpose of the respective matrix.

Since
\widehat{\rm{Ct}}^{(r)}
is the inverse of the matrix
{\rm{Ct}}^{(r)}
, we immediately obtain, if
n
is square-free,

\widehat{\rm{Ct}}^{(r)}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(\widehat{B}^{(r)})^{T}\cdot S^{-1},&\hbox{if $r$ is odd;}\\
(\widehat{B}^{(r)})^{T}\cdot C^{-1},&\hbox{if $r$ is even,}\end{array}\right.,

(15)

where
\widehat{B}^{(r)}=(\widetilde{B}^{(r)})^{-1}
. We write
\widehat{B}^{(r)}_{j,k}
,
j,k\in{\cal R}
, for the entries of the matrix
\widehat{B}^{(r)}
.

The inverse matrices of
S
and
C
have been given, in the square-free case, in
[
8
]
. For the convenience of the reader we repeat their definition here.
For
k\in\mathbb{Z}
,
(k,n)=1
, put

\lambda(k)=|\{q;q\geq 3,q\,|\,n,k\equiv 1\mbox{ mod }q\}|.

Moreover, define, for
k\in\mathbb{Z}
,
(k,n)=1
,

\widehat{s}_{k}=\frac{-1}{n}\sum_{l\in{\cal R}}(\lambda(kl)-\lambda(-kl))s_{l}

and

\widehat{c}_{k}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{l\in{\cal R}}(\lambda(kl)+\lambda(-kl)+\rho_{n})c_{l},

where

\rho_{n}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}2,&\hbox{if $n$ is odd;}\\
4,&\hbox{if $n$ is even.}\end{array}\right.

Then

S^{-1}=(\widehat{s}_{jk^{*}})_{j,k\in{\cal R}},\kern 5.0ptC^{-1}=(\widehat{c}_{jk^{*}})_{j,k\in{\cal R}}.

Now we are in a position to express the numbers
\widehat{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{j}
,
j\in{\cal R}
, in an explicit way, up to the inversion of the matrx
\widetilde{B}^{(r)}
.

Theorem 1

If
n\geq 3
is square-free, we have

\widehat{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{1}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\sum_{j\in{\cal R}}\widehat{B}^{(r)}_{j,1}\widehat{s}_{j},&\hbox{ if $r$ is odd;}\\
\sum_{j\in{\cal R}}\widehat{B}^{(r)}_{j,1}\widehat{c}_{j},&\hbox{ if $r$ is even.}\end{array}\right.

Theorem
1
allows writing
\widehat{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{1}
in the form

\widehat{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{1}=\sum_{j\in{\cal R}}b_{j}s_{j},\>\mbox{ and }\>\widehat{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{1}=\sum_{j\in{\cal R}}b_{j}c_{j},\kern 5.0ptb_{j}\in\mathbb{Q},

if
r
is odd and
r
is even, respectively. If we apply
\sigma_{k^{*}}
,
(k,n)=1
, we obtain

\widehat{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{k}=\sum_{j\in{\cal R}}b_{j}s_{jk^{*}},\kern 5.0pt\widehat{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{k}=\sum_{j\in{\cal R}}b_{j}c_{jk^{*}},\mbox{ respectively},

(recall (
8
)).

In other words, the number
\widehat{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{k}
has, as a linear combination of
s_{j}
and
c_{j}
,
j\in{\cal R}
,
(up to sign changes, if
r
is odd) the same coefficients as
\widehat{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{1}
, but in a permuted order.
Hence it suffices to render
\widehat{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{1}
in this form, as in Theorem
1
and in the examples of the next section.

Remark.
Let
n=p\geq 3
be a prime. Then the entry
\widetilde{B}^{(r)}_{jk^{*}}
of the matrix
\widetilde{B}^{(r)}
simplifies to

(-1)^{r}\frac{2^{r}p^{r-1}}{r}B^{(r)}(jk^{*}/p)\>\mbox{ and }\>(-1)^{r}\frac{2^{r}p^{r-1}}{r}(B^{(r)}(jk^{*}/p)+B^{(r)}(1))

if
r
is odd and even, respectively (recall the convention for
jk^{*}
made in connection with (
14
)). Therefore, the matrix
\widetilde{B}^{(r)}
is
basically the matrix of a generalized
Maillet determinant
(see, for instance,
[
2
]
and
[
12
]
).

On the other hand, the numbers
\widehat{s}_{j}
and
\widehat{c}_{j}
simplify to

\widehat{s}_{j}=-s_{j^{*}}/p\>\mbox{ and }\>\widehat{c}_{j}=(c_{j^{*}}-2)/p,\>(j,p)=1.

(16)

In view of Theorem
1
, this means that
\widehat{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{1}
can be found on combining certain entries of the inverse of a simple Maillet-type matrix
with the numbers of (
16
).

4. Some examples

In this section we give a list of the numbers
\widehat{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{1}
for
n
square-free,
11\leq n\leq 15
, and
1\leq r\leq 4
(up to one exception for reasons of magnitude).
It is more convenient to work with
s^{*}_{j}=i\sin(\pi j/n)
and
c^{*}_{j}=\cos(\pi j/n)
instead of
s_{j}
and
c_{j}
. The cases
n=11,13

6. Eigenvalues and character coordinates

In this section let
n
be an integer,
n\geq 3
.
Let
a\in\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{n})
and
\chi
a Dirichlet character mod
n
with conductor
f_{\chi}
. Let
\chi_{f}
denote the Dirichlet character mod
f_{\chi}
attached to
\chi
(so
\chi_{f}(j)=\chi(j)

if
(j,n)=1
). The
\chi
-
coordinate

y(\chi|a)
was introduced by Leopoldt (see
[
13
]
) and is defined by the equation

y(\chi|a)\tau(\overline{\chi}_{f})=\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{1}{1\le j\le n}{(j,n)=1}}\overline{\chi}(j)\sigma_{j}(a);

here
\overline{\chi}=\chi^{-1}
is the complex-conjugate (or inverse) character of
\chi
and
\tau(\chi_{f})
the
Gauss sum

\sum_{j=1}^{f_{\chi}}\chi_{f}(j)\zeta_{f_{\chi}}^{-j},

see
[
5
]
. Character coordinates are important for the study of Galois modules. For instance, the
\mathbb{Q}
-dimension of

\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{1}{1\le j\le n}{(j,n)=1}}\mathbb{Q}\sigma_{j}(a)

equals the number of characters (mod
n
) such that
y(\chi|a)\neq 0
.

An important property of the
\chi
-coordinate says

y(\chi|\sigma_{k}(a))=\chi(k)y(\chi|a).

(20)

We also need the
reconstruction formula

a=\frac{1}{\varphi(n)}\sum_{\chi\in{\cal X}}y(\chi|a)\tau(\overline{\chi}_{f}),

(21)

where
{\cal X}
is the group of Dirichlet characters mod
n
.
This formula easily follows from the orthogonality relation for Dirichlet characters.

For a positive integer
r
, put

{\cal X}^{(r)}=\{\chi\in{\cal X};\chi(-1)=(-1)^{r}\}.

Therefore,
{\cal X}^{(r)}
is the set of odd (even, respectively) characters if
r
is odd (even, respectively).
Suppose, henceforth, that
a\in\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{n})
is purely imaginary if
r
is odd, and real if
r
is even.
Then
y(\chi|a)\neq 0
only if
\chi\in{\cal X}^{(r)}
.

We consider the matrix

A=(\sigma_{jk^{*}}(a))_{j,k\in{\cal R}}.

(22)

Further, we suppose that the set
{\cal X}^{(r)}
is arranged in some order, so that we can form the matrix

X=\sqrt{2/\varphi(n)}(\chi(k))_{k\in{\cal R},\chi\in{\cal X}^{(r)}}.

This matrix is
unitary
, i.e.,
X\overline{X}^{T}=I
, where
I
denotes the unit matrix (see
[
8
]
).
Let
\Delta
be the diagonal matrix defined by

\Delta=\left(\frac{1}{2}y(\chi|a)\tau(\overline{\chi}_{f})\delta_{\chi,\psi}\right)_{\chi,\psi\in{\cal X}^{(r)}}.

Then the reconstruction formula (
21
) and formula (
20
) show

X\Delta\overline{X}^{T}=A.

(23)

In other words, the character coordinates of
a
are essentially the eigenvalues of
A
.

In the special case
a={\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{1}
we have
A={\rm{Ct}}^{(r)}
, a matrix which, by (
23
), is unitarly congruent to

\Gamma=\left(\frac{1}{2}y(\chi|{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{1})\tau(\overline{\chi}_{f})\delta_{\chi,\psi}\right)_{\chi,\psi\in{\cal X}^{(r)}}.

Since
{\rm{Ct}}^{(r)}
is invertible, its inverse
\widehat{\rm{Ct}}^{(r)}
is unitarily congruent to
\Gamma^{-1}
.

Note, however, that
\widehat{\rm{Ct}}^{(r)}
is
not
of the form
(\sigma_{jk^{*}}(\widehat{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{1}))_{j,k\in{\cal R}}
(i.e., it does not go with (
22
)).
But
(\widehat{\rm{Ct}}^{(r)})^{T}
has this form. From the identities

\widehat{\rm{Ct}}^{(r)}=X\Gamma^{-1}\overline{X}^{T},\kern 5.0pt(\widehat{\rm{Ct}}^{(r)})^{T}=X\widehat{\Gamma}\overline{X}^{T},

where
\widehat{\Gamma}
is the diagonal matrix

\widehat{\Gamma}=\left(\frac{1}{2}y(\chi|\widehat{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{1})\tau(\overline{\chi}_{f})\delta_{\chi,\psi}\right)_{\chi,\psi\in{\cal X}^{(r)}}

(24)

we obtain

\widehat{\Gamma}=\overline{X}^{T}\overline{X}\Gamma^{-1}X^{T}X.

Some calculation shows

\widehat{\Gamma}=(2y(\overline{\chi}|{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{1})^{-1}\tau(\chi_{f})^{-1}\delta_{\chi,\psi})_{\chi,\psi\in{\cal R}}

and, in view of (
24
),

y(\chi|\widehat{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{1})\tau(\overline{\chi}_{f})=4y(\overline{\chi}|{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{1})^{-1}\tau(\chi_{f})^{-1}.

Moreover, we use the identity

\tau(\chi_{f})\tau(\overline{\chi}_{f})=(-1)^{r}f_{\chi},

see
[
15
, p. 36]
. Then we may write

y(\chi|\widehat{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{1})=\frac{4(-1)^{r}}{f_{\chi}}y(\overline{\chi}|{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{1})^{-1}.

(25)

In other words, the character coordinates of the number
\widehat{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{1}
are, in the main, the inverses of the character coordinates of the cotangent number
{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{1}
.
We think that also for this reason we may speak of “inverse” cotangent numbers.

The character coordinates of
{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{1}
are given in
[
5
]
. Indeed,

y(\chi|{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{1})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0,&\hbox{if $\chi\not\in{\cal X}^{(r)}$;}\\
(2n/f_{\chi})^{r}\prod_{p\,|\,n}(1-\overline{\chi}_{f}(p)/p^{r})B^{(r)}_{\chi_{f}}/r,&\hbox{if $\chi\in{\cal X}^{(r)}$.}\end{array}\right.

(26)

Here
B^{(r)}_{\chi_{f}}
is the generalized Bernoulli number

B^{(r)}_{\chi_{f}}=f_{\chi}^{r-1}\sum_{j=1}^{f_{\chi}}\chi_{f}(j)B^{(r)}(j/f_{\chi}),

where
B^{(r)}
is, as above, the
r
th Bernoulli polynomial.
Now the reconstruction formula (
21
), combined with (
25
) and (
26
), gives
the following theorem.

Theorem 2

For all integers
n\geq 3
and
r\geq 1
,

\widehat{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{1}=(-1)^{r}\frac{2^{2-r}r}{\varphi(n)n^{r}}\sum_{\chi\in{\cal X}^{(r)}}f_{\chi}^{r-1}\prod_{p\,|\,n}(1-\chi_{f}(p)/p^{r})^{-1}\frac{\tau(\overline{\chi}_{f})}{B^{(r)}_{\overline{\chi}_{f}}}.

Example.

In the case
n=15
,
r=3
there are four odd Dirichlet characters
\chi_{1}
,…,
\chi_{4}
, mod
n
of conductors
3
,
5
,
5
,
15
, respectively. Then

\displaystyle\widehat{\rm{ct}}^{(3)}_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{3}{16\cdot 15^{3}}\left(9(1-\chi_{1,3}(5)/125)^{-1}\frac{\tau(\chi_{1,3})}{B^{(3)}_{\chi_{1,3}}}+25(1-\chi_{2,5}(3)/27)^{-1}\frac{\tau(\chi_{3,5})}{B^{(3)}_{\chi_{3,5}}}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+25(1-\chi_{3,5}(3)/27)^{-1}\frac{\tau(\chi_{2,5})}{B^{(3)}_{\chi_{2,5}}}+225\frac{\tau(\chi_{4,15})}{B^{(3)}_{\chi_{4,15}}}\right).

The second subscript of the
\chi
’s is always the conductor.
So
\chi_{1,3}
is the character mod 3 attached to
\chi_{1}
. It is given by
\chi_{1,3}(2)=-1
. In the same way
\chi_{2,5}
is given by
\chi_{2,5}(2)=i
,
and
\chi_{3,5}=\overline{\chi_{2,5}}
. Finally,
\chi_{4,15}
is given by
\chi_{4,15}(2)=1
and
\chi_{4,15}(-1)=-1
. The values of the Gauss sums are as follows:

\tau(\chi_{1,3})=-s_{1,3}
,
\tau(\chi_{2,5})=-s_{1,5}-is_{2,5}
,
\tau(\chi_{3,5})=-s_{1,5}+is_{2,5}
, and
\tau(\chi_{4,15})=-s_{1,15}-s_{2,15}-s_{4,15}+s_{7,15}
.
Here
s_{j,m}
stands for
2i\sin(2\pi j/m)
. The corresponding Bernoulli numbers take the following values:
B^{(3)}_{\chi_{1,3}}=2/3
,
B^{(3)}_{\chi_{2,5}}=(12+6i)/5
,

B^{(3)}_{\chi_{3,5}}=(12-6i)/5
, and
B^{(3)}_{\chi_{4,15}}=48
. Altogether, we obtain

\widehat{\rm{ct}}^{(3)}_{1}=i(\sin(\pi/3)/672+11\sin(2\pi/5)/5840+\sin(\pi/5)/1168+\sin(2\pi/15)/1920

+\sin(4\pi/15)/1920+\sin(7\pi/15)/1920-\sin(\pi/15)/1920)
.

So we have expressed
\widehat{\rm{ct}}^{(3)}_{1}
as a rational linear combination of powers of
\zeta_{15}
, but these powers are not
\mathbb{Q}
-linearly independent here – in contrast with the respective result of Section 4.

Remarks
. 1. Let
n=p\geq 3
be a prime and
r
odd. Then all odd Dirichlet characters mod
p
have the conductor
p
. Accordingly, Theorem
2
says

\widehat{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{1}=-\frac{2^{2-r}}{(p-1)p}\sum_{\chi\in{\cal X}^{(r)}}\frac{\tau({\chi}_{f})}{B^{(r)}_{\chi_{f}}}.

This formula looks remarkably simple. Maybe Hecke envisioned something of this kind when he said that the case of a prime number
n
is easier than the composite case (see Section 1).

2. The character coordinates of the numbers
{\rm{ct}}^{(r)}_{1}
are closely related to those of
i^{r}\cot(\pi/n)^{r}
, since
\cot(x)^{r}
is a rational linear combination of derivatives of
\cot(x)
.
The character coordinates of the last-mentioned numbers can be found in
[
10
, Cor. 4.4]
and, for primitive characters, in
[
4
, Cor. 2.19]
, but without any reference to this name and, consequently,
to our paper
[
5
]
.
