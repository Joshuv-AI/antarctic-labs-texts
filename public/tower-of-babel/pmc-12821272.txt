Comparative efficacy of hip arthroscopy versus structured non-surgical treatment for femoroacetabular impingement syndrome: a mid-term retrospective cohort study
Authors: Shunjie Yang, Hui Wang, Lingcheng Wang, Xiaohe Tian, Gang Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFemoroacetabular impingement syndrome (FAIS) is a prevalent cause of hip pain, yet comparative evidence on the clinical efficacy of hip arthroscopic surgery (HAS) versus non-surgical treatment (NST) remains limited. This study evaluated their short- to mid-term outcomes to inform optimal therapeutic strategies for FAIS.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted on 85 patients with FAIS. Fifty-one patients underwent HAS, and 34 received a structured NST program. The Visual Analogue Scale (VAS), modified Harris Hip Score (mHHS), Non-Arthritic Hip Score (NAHS), and Hip Outcome Score-Activities of Daily Living (HOS-ADL) were compared between the two groups at pre-treatment and 1 month, 6 months, and final follow-up (mean 21.73 months).ResultsBoth groups demonstrated significant improvements, with 100% of patients achieving the minimum clinically important difference (MCID) and substantial clinical benefit (SCB) thresholds at the final follow-up. At 1 month follow-up, the NST group showed superior outcomes across all scores compared to the HAS group (P < 0.05). However, this pattern reversed at 6 months and final follow-up, where the HAS group demonstrated significantly better pain and function scores than the NST group (P < 0.05 for VAS, mHHS, NAHS, and HOS-ADL). The interaction analysis confirmed that improvement was greater in the NST group at 1 month follow-up but became greater in the HAS group thereafter.ConclusionsBoth HAS and structured NST are associated with satisfactory clinical outcomes in FAIS patients at mid-term follow-up. The recovery trajectories, however, differed, with NST associated with faster initial improvement and HAS associated with superior outcomes beyond the 6-month time point.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-03663-4.Keywords: Femoroacetabular impingement syndrome, Hip arthroscopic surgery, Non-surgical treatment, Clinical outcomes

Background

Femoroacetabular impingement syndrome (FAIS) is a common hip disorder characterized by premature contact between the femoral head–neck junction and the acetabular rim during terminal motion. This repeated mechanical conflict can lead to structural damage, particularly to the acetabular labrum and articular cartilage, and is considered a precursor to hip osteoarthritis [1, 2]. With an estimated prevalence of approximately 20% in the general population, the incidence of FAIS varies based on factors such as age, gender, and activity level [3]. Clinically, FAIS primarily presents with hip pain, restricted range of motion, and impaired functional capacity, significantly affecting patients’ quality of life [2].

The contemporary management of FAIS primarily involves two main strategies: hip arthroscopic surgery (HAS) and non-surgical treatment (NST). HAS is a minimally invasive procedure aimed at addressing the underlying morphological abnormalities through osteoplasty of the proximal femur or acetabulum, combined with the repair of associated soft-tissue injuries, such as labral tears and chondral lesions [2, 4]. In contrast, NST typically encompasses a regimen of structured physical therapy, activity modification, and targeted exercises designed to enhance dynamic hip stability and optimize movement patterns [4, 5].

Despite the widespread application of both approaches, considerable controversy persists regarding their comparative clinical efficacy [4, 6, 7]. Some meta-analyses of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have suggested superior outcomes with HAS [8], while others have found surgery to be no more effective than high-quality conservative care [6]. This ongoing debate underscores a critical gap in evidence, particularly concerning their comparative short- to mid-term effectiveness. Therefore, this retrospective cohort study aimed to evaluate and compare the clinical outcomes of HAS versus a structured NST program in patients with FAIS over a mid-term follow-up period.

Patients and methods

Patients

Patient enrollment

This retrospective cohort study analyzed data from patients diagnosed with FAIS. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Medical Ethics Committee of West China Hospital, Sichuan University [Approval No. 1253 (2022 Review)]. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

The enrollment process is detailed in Fig. 1. In brief, 111 hips were initially screened from September 2022 to August 2023. To establish a well-defined and comparable cohort to analyze treatment strategies, stringent inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied. Key exclusions included patients with advanced osteoarthritis (Tönnis grade ≥ 3), acetabular dysplasia, or other complex pathologies (e.g., loose bodies, previous hip fracture). These conditions were excluded as they typically necessitate a specific treatment path (often surgery), which would confound a direct comparison between HAS and NST. Additionally, patients with a history of prior HAS or formal NST treatment within the preceding 12 months were excluded to minimize the potential influence of previous interventions. Following these exclusions, 85 patients (85 hips) were enrolled for the final analysis.

Treatment allocation

Assignment to the HAS group or the NST group was not randomized but was intended to reflect real-world clinical practice. The treatment pathway for each patient was determined through a shared decision-making process between the patient and the treating orthopedic surgeon. This process integrated a comprehensive assessment of the patient’s clinical presentation, physical examination findings, imaging characteristics, and personal preference.

Patients were more frequently considered for and counseled about HAS if they presented with clinical profiles suggestive of potential benefit from surgical correction. These profiles included: (1) persistent, activity-limiting symptoms refractory to a prior trial of conservative management; (2) clinical and imaging findings indicative of a substantial mechanical pathology amenable to arthroscopic intervention, such as a prominent CAM deformity (alpha angle > 60°) or a combined labral–chondral lesion; or (3) high functional demands or athletic goals requiring a predictable and efficient recovery trajectory.

The structured NST program was typically recommended as a first-line option for patients with: (1) a strong personal preference to pursue non-operative management; (2) clinical presentations featuring recent symptom onset or those who had not yet undergone a formal trial of supervised physical therapy; or (3) less pronounced morphological abnormalities on imaging. Patients with comorbidities conferring increased perioperative risk were also directed toward this pathway.

The final treatment decision was made following a detailed discussion of the potential benefits, risks, and expected outcomes of both options. It is important to note that these clinical considerations were not mutually exclusive. Patients often presented with overlapping features, and the ultimate choice was reached through a synthesis of the surgeon’s clinical assessment and the patient’s individual values and circumstances. All patients in the NST group provided informed consent for this specific conservative protocol after declining, or opting to defer, the surgical option.

Assessment of baseline comparability

To address potential confounding inherent to the non-randomized study design, we rigorously evaluated the baseline comparability of the two groups. Demographics, symptom duration, and the prevalence of key intra-articular lesions were systematically compared between the HAS and NST groups. Crucially, to ensure a consistent and unbiased baseline comparison, the presence of intra-articular lesions for both groups was determined using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) prior to any treatment allocation. Methods

HAS group

Preoperative preparation

Routine preoperative examinations were completed to exclude surgical contraindications. All patients provided informed consent for the surgical procedure. All hip arthroscopic surgeries were performed by the same senior consultant orthopedic surgeon. The procedure was conducted under general anesthesia with the patient in the supine position. Both lower limbs were secured on a traction table with adequate padding to protect the perineum. The surgical field on the affected side was routinely prepared and draped in a sterile fashion.

Intraoperative procedures

With the patient positioned, traction was applied to the affected limb. Under fluoroscopic guidance using a C-arm, the hip joint space was gradually distracted by 8–10 mm, confirmed by the appearance of the vacuum sign. The standard anterolateral portal was established first, followed by the insertion of the arthroscope. Under direct arthroscopic visualization, the auxiliary mid-anterior portal was then created. A transverse capsulotomy was performed to connect these two portals, providing access to the central compartment. The arthroscope was introduced into the central compartment for diagnostic evaluation. The condition of the acetabular labrum was systematically assessed (Fig. 2a). Based on the severity of the injury and tissue quality, the labrum was managed either by debridement or suture refixation using suture anchors (Fig. 2b).

Key arthroscopic steps and intraoperative findings in the treatment of FAIS. A Exploration of acetabular labrum injury. B Repair of the injured acetabular labrum. C Exposure and removal of the CAM deformity in the proximal femur. D Exposure and removal of hyperplastic acetabular bone. E Observation and treatment of the articular cartilage injury. F Observation and debridement of the relaxed or injured round ligament. FAIS Femoroacetabular impingement syndrome; HAS Hip arthroscopic surgery

Subsequently, traction was released, and the hip was flexed to 40°–45°. The arthroscope was then advanced into the peripheral compartment. If CAM deformity was present, a longitudinal incision was made in the capsule along the femoral neck axis, creating a “T”-shaped capsulotomy to fully expose the aberrant bone. The hyperplastic bone was then meticulously resected using a burr until the normal concavity of the femoral head–neck junction was restored (Fig. 2c). The adequacy of resection was dynamically assessed intraoperatively by examining the range of motion and confirming the elimination of impingement under arthroscopic visualization. The criterion for sufficient resection was the smooth passage of the acetabular labrum over the femoral head–neck junction without impingement during maneuvers of 90° hip flexion, adduction with internal rotation, and abduction with external rotation beyond 30°.

If prominent anterior inferior iliac spine (AIIS) subspinal impingement was identified, the hyperplastic bone in this region was also contoured using a burr to fully expose the groove between the AIIS and the acetabular rim (Fig. 2d). The articular cartilage of the femoral head (Fig. 2e) and acetabulum, as well as the ligamentum teres (Fig. 2f), were carefully examined. Cartilage injuries were graded according to the Outerbridge classification [9]. Grade I–III lesions were treated with radiofrequency ablation, while Grade IV lesions were managed with the microfracture technique. Any damaged ligamentum teres tissue was debrided, and hyperplastic synovium was resected as necessary. Upon completion of all procedures, the arthroscopic instruments were removed. The capsule and skin incisions were closed routinely in layers.

Postoperative rehabilitation

A standardized, phased rehabilitation protocol was followed. On the first day after the operation, patients were permitted to ambulate with double crutches, bearing up to 25% of body weight on the operated limb, and could begin passive hip flexion to 90°. From weeks 1 to 4, patients progressively increased weight-bearing within tolerance while continuing to use crutches. Active hip flexion was limited to 90°, and external rotation and extension were restricted. Muscle strengthening exercises for the gluteus medius, lumbar, dorsal, and quadriceps muscles were initiated and gradually intensified.

Between weeks 5 and 12, patients transitioned to full weight-bearing walking and were encouraged to restore a normal range of motion. Rehabilitation during this phase focused on advancing the strength, stability, and endurance of the hip musculature, allowing a gradual return to daily activities such as jogging and stair climbing. After 12 weeks, patients could progressively commence sport-specific training.

NST group

Patients allocated to the NST group underwent a structured, evidence-based conservative management program called “Personalized Hip Therapy”. This protocol was developed through the Delphi method, nominal group technique, and consensus development sessions, and was grounded in current best practices for FAIS. The program emphasized enhancement of motor control, hip joint stability, and functional movement patterns. The core protocol consisted of the following standardized components, administered by qualified rehabilitation physicians specializing in musculoskeletal disorders.

Comprehensive initial assessment

Prior to treatment initiation, all patients underwent a detailed, standardized assessment conducted by a rehabilitation physician. This evaluation included assessment of hip range of motion (FABER test; Thomas test; flexion; internal rotation in prone and sitting positions), lumbar spine range of motion (in the quadruped position), gluteus medius muscle control (assessed through lateral backward movement), proprioception (evaluated via reverse lunge movement), and neuromuscular control of the lower limb [10].

Structured treatment delivery

Throughout the 6-month core treatment period, patients received a minimum of 4–6 supervised sessions per month (averaging one 60-min session per week). Each supervised session incorporated several core intervention components: patient education, joint mobility techniques, individualized therapeutic exercise program, movement pattern correction, and a structured home exercise regimen. Rehabilitation physicians tailored a progressive and function-oriented exercise plan for each patient based on their specific deficits and goals. This regimen focused on core stability training (e.g., planks, bird-dog variations), activation and strengthening of hip musculature (e.g., glute bridges, clamshells, side-lying abduction, controlled rotation exercises), motor control retraining (e.g., emphasizing pelvic stability during squats, lunges, step-ups), and neuromuscular re-education and proprioceptive training. During functional activities and exercises, guidance on optimizing posture and biomechanics was provided. Based on the supervised sessions, an individualized home exercise program was prescribed for daily practice, requiring approximately 20–30 min. This program included key exercises targeting the same domains as the supervised sessions (core, hip stability, motor control) and was progressively updated. Adherence was monitored through exercise logs reviewed during supervised sessions.

Pain management protocol

If pain (typically defined as a VAS score ≥ 5/10) significantly impeded the patient’s ability to participate in or progress through the core therapeutic exercise program for a sustained period (e.g., ≥ 2 weeks), a single ultrasound-guided intra-articular corticosteroid injection (e.g., 40 mg triamcinolone acetonide or equivalent mixed with 3–5 ml of 1% Lidocaine) was offered as an adjunctive therapy [11]. A maximum of one injection was permitted during the 6-month program.

Post-core period support

Following the intensive 6-month core program, patients were eligible for up to 2 additional consultation sessions with the rehabilitation physician within the subsequent 3 months. These sessions provided guidance on exercise progression, maintenance strategies, or addressing any new concerns.

Individualization

While the core components—including assessment domains, minimum session frequency, intervention categories, home exercise program requirement, and injection criteria—were standardized for all participants, the specific selection, intensity, and progression of exercises and techniques within each category were personalized by the rehabilitation physician. This individualization was based on continuous assessment of the patient’s impairments, functional limitations, and treatment response.

Follow-up and outcome measures

Patients were evaluated on an outpatient basis. Follow-up assessments were scheduled at 2 weeks, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, and 12 months after the initiation of treatment, and annually thereafter. Outcome measures, including the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) for pain, the modified Harris Hip Score (mHHS), the Non-Arthritic Hip Score (NAHS), and the Hip Outcome Score-Activities of Daily Living (HOS-ADL), were compared between the two groups at the following timepoints: pre-treatment (Pre), 1-month post-treatment (Post-1mo), 6-month post-treatment (Post-6mo), and the final follow-up (FFU). Complications were also recorded and analyzed.

The VAS was used to assess the intensity of hip pain, ranging from 0 (no pain) to 10 (the most severe pain imaginable) [12]. The mHHS, NAHS, and HOS-ADL were employed to evaluate the hip joint function [13–15]. The mHSS assesses domains of pain, function, deformity, and range of motion. It is scored out of a total of 100 points, with results categorized as follows: > 90 (excellent), 80–89 (good), 70–79 (fair), and < 70 (poor). The NAHS evaluates pain, symptoms, hip function, and the ability to participate in daily activities. It comprises 20 items, each scored from 0 to 4, yielding a raw total of 80 points that is conventionally converted to a 100-point scale. A higher score indicates better hip function. The HOS-ADL focuses on the hip joint’s capacity to perform various daily activities. It contains 17 items, with a total raw score of 68 points. The scoring rules are identical to those of the NAHS, and a higher score likewise denotes superior function.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corp.) and R 4.3.0 (utilizing the lme4, emmeans, and lmerTest packages). Continuous variables were assessed for normality with the Shapiro–Wilk test and visual inspection of Q–Q plots. Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), non-normally distributed data as median [interquartile range, IQR], and categorical data as counts (%). Baseline comparisons between groups for continuous variables were performed using independent t-tests (with Levene’s test for equality of variances) or the Mann–Whitney U tests, as appropriate. Categorical variables were compared using the χ2 or Fisher’s exact tests.

Longitudinal outcomes were analyzed using linear mixed-effects models for mHHS, NAHS, and HOS-ADL, and an ordered logistic mixed-effect model for VAS scores. The models incorporated fixed effects for treatment group, time point, and group × time interaction term, with a random intercept for each participant to account for within-subject correlations. Parameters were estimated via restricted maximum likelihood. An unstructured covariance matrix was selected based on the lowest Akaike Information Criterion value. Model assumptions were verified by visual inspection of residual Q–Q plots for normality and scale-location plots for homoscedasticity, alongside Levene’s test for homogeneity of variance. Additional diagnostics included convergence test, random effects distribution checks, and visual comparison of model predictions against observed data trends (Supplementary Figure S1-S5). Missing data, which constituted < 5% of observations at all timepoints, were handled using maximum likelihood estimation.

Effect sizes for parametric analyses are reported as Cohen’s d with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), interpreted as follows: |d|< 0.2 = trivial, 0.2 ≤|d|< 0.5 = small, 0.5 ≤|d|< 0.8 medium, |d|≥ 0.8 large [16, 17]). Sensitivity analyses were conducted to confirm the robustness of the findings via: (a) generalized estimating equations with exchangeable correlation structure; (b) comparison of maximum likelihood estimation with complete-case analysis to evaluate the impact of missing data; and (c) non-parametric bootstrapping with1000 resamples to derive bias-corrected CIs (Supplementary Table S1).

The primary analyses focused on between-group differences in raw scores. To contextualize these differences clinically, we calculated the proportions of patients achieving the minimum clinically important difference (MCID) and substantial clinical benefit (SCB). MCID thresholds were defined as follows: VAS decrease ≥ 2.0 points [18, 19], mHHS increase ≥ 9.5 points [20, 21], NAHS increase ≥ 8.5 points [22], HOS-ADL increase ≥ 9.8 points [20, 21]; SCB thresholds as follows: VAS decrease ≥ 2.5 points [23, 24], mHHS increase ≥ 19.8 points [25], NAHS increase ≥ 24.4 points [26], HOS-ADL increase ≥ 10.0 points [25]. Between-group differences in MCID/SCB achievement rates were tested via χ2 or Fisher’s exact tests, with results expressed as odds ratios (ORs). The number needed to treat (NNT) was calculated for statistically significant outcomes. A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant after adjusting for multiple comparisons.

Results

Patient baseline characteristics and intra-articular lesions

A total of 85 FAIS patients were enrolled in the final analysis, comprising 32 males and 53 females, with an age range of 18–60 years [mean (38.71 ± 11.44) years]. The cohort was divided into two groups: 51 patients underwent HAS, and 34 patients received NST. All patients were followed up for 15–27 months [mean (21.73 ± 3.61) months]. Comparisons of baseline demographic and clinical characteristics revealed no statistically significant differences between the HAS and NST groups in terms of the basic information, there was no significant difference in gender, age, body mass index (BMI), symptoms duration, or final follow-up time (P > 0. 05 for all), as detailed in Table 1.

The prevalence of key intra-articular lesions before treatment allocation is summarized in Table 2. No significant differences were observed between the two groups for labrum injury (76% vs. 68%), cartilage injury (31% vs. 26%), CAM deformity (71% vs. 47%), clamp deformity (8% vs. 9%), and round ligament injury (24% vs. 20%) (P > 0. 05 for all).

In the HAS group, transient complications included anterolateral thigh numbness in 3 cases (5.9%) and perineal numbness in 4 cases (7.8%). These symptoms resolved completely within 2 weeks following neurotrophic medication and symptomatic management. No physical therapy or exercise-related complications occurred in the NST group. Notably, no patients in the NST group crossed over to undergo HAS during the follow-up period.

Pain outcomes (VAS)

The probability distribution of VAS scores (Fig. 3a) demonstrated a shift toward lower scores over time in both groups, consistent with pain relief after treatment. No significant between-group difference was observed at baseline (Pre). At Post-1mo, the probability of higher pain scores was notably greater in the HAS group compared to the NST group. In contrast, at Post-6mo and FFU, the probability distribution shifted, with a higher likelihood of lower pain scores in the HAS group (Fig. 3b). The cumulative probability of achieving mild pain (VAS ≤ 3) increased more rapidly in the HAS group between Post-1mo and Post-6mo, resulting in a higher final proportion at FFU (85% vs. 50%) (Fig. 3c).

Model estimates confirmed no significant between-group difference at baseline (Table 3, Fig. 3d). At Post-1mo, VAS scores in the NST group were significantly lower than those in the HAS group [P < 0.05, Cohen’s d: 1.1 (0.6, 1.5)]. However, at Post-6mo and FFU, this pattern reversed, with the HAS group showing significantly lower VAS scores than the NST group [P < 0.05 for all, |Cohen’s d|> 0.8]. The group × time interaction indicated that the magnitude of improvement was greater in the NST group at Post-1mo [Cohen’s d: 0.9 (0.4, 1.5)], but greater in the HAS group at Post-6mo and FFU [p < 0.05, |Cohen’s d|> 2.6 for all].

Discussion

The primary finding of this retrospective cohort study is that both HAS and a structured NST program were associated with significant and clinically meaningful improvements in pain and hip function in patients with FAIS over a mean follow-up of 22 months. The trajectory of improvement, however, differed between the two treatment strategies. NST was associated with a more rapid initial improvement at 1 month follow-up, whereas HAS was associated with superior outcomes at the 6-month and final follow-up.

The efficacy of HAS observed in our study finds support in the existing literature. Prior studies have consistently reported significant short-to-mid-term improvements in patient-reported outcomes following HAS for FAIS [27–29]. Similar to the findings of Philippon et al. [30] and Byrd et al. [31], the present study documented substantial increases in functional scores (mHHS, NAHS, HOS-ADL) and high rates of MCID/SCB achievement in the HAS group. The low complication rate in our cohort, with transient nerve traction symptoms resolving promptly, further supports the existing evidence that HAS is a safe and effective intervention for FAIS when performed by experienced surgeons [32, 33].The mechanistic rationale for HAS likely involves the direct correction of morphological abnormalities (e.g., CAM deformity) and the repair of intra-articular soft-tissue lesions (e.g., labral tears), thereby restoring more normal hip biomechanics [27, 34].

Concurrently, our results contribute to the growing body of evidence indicating that a structured, personalized NST program can serve as an effective management strategy for a substantial proportion of FAIS patients. The significant improvements and high rates of clinically meaningful response in the NST group align with the findings of long-term follow-up studies [35] and programs incorporating intensive rehabilitation and injections [36]. The initial superiority of NST at the 1-month mark may be attributable to the effects of active rehabilitation and the avoidance of immediate post-surgical pain and inflammation. The theoretical basis for NST may lie in its ability to optimize dynamic hip stability and movement patterns, potentially mitigating the consequences of femoroacetabular impingement [37].

When comparing the two strategies, our findings of superior medium-term outcomes for HAS are consistent with several multicenter randomized trials [11, 38] and meta-analyses [8]. The convergence of these results suggests that the biomechanical correction afforded by surgery may provide an incremental benefit in specific functional domains over a structured rehabilitation program alone for certain patient profiles in the medium term. However, it is crucial to contextualize this finding. Other prospective studies, such as the one by Mansell et al. [39], reported no significant difference in outcomes between HAS and physical therapy at 2 years. This discrepancy may be related to differences in the NST protocols, patient selection, or crossover rates. The conclusion that HAS is superior is not universal, and the variability in NST effectiveness highlighted by Spencer-Gardner et al. [40] underscores the importance of protocol quality and adherence.

We acknowledge that our study has several limitations that must be considered when interpreting the results. First, the non-randomized, retrospective design is susceptible to selection bias, as the treatment allocation was based on shared decision-making rather than randomization. Although we observed no significant differences in measured baseline characteristics, the influence of unmeasured confounders cannot be ruled out. Second, the mean follow-up of approximately 22 months is sufficient for evaluating short-to-mid-term efficacy but is inadequate for assessing long-term outcomes, such as the progression of osteoarthritis or the need for subsequent hip arthroplasty. Future studies with follow-up durations of 5 years or longer are warranted to confirm the long-term superiority of either treatment strategy. The claim of medium-term benefit for HAS should be understood within this specific time frame, and longer-term studies are warranted. Finally, while the use of preoperative MRI for baseline comparison of lesions mitigated diagnostic bias, the gold-standard arthroscopic confirmation was only available for the HAS group, which may still influence the true prevalence of certain lesions.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the findings of this study suggest that both HAS and a structured NST program are viable and effective treatment options for FAIS, each with a distinct recovery trajectory. Within the limitations of a retrospective design and a medium-term follow-up, our data indicate that HAS may be associated with superior pain relief and functional outcomes at the 6-month to 2-year time horizon. These results can inform the shared decision-making process between clinicians and patients. Future large-scale, multicenter, prospective randomized controlled trials with long-term follow-up are essential to definitively establish the comparative efficacy of these treatment pathways and to identify the patient subgroups most likely to benefit from each approach.
