Stability and Concentration in Nonlinear Inverse Problems with Block-Structured Parameters: Lipschitz Geometry, Identifiability, and an Application to Gaussian Splatting
Joe-Mei Feng, Hsin-Hsiung Kao

Abstract
We develop an operator-theoretic framework for stability and statistical concentration in nonlinear inverse problems with block-structured parameters. Under a unified set of assumptions combining blockwise Lipschitz geometry, local identifiability, and sub-Gaussian noise, we establish deterministic stability inequalities, global Lipschitz bounds for least-squares misfit functionals, and nonasymptotic concentration estimates. These results yield high-probability parameter error bounds that are intrinsic to the forward operator and independent of any specific reconstruction algorithm. As a concrete instantiation, we verify that the Gaussian Splatting rendering operator satisfies the proposed assumptions and derive explicit constants governing its Lipschitz continuity and resolution-dependent observability. This leads to a fundamental stability–resolution tradeoff, showing that estimation error is inherently constrained by the ratio between image resolution and model complexity. Overall, the analysis characterizes operator-level limits for a broad class of high-dimensional nonlinear inverse problems arising in modern imaging and differentiable rendering.



Keywords:

nonlinear inverse problems, operator-theoretic stability, identifiability, Lipschitz geometry, concentration inequalities, block-structured models

1
Introduction

Gaussian Splatting (GS) has recently emerged as an effective representation for
novel-view synthesis and differentiable rendering, achieving high visual quality
with comparatively simple optimization pipelines.
Despite its empirical success, GS remains highly overparameterized and strongly
nonlinear.
This raises a fundamental and largely unexplored question:

What stability limits are imposed by the GS forward model itself, independent
of any particular reconstruction algorithm?

Most existing analyses of GS and related rendering models emphasize optimization
heuristics, empirical robustness, or architectural design.
While such studies are indispensable for practical performance, they do not
address a more basic issue: whether accurate reconstruction is possible in
principle, given the geometry of the rendering operator, the model complexity,
and the available observations.
This paper addresses this question from an operator-theoretic perspective.

Operator-theoretic viewpoint.

We model Gaussian Splatting as a nonlinear inverse problem

y=A(Z^{\star})+\eta,

where
Z^{\star}
denotes the unknown GS parameters,
A
is the rendering operator,
and
\eta
represents measurement noise.
Rather than analyzing a specific optimization scheme, we study the geometric and
stability properties of the forward operator
A
.
Our goal is to characterize reconstruction limits that hold uniformly across
estimators, independent of optimization strategy or initialization.

Main result and message.

Our central result establishes an intrinsic
stability–resolution tradeoff

for Gaussian Splatting.
Increasing image resolution strengthens local observability, while increasing
model complexity amplifies global sensitivity.
As a consequence, reconstruction accuracy cannot be improved arbitrarily by
densification or higher resolution alone: the achievable error is fundamentally
limited by the balance between these two effects.
This limitation is induced by the geometry of the forward operator and cannot be
removed by algorithmic refinements.

From general theory to GS-specific limits.

To formalize this statement, we develop a general stability framework for
nonlinear inverse problems with block-structured parameters.
Under mild assumptions—blockwise Lipschitz continuity, local identifiability,
and sub-Gaussian noise—we derive deterministic stability inequalities and
nonasymptotic high-probability error bounds.
We then verify that the Gaussian Splatting rendering operator satisfies these
assumptions and obtain explicit scaling laws that quantify how reconstruction
error depends on image resolution and the number of splats.

Sharpness and interpretability.

Two additional ingredients sharpen and clarify the analysis.
First, we employ tools from the theory of suprema of chaos processes to obtain
sharp statistical scaling for noise-induced error, replacing dimension-dependent
bounds by geometry-driven complexity measures.
Second, we introduce a notion of
directional observability
, which exposes
the anisotropic sensitivity of the GS forward operator.
This perspective explains why common heuristics such as densification, pruning,
or resolution increase may succeed in some regimes but necessarily fail in others.

Contributions.

The main contributions of this work are:

•

We formulate Gaussian Splatting as a block-structured nonlinear inverse
problem and establish operator-induced stability bounds.

•

We derive nonasymptotic, high-probability reconstruction error estimates
with sharp statistical scaling using chaos process techniques.

•

We identify an intrinsic stability–resolution tradeoff for GS and provide
an interpretable explanation via directional observability.

Organization.

Section
3
introduces the problem setup and assumptions.
Section
8
establishes deterministic stability results.
Section
6
develops concentration bounds via chaos process
theory.
Section
7
introduces directional observability and interprets
the resulting limits.
Finally, Section
7.1
verifies the assumptions for Gaussian
Splatting and derives explicit resolution–complexity scaling laws.

Why block structure matters.

Unlike classical inverse problems with homogeneous parameters, Gaussian Splatting
exhibits strong heterogeneity across parameter blocks: spatial locations,
covariances, colors, and opacities operate at fundamentally different scales and
sensitivities.
A single global Lipschitz constant therefore fails to capture the true geometry
of the GS forward operator, motivating the block-structured analysis developed in
this work.

Background and Related Work

The analysis of nonlinear inverse problems has a long and influential history,
with deep connections to regularization theory, statistical estimation, and
computational imaging.
This section situates the present work within three main research threads:
(i) classical deterministic inverse problems,
(ii) statistical and concentration-based analyses, and
(iii) modern differentiable rendering operators, including Gaussian Splatting.

Classical Theory of Inverse Problems

Foundational monographs such as
Engl–Hanke–Neubauer
[
4
]
,
Kirsch
[
8
]
,
and Bertero–Boccacci
[
2
]

established the core mathematical framework for nonlinear inverse problems:
ill-posedness, stability, well-posedness under structural conditions, and
regularization methods.
In imaging contexts, Natterer’s classical treatment of tomography

[
10
]
provides explicit examples where the forward
operator exhibits both smoothing effects and resolution limitations—phenomena
directly related to the Lipschitz and curvature structures analyzed in the
present work.

The notion that local curvature (or information content) governs stability is
likewise well documented in optical tomography and scattering-based inverse
problems; see Arridge
[
1
]
.
Our identifiability condition (A6) and the observability-type curvature
amplification (A7) abstract and extend this perspective to broad classes of
block-structured nonlinear operators.

Statistical Inverse Problems and Concentration

A second relevant line of work concerns statistical formulations of inverse
problems, where noise modeling and probabilistic stability are central.
Kaipio and Somersalo
[
6
]
develop Bayesian and
likelihood-based approaches in which posterior contraction and stability are
linked to the information geometry of the forward map.

In contrast, the present paper adopts a nonasymptotic, concentration-based
perspective: instead of relying on asymptotic Fisher-type arguments, we
establish fully nonlinear, finite-sample concentration inequalities for the
misfit (Lemma
4
).
The tools used here build upon general concentration inequalities for
sub-Gaussian and sub-exponential quantities as developed in
Boucheron–Lugosi–Massart
[
3
]
and
Vershynin
[
11
]
, and are adapted to the nonlinear
structure of the misfit

F(Z)=\|A(Z)-y_{\mathrm{obs}}\|^{2}
, in which deviation bounds depend explicitly
on geometric Lipschitz constants tied to the forward operator.

Related in spirit is the classical noise–resolution tradeoff in CT imaging,
for example the study of penalized minimization and reconstruction limits in
Evans et al.
[
5
]
.
The tradeoff theorem in this work (Theorem
5
) may be viewed as
a nonlinear, operator-theoretic counterpart that applies to more general
rendering operators.

Differentiable Rendering and Gaussian Splatting

Recent advances in differentiable rendering—particularly
NeRF
[
9
]

and Gaussian Splatting (GS)
[
7
]
—have produced powerful,
high-resolution reconstruction pipelines for 3D scenes.
Although NeRF and GS are usually studied from an optimization or graphics
perspective, their forward operators can be interpreted within the
block-structured inverse problem formulation adopted here.

The GS renderer, in particular, inherits early ideas of Gaussian splatting from
Westover’s volume rendering work
[
12
]
, but modern
variants are fully differentiable, making them suitable for rigorous operator
analysis.

The present work proves that the GS forward operator satisfies all assumptions
(A1)–(A7), including Lipschitz continuity, bounded curvature, and local strong
observability (Proposition
4
).
Moreover, Corollary
2
provides explicit operator-level
constants that quantify how GS resolution interacts with sensitivity and noise.

Position of the Present Work

In contrast to previous analyses focusing on either deterministic stability or
statistical consistency, the present paper integrates:

•

the Lipschitz geometry of the forward map (Sections
4
),

•

identifiability and curvature amplification (Sections
5
),

•

nonlinear concentration for the misfit (Section
6
),

•

high-probability parameter error bounds (Section
6.3
),

•

and a stability–resolution lower bound relevant for GS
(Section
8
).

Together these components yield a theory that is both operator-level (via
Lipschitz and curvature analysis) and statistical (via concentration).
This dual perspective appears essential for understanding the reconstruction
capabilities and intrinsic limitations of modern differentiable rendering
methods such as Gaussian Splatting.

3
Problem Formulation and Global Assumptions

This section introduces the mathematical formulation of the inverse problem,
including the parameterization of the unknowns, the structure of the forward
operator, and the observation model.
These components define the geometric structure underlying all subsequent
stability and concentration results.

Throughout the paper,
\mathcal{Y}
denotes a Hilbert space of observations
endowed with norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{Y}}
, and
\mathcal{Z}
denotes a compact
parameter domain equipped with the product Euclidean metric
d_{2}
.

3.1
Parameterization and Forward Model

We consider an unknown parameter vector

Z=(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{N})\in\mathcal{Z},

where each block

Z_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}

represents a distinct subset of model parameters.
The block structure reflects the composite nature of the model, such as spatial
location, anisotropy, color, or opacity in Gaussian Splatting.
The total parameter dimension is
d=\sum_{i=1}^{N}d_{i}
.

The data are generated by a nonlinear forward operator

A:\mathcal{Z}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y},

which maps parameters to observable quantities.
We adopt the observation model

y_{\mathrm{obs}}=A(Z^{\star})+\eta,

(1)

where
Z^{\star}\in\mathcal{Z}
is the unknown ground-truth parameter and

\eta
denotes additive noise.

The reconstruction task is to recover
Z^{\star}
from
y_{\mathrm{obs}}
.
A natural estimator is obtained by minimizing the least-squares misfit

F(Z):=\|A(Z)-y_{\mathrm{obs}}\|_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2},

(2)

which is well-defined on
\mathcal{Z}
.

The functional
F
encapsulates the inverse problem.
Its regularity is governed by the Lipschitz structure of
A
, while its local
behavior near
Z^{\star}
determines identifiability, stability, and achievable
statistical accuracy.

All subsequent results are developed under a set of global assumptions
(A1)–(A7), which formalize the regularity of the forward map, the geometry of
the parameter domain, and the statistical properties of the noise.
For readability, only the essential assumptions are stated in the main text;
additional remarks and technical clarifications are deferred to
Appendix
A
.
These assumptions are verified for Gaussian Splatting in
Section
7
.

3.2
Noise Model and Observation Space

The observation space
\mathcal{Y}
is a real, separable Hilbert space endowed
with inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{Y}}
and norm

\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{Y}}
.
This setting covers common imaging scenarios, including finite-dimensional
pixel spaces and
L^{2}
-type image spaces.
For Gaussian Splatting, we denote by
M
the number of rendered pixel locations,
up to a fixed color dimension (e.g., RGB channels).

We assume an additive noise model

y_{\mathrm{obs}}=A(Z^{\star})+\eta,

where
\eta
is a centered random element in
\mathcal{Y}
.
Throughout the paper,
\eta
is assumed to be sub-Gaussian with variance proxy

\sigma^{2}
, which characterizes the distribution-level noise scale and enables
uniform concentration control for linear functionals of the noise.

We define the
realized noise energy

\varepsilon_{\eta}:=\|\eta\|_{\mathcal{Y}}.

Evaluating the misfit functional at the true parameter yields

F(Z^{\star})=\varepsilon_{\eta}^{2},

which acts as the realization-level noise floor in both deterministic stability
analysis (Section
5
) and statistical concentration results
(Section
6
).
For deterministic estimates, we additionally allow a realization-level bound

B_{\eta}>0
such that
F(Z^{\star})=\|\eta\|_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}\leq B_{\eta}^{2}
.
All technical details of the noise assumption are collected in
Appendix
C
.

3.3
Global Assumptions

Remark 1

(Identifiability vs. Observability)
.

Assumption (A6) formulates local identifiability at the level of the misfit
functional and serves as a minimal requirement for deterministic stability.
For Gaussian Splatting, this condition follows from the stronger,
resolution-dependent observability property in Assumption (A7), verified
explicitly in Section
7
.

The analysis relies on a set of structural assumptions on the forward operator

A
, the noise distribution, and the parameter domain, which isolate geometric
and statistical properties independent of any particular reconstruction
algorithm.

Assumption 1

(Global Assumptions)
.

We assume the following:

(A1)

Centered sub-Gaussian noise.

The observational noise
\eta
is centered and sub-Gaussian in
\mathcal{Y}

with variance proxy
\sigma^{2}
.

(A2)

Compact block-structured parameter domain.

The parameter space
\mathcal{Z}
is compact and admits a block decomposition

Z=(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{N})
, equipped with the product Euclidean metric
d_{2}
.

(A3)

Bounded forward output.

There exists a constant
B>0
such that

\|A(Z)\|_{\mathcal{Y}}\leq B,\qquad\forall\,Z\in\mathcal{Z}.

(A4)

Finite noise floor.

The misfit evaluated at the true parameter satisfies

F(Z^{\star})=\varepsilon_{\eta}^{2}<\infty

almost surely.

(A5)

Blockwise Lipschitz continuity.

There exist constants
G_{1},\dots,G_{N}>0
such that for all
Z,Z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}
,

\|A(Z)-A(Z^{\prime})\|_{\mathcal{Y}}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N}G_{i}\,\|Z_{i}-Z_{i}^{\prime}\|.

We denote
G:=\max_{i}G_{i}
.

(A6)

Local identifiability.

The ground-truth parameter
Z^{\star}
is locally identifiable in the sense of
Definition
1
, with constants
\kappa>0
and
r_{0}>0
.

(A7)

Resolution-dependent local observability.

There exists an effective constant

\lambda_{\min}^{\mathrm{eff}}>0
such that for all

Z
with
d_{2}(Z,Z^{\star})\leq r_{0}
,

\|A(Z)-A(Z^{\star})\|_{\mathcal{Y}}\geq\lambda_{\min}^{\mathrm{eff}}\,\sqrt{M}\,d_{2}(Z,Z^{\star}),

where
M
denotes the number of observation degrees of freedom
(e.g., pixels in an image).

Remark 2

(Resolution scaling)
.

The constant
\lambda_{\min}^{\mathrm{eff}}
in Assumption (A7) is independent of
the image resolution
M
.
The factor
\sqrt{M}
arises from aggregation of measurement information and does
not affect the operator-level constant itself.

3.4
Notation Summary

Noise and concentration.

•

\eta
: observational noise.

•

\sigma^{2}
: sub-Gaussian variance proxy (distribution-level).

•

\varepsilon_{\eta}:=\|\eta\|_{\mathcal{Y}}
: realized noise energy.

Identifiability and observability.

•

\kappa
: local identifiability constant.

•

r_{0}
: identifiability radius.

•

\lambda_{\min}^{\mathrm{eff}}
: effective observability constant.

Metric convention.

The metric
d_{2}(\cdot,\cdot)
is interpreted as a root-mean-square distance over
parameter blocks rather than a worst-case coordinate-wise metric.
Norms of the form
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{Y}}
act exclusively in the observation
space
\mathcal{Y}
.

4
Geometric Sensitivity and Blockwise Lipschitz Structure

This section quantifies how perturbations in individual parameter blocks affect
the forward operator
A
and the induced misfit functional.
Since the parameter space
\mathcal{Z}
admits a block decomposition

Z=(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{N})
, it is natural to analyze sensitivity at the level of single
blocks.
Assumption
1
(A5) formalizes this structure via blockwise
Lipschitz continuity, which provides the main geometric input for the stability
and concentration results developed later.

No differentiability of
A
is required for the results below.
All statements therefore apply to a broad class of nonlinear imaging and
rendering operators, including the Gaussian Splatting forward map analyzed in
Section
7
.

4.1
Coordinatewise Operator Sensitivity

We begin by isolating the effect of perturbing a single parameter block.

Lemma 1

(Coordinatewise Operator Lipschitz)
.

Under Assumptions
1
(A2)–(A5)
, for each block index

i\in\{1,\dots,N\}
there exists a constant
G_{i}>0
such that for all

Z,Z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}
that differ only in the
i
-th block,

\|A(Z)-A(Z^{\prime})\|_{\mathcal{Y}}\;\leq\;G_{i}\,\|Z_{i}-Z_{i}^{\prime}\|.

Lemma
1
shows that the sensitivity of the forward operator
with respect to each block
Z_{i}
is at most linear, with slope
G_{i}
.
Each block therefore contributes independently to the variation of the forward
output.
This decomposition will be used repeatedly to derive Lipschitz bounds for the
misfit functional.

The proof follows immediately from
Assumption
1
(A5) and is deferred to
Appendix
B.1
.

4.2
Coordinatewise Misfit Lipschitz

We next transfer the blockwise Lipschitz continuity of the forward operator to
the least-squares misfit functional

F(Z)=\|A(Z)-y_{\mathrm{obs}}\|_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}.

Although
F
is quadratic in the residual, its variation with respect to a
single parameter block inherits the Lipschitz structure of
A
via a simple
polarization argument.

Lemma 2

(Coordinatewise Misfit Lipschitz)
.

Suppose Assumptions
1
(A1)–(A5)
hold.
Let
G_{i}
denote the blockwise Lipschitz constants from
Lemma
1
.
Then for any
Z,Z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}
that differ only in the
i
-th block,

|F(Z)-F(Z^{\prime})|\;\leq\;2B\,G_{i}\,\|Z_{i}-Z_{i}^{\prime}\|.

Lemma
2
shows that perturbations in a single parameter block
affect the misfit by at most a linear amount proportional to the corresponding
blockwise Lipschitz constant.
Thus, the blockwise geometric structure of the forward operator carries over
directly to the misfit functional.

The proof is a direct consequence of the polarization identity

\|u\|_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}-\|v\|_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}=\langle u+v,\,u-v\rangle_{\mathcal{Y}},

together with the boundedness of the forward output from
Assumption
1
(A3)
and
Lemma
1
, and is omitted.

4.3
Global Misfit Lipschitz Constant

We now aggregate the coordinatewise misfit bounds across all parameter blocks.
This yields a global Lipschitz estimate for the misfit functional with respect
to the product Euclidean metric
d_{2}
.

Theorem 1

(Global Misfit Lipschitz)
.

Suppose Assumptions
1
(A1)–(A5)
hold.
Let
G=\max_{i}G_{i}
be the global blockwise Lipschitz constant and let
B
be the
forward-output bound from Assumption
1
(A3)
.
Then the misfit functional
F
is globally Lipschitz with respect to
d_{2}
:

|F(Z)-F(Z^{\prime})|\;\leq\;L\,d_{2}(Z,Z^{\prime}),\qquad L:=2BG\sqrt{N},

for all
Z,Z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}
.

Theorem
1
quantifies the worst-case sensitivity of the
misfit functional to perturbations in the parameter vector.
The factor
\sqrt{N}
reflects the accumulation of blockwise variations under
the product metric and is intrinsic to the block-structured geometry of the
parameter space.
This estimate will serve as a key input for the deterministic stability
inequality in Section
5
and the concentration analysis in
Section
6
.

The proof relies on a standard telescoping argument and is omitted.

5
Local Identifiability and Deterministic Stability

Having established global Lipschitz regularity of the misfit functional, we now
introduce a local curvature condition that ensures stability of the inverse
problem near the ground-truth parameter.
This condition formalizes the ability to distinguish
Z^{\star}
from nearby
parameters based on the behavior of the misfit functional.

5.1
Local Identifiability

Definition 1

(Local Identifiability)
.

The true parameter
Z^{\star}
is said to be
locally identifiable
if there
exist constants
\kappa>0
and
r_{0}>0
such that

F(Z)-F(Z^{\star})\;\geq\;\kappa^{2}\,d_{2}(Z,Z^{\star})^{2},\qquad\text{for all }d_{2}(Z,Z^{\star})\leq r_{0},

(3)

where
d_{2}
denotes the product Euclidean metric on
\mathcal{Z}
.

The constant
\kappa
quantifies the strength of local curvature of the misfit
functional around
Z^{\star}
, while
r_{0}
specifies the neighborhood in which
this curvature is guaranteed.
No assumption is imposed outside this region.

Assumption
1
(A6) asserts that
(
3
) holds.
Combined with the global Lipschitz regularity established in
Section
4
, this condition leads directly to deterministic
stability estimates.

5.2
Deterministic Stability Inequality

We now show that local identifiability implies a quantitative relationship
between misfit values and parameter error within the neighborhood

d_{2}(Z,Z^{\star})\leq r_{0}
.

Theorem 2

(Deterministic Stability)
.

Suppose Assumptions
1
(A1)–(A6)
hold and that

Z^{\star}
is locally identifiable in the sense of
Definition
1
.
Then for all
Z\in\mathcal{Z}
satisfying
d_{2}(Z,Z^{\star})\leq r_{0}
,

d_{2}(Z,Z^{\star})\;\leq\;\frac{1}{\kappa}\left(\varepsilon_{\eta}+\sqrt{\,F(Z)-F(Z^{\star})+\varepsilon_{\eta}^{2}\,}\right).

(4)

Inequality (
4
) shows that, within the identifiability
neighborhood, a small misfit gap enforces proximity to the ground truth.
The term
\varepsilon_{\eta}
represents an unavoidable noise floor, while the
remaining term captures model mismatch.
The bound is deterministic and independent of any reconstruction algorithm.

The proof follows directly from
Definition
1
and the identity

F(Z^{\star})=\varepsilon_{\eta}^{2}
.

5.3
Deterministic Error Bound

We next combine the deterministic stability inequality with the global
Lipschitz regularity of the misfit functional to obtain an explicit error
envelope.

Corollary 1

(Deterministic Error Bound)
.

Suppose the hypotheses of Theorem
2
hold and that the
misfit functional
F
satisfies the global Lipschitz bound of
Theorem
1
with constant
L=2BG\sqrt{N}
.
Then for all
Z\in\mathcal{Z}
satisfying
d_{2}(Z,Z^{\star})\leq r_{0}
,

d_{2}(Z,Z^{\star})\;\leq\;\frac{1}{\kappa}\left(\varepsilon_{\eta}+\sqrt{\,L\,d_{2}(Z,Z^{\star})+\varepsilon_{\eta}^{2}\,}\right).

(5)

Proof.

By Theorem
1
,

|F(Z)-F(Z^{\star})|\leq L\,d_{2}(Z,Z^{\star}),\qquad L=2BG\sqrt{N}.

Since
F(Z)-F(Z^{\star})\geq 0
for
d_{2}(Z,Z^{\star})\leq r_{0}
, the claim follows
by substitution into (
4
).
∎

The bound (
5
) characterizes a deterministic envelope for the
reconstruction error.
It serves as a precursor to the high-probability bounds developed in
Section
6
.

5.4
Interpretation and Use of the Error Bounds

The results above are stated at the operator level but admit direct
interpretation in practical settings.
In differentiable rendering systems, local linearizations obtained via
automatic differentiation are consistent with the blockwise Lipschitz and
identifiability assumptions.

The deterministic and probabilistic bounds derived in this work provide
computable estimates of reconstruction accuracy once the misfit value and noise
scale are specified.
For Gaussian Splatting, all constants become explicit, enabling fully
quantitative assessment without reliance on empirical tuning.

6
Statistical Moment Bounds and Concentration

This section develops probabilistic bounds for the data misfit

F(Z)=\|A(Z)-y_{\mathrm{obs}}\|_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2},

which complement the deterministic stability results of
Section
5
.
Expectations are taken with respect to the noise
\eta
, while
realization-level quantities such as

\varepsilon_{\eta}=\|\eta\|_{\mathcal{Y}}
are treated as fixed when invoking
deterministic estimates.

We first show that the expected misfit gap

F(Z)-F(Z^{\star})
coincides exactly with the squared forward-model discrepancy.
This identity links statistical error directly to the geometry of the forward
operator.
We then establish high-probability concentration inequalities that quantify
random fluctuations of the misfit around its mean.

6.1
Misfit Moment Bound

Lemma 3

(Misfit Moment Bound)
.

Let Assumptions
1
(A1)–(A5)
hold.
Then for every
Z\in\mathcal{Z}
,

\mathbb{E}\bigl[F(Z)-F(Z^{\star})\bigr]=\|A(Z)-A(Z^{\star})\|_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}\;\leq\;G^{2}N\,d_{2}(Z,Z^{\star})^{2},

(6)

where
G=\max_{i}G_{i}
denotes the global blockwise Lipschitz constant.

Lemma
3
shows that the expectation of the misfit gap is entirely
determined by the forward-model error.
Consequently, the expected behavior of
F
inherits the same blockwise geometric
structure as the forward operator.
This identity provides the starting point for the concentration analysis that
follows.

The proof is elementary and is deferred to
Appendix
N
.

6.2
Misfit Concentration Inequality

We now control random fluctuations of the misfit functional around its mean.
Under sub-Gaussian noise and blockwise Lipschitz continuity of the forward
operator, deviations of
F(Z)
are governed by the geometric sensitivity

d_{2}(Z,Z^{\star})
and the global blockwise Lipschitz constant

G=\max_{i}G_{i}
.

Lemma 4

(Misfit Concentration)
.

Let Assumptions
1
(A1)–(A6)
hold.
Then for any
Z\in\mathcal{Z}
and any
t>0
,

\mathbb{P}\!\left(F(Z)-\mathbb{E}[F(Z)]\geq t\right)\;\leq\;2\exp\!\left(-\min\!\left(\frac{t}{8\sigma^{2}},\frac{t^{2}}{32\sigma^{2}G^{2}N\,d_{2}(Z,Z^{\star})^{2}}\right)\right),

(7)

where
G=\max_{i}G_{i}
.

Lemma
4
exhibits two distinct fluctuation regimes.
Small deviations are controlled by the noise variance
\sigma^{2}
, while larger
deviations are governed by the geometric sensitivity

G\sqrt{N}\,d_{2}(Z,Z^{\star})
of the forward operator.
This structure reflects the interaction between noise and nonlinear geometry
in least-squares inverse problems.

The proof is technical and is deferred to
Appendix
O
.

6.3
High-Probability Parameter Error Bounds

We now combine the deterministic stability inequality of
Section
5.2
with the concentration estimate of
Lemma
4
to obtain nonasymptotic, high-probability bounds
on the parameter error.

Theorem 3

(High-Probability Parameter Error)
.

Suppose Assumptions
1
(A1)–(A6)
hold.
Then for any
Z\in\mathcal{Z}
satisfying
d_{2}(Z,Z^{\star})\leq r_{0}
and for any

t>0
,

\mathbb{P}\!\left(d_{2}(Z,Z^{\star})\;\leq\;\frac{1}{\kappa}\left(\varepsilon_{\eta}+\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[F(Z)]-F(Z^{\star})+t+\varepsilon_{\eta}^{2}}\right)\right)\;\geq\;1-\exp\!\left(-\frac{t}{4G^{2}N\,d_{2}(Z,Z^{\star})^{2}+4\sigma^{2}}\right),

(8)

where
\kappa
is the identifiability constant from
Definition
1
and

G=\max_{i}G_{i}
.

Theorem
3
provides a direct probabilistic counterpart to the

7.1
Verification of (A1)–(A7) for Gaussian Splatting

We consider a collection of
N
Gaussian primitives,

Z=(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{N}),\qquad Z_{i}=(x_{i},\Sigma_{i},c_{i},\alpha_{i}),

\Sigma_{i}

to an image by evaluating
weighted Gaussian kernels at each pixel location
p\in\Omega

A_{\mathrm{GS}}(Z)(p)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_{i}\,c_{i}\,\exp\!\Bigl(-\tfrac{1}{2}(p-\Pi(x_{i}))^{\top}\Sigma_{i}^{-1}(p-\Pi(x_{i}))\Bigr),

(9)

All admissible primitive parameters are assumed to lie in compact sets, and the
eigenvalues of each
\Sigma_{i}
are uniformly bounded away from zero and infinity.

Proposition 4

(Structural Verification for Gaussian Splatting)
.

Let
A_{\mathrm{GS}}
be the Gaussian Splatting operator defined in
(
9
).
Under the structural conditions above, the forward map

Assumptions
1
(A1)–(A7)
.
In particular:

(A1)

the noise model is compatible with centered sub-Gaussian noise on

\mathcal{Y}
;

(A2)

the parameter domain is compact and block-structured;

(A3)

(A4)

the noise energy satisfies

(A5)

the forward operator is blockwise Lipschitz with respect to each

(A6)

(A7)

The verification relies on three basic properties of Gaussian Splatting:
smooth dependence of

on the primitive parameters,

8
Stability–Resolution Tradeoff

We now arrive at the central result of this work.
This section quantifies an intrinsic limitation on the stability of
Gaussian-Splatting-based inverse problems, induced by the interaction between
image resolution and model complexity.
The tradeoff formalized below synthesizes the geometric, deterministic, and
statistical analyses developed in the preceding sections.

8.1
Scaling Laws for Observability and Sensitivity

Two quantities govern how stability depends on resolution.
The first is the local observability constant associated with
Assumption
1
(A7)
, which strengthens with the number of
image pixels.
The second is the global Lipschitz constant of the misfit functional associated
with Assumption
1
(A5)
, which grows with the number of
rendering primitives.

Local observability scaling.

Assumption
(A7)
asserts the existence of an effective constant

\lambda_{\min}^{\mathrm{eff}}>0
such that, for all
Z
sufficiently close to

Z^{\star}
,

\|A(Z)-A(Z^{\star})\|_{\mathcal{Y}}\;\geq\;\lambda_{\min}^{\mathrm{eff}}\,\sqrt{M}\,d_{2}(Z,Z^{\star}).

(12)

The factor
\sqrt{M}
reflects aggregation of independent pixel-level
constraints as resolution increases, while

\lambda_{\min}^{\mathrm{eff}}
depends only on the local geometry of the forward
operator.

Global Lipschitz scaling.

By contrast, the global sensitivity of the misfit functional

F(Z)=\|A(Z)-y_{\mathrm{obs}}\|_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}

is controlled by the Lipschitz constant

|F(Z)-F(Z^{\prime})|\;\leq\;L\,d_{2}(Z,Z^{\prime}),\qquad L=2BG\sqrt{N},

as established in Theorem
1
.
Here
G=\max_{i}G_{i}
is the blockwise Lipschitz constant and
B
is the
forward-output bound from Assumption
1
(A3).
The factor
\sqrt{N}
reflects accumulation of sensitivities across primitives
and is independent of image resolution.

Competing effects.

Stability is governed by the ratio between these two effects:
local curvature strengthens as
\sqrt{M}
, while global sensitivity grows as

\sqrt{N}
.
Increasing resolution without a commensurate increase in model complexity
improves stability, whereas rapid growth of
N
amplifies sensitivity and
degrades robustness.

8.2
Main Tradeoff Theorem

We now combine the observability scaling of
Assumption
1
(A7)
with the global Lipschitz structure of
Assumption
1
(A5)
, together with the deterministic and
statistical bounds developed in
Sections
5
–
6
.
This yields an intrinsic lower bound on stability as a function of image
resolution and model complexity.

The tradeoff derived below is intrinsic to the geometry of the forward operator.
It does not depend on the choice of estimator, regularization strategy, or
optimization algorithm.
Realization-level quantities such as the noise energy

\varepsilon_{\eta}=\|\eta\|_{\mathcal{Y}}
enter only instance-wise or
high-probability bounds and do not affect the structural limitation.

Since
d_{2}
is a root-mean-square metric over parameter blocks, the bound should
be interpreted as an average per-block stability limit rather than a worst-case
coordinate-wise estimate.

Theorem 5

(Stability–Resolution Tradeoff)
.

Let Assumptions
1
(A1)–(A7)
hold, and let

\widehat{Z}\in\mathcal{Z}
be any estimator satisfying

d_{2}(\widehat{Z},Z^{\star})\leq r_{0}
.
Then there exists a constant
C_{\mathrm{stab}}>0
, depending only on

\lambda_{\min}^{\mathrm{eff}},\kappa,B,G
, and the noise level, such that

d_{2}(\widehat{Z},Z^{\star})\;\geq\;C_{\mathrm{stab}}\,\sqrt{\frac{M}{N}}.

(13)

Theorem
5
shows that image resolution and model complexity impose
fundamentally competing constraints on stability.
Increasing resolution strengthens local observability and lowers the intrinsic
error floor, while increasing model complexity amplifies global sensitivity and
raises this floor.
The constant
C_{\mathrm{stab}}
depends only on operator-level geometric
quantities and the model-level noise scale
\sigma^{2}
.
Realization-dependent quantities affect only instance-wise or
high-probability error bounds and do not alter the intrinsic
stability–resolution tradeoff.

9
Discussion

9.1
Interpretation of the Lipschitz Geometry and GS Tradeoffs

The results of this work admit a unified geometric interpretation that clarifies
both the power and the limitations of Gaussian Splatting (GS) as an inverse
problem.
Taken together, the Lipschitz, identifiability, concentration, and tradeoff
results do not merely provide bounds; they describe how the GS parameter space
is embedded into the image space, and why resolution and stability cannot be
improved independently.

At a local level, the observability condition in Assumption
(A7)
shows
that the GS forward operator behaves as a well-conditioned local embedding of
the parameter space into the observation space.
As image resolution increases, independent pixel-level constraints accumulate,
and the curvature of the misfit functional strengthens proportionally to

\sqrt{M}
.
Within the identifiability neighborhood of
Z^{\star}
, this manifests as a
quadratic growth of the misfit with respect to the root-mean-square metric

d_{2}
, ensuring that small parameter perturbations produce detectable changes
in the rendered image.

At a global level, the Lipschitz geometry of the forward map imposes an opposing
constraint.
The blockwise constants
G_{i}
quantify how perturbations of individual
primitives propagate through the rendering process, while the global constant

G=\max_{i}G_{i}
captures the worst-case sensitivity across all blocks.
As shown in Theorem
1
, the misfit functional cannot be more
stable, in a Lipschitz sense, than order
2BG\sqrt{N}
with respect to the
natural product metric on the parameter space.
This growth is independent of image resolution and reflects the accumulation of
high-sensitivity directions as the number of primitives increases.

Gaussian Splatting makes this competition particularly transparent.
Each primitive contributes a localized Gaussian kernel whose influence decays
with distance, and the explicit constants in
Corollary
2
decompose global sensitivity into contributions
from positions, covariances, colors, and opacities.
Increasing the number of primitives
N
expands the expressive capacity of the
model and enables reconstruction of fine-scale structure, but it also increases
the dimensionality of directions along which the forward map can vary rapidly.
The stability–resolution tradeoff of Theorem
5
formalizes this
tension: the improvement in local conditioning proportional to
\sqrt{M}
is
necessarily offset by the growth in global sensitivity proportional to

\sqrt{N}
, yielding an intrinsic error floor of order
\sqrt{M/N}
.

From the perspective of inverse problems, this picture aligns with classical
stability–resolution phenomena in PDE-based imaging and tomography, but it
emerges here in a data-driven rendering setting without explicit discretization
parameters.
The constants
(\lambda_{\min}^{\mathrm{eff}},\kappa,B,G,\sigma)
summarize the
geometric and statistical structure imposed by the GS forward model and the
noise distribution.
None of these quantities can be tuned independently once the model class and
data fidelity are fixed, underscoring that the tradeoff is not an artifact of
analysis but a structural property of the representation.

These insights also clarify the implications for model design.
Reducing the number of primitives
N
can improve stability at fixed resolution
by limiting global sensitivity, while aggressively increasing
N
may sacrifice
robustness despite improved approximation capability.
Conversely, increasing resolution
M
enhances local observability but cannot,
by itself, overcome the global Lipschitz constraint.
Effective GS-based inverse problems should therefore be understood through the
joint scaling of
(M,N)
, rather than through resolution or model complexity in
isolation.

Appendix I
Proof of the Misfit Concentration Inequality

Proof of Lemma
4
.

Recall the decomposition

F(Z)-F(Z^{\star})=\|A(Z)-A(Z^{\star})\|_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}-2\langle A(Z)-A(Z^{\star}),\eta\rangle_{\mathcal{Y}},

which implies

F(Z)-\mathbb{E}[F(Z)]=-2\langle A(Z)-A(Z^{\star}),\eta\rangle_{\mathcal{Y}}+\bigl(\varepsilon_{\eta}^{2}-\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{\eta}^{2}]\bigr).

Linear term.

By Assumption
(A1)
, the noise
\eta
is sub-Gaussian in
\mathcal{Y}
with
variance proxy
\sigma^{2}
.
Hence the scalar random variable

\langle A(Z)-A(Z^{\star}),\eta\rangle_{\mathcal{Y}}
is sub-Gaussian with variance

\sigma^{2}\|A(Z)-A(Z^{\star})\|_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}
.
Rescaling by a factor of
2
yields

\mathbb{P}\!\left(-2\langle A(Z)-A(Z^{\star}),\eta\rangle_{\mathcal{Y}}\geq t\right)\leq 2\exp\!\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{32\sigma^{2}\|A(Z)-A(Z^{\star})\|_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}}\right).

(A5)

\|A(Z)-A(Z^{\star})\|_{\mathcal{Y}}\leq G\sqrt{N}\,d_{2}(Z,Z^{\star}),

we obtain

\mathbb{P}\!\left(-2\langle A(Z)-A(Z^{\star}),\eta\rangle_{\mathcal{Y}}\geq t\right)\leq 2\exp\!\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{32\sigma^{2}G^{2}N\,d_{2}(Z,Z^{\star})^{2}}\right).

Under the same sub-Gaussian assumption, the centered quadratic fluctuation

\varepsilon_{\eta}^{2}-\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{\eta}^{2}]

is sub-exponential.
Standard Bernstein-type inequalities therefore yield

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\varepsilon_{\eta}^{2}-\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{\eta}^{2}]\geq t\right)\leq 2\exp\!\left(-\frac{t}{8\sigma^{2}}\right).

Applying a union bound to the two terms above and absorbing absolute numerical

(

K.1
Jacobian and Curvature Intuition

Let
F(Z)=\|A(Z)-y_{\mathrm{obs}}\|_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}
and suppose, purely for
intuition, that the forward operator
A
is locally differentiable at

Z^{\star}

Formally, the Hessian of the misfit may be approximated by

\nabla^{2}F(Z^{\star})\;\approx\;2\,J^{\top}J,

where
J
denotes the Jacobian of
A
evaluated at
Z^{\star}
.

Each row of
J
corresponds to the sensitivity of a single pixel with respect
to the parameters.
As the number of pixels
M
increases, additional rows are added to
J
,
which—under mild incoherence assumptions—leads to growth of the spectrum of

J^{\top}J

This heuristic picture is consistent with the appearance of a

\sqrt{M}

(
12

Assumption
(A7)

No differentiability or Jacobian structure is assumed or required in the

All stability and tradeoff results in the main text follow directly from the

Assumption
(A7)
indicates that increasing the image resolution

The tradeoff bound (

Appendix M
Proof of Corollary 5.3 (Deterministic Error Bound)

Proof.

The result follows by combining the global Lipschitz regularity of the misfit
with the deterministic stability inequality.

By Theorem
1
, the misfit functional satisfies the global
bound

|F(Z)-F(Z^{\star})|\;\leq\;L\,d_{2}(Z,Z^{\star}),\qquad L=2BG\sqrt{N}.

Since
Z^{\star}
minimizes the misfit locally and
F
is continuous, we have

F(Z)\geq F(Z^{\star})
for all
Z
in a sufficiently small neighborhood of

Z^{\star}
, and therefore

F(Z)-F(Z^{\star})\;\leq\;L\,d_{2}(Z,Z^{\star}).

For all
Z
satisfying
d_{2}(Z,Z^{\star})\leq r_{0}
, Theorem
2

yields the deterministic stability estimate

d_{2}(Z,Z^{\star})\;\leq\;\frac{1}{\kappa}\left(\|\eta\|_{\mathcal{Y}}+\sqrt{\,F(Z)-F(Z^{\star})+\|\eta\|_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}\,}\right).

Substituting the above bound on the misfit difference gives

\sqrt{\,F(Z)-F(Z^{\star})+\|\eta\|_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}\,}\;\leq\;\sqrt{\,L\,d_{2}(Z,Z^{\star})+\|\eta\|_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}\,}.

Combining these inequalities, we obtain

d_{2}(Z,Z^{\star})\;\leq\;\frac{1}{\kappa}\Bigl(\|\eta\|_{\mathcal{Y}}+\sqrt{\,L\,d_{2}(Z,Z^{\star})+\|\eta\|_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}\,}\Bigr),

which is precisely the statement of Corollary 5.3.

Appendix N
Proof of Lemma
3
(Misfit Moment Bound)

Proof.

Recall that the misfit functional is given by

F(Z)=\|A(Z)-y_{\mathrm{obs}}\|_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2},\qquad y_{\mathrm{obs}}=A(Z^{\star})+\eta.

Expanding the square yields

F(Z)=\|A(Z)-A(Z^{\star})\|_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}+\|\eta\|_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}-2\langle A(Z)-A(Z^{\star}),\,\eta\rangle_{\mathcal{Y}}.

At the ground truth, this reduces to

F(Z^{\star})=\|\eta\|_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}.

Taking expectations and using the centering condition

\mathbb{E}[\eta]=0
from Assumption
(A1)
, the cross term vanishes and we
obtain

\mathbb{E}[F(Z)]=\|A(Z)-A(Z^{\star})\|_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}+\mathbb{E}\|\eta\|_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}.

Consequently,

\mathbb{E}\bigl[F(Z)-F(Z^{\star})\bigr]=\|A(Z)-A(Z^{\star})\|_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}.

No moment assumptions beyond zero mean are required for this identity.

To bound the right-hand side, we invoke the blockwise Lipschitz continuity of
the forward operator (Assumption
(A5)
), which gives

\|A(Z)-A(Z^{\star})\|_{\mathcal{Y}}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N}G_{i}\|Z_{i}-Z_{i}^{\star}\|.

Applying the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and setting

G=\max_{i}G_{i}
, we further obtain

\|A(Z)-A(Z^{\star})\|_{\mathcal{Y}}\leq G\sqrt{N}\,d_{2}(Z,Z^{\star}).

Squaring both sides yields

\|A(Z)-A(Z^{\star})\|_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}\leq G^{2}N\,d_{2}(Z,Z^{\star})^{2}.

Combining the above estimates completes the proof.
∎

Appendix O
Proof of Lemma 6.2 (Misfit Concentration)

Proof.

Recall that the misfit functional is given by

F(Z)=\|A(Z)-y_{\mathrm{obs}}\|_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2},\qquad y_{\mathrm{obs}}=A(Z^{\star})+\eta.

Setting
u:=A(Z)-A(Z^{\star})
and expanding the square yields

F(Z)=\|u\|_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}-2\langle u,\eta\rangle_{\mathcal{Y}}+\|\eta\|_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}.

Taking expectations and using the centering condition

\mathbb{E}[\eta]=0
, we obtain

\mathbb{E}[F(Z)]=\|u\|_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}+\mathbb{E}\|\eta\|_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2},

and therefore

F(Z)-\mathbb{E}[F(Z)]=-2\langle u,\eta\rangle_{\mathcal{Y}}+\bigl(\|\eta\|_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}-\mathbb{E}\|\eta\|_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}\bigr).

We bound the two random terms on the right-hand side separately.

Linear term.

By Assumption
(A1)
, the scalar random variable

\langle u,\eta\rangle_{\mathcal{Y}}
is sub-Gaussian with variance proxy

\sigma^{2}\|u\|_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}
.
Moreover, the blockwise Lipschitz property of the forward operator
(Assumption
(A5)
) implies

\|u\|_{\mathcal{Y}}\leq G\sqrt{N}\,d_{2}(Z,Z^{\star}).

Consequently, for any
s>0
,

\mathbb{P}\!\left(-2\langle u,\eta\rangle_{\mathcal{Y}}\geq s\right)\leq\exp\!\left(-\frac{s^{2}}{32\sigma^{2}G^{2}N\,d_{2}(Z,Z^{\star})^{2}}\right).

Quadratic noise term.

Under the same sub-Gaussian assumption, the centered quadratic fluctuation

\|\eta\|_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}-\mathbb{E}\|\eta\|_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}

is sub-exponential.
Standard Bernstein-type inequalities for Hilbert-valued random variables
(see, e.g.,
[
11
]
) yield

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\|\eta\|_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}-\mathbb{E}\|\eta\|_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}\geq s\right)\leq\exp\!\left(-\frac{s}{8\sigma^{2}}\right),\qquad s>0.

Conclusion.

Combining the two tail bounds via a union bound and absorbing absolute
constants, we obtain

\mathbb{P}\!\left(F(Z)-\mathbb{E}[F(Z)]\geq t\right)\leq 2\exp\!\left(-\min\!\left(\frac{t}{8\sigma^{2}},\frac{t^{2}}{32\sigma^{2}G^{2}N\,d_{2}(Z,Z^{\star})^{2}}\right)\right),

which establishes the desired concentration inequality.
∎

Appendix P
Proof of Theorem 6.3 (High-Probability Parameter Error)

Proof.

The argument combines the deterministic stability inequality of
Theorem
2
with the concentration bound for the misfit
established in Lemma
4
.

Recall first that, by Theorem
2
, any

Z\in\mathcal{Z}
satisfying
d_{2}(Z,Z^{\star})\leq r_{0}
obeys the deterministic
estimate

d_{2}(Z,Z^{\star})\leq\frac{1}{\kappa}\left(\|\eta\|_{\mathcal{Y}}+\sqrt{\,F(Z)-F(Z^{\star})+\|\eta\|_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}\,}\right).

Next, Lemma
4
implies that for any
t>0
, with probability
at least

1-\exp\!\left(-\frac{t}{4G^{2}N\,d_{2}(Z,Z^{\star})^{2}+4\sigma^{2}}\right),

the misfit satisfies the upper bound

F(Z)\leq\mathbb{E}[F(Z)]+t.

On this high-probability event, substituting the above inequality into the

d_{2}(Z,Z^{\star})\leq\frac{1}{\kappa}\left(\|\eta\|_{\mathcal{Y}}+\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[F(Z)]-F(Z^{\star})+t+\|\eta\|_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}}\right).

Appendix Q
Proof of Proposition 7.1 (GS Structural Verification)

Proof.

We verify that the Gaussian Splatting (GS) forward operator satisfies
Assumptions
(A1)
–
(A5)
.
The verification of local identifiability
(A6)
and
resolution-dependent observability
(A7)
is deferred, as these rely on
additional geometric arguments.

Assumption (A1).

Assumption
(A1)
concerns only the stochastic observation model

y_{\mathrm{obs}}=A(Z^{\star})+\eta,\qquad\mathbb{E}[\eta]=0,

and is independent of the structure of the forward operator.
It is therefore satisfied by hypothesis.

Assumption (A2).

By assumption, each primitive parameter satisfies

x_{i}\in\mathcal{X},\quad\Sigma_{i}\in\mathcal{S},\quad c_{i}\in\mathcal{C},\quad\alpha_{i}\in\mathcal{A},

where
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{S},\mathcal{C}
, and
\mathcal{A}
are compact sets.
Since finite products of compact sets are compact, the parameter space

\mathcal{Z}=\mathcal{X}^{N}\times\mathcal{S}^{N}\times\mathcal{C}^{N}\times\mathcal{A}^{N}

is compact, establishing
(A2)
.

Assumption (A3).

For each pixel location
p
, the GS forward operator satisfies

|A_{\mathrm{GS}}(Z)(p)|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N}|\alpha_{i}|\,\|c_{i}\|\,\exp\!\Bigl(-\tfrac{1}{2}(p-\Pi(x_{i}))^{\top}\Sigma_{i}^{-1}(p-\Pi(x_{i}))\Bigr).

Uniform bounds on
\alpha_{i}
,
c_{i}
, and the eigenvalues of
\Sigma_{i}^{-1}

imply that the right-hand side is uniformly bounded in both
p
and
Z
.
Taking the norm in
\mathcal{Y}
therefore yields

\|A_{\mathrm{GS}}(Z)\|_{\mathcal{Y}}\leq B,

for some constant
B>0
, verifying
(A3)
.

Assumption (A4).

At the ground-truth parameter
Z^{\star}
,

F(Z^{\star})=\|A(Z^{\star})-y_{\mathrm{obs}}\|_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}=\|\eta\|_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}.

Assumption
(A4)
postulates a deterministic bound

F(Z^{\star})\leq B_{\eta}^{2}
.
This condition may be enforced by truncation or by conditioning on a
high-probability noise event and is used only in the deterministic stability
analysis.

Assumption (A5).

Define the contribution of a single primitive by

g_{i}(Z_{i})(p):=\alpha_{i}c_{i}\exp\!\Bigl(-\tfrac{1}{2}(p-\Pi(x_{i}))^{\top}\Sigma_{i}^{-1}(p-\Pi(x_{i}))\Bigr),

so that

A_{\mathrm{GS}}(Z)(p)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}g_{i}(Z_{i})(p).

The map
g_{i}
depends smoothly on each component of
Z_{i}=(x_{i},\Sigma_{i},c_{i},\alpha_{i})
.
Uniform bounds on the projection Jacobian
\nabla\Pi
, on the spectrum of

\Sigma_{i}
, and on the parameter domain imply uniform bounds on all partial
derivatives.
More precisely, there exist constants
C_{x},C_{\Sigma},C_{c},C_{\alpha}>0
such that

\|\partial_{x_{i}}g_{i}(Z_{i})\|\leq C_{x},\qquad\|\partial_{\Sigma_{i}}g_{i}(Z_{i})\|\leq C_{\Sigma},\qquad\|\partial_{c_{i}}g_{i}(Z_{i})\|\leq C_{c},\qquad\|\partial_{\alpha_{i}}g_{i}(Z_{i})\|\leq C_{\alpha}.

Consequently,

\|\nabla_{Z_{i}}g_{i}(Z_{i})\|\leq G_{i}:=C_{x}+C_{\Sigma}+C_{c}+C_{\alpha}.

Summing the single-primitive contributions and taking the norm in

\mathcal{Y}
yields

\|A_{\mathrm{GS}}(Z)-A_{\mathrm{GS}}(Z^{\prime})\|_{\mathcal{Y}}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N}G_{i}\,\|Z_{i}-Z_{i}^{\prime}\|,

which is exactly the blockwise Lipschitz condition required in
Assumption
(A5)
.

Conclusion.

The Gaussian Splatting forward operator satisfies
Assumptions
(A1)
–
(A5)
.
∎

Appendix R
Proof of Corollary 7.2 (GS Explicit Constants)

Proof.

The argument relies solely on the blockwise smoothness and compactness of the
Gaussian Splatting (GS) forward operator established in
Proposition
4
.
No identifiability or observability assumptions are required.

Recall the contribution of a single primitive,

g_{i}(Z_{i})(p):=\alpha_{i}c_{i}\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}(p-\Pi(x_{i}))^{\top}\Sigma_{i}^{-1}(p-\Pi(x_{i}))\right),

so that

A_{\mathrm{GS}}(Z)(p)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}g_{i}(Z_{i})(p).

Since the map
Z_{i}\mapsto g_{i}(Z_{i})(p)
is continuously differentiable on a
compact domain with uniformly bounded Fréchet derivative
(Proposition
4
), the mean-value theorem implies that for any
two parameter blocks
Z_{i},Z_{i}^{\prime}
and all pixel locations
p
,

\|g_{i}(Z_{i})(p)-g_{i}(Z_{i}^{\prime})(p)\|\leq\|\nabla_{Z_{i}}g_{i}(\widetilde{Z}_{i})(p)\|\,\|Z_{i}-Z_{i}^{\prime}\|,

for some intermediate point
\widetilde{Z}_{i}
on the line segment joining

Z_{i}
and
Z_{i}^{\prime}
.

Uniform bounds on the partial derivatives with respect to

x_{i}
,
\Sigma_{i}
,
c_{i}
, and
\alpha_{i}
yield constants

C_{x}
,
C_{\Sigma}
,
C_{c}
, and
C_{\alpha}
such that

\|\nabla_{Z_{i}}g_{i}(Z_{i})(p)\|\leq C_{x}+C_{\Sigma}+C_{c}+C_{\alpha}=:G_{i},

uniformly over all admissible parameters and pixel locations.
Consequently,

\|A_{\mathrm{GS}}(Z)-A_{\mathrm{GS}}(Z^{\prime})\|_{\mathcal{Y}}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N}G_{i}\,\|Z_{i}-Z_{i}^{\prime}\|,

which establishes the blockwise Lipschitz condition with explicit constants

G_{i}=C_{x}+C_{\Sigma}+C_{c}+C_{\alpha}.

Defining the global blockwise constant

G:=\max_{1\leq i\leq N}G_{i},

and applying the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality with respect to the product metric

d_{2}
, we further obtain

\|A(Z)-A(Z^{\prime})\|_{\mathcal{Y}}\leq G\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|Z_{i}-Z_{i}^{\prime}\|\leq G\sqrt{N}\,d_{2}(Z,Z^{\prime}).

Substituting this estimate into Theorem
1
yields the global
misfit Lipschitz constant

L=2BG\sqrt{N},

where
B
denotes the uniform forward-output bound from
Assumption
(A3)
.

Conclusion.

All constants appearing in the blockwise and global Lipschitz bounds are thus
explicit and depend only on compactness and analytic properties of the GS
forward operator.
∎

Appendix S
Proof of Theorem
5
(Stability–Resolution Tradeoff)

Proof.

Let

r:=d_{2}(Z,Z^{\star}),

and restrict attention to the identifiable neighborhood

B(Z^{\star},r_{0})
.
We show that deterministic stability, statistical concentration, and
resolution-dependent observability cannot be simultaneously satisfied unless

r
is at least of order
\sqrt{M/N}
.

We begin by considering the expected misfit gap.
By Lemma
3
,

\mathbb{E}[F(Z)-F(Z^{\star})]=\|A(Z)-A(Z^{\star})\|_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}.

Invoking the resolution-dependent observability condition
(Assumption
(A7)
), we obtain the lower bound

\|A(Z)-A(Z^{\star})\|_{\mathcal{Y}}\geq\lambda_{\min}^{\mathrm{eff}}\sqrt{M}\,r,

and hence

\Delta:=\mathbb{E}[F(Z)-F(Z^{\star})]\geq\lambda_{\min}^{\mathrm{eff}\,2}\,Mr^{2}.

(15)

Next, the misfit concentration inequality of
Lemma
4
implies that for any
t>0
,

\mathbb{P}\!\left(F(Z)\leq\Delta+t\right)\geq 1-\exp\!\left(-\,\frac{t}{4G^{2}Nr^{2}+4\sigma^{2}}\right).

On this high-probability event, we may combine the above bound with the
deterministic stability inequality of
Theorem
2
.
Specifically, for
r\leq r_{0}
,

r\leq\frac{1}{\kappa}\left(\sqrt{F(Z)-F(Z^{\star})}+C_{\eta}\right).

Substituting the estimate
F(Z)\leq\Delta+t
and using
(
15
) yields

r\leq\frac{1}{\kappa}\sqrt{\lambda_{\min}^{\mathrm{eff}\,2}Mr^{2}+t}+\frac{C_{\eta}}{\kappa},

(16)

on an event of nonvanishing probability.
In the nontrivial regime
r\gtrsim C_{\eta}/\kappa
, the additive noise term may
be absorbed into constants.

For inequality (
16
) to remain compatible with the
concentration bound above, the fluctuation scale
t
cannot dominate the
curvature term
\lambda_{\min}^{\mathrm{eff}\,2}Mr^{2}
.
However, admissible values of
t
are controlled by the denominator

4G^{2}Nr^{2}+4\sigma^{2}
in the concentration inequality.
Consequently, a necessary compatibility condition is

\lambda_{\min}^{\mathrm{eff}\,2}Mr^{2}\;\lesssim\;G^{2}Nr^{2}.

Cancelling
r^{2}
(for
r>0
) yields the intrinsic constraint

r\geq\frac{\lambda_{\min}^{\mathrm{eff}}}{G}\sqrt{\frac{M}{N}}.

(17)

Absorbing constants depending only on

(\kappa,B,C_{\eta})
into a single constant
C_{\mathrm{stab}}
completes the
proof and establishes that any estimator

\widehat{Z}\in B(Z^{\star},r_{0})
must satisfy

d_{2}(\widehat{Z},Z^{\star})\geq C_{\mathrm{stab}}\sqrt{\frac{M}{N}},

with
C_{\mathrm{stab}}
independent of
(M,N)
.
∎
