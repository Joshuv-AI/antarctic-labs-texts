Faithful global convergence for the rescaled Consensus–Based Optimization
Hui Huang, Hicham Kouhkouh, Lukang Sun

Abstract
. We analyze the Consensus-Based Optimization (CBO) algorithm with a consensus point rescaled by a fixed parameter κ ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) \kappa\in(0,1) . Under minimal assumptions on the objective function and the initial data, we establish its unconditional convergence to the global minimizer. Our results hold in the asymptotic regime where both the time–horizon t → ∞ t\to\infty and the inverse–temperature α → ∞ \alpha\to\infty , providing a rigorous theoretical foundation for the algorithm’s global convergence. Furthermore, our findings extend to the case of multiple and non–discrete set of minimizers.

1.
Introduction

Consensus–Based Optimization (CBO) is a derivative–free algorithm designed to tackle large–scale, highly non–convex optimization problems. This algorithm and its analytical foundations were established in
[
43
,
13
]
. It has since then been extensively studied, both theoretically and practically, demonstrating its effectiveness in solving complex optimization challenges that arise in real–world applications.

Inspired by the collective behavior observed in social and biological systems, CBO exploits the interactions of multiple agents, or particles, to explore the search space in a coordinated and efficient manner. The method uses a stochastic consensus mechanism, where particles are dynamically guided toward regions of lower objective values, ultimately converging to an optimal solution. This decentralized and adaptive search strategy makes CBO particularly well-suited for problems where gradient information is unavailable or unreliable.
Motivated by its broad applicability, researchers have extended and refined the original CBO framework to accommodate various settings and demonstrated strong empirical success in solving wide range of global optimization problems

[

,
30
,

,

,

,

,

,

,

,

,

,
4
,

,
37
,
19
]
. These advancements have further enhanced the algorithm’s robustness and efficiency, making it a versatile tool across a wide range of scientific and engineering disciplines.
Typical convergence result of CBO establishes that under suitable assumptions on the objective function (e.g., continuity, isolated global minimizers, and a “localization” condition such as the so-called “well-behaved” landscape around the global minimum), the mean-field limit of CBO approximates a Dirac mass concentrated at the global minimizer when
t
and
\alpha
are sufficiently large. This is a probabilistic and asymptotic guarantee in the mean-field regime, not a worst-case computational complexity statement.

One of the key contributions of this manuscript is to establish its convergence, thus providing further theoretical validation of the method.

Let us consider the following optimization problem:

\text{Find }\;x_{*}\in\argmin_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f(x),\,

where
f
can be a non-convex non-smooth objective function that one wishes to minimize. Then the CBO dynamic considers the following system of
N
interacting particles, denoted as
\{X_{\cdot}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, which satisfies

(1.1)

{\rm d}X_{t}^{i}=-\lambda(X_{t}^{i}-\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho_{t}^{N}))\,{\rm d}t+\sigma D(X_{t}^{i}-\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho_{t}^{N}))\,{\rm d}B_{t}^{i},\quad i=1,\dots,N=:[N]\,,

where
\sigma>0
is a real constant,
\rho_{t}^{N}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{X_{t}^{i}}
is the empirical measure associated to the particle system, and
\{B_{.}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
are
N
independent
d
-dimensional Brownian motions. Moreover, the particle system is initialized with independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) data
\{X_{0}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, where each
X_{0}^{i}
is distributed according to a given measure
\rho_{0}\in\mathscr{P}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
for all
i\in[N]
.

Here, we employ the anisotropic diffusion in the sense that
D(X):=\mbox{diag}(|X_{1}|,\dots,|X_{d}|)
for any
X\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, which has been proven to handle high-dimensional problems more effectively
[
15
,
24
]
.
The
current global consensus
point
\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho_{t}^{N})
is meant to approximate the global minimum, and is defined with a weighted average of the particles as

(1.2)

\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho_{t}^{N}):=\frac{\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}x\,\omega_{\alpha}^{f}(x)\;\rho_{t}^{N}({\rm d}x)}{\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\omega_{\alpha}^{f}(x)\;\rho_{t}^{N}({\rm d}x)}\,,\quad\text{ where }\quad\omega_{\alpha}^{f}(x):=\exp(-\alpha f(x)).

This choice of weight function
\omega_{\alpha}^{f}(\cdot)
is motivated by the well-known Laplace’s principle
[
42
,
21
]
, see also
[
35
, Appendix A.2]
. Formally, this means that

(1.3)

\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho_{t}^{N})\;\approx\;\argmin_{1\leq i\leq N}f(X_{t}^{i})\quad\text{as }\alpha\to\infty.

In other words, as
\alpha\to\infty
, the consensus point
\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho_{t}^{N})
converges to the position of the particle with the smallest objective value at time
t
, i.e., the best location found by the ensemble
\{X_{t}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
at time
t
. Once consensus is reached, all particles concentrate around the consensus point
\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho_{t}^{N})
, in the sense that

(1.4)

X_{t=\infty}^{i}\;\approx\;\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho_{t=\infty}^{N})\quad\text{for all }i\in\{1,\dots,N\}.

Consequently, provided the number of particles
N
is sufficiently large and
\alpha\to\infty
, the ensemble concentrates near the global minimizer
x_{*}
. We refer to
[
33
, Section 1.2]
for more details.

The convergence proof of the CBO method is typically conducted in the context of large-particle limit
[
29
,
36
]
. Specifically, rather than analyzing the
N
–particle system (
1.1
) directly, one considers the limit as
N
approaches infinity and examines the corresponding McKean–Vlasov process
{\overline{X}}_{\cdot}
, which is governed by the following equation:

(1.5)

\displaystyle{\rm d}{\overline{X}}_{t}=

\displaystyle-\lambda\left({\overline{X}}_{t}-\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{t})\right)\,{\rm d}t\,+\,\sigma D\left({\overline{X}}_{t}-\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{t})\right)\,{\rm d}B_{t}\,,

\displaystyle\text{with }\;\rho_{t}^{\alpha}\,:=\,\text{Law}({\overline{X}}_{t}).

1.1.
The convergence problem in the literature

There are numerous results in the literature on the convergence analysis of CBO methods, which can be broadly categorized into two different approaches. For instance, in
[
25
]
, the authors employ
\mathbb{E}[|\overline{X}_{t}-x_{*}|^{2}]
as a Lyapunov functional for (
1.5
). Their convergence result states that for any given accuracy
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a time
T_{*}>0
depending on
\varepsilon
, such that for
\alpha>\alpha_{0}
(also depending on
\varepsilon
), the following holds:

\mathbb{E}\left[|\overline{X}_{t}-x_{*}|^{2}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[|\overline{X}_{0}-x_{*}|^{2}\right]e^{-\theta t}

for some constant
\theta>0
and for all
t\in[0,T_{*}]
. Moreover, it holds that

\mathbb{E}\left[|\overline{X}_{T_{*}}-x_{*}|^{2}\right]\leq\varepsilon.

This approach has been further extended to establish the convergence of various CBO variants, including those incorporating general constraints
[
8
,
3
]
, multi-player games
[
18
]
, min-max problems
[
9
]
, multiple-minimizer problems
[
27
]
, clustered federated learning
[
16
]
, and bi-level optimization
[
44
]
.

However, a limitation of this method is that it does not allow for taking the limits
t\to\infty
or
\alpha\to\infty
, meaning that convergence is guaranteed only within a neighborhood of
x_{*}
with accuracy
\varepsilon
.
More precisely, the proof of this method relies on a quantitative version of Laplace’s principle, which requires that
\rho_{0}(B_{r_{\varepsilon}}(x^{*}))>0
. However, as
\varepsilon\to 0
, the radius
r_{\varepsilon}\to 0
, and consequently
\rho_{0}(B_{r_{\varepsilon}}(x^{*}))\to 0
, leading to a degeneracy that violates this condition.

One possible approach to achieving convergence as
t\to\infty
is through a variance-based method described in
[
13
]
. This approach involves defining the variance function

V(t)=\mathbb{E}\left[|\overline{X}_{t}-\mathbb{E}[\overline{X}_{t}]|^{2}\right].

The proof is then divided into two steps. First, it is shown that under well-prepared initial data and appropriately chosen parameters, for any fixed
\alpha
, the function
V(t)
converges to zero exponentially fast as
t\to\infty
. This implies that
\overline{X}_{t}
converges to some consensus point
x^{\alpha}
as
t\to\infty
. Second, it is verified that the consensus point
x^{\alpha}
can be made arbitrarily close (up to any given accuracy
\varepsilon
) to the global minimizer
x_{*}
by choosing some fixed
\alpha
that is sufficiently large. This argument has been further extended to establish the convergence of such as particle swarm optimization
[
34
]
, CBO constrained on the sphere
[
23
]
, CBO for saddle point problems
[
35
]
, and CBO with mirror descent
[
10
]
. A similar idea is also employed in the convergence proof at the particle level
[
40
]
.
However, similar to the first method, this approach does not allow taking the limit
\alpha\to\infty
and it only guarantees convergence within an
\varepsilon
–neighborhood of the global minimizer. More precisely, this method requires that the initial variance satisfies
V(0)\leq\frac{C}{\alpha}
and that the initial distribution contains the global minimizer
x_{*}
. Consequently, as
\alpha\to\infty
, the condition
V(0)\leq\frac{C}{\alpha}
forces the initial distribution to concentrate at
x_{*}
already.

In this paper, we will establish the first
true
convergence result for CBO method, demonstrating that both limits,
t\to\infty
and
\alpha\to\infty
, can be taken. Up to our knowledge, this is the first result of this kind. The main idea relies on the following.

1.2.
Motivation and the
rescaled
CBO

As it has been observed in
[
32
]
, see also
[
33
, Remark 1.1]
, any Dirac measure is invariant for the dynamics (
1.5
) which makes it difficult to study its asymptotic properties. This non–uniqueness of an invariant probability measure is mainly due to two reasons: in the drift, the term
-X
is not strong enough with respect to the
mean–field term
\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\cdot)
, and the diffusion degenerates. In practice, in such situations, the particle population are often observed to collapse prematurely to a Dirac delta distribution before the global minimizer has been reached (see Figure
1
) due to the global minimizer not being contained in the initial distribution. Moreover, on the theoretical side, while convergence in mean-field law over finite time
t<T
and finite
\alpha
has been achieved in the literature (as discussed in Section 1.1), proving global convergence over an infinite time horizon has remained elusive.

This is the main reason behind our modification of (
1.5
) whose aim is to address exactly these two mentioned issues, and which has already been proposed in
[

,

]
.
Hence, we consider a
rescaled
CBO given by

(1.6)

\displaystyle{\rm d}{\overline{X}}_{t}=

\displaystyle-\lambda\left({\overline{X}}_{t}-\kappa\,\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{t})\right)\,{\rm d}t\,+\,\sigma\left(\delta\,\mathds{I}_{d}+D\left({\overline{X}}_{t}-\kappa\,\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{t})\right)\right)\,{\rm d}B_{t}

\displaystyle\text{with }\;\rho^{\alpha}_{t}\,:=\,\text{Law}({\overline{X}}_{t}).

complemented with an initial condition
{\overline{X}}_{0}\sim\rho_{0}\in\mathscr{P}_{4}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, where
\mathds{I}_{d}
is the
d
-dimensional identity matrix,
0<\kappa<1
is a small positive constant, and
\delta>0
is any positive constant. The name is justified by the fact that the contribution of the mean–field term
\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\cdot)
is re-
scaled
with a parameter
\kappa
when compared to the contribution of
-{\overline{X}}
, thus promoting
dissipation
(
-{\overline{X}}
) over
interaction
(
\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\cdot)
). To implement the model numerically, one considers the underlying system of
N
interacting particles which is of the form

(1.7)

{\rm d}X_{t}^{i}=-\lambda\left(X_{t}^{i}-\kappa\,\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho_{t}^{N})\right){\rm d}t+\sigma\left(\delta\,\mathds{I}_{d}+D(X_{t}^{i}-\kappa\,\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho_{t}^{N})\right){\rm d}B_{t}^{i},\quad i\in[N]\,

and
\rho_{t}^{N}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{X_{t}^{i}}
is the empirical measure associated to the particle system.

Introducing the parameters
\kappa
and
\delta
makes all Dirac measures not invariant any more, and intuitively, creates a disconnection between the limits in the time variable
t\to\infty
and the inverse–temperature
\alpha\to\infty
, hence allowing to easily handle the convergence of the dynamics towards the (to–be) global minimum. We refer to
[
33
, Section 1.2]
for more details.

Moreover, the positive parameter
\delta\,
guarantees ellipticity (non–degeneracy) of the PDE satisfied by the invariant probability measure of the dynamics, and grants it nice properties. More precisely, we need
\delta>0
in order to guarantee existence of an invariant probability measure (Theorem
3.1
below), and to ensure suitable regularity (Theorem
3.8
below). Uniqueness then follows using the
\mathbb{W}_{2}
–contraction (Theorem
3.3
below). Its value however does not influence the asymptotic analysis that we shall perform in the sequel, or the results that we aim for. Indeed, as we will later see in (
4.4
), the constant
\delta
appears in a martingale term which will vanish after taking the expectation, hence its value will not influence our convergence result as long as it is positive.

The advantage of the rescaled CBO (
1.6
) manifests already in several contexts:

•

In
[
31
]
, it ensures the validity of an
averaging principle
which reduces a singularly perturbed (or, multi–scaled) dynamics of the form (
1.6
), into an averaged one. This can be seen as a model reduction technique for solving multi–level optimization problems using multiple populations of interacting particles.

•

In
[
32
]
, it allows to prove existence and uniqueness of an invariant probability measure
\rho_{*}^{\alpha}
,
and to establish the long–time behavior of its law. Moreover, its approximation with empirical measures has been proven with an explicit rate of convergence, leading to the so-called
Self–interacting CBO
.

•

In
[
33
]
, it helped to guarantee a uniform–in–time propagation of chaos for the population of particles evolving along the dynamics (
1.7
). This property is crucial for ensuring a stable and reliable long–term convergence, which secure the practical effectiveness of CBO methods.

[

]
, where the proof of global convergence is absent. Our main contributions are discussed next.

1.3.
Highlights

The three main features of the present manuscript are as follows.

•

Asymptotic behavior when
t\to\infty
and
\alpha\to\infty
:
We obtain the first
true
convergence result in the literature in the sense that both limits,
t\to\infty
and
\alpha\to\infty
, can be taken. More specifically, we establish the asymptotic behavior of the unique invariant (
t\to\infty
) probability measure
\rho_{*}^{\alpha}
of (
1.6
) as
\alpha\to\infty
.
More precisely, when the function
f(\cdot)
to be minimized has a unique global minimizer
x_{*}
, we prove that
\mathbb{W}_{2}(\rho^{\alpha}_{t},\rho^{\alpha}_{*})^{2}\longrightarrow 0
when
t\to\infty
, and

\lim_{\alpha\to\infty}\lim_{t\to\infty}\;\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{t})=\lim_{\alpha\to\infty}\lim_{t\to\infty}\;\kappa^{-1}\,{\mathbb{E}}\left[\,{\overline{X}}_{t}\,\right]=x_{*}\,,

where
{\overline{X}}_{\cdot}
satisfies (
1.6
).

•

Unconditional global convergence:
We are able to bypass classical assumptions in the literature.

–

Our results hold without any prior assumptions on the initial distribution
\rho_{0}
of (
1.6
) or its corresponding interacting particles system (
1.7
), provided it has sufficiently many finite moments. In particular, we do not assume that a global minimizer of
f(\cdot)
is contained within the support of
\rho_{0}
, a crucial assumption in both
[
13
]
and
[
25
]
. Very recently,
[
28
]
demonstrated that this assumption can actually be removed by instead requiring regular initial data
\rho_{0}\in H^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
Or in
[
37
]
, a feedback control is used to drive the particles nearby the minimizer.
In contrast, our approach does not use control and does not impose any regularity conditions on
\rho_{0}
, thereby allowing cases where
\rho_{0}
is a Dirac measure, as has been considered in the self–interacting CBO model
[
32
]
.

–

No regularity assumptions on the function
f(\cdot)
are needed, besides being positive, locally Lipschitz continuous, and with a quadratic growth (see Assumption
2.1
). These assumptions were used in
[
13
]
to guarantee the well-posedness of the CBO dynamics (
1.5
). To establish global convergence,
[
13
, Assumption 4.1]
imposes additional conditions, such as
f\in\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and the boundedness of
\|\nabla^{2}f\|_{\infty}
. In the framework of
[
25
, Definition 8]
, some additional local growth conditions on
f
around the global minimizer are required. For our rescaled CBO we do not require these additional assumptions for the convergence proof.

•

Multiple–minimizer case:
Although our rescaled CBO is not specifically designed to address the multiple minimizer problem, we can still establish its asymptotic properties in the case where the objective function
f(\cdot)
has multiple non-trivial minimizers, whether they are discrete points or compact subsets (see Theorem
4.9
).
More precisely, denoting
\mathcal{M}
the set of minimizers, we prove

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{\alpha\to\infty}\,\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}\operatorname{dist}(\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{t}),\,\mathcal{M})=\lim\limits_{\alpha\to\infty}\,\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}\operatorname{dist}\!\left(\kappa^{-1}\,{\mathbb{E}}\left[\,{\overline{X}}_{t}\,\right],\,\mathcal{M}\right)=0.

Let us mention here that specially designed CBO models aimed at addressing multiple-minimizer problems have been introduced in

.

2.
Useful estimates

We start by recalling some notation.
When a measure
\mu
has a density
\varrho
with respect to Lebesgue measure that we denote by
\mbox{\rm d}x
, then
\mu
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\text{d}x
, we write
\mu\ll\mbox{\rm d}x
and
\varrho=\frac{\text{d}\mu}{\text{d}x}
is Radon–Nikodym derivative of
\mu
with respect to
\mbox{\rm d}x
. Unless a confusion arises, we shall use the same notation for a measure and for its density (when it exists).
We shall also denote by
W^{k,p}(\Omega)
,
p\geq 1,k\geq 0
the standard Sobolev space of functions whose generalized derivatives up to order
k
are in
L^{p}(\Omega)
, and we denote by
W^{k,p}_{loc}(\Omega)
the class of functions
f
such that
\chi f\in W^{k,p}(\Omega)
for each
\chi\in\mathcal{C}_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
the class of infinitely differentiable functions with compact support in
\Omega
.

In what follows,
\|\cdot\|
denotes the Frobenius norm of a matrix and
|\cdot|
is the standard Euclidean norm in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
;
\mathscr{P}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
denotes the space of probability measures on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, and
\mathscr{P}_{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
with
p\geq 1
contains all
\mu\in\mathscr{P}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
such that
\mu(|\cdot|^{p}):=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|x|^{p}\mu({\rm d}x)<\infty
; it is equipped with
p
–Wasserstein distance
\mathbb{W}_{p}(\cdot,\cdot)
.

Our main assumption concerns the function
f(\cdot)
to be minimized, and can be stated as follows.

Assumption 2.1
.

We assume the following properties for the objective function.

(1)

f:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is bounded from below by
\underline{f}:=\min f
, and there exists some constant
L_{f}>0
such that

|f(x)-f(y)|\leq L_{f}(1+|x|+|y|)|x-y|,\quad\forall x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\,.

(2)

There exist constants
c_{\ell},c_{u}>0
and
M>0
such that

f(x)-\underline{f}\leq c_{u}(|x|^{2}+1),\quad\forall x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\;\mbox{ and }\quad c_{\ell}|x|^{2}\leq f(x)-\underline{f}\;\mbox{ for }|x|\geq M\,.

We note that the bound
f(x)-\underline{f}\leq c_{u}(|x|^{2}+1)
is an immediate consequence of the Lipschitz growth condition in assumption (1). We retain it here to make the dependence on the constant
c_{u}
explicit in the subsequent results.

Remark 2.2
.

The conditions described in Assumption
2.1
are satisfied in practice by a wide range of functions.

•

Local Lipschitz growth:
This condition is quite standard and guarantees, in particular, the well-posedness of the dynamics (the associated SDE).

•

Quadratic growth outside a ball:
This assumption is satisfied by essentially any objective function whose minimizer (or minimizers) lie within a known bounded region. In practice, optimization procedures often restrict the search domain to a closed ball. One may therefore truncate the objective function on this ball and extend it with a quadratic term outside, e.g.
f(x)\leftarrow f(x)\mathds{1}_{B}(x)+\bigl(|x|^{2}+C\bigr)\mathds{1}_{B^{c}}(x)
, for some appropriate constant
C
. With this modification, all polynomial objective functions, among many others, fall within our framework.

The mean–field term
\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\mu)
plays a crucial role in the behavior of the dynamics. Its properties are essential for our analysis to be carried out. Thus we shall recall an estimate on
\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\mu)
that we borrow from
[
13
, Lemma 3.3]
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
f(\cdot)
satisfy Assumption
2.1
, then it holds that

(2.1)

|\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\nu)|^{2}\leq\frac{\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|x|^{2}e^{-\alpha f(x)}\nu(\mbox{\rm d}x)}{\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}e^{-\alpha f(x)}\nu(\mbox{\rm d}x)}\leq b_{1}+b_{2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|x|^{2}\mbox{\rm d}\nu\quad\forall\nu\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\,,

where

(2.2)

b_{1}=M^{2}+b_{2}\quad\text{ and }\quad b_{2}=2\,\frac{c_{u}}{c_{\ell}}\left(1+\frac{1}{\alpha\,M^{2}\,c_{\ell}}\right).

Remark 2.4
.

In fact, as it has been already observed in
[
13
, Remark.1]
, the constant
b_{2}
can be chosen to be independent of
\alpha
for
\alpha\geq 1
.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
f(\cdot)
satisfy Assumption
2.1
and
\rho_{0}\in\mathscr{P}_{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
for any
p\geq 2
, then the dynamics (
1.6
) has a unique strong solution and satisfies

(2.3)

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\,{\mathbb{E}}\left[\,|{\overline{X}}_{t}|^{p}\,\right]<\infty\quad\quad\forall\,0<T<\infty.

Proof.

The proof of well–posedness for the particle system (
1.7
) follows the same reasoning as in
[
13
, Theorem 2.1]
or
[
29
, Theorem 2.2]
, relying on the fact that the coefficients in (
1.7
) are locally Lipschitz.
For the mean-field dynamics (
1.6
), well-posedness can be established using the Leray-Schauder fixed-point theorem, as demonstrated in
[
13
, Theorem 3.1]
or
[
29
, Theorem 2.2]
.
Finally, the moment estimates in (
2.3
) are derived from
[
13
, Lemma 3.4]
or
[
29
, Lemma 3.5]
by using the Burkholder–Davis–Gundy inequality.
∎

We can now prove the following uniform–in–time second moment bound for
{\overline{X}}_{t}
which will play a key role in the sequel.

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
f(\cdot)
satisfy assumption
2.1
and
\rho_{0}\in\mathscr{P}_{4}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
. Consider
({\overline{X}}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
with
\rho_{0}
-distributed initial data satisfying (
1.6
). Then for sufficiently small
\kappa
there exists some constant
C_{2}
depending only
\lambda,\sigma,d,\delta,\kappa
and
f
such that it holds

(2.4)

\sup_{t\geq 0}\;{\mathbb{E}}\left[\,|{\overline{X}}_{t}|^{2}\,\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\,|{\overline{X}}_{0}|^{2}\,\right]+C_{2}\,.

Before we proceed with the proof, let us recall Itô’s formula of the sake of self-containedness: denote the dynamics of the process
\overline{X}
by the shorthand notation

\mbox{\rm d}\overline{X}_{t}=\bm{\mu}_{t}\,\mbox{\rm d}t+\bm{\varsigma}_{t}\,\mbox{\rm d}B_{t}

Itô’s formula states that for any function
h\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
one has

(2.5)

\displaystyle\mbox{\rm d}h(\overline{X}_{t})

\displaystyle=\left\{\,\nabla h(\overline{X}_{t})\cdot\bm{\mu}_{t}+\frac{1}{2}\text{trace}(\bm{\varsigma}_{t}\cdot D^{2}h(\overline{X}_{t})\,\bm{\varsigma}_{t})\,\right\}\mbox{\rm d}t

\displaystyle+\nabla h(\overline{X}_{t})\cdot\bm{\varsigma}_{t}\,\mbox{\rm d}B_{t}.

Proof.

Let
h(x):=\frac{1}{2}|x|^{2}
, for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and let
\overline{X}
be the process defined in (
1.6
). Then applying Itô’s formula (
2.5
) leads to

\displaystyle{\rm d}h({\overline{X}}_{t})

\displaystyle=\bigg\{-\lambda\nabla h({\overline{X}}_{t})\cdot\big({\overline{X}}_{t}-\kappa\,\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho_{t})\big)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\text{trace}\left[D^{2}h({\overline{X}}_{t})\bigg(\delta\,\mathds{I}_{d}+D\big({\overline{X}}_{t}-\kappa\,\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho_{t})\big)\bigg)^{2}\right]\bigg\}\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad+\sigma\,\nabla h({\overline{X}}_{t})^{\top}\bigg(\delta\,\mathds{I}_{d}+D\big({\overline{X}}_{t}-\kappa\,\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho_{t})\big)\bigg)\,{\rm d}B_{t}

\displaystyle=:F(t,{\overline{X}}_{t})\,{\rm d}t+G(t,{\overline{X}}_{t})\,{\rm d}B_{t}.

One could observe that, thanks to Lemma
2.5
we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{t}G(s,{\overline{X}}_{s})\,{\rm d}B_{s}\right]=0,\quad\forall\,t>0.

This is true in particular because of the well-posedness of the process
t\mapsto{\overline{X}}_{t}
according to Lemma
2.5
with
X_{0}^{i}\in\mathscr{P}_{4}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, and its finite moments which guarantee that
\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{t}|G(s,{\overline{X}}_{s})|^{2}{\rm d}s\right]\leq C\,{\mathbb{E}}\left[\int_{0}^{t}|{\overline{X}}_{s}|^{4}{\rm d}s\right]<+\infty
.
Therefore we have

(2.6)

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{2}|{\overline{X}}_{t}|^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}[F(t,{\overline{X}}_{t})],\quad\forall\,t>0.

We can now further estimate the right-hand side as follows. Recall

\displaystyle F(t,{\overline{X}}_{t})

\displaystyle=-\lambda\nabla h({\overline{X}}_{t})\cdot\big({\overline{X}}_{t}-\kappa\,\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{N}_{t})\big)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\text{trace}\left[D^{2}h({\overline{X}}_{t})\bigg(\delta\,\mathds{I}_{d}+D\big({\overline{X}}_{t}-\kappa\,\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{N}_{t})\big)\bigg)^{2}\right]

and with
h(x)=\frac{1}{2}|x|^{2}
we have
\nabla h(x)=x
, and
D^{2}h(x)=\mathds{I}_{d}
.
We deal first with the trace term in
F(t,{\overline{X}}_{t})
. We have

\displaystyle\text{trace}\left[D^{2}h({\overline{X}}_{t})\bigg(\delta\,\mathds{I}_{d}+D\big({\overline{X}}_{t}-\kappa\,\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho_{t})\big)\bigg)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\quad\quad=\;\text{trace}\left[D^{2}h({\overline{X}}_{t})\bigg(\delta^{2}\,\mathds{I}_{d}+D\big({\overline{X}}_{t}-\kappa\,\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho_{t})\big)^{2}+2\delta\,D\big({\overline{X}}_{t}-\kappa\,\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho_{t})\big)\bigg)\right]

\displaystyle\quad\quad=\;\delta^{2}\,\text{trace}(D^{2}h({\overline{X}}_{t}))+\text{trace}\big[D^{2}h({\overline{X}}_{t})D({\overline{X}}_{t}-\kappa\,\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho_{t}))^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad+2\delta\,\text{trace}\big[D^{2}h({\overline{X}}_{t})D({\overline{X}}_{t}-\kappa\,\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho_{t}))\big]\;

\displaystyle\quad\quad\leq\;\delta^{2}d+\sum\limits_{k=1}^{d}|({\overline{X}}_{t}-\kappa\,\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho_{t}))_{k}|^{2}+2\delta\sum\limits_{k=1}^{d}|({\overline{X}}_{t}-\kappa\,\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho_{t}))_{k}|

\displaystyle\quad\quad\leq\;\delta^{2}d+\delta d+(\delta+1)|{\overline{X}}_{t}-\kappa\,\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho_{t})|^{2}.

In the last line, we used
|({\overline{X}}_{t}-\kappa\,\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho_{t}))_{k}|\leq\frac{1}{2}(|({\overline{X}}_{t}-\kappa\,\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho_{t}))_{k}|^{2}+1)
, then the sum yields
\sum\limits_{k=1}^{d}|({\overline{X}}_{t}-\kappa\,\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho_{t}))_{k}|\leq\frac{1}{2}(|{\overline{X}}_{t}-\kappa\,\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho_{t})|^{2}+d)
.
Therefore we have

\displaystyle F(t,{\overline{X}}_{t})

\displaystyle\leq-\lambda\left(1-\frac{\kappa}{2}\right)|{\overline{X}}_{t}|^{2}+\lambda\,\frac{\kappa}{2}|\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho_{t})|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\delta(\delta+1)d+\sigma^{2}(\delta+1)|{\overline{X}}_{t}|^{2}+\sigma^{2}(\delta+1)\kappa^{2}|\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho_{t})|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\{-\lambda\left(1-\frac{\kappa}{2}\right)+\sigma^{2}\left(1+\delta\right)\right\}\,|{\overline{X}}_{t}|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\left\{\lambda\,\frac{\kappa}{2}+\sigma^{2}\left(1+\delta\right)\kappa^{2}\right\}\,|\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho_{t})|^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\delta(\delta+1)d.

Recalling (
2.1
), it holds that

|\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho_{t})|^{2}\leq b_{1}+b_{2}\,{\mathbb{E}}[|{\overline{X}}_{t}|^{2}]\,,

which implies that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[F(t,{\overline{X}}_{t})]

\displaystyle\leq\left\{-\lambda\left(1-\frac{\kappa}{2}\right)+\sigma^{2}\left(1+\delta\right)+b_{2}\left(\lambda\,\frac{\kappa}{2}+\sigma^{2}\left(1+\delta\right)\kappa^{2}\right)\right\}\,{\mathbb{E}}[|{\overline{X}}_{t}|^{2}]

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\left(\lambda\,\frac{\kappa}{2}+\sigma^{2}\left(1+\delta\right)\kappa^{2}\right)b_{1}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\delta(\delta+1)d.

The latter, together with (
2.6
) finally yields

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{2}|{\overline{X}}_{t}|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[F(t,{\overline{X}}_{t})\right]\leq-\gamma\,\mathbb{E}\left[|{\overline{X}}_{t}|^{2}\right]+C_{2}

where

(2.7)

\displaystyle\gamma:=\lambda\left(1-\frac{\kappa}{2}\right)-\sigma^{2}\left(1+\delta\right)-b_{2}\left(\lambda\,\frac{\kappa}{2}+\sigma^{2}\left(1+\delta\right)\kappa^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\text{and }\quad C_{2}:=\left(\lambda\,\frac{\kappa}{2}+\sigma^{2}\left(1+\delta\right)\kappa^{2}\right)b_{1}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\delta(\delta+1)d.

It suffices to choose
\lambda,\sigma,\delta
, and
\kappa
in order to guarantee
\gamma>0
,
which together with Grönwall’s inequality yields

(2.8)

\mathbb{E}\left[|{\overline{X}}_{t}|^{2}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[|{\overline{X}}_{0}|^{2}\right]\,e^{-\gamma\,t}+C_{2}\,\leq\mathbb{E}\left[|{\overline{X}}_{0}|^{2}\right]+C_{2}.

∎

Remark 2.7
.

An example of a simple configuration of the parameters where (
2.4
) is guaranteed can be obtained as follows.
Recalling the notation in (
2.2
) and then in (
2.7
), we can choose for example
\delta=1
, then we need to satisfy the inequality

\displaystyle\gamma=\lambda\left(1-\frac{\kappa}{2}\right)-2\,\sigma^{2}-b_{2}\left(\lambda\,\frac{\kappa}{2}+2\sigma^{2}\kappa^{2}\right)\;>0

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\quad

\displaystyle\lambda\;>\lambda\,\frac{\kappa}{2}(1+b_{2})+2\,\sigma^{2}(1+b_{2}\kappa^{2})

\displaystyle\Leftarrow\quad

\displaystyle\lambda\;>\lambda\,\kappa\,(1+b_{2})+4\,\sigma^{2}(1+b_{2}\kappa^{2})

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\quad

\displaystyle\lambda\;(1-\,\kappa\,(1+b_{2}))>4\,\sigma^{2}(1+b_{2}\kappa^{2})

Choosing
\kappa<\frac{1}{2}(1+b_{2})^{-1}
yields in particular
(1-\,\kappa\,(1+b_{2}))>\frac{1}{2}
and
b_{2}\,\kappa^{2}<1
. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\lambda\;(1-\,\kappa\,(1+b_{2}))>\frac{\lambda}{2}\quad\text{ and }\quad 8\,\sigma^{2}>4\,\sigma^{2}(1+b_{2}\kappa^{2}).

Conclusion: to guarantee
\gamma>0
, an example of sufficient conditions is when

\displaystyle\delta=1,\quad\kappa<\frac{1}{2(1+b_{2})},\quad\text{ and }\quad\lambda>16\,\sigma^{2}.

\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\mu)
from
[
29
, Corollary 3.3, Proposition A.3]
. It will be used in the proof of the subsequent results.

Lemma 2.8
.

Suppose
f(\cdot)
satisfies Assumption
2.1
. Then for all
R>0
,
there exists some constant
L_{\mathfrak{m}}>0
depending on
R
such that

(2.9)

|\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\mu)-\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\nu)|\leq L_{\mathfrak{m}}\mathbb{W}_{2}(\mu,\nu)\quad\forall(\mu,\nu)\in\mathscr{P}_{2,R}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\times\mathscr{P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\,.

3.
On the invariant measure

This section is devoted to the limit
t\to\infty
. We will first prove existence of an invariant measure for (
1.6
) in §
3.1
. Then we will we will establish in §
3.2
a
\mathbb{W}_{2}
–exponential contraction for the convergence of the law of (
1.6
) towards the invariant measure. This shall be next used in §
3.3
where we will provide a uniform-in-
\alpha
second moment of the invariant measure (that will be later needed in Section
4
). Finally, we will show that the invariant measure verifies a Harnack type inequality which will lead to a positive and uniform-in-
\alpha
lower bound on its support: a key result for Laplace’s principle in Section
4
.

3.1.
Existence

The following result ensures existence of an invariant measure for the process (
1.6
).

Theorem 3.1
.

Suppose
f(\cdot)
satisfies Assumption
2.1
. For sufficiently small
\kappa\in(0,1)
, and for a large
\lambda>0
, there exists an invariant measure
\rho^{\alpha}_{*}\in\mathscr{P}_{2,R}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, for some sufficiently large
R
that depends on
\kappa,\delta,\lambda,\sigma,b_{1},b_{2}
and the initial distribution.

Proof.

The proof relies on
[
46
, Theorem 2.2]
. See Appendix
A
.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

As can be seen in Appendix
A
, the precise condition on the parameters is explicitly given by (
A.3
) that is

2\lambda-4>\lambda\,\kappa\,(1+b_{2})+4\,\kappa^{2}b_{2}

where we recall
b_{2}
is defined in (
2.2
).
A simple example of sufficient conditions is when

\kappa<\frac{1}{2(1+b_{2})}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\lambda>4.

3.2.
Long-time limit

The next result provides a
\mathbb{W}_{2}
–exponential contraction for the evolution of the distribution of
\overline{X}
as defined in (
1.6
) and cements its long-time limit.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let us consider (
1.6
) whose distribution is
\rho^{\alpha}_{t}:=\operatorname{Law}(\overline{X}_{t})
for all
t\geq 0
. Suppose
f(\cdot)
satisfies Assumption
2.1
. Then for sufficiently small
\kappa\in(0,1)
and
\sigma>0
, there exists
\Theta>0
such that

(3.1)

\mathbb{W}_{2}(\rho^{\alpha}_{t},\rho^{\alpha}_{*})^{2}\leq e^{-\Theta\,t}\,\mathbb{W}_{2}(\rho^{\alpha}_{0},\rho^{\alpha}_{*})^{2}\quad\forall\,t\geq 0

and
\Theta
depends on
\lambda,\sigma,\kappa
and
L_{\mathfrak{m}}
. In particular, the invariant measure
\rho^{\alpha}_{*}
of (
1.6
) is unique.

We refer to Remark
3.6
below for a precise definition of the smallness of the parameters.

Remark 3.4
.

The standard CBO (
1.5
) (i.e. when
\kappa=1
and
\delta=0
in (
1.6
)) admits infinitely many invariant measures: see
[
33
, Remark 1.1]
.

An immediate consequence of Theorem
3.3
and Lemma
2.8
is the following corollary.

Corollary 3.5
.

In the situation of Theorem
3.3
, it holds

|\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{t})-\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{*})|\leq L_{\mathfrak{m}}e^{-\frac{\Theta}{2}\,t}\,\mathbb{W}_{2}(\rho^{\alpha}_{0},\rho^{\alpha}_{*})\quad\forall\,t\geq 0.

In particular, one has
\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{t})=\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{*})
.

Proof of Theorem
3.3
.

Let us consider two processes governed by the same dynamics, but starting from different initially distributed positions. The first one is (
1.6
) which we recall is

(3.2)

\displaystyle{\rm d}{\overline{X}}_{t}=

\displaystyle-\lambda\left({\overline{X}}_{t}-\kappa\,\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{t})\right)\,{\rm d}t\,+\,\sigma\left(\delta\,\mathds{I}_{d}+D\left({\overline{X}}_{t}-\kappa\,\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{t})\right)\right)\,{\rm d}B_{t}

\displaystyle\text{with }\;\rho^{\alpha}_{t}\,:=\,\text{Law}({\overline{X}}_{t})\quad\text{ and }\quad{\overline{X}}_{0}\sim\rho^{\alpha}_{0}.

We also have, thanks to Theorem
2.6
,
\operatorname{Law}(X_{t})=\rho^{\alpha}_{t}\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for all
t\geq 0
.

The second process is the stationary counter-part of (
3.2
), whose initial condition has the invariant measure as distribution, and that is

(3.3)

\displaystyle{\rm d}\overline{Z}_{t}=

\displaystyle-\lambda\left(\overline{Z}_{t}-\kappa\,\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{*})\right)\,{\rm d}t\,+\,\sigma\left(\delta\,\mathds{I}_{d}+D\left(\overline{Z}_{t}-\kappa\,\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{*})\right)\right)\,{\rm d}B_{t}

\displaystyle\text{with }\;\rho^{\alpha}_{*}\,:=\,\text{Law}(\overline{Z}_{t})\quad\forall\,t\geq 0.

Indeed, by definition of
\rho^{\alpha}_{*}
being an invariant measure for (
3.2
), the law of
\overline{Z}_{t}
remains equal to
\rho^{\alpha}_{*}
for all
t\geq 0
, hence the process is stationary.

We have

\displaystyle{\rm d}(\overline{X}_{t}-\overline{Z}_{t})

\displaystyle=-\lambda\bigg((\overline{X}_{t}-\overline{Z}_{t})-\kappa\big(\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{t})-\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{*})\big)\bigg)\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad+\sigma\bigg(D(\overline{X}_{t}-\kappa\,\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{t}))-D(\overline{Z}_{t}-\kappa\,\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{*}))\bigg)\,{\rm d}B_{t}.

As in the proof of Theorem
2.6
, we use the function
h:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, such that
h(z)=\frac{1}{2}|z|^{2}
, and we apply Itô’s formula (
2.5
) which leads to

\displaystyle{\rm d}h(\overline{X}_{t}-\overline{Z}_{t})=

\displaystyle\bigg\{-\lambda(\overline{X}_{t}-\overline{Z}_{t})\cdot\big[(\overline{X}_{t}-\overline{Z}_{t})-\kappa(\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{t})-\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{*}))\big]

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\,\text{trace}\bigg[\bigg(D(\overline{X}_{t}-\kappa\,\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{t}))-D(\overline{Z}_{t}-\kappa\,\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{*}))\bigg)^{2}\bigg]\bigg\}\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\sigma(\overline{X}_{t}-\overline{Z}_{t})\cdot\bigg[D(\overline{X}_{t}-\kappa\,\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{t}))-D(\overline{Z}_{t}-\kappa\,\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{*}))\bigg]\,{\rm d}B_{t}

\displaystyle=:\mathbf{F}_{t}\,{\rm d}t+\mathbf{G}_{t}\,{\rm d}B_{t}.

The same arguments as in the beginning of the proof of
[
33
, Theorem 2.4]
(see in particular the proof of
[
33
, equation (2.6)]
), allow us to write

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}{\mathbb{E}}\left[|\overline{X}_{t}-\overline{Z}_{t}|^{2}\right]=2\,{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathbf{F}_{t}\right]

\displaystyle\quad=-2\,\lambda\,{\mathbb{E}}\left[|\overline{X}_{t}-\overline{Z}_{t}|^{2}\right]+2\,\lambda\,\kappa\,{\mathbb{E}}\left[(\overline{X}_{t}-\overline{Z}_{t})\cdot\big(\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{t})-\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{*})\big)\right]

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad+\sigma^{2}\,{\mathbb{E}}\left[\text{trace}\bigg(\big(D(\overline{X}_{t}-\kappa\,\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{t}))-D(\overline{Z}_{t}-\kappa\,\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{*}))\big)^{2}\bigg)\right].

We can now estimate each of the terms above. We have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[(\overline{X}_{t}-\overline{Z}_{t})\cdot\big(\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{t})-\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{*})\big)\right]\leq\frac{1}{2}{\mathbb{E}}\left[|\overline{X}_{t}-\overline{Z}_{t}|^{2}\right]+\frac{1}{2}|\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{t})-\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{*})|^{2}

and

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\text{trace}\bigg(\big(D(\overline{X}_{t}-\kappa\,\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{t}))-D(\overline{Z}_{t}-\kappa\,\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{*}))\big)^{2}\bigg)\right]

\displaystyle\quad=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{d}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\bigg(|\{\overline{X}_{t}-\kappa\,\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{t})\}_{k}|-|\{\overline{Z}_{t}-\kappa\,\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{*})\}_{k}|\bigg)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\quad\leq\sum\limits_{k=1}^{d}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\big\{\overline{X}_{t}-\overline{Z}_{t}\big\}_{k}-\kappa\,\{\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{t})-\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{*})\big\}_{k}\right|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\quad\leq 2\sum\limits_{k=1}^{d}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\big\{\overline{X}_{t}-\overline{Z}_{t}\big\}_{k}\right|^{2}+\kappa^{2}\,\left|\{\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{t})-\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{*})\big\}_{k}\right|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\quad\leq 2\,{\mathbb{E}}\left[|\overline{X}_{t}-\overline{Z}_{t}|^{2}\right]+2\,\kappa^{2}\,|\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{t})-\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{*})|^{2}.

Therefore we have

(3.4)

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}{\mathbb{E}}\left[|\overline{X}_{t}-\overline{Z}_{t}|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq(-2\,\lambda+\lambda\,\kappa\,+2\,\sigma^{2})\,{\mathbb{E}}\left[|\overline{X}_{t}-\overline{Z}_{t}|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle+(\lambda\,\kappa\,+2\,\sigma^{2}\,\kappa^{2})\,|\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{t})-\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{*})|^{2}.

For sufficiently small values of
\kappa
and
\sigma
, one guarantees
-2\,\lambda+\lambda\,\kappa\,+2\,\sigma^{2}<0
. Let us define
\theta:=2\,\lambda-\lambda\,\kappa\,-2\,\sigma^{2}>0
, and
\gamma:=\lambda\,\kappa\,+2\,\sigma^{2}\,\kappa^{2}
.
Therefore, Grönwall’s inequality yields

(3.5)

\mathbb{E}\left[\,|\overline{X}_{t}-\overline{Z}_{t}|^{2}\,\right]\leq e^{-\theta\,t}\mathbb{E}\left[\,|\overline{X}_{0}-\overline{Z}_{0}|^{2}\,\right]+\gamma\int_{0}^{t}|\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{s})-\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{*})|^{2}\,e^{-\theta(t-s)}\,\mbox{\rm d}s.

Using
\mathbb{W}_{2}(\mu,\nu)^{2}=\inf\limits_{(Y\sim\mu,\,Y^{\prime}\sim\nu)}\mathbb{E}[|Y-Y^{\prime}|^{2}]
, we can take the infimum first in the left-hand side of (
3.5
), then in the right-hand side, and obtain

\mathbb{W}_{2}(\rho^{\alpha}_{t},\rho^{\alpha}_{*})^{2}\leq e^{-\theta\,t}\mathbb{W}_{2}(\rho^{\alpha}_{0},\rho^{\alpha}_{*})^{2}+\gamma\int_{0}^{t}|\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{s})-\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{*})|^{2}\,e^{-\theta(t-s)}\,\mbox{\rm d}s.

We are left with the last term which we estimate as follows. Since
\rho^{*}_{\alpha}\in\mathscr{P}_{2,R}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we can use (
2.9
) and obtain

|\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{t})-\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{*})|\leq L_{\mathfrak{m}}\mathbb{W}_{2}(\rho^{\alpha}_{t},\rho^{\alpha}_{*}).

Subsequently, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{W}_{2}(\rho^{\alpha}_{t},\rho^{\alpha}_{*})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\theta\,t}\mathbb{W}_{2}(\rho^{\alpha}_{0},\rho^{\alpha}_{*})^{2}+\gamma\,L_{\mathfrak{m}}^{2}\,\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{W}_{2}(\rho^{\alpha}_{s},\rho^{\alpha}_{*})^{2}\,e^{-\theta(t-s)}\,\mbox{\rm d}s

3.4.
Harnack’s inequality

The result in (
3.7
) together with (
2.1
) ensure that
\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\cdot)
is well-defined (finite) when evaluated in the invariant probability measure
\rho_{*}^{\alpha}
. Namely, it holds that

(3.9)

|\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho_{*}^{\alpha})|\leq(b_{1}+b_{2}\rho_{*}^{\alpha}(|\cdot|^{2}))^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq(b_{1}+b_{2}(\rho_{0}(|\cdot|^{2})+C_{2}))^{\frac{1}{2}}<\infty\,.

Therefore, we can introduce the following matrix-valued and vector-valued functions defined for
(x,\mu)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times\mathscr{P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
by

(3.10)

\displaystyle A:=(a^{ij})_{i,j},\quad\text{ such that }\quad a^{ij}(x,\mu):=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}(\delta+|(x-\kappa\,\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\mu))_{i}|)^{2}\delta_{ij}

\displaystyle\mbox{ and }

\displaystyle b:=(b^{i})_{i},\quad\text{ such that }\quad b^{i}(x,\mu):=-\lambda(x-\kappa\,\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\mu))_{i}

\delta_{ij}
being the Kronecker symbol whose value is
0
whenever
i\neq j
(not to be confused with the positive constant
\delta
).
Moreover, the matrix
A
is positive–definite because it is a diagonal matrix whose diagonal entries satisfy
\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}(\delta+|(x-\kappa\,\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\mu))_{i}|)^{2}\geq\frac{(\sigma\delta)^{2}}{2}>0
.
In particular, when evaluated in the invariant probability measure
\rho_{*}^{\alpha}
, one obtains smooth coefficients (in
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
)

\displaystyle A_{*}:=(a^{ij}_{*})_{i,j},\quad\text{ such that }\quad a^{ij}_{*}(x):=a^{ij}(x,\rho_{*}^{\alpha})

and

\displaystyle b_{*}:=(b^{i}_{*})_{i},\quad\text{ such that }\quad b^{i}_{*}(x):=b^{i}(x,\rho_{*}^{\alpha}).

This identification is possible since the dependence of
A
and
b
on the measure is non-local – that is, it involves a quantity of the form
\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}K(y)\mbox{\rm d}\mu(y)
which is moreover constant in
x
(since the kernel
K
does not involve
x
)–, rather than a pointwise (local) evaluation of (the density of) the measure
\mu
. In other words, regularity of (the density of) the measure
\mu
does not influence regularity of the coefficients
A
and
b
.

More generally, given a measure
\nu\in\mathscr{P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, the quantity
|\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\nu)|
is well-defined (finite) thanks to (
2.1
), and we can define the following functions on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

\displaystyle A_{\nu}:=(a^{ij}_{\nu})_{i,j},\quad\text{ such that }\quad a^{ij}_{\nu}(x):=a^{ij}(x,\nu)

and

\displaystyle b_{\nu}:=(b^{i}_{\nu})_{i},\quad\text{ such that }\quad b^{i}_{\nu}(x):=b^{i}(x,\nu).

The diffusion (linear) operator whose coefficients are
A_{\nu}
and
b_{\nu}
is given by

L_{A,b,\nu}\psi(x)=\text{trace}\big(A_{\nu}(x)D^{2}\psi(x)\big)+b_{\nu}(x)\cdot\nabla\psi(x)\quad\forall\;\psi\in\mathcal{C}_{c}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}),

Its adjoint is denoted by
L_{A,b,\nu}^{*}
, and a measure
\mu
is solution to
L_{A,b,\nu}^{*}\mu=0
if it is a solution in the distributional sense that is

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}L_{A,b,\nu}\psi(x)\;\mbox{\rm d}\mu(x)=0\quad\forall\;\psi\in\mathcal{C}_{c}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}).

The diffusion (linear) operator whose coefficients are
A_{*}
and
b_{*}
will be denoted by
L_{A,b,*}
, and its adjoint is

. It should be noted that the choice of the test functions being

can be relaxed to include a larger space of functions but which we shall not do here for the sake of simplicity.

When we allow the coefficients
A
and

to be also functions of the measure argument, the resulting diffusion operator is non-linear. Analogously, we say that
\mu
is solution to
L_{A,b,\mu}^{*}\mu=0
if it is a solution in the distributional sense that is

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}L_{A,b,\mu}\psi(x)\;\mbox{\rm d}\mu(x)=0\quad\forall\;\psi\in\mathcal{C}_{c}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}).

The following result is well-known and can be found for example in

4.

We are now ready to state and prove our main results concerning the limit

to be minimized has a unique minimizer in §

Let us recall that our results hold without any prior assumptions on the initial distribution

Theorem 4.3
.

Assume
f(\cdot)
satisfies Assumptions
2.1

4.1

be the unique invariant probability measure of the rescaled CBO (

\eta_{*}^{\alpha}(\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}:|x-x_{*}|\geq\varepsilon\})\longrightarrow 0\quad\mbox{ as }\quad\alpha\to\infty.

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon>0
be fixed, and let us define

\overline{f}_{\varepsilon}:=\sup\{f(x):|x-x_{*}|\leq\varepsilon\}\mbox{ and }\underline{f}_{\varepsilon}:=\inf\{f(x):|x-x_{*}|\geq\varepsilon\}.

Then we compute

\displaystyle\eta_{*}^{\alpha}(\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}:|x-x_{*}|\geq\varepsilon\})

\displaystyle=\frac{\int_{|x-x_{*}|\geq\varepsilon}e^{-\alpha f(x)}\,\rho_{*}^{\alpha}(\mbox{\rm d}x)}{\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}e^{-\alpha f(x)}\,\rho_{*}^{\alpha}(\mbox{\rm d}x)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\int_{|x-x_{*}|\geq\varepsilon}e^{-\alpha(f(x)-\overline{f}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}})}\,\rho_{*}^{\alpha}(\mbox{\rm d}x)}{\int_{|x-x_{*}|\leq\varepsilon^{\prime}}e^{-\alpha(f(x)-\overline{f}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}})}\,\rho_{*}^{\alpha}(\mbox{\rm d}x)}.

• In
\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}:|x-x_{*}|\geq\varepsilon\}
, we have

, hence

\displaystyle e^{-\alpha(f(x)-\overline{f}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}})}\leq e^{-\alpha(\underline{f}_{\varepsilon}-\overline{f}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}})}\leq e^{-\alpha(\overline{f}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}-\underline{f})}

and
\rho_{*}^{\alpha}
being a probability measure, we have

\displaystyle\int_{|x-x_{*}|\geq\varepsilon}e^{-\alpha(f(x)-\overline{f}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}})}\,\rho_{*}^{\alpha}(\mbox{\rm d}x)\leq e^{-\alpha(\overline{f}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}-\underline{f})}\;\to 0\;\mbox{ when }\;\alpha\to\infty.

• In

, hence

\displaystyle\int_{|x-x_{*}|\leq\varepsilon^{\prime}}e^{-\alpha(f(x)-\overline{f}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}})}\,\rho_{*}^{\alpha}(\mbox{\rm d}x)\geq\rho_{*}^{\alpha}(B_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}(x_{*}))\geq C_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}>0

Finally, one gets

\displaystyle\eta_{*}^{\alpha}(\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}:|x-x_{*}|\geq\varepsilon\})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{e^{-\alpha(\overline{f}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}-\underline{f})}}{C_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}}\,\;\to 0\;\mbox{ when }\;\alpha\to\infty.

\rho^{\alpha}_{s}:=\text{Law}({\overline{X}}_{s})
.
In particular, when choosing as initial distribution
\text{Law}({\overline{X}}_{0})=\rho_{*}^{\alpha}
, then it holds

\text{Law}({\overline{X}}_{t})=\rho_{*}^{\alpha}
for all
t>0
since the latter probability is invariant.

Let us now consider the (stationary) process (

) with initial distribution
{\overline{X}}_{0}\sim\rho_{*}^{\alpha}
. It is well-posed thanks to (
3.9
). Then taking the expectation

4.2.
Multiple–minimizer

The strategy developed in the previous subsection can be further generalized to a set of minimizers that is neither a unique singleton, nor a collection of discrete points. More specifically, we shall drop the assumption on
f(\cdot)
having a unique minimizer, and we consider the following.

Assumption 4.5
.

The function
f(\cdot)
attains its minimal value on a set
\mathcal{M}=\cup_{i=1}^{m}\,\mathcal{O}_{i}
, with
1\leq m<\infty
and where every
\mathcal{O}_{i}
is a compact and connected subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

On each
\mathcal{O}_{i}
, the function
f(\cdot)
is constant equal to its minimal value. When
\mathcal{O}_{i}
is not a singleton, the graph (landscape) of
f(\cdot)
is flat, which is why these are sometimes called
flat minimizers
. It is also worth mentioning that a particular example of Assumption
4.5
is when
f(\cdot)
has
m
global minimizers
\{x_{*}^{1},\dots,x_{*}^{m}\}=:\mathcal{M}
. In this case,
\mathcal{O}_{i}=\{x_{*}^{i}\}
.

Remark 4.6
.

The function
f(\cdot)
being continuous and coercive thanks to Assumption
2.1
, for all arbitrary
\varepsilon>0
it holds that

(4.6)

\inf\{\,f(x):x\in\mathcal{M}^{c}_{\varepsilon}\,\}>\underline{f}\,

where

\mathcal{M}_{\varepsilon}:=\cup_{i=1}^{m}\,\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}:\mathrm{dist}(x,\mathcal{O}_{i})\leq\varepsilon\}

and
\mathcal{M}^{c}_{\varepsilon}={\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus\mathcal{M}_{\varepsilon}
its complement. Here,
\mathrm{dist}(x,\mathcal{O})
is the Euclidean distance between a point
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and a compact set
\mathcal{O}
, and which is equal to
0
when
x\in\mathcal{O}
.

Remark 4.7
.

Property (
4.6
), which in the case of a single minimizer is (
4.1
), has already been discussed in
[
1
, equation (H)]
; see
[
1
, Remark 2]
for a counter-example, and a variation in
[
2
, Assumption (2.3)]
. A qualitative interpretation can be found in
[
39
, Assumption (A3)]
and it is also used in
[
38
, (A3)]
where several examples of optimization problems satisfying this condition are provided. All of the works cited in this remark invoke this property in their respective treatments of global optimization, thereby emphasizing its natural place as a standard assumption.

The first result we need in the sequel is the following tightness of the weighted probability measures. Recall that existence and uniqueness of the invariant measure
\rho_{*}^{\alpha}
were the object of Theorem
3.1
and Theorem
3.3
(see also Remark
3.6
).

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
\rho_{*}^{\alpha}
be the unique invariant probability measure of the rescaled CBO (
1.6
), and let
\eta_{*}^{\alpha}
be defined as in (
4.2
).
Then
\{\eta_{*}^{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\geq 1}
is tight.

Proof.

It follows from (
2.1
) and (
3.7
)

\eta_{*}^{\alpha}(|\cdot|^{2})\leq b_{1}+b_{2}\,\rho_{*}^{\alpha}(|\cdot|^{2})\leq b_{1}+b_{2}\big(\rho_{0}(|\cdot|^{2})+C_{2}\big)=:C_{*}\,.

Therefore, for any
\varepsilon
, there exists a compact set
K_{\varepsilon}:=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\,:\,|x|^{2}\leq\frac{C_{*}}{\varepsilon}\}
such that, by Markov’s inequality, it holds

\eta_{*}^{\alpha}(K_{\varepsilon}^{c})=\eta_{*}^{\alpha}\left(\left\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\,:\,|x|^{2}>\frac{C_{*}}{\varepsilon}\right\}\right)\leq\frac{\varepsilon\,\eta_{*}^{\alpha}(|\cdot|^{2})}{C_{*}}\leq\varepsilon.

∎

Thanks to the latter Lemma, one applies Prokhorov’s theorem and concludes that there exist a subsequence
\{\eta_{*}^{\alpha_{k}}\}_{k\geq 1}
and a probability measure
\eta_{*}\in\mathscr{P}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
such that

\eta_{*}^{\alpha_{k}}
converges weakly to
\eta_{*}
as
k\to\infty
. In the following we still use the notation
\eta_{*}^{\alpha}
to denote the subsequence.

The next theorem can be viewed as Laplace’s principle in the case of multiple non-trivial minimizers.

Theorem 4.9
.

Let
f(\cdot)
satisfy Assumptions
2.1
and
4.5
, and let
\rho_{*}^{\alpha}
be the unique invariant probability measure of the rescaled CBO (
1.6
). Let
\eta_{*}^{\alpha}
be defined as in (
4.2
).
Then for all
\varepsilon>0
, we have

\eta_{*}^{\alpha}(\mathcal{M}_{\varepsilon})\;\to 1\;\mbox{ when }\;\alpha\to\infty.

Moreover, when
\alpha\to\infty
, any weak limit
\eta_{*}
of the sequence
\{\eta_{*}^{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\geq 1}
concentrates on
\mathcal{M}
.

Proof.

For any
\varepsilon>0
, let us define

\overline{f}_{\varepsilon}:=\sup\{f(x):x\in\mathcal{M}_{\varepsilon}\}\mbox{ and }\underline{f}_{\varepsilon}:=\inf\{f(x):x\in\mathcal{M}_{\varepsilon}^{c}\}

where
\mathcal{M}_{\varepsilon}
is as defined in Remark
4.6
.
Since
f
is continuous, we know that for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists some
\varepsilon^{\prime}>0
such that

(4.7)

\overline{f}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\leq\frac{1}{2}(\underline{f}_{\varepsilon}+\underline{f}),\mbox{ that is }\quad 2(\overline{f}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}-\underline{f})\leq(\underline{f}_{\varepsilon}-\underline{f})\,.

Then we compute

\displaystyle\eta_{*}^{\alpha}(\mathcal{M}_{\varepsilon}^{c})

\displaystyle=\frac{\int_{\mathcal{M}_{\varepsilon}^{c}}e^{-\alpha f(x)}\,\rho_{*}^{\alpha}(\mbox{\rm d}x)}{\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}e^{-\alpha f(x)}\,\rho_{*}^{\alpha}(\mbox{\rm d}x)}\;\leq\frac{\int_{\mathcal{M}_{\varepsilon}^{c}}e^{-\alpha(f(x)-\overline{f}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}})}\,\rho_{*}^{\alpha}(\mbox{\rm d}x)}{\int_{\mathcal{M}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}}e^{-\alpha(f(x)-\overline{f}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}})}\,\rho_{*}^{\alpha}(\mbox{\rm d}x)}.

• In the set
\mathcal{M}_{\varepsilon}^{c}
, we have
f(x)\geq\underline{f}_{\varepsilon}
, hence
e^{-\alpha(f(x)-\overline{f}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}})}\leq e^{-\alpha(\underline{f}_{\varepsilon}-\overline{f}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}})}
. Moreover using (
4.7
), it holds

\underline{f}_{\varepsilon}-\overline{f}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}=(\underline{f}_{\varepsilon}-\underline{f})-(\overline{f}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}-\underline{f})\geq(\overline{f}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}-\underline{f})>0.

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle e^{-\alpha(f(x)-\overline{f}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}})}\leq e^{-\alpha(\underline{f}_{\varepsilon}-\overline{f}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}})}\leq e^{-\alpha(\overline{f}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}-\underline{f})}

and
\rho_{*}^{\alpha}
being a probability measure, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{M}_{\varepsilon}^{c}}e^{-\alpha(f(x)-\overline{f}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}})}\,\rho_{*}^{\alpha}(\mbox{\rm d}x)\leq e^{-\alpha(\overline{f}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}-\underline{f})}\;\to 0\;\mbox{ when }\;\alpha\to\infty.

• In the set
\mathcal{M}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}
, we have
f(x)\leq\overline{f}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}
, hence
e^{-\alpha(f(x)-\overline{f}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}})}\geq 1
, and

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{M}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}}e^{-\alpha(f(x)-\overline{f}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}})}\,\rho_{*}^{\alpha}(\mbox{\rm d}x)\geq\rho_{*}^{\alpha}(\mathcal{M}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}})\geq C_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}>0

where in the last inequality
C_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}
comes from Lemma
3.10
, which is independent of
\alpha
.

Finally, one gets

(4.8)

\eta_{*}^{\alpha}(\mathcal{M}_{\varepsilon}^{c})\leq\frac{e^{-\alpha(\overline{f}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}-\underline{f})}}{C_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}}\,\;\to 0\;\mbox{ when }\;\alpha\to\infty.

It follows from the fact that
\eta_{*}^{\alpha}
converges weakly to
\eta_{*}
as
\alpha\to\infty
(Lemma
4.8
), that

\eta_{*}(A)\leq\liminf_{\alpha\to\infty}\eta_{*}^{\alpha}(A)

for any open set
A\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. It is obvious that

\mathcal{M}_{\varepsilon}^{c}
is an open set. Thus we have
\eta_{*}(\mathcal{M}_{\varepsilon}^{c})\leq 0\,
, which implies that
\eta_{*}(\mathcal{M}_{\varepsilon})=1\,
.
Since
\varepsilon
can be made arbitrarily small, we have indeed proved that
\eta_{*}
concentrates on
\mathcal{M}
, that is
\eta_{*}(\mathcal{M})=1
.
∎

As a consequence of the previous theorem, we obtain the following convergence when the objective function
f(\cdot)
has multiple non-trivial minimizers. It indicates that when
f(\cdot)
admits multiple global minimizers, the consensus point
\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{t})
(or the rescaled mean
\kappa^{-1}\,{\mathbb{E}}\left[\,{\overline{X}}_{t}\,\right]
) of the CBO dynamics converges to a point in
\mathcal{M}
, that is to one of the global minimizers.

Corollary 4.10
.

In the situation of Theorem
4.9
, the following hold

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{\alpha\to\infty}\,\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}\operatorname{dist}\!\big(\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{t}),\,\mathcal{M}\big)=\lim\limits_{\alpha\to\infty}\operatorname{dist}\!\big(\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{*}),\,\mathcal{M}\big)=0,

\displaystyle\text{and}

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{\alpha\to\infty}\,\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}\operatorname{dist}\!\left(\kappa^{-1}\,{\mathbb{E}}\left[\,{\overline{X}}_{t}\,\right],\,\mathcal{M}\right)=0.

Proof.

Let
{\overline{X}}_{.}
be the solution to the rescaled CBO (
1.6
), and let
\rho_{*}^{\alpha}
be its unique invariant probability measure whose existence and uniqueness are given by Theorem
3.1
and Theorem
3.3
(see also Remark
3.6
for a discussion on the parameters). Besides Assumption
2.1
, we are assuming
f(\cdot)
to satisfy Assumption
4.5
as well, and recall
\mathcal{M}
being the set of its minimizers.
We proceed in several steps.

Step 1. (We show
\lim\limits_{\alpha\to\infty}\operatorname{dist}(\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{*}),\,\mathcal{M})=0
.)

Recall

\eta_{*}^{\alpha}(A)=\frac{\int_{A}e^{-\alpha f(x)}\rho_{*}^{\alpha}(\mbox{\rm d}x)}{\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}e^{-\alpha f(y)}\rho_{*}^{\alpha}(\mbox{\rm d}y)},\quad A\in\mathcal{B}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{*})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\,x\;\eta_{*}^{\alpha}(\mbox{\rm d}x).

We have

(4.9)

\displaystyle\operatorname{dist}(\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{*}),\,\mathcal{M})

\displaystyle=\inf\limits_{z\in\mathcal{M}}|\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{*})-z|

\displaystyle=\inf\limits_{z\in\mathcal{M}}\left|\int_{\mathbb{R^{d}}}x\;\eta_{*}^{\alpha}(\mbox{\rm d}x)-z\right|\leq\inf\limits_{z\in\mathcal{M}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x-z|\;\eta_{*}^{\alpha}(\mbox{\rm d}x).

Now we prove that

(4.10)

\inf\limits_{z\in\mathcal{M}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x-z|\;\eta_{*}^{\alpha}(\mbox{\rm d}x)\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\operatorname{dist}(x,\mathcal{M})\,\eta_{*}^{\alpha}(\mbox{\rm d}x).

Let
\varepsilon>0
, and let
z_{\varepsilon}
be a quasi-minimizer for
\min\limits_{z\in\mathcal{M}}|x-z|=\operatorname{dist}(x,\mathcal{M})
, that is

|x-z_{\varepsilon}|\leq\varepsilon+\operatorname{dist}(x,\mathcal{M}).

Then one gets

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x-z_{\varepsilon}|\,\eta_{*}^{\alpha}(\mbox{\rm d}x)\leq\varepsilon+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\operatorname{dist}(x,\mathcal{M})\,\eta_{*}^{\alpha}(\mbox{\rm d}x).

Moreover, the following always holds

\inf\limits_{z\in\mathcal{M}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x-z|\,\eta_{*}^{\alpha}(\mbox{\rm d}x)\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x-z_{\varepsilon}|\,\eta_{*}^{\alpha}(\mbox{\rm d}x).

Combining the latter two inequalities, one obtains

\inf\limits_{z\in\mathcal{M}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x-z|\,\eta_{*}^{\alpha}(\mbox{\rm d}x)\leq\varepsilon+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\operatorname{dist}(x,\mathcal{M})\,\eta_{*}^{\alpha}(\mbox{\rm d}x).

These quantities being independent from
\varepsilon
, we can send
\varepsilon\to 0
and get the desired result (
4.10
).

Next, we continue with (
4.9
) which together with (
4.10
) yield

\displaystyle\operatorname{dist}(\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{*}),\,\mathcal{M})

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\operatorname{dist}(x,\mathcal{M})\,\eta_{*}^{\alpha}(\mbox{\rm d}x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{M}_{\varepsilon}}\operatorname{dist}(x,\mathcal{M})\,\eta_{*}^{\alpha}(\mbox{\rm d}x)+\int_{\mathcal{M}_{\varepsilon}^{c}}\operatorname{dist}(x,\mathcal{M})\,\eta_{*}^{\alpha}(\mbox{\rm d}x)

where
\varepsilon>0
is arbitrarily fixed, and
\mathcal{M}_{\varepsilon}
is as defined in Remark
4.6
.

The integrand in the first integral is upper-bounded by
\varepsilon
since
x\in\mathcal{M}_{\varepsilon}
, which means
\operatorname{dist}(x,\mathcal{M})\leq\varepsilon
. Thus we have
\int_{\mathcal{M}_{\varepsilon}}\operatorname{dist}(x,\mathcal{M})\,\eta_{*}^{\alpha}(\mbox{\rm d}x)\leq\varepsilon
.

For the second integral, we shall proceed as in the proof of the second statement in Corollary
4.4
.
Using Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{M}_{\varepsilon}^{c}}\operatorname{dist}(x,\mathcal{M})\,\eta_{*}^{\alpha}(\mbox{\rm d}x)

\displaystyle\leq\bigg(\eta_{*}^{\alpha}(\mathcal{M}_{\varepsilon}^{c})\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}\bigg(\int_{\mathcal{M}_{\varepsilon}^{c}}\operatorname{dist}(x,\mathcal{M})^{2}\,\eta_{*}^{\alpha}(\mbox{\rm d}x)\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\bigg(\eta_{*}^{\alpha}(\mathcal{M}_{\varepsilon}^{c})\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}\bigg(2\,\int_{\mathcal{M}_{\varepsilon}^{c}}|x|^{2}+|z|^{2}\,\eta_{*}^{\alpha}(\mbox{\rm d}x)\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}\quad\text{ for some }z\in\mathcal{M}

\displaystyle\leq\bigg(\eta_{*}^{\alpha}(\mathcal{M}_{\varepsilon}^{c})\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}\bigg(2\,|z|^{2}+2\,\eta_{*}^{\alpha}(|\cdot|^{2})\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Therefore, we obtain

\displaystyle\operatorname{dist}(\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{*}),\,\mathcal{M})\leq\varepsilon+\bigg(\eta_{*}^{\alpha}(\mathcal{M}_{\varepsilon}^{c})\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}\bigg(2\,|z|^{2}+2\,\eta_{*}^{\alpha}(|\cdot|^{2})\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Recalling (
2.1
) and (
3.7
), the second moment
\eta_{*}^{\alpha}(|\cdot|^{2})
is bounded as in (
4.5
). Finally, using Theorem
4.9
(see in particular (
4.8
)), we know that
\eta_{*}^{\alpha}(\mathcal{M}_{\varepsilon}^{c})\,\to 0\,
as
\alpha\to\infty
. Here
\varepsilon
being arbitrarily chosen, this yields
\lim\limits_{\alpha\to\infty}\operatorname{dist}(\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{*}),\,\mathcal{M})=0
.

Step 2. (We show
\lim\limits_{\alpha\to\infty}\,\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}\operatorname{dist}(\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{t}),\,\mathcal{M})=0
.)

We shall use Corollary
3.5
, together with the result in
Step 1
. Indeed, using the triangular inequality, one has

\operatorname{dist}(\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{t}),\,\mathcal{M})\leq\operatorname{dist}(\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{*}),\,\mathcal{M})+|\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{t})-\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{*})|.

It suffices now to take the limit first as
t\to\infty
using Corollary
3.5
, then send
\alpha\to\infty
using the conclusion of
Step 1
above.

Step 3. (We show
\lim\limits_{\alpha\to\infty}\,\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}\operatorname{dist}\left(\kappa^{-1}\,{\mathbb{E}}\left[\,{\overline{X}}_{t}\,\right],\,\mathcal{M}\right)=0
.)

Using the triangular inequality, one has

\operatorname{dist}\left(\kappa^{-1}\,{\mathbb{E}}\left[\,{\overline{X}}_{t}\,\right],\,\mathcal{M}\right)\leq\operatorname{dist}(\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{*}),\,\mathcal{M})+|\kappa^{-1}\,{\mathbb{E}}\left[\,{\overline{X}}_{t}\,\right]-\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{*})|.

The conclusion thus follows using the first statement of Corollary
4.4
which holds under Assumption
2.1
, together with
Step 1
above.
∎

Several additional numerical experiments (using dimensions up to 20 and a variety of objective functions) are reported in
[
31
, Section 5]
. In fact, the optimization problem studied in
[
31
]
is multi-level such as

\displaystyle\min\limits_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\,F(x,y)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}\quad y\in\argmin_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\,G(x,y),

where
F,G:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
for some
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
.
In order to fit it into the present framework, it suffices to take the objective function
F
to be constant (a simplification) and drop the dependence on its corresponding
x
variable (by setting it to a fixed constant) in the objective function
G
. The resulting optimization problem becomes

\left.\begin{aligned} &\min\limits_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\quad 1\\
&\quad\text{s.t.}\quad y\in\argmin_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\,G(0,y)\end{aligned}\quad\right\}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad\min\limits_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\,\overline{G}(y)

where
\overline{G}(y):=G(0,y)
. Hence, the multi-scale dynamics
[
31
, equation (3.1)]
reduces to
(0,\mathds{Y}_{t})
where the process
\mathds{Y}
solves (
1.6
).
With this simplification, the model in
[
31
]
encompasses the one considered here, and the remainder of
[
31
, Section 5]
is fully compatible with our setting. Consequently, the numerical tests and the results obtained therein apply directly and would be identical in our case. See also
[
31
, Remark 5.1]
.

6.
Conclusion

6.1.
Summary of main findings

We have studied asymptotic properties of the rescaled CBO dynamics

{\rm d}{\overline{X}}_{t}=-\lambda\left({\overline{X}}_{t}-\kappa\,\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho_{t}^{\alpha})\right)\,{\rm d}t+\sigma\left(\delta\,\mathds{I}_{d}+D({\overline{X}}_{t}-\kappa\,\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho_{t}^{\alpha}))\right){\rm d}B_{t},\quad\rho_{t}^{\alpha}=\text{Law}({\overline{X}}_{t})

under suitable conditions on the parameters, for example those in Remark
3.6
.
We do not make any assumptions on the initial distribution
{\overline{X}}_{0}\sim\rho_{0}
besides satisfying
\rho_{0}\in\mathscr{P}_{4}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
. Supposing that
f(\cdot)
satisfies Assumption
2.1
:

•

We proved that such a process admits a unique invariant probability measure
\rho^{\alpha}_{*}
, and its law enjoys a
\mathbb{W}_{2}
–exponential contraction (
3.1
).

•

When
f(\cdot)
has a unique minimizer
x_{*}
, we proved that

\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha\to\infty}\lim_{t\to\infty}\;\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{t})=\lim_{\alpha\to\infty}\;\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{*})=x_{*},

\displaystyle\text{ and }

\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha\to\infty}\lim_{t\to\infty}\;\kappa^{-1}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\,{\overline{X}}_{t}\,\right]=\lim_{\alpha\to\infty}\;\kappa^{-1}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}x\,\mbox{\rm d}\rho^{\alpha}_{*}(x)\,=\,x_{*},

•

When
f(\cdot)
has multiple minimizers as
\mathcal{M}
, we proved that

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{\alpha\to\infty}\,\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}\operatorname{dist}(\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{t}),\,\mathcal{M})=\lim\limits_{\alpha\to\infty}\operatorname{dist}(\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\alpha}_{*}),\,\mathcal{M})=0,

\displaystyle\text{and}

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{\alpha\to\infty}\,\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}\operatorname{dist}\!\left(\kappa^{-1}\,{\mathbb{E}}\left[\,{\overline{X}}_{t}\,\right],\,\mathcal{M}\right)=0.

The absence of any conditions on the initial distribution is precisely what ensures the
global
nature of the convergence, making this result, to the best of our knowledge, the first –and, as of now, the only– one that guarantees global convergence as both time and inverse temperature
\alpha
tend to infinity.

6.2.
Future research directions

The present analysis paves the way for several extensions. In particular, we intend to investigate the following topics in future work:

•

A faithful convergence for the polarized CBO
[
11
,
27
]
to effectively find multiple minimizers (not only one of them as it is proved in the present paper).

•

An adaptive convergence where the growth of
\alpha
is a function of time.

•

An extension to stochastic optimization as in
[
6
,
4
]
, where the objective function depends on a random parameter.

•

A sharper qualitative and quantitative analysis on the values of the parameters
\kappa,\delta,N,\alpha,\sigma,T
. Some insights have been discussed in
[
31
, Section 5]
.

Appendix A
Existence of invariant measure

This section is devoted to verifying Theorem
3.1
on the existence of an invariant measure to the distribution-dependent SDE (DDSDE) (
1.6
) which we recall is

(A.1)

{\rm d}X_{t}=-\lambda(X_{t}-\kappa\,\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\operatorname{Law}(X_{t})){\rm d}t+\sigma\left(\delta\,\mathds{I}_{d}+D(X_{t}-\kappa\,\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\operatorname{Law}(X_{t}))\right){\rm d}B_{t}\,.

We use the following notation for brevity:
for
(x,\mu)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

b(x,\mu):=-\lambda(x-\kappa\,\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\mu)),\quad\text{ and }\quad\bm{\sigma}(x,\mu):=\sigma\delta\,\mathds{I}_{d}+\sigma D(x-\kappa\,\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\mu)),

where
\lambda,\sigma,\kappa,\alpha
are positive constants.

We shall provide a self-contained verification of the existence of an invariant measure to (
A.1
) which we recall was proven in
[
32
, Proposition 3.4]
(see also Appendix A.2 therein).
To do so, we shall verify the validity of the assumptions needed for the result of S.-Q. Zhang
[
46
, Theorem 2.2]
which will guarantee existence of an invariant measure in
\mathscr{P}_{2,R}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for some sufficiently large fixed
R>0
.

• Assumption
[
46
, (H1)]

We verify the validity of this assumption with
r_{1}=r_{2}=1
and
r_{3}=1+r_{2}=2
. Then we have, for all
\nu\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

\displaystyle 2\langle b(x,\nu),x\rangle+\|\bm{\sigma}(x,\nu)\|^{2}=2\langle-\lambda x+\lambda\,\kappa\,\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\nu),x\rangle+\text{trace}(\bm{\sigma}\bm{\sigma}(x,\mu)^{\top})

\displaystyle\quad\quad=-2\lambda|x|^{2}+2\lambda\,\kappa\,\langle x,\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\nu)\rangle+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\left(\sigma\,\delta+|\{x-\kappa\,\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\nu)\}_{i}|\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\leq-2\lambda|x|^{2}+\lambda\,\kappa\left(|x|^{2}+|\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\nu)|^{2}\right)+2\,d\,\left(\sigma\,\delta\right)^{2}+2|x-\kappa\,\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\nu)|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\leq-2\lambda|x|^{2}+\lambda\,\kappa\left(|x|^{2}+|\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\nu)|^{2}\right)+2\,d\,\left(\sigma\,\delta\right)^{2}+4|x|^{2}+4\,\kappa^{2}\,|\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\nu)|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\leq(-2\lambda+\lambda\,\kappa+4)|x|^{2}+(\lambda\,\kappa+4\,\kappa^{2})|\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\nu)|^{2}+2\,d\,(\sigma\,\delta)^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\leq-(2\lambda-\lambda\,\kappa-4)|x|^{2}+(\lambda\,\kappa+4\,\kappa^{2})|\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\nu)|^{2}+2\,d\,(\sigma\,\delta)^{2}

Using Lemma
2.3
, we can bound
|\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\nu)|^{2}
as in (
2.1
) and obtain

(A.2)

\displaystyle 2\langle b(x,\nu),x\rangle+\|\bm{\sigma}(x,\nu)\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq-(2\lambda-\lambda\,\kappa-4)|x|^{2}+(\lambda\,\kappa+4\,\kappa^{2})[b_{1}+b_{2}\nu(|\cdot|^{2})]+2\,d\,(\sigma\,\delta)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq-(2\lambda-\lambda\,\kappa-4)|x|^{2}+(\lambda\,\kappa+4\,\kappa^{2})\,b_{2}\,\nu(|\cdot|^{2})+2\,d\,(\sigma\,\delta)^{2}+b_{1}\,(\lambda\,\kappa+4\,\kappa^{2})

\displaystyle=-\widetilde{C}_{1}\,|x|^{2}+\widetilde{C}_{2}+\widetilde{C}_{3}\,\nu(|\cdot|^{2})

where

\displaystyle\widetilde{C}_{1}=2\lambda-\lambda\,\kappa-4,\qquad\widetilde{C}_{2}=2\,d\,(\sigma\,\delta)^{2}+b_{1}\,(\lambda\,\kappa+4\,\kappa^{2}),\qquad\widetilde{C}_{3}=(\lambda\,\kappa+4\,\kappa^{2})\,b_{2}.

We need to verify that
\widetilde{C}_{1}>0
and
\widetilde{C}_{1}>\widetilde{C}_{3}
. Observe that
\widetilde{C}_{3}=\kappa\,(\lambda+4\,\kappa)\,b_{2}=\mathcal{O}(\kappa)
which means that it can be made arbitrarily small when
0<\kappa\ll 1
. We have

(A.3)

\displaystyle\widetilde{C}_{1}>\widetilde{C}_{3}\;\Leftrightarrow\;2\lambda-\lambda\,\kappa-4>(\lambda\,\kappa+4\,\kappa^{2})\,b_{2}\;\Leftrightarrow\;2\lambda-4>\lambda\,\kappa\,(1+b_{2})+4\,\kappa^{2}b_{2}

Then,
sufficient
conditions are for example
\kappa<\frac{1}{2}(1+b_{2})^{-1}\;\text{ and }\;\lambda>4
, where
b_{2}
is as defined in (
2.2
). Indeed in this case, recalling
\kappa\in(0,1)
, one has

\displaystyle\left(\lambda\,\kappa(1+b_{2})<\frac{\lambda}{2}\right)\;\;\text{ and }\;\;\bigg(\kappa\,b_{2}<1\Rightarrow\;4\,\kappa^{2}\,b_{2}<2\bigg)\;\Rightarrow\;\lambda\,\kappa(1+b_{2})+4\kappa^{2}\,b_{2}<\frac{\lambda}{2}+2

Finally,
\lambda>4\Leftrightarrow 2\lambda-4>\frac{\lambda}{2}+2
together with the previous inequality guarantee (
A.3
). Moreover,
\lambda>4
together with
\kappa\in(0,1)
ensure
\widetilde{C}_{1}>0
.

• Assumption
[
46
, (H2.i)]

For
\nu
fixed in
\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we need to check that the drift and diffusion terms are locally Lipschitz, that is:

For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\nu\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, there exists
K_{n}>0
such that for all
|x|\vee|y|\leq n
we have

|b(x,\nu)-b(y,\nu)|+\|\bm{\sigma}(x,\nu)-\bm{\sigma}(y,\nu)\|\leq K_{n}|x-y|.

This is easily verified from the definition of
b
and
\bm{\sigma}
(in fact,
K_{n}=\lambda+\sigma
).

• Assumption
[
46
, (H2.ii)]

We need to check that the drift has a polynomial growth. More precisely we want to check that: there exists a locally bounded function
\mathfrak{h}:[0,+\infty)\to[0,+\infty)
such that

|b(x,\nu)|\leq\mathfrak{h}(\nu(|\cdot|^{2}))\,(1+|x|),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\;\nu\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

This holds true, noting that

\displaystyle|b(x,\nu)|

\displaystyle=\lambda|x-\kappa\,\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\nu)|\leq\lambda\big(|x|+\kappa|\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\mu)|\big)

\displaystyle\leq\lambda|x|+\lambda\kappa\bigg(b_{1}+b_{2}\,\nu(|\cdot|^{2})\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}},\quad\text{using Lemma \ref{lem: useful estimates}}

\displaystyle\leq\lambda(1+|x|)+\lambda\bigg(b_{1}+b_{2}\,\nu(|\cdot|^{2})\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}(1+|x|),\quad\text{using $\kappa<1$}

\displaystyle\leq\mathfrak{h}(\nu(|\cdot|^{2}))\,\big(1+|x|\big)

where we have set
\mathfrak{h}(\xi)=\lambda\left(1+(b_{1}+b_{2}\,\xi)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)
for every
\xi\in[0,+\infty)
.

• Assumption
[
46
, (H3)]

We need the drift and diffusion coefficients to be continued on
\mathscr{P}_{2,R}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
equipped with the Wasserstein metric. This is guaranteed thanks to Lemma
2.8
.

• Assumption
[
46
, (H4)]

We need the diffusion matrix to be non-degenerate on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, that is

\bm{\sigma}(x,\nu)\bm{\sigma}^{*}(x,\nu)>0,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\;\nu\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

This is guaranteed thanks to the additional term
\delta\,\mathds{I}_{d}
in the definition of
\bm{\sigma}
, noting that
D(\cdot)
is a non-negative matrix. Indeed, the matrix
\bm{\sigma}
is a diagonal matrix whose diagonal entries satisfy
\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}(\delta+|(x-\kappa\,\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\mu))_{i}|)^{2}\geq\frac{(\sigma\delta)^{2}}{2}>0
.

• Conclusion:

The latter assumptions being satisfied, we can then apply
[
46
, Theorem 2.2]
, which guarantees that the DDSDE (
A.1
) has a stationary distribution. In fact, from the proof in
[
46
]
(see the last line in page 8, and the lines between equation (2.22) and equation (2.23) in page 10), there exists
R_{0}>0
depending only on the constants of the problem (
\lambda,\kappa,\sigma,\delta
, together with
b_{1},b_{2}
defined by (
2.2
) in Lemma
2.3
) such that the stationary distribution exists in
\mathscr{P}_{2,R}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and
R\geq R_{0}
is determined by the initial distribution.

The precise conditions that the parameters need to satisfy in order to guarantee existence of an invariant measure are ruled by the inequality
\tilde{C_{1}}>\tilde{C}_{3}
that is (
A.3
). An example of sufficient conditions are
\kappa<\frac{1}{2(1+b_{2})}\;\text{ and }\;\lambda>4\;
where we recall
b_{2}
is as defined in (
2.2
).
