On the h h -polynomials of cyclotomic standard graded commutative algebras
Akihiro Higashitani, Kenta Ueyama

Abstract
. We call a standard graded commutative 𝕜 \Bbbk -algebra cyclotomic if its h h -polynomial has all its roots on the unit circle in the complex plane. 1.
Introduction

Throughout this paper, let
\Bbbk
denote a field. We say that
R
is a
standard graded

\Bbbk
-algebra
if
R=\bigoplus_{n\geq 0}R_{n}
is a commutative graded
\Bbbk
-algebra with
R_{0}=\Bbbk
and finitely generated in degree one.
(A standard graded
\Bbbk
-algebra is also called a
homogeneous

\Bbbk
-algebra in other literature.)

1.1.
Background

The Hilbert series of a (not necessarily commutative) graded
\Bbbk
-algebra is one of the most fundamental invariants in the study of graded
\Bbbk
-algebras.
It provides a concise analytic expression encoding the dimensions of the homogeneous components and thus captures the growth and structural features of the
\Bbbk
-algebra as a
\Bbbk
-vector space.
It is a classical and well-known fact that if a standard graded
\Bbbk
-algebra is Cohen-Macaulay, then the numerator polynomial of its Hilbert series, called the
h
-polynomial
, has all positive coefficients.
This fact is one of the cornerstones in graded commutative algebra.
Furthermore, several algebraic properties can be effectively characterized in terms of the Hilbert series.
A prominent theorem is Stanley’s characterization of the Gorenstein property via the symmetry of the Hilbert series (
[
10
]
),
which has inspired subsequent studies establishing necessary conditions for generalized notions of Gorensteinness,
such as almost Gorensteinness (
[
4
]
), nearly Gorensteinness (
[
9
]
), and Artin-Schelter Gorensteinness (
[
5
]
).

It is also known that when a standard graded
\Bbbk
-algebra is a complete intersection, its Hilbert series takes a particularly simple form.
In fact, for a positive integer
m
, let

\Psi_{m}(t)=1+t+\cdots+t^{m-1}.

Then the
h
-polynomial of a standard graded complete intersection is of the form of a product of a finite collection of
\Psi_{m}(t)
’s (see Proposition
2.1
).
In addition, the Hilbert series also serves as an essential tool in investigating noncommutative graded complete intersections (see
[
6
,
7
]
).

1.2.
Cyclotomic standard graded
\Bbbk
-algebras and polynomials of type CI

In recent years, increasing attention has been directed to the finer analytic behavior of the Hilbert series of standard graded
\Bbbk
-algebras, particularly the roots of their
h
-polynomials.
These roots encode subtle structural information and sometimes admit striking combinatorial interpretations
(see, e.g., the survey
[
2
]
for combinatorial consequences of the real-rootedness of the numerator polynomials of generating series).

Motivated by this perspective, we focus on the following notion for standard graded
\Bbbk
-algebras:

Definition 1.1

(cf.
[
1
,
6
]
)
.

A standard graded
\Bbbk
-algebra
R
is called
cyclotomic
if all roots of its
h
-polynomial
h_{R}(t)
lie on the unit circle in the complex plane.

Monic polynomials with integer coefficients whose roots all lie on the unit circle are sometimes called
Kronecker polynomials
.
It is known that every such polynomial can be expressed as a product of cyclotomic polynomials.
Note that the
h
-polynomials of cyclotomic
\Bbbk
-algebras are
not necessarily cyclotomic polynomials themselves
, but rather products of cyclotomic polynomials.
We say that a polynomial is
of type CI
if it can be written as a product of
\Psi_{m}(t)
’s.
In particular, the
h
-polynomials of standard graded complete intersections are of type CI.
However, the converse does not hold in general; see, for example,
[
10
, Example 3.7]
.
Borzi and D’Alì showed that the converse does hold when
R
is Koszul
[
1
, Theorem 3.9]
.
Moreover, it was proved that a cyclotomic polynomial
\Phi_{m}(t)
coincides with
h_{R}(t)
of some standard graded
\Bbbk
-algebra
R
if and only if
m
is prime and
R
is a hypersurface
[
1
, Theorem 4.3]
.

Despite these results, in general, one cannot expect the ring-theoretic structure of
R
to be determined by the cyclotomicity of its
h
-polynomial.
This naturally leads to the following question:

Question 1.2
.

Which polynomials can occur as the
h
-polynomials of cyclotomic standard graded
\Bbbk
-algebras, other than those of type CI?

For example,
[
1
, Theorem 4.3]
indicates that a single cyclotomic polynomial
\Phi_{m}(t)
is rarely equal to the
h
-polynomial of a standard graded
\Bbbk
-algebra.

1.3.
Main Results

The aim of this paper is to make a contribution to this question.
Our main result is stated as follows.

Theorem 1.3

(Main Result)
.

(1) The
h
-polynomial
h_{R}(t)
of any cyclotomic standard graded
\Bbbk
-algebra
R
is of type CI if
h_{R}(1)\in\{1,4,6\}
or
h_{R}(1)
is prime.

(2) There exists a cyclotomic standard graded
\Bbbk
-algebra
R
whose
h
-polynomial
h_{R}(t)
is not of type CI and satisfies
h_{R}(1)=n
if
n
is a non-prime number at least
8
.

Note that
h_{R}(1)
is nothing but the multiplicity of
R
(see
[
3
, Proposition 4.1.9]
), which is one of the important invariants of graded modules.

Structure of the paper

2

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we prepare several preliminary notions and results used throughout this paper.

2.1.
Hilbert series of standard graded
\Bbbk
-algebras

Let
R=\bigoplus_{n\geq 0}R_{n}
be a standard graded (commutative)
\Bbbk
-algebra.
For
n\geq 0
, define
H(R,n)=\dim_{\Bbbk}R_{n}
. We call the function
H(R,-):\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
the
Hilbert function
of
R
.
Let
\operatorname{Hilb}(R,t)
denote the
Hilbert series
of
R
, that is,
\operatorname{Hilb}(R,t)=\sum_{n\geq 0}H(R,n)\,t^{n}
.
Then
\operatorname{Hilb}(R,t)
can be written in the form

\operatorname{Hilb}(R,t)=\frac{h_{R}(t)}{(1-t)^{d}},

where
d
is the Krull dimension of
R
and
h_{R}(t)
is a polynomial with integer coefficients satisfying
h_{R}(1)>0
(see, e.g.,
[
3
, Corollary 4.1.8]
).
We call the polynomial
h_{R}(t)
appearing in the numerator the
h
-polynomial
of
R
.
It is known that
h_{R}(1)
coincides with the multiplicity of
R
(
[
3
, Proposition 4.1.9]
).

Note that the Hilbert function of
R
can be recovered from its
h
-polynomial and Krull dimension.
Indeed, if
h_{R}(t)=\sum_{i\geq 0}h_{i}t^{i}
and
d
is the Krull dimension of
R
, then

(2.1)

\displaystyle H(R,n)=\sum_{i\geq 0}h_{i}\binom{d-1+n-i}{n-i}.

For more details on basic facts about Hilbert functions and Hilbert series, see
[
3
, Section 4]
.

Regarding the
h
-polynomials of standard graded complete intersections, we have the following result.

Proposition 2.1

(cf.
[
10
, Corollary 3.4]
)
.

Let
R
be a standard graded
\Bbbk
-algebra.
If
R
is a complete intersection, then the
h
-polynomial of
R
is of type CI, that is,
h_{R}(t)=\prod_{i=1}^{r}\Psi_{m_{i}}(t)
for some non-negative integer
r
and positive integers
m_{1},\dots,m_{r}
.

2.2.
Cyclotomic polynomials

Next, we recall some fundamental facts about cyclotomic polynomials.
For an introduction to cyclotomic polynomials, see, e.g.,
[
8
]
.

For a positive integer
m
, let
\Phi_{m}(t)
denote the
m
-th cyclotomic polynomial
, i.e.,

\Phi_{m}(t)=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq k\leq m\\
\gcd(k,m)=1\end{subarray}}\left(t-e^{2\pi k\sqrt{-1}/m}\right).

We collect some well-known properties of cyclotomic polynomials:

(1)

\Phi_{m}(t)
is an irreducible polynomial in
\mathbb{Z}[t]
of degree
\varphi(m)
, where

\varphi(m)=|\{\,k\in\{1,\ldots,m\}:\gcd(k,m)=1\}|.

(2)

\Phi_{m}(t)
is palindromic, i.e.,
t^{\varphi(m)}\Phi_{m}(t^{-1})=\Phi_{m}(t)
.

(3)

For
m\geq 2
,

(2.2)

\displaystyle\Phi_{m}(1)=\begin{cases}p&\text{if $m$ is a power of a prime $p$,}\\
1&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(4)

For a prime power
p^{c}
,

(2.3)

\displaystyle\Phi_{p^{c}}(t)=\Phi_{p}(t^{p^{c-1}}).

(5)

For any
m
,

\Psi_{m}(t)=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1<d\leq m\\
d\mid m\end{subarray}}\Phi_{d}(t).

In particular,
\Psi_{m}(t)=\Phi_{m}(t)
if
m
is prime.

(6)

Any monic polynomial with integer coefficients whose roots all lie on the unit circle in the complex plane can be expressed as a product of cyclotomic polynomials.

Example 2.2
.

We list the cyclotomic polynomials of small degrees:

\displaystyle\deg 1:\;

\displaystyle\Phi_{1}(t)=t-1,\quad\Phi_{2}(t)=1+t;

\displaystyle\deg 2:\;

\displaystyle\Phi_{3}(t)=1+t+t^{2},\quad\Phi_{4}(t)=\Phi_{2}(t^{2})=1+t^{2},\quad\Phi_{6}(t)=1-t+t^{2};

\displaystyle\deg 4:\;

\displaystyle\Phi_{5}(t)=1+t+t^{2}+t^{3}+t^{4},\quad\Phi_{8}(t)=\Phi_{2}(t^{4})=1+t^{4},

\displaystyle\Phi_{10}(t)=1-t+t^{2}-t^{3}+t^{4},\quad\Phi_{12}(t)=\Phi_{6}(t^{2})=1-t^{2}+t^{4}.

2.3.
Simplicial complexes and their
h
-polynomials

Here, we review simplicial complexes and their
h
-polynomials.

For a given non-empty finite set
V
, let
2^{V}
denote the collection of all subsets of
V
(including the empty set).
A subset
\Delta\subset 2^{V}
is called a
simplicial complex
if it satisfies the following conditions:

•

\{v\}\in\Delta
for every
v\in V
;

•

if
F\in\Delta
and
F^{\prime}\subset F
, then
F^{\prime}\in\Delta
.

Note that
\varnothing\in\Delta
for every simplicial complex
\Delta
.

An element
F\in\Delta
is called an
i
-face
if
|F|=i+1
. In particular,
\varnothing
is the unique
(-1)
-face.
We define the
dimension
of
\Delta
by

\dim\Delta=\max\{\,|F|-1:F\in\Delta\,\}.

We then set

f_{i}(\Delta)=|\{\,F\in\Delta:F\text{ is an $i$-face}\,\}|\quad\text{and}\quad f(\Delta)=(f_{-1}(\Delta),f_{0}(\Delta),\ldots,f_{d-1}(\Delta)),

where
d-1=\dim\Delta
. The vector
f(\Delta)
is called the
f
-vector
of
\Delta
.
The
h
-polynomial
of
\Delta
is defined by

(2.4)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{d}h_{i}(\Delta)t^{d-i}=\sum_{i=0}^{d}f_{i-1}(\Delta)(t-1)^{d-i}.

(Note that the
h
-polynomial carries the same information as the
f
-vector via (
2.4
).)

For a finite set
V
, let
\Delta_{V}=2^{V}
.
Clearly,
\Delta_{V}
is a simplicial complex of dimension
|V|-1
, which is usually called the
simplex
on
V
.
We define the
boundary complex
of a simplex by
\partial\Delta_{V}=\Delta_{V}\setminus\{V\}
.
We write
\Delta_{d}
(resp.
\partial\Delta_{d}
) for
\Delta_{V}
(resp.
\partial\Delta_{V}
) when
|V|=d
.
Then, as an easy exercise, one obtains

(2.5)

\displaystyle h_{\Delta_{d}}(t)=1\quad\text{and}\quad h_{\partial\Delta_{d}}(t)=\Psi_{d}(t).

2.4.
Stanley-Reisner rings of simplicial complexes

We now provide a brief introduction to the theory of Stanley-Reisner rings (cf.
[
3
, Section 5]
).
This theory plays a crucial role in analyzing the Hilbert series of standard graded
\Bbbk
-algebras.

For a simplicial complex
\Delta
on
V=\{1,\ldots,d\}
, define

\Bbbk[\Delta]:=\Bbbk[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]/I_{\Delta},\quad\text{where}\quad I_{\Delta}=\left(\prod_{i\in F}x_{i}\;:\;F\subset V,\,F\notin\Delta\right).

The standard graded
\Bbbk
-algebra
\Bbbk[\Delta]
(resp. the ideal
I_{\Delta}
) is called the
Stanley-Reisner ring
(resp. the
Stanley-Reisner ideal
) of
\Delta
.
The following facts are well known:

•

The Krull dimension of
\Bbbk[\Delta]
is equal to
\dim\Delta+1
.

•

The
h
-polynomial of
\Bbbk[\Delta]
coincides with the
h
-polynomial of
\Delta
.

•

The Cohen-Macaulayness and Gorensteinness of
\Bbbk[\Delta]
can be characterized in terms of the reduced homology of
\Delta
.

From (
2.5
), we obtain the following equalities, which will be used several times in the sequel:

\operatorname{Hilb}(\Bbbk[\Delta_{d}],t)=\frac{1}{(1-t)^{d}}\quad\text{and}\quad\operatorname{Hilb}(\Bbbk[\partial\Delta_{d}],t)=\frac{\Psi_{d}(t)}{(1-t)^{d-1}}.

2.5.
Macaulay’s theorem and
g
-theorem

Finally, let us review Macaulay’s theorem and the
g
-theorem. To state them, we first recall the notion of a binomial sum expansion.

Given a positive integer
a
, there exists a unique expression

(2.6)

\displaystyle a=\binom{k(d)}{d}+\binom{k(d-1)}{d-1}+\cdots+\binom{k(d^{\prime})}{d^{\prime}},

for any fixed positive integer
d
, where
d^{\prime}
is some positive integer satisfying
d\geq d^{\prime}
and
k(d)>k(d-1)>\cdots>k(d^{\prime})\geq d^{\prime}
(see
[
3
, Lemma 4.2.6]
).
We call the expression (
2.6
) the
binomial sum expression
of
a
with respect to
d
. We then define

a^{\langle d\rangle}=\binom{k(d)+1}{d+1}+\binom{k(d-1)+1}{d}+\cdots+\binom{k(d^{\prime})+1}{d^{\prime}+1}.

Theorem 2.3

(Macaulay’s theorem, cf.
[
3
, Theorem 4.2.10]
and
[
11
, Chapter II, Theorem 2.2]
)
.

Let
h:\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
be a function with
h(0)=1
.
Then there exists a standard graded
\Bbbk
-algebra
R
satisfying
H(R,n)=h(n)
for all
n\geq 0
if and only if
h(n+1)\leq h(n)^{\langle n\rangle}
for every
n>0
.

Example 2.4
.

We illustrate how to use Macaulay’s theorem.
Let
h(t)=\sum_{i=0}^{s}h_{i}t^{i}
be a polynomial such that
h_{0}=h_{1}=\cdots=h_{a-1}=1
and
h_{a}>1
for some
1<a\leq s
.
Then
h(t)
cannot be the
h
-polynomial of any standard graded
\Bbbk
-algebra.
Indeed, suppose there exists such an algebra
R
, and let
d
denote its Krull dimension. Then

\displaystyle H(R,a-1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\geq 0}h_{i}\binom{d-1+a-1-i}{a-1-i}=\binom{d+a-2}{a-1}+\binom{d+a-3}{a-2}+\cdots+\binom{d-1}{0}

\displaystyle=\binom{d+a-1}{a-1},

\displaystyle\text{while}\quad H(R,a)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\geq 0}h_{i}\binom{d-1+a-i}{a-i}=\binom{d+a-1}{a}+\cdots+\binom{d}{1}+h_{a}\binom{d-1}{0}

\displaystyle=\binom{d+a}{a}+h_{a}-1.

Since
H(R,a-1)^{\langle a-1\rangle}=\binom{d+a}{a}
, we obtain

H(R,a-1)^{\langle a-1\rangle}-H(R,a)=1-h_{a}<0,

a contradiction to Macaulay’s theorem.

Example 2.5
.

We next describe another polynomial that cannot occur as the
h
-polynomial of any standard graded
\Bbbk
-algebra.
Consider
h(t)=1+2t+t^{2}+3t^{5}
, and suppose that there exists a standard graded
\Bbbk
-algebra
R
of Krull dimension
d
with this
h
-polynomial.
Then, from (
2.1
), we have

\displaystyle H(R,4)

\displaystyle=\binom{d+3}{4}+2\binom{d+2}{3}+\binom{d+1}{2}\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle H(R,5)

\displaystyle=\binom{d+4}{5}+2\binom{d+3}{4}+\binom{d+2}{3}+3.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that
d\geq 2
.
By a direct computation, the binomial sum expansion of
H(R,4)
is

\displaystyle\binom{d+3}{4}+2\binom{d+2}{3}+\binom{d+1}{2}=\binom{d+4}{4}+\binom{d+1}{3}+\binom{d-1}{2}+\binom{d-2}{1}.

Hence,

H(R,4)^{\langle 4\rangle}=\binom{d+5}{5}+\binom{d+2}{4}+\binom{d}{3}+\binom{d-1}{2}.

In this case, one can show that

H(R,4)^{\langle 4\rangle}-H(R,5)=-1,

which contradicts Macaulay’s theorem. However, establishing this fact is far from trivial.

As illustrated by this example, the computation of binomial expansions is generally rather involved,
and hence it is not easy to apply Macaulay’s theorem to determine whether a given polynomial can be realized as an
h
-polynomial.

For a simplicial polytope
P
with vertex set
V
, the collection

\{F\subset V:\mathrm{conv}(F)\text{ is a proper face of }P\}

forms a simplicial complex on
V
, called the
boundary complex
of
P
.
Note that the boundary complex of a simplex of dimension
d-1
is precisely
\partial\Delta_{d}
.

A characterization of the
h
-polynomials of boundary complexes of simplicial polytopes is known as the
g
-theorem
,
one of the most celebrated results in Stanley-Reisner theory.

Theorem 2.6

(
g
-theorem, cf.
[
11
, Chapter III, Theorem 1.1]
)
.

Let
(h_{0},h_{1},\ldots,h_{d})\in\mathbb{Z}^{d+1}
.
Then there exists a simplicial polytope
P
of dimension
d
whose boundary complex has the
h
-polynomial
\sum_{i=0}^{d}h_{i}t^{i}

if and only if the following two conditions are satisfied:

(1)

h_{i}=h_{d-i}
for
0\leq i\leq\lfloor d/2\rfloor
, and

(2)

h_{0}=1
and
0\leq h_{i+1}-h_{i}\leq(h_{i}-h_{i-1})^{\langle i\rangle}
for
1\leq i\leq\lfloor d/2\rfloor-1
.

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.3
(1)

This section is devoted to proving Theorem
1.3
(1).

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\Delta
be a simplicial complex of dimension
D-1
and let
f_{D-1}(\Delta)=a
. Then

f_{D-2}(\Delta)\geq\begin{cases}aD-\binom{a}{2}&\text{if }a\leq D+1,\\
\binom{D+1}{2}&\text{if }a>D+1.\end{cases}

Proof.

If
a=1
, since there should be at least
D
faces of dimension
(D-2)
, we get
f_{D-2}(\Delta)\geq D
.

Let
a\leq D+1
and let
F_{1},\ldots,F_{a}
be the
(D-1)
-faces of
\Delta
.
Let

\Delta^{\prime}=\langle F_{1},\ldots,F_{a-1}\rangle=\{G\in\Delta:G\subset F_{i}\text{ for some }i\}.

Since
f_{D-2}(\Delta^{\prime}\cap F_{a})\leq a-1
, we see by induction on
a
that

\displaystyle f_{D-2}(\Delta)=f_{D-2}(\Delta^{\prime})+D-f_{D-2}(\Delta^{\prime}\cap F_{a})\geq(a-1)D-\binom{a-1}{2}+D-(a-1)=aD-\binom{a}{2}.

If
a>D+1
, let
\Delta^{\prime\prime}
be a subcomplex of
\Delta
consisting of some
(D-1)
-faces
F_{1},\ldots,F_{D+1}\in\Delta
.
Then
f_{D-2}(\Delta^{\prime\prime})\geq(D+1)D-\binom{D+1}{2}=\binom{D+1}{2}
by the first case. Thus,

f_{D-2}(\Delta)\geq f_{D-2}(\Delta^{\prime\prime})\geq\binom{D+1}{2},

as required.
∎

The following proposition plays the essential role for the proof of Theorem
1.3
(1).

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
R
be a standard graded
\Bbbk
-algebra whose
h
-polynomial is palindromic.
Then we have
\deg(h_{R}(t))\leq h_{R}(1)-1
.

Proof.

We employ a similar technique used in the proof of
[
1
, Lemma 4.6]
.

Let
h_{R}(t)=\sum_{i=0}^{s}h_{i}t^{i}
, where
s
is the degree of
h_{R}(t)
. Since
h_{R}(t)
is palindromic, i.e.,
h_{i}=h_{s-i}
for
i=0,1,\ldots,s
. We set
a=h_{R}(1)
.

Let
R\cong S/I
, where
S
is a polynomial ring and
I
is an ideal of
S
.
Since the Hilbert series does not change when passing to the initial ideal of
I
, we can assume without loss of generality that
I
is a monomial ideal.
Moreover, since polarization preserves
h_{R}(t)
, we can further assume that
I
is a squarefree monomial ideal.
Hence,
I
is the Stanley-Reisner ideal of some simplicial complex
\Delta
.
Let
\dim\Delta=D-1
. Then
s\leq D
and
h_{\Delta}(t)=h_{R}(t)
, that is,
h_{i}(\Delta)=h_{i}
for every
0\leq i\leq s
.
By (
2.4
), we see that

\displaystyle f_{D-1}(\Delta)

\displaystyle=h_{R}(1)=a,\text{ and}

\displaystyle f_{D-2}(\Delta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{s}(D-i)h_{i}(\Delta)=(D-s)\sum_{i=0}^{s}h_{i}(\Delta)+\frac{s}{2}\sum_{i=0}^{s}h_{i}(\Delta)=\left(D-\frac{s}{2}\right)a.

Suppose that
a\leq D+1
. Then, by Lemma
3.1
, we have

\displaystyle f_{D-2}(\Delta)=\left(D-\frac{s}{2}\right)a\geq aD-\binom{a}{2}

and hence
s\leq a-1
. This is the desired conclusion.

Suppose that
a>D+1
. Then, by Lemma
3.1
, we have

\displaystyle f_{D-2}(\Delta)=\left(D-\frac{s}{2}\right)a\geq\binom{D+1}{2}.

This implies
2Da-D^{2}-D\geq sa

\displaystyle I_{k}

\displaystyle=\{k(q-1)+p-1,\ldots,k(q-1)+p-1+q-2\}\text{ for }k=0,1,\ldots,(p-7)/2,

\displaystyle I_{(p-5)/2}

\displaystyle=\{(p-5)(q-1)/2+p-1,\ldots,\underbrace{(p-5)(q-1)/2+p-1+q-3}_{=(p-1)(q-1)/2-2}\},\text{ and }

\displaystyle I_{(p-3)/2}

\displaystyle=\{(p-1)(q-1)/2-1\}.

See Example
4.5
for illustrations.

Note that
f_{p,q}(t)
is a palindromic polynomial whose constant term is
1
(see Lemma
4.4
).
Hence, according to Theorem
2.6
, in order for there to exist a simplicial polytope whose
h
-polynomial is
f_{p,q}(t)
, it suffices to verify the following inequalities:

0\leq c_{i+1}\leq c_{i}^{\langle i\rangle}\;\text{ for each }\;i=1,\ldots,(p-1)q/2-2.

The first inequality
0\leq c_{i+1}
holds for each
i
. Regarding the second one, we can get a binomial sum expression as follows:

\displaystyle c_{i}=\begin{cases}\binom{i+1}{i}&\text{if }i=0,1,\ldots,p-2,\\
\binom{i}{i}+\binom{i-1}{i-1}+\cdots+\binom{i-c_{i}+1}{i-c_{i}+1}&\text{if }i=p-1,\ldots,(p-1)q/2-2,\end{cases}

where we notice that
c_{i}\leq i
if
i\geq p-1
. Hence,

\displaystyle c_{i}^{\langle i\rangle}=\begin{cases}\binom{i+2}{i+1}=i+2=c_{i+1}&\text{if }i=0,1,\ldots,p-3,\\
\binom{p}{p-1}=p=c_{p-1}+1&\text{if }i=p-2,\\
\binom{i+1}{i+1}+\binom{i}{i}+\cdots+\binom{i-c_{i}+2}{i-c_{i}+2}=c_{i}&\text{if }i=p-1,\ldots,(p-1)q/2-2.\end{cases}

Since
c_{i}\geq c_{i+1}
holds for each
i
with
p-2\leq i\leq(p-1)q/2-2
(see (
4.2
) and (
4.3
)), we conclude the desired condition.
∎

Now, we are ready to give a proof of Theorem
4.2
(3).

Proof of Theorem
4.2
(3).

By definition of
f_{p,q}(t)
, we see the following equality:

\displaystyle\frac{\Psi_{p}(t)\Psi_{q}(t)\Phi_{6}(t^{(p-1)(q-1)/2})}{(1-t)^{pq-1}}=\frac{\Psi_{pq}(t)}{(1-t)^{pq-1}}+\frac{\Psi_{q-1}(t)f_{p,q}(t)}{(1-t)^{pq-3}}-\frac{\Psi_{q-1}(t)f_{p,q}(t)}{(1-t)^{pq-4}}.

Since
h_{\partial\Delta_{pq}}(t)=\Psi_{pq}(t)
and
\partial\Delta_{pq}
has dimension
pq-2
,
by the same argument as before, it is enough to show the existence of a subcomplex of
\partial\Delta_{pq}
of dimension
pq-5
whose
h
-polynomial is equal to
\Psi_{q-1}(t)f_{p,q}(t)
.
We can construct such a subcomplex as
\partial\Delta_{q-1}*\Delta^{\prime}*\Delta_{1}
inside
\partial\Delta_{pq}
,
where
\Delta^{\prime}
is a simplicial complex guaranteed by Proposition
4.7
, because
the number of vertices is equal to

\underbrace{q-1}_{=\partial\Delta_{q-1}}+\underbrace{(p-1)q-1}_{=\Delta^{\prime}}+1=pq-1,

which is less than the number of vertices of
\partial\Delta_{pq}
.
Moreover, the dimension is equal to
q-2+(p-1)q-5+0+2=pq-5
.

Therefore, we complete the proof.
∎

What we have shown in this section is that, if
p=2
and
q\geq 5
is odd, or if
p,q\geq 3
are both odd,
then there exists a standard graded
\Bbbk
-algebra (indeed, a simplicial complex) whose
h
-polynomial is equal to
\Psi_{p}(t)\Psi_{q}(t)\Phi_{6}(t^{(p-1)(q-1)/2})
.
It is natural to consider a “more direct” application of Macaulay’s theorem to verify the existence of a standard graded
\Bbbk
-algebra having this
h
-polynomial.
However, such an approach seems to be highly nontrivial.
Indeed, one must translate the coefficients of the polynomial
\Psi_{p}(t)\Psi_{q}(t)\Phi_{6}(t^{(p-1)(q-1)/2})
into the language of Hilbert functions via (
2.1
),
and the resulting expressions quickly become intricate.
For instance, let
p=2
and
q\geq 5
be odd. Then

\displaystyle\Psi_{2}(t)\Psi_{q}(t)\Phi_{6}(t^{(q-1)/2})

\displaystyle=1+2(t+\cdots+t^{(q-3)/2})+t^{(q-1)/2}+t^{q-1}+t^{q}+t^{(3q-1)/2}

\displaystyle\quad+2(t^{(3q+1)/2}+\cdots+t^{2q-2})+t^{2q-1}.

Let
d
be a sufficiently large integer, and define

f(n)=\sum_{i\geq 0}h_{i}\binom{d-1+n-i}{n-i}

with (
2.1
) in mind,
where
h_{i}
denotes the coefficient of
t^{i}
in
\Psi_{2}(t)\Psi_{q}(t)\Phi_{6}(t^{(q-1)/2})
.
To apply Macaulay’s theorem, we need to verify that

(4.4)

\displaystyle f(n+1)\leq f(n)^{\langle n\rangle}\quad\text{for all $n\geq 1$.}

Although these inequalities are guaranteed by our result (the proof of Theorem
4.2
), we now attempt a direct verification. For example, we have

\displaystyle f(q-2)

\displaystyle=\binom{d-1+q-2}{q-2}+2\left(\binom{d-1+q-3}{q-3}+\cdots+\binom{d-1+(q-5)/2}{(q-5)/2}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\binom{d-1+(q-3)/2}{(q-3)/2},

\displaystyle f(q-1)

\displaystyle=\binom{d-1+q-1}{q-1}+2\left(\binom{d-1+q-2}{q-2}+\cdots+\binom{d-1+(q-3)/2}{(q-3)/2}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\binom{d-1+(q-1)/2}{(q-1)/2}+\binom{d-1}{0},

and one needs to estimate
f(q-2)^{\langle q-2\rangle}-f(q-1)
,
which requires explicit and complicated computations of the binomial sum expression
f(q-2)^{\langle q-2\rangle}
.
Consequently, a direct verification of (
4.4
) seems quite difficult.
Our approach, which consists of finding a tractable polynomial and applying the
g
-theorem, is technical but significant, as it allows us to avoid eraborate computations of binomial sum expressions.
