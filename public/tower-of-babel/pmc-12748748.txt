Taxonomic reassessment of captive sugar gliders using genetic analyses and complementary acoustic data
Authors: Miroslav Mulko, Irena Schneiderová, Barbora Černá Bolfíková
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate species identification is crucial for managing ex-situ populations, especially in cryptic species complexes where taxonomic uncertainty may compromise conservation. The sugar glider (Petaurus breviceps s.l.) is a small nocturnal marsupial commonly bred in zoos and is popular in the exotic pet trade. Recent taxonomic revisions revealed substantial cryptic diversity within the complex, raising concerns about species identity and geographic origin of captive individuals. We used an integrative approach combining genetic and acoustic analyses to assess the taxonomic status of captive P. breviceps s.l. populations. Phylogenetic analyses of mitochondrial ND2 and ND4 genes showed a strong genetic affinity between European and United States captive populations, suggesting a shared origin within the New Guinean lineage. These findings support their reclassification as Petaurus papuanus. Despite their captive origin, these populations showed unexpectedly high haplotype diversity, likely due to repeated introductions from genetically distinct but geographically close wild populations. However, within-group homogeneity indicates limited genetic exchange among breeding lines. Acoustic analyses of the barking call revealed intraspecific variability but little species-specificity, indicating a minor role in reproductive isolation. Our findings underscore the importance of taxonomic clarity and structured genetic management for conserving captive population integrity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-31262-0.Keywords: Bioacoustics, Captive breeding, Genetic diversity, Marsupialia, Pet trade, PetauridaeSubject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Evolution, Genetics, Zoology

Introduction

The global trade and captive breeding of mammals, spanning institutional and individual levels (from zoos to pet breeders), has expanded significantly in recent decades1. Combination of conservation goals, commercial interests, and public demand for exotic pets has created complex ecological, ethical, and regulatory challenges2. While zoological institutions typically present captive breeding as a conservation strategy, private breeding is often driven by aesthetic preferences, economic gain, or personal interest. A better understanding of the biology, taxonomy, and population structure of traded species is therefore essential, particularly when individuals are moved between breeding facilities. Without accurate taxonomic insight, there is a risk of unintentionally hybridizing distinct evolutionary lineages, which can obscure species boundaries and compromise long-term conservation goals3.

These risks are exacerbated in taxonomically complex groups, particularly among nocturnal mammals, where cryptic species are frequently misclassified4. This phenomenon is particularly prevalent in groups such as bats5, tree hyraxes6, nocturnal primates7–9, and marsupials10,11. In these taxa, species identification has increasingly relied on integrative approaches, including molecular and acoustic data. In particular, vocalizations, often species-specific, have proven useful for distinguishing morphologically similar species12–15.

Molecular techniques and bioacoustic analyses have significantly improved our ability to detect and describe cryptic diversity, especially in elusive or nocturnal species7,9,15–17. When used in combination, methodologies such as acoustic and genetic analyses provide a powerful framework for understanding population structure, species richness, and phylogenetic relationships18–21.

Persistent taxonomic uncertainty continues to challenge our understanding of species boundaries within the genus Petaurus, particularly in the P. breviceps species complex. Although six species are currently recognized22–28, the delineation among them remains unresolved and in need of further taxonomic revision. This ambiguity poses significant challenges for conservation planning and management, especially in ex-situ breeding programs where accurate species identification is essential.

Petaurus species are nocturnal marsupials inhabiting a range of forest habitats across northwestern and eastern Australia, Papua New Guinea, and parts of Indonesia29. Their distributions are closely associated with specific environmental conditions and vegetation types. These marsupials are also known for their vocal activity, producing a diverse repertoire of calls used in social and defensive contexts30–32. Loud barking calls, in particular, are frequently emitted in the early night and can be heard over considerable distances. The loud and stereotypic nature of these vocalizations makes them promising candidates for bioacoustic monitoring and may aid in species delineation. In addition to vocal signals, chemical communication is also essential for social organization. Odor cues from scent glands and urine are used to identify individuals and mark their home ranges30,31.

Historically, phenotypic variation led to the classification of P. breviceps s.l. into seven subspecies30. However, the study of Malekian et al.22 highlighted the need for phylogenetic analyses to assess the presence of cryptic diversity within the group. Cremona et al.29 subsequently reclassified three Australian subspecies and elevated them as distinct species: P. ariel (northern Australia), P. notatus (eastern Australia and Tasmania), and P. breviceps (eastern coastal Australia). More recently, Powley and Mikac33 supported the separation of New Guinean populations from Australian taxa, suggesting species-level distinctions. Despite observed morphological differences between island and mainland populations, the taxonomy of Petaurus populations across the New Guinea region remains poorly studied33.

The sugar glider’s popularity in the exotic pet trade has led to the widespread captivity of individuals whose geographic origins are not always well-documented. While genetic research suggests that sugar gliders in the United States pet trade are most closely related to populations from Indonesian islands34, their precise provenance often remains unclear. Captive populations are also commonly maintained in zoological institutions worldwide, where accurate species and subspecies identification is essential. Unresolved taxonomy within the Petaurus breviceps complex poses a risk of unintentional hybridization and outbreeding depression if individuals from distinct evolutionary lineages are housed or bred together3,35–38. Clear phylogenetic insight is therefore critical for informed management and conservation practices across both private and institutional settings.

In this study, we aimed to determine the geographic origin and clarify the species identity of captive Petaurus breviceps sensu lato populations bred in Europe. Using a combination of genetic and acoustic data, we assessed their phylogenetic relationships and evaluated whether vocalizations could support species delineation. By identifying their likely source populations and taxonomic affiliations, this research contributes to improved management of captive breeding programs, helping to prevent hybridization, maintain genetic integrity, and support evidence-based conservation practices.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

This study involved the non-invasive collection of buccal swabs from sugar gliders (Petaurus breviceps s.l.) for the purpose of genetic analysis. All procedures were conducted in full compliance with current Czech and European Union legislation regarding the use of animals for scientific purposes. The collection of buccal swabs is considered a non-invasive and non-harmful procedure. The animals were handled gently to minimize stress, and no anesthesia, sedation, or surgical procedures were involved. All swabs were collected by trained personnel in accordance with best practice guidelines for animal handling and welfare. All protocols performed within this study were approved by the Expert Committee for Ensuring the Welfare of Experimental Animals Czech University of Life Sciences Prague, and the study was conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines.

Genetic samples were obtained from five zoos and seven private breeders across four European countries. In total, 76 samples were collected, including 41 from zoos and 35 from private breeders. Detailed information about the number of sampled individuals per group is provided in Table 1. Sample ID, together with accession numbers to the NCBI GenBank, are deposited in Table S1. The majority of samples consisted of buccal swabs using FLOQSwab 501CS01 (Copan Italia Spa, Brescia, Italy). The buccal swab samples were dried and stored at -20 °C. Only two tissue samples were collected from deceased individuals and were stored in 96% ethanol at -20 °C.

Additionally, sequences of 286 individuals (both wild22,29 and captive34 sugar gliders) were retrieved from the NCBI GenBank database (Table S2).

In total, 4,232 recordings were collected. Automatic recorders Song Meter SM4 and Song Meter Mini (Wildlife Acoustics, Concord, Massachusetts, USA) were used in five zoos and three private breeders (two in Portugal and one in Czechia), resulting in 4,225 recordings. The automatic recording devices were configured to record at a sampling rate of 48 kHz with 16-bit resolution over several days per group. Each recorder was programmed to operate for 12 h daily, with 14-minute recording intervals separated by one-minute breaks, and to record during the dark period of each group’s day/night light cycle. Private breeders collected the remaining seven recordings on their mobile devices. Table 1 provides more detailed information about the group size, recording dates, schedules, and the number of recordings and detected barking calls.

Ninety-one recordings were sourced from the iNaturalist.com platform (https://www.inaturalist.org/) up to February 29th, 2024, for comparative analysis with wild individuals (Table S3). Of these, 71 recordings were analyzed, while 20 were excluded due to poor quality, species misidentification, or copyright issues.

Molecular laboratory methods

DNA extraction was performed using the Presto™ Buccal Swab gDNA Extraction Kit (Geneaid) for buccal swabs and the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen) for tissue samples, following the protocols provided by the manufacturers.

Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was employed to amplify sequences from two mitochondrial genes – NADH dehydrogenase subunit 2 (ND2, ~ 700 bp) and NADH dehydrogenase subunit 4 (ND4, ~ 700 bp), and one nuclear gene – ω-globin (~ 700 bp). The selection of genetic markers was determined by the need to ensure direct comparability with the datasets of the three key studies22,29,34, to evaluate species status of the European captive population and to compare the European and US captive groups. Primers for each genetic marker were used according to the protocols established in these studies, and the complete list of primers is provided in Table S3.

The PCR products were analyzed via electrophoresis on 1% agarose gels, and successfully amplified fragments were purified using the Gel/PCR DNA Fragments Extraction Kit (Geneaid) protocol. Sanger sequencing of the purified fragments was subsequently performed in the service laboratory at the Faculty of Science, Charles University. For sequencing, forward primers were utilized.

Acoustic analyses

All recordings were analyzed using Avisoft SASLab Pro 5.3.2 software (Avisoft Bioacoustics, Berlin, Germany). Due to its suitable features for acoustic monitoring, the barking call was selected as the focal vocalization. To unify the recordings, all were converted to mono format. As the vocalization did not exceed 6 kHz, the sampling frequency was reduced from 48 kHz to 12 kHz. In cases when the barking call was not well visible on the spectrogram, the “Change Volume” function in the Editing bar was selected for amplification. As this function also enhances the background noise, the volume in certain parts was increased only by 3.5 dB.

Each recording was carefully reviewed, and barking calls were manually labeled. To measure acoustic parameters for subsequent statistical analyses, barking calls were visualized using an oscillogram, power spectrum, and spectrogram with the following settings: FFT length of 1,024, frame size of 25%, and Hamming window. These parameters were chosen for optimal resolution, specifically temporal resolution of 2.67 ms and frequency resolution of 12 Hz. Out of all 4,232 recordings, 76 barking calls were analyzed.

All 71 good-quality recordings obtained from iNaturalist were categorized as belonging to Krefft’s glider (Petaurus notatus) or the sugar glider (Petaurus breviceps) based on the recording location and the proposed species distribution1.

Eight acoustic parameters were measured manually (Fig. 1): the total number of barks in a whole call (N bark), duration of the entire call (Dur), the interval between barks (Int), duration of individual barks (Dur bark), their peak frequency (Peak), peak fundamental frequency (Peak f0), maximum fundamental frequency (Max f0). The barking rate (Rate) was also calculated as the number of barks divided by the call duration. The maximum number of recordings containing the vocalization from a single group was 118, while the minimum was one. On average, each group contributed 19.7 recordings featuring the vocalization. Since it was not possible to reliably determine how many individuals contributed vocalizations within each group, we treated each group as a single vocal unit in the analysis. Barking call measurements from captive populations were first averaged per group using a weighted mean. This resulted in 12 group-level vocalizations from captive sugar gliders. These were analyzed alongside 38 barking calls from wild sugar gliders and 33 from Krefft’s gliders.

Data processing and analyses

The sequences were manually edited and aligned using Geneious Prime 2023.1.2 software (www.geneious.com). For the sequence alignment, we have applied Clustal Omega 1.2.239–41, and two different datasets for phylogenetic analyses were generated. The first dataset was composed of only ND2 sequences and comprised 359 ingroup (76 newly sequenced and 283 obtained from GenBank) and 3 outgroup individuals (also obtained from GenBank). This dataset was built to evaluate the species status of the captive populations within the taxonomic classification proposed by Cremona et al.29. To increase the robustness of the reclassification and to compare genetic diversity between captive groups, a second dataset was constructed using concatenated ND2 and ND4 sequences. This dataset included 266 sequences in total, comprising 263 (76 generated in this study and 187 retrieved from GenBank) ingroup and 3 outgroup sequences (also from GenBank). ND4 data were not available for wild specimens from Australia29, and three captive individuals34, which explains the reduced sample size compared to the ND2 dataset.

Phylogenetic analyses were conducted using Geneious Prime 2023.1.2, incorporating the MrBayes 3.2.6 plugin42. Based on Malekian et al.22, Petaurus abidi, the closest sister group, was chosen as the outgroup. The substitution model was determined using jModelTest43,44. While TIM2 + I + G was identified as the most appropriate model, the GTR substitution model was selected as an alternative due to its availability in Geneious Prime. The analysis was conducted with gamma variation, a chain length of 1.1 million, a subsampling frequency of 200, and a burn-in length of 100,000. Phylogenetic trees were visualized and edited using FigTree v1.4.445.

Pairwise p-distance were subsequently calculated among captive populations and main clades representing P. ariel, P. breviceps, P. notatus, and P. papuanus using MEGA1246 based on the ND2 dataset from the phylogenetic analysis, excluding outgroup sequences.

A haplotype network was generated using PopART software47 based on an alignment of 266 ND2 sequences from Europe, the United States34, Indonesia, Papua New Guinea, and Australia22. The sequences from the study of Cremona et al.29 were excluded due to shorter fragment length resulting in missing data. The network was constructed using the TCS algorithm48.

Genetic diversity within captive groups was assessed using DnaSP v6.12.0349 on concatenated ND2 and ND4 sequences. Metrics, including the number of haplotypes (Nh), haplotype diversity (Hd), nucleotide diversity (Pi), and the standard deviation of haplotype diversity, were calculated. In addition, DnaSP was used to identify the number of parsimony‑informative sites for each marker.

For acoustic data all descriptive statistics, one-way ANOVA and principal component analysis (PCA) were performed using R 4.3.3 software50 with the following packages: AICcmodavg51, broom52, car53, carData54, corrplot55, factoextra56, ggcorrplot57, ggfortify58,59, ggplot260, ggpubr61, loadings62, magrittr63, readxl64, rstatix65, and tidyverse66.

We calculated each measured acoustic parameter’s mean, maximum, minimum, and standard deviation. The parameters Nb and Dur were excluded from further analysis because recordings from iNaturalist might not capture complete vocalizations, making these measures unreliable.

To compare barking calls among three groups, captive European sugar gliders (recorded in this study), wild Petaurus notatus, and P. breviceps (from iNaturalist recordings), one-way ANOVA was conducted for the remaining parameters (Rate, Dur bark, Int, Peak, Peak f0, and Max f0). The significance level was set to 0.05; therefore, each variable with a lower p-value was identified as significant. Variables were tested for normal distribution and homogeneity of variance using Q-Q plots, residuals vs. fitted plots, the Shapiro-Wilk test, and Levene’s test. Non-normal variables (Int and Peak) were log-transformed. However, despite the transformation, Peak did not meet the normality assumption. Nonetheless, ANOVA was applied as it is robust against minor violations of normality67. Afterwards, the Tukey post-hoc test was performed in order to determine the differences among the selected groups.

Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed to reduce the six correlated acoustic parameters into uncorrelated principal components and to evaluate the position of barking calls within a dimension of the first two principal components in a scatterplot. Before performing PCA, all variables were standardized by subtracting their means and dividing by their standard deviations.

Results

Phylogenetic analyses

Sequences of the ND2 and ND4 mitochondrial genes were successfully obtained from all 76 samples. Following sequence alignment and editing, 666 base pairs (bp) of ND2 and 657 bp of ND4 were retained for further analyses. For the ω-globin nuclear gene, 639 bp were analyzed. However, after sequencing 23 individuals representing 7 out of 12 groups, the variability of this marker proved to be very low. Because of this low variability, the sequencing was discontinued, and the marker was not used in subsequent analyses. All obtained sequences (ND2, ND4, and ω-globin) can be found in the NCBI GenBank database under the accession numbers PV701819-PV701993 (Table S1).

The Bayesian phylogenetic analysis based on ND2 marker confirmed the clades previously proposed by Malekian et al.22 for populations from New Guinea and its surrounding islands (NG) and Australia (Fig. 2). High posterior probabilities (0.87–0.97) supported the distinction between the NG complex and the Australian clades. Additionally, the strong posterior values separating the NG complex from the Australian taxa suggest species-level differentiation of Petaurus papuanus (brown) from P. ariel (red), P. notatus (blue), and P. breviceps (green) (Fig. 2A). In the phylogeny based on the concatenated ND2 and ND4 sequences (Fig. 2B), P. ariel appeared with a different branching pattern compared to the ND2-only tree. However, the corresponding node received only weak support (posterior probability 0.55). Two sequences from Papua New Guinea were also placed outside the main NG clades, forming a small branch adjacent to P. ariel. This pattern should be interpreted with caution, as the restricted marker length and incomplete geographic sampling may obscure deeper phylogenetic relationships.

The European captive population grouped with captive individuals from the United States, forming a monophyletic clade (Fig. 2). This lineage showed its closest affinity to individuals from Kai Besar Island (Indonesia), a region geographically adjacent to New Guinea. Notably, Australian genotypes were absent from the captive populations, suggesting an NG origin of the captive populations. Across all phylogenetic analyses, the captive individuals previously identified as P. breviceps consistently clustered within P. papuanus.

Pairwise genetic p-distance values (Table S5) further supported these findings, indicating a close genetic relationship within the captive populations (0.0038) and between captive individuals and Petaurus papuanus (0.0761). In contrast, the highest p-distance values were observed between the captive populations and P. breviceps (0.1307), confirming the genetic differentiation between these species and offering further support for the proposed phylogenetic relationships.

The haplotype network (Fig. 3) was constructed using 266 sequences, each 666 bp. A total of 55 haplotypes were identified, 23 of which were associated with captive populations from the United States and Europe (depicted in yellow and dark yellow in Fig. 3). The captive populations differed by 16 mutation steps from the Indonesian population (grey in Fig. 3), which includes sequences from Kai Besar Island. The closest haplotypes from Papua New Guinea (brown in Fig. 3) were separated by 26 mutation steps from the captive populations.

Acoustic analysis

The barking call exhibited substantial variation across groups. The mean number of barks (N bark) per call ranged from 17.9 to 48.7. Among the group-averaged barking calls, the longest mean duration (Dur) was 71.4 s, while the shortest was 22.3 s. The mean barking rate (Rate) varied between 0.652 and 0.926 barks per second. The average duration of an individual bark (Dur bark) ranged from 0.101 to 0.115 s. The interval between barks (Int) also varied, with the longest mean interval recorded at 1.63 s and the shortest at 1.33 s.

Regarding frequency parameters, the highest group-level mean of peak frequency (Peak) reached 1634 Hz, while the lowest was 1278 Hz. The maximum fundamental frequency (Max f0) had group-level means ranging from 884 Hz to 943 Hz. Similarly, peak fundamental frequency (Peak f0) varied between a mean of 806 Hz and 860 Hz. Detailed values for each group are listed in Table 3.

The one-way ANOVA did not detect significant differences between groups for most of the tested acoustic parameters. However, the analysis revealed significant differences for two parameters, Rate and Interval (Int). Tukey’s post hoc test showed that while the Interval was not significantly different among groups, the Rate exhibited substantial differences. Specifically, the Rate parameter differed substantially between recordings of Petaurus breviceps and P. notatus and P. breviceps and P. papuanus (See Table S7). In contrast, no significant difference in Rate was observed between P. notatus and P. papuanus (Table S7). These results suggest that wild sugar gliders produce barks faster than the other two groups (Fig. S1). For visual comparison of the barking calls, spectrograms of the three species are provided in the Supplementary Materials (Fig. S2).

The PCA identified two principal components with eigenvalues higher than 1, collectively explaining over 70% of the variability in the dataset. The first principal component (PC1) was primarily associated with frequency parameters derived from the fundamental harmonic, specifically, Peak f0 and Max f0. The second principal component (PC2) strongly correlated with parameters related to call rate, namely Rate and Interval (Int). The complete factor loadings for all variables are provided in Supplementary Table S8.

Discussion

Accurate species identification remains a cornerstone of effective ex-situ management and breeding programs, as it ensures that captive populations retain their genetic, morphological, and behavioral integrity3. Our findings highlight the potential risks associated with taxonomic uncertainty, particularly in cryptic species complexes like Petaurus breviceps s.l., where misidentification may result in unintended hybridization or reduced conservation value36,37. Moreover, understanding the geographic origin of zoo- or privately housed individuals is vital for informed conservation planning and any future reintroduction efforts35. Individuals with unclear taxonomic or geographic provenance are therefore unsuitable for such initiatives.

Our study found a strong genetic connection between captive populations in Europe and the United States, as both formed a single cluster in phylogenetic analyses. The haplotype network further supported this result, with shared haplotypes suggesting a common origin. These individuals appear closely related to those from the New Guinean complex, particularly from Kai Besar Island. However, this inference may be affected by limited sampling from that locality (N = 2) and the broader New Guinean region. Due to these constraints, we were unable to precisely identify the origin of the captive populations, as none of their haplotypes exactly matched those from wild populations. Our findings are consistent with Campbell et al.34, who proposed that the United States captive population likely originated from Sorong, Indonesia. This ambiguity further emphasizes Southeast Asia’s central role as a global hub for wildlife trade68.

Captive populations are often founded by a limited number of individuals, which can result in genetic bottlenecks followed by reduced genetic diversity due to genetic drift and inbreeding69. This pattern has been documented in several mammalian species, where founder effects and limited gene flow have led to low mitochondrial haplotype diversity and long-term risks for population viability70–72. However, in some cases, novel haplotypes have emerged in captivity, suggesting more complex demographic histories73. In contrast to expectations based on founder effects, we found no evidence of reduced genetic diversity in the captive Petaurus populations. On the contrary, the observed haplotype diversity was remarkably high for a captive group, likely reflecting the repeated importation of animals from multiple, geographically proximate populations. Nevertheless, within individual breeding groups, most individuals shared the same haplotype, emphasizing the need for structured genetic management and exchange between breeding lines to prevent future inbreeding and maintain population viability.

Increased genetic diversity in captive populations can also arise as a consequence of hybridization, either between divergent lineages within a species74 or between distinct species75. While such mixing may temporarily elevate genetic variation, it poses significant risks for long-term conservation efforts, especially when it blurs taxonomic boundaries or disrupts locally adapted gene complexes3,35,36. Cases of species misidentification or poorly resolved taxonomy have repeatedly led to unintentional hybridization in captive settings, complicating breeding programs and requiring substantial corrections to management strategies37. These challenges underline the importance of accurate species classification.

Our phylogenetic analyses, aligned with recent taxonomic revisions by Powley and Mikac33, indicate that the captive populations studied here should be classified as Petaurus papuanus rather than P. breviceps. Captive individuals consistently clustered within the New Guinean lineage, forming a distinct clade with high posterior probabilities (0.87–0.97) separating them from Australian taxa. The species-level differentiation between P. breviceps and P. papuanus is corroborated by morphological analyses that revealed significant differences in skull size and shape between New Guinean and Australian populations33. Consequently, the classification of captive individuals should reflect these updated taxonomic insights. Additionally, the morphological variation observed among island populations within the New Guinean complex33 suggests the possible existence of subspecies, warranting further investigation.

Unstable phylogenetic signal and the presence of PNG haplotypes outside the New Guinean clades indicates that the diversity of sugar gliders in Papua New Guinea may be more extensive than previously recognised. This pattern could be a result of cryptic lineages or incomplete taxonomic resolution, emphasising the necessity for increased sampling across New Guinea. Importantly, given the recent taxonomic split among all recognised species, such hidden diversity may imply that conservation assessments underestimate the true vulnerability of these newly defined taxa. Therefore, a reassessment of species status by NGOs and conservation authorities is required, as unresolved taxonomy can directly affect conservation priorities and management strategies.

Despite apparent genetic and morphological differences among Petaurus species, our study found no significant interspecific variation in the barking call, except for a statistically significant difference in the barking rate. Specifically, P. breviceps exhibited a faster barking rate than the other species in our study, indicating potential behavioral or ecological differences in vocal communication. Similar patterns in interspecific/intersubspecific vocal variation among closely related species have been observed in other nocturnal mammals, such as Galago crassicaudatus and G. garnetti12, as well as Otolemur garnettii panganiensis and O. g. kikuyuensis14.

The barking call, a loud, stereotypical vocalization that reaches frequencies up to 6 kHz, consists of repeated, harmonic, and highly variable syllables. Our analysis revealed substantial variation in call parameters. Interestingly, we observed a notable discrepancy in call duration and bark count between recordings from captive and wild individuals, likely due to differences in recording methods. Wild recordings from iNaturalist, which are often used for species identification, were significantly shorter in duration.

The function of the barking call remains uncertain, although previous studies suggest it may serve as an alarm call30–32. Similar loud, long-distance vocalizations are common among many nocturnal arboreal mammals, including galagos12,14,76,77 and tree hyraxes15,78, where they are mostly uttered as social contact calls. These calls are often highly stereotyped and species-specific, making them useful for species identification. However, our findings suggest that the barking call in Petaurus species does not exhibit strong species-specificity. This could indicate weak selective pressure for call differentiation, implying that this vocalization may not play a crucial role in species recognition or mate selection. Instead, chemical communication might be more critical for these species. Additionally, given the recent taxonomic divergence among Petaurus species29, species-specific vocal distinctions may not have fully developed yet.

Although our phylogenetic analyses revealed clear genetic structuring and supported the distinctiveness of captive Petaurus papuanus, the acoustic data did not uncover strong species-specific differences in the barking call. This emphasizes the limitations of relying on single data types. To fully resolve the taxonomic boundaries and understand the evolutionary history of Petaurus, future studies should integrate genome-wide markers with a more extensive sampling, as demonstrated by recent genomic research on the closely related mahogany glider (Petaurus gracilis) and squirrel glider (Petaurus norfolcensis)79.

Conclusions

Our study highlights the importance of an integrative approach that combines different methodologies to resolve species identity in captive populations, particularly within cryptic complexes such as Petaurus breviceps s.l. Through phylogenetic analysis, we demonstrate that captive populations in Europe and the United States are best assigned to Petaurus papuanus, reflecting recent taxonomic revisions and highlighting the need for updated classifications in ex-situ programs. The unexpectedly high haplotype diversity observed in captivity likely stems from multiple introductions from genetically distinct but geographically proximate populations. While this diversity may offer short-term benefits, it also raises concerns about unrecognized hybridization and the long-term genetic integrity of captive lines. Our acoustic data suggest that barking calls lack species specificity, limiting their utility for species identification and reinforcing the need for genetic tools in cryptic taxa. Accurate taxonomic identification remains essential not only for effective captive management and conservation planning but also for ensuring that future reintroductions or conservation translocations are ecologically sound and genetically appropriate.
