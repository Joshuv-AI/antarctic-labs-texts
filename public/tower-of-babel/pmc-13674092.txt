Robotic repair of destructive tricuspid endocarditis.

Central Message

Robotic tricuspid valve repair is a feasible minimally invasive option for selected patients with tricuspid-valve endocarditis, enabling complete debridement while preserving the native valve.

The risk of tricuspid-valve infective endocarditis (TVIE) is strongly associated with intravenous drug use and Staphylococcus aureus infection. Surgery is indicated for persistent sepsis, recurrent septic pulmonary emboli, severe regurgitation, or extensive leaflet destruction.1 Repair is favored when feasible, especially in young patients with substance-use disorder who face recurrent bacteremia, reinfection, and reoperation risk after prosthetic implantation.1 We report port-access robotic tricuspid repair for healed methicillin-sensitive S aureus endocarditis with posterior leaflet perforation and severe regurgitation.

Case Presentation

Institutional review board approval was not required for this single-patient report. Written consent was obtained from the patient. A 28-year-old woman with opioid-use disorder in remission and hepatitis C presented with constitutional symptoms, leukocytosis, anemia, and methicillin-sensitive S aureus bacteremia. Findings of a computed tomography scan showed bilateral septic pulmonary emboli, and transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) showed a posterior tricuspid vegetation with moderate-to-severe regurgitation (Figure 1). Following microbiologic clearance of the blood stream infection, the initial therapy was medical. After 6 weeks of intravenous cefazolin, TEE demonstrated a persistent 10.6- × 9-mm mobile vegetation and a large perforation of the posterior tricuspid valve leaflet with severe regurgitation (Figure 2). Tricuspid surgery was recommended, and computed tomography scanning confirmed favorable iliofemoral anatomy. Operative Technique

Robotic-assisted tricuspid repair was performed through a right port-access approach using femoral arterial and venous cannulation. Valve competency was assessed by filling the right ventricle with saline via the suction irrigator until adequate chamber pressurization was achieved, without placement of a main pulmonary artery clamp, allowing direct visualization of leaflet coaptation under physiologic low-pressure conditions. After cardioplegic arrest and caval snaring, the right atrium was opened. A large vegetation involved nearly the entire posterior leaflet and extended toward the anterior leaflet. Infected and devitalized tissue was excised while preserving viable leaflet remnant, as demonstrated in Video 1. The residual tissue was thin and friable, requiring small, evenly spaced, interrupted pledgeted sutures soaked in rifampin and handled gently to avoid tearing. The posterior leaflet defect and posterior-septal commissural gap were closed without tension; saline testing confirmed coaptation. A 26-mm annuloplasty band was used to stabilize the annulus. Valve competency was assessed by pressurizing the right ventricle with saline delivered through the suction irrigator until adequately filled, without main pulmonary artery clamping, allowing direct visualization of leaflet coaptation (Figure 3). The annuloplasty band was secured using a running V-Loc PBT monofilament suture (Medtronic) to reduce the amount of implanted foreign material compared with conventional interrupted braided sutures. The 26-mm annuloplasty band was sized according to standard tricuspid annular anatomic landmarks, and the right pericardium was closed at the completion of the procedure. Bypass and crossclamp times were 152 and 77 minutes, respectively, attributed to the complexity of debridement. Postrepair TEE showed trivial residual regurgitation, mean gradient 1 mm Hg, and preserved ventricular function. Discussion

This case demonstrates that robotic tricuspid-valve repair is a feasible minimally invasive option for carefully selected patients with infective endocarditis. The robotic platform provides excellent visualization of the tricuspid valve and subvalvular apparatus, facilitating precise tissue handling and reconstruction while preserving the native valve.

This case highlights why repair is particularly attractive in drug abuse-related TVIE. Repair avoids a large prosthetic surface in patients vulnerable to recurrent bacteremia. Reinfection after valve injury or replacement in intravenous drug users has been reported up to 28%.1 Replacement adds risks of prosthetic-valve endocarditis, structural degeneration, right-sided prosthetic thrombosis, and other challenges if a mechanical valve is used. These hazards are amplified in young patients with substance-use disorder. Contemporary reviews therefore advocate repair over replacement when anatomically feasible.1 In 1 endocarditis series, reinfection was absent after repair with annuloplasty, whereas 21% of patients who underwent replacement required late reoperation for prosthetic-valve endocarditis.1 Data from the Society of Thoracic Surgeons database showed similar operative mortality for repair and replacement but greater morbidity after replacement, including more reoperations and prolonged ventilation.2

There is a narrow margin between sufficient radical debridement and preservation of enough tissue for durable coaptation. Here, the posterior leaflet was attenuated and perforated after debridement. Precise tissue assessment, avoiding traction, distributing force with interrupted pledgeted sutures, closing defects only when tension-free, and using annuloplasty to reduce leaflet stress were all prioritized and facilitated with robotic instruments, which is narrated in Video 1 and illustrates the complete operative technique. Published experience confirms that even heavily damaged tricuspid leaflets can be repaired with debridement, patch augmentation, neochordae, and annuloplasty when needed.1,3 Our case did not require patch or neochordae but followed the same principles: excise infected tissue, preserve viable structures, rebuild coaptation, and stabilize the annulus. A port-access approach without rib spreading facilitated the avoidance of narcotics, which is paramount in patients with substance abuse. Robotic tricuspid repair remains uncommon but feasible; a recent 70-patient series reported no conversion to sternotomy, 1.4% early mortality, and none-to-mild residual regurgitation in 93%.4

Careful patient selection is essential for robotic tricuspid-valve repair in infective endocarditis. Favorable candidates include those undergoing first-time cardiac surgery with preserved biventricular function, those with anatomy suitable for robotic access and peripheral cannulation, and those with localized valve pathology amenable to repair. A recent report published from the Nationwide Readmissions Database studied 40,738 patients undergoing surgical mitral valve repair, of whom 9.8% underwent robotic mitral valve repair. The authors demonstrated that patients who underwent robotic repair had similar short-term outcomes compared with conventional surgery and slightly increased cost overall whereas high-volume programs had similar risk adjusted cost with both approaches.5 Robotic repair should be reserved for carefully selected patients and performed by experienced minimally invasive valve surgeons.

Conclusions

Robotic port-access tricuspid repair can be an effective valve-preserving strategy for selected patients with drug abuse-related TVIE. It avoids prosthetic-valve hazards and minimizes narcotic use while preserving future options. Durable repair requires complete debridement, delicate handling of friable tissue, tension-free reconstruction, and annular stabilization.
