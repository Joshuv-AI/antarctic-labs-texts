A cost-effectiveness analysis of a new and more effective treatment for unresectable hepatocellular carcinoma—from a Chinese perspective
Authors: Jianhua Chen, Zuojun Dong, Bingchao Shou, Zimeng Huang, Li Huang, Guojun Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAnlotinib plus penpulimab has been proven to have good efficacy and safety in the treatment of unresectable hepatocellular carcinoma (uHCC). This study aims to evaluate the cost-effectiveness of anlotinib plus penpulimab compared with sorafenib in the treatment of uHCC from the perspective of the Chinese health system.MethodsBased on the APOLLO clinical trial, a Markov model was constructed, with a simulation time range of 10 years and a cycle of 3 weeks. Costs and quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) were used as model output indicators. The incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) was calculated through cost-effectiveness analysis, and a willingness-to-pay (WTP) threshold of $37,944.50 was set, which is three times the per capita GDP of China in 2023, to determine the economic feasibility of anlotinib plus penpulimab compared with sorafenib. Sensitivity analysis and scenario analysis were utilized to test the robustness of the model.ResultsThe basic analysis results indicated that the anlotinib plus penpulimab group spent $11,191 more than the sorafenib group but gained 0.56 QALYs. The ICER was $19,983.93/QALY, which was lower than three times the per capita GDP of China. The scenario analysis and sensitivity analysis demonstrated the robustness of the model. In 99.9% of the simulations, the incremental cost of each QALY gained by the anlotinib plus penpulimab group was less than $37,944.50.ConclusionUnder the threshold of three times China’s per capita GDP, anlotinib plus penpulimab is more cost-effective compared with sorafenib.

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is regarded as one of the main causes of cancer-related deaths in China [1]. The majority of HCC cases are diagnosed at an advanced stage, with a 5-year survival rate of less than 10% [2]. A study projected the economic cost of liver cancer in China from 2019 to 2030, indicating that the overall burden would rise from 11.1 billion US dollars in 2019 to 34 billion US dollars in 2030, hence placing a significant strain on society and the economy [3]. In the last ten years, many medications have received approval for the first-line treatment of uHCC, including immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), such as Tislelizumab [4], multi-target tyrosine kinase inhibitors, such as sorafenib [5,6], donafenib [7], and lenvatinib [8]. To overcome the limitations of single-agent therapy and provide better clinical outcomes, several combination therapies have also been approved, including atezolizumab and bevacizumab [9,10], a combination of sintilimab and a biosimilar of bevacizumab [11], and camrelizumab plus rivoceranib [12].

Wei J. et al. analyzed the cost-effectiveness of camrelizumab plus rivoceranib versus sorafenib in the treatment of uHCC from the perspective of Chinese consumers. The results showed that the new combination therapy gained an additional 0.64 QALYs, with an ICER value of $30,410.56/QALY, demonstrating economic efficiency [13]. Moreover, Liu L et al. found that from the perspective of the Chinese medical system, compared with sorafenib, atezolizumab and bevacizumab for the treatment of uHCC could gain an additional 0.47 QALYs, but the ICER value was $181,552.71/QALY, which was not cost-effective [14]. It can be seen that different treatment regimens result in different economic benefits. Recently, a phase III clinical trial (APOLLO) demonstrated that anlotinib plus penpulimab compared with sorafenib in the first-line treatment of uHCC significantly improved the median progression-free survival (6.9 months vs 2.8 months; hazard ratio [HR] 0.52; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.41–0.66; p < 0.0001) and median overall survival (16.5 months vs 13.2 months; HR 0.69 [95% CI 0.55–0.87]; p = 0.0014).

In the past, researchers conducted cost-effectiveness analyses of anlotinib and penpulimab for the treatment of small cell lung cancer [15,16], but there was a lack of cost-effectiveness analysis for anlotinib plus penpulimab in the treatment of uHCC. The absence of relevant cost-effectiveness analysis may hinder the use of this treatment regimen in China [17]. Therefore, our study aims to analyze the cost-effectiveness of anlotinib plus penpulimab as a first-line therapy for uHCC. This study utilized individual patient data from the APOLLO study, publicly available drug costs in China, and Chinese contemporary quality-of-life utility values to estimate total costs, QALYs, and ICERs. The research results are expected to provide economic evidence for health decision-making regarding the first-line treatment of uHCC with anlotinib plus penpulimab.

Methods

Study design

The characteristics of the model population in this study are derived from APOLLO [18]. In the APOLLO trial, patients in the anlotinib plus penpulimab group received oral anlotinib at a daily dosage of 10 mg (days 1–14) and intravenous penpulimab at a dosage of 200 mg (day 1). Conversely, patients in the sorafenib group received oral sorafenib at a dosage of 400 mg twice daily, following a 21-day cycle, until disease progression (PD) or the emergence of intolerable toxicity. According to the APOLLO trial, some patients received subsequent treatments (programmed death-(ligand) 1 inhibitors treatment, targeted therapy, interventional therapy, and traditional Chinese medicine treatment) after entering PD. Given that the APOLLO trial did not delineate the treatment protocols for future therapies, this investigation adheres to the Guidelines for the diagnosis and treatment of primary liver cancer [19], it was assumed that patients receiving programmed death-(ligand) 1(PD-(L) 1) inhibitors treatment received intravenous camrelizumab at a dose of 200 mg, with a 21-day cycle. Patients receiving targeted therapy treatment received oral apatinib at a dose of 750 mg once daily. Patients receiving interventional therapy treatment underwent transcatheter arterial chemoembolization (TACE) only at the beginning of the PD period. Patients receiving traditional Chinese medicine treatment received oral epimedium flavonoid soft capsules at a dose of 1200 mg once daily.

Model structure

Based on the APOLLO trial, we developed a Markov model using TreeAge Pro 2022, which included three independent states (Figure 1): progression-free survival (PFS), disease progression, and death. The model cycle length was set to 21 days based on the dosing schedule of the APOLLO trial. The simulation period was set to 10 years (determined as the time point when 99% of patients in the cohort died), allowing almost all patients in both groups to reach the death state. Therefore, this study was a lifetime analysis. All patients entered the model in the PFS state, and as the cycles progressed, patients could only transition from one state to another or remain in the current state. In each cycle, patients received the corresponding drug treatment, generating corresponding costs and health outcomes. According to the Chinese Guidelines for Pharmacoeconomic Evaluation (2020), when the study duration is more than one year, the study should discount future costs and health outputs. Therefore, we set the discount rates for costs and utilities at 5% as recommended by the guidelines [20]. The main outputs of this model included costs, QALYs, and ICERs.

Clinical data

We used the GetData Graph Digitizer software and R4.5.1 software for data processing. We calculated parameters and selected the best-fitting distribution using various distributions, including exponential, Weibull, Gamma, Gompertz, loglogistic, and lognormal [21]. Based on the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) (Figures 2 and 3), and visual inspection, the best parameter distribution with the lowest AIC/BIC and the best visual consistency was selected [22]. Subsequently, the best fitting curves for PFS and OS were further confirmed according to the opinions of oncologists. The results showed that the loglogistic distribution was more accurate for fitting the OS of the anlotinib plus penpulimab group, and the lognormal distribution was more accurate for fitting the OS of the sorafenib group and the PFS of both groups. Cost and utility values

This model only includes direct medical expenses within the context of China’s health system. This include pharmaceutical expenses, routine follow-up expenditures, charges related to the treatment of adverse events (AEs), and expenses for end-of-life care. Only adverse events of grade 3 or higher with an incidence rate above 5% were included in the cost calculation. Moreover, it is assumed that all adverse events occur in the first treatment cycle, and the same patient can experience multiple AEs. All cost statistics were expressed in 2023 US dollars ($), with Chinese yuan (RMB) converted to USD utilising the 2023 exchange rate (1 USD = 7.2537 RMB). Costs not initially provided in 2023 values were modified to correspond with 2023 prices utilising the consumer price index. Cost estimations were obtained from public databases and peer-reviewed literature. The incidence rates of serious AEs and subsequent treatment patterns were obtained from the APOLLO study. According to data from the National Bureau of Statistics (https://data.stats.gov.cn/index.htm), the per capita GDP in 2023 was $12,648.2. As the APOLLO trial lacked corresponding utility values, Utility values were derived from previously published research [14]. Utility value of 0.76 was assigned to patients in PFS stage, whereas utility value of 0.68 was designated for those in PD stage. The model parameters are included in Table 1.

Sensitivity analysis

We used one-way sensitivity analysis (OWSA) and probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA) to verify the robustness of the changes in key parameters in the model. In the OWSA, each variable was adjusted within a reasonable range, with the 95% CI range based on relevant literature or ±20% change relative to the baseline value, to identify variables that have a significant impact on economic outcomes. According to the Chinese Guidelines for Pharmacoeconomic Evaluation (2020) [20], we set the maximum and minimum values of the discount rate at 0.08 and 0. The results would be presented in the form of tornado diagrams. In the PSA, parameters were set as random variables with specific distributions, and 10,000 Monte Carlo simulations were conducted to obtain scatter plots, thereby testing the robustness of the results. The results were presented in the form of scatter plots and acceptability curves.

Scenario analysis

In the pharmacoeconomic analysis, the price changes of anlotinib and penpulimab affected the model results and ICER. To better understand the influence of drug pricing policies in China, a scenario analysis was conducted to examine how different levels of price reductions affect the ICER. In this context, newly approved therapies often experience significant price decreases through government-led negotiations after market approval [24]. We looked up the prices of anlotinib (in 2018) and penpulimab (in 2021) when they were first launched in China, which were the prices prior to their inclusion in China’s National Reimbursement Drug List (NRDL), to assess the impact of such price adjustments on cost-effectiveness. Additionally, we calculated the probability that the new treatment regimens would be deemed cost-effective under WTP thresholds corresponding to 1, 2, and 3 times the national per capita GDP.

Results

Basic analysis results

Based on the Markov model run over a 10-year simulation period, the basic analysis results for each treatment group were generated, including total costs and QALYs. The anlotinib plus penpulimab group was associated with total costs of $16,440 and a cumulative QALY value of 1.82, compared to $5249 in total costs and 1.26 QALYs for the sorafenib monotherapy group. Compared with the sorafenib group, the anlotinib plus penpulimab group exhibited an additional cost of $11,191, yielding an ICER of $19,983.93 per QALY gained. When the WTP was established at three times China’s per capita GDP in 2023 ($37,944.50), the WTP surpassed the ICER, indicating that the combination of anlotinib plus penpulimab was a cost-effective alternative. Scenario analysis

According to the results of the scenario analysis (Table 3), in China, if the price of anlotinib combined with penpulimab remains at pre-listing market price, the ICER will surpass three times the per capita GDP of China in 2023. Nonetheless, with 90% decrease in medicine price, the ICER diminishes to $7787.5 per QALY, well below the threshold of $12,648.17—corresponding to the 2023 per capita GDP of China. The ICER is also lower than the $19,983.93 per QALY seen following the drug’s inclusion in the NRDL. The probabilities of anlotinib plus penpulimab being economical is 99.9%, 83.2%, and 5.2% when the WTP threshold is set at three times, twice, and once the 2023 per capita GDP of China, respectively (Figure 7).

Discussion

According to our research findings, in the first-line treatment of uHCC, anlotinib plus penpulimab group is more cost-effective compared to the sorafenib group. This result was further validated by sensitivity analysis. The single-factor sensitivity analysis showed that the cost of anlotinib plus penpulimab group had the greatest impact on the outcome, followed by the utility value of PD, and then the utility value of PFS. The ICER value obtained from this study’s analysis was $19,983.93/QALY. Compared with the ICER values of camrelizumab plus rivoceranib ($30,410.56/QALY) [13], atezolizumab plus bevacizumab ($181,552.71/QALY) [14], donafenib ($121,059/QALY) [2], and lenvatinib ($68,869/QALY) [2] reported under Chinese conditions, the current model indicates that anlotinib plus penpulimab may represent a more economically efficient option in first-line treatment. These comparisons highlight the emerging role of domestic drug combinations in achieving cost-effectiveness under national reimbursement restrictions. Moreover, recent evidence highlights the clinical benefit of combining TACE with systemic therapy in advanced HCC, which should also be considered in future cost-effectiveness models [27].

According to the statistics in the China Statistical Yearbook [28], the per capita regional GDP varies across different regions in China. Therefore, the cost-effectiveness of the new treatment plan also differs in various regions. To demonstrate the cost-effectiveness of the new treatment plan, regions with a high per capita regional GDP, such as Beijing ($27,610.46), only need to set the WTP at one times the per capita regional GDP. Regions with a relatively high per capita regional GDP, such as Zhejiang ($17,238.51), need to set the WTP at two times the per capita regional GDP. Regions with a lower per capita regional GDP, such as Jiangxi ($9817.89), need to set the WTP at three times the per capita regional GDP. However, in regions with a very low per capita regional GDP, such as Gansu ($6598.98), even setting the WTP at three times the per capita regional GDP, the new treatment plan still does not show cost-effectiveness.

HCC is a common malignant tumor with limited effective treatment options [29]. At the same time, the treatment cost for HCC is also quite high. The Chinese government has made tremendous efforts in reimbursement policies to enable the public to access and afford these drugs. Since 2017, the marketing authorization holders (MAH) and the national medical insurance agency have conducted price negotiations. Drugs that successfully negotiate will be included in the national medical insurance drug list [30]. The average price reduction for drugs included in the national medical insurance drug list ranges from 44.0% to 60.7% [31]. The average accessibility of cancer treatment drugs in China has increased by 19.89% [32]. This has to some extent alleviated the financial pressure on liver cancer patients and enabled more patients to receive drug treatment. Our research results show that in the vast majority of regions in China, the new treatment plan is more cost-effective than sorafenib. However, in regions with a low per capita regional GDP, sorafenib is more cost-effective for the treatment of uHCC. If the relatively expensive penpulimab in the new treatment plan can be further reduced in price, the cost-effectiveness of the new treatment plan will be even higher.

There are some limitations in this analysis. First, we used parameter distribution to fit the Kaplan-Meier PFS and OS data in APOLLO to estimate the health benefits beyond the follow-up period. Although efforts have been made to validate the model and observational data, this approach may introduce uncertainty to the model output. If this method is used for future research, medical artificial intelligence can be utilized for further validation to reduce the uncertainty of model input [33–34]. Second, The drug prices in this study are based on those under China’s medical reimbursement policy. However, the medical reimbursement policies vary from country to country, the external validity of its results for other global populations is limited. Additional studies are required to assess the cost-effectiveness of anlotinib plus penpulimab regimen in various geographical settings. Third, the subsequent treatment strategies in the APOLLO trial were not clearly specified. To estimate second-line treatment costs, we selected camrelizumab (PD-(L)1 inhibitor), apatinib (targeted therapy), TACE (interventional therapy), and epimedium flavonoid soft capsules (traditional Chinese medicine) as representative examples. However, alternative agents within these categories may also be used in clinical practice. Fourth, our model input only included the costs related to AEs of grade 3 and above that exceeded 5%, which might lead to an overestimation of the economic benefits of anlotinib plus penpulimab. However, according to the results of the OWSA, the AEs costs had a minor impact on the model output, so this overestimation would not affect the cost-effectiveness results. Lastly, in this analysis, utility values for patients with uHCC were sourced from existing literature. However, evidence from APOLLO suggests that the combination of anlotinib and penpulimab significantly postponed the deterioration of quality of life during both the PFS and PD phases. Utilizing the same utility coefficients for both treatment arms may result in an underestimation of the real-world clinical benefits associated with this combination therapy.

Conclusions

Our research findings indicate that, within the context of China’s healthcare system, anlotinib combined with penpulimab offers a cost-effective treatment option for patients with uHCC compared to sorafenib, especially when considering the current reimbursement structure.
