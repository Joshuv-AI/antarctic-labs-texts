1.
Introduction

Let
U
be a space with the property that its one point compactification
U^{*}
admits a finite cell structure for which the added point
*
is the only
0
-cell. So such a space cannot have a compact connected component. An example to keep in mind is that of the interior of a compact topological manifold with boundary without closed components (so that
U^{*}
is obtained by contracting the boundary); we then say that
U
is an
open manifold of finite type
.

This paper is about the configuration spaces of such
U
. We find it convenient to approach these as a functor.
To be concrete, we consider for any finite set
N
of size
n\geq 0
the space
\mathscr{C}\!\mathit{onf}\!_{N}(U)
of injective maps
N\to U
(so this is a singleton when
N=\emptyset
). When we write
\mathscr{C}\!\mathit{onf}\!_{n}(U)
, it is understood that
n
takes the role of
[n]=\{1,2,\dots,n\}
.

We regard
\mathscr{C}\!\mathit{onf}\!_{N}(U)
as a subset of
U^{*}{}^{N}
. Its complement, which we shall denote by
D_{N}(U)
, consists of the set of maps
f:N\to U^{*}
which have
*
in their image or fail to be injective (so if
N=\emptyset
, then
D_{N}(U)=\emptyset
). This subset is obviously closed. Here is a simple, but as we will see, basic example.

Example 1.1
.

If
U={\mathbb{R}}
, then its
1
-point compactification is the real projective line
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}({\mathbb{R}})
. For any injective map

f:N\to{\mathbb{R}}
from a finite set
N
, the natural order of
{\mathbb{R}}
induces a total order on
N
. This total order, given by a bijection
\varphi:[n]\cong N
, characterizes the connected component of
f
in
\mathscr{C}\!\mathit{onf}\!_{N}({\mathbb{R}})
, for it will consist of the subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
given by

t_{\varphi(1)}<t_{\varphi(1)}<\cdots<t_{\varphi(n)}
. We shall think of this as the relative interior of the
n
-simplex
-\infty\leq s_{1}\leq\cdots\leq s_{n}\leq\infty
mapped to
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}({\mathbb{R}})^{N}
according to
\varphi
, so that its boundary maps to
D_{N}({\mathbb{R}})
. If
{\mathbf{N}}
stands for
N
equipped with this total order, then we denote the
n
-simplex thus defined by
\triangle^{\mathbf{N}}
.

This example also illustrates the following (rather easy) theorem, which will be our point of departure.

Theorem 1.2
.

The pair
(U^{*}{}^{N},D_{N}(U))
is
(n-1)
-connected. In fact, it has the structure of a relative finite cell complex with cells of dimension
\geq n
only, such that
the
n
-cells of
\mathscr{C}\!\mathit{onf}\!_{N}(U)
are the connected components of
\mathscr{C}\!\mathit{onf}\!_{N}
of the union of the
1
-cells in
U
and, more generally,
every
(n+d)
-cell of
\mathscr{C}\!\mathit{onf}\!_{N}(U)
is contained in
\mathscr{C}\!\mathit{onf}\!_{N}
of the
d
-skeleton of
U
.

So the first possibly nonzero homology group of this pair is in degree
n
; we denote that homology group

{\mathcal{H}}_{N}(U):=H_{n}(U^{*}{}^{N},D_{N}(U)).

Observe that this is also the homology with closed support
H_{n}^{cl}(\mathscr{C}\!\mathit{onf}\!_{N}(U))
and that for
N
empty resp. a singleton, this is
{\mathbb{Z}}
resp.
H_{1}^{cl}(U)
.

Example
1.1
shows that
{\mathcal{H}}_{N}({\mathbb{R}})
is the free abelian group generated by the collection
\triangle^{\mathbf{N}}
, which is indexed by the set of total orders on
N
.

The assignment
N\mapsto{\mathcal{H}}_{N}(U)
has a number of naturality properties worth spelling out. First, there is the functoriality in
N
:
a bijection of finite sets
N\cong N^{\prime}
induces an isomorphism
{\mathcal{H}}_{N}(U)\cong{\mathcal{H}}_{N^{\prime}}(U)
; in particular, the permutation group
\mathfrak{S}_{N}
of
N
acts on
{\mathcal{H}}_{N}(U)
. So this defines a functor from the groupoid

\underline{\mathcal{F\!B}}
whose objects are finite sets and whose morphisms are bijections (
F
for finite,
B
for bijections) to the category of abelian groups:

{\underline{{\mathcal{H}}}}(U):\underline{\mathcal{F\!B}}\to\underline{\mathcal{A}b}.

This functor is monoidal: if
N
is the disjoint union of
N^{\prime}
and
N^{\prime\prime}
, then
the inclusion
\mathscr{C}\!\mathit{onf}\!_{N}(U)\subset\mathscr{C}\!\mathit{onf}\!_{N^{\prime}}(U)\times\mathscr{C}\!\mathit{onf}\!_{N^{\prime\prime}}(U)
defines a Künneth product

\times:{\mathcal{H}}_{N^{\prime}}(U)\otimes{\mathcal{H}}_{N^{\prime\prime}}(U)\to{\mathcal{H}}_{N}(U)

that is associative and functorial in its arguments and is the obvious isomorphism if
N^{\prime}
or
N^{\prime\prime}
is empty.

The other type of functoriality is in the spatial argument: for
U
and
U^{\prime}
as above, a continuous map
f:(U^{*},*)\to(U^{\prime}{}^{*},*)
of pointed spaces induces a map of pairs
(U^{*}{}^{N},D_{N}(U))\to(U^{\prime}{}^{*}{}^{N},D_{N}(U^{\prime}))
and hence induces a map
{\mathcal{H}}_{N}(U)\to{\mathcal{H}}_{N}(U^{\prime})
. This defines a natural transformation

{\underline{{\mathcal{H}}}}(f):{\underline{{\mathcal{H}}}}(U)\Rightarrow{\underline{{\mathcal{H}}}}(U^{\prime})

of monoidal functors. It is clear that this depends only on the relative homotopy class of
f
. In particular the group

\operatorname{Htp}(U^{*},*)
of self-homotopy equivalences of
(U^{*},*)
acts on
{\mathcal{H}}_{N}(U)
, an action which commutes with that of
\mathfrak{S}_{N}
.
So if we let
\underline{{\mathcal{H}}\!pt}^{*}
stand for the category whose objects are the spaces
U
considered here and morphisms are pointed homotopy classes as above, then we have defined
a functor

(1.1)

{\underline{{\mathcal{H}}}}:\underline{{\mathcal{H}}\!pt}^{*}\to\underline{\mathbf{Mon}}(\underline{\mathcal{F\!B}},\underline{\mathcal{A}b})

to the functor category whose objects are monoidal functors and morphisms are their monoidal natural transformations. This functor is itself monoidal and is so in an even stronger sense: if
U
is the disjoint union of open subsets
U^{\prime}
and
U^{\prime\prime}
, then

\mathscr{C}\!\mathit{onf}\!_{N}(U)
is the disjoint union of the products
\mathscr{C}\!\mathit{onf}\!_{N^{\prime}}(U^{\prime})\times\mathscr{C}\!\mathit{onf}\!_{N\smallsetminus N^{\prime}}(U^{\prime\prime})
, where
N^{\prime}
runs over all the subsets of
N
and hence we have an identification

(1.2)

{\mathcal{H}}_{N}(U)\cong\oplus_{N^{\prime}\subset N}{\mathcal{H}}_{N^{\prime}}(U^{\prime})\otimes{\mathcal{H}}_{N\smallsetminus N^{\prime}}(U^{\prime\prime}).

by the Künneth formula.
The right hand side comes from an obvious monoidal structure on
\underline{\mathbf{Mon}}(\underline{\mathcal{F\!B}},\underline{\mathcal{A}b})
, so that this amounts to a strict equivalence

{\underline{{\mathcal{H}}}}(U)\sim{\underline{{\mathcal{H}}}}(U^{\prime})\otimes{\underline{{\mathcal{H}}}}(U^{\prime\prime})
.
Both functors
{\underline{{\mathcal{H}}}}(U)
and
{\underline{{\mathcal{H}}}}
become
symmetric
monoidal if we replace
\underline{\mathcal{A}b}
by the category
graded
abelian groups (which assigns to
{\mathcal{H}}_{N}(U)
the degree
|N|
), as there is a Koszul rule to obey when we exchange factors.

Since the fundamental group
\pi_{1}(U^{*},*)
plays a central role in what follows, we shall abbreviate this group by
\pi_{U}
.
Theorem
1.2
implies that
{\mathcal{H}}_{N}(U)
depends only on the
2
-skeleton of
U^{*}
, but the homotopy discussion leads even to:

Corollary 1.3
.

The group
{\mathcal{H}}_{N}(U)
depends only on the fundamental group
\pi_{U}=\pi_{1}(U^{*},*)
; in particular,
\operatorname{Htp}(U^{*},*)
acts on
{\mathcal{H}}_{N}(U)
through its action on
\pi_{U}
. To be precise, the monoidal functor

{\underline{{\mathcal{H}}}}
factors through a monoidal functor
\underline{\pi{\mathcal{H}}}
from the category
of finitely presented groups to
\underline{\mathbf{Mon}}(\underline{\mathcal{F\!B}},\underline{\mathcal{A}b})
taking a free product
G_{1}*G_{2}
to
\underline{\pi{\mathcal{H}}}(G_{1})\otimes\underline{\pi{\mathcal{H}}}(G_{2})
, such that an isomorphism
G\cong\pi_{U}
induces an equivalence

\underline{\pi{\mathcal{H}}}(G)\sim{\underline{{\mathcal{H}}}}(U)
.

If
U
happens to be an oriented open
d
-manifold of finite type, then a total order on
N
gives an orientation of
U^{N}

and then

{\mathcal{H}}_{N}(U)\cong H^{(d-1)n}(\mathscr{C}\!\mathit{onf}\!_{N}(U))\otimes(\operatorname{sign}_{N})^{\otimes d}

by Poincaré–Lefschetz duality, where
\operatorname{sign}_{N}:\mathfrak{S}_{N}\to\{\pm\}
is the sign character. In this setting, it is natural to restrict the
\operatorname{Htp}(U^{*},*)
-action to that of the topological mapping class group
\operatorname{Mod}(U)
, i.e., the connected component group of the group of orientation preserving self-homeomorphisms of
U
. Corollary
1.3
above shows that this action is through its action on
\pi_{U}
.
When
d=2
and
U
is connected (which in the end is the case that interests us most), then
U
is of the form
X\smallsetminus\{p_{0},\dots,p_{l}\}
, where
X
is a closed oriented surface and the
p_{i}\in X
are distinct. So
U^{*}
has then the homotopy type of a wedge of
X
and
l
circles.
Since the latter is the
1
-point compactification of the disjoint union of
X\smallsetminus\{p_{0}\}
and
l
copies of
{\mathbb{R}}
, it follows that

{\underline{{\mathcal{H}}}}(U)\cong{\underline{{\mathcal{H}}}}(X\smallsetminus\{p_{0}\})\otimes{\underline{{\mathcal{H}}}}({\mathbb{R}})^{\otimes l}.

This gives the case
k=0
of the following more general theorem.

Theorem 1.4
.

For
0\leq k\leq n
and a set
N
of size
n
, the abelian group
H^{n-k}(\mathscr{C}\!\mathit{onf}\!_{N}(U))
is isomorphic to

\oplus_{(N_{0},N_{1},\dots,N_{l})}H^{n_{0}-k}(\mathscr{C}\!\mathit{onf}\!_{N_{0}}(X\smallsetminus\{p_{0}\}))\otimes H^{n_{1}}(\mathscr{C}\!\mathit{onf}\!_{N_{1}}({\mathbb{R}}))\otimes\dots H^{n_{l}}(\mathscr{C}\!\mathit{onf}\!_{N_{l}}({\mathbb{R}})),

where the sum is over all partitions
N=N_{0}\sqcup N_{1}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup N_{l}
of
N
into numbered subsets and
n_{i}=|N_{i}|
. For
k<0
, both
H^{n-k}(\mathscr{C}\!\mathit{onf}\!_{N}(U))
and the direct sum vanish.

In Section
3.6
we discuss to what extent this isomorphism is natural.

Here is a direct way to produce elements of
{\mathcal{H}}_{N}(U)
from the fundamental group
\pi_{U}
.
Represent any
\gamma\in\pi_{U}
by a map
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}({\mathbb{R}})\to U^{*}
which takes
\infty
to
*
so that is defined

{\mathcal{H}}_{N}(\gamma):{\mathcal{H}}_{N}({\mathbb{R}})\to{\mathcal{H}}_{N}(U)
. Every total order on
N
defines a generator
\triangle^{\mathbf{N}}
of
{\mathcal{H}}_{N}({\mathbb{R}})
and therefore gives us an element
{\mathcal{H}}_{N}(\gamma)(\triangle^{\mathbf{N}})\in{\mathcal{H}}_{N}(U)
. We shall denote the latter by
\triangle^{\mathbf{N}}(\gamma)
. It is clear that this extends to a linear map from the group ring
{\mathbb{Z}}\pi_{U}
of
\pi_{U}
to
{\mathcal{H}}_{N}(U)
. Let us write
\Lambda_{U}
for
{\mathbb{Z}}\pi_{U}
and denote the kernel of the augmentation

{\mathbb{Z}}\pi_{U}\to{\mathbb{Z}}
(which is constant
1
on the basis of
\pi_{U}
) by
{\mathcal{I}}_{U}
. A theorem that goes back to Beilinson (see e.g.
[
5
]
) implies that
\triangle^{\mathbf{N}}(\gamma)
depends only on the image of
\gamma
in the truncated group ring
\Lambda_{U}|_{n}:=\Lambda_{U}/{\mathcal{I}}_{U}^{n+1}
, so that this construction factors through a linear map

\displaystyle\triangle_{U}^{\mathbf{N}}:\Lambda_{U}|_{n}\to{\mathcal{H}}_{N}(U).

This map is clearly
\operatorname{Htp}(U^{*},*)
-equivariant. The obvious map
\pi_{U}\to\Lambda_{U}|_{n}
takes values in the group of units of
\Lambda_{U}|_{n}
and is then a group homomorphism. Since the action of
\pi_{U}
on
\Lambda_{U}|_{n}
by left multiplication is unipotent of degree
n
(it acts trivially on each successive quotient

{\mathcal{I}}_{U}^{k}/{\mathcal{I}}_{U}^{k+1}
), the kernel of this map contains the
(n+1)
st term

\pi_{U}^{(n+1)}
of the central descending series, so that it factors through
\pi_{U}|_{n}:=\pi_{U}/\pi_{U}^{(n+1)}
.
Let us also observe that since
1\in\Lambda_{U}
represents the constant loop,
\triangle_{U}^{\mathbf{N}}
takes on
1
the value
0
when
n>0
. So then the image of
\triangle_{U}^{\mathbf{N}}
does not change if we restrict it to
{\mathcal{I}}_{U}|_{n}:={\mathcal{I}}_{U}/{\mathcal{I}}_{U}^{n+1}
.

With the help of the monoidal structure we now get many more elements: for any partition
N=N_{1}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup N_{r}
into nonempty subsets (so the size
n_{i}
of
N_{i}
is positive) and a choice of a total order on each
N_{i}
(indicated by the use of the boldface font
{\mathbf{N}}_{i}
), we get a linear map

(1.3)

\triangle_{U}^{({\mathbf{N}}_{1},\dots,{\mathbf{N}}_{r})}:{\mathcal{I}}_{U}|_{n_{1}}\otimes{\mathcal{I}}_{U}|_{n_{2}}\otimes\cdots\otimes{\mathcal{I}}_{U}|_{n_{r}}\xrightarrow{\triangle_{U}^{{\mathbf{N}}_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\triangle_{U}^{{\mathbf{N}}_{r}}}\\
\to{\mathcal{H}}_{N_{1}}(U)\otimes{\mathcal{H}}_{N_{2}}(U)\otimes\cdots\otimes{\mathcal{H}}_{N_{r}}(U)\to{\mathcal{H}}_{N}(U).

Theorem 1.5
.

Assume
n>0
. Then the images of the maps (
1.3
) generate
{\mathcal{H}}_{N}(U)
. We already get a set of generators by evaluating these maps on tensors of the form
(\gamma_{c_{1}}-1)|_{n_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes(\gamma_{c_{r}}-1)|_{n_{r}}
, where each
c_{i}
is a
1
-cell of
U^{*}
and
\gamma_{c_{i}}\in\pi_{U}
is defined by an orientation of
c_{i}
.

In particular, the group
\operatorname{Htp}(U^{*},*)
of self-homotopy equivalences acts on
{\mathcal{H}}_{N}(U)
through its action on
{\mathcal{I}}_{U}|_{n}
.

This gives rise to a descending filtration
F^{\scriptscriptstyle\bullet}{\mathcal{H}}_{N}(U)
of
{\mathcal{H}}_{N}(U)
by taking for
F^{s}{\mathcal{H}}_{N}(U)
the subgroup of

{\mathcal{H}}_{N}(U)
spanned by the images of the maps
{\mathcal{H}}_{N_{1}}(U)\otimes{\mathcal{H}}_{N_{2}}(U)\otimes\cdots\otimes{\mathcal{H}}_{N_{r}}(U)\to{\mathcal{H}}_{N}(U)
for which
N_{i}\not=\emptyset
and
r\geq s
, so that

{\mathcal{H}}_{N}(U)=F^{1}{\mathcal{H}}_{N}(U)\supset\cdots\supset F^{n}{\mathcal{H}}_{N}(U)\supset F^{n+1}{\mathcal{H}}_{N}(U)=0.

So
F^{n}{\mathcal{H}}_{N}(U)
appears as a quotient of
H^{cl}_{1}(U)^{\otimes N}
. It follows from Theorem
1.5
that
F^{k}{\mathcal{H}}_{N}(U)/F^{k+l}({\mathcal{H}}_{N}U)
is a quotient of a direct sum of a finite number of copies of
{\mathcal{I}}_{U}|_{l+1}
.

With the help of the
\triangle
-maps, we can also describe
\underline{\pi{\mathcal{H}}}(G)
in terms of a finite presentation of
G
(Corollary
2.11
). In the case of only one relation, or equivalently one
2
-cell, we will find a categorical bar complex which allows us to address all homological degrees.

Theorem 1.6
.

If
U^{*}
is a
2
-complex with a single
2
-cell, then
\operatorname{Htp}(U^{*},*)
acts on
H^{cl}_{n+k}(\mathscr{C}\!\mathit{onf}\!_{N}(U))

through its action on
\Lambda_{U}|_{n-k}

In this case
\pi_{U}
is a one relator group. If furthermore the relation is
primitive
(i.e. not a proper power of another element of the free group), then a theorem of Labute
[
4
]
tells us that
({\mathcal{I}}_{U}^{n+1}+1)\cap\pi_{U}=\pi_{U}^{(n+1)}
, so that the kernel of the
\operatorname{Htp}(U,*)
-action on the quotient
\pi_{U}|_{n}=\pi_{U}{/\pi_{U}^{(n+1)}}
is the same as that of its action on
\Lambda_{U}|_{n}
(or
{\mathcal{I}}_{U}|_{n}
for that matter).

This covers the case when
U
is a connected oriented open surface of finite type.
So then the kernel of the
\operatorname{Htp}(U,*)
-action on
\pi_{U}|_{n}
is the same as that of its its action on
\Lambda_{U}|_{n}
(or
{\mathcal{I}}_{U}|_{n}
for that matter). The mapping class group
\operatorname{Mod}(U)
acts on
H^{k}(\mathscr{C}\!\mathit{onf}\!_{N}(U))\cong H^{cl}_{2n-k}(\mathscr{C}\!\mathit{onf}\!_{N}(U))
through
\operatorname{Htp}(U^{*},*)
and Theorem

implies that it then does so via its action on
\pi_{U}|_{k}
. So the kernel of the latter action, which is known as the
k
th Johnson group
J^{k}\operatorname{Mod}(U)
, acts trivially on
H^{k}(\mathscr{C}\!\mathit{onf}\!_{N}(U))
.
We derive from this a similar result for a closed surface (with a better range for the cohomology in top degree
n+1
, see Corollary
3.3
).

When
U
is a once punctured surface (so that
U^{*}
is a closed surface), this is due to Bianchi–Miller–Wilson
[
1
]
. They also conjectured that for
k
as above, the group
J^{k}\operatorname{Mod}(U)
is exactly the kernel of this action. We find however that
this is not so: we show that the common kernel
{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathit{cfg}}_{n}(U)\subset{\mathcal{I}}_{U}|_{n}
of the maps

\triangle_{U}^{\mathbf{N}}:{\mathcal{I}}_{U}|_{n}\to{\mathcal{H}}_{N}(U)
(which is of course
\operatorname{Mod}(U)
-invariant) is nonzero when the genus of
U
is
\geq 2
and
n\geq 4

and we construct elements of
\operatorname{Mod}(U)
that act nontrivially on
{\mathcal{I}}_{U}|_{n}
but trivially on
{\mathcal{I}}_{U}|_{n}/{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathit{cfg}}_{n}(U)
.
In order to determine the exact kernel, we must determine
{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathit{cfg}}_{n}(U)
. This will be the subject of a subsequent paper by the second author.

Related work

The first instance we are aware of concerns the case when
U^{*}
is a compact oriented compact
surface of genus
g>0
with connected boundary and
*
is a boundary point. In this case, we can arrange that the
1
-skeleton
U_{1}^{*}
is a wedge if
2g
circles and the inclusion
(U_{1}^{*},*)\subset(U^{*},*)
is then a homotopy equivalence. Moriyama
[
6
]
proved that then the reduced homology of
\mathscr{C}\!\mathit{onf}\!_{n}(U)
is concentrated in the middle dimension
n
and that the action of

\operatorname{Mod}(U)
on
{\mathcal{H}}_{n}(U)
has the same kernel as its action on
\pi_{U}|_{n}
(note that the first assertion is a special case of Theorem
1.2
; we will reprove the second assertion below). Subsequently Bianchi–Miller–Wilson
[
1
]
dealt with the case when

U
is a once-punctured connected oriented closed surface of genus
g>0
, as mentioned above.
Bianchi–Stavrou
[
2
]
exhibited elements of
J^{n-1}\operatorname{Mod}(U)
(
n\geq 2
) that act nontrivially on
H_{n}(\mathscr{C}\!\mathit{onf}\!_{n}(U))
.

Richard Hain and Clément Dupont recently obtained results similar to ours, such as a version of Theorem
1.6
. Their main tool is mixed Hodge theory. This leads them to use cohomology with rational coefficients and to use the ‘weight analogue’ of the Johnson filtration. This weight filtration is in general coarser than the Johnson filtration, but the two coincide for a closed surface
X
. They informed us that in that case they established that if the genus of
X
is
\geq 2
and
n\leq 5
, then the kernel of
\operatorname{Mod}(X)
on
H^{n}(\mathscr{C}\!\mathit{onf}\!_{n}(X);{\mathbb{Q}})
is precisely the Johnson subgroup
J^{n}\operatorname{Mod}(X)
.

Notational conventions

A composition of
k\geq 2
paths

\gamma_{1},\cdots,\gamma_{k}
with domain
[0,1]
without parentheses:
\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}\cdots\gamma_{k}
, denotes their
concatenation
, which is by definition their juxtaposition defined on
[0,k]
, followed by rescaling by
1/k
.

If
V
is a finitely generated abelian group, then for every finite set
I
,
V^{\otimes I}
is defined as usual, namely as a quotient of the free abelian group generated by
V^{I}
. This quotient is characterized by the universal property that for every multilinear map from
V^{I}
to an abelian group
V^{\prime}
uniquely factors through a linear map
V^{\otimes I}\to V^{\prime}
. So
V^{\emptyset}={\mathbb{Z}}
and
V^{I}\otimes V^{J}
is canonically identified with
V^{I\sqcup J}
. For an integer
n\geq 0
,
V^{\otimes n}
stands for
V^{[n]}
. If
I
is totally ordered and of size
n
, then we have a unique order preserving bijection of
I
with
\{1,2,\dots,n\}
giving an isomorphism
V^{\otimes I}\cong V^{\otimes n}
.

We use the boldface font to indicate a totally ordered set. For example, if a set
N
has been endowed with a total order, we may denote it by
{\mathbf{N}}
.

2.
Basic properties of our monoidal functor

We begin with the proof of the first two properties stated in the introduction. Unless mentioned otherwise,
(U^{*},*)
is a pointed finite complex having
\{*\}
as its only
0
-cell. We also assume that
each
1
-cell
c
has been oriented
, so that it defines an element
\gamma_{c}\in\pi_{U}
. Let
K
be a finite set. We construct a cell decomposition for
U^{*}{}^{K}
which makes

a subcomplex.

2.1.
The lexicographic stratification of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

We first do this for
U={\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, so that
\mathscr{C}\!\mathit{onf}\!_{K}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
becomes a union of cells of dimension
\geq|K|+d-1
. We note that Example
1.1
is the special case of this in
d=1
; for
d=2
, this is known as the Fuchs-Neuwirth stratification.

Firstly, given any
f\in({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{K}
, i.e., a map
f:K\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, we let
K\twoheadrightarrow\overline{K}
be the quotient of
K
such that
f
factors through an injection of
{\overline{K}}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Conversely, this quotient must be thought of as defining the ‘diagonal stratum’ of
({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{K}
that consists of all

f\in({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{K}
that factor through an embedding of
\overline{K}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Observe that
\mathscr{C}\!\mathit{onf}\!_{K}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
here appears as the stratum defined by the case
\overline{K}=K
.

Now, consider the lexicographic total order
\prec
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
defined by

x\prec y
if, for some
k\in[d]
,
x_{i}=y_{i}
for all
i=1,\dots,k-1
and
x_{k}<y_{k}
. Then the induced injection
\bar{f}:\overline{K}\hookrightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
endows also
\overline{K}
with a total order
inherited from
\prec
. This total order has an additional structure that comes from the lexicographical nature of
\prec
, and it is this structure that defines the cell containing
f
. We obtain it by taking the successive images of
K
under the maps
(f_{1},\dots,f_{i})
, where
f=(f_{1},\dots,f_{d})
, i.e. the maps that remember only the first
i
coordinates of
f
. This gives a sequence of surjections

\overline{K}=K_{d}\twoheadrightarrow K_{d-1}\twoheadrightarrow\cdots K_{1}\twoheadrightarrow K_{0}

(where we added the map to the singleton
K_{0}={\mathbb{R}}^{0}
for convenience) with a total order on each term such that the maps between them are non-decreasing. We call this a
lexicographic structure
on
K
.

We claim that the collection of
f\in({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{K}
giving rise to the same lexicographic structure is in a natural manner a product of open simplices (and hence makes up a cell). In fact, every
a\in K_{i}
for all
0\leq i\leq d-1
contributes a factor to this product as follows.
The points in the preimage
K_{d}(a)\subset K_{d}
of
a
under
K_{d}\twoheadrightarrow K_{i}
must agree in their first
i
-coordinates when mapped by
f
(the parametrisation of these
i
coordinates is the job of other simplices). The quotient
K(a)\subset K_{i+1}
of
K_{d}(a)
(which is also the preimage of
a
under
K_{i+1}\twoheadrightarrow K_{i}
) groups these points by common
(i+1)
st coordinates: these are given by an order preserving embedding
K(a)\hookrightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
. So this defines an open simplex in

{\mathbb{R}}^{K(a)}
(of dimension
|K(a)|
) that we denote by
\triangle^{K(a)}
.

So the cell thus defined is identified with
\prod_{a\in K^{\prime}}\triangle^{K(a)}
and has dimension
\sum_{j=1}^{d}|K_{j}|
. This cell lies in
\mathscr{C}\!\mathit{onf}\!_{K}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
if and only if
K=\overline{K}
. It follows that its dimension then lies between
|K|+(d-1)
and
d|K|
with the minimal value

|K|+(d-1)
taken by the connected components of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d-1}\times\mathscr{C}\!\mathit{onf}\!_{K}({\mathbb{R}})\subset\mathscr{C}\!\mathit{onf}\!_{K}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
.

2.2.
Proofs of some of the theorems

As before, let
N
stands for a finite set of size
n
.
For the proof of Theorem
1.2
, all we need to do is to endow the pair
(U^{*}{}^{N},D_{N}(U))
, for a general
U
, with a relative cell decomposition such that
\mathscr{C}\!\mathit{onf}\!_{N}(U)
becomes a union of cells of dimension
\geq n
. We do this by using the lexicographic stratification.

Proof of Theorems
1.2
and
1.5
.

We start with the product cell structure on
U^{*}{}^{N}
and refine where necessary. Since a product of cells is a product of powers of pairwise distinct cells, we only need to decompose into the cells a product
c^{K}
, where

c
is a cell in
U
and
K\subset N
. We must do this in such a manner that
\mathscr{C}\!\mathit{onf}\!_{K}(c)
becomes a union of cells, each of which is of dimension
\geq d-1+|K|
with equality when
d=1
.
But then a homeomorphism
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\xrightarrow{\cong}c
, where
d=\dim c
, which extends to a continuous map
[-\infty,\infty]^{d}\to U^{*}
, prescribes a way to decompose
c^{K}
according to the lexicographic stratification. All its cells have dimensions
\geq(d-1)+|K|
as desired.

This shows at the same time that any
n
-cell in
\mathscr{C}\!\mathit{onf}\!_{N}(U)
is given by a collection of pairwise distinct
1
-cells
c_{1},\dots,c_{r}
, a decomposition

N=N_{1}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup N_{r}
into nonempty subsets, and a total order on each of the parts (denoted
{\mathbf{N}}_{i}
). This cell is then identified with
the product of open simplices of dimensions
|N_{1}|,\dots,|N_{r}|
. The element of
{\mathcal{H}}_{N}(U)
it defines is precisely the image of

(\gamma_{c_{1}}-1)\otimes\cdots\otimes(\gamma_{c_{r}}-1)
under

\triangle_{U}^{({\mathbf{N}}_{1},\dots,{\mathbf{N}}_{r})}
. This proves that the maps (
1.3
) generate
H_{n}(U^{*}{}^{N},D_{N}(U))={\mathcal{H}}_{N}(U)
and thus establishes Theorem
1.5
.
∎

Proof of Corollary
1.3
.

The preceding shows that
{\mathcal{H}}_{N}(U)
depends only on the homotopy type of the
2
-skeleton of
U^{*}
. In order to show that it depends only on the fundamental group
\pi_{U}
it suffices to show that taking the wedge of
U^{*}
with a
2
-sphere does not affect
{\mathcal{H}}_{N}
. If we think of this

2
-sphere as the Riemann sphere, then this amounts to saying that the inclusion
U\subset U\sqcup{\mathbb{C}}
induces an isomorphism on
{\mathcal{H}}_{N}
.
In view of the monoidal property (
1.2
), it then suffices to show that
{\mathcal{H}}_{I}({\mathbb{C}})=H^{cl}_{|I|}(\mathscr{C}\!\mathit{onf}\!_{I}({\mathbb{C}}))
is zero
whenever
I
is nonempty. By Poincaré–Lefschetz duality, we have
H^{cl}_{|I|}(\mathscr{C}\!\mathit{onf}\!_{I}({\mathbb{C}}))\cong H^{|I|}(\mathscr{C}\!\mathit{onf}\!_{I}({\mathbb{C}}))
.
As is well-known, the homotopy type of
\mathscr{C}\!\mathit{onf}\!_{I}({\mathbb{C}})
is a finite cell complex of dimension
|I|-1
and hence
H^{|I|}(\mathscr{C}\!\mathit{onf}\!_{I}({\mathbb{C}}))=0
.
∎

2.3.
A ring homomorphism

We let
\triangle^{n}
be short for
\triangle^{1<2<\cdots<n}
. The
decomposition formula
says that
if
\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1}
are composable homotopy classes of arcs in some space, then

(2.1)

\textstyle\triangle^{n}(\gamma_{0}\gamma_{1})=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\triangle^{k}(\gamma_{0})\times\triangle^{n-k}(\gamma_{1}).

It merely expresses the fact that if we have
0=t_{0}\leq t_{1}\leq\cdots\leq t_{n+1}=1
, then there exists a
k\in[n]
such that
t_{k}\leq{\tfrac{1}{2}}\leq t_{k+1}
.

This suggests that in our situation we consider

\textstyle\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\bullet}(U):=\prod_{n=0}^{\infty}{\mathcal{H}}_{n}(U)t^{n}.

The Künneth product makes this a graded algebra. This algebra was considered earlier by Moriyama in case
U
is a disjoint union of copies of
{\mathbb{R}}
and so we will refer to
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\bullet}(U)
as the
Moriyama algebra
of
U
.

If we denote the
{\mathcal{I}}_{U}
-adic completion of the group ring
\Lambda_{U}={\mathbb{Z}}\pi_{U}
by

\textstyle\hat{\Lambda}_{U}:=\varprojlim_{n}\Lambda_{U}|_{n},

then the decomposition formula implies that we have a ring homomorphism

(2.2)

\textstyle\triangle_{U,t}:\hat{\Lambda}_{U}\to\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\bullet}(U),\quad\triangle_{U,t}(\gamma):=\sum_{n\geq 0}\triangle_{U}^{n}(\gamma)t^{n}.

2.4.
The case of the real line

Recall that
{\underline{{\mathcal{H}}}}({\mathbb{R}})
assigns to
N
the free abelian group generated by the set of total orders on
N
. The following lemma makes the description of

{\underline{{\mathcal{H}}}}({\mathbb{R}})
as a monoidal functor complete.

Lemma 2.1
.

The exterior product is given by the Eilenberg-Zilber formula

\textstyle\triangle^{\mathbf{I}}\times\triangle^{\mathbf{J}}=\sum_{\mathbf{K}}\operatorname{sign}(\mathbf{I}\mathbf{J},{\mathbf{K}})\hskip 2.0pt\triangle^{\mathbf{K}},

where the sum is over all shuffles of
\mathbf{I}
and
\mathbf{J}
, that is, total orders
{\mathbf{K}}
on
I\sqcup J
which extend the
total orders
\mathbf{I}
and
\mathbf{J}
, and
\operatorname{sign}(\mathbf{I}\mathbf{J},{\mathbf{K}})
is the sign of the permutation
that takes the concatenation
\mathbf{I}\mathbf{J}
to
{\mathbf{K}}
.

Proof.

This is indeed a consequence of the Eilenberg-Zilber formula.
The product of the two open simplices given by
s_{1}<\cdots<s_{k}
and
t_{1}<\cdots<t_{l}
is a signed sum of open
(k+l)
-simplices. Each such simplex comes from putting the
s_{i}
and
t_{j}
in increasing order: this defines a shuffle of
[k]
and
[l]
which takes the sign of the resulting permutation of the lexicographically ordered
(s_{1},\dots,s_{k},t_{1},\dots,t_{l})
.
∎

We can also state this in terms of the truncated group ring of
\pi_{\mathbb{R}}=\pi_{1}({\mathbb{P}}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}),\infty)
.
If
\gamma_{o}\in\pi_{\mathbb{R}}
is the positive generator, then this tells us that

\textstyle\triangle^{n}_{\mathbb{R}}(\gamma_{o}\gamma_{o})=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\triangle^{k}_{\mathbb{R}}(\gamma_{o})\times\triangle^{n-k}_{\mathbb{R}}(\gamma_{o})=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\triangle^{k}\times\triangle^{n-k}

and more generally that

\triangle^{n}_{\mathbb{R}}(\gamma_{o}^{r})
is a noncommutative polynomial in the
\{\triangle^{k}\}_{k=1}^{n}
that is homogeneous of degree
n

if we assign to
\triangle^{k}
the weight
k
.

On the other hand, if we put
c_{o}:=\gamma_{o}-1
, then
\Lambda_{\mathbb{R}}|_{n}={\mathbb{Z}}[c_{o}]/(c_{o})^{n+1}
and
{\mathcal{I}}_{\mathbb{R}}|_{n}=(c_{o})/(c_{o})^{n+1}
.
The above formula shows that the
\{\gamma_{o}^{k}-1\}_{k=1}^{n}
maps to linearly independent elements
of
{\mathcal{H}}_{n}({\mathbb{R}})
. We thus find:

Corollary 2.2
.

The map
\triangle^{n}_{\mathbb{R}}:{\mathcal{I}}_{\mathbb{R}}|_{n}\to{\mathcal{H}}_{n}({\mathbb{R}})
is injective.

If
U
is a disjoint union of a finite number copies of
{\mathbb{R}}
, i.e., of the form
U_{E}:=E\times{\mathbb{R}}
, where
E
is a finite set, then
U_{E}^{*}
is a wedge of copies of
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}({\mathbb{R}})
with base point
\infty
. If we put a total order on
E

so that it gets identified with
[r]
for some
r
, then the cells in
\mathscr{C}\!\mathit{onf}\!_{N}(U_{E})
are naturally oriented and we get an identification of

{\underline{{\mathcal{H}}}}(U_{E})
with the
r
-fold tensor product of

{\underline{{\mathcal{H}}}}({\mathbb{R}})
.

2.5.
Degeneracy and truncation

Given distinct
i,j\in N
, denote by
N\twoheadrightarrow N_{ij}
the quotient of
N
obtained by identifying these two elements.
So
N_{ij}
has size
n-1
. We define a
degeneracy map

\partial_{ij}=\partial^{U}_{ij}:{\mathcal{H}}_{N}(U)\to{\mathcal{H}}_{N_{ij}}(U)

(which will be symmetric in
i
and
j
) as follows.
The surjection
N\to N_{ij}
induces an embedding
s_{ij}:U^{*}{}^{N_{ij}}\to U^{*}{}^{N}
which parametrizes a hyperdiagonal and thus lands in

D_{N}(U)
. Let
D^{ij}_{N}(U)
denote the closure of the complement of the image of
s_{ij}
in
D_{N}(U)
.
Then
D_{N}(U)
is of course the union of the image of
s_{ij}
and
D^{ij}_{N}(U)
. We find that
s_{ij}^{-1}D^{ij}_{N}(U)=D_{N_{ij}}(U)
so that
s_{ij}
defines a relative homeomorphism of the pair
(U^{*}{}^{N_{ij}},D_{N_{ij}}(U))
onto the pair
(D_{N}(U),D^{ij}_{N}(U))
. We then let

\partial_{ij}:{\mathcal{H}}_{N}(U)\to{\mathcal{H}}_{N_{ij}}(U)
be the connecting homomorphism of the long exact homology sequence for the triple

(U^{*}{}^{N},D_{N}(U),D^{ij}_{N}(U))
:

(2.3)

\cdots\to H_{n}(U^{*}{}^{N},D_{N}(U)))\xrightarrow{\partial_{ij}}H_{n-1}(D_{N}(U),D^{ij}_{N}(U)))\to\\
\to H_{n-1}(U^{*}{}^{N},D^{ij}_{N}(U))\to\cdots

This map is functorial in
U
and so we have for every
\gamma\in\pi_{U}
a commutative diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{H}}_{N}({\mathbb{R}})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{H}}_{N}(U)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{H}}_{N_{ij}}({\mathbb{R}})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{H}}_{N_{ij}}(U).}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{H}}_{N}(\gamma)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\partial^{\mathbb{R}}_{ij}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\partial^{U}_{ij}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{H}}_{N_{ij}}(\gamma)}

If
N
is equipped with a total order
{\mathbf{N}}
, then (by definition) the top arrow sends the basis element
\triangle^{\mathbf{N}}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{N}({\mathbb{R}})
to
\triangle^{\mathbf{N}}_{U}(\gamma)
.
Similarly, the evaluation of
\partial_{ij}
on
\triangle_{U}^{{\mathbf{N}}_{1}}(\gamma_{1})\times\cdots\times\triangle_{U}^{{\mathbf{N}}_{r}}(\gamma_{r})\in{\mathcal{H}}_{N}(U)
is determined by the evaluation of
\partial_{ij}
on
\triangle^{{\mathbf{N}}_{1}}\times\cdots\times\triangle^{{\mathbf{N}}_{r}}
, where
\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{r}\in\pi_{U}
, and
{\mathbf{N}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbf{N}}_{r}
is a partition of
N
with a total order on each part. It is clear that if the pair
(i,j)
appears consecutively in the same part of the partition, (i.e., if one is a successor of the other for this total order) we have an induced partition
{\mathbf{N}}_{1,ij},\ldots,{\mathbf{N}}_{r,ij}
on the set
N_{ij}
with a total order on each part.

Lemma 2.3
.

If the pair
\{i,j\}
appears consecutively in the same part of the partition
{\mathbf{N}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbf{N}}_{r}
, and
pr(\{i,j\})
is the number of predecessors of that pair in the concatenated total order
{\mathbf{N}}_{1}{\mathbf{N}}_{2}\cdots{\mathbf{N}}_{r}
, then put

\varepsilon_{ij}({\mathbf{N}}_{1},{\mathbf{N}}_{2},\ldots,{\mathbf{N}}_{r}):=(-1)^{{pr(\{i,j\})}}
; otherwise put
\varepsilon_{ij}({\mathbf{N}}_{1},{\mathbf{N}}_{2},\ldots,{\mathbf{N}}_{r}):=0
. Then

\partial_{ij}(\triangle^{{\mathbf{N}}_{1}}\times\cdots\times\triangle^{{\mathbf{N}}_{r}})=\varepsilon_{ij}({\mathbf{N}}_{1},{\mathbf{N}}_{2},\ldots,{\mathbf{N}}_{r})\triangle^{{\mathbf{N}}_{1,ij}}\times\cdots\times\triangle^{{\mathbf{N}}_{r,ij}}

(so read zero if the pair
\{i,j\}
appears nonconsecutively or in different parts of the partition).

Proof.

In the case
r=1
, assume that
{\mathbf{N}}_{1}
is, without loss of generality, the order
1<2<\cdots<n
. Then the interior of
\triangle^{{\mathbf{N}}}
is the subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}

given by
t_{1}<t_{2}<\cdots<t_{n}
and the locus
t_{i}=t_{j}
appears as a codimension one face only if
i,j
is a consecutive pair; in that case it is the
(pr(\{i,j\})+1)
-th face and acquires the claimed sign. For
r>1
, we only get a contribution from the boundary of a simplex containing
i
and
j
consecutively; in that case, the Leibniz rule gives us the correct sign.
∎

Corollary 2.4
.

The degeneracy map
\partial_{ij}:{\mathcal{H}}_{N}(U)\to{\mathcal{H}}_{N_{ij}}(U)
is onto.

Proof.

We first observe that every numbered partition of
N_{ij}
endowed with a total order on each part, determines such a structure for
N
: a part of
N
is a preimage of a part on
N_{ij}
and such a part inherits a total order from its image in
N_{ij}
, where for the part containing the pair
\{i,j\}
, we stipulate that
j
is the successor of
i
. Then
each generator of
{\mathcal{H}}_{N_{ij}}(U)
provided by Theorem
1.5
, is the image of a generator it provides for
{\mathcal{H}}_{N}(U)
.
∎

By taking
N=[n]
and
\{i,j\}=\{n-1,n\}
, we get a map
\partial_{n-1,n}:{\mathcal{H}}_{n}(U)\to{\mathcal{H}}_{n-1}(U)
.
We call this a
truncation map
, because of the following lemma.

Lemma 2.5
.

The diagram of truncations

(2.4)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{I}}_{U}|_{n}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{H}}_{n}(U)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{I}}_{U}|_{n-1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{H}}_{n-1}(U)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\partial_{n-1,n}}

commutes up to the sign
(-1)^{{n}}
.

Proof.

The horizontal maps are induced by
\gamma\in\pi_{U}\mapsto\triangle^{n}(\gamma)
resp.
\triangle^{n-1}(\gamma)
.
Since the pair
(n-1,n)
has
n-2
predecessors, Lemma
2.3
implies that
\partial_{n-1,n}(\triangle^{n})=(-1)^{n}\triangle^{n-1}
and so the left vertical map is given by restriction.
In terms of the group rings, this restriction amounts to a truncation.
∎

If
k\leq n
, we may iteratively apply the degeneracy maps
\partial_{n-1,n},\partial_{n-2,n-1},\ldots,

\partial_{k,k+1}
to get a map surjective map
\partial^{k}_{n}:{\mathcal{H}}_{n}(U)\to{\mathcal{H}}_{k}(U)
.

Lemma 2.6
.

The iterated degeneracy
{\mathcal{H}}_{n}(U)\to{\mathcal{H}}_{k}(U)

annihilates the subspace
F^{k+1}{\mathcal{H}}_{n}(U)
.

Proof.

From Lemma
2.3
, the only way this iterated degeneracy might not vanish on an element

\triangle^{{\mathbf{N}}_{1}}(\gamma_{1})\times\cdots\times\triangle^{{\mathbf{N}}_{r}}(\gamma_{r})
is if all elements
k,k+1,\ldots,n
appear in the same part of the partition
N_{1},\ldots,N_{r}
. On the other hand, the space
F^{k+1}{\mathcal{H}}_{n}(U)
is spanned by elements
\triangle^{{\mathbf{N}}_{1}}(\gamma_{1})\times\cdots\times\triangle^{{\mathbf{N}}_{r}}(\gamma_{r})
with
r\geq k+1
and with each
N_{1},\ldots,N_{r}
non-empty. This forces the size of each part
N_{i}
to be
\leq n-r+1\leq n-k
so that, in particular, it cannot contain the
n-k+1
elements
k,k+1,\ldots,n
. The conclusion follows.
∎

Remark 2.7
.

If
U
is a Riemann surface of finite type, then the degeneracy map can be understood as a residue map (we are taking the residue of a logarithmic
n
-form on
\mathscr{C}\!\mathit{onf}\!_{N}(U)
along the hyperdiagonal
z_{i}-z_{j}
). This is a good mnemonic for the sign rules
when we consider iterated degeneracies. Suppose
\{i,j\}
and
\{k,l\}
are
distinct
unordered pairs in
N
. Then the associated quotients
N\to N_{ij}
and
N\to N_{kl}
fit in pushout diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces N}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces N_{ij}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces N_{kl}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces N_{ij,kl}}

If the two pairs are disjoint, then
\partial_{ij}\partial_{kl}=-\partial_{kl}\partial_{ij}
. Otherwise, we can assume that the second pair is
\{j,k\}
with

pairwise distinct.
In that case
\partial_{i[jk]}\partial_{jk}+\partial_{j[ki]}\partial_{ki}+\partial_{k[ij]}\partial_{ij}=0
, where

stands for the image of
\{i,j\}
in

. These operations appear implicitly in the work of Cohen-Taylor
[

]
.

2.6.
The cellular chain complex

In the proof of Theorem
1.2
we put a total ordering on each cell of
U
. This orients that cell. We want to orient the cells of
\mathscr{C}\!\mathit{onf}\!_{N}(U)
as well. If
c
is a cell in
U
, then
given
f\in\mathscr{C}\!\mathit{onf}\!_{K}(c)
, the map
f:K\to c
is injective. The iterated lexicographical total order on
c
(with as many levels as the dimension of
c
) determines then also such a structure on
K
. The cell which contains
f
is the connected component of the set of all
f^{\prime}\in\mathscr{C}\!\mathit{onf}\!_{K}(c)
that give rise to the same such structure on
K
. Note that this structure also determines an orientation of that cell. A cell of
\mathscr{C}\!\mathit{onf}\!_{N}(U)

is a product of cells of this type where the product is over a collection of pairwise distinct cells of
U
. Hence an orientation of this
cell is obtained by imposing a total order on the collection of the cells of
U
. We make this assumption from now on so that each cell determines a generator of the cellular chain complex

{\mathscr{C}}_{N}(U)_{\scriptscriptstyle\bullet}:={\mathscr{C}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\bullet}(U^{*}{}^{N},D_{N}(U)).

This complex is zero in degree
<n
and its homology is that of the homology with closed support of
\mathscr{C}\!\mathit{onf}\!_{N}(U)
.
The assignment
N\mapsto{\mathscr{C}}_{N}(U)_{\scriptscriptstyle\bullet}
defines a monoidal functor on
\underline{\mathcal{F\!B}}
that takes values in the category of bounded below chain complexes.

2.7.
Attaching a

-cell

In view of the central role of
{\underline{{\mathcal{H}}}}({\mathbb{R}})
in what is going to follow, we often suppress its argument and write

).

of

such that

. In other words,

.

It will be convenient to assume that the

It is clear that the inclusion

\operatorname{Mod}(U)

.

The shifted upper half plane defined by

In general the image of

Proof.

For

3.2.
Configuration spaces of a closed surface

We derive from the preceding a similar statement about the top cohomology of the configuration spaces of the closed surface
X
.
Assume
P=\{*\}
, so that
U=X\smallsetminus\{*\}
and
\pi_{U}=\pi_{1}(X,*)
. The Birman exact sequence can be understood as saying that
\pi_{U}
appears as a point pushing normal subgroup of
\operatorname{Mod}(U)=\operatorname{Mod}(X,\{*\})
with quotient
\operatorname{Mod}(X)
. The action of
\operatorname{Mod}(U)
on
\pi_{U}
is faithful and makes
\pi_{U}
act by inner automorphisms so that we have residual (faithful) outer action of
\operatorname{Mod}(X)
on
\pi_{U}
.
The Johnson filtration of
\operatorname{Mod}(X)
is defined as the image of the Johnson filtration of
\operatorname{Mod}(U)
. So a mapping class of
X
belongs to
J^{k}\operatorname{Mod}(X)
if and only if it can be lifted to an element of
J_{k}\operatorname{Mod}(U)
or equivalently, if the associated outer automorphism of
\pi_{U}|_{k}
is trivial.

Corollary 3.3
.

Assume
N\not=\emptyset
. Then the space
\mathscr{C}\!\mathit{onf}\!_{N}(X))
has the homotopy type of a finite complex of dimension
n+1
.

The group
J^{k}\operatorname{Mod}(X)
acts trivially on
H^{k}(\mathscr{C}\!\mathit{onf}\!_{N}(X)
for all
k
, but if
k=n+1
, then even

J^{n-1}\operatorname{Mod}(X)
acts trivially.

Proof.

In order to prove the first assertion, we choose
o\in N
. Then the
map
\pi:\mathscr{C}\!\mathit{onf}\!_{N}(X)\to X
,
f\mapsto f(o)
is a fiber bundle whose fiber over
x\in X
is
\mathscr{C}\!\mathit{onf}\!_{N\smallsetminus\{o\}}(X\smallsetminus\{x\})
.
The latter has the homotopy type of a finite complex of dimension
n-1
. The base is a surface and hence
\mathscr{C}\!\mathit{onf}\!_{N}(X)
has the homotopy type of a finite complex of dimension
n+1
.

To prove the second assertion, recall that we endowed
X^{N}=U^{*N}
with a cell decomposition for which
\mathscr{C}\!\mathit{onf}\!_{N}(U)
is an open union of cells of dimension
\leq n
with the union of
n
-cells in
\mathscr{C}\!\mathit{onf}\!_{N}(U)
being
\mathscr{C}\!\mathit{onf}\!_{N}(U_{1})
. This cell decomposition has the property that
\mathscr{C}\!\mathit{onf}\!_{N}(X)
is also an open union of cells: the cells
we need to add have exactly one factor equal to
\{*\}
. Thus
\mathscr{C}\!\mathit{onf}\!_{N}(X)\smallsetminus\mathscr{C}\!\mathit{onf}\!_{N}(U)
is the disjoint union of the configuration spaces
\mathscr{C}\!\mathit{onf}\!_{N\smallsetminus\{i\}}(U)
,

. Then
{\mathscr{C}}_{N}(X)_{\scriptscriptstyle\bullet}
is the mapping cone of the boundary map

\partial^{\prime}:{\mathscr{C}}_{N}(U)[-1]_{\scriptscriptstyle\bullet}\to\oplus_{i\in N}{\mathscr{C}}_{N\smallsetminus\{i\}}(U)_{\scriptscriptstyle\bullet}.

This mapping cone regards the above as a double complex: we put the source in row
1
(and multiply its boundary operator by
-1
) and put the target in row
0
(this makes the target a subcomplex). The homology in degree
l
of the associated single complex is then

.
An argument similar to the proof of Corollary

shows that

acts trivially on the

th summand of this complex and we then conclude as before. The case

\oplus_{i\in N}{\mathscr{C}}_{N\smallsetminus\{i\}}(U)_{n-1}
.
∎

3.3.

That moment has certainly arrived. Returning to the punctured surface

\textstyle\mu:=\sum_{i=1}^{g}(a_{i}\otimes a_{-i}-a_{-i}\otimes a_{i})\in H_{1}(X)^{\otimes 2}\subset H_{1}(X,P)^{\otimes 2}=S_{1}(U_{1}^{*}).

We have

Lemma 3.7

We have that

\textstyle\triangle_{U_{1}}^{3}(\zeta)=\sum_{i=\pm 1}^{\pm g}\operatorname{sign}(i)[\triangle^{2}(\alpha_{i}),a_{-i}]-\sum_{i=1}^{g}[a_{i},a_{-i}](a_{i}+a_{-i})

Here, the term

{\mathcal{H}}_{1}(U_{1})\cong S_{1}(U_{1})\xrightarrow{\mu_{1,3}}S_{1}(U_{1})^{\otimes 3}\xrightarrow{\cong}F^{3}{\mathcal{H}}_{3}(U_{1}).

For the first assertion, we apply twice the composition formula modulo

\hat{\Lambda}^{\mathit{cfg}}_{U}=\varprojlim_{n}\Lambda^{\mathit{cfg}}_{U,n}
and embeds in
S^{\mathit{cfg}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\bullet}\langle\!\langle U\rangle\!\rangle
.

3.6.
Many punctures in terms of one

We conclude this section with a proof of Theorem
1.4
. The key observation is that the word
\zeta
does not involve the
\beta_{j}
-loops. Then the differential
\partial
of the complex
\underline{{\mathscr{C}}}(U)_{\scriptscriptstyle\bullet}
, which interacts with the subcomplex
\underline{{\mathscr{C}}}(U_{1})_{\scriptscriptstyle\bullet}
only via the map
\times_{\zeta}
, also does not involve the
\beta_{j}
-cells, so that the
\beta_{j}
-part splits off in the form of the decomposition of
\underline{\mathcal{F\!B}}
-chain complexes

(3.12)

(\underline{{\mathscr{C}}}(U)_{\scriptscriptstyle\bullet},\partial)\cong(\underline{{\mathscr{C}}}(X\smallsetminus\{p_{0}\})_{\scriptscriptstyle\bullet},\partial)\otimes({\underline{{\mathcal{H}}}}^{\otimes l},0).

Here the second factor embeds in
(\underline{{\mathscr{C}}}(U)_{\scriptscriptstyle\bullet},\partial)
using the maps

\beta_{j}:({\mathbb{P}}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}),\infty)\to(U_{1}^{*},*)

for
j=1,\ldots,l
.
Taking homology, tells us that the
\underline{\mathcal{F\!B}}
-functor
N\mapsto H^{cl}_{\scriptscriptstyle\bullet}(\mathscr{C}\!\mathit{onf}\!_{N}(U))
is the
\underline{\mathcal{F\!B}}
-tensor product of the functors
N\mapsto H^{cl}_{\scriptscriptstyle\bullet}(\mathscr{C}\!\mathit{onf}\!_{N}(X\smallsetminus\{p_{0}\}))
and
{\underline{{\mathcal{H}}}}^{\otimes l}
. Theorem
1.4
is then a spelling out of this
\underline{\mathcal{F\!B}}
-product and an application of Poincaré–Lefschetz duality.

This isomorphism is natural to some extent. In order to exhibit this, we choose a closed disk
D\subset X
which contains the union of the paths
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{l}
such that this union meets

\partial D
in
\{p_{0}\}
only. Then viewing the subcomplex
(\underline{{\mathscr{C}}}(X\smallsetminus\{p_{0}\})_{\scriptscriptstyle\bullet},\partial)
as
(\underline{{\mathscr{C}}}(X\smallsetminus D)_{\scriptscriptstyle\bullet},\partial)
we get an action by
\operatorname{Mod}(X\smallsetminus D)
on the former complex. Extending by the trivial action on the second factor in the decomposition (
3.12
) and acting naturally on the left, makes the isomorphism on homologies
\operatorname{Mod}(X\smallsetminus D)
-equivariant.

4.
Comparison with the Johnson filtration

We show that in general the Johnson filtration strictly refines the modified version defined above. For a class of examples we take

P
to be a singleton
\{*\}
(so that
U^{*}=X
) and assume that the genus
g
of
X
is
\geq 2
.

We simplify notation by putting
S_{n}:=S_{n}(X)
,
\pi:=\pi_{1}(X,*)
and denote the associated graded Lie algebra by
L_{\scriptscriptstyle\bullet}:=\oplus_{n\geq 1}\pi^{(n)}/\pi^{(n+1)}
. We recall that this Lie algebra is generated by
H_{1}(X)
and has
\sum_{i=1}^{g}[a_{i},a_{-i}]=0
as a defining relation. Its universal enveloping algebra
S_{\scriptscriptstyle\bullet}
is the tensor algebra on
H_{1}(X)
modulo the 2-sided ideal generated by
\mu=\sum_{i=1}^{g}(a_{i}\otimes a_{-i}-a_{-i}\otimes a_{i})
.
We write
{\mathcal{I}}\subset{\mathbb{Z}}\pi
for the augmentation ideal.

The group
\operatorname{Mod}(X,*)
acts faithfully on
\pi
and its image contains the group of inner of automorphisms of
\pi
.
Since
g\geq 2
,
\pi
has trivial center and so this defines an embedding
\iota:\pi\to\operatorname{Mod}(X,*)
. It is realized geometrically by ‘point pushing’ the base point
*
along a loop.

Since
J^{n}\operatorname{Mod}(X,*)
is the kernel of the action of
\operatorname{Mod}(X,*)
on
\pi_{n}=\pi/\pi^{(n+1)}
, it follows that if
\alpha\in\pi
, then

\iota(\alpha)\in J^{n}\operatorname{Mod}(X,*)
if and only
(\alpha,\beta)\in\pi^{(n+1)}
for all
\beta\in\pi
. The latter is implied by (but is in fact equivalent with)

\alpha\in\pi^{(n)}
. In particular, we get a homomorphism of
{\mathbb{Z}}
-modules

\operatorname{gr}^{n}(\iota):L_{n}\to J^{n}\operatorname{Mod}(X,*)/J^{n+1}\operatorname{Mod}(X,*)

If
\varphi\in\operatorname{Mod}(X,*)
, then
\varphi\in J^{n}\operatorname{Mod}(X,*)
if and only if
\varphi_{*}-1
maps
{\mathbb{Z}}\pi
to
{\mathcal{I}}^{n}
. It follows that the assignment
\varphi\mapsto\varphi_{*}-1
defines an embedding of (abelian) groups

J^{n}\operatorname{Mod}(X,*)/J^{n+1}\operatorname{Mod}(X,*)\hookrightarrow\operatorname{Hom}(S_{1},S_{1+n}).

This is the
n
th Johnson homomorphism. If we precompose this map with
\operatorname{gr}^{n}(\iota)
, we obtain

L_{n}\to\operatorname{Hom}(S_{1},S_{1+n}),\quad\alpha\mapsto(b\mapsto\alpha b\alpha^{-1}-b)

which is induced by
\alpha\mapsto\alpha-1
. Equivalently, the map is given by

\operatorname{ad}^{1}_{1+n}:L_{n}\to\operatorname{Hom}(S_{1},S_{1+n}),\quad\operatorname{ad}^{1}_{1+n}(a)(x)=[a,x]

So if we succeed in finding an
a\in L_{n}
with the property that
\operatorname{ad}^{1}_{1+n}(a)
is nonzero, but takes its values in the kernel of

S_{1+n}\to S^{\mathit{cfg}}_{1+n}
, then we have obtained a nonzero element of
J_{\mathit{cfg}}^{n-1}\operatorname{Mod}(X,*)\smallsetminus J^{n-1}\operatorname{Mod}(X,*)
.
The map
S_{\scriptscriptstyle\bullet}\to S^{\mathit{cfg}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\bullet}
is an algebra homomorphism and hence the last condition is certainly fulfilled if we take

a
itself in the kernel of
L_{n}\to S^{\mathit{cfg}}_{n}
. For example, if
n\geq 2
, then we may take

\textstyle a=\sum_{i=1}^{g}\Big([a_{i}[c_{1},[c_{2},[\cdots,[c_{n-2},a_{-i}]\cdots]\!]\!]-[a_{-i},[c_{1},[c_{2},[\cdots,[c_{n-2},a_{i}]\cdots\!]\!]\!]\Big),

where
c_{1},\dots,c_{n-2}
are in
S_{1}
. It then remains to find such
c_{\nu}
such that
\operatorname{ad}^{1}_{1+n}(a)\not=0
.

In the simplest possible nontrivial case,
namely when
n=3
, a straighforward computation shows that then
a=0
. We therefore take
n=4
. Let us write out one of its terms:

[a_{i}[c_{1},[c_{2},a_{-i}]]]=[a_{i},c_{1}(c_{2}a_{-i}-a_{-i}c_{2})-(c_{2}a_{-i}-a_{-i}c_{2})c_{1}]=\\
=a_{i}\big(c_{1}(c_{2}a_{-i}-a_{-i}c_{2})-(c_{2}a_{-i}-a_{-i}c_{2})c_{1}\big)-\big(c_{1}(c_{2}a_{-i}-a_{-i}c_{2})-(c_{2}a_{-i}-a_{-i}c_{2})c_{1}\big)a_{i}

It is convenient to collect the terms of the right hand side according to the two positions in which the basis elements
a_{j}
appear:

-a_{i}c_{1}a_{-i}c_{2}-a_{i}c_{2}a_{-i}c_{1}

(1,4)

In order to get
a
, we must sum over
i=1,\dots,g
and subtract from this the same expression with

and

exchanged. Since
\sum_{i}a_{i}a_{-i}-a_{-i}a_{i}=0
, we only get contributions from
