A miR-200b–Filamin A axis drives epicardial contribution to cardiogenesis
Authors: Cristina Sánchez-Fernández, Carlos García-Padilla, Estefanía Lozano-Velasco,, Francisco Hernandez-Torres, Óscar Ocaña, Ana Quintas, Enrique Vázquez, Laura Alonso-Herranz, Mercedes Ricote, Beatriz Román-Payan, Rita Carmona, Diego Franco, Jorge N Domínguez, Amelia E Aránega
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe epicardium provides essential cellular and molecular cues required for proper cardiogenesis and cardiac repair. Epicardial-derived cells (EPDCs) play a pivotal role in establishing cardiac structure, contributing to coronary vasculature formation, connective tissue organization, and post-ischemic cardiac remodeling. During EPDC emergence, the epicardium must preserve a precise balance between cellular motility and epithelial integrity. However, the mechanisms determining why some epicardial cells undergo epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition to become EPDCs while others retain an epithelial state remain unclear. We show that miR-200b is expressed in a subset of epicardial cells during embryonic EPDC formation. Gain-and loss-of-function experiments reveal that miR-200b regulates the overall number of EPDCs by modulating the proportion of symmetric and asymmetric cell divisions. RNA pull-down coupled with RNA-seq, together with in vitro and ex vivo functional assays, identified filamin A (FLNA)—a key regulator of spindle positioning during asymmetric division—as a direct miR-200b target in epicardial cells. FLNA loss reduced asymmetric divisions, supporting its role in promoting this division mode. Overall, our study defines a miR-200b–FLNA axis that governs symmetric versus asymmetric division to control epicardial tissue dynamics during cardiogenesis. Introduction

The outermost cell layer of the vertebrate heart, the epicardium, comprises a population of multipotent progenitor cells that serve as a source of both cells and molecular signals essential for proper cardiac morphogenesis. During heart development, a subset of epicardial cells undergoes an epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) between embryonic days E12.5 and E15.5 in mice, migrating into the myocardium as epicardial-derived cells (EPDCs)1,2. Within the myocardium, EPDCs give rise to multiple cardiac cell types, including coronary vascular smooth muscle cells, cardiac fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and possibly a subpopulation of cardiomyocytes, thereby contributing to complete heart formation3–6. After birth, the epicardium remains largely quiescent; however, EPDC formation can be reactivated following cardiac injury, contributing to the remodeling of the damaged myocardium7–10. Despite the central role of EPDC generation in both development and disease, the mechanisms determining why only a subset of epicardial cells undergo EMT while others retain their epithelial identity remain poorly understood. Previous studies have shown that cell division in the epicardium occurs in two distinct orientations, with perpendicular divisions aligning with the pattern of EPDC formation during development11. However, the molecular mechanisms governing this process remain unknown11,12.

Over the past decades, extensive analyses of the EMT process have revealed additional layers of gene regulation, including post-transcriptional control by microRNAs (miRNAs). Among these, the miR-200 family has emerged as a key regulator of EMT, acting by repressing transcription factors such as SNAIL, TWIST, and ZEB, which orchestrate the epithelial-to-mesenchymal switch. This miRNA family also plays a crucial role in maintaining epithelial identity and preventing aberrant mesenchymal transition13–17. Recent studies have further shown that the miR-200 family modulates gene dosage of multiple transcription factors during early murine heart development; however, its specific function in the epicardium remains unclear18.

In this study, we investigate the molecular mechanisms by which miR-200b regulates epicardial cell behavior during cardiogenesis. We identify a subpopulation of miR-200b–expressing cells in the epicardium, restricted to the developmental window of EMT. Through RNA pull-down, RNA sequencing, and gain- and loss-of-function assays, we demonstrate that miR-200b targets Filamin A (FLNA) in epicardial cells, and that FLNA levels influence the frequency of asymmetric cell divisions, from the epicardial epithelial sheet as inferred from division orientation and position. Furthermore, we show that miR-200b expression is altered in murine infarcted hearts and in blood samples from patients.

Material and methods

Cell lines, animals and human samples

The MEC1 mouse embryonic epicardial cell line (SCC187, Merck Millipore) was stored and cultured according to the manufacturer’s instructions. This immortalized cell line does not express myocardial or endothelial markers and retains the morphology of early primary epicardial cells. The NIH/3T3 (CRL-1658, ATCC) cell was cultured following the manufacturer´s recommendations.

The mWt1/IRES/GFP-Cre mouse line (hereafter WT1Cre) has been used in previous studies to trace Wt1 lineages19,20. For lineage tracing experiments, WT1Cre mice were crossed with homozygous Rosa26-EYFP [B6.129 × 1-Gt(ROSA)26Sortm1(EYFP)Cos/J] mice21, allowing permanent reporter expression in WT1-expressing cell lineages. WT1Cre-YFP mice were kindly provided by the laboratory of Ramón Muñoz-Chapuli (University of Málaga, UMA). Procedures involving CD1 (Janvier Labs) mouse embryos were approved by the Ethics Committee on Animal Experimentation of the University of Jaén (UJA) and performed in compliance with national and European guidelines for animal care and handling in the facilities of the UJA and the University of Granada (UGR). Additionally, 2 to 3-month-old male and female C57BL/6 mice (Charles River) were included in the study to undergo myocardial infarction (MI) procedures. These animals were conducted in accordance with European Union (EU) Directive 2010/63/EC, enforced in Spanish law under Real Decreto 53/2013.

Mice were maintained under standard conditions (12 h light/dark cycle, 22 ± 0.1 °C, 50–55% humidity) with ad libitum access to food (AIN-93G diet) and water. WT1Cre-YFP, CD1, and C57BL/6 J mice were bred, and embryos were collected at embryonic days (E11.5 to E16.5).

Human blood samples were obtained with informed consent from the participants or their legal guardians. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (1975) and approved by the Ethics Committees of the University of Jaén and Hospital Universitario Virgen de las Nieves in Granada.

Mouse and human sample collection and processing

Pregnant female mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation. Embryonic hearts were then carefully isolated and dissected using fine forceps and a micro-scalpel. Collected embryonic tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), washed with 1 × phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), cryoprotected through a series of increasing PBS-sucrose concentrations, and subsequently frozen in liquid nitrogen-cooled isopentane (PanReac AppliChem, 143501.1611) for sectioning. For histological analysis, tissues were sectioned at 10 µm thickness using a cryostat microtome (CM1510S, Leica) and mounted on Epredia™ SuperFrost Ultra Plus Adhesion Slides (10149870, Fisher Scientific).

Human blood samples from patients with myocardial infarction were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C to obtain plasma.

RNA isolation (mRNA and miRNA), cDNA synthesis and RT-qPCR

Total RNA from cardiac explants and transfected cells was extracted using the ReliaPrep™ RNA Cell Miniprep System (Promega, Z6011) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration was measured using 1.5 µL of each sample on a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For miRNA isolation from human plasma, the miRNeasy Serum/Plasma Advanced Kit (Qiagen, 217204) was used following the manufacturer’s protocol.

For miRNA reverse transcription, 10 ng of total RNA was used as a template with the miRCURY LNA Universal RT Kit (Qiagen, 203301). For mRNA analysis, 500 ng of total RNA was reverse transcribed using the Maxima First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, K1642) on a Bio-Rad C1000 Touch™ Thermal Cycler, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. At least three independent pooled samples were used for each RT-qPCR experiment.

miR-200b expression was quantified by real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) using diluted cDNA and the GoTaq qPCR Master Mix (Promega, A6001) on a CFX Connect Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-rad C1000 Touch™ Thermal Cycler) in line with the manufacturer’s instructions. In all cases, at least three distinct pooled samples were used to perform the corresponding RT-qPCR experiments.

The expression levels of miR-200b were quantified by real-time semi-quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) using diluted cDNA obtained from the reverse transcription reaction, following the GoTaq qPCR Master Mix protocol (Promega; A6001). All RT-qPCR reactions were performed on a CFX Connect Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad Laboratories) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The cycling program consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s (denaturation) and 60 °C for 1 min (annealing and extension). Melt curves were generated with an initial step at 95 °C for 5 s, followed by 0.5 °C increments every 7 s from 65 to 95 °C. miR-200b expression was normalized to U6 snRNA and 5S rRNA, and relative expression levels were calculated as the ratio of miR-200b to the reference RNAs. Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH)

FISH for miR-200b was performed on cryosections of hearts from WT1Cre-YFP mouse embryos (E11.5–E16.5). Sections were first washed for 10 min with 0.03% H₂O₂ in PBS to block endogenous peroxidase activity, followed by permeabilization in 2% acetone/PBS for 5 min. After a single wash with 30% formamide/2 × saline-sodium citrate (SSC) buffer (300 mM NaCl, 30 mM Na₃Citrate·2H₂O, pH 5) for 10 min, pre-hybridization was performed for 30 min at 48 °C using a hybridization mix containing 50% formamide, 1.3 × SSC, 0.5 mg/mL yeast tRNA, 0.2 × CHAPS, 0.5 M EDTA (pH 8), and 0.2% Tween-20.

Hybridization was carried out with 25 nM hsa-miR-200b-3p double DIG-labeled LNA probe (Qiagen; 33,911 YD00619853-BCG) diluted in hybridization mix at 48 °C for 2 h 30 min. Sections were then washed sequentially in 2 × SSC and 1 × SSC at 48 °C, with a final wash at room temperature (RT). Sections were rinsed in 1 × PBS prior to signal amplification using either the Alexa Fluor Tyramide SuperBoost Kit (Invitrogen; B40943 or B40912) or TSA Plus Cyanine 5 (Akoya Biosciences; NEL745001KT).

Samples were blocked for 1 h with Blocking Buffer (Invitrogen) and rinsed three times with 1 × PBS. The appropriate anti-DIG antibody (Invitrogen; rabbit 9H27L19 or mouse ab420) was applied at 5 µg/mL in Blocking Buffer and incubated overnight at 4 °C. The next day, sections were rinsed three times with 1 × PBS and incubated with the corresponding poly-HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (Invitrogen; rabbit B40943 or mouse B40912) at RT for 60 min. Following three washes in 1 × PBS, Tyramide Working Solution was added for 8 min at RT according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The reaction was stopped with Stop Reagent for 30 s, and sections were rinsed in 1 × PBS prior to subsequent immunostaining. Images were acquired using a Leica TCS SPE-CTR6500 LAS AF confocal microscope, and analysis was performed with ImageJ (version 1.54 of ImageJ (National Institutes of Health); https://imagej.nih.gov/ij/). Images were obtained over the full extent of the epicardium in each sample.

After removing cardiac atria, ventricles were placed inverted on the culture plate, previously treated with collagen (Corning™ 354236; #11563550), cultured in DMEM (Gibco™ DMEM high 10 glucose, no glutamine; #11960044) supplemented with 10% Fetal bovine serum (onward FBS; Sigma-Aldrich, F7520) and 2% penicillin/streptomycin (Sigma-Aldrich; P0781) and incubated at 37 °C, 5% CO2. Affinity pull-down of RNA assay

To identify miRNA targets and provide experimental evidence of direct interaction between miR-200b and its mRNA targets, an affinity pulldown of biotinylated RNA approach was employed. Biotinylated miR-200b mimics (Premium miRNA Mimic; YM00472051-BDI) and scramble control miRNA (Premium miRNA Mimic; YM00479902-BDI) were synthesized and obtained from IDT Technologies, and delivered to epicardial cells via transfection.

Transfected epicardial cells (2 × 105 per dish) were harvested 48 h post-transfection and lysed using the miRNeasy Tissue/Cells Advanced kit lysis buffer (Qiagen; 217604). Complexes of biotinylated RISC and associated mRNAs were captured using streptavidin-coated magnetic beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Dynabeads M-280, 11206D), with background reduction achieved via Argonaute immunoprecipitation. Following wash steps, the pulled-down RNA was purified and subjected to next-generation sequencing (RNA-seq) for target identification.

RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq)

A total of 2.5 ng of RNA was used to generate cDNA using the SMART-Seq v4 Ultra Low Input RNA Kit (Clontech-Takara). From this, 1 ng of amplified cDNA was used to prepare barcoded sequencing libraries with the Nextera XT DNA Library Preparation Kit (Illumina), in which cDNA fragmentation and adapter ligation occur in a single reaction, followed by amplification and cleanup. Library size was assessed using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer High Sensitivity DNA chip, and concentrations were determined with a Qubit® fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Libraries were sequenced on a HiSeq 2500 platform (Illumina), and base calling was performed with RTA v1.18.66.3. FastQ files for each sample were generated using bcl2fastq v2.20.0.422 (Illumina). Sequencing reads were aligned to the mouse reference transcriptome (mm10 v92) and quantified with RSEM v1.3.1. Raw counts were normalized using TPM (Transcripts per Million) and TMM (Trimmed Mean of M-values) methods, transformed to log2 expression values (log2(rawCount + 1)), and used to calculate fold changes and adjusted p-values. Differential expression was defined as Log2FC > 0.584 (1.5 ×) with adjusted p < 0.05. Only mRNAs detected in all three transcriptomes were included in the analysis.

Tissue electroporation

Loss-of-function of miR-200b was induced in mouse embryonic hearts following the protocol described by Mañes-García et al.23. Embryonic hearts from C57BL/6 J mice at E14.5 were exposed and placed in a Petri dish containing warm 1 × PBS. Hearts were incubated in an electroporation mixture consisting of 6% sucrose in 1 × PBS. To ensure efficient delivery, multiple injections of pre-miR-200b precursor (hsa-miR-200b-3p; PM10492, Life Technologies), scramble control (Thermo; #4464077), or anti-miR-200b inhibitor (hsa-miR-200b-3p; AM10492, Life Technologies) were performed.

Electroporation was carried out with the heart positioned between electrodes using the following parameters: 5 cycles of 50 ms at 40 V with 900 ms intervals (Nepa Gene, CUY664-10X15 and CUY650P5). Following electroporation, hearts were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for 24 h.

Mouse model of myocardial infarction (MI)

Two- to three-month-old male and female C57BL/6 mice (Charles River) were used for all experiments. Myocardial infarction (MI) was induced by permanent ligation of the left anterior descending (LAD) coronary artery, following established protocols24. The average preoperative weight of the mice was 27.00 ± 4.23 g. Briefly, mice were anesthetized with sevoflurane (5% for induction, 2–3% for maintenance) and intubated using a blunt-tipped 24-gauge intravenous catheter. Mechanical ventilation was maintained with a rodent ventilator (Minivent 845) delivering a 1:1 mixture of oxygen and air at 160 breaths per minute and a tidal volume of 250 µL. Body temperature was kept at 37 °C using a heating pad. A left thoracotomy was performed at the fourth intercostal space to expose the heart. After incision of the pericardium, the proximal LAD was visualized and permanently ligated using a 7–0 silk suture (Lorca Marín). The chest wall and skin were closed with 6–0 silk sutures (Lorca Marín). Postoperative analgesia was provided with buprenorphine (0.01 mg/kg; Buprex, Merck). Mice were euthanized at 1, 3, 7 or 14 days post-MI via CO₂ inhalation. Sham-operated animals were used as physiological controls (day 0).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted by Statgraphics Centurion (version 19) and GraphPad Prism (version 10.2.0 (341) of the GraphPad Software, LLC; https://www.graphpad.com/). Data were obtained from independent experiments performed in triplicate and are presented as mean ± standard deviation. The number of independent experiments (n) is specified in the corresponding figure legends.

Differences between two groups were analyzed using the unpaired Student’s t test. When more than two groups were compared, one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett´s post hoc test was performed.

Results

miR-200b is expressed in a subset of cells within epicardial epithelial layer in the developing heart

The embryonic epicardium originates from the proepicardium (PE), a cluster of mesothelial cells initially located external to the heart that subsequently spreads across the myocardial surface to form a continuous outer layer, the epicardium2. Following this process, a subset of epicardial cells undergoes epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT), generating cell populations that play essential roles in cardiac development. However, the mechanisms determining why certain epicardial cells transition into epicardium-derived cells (EPDCs) while others remain within the epithelial sheet are not fully understood. The loss of epithelial markers and acquisition of mesenchymal traits are key steps in EMT, and these processes have been reported to be regulated by members of the miR-200 family in various biological contexts. In this regard, previous RNA-seq analyses from our laboratory revealed that miR-200b is differentially expressed during the transition from PE to the early embryonic epicardium, but did not significantly influence EMT marker expression (such as Snail or Prrx1) in epicardial cell cultures. These findings suggest that the role of miR-200b in the epicardium may not be directly related to the epicardial-to-mesenchymal transition25.

To further elucidate the function of miR-200b within the epicardial layer, we first analyzed its expression pattern at different stages of murine heart development by RT-qPCR. As shown in Fig. 1A, and in comparison with other miRNAs involved in cardiac development such as miR-1 and miR-133, miR-200b expression was first detected in the embryonic heart at E12.5, reached its maximum at E15.5, and became undetectable by E16.5. These results indicate that miR-200b expression is restricted to a specific developmental window during which the epicardium actively contributes to cardiac morphogenesis.

To examine miR-200b expression in epicardial cell populations associated with EPDC emergence, we used WT1Cre-eYFP reporter mice, in which YFP is permanently expressed in the WT1 cell lineage26. Since WT1 is widely recognized as a key marker of the embryonic epicardium, WT1-driven eYFP expression enables the identification of epicardial and epicardium-derived cells26. miR-200b LNA in situ hybridization in WT1Cre-eYFP embryos demonstrated that miR-200b expression was restricted to YFP⁺ epicardial cells throughout the entire epicardial layer (Fig. 1B). Expression of miR-200b was first detected at E12.5 and was confined to a subset of cells within the epicardial layer. The number of miR-200b⁺ cells increased at E13.5 and E14.5, with the highest proportion observed at E15.5 (Fig. 1B,C). In contrast, no miR-200b⁺ cells were detected at E11.5 or E16.5 (Supplementary Fig. 1). During the developmental window associated with elevated miR-200b expression (E12.5–E15.5), the number of EPDCs undergoing internalization into the myocardium markedly increased, as inferred from WT1-Cre–driven YFP labeling and their anatomical localization (Fig. 1C,D). Notably, miR-200b levels peaked at E15.5, coinciding with the onset of a decline in EPDC-YFP+ numbers; by E16.5, EPDC-YFP+ internalization was no longer detectable, consistent with the completion of EPDC emergence (Fig. 1A, C,D). Collectively, these findings support a regulatory role for miR-200b in modulating epicardial dynamics during EPDC emergence.

To further confirm miR-200b expression in epicardial cells, YFP⁺ cells were isolated from the hearts of WT1Cre-eYFP embryos at E13.5 by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS), and miR-200b levels were quantified by RT-qPCR (Fig. 1E,F). As shown in Fig. 1F, YFP⁺ cells exhibited significantly higher levels of miR-200b compared with YFP⁻ cells, reinforcing the conclusion that miR-200b is expressed in embryonic epicardial cells. Altogether, these results demonstrate that miR-200b is expressed in a subset of epicardial cells during the developmental stages associated with EPDCs emergence from the epicardium.

miR-200b regulates EPDCs generation by modulating mitotic spindle orientation in epicardial cells

To further investigate the function of miR-200b in the epicardium, we sought to evaluate its involvement in the emergence of EPDCs from the epicardial layer. To this end, ex vivo gain- and loss-of-function experiments were performed by transfecting premiR-200b or antimiR-200b molecules into E12.5 WT1Cre-YFP embryonic heart explants (Fig. 2A,B). Following 24 h of premiR-200b transfection, the number of YFP⁺ EPDCs observed within the myocardium was markedly reduced compared to control (Scramble-treated) explants (Fig. 2C,D). Conversely, antimiR-200b–treated heart explants exhibited a significant increase in YFP⁺ cells that had migrated into the myocardium (Fig. 2C,D). These findings indicate that miR-200b inhibits EPDCs formation and subsequent myocardial invasion, suggesting that this miRNA modulates the generation of EPDCs from the embryonic epicardium.

Previous studies have demonstrated that mitotic spindle orientation plays a critical role in regulating epicardial cell entry into the myocardium. In the embryonic epicardium, symmetric cell divisions give rise to two daughter cells that both remain within the epithelial layer, whereas asymmetric divisions, oriented perpendicularly to the basement membrane, contribute to generate EPDCs11 (Fig. 3A,B). In agreement with prior observations by Wu et al. (2010), we found that both division types occur between E12.5 and E14.5, with asymmetric divisions predominating at E13.5 (Fig. 3C). By E15.5, corresponding to the completion of EMT, mitotic spindles in dividing epicardial cells were predominantly oriented symmetrically (Fig. 3C).

Given our earlier results showing that miR-200b overexpression suppresses EPDCs generation and myocardial internalization, we next examined whether miR-200b influences EPDCs formation by modulating epicardial mitotic spindle orientation. To address this, we performed additional gain- and loss-of-function experiments through ex vivo transfection of premiR-200b and antimiR-200b molecules into E12.5 heart explants (Fig. 3D,E). Subsequent Aurora B immunostaining was used to identify dividing epicardial cells (Fig. 3F). As shown in Fig. 3G, miR-200b overexpression significantly increased the proportion of symmetric divisions relative to control explants, whereas the fraction of asymmetric divisions decreased from approximately 40% to less than 20%. In contrast, antimiR-200b treatment enhanced the occurrence of asymmetric divisions compared to the scramble control. Moreover, miR-200b gain-of-function conditions led to a marked expansion of the symmetric division population (Fig. 3G).

Collectively, these data suggest that miR-200b modulates the balance between symmetric and asymmetric divisions in the epicardial epithelium, thereby influencing EPDCs formation during heart development.

miR-200b regulates symmetric/asymmetric divisions by targeting FLNA in epicardial cells

To dissect the mechanism by which miR-200b controls symmetric versus asymmetric divisions in epicardial cells, we searched for mRNAs that physically interact with miR-200b. To address this, we conducted a biotinylated miR-200b RNA pull-down assay using the MEC1 mouse embryonic epicardial cell line, a well previously established in vitro model of epithelial epicardial cells27–29, followed by RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) (Fig. 4A). This approach enables high specificity by isolating RNAs that directly associate with miR-200b within the RISC complex, thereby identifying direct mRNA targets, unlike methods based solely on miRNA overexpression, which may yield indirect or secondary effects30. RNA-seq analysis identified 32 mRNAs specifically bound to miR-200b in MEC1 cells (Fig. 4B). Based on our previous findings, we focused on miR-200b targets potentially involved in regulating spindle orientation. Among these, Filamin A (FLNA) was of particular interest, as it has been shown to be essential for spindle migration and asymmetric division in mouse oocytes31. Moreover, FLNA loss-of-function causes severe cardiac septation defects, a process linked to both endothelial and epicardial EMT32. Notably, significant changes in Flna expression levels occur in the developing heart during the stages in which EMT takes place (Supplementary Fig. 2) and Luciferase reporter assays further validated Flna as a direct target for miR-200b (Fig. 4C).

To more thoroughly validate FLNA as a bona fide miR-200b target, we performed gain- and loss-of-function assays in MEC1 cells by transfecting pre-miR-200b or anti-miR-200b molecules (Fig. 4D,E). miR-200b overexpression significantly reduced Flna mRNA levels compared with scramble controls, whereas miR-200b inhibition markedly increased Flna expression (Fig. 4F). Consistent with these results, FLNA immunolabeling intensity decreased following pre-miR-200b transfection and increased in anti-miR-200b–treated cells relative to controls (Fig. 4G,H). Together, these findings indicate that miR-200b post-transcriptionally represses FLNA in epicardial cells and support a strong mechanistic link to the regulation of asymmetric divisions.

To confirm this regulation in tissue, we conducted ex vivo gain- and loss-of-function assays by electroporating pre-miR-200b or anti-miR-200b into E14.5 embryonic heart explants (Fig. 5A,B). Consistent with our in vitro data, FLNA immunostaining intensity was significantly reduced after miR-200b overexpression, whereas anti-miR-200b–electroporated explants displayed FLNA levels comparable to scramble controls (Fig. 5C,D). Together, these results confirm that FLNA is post-transcriptionally regulated by miR-200b in the epicardial layer.

Finally, to determine whether FLNA mediates the regulation of symmetric versus asymmetric divisions, we performed FLNA loss-of-function experiments using siRNA-mediated knockdown in embryonic heart explants (Fig. 6A,B). In agreement with the effects of miR-200b overexpression, FLNA silencing led to a significant reduction in asymmetric divisions (Fig. 6C,D). Thus, FLNA depletion recapitulates the miR-200b phenotype, highlighting a critical role for FLNA in promoting asymmetric division in epicardial cells.

Together, these results demonstrate that miR-200b modulates epicardial cell division dynamics by post-transcriptionally repressing FLNA, thereby influencing the balance between symmetric and asymmetric divisions during heart development.

miR-200b expression is altered after myocardial infarction in the mouse and human

It has been demonstrated that following experimental myocardial infarction (MI) in mice, the remote epicardium retains its epithelial morphology, whereas the epicardium in the infarcted region undergoes significant epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT)33. Given that miR-200b regulates EPDC emergence in the developing epicardium, we sought to investigate whether miR-200b expression could also be reactivated in the adult heart following MI. RT-qPCR analysis of bulk cardiac tissues from mice subjected to experimental MI revealed that miR-200b expression is upregulated between 3 and 14 days post-MI, coinciding with the period during which epicardial EMT occurs in the infarcted myocardium (Fig. 7A,B)33. Importantly, we also observed altered miR-200b levels in the peripheral blood of patients following MI (Fig. 7C,D). Collectively, these findings suggest that miR-200b may play a role after MI.

Discussion

The contribution of the epicardium to heart development is a highly complex process that depends on the precise coordination of cell proliferation and differentiation. Epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) represents a pivotal event through which epicardial cells lose polarity, migrate into the myocardium as epicardium-derived cells (EPDCs), and differentiate into distinct cardiac lineages during development and repair34. Proper cardiac morphogenesis and/or remodeling requires a finely tuned balance within the epicardial layer between cell motility and the preservation of epithelial integrity. However, the molecular signals and mechanisms that maintain this balance remain poorly understood. Here, we identify a previously unrecognized miR-200b–FLNA pathway that modulates cell division orientation, thereby defining epithelial dynamics during EPDC formation.

During mouse development, the embryonic epicardium is considered “active” between embryonic days E11.5 and E15.5, a period characterized by intense proliferative activity and successiveregion-specific waves of EMT that generate epicardium-derived mesenchymal cells10,35,36. Although miR-200b has been widely described as an EMT inhibitor and tumor suppressor37,38, our previous findings indicated that miR-200b does not affect EMT marker expression in the epicardium25. Several studies have shown that, among the many miRNAs with critical developmental functions, members of the miR-200 family—including miR-200b—are enriched in epithelial tissues and contribute to maintaining epithelial phenotypes, as observed during lung development13,39–41. Here, we report that miR-200b expression appears to be activated in a subset of epithelial epicardial cells at the onset of EPDC emergence (E12.5). The proportion of miR-200b+ cells increases as EPDC formation progresses toward completion (E15.5), but its expression is no longer detectable once EPDC generation has concluded. Moreover, gain-of-function experiments revealed that miR-200b impairs EPDC formation. Collectively, these findings suggest that miR-200b regulates epithelial dynamics during EPDC genesis. Consistent with this, other studies have reported that several miR-200 family members are expressed in the developing heart between E11.5 and E15.518, coinciding with the temporal window in which we detect epicardial miR-200b expression. While previous analyses were performed at the whole-heart level without epicardial resolution, our results demonstrate, for the first time, the activation of miR-200b specifically within the epicardial layer—highlighting its potential role in regulating epithelial behaviour during EPDC development.

Epicardial cell proliferation and spindle orientation are key determinants of cell ingression into the myocardium. Although β-catenin has been proposed to regulate epicardial polarity and spindle orientation, its reduction does not affect epicardial cell entry into the myocardium11. Consequently, the mechanisms that define which epicardial cells gain the capacity to generate EPDCs, while others remain attached are still poorly understood. Here, we show that modulating miR-200b expression significantly alters EPDC numbers. Because changes in miR-200b levels markedly shift the balance between symmetric and asymmetric divisions, our findings indicate that miR-200b is essential for maintaining the proper equilibrium of division modes within the epicardial layer, thereby ensuring correct EPDC formation, whether this effect is cell-autonomous remains to be determined. The observation that miR-200b expression peaks in the embryonic epicardium at E15.5 coinciding with predominant symmetric division, together with the increase in asymmetric division upon miR-200b loss of function and the expansion of symmetric division upon gain of function, supports the idea that miR-200b regulates the balance between maintenance of epicardial self-renewal and EPDC formation. High miR-200b expression at E15.5 may therefore mark the termination of EPDC generation.

Mechanistically, our data identify miR-200b as a regulator of epicardial dynamics through direct repression of FLNA, a key mediator of asymmetric division. Although FLNA is classically associated with cytoskeletal organization and cell migration42,43, the cardiac malformations observed in Flna-null embryos—outflow tract anomalies and persistent truncus arteriosus—arise from defective development of epicardium-dependent structures such as the endocardial cushion, rather than impaired cell motility32,44. Combined with previous evidence linking FLNA to spindle positioning, these findings establish FLNA as a pivotal effector coupling cytoskeletal architecture to asymmetric division and EPDC formation in the epicardium.

A growing body of evidence underscores the central role of epicardial biology in cardiac repair. Following myocardial injury, epicardial cells become reactivated, forming an expanded layer of EPDCs that differentiate into cardiac fibroblasts and contribute to coronary vessel formation45,46. As neovascularization and fibrosis are key processes in cardiac remodeling and heart failure progression, identifying molecular regulators of EPDC emergence is of significant interest for the development of novel therapeutic strategies. Notably, we found that miR-200b is upregulated in murine infarcted hearts. We further demonstrate that reactivated miR-200b expression occurs within the same temporal window during which EPDCs emergence has been reported in infarcted murine myocardium47–51. Additionally, changes in miR-200b levels were detected in the blood of myocardial infarction (MI) patients. However, as mouse data derive from whole cardiac tissue and the human data from peripheral blood, these findings do not directly resolve epicardial-specific regulation in the adult heart. Instead, they suggest a potential association between miR-200b dynamics and the cardiac injury response, consistent with a possible involvement of epicardial activation. Although decreased circulating levels of miR-200b have been reported in patients with chronic heart failure51, there is currently no evidence regarding its expression in the blood following acute injury. Given its reported involvement in inflammatory processes51, further investigations are needed to clarify the functional contribution of miR-200b to cardiac injury and subsequent repair mechanisms.

This study has several limitations that should be considered. First, the identification and fate of epicardial cells and EPDCs were inferred based on WT1-Cre–YFP lineage tracing and anatomical positional criteria, which may not fully exclude contributions from other cell populations or definitively resolve lineage heterogeneity. Second, although FLNA was identified as a relevant target of miR-200b, we did not directly test whether FLNA is sufficient to recapitulate the observed phenotypes, nor whether its manipulation fully phenocopies the effects of miR-200b modulation. Third, post-MI analyses rely on bulk cardiac tissue in mice and circulating measurements in humans, limiting cellular resolution and rendering these findings correlative rather than causative. Despite these limitations, our findings provide a framework for understanding the miR–FLNA axis during cardiac development and suggest testable hypotheses for its potential involvement in cardiac injury and repair.

In conclusion, we identify miR-200b as a critical regulator of EPDC formation, acting through FLNA to control division symmetry within the epicardium. We show that, in the activated epicardium, miR-200b–mediated repression of FLNA maintains epithelial integrity by restricting asymmetric divisions, whereas its downregulation permits EPDCs generation (Graphical Abstract). These findings uncover a previously unrecognized mechanism balancing epithelial cohesion and EPDCs emergence.
