Visible-Light-Induced Autocatalytic Alkenylation of Quinoxalin-2(1H)‑ones with Indolin-2-ones in Ionic Liquid
Authors: Yubin Liu, Kuaile Lin, Xi Yan, Gang Li, Hongming Cai, Xinyu Wu, Jialiang Zhong, Zhengyan Cai, Dong Yi, Ronghua Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The incorporation of alkene bonds into aromatic heterocyclic skeletons offers an efficient and straightforward strategy for molecular splicing and extension of conjugated systems, while also paving the way for the development of novel functional molecules. However, conventional approaches often depend on aldehyde or ketone precursors, involve multistep sequences, or require harsh reaction conditions, which considerably restrict their applicability. Herein, we present a single-step photoinduced alkene coupling reaction using quinoxalinones and indoleones that proceeds in high yield within a recyclable ionic liquid medium. This process exploits the inherent photosensitivity of the substrates to achieve autocatalysis, eliminating the need for an external photocatalyst, and proceeds under mild conditions with only a stoichiometrically minimal amount of oxidant. The method demonstrates strict stereoselectivity, broad substrate scope, and good functional group tolerance. Mechanistic investigations indicate a radical-based mechanism, wherein photoexcitation generates radical species that are regenerated via hydrogen atom transfer between the photoactive substrate and the radical intermediate. Given the significance of aromatic heterocyclic conjugated systems in pharmaceuticals and energy-related materials, this photocatalytic alkene coupling strategy holds considerable potential for practical applications.

Introduction

Unsaturated carbon–carbon double bonds (CC) represent one of the most prevalent and essential functional groups in organic compounds, for which numerous synthetic methodologies are available. Conventional strategies frequently employ aldehydes as starting materials, which react under basic conditions with substrates bearing ester, phosphonate, or triphenylphosphonium groups to form new olefin linkages between the two molecules, as exemplified by the Still–Gennari reaction, Wittig reaction, and related methods. − Alternatively, ketones can also serve as substrates, reacting under alkaline conditions with reagents bearing trimethylsilyl groups or succinimide motifs to generate olefin bonds in situ, such as in the Peterson reaction and Stobbe condensation (Scheme a). , When the carbon atom to be functionalized lacks a reactive carbonyl group (e.g., as in indole ketones), alkenylation at the C3 position typically proceeds via a two-step sequence: initial site activation through oxidation, halogenation, or diazotization, − followed by construction of the new CC bond under conditions such as heating, basic media, or photocatalytic initiation, thereby coupling the two molecular segments. − Despite their broad utility, these conventional approaches suffer from notable limitations, including strict substrate constraints, dependence on strongly basic conditions, and multistep synthetic sequences. Accordingly, developing new strategies for CC bond formation under simpler and milder conditions remains an active area of research (Scheme b).

In recent years, photocatalytic C–C coupling has demonstrated considerable potential, offering convenient synthetic routes with high atom economy and low energy consumption. − However, such methods typically rely on noble-metal catalysts (Ir, Rh or Ru complexes) or organic dyes (pyrylium or acridinium salt) as photocatalysts (PCs). The use of exogenous PCs introduces additional challenges, such as the costs associated with catalyst synthesis and the need for their subsequent removal during purification. − Recently, visible-light-induced organic transformations that operate without exogenous photosensitizers have received considerable attention, offering an attractive yet challenging direction for further research. , These reactions capitalize on the intrinsic photophysical properties of the substrate molecules themselves, such as their ability to absorb light and undergo photoexcitation, to facilitate energy transfer and electron transfer during the reaction process. For example, Li et al. reported that dihydropyrimidoquinolinone derivatives undergo rapid homolytic cleavage and electron transfer upon photoexcitation, thereby functioning as both the photosensitizer and substrate in photocatalytic coupling without the need for an additional photocatalyst. Similarly, several research groups have documented that quinoxalinones and their derivatives exhibit bright fluorescence upon irradiation and can participate in self-photocatalytic coupling with groups such as methyl, , trifluoromethyl, cycloalkyl, and benzoyl , without external PCs (Scheme c). − It has been proposed that these reactions may proceed via in situ formation of an electron donor–acceptor complex between quinoxalinone and the coupling partner, which subsequently undergoes photoexcitation. − Meanwhile, ionic liquids have been widely used in organic transformations, including enzyme catalysis and electrocatalysis, − due to their safety, recyclability, environmental friendliness, and ability to lower reaction energy barriers and improve efficiency. , Among them, 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium tetrafluoroborate (EMIMBF4) has been widely employed in coupling, photocatalytic, and electrochemical reactions. , These emerging methodologies open new avenues for developing innovative strategies for CC bond formation. Inspired by these advances, we sought to integrate photocatalytic and ionic liquid strategies to establish a green and efficient one-step alkenylation protocol that operates under mild conditions without relying on aldehyde or ketone functional groups.

Herein, we report a one-step photocatalytic olefination between quinoxalinones and indolones. This reaction proceeds under mild conditions in a recyclable ionic liquid medium, affording olefin-bridged π-conjugated heteroaromatics in moderate to high yields. The resulting architecture, featuring heterocycles linked via rigid olefin spacers, extends π-conjugation, thereby enhancing stability and modulate photophysical properties. Consequently, these structures serve as a valuable molecular platform with demonstrated biological relevance (e.g., in antiallergic, anticancer, antileukemia, and anti-infective applications) and outstanding optoelectronic characteristics, enabling applications in fluorescent probes and solar energy harvesting (Scheme d).

Results and Discussion

The model reaction between 1a and 2a was conducted under oxidative photocatalytic conditions (Scheme a). Although H-migration and Z/E isomerism of the new double bond could give three potential products (3A, 3a (Z), 3a (E)), single-crystal X-ray analysis of the purified material identified the product as the olefin-linked 3a with exclusive Z-configuration (Scheme b). Its structure revealed an intramolecular hydrogen bond (N1–H1A···O2) forming a stable six-membered ring, which likely accounts for the observed stereoselectivity (see SI, Tables S3–S7).

Based on the model reaction outcomes, we proceeded to optimize the reaction conditions. When K2S2O8 (1.0 equiv) was employed as the oxidant in various solvents, comparable yields were obtained in water, DMSO, and ionic liquid, affording the target product 3a in 90%, 87%, and 91% yields, respectively (Table ). In contrast, other solvents (entries 4, 8–11) gave little or no product. Notably, the use of an ionic liquid as the solvent not only gave a higher yield but also significantly shortened the reaction time to 14 h (entry 13), demonstrating a clear advantage. For substrates bearing electron-withdrawing groups, the reaction failed completely in water, whereas moderate yields were obtained in ionic liquids (entries 17–20), highlighting the superiority of the latter. We then evaluated several oxidants and their loadings at room temperature. K2S2O8 was identified as the optimal oxidant. Subsequent screening of the oxidant equivalents in ionic liquid and water (entries 2, 12, 14) revealed that while 0.1 equiv of K2S2O8 slowed the reaction, a yield of 76% was still achieved after 72 h (entry 12), indicating regeneration of the oxidant. In contrast, 0.5 equiv in the ionic liquid achieved >90% yield within 14 h, establishing this loading as the most economical and efficient condition. Thus, the standard conditions were established as follows: 1a (0.2 mmol), 2a (1.0 equiv), K2S2O8 (0.5 equiv), 3 W blue LED (440 nm), room temperature, under air, in EMIMBF4 (2 mL).

The substrate scope was examined using various substituted quinoxalin-2-ones under the standard conditions (Scheme ). The corresponding products 3a–3x were obtained in 47–93% yields. N-Alkyl substituents (≤C4) afforded high yields (∼90%), while N-alkenyl, alkynyl, or aryl groups gave moderate yields (47–70%). The presence of long, unsaturated N-substituents increased byproduct formation, lowering yields to ∼50% (e.g., 3d). Halogen substituents on the arene ring reduced solubility and slightly decreased yields. The dihalogenated analog 3u yielded poorly, and the highly insoluble substrate for 3v was unreactive. Electron-donating groups (e.g., methyl, methoxy) enhanced reactivity; however, prolonged reaction times promoted side reactions (oxidation, dimerization), necessitating a shortened 10-h window to maximize yield. In contrast, electron-withdrawing groups led to lower reactivity and diminished yields. These results demonstrate broad substrate compatibility, although sterically demanding or unsaturated N-substituents promote byproducts, and arene substituents significantly modulate reactivity and selectivity.

Under the optimized conditions, the substrate scope was further evaluated using 1a and various substituted indolin-2-ones (Scheme ). Good yields were obtained even with NH-unprotected indolin-2-ones. A wide range of functional groups on the arene ring, including electron-donating and electron-withdrawing substituents, as well as N-aryl groups, were well tolerated, affording products 3aa–3ar in 58–92% yields. Notably, the reaction could be extended to bioisosteric analogues: benzofuran-2-one and pyrrolo­[3,4-b]­pyridine-5,7-dione derivatives also reacted smoothly, yielding 3an and 3ao in 92% and 72% yields, respectively, further demonstrating the broad applicability of this methodology.

To demonstrate the scalability and practical utility of this methodology, a gram-scale reaction was conducted between 1a (1 g) and 2a (0.91 g) under the standard conditions. The target product 3a was obtained in 78% yield, highlighting the potential of this approach for practical synthesis. Throughout the scale-up process, the ionic liquid was efficiently recovered with a 95% recovery rate. Analytical comparisons, including appearance, NMR and IR spectroscopy, K+ concentration, and moisture content, confirmed that the recovered ionic liquid was consistent with the freshly obtained material (see SI). The recovered ionic liquid was then reused in consecutive cycles under the standard reaction conditions, maintaining high yields 92% for the first reuse, 90% for the second, 90% for the third, 89% for the fourth, and 91% for the fifth cycle. These results demonstrate that the ionic liquid can be recovered and reused at least five times without significant loss in reaction efficiency, further confirming the robustness and practicality of this protocol.

Mechanistic Studies

To gain further insight into the reaction mechanism, a series of control experiments were conducted (Scheme S2). The reaction proceeded in only very low yield in the absence of light (Scheme S2a), indicating that continuous irradiation is essential. When K2S2O8 was omitted, the reaction was largely suppressed under air, nitrogen, or oxygen atmospheres (Scheme S2b,c), suggesting that K2S2O8 acts as an essential oxidant, while molecular oxygen plays a negligible role. The addition of the radical scavenger TEMPO completely inhibited the reaction, whereas the weaker radical inhibitor BHT led to a significant decrease in yield. LC-MS analysis detected adducts of BHT with both 2a and 3a, supporting the involvement of radical intermediates (Scheme S2d,e). Furthermore, when the reducing radical quencher DABCO was introduced, the reaction was almost completely suppressed (Scheme S2f), indicating inhibition of the subsequent oxidation activation pathway involving the strongly oxidizing sulfate radical anion (SO4 •– , E° = 2.6 V) (see SI). Furthermore, UV–vis absorption spectroscopy revealed strong absorption by both 1a and 3a at 440 nm under the reaction conditions (Figure S7), indicating their pronounced photosensitivity at the irradiation wavelength.

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations using the M06–2X functional in an ionic liquid continuum model , provided triplet energies and singlet–triplet energy gaps (ΔE ST) for 1a, 2a, and 3a, further supporting the higher photosensitivity of 1a and 3a. Owing to its significantly greater solubility in the ionic liquid medium compared to 3a, 1a is more likely to serve as the effective photocatalyst in the system.

Subsequently, fluorescence quenching experiments on 1a were performed using HSO4 – and K2S2O8 as potential quenchers. The fluorescence of 1a was effectively quenched by HSO4 – but not by K2S2O8 (Scheme a). Stern–Volmer analysis indicated that this quenching is dynamic, suggesting that 1a in hydrogen atom transfer (HAT) steps during catalysis (Scheme b). DFT calculations identified key intermediates and supported a direct HAT pathway. ADCH charge analysis showed that the sulfate anion loses 0.28 and 0.30 e after abstracting one and two H atoms from 1a, respectively. While an LC-MS signal for the two-H atom abstraction product was detected, the single-abstraction intermediate was likely too transient to observe. The resulting HSO4 – species transforms into SO4 •– . Spin density analyses of intermediates Int2 and Int3 (Scheme c) further confirmed the H atom abstraction capability of 1a, highlighting its essential role in the SET cycle.

Based on the control experiments (Table ) and previous literature reports, ,− a plausible reaction mechanism for the formation of 3a is proposed in Scheme d. The transformation is proposed to proceed as follows: initially, under photocatalytic conditions, persulfate (S2O8 2– ) decomposes to generate sulfate radical anions (SO4 •– ). Subsequently, 2a undergoes oxidation via SO4 •– through a combined hydrogen atom transfer and single-electron transfer (HAT/SET) process, yielding radical intermediate 2a′, while SO4 •– is reduced to HSO4 – . The resulting radical 2a′ then attacks the C3 position of 1a, forming the Z-configured radical intermediate 3a′, likely stabilized by an intramolecular hydrogen bond. Finally, 3a′ undergoes a second HAT/SET oxidation by persulfate, affording the olefin product 3a. The HSO4 – species is regenerated to K2S2O8 through direct HAT catalysis mediated by 1a (or 3a), thus closing the catalytic cycle.

Conclusions

In summary, we have developed a photocatalytic method for direct (Z)-selective alkenylation of quinoxalin-2­(1H)-ones with indolin-2-ones. This approach requires no external photocatalyst, employs only a substoichiometric amount of oxidant, and utilizes a recyclable ionic liquid as an environmentally benign reaction medium. The reaction proceeds under mild conditions with high stereoselectivity, affording the products in a single configurational isomer. As shown by the model reactions, moderate to high yields were obtained across a broad range of substituted substrates and their bioisosteric analogues, highlighting broad functional group tolerance and general applicability. Mechanistic studies based on control experiments and computational analysis suggest a cyclic reaction pathway involving regeneration of both the catalyst and oxidant. Notably, this methodology provides a practical and efficient synthetic tool for constructing alkene-bridged rigid skeletons with extended π-conjugated systems. The structural features of these products make them valuable not only as privileged scaffolds for the discovery of novel bioactive molecules in medicinal chemistry but also as promising candidates with optical activity for materials science applications.
