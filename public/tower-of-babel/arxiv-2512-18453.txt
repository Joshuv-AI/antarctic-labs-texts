NOVA: Discovering Well-Conditioned Winograd Transforms through Numerical Optimization of Vandermonde Arithmetic
Jayant Lohia

Abstract
Winograd convolution is the standard algorithm for efficient inference, reducing arithmetic complexity by 2.25 × \times for 3 × \times 3 kernels. However, it faces a critical barrier in the modern era of low-precision computing: numerical instability. As tiles scale to maximize efficiency (e.g., F(6,3), F(8,3)), the condition numbers of standard integer-based transforms explode—reaching κ ≈ 2 × 10 5 \kappa\approx 2\times 10^{5} for F(8,3)—rendering them unusable in FP16 or Int8. We introduce NOVA ( N umerical O ptimization of V andermonde A rithmetic), a discovery framework that breaks the decades-old convention of integer interpolation. Treating Winograd point selection as a continuous optimization problem, NOVA searches the manifold ℝ n − 1 \mathbb{R}^{n-1} via Evolution Strategy, snaps candidates to simple rationals, and guarantees correctness via symbolic verification. This process uncovers a hidden landscape of stable, fractional configurations—such as { ± 5 / 6 , ± 7 / 6 , ± 3 / 5 } \{\pm\nicefrac{{5}}{{6}},\pm\nicefrac{{7}}{{6}},\pm\nicefrac{{3}}{{5}}\} —that defy traditional vocabulary constraints. The impact is transformative: NOVA improves the conditioning of F(8,3) by 415 × \times in 1D, which squares to a 172,484 × \times improvement for 2D convolution. In real-world FP16 ImageNet inference, where standard transforms collapse to random chance (e.g., 4.7% accuracy on VGG-16), NOVA’s points restore full accuracy (75–78%), recovering over 70 percentage points without retraining, calibration, or learned parameters. These discovered transforms act as drop-in replacements, effectively unlocking the efficiency of large-tile Winograd convolution for next-generation hardware.

Background

We review the Cook-Toom algorithm for fast convolution, the Vandermonde conditioning problem, and the structure of the search space that enables open discovery.

2.1
Winograd Convolution via Cook-Toom

Consider a 1D convolution of an input signal
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
with a kernel
\bm{g}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
, producing output
\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
where
y_{i}=\sum_{k=0}^{r-1}g_{k}\cdot x_{i+k}
. Direct computation requires
m\cdot r
multiplications.

The Cook-Toom algorithm
(
21
)
reduces this to
n=m+r-1
multiplications by interpreting convolution as polynomial multiplication. Let
x(t)=\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}x_{i}t^{i}
and
g(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{r-1}g_{k}t^{k}
. The product
y(t)=x(t)\cdot g(t)
has degree
m+r-2
, so
n
evaluations at distinct points suffice to recover
y(t)
via interpolation.

Algorithm Structure.

Choose
n
distinct interpolation points
\mathcal{S}=\{\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n-2},\infty\}
. The algorithm proceeds:

1.

Input Transform
:
\tilde{\bm{x}}=\bm{B}^{T}\bm{x}
(evaluate input polynomial at each point)

2.

Kernel Transform
:
\tilde{\bm{g}}=\bm{G}\bm{g}
(evaluate kernel polynomial at each point)

3.

Element-wise Multiply
:
\tilde{\bm{y}}=\tilde{\bm{x}}\odot\tilde{\bm{g}}
(only
n
multiplications)

4.

Output Transform
:
\bm{y}=\bm{A}^{T}\tilde{\bm{y}}
(interpolate to recover output)

The matrices
\bm{A},\bm{B},\bm{G}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times*}
are derived from Lagrange interpolation systems based on
\mathcal{S}
.

The Point at Infinity.

To achieve the minimal
n=m+r-1
multiplications, one interpolation point is placed at infinity (
\alpha_{n-1}=\infty
). This corresponds to extracting the leading coefficient of the product polynomial. We denote the
n-1
finite points as
\mathcal{S}_{\text{fin}}=\{\alpha_{0},\ldots,\alpha_{n-2}\}
.

Scope
: Our formulation follows the standard Cook-Toom/Winograd construction
(
14
;
23
)
where the infinity point enables minimal multiplication count. Alternative formulations exist: (1) modular/CRT-based methods
(
24
)
avoid infinity via exact arithmetic; (2) some implementations use
n
finite points with one additional multiplication. Our discovered points apply to the standard formulation used in cuDNN, TensorRT, and most deep learning frameworks.

2.2
Vandermonde Conditioning

The transformation matrices are fundamentally tied to Vandermonde matrices. For the
n-1

finite
points
\mathcal{S}_{\text{fin}}=\{\alpha_{0},\ldots,\alpha_{n-2}\}
:

\bm{V}(\mathcal{S}_{\text{fin}})=\begin{bmatrix}1&\alpha_{0}&\cdots&\alpha_{0}^{n-2}\\
1&\alpha_{1}&\cdots&\alpha_{1}^{n-2}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
1&\alpha_{n-2}&\cdots&\alpha_{n-2}^{n-2}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-1)\times(n-1)}

(1)

Handling the Infinity Point.

The infinity point
\alpha_{n-1}=\infty
corresponds to extracting the leading coefficient of the product polynomial and does not appear in the Vandermonde matrix. Its contribution to the transform matrices is a single row/column of
[0,\ldots,0,1]
, which is perfectly conditioned. Thus,
we compute
\kappa_{2}(\bm{V})
using only the finite points
; the infinity point does not affect Vandermonde conditioning.

Condition Number Definition.

We use the spectral (2-norm) condition number throughout:
\kappa_{2}(\bm{V})=\|\bm{V}\|_{2}\cdot\|\bm{V}^{-1}\|_{2}=\sigma_{\max}/\sigma_{\min}
, where
\sigma_{\max},\sigma_{\min}
are the largest and smallest singular values. Vandermonde matrices are notoriously ill-conditioned
(
8
)
, with
\kappa_{2}(\bm{V})
growing exponentially in
n
for poorly chosen points.

Theorem 2.1

(Vandermonde Conditioning,
8
)
.

For Vandermonde matrix
\bm{V}
with real points
\{\alpha_{i}\}
,
\kappa(\bm{V})
grows at least as fast as the inverse of minimum point separation. Well-conditioned configurations cluster points like Chebyshev nodes.

Relationship to Transform Matrices.

The matrices
\bm{A}
,
\bm{B}
,
\bm{G}
are derived from
\bm{V}
via Lagrange interpolation systems. While the exact relationship depends on the construction,
\kappa(\bm{V})
serves as a
proxy
for overall numerical stability: points that minimize
\kappa(\bm{V})
consistently yield lower
\kappa(\bm{A})
,
\kappa(\bm{B})
,
\kappa(\bm{G})
. We validate this empirically in Section
4
(Table
3
), showing
\kappa(\bm{V})
improvements propagate to all transform matrices with bounded proportionality (70–415
\times
for
\kappa(\bm{V})
vs. 70–270
\times
for
\kappa(\bm{A})
).

Limitation:
We do not provide a formal backward error analysis for the complete Winograd pipeline. The condition number
\kappa
bounds worst-case error amplification but does not account for error cancellation, input statistics, or accumulation patterns. Our empirical CNN validation (Section
4
) demonstrates that
\kappa
improvements translate to accuracy gains in practice.

The Search Space

Our key insight is that the search space for Winograd point discovery has a simple structure:

Definition 2.2

(Search Space)
.

For Winograd F(
m
,
r
), the search space is:

\mathcal{S}=\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\quad\text{where }n=m+r-1

(2)

Each point in
\mathcal{S}
specifies
n-1

This continuous formulation enables optimization via Evolution Strategy. After ES converges, we

Why Not Chebyshev Nodes?

Chebyshev nodes provide optimal conditioning for polynomial interpolation but are irrational:

x_{k}=\cos\left(\frac{2k+1}{2n}\pi\right),\quad k=0,\ldots,n-1

(3)

For Winograd, we require
exact
transformation matrices to avoid floating-point errors in the decomposition. Irrational points would introduce approximation error. Our approach discovers
simple rational
points that approximate optimal conditioning while maintaining exact arithmetic.

2.4
Symbolic Verification

All discovered configurations must satisfy exact decomposition:

\left\|\mathcal{T}_{\text{conv}}-\sum_{p=0}^{n-1}\bm{A}_{:,p}\otimes\bm{B}_{:,p}\otimes\bm{G}_{p,:}\right\|_{F}<10^{-14}

(4)

We implement verification using SymPy with exact rational arithmetic:

1.

Represent points as Python
Fraction
objects

2.

Handle infinity via projective coordinates
(1:0)

3.

Construct
\bm{A}
,
\bm{B}
,
\bm{G}
symbolically via Lagrange interpolation

4.

Verify tensor decomposition algebraically (no floating-point errors)

This eliminates false positives from floating-point verification errors and guarantees that discovered configurations are mathematically correct.

Method: Open Discovery

We present an open discovery framework for Winograd interpolation points that operates in continuous space without relying on any predefined vocabulary.

3.1
Search Space Formulation

Fact 3.1

(Infinity Point Requirement
(
21
)
)
.

Every valid Winograd F(
m
,
r
) configuration requires the point at infinity to achieve the minimal multiplication count
n=m+r-1
.

This reduces the search space to
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
for choosing
n-1
finite points, with infinity always included.

3.2
Evolution Strategy

We use Evolution Strategy (ES) to search
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
for well-conditioned configurations.

Fitness Function.

\displaystyle f(\bm{p})

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\|\mathcal{T}-\mathcal{T}_{\text{rec}}(\bm{p})\|_{F}}_{\text{tensor error}}+\lambda_{1}\underbrace{\frac{\|\bm{p}\|^{2}}{n-1}}_{\text{magnitude}}

\displaystyle\quad+\lambda_{2}\underbrace{\log_{10}(\kappa(\bm{V})+1)}_{\text{conditioning}}

(5)

where
\mathcal{T}
is the ground-truth convolution tensor,
\mathcal{T}_{\text{rec}}(\bm{p})
is the CP decomposition, and
\lambda_{1}=0.001
,
\lambda_{2}=0.0001
.

Algorithm.

ES maintains a population with adaptive mutation (Algorithm
1
in Appendix
B
). Each generation: (1) sample candidates around mean
\bm{\mu}
, (2) evaluate fitness, (3) select top 25% elite, (4) update
\bm{\mu}
to elite mean, (5) adapt step size
\sigma
.

3.3
Snap-to-Rational and Verification

After ES converges, we convert continuous values to simple rationals:

\text{snap}(x;d_{\max})=\argmin_{a/b:1\leq b\leq d_{\max}}|x-a/b|

(6)

with
d_{\max}=10
. All configurations undergo exact verification using SymPy rational arithmetic, checking
\|\mathcal{T}_{\text{conv}}-\sum_{p}\bm{A}_{:,p}\otimes\bm{B}_{:,p}\otimes\bm{G}_{p,:}\|_{F}<10^{-10}
.

3.4
2D Extension

For 2D convolution, transformation matrices are Kronecker products:
\bm{G}_{2D}=\bm{G}\otimes\bm{G}
, etc. Under the spectral norm, singular values of Kronecker products multiply:
\sigma_{i}(\bm{A}\otimes\bm{A})=\sigma_{j}(\bm{A})\sigma_{k}(\bm{A})
, yielding
\kappa_{2}(\bm{A}\otimes\bm{A})=\kappa_{2}(\bm{A})^{2}

(
10
)
. Thus 1D improvements
square
in 2D: a 27
\times
1D improvement becomes
\sim
730
\times
in 2D.

Verification:
We confirmed this property by directly computing
\kappa_{2}(\bm{A}\otimes\bm{A})
for F(4,3) and F(6,3). All ratios
\kappa_{2}(\bm{A}\otimes\bm{A})/\kappa_{2}(\bm{A})^{2}=1.0000
exactly, validating the squaring relationship.

INT8 Setup.

We evaluate two quantization modes to isolate conditioning effects:

•

•

Per-channel INT8 reduces sensitivity to conditioning by introducing per-channel normalization, an architectural mitigation rather than an algorithmic fix. Conditioning remains the dominant factor in regimes where such normalization is constrained (e.g., per-tensor quantization or fixed-scale accelerators).

Related Work

Winograd Convolution in Deep Learning.

Lavin and Gray
(
14
)
introduced Winograd convolution to deep learning, achieving significant speedups for 3
\times
3 kernels. Subsequent work extended this to sparse networks
(
16
)
and optimized implementations for various hardware
(
11
)
. These works use standard integer interpolation points without optimizing for numerical stability.

Numerical Stability Analysis.

Barabasz and Gregg
(
4
)
provided comprehensive error analysis of Winograd convolution, demonstrating that errors grow exponentially with tile size due to Vandermonde conditioning. Vincent et al.
(
22
)
proposed post-pass scaling to mitigate errors. These works analyze the problem but do not address point selection.

Interpolation Point Optimization.

Alam et al.
(
1
)
(ACM TECS 2022) search a parameterized family of symmetric reciprocal points using deterministic grid and empirical search. They demonstrate that non-integer configurations can achieve lower error than integers. Our work differs in three ways: (1) we search the full continuous space
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
via Evolution Strategy rather than a constrained parameterized family; (2) we do not enforce reciprocal symmetry, enabling discovery of clustered points like
\nicefrac{{5}}{{6}}
and
\nicefrac{{7}}{{6}}
; (3) we employ exact symbolic verification via SymPy rather than floating-point checks.

Zhang et al.
(
25
)
(HAW, IEEE 2023) use RL for hardware-aware point selection, optimizing for both accuracy and circuit area in FPGA implementations. Their focus is on gate count rather than conditioning.

Our Contribution
: We achieve conditioning improvements of 2.9
\times
to 415
\times
by searching the full continuous space and discovering fractional points that cluster more tightly than standard integers.

Alternative Stability Approaches.

Prior work has explored several alternatives to point selection:

Legendre basis transforms
: Barabasz et al.
(
2
)
proposed changing from the standard monomial basis to orthogonal Legendre polynomials to reduce numerical error. Critically, this approach provides conditioning benefits during
training
when weights are stored in Legendre coefficient form. For
drop-in inference
with pretrained models (where inputs/outputs remain in monomial form), the relationship
\bm{L}=\bm{V}\bm{P}
(where
\bm{L}
is Legendre evaluation,
\bm{V}
is Vandermonde,
\bm{P}
is the coefficient matrix) means
\bm{L}\bm{P}^{-1}=\bm{V}
—the effective transform equals the standard Vandermonde.

Key distinction
: Legendre basis and optimal point selection address
different use cases
:

•

Legendre basis
: Training-time benefit; requires storing weights in Legendre form

•

Optimal points
: Inference-time benefit; works with existing pretrained models

For the pretrained ResNet-18 experiments in Section
4
, Legendre basis is not applicable without retraining. Our discovered points provide a
drop-in improvement
for pretrained models—no calibration data or learned parameters required. Alternative post-training methods such as learned scaling
(
12
)
can also rescue pretrained Winograd inference but require optimizing scale factors. Interestingly, our analysis shows that combining optimal points with Legendre basis (for future training) would achieve even better conditioning:
\kappa(\bm{L})=6.2
for discovered points vs.
\kappa(\bm{L})=76.0
for standard points.

Tap-wise quantization
: Chikin and Kryzhanovskiy
(
6
)
proposed learned per-tap power-of-two scaling factors that enable near-FP32 accuracy for F(4,3) with INT8 arithmetic. Their hardware-friendly execution model maintains efficiency while addressing quantization error. This approach and ours solve the same underlying problem (numerical instability under low precision) from different angles: they scale the transform outputs adaptively, while we reduce conditioning at the source.

Why no direct comparison
: Tap-wise scaling and optimal point selection are
orthogonal
interventions. Tap-wise adds per-tap scale factors to compensate for quantization error post-hoc; optimal points reduce error at the source via better-conditioned transforms. A “Standard + Tap-wise” vs “Discovered” comparison conflates these mechanisms. The appropriate comparison is “Standard” vs “Discovered” (our Table
29
) and separately “+Tap-wise” vs “-Tap-wise”. Combining discovered points with tap-wise scaling is viable future work that may yield further improvements.

RNS exact arithmetic
: Yepremyan and Knowles
(
24
)
proposed using Residue Number System arithmetic to enable exact computation in Winograd transforms, eliminating rounding error entirely for arbitrarily large tiles. This changes the arithmetic model fundamentally (requiring CRT reconstruction) and trades off implementation complexity for exactness. Our approach operates within standard floating-point/integer pipelines and does not require RNS infrastructure, making it a drop-in improvement for existing implementations.

Symbolic Fourier Convolution (SFC)
: He et al.
(
9
)
(arXiv 2024) proposed extending DFT with symbolic computing to avoid irrational numbers in low-precision convolution. SFC uses only additions for transformation at specific points, eliminating floating-point error from irrational DFT coefficients. This represents an alternative to Winograd entirely. Our work optimizes
within
the Winograd framework, making it complementary: SFC may be preferred for very large tiles where even optimized Winograd remains unstable.

Complex-field transforms
: Budagyan and Shumikhin
(
5
)
proposed using complex arithmetic to reduce Winograd transform sizes. Complex-field approaches trade implementation complexity (complex multipliers, FFT integration) for theoretical efficiency gains. Our approach maintains real-valued transforms that are directly compatible with existing hardware accelerators.

Winograd-aware training
: Liu et al.
(
17
)
proposed jointly optimizing network weights with Winograd transform awareness during training. This model-specific approach requires retraining but can adapt the network to work better with Winograd’s numerical characteristics. Our discovered points are compatible with Winograd-aware trained models and provide orthogonal benefits without requiring retraining.

Symmetric/reciprocal points
: Pan et al.
(
18
)
explored symmetric point families that reduce error through structural constraints. Our symmetric search similarly exploits this structure, though we search a broader space of symmetric configurations.

Positioning
: We do not claim that optimal points alone supersede all prior work. Rather, point selection is one lever among several (basis changes, scaling, exact arithmetic) for addressing Winograd stability. Our contribution is demonstrating that continuous-space search with rational snapping yields simple, exactly-computable points that improve conditioning by 2.9–415
\times
within existing pipelines.

Low-Precision Winograd.

Li et al.
(
15
)
(PAW, NeurIPS 2023) proposed PTQ-Aware Winograd, which applies post-training quantization to Winograd transforms. They optimize the transformation matrices
\bm{A}
,
\bm{B}
,
\bm{G}
given fixed interpolation points. Similarly, LoWino
(
7
)
(ACM TACO 2024) applies linear quantization in the Winograd domain.

Koppula et al.
(
13
)
(Wino Vidi Vici, WACV 2024) address 8-bit Winograd stability through improved accumulation strategies.

Key Difference
: These approaches treat the interpolation points as fixed and apply quantization post-hoc. We address the problem at its source by discovering better interpolation points. Our dtype-aware discovery additionally constrains points to be exactly representable in float16, combining conditioning optimization with representability.

Vandermonde Matrix Conditioning.

The ill-conditioning of Vandermonde matrices is well-studied
(
8
;
10
)
. Chebyshev nodes provide theoretically optimal conditioning for polynomial interpolation but are irrational. Our experiments show that Chebyshev approximations achieve
\kappa(\bm{V})\approx 20
–
627
for F(4–8,3), while our discovered rational points achieve
\kappa(\bm{V})\approx 14.5
–
474
, demonstrating 1.3–1.4
\times
further improvement through rational snapping (see Table
25
).

Evolution Strategy for Optimization.

ES has been applied to neural network optimization
(
20
)
and hyperparameter tuning. We apply ES to the interpolation point discovery problem, which has a smooth fitness landscape well-suited to gradient-free optimization. Unlike RL approaches that require reward shaping and policy networks, ES directly optimizes the fitness function in continuous space.

6
Limitations

Tile Size Scope.

We evaluate tiles F(2,3) through F(8,3), covering the most common deep learning use cases. Larger tiles (e.g., F(16,3)) may exhibit different behavior, though the exponential growth of condition numbers with standard points makes very large tiles impractical regardless of point selection.

Symmetric Configuration Bias.

Our symmetric search preferentially finds symmetric configurations (e.g.,
\pm\nicefrac{{5}}{{6}}
). While symmetric points are natural for convolution and achieve excellent conditioning, this may miss asymmetric configurations that could achieve even better results in some cases. ES does explore asymmetric configurations, but the fitness landscape may have local minima near symmetric solutions.

Kernel Size Restriction.

We focus on
r=3
(3
\times
3 kernels), the dominant kernel size in CNNs. Extensions to
r=5
and
r=7
are straightforward—our method applies unchanged—but require larger tile sizes and correspondingly longer search times.

1D to 2D Extension.

We use Kronecker products for 2D extension, which is standard but may not be optimal. Direct 2D point selection could potentially find better configurations that do not factor as products of 1D transforms. However, the search space for direct 2D optimization is substantially larger.

Float16 Trade-off.

As demonstrated in Section
D.4
, discovered fractional points have better conditioning but worse float16 representation than integers. The optimal choice depends on application-specific trade-offs. Our dtype-aware discovery addresses this, but finding globally optimal configurations that balance both criteria remains challenging.

Empirical Validation Scope.

Our validation includes both controlled workloads (100K synthetic samples) and comprehensive end-to-end CNN evaluation on ImageNetV2 (30,000 images) across 6 architectures (ResNet-18/50, VGG-16/16-BN, MobileNet-V2, EfficientNet-B0). We demonstrate 67–73 percentage point accuracy recovery for FP16 F(6,3). The relationship between
\kappa
reduction and error reduction is well-established in prior work
(
3
)
, and we extend this with A, B, G analysis in the Appendix. Combining discovered points with PTQ pipelines (e.g., learned scales) remains valuable future work for production deployment. Our discovered points are drop-in compatible with existing implementations and integrate seamlessly with such pipelines.

Condition Number vs. Actual Error.

We optimize Vandermonde condition number
\kappa(\bm{V})
as a proxy for numerical stability. While
\kappa(\bm{V})
is a well-established indicator, the actual error in low-precision inference depends on additional factors including transform matrix norms, dynamic range, and quantization strategies. The exact relationship between
\kappa(\bm{V})
and end-to-end inference error deserves further analysis.

Transform Matrix Properties.

We optimize
\kappa(\bm{V})
as our primary objective, but show in Appendix that improvements propagate to all transform matrices
\bm{A}
,
\bm{B}
,
\bm{G}
. While we now report these metrics, the exact relationship between individual matrix norms and end-to-end inference error merits further theoretical analysis.

Appendix A
Proofs and Derivations

A.1
Vandermonde Matrix Construction

Proof of Vandermonde Conditioning (Theorem
2.1
).

The Vandermonde matrix
\bm{V}(\mathcal{S})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
for points
\mathcal{S}=\{\alpha_{0},\ldots,\alpha_{n-1}\}
has entries
V_{ij}=\alpha_{i}^{j}
. The condition number is:

\kappa(\bm{V})=\|\bm{V}\|_{2}\cdot\|\bm{V}^{-1}\|_{2}

(7)

For points spread across a large range (e.g., integers
\{0,\pm 1,\pm 2,\ldots\}
), the rows corresponding to large points have entries that grow exponentially in the column index. This creates large differences in row norms, increasing
\|\bm{V}\|
while making inversion numerically unstable.

Chebyshev-like node distributions minimize conditioning by clustering points toward the interval endpoints. However, Chebyshev nodes are irrational and cannot be used directly for symbolic Winograd construction.

Our key insight is that rational fractions like
\nicefrac{{5}}{{6}}
and
\nicefrac{{7}}{{6}}
provide a middle ground: they keep points closer together than integers (improving conditioning) while remaining exact rationals (enabling symbolic computation).
∎

A.2
Cook-Toom Correctness

Theorem A.1

(Cook-Toom Decomposition)
.

For any set of
n=m+r-1
distinct points
\mathcal{S}
including infinity, the Cook-Toom algorithm produces transformation matrices
\bm{A}
,
\bm{B}
,
\bm{G}
such that:

\bm{y}=\bm{A}^{T}\left((\bm{G}\bm{g})\odot(\bm{B}^{T}\bm{x})\right)

(8)

computes the linear convolution
\bm{y}=\bm{x}*\bm{g}
exactly.

Proof.

The convolution
y(t)=x(t)\cdot g(t)
of polynomials
x(t)=\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}x_{i}t^{i}
and
g(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{r-1}g_{k}t^{k}
has degree
m+r-2
, so
n=m+r-1
evaluations at distinct points uniquely determine
y(t)
via Lagrange interpolation.

The matrices are constructed as:

•

\bm{B}^{T}\bm{x}
: Evaluates
x(t)
at each point in
\mathcal{S}

•

\bm{G}\bm{g}
: Evaluates
g(t)
at each point in
\mathcal{S}

•

Element-wise product: Gives
y(\alpha_{i})=x(\alpha_{i})\cdot g(\alpha_{i})

•

\bm{A}^{T}
: Interpolates from point evaluations back to polynomial coefficients

The point at infinity contributes the leading coefficient via the limit
\lim_{t\to\infty}y(t)/t^{n-1}
.

Symbolic verification: We check
\|\mathcal{T}_{\text{conv}}-\sum_{p}\bm{A}_{:,p}\otimes\bm{B}_{:,p}\otimes\bm{G}_{p,:}\|_{F}/\|\mathcal{T}_{\text{conv}}\|_{F}<10^{-10}
using exact rational arithmetic via SymPy.
∎

A.3
Fact: Kronecker Product Condition Number

Fact A.2

(Well-known
(
10
)
)
.

For Kronecker product of matrices:
\kappa(\bm{A}\otimes\bm{A})=\kappa(\bm{A})^{2}
.

Proof.

The singular values of
\bm{A}\otimes\bm{A}
are all products
\sigma_{i}\cdot\sigma_{j}
where
\sigma_{i}
,
\sigma_{j}
are singular values of
\bm{A}
. Thus:

\displaystyle\sigma_{\max}(\bm{A}\otimes\bm{A})

\displaystyle=\sigma_{\max}(\bm{A})^{2}

\displaystyle\sigma_{\min}(\bm{A}\otimes\bm{A})

\displaystyle=\sigma_{\min}(\bm{A})^{2}

∎

This has critical implications: a 19
\times

\times
improvement in 2D.

A.4
Symmetric Point Configurations

Lemma A.3

(Symmetric Point Benefit)
.

For symmetric point sets
\mathcal{S}=\{0,\alpha_{1},-\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},-\alpha_{2},\ldots,\infty\}
, the Vandermonde matrix exhibits structure that improves conditioning.

Sketch.

Under the change of variables
u=t^{2}
, the Vandermonde system separates into even and odd polynomial components. Each sub-problem involves a smaller Vandermonde matrix with points
\{\alpha_{1}^{2},\alpha_{2}^{2},\ldots\}
, which is typically better conditioned than the original.

This explains why symmetric configurations achieve excellent conditioning: the point set
\{0,\pm\nicefrac{{5}}{{6}},\pm\nicefrac{{7}}{{6}}\}
inherently exploits this structure.
∎

A.5
Optimal Point Characterization

Remark A.4

(Fractional Points Intuition)
.

Why do fractional points like
\nicefrac{{5}}{{6}}
outperform integers like
2
?

Consider the spacing of points. Standard integers
\{0,1,-1,2,-2\}
span the interval
[-2,2]
with non-uniform spacing. The points
\pm 2
are far from zero, creating large Vandermonde entries in higher-order columns.

Our discovered points

cluster closer to 1 than integers do:

•

\nicefrac{{5}}{{6}}\approx 0.833
vs. integer 2

•

\nicefrac{{7}}{{6}}\approx 1.167
vs. integer 2

This clustering keeps all points within approximately
[-1.2,1.2]
, dramatically reducing Vandermonde entry magnitudes and improving conditioning.

The optimal configurations discovered by ES follow a consistent pattern:

•

Include
\{0,\infty\}
(required structural points)

•

Cluster finite points closer together than integers

•

Achieve points like
\nicefrac{{5}}{{6}}
,
\nicefrac{{7}}{{6}}
,
\nicefrac{{3}}{{5}}
that are outside typical vocabularies

A.6
Search Space Structure

Fact A.5

(Infinity Point Requirement
(
21
)
)
.

Every valid Winograd F(
m
,
r
) configuration achieving the minimal
n=m+r-1
multiplications requires the point at infinity. This is a standard result from Cook-Toom theory.

Proof.

The product polynomial
y(t)=x(t)\cdot g(t)
has degree
m+r-2
. Evaluating at
n-1
finite points provides
n-1
values, but we need
n
values for unique interpolation. The infinity point provides the
n

[t^{m+r-2}]y(t)=[t^{m-1}]x(t)\cdot[t^{r-1}]g(t)
.

Without infinity, we would need
n
finite points, which would not achieve the minimal multiplication count.
∎

This justifies our search space formulation: search
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
for finite points, always append infinity.

A.7
Quantized Winograd Error Bound

Proposition A.6

(Quantized Winograd Error)
.

For Winograd convolution
\bm{y}=\bm{A}^{T}[(\bm{G}\bm{g})\odot(\bm{B}^{T}\bm{x})]
with quantization error
\epsilon_{q}
(e.g.,
\epsilon_{q}\approx 2^{-7}
for INT8), the output error satisfies:

\frac{\|\bm{y}_{\text{quant}}-\bm{y}_{\text{exact}}\|}{\|\bm{y}_{\text{exact}}\|}\leq\kappa(\bm{V})\cdot\epsilon_{q}+O(\epsilon_{q}^{2})

(12)

where
\kappa(\bm{V})
is the condition number of the Vandermonde matrix from which
\bm{A}
,
\bm{B}
,
\bm{G}
are derived.

Proof sketch.

The Winograd computation involves three linear transforms and one element-wise product. By standard perturbation analysis
(
10
)
, each transform amplifies errors by at most its condition number. Since
\bm{A}
,
\bm{B}
,
\bm{G}
are derived from the same Vandermonde matrix
\bm{V}
via Lagrange interpolation:

•

\kappa(\bm{A})\leq C_{A}\cdot\kappa(\bm{V})
for some constant
C_{A}

•

\kappa(\bm{B})\leq C_{B}\cdot\kappa(\bm{V})
for some constant
C_{B}

•

\kappa(\bm{G})\leq C_{G}\cdot\kappa(\bm{V})
for some constant
C_{G}

The total error bound involves
\kappa(\bm{A})\cdot\kappa(\bm{B})\cdot\kappa(\bm{G})
, but since these are correlated (derived from same points), the dominant term scales as
\kappa(\bm{V})\cdot\epsilon_{q}
. Our empirical results (Table
20
) confirm this scaling: error improvements track
\sqrt{\kappa}
improvements.
∎

Implication.

This proposition provides the theoretical justification for optimizing
\kappa(\bm{V})
: reducing
\kappa(\bm{V})
directly reduces the quantization error bound. For CNN deployment with
L
Winograd layers, errors compound:
(1+\kappa\cdot\epsilon_{q})^{L}
, making
\kappa
reduction critical.

Appendix B
Full Method Details

We present an open discovery framework for Winograd interpolation points that operates in continuous space without relying on any predefined vocabulary. The method consists of three stages: (1) Evolution Strategy search in
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
, (2) snap-to-rational conversion, and (3) symbolic verification.

B.1
Search Space Formulation

The key insight enabling open discovery is recognizing the structure of valid Winograd configurations:

Fact B.1

(Infinity Point Requirement
(
21
)
)
.

Every valid Winograd F(
m
,
r
) configuration requires the point at infinity to achieve the minimal multiplication count
n=m+r-1
. This is a standard result from Cook-Toom theory.

This reduces our search space from choosing
n
arbitrary points to choosing
n-1

finite
points:

Definition B.2

(Open Search Space)
.

For Winograd F(
m
,
r
), the search space is:

\mathcal{S}=\mathbb{R}^{n-1},\quad n=m+r-1

(13)

Each vector
\bm{p}=(p_{0},p_{1},\ldots,p_{n-2})\in\mathcal{S}
specifies
n-1
finite interpolation points. The complete configuration is
\{p_{0},p_{1},\ldots,p_{n-2},\infty\}
.

This formulation differs fundamentally from vocabulary-based methods: we search the
entire continuous space
, not a predefined discrete set.

B.2
Evolution Strategy

We use Evolution Strategy (ES) to search
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
for well-conditioned point configurations.

Fitness Function.

Our fitness function balances reconstruction accuracy, numerical conditioning, and point magnitude:

\displaystyle f(\bm{p})

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\|\mathcal{T}-\mathcal{T}_{\text{rec}}(\bm{p})\|_{F}}_{\text{tensor error}}+\lambda_{1}\underbrace{\frac{\|\bm{p}\|^{2}}{n-1}}_{\text{magnitude}}

\displaystyle\quad+\lambda_{2}\underbrace{\log_{10}(\kappa(\bm{V})+1)}_{\text{conditioning}}

(14)

where
\mathcal{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times r\times n}
is the ground-truth convolution tensor with
\mathcal{T}_{ijk}=\mathbf{1}[i+k=j]
, and
\mathcal{T}_{\text{rec}}(\bm{p})
is the CP decomposition from Cook-Toom matrices. We use
\lambda_{1}=0.001
and
\lambda_{2}=0.0001
. The magnitude term prevents drift toward large points, and the conditioning term biases toward well-conditioned Vandermonde matrices.

Role of Reconstruction Error in ES.

A natural question is why include reconstruction error when symbolic verification later enforces exactness. The answer is that ES operates in
continuous
space where floating-point evaluation provides gradient signal. The reconstruction error term guides ES toward regions where snap-to-rational is likely to succeed—configurations with near-zero floating-point error tend to have nearby exact rational solutions. Empirically, removing this term causes ES to converge more slowly and occasionally to spurious local minima. The symbolic verification stage acts as a filter, not a fitness component.

Hardware Implementation Note.

A reviewer concern is whether larger denominators (e.g.,

\nicefrac{{5}}{{6}}

Rational Coefficients in Practice.

In our INT8/FP16 experiments, transform matrices

,
\bm{B}

\bm{B}
,

by the LCM of denominators (e.g., 6 for our F(4,3) points), perform integer arithmetic, then rescale—but this is not required for our FP32-transform setup and would increase dynamic range. Our dtype-aware dyadic points (Section

For faster discovery with good conditioning, we also search symmetric configurations:

B.6
2D Extension via Kronecker Products

For 2D convolution F(
m\times m
,
r\times r
), transformation matrices are Kronecker products:

\displaystyle\bm{G}_{2D}

\displaystyle=\bm{G}\otimes\bm{G}

(17)

\displaystyle\bm{B}_{2D}^{T}

\displaystyle=\bm{B}^{T}\otimes\bm{B}^{T}

(18)

\displaystyle\bm{A}_{2D}

\displaystyle=\bm{A}\otimes\bm{A}

(19)

Fact B.5

(Kronecker Condition Number
(
10
)
)
.

Under the spectral norm, singular values of Kronecker products multiply:
\sigma_{i}(\bm{A}\otimes\bm{A})=\sigma_{j}(\bm{A})\sigma_{k}(\bm{A})
, yielding
\kappa_{2}(\bm{A}\otimes\bm{A})=\kappa_{2}(\bm{A})^{2}
.

This means 1D conditioning improvements
square
in 2D: a 2.9
\times
1D improvement (F(4,3)) becomes
2.9^{2}\approx 8.5\times
in 2D, and a 27
\times
1D improvement (F(6,3)) becomes
27^{2}\approx 733\times
in 2D. This amplification makes optimal point selection critical for practical 2D convolutions.

Error Cancellation Analysis.

A natural concern is whether the
\kappa^{2}
bound is overly conservative—errors in
\bm{A}
,
\bm{B}
, and
\bm{G}
might partially cancel rather than compound. We address this in two ways:

Theoretical
: The Winograd computation
\bm{y}=\bm{A}[(\bm{G}\bm{g})\odot(\bm{B}^{T}\bm{x})]\bm{A}^{T}
chains three transform stages. Error analysis
(
10
)
shows that for independent rounding errors, the total error scales as
\kappa(\bm{A})\cdot\kappa(\bm{G})\cdot\kappa(\bm{B})
, not merely their sum. Since all transforms derive from the same interpolation points, their condition numbers are correlated, making cancellation unlikely.

Empirical
: Our INT8 experiments (Table
20
, Section
D.6
) validate that measured error improvements track
\sqrt{\kappa}
improvements: F(4,3) achieves
3.4\times
error reduction vs.
\sqrt{2.9^{2}}\approx 2.9\times
predicted; F(6,3) achieves
322\times
vs. the
\sqrt{733}\approx 27\times
lower bound (limited by standard saturation). The CNN-level validation (Table
29
) shows
96\times
error reduction across 14 Winograd layers in ResNet-18, confirming that conditioning improvements compound rather than cancel. These measurements support the
\kappa^{2}
model as predictive, not merely worst-case.

B.7
Dtype-Aware Discovery

For deployment in low-precision environments (float16, int8), we extend ES with dtype-awareness:

Representability Constraint.

Float16 (IEEE 754 binary16) exactly represents
dyadic rationals
—rationals whose denominators are powers of 2. Specifically, a value
v=m\cdot 2^{e}
is exactly representable if the mantissa
m
requires at most 11 significant bits and the exponent
e
lies in the range
[-24,15]
(accounting for subnormals). For interpolation points in the range
|p|\leq 2
, this includes values like
0,\pm\frac{1}{2},\pm\frac{3}{4},\pm 1,\pm\frac{5}{4},\pm\frac{3}{2}
, but excludes non-dyadic fractions like
\frac{5}{6}
and
\frac{2}{3}
which incur representation error. We add a representability penalty to the fitness function:

\displaystyle f_{\text{dtype}}(\bm{p})

\displaystyle=f(\bm{p})+\lambda_{\text{repr}}\cdot R(\bm{p})

\displaystyle R(\bm{p})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=0}^{n-2}d(p_{i},\text{nearest repr.})

(20)

Biased Mutation.

The dtype-aware ES biases 30% of mutations toward exactly representable values, improving convergence to hardware-friendly configurations.

Trade-off Observation.

We identify a nuanced trade-off: discovered fractional points achieve better conditioning but may have worse float16 representation error than exactly-representable integers. Standard points
\{0,\pm 1,\pm 2\}
are exactly representable, while
\nicefrac{{5}}{{6}}
incurs quantization error. This motivates task-specific discovery: optimize for conditioning when using float32, or for representability when deploying in float16.

=0.072
(7.2% relative error—within acceptable range for INT8 inference).

Interpreting Error Values.

An output error of 6.89 means the network outputs differ from the reference by 689% on average—equivalent to random noise. An error of 0.072 (7.2%) is consistent with typical INT8 quantization overhead and indicates the network produces meaningful outputs. Prior work
(
15
)
shows that output errors below 10% typically preserve classification accuracy within 1-2% of FP32 baseline.

Why Standard INT8 Fails.

The error bound for quantized Winograd is
\|\bm{y}_{INT8}-\bm{y}_{exact}\|\leq\kappa(\bm{V})\cdot\epsilon_{quant}\cdot\|\bm{y}_{exact}\|
, where
\epsilon_{quant}\approx 2^{-7}
for INT8. With standard
\kappa=42.5
for F(4,3), a single layer accumulates
\approx 0.33
relative error. After 14 Winograd layers in ResNet-18 (stride-1 convolutions only), errors compound multiplicatively:
(1+0.33)^{14}\approx 75
, explaining the severe final error. Discovered points with
\kappa=14.5
reduce per-layer error to
\approx 0.11
, giving
(1+0.11)^{14}\approx 4.3
—still compounded, but bounded.

Validation of Tile-Level Predictions.

Our tile-level INT8 validation (Table
20
) predicted 3.4
\times
error improvement for F(4,3). The CNN-level improvement of 96
\times
exceeds this because: (1) errors compound across 14 Winograd layers (stride-1 convolutions), and (2) standard points at CNN scale push numerical precision beyond recoverable limits (error
>1
). This demonstrates that tile-level conditioning improvements
underestimate
CNN-level benefits.

Practical Implication.

Standard Winograd F(4,3) at INT8 is
unusable
for CNN inference—the 689% output error would produce random classifications. Our discovered points make INT8 Winograd viable, with 7.2% error that preserves network functionality. This demonstrates that better-conditioned Winograd transforms enable practical INT8 deployment within standard pipelines.

Mathematical Relationship.

The Legendre evaluation matrix
\bm{L}
and Vandermonde matrix
\bm{V}
are related by
\bm{L}=\bm{V}\bm{P}
, where
\bm{P}
is the Legendre-to-monomial coefficient matrix. For drop-in inference with monomial I/O:

\text{Effective transform}=\bm{L}\bm{P}^{-1}=\bm{V}\bm{P}\bm{P}^{-1}=\bm{V}

The Legendre basis cancels out when inputs/outputs remain in monomial form.

Key Finding.

Table
37
shows conditioning for both bases. Crucially, the
effective
conditioning for pretrained inference equals
\kappa(\bm{V})
regardless of whether Legendre basis is used internally. The Legendre conditioning
\kappa(\bm{L})
only applies when weights are stored in Legendre coefficient form throughout training.

For pretrained model inference (our primary use case), our discovered points provide a drop-in improvement without requiring calibration or learned parameters:
