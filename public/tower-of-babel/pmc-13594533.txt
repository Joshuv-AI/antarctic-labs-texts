Association between coronary vasomotor dysfunction and autoimmune disease in patients undergoing invasive coronary function testing
Authors: Nida Latif, Yukang Zeng, Spyridon Kostantinis, Margaret Furman, Attila Feher, Monique Hinchcliff, Natasha Cigarroa, Guangyu Tong, Steffne J. Kunnirickal, Natalija Odanovic, Samit M. Shah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAutoimmune diseases (AD) are associated with increased cardiovascular risk, but their relationship with coronary vasomotor dysfunction (CVDys) in patients with angina with nonobstructive coronary arteries (ANOCA) remains unclear.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 242 adults with ANOCA who underwent invasive coronary function testing. Patients were categorized by AD status, with systemic and organ-specific AD also evaluated separately. CVDys endotypes, including coronary microvascular dysfunction (CMD), vasospastic angina (VSA), and endothelial dysfunction, were compared using adjusted risk ratios. Clinical outcomes were assessed with time-to-event analyses.ResultsOf 242 patients (74.8% female), 91 (37.6%) had AD. CVDys was common in both patients with and without AD (81.3% vs 80.8%; p = 0.71). Among patients with AD, VSA and endothelial dysfunction were the most prevalent endotypes (28.6% each). Organ-specific AD showed numerically higher risks of CMD (adjusted RR, 1.47; 95% CI, 0.94–2.30; p = 0.093) and endothelial dysfunction (adjusted RR, 1.58; 95% CI, 0.98–2.55; p = 0.062), although neither association was statistically significant. Adjusted event-free survival did not differ among the normal-physiology, no AD + CVDys, and AD + CVDys groups (p = 0.155). Among patients with CVDys, AD was not associated with chest-pain hospitalization (HR, 0.65; 95% CI, 0.26–1.63; p = 0.356).ConclusionsAutoimmune disease was not associated with higher CVDys prevalence or adverse cardiovascular outcomes in patients with ANOCA undergoing invasive CFT. Invasive testing identified diverse CVDys endotypes among patients with AD, supporting the utility of comprehensive phenotyping in this population.

Introduction

Autoimmune disease (AD) is associated with increased cardiovascular morbidity and mortality, independent of traditional cardiometabolic risk factors [1]. This may be due to immune dysregulation resulting in chronic inflammation and immune-mediated vascular injury, as well as treatment-related effects of medications such as glucocorticoids and JAK inhibitors [2], [3]. AD is associated with atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD); however, a recent Danish registry study found that despite this association, AD was not linked to an increased risk of obstructive coronary artery disease (CAD) [4]. These observations suggest that pathways beyond flow-limiting atherosclerosis may contribute to ischemic heart disease and cardiovascular symptoms in AD. An emerging body of evidence supports inflammation as a potential mechanistic link between autoimmunity and coronary vasomotor dysfunction (CVDys), which can present clinically as angina with nonobstructive coronary arteries (ANOCA) [5], [6]. ANOCA is often used interchangeably with ischemia with nonobstructive coronary arteries (INOCA), although definitions may vary slightly across studies [7]. ANOCA encompasses coronary vasomotor disorders, including CMD, endothelial dysfunction, and microvascular and epicardial coronary vasospasm, as well as other nonobstructive causes of angina such as symptomatic myocardial bridging [8]. Similar to AD, ANOCA is more prevalent in women than men [9] and is associated with persistent cardiovascular symptoms, decreased quality of life, frequent hospital admissions, and an increased risk of major adverse cardiovascular events [8], [10].

From a pathophysiologic standpoint, AD may be associated with ANOCA due to immune-mediated vascular abnormalities. Chronic immune activation can precipitate endothelial dysfunction, microvascular remodeling, and accelerated atherosclerosis [11]. Additionally, elevated levels of inflammatory biomarkers are associated with impaired myocardial flow reserve detected on cardiac imaging, and immune-modulating therapies may improve coronary vascular function [5], [12].

Prior studies using noninvasive cardiovascular imaging have primarily focused on specific autoimmune diseases and measures of myocardial or coronary flow reserve, without detailed characterization of the underlying ANOCA endotype. As a result, the relationship between autoimmunity and specific ANOCA endotypes remains unclear [13], [14]. To address this knowledge gap, we investigated patients with ANOCA undergoing invasive CFT to characterize CVDys endotypes in patients with AD and determine whether endotype distribution and long-term outcomes differed according to AD status.

Methods

The deidentified data, analytic methods, and study materials that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Study design and population: A retrospective cohort analysis was performed of participants who underwent clinically indicated invasive coronary angiography and CFT at a tertiary academic medical center between October 2017 and July 2025. Eligible patients were ≥ 18 years old and referred for evaluation of suspected ischemic heart disease. We excluded patients with ≥50% diameter stenosis in any epicardial coronary artery. This study was approved by the institutional review board and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective design and minimal-risk nature of the study.

Clinical variables: Clinical variables, including demographic data, medical comorbidities, medication use, coronary physiological indices, and post-procedure diagnoses, were obtained via medical record review. Baseline heart failure was defined as a documented clinical diagnosis of heart failure before the index procedure. Lung disease was defined as a documented diagnosis of chronic obstructive or restrictive pulmonary disease, including chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, emphysema, asthma, or chronic interstitial lung disease. Autoimmune diseases were identified by review of documented diagnoses in the medical record. Subjects were categorized based on AD status and were further stratified into systemic AD and organ-specific AD based on underlying diagnosis and affected organ system(s); qualifying diagnoses are listed in Table S1.

Coronary function testing: Invasive coronary function testing was performed according to a previously published protocol [15], which involved invasive coronary angiography, followed by coronary vasoreactivity testing with intracoronary acetylcholine and bolus thermodilution using PressureWire X (Abbott Vascular, Santa Clara, CA). Detailed procedural information is provided in the Supplemental Methods. Clinical outcomes: The primary outcome was the composite of all-cause death, non-fatal myocardial infarction, heart-failure hospitalization, or any other unplanned hospitalization requiring >24 h of inpatient care for a cardiovascular event. Other unplanned cardiovascular hospitalizations were predefined to include clinically significant arrhythmia, conduction disorders requiring urgent intervention, and acute arterial thromboembolic events. Follow-up data were obtained through chart review of outpatient encounters, inpatient hospitalizations, and patient messages in the electronic medical record through July 31, 2025. For time-to-event analyses, patients with isolated myocardial bridging in the absence of CVDys were excluded because myocardial bridging represents a distinct anatomic coronary abnormality and may influence clinical outcomes independently. Patients with myocardial bridging and concomitant CVDys were retained. We evaluated the time interval between the index procedure (CFT) and the first qualifying event. Participants without an event were censored at 4 years, at the last clinical contact, or July 31, 2025, whichever came first. Kaplan–Meier curves were displayed through 4 years because of sparse events beyond that time point. The secondary outcome was time to first chest-pain hospitalization. For this analysis, follow-up was censored at 4 years after the index procedure; participants were otherwise censored at last contact or July 31, 2025. Nonfatal cardiovascular events other than chest-pain hospitalization were not treated as censoring events, so patients remained under follow-up for subsequent chest-pain hospitalizations, reflecting ongoing symptom burden. For time-to-event analyses, patients were stratified into three mutually exclusive groups: (1) normal coronary physiology (absence of CVDys); (2) AD + CVDys; and (3) no AD + CVDys.

Statistical analysis: No prospective sample-size calculation was performed because this was a retrospective analysis of all eligible patients in the available cohort. Continuous variables were summarized using mean and standard deviation (SD) or median and interquartile range [IQR] and counts with percentages for categorical variables. Differences between continuous variables were evaluated with the independent samples t-test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test, whereas categorical variables were evaluated with the chi-square test or Fisher's exact test. For binary outcomes including the presence of specific post-procedure diagnoses, relative risks (RRs) and 95% confidence intervals were calculated using log-binomial regression models. When convergence issues occurred, modified Poisson regression with robust variance estimation (Zou method) was utilized as an alternative approach. Adjusted relative risks incorporated age and sex as covariates. Event-free survival and time to first chest-pain hospitalization were analyzed using Kaplan–Meier methods with group comparisons performed using the log-rank test. Median follow-up time was estimated using the reverse Kaplan–Meier method. Cox proportional-hazards regression models were employed to calculate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals for each patient group compared to the reference group (normal coronary physiology), with adjustment for age and sex. The overall effect of the three-level group factor was assessed using a likelihood ratio test with 2 degrees of freedom. Given the limited sample size, adjustment for the primary AD-status and clinical outcome analyses was limited to age and sex to avoid overfitting; subtype analyses additionally adjusted for the alternate autoimmune subtype. For the chest-pain endpoint, Cox models were restricted to patients with CVDys because no chest-pain hospitalizations occurred in the normal-physiology group, precluding estimation of a hazard ratio versus normal physiology. Outcome analyses were not performed separately for systemic and organ-specific AD because of the small and overlapping subtype groups and the limited number of outcome events, which would have resulted in unstable effect estimates. In an exploratory supplementary analysis, event-free survival was also evaluated according to CFR status (≤2.5 vs >2.5) using Kaplan–Meier and age- and sex-adjusted Cox analyses. The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals. Statistical significance was set at a two-sided p-value <0.05. Descriptive analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 27 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY), and R version 4.3 with the survival and survminer packages for regression models and time-to-event analyses.

Results

Clinical characteristics: 242 patients were included (Fig. 1), of whom 91 (37.6%) had AD and 151 (62.4%) did not have AD. The mean age was 57.8 (SD 11) years, and 181 (74.8%) were female. The proportion of females was higher in the AD group (83.5% vs. 69.5%; p = 0.02). Lung disease was more prevalent in AD (46.2% vs 24.5%; p < 0.001), while the prevalence of other comorbid conditions was similar between groups (Table 2). Among patients with AD, the median duration of AD was 9.5 [6–16] years, and 29.7% of AD patients had Raynaud phenomenon (Table S1). Among the 91 patients with AD, 59 (64.8%) had at least one systemic AD and 50 (54.9%) had at least one organ-specific AD; these categories were not mutually exclusive, and 18 patients had diagnoses in both categories. The most prevalent systemic ADs were systemic lupus erythematosus (n = 13), rheumatoid arthritis (n = 9), Sjögren syndrome (n = 9), and scleroderma (n = 9). Among organ-specific ADs, the most common diagnoses were autoimmune thyroid disease (n = 26), followed by psoriasis (n = 11), and inflammatory bowel disease (n = 7) (Table S1). Invasive physiology and post-procedure diagnosis: There were no differences in median CFR, IMR, and mean FFR between patients with AD and those without AD, and the proportion with CFR ≤2.5 was similar (26.4% AD vs 31.9% no AD; p = 0.50) (Table 2). The prevalence of CVDys (composite of CMD, vasospasm, or endothelial dysfunction) was high and did not differ by AD status (81.3% AD vs 80.8% no AD; adjusted RR 1.02 (0.90–1.16); p = 0.71) (Table 3). Among patients with AD, the most frequent endotypes were vasospastic angina (VSA) and endothelial dysfunction (28.6% each); VSA also predominated in the no AD group (33.1%) (Fig. 2). Clinical outcomes: Among 232 patients included in the survival analysis, 17 cardiovascular events occurred over a median follow-up of 2.45 years [IQR 1.65–3.72]. The median follow-up duration was shorter in patients with AD compared to those without AD (2.03 years [IQR 0.92–2.74] vs. 2.76 years [IQR 1.91–3.91]). The proportions experiencing events were 9.1% and 6.2%, respectively. The most frequent event was heart-failure hospitalization (n = 5) followed by unplanned cardiovascular hospitalization (n = 4; arrhythmia n = 3, arterial thromboembolism n = 1), hospitalization for nonfatal myocardial infarction (n = 4), and death (n = 4). For the primary composite endpoint, there was no significant difference in event-free survival among the three groups. In the age- and sex-adjusted model, hazard ratios versus normal physiology were 2.48 (95% CI 0.31–19.8) for no AD + CVDys and 5.17 (95% CI 0.63–42.59) for AD + CVDys (p = 0.155) (Fig. S1). For the secondary outcome, chest-pain hospitalization occurred in 26 patients (11.2%); all had CVDys, including 20 without AD and 6 with AD. No patients with normal coronary physiology were hospitalized for chest pain (Table S4). Freedom from chest-pain hospitalization differed among the three groups (log-rank p = 0.026), with all chest-pain hospitalizations occurring in patients with CVDys. Because no chest-pain hospitalizations occurred in the normal-physiology group, the adjusted Cox models were restricted to patients with CVDys. In this subgroup, autoimmune status was not associated with chest-pain hospitalization (HR 0.65, 95% CI 0.26–1.63 for AD + CVDys vs no AD + CVDys; p = 0.356) (Fig. S2). In an additional supplementary analysis, patients with CFR ≤2.5 had worse unadjusted event-free survival than those with CFR >2.5 (log-rank p = 0.047), although this association was attenuated after adjustment for age and sex (p = 0.085) (Fig. S3).

Discussion

In this study of patients with ANOCA undergoing invasive CFT, CVDys was highly prevalent regardless of autoimmune disease status, occurring in approximately 81% of patients in both groups. We observed no statistically significant differences in invasive physiological indices, overall CVDys prevalence, individual vasomotor endotypes, or adverse cardiovascular outcomes according to AD status. Among patients with AD, VSA and endothelial dysfunction were each at least as common as CMD. These findings suggest that coronary vasomotor abnormalities in patients with AD and ANOCA are heterogeneous and may not be fully captured by assessment of coronary microvascular function alone.

Prior studies, predominantly using noninvasive cardiovascular imaging, have reported impaired coronary microvascular function and endothelial dysfunction in patients with autoimmune diseases [11], [13], [16], [17], [18], [19], [20]. However, most investigations have relied on noninvasive measures of myocardial or coronary flow reserve and therefore primarily assessed CMD. In contrast, invasive CFT enables evaluation of acetylcholine-provoked vasospasm and endothelial dysfunction in addition to microvascular function. Although overall CVDys prevalence was similar in patients with and without AD, VSA and endothelial dysfunction were prominent among patients with AD. Thus, studies focused primarily on coronary flow reserve may not capture the full spectrum of coronary vasomotor abnormalities in this population.

Several methodological and population differences may explain the discrepancy between prior reports and our findings. Some earlier studies included asymptomatic cohorts and patients who were not specifically evaluated for ANOCA [18], [19], [21]. In contrast, our cohort consisted of symptomatic patients referred for invasive evaluation, such that both autoimmune and non-autoimmune groups likely had a high pretest probability of coronary vasomotor dysfunction. This may have reduced observable between-group differences. Autoimmune diseases are also biologically heterogeneous [22]. Within our sample, organ-specific AD showed numerically higher estimated risks of endothelial dysfunction and CMD, whereas systemic autoimmune disease showed a numerically lower estimated risk of CMD. Although these associations did not reach statistical significance, they raise the possibility that organ-specific autoimmune diseases may have vascular effects beyond the primary target organ, including the coronary microvascular bed [17], [20]. Differences in disease duration, severity, inflammatory activity, and treatment with immunomodulatory agents may also influence coronary physiology [14], [19], [23]. We did not have standardized measures of disease activity or inflammatory burden, which may have contributed to the lack of observed differences in CFR and coronary vasoreactivity. Our invasive findings are consistent with the WISE-CVD substudy, which similarly found no significant differences in invasive coronary function parameters between women with and without autoimmune rheumatic disease, despite lower myocardial perfusion reserve index in the autoimmune rheumatic disease group [24].

We also did not observe a significant association between autoimmune status and adverse cardiovascular outcomes. The overall event rate was low, and follow-up duration was shorter in patients with AD, limiting power to detect differences between groups. In supplementary analyses, patients with CFR ≤2.5 had worse unadjusted event-free survival, although this association was attenuated after adjustment for age and sex, which are independent predictors of CFR [25]. All chest-pain hospitalizations occurred among patients with CVDys; within this group, however, autoimmune status was not associated with chest-pain hospitalization after adjustment for age and sex. In contrast, prior noninvasive imaging studies have reported higher risks of adverse cardiovascular events and mortality among patients with AD and coronary microvascular dysfunction, and impaired CFR has been identified as a prognostic marker [26], [27], [28].

These findings have practical implications for the evaluation of patients with AD and angina. In symptomatic patients with AD and nonobstructive coronary arteries, invasive CFT can distinguish CMD, VSA, and endothelial dysfunction and may inform endotype-specific therapy. The CorMicA trial demonstrated that CFT-guided stratified medical therapy improves angina and quality of life in patients with ANOCA [29]. A real-world study similarly showed that CFT increases diagnostic yield and is associated with greater intensification of antianginal therapy compared with angiography alone [30]. Our findings support a comprehensive approach to invasive phenotyping in patients with AD and ANOCA.

Study limitations

As a single-center, retrospective study of a referred cohort undergoing invasive coronary function testing, our findings are subject to selection and referral bias and may not be generalizable to broader populations with AD. The proportion of patients with AD in this selected referral cohort should not be interpreted as an estimate of AD prevalence among patients with ANOCA in the general population. Although patients without AD served as an internal comparison group, all participants were symptomatic patients referred for invasive coronary function testing. The absence of an asymptomatic control group limits our ability to determine whether the prevalence of CVDys in patients with AD differs from that in the broader population. The modest sample size and limited number of outcome events reduced statistical power for subtype and outcome analyses. AD status was based on documented clinical diagnoses, and subtype analyses included heterogeneous autoimmune conditions. Disease activity and inflammatory biomarkers were not consistently available, and residual confounding by clinical characteristics and medication exposure cannot be excluded. Follow-up duration was shorter in patients with AD, which may have affected outcome estimates. Finally, post-procedural treatment modifications were not captured, precluding assessment of whether endotype-guided therapy influenced subsequent symptoms or clinical outcomes.

Conclusion

In patients with ANOCA undergoing invasive CFT, CVDys was common regardless of AD status, with no significant differences in specific vasomotor endotypes or adverse cardiovascular outcomes. Invasive CFT nevertheless identified diverse vasomotor abnormalities among patients with AD. Larger studies are needed to determine whether specific autoimmune subtypes are associated with distinct CVDys endotypes and long-term cardiovascular risk.
