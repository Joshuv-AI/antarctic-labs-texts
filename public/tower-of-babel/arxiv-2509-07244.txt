On necessary conditions of rational-infinite divisibility for distributions with non-zero discrete parts
A. A. Khartov

Abstract
We consider the new class 𝑸 \boldsymbol{Q} of rational-infinitely (or quasi-infinitely) divisible distribution functions on the real line. By definition, F ∈ 𝑸 F\in\boldsymbol{Q} if there are some infinitely divisible distribution functions F 1 F_{1} and F 2 F_{2} such that F 1 = F ∗ F 2 F_{1}=F*F_{2} , where “ ∗ * ” is the convolution. The characteristic function of such F F admits the Lévy–Khintchine-type representation with a “signed spectral measure”. The class 𝑸 \boldsymbol{Q} is a significant extension of the family of infinitely divisible distribution functions and it have already found some applications in several areas. So there is an active interest in this class. In particular, a lot of results have recently appeared on the problem of belonging to the class 𝑸 \boldsymbol{Q} in terms of characteristic functions. In the paper, we continue this series of results by proposing two necessary conditions for distribution functions from 𝑸 \boldsymbol{Q} with non-zero discrete parts. Namely, the characteristic functions of such a distribution function and its discrete part are always separated from zero.

1
Introduction

Let
F
be a distribution function on the real line
\mathbb{R}
. Let
f
be the characteristic function of
F
, i.e.

\displaystyle f(t):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}\,dF(x),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

We call a distribution function
F
(and the corresponding probability law)
rational-infinitely divisible
if there are some infinitely divisible distribution functions
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
on
\mathbb{R}
such that
F_{1}=F*F_{2}
, where “
*
” denotes the convolution operation. This equality may be written in the form
f(t)=f_{1}(t)/f_{2}(t)
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
, where
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
denote the characteristic functions of
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
, respectively. Let us denote by
\boldsymbol{Q}
the class of all rational-infinitely divisible distribution functions. It is clear that
\boldsymbol{Q}
is an extension of the class
\boldsymbol{I}
of all infinitely divisible distribution functions. There are some examples of distribution functions from
\boldsymbol{Q}\setminus\boldsymbol{I}
in the classical monographs
[
10
]
(p. 81–83) and
[
21
]
(p. 165).

The classes
\boldsymbol{I}
and
\boldsymbol{Q}
have very similar properties. So it is seen from the definition that the characteristic function
f
of any
F\in\boldsymbol{Q}
doesn’t have zeroes on
\mathbb{R}
and it admits
the Lévy–Khinchine-type representation
:

\displaystyle f(t)=\exp\biggl\{it\gamma+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Bigl(e^{itx}-1-it\sin(x)\Bigr)\dfrac{1+x^{2}}{x^{2}}\,dG(x)\biggr\},\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

(1)

with the
shift parameter

\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
, and the
spectral function

G:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
of bounded total variation on
\mathbb{R}
. The function
G
is assumed to be right-continuous at every point of the real line with the condition
G(-\infty)=0
. Note that
G
is non-monototic in general. Nevertheless, the
spectral pair
(\gamma,G)
is uniquely determined by
f
and hence by
F
as for the class
\boldsymbol{I}
(see
[
10
]
, p. 80). Next, let
f
be a characteristic function represented by formula (
1
) with some
\gamma
and
G
satisfying the above-mentioned conditions. Following Lindner and Sato
[
20
]
, the corresponding distribution function
F
(and the corresponding probability law) is called
quasi-infinitely divisible
. Due to the Hahn–Jordan decomposition for
G
, it is not difficult to show that
F
is rational-infinitely divisible.

The class
\boldsymbol{Q}
is actively studied now. The main reference is the paper
[
19
]
by Lindner, Pan, and Sato, which contains the detailed analysis of this class. Multivariate analog of
\boldsymbol{Q}
is considered in
[
2
]
,
[
5
]
, and
[
6
]
. There are interesting applications in theory of stochastic processes (see
[
20
]
and
[
25
]
), number theory (see
[
23
]
), physics (see
[
7
]
and
[
9
]
), and insurance mathematics (see
[
26
]
). The class
\boldsymbol{Q}
penetrates statistics (see
[
24
]
).

This short note is connected with the problem of belonging to the class
\boldsymbol{Q}
. The general statement of the problem is to obtain the necessary and/or sufficient conditions for which a given distribution function
F
belongs to
\boldsymbol{Q}
. For instance, there is the following simple fact (see
[
8
]
, Theorem 4.3.7): if a probability law has a mass
>1/2
at some point, then its distribution function belongs to
\boldsymbol{Q}
. By now a number of deeper results have accumulated. We will give a brief survey of them now. Let
F
be a distribution function on
\mathbb{R}
. According to the Lebesgue decomposition theorem,
F
admits the representation:

\displaystyle F(x)=c_{d}F_{d}(x)+c_{a}F_{a}(x)+c_{s}F_{s}(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R},

(2)

where
F_{d}
,
F_{a}
, and
F_{s}
are discrete, absolutely continuous and continuous singular distribution functions, respectively. Here the coefficients
c_{a}
,
c_{a}
, and
c_{s}
are non-negative constants such that
c_{d}+c_{a}+c_{s}=1
. The characteristic function
f
of
F
is represented accordingly:

\displaystyle f(t)=c_{d}f_{d}(t)+c_{a}f_{a}(t)+c_{s}f_{s}(t),\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

(3)

where
f_{d}
,
f_{a}
, and
f_{s}
are the characteristic functions corresponding to
F_{d}
,
F_{a}
, and
F_{s}
, respectively. The most of existing results are formulated in terms of characteristic functions. The first rather general result was obtained by Lindner, Pan, and Sato in
[
19
]
(Theorem 8.1, p. 30) for the case
c_{d}=1
and discrete lattice
F=F_{d}
. It states that such
F
belongs to the class
\boldsymbol{Q}
if and only if
f(t)\neq 0
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
. The criterion for arbitrary discrete
F
(exactly the case
c_{d}=1
) was obtained in
[
11
]
(see also
[
1
]
):
F\in\boldsymbol{Q}
if and only if
f
is separeted from zero, i.e.
\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|f(t)|>0
. This criterion generalizes the previous one, because the absolute value
|f(\,\cdot\,)|
is a periodic continuous function on
\mathbb{R}
for lattice
F
and therefore, in the lattice case,
f
is zero-free on the period segment (and hence on
\mathbb{R}
) if any only if it is separated from zero.
By the way, there is a recent result in paper
[
17
]
, where this criterion is formulated in terms of the support points of the discrete distribution and their probabilities (see also
[
16
]
). Next, in the paper
[
4
]
, Berger and Kutlu considered the case
c_{d}>0
,
c_{a}\geqslant 0
, and
c_{s}=0
. They proved that, under these assumptions,
F\in\boldsymbol{Q}
if and only if
1)

f(t)\neq 0
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
and
2)

\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|f_{d}(t)|>0
. Conditions
1)
and
2)
together are equivalent to one condition
3)

\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|f(t)|>0
. We note that this result is a generalization of an earlier criterion by Berger (see
[
3
]
), where
F_{d}
is assumed to be lattice. Next, it is asserted in
[
15
]
that the previous criterion is valid under a more general assumption of the dominated continuous singular part. More precisely, let us assume that
c_{d}>0
,
c_{a}\geqslant 0
,
c_{s}\geqslant 0
, and define
\mu_{d}:=\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|f_{d}(t)|
. We additionally assume that
c_{s}<c_{d}\mu_{d}
if
\mu_{d}>0
and
c_{s}=0
if
\mu_{d}=0
. Then
F\in\boldsymbol{Q}
if and only if conditions
1)
and
2)
hold together or equivalent condition
3)
is satified. It is important to note, however, that there is an interesting example of
F\notin\boldsymbol{Q}
, for which
c_{s}=\mu_{d}c_{d}>0
,
1)
and
2)
are satisfied, but
3)
is not (see
[
15
]
, Example 3). In other words, the pair of
1)
and
2)
is not equivalent to
3)
in general case and both
\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|f_{d}(t)|>0
and
\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|f(t)|>0
are important for belonging to the class
\boldsymbol{Q}
in the case
c_{d}>0
. In addition to those listed, there are several results where other approaches are used (see
[
6
]
,
[
13
]
, and
[
14
]
).

In this note, we propose two results, where we will deal with the case
c_{d}>0
too. We will show that, actually, the conditions
\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|f_{d}(t)|>0
and
\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|f(t)|>0
(without any additional assumptions) are always necessary for belonging
F
to the class
\boldsymbol{Q}
. We hope that these results will make some progress to the most general potential criterion of membership of the class
\boldsymbol{Q}
in the case
c_{d}>0
or they will be some useful tools for further results of other kind in this theme.

2
Results

Let
F
be a (right-continuous) distribution function on
\mathbb{R}
with the characteristic function
f
as before. For further convenience we rewrite formulas (
2
) and (
3
) in the following forms

\displaystyle F(x)=c_{d}F_{d}(x)+(1-c_{d})F_{c}(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R},\quad\text{and}\quad f(t)=c_{d}f_{d}(t)+(1-c_{d})f_{c}(t),\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

(4)

respectively. Here
c_{d}\in[0,1]
,
F_{c}
denotes a continuous distribution function and
f_{c}
denotes its characteristic function. If
c_{d}=1
then
F_{c}
can be chosen arbitrarily. If
c_{d}<1
, i.e.
c_{a}+c_{s}=1-c_{d}>0
, then

\displaystyle F_{c}(x)=\dfrac{c_{a}F_{a}(x)+c_{s}F_{s}(x)}{c_{a}+c_{s}},\quad x\in\mathbb{R},\quad\text{and}\quad f_{c}(t)=\dfrac{c_{a}f_{a}(t)+c_{s}f_{s}(t)}{c_{a}+c_{s}},\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

Note that we don’t assume a priori that
F\in\boldsymbol{Q}
(namely, in Lemma
2
, Proposition
1
and
2
below).

Let us formulate the first main result of the paper.

Theorem 1

If
F\in\boldsymbol{Q}
and
c_{d}>0
, then
\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|f(t)|>0
.

We will need the following simple lemmata to prove this and further assertions.

Lemma 1

Suppose that
F\in\boldsymbol{Q}
. Then there are constants
B\geqslant 0
and
C>0
such that

\displaystyle\dfrac{|f(t-h)f(t+h)|}{|f(t)|^{2}}\leqslant Ce^{Bh^{2}}\quad\text{for any}\quad t\in\mathbb{R},\,\,h\in\mathbb{R}.

Proof of Lemma
1
.
Since
F\in\boldsymbol{Q}
, the function
f
admits representation (
1
) with some
\gamma
and
G
. Using this formula, it is not difficult to show that

\displaystyle\dfrac{f(t-h)f(t+h)}{f(t)^{2}}=\exp\biggl\{-\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}\bigl(1-\cos(hx)\bigr)\dfrac{1+x^{2}}{x^{2}}\,dG(x)\biggr\}

for any real
t
and
h
(see
[
12
]
, p. 359, or
[
14
]
, p. 4, for more details).

\displaystyle\dfrac{|f(t-h)f(t+h)|}{|f(t)|^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\biggl\{-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\cos(tx)\bigl(1-\cos(hx)\bigr)\dfrac{1+x^{2}}{x^{2}}\,dG(x)\biggr\}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\exp\biggl\{\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigl(1-\cos(hx)\bigr)\dfrac{1+x^{2}}{x^{2}}\,d|G|(x)\biggr\},

where
|G|(x)
denotes the total variation of
G
on
(-\infty,x]
for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
. It is known that
(1-\cos(hx))\tfrac{1+x^{2}}{x^{2}}\leqslant\tfrac{h^{2}}{2}+2
for any real
x
and
h
(see
[
12
]
, p. 358). Therefore

\displaystyle\dfrac{|f(t-h)f(t+h)|}{|f(t)|^{2}}\leqslant\exp\biggl\{\biggl(\dfrac{h^{2}}{2}+2\biggr)\cdot\|G\|\biggr\}\quad\text{for any}\quad t\in\mathbb{R},\,\,h\in\mathbb{R},

where
\|G\|
denotes the total variation of
G
on
\mathbb{R}
. Defining
B:=\tfrac{1}{2}\,\|G\|\geqslant 0
and
C:=e^{2\|G\|}>0
, we come to the required estimate.
\Box

Lemma 2

It is always valid that

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\biggl\{\dfrac{1}{2T}\int_{-T}^{T}|f_{c}(t+h)|^{2}\,dh\biggr\}\to 0\quad\text{as}\quad T\to\infty.

Proof of Lemma
2
.
By definition,
f_{c}
is the characteristic function for
F_{c}
. Hence the function
t\mapsto|f_{c}(t)|^{2}
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
, is the characteristic function of the symmetrized version of
F_{c}
, say
\widetilde{F}_{c}
(see
[
22
]
, p. 38). Thus we have

\displaystyle|f_{c}(t)|^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}\,d\widetilde{F}_{c}(x),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

Let us consider the following family of integrals:

\displaystyle M(t,T):=\dfrac{1}{2T}\int_{-T}^{T}|f_{c}(t+h)|^{2}\,dh=\dfrac{1}{2T}\int_{-T}^{T}\biggl(\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{i(t+h)x}\,d\widetilde{F}_{c}(x)\biggr)\,dh,\quad t\in\mathbb{R},\,\,T>0.

It is clear that we can write

\displaystyle M(t,T)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}\biggl(\dfrac{1}{2T}\int_{-T}^{T}e^{ihx}\,dh\biggr)\,d\widetilde{F}_{c}(x),\quad t\in\mathbb{R},\,\,T>0.

Then

\displaystyle M(t,T)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\{0\}}\biggl(\dfrac{1}{2T}\int_{-T}^{T}\,dh\biggr)\,d\widetilde{F}_{c}(x)+\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}}e^{itx}\biggl(\dfrac{1}{2T}\int_{-T}^{T}e^{ihx}\,dh\biggr)\,d\widetilde{F}_{c}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\{0\}}\,d\widetilde{F}_{c}(x)+\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}}e^{itx}\,\dfrac{\sin(Tx)}{Tx}\,d\widetilde{F}_{c}(x),\quad t\in\mathbb{R},\,\,T>0.

Here
\int_{\{0\}}\,d\widetilde{F}_{c}(x)=\widetilde{F}_{c}(0)-\widetilde{F}_{c}(0-)=0
, because
\widetilde{F}_{c}
, being the simmetrization of the continuous distribution function
F_{c}
, is continuous too. Consequently, for any
T>0

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|M(t,T)|=\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\biggl|\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}}e^{itx}\,\dfrac{\sin(Tx)}{Tx}\,d\widetilde{F}_{c}(x)\biggr|\leqslant\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}}\biggl|\dfrac{\sin(Tx)}{Tx}\biggr|\,d\widetilde{F}_{c}(x).

Observe that, in the latter integral, the integrand function is bounded by
1
and it vanishes as
T\to\infty
. Therefore this integral vanishes too by Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem. Thus we obtain
\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|M(t,T)|\to 0
as
T\to\infty
as required.
\Box

Now we are ready to prove our first result.

Proof of Theorem
1
.
By assumption,
F\in\boldsymbol{Q}
and hence we know that
f(t)\neq 0
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
(see introduction). In order to prove
\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|f(t)|>0
, we will use the quotient
f(t-h)f(t+h)/f(t)^{2}
with real
t
and
h
. Namely, let us consider the following integral

\displaystyle I(t,\tau):=\dfrac{1}{2\tau}\int_{-\tau}^{\tau}\dfrac{|f(t-h)f(t+h)|}{|f(t)|^{2}}\,\,dh

(5)

for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
and
\tau>0
. According to Lemma
1
, this integral admits the estimate

\displaystyle I(t,\tau)\leqslant\dfrac{1}{2\tau}\int_{-\tau}^{\tau}Ce^{Bh^{2}}\,dh\leqslant Ce^{B\tau^{2}}<\infty

with some constants
B\geqslant 0
and
C>0
. In particular, we see that

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}I(t,\tau)<\infty\quad\text{for any}\quad\tau>0.

(6)

We now assume, contrary to our claim, that
\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|f(t)|=0
. We will show below that for some
\tau>0
it yields
\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}I(t,\tau)=\infty
, which contradicts (
6
). For this purpose we will obtain a lower estimate for the integral

\displaystyle J(t,\tau):=I(t,\tau)\cdot|f(t)|^{2}=\dfrac{1}{2\tau}\int_{-\tau}^{\tau}|f(t-h)f(t+h)|\,\,dh

(7)

for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
and
\tau>0
.

Let us consider the integrand function in (
7
). Using the decomposition for
f
from (
4
), for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
and
h\in\mathbb{R}
we write

\displaystyle f(t-h)f(t+h)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigl(c_{d}f_{d}(t-h)+(1-c_{d})f_{c}(t-h)\bigr)\cdot\bigl(c_{d}f_{d}(t+h)+(1-c_{d})f_{c}(t+h)\bigr)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{d}^{2}f_{d}(t-h)f_{d}(t+h)+(1-c_{d})^{2}f_{c}(t-h)f_{c}(t+h)

\displaystyle+c_{d}(1-c_{d})f_{d}(t+h)f_{c}(t-h)+c_{d}(1-c_{d})f_{d}(t-h)f_{c}(t+h).

So it follows that

\displaystyle|f(t-h)f(t+h)|

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle c_{d}^{2}|f_{d}(t-h)f_{d}(t+h)|-(1-c_{d})^{2}|f_{c}(t-h)f_{c}(t+h)|

\displaystyle-c_{d}(1-c_{d})|f_{d}(t+h)f_{c}(t-h)|-c_{d}(1-c_{d})|f_{d}(t-h)f_{c}(t+h)|

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle c_{d}^{2}|f_{d}(t-h)f_{d}(t+h)|-(1-c_{d})^{2}|f_{c}(t-h)f_{c}(t+h)|

\displaystyle{}-c_{d}(1-c_{d})|f_{c}(t-h)|-c_{d}(1-c_{d})|f_{c}(t+h)|,

where we used the inequality
|f_{d}(s)|\leqslant 1
,
s\in\mathbb{R}
. Then for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
and
\tau>0

\displaystyle J(t,\tau)

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle\dfrac{c_{d}^{2}}{2\tau}\int_{-\tau}^{\tau}|f_{d}(t-h)f_{d}(t+h)|\,\,dh-\dfrac{(1-c_{d})^{2}}{2\tau}\int_{-\tau}^{\tau}|f_{c}(t-h)f_{c}(t+h)|\,\,dh

\displaystyle-\dfrac{c_{d}(1-c_{d})}{2\tau}\int_{-\tau}^{\tau}|f_{c}(t-h)|\,\,dh-\dfrac{c_{d}(1-c_{d})}{2\tau}\int_{-\tau}^{\tau}|f_{c}(t+h)|\,\,dh.

It is easily seen that

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2\tau}\int_{-\tau}^{\tau}|f_{c}(t-h)|\,\,dh=\dfrac{1}{2\tau}\int_{-\tau}^{\tau}|f_{c}(t+h)|\,\,dh.

Due to the inequality
|f_{c}(s)|\leqslant 1
for any
s\in\mathbb{R}
, we get

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2\tau}\int_{-\tau}^{\tau}|f_{c}(t-h)f_{c}(t+h)|\,\,dh\leqslant\dfrac{1}{2\tau}\int_{-\tau}^{\tau}|f_{c}(t+h)|\,\,dh.

Therefore

\displaystyle J(t,\tau)\geqslant\dfrac{c_{d}^{2}}{2\tau}\int_{-\tau}^{\tau}|f_{d}(t-h)f_{d}(t+h)|\,\,dh-\dfrac{(1-c_{d})^{2}}{2\tau}\int_{-\tau}^{\tau}|f_{c}(t+h)|\,\,dh-\dfrac{c_{d}(1-c_{d})}{\tau}\int_{-\tau}^{\tau}|f_{c}(t+h)|\,\,dh.

Thus for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
and
\tau>0
we obtain

\displaystyle J(t,\tau)\geqslant\dfrac{c_{d}^{2}}{2\tau}\int_{-\tau}^{\tau}|f_{d}(t-h)f_{d}(t+h)|\,\,dh-\dfrac{1-c_{d}^{2}}{2\tau}\int_{-\tau}^{\tau}|f_{c}(t+h)|\,\,dh\geqslant c_{d}^{2}J_{d}(t,\tau)-J_{c}(t,\tau),

(8)

where we define

\displaystyle J_{d}(t,\tau):=\dfrac{1}{2\tau}\int_{-\tau}^{\tau}|f_{d}(t-h)f_{d}(t+h)|\,\,dh,\qquad J_{c}(t,\tau):=\dfrac{1}{2\tau}\int_{-\tau}^{\tau}|f_{c}(t+h)|\,\,dh,\quad t\in\mathbb{R},\,\,\tau>0.

Next, according to the assumption
\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|f(t)|=0
, we choose a sequence
(t_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
|f(t_{k})|\to 0
as
k\to\infty
(here and below,
\mathbb{N}
denotes the set of positive integers). Since
f(t)\neq 0
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
and
f
is a continuous function on
\mathbb{R}
, it follows that
|t_{k}|\to\infty
as
k\to\infty
. Without loss of generality,
we can assume that
t_{k}\to\infty
as
k\to\infty
, because
|f(t)|=|f(-t)|
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Next, using the sequence
(t_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, we define the functions
\varphi_{k}(h):=f_{d}(t_{k}+h)
,
h\in\mathbb{R}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Since
f_{d}
is an almost periodic function, the sequence
(\varphi_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is relatively compact in the topology of uniform convergence on
\mathbb{R}
(see
[
18
]
, pp. 23–24). So there exists a subsequence
(\varphi_{k_{l}})_{l\in\mathbb{N}}
that uniformly converges to an almost periodic function
\varphi
, i.e.

\displaystyle\sup_{h\in\mathbb{R}}|f_{d}(t_{k_{l}}+h)-\varphi(h)|\to 0,\quad l\to\infty.

(9)

From this we have

\displaystyle\sup_{h\in\mathbb{R}}|f_{d}(t_{k_{l}}+h)f_{d}(t_{k_{l}}-h)-\varphi(h)\varphi(-h)|\to 0,\quad l\to\infty.

(10)

Here the function
h\mapsto\varphi(h)\varphi(-h)
,
h\in\mathbb{R}
, is an almost periodic (as a product of two almost periodic functions, see
[
18
]
, p. 27) and it is known that there exists
h_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\varphi(h_{0})\varphi(-h_{0})\neq 0
(see
[
11
]
, p. 3, for more details). In addition, by the Parseval identity for almost periodic functions (see
[
11
]
, p. 45), the following limit exists

\displaystyle A:=\lim\limits_{T\to\infty}\dfrac{1}{2T}\int_{-T}^{T}|\varphi(h)\varphi(-h)|^{2}\,dh,

(11)

and
A
is the sum of squared absolute values of all the Fourier coefficients of
\varphi(h)\varphi(-h)
,
h\in\mathbb{R}
. Since this function is not identically zero, we conclude that
A>0
(see
[
11
]
, p. 45).

We now turn to the sequence

\displaystyle J_{d}(t_{k_{l}},\tau)=\dfrac{1}{2\tau}\int_{-\tau}^{\tau}|f_{d}(t_{k_{l}}-h)f_{d}(t_{k_{l}}+h)|\,\,dh,\quad l\in\mathbb{N},

with arbitarary
\tau>0
. The following inequalities hold

\displaystyle J_{d}(t_{k_{l}},\tau)

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2\tau}\int_{-\tau}^{\tau}|\varphi(h)\varphi(-h)|\,\,dh-\dfrac{1}{2\tau}\int_{-\tau}^{\tau}|f_{d}(t_{k_{l}}+h)f_{d}(t_{k_{l}}-h)-\varphi(h)\varphi(-h)|\,\,dh

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2\tau}\int_{-\tau}^{\tau}|\varphi(h)\varphi(-h)|\,\,dh-\sup_{h\in\mathbb{R}}\bigl|f_{d}(t_{k_{l}}+h)f_{d}(t_{k_{l}}-h)-\varphi(h)\varphi(-h)\bigr|.

Let us fix arbitrary
\varepsilon\in(0,\tfrac{1}{4})
. Due to (
10
), there exists
l_{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for any integer
l\geqslant l_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\sup_{h\in\mathbb{R}}|f_{d}(t_{k_{l}}+h)f_{d}(t_{k_{l}}-h)-\varphi(h)\varphi(-h)|<\varepsilon A,

and, consequently, we have

\displaystyle J_{d}(t_{k_{l}},\tau)\geqslant\dfrac{1}{2\tau}\int_{-\tau}^{\tau}|\varphi(h)\varphi(-h)|\,\,dh-\varepsilon A.

Next, we know that
|f_{d}(s)|\leqslant 1
for any
s\in\mathbb{R}
. Due to (
9
), the same inequality is true for
\varphi
, i.e.
|\varphi(s)|\leqslant 1
for any
s\in\mathbb{R}
. Then
|\varphi(\pm h)|\geqslant|\varphi(\pm h)|^{2}
for any
h\in\mathbb{R}
and we have

\displaystyle J_{d}(t_{k_{l}},\tau)\geqslant\dfrac{1}{2\tau}\int_{-\tau}^{\tau}|\varphi(h)\varphi(-h)|^{2}\,\,dh-\varepsilon A

(12)

for any integer
l\geqslant l_{\varepsilon}
and
\tau>0
.

We next consider the integrals
J_{c}(t_{k_{l}},\tau)
,
l\in\mathbb{N}
,
\tau>0
. It is
convenient to proceed with the squared integrand:

\displaystyle J_{c}(t_{k_{l}},\tau)=\dfrac{1}{2\tau}\int_{-\tau}^{\tau}|f_{c}(t_{k_{l}}+h)|\,\,dh\leqslant\sqrt{\dfrac{1}{2\tau}\int_{-\tau}^{\tau}|f_{c}(t_{k_{l}}+h)|^{2}\,\,dh},\quad l\in\mathbb{N},\,\,\tau>0.

We now choose a suitable value of
\tau
. According to (
11
), for the fixed
\varepsilon
there exists
\tau_{d,\varepsilon}>0
such that for any
\tau\geqslant\tau_{d,\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2\tau}\int_{-\tau}^{\tau}|\varphi(h)\varphi(-h)|^{2}\,\,dh\geqslant(1-\varepsilon)A.

Hence, on account (
12
), we have
J_{d}(t_{k_{l}},\tau)\geqslant(1-\varepsilon)A-\varepsilon A=(1-2\varepsilon)A
for any integer
l\geqslant l_{\varepsilon}
and
\tau\geqslant\tau_{d,\varepsilon}
. Next, by Lemma
2
, for the fixed
\varepsilon
we find
\tau_{c,\varepsilon}>0
such that for any
\tau\geqslant\tau_{c,\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\biggl\{\dfrac{1}{2\tau}\int_{-\tau}^{\tau}|f_{c}(t+h)|^{2}\,\,dh\biggr\}\leqslant c_{d}^{4}\,\varepsilon^{2}A^{2}.

It is important that here
c_{d}>0
by the assumption of the theorem. Consequently,
J_{c}(t_{k_{l}},\tau)\leqslant c_{d}^{2}\,\varepsilon A
for any
l\in\mathbb{N}
and
\tau\geqslant\tau_{c,\varepsilon}
. Let
\tau_{\varepsilon}:=\max\{\tau_{d,\varepsilon},\tau_{c,\varepsilon}\}
. Then
J_{d}(t_{k_{l}},\tau_{\varepsilon})\geqslant(1-2\varepsilon)A
and
J_{c}(t_{k_{l}},\tau_{\varepsilon})\leqslant c_{d}^{2}\,\varepsilon A
are satisfied for any integer
l\geqslant l_{\varepsilon}
. We combine these inequalities in (
8
):

\displaystyle J(t_{k_{l}},\tau_{\varepsilon})\geqslant c_{d}^{2}J_{d}(t_{k_{l}},\tau_{\varepsilon})-J_{c}(t_{k_{l}},\tau_{\varepsilon})\geqslant c_{d}^{2}(1-2\varepsilon)A-c_{d}^{2}\,\varepsilon A=c_{d}^{2}(1-3\varepsilon)A.

Recall that
\varepsilon<\tfrac{1}{4}
. So, in particular,
J(t_{k_{l}},\tau_{\varepsilon})>\tfrac{1}{4}c_{d}^{2}A>0
.

Let us return to the function
I
defined by (
5
). According to (
7
) and the above estimate, we have

\displaystyle I(t_{k_{l}},\tau_{\varepsilon})=\dfrac{J(t_{k_{l}},\tau_{\varepsilon})}{|f(t_{k_{l}})|^{2}}>\dfrac{c_{d}^{2}A}{4|f(t_{k_{l}})|^{2}}

for any integer
l\geqslant l_{\varepsilon}
. There is the strictly positive constant
c_{d}^{2}A
in the numerator, but
|f(t_{k_{l}})|\to 0
as
l\to\infty
by the assumption. Hence
I(t_{k_{l}},\tau_{\varepsilon})\to\infty
as
l\to\infty
, which contradicts (
6
) as we expected. Thus
\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|f(t)|=0
can not be valid and we come to the opposite, i.e.
\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|f(t)|>0
.
\Box

Proposition 1

If
\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|f(t)|>0
then
c_{d}>0
.

Proof of Proposition
1
.
Contrary to our claim, suppose that
c_{d}=0
. Hence
F=F_{c}
and
f=f_{c}
according to (
4
). Then, on the one hand,

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2T}\int_{-T}^{T}|f(h)|^{2}\,dh\to 0\quad\text{as}\quad T\to\infty

by Lemma
2
. On the other hand, by the assumption of the theorem,
\mu_{d}:=\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|f(t)|>0
, and hence

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2T}\int_{-T}^{T}|f(h)|^{2}\,dh\geqslant\dfrac{1}{2T}\int_{-T}^{T}\mu_{d}^{2}\,dh=\mu_{d}^{2}\quad\text{for any}\quad T>0.

It contradicts to the previous convergence and thus
c_{d}=0
is not possible, i.e. we have
c_{d}>0
.
\Box

On account of this proposition, we can modify the statement of Theorem
1
in the following way.

Corollary 1

Suppose that
F\in\boldsymbol{Q}
. Then
\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|f(t)|>0
if and only if
c_{d}>0
.

The next assertion significantly complement Proposition
1
.

Proposition 2

If
\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|f(t)|>0
then
\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|f_{d}(t)|>0
.

Proof of Proposition
2
.
If
c_{d}=1
, i.e.
f=f_{d}
, the assertion is obvious. We focus on the case
c_{d}\in(0,1)
. Let
\mu:=\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|f(t)|>0
. Suppose, contrary to our claim, that

\displaystyle\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|f_{d}(t)|=0.

(13)

So we fix arbitrary
\varepsilon\in\bigl(0,\tfrac{1}{4}\bigr)
and we choose
t_{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
|f_{d}(t_{\varepsilon})|<\varepsilon\mu
. Since
f_{d}
is an almost periodic function, there exists an
\ell_{\varepsilon}>0
such that every segment
[a,a+\ell_{\varepsilon}]
with
a\in\mathbb{R}
contains at least one number
\tau_{\varepsilon}
, for which the inequality
|f_{d}(t+\tau_{\varepsilon})-f_{d}(t)|<\varepsilon\mu
holds for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
(see
[
18
]
, p. 20). Let
\mathbb{Z}
denote the set of integers as usual. So we introduce the following family of segments

\displaystyle S_{\varepsilon,k}:=\bigl[\bigl(2k-\tfrac{1}{2}\bigr)\ell_{\varepsilon},\bigl(2k+\tfrac{1}{2}\bigr)\ell_{\varepsilon}\bigr],\quad k\in\mathbb{Z},

and for every
k\in\mathbb{Z}
we choose a point
\tau_{\varepsilon,k}\in S_{\varepsilon,k}
as above, i.e.

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\bigl|f_{d}(t+\tau_{\varepsilon,k})-f_{d}(t)\bigr|<\varepsilon\mu,\quad k\in\mathbb{Z}.

(14)

Since
0\in S_{\varepsilon,0}
, it is clear that we can set
\tau_{\varepsilon,0}:=0
. Next, by the continuity of
f_{d}
on
\mathbb{R}
, we find
\delta_{\varepsilon}\in\bigl(0,\tfrac{1}{2}\ell_{\varepsilon}\bigr]
such that
|f_{d}(t_{\varepsilon}+\delta)-f_{d}(t_{\varepsilon})|<\varepsilon\mu
for any
\delta\in[0,\delta_{\varepsilon})
. Due to (
14
), we have

\displaystyle\bigl|f_{d}(t_{\varepsilon}+\delta+\tau_{\varepsilon,k})-f_{d}(t_{\varepsilon}+\delta)\bigr|<\varepsilon\mu,\quad k\in\mathbb{Z},

for any
\delta\in[0,\delta_{\varepsilon})
. Next, we define the sequence
t_{\varepsilon,k}:=t_{\varepsilon}+\tau_{\varepsilon,k}
,
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, where
t_{\varepsilon,0}=t_{\varepsilon}
. According to the definitions, the numbers
t_{\varepsilon,k}
are distinct and, more precisely,

\displaystyle\ldots<t_{\varepsilon,-2}<t_{\varepsilon,-1}<t_{\varepsilon,0}<t_{\varepsilon,1}<t_{\varepsilon,2}<\ldots

Moreover, the intervals
(t_{\varepsilon,k}-\delta_{\varepsilon},t_{\varepsilon,k}+\delta_{\varepsilon})
,
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, are disjoint, i.e.

\displaystyle t_{\varepsilon,k}+\delta_{\varepsilon}<t_{\varepsilon,k+1}-\delta_{\varepsilon},\quad k\in\mathbb{Z}.

(15)

Let us estimate the absolute values of
f_{d}
on these intervals. We first observe that for any
\delta\in[0,\delta_{\varepsilon})
and
k\in\mathbb{Z}

\displaystyle\bigl|f_{d}(t_{\varepsilon,k}+\delta)\bigr|

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\bigl|f_{d}(t_{\varepsilon}+\delta)\bigr|+\bigl|f_{d}(t_{\varepsilon,k}+\delta)-f_{d}(t_{\varepsilon}+\delta)\bigr|

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\bigl|f_{d}(t_{\varepsilon})\bigr|+\bigl|f_{d}(t_{\varepsilon}+\delta)-f_{d}(t_{\varepsilon})\bigr|+\bigl|f_{d}(t_{\varepsilon,k}+\delta)-f_{d}(t_{\varepsilon}+\delta)\bigr|.

Hence, by the above inequalities, we have
|f_{d}(t_{\varepsilon,k}+\delta)|<3\varepsilon\mu
for any
\delta\in[0,\delta_{\varepsilon})
and
k\in\mathbb{Z}
.

We now turn to the function
f_{c}
from the decomposition. Due to
c_{d}<1
, it is expressed by the formula
f_{c}(t)=\tfrac{f(t)-c_{d}f_{d}(t)}{1-c_{d}}
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
. So we observe that

\displaystyle|f_{c}(t_{\varepsilon,k}+\delta)|=\biggl|\dfrac{f(t_{\varepsilon,k}+\delta)-c_{d}f_{d}(t_{\varepsilon,k}+\delta)}{1-c_{d}}\biggr|\geqslant\dfrac{|f(t_{\varepsilon,k}+\delta)|-c_{d}|f_{d}(t_{\varepsilon,k}+\delta)|}{1-c_{d}}.

Hence the obtained estimates yield

\displaystyle|f_{c}(t_{\varepsilon,k}+\delta)|\geqslant\dfrac{\mu-3c_{d}\varepsilon\mu}{1-c_{d}}>(1-3\varepsilon)\mu

(16)

for any
\delta\in[0,\delta_{\varepsilon})
and
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. We next consider the following sequence of integrals

\displaystyle I_{\varepsilon,n}:=\dfrac{1}{2T_{\varepsilon,n}}\int_{-T_{\varepsilon,n}}^{T_{\varepsilon,n}}|f_{c}(t_{\varepsilon}+h)|^{2}\,dh,\quad n\in\mathbb{N}_{0},

with
T_{\varepsilon,n}:=(2n+1)\ell_{\varepsilon}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, where
\mathbb{N}_{0}:=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
. On the one hand, since
T_{\varepsilon,n}\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
, we know that

\displaystyle I_{\varepsilon,n}\to 0\quad\text{as}\quad n\to\infty

(17)

by Lemma
2
. On the other hand, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
we have

\displaystyle U_{\varepsilon,n}:=\bigcup_{k=-n}^{n}(t_{\varepsilon,k}-\delta_{\varepsilon},t_{\varepsilon,k}+\delta_{\varepsilon})\subset\bigl[t_{\varepsilon}-T_{\varepsilon,n},t_{\varepsilon}+T_{\varepsilon,n}\bigr].

(18)

Indeed, it follows from
(
15
)
and the inequalities

\displaystyle t_{\varepsilon,n}+\delta_{\varepsilon}=t_{\varepsilon}+\tau_{\varepsilon,n}+\delta_{\varepsilon}<t_{\varepsilon}+\bigl(2n+\tfrac{1}{2}\bigr)\ell_{e}+\tfrac{1}{2}\ell_{\varepsilon}=t_{\varepsilon}+(2n+1)\ell_{\varepsilon}=t_{\varepsilon}+T_{\varepsilon,n},

\displaystyle t_{\varepsilon,-n}-\delta_{\varepsilon}=t_{\varepsilon}+\tau_{\varepsilon,-n}-\delta_{\varepsilon}>t_{\varepsilon}+\bigl(-2n-\tfrac{1}{2}\bigr)\ell_{e}-\tfrac{1}{2}\ell_{\varepsilon}=t_{\varepsilon}-(2n+1)\ell_{\varepsilon}=t_{\varepsilon}-T_{\varepsilon,n}.

Due to (
18
), we have

\displaystyle I_{\varepsilon,n}=\dfrac{1}{2T_{\varepsilon,n}}\int_{t_{\varepsilon}-T_{\varepsilon,n}}^{t_{\varepsilon}+T_{\varepsilon,n}}|f_{c}(h)|^{2}\,dh\geqslant\dfrac{1}{2T_{\varepsilon,n}}\int_{U_{\varepsilon,n}}|f_{c}(h)|^{2}\,dh=\dfrac{1}{2T_{\varepsilon,n}}\sum_{k=-n}^{n}\int_{t_{\varepsilon,k}-\delta_{\varepsilon}}^{t_{\varepsilon,k}+\delta_{\varepsilon}}|f_{c}(h)|^{2}\,dh,\quad n\in\mathbb{N}_{0},

where, on account of (
16
),

\displaystyle\int_{t_{\varepsilon,k}-\delta_{\varepsilon}}^{t_{\varepsilon,k}+\delta_{\varepsilon}}|f_{c}(h)|^{2}\,dh=\int_{-\delta_{\varepsilon}}^{\delta_{\varepsilon}}|f_{c}(t_{\varepsilon,k}+\delta)|^{2}\,d\delta\geqslant(1-3\varepsilon)^{2}\mu^{2}\int_{-\delta_{\varepsilon}}^{\delta_{\varepsilon}}\,d\delta=2\delta_{\varepsilon}(1-3\varepsilon)^{2}\mu^{2}.

Then

\displaystyle I_{\varepsilon,n}\geqslant\dfrac{1}{2T_{\varepsilon,n}}\sum_{k=-n}^{n}2\delta_{\varepsilon}(1-3\varepsilon)^{2}\mu^{2}=\dfrac{(2n+1)\delta_{\varepsilon}(1-3\varepsilon)^{2}\mu^{2}}{T_{\varepsilon,n}}=\dfrac{\delta_{\varepsilon}(1-3\varepsilon)^{2}\mu^{2}}{\ell_{\varepsilon}}>\dfrac{\delta_{\varepsilon}\mu^{2}}{16\ell_{\varepsilon}},

where we used that
\varepsilon\in\bigl(0,\tfrac{1}{4}\bigr)
. So we see that
I_{\varepsilon,n}\nrightarrow 0
as
n\to\infty
and we come to a contradiction with (
17
). Thus (
13
) is impossible and we conclude that
\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|f_{d}(t)|>0
.
\Box

Theorem
1
and Proposition
2
yield at once the following assertion, which is the second main result of the paper.

Theorem 2

If
F\in\boldsymbol{Q}
and
c_{d}>0
, then
\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|f_{d}(t)|>0
.

Thus we proved that the conditions
\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|f(t)|>0
and
\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|f_{d}(t)|>0
are necessary for belonging
F
to the class
\boldsymbol{Q}
in the case
c_{d}>0
. Since the first condition is always stronger than the second one (as we showed above) and they are not equivalent in general case (see introduction), only the first inequality should be included in a group of potential sufficient conditions for generalization of the mentioned existing results.
