Histone methyltransferase DOT1L maintains cell state and restricts cytotoxic potential of CD8 T cells
Authors: Muddassir Malik, Willem-Jan de Leeuw, Muhammad Assad Aslam, Eliza Mari Kwesi-Maliepaard, Teun van den Brand, Bram van den Broek, Maxime Kempers, Liesbeth Hoekman, Natalie Proost, Tibor van Welsem, Nikolina Bąbała, Elselien Frijlink, Elzo de Wit, Jannie Borst, Heinz Jacobs, Fred van Leeuwen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The histone methyltransferase DOT1L is emerging as a central epigenetic regulator in immune cells. Loss of DOT1L during development of CD8 T cells in vivo leads to gain of memory characteristics but has also been reported to compromise CD8 T cell viability and antitumor reactivity. Here, we determined the cell-intrinsic role of DOT1L in mature mouse CD8 T cells. After conditional deletion of Dot1L in vitro, CD8 T cells retained in vivo proliferative capacity and antitumor reactivity. Moreover, Dot1L knockout CD8 T cells showed increased antigen-specific cytotoxicity toward tumor cells in vitro. Mechanistically, loss of DOT1L resulted in an altered cell state with loss of T cell and gain of innate-like features. These transcriptional changes were mediated by loss of DOT1L methyltransferase activity in a dose-dependent manner. Our findings show that in mature CD8 T cells, ablation of DOT1L activity is well tolerated and reprograms them to gain innate-like memory cell characteristics and enhance intrinsic cytotoxic capacity.

INTRODUCTION

Cytotoxic CD8-positive (CD8) T cells are key executors of the adaptive immune system. Their differentiation and activation are tightly regulated by key transcription factors and guided by epigenetic mechanisms (1–3). The toolbox for manipulating epigenetic regulators is rapidly expanding, thereby offering new opportunities to influence CD8 T cell fate and function. However, harnessing the regulatory potential of the epigenome to modulate CD8 T cell–mediated immunity requires the identification and mechanistic understanding of the key epigenetic regulators involved.

We previously uncovered a central role for the histone methyltransferase DOT1L in mouse CD8 T cell development (4). DOT1L methylates histone H3 lysine 79 (H3K79) and “writes” H3K79 methylation (H3K79me) at the 5′end of gene bodies of actively transcribed genes, where the degree of H3K79 dimethylation (H3K79me2) correlates with the level of gene activity (5). While the molecular functions of DOT1L and H3K79me are still largely unknown, DOT1L has been recognized as a barrier to differentiation in the immune system (4, 6–10). Moreover, highly specific inhibitors have been developed that allow for precision targeting of DOT1L in vitro or in vivo, as has been demonstrated for tumor cells in which DOT1L activity forms a cell type–specific dependency (11–14). However, not all molecular and cellular functions of DOT1L are dependent on H3K79me, indicating that DOT1L might have additional roles beyond its canonical enzymatic function (15–20).

Using a conditional mouse knockout (KO) model in which Dot1L is deleted early during thymic T cell development (Lck-Cre;Dot1L), we observed that Dot1L KO CD8 T cells differentiate in an antigen-independent manner toward memory-phenotype cells (referred to as TAIM cells) (4, 21). This premature differentiation from naïve T cells toward cells with enhanced memory characteristics suggests that loss of DOT1L may promote more effective and sustained immunological responses (21–23). However, in the same Dot1L KO mice, the CD8 T cell response against Listeria monocytogenes infection and to vaccination was impaired (4). In addition, deletion of Dot1L later during thymic T cell development (CD4-Cre;Dot1L) in vivo has been linked to the development of CD8 T cells with molecular changes indicating impaired function (24). Mice of this latter model showed reduced control of MC38 tumor cell outgrowth compared to wild-type (WT) mice and increased apoptosis in CD8 T cells in tumor-draining lymph nodes and tumor tissues, leading to a model of metabolic competition for methionine between tumor and T cells resulting in lowered DOT1L-mediated H3K79me in T cells and compromised antitumor immunity (24). However, in these Dot1L KO models, Dot1L is also deleted in other immune cell types, leading to a lack of development of CD4+ T and invariant NKT cells (4). Given DOT1L’s impact on various members of the lymphoid lineage, these additional confounding changes may influence the differentiation of CD8 T cells and the overall immune responses in these animals, affecting T cells both directly and indirectly. Last, treatment of human T cells with DOT1L inhibitor (DOT1Li) SGC-0946 reportedly elevates the activation threshold of CD8 T cells by lowering miR-181a expression, which increases the extracellular signal–regulated kinase (ERK) phosphatase DUSP6 (25). This elevated threshold reduces the graft-versus-host response in vivo upon transfer of the DOT1Li-treated T cells to immunodeficient NSG mice, while CAR-T cell–mediated tumor control activity was maintained (25). However, in this chemical perturbation model, DOT1L inactivation is not permanent, with regain of H3K79me and other DOT1L functions expected in the absence of DOT1Li following transfer.

Thus, from a T cell therapy perspective, DOT1L loss in CD8 T cells has been linked not only to positive effects, specifically the acquisition of memory features, but also to negative effects, such as increased apoptosis, reduced viability, and reduced antitumor immunity. In this study, we combined inducible genetic and chemical conditional inactivation models in mature CD8 T cells to overcome previous confounding factors and thereby understand the cell-intrinsic role of DOT1L in mature CD8 T cells in a setting relevant for human adoptive T cell therapy protocols. The results of these models suggest that, in contrast to early-deletion models, mature CD8 T cells lacking DOT1L activity are highly proficient in antigen-specific proliferation and maintain their ability to control tumor growth in vivo. Dot1L KO CD8 T cells showed accelerated and enhanced antigen-dependent tumor cell killing in vitro. Mechanistically, this role was mediated by the catalytic activity of DOT1L, as chemical inhibition phenocopied genetic deletion of Dot1L in CD8 T cells. Multiomic phenotyping of the Dot1L-KO and DOT1L-inhibited CD8 T cells provided evidence for altered differentiation with partial loss of canonical CD8 T cell characteristics and gain of innate-like cell features. Our findings show that targeting of DOT1L in mature CD8 T cells does not compromise their viability and function but instead rewires them to support enhanced tumor cell killing activity. These insights support further investigations into strategic targeting of DOT1L coupled with other molecular modifications to modulate immune responses in vivo.

RESULTS

Mature CD8 T cells tolerate loss of DOT1L and H3K79me

In previous studies on DOT1L in CD8 T cells, Dot1L was permanently deleted early in the T cell lineage or transiently inactivated by chemical inhibition (4, 24–26). To overcome the limitations of those models and to determine the cell-intrinsic role of DOT1L in mature CD8 T cells, we generated a tamoxifen [4-hydroxytamoxifen (4-OHT)]–inducible deletion model (Cre-ERT2;Dot1Lfl/fl) allowing genetic ablation of Dot1L in spleen-derived CD8 T cells in vitro (Fig. 1A). In this model, CD8 T cells also expressed the transgenic ovalbumin (OVA)–specific T cell receptor (TCR) OT-I (27), which recognizes the chicken OVA-derived SIINFEKL peptide in the context of H-2Kb.

Isolated splenic OT-I CD8 T cells were activated with CD3 and CD28 agonists. The activated T cells were treated with 4-OHT and expanded for 8 days in vitro (Fig. 1, A and B). CD8 T cells derived from Cre-ERT2;Dot1Lwt/wt mice were used as WT controls. At the time of activation, both Dot1Lwt/wt and Dot1Lfl/fl CD8 T cells were still DOT1L proficient. Genomic deletion of exon 2 of Dot1L was efficient and nearly complete at day 2 in Dot1Lfl/fl (fig. S1A). H3K79me was gradually lost over time and mainly started to decay only after day 2, as determined by flow cytometry analysis of H3K79me2 (Fig. 1C). The slow decay of the H3K79me mark agrees with the absence of a known H3K79-specific demethylase. Loss of H3K79me is therefore mainly determined by passive dilution upon cell proliferation (28, 29). Consequently, the loss of DOT1L-mediated H3K79me cannot be studied without (induction of) cellular replication. H3K79me2 staining was strongly reduced after 4 days of T cell expansion and stabilized around the lowest level on day 6 (Fig. 1C and fig. S1B). Mass spectrometry analysis further confirmed that in Dot1L KO cells on day 8, no methylation was detectable, indicating that DOT1L is the sole methyltransferase for H3K79 in T cells [fig. S1C and (4)].

Despite the rapid deletion of Dot1L and subsequent passive loss of H3K79me, Dot1L KO CD8 T cells expanded at a similar rate as WT cells (Fig. 1B). Moreover, no loss of viability was observed (Fig. 1, D and E), consistent with previous results (4) but contrasting observations when Dot1L is deleted early in the T cell lineage (24). We next analyzed the expression of key CD8 T cell surface proteins and transcription factors. In our experimental setup, naïve cells acquired CD44 expression following activation. Deletion of Dot1L did not affect this differentiation pattern (Fig. 1, F and G). Surface levels of CD3 (TCR complex) and the CD8 coreceptor were reduced upon Dot1L KO in mature T cells, in the absence of antigen exposure, potentially indicating partial loss of key CD8 T cell–specific markers (Fig. 1, H and I, and fig. S1D). Last, loss of DOT1L led to increased expression of the CD8 T cell transcription factor Eomesodermin (EOMES), while T-bet was unaffected (Fig. 1, J and K, and fig. S1, E to G). These observations partially recapitulated the characteristics of Dot1L-deficient CD8 TAIM cells in the Lck-Cre;Dot1L model, in which reduced CD8 and CD3 surface expression and increased EOMES and T-bet expression were observed (4). T-bet and EOMES are two transcription factors that determine effector CD8 T cell differentiation and function (3, 30). These findings show that, apparently, loss of DOT1L modulates key aspects of CD8 T cell identity without impairing their viability.

Proficient tumor control in vivo by Dot1L KO CD8 T cells upon adoptive cell transfer

Mice in which Dot1L is deleted early in the T cell lineage show reduced immune responses (4, 24), including compromised tumor control (24). In line with this observation, impaired CD8 T cell functionality due to limited methionine availability in the tumor microenvironment has been linked to reduced DOT1L activity (24). To address whether the observed loss of functionality in vivo upon early deletion of Dot1L reflects a cell-intrinsic role of DOT1L, we here used an in vitro deletion approach in mature CD8 T cells. To determine the antitumor potential of mature CD8 T cells lacking DOT1L, B16F10 melanoma cells expressing OVA (B16F10-OVA) were subcutaneously injected in CD45.1+ recipient mice to initiate tumor outgrowth. After 8 days of tumor outgrowth, the tumor-bearing mice received adoptive cell therapy (ACT) with in vitro expanded, 4-OHT–treated CD45.2+ OT-I CD8 T cells (Fig. 2A). Loss of H3K79me2 as well as reduced surface expression of the TCR and CD8 coreceptor on day 8 of in vitro expansion of Dot1L KO T cells was confirmed by flow cytometry (Fig. 2, B to D, and fig. S2, A and B). The cumulative tumor outgrowth was significantly delayed after ACT of WT (mean: 38.4 ± 4.9 days) as well as KO (mean: 39.1 ± 5.8 days) CD8 T cells compared to vehicle control (mean: 25.0 ± 7.3 days) but was not different between KO and WT (Fig. 2E and fig. S2C). Similarly, mice treated with vehicle [Hanks’ balanced salt solution (HBSS)] had a median survival of 27 days, while mice that received WT CD8 T cells had a significantly increased median survival of 42 days (fig. S2D). However, no difference in survival was observed between WT and KO ACT treatment (median survival of 44 days; (Fig. 2E and fig. S2D). No significant endpoint bias could be observed based on tumor weight at the time of sacrifice (fig. S2E).

In addition to monitoring tumor growth, we analyzed the abundance and phenotype of transferred CD45.2+ OT-I CD8 T cells isolated from blood (days 14 and 21 and endpoint) and the spleen and tumor tissue at endpoint (time of sacrifice, which was variable between individual animals). Although at day 14, the relative abundance of KO cells in blood was lower than that of WT transferred cells, WT and KO donor CD8 T cells expanded to the same relative abundance at day 21 in blood and at endpoint in spleen and tumor tissue (Fig. 2, F and G). The immune composition in blood and spleen at endpoint was predominantly made up of CD44 and CD62L (l-selectin) double-positive cells with no significant differences observed between WT and KO (fig. S2, G and H). In contrast, in the tumor at endpoint, the Dot1L KO tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) showed a gain of the CD44+CD62L− and loss of the CD44+CD62L+ population compared to WT, indicating increased differentiation toward effector-like cells (Fig. 2H). Moreover, their expression of CD49d, a marker of cognate antigen experience, was higher, as was the percentage of CD49d+ TILs (Fig. 2I and fig. S2I). To determine to what extent the loss of DOT1L and the concomitant reduction in TCR and CD8 surface expression affected T cell exhaustion or dysfunction, we evaluated expression of Ly108, a surrogate marker for activity of T cell factor 1, in combination with CD69 as well as the expression of the inhibitory receptors PD-1 and TIM-3 to assess exhaustion states of CD8 T cells (31). No differences could be found in exhaustion between WT and Dot1L KO CD8 T cells in the tumor at endpoint (fig. S2, J to L). It should be noted, however, that the number of CD45.2+ TILs at endpoint was generally low (fig. S2F), which likely relates to the rapid outgrowth of this aggressive tumor model.

To independently confirm that loss of DOT1L does not compromise the viability and function of CD8 T cells in vivo, we performed in vivo cytotoxicity assays (32). For that purpose, we systemically induced deletion of Dot1L in vivo by injection with tamoxifen and adoptively transferred enriched naïve WT and Dot1L-KO OT-I CD8 cells into recipient animals. Mice were subsequently treated with tattoo-based OVA vaccination on days 0, 3, and 6 after adoptive transfer (Fig. 2J). In two independent experiments, we observed that WT and Dot1L-KO cells expanded after vaccination, demonstrating the proficiency of Dot1L-KO cells to respond to vaccination despite the reduction in CD8 and TCR expression (Fig. 2, K to N, and fig. S2, M and N). Upon injection of a mixture of OVA peptide–pulsed and unpulsed splenocytes, WT and Dot1L-KO cells were highly effective in killing of the OVA-pulsed splenocytes at 3 and 6 hours after injection, confirming the killing capacity of Dot1L-KO cells (Fig. 2, K to N). Comparing WT versus KO was difficult due to some variation in expansion between animals and experiments. Loss of Dot1L did not affect the expression of exhaustion markers under these conditions at endpoint (fig. S2, O to P).

Together, these results show that expansion, viability, and in vivo tumor control and cytotoxicity upon vaccination by adoptively transferred CD8 T cells were not negatively affected by deletion of Dot1L and concomitant loss of H3K79me. Moreover, at the endpoint in the B16F10-OVA tumors, Dot1L KO cells adapted a more effector memory–like state and increased up-regulation of CD49d. To investigate whether these changes reflect a possible role of DOT1L in restricting activation and effector functions of mature CD8 T cells, we focused our further analyses on studying their cytotoxic function in vitro.

Deletion of Dot1L increases the tumor killing potential of mature CD8 T cells in vitro

To quantitatively measure their cytotoxic potential, in vitro–expanded Dot1L WT and KO OT-I CD8 T cells (day 8) were cocultured with mCherry expressing B16F10-OVA cells (Fig. 3A). Tumor cell killing was determined by quantifying the confluency of mCherry+ B16F10-OVA target cells using 4-hour interval imaging over a time course of 4 days. Under these conditions, WT OT-I CD8 T cells showed dose-dependent killing of target tumor cells over time. In comparison, Dot1L KO T cells showed enhanced control of target cell outgrowth (Fig. 3, B and C). Moreover, at the highest CD8 T cell–to–target cell ratio, the killing was most pronounced, as target cell clearance was near complete within the first 8 hours of coculture (Fig. 3, B and C; fig. S3, A to C; and movies S1 to S3).

To gain further phenotypic insight into the enhanced killing by Dot1L KO CD8 T cells, WT and KO cells were analyzed just before (0 hours) and during (6 hours) coculture by flow cytometry. After exposure to target cells, WT CD8 T cells down-regulated their TCR (CD3; a TCR complex member) and coreceptor (CD8) surface expression (Fig. 3, D and E), as expected for CD8 cells that are exposed to their cognate antigen (33). KO T cells showed lower CD3 and CD8 surface expression at 0 hours, as observed earlier, and this further decreased at 6 hours to levels comparable to (CD3) or lower (CD8) than WT (Fig. 3, D and E). The surface expression of CD5 was also reduced in KO CD8 T cells, as was the CD5+ fraction before and during coculture (Fig. 3F and fig. S3D). Since CD5 is a negative regulator of TCR signaling that has been suggested to be part of a feedback loop (34), the lower expression of CD5 might reflect a compensatory mechanism for the reduced TCR and CD8 surface levels. After 6 hours of coculture, the up-regulation of CD69, an activation marker and metabolic gatekeeper of CD8 T cells (35), was reduced in Dot1L KO compared to WT (Fig. 3G), although the fraction of CD8 T cells that expresses CD69 was similar (fig. S3E). This suggests that Dot1L-KO CD8 T cells show reduced activation strength.

Effector cytokine release and cytolytic markers were measured by flow cytometry to provide insight into the increased effector function in CD8 T cells during the response against target cells. Production of interferon-γ (IFN-γ) was not altered (fig. S3, F and I to J), while the production of tumor necrosis factor–α (TNFα) was reduced for KO CD8 T cells (fig. S3, G, K, and L), the latter of which has also been observed for CD8 T cells in the CD4-Cre;Dot1L model (24). Moreover, CD107a (LAMP-1), a marker for active degranulation (36), was increased in KO after 5 hours of coculture, without affecting the fraction of cells that was actively degranulating (CD107a+) (fig. S3, H, M, and N), indicating increased granulation potential in Dot1L KO CD8 T cells. However, the abundance of cytolytic enzyme granzyme B (GzmB) was similar between WT and KO CD8 T cells (fig. S3O). We confirmed that the activation of OT-I CD8 T cells upon challenge with target cells was strictly antigen dependent, since no phenotypic changes were observed when T cells were cocultured with B16F10 cells lacking OVA expression (MOCK) (fig. S3, F to O) and no killing of these MOCK target cells was observed (see below). To explore the underlying molecular mechanism of enhanced killing response of KO T cells, two key transcription factors, T-bet and EOMES, were analyzed. Upon challenge with target cells, WT CD8 T cells shifted from a mainly T-bet EOMES double-positive state to a double-negative or T-bet single-positive state. This response was altered in Dot1L KO, in which T cells tended to retain the double-positive state (Fig. 3H and fig. S3P). The impact on differentiation upon target cell challenge was further determined by staining for CD44 and CD62L. From 0 to 6 hours, WT CD8 T cells shifted from mainly (CD44intCD62L+) to more-intermediate and terminal effector-like states (CD44highCD62Lint and CD44intCD62L−, respectively). In Dot1L KO T cells, this differentiation shift was skewed toward the intermediate differentiation state (fig. S3, Q to T). Loss of DOT1L did not affect the expression of exhaustion markers under these conditions (fig. S3, U to X). Last, we observed that the viability of the CD8 T cells (percentage of live cells) was comparable between WT and KO before coculture (Fig. 3I). However, significantly more viable Dot1L KO than WT CD8 T cells were present after 48 hours of exposure to B16F10-OVA tumor cells (Fig. 3J and fig. S3Y). This increase in cell number might be caused by increased survival of activated Dot1L KO T cells or because the accelerated elimination of target cells led to reduced activation-induced cell death. Combined, these results indicate that DOT1L-deficient mature CD8 T cells acquire a molecular state with altered T cell activation properties and differential expression of key CD8 T cell surface proteins, coinciding with enhanced cell-intrinsic cytotoxic killing potential and increased survival.

CD8 T cells lacking DOT1L acquire innate-like characteristics and up-regulate cytolytic factors

To gain a deeper and unbiased understanding of the altered state of Dot1L KO CD8 T cells, we determined the transcriptomic changes upon Dot1L deletion in mature CD8 T cells after 8 days of culture. RNA sequencing analysis revealed that 1289 genes were up-regulated and 1309 were down-regulated upon Dot1L deletion (Fig. 4A and table S2). When subjected to gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) based on Gene Ontology (GO), KO CD8 T cells showed a negative enrichment for genes associated with “TCR signaling” (Fig. 4B and table S3). This potentially agrees with the reduced surface expression of TCR and CD8 observed by flow cytometry. A positive enrichment was found for genes associated with “immune response to tumor cells” and “leukocyte degranulation” (fig. S4A), in agreement with the increased killing potential and increased levels of degranulation marker CD107a during the response against target cells in vitro. Furthermore, on the basis of cell type–specific curated gene sets, GSEA analysis showed a positive enrichment for genes annotated as “antigen response” in KO CD8 T cells (37), despite lack of antigen exposure (Fig. 4C and table S4). Apparently, Dot1L-KO CD8 T cells acquire a more effector memory–like state, potentially contributing to the observed rapid killing in vitro. Notably, KO CD8 T cells were enriched for gene signatures related to CD8 T cells that lose their T cell lineage commitment and differentiate toward NK-like T cells (T to NK CD8 T cells) (Fig. 4C) (38) and to bona fide natural killer cells (fig. S4A) (39). While the three enriched signatures shown in Fig. 4 (B and C) all relate to cytotoxic functions, the gene sets showed little overlap (fig. S4B), indicating that they might represent distinct processes. Combined, these findings suggest that mature T cells lacking DOT1L down-regulate some of the key T cell features [lower TCR, CD8; reduced activation (based on CD69)] and partially gain properties of innate immune cells (elevated CD107a and increased T to NK and NK cell signature genes), which could contribute to a gain in effector potential.

These molecular findings guided us to focus on known regulators of T and NK cell differentiation to provide more insight into the altered state of Dot1L KO CD8 T cells (Fig. 4D). Dot1L KO CD8 T cells show increased expression of Id2 and Prdm1 and reduced expression of Id3. ID2 (encoded by Id2) and ID3 (encoded by Id3) are transcription factors known to play a complex codependent role in CD8 T cell differentiation and are essential in the formation of effector and memory subsets (3). ID3 is essential for memory CD8 T cell formation, and its expression is transcriptionally repressed by Blimp-1 (encoded by Prdm1) in effector CD8 T cells (3, 40). Blimp-1 and Hobit (homolog of Blimp-1 in T cells, encoded by Zfp683) are expressed throughout effector and memory cells, and upon antigen stimulation, Hobit is down-regulated in human CD8 T cells (41). Notably, Hobit expression was also down-regulated in KO. Zeb2 was increased in Dot1L KO CD8 T cells. ZEB2 (encoded by Zeb2) is known to be essential in the CD8 T cell response and known to be a positive regulator in memory and effector memory differentiation (42). CCR7 (encoded by Ccr7), an important chemokine receptor for T cell homing and typically highly expressed in naïve CD8 T cells (43, 44), was down-regulated in KO CD8 T cells (Fig. 4D). Together, these changes suggest that loss of Dot1L induces several transcriptional programs associated with activated and effector memory CD8 T cells. Considering the cytotoxic capacity, CD160 (encoded by Cd160) expression is associated with cytolytic activity and active effector responses of CD8 T cells; it is also linked to up-regulation of 4-1BB (encoded by Tnfrsf9) (45, 46). Dot1L-deficient CD8 T cells, which displayed enhanced effector function, showed increased expression of both Cd160 and Tnfrsf9 (Fig. 4D).

Several of the up-regulated factors in KO (e.g., Id2, Zeb2, Il2rb, Ccr7, Tim3, Lag3, 2b4 and Xcl1) also play important roles in NK cell differentiation, function, and fitness (42, 47–50). Furthermore, in the context of NK differentiation, Il2rb (CD122), Cd244 (2b4), and Klrb1c [encoding the NK receptor NK1.1 (in humans CD161)], all up-regulated in KO, are markers often associated with mature NK cells (47) and are found on several mouse NK precursors (51). For conventional mature NK cells, the expression of Blimp-1 is high, and Hobit is low (41), matching the observations in KO. Last, Bcl11b (encoding BCL11B), a transcriptional repressor key in maintenance of T cell lineage commitment and repression of NK differentiation (38, 52), was decreased in Dot1L KO CD8 T cells (Fig. 4D), likely contributing to the enriched NK gene signature (Fig. 4C). It should, however, be noted that most of the fold changes (FCs) are modest, indicating that it is likely a combined effect of multiple changes that drive the altered transcriptional state observed in the absence of DOT1L.

Combined, our findings show that DOT1L-deficient CD8 T cells lose certain aspects of their T cell identity, gain effector CD8 and NK cell characteristics, and potentially acquire a rewired state in favor of increased cytolytic activity. Whether the mentioned transcriptional changes of specific genes in Dot1L KO T cells are directly mediated through the absence of DOT1L, rather than acquired through secondary effectors, still needs to be established.

To explore how the transcriptional changes due to loss of DOT1L correspond to changes at the protein level, whole-cell proteomics was performed on expanded CD8 T cells on day 8. Although proteomics was not as sensitive as transcriptomics and was biased toward more abundant factors, several important observations were made from this analysis. In total, 282 proteins were up-regulated and 113 down-regulated in KO CD8 T cells (Fig. 4E and table S5). Overlaying the proteome changes and the transcriptome changes indicated a similar expression trend (Fig. 4, F and G). Among the most up-regulated proteins in Dot1L KO, many cytotoxic granule proteins (granzymes and perforin, except GzmB) were found (Fig. 4, E and H). This suggests that besides the increased degranulation upon exposure to target cells (fig. S3, H, M, and N), Dot1L KO cells have a higher intrinsic cytotoxic capacity. On the other hand, among the most down-regulated proteins in KO were CD8 and CD5, confirming the surface staining results (Fig. 4, H and I). However, TCR-associated CD3 γ, δ, ε, and ζ (CD247) proteins were not uniformly altered in the whole-cell proteome of Dot1L KO T cells nor at the transcriptional level (Fig. 4, H and I). Their altered surface expression levels observed by flow cytometry (Fig. 2C and fig. S2B) might be explained in part by the reduced mRNA and protein expression of CD247, encoding the CD3ζ chain, which is rate limiting for the assembly and transfer of TCR/CD3 complexes on the cell surface (53). This would be in line with previous findings in the LcK-Cre;Dot1L model, in which Dot1L was deleted early in the T cell lineage (4). CD6 was also strongly down-regulated at the protein level (Fig. 4H). This relatively understudied signal-transducing transmembrane receptor is expressed by T cells and some B and NK cells. Antibodies blocking the interaction between CD6 and its ligands augment antitumor immune responses, although they have effects on CD8 T and NK cells (54–56). In agreement with the global correlations, the alterations in the expression levels of the selected proteins largely correlated with changes at the RNA level (Fig. 4, H and I). Together, both transcriptome and proteome changes confirm a rewiring in Dot1L KO CD8 T cells toward a noncanonical intermediary innate-like and CD8 effector T cell phenotype.

DOT1L affects CD8 T cell state and function via its catalytic activity

DOT1L and its ortholog Dot1 in yeast have been shown to execute functions by methylation-dependent as well as methylation-independent mechanisms (15, 16, 18–20). To delineate whether the transcriptional role of DOT1L in CD8 T cells is linked to its methyltransferase activity, we took advantage of the availability of highly specific DOT1Lis. Instead of adding 4-OHT, which genetically deletes Dot1L, the DOT1Li SGC-0946 was added during the in vitro activation and expansion of isolated OT-I CD8 T cells (Fig. 5A) (57). Treatment with SGC-0946, which inhibits DOT1L without affecting its protein expression level (57), slightly impaired the initial proliferative capacity of CD8 T cells during in vitro expansion (Fig. 5B). Like Dot1L KO, treatment with SGC-0946 did not affect H3K79me2 immediately but resulted in the loss of H3K79me2 over time and reached a level similar to Dot1L KO at day 8 (Fig. 5C).

Having established that H3K79me2 was lost, we subjected the DOT1L-inhibited and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO)–treated control cells of day 8 to transcriptomic and proteomic analyses. At the RNA level, 1235 genes were up-regulated and 1196 genes were down-regulated in SGC-0946 compared to DMSO-treated CD8 T cells (Fig. 5D and table S2). The differential gene expression pattern caused by DOT1L inhibition and Dot1L KO strongly correlated (Fig. 5E). Moreover, the differentially expressed genes in SGC-0946–treated cells showed the same gene set enrichments as Dot1L KO CD8 T cells, further strengthening this conclusion (fig. S5, A to C, and table S6 and S7). At the proteome level, 319 proteins were up-regulated, 94 proteins were down-regulated upon SGC-0946 treatment (Fig. 5F and table S5), and the proteome changes correlated with their corresponding transcriptome changes (Fig. 5G and fig. S5, D and E). Like the results from Dot1L KO, CD8, CD5, and CD6 proteins were down-regulated, and multiple granzymes and perforin were up-regulated upon SGC-0946 treatment [Fig. 5H and fig. S5, F to H, and see the corresponding transcriptome changes in fig. S5 (D and E)]. These results reveal that the molecular changes in SGC-0946–treated and SGC-0946–expanded CD8 T cells closely resemble those observed in Dot1L KO.

In line with these findings, SGC-0946–treated OT-I CD8 T cells showed enhanced target cell control in vitro compared to DMSO-treated cells (Fig. 5I). Moreover, the killing was notably accelerated and resulted in near complete eradication of all target cells within 8 hours of coculture (Fig. 5, I and J, and fig. S5, I and J). SGC-0946 treatment by itself did not affect B16F10-OVA outgrowth (fig. S5K). During coculture with target cells, SGC-0946–treated CD8 T cells showed reduced expression of the activation marker CD69 compared to DMSO-treated cells, with an increased expression of the degranulation marker CD107a and decreased TNFα production, strongly resembling the phenotypes of Dot1L KO (fig. S5, L to N). Moreover, like Dot1L KO, SGC-0946 treatment did not compromise viability of CD8 T cells before or after 6 hours of coculture (fig. S5O). SGC-0946 treatment led to retention of a T-bet and EOMES double-positive state and CD44int CD62L+, suggesting a bias toward an intermediate state instead of a terminal differentiation state (fig. S5, P to S). Expression of exhaustion markers was not affected by DOT1L inhibition (fig. S3, T and U). Combined, these findings suggest that DOT1L affects CD8 T cell identity and function at least largely via its catalytic activity.

The catalytic function of DOT1L is dose dependent and associated with H3K79me

Following their discovery as epigenetic writers, many histone methyltransferases have been shown to also act on noncanonical substrates. Therefore, we investigated how the role of DOT1L in T cells relates to methylation of H3K79. Upon loss of DOT1L activity, H3K79me loss is slow and dependent on passive dilution mediated by cellular replication (Fig. 1C and fig. S6A) (28, 29, 58, 59). These slow kinetics severely hamper distinguishing early from late effects and direct from indirect effects of H3K79me, respectively. However, gaining more mechanistic insight into these effects could be useful for potential targeted therapies in the future. Looking at when cellular changes occurred following loss of H3K79me, surface expression of CD3, CD8, and CD5 and expression of EOMES were unaffected 2 days following treatment with DOT1Li SGC-0946 (H2K79me2 high), while all except EOMES were reduced after 8 days of expansion (H3K79me2 low; fig. S6A). These results suggest that manifestation of the molecular and phenotypic changes associated with the loss of DOT1L activity likely relates to the subsequent and progressive loss of H3K79me due to passive dilution.

To further test this model in an independent way, we modified the experimental protocol to take advantage of the slow turnover of H3K79me. In this genetic approach, instead of deletion of Dot1L in vitro, Cre-ERT2;Dot1Lfl/fl;OT-I mice were treated for 3 consecutive days with tamoxifen to induce deletion of Dot1L in vivo before T cell isolation. One day after the last treatment, denoted as day 0, CD8 T cells were isolated and analyzed. While Dot1L deletion was efficient (fig. S6, B and C), global H3K79me2 was not detectably altered in these cells (Fig. 6B and fig. S6D) nor in splenocytes harvested many days later, i.e., 13 days after the induction of deletion of Dot1L [denoted as day 9 in relation to day 0 in fig. S6 (E and F)]. The KO status of the splenocytes was confirmed by polymerase chain reaction (PCR), showing 96.5 ± 1.4% deletion efficiency (n = 10). Since DOT1L has been reported to have a relatively short half-life of ~2 hours (60, 61), this result supports the notion that upon loss of DOT1L protein, H3K79me2 loss in T cells is mainly dependent on replication-dependent dilution of posttranslationally modified histones by incorporation of newly synthesized unmodified ones. Coculture of the day 0 Dot1L KO H3K79me2+ CD8 T cells with B16F10-OVA-mCherry tumor cells revealed equally capable killing of target cells, compared to WT (Fig. 6C and fig. S6, G to J). When these Dot1L KO H3K79me2+ cells were activated and expanded for 8 days in vitro and then tested, the Dot1L KO cells did lose H3K79me2 and showed enhanced tumor cell killing (fig. S6, K to M), in line with what was observed following induction of Dot1L KO or DOT1L inhibition in vitro. Together, these findings support the idea that the loss of H3K79me is responsible for the molecular and cellular changes observed after Dot1L KO or DOT1Li. This indicates that the role of DOT1L in T cells is at least in part mediated via its canonical epigenetic writing function.

Although distinguishing direct from indirect effects of loss of H3K79me2 is challenging, we performed chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) on T cells treated with DMSO (0.1%, vehicle) or the DOT1Li SGC-0946 (10 μM) for 8 days to gain more insight into how H3K79me and its loss might affect the identity of CD8 T cells. While SGC-0649 can be used as a negative control compound for SGC-0946 to exclude off-target effects (26), we here used an independent DOT1Li (10 μM; EPZ-5676, Pinometostat) to confirm the on-target effects. Treatment with SGC-0946 led to undetectable levels of H3K79me similar to those in KO, whereas EPZ-5676 resulted in partial loss of H3K79me (fig. S6, N to O). While in previous studies much higher doses of the structurally related DOT1Li EPZ004777 were used to inhibit DOT1L in T cells (100 to 200 μM) (24), we used a maximum concentration of 10 μM. This concentration was sufficient to efficiently reduce H3K79me in mouse CD8 T cells and is within the range that has been commonly used as the highest dose to accomplish the loss of H3K79me marks, and higher concentrations have been reported to be toxic to cells that are otherwise known to be resistant to loss of DOT1L activity [e.g., see (12, 25, 62)].

To enable a comparative quantitative ChIP analysis of DOT1Li-treated and DOT1Li-untreated cells, we developed a spike-in protocol with an external reference that can be used for normalization independent of global changes in a sample. For that purpose, a yeast strain was generated that expresses hemagglutinin (HA)–tagged H3 and in which DOT1 is deleted to eliminate all H3K79me. When mixed with mouse T cell chromatin, and when in addition to the anti-H3K79me antibody an anti-HA antibody is coupled to the beads, the yeast chromatin is enriched independently of the mouse H3K79me chromatin (Fig. 6D and fig. S6, P to T). The spike-in approach is universal and convenient, and given the ease of yeast engineering, it can be easily modified for application to other epigenetic marks. Using this external spike-in method, we performed ChIP for H3K79me2, because this methylation state shows a clear demarcation of 5′ ends of gene bodies and correlates well with transcriptional activity (see below) (4, 6, 63–66). When normalized to the yeast spike-in, SGC-0946–treated CD8 T cells showed near-complete loss of H3K79me2 ChIP signal across the genome, while EPZ-5676 showed a partial loss (Fig. 6, E and F; fig. S6, U to W; and table S8). Inspection of the H3K79me2 levels around the transcriptional start sites (TSSs) of all protein-coding genes in EPZ-5676–treated cells showed a proportional loss of signal compared to that in DMSO-treated cells and a reduction in the number of H3K79me2-marked TSSs largely without the gain of new ones (Fig. 6F and fig. S6U). This indicates that partial DOT1L inhibition affected all H3K79me2 demarcated genes in an equal manner and that DOT1L in expanding T cells does not have a subset of preferred target genes that maintain WT levels of H3K79me2 when DOT1L activity becomes limiting.

The global intermediate loss of H3K79me2 in EPZ-5676–treated cells compared to near complete loss upon SGC-0946 treatment correlated with intermediate effects on mRNA expression (Fig. 6G). Positive and negative changes in gene expression were observed in DOT1Li-treated cells, and in agreement with the partial loss of H3K79me2 for EPZ-5676 treatment, the magnitude of changes was less pronounced in both compared to SGC-0946 treatment (correlation between both inhibitors R2 = 0.613; differential effect size, 1.8-fold; Fig. 6G). Apparently, in CD8 T cells, the loss of DOT1L affects the global H3K79me2 methylome and transcriptome in a dose-dependent manner. To investigate how DOT1L activity might affect gene expression, we first confirmed that H3K79me2 correlates with the level of gene transcription (fig. S6W, left) (63, 67). The H3K79me2 signal near the TSS correlated with transcript length normalized transcription (ρ = 0.6086). Partial DOT1L inhibition due to EPZ-5676 treatment led to partial and proportional loss of H3K79me2, with lowly expressed genes dropping to baseline levels and highly expressed genes dropping to intermediate levels of H3K79me2 (fig. S6W, middle). For the latter set of genes, lowering H3K79me2 to an intermediate level did not affect their expression, indicating a threshold level of H3K79me2 required for maintaining gene expression.

Overlaying the H3K79me2 signals around the TSS in DMSO-treated cells onto the mRNA changes in DOT1Li versus DMSO treatment revealed that H3K79me2 was also more enriched on highly transcribed genes [high mean fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM)] and nearly absent from lowly expressed genes (low FPKM) (Fig. 6H). Since up-regulation of gene expression was mostly observed for lowly expressed genes, which lacked H3K79me2, this suggests that in general, the gene derepression effect observed upon DOT1L inhibition was most likely not causally related to loss of H3K79me2. Within the more highly expressed genes, the largest FCs in expression were observed for genes that were down-regulated following SGC-0946 treatment (Fig. 6H). This suggests that DOT1L’s main impact is likely through promoting gene expression of a subset of the genes that it methylates. At this point, it is unknown which of the genes that DOT1L methylates directly require DOT1L and H3K79me2 for their expression and which genes are regulated by indirect effects. However, our results do suggest that among all the differentially expressed genes, a relatively small subset is likely to be directly regulated by H3K79me2.

CD8 T cells lacking DOT1L activity reversibly gain innate- and memory-like characteristics

To gain further insight into the potential direct and indirect targets of DOT1L, the relationship between H3K79me2 level and changes in RNA and protein abundance upon Dot1L-KO or DOT1Li is summarized for a selected set of factors that were consistently affected in Dot1L-KO and DOT1Li and at RNA and protein levels (Fig. 7A). The expression of the CD5, CD6, and CD8 surface markers, the transcriptional repressor BCL11B, and the cytokine receptor IL2RA are likely controlled by the catalytic activity of DOT1L, while the high H3K79me2 levels of their corresponding genes suggest that they may be among the direct targets of DOT1L. In contrast, the increased expression of granzymes and perforin is most likely caused by indirect effects because the genes harbor low levels of H3K79me2. A similar observation is found for certain molecules belonging to T-to-NK differentiation and cytolytic activity. To further validate some of these changes, the surface expression of innate-related factors KIT, NK1.1, and the IL-18 receptor subunit IL18R1, which synergizes with the IL-12 receptor to potently activate various immune cell subtypes (68), was analyzed by flow cytometry. The surface expression of IL18R1 was uniformly (Fig. 7B) and significantly increased both in Dot1L KO and SGC-treated CD8 T cells (Fig. 7C). Similarly, Dot1L KO and SGC-treated CD8 T cells increased their surface expression of both KIT (Fig. 7D) and NK1.1 (Fig. 7E), thereby confirming that the increase in total protein and RNA abundance, as in bulk cells, led to increased surface expression in most cells. Of note, increased expression of the IL-12 and IL-18 receptors, as well as up-regulation of granzyme A and perforin (Prf1) seen in the absence of DOT1L, has also been observed in helped memory CD8 T cells, suggesting that T cell help promotes an innate-like recall function of memory T cells (32). Therefore, our results suggest that the loss of DOT1L activity causes CD8 T cells to differentiate toward to a more innate- and memory-like state.

To better understand the altered differentiation state, expanded CD8 T cells were stimulated with the proinflammatory cytokines IL-12 and IL-18. Unexpectedly, the fraction CD8 T cells that produced IFN-γ during 5 hours of stimulation was significantly decreased in both KO and SGC-0946–treated CD8 T cells (Fig. 7F). Stimulating the cells with phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) and ionomycin reproduced these results, indicating that DOT1L affects the response of T cells to activating stimuli independent of surface receptors. Nevertheless, both Dot1L-KO and DOT1Li CD8 T cells did respond to IL-12/18 stimulation, as their surface expression of IL18r1 (fig. S7, A and B) and CD69 (Fig. 7G) was increased after 24 hours, both to levels comparable to WT and DMSO controls. In contrast, stimulation with PMA/ionomycin led to a significant decrease in CD69 expression in both conditions (Fig. 7G), suggesting that stimulation with IL-12/18 could partially overcome the defect in Dot1L-KO and DOT1Li T cells.

IL-12/18 stimulation led to up-regulation of KIT in WT and DMSO-treated CD8 T cells. In contrast, expression of KIT was down-regulated in KO and SGC-treated cells (fig. S7, C and D). This opposing effect was specific to KIT, as NK1.1 expression was modestly elevated by IL-12/18 in the absence and presence of DOT1L (fig. S7, E and F). Unexpectedly, IL-12/18 stimulation also significantly up-regulated the surface expression of the CD8 coreceptor in all conditions (fig. S7, G and H), restoring CD8 expression in Dot1L-KO and DOT1Li cells to levels similar to those in WT cells before stimulation. Last, the viability of KO and SGC-treated CD8 T cells was higher than controls after IL-12/18 and PMA/ionomycin-based stimulation (Fig. 7H). Expression of the exhaustion marker PD-1 was not affected after IL-12/18 but decreased upon PMA/ionomycin-based stimulation (Fig. 7I). Combined, these results highlight that Dot1L-KO and DOT1Li CD8 T cells are in an altered cellular state compared to DOT1L-proficient cells. They respond differently to IL-12/18 stimulation, as IFN-γ production was down-modulated and viability was higher, while expression of the activation marker CD69 was unaffected.

While further studies are needed to deconstruct the mechanistic network underpinning the molecular and cellular changes, our data provide a valuable resource for untangling the downstream effects of DOT1L and for further tuning of T cells to harness the improved cell-intrinsic cytotoxic potential upon DOT1L targeting.

DISCUSSION

Deconstructing the role of epigenetic mechanisms in cytotoxic CD8 T cells is crucial to understand CD8 T cell cellular plasticity and differentiation trajectories and determine how epigenome perturbation can be exploited to modulate their immunological responses. The histone methyltransferase DOT1L is emerging as a key epigenetic and regulatory player in cellular differentiation, particularly in immune cells (69, 70). In various mouse models, the absence of DOT1L leads to altered cellular states, with a proposed role for DOT1L, and by inference H3K79me, in establishing an epigenetically based differentiation barrier to maintain cell identity. However, the cell-intrinsic role of DOT1L in CD8 T cell remains poorly defined (4, 24, 25).

Here, we combined three different conditional models to establish the cell-intrinsic role of DOT1L in mature CD8 T cells in the mouse: deletion in vitro, inhibition in vitro, and deletion in vivo. Together, these findings suggest that DOT1L maintains the epigenetic and transcriptomic state of CD8 T cells, prevents acquisition of innate-like characteristics, and restricts a cell-intrinsic cytotoxic tumor cell killing potential.

Our findings that DOT1L-deficient CD8 T cells are highly viable and potent killers in vitro and in vivo provide an alternative perspective to the role of DOT1L proposed in the study by Bian et al. (24). In that study, lowered DOT1L-mediated H3K79me in T cells led to increased CD8 T cell apoptosis and compromised antitumor immunity. The different outcomes might relate to the different experimental models used. Bian et al. used a mouse model in which Dot1L is deleted early during T cell development and used a different DOT1L inhibition regimen. Here, we used genetic ablation and pharmacological inhibition and focused on the cell-intrinsic role of DOT1L activity in mature CD8 T cells in a model relevant for adoptive T cell therapy. The finding that ablation of DOT1L in mature CD8 T cells can enhance memory-related properties and cytotoxic activity toward cancer cells is a strong starting point for exploring strategies for improvement of T cell responses by epigenetic perturbation.

Our results show that DOT1L controls CD8 T cell fate via its catalytic activity and that the loss-of-function phenotypes are correlated with loss of H3K79me. This strongly supports the idea that chromatin-based epigenetic mechanisms of gene regulation play a central role (Fig. 7J). DOT1L likely operates by nudging gene expression rather than by on-off switches because the changes in gene expression upon treatment with DOT1Li are relatively modest and dynamically dose dependent. This also suggests that the altered cell state and function of CD8 T cells upon inactivation of DOT1L is likely mediated by a combinatorial effect of multiple changes. What remains to be further explored are the mechanisms by which blocking DOT1L rewires molecular and cellular networks and which of these components are the cause or consequence of this altered differentiation. The integrative analysis of transcriptome, proteome, and quantitative changes in H3K79me levels using a novel spike-in method provides an important framework for identifying indirect and candidate direct effects.

Upon early in vivo deletion of Dot1L, CD8 T cells differentiate prematurely toward a memory-phenotype state in an antigen-independent manner (4). A propensity to differentiate to memory-like cells would be compatible with the enhanced and accelerated cytotoxic response of Dot1L-ablated mature T cells and the increased viability and survival of T cells after challenge with tumor cells. However, in our current in vitro deletion model, differentiation is mainly driven by the activation of T cells to allow for replication-mediated loss of H3K79me upon Dot1L deletion, resulting in effector-memory and memory-like cell states. Nevertheless, in Dot1L KO, the transcriptome was positively enriched for genes annotated as antigen response and “T to NK cell” and showed overlap with changes seen in CD8 T cells with an innate or memory recall function (e.g., up-regulation of granzyme A, perforin, and IL-12/18 receptors) (32, 71). This suggests that loss of Dot1L in CD8 T cells initiates a noncanonical differentiation trajectory that results in up-regulation of a subset of factors associated with efficient cytotoxic responses. However, activation of DOT1L-ablated OT-I CD8 T cells by target cells, as well as their increased cytotoxicity toward tumor cells in vitro, was still dependent on the TCR-antigen interaction [see MOCK controls in figs. S3 (I to O) and S6M] indicating functional characteristics of their T cell identity. Of note, DOT1L loss was recently shown to also control differentiation of bona fide NK cells (10).

Dot1L KO CD8 T cells showed enhanced tumor cell control in vitro despite the reduction in surface expression of TCR and CD8 before exposure to target cells and reduced expression of the activation marker CD69 upon challenge with target cells. Several factors might contribute to these phenotypic and functional changes. CD5, a negative regulator of TCR signaling (34, 72), is down-regulated, which might compensate for the lower TCR and CD8 levels. Down-regulation of CD6 might also be involved. CD6 has recently been proposed as a negative regulator in CD8 T cells, and perturbing its interaction with ligands by blocking antibodies improves antitumor responses (54, 56, 73). Little is known about how CD6 is regulated, but here, we identify DOT1L as a factor that promotes CD6 expression. Another factor is that the high-affinity OT-I TCR (74) might compensate for the lower TCR expression to maintain the normal threshold for TCR stimulation. Of note, DOT1L inhibition has been shown to elevate the TCR stimulation threshold in human T cells, specifically in low-avidity T cell responses, which is related to the increased ERK phosphatase DUSP6 expression (25). In our study, we observed that CD8 T cells lacking DOT1L fail to deposit normal surface levels of the TCR and CD8 coreceptor and show reduced expression of TCR signaling signature genes. These changes upon loss of DOT1L potentially provide an independent complementary explanation for the reduced TCR-mediated activation threshold upon DOT1L inhibition, which warrants further study.

The noncanonical differentiation state of CD8 T cells lacking DOT1L includes the up-regulation of factors associated with NK cells, including NK1.1, KIT, and the cytokine receptor IL18r1. Stimulation with IL-12 and IL-18, which synergize to activate several immune cell types (68), revealed that DOT1L-ablated T cells, while similarly activated on the basis of CD69 expression, had an impaired IFN-γ production and elevated viability compared to WT CD8 T cells. IL-12/18 partially reversed the innate-like signature, as IL18R1 and KIT expressions were similar between WT and KO after stimulation. KIT expression was down-regulated in KO CD8 T cells, in line with the role of IL-12 in down-modulating KIT (75), while WT CD8 T cells increased KIT expression. In addition, while the CD8 coreceptor is generally down-regulated upon activation (76), IL-12/18 increased the surface expression of CD8 in both WT and KO. This highlights that IL-12/18 supplementation might be used to revert the decreased CD8 expression observed in KO CD8 T cells. However, follow-up studies will be required to decipher the mechanistic underpinning of these changes and the interplay that DOT1L might have with them.

T cells and NK cells cannot always be developmentally separated but may instead represent a continuum of cell fates. T cells and NK cells show many similarities in their cytotoxic machinery and how they differentiate upon activation (77, 78). Moreover, specific subsets of CD8 T cells express markers associated with NK cells, and this has been associated with enhanced cytotoxic activity (79, 80). Recently, the dual blockade of PD-1 and LAG-3 in mouse CD8 T cells was shown to lead to expression of NK receptors and a stronger effector CD8 T cell program (81–84). NK cells are also emerging as promising targets for engineering, offering adoptive cell immunotherapy strategies that are complementary to and sometimes superior to those based on CD8 and CD4 T cells (85, 86). The various mechanisms underlying the induction of NK-like behavior in CD8 T cells and the implications of the observed molecular changes for the cellular activities are still incompletely understood (78, 84). Our studies point to DOT1L as an epigenetic factor that warrants the identity and behavior of CD8 T cells and restricts the acquisition of NK-like characteristics in CD8 T cells. Future studies will be aimed at determining the contribution of the various observed changes to the enhanced cytotoxic killing of Dot1L KO CD8 T cells. However, the net result is that loss of DOT1L activity in mature OT-I CD8 T cells improves their cytotoxic activity toward tumor cells in a cell-intrinsic manner in vitro.

Limitations of the study

Loss of Dot1L in vitro was well tolerated, and upon adoptive cell transfer, Dot1L KO CD8 T cells were as proficient as WT CD8 T cells in controlling tumor outgrowth. While inactivation of DOT1L increased the cytotoxic potential of CD8 T cells, it did not improve the in vivo cytotoxicity during tumor control or following vaccination. That no increased tumor control was observed could relate to several factors. The B16-OVA model is highly aggressive, rapidly leading to escape, which may have obscured potential benefits of loss of DOT1L. Another potential explanation lies in the response to cytokines. Stimulation of CD8 T cells with the proinflammatory cytokines IL-12 and IL-18 partially suppressed the up-regulation of innate-like markers and the down-regulation of CD8 expression. Therefore, these cytokines and potentially other cell and cytokine interactions in the tumor microenvironment or secondary lymphoid organs might modify the behavior of Dot1L-KO CD8 T cells. It will be important to delineate these interactions and determine how the increased cytotoxic potential by loss of DOT1L can be harnessed in vivo.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Mice

The derivation of Lck-Cre;Dot1Lfl/fl;OT-I mice has been described previously (4, 87, 88). Rcm2;Dot1Lfl/fl mice were derived by crossing Cre-ERT2 mice with Dot1Lfl/fl mice. Cre-ERT2 mice have been described elsewhere and were generated by inserting Cre fused to the human estrogen receptor (Cre-ERT2) into the ROSA-26 locus (87). Dot1Lfl/fl mice were derived from the Dot1Ltm1a(KOMP)Wtsi line generated by the Wellcome Trust Sanger Institute and obtained from the Knockout Mouse Project (KOMP) Repository (www.komp.org) (88). Cre-ERT2;Dot1Lfl/fl;OT-I mice were generated by crossing Cre-ERT2;Dot1Lfl/fl mice with OT-I (B6J carrying the OT-I TCR transgenes) mice [gifted by the Ton Schumacher group (Netherlands Cancer Institute), originally from the Jackson Laboratory). All strains were bred, and all experiments were performed in house, under specific pathogen–free conditions. Mice used in experiments were between 6 weeks and 10 months old and gender and age matched between experimental groups. All experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the NKI (Centrale Commissie Dierproeven number: AVD30100202215981) and performed in accordance with institutional, national, and European guidelines for animal care and use.

CD8 T cell enrichment and expansion

An overview of reagents used can be found in the Supplementary Materials (table S1). Spleens were harvested, and single-cell suspensions were made by meshing cells through a 70-μm strainer (BD Biosciences Falcon). Erylysis was performed by incubating the cells in red blood cell lysis buffer (0.15 M NH4Cl, 10 mM KHCO3, and 0.2 mM EDTA) for 3 min on ice. Cell suspensions were enriched for T cells by Pan T cell isolation (Miltenyi) or CD8 (Dynabeads untouched) isolation (Thermo Fisher Scientific). These enrichment procedures were performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Before CD8 T cell expansion, 12-well plates were coated with aCD3 [5 μg/ml in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS); BD Biosciences] and incubated overnight (o/n) at 4°C. CD8 T cells were expanded by resuspension in whole T cell media [RPMI (Gibco), 10% FBS heat-inactivated, 2 mM l-glutamine, 28.6 mM 2-mercaptoethanol, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, and 10 mM Hepes, 0.5mg/ml Penicillin-Streptomycin] supplemented with aCD28 (22 μg/ml; BD Biosciences) and subsequent plating on PBS-rinsed aCD3 precoated wells at 1 to 2 million cells per well in 2 ml and cultured at 37°C with 5% CO2. For Cre-ERT2 CD8 T cell experiments, the media was further supplemented with 10 nM 4-OHT (Sigma-Aldrich). One day after plating, IL-2 (Novartis) in whole T cell media was supplemented to a final concentration of 50 U/ml. From day 2 until day 8 (after plating), CD8 T cells were counted and resuspended in fresh whole T cell media supplemented with IL-2 (50 U/ml), IL-7 (5 ng/ml; PeproTech), and IL-15 (5 ng/ml; PeproTech), every other day. For inhibitor studies, OT-I CD8 T cells were treated with 10 μM EPZ-5676 (Selleck), 10 μM SGC-0946 (Bio-Connect), or DMSO (0.1%) from day 0. Resuspension in fresh cytokine-supplemented T cell media with inhibitors or DMSO was performed every other day, when CD8 T cells were counted. For analysis of primary, Dot1L-KO nonexpanded CD8 T cells, Dot1L-floxed and Dot1L-WT Cre-ERT2 OT-I mice were treated with tamoxifen (Sigma-Aldrich) in corn oil (20 mg/ml; dose, 75 mg/kg) for 3 consecutive days. Twenty-four hours after the last treatment, mice were euthanized, and splenic CD8 T cells were enriched using the Dynabeads Untouched Mouse CD8 Cells Kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Generation of lentivirus

An overview of all plasmids can be found in the Supplementary Materials (table S1). The FlipGFP-T2A-mCherry gene insert (89) (Addgene #124428) was cloned into a lentiviral backbone (90) to create LentiV5-FlipGFP-Puro. HEK293T cells were transfected with LentiV5-FlipGFP-Puro, pLenti-dOVA(SIINFEKL)-Hygro, or pLenti-MS2-P65-HSF1-Hygro control vector (OVA and MOCK control vector were gifts from the laboratory of D. Peeper, NKI), combined with lentiviral packaging plasmids pMD2G, pMDLg, and pRSV-Rev (91) (Addgene #12259, #12251, and #12253) in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% FBS and P/S and cultured at 37°C 5% CO2. At 24 and 48 hours, the supernatant was collected, 0.45-μm filtered, and stored at −80°C for later use in viral transductions.

Generation and culture of mCherry-positive B16F10-OVA and B16F10-MOCK control target cells

B16F10 cells (gift from the laboratory of D. Peeper, NKI) were cultured in DMEM (Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS and P/S at 37°C 5% CO2. The cells were transduced o/n with lentivirus containing LentiV5-FlipGFP-Puro, followed by 2-day puromycin (1 μg/ml; Invivogen) selection. After puromycin selection, clones were generated and measured for mCherry positivity by microscopy. Selected clones were subsequently transduced o/n with lentivirus containing Lenti-dOVA-Hygro or the control insert, followed by 7 days hygromycin B (200 μg/ml; Life Technologies) selection. FlipGFP-OVA pools were treated for 48 hours with IFN-γ (5 μg/ml), gently treated with Accutase for 2 min, collected, washed with PBS, stained for anti-SIINFEKL-H2Kb (BioLegend, 141605), and fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) sorted on the FACSAria Fusion (BD Biosciences) for high OVA peptide presentation on H2Kb.

Live cell imaging cytotoxicity assay

Before seeding, B16F10 cells were treated for 48 hours with IFN-γ (5 μg/ml) to up-regulate major histocompatibility complex I expression (92). Subsequently, cells were gently detached by treatment with Accutase for 2 min at 37°C and collected by rinsing with DMEM (Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS and P/S. A total of 7500 B16F10-OVA cells were seeded per well in 96-well μClear plates (Greiner) and incubated at 37°C 5% CO2 for 4 to 5 hours in target cell media. Thirty minutes before imaging, the supernatant was aspirated, and harvested CD8 T cells in T cell media supplemented with IL-2 (50 U/ml; Novartis) were added in the corresponding numbers and ratios to the target cells. Live cell imaging was thereafter performed using the Incucyte ZOOM imaging system. Bright-field and mCherry images were obtained every 4 hours using the following settings: red 800 ms, 1392 × 1040 at 1.22 μm/pixel, Nikon 10× objective, dual color model 4459 filter module. After acquisition, time-course movies were generated, and bright-field and mCherry channel–specific images were exported using the Incucyte ZOOM software (TIFF file format). The viable cell confluence over time was quantified using Fiji (ImageJ 2.3.0/1.53q /Java 1.8.0_322) and a custom build script optimized for quantification of mCherry from exported images. All images of a plate were used to generate a background subtraction file, sigma of Gaussian blur was set on 3, threshold bias at 0.5, opening iterations at 2, and dilation iterations at 2. Quantified data were plotted, and the area under the curve was quantified for statistical comparisons for killing capacity.

Fixed timepoint cytotoxicity assay

B16F10 cells were treated with IFN-γ and collected as described above. A total of 70,000 B16F10-OVA cells were plated in 12-well plates (Greiner) and incubated at 37°C, 5% CO2 for 4 to 5 hours in DMEM (Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS and P/S. Media was aspirated, and expanded CD8 T cells were added to the target cells in corresponding ratios to target cells in complete T cell media supplemented with IL-2 (50 U/ml). After either 6- or 48-hour coculture at 37°C, 5% CO2, CD8 T cells were carefully collected and used in flow cytometry experiments. For 48-hour incubated cocultures, target cells were fixed on the plate using 4% formaldehyde (diluted from 37%; Honeywell) in PBS for at least 30 min and subsequently stained in crystal violet staining solution (0.5%; in Milli-Q 20% methanol) for 20 min at room temperature. After staining, the wells were washed three times by gently submerging the plate in water and air dried for 2 to 3 days. The plates were subsequently imaged using the Epson Perfection V750 PRO scanner (dual lid, 24-bit color, 300 dots per inch). Degranulation and cytokine production by CD8 T cells were determined by coculturing B16F10-OVA cells with expanded CD8 T cells as described above for 5 hours, in the presence of GolgiStop (BD Biosciences), monensin (BioLegend), and CD107a (1:200) as previously described (36). CD8 T cells were collected by careful pipetting and subsequently stained for flow cytometry as described above.

In vivo tumor control

Recipient CD45.1 mice, female and aged 8 to 12 weeks, were injected subcutaneously with 200,000 B16F10-OVA tumor cells in PBS/Matrigel (1:1; 100 μl) in one flank. At day 7 after injection of tumor cells, all mice were subjected to 5-Gy total body irradiation (SmART+ irradiator, Precision). At day 8, recipient mice received either HBSS vehicle or 4 million in vitro–expanded Dot1L-KO or WT OT-I CD8 T cells (pooled cells from four female donor mice, eight recipient mice per arm, age matched groups, blinded, and cage randomized). The number of mice used per treatment arm was based on a power analysis accommodating for n = 1 drop-out per group. After adoptive cell transfer, the mice were treated 3 consecutive days (days 8 to 10) with 100,000 IU of IL-2 (Novartis) by intraperitoneal injections. Tumor size was monitored, and tail vein punctures were taken over time to determine the presence and quantity of adoptively transferred OT-I CD8 T cells. At the endpoint, which was either welfare related or after reaching a tumor size of 1000 mm3, mice were euthanized, and tumor, spleen, and blood samples were used for flow cytometry analysis. Spleen and blood samples were erylyzed on ice (3 and 30 min, respectively). Mice that died prematurely were excluded from sample work-up but included in tumor growth and survival plots if they developed tumors of near endpoint size. Mice that suffered welfare-related issues with near endpoint size tumors were not excluded from the analysis. Mice that did not develop a tumor were excluded from the analysis in its entirety. Leukocytes were enriched by mashing the tumors through a 70-μm filter. Filters were rinsed with media, and 10 ml of Ficoll (Sigma-Aldrich) was carefully added in the bottom of the 50-ml tube containing the mashed cell suspension, bringing the total around 35 to 40 ml. The tubes were centrifuged for 20 min at 536g at 4°C without brakes. The leukocyte-rich interphase was collected and used for staining. Two million splenocytes and all the remaining blood- and tumor-enriched leukocytes were stained for flow cytometry analysis. Tumor-enriched leukocyte samples derived with less than 150 cells in their respective parent gate were excluded from the analysis.

In vivo cytotoxicity assay

Dot1L-floxed and Dot1L-WT Cre-ERT2 OT-I mice were treated with tamoxifen (Sigma-Aldrich) in corn oil (20 mg/ml, dose 75 mg/kg) for 3 consecutive days. Twenty-four hours after the last treatment, mice were euthanized, and splenic CD8 T cells were enriched using the Dynabeads Untouched Mouse CD8 Cell Kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The in vivo cytotoxicity assay was adjusted from Ahrends et al. (32). Briefly, recipient mice were injected with HBSS (vehicle control) or 50,000 Dot1L KO or WT OT-I CD8 T cells in HBSS retro-orbitally, 10 mice per arm. Mice were subsequently vaccinated with OVA-HELP vaccine via tattooing of one paw at day 0 (injection), day 3, and day 6. Control mice were tattooed with Tris-EDTA (TE) buffer. Over the course of the experiment, blood samples were collected to monitor the expansion of injected OT-I CD8 T cells. At day 9 or day 10, recipient mice received a 1:1 ratio carboxyfluorescein diacetate succinimidyl ester (CFSE) high (2 μM) and CFSE low (0.1 μM) labeled and SIINFEKL (1 μg/ml; Invivogen) peptide-pulsed splenocytes, derived from WT mice. Using flow cytometry, specific killing normalized to unpulsed cells was calculated, according to Ahrends et al. (32). Blood and spleen samples at endpoint were collected for evaluation of the transferred cells by flow cytometry. Groups were age matched, blinded, and cage randomized. The number of mice used per treatment arm was based on a power analysis accommodating for n = 1 drop-out per group.

Flow cytometry

Enriched, expanded, or cocultured CD8 T cell single-cell suspensions (typically 200,000 cells per stain) were washed with PBS and subsequently stained with Zombie NIR in PBS for 30 min on ice. Cells were pelleted and subsequently fluorescently labeled by antibodies in FACS buffer (0.5% bovine serum albumin and 2 mM EDTA in PBS) at dilutions indicated (table S1). Intracellular staining was performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol, using the Transcription Factor Buffer kit (BD Biosciences). H3K79me2 staining was performed according to the method described previously (4). Briefly, after cell permeabilization, cells were resuspended in Transcription Factor Buffer kit Perm/Wash buffer supplemented with 0.25% SDS and stained with anti-H3K79me2 (Millipore) followed by 1:1000 secondary antibody staining (goat anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 488; Invitrogen) in Perm/Wash. Annexin V (AnnV) and 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) staining was performed by staining 50,000 to 100,000 CD8 T cells in 50-μl AnnV binding buffer [10 mM Hepes (pH7.4), 0.14 M NaCl, and 2.5 mM CaCl2, freshly prepared from 10× stock] at indicated dilutions of AnnV and DAPI (table S1) for 20 min at room temperature (dark). Subsequently, the samples were diluted three times in binding buffer and directly measured. Flow cytometry was performed using the LSR-Fortessa II (BD Biosciences). Data were analyzed using FlowJo v10.9.0 (BD Biosciences). Enriched CD8 T cells with aberrant phenotypes at day 0, e.g., phenotypes suggesting unintended immune challenge or genetic aberrations in our specific pathogen–free housed mice, were excluded from further analysis.

RNA sequencing sample preparation

CD8 T cells were activated and expanded for 8 days as described above in whole T cell media. A total of 1 × 106 cells were transferred to ribonuclease (RNase)/deoxyribonuclease (DNase)–free Eppendorf tubes, pelleted at 500g for 5 min, washed in 750 μl of PBS, centrifuged for 10 min at 600g at 4°C, and resuspended in RLT buffer (QIAGEN) supplemented with 1% 2-mercaptoethanol. Samples were stored at −80°C until further use.

RNA sequencing sample work-up and library preparation

Total RNA was isolated according to the manufacturer’s protocol using the RNAeasy mini kit (QIAGEN). Quality and quantity control was performed on the 2100 Bioanalyzer on the Agilent RNA nanochip. For Dot1L KO and WT and EPZ-5676 (10 μM)– and DMSO (0.1%)–treated CD8 T cells, RNA samples with an RNA integrity number > 8 were subjected to library preparation. For SGC-0946 (10 μM)– and DMSO (0.1%)–treated CD8 T cells, the RNA samples with an integrity number > 6 were subjected to library preparation. Strand-specific cDNA libraries were generated using the TruSeq Stranded mRNA sample preparation kit (Illumina) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The libraries were analyzed for size and quantity of cDNAs on a 2100 Bioanalyzer using a DNA 7500 chip (Agilent), diluted, and pooled in multiplex sequencing pools. The paired-end 51-bp reads were sequenced using the NovaSeq 6000.

RNA sequencing preprocessing

Strand-specific RNA reads (11 to 33 million reads per sample), 51-bp paired-end, were aligned against the mouse reference genome (Ensembl build 38) using TopHat (version 2.1, bowtie version 1.1). TopHat was supplied with a Gene Transfer File (GTF; Ensembl version 77) and was supplied with the following parameters: “prefilter-multihits –no-coverage-search – bowtie1 –library-type fr-firststrand.” To count the number of reads per gene, a custom script which is based on the same ideas as HTSeq-count has been used. A list of the total number of uniquely mapped reads for each gene that is present in the provided GTF file was generated.

RNA sequencing data analysis

Subsequent analysis was performed in R version 4.2.2 with Bioconductor packages from release 3.16. Genes that have no expression across all samples within the dataset were filtered out. Data were prefiltered, and the analysis was restricted to the genes that have at least 20 counts in at least four samples to exclude very low abundance genes. Principal components analysis was performed using the “prcomp” function on variance-stabilizing transformed data with the “vst” function from the DESeq2 package (1.38.3) using default arguments and plotted by using ggplot2 package (3.5.0). Sample to sample distances obtained using the “dist” function on variance-stabilizing transformed data were subjected to hierarchical cluster analysis and dendrogram preparation. Hierarchical cluster analysis for the samples was performed using the “hclust” function with default arguments. The dendrogram was made by using the “dendro_data” function from ggdendro package (0.2.0). Normalized counts from DESeqDataSet from the DESeq2 package were subjected to calculate correlation among the samples by using the “cor” function using the Spearman method. Differential gene expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 package and default arguments with the design set either according to the genotypes (WT and Dot1L KO) or treatment conditions (DMSO and SGC-0946) and plotted by using ggplot2 package (3.5.0). Adaptive effect size shrinkage was performed with the ashr package version 2.2.63 to control for the lower information content in low abundance transcripts. Genes were considered to be differentially expressed when the P value of the negative binomial Wald test was below 0.01 after the Benjamini-Hochberg multiple testing correction. Pearson correlation between log2 FCs from different comparisons (KO versus WT and SGC versus DMSO) was calculated by using the “cor.test” function and plotted by using the “ggscatter” function under ggpubr package (0.6.0). Differentially expressed genes are listed in table S2.

For the effect size comparison between EPZ-5676 and SGC-0946 treatments, normalized count tables for EPZ and SGC treatment were merged retaining the intersection of genes. For the symmetric difference set of genes, we confirmed low expression. To filter out genes with low expression, genes that did not have more than 10 counts in at least four samples were removed for further analysis. The DESeq2 R/Bioconductor package was used to test for differential expression using “~batch + treatment” as the design argument and otherwise default argument. Results from a negative binomial Wald test were retrieved with “contrast = c(“treatment,” “EPZ”/“SGC,” “DMSO”), alpha = 0.05, lfcThreshold = 0.1.” A gene was considered to be differentially expressed when the false discovery (FDR)–corrected P value was below 0.05. To compare effect sizes between EPZ and SGC treatment, a total least squares coefficient was obtained using the “prcomp” R function.

For visualization, the mean library size–corrected expression value for each gene between all samples (BaseMean) was plotted. To enable quantitative comparisons with ChIP-seq data (described below), these gene counts were further normalized for transcript length using the “fpkm” function from the DESeq2 package (1.38.3) using default arguments but otherwise analyzed as described above.

Gene set enrichment analysis

GSEA was performed using the software provided by the UC San Diego and Broad institute (93) in combination with v2022.1.Mm of the Molecular Signatures Database and v2022.1.Mm for symbols. GO analysis performed in GSEA includes molecular function, biological processes, and cellular components. Phenotype permutation was set at 1000 times, and the weighted signal to noise statistical approach was implemented to rank genes. For this study, we focused on biological processes and selected features based on a nominal P value < 0.05 and an FDR q value < 0.25 and relevance to CD8 T cell function. Moreover, GSEA-based curated gene set enrichment was performed to compare enrichment in DOT1L-ablated or DOT1L-inhibited cells with documented cell types and cellular states. Similarly, a cutoff was set at nominal P value < 0.05 and an FDR q value < 0.25. GO and curated GSEA and signature gene sets used in this study are listed in tables S3, S4, S6, and S7.

Proteomics

CD8 T cells were activated and expanded for 8 days in whole T cell media as described above. For protein digestion, snap-frozen cell pellets (2.5 × 106 cells; kept at −80°C until used) were lysed in 5% SDS lysis buffer, boiled, and sonicated. Aliquots corresponding to 50 μg of protein were digested using S-Trap micro-columns (ProtiFi) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. In short, samples were reduced and alkylated using dithiothreitol (20 mM, 15 min, 55°C) and indole-3-acetic acid (40 mM, 10 min). The samples were acidified, and a methanol triethylammonium bicarbonate (TEAB) buffer was added, before loading on the S-Trap column. Trapped proteins were washed four times with the methanol TEAB buffer and then digested for 2 hours at 47°C using trypsin (Sigma-Aldrich). Digested peptides were eluted and dried in a vacuum centrifuge before liquid chromatography (LC)–mass spectrometry analysis.

For Dot1L WT and KO CD8 T cells, before mass spectrometry analysis, the peptides were reconstituted in 2% formic acid. Peptide mixtures were analyzed by nano LC–tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) on an Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer equipped with an EASY-NLC 1200 system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Samples were directly loaded onto the analytical column (ReproSil-Pur 120 C18-AQ, 2.4 μm, 75 μm by 500 mm, packed in-house). Solvent A was 0.1% formic acid/water, and solvent B was 0.1% formic acid/80% acetonitrile. Samples were eluted from the analytical column at a constant flow of 250 nl/min in a 90-min gradient, containing a 78-min linear increase from 6 to 30% solvent B, followed by a 12-min wash at 90% solvent B. The Exploris 480 was run in data-independent acquisition (DIA) mode, as described above.

For DMSO- and SGC-0946–treated CD8 T cells, before mass spectrometry analysis, the peptides were reconstituted in 0.1% formic acid and loaded on the Evotip Pure (Evosep). Peptide mixtures were analyzed by nano LC-MS/MS on an Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer equipped with an Evosep One LC system. Peptides were separated using the preprogrammed gradient (extended method, 88-min gradient) on an EV1137 (Evosep) column with an EV1086 (Evosep) emitter. The Exploris 480 was run in DIA mode, with full MS resolution set to 120,000 at mass/charge ratio 200, MS1 mass range was set from 350 to 1400, normalized AGC target was 300%, and the maximum injection time (IT) was 45 ms. Data-independent acquisition (DIA) was performed on precursors from 400 to 1000 in 48 windows of 13.5 Da with an overlap of 1 Da. Resolution was set to 30,000, and normalized collision energy (CE) was 27.

Raw data were analyzed by DIA-NN (version 1.8) (94) without a spectral library and with “Deep learning” option enabled. The SwissProt Mus Musculus database (17,082 entries, release 2021_07) was added for the library-free search. The quantification strategy was set to robust LC (high accuracy), and the match-between-runs (MBR) option was enabled. The other settings were kept at the default values. Protein group abundances were extracted from the DIA-NN result files, imported into Perseus (version 2.0.7.0) (95), and log2-transformed. Values were filtered for the presence in at least three of four replicates in at least one condition. Missing values were replaced by imputation based on a normal distribution (width: 0.3 and downshift: 2.4). Differentially expressed proteins were determined using a Student’s t test (minimal threshold: FDR < 0.05 and log2FC > 0.6 or < −0.6). A list of differentially expressed proteins can be found in table S5.

Box plots showing the mRNA changes of three groups of proteins that were differentially down-regulated, up-regulated, or not significantly changed were generated using ggplot2 package (3.5.0). The interquartile range (IQR) hinges correspond to first and third quartiles, whiskers show 1.5 IQR from upper and lower hinge, and the central horizontal line within the box represents the median value of the log2 FC in RNA expression. Significance was determined using Wilcoxon pairwise statistical analysis.

H3K79me mass spectrometry sample preparation and data analysis

To measure H3K79me levels, samples were prepared and analyzed in the same way as described previously (96) with the following exceptions. Peptide mixtures were analyzed by nano LC-MS/MS on a Thermo Orbitrap Fusion hybrid mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) equipped with an EASY-NLC 1200 system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Samples were directly loaded onto the analytical column (ReproSil-Pur 120 C18-AQ, 2.4 μm, 75 μm by 500 mm, packed in-house). Solvent A was 0.1% formic acid/water, and solvent B was 0.1% formic acid/80% acetonitrile. Samples were eluted from the analytical column at a constant flow of 250 nl/min in a 45-min gradient, containing a 30-min linear increase from 10 to 50% solvent B, followed by a 15-min wash at 90% solvent B. Nanospray was achieved using the Nanospray Flex Ion source with a liquid junction setup at 1.95 kV. The instrument was run in top speed mode with 2-s cycles. Parameters for quantification of the different methylation states were optimized using a set of four purified synthetic peptides with the sequence EIAQDFK*TDLR (K*: K-me0, -me1, -me2, and -me3), as described previously (96).

ChIP-seq sample preparation

After 8 days of expansion in DMSO-, EPZ-5676–, or SGC-0946–containing media (final concentration, 10 μM), 2.5 × 106 CD8 T cells were cross-linked in whole T cell media supplemented with 1% methanol-free formaldehyde (Thermo Fisher Scientific) by incubating at room temperature for 10 min under gentle agitation. Cross-linking was neutralized by adding glycine (final concentration, 125 mM) and incubating for 5 min. The fixed samples were centrifuged for 5 min at 600g, washed once using PBS + protease inhibitor cocktail (PIC; Roche), and resuspended in PBS + PIC + 10% FBS. Samples then were transferred to RNase/DNase-free Eppendorf tubes, centrifuged for 5 min at 600g, aspirated, snap-frozen, and stored at −80°C till subsequent use.

ChIP-seq yeast spike-in preparation

To normalize all ChIP reads, a yeast spike-in strain was generated, in which one gene copy encoding for histone H3 (HHT2) was genomically tagged with 3xHA (using plasmid pFvL160; Addgene #64753), and DOT1 was replaced with a NatMX selection cassette. This yeast strain (NKI2575; MATa lys2∆0 trp1∆63 his3∆200 ade2∆::hisG ura3∆0 leu2∆0 met15∆0 ADE2-TEL-VR URA3-TEL-VIIL dot1∆::NATMX set1∆::KANMX hht2::HHT2-LoxP-3xHA-HYG-LoxP-3xT7) was cultured in standard Yeast Extract Peptone Dextrose (YEPD) media at 30°C to an OD660 (optical density at 660 nm) of 0.5 before cross-linking. Cross-linking was performed by adding 10% (v/v) fix solution [50 mM Hepes-KOH (pH 7.5), 100 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, and 11% formaldehyde (Thermo Fisher Scientific)] and incubating at room temperature under gentle agitation for 20 min, after which glycine (final concentration, 125 mM) was added for 5 min to quench the cross-linking. The fixed cells were centrifuged for 5 min at 4°C, 4000g, washed once using ice-cold tris-buffered saline (TBS), and resuspended in TBS + phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (Roche) + 10% FBS. The samples were then transferred to Eppendorf tubes, snap-frozen, and stored at −80°C as described above.

From these samples, chromatin was isolated by resuspending the pellet in ice-cold breaking buffer [100 mM tris (pH 7.9) and 20% glycerol + PIC] with silica/zirconia beads (0.5 mm; BioSpec Products) and bead beating for two times for 2 min using the Mini-Beadbeater (BioSpec Products). Samples were then homogenized with FA buffer [50 mM Hepes-KOH (pH 7.5), 140 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1% Triton X-100, and 0.1% Na deoxycholate + PIC] and transferred to RNase/DNase-free Eppendorf tubes. Chromatin was pelleted by centrifugation at 13,000g for 1 min, washed once using FA buffer + 0.13% SDS, and resuspended in FA buffer + 0.8% SDS. Samples were sonicated using the BioRuptor (PICO) for 6 cycles of 30 s on and 30 s off, centrifuged for 5 min at 13,000g, resuspended in FA buffer, and stored at −80°C. An aliquot was subsequently thawed, treated with RNase A (10 mg/ml; Sigma-Aldrich) and proteinase K (10 mg/ml; Sigma-Aldrich), and incubated for 1 hour at 50°C and then overnight at 65°C to reverse cross-links. DNA was eluted using a PCR purification kit (QIAGEN) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Eluted DNA was checked for shearing efficacy by running the samples through a 1% agarose gel, and DNA concentration was determined by using the Qubit dsDNA Quantification Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Normalized H3K79me2 ChIP-seq sample work-up and library preparation

Cross-linked CD8 T cells were washed once with PBS + PIC, centrifuged for 5 min at 600g, resuspended in ice-cold nuclei lysis buffer [50 mM tris-HCl (pH 8), 10 mM EDTA (pH 8), and 1% SDS + PIC], and incubated on ice for 10 min. Samples were then sonicated for 3 min (3× 30 s on followed by 30 s off), after which debris was pelleted by centrifugation for 10 min at 4°C, 13,000g. The supernatant was transferred to an RNase/DNase-free Eppendorf tube and supplemented with sample buffer {nine parts of ChIP dilution buffer [50 mM tris-HCl (pH 8), 0.167 M NaCl, 1.1% Triton X-100, and 0.11% sodium deoxycholate] and five parts of RIPA-150 [50 mM tris-HCl (pH 8), 0.15 M NaCl, 1 mM EDTA (pH 8), 0.1% SDS, 1% Triton X-100, and 0.1% sodium deoxycholate + PIC] per one part of the sample}. Shearing efficiency was confirmed by transferring 10% of the suspension to an RNase/DNase-free Eppendorf tube, reversing the cross-links, eluting the DNA, and running a gel and Qubit analysis as described above.

On the basis of Qubit DNA quantifications, the yeast chromatin spike-in was added 1:1000. Samples were homogenized and diluted in sample buffer, after which 10% was removed and stored at −20°C as input. To the remaining sample, 0.5 μg of mouse anti-HA antibody (manufactured at NKI) and 1 μl of rabbit anti-H3K79me2 (Millipore) were added, after which samples were incubated under rotation at 4°C overnight. Following incubation, Dynabeads Protein G (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in sample buffer were added, and samples were incubated under rotation at 4°C for 2 hours. The beads were then washed using the DynaMag-2 magnetic rack (Thermo Fisher Scientific), by aspirating the supernatant and adding 800 μl of RIPA-150, followed by incubation under rotation at 4°C for 5 min. The washing steps were repeated twice using RIPA-500 [50 mM tris-HCl (pH 8), 0.5 M NaCl, 1 mM EDTA (pH 8), 0.1% SDS, 1% Triton X-100, and 0.1% sodium deoxycholate], twice using RIPA-LiCl [50 mM tris-HCl (pH 8), 1 mM EDTA (pH 8), 1% NP-40, 0.7% sodium deoxycholate, and 0.5 M LiCl2], and once using TE. Subsequently, samples were resuspended in 150 μl of Direct Elution buffer [10 mM tris-HCl (pH 8), 0.3 M NaCl, 5 mM EDTA (pH 8), and 0.5% SDS]. Samples were de–cross-linked, quantified, and quality checked as described above. All samples were library amplified and sequenced using the NovaSeq with paired-end 51-bp reads.

ChIP-seq data processing

ChIP-seq samples were mapped to a hybrid genome made from concatenating the mouse mm10 genome (Ensembl GRCm38) with the yeast sacCer3 genome. Mapping was performed using BWA-MEM (version 0.7.17-r1188) with the option “-M” and filtered for a mapping quality of 10. Duplicate alignments were removed using MarkDuplicates from the Picard toolset (version 3.0.0) with the option “-REMOVE_DUPLICATES true.” Paired-end reads were then converted to fragments by reading in mapped, properly paired reads using the GenomicAlignments R/Bioconductor package (release 3.17). Peaks were called using MACS2 version 2.2.7.1 using samples pooled by treatment and pooled input samples as the “control” argument using the options “-f BEDPE -g mm --keep-dup all --broad.” Peaks detected on yeast chromosomes were removed. Genomic bigwig tracks were generated for both individual samples and samples pooled by treatment from fragments mapping to the mouse genome using a custom R script using the “coverage” function from the GenomicRanges R/Bioconductor package. The track heights were multiplied by a factor “1·106/number of yeast reads” for values to represent “coverage per million yeast reads” to allow quantitative comparisons. Signal values per peak were obtained by using the BigWigFile method of the “summary” function in the rtracklayer R/Bioconductor package. For aggregate visualization of coverage data, the tornadoplot R/GitHub package was used, and TSS/exons were determined using the EnsDb.Mmusculus.v75 R/Bioconductor package. To calculate distances to the first internal exon, exons within a gene were first collapsed to disjoint ranges using the “reduce” function from the GenomicRanges R/Bioconductor package. The distance was then taken as the number of base pairs between the 3′ end of the most 5′ exon and the 5′ end of the subsequent exon for genes that have at least two distinct exons. A list of these normalized TSS peak counts can be found in table S8.

Polymerase chain reaction

Enriched CD8 T cells were lysed using DirectPCR Lysis Reagent (mouse tail) (Viagen Biotech) with proteinase K (1 mg/ml; Sigma-Aldrich). Dot1L WT, floxed, and deleted (exon 2) alleles were detected by PCR using the following primers, as described in (4). Dot1L_FWD: GCAAGCCTACAGCCTTCATC, Dot1L_REV: CACCGGATAGTCTCAATAATCTCA and Dot1L_∆: GAACCACAGGATGCTTCAG. PCR reactions were performed using MyTaq Red Mix (GC Biotech). Agarose gel electrophoresis was performed to determine the genotype. Where relevant, the efficacy of floxing out exon 2 was determined by quantifying band densities using ImageJ (version 1.54g), correcting for product size and calculating the percentage by dividing the band density of the floxed-out band over the total band density within the sample.

Activation assay

CD8 T cells were activated and expanded for 8 days in whole T cell media as described above. On day 8, T cells were switched to medium containing recombinant mouse IL-12 p70 (10 ng/ml; BioLegend) and recombinant mouse IL-18 (50 ng/ml; BioLegend). CD8 T cells were cultured in IL-2 (50 U/ml; Novartis) as a negative control and in IL-2 (50 U/ml) supplemented 1:500 with eBioscience Cell Stimulation Cocktail (Invitrogen) as a positive control. After 24 hours, cells were analyzed by flow cytometry as described above. In addition, cytokine production was measured for 5 hours in the presence of GolgiStop (BD Biosciences) and monensin (BioLegend) as described above.

Visualization and statistical analysis

Data were visualized, and statistical analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism 9, unless otherwise indicated. Paired or unpaired statistical analysis was applied according to the experimental setup used. Subtests included all groups of a specific comparison, and relevant P values were plotted.
