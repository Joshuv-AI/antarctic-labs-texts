Mindfulness-based intervention alters nonlinear heart rate variability in a pilot study
Authors: Yu Gao, Liat Zitron, Serena Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMindfulness-based interventions (MBIs) have been consistently associated with reductions in perceived stress and anxiety. One proposed mechanism underlying these effects involves a shift toward parasympathetic dominance during stress exposure, facilitating enhanced emotion regulation and physiological adaptability; however, empirical evidence on this mechanism remains inclusive. In this randomized controlled trial, 25 first-year college students were assigned to either a six-week MBI program or an active control condition. Psychophysiological responses to acute stressors were assessed in conjunction with self-reported measures of stress and anxiety, both before and after the intervention. Linear (RMSSD) and nonlinear (sample entropy and DFA α1) indices of heart rate variability (HRV) were compared between groups. Although both groups reported significant post-intervention reductions in stress and anxiety, participants in the MBI group exhibited significantly lower DFA α1 values in response to acute stressors as compared to the control group. No such differences were found for heart rate or RMSSD. These preliminary findings suggest that mindfulness may modulate the dynamic organization of autonomic stress responding during active challenge, and that nonlinear HRV metrics may be particularly sensitive to mindfulness-related changes in cardiovascular regulation under acute stress.Keywords: Meditation, Sympathetic, Parasympathetic, Heart rate variability, Stress

Introduction

Mindfulness-based interventions (MBIs) refer to structured and evidence-based programs that integrate mindfulness practices, such as meditation, mindful awareness, and present-moment attention, to enhance psychological and physical well-being [61]. The beneficial effects of MBIs on mental health and wellbeing, including anxiety and stress reduction, have been widely reported [2, 12, 20, 25]. However, the neurobiological mechanism underlying its effect is less known. Heart rate variability (HRV) has been used as an objective biomarker capable of demonstrating MBI effects, although prior findings have been mixed and inclusive [37]. One caveat is that no prior studies have examined the effects of MBI on nonlinear HRV metrics, measures that provide more detailed information about the complexity of the cardiovascular system than the commonly used linear HRV metrics [45]. Other limitations in prior research include lack of control group, or the control group was a waitlist or “treatment as usual” group that may render the expectations of treatment and control groups to be inequivalent [62]. The current study aimed to address these issues by examining effects of MBI on both linear and nonlinear HRV metrics utilizing a well-constructed randomized-control trial with an active control group.

Effects of mindfulness-based intervention on heart rate variability

Heart rate variability (HRV) refers to the variation in the time intervals between consecutive heartbeats, typically measured through electrocardiography (ECG). A healthy heart displays dynamic fluctuations, reflecting the body’s ability to adapt to stressors and maintain homeostasis. HRV is primarily regulated by the autonomic nervous system (ANS), which consists of the sympathetic and parasympathetic branches. The sympathetic nervous system, often associated with the “fight-or-flight” response, reduces HRV by promoting faster, more regular heartbeats during stress or arousal. In contrast, the parasympathetic nervous system, linked to “rest-and-digest” functions via the vagus nerve, increases HRV by allowing greater variability in heart rate, especially during states of relaxation and recovery [40]. Thus, higher HRV generally indicates greater adaptability, resilience, and autonomic balance, whereas lower HRV is often associated with stress, fatigue, or certain health risks. Therefore, HRV is widely used as a noninvasive biomarker for physiological and psychological well-being [55].

In an earlier review and meta-analysis based on 10 studies that comprised in total 607 participants, researchers reported mixed and inconclusive results on the effects of MBI on HRV [42]. In a review based on 17 MBI studies (11 experimental and 6 quasi-experimental) that included HRV assessment, researchers found an increase in HRV reactivity (corresponding to adaptive changes of HRV in response to a task/stressor) following implementation of an MBI among non-clinical samples [9]. Morton et al., [37] conducted a systematic review examining how MBI influences psychophysiological responses to acute stress via the Trier Social Stress Test (TSST), an empirically validated laboratory protocol. They found that among the six out of 15 studies that used cardiovascular measures, four examined HRV, with only one reporting stress-buffeting effects of MBI [1], whereas the other three did not find such effects [17, 29, 38]. In a meta-analysis that specifically focused on resting-state HRV based on 19 randomized-control trials, researchers found that MBIs were not efficacious in increasing vagally-mediated resting-state HRV relative to control conditions [7]. Although review findings overall support the utility of HRV as an objective biomarker capable of demonstrating MBI effects on improving stress responses, more research is needed to address the high variability in methods across studies.

More importantly, among the few randomized-control trials that examined the effects of MBI on HRV, all have used linear HRV measures, and none has examined nonlinear HRV measures. Compared to linear HRV metrics, non-linear measures provide a more nuanced understanding of the complex cardiovascular system and underlying regulatory mechanisms [60]. Among a variety of nonlinear metrics, two widely employed non-linear analysis methods in psychological research are sample entropy and detrended fluctuation analysis (DFA) α1. Entropy refers to the level of disorder or unpredictability within a system. Perhaps counterintuitively, in cardiovascular physiology, a certain degree of controlled variability, or “chaos,” is beneficial, as it reflects the system’s flexibility and adaptability to external stressors. This adaptability is critical for maintaining homeostasis during physiologically demanding situations. In HRV analysis, entropy quantifies how unpredictable or irregular the sequence of R-R intervals (time between heartbeats) is. A high entropy value means the signal is more complex and less predictable and typically reflects a more adaptable cardiovascular system. In contrast, a low value indicates greater regularity or repetitiveness and is generally considered to reflect physiological rigidity — the inability of the nervous system to respond to internal and external changes [54]. Thus, low sample entropy value, reflecting high rigidity, is often associated with maladaptive physiological states, such as chronic stress, anxiety, and poorer emotional regulation, whereas high entropy is linked to decreased anxiety and improved effortful control [13, 18, 53].

DFA α1 has also emerged as a prominent nonlinear method for HRV analysis that quantifies the complexity and self-similarity of heart rhythm dynamics. Unlike time-domain or frequency-domain HRV metrics that quantify the magnitude of variability, DFA α1 captures the fractal correlation properties of intervals between heartbeats and reflects how strongly current heartbeat fluctuations are related to past fluctuations [39]. It is influenced more by parasympathetic than sympathetic activity [48] and is therefore presumably relevant to the regulation process. A healthy cardiovascular system typically operates with a balance between complete predictability and randomness, which is represented by an optimal DFA α1 value near 1.0. This indicates a well-regulated, adaptable autonomic system capable of responding to both internal and external demands. Very low DFA α1 scores indicate low correlation in heartbeat dynamics, reflecting a more random, less regulated heart rhythm that is sympathetically dominant and typically linked to high stress. Conversely, high DFA α1 scores indicate predictable heart rhythm and rigidity, thus reduced adaptability of the system. Chronic deviations from the healthy DFA α1 range have been linked to greater risk for cardiovascular disease, heart failure, and impaired stress response [13, 24, 53], making this measurement a potential clinical biomarker for early detection of possible areas of health concern.

Because of the wide application and thorough insight provided by these nonlinear measures, it is now generally accepted that nonlinear techniques can describe HRV more effectively than traditional linear approaches [45]. Studies of effects of meditation on cardiovascular measures have overall observed a reduced dynamical complexity and fractal dimension during meditation (see review by [14]). For example, in a group of experienced Tibetan Buddhist monks, researchers found that deep meditation was associated with a dynamic HRV process, including increased ratio of low and high frequency power, standard deviation of RR intervals and fractal scaling exponents of DFA, indicating that meditation is dominated by activated sympathetic nervous system [23]. However, such effects may not generalize to non-practitioners, and a well-designed randomized control trial is warranted.

Heart rate variability changes in response to acute stressor

Acute stressor provokes changes in linear and nonlinear parameters of HRV. For linear HRV metrics, induced stress has been linked to reduced root mean square of successive differences (RMSSD), a time-domain linear metric that quantifies fluctuations in heart rate between consecutive heartbeats (e.g., Brugnera et al., [8]; [27, 50]), although some studies have failed to show such effect [32]. Only a few studies have investigated the impact of acute stressors on HR measures of chaos in healthy individuals using nonlinear HRV measurements. Limited evidence has suggested that acute stress is associated with decreased entropy and/or increased DFA α1, reflecting reduced complexity of the cardiac signal [8, 36]. For example, one study reported that increased level of state anxiety anticipating an exam was associated with decreased HR complexity [15]. Similar effects of state anxiety on entropy measures have been reported in the context of high-stress musical performance [58] and arithmetic stress [57]. This reduced HR complexity is believed to reflect a shift toward sympathetic predominance [41].

In a recent review and meta-analysis on the effects of psychological stress on linear as well as nonlinear HRV measures, researchers found that among the 15 studies that examined the HRV-stress relationship via repeated measurements and validated psychometric instruments, RMSSD was the most reported HRV metric, and that very few studies have used nonlinear metrics [26]. Overall, they concluded that induced stress causes the autonomic nervous system to shift toward sympathetic predominance because of parasympathetic withdrawal, which provokes a characteristic defense-arousal reaction in the physiological system.

The current study

In summary, research on the effects of MBI on HRV measures, particularly the nonlinear dynamics in response to stress, is limited. In this randomized-control trial, we tested the effect of MBI on emotional and psychophysiological responses to social and arithmetic stressors in college students. Participants were randomly assigned to a 6-week MBI or active control group, and before and after intervention they completed stressful tasks in the laboratory while their psychophysiological responses were recorded. In addition to commonly used linear HRV metrics (i.e., RMSSD), we focused on the nonlinear HRV metrics that are believed to provide more information on the complex cardiovascular system [45]. Specifically, we tested the hypothesis that as compared to the active control group, the MBI group would show a decrease in state anxiety and perceived stress, as well as increased RMSSD and complexity of HRV, reflecting their improved emotion regulation capability and relaxed states in response to acute stress.

Furthermore, because previous findings (see for example [46, 47, 49]) support the notion that different stressor protocols (e.g., verbal vs. non-verbal; prominent social-evaluative aspects vs. prominent executive functioning components) may lead to different cardiovascular responses, we expected specific, distinct patterns of HRV responses between speech and arithmetic stress tasks [3]. Therefore, participants were administered different stress tasks and effects on each task were examined separately. Finally, an active control group was used in this study to account for nonspecific factors such as expectancy effects, time spent in structured sessions, instructor contact, and engagement with a novel activity, all of which can independently influence stress and physiological outcomes. This design strengthens internal validity by allowing observed differences to be more confidently attributed to mindfulness-specific mechanisms rather than to general intervention exposure or placebo-like effects.

Method

Participants

The sample included 33 first year college students from a public college in a metropolitan area in the Northeast of the United States. Flyers were distributed at campus orientation events, and information was posted at the Psychology Department subject pool. Inclusion criteria included male or female first-year students aged between 18 and 25 years and not concurrently enrolled in another study that involved mindfulness or other stress reduction skills training. Of the 33 students who were scheduled for their first lab visit, 25 completed all parts of the study and therefore included in the following analyses. The final sample included 16 females and 9 males, with an average age of 18.4 years (S.D. = 1.15, range = 18–22) and a diverse ethnic background. More detailed demographic information is listed in Table 1. Three participants reported some prior experiences with meditation but did not practice any mindfulness techniques in the month before the current study, with the remaining participants reporting no prior meditation experiences. All participants received either course credits or monetary compensation at the end of the study. The study was not pre-registered and hence all results presented in this manuscript are based on exploratory analyses. However, to increase transparency, we have made the data and analysis script publicly available on the Open Science Framework (https://osf.io/xgz76/overview?view_only=74651b06d38d4b98827887b9e428184d). Study procedures were approved by the University IRB (protocol # 2017 − 0667).

Procedures

After sign-up and eligibility screening, participants arrived at the laboratory located in the Psychology Department between mid-September and mid-October in 2017 to become acquainted with the lab setting and to receive a briefing on the study. This first laboratory visit lasted about 60 to 90 min, and during this time, they received in-depth information about the study, provided informed consent in accordance with the study’s IRB-approved protocol, and completed a demographic survey, a mindfulness predisposition scale, and self-report psychological questionnaires (see below for details) to assess current level of stress and anxiety.

Following completion of the self-report measures, participants underwent a modified version of the Trier Social Stress Test (TSST; [30]), a widely used protocol for inducing and measuring social-evaluative stress in laboratory settings. In the current study, the task started with a two-minute rest period, during which participants were instructed to stay seated while looking at a cross at the center of a computer screen. The next two-minute period consisted of a participant preparing a speech regarding a personal fault or weakness. They were informed that the experimenter would stay in the room during the speech, take notes, and record the speech on video. After preparation, the experimenter re-entered the room, turned on the camera, and asked the participant to start talking for the next two minutes. If the participant paused for longer than 10 s during the two-minute speech, the experimenter reminded the participant that there was still time left and instructed the participant to keep on speaking. To further augment the stress level, the experimenter maintained a neutral facial expression throughout the task and wore a white lab coat. Once the speech task concluded, the participant had another two-minute break, before a two-minute mental math task commenced. In the math task, the participant was asked to subtract 13 from 1,022 as quickly and accurately as possible. If an incorrect answer was delivered, the participant was asked to start again from 1,022. A countdown clock on the screen informed the participant on time remaining on the task. This arithmetic task was followed by a final two-minute rest period. Physiological responses were continuously recorded during the task to assess autonomic nervous system activity. See Fig. 1 for the sequence of the tasks.

Procedural flowchart

Upon completing these tasks and concluding the first lab visit, participants were randomly assigned to the MBI group (N = 15) - consisting of mindfulness training (Learning to Breathe; [35]), or a control group (N = 15) - involving ‘sham’ mindfulness training that consisted of educational content on the stress response, along with art-based group activities aimed at reducing stress and enhancing interpersonal relationship skills. Both groups were trained by the second author (L.Z.) for 45 min once a week, for six consecutive weeks, on campus during Tuesday common hours (12 pm − 2 pm). At the end of the 6-week training program, all participants were invited to repeat the procedures as in the first laboratory visit.

Intervention

The MBI used in this study, Learning to BREATHE (L2B; [5]), is an evidence- and skill-based universal prevention program based on the mindfulness-based stress reduction (MBSR) program. It aims to promote students’ stress-management capabilities, emotion regulation, and well-being [4]. This program was initially developed with an emphasis on improving adolescent well-being, yet in the current study, the focus was on undergraduate students [16]. During each of the six 45-minute weekly sessions for the MBI group, a specific module in the program was targeted using mindfulness tools, which corresponded to a letter in the acronym BREATHE: Body scan (conscious monitoring of physical sensation), Reflections on thoughts and healthy Emotions, Attention, Tenderness (compassion towards self and others), and developing healthy Habits leading to self-Empowerment. Each session started with a brief meditation and review of skills taught in the previous session. Participants were provided yoga mats and were taught various resiliency, emotion regulation, and stress reduction skills through lectures, meditations, and activities. They were encouraged to utilize these new skills beyond the weekly sessions.

The control group also went through six 45-minute stress-reduction training sessions. For the first 15 min of each session, participants received psychoeducational information about stress response and triggers relevant to common student concerns, such as procrastination, test anxiety, and interpersonal conflict. In the next 15 min, participants engaged in theme-related group exercise or discussion before moving on to the final 15 min of the session. Participants concluded the session by engaging in relaxing activity, such as drawing or coloring mandala pages, followed by a brief ‘nap’ on yoga mats while soothing music was played. Training sessions for both groups took place in the same location and approximately at the same time of the day (e.g., 12 pm − 12:45 pm and 1 pm − 1:45 pm). All sessions were administered by the second author (L.Z.) who distributed materials and took attendance.

Out of the 33 participants who came for their first lab visit, three did not show up for any intervention sessions and were therefore excluded from any analyses. 80% of the remaining 30 participants had attended at least four out of six intervention sessions (73% in the MBI group, and 86% in the control group). Five participants dropped out of the study after one or two sessions (17%): three in the MBI group, and two in the control group. The two groups were initially balanced for gender but were unmatched for gender at the study’s completion due to drop-out. All the remaining 25 participants (MBI group N = 11, control group N = 14) had completed both the pre- and post-intervention lab visits, including all psychological and physiological assessments, and attended the majority of intervention sessions. On average, participants in the MBI group attended 4.91 sessions (S.D. = 0.99), and participants in the control group attended 5.15 sessions (S.D. = 0.55). Prior to statistical analyses, one outlier (3 standard deviations beyond the mean) was identified and removed from the physiological data, as constant coughing during this participant’s first visit rendered her data unusable. Measures

Perceived stress scale (PSS; [10])

Participants completed the 10-item self-report questionnaire designed to assess the degree to which they perceive life situations as stressful. This scale has been widely used in college populations with good psychometric properties [31]. Example questions include: “In the last month, how often have you felt that difficulties were piling up so high that you could not overcome them?” Participants respond to each item using a 5-point scale from 0 (Never) to 4 (Very often). A total score was created based on the sum of the 10 items, with higher score indicating more perceived stress.

Generalized anxiety disorder 7-item scale (GAD-7; [52])

Participants completed this 7-question scale concerning specific anxiety symptoms experienced over the last two weeks. Example questions include: “worrying too much about different things”. Participants note the frequency of each symptom on a 4-point scale ranging from 0 (Not at all) to 3 (Nearly every day). Resulting scores are then added and a higher score indicates greater severity of anxiety. Good reliability and validity have been demonstrated in prior studies [34].

Adult manifest anxiety scale – college version (AMAS-C; [43])

This anxiety scale, designed to specifically assess anxiety levels in college students, consists of 49 items answered with a “Yes” or “No.” It includes four subscales: worry/oversensitivity (12 items, e.g., “I worry a lot of the time” ), social concern/stress (7 items, e.g., “I feel that others do not like the way I do things”), physiological anxiety (8 items, e.g., “my muscles feel tense”), test anxiety (15 items, e.g., “I worry too much about tests and exams”), and a validity/lie scale (7 items, e.g., “I like everyone I know”). Sum of the items (excluding validity items) were created, and higher scores indicate a higher anxiety level. Good psychometric properties have been reported [33].

State and trait anxiety index – trait subscale (STAI-T; [51])

This 20-item self-report questionnaire aims to assess dispositional, or trait, anxiety. Example questions include “some unimportant thought runs through my mind and bothers me”. Participants rate each item on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (Not at all) to 4 (Very much).” Scores are summed and higher scores indicate a greater general tendency to experience anxiety. The STAI has shown good psychometric properties [19].

Psychophysiological data recording and quantification

All physiological data were acquired using the Biopac MP150 system (Biopac Systems, Inc, Goleta, CA). Electrocardiography (ECG) signals were recorded using an ECG100C amplifier with two electrodes placed at a modified Lead II configuration (on both sides of the chest). Respiration was recorded using an RSP100C amplifier with a belt around the chest. Heart rate and heart rate variability (HRV) were derived from the ECG100C data and analyzed offline using Kubios HRV software (Version 2.2; [54]), which was used for all HRV analyses. Raw ECG data for each participant were divided into 5 two-minute epochs, including rest (baseline), speech preparation, speech, arithmetic, and recovery. Files were then quality checked to ensure that QRS complexes were correctly classified. Displaced, incorrect or missing R peaks in the ECG segments were manually corrected by adding or removing R wave markers. When the correct time instances for R waves could not be visually observed, low level artifact correction was utilized.

For the linear HRV metric, root mean square successive differences (RMSSD), the square root of the mean of the squares of the successive differences between adjacent normal to normal R-R intervals, was calculated using Kubios HRV software. Two non-linear methods were applied to the raw data and computed using Kubios. For sample entropy (SampEn), m (embedding dimensions) was set with a value of 2, and r (tolerance) to a fraction of the standard deviation of the RR data (r = .2 x SDNN). For DFA, the fractal correlation properties within the signal at different time scales, short-term fluctuation (α1; modulated by the sympathetic system; [15]) slope was estimated within the range of 4–16 beats. All psychophysiological variables during each task were subtracted by the baseline measurement (i.e., rest period before speech preparation) to calculate the task-related reactivity. Respiration rate was controlled for in all analyses for HRV measures.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were performed with SPSS 29.0 (IBM Corp.). Independent samples t-tests were used to test the differences in all main study variables at baseline (before intervention) between the two groups. Next, to test the effects of group and time on psychophysiological measures, a 2 (pre vs. post intervention) x 2 (MBI vs. control group) x 4 (task: speech preparation, speech, arithmetic, and recovery) repeated measures MACOVA with respiration rate as a covariate was conducted for each HRV measure (RMSSD, Sample entropy, and DFA α1), followed by univariate ANOVA for each task. To test the effects of group and time on stress and anxiety scores, a 2 (pre vs. post intervention) x 2 (MBI vs. control group) x 4 (measure: PSS, GARD-7, AMAS-C, and STAI-T) repeated measures MANOVA was conducted for all stress/anxiety measures, followed by univariate ANOVA for each stress/anxiety measure. Effect sizes were reported using partial η². All tests were two tailed with an alpha level of 0.05. Given that this pilot study was exploratory and hypothesis-generating, raw p values were reported without adjustment for multiple comparisons.

Results

Descriptive statistics

The two groups did not differ on sex or age, although the MBI group had slightly more African Americans and fewer Caucasian than the control group. See Table 1 for more information. The groups did not differ on the study variables pre-intervention: GAD-7 (t (22) = -0.82, p = .424); PSS (t (22) = -0.73, p = .475); AMAS-C (t (22) = -0.37, p = .715); STAI-T (t (22) = -0.09, p = .928), nor any of the HRV measures at pre-intervention. Discussion

In this pilot study, we examined the effects of a brief MBI on both linear and non-linear HRV measures, along with self-reported measures of anxiety and stress in a small group of college students using a randomized control trial. It was found that overall, all participants showed significant reduction in self-reported anxiety and stress levels from pre- to post- intervention. More interestingly, a significant group × time interaction for DFA α1 emerged during both the speech and arithmetic tasks. Participants in the MBI group exhibited reduced DFA α1 from pre- to post-intervention, whereas the control group did not demonstrate comparable changes. For entropy, the overall group × time interaction reached marginal significance, with follow-up analyses indicating that entropy increased from pre- to post-intervention within the MBI group during stress tasks, whereas no significant changes were observed in the control group. Although this pattern should be interpreted cautiously given the marginal interaction and modest sample size, it converges directionally with the DFA α1 findings in suggesting stress-specific modulation of nonlinear cardiac dynamics following mindfulness training.

These findings contribute to a growing body of literature examining how mindfulness training influences nonlinear autonomic regulation under stress. Lower DFA α1 values are often interpreted as reflecting reduced long-range correlations in heart rate dynamics, while higher entropy indicates greater signal complexity. However, nonlinear HRV metrics are not unidirectional indicators of “health.” Instead, they index the dynamic organization of cardiac control, which may reflect adaptive flexibility in some contexts and dysregulation in others [21, 56]. That is, increased entropy can reflect enhanced adaptability, but it may also reflect increased stochasticity depending on signal properties and context [11, 41]. Likewise, reduced DFA α1 does not uniformly indicate improved regulation [39, 45]. In the current study, the nonlinear changes emerged during stress exposure and were accompanied by reductions in subjective stress and anxiety in the MBI group. Acute stress is frequently associated with constrained autonomic dynamics and reduced complexity (e.g., Voss et al., [56]. For example, prior research on nonlinear HRV during exam stress and performance anxiety (e.g., Kazdagli et al., [28], Yoo [59], where acute stress has been associated with alterations in fractal scaling and complexity metrics. From this perspective, the observed reduction in DFA α1—alongside increases in entropy within the MBI group—may reflect a shift toward greater dynamic responsiveness under challenge conditions. Adaptive stress responding requires flexible modulation between sympathetic activation and parasympathetic regulation [40, 55]. The current pattern is consistent with the possibility that even brief MBI can influence the fractal and complexity characteristics of cardiac dynamics during stress.

Interestingly, while the MBI group demonstrated modulation during active stress exposure, these effects did not clearly extend into post-stressor recovery. This distinction aligns with prior work suggesting that experienced meditators may show faster physiological recovery, whereas novice practitioners or brief interventions more reliably influence stress reactivity than recovery dynamics [22]. The present findings therefore suggest that early-stage mindfulness training may first alter the organization of autonomic dynamics during stress, with recovery-related adaptations potentially emerging only with more extensive practice.

Considering the importance of stress reduction for first-year college students, the program appears to have been successful in lowering stress as they adapted to college life. Both groups showed significant reduction in stress and anxiety scores, although no significant group differences emerged. Post-hoc analyses revealed that the MBI group exhibited significant improvements across all measures, while the control group showed limited change, primarily in the AMAS-C. These findings are consistent with a growing body of evidence supporting MBI as a stress-reduction tool, particularly during transitional periods such as the first year of college. Achieving more pronounced changes in self-reported stress may require longer, consistent mindfulness practice. Over time, such practice may shift benefits from short-term, situational (“state”) improvements—like those observed here—to more enduring (“trait”) changes, including enhanced emotion regulation, reduced negative self-evaluations, and increased well-being [6, 44]. More research is needed to clarify if more intense/longer MBI may induce substantial changes in trait-like changes in anxiety or HRV recovery.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, the final sample was modest, limiting power for detecting higher-order interactions and contributing to the marginal nature of the entropy interaction. Multiple task-specific analyses were conducted, increasing the risk of Type I error. Accordingly, task-specific nonlinear HRV findings—particularly those related to entropy—should be interpreted as preliminary and hypothesis-generating. Attrition was modest and comparable across groups, and one outlier was excluded from the mindfulness group. Because nonlinear HRV indices are sensitive to extreme values and small samples, replication in larger cohorts with pre-registered analytic strategies will be important to confirm the stability of both DFA α1 and entropy effects. Second, although groups were balanced on most demographic factors, the slight imbalance in racial composition may have introduced unmeasured cultural or contextual influences on stress and coping. Third, although previous findings suggest that different protocols (e.g., verbal vs. non-verbal; prominent social-evaluative aspects vs. prominent executive functioning components) may lead to different cardiovascular responses, our findings showed effects of MBI on both social (speech) and non-social (arithmetic) tasks. However, both these tasks involved verbal activity, therefore our findings could not specify if there were distinct patterns of HRV for verbal or non-verbal stress tasks. Future studies should employ larger, randomized controlled designs and extended follow-up periods to determine the durability of effects. Incorporating ecological momentary assessment and daily practice logs may also clarify the dose–response relationship between mindfulness practice, HRV dynamics, and stress resilience.

Despite these limitations, the study makes several meaningful contributions. It extends mindfulness research beyond traditional time- and frequency-domain HRV metrics to examine fractal and complexity dynamics under experimentally induced stress. It demonstrates that even brief MBI may influence the nonlinear organization of cardiac activity during acute challenge. And it distinguishes stress reactivity from recovery processes, offering a more differentiated view of how autonomic adaptations to mindfulness may unfold.

In conclusion, brief mindfulness training was associated with altered fractal scaling properties and suggestive increases in entropy during acute psychosocial stress. These findings indicate that mindfulness may modulate the dynamic organization of autonomic stress responding, particularly during active challenge. While replication in larger, adequately powered trials is necessary, the present results provide a theoretically informative and methodologically transparent foundation for future work on nonlinear cardiac regulation and mindfulness-based stress reduction.
