Characteristics of pediatric olfactory airflow based on computational fluid dynamics analysis
Authors: Hironobu Nishijima, Takahisa Yamamoto, Kaoru Yamakawa, Roji Kagoya, Kei Ogawa, Rumi Ueha, Kenji Kondo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposePediatric olfactory development remains poorly understood, particularly regarding how age-related nasal morphological changes influence olfactory airflow dynamics and odorant transport. This study aimed to characterize age-dependent variations in olfactory airflow patterns and odorant distribution within the pediatric nasal cavity using computational fluid dynamics (CFD) analysis.MethodsThree-dimensional nasal cavity models were reconstructed from high-resolution sinus CT scans of 11 pediatric patients (ages 3-18 years) using Mimics Innovation Suite. CFD simulations of inspiratory airflow were performed using OpenFOAM with physiologically scaled, age- and sex-dependent flow rates. Odorant transport was modeled as a dimensionless passive scalar representing an inert, non-reactive tracer gas to analyze concentration dynamics and arrival times at the olfactory mucosa across age groups.ResultsThe olfactory cleft volume and airflow generally followed an upward trend from early childhood until approximately 15 years of age, with more pronounced enlargement observed in late adolescence (15–18 years). Young children (3-6 years) demonstrated markedly reduced olfactory airflow delivery compared to late adolescents (15-18 years). Odorant transport analysis revealed prolonged arrival times and reduced peak concentrations at the olfactory mucosa in younger subjects, indicating that anatomical immaturity constrains olfactory efficiency in early childhood.ConclusionAge-related nasal morphological development substantially influences olfactory airflow dynamics and odorant transport efficiency in children. These computational findings provide mechanistic insights into pediatric olfactory development and may inform clinical assessment strategies for childhood olfactory disorders.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00405-026-10260-6.Keywords: Olfaction, Pediatric olfactory airflow, Odor dynamics, Computational fluid dynamics

Introduction

The sense of smell is essential for daily life, contributing not only to flavor perception and social communication but also to the detection of environmental hazards. In pediatric populations, olfaction plays a pivotal role in neurocognitive development, language acquisition, and the formation of dietary habits, underscoring its impact on health and well-being during childhood [1–5]. Despite its importance, the mechanisms underlying the maturation of olfactory function throughout development are not yet fully understood. Previous psychophysical research demonstrates that odor identification and discrimination abilities develop progressively from early childhood through adolescence [1, 4, 6–9]. However, the physiological and anatomical mechanisms driving these developmental gains remain incompletely understood. Among the various determinants of olfactory performance, the anatomical development of the nasal cavity and associated alterations in intranasal airflow have emerged as critical, though underexplored, contributors.

As children grow, the nasal cavity undergoes significant morphological remodeling, including overall volume expansion, enlargement of the olfactory cleft, and maturation of the turbinates [10–12]. These structural adaptations are hypothesized to modulate airflow dynamics, optimizing the delivery of odorant molecules to the olfactory mucosa. While adult olfactory airflow patterns have been studied extensively, the comprehensive characterization of age-dependent variations in pediatric olfactory airflow and odorant transport remains limited. In our previous study, we quantitatively analyzed age-dependent volumetric changes of the pediatric nasal cavity and paranasal sinuses based on three-dimensional CT segmentation [10]. However, the functional implications of these anatomical changes for intranasal airflow and odorant transport were not explored. The present study addresses this gap by applying computational fluid dynamics (CFD) to the previously segmented models to assess age-dependent differences in olfactory airflow characteristics. CFD allows precise visualization and quantification of intranasal airflow and odorant movement, providing mechanistic insights into these developmental changes.

The present study aims to elucidate how age-dependent anatomical changes affect olfactory airflow patterns in pediatric subjects across a wide age range. Specifically, we assess the impact on odorant transport and distribution and examine the physiological and clinical significance of these findings for pediatric olfactory function. Through this approach, we seek to advance the understanding of olfactory development in children.

Materials and methods

Study design and subjects

This retrospective observational study included 11 pediatric patients aged 3 to 18 years who underwent high-resolution paranasal sinus computed tomography (CT) at the University of Tokyo Hospital between 2019 and 2021. Inclusion criteria required the absence of sinonasal pathology or anatomical abnormalities that could potentially affect nasal airflow dynamics. Clinical and demographic data, including age and sex, were retrospectively extracted from medical charts and radiologic records.

This study utilizes a subset of the CT dataset from our previous morphological analysis [10]. Specifically, 11 representative pediatric cases were selected from the original cohort of 137 for detailed CFD analysis in this study. The study protocol was approved by the institutional review board (No. 2487), which provides comprehensive coverage for retrospective studies involving secondary analysis of anonymized clinical imaging data. Individual informed consent was waived due to the retrospective design and the use of anonymized data.

Three-dimensional model construction

Patient-specific three-dimensional models of the upper airway were reconstructed from anonymized DICOM CT datasets for the 11 subjects (spanning ages 3 to 18 years). Segmentation was performed using Mimics Innovation Suite (version 23.0, Materialise, Leuven, Belgium) to isolate the nasal airway from the external nares to the nasopharyngeal outlet. To simulate the external inspiratory environment, a hemispherical external domain (“breathing zone”) was attached anterior to the face. The hemisphere was centered at the nasal tip, and its radius was selected for each subject as the minimum value required to fully enclose the anterior facial contour, including the regions from the chin to the forehead and the surroundings of the nostrils. In the present cohort, the selected radius ranged from 45 to 60 mm, with smaller radii in younger subjects and larger radii in older subjects. In the present study, the term external nares refers to the bilateral nostril-aperture surfaces at the interface between the patient-specific nasal airway and the added external domain (Supplementary Fig. S1). For the odorant-transport simulations described below, the initial tracer bolus was prescribed as a spherical cloud located inside this breathing zone, immediately anterior to the nares. The surface models were meshed using ICEM-CFD (ANSYS, Inc., Canonsburg, PA, USA), generating high-quality unstructured tetrahedral grids containing approximately 2 × 10⁶ elements per model. Detailed protocols regarding segmentation thresholds, anatomical processing, and mesh generation parameters are provided in the Supplementary Material S1 Text S1.

Mesh independence analysis

To ensure numerical validity, a mesh independence analysis was performed on a representative nasal model by comparing meshes of varying refinement levels (coarse, medium, and fine). The variation in the pressure drop (ΔP) between the medium and fine meshes was found to be less than 1%, indicating that the solution was grid-independent. Consequently, the medium-resolution mesh (approximately 1.3 × 106 cells, representative cell size h ≈ 0.8 mm) was adopted for all simulations to balance accuracy and computational efficiency. The detailed validation process and convergence metrics are described in the Supplementary Material S1 Text S2.

Age- and sex-dependent inspiratory flow rates

To simulate realistic and physiologically accurate respiratory conditions, boundary-condition flow rates were derived from age- and sex-specific ventilation parameters reported in the International Commission on Radiological Protection (ICRP) Publication [13]. We selected the total minute ventilation values for the “sitting awake” condition as representative of quiet nasal breathing. For specific ages not explicitly tabulated in the reference report, flow rates were obtained via linear interpolation to ensure a continuous and smooth progression of respiratory parameters across the pediatric cohort. Detailed equations and derivation methods, including flow rates used for the sensitivity analysis, are provided in the Supplementary Material S1 Text S3.

Airflow simulation

Steady inspiratory airflow was simulated for each age-specific nasal geometry using the finite-volume CFD package OpenFOAM (GNU General Public License). The flow was modeled as incompressible and laminar at a physiological air temperature of 20 °C. Boundary conditions consisted of atmospheric pressure prescribed at the outer surface of the hemispherical breathing zone and age- and sex-dependent total inspiratory flow rate (L/min) derived from ICRP reference data and prescribed at the nasopharyngeal outlet [13]. Atmospheric pressure was therefore not imposed at the external nares themselves; rather, the external nares served as interface between the nasal airway and the added external domain. With this setup, the bilateral total flow rate was fixed by the outlet condition. In contrast, the left–right flow split and the associated nasal pressure drop emerged naturally from the subject-specific airway resistance. All airway walls were treated as rigid, impermeable, no-slip boundaries. The governing equations and numerical discretization schemes are detailed in the Supplementary Material S1 Text S4.

Odorant transport and olfactory region definition

Odorant transport was modeled using a representative gas-phase molecular diffusivity (D = 2.3 × 10⁻⁵ m²/s) for an inert, non-reactive, and non-soluble tracer gas. The tracer concentration C is a dimensionless passive scalar initialized to C₀ = 1 within a spherical tracer cloud (radius = 20 mm) placed in front of the nostrils inside the hemispherical breathing zone, while C = 0 elsewhere; thus, the reported normalized concentration is Ĉ = C/C₀, where C₀ denotes the initial cloud concentration (not a prescribed inlet value). Accordingly, the simulation represents a finite tracer bolus, rather than a constant-concentration inlet condition. At the outer boundary of the breathing zone, the tracer concentration was prescribed as a fixed value of C = 0 to represent clean ambient air. The simulations employed the “frozen-flow” approximation, in which transient tracer transport is solved on a fixed, steady-state velocity field. This approach is widely used in computational studies of nasal olfaction and is considered appropriate for the quiet inspiratory conditions simulated in this study [14, 15, 16]. A detailed justification of this approximation, including fluid-mechanical validity and limitations regarding unsteady effects, is provided in Supplementary Material S1 Text S5.

To quantify tracer delivery, an “olfactory region” was geometrically defined for each subject as the upper 20% of the total nasal cavity height in the coronal direction, measured from the floor of the inferior meatus to the roof of the superior meatus. This definition serves as a conservative geometric surrogate for the olfactory epithelium, consistent with anatomical conventions in nose-to-brain delivery studies [17]. Tracer delivery was quantified by integrating the scalar concentration within this mask over time.

Transient scalar transport was simulated using scalarTransportFoam with a fixed time step of Δt = 1.0 × 10⁻⁵ s up to t = 1.5 s (results written every 0.01 s). The time step was chosen such that the maximum Courant number satisfied Co ≤ 1.0, based on the frozen (steady) velocity field. Time integration employed a first-order implicit Euler scheme. Spatial discretization used an upwind-biased scheme for the convective term and a corrected Gauss linear Laplacian for diffusion. At each time step, the resulting sparse linear system for C was solved using an iterative Krylov-subspace method with an incomplete-factorization-type preconditioner, using an absolute residual tolerance of 1 × 10⁻⁶. The governing advection–diffusion equation, boundary conditions, numerical schemes, and further justification for the anatomical definition are detailed in the Supplementary Material S1 Text S6.

Data analysis and outcome measures

Data visualization and quantitative analysis were performed using ParaView (Kitware, Inc.). To characterize age-dependent changes, subjects were categorized into three groups: preschool children (3–6 years), middle childhood (10–12 years), and late adolescents (15–18 years). Primary outcome measures included: (i) Anatomical Metrics: Olfactory cleft volume and nasal cavity volume. (ii) Aerodynamic Metrics: Absolute olfactory inflow rate (L/min) and the olfactory inflow fraction (OIF, %), defined as %, to assess distribution efficiency independent of total ventilation. (iii) Odorant Transport Metrics: Temporal profiles of odorant concentration at the olfactory mucosa, specifically the time to initial arrival and peak concentration. Additionally, odorant propagation was visualized using isosurfaces representing a 20% concentration threshold.

Results

Nasal morphology and olfactory airflow characteristics

To characterize age-related remodeling of the upper airway, we first quantified the olfactory cleft volume in each patient-specific model. In the present cohort, the olfactory cleft volume generally followed an upward trend with age (Fig. 1 A). Although individual anatomical variations resulted in non-monotonic fluctuations at specific developmental stages, a more pronounced enlargement was observed in the late-adolescent models (15–18 years). In the 3–6-year models, the olfactory cleft was restricted and slit-like, whereas in late adolescence, it was markedly expanded, providing a larger anatomical capacity for airflow (Supplementary Fig. S2).

Computational fluid dynamics (CFD) analysis of olfactory airflow characteristics at each age group. (A) Age-dependent changes in olfactory cleft volume. Three-dimensional reconstructions illustrate an overall increase in the olfactory cleft volume with age. Although individual anatomical variations were observed across different ages, a more pronounced expansion is evident in subjects older than 15 years. (B) Olfactory inflow fraction (OIF) under physiologically scaled inspiratory flow rates. OIF was defined as the inflow rate into the predefined olfactory-region mask across its inlet plane, computed as the positive volumetric flux and normalized by the total inspiratory flow rate . The olfactory inflow fraction was consistently lower in early childhood and increased with age, indicating more efficient aerodynamic access of inspired air to the superior nasal cavity with growth. C: Representative airflow velocity vectors during inspiration. Velocity vectors (colored by magnitude, m/s) show limited upward airflow reaching the olfactory region in younger children compared to the more directed flow into the olfactory cleft observed in older subjects due to structural maturation

This morphological maturation directly influenced the quantitative volume of air reaching the olfactory region. With advancing age, the absolute inspiratory inflow rate into the olfactory region increased substantially. Notably, the olfactory inflow fraction (OIF)—defined as the fraction of the total inspiratory flow rate that enters the olfactory region—also demonstrated an increasing trend (Fig. 1B). The highest OIF values were observed in the 15–18-year models, indicating that anatomical remodeling during adolescence not only accommodates larger air volumes but also improves the efficiency of airflow distribution toward the olfactory mucosa relative to the total inspiration.

Visualization of airflow velocity vectors further clarified these quantitative findings. In younger models, inspiratory airflow predominantly passed through the middle meatus, with only a limited portion of vectors deviating upward toward the olfactory cleft (Fig. 1 C). Vectors approaching the superior nasal cavity tended to be deflected by the nasal valve and turbinates, resulting in a relatively poorly ventilated olfactory region. In contrast, the late-adolescent models exhibited a more direct pathway, where expanded olfactory cleft dimensions allowed a greater density of high-velocity vectors to access the olfactory mucosa efficiently.

Odorant transport dynamics

Using physiologically relevant inspiratory flow rates derived from ICRP Publication [13], we computed the time-dependent odorant concentration in the olfactory region. The spatial distributions and temporal profiles exhibited clear age-dependent patterns (Fig. 2A–C).

Odorant transport dynamics under physiologically scaled flow rates. (A) Age-dependent odorant distributions. Representative images display the spatial propagation of the normalized odorant concentration field () within the nasal cavity during inspiration. Younger children exhibit more limited and delayed distribution patterns compared to older subjects. (B) Time-dependent odorant concentration (olfactory region average). Line graphs show the temporal profiles of the volume-averaged normalized odorant concentration within the predefined olfactory-region mask (dimensionless). Younger children exhibit longer latency and lower peak values than late-adolescent models. Note that the rise-peak-decay behavior reflects the passage of a finite tracer bolus through the olfactory region and subsequent washout by clean air () at the breathing-zone boundary, rather than a constant-concentration inlet condition. (C) Time course of normalized odorant concentration under ICRP-based physiological flow rates. The temporal profiles of the volume-averaged normalized odorant concentration within the predefined olfactory-region mask (dimensionless) are shown for each age-specific model, using age- and sex-specific inspiratory flow rates derived from ICRP Publication 89. Late-adolescent models (15–18 years) exhibit a rapid rise and a high peak in normalized concentration shortly after the onset of inspiration. In contrast, younger children show a slower build-up and lower peak levels. The 3-year-old model, in particular, demonstrates a delayed, gradually increasing profile with no distinct sharp peak. These results indicate that odorant access to the olfactory region remains less efficient in early childhood, even when physiological, age-appropriate flow rates are applied. For clarity and space constraints, panels (A) and (B) show only representative ages, whereas panel (C) includes all available age-specific models (including the remaining cases)

Specifically, the normalized concentration curves (Fig. 2C) revealed distinct quantitative differences. In the late-adolescent models (15–18 years), the odorant concentration rose rapidly after inspiration onset, reaching a high peak within approximately 0.2–0.3 s before decaying as the odorized air passed through the nasal cavity. In contrast, the 3-year-old models displayed a markedly delayed and blunted response, characterized by a gradual, nearly monotonic increase in concentration over the inspiratory phase without a distinct peak. The 6–12-year-old subjects showed intermediate behavior, with earlier and higher peaks than the 3-year-olds, but these were still notably lower than those of the late-adolescent group. These findings indicate that even when age- and sex-dependent physiological flow rates are applied, odorant delivery to the olfactory region remains substantially less efficient in younger children compared to late adolescents. This suggests that the age-related improvement in olfactory transport is strongly influenced by the underlying expansion of the olfactory cleft and morphological maturation, rather than being solely a function of increased inspiratory flow magnitude.

Discussion

Mechanistic insights into pediatric olfactory airflow

The present computational study provides a mechanistic description of how age-dependent changes in nasal anatomy influence olfactory airflow and odorant transport from early childhood through late adolescence. While our previous publication provided a detailed volumetric assessment of the pediatric nasal cavity [10], it did not investigate the functional consequences of these anatomical changes. The present study extends those morphological findings by applying CFD-based analysis to directly visualize and quantify the impact of growth-related remodeling on airflow dynamics.

Using CT-derived, patient-specific models spanning ages 3 to 18 years, we first demonstrated that the olfactory cleft and adjacent superior nasal cavity undergo marked enlargement and improved aeration with growth. In the youngest models, the olfactory cleft was narrow and poorly ventilated, and inspiratory streamlines predominantly passed through the middle meatus, with only a small fraction of flow reaching the superior region. By late adolescence, the olfactory cleft volume and its connection to the main nasal airway had increased substantially, redirecting a larger proportion of inspiratory air toward the olfactory region (Fig. 1).

Odorant transport dynamics

When odorant transport was simulated on these age-specific airflow fields, clear age-dependent differences emerged in both the temporal profile and magnitude of odorant delivery to the olfactory region. Younger children exhibited a delayed and blunted build-up of odorant concentration, often characterized by a gradual, monotonic increase without a distinct peak. In contrast, the 15–18-year-old models displayed a rapid rise and high peak in olfactory concentration, followed by a decay as the odorized air passed through the nasal cavity. This pattern indicates that anatomical maturation enables more efficient and timely access of odorized air to the olfactory mucosa, potentially correlating with the psychophysical development of olfactory discrimination speeds reported in literature.

Anatomical geometry vs. flow rate magnitude

A critical question is whether the observed age differences are driven by anatomy or simply by larger respiratory volumes. Our findings strongly support the dominance of anatomical factors. First, the olfactory inflow fraction (OIF, %)—the normalized percentage of inhaled air that enters the olfactory cleft—increased with age, demonstrating that the superior nasal cavity becomes aerodynamically more accessible independent of the total flow rate. Furthermore, this qualitative pattern appears robust to the choice of inspiratory flow condition. As shown in our sensitivity analysis (Supplementary Fig. S4S4A-C), simulations imposing a constant flow rate (e.g., 15 L/min) across all ages yielded the same ordering as the physiologically scaled ICRP simulations: late-adolescent models consistently achieved earlier and higher concentration peaks than younger children. If lower olfactory delivery in young children were primarily due to smaller tidal volumes, applying equalized flow rates would be expected to normalize the profiles. The persistence of these differences suggests that the primary limiting factor is structural—specifically, the narrow, poorly aerated olfactory cleft and constricted nasal valve in early childhood—rather than ventilatory.

Clinical implications

Our findings corroborate prior adult-focused CFD investigations emphasizing the importance of olfactory cleft patency and extend them to the pediatric population [18, 19]. In young children, underdeveloped nasal anatomy emerges as a key limiting factor in olfactory function. Clinically, this suggests that olfactory testing in young children should be interpreted in the context of developmental anatomy; deviations from normative performance may reflect immature structural conditions rather than pathological dysfunction. This perspective is essential when evaluating pediatric patients for suspected olfactory impairment.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the analysis was conducted in a relatively modest, single-center cohort of 11 pediatric patients. With only one or two subjects per age category, this study functions as an exploratory computational case series rather than a population-based statistical study. The inability to fully separate age effects from inter-individual variability or sex-related differences is a significant constraint. Therefore, the reported trends should be interpreted with caution and validated in larger, age-stratified cohorts.

Second, the anatomical models were reconstructed from static clinical CT scans. While consistent with standard CFD workflows, this neglects dynamic changes in the nasal valve during the respiratory cycle. Additionally, the olfactory region was defined geometrically (upper 20% of nasal height) as a surrogate for the olfactory epithelium. While pragmatic, this definition cannot capture histological variability between subjects.

Third, the “frozen-flow” approximation and rigid-wall assumption were employed to reduce computational cost. While justified for the quiet breathing conditions simulated here, these simplifications may underestimate unsteady effects that occur during forceful sniffing. Future studies incorporating fully coupled unsteady simulations and subject-specific sniffing profiles would allow for a more comprehensive assessment of active olfactory exploration.

In conclusion, the anatomical remodeling of the pediatric nasal cavity is a fundamental driver of olfactory transport efficiency. By elucidating how nasal growth optimizes airflow to the olfactory cleft, these findings offer a mechanistic foundation for understanding pediatric olfactory development.
