School climate as a mediator between job satisfaction and career sustainability among native language teachers in rural Türkiye
Authors: Tuğrul Gökmen Şahin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the relationships among job satisfaction, school climate, and career sustainability among native language teachers working in rural regions of Türkiye, with a particular focus on the mediating role of school climate. Designed within a correlational research framework, the study aims to address a gap in the literature regarding factors influencing teachers’ long-term professional continuity in rural educational settings. Data were collected from 313 teachers using the Career Sustainability Scale, the Job Satisfaction Short Index, and the School Climate Scale, and analyzed through structural equation modeling. The validity and reliability of the measurement instruments were confirmed via confirmatory factor analysis. Findings indicate a strong and significant positive association between job satisfaction and career sustainability, underscoring the central role of job satisfaction in supporting teachers’ long-term engagement in rural contexts. Job satisfaction was also positively related to perceptions of school climate. However, no significant relationship emerged between school climate and career sustainability. Mediation analysis further revealed that school climate does not significantly mediate the link between job satisfaction and career sustainability. Overall, the results suggest that teachers’ career sustainability in rural settings is shaped predominantly by job satisfaction, individual efficacy perceptions, and structural conditions rather than by school climate.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04676-2.

Introduction

Teaching, as a profession, may be counted among the central pillars of educational development and sustainability in rural contexts where resource constraints and socioeconomic challenges can markedly shape teachers’ experiences and retention. Job satisfaction is a key component of this context, as it not only influences teacher retention but also affects the overall quality of instruction [1]. Yet, it is often undermined by limited professional development opportunities, comparatively low salaries, and insufficient community support in rural settings [2]. These conditions highlight the need to examine the mechanisms through which rural teachers’ job satisfaction informs their career sustainability and, particularly, understand the mediating role of school climate in this relation.

The learning environment is likely to mediate teachers’ job satisfaction and career sustainability. Characterized by supportive leadership, collegiality, and a culture of appreciation, a positive school climate was shown to enhance teachers’ job satisfaction and retention [3, 4]. In rural regions, where teachers often confront unique challenges, a nurturing climate can mitigate feelings of isolation and foster greater engagement. This highlights the importance of cultivating such environments to sustain teachers’ long-term career trajectories [5]. These findings, therefore, support the notion that school climate may serve as a critical mediating variable shaping teachers’ professional commitment and career sustainability within rural contexts.

The systemic challenges faced by rural schools could be resolved by comprehensive, structural policy interventions. Policy measures that support teacher recruitment, professional commitment, and ongoing professional development are essential not only for enhancing job satisfaction but also for establishing a sustainable human resource structure in rural educational institutions [6–8]. When the interconnected dynamics among teacher satisfaction, learning ecosystem, and professional continuity are clearly understood, stakeholders are better positioned to design targeted intervention strategies that can elevate the quality of teaching and improve educational outcomes in rural areas.

Teachers’ job satisfaction and career sustainability

The link between teachers’ job satisfaction and career sustainability occupies a key position in educational research as it reflects an enduring challenge inherent in the teaching profession [9]. Job satisfaction influences teachers’ retention, commitment, and overall well-being. Moreover, factors such as self-efficacy, support systems, and leadership styles interact in complex ways with job satisfaction and collectively shape teachers’ career sustainability.

The literature consistently demonstrates that job satisfaction plays a decisive role in shaping teachers’ intentions to remain in the profession. High levels of satisfaction positively influence teachers’ professional commitment and inclination to sustain their careers [10]. This effect becomes even more salient when considered alongside related psychological and professional variables (e.g., self-efficacy). Indeed, elevated levels of self-efficacy were shown to enhance teachers’ job satisfaction and contribute to their long-term professional development [11, 12]. These findings suggest that reinforcing teachers’ self-efficacy may foster greater satisfaction and commitment, which, in turn, supports sustained engagement in the profession.

Teaching is a profession whose sustainability largely depends on teachers’ subjective evaluations of their work, namely their job satisfaction. Job satisfaction is conceptualized as a multidimensional construct encompassing individuals’ cognitive and affective evaluations of their professional roles. This framework provides a functional basis for understanding the core components of job satisfaction and has been widely supported in educational research [13, 14]. Prior research consistently emphasizes that sustainable success in education is closely linked to the quality of teaching and the professional stability of educators [15, 16]. Within this context, job satisfaction functions as a critical mechanism shaping teachers’ psychological well-being, professional commitment, and decisions to remain in the profession. These processes directly contribute to career sustainability, positioning job satisfaction as a central predictor of teachers’ long-term professional continuity [14, 17, 18].

In sum, the relation between teachers’ job satisfaction and career sustainability reflects a multifaceted interplay of factors, including self-efficacy, organizational support, collegial relationships, and professional calling. Addressing these factors through an integrated perspective has the potential to enhance teachers’ job satisfaction, foster their professional continuity, and facilitate more sustainable career pathways for them.

Teachers’ job satisfaction and school climate

Teachers’ job satisfaction is a multidimensional construct that reflects their emotional and professional fulfillment regarding their working conditions and professional roles. Research indicated that high levels of job satisfaction could enhance instructional quality, promote student engagement, and contribute to a positive learning environment by reducing teacher turnover [19, 20]. Therefore, teacher job satisfaction emerges as a key determinant of an effective teaching and learning environment.

Prior research has frequently examined the relationship between school climate and teachers’ job satisfaction, often highlighting that supportive and well-structured school environments are associated with higher levels of teacher satisfaction. A positive school climate characterized by safety, collegiality, instructional support, and constructive organizational practices has been linked to improved professional experiences and well-being among teachers [21–24]. Teachers who perceive their environments as supportive tend to report greater satisfaction, which may contribute to more effective instructional engagement and positive educational outcomes [23, 25]. While these studies primarily conceptualize school climate as an antecedent of job satisfaction, the present study adopts a directional framework in which teachers’ job satisfaction is modeled as a predictor of perceived school climate in order to examine its mediating role in career sustainability.

While reciprocal associations between job satisfaction and school climate have been discussed in the literature [26, 27], the present study adopts a theoretically grounded directional framework to examine how teachers’ job satisfaction shapes perceptions of school climate and, in turn, career sustainability. Prior findings indicate that both constructs are independently associated with important educational outcomes, including teacher retention, professional engagement, and instructional effectiveness [28, 29]. Rather than modeling a bidirectional relationship, this study focuses on a specific causal pathway to clarify the mediating function of school climate within a coherent theoretical structure. From this perspective, identifying the factors that connect teachers’ job satisfaction to career sustainability remains a timely and meaningful area of inquiry, particularly for native language teachers who play a central role in developing learners’ expressive and receptive skills.

Although many studies conceptualize school climate as an antecedent of teacher job satisfaction, the present study adopts an alternative perspective by considering job satisfaction as a factor that may shape teachers’ perceptions of school climate. From a psychological standpoint, teachers who experience higher levels of job satisfaction tend to evaluate their working environments more positively and interpret organizational conditions in a more supportive manner. In this sense, job satisfaction can function as an individual-level lens through which teachers perceive and interpret school climate. Previous research suggests that teachers’ affective evaluations of their profession influence how they perceive collegial relationships, administrative support, and the overall organizational atmosphere [30, 31]. Therefore, conceptualizing job satisfaction as a predictor of perceived school climate is theoretically plausible, particularly in rural educational contexts where individual perceptions may play a stronger role in shaping evaluations of the school environment.

School climate and career sustainability

Understanding the relation between school climate and career sustainability is essential for designing efficient learning processes and building environments that support meaningful professional growth. Establishing a positive school climate is not only fundamental for academic achievement but also for fostering one’s long-term career adaptability and development. For example, supportive interactions in these environments enhance teachers’ motivation and professional learning, while a favorable school atmosphere plays a pivotal role in sustaining teacher performance, overall job satisfaction, and long-term career continuity [32]. This body of evidence may provide a reasonable foundation for considering school climate as a mediating variable in the relation between teachers’ job satisfaction and their career sustainability.

Extensive research demonstrates that perceived school climate has a significant influence not only on teachers but also on students’ experiences and academic outcomes. A positive school climate facilitates the development of healthy teacher–student interactions, which, in turn, enhances students’ academic engagement and performance [33]. In addition, factors such as administrative support and collaborative peer relationships play a crucial role in fostering teachers’ professional competence and self-efficacy. Smith [34] found that supportive school relationships and effective administrative practices significantly contribute to a positive school climate and teacher job satisfaction. Conversely, school environments marked by conflict and inadequate structural support are associated with diminished job satisfaction and increased turnover rates linked to professional burnout [29].

The interconnected nature of the factors discussed urges the need to view school climate as key to teachers’ career sustainability. Effective school leaders cultivate supportive relationships that promote professional growth and continuity; therefore, they can create a school environment grounded in positive communication [35, 36]. The implications for theory and practice are clear: schools can enhance satisfaction, reduce burnout, and ultimately improve student achievement by investing in school climate initiatives.

Teachers’ job satisfaction and career sustainability are widely recognized as key constructs shaped by school climate [19, 20, 22–24, 32]. However, existing research seems to overlook the context for native language teachers. Native language teachers occupy a central position in students’ academic development as well as in the formation of their social identities. Beyond fostering linguistic competence, they provide a foundation for interdisciplinary learning processes. Native language education extends far beyond the transmission of grammar rules, literacy techniques, and literary knowledge,it directly contributes to identity construction, the reinforcement of cultural belonging, and the development of critical literacy skills [37, 38]. Accordingly, the quality of native language instruction and the professional preparation of teachers working in this field exert a direct influence on broader learning outcomes within educational systems. For this reason, native language education represents a priority domain within educational policy and teacher preparation programs [39, 38].

Native language teachers also play a vital role in integrating the cultural meanings embedded in language and students’ multicultural experiences into instructional practices, thereby supporting identity development and intercultural competence [40, 38]. By adopting pedagogical approaches responsive to linguistic and cultural diversity, they further contribute to promoting equity and accessibility in education [40]. Importantly, the impact of native language instruction is not confined to language classrooms. Teachers’ patterns of classroom discourse significantly shape students’ learning processes in science, mathematics, and social studies. Through verbal scaffolding and conceptual explanations, teachers foster interdisciplinary meaning-making and academic language development, demonstrating that strong native language education positively influences the overall learning climate across the curriculum [37, 41]. In this context, native language teachers assume a key role in equipping students with the linguistic competencies necessary to support discipline-based academic achievement [41, 42].

This gap is noteworthy, as native language teachers play a central role not only in knowledge transmission but also in preserving cultural heritage, fostering civic engagement, and supporting students’ cognitive and academic development [43]. Although prior literature frequently emphasizes that investments in school climate can enhance teacher satisfaction and educational outcomes, the present study adopts a different theoretical lens by conceptualizing teachers’ job satisfaction as an internal professional evaluation that shapes how educators interpret and experience their working environment. From this perspective, school climate is treated as a perceptual construct that may be influenced by teachers’ psychological and professional states rather than solely as an external organizational condition. Modeling job satisfaction as a predictor allows the study to examine how teachers’ internal evaluations translate into perceptions of school climate and subsequently influence career sustainability. This directional framework is therefore theoretically grounded in the assumption that individual professional appraisals can shape contextual perceptions, making the proposed mediation model conceptually coherent and aligned with the study’s research objectives. Building on this significance, it is reasonable to expect that school climate may function as a critical mediating variable in the relation between job satisfaction and career sustainability among native language teachers. Examining this mediating pathway not only addresses a clear gap in the literature but also has the potential to tailor policies and practices to enhance these teachers’ motivation, commitment, and long-term development. Method

Research design

This study employs a correlational design to examine the relation between teachers’ job satisfaction and career sustainability and how this relation is shaped in the context of school climate. This design is suitable for identifying relations without manipulating study conditions [44]. The choice of this approach also aligns with our aim to capture both direct and indirect pathways between the study variables [45].

Measures

Career sustainability scale

Chin et al. [46] developed the Career Sustainability Scale (CSC) to assess employees’ levels of career sustainability. The original version of this measurement instrument consists of four dimensions and twelve items. These dimensions are Resourceful (3 items), Flexibility (3 items), Renewability (3 items), and Integrative Capacity (3 items). In the original version of the scale, the internal consistency reliability coefficient (Cronbach’s alpha) was calculated as α = 0.91. The sample items in the original scale are as follows: “My career enables me to have a good standard of living” (Resourceful), “My career allows me to continuously learn new things” (Flexibility), “My career provides me with opportunities to update my skills” (Renewability), and “My career enables me to critically evaluate information obtained from different sources” (Integrative Capacity). It was adapted into Turkish by Arar and Çetiner [47]. The adapted version preserves the original structure consisting of 12 items across two subscales. Items are rated on a 6-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (Strongly disagree) to 6 (Strongly agree). Arar and Çetiner [47] reported excellent internal consistency for the Turkish version (α = 0.96). In this study, the scale also demonstrated a high level of reliability (α = 0.95).

Short index of job satisfaction

The 18-item Job Satisfaction Scale (JSS) was originally developed by Brayfield and Rothe [48] for the context of teachers. The scale was later adapted into a 5-item short version by Sinval and Marôco [49] for the Portuguese context. Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (Strongly disagree) to 5 (Strongly agree). In this study, since the short form of the Short Index of Job Satisfaction (SIJS) translated from Portuguese into Turkish was used, the scale was employed as a unidimensional construct in line with the approach adopted by Özbilen et al. [10]. The sample items in the original scale are as follows: 2. Most days I am enshusiastic about my job. 4. enjoyment in my work I find real. The Portuguese adaptation demonstrated high internal consistency (α = 0.89). The Turkish version also demonstrated high internal consistency (α = 0.854) and test–retest reliability (α = 0.857, [10]. In this study, we calculated high internal consistency for the JSS (α = 0.87).

School climate scale

We measured participating teachers’ perceived school climate using the School Climate Scale (SCS; [50]. The scale consists of 23 items within five subscales. The distribution of sub-dimensions and items in the scale is as follows: "Democracy and School Commitment – 6 items", "Leadership and Interaction – 6 items", "Achievement Factors – 4 items", "Sincerity – 3 items", and "Conflict – item". The sample items in the original scale are as follows: 1.Teacher–student relationships in our school are democratic (Democracy and School Commitment). 10.Problems in our school are discussed openly (Leadership and Interaction). 14.The performance level of teachers in our school is high (Achievement Factors). 18.Staff members in our school enjoy spending time together (Sincerity). 22. There are social groupings among the staff members in our school (Conflict). The internal consistency coefficients reported in the original study ranged from α = 0.730 to 0.908, while test–retest reliability coefficients ranged from α = 0.826 to 0.962. In this study, we calculated Cronbach’s alpha to be α = 0.84 for the total SCS score.

Statistical analysis

This research examines whether school climate functions as a mediator in the relation between Turkish native language teachers’ job satisfaction and career sustainability. Descriptive results are reported using frequencies (n), percentages (%), means (M), and standard deviations (SD). Preliminary checks for univariate normality indicated that the dataset contained no missing continuous values and that skewness and kurtosis estimates fell within the recommended ± 1 threshold [51]. Prior to conducting the main analyses, multivariate outliers were screened using Mahalanobis distance based on the three scale scores (n = 313, df = 3, p < 0.001, [52]. We initially examined the associations between the variables through Pearson correlation coefficients. Then, we employed structural equation modeling (SEM) to evaluate both the measurement model and the proposed hypotheses. All measurement instruments underwent confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) to assess construct validity and ensure that the overall measurement model demonstrated adequate fit. We performed all statistical procedures using SPSS 27.0 and SPSS AMOS 24.0 and adopted a significance level of p < 0.05 throughout the analyses [53].

Discussion and conclusions

This study explored the associations between school climate, job satisfaction, and career sustainability among native language teachers employed in rural areas of Türkiye. We further examined whether school climate mediates the relation between job satisfaction and career sustainability. The results indicated job satisfaction to be positively associated with perceived school climate and career sustainability. Contrary to expectations, however, we found no significant relation between school climate and career sustainability. Likewise, school climate did not mediate the relation between job satisfaction and career sustainability. Building on these results, this section provides a detailed interpretation and discussion of each hypothesis.

In our analyses, job satisfaction significantly predicted career sustainability, which may highlight the central role of job satisfaction in shaping the long-term professional trajectories of native language teachers working in rural areas. Consistent with this result, Özbilen et al. [10] also identified a positive association between job satisfaction and career sustainability among native language teachers. Similarly, Skaalvik and Skaalvik [64] reported a significant link between Norwegian teachers’ job satisfaction and intentions to remain in the profession. Additional evidence from the Turkish context indicated a moderate relationship between organizational commitment and job satisfaction among 371 teachers working in rural Türkiye [65]. Numerous other studies consistently demonstrated that higher levels of job satisfaction are associated with greater teacher retention [4, 20, 66]. However, contrasting evidence was reported in a study conducted in Qatar, which found no significant association between job satisfaction and career sustainability [67]. In this study, we also found a moderate association between job satisfaction and career sustainability. Taken together, our results suggest that job satisfaction is likely to assume a key role not only in supporting teachers’ short-term motivation but also in sustaining their long-term professional commitment and continuity. The results, therefore, align with a well-established body of literature emphasizing that job satisfaction remains one of the strongest predictors of sustainable career pathways even in rural contexts. From the perspective of native language teachers, this finding carries particular significance because their professional roles extend beyond subject instruction to include the development of students’ academic language, cultural identity, and critical literacy. Sustained job satisfaction among these teachers is therefore closely linked not only to individual well-being but also to the continuity and quality of language-based learning processes. In contexts where native language instruction forms the foundation of interdisciplinary academic development, teachers’ professional commitment becomes a stabilizing force that supports long-term educational outcomes. Accordingly, the observed association between job satisfaction and career sustainability suggests that strengthening native language teachers’ professional satisfaction may contribute to preserving instructional continuity, cultural transmission, and academic language development within the broader educational ecosystem [37, 38].

The complex relation between teachers’ job satisfaction and perceptions of school climate represents a central focus in contemporary educational research, as both constructs jointly shape instructional quality, teacher retention, and student outcomes. While being widely recognized as a key factor for teachers’ performance, commitment, and well-being, job satisfaction simultaneously influences broader school climate dynamics [20, 68]. Prior research consistently shows that positive school climates tend to foster higher levels of job satisfaction, which, in turn, enhances instructional effectiveness, collaboration, and teachers’ willingness to engage meaningfully with students. In parallel with these findings, we found that job satisfaction significantly predicted teachers’ perceived school climate, supporting our second hypothesis. This result mirrors earlier studies demonstrating that teachers’ self-efficacy is closely linked to their job satisfaction [69, 70]. Aldridge and Fraser [71] also identified significant relations between school climate, teacher self-efficacy, and professional satisfaction.

The literature underscores the strong link between supportive school climates and higher job satisfaction. Research further indicates that teacher self-efficacy serves as a mediator in the relation between school climate and job satisfaction, which suggests that teachers with positive perceptions of school environment tend to feel more competent and satisfied in their roles [68, 72]. Additional studies highlight that teachers with higher levels of job satisfaction are more effective in cultivating positive disciplinary climates and contributing to more conducive learning environments for students [22, 23]. Taken together, the acceptance of our second hypothesis implies that job satisfaction could activate both cognitive and emotional mechanisms that reinforce teachers’ perceptions of school climate. In this regard, job satisfaction may emerge as an important factor in fostering school climate and broader educational outcomes.

The link between teachers’ perceived school climate and career sustainability has become a significant area of inquiry in educational research. Contrary to expectations, we could conclude a significant relation between these constructs and rejected our third hypothesis. It can reasonably be asserted that even teachers’ positive perceptions of school climate do not directly translate into a stronger capacity or intention to sustain their careers over time. In the literature, however, positive school climates were found to yield beneficial psychological outcomes for both students and educators [73] . Previous research also demonstrated that learning environments characterized by collaboration and mutual respect are typically associated with lower teacher turnover rates [74]. Similarly, Lochmiller et al. [75] reported that school leadership styles substantially shape teachers’ perceptions of school climate and influence their decisions to remain in the profession. Ju et al. [76] found that teachers with higher emotional intelligence experience reduced burnout and greater job satisfaction when they work in positive school climates.

An unexpected finding of the present study is that school climate did not significantly predict career sustainability. One possible explanation for this result may be related to the contextual characteristics of rural educational settings. In rural regions, teachers’ career sustainability may be influenced more strongly by personal, professional, and socio-economic factors rather than by organizational climate alone. For instance, factors such as job security, limited alternative employment opportunities, professional commitment, or attachment to the local community may play a more decisive role in teachers’ decisions to remain in the profession [77, 78]. In this context, teachers may continue their careers despite experiencing less favorable school climate conditions. This interpretation suggests that individual-level motivations and structural factors may outweigh organizational climate in shaping long-term career sustainability in rural education contexts [79].

Interpreting these findings within the rural Turkish context adds an important layer of meaning. Rural schools often operate under structural constraints such as limited resources, professional isolation, and reduced access to institutional support. Under such conditions, teachers’ perceptions of their work environment and professional satisfaction may exert a disproportionately strong influence on their willingness to remain in the profession. For native language teachers in rural settings, sustained engagement is especially critical because they frequently serve as central figures in maintaining academic continuity and cultural learning. The results therefore suggest that enhancing job satisfaction and supportive school environments in rural areas is not merely an organizational concern but a strategic necessity for sustaining educational equity and stability.

Much of the prior research also suggests that negative perceptions of school climate often trigger teacher burnout and threaten career sustainability. Environmental factors (e.g., school policies, peer relations, and administrative support) were shown to either mitigate or exacerbate burnout among teachers [80]. Insufficient support or a toxic work culture frequently elevates stress levels and prompts some teachers to consider leaving the profession [81]. One plausible explanation for the discrepancy between previous findings and our result may relate to the nature of the SCS, which primarily captures school climate from a positive standpoint. The interpretation of the findings should be considered in light of the measurement instruments employed in the present study. Specifically, school climate was operationalized through a scale that primarily captures teachers’ perceptions of supportive, collaborative, and relational aspects of the school environment. Therefore, the observed relationships between school climate, job satisfaction, and career sustainability should be interpreted within the scope of these dimensions, rather than as reflecting the full range of possible school climate characteristics.

It is important to acknowledge that school climate is a multidimensional construct that may be conceptualized and measured in different ways, such as emphasizing cooperative, competitive, or control-oriented dimensions. Consequently, the use of alternative measurement tools focusing on different facets of school climate might yield different patterns of relationships. From this perspective, the present findings do not suggest that all forms of school climate are uniformly associated with the examined variables; rather, they indicate that the specific dimensions of school climate assessed in this study are meaningfully related to teachers’ job satisfaction and career sustainability. Future research employing diverse measurement instruments is recommended to further clarify how different conceptualizations of school climate may influence these relationships.

Yet, for teachers working in rural contexts, career sustainability may be shaped less by school climate itself and more by structural factors (e.g., working conditions, economic expectations, and access to professional development), as well as individual-level variables (e.g., professional competence, intrinsic motivation, and job satisfaction). In rural settings, these structural and personal factors may overshadow the influence of school climate and weaken its direct predictive effect on career sustainability.

Our findings showed no mediating effect of perceived school climate on the relation between teachers’ job satisfaction and career sustainability. The primary reason for the absence of mediation lies in the fact that school climate failed to predict career sustainability in the tested model. The robust effect of job satisfaction on school climate and career sustainability suggests that teachers’ long-term career continuity may largely be shaped by psychological and professional satisfaction rather than their broader perceptions of the school environment. Therefore, our result implies that any direct influence of school climate on career sustainability may be limited, which reinforces the notion that career sustainability is inherently multifaceted.

As Day and Gu [79] argue, teachers’ ability to sustain their careers is closely tied to personal motivation, opportunities for professional growth, and supportive organizational environments. The literature similarly suggests that school climate may exert its influence not through direct pathways but through indirect mechanisms operating via constructs such as perceived organizational support and job satisfaction. For instance, Murdiyani et al. [82] demonstrated that job satisfaction mediates the effects of perceived organizational support and proactive personality on work engagement. In this light, our findings could not rule out the possibility of indirect or more complex relational pathways through which school climate may influence career sustainability,instead, they highlight the need for future research to examine these nuanced mechanisms. Although several studies emphasize that supportive school climates enhance job satisfaction, they also caution that such effects may be insufficient to directly predict teachers’ long-term career sustainability [83, 84]. Factors such as individual resilience, opportunities for professional advancement, and broader labor market conditions may overshadow or suppress the potential mediating role attributed to school climate [85]. These considerations help explain why the mediation pathway did not work in our study and point out the importance of considering both contextual and individual factors when modeling career sustainability among teachers in rural settings.

Overall, our results suggest that organizational factors such as school climate may play a relatively limited indirect role in the career sustainability of teachers working in rural settings, whereas job satisfaction may be a strong and independent predictor of it. The rejection of the mediation hypothesis, therefore, advances an important theoretical insight: career sustainability appears to be driven primarily by individual dispositions and structural conditions rather than by broader organizational perceptions in rural contexts.

Practical implications

The findings of this study suggest several practical implications, particularly for native language teachers working in rural areas of Türkiye. The observed link between job satisfaction and career sustainability indicates that supporting teachers’ professional well-being should be viewed as a long-term investment in educational continuity. In rural schools, where teachers often work under resource limitations and professional isolation, efforts such as accessible professional development, balanced workloads, and collegial support structures may help sustain teachers’ engagement in their roles. For school leaders, recognizing the instructional and cultural responsibilities carried by native language teachers can foster a more supportive working environment, which in turn reinforces professional commitment. From a career development standpoint, mentoring practices and collaborative learning opportunities tailored to language instruction may strengthen teachers’ sense of purpose and resilience. Taken together, these implications highlight that enhancing teachers’ professional satisfaction is closely tied to sustaining instructional stability and educational equity in rural contexts.

Limitations and future directions

Although our study offers valuable insights, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design of the study hinders the causality between the associations concluded. Second, the use of self-report measures carries a residual risk of common method bias, even if this risk is relatively minimal. Third, the sample was also restricted to native language teachers working in rural regions of Türkiye, which limits the generalizability of the findings to other geographical contexts or teacher groups. Finally, the nonsignificant structural link between school climate and career sustainability indicates that this relation may be more context-dependent or influenced by cultural, environmental, or scale-related nuances that need further examination.

Future research should employ longitudinal designs to more comprehensively elucidate the dynamics that shape teachers’ long-term job satisfaction and career sustainability. Such designs enable the examination of changes in key variables over time, while the incorporation of multiple data sources such as administrator interviews, classroom observations and peer evaluationscan reduce common method bias and support a more objective and multifaceted assessment of school climate. This approach would provide a more robust foundation for developing effective interventions aimed at enhancing teachers’ professional well-being and sustaining their careers. Finally, integrating additional variables such as psychological capital, organizational commitment, professional development opportunities, or burnout into the analytical model would offer a more comprehensive understanding of the multidimensional nature of career sustainability.
