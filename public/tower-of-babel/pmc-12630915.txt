Robust missing data reconstruction in schizophrenia using tracking-removed autoencoder with fuzzy confidence integration
Authors: Moazzama Mateen, Ubaida Fatima
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeural network models for outcome prediction play a pivotal role in neurological disease research, particularly for baseline risk assessment. Schizophrenia, a complex and relatively rare neuropsychiatric disorder, presents significant diagnostic challenges due to its heterogeneous symptomatology and reliance on subjective clinical evaluations. One of the primary barriers to accurate modelling in Schizophrenia research is the prevalence of incomplete clinical data, often stemming from inconsistent reporting, participant dropout, and variability in assessment protocols. To address these limitations, an advanced deep learning framework has been developed that integrates the Tracking-Removed Autoencoder (TRAE) with Multi-View Progressive Training (MVPT). This architecture is specifically designed for predicting missing features within schizophrenia datasets. The model treats missing data as learnable information rather than simply marking it as absent. By restructuring how inputs are processed, it incorporates these gaps directly into training, allowing the system to detect meaningful patterns and remain robust even when clinical records are incomplete. Fuzzy confidence measures assess how reliable each imputed feature is by determining its membership in clinically meaningful categories, or fuzzy sets. This methodology, grounded in fuzzy set theory, can generate linguistic confidence measures, converting fuzzy memberships into interpretable descriptors that convey meaningful information to clinicians and researchers. The linguistic framework captures uncertainty and complexity while facilitating the integration of higher-level clinical knowledge. Within the TRAE + MVPT framework, this means the model can not only fill in missing data but also indicate how much confidence clinicians and researchers should place in each reconstructed value. Such quantification is particularly important in schizophrenia, where overlapping symptoms and diagnostic ambiguity often reduce clarity. This novel framework strengthens psychiatric data modelling by improving both the interpretability and reliability of reconstructed clinical features, directly addressing challenges of diagnostic uncertainty. It supports more transparent decision-making and clearer symptom tracking, while in research it provides a rigorous way to evaluate both model reliability and imputation quality. Introduction

Schizophrenia is one of the most incapacitating neuro affective disorders in psychiatry, impacting over 21 million individuals globally, with a higher prevalence in men than women. It is characterized by profound disturbances in thought, behavior, hallucinations, and delusions. Although the exact causes remain unknown, research suggests that environmental factors and genetic predisposition interact in a complex manner1. While associated with abnormal brain structure and function, diagnosis is often influenced by clinician subjectivity2. Nevertheless, conventional neural networks exhibit limitations in interpretability, in assuming fixed noise, and in capturing intricate attribute relationships2. In recent years, the integration of intelligent systems in medical diagnostics has gained momentum, particularly for complex neurological disorders. While various network architectures have been developed to reconstruct missing features in disease diagnosis, there has been limited exploration of advanced neural imputation with fuzzy logic enhancement, particularly for brain abnormalities. However, neural networks (NN) face challenges in interpretability, limiting their effectiveness in applications where transparency is essential. In contrast, Fuzzy Inference Systems (FIS), rooted in Fuzzy Logic, effectively manage uncertainty and imprecision by using fuzzy rules that enable clear, adaptable, and intuitive reasoning3. There are few psychological therapies accepted by the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE), which makes their effective implementation in the National Health Service (NHS) challenging. Therefore, insights from relapse prevention studies and scalable, innovative approaches are essential to improve patient outcomes4.

Existing studies have employed questionnaires, visual cues, regression models, and machine learning techniques for neuro-related disease recognition and treatment. However, accurate detection of depression and related psychiatric conditions remains a significant challenge. In recent years, deep learning has been increasingly adopted to improve feature extraction and enhance classification accuracy in depression recognition and assessment tasks5. Autoencoders exhibit inherent limitations: they reconstruct missing values primarily based on patterns present in the training data and are prone to learning identity mappings, which constrains their capacity to capture complex inter-feature dependencies in neuropsychiatric datasets. To address these limitations, we propose a Tracking-Removed Autoencoder (TRAE) integrated with Multi-View Progressive Training (MVPT)6. TRAE introduces a dynamic input-hidden neuron structure to reduce self-trackability, enabling the network to capture cross-correlations among neurobiological features. Simultaneously, MVPT enables the inclusion of incomplete records during training, instead of excluding them, thereby improving reconstruction performance even when missing rates are high. Importantly, this strategy has seen limited application in neurological research, and its potential in psychiatric disorders remains largely untapped, presenting a promising direction for enhancing diagnosis and treatment. To evaluate the clinical relevance of the imputed values, fuzzy confidence is employed, highlighting their conformity with disease interpretations. In this disorder, where overlapping symptoms and diagnostic ambiguity are common, fuzzy confidence is especially valuable, as it quantifies the uncertainty associated with each predicted feature and highlights which reconstructed values are more reliable. Within these data, fuzzy confidence provides a quantifiable measure of certainty for each predicted value. This not only enhances the interpretability of imputed results but also supports clinicians in making more informed and cautious decisions in uncertain diagnostic contexts. The illness can be effectively treated when diagnosed early and accurately; hence, a computer-aided method with low computational complexity and high accuracy can support its identification7.

Schizophrenia

Cognitive dysfunction is a core manifestation of schizophrenia and one of the best predictors of long-term disability8. Schizophrenia is a chronic and debilitating mental disorder characterized by a range of symptoms including delusions hallucinations, disorganized thinking and speech, and significant cognitive deficits. Its early onset and progressive nature often lead to profound functional impairment and long-term disability. The impact of schizophrenia extends beyond the individual, affecting families and caregivers as well9. This severe psychiatric condition manifests through both positive symptoms (such as hallucinations and delusions) and negative symptoms (such as social withdrawal and lack of motivation).

Clinical features and symptomatology of negative schizophrenia

Depression is a common psychiatric disorder marked by sadness, loss of interest, and lack of attention, with both psychological and physical symptoms. Its causes involve complex biological, social, and psychological factors, and life events such as trauma, loss, or substance abuse can increase its risk4. Schizophrenia presents with both positive and negative symptomatology, with negative symptoms posing some of the most challenging aspects of the illness10. Nevertheless, therapeutic efforts have historically concentrated more on alleviating positive symptoms10. Negative symptoms in schizophrenia refer to a reduction or absence of normal functions related to motivation, emotional expression, and social engagement11. Rather than focusing solely on observable deficits, Skinner (1979) suggested that negative symptoms should be understood in terms of what individuals no longer do emphasizing behavioral absence as central to the disorder12. These symptoms are typically grouped into two primary subdomains: Avolition/Apathy (AA) and Diminished Emotional Expression (DEE)13. AA represents a marked decline in self-initiated, goal-directed behavior. According to the National Institute of Mental Health Consensus Development Conference, it encompasses a lack of motivation and purposeful activity, reflecting impairments in internal drive and behavioral engagement. It includes features such as blunted affect (a reduction in expressive facial and vocal emotions) and avolition (reduced persistence and initiation in achieving personal goals)13. DEE refers to a noticeable decrease in emotional expressiveness and communicative behavior, including reduced facial expressions, limited speech (alogia), and a flattened affect. These deficits hinder effective social interaction and can significantly impair a person’s ability to form and maintain interpersonal relationships12. Avolition-apathy and diminished emotional expression constitute central negative symptoms of schizophrenia, characterized by deficits in motivation, self-initiated action, and emotional responsiveness. These symptoms arise from disrupted neural circuits governing cognitive control, reward processing, and social behavior. Modeling such complex and heterogeneous manifestations in neural data is inherently challenging. Precise imputation of missing symptom data is crucial to reveal underlying neural patterns and improve clinical prediction and treatment optimization.

Interestingly, findings from various studies suggest that the AA subtype represents a more pathologically severe and phenomenologically distinct profile than DEE. This is notable despite DEE typically presenting with higher total scores on negative symptom scales14. Key features of Schizophrenia are summarized in Table 1; Fig. 1.

Data sets

Two schizophrenia-related datasets were employed in this study. The first dataset, sourced from www.kaggle.com, includes 5000 records with 9 symptom-based features, offering a broad representation of schizophrenia symptomatology, as outlined in Table 1. The second dataset focuses specifically on negative symptoms of schizophrenia and was obtained from PsychArchives, a reputable repository for psychological research data. This dataset, titled “Emotional Experience of People with Schizophrenia and People at Risk for Psychosis: A Meta-Analysis of Emotion Induction Studies” (PsychArchives, 2023), contains 31 entries and 22 feature, detailing the emotional and behavioral responses of individuals with schizophrenia to negative stimuli.

TRAE + MVPT framework:Novel algorithm

This study proposes a robust framework for predicting missing features in the Schizophrenia dataset by leveraging advanced neural network-based imputation model Tracking-Removed autoencoder (TRAE), integrated with Multi-View Progressive Training (MVPT), a technique that remains underexplored in the domain of disease modelling, particularly for neurological disorders and researchers are exploring genetics and advanced technologies to gain deeper insights into the brain’s structure15. The missing values are imputed using advanced neural network-based models, including the Denoising Autoencoder (DAE), a modified and simple neural network Tracking-Removed Autoencoder (TRAE), Multi-View Progressive Training (MVPT). Additionally, hybrid approaches combining TRAE with MVPT and DAE with MVPT are employed to further enhance imputation accuracy and robustness. This dual combination allows the framework to leverage the benefits of temporal context removal and progressive multi-view learning while thoroughly evaluating which pattern yields the most consistent and fluent performance across the dataset. However, not all imputed values can be assumed equally reliable. Instead of relying indiscriminately on all predictions, the framework incorporates fuzzy confidence estimation to allow a more nuanced interpretation of the results. Denoising autoencoder (DAE) structure

An autoencoder is a neural network that learns to reproduce its input at the output. To prevent it from merely learning an identity function which would fail to extract meaningful information, the hidden layer is typically designed with fewer neurons than the input and output layers, forcing the network to learn a compact, informative representation of the data6. The Denoising Autoencoder (DAE) is designed to reconstruct an original input vector from a corrupted version of itself. The model learns to remove noise and infer missing or distorted values, thus improving its robustness and generalization. MVPT training scheme for missing value imputation

In most neural network-based imputation methods, only complete records are used for training, which severely reduces training accuracy as the missing rate increases6. A more reasonable training approach involves utilizing incomplete records, rather than discarding them, for model training. To address this, we propose a training scheme called Multi-View Progressive Training (MVPT), which allows missing values to participate in the network’s training process alongside complete data.

Fuzzy confidence

The proliferation of information in culture has led to the simplification of life through scientific methods; however, decision-making remains complex. Fuzzy sets16, and their extensions, such as intuitionistic fuzzy sets (IFS)17 and complex fuzzy sets (CFS)18, provide tools to manage ambiguity. CFS, incorporating phase and amplitude terms, enhance data handling in decision-making. Researcher have developed aggregation operators, distance measures, and entropy measures in CFS for improved application in real-world problems19,20 &21. After imputation, fuzzy confidence helps reveal how well the imputed values conform to clinically meaningful interpretations.

Our fuzzy logic framework demonstrates consistent performance in modelling imprecise, patient-reported clinical features across different imputation methods. Features such as Fatigue, Pain, and Slowing exhibited higher fuzzy confidence scores (0.37–0.65), indicating strong reliability in their fuzzified representations and greater contribution to the soft diagnostic inference process. In contrast, features like Age and Marital Status showed lower scores (~ 0.16–0.24), reflecting natural variability and uncertainty in patient-reported data. Importantly, fuzzy confidence remained stable across AE, TRAE, AE + MVPT, and TRAE + MVPT imputations, confirming the robustness of the hybrid approach.

For instance, a married male with poor hygiene, moderate fatigue, and moderate schizophrenia proneness is fuzzified across multiple features. Here, Fatigue contributes strong diagnostic weight due to higher fuzzy confidence, while Hygiene and Marital Status introduce uncertainty. Despite this, the fuzzy model integrates all features to predict a moderate-to-high schizophrenia risk. Figures 6, 7, 8 and 9 further highlight that TRAE + MVPT maintain high fuzzy confidence when reconstructing categorical features, preserving underlying stimulus-response structures.

This innovative model leverages brain structure data to enhance feature prediction, thereby improving the accuracy, robustness, and reliability of schizophrenia diagnosis. Notably, this approach has not yet been explored extensively for neurological diseases, and its potential remains underexplored in the context of psychiatric disorders, offering a novel avenue for advancing diagnosis and treatment.

Discussion

Recent advances in computational neuroscience have provided powerful tools to unravel the complexity of psychiatric disorders, enabling more precise characterization and analysis of neural data associated with heterogeneous clinical presentations. The application of computational neuroscience to psychiatric research has shown great promise in bridging the gap between the phenomenological aspects of mental disorders and their underlying pathophysiological mechanisms22. In neuropsychiatric datasets, which often exhibit overlapping symptoms, missing or incomplete data can reduce the reliability of research findings and compromise diagnostic accuracy. To address this, the framework reconstructs incomplete data6, while fuzzy confidence quantifies the reliability of each imputed value, indicating how closely it aligns with the underlying patterns observed in complete patient records19. High fuzzy confidence reflects the preservation of critical correlations between neurological attributes, whereas low confidence signals potential uncertainty due to irregular missing patterns. By providing this measure, fuzzy confidence enables researchers to distinguish between robustly reconstructed features and those requiring cautious interpretation, enhancing both the interpretability and clinical utility of the imputed schizophrenia data. Furthermore, by translating fuzzy memberships into linguistic descriptors, such as natural-language indicators of reliability, this approach allows clinicians to quickly grasp the certainty of each reconstructed feature and make informed decisions in patient assessment and treatment planning23.

Computational complexity

The TRAE + MVPT model offers greater efficiency compared to some alternative approaches; however, training it with fuzzy confidence integration on large datasets can still be computationally intensive24,25. Extremely high rates of missing data or rare patterns not present in the training set may also reduce imputation reliability26,27. Finally, although fuzzy confidence enhances interpretability, real-world clinical validation is necessary to ensure that imputed values effectively support diagnostic and treatment decisions24.

Conclusion

In this study, among the many neural disorders affecting individuals worldwide, schizophrenia stands out as one of the most destructive and currently incurable mental illnesses. While it cannot be permanently cured, it can be managed to support a relatively normal life provided its features are thoroughly understood and accurately analyzed. However, the high degree of uncertainty and missing information in schizophrenia-related data poses a significant challenge. To address this, we propose an advanced neural imputation framework combining the Tracking-Removed Autoencoder (TRAE) with Multi-View Progressive Training (MVPT), which effectively reconstructs missing features while preserving critical data patterns. The imputation accuracy of the network trained using MVPT is higher than that of networks trained with traditional schemes. To further address the inherent uncertainty in the disorder, fuzzy logic is applied to assess the confidence in the imputed values. This integration not only enhances prediction accuracy but also improves transparency and trust in model outputs. Overall, the demonstrated proficiency of the model highlights its potential as a reliable, interpretable, and adaptable imputation framework for handling incomplete neuropsychiatric datasets, with classification enhanced through fuzzy confidence. Its application in neurological and psychiatric research may significantly aid in diagnostic modelling, risk stratification, and personalized treatment planning, offering a promising avenue for advancing computational neuro diagnostics.

In future work, we plan to incorporate additional data modalities and expand our datasets to enable more comprehensive analyses at the feature level, particularly focusing on the accurate reconstruction and interpretation of clinically relevant features. These efforts aim to enhance the understanding of the neurological patterns underlying schizophrenia and support the development of more personalized and precise approaches for diagnosis and feature-specific assessment, thereby improving both clinical decision-making and the interpretability of imputed data.
