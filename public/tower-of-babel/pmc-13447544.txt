In mantle cell lymphoma, whether 18 F-FDG PET/CT or flow cytometry provide superior diagnostic performance compared to biopsy in the determination of bone marrow involvement and enhance prognostic predictive value
Authors: Di Wang, Haochen Wu, MengHan Li, Jinmiao Sun, Ziteng Xie, Mingzhi Zhang, Lei Zhang, Yu Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To investigate whether 18 F-FDG Positron Emission Tomography/Computed Tomography (PET/CT) and Flow Cytometry (FCM) can serve as alternatives to bone marrow biopsy (BMB) in diagnosing bone marrow involvement (BMI), prognostic prediction, and clinical application of mantle cell lymphoma (MCL). This retrospective analysis was conducted to assess the impact of PET/CT, FCM, and BMB results on BMI in 122 patients diagnosed with MCL who underwent PET/CT and bone marrow examinations, and 97 patients with complete survival data were included in the survival analysis. Taking BMB positive as the standard, the sensitivity of PET/CT was 57.4% (31/54), with a specificity of 55.9% (38/68). The positive predictive value (PPV) was 50.8% (31/61), while the negative predictive value (NPV) was 62.3% (38/61). A ratio of lymphoma cells exceeding 5.925% was defined as FCM positive (Supplemental Fig. 1), the sensitivity of FCM increased to 90.7% (49/54), and specificity was 77.9% (53/68). The PPV was 76.6% (49/64), and the NPV reached 91.4% (53/58). Multivariate analysis indicated that age, a MIPI score of 6 or higher, and morphological variants are independent prognostic factors for progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) (all P < 0.05). The ratio of lymphoma cells ≥ 30% is an independent prognostic factor for PFS (P < 0.05). The PET/CT high-risk group showed lower PFS and OS than the control group, even though not an independent prognostic factor. The PFS prediction model incorporated the above independent prognostic factors and demonstrated excellent prognostic predictive value.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00277-026-07104-y.

Introduction

Mantle cell lymphoma (MCL) is a subtype of non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL) that originates from mature B-cells and accounts for 6% to 8% of all NHL cases [1]. The majority of patients are diagnosed at advanced stage of the disease (Stage III-IV), with bone marrow involvement being prevalent [2]. The methods to assess bone marrow involvement include both bone marrow examinations and imaging techniques. The former predominantly consists of BMB and FCM, while imaging techniques mainly involve PET/CT and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Currently, BMB is considered the gold standard for detecting bone marrow involvement [3–5]. However, its relatively low sensitivity results in a high rate of false negatives [6] and can cause patient discomfort and pain.

With the widespread application of PET/CT in the field of lymphoma, an increasing body of research has demonstrated its value in diagnosing bone marrow involvement. For instance, studies related to Hodgkin lymphoma have indicated that PET/CT can potentially eliminate the need for bone marrow biopsy [7,8]. In cases of newly diagnosed diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, the assessment of bone marrow involvement through PET/CT exhibits superior diagnostic performance and prognostic stratification value compared to traditional bone marrow biopsy [9,10]. Flow cytometry is a sensitive and rapid quantitative analysis tool for cells and particles, which has been validated by several cohort studies to demonstrate superior sensitivity compared to BMB in the context of diffuse large B-cell lymphoma [11–13]. Particularly, when a ratio of lymphoma cells is low, flow cytometry detection offers enhanced prognostic assessment and serves as an independent factor for disease progression [14]. Therefore, in the diagnosis of bone marrow involvement in MCL, the non-invasive and comprehensive characteristics of PET/CT, combined with the sensitivity of FCM, offer distinct advantages over traditional bone marrow biopsy.

This research evaluated the efficacy of PET/CT, FCM, and BMB in diagnosing bone marrow involvement in MCL. Additionally, it examined the impact of these diagnostic methods on disease prognosis. Furthermore, our study investigated the prognostic significance of bone marrow involvement in MCL, evaluated the necessity of BMB, and explored whether PET/CT or FCM can provide superior diagnostic performance and enhance prognostic predictive value to BMB in the clinical management of MCL.

Materials and methods

Patients

The retrospective analysis was performed on the clinical data of patients diagnosed with MCL who underwent PET/CT and bone marrow examinations at the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University between January 2010 and July 2024. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Patients diagnosed with MCL by pathologists in accordance with the World Health Organization (WHO) classification standards [1]; (2) Availability of complete pre-treatment clinical data, including PET/CT and bone marrow examination results; (3) Provision of comprehensive treatment and follow-up information. The exclusion criteria included: (1) Patients with concurrent hematological diseases or active cancers requiring simultaneous treatment; (2) Patients lacking usable bone marrow examinations or imaging-related results. This study adheres to the Declaration of Helsinki and has been approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University (Ethics No.:2023-KY-1018).

All patients were monitored through a review of hospitalization records and telephone follow-up. The clinical data included the following parameters: patient gender, age, Ann Arbor stage, Mantle Cell Lymphoma International Prognostic (MIPI) Score, status of involved organs, PET/CT imaging findings, BMB and FCM results, white blood cell (WBC) count, platelet count, hemoglobin levels, Ki-67 index, among other parameters. Progression-free survival (PFS) was defined as the duration from the initiation of treatment to disease progression, disease-related mortality, or the last follow-up. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the duration from the initiation of treatment to death or the last follow-up.

Analysis of PET/CT images

All PET/CT images were interpreted by at least two members of the nuclear medicine team at the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University. The analysis included assessing the extent of bone marrow involvement, evaluating the degree of 18F-FDG metabolism, measuring the maximum standardized uptake value (SUVmax) of lesions, and documenting both the location and number of affected organs. In cases where discrepancies arose in diagnosis, a consensus was achieved through discussion.

Criteria for evaluating bone marrow involvement by PET/CT: A ratio of bone marrow SUVmax to liver SUVmax less than 1, or the absence of increased [18] F-FDG uptake, was considered PET negative. Conversely, when a ratio of bone marrow SUVmax to liver SUVmax was equal to or greater than 1, increased diffuse whole-body bone marrow uptake of 18F-FDG or heightened abnormal focal uptake in one or more sites was classified as PET positive [5], [15–17].

Results of bone marrow examinations

All enrolled MCL patients underwent bone marrow aspiration and biopsy. Pathological examination of the bone marrow confirmed the presence of invasion, the results were independently reviewed by two experienced pathologists. Bone marrow involvement in FCM was determined based on the ratio of lymphoma cells relative to all nucleated cells in the bone marrow aspirate. In the flow cytometry of the bone marrow fluid in MCL, at least CD5, CD10, CD19, CD20, CD23, CD45, FMC7, and surface light chain were evaluated. The results are derived from separate reports. Prior to the dissemination of these reports, they underwent thorough review by at least two pathology experts.

The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was generated by utilizing the proportion of positive BMB and the percentage of lymphoma cells, the area under the curve (AUC) and the cut-off value were subsequently calculated. This cut-off value was established as a demarcation for FCM to ascertain whether bone marrow involvement had occurred.

The temporal interval between PET/CT, BMB and FCM

All enrolled patients underwent PET/CT, BMB, and FCM of bone marrow fluid prior to treatment, so as to mitigate the potential impact of subsequent treatment regimens on the research results. Bone marrow examination includes BMB and FCM of bone marrow fluid. In clinical practice, BMB is typically performed immediately following bone marrow aspiration, with the interval less than 10 min. Concerning the time between PET/CT and bone marrow examination, BMB and PET/CT performed at the initial diagnosis are typically completed within a period of 2 to 7 days. The median interval between PET/CT and BMB was 4d.

Statistical analysis

Data processing was carried out using SPSS version 26.0, Graph Pad version 9.3 and R language version 4.4.1. Rates and percentages (%) were employed to describe categorical data. Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) were essential metrics for assessing diagnostic accuracy. PFS and OS were estimated utilizing the Kaplan-Meier method. Cox risk regression model was used for univariate and multivariate survival analyses of significant factors, with the Schoenfeld residual test being performed to evaluate the proportional hazards assumption. Independent prognostic factors were integrated into a nomogram to develop a prognostic model. P values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

For missing data handling: Where missing values are Missing Completely at Random (MCAR) with a missing count < 5 (missing rate < 5%), listwise deletion is employed; where missing values are Missing at Random (MAR) with a missing rate between 5% and 30%, imputation methods (including mean imputation and multiple imputation) are applied to supplement missing values. Sensitivity analysis is performed post data processing to validate the credibility of the results.

Results

Clinical data of patients

A total of 122 cases meeting the inclusion criteria were screened. Among these, 25 cases were merely utilized to assess the diagnostic value of PET/CT, FCM, and BMB in bone marrow involvement of MCL due to loss of follow-up or insufficient survival data. Prognostic analysis and prediction model were conducted on the 97 patients with complete survival follow-up data.

Among the 122 patients, 92 cases were male (75.4%) and 30 cases were female (24.6%). The median age was 61.5 years (range:39–79) notably, 47 patients (38.5%) were over the age of 60. According to the Ann Arbor stage, 10 cases were classified as stage I-II (8.2%), 9 cases as stage III (7.4%), and a significant majority of 103 cases as stage IV (84.4%). More than two organs involved were observed in 42 cases (34.4%), and 22 cases (18.0%) exhibited a MIPI score of 6 or higher. There were 82 patients diagnosed with classic-type MCL (67.2%), 29 patients with aggressive-type MCL (23.8%), and 11 cases that could not be classified (9.0%). Among the aggressive-type group, 17 cases were identified as the blastodisc variant, while 12 cases were categorized as the pleomorphic variant. Based on the Deauville score from initial PET/CT scans, 65 cases (53.3%) had scores ranging from 1 to 3, and 57 cases (46.7%) had scores of 4 to 5; further details are provided in Table 1. The follow-up period ended in July 2024. Among the 97 patients, the median follow-up period and PFS duration were 33 months (3-118) and 24 months (18–36), respectively.

Patient characteristics

Consistency Analysis between BMB, PET/CT, and FCM

Of the 122 patients, PET/CT results revealed that 61 cases (50.0%) exhibited bone marrow involvement. Among these, 21 cases (34.4%) demonstrated focal hypermetabolism in the bone marrow, while 40 cases (65.6%) exhibited diffuse hypermetabolism. Utilizing the positive results from bone marrow biopsy and the varying ratios of lymphoma cells in the bone marrow aspirate, we constructed a ROC curve (Supplemental Fig. 1) and calculated the AUC and the cut-off value. The results indicated that when the ratio of lymphoma cells exceeds 5.925%, bone marrow biopsy is frequently positive, the AUC was 0.856 (P < 0.001). Based on this cut-off value, FCM revealed that 64 cases (52.5%) exhibited bone marrow involvement (flow cytometry positive). Bone marrow biopsy findings demonstrated that 54 patients (44.3%) had evidence of bone marrow involvement, with 23 patients showing a negative result on PET/CT and 31 patients presenting a positive result on PET/CT. However, there were 49 patients with FCM positive and 5 patients with FCM negative. The consistency analysis of BMB in conjunction with PET/CT and FCM is presented in Tables 2A-B. When considering positive bone marrow biopsy as the standard, PET/CT demonstrated a sensitivity of 57.4% (31/54), specificity of 55.9% (38/68), PPV of 50.8% (31/61), and NPV of 62.3% (38/61) for detecting bone marrow involvement Tables 2A. In contrast, FCM exhibited a sensitivity of 90.7% (49/54), specificity of 77.9% (53/68), PPV of 76.6% (49/64), and NPV of 91.4% (53/58) Table 2B.

The significance of three diagnostic methods for assessing bone marrow involvement in prognostic prediction

A total of 97 patients with complete survival data were included in the PFS and OS analysis. The 2-year PFS rate and OS rate in 97 patients were 55.7% (54/97) and 76.3% (74/97), respectively. For patients in the BMB positive group, the 2-year PFS and OS rates were 55.6% and 75.6%, respectively. In contrast, the corresponding rates in the BMB negative group were 55.8% and 76.9%. Patients classified as FCM negative exhibited 2-year PFS and OS rates of 58.5% and 85.4%, while those in the FCM positive group had rates of 53.6% and 69.6%. Among patients in the PET/CT positive group, the observed 2-year PFS rate was 54.9%, with an OS rate of 72.5%; conversely, those in the PET/CT negative group demonstrated rates of 56.5% for PFS and 80.4% for OS. As of the follow-up date, neither positive nor negative results from BMB, FCM, or PET/CT showed a statistically significant impact on PFS or OS (P > 0.05, Fig. 1A-F). Based on the concordance between PET/CT and BMB, patients were categorized into four groups: PET+/BMB+, PET+/BMB-, PET-/BMB-, and PET-/BMB+. The 2-year PFS rates for these groups were 55.2%, 54.5%, 56.7%, and 56.3%, respectively, while the corresponding 2-year OS rates were 75.9%, 68.2%, 83.3%, and 75.0%. No significant differences in PFS or OS were observed among the four groups (P > 0.1, Supplemental Fig. 2A-B). Similarly, the consistency of FCM with BMB was analyzed by dividing patients into four groups: FCM+/BMB+, FCM+/BMB-, FCM-/BMB-, and FCM-/BMB+. Due to the limited sample size of 3 cases in the FCM-/BMB+ group, this category was excluded from further analysis. The 2-year PFS rates for the remaining groups were 54.8%, 50.0%, and 57.9%, and their respective 2-year OS rates were 73.8%, 57.1%, and 84.2%. Again, no significant differences in PFS or OS emerged among these three groups (P > 0.1, Supplemental Fig. 2C-D).

Based on the results of PET/CT imaging, 24 cases exhibiting focal bone marrow uptake or with a Deauville score of 5 were classified as the “PET/CT high-risk group"5,18,19. In contrast, the remaining 73 cases were categorized as the “PET/CT low-risk group.” The 2-year PFS rates for the PET/CT high-risk and low-risk groups were 41.7% and 60.3%, respectively, while the corresponding OS rates were 62.5% and 80.8%. Throughout the complete follow-up period, both PFS and OS in the PET/CT low-risk group demonstrated better superiority over the PET/CT high-risk group (PFS P = 0.028; OS P = 0.015; Fig. 2A-B).

The significance of clinical characteristics in prognostic evaluation

We conducted a stratified prognostic analysis of the various clinical characteristics of the patients. As of the follow-up date, both PFS and OS rates for the older age group (age > 60 years), high-risk group according to the MIPI score (range: 6–11 points), aggressive-type MCL group, and ki-67 > 40% group were significantly lower than those in their respective control groups (All P < 0.05, Supplemental Fig. 3A-F, Supplemental Fig. 4A-B). The younger and older patient cohorts exhibited 2-year OS rates of 81.5% and 65.6%, respectively, alongside corresponding PFS rates of 61.5% and 43.8%. According to the MIPI score, the low-risk group (range: 0–5 points) had a 2-year PFS rate of 64.5%, compared to 17.6% in the high-risk group; similarly, their 2-year OS rates were 82.9% versus 41.2%.

Furthermore, when comparing patients based on organs involved, specifically between those affected in two or fewer organs versus more than two, the former displayed superior medians: a median PFS of 27 months against just 18 months for those with more than two organs involved; likewise, the median OS was 73 months versus 70 months, respectively. Elements for prognostic analysis of survival and model development

The univariate analysis encompassed the following factors: age, MIPI score, morphological variants, PET/CT risk stratification, bone marrow SUVmax/liver SUVmax ratio, ratio of lymphoma cells, number of affected organs, Ki-67 index, BMB positive, WBC count, platelet count, and hemoglobin levels. The results indicated that age over 60 years, a MIPI score of 6 or higher, the presence of Plepmorphic-Blastoid subtype, PET/CT high-risk group, a ratio of lymphoma cells ≥ 30%, and Ki-67 index >40% were all significant predictors in the univariate analysis for PFS and OS (All P < 0.05). Conversely, factors such as the number of organs involved, BMB positive, WBC count, and other indicators did not show a significant correlation with PFS and OS in patients with MCL (Supplemental Fig. 5A-B).

Significant factors (P < 0.05) identified in the univariate analysis were incorporated into the multivariate analysis. The results indicated that age over 60 years (HR = 2.00, 95% CI: 1.03–3.90, P = 0.041), a MIPI score of 6 or higher (HR = 3.63, 95% CI: 1.68–7.84, P = 0.001), and morphological variants (HR = 2.65, 95% CI: 1.28–5.46, P = 0.008), as well as a ratio of lymphoma cells ≥ 30% (HR = 2.36, 95% CI:1.22–4.56, P = 0.011), were determined to be independent prognostic factors for PFS (Fig. 3). Additionally, age over 60 years (HR = 3.50, 95%CI:1.40–8.78, P = 0 0.008), a MIPI score of 6 or higher (HR = 2.89, 95%CI:1.07–7.82, P = 0.036), and morphological variants (HR = 3.57 ,95%CI:1.31–9.67, P = 0 0.013) emerged as independent prognostic risk factors for OS (Supplemental Fig. 5C).

The nomogram was employed to illustrate the impact of independent prognostic factors on both the 1-year and 2-year PFS rates. Additionally, ROC curves, calibration curves, and decision analysis curves were analyzed to evaluate the performance of the prognostic model (Fig. 4, Supplemental Fig. 6A-F). To evaluate the impact of high-risk factors on 1-year and 2-year PFS rates, the prognostic model was employed that utilized line segments with scores to quantify the impact of each independent prognostic factor. Ultimately, the cumulative score corresponded to a scale of survival probabilities for the prediction of 1-year and 2-year PFS rates.

According to the analysis of the ROC curve (Supplemental Fig. 6A-B), the AUC for the 1-year and 2-year PFS prediction models was 0.82 and 0.80, respectively, indicating that the model demonstrates a high level of accuracy. The calibration curves (Supplemental Fig. 6C-D) further validated the model’s optimal predictive power, while the decision curve analysis (Supplemental Fig. 6E-F) confirmed its significant clinical value. Given that the independent prognostic risk factors associated with OS do not encompass PET/CT or FCM related prognostic indicators, the model for predicting OS had yet to be established.

Discussion

The proportion of bone marrow involvement in patients with MCL at initial diagnosis is notably high. BMB has consistently been regarded as the gold standard for diagnosing bone marrow involvement in MCL. However, related studies have indicated that BMB is often associated with patient anxiety and pain [20]. Additionally, it is susceptible to false-negative results in patients presenting with focal bone marrow lesions [6]. With continuous advancements in diagnostic technology, varying methods are now available to evaluate bone marrow involvement and prognosis prediction in lymphoma, including BMB, FCM, PET/CT imaging, bone marrow smears, among others. These techniques had demonstrated varying diagnostic efficacy and prognostic predictive value [7–14, 21–24]. For instance, in the research related to MCL, Ródenas et al. and Mayerhoefer et al. both indicated low sensitivity and accuracy in the diagnosis of bone marrow involvement by PET/CT when compared to BMB [23, 24]. Ródenas et al. also observed that in exploring the impact of PET/CT on the prognosis of MCL, findings from PET/CT imaging such as unifocal lesion, bifocal, or multifocal lesions, or elevated bone marrow uptake were suggestive of a poor prognosis [23]. However, their study did not encompass FCM in the research of MCL. Notably, FCM exhibits high sensitivity and specificity, provides a more intuitive representation of the tumor burden associated with bone marrow involvement and possesses superior prognostic predictive value when compared to BMB and PET/CT. Consequently, FCM holds significant clinical value in the diagnosis, management, and prognosis of lymphoma, which should not be overlooked. Our study innovatively integrates FCM into the investigation of diagnostic efficiency of bone marrow involvement and prognostic evaluation in MCL, and examined how different examination methods influence patient prognosis. Specifically, our study aims to investigate the prognostic significance of bone marrow involvement in MCL, assess the necessity of bone marrow biopsy, and evaluate the priority of utilizing PET/CT and FCM in the clinical diagnosis and management of MCL.

In comparison to BMB, PET/CT provides several advantages, including being non-invasive, comprehensive, and convenient. It is also a crucial examination for therapeutic efficacy evaluation and staging in lymphoma [6, 25]. However, according to our study, PET/CT may not serve as a substitute for bone marrow biopsy in diagnosing bone marrow involvement in MCL due to its limited sensitivity, specificity, and predictive capability, which align with the results in MCL presented by Ródenas et al. and Mayerhoefer et al. [23, 24]. Similarly, research by Adams et al. [26] and Hong et al. [21] on diffuse large B-cell lymphoma indicates that PET/CT cannot replace bone marrow biopsy in this context. Concerning the prognostic predictive value of PET/CT in MCL, there have been no conclusive studies demonstrating the independent prognostic significance of bone marrow involvement as assessed by PET/CT in lymphoma [3, 10, 15, 19, 27–29]. Our study found that the straightforward PET/CT- or PET/CT+ results cannot serve as a prognostic risk factor. However, the PFS and OS of the high-risk group identified by PET/CT were significantly worse than those of the control group. This finding suggested that PET/CT imaging manifestations, such as focal bone marrow uptake or a Deauville score of 5, may also be considered adverse prognostic factors for MCL in this study. Not merely confined to MCL, similar adverse prognostic factors have also been identified utilizing PET/CT in the following studies, as reported by Chen et al. in diffuse large B-cell lymphoma [5], and by Barrington et al. concerning early-stage Hodgkin lymphoma [18]. There are also related studies indicating that PET/CT can enhance the accuracy of MCL staging [30], thereby providing a foundation for our investigation into the clinical value of PET/CT. Consequently, it is essential to prioritize high-risk prognostic factors and disease staging as assessed by PET/CT in MCL.

Regarding FCM, in theory, it will demonstrate better superiority in diagnosing minimal tumor cell infiltration in the bone marrow due to its higher sensitivity [31]. As expected, FCM demonstrated high sensitivity and specificity, along with a favorable negative predictive value in the study. Our findings indicated that in MCL, a ratio of lymphoma cells exceeding 5.925% exhibited strong discriminatory capability for identifying bone marrow involvement. Furthermore, Hong et al. [32] reported that a lymphoma cell ratio greater than 2% provided significant diagnostic efficacy for assessing bone marrow involvement in diffuse large B-cell lymphoma. Consequently, we propose that quantifying the proportion of lymphoma cells in bone marrow aspirates may be a higher-priority alternative in diagnosing bone marrow involvement in MCL when compared to bone marrow biopsy, to some extent. It is widely acknowledged that there is no absolute gold standard exists in clinical practice that achieves both high sensitivity and specificity. Although FCM offers theoretical advantages for detecting bone marrow involvement and demonstrated robust performance in this research, a subset of patients still exhibit negative FCM results despite positive findings on BMB. Consequently, when FCM yields unrepresentative or false-negative results, BMB remains a clinically valuable and viable alternative diagnostic approach.

Additionally, the PFS and OS rates of patients with a ratio of lymphoma cells ≥ 30.0% were significantly lower than those of the control group. This finding suggested that a ratio of lymphoma cells ≥ 30.0% may serve as a poor prognostic factor for MCL. Through the research on the prognostic value of various examination methods in the diagnosis of bone marrow involvement, we found that bone marrow involvement may not influence survival prognosis of MCL patients. However, a ratio of lymphoma cells in bone marrow aspirates ≥ 30.0% was indicative of the poor prognosis. Therefore, measuring the percentage of lymphoma cells in bone marrow aspirates via FCM can be utilized for prognostic evaluation in MCL.

It is important to highlight that whether BMB, FCM, or PET/CT were positive, as well as the consistency among BMB, FCM, and PET/CT, had no significant impact on progression-free survival and long-term survival. Therefore, it is imperative to reassess the prognostic significance of bone marrow involvement and the necessity for bone marrow biopsy in MCL. The MIPI score stratifies prognosis based solely on four indicators: age, ECOG performance status (Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group Performance Status), lactate dehydrogenase levels, and WBC count [33]. Notably, it does not account for factors such as bone marrow involvement or staging. This omission prompts us to critically evaluate the prognostic relevance of bone marrow involvement in MCL. Such as Sehn et al. [34] replaced the number of involved sites with specific organ involvement (e.g., lung, liver, gastrointestinal tract, central nervous system, or bone marrow) to enhance the differentiation between low-risk and high-risk subgroups in diffuse large B-cell lymphoma. Their findings demonstrate that there is a high or low risk of external organs involved. According to the research, we proposed that bone marrow involvement may not represent a significant dangerous prognostic factor among various adverse prognostic indicators in MCL, or perhaps the failure of previous studies to perform refined stratification of tumor burden associated with bone marrow involvement may have resulted in the insufficient demonstration of its value in prognostic assessment. Consequently, baseline staging serves as the primary purpose of BMB in MCL [35]. In cases where clinical evidence—such as PET/CT or other reliable imaging data—suggests advanced disease, or when FCM results indicate bone marrow involvement, performing the BMB may be considered unnecessary.

In our study, we included clinical characteristics in the survival analysis, such as age, MIPI score, morphological variants, Ki-67 index, number of organs involved, and WBC count. The results showed that patients with a MIPI score of 6 or higher exhibited significantly lower PFS and OS compared to the control group. Furthermore, individuals aged over 60 years and those diagnosed with aggressive MCL demonstrated markedly poorer survival prognosis than the control group, which aligns with internationally recognized adverse prognostic indicators for MCL [36].

The significant prognostic factors, along with FCM, BMB, and PET/CT results were incorporated into both univariate and multivariate analyses. The findings revealed that age, MIPI score, and morphological variants were identified as independent prognostic factors for PFS and OS. Notably, a ratio of lymphoma cells ≥ 30% emerged as an independent prognostic factor solely for PFS. This observation aligns with the prognostic significance of FCM reported by Greenbaum et al. [14], further underscoring the necessity to elevate the priority of flow cytometry in the diagnosis and prognosis of MCL. Meanwhile, accurate information regarding age, MIPI score, and morphological variants was particularly crucial for prognosis. Subsequently, independent prognostic factors associated with PFS including age, MIPI score, morphological variants, and a ratio of lymphoma cells were utilized to develop a prognostic nomogram. The risk prediction model for PFS demonstrated significant clinical applicability, as evidenced by its validation in terms of accuracy, predictive capability, and clinical net benefit. In comparison to the MIPI score alone, this model incorporated more definitive adverse prognostic factors for MCL in the era of immunochemotherapy. Consequently, it enhances the precision and comprehensiveness of PFS prediction in MCL. The prediction model quantified the impact of each independent prognostic factor on PFS rate through the use of line segments with corresponding scores. Both morphological variants and the ratio of lymphoma cells demonstrated strong performance, offering significant predictive value for prognosis. This highlights the importance of morphological variants and flow cytometry in MCL.

In recent years, a series of large-scale, multi-center studies have successively demonstrated the significant outcomes of Bruton’s tyrosine kinase (BTK) inhibitors, antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs), and chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR-T) therapy in the treatment and prognosis of MCL [37–39]. Notably, in MCL patients with high risk features including blastoid/pleomorphic variants and high Ki-67 index, intensified treatment with the aforementioned agents exhibits considerable potential for survival benefit, which aligns closely with the high risk prognostic factors identified in our study. As treatment regimens for MCL continue to advance, precise molecular signature and prognostic risk stratification have become increasingly critical for MCL patients. Quantitative detection of circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) and assessment of minimal residual disease (MRD) have emerged as burgeoning research directions for prognostic evaluation and clinical decision support. These approaches facilitate the identification of patient cohorts with distinct prognostic outcomes, thereby advancing the development of personalized treatment strategies. Thus, in this study, when investigating the prognostic value of bone marrow involvement in MCL, our analysis extended beyond the binary “presence or absence” of bone marrow involvement to further demonstrate the superior performance of the ratio of lymphoma cells in prognostic stratification and its robust performance in the prognostic model. This finding provides a valuable reference for prognostic assessment and clinical decision-making for MCL in the era of immune-targeted therapy and cellular therapy.

Meanwhile, With the discovery by Ouyang et al. that the ctDNA level is significantly associated with treatment response and prognosis in MCL [40], which offers a more scientific and diversified basis for the diagnosis and treatment of MCL in the era of immune - targeted therapy. As reported by Dondolin et al., in newly diagnosed DLBCL, the integration of PET/CT radiomics, circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) levels, and molecular subtyping can be performed complementarily. This comprehensive approach significantly enhances the predictive capability regarding the prognosis of DLBCL patients [41]. Furthermore, the prognostic model developed from these factors demonstrates superiority over traditional metrics such as the International Prognostic Index (IPI) and cell-of-origin (COO), indicating substantial potential for clinical application. Therefore, when circumstances permit, it is recommended to integrate plasma ctDNA from patients with MCL into subsequent relevant research endeavors, which facilitates a more scientific and comprehensive assessment of disease risks and prognoses. This methodology laying the groundwork for more in-depth investigations in the future.

Our study is a real-world retrospective analysis. Limitations of this study include a limited sample size, incomplete clinical data for some cases, and potential biases. To further validate the findings, it is essential to expand the sample size and enhance the collection of relevant clinical indicators. Given the inadequate availability of data regarding TP53 and IGH/CCND1 gene mutations, these parameters were excluded from the scope of this study. Meanwhile, while PET/CT theoretically exhibits high sensitivity for diagnosing bone marrow involvement and assessing tumor burden in MCL, the absence of abnormal tracer uptake on imaging typically indicates the lack of bone marrow involvement. However, drawing on multiple studies investigating PET/CT diagnostic criteria for bone marrow involvement, this research adopts the following definition for PET/CT negativity: a ratio of bone marrow SUVmax to liver SUVmax < 1, or the absence of increased 18 F-FDG uptake. In accordance with the criteria employed in study, a subset of cases characterized by low-volume or diffuse infiltration may have been classified into the PET/CT negative group. Consequently, PET/CT exhibits limited sensitivity for detecting bone marrow involvement in MCL. As a result, negative PET/CT results may not exclude marrow disease completely.

This research demonstrated that flow cytometry exhibited excellent sensitivity, specificity, and negative predictive value in diagnosing bone marrow involvement in MCL. Furthermore, a flow cytometry-determined ratio of lymphoma cells in bone marrow aspirates ≥ 30% was identified as an independent prognostic factor for MCL. Consequently, flow cytometry surpasses bone marrow biopsy in both the diagnosis of bone marrow involvement and prognosis evaluation. PET/CT can not serve as an alternative to BMB in assessing bone marrow involvement in MCL. Although focal uptake of bone marrow observed on PET/CT and a Deauville score of 5 represented high-risk indicators from the perspective of long-term survival outcomes and held significant prognostic value [5, 8], they were not considered independent prognostic factors for MCL. This study highlights the restricted necessity of bone marrow biopsy and underscores the limited diagnostic value of bone marrow involvement in the clinical management of MCL. In conclusion, a comprehensive comparative analysis between FCM, PET/CT and bone marrow biopsy in MCL reveals that determining the proportion of lymphoma cells through FCM can offer greater clinical value and provide more comprehensive guidance for prognosis than traditional bone marrow biopsy, which implies the importance of prioritizing FCM in the clinical application for MCL.
