First record of fatal Plasmodium relictum (GRW4) infection in a highly endangered Fatu Hiva monarch (Pomarea whitneyi) fledgling: a case report
Authors: María Igual i Beltrán, Mélanie Duc, Caroline Blanvillain, Simon Bruslund, Gediminas Valkiūnas, Helene Pendl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Fatu Hiva monarch (Pomarea whitneyi) is a Critically Endangered (IUCN) passerine species restricted to a single island in French Polynesia, with fewer than 20 individuals remaining. In the Pacific region, avian malaria caused by Plasmodium relictum lineage GRW4 is a major conservation concern because it has been linked to severe population declines and extinctions in Hawaiʻi and poses a documented threat to endemic birds in New Zealand.Case presentationOn 17 June 2022, a recently fledged juvenile Fatu Hiva Monarch (Pomarea whitneyi) was found dead in its natural habitat on Fatu Hiva. A necropsy was performed on site in humid tropical conditions, with real-time online guidance from an expert based in Switzerland. Gross examination revealed severe emaciation, an empty gastrointestinal tract, diffuse pallor with scant intravascular blood and hepatosplenomegaly with dark discolouration. Despite advanced autolysis having occurred, cytologic tissue imprint and blood-smear assessment revealed high parasitaemia with intraerythrocytic developmental stages and abundant birefringent malarial pigment. Histopathology confirmed a hemozoin-producing haemosporidian infection due to the presence of widespread polarisation-positive and Prussian blue-negative intracytoplasmic pigment in erythrocytes and macrophages. Molecular testing was performed on dried blood pressed onto household filter paper, that had been stored and shipped at ambient temperature for several weeks. Nested cytochrome b polymerase chain reaction and sequencing confirmed a single infection with the GRW4 lineage of Plasmodium relictum.ConclusionsThis first confirmed fatal case of avian malaria in this critically depleted island endemic species highlights the urgent need to integrate disease surveillance and vector management into conservation planning. The results suggest that acute severe P. relictum (GRW4) infection was the proximate cause of death, likely occurring against a background of prolonged negative energy balance and chronic stress exposure. Although the direction of causality cannot be fully determined post-mortem, aggressive malaria prevention measures remain justified to avoid further demographic decline in the critically low Fatu Hiva monarch population. To effectively reduce risk, vector control measures must be combined with measures to enhance host resilience and continuous surveillance to monitor transmission and guide adaptive management. Furthermore, this case study shows that a scientifically defensible, lineage-level diagnosis can be achieved using simple, low-cost samples and techniques adapted for remote tropical fieldwork, even when carcass quality is compromised by warm, humid conditions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12936-026-05924-x.Keywords: Avian malaria, Hemozoin, Field necropsy, Cytology, Histology, Remote guidance

Background

The critically endangered Fatu Hiva Monarch (Pomarea whitneyi)

The Fatu Hiva Monarch (Pomarea whitneyi) is a medium-sized passerine endemic to Fatu Hiva Island in the Marquesas archipelago (Figs. 1, 2). Its population is restricted to a single valley on the Island (Omoa valley) and has experienced a catastrophic decline due to alien invasive species, in particularly ship rats (Rattus rattus) and feral cats (Felis catus), with fewer than 20 individuals remaining [1–3]. Classified as Critically Endangered (IUCN), it is among the most threatened and range-restricted bird species globally [4] and ongoing conservation management has been vital to prevent its extinction [5].

At such critically low numbers, small island populations are particularly vulnerable to demographic and environmental stochasticity, rare catastrophic events, and genetic deterioration [6, 7]. Although nature conservation management on Fatu Hiva, which focused on controlling predators such as rats and cats, initially led to an improvement in breeding performance in the managed areas, recruitment was insufficient to sustain population recovery [4]. Between 2015 and 2022, 62 chicks hatched, of which 49 fledged. Thirty individuals survived longer than 3 months, resulting in a final recruitment of only 19 (30.6%). Of the 43 documented failures, 16 carcasses (37%) were found in the nest or on the ground. Predators were ruled out as the cause of death. This raised concern about the presence of an unidentified threat compromising juvenile survival.

Plasmodium relictum lineage GRW4 as a threat to birds in the Pacific region

Due to limited prior exposure and restricted demographic resilience in small, isolated groups, island bird populations are highly vulnerable to introduced pathogens [8, 9]. Monitoring of haemosporidia in wild populations, particularly on remote Pacific islands, is inadequate. However, evidence from conservation-managed and ex situ settings suggests that endangered Pacific birds are highly susceptible to infection. In New Zealand, for instance, avian malaria caused a documented outbreak among captive mohua (Mohoua ochrocephala) [10]. Fatal avian malaria has also been reported in kiwis (Apteryx spp.), a species closely associated with captive breeding and intensive conservation management in New Zealand [11]. In Hawaii, captive-reared 'alalā (Corvus hawaiiensis) that were naive to malaria developed Plasmodium relictum parasitaemia following exposure [12].

In wild bird populations, the Plasmodium relictum lineage GRW4 poses a particular conservation threat, causing mortality in endemic bird species in New Zealand [13] and severe declines in native Hawaiian forest bird populations. In Hawaii, Culex quinquefasciatus is the only identified natural vector [14], and the distribution of affected honeycreepers is strongly influenced by their susceptibility to avian malaria [15, 16]. This susceptibility varies markedly among species, ranging from around 60% to 90–100% in experimental infections [16, 17], with survivors becoming immune to reinfection in one experiment [18]. In French Polynesia, the introduction and permanent establishment of mosquito species is well documented [19]. Culex quinquefasciatus was introduced during the nineteenth century [20] and is now established in several archipelagos; records from the Marquesas include Fatu Hiva as an affected island [21]. In contrast, data on the prevalence, diversity of lineages and demographic impact of avian haemoparasites are comparatively scarce [20]. Notably, the P. relictum lineage GRW4 has also been identified in introduced bird species in French Polynesia, such as the Common mynah (Acridotheres tristis) in Tahiti and Moorea, the Red-browed firetail (Neochmia temporalis) and the Silvereye (Zosterops lateralis) in Moorea, as well as in 9 of 11 endemic Northern Marquesan Reed Warblers (Acrocephalus mendanae percernis) sampled in 1987 on Nuku Hiva [15]. This indicates active circulation of avian malaria within the archipelago, with the potential for spillover into native hosts wherever competent mosquito vectors and reservoir hosts coexist [20]. However, despite their capacity to influence survival rates and population trends, disease emergence and transmission dynamics are often insufficiently incorporated into conservation planning [22].

This study documents the first confirmed fatal case of avian malaria in the critically endangered Fatu Hiva monarch, identifying the GRW4 lineage of Plasmodium relictum as a new threat to consider in the species. This evidence supports the need to reassess conservation strategies to integrate disease risk management. Additionally, the study presents a diagnostic workflow optimized for remote tropical field conditions with limited infrastructure.

Case history

On 17 June 2022, the carcass of a fledgling from one of the last breeding pairs of the species, known as the 'Turia' pair, was found on Fatu Hiva. The fledgling was assigned to this pair based on the location of its parents' territory, the fact that only one egg is laid per clutch, and the absence of any other active nests in the population at that time. This identification is consistent with previously established methods of identifying unbanded juveniles based on behaviour, association with parents, and distinctive juvenile and developing plumage characteristics [3].

The chick was estimated to have hatched between 19 and 20 March,based on direct observations of the nest made during routine monitoring of the breeding territory. As part of the Fatu Hiva Monarch monitoring programme, occupied territories are checked at least twice monthly and suspected nests are searched intensively. Once nest-building is confirmed, nests are monitored one to three times weekly until fledging [3]. Given the species’ single-egg clutch and an incubation period of around 17 days, this monitoring allowed the hatching of this chick to be constrained to between 19 and 20 March.

The chick fledged on 8 April. By this time, it had begun to forage independently but still received parental feeding. In accordance with the monitoring programme, it was observed for 2 months after fledging. Observations lasting at least 30 min were conducted one to three times per week until it was emancipated from the parental territory. This post-fledging period is considered to be the most vulnerable [3]. Field notes recorded 'weak' begging on 22 April, independent foraging on 1 June, and persistent, heavy rainfall for approximately 15 days prior to the bird's death. On 14 June, the juvenile was described as alert and actively begging for food. It displayed complete juvenile plumage with no visible down feathers and a transition in beak colour towards adult tones. The parents were not observed that day. When the carcass was found 3 days later, the parents reportedly called continuously for around 25 min.

Methods

Necropsy, cytology, histology

A necropsy was performed on site on the island by one author (MI) and was guided in real time via Skype live video by a second author (HP) on the day the carcass was found. Gross observations were documented contemporaneously and subsequently reviewed using photographs and video recordings. The necropsy followed a standardised protocol [23] adapted to field conditions.

External examination included determination of body weight, assessment of post-mortem status (rigor mortis), body condition and musculature, inspection of natural body openings and eyes, palpation of the vertebral column and appendicular skeleton (including joint mobility and bilateral symmetry), and evaluation of skin and plumage. Standard morphometrics were obtained using a ruler as no callipers were available. The measurements included tarsus length, primary feather length (P8), central rectrix length, and beak and head measurements.

Internal examination aimed to evaluate at least one organ of all major organ systems (i.e. cardiovascular, respiratory, gastrointestinal-hepatic, urogenital, hemolymphatic, endocrine, and nervous system).

Comprehensive sampling was performed for ancillary diagnostics. Frozen tissues at −20 °C included heart, liver, kidney, spleen, bursa, lung, trachea, ingluvies/oesophagus, proventriculus, ventriculus, and intestines; the remaining carcass with feathers was stored and frozen in a plastic bag. Samples fixed in 10% buffered formalin for histology included heart, liver, kidney (with gonads and adrenal gland attached), lung, spleen, thyroid glands (two samples placed in a separate Eppendorf tube), bursa, intestine, left femur with bone marrow, and skull with brain.

Cytological samples were taken from the surface and the parenchyma of the heart, liver, spleen, kidneys and lungs. The blood smear technique was used to preserve the liquid components of heart and lung. Tissue samples from the liver, lung, spleen, kidney and heart were taken and used for imprint and squash preparations. Several samples were taken from each organ. A preliminary selection of cytology slides, consisting of simply air-dried specimens was sent immediately to one of the authors (HP) in Switzerland. Due to unavoidable transport delays from Fatu Hiva to Tahiti and on to Europe, it took over 6 weeks for these samples to arrive. They were stained using a one-step Wright-Giemsa staining protocol and mounted with a coverslip using Entellan New™ as a mounting medium. The remainder of cytology slides consisting of specimens from the liver, lungs, kidneys, and spleen was kept on site as a reserve and fixed and stained using the FIX RAL 555™ staining kit once it became available to the working group. This batch of slides was sent to Switzerland in June 2024 for re-evaluation and preparation for publication to confirm the results of the first set.

The formalinised tissue samples were embedded in paraffin and histological sections 2 µm thick were prepared using haematoxylin and eosin (H&E), periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) and Prussian blue (PB) stains according to standard techniques at the former Fachpraxis für Tierpathologie, Hartelstraße 30, D-80689 Munich, Germany, now Antech Lab Germany GmbH—Tierpathologie München, Staffelseestrasse 6, D-81477 Munich.

Histopathological and cytopathological evaluation of the samples was carried out using an Olympus BX41 light microscope at × 40, × 100, and × 1000 magnification. Photographic documentation was performed using a ProgRes® C10 Plus digital camera and ProgRes® CaptivePro v2.8.8 imaging software from Jenoptik Optical Systems GmbH, Germany.

Parasitology

Following the cytology and histology results, which raised the tentative diagnosis of a haematozoan infection, pieces of frozen reserve organ samples were thawed at room temperature and pressed onto household filter paper to create dried blood patches. These samples were dispatched from Tahiti on 23 March 2023 and received at the P. B. Šivickis Laboratory of Parasitology at the State Scientific Research Institute Nature Research Centre, Lithuania, on 16 April 2023. The blood spots were cut off and placed in a tube with 250 μl of SET buffer. The sample was processed for DNA extraction following an ammonium acetate protocol [24]. 2 μl of the extracted DNA was used as template in a nested PCR. The primers HAEMNR3/HAEMNFI [25] were used for the first PCR, its PCR product (as template) and the primers HAEMF/HAEMR2 [26] were used for the second PCR. PCR profiles were as described [25]. This nested PCR amplifies 478 bp of the mitochondrial cytochrome b (cytb) gene, referred as a barcoding region, for the lineages of Haemoproteus and Plasmodium. A positive control (previously confirmed Haemoproteus-Leucocytozoon positive sample) and negative control (ddH2O) were used to confirm the success and possible contamination of the PCR. The PCR product was checked on 2% agarose gel, and the positive sample was then prepared for sequencing. The sample was sequenced from 3’ and 5’ ends with Big Dye Terminator V3.1 Cycle Sequencing Kit and ABI PRISM™ 3100 capillary sequencing robot (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, California). Corresponding chromatograms were aligned and checked for possible presence of co-infection in Geneious Prime 2022.0.2 software (https://www.geneious.com). The obtained sequence was then BLAST in NCBI GenBank (https://www.ncbi.gov/genbank/) and MalAvi database [27]. In GenBank dataset, BLASTn was used with the default settings (standard databases, Core nucleotide database (core_nt), and optimized for highly similar sequences (megablast)). In MalAvi database, a BLAST tool was available and used, which tested the query sequences to a local database of all deposited avian haemosporidian lineages in MalAvi.

Results

Necropsy, cytology, histopathology

Necropsy

External examination

The fledgling weighed 32 g; rigor mortis had resolved. The animal was in poor body condition, appearing extremely thin with generalized muscle wasting. According to the EURING classification scheme for categorizing muscle mass and fat reserves in wild birds, both values were 0, indicating severe emaciation and an absence of fat deposits [28]. The oropharynx and cloaca showed no obvious lesions; urates were present within the cloaca. The cornea was dry and opaque; palpation of the vertebral column and skeletal structures revealed no abnormalities. Joints were non-swollen, with an even range of motion bilaterally, and there were no gross indications of fractures. Remaining external features were grossly unremarkable.

Plumage assessment identified mild fault bars on the rachis of every primary flight feather of the left wing and on primaries P2–P8 of the right wing. The skin showed one small opening on the back; a few ants and one centipede were present, and no hemorrhage or hematoma was evident. Morphometric measurements (ruler-based) were as follows: tarsus 37 mm; primary P8 109/110/110 mm (three repeated measurements); central rectrix 97 mm; beak length 20 mm (apparent) and 25 mm (from cranium); head plus beak 48 mm.

Internal examination

Both the liver and the spleen were enlarged and showed homogeneous greenish–olive to black discoloration (Fig. 3). A small volume (< 1 mL) of greenish-yellow translucent fluid was present in the right hepatic peritoneal region and was suspected to represent bile. The remaining organs were conspicuously pale, and only small quantities of blood were observed within vessels and the heart. The thyroid glands were bilaterally symmetrical and exhibited a translucent-glassy, pale, appearance (approximately 1 × 1 × 2 mm each).

The proventriculus and ventriculus were grossly unremarkable but completely empty. The small intestine was gas-distended and lacked contents; the large intestine contained a small amount of feces. Both kidneys were enlarged with a visible lobular pattern and showed mild to moderate urate congestion. The bursa of Fabricius was well developed and measured approximately 4 × 4 × 4 mm.

Summary necropsy—gross morphologic diagnoses

Based on the gross examination, the primary morphologic diagnoses were: (i) cachexia/emaciation with generalized muscle wasting; (ii) suspected anemia, supported by diffuse pallor and scant blood within the cardiovascular system; (iii) hepatosplenomegaly with marked dark greenish-brown discoloration; and (iv) mild to moderate renal urate congestion.

The empty proventriculus/ventriculus and the absence of small intestinal contents indicated prolonged lack of food intake prior to death.

Cytology

In the first batch of airdried specimens evaluated in June 2022 the samples of lung, heart, spleen, and liver showed progressed deterioration and loss of material from the slide. However, the stained kidney imprint contained several still-recognisable cell aggregates. Most tissue cells formed a nucleodense background with marked cytoplasmic loss consistent with lysis, against which well-preserved heterophils and erythrocytes were clearly distinguishable. Many of the latter contained intracytoplasmic inclusions morphologically consistent with developmental stages of a hemozoin-producing hemoparasite (Fig. 4).

The second batch of cytologic spleen, liver, lung, and kidney samples from May 2024 remained diagnostically interpretable despite significant post-mortem changes. Autolysis was pronounced in the spleen and liver, with mostly amorphous tissue debris and few intact parenchymal cells. Kidney imprints showed severe epithelial deterioration and bacterial presence indicative of putrefaction. Intact erythrocytes were preserved across organs, allowing assessment of hemoparasites identified earlier. Many erythrocytes contained intracytoplasmic parasitic forms—either small single units or clustered “grape-like” aggregates—with parasitemia exceeding 50% in some fields. Rarely, large parasitic structures appeared in cells with atypical nuclear morphology for erythrocytes.

A prominent feature was the presence of dark, birefringent pigment granules varying in size and shape, predominantly accumulating in the spleen and liver, with lower concentrations in the lung and kidney (Fig. 5).

Histology

Advanced post-mortem autolysis significantly impaired histologic evaluation, affecting the identification of organs in the testicles, adrenal gland, bursa of Fabricius, bone marrow, and brain. Peripheral blood showed coagulation and separation. Putrefactive changes were present, as indicated by numerous slender fungal hyphae and rod-shaped bacteria without clear inflammatory response, in vessels, myocard, lung parenchyma, tracheal lumen, ingluvies, and oesophagus. Where possible, the assessment focused on the more central and better-preserved areas of the tissue sections.

The ventriculus appeared well-preserved without lesions; rarely, PAS-positive bacteria were detected in the koilin layer without associated tissue reaction. Intestinal loops were largely autolyzed, with no parasitic structures identified by PAS staining. H&E staining revealed abundant amounts of thick, spore-bearing rods indicative of significant bacterial overgrowth.

Thyroid tissue comprised only a very small piece in both the H&E and PAS sections. Follicles were lined by cuboidal, densely cellular epithelium and colloid was absent. Adjacent autonomic peripheral ganglia showed no significant lesions.

Pulmonary, renal, tracheal, and splenic architecture was still identifiable, whereas the liver showed near-complete parenchymal disintegration.

Despite the specimen's advanced state of decomposition, spherical, brownish-black granular inclusions that were strongly birefringent under polarized light, but Prussian blue–negative were detectable in numerous erythrocytes in heart blood and vessels, the lungs (Fig. 5), kidney, and perithyroidal clots. Intracytoplasmic accumulation of blackish-brown granular pigment with identical features was prominent in the liver (Fig. 5) and spleen (Fig. 6).

Parasitology

The chromatograms show no presence of high double peaks, presenting a single infection. The consensus sequence was identified through BLAST as GRW04, a known lineage of the avian haemosporidian species P. relictum. The sequence was deposited in GenBank (accession number PZ000006) and MalAvi.

Ancillary diagnostic tests

Further tests to identify other possible infections or causes of death were not initially performed due to evident macroscopic post-mortem decomposition. Instead, reserve samples were stored frozen for potential subsequent analysis. However, no additional investigations were undertaken due to financial constraints, repeated power outages on Fatu Hiva, which further reduced the likelihood of obtaining meaningful results, and the absence of additional filter paper based reserve samples.

Discussion

This case provides the first evidence of a fatal infection with P. relictum in a critically endangered endemic bird species in French Polynesia. Evaluation of cytologic, haematologic, and histologic specimens raised the possibility of avian malaria, and genetic analysis confirmed a single infection with P. relictum GRW4.

Field notes described “weak” begging on 22 April (14 days post-fledging). Although the juvenile was observed foraging independently on 1 June, begging for food was still recorded on 14 June, 3 days before the bird was found dead. Continued begging after fledging can be part of the normal post-fledging dependence period and may persist for several weeks, typically decreasing gradually as juveniles approach nutritional independence and parental investment wanes [29, 30]. Therefore, this behaviour did not immediately raise suspicions of a health-related problem, particularly as there were very few individuals remaining and behavioural variation was difficult to interpret without a meaningful reference framework. Immune activation, however, can alter nestling begging behaviour. Experimentally induced sickness reduced begging time and other components of begging in LPS-treated nestlings [31]. Severe systemic pathologies, including anemia caused by haemosporidian infections, have been associated with decreased activity levels and deterioration in physical condition [16–18]. The nutritional status of the host can strongly influence infection dynamics and parasite virulence, suggesting a plausible interaction between infection-related diseases and nutritional constraints [32]. Therefore, a combined influence of infection-related disease and nutritional deficiencies is plausible. It must be emphasized, however, that behavioural observations alone cannot confirm the underlying pathology or cause of death.

The persistent heavy rainfall for about 2 weeks in early June is likely to have reduced the availability of prey, the efficiency of foraging and the visitation rates by parent birds. This is well documented for insectivorous passerines [33, 34]. During rainy periods, insects are less active, harder to spot and die more frequently [35]. As a result, nestlings receive less food [33], become hungrier and beg more, establishing a direct link between periods of bad weather and increased begging behaviour. However, prolonged food shortages lead to weight loss and increasing weakness in nestlings and fledglings. If pushed below a recovery threshold, they may die from hypothermia or starvation [33, 34]. These mechanisms explain the decline in overall breeding success and survival rates after fledging during heavy and prolonged rainfall [33, 34, 36].

Necropsy findings indicated a protracted catabolic, severely emaciated state. Marked underweight (32 g vs. expected 37–43 g body weight for juvenile birds less than 1 year old), severe hypotrophy of pectoral muscle and absence of fat deposits (score 0 for both), and an almost completely empty gastrointestinal tract were collectively consistent with prolonged anorexia or failure to obtain/assimilate food. At the same time, gross findings suggestive of anaemia (diffuse pallor and low intravascular blood volume), hepatosplenomegaly with dark discolouration, and mild-to-moderate renal urate congestion raised suspicion of a systemic process consistent with haemolysis and pigment accumulation. In avian malaria, acute high-grade parasitaemia can cause profound anaemia and multisystem involvement, including deposition of malarial pigment in the spleen and liver that may be apparent as gross darkening [37, 38]. These gross impressions were consistent with the subsequent microscopic findings.

Despite pronounced post-mortem changes, cytology and histology provided convergent evidence of an acute, severe haemosporidian infection. Cytology demonstrated high-grade parasitaemia with various intraerythrocytic developmental stages, including trophozoites, gametocytes and erythrocytic meronts (or schizonts), accompanied by prominent pigment depositions consistent with hemozoin. Morphologically, the presence of intraerythrocytic meronts due to the erythrocytic merogony (or schizogony) clearly points towards Plasmodium species than Haemoproteus spp., since Haemoproteus parasites do not produce asexual division stages in blood, where only gametocytes are detectable [39].

Histologically, the most consistent lesion across tissues and vascular compartments was abundant intracytoplasmic brown-black granular pigment within blood cells and likely macrophages in liver and spleen. The pigment was strongly birefringent under polarised light and Prussian blue–negative, supporting hemozoin in disfavour of hemosiderin, and aligning well with the grossly noted dark discoloration of the enlarged liver and spleen. Formalin pigment (acid formaldehyde hematin) precipitation is a theoretical differential for dark pigmentation in histologic sections. Several features, however, argued against artefact, such as clear intracytoplasmic localisation, strong polarisation positivity, Prussian blue negativity, and—critically—the presence of identical pigment in non-formalinised cytologic preparations. The distribution pattern (abundant in spleen and liver, comparatively limited in lung and kidney) further supported intravital accumulation typical of malaria pigment rather than environmental contamination.

These observations are consistent with the well documented utility of hemozoin as a cyto- and histopathological hallmark of haemosporidian infections. In avian haemosporidian parasites, pigment granules are present in Plasmodium and Haemoproteus blood stages but absent in Leucocytozoon blood stages, aiding genus-level differentiation by light microscopy [40, 41]. Hemozoin morphology may also vary between Plasmodium species and has been explored for discriminatory value [42]. Clinically, avian malaria is a well-documented cause of acute mortality in susceptible birds, with diagnosis often relying on necropsy and histopathology alongside ancillary tests [38, 43] with pigment as an important supportive microscopic feature [37, 44].

Persistent pigment stability and polarisation positivity despite tropical decomposition of the carcass, storage and transport of the samples over several weeks were central to form a diagnosis. Structurally, hemozoin is a crystalline heme detoxification product (β-hematin) [45, 46] that is relatively resistant to biological degradation and may persist after parasite clearance, leaving a diagnostic “footprint” of infection. Its birefringence makes it readily detectable by polarisation microscopy, which increases sensitivity of microscopic evaluation and can remain informative even after prolonged fixation [47, 48], thereby supporting post-mortem diagnosis even when parasites are scarce or degraded. This interpretation is further supported by consensus pathology criteria that describe hemozoin as a characteristic birefringent pigment, as well as by established autopsy-based histological approaches that evaluate malarial pigment under polarised light.

Thyroid histology was limited by scant, autolysed tissue but showed a hypercellular cuboidal follicular epithelium with marked colloid depletion. Although this pattern is histologically compatible with TSH-driven compensatory follicular hyperplasia (e.g., when thyroxine synthesis is impaired), thyroid hyperplasia/goitre in birds is typically associated with gross enlargement [49], whereas the glands here were grossly small and bilaterally symmetrical rather than enlarged. Given severe emaciation/catabolic stress—known in birds to alter thyroid hormone physiology during fasting/starvation [50, 51]—and likely a certain post-mortem colloid loss, the findings were considered non-specific and insufficient to support a goitre-type hypothyroidism.

The cytologic and histologic suspicion of avian malaria was corroborated by molecular testing of dried blood, yielding lineage-level identification of a single P. relictum infection (GRW4). Lineage assignment from small avian blood volumes is well established for haemosporidians using partial cytochrome b gene sequencing [25].

Integrated pathology findings, including high parasitaemia and widespread hemozoin deposition, along with genomic confirmation, strongly support the hypothesis that acute severe avian malaria was the immediate cause of death [17, 40, 52]. However, the marked emaciation and lesions consistent with a prolonged negative energy balance cannot be unequivocally attributed to either the cause or the consequence of this infection. A poor nutritional state can increase the susceptibility to, and the severity of an avian malaria infection [14, 32], whereas a severe P. relictum infection itself can reduce food intake and induce body mass loss [18]. The biological plausibility of predisposing factors has been discussed in several studies addressing the interactions among parasite load, immune competence, nutrition, and physiological stress [14, 17]. Susceptibility determinants are often multifactorial, particularly in small or declining populations with limited demographic buffering capacity [53, 54].

Clinical wildlife pathology findings further underscore the significance of pre-existing diseases in modulating the severity of malaria infections. For example, a recent case study involving a rock pigeon (Columba livia forma domestica) with a fatal P. relictum infection documented concurrent thyroid carcinoma and discussed tumor-associated immunosuppression as a potential factor contributing to the unusually severe progression of the infection [37]. This case supports the broader conclusion that severe parasitic infections disproportionately affect avian hosts with concomitant diseases, chronic weakness, or compromised health status.

From a conservation and management perspective, infection prevention alone is insufficient; effective reduction of infectious mortality requires integration with strategies aimed at enhancing baseline fitness, minimizing chronic stressors, and promoting recruitment [14, 55]. Rehabilitation centers provide a practical example, as some programs employ serial blood smear monitoring to assess haemosporidian infection status [56]. Detection of parasites in these contexts has been associated with clinically significant outcomes, including increased mortality risk [57] and prolonged treatment duration [58]. These observations, together with reports emphasizing the importance of investigating and monitoring haemosporidian infections due to their potential to impede recovery in rehabilitated birds, reinforce the utility of parasite load or infection status as relevant clinical outcome measures rather than incidental findings.

Despite these possibly multifactorial conditions and the fact that the direction of causality cannot be determined with certainty from postmortem findings alone, aggressive malaria prevention remains justified for the Fatu Hiva monarch. The population is extremely small [59] and even a small number of additional deaths can have a disproportionate demographic impact, as the example of Hawaiʻi shows: the introduction of P. relictum led to very high mortality among immunologically naive endemic bird species. The populations of endemic honey eaters suffered catastrophic declines, even leading to local extinction [60, 61]. These experiences underscore the need for early action in French Polynesia: with a population reduced to a few individuals, the ‘tolerable’ burden of mortality associated with haemoparasites is zero.

It should be noted that the experiences from Hawaiʻi cannot be transferred one-to-one to Fatu Hiva. A central element of conservation management in Hawaiʻi has been the reliance on, and protection of, high-elevation thermal refugia (defined as approximately > 1500 m) where cooler temperatures historically limited mosquito development and Plasmodium parasite sporogony, thereby reducing avian malaria transmission. Climate warming, however, is progressively eroding the protective effect of elevational refugia, as it is expected to further intensify avian malaria risk by expanding mosquito distributions and prolonging seasonal transmission windows, thereby increasing disease pressure on Polynesian forest birds [62–65]. At the same time, relocation to higher-elevation islands or other lower-risk areas was considered as a last resort [66]. However, this approach is currently considered not feasible for the Fatu Hiva monarch, as there are no islands in French Polynesia with suitable forest habitats that are free of ship rats (Rattus rattus). These rats have been one of the main causes of the population decline since their arrival in the late 1980 s [67].

By contrast to Hawaii, Fatu Hiva is a comparatively 'low' high island, reaching only ~ 1,125 m at Mount Touaouoho. Consequently, Fatu Hiva lacks an obvious, landscape-scale elevational refuge in which monarchs could persist, largely protected from vectors. Therefore, models developed for mid- and low-elevation areas in Hawaiʻi are likely to be more relevant for Fatu Hiva than models based on high-elevation refugia [63]. These models indicate that relying on a single intervention is insufficient. Effective risk reduction requires an integrated approach combining vector control [68], host-focused strategies and ongoing surveillance [55, 68].

Firstly, transmission can be reduced by suppressing mosquito populations. This can be achieved by reducing the numbers of adult mosquitoes using traps and by eliminating larval habitats (including indirect approaches, such as managing feral pigs that create breeding sites [69]), as well as applying novel vector-control methods where suitable. Examples of the latter include (a) releasing modified (i.e. sterile/incompatible) males [69, 70], (b) releasing non-infective populations of a modified mosquito strain (i.e. both genders) to competitively replace the infective vector population [71], (c) releasing genetically engineered refractory mosquitoes [72], a gene editing technique has also been discussed for the threatened bird hosts to lower their susceptibility [73], and (d) reducing the development of immature mosquitoes by applying Bacillus thuringiensis var. israelensis (Bti), a microbial larvicide that targets aquatic larval stages [74, 75]. Secondly, since fatal disease outcomes are likely facilitated by poor host condition [14, 68], measures that enhance host resilience by reducing non-disease stressors such as predator control and targeted nutritional support during vulnerable periods can help protect small populations from infection-related losses [76]. Thirdly, these interventions must be supported by routine molecular surveillance [25, 27, 68] to monitor transmission patterns, identify vector-reservoir relationships [14, 77], and detect changes critical for adaptive management.

Once avian malaria was identified as an immediate threat to the Fatu Hiva monarch, urgent management protocols and targeted research initiatives were introduced to supplement the long-term conservation programme established in 2006. Since early 2023, initial on-site measures have focused on reducing mosquito density in breeding areas using gravid traps [78], as well as surplus feeding of locally produced mealworms [79] to improve the condition of the birds. Currently, only limited systematic results data is available, and a formal evaluation is required to quantify the effectiveness of the measures and enable their optimization.

At the same time, defining the local transmission system—particularly identifying the vector species involved, and characterizing spatial persistence and transmission intensity in the vicinity of the breeding areas—has been declared a priority for evidence-based management. These issues are being addressed in collaboration with the Louis Malardé Institute (ILM) in Paofai, Papeete, French Polynesia.

Furthermore, to protect the species from the ongoing demographic risk, an ex-situ conservation program is currently being developed in collaboration with a consortium of international experts. The program focuses on collecting eggs and/or chicks from the wild and rearing them in captivity [80, 81]. The aim is to support recruitment by protecting the young birds during the critical stages when avian malaria can be fatal. This will help ensure the survival of the population and reverse its ongoing decline. The situation worsened again in 2024 when a further three individuals were lost, leaving only 16 [59].

Methodologically, the case demonstrates that, despite the logistical constraints typically associated with tropical fieldwork and compromised carcass quality due to warm, humid conditions, a scientifically defensible lineage-level diagnosis can be achieved in a small passerine. Three aspects are notable: firstly, it shows that a novice can conduct a guided necropsy in a remote setting with real-time online support from an expert. Secondly, cytological and histological findings that are diagnostic can be retained despite advanced autolysis. Thirdly, low-cost sampling is effective in identifying an avian malaria agent at lineage level: Dried blood spotted onto household filter paper and shipped at ambient temperature for several weeks (without additional preservative) remained suitable for genetic confirmation. This is consistent with field experience and surveys of researcher's practice of avian blood/DNA preservation approaches, which support paper-based storage as a practical option for downstream molecular work [82]. Comparative data further indicate that detection (and especially quantification) of avian blood parasites depends on storage medium and duration, but that presence/diagnostic detection can remain feasible across common field storage methods, supporting robustness under non-ideal conditions [83].

The diagnostic approaches applied in the present study adhere to well-established standards in avian haemosporidian research. Romanowsky-type stains, particularly Giemsa-stained blood smears, remain essential for morphologic confirmation of circulating haemosporidian stages and other blood parasites [40]. The same principle applies to cytologic preparations from organs and tissues. In addition, DNA extraction followed by PCR continues to be a valuable approach for lineage detection and, where lineage–species links have been established, for species confirmation in blood samples and suitably preserved organ material. Together, these methods provide a robust basis for routine surveillance and diagnostic confirmation.

At the same time, recently introduced methodological refinements may further improve surveillance by making workflows more time-effective. Examination of the buffy coat from fresh blood samples can facilitate the detection of blood parasites, particularly Haemoproteus and Trypanosoma spp. as well as microfilariae. However, the method is currently not suitable for the detection of Plasmodium and Leukozytozoon spp. [2].

Moreover, non-invasive sampling strategies have proven useful for detecting haemosporidian infections. Molecular detection of haemosporidian DNA from blood residues in feather shafts has been successfully applied in both wild and captive bird species, including the Endangered (IUCN) Sumatran laughingthrush [84]. Similarly, non-invasive feather sampling has been employed to evaluate parasite prevalence and genetic diversity in protected wild birds of prey [85]. Such approaches are particularly relevant in conservation settings, as they enable the monitoring of infections in endangered or stress-sensitive species without the need for capture and venipuncture.

Beyond blood-based and non-invasive approaches, complementary tissue-based diagnostic techniques offer considerable potential for expanding our understanding of the pathogenic processes associated with avian haemosporidian infections. In particular, histology and in situ hybridisation can provide information that cannot be inferred from haemozoin detection alone, especially with respect to exoerythrocytic development and tissue tropism. Haematoxylin and eosin staining of formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) sections allows morphological assessment of parasite stages and associated lesions [86], whereas chromogenic (CISH) and fluorescence (FISH) in situ hybridisation enable targeted detection and taxonomic classification of parasite stages within tissues; for Plasmodium spp., genus- and species-specific probes have been successfully applied in paraffin-embedded tissues from naturally infected birds according to established protocols [87–89]. The level of achievable resolution depends on the design of the probe [89]. RNAscope may further improve sensitivity for the detection of parasite stages in tissue sections [90], and laser microdissection followed by DNA extraction and PCR can enable lineage identification directly from selected tissue stages in histological sections [91]. However, the suitability of formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) material for downstream molecular analyses depends on the conditions and duration of fixation, since prolonged exposure to formalin results in the progressive degradation of DNA and RNA. This is consistent with our own findings: unlike the positive sample obtained from the household filter paper, the FFPE-derived samples remained negative. Whenever logistically feasible, samples should be collected in ethanol or snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for DNA extraction and PCR testing.

Conclusion

This is the first confirmed fatal case of avian malaria in Fatu Hiva monarch. By combining field observations with pathology, cytology, histology, and molecular diagnostics, an acute severe infection with P. relictum (GRW4) was identified as the immediate cause of death, likely exacerbated by prolonged negative energy balance and chronic stress. The detection of this highly pathogenic lineage in a critically endangered island-endemic bird highlights the importance of integrating disease surveillance and vector control into conservation strategies.

The diagnostic approach was suitable for remote tropical field conditions with limited resources and shows that lineage-level diagnosis is achievable using simple, low-cost sample types, even under challenging logistical constraints and despite compromised carcass quality.

Looking ahead, incorporating classical parasitologic methods alongside pathologic, cytologic, histologic, and molecular techniques into a routine approach is likely to provide a more comprehensive framework, essential for the future surveillance and pathological investigation of avian haemosporidian infections. This combined approach could not only improve diagnostic accuracy but also provide deeper insights into tissue tropism, exoerythrocytic development, and host-vector-parasite relationship, essential prerequisites for a more comprehensive understanding of infection dynamics and pathogenicity. This, in turn, will benefit the early detection and risk assessment necessary for the implementation of an effective, interdisciplinary disease surveillance and prevention programme within the conservation plan for this highly endangered species. Ideally, the programme should combine host resilience enhancement with continuous parasite, vector and transmission surveillance, while also providing guidance for adaptive management.

Supplementary Information

Supplementary material 1. Table S1. Comparison between a standard avian necropsy workflow [23] and the field-adapted protocol applied in this study. While retaining the basic anatomical logic of a conventional avian post-mortem examination, the field protocol was adapted to account for conditions on remote islands, limited access to sterile facilities and specialist equipment, restricted cold-chain capacity and a lack of local expertise in avian pathology. A trained expert guided an inexperienced examiner through the process step by step in real time via Zoom, and subsequently integrated live observations, photos, videos, and written field notes into a telemedical post-mortem report. This approach aligns with wildlife pathology guidelines that emphasise systematic examination, contamination reduction, biosecurity measures, and redundant sampling in the field [92–95]. It also aligns with new concepts in veterinary telepathology that advocate remote expert support for post-mortems conducted outside the laboratory [96, 97].
