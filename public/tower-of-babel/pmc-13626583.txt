Cytokine panels in the treatment and diagnosis of rheumatic diseases: current practices and future promise
Authors: Elliot A. Philips, Sara Stream, Michael H. Pillinger, Jonathan Samuels
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCytokine panels measure plasma levels of a set of soluble cell-effector biomarkers and are designed to provide a snapshot of the inflammatory milieu present in a patient at the time of sampling. During the uncertainty of the COVID-19 pandemic, patients demonstrated an inflammatory response with unequivocal marked cytokine storm. Thus, physicians and investigators ordered a superfluity of cytokine panels to gain mechanistic understanding of the pathology driven by the deadly viral infection. Although cases of COVID-19 have since waned, physicians continue to send cytokine panels with evolving evidence-based indications to evaluate other inflammatory syndromes. Cytokine panels hold diagnostic utility and promise, but more research is needed to determine the extent to which these panels can be used and the clinical contexts in which they can improve diagnostic and management capabilities.

Rheumatologists often face diagnostic uncertainty when patients present with complex symptoms without clear objective data to support a specific diagnosis. In these cases, additional objective testing in the form of a cytokine panel could be a critical tool for guiding accurate diagnosis and effective treatment. For example, rheumatologists often consult on patients who have been prudently begun on high-dose steroids with clinical scenarios concerning for giant cell arteritis (GCA), but with subsequent temporal artery biopsy results negative for inflammation. These patients may initially present with clinical symptoms of GCA, including sudden-onset vision loss, temporal headaches, temporal tenderness, scalp sensitivity, and/or concurrent symptoms of polymyalgia rheumatica, such as shoulder and hip stiffness and pain. Further workup may reveal elevated inflammatory markers (often erythrocyte sedimentation rate [ESR] > C-reactive protein [CRP]1), with or without fundoscopic evidence of anterior ischemia. Despite clinical suspicion for GCA, temporal artery biopsy is considered the gold standard for the diagnosis of GCA and typically determines further management. The question then arises: should the rheumatologist continue the immunosuppression with a negative biopsy result?

If the history is convincing and the biopsy result is negative, rheumatologists commonly opt to treat conservatively with a prolonged steroid taper, often with the addition of a steroid-sparing agent such as tocilizumab,2 and attribute a negative temporal artery biopsy result as potentially subject to sampling error.3 When the history is less convincing, the multidisciplinary team may preemptively initiate high-dose steroids for a patient out of an abundance of caution, given the potential catastrophe of permanent visual loss, while awaiting results of further testing. In this case, rheumatologists sometimes taper off steroids after a negative temporal artery biopsy and schedule frequent follow-up visits to ensure that symptoms and acute-phase reactants do not rebound off steroids. However, the physician often faces uncertainty in these scenarios. Could an inflammatory signature measured by a spot cytokine panel help with decision making in this predicament of GCA, as well as with other difficult clinical scenarios in patients with inflammatory diseases?

Commercially available cytokine panels typically measure the levels of 13 biomarkers, including IFN-γ, IL-1β, IL-10, IL-12, IL-13, IL-17, IL-2, sIL-2R, IL-4, IL-5, IL-6, IL-8, and tumor necrosis factor (TNF; Table 1). Numerous studies highlight individual or selected cytokines that provide insight into rheumatic disease pathogenesis, but few studies have assessed the utility of these multiplex panels in diagnosing or managing inflammatory conditions.

To provide historical context for cytokine panel usage: before the COVID-19 pandemic, rheumatologists rarely ordered cytokine panels. When the pandemic emerged, clinicians and researchers began sending these panels regularly, scrambling to elucidate the pathophysiology of the novel coronavirus infection. Severe, life-threatening cases of COVID-19 often exhibited signs and symptoms consistent with cytokine storm4—with astoundingly high acute-phase reactants including ferritin, ESR, CRP, and D-dimer. Cytokine panels were often sent with the intention of gaining mechanistic and potentially actionable insight into the deadly pandemic. NYU Langone Health, an urban private teaching hospital, was no exception to this practice, with 1846 cytokine panels sent in 2020, compared with just 2 sent in 2018 (Fig. 1). The utility of these initial pandemic tests is hard to determine because therapeutic strategies were fluid and knowledge increased exponentially between 2020 and 2021. One study found that IL-6, IL-8, IL-1β, TNF, CRP, and procalcitonin were significantly elevated in patients with COVID-19 who required intensive care unit (ICU)–level care.5 The cytokine storm sparked by COVID-19 was compared with that seen in adult-onset Still disease (AOSD), and it was found that IL-6 and IL-10 levels were even higher in COVID-19.6 By contrast, no differences were seen for IL-1β and TNF, but the fold change of IL-18 and the ferritin levels were lower in COVID-19 than seen in AOSD.6 Although the pandemic has waned, cytokine panel usage for patients without COVID-19 has not, with approximately 380 tests sent at NYU Langone in both 2023 and 2024 (Fig. 1). The question is implicit: in patients without COVID-19, how are cytokine panels being used to guide clinical care?

The utility of a cytokine panel was addressed in a single-center pediatric context.7 At the Children's Hospital of Philadelphia, a multiplex cytokine panel was implemented in April of 2020. Since then, cytokine levels have correlated with patient acuity of care, with ICU patients having the highest levels. However, the cost of a cytokine panel is approximately $345, and there was no evidence presented to suggest that it performed better than a ∼$25 test for ESR in triaging ICU patients. Accordingly, formal studies evaluating the cost-effectiveness of cytokine panels relative to standard inflammatory markers are needed to determine their appropriate clinical role. Nonetheless, cytokine panel patterns in certain high-acuity illness states have emerged, with an increased IFN-γ, IL-10, and TNF signature seen in familial hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (fHLH), whereas patients undergoing treatment for sepsis exhibited elevated IL-6 and IL-8 with only modest IFN-γ elevation.7 Cytokine panels have also been used to characterize cytokine release syndrome in the context of chimeric antigen receptor T (CAR-T) cell therapy with a signature indistinguishable from fHLH.

Understanding the cytokine pattern of a particular disease, or that within a particular patient longitudinally, does help clinicians to differentiate between disease activity and infection. As mentioned above, patterns and ratios of cytokines can differentiate fHLH from sepsis.7 Moreover, certain cytokine profiles measured in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) have been correlated with infection, which provides useful insight to help differentiate central nervous system infection from autoimmune or inflammatory disease activity.8

Understanding key cytokine drivers of particular diseases has led to immense advancements. Recent understanding of the critical role that IL-17 plays in the pathogenesis of axial spondyloarthropathy (AxSpA) and psoriatic arthritis has led to novel cytokine-directed therapies.9,10 IL-17 and IL-22 drive hallmark features of AxSpA such as bone erosion, osteitis, and new bone formation.11,12 IL-4 was shown to play a pathogenic role in systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), with its levels associated with lupus nephritis (LN) disease activity, along with IL-6, IL-10, and IL-8, consistent with B-cell stimulation.13 It follows logically that B-cell depletion is a hallmark strategy to address LN.14

Cytokine profiles can also help differentiate between different anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA)–associated vasculitides. IL-6, GM-CSF, IL-15, and IL-18 differentially circulate in granulomatosis with polyangiitis vs microscopic polyangiitis.15 More broadly, IL-6, IL-8, and IL-10 are elevated in patients with ANCA-associated vasculitis (AAV) compared with healthy controls and correlate with disease activity.16 PTX3, IL-32, and IL-2RA have also been implicated in monitoring AAV disease activity and helping establish disease remission.17

Biomarker panels have been developed to monitor disease activity for rheumatoid arthritis (RA) and SLE. The Vectra RA disease activity (DA) test measures VCAM-1, EGF, VEGF-A, IL-6, TNF-RI, MMP-1, YKL-40, leptin, resistin, SAA, and CRP, with the aim of monitoring RA disease activity and predicting progression risk.18 While some studies suggest that multiplex RA activity scores may correlate with joint inflammation and radiographic progression,19 other evidence indicates that the Vectra RA DA panel is a less reliable predictor of treatment response.20 In SLE, the AVISE connective tissue disease test measures cell-bound complement activation products in addition to standard disease markers, such as ANA, double-stranded DNA, Smith, Ro, La, RNP, and CL antibodies, and may be used to aid in diagnostic clarity when faced with early or equivocal SLE findings.21–23

Although some diagnostic signatures and risk stratification applications for the cytokine panel are established, caution is needed to avoid overinterpretation of the results. Clinicians often try to use these panels to rationally guide treatment, without evidence of their actual utility. For example, a patient was admitted to NYU Langone and diagnosed with a case of severe and refractory HLH with multiorgan failure. Cytokine levels were checked on several occasions, and the trends were used as arguments for specific therapeutic strategies. This patient was hospitalized for months because of refractory disease and had markedly elevated IFN-γ levels at initial presentation. IFN-γ levels waned to near normal after combination therapy with steroids, anakinra, and etoposide. Nonetheless, when the patient's improvement plateaued, his physicians used the initial IFN-γ elevation to argue for approval of emapalumab-lzsg (Gamifant; anti-IFN-γ mAb) in an attempt to target a key driver of his inflammatory disease. Ultimately, emapalumab was discontinued because of a lack of robust improvement coupled with the immense price tag of the medication (∼$7000 per dose), although the rationale to use this targeted anticytokine therapy may have been flawed from the start.

Another concern is that cytokine levels may fluctuate too wildly to reliably guide management. It can be difficult to interpret the clinical significance of cytokine panels because of the differential stability of the measured bioactive peptides in serum, particularly when storage and transfer conditions vary. Cytokine panels are often drawn when patients are acutely febrile, which may skew the signature, further confounding interpretation. A recent report on the use of cytokine panels to steer anticytokine therapy in response to CAR-T cell toxicity found it difficult to render conclusions because of substantial variability of platforms used and incomplete reporting of cytokine profiles.24 Thus, to improve cytokine panel interpretation, standardized operating procedures are needed.

When simultaneously measuring multiple variables, such as in cytokine panels and other multiplex assays, statistical correction is needed to rule out false positives. The probability of incorrectly identifying 1 significantly abnormal cytokine is high when measuring 13 cytokines simultaneously. The probability of at least 1 false positive in a 13-cytokine panel is 1-(1-0.05)13 = 0.487 or 49%. Statistical corrections can be applied to control the family-wise error rate. One such method of statistical correction is the Bonferroni correction, which adjusts the significance threshold by dividing the standard P-value used (.05) by the number of tests.25 Less conservative corrections include the measurement of the false discovery rate with the Benjamin-Hochberg procedure,26 which is often used in larger panels. These types of statistical adjustments are needed to accurately interpret individual results from studies that use multiplex assays but are not frequently applied.

Nevertheless, cytokine panels do provide a glimpse into the inflammatory milieu of a given patient and hold immense promise for personalized medicine. Ultimately, carefully designed studies are needed before physicians can reliably apply information from a cytokine panel to make clinical decisions in ambiguous cases, such as whether to continue immunosuppressives in a biopsy-negative patient with possible GCA. One could imagine a prospective clinical trial in which a cytokine panel is sent on all new GCA cases before temporal artery biopsy. Biopsy-proven cases would act as the positive control and define the cytokine signature of interest. Some biopsy-negative patients would be tapered off immunosuppression, while others would remain immunosuppressed, and the cytokine profiles of these patients could be compared with the biopsy-proven cases to see whether a cytokine signature can identify at-risk patients who should not be weaned from immunosuppression.

With continued use of cytokine panels in the wake of the pandemic, investigators will need to discover, through multicenter collaborations and meta-analyses, how to harness the information gleaned from them to personalize therapy and improve patient outcomes. Furthermore, in this age of artificial intelligence, models to facilitate complex diagnostics and management may demand more objective data on patients than is sent during a typical workup. The addition of multiplex assays such as the cytokine panel may provide the added data points a neural network needs to contribute to management.
