Integrating Telemedicine into Iran’s Undergraduate Medical Curriculum: A Narrative Review of Educational Needs, Benefits, Barriers, and Curriculum Gaps
Authors: Sobhan Rahimi Esbo, Zahra Nazari Taloki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe increasing use of telemedicine has changed patterns of healthcare delivery and created new educational requirements for medical graduates. However, the extent to which telemedicine competencies have been incorporated into undergraduate medical education remains variable, including in Iran. This narrative review examines the available evidence on telemedicine education, including reported educational benefits, student and graduate awareness and competencies, implementation barriers, and considerations for integrating telemedicine-related competencies into undergraduate medical education in Iran.MethodsThis study was conducted as a narrative review. A targeted literature search was performed in PubMed, Scopus, and Google Scholar between January and February 2026 to identify literature published from 2020 to 2026. Search concepts included telemedicine education, medical curriculum, awareness, attitudes, competencies, and barriers to implementation. Original studies, systematic reviews, and mixed-methods studies relevant to undergraduate medical education and telemedicine were considered. Given the narrative design, the search was intended to identify relevant evidence rather than provide a systematic or exhaustive synthesis of the literature.ResultsThe reviewed literature suggests that telemedicine education may support students’ preparedness for remote healthcare delivery, digital communication, interprofessional collaboration, and continuity of care during health-system disruptions. However, the evidence also identifies important limitations, including challenges related to remote clinical assessment, physical examination, diagnostic decision-making, faculty workload, infrastructure, privacy, and curriculum integration. Several studies report gaps in telemedicine-related knowledge, awareness, or practical skills among medical students and graduates, although these findings vary across populations and are frequently based on self-reported measures. In Iran, implementation may be further influenced by infrastructure, regulatory, cultural, and educational factors. International educational models provide potentially useful examples, but their direct applicability to the Iranian medical education system has not been established.ConclusionThe available evidence supports consideration of telemedicine-related competencies within undergraduate medical education, but the evidence base does not establish a single validated curriculum model for Iran. International experiences may inform curriculum development when adapted to the structure, educational progression, and scope of practice of Iranian medical students. Further research, including pilot implementation and prospective evaluation of telemedicine-specific competencies, is needed before broader curricular adoption.

Introduction

The digital transformation of healthcare has positioned telemedicine as an increasingly important component of contemporary medical practice. Telemedicine, defined as the provision of healthcare services, consultation, and education at a distance using information and communication technologies, expanded substantially during and after the COVID-19 pandemic.1 Although this expansion has increased the use of remote healthcare in many settings, it has also highlighted the need for healthcare professionals to develop competencies specific to virtual care. Despite the growing use of telemedicine, its integration into undergraduate medical education remains variable across countries and educational systems.2 Telemedicine-related competencies extend beyond familiarity with digital platforms and may include remote clinical communication, appropriate patient selection, recognition of situations requiring in-person assessment, informed consent, privacy and data security, documentation, and awareness of relevant legal and ethical requirements.3,4 The absence of structured educational opportunities may leave some graduates with limited preparation for these aspects of clinical practice.

The COVID-19 pandemic accelerated the adoption of telemedicine and exposed differences in the preparedness of healthcare systems and professionals. While telemedicine supported continuity of healthcare delivery when face-to-face encounters were restricted, its rapid implementation also highlighted practical challenges, including technical difficulties, uncertainty regarding clinical responsibility, limitations of remote physical examination, communication barriers, and concerns regarding the quality and safety of care.3,5 The potential effectiveness and accessibility of telemedicine are also influenced by contextual factors, including digital literacy, internet access, socioeconomic conditions, language, disability, and patient preferences.6 Consequently, education in telemedicine may need to address not only the technical and clinical aspects of remote care but also patient safety, communication, privacy, and equity. Importantly, positive attitudes toward telemedicine should not be interpreted as evidence of clinical competence, particularly when preparedness is assessed primarily through self-reported measures.

In Iran, telemedicine has received increasing attention in the context of geographical variation in healthcare access, the distribution of health professionals, and the need to maintain healthcare delivery during emergencies. Iran has also undertaken health-information-technology and electronic-health initiatives, although the extent to which these developments have been accompanied by systematic preparation of medical students and faculty members remains unclear.5,7,8 Iranian studies have reported barriers to telemedicine implementation, including limitations related to infrastructure, training, organizational support, privacy, and other contextual factors.7–10 Some studies have also identified gaps in telemedicine-related awareness, knowledge, or practical skills among medical students and healthcare professionals.6,11–17

However, the available evidence is heterogeneous, and findings from different educational and healthcare contexts may not be directly transferable to Iran. Accordingly, this narrative review examines telemedicine education, focusing on its benefits and limitations, student preparedness, implementation barriers, and relevant curriculum approaches, to inform a context-sensitive framework for undergraduate medical education in Iran.

Methods

This study was designed and conducted as a narrative review. A targeted literature search was performed in PubMed and Scopus between January and February 2026, supplemented by manual searching of Google Scholar to identify additional relevant literature. The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary (Medical Subject Headings [MeSH], where applicable) and free-text terms related to telemedicine, digital health, undergraduate medical education, medical curriculum, medical students, and Iran. Terms related to awareness, attitudes, and implementation barriers were considered as relevant themes during the literature synthesis. Database-specific search syntax and field options were adapted to the requirements of each database.

The PubMed search strategy was as follows:

(“Telemedicine”[Mesh] OR “telemedicine”[tiab] OR “telehealth”[tiab] OR “teleconsultation”[tiab] OR “teleconsultations”[tiab] OR “remote consultation”[tiab] OR “remote consultations”[tiab] OR “virtual care”[tiab] OR “virtual healthcare”[tiab] OR “Digital Health”[Mesh] OR “digital health”[tiab] OR “digital healthcare”[tiab]) AND (“Education, Medical, Undergraduate”[Mesh] OR “Students, Medical”[Mesh] OR “Education, Medical”[tiab] OR “undergraduate medical education”[tiab] OR “medical education”[tiab] OR “medical curriculum”[tiab] OR “medical curricula”[tiab] OR “medical student”[tiab] OR “medical students”[tiab] OR “undergraduate medical student”[tiab] OR “undergraduate medical students”[tiab]) AND (Iran[Mesh] OR Iran[tiab] OR Iranian[tiab]) AND (“2020/01/01”[Date - Publication]: “2026/12/31”[Date - Publication]).

The Scopus search strategy was:

TITLE-ABS-KEY (telemedicine OR telehealth OR teleconsult* OR “remote consultation” OR “remote consultations” OR “virtual care” OR “virtual healthcare” OR “digital health” OR “digital healthcare”) AND TITLE-ABS-KEY (“undergraduate medical education” OR “medical education” OR “medical curriculum” OR “medical curricula” OR “medical student*” OR “undergraduate medical student*”) AND TITLE-ABS-KEY (Iran OR Iranian) AND PUBYEAR > 2019 AND PUBYEAR < 2027.

Studies published between January 2020 and February 2026 were considered eligible. Eligible studies included original research articles, systematic reviews, scoping reviews, and mixed-methods studies addressing telemedicine education, digital competencies, curriculum development, student preparedness, or barriers to telemedicine implementation in undergraduate medical education. Studies were eligible if they focused on undergraduate medical students or undergraduate medical education and addressed educational, competency-related, curricular, or implementation aspects of telemedicine. Editorials, conference abstracts, opinion papers without supporting evidence, studies focusing exclusively on postgraduate education, and articles unrelated to medical education were excluded. Only articles published in English were considered.

The database searches identified 13 records in PubMed and 23 records in Scopus. Google Scholar was searched manually as a supplementary source to identify potentially relevant literature not retrieved through the database searches. Following screening and application of the predefined eligibility criteria, 26 articles were selected for full-text assessment and final narrative synthesis. Given the narrative design and targeted nature of the search, the search process was not intended to identify all available evidence on telemedicine education and was not conducted as a systematic review.

Article screening, selection, and data extraction were performed by both authors. Articles were assessed using predefined relevance and eligibility criteria based on the objectives of the review, and the same criteria were applied consistently throughout the screening process. Given the narrative design of the review and its intended thematic synthesis, no formal quality appraisal or quantitative synthesis was undertaken. Instead, the included literature was examined thematically, with emphasis on telemedicine competencies, curriculum approaches, reported educational benefits and limitations, student and healthcare-professional preparedness, awareness and attitudes, faculty and institutional requirements, and barriers to implementation.

Findings

Benefits of Integrating Telemedicine into the Medical Curriculum

Multiple studies have reported potential benefits of integrating telemedicine education into undergraduate medical curricula. First, telemedicine training may improve students’ preparedness to provide care to remote and underserved populations by helping them understand how digital consultations can be used to address geographical barriers and reduce the need for patient travel.18 These competencies may be particularly relevant to follow-up visits, chronic disease management, and selected mental health services in which in-person examination is not always required.18

Second, telemedicine education may support interprofessional collaboration. Learning to communicate and coordinate care with geographically distributed healthcare professionals can strengthen students’ exposure to interprofessional communication and teamwork. Telemedicine -based care may involve physicians, nurses, pharmacists, social workers, and other healthcare professionals working through digital platforms, creating opportunities for students to develop competencies in communication, coordination, and collaborative care.19

Third, telemedicine training may contribute to preparedness for health -system disruptions. The COVID-19 pandemic highlighted the importance of having healthcare professionals who are familiar with remote care delivery and digital communication. Telemedicine competencies may also be relevant in selected disaster-response settings, military medicine, and situations in which physical access to healthcare facilities is disrupted. However, the extent to which these competencies improve preparedness may depend on the availability of appropriate infrastructure, institutional protocols, and opportunities for supervised practice.20

Fourth, telemedicine education can address issues related to digital health equity by preparing future physicians to recognize barriers that may limit patients’ ability to access and use digital health services. Telemedicine may either reduce or exacerbate existing health disparities depending on how services are designed and implemented. Accordingly, students should be trained to recognize barriers such as limited broadband access, inadequate digital literacy, language barriers, and difficulties experienced by older or otherwise vulnerable populations.6

Additional potential benefits reported in the literature include patient satisfaction associated with reduced waiting and travel times, possible improvements in medication adherence through remote follow-up, reduced missed appointments, and potential reductions in healthcare costs. Students who receive telemedicine training have also reported greater confidence in managing selected chronic conditions and increased comfort with technology-supported care delivery.4,19 These findings should, however, be interpreted in light of differences in study design, educational context, and outcome measurement across the available literature.

Limitations and Challenges of Telemedicine Education

Despite these potential benefits, the literature also identifies important clinical, educational, and implementation challenges associated with telemedicine. One major limitation concerns diagnostic assessment. Remote consultations cannot fully replace hands-on physical examination when clinical assessment requires palpation, auscultation, or detailed inspection.21 Some studies have reported that important physical findings may be missed during telemedicine encounters, potentially affecting diagnostic decision-making in conditions that require direct examination.22 Therefore, telemedicine curricula should explicitly address the limitations of remote assessment, appropriate patient selection, recognition of situations requiring in-person evaluation, and escalation pathways for patients who cannot be safely managed remotely.

Curriculum overcrowding represents another important implementation challenge. Medical curricula already incorporate an increasing number of emerging areas, including genomics, artificial intelligence, and social determinants of health, within limited curricular time.23 Introducing telemedicine competencies without identifying opportunities for integration, consolidation, or prioritization may increase the burden on students and faculty. Lack of available curricular time has also been identified as a barrier to the implementation of telemedicine education.24 Telemedicine competencies should therefore be integrated into existing clinical and communication curricula where appropriate rather than treated exclusively as an additional standalone content area.

Faculty capacity and institutional resources constitute additional barriers. Effective telemedicine education may require faculty development, appropriate technology platforms, simulation or practice environments, and protected teaching time.4 The need for faculty upskilling is particularly relevant because educators may themselves have limited experience with telemedicine, creating a potential train-the-trainer challenge.25 Institutions may also face resource constraints related to the acquisition, maintenance, and technical support of telemedicine platforms and educational infrastructure.

Student attitudes toward telemedicine education have not been uniformly positive. Although many studies have reported favorable perceptions, some students have expressed concerns regarding the quality of patient interaction, technical difficulties during remote consultations, and a preference for in-person clinical experiences.26 Other studies have reported limited perceived usefulness of telemedicine training or concerns that training did not adequately prepare students for aspects of clinical practice.27 These findings suggest that educational outcomes may depend not only on whether telemedicine is introduced into the curriculum, but also on the quality of curriculum design, faculty preparation, technological support, supervision, and opportunities for authentic clinical practice.

Taken together, the available evidence suggests that telemedicine education has potential educational and health-system benefits, but these benefits should not be assumed to occur automatically. Curriculum developers should balance potential advantages against documented clinical and educational limitations and should emphasize appropriate patient selection, recognition of situations requiring in-person care, faculty preparation, technological support, and integration with traditional clinical education rather than its replacement.

Successful International Models of Telemedicine Education

Several medical schools have developed telemedicine curricula that may provide useful examples for Iranian curriculum reform. Greene et al described a comprehensive longitudinal multidisciplinary telehealth curriculum implemented across the medical school program. This curriculum begins with foundational knowledge about telemedicine in year one, progresses to simulated telemedicine encounters in year two, includes supervised telemedicine rotations in year three, and culminates in independent telemedicine practice in year four. Evaluation of the program reported improvements in selected student competencies, including confidence, communication skills, and technical proficiency.4 However, the structure and level of clinical responsibility described in this model reflect the educational context in which it was developed and should not be assumed to be directly applicable to Iranian medical education.

Similarly, Mahabamunuge et al implemented and assessed a novel telehealth education curriculum that included didactic sessions, standardized patient encounters via video, and reflection exercises. Students who completed this curriculum demonstrated improved performance on telemedicine-specific clinical skills assessments compared with the relevant comparison group.19 This model illustrates the potential value of combining didactic instruction with simulation, standardized-patient encounters, and reflective learning, but its specific curricular structure would require adaptation to the Iranian educational context.

In the European context, Machleid et al conducted a mixed-methods survey of medical students across multiple countries, finding that students who received any form of digital health education reported greater readiness for clinical practice and more positive attitudes toward telemedicine. However, only a minority of students reported receiving such education, highlighting variation in the integration of digital health education even across well-resourced educational settings.28 Because these findings are based on student-reported experiences and perceptions, they should be interpreted as evidence of perceived preparedness and attitudes rather than objective evidence of clinical competency.

Collectively, these international studies provide potentially useful educational principles rather than directly transferable curriculum models. Their applicability to Iran is limited by differences in program length, curricular organization, clinical training structures, supervision, and students’ scope of practice. In particular, the progression from introductory instruction to supervised and subsequently independent telemedicine practice described in some international programs should not be adopted uncritically within the Iranian medical education system. Instead, any proposed Iranian framework should be adapted to the local undergraduate medical education structure, with the level of telemedicine responsibility aligned with students’ stage of training, clinical competencies, supervision requirements, and scope of practice.

Lack of Awareness and Skills Among Medical Graduates

Findings from this review indicate that awareness and knowledge of telemedicine remain limited among medical students and graduates, although attitudes toward telemedicine may be relatively favorable in some settings. A systematic review in 2023 showed that most medical students worldwide lack sufficient knowledge about the principles, safety, privacy, and clinical applications of telemedicine. This review synthesized data from 15 cross-sectional studies across multiple continents. While the pooled analysis reported that fewer than 30% of medical students could correctly identify legal requirements for telemedicine consent, and less than 20% had ever practiced a telemedicine encounter during medical school, these figures represent summary estimates from heterogeneous studies with varying instruments and populations.12 The review authors reported substantial between-study heterogeneity (I2 > 80%), indicating that findings varied considerably across regions and should be interpreted with caution. Additionally, these data are based on self-reported measures, which are subject to recall and social desirability biases, rather than objective competency assessments.

In a study from northern Iran, more than half of medical students and interns reported receiving no formal training in this field.11 Importantly, even among those who expressed positive attitudes toward telemedicine, the majority acknowledged that they would not feel comfortable conducting a teleconsultation independently. This disconnect between favorable attitudes and limited self-perceived competence suggests that positive perceptions of telemedicine do not necessarily translate into clinical readiness in the absence of structured education.11

Similarly, a study from Kerala, India, found that physicians expressed concern about their lack of practical skills in using telemedicine systems.13 Related concerns have also been reported among nurses,14 paramedical students,15 and pharmacists.16 Taken together, these findings suggest that gaps in telemedicine knowledge and skills may represent a broader implementation barrier across different healthcare professions, although the available studies differ in population, setting, and outcome measurement. A meta-analysis by Chereka et al in Ethiopia reported that fewer than 40% of health professionals had adequate knowledge of telemedicine and identified lack of training as an important predictor of inadequate knowledge.17

Although the available evidence shows a recurring pattern of limited knowledge and training, the strength of this evidence should be interpreted in light of the characteristics of the underlying studies. Several findings in this review are derived from a relatively small number of frequently cited primary studies, particularly in the Iranian context. Therefore, the consistency of reported findings should not be interpreted as evidence of a large or uniformly measured evidence base. Differences in study populations, measurement instruments, and study designs also limit direct comparison across settings.

Absence of Curriculum-Based Training as a Barrier to Telemedicine Implementation in Iran

One of the most important barriers to successful telemedicine implementation in health systems is the shortage of trained human resources.17 A systematic review by Hoffman et al showed that medical education systems have not yet developed a standard, longitudinal telemedicine curriculum.29 In Iran, multiple structural barriers—including lack of sustainable infrastructure, legal and privacy concerns,9 cultural policy gaps,10 and limited systematic education and training7 have been identified as potential contributors to the underutilization of telemedicine services.

A deeper examination of the Iranian context reveals several educational and implementation challenges. First, the available literature suggests that telemedicine education has not yet been systematically integrated into undergraduate medical education, meaning that motivated students may have limited formal opportunities to acquire these competencies.7 Second, faculty members themselves may have limited access to telemedicine training, creating a potential “train-the-trainer” problem. Third, existing telemedicine initiatives in Iran have been described as fragmented, with limited integration into undergraduate clinical training. Fourth, cultural factors—including patient preferences for in-person care and physician concerns about medicolegal liability—may further complicate telemedicine adoption.7,10

A systematic review of barriers to telemedicine in Iran identified lack of user skills and knowledge as one of the top three obstacles. This review, which analyzed 18 studies conducted across Iranian provinces, suggested that infrastructure alone may not be sufficient to ensure effective telemedicine utilization. Even when telemedicine equipment is available, limited knowledge and skills may reduce effective use of these systems. The authors recommended that medical education reform be considered alongside infrastructure and technology investments.8

Recent Iranian studies have also highlighted differences in attitudes across professional or training groups. Sharififar et al found that younger medical students and interns demonstrated more positive attitudes toward telemedicine than older residents and faculty. However, the cross-sectional nature of such findings does not establish that positive attitudes will decline over time in the absence of systematic training.11 Nevertheless, the finding of relatively favorable attitudes among younger learners suggests that early curricular exposure could be explored as one potential strategy for developing telemedicine competencies, provided that such an approach is evaluated prospectively.

Discussion

The results of this review demonstrate that although telemedicine and digital health are rapidly expanding, the medical education system in Iran—and many other countries—has not yet fully incorporated telemedicine-related competencies into undergraduate medical education. Rather than interpreting this as evidence of a uniform or universal educational deficit, the available literature suggests considerable variation in the extent and structure of telemedicine education across settings. While multiple studies report potential benefits of telemedicine education,1,4,19 the availability of structured and mandatory telemedicine curricula remains limited in many medical schools.2

The COVID-19 pandemic accelerated the adoption of telemedicine and exposed challenges related to preparedness, including technical difficulties, patient selection, documentation, communication, and medicolegal considerations.5,20 These experiences highlight the importance of educational preparation for telemedicine, while also demonstrating that training alone cannot address all implementation barriers. Infrastructure, institutional support, regulatory frameworks, faculty preparedness, and patient-related factors may also influence the quality and feasibility of remote care.

Telemedicine education should therefore be considered as one component of broader implementation readiness rather than as a standalone solution. Graduates require appropriate knowledge and skills related to telemedicine principles, communication, privacy, documentation, patient selection, and recognition of situations requiring face-to-face assessment.11,20 At the same time, the evidence concerning the educational effectiveness of telemedicine training remains heterogeneous, and positive attitudes or self-reported preparedness should not be equated with demonstrated clinical competence.

In Iran, geographical variation in healthcare access, distribution of healthcare professionals, and experience with health-system disruptions provide a relevant context for considering telemedicine education.5,8 However, these contextual factors do not by themselves establish that a specific curriculum model is required or that a particular international curriculum can be directly transferred to Iran. Domestic studies have reported barriers related to training, infrastructure, organizational support, and other implementation factors.7,8 Accordingly, curriculum development should be approached as a context-sensitive educational intervention that requires local adaptation and empirical evaluation.

Proposed Curriculum Framework: An Adaptation, Not a Validated Model

Based on the international literature and the contextual considerations identified in this review, we propose a curriculum framework as an adaptation informed by international experience rather than as a curriculum model validated for the Iranian setting. The international models informing this framework provide useful examples of longitudinal and structured telemedicine education;4,19 however, their direct transferability to Iran has not been established. The proposed framework therefore re-maps telemedicine competencies onto the four major phases of undergraduate medical education in Iran—Basic Sciences, Physiopathology, Clerkship, and Internship—with the level of competency, supervision, and autonomy progressively aligned with students’ educational development and legal scope of practice.

This progression is intended to avoid introducing advanced clinical telemedicine activities before students have acquired the foundational knowledge and clinical skills required to perform them safely. Accordingly, telemedicine education should develop progressively from foundational knowledge and professional competencies in the early years toward supervised clinical application during Clerkship and more advanced supervised practice during Internship.

Basic Sciences

During the Basic Sciences phase, students are primarily developing foundational knowledge in anatomy, physiology, and other biomedical sciences and have not yet acquired the clinical competencies required for independent patient assessment. Telemedicine education at this stage should therefore focus on foundational and non-clinical competencies, including the principles and history of telemedicine, basic digital health concepts, ethical and legal considerations, privacy and data security, professional communication in digital environments, and the potential benefits and limitations of remote care.

Advanced clinical activities such as remote physical examination should not be a primary competency at this stage. Students require an adequate foundation in physical diagnosis and clinical communication before they can meaningfully adapt these skills to remote clinical encounters. Early exposure may instead use introductory cases, demonstrations, and low-stakes educational activities to familiarize students with the principles of virtual care without implying clinical autonomy.

Physiopathology

During the Physiopathology phase, students begin to connect foundational biomedical knowledge with clinical reasoning and disease processes. Telemedicine education can consequently progress from conceptual knowledge toward introductory clinical application. Structured case-based learning, standardized-patient simulations, exposure to telemedicine platforms, and training in remote communication—including rapport building through video-based encounters—may be introduced at this stage.19

The emphasis should remain on developing the ability to recognize the opportunities and limitations of remote care rather than on independent clinical management. Students can learn to identify appropriate and inappropriate telemedicine scenarios, communicate effectively with patients remotely, and recognize situations in which an in-person assessment may be required.

Clerkship

During Clerkship, students begin to participate more directly in clinical environments but remain under supervision and do not have the same level of clinical responsibility or autonomy as interns. Telemedicine activities at this stage should therefore consist of supervised clinical experiences, structured observation, simulation, and directly supervised participation rather than independent telemedicine practice.

Competencies may include remote history taking, digital clinical communication, patient identification and selection, basic triage, recognition of red flags, documentation, informed consent, privacy protection, and identification of cases requiring face-to-face evaluation. These activities should be conducted under the supervision of trained clinical educators, with direct observation and feedback where appropriate.4

The distinction between Clerkship and Internship is important because progression in telemedicine education should reflect the student’s increasing clinical knowledge and responsibilities rather than simply progression by academic year. Accordingly, activities involving independent patient care, prescribing, or primary clinical decision-making should not be assigned to students during Clerkship.

Internship

During Internship, students have greater clinical exposure and responsibility and can therefore undertake more advanced telemedicine-related learning activities within the supervision and scope of practice applicable to this stage. Rather than proposing semi-independent telemedicine practice without qualification, the framework recommends advanced supervised telemedicine practice, with the degree of autonomy determined by institutional policies, supervising clinicians, and the applicable legal and professional requirements.

Competencies at this stage may include supervised clinical decision-making in appropriate telemedicine cases, advanced remote communication, recognition of diagnostic limitations, appropriate escalation to face-to-face care, documentation, patient safety, and integration of telemedicine into routine clinical workflows.4,19 Quality-improvement activities related to telemedicine services may also be incorporated to help interns understand system-level aspects of virtual care.

Faculty Development and Institutional Readiness

Curriculum implementation cannot rely solely on student training. Faculty development should be considered an integral component of telemedicine curriculum implementation because clinical educators require sufficient knowledge, confidence, and practical skills to supervise and assess students in virtual clinical environments. The “train-the-trainer” challenge is therefore an important consideration when introducing telemedicine education.25

Faculty development may include structured workshops on telemedicine platforms, remote clinical communication, privacy and data security, informed consent, documentation, patient selection, recognition of cases requiring in-person assessment, and assessment of telemedicine competencies. Faculty should also receive opportunities for supervised practice and feedback rather than relying exclusively on theoretical training.

At the institutional level, implementation may require protected faculty-development time, access to appropriate telemedicine platforms and simulation resources, technical support, standardized educational materials, and clear institutional policies regarding supervision, documentation, privacy, and escalation of clinical concerns. These requirements should be considered during curriculum planning because insufficient faculty or institutional readiness could limit the feasibility of even a well-designed student curriculum.

Assessment of Telemedicine Competencies

The integration of telemedicine into the curriculum should be accompanied by explicit assessment of the competencies that students are expected to develop. Assessment should therefore be aligned with the progressive curriculum described above rather than relying solely on students’ self-reported confidence, knowledge, or attitudes.

Telemedicine-specific Objective Structured Clinical Examinations (OSCEs) may be particularly useful for assessing competencies such as remote history taking, digital communication, informed consent, privacy protection, patient selection, triage, recognition of red flags, and decisions regarding the need for face-to-face evaluation. Standardized-patient scenarios conducted through video platforms could provide structured opportunities to assess these skills in a controlled environment.

Direct Observation of Procedural Skills (DOPS) or comparable workplace-based assessment approaches may also be considered for competencies that are best evaluated during supervised clinical practice. Direct observation can provide information about students’ communication, documentation, clinical reasoning, and adherence to telemedicine-specific safety procedures.

The assessment strategy should therefore progress alongside the curriculum: foundational knowledge and ethical competencies can be assessed during the early phases, simulated communication and case management can be assessed during Physiopathology and Clerkship, and more advanced supervised clinical competencies can be evaluated during Internship. Importantly, these assessment approaches should be pilot-tested and their validity and feasibility established in the Iranian educational context before being incorporated into national assessment standards.

Implementation in the Iranian Context

The proposed framework should not be interpreted as a recommendation for immediate nationwide implementation. Given the absence of empirical validation of this framework within Iran, a phased implementation strategy would be more appropriate. Initial pilot implementation in a small number of medical schools could provide an opportunity to examine feasibility, faculty readiness, student acceptability, resource requirements, and changes in telemedicine-related competencies.

Pilot evaluation should use clearly defined educational outcomes and appropriate assessment methods, including knowledge, observed skills, communication performance, patient-safety competencies, and learner attitudes. Findings from pilot implementation could then be used to refine the curriculum, identify context-specific barriers, and determine which components are feasible across different institutional settings.

This iterative approach would allow the proposed framework to evolve from an adaptation informed by international evidence into a locally evaluated educational model. Such evaluation is particularly important because differences among Iranian medical schools in infrastructure, faculty expertise, student exposure to clinical care, and institutional resources may affect implementation and educational outcomes.

Economic Considerations

The implementation of telemedicine education requires investment in faculty development, educational technology, simulation resources, and institutional infrastructure. However, the current evidence base identified in this review does not provide sufficient data to determine whether these investments are cost-effective in the Iranian context. Therefore, claims that the costs of inaction are greater than the costs of implementation cannot be established from the available literature. Economic considerations should instead be examined prospectively as part of future pilot implementation studies, using formal cost-effectiveness or economic evaluation methods where appropriate.

Conclusion

This narrative review indicates that telemedicine education may contribute to medical students’ preparedness for remote healthcare delivery, including competencies related to digital communication, patient access, and continuity of care. However, the available evidence is heterogeneous, and findings regarding students’ knowledge, attitudes, confidence, and competencies vary across populations and educational settings. Much of the existing evidence is based on cross-sectional and self-reported data, which limits the extent to which reported preparedness can be interpreted as demonstrated clinical competence. The literature also identifies important challenges associated with telemedicine education and implementation, including limitations in remote clinical assessment, curriculum capacity, faculty workload and preparedness, infrastructure, privacy, and institutional support.

Taken together, the evidence supports consideration of telemedicine-related competencies within undergraduate medical education, but it does not establish a single optimal curriculum or demonstrate that an international curriculum model is directly transferable to Iran. The curriculum framework proposed in this review should therefore be regarded as an adaptation informed by international experience and contextual considerations, rather than as an evidence-validated curriculum for the Iranian medical education system.

Based on the available evidence and the contextual considerations identified in this review, a progressive approach may be appropriate, with foundational telemedicine competencies introduced during Basic Sciences, increasing clinical application during Physiopathology and Clerkship under appropriate supervision, and more advanced supervised competencies during Internship. Implementation should also be accompanied by faculty development and competency-based assessment rather than relying solely on students’ self-reported knowledge or attitudes.

As a practical next step, the proposed framework should be pilot-tested in a small number of Iranian medical schools and evaluated using predefined educational outcomes and appropriate assessment methods, including telemedicine-specific OSCEs and workplace-based assessments such as DOPS. Pilot evaluation should examine feasibility, faculty readiness, student acceptability, resource requirements, and changes in objectively assessed telemedicine competencies. Evidence generated through such pilot studies could then inform iterative curriculum refinement and, if supported by subsequent evaluation, consideration of broader implementation.
