A PSAT1 buff of YBX1 transcriptionally sustains HLA-E-mediated evasion of NK immunity
Authors: Yangyi Zhang, He Ren, Chaobing Ma, Changhong Shi, Ruigang Yang, Chenxi Wang, Pengfei Feng, Bo Zhang, Chenyu Liu, Zubiao Niu, Yalan Yang, You Zheng, Zhuoran Sun, Ying Zhang, Shinan Zhang, Gerry Melino, Hongyan Huang, Qiang Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceDespite advances in immunotherapy, immune-cold tumors remain a clinical challenge due to their suppressive microenvironment. This study reveals that phosphoserine aminotransferase 1 (PSAT1) promotes tumor progression through a nonautonomous mechanism, i.e. sustaining HLA-E expression to evade natural killer (NK) cell immunity. A molecular buff, consisting of PSAT1, MARK2 and YBX1, was identified for HLA-E. Disrupting this molecular buff effectively enhances NK cell–mediated tumor suppression in vitro and in vivo, offering a potential strategy to overcome immune resistance.

Prostate cancer is the second most prevalent malignancy among men worldwide and is frequently characterized by therapy resistance and a high risk of recurrence (1, 2). Although immunotherapies such as immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) and adoptive cell transfer have shown remarkable efficacy across multiple cancer types due to their specificity and sustained activity (3, 4), prostate cancer typically responds poorly to these approaches. Its immunologically “cold” phenotype, marked by minimal immune infiltration and a suppressive microenvironment, poses a major therapeutic challenge (5–7).

Phosphoserine aminotransferase 1 (PSAT1), a key enzymes of the de novo serine synthesis pathway, has been implicated in cancer progression through both enzymatic and nonenzymatic mechanisms (8–12). Recent bioinformatic evidence suggests that PSAT1 expression correlates with impaired immune function and unfavorable prognosis in several malignancies (13). Studies in melanoma indicate that PSAT1 supports an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment by enhancing serine synthesis and expanding immunosuppressive macrophage populations (14). However, the role of PSAT1 in modulating antitumor immunity—particularly in immune-cold tumors such as prostate cancer—remains poorly understood.

Here, we investigate the function of PSAT1 in prostate cancer innate immunity. We demonstrate that inhibition of PSAT1 potently enhances natural killer (NK) cell-mediated killing of prostate cancer cells in vitro and in vivo. Mechanistically, we identify a positive feedback loop in which PSAT1 promotes phosphorylation of Y-box binding protein 1 (YBX1) by microtubule affinity-regulating kinase 2 (MARK2); phosphorylated YBX1 then translocates to the nucleus and drives PSAT1 transcription. Notably, one branch of this PSAT1–YBX1 axis upregulates the expression of human leukocyte antigens E (HLA-E), a ligand for inhibitory killer cell immunoglobulin-like receptors (KIRs) that dampen NK cell activity. Thus, the PSAT1–YBX1 feedback circuit facilitates prostate cancer immune evasion by maintaining HLA-E expression. This autoregulatory pathway represents a distinctive mechanism of NK suppression and highlights PSAT1 as a potential target for immunotherapy-resistant prostate cancer.

Results

PSAT1 Is Upregulated in Prostate Cancer and Drives Evasion of NK Cell Immunity.

Analysis of The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database established that PSAT1 is significantly upregulated in prostate cancer tissues (Fig. 1A). Its expression level was positively associated with disease progression markers, including Gleason score and advanced T stage (Fig. 1 B and C), and predicted shorter relapse-free and overall survival (OS) (Fig. 1 D–F). Notably, PSAT1 expression exhibited a broad negative correlation with the infiltration of key immune effector cells in the tumor microenvironment, including CD8+ T cells, B cells, and notably, NK cells (Fig. 1 G–L). This clinical association was further strengthened by immunohistochemistry (IHC) confirming PSAT1 protein upregulation in tumor specimens (Fig. 1 M and N). Single-cell RNA-seq analysis of a clinical cohort (GSE141445) specifically pinpointed this high PSAT1 expression to tumor epithelial cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S1C), where it positively correlated with the immune checkpoint molecule HLA-E (SI Appendix, Fig. S1D).

Based on these clinical observations—particularly the inverse relationship between PSAT1 and NK cell infiltration—we hypothesized that PSAT1 enables tumor cells to evade NK cell surveillance. To test this, we modulated PSAT1 expression in prostate cancer cell lines (Fig. 2A). RNA-sequencing revealed that PSAT1 depletion led to significant enrichment of immunomodulatory pathways, with the NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity pathway being among the most activated (Fig. 2 B–E). This bioinformatic prediction was functionally confirmed: PSAT1 knockdown robustly sensitized LNCaP and PC3 cells to killing by both NK92MI cells (Fig. 2 H–K) and, critically, primary human NK cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 A and B), as quantified by cell death assays and real-time live imaging (Fig. 2 L–O). Conversely, PSAT1 overexpression conferred significant resistance to NK92MI-mediated killing (Fig. 2 R–Y).

Thus, PSAT1 is not only a biomarker of an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment and poor prognosis but also a functional driver of immune evasion, specifically conferring resistance to NK cell–mediated cytotoxicity.

PSAT1 Upregulates HLA-E Expression to Evade NK Immunity.

To elucidate how PSAT1 regulates innate immunity, we analyzed transcriptomes of control and PSAT1-knockdown LNCaP cells. RNA-seq identified HLA-E, a potent inhibitory ligand for NK cells (15); as a top candidate downregulated upon PSAT1 loss (Fig. 3A). PSAT1 knockdown consistently reduced HLA-E mRNA, total protein, and surface expression in LNCaP and PC3 cells, while its overexpression had the opposite effect (Fig. 3 B–F). Functionally, HLA-E knockdown markedly sensitized prostate cancer cells to NK92MI-mediated killing (Fig. 3 H–K). Critically, depleting HLA-E completely rescued the resistance to NK cell cytotoxicity conferred by PSAT1 overexpression, establishing HLA-E as the essential downstream effector for PSAT1-mediated immune evasion (Fig. 3 L and M). This PSAT1–HLA-E axis was clinically relevant, as their protein levels were positively correlated in a prostate cancer tissue microarray (TMA) (Fig. 3 N and O).

We further assessed the broader relevance of this axis in other solid tumors. PSAT1 knockdown significantly downregulated HLA-E and enhanced sensitivity to primary human NK cell killing in A549 (lung cancer) and U251 (glioma) cells. This effect was not universal, as it was absent in LOVO and U87 cells, indicating context-dependent regulation (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 A–D). These results validate the PSAT1–HLA-E–NK cell axis as a conserved mechanism of immune evasion in select cancer types.

PSAT1 Promotes YBX1 Nuclear Translocation to Drive HLA-E Transcription.

Given that PSAT1 inherently lacks transcriptional activity, we hypothesized that PSAT1 might regulate HLA-E mRNA expression through interaction with proteins that possess transcriptional functions. Accordingly, coimmunoprecipitation assays followed by mass spectrometry analysis were performed, which identified 286 candidate binding partners for PSAT1, and three of them were potential transcription factors of HLA-E as predicted by Animal TFDB3.0 website (https://guolab.wchscu.cn/AnimalTFDB#!/), including YBX1, TRIM28, and UBTF (Fig. 4A). Among the candidates, only YBX1 knockdown significantly reduced HLA-E mRNA and protein levels in both LNCaP and PC3 cells (Fig. 4 A–G). Conversely, YBX1 overexpression upregulated HLA-E transcription and cell surface expression (Fig. 4 H–K). A luciferase reporter assay confirmed that YBX1 directly activates the HLA-E promoter (Fig. 4L). Critically, YBX1 depletion abolished PSAT1-induced HLA-E upregulation, positioning YBX1 as an essential downstream effector of PSAT1 (Fig. 4M).

We next investigated the mechanism of YBX1 activation. Since YBX1 must enter the nucleus to function as a transcription factor, we hypothesized that PSAT1 promotes its nuclear translocation. Indeed, PSAT1 knockdown significantly reduced nuclear YBX1, while its overexpression enhanced YBX1 nuclear accumulation, a pattern that correlated closely with HLA-E expression levels (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 A–G). This process depended on phosphorylation of YBX1 at serine 102 (S102), a known prerequisite for its nuclear import (16). PSAT1 was indispensable for this phosphorylation event, as its depletion drastically reduced YBX1 S102 phosphorylation without affecting total YBX1 levels (SI Appendix, Fig. S4I). Accordingly, a phospho-dead YBX1 mutant (S102A) failed to rescue HLA-E expression upon PSAT1 overexpression, unlike the wild-type protein (SI Appendix, Fig. S4J).

In conclusion, PSAT1 interacts with YBX1 to promote its S102 phosphorylation and subsequent nuclear translocation, thereby enabling YBX1 to act as a transcriptional activator of HLA-E.

PSAT1 Recruits MARK2 to Phosphorylate YBX1 and Initiate a Transcriptional Positive Feedback Loop.

To elucidate how PSAT1 promotes YBX1 phosphorylation at S102, we performed immunoprecipitation of PSAT1 followed by mass spectrometry, which identified MARK2 as a candidate kinase (Fig. 5A). Coimmunoprecipitation confirmed that MARK2 interacts with both PSAT1 and YBX1 in LNCaP and PC3 cells (Fig. 5B). MARK2 knockdown effectively reduced YBX1 S102 phosphorylation and impeded its nuclear translocation, while MARK2 overexpression enhanced both YBX1 phosphorylation and nuclear localization (Fig. 5 C–H). Rescue experiments demonstrated that MARK2 overexpression restored YBX1 phosphorylation and HLA-E expression in PSAT1-deficient cells, whereas MARK2 ablation abolished PSAT1-driven YBX1 activation and HLA-E induction (Fig. 5 I and J). Pharmacological and genetic inhibition of MARK2 consistently reduced levels of PSAT1, HLA-E, and p-YBX1, and sensitized tumor cells to NK-mediated killing (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 A–D).

Notably, we observed that YBX1 not only activates HLA-E but also transcriptionally upregulates PSAT1, forming a positive feedback loop. YBX1 overexpression increased PSAT1 expression, an effect dependent on S102 phosphorylation and nuclear localization (SI Appendix, Fig. S6A). Phospho-active YBX1 (S102E) showed reduced interaction with PSAT1 and MARK2, suggesting phosphorylation triggers complex dissociation and nuclear entry of YBX1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 B and C). YBX1 knockdown decreased PSAT1 mRNA and protein, while its overexpression elevated PSAT1 transcript levels (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 D–F). Luciferase reporter assays confirmed YBX1 transactivates the PSAT1 promoter, with a key responsive region located within –500 bp of the transcription start site (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 G–I).

Together, these results delineate a PSAT1–MARK2–YBX1 signaling axis wherein PSAT1 recruits MARK2 to phosphorylate YBX1, driving HLA-E transcription and NK immune evasion, while nuclear YBX1 in turn enhances PSAT1 expression, creating a feedforward circuit that amplifies immune suppression in prostate cancer.

PSAT1 Inhibition Enhances Antitumor Immunity of NK Cells In Vivo.

To validate the role of the PSAT1-mediated loop in immune evasion in vivo, we established cell-derived xenograft (CDX) models using PSAT1-knockdown LNCaP and PC3 cells in immunodeficient NOD-SCID mice. Tumor-bearing mice received intravenous injections of NK cells or a control treatment once tumors reached ~80 mm3. In both models, PSAT1 depletion effectively sensitized tumors to NK cell therapy, leading to significant and sustained suppression of tumor growth compared to controls, without adverse effects on mouse physiology (Fig. 6 A–E and SI Appendix, Fig. S7 A–E). IHC analysis of tumor samples revealed that the enhanced NK cell cytotoxicity and tumor suppression were associated with reduced expression of HLA-E and the proliferation marker Ki67 (Fig. 6F and SI Appendix, Fig. S7F).

To generalize these findings in an immunocompetent setting, we employed a mouse syngeneic model using RM-1 cells. Knockdown of Psat1 led to the downregulation of Qa-1b (the mouse ortholog of HLA-E) and significantly reduced tumor size in vivo (SI Appendix, Fig. S8 A–D). Histological analysis confirmed decreased Psat1 and Qa-1b expression, along with increased infiltration of CD56+ NK cells in the Psat1-knockdown tumors (SI Appendix, Fig. S8E).

Furthermore, to confirm that the observed immune evasion is mediated through the identified signaling axis, we targeted MARK2, the key downstream kinase. In vivo depletion of MARK2 via intratumoral siRNA injection similarly enhanced NK cell–mediated tumor suppression and downregulated HLA-E expression (SI Appendix, Fig. S9), phenocopying the effects of PSAT1 inhibition.

Collectively, these data from multiple in vivo models demonstrate that targeting the PSAT1–MARK2 axis potently enhances NK cell-mediated immunity against prostate cancer by disrupting the HLA-E-mediated immune checkpoint, providing a compelling strategic approach for cancer immunotherapy.

Killer Cell Lectin Like Receptor C1 (NKG2A) Blockade Enhances NK Cell-Mediated Tumor Suppression in PDX Models.

To validate HLA-E’s functional role in immune suppression, we treated tumor cells with Monalizumab, a blocking antibody against the HLA-E receptor NKG2A. NKG2A blockade significantly enhanced primary NK cell killing of both control and PSAT1-overexpressing tumor cells, with the strongest effect observed in control cells, confirming HLA-E as a key mediator of PSAT1-driven immune evasion (SI Appendix, Fig. S10A).

We next translated these findings using a patient-derived xenograft (PDX) model established from a tumor with high PSAT1/HLA-E coexpression (SI Appendix, Fig. S10B). Mice bearing these tumors were treated with a combination of NKG2A blockade and human NK cells (Fig. 7A). The combination therapy significantly suppressed tumor growth (Fig. 7 B–D), enhanced intratumoral infiltration of CD56+ NK cells (Fig. 7F), and was well-tolerated (Fig. 7E).

In summary, NKG2A blockade effectively reverses HLA-E-mediated immunosuppression and enhances NK cell-mediated antitumor immunity in vivo, supporting its therapeutic potential in PSAT1-HLA-E-high cancers.

Discussion

In this study, we identify a positive feedback loop comprising PSAT1, MARK2, and YBX1 that promotes immune evasion in prostate cancer by sustaining the expression of HLA-E, a key inhibitor of NK cell function. The central role is played by YBX1, which, upon phosphorylation by MARK2 and dissociation from PSAT1, translocates to the nucleus and acts as a transcriptional activator of HLA-E (Fig. 8). This cell-intrinsic pathway provides a molecular basis for the immune-“cold” phenotype and poor immunotherapy responses characteristic of prostate cancer. Therapeutically, targeting this axis, particularly in combination with adoptive NK cell transfer, presents a promising strategy, as validated in our animal models.

A working model for the PSAT1 buff of YBX1 transcriptionally sustains HLA-E-mediated evasion of NK immunity.

While PSAT1 upregulation has been documented in various cancers (17–21), its role was primarily attributed to cell-autonomous promotion of proliferation (17–22). Here, we confirm PSAT1’s upregulation in prostate cancer and its association with disease progression and poor survival. Crucially, we unveil a nonautonomous mechanism: PSAT1-dependent HLA-E induction enables tumors to evade NK cell surveillance. The synergistic suppression of tumor growth by PSAT1 knockdown and NK cell infusion underscores the therapeutic potential of disrupting this immune evasion route. It remains to be explored whether this mechanism operates in other PSAT1-high malignancies.

HLA-E is one of the inhibitory KIRs (23) that, via interacting with CD94–NKG2A on NK cells, initiate inhibitory signals to inhibit NK cell killing (15, 24), disrupting the HLA-E: CD94–NKG2A axis could enhance NK killing in several cancers, such as chronic lymphocytic leukemia (25), multiple myeloma (26), human pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (15) etc. In this work, we revealed in prostate cancer, HLA-E was a downstream target of PSAT1 via YBX1, further extending the cancer profile impacted by HLA-E and highlighting a pivotal role of HLA-E in tumor evasion of immune killing. It is noted that the identification of HLA-E as an important effector molecule that suppress antitumor immunity does not rule out the involvement of other suppressive factor against NK immunity in prostate cancer. It is advised that molecule-based stratification precedes effective immunotherapy.

YBX1 is phosphorylated at Ser102 by multiple kinases [e.g., Protein Kinase B (AKT), Ribosomal S6 Kinase (RSK)] in different contexts, leading to the expression of distinct gene sets (16, 27–29). This suggests a model where a core YBX1 module is co-opted by context-specific “plug-ins” to address diverse cellular stresses. In prostate cancer, the PSAT1–MARK2 feedback loop serves as such a plug-in, driving YBX1 phosphorylation and nuclear translocation to specifically sustain HLA-E transcription and immune evasion.

Notably, the PSAT1–YBX1 axis represents a tumor-intrinsic, metabolically linked pathway for constitutive HLA-E expression, distinct from the canonical, cytokine-induced IFN-γ (Interferon-gamma)–JAK (Janus Kinase)–STAT1 (Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription 1) axis (26). This autonomy may underpin the persistent immunosuppressive phenotype of “cold” tumors like prostate adenocarcinoma, which are resistant to conventional immunotherapies (30, 31). Targeting such a stable, intrinsic pathway could offer more specific and durable suppression of HLA-E compared to blocking transient cytokine signals (32), and aligns with the emerging paradigm of metabolic enzymes shaping antitumor immunity (33, 34).

Collectively, our findings position the PSAT1–YBX1–HLA-E axis as a promising metabolic immune checkpoint and a compelling target for restoring NK cell immunity in prostate cancer. Preliminary data in lung cancer and glioma models suggest that this axis may be relevant in select other HLA-E-high malignancies, indicating a broader therapeutic potential that warrants further clinical investigation.

Methods Summary

Prostate cancer cell lines (LNCaP, PC3) and NK cell lines (NK92MI, primary human NK) were utilized for in vitro functional assays, including cytotoxicity measurement by flow cytometry and live-cell imaging. Gene modulation (overexpression and knockdown) was achieved via lentiviral transduction or transient transfection. Key molecular interactions were assessed by coimmunoprecipitation coupled with mass spectrometry (Co-IP/MS), RNA sequencing, and dual-luciferase reporter assays. The physiological relevance of PSAT1 was validated in vivo using xenograft, CDX, and PDX models in immunodeficient mice, with or without adoptive transfer of NK cells. Detailed descriptions of all experimental procedures, including cell culture conditions, antibody information, bioinformatic analysis pipelines, and animal study protocols, are provided in SI Appendix.
