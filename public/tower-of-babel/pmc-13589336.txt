Background

Vitamin A deficiency (VAD), defined as a serum retinol concentration below 0.70 μmol/L, remains a significant public health concern, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Sub-Saharan Africa and Southeast Asia bear the highest burden [1]. VAD contributes to preventable blindness, accounting for 2% of deaths among children under 5 years of age [1]. Additionally, approximately 10-20% of pregnant women in low-income countries develop VAD, particularly in Africa and Asia [2]. In the Philippines, VAD continues to be a moderate public health issue, with a prevalence rate of 13.9% in 2023 among children aged 6 months to 5 years. This burden is higher in the rural population than in the urban areas. Among pregnant and lactating women, the prevalence rate was 4.1% and 1.7%, respectively, over the same period [3].

In many developing countries, more than 70% of daily vitamin A intake comes from provitamin A carotenoids, particularly β-carotene from plant-based foods [4, 5]. According to the 2023 National Nutrition Survey (NNS) conducted by the Department of Science and Technology-Food and Nutrition Research Institute (DOST-FNRI), the Food Consumption Survey revealed that the average Filipino household’s daily food intake primarily consists of plant sources, accounting for 61% of the total energy intake. However, the vitamin A intake in urban and rural areas generally remains low, at 23.9% and 22.8%, respectively [3].

The traditional assessment of vitamin A status based on serum retinol concentration is limited by tight homeostatic regulation and may not accurately reflect the total body stores or carotenoid conversion efficiency [6]. In contrast, stable isotope techniques provide a more robust approach, especially with the latest high-precision analytical tools now available, as they can study β-carotene bioefficacy and vitamin A status in human subjects simultaneously [7].

However, the efficiency of β-carotene bioconversion to vitamin A is highly variable [4, 8], ranging from 3.6:1 to 28:1 by weight [5]. This variability is influenced by both intrinsic factors (e.g., host factors such as age, body mass index, nutrient status, gut health, and genetic polymorphisms) and extrinsic factors (e.g., species of carotenoids, food matrix, dose, fiber content, and co-ingested fats). Nonetheless, most of the available data are derived from non-Filipino populations [9–12] with limited evidence from populations consuming a predominantly plant-based diet, such as Filipinos.

Understanding the variability in β-carotene bioconversion is vital to determine whether VAD risk is primarily driven by impaired absorption (bioavailability) or inefficient enzymatic conversion (bioconversion)—a distinction essential for designing evidence-based, context-specific nutritional interventions and policies.

This systematic review aims to synthesize global evidence on β-carotene bioconversion to vitamin A using stable isotope methodologies and evaluate their applicability across diverse populations, particularly in resource-limited settings.

Methods/design

Objectives

The objective of our study is to systematically review the studies that estimate β-carotene bioconversion efficiency in humans, particularly comparing the reported conversion factors across diverse population groups. Our secondary aim is to critically synthesize the methodological strengths and limitations of isotope-based approaches and their feasibility, including challenges related to standardization, invasiveness, analytical requirements, and suitability across different age groups and study settings.

This protocol is developed in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Protocols (PRISMA-P) 2015 [13].

To comprehensively capture global evidence, we will include the following study designs: experimental studies, like metabolic studies and tracer-based studies, as well as clinical or controlled feeding studies.

Additionally, we will include observational studies that utilize stable isotope measurements to evaluate β-carotene bioconversion efficiency. These studies often provide data on the bioconversion ratio for a specific population, without the need to compare it to a control group or different interventions within the same study. To ensure that our synthesis is based on robust and detailed evidence, we will use only peer-reviewed primary research studies as our main sources for searching and data extraction. Owing to practical constraints in the review process, and to maintain high methodological rigor, only studies with complete datasets will be analyzed. We will exclude non-primary publications, including review articles (narratives, scoping reviews, systematic reviews, and meta-analyses), case reports, letters, editorials, and commentaries. We will also exclude grey literature, including theses, dissertations, and conference abstracts, as these formats typically lack the detailed technical isotopic data (such as enrichment levels and background correction) required for a thorough methodological review. Additionally, we will exclude academic book-length sources (including individual chapters and full monographs) to minimize heterogeneity in study design and reporting standards, thereby enhancing the internal validity and reproducibility of the review.

Participants

Studies involving human participants across all life stages will be included. Both apparently healthy individuals and nutritionally at-risk populations will be considered. There will be no restrictions based on location; research conducted in any country and any setting (e.g., clinical, community, or metabolic ward).

Interventions and settings

Studies that report specific quantitative estimates of β-carotene bioconversion, such as conversion ratios (e.g., weight-based ratios of β-carotene to retinol), derived from stable isotope tracer methodologies will be included. Eligible studies must provide sufficient methodological detail to support quantitative interpretation, including measures such as isotopic enrichment or specific activity, or the baseline/natural abundance of isotopes, along with the specified background correction methods. On the other hand, studies that utilized non-isotopic methods to assess vitamin A status—specifically those relying solely on serum retinol measurements without tracer methodology—will be excluded from the review. Additionally, research that estimates the retinol activity equivalent (RAE) or bioconversion based only on dietary intake records or food composition tables, as well as studies that exclusively use radioactive isotopes (e.g., 14C), will also be excluded.

Comparators

The nature of the comparators will include studies that assess the impact of co-ingested factors such as fat or fiber, dosage, as well as the effects of different food matrices on isotopic responses (e.g., comparing results after 6 h to 14 days). If possible, we will also consider the effects of dietary habituation in our analysis. Studies that do not include a methodological or matrix-based comparison relevant to the quantification of the β-carotene bioconversion efficiency, rely on non-tracer methods, such as simple changes in serum retinol concentration that do not involve isotopic labeling, will be excluded.

Outcomes

We operationally define bioavailability as the fraction of an ingested β-carotene dose that is successfully absorbed by the intestinal mucosa.

Conversely, bioconversion efficiency refers to the capacity with which that absorbed β-carotene is metabolically cleaved and converted into retinol.

The main outcome of this review will be the quantitative estimates of the efficiency of intestinal β-carotene bioconversion. We will include studies that report calculated β-carotene to retinol conversion ratios (typically expressed by weight, such as 3.6:1 to 28:1) or by percentage. The measurements will be made by analyzing blood samples at specific time points following the administration of a labeled dose, often in comparison to a reference dose of vitamin A. Where possible, reported outcomes will be standardized to enable comparison between studies.

The secondary outcomes will evaluate the methodological rigor of the isotopic protocols, aiming to identify the most feasible and accurate tools for different settings. Assessment of analytical sensitivity, defined as the isotopic enrichment levels and detection limits of the mass spectrometry tools used, will be included. Furthermore, protocol feasibility will be evaluated, which includes the number of blood samples required, the total duration of the study, and the standardization measures, such as the use of baseline background correction, fasting state protocols, and controls for natural isotopic abundance. These factors will be synthesized to evaluate sources of variability and challenges in standardizing β-carotene bioconversion estimates across studies.

Outcomes based solely on serum retinol concentration or other indicators of vitamin A status will be excluded. Moreover, studies that estimate β-carotene bioconversion efficiency based exclusively on dietary intake data or food composition tables will be excluded owing to their inability to account for biological variability. Studies that only look at the reporting of outcomes without providing an assessment of the value of the tool for clinical or nutritional application will likewise be excluded.

Language

We will limit our selection to publications available in English.

Search strategy

Electronic database searches were planned and completed by February 16, 2026. These searches encompassed studies published between 1 January 2013 and 31 January 2026. This start date marks a significant methodological milestone in the use of specialized liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) techniques for stable isotopes. The 2013 publication of a validated LC–MS/MS method enabled the first simultaneous assessment of β-carotene bioavailability, bioconversion, and total vitamin A status in human subjects [7].

The database sources included Embase, Web of Science, Scopus, ProQuest, CINAHL, MEDLINE, and Academic Search Ultimate, which were searched via the EBSCOhost platform. Citation tracking will be conducted after the cutoff date for the database searches to identify any additional relevant studies.

Two authors, namely, RDF and HTL, will independently abstract all studies included in this review using a search strategy that incorporates text words, truncation, and Boolean operators. The search terms will include the following: (“Beta-carotene” OR “B-carotene” OR “Provitamin A”) AND (“Vitamin A” OR “Retinol” OR “Retinol Activity Equivalent” OR RAE) AND (Bioconversion OR Conversion OR Absorption OR Metabolism OR Intestinal) AND (Isotope OR “Stable Isotope” OR “Isotope dilution” OR “Carbon-13” OR Labelled OR Deuterium OR Tracer*) AND (Human* OR Participant* OR Subject* OR Volunteer*).

Selection process

All retrieved records will be uploaded directly to the web-based Covidence software for automated deduplication and screening [14]. The total number of duplicates removed at each stage will be automatically displayed in the PRISMA-P flow diagram.

Data collection

We will utilize the Covidence systematic review software to aid in screening studies, extracting data, and assessing the risk of bias in our review [14]. Two reviewers (RDF and HTL) will independently screen the titles and abstracts for potential relevance using predefined eligibility criteria. Any discrepancies between their assessments will be resolved by a third reviewer (MPZ).

For the full-text screening, three independent reviewers (RDF, HTL, and JLS) will thoroughly examine each article to ensure that it addresses the initial research question and does not contain any previously established exclusion criteria. Each reviewer will have the option to include or exclude an article. If an article is excluded, the reviewer must justify its exclusion. In case of disagreements, the fourth reviewer (MPZ) will be consulted to resolve any discrepancies. Articles marked as “included” will be used in the systematic review.

Next, we will extract data from the included studies. Three independent reviewers (RDF, HTL, and JLS) will carefully extract detailed information from each article. If any conflicts arise, we will resolve them through discussion, and when necessary, with the help of a fourth reviewer (MPZ).

We will use the Covidence data extraction sheet, which will be pilot tested using randomly selected studies by the review team. This testing will help us capture relevant data, including but not limited to the study ID (author and year), location, population type, study design, study objective, sample size, recruitment methods, sample eligibility criteria, sociodemographic characteristics, isotope type, label position, β-carotene dose, carrier/matrix (e.g., oil, capsule, spinach, and golden rice), reference dose (amount), meal context, technique name, analytical tool, background correction, sampling time points (e.g., 6 h, 9 h, 12 h, or 14 days), standardization (e.g., fasting state, vitamin A status at baseline), and conversion ratio.

Following the data extraction, we will assess the risk of bias using the appropriate tools for each study design: the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool for randomized controlled trials [15, 16] and for non-randomized studies of Interventions (NRSIs) will be evaluated using the ROBINS-I tool [16, 17] and JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist for analytical cross-sectional and pure observational studies [18]. Two independent reviewers (RDF and JMD) will conduct this assessment. Any discrepancies that arise between the two reviewers will be addressed through discussions with a third reviewer (MPZ) until a consensus is achieved.

If we encounter ambiguous or missing data, we will contact the primary authors, provided we have their contact details, and document these communications. Additionally, we will manually search for other potentially relevant studies that may not have been found in electronic databases due to variations in keywords, indexing terms, or other factors by reference list checking (backward citation searching) and looking through all the articles that cite the papers included in the review (forward searching).

To assess the overall certainty of evidence for key outcomes, we will use the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) methodology [19] across diverse study designs as follows:

Randomized controlled trials (RCTs): Will begin at an initial baseline of high certainty and will be evaluated for downgrading based on our RoB 2 assessments.

Randomized controlled trials (RCTs): Will begin at an initial baseline of high certainty and will be evaluated for downgrading based on our RoB 2 assessments.

Non-randomized studies of interventions (NRSIs), evaluated using the ROBINS-I tool will also begin at an initial baseline of high certainty (per standard GRADE extensions for ROBINS-I) and will be adjusted downward based on identified risks of bias.

Purely observational studies, assessed using the JBI checklists (e.g., cross-sectional and cohort designs) will begin at an initial baseline of low certainty. These studies can be downgraded further or upgraded if they meet specific criteria, such as a strong dose–response gradient or a large magnitude of effect.

Purely observational studies, assessed using the JBI checklists (e.g., cross-sectional and cohort designs) will begin at an initial baseline of low certainty. These studies can be downgraded further or upgraded if they meet specific criteria, such as a strong dose–response gradient or a large magnitude of effect.

We will assess the following domains: risk of bias, consistency, directness, precision, and publication bias. Ratings will include high, moderate, low, and very low. This will be performed by two independent reviewers (RDF and JMD), with the third reviewer (MPZ) resolving any discrepancies to ensure accuracy. Summary of Findings (SoF) tables will be generated using GRADEpro GDT software [20].

Data analysis

Owing to the anticipated heterogeneity in study design, isotope methodologies, and outcome measures, we do not expect to conduct a meta-analysis. Instead, we will narratively synthesize the findings in accordance with the Synthesis Without Meta-analysis (SWiM) guidelines [21] and provide a descriptive summary. Where possible, we will categorize results based on the methodological approach (e.g., dual-tracer vs. single-sample ratio), the life cycle stage of the population, and the food matrix (e.g., oil, supplement, or whole food).

We will analyze the primary outcome by comparing the results across these categories. To assess the impact of secondary outcomes, we will perform a qualitative sensitivity analysis. This analysis will involve comparing the β-carotene bioconversion results from “high-rigor” studies—those that use baseline background correction and high-precision mass spectrometry/mass spectrometry (MS/MS)—with those of studies that have lower levels of standardization. Any discrepancies in β-carotene bioconversion estimates will be interpreted by considering the analytical precision and subject standardization protocols.

Dissemination plans

The results of this systematic review will be published in a peer-reviewed journal focused on human nutrition or stable isotope research. The results will be reported according to the most recent version of PRISMA 2020 statement.

Furthermore, the findings will constitute a key chapter of a doctoral dissertation at the University of Santo Tomas in Manila and may be presented at relevant national or international nutrition conferences.

Discussion

The objective of our study is to systematically review stable isotope protocols used to estimate β-carotene bioconversion efficiency in humans across diverse populations and settings. The results aim to guide the selection of suitable stable isotope methods for assessing intestinal β-carotene efficiency in vulnerable populations with resource-limited settings, like the Philippines. This review also aims to provide a comprehensive understanding of the strengths and limitations of isotope methodologies to support informed decision-making.

Potential limitations of this review include restriction in English-language publication.

However, during our initial scoping search, we observed that the vast majority of high-impact, peer-reviewed literature utilizing stable isotopes to assess β-carotene bioconversion and vitamin A stores, even when conducted in non-English speaking regions of Southeast Asia, Africa, or Latin America—is predominantly published in English to maximize global reach and impact [9, 11, 22–25] Consequently, as a research team operating within a developing country context, we pragmatically limited our inclusion criteria to English-language publications due to a lack of specialized translation services. This approach ensures strict data integrity during extraction and quality assessment, thereby avoiding the well-documented risks of data misinterpretation inherent in automated translation tools.

Additionally, the anticipated heterogeneity in study designs, isotope methodologies, and outcome measures may limit direct comparability of findings. Nonetheless, the structured synthesis approach is intended to provide a transparent and rigorous overview of the current evidence base and identify priorities for future research.
