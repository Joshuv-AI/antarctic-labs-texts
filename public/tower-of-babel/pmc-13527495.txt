ABSTRACT
SummaryClinical waste management is a central component to infection prevention and control (IPC) practice across the UK and Ireland, with effective implementation of waste management being critical to a functioning healthcare system. Healthcare organisations have a duty of care to provide a safe service that correctly manages potentially hazardous clinical waste for the safety of both staff and patients.Key guidance for best practice clinical waste management in the National Health Service (NHS) is outlined in the ‘UK: Health Technical Memorandum 07-01: Safe and sustainable management of healthcare waste’ and aligns with the NHS clinical waste strategy published in March 2023.Significant barriers to the successful implementation of these guidelines in the healthcare setting are often encountered. The infection prevention specialist plays an essential role in the education of healthcare staff to ensure the guidelines are adhered to at a practical level and to provide understanding of the waste management hierarchy. It is important to involve all key stakeholders to ensure the best chance of a positive outcome. Hospital management is responsible for ensuring there is a clear waste management policy and ensuring the engagement of competent, certified waste contractors. The IPC team members are key stakeholders with responsibility for setting and implementing standards on how waste is segregated to prevent infection spread. The hospital’s Green Committee should also be involved to support delivery of a service with sustainability and costs to the forefront, in accordance with the ‘Net Zero Carbon’ targets.Keywords: Clinical waste management, Infection prevention and control, Sustainability

Background

The healthcare sector is a significant contributor to the climate crisis, with the global healthcare sector accounting for 5% of total emissions (World Health Organization [WHO] 2025). The National Health Service (NHS) generates an estimated 156,000 tonnes of healthcare waste annually on average via secondary care (NHS, ERIC data 2020/21) [3], resulting in an estimated 100,000 tonnes of carbon dioxide emissions per year. Therefore, addressing this contribution and implementing a sustainable safe clinical waste management system is imperative for present and future generations.

Guidelines and evidence

Key guidance for clinical waste management is outlined in the Health Technical Memorandum (HTM) 07-01, a framework for best practice in clinical waste management in the UK [4]. This guidance aims to transform the management of clinical waste in all healthcare settings by eliminating unnecessary waste, finding innovative ways to reuse waste, and ensuring waste is processed in the most cost-effective, efficient and sustainable way, in accordance with the NHS clinical waste strategy published in March 2023.

HTM 07-01 outlines the Principles of Sustainable Waste Management, describing the core components of ‘Waste Hierarchy’ (Figure 1).

HTM 07-01 classifies clinical waste into the following categories: infectious waste, highly infectious waste, sharps waste, pharmaceutical waste, cytotoxic and cytostatic waste, and offensive waste. Clinical waste is segregated into categories at the point of generation, for example, on the ward, in the laboratory or in theatre. Segregation via a colour-coding system is recommended with waste containers colour-coded according to their content to facilitate ease of identification by healthcare workers. Correct segregation of clinical waste at the point of generation has been shown to play a key role in effective management, both in terms of environmental and cost impacts as it minimises unnecessary incineration of waste.

Addressing key areas to achieve a more sustainable clinical waste management system is a priority in HTM 07-01. ‘Delivering a Net Zero NHS’ is a strategy outlined in 2020 by NHS England which aims to achieve net zero for the carbon emissions controlled by the NHS directly by 2040 and to achieve net zero for the carbon emissions that the NHS can influence by 2045 [6].

Substandard clinical waste management contributes to a higher risk of occupational injury for healthcare workers and waste handlers and can lead to healthcare-associated infections and needle-stick injuries [7].

Supporting the successful implementation of these comprehensive guidelines is a significant challenge faced by infection prevention and control (IPC) teams. Educating healthcare workers has been shown to be a major determinant of correct waste segregation, with centres where guidance and training on waste management are provided demonstrating better practice [8].

There are many tools and recommendations available from various bodies across the UK and Ireland which aid in the education and implementation of the best practice guidelines at a practice level.

In 2018, the Royal College of Physicians published a sustainability report ‘Less waste, more health: A health professionals guide to reducing waste’ which included case studies and recommendations on how a trust can reduce waste production and increase waste prevention [9].

The NHS England provides a waste planning toolkit, an interactive online tool that is designed to help NHS trusts improve waste segregation, reduce carbon emissions and meet net-zero targets [5].

In the Irish health service, the HSE, the Green Healthcare Programme works with Irish Hospitals to reduce healthcare-risk waste and increase recycling. The website provides a sustainability tool for health professionals to understand the volume of waste generated by their facility, along with a bank of resources made up of sample campaigns and projects on waste management running across the health service [2].

When rolling out guidelines and associated education, it is important to involve all key stakeholders (i.e. estates, nurse managers, etc.) to ensure the best chance of a positive outcome. The success of the implementation of recommended best practice guidelines into clinical practice should be regularly audited by the infection prevention specialist and their IPC teams. This allows areas of practice requiring improvement to be identified so targeted education can be delivered, with the aim of improving practice to meet the recommended standards.

Recent literature on the topic

The NHS Clinical Waste Strategy is a rolling 10-year strategy published in 2023 that focuses on enhancing clinical waste management in the NHS with six key areas targeted: data, workforce, compliance, commercial, infrastructure and sustainability. It aims to cut carbon emissions by approximately 30% with predicted savings of £11 million annually in accordance with the net-zero targets. Key contributing factors highlighted include poor waste segregation, inadequate volume of recycling and the environmental impact of incineration. It focusses on the circular economy and promotes reusing and recycling to minimise healthcare waste.

It is the role of the IPC team to prevent the spread and onward risk of infection within the healthcare setting. With this comes guidance and education around the use of personal protective equipment (PPE) among healthcare professionals. Striking the balance between appropriate use of PPE to prevent onward transmission of healthcare-related infection and inappropriate overuse of PPE leading to unnecessary clinical waste can be difficult.

Since the COVID-19 pandemic, much literature has been published on the large volume of healthcare waste produced during that period [1]. An extensive WHO report outlines the lessons learned from the pandemic, along with recommendations on clinical waste management for the future [11].

Particular focus has been put on the overuse of gloves as a large generator of unnecessary clinical waste. The literature has shown that healthcare workers often substitute gloves for hand hygiene, not only increasing contamination and spread of infection but also increasing unnecessary clinical waste [10]. Campaigns have been rolled out across the UK and Ireland by infection prevention specialists and affiliated groups to raise awareness around glove overuse in healthcare.
