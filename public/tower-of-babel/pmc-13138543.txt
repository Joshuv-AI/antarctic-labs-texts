Comparison of the efficacy of alfuzosin and mirabegron on symptoms associated with Double-J stent placement after retrograde intrarenal surgery
Authors: Mehmet Öztürk, Ferhat Taskir, Sakip Erturhan, Onur Zeytun, Omer Bayrak, Ilker Seckiner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Aims:This study aimed to evaluate the efficacy of alfuzosin, mirabegron, and their combination in terms of stone-free rates, residual fragments, stent-related pain, and lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) in patients undergoing Double-J (DJ) stent placement following retrograde intrarenal surgery (RIRS).Materials and Methods:A prospective, randomized, single-center trial was conducted between February 2022 and December 2023 at Gaziantep University. A total of 103 patients who underwent RIRS for unilateral renal stones measuring 1–2 cm followed by DJ stenting were enrolled. Patients were randomized into four groups: Group 1 (control group, no medication), Group 2 (alfuzosin 10 mg/day), Group 3 (mirabegron 50 mg/day), and Group 4 (combination therapy with alfuzosin and mirabegron). Pain and LUTS were assessed 30 days postoperatively using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) and the Overactive Bladder-Validated 8 (OAB-V8) questionnaire.Results:The mean age was 43.55 ± 13.93 years. No statistically significant difference was found between groups with respect to stone-free rates, residual stones, pain, or LUTS (P > 0.05). Group 2 (alfuzosin) had the lowest mean VAS (1.69 ± 1.52) and OAB-V8 (13.5 ± 7.37) scores; however, these differences were not statistically significant when compared to the control group (P = 0.112 and P = 0.592, respectively). Residual stones were detected in 15.5% of patients, and stone size was identified as a significant predictor (P = 0.025).Conclusions:The use of alfuzosin and mirabegron, either alone or in combination, did not have a significant effect on stone-free rates, stent-related pain, and LUTS. Although lower pain and symptom scores were observed in the alfuzosin group, these findings should be interpreted with caution due to the lack of statistical significance. The relatively small sample size and single-center design represent important limitations of this study. Further large-scale, multicenter studies are warranted to clarify the potential role of these agents in the management of DJ stent-related symptoms.

INTRODUCTION

Urinary tract stone disease is a common and recurrent urologic health problem leading to substantial morbidity worldwide.[1] In recent years, the widespread use of modern imaging modalities and the development of minimally invasive surgical techniques and laser technology have led to a significant paradigm shift in the management of this disease. In this context, retrograde intrarenal surgery (RIRS) has become an increasingly preferred method, particularly for the treatment of renal stones ≤2 cm.[2]

RIRS allows the fragmentation of stones within the kidney via transurethral access, using a flexible ureterorenoscope and laser technology. However, Double-J (DJ) stents are frequently placed postoperatively to facilitate the spontaneous passage of stone fragments and to prevent obstructive complications.[3] Although DJ stenting enhances urinary drainage and stone fragment passage, it is associated with side effects such as storage symptoms (frequency, urgency, pollakiuria, and nocturia), dysuria, low back pain, suprapubic discomfort, and a deterioration in overall quality of life.[4] These symptoms often negatively affect patients’ quality of life and necessitate additional medical treatment.

Various pharmacological agents have been proposed to reduce the symptoms associated with the DJ stent placement. Among these, alpha-1 adrenergic receptor antagonists, particularly alfuzosin and tamsulosin, facilitate stone passage by relaxing ureteral smooth muscle and alleviating stent-related symptoms.[5] The therapeutic effect of alpha-blockers is attributed to a reduction in sympathetic noradrenergic tone within the ureteral wall. In contrast, mirabegron, which acts through β3-adrenergic receptors in the bladder, prolongs the storage phase, decreases urinary frequency, and improves urgency control by inducing relaxation of the detrusor muscle. It has been suggested that these favorable effects of mirabegron may reduce DJ stent-related bladder irritability.[6]

Although the effects of alpha-blockers and mirabegron on DJ stent-related symptoms have been evaluated in numerous studies, a limited number of prospective trials have directly compared these agents either alone or in combination. Moreover, the available evidence remains conflicting. In this context, our study was designed to compare the efficacy of alfuzosin and mirabegron on symptoms in patients with DJ stent implantation after RIRS. In addition, the effects of these agents, both as monotherapy and in combination, on stone-free rates, the presence of residual stones, DJ stent-related pain, and lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) were evaluated.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This prospective, comparative, single-center clinical trial was conducted at the Urology Clinic of Gaziantep University Faculty of Medicine between February 2022 and December 2023. The study was prospectively designed and retrospectively documented (Gaziantep University Clinical Research Ethics Committee; approval number 2024/05). The study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT07018934); however, registration was completed after enrollment had begun (retrospective registration). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. All procedures were performed in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Patient selection

Adult patients aged ≥18 years with unilateral kidney stones measuring <3 cm in diameter who underwent RIRS followed by DJ stent placement were included in the study. Only patients who completed regular postoperative follow-up visits were enrolled.

Patients with a history of previous urinary tract surgery, bilateral stone disease, a solitary kidney, neurogenic bladder, bladder tumor, or active urinary tract infection, as well as those who were unable to tolerate or were noncompliant with the prescribed medical therapy, were excluded from the study.

Patient groups and treatment protocols

Patients were randomly assigned to four treatment groups. Patients who met the inclusion criteria were numbered consecutively starting from 1 according to the order of presentation. Randomization was performed sequentially as follows:

→ Group 1: Control group (no medical treatment)→ Group 2: Alfuzosin 10 mg/day orally→ Group 3: Mirabegron 50 mg/day orally→ Group 4: Combination therapy with alfuzosin and mirabegron.

This allocation sequence was repeated cyclically, starting with the fifth patient.

Medical treatments were initiated on the first postoperative day and continued for a minimum of 30 days.

Surgical procedure

All procedures were performed under general anesthesia with the patient placed in the lithotomy position. Initially, ureteral access was achieved using rigid ureterorenoscopy. Subsequently, a ureteral access sheath (Navigator™, Boston Scientific) with a diameter of 11–13 Fr and a length of 46 cm was inserted over a guidewire under fluoroscopic guidance. The stones were accessed using a flexible digital ureterorenoscope (OTU-100 SR, WiScope®) and fragmentation was achieved using holmium: YAG laser (n = 96) or thulium fiber laser (n = 7). A 6 Fr DJ stent was implanted in all patients at the end of the procedure.

Evaluation and follow-up

Patients were scheduled for follow-up 4 weeks postoperatively. Residual stone status was evaluated using kidney, ureter, and bladder radiography for radiopaque stones and urinary ultrasonography (US) for radiolucent stones. Stones <3 mm in diameter were considered clinically insignificant residual fragments. The severity of stent-related pain was assessed using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) (0-10). LUTS were evaluated using the Overactive Bladder-Validated 8 (OAB-V8) questionnaire.[78]

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and MedCalc version 19.7.1. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed data and as median (minimum–maximum) for nonnormally distributed data, whereas categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages (%).

The distribution of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For variables that followed a normal distribution, the Student’s t-test was used for pairwise comparisons and one-way analysis of variance for comparisons among the four groups. For variables that were not normally distributed or when normality assumptions were not met, the Mann–Whitney U test was applied for pairwise comparisons and the Kruskal–Wallis test for comparisons among the four groups.

When a statistically significant difference was detected in overall group comparisons, post hoc pairwise analyses were performed using the Bonferroni correction to adjust for multiple comparisons and reduce the risk of type I error. Associations between categorical variables were evaluated using the Chi-square test. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

A total of 112 patients who underwent DJ stenting after RIRS and were followed for at least 1 month were initially included in the study. Nine patients were excluded due to non-compliance with the follow-up protocol, and therefore, data from 103 patients were analyzed. The number of patients in each group is illustrated in Figure 1.

Distribution of patients by groups

The mean age of the patients was 43.55 ± 13.93 years (18–85 years). Male patients accounted for 62.1%, whereas 37.9% were female. Renal stones were most commonly localized in the renal pelvis (46.6%), followed by the lower calyx (34.0%). In the preoperative period, 61.2% of the patients had a DJ stent in place. The laser modality used during the procedure was holmium: YAG in 93.2% of cases [Table 1]. There was no statistically significant difference among the four groups with respect to stone localization, preoperative DJ stent presence, or type of laser therapy applied (P > 0.05). Residual stone rates were also comparable among the groups [Table 2].

Based on VAS scores ranging from 0 to 10, the lowest mean pain score was observed in the alfuzosin group (1.69 ± 1.52). However, no statistically significant difference was found between the treatment groups and the control group or among the groups themselves (Group 1 vs. Group 2: P =0.112; Group 1 vs. Group 3: P =0.911; Group 1 vs. Group 4: P =0.550; overall intergroup comparison P = 0.301).

The mean LUTS scores assessed using the OAB-V8 questionnaire were 14.64 ± 8.21 in the control, 13.5 ± 7.37 in the alfuzosin, 16.22 ± 9.71 in the mirabegron group and 14.5 ± 10 in the combination group (Group 1 vs. Group 2: P =0.592; Group 1 vs. Group 3: P =0.519; and Group 1 vs. Group 4: P =0.957, intergroup comparison P = 0.732). Although the lowest mean scores were observed in the alfuzosin group, these differences did not reach statistical significance [Table 2].

Imaging studies performed during the 1 month follow-up visit showed residual calculi in 16 of 103 patients (15.5%). Variables including age, sex, stone location, type of laser therapy, preoperative DJ stent presence, stone density (Hounsfield Unit), pain scores, and OAB-V8 scores were not significantly associated with the presence of residual stones. However, stone size was significantly associated with residual stone presence (P = 0.025) [Table 3].

DISCUSSION

In a systematic review and meta-analysis by Nabi et al., data from patients who underwent uncomplicated ureteroscopy with DJ stent placement were compared with those without stenting, and LUTS such as frequency, urgency, and dysuria were found to be significantly more common in the stented group (P = 0.02).[9] It has been reported that approximately 80% of patients undergoing ureteral stenting experience ureteral stent-related pain.[10] Therefore, effective symptom management during the stent indwelling period is important for maintaining the patient quality of life.

In a study by Beddingfield et al., patients with DJ stents were randomized to receive alfuzosin or placebo, and flank pain was assessed using a 0-10 VAS score. The authors reported significantly lower pain scores in the alfuzosin group (P = 0.017).[11] Similarly, Damiano et al. demonstrated that tamsulosin significantly improved stent-related symptoms, including pain, dysuria, and hematuria.[12] Kwon et al. reported symptom improvement with both tamsulosin and alfuzosin.[13] However, the magnitude of symptom relief appears to vary depending on drug selectivity, dosage, treatment duration, and patient-related factors. In the present study, although mean pain scores were numerically lower in the alfuzosin group, this difference did not reach statistical significance. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and cannot be considered evidence of a definitive clinical benefit. The lack of statistical significance may be related to the relatively small sample size and individual variability in symptom perception.

Li et al. conducted a meta-analysis on the efficacy and safety of mirabegron for ureteral stent-related symptoms and reported significant symptom improvement with a favorable safety profile.[14] In a prospective randomized study by Tae et al., mirabegron significantly reduced overall pain scores compared with controls in patients undergoing DJ stenting after URS/RIRS (P = 0.002).[15] Similarly, Javid et al. demonstrated that mirabegron was more effective than tamsulosin in reducing urinary symptoms and body pain based on the Ureteral Stent Symptom Questionnaire (USSQ).[16] In contrast to these findings, our study did not demonstrate a statistically significant difference between treatment groups in terms of LUTS as assessed by the OAB-V8 questionnaire. Mirabegron exerts its effect by inducing detrusor relaxation and prolonging the bladder storage phase; although its efficacy in overactive bladder syndrome is well established, its short-term impact on stent-related irritative symptoms remains unclear.[17] The comparable OAB-V8 scores between the mirabegron and control groups in our cohort suggest that any potential benefit may be limited during the early postoperative period. Previous studies have indicated that mirabegron-related symptom improvement may become more apparent after 6–8 weeks of treatment,[18] which may partly explain the lack of observed benefit within the 1-month follow-up period of our study.

Zhang et al. compared mirabegron and tamsulosin combination therapy with tamsulosin monotherapy after ureteroscopic lithotripsy and silicone stent placement. They found no significant differences in analgesic use or USSQ scores, concluding that combination therapy did not provide any additional pain control benefits.[19] Consistent with these findings, no statistically significant difference was observed between the combination therapy group and the other treatment groups in our study.

Stone-free rates were comparable among all treatment groups, and no significant association was found between medical therapy and spontaneous stone clearance. However, a significant correlation was observed between residual stone presence and stone size. This finding is consistent with previous studies demonstrating that stone size is a major determinant of stone-free status after RIRS.[20] Likewise, the European Association of Urology guidelines indicate a significantly increased risk of residual fragments following RIRS in stones larger than 15 mm.[21]

The preoperative DJ stenting did not significantly affect postoperative pain or LUTS scores. This suggests that postoperative stent-related symptoms are primarily attributable to the indwelling stent itself rather than preoperative DJ stent placement. Similar results were reported by Yuk et al., who found no significant impact of preoperative stenting on symptom burden.[22]

The strengths of this study include the homogeneous distribution of treatment groups and the multidimensional evaluation of both stone-free outcomes and symptom scores. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. The relatively small sample size may have limited the statistical power to detect subtle differences, and the follow-up period was restricted to the first postoperative month, precluding assessment of long-term symptom trajectories. In addition, the allocation of patients to treatment groups was performed using a sequential, cyclic assignment based on the order of enrollment rather than a fully concealed or computer-generated randomization method. This approach may have introduced selection bias and limited the robustness of group comparability, despite similar baseline characteristics among groups. Another limitation of this study is that the OAB-V8 questionnaire, although validated for storage-phase LUTS, is not specifically designed for stent-related symptoms.

CONCLUSIONS

Alfuzosin and mirabegron, whether administered alone or in combination, did not result in statistically significant improvements in stone-free rates, pain scores, and LUTS in patients undergoing DJ stenting after RIRS. Although alfuzosin demonstrated numerically lower pain and storage symptom scores, these differences were not statistically significant and should not be interpreted as definitive clinical benefit. Larger, multicenter studies with extended follow-up periods are warranted to more accurately define the potential role of these agents in the management of ureteral stent-related symptoms and to clarify whether specific patient subgroups may derive measurable benefit.
