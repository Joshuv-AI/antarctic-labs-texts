Development of multiplex real-time RT‒PCR assays for the simultaneous detection and lineage differentiation of H9 avian influenza viruses
Authors: Se-Hee An, Gyeong-Beom Heo, Bina Lee, Kwang-Nyeong Lee, Youn-Jeong Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractH9 avian influenza viruses (AIVs) have caused continuous economic losses in poultry and pose a risk of introducing novel lineages through wild birds and human activities, highlighting the importance of rapid detection and lineage discrimination for effective surveillance. In this study, we developed a multiplex real-time reverse transcription PCR (rRT‒PCR) assay that simultaneously detects the H9 gene and differentiates the Y and B lineages using a single set of common primers with one H9 common probe (H9-Probe) and two B and Y lineage-specific probes (B-Probe and Y-Probe). The assay displayed high analytical specificity (100%) in detecting H9 and differentiating B and Y lineages in tests with 50 reference strains, 6 synthetic G lineage H9 genes and 28 field samples. Experiments with synthesized G lineage constructs showed fluorescence signals with the H9-Probe but not the B-Probe and Y-Probe, suggesting its potential to indicate the presence of G lineage AIVs. The limit of detection was 10–10² EID₅₀, and the performance with field samples was comparable to that of a commercial kit. In the mixed B and Y lineage sample, each lineage was reliably detected and distinguished according to its relative concentration, but detection was slightly delayed when the sample was mixed with high concentrations of the virus. Collectively, this multiplex H9 rRT‒PCR assay provides a sensitive, specific, and cost-efficient tool for the simultaneous detection and lineage differentiation of H9 AIVs, supporting the large-scale surveillance and early identification of emerging variants.Keywords: Multiplex, H9, Lineage differentiation, Avian influenza virus, Real-time PCR

Introduction

Influenza A virus (IAV) has eight negative-sense single-stranded RNA genome segments and is classified according to the subtypes of two surface glycoproteins: hemagglutinin (HA) and neuraminidase (NA) (Webster et al., 1992). The natural host of avian influenza virus (AIV) is wild waterfowl, which can be infected with the H1–H16 and N1–N9 subtypes of AIVs (Spackman, 2020). The pathogenicity of AIVs is determined by their virulence in infected poultry, and the pathogenicity of H5 and H7 subtype AIVs usually increase during circulation in poultry (Alexander, 2000). Although the H9 subtype AIV is a low-pathogenicity avian influenza (LPAI) virus, it can cause economic losses in the poultry industry through the induction of egg drop in chickens and mortality when a coinfection with other avian viruses or bacteria occurs (Guo et al., 2000; Kishida et al., 2004; Kye et al., 2021). Additionally, H9N2 AIVs are known as internal gene donors for high-pathogenicity avian influenza (HPAI) viruses because of genetic reassortment, and cases of human infection by endemic H9 AIVs have been reported in various countries through direct contact with infected poultry (Guan et al., 1999; Di Liu and Gao, 2014; Buczkowski et al., 2025).

H9 subtype AIV is widely distributed in wild birds and has become endemic to poultry in many countries in Asia, Africa and the Middle East (Peacock et al., 2019). Given that the H9 gene has undergone extensive genetic evolution, resulting in the emergence of numerous sublineages, an international consortium conducted a comprehensive analysis of all available H9 genes and proposed a standardized nomenclature for H9 AIVs in 2024 (Fusaro et al., 2024). Traditionally, H9 AIVs are classified into four major lineages: Y280 (BJ94, G9), Y439 (Korean), G1 and American (Li et al., 2017; Peacock et al., 2019; Fusaro et al., 2024). In the study by Fusaro et al. (2024), the H9 gene was classified into three major lineages—Y, B, and G—and both the American lineage and the Y439 lineage were grouped with the Y lineage (Fusaro et al., 2024). The increasing genetic diversification of H9 AIVs and the co-circulation of multiple lineages pose significant challenges for surveillance and control programs. Although several real-time RT-PCR assays have been developed for the detection of H9 AIVs, most diagnostic methods are designed for subtype detection only and do not allow lineage differentiation (Saito et al., 2019; Panzarin et al., 2022; WOAH, 2023). As different H9 lineages exhibit distinct epidemiological characteristics, transmission dynamics, and geographic distributions, the inability to rapidly discriminate lineages limit early detection of newly introduced variants and reduces the effectiveness of surveillance systems (Peacock et al., 2019; Sagong et al., 2022).

In Korea, Y9 (Y439) lineage H9N2 AIVs, according to the new nomenclature, were first introduced in 1996 and have been diversified into multiple genotypes by genetic reassortment with LPAI viruses since they became endemic after 2000 (Lee et al., 2007; Youk et al., 2020). Although vaccination of laver and broiler chickens has been conducted since 2007, the virus was continuously detected primarily in live bird markets (LBMs) until 2018, and no cases have been reported in poultry to date in Korea (Sagong et al., 2022; Fusaro et al., 2024). In 2020, B (Y280) lineage H9N2 AIVs were first detected in the LBM of Korea and have since continued to circulate on poultry farms and LBMs (Heo et al., 2021; Cho et al., 2025). This virus was very closely related to the Y280 lineage H9N2 virus isolated in China (represented by A/chicken/Shangdong/1844/2019) and clustered with the B4.6 lineage H9 gene (Heo et al., 2021; Fusaro et al., 2024). Y3 and Y8 (Y439) lineage H9 AIVs have been continuously detected in wild birds in Korea, and the possibility of transmission of viruses of these lineages to poultry cannot be excluded (Na et al., 2021). Furthermore, G and B lineage H9N2 AIVs endemic to many countries in Asia, Africa and the Middle East can be newly introduced into Korea via human-mediated activities. Therefore, rapid lineage discrimination is essential in Korea to distinguish endemic strains from newly introduced viruses originating from wild birds or international transmission routes.

Effective surveillance of H9 AIV requires not only sensitive detection but also rapid lineage identification to monitor viral evolution and detect potential introductions of novel strains. The Animal and Plant Quarantine Agency (APQA) developed two different H9 rRT-PCR assays in singleplex and multiplex formats to detect H9 AIVs (Sagong et al., 2023; An et al., 2024). However, these methods cannot differentiate H9 lineages and additional sequencing analyses are required for lineage identification, which increases diagnostic time and workload. Therefore, this study aimed to develop and evaluate a multiplex H9 rRT‒PCR assay capable of simultaneously detecting the H9 gene and differentiating between the Y and B lineages, providing a cost-efficient and rapid tool for large-scale surveillance and early identification of emerging H9 lineage.

Materials and methods

Design of primer and probe sets for three H9 lineages (Y, B, and G) and in silico analysis

A total of 288 reference sequences of H9 genes (84 for the Y lineage, 92 for the B lineage and 112 for the G lineage) used in the H9 nomenclature system proposed by Fusaro, Pu et al. and 364 H9 sequences reported from 2022 to 2024 were collected from GISAID (https://www.gisaid.org) to design the primers and probes (Fusaro et al., 2024). Multiple sequence alignment and analysis were performed to determine the optimal primer and probe binding sites using CLC workbench ver.20. Two forward (H9-F-1 and 2) primers and one reverse (H9-R) primer and one probe (H9-Probe) were designed to be used for all H9 genes regardless of lineage, and two probes (B-Probe and Y-Probe) were used for the B lineage and Y lineage, respectively (Table 1). The primer binding site was located between nucleotides 1366 and 1598 in the coding region of the H9 gene (Fig. 1). In silico sequence matching of the designed primers and probes was performed with the CLC Main workbench ver.20 using Find binding sites and the create fragments function with the match criteria defined as allowing no more than three mismatches with at least three consecutive matches at the 3′ end of the overall primer and probe sequence.

Viruses, synthesis of the partial G lineage H9 gene and titration of the viruses

Fifty AIV strains (H1-H15), including 7 H9 AIVs isolated by APQA from clinical samples collected during national surveillance programs obtained from wild birds and poultry in Korea, were used in this study for analytical specificity and sensitivity test (Table 2). Based on the nomenclature system proposed by Fusaro et al., the seven H9 viruses were classified into the Y and B lineages, formerly referred to as the Y439 and Y280 lineages, respectively, and were further classified into the Y3, Y8, Y9 and B4.6 sublineages (Fusaro et al., 2024) (Table 2). A/duck/Alberta/60/76 (H12N5) was kindly provided by Prof. Kida Hiroshi of Hokkaido University, and A/mallard/Gurjev/263/82 (H14N5) and A/shearwater/Australia/2576/79 (H15N9) were obtained from Prof. Ian Brown of the Animal and Plant Health Agency. All AIVs listed in Table 2 were propagated in 10-day-old embryonated chicken eggs (ECEs) for 72 h of incubation at 37°C, and the presence of the virus in the harvested allantoic fluid was confirmed by a hemagglutination (HA) assay and RT‒PCR using the in-house subtyping method of APQA. The virus-propagating allantoic fluid was aliquoted and stored at −80°C, and a fresh aliquot was used for each experiment. The viral titer used in the analytical sensitivity assay was determined as the 50% egg infectious dose (EID50). Tenfold serially diluted virus was inoculated into five 10-day-old ECEs per dilution and incubated at 37°C for 72 h. Viral replication at each dilution was assessed using an HA assay, and the EID50 was calculated with the Spearman–Karber method (Hamilton et al., 1977).

As G (referred to the G1 lineage) lineage H9 AIVs have not been detected in Korea, several G lineage H9 genes were synthesized based on the GISAID database. The assay target region is located within the HA2 coding region and full-length HA gene synthesis was not always feasible depending on sequence characteristics; therefore, the HA2 sequences spanning from the GLF motif to the stop codon of the HA gene (660 bp), which include all primer and probe binding sites (1366 and 1598), were synthesized (Table 2). The selected strains included A/quail/Shantou/1235/2001 (G3 lineage), A/environment/GZ/NIOSH-25/2024 (G4 lineage), A/broiler chicken/Iran/UTMarjan Mehr/2022 (G5.3.2 lineage), A/chicken/Israel/402037/2021 (G5.5 lineage) and A/chicken/Egypt/DT21108OP/2023 (G5.6 lineage) were synthesized in a cloned form within the pMG-AMP vector (2710 bp) (Macrogen, Korea) and used as plasmid DNA templates. Copy numbers were calculated based on plasmid length and concentration, adjusted to 1010 copies/μl, and diluted to a final concentration of 105 copies/μl for evaluation in the multiplex H9 rRT‒PCR assay.

Extraction of viral RNA and multiplex H9 rRT‒PCR assay

Viral RNA was extracted from 100 μl of allantoic fluid using an NX-48 viral NA kit with a Nextractor® NX-48 N automatic RNA extraction instrument (Genolution, Korea) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Multiplex rRT‒PCR was performed with a Bio-Rad CFX96 real-time PCR instrument (Bio-Rad, USA) using an AgPath-ID™ One-Step RT‒PCR kit (Applied Biosystems, USA). Each rRT-PCR contained 250 nM each of H9-F-1 and H9-F-2 (0.05 μl from 100 pmol/μl), 500 nM of H9-R (0.1 μl from 100 pmol/μl), 166 nM each of the three probes (100 pmol/μl, 0.033 μl) and 5 µl of total RNA (25 µl volume) with the rRT-PCR reagents provided with the AgPath-ID™ One-Step RT‒PCR kit. For experimental consistency, master mixes equivalent to 50–100 reactions were prepared as working volumes and aliquoted to each reaction, allowing all panel viruses to be evaluated using the same mixture across independent experimental replicates. The thermal cycling conditions consisted of 45°C for 20 min and 95°C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles at 95°C for 15 s and 58°C for 1 min. The baseline threshold was automatically calculated using Bio-Rad CFX Maestro software version 2.2 based on negative control signals. All experiments were independently performed in triplicate, with three technical replicates per experiment, and the average Ct values from three independent experiments are presented as the final results.

For comparison, a commercial rRT‒PCR kit targeting the M gene, the VDx® AIV rRT‒PCR kit (Median Diagnostics, Republic of Korea), and a commercial rRT‒PCR kit for the M and H9 genes, AnyQvet AIV M/H9 qRT‒PCR kit (Koreagene, Korea), were used to obtain reference Ct values according to the manufacturers’ instructions.

Evaluation of analytical specificity and sensitivity

Analytical specificity was tested using 50 AIV strains (H1–H15), and the analytical sensitivity and limit of detection (LoD) of the multiplex rRT‒PCR assay were assessed for five H9 strains, including two Y9 lineage strains isolated in 2001 and 2018 (A/chicken/Korea/01310/2001 (01310) and A/chicken/Korea/LBM294/2018 (LBM294)), one Y8 lineage strain (A/wild bird/Korea/21H225/2021 (21H225)), one Y3 lineage strain (A/wild bird/Korea/19H0019/2019 (19H019)) and one B4.6 lineage strain (A/chicken/Korea/C479/2023 (C479)) (Table 2 and Fig. 2). Each virus was serially diluted with PBS from 106 to 10-1 EID50/0.1 ml, and the LoD was defined as the lowest viral titer (EID50/ml) of each virus at which at least 95% of the replicates were detected in the three independently performed experiments with three technical replicates (Broeders et al., 2014). A standard curve was drawn with the average Ct value of the three replicates from independent experiments, and the regression equation, correlation coefficient (R2), PCR efficiency (E) and amplification factor (AF) were also calculated with the average Ct value for each fluorescent dye (Kralik and Ricchi, 2017).

Evaluation of the performance of the multiplex H9 rRT‒PCR assay on field samples

H9 subtype field samples collected through nationwide surveillance programs conducted from 2018 to 2025 were used to evaluate the performance of the multiplex H9 rRT‒PCR assay (Table 3). Oropharyngeal and cloacal swabs and fecal samples from chickens and Korean native chickens were obtained from poultry at the LPM and cloacal swabs of spot-billed duck were obtained from captive wild birds during the winter seasons of 2020 and 2023. Fecal samples were prepared as 10% (w/v) suspensions with PBS supplemented with antibiotics by adding 1 g of feces to 9 ml of PBS, and oropharyngeal and cloacal swab samples were collected in 3 ml virus transporting medium for direct use. After the samples were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 5 min, RNA was extracted from 100 μl of the supernatant. The subtypes of those samples were previously determined to be H9 by singleplex rRT‒PCR targeting the H9 gene and were confirmed again with a commercial rRT‒PCR kit in this study for comparison.

Simultaneous detection of mixed lineage samples using multiplex H9 by rRT‒PCR

Three viruses belonging to the Y8 (21H225), 2018 Y9 (LBM294) and B4.6 (C479) lineages detected in Korea were mixed at various concentrations and tested to assess the ability of the multiplex H9 rRT–PCR assay to simultaneously detected two different H9 lineages mixed in one sample (Table 4). Specifically, 21H225 (Y8), which is frequently detected in wild birds in Korea; LBM294 (2018 Y9), which circulated in poultry in Korea until 2018; and C479 (B4.6), which has been predominant in Korea since 2020, were selected, and equal volumes of viral genomes extracted from seven different concentrations (106 and 10° EID50/0.1 ml) of viruses were mixed in various combinations of the two viruses. Five microliters of each RNA mixture was tested using the multiplex H9 rRT‒PCR and an AgPath-ID™ One-Step RT‒PCR kit as described above, and D.W. was added instead of RNA to the single RNA samples of each virus.

Results

Design of primer/probe sets of multiplex H9 rRT-PCR assay and an in silico analysis

A common primer pair for all H9 lineages was designed to amplify a conserved genomic region of all three H9 lineages, minimize amplification bias, and ensure a comparable amplification efficiency among targets (Table 1 and Fig. 1). One probe (H9-Probe) was designed to universally detect all H9 lineages by targeting a conserved sequence in the H9 gene, thereby confirming the presence of the H9 virus. In parallel, additional probes specific to the B lineage (B-Probe) and Y lineage (Y-Probe) labeled with a distinct fluorophore were designed to hybridize to lineage-defining nucleotide variants within the same amplicon, allowing the simultaneous confirmation of the presence of H9 and lineage differentiation in a single multiplex H9 rRT–PCR reaction (Table 1). For this purpose, we compared 652 H9 sequences to select highly conserved regions for common forward and reverse primers and probe among three H9 lineages and lineage-specific variable regions for lineage-specific probe binding regions (Fig. 1). In particular, primer and probe design prioritized viral sequences relatively frequently detected in wild birds (Y3 and Y8 lineage) or poultry (Y9 lineage up to 2018 and B4.6 lineage since 2020) in Korea.

The common forward primers (H9-F-1 and H9-F-2), reverse primer (H9-R) and probe (H9-Probe) showed high sequence identity with all three H9 lineages in the in silico analysis, from 97.6 to 100.0% in the Y lineage, 96.7-100.0% in the B lineage, and 97.3-99.1% in the G lineage (Table 1). The mismatched strains in the in silico analysis contained a single-nucleotide mismatch located at positions 1 to 3 from the 3′ end of the primer or probe sequences, and the total number of mismatches was no more than three across the entire target sequence. Only two strains belonging to the Y2.2 or G4 lineages had four mismatches across the target sequence of H9-R or H9-Probe, respectively, and no mismatches at the 3′ end. The Y-Probe showed an 89.3% match with the Y lineage, while it did not match the B or G lineages, except for one strain belonging to the G5.7 lineage (Table 1). Most Y lineage sequences that did not match Y-Probe belonged to the Y-like lineage isolated in 1978–1979, the Y1 lineage isolated in 2002–2009 and the Y4 lineage isolated in the America. They were inevitably underrepresented during probe design and had four mismatches in the probe sequence without mismatches at the 3' end. The B-Probe also showed an 88.0% match with the B lineage and a 0.0% match with the Y lineage, but it also showed a 25.0% sequence match with the G lineage strains. Most of the strains of the B lineage that matched with B-Probe only had less than two mismatches, and the unmatched sequences had a single-nucleotide mismatch at the 3′ end of the probe sequence without more than two mismatches. However, the G lineage strains that matched with the B-Probe strains had more than two mismatches, and most of the strains were G-like, G2, G3 and G5.1 lineages isolated during 1999–2005, and only one or two strains belonging to the G5.3.2, G5.5, G5.6 and G5.7 lineages were included.

Analytical specificity, analytical sensitivity and limit of detection of multiplex H9 rRT–PCR assays

The multiplex H9 rRT‒PCR assay detected only H9 AIVs among the 50 reference strains and did not show cross-reactivity with other subtypes (H1–H8 and H10–H15), with 100% specificity and sensitivity for each target H9 gene and lineage within the tested panel (Table 2). The H9-Probe detected all H9 lineages, whereas the B-Probe detected B4.6 lineage H9 viruses, and the Y-Probe detected Y3, Y8 and Y9 lineage H9 viruses. Additionally, six G (G3, G4, G5.3.2, G5.5, G5.6, and G5.7) lineage H9 genes were also detected with the H9-Probe but not with the Y-Probe or B-Probe although they were synthesized in only the HA2 region, including the target site of the multiplex H9 rRT‒PCR assay. These results indicate that despite the use of only a single set of forward and reverse primers, the primers and three probes were designed to achieve high sensitivity and specificity for their target genes, enabling the H9-Probe to detect all H9 lineages, whereas the Y-Probe and B-Probe reacted exclusively with the Y lineage and B lineage, respectively.

For the analytical sensitivity test, five different H9 lineage viruses isolated in Korea were selected: a B4.6 lineage (C479) currently circulating in poultry, two Y9 lineages (01310 isolated in 2001 and LBM294 in 2018) that circulated in poultry until 2018, and Y3 (19H019) and Y8 (21H225) lineages isolated in wild birds. The multiplex H9 rRT‒PCR assay detected C479 (B4.6), LBM294 (2018 Y9) and 21H225 (Y8) up to 10 EID50/0.1 ml and 01310 (2001 Y9) and 19H019 (Y3) up to 102 EID50/0.1 ml about the H9-Probe, which were comparable with the LoD of the commercial M/H9 rRT‒PCR assay for H9 (Fig. 2). The LoD of the Y-Probe for Y lineage viruses was identical to that of the H9-Probe; however, for the B lineage virus C479, the LoD of the B-Probe was determined to be 102 EID50/0.1 ml, representing a tenfold lower sensitivity than H9-Probe, with detection observed in only one of three replicates of each three independent experiments. The correlation coefficient (R2) was greater than 0.99 across all the targets amplified simultaneously, indicating excellent linearity across the dilution range and confirming the highly consistent amplification efficiency and reliable quantitative performance of this multiplex H9 rRT‒PCR assay. For most viruses, the PCR efficiency (E) was approximately 80% (76.01%–83.29%), with an amplification factor (AF) of approximately 1.8 (1.76–1.83) for all the targets except for C479, which had a higher E of 92% and AF of 1.9 in both targets. This level of efficiency is generally considered acceptable for reliable detection and comparative quantification, as multiplex systems are accompanied by competition among target genes, suggesting the moderate amplification performance of this assay.

Detection and differentiation of the Y and B lineage H9 genes in field samples using multiplex H9 rRT‒PCR

Multiplex H9 rRT–PCR identified H9 genes and differentiated Y and B lineages in 27 of the 28 field samples in which H9 was previously detected (Table 3). The single sample that was not detected by multiplex H9 rRT‒PCR had a Ct value of 39.3 for the H9 gene when tested with the commercial M/H9 rRT‒PCR kit, while the M gene was not detected. Field samples collected from the LBM of Korea in 2022 and 2025 were the B4.6 lineage, which is the only H9 lineage in poultry detected in Korea after 2020, and they were detected with both the H9-Probe and B-Probe but not with the Y-Probe. However, the Ct values for the H9-Probe and B-Probe differed according to the sample collection year. While samples collected in 2022 were detected much more sensitively by the B-Probe than the H9-Probe, samples collected in 2025 were much more sensitively detected by the H9-Probe. Notably, the samples collected in 2025 also showed delayed or failed H9 detection when tested using a commercial M/H9 rRT‒PCR kit (Table 3). The poultry samples collected in 2018 and the wild bird-derived samples were Y lineages detected with both the H9-Probe and Y-Probe, and the Ct values of the two target genes were similar.

Evaluation of multiplex H9 rRT‒PCR for the detection and differentiation of samples containing two mixed lineages

The performance of the multiplex rRT‒PCR assay was evaluated using samples containing mixtures of Y8 (21H225), Y9 (LBM294), and B4.6 (C479) lineage viruses to assess whether both H9 lineages could be simultaneously detected in mixed infection cases. The detection of target genes was not affected when both mixed viruses were present at the same concentration, but the detection of viruses at lower concentrations was delayed because of competitive inhibition (Table 4). This tendency was more pronounced with the B-Probe, as a delay in detection was observed when C479 (B4.6) was present at a tenfold lower concentration than LBM294 (2018 Y9) or 21H225 (Y8). In contrast, LBM294 (Y9) showed delayed detection only when mixed with C479 (B4.6) at a 1,000-fold concentration difference, and 21H225 (Y8) exhibited delayed detection at a 100-fold concentration difference.

For the H9-Probe, the Ct values converged to those of the virus present at higher concentrations regardless of the difference in concentration between the target gene, which was the same for all H9 viruses (Table 4). When the viruses were mixed at equal concentrations, earlier H9 gene detection by H9-Probe was expected because of an increased total amount of H9 gene; however, the Ct values were comparable to those obtained with each virus alone at the same concentration.

Discussion

In Korea, the Y9 lineage endemically circulated in poultry from 2000 to 2018, and the B4.6 lineage has persisted since 2020, causing economic losses in the poultry industry. Moreover, the Y3 and Y8 lineages have been continuously detected in wild birds, indicating the potential for transmission to poultry, and G lineage viruses that are endemic in multiple countries across Asia, Africa, and the Middle East could also be introduced through human activity. Therefore, continuous monitoring of the lineage of H9 viruses is necessary, and a multiplex H9 rRT‒PCR assay capable of not only detecting H9 but also simultaneously differentiating lineages was developed and evaluated for rapid screening tests in large-scale surveillance programs.

Compared with previously reported H9 rRT-PCR assays, which primarily focus on H9 subtype detection using a probe for universal H9 gene detection, the present multiplex assay additionally introduces lineage discrimination capability through the use of lineage-specific hydrolysis probes targeting conserved nucleotide signatures unique to the Y and B lineages (Fig. 1 and Table 1) (Saito et al., 2019; Panzarin et al., 2022; Sagong et al., 2023). Furthermore, unlike other previously reported multiplex rRT-PCR assays requiring employing independent primer and probe sets for each target gene, this assay utilizes a single common primer sets combined with three different probes in same target region (Fig. 1 and Table 1) (Monne et al., 2008; Liu et al., 2018). This strategy minimizes assay complexity, reduce optimization burden, and lowers diagnostic cost. Designed primers and probes could simultaneously detect all H9 lineages and differentiate the Y and B lineages of H9 AIVs, but the Y and B lineages isolated in Korea were primarily considered because the G lineage has not been detected in Korea. Nevertheless, the common H9 forward primers (H9-F-1 and 2), reverse primer (H9-R) and probe (H9-Probe) exhibited high sequence matching rates across the B, Y, and G lineages (Table 1). Additionally, the Y-Probe showed a high matching rate exclusively in the Y lineage but not in the B and G lineages, whereas the B-Probe did not match the Y lineage but exhibited approximately 25% sequence matching with the G lineage in the in silico analysis (Table 1). However, when the synthesized G lineage H9 genes belonging to the G3, G4, G5.3.2, G5.5, G5.6, and G5.7 lineages were experimentally tested, only the H9-Probe detected G lineage H9 genes and neither the fluorescence of the Y-Probe nor that of the B-Probe was detected (Table 2). This finding indirectly suggested that discrimination of the G lineage may also be feasible with this multiplex H9 rRT‒PCR assay and that viruses detected only with the H9-Probe but not with the B- or Y-Probe during surveillance may represent other lineages, such as the G lineage, indicating the need for further confirmatory analysis.

This assay showed no cross-reactivity in H9 gene detection and lineage differentiation with other subtype of AIV originated from various species, suggesting high analytical specificity across diverse host-derived virus pools comparable with other previously reported H9 rRT-PCR assays (Table 2) (Saito et al., 2019; Panzarin et al., 2022). Robustness of the assay was supported by consistent performance across diverse sample types, including reference strains, clinical field samples (Oropharyngeal and cloacal swab, and feces), and experimentally mixed-virus samples representing varying viral loads (Table 2, Table 3, Table 4). Considering that AIV surveillance involves samples collected from multiple avian species and environmental matrices, such specificity is essential to minimize false-positive lineage assignment. These characteristics demonstrate that the developed multiplex rRT-PCR assay is suitable for large-scale surveillance programs requiring rapid, reliable lineage-level information.

The diagnostic sensitivity of the multiplex H9 rRT‒PCR assay ranged from 10 to 102 EID50, which is comparable to that of the other multiplex rRT‒PCR assays, and differed according to the lineage and year of isolation like other H9 rRT-PCR assays showing different LoD in different lineage and strains (Fig. 2) (Monne et al., 2008; Saito et al., 2019; Sagong et al., 2023). Because sequences from viruses that were relatively recently and more prevalently isolated in Korea were preferentially reflected in primer and probe design, the B4.6 (C479), 2018 Y9 (LBM294) and Y8 (21H225) lineages presented 10-fold lower LoDs than the other H9 lineages did (2001 Y9 and Y3) (Fig. 2). Additionally, the slopes of the standard curve of each H9 virus except C479 were approximately −3.8, with a PCR efficiency of approximately 80%, which was caused by competitive inhibition of the primer and PCR components because this is multiplex assay and EID50 was used at concentrations that were not exactly the same as the RNA copy numbers (Mathieu-Daudé et al., 1996; Lee and Suarez, 2004). However, the LoD and performance for field samples were comparable to those of commercial M/H9 rRT‒PCR kits; thus, the detection of the H9 gene and differentiation of the H9 lineage can be reliably achieved (Fig. 2 and Table 3).

Field samples collected in 2025 showed delays in detection with both the multiplex H9 rRT‒PCR assay in this study and the commercial M/H9 rRT‒PCR kit, as B4.6 lineage viruses have continuously evolved through genetic mutation while circulating on poultry farms in Korea (Table 3) (Cho et al., 2025). Indeed, in an in silico analysis of H9 sequences isolated from poultry farms in Korea between 2024 and 2025, a primer/probe binding analysis of the B-Probe revealed no mismatches in viruses isolated up to 2023, whereas viruses isolated during 2024–2025 showed a single mismatch at the fifth nucleotide position from the 5′ end of the B-Probe. Although direct sequencing of the clinical samples used in this study was not feasible due to low viral RNA concentrations, H9N2 viruses isolated from field samples obtained from national surveillance program during 2024–2025 in Korea consistently possessed identical mismatch within the B-probe binding region. Therefore, it is reasonable to infer that the reduced sensitivity of the B-Probe observed in the clinical samples analyzed in this study may be associated with similar mutations present in circulating field strains. Additionally, the C479 lineage with delayed detection by the B-Probe in the specificity test and LoD test also had a single-nucleotide mismatch at the fifth nucleotide (Table 2 and Fig. 2). Although it is located at the 5′ end, this mismatch may contribute to the reduced probe binding efficiency and delayed detection observed in recent isolates. At the time of probe design, only sequences isolated up to 2023 in Korea were available in the GISAID database; therefore, genetic variants that primarily accumulated after 2024 could not be detected. Given the inevitable accumulation of mutations in RNA viruses such as avian influenza virus, ongoing surveillance is essential to continuously evaluate and update primer and probe performance. For the same reason, the detection of the C479 (B4.6) virus was delayed even at concentrations only 10-fold lower than those of the Y8 and Y9 lineage viruses, whereas Y lineage viruses were detected more consistently, with no delay even when the Y lineage was present at concentrations up to 100-fold (Y8) and 1,000-fold (Y3) lower than that of C479 (B4.6) (Table 4). Thus, compared with other Y lineage viruses, C479 (B4.6) relatively lower PCR efficiency because of a single mismatch in the B-probe, which resulted in competitive inhibition and exhaustion of the restricted PCR components by the template present at a higher concentration (Mathieu-Daudé et al., 1996; Liu et al., 2018; An et al., 2024). Additionally, the signal for the H9-Probe was dominated by the template present at a higher concentration, as the template of the H9-Probe was the same for all lineages of the H9 gene, and consequently, the Ct values of the H9-Probe reflected the higher-abundance virus rather than the combined target copy number. Collectively, these findings suggest that the H9-Probe has no lineage bias and can stably detect the dominant strain in mixed infections, supporting its use as a pan-H9 screening probe.

In conclusion, the multiplex H9 rRT‒PCR assay developed in this study enables the simultaneous detection of all H9 lineages and differentiation between the B and Y lineages, with high diagnostic specificity and sensitivity comparable to those of commercial rRT‒PCR assays. Unlike other multiplex rRT‒PCR assays, the design of pan-H9, B and Y lineage-specific probes with high specificity and sensitivity within single primer sets for all H9 lineages can reduce reagent costs, simplify assay optimization, minimize amplification bias and ensure consistent amplification efficiency across targets. Although G1 lineage viruses could not be evaluated using live isolates, test results using the partial G1 lineage H9 gene suggested the potential discrimination capability of this assay. A limitation of this study is the relatively small number of field samples (n = 28) included for clinical validation. While the assay successfully detected and differentiated H9 lineages in tested samples, expanded evaluation using a larger surveillance dataset representing multiple host species and epidemiological situations will be required to further confirm diagnostic sensitivity and specificity under field conditions. Additionally, the reduced detection efficiency observed in some recent B4.6 lineage isolates highlights the ongoing genetic evolution of H9 avian influenza viruses and underscores the need for continuous sequence surveillance and periodic updates of primer and probe sets. Overall, this assay provides a practical and robust tool for enhanced surveillance of H9 AIVs in poultry and wild birds not only in Korea but also in various countries where H9 AIVs cause economic losses to the poultry industry.
