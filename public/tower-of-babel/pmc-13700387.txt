Plasmablastic non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma: a case report and review of the literature

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPlasmablastic lymphoma (PBL) is an unusual and aggressive form of non-Hodgkin lymphoma that most frequently occurs in immunosuppressed patients, especially those suffering from HIV infection. The association between PBL and the oral cavity is very rare, and the clinical features may mimic odontogenic and epithelial tumors, which causes a delayed diagnosis. Here, we describe an extremely rare case of localized PBL involving the anterior mandible in an HIV-infected patient clinically resembling carcinoma of the lower anterior alveolus.Case presentationA 43-year-old man, HIV positive, Indian origin, with progressive enlargement of painful swelling over chin area for three months was seen. Examination showed a hard swelling with hypopigmented skin over the lower anterior chin area. Intraorally, there was presence of a nodular, erythematous, pebble like appearance of the mucosa in the edentulous anterior mandibular region, without any ulcerations. No cervical lymphadenopathy could be palpated. Clinically, the provisional diagnosis was made as carcinoma of the lower anterior alveolus, with lymphoma and Kaposi sarcoma being the differentials. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography scan showed a destructive mass with destruction of the anterior mandible and involvement of the surrounding soft tissue areas. Histopathology showed diffuse sheets of plasmablast-like cells. Immunohistochemistry showed MUM-1 and CD45 positivity and CD20 and PAX-5 negativity and high ki67 proliferation index (80–85%). Thus, the diagnosis of plasmablastic lymphoma was confirmed. The staging showed Stage IE extranodal disease without any significant lymphadenopathy and systemic symptoms. The patient was put on antiretroviral and systemic chemotherapeutic treatment.ConclusionsThis case demonstrates the difficulty in making a diagnosis for plasmablastic lymphoma mimicking a localized mandibular mass, similar to an oral squamous cell carcinoma. The differential diagnosis must include lymphoma in cases where there is an unusual mandibular swelling, especially when there is an underlying immunocompromised status. Imaging, histopathologic, and immunohistologic studies are crucial in making an early diagnosis and appropriate management of the disease. Background

Plasmablastic lymphoma (PBL) is a rare, highly aggressive subtype of diffuse large B-cell lymphoma that was first described by Delecluseet al. in 1997 as an oral cavity-associated lymphoma in patients with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection. Although initially recognized as an HIV-associated malignancy, subsequent reports have documented its occurrence in HIV-negative and other immunocompromised individuals. PBL accounts for a small proportion of all non-Hodgkin lymphomas but is associated with rapid disease progression, a high proliferative index, and poor overall survival. Histologically, it is characterized by plasmablastic morphology with loss of conventional B-cell markers such as CD20 and PAX5 and expression of plasma cell-associated markers including CD138, CD38, and MUM1, making immunohistochemistry indispensable for establishing the diagnosis and differentiating it from plasmablastic myeloma and other high-grade lymphomas. The oral cavity is the most frequently affected extranodal site, particularly the gingiva, palate, and jaws; however, primary involvement of the anterior mandible is distinctly uncommon. Because the clinical and radiographic features often resemble oral squamous cell carcinoma, odontogenic malignancies, chronic osteomyelitis, or severe periodontal disease, diagnosis is frequently delayed until histopathological and immunohistochemical evaluation is performed. Current treatment generally consists of intensive multi-agent chemotherapy, with highly active antiretroviral therapy serving as an essential adjunct in HIV-positive patients, although the prognosis remains guarded owing to the tumor's aggressive biological behavior and high risk of systemic dissemination. Reports of localized anterior mandibular PBL presenting as a carcinoma-like lesion are limited, highlighting the need for increased awareness among oral and maxillofacial surgeons, oral pathologists, and oncologists. The aim of this case report is to describe a rare case of Stage IE plasmablastic lymphoma involving the anterior mandible in an HIV-positive patient that clinically mimicked carcinoma of the lower anterior alveolus, emphasizing the diagnostic challenges, the pivotal role of histopathological and immunohistochemical evaluation in achieving an accurate diagnosis, and the importance of early multidisciplinary management to facilitate prompt treatment and improve clinical outcomes.

The was patient recently diagnosed with HIV-positive and was started on regular antiretroviral therapy (ART). He denied any history of fever or weight loss.

Discussion

Extranodal non-Hodgkin’s lymphomas (NHLs) are lymphoid malignancies that arise outside the lymph nodes and account for about 20–30% of all NHLs. Among extranodal sites, involvement of the head and neck region is relatively rare, although it is the second most common site after the gastrointestinal tract [1].

Primary lymphomas in the head and neck area account for 2.2% of all head neck cancers and 3.5% of intraoral cancers, making them the second or third most prevalent malignancies behind squamous cell carcinomas and salivary gland tumors. Three main types of lymphoid malignancies are recognized by the World Health Organization’s (WHO's) revision of the Revised EuropeanAmerican Lymphoma Classification: Hodgkin's lymphoma, T-cell/NK cell lymphoma, and B-cell lymphoma [2].

Plasmablastic lymphoma (PBL) is an uncommon but aggressive subtype of diffuse, large Bcell lymphoma. Despite significant advances in the field of lymphomas since this subtype was first described in a case series by Delecluse et al in 1997, PBL continues to pose challenges with diagnosis and management. [3].

It is classically seen in association with HIV infection. The primary organs involved are the gastrointestinal system, lymph nodes, oral mucosa, and sometimes the skin. PBL is diagnosed primarily through histologic evaluation with or without genetics and immunophenotyping. However, diagnosing PBL is difficult due to overlapping features with plasmablastic myeloma and other high-grade lymphomas with plasmablastic morphology [4].

Immunophenotyping can aid in diagnosing PBL as staining can help differentiate this condition from other plasma cell neoplasms. The immunophenotype for plasmablastic lymphoma is also similar to that of plasma cell neoplasms with positive immunohistochemical (IHC) staining for CD79a, IRF-4/MUM-1, CD38, and CD138. Unlike other B-cell lymphomas, PBL is usually negative for CD19, CD20, and PAX-5 but may be weakly positive for CD45 [5].

The Ki-67 proliferation index is invariably high and often exceeds 90% [6].

Immunohistochemistry showed differences between HIV positive and negative patients, the former had significantly higher CD20 and CD56 expression than the latter [7].

The high association of PBL with immunosuppression, oral cavity involvement, male gender, Epstein Barr Virus (EBV) infection, and aggressive clinical behavior has been traditionally stressed in the literature [8].

Clinical mimicry of periodontal or infectious conditions

Patients with underlying neoplastic conditions affecting the oral cavity may initially present with signs and symptoms that closely mimic common periodontal or infectious diseases. Tooth mobility, localized pain, and swelling are frequent complaints that can be indistinguishable from advanced periodontitis or dental abscesses. Gingival enlargement or ulcerated lesions may resemble inflammatory hyperplasia or necrotizing ulcerative gingivitis, particularly in immunocompromised individuals such as those with HIV. Radiographic evaluation often reveals bone loss or osteolytic lesions, findings that can be misinterpreted as osteomyelitis or periodontal bone destruction. A crucial clinical indicator is the lack of response to conventional periodontal therapy or antibiotics; this should prompt clinicians to consider alternative diagnoses, including malignancies. In some cases, systemic symptoms like fever, unexplained weight loss, and generalized malaise accompany local oral findings, further complicating the clinical picture and potentially leading to an initial misdiagnosis of systemic infection rather than a neoplastic process [9].

This is a case of Stage IE primary extranodal plasmablastic lymphoma (PBL) involving the anterior mandible in an HIV-positive individual, clinically mimicking carcinoma of the lower anterior alveolus. The diagnosis was established based on imaging, histopathology, and immunohistochemistry findings.

Antiretroviral therapy (ART)

For HIV-positive patients, maintaining or initiating effective ART is essential to support treatment efficacy and immune recovery—consistent with NCCN policy for HIV-related lymphomas.

Localized disease management

For Stage I–II disease, involved-site radiotherapy (ISRT) may be employed after chemo to improve local control [10].

Conclusion

Plasmablastic lymphoma of the oral cavity is a rare but aggressive malignancy that can clinically mimic carcinoma or periodontal pathology. This case underscores the importance of considering lymphoma in the differential diagnosis of persistent oral swellings, particularly in immunocompromised patients. A multidisciplinary approach incorporating imaging, histopathology, immunohistochemistry, systemic chemotherapy, and continuation of antiretroviral therapy is essential for accurate diagnosis and effective management. Early detection and tailored treatment are vital to improving prognosis in this challenging disease entity.
