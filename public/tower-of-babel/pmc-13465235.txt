Risk factors and clinical significance of functional outcomes after microfracture-based surgical treatment of osteochondral lesion of the talus
Authors: Shengxuan Cao, Jinyang Lyu, Ce Han, Xiang Geng, Xin Ma, Chao Zhang, Xu Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe aims of the current study are to identify risk factors after microfracture-based surgical treatment of osteochondral lesion of the talus (OLT), and to determine the minimal clinically important difference (MCID), substantial clinical benefit (SCB), and patient acceptable symptomatic state (PASS) values for clinical outcomes.MethodsThe relationship between the superior and inferior outcome (Karlsson score < 90) groups in the bivariate analysis was tested with the independent t-test or Fisher exact test. A multivariate logistic regression model and a multiple linear regression model were developed to investigate the influence of the selected factors on inferior outcome. The MCID, SCB, and PASS for the American Orthopedic Foot and Ankle Society (AOFAS) score, visual analogue scale for pain (VAS score), and Karlsson scores were derived through anchor-base methods.ResultsOne hundred and thirty-seven patients were followed up for 49.9 ± 17.2 months. Longer symptom duration (adjusted odds ratio, 1.015; 95% Confidential Interval (CI), 1.002 to 1.027; p = 0.020), and higher fasting blood glucose (adjusted odds ratio, 1.555; 95% CI, 1.066 to 2.270; p = 0.022) were independent predictors of inferior outcomes. Male gender (adjusted odds ratio, 0.317; 95% CI, 0.121 to 0.833; p = 0.020) and higher preoperative AOFAS score (adjusted odds ratio, 0.937; 95% CI, 0.887 to 0.989; p = 0.018) were independent predictors of superior outcomes. The MCID, SCB, and PASS values for the AOFAS score were 6.5, 10.5, and 85.5; for VAS score were 3.5, and 3.5 (SCB and PASS); and for Karlsson score were 15.5, 25.5, and 80.5, respectively.ConclusionHigher fasting blood glucose and lower preoperative functional scores are associated with inferior outcomes after microfracture-based surgical treatment for OLT. Reliable SCB and PASS values for AOFAS, VAS and Karlsson scores were obtained from patient evaluations after microfracture-based surgical treatment of OLT.Level of evidence III Retrospective comparative series.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13018-026-07016-7.

Introduction

Osteochondral lesion of the talus (OLT) is a common reason for ankle pain and decreased ankle function, especially in the context of chronic ankle instability (CAI) [1–5]. Several treatment methods have been advocated for the management of OLT [6–11], among which microfracture is widely accepted for primary OLT [12]. However, the failure rate of microfracture for primary OLT is 18% according to a systemic review [13]. Clinical outcomes after microfracture may degrade over time, and a substantial proportion of patients report persistent symptoms or require reoperation during longer follow-up. The pooled clinical success rate was further decreased to 61% in patients with nonprimary OLT treated with arthroscopic microfracture, and the rate of return to pre-injury level of sport was 55% [14].

Many studies identified several risk factors for inferior outcome after surgically treated OLT. Lesion size is negatively correlated with functional outcome [15, 16]. The ideal size for use of bone marrow stimulation are lesions < 10 mm in diameter and < 5 mm in depth, and some authors have proposed lesion area over 150 mm² or diameter over 15 mm as contradictions to bone marrow stimulation treatment [17, 18]. The presence of grade 1 osteoarthritis on the Van Dijk classification and presence of osteophytes negatively affect outcome [19, 20]. Higher preoperative range of motion is predictive of good results in the surgical management of OLT [19]. Higher age, female gender and higher body mass index (BMI) were also reported to be risk factors in previous studies [18, 20, 21], but controversial results were present in other studies [22–24]. Of postoperative factors, serial platelet rich plasma injections and reduction in BMI from baseline are correlated with better 5-year American Orthopedic Foot and Ankle Society (AOFAS) ankle-hindfoot scale [24]. Subchondral bone marrow edema volume change correlated with clinical outcomes after arthroscopic microfracture for OLT [23]. However, many identified risk factors, such as age, gender, lesion size and the presence of osteoarthritis are unable to intervene preoperatively, and thus less meaningful for guiding preoperative intervention. The effects of comorbidities such as hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and hyperuricemia are seldom reported in previous studies.

Minimal clinically important difference (MCID), substantial clinical benefit (SCB), and patient acceptable symptomatic state (PASS) are emerging criteria for patient-based treatment assessments. However, few studies have investigated these measures after microfracture of OLT [25–27]. A systemic review highlighted the importance of MCID in evaluate surgical outcomes of OLT [28], but referred to values acquired from a hallux valgus study [29].

The aims of the current study are to identify the influence of pre- and intra-operative risk factors on surgical outcomes of OLT, and to determine the MCID, SCB, and PASS values for AOFAS score, visual analogue scale for pain (VAS score) and Karlsson score after microfracture-based surgical treatment for OLT. We hypothesize that chronic comorbidities are risk factors of inferior outcomes after microfracture for OLT.

Methods

Participants

This study is approved by our institutional review board. All patients underwent arthroscopic microfracture surgery for treating primary OLT in our department during Jan 2017 and Jan 2023 were included in the current study. The inclusion criteria for patients with OLT are as follows: (1) ankle pain or restriction of ankle motion during activities of daily living; (2) evidence of OLT with a diameter of less than 15 mm and a lesion area of less than 150 mm² based on the MRI; (3) failure of at least 3 months of conservative treatment and scheduled for arthroscopic microfracture; and (4) willing to participant in the current study. Individuals were excluded based on the following exclusion criteria: (1) history of fracture requiring realignment or musculoskeletal surgery in either lower extremity; (2) syndesmotic diastasis over 4 mm; (3) general joint hypermobility (Beighton score of at least 4); and (4) neuromuscular diseases, or other conditions that could affect activities of daily living.

One hundred and fifty-two individuals with OLT underwent arthroscopic microfracture surgery from Jan 2017 and Jan 2023, of whom nine patients were lost to follow-up, three patients were excluded because of general joint hypermobility, two patient was excluded because of surgical history of ankle, and one patient was excluded because of impaired muscle strength. One hundred and thirty-seven patients were included in the final analysis.

Surgical procedure and postoperative rehabilitation

OLT, Anterior talofibular ligament (ATFL) injury [30], syndesmotic widening (over 2 mm assessed using a ball-tipped probe) [31], soft tissue impingement, synovitis, osteophytes, loose body, and ossicles were identified under arthroscopy [32]. In patients with soft tissue impingement, synovitis, or osteophytes presents, these lesions were released, debrided or removed using thermal radiofrequency, shaver or osteotome. Loose bodies were removed and ossicles at the tip of the fibular were excised while care was taken not to injure the ligament. The OLT was arthroscopically debrided to create a stable base and was measured using the end of a probe with a known length for reference. A curette was used to remove a thin layer of subchondral bone, and microfracture was performed utilizing 1.8-mm-diameter tapered arthroscopic microfracture picks to a depth of 3 mm with each pass and spaced 3 to 4 mm apart until subchondral bone bleeding was visualized [33]. Ninety of all 137 patients with concomitant ATFL injury were treated with modified Broström procedure. If the OLT was in hard-to-reach region under arthroscopy even under distraction, medial malleolar osteotomy was performed to fully expose the lesion, and the osteotomy fragment was reduced and fixed with two 3.5 mm cannulated screw after microfracture. Seven patients with peek-a-boo heel and a positive Coleman block test were treated with lateral sliding or lateral closing wedge calcaneal osteotomy.

After surgical management, patients were immobilized in a short-leg plaster cast for 3 weeks, which inhibited postoperative weightbearing. At 4–6 weeks postoperatively, patients began non-weightbearing ankle exercises. Six weeks postoperatively, the patients initiated partial to full weightbearing in walking boots. Walking, jogging, and formal physical therapies, which incorporates heel rise and wobble board exercises, were initiated when patients showed capability to tolerate them.

Clinical analysis

Preoperatively, baseline medical information was carefully recorded. Generalized joint hypermobility was assessed as Beighton Score over 4 of a 9-point scale [34]. Manual anterior drawer test was conducted as Lee et al. [35] described where grades II and III were regard as manual ankle laxity.

All patients were followed for a minimum of 2 years. The AOFAS score, VAS score, Karlsson score, and Ankle Activity Score were acquired by two independent orthopedic surgeons preoperatively and at final follow-up. Scores of the two surgeons were cross-validated, and discrepancies were mediated by a third senior surgeon. The Karlsson scores at final follow-up were graded according to the criteria of Okuda et al. [36] (good, 90–100; fair, 75–89; poor, < 75). We considered good (more than 90 points) as superior outcome and fair and poor (less than 90) as inferior outcome.

The MCID, SCB, and PASS for the AOFAS score, VAS score, and Karlsson score were derived through anchor-based methods. Patients rated their symptomatic change using a 4-point Likert scale (none, poor, good, excellent), reflecting their perceived postoperative improvement. The Spearman correlation coefficients between the anchor question and the change in each score were calculated, all of which exceeded 0.6, confirming the validity of our anchor (Supplementary Table S1). An additional anchor question, if the patient was satisfied with the surgery given the current pain level, function, and daily life, was used to derive the PASS. Those who answered yes were classified as satisfied, and those who answered no were classified as unsatisfied.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed utilizing SPSS software (version 26.0, SPSS Inc., USA) and MedCalc software (version 23, MedCalc Software Ltd., Belgium).

The unchanged group included patients who answered none or poor on the anchor question. The changed group included patients who answered good, and the improved group included patients who answered excellent. The MCID, SCB, and PASS were derived using the sensitivity- and specificity-based and the between patients change approaches. We plotted receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves for each outcome score. In the sensitivity- and specificity-based analysis, the MCID was defined as the optimal cut-off point on the ROC curve differentiating the “unchanged” and “changed” groups. Similarly, the SCB was determined as the optimal cut-off point between the “unchanged” and “improved” groups, and the PASS as the optimal cut-off point between the “unsatisfied” and “satisfied” groups. The discriminative ability of the ROC curves was assessed by the area under the curve (AUC), with values of ≥ 0.7 and ≥ 0.8 indicating acceptable and excellent discrimination, respectively. The Ankle Activity Score demonstrated sub-acceptable discriminant ability (AUC = 0.573 for MCID). Consequently, we have removed all related MCID, SCB, and PASS results for this score. In the between-patients change analysis, we calculated the MCID and SCB as the mean difference in score improvement between the “changed” and “unchanged” groups, and between the “improved” and “unchanged” groups, respectively. The PASS threshold was established as the mean postoperative score of the patients who considered themselves “satisfied”. Cutoff values of the sensitivity- and specificity-based approach were mainly used to determine the final MCID, SCB, and PASS values, although the clinical implications of the values that were derived from the between-patient change were also fully considered.

The relationship between the superior and inferior outcome groups in the bivariate analysis was tested with the independent t-test for the continuous variables or the Pearson chi-squared tests (for categorical variables with expected cell counts ≥ 5) and Fisher exact tests (for categorical variables with expected cell counts < 5) for dichotomous variables. A multivariate logistic regression model was developed to investigate the influence of the selected factors on inferior outcome. Independent variables were eligible for entry into the model if they were significantly (p < 0.05) associated with inferior outcome in the bivariate analysis.

We re-fitted our primary analysis using a multiple linear regression model with the final Karlsson score as a continuous dependent variable. The model was adjusted for the following a-priori confounders: baseline Karlsson score, age, gender, BMI, Hepple grade, fasting blood glucose, lesion location (zone 4 vs. other), gout symptom, symptom duration and concomitant procedures (modified Broström procedure, medial malleolar osteotomy, and calcaneal osteotomy). The variance inflation factors for all predictors were below 5, indicating no concerning multicollinearity. Residual plots showed no strong patterns, supporting the linearity assumption. The adjusted R² of the model was 0.217.

Demographics and outcomes of interested and control groups were analyzed using independent t test for the continuous variables or the Pearson chi-squared tests for dichotomous variables. Preoperative and postoperative patient-reported outcome were analyzed using paired t-test. Values were presented as mean ± standard deviation. The differences were regarded as statistically significant when p < 0.05. The 95% confidential intervals (CIs) for odds ratios, regression coefficients, AUC, MCID, SCB, PASS, and their corresponding sensitivity and specificity values were reported.

Results

One hundred and thirty-seven patients were followed up for 49.9 ± 17.2 months, with a mean age of 43.9 ± 14.5 years, a mean symptom duration of 34.1 ± 43.8 months, and a mean BMI of 25.8 ± 4.0 kg/m2, of whom 89(65.0%) were men, 71(51.8%) had CAI, 88(64.2%) had sprain history, 7(5.1%) had subtle cavus deformity treated with osteotomy, and 30(21.9%) had osteochondral lesion of the tibia plafond. The Hepple grade of OLT in all patients were 5(3.6%) of grade II, 19(13.9%) of grade III, 62(45.3%) of grade IV, and 51(37.2%) of grade V. The location of OLT in all patients according to the nine grid scheme [37] were 3(2.2%) in zone 1, 3(2.2%) in zone 2, 5(3.6%) in zone 3, 101(73.7%) in zone 4, 4(2.9%) in zone 5, 12(8.8%) in zone 6, 6(4.4%) in zone 7, 1(0.7%) in zone 8, and 2((1.5%) in zone 9. The Pritsch grade modified by Takao et al. under arthroscopy of OLT in all patients were 3(2.2%) of grade 1, 5(3.6%) of grade 2, 41(29.9%) of grade 3, and 88(64.2%) of grade 4.

Preoperative and postoperative outcomes of the patients were listed in Table 1. AOFAS score were 63.2 ± 14.7 preoperatively and 85.5 ± 9.8 at final follow-up (p < 0.001). VAS score were 6.2 ± 1.7 preoperatively and 2.6 ± 1.7 at final follow-up (p < 0.001). Karlsson score were 55.0 ± 13.7 preoperatively and 83.6 ± 12.7 at final follow-up (p < 0.001). Ankle Activity Score were 2.9 ± 1.4 preoperatively and 3.6 ± 1.6 at final follow-up (p < 0.001). Analyzed using pre- and postoperative Ankle Activity Score, 125 (91.2%) participants returned to their pre-injury level of sport. Overall complication rate was 6.6%. 1(0.7%) had superficial peroneal nerve injury, 4(2.9%) had recurrence or enlargement of OLT and reoperation, 3(2.2%) had removal of internal fixation implants, and 1(0.7%) had infection around the incision and was treated with antibiotics and debridement.

Bivariate analysis of preoperative or intraoperative findings for superior and inferior outcome was listed in Table 3. There were significantly younger age (p = 0.024), more male patients (p = 0.015), shorter symptom duration (p = 0.007), less osteochondral lesion of the tibia (p = 0.030), less patients with gout symptom (p = 0.035), lower fasting blood glucose (p = 0.012), higher preoperative AOFAS score (p < 0.001), lower preoperative VAS score (p = 0.001), higher preoperative Karlsson score (p < 0.001), and higher preoperative Ankle Activity Score (p = 0.012) in superior outcome group. The 10 significant variables were entered into a multivariate logistic regression model, which revealed that longer symptom duration (adjusted odds ratio, 1.015; 95% CI, 1.002 to 1.027; p = 0.020), and higher fasting blood glucose (adjusted odds ratio, 1.555; 95% CI, 1.066 to 2.270; p = 0.022) were independent predictors of inferior outcomes (Table 4). Male gender (adjusted odds ratio, 0.317; 95% CI, 0.121 to 0.833; p = 0.020) and higher preoperative AOFAS score (adjusted odds ratio, 0.937; 95% CI, 0.887 to 0.989; p = 0.018) were independent predictors of superior outcomes. When Karlsson score more than 80.5 points was deemed superior outcome according to PASS value, gout symptom (adjusted odds ratio, 7.952; 95% CI, 1.292 to 48.952; p = 0.025), and higher fasting blood glucose (adjusted odds ratio, 1.537; 95% CI, 1.133 to 2.085; p = 0.006) were independent predictors of inferior outcomes (Supplementary Table S3). The multiple linear regression model showed that preoperative Karlsson score (unstandardized regression coefficients, 0.192; 95%CI, 0.038 to 0.346; p = 0.015, Table 5) and fasting blood glucose (unstandardized regression coefficients, − 2.469; 95%CI, − 3.837 to − 1.102; p = 0.001) significantly correlated with the Karlsson scores at final follow-up.

Discussion

The main findings are that higher fasting blood glucose and lower preoperative functional scores are associated with inferior outcomes after microfracture-based treatment of OLT; and the MCID, SCB, and PASS for the AOFAS score, VAS score, and Karlsson score were derived through anchor-based methods.

A previous study reported the outcomes of 30 OLT cases with average disease duration of 28.30 ± 21.25 months and reported that the prognosis of OLT is not related to duration of disease [38]. In the current study, we found that longer symptom duration is one of the risk factors of inferior outcomes after microfracture for OLT when following a larger sample size. Postinjury duration of 5 years or longer, older age, and male sex are risk factors for OLT presence in patients with CAI [39]. Also, the patients with OLT exhibit increased dorsiflexion and eversion moment, as well as anterior and medial ankle joint reaction force during stair descent [40]. Biomechanical maladaptation with a longer duration would result in impairments functionally and structurally, and thus affect the surgical outcomes.

Patients with OLT have increased reliance on bony stability, and decreased reliance on injured lateral ligaments, compared with those with CAI [41]. A previous study reported that the preoperative functional outcome score reflects the ankle stability [42], and thus lower preoperative functional outcome score would reflect ankle instability and bony impingement, which hindered OLT from recovery postoperatively.

Female gender was also reported to be one of the risk factors in a previous study, in which a Cox proportional hazard model revealed 1.6-times higher risk of graft failure for females [21]. A study on cartilage lesions in the knees revealed similarly high risk of graft failure for females [43]. In the current study, female gender was also identified as a risk factor. Differences in anatomy, hormone production, and neuromuscular patterns between female and male can contribute to disparities in surgical outcomes, but remain to be further investigated.

The predictive model in the current study considers comorbid conditions, and proposes higher fasting blood glucose as a new predictive factor for microfracture, which is missing in previous studies. Complicated diabetes increases the risk of surgical site infection after foot and ankle surgery [44]. A previous study demonstrated patients with poor short- and long-term glucose control experienced more complications when undergoing foot and ankle arthrodesis [45]. Molecularly in diabetic rat models, premature chondrocyte apoptosis and compensatory upregulation of chondroregulatory protein expression were identified, which helps explain how glucose control problem leads to impaired OLT recovery [46]. The results of the study can help screen patients with possibly poor surgical outcomes, thus facilitating preoperative intervention.

Another strength of the current study is to report the MCID, SCB, and PASS for the AOFAS score, VAS score, and Karlsson score. A previous systemic review highlighted the importance of MCID in evaluate surgical outcomes of OLT [28], but referred to values acquired from a hallux valgus study [29]. In the systemic review, the MCID for the AOFAS score and VAS score were between 17 and 30.2, and between 1.4 and 3, respectively [28]. In the current study, the MCID values for the AOFAS score, VAS score, and Karlsson score were 6.5, 0.5, and 15.5, respectively, however, the MCID values for the Ankle Activity Score were not reliable enough according to the ROC analysis. The MCID values for the AOFAS score and VAS score were lower than those of the previous study [28], which implicate more sensitivity of clinical change in patients with OLT. Some results close to or below the MCID in previous studies need to be further discussed. For the VAS score, the MCID derived from ROC analysis was 0.5. However, because our VAS was recorded as integer values from 0 to 10, we do not interpret this value as a valid MCID. Consistent with recent recommendations [47], we emphasize the PASS and SCB thresholds (both 3.5 for VAS) as more patient-centered and clinically meaningful outcome measures. In the current study, postoperative Karlsson score of impaired fasting glucose group were lower than the control groups. The mean postoperative AOFAS score of control and impaired fasting glucose groups were 86.3 and 82.8, respectively, which did not reach statistical significance. However, only the AOFAS score of control group reached the PASS value derived from the sensitivity- and specificity-based approach, which also supports the conclusion of the current study. The results in the current study can be references for future study on OLT.

There are several limitations of the current study. First, this study is of a relatively small sample size, especially for subgroup analyses, and the findings require validation in larger, more homogeneous cohorts using validated outcome measures. Second, we did not record exact lesion size or depth; although Hepple grade and Pritsch-Takao grade (which incorporate depth and subchondral involvement) were not significant in bivariate analysis, residual confounding cannot be excluded. Third, the linear regression model explained only 22% of the variance in postoperative Karlsson scores (adjusted R² = 0.217), indicating limited predictive capacity; the model should not be used as a strong individual prediction tool. Fourth, the AOFAS score is not a formally validated patient-reported outcome measure; future studies should consider PROMIS. Fifth, our primary outcome classification used a Karlsson score of 90 for comparability with the literature [36], whereas our derived PASS threshold was 80.5. A sensitivity analysis using the PASS cutoff confirmed that higher fasting blood glucose remained significant, but other predictors differed, indicating that the choice of cutoff affects the identification of some risk factors. Sixth, a substantial proportion of patients underwent concomitant procedures (modified Broström, osteotomies). Although adjusting for these did not alter the main finding regarding fasting blood glucose, the study is underpowered to attribute outcomes to microfracture alone. Our results should therefore be interpreted as risk factors after a combined surgical strategy commonly used for OLT with concomitant pathologies.

Conclusion

Higher fasting blood glucose and lower preoperative functional scores are associated with inferior outcomes after microfracture-based surgical treatment for OLT. Reliable SCB and PASS values for AOFAS, VAS and Karlsson scores were obtained from patient evaluations after microfracture-based surgical treatment of OLT.
