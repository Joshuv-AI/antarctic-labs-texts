Household livelihood vulnerability and child health in southwestern coastal Bangladesh: determinants and implications
Authors: Shahinur Akter, Aranya Siriphon, Arratee Ayuttacorn, Waraporn Boonchieng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHousehold livelihood vulnerability (HLV) remains a critical yet underexplored determinant of child health in climate-sensitive coastal regions of Bangladesh, where recurrent climate hazards disrupt livelihoods, reduce income stability, intensify food insecurity, and limit access to essential health services. Therefore, this study examines the extent and determinants of HLV and its implications for child health in southwestern coastal Bangladesh.MethodsFollowing the survey method, this community-based cross-sectional study was carried out in six villages of Dacope upazila in Khulna district of Bangladesh. Data were collected from 348 randomly selected caregivers of under-five children at the household level from July to October 2024.ResultsThe findings demonstrated that the prevalence of HLV was 58.6% in the study area. HLV showed significant spatial and occupational variation, with the highest prevalence observed in Sutarkhali village and among households primarily dependent on Sundarbans-based natural resource extraction. Multivariate logistic regression analysis revealed that higher odds of livelihood vulnerability were examined among Muslim households, households with multiple dependents, food insecure households, those that had recently borrowed money from friends or relatives, and those relying on local government assistance. Conversely, household heads with primary, secondary, or higher education, monthly savings of BDT ≤ 1,000, and households with multiple income earners had lower odds of livelihood vulnerability. Furthermore, child health analysis revealed a significant relationship between household vulnerability and child undernutrition, indicating that children from vulnerable households were more likely to be severely underweight than peers from non-vulnerable households.ConclusionThis study highlights the need for integrated policy responses that simultaneously address livelihood insecurity and child health particularly, strengthening climate-resilient and diversified livelihoods, expanding education, vocational training, and financial inclusion programs (e.g., savings schemes and microcredit), and the integration of vulnerability assessments into child-nutrition programs through targeted cash transfers, supplementary feeding, growth monitoring, and nutrition counseling in coastal Bangladesh.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27565-0.Keywords: Household livelihood vulnerability, Determinants, Child health implications, Southwestern coastal Bangladesh, Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), SDG 3 (Good Health and Well-being), SDG 10 (Reduced Inequalities)

Introduction

Disaster vulnerability remains a critical development challenge across many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) due to high exposure to climate-related hazards and limited adaptive capacity. Countries such as Philippines, Indonesia, India, Myanmar, Pakistan, Mozambique, and Somalia freqnently experience cyclones, floods, droughts, and other extreme climate events that disrupt livelihoods, damage infrastructure, and exacerbate socioeconomic ineqaulities [1]. These countries consistently rank among the most disaster-prone globally due to the combined effects of high hazard exposure, socioeconomic disparities, fragile infrastructure, and limited disaster preparedness, highlighting the concentration of disaster vulnerability in South and Southeast Asia and parts of Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) [1, 2]. In such settings, recurrent environmental shocks weaken household livelihood security by reducing income opportunities, intensifying food insecurity, and limiting access to essential health services, thereby increasing children’s vulnerability to communicable diseases, undernutrition, and impaired development. Consequently, disaster-induced livelihood instability has emerged as a critical pathway linking climate vulnerability to adverse child health outcomes, particularly in LMICs where adaptive capacity and social protection mechanisms remain limited [1–3].

Within the global landscape, Bangladesh ranks 13th among the world’s most disaster-prone nations and is considered the most vulnerable country in South Asia (SA) due to its low-lying geography and exposure to extreme climatic shocks [4]. Its southern coastal belt frequently experiences cyclones, tidal surges, salinity intrusion, floods, and riverbank erosion, which continuously disrupt the livelihoods of coastal communities and heighten household livelihood vulnerability (HLV) [5, 6]. Livelihood refers to the means by which people secure a living encompassing their capabilities, assets, and activities [7], whereas vulnerability reflects the physical, social, economic, and environmental conditions that heighten the exposure of individuals, households, assets, or systems to hazardous events [8]. Livelihood vulnerability therefore denotes the extent to which livelihoods are susceptible to, and unable to recover from adverse shocks and stresses such as climate change or market instability [9]. In coastal Bangladesh, this vulnerability is multidimensional and shaped by limited income opportunities, fragile natural resource dependence, weak social safety nets, and inadequate access to education, healthcare, safe water, and sanitation [10]. These interlocking factors erode household resilience and perpetuate cycles of poverty, illness and undernutrition, particularly among children under five years of age.

In addition to the fragile geophysical setting of the coastal belt of Bangladesh, entrenched sociopolitical constraints restrict access to resources and exacerbate existing vulnerabilities [11]. Coastal livelihoods remain predominantly agriculture-based [12], yet rising sea levels, salinity intrusion, and more frequent storm surges increasingly threaten agriculture-dependent livelihoods. As in many developing countries, the poorest households often inhabit the most hazard-prone areas, rely on climate-sensitive natural resources, and possess limited capacity for long-term adaptation [13]. Climate change and recurrent natural hazards severely disrupt both livelihoods and public health [14–16] by limiting access to freshwater, degrading infrastructure, and constraining the employment opportunities of coastal communities in Bangladesh. Natural hazards such as cyclones and floods further compromise sanitation and essential services, amplifying the risk of communicable diseases including diarrhea, cholera, and acute respiratory infection (ARI). particularly among children and the elderly [17–19]. Understanding the nexus between livelihood vulnerability and adverse health outcomes is therefore critical for designing effective adaptation policies and interventions to safeguard both livelihoods and public health in coastal Bangladesh.

Communicable diseases particularly, ARI, fever, and diarrhea remain major contributors to childhood morbidity and mortality in developing countries and are closely linked to nutritional deficiencies, weakened immunity, and impaired growth among children under five [20, 21]. Worldwide, 1.7 billion childhood diarrhea cases occur annually, resulting in 0.44 million deaths among children under five each year [22], while ARI accounts for 15% of global under-five mortality [23]. Undernutrition substantially amplifies this burden, accounting for nearly half of all under-five deaths and afffecting millions of children worldwide; in 2022, an estimated 149 million children under five were stunted, 45 million were wasted, and 390 million were underweight [24]. In Bangladesh, ARI, pneumonia, fever, and diarrhea, together with persistent childhood undernutrition, remain major contributors to illness, impaired growth, and mortality among under-five children [25, 26]. According to the 2022 Bangladesh Demographic and Health Survey (BDHS), 1.4% of under-five children experienced ARI, 31% had fever, and 5% confronted diarrhea [27], while the prevalence of stunting, wasting, and underweight was 24%, 11%, and 22%, respectively [27]. These statistics highlight the persistent dual burden of infectious diseases and undernutrition among under-five children in Bangladesh.

In vulnerability research, scholars have employed a variety of assessment frameworks across multiple spatial and thematic scales including the livelihood vulnerability index (LVI) [28–30], and household vulnerability to climate change models [31–34]. Notably, LVI is the most widely applied and cited approach, as it integrates a comprehensive set of socioeconomic, environmental, and climatic parameters [28–30]. Previous research in coastal Bangladesh identified key socioeconomic and environmental determinants of household vulnerability to cyclones, including geospatial location, livelihood type and diversity, work experience, household assets, land ownership, access to credit, and livelihood capital [35]. Households with low income, limited education, agriculture-dependent livelihoods, and high dependency ratios face heightened risk of vulnerability from cyclones or climate-induced hazards [36, 37], whereas access to social networks, diversified income, and local government services enhances resilience [37]. Moreover, climate-induced hazards such as cyclones, floods, and salinity intrusion both intensify and stem from livelihood vulnerability, reinforcing cycles of economic fragility in coastal communities [38–41].

Although few studies have linked household vulnerability to child health demonstrating increased risks of diarrheal disease and undernutrition among children from highly vulnerable households [20, 42, 43], these studies primarily focus on general socioeconomic or poverty-related vulnerability and do not explicitly examine HLV as a multidimensional construct or its pathways affecting child health outcomes. In Bangladesh, existing research has largely addressed separate dimensions of vulnerability such as social vulnerability [44], livelihood vulnerability due to climate-induced disasters [17, 37, 45], disaster vulnerability [35, 46, 47], and livelihood vulnerability in relation to public health, particularly adult people [16]. However, these studies rarely integrate livelihood vulnerability with child health outcomes within a single analytical framework, and none have specifically investigated the determinants of HLV and its direct implications for childhood morbidity and undernutrition in the climate-exposed southwestern coastal region of Bangladesh. This gap limits the understanding of how livelihood vulnerability translates into adverse child health outcomes in highly disaster-prone coastal settings. Employing a composite livelihood vulnerability index encompassing seven dimensions including sociodemographic profile, livelihood strategies, social networks, health, food, water and disaster exposure, this study provides an integrated assessment of HLV, its determinants and its impact on child health. By integrating livelihood vulnerability with child nutritional and disease outcomes, this research provides an evidence base for designing region-specific policies that foster diversified, climate-resilient livelihoods and safeguard child health and well-being in the coastal region of Bangladesh and other countries confronting comparable socioeconomic and environmental challenges.

Methods

Study area

This explanatory study was conducted in Dacope upazila (subdistrict) of Khulna district, Bangladesh (See Fig. 1) employing survey method. Khulna, one of the country’s 19 coastal districts, lies within the lower deltaic floodplain of the Ganges-Brahmaputra-Meghna river system and is located adjacent to the Sundarbans, the world’s largest mangrove forest, which strongly shapes the regions’ ecological conditions and livelihood patterns [48, 49]. The area is predominantly low-lying and highly exposed to climate-related hazards such as cyclones, tidal surges, flooding, salinity intrusion, and river erosion. Seasonal salinity and fragile embankment systems further constrain agriculture and access to safe drinking water [48, 50]. The district covers approximately 4,394 km² and has a population of 2.6 million across 6,70,861 households. Socioeconomic conditions remain challenging, with a substantial proportion of residents (46.7%) living in katcha houses, limited access to improved water (75.2% rely on tubewell water) and sanitation (56.1% have access to safe sanitation facilities) [51], and a high dependence on agriculture (85.32%) [52] and natural resource-based livelihoods, which are particularly vulnerable to climatic and environmental shocks.

Study area map of Dacope upazila in Khulna district of Bangladesh

Khulna district was selected as the study site because it typifies the southwestern coastal region of Bangladesh, where communities experience heightened vulnerabilities relative to other areas of the country including frequent natural hazards, salinity intrusion, pervasive poverty, natural resources and agriculture-based unstable economies, limited access to improved water and sanitation, inadequate healthcare infrastructure, and underdeveloped roads and communication systems [53–55]. Within this district, Dacope upazila was purposively chosen for its proximity to the Bay of Bengal and its high exposure to recurrent coastal hazards that shape local livelihoods, food security, and health conditions. The area exhibits a subtropical monsoon climate characterized by pronounced seasonal variation in temperature, rainfall, and humidity. Four distinct seasons are observed: pre-monsoon (hot and dry with occasional storms), monsoon (heavy rainfall and high humidity), post-monsoon (warn with sporadic cyclones), and winter (cool and dry) [56]. Rainfall is highly concentrated during the monsoon period, with monthly averages ranging from approximately 159 mm to 445 mm, reflecting strong monsoonal dominance [56, 57]. Owing to its coastal setting, Dacope remains highly vulnerable to extreme climatic events, including cyclones, storm surges, tidal flooding, and salinity intrusion, which substantially impact ecosystems, agriculture, and livelihoods [43, 57]. Overall, the study area typifies the climate-sensitive and hazard-prone nature of Bangladesh’s southwest coastal belt under increasing climate change pressures.

Moreover, the subdistrict covers about 991.6 km² and has a population of 1,59,369 across 42,186 households [51]. Dacope is predominantly rural (71.45%) and characterized by livelihoods that largely depend on natural resources, alongside considerable deficits in housing and basic infrastructure (77% of residents live in katcha houses), whereas access to safe drinking water remain precarious, with households relying mainly on rainwater (34.11%), surface water (31.55%), and tubewell (25.95%) [51]. Sanitation conditions remain inadequate, as only 35.4% of households use improved sanitation facilities, while 37.11% depend on pit latrines or pit latrines with slabs [51]. To capture variations in vulnerability within the study area, two unions namely Pankhali and Sutarkhali were purposively selected, and six villages (Pankhali, Hoglabunia, Katabunia, Sutarkhali, Nolian, and Kalabogi villages), three from each union, were included as study sites. This setting provides a representative context for examining HLV and its implications for child health in the hazard-prone coastal region of southwestern Bangladesh.

Participants and sampling

The participants were recruited on the basis of the following eligibility criteria: (i) caregivers (mothers, fathers or grandparents) of at least one child aged 6 − 59 months, and (ii) child had lived continuously with the caregivers in the study area for a minimum of five years. The residency requirement ensures a stable population, stronger community integration, and a deeper understanding of the health status of under-five children in terms of the prevalence of communicable diseases and undernutrition while enabling a more accurate assessment of livelihood vulnerability and its influence on child health outcomes.

A household census was conducted to gather essential information about the population in the study area, while strictly following the inclusion criteria of the participants. A population list was developed with a serial number and the census yielding a total population of 3,650 caregivers. On the basis of Cochran’s [58] formula, a sample of 348 caregivers was calculated with a 95% confidence level and a 5% margin of error. A disproportionate stratified random sampling design was applied to ensure balanced representation across the two unions, Sutarkhali (adjacent to the Bay of Bengal) and Pankhali (further inland) to capture potential coastal-inland differences in HLV. From this frame, 174 participants from each union and 58 participants per village were randomly selected from the population list, guaranteeing equal representation of the six study villages and minimizing selection bias.

Data collection

Data were collected following interview method using a semi-structured interview schedule designed to capture both household and child-level characteristics relevant to livelihood vulnerability and health outcomes (available at: 10.1186/s12889-025-24415-3). The interview schedule comprised multiple sections addressing sociodemographic and economic variables including residence, religion, education and occupation of household heads, monthly household income and savings, as well as the number of earning members and dependents to ensure robust contextualization of household dynamics. Furthermore, the tool assesses household assets, food insecurity and multidimensional livelihood vulnerability including sociodemographic profiles, livelihood strategies, social networks, health access, food and water security, and exposure to natural hazards. Child health information was also collected, with a focus on the prevalence of ARI, fever, and diarrhea, alongside anthropometric measurements (age, height, and weight) to evaluate nutritional status. This comprehensive design enables an integrated analysis of how coastal livelihood vulnerability shapes both the prevalence of childhood illnesses and undernutrition. The data collection tool underwent rigorous evaluation to ensure content validity. Three subject-matter experts reviewed the interview schedule and assessed the index of item-objective congruence (IOC) values, providing detailed feedback that informed subsequent revisions. Following institutional ethics approval, the first author, supported by trained enumerators, conducted face-to-face interviews between July and October 2024.

For caregivers with multiple under-five children, information was collected about the youngest child to capture a precise picture of the prevalence of communicable diseases, including ARI, fever and diarrhea. To assess children’s nutritional status, specifically, stunting, wasting, and underweight, standardized anthropometric procedures were employed. Children’s height was measured in centimeters using calibrated vertical stadiometers, ensuring proper alignment of the head, shoulders, buttocks, and heels, whereas weight was recorded in kilograms with digital scales to maintain measurement accuracy. The research adhered to the principles of the ‘Declaration of Helsinki’, with written informed consent obtained from all the participants. The respondents were assured of confidentiality and anonymity, and data collection was conducted at the household level using a replacement strategy to increase both the accuracy and reliability of the findings.

Variables

In this study, several groups of variables were included to comprehensively examine the relationship between HLV and child health in the southwestern coastal Bangladesh. Details about the assessments of different variables and categorization are presented in the Supplementary Table 1.

Sociodemographic and economic information

Sociodemographic and economic information was included to capture the background characteristics that shape HLV and child health in the southwestern coastal region of Bangladesh. These variables include education of the household head which was grouped as non-literate, primary, secondary, or higher education, as education is closely associated with livelihood opportunities, income generation, and health awareness. The occupation of the household head was categorized into farming, fishing or fish cultivation, business, day labor, natural resource collection from the Sundarbans, and other service-related activities (e.g. teaching, animal doctoring, driving) to reflect the dominant livelihood structure of southwestern coastal Bangladesh. This classification is grounded in the sustainable livelihood framework [59, 60], which emphasizes livelihood diversification as a key strategy for coping with ecological and economic uncertainty. In the climate-vulnerable coastal context characterized by salinity intrusion, cyclones, and seasonal employment fluctuations households commonly combine agriculture, fisheries, forest resource extraction, and wage labor to sustain their livelihoods. Empirical evidence from the region [20, 43, 61] similarly identifies these sectors as the principal livelihood strategies. Therefore, occupation was treated as a key analytical variable because it reflects the primary livelihood pathway through which households generate income, access resources, and manage environmental risks. Different occupational groups experience varying levels of exposure to environmental hazards, income instability, and resource dependency, which ultimately influence household adaptive capacity, livelihood vulnerability, and child health and nutritional outcomes.

In addition, economic variables were included to assess household financial capacity and stability. Monthly household income was classified as Bangladeshi Taka (BDT) ≤ 10,000, 10,001 − 20,000, 20,001 − 30,000, or ≥ 30,001, while monthly household savings were recorded as no savings, BDT ≤ 1,000, 1,001 − 5,000, and ≥ 5,001. These indicators were used to evaluate the financial resilience of households in managing daily needs and coping with shocks. Additional variables included the number of income earners in the household (1 and ≥ 2), and number of dependents (1, 2, and ≥ 3), borrowing money from friends or relatives in the previous month (no/yes), and seeking assistance from local government within the previous year (no/yes). These factors were incorporated because household economic pressure, debt reliance, and external support mechanism are important determinants of livelihood vulnerability and can directly or indirectly influence household food security, healthcare access, and child health in the climate-affected coastal communities of Bangladesh. Moreover, this study considers variables such as residence which was categorized by village (Pankhali, Hoglabunia, Katabunia, Sutarkhali, Nolian, and Kalabogi), while religion was classified as Muslim or non-Muslim to account for possible social and cultural variations in HLV and child health outcomes in the southwestern coastal Bangladesh.

Household assets

An index adapted from the 27 item measure employed in the BDHS [62] was used to measure the socioeconomic status of households based on ownership of selected assets and productive resources and grouped into three main categories: household effects, transport ownership, and farm assets. Household effects include access to electricity or solar power and ownership of consumer goods such as radio, television, mobile or non-mobile telephone, refrigerator, almirah/wardrobe, electric fan, DVD/VCD player, water pump, IPS/generator, air conditioner, and computer or laptop. Transport assets include car/truck/microbus, auto bike/tempo/CNG, rickshaw or van, bicycle, motorcycle, motorized boat, and non-motorized boat. The index also incorporates productive assets such as ownership of agricultural land and farm animals including buffaloes, cows, goats or sheep, chickens or ducks, and other livestock. Each item was recorded dichotomously (yes = 1 and no = 0) and the household asset index was classified into three groups: low, moderate, and high.

Household food insecurity

Household food insecurity was assessed using the Household Food Insecurity Access Scale (HFIAS) developed by Coates et al. [63]. On the basis of established scoring guidelines, households were subsequently categorized as food secure, mildly food insecure, moderately food insecure or severely food insecure.

Household livelihood vulnerability

HLV was assessed using a modified livelihood vulnerability index adapted from the framework developed by Hahn et al. [28]. The index captures multiple dimensions of vulnerability that influence household resilience in climate-sensitive environments. In this study, the index incorporated seven key dimensions: sociodemographic profile, livelihood strategies, social networks, health vulnerability, food security, water access and exposure to natural disasters. A total of 23 indicators were used to represent these domains, with each variable coded in binary form (0 = not vulnerable and 1 = vulnerable) (See Supplementary Table 2). The indicators assessed key aspects such as dependency ratio, female-headed households, limited livelihood diversification, chronic illness in the family, reliance on food purchases, seasonal food shortages, access to safe water, and exposure to floods or cyclones. Scores for each indicator were summed to generate a composite vulnerability score ranging from 2 to 17, where higher values indicate greater livelihood vulnerability. The median score of 10 was used as the cut-off point to categorize households: those scoring below 10 were classified as not vulnerable, whereas households scoring ≥ 10 were categorized as vulnerable. This multidimensional index provides a comprehensive measure of household vulnerability by integrating socioeconomic conditions, livelihood strategies, environmental exposure, and access to essential resources in the coastal context of Bangladesh.

Child health

Child health indicators were assessed using standardized clinical and anthropometric criteria. ARI prevalence among children under five was assessed on the basis of caregiver-reported cough, runny nose, and chest-related rapid or difficult breathing within the preceding four weeks (coded as yes = 1 and no = 0). Fever was recorded when the child experienced a core body (rectal) temperature of ≥ 38.0 °C (100.4 °F) during the same recall period (yes = 1 and no = 0). Diarrhea prevalence was defined as three or more loose or watery stools per day, or the presence of blood in stools (yes = 1 and no = 0) [20]. Besides, children’s nutritional status was evaluated using three World Health Organization (WHO) recommended growth indices including stunting, wasting, and underweight following established Z-score thresholds [64, 65]. Z-scores were manually derived from the WHO reference growth standards for children aged 0 − 5 years, separately for boys and girls. Stunting and wasting were classified as severely stunted or wasted, moderately stunted or wasted, or normal height or weight. Underweight was categorized as severely underweight, moderately underweight, normal weight, or overweight [66].

Data analysis

Data analysis was performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 21. Descriptive statistics (percentages) were conducted to summarize household sociodemographic and economic characteristics, child health indicators, and HLV. Bivariate analyses using Pearson’s χ2 and Fisher’s exact tests were used to examine associations between exposure variables and HLV, as well as between livelihood vulnerability and child health indicators. To identify the determinants of HLV, multivariate binary logistic regression analysis was conducted with a significance threshold of p < 0.05, and the results are reported as adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with 5% confidence intervals (CI). To assess the relationship between livelihood vulnerability and child health, binary logistic regression was used to estimate associations with the prevalence of ARI, fever, and diarrhea, whereas multinominal logistic regression was used to evaluate the effects on nutritional status such as stunting, wasting, and underweight. These child health analyses are reported as unadjusted odds ratio (UOR) with 95% CI.

Results

The results of this study are presented and organized into three sections in accordance with the three research questions: (1) prevalence and distribution of HLV, (2) determinants of HLV, and (3) implications of HLV for child health. Descriptive statistics are presented first to illustrate the prevalence and patterns of vulnerability across villages and occupational groups, followed by analytical results identifying the key determinants and health implications.

Prevalence and distribution of HLV

This section addresses the first research question by presenting the overall prevalence of HLV in the southwestern coastal region of Bangladesh and examining its variation across the six study villages and occupational groups of household heads. These descriptive findings provide an important foundation for identifying vulnerable communities and livelihood groups and contextualizing the subsequent analysis of the determinants and health implications of HLV.

Prevalence of HLV and distribution across villages

Figure 2 shows the prevalence and spatial distribution of livelihood vulnerability among coastal households in southwestern Bangladesh. Overall, more than half of the households (58.6%) were classified as vulnerable, while 41.4% were categorized as not vulnerable. The distribution of livelihood vulnerability varied significantly across the six study villages (χ2 = 40.230, p < 0.001) (See Table 1). Kalabogi village exhibited the highest percentage of vulnerable households (26%), followed by Sutarkhali village (19.6%). Conversely, the lowest prevalence of livelihood vulnerability (12.7%) was observed in Pankhali and Nolian villages.

Determinants of HLV

This section addresses the second research question by identifying the socioeconomic and household factors associated with livelihood vulnerability among coastal households. The findings are presented in two stages: first, descriptive characteristics of the surveyed households, followed by analytical results examining the determinants of livelihood vulnerability.

Descriptive characteristics of coastal households

Table 1 summarizes the socioeconomic characteristics of households across the six surveyed villages. The majority of households were Muslim (81%), and 9.2% of household heads had no formal education. In terms of occupation, coastal household heads were involved mainly in day labor (25.9%), followed by service-related occupations such as teaching, animal doctoring, and driving (24.4%), fishing or fish cultivation (13.2%), collecting natural resources from the Sundarbans (12.9%), farming (12.6%), and business (10.9%). More than half of the household heads (54%) reported a monthly income between BDT 10,001 − 20,000, while nearly 75% of households reported having no monthly savings. In addition, 63.5% of households relied on a single income earner (63.5%), and approximately 67% of households had two or more dependent members. Regarding household assets, 52.3% had low asset ownership. Food insecurity was highly prevalent, with 71.8% of households classified as food insecure, while only 28.2% were food secure. Regarding financial coping strategies, about 66% of households reported borrowing money from friends or relatives in the previous month, and nearly 40% of households had sought assistance from local government support programs during the previous year.

The bivariate analysis (See Table 1) further indicates that HLV was significantly associated with several demographic, socioeconomic, and household-level characteristics. Spatial disparities were evident across villages in terms of place of residence (p < 0.001). Characteristics of the household head including religion, education, and occupation were significantly related to HLV (p < 0.01). Economic conditions such as, monthly household income, savings, asset ownership, and food insecurity also showed strong associations (p < 0.001). In addition, household composition including number of earning members and dependents were also significant (p < 0.002). Furthermore, coping-related behaviors such as borrowing money from friends or relatives and seeking assistance from local government were significantly linked with HLV (p < 0.001).

Predictors of HLV

Table 2 presents the results of the binary logistic regression analysis examining the determinants of HLV. HLV was the outcome variable (vulnerable = 1 and not vulnerable = 0) and variables identified as significant in the bivariate analyses were included in the regression model. The analysis identified several significant determinants of HLV, including place of residence, religion, education and occupation of the household head, monthly household savings, number of earning members, number of dependent members, household food insecurity, borrowing money from friends or relatives, and seeking assistance from local government.

The findings revealed that households in Sutarkhali village had 5.465 times greater odds of vulnerability than did those in Pankhali village due to greater environmental exposure and limited livelihood opportunities. Sutarkhali is located closer to the Sundarbans, making it more vulnerable to cyclones, tidal flooding, and salinity intrusion, which negatively affect agriculture, freshwater availability, and income stability. As a result, many households depend on climate-sensitive activities such as fishing, forest resource collection, and day labor, leading to higher livelihood vulnerability compared with households in Pankhali village. In addition, Muslim households had 4.890 times greater odds of being vulnerable than non-Muslim households which can be interpreted in the context of local socioeconomic patterns in the coastal region. In many villages of Dacope upazila, the Muslim population is more likely to depend on climate-sensitive livelihoods such as small-scale farming, fishing, forest resource collection from the Sundarbans, and day labor. These occupations are highly vulnerable to environmental hazards including cyclones, salinity intrusion, and tidal flooding that are common in Khulna district. In contrast, many non-Muslim (particularly Hindus) households in this region are historically engaged in relatively stable activities such as small businesses, trading, or service-related occupations, which may provide more regular income and lower exposure to environmental risks. Consequently, differences in livelihood structures and economic opportunities may contribute to the higher observed vulnerability among Muslim households in the southwestern coastal context.

Additionally, households headed by individuals with primary, secondary, or higher education had significantly lower odds of livelihood vulnerability than households whose heads were non-literate. The very low odds ratios (AOR = 0.054, 0.061, and 0.036) suggest that education acts as a strong protective factor against HLV. In the climate-vulnerable coastal context of Dacope upazila, education improves access to diversified livelihood opportunities, enhances decision-making capacity, and increases awareness of environmental and health risks. As a result, higher education contributes to improving livelihood security and lowers the likelihood of household vulnerability.

Moreover, households whose heads were engaged in collecting natural resources from the Sundarbans exhibited 37fold higher odds of vulnerability compared with other occupational groups in southwestern coastal Bangladesh. This can be attributed to the precarious nature of forest-based livelihoods, which are commonly practiced by economically marginalized and landless households and rely on irregular, seasonal income from activities such as fishing, crab collection, honey collecting, and wood harvesting. Income from these activities is highly dependent on environmental conditions and market fluctuations, increasing livelihood insecurity. Moreover, resource collectors face substantial occupational and environmental risks, including exposure to cyclones, tidal surges, and dangerous wildlife, which can lead to injury, loss of working capacity, or death, further intensifying household vulnerability.

Besides, the findings of the study indicate that economic capacity, household structure, and food security play critical roles in shaping HLV. Households with monthly savings of BDT ≤ 1,000 had 0.298 times lower odds of vulnerability compared to those without savings because even small savings provide a financial buffer during shocks such as crop loss, illness, or climate-related disruptions. Similarly, households with two or more earning members had significantly 0.247 times lower odds of being vulnerable than households that relied on a single earner since diversified income sources increase economic stability and enhance coping capacity. Conversely, households with two dependents had 2.458 times greater odds of vulnerability because a larger dependency burden increases household consumption needs while reducing the proportion of income earners. Food insecurity also showed a strong association with vulnerability as expected, moderately and severely food insecure households had substantially 3.466 times and 7.595 times higher odds of vulnerability than food secure households. In climate-affected coastal areas where salinity intrusion, cyclones, and fluctuating agricultural productivity disrupt food production and income, food insecurity reflects deeper livelihood instability. Likewise, households that borrowed money from friends or relatives (AOR = 7.376) or sought assistance from local government (AOR = 4.642) were significantly more likely to be vulnerable. These behaviors often represent coping responses to financial stress, indicating limited savings, unable income, and insufficient livelihood resilience. Overall, these findings highlight the multidimensional nature of livelihood vulnerability among coastal households, reflecting the combined influence of socioeconomic conditions, livelihood structures, and household resource constraints.

Discussion

This study investigates the prevalence of livelihood vulnerability among households in the southwestern coastal region of Bangladesh, identifies the key determinants and evaluates their implications for child health outcomes. The findings revealed that 58.6% of the households experienced livelihood vulnerability, aligning with evidence from other coastal contexts. For instance, within Bangladesh, Mudasser, Hossain [17] reported that 23% of households were highly vulnerable and 21% were less vulnerable, whereas Hossain, Shohel [35] documented a 49.7% prevalence of cyclone-related household vulnerability. Comparable patterns are observed internationally; in Vietnam, Thuy and Anh [67] reported that 26.3% of households were moderately vulnerable and 73.3% were highly vulnerable, whereas studies from the Philippines and Indonesia also indicated higher levels of livelihood vulnerability in hazard-prone coastal areas [68, 69]. The variations in reported household vulnerability across studies likely reflect differences in geographic exposures, measurement methods, and underlying socioeconomic context.

Consistent with previous research, the current study identifies pronounced regional disparities in HLV across the coastal belt of Bangladesh [35, 37]. We found that households in Sutarkhali village presented greater vulnerability than did those in Pankhali village. This may be interpreted from diverse perspectives. Firstly, the geographic position adjacent to the Bay of Bengal exposes it to recurrent cyclonic storm surges, tidal flooding, riverbank erosion, salinity intrusion, and gradual land degradation, all of which intensify livelihood and health risks [48, 49]. Secondly, heavy dependence on Sundarbans-based livelihoods heightens sensitivity to seasonal and climatic fluctuations [48, 50]. Thirdly, limited infrastructure development, particularly poor roads and communication networks, further constrains mobility, market access, and emergency response. These interlinked ecological and structural vulnerabilities collectively intensify HLV in Sutarkhali underscoring the importance of local adaptation and resilience strategies.

The current study revealed that Muslim households experienced greater livelihood vulnerability than non-Muslim households. This pattern may reflect the local socioeconomic structure of the coastal region. In many villages of Dacope upazila, Muslim households are more likely to rely on climate-sensitive livelihoods such as small-scale agriculture, fishing, forest resource collection from the Sundarbans, and day labor. These occupations are particularly exposed to environmental hazards including cyclones, salinity intrusion, and tidal flooding that frequently affect Khulna district. In contrast, many non-Muslim households, particularly Hindus, are historically involved in relatively stable income-generating activities such as small businesses, trading, and service-related occupations, which may provide more consistent income and lower exposure to environmental shocks. Therefore, variations in livelihood structures and economic opportunities may explain the higher vulnerability observed among Muslim households in the southwestern coastal region. Moreover, socioeconomic disparities, including differential access to resources and social capital, further influence adaptive capacity and resilience across religious groups. However, the influence of religion as an independent determinant of HLV remains underexplored, highlighting the need for further research across diverse contexts.

HLV was inversely associated with the educational attainment of the household head, with households headed by individuals with primary, secondary, and higher education demonstrating significantly lower odds of vulnerability compared to those headed by non-literate individuals. This finding is consistent with prior studies in Bangladesh [17, 37, 47], Nepal [70] and South Africa [71] showing that higher educational attainment reduces the level of vulnerability. This may be because non-literate household heads are more likely to rely on irregular, low-wage manual labor, leaving them seasonally unemployed and economically insecure, which heightens their vulnerability. It is also evident that education enhances livelihood diversification (the adoption of multiple and complementary income-generating activities) and access to stable income sources, reducing both socioeconomic and environmental vulnerability [36, 40]. Moreover, educated household heads possess greater awareness of disaster preparedness and adaptation strategies enabling effective mitigation of environment-related shocks and strengthening household resilience [72].

The occupation of the household head was another significant predictor of coastal HLV, with households reliant on Sundarbans-based resource extraction livelihoods exhibited 37folds greater odds of being vulnerable. These findings align with evidence from coastal Bangladesh demonstrating that dependence on Sundarbans resources significantly heightens household vulnerability [35]. The justification for this finding is that Sundarbans’ dependent livelihoods are inherently seasonal, typically providing income for around six to nine months a year, leaving extended lean periods of unemployment and financial precarity. Moreover, government-imposed restrictions or temporary closures of the Sundarbans further disrupt access to resources. Therefore, affected households are often forced to rely on informal borrowing from relatives, neighbors, non-governmental organizations (NGOs), or local moneylenders, thereby deepening cycles of debt and socioeconomic insecurity. This structural dependence on an ecologically fragile and highly regulated resource base underscores the intersection of environmental risk, livelihood instability, and chronic poverty, which defines coastal HLV.

Household savings, as an essential financial asset, emerged as a key determinant of livelihood vulnerability, with households saving BDT ≤ 1,000 per month showing significantly lower odds of vulnerability than those without savings. Consistent with prior studies from Bangladesh [17] and Mexico [73], access to savings or financial resources reduces vulnerability and enhances household resilience. Therefore, the justification might be that savings functions as a buffer against adverse events by stabilizing consumption, securing essential needs, and enabling investment in adaptive strategies, ultimately reducing vulnerability. Conversely, limited or depleted savings heighten vulnerability by forcing asset liquidation or reliance on external assistance, reinforcing cycles of vulnerability and reducing long-term resilience.

Furthermore, the number of economically active members within a household has emerged as a critical determinant of HLV. Consistent with earlier research in SA [37, 70], the current study demonstrated that households with two or more earning members presented substantially lower odds of vulnerability than households dependent on a single breadwinner. This might be interpreted as multiple earners diversifying income sources and reducing dependence on any single, seasonally constrained or climate-sensitive livelihood, shielding households against sudden income shocks, crop failures, or natural hazards. Besides, in coastal areas of Bangladesh, where agriculture, fishing, and Sundarbans-based livelihoods are highly sensitive to salinity intrusion, cyclones, and flooding, multiple earning members can stabilize household cash flow, ensuring more consistent access to food, healthcare, and savings for emergencies. Moreover, additional earners can invest in adaptive measures such as improved housing, water treatment, or disaster preparedness, thereby strengthening both socioeconomic and physical resilience.

Households with more dependents, particularly those with two dependents, had significantly higher odds of livelihood vulnerability than households with only one dependent. This observation is corroborated by previous research from Bangladesh and Tanzania showing that a higher dependency ratio amplifies livelihood vulnerability and vulnerability to poverty and food insecurity [37, 74]. A plausible explanation lies in the economic constraints imposed by a greater share of non-earning members, which reduces per capita income and limits resources for food, healthcare, education, and housing. Limited earnings stretched across more nonproductive members, constrained savings and diminished capacity to cope with shocks. This economic strain restricts investments in risk-reducing measures such as improved housing, safe water, or disaster preparedness, exacerbating exposure to climate and health hazards. In coastal Bangladesh, where livelihoods are already threatened by seasonal unemployment, cyclones, and salinity intrusion, multiple dependents intensify financial instability during lean periods. Households often resort to negative coping strategies, including debt, and reduced meal quality or frequency reinforcing cycles of poverty, malnutrition, and vulnerability.

The present study revealed that households experiencing moderate to severe food insecurity faced significantly greater odds of livelihood vulnerability than food-secure households. This finding is consistent with earlier studies showing that coastal populations are disproportionately exposed to food insecurity because they are more vulnerable to poverty [75] and limited income diversification [76]. This may be because household food insecurity emerges as both a driver and a consequence of vulnerability, reflecting restricted access to income, productive assets, and cultivable land, which heightens exposure to economic and climatic shocks. Vulnerable livelihoods marked by limited assets and income heighten exposure to food shortages during chronic or economic shocks, whereas chronic food insecurity erodes savings and productive assets, restricting investment in more resilient livelihoods. Strengthening livelihoods through diversification, asset protection, and improved access to resources and services is therefore essential to breaking this cycle and enhancing household resilience.

Furthermore, households relying on informal or formal assistance presented markedly greater odds of vulnerability. Households that borrowed money from friends or relatives in the past month were significantly more vulnerable than those who did not, corroborating evidence that households are more vulnerable if they receive support from their relatives or friends [28, 67], as dependence on social support signals weakened coping capacity however, this contrasts with the findings of a prior study in Bangladesh [37]. This can be explained by the fact that although social capital is frequently celebrated as a buffer against shocks, its protective function is not universal. In a resource-constrained context, repeated borrowing from kin or neighbors can operate as a symptom of vulnerability rather than a safeguard, often reflecting chronic livelihood insecurity and depleted coping capability.

Similarly, households that sought local government assistance demonstrated significantly greater odds of livelihood vulnerability. This finding aligns with prior evidence showing that households receiving local government support in the form of social safety nets often experience greater vulnerability [67] however, this contrasts with the findings of prior studies from Bangladesh and Tanzania [37, 77]. The justification for this finding may be that while public assistance programs are intended to cause cushion shocks, frequent dependence on such aid can signal chronic livelihood insecurity and limited adaptive capacity. Rather than reflecting proactive risk management, the need for repeated government help often emerges when households face persistent stressors such as income instability, food insecurity, or recurrent environmental hazards that exceed their coping resources. In this context, external support functions less as a protective cushion and more as an indicator of entrenched exposure to risk. These findings suggest that social and governmental support are essential safety nets, frequent reliance on them often serves as a marker of structural fragility and limited resilience, underscoring that social capital does not invariably mitigate vulnerability but can instead reveal deeper livelihood insecurity. Therefore, strengthening local infrastructure, diversifying livelihoods, and enhancing household-level adaptive capacities are necessary to reduce coastal households’ livelihood vulnerability.

Furthermore, the present study revealed a significant association between HLV and underweight status, showing that children from non-vulnerable households were less likely to be severely underweight than those from vulnerable households. This finding is consistent with previous studies indicating that HLV is a key determinant of child undernutrition and morbidity, with children from highly vulnerable households facing greater risks of stunting, wasting, and diarrheal diseases than those from less vulenrable housheolds [20, 43]. It is also documented in prior studies from Bangladesh that children from the poorest households bear a substantially higher risk of being underweight than those from affluent households [78, 79]. This finding can be interpreted that HLV largely reflects underlying socioeconomic disadvantages, as poorer households often lack stable income, adequate food access, safe water, sanitation, and healthcare services. Consequently, limited financial resources constrain investments in nutritious diet, healthcare utilization, and healthy living environments, thereby increasing the likelihood of growth failure and illness among children [20, 42, 43, 64, 80–82]. Moreover, poor housing conditions, unsafe drinking water, and inadequate sanitation increase children’s exposure to infections, which impair nutrient absorption and contribute to the self-reinforcing cycle of infection and undernutrition, weakening immunity and increasing susceptibility to infectious diseases [55, 83–85]. The present findings therefore advance the understanding of the pathways through which HLV contributes to child undernutrition and underscore the need for integrated, multisectoral strategies that combine economic resilience with targeted health and nutrition interventions in Bangladesh’s climate-sensitive coastal belt and in other similar settings globally.

Limitations of the study

Despite several strengths, this study has some limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the research was conducted exclusively in rural coastal communities of southwestern Bangladesh; therefore, the findings may not be fully generalizable to urban settings or other ecological regions with different socioeconomic and environmental contexts. Second, although the use of a structured livelihood vulnerability index allowed for a comprehensive quantitative assessment of demographic, social, health, and climate-related dimensions of vulnerability, the reliance on quantitative survey data limited the depth of contextual insights that could have been obtained through qualitative approaches. Third, information on child morbidity was based on caregivers’ self-reports rather than clinical records, which may introduce recall bias. Finally, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to draw causal inferences between HLV and child health outcomes, as the observed associations represent a single point in time rather than dynamic processes. Notwithstanding these limitations, this study offers valuable evidence to inform region-specific strategies aimed at reducing households’ livelihood vulnerability and improving health outcomes for children under five both within Bangladesh and in other coastal regions facing similar socioenvironmental challenges.

Areas for further research

Future studies should adopt longitudinal research design to better capture the temporal dynamics of livelihood vulnerability and its causal pathways influencing child health outcomes. Incorporating mixed methods that combine quantitative surveys with qualitative techniques would provide deeper insight into the social, cultural, and institutional mechanisms shaping household vulnerability and caregiving practices in coastal communities. Additionally, comparative studies across different ecological zones or urban-rural contexts in Bangladesh could enhance understanding of how environmental exposure and socioeconomic conditions interact to influence livelihood vulnerability. Further research should also explore the effectiveness of targeted interventions such as climate-resilient livelihood programs, social protection mechanisms, and nutrition-sensitive policies in mitigating household vulnerability and improving child health outcomes in hazard-prone coastal regions.

Conclusion and policy implications

This study investigated the prevalence and determinants of HLV in the southwestern Bangladesh and its implications for child health. The findings revealed that livelihood vulnerability was concentrated among households in Sutarkhali village, Muslim households, households headed by individuals without formal education, and households dependent on Sundarbans-based resource extraction. Additional contributing factors included the absence of household savings, reliance on a single earner, multiple dependent, household food insecurity, recent borrowing from social networks, and dependence on local government assistance. Importantly, children from vulnerable households were significantly more likely to be severely underweight, highlighting the critical link between HLV and child undernutrition. These findings highlight the need for integrated policy responses that simultaneously address livelihood insecurity and child health. Strengthening climate-resilient and diversified livelihoods such as climate-adaptive agriculture, aquaculture, and small-scale enterprises can reduce dependence on high-risk resource extraction from Sundarbans and enhance household income stability. Expanding education, vocational training, and financial inclusion programs (e.g., savings schemes and microcredit) can further improve households’ adaptive capacity and economic resilience. At the same time, child health and nutrition programs should incorporate household vulnerability assessments to prioritize support for high-risk families through targeted cash transfers, supplementary feeding, growth monitoring, and nutrition counseling. Improving the effectiveness and transparency of local government social protection programs will also be essential to ensure that assistance reaches the most vulnerable households. Overall, coordinated livelihood, nutrition, and social protection interventions are critical for reducing vulnerability and improving child health outcomes in climate-exposed coastal region of Bangladesh and other regions with comparable socioeconomic and environmental challenges.
