Abstract
. q , t q,t -deformed matrix models give rise to representations of the deformed Virasoro algebra and more generally of the quantum toroidal 𝔤 ​ 𝔩 1 \mathfrak{gl}_{1} algebra. These representations are described in terms of finite difference equations that induce recursion relations for correlation functions. Under suitable assumptions, these recursions admit unique solutions expressible through “superintegrability” formulas, i.e. explicit closed formulas for averages of Macdonald polynomials. In this paper, we discuss examples arising from localization of 3d 𝒩 = 2 \mathcal{N}=2 theories, which include q , t q,t -deformation of well known classical ensembles: Gaussian, Laguerre and Jacobi. We explain how relations in the quantum toroidal algebra can be used to give a new and universal proof of the known superintegrability formulas, as well as to derive new formulas for models that have not been previously studied in the literature. Finally, we make some remarks regarding the relation between superintegrability and orthogonal polynomials.



1.
Introduction

In this paper we study certain eigenvalue-like integrals that arise as
q,t
-deformations of ordinary Hermitian matrix ensembles, namely

(1.1)

\oint_{\mathcal{C}}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\mathrm{d}x_{i}\,\prod_{1\leq i\neq j\leq N}\frac{(x_{i}/x_{j};q)_{\infty}}{(tx_{i}/x_{j};q)_{\infty}}\,f(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})\prod_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}^{\frac{\log t}{\log q}(N-1)}w(x_{i})\,.

These models appear in various contexts, in particular, as a result of supersymmetric localization of certain three-dimensional gauge theories or in
q
-deformed CFTs.
In the context of multivariable orthogonal polynomials, these integrals give rise to inner products on the space of symmetric polynomial functions and, for specific choices of the weight function
w(x)
, they fit into the multivariable generalization of the
q
-Askey scheme.

More concretely, the matrix models considered in this paper are of two kinds, distinguished by their physical origin.
The first type corresponds to a family of integrals that can be given a triality of interpretations, in the sense of
[
1
,
2
]
. These can be labeled by the
3d

\mathcal{N}=2
gauge theory on
D^{2}\times_{q}S^{1}
from which they are obtained as a result of supersymmetric localization.
The resulting integral formulas can be understood as the Dotsenko–Fateev representation of
q
-Virasoro conformal blocks. The third description follow by a direct residue calculation, which shows that evaluation of these integrals produces sums over integers partitions analogous to those that compute the
5d
Nekrasov partition functions.
In what follows we restrict ourselves to theories with a single
U(N)
gauge group and not more than two (anti-)fundamental matter multiplets.
Integrals in this class represent deformations and generalizations of well-known classical ensembles, such as the
q
(or
q,t
)-Gaussian,
q
-Selberg and
q
-Laguerre models.

The second type of integral we consider represents a special standalone case—the matrix integral associated to the refined Chern–Simons (rCS) theory, as defined by Aganagic and Shakirov
[
3
]
. This theory is proposed as a deformation or refinement of the standard Chern–Simons theory on
S^{3}
. At
t=q
one obtains the so called Stieltjes–Wigert ensemble, which is the matrix model associated to Chern–Simons theory
[
4
]
.
Expectation values in this model compute the colored HOMFLY–PT quantum knot invariants of the unknot, and in the refined case, the superpolynomials of certain knot homology theories
[
5
]
.

Computing observables in the physical theory corresponds to evaluating expectation values of symmetric functions in the matrix model. In certain cases, these models exhibit a remarkable property: in a special basis of the space of symmetric functions—the Macdonald polynomials—the expectation values take exceptionally simple forms and can be computed exactly.
Although examples of this property were known previously, the phenomenon appears to be quite universal and has therefore been given a special name:
superintegrability

[
6
]
. For the models considered in this paper, it manifests in the following way.
Expectation values of Macdonald symmetric functions
P_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x})
are given by

(1.2)

\Big\langle P_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x})\Big\rangle^{w}=\mathsf{C}_{\lambda}^{w}\,P_{\lambda}(p_{k}=\varphi^{w}_{k})

where
\varphi^{w}_{k}
is a certain evaluation locus and the function
\mathsf{C}_{\lambda}^{w}
is a factorized expression, i.e. a product over boxes of the partition
\lambda
:

(1.3)

\mathsf{C}_{\lambda}^{w}=\prod_{(i,j)\in\lambda}g^{w}(q^{j-1}t^{-i+1})\,.

Both
\varphi^{w}_{k}
and the function
g^{w}
depend explicitly on the choice of weight function
w(x)
.

In Theorem
5.2
, we give a list of ten different
q,t
-models for which superintegrability holds and we list explicitly the corresponding values of the coefficients
\mathsf{C}_{\lambda}^{w}
and the evaluations
\varphi^{w}_{k}
.

Different special cases of this formula have been known in the literature. In particular, for the refined Chern–Simons model, this formula is a consequence of the celebrated Cherednik–Macdonald–Mehta identities
[
7
,
8
]
for root systems of A-type. One case, corresponding to the
q
-Selberg integral was studied and proven by Kaneko
[
9

, while a formula for the
q,t
-Gaussian model was conjectured by Morozov, Popolitov and Shakirov
[
10

and recently proven by Forrester and Byun
[
11

. Our first goal in this paper is to provide a comprehensive classification of all such
q,t
-matrix models, that can be shown to have superintegrability using available techniques. The main feature of our approach to prove superintegrability is that it applies universally to all the cases considered.

Our approach is based on solving differential/difference equations known as Ward identities. In the context of classical matrix models, these are usually known as the Virasoro constraints or loop equations. These are equations that can be phrased either as certain linear recursion relations between correlation functions or as certain differential/difference equations satisfied by the generating functions
\mathsf{Z}^{w}(\mathbf{p})
. In the deformed context, these are known as
q,t
-Virasoro constraints
[
12
]
. Using a specific choice of resummation of such constraint equations, we are able to obtain a single more tractable “
recursion operator
” equation of the form

(1.4)

\hat{\mathsf{A}}^{w}\cdot\mathsf{Z}^{w}(\mathbf{p})=0\,.

Explicitly solving these equations allows us to derive and prove explicit expressions for the superintegrability data
\mathsf{C}_{\lambda}^{w}
and
\varphi^{w}_{k}
in (
1.2
).

Another crucial feature of our approach is that it reveals another hidden algebraic structure behind these matrix models. It turns out, that the
q,t
-Virasoro algebra is actually enhanced to the full quantum toroidal algebra
U_{q,t}(\hat{\hat{\mathfrak{gl}}}_{1})
.
It is in fact natural to express the operator
\hat{\mathsf{A}}^{w}
as a linear combinations of generators of
U_{q,t}(\hat{\hat{\mathfrak{gl}}}_{1})
in the (level-1) Fock representation.
This is of course not the first observation of the role played by this algebra in the context at hand. On the physical side it is expected that quantum toroidal algebras are the hidden symmetries behind the AGT duality. In particular the
q,t
-Virasoro Ward identities are examples of
qq
-character relations
[
13
,
14

15
]
.

The refined Chern–Simons model deserves special attention because of its relation to knot invariants. Surprisingly, the
U_{q,t}(\hat{\hat{\mathfrak{gl}}}_{1})
-valued recursion operator which annihilates the generating function has a clear geometric meaning in this case. We show, that in the unrefined limit (i.e.

[
16

17

18

\mathfrak{gl}_{1}
algebra. Hence, it is natural to conjecture that the recursion operators obtained in this paper are the correct categorification of the usual skein recursion relations.

Our method for proving superintegrability reformulates the problem entirely in algebraic terms. One of the byproducts of this reformulation is a relation of our approach to the theory of multivariate orthogonal polynomials. Going further along this direction, we establish a connection between the “
gauge transformation operators

\hat{G}_{w}

P_{\lambda}

\hat{\mathsf{A}}^{w}
can be written as

Outline of the paper

After collecting some notations and conventions on symmetric functions in Section
2
, we review superintegrability formulas for classical Hermitian matrix models and their operator formulation in Section
3

q,t

Finally, in Section

\Delta^{+}_{\lambda}(z)=\frac{P_{\lambda}\left(p_{k}=\frac{1-z^{k}}{1-t^{k}}\right)}{P_{\lambda}\left(p_{k}=\frac{1}{1-t^{k}}\right)}\,,

which follows from
[
21
, Ch.VI, §6, (6.17)]
. It is also useful to recall that

(2.20)

\Delta^{+}(z)=(-z)^{\hat{D}}\mathsf{T}\Delta^{-}(z^{-1})\,,

where
\hat{D}:=\sum\limits_{k\geq 1}kp_{k}\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{k}}
is the degree operator

(2.21)

\hat{D}\cdot s_{\lambda}=|\lambda|\,s_{\lambda}\,.

Equivalently, one can define Macdonald functions as the orthogonal basis w.r.t. the Macdonald inner product defined as

(2.22)

\langle p_{\lambda},p_{\mu}\rangle_{q,t}=z_{\lambda}\delta_{\lambda,\mu}\prod_{k\in\lambda}\frac{1-q^{k}}{1-t^{k}}

with normalization fixed by

(2.23)

\langle P_{\lambda},P_{\lambda}\rangle_{q,t}=b_{\lambda}^{-1}\,,\hskip 30.0ptb_{\lambda}=\prod_{(i,j)\in\lambda}\frac{1-q^{\lambda_{i}-j}t^{\lambda_{j}^{\prime}-i+1}}{1-q^{\lambda_{i}-j+1}t^{\lambda_{j}^{\prime}-i}}\,.

Similarly to the case of the Hall pairing, we can define adjoint operators of the power sums
p_{k}
as the differential operators
p_{k}^{\perp}
,

(2.24)

\langle p_{k}f,g\rangle_{q,t}=\langle f,p_{k}^{\perp}g\rangle_{q,t}\,,\hskip 30.0ptp_{k}^{\perp}=k\frac{1-q^{k}}{1-t^{k}}\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{k}}\,.

The reproducing kernel associated to the product
\langle\,,\,\rangle_{q,t}
can be written as

(2.25)

\exp\left(\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{1-t^{k}}{1-q^{k}}\frac{p_{k}\otimes p_{k}}{k}\right)=\sum_{\lambda}b_{\lambda}P_{\lambda}\otimes P_{\lambda}\,,

where the equality between the two expressions generalizes the Cauchy identity (
2.9
) and reduces to it in the limit
t=q
.

The Macdonald inner product can be obtained as the
N\to\infty
limit of the finite integral

(2.26)

\langle f,g\rangle^{\prime}_{q,t}=\frac{1}{N!}\oint\prod_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{i}}{2\pi\mathrm{i}x_{i}}\Delta_{q,t}(\mathbf{x})f(\mathbf{x})g(\mathbf{x}^{-1})\,,\hskip 30.0pt\Delta_{q,t}(\mathbf{x})=\prod_{i\neq j}\frac{(x_{i}x_{j}^{-1};q)_{\infty}}{(tx_{i}x_{j}^{-1};q)_{\infty}}

where
\Delta_{q,t}(\mathbf{x})
is the Macdonald deformation of the Vandermonde determinant, and

(2.27)

(z;q)_{\infty}=\prod_{k=0}^{\infty}(1-q^{k}z)

is the
q
-Pochhammer symbol. We then have

(2.28)

\frac{\langle P_{\lambda},P_{\mu}\rangle^{\prime}_{q,t}}{\langle 1,1\rangle^{\prime}_{q,t}}=(b^{(N)}_{\lambda})^{-1}\delta_{\lambda,\mu}

with
\lim\limits_{N\to\infty}b^{(N)}_{\lambda}=b_{\lambda}
.

Finally, we introduce some standard notation regarding
q
-analogues of ordinary differential and integral operators.
The
q
-analogue of the derivative w.r.t.
x
is the
q
-derivative
D^{q}_{x}
, which is defined as

(2.29)

D_{x}^{q}f(x)=\frac{1}{x}\frac{1-q^{x\frac{\partial}{\partial x}}}{1-q}f(x)=\frac{f(x)-f(qx)}{x(1-q)}\,.

and the Jackson integral is defined as its formal inverse, i.e. the discrete sum

(2.30)

\int\limits_{0}^{a}\mathrm{d}_{q}x\,f(x)=\frac{1-q}{1-q^{a\frac{\partial}{\partial a}}}af(a)=(1-q)\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}q^{na\frac{\partial}{\partial a}}af(a)=(1-q)\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(q^{n}a)f(q^{n}a)

and

(2.31)

\int\limits_{a}^{b}\mathrm{d}_{q}x\,f(x)=\int\limits_{0}^{b}\mathrm{d}_{q}x\,f(x)-\int\limits_{0}^{a}\mathrm{d}_{q}x\,f(x)\,.

In the limit
q\to 1
, one recovers the usual definitions of derivative and integral.

3.
Classical Matrix Models

In this section, we review the classical Hermitian random matrix ensembles focusing on the Virasoro constraints that they satisfy, as a preamble to the next sections where we will consider the Macdonald deformation of these models. We use this section to also clarify what we mean by
superintegrability
and we give a definition which will allow us to easily generalize to the Macdonald (refined) case later on.

3.1.
Hermitian matrix models

We start by considering the random matrix model whose partition function is given by the integral over Hermitian
N
-by-
N
matrices
M\in H_{N}
,

(3.1)

\mathsf{Z}^{V}=\int_{H_{N}}\mathrm{d}M\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{Tr}V(M)}\,,

with
V(M)
some function known as the
potential
of the matrix model. We will take
V(M)
to be invariant under the action of the group
U(N)
on
H_{N}
by conjugation.

This allows us to rewrite the matrix integral as an integral over the eigenvalues
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
of the matrix
M
, and we have

(3.2)

\mathsf{Z}^{V}=\frac{1}{N!}\int\prod_{i=1}^{N}\mathrm{d}x_{i}\prod_{i\neq j}(x_{i}-x_{j})\,\mathrm{e}^{-\sum_{i=1}^{N}V(x_{i})}\,,

where
\prod_{i\neq j}(x_{i}-x_{j})
is the Vandermonde determinant.
The observables of the matrix model are the expectation values of operators corresponding to functions of
M
which are also invariant under the
U(N)
-action. These correspond to insertions of symmetric polynomials in the eigenvalues
x_{i}
. Hence, we define the normalized correlation functions as

(3.3)

\Big\langle f\Big\rangle^{V}=\dfrac{\displaystyle\int_{H_{N}}\mathrm{d}M\,f(M)\,\exp\left({-\mathrm{Tr}\,V(M)}\right)}{\displaystyle\int_{H_{N}}\mathrm{d}M\,\exp\left({-\mathrm{Tr}\,V(M)}\right)}=\dfrac{\displaystyle\int\prod\limits_{i=1}^{N}\mathrm{d}x_{i}\prod_{i\neq j}(x_{i}-x_{j})\,f(\mathbf{x})\,\exp\left({-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}V(x_{i})}\right)}{\displaystyle\int\prod\limits_{i=1}^{N}\mathrm{d}x_{i}\prod_{i\neq j}(x_{i}-x_{j})\,\exp\left({-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}V(x_{i})}\right)}\,,

and it is understood that the matrix model average
\Big\langle f\Big\rangle^{V}
depends on the choice of potential.

Instead of considering individual correlation functions, it is often useful to collect all of them into a generating function,

(3.4)

\mathsf{Z}^{V}(\mathbf{p})=\Big\langle\exp\Big(\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{p_{k}}{k}\mathrm{Tr}(M^{k})\Big)\Big\rangle^{V}=\Big\langle\exp\Big(\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{p_{k}}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}^{k}\Big)\Big\rangle^{V}

where
p_{k}\in\Lambda
are regarded as formal generating function parameters also known as
higher times
and
\mathbf{p}=(p_{1},p_{2},\dots)
. Because of this, we will sometimes refer to
\mathsf{Z}^{V}(\mathbf{p})
as the
time-dependent
partition function.

As a formal power series in
p_{k}
’s,
\mathsf{Z}^{V}(\mathbf{p})
is not necessarily convergent, however we will assume that
V
and the contours of integration are chosen is such a way that the coefficients in the
p_{k}
expansion are all well-defined convergent integrals. Moreover, because of the normalization of the correlators, we automatically have that
\mathsf{Z}^{V}(0)=1
.

Making use of the Cauchy identity (
2.9
), we can rewrite the exponential in the generating function so that we have

(3.5)

\mathsf{Z}^{V}(\mathbf{p})=\sum_{\lambda}\mathsf{c}_{\lambda}\,\frac{p_{\lambda}}{z_{\lambda}}

where the sum is over all partitions
\lambda
, and the coefficient
\mathsf{c}_{\lambda}
is given by

(3.6)

\mathsf{c}_{\lambda}=\Big\langle\prod_{k\in\lambda}\mathrm{Tr}(M^{k})\Big\rangle^{V}=\Big\langle\prod_{k\in\lambda}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{N}x^{k}_{i}\Big)\Big\rangle^{V}\,,

which are the standard multi-trace correlation functions often of interest in the theory of matrix models. It turns out that these are not easy to evaluate in closed form for generic
\lambda
and
N
. It is indeed often the case that different basis of observables exhibit different properties, and only some of them admit “nice” formulas. As we will show later, this choice of basis relates to the definition of superintegrability.

It is useful to introduce another equivalent way to rewrite the expansion, which is through the use of Schur functions, as

(3.7)

\mathsf{Z}^{V}(\mathbf{p})=\sum_{\lambda}\Big\langle s_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x})\Big\rangle^{V}\,s_{\lambda}(\mathbf{p})

where we regard
s_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x})=s_{\lambda}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})\in\Lambda_{N}
as a symmetric polynomial in the integration variables
x_{i}
, while
s_{\lambda}(\mathbf{p})\in\Lambda
is regarded as a symmetric function expressed as a polynomial in the time variables
p_{k}
.

3.2.
Superintegrability

Matrix model partition functions of the type (
3.4
) are known to provide examples of
\tau
-functions of integrable hierarchies (see
[
22
]
for a review). This is in fact true of all
\mathsf{Z}^{V}(\mathbf{p})
independently of the choice of potential.
However, for special choices of potentials, additional properties do appear.
As suggested by the Cauchy expansion (
3.7
) of the time-dependent partition function, expectation values of Schur polynomials can be used instead of multi-trace correlators. It turns out that these do admit a closed expression as a function of
\lambda
, for special choices of
V(M)
.
This property was first observed in
[
23
,
24
]
and it is now know under the name of
superintegrability
. We will now show this in some examples.

Example 3.1
.

Let us consider the case of
V(x)=\frac{x^{2}}{2}
, i.e. the
Gaussian
matrix model or
Gaussian Unitary Ensemble
(GUE). We denote matrix expectation values as
\Big\langle f\Big\rangle^{\rm GUE}
, i.e.

(3.8)

\Big\langle f\Big\rangle^{\rm GUE}=\frac{\dfrac{1}{N!}\displaystyle\int\prod\limits_{i=1}^{N}\mathrm{d}x_{i}\prod_{i\neq j}(x_{i}-x_{j})\,f(\mathbf{x})\,\exp\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{x_{i}^{2}}{2}\right)}{\dfrac{1}{N!}\displaystyle\int\prod\limits_{i=1}^{N}\mathrm{d}x_{i}\prod_{i\neq j}(x_{i}-x_{j})\,\exp\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{x_{i}^{2}}{2}\right)}\,.

The expectation value of Schur polynomials then assumes the following form
[
25
]
:

(3.9)

\Big\langle s_{\lambda}\Big\rangle^{\rm GUE}=\mathsf{C}^{\rm GUE}_{\lambda}\cdot s_{\lambda}(p_{k}=\delta_{k,2})\,,

with

(3.10)

\mathsf{C}^{\rm GUE}_{\lambda}:=\prod_{(i,j)\in\lambda}(N+j-i)=\frac{s_{\lambda}(p_{k}=N)}{s_{\lambda}(p_{k}=\delta_{k,1})}\,.

Example 3.2
.

Another interesting example is the Wishart–Laguerre (WL) model,
V(x):=x-\alpha\log(x)
, where the expectation values of Schur polynomials have the form
[
25
]
:

(3.11)

\Big\langle s_{\lambda}\Big\rangle^{\rm WL}=\mathsf{C}^{\rm WL}_{\lambda}\cdot s_{\lambda}(p_{k}=\delta_{k,1})\,,

with

(3.12)

\mathsf{C}^{\rm WL}_{\lambda}:=\prod_{(i,j)\in\lambda}(N+j-i)\prod_{(i,j)\in\lambda}(N+\alpha+j-i)=\frac{s_{\lambda}(p_{k}=N)}{s_{\lambda}(p_{k}=\delta_{k,1})}\frac{s_{\lambda}(p_{k}=N+\alpha)}{s_{\lambda}(p_{k}=\delta_{k,1})}\,.

Let us now give a working definition for superintegrability, that we will use in this paper.

Definition 3.3

(Schur superintegrability of Hermitian eigenvalue models)
.

A matrix model is said to be Schur superintegrable if
there exist an operator
\hat{\mathsf{C}}^{V}:\Lambda\to\Lambda
diagonal on Schur functions,

(3.13)

\hat{\mathsf{C}}^{V}\cdot s_{\lambda}=\mathsf{C}_{\lambda}^{V}\,s_{\lambda}\,,\hskip 30.0pt\text{with eigenvalues}\hskip 30.0pt\mathsf{C}_{\lambda}^{V}=\prod_{(i,j)\in\lambda}g^{V}(i,j)\,,

for some function
g^{V}(i,j)
of the coordinates of the boxes in the partition
2
2

2

In all known examples, this is a function of
j-i
only.
, which depends on the choice of potential
V
, and a ring homomorphism
\varphi^{V}:\Lambda\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
, that acts as a specialization of the power sums to some
V
-dependent locus
such that

(3.14)

\Big\langle f\Big\rangle^{V}=\varphi^{V}\left(\hat{\mathsf{C}}^{V}\cdot f(\mathbf{p})\right)\,,

for any symmetric function
f\in\Lambda
.

With a slight abuse of notation, we will sometimes write

(3.15)

\varphi^{V}_{k}=\varphi^{V}(p_{k})

for the evaluation of the
k
-th powersum.
In particular, this means that the averages of Schur polynomials in a superintegrable model can be written as

(3.16)

\Big\langle s_{\lambda}\Big\rangle^{V}=\mathsf{C}_{\lambda}^{V}\,\varphi^{V}(s_{\lambda})=\mathsf{C}_{\lambda}^{V}\,s_{\lambda}(p_{k}=\varphi_{k}^{V})\,.

In the examples above, we have:

(3.17)

\varphi^{\rm GUE}(p_{k})=\delta_{k,2}\,,\hskip 30.0ptg^{\rm GUE}(i,j)=(N+j-i)\,,

and

(3.18)

\varphi^{\rm WL}(p_{k})=\delta_{k,1}\,,\hskip 30.0ptg^{\rm GUE}(i,j)=(N+j-i)(N+\alpha+j-i)\,.

3.3.
Virasoro constraints

The operation of computing the matrix model average of a symmetric polynomial in
\Lambda_{N}
is linear, hence it can be regarded as a module homomorphism (but not ring homomorphism in general),

(3.19)

\Big\langle\,\Big\rangle^{V}:\Lambda_{N}\to\mathbb{C}\,.

This morphism has a non-trivial kernel corresponding to certain linear relations that the correlation functions satisfy. If we regard the matrix integral as a zero-dimensional path integral, then these relations can be regarded as the Ward identities of the theory.

Equivalently, we can recast such relations as the vanishing of certain differential operators in times
p_{k}
acting on the generating function
\mathsf{Z}^{V}(\mathbf{p})
. In fact, the differential operators in times act on the generating function by introducing an insertion of the corresponding monomial in power sums in the integration variables, for example

(3.20)

n\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{n}}\mathsf{Z}^{V}(\mathbf{p})=n\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{n}}\Big\langle\exp\Big(\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{p_{k}}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}^{k}\Big)\Big\rangle^{V}=\Big\langle\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}^{n}\,\exp\Big(\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{p_{k}}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}^{k}\Big)\Big\rangle^{V}\,.

Among all these relations, there is a special infinite family known as the Virasoro constraints which can be obtained through the insertion of a total differential operator in the matrix integral. Namely,

(3.21)

\int\prod_{i=1}^{N}\mathrm{d}x_{i}\sum_{l=1}^{N}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{l}}\left(x_{l}^{m+1}\prod_{i\neq j}(x_{i}-x_{j})\,\exp\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{N}V(x_{i})+\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{p_{k}}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}^{k}\right)\right)=0\,,

where equality with zero follows from the application of Stokes’ theorem.
3
3

3

We assume that the term inside of the parenthesis vanishes at the boundary of the integration domain whenever such boundary is not empty. If this is not the case, then one obtains a non-homogeneous set of equations
[
26
]
.

Rewriting the derivative in the integrand as the insertion of a linear combination of polynomial functions in
x_{i}
, and then rewriting each term as a combination of derivatives in times as in (
3.20
), we can express the constraints as the differential equations

(3.22)

\hat{L}_{m}\cdot\mathsf{Z}^{V}(\mathbf{p})=0\,,

w.r.t. the formal variables
p_{k}
.

The name “Virasoro constraints” comes from the fact that the operators
\hat{L}_{m}
form a D-module representation of (a parabolic subalgebra of) the Virasoro algebra,

(3.23)

[\hat{L}_{m},\hat{L}_{n}]=(n-m)\hat{L}_{m}

Because derivatives in times can only reproduce insertions of polynomials in
x_{i}
with positive powers, it might happen that some of the constraints obtained as in (
3.21
) cannot be rewritten as differential operators acting on
\mathsf{Z}^{V}(\mathbf{p})
.
In the example of the GUE, for instance, only the constraints with
m\geq-1
can be written in terms of operators
\hat{L}_{m}
, and similarly, in the example of the WL model, we only have
\hat{L}_{m}
with
m\geq 0
.

The Virasoro constraints can be used to determine the coefficients
\mathsf{c}_{\lambda}
of the series expansion (
3.5
) of the generating function of the matrix models, up to a set of initial conditions. These initial conditions parametrize the possible choices of contour, or equivalently the saddles of the potential
V
.
Here we only focus on the cases when the solution to the constraints is unique up to an overall constant. Since the constraints fix the generating function uniquely, this should imply that superintegrability must follows from the Virasoro constraint equations.
We now show that this is indeed true, at least in the examples of the GUE and WL models.

First, we construct the solution to the constraint equations. One way to find the solution is to rewrite the infinite set of constraints as a single more manageable, but equivalent, equation. This single equation is also known as the
W
-operator constraint
[
27
]
. Let us briefly recall the idea, since it is going to be instrumental later on for the Macdonald deformation.

Example 3.4
.

For the GUE, the constraints are well-defined for
m\geq-1
and the solution is unique up to a constant. The solution can be constructed by re-summing the Virasoro operators

(3.24)

\hat{L}_{m}=-(m+2)\partial_{p_{m+2}}+\sum_{a+b=m}ab\partial_{p_{a}}\partial_{p_{b}}+\sum_{k>0}(k+m)p_{k}\partial_{p_{k+m}}+2Nm\partial_{p_{m}}+N^{2}\delta_{m,0}+Np_{1}\delta_{m,-1}

as suggested in
[
27
]
, namely

(3.25)

-\sum_{m=-1}^{\infty}p_{m+2}\hat{L}_{m}=\hat{D}-\hat{W}_{-2}\,,

where
\hat{D}
is the degree-zero operator (
2.21
) and
\hat{W}_{-2}
is a degree-two (shifted) cut-and-join operator defined as

(3.26)

\hat{W}_{-2}=\sum_{a,b=1}^{\infty}abp_{a+b+2}\partial_{p_{a}}\partial_{p_{b}}+\sum_{a,b=1}^{\infty}(a+b-2)p_{a}p_{b}\partial_{p_{a+b-2}}+2N\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}kp_{k+2}\partial_{p_{k}}+N^{2}p_{2}+Np_{1}^{2}\,.

Then Virasoro constraints are equivalent to

(3.27)

\left(\hat{D}-\hat{W}_{-2}\right)\cdot\mathsf{Z}^{\rm GUE}(\mathbf{p})=0\,.

We now observe the following: by the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula
\mathrm{e}^{X}Y\mathrm{e}^{-X}=Y+[X,Y]+\frac{1}{2!}[X,[X,Y]]+\dots
, together with
[\hat{D},\hat{W}_{-2}]=2\hat{W}_{-2}
, we find that we can write

(3.28)

\hat{D}-\hat{W}_{-2}=\hat{D}+[\hat{W}_{-2}/2,\hat{D}]=\exp(\hat{W}_{-2}/2)\hat{D}\exp(-\hat{W}_{-2}/2)\,.

It becomes now evident that (
3.27
) is a sort of “
gauge transformed
” version of the simple equation
\hat{D}\cdot 1=0
, where the gauge transformation is realized by the operator
\exp(\hat{W}_{-2}/2)
.
This implies that

(3.29)

\mathsf{Z}^{\rm GUE}(\mathbf{p})=\exp(\hat{W}_{-2}/2)\cdot 1

is a solution, where
1
is regarded as the vacuum vector in the Fock module
\Lambda
. A rescaling of the vacuum by an arbitrary constant still gives a solution to the
W
-operator constraint, however we will always fix this constant to
1
in order to have the normalization property
\mathsf{Z}^{V}(0)=1
.

One may wonder how this is related to superintegrability. In order to answer this question, we observe the following crucial identity
[
28
]

(3.30)

\hat{\mathsf{C}}^{\rm GUE}\cdot p_{2}\cdot(\hat{\mathsf{C}}^{\rm GUE})^{-1}=\hat{W}_{-2}

with
\hat{\mathsf{C}}^{\rm GUE}
the operator diagonal on Schur functions with eigenvalues as in (
3.10
). It then immediately follows that

(3.31)

\displaystyle\mathsf{Z}^{\rm GUE}(\mathbf{p})

\displaystyle=\exp(\hat{W}_{-2}/2)\cdot 1

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathsf{C}}^{\rm GUE}\cdot\exp(p_{2}/2)\cdot(\hat{\mathsf{C}}^{\rm GUE})^{-1}\cdot 1

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathsf{C}}^{\rm GUE}\cdot\exp\Big(\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{\delta_{k,2}p_{k}}{k}\Big)

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathsf{C}}^{\rm GUE}\cdot\sum_{\lambda}s_{\lambda}(p_{k}=\delta_{k,2})s_{\lambda}(\mathbf{p})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda}\hat{\mathsf{C}}^{\rm GUE}_{\lambda}\varphi^{\rm GUE}(s_{\lambda})s_{\lambda}(\mathbf{p})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda}\varphi^{\rm GUE}(\hat{\mathsf{C}}^{\rm GUE}\cdot s_{\lambda})s_{\lambda}(\mathbf{p})

from which we can read the matrix model averages of the Schur observables as

(3.32)

\Big\langle s_{\lambda}\Big\rangle^{\rm GUE}=\varphi^{\rm GUE}(\hat{\mathsf{C}}^{\rm GUE}\cdot s_{\lambda})

This is precisely the definition of Schur superintegrability in Definition
3.3
.
In this way, we have re-proven that the GUE is superintegrable by using the Virasoro constraints. Notice that this is completely equivalent to the proof of
[
29
,
30
]
.

Example 3.5
.

The same can be done in the WL case. The Virasoro constraints are well-defined for
m\geq 0

[
25
, (2.28)]
and, after resummation, they give rise to the equivalent equation

(3.33)

\left(\hat{D}-\hat{W}_{-1}\right)\cdot\mathsf{Z}^{\rm WL}(\mathbf{p})=0\,,

with

(3.34)

\hat{W}_{-1}=\sum_{a,b=1}^{\infty}abp_{a+b+1}\partial_{p_{a}}\partial_{p_{b}}+\sum_{a,b=1}^{\infty}(a+b-1)p_{a}p_{b}\partial_{p_{a+b-1}}+(2N+\alpha)\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}kp_{k+1}\partial_{p_{k}}+N(N+\alpha)p_{1}\,.

As before, we can rewrite the recursion operator as the conjugate of
\hat{D}
by some invertible gauge transformation,

(3.35)

\hat{D}-\hat{W}_{-1}=\hat{D}+[\hat{W}_{-1},\hat{D}]=\exp(\hat{W}_{-1})\hat{D}\exp(-\hat{W}_{-1})\,,

so that we can write the solution as

(3.36)

\mathsf{Z}^{\rm WL}(\mathbf{p})=\exp(\hat{W}_{-1})\cdot 1\,.

Moreover, observing that
\hat{\mathsf{C}}^{\rm WL}\cdot p_{1}\cdot(\hat{\mathsf{C}}^{\rm WL})^{-1}=\hat{W}_{-1}
, with
\hat{\mathsf{C}}^{\rm WL}
diagonal on Schur with eigenvalue as in (
3.12
), we obtain

\mathsf{Z}^{\rm WL}(\mathbf{p})=\hat{\mathsf{C}}^{\rm WL}\cdot\mathrm{e}^{p_{1}}
which leads to the proof of Schur superintegrability for the WL model, i.e.

(3.37)

\Big\langle s_{\lambda}\Big\rangle^{\rm WL}=\varphi^{\rm WL}(\hat{\mathsf{C}}^{\rm WL}\cdot s_{\lambda})\,.

3.4.
Cut-and-join or
W
-representation

In general, if a model is Schur superintegrable, we can write the generating function
\mathsf{Z}^{V}(\mathbf{p})
as an exponential operator acting on the vacuum state, as follows

(3.38)

\displaystyle\mathsf{Z}^{V}(\mathbf{p})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda}\varphi^{V}(\hat{\mathsf{C}}^{V}\cdot s_{\lambda})s_{\lambda}(\mathbf{p})

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathsf{C}}^{V}\exp\left(\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{\varphi^{V}(p_{k})p_{k}}{k}\right)(\hat{\mathsf{C}}^{V})^{-1}\cdot 1

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{\varphi^{V}_{k}}{k}\hat{\mathsf{C}}^{V}p_{k}(\hat{\mathsf{C}}^{V})^{-1}\right)\cdot 1

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{\varphi^{V}_{k}}{k}\hat{W}^{V}_{-k}\right)\cdot 1\,,

where we defined the operators
\hat{W}^{V}_{-k}:=\hat{\mathsf{C}}^{V}p_{k}(\hat{\mathsf{C}}^{V})^{-1}
, which act on the Schur basis as

(3.39)

\hat{W}^{V}_{-k}\cdot s_{\lambda}=\sum_{\nu\supset\lambda}\frac{\mathsf{C}^{V}_{\nu}}{\mathsf{C}^{V}_{\lambda}}\,d^{(k)}_{\nu/\lambda}\,s_{\nu}\,,\hskip 30.0ptd^{(k)}_{\nu/\lambda}:=\langle s_{\nu},p_{k}\,s_{\lambda}\rangle\,.

Notice that, since
p_{k}
form a commutative algebra and conjugation by the diagonal operator
\hat{\mathsf{C}}^{V}
is an automorphism, it follows that all the operators
\hat{W}^{V}_{-k}
commute with each other (for a given choice of
V
).
We note also that the operators
\hat{W}^{V}_{-k}
can be regarded as elements of a representation of the
W_{1+\infty}
algebra in a single free boson Fock space
[
31
,
32
]
.

4.
q,t
-deformation

We are now ready to introduce the
q,t
-deformed matrix models, which are the main objects of interest in what follows. It turns out that many properties of the classical Hermitian models can be extended to the
q,t
-deformed case.

4.1.
Definition of the models

The main ingredient of deformed matrix model is the substitution of the Vandermonde determinant with the function

(4.1)

\Delta_{q,t}(\mathbf{x})=\prod_{1\leq i\neq j\leq N}\frac{(x_{i}/x_{j};q)_{\infty}}{(tx_{i}/x_{j};q)_{\infty}}\,,

which plays the role of Macdonald kernel as in (
2.26
).
Observe that for
t=q
, the ratio of
q
-Pochhammers simplifies drastically and one recovers the usual Vandermonde determinant,

(4.2)

\Delta_{q,q}(\mathbf{x})=\prod_{i\neq j}(1-x_{i}/x_{j})=\prod_{i\neq j}(x_{i}-x_{j})\prod_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}^{-N+1}\,.

We denote the potential (weight function) by
w(x)
, with the identification
w(x)=\mathrm{e}^{-V(x)}
. We use this notation because, in the
q,t
-deformed case, the potential appears less natural in its exponentiated form. This also allows us to distinguish the two cases more clearly. Accordingly, we write the normalized expectation values as

(4.3)

\Big\langle f\Big\rangle^{w}=\dfrac{1}{\mathsf{Z}^{w}}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\mathrm{d}x_{i}\,\Delta_{q,t}(\mathbf{x})\,f(\mathbf{x})\prod_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}^{\frac{\log t}{\log q}(N-1)}w(x_{i})\,.

Here
w(x)
is part of the measure and characterizes the specific model while
\mathsf{Z}^{w}
is simply a normalization factor corresponding to the partition function, i.e. the integral without any observable insertion:

(4.4)

\mathsf{Z}^{w}=\oint_{\mathcal{C}}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\mathrm{d}x_{i}\,\Delta_{q,t}(\mathbf{x})\,\prod_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}^{\frac{\log t}{\log q}(N-1)}w(x_{i})\,.

As before, this definition ensures that
\Big\langle 1\Big\rangle^{w}=1
.

The choice of the integration contour
\mathcal{C}
depends on the specific model under consideration and is generally a subtle and nontrivial problem. In order to be able to derive
q,t
-Virasoro constraints later, we will assume that the contour satisfies the following condition: if a pole of the integrand is enclosed by
\mathcal{C}
, then all the
q
-shifted images of the pole are also enclosed by
\mathcal{C}
. When this is the case, the insertion of a total
q
-derivative in the integral is identically zero, which leads to a
q
-deformation of Stokes’ theorem. This is a key ingredient in the derivation of
q
-deformed Ward identities.
We will return to the question of contours, when we define the respective models in the subsections below. We would like mention here that in the specific models that we will consider later, there will always be a unique way to make this choice.

Remark 4.1
.

In general, it is possible to rescale the weight function
w(x)
by an arbitrary
q
-constant function

, i.e.

(4.5)

\tilde{w}(x)=c_{q}(x)w(x)\,,\hskip 30.0ptD_{x}^{q}c_{q}(x)=0\,,

See the discussion in
[
33
]
and in Appendix

-constant function does not affect normalized expectation values, so that we can drop the distinction between

Remark 4.2

4.2.
q,t
-Virasoro constraints

Similarly to the case of classical Hermitian matrix models, one can derive a set of linear identities satisfied by the correlation functions of the
q,t
-deformed ensembles. These correspond to the Ward identities of the matrix model and they are derived by inserting certain total
q
-difference operators in a way which is analogous to (

For an appropriate choice of difference operators, one is able to derive constraints which realize a

[

Here we rely on these results to derive the general form of

The existence of the constraints relies on the following property of the
q
-derivative.
For a contour
\mathcal{C}
and a function

such that, if for any pole of

that sits inside of
\mathcal{C}

is also inside of

(4.19)

\oint_{\mathcal{C}}\mathrm{d}x\,D_{x}^{q}f(x)=0\,.

The same idea can also be applied to Jackson integrals. In particular,

(4.20)

\int_{a}^{b}\mathrm{d}_{q}x\,D_{x}^{q}f(x)=f(b)-f(a)

so that, choosing
a
and
b
such that
f(a)=f(b)
, one obtains a vanishing integral.

We define the generating function of the expectation values of the
q,t
-deformed matrix model with weight function

(4.21)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathsf{Z}^{w}}\oint\prod_{i=1}^{N}\mathrm{d}x_{i}\,\Delta_{q,t}(\mathbf{x})\prod_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}^{\frac{\log t}{\log q}(N-1)}w(x_{i})\exp\Big(\sum_{k\geq 1}\dfrac{1-t^{k}}{1-q^{k}}\dfrac{p_{k}\,x_{i}^{k}}{k}\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big\langle\exp\Big(\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{1-t^{k}}{1-q^{k}}\frac{p_{k}}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}^{k}\Big)\Big\rangle^{w}=\sum_{\lambda}b_{\lambda}\Big\langle P_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x})\Big\rangle^{w}P_{\lambda}(\mathbf{p})\,.

The Virasoro constraints for generic models of type (

As we can see from (

-Virasoro constraints are invariant under a redefinition of

by a multiplicative

-constant function, since this contribution to the integrand simply cancels in the ratio
\frac{w(z)}{w(q^{-1}z)}
. Then we have the following.

Corollary 4.1
.

Let us consider two matrix models with weight functions
w_{1}(x)
and
w_{2}(x)
, respectively, such that
\frac{w_{1}(x)}{w_{2}(x)}=c_{q}(x)
is some

q,t
-Virasoro constraints, so that

(4.31)

\hat{\mathsf{U}}^{w_{1}}(z)=\hat{\mathsf{U}}^{w_{2}}(z)\,.

Moreover, if the space of solutions of these constraints is one dimensional, then these generating functions are equal,

(4.32)

\mathsf{Z}^{w_{1}}(\mathbf{p})=\mathsf{Z}^{w_{2}}(\mathbf{p})\,.

In the models considered in this paper, this situation indeed occurs. This corollary confirms the validity of the definitions introduced in the previous subsection, as it establishes that the Virasoro constraints in these models admit a unique solution. In particular, as noted above, replacing a contour integral with a Jackson integral entails modifying the weight function by a
q
-constant function. Since such a modification is absorbed by the normalization, the normalized expectation values remain unchanged.

Proposition 4.1
.

Suppose the potential

is such that

and

are polynomials of degree at most

Proof.

We will prove this statement case by case in the next section, by deriving explicitly the unique solution.

Note that for higher–degree polynomials, the solution space of the

5.
Superintegrability from recursions

In this section we prove superintegrability for the
q,t
-models introduced in the preceding section. Our strategy is to reformulate the problem in a purely algebraic framework. By combining the
q,t
-Virasoro constraints in an appropriate way, we are able to exploit specific properties of the quantum toroidal algebra
U_{q,t}(\hat{\hat{\mathfrak{gl}}}_{1})
to find closed formulas for the solutions.

Superintegrability of
q,t
-deformed matrix models was first conjectured in some examples in
[
10
,
25
]
and recently proved in the case of the
q,t
-Gaussian model using orthogonal polynomials techniques in
[
11
]
. Superintegrability of the rCS matrix model follows instead from earlier work of Cherednik on the Macdonald–Mehta identities
[
7
,
8
]
. Here we give an alternative proof based on the deformed Virasoro constraints, as well as new proofs for those cases which have not been considered before in the literature.

Just as in the case of classical matrix models, we first need to provide a definition.

Definition 5.1

(Macdonald superintegrability of refined matrix models)
.

A
q,t
-matrix model is said to be Macdonald superintegrable if there exist an operator
\hat{\mathsf{C}}^{w}:\Lambda\to\Lambda
diagonal on Macdonald functions,

(5.1)

\hat{\mathsf{C}}^{w}\cdot P_{\lambda}=\mathsf{C}_{\lambda}^{w}\,P_{\lambda}\,,\hskip 30.0pt\text{with eigenvalues}\hskip 30.0pt\mathsf{C}_{\lambda}^{w}=\prod_{(i,j)\in\lambda}g^{w}(i,j)\,,

for some function
g^{w}(i,j)
of the coordinates of the boxes in the partition, which depends on the choice of potential
w
, and a ring homomorphism
\varphi^{w}:\Lambda\to\mathbb{C}
, that acts as a specialization of the power sums to some
w
-dependent locus such that

(5.2)

\Big\langle f\Big\rangle^{w}=\varphi^{w}\left(\hat{\mathsf{C}}^{w}\cdot f(\mathbf{p})\right)\,,

for any symmetric function
f\in\Lambda
.

In particular, this means that the averages of Macdonald
polynomials in a superintegrable
q,t
-model can be written as

(5.3)

\Big\langle P_{\lambda}\Big\rangle^{w}=\varphi^{w}\left(\hat{\mathsf{C}}^{w}\cdot P_{\lambda}(\mathbf{p})\right)=\mathsf{C}^{w}_{\lambda}\,P_{\lambda}(p_{k}=\varphi^{w}_{k})\,,

where we denoted as
\varphi^{w}_{k}:=\varphi^{w}(p_{k})
the evaluation of the
k
-th power sum.
Equivalently, if we define the normalized Macdonald polynomials

(5.4)

\tilde{P}^{w}_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}):=\frac{P_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x})}{P_{\lambda}(p_{k}=\varphi^{w}_{k})}\,,

then superintegrability becomes

(5.5)

\Big\langle\tilde{P}^{w}_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x})\Big\rangle^{w}=\mathsf{C}^{w}_{\lambda}\,,

which one should regard as a version of a polynomial difference Fourier transform in the sense of
[
44
,
45
,
46
]
. In particular, we regard
\tilde{P}^{w}_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x})
as the (polynomial) kernel of the transform and the weight function
w(\mathbf{x})=\prod_{i}w(x_{i})
as the argument of the transform. The case of rCS, in this language, corresponds to the Fourier transform of the generalized Gaussian
w^{\mathrm{rCS}}(\mathbf{x})
.

Remark 5.1
.

In all known examples, the function
g^{w}(i,j)
is a function of the content (
2.2
) of the box only. The generating function
\mathsf{Z}^{w}(\mathbf{p})
can then be regarded as a specialization of a weighted
q,t
-tau function of the type recently considered in
[
47
]
.

With this definition at hand, we are now ready to state the main result of this paper.

5.1.
Summing up Virasoro constraints

Similarly to the case of the classical matrix models, the
q,t
-Virasoro constraints can be combined into a single equation, that can be used to determine the generating function uniquely. We start by presenting the general form of the resummed constraint equation. Next, we prove the announced results case by case. The proof uses the lemma below, which is one of the key technical result of this paper. It relies on a simple yet important idea, that one has to resum the Virasoro constraints, to bring them to a form, where they can be solved explicitly.
Yet, the resummation should be done in such a way that the resulting recursion operator has some “
nice
” algebraic properties, as we explain below.
Resumming the constraints as in the undeformed case in Section
3.3
, for example, does not result in a particularly useful operator, as discussed in
[
25
]
.

Lemma 5.1
.

The matrix model generating function
\mathsf{Z}^{w}(\mathbf{p})
satisfies the following equations for
d_{-}\leq 1+m-m_{0}
, where
m_{0}
is as in Lemma
4.1
:

(5.18)

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{l=0}^{d_{-}}M_{l}^{-}\Bigg\{(t/q)^{m-l}h_{m-l}(-\left(1-t^{-k}\right)p_{k})-\delta_{l,m}-Q^{-1}\left(q^{l-m}x^{-}_{l-m}-h_{l-m}((1-t^{k})p_{k}^{\perp})\right)\Bigg\}\right.

\displaystyle+qt^{-1}\sum_{l=0}^{d_{+}}M^{+}_{l}\Bigg\{\delta_{l,m}-(t/q)^{m-l}h_{m-l}((1-t^{-k})p_{k})

\displaystyle\left.+Q(t/q)^{m-l}\left(x^{+}_{l-m}-h_{l-m}(-(1-t^{k})p_{k}^{\perp})\right)\Bigg\}\right)\cdot\mathsf{Z}^{w}(\mathbf{p})=0

where
h_{n}
are the homogeneous symmetric functions regarded as functions of power sums
p_{k}
, as in (
2.8
), and
p_{k}^{\perp}
is the adjoint of
p_{k}
w.r.t. the Macdonald inner product.

Proof.

The proof is a direct computation.
Multiplying the constraints from the left, we obtain

(5.19)

\oint\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{2\pi\mathrm{i}z}z^{-m}\left\{\exp\left(-\sum_{k\geq 1}(1-t^{-k})(t/q)^{k}z^{k}\frac{p_{k}}{k}\right)-1\right\}\hat{\mathsf{U}}^{w}(z)\cdot\mathsf{Z}^{w}(\mathbf{p})=0\,.

According to Lemma
4.1
, the modes of
\hat{\mathsf{U}}^{w}(z)
starting with
\hat{\mathsf{U}}_{m_{0}}^{w}
annihilate the partition function. Since we are multiplying by an exponent that contains only positive powers of
z
, the resulting operator annihilates the partition function for
m\leq 1-m_{0}
. This condition guarantees that only those modes of the
q
-Virasoro constraints, that annihilate the partition function appear in the resulting operator.

Next we substitute the explicit form of
\hat{\mathsf{U}}^{w}(z)
into the expression and combine the exponents, some of which produce vertex operators for the currents of the quantum toroidal algebra. See Appendix
C
for details. This produces the equation

(5.20)

\displaystyle\oint\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{2\pi\mathrm{i}z}z^{-m}\Big(

\displaystyle M^{-}(z)\Big\{\exp\left(-\sum_{k\geq 1}(1-t^{-k})(t/q)^{k}z^{k}\frac{p_{k}}{k}\right)-1-Q^{-1}x^{-}(q^{-1}z)

\displaystyle+Q^{-1}\exp\left(\sum_{k\geq 1}(1-q^{k})z^{-k}\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{k}}\right)\Big\}

\displaystyle+qt^{-1}M^{+}(z)\Big\{1-Q\exp\left(-\sum_{k\geq 1}(1-q^{k})(t/q)^{-k}z^{-k}\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{k}}\right)

\displaystyle-\exp\left(\sum_{k\geq 1}(1-t^{-k})(t/q)^{k}z^{k}\frac{p_{k}}{k}\right)+Qx^{+}(tq^{-1}z)\Big\}\Big)\cdot\mathsf{Z}^{w}(\mathbf{p})=0

Taking the residue at zero extracts the
m
-th mode of the resulting operator and we obtain the equation in (
5.18
).
∎

Out of all the equations for each
m
we are going to be mainly interested in the lowest mode. Suppose for simplicity, that
d_{-}\geq d_{+}
, then, by taking
m=d_{-}
we obtain the following.

Corollary 5.1
.

When
d_{-}\leq 1-m_{0}
, the generating function satisfies the linear equation

(5.21)

\hat{\mathsf{A}}^{w}\cdot\mathsf{Z}^{w}(\mathbf{p})=0

where the recursion operator
\hat{\mathsf{A}}^{w}
is given by

(5.22)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathsf{A}}^{w}:=\sum_{l=0}^{d_{-}}M^{-}_{d_{-}-l}\Big\{Q^{-1}(\delta_{l,0}-q^{-l}x^{-}_{-l})+(t/q)^{l}h_{l}(-(1-t^{-k})p_{k})-\delta_{l,0}\Big\}

\displaystyle-qt^{-1}\sum_{l=0}^{d_{+}}M^{+}_{d_{+}-l}\Big\{Q(t/q)^{d_{-}-d_{+}+l}\left(\delta_{d_{+},d_{-}+l}-x^{+}_{d_{+}-d_{-}-l}\right)+h_{d_{-}-d_{+}+l}((1-t^{-k})(t/q)^{k}p_{k})-\delta_{d_{+},d_{-}+l}\Big\}\,.

This equation is one of the main results of this work. Below we discuss how to solve this equation explicitly once we specialize to the models defined in Section
4.1
. The condition
d_{-}\leq 1-m_{0}
appears, since we had
m\leq 1-m_{0}
and translates to either
d_{-}\leq 1
for generic
r
or
d_{-}\leq 2
, when
r=1
. This is another manifestation of the fact, that for potentials with higher
d_{-}
our approach should be modified and is the same issue as the one covered in
[
25
]
.

Similarly, if
d_{+}\geq d_{-}
, we can fix
m=d_{+}
in (
5.18
) and we obtain an analogous recursion operator, provided
d_{+}\leq 1-m_{0}
.

Remark 5.2
.

Since the recursion equation (
5.21
) is homogeneous, the recursion operator
\hat{\mathsf{A}}^{w}
is only defined up to an overall constant, which is in fact equivalent to the fact that the polynomials
M^{\pm}(z)
can be simultaneously rescaled by the same scalar factor without changing the
q,t
-Virasoro constraints in Lemma (
4.1
).
In what follows, the operators are presented in rescaled form, with the rescaling absorbed into their definitions.

5.2.
Refined Chern–Simons theory

5.2.1.
Proof of
(SI.1)

The refined Chern–Simons model is arguably the simplest example, which we will discuss in more detail. The techniques applied in this case will carry on almost directly to the other models.

Using the explicit expression for the weight function
w^{\mathrm{rCS}}(x)
, we obtain

(5.23)

M^{-}(z)=z\,,\hskip 30.0ptM^{+}(z)=rq^{\frac{1}{2}}\,.

Therefore, setting
d_{-}=1
in (
5.22
) and substituting (
5.23
) there, we obtain the recursion equation

(5.24)

\hat{\mathsf{A}}^{\mathrm{rCS}}\cdot\mathsf{Z}^{\mathrm{rCS}}(\mathbf{p})=0\,,

with

(5.25)

\hat{\mathsf{A}}^{\mathrm{rCS}}:=(1-x^{-}_{0})-(rq^{\frac{1}{2}}Qt^{-1}q)\left((1-t^{-1})(t/q)p_{1}-Q(t/q)x^{+}_{-1}\right)\,.

The recursion operator
\hat{\mathsf{A}}^{\mathrm{rCS}}
has two types of terms: of degree zero and degree one. Hence, if we were to expand the partition function as a series in
p_{k}
, the equation would lead to a recursion of step one on the coefficients of the expansion. One can show, that this recursion has a unique solution, which can be found using combinatorial identities. Instead of doing so, we would like to solve the equation algebraically. Note, that
r
enters the equation in a simple way, namely, we can reabsorb it by rescaling the variables as
p_{k}\mapsto r^{-k}p_{k}
, which corresponds to the action of the operator
r^{-\hat{D}}
.

To solve the equation, let us define the “
gauge transformation
” operator
\hat{G}_{\mathrm{rCS}}
as

(5.26)

\hat{G}_{\mathrm{rCS}}:=\Delta^{+}(Q)\mathsf{T}\exp\left(\sum_{k\geq 1}(rq^{\frac{1}{2}}Qt^{-1}q)^{k}\frac{p_{k}}{k(1-q^{k})}\right)\mathsf{T}^{-1}\Delta^{+}(Q)^{-1}\,.

The main idea is to use this operator, to show that the operator in (
5.24
) is conjugate to a much simpler one, whose kernel is one-dimensional and known explicitly. In particular, using relations from Lemma
C.1
and Corollary
C.1
we compute

(5.27)

\displaystyle\hat{G}_{\mathrm{rCS}}(1-x^{-}_{0})\hat{G}_{\mathrm{rCS}}^{-1}=

\displaystyle=\Delta^{+}(Q)\mathsf{T}\exp\left(\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{(rq^{\frac{1}{2}}Qt^{-1}q)^{k}p_{k}}{k(1-q^{k})}\right)(1-x^{-}_{0})\exp\left(-\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{(rq^{\frac{1}{2}}Qt^{-1}q)^{k}p_{k}}{k(1-q^{k})}\right)\mathsf{T}^{-1}\Delta^{+}(Q)^{-1}

\displaystyle=(1-x^{-}_{0})-(rq^{\frac{1}{2}}Qt^{-1}q)\left((1-t^{-1})(t/q)p_{1}-Q(t/q)x^{+}_{-1}\right)

which is exactly the recursion operator in (
5.24
).
Therefore we can prove the following.

Lemma 5.2
.

The equation (
5.24
) for the refined Chern–Simons generating function has a unique solution given by

(5.28)

\mathsf{Z}^{\mathrm{rCS}}(\mathbf{p})=\hat{G}_{\mathrm{rCS}}\cdot 1=\Delta^{+}(Q)\mathsf{T}\cdot\exp\left(\sum_{k\geq 1}(rq^{\frac{1}{2}}Qt^{-1}q)^{k}\frac{p_{k}}{k(1-q^{k})}\right)\,.

Proof.

The form of the solution follows directly from the calculation above. Taking into account the conjugation relation, we can write the recursion equation as

(5.29)

\hat{G}_{\mathrm{rCS}}\,(1-x^{-}_{0})\,\hat{G}_{\mathrm{rCS}}^{-1}\cdot\mathsf{Z}^{\mathrm{rCS}}(\mathbf{p})=0\,.

The operator
\hat{G}_{\mathrm{rCS}}
is invertible hence it has trivial kernel. Therefore
\hat{G}_{\mathrm{rCS}}^{-1}\cdot\mathsf{Z}^{\mathrm{rCS}}(\mathbf{p})
lies in the kernel of
(1-x^{-}_{0})
, which is one dimensional and consists of constant symmetric functions. This is because
1
is the eigenvalue of
x^{-}_{0}
when acting on the Macdonald polynomial
P_{\varnothing}\equiv 1
.
Using the fact that the generating function is normalized such that
\mathsf{Z}^{\mathrm{rCS}}(0)=1
, we find that
\hat{G}_{\mathrm{rCS}}^{-1}\cdot\mathsf{Z}^{\mathrm{rCS}}(\mathbf{p})=1
, which is equivalent to (
5.28
).
∎

Superintegrability of the rCS model then follows as a simple corollary of this lemma. From (
5.28
), we have

(5.30)

\mathsf{Z}^{\mathrm{rCS}}(\mathbf{p})=\Big\langle\exp\left(\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{1-t^{k}}{1-q^{k}}\frac{p_{k}}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}^{k}\right)\Big\rangle^{\mathrm{rCS}}=\Delta^{+}(Q)\mathsf{T}\cdot\exp\left(\sum_{k\geq 1}(rq^{\frac{1}{2}}Qt^{-1}q)^{k}\frac{p_{k}}{k(1-q^{k})}\right)

and by identifying the coefficients of
P_{\lambda}(\mathbf{p})
on both sides of the second equality, we obtain

(5.31)

\Big\langle P_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x})\Big\rangle^{\mathrm{rCS}}=\Delta^{+}_{\lambda}(Q)\,\mathsf{T}_{\lambda}\,P_{\lambda}\left(p_{k}=\frac{(rq^{\frac{1}{2}}Qt^{-1}q)^{k}}{1-t^{k}}\right)\,.

This proves point
(SI.1)
in Theorem
5.2
.

Remark 5.3
.

Let us now compare the superintegrability formula with the CMM identity (
4.17
) for
\mu=\varnothing
.
Using the five-term relation (
C.11
) and the identity (

(5.32)

\displaystyle\mathsf{Z}^{\mathrm{rCS}}(\mathbf{p})

\displaystyle=\mathsf{T}\Delta^{+}(Q)\exp\left(\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{p_{k}}{k(1-q^{k})}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{T}\exp\left(\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{p_{k}}{k(1-q^{k})}\right)\mathsf{T}\exp\left(\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{(-Q)^{k}p_{k}}{k(1-q^{k})}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{T}\exp\left(\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{1-Q^{k}}{1-q^{k}}\frac{p_{k}}{k}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda}b_{\lambda}\mathsf{T}_{\lambda}\,P_{\lambda}\left(p_{k}=\frac{1-Q^{k}}{1-t^{k}}\right)P_{\lambda}\,.

This implies that the expectation value of

can be written as

(5.33)

\Big\langle P_{\lambda}\Big\rangle^{\mathrm{rCS}}=\mathsf{T}_{\lambda}\,P_{\lambda}\left(p_{k}=\frac{1-Q^{k}}{1-t^{k}}\right)

which matches precisely with (

6.1.
Interpolation Macdonald polynomials and orthogonal polynomials for rCS

As observed in (

One may regard this as the definition of an Hermitian inner product on the space of symmetric polynomials in the variables
\mathbf{x}

(6.1)

It is not hard to see that this product is related to the so called Fourier/Hopf pairing

(P_{\lambda},P_{\mu})^{\mathrm{rCS}}=(\mathsf{T}\cdot P_{\lambda},\mathsf{T}\cdot P_{\mu})_{\rm F}\,,

where the Fourier/Hopf pairing has the property

(6.3)

(f,P_{\lambda})_{\rm F}=f(u_{\lambda})\,P_{\lambda}(u_{\varnothing})

for any symmetric function

and it was shown in
[
56
,
57
]
that they can be obtained from the usual Macdonald functions via an exponential operator as

(6.5)

in a similar way to how the classical orthogonal polynomials can be obtained from Schur/Jack via some exponential operators in the spirit of the Lassalle–Nekrasov correspondence

58

59
]
. From (

\mathsf{W}^{\mathrm{rCS}}_{\lambda}=(\hat{G}_{\mathrm{rCS}}^{\perp})^{-1}\cdot P_{\lambda}=P_{\lambda}+(\text{lower degree terms})\,.

Following the work of Olshanski in

, one can define a dual basis w.r.t. the ordinary Macdonald inner product which we denote as

6.2.
Orthogonal polynomials for models with matter

Given our previous discussion of the orthogonal polynomials for the rCS matrix models, we would like to generalize those ideas to other superintegrable models as well. For this reason, whenever the matrix model with weight function
w(x)
is Macdonald superintegrable as in Definition
5.1
, we are lead to define the following two families of symmetric functions,

(6.15)

\mathsf{W}^{w}_{\lambda}:=(\hat{G}_{w}^{\perp})^{-1}\cdot P_{\lambda}\,\hskip 30.0pt\mathsf{Z}^{w}_{\lambda}:=\hat{G}_{w}\cdot P_{\lambda}\,,

for
\hat{G}_{w}
defined as

(6.16)

\hat{G}_{w}:=\hat{\mathsf{C}}^{w}\cdot\exp\left(\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{1-t^{k}}{1-q^{k}}\frac{\varphi^{w}_{k}\,p_{k}}{k}\right)\cdot(\hat{\mathsf{C}}^{w})^{-1}\,,

which then implies

(6.17)

(\hat{G}_{w}^{-1})^{\perp}=(\hat{\mathsf{C}}^{w})^{-1}\cdot\exp\left(-\sum_{k\geq 1}\varphi^{w}_{k}\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{k}}\right)\cdot\hat{\mathsf{C}}^{w}\,.

The functions
\mathsf{W}^{w}_{\lambda}
are non-homogeneous polynomials of top degree
|\lambda|
, while
\mathsf{Z}^{w}_{\lambda}
are formal series of symmetric functions, whose lowest degree term has degree
|\lambda|
.

Following from the definition, we have the orthogonality relation and Cauchy identity

(6.18)

\langle\mathsf{W}^{w}_{\mu},\mathsf{Z}^{w}_{\lambda}\rangle_{q,t}=b_{\lambda}^{-1}\delta_{\lambda,\mu}\,,\hskip 30.0pt\exp\left(\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{1-t^{k}}{1-q^{k}}\frac{p_{k}(\mathbf{x})\,p_{k}(\mathbf{y})}{k}\right)=\sum_{\lambda}b_{\lambda}\,\mathsf{W}^{w}_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x})\,\mathsf{Z}^{w}_{\lambda}(\mathbf{y})\,,

as well as explicit formulas for the expansion in the Macdonald basis,

(6.19)

\displaystyle\mathsf{W}^{w}_{\mu}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda\subset\mu}\dfrac{\mathsf{C}^{w}_{\mu}}{\mathsf{C}^{w}_{\lambda}}P_{\mu/\lambda}\left(p_{k}=-\varphi^{w}_{k}\right)P_{\lambda}\,,

\displaystyle\mathsf{Z}^{w}_{\mu}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu\subset\lambda}\dfrac{\mathsf{C}^{w}_{\lambda}}{\mathsf{C}^{w}_{\mu}}P_{\lambda/\mu}\left(p_{k}=\varphi^{w}_{k}\right)P_{\lambda}\,,

and the eigenvalue equations

(6.20)

(\hat{G}_{w}^{\perp})^{-1}\,x_{0}^{+}\hat{G}_{w}^{\perp}\cdot\mathsf{W}^{w}_{\mu}=x_{\mu}\,\mathsf{W}^{w}_{\mu}\,,\hskip 30.0pt\hat{G}_{w}\,x_{0}^{+}\hat{G}_{w}^{-1}\cdot\mathsf{Z}^{w}_{\mu}=x_{\mu}\,\mathsf{Z}^{w}_{\mu}\,,

(6.21)

(\hat{G}_{w}^{\perp})^{-1}\,x_{0}^{-}\hat{G}_{w}^{\perp}\cdot\mathsf{W}^{w}_{\mu}=x_{\mu}^{\vee}\,\mathsf{W}^{w}_{\mu}\,,\hskip 30.0pt\hat{G}_{w}\,x_{0}^{-}\hat{G}_{w}^{-1}\cdot\mathsf{Z}^{w}_{\mu}=x_{\mu}^{\vee}\,\mathsf{Z}^{w}_{\mu}\,.

At this point we would like to claim that the
\mathsf{W}^{w}_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x})
are the orthogonal polynomials for the matrix model with weight function
w(x)
, however, computer experiments suggest that this is not true in general. In particular, we have checked that this seems to be the case only when the recursion operator
\hat{\mathsf{A}}^{w}
satisfies either

(6.22)

\hat{\mathsf{A}}^{w}=\hat{G}_{w}(1-x^{-}_{0})\hat{G}_{w}^{-1}\,,

or

(6.23)

\hat{\mathsf{A}}^{w}=\hat{G}_{w}(1-x^{+}_{0})\hat{G}_{w}^{-1}\,,

or perhaps a linear combination of the two.
When this is the case (e.g. in the models from
(SI.1)
to
(SI.6)
), we conjecture that the matrix model integral induces an inner product such that

(6.24)

\Big\langle\mathsf{W}_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x})\,\mathsf{W}_{\mu}(\mathbf{x})\Big\rangle^{w}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle?}}{{=}}b_{\lambda}^{-1}K^{w}_{\lambda}\delta_{\lambda,\mu}

for some model-dependent constant
K^{w}_{\lambda}
. We can then formally define the symmetric bilinear pairing

(6.25)

(f,g)^{w}:=\langle f,\hat{G}_{w}\cdot\hat{K}^{w}\cdot\hat{G}_{w}^{\perp}\cdot g\rangle_{q,t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle?}}{{=}}\Big\langle f(\mathbf{x})\,g(\mathbf{x})\Big\rangle^{w}

in analogy with the case of rCS in (
6.12
), where
\hat{K}^{w}
is diagonal on Macdonald functions with eigenvalue
K^{w}_{\lambda}
. The functions
\mathsf{W}^{w}_{\lambda}
are, by construction, mutually orthogonal w.r.t. this inner product.

We observe that, as a straightforward corollary, we have the following exact expression (which seems to be related to a property called strong superintegrability in
[
61
]
)

(6.26)

\displaystyle\Big\langle\mathsf{W}^{w}_{\mu}(\mathbf{x})\,P_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x})\Big\rangle^{w}

\displaystyle=b^{-1}_{\mu}K^{w}_{\mu}\frac{\mathsf{C}^{w}_{\lambda}}{\mathsf{C}^{w}_{\mu}}P_{\lambda/\mu}(p_{k}=\varphi_{k}^{w})

\displaystyle=b^{-1}_{\mu}\frac{K^{w}_{\mu}}{\mathsf{C}^{w}_{\mu}}\frac{P_{\lambda/\mu}(p_{k}=\varphi_{k}^{w})}{P_{\lambda}(p_{k}=\varphi_{k}^{w})}\Big\langle P_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x})\Big\rangle^{w}\,.

By the reproducing property of the Macdonald kernel, we also obtain

(6.27)

\Big\langle\!\!\Big\langle f(\mathbf{x})\Big\rangle\!\!\Big\rangle^{w}=\Big\langle f(\mathbf{x})\,\exp\left(\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{1-t^{k}}{1-q^{k}}\frac{p_{k}}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}^{k}\right)\Big\rangle^{w}=\hat{G}_{w}\hat{K}^{w}\hat{G}^{\perp}_{w}\cdot f

and in the case

, we find

(6.28)

\Big\langle\!\!\Big\langle\mathsf{W}^{w}_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x})\Big\rangle\!\!\Big\rangle^{w}=K^{w}_{\lambda}\,\mathsf{Z}^{w}_{\lambda}(\mathbf{p})\,.

For
\lambda=\varnothing
,

and one recovers the usual matrix model generating function (
4.21

In general, we expect that one should be able to prove the conjectural identity between
\mathsf{W}^{w}_{\lambda}
and the orthogonal polynomials by showing that the eigenoperators
(\hat{G}_{w}^{\perp})^{-1}\,x_{0}^{\pm}\hat{G}_{w}^{\perp}
are Hermitian w.r.t. the weight

In the case of the

62

and, as we show explicitly in Appendix

does not lead to a Macdonald operator. Let us consider the

Appendix A
Jackson integrals and contour integrals

The matrix models discussed in the paper admit two equivalent representations: one as a contour integral and the other as a Jackson integral. Here we show that the latter can be transformed into the contour integral form by inserting an appropriate
q
-constant function.

Define the function
\gamma_{q}(x)
as

(A.1)

\gamma_{q}(x):=\exp\left(-\dfrac{\log^{2}(-x)}{2\log q}\right)\frac{x^{1/2}}{\theta_{q}(x)}

where

(A.2)

\theta_{q}(x):=(q;q)_{\infty}(x;q)_{\infty}(qx^{-1};q)_{\infty}

is the Jacobi theta function.
The function
\gamma_{q}(x)
is a
q
-constant, i.e.

(A.3)

\gamma_{q}(qx)=\gamma_{q}(x)\,\quad\Rightarrow\quad D^{q}_{x}\gamma_{q}(x)=0

Then, the following equality allows us to transform a contour integral into a Jackson integral:

(A.4)

\int_{\mathcal{C}_{q,a}}\mathrm{d}x\,\gamma_{q}(qxa^{-1})f(x)=-\dfrac{\exp{\left(\dfrac{\pi^{2}}{2\log q}\right)}}{(q,q)_{\infty}^{3}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(q^{n}a)f(q^{n}a)=\kappa_{q}\int_{0}^{a}\mathrm{d}_{q}xf(x)

where
\mathcal{C}_{q,a}
is the contour that goes around the poles at
x=q^{n}a
, for
n\geq 0
, coming from zeros of the theta function and avoids any poles of
f(x)
, if any.

Therefore, we define the following two functions:

(A.5)

\displaystyle\gamma_{q}(x|a)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\kappa_{q}}\gamma_{q}(qxa^{-1})\,,

\displaystyle\gamma_{q}(x|a,b)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\kappa_{q}}\Big(\gamma_{q}(qxb^{-1})-\gamma_{q}(qxa^{-1})\Big)\,.

Both functions are
q
-constant, since they involve only an argument rescaling compared to
\gamma_{q}(x)
. With these definitions, we have that

(A.6)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{C}_{q,a}}\mathrm{d}x\,\gamma_{q}(x|a)f(x)=\int_{0}^{a}\mathrm{d}_{q}xf(x)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{C}_{q,a,b}}\mathrm{d}x\,\gamma_{q}(x|a,b)f(x)=\int_{a}^{b}\mathrm{d}_{q}xf(x)

Here
\mathcal{C}_{q,a,b}
goes around the respective poles of both
\gamma_{q}(qxa^{-1})
and
\gamma_{q}(qxb^{-1})
.

Appendix B
Semiclassical limit

In this section we consider the semiclassical limit, which is defined by setting

(B.1)

q=\mathrm{e}^{\hbar}\,,\hskip 30.0ptt=\mathrm{e}^{\hbar\beta}

and then taking the limit

(B.2)

\hbar\rightarrow 0

This limit is well understood at the level of superintegrability formulas for most of the examples that we study in this paper. After reviewing some of these results, we will explain how the standard cut-and-join/
W
-operator equations are reproduced from our recursions equations, in this limit.
At the level of the algebra, we expect that elements of the quantum toroidal algebra degenerate to elements of the affine Yangian
Y(\hat{\mathfrak{gl}_{1}})

[
65
]
.

Throughout this section we will use the
N_{\mathrm{f}}=1
model as our main example, however most of the formulas here generalize to other models as well (with the exception of rCS).

First, it is important to recall that the semiclassical limit can be taken at the level of the integrand of the matrix model. We will sketch how it works in some examples. There are many caveats, as, for example, the possibility to take the limits in different ways to obtain new matrix models
[
66
]
, which we will not discuss here.

Consider, for example, the model with
(N_{\mathrm{f}},N_{\bar{\mathrm{f}}})=(1,0)
and
\ell=0
in its Jackson integral formulation. To take the limit, we fix
u_{1}=-(1-q^{-1})a_{1}
and
r=q^{\alpha}
. Then, the potential becomes

(B.3)

\lim_{q\to 1}x^{\frac{\log r}{\log q}}(-q(1-q^{-1})a_{1}x;q)_{\infty}=x^{\alpha}\exp(-a_{1}x)\,.

At the same time the Jackson integral becomes an ordinary integral, and taking into account, that the upper limit of integration goes to
\infty
under the limit, we obtain
