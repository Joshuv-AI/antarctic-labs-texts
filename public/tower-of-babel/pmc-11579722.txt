Saturate Acts as Both a “Lubricant” and an “Activator” during the Conversion Process of Mesophase in Fluid Catalytic Cracking Slurry Oil
Authors: Gen Liao, Kui Shi, Chong Ye, Zhen Fan, Tongqi Li, Feng Qian, Dong Huang, Fei Han, Hongbo Liu, Jinshui Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractSaturate (Sa), the lightest component in fluid catalytic cracking (FCC) slurry oil, exhibits a poorly understood influence on the formation and development of mesophase, thereby constraining the production of premium-quality mesophase pitch. To address this issue, Sa is isolated from FCC slurry oil, and its concentration is modulated to investigate its impact on the formation and development of mesophase. The results indicate that Sa contains a high concentration of long alkane side chains and naphthenic structures, which render it an effective “lubricant” and “activator” within the reaction system. The addition of a small amount of Sa (≤5 wt %) maintains a low viscosity state of the reaction system, further facilitating the migration of aromatic molecules and promoting mesophase formation. It is crucial to note that excessive amounts of Sa (>10 wt %) significantly reduce the aromaticity and reactivity of the reaction system, further delaying the formation of mesophase. The reaction system exhibits moderate aromaticity and reactivity with low viscosity at 10 wt % Sa, leading to the formation of mesophase pitch with a moderate softening point (270 °C) and a wide-area optical texture (anisotropy length >60 μm, width >10 μm) within a short time of 6 h. This work establishes a robust theoretical foundation for producing premium-quality mesophase pitch through the regulation of raw material components.

Introduction

Mesophase pitch is a liquid crystalline material characterized by nematic order, composed of disc-shaped polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons with a molecular weight range from 370 to m/z 2000. Due to its high aromaticity, substantial carbon content, and diverse sources of raw materials, mesophase pitch serves as a valuable precursor for producing advanced carbon materials, including pitch-based carbon fibers, mesophase carbon microspheres, carbon foams, and needle coke, among others.1−5 It is extensively utilized in the aerospace, national defense and military, medical machinery, and electrical/electronic fields.6,7

Various factors, such as external magnetic and electric fields, additives, and thermal polymerization conditions, greatly influence the performance of the mesophase pitch. The formation of mesophase pitch is predominantly influenced by the properties of the raw materials, outweighing other factors in terms of significance,8−10 because the molecular structure of raw materials is the determinant of their reactivity. The varying reactivity among different molecular species in the raw materials significantly affects both the quantity and the dimensions of the mesophase spheres.

Currently, raw materials such as coal-based pitch, naphthalene-based pitch, and petroleum-based pitch are commonly used for preparing mesophase pitch. The coal-based pitch has a planar polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon molecular structure (discoid molecule)11 and experiences a sharp increase in viscosity during thermal polymerization due to its limited number of alkyl branches and high aromaticity, further affecting the growth and coalescence of mesophase spheres. The mesophase pitch prepared from a coal-based pitch usually has mosaic optical texture and high softening point, making it difficult to prepare high-performance carbon materials. Naphthalene-based pitch is typically a polymer with linear structure composed of rodlike molecules.12 Due to the catalytic polymerization process in preparation of the mesophase pitch, its large-scale production is limited due to high equipment requirement and a low safety factor. Petroleum-based pitch as a byproduct of crude oil processing includes heavy residue pitch, catalytic cracking (FCC) slurry pitch, and ethylene tar pitch, and it contains more alkyl side chains compared with coal-based pitch.13 This structure makes petroleum-based pitch exhibit a higher reactivity and lower viscosity during thermal polymerization. Consequently, the obtained mesophase pitch typically shows a low softening point and excellent optical texture.

FCC slurry oil is a commonly used raw material for preparing a mesophase pitch. However, the complex composition makes it challenging to regulate the performance of the mesophase pitch. FCC slurry oil can be divided into four components based on the molecule polarity: saturate (Sa), aromatic, resin, and asphaltene, whereby mesophase pitch with excellent performance can be produced by adjusting the proportion of the four components.14 Koral et al. identified a correlation between the molecular architecture of mesophase and the concentration of aromatic hydrocarbons in the feedstock.15 The higher aromatic hydrocarbon content led to a striped or clubbed molecular structure of the mesophase. The polymerization and mesophase formation rate were increased because of the higher aromaticity in the raw material. However, higher aromaticity also caused an increase in the mosaic texture in the pyrolysis product. It was found that a suitable amount of resin could promote the formation of mesophase with wide-area optical texture, but excessive resin was detrimental.16 Kumar et al. discovered that excessive asphaltene hindered the formation of mesophase and led to a mosaic-type optical texture.17

Saturate is the lightest component in the FCC slurry oil. Marques et al. characterized its structure and concluded that it is composed of linear side chains and cyclic hydrocarbon molecules,18 including paraffin-like and cycloalkane compounds.1,2 Abrahamson et al. proposed that the thermal polymerization reaction system can maintain a low-viscosity state with an appropriate amount of light components and short alkane side chains, thus increasing the collision frequency between molecules.19,20 Özel and Bartle utilized supercritical methods to decrease the high-activity saturated alkane components in raw materials21 and obtained needle coke with higher orientation optical texture.

Previous studies primarily focused on the analysis of the light components or alkane side chains in feedstocks. Saturate, as a lightweight component of FCC slurry oil, plays a significant role in the formation of the mesophase. However, the exact function of saturate in the conversion process of mesophase remains unclear. Therefore, this paper aims to separate saturate from FCC slurry oil using column chromatography and investigate the effect of saturate content in the conversion process of mesophase. Ultimately the optimal saturate ratio for producing premium-quality mesophase pitch was determined. The comprehensive study offers a solid theoretical framework that supports the development of a premium-quality mesophase pitch through the deliberate adjustment of individual components within FCC slurry oil.

Experimental Section

Raw Materials

The primary material utilized in this article is refined FCC slurry oil sourced from China Petroleum and Chemical Corporation. This oil consists of a specific blend of components: 13.6 wt % saturates, 68.1 wt % aromatics, 16.9 wt % resins, and 1.4 wt % asphaltenes. Its fundamental physicochemical attributes are detailed as follows: the elemental composition includes carbon (91.12 wt %), hydrogen (6.98 wt %), nitrogen (0.53 wt %), and sulfur (0.75 wt %). Additionally, it possesses a density of 1143.3 kg/m3 and an ash content less than 20 ppm.

Separation of Saturate

The FCC slurry oil underwent complete extraction using a Soxhlet extractor with n-heptane as the solvent at an oil-to-solvent ratio (moil: Vn-heptane) of 1:40, resulting in the separation of the filter residue (asphaltene) and filtrate. The filtrate was concentrated to obtain deasphalted oil. Utilizing the SARA analysis technique, the deasphalted oil was fractionated into its components of saturate, aromatic, and resin by sequentially passing it through a column packed with neutral alumina (100–200 mesh) and employing distinct solvents: initially n-heptane, followed by toluene, and finally ethanol. The remaining Sa-free components were uniformly mixed and defined as FCC-exSa.

Thermal Polymerization Process

Five experimental groups were conducted by adding 0, 2.5, 5, 10, and 20 wt % Sa to FCC-exSa, respectively, and mixing uniformly. A total of 50 g of the mixed slurry oil was subjected to thermal polymerization in the reaction furnace. During the thermal polymerization process, a continuous nitrogen flow rate of 600 mL/min was sustained at atmospheric pressure, while consistent stirring ensured thorough mixing of FCC-exSa and Sa throughout the procedure. The mixed slurry oil was heated at a gradual rate of 3 °C/min until it reached 420 °C, where it was maintained for a duration of 2 to 7 h. During this period, samples were withdrawn from the reaction vessel every half hour, and their optical texture was examined under a polarization microscope. Characterization

The pyrolysis products underwent sequential extraction processes using n-heptane, toluene, and pyridine to isolate distinct fractions: the n-heptane soluble fraction (HS), the n-heptane insoluble toluene soluble fraction (HI-TS), the toluene insoluble pyridine soluble fraction (TI-PS), and finally, the pyridine insoluble fraction (PI).

The optical texture of the samples was visually inspected by utilizing a polarization microscope (Olympus BX53M). Subsequently, Image-Pro Plus software was employed to quantitatively assess the anisotropic properties of these samples, adhering to the guidelines stipulated in national standard GB/T38396-2019 based on the optical microscopic imagery. The elemental composition of the samples, specifically the concentrations of carbon (C), hydrogen (H), nitrogen (N), sulfur (S), and others, was precisely determined using an element vario macro cube. The softening point of the pyrolysis products was ascertained by utilizing a capillary rheometer (CFT-100EX). The process involved milling the sample and then compressing 1.5 g of the resulting powder into a solid cylindrical shape within a mold. The mold, designed with an inner diameter and a length of precisely 1.0 cm, was subjected to an applied load of 10 kg. Subsequently, the cylinder-shaped specimen was inserted into the material chamber of the capillary rheometer and subjected to gradual heating at a rate of 5 °C/min from 30 °C. As the temperature increased, the moment when the sample began to extrude through the die orifice was recorded, and this corresponding temperature was designated as the softening point. The residual carbon content of the pyrolysis products was quantified utilizing a thermogravimetric analyzer (NETZSCH STA449-F5), adhering to specific experimental parameters: a sample of 10 to 15 mg was subjected to a temperature increase from 20 to 1000 °C at a steady rate of 5 °C/min, while maintaining a constant nitrogen flow rate of 20 mL/min throughout the process.

To analyze the infrared spectroscopic properties (FT-IR) of the samples, a Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Nicolet iS10) was employed. The samples were mixed with KBr in a 1:100 ratio, pulverized, and subsequently compressed into a thin slice for examination. The spectral scan ranged from 4000 to 400 cm–1 and was executed 32 times with a spectral resolution of 4 cm–1. According to relevant studies,22,23 distinct absorption peaks observed at 2960, 2920, 2870, 2850, 1460, and 1380 cm–1 are indicative of stretching and bending vibrations associated with alkyl methylene and methyl groups. The spectral region between 910 and 720 cm–1 signifies the out-of-plane aromatic C–H bond vibrations, while the peak at 3050 cm–1 corresponds to the stretching vibrations of C–H bonds within the aromatic nucleus. The C=C bonds within aromatic rings exhibit stretching vibrations, represented by the absorption peak spanning 1670 to 1600 cm–1. The Ios index, a metric for quantifying the proportion of adjacent substituents (particularly four neighboring hydrogen atoms) on an aromatic ring, is calculated using the formula Ios = Abs750/ (Abs750 + Abs814 + Abs840 + Abs880). The magnitude of Ios serves as an indicator of the size of aromatic molecules, with higher values pointing toward larger molecular structures. The Iar (aromaticity index) is derived from the ratio Abs3050/(ABs3050 + Abs2920), providing a quantitative measure of the aromatic character of the molecule. Meanwhile, the alkyl index R, which reflects the extent of alkyl side chains attached to polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, is calculated by utilizing the CH2 absorption peak at 1460 cm–1 and the CH3 absorption peak at 1380 cm–1. Specifically, R is determined by the formula R = 3.07 × Abs1460/Abs1380 −3.72. An increase in the R value corresponds to a longer alkyl side chain attached to the molecule.

The hydrogen distribution of the samples was investigated using a nuclear magnetic resonance spectrometer (Bruker Avance DMX-500). The sample was thoroughly ground to create a solution with concentration of 10 mg/mL, employing chloroform (CDCl3) as the solvent and tetramethylsilane as the internal reference. Subsequently, the distribution of molecular weights within the samples was analyzed through matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (Bruker Daltonics AB SCIEX-58000).

The microcrystalline structure of the samples was examined by utilizing a X-ray diffractometer (Bruker D8 advance) after meticulous grinding. The diffractometer performed a continuous scan from 10 to 80° (2θ) at a scanning rate of 5°/min using Cu Kα (λ = 0.15406 nm) as the emission source. The ensuing X-ray diffraction patterns were analyzed to calculate the microcrystalline structural parameters, adhering to both the Bragg equation and the Scherrer formula.24,25

Among these parameters, λ represents the wavelength of the emission source, β002 is the full width at half-maximum of the (002) peak, k is the shape factor (0.89), θ002 is the Bragg diffraction angle of the (002) crystal plane, d002 is the interlayer spacing, Lc is the microcrystalline stacking height, and n is the number of microcrystalline stacking layers.

The degree of structural order of the samples was analyzed using laser Raman spectroscopy (Thermo Scientific DXR2) with Nd laser as the light source emitting at the wavelength of 532 nm and the laser energy of 3.2 mW. The G peak, situated around 1625 cm–1, signifies the presence of ordered structures in the mesophase pitch, whereas the D peak, located approximately at 1350 cm–1, represents disordered structures. The extent of disorder within the mesophase pitch can be quantitatively assessed by calculating the ratio of the integrated intensities of these two peaks, ID/IG. A higher value of this ratio corresponds to a greater degree of disorder within the mesophase pitch.26

Results and Discussion

Characterization and Optical Texture Analysis of Pyrolysis Products

The extent of the reaction can be reflected by examining the optical texture of the pyrolysis products. Figure 2 illustrates the changes in the optical texture over time during the thermal polymerization of FCC slurry oil with varying Sa contents. Mesophase spheres with diameter of 1–5 μm can be observed after reacting 4 h in the absence of Sa. The majority is transformed into bulk mesophase after reacting 5.5 h except for some remaining isotropic pitch component. When 2.5 wt % of Sa is added, mesophase spheres with size of 10–30 μm are observed after reacting 3.5 h. Some of these mesophase spheres begin to coalesce and form the bulk mesophase after reacting 4.5 h. Most of the raw material is converted to the bulk mesophase after reacting for 5.5 h, leaving only a small amount of isotropic pitch component, which is significantly less than that shown in Figure 2(a5).

Series of polarized light microscopic images of the pyrolysis products with different Sa contents: (a) 0, (b) 2.5, (c) 5, (d) 10, and (e) 20 wt %.

Following the incorporation of 5 wt % of Sa, an abundant presence of mesophase spheres, with diameters ranging from 40 to 100 μm, emerges within 3.5 h. The mesophase spheres coalesce to form bulk mesophase after reacting for 4.5 h, and the anisotropy content of the pyrolysis product is close to 100% after reacting for 5.5 h. However, the diameter and content of mesophase spheres in the pyrolysis product with adding 10 wt % Sa [Figure 2(d1)] are significantly smaller than those in Figure 2(c1). The mesophase spheres coalesce but a large number of isotropic pitch components are still presented after reacting 5 h. When the addition of Sa is 20 wt %, the reaction rate is significantly slower than that of other experimental groups. No mesophase spheres are observed after reacting 4 h, and a large number of mesophase spheres with 30–60 μm diameter are formed after reacting 5.5 h. The optical texture analysis of pyrolysis products reveals that a low concentration of Sa (<5 wt %) accelerates the formation and development of mesophase, while a high concentration of Sa (>5 wt %) shows the opposite trend.

To enable a comparison of the reactivity of FCC slurry oil with varying Sa contents, the pyrolysis products with a thermal polymerization time of 4.5 h are selected for characterization in each experiment group. The pyrolysis products are labeled as P–Sa–x, where x represents the Sa content in FCC slurry oil. Five groups of pyrolysis products are labeled as P–Sa–0, P–Sa–2.5, P–Sa–5, P–Sa–10, and P–Sa–20, respectively. Figure 3a illustrates that the softening point and anisotropy content of pyrolysis products initially increase but then decrease with the increase of Sa content, and the maximum value is observed in P–Sa–5 (softening point: 258 °C, anisotropy content: 86%).

(a) Anisotropic content, softening point, and (b) subfractions content of pyrolysis products.

Figure 3b shows the change trend of the subfraction content of pyrolysis products. The addition of Sa results in a decrease followed by an increase in the light component (HS and HI-TS) content. The contents of HS and HI-TS are the lowest in P–Sa–5, which are 4.19 and 13.34 wt %, respectively. However, there is an opposite trend in the heavy components (TI-PS and PI), and the maximum value appears in P–Sa–5. The contents of TI-PS and PI are 37.24 and 45.23 wt %, respectively. During thermal polycondensation, the reaction extent intensifies as alkane side chains undergo cracking, leading to increased cyclization and aromatization, which transforms the light components (HS and HI-TS) into the heavy components (TI-PS and PI).27 The progression of pyrolysis product subfractions indicates that a minimal presence of Sa (0–5 wt %) in FCC slurry oil accelerates the conversion of light components (HS and HI-TS) toward heavy components (TI-PS and PI). However, an excessive concentration of Sa (5–20 wt %) acts as a hindrance, limiting the conversion of HS and HI-TS into TI-PS and PI.

As shown in Table 1, the C content and carbon residue values of the pyrolysis products initially exhibit an upward trend, followed by a decline, directly correlating with the increasing Sa content. Notably, the peak values are achieved by P–Sa–5, with 94.12 wt % for C content and 68.44 wt % for the carbon residue. Conversely, the H content and H/C atomic ratio show the opposite trend. The contents of N and S remained relatively constant. Elemental analysis indicates that P–Sa–5 has the highest aromatic hydrocarbon condensation degree because small aromatic molecules undergo thermal polymerization to form large-sized polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons with high condensation degree. Moreover, during the thermal polycondensation process, a small amount of Sa (0–5 wt %) promotes the dehydrogenation condensation reaction, which is conducive to form polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons with large molecular weight and high condensation degree. However, excessive Sa (5–20 wt %) has the opposite effect, which is consistent with the result of optical texture analysis of pyrolysis products.

Chemical Kinetic Analysis

The aromatic molecules undergo a series of complex chemical reactions during thermal polymerization which changes the solubility of the generated mesophase molecules.28 The reaction kinetics in the thermal polymerization process are studied to investigate the effect of Sa on the reaction process. A kinetic model of mesophase formation is developed based on two assumptions: (1) the mesophase pitch formation process follows a first-order reaction and (2) the increase of pyridine insoluble (PI) contents represent the rate of mesophase formation.29,30 A function reflecting the progress of the intermolecular reaction in the system is established from the PI content and the thermal polymerization time, as shown in Figure 4 and Table 2. FCC-Sa-5 exhibits the fastest reaction rate, with a reaction rate constant of 2.04 × 10–3 min–1. The reaction rate constants of FCC-Sa-0, FCC-Sa-2.5, FCC-Sa-10, and FCC-Sa-20 are 1.36 × 10–3 min–1, 1.76 × 10–3 min–1, 1.71 × 10–3 min–1, and 0.95 × 10–3 min–1, respectively. It demonstrates the significant influence of Sa on the formation and development of the mesophase. A small amount of Sa promotes the formation of the mesophase, while an excessive amount of Sa suppresses its formation. The results of the reaction kinetics are in agreement with those of the optical texture analysis and the subfractions content of pyrolysis products.

Molecular Structure Analysis of Feedstocks and Pyrolysis Products

The molecular structure of feedstocks (Sa and FCC-exSa) is analyzed to investigate the influence mechanism of Sa on the formation and development of the mesophase. Table 1 shows that FCC-exSa has a higher carbon content and carbon residue value, while Sa has a larger H/C ratio and almost no heteroatoms N and S. Based on the data presented in Figure 5a,b, and Tables S2 and S5, it is evident that the molecules in Sa are nonaromatic, small in size, and contain a small amount of Hα, a certain amount of HN, and a large amount of Hβ and Hγ, indicating the presence of naphthenic structures and long alkyl side chains in the molecule of Sa. Generally, the naphthenic structures and alkyl side chains in feedstock are crucial to the formation of mesophase. Naphthenic hydrogen can act as a hydrogen donor during the thermal polymerization process, providing hydrogen transfer and maintaining the reactivity of the system. The long alkyl side chain contributes to the low viscosity of the reaction system, which is conducive to the ordered arrangement of planar aromatic hydrocarbons.31 Therefore, it is evident from FT-IR and 1H NMR that an appropriate amount of Sa can maintain the reactivity and low viscosity of the system, which helps the formation of the mesophase.

The trend of the 1H NMR structural parameters of pyrolysis products with different Sa contents (Figure 6d and Table S6) is analyzed by combining the 1H NMR spectra (Figure 6b) and the structural parameters (Table S5). However, the reaction rate of thermal polycondensation is decreased with the excess Sa of >5 wt %, further prolonging the time of mesophase formation.

Effect of Sa on the Optical Texture and Microcrystalline Structure of the Mesophase Pitch

Mesophase pitch with anisotropy content close to 100% is selected, and its optical texture and microcrystalline structure are analyzed to clarify the effect of Sa on the mesophase pitch properties. The samples are labeled as MP-Sa-x-y h, where x and y represent the Sa content and thermal polymerization time, respectively. The samples selected for analysis are labeled as MP-Sa-0–6.5 h, MP-Sa-2.5–6 h, MP-Sa-5–5.5 h, MP-Sa-10–6 h, and MP-Sa-20–8 h. According to the classification of mesophase optical texture, it can be seen from the polarizing micrograph (Figure 7) that MP-Sa-0–6.5 h has coarse-mosaic optical texture with an anisotropic radius between 5 and 10 μm. MP-Sa-2.5–6 h presents a medium-streamlined optical texture with an anisotropic length of <30 μm and width of <5 μm. MP-Sa-5–5.5 h and MP-Sa-20–8 h exhibit wide-streamlined optical texture with an anisotropic length of 30–60 μm and width of 5–10 μm. MP-Sa-10–6 h shows wide-area streamlined optical texture with an anisotropic length of >60 μm and width of >10 μm.

Mechanism Schematic Diagram of Sa in the Process of Mesophase Formation

Hydrocarbons are subjected to chain cleavage reaction (generating hydrocarbons with low-molecular weight and low-boiling point) and condensation reaction (generating polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons with high-molecular weight) at high temperature.35 It can be explained by the characteristics of thermal polymerization of hydrocarbons and the theory of free radical chain reaction:36 alkane side chain: (1) for alkanes with the same number of carbon atoms, the C–H bond energy is larger than the C–C bond energy, thus leading to the easier occurrence of chain cleavage reaction compared with the dehydrogenation reaction; (2) the longer alkane side chain is more prone to the chain cleavage reaction; and (3) alkane chains containing branched chains are in favor of the chain cleavage reaction. Naphthenic structure: (1) the alkane side chain is easier to crack than the naphthenic structure; (2) dehydrogenation of naphthenic structure to form aromatics is easier to happen than the ring-opening reaction to produce olefins. Aromatic structure: the aromatic structure cannot be easily cracked to generate small molecular olefins. Instead, it can undergo only dehydrogenation condensation reactions to generate polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, which eventually lead to coking.

There are studies with respect to the thermal decomposition reaction of n-butylbenzene under pressure, it is found that styrene is the main product at the initial stage of the reaction.35 The formation of styrene is caused by the cleavage of the C2–C3 bond on the alkyl substituent, and then the hydrogen radical is rapidly captured to convert into styrene ethane. It is evident that the alkane chains usually preferentially generate free radicals before C2–C3 bond cleavage in the thermal decomposition reaction of hydrocarbons containing long alkane side chains. Conclusions

The effect of Sa on the formation and development of the mesophase was investigated by controlling its content in FCC slurry oil in this paper. The results indicate that Sa is rich in long alkane side chains and cycloalkane structures. Sa with small amount (≤5 wt %) can act as a “lubricant” and “activator” in the reaction system, thus promoting molecule migration and accelerating mesophase formation. An amount of Sa greater than 10 wt % results in low reactivity due to a significant decrease in the aromaticity of the reaction system, which slows the formation of the mesophase. Furthermore, when the reaction system contains 10 wt % Sa, the activity of reaction system is moderate to form a high-quality mesophase pitch (d002 = 3.486, Lc = 19.312 Å, R = 0.6040) with a wide-area streamlined optical structure (anisotropic length >60 μm, width >10 μm) and a softening point of 270 °C in a short time (t = 6 h). This work provides a theoretical framework for regulating the properties of FCC slurry oil and preparing high-quality mesophase pitch.
