Blood multiomics reveal a dysregulated lipid mediator-mitochondrial network associated with the outcome of advanced cirrhosis
Authors: Cristina López-Vicario, Ferran Aguilar, Fleur Chapus, Carlos de la Peña-Ramirez, François Fenaille, Florence Castelli, Marta Duran-Güell, Bryan J Contreras, Berta Romero-Grimaldo, María Belén Sánchez-Rodríguez, Laura Valls-Roca, Glòria Garrabou, Sara Palomino-Echeverria, Estefania Huergo, Cristina Sanchez-Garrido, Wim Laleman, David Gómez-Cabrero, Alberto Queiroz Farias, Sebastián Marciano, Paolo Caraceni, Jonel Trebicka, Javier Fernández, Christophe Junot, Esther Titos, Richard Moreau, Vicente Arroyo, Pierre-Emmanuel Rautou, Joan Clària
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe mechanisms driving the progression from acutely decompensated (AD) cirrhosis to acute-on-chronic liver failure (ACLF), a high mortality condition, remain poorly understood. In this study, we integrate multiomics and clinical data from 766 AD patients to construct multiscale, multifactorial response networks associated with two major outcomes: ACLF development and death. Among 291 features, 22 are linked to ACLF development and 16 to mortality. These features constitute a network connecting mitochondrial dysfunction with the accumulation of the lipid mediator 20-hydroxyeicosatetraenoic acid (20-HETE). In vitro validation of this network in human peripheral leukocytes shows that 20-HETE induces mitochondrial oxidative stress and impairs mitochondrial respiration via a GPR75-Akt signaling pathway. Network features also act as early predictors of AD outcomes, a finding validated in an independent cohort of 580 patients. Here, we show a strong link between dysregulated immunomodulatory lipid mediators and mitochondrial dysfunction driving ACLF development and mortality risk in advanced cirrhosis.Subject terms: Liver cirrhosis, Molecular medicine, Biomarkers

Introduction

Episodes of acute decompensation, defined by the development of large ascites, hepatic encephalopathy, gastrointestinal bleeding, bacterial infection, or any combination of these, are extremely common in patients with cirrhosis1,2. The disease course of acutely decompensated (AD) cirrhosis is further aggravated by impairment in the function of extrahepatic organs, including the kidneys, lungs, and brain, and circulatory and coagulation systems, a clinical entity associated with high short-term mortality and known as acute-on-chronic liver failure (ACLF)1,2. In recent years, it has been demonstrated that cirrhosis severity, development of ACLF, and its poor clinical outcome are closely associated with the intensity of systemic inflammation3,4. The hyperinflammatory response in cirrhosis is a dynamic feature and develops as early as the compensated stage, but it is most prominent in severe and later phases of AD cirrhosis in which the clinical conditions mimic a sepsis-like state that can culminate in the development of ACLF1. Furthermore, these patients bear a high risk of infections because the host immune system against pathogens is severely impaired, which along with systemic hyperinflammation, is part of the so-called cirrhosis-associated immune dysfunction5,6.

While the burden of AD cirrhosis is higher than that of other non-communicable diseases7,8, the identification of the key molecular mechanisms underlying systemic immune dysfunction in ACLF represents an unmet need to fully understand its pathophysiology. Furthermore, patients with AD cirrhosis present different etiologies and large inter-individual variability of precipitating events and clinical presentations in addition to the influence of factors such as age, sex, and ancestry9,10. Therefore, the existing heterogeneity of patients with AD cirrhosis calls for the use of high-throughput omics technologies that enable the collection of large molecular datasets, whose comprehensive analysis may lead to the identification of drivers and signatures associated with this condition.

Since small, secreted proteins and lipids and low-molecular weight metabolites play a major role in immunometabolic and inflammatory processes, in this study, we integrated targeted lipidomics, untargeted metabolomics, and cytokinomics with clinical data from 766 patients with AD cirrhosis to construct multiscale, multifactorial response networks relevant to this disease. This approach allowed us to identify relevant drivers and pathways underlying two common clinical outcomes after decompensation of cirrhosis, namely ACLF development at 28 days and short-term mortality at 90 days. Special attention was given to profile the metabolic and inflammatory landscapes present in patients with AD cirrhosis with active infections, which is one of the main factors contributing to ACLF development1,5. Finally, we identified markers and predictors that could guide the clinical management of the progression from AD to ACLF and prevent short-term mortality.

Results

This investigation was performed in patients with AD cirrhosis (see Methods section for definition of this condition) from the PREDICT cohort fulfilling the inclusion criteria of not presenting ACLF at inclusion and having complete clinical and laboratory data and enough biological samples to perform omics studies. According to these criteria, among the 1273 patients from the PREDICT cohort, 202 patients were excluded because they already had ACLF at hospital admission; therefore, the number of remaining patients with mere AD cirrhosis was 1071 (Supplementary Fig. 1). Among these, 305 were lacking complete clinical and/or routine laboratory data or did not have enough serum and plasma samples to perform metabolomics, lipidomics and cytokinomics studies. The final cohort for this omics study comprised 766 patients with AD cirrhosis, selected irrespective of disease severity (see below). Patients with AD cirrhosis were also stratified according to the three distinct clinical courses after hospital admission defined by Trebicka et al.11: stable (SDC, rarely requiring hospital readmission within 90 days and very low one-year mortality risk); unstable (UDC, requiring frequent hospitalizations within 90 days after admission unrelated to ACLF and associated with moderate mortality risk) and pre-ACLF (developing ACLF and an imminent risk of death) (see Methods section for more details on these three clinical courses). As expected, in our multiomics study, disease severity followed an increasing trend from SDC to UDC peaking at the pre-ACLF group, whose patients had higher rate of ascites and infections, higher serum bilirubin, INR, creatinine, ALT, leukocyte and neutrophil counts, and CRP, and lower hematocrit, mean arterial pressure, and serum levels of sodium and albumin (Supplementary Table 1). Patients of the pre-ACLF group also had higher MELDNa and CLIF-C AD scores, exhibited more commonly liver and renal dysfunctions, and had a higher mortality rate at 90 days. Comparable clinical characteristics between included and excluded patients in this multiomics study indicate the absence of selection bias in the PREDICT cohort (Supplementary Table 2).

Figure 1A shows the strategy of sample analysis followed in this investigation. Briefly, serum and plasma samples from the 766 patients with AD cirrhosis collected at hospital admission were profiled by multiple omics technologies, including targeted lipidomics (bioactive lipid mediators), untargeted metabolomics (low-molecular weight metabolites), and cytokinomics (cytokines and chemokines, from now on merely referred as cytokines). Metabolomics and lipidomics data were gathered by LC-MS methods, and cytokinomics data were obtained using a 20-plex Luminex platform. PCA analysis of the whole data revealed that levels of lipid mediators, metabolites, and cytokines clustered together in all patients, except for one, in whom cytokines were outside the outlier boundaries and were excluded from subsequent analysis (Supplementary Fig. 2A). Another patient had missing values for all metabolites measured in the metabolomics analysis and was also excluded. Therefore, the final number of patients included in the multiomics dataset was 764. For simplification purposes, this manuscript will first present the analysis of each omics separately, interrogating two common clinical outcomes after decompensation of cirrhosis: 28-day ACLF development and 90-day mortality (Fig. 1A, right panel), and subsequently construct multiscale, multifactorial response networks to identify pathways associated with these two clinical outcomes.

Association of a specific fingerprint of lipid mediators with ACLF development and/or short-term mortality

Among the 41 lipid mediators included in the targeted lipidomics analysis, 7 were not detected in our patient cohort, and 6 were below the lower limit of quantification in more than 15% of the patients (Supplementary Table 3). The number of lipid mediators included in the final analysis was 28 (Supplementary Fig. 2B). Figure 1B shows the volcano plot considering the extent of both fold-changes and statistical significance (p values) of each lipid mediator between AD patients who later developed ACLF within 28 days and those who did not. This comparison identified 3 lipid mediators with statistically significant value: tridecanoic acid and hydroxydodecanoic acid (HDA) with positive fold-changes, and sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P) with a negative fold-change (Fig. 1B). Of interest, changes in S1P and HDA were already observed in the comparison between healthy subjects (HS) and AD patients (Supplementary Fig. 3A). Moreover, S1P levels were significantly lower (Fig. 1C) and those of HDA significantly higher (Supplementary Fig. 3B) in patients of the pre-ACLF group compared to those of the SDC and/or UDC groups. Of note, decreased S1P levels were associated with down-regulation of sphingosine kinase 1 (SPHK1), the main sphingosine kinase implicated in its biosynthesis12,13, in the peripheral leukocytes of patients with AD cirrhosis (Fig. 1D). Moreover, leukocytes from these patients showed up-regulation of S1P lyase 1 (SGPL1), the enzyme that breaks down S1P12,13 (Fig. 1D). No differences were observed in the expression of SPHK2, S1P phosphatase 1 [SGPP1] and phospholipid phosphatase 1 [PLPP1], the last two related to S1P dephosphorylation (Supplementary Fig. 3C). Moreover, leukocytes from AD patients showed significant downregulation of the S1P receptor 1 (S1PR1), suggesting that S1P signaling is also deficient in this condition (Fig. 1E). Other less widely expressed S1P receptors including S1PR2, S1PR3, S1PR4 and S1PR5 remained unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 3D). S1P did not appear in the volcano plot comparing patients who died within 90 days with those surviving (Supplementary Fig. 4). In this comparison, elevated levels of the polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) arachidonic acid (AA), linoleic acid (LA), eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and the docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) derivative 17-hydroxy-DHA (17-HDHA) were identified (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Association of metabolites and inflammatory markers with ACLF development and mortality in patients with AD cirrhosis

Among the 286 metabolites detected by untargeted metabolomics, thirteen were not detected in more than 15% of the patients and were excluded from the analysis. Drug metabolites and bile acids were also excluded. The number of metabolites included in the final analysis was 253 (Supplementary Fig. 2C). Figure 2A shows the volcano plot considering the extent of fold-changes and p values for each metabolite between AD patients that later developed ACLF within 28 days and those who did not. This comparison revealed increased levels of 12 metabolites, being deoxy-methylthio-adenosine (a metabolite of S-adenosylmethionine), ergothioneine (a histidine metabolite), galacturonic acid (a sugar acid isomer to glucuronic acid which is related to both alternative extramitochondrial pathways for glucose oxidation and conjugation of toxic substances14), aspartyl-phenyalanine (a dipeptide without any known biological activity) and hexanoylcarnitine (a marker of impaired mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation) the metabolites with the highest statistical significance (Fig. 2A). In the comparison between patients who died within 90 days and those who survived, galacturonic acid and 4-hydroxy-3-methoxyphenylglycol (MPHG) sulfate (a metabolite of norepinephrine) were the two metabolites with the highest positive significance (Fig. 2B). Figure 2C shows the metabolites identified in these comparisons that were significantly increased in patients of the pre-ACLF group in comparison to those of the SDC and/or UDC groups. Of note, galacturonic acid, hexanoylcarnitine, and MPHG sulfate are the three metabolites composing the metabolomics prognostic model (CLIF-C MET) described by Weiss et al. using data from the CANONIC study15. Except for hexanoylcarnitine, these metabolites were already elevated in AD patients relative to HS (Supplementary Fig. 5A).

Integration of multiomics data: the network associated with ACLF

We next integrated the 3 datasets (lipid mediators, metabolites and cytokines) inspired by a method described by Li et al.16 This method was based on a regression model that built correlation networks for each of the 3 datasets and then integrated them into a network which was used to query the development of ACLF within 28 days and mortality by 90 days as major clinical outcomes after decompensation (Fig. 3A). The results were adjusted considering age and sex as confounders and plotted in networks of nodes and edges including only features that achieved statistically significance. The color intensity of the node indicates the weight of the positive (red) or negative (blue) association with the specific outcome in its own omics dataset. The edges represent statistically significant correlations higher than 0.3 between nodes. Grey edges indicate association within the same omics dataset, and red edges mean association between members of different datasets. Figure 3B shows the results of querying ACLF development as outcome of AD cirrhosis. Among the 291 features included in the integrated omics analysis, we identified 22 (10 lipid mediators, 7 metabolites and 5 cytokines) associated with this clinical outcome (Fig. 3B). Using the variance-weighted importance of each loading of the 22 features composing the ACLF network, we identified the lipid mediator 20-hydroxyeicosatetraenoic acid (20-HETE) as the feature with the highest positive association with ACLF development (Supplementary Fig. 6A). IL-6 and hexanoylcarnitine were the top features associated with ACLF within the cytokine and metabolite datasets, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 6A). On the other hand, only the lipid mediator S1P showed negative association with the development of ACLF (Fig. 3B). Analysis of the interconnections within features of different datasets demonstrated that 20-HETE correlated with both hexanoylcarnitine and acetylcarnitine, suggesting coordinated metabolic regulation (Fig. 3B). This finding has pathophysiological relevance because it connects 20-HETE, a lipid mediator known to promote mitochondrial superoxide anion production17, with acylcarnitines, central carriers of fatty acids into mitochondria for β-oxidation and whose elevated circulating levels are indicative of mitochondrial dysfunction18. The omega-6-PUFA eicosatrienoic acid (also known as dihomo-gamma-linolenic acid (DGLA) and its precursor LA were also interconnected to hexanoylcarnitine (Fig. 3B). HDA, which is a medium chain hydroxylated fatty acid associated with fatty acid oxidation disorders19, was connected to acetylcarnitine. Finally, AA and IL-8 correlated with galacturonic acid and IL-6 with pentoses (Fig. 3B).

Integration of multiomics data: the network associated with mortality

We then used the regression model to integrate the features in a network, querying death within 90 days after decompensation as the other major clinical outcome. Again, the results were adjusted for age and sex. Among the 291 features included in the integrated network analysis, we identified 16 (7 lipid mediators, 6 metabolites and 3 cytokines) closely associated with mortality (Fig. 3C). The features demonstrating the highest positive association with this clinical outcome were 5-HEPE and 17-HDHA (Supplementary Fig. 6B). The most prominent lipid-metabolite connection was once again observed between 20-HETE and both hexanoylcarnitine and acetylcarnitine (Fig. 3C). Despite showing some unique features, such as the presence of MPHG and 18-HEPE and the absence of HDA, tridecanoic acid, LA, S1P, aspartyl-phenyalanine, deoxy-methylthio-adenosine, MCP-1, and MIP-1β, the mortality network essentially overlapped with the ACLF network. This finding is consistent with ACLF being a major cause of death in patients with AD cirrhosis20.

Relationships between features, peripheral blood cell counts, and organ failures

Considering the pivotal role of peripheral blood immune cells in the pathogenesis of systemic inflammation and organ failure in AD cirrhosis3,4,6, we analyzed the relationship between clinical blood counts and the features constituting the integrated networks. Supplementary Fig. 7A shows the peripheral immune cell landscape of patients with AD cirrhosis included in the study, which was characterized by neutrophilia, monocytosis, lymphopenia, and thrombocytopenia. Furthermore, patients who later developed ACLF within 28 days showed increased monocyte and neutrophil counts at study inclusion whereas patients who died within 90 days primarily showed increased neutrophil count (Supplementary Fig. 7B). The monocyte and neutrophil counts paralleled disease severity and increased gradually across the three different clinical courses of AD cirrhosis while lymphopenia remained steady (Supplementary Fig. 7C). We then generated unsupervised clustered correlation matrices between the features constituting the ACLF and mortality networks shown in Fig. 3B, C and the different blood cell types. These analyses showed that the main network features (20-HETE, galacturonic acid, and IL-6) were strongly correlated with neutrophils and, more weakly, with monocytes (Fig. 4A). Notably, S1P exhibited a strong correlation with platelets (Fig. 4B). Correlation analyses of network features with biochemical and clinical parameters revealed that most of the lipid mediators, along with galacturonic acid, were positively associated with bilirubin and INR (markers of liver and coagulation failure, respectively) (Fig. 4C). Metabolites, on the other hand, preferentially correlated with creatinine (a marker of renal failure). These findings might underscore the key role of the liver in maintaining lipid homeostasis and highlight the important contribution of the kidney to the clearance of circulating metabolites. Additionally, patients with AD cirrhosis and active infections exhibited globally increased plasma levels across all measured features, with particularly elevated IL-6 and IL-1RA, compared with non-infected patients. (Fig. 4D).

Relationships between features, etiology, and treatment

We next scrutinized how features of the ACLF and mortality networks vary according to the etiology and treatments of the patients by visualizing the concordance of the loadings for each feature across multiple heatmaps. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 8A, the ACLF network features were highly concordant in patients with alcohol (86% concordance) and hepatitis C virus (100% concordance) etiologies, but less concordant in those with metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD) (52% concordance). Patients with alcohol etiology also showed a high concordance (83%) of features in the mortality network (Supplementary Fig. 8B). Given that 65% of the patients included in these multiomics study had alcohol-related etiology, the observed networks are likely largely shaped by features specific to this subgroup. With respect to treatments, the concordance of the ACLF network features was relatively consistent among patients treated with proton pump inhibitors (72% concordance) or beta-blockers (63% concordance), whereas it was lower in patients receiving antibiotics (50% concordance) (Supplementary Fig. 8C). Mortality network feature concordance was similar in patients on proton pump inhibitors (88%) or antibiotics (83%), but slightly lower in those receiving beta-blockers (66%) (Supplementary Fig. 8D).

Relationship between features and infections

We first analyzed the integrated network comparing infected versus non-infected patients, regardless of their clinical outcome. Among the 291 features included in the analysis, we identified 18 (8 lipid mediators, 7 metabolites and 3 cytokines) closely associated with infection (Supplementary Fig. 9A). The features demonstrating the highest positive association and variance-weighted importance in this condition were 20-HETE, IL-6 and AA (Supplementary Fig. 9B). Interestingly, the anti-inflammatory and pro-resolving marker 15-HETE was negatively associated with infections, suggesting an impaired resolution capacity in patients with AD cirrhosis. Of note, most of the significant positive correlations between lipid mediators and metabolites converged into hexadecanedioic acid, a carboxylic acid described as a mitochondrial uncoupling agent21. The features of the infection network were fully concordant (100%) in patients with bacterial infections, probably reflecting the predominance of this infection type in the study cohort (Supplementary Fig. 9C).

Biological activity of the lipid mediators composing the integrated networks

To gain deeper insight into the pathophysiological roles of the key features identified in the integrated network analysis, we performed functional experiments using peripheral blood immune cells, which serve as a useful model for testing metabolic stressors in vitro22. Since the integrated network analysis revealed that several bioactive lipids (i.e., 20-HETE, HDA, LA, and DGLA) strongly associated with acylcarnitines (markers of mitochondrial dysfunction), we examined whether these lipids could induce cellular stress and exert detrimental effects on the mitochondrial compartment of peripheral immune cells. To exclude any possibility that these lipids at the micromolar range (1–10 µM) induce lipotoxic cell death, we first confirmed the absence of changes in cell viability in THP-1 cells and primary cultures of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) (Fig. 5A). We neither observed any effect on the viability of polymorphonuclear neutrophils (PMN) (Supplementary Fig. 10). We next assessed the effects of 20-HETE, HDA, LA and DGLA on a panel of genes related to cellular stress in PBMCs. Figure 5B shows that 20-HETE significantly down-regulated the expression of activating transcription factor 3 (ATF3), which is a stress-induced transcription factor acting as a negative regulator of inflammatory injury23,24. On the other hand, the two HDA isomers 2- and 12-HDA down-regulated the expression of NFE2L2, the gene coding for nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (NRF2), which protects the cell against oxidative stress25 (Fig. 5B). Downregulation of these genes is better illustrated in Fig. 5C. LA, DGLA, and 3-HDA promoted cytokine expression, underscoring the proinflammatory properties of these fatty acids (Fig. 5B). Consistent with the lack of effect on cell viability, none of the lipids modified the expression of DNA damage-inducible transcript 3 (DDIT3) (coding for the ER stress dependent cell death marker CHOP) or affected the expression of the stress-response mediators heme oxygenase 1 (HMOX1), superoxide dismutase 2 (SOD2) and activating transcription factor 4 (ATF4) (Fig. 5B). Since these findings point into the direction that 20-HETE and HDA might result in damaging effects at the cellular level, we next explored whether these lipids directly induce detrimental actions on mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation by using the Seahorse technology to monitor changes over time in oxygen consumption rate (OCR) following the administration of mitochondrial electron transport chain modulators. Figure 5D, E shows that 20-HETE was the only lipid that consistently impaired all parameters of mitochondrial respiration, including, among others, basal respiration, ATP-linked respiration, maximal respiration, and spare respiratory capacity, which serve as markers of mitochondrial fitness. We finally explored the effects of these lipids on mouse liver and kidney tissues using precision-cut liver and kidney slices (PCLS and PCKS, respectively) as proxies of injury in both hepatic and extra-hepatic organs. Figure 5F shows that HDA down-regulated Nfe2l2 expression in PCLS, while 20-HETE significantly downregulated the expression of genes involved in antioxidant and cellular stress responses (i.e., Nfe2l2 and Atf4) in PCKS.

Exploration of molecular mechanisms and cell signaling of 20-HETE in peripheral leukocytes

Since the actions of 20-HETE, but not HDA, are receptor-mediated, we focused on delineating the mechanisms driving its mitochondrial injury in peripheral leukocytes. 20-HETE has been reported to bind and activate the G-protein–coupled receptor 75 (GPR75) with high affinity in human endothelial and vascular smooth muscle cells, as well as in prostate cancer cell lines26,27. Therefore, we first explored whether GPR75 is also present in human leukocytes. Figure 6A provides evidence that GPR75 in expressed in human PBMCs from both HS and patients with AD cirrhosis. We next examined whether 20-HETE triggers mitochondrial oxidative stress as a key driver of mitochondrial injury and dysfunction. To test this, we used a live-cell mitochondrial superoxide indicator, which revealed that 20-HETE induces mitochondrial superoxide production to a similar extent as the well-known mitochondrial stressor doxorubicin (Fig. 6B). In peripheral leukocytes, the 20-HETE–induced mitochondrial superoxide production was time-dependent (Fig. 6C). In addition, this response was mediated by GPR75, as the selective GPR75 antagonist AAA (20-hydroxyicosa-6Z,15Z-dienoyl)aspartate) blocked the 20-HETE-induced release of mitochondrial superoxide (Fig. 6D). Lastly, we investigated the signaling pathway(s) mediating the actions of 20-HETE. PBMCs were treated with 20-HETE, and changes in the relative levels of phosphorylation at 37 kinase phosphorylation sites and 2 related total proteins were evaluated using a human phospho-kinase array. This assay revealed that the Akt serine/threonine kinases, encompassing all three isoforms (Akt1-3), showed the strongest phosphorylation signal in response to 20-HETE (Fig. 6E). Although to a lesser extent, 20-HETE also induced HSP60 phosphorylation, a key post-translational modification that profoundly affects the function of this mitochondrial chaperone (Fig. 6E). Together, these findings suggest that the mitochondrial-damaging effects of 20-HETE in human leukocytes are mediated through a GPR75/Akt signaling axis (Fig. 6F).

Predictors of ACLF development and survival in AD patients

We finally sought to assess the predictive value of each feature (i.e., lipid, metabolite, and cytokine) included in the integrated networks for the two clinical outcomes of AD cirrhosis considered in this study. The prognostic estimates were derived from 764 patients of the PREDICT cohort and were validated in 580 patients of the ACLARA cohort, a multicenter observational study performed in Latin American countries28. We first represented in a heatmap the area under the ROC curve (AUC) for each feature to predict ACLF. This analysis identified a cluster of four features, including galacturonic acid, HDA, hexanoylcarnitine, and IL-6, that exhibited the highest average AUC values among the different feature clusters, indicating their predictive potential for ACLF (Fig. 7A). The features demonstrated similar predictive values when comparing the PREDICT and ACLARA cohorts (Supplementary Table 4). Sensitivity and specificity for each ACLF predictive value are detailed in Supplementary Table 5. We next performed longitudinal analysis of the features to examine their dynamic changes and validate the stability of their predictive values. The results of this analysis were visualized in a plot showing the correlation between the AUC of the predictive value at the study inclusion (baseline) and during the follow up (Fig. 7B). The overall correlation was of 0.5 and reached statistical significance, being S1P, galacturonic and deoxy-(methylthio)adenosine the features of higher predictive value and stability (Fig. 7B). We then used the Cox model within the Fine and Gray competing-risks framework, with liver transplantation treated as a competing event to assess survival at 90 days. The AUCs for each feature in predicting survival are presented in Fig. 7C, while the comparison of predictive values between the PREDICT and ACLARA cohorts is detailed in Supplementary Table 6. Of particular interest, 17-HDHA, a key marker in the biosynthesis of pro-resolving lipid mediators29, demonstrated high prognostic value in the PREDICT cohort. This result further reinforces the notion that impaired resolution of inflammation may play a significant role in the outcome of AD cirrhosis. Sensitivity and specificity for each survival predictive value are detailed in Supplementary Table 7. We also investigated how well the network features perform compared to established clinical predictors such as CLIF-C OF score (commonly used to predict ACLF development), as well as CLIF-C AD and MELDNa (frequently used for survival prediction). As shown in Fig. 7D, 15 out of 22 features showed better ACLF predictive value than the CLIF-C OF score. On the other hand, 12 out of 16 features (i.e., 20-HETE and 17-HDHA) were better predictors of survival than CLIF-C AD score, while 11 of them, including also 20-HETE and 17-HDHA, showed higher prediction of survival than MELDNa (Fig. 7D). We finally used multivariate regression models to calculate the odds ratios with a 95% confidence interval for each feature. Increased levels of IL-6, EPA, tridecanoic acid, hexanoylcarnitine, and galacturonic acid were found to confer an estimated significant higher risk of developing ACLF within 28 days in both PREDICT and ACLARA cohorts (Supplementary Table 8). On the other hand, IL-8 and MPHG sulfate conferred higher odds ratio of not surviving at 90 days, matching both PREDICT and ACLARA (Supplementary Table 9).

Discussion

The current study performed integrated multiomics analysis to identify networks of lipid mediators, metabolites, and cytokines associated with either development of ACLF or short-term mortality in a well clinically characterized cohort of 766 patients with AD cirrhosis. To our knowledge, this study is the first to combine three large datasets obtained using high-throughput technologies, including serum metabolomics, plasma lipidomics, and cytokine analysis, to identify molecular networks associated with two clinical outcomes commonly encountered by patients with AD cirrhosis. Notably, the network integration results were further supported by additional functional and mechanistic studies in peripheral leukocytes, linking the results of the exploratory investigations to functional cell responses. Moreover, we identified several predictors for ACLF development and death, which could be routinely assessed in patients with AD cirrhosis admitted to hospital, and we tested these predictors in an independent cohort of AD patients, which allowed for their cross-validation.

The most important finding of this study was that our integrative approach identified a molecular network in patients with AD cirrhosis who later developed ACLF during a short period of time, linking the accumulation of acylcarnitines with the lipid mediator 20-HETE. While acylcarnitines are established markers of impaired mitochondrial fatty acid beta-oxidation18, 20-HETE is an eicosanoid generated through the hydroxylation of AA by the cytochrome P450 system17. 20-HETE has been described as a potent vasoconstrictor primarily biosynthesized by smooth muscle cells and with a role in the activation of the renin-angiotensin system and the development of hypertension, cerebral vasospam and ischemia-reperfusion injury30. In addition, 20-HETE is known to elicit mitochondrial superoxide production in vascular smooth muscle cells17, and its inhibition has been shown to mitigate mitochondrial dysfunction in traumatic brain injury31. Of note, elevated 20-HETE levels in patients with cirrhosis, especially in those with ascites, have been linked to renal dysfunction and failure32,33. Since mitochondrial dysfunction is a hallmark in the progression of decompensated cirrhosis to ACLF34, the observed association between 20-HETE and acylcarnitines in patients at imminent risk of developing ACLF identifies a potential novel target for preventing this fatal complication. Beyond 20-HETE, our integrative network analysis also revealed a link between acylcarnitines and the hydroxylated medium-chain fatty acid HDA, another lipid species implicated in impaired fatty acid oxidation19. Of interest, this hydroxylated fatty acid is commonly esterified to the lipid A backbone of lipopolysaccharide of gram-negative bacteria35, suggesting that lipids of non-human origin might also contribute to the metabolic alterations leading to the development of ACLF in patients with AD cirrhosis. Together, these results emphasize a close interaction between lipid mediators and markers of mitochondrial dysfunction in patients with AD cirrhosis who will develop ACLF. Surprisingly, the integrated ACLF network did not reveal any direct associations between markers of mitochondrial dysfunction and classical inflammatory mediators, such as cytokines.

Another meaningful finding of our study was the strong inverse relationship between circulating levels of S1P, a lysophospholipid signaling molecule that plays a key role in regulating lymphocyte trafficking (i.e., the egress of lymphocytes from lymph nodes12,13), and the development of ACLF. Indeed, lymphopenia is often associated with poor prognosis in patients with AD cirrhosis, in whom reduced lymphocyte count can exacerbate the risk of infections and complications due to immunosuppression36. In our investigation, reduced S1P levels in the peripheral circulation of patients with AD cirrhosis were associated with a significant down-regulation of the main sphingosine kinase implicated in its biosynthesis (i.e., SHPK1) as well as with a notorious up-regulation of the enzyme responsible for its degradation (i.e., SGPL1). In addition, platelets produce vast amounts of S1P, and it is well known that patients with AD cirrhosis present thrombocytopenia and altered platelet function37. Furthermore, peripheral cells from patients with AD cirrhosis also showed reduced expression of the main S1P receptor (i.e., S1PR1), suggesting a widely distributed deficit in S1P signaling. Taken together, our observations allow us to speculate that reduced S1P biosynthesis and/or signaling, accompanied by increased S1P degradation, may contribute to the progressive lymphopenia characteristic of AD patients with poor outcomes. These findings add another layer to the recently proposed mechanism whereby lymphopenia arises secondary to maladaptive emergency granulopoiesis, which skews hematopoietic stem and progenitor cell differentiation toward granulopoiesis at the expense of lymphopoiesis in patients with poor outcomes38.

This study has some limitations. Firstly, due to the inability to have access to solid organs in patients with cirrhosis without the use of invasive procedures, we could not confirm the network linking impaired mitochondrial function with the accumulation of fatty acid derivatives in tissues other than peripheral blood. However, we confirmed the detrimental effects of designated lipids on mouse PCLS and PCKS, which serve as proxy models of experimental liver and kidney tissue injuries. Secondly, although we performed longitudinal analysis of the features with highest predictive values in ACLF development and survival, proving the stability of these predictors, we did not perform a complete assessment of the dynamic changes evolving within the integrative networks over the disease course, which would be very helpful for fully understanding the pathophysiology of ACLF development. Thirdly, despite several features included in our integrative analysis were better predictors of ACLF and survival than established scores such as CLIF-C OF, CLIF-C AD, and MELDNa, future studies are needed to uncover the full potential of these features when used in more complex predictive models in combination with other clinical and biological variables. Fourth, although our study included multiomics data from 766 patients with AD cirrhosis from the well clinically characterized PREDICT cohort, larger sample sizes will be likely needed to overcome the considerable heterogeneity existing in these patients. For instance, the networks identified in our study were mostly representative of patients with AD cirrhosis of alcohol-related etiology and/or bacterial infections. Therefore, other etiologies and other infection subtypes were underrepresented because of limited number of cases. Finally, further work is needed to define whether the predictors identified in this investigation are practical, feasible, and cost-effective for clinical applicability. Implementing, as a point of care the recently developed metabolomic score, which has a very high prognostic value for 90-day mortality (C-index 0.94) and includes galacturonic acid, hexanoylcarnitine, and MPHG, three of the features with highest predictive value in this study, represents an urgent unmet need for patients hospitalized for cirrhosis decompensation.

In summary, the results of the current study uncover a close association between immunomodulatory lipid mediators and alterations in mitochondrial function in patients with AD cirrhosis with imminent risk of developing ACLF and die. Overall, our study highlights the role of immunometabolism, which is the combination of immune dysfunction and metabolic alterations, in the development of ACLF and risk of death in patients with AD cirrhosis.

Methods

Patient cohort and samples

The study was performed in biobanked plasma and serum samples from 766 patients with AD cirrhosis non-electively hospitalized for decompensation, included in the multicenter, prospective, observational PREDICT study11. Inclusion and exclusion criteria for PREDICT are detailed in Supplementary Table 10. The diagnostic criteria defining AD cirrhosis was based on the presence of ascites (new onset ascites, worsening of ascites or development of refractory ascites as defined as need for large-volume paracentesis), encephalopathy (development of acute episodes of hepatic encephalopathy grade 2 or greater, according to West Haven criteria), gastrointestinal hemorrhage (portal hypertension-related hemorrhage that requires hospital admission) or any combination of these at non-elective hospital admission. The criteria for selection of samples from patients included in the current investigation were the following: (1) absence of ACLF at inclusion; (2) availability of full medical history and clinical and laboratory data at enrolment and during 90-day follow-up; and (3) availability of biological samples. In some analysis, the selected patients with AD cirrhosis were stratified by their clinical course as SDC (those who did not develop ACLF, did not require hospital readmission, nor die during the 3-month follow-up period, and showed very low mortality (9.5%) within the 1-year after enrollment), UDC (patients who did not develop ACLF, but died or required at least one hospital readmission within the 3-month follow-up period, and had 3-month and 1-year mortality rates of 21.0% and 35.6%, respectively), and pre-ACLF (those characterized by episodes of ACLF and 3-month and 1-year mortality rates of 53.7% and 67.4%, respectively). For further details, see Trebicka et al.11. The ethics committee of each hospital participating in the PREDICT study authorized the use of biobanked materials for omics investigations. The current work received the Hospital Clínic of Barcelona institutional review board approval (authorization number HCB/2020/0426). All individuals or their legal representatives gave their written informed consent. The study also included plasma and serum samples from 30 healthy subjects (HS) recruited at the Blood and Tissue Bank of Catalonia (institutional review board approval authorization number #HCB/2015/0692). Criteria for inclusion were males and females aged between 30 and 60 years without obesity and liver disease, and not taking non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs in the previous 10 days. All samples were handled in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Targeted high-throughput immunoassays

The levels of plasma protein mediators of inflammation (i.e., cytokines, chemokines, and growth factors) were determined by high-throughput multiplex assays using the Luminex® platform. The color-coded magnetic beads panel of Milliplex® MAP included 11 cytokines (TNFα, IL-6, IL-1ra, IFNγ, IL-1β, IFNα2, IL-10, IL-1α, IL-17A, IL-7 and IL-4), 6 chemokines (eotaxin, IP-10/CXCL10, MCP-1, MIP-1α, MIP-1β and IL-8) and 3 growth factors (VEGF-A, G-CSF and GM-CSF) which are strongly associated with the severity, frequency and clinical progression (i.e., improvement, steady course or worsening) of cirrhosis4. Data analysis was performed using the Belysa immunoassay curve-fitting software. The readouts were analyzed with the standard version of the Milliplex Analyst software (Merck Millipore, Burlington, MA). A five-parameter logistic regression model was used to generate standard curves (pg/mL) and to calculate the concentration of each sample. The quality controls (QC) were conducted to verify the assay performance. Each kit included QC1 (low dilution) and QC2 (high dilution) of recombinant proteins for each analyte.

Untargeted metabolomics

The analysis of the serum metabolome was performed by liquid chromatography coupled to high-resolution mass spectrometry (LC-HRMS)39,40. Briefly, metabolites were extracted from serum samples via methanol-based protein precipitation and subsequently analyzed using an Ultimate 3000 chromatographic system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) coupled to Exactive/Q-ExactiveTM HR mass spectrometers (Thermo Fisher Scientific) fitted with an electrospray (ESI) source and operating in the positive and negative ion modes for metabolite separations on C18 and ZIC-pHILIC columns, respectively. A combination of two complementary chromatographic methods was used to profile metabolites and get a broader metabolome coverage. Data acquisition was performed in five independent batches. QC samples were obtained by pooling 20 μL of each of the samples to be analyzed. Data normalization/standardization was achieved by injecting QC samples at regular intervals every 10 samples throughout the experiments to evaluate the signal variations of any metabolite. Raw data were automatically processed by using the Workflow4Metabolomics (W4M) Galaxy, an open-source and collaborative online platform for computational metabolomics41. Metabolite features were first annotated using our in-house reference library (compiled from ~1200 pure authentic standards) containing accurately measured masses and chromatographic retention times. Confirmation of metabolite annotations was then accomplished by running additional LC-MS/MS experiments using a Q-Exactive instrument operated under non-resonant collision-induced dissociation conditions using higher-energy C-trap dissociation. To be further considered, ions had to match at least two orthogonal criteria (among accurate measured mass, retention time, and MS/MS spectrum) to those of an authentic chemical standard analyzed under the same analytical conditions, as proposed by the Metabolomics Standards Initiative42.

To ensure accurate and consistent metabolite quantification, chromatographic peaks of the annotated metabolites were integrated for subject, patient, and QC samples by using the Trace Finder software (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Peak integrations were manually reviewed for each sample. The resulting peak areas were then normalized with respect to QC samples with the LOESS algorithm, to correct for variation resulting from batch-to-batch signal differences. Metabolite signals were then filtered according to the following criteria: (a) the correlation between QC dilution factors and areas of chromatographic peaks (relevant metabolites should have coefficients of correlation above 0.7), (b) repeatability (the coefficient of variation obtained on chromatographic peak areas of QC samples should be below 30%), and (c) ratio of chromatographic peak areas of biological to blank samples above a value of 3. Overall, 286 metabolites passed these tests and were robustly measured. As a note of caution, under the experimental conditions used in this investigation, the metabolites galacturonic acid and its isomer glucuronic acid could not be completely resolved. For clarity and consistency with previous publications15,40, we retain the designation galacturonic acid.

UPLC-MS/MS-based targeted lipidomics

The targeted lipidomics study analyzed 41 lipid mediators in plasma samples using UPLC-MS/MS method43,44 and following current guidelines44. Briefly, 500 µL of plasma samples were spiked with a mixture of internal standards (LTB4-d4, PGE2-d4; 20-HETE-d6, 15-HETE-d8, 13-HODE-d4, and 12,13-EpOME-d4, 10 ng/mL each, Cayman Chemical, Ann Arbor, MI) in 500 μL acetonitrile and 100 μL water and 5 μL 2,6-di-tertbutyl-4-methylphenol, and shaken vigorously. The pH was adjusted to 6.0 by the addition of 2 mL phosphate buffer (0.15 M), and butylhydroxytoluene (10 mg/mL) was added to prevent lipid autooxidation during sample workup and storage. After centrifugation, supernatants were submitted to solid-phase extraction (SPE) in Bond Elute Certify II columns (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA) preconditioned with 3 mL methanol and 3 mL of 0.15 M phosphate buffer (pH 6.0) in a SPE Vacuum Manifold. The SPE-columns were then washed with 3 mL methanol/H2O (50/50, vol/vol) and eluted with a mixture of ethyl acetate/n-hexane/acetic acid (74/25/1, vol/vol/vol). The eluate was evaporated on a heating block at 40 °C under a stream of nitrogen. The solid residues were dissolved in 70 μL acetonitrile/H2O (50/50, vol/vol) and analyzed using a UPLC system with a solvent method consisting of acetic acid (0.05%) and acetonitrile/methanol (50/50, vol/vol) on an Acquity UPLC separation module (Waters, Milford, MA). The UPLC system was connected to a triple-quadrupole linear ion trap mass spectrometer (ABI Sciex 5500 QTRAP, Framingham, MA) equipped with a Turbo V ion source, together with the Analyst 1.5.1 software package. Analysis was performed with multiple reaction monitoring in negative mode. All plasma samples were analyzed blindly in independent batches. Stable isotope labeled-internal standards were used in all the sample extractions and for calibration. The QC sample was prepared by spiking certain amount of native and labeled standards into pooled plasma from all samples, and QC was analyzed along with the samples in each batch to ensure the reliability of the method and the instrument stability45. These lipidomics methods and data generated comply with the community requirements described by O’Donnell et al.46.

Isolation of blood leukocytes

Peripheral blood leukocytes were isolated from healthy donors recruited at the Blood and Tissue Bank of Catalonia (authorization number #HCB/2015/0692), in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All donors gave their written informed consent. Inclusion criteria comprised males and females aged 30–60 years, without obesity or liver disease, and not taking non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs within the previous 10 days. Briefly, PBMCs and PMNs were isolated from 20 mL of whole blood collected in ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid tubes using a Ficoll-Paque PLUS (GE Healthcare Life Sciences, Chicago, IL) density gradient. Following centrifugation at 200 × g for 10 min, plasma was removed, and the cell pellet was resuspended in Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline without magnesium and calcium (DPBS−/−) to a final volume of 20 mL. Diluted blood was layered over 13.3 mL of Ficoll-Paque PLUS and centrifuged at 500 × g for 25 min. The PBMC layer was collected and washed with DPBS−/− and centrifuged again at 400 × g for 5 min. The pellet containing the PMN was incubated with pre-warmed ammonium-chloride-potassium lysis buffer for 10 min at room temperature to remove red blood cells and then centrifuged at 400 × g for 5 min. The red blood lysis procedure was repeated, and the resultant pellet was washed with DPBS−/−. Both PBMCs and PMNs were resuspended in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 medium with penicillin (100 U/mL), streptomycin (100 U/mL), and L-glutamine (2 mM) containing 1% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS). Thereafter, cells were counted using the Countess™ II FL Automated Cell Counter (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and seeded at a density of 5–6 × 106 cells/mL and kept resting for 3 h in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator at 37 °C prior to incubation experiments.

Culture and passaging of the human monocytic THP-1 cell line

The human monocytic THP-1 cell line, authenticated by short tandem repeat profiling, was obtained from ATCC (Manassas, VA). Cells were maintained in a 75 cm2 flask containing 10 ml RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with penicillin (100 U/mL), streptomycin (100 U/mL), and L-glutamine (2 mM) and 10% heat-inactivated FBS in a humidified incubator at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Cell density was maintained between 3–8 × 105 cells/mL, and cells were passaged every 2–3 days to ensure optimal growth conditions. The cell line used in this study was confirmed to be mycoplasma-free and was not listed as misidentified or contaminated according to the ICLAC register.

Cell incubations for lipid bioassays

PBMCs, PMNs, and THP-1 cells were incubated with either vehicle (0.3% ethanol), LA (10 µM), DGLA (10 µM), 20-HETE (1 µM), 2-HDA (10 µM), 3-HDA (10 µM), and 12-HDA (10 µM) (all from Cayman Chemical) for 18 h in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator at 37 °C. The selected concentrations of lipids were in the range of the levels detected in AD patients and those described in the literature17,44,47,48. At the end of the incubation period, samples were centrifuged at 1000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C, and supernatants and cell pellets were collected for further analysis.

Cytotoxicity assay

Cell viability was assessed by using the colorimetric reagent 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyl tetrazolium bromide (MTT). Briefly, PBMCs, PMNs, and THP-1 cells were seeded at 500,000 cells per well in 24-well plates and exposed to either vehicle or lipids at the same conditions as described above. At the end of the incubation period, cells were washed with DPBS−/−, centrifuged at 400 × g for 5 min, and resuspended in 450 μl serum-free medium. Then, 50 μl of MTT (5 mg/mL) was added to each well and cells incubated for 3 h in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator at 37 °C. The formazan crystals were solubilized by the addition of 500 μl isopropanol, followed by incubation at room temperature for 30 min in a plate shaker at 300 rpm. Once the crystals were completely dissolved, 200 μl from each well was transferred into a 96-well plate and the absorbance measured at 570 nm using the FLUOstar Optima microplate reader (BMG LABTECH, Ortenberg, Germany).

RNA isolation, reverse transcription, and real-time PCR

Isolation of total RNA from PBMCs, PMNs, and PCLS was performed using the TRIzol reagent following manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentrations were assessed in a NanoDrop One spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). cDNA synthesis of 1000 ng of total RNA was performed using the High-Capacity cDNA Archive Kit (Applied Biosystems). For real-time PCR analysis, validated and pre-designed TaqMan Genes Expression Assays from Applied Biosystems were used for human (Hs) and mouse (Mm) samples: NFE2L2 (Hs00975961_g1, Nfe2l2: Mm00477784_m1), SOD2 (Hs00167309_m1, Sod2: Mm01313000_m1), IL6 (Hs00985639_m1, Il6: Mm00446190_m1), CXCL8 (Hs00174103_m1, Cxcl2: Mm00436450_m1), ATF3 (Hs00231069_m1, Atf3: Mm00476032_m1), ATF4 (Hs00909569_g1, Atf4: Mm00515325_g1), DDIT3 (Hs00358796_g1, Ddit3: Mm01135937_g1), HMOX1 (Hs01110250_m1, Hmox1: Mm00516005_m), GPR75 (Hs01922024_s1), S1PR1 (Hs05021992_s1), S1PR2 (custom w2503113291000), S1PR3 (Hs00245464_s1), S1PR4 (Hs02330084_s1), S1PR5 (Hs00928195_s1), PLPP1 (Hs05045242_g1), SPHK1 (Hs00184211_m1), SPHK2 (Hs01016543_g1), SGPP1 (Hs04189357_m1), using β-actin (ACTB: Hs99999903_m1, Actb: Mm00607939_s1) as endogenous control. Real-time PCR amplifications were performed in a QuantStudio 7 Pro Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). Quantitative PCR results were analyzed with Design and Analysis Software 2.6 (Applied Biosystems), and relative quantification of gene expression was performed using a comparative Ct method. The amount of the target gene, normalized to β-actin and relative to a calibrator, was determined by the arithmetic equation 2-ΔΔCt, as described in the comparative Ct method (https://assets.thermofisher.com/TFS-Assets/LSG/manuals/MAN0018747_RelativeQuantificatAnalysisModule_QSDASWv2_UG.pdf).

Seahorse XF HS mini analyzer

Mitochondrial respiration in PBMCs was monitored in the Agilent Seahorse XF HS mini (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA). Freshly isolated PBMC were pre-treated with vehicle (0.3% ethanol), 20-HETE (1 µM), 2-HDA (10 µM), or 12-HDA (10 µM) for 18 h (37 °C, 5% CO2) in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with penicillin (100 U/mL), streptomycin (100 U/mL), and L-glutamine (2 mM). Thereafter, the cell culture medium was replaced with Seahorse XF DMEM medium (pH 7.4) supplemented with 10 mM glucose, 2 mM glutamine, and 1 mM pyruvate, and 80,0000 cells/well were seeded on Seahorse XFp HS Mini 8-well plates. The respirometry was evaluated using the Cell Mito Stress Test kit (103010-100, Agilent Technologies) following the injection of 1.5 µM oligomycin, 2 µM carbonyl cyanide-p-trifluoromethoxyphenylhydrazone, and 0.5 µM rotenone/antimycin-A. OCR measurements were normalized to the amount of total protein.

Mitochondrial oxidative stress

PBMCs were seeded on Poly-L-Lysin-coated flat-bottom 96-well plates (2 × 105 cells/well) and rested for 1 h with RPMI medium supplemented with penicillin (100 U/mL), streptomycin (100 U/mL), and L-glutamine (2 mM) and 1% heat-inactivated FBS in a humidified incubator at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Then, the cells were incubated in the absence or presence of the GPR75 antagonist AAA (5 mM, Cayman Chemicals) for 30 min followed by the addition of either vehicle (0.3% ethanol) or 20-HETE (1 µM) for 4 h. Doxorubicin (3 μM) was used as positive control. Then, mitochondrial oxidative stress was determined using MitoSOX™ Red mitochondrial superoxide indicator (Molecular Probes) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, the MitoSOX reagent was dissolved in 13 μL of dimethyl sulfoxide to prepare a 5 mM stock solution and subsequently a 5 μM working solution. Then, the MitoSOX reagent was added to the cells for 10 min at 37 °C and 5% CO2 protected from light. Cells were then washed with DPBS+/+ to remove background fluorescence, and the plate was read with a monochromator fluorescence reader (Infinite 200 PRO, TECAN Life Sciences) at 510 and 580 nm as excitation and emission wavelengths. Cells were further lysed in RIPA buffer, and total protein quantity was assessed by Micro BCA™ Protein Assay (Thermo Scientific).

Human phospho-kinase profiling

The Proteome Profiler Human Phospho-Kinase Array Kit (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN) was used to assess the changes in the relative levels of phosphorylation at 37 kinase phosphorylation sites and 2 related total proteins by following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, 200 µg of protein from PBMCs were incubated overnight with nitrocellulose membranes, each pre-spotted with the capture antibodies in duplicate. Bound phospho-kinases were detected using ready-to-use cocktails of biotinylated detection antibodies provided within the kit. The membrane images were analyzed using the ImageQuant LAS 4000 (GE Healthcare Life Sciences, Little Chalfont, UK) and quantified with the ImageQuant TL analysis software. Relative protein or phosphorylation levels of the indicated protein kinases were obtained by subtraction of the background and normalization to the positive controls of the same membrane.

Collection of liver and kidney mouse tissues and preparation of PCLS and PCKS

Male C57BL/6J mice (n = 8, Charles River Laboratories, Saint-Aubin-lès-Elbeuf, France) were used in this study to reduce sex-related variability and ensure experimental consistency. Animals were housed in conventional cages on woodchip bedding provided with environmental enrichments such as bedding material, chew implements, and hiding structures. A maximum of five animals were allocated in each cage, placed in a 20–25 °C temperature-controlled facility with 50–60% humidity and 12 h light/dark cycle, and given ad libitum access to standard chow diet (Envigo, Barcelona, Spain) and drinking water. After anesthesia with an overdose of 100 mg/kg ketamine and 10 mg/kg xylazine, the inferior cava vein was cut off, blood allowed to drain for 1 min and the liver excised and placed into ice-cold DPBS−/−. For PCLS, liver blocks (0.5 cm3) were cut from the main lobe with a scalpel and embedded in 4% UltraPure low-melting agarose (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA) diluted in Hanks balanced salt solution without calcium and magnesium. For PCKS, right and left kidneys were retrieved and placed into ice cold DPBS−/−, being the perirenal adipose tissue removed before being embedded in agarose. Agarose cubes with the tissue inside were cut into 250-μm-thick slices using a vibrating blade VT1000S microtome (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany), individually transferred to P-35 Petri dishes containing Millicell cell culture inserts (EMD Millipore, Burlington, MA) and pre-balanced for 20 min with warmed William’s E medium containing GlutaMAX-I, D-glucose (25 mM), and gentamicin (50 μg/mL). PCLS and PCKS were maintained at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator for 3 h and then incubated with either vehicle or lipids for 18 h. At the end of the incubation period, PCLS and PCKS were collected, weighted (2–3 mg weight) and kept at −80 °C in TRIzol reagent for further gene expression analyses. Animal studies were conducted in accordance with European Union Directive 2010/63/EU and were approved by the Animal Experimentation Ethics Committee of the University of Barcelona (approval number OB147/19).

Statistical analysis

In the analysis of the final multiomics dataset, features with missing values (measurements below the lower limit of detection) in more than 15% of patients were excluded. This stringent 15% missingness threshold was chosen to improve the robustness of the analysis while balancing data retention against the risk of biased or incomplete conclusions. Conversely, lipidomic and metabolomic features with missing values in fewer than 15% of patients were imputed using the lower limit of quantitation divided by four. For cytokines, missing values were imputed using the lower limit of detection specified in the Milliplex panel kit. The features of the final multiomics dataset were normalized through logarithmic transformation, and PCA was separately represented for each omics dataset to detect outliers. Heatmaps were computed through hierarchical clustering and according to variable correlations. For metabolomics data, a maximum value of selection was established, allowing for a 25% of all the metabolites to be included in the models. Then, the model with the selected number of components and features was fitted, and the loadings’ weights for each feature were estimated to build the networks. Features were related to each other through Pearson correlations, and only significant correlations >0.30 were included. This integrative analysis is constructed resembling the multiscale, multifactorial response networks described by Li et al.16. Our pipeline was used to interrogate the omics data sets for the two outcomes of patients with AD cirrhosis considered in this study (development of ACLF at 28 days and survival at 90 days). The feature relevance when explaining the clinical outcome was computed using the variance-weighted importance of each loading. For the ACLF development outcome, selected features were used in logistic regression models to compute the AUCs of their predictive values and their ORs. For survival as outcome, a Cox-based approach within the Fine and Gray model considering transplant as a competing event was used to compute the AUC predictive values and their OR, respectively. When multiple comparisons were performed, P values were adjusted to control for the false discovery rate (FDR). For correlations, Bonferroni corrections were applied. For the analysis of wet-lab experiments, the results are presented as mean ± SEM or median with interquartile range (IQR), depending on data distribution. For single comparisons, Mann–Whitney test was used for nonparametric distribution. For multiple comparisons, one-way or mixed-model ANOVA was used, followed by post-hoc testing. P values < 0.05 were considered significant. The representation of the results was performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.0.2 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA) and R version 4.0.2 (R-Foundation for statistical computing, www.Rproject.org).
