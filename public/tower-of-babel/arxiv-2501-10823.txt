Making mathematical online resources FAIR: at the example of small phylogenetic trees
Tabea Bacher, Marina Garrote-López, Christiane Görgen, Marius Neubert

Abstract
We report on the process of taking an early 2000’s mathematical library, the Small Phylogenetic Trees, and transforming it into a FAIR, modern, and sustainable repository for data from algebraic phylogenetics. This process is based on a three-fold strategy: (1) writing a software package which enables the user to reproduce results of the database; (2) setting up a user-friendly new website with cross links to theoretical publications, code snippets, and serialized output of computations; and (3) all-the-while documenting the steps we take in order to derive lessons learned which may be generalised to other such projects. This paper addresses (3). (1) is found in [ 7 ] , and (2) is located at algebraicphylogenetics.org .

In those early 2000s, the internet was being born. The now famous On-Line Encyclopedia of Integer Sequences

The two decades that have passed since have seen a steep rise in the importance of digital resources for mathematicians and in the importance and potential of web technology. For instance, digital object identifiers (DOIs), introduced at the turn of the century, have all but taken over the role of ISBNs identifying books. GitHub and institutional

Open-source software for symbolic computations is available to match corporate technologies. Mathematical results can be linked to formalizations and papers.

By nowadays standards, the library Small Phylogenetic Trees is all but modern. Is it FAIR? Yes and no, as we will see in the next section.

In this text, we take Small Phylogenetic Trees as an example of a valuable and influential mathematical resource with true and beautiful mathematical results but with a deprecated presentation, and we discuss how to use modern web technology to make it as FAIR as possible: taking the library way into the 21st century, while ensuring reproducibility, sustainability, and reusability of results. To do this, we pursue a three-fold strategy. For one, we launch a modern version of the original website, available now at
algebraicphylogenetics.org
. Two, we decide on one software that can provide computational proof for every result displayed on the website, and we use it to write a comprehensive package for algebraic phylogenetics; now available as
AlgebraicPhylogenetics.jl

[
7
]
. And, three, we supplement these two work packages by the text you are reading, documenting our thought processes and their implementation. This latter point had been neglected in the original book chapter on Small Phylogenetic Trees but we believe it to be central. Our detailed project report, informed by the expertise of academic research-data managers and working mathematicians, can function as a solid bridge for many a no-longer-actively-maintained math website into a future where we raise and preserve all sorts of digital mathematical treasures. To achieve this, we let ourselves be guided by the FAIR Principles and give general recommendations on how to FAIR your own mathematical database.

Changing the status quo

Online mathematical libraries like OEIS, LMFDB or Small Phylogenetic Trees have now taken the role of twentieth-century look-up tables and classifications in book form. They may be used to test out and contradict conjectures, to discover for what proportions of well-studied cases a statement is true or false, or to make available results that are hard to obtain—thus saving time, brain, and energy resources. In addition, online resources are much more easily updated than their print counterparts, gathering information in a single location rather than spread out over a series of articles.

For instance, computing the maximum likelihood degree of a three-taxa phylogenetic tree under the Jukes-Cantor model (which equals 23, as per the table in Figure
1
) might be easily doable on a laptop. However, it involves Gröbner basis computations and these are notoriously hard even in relatively small cases. Already the maximum likelihood degree of the five-star tree under the Jukes-Cantor model is unknown. In fact, without redoing any computations on your own, Small Phylogenetic Trees tells you its variety is of dimension 6, degree 115, and lives in a 51-th dimensional linear space. The cardinality of the smallest set of generators defining its ideal is 175 and the generators have degree 4 and 5. These invariants reflect model complexity.

Even more straightforwardly, if a researcher had a conjectured formula specifying the maximum likelihood degree of phylogenetic tree models, they could immediately test it with all such numbers available in the library. And they would know which, as of yet unknown, cases they could tackle to gather further evidence before attempting a theoretical proof.

At the outset

A lot of careful thought and effort has gone into Small Phylogenetic Trees, ensuring usability to this date. For instance, with the title as stated in the original publication
[
1
]
, the library can be found by any online search engine. Researchers may use the URL, provided by a trustworthy academic institution, to refer to Small Phylogenetic Trees or cite the corresponding book chapter. All original author names are listed on the landing page, central maintainers in bold at the top, and linked to their personal webpages: cf.  Figure
1
. As in a catalog and as was typical in the early 2000s, the landing page sports a table of contents: one retracing the mathematical background, one stating tree-labeling conventions, one for notation of indeterminates, one rectifying errata of the print version, one providing a table of all small trees considered, and one containing a handful of news items dated 2005 up to 2007.
The table of trees enables the user to dive deeper into the website structure, navigating through a subpage listing all models for a given tree, to a subpage displaying algebraic invariants of that particular choice of tree and model. Each subpage is supplied with a time stamp, typically 2006, and author name, typically the key maintainer Luis David Garcia Puente. Standard algebraic-phylogenetics definitions, illustrations, and code help the user trace a mathematical argument—addressing interoperability as well as reproducibility and trustworthiness of results.

The supply of all of the above-listed information is hugely helpful for researchers. However, whilst timeless in its logic, the original nested website design hinders the catalog to unfold its full potential.

Because every instance of a phylogenetic model lives on its own subpage—unintuitively numbered rather than named by subject matter, and reachable by clicking your way through a landing page, tree graphs, and model names—the unfamiliar user can experience some trouble efficiently navigating the website or, worse, might fail to get a grasp of its full content and functionality. The citeable book chapter can be of help here but it provides no detailed usage instructions, for instance about the licensing of results. Many links are broken. Together with twenty-year-old time stamps, the website gives no hint about active maintenance or completeness of results. Interoperability of the available code is hindered by a split into distinct files with no metadata and no detailed software specification available and very sparse documentation; leaving the user to guess which version of the named software they might need, how exactly variable names in the code correspond to variable names in theory, and which—to the original maintainers standard—steps are missing to obtain the listed invariants.

We decided to address these concerns carefully and separately by splitting the computation of results from their display: by supplementing a new website with a new software package. This enables us to both modernize the presentation and, independently, to provide proof in a single software package that stores all necessary information (definitions, commands, and outputs) in one place. The software we decide on is the computer-algebra system
OSCAR

[
5
]
, based on
Julia
. It is able to provide more than text files of output, and can in fact save mathematical objects together with their context: e.g.  for each polynomial a specification of the ring environment in which the polynomial lives. The software is further able to call both symbolic and numerical methods, so there is no need to switch systems when trying out different approaches to compute invariants.

Usability, both technically as well as visually, is our foremost concern, and our website-and-software approach allows us to differentiate between levels of user need and skill. The website itself shall be easy to navigate for the interested pure mathematician or newcomer with no coding skills. Additionally, each result displayed shall be linked back to a paragraph in the software package’s documentation as well as to the respective lines of underlying code and a serialization file; ideally supplemented also with citations of theory. All technical details are thus made available for advanced or applied researchers to embed existing computational results and environments directly into their own work. This interconnectedness of data (invariants, formulae, formalizations, code, and publications) enables both human users and machines to grasp mathematical results and generate knowledge.

Making a plan

For efficient teamwork, we draft three plans: one data layout plan and two research-data management plans. The former describes what sorts of data we find on Small Phylogenetic Trees and how results build on each other. The latter formulate a strategy for software and website each, clarifying how to treat all occurring files and data formats during and after the project.

FAIRness discussed

We made improvements both in the handling of mathematical data as well as in their metadata description. These are, in particular, of the following kind.

Findability

The new name and domain name,
“Algebraic-phylogenetics database”
and
algebraicphylogenetics.org
, refer directly to the mathematical field, a subfield of algebraic statistics. We state the respective MSC identifiers 62R01, 65H10, 92D15, 68W30, and 14Q30 on the website. The homepage has its own citation guideline
[
3
]
which is distinct from text reports and from the software citation for added precision and appropriate credit. Each past and current version of the website is preserved via its GitHub/Zenodo release. These means increase findability both for the human user and for automated online search engines.

Additionally, we link all authors to their orcids and personal webpages, and we set up a maintainers email address. This enables users to directly and correctly identify lasting contact points. Relevant publications in algebraic phylogenetics are linked to via persistent DOIs.

Accessibility

Metadata for each combination of phylogenetic tree, model, and algebraic characterization are gathered in machine-readable
.yaml
files, hosted on GitHub and accessible via GitHub’s REST API. Each mathematical object is available in open-source
.mrdi
files, containing valuable metadata (such as which polynomial ring computations were performed in) in machine-readable
JSON
format. Formulae are machine-readable rendered using
MathJax
, trees coded in
tikzjax
. Clear license statements on the GitHub projects underlying the website as well as the software give access to any interested user.

Interoperability

Regarding semantic interoperability, we reiterate standard notation, document the workflow and provide usage examples for the software package to facilitate integration into external projects.

Maximum computational interoperability is achieved by choosing the open-source computer algebra system

Reusability

Extensive documentation both on the website itself and externally in
OSCAR

Every result is further linked to a copyable

X

We are confident to have thus created a database that is as FAIR as our technical expertise, time, and research-data infrastructure existing for mathematics can enable us to build.

Lessons learned

Being a rather large team of mathematicians with pretty diverse areas of expertise, we built obstacles for ourselves: inefficiently handling
git
repositories, using newest technology, and managing three projects simultaneously. In particular, we learned the following.

The distributed and version-controlled system to collaboratively work on both software and website—should it be the corporate GitHub, de-facto standard in coding mathematics but without any guarantee of persistency, data security or long-term financial implications? Or should it be a
gitlab
repository with academic credibility and long-term security but also with added hurdles in the creation of accounts and without the wider visibility a single big provider can offer? Unable to decide, at some point we handled master branches of six different repositories, only some actual forks of others: three on GitHub forking
OSCAR
, one for each
AlgebraicPhylogenetics.jl
,
AlgebraicStatistics.jl
, and serialization; one which tested our experimental
OSCAR
code externally; one on GitHub for the website; and one on
gitlab
for the same website with different code and no shared history. We were using modern web technology but effectively losing a lot of functionality. Drafting our research-data management plan even before allocating time and funding and pre-checking technical and legal requirements of academic vs industry website hosting (like dynamic content or the call of external data) before creating repositories could have avoided this confusion.

At the time of writing our software package, the interoperable
.mrdi
serialization was fully functional and thoroughly thought through but it had just been launched: as a consequence, our new object type
PhylogeneticModel
was not yet available. We naively assumed it to be a tuple of serialized components—simple ones like
integer
; highly complex ones like
QQMPoly
, a matrix whose entries are polynomials in some ring over the rationals. This was sadly not the case, and nested recursive calling of metadata had to be resolved with tailored methods provided by the
OSCAR
group. Whilst the result was definitely worth our efforts, our naive understanding of the technology paired with functionalities in active development required a time commitment we did not foresee. In particular, Marius Neubert continued to work for free months after his student assistance had officially ended (preparing himself exceptionally well for a career in academia).

Managing one team to work on the software, one on the website, and one on project documentation—each with a fluctuant overlap of members in different roles, plus different external communication—made it hard for us to be efficient. Handwritten notes taken in Zoom meetings, email reminders, Slack messages, and phone calls between individuals tended to increase confusion about rather than clarity of deadlines and priorities. Typical academic hurdles like researchers changing institutions and international travel were the straw that broke the camel’s back and led to a project runtime of nearly two years, rather than the optimistically anticipated couple of months. Too late we realized that digital, free project-management tools exist to help keep track of our decentralized but highly dependent tasks.

How to

The algebraic-phylogenetics database in its most recent form has made use of the newest web and mathematical research-data management tools to ensure usability and longevity of results. Discussions with data stewards, information specialists, computer scientists, computer algebraists, mathematicians in our community or owning similar projects in other fields have helped us shape an idea of what is possible and what is needed to make algebraic-phylogenetics data sufficiently FAIR.

In particular, we have achieved this by allowing us to be guided by the following key concepts.

Usability

Who uses the database actively? Who does not but would? How can we make content understandable for all?
E.g., researchers use it as a look-up table but find it hard to get a grasp of the full content and its limitations; some expert knowledge is needed to reuse results.

Needs

How can we lower the entry barrier? What would people using the database ideally do? How can they integrate it easily into their daily research life?
E.g., link computational content and theory more directly.

Gaps

What has stopped people from adding to the original presentation? How has data evolved since and how can we merge new and old results?
E.g., no contribution guidelines available, data spread out over web and articles, each researcher writes their own code to fill gaps between presented results and question of interest; set up a collaborative software project.

Sustainability

The job-interview question: where will our database be ten years from now? The math will still be true. Will it be findable, accessible, interoperable, usable?
E.g., find a trustworthy institution to host the website for the foreseeable future, independently of career trajectories; community of maintainers with shared contact info, one person with main responsibility.

To reach this point, we have based our work on advice from research-data initiatives like
FAIRsFAIR.org
and
mardi4nfdi.org
as well as recommendations
[
4
]
and implementation discussions following the original publication of FAIR Principles.
We learned on the way that involving all interest groups is key to get a broad picture of what is possible and how we should manage priorities: e.g., by following the outlined website-and-software-and-paper approach and focusing on reproducing existing data first.

Now, of course, programming languages die, software becomes outdated, web technology evolves super fast---and we have to face the reality that our, at this point very modern presentation of the database, might, come time, be as outdated as look-up tables in book form are today. To mitigate the entropy, we put our main priority on documentation: this includes making all code readable for humans, providing versioning of all digital resources, having permanent digital identifiers, and interlinking as many objects (models, papers, code, etc.) as possible. The FAIR Principles require metadata to be persistent, even if the data themselves are no longer available. To achieve this, we plan as a next step to put relevant metadata of the classification on Wikidata to supplement our Zenodo snapshots. This way, both data and metadata can be automatically harvested by online search engines and integrated into broader knowledge graphs for mathematics and neighboring disciplines.
