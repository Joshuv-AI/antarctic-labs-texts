Bilateral anterior lens luxation with secondary glaucoma strongly associated with self-application of a percussion massage gun to closed eyelids: a case report
Authors: Yu Chen, Zipei Wang, Rongrong Zhou, Yiyu Zhao, Xinyu Liu, Yue Gou, Huixuan Ren, Bing Xie, Dezhi Li, Hong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPercussion massage guns (PMGs) have gained widespread popularity for muscle recovery; however, standardized safety guidelines for application to anatomically vulnerable regions are lacking. Previously reported PMG-associated ocular injuries have been limited to unilateral or mild bilateral involvement. We report the first documented case of bilateral anterior lens luxation with secondary glaucoma strongly associated with self-application of a PMG to closed eyelids.Case presentationA 62-year-old Chinese male with type 2 diabetes mellitus and bilateral meibomian gland dysfunction presented with progressive visual decline and headache for 20 and 10 days, respectively, beginning approximately 7 days after discontinuing self-application of a PMG to closed eyelids (medium speed, 30–33 Hz; 10 min per session, 2–3 times daily for 3 consecutive days) used to relieve ocular dryness. Visual acuity was 0.12 OD and 0.2 OS, with intraocular pressure 51 mmHg OD and 49 mmHg OS. Ultrasound biomicroscopy revealed bilateral anterior lens luxation with 360° zonular dehiscence and complete angle closure. Hereditary, pseudoexfoliative, and biometric causes (including microspherophakia and phacomorphic glaucoma) were considered unlikely. Both eyes underwent sequential 25-gauge pars plana vitrectomy with lensectomy and closed four-point scleral fixation of a posterior chamber intraocular lens. At the latest follow-up (12 months postoperatively), best-corrected visual acuity was 1.0 OD and 0.8 OS, intraocular pressure was normal without antiglaucoma medication, and no glaucomatous optic neuropathy was detected on RNFL OCT.ConclusionsPMG application to the eyelids may be associated with severe bilateral ocular injury, including complete zonular dehiscence and secondary glaucoma, particularly in eyes with predisposing factors such as age-related zonular weakness or diabetes-related extracellular matrix changes. Manufacturers should incorporate explicit safety warnings against periorbital use. Clinicians should proactively guide diabetic patients with dry eye disease toward appropriate ophthalmic care to prevent harmful self-treatment.

Background

Percussive therapy is a physical therapy modality that integrates traditional massage with high-frequency vibration principles, typically utilizing a handheld device—the percussion massage gun (PMG)—to deliver rhythmic percussive force to targeted muscle regions [1]. Systematic reviews have demonstrated PMG efficacy for post-exercise muscle recovery, and its use has expanded rapidly among the general public [2, 3].

With the expanding PMG user population, cases of ocular injuries resulting from improper application to the eye and periorbital region have been increasingly reported in the literature, including anterior subcapsular cataract [4], traumatic cataract [5], and unilateral lens subluxation with secondary acute angle-closure glaucoma [6]. However, all previously reported cases were either unilateral or involved only mild bilateral injuries, and no case of PMG-associated bilateral anterior lens luxation with secondary glaucoma has been documented to date.

Herein, we report a case of a 62-year-old male patient with type 2 diabetes mellitus and dry eye disease who developed bilateral anterior lens luxation (i.e., complete 360° zonular dehiscence resulting in loss of lens support, anterior displacement of the lens into close apposition with the posterior iris surface, and consequent pupillary block) with secondary glaucoma after self-applying a PMG to closed eyelids. Following pars plana vitrectomy combined with closed four-point scleral fixation (CFSF) of a posterior chamber intraocular lens (IOL) with continuous-loop suture [7], the patient achieved sustained visual recovery and normalization of intraocular pressure (IOP).

Case presentation

A 62-year-old Chinese male was first seen at our department on Day + 30 (April 21, 2025). Progressive bilateral visual decline had begun on Day + 10 (April 1, 2025), accompanied by mild photophobia, and a dull frontotemporal headache had developed on Day + 20 (April 11, 2025), without tearing, nausea, or vomiting. His past medical history included type 2 diabetes mellitus for 7 years (HbA1c < 6.5% on lifestyle management) and hypertension for 15 years (controlled with oral antihypertensive therapy). He denied any history of ocular trauma or ophthalmic surgery, and reported no family history of hereditary connective tissue disorders. General examination showed normal limb proportions without arachnodactyly or joint hypermobility.

On further detailed questioning, the patient reported that prior to the onset of symptoms, he had been experiencing chronic asthenopia and bilateral ocular dryness, for which he did not consult an ophthalmologist. He purchased a commercially available percussion massage gun (PMG; BOOSTER M2) on his own. Per manufacturer specifications, the device features a 50-mm ball-shaped attachment head, a maximum massage force of ≈ 245 N (≈ 25 kgf), and a vibration frequency range of 900–3200 revolutions per minute (15–53 Hz). The patient had previously used this PMG to massage the lumbar muscles to relieve muscle soreness. In the present instance, the patient directly applied the ball-shaped attachment head to the closed eyelids of both eyes in an attempt to alleviate the dryness symptoms, using the medium speed setting (approximately 30–33 Hz), for approximately 10 min per session, 2–3 times daily, continuously for approximately 3 days beginning Day 0 (March 22, 2025), and discontinued use around Day + 3. No immediate ocular pain or exacerbation of discomfort was noted during use. Approximately 7 days after discontinuation, the patient began to notice progressively worsening vision in both eyes.

Ophthalmic examination revealed: visual acuity was 0.12 (Snellen 20/160) OD and 0.2 (Snellen 20/100) OS, with no improvement with best correction. Non-contact tonometry revealed an IOP of 51 mmHg OD and 49 mmHg OS. Slit-lamp biomicroscopy demonstrated bilateral mild conjunctival injection, corneal edema, markedly shallow anterior chambers, pupil diameter of approximately 4 mm, sluggish pupillary light reflexes, and lens opacification; detailed slit-lamp grading of the cataract was precluded by the severe corneal edema, but intraoperative inspection characterized the lens opacity as LOCS III C1N2P1 (mild cortical, moderate nuclear, mild posterior subcapsular) in both eyes. Assessment for phacodonesis was likewise limited at presentation by the corneal edema and markedly shallow anterior chamber. Ultrasound biomicroscopy (UBM) revealed bilateral anterior displacement of the lens into close contact with the posterior iris surface, with no identifiable zonular echoes circumferentially (indicating 360° complete zonular dehiscence with anterior lens luxation), markedly shallow anterior chambers (central anterior chamber depth: 1.21 mm OD and 1.33 mm OS), and 360° angle closure (Fig. 2A and B). The posterior lens capsule was faintly visible but not optimally resolved on UBM owing to increased intralenticular echogenicity, and the limited spatial resolution of UBM for thin zonular fibers is acknowledged. Fundus imaging (ultra-widefield Optos and OCT) was limited by media opacity; no retinal hemorrhage, exudation, or structural abnormality was identified in the visualized areas. Comprehensive dry eye evaluation indicated bilateral meibomian gland dysfunction.

Regarding differential diagnosis, hereditary connective tissue disorders associated with ectopia lentis (Marfan syndrome, homocystinuria, Weill-Marchesani syndrome) were considered unlikely given the late age of onset (62 years), the absence of systemic manifestations, and a negative family history. Microspherophakia was excluded by the absence of a spherophakic lens configuration on UBM. Phacomorphic glaucoma was excluded by optical biometry (IOLMaster 700) showing normal lens thickness (4.91 mm OD, 4.96 mm OS) and axial length (23.62 mm OD, 23.77 mm OS), indicating that the angle closure resulted from anterior lens displacement rather than lens enlargement. Pseudoexfoliation syndrome (PEX), occult blunt trauma, and age-related zonulopathy were considered and not supported by available clinical evidence: no pseudoexfoliative material was observed on the lens capsule or within the anterior chamber intraoperatively, the patient denied any history of ocular trauma, and there was no prior record of glaucoma or abnormal intraocular pressure. However, severe corneal edema at presentation precluded definitive slit-lamp evaluation for pseudoexfoliative material, and subclinical predisposing factors—such as age-related zonular weakness, occult PEX masked by corneal edema, or diabetes-mediated glycation of zonular microfibrils—cannot be entirely ruled out. Given the clearly documented temporal association between PMG application to closed eyelids and the subsequent onset of bilateral visual decline, together with UBM evidence of no identifiable zonular echoes circumferentially, the bilateral anterior lens luxation and secondary glaucoma were strongly associated with repetitive vibratory loading from the PMG, likely acting as a precipitating factor against a background of possible subclinical zonular susceptibility.

The admission diagnoses were as follows: (1) bilateral secondary glaucoma (phacogenic); (2) bilateral anterior lens luxation; (3) bilateral cataract (etiology under investigation; traumatic contribution suspected); (4) type 2 diabetes mellitus; (5) hypertension; (6) bilateral meibomian gland dysfunction.

Following admission, initial management focused on controlling IOP and stabilizing systemic status. Topical timolol and brinzolamide eye drops (each twice daily, both eyes) were administered for IOP reduction, supplemented by intravenous infusion of 20% mannitol 300 mL to reduce IOP. Blood pressure and glycemic control were concurrently optimized. Once systemic conditions were stabilized and IOP was partially controlled, surgery was performed sequentially on each eye under retrobulbar anesthesia. The right eye underwent surgery first on Day + 37, followed by the left eye on Day + 46 (an interval of 9 days). An identical procedure was performed on both eyes.

Both eyes underwent transconjunctival 25-gauge pars plana vitrectomy with lensectomy, followed by CFSF of a posterior chamber IOL using a continuous-loop suture technique [7]. In both eyes, the luxated lens demonstrated free mobility within the anterior segment without any zonular tethering, and no residual zonular fibers were identifiable at the lens equator at any clock hour, consistent with complete 360° zonular dehiscence. The posterior capsule was confirmed intact prior to and throughout en-bloc lensectomy with the vitrectomy cutter. Implanted IOL powers were + 20.5 D (OD) and + 20.0 D (OS). At completion, both IOLs were well-centered with watertight wounds.

Following discharge after right-eye surgery, the patient was managed as an outpatient on continuous topical timolol 0.5% and brinzolamide 1.0% (twice daily, both eyes), without intravenous mannitol or oral carbonic anhydrase inhibitors. Despite this maintained topical regimen, left-eye IOP rose progressively from 49 to 60 mmHg by Day + 45, illustrating that medical therapy alone could not relieve the mechanical angle closure imposed by the anteriorly luxated lens and reinforcing the indication for prompt surgical decompression.

The postoperative topical medication regimen consisted of tobramycin–dexamethasone eye drops (Tobradex®), administered four times daily during the first week, three times daily during the second week, twice daily during the third week, and once daily during the fourth week, in a stepwise taper. The patient was also instructed to apply warm compresses to both eyes to alleviate dry eye symptoms. At the 2-month postoperative follow-up (Day + 101), the patient reported no complaints. Uncorrected visual acuity was 0.8 (Snellen 20/25) in both eyes; best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) was 1.0 (Snellen 20/20) in the right eye (− 1.00/−0.50 × 150°) and 1.0 (Snellen 20/20) in the left eye (− 0.50/−1.00 × 140°). IOP was 18 mmHg in the right eye and 20 mmHg in the left eye. Slit-lamp biomicroscopy revealed bilateral clear corneas, normal anterior chambers, and well-centered IOLs. UBM demonstrated deepened anterior chambers (4.07 mm OD and 3.74 mm OS), open angles, and the IOL secured in the ciliary sulcus (Fig. 2C and D — postoperative panels). Postoperative ultra-widefield retinal imaging and macular OCT demonstrated no abnormalities at the 2-month follow-up (data not shown). No suture loosening or erosion was observed at the scleral fixation sites. No IOL-related or retinal complications were observed during follow-up.

Extended Follow-Up at 12 Months: The patient was reassessed on Day + 413 (May 9, 2026), approximately 12 months after the second surgery. BCVA was 1.0 OD and 0.8 OS, with intraocular pressure well controlled bilaterally without antiglaucoma medication. Both intraocular lenses remained well centered. Dilated fundus examination demonstrated normal-appearing optic discs bilaterally, with sharp disc margins, healthy pink neuroretinal rims, and no pallor, notching, or disc hemorrhage. Peripapillary RNFL OCT (Cirrus HD-OCT, Optic Disc Cube 200 × 200 protocol) showed average RNFL thickness of 105 μm OD and 91 μm OS, with all four quadrants within the normal reference range bilaterally. Average cup-to-disc ratios were 0.46 OD and 0.48 OS. These findings indicate no evidence of glaucomatous optic neuropathy at 12-month follow-up despite the preoperative IOP elevations of 51 mmHg (OD) and 60 mmHg (OS), supporting the timeliness of surgical decompression in this case.

Specular microscopy showed endothelial cell density of 2316 cells/mm² OD and 1190 cells/mm² OS, with central corneal thickness of 565 μm and 564 μm, respectively, and both corneas remained clinically clear. The reduced left-eye endothelial cell density likely reflects the more severe preoperative phacogenic insult (peak IOP 60 mmHg; UBM-documented anterior-chamber depth of 1.33 mm with iridocorneal apposition), and long-term endothelial monitoring is planned.

Discussion and conclusions

The patient’s longstanding type 2 diabetes mellitus and bilateral meibomian gland dysfunction —a recognized association in diabetic patients [8–10]—prompted the self-application of a PMG to closed eyelids without ophthalmic consultation. This sequence underscores the need for clinicians to proactively guide diabetic patients with dry eye disease toward appropriate ocular surface management, preventing harmful self-treatment.

Zonular fibers are composed primarily of fibrillin-1, a long-lived extracellular matrix protein [11]. Chronic hyperglycemia promotes accumulation of advanced glycation end-products (AGEs) and pathological cross-linking in similar long-lived proteins [12–14], and diabetes has been clinically associated with zonular weakness and IOL dislocation [15]. The patient’s 7-year diabetic history may therefore have rendered the zonular apparatus more susceptible to vibratory injury, although direct measurement of AGEs in zonular tissue is lacking and this remains an extrapolation.

Commercial PMGs typically operate at 17–53 Hz [16], with each percussive impact delivering 0.53–0.98 foot-pounds of energy [5]—sufficient to injure intraocular structures. Vibration-induced ocular injury has been documented in occupational and recreational settings [17–20], and a recent biomechanical review synthesizes energy thresholds underlying intraocular injury from blunt impact [21]. Because the eyelid is the thinnest cutaneous structure in the body, direct application of the percussion head to closed eyelids allows near-unattenuated transmission of these forces to the globe, supporting the mechanism of repetitive vibratory loading proposed in this case.

A noteworthy feature of this case is the 7-day delay between PMG cessation (Day + 3) and the onset of visual decline (Day + 10). Rather than two discrete events, the sequential appearance of blurred vision followed by IOP elevation most likely represents a continuum of the same pathophysiological process: repetitive vibratory loading produced progressive subclinical zonular damage that continued to advance under physiological stresses (accommodation, ocular movements, blinking) until complete dehiscence occurred [22], at which point anterior lens displacement and pupillary block produced the observed clinical presentation.

The anteriorly luxated lens causes angle closure and acute IOP elevation through a pupillary block mechanism [23, 24], and the bilateral clinical presentation in this case was consistent with the typical features of phacogenic secondary angle-closure glaucoma. The progressive left-eye IOP rise to 60 mmHg while awaiting surgery confirmed that removal of the anteriorly luxated lens is the definitive approach to IOP reduction [23].

An atypical feature of this case is that both lenses were displaced anteriorly rather than posteriorly. Aqueous misdirection was considered; however, intraoperative findings argued against this mechanism: scleral incisions produced no vitreous expulsion, and the anterior chamber deepened only after lens extraction, not after vitrectomy alone. Three factors more plausibly account for the anterior displacement: complete circumferential zonular dehiscence with an intact posterior capsule rendered the lens–capsule complex a free-floating disc; pre-existing shallow anterior chambers (central ACD 1.21/1.33 mm OD/OS on UBM) with normal axial length and lens thickness indicate an angle-closure-prone configuration in which any forward lens shift readily produces pupillary block; and the only previously reported PMG-associated lens dislocation [6] likewise showed an anteriorly displaced, well-centered lens, suggesting a shared pathomechanism.

Compared with previously reported PMG-associated ocular injuries—mild bilateral anterior subcapsular cataract [4], unilateral traumatic cataract [5], and unilateral lens subluxation with poor visual outcome [6]—the present case is the first to demonstrate complete bilateral 360° zonular dehiscence with anterior lens luxation, while achieving favorable bilateral visual recovery (BCVA 1.0 OD, 0.8 OS) through timely surgical intervention.

CFSF with a continuous-loop suture [7], employed in both eyes here, offers superior IOL centration over the traditional two-point technique, with established efficacy and refractive predictability [25, 26]. The well-centered IOLs and favorable visual outcomes at 12 months further support its reliability for surgical management of anterior lens luxation with phacogenic glaucoma.

PMG application to other anatomically vulnerable regions has similarly caused rhabdomyolysis [27], vertebral artery dissection [28, 29], and benign paroxysmal positional vertigo [30]; yet standardized safety guidelines remain absent.

This study has several limitations. First, as a single case report, the causal relationship was established through clinical inference based on temporal correlation, exclusion of alternative etiologies, and biomechanical plausibility. Although the specific operating parameters of the PMG were obtained, the vibratory energy actually transmitted to intraocular structures depends on eyelid tissue characteristics, contact angle, and applied pressure, and precise quantification requires further investigation. Second, although follow-up has now been extended to 12 months postoperatively with stable visual and structural outcomes, longer-term observation remains warranted to confirm the durability of four-point scleral IOL fixation. Continued endothelial monitoring is also planned given the borderline left-eye endothelial cell density. Finally, although intraoperative findings argued against aqueous misdirection in this case, this mechanism cannot be definitively excluded without intraoperative vitreous-cavity pressure measurement.

In conclusion, this case documents, to our knowledge, the first reported instance of bilateral anterior lens luxation with secondary glaucoma strongly associated with PMG use, with sustained visual recovery following timely surgical management. PMGs should not be applied to the eyelids or periorbital regions, manufacturers should incorporate explicit safety warnings, and clinicians should proactively guide diabetic patients with dry eye disease toward appropriate ocular surface care to prevent harmful self-treatment.
