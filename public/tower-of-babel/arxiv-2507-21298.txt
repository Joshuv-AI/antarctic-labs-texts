Slomads Rising: Stay-Length Shifts in Digital-Nomad Travel, United States 2019–2024
Harrison Katz, Erica Savage

Abstract
We analyze every booking‑weighted U.S. Airbnb reservation created between 1 January 2019 and 31 December 2024. The booking‑weighted mean nights‑per‑booking (NPB) rose from 3.7 in the pre‑COVID baseline to a post‑2021 plateau of 4.1–4.4—an increase of roughly ten percent—while the median shifted permanently from two to three nights and the weighted standard deviation almost doubled to seven nights. A two‑parameter log‑normal density outperforms Poisson‑lognormal and Gamma rivals ( Δ ​ AIC ≥ 6.5 × 10 7 \Delta\mathrm{AIC}\geq 6.5\times 10^{7} ), confirming the emergence of a heavy‑tailed stay‑length distribution. Weighted negative‑binomial regression shows that, relative to the COVID‑restriction phase, post‑vaccine bookings are 6.5 % shorter and pre‑COVID bookings 16 % shorter, with only mild seasonal effects ( ± 3 % \pm 3\% ). A two‑part hurdle model reveals that the probability of a ≥ 28 \geq 28 -night stay almost doubled during restrictions (1.5 % to 2.9 %) and has stabilized at 2.2 % post‑vaccine, whereas the conditional mean length of those long stays remains near 43–46 nights. Hence the higher average NPB is driven chiefly by a greater frequency of month‑plus bookings rather than longer durations among existing long‑stay guests. Finally, a SARIMA ( 0 , 1 , 1 ) ​ ( 0 , 1 , 1 ) 12 (0,1,1)(0,1,1)_{12} model augmented with pandemic‑phase dummies reduces AIC by more than 20 and leaves white‑noise residuals, demonstrating that regime indicators—not higher‑order dynamics—capture the structural level shift. Together the results show that remote‑work “slomads” have durably thickened the long‑stay tail of the U.S. short‑term‑rental market, implying a need for heavy‑tailed pricing, inventory, and tax models. Keywords: Airbnb; Nights per booking; pandemic; weighted statistics; negative-binomial regression; United States



1
Introduction

Length of stay—here operationalised as nights per booking (NPB)—is a
first‑order driver of revenue yield, labour scheduling and
environmental pressure in the visitor economy.
A single‑night shift in the mean NPB can reshape occupancy curves,
staffing requirements and per‑trip carbon footprints, so destinations
and accommodation providers track NPB as closely as arrivals or RevPAR.

Classic empirical work finds that, in many mature mass‑tourism
destinations, average stays have gradually shortened under the combined
influence of low‑cost carriers, modular itineraries and a
“short‑break” culture

(
Alegre and Pou 2006
;
Gokovali et al. 2007
;
Martínez-García and Raya 2008
;
de Menezes et al. 2008
;
Salmasi et al. 2012
;
Thrane 2012
;
Yang and Zhang 2015
)
.
Meta‑analyses and cross‑country panels show that younger,
budget‑constrained tourists often allocate a fixed holiday budget across
multiple brief trips rather than one long vacation

(
Gössling et al. 2018
)
.
Conversely, specific segments—senior travellers, family parties,
nature‑based tourists and long‑haul visitors—regularly book extended
vacations

(
Alén et al. 2014
;
Boto-García et al. 2019
;
Bavik et al. 2021
;
Santos et al. 2015
;
Gómez-Déniz and Pérez-Rodríguez 2019
)
, and peer‑to‑peer rentals
exhibit a distinct heavy tail of week‑plus stays

(
Tussyadiah and Pesonen 2016
;
Moreno-Izquierdo et al. 2019
)
.

The COVID‑19 pandemic injected a new layer of complexity.
Lock‑downs initially curtailed mobility, yet remote‑work policies
simultaneously enabled a cohort of “work‑from‑anywhere” nomads whose
stays stretch beyond 28 nights

(
Baños-Pino et al. 2021
;
Jeon and Yang 2021
;
Santos and Moreira 2021
;
Zheng et al. 2021
)
.
Large‑scale evidence on how this cohort reshaped the overall NPB
distribution in the United States, however, remains scarce.

At the firm level, Airbnb and other short‑term‑rental platforms
report an increasing share of long‑stay bookings and a growing emphasis
on “livability” amenities

(
Chen and Xie 2017
;
Ert et al. 2016
;
Guttentag and Smith 2017
;
Wang and Nicolau 2017
;
Tang et al. 2019
;
Poon and Huang 2017
)
.
Because heavy‑tailed stay‑length distributions confound normal and
gamma assumptions, revenue managers are turning to negative‑binomial,
hurdle and zero‑inflated count models

(
Lambert 1992
;
Böhning et al. 1999
;
Gurmu and Trivedi 1996
;
Famoye and Singh 2006
;
Cameron and Trivedi 1986
;
Cameron and Trivedi 1998
)
and to recently proposed
bimodal or bounded‑support variants

(
Gómez-Déniz et al. 2020
;
Gómez-Déniz et al. 2020
;
Liu et al. 2018
;
Zhang et al. 2016
)
.

Pandemic‑era shifts were not confined to stay length: a normalized‑
L_{1}
analysis of Airbnb booking windows showed a wholesale contraction toward departure dates across major U.S. cities
(
Katz et al. 2025
)
, and platform‑level currency‑share volatility spiked in 2020, signaling abrupt changes in guest origin and payment behavior
(
Katz and Weiss 2025
)
.

This study contributes by mapping U.S. Airbnb NPB trajectories from
2019 through 2024—covering the pre-COVID baseline, restriction period
and vaccine era—using booking-count weights to recover population
frequencies. Combining weighted maximum-likelihood density estimation,
negative-binomial and hurdle count models, and SARIMA time-series
analysis, we (i) quantify shifts in the entire NPB distribution,
(ii) isolate pandemic-phase effects net of seasonality, and
(iii) gauge the relative importance of an emergent long-stay mode.
By situating the U.S. evidence within the diverse global record
summarized above, we provide actionable insight for destinations and
platforms seeking to balance short‑ and long‑stay tourism in the
post‑pandemic marketplace.

2
Materials and Methods

2.1
Data Structure and Weighting

The study draws on all U.S. Airbnb reservations created between
1 January 2019 and 31 December 2024.
Each booking contributes a stay‐length (nights‑per‑booking, NPB)

y_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
and an integer weight
w_{i}
equal to the count of
identical reservations collapsed into row
i
.
Bookings longer than 180 nights are excluded.
Three pandemic phases are defined:
Pre‑COVID (to 14 Mar 2020), COVID‑restriction (15 Mar 2020–14 Jun 2021,
baseline) and Post‑vaccine (15 Jun 2021 onward).

2.2
Weighted Descriptive Statistics

For calendar month
t
let
\mathcal{I}_{t}
denote the index set of
bookings. The weight‐adjusted mean, variance and empirical quartiles
are

\bar{y}_{t}=\frac{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{t}}w_{i}y_{i}}{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{t}}w_{i}},\qquad s_{t}^{2}=\frac{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{t}}w_{i}(y_{i}-\bar{y}_{t})^{2}}{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{t}}w_{i}},

with medians and quantiles obtained by inverting the weighted empirical
CDF
(
Arachchige et al. 2020
)
.

2.3
Parametric Density Estimation

To characterize the full distribution of nights‑per‑booking (NPB) we fit
three candidate parametric families: the continuous Gamma and
log‑normal, which are standard in hospitality analytics, and the

discrete
Poisson–lognormal (PLN), whose support matches the
integer nature of NPB. For each model we maximise the
booking‑weighted log‑likelihood

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\arg\max_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\,\sum_{i}w_{i}\,\log f(y_{i}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}),

where
w_{i}
is the number of identical reservations collapsed into
row
i
. Starting values are obtained from weighted first and second
moments; optimization uses L‑BFGS‑B for the two continuous families and
Nelder–Mead for the PLN because the latter’s gradient is numerically
unstable near the optimum. Models are ranked by Akaike and Bayesian
information criteria (AIC, BIC), and their empirical adequacy is judged
visually with overlays of empirical versus
fitted cumulative‑distribution functions.

2.4
Negative‑Binomial Regression

Over‑dispersion (
\operatorname{Var}(y_{i})>\operatorname{E}(y_{i})
)
motivates a weighted negative‑binomial GLM:

\log\operatorname{E}[y_{i}]=\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}\mathbb{1}_{\mathrm{Post},i}+\beta_{2}\mathbb{1}_{\mathrm{Pre},i}+\sum_{m=2}^{12}\gamma_{m}\,\mathbb{1}\{\mathrm{month}(i)=m\},

where January is the reference month.
Exact weighted likelihoods are obtained with

countreg::glmnb()
.
Incidence‑rate ratios (IRR) and Wald 95 % confidence intervals are
reported.

2.5
Two‑Part “Long‑Stay” Model

Long stays are defined as
y_{i}\geq 28
. Part 1 predicts the probability of a long stay via a weighted
logistic model

\Pr(y_{i}\geq 28)=\operatorname{logit}^{-1}\!\bigl(\alpha_{0}+\alpha_{1}\mathbb{1}_{\mathrm{Post},i}+\alpha_{2}\mathbb{1}_{\mathrm{Pre},i}+\textstyle\sum_{m}\delta_{m}\mathbb{1}\{\mathrm{month}=m\}\bigr).

Part 2 conditions on
y_{i}\geq 28
and regresses NPB on the same
covariates using a weighted negative‑binomial GLM:

\log\operatorname{E}[y_{i}\mid y_{i}\geq 28]=\zeta_{0}+\zeta_{1}\mathbb{1}_{\mathrm{Post},i}+\zeta_{2}\mathbb{1}_{\mathrm{Pre},i}+\textstyle\sum_{m}\eta_{m}\mathbb{1}\{\mathrm{month}=m\}.

2.6
Monthly Time‑Series Model

Phase indicators are first‑differenced so that regressors and
response share the same order of integration.
The differenced specification is

(1-\nabla)(1-\nabla_{12})\bar{y}_{t}=\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}\nabla D_{\mathrm{Post},t}+\beta_{2}\nabla D_{\mathrm{Pre},t}+(1-\theta B)(1-\Theta B^{12})\varepsilon_{t},

where
D_{\cdot,t}
are the undifferenced dummies.
Model selection follows the AIC/BIC protocol described earlier.

2.7
Diagnostics and Robustness

Model adequacy was assessed with information criteria (AIC, BIC),
weighted Pearson
\chi^{2}
statistics, deviance-residual graphics and
Ljung–Box portmanteau tests of time-series residuals. Computations were performed in

R
4.3
(
R Core Team 2023
)
using

fitdistrplus
(
Delignette-Muller and Dutang 2015
)
for weighted density
estimation,
MASS
(
Venables and Ripley 2002
)
for negative-binomial
and hurdle models,
matrixStats
(
Bengtsson 2023
)
for weighted
moments and quantiles,
fable
(
O’Hara-Wild et al. 2021
)
for SARIMA
forecasting, and the
tidyverse
(
Wickham et al. 2019
)
suite for data
manipulation and visualization.

4
Discussion

The national‐scale evidence presented here shows that COVID‑19 produced a durable structural shift in U.S. short‑term‑rental behavior. Mean nights‑per‑booking (NPB) stabilized between 4.1 and 4.4—about ten percent higher than the 2019 baseline—while the booking‑weighted median rose permanently from two to three nights. Dispersion increased in parallel: the post‑2021 standard deviation hovers near seven nights, almost double its pre‑pandemic value. Together these facts confirm that the transient spring‑2020 spike gave way to a lasting elongation of typical Airbnb stays.

The distributional diagnostics reinforce this conclusion. A heavy‑tailed log‑normal density dominates both Gamma and Poisson‑lognormal alternatives by wide information‑criterion margins and reproduces the distinctive 14‑, 21‑ and 28‑night shoulders that the Gamma misses. Because the log‑normal tail contains a small but influential share of bookings, revenue‑management systems that assume light‑tailed priors would systematically under‑predict demand for month‑plus residencies and thus mis‑price weekly and monthly rate fences.

The two‑part hurdle analysis clarifies the mechanism behind the longer average. Lock‑downs nearly doubled the probability that a booking crosses the 28‑night threshold, from 1.5 percent pre‑COVID to 2.9 percent at the height of restrictions;that prevalence settled at 2.2 percent post‑vaccine, still 48 percent above the 2019 level. In contrast, the conditional mean length of long stays changed little, hovering around 43–46 nights. Hence the elevated mean NPB is driven by more travelers choosing month‑plus itineraries rather than by ever‑longer residencies among those who were already long‑stay guests.

Seasonality now plays a secondary role. The negative‑binomial regression reveals a shallow U‑shape with April and December about three percent longer than January, and June and September about three percent shorter. Pandemic‑phase effects dwarf these month‑to‑month swings. Time‑series analysis reaches the same verdict: an ARIMA
(0,1,1)(0,1,1)_{12}
augmented with phase dummies outperforms a dummy‑free specification by more than twenty AIC points, while the estimated pre‑COVID coefficient of
-1.17
nights confirms a discrete step in 2020 that has persisted.

These empirical patterns carry clear practical implications. Hosts and revenue managers should migrate from Gamma‑based heuristics to heavy‑tailed priors such as the log‑normal when setting discount ladders, or risk under‑pricing the now larger share of month‑plus stays. Platform operators that report booking counts alone may understate growth in nights sold, because longer stays inflate inventory‑day utilization more than they affect bookings. Property investors, finally, can interpret the shift as evidence that a mixed short‑term/medium‑term strategy is becoming structurally more viable.

The analysis has limits. Collapsing identical bookings into weighted rows sacrifices host identifiers, price dispersion and cancellation patterns; future studies should exploit those micro‑features. Geographic heterogeneity merits dedicated work, as broadband access, state regulation and urban density likely modulate the national averages reported here. Phase dummies capture timing, not causality; natural experiments such as corporate return‑to‑office mandates or visa‑policy changes could pin down mechanisms more cleanly. Monitoring whether the 28‑night tail thickens or thins as remote‑work policies evolve will be essential for accurate forecasting and effective regulation.

In summary, five years of data reveal that the pandemic’s travel shock was not merely a fleeting “slow-travel” episode; the stay-length distribution remains higher and more heavy-tailed than before. Forecasting models and policy frameworks still anchored to 2019 assumptions are therefore prone to systematic bias. Robust revenue optimization and destination management now require heavy-tailed stay-length priors and explicit regime indicators.
