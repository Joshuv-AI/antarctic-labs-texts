Prognostic value of PSA Kinetic Ratio in high-volume mCSPC patients treated with abiraterone or enzalutamide: a single-center retrospective study
Authors: Sila Oksuz, Oguzcan Kınıkoglu, Nisanur Sariyar, Mert Ozdemir, Deniz Isik, Heves Surmeli, Seval Ay, Hatice Odabas, Nedim Turan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe use of androgen receptor pathway inhibitors (ARPIs), such as abiraterone and enzalutamide, in combination with androgen deprivation therapy (ADT), has significantly enhanced outcomes in metastatic castration-sensitive prostate cancer (mCSPC). However, validated early prognostic markers to guide treatment decisions are still limited. The PSA Kinetic Ratio, a simplified kinetic model based on PSA kinetics, may provide a practical method for early risk assessment.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective study of 146 high-volume mCSPC patients treated with abiraterone or enzalutamide plus ADT. The PSA Kinetic Ratio, calculated from three PSA measurements within the first 100 days of therapy (PSA₃/PSA₁), was used to classify patients into favorable (< 1) or unfavorable (≥ 1) PSA kinetics. The primary endpoint was progression-free survival (PFS). Kaplan–Meier and Cox regression analyses were employed to assess the relationships between the PSA Kinetic Ratio, treatment type, and survival outcomes.ResultsPatients with favorable PSA Kinetic Ratio (PSA Kinetic Ratio <1) experienced significantly longer median PFS compared to those with unfavorable PSA kinetics in both treatment groups (log-rank p < 0.001). Multivariate Cox analysis confirmed that unfavorable PSA kinetics (PSA Kinetic Ratio ≥1) were independently associated with shorter PFS (HR = 2.10; 95% CI: 1.45–3.05; p < 0.001). No significant difference in PFS was observed between abiraterone and enzalutamide within each PSA Kinetic Ratio subgroup.ConclusionThe PSA Kinetic Ratio seems to be a promising and practical prognostic biomarker in high-volume mCSPC. Early PSA decline (Kelim < 1) correlates with better outcomes, regardless of ARPI type. Incorporating it into clinical decision-making may allow for timely treatment adjustments. Prospective validation is needed.

Introduction

The addition of androgen receptor pathway inhibitors (ARPIs), including abiraterone acetate and enzalutamide, to androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) has significantly improved survival outcomes in patients with metastatic castration-sensitive prostate cancer (mCSPC), particularly among those with high-volume disease, as shown in pivotal trials such as LATITUDE and ENZAMET [1, 2].

Although both agents target androgen signaling, they act through distinct mechanisms within the androgen axis.

Abiraterone acetate is a selective, irreversible inhibitor of CYP17A1, a key enzyme involved in the biosynthesis of androgens within the tumor, adrenal glands, and testes. By reducing androgen production at multiple points, abiraterone effectively lowers circulating and intratumoral androgen levels to castrate or even subcastrate levels [3, 4].

In contrast, enzalutamide is a powerful, competitive inhibitor of the androgen receptor (AR) that works directly at the receptor level. It prevents androgen from binding to AR and stops AR from moving to the nucleus, binding DNA, and activating transcription, thereby interfering with downstream AR signaling even when residual androgens are present [5, 6]. This mechanistic divergence may result in different biological effects, pharmacodynamic profiles, and resistance patterns. For example, abiraterone’s upstream inhibition of androgen biosynthesis can broadly impact steroidogenic pathways, while enzalutamide’s direct AR antagonism can remain effective in tumors with low ligand levels but ongoing AR overexpression or mutations [7, 8].

Despite these advances, clinically validated prognostic biomarkers that refine risk stratification and guide treatment choices among these agents remain limited. Incorporating dynamic markers, such as prostate-specific antigen (PSA) kinetics, and new indicators, like the PSA kelim score, into clinical decision-making may open new possibilities for personalizing therapy. PSA kinetics are easily accessible and cost-effective biomarkers that indicate tumor burden, biological aggressiveness, and treatment response. In addition to baseline PSA levels, early changes in PSA during the first 3 months of therapy have consistently demonstrated prognostic value in metastatic prostate cancer. Several studies have shown that patients who achieve a substantial PSA decline—typically defined as a ≥ 50% reduction from baseline within 12 weeks—experience significantly longer progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) compared to those who do not achieve an early biochemical response [9–11].

In the context of mCSPC, early PSA suppression appears to indicate effective inhibition of androgen receptor signaling and the tumor’s reliance on androgen-driven pathways. Conversely, a suboptimal PSA decline within the first 3 months may signal intrinsic resistance mechanisms, AR pathway independence, or a more aggressive tumor biology [11].

Furthermore, both time to PSA nadir and the depth of PSA response independently predict long-term outcomes. Achieving a low PSA nadir quickly (e.g., < 0.2 ng/mL) is associated with improved survival, whereas persistently detectable PSA levels after initiation of systemic therapy are associated with worse outcomes [12, 13]. These findings underscore the value of incorporating early PSA dynamics into prognostic assessment.

The PSA Kelim (short for elimination rate constant) Score, derived from sequential PSA measurements, has recently emerged as a simplified mathematical representation of PSA kinetics. This idea is based on previous modeling of CA-125 kinetics in ovarian cancer, which showed that the CA-125 elimination rate constant is a strong early predictor of chemotherapy effectiveness and survival [14, 15]. In that context, a rapid decline in CA-125 levels during initial treatment reflected chemosensitivity, whereas a slower decline indicated drug resistance and poorer prognosis.

In prostate cancer, the PSA Kelim Score measures the rate of PSA decline during early treatment, typically within the first 90 days. By capturing the speed of PSA decrease, this parameter may indicate early tumor response and inherent sensitivity to androgen receptor pathway inhibition. Unlike static PSA thresholds, the Kelim model incorporates temporal changes, providing a continuous, personalized measurement. Preliminary studies suggest that PSA kinetic modeling can stratify patients beyond conventional risk classifications (e.g., high- versus low-volume), serving as a practical and reproducible tool for early therapeutic decision-making in hormone-sensitive prostate cancer [10]. The PSA Kelim Score may thus help clinicians distinguish early from poor responders, potentially guiding real-time treatment intensification or de-escalation strategies [16, 17].

The present study aimed to evaluate the prognostic significance of the PSA Kinetic Ratio among high-volume mCSPC patients treated with abiraterone or enzalutamide plus ADT, with PFS as the primary endpoint.

For consistency, the term “PSA kinetics” is used throughout the manuscript to describe early changes in PSA levels during treatment.

Study design and patient population

High-volume disease was defined as ≥ 4 bone metastases, with or without pelvic lymph node involvement. Patients with visceral metastases at diagnosis were excluded.

PSA Kinetic Ratio calculation

The PSA Kelim Score, short for the prostate-specific antigen elimination rate constant, is a mathematical parameter adapted from the Ca-125 Kelim model used in ovarian cancer. In that setting, Ca-125 kinetic modeling demonstrated that the rate of decline during early chemotherapy exposure was a robust predictor of chemosensitivity and long-term survival outcomes [14].

Analogously, the PSA Kelim Score quantifies the early exponential decline in PSA levels under systemic therapy for prostate cancer, particularly during the first 90–100 days of treatment. The model assumes a log-linear decrease in PSA and aims to reflect reductions in tumor burden and drug sensitivity.

In this study, three consecutive PSA values were recorded during the first 100 days after treatment initiation (Day 0 = baseline, Day ~ 30–40, and Day ~ 80–100). The PSA Kinetic Ratio was calculated using the simplified formula:

PSA Kinetic Ratio = PSA₃ / PSA₁.

Patients were classified based on PSA Kinetic Ratio as follows: a PSA Kinetic Ratio <1 was considered favorable, indicating a decline in PSA levels over time and suggesting an early response to treatment, while a PSA Kinetic Ratio ≥ 1 was considered unfavorable, showing stable or rising PSA levels that suggest treatment resistance. Although the PSA Kinetic Ratio was calculated using a simplified ratio (PSA₃/PSA₁) instead of a full log-linear regression model, this method was chosen for its simplicity and usefulness in routine clinical practice. While it does not fully reflect the exponential kinetics of PSA decline, it offers a practical approximation of early PSA changes.

This stratification approach is supported by recent efforts to validate PSA-based Kelim models in prostate cancer, using a methodology similar to that used in ovarian cancer and metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC) [16]. In this study, this stratification was used to evaluate its association with survival outcomes in mCSPC.

The parameter used in this study is a simplified PSA-based ratio (PSA₃/PSA₁) and does not correspond to the validated model-based KELIM parameter. It was used as a pragmatic surrogate to reflect early PSA kinetics in routine clinical practice.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation or median (range), as appropriate. The Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare continuous variables between groups.

Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Group comparisons were performed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

Survival curves were generated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared with the log-rank test.

In addition to Kaplan–Meier analyses, univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression models were performed to identify independent predictors of PFS. Variables entered into the multivariate model included:

Discussion

This study suggests the PSA Kinetic Ratio could be a prognostic biomarker for high-volume mCSPC patients on ARPI therapy. Patients with a PSA Kinetic Ratio < 1, showing favorable PSA kinetics, had longer progression-free survival regardless of treatment. While overall survival data are still immature, ongoing follow-up should clarify its prognostic value. A score < 1 indicates PSA decline and early treatment sensitivity; a score ≥ 1 suggests resistance. The definition aligns with biological expectations, and the separation of Kaplan–Meier curves supports its role as an early biomarker.

The PSA Kinetic Ratio was estimated using a simplified ratio (PSA₃/PSA₁). A value < 1 indicates declining PSA over time; a value ≥ 1 suggests stability or an increase. While this approach doesn’t replicate the full log-linear regression KELIM model, it offers a practical approximation of early PSA kinetics. It doesn’t fully capture the exponential nature of PSA decline and should be viewed as a surrogate rather than a direct substitute for validated KELIM modeling.

It is important to note that the PSA Kinetic Ratio used in this study differs from the original model-based KELIM parameter described in previous literature. In traditional KELIM modeling, which relies on log-linear regression of serial PSA measurements, higher KELIM values are linked to faster PSA decline and better clinical outcomes. In contrast, the current study used a simplified ratio (PSA₃/PSA₁) as a practical route for PSA kinetics. Within this framework, values < 1 reflect a decline in PSA levels and are therefore considered favorable, whereas values ≥ 1 indicate stable or increasing PSA levels and are considered unfavorable. The simplified ratio is more accessible and reproducible but has limitations, as it ignores intermediate PSA values and the shape of the curve, potentially underestimating the complexity of PSA kinetics. It should be viewed as a surrogate, not a substitute, for validated KELIM modeling. Prospective validation with full models is needed.

No significant difference in PFS was observed between abiraterone and enzalutamide within the same PSA Kinetic Ratio subgroup, suggesting early PSA kinetics may better reflect tumor biology and androgen sensitivity than the specific ARPI used. The overall cohort’s lack of significance may be due to limited power, heterogeneity, and retrospective design, which may have weakened the observed association when treatment groups were combined. Excluding patients with visceral metastases aimed to create a more homogeneous population but limits applicability, especially for aggressive disease. Future studies should assess the prognostic value of the PSA Kinetic Ratio in this subgroup.

Early PSA decline—particularly within the first 12 weeks of systemic therapy—is already recognized as a strong prognostic indicator in mCSPC and mCRPC, with associations with PFS and OS in multiple prior studies [18–20]. However, standard PSA decline metrics (e.g., ≥ 50% decrease) remain categorical and time-point dependent, potentially missing more nuanced kinetic patterns of treatment response.

The PSA Kelim Score, adapted from the well-established CA-125 Kelim model in ovarian cancer, provides a continuous, dynamic measure of tumor marker clearance [14, 15]. Originally validated as a predictor of chemosensitivity and survival in high-grade serous ovarian cancer, the Kelim parameter has more recently gained traction in prostate cancer, particularly in the mCRPC setting, where it has shown prognostic value among patients treated with taxanes or ARPIs [21, 22]. Despite its potential, PSA Kelim’s role in hormone-sensitive prostate cancer (mCSPC) is underexplored, with few studies. Our research indicates the PSA Kinetic Ratio could give early prognostic insights, guiding treatment. It is a cost-effective, reproducible biomarker requiring only serial PSA values in the first 2–3 months, a standard follow-up period, making it practical. Time to PSA nadir and PSA response depth are established biomarkers; integrating them with the PSA Kinetic Ratio may provide a more comprehensive assessment of tumor and response.

Previous studies show that early PSA kinetics, such as a ≥ 50% decline within 12 weeks, predict better survival in mCSPC and mCRPC. Parameters like time to PSA nadir relate to disease progression [23]. The PSA Kelim Score provides a continuous, time-dependent assessment, potentially offering more detailed insights into tumor dynamics than binary measures. Compared with traditional PSA-based metrics such as PSA nadir and time to PSA nadir, the PSA Kelim Score provides real-time, early evaluation of treatment response. While PSA nadir indicates the depth of response, PSA Kelim measures the speed of decline, potentially providing additional prognostic insights.

Building on these findings, the PSA Kelim Score offers a dynamic, continuous, time-dependent model of PSA response that moves beyond static thresholds. Adapted from Ca-125 kinetic modeling in ovarian cancer [14], the Kelim approach has recently been validated in patients with mCRPC treated with taxanes and ARPIs [17].

Although a simplified PSA Kinetic Ratio formula (PSA₃/PSA₁) was used rather than full log-linear regression modeling, previous studies have demonstrated that such simplified surrogates can retain prognostic value. Carrot et al. have validated similar approximations in mCRPC populations, showing consistent association with treatment outcomes [16]. Thus, this pragmatic approach may be particularly useful in routine clinical practice where software-based exponential modeling may not be feasible.

Our multivariate analysis showed that the PSA Kinetic Ratio was independently associated with progression-free survival after adjustment for age, treatment type, baseline PSA, and metastatic burden. These results align with Carrot et al. [16], who found PSA Kelim kinetics predicted early progression in mCRPC and stratified patients independently of disease volume or baseline PSA. Collectively, these findings suggest that PSA kinetics, not just treatment choice, reflect tumor biology, including androgen receptor dependency and growth dynamics.

Recent studies have also emphasized the prognostic value of the PSA Kelim Score in prostate cancer [16, 17]. Consistent with these reports, our findings suggest that early PSA kinetics can serve as a practical prognostic marker in high-volume mCSPC. Stratifying patients based on PSA kinetics in the first 3 months of therapy may enable early risk assessment and more personalized treatment approaches.

Clinically, the PSA Kinetic Ratio aids early treatment decisions. Patients with unfavorable PSA kinetics (Score ≥ 1) might need aggressive treatment or clinical trials. Those with favorable kinetics (Score < 1) could be candidates for conservative strategies, pending validation. The simple kinetic parameter aligns with real-world practice, favoring quick, understandable biomarkers over intricate models. Importantly, although subgroup analyses showed statistically significant differences in progression-free survival, the overall cohort analysis did not reach statistical significance. This discrepancy may be due to limited statistical power and cohort heterogeneity. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted with caution, and the observed trends need confirmation in larger, adequately powered prospective studies.

Although the PSA Kelim Score shows promise for prognosis, it has not yet gained widespread use in clinical practice. This could be because it needs validation in large prospective studies, lacks methodological standardization, and is not well known among clinicians. More research is necessary to clarify its role in routine decision-making and to develop standardized methods for its implementation.

Importantly, our findings should be interpreted in light of several methodological constraints. The PSA Kinetic Ratio used in this study was based on a simplified ratio (PSA₃/PSA₁) rather than the original model-based KELIM parameter derived from log-linear regression. While this approach is practical in routine clinical settings, it may not fully capture the dynamic, exponential nature of PSA decline, potentially limiting its precision as a biomarker. In addition, the multivariable analysis was limited by the incomplete availability of certain clinically relevant variables, which may have introduced residual confounding. Therefore, the observed associations should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive.

Limitations

This study has limitations due to its retrospective, single-center design. Treatment was based on the physician’s choice rather than randomization, which may introduce bias. Although baseline characteristics were mostly balanced, residual confounding remains possible. Important clinical confounders such as Gleason score, ECOG performance status, and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) were not consistently available for all patients and therefore could not be included in the multivariable analysis. The lack of these variables may reduce the robustness of the adjusted model and should be taken into account when interpreting the results. Advanced statistical methods like propensity score matching weren’t used due to sample size and the retrospective nature. As a result, findings should be interpreted cautiously, and causality isn’t confirmed. Also, a sensitivity analysis comparing the simplified PSA Kinetic Ratio with a full PSA KELIM model was not done, which is a study limitation.

Despite these limitations, the consistency of findings across subgroup analyses supports the potential robustness of PSA Kinetic Ratio as a prognostic marker.

Conclusion

The PSA Kinetic Ratio may represent a practical and accessible prognostic biomarker in high-volume mCSPC. However, given the methodological limitations, including the use of a simplified kinetic model and restricted multivariable adjustment, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Prospective studies with standardized modeling approaches and comprehensive clinical data are required before clinical implementation.
