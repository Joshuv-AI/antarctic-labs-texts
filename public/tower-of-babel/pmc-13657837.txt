Extracellular adenosine triphosphate upregulates S100A4 expression and promotes the migration and invasion of breast cancer cells via P2Y2/SOX9 signaling
Authors: Yue Cui, Fengming Ran, Guangxiong Ruan, Sheng Huang, Ying Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe tumor microenvironment (TME), composed of tumor cells, mesenchymal cells, and diverse extracellular molecules, plays a pivotal regulatory role in tumor initiation, malignant progression, and the development of anti-cancer therapeutic resistance. Extracellular adenosine triphosphate (ATP) is a critical bioactive signaling molecule in the TME. Previous studies from our group have confirmed that extracellular ATP is extensively involved in multiple malignant phenotypes of prostate cancer and breast cancer, including tumor cell migration, invasion, chemotherapeutic resistance, and the maintenance of cancer stem cell properties. In our prior work, we demonstrated that extracellular ATP acts on stromal fibroblasts in the breast cancer microenvironment to facilitate the formation of a pro-tumorigenic niche. However, the full molecular mechanism governing this regulatory axis remains incompletely elucidated. Here, we aim to uncover its underlying mechanism, thereby providing new insights for the development of breast cancer therapeutic strategies.MethodsIn this study, primary breast cancer cells isolated from clinical breast cancer tissue specimens were used as the core research model. A series of experimental approaches were applied, including extracellular ATP stimulation assays, P2 receptor intervention, SOX9 gene loss-of-function manipulations, S100A4 expression detection at both transcriptional and translational levels, transwell invasion assays of primary breast cancer cells and fibroblasts, to evaluate the formation efficiency of cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs).ResultsOur experimental results revealed that extracellular ATP can significantly upregulate the expression of S100A4 in primary breast cancer cells. In terms of mechanism, this regulatory effect is mediated by the P2 receptor-SOX9 signaling axis. Specifically, the binding of extracellular ATP to its membrane P2 receptors activates the downstream transcription factor SOX9, which further transcriptionally promotes the expression of target gene S100A4. Functional validation experiments confirmed that this signaling cascade subsequently enhances the migration and invasion capacities of breast cancer cells.ConclusionsThis study identified a novel regulatory pathway mediated by extracellular ATP in the breast cancer microenvironment; that is, the P2 receptor-SOX9-S100A4 signaling axis, which promotes the malignant progression of breast cancer and the remodeling of the tumor stroma. These findings not only expand the mechanistic understanding of extracellular ATP in the tumor-promoting microenvironment but also provide a potential new molecular for the targeted intervention of breast cancer metastatic progression and stromal therapy resistance.

Key findings

• Our study identified the P2 receptor-SOX9-S100A4 signaling axis as novel regulatory pathway mediated by extracellular adenosine triphosphate (ATP) in the breast cancer microenvironment. This pathway promotes the malignant progression of breast cancer and the remodeling of the tumor stroma. These findings provide insights into the mechanism governing extracellular ATP’s influence in the tumor microenvironment (TME) and indicate a novel molecular target for intervention into the metastatic progression of breast cancer and resistance to stromal therapy.

Introduction

Breast cancer is a common female tumor with a high incidence and fatality rate, and few treatments are available for those patients who experience metastasis and drug resistance. The purpose of traditional anticancer therapies is primarily to kill cancer cells, yet their curative effect is weakened once tumor metastasis occurs.

Tumor metastases are multistep, complex biological processes. Several key mechanisms are involved in the metastasis of cancer cells, such as epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), unregulated DNA repair mechanism, and cancer cell stemness. Numerous studies have revealed that drug resistance in tumor cells is often accompanied by the upregulated expression of EMT-associated proteins, which can lead to significantly enhanced migration and metastasis capabilities (1-3). With the development of tumor biology and immunology research, the tumor microenvironment (TME) has come to the fore as a key factor in tumor metastasis. The TME, which consists of tumor cells, fibroblasts, immune cells, and extracellular molecules, facilitates tumor initiation and progression. Furthermore, it promotes tumor metastasis by supporting angiogenesis, cell survival, cell motility, and invasion (4).

In response to different stimuli such as mechanical stress, hypoxia, and inflammation, adenosine triphosphate (ATP) is released into the extracellular space by various types of cells, including tumor cells, inflammatory cells and mesenchymal cells. Once in the extracellular space, ATP can act as a signaling molecule by binding to cell surface purinergic receptors known as P2 receptors. P2 receptors can be divided into two main subtypes: P2X receptors, which are ligand-gated ion channels, and P2Y receptors, which are G protein-coupled receptors. These receptors are expressed in various cell types, including tumor cells, and their activation by extracellular ATP can modulate an array of cellular processes involved in tumor progression (5).

Fang et al. were the first to discover that extracellular ATP inhibits the proliferation of prostate cancer cells (6) and promotes migration and invasion in vitro (7). In terms of the related mechanism, it was subsequently found that extracellular ATP promotes the migration and invasion of prostate and breast cancer cells by activating the ERK1/2, p38, and PI3K/AKT pathways via P2X7 and/or P2Y2 receptor. Additionally, ATP activates Rac1 and CDC42 to induce the formation of filopodia and lamellipodia in prostate cancer cells, thus promoting their ability to migrate. ATP can also upregulate the expression of matrix metalloproteinases and facilitate cancer cell invasion. Moreover, P2Y2 receptor is known to exert a pivotal function in ATP-promoted invasion (7-10). Informed by these findings, Fang et al. proposed that extracellular ATP is a pro-invasive factor in the TME, providing a novel scientific basis for understanding the regulatory mechanisms governing tumor metastasis (6,7). Further studies revealed that ATP promotes the invasion of breast cancer cell through activation of Wnt-β/catenin signaling (11) and HIF-2α pathways (12). In addition, the ATP/IL-6/SOX9 signaling was found to play an important part in breast cancer’s resistance to chemotherapeutic drugs (10). Moreover, in a murine study, it was demonstrated that the extracellular ATP-P2Y2 receptor axis also facilitates the formation of hepatocellular carcinoma by exacerbating DNA damage (13). Other research indicates that extracellular ATP promotes angiogenesis and the adhesion of triple-negative breast cancer cells to endothelial cells via the upregulation of CTGF (14). Besides, extracellular ATP promotes tumor progression in radiotherapy-resistant breast cancer cells and breast cancer cells by modulating invasion and associated molecules through the P2Y2R-inflammasome activation pathway (15).

SOX9 (sex-determining region Y-box 9) is a transcription factor that plays a critical role in embryonic development and tissue differentiation. SOX9 is a transcription factor that controls cell fate in a wide range of tissues (16) and is involved in the development of various organs. It is widely expressed in a variety of cancers, including breast cancer (17). Studies have shown that SOX9 is overexpressed in basal-like breast cancer, and high SOX9 expression has been associated with poor prognosis and increased tumor aggressiveness. SOX9 has also been found to promote tumor growth, invasion, and metastasis in breast cancer cells, and it has been implicated in the regulation of cancer stem cells, which are considered to be related to tumor initiation, progression, and drug resistance (10). SOX9 expression has further been shown to enhance the self-renewal capacity and tumorigenic potential of breast cancer stem cells (18). Given these discoveries, targeting SOX9 in has emerged as a potential therapeutic strategy for breast cancer. In preclinical studies, inhibition of SOX9 suppressed tumor growth and sensitized breast cancer cells to chemotherapy and targeted therapies (19,20). However, further research is needed to fully clarify the mechanisms underlying SOX9’s effect in breast cancer and to develop effective therapeutic strategies targeting this transcription factor.

In our previous work, we discovered that extracellular ATP upregulates S100A4 expression in breast cancer cells to promote cell migration and can also upregulate S100A4 in fibroblasts, facilitating their transition to cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs). Extracellular ATP in TME can increase the expression of S100A4 in both breast cancer cells and fibroblasts to promote cancer cell migration and invasion, thus facilitating breast cancer metastasis and progression (21).

S100A4, also known as FSP1 (fibroblast-specific protein 1) or metastatin 1, is one of 21 members of the S100 calcium-binding protein family (22). As S100 proteins exert no enzymatic activities, they function by regulating the activity of other proteins (23,24). S100 proteins have been reported to be abnormally expressed in a number of human pathological conditions, including cancer, inflammation, cardiomyopathy and neurodegenerative disorders (25). Recently, S100A4 has garnered heightened interest due to its metastasis-promoting properties and is known to be a marker of activated CAFs. S100A4 is produced in the cytoplasm and may be located in the nucleus and cytoplasm or be secreted to extracellular spaces via autocrine and/or paracrine signaling in response to stress. Extracellular S100A4 regulates the expression of target genes and participates in intercellular communication by acting on specific receptors on the cell surface (26), such as receptor for advanced glycation end products (RAGE), annexin II, and heparan sulfate proteoglycans (27-29). Generally, the biological activity of extracellular S100A4 is associated with multimeric forms of protein (28,30,31). Neutralizing the antibody of S100A4 inhibits breast cancer metastasis, which suggests that extracellular S100A4 exerts a metastasis-promoting effect (21). Furthermore, studies have found that extracellular S100A4 stimulates tumor angiogenesis (32) and acts on tumor cells and immunocytes, thus forming the foundation of the tumor-promoting microenvironment (33,34). A recent study has revealed a potential mechanism by which lactylation affects breast cancer progression through lactic acid-mediated regulation of the immune microenvironment, and also highlights the potential involvement of S100A4 in this process, providing novel insights for the diagnosis and treatment of breast cancer (35). Pan-cancer single-cell atlases have consistently identified S100A4 as a conserved mediator that drives PD-L1 upregulation in tumor cells, ultimately leading to pervasive immunosuppression and innate resistance to next-generation immune checkpoint therapy (36).

In summary, extracellular ATP in the breast cancer microenvironment drives malignant phenotypes, while P2Y2, SOX9 and S100A4 are individually linked to tumor progression respectively. However, their unreported cascade relationship and synergistic metastasis-driving mechanism remain unclarified, hindering targeted translational advances. This study systematically explores the previously uncharacterized regulatory axis by which extracellular ATP drives metastasis via coordinated regulation of fibroblast-tumor cell crosstalk, filling a critical gap in pathway understanding and identifying potential novel intervention targets for breast cancer metastasis. Methods

Chemicals and antibodies

ATP was purchased from Xi’an Tianbao Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Xi’an, China), anti-S100A4 antibody was obtained from Cell Signal Technology (cat. no. 13018; Danvers, MA, USA), anti-SOX9 (cat. no. AF6330) and anti-P2Y2 (cat. no. DF10259) antibody were purchased from Affinity Biosciences (Cincinnati, OH, USA). Anti-β-actin (cat. no. 66009-1-Ig) and anti-CA15-3 antibody (cat. no. 23614-1) were obtained from Proteintech (Rosemont, IL, USA), and horseradish peroxidase-conjugated goat anti-mouse (cat. no. GB23301) and goat anti-rabbit IgG (cat. no. GB23303) were purchased from Servicebio (Wuhan, China).

Primary culture of breast cancer cells and CAFs

All tumor samples in this study were obtained via simple random sampling from untreated female patients (aged 40–60 years) with non-special invasive ductal breast carcinoma at ‌Yunnan Cancer Hospital‌. Patients who had received any form of treatment were excluded. The tumor tissue was sliced into tiny pieces about 1 mm3 in size, digested by collagenase IV and dispase at 37 ℃, and then filtered through a 200-mesh cell strainer to dislodge any remaining tissue. The filtered cells were collected via centrifugation at 800 rpm/min for 10 min and resuspended in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) for subsequent culture. After 30 min, a majority of the fibroblasts had already adhered to the culture dish, but tumor cells remained in suspension. Following this, fibroblasts were cultured for later experiments, and the suspension was transferred to a new culture dish to culture primary cancer cells. At least three stable cell lines were established from primary cultures, and stably passaged cells were used for subsequent experiments. Meanwhile, immunocytochemical staining for CA153 (MUC-1) was performed to confirm the epithelial tumor origin of the primary cells isolated from fresh human breast carcinoma specimens.

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Yunnan Cancer Hospital with the review number KYCS2021153 and informed consent was taken from all the patients.

Immunofluorescence

Cells were grown on glass coverslips in 12-well culture plates for 24 h and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 ℃ for overnight. The cells were then treated with 0.1% Triton X-100 in phosphate-buffered saline for 10 min. Primary cultured cells were cocultured with fibroblast activation protein (Affinity Biosciences, Cincinnati, OH, USA) and fibronectin (Proteintech) antibodies, and then fibroblasts were identified.

Immunohistochemistry and H-score

Immunohistochemistry was performed on FFPE tissue sections. Following deparaffinization and antigen retrieval, endogenous peroxidase was blocked. Primary antibodies were incubated overnight at 4 ℃. Staining was developed with DAB, and nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin.

The H‑score was used for semi‑quantitative assessment. It was calculated as: H‑score = (1× percentage of weak‑staining cells) + (2× percentage of moderate‑staining cells) + (3× percentage of strong‑staining cells), ranging from 0 to 300. Two independent observers blinded to clinical groups evaluated the staining, and any disagreement was resolved by joint review. For each slide, 3–5 non‑overlapping high‑power fields were randomly selected for scoring and image capture.

Transwell invasion assay

Transwell invasion assays were performed with a 24-well Transwell chamber (Costar, San Diego, CA, USA). Briefly, cells were harvested and resuspended in high glucose DMEM with 0.5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) at a density of 5×105 cells/mL. Cancer cells were placed in the upper chamber coated with Matrigel and maintained in culture media containing 0.5% FBS, culture media containing 30% FBS were filled in the lower chamber as a chemo-attractant, and cells were allowed to invade for 24 h at 37 ℃ with or without ATP treatment. Subsequently, the invaded cells were fixed with 4% formaldehyde and stained with crystal violet. The number of invaded cells was then counted, and the mean for each chamber was determined.

Wound-healing assay

A wound-healing assay was performed as previously described (37).

siRNA and transfection

P2Y2 and SOX9 siRNAs were purchased from Genepharma (Shanghai, China), and their sequences were listed in Table S1. To knock down P2Y2 and SOX9 expression, 100 nM siRNA against P2Y2 and SOX9 were transfected to breast cancer cells using SwsScript All-in-One First-strand-cDNA-synthesis SuperMIx for qpcr (One step gDNA Remover) (Guangzhou, China) as suggested by the manufacturer.

Reverse transcription and quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Reverse transcription and qRT-PCR were conducted as outlined in our previous work (21). Forward and reverse primers were obtained from GenePharma (Suzhou, China). The 2–ΔΔCt method was employed for relative quantification as described previously (38).

Western blotting

Protein extraction and Western blotting were performed as described by Li et al. (8). Equal amounts of total protein were separated via sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and subsequently transferred onto a polyvinylidene difluoride membrane (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). The membrane was incubated at 4 ℃ overnight with primary antibodies against S100A4 (1:2,000), SOX9 (1:2,000), P2Y2 (1:2,000), and β-actin (1:25,000) and then with secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 hour. The immunoreactive bands on the membrane were visualized and quantified.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

The secreted S100A4 protein levels in the media cultured with cells and mouse serum were measured with a human S100A4 ELISA kit (Jianglaibio, Shanghai, China). The total protein concentration was examined, and then the concentrations of secreted protein of different samples were normalized to the corresponding total protein.

Statistical analysis

All the experiments were performed in triplicate for both technical and biological replicates.

The unpaired t-test was carried out via GraphPad Prism 10 (Dotmatics, Boston, MA, USA). P values <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Differences in cell migration, invasion, gray scale of western blot and H‑score among different groups were evaluated using the Kruskal‑Wallis test, followed by Dunn’s multiple comparisons test for pairwise comparisons between all groups.

Results

ATP treatment promoted the migration and invasion of primary breast cancer cells

Our previous studies demonstrated that extracellular ATP can promote the migration and invasion breast cancer cells. In this work, a primary culture of cells from human breast cancer was carried out (Figure 1). In primary cancer cell, the positive rate of CA153 staining exceeded 90%, and the isolated primary fibroblasts also exhibited FAP and fibronectin staining positive rates of over 90%, which demonstrated the origin and properties of the cells. To further confirm that extracellular ATP is a driver of breast cancer invasion, we treated human primary breast cancer cells with 100 μM of ATP. Subsequently, wound-healing and Transwell invasion assays showed that the migration and invasion ability of cancer cells after ATP treatment were enhanced (Figure 2).

P2Y2 receptor was highly expressed in primary breast cancer cells and breast cancer tissues and was associated with the migration, invasion, and tumor metastasis of primary breast cancer cells

ATP mediates various physiological and pathological processes by binding to purinergic receptors, mainly P2 receptors. Thus, we examined P2 receptors subtypes in primary breast cancer cells via qRT-PCR, and we found P2Y2 was highly expressed in cancer cells (Figure 3). To further clarify the function of P2Y2 receptor in breast cancer, we knocked down P2Y2 receptor with small interfering RNA (siRNA) in primary breast cancer cells (the validation results of P2Y2 knockdown efficiency are shown in Figure S1). Subsequently, ATP-enhanced migration and invasion were observed to be significantly suppressed, indicating that P2Y2 plays a key role in the above-mentioned pathological process (Figure 4).

We further implemented immunohistochemical staining to detect P2Y2 expression in human breast cancer tissues. We found that P2Y2 was highly expressed at the invasive tumor front in breast cancer. Moreover, in sites of metastasis, the expression of P2Y2 was higher than that of primary tumor and normal tissue (Figure 5).

Extracellular ATP upregulated SOX9 and promoted the invasion of primary breast cancer cells via P2Y2 receptor

To identify the potential downstream genes of ATP treatment, a complement DNA microarray was carried out in MCF-7 breast cancer cells. SOX9 was subsequently found to be one of the upregulated genes after ATP treatment. We further validated its upregulation in primary breast cancer cells. In our previous study, we discovered that SOX9 signaling plays a key role in ATP-driven tumor progression and therapy resistance (10), and P2Y2 is critical to ATP-driven tumor progression (8,11). Therefore, in this study, sought to clarify whether P2Y2 receptor mediates SOX9 upregulation via extracellular ATP. We thus knocked down P2Y2 in primary breast cancer cells and found that ATP-promoted SOX9 upregulation was substantially inhibited, indicating that ATP regulates SOX9 via P2Y2 receptor in primary breast cancer cells (Figure 6).

ATP upregulated SOX9 through P2Y2 in breast cancer cells. (A) The level of SOX9 mRNA was upregulated in primary breast cancer cells after treatment with 100 μM of ATP. (B,C) ATP-enhanced S100A4 and SOX9 protein expression was suppressed after knock down of P2Y2. **, P<0.01; ***, P<0.001. ATP, adenosine triphosphate.

S100A4 was regulated by SOX9 and mediated the extracellular ATP-driven invasion and migration of primary breast cancer cells

In our previous study, we demonstrated that treatment with 100 μmol/L of ATP could increase S100A4 expression in breast cancer cells. In this study, we treated primary cultured breast cancer cells and fibroblasts with 100 and 50 μmol/L of ATP respectively. Similarly, we found that the S100A4 expression and secretion were upregulated (Figure 7). In our preliminary work, we knocked down P2Y2 receptor in MCF-7 breast cancer cells and found out that P2Y2 receptor was critical to ATP’s upregulation of S100A4 expression. We additionally demonstrated that P2Y2 receptor mediated SOX9 and S100A4 upregulation via extracellular ATP (Figure 6). Thus, we sought to determine if SOX9 is involved in the ATP-driven upregulation of S100A4. We used siRNAs to target SOX9 transcription in primary breast cancer cells (the validation results of SOX9 knockdown efficiency are shown in Figure S1), and found that the upregulation of S100A4 after ATP treatment was markedly diminished. This suggests that SOX9 exerts a critical function in ATP’s regulation of S100A4 (Figure 8).

The ATP→P2Y2 receptor→SOX9→S100A4 pathway participated in primary breast cancer cell migration and invasion and the formation of CAFs

Our previous studies revealed that ATP-S100A4 signaling is involved in breast cancer metastasis (21). To further clarify the mechanism underlying this relationship, we used siRNA to knockdown P2Y2 receptor in primary breast cancer cells. We found that the ATP-driven upregulation of S100A4 and enhancement of the migration and invasion of cancer cells were significantly attenuated. Therefore, we concluded that P2Y2 receptor mediates ATP-promoted S100A4 expression and cancer cell migration and invasion in primary breast cancer cells. Moreover, after SOX9 expression was knocked down, we observed that the ATP-promoted migration and invasion of primary breast cancer cells were inhibited (Figure 8). These results indicate that P2Y2 and SOX9 mediate the ATP-driven migration and invasion of primary breast cancer cells.

Discussion

Therapeutic resistance is central obstacle to achieving optimal treatment outcomes among patients with tumor therapy. The eradication of cancer cells has been the traditional mainstay of tumor treatment; however, it provides limited efficacy and may produce significant side-effects. Developments in medical science research have revealed that the TME plays a vital role in tumor progression.

The role of the TME in antitumor therapy is a complex and key topic in cancer research. The TME includes the surrounding cellular and non-cellular components that coexist with tumor cells within the tumor mass. These components include immune cells, fibroblasts, blood vessels, extracellular matrix (ECM), and various signaling molecules, among others. Understanding the TME is crucial for developing effective antitumor therapies (39). Research in this area has focused on immune cells, angiogenesis, ECM, stromal cells, and metabolic reprogramming. The immune system plays an important role in recognizing and eliminating tumor cells (40). However, tumors can create an immunosuppressive microenvironment that hinders immune cell function. This can be achieved through various mechanisms, such as the recruitment of regulatory T cells or the secretion of immunosuppressive cytokines (41). Additionally, tumors require a blood supply to grow and metastasize. The TME promotes the formation of new blood vessels (angiogenesis) through the secretion of proangiogenic factors (42). Targeting these factors or inhibiting angiogenesis can disrupt the tumor’s blood supply, leading to tumor regression. The ECM provides structural support to the tumor and influences its behavior. Tumor cells can modify the composition of the ECM, making it more conducive to tumor growth and invasion. Targeting the ECM or its remodeling enzymes can hinder tumor progression and enhance the efficacy of antitumor therapies. Fibroblasts and other stromal cells within the TME contribute to tumor growth, invasion, and therapy resistance. These cells can secrete growth factors and cytokines that promote tumor cell survival and proliferation (43,44). Modulating the interactions between tumor cells and stromal cells can improve therapeutic outcomes (45). Tumor cells undergo metabolic reprogramming to support their rapid growth and survival. The TME can influence this metabolic reprogramming through nutrient availability and signaling molecules. Targeting specific metabolic pathways in tumor cells or altering the TME’s metabolic conditions can sensitize tumors to therapy (46).

Within this microenvironment, both ATP and S100A4 are key signaling molecules and are generated by tumor cells and/or mesenchymal cells to interact with one another and participate in the emergence and progression of tumors. In this study, we probed into the mechanism of ATP-driven migration and invasion in primary breast cancer cells. ATP-P2Y2 signaling activates fibroblasts and upregulates S100A4 through the downstream SOX9 pathway, thus enhances cancer cell migration and invasion capacity. Nonetheless, we have not yet experimentally clarified how extracellular ATP, through P2Y2-mediated signaling, modulates the activation, phenotypic transition and functional maturation of resident mammary fibroblasts, and how this CAF remodeling process further feeds back to promote the migration and invasion of neighboring breast cancer cells. As a result, the direct causal relationship between ATP-driven TME remodeling and the aggressive malignant phenotype of breast cancer cells still requires more rigorous co-culture and in vivo orthotopic model validation, and correlation analysis of clinical tissue samples are of critical value for improving the conclusion system of this study. Accordingly, systematic in vivo animal assays and a retrospective cohort study with expanded clinical sample sizes should be implemented in subsequent independent investigations.

With respect to the cell line authentication concern, we recognize that CA153 alone cannot identify primary breast cancer cells. CA153 is a well-validated biomarker in serum monitoring settings (47), but its immunohistochemical signal might also be detected in non-tumor epithelial cells, stromal cells, and inflammatory cells may also show weak CA153 background staining, making it difficult to completely exclude non-malignant epithelial contamination. Combined with the original tumor tissue morphology, clinical pathological subtype information, and consistent morphology compared with the corresponding primary tumor, further validation with epithelial and breast cancer markers will be carried out in future work. CAFs were identified by FAP and fibronectin, which are widely accepted markers for activated fibroblasts (48). Another limitation of our work is the lack of quantitative measurement of contaminating endothelial cells and leukocytes in primary cell cultures. Cell purity was controlled by pathological trimming, differential adhesion purification and morphological observation only, without flow cytometry quantification of CD31+ endothelial cells and CD45+ lymphocytes, which should be complemented in future study. In clarifying the relevance of the TME in antitumor therapy, it is necessary to consider its complexity and heterogeneity. Additionally, examining therapeutic strategies that may target specific components of the TME can provide valuable insights into improving antitumor therapies. In this study, we investigated the function and underlying mechanism of extracellular ATP and S100A4 in the progression of breast cancer, providing novel insights into breast cancer treatment, which elaborates on the potential value of the P2Y2/SOX9/S100A4 signaling axis as an early warning biomarker for peripheral blood metastasis of breast cancer, as well as the clinical application prospect of targeted blockade of this pathway to reverse tumor stromal fibrosis and improve the delivery efficiency of chemotherapeutic drugs.

Conclusions

This study systematically identifies a previously uncharacterized regulatory pathway mediated by extracellular ATP within the breast cancer microenvironment, specifically the P2 receptor-SOX9-S100A4 signaling axis, which drives the malignant progression of breast cancer and the pathological remodeling of the tumor stromal compartment. Mechanistically, extracellular ATP accumulates in the hypoxic, nutrient-deprived TME, where it engages with cell-surface P2 purinergic receptors on both tumor cells and stromal fibroblasts to trigger downstream transcriptional activation of the core stemness regulator SOX9. Elevated SOX9 expression in turn transcriptionally upregulates the downstream effector S100A4, a well-documented mediator of cytoskeletal rearrangement, myeloid cell recruitment, and metastatic niche formation. This sequential signaling cascade ultimately enhances breast cancer cell invasion, distant metastatic colonization, and the acquisition of stroma-associated therapeutic resistance. These findings not only substantially expand our mechanistic understanding of the multifaceted tumor-promoting roles of extracellular ATP in the dysregulated breast cancer microenvironment, but also nominate a promising new molecular target for the development of precision therapeutic strategies aimed at blocking metastatic progression and overcoming stromal-mediated treatment resistance in clinical breast cancer management.
