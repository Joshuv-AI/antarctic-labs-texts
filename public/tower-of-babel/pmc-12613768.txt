Attitudes and influencing factors of nursing interns towards hospice care in Wuhu, China: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Liu Yang, Quan Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNursing interns are future frontline providers of hospice care, and their attitudes toward end-of-life care directly affect the quality of hospice services. However, current nursing education often lacks targeted hospice care training, leading to gaps in nursing interns’ preparedness to meet the needs of terminally ill patients. Nursing education plays a crucial role in equipping nursing interns with the necessary skills and knowledge to provide compassionate care for dying patients. Nursing programs need to prioritize end-of-life care training, as it prepares these future nurses to address the unique physical, emotional, and spiritual needs of individuals nearing the end of their lives.MethodsThe sample for this study consisted of 374 nursing college interns who were assessed using the general data questionnaire and the Chinese version of the FATCOD-B-C (Frommelt Attitude Toward Care of the Dying Scale–Form B, Chinese Version). The measurement data were characterized using the mean and standard deviation, while the counting data were described by the number of individuals.ResultsThe mean score of the FATCOD-B-C items was 114.79 (SD = 10.68). The regression equation ultimately included a total of seven variables (p < 0.05), including attitudes towards death, gender, internship duration (mth), encouraging your loved ones to consider hospice care, the experience of witnessing the death of any relative, hoping to offer specialized hospice courses and provide knowledge about hospice care to others. Regarding the hospice care content included in the geriatric nursing course (a core course in the nursing program), 72.5% of participants were satisfied with its delivery. According to the survey, 56.7% of participants expressed their willingness to contribute towards hospice care development.ConclusionThe level of enthusiasm towards hospice care among nursing interns in China requires enhancement. It is suggested to establish the correct view of death for nursing interns, constantly improve the nursing curriculum system, and increase the study of hospice care courses to improve the enthusiasm of these nursing interns for hospice care.Keywords: Nursing interns, FATCOD-B-C, Hospice care, End-of-life care education, China

Introduction

The advancement of medical technology and the enhancement of residents’ living standards have driven significant changes in the spectrum of human diseases and deaths globally [1]. Chronic diseases (e.g., heart failure, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease) and cancer—conditions that often progress to long or short terminal stages—are increasingly prevalent, creating a growing demand for high-quality end-of-life care [2]. This demand is not limited to specific age groups; instead, it encompasses all individuals facing terminal illnesses, as each requires compassionate support to address their unique physical, emotional, and spiritual needs during the final stages of life.

Against this backdrop, hospice care has emerged as a critical approach to end-of-life care, yet its development faces substantial gaps in many regions—including China [3]. Research indicates that approximately 7.5 million dying individuals in China require professional hospice care services annually, but the coverage rate for such services is only 4.7%, failing to meet the broader needs of terminally ill populations [4]. This gap underscores the urgency of improving hospice care provision, which hinges largely on the knowledge and attitudes of future healthcare providers—particularly nursing interns, who will be frontline caregivers for terminally ill patients.

This study aims to assess nursing interns’ attitudes toward hospice care for all terminally ill patients, without focusing on a specific age group. In the context of Chinese nursing education, “nursing interns” in this study refer to those in the final phase of their program. They have completed all academic coursework, including theoretical and laboratory training, and must complete a mandatory 6–12 month clinical internship to graduate. Today’s students are tomorrow’s nurses, and their understanding of and perspective on hospice care will have a direct impact on the quality of care provided to dying patients in the future. This inclusive approach aligns with the core mission of hospice care (to serve all individuals with terminal illnesses) and ensures the findings can inform training for caring for diverse end-of-life populations. The National Health Commission of China anticipates that the demographic group aged 60 and above will increase from 280 million to over 400 million by 2035, which is equivalent to the combined current populations of both the United Kingdom and the United States [5]. This aging trend and its specific end-of-life care needs will be a crucial factor in nursing education, though it will not limit the study’s broader scope.

As medical technology advances, nurses face challenges in providing compassionate end - of - life care while prolonging life. In China, hospice care has been stigmatized by cultural taboos around death, influenced by Confucian values on avoiding mortality discussions for ‘harmony’ and superstitious beliefs of death being ‘inauspicious’ [6]. This has led to passive or negative attitudes toward hospice care among the public and healthcare providers, including nurses. Prior studies show many nurses avoid end - of - life care due to fear of death or lack of confidence in meeting patients’ spiritual/emotional needs [7].

Despite challenges, nurses can drive progress in hospice care through direct care and advocating for reform. Their daily patient - side presence allows them to see how inadequate hospice policies (e.g., limited insurance, lack of training) worsen care gaps [8]. Their attitudes towards hospice influence their advocacy: those with positive attitudes are more likely to engage with policymakers, share patient - centered insights, and push for improvements. For instance, nurses who view hospice as crucial for patient dignity are more proactive in advocating for laws to expand access for elderly or terminally ill patients [9].

Therefore, improving students’ knowledge about hospice care and changing attitudes through hospice care education is important [10]. In conclusion, enhancing students’ knowledge about palliative care through comprehensive educational programs has far-reaching implications for the quality of end-of-life experiences provided by future nurses [11]. Nursing care education expands and advances nursing education, creating a new realm for nursing practice, and they complement each other. The formation of positive life - death attitudes and acquisition of professional hospice - care skills among nursing interns impact hospice service quality and the discipline’s future development. This study examines influencing factors of attitudes towards hospice care in the current context, providing a basis for nursing education researchers to design comprehensive hospice - care training programs.

Materials and methods

Participants

Convenience sampling was used to recruit 374 nursing interns undergoing clinical training at Yijishan Hospital, a tertiary general hospital in Anhui, China. These interns had completed all theoretical and laboratory training and were in the mandatory 6–12 - month clinical internship. The hospital has a geriatric ward and a hospice care unit, and about 65% of its inpatients are older adults, with the proportion reaching 82% in these two units. The hospice care unit has 15 beds and serves over 200 terminally ill patients annually, 90% of whom are older adults with chronic diseases or advanced cancer. As a teaching hospital, it offers a 4 - week preceptorship program in hospice care. The 374 participants had completed all nursing coursework and entered clinical practice, focusing on the hospice care unit. They all had at least six months of hospice care experience, volunteered for the study, and met the inclusion criteria. Importantly, researchers in this study had no teaching, supervision, or assessment roles for the interns. This was clearly stated during the informed consent process to reduce potential response bias.

Demographic information questionnaire

The demographic data encompass variables such as gender, age, birth order status, duration of internship, religious affiliation, bereavement experiences within the family, familiarity with the specific implementation steps of hospice care, and preferred mode of acquiring knowledge on hospice care.

Frommelt attitude toward care of the dying Scale–Form B, Chinese version (FATCOD-B-C)

To measure nursing interns’ attitudes toward hospice care, we used the Frommelt Attitude Toward Care of the Dying Scale–Form B, Chinese Version (FATCOD-B-C)—a revised instrument developed by Wang Liping and widely validated for use in Chinese nursing populations [12]. The original FATCOD-B (English version) is a 30-item tool, but during the development of the Chinese version (FATCOD-B-C), Factor 7 was removed by Wang Liping based on the principle of methodological reasonableness, as it contained only one item; this revision resulted in a 29-item scale.

The FATCOD-B-C uses a 5-point Likert scale (1 = “Strongly Disagree” to 5 = “Strongly Agree”) to assess respondents’ attitudes toward caring for dying patients. The total score ranges from 29 to 145 (corrected from the original text’s “30 to 145,” to align with the 29-item structure), with higher scores indicating more positive attitudes toward hospice care.

For psychometric properties, we relied on prior validation results of the FATCOD-B-C: The scale demonstrates good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.790 for the total scale; Cronbach’s α for subscales ranges from 0.610 to 0.863), satisfactory test-retest reliability (r = 0.959, p < 0.001), and high content validity (content validity index = 0.92). No additional factor analysis or psychometric testing was conducted in the current study, as the instrument’s validity and reliability have already been established for Chinese nursing samples [13].

Scoring for the FATCOD-B-C follows standard procedures: Positive items (1、2、4、16、18、20、21、22、23、24、25、27 和 30) are scored directly from 1 to 5 (Strongly Disagree to Strongly Agree). Conversely, negative items (all items not listed above) are reverse-coded—i.e., a response of “Strongly Disagree” (1) is recoded to 5, and “Strongly Agree” (5) is recoded to 1—before calculating the total score.

Questionnaire regarding hospice care education

The questionnaire regarding hospice care education encompasses students’ satisfaction with the course content, their preferences for teaching methods, suggestions for enhancing the course’s effectiveness, the impact of hospice care on fostering students’ moral values, and their interest in participating in volunteer services related to hospice care.

Data analysis

The data were entered using Epidata version 3.1, which includes the capability to detect and exclude input outliers. Two independent researchers were involved in the data entry and verification processes. Data analysis was performed using SPSS version 18.0. A two-tailed probability level of p<0.05 was selected as the threshold for statistical significance. Descriptive statistics (mean ± standard deviation for continuous variables and frequency and percentage for categorical variables) were used to summarize participants’sociodemographic characteristics and attitude scores according to cross - sectional study guidelines.One - way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed to compare the effects of participants’ characteristics on their attitudes toward caring for dying patients, suitable for comparing means across three or more independent groups. Stepwise multiple linear regression was used to identify factors influencing the FATCOD - B - C total score. It’s appropriate for selecting predictor variables explaining variance in a continuous outcome, with entry and removal criteria of p < 0.05 and p > 0.10 respectively, as recommended in nursing research.

Ethics approval

In addition to obtaining written informed consent from all participants, the researchers also ensured that each participant fully understood the study’s core purpose (assessing nursing interns’ attitudes toward hospice care and identifying the factors influencing these attitudes) and specific procedures. The confidentiality of participants’ personal information was strictly maintained throughout the data collection process: all questionnaire data were de-identified (with no names, student IDs, or other identifying information) and stored in a password-protected electronic database accessible only to the research team. This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Wannan Medical College (approval number: 2021–3), and all methods were performed in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant national research regulations in China. By adhering to these rigorous ethical protocols, we ensured participant safety and well-being while conducting responsible scientific inquiry to address the study’s stated objectives—consistent with the aims detailed in the Introduction and Abstract.”

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics

A total of 374 nursing interns participated in the study. There was a clear gender distribution: 275 (73.5%) were female and 99 (26.5%) were male, consistent with China’s nursing profession’s gender composition. The participants aged from 17 to 23 years, with an average of 20.72 ± 1.64 years, typical of post - secondary nursing interns. In terms of educational background, 251 (67.1%) had a college degree and 123 (32.9%) were undergraduates, covering both junior college and undergraduate nursing education levels. Regarding internship duration, 259 (69.3%) had completed 7 - month or longer internships and 115 (30.7%) had 1–6 - month internships, indicating most had some clinical experience. Regarding end - of - life care experiences and attitudes, 273 (73.0%) had witnessed a relative’s death. A large majority (351, 93.9%) were willing to encourage loved ones to consider hospice care in terminal illness, and 329 (88.0%) hoped for specialized hospice courses, showing a strong demand for hospice care education.

Regarding attitudes toward death, 178 (47.6%) chose to “face death calmly”, 158 (42.2%) were “neutral”, and 38 (10.2%) tended to “avoid mentioning death”, meaning nearly half had a positive attitude towards accepting death while a small proportion showed avoidance. In terms of willingness to popularize hospice care knowledge, 49 (13.1%) “strongly agreed”, 170 (45.5%) “agreed”, 129 (34.5%) were “not sure”, 17 (4.5%) “disagreed”, and 9 (2.4%) “strongly disagreed”, indicating more than half of the interns were willing to promote such knowledge (Table 1).

Attitudes of nursing interns toward hospice care (FATCOD-B-C scale Scores)

Overall and item-level scores

The total score of the Chinese version of the Frommelt Attitude Toward Care of the Dying Scale (FATCOD-B-C) for the 374 nursing interns was 114.79 ± 10.68, with scores ranging from 87 to 145, reflecting a moderate level of positive attitude toward hospice care among the participants.

At the item level, the item “Giving care to the dying person is a worthwhile experience” scored highest (4.55 ± 0.70), showing most interns recognized the value of caring for dying patients. In contrast, “Addiction to pain - relieving medication should not be a concern when caring for a dying person” scored lowest (2.92 ± 1.25), suggesting interns were concerned about addiction risk and it may affect their medication use in practice.

Other high - scoring items were “Families should be concerned about helping their dying member make the best of his/her remaining life” (4.53 ± 0.65) and “As a patient nears death, the nonfamily caregiver should withdraw his/her involvement with the patient” (4.51 ± 0.62), indicating interns valued the family role in end - of - life care and recognized non - family caregiver withdrawal at the end stage. Low - scoring items included “Care should extend to the family of the dying person” (3.50 ± 1.04) and “Dying persons should be given honest answers about their condition” (3.22 ± 0.91), showing interns lacked awareness of extending care to families and were conservative in providing honest patient information to the dying (Table 2).

Dimension-level scores

The FATCOD-B-C scale was divided into six dimensions to evaluate the interns’ attitudes toward different aspects of hospice care. Among them, “Attitude towards caring for the dying person” (Dimension II) had the highest mean score (23.88 ± 2.87), followed by “Attitude towards the interests of the dying person” (Dimension I, 21.95 ± 3.14) and “Attitude towards the necessity of family support” (Dimension III, 21.18 ± 2.39). This shows that the interns had the most positive attitude toward the act of caring for dying patients itself, and also paid more attention to the interests of dying patients and the necessity of family support in end-of-life care.

The dimension with the lowest mean score was “Attitude towards communication with the dying person” (Dimension V, 11.53 ± 1.73), followed by “Attitude towards caring for the dying person’s family” (Dimension VI, 16.24 ± 2.09) and “Attitude towards fear of caring for the dying person” (Dimension IV, 20.00 ± 2.27). This indicates that the interns had relatively weak abilities and confidence in communicating with dying patients, insufficient attention to the care needs of the dying patient’s family, and a certain degree of fear when facing the care of dying patients (Table 3).

Factors influencing attitudes of nursing interns toward hospice care

Stepwise multiple linear regression analysis explored factors influencing nursing interns’ FATCOD - B - C scale total score. Before regression, the Durbin - Watson statistic was 0.340 (0–4), and a value < 1.5 (acceptable for cross - sectional study) indicated positive autocorrelation among variables. The regression model was significant (F = 12.438, p < 0.001), meaning selected variables could explain interns’ attitudes toward hospice care. Seven variables entered the final equation (all p < 0.05):

Attitudes toward death: Unstandardized coefficient (B) was 3.634, standardized (Beta) was 0.225 (p < 0.001). A more positive attitude toward death was linked to a higher FATCOD - B - C scale score and a more positive attitude toward hospice care, being the most influential factor. Gender: B was − 5.005, Beta was − 0.207 (p < 0.001). After controlling for other variables, male interns had a higher scale score than females, showing a more positive attitude.

The internship duration was 4.496 months on average, with a standard deviation of 0.194 (p < 0.001). Longer internships correlated with higher scale scores. Interns with internships lasting seven or more months exhibited a more positive attitude compared to those with internships lasting one to six months. Encouraging loved ones to consider hospice care had a B value of -4.225 and a Beta of -0.118 (p = 0.014), indicating a positive correlation with a higher scale score, reflecting personal advocacy. Experiencing a relative’s death had a B value of -2.508 and a Beta of -0.104 (p = 0.028), showing that interns with this experience had higher scale scores, suggesting that personal exposure to death within the family positively influenced their attitudes. Desiring specialized hospice courses had a B value of -3.396 and a Beta of -0.104 (p = 0.033), indicating that interns who hoped for such courses had higher scale scores, reflecting a strong demand for education and its positive impact on attitude. Willingness to share knowledge about hospice care had a B value of -1.192 and a Beta of -0.096 (p = 0.048), leading to a slightly higher scale score, indicating a weak but positive influence on attitude (Table 4).

Nursing interns’ willingness to receive hospice care education

The geriatric nursing course, a core component of the nursing curriculum, includes hospice care content. Survey results indicated that 72.5% of nursing interns were satisfied with the delivery of this content, though a smaller percentage were dissatisfied, suggesting areas for improvement to better meet all interns’ learning needs. When asked about their willingness to contribute to hospice care development, 56.7% of interns expressed a positive attitude, while some were uncertain or unwilling, potentially due to factors such as insufficient knowledge, fear of death, and unclear career paths. Notably, 93.5% of interns recognized the importance of hospice care and end-of-life education, with only a few considering it unnecessary, affirming that integrating such education into nursing curricula aligns with most interns’ learning expectations. Regarding the impact of hospice care studies on nursing quality and competence, 91.2% of interns held a positive view, believing it enhances care for terminally ill patients and supports overall professional development. A minority were uncertain or thought it unhelpful, indicating that most interns acknowledge the professional value of hospice care learning.

Discussion

This study offers insights into Chinese student nurses’ attitudes towards end-of-life care. The total FATCOD-B-C score of 114.79 ± 10.68 (range: 87–145) indicates nursing interns held a moderate level of positive attitude toward hospice care—a result consistent with prior observations of Chinese nursing populations but lower than scores from Western studies (e.g., mean scores of 125–130 in U.S. and European samples using the same scale) [14–16]. This discrepancy can be attributed to deep-rooted cultural taboos around death in China, a factor directly supported by our data: 10.2% of interns reported “avoiding mentioning death” (Table 1), and low scores on death-related items—such as “Dying persons should be given honest answers about their condition” (3.22 ± 0.91, Table 2)—reflect reluctance to confront mortality, a tendency shaped by Confucian values emphasizing “harmony” and avoidance of “inauspicious” topics [17]. Nurses, including interns, frequently encounter death in clinical settings, impacting their behavior and hospice care quality. Studies show that negative attitudes can lead to avoidance, increasing patients’ loneliness. A positive attitude is crucial for high-quality hospice care [18] [19].

The highest mean score in ‘Attitude towards caring for the dying person’ (Dimension II: 23.88 ± 2.87) underscores a significant strength: interns value direct care for terminally ill patients. This aligns with their clinical experience, as 69.3% of interns have completed at least seven months of internship, including time in Yijishan Hospital’s hospice care unit, which annually serves over 200 terminally ill patients, mostly older adults with chronic diseases or advanced cancer. Conversely, the lowest score in ‘Attitude towards communication with the dying person’ (Dimension V: 11.53 ± 1.73) shows a critical gap. Interns are at ease with physical care but lack confidence in discussing death, addressing emotional needs, or answering prognosis - related questions. This mirrors previous findings that Chinese nursing curricula have inadequate communication training, with hospice content often confined to one chapter in geriatric nursing courses. Although 72.5% of interns are satisfied with this content, many still hesitate to communicate.

In traditional Chinese culture, discussing death is a cultural taboo because of ingrained beliefs and superstitions. This reluctance to face mortality results from multiple factors, including religious and philosophical influences. One contributing factor to this taboo is the influence of Confucianism, which emphasizes harmony and respect for ancestors [20]. In Confucian thought, discussions about death are considered disrespectful or ominous as they may disrupt the balance between the living and the deceased. So, individuals avoid death - related conversations for fear of offending ancestors or inviting bad luck. Many think keeping away from the word “death” keeps them from bad luck. They reject “death” mentally and thus lack mental or behavioral preparation for it. Nevertheless, it’s important to recognize that death is an inevitable and natural part of life that every living being experiences [17].

Item-level results further clarify these gaps. The highest-scoring item—“Giving care to the dying person is a worthwhile experience” (4.55 ± 0.70, Table 2)—confirms interns’ recognition of caregiving value, while the lowest-scoring item—“Addiction to pain-relieving medication should not be a concern when caring for dying person” (2.92 ± 1.25, Table 2)—exposes fears of medical risks. This fear may stem from limited training in palliative pain management, as noted in prior surveys where only 20.6% of Chinese healthcare providers reported feeling competent to discuss end-of-life care with patients and families [21]. Together, these scores illustrate a “care-action gap”: interns hold positive views of hospice care in principle but lack the knowledge and confidence to translate this into comprehensive, patient-centered practice.

The study found that men generally had higher attitude scores towards hospice care compared to women. This suggests that there may be differences in how men and women perceive and value end-of-life care. It is important to note that these findings do not imply that all men have a positive attitude towards hospice care or that all women have a negative attitude [22]. Research has shown that men tend to prioritize problem-solving approaches while women may focus more on emotional support when dealing with difficult situations like end-of-life care decisions. According to the study conducted by Karla T, female caregivers experience a significant decrease in their self-esteem and face various negative impacts on their daily schedule, health, and family support [23]. This research sheds light on the challenges faced by women who take on caregiving responsibilities. Additionally, it is worth considering the possibility of differences in exposure or awareness of hospice services among men and women. Factors such as healthcare access, caregiving responsibilities, or previous experiences with terminal illness might influence one’s perception of hospice care.

The study found that a significant number of participants (73%) had experienced the loss of a loved one. This experience seemed to have influenced their attitudes towards hospice care, as they were found to be more receptive and open-minded towards it compared to those who had not gone through such an ordeal. The findings suggest that personal experiences with death and dying can shape our perceptions and beliefs about end-of-life care [24]. It also highlights the importance of providing adequate support and resources for individuals who are dealing with grief and loss, as this can have a profound impact on their overall well-being [25]. When participants were presented with the choice question regarding their attitude towards death, only 47.6% opted for the “Face calmly” alternative. The choice of maintaining a calm demeanor in the face of mortality reflects an acceptance and peace with the concept of death.

In the current investigation, nursing interns with a greater wealth of clinical experience exhibited more favorable attitudes towards hospice care; conversely, nurses who possessed only six months of clinical experience demonstrated lower attitude scores. This finding is also consistent with the findings of previous studies suggesting that clinical experience plays a significant role in shaping nurses’ attitudes towards hospice care [26]. These studies have consistently shown that nurses who have more exposure to working in hospice settings tend to develop a deeper understanding and appreciation for the importance of end-of-life care. The plausibility arises from the likelihood that seasoned nurses may have encountered a greater number of terminal cases in their daily practice, thereby enhancing their comprehension of patients nearing the end of life in an accumulative fashion [27]. Therefore, nursing interns with limited practical experience may constitute a crucial target demographic for institutional hospice education.

The survey found that 88% of trainee nursing interns expressed their desire for a dedicated hospice course. This indicates the growing recognition and importance of specialized training in end-of-life care within the nursing profession. The existing chapters on hospice-related topics in geriatric care were unable to fully meet the needs and expectations of these aspiring nurses. While geriatric care does touch upon certain aspects of palliative care, there is a clear demand for more comprehensive education specifically focused on hospice practices. By offering a dedicated hospice course, nursing schools can equip their nursing interns with essential knowledge and skills required to provide compassionate and effective end-of-life care. Such a curriculum could cover various areas including pain management, emotional support for patients and families, ethical considerations, communication techniques, grief counseling, and interdisciplinary collaboration. In conclusion, it is evident from the survey results that trainee nursing interns are eager for a dedicated hospice course as they recognize its significance in providing quality end-of-life care. By tailoring educational programs accordingly and ensuring exposure to real-world scenarios through internships or clinical placements at hospices, we can better prepare future nurses who will play an integral role in supporting individuals during their final stages of life. The Medical Humanities program at Indiana University-Purdue University Indianapolis (IUPUI) offers an experiential learning course that integrates student volunteer hospice service with a reflective analysis of the intricate relationship between end-of-life care, medicine, and patient well-being, while emphasizing the intrinsic value of narrative sharing [28].

In this study, the necessity of education on hospice knowledge was acknowledged by 93.5% of the participants. A significant majority of the participants (91.2%) acknowledged that acquiring hospice knowledge enhanced the fundamental qualities and overall competence within the nursing profession. Furthermore, by recognizing that acquiring hospice knowledge enhances fundamental qualities and overall competence within the nursing profession, participants are emphasizing its potential benefits not only for patient care but also for professional growth. This acknowledgment reflects a desire among nurses to continuously improve their skills and expand their expertise to provide comprehensive and compassionate care to patients facing terminal illnesses. By embracing education on hospice knowledge, nurses can develop a deeper understanding of pain management techniques, symptom control strategies, emotional support approaches, communication skills with patients and families, ethical considerations surrounding end-of-life decisions, as well as cultural sensitivity when dealing with diverse populations. Such enhanced competencies enable nurses to deliver personalized care tailored to each patient’s unique needs while respecting their values and preferences.

Hospice care service plays a crucial role in providing comprehensive support and comfort to patients who are facing life-limiting illnesses [29]. In addition to reducing the physical pain experienced by patients, hospice care also focuses on enhancing their overall quality of life [30]. This includes addressing emotional, spiritual, and social needs, ensuring that patients receive holistic care. It represents a crucial form of social service, with its significance for healthcare professionals being self-evident. Although they are nursing students with certain professional qualities, they will still be uneasy and escape in the face of death. This is because death is an inevitable part of their future clinical practice. As healthcare professionals, nurses often find themselves confronted with life-threatening situations and end-of-life care. It is crucial for us to help these nursing interns establish a correct view of death during their student days. In order to do so, we can provide them with comprehensive education on palliative care and end-of-life support. By teaching them about the physical, emotional, and spiritual aspects of dying, we can equip them with the necessary knowledge and skills to navigate these challenging situations confidently. Furthermore, it is essential to create a supportive learning environment where students feel comfortable discussing their fears and concerns surrounding death [31]. Encouraging open dialogue among peers and faculty members can foster empathy and understanding towards patients who are nearing the end of their lives [32]. Additionally, exposing nursing interns to real-life scenarios through simulations or clinical rotations can help desensitize them to the fear associated with death. By gradually introducing them to such experiences under proper guidance, we can assist in building their resilience while ensuring patient safety remains paramount. Moreover, incorporating reflective practices into their curriculum allows nursing interns to process their emotions related to mortality. Engaging in self-reflection exercises or participating in group discussions enables them to explore personal beliefs about life’s fragility while promoting personal growth as future healthcare providers [33].

China’s death education is still in its initial stage, and the development of hospice care curricula remains at an early stage [34]. This aligns with our study’s observations: while 93.5% of nursing interns recognized the necessity of hospice knowledge and 88% hoped for specialized hospice courses, current nursing education often limits hospice content to a single chapter in geriatric nursing courses—resulting in gaps in interns’ communication confidence (Dimension V: 11.53 ± 1.73) and death-related attitudes (10.2% of interns avoid mentioning death). Some forward-looking institutions (e.g., the Third Military Medical University) have begun exploring death education curriculum reforms, reflecting a growing recognition of this gap. These institutions have implemented innovative approaches to incorporate “death education” into the curriculum for medical students [35]. For example, the Third Military Medical University has developed a comprehensive framework for the core content of this course using early expert enquiry methods. Their curriculum includes case discussions that allow nursing interns to analyze real-life scenarios related to death, providing practical insights for their future careers [36]. Additionally, field visits to professional funeral places offer students firsthand exposure to different cultural practices and rituals associated with death, broadening their understanding and fostering empathy towards individuals from diverse backgrounds [37]. Screening movies related to death during classes also evokes emotional resonance and facilitates meaningful discussions among nursing interns.

Our study highlights the urgent need to address unmet educational needs in hospice and death education for nursing interns. Despite high demand for specialized training, the current fragmented curriculum leaves gaps in knowledge and attitudes, such as death communication and pain management confidence. Future nursing education reforms should focus on expanding structured hospice and death education to bridge these gaps. Subsequent research should evaluate the impact of various educational approaches on nursing students’ understanding and acceptance of hospice care, aiming to enhance the quality of end-of-life care provided by future healthcare professionals.

Strengths and limitations

It employs the validated FATCOD-B-C scale, yielding reliable and comparable attitude scores (e.g., total score 114.79 ± 10.68) and circumventing the issue of unstandardized questionnaires seen in prior Chinese studies. Additionally, the study’s detailed clinical context and targeted sample of 374 nursing interns from Yijishan Hospital, all possessing significant clinical internship experience, fill gaps in previous research and render the findings pertinent to future hospice caregivers. Moreover, the comprehensive analysis of influencing factors via stepwise multiple linear regression pinpoints actionable predictors tied to nursing education and clinical practice, offering valuable insights for curriculum reform and bolstering the study’s practical value.

The studied sample was mainly from Wuhu, which may lead to insufficient representation to fully reflect the overall attitude towards hospice care in China. Cross - sectional study findings are limited to a specific time and can’t capture dynamic changes in nursing students’ attitudes. Relevant factors influencing their attitudes may not be fully considered. The FATCOD - B - C scale may not accurately reflect student nurses’ attitudes due to cultural or language variances. Future research should use larger, more diverse samples and longitudinal designs to address these limitations. Also, further validation of measurement tools in Chinese nursing education is recommended.

Conclusion

This study evaluated the attitudes of 374 nursing interns toward hospice care in Wuhu using the FATCOD-B-C scale, revealing that interns had a moderate positive attitude (total score 114.79 ± 10.68). They recognized the value of caregiving but had gaps in death communication. Key predictors of positive attitudes included attitude toward death, internship duration, and demand for specialized hospice courses. Significantly, 88% of interns desired dedicated hospice education. Future directions, aligned with China’s end-of-life care needs, include integrating structured death education and hospice courses into nursing curricula, enhancing internship quality with mentored hospice rotations, and conducting nationwide longitudinal studies to track the impact of these interventions. Adapting the FATCOD-B-C scale to better reflect Chinese cultural contexts is also crucial. These steps can help bridge the hospice care gap in China, preparing future nurses to meet the rising demand for compassionate end-of-life care as the population ages.
