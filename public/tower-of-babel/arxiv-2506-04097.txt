Unveiling coherent dynamics in non-Markovian open quantum systems: exact expression and recursive perturbation expansion
Alessandra Colla, Heinz-Peter Breuer, Giulio Gasbarri

Abstract
We introduce a systematic framework to derive the effective Hamiltonian governing the coherent dynamics of non-Markovian open quantum systems. By applying the minimal dissipation principle, we uniquely isolate the coherent contribution to the time-local generator of the reduced dynamics. We derive a general expression for the effective Hamiltonian and develop a recursive perturbative expansion that expresses it in terms of system-bath interaction terms and bath correlation functions. This expansion provides a systematic tool for analyzing energy renormalization effects across different coupling regimes. Applying our framework to paradigmatic spin systems, we reveal how environmental correlations influence energy shifts and eigenbasis rotations, offering new insights into strong-coupling effects and non-Markovian quantum thermodynamics.



Introduction —

The dynamics of open quantum systems are shaped by the intricate interplay between coherent evolution and environmental interactions
Breuer and Petruccione 2007
;
Vacchini 2024
.
Beyond inducing dissipation and decoherence, these interactions can renormalize the system’s energy structure, leading to phenomena such as the Lamb
Bethe 1947
;
Lamb and Retherford 1947
and AC Stark shifts
Autler and Townes 1955
.
The Lamb shift, a prototypical example of renormalization effects, arises from electromagnetic vacuum fluctuations that shift atomic energy levels.
This effect exemplifies how environmental couplings can modify the intrinsic properties of quantum systems, with profound implications for both theoretical understanding and practical applications.

Within the Lindblad formalism, this shift naturally emerges from tracing out the environmental degrees of freedom, reflecting weak-coupling and Markovian assumptions
Breuer and Petruccione 2007
.
However, extending such concepts to strong coupling, non-Markovian regimes, and general bipartite systems remains an open challenge
Hossein-Nejad et al. 2015
;
Malavazi and Brito 2022
;
Hernandes Alves Malavazi 2022
;
Nicacio and Maia 2023
;
Neves and Brito 2023
;
Neves and Brito 2024
;
Tiwari et al. 2025
.

A powerful strategy for the treatment of non-Markovian dynamics is provided by time-convolutionless master equations
Breuer et al. 2001
;
Breuer and Petruccione 2007
, which are a complete description of the open system dynamics. Such master equations are local in time and involve a generally time-dependent generator
\mathcal{L}_{t}
, which can be expressed in a generalized Lindblad form
Hall et al. 2014
;
Breuer 2012
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{t}[X]=-i[K(t),X]+\mathcal{D}_{t}[X].

(1)

Here,
K(t)
is an effective Hamiltonian that encodes coherent energy shifts, while
\mathcal{D}_{t}[X]
represents dissipative contributions, potentially including negative jump rates – a possible signature of non-Markovian behavior
Rivas et al. 2014
;
Breuer et al. 2016
.

Unfortunately, an intrinsic symmetry of the generator renders the decomposition of
\mathcal{L}_{t}
into a commutator with
K(t)
and a dissipator
\mathcal{D}_{t}
not unique
Breuer and Petruccione 2007
;
Chruściński 2022
, making the definition of
K(t)
inherently ambiguous.
Resolving this ambiguity is critical to quantifying coherent energy shifts in non-Markovian systems and for arbitrary coupling regimes.

Recent work by Hayden and Sorce introduced a procedure to address this matter
Sorce and Hayden 2022
.
Their method defines an effective Hamiltonian via minimizing the dissipation, thus isolating the coherent contribution to the system’s dynamics.
This results in a dissipator characterized by traceless Lindblad operators, consistently recovering the Markovian and weak-coupling regime case where the renormalization of the Hamiltonian is well understood.
Notably, the suitability of the effective Hamiltonian obtained via the minimal dissipation principle for determining energy shifts has been experimentally tested using trapped ions also in a highly non-Markovian setting
Colla et al. 2025
, where the energy level shifts are typically time-dependent.

The concept of an effective Hamiltonian is not only important for a deeper understanding of open quantum systems, but also serves as a critical bridge to quantum thermodynamics, particularly in strong coupling and non-Markovian regimes
Colla and Breuer 2022
.
In the context of quantum thermal machines, the effective Hamiltonian captures essential energy renormalization effects due to system-bath interactions, which can potentially impact the performance of quantum heat engines and refrigerators
Picatoste et al. 2024
.

For non-Markovian environments, time-dependent effective Hamiltonians can induce work transfer between the system and its environment, revealing novel thermodynamic behaviors and challenging conventional understanding of energy flow in quantum systems, as recently shown in
Colla and Breuer 2022
;
Huang and Zhang 2022
;
Colla and Breuer 2024
.

Despite its importance, computing
K(t)
often involves substantial analytical or numerical challenges, particularly in complex models or strong-coupling regimes, as time-local generators are difficult to find for general open quantum systems.
Exact analytical expressions are known only for a set of paradigmatic integrable models, such as the Jaynes-Cummings
Smirne and Vacchini 2010
, Fano-Anderson
Huang and Zhang 2022
;
Picatoste et al. 2024
;
Colla and Breuer 2024
, and Caldeira-Leggett Hamiltonians
Hu et al. 1992
, linearly coupled Gaussian systems
Ferialdi 2016
, and pure dephasing models
Łuczka 1990
;
Doll et al. 2008
.

In more complicated cases, time-local generators may be found via numerical simulation techniques as HEOM
Tanimura and Kubo 1989
or pseudomodes
Tamascelli et al. 2018
;
Mascherpa et al. 2020
, or experimentally through quantum process tomography.
In well-behaved regimes, a time-convolutionless (TCL) perturbation expansion for weak-coupling parameters may instead be sufficient for an appropriate description of the system dynamics.
In all these cases, finding the minimal dissipation splitting, and thus the effective Hamiltonian
K(t)
, may require heavy manipulation.

In this Letter, we provide an operational expression for the effective Hamiltonian
K(t)
directly from the time-local generator
\mathcal{L}_{t}
, allowing for an easy extraction of the coherent dynamics once
\mathcal{L}_{t}
is known.
We also provide a recursive formula to derive
K(t)
as a perturbative series in terms of the coupling strength, expressed using system-bath interaction operators and bath correlation functions.
This recursive formula allows for a systematic analysis of the effective Hamiltonian’s behavior at different coupling strengths, in all cases in which an exact analytical expression of
\mathcal{L}_{t}
is not known.
We apply the series to paradigmatic spin models, and show how certain characteristics of the environment affect the structure of the spin’s Hamiltonian renormalization.

Effective Hamiltonian –

The procedure recently proposed by Hayden and Sorce to uniquely fix the effective Hamiltonian is based on projecting the generator
\mathcal{L}
onto the subspace of superoperators that act as commutators with Hermitian operators, i.e.,
\mathcal{H}=-i[H,\cdot]
, where
H=H^{\dagger}

Sorce and Hayden 2022
. This projection is performed using a scalar product defined by:

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{L}_{1},\mathcal{L}_{2}\rangle:=\overline{\bra{\psi}\overline{\mathcal{L}_{1}[\ket{\phi}\bra{\phi}]\mathcal{L}_{2}[\ket{\phi}\bra{\phi}]}\ket{\psi}},

(2)

where the overline denotes averaging over Haar-random pure states
\ket{\phi}
and
\ket{\psi}
.
This approach minimizes the dissipator assuming maximum uncertainty over input and output states (i.e. uniformly sampling over pure states), yielding a canonical effective Hamiltonian
K
for any linear, Hermiticity-preserving, trace-annihilating generator
\mathcal{L}\in\mathfrak{htp}(\mathscr{H})
(where
\mathscr{H}
denotes the Hilbert space of the reduced system).

The physical interpretation of this procedure is better understood via the average fidelity.
The fidelity between a noiseless von Neumann generator
\mathcal{H}=-i[H,\cdot]
, associated with some Hamiltonian
H
, and the generator of a noisy channel described by
\mathcal{L}
is defined as:

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{L})=\int d\psi\,\mathrm{Tr}\left\{\mathcal{H}[\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi}]\,\mathcal{L}[\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi}]\right\}.

(3)

This quantity measures how “faithfully” the noisy dynamics generated by
\mathcal{L}
approximates that of the noiseless
\mathcal{H}
when applied to randomly chosen pure states, and is connected to the scalar product (
2
) between the generators simply through a scaling with the Hilbert space dimentions
d=\dim(\mathscr{H})
, i.e.

\braket{\mathcal{H},\mathcal{L}}=\frac{1}{d}\overline{\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{L})
.

Expanding
H
in a basis of traceless Hermitian operators
\{H_{j}\}_{j}
, orthonormal under the Hilbert-Schmidt product, the effective Hamiltonian is expressed as (see end matter):

\displaystyle K=\frac{d+1}{2}\sum_{j}\overline{\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{H}_{j},\mathcal{L})H_{j},

(4)

where
\mathcal{H}_{j}=-i[H_{j},\cdot]

This expression for
K
establishes a direct connection between the average fidelity and the effective Hamiltonian.
It reveals that the effective Hamiltonian is a weighted sum of basis operators
H_{j}
, with weights determined by the average fidelity between
\mathcal{L}
and the generators corresponding to each basis operator.
It quantifies how well different components of the dynamics preserve quantum information on average and clarifies the equal apriori probability – or maximum uncertainty – principle embedded in the choice of the norm induced by the scalar product (
2
).

Equation (
4
) provides physical insight into
K
, but it is not practical for calculations.
Therefore, we provide a general expression for the effective Hamiltonian associated with the canonical splitting under
(
2
) of any given generator
\mathcal{L}\in\mathfrak{htp}(\mathscr{H})
, which constitutes our first result.

Proposition
.

Let
\{F_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha=0}^{d^{2}-1}
be any orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{B}(\mathscr{H})
with respect to the Hilbert-Schmidt scalar product,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\{F_{\alpha}^{{\dagger}}F_{\beta}\}=\delta_{\alpha\beta}\;,\forall\;\alpha,\beta\;.

(5)

Then, for any generator
\mathcal{L}\in\mathfrak{htp}(\mathscr{H})
its corresponding effective Hamiltonian is

\displaystyle K=\frac{1}{2id}\sum_{\alpha=0}^{d^{2}-1}[F_{\alpha}^{{\dagger}},\mathcal{L}[F_{\alpha}]]\;.

(6)

The proof can be found in the end matter.
Using the operator basis
F_{\alpha}=|j\rangle\langle k|
, where
|j\rangle
form an orthonormal basis on
\mathscr{H}
, this becomes:

\displaystyle K=\frac{1}{2id}\sum_{j,k=1}^{d}[|j\rangle\langle k|,\,\mathcal{L}[|k\rangle\langle j|]].

(7)

These new expressions are applicable to a wide range of quantum systems and coupling regimes, and offer a more practical approach for computing
K
whenever the exact generator is explicitly known. However, in most cases, an explicit form of
\mathcal{L}
is only accessible via perturbative treatment. In these cases, the form of the generator is typically given by
\mathcal{L}[X]=\sum_{ij}\omega_{ij}V_{i}XW_{j}
, with
V_{i}
and
W_{j}
satisfying Hermiticity-preservation and trace-annihilation conditions. This leads to an effective Hamiltonian of the following form:

\displaystyle K=\frac{1}{2id}\sum_{ij}\omega_{ij}\left(\mathrm{Tr}(V_{i})W_{j}-\mathrm{Tr}(W_{j})V_{i}\right),

(8)

where the result from
Sorce and Hayden 2022
is recovered when
\omega_{ij}
is diagonal (pseudo-Kraus representation). In what follows, we provide a technique to recover the contributions to the effective Hamiltonian at each order of the perturbation expansion.

Systematic perturbation expansion —

The generator
\mathcal{L}_{t}
of an open quantum system typically arises from the microscopic unitary evolution of a closed bipartite system, where the system
S
interacts with an environment
E
(the irrelevant degrees of freedom that are traced out). The total Hamiltonian governing this evolution is given by:

\displaystyle H=H_{S}+H_{E}+\lambda H_{I},

(9)

where
H_{S}
and
H_{E}
are the free Hamiltonians of the system and environment, respectively, and
\lambda
is the coupling parameter for the interaction
H_{I}
. For simplicity, we consider a factorized interaction Hamiltonian,
H_{I}=A\otimes B
, where
A
and
B
act on the system and environment, respectively. Extensions to sums of such terms are straightforward. The total initial state is assumed to factorize as
\rho_{SE}(0)=\rho_{S}\otimes\rho_{E}
, a common assumption in open quantum systems
Colla et al. 2022
.

Finding the exact effective Hamiltonian
K
for the reduced system
S
is generally challenging without an explicit expression for the time local generator
\mathcal{L}_{t}
associated to the reduced dynamics of the system only. Here, we provide a systematic perturbation expansion for
K
in powers of the coupling strength
\lambda
.
This constitutes the second main result of this work.
Below, we outline the strategy; technical details are provided in
Colla et al.
.

The interaction picture is used to simplify calculations, where the interaction Hamiltonian becomes time-dependent:
\tilde{H}_{t}=A(t)\otimes B(t)
.
The total unitary evolution can then be written as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{t}[\rho_{SE}(0)]=\mathcal{T}\left(e^{-i\int_{0}^{t}d\tau(\tilde{H}\tau^{L}-\tilde{H}\tau^{R})}\right)[\rho_{S}\otimes\rho_{E}],

(10)

where
\mathcal{T}
denotes the time ordering and
\tilde{H}_{\tau}^{L/R}
represents the operator acting on
\rho
from the left and from the right
Diósi 1993
;
Diósi 1990
;
Chou et al. 1985
.

The reduced dynamics of the system are obtained by tracing out the environment degrees of freedom yielding a dynamical map:

\Phi_{t}[\rho_{S}]=\mathrm{Tr}_{E}\{\mathcal{U}_{t}[\rho_{S}\otimes\rho_{E}]\}
.
Expanding the unitary evolution in a Taylor series, we express
\Phi_{t}
as:

\displaystyle\Phi_{t}=1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-i)^{n}\lambda^{n}\mu_{n},

(11)

where each term
\mu_{n}
depends on time-ordered integrals over system and bath operators. These contributions can be decomposed as:

\displaystyle\mu_{n}^{k}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}d\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k}d\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k}D(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k},\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k})A^{L}(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k})A^{R}(\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k}),

(12)

where we have defined

\displaystyle{A^{L/R}}(\bm{t}_{1}^{k})[\,\cdot\,]

\displaystyle:=A^{L/R}_{t_{1}}\circ{A^{L/R}}_{t_{2}}\circ...\circ{A^{L/R}}_{t_{k}}\;,

(13)

and with shorthand integral notation
\int_{0}^{t}d\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k}d\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k}
indicating integration over
k
times
{\tau_{1},...,\tau_{k}}
and
(n-k)
times
{s_{1},...,s_{n-k}}
. Here,
D(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k},\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k})
encodes environmental correlations via
n
-point bath correlation functions:

\displaystyle D(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k},\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k})=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\left\{B^{R}(\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k})B^{L}(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k})\rho_{E}\right\}\ \theta_{\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k}}\theta_{\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k}},

where
\theta_{\bm{\tau}_{1}^{n}}
ensures time ordering within each set of variables
\{\tau_{j}\}_{j\in[1,n]}
, i.e.
\theta_{\bm{\tau}_{1}^{n}}
is equal to 1 if
t_{1}>t_{2}>\dots\tau_{n}
and zero otherwise.

Following
Gasbarri and Ferialdi 2018
, the generator can be expressed as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{t}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\lambda^{n}\mathcal{L}_{n},

(14)

where each term
\mathcal{L}_{n}
depends on combinations of
\mu_{n}
terms and their time derivatives.
Applying eq. (
8
) for each order of
\mathcal{L}_{n}
, we obtain a perturbative expansion for the effective Hamiltonian:

\displaystyle K(t)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\lambda^{n}K_{n}.

(15)

With notation
\braket{X}_{1/d}:=\mathrm{Tr}\{X\}/d
, and defining the operator
{\mathbb{A}}\!\left(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k}\!,\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k}\right):=\langle A(\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k})^{\dagger}\rangle_{1/d}A(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k})
, the
n
th-order contribution to
K(t)
is given by:

\displaystyle K_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{-(+i)^{n}}{2i}\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-)^{k}\int_{0}^{t}d\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k}d\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k}\left[\mathcal{D}(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k},\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k}){\mathbb{A}}\!\left(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k}\!,\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k}\right)-(-)^{n}\text{h.c.}\right]\;,

(16)

where the functions
\mathcal{D}
are bath
n
-ordered cumulants and are linear combinations of products of the
n
-point correlation functions (see companion paper
Colla et al.
) which can be recovered via the following recursion formula:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k},\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k})

\displaystyle=\dot{D}(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k},\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k})

\displaystyle\quad-\sum_{l=0}^{k}\sum_{r=0}^{n-k}\mathcal{D}(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{l},\bm{s}_{1}^{r}){D}(\bm{\tau}_{l+1}^{k},\bm{s}_{r+1}^{n-k})

(17)

where

\displaystyle\dot{D}(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k},\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k})=D(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k},\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k})\big(\delta_{\tau_{1},t}+\delta_{s_{1},t}\big)\;,

(18)

and we have taken the convention
\int_{0}^{t}d\tau\delta_{\tau,t}f(\tau)=f(t)
for any function of
f
. The time ordering functions
\theta
inside each term
D
are responsible for the time ordering of all operators and functions in the integrals, and will give rise to a number of different terms that are ordered in “chunks”, similarly to the ordered cumulants of van Kampen
Van Kampen 1974a
;
Van Kampen 1974b
.

These expressions allow systematic computation of
K(t)
at any order in
\lambda
, revealing how system-bath interactions renormalize energy levels. Notably, eq. (
16
) provides explicit information about the structure of
K(t)
in terms of system operators (
A
) and their interaction-picture dynamics and the
n
-point correlation functions of the bath.

Spin systems —

To elucidate the structure of the effective Hamiltonian, we apply our results to open spin systems. Without loss of generality, we take
H_{S}=\frac{\omega}{2}\sigma_{z}
.
The recursive series for
K
here derived reveals whether the interaction with a thermal bath generates only a shift of the energy levels of the free Hamiltonian or also a rotation of its eigenvectors.

Suppose that
A=\sigma_{z}
, resulting in a situation of pure dephasing for the spin, where
A(t)\equiv A

\forall t
.
Using (
16
), we find (see end matter) that the contribution of the odd terms of the series commute with the free Hamiltonian, and the even terms are vanishing.
This leads to an effective Hamiltonian for pure dephasing of the form

\displaystyle K(t)=\frac{\omega_{r}(t)}{2}\sigma_{z}\;,

(19)

where only the odd ordered cumulants of the bath contribute to the time-dependent energy shift.

This result agrees with time-dependent shifts obtained in
Seegebrecht and Schilling 2024
(dispersive spin oscillator) and in
Neves and Brito 2024
(pure qubit-qubit dephasing).
It furthermore explains the absence of renormalization found in
Seegebrecht and Schilling 2024
(displaced spin oscillator), and in the well-known dephasing spin-boson model
Leggett et al. 1987
:

\displaystyle H=\frac{\omega}{2}\sigma_{z}+\sum_{j}\omega_{j}a^{\dagger}_{j}a_{j}+\sum_{j}g_{j}\sigma_{z}\otimes(a^{\dagger}_{j}+a_{j})

(20)

with an initial state of the environment that is diagonal in the Fock basis (including, in particular, a thermal state), for which the exact TCL master equation (already exact at second order
Doll et al. 2008
) is well known to be

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}_{S}(t)=-i\left[\frac{\omega}{2}\sigma_{z},\rho_{S}(t)\right]+\gamma(t)[\sigma_{z}\rho_{S}(t)\sigma_{z}-\rho_{S}(t)]\;.

(21)

This drastic difference between the nature of even and odd contributions of the series is not just limited to dephasing models. Let us consider an unbiased spin model, where the interaction term is described by an operator that is orthogonal, under the Hilbert-Schmidt scalar product, to the Hamiltonian term, such as
A=\sigma_{x}
.

In this case, the odd terms of the series do not commute with the free Hamiltonian, i.e.,
[K_{2m+1},H_{S}]\neq 0
, inducing a rotation of the eigenvectors of the Hamiltonian (see end matter).
In contrast, the even terms do commute with
H_{S}
, contributing to a shift in the energy spectrum. Thus, when odd orders vanish, the effective Hamiltonian only results in a renormalization of the spin frequency, as expressed in eq. (
19
).
Notably, this is the case for a spin-boson model at finite temperature:

\displaystyle H=\frac{\omega}{2}\sigma_{z}+\sum_{j}\omega_{j}a^{\dagger}_{j}a_{j}+\sum_{j}g_{j}\sigma_{x}\otimes(a^{\dagger}_{j}+a_{j})\;.

(22)

This result proves analytically what was conjectured in
Gatto et al. 2024
due to numerical evidence.

Commonly studied scenarios are those in which the dynamics is characterized by two-point correlation functions, as in the case of linearly coupled, zero-mean Gaussian baths.
The present analysis clarifies why such models exhibit simpler renormalization effects: in the case of pure dephasing, renormalization is entirely absent, while for orthogonal couplings, the renormalized Hamiltonian remains diagonal in the original eigenbasis. In contrast, when the bath distribution exhibits time asymmetry (i.e., when odd-order cumulants are nonzero), more intricate behaviors emerge, such as rotations of the Hamiltonian eigenbasis.

Structure of the Hamiltonian —

The examples above illustrate how the effective Hamiltonian exhibits qualitatively distinct behaviors depending on the properties of the interaction and the structure of the environment. More generally, the perturbative expansion reveals a fundamental symmetry distinction between even and odd orders. This distinction is not restricted to spin models; it emerges explicitly upon decomposing the bath’s ordered cumulants into their real and imaginary parts,
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{R}}+i\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{I}}
. Importantly, these two contributions are respectively symmetric and antisymmetric under time ordering reversal. A similar decomposition applies to the system-side contribution, by writing
{\mathbb{A}}\!\left(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k}\!,\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k}\right)
in terms of its real and imaginary components – namely,
{\mathbb{A}_{\mathcal{R}}}\!\left(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k}\!,\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k}\right)
and
{\mathbb{A}_{\mathcal{I}}}\!\left(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k}\!,\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k}\right)
, which are also symmetric and antisymmetric under time ordering reversal.

Making these decompositions explicit in eq. (
16
) we find that even-order terms
K_{2m}
couple bath and system contributions of opposing time symmetry classes, namely

\displaystyle K_{2m}\!=\!\!\!\sum_{k=0}^{2m}

\displaystyle(-)^{m+k+1}\!\!\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\!\!d\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k}d\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k}\!\!\left[\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{R}}\!\left(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k}\!,\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k}\right){\mathbb{A}_{\mathcal{I}}}\!\left(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k}\!,\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{I}}\!\left(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k}\!,\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k}\right){\mathbb{A}_{\mathcal{R}}}\!\left(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k}\!,\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k}\right)\right]\;,

(23)

ensuring an overall antisymmetric structure under time-ordering reversal.
Conversely, the odd terms
K_{2m+1}
couple terms of the same symmetry class and are overall symmetric

\displaystyle K_{2m\!+\!1}\!=\!\!\!\!\sum_{k=0}^{2m+1}\!\!

\displaystyle(-)^{m+k}\!\!\!\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\!\!\!d\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k}d\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k}\!\!\left[\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{I}}\!\left(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k}\!,\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k}\right)\!{\mathbb{A}_{\mathcal{I}}}\!\left(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k}\!,\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\left.-\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{R}}\!\left(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k}\!,\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k}\right){\mathbb{A}_{\mathcal{R}}}\!\left(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k}\!,\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k}\right)\right]\;,

(24)

highlighting the fundamentally distinct symmetry properties and structure of even and odd orders in the effective Hamiltonian.

From this perspective, we can clearly interpret the terms up to second order.
The first order contribution arises only if the interaction-picture bath operator has a non-zero average with respect to the bath’s initial state – often implying the presence of non-zero initial coherences in the environment
Colla and Breuer 2024
– and corresponds to a driving of the system resulting from a displacement of the bath state relative to the interaction Hamiltonian.

The second order contribution, instead, can be understood via linear response theory
Kubo 1957
as arising from fluctuations and dissipation in the environment. In this framework, dissipation is associated with
the imaginary part of the correlation function
\chi(t,s):=i\langle[{B}(t),{B}(s)]\rangle
, i.e. the response function, which
is antisymmetric under time reversal. This component
reflects the causal response of the environment and accounts for the irreversible energy exchange associated with it. Diffusion, on the other hand, is associated
to the real part of the correlation function
S(t,s):=\frac{1}{2}\langle\{{B}(t),{B}(s)\}\rangle-\langle{B}(t)\rangle\langle{B}(s)\rangle
, which is symmetric under time reversal. This symmetry represents the environmental fluctuations that drive random perturbations in the system state.
These random perturbations are responsible for
processes such as decoherence, and the spreading of the
system’s distribution over time.
In terms of these quantities, the second order contribution to
K
can be written as

\displaystyle K_{2}=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}d\tau\Big[\frac{1}{2i}S(t,\tau)[A(t),A(\tau)]

(25)

\displaystyle-\frac{\chi(t,\tau)}{4}\left(\{A(t),A(\tau)\}-\langle\{A(t),A(\tau)\}\rangle_{1/d}\right)\Big]\;.

These insights suggest that distinct environmental features underpin the structural differences observed between even and odd orders of the renormalized Hamiltonian. Although our explicit analysis has been limited to second-order terms, it is reasonable to expect higher-order terms to similarly reflect more intricate environmental correlations.

Conclusions –

We introduced a systematic framework to obtain the effective Hamiltonian governing coherent dynamics in non-Markovian open quantum systems. By employing the minimal dissipation principle, we uniquely isolated the coherent component of the time-local generator. We provided an operational expression enabling the direct extraction of the effective Hamiltonian from the generator, as well as a recursive perturbative expansion in terms of bath correlation functions and system-bath interactions.

Our perturbative analysis clarified how environmental correlations and their interplay with system operators lead to energy renormalization, demonstrating clear structural distinctions between even and odd orders of the expansion. These distinctions are used to explain recently observed behaviors in spin systems, including eigenbasis rotations and time-dependent energy shifts.

Our results establish a pathway to systematically characterize coherent renormalization effects beyond weak coupling, with direct relevance for strong-coupling quantum thermodynamics and the engineering of quantum thermal machines.

\braket{\mathcal{L}_{1},\mathcal{L}_{2}}=\int\!d\phi\!\int\!d\psi\bra{\phi}\mathcal{L}_{1}\left[\ket{\psi}\!\!\bra{\psi}\right]\mathcal{L}_{2}\left[\ket{\psi}\!\!\bra{\psi}\right]\ket{\phi}\;,

(26)

where
\psi
and
\phi
are Haar-random generated pure states.
The average fidelity
Nielsen 2002
between generators of a gate and a noisy channel defined in the main text is then directly proportional to the scalar product (
26
) of the generators:

\overline{\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{L})=\int d\psi\mathrm{Tr}\left\{\mathcal{H}\left[\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi}\right]\mathcal{L}\left[\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi}\right]\right\}=d\braket{\mathcal{H},\mathcal{L}}\;,

(27)

since
\int d\phi\bra{\phi}X\ket{\phi}=\mathrm{Tr}\{X\}/d
.
Then, in
Sorce and Hayden 2022
one chooses a basis of traceless Hamiltonians
\{H_{j}\}_{j}
such that their associated generators
\mathcal{H}_{j}
form an orthonormal basis for
\mathfrak{ham}(\mathscr{H})
under (
26

K=\sum_{j}H_{j}\braket{\mathcal{H}_{j},\mathcal{L}}
.
The Hamiltonian elements

are orthogonal under the Hilbert-Schmidt scalar product but not orthonormal, as
\mathrm{Tr}\{H_{j}^{2}\}=d(d+1)/2
. By defining a normalized basis for Hilbert-Schmidt

\displaystyle\tilde{H}_{j}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{d(d+1)}}H_{j}

(28)

Proof of Proposition 1

The proof is given as in
Colla and Breuer 2022
and
Sorce and Hayden 2022
by projecting the generator onto the subspace
\mathfrak{ham}(\mathscr{H})
of superoperators acting as
-i
times a commutator with a traceless Hamiltonian,

\mathcal{H}=\Pi(\mathcal{L})=\sum_{j}\mathcal{H}_{j}\langle\mathcal{H}_{j},\mathcal{L}\rangle\;,

(29)

with
\{\mathcal{H}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{d^{2}-1}
an orthonormal basis of
\mathfrak{ham}(\mathscr{H})
, through the scalar product (
26
) on
\mathfrak{htp}(\mathscr{H})
. Given the Hamiltonians
H_{j}
associated to the commutators in each
\mathcal{H}_{j}
, which obey
\mathrm{Tr}\{H_{i}H_{j}\}=\frac{d(d+1)}{2}\delta_{ij}
, the effective Hamiltonian
K
associated to
\mathcal{H}
reads
K=\sum_{j}H_{j}\langle\mathcal{H}_{j},\mathcal{L}\rangle

Colla and Breuer 2022
.
Using the second moment Haar integral and the cyclic properties of the trace, the coefficients
\langle\mathcal{H}_{j},\mathcal{L}\rangle
are given simply by

\langle\mathcal{H}_{j},\mathcal{L}\rangle=-\frac{i}{d}\mathrm{Tr}\{H_{j}\overline{\big[\ket{\phi}\bra{\phi},\mathcal{L}[\ket{\phi}\bra{\phi}]\big]}\}\;.

(30)

Given that for any operator
X
it holds that
\sum_{j}H_{j}\mathrm{Tr}\{H_{j}X\}=\frac{d(d+1)}{2}X
, we find

\displaystyle K=

\displaystyle\frac{d+1}{2i}\overline{\big[\ket{\phi}\bra{\phi},\mathcal{L}[\ket{\phi}\bra{\phi}]\big]}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(d+1)\text{Im}\Big(\overline{\ket{\phi}\bra{\phi}\mathcal{L}[\ket{\phi}\bra{\phi}]}\Big)\;.

(31)

To evaluate the fourth moment Haar integral for an unknown generator, consider an orthonormal basis
\{\ket{i}\}_{i=1}^{d}
of
\mathscr{H}
, and take a specific element
\ket{\bar{k}}=:\ket{\phi_{0}}
as reference state such that
\ket{\phi}=U\ket{\bar{k}}=\sum_{i}u_{i\bar{k}}\ket{i}
with
U
a Haar random unitary transformation. Then

\ket{\phi}\bra{\phi}\mathcal{L}[\ket{\phi}\bra{\phi}]=\sum_{ijkl}u_{i\bar{k}}u_{k\bar{k}}u^{*}_{j\bar{k}}u^{*}_{l\bar{k}}\ket{i}\!\!\bra{j}\mathcal{L}[\ket{k}\!\!\bra{l}]\;.

(32)

The Haar average therefore acts only on the unitary transformation elements, and the fourth moment is given in terms of the Weingarten functions
Wg
,
Gessner and Breuer 2013
:

\displaystyle\langle u_{i\bar{k}}u_{k\bar{k}}u^{*}_{j\bar{k}}u^{*}_{l\bar{k}}\rangle=

\displaystyle(\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}\delta_{\bar{k}\bar{k}}+\delta_{il}\delta_{jk}\delta_{\bar{k}\bar{k}})(\text{Wg}[1]+\text{Wg}[2])

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d(d+1)}(\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}+\delta_{il}\delta_{jk})\;,

(33)

which gives

\displaystyle\overline{\ket{\phi}\!\!\bra{\phi}\mathcal{L}[\ket{\phi}\!\!\bra{\phi}]}=\frac{1}{d(d+1)}\sum_{jk}

\displaystyle\Large(\ket{j}\!\!\bra{j}\mathcal{L}[\ket{k}\!\!\bra{k}]

\displaystyle+\ket{j}\!\!\bra{k}\mathcal{L}[\ket{k}\!\!\bra{j}]\Large)\;.

(34)

The first term is Hermitian and drops out of the imaginary part, so that

\displaystyle K=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d}\sum_{j,k=1}^{d}\text{Im}\Big(\ket{j}\bra{k}\mathcal{L}[\ket{k}\!\!\bra{j}]\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2id}\sum_{j,k=1}^{d}\big[\ket{j}\!\!\bra{k},\mathcal{L}[\ket{k}\!\!\bra{j}]\big]\;.

(35)

Since the choice of the basis
\ket{i}
is arbitrary, one can perform any unitary transformation of the basis
\{\ket{j}\!\!\bra{k}\}_{j,k}
onto any basis of operators
F_{\alpha}
that is orthonormal with respect to the Hilbert-Schmidt product of operators, see e.g.
Gorini et al. 1976
, to find (
5
).

Basis choice: generators of
\mathfrak{su}(d)

Let us choose the orthonormal basis
F_{0}=\mathbb{1}/\sqrt{d}
and
F_{i}=\sigma_{i}/\sqrt{d}
for
i\neq 0
with
\sigma_{i}
generators of
\mathfrak{su}(d)
, with algebraic relations

\sigma_{j}\sigma_{k}=\delta_{jk}+\sum_{l=1}^{d^{2}-1}(if_{jkl}+d_{jkl})\sigma_{l}

(36)

where
f_{jkl}
are the completely antisymmetric structure constants of
\mathfrak{su}(d)
, and
d_{jkl}
completely symmetric.
Then the contribution from
F_{0}
drops out and the effective Hamiltonian is given in terms of the generators only:

K=\frac{1}{2id}\sum_{j=1}^{d^{2}-1}\left[\sigma_{j},\mathcal{L}[\sigma_{j}]\right]\;.

(37)

This expression might be particularly useful for systems with low-dimensional Hilbert spaces. If we also write the generator in terms of this basis,
\mathcal{L}=\sum_{\alpha\beta}l_{\alpha\beta}F_{\alpha}\cdot F_{\beta}
we get simply

K=\frac{1}{2id}\sum_{j}\left(l_{0j}-l_{j0}\right)\sigma_{j}\;.

(38)

Contributions to
K
in spin systems

Pure decoherence

Suppose that
A=\sigma_{z}
, so that
A(t)\equiv A

\forall t
, namely a situation of pure decoherence of the spin. Then we have
{\mathbb{A}}\!\left(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k}\!,\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k}\right)=\langle\sigma_{z}^{n-k}\rangle_{1/d}\sigma_{z}^{k}
, which is easily found depending on the parity of
n
and
k
:

{\mathbb{A}}\!\left(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k}\!,\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k}\right)=\begin{cases}\sigma_{z}&\text{($n$ odd, $k$ odd)}\\
\mathbb{1}&\text{($n$ even, $k$ even)}\\
0&\text{elsewhere}\end{cases}\;.

(39)

This means that there are no even order terms in the effective Hamiltonian – as the final Hamiltonian is always traceless and any term proportional to the identity will vanish – and that any odd order term will give a contribution proportional to
\sigma_{z}
.
Thus, we conclude that for a spin under pure decoherence, the effective Hamiltonian is in general of the form
K(t)=\frac{\omega(t)}{2}\sigma_{z}
.

Unbiased spin system

Suppose now that the interaction term is orthogonal to
\sigma_{z}
. For example, take
A=\sigma_{x}
. Then
A(t)=\sigma_{+}e^{i\omega t}+\sigma_{-}e^{-i\omega t}
, and the terms
A(\bm{t}_{1}^{m})
have a different structure depending on the parity of
m
. For odd
m
:

A(\bm{t}_{1}^{m})=\sigma_{+}e^{i\phi(\bm{t}_{1}^{m})}+\sigma_{-}e^{-i\phi(\bm{t}_{1}^{m})}\;,

(40)

while for even
m
:

\displaystyle A(\bm{t}_{1}^{m})

\displaystyle=\ket{1}\bra{1}e^{i\phi(\bm{t}_{1}^{m})}+\ket{0}\bra{0}e^{-i\phi(\bm{t}_{1}^{m})}

\displaystyle={\mbox{$1\hskip-2.84526pt{\bf l}$}}\,\cos(\phi(\bm{t}_{1}^{m}))+i\sigma_{z}\,\sin(\phi(\bm{t}_{1}^{m}))\;,

(41)

where the phases are defined as

\phi(\bm{t}_{1}^{m}):=-\omega\sum_{j=1}^{m}(-1)^{j}t_{j}\;.

(42)

This leads to

{\mathbb{A}}\!\left(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k}\!,\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k}\right)=\begin{cases}{\mathbb{A}_{1}}\!\left(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k}\!,\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k}\right)&\text{($n$ odd, $k$ odd)}\\
{\mathbb{A}_{2}}\!\left(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k}\!,\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k}\right)&\text{($n$ even, $k$ even)}\\
0&\text{elsewhere}\end{cases}\;,

(43)

with

\displaystyle{\mathbb{A}_{1}}\!\left(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k}\!,\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k}\right)=

\displaystyle\cos(\phi(\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k}))\left[\sigma_{+}e^{i\phi(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k})}+\sigma_{-}e^{-i\phi(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k})}\right]

(44)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{A}_{2}}\!\left(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k}\!,\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k}\right)=

\displaystyle\cos(\phi(\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k}))\left[\mathbb{1}+i\sin(\phi(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k}))\sigma_{z}\right]

(45)

From the above it is clear that even orders give contributions on the diagonal of
K
, thus maintaining a structure proportional to
\sigma_{z}
, while odd orders give only contributions on off-diagonals of the effective Hamiltonian, thus changing its eigenbasis.
