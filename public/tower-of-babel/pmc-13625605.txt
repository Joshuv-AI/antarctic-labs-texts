Fibrosis-Driven Cardiovascular Risk in Metabolic Dysfunction-Associated Steatotic Liver Disease: Emerging Links With Atherosclerotic Cardiovascular Disease
Authors: Nakul Mahajan, Nazdeep Kaur, Samar Pal S Sandhu, Vyoma Patel, Hunardeep Kaur Boparai, Kanwarmandeep Singh, FNU Rajinderpal Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is increasingly recognized as a multisystem cardiometabolic disorder with major implications for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD). Although hepatic steatosis has traditionally been considered the hallmark of MASLD, emerging evidence suggests that hepatic fibrosis severity rather than steatosis alone appears to be an important prognostic marker of adverse cardiovascular outcomes. These observations have prompted growing interest in incorporating fibrosis assessment into cardiovascular risk stratification and integrated cardio-hepatic care. This narrative review explores the evolving concept of the "fibrotic cardio-hepatic axis", highlighting the potential mechanistic pathways linking hepatic fibrosis with vascular injury and atherogenesis. Key mechanisms include chronic systemic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, proatherogenic lipid alterations, gut-liver-vascular interactions, and prothrombotic states. The review further discusses the expanding role of noninvasive fibrosis assessment tools, including the fibrosis-4 index, enhanced liver fibrosis score, and transient elastography, as emerging cardiovascular risk stratification instruments. Recent imaging studies linking fibrosis severity with coronary artery calcium progression, carotid atherosclerosis, coronary plaque burden, and myocardial remodeling are also examined. Additionally, contemporary therapeutic approaches including glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists, sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors, and novel anti-fibrotic agents are reviewed for their potential to simultaneously improve hepatic fibrosis and reduce ASCVD burden. Finally, future directions involving fibrosis-integrated cardiovascular risk models, precision medicine, artificial intelligence, and multidisciplinary cardio-hepatology care pathways are discussed. Recognition of fibrosis as an important cardiovascular risk marker may refine risk assessment and therapeutic strategies in MASLD.

Introduction and background

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), previously termed nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), has emerged as the hepatic manifestation of systemic metabolic dysfunction and is now recognized as a major contributor to cardiovascular morbidity and mortality. The recent transition from NAFLD to MASLD reflects an evolving understanding that hepatic steatosis is closely integrated with obesity, insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, and chronic low-grade inflammation rather than being an isolated liver disorder [1]. Affecting nearly one-third of the global population, MASLD represents a substantial public health burden with implications extending far beyond progressive liver injury [2].

Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) has consistently remained the leading cause of death among patients with MASLD, surpassing liver-related complications in most longitudinal cohorts [3]. Earlier investigations primarily attributed this increased cardiovascular risk to shared metabolic comorbidities; however, accumulating evidence suggests that the severity of hepatic fibrosis itself independently predicts adverse cardiovascular outcomes [4]. This paradigm shift has redirected attention from simple steatosis toward fibrosis-associated systemic vascular injury and has introduced the concept of a "fibrotic cardio-hepatic axis", describing potential biological links between progressive hepatic fibrogenesis, inflammatory and metabolic alterations, and cardiovascular disease.

Importantly, cardiovascular risk appears heterogeneous across the histologic spectrum of MASLD. Patients with isolated steatosis often demonstrate comparatively modest cardiovascular risk, whereas those with steatohepatitis and advanced fibrosis exhibit significantly higher incidences of myocardial infarction, stroke, heart failure, and cardiovascular mortality [5]. Studies have reported that advanced fibrosis assessed using noninvasive fibrosis scores was independently associated with major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) even after adjustment for traditional cardiovascular risk factors [6]. Similarly, liver stiffness measured through transient elastography has shown strong correlations with coronary artery calcification, carotid intima-media thickness, and subclinical atherosclerosis, further supporting an association between fibrosis severity and cardiovascular pathology [7].

The mechanistic basis underlying fibrosis-associated ASCVD is multifactorial. The proposed association involves interconnected inflammatory, metabolic, endothelial, and thrombotic pathways linking hepatic fibrosis with vascular injury [8]. Moreover, recent studies have demonstrated that fibrosis severity predicts incident heart failure and atrial fibrillation independent of obesity and diabetes status, emphasizing the systemic nature of fibrotic MASLD [9,10]. These findings challenge the traditional organ-specific perception of MASLD and support its recognition as a multisystem cardiometabolic disease.

Despite growing recognition of the association between hepatic fibrosis and cardiovascular disease in MASLD, the existing evidence remains fragmented across mechanistic, epidemiological, and clinical domains. In particular, uncertainty remains regarding the extent to which fibrosis severity contributes to cardiovascular risk beyond conventional metabolic risk factors, the utility of noninvasive fibrosis measures for cardiovascular risk stratification, and how these findings may be translated into integrated cardio-hepatic management. A comprehensive synthesis of these emerging data is therefore needed to clarify the clinical significance of fibrosis within the cardiovascular spectrum of MASLD.

Accordingly, this narrative review synthesizes the emerging evidence linking hepatic fibrosis with ASCVD, with particular emphasis on the mechanistic pathways underlying the cardio-hepatic axis, the role and limitations of noninvasive fibrosis assessment, associations with subclinical atherosclerosis and clinical cardiovascular outcomes, and evolving therapeutic implications for integrated cardiovascular and hepatic risk management.

Review

Methodology

The present study was conducted as a structured narrative review to evaluate the association between MASLD, hepatic fibrosis, and ASCVD, with emphasis on proposed pathophysiological mechanisms, clinical evidence, noninvasive fibrosis assessment, and emerging therapeutic strategies. A structured literature search was performed in PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, the Cochrane Library, and Web of Science to identify relevant studies published in English from database inception through June 2026. Additional records were identified through manual screening of reference lists and citation searching to identify pertinent studies that were not captured through the database search. Search terms included combinations of "MASLD", "metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease", "hepatic fibrosis", "atherosclerosis", "atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease", "coronary artery disease", "cardiovascular risk", "FIB-4", "transient elastography", and related keywords combined using the Boolean operators AND/OR. Database-specific adaptations of these terms were used according to the indexing requirements of each database. The search was limited to English-language publications, with no restriction on publication year.

Studies were considered eligible if they evaluated MASLD or NAFLD in relation to hepatic fibrosis, cardiovascular outcomes or subclinical cardiovascular disease, potential mechanistic links between hepatic fibrosis and cardiovascular disease, noninvasive assessment of hepatic fibrosis with potential implications for cardiovascular risk, or therapeutic strategies with relevant hepatic and/or cardiovascular effects. Randomized controlled trials, prospective and retrospective cohort studies, case-control studies, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and major clinical practice guidelines were considered. Studies were excluded if they were unrelated to the review objectives, lacked clinically relevant hepatic or cardiovascular outcomes, consisted solely of experimental findings without sufficient relevance to the clinical context, or were conference abstracts, editorials, letters, or duplicate publications without substantive additional information.

The literature identification and selection process was documented using the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 framework for transparency (Figure 1) [11]. However, the present study was not conducted as a systematic review or meta-analysis. A total of 1248 records were identified, including 1206 from electronic databases and 42 from other sources. After the removal of duplicates (n=287), records excluded by automation tools (n=13), and records removed for other reasons (n=12), 894 records underwent title and abstract screening, of which 742 were excluded. Full texts of 194 reports were assessed for eligibility (152 from the databases and 42 from websites and citation searching), and 106 were excluded because of insufficient data, lack of direct relevance to the review objectives, or inadequate hepatic or cardiovascular outcome information. Ultimately, 88 studies were included in the qualitative synthesis. A summary of the characteristics and principal findings of all 88 studies included in the qualitative synthesis is provided in the Appendices.

Because this was a structured narrative review rather than a systematic review or meta-analysis, no formal study-level risk-of-bias assessment or quantitative data pooling was undertaken. Instead, the identified literature was critically synthesized according to study design, study population, fibrosis assessment method, cardiovascular outcomes, and relevance to the objectives of the review. Particular emphasis was placed on contemporary and clinically relevant evidence, while findings from observational studies were interpreted as associations and not as definitive evidence of causality. Mechanistic and experimental evidence was considered primarily to provide biological context for the observed clinical associations. The synthesis therefore focused on the emerging evidence linking hepatic fibrosis with cardiovascular risk while acknowledging the limitations of the predominantly observational clinical evidence.

Mechanistic pathways linking hepatic fibrosis to atherosclerosis

Chronic Systemic Inflammation in Fibrotic MASLD

Progressive hepatic fibrosis in MASLD is characterized by persistent low-grade systemic inflammation that may contribute to vascular injury and atherogenic processes. Fibrotic liver tissue demonstrates enhanced activation of Kupffer cells, hepatic stellate cells, and inflammasome signaling pathways, resulting in increased circulating levels of proinflammatory mediators including interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP) [12]. These inflammatory cytokines promote endothelial activation, monocyte recruitment, and smooth muscle proliferation within the vascular wall, which may collectively contribute to vascular injury and plaque instability [13].

The NOD-, LRR-, and pyrin domain-containing protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome has emerged as a critical molecular link between hepatic fibrosis and ASCVD. Activation of inflammasome pathways promotes the secretion of IL-1β and IL-18, amplifying systemic vascular inflammation and endothelial injury [14]. Experimental studies have shown that fibrotic MASLD is associated with heightened inflammasome activity in both hepatic and vascular tissues, suggesting shared inflammatory signaling between the liver and arterial wall. Additionally, chronic inflammation associated with fibrosis contributes to macrophage polarization toward proatherogenic phenotypes, facilitating plaque progression and necrotic core formation [15].

Endothelial Dysfunction and Oxidative Stress

Endothelial dysfunction represents an early and pivotal step in fibrosis-associated atherosclerosis. Advanced hepatic fibrosis is associated with impaired nitric oxide bioavailability, increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, and vascular stiffness, all of which contribute to accelerated arterial aging [8]. Hepatic oxidative stress promotes systemic lipid peroxidation and mitochondrial dysfunction, resulting in endothelial injury and impaired vasodilation. A cross-sectional study of 7507 adults demonstrated that increasing MASLD severity was independently associated with greater coronary artery calcification, indicating a progressive relationship between disease severity and subclinical atherosclerosis beyond traditional cardiovascular risk factors [16].

Fibrotic MASLD is also associated with increased circulating asymmetric dimethylarginine (ADMA), an endogenous nitric oxide synthase inhibitor linked to impaired endothelial function and adverse cardiovascular outcomes [17]. Furthermore, ROS-mediated oxidation of low-density lipoproteins enhances foam cell formation and plaque vulnerability. Experimental models have demonstrated that transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β), a key mediator of hepatic fibrogenesis, simultaneously promotes vascular fibrosis and arterial stiffness, reinforcing the concept of a shared fibrotic vascular phenotype [18].

Proatherogenic Lipoprotein and Metabolic Alterations

Advanced fibrosis in MASLD is associated with profound alterations in lipid metabolism that favor atherogenesis. Patients with fibrotic MASLD frequently exhibit elevated apolipoprotein B (ApoB), small dense low-density lipoprotein (sdLDL), remnant cholesterol, and severe insulin resistance [19]. These lipoprotein abnormalities are highly atherogenic due to their increased susceptibility to oxidation and enhanced arterial wall penetration. In a cross-sectional analysis of 2550 individuals with biopsy-confirmed MASLD, advanced fibrosis was independently associated with significantly higher ApoB levels and a 38% increase in coronary plaque burden on coronary computed tomography angiography [20].

Insulin resistance further amplifies cardiovascular risk through increased hepatic very-low-density lipoprotein secretion, impaired glucose utilization, and endothelial dysfunction. Importantly, recent metabolomic studies suggest that fibrosis severity correlates more strongly with proatherogenic lipid signatures than hepatic steatosis alone, supporting the concept that fibrosis represents a metabolically malignant phenotype within MASLD [21].

Gut-Liver-Vascular Axis

The gut-liver-vascular axis has recently gained significant attention as a mechanistic driver of fibrosis-associated ASCVD. Progressive fibrosis is associated with gut microbial dysbiosis, increased intestinal permeability, and systemic endotoxemia [22]. Translocation of bacterial endotoxins such as lipopolysaccharide activates toll-like receptor signaling, promoting hepatic inflammation and vascular endothelial injury. In a recent metagenomic study, patients with advanced MASLD fibrosis demonstrated significantly higher circulating endotoxin levels and increased prevalence of proatherogenic microbial species compared with individuals with isolated steatosis [23].

Trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO), a gut-derived metabolite implicated in atherosclerosis, has also been linked with fibrosis severity. Elevated TMAO concentrations promote macrophage foam cell formation, platelet hyperreactivity, and endothelial dysfunction [24]. A comprehensive review highlighted that elevated TMAO is strongly associated with cardiovascular disease and is influenced by diet, gut microbial composition, hepatic flavin-containing monooxygenase 3 (FMO3) activity, and renal function [25]. These findings suggest that gut microbial alterations may represent a shared pathogenic pathway linking hepatic fibrosis with systemic vascular disease.

Prothrombotic and Plaque-Destabilizing State

Fibrotic MASLD is increasingly recognized as a prothrombotic condition characterized by platelet activation, coagulation imbalance, and vascular inflammation [26]. Advanced fibrosis is associated with elevated fibrinogen, factor VIII, plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (PAI-1), and von Willebrand factor levels, all of which contribute to thrombogenesis and plaque instability [27]. Moreover, activated platelets release inflammatory mediators and microparticles that further exacerbate endothelial dysfunction and atherosclerotic progression.

In a longitudinal cohort study, patients with advanced fibrosis exhibited a significantly higher incidence of acute coronary syndromes and ischemic stroke compared with those without fibrosis, even after adjustment for metabolic risk factors [28]. Histopathologic analyses additionally demonstrate increased intraplaque inflammation and vascular macrophage infiltration among patients with fibrotic MASLD, supporting the hypothesis that hepatic fibrosis may be linked to processes involved in plaque formation, plaque destabilization, and thrombotic cardiovascular events [29]. The interconnected inflammatory, metabolic, endothelial, microbial, and thrombotic pathways linking hepatic fibrosis with vascular injury collectively constitute the proposed fibrotic cardio-hepatic axis in MASLD (Figure 2).

Noninvasive fibrosis assessment and detection of subclinical ASCVD

Fibrosis Scores as Cardiovascular Risk Predictors

The growing recognition of hepatic fibrosis as an important prognostic marker of cardiovascular risk in MASLD has intensified interest in noninvasive fibrosis assessment tools as predictors of ASCVD. Among these, the fibrosis-4 (FIB-4) index has emerged as one of the most extensively validated markers linking hepatic fibrosis with MACE, cardiovascular mortality, and subclinical coronary disease [30]. A retrospective cohort study of 19,395 patients with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) demonstrated that a high baseline FIB-4 score was independently associated with increased risks of major adverse liver outcomes (aHR: 4.29; 95% CI: 3.87-4.77) and MACE (aHR: 1.44; 95% CI: 1.25-1.66), while progression to a high-risk FIB-4 category further increased the risk of adverse liver outcomes (aHR: 2.62; 95% CI: 1.92-3.58) [31].

Similarly, the NAFLD fibrosis score (NFS) has shown strong associations with coronary artery disease (CAD) and adverse cardiovascular outcomes independent of traditional cardiometabolic risk factors [32]. In a prospective Korean cohort study, elevated NFS independently predicted incident ischemic stroke and coronary artery calcification progression, even after adjustment for diabetes, obesity, and hypertension [33]. The enhanced liver fibrosis (ELF) score has also gained attention due to its ability to directly reflect extracellular matrix turnover and active fibrogenesis. In a recent European study involving biopsy-confirmed MASLD, higher ELF scores were associated with significantly increased cardiovascular mortality and heart failure hospitalization rates, suggesting that active fibrotic remodeling may parallel systemic vascular injury [34].

Transient elastography, which quantifies liver stiffness noninvasively, has further strengthened the clinical connection between fibrosis and ASCVD. Elevated liver stiffness measurements have consistently correlated with CAD severity, vascular stiffness, and incident cardiovascular events [35]. It has been reported that patients with liver stiffness measurement ≥12 kPa demonstrated nearly a threefold increase in coronary plaque burden and significantly higher coronary artery calcium (CAC) scores compared with patients without advanced fibrosis [36]. These findings collectively suggest that fibrosis biomarkers may serve as clinically useful cardiovascular risk stratification tools in MASLD populations. More recent longitudinal evidence has further strengthened the prognostic utility of fibrosis biomarkers in MASLD. In a large nationwide cohort involving 134,686 individuals with MASLD, Lee and Lee demonstrated that higher FIB-4 scores were independently associated with a 48.9% increase in all-cause mortality risk and a 47.8% increase in cardiovascular mortality risk [37]. Notably, the association was more pronounced among younger individuals, suggesting that fibrosis-related cardiovascular risk may emerge earlier than traditionally appreciated. Similarly, dynamic changes in fibrosis biomarkers appear clinically relevant. A recent longitudinal study reported that persistently elevated or rising FIB-4 trajectories were independently associated with progressive CAC accumulation, particularly among individuals older than 40 years with MASLD [38]. These findings support the concept that serial fibrosis assessment may provide incremental value beyond static single-time-point measurements for cardiovascular risk prediction.

Imaging Evidence of Subclinical Atherosclerosis

Advanced fibrosis in MASLD is increasingly associated with imaging markers of subclinical atherosclerosis, supporting the concept that vascular injury begins before overt cardiovascular events become clinically apparent. Recent evidence additionally suggests that changes in liver fibrosis measures may be associated with changes in subclinical atherosclerosis burden, further supporting an association between fibrosis severity and vascular disease progression [20]. CAC scoring has emerged as a particularly valuable imaging modality in this context. Multiple studies have demonstrated that increasing fibrosis severity is independently associated with higher CAC progression and greater plaque burden [39]. In a cohort of 1173 asymptomatic adults, CAC progression occurred in 20.5% of participants without NAFLD, 27.5% with NAFLD and low FIB-4, and 35.9% with NAFLD and intermediate/high FIB-4, with the latter group showing greater adjusted odds of progression than participants without NAFLD (OR: 1.70; 95% CI: 1.12-2.58) [40].

Carotid intima-media thickness, a well-established marker of early atherosclerosis, also correlates strongly with fibrosis severity. Patients with advanced fibrosis exhibit significantly higher carotid intima-media thickness values and increased carotid plaque prevalence compared with those with isolated steatosis [41]. In a meta-analysis comprising over 12,000 MASLD patients, fibrosis severity demonstrated a stepwise association with subclinical carotid atherosclerosis independent of conventional cardiovascular risk factors [42].

Coronary computed tomography angiography studies have further revealed that fibrotic MASLD is associated with high-risk plaque phenotypes characterized by positive remodeling, low-attenuation plaques, and increased noncalcified plaque burden [43]. Additionally, epicardial adipose tissue, an inflammatory visceral fat depot closely linked with coronary atherosclerosis, is significantly increased in advanced fibrosis and correlates with both liver stiffness and vascular inflammation [44]. These imaging findings support an association between hepatic fibrosis and a systemic proatherogenic phenotype.

Fibrosis Severity and Cardiovascular Phenotypes

Emerging evidence suggests that fibrosis severity influences not only generalized ASCVD risk but also distinct cardiovascular phenotypes including CAD, heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), atrial fibrillation, and vascular stiffness. In a nationwide cohort study involving more than 10,000 patients with MASLD, advanced fibrosis independently predicted incident CAD with adjusted HRs exceeding 2.0 after controlling for metabolic risk factors [45]. Furthermore, fibrosis severity correlated directly with coronary plaque complexity and multivessel disease burden.

Additional evidence supporting the cardiovascular burden of MASLD comes from a multicenter propensity-matched longitudinal analysis of 48,552 patients derived from the TriNetX electronic health record network [46]. Compared with matched controls, patients with MASLD experienced a significantly higher five-year incidence of MACE, including myocardial infarction, stroke, and cardiovascular death. Notably, cardiovascular event curves diverged within 24 months, while statin therapy was associated with a significant reduction in cardiovascular risk, underscoring the importance of early cardiovascular risk assessment and preventive management in patients with MASLD.

HFpEF has also emerged as an important cardiovascular manifestation of fibrotic MASLD. Chronic systemic inflammation, myocardial fibrosis, endothelial dysfunction, and ventricular stiffness associated with hepatic fibrosis may collectively promote diastolic dysfunction [47]. In a recent cardiac magnetic resonance imaging study, patients with advanced MASLD fibrosis demonstrated significantly higher myocardial extracellular volume fractions and impaired diastolic strain parameters compared with controls, suggesting diffuse myocardial remodeling [48].

Similarly, fibrosis-associated atrial remodeling may increase susceptibility to atrial fibrillation. A large population-based analysis reported that patients with elevated FIB-4 scores had a 1.8-fold increased risk of incident atrial fibrillation independent of obesity and diabetes [49]. Vascular stiffness, another hallmark of fibrotic MASLD, has consistently correlated with liver stiffness measurements and endothelial dysfunction biomarkers [50]. Collectively, these observations reinforce the concept that hepatic fibrosis represents a systemic fibro-inflammatory state capable of influencing multiple cardiovascular phenotypes beyond traditional atherosclerosis alone. Beyond coronary and cardiac phenotypes, recent studies have also linked fibrosis severity with peripheral vascular disease manifestations. In a 2025 cross-sectional study involving 481 patients with MASLD, higher FIB-4 categories were independently associated with lower extremity arterial disease after adjustment for metabolic and lipid-related variables [51]. Additionally, fibrosis-integrated inflammatory markers such as systemic inflammation response index and neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio have demonstrated improved prediction of cardiovascular mortality when combined with FIB-4, highlighting the growing potential for multidimensional fibrosis-based cardiovascular risk models [52]. Collectively, these studies demonstrate that hepatic fibrosis severity consistently predicts subclinical atherosclerosis, adverse cardiovascular phenotypes, and major cardiovascular events across diverse MASLD populations (Table 1).

Therapeutic implications: can treating fibrosis reduce ASCVD risk?

Glucagon-Like Peptide-1 Receptor Agonists (GLP-1RAs)

GLP-1RAs have emerged as promising therapeutic agents capable of simultaneously targeting hepatic fibrosis and ASCVD risk in MASLD. Beyond their glucose-lowering effects, GLP-1RAs exert anti-inflammatory, antiatherogenic, and anti-fibrotic actions that may interrupt the fibrotic cardio-hepatic axis [53]. Semaglutide has demonstrated significant histologic benefits in patients with MASH. In a phase 2 trial involving 320 patients with biopsy-confirmed MASH, semaglutide achieved MASH resolution without worsening fibrosis in 59% of patients compared with 17% in the placebo group after 72 weeks [54]. Although fibrosis improvement was modest, semaglutide significantly reduced inflammatory activity and cardiometabolic risk factors associated with vascular injury.

More recently, the ESSENCE phase 3 trial demonstrated that semaglutide resulted in clinically meaningful reductions in liver stiffness, body weight, hsCRP, and atherogenic lipid parameters, suggesting potential cardiovascular benefit through the attenuation of systemic inflammation and endothelial dysfunction [55]. Tirzepatide, a dual glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP)/GLP-1RA, has shown even greater metabolic and hepatic efficacy. In the SYNERGY-NASH trial, tirzepatide achieved MASH resolution rates approaching 74% with fibrosis improvement in up to 51% of participants after 52 weeks [56]. Whether fibrosis improvement with tirzepatide translates into long-term ASCVD risk reduction remains to be established. Importantly, GLP-1RAs have already demonstrated robust cardiovascular protection in large cardiovascular outcome trials, including reductions in myocardial infarction, stroke, and cardiovascular mortality [57].

Sodium-Glucose Cotransporter-2 (SGLT2) Inhibitors

SGLT2 inhibitors have also gained substantial attention for their pleiotropic hepatic and cardiovascular benefits in MASLD. Experimental and clinical studies indicate that SGLT2 inhibitors reduce hepatic steatosis, improve insulin resistance, attenuate oxidative stress, and suppress inflammatory signaling pathways implicated in fibrogenesis [58]. In a multicenter prospective study, empagliflozin therapy for 48 weeks significantly reduced liver stiffness measurements and improved fibrosis biomarkers alongside reductions in hsCRP and vascular stiffness indices [59].

Cardiovascular benefits associated with SGLT2 inhibitors appear particularly relevant in fibrotic MASLD due to their favorable effects on endothelial function, myocardial energetics, and systemic inflammation. In patients with MASLD and type 2 diabetes mellitus, dapagliflozin therapy was associated with reductions in epicardial adipose tissue volume and improvements in coronary flow reserve, supporting a direct antiatherogenic effect [60]. Additionally, SGLT2 inhibitors consistently reduce hospitalization for heart failure and cardiovascular mortality across diverse cardiometabolic populations [61]. Given the strong association between hepatic fibrosis and HFpEF, these agents may provide dual cardio-hepatic protection by simultaneously mitigating myocardial and hepatic fibrosis.

Emerging Anti-fibrotic Therapies

Recent advances in anti-fibrotic pharmacotherapy have introduced novel agents specifically targeting pathways involved in hepatic fibrogenesis and systemic metabolic dysfunction. Resmetirom, a selective thyroid hormone receptor-β agonist, became the first therapy approved for MASH with fibrosis after demonstrating significant reductions in liver fibrosis and inflammatory activity [62]. In the MAESTRO-NASH trial, fibrosis improvement by at least one stage without worsening steatohepatitis occurred in 24-26% of patients receiving resmetirom compared with 14% in the placebo group [63]. Importantly, resmetirom also reduced low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, ApoB, and triglyceride levels, suggesting potential ASCVD risk reduction beyond hepatic benefits.

Lanifibranor, a pan-peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor agonist, has shown promising effects on fibrosis regression and vascular inflammation. In the NATIVE trial, lanifibranor achieved significant fibrosis improvement and reductions in inflammatory biomarkers linked with endothelial dysfunction [64]. Similarly, survodutide, a dual glucagon/GLP-1RA, demonstrated marked reductions in liver fat content, body weight, and fibrosis biomarkers in phase 2 studies [65].

Emerging incretin-based therapies such as pemvidutide and retatrutide may further redefine treatment paradigms in fibrotic MASLD. Pemvidutide has shown substantial reductions in hepatic fat fraction and cardiometabolic risk markers including triglycerides and hsCRP [66]. Retatrutide, a triple agonist targeting GLP-1, GIP, and glucagon receptors, has demonstrated profound weight reduction exceeding 24% alongside improvements in insulin resistance and inflammatory biomarkers associated with vascular dysfunction [67]. Although long-term cardiovascular outcome data remain limited, these agents may ultimately offer integrated treatment strategies capable of simultaneously attenuating hepatic fibrosis and ASCVD burden.

Lifestyle Interventions and Fibrosis Regression

Lifestyle modification remains the cornerstone of MASLD management and continues to provide the strongest evidence for simultaneous hepatic and cardiovascular benefit. Sustained weight reduction is closely associated with fibrosis regression and improved vascular outcomes. A prospective study involving serial liver biopsies demonstrated that weight loss ≥10% resulted in fibrosis regression in nearly 45% of patients and complete resolution of steatohepatitis in a substantial proportion [68]. Even moderate weight reduction of 7-10% has been associated with improvements in endothelial function, arterial stiffness, and inflammatory biomarkers linked to ASCVD [69].

The Mediterranean diet has consistently demonstrated favorable effects on both hepatic fibrosis and cardiovascular health. Rich in monounsaturated fats, polyphenols, and anti-inflammatory nutrients, the Mediterranean dietary pattern reduces oxidative stress and improves insulin sensitivity [70]. In randomized clinical trials, adherence to a Mediterranean diet significantly reduced liver stiffness measurements and carotid intima-media thickness while improving lipid profiles and endothelial function [71].

Exercise independently contributes to fibrosis regression and vascular protection through improvements in mitochondrial function, nitric oxide bioavailability, and systemic inflammation. Aerobic and resistance exercise interventions have demonstrated reductions in hepatic fat content, vascular stiffness, and epicardial adipose tissue even in the absence of significant weight loss [72]. Future directions in fibrosis-based cardiovascular risk stratification

Should Fibrosis Scores Be Incorporated Into ASCVD Risk Models?

Conventional cardiovascular risk prediction tools, including the Framingham Risk Score and Systematic Coronary Risk Evaluation 2 (SCORE2), were developed primarily around established cardiovascular risk factors and do not specifically account for hepatic fibrosis or systemic fibro-inflammatory burden [73]. Consequently, they may not fully capture cardiovascular risk in patients with MASLD, particularly among those with advanced fibrosis. Emerging cohort data suggest that fibrosis-based markers may provide incremental prognostic information beyond conventional risk models [74]. Although much of this evidence is not specific to MASLD populations, it provides support for the broader prognostic relevance of fibrosis markers in cardiovascular disease. In a prospective cohort of 2726 participants with heart failure followed for nine years in the MyoVasc study, the FIB-4 index independently predicted all-cause mortality (HR: 1.341; 95% CI: 1.273-1.412; p<0.0001) and was associated with adverse cardiac functional and structural changes; notably, these associations were strongest in patients without MASLD [75].

Similarly, liver stiffness measured by transient elastography independently predicted cardiovascular mortality after adjustment for diabetes, hypertension, obesity, and dyslipidemia [76]. Although these findings do not establish that fibrosis scores improve ASCVD risk prediction specifically in MASLD, they suggest that hepatic fibrosis may represent a clinically relevant cardiovascular risk-enhancing characteristic beyond conventional risk factors. Further studies in well-characterized MASLD populations are therefore needed to determine whether incorporating fibrosis-based measures into established ASCVD risk models improves risk discrimination, calibration, and clinical decision-making.

Importantly, fibrosis severity may provide information that is not captured by hepatic steatosis alone. Emerging evidence suggests that cardiovascular risk may remain substantial in patients with relatively modest hepatic fat accumulation when fibrosis burden is high [77]. Nevertheless, whether fibrosis measures should be formally incorporated into established ASCVD risk calculators remains uncertain. Future studies should determine whether adding FIB-4, liver stiffness, or other validated fibrosis measures improves risk reclassification, clinical decision-making, and ultimately patient outcomes.

Multi-omics and Precision Medicine

The increasing availability of genomic, metabolomic, proteomic, and computational approaches may further refine fibrosis-based cardiovascular risk assessment. Genetic variants such as patatin-like phospholipase domain-containing protein 3 (PNPLA3) and transmembrane 6 superfamily member 2 (TM6SF2) are associated with hepatic lipid accumulation and fibrosis progression, while emerging evidence suggests that genetic determinants of liver disease may also intersect with systemic inflammatory and vascular pathways [78,79].

Metabolomic studies have similarly identified lipidomic and inflammatory signatures associated with both advanced fibrosis and coronary plaque burden. Associations involving branched-chain amino acids, ceramides, and oxidized phospholipids suggest that metabolic remodeling accompanying fibrosis may provide additional information regarding cardiovascular phenotype and disease burden [80]. These findings raise the possibility that future risk models could integrate clinical variables with fibrosis biomarkers, imaging, and molecular signatures rather than relying on a single fibrosis measure.

Artificial intelligence (AI) and machine-learning approaches may facilitate such multidimensional risk assessment. Models incorporating fibrosis biomarkers, imaging characteristics, genomic information, and conventional cardiovascular variables have demonstrated improved predictive performance compared with traditional risk calculators [81]. A retrospective cohort study of 2000 patients found that AI-based models, particularly deep neural networks, outperformed the Framingham Risk Score and ASCVD estimator for five-year cardiovascular event prediction, achieving an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.91 versus 0.76 and 0.74, respectively [82]. Although these findings are promising, external validation, calibration across diverse populations, and prospective assessment of clinical utility are required before such models can be incorporated into routine practice.

Cardio-Hepatology Clinics and Integrated Care

The overlapping metabolic, hepatic, and cardiovascular manifestations of MASLD provide a rationale for integrated models of care. Conventional organ-specific approaches may inadequately address the interaction between hepatic fibrosis, metabolic dysfunction, and cardiovascular disease, particularly in patients with advanced fibrosis and multiple cardiometabolic risk factors [83]. Multidisciplinary cardio-hepatology pathways involving hepatologists, cardiologists, endocrinologists, dietitians, and preventive-care specialists could facilitate coordinated assessment and management.

Integrated screening approaches combining fibrosis assessment with cardiovascular risk evaluation and metabolic profiling may improve the recognition of subclinical cardiovascular disease [84]. In a pilot multidisciplinary clinic study, combining liver stiffness assessment with cardiovascular imaging increased the detection of previously unrecognized high-risk cardiovascular phenotypes among patients with advanced fibrosis [85]. Such models may also facilitate coordinated use of therapies with complementary hepatic and cardiovascular effects, including GLP-1RAs and SGLT2 inhibitors.

Beyond diagnostic assessment, integrated care may improve the implementation of lifestyle interventions and cardiovascular prevention through coordinated patient education and longitudinal follow-up [86]. However, the clinical and cost-effectiveness of dedicated cardio-hepatology pathways remain to be established. Future studies should therefore evaluate whether integrated care models improve cardiovascular outcomes, treatment adherence, and healthcare utilization compared with conventional care.

Key Knowledge Gaps

Despite substantial progress in defining the relationship between hepatic fibrosis and cardiovascular disease, several important uncertainties remain. First, the extent to which fibrosis directly contributes to vascular disease versus serving as a marker of underlying systemic metabolic dysfunction has not been definitively established [87]. Although mechanistic evidence supports biological pathways linking hepatic fibrogenesis with vascular injury, much of the clinical evidence remains observational, limiting causal inference.

Second, fibrosis-focused randomized controlled trials evaluating hard cardiovascular outcomes remain limited. Most therapeutic studies have primarily assessed histologic improvement, liver stiffness, or metabolic surrogate outcomes rather than incident myocardial infarction, stroke, MACE, or cardiovascular mortality [88]. Consequently, improvement in hepatic fibrosis should not yet be assumed to translate directly into cardiovascular benefit.

Third, long-term cardiovascular outcome data for several emerging therapies remain incomplete. Although agents such as resmetirom, tirzepatide, and survodutide have demonstrated improvements in hepatic and cardiometabolic parameters, their effects on coronary plaque progression, vascular inflammation, and cardiovascular mortality require longer-term evaluation [89]. Similarly, clinically meaningful thresholds for fibrosis-based cardiovascular risk stratification and the optimal frequency of reassessment remain uncertain.

Future research should therefore prioritize prospective cardiovascular outcome studies, external validation of fibrosis-integrated ASCVD prediction models, standardized fibrosis thresholds for cardiovascular risk assessment, and mechanistic studies addressing the bidirectional relationship between hepatic fibrogenesis and vascular disease. Studies should also determine whether fibrosis-guided screening and treatment strategies improve patient-centered outcomes compared with conventional cardiometabolic risk assessment. Ultimately, an integrated approach combining noninvasive fibrosis assessment, selective evaluation of subclinical ASCVD, and coordinated multidisciplinary management may offer a practical framework for identifying and treating patients with MASLD who have the greatest cardiovascular risk (Figure 3).

Conclusions

Emerging evidence increasingly supports hepatic fibrosis as an important marker of cardiovascular risk in MASLD, extending the clinical significance of the disease beyond hepatic injury alone. Greater fibrosis severity is associated with systemic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, subclinical atherosclerosis, and adverse cardiovascular phenotypes including CAD, heart failure, and atrial fibrillation. These observations support the concept of a fibrotic cardio-hepatic axis while acknowledging that the extent to which fibrosis itself contributes causally to cardiovascular disease remains incompletely established.

Noninvasive fibrosis assessment tools such as FIB-4, ELF score, and transient elastography may therefore have future utility in cardiovascular risk stratification among patients with MASLD. Emerging metabolic and anti-fibrotic therapies may simultaneously improve hepatic and cardiometabolic outcomes, although whether fibrosis improvement translates into fewer ASCVD events remains uncertain. Prospective fibrosis-focused cardiovascular outcome studies are needed to establish the clinical utility of fibrosis-based risk stratification and integrated cardio-hepatic management.
