Factors influencing quality of life in patients with pulmonary tuberculosis: a protocol for a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Jingsong Yuan, Yuanling Wu, Yi Lin, Guofang Deng, Jingfang Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPulmonary tuberculosis (PTB) continues to pose a significant global public health challenge and is associated with substantial physical, psychological and social burdens. Beyond traditional clinical outcomes, quality of life (QoL) has received increasing attention in PTB research and care because it reflects patients’ physical, psychological and social well-being. Although several primary studies have explored factors associated with QoL among patients with PTB, the existing evidence is fragmented, with considerable variation in study populations, disease characteristics and assessment instruments. To date, no comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis has synthesised the determinants of QoL among patients with PTB. This study aims to systematically identify, integrate and quantitatively summarise factors associated with QoL among patients with PTB, thereby providing an evidence base to inform targeted nursing interventions and patient management strategies.Methods and analysisThis protocol was guided by the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Protocols statement to ensure rigorous reporting. A systematic and comprehensive literature search will be conducted in eight electronic databases, comprising five English databases (PubMed, Web of Science, The Cochrane Library, Embase and Scopus) and three Chinese databases (China National Knowledge Infrastructure, Chinese Scientific Journals Database and Wanfang Database), from inception to 30 November 2025. Observational studies examining associations between potential influencing factors and QoL or health-related quality of life (HRQoL) among adult patients with PTB will be eligible for inclusion. Two reviewers will independently screen studies, extract data and assess methodological quality, with disagreements resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer. Meta-analysis will be performed only when studies are sufficiently comparable in terms of population, influencing factors, QoL or HRQoL measurement and effect estimates; otherwise, findings will be summarised narratively. Where meta-analysis is appropriate, quantitative synthesis will be performed using Review Manager software and subgroup and sensitivity analyses will be conducted where data permit.Ethics and disseminationSince this study involves the synthesis and secondary analysis of previously published data, formal ethical approval is not required. The results of this study will be disseminated through publication in a peer-reviewed journal and presentation at relevant academic conferences. They will provide a robust evidence base for clinical nursing practice and guide the development of future research directions.PROSPERO registration numberCRD420251196862.

Introduction

Pulmonary tuberculosis (PTB) is a common chronic respiratory infectious disease. According to the Global Tuberculosis Report 2025, an estimated 10.7 million new tuberculosis (TB) cases occurred worldwide in 2024, corresponding to an incidence rate of 131 per 100 000 population, and approximately 1.25 million deaths were attributed to the disease.1 Epidemiological evidence indicates that PTB is the most common form of the disease, accounting for approximately 85% of all reported TB cases worldwide.2 The burden of TB remains particularly heavy in low- and middle-income countries, posing significant challenges to healthcare systems. In TB research and clinical practice, researchers have primarily focused on treatment efficacy, drug resistance and mortality rates, while patients’ quality of life (QoL) has received relatively less attention.3 Given the prolonged disease course, lengthy treatment duration and frequent adverse drug reactions, PTB not only causes significant physical impairment but also has persistent negative effects on psychological well-being and social functioning, while increasing patients’ economic burden. The combined impact of these factors ultimately leads to a marked deterioration in patients’ QoL.4–8

QoL is defined by the WHO as an individual’s subjective perception of their status, situated within the context of the culture and value systems in which they live, and in relation to their goals, expectations, standards and concerns.9 China’s National Tuberculosis Control Plan (2024–2030) further emphasises strengthening the health management and treatment of patients with PTB, with particular attention to improving psychological well-being and QoL.10 Previous review evidence also suggests that greater attention should be paid to QoL and its associated factors among patients with TB.11

This concern is clinically relevant because impaired QoL may contribute to delayed healthcare seeking, poor adherence to anti-TB treatment and even treatment failure.12 13 However, factors associated with QoL are multidimensional, spanning sociodemographic characteristics (eg, age, sex, income and educational attainment), disease-related factors (eg, disease duration, drug resistance and comorbidities), psychological factors (eg, depression, anxiety and stigma), as well as social support and family environment.14–17 Substantial heterogeneity exists across studies, partly due to differences in study design, sample size, patient populations, types of TB and QoL instruments and geographical settings. For example, some studies report better QoL among married patients, potentially because they receive greater family support.15 In contrast, other studies suggest that family responsibilities and caregiving burden may reduce QoL, particularly among those with heavy childcare or eldercare demands.16 Similarly, lower educational attainment has been linked to poorer QoL, possibly because limited access to health information may impair TB-related self-management, including understanding treatment requirements, adhering to medication, monitoring symptoms and seeking timely healthcare.18 Conversely, higher educational attainment has also been associated with worse QoL, which may reflect greater sensitivity to health risks and social evaluation and, consequently, a heavier psychological burden.19 Moreover, QoL assessment tools vary across studies. Some studies have used the World Health Organization Quality of Life-BREF (WHOQOL-BREF) to evaluate QoL across physical, psychological, social relationships and environmental domains.18 19 Other studies have used generic instruments such as the Short Form-36 Health Survey (SF-36) or EuroQol five-dimension questionnaire (EQ-5D), which emphasise different aspects of health status and may therefore limit comparability across findings.20–22

Although multiple primary studies have explored factors associated with QoL among patients with PTB, the existing evidence remains largely fragmented. Most studies have been conducted in single regions or have been based on relatively small samples, and differences in study populations, types of TB and statistical methods further limit the consistency and generalisability of the findings. Overall, no rigorous systematic review or meta-analysis has been conducted to address the factors associated with QoL among patients with PTB and to comprehensively synthesise and quantitatively summarise the available evidence. Therefore, it is imperative to conduct a systematic review and meta-analysis to quantify the associations between key influencing factors and QoL, explore sources of heterogeneity and identify potentially modifiable factors. Such evidence may provide a more robust basis for developing targeted nursing interventions and patient management strategies for patients with PTB, ultimately contributing to improvements in their QoL. This systematic review aims to identify and synthesise factors associated with QoL among patients with PTB. The specific objectives are as follows: (1) summarise the associations of demographic, clinical, psychological and social factors with QoL in patients with PTB; (2) conduct meta-analyses of quantifiable data, where feasible, and calculate corresponding effect sizes and 95% CIs; and (3) explore potential sources of heterogeneity related to geographical region, QoL instruments and patient characteristics.

Methods

The protocol was developed in strict accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis Protocols (PRISMA-P) statement23 and was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) on 23 December 2025 (registration number: CRD420251196862). Any important amendments to this protocol will be documented in PROSPERO and reported in the final review. The completed PRISMA-P checklist, including page numbers, has been submitted as an online supplemental file 1 research checklist.

Criteria for included and excluded studies

Inclusion criteria: Studies will be included if they meet the following criteria. (1) study design: observational studies, including cross-sectional, case-control and cohort studies. (2) Study population: adults aged 18 years or older with a confirmed diagnosis of PTB. No restriction will be placed on the time since TB diagnosis or treatment stage; however, information on disease duration, treatment phase and the timing of QoL or health-related quality-of-life (HRQoL) assessment will be extracted and considered in subgroup or narrative analyses where data permit. (3) Exposure factors: studies examining one or more factors potentially associated with QoL or HRQoL, including sociodemographic, clinical, treatment-related, psychosocial or behavioural factors. (4) Outcome measures: QoL or HRQoL assessed using validated generic or disease-specific instruments, such as the SF-36, WHOQOL-BREF, EQ-5D or St George’s Respiratory Questionnaire. QoL and HRQoL instruments will be considered eligible if they provide interpretable total scores, domain scores or utility values. (5) Association measures: studies reporting at least one eligible effect estimate for the association between potential influencing factors and QoL or HRQoL, including ORs, risk ratios (RRs), HRs, regression coefficients, mean differences, standardised mean differences or correlation coefficients, or studies providing sufficient data to calculate these estimates. When both adjusted and unadjusted estimates are available, adjusted estimates will be prioritised.

Exclusion criteria: Studies will be excluded if they meet any of the following criteria. (1) reviews, editorials, letters, comments, case reports, case series, qualitative studies or conference abstracts without full text. (2) Studies involving children, adolescents, patients with latent TB infection or patients with extrapulmonary TB only. (3) Studies including mixed populations in which data for adults with PTB cannot be extracted separately. (4) Studies that do not assess QoL or HRQoL using a validated instrument or that use self-developed instruments without sufficient psychometric information. (5) Studies that only describe QoL or HRQoL scores but do not report associations between potential influencing factors and QoL or HRQoL. (6) Studies for which the required data cannot be obtained after contacting the authors. (7) Duplicate publications. (8) Studies published in languages other than English or Chinese.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome of interest in this review will be the association between potential influencing factors and QoL or HRQoL among patients with PTB. Scores from validated generic or disease-specific QoL or HRQoL instruments will be extracted as outcome variables, as reported in the original studies. However, the main focus of this review will be the reported associations between potential influencing factors and QoL or HRQoL, rather than pooled estimates of QoL or HRQoL levels themselves.

Search strategy

A comprehensive literature search will be conducted in eight medical databases, comprising five English-language databases (PubMed, Web of Science, The Cochrane Library, Embase and Scopus) and three Chinese databases (China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), Chinese Scientific Journals Database (CSTJ) and Wanfang Database (WANFANG)), to identify observational studies on QoL among patients with PTB. The search will cover all records from database inception to 30 November 2025. The search strategy will be developed by integrating subject headings (eg, Medical Subject Headings) with relevant keywords and free-text terms. The Chinese search terms will mainly include “pulmonary tuberculosis”, “quality of life”, “health-related quality of life” and “influencing factors”. The English search terms will mainly include “Pulmonary Tuberculosis”, “Quality of Life”, “Health-Related Quality of Life”, “associated factors”, “risk factors” and “protective factors”. The predefined search strings will be appropriately adapted to the search syntax and requirements of each database. The complete search strategies for PubMed and CNKI are presented in boxes 1 and 2, and the search strategies for the remaining databases (Embase, Web of Science, The Cochrane Library, Scopus, CSTJ and WANFANG) are provided in online supplemental tables 3–8.

Study selection

All records will be imported into EndNote V.21.0 for deduplication, after which the Rayyan platform will be used for study screening.24 25 Literature screening and data extraction will be performed independently and in duplicate by two reviewers. The results will then be cross-checked to ensure accuracy and consistency. Any discrepancies will be resolved through discussion. If consensus cannot be reached, a third senior investigator will be consulted to reach a final decision. The screening process will be conducted in two distinct stages. First, titles and abstracts will be screened to exclude studies that are clearly ineligible based on the predefined criteria. Second, full-text of potentially eligible articles will be retrieved and assessed to determine final inclusion. For studies with missing key information, the research team will attempt to contact the original authors to obtain supplementary data. If the required information cannot be obtained, the study will be assessed on the basis of the available data or excluded.

No artificial intelligence tools will be used to determine study eligibility during title and abstract screening or full-text review.

Quality assessment

The included studies will be assessed independently by two reviewers. Following the individual assessments, the reviewers will compare their assessments to ensure consistency and objectivity in the appraisal process. Any disagreements will be resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer.

The quality of cross-sectional studies will be assessed using the 11-item checklist recommended by the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ),26 with a maximum score of 11. Studies scoring 0–3, 4–7 and 8–11 will be classified as low, moderate and high quality, respectively. Cohort studies and case–control studies will be assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS),27 which has a maximum score of 9 stars. Studies scoring 0–3, 4–6 and 7–9 will be classified as low, moderate and high quality, respectively.

The detailed scoring criteria for the AHRQ checklist and the NOS have been prespecified and are provided in online supplemental appendix 1 within the supplementary materials. Quality ratings will not be used as an automatic exclusion criterion but the influence of study quality on the findings will be considered in sensitivity or narrative analyses where data permit.

Data extraction

Two reviewers will manage and review the full texts using Zotero reference management software. Microsoft Excel 2016 will be used to facilitate standardised data extraction and ensure consistent recording of variables across all included studies. The two reviewers will independently extract data from all included studies and will cross-check the extracted data. Discrepancies will be resolved through discussion with arbitration by a third reviewer when necessary. The following information will be extracted: first author, year of publication, country or region, study design, sample size, participant characteristics (eg, age and sex), TB-related characteristics (eg, disease duration, drug resistance and comorbidities), instruments used to assess QoL or HRQoL and their domains, primary outcomes, influencing factors included in the analyses, effect estimates (including correlation coefficients, regression coefficients, ORs, RRs or HRs) with 95% CIs and covariates included in multivariable analyses.

If multiple articles are based on the same dataset, only the article with the largest sample size or the most comprehensive information will be included. For studies with missing key information or incomplete reporting, we will attempt to contact the original authors for additional data. If the required information cannot be obtained, analyses will be conducted using the available data or the study will be excluded as appropriate. The extraction form will be pilot-tested and refined if necessary before formal data extraction.

Data analysis

Before quantitative synthesis, we will assess whether meta-analysis is appropriate based on clinical, methodological and statistical considerations. Meta-analysis will be performed only when at least three studies report the same or clinically comparable influencing factor, include comparable adult PTB populations, use conceptually comparable QoL or HRQoL instruments or domains and provide the same type of effect estimate or data that can be converted to a common metric. Studies involving mixed TB populations will be pooled only if data for adults with PTB can be extracted separately. Studies using different QoL or HRQoL instruments, different influencing factors or different study designs will not be pooled in the primary meta-analysis unless they are clinically and methodologically comparable. Otherwise, the findings will be summarised narratively.

Where meta-analysis is appropriate, quantitative synthesis will be performed using Review Manager software.28 Effect estimates will be pooled according to their type. For continuous outcomes measured using the same scale, mean differences with 95% CIs will be used. When continuous outcomes are assessed using different but conceptually comparable instruments, standardised mean differences with 95% CIs will be used. For ratio measures such as ORs, RRs or HRs, pooled estimates with 95% CIs will be calculated when the measures are sufficiently comparable. Regression coefficients and correlation coefficients will be pooled separately only when the scales, model specifications and adjustment factors are sufficiently comparable; otherwise, they will be summarised narratively.

Pooled estimates will be calculated using the inverse-variance method where appropriate. Given the expected clinical and methodological heterogeneity across observational studies, a random-effects model will be used as the primary model.29 A fixed-effect model will be considered only when the included studies are sufficiently homogeneous in terms of population, exposure definition, outcome measurement and effect estimate.

Between-study heterogeneity will be assessed from both methodological and statistical perspectives. Methodological heterogeneity will be evaluated by comparing study design, participant characteristics, exposure definitions and QoL or HRQoL instruments. Statistical heterogeneity will be assessed using Cochran’s Q test, with p<0.10 indicating statistical significance, and the I² statistic. Where sufficient data are available, subgroup analyses will be conducted according to geographical region, QoL or HRQoL instrument, treatment stage, study design and patient characteristics. Where sufficient data are available, sensitivity analyses will be performed by excluding studies at high risk of bias or low methodological quality. Publication bias will be assessed using funnel plots when at least ten studies are included in a meta-analysis.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the development of the research question or the design of this protocol. However, given the patient-centred nature of QoL and HRQoL outcomes, we plan to consult patient representatives and healthcare professionals involved in TB care during the interpretation and dissemination of the review findings. Their feedback will be used to help contextualise the findings and improve the accessibility of dissemination materials.

Discussion

Existing studies on QoL or HRQoL among patients with PTB show substantial variation in study populations, TB-related clinical characteristics and QoL assessment instruments, which poses methodological challenges for evidence synthesis. For example, differences in treatment stages as well as variations in the structure, domains and scoring systems of QoL instruments may compromise the comparability and interpretability of findings across studies. In this context, ensuring a methodologically rigorous synthesis of the available evidence represents a key consideration in the present study.

This study is designed to systematically identify and synthesise factors associated with QoL in patients with PTB using clearly predefined eligibility criteria and independent study selection and quality assessment by two reviewers. During data analysis, both statistical heterogeneity and potential methodological heterogeneity will be carefully considered. Where data permit, subgroup analyses will be conducted to explore the influence of different study characteristics on the observed associations, with the aim of improving the robustness and transparency of evidence synthesis. Nevertheless, several anticipated limitations should be acknowledged. The included evidence is expected to consist predominantly of cross-sectional observational studies, thereby limiting causal inference. In addition, the use of different QoL instruments across studies may contribute to increased heterogeneity in pooled analyses. Despite these limitations, by systematically integrating the existing evidence, this review is expected to provide informative evidence to help nursing professionals identify patients with impaired QoL who may be at higher risk, refine priorities for nursing interventions and support the development of patient management strategies. Furthermore, the findings may help inform the design of future longitudinal and intervention studies in this field.

Ethics and dissemination

This study is a review of published research, and therefore ethical approval is not required. The findings will be published in a peer-reviewed journal or disseminated at relevant conferences. Regardless of whether this review is ultimately completed and its findings published, we plan to separately publish the study protocol to inform future related research and facilitate methodological exchange and development in this field.
