Improving the functional status of young boxers in the preparatory period of training with biofeedback
Authors: Svetlana Kubiyeva, Amangazy Syzdykov, Tulegen Botagariyev, Olga Makunina, Nurolla Mambetov, Alpysbai Aralbayev
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
There is little systematic research on how the training load of an athlete in the preparatory period affects their functional state. This study aims to develop and test a new targeted training method for improving the functional state of boxers aged 15–16 (15.6 ± 0.5) years using heart rate variability (HRV) biofeedback. A total of 50 teenage male athletes participated in the study. The proposed program involved biofeedback-based physical training coupled with breathing and emotional regulation exercises. Here, the functional status assessment involved psychological (attention, working memory, physiological stress), neurodynamic (simple sensorimotor reaction, maximal voluntary strength, strength endurance of the wrist flexor muscles), and neurovegetative (stress index, reactivity and tension indicators of regulatory systems) variables. The results demonstrated significant improvements in attention (Cohen’s d = 3.85) and working memory (Cohen’s d = 2.12) and a decrease in physiological stress (Cohen’s d = 0.87) in the HRV-BF group compared to the control group. The proposed framework can be integrated into boxer training programs in youth sports schools and specialized training centers, as it contributes not only to enhancing cognitive characteristics but also to optimizing neurovegetative balance through the reduction of physiological stress. Future research should focus on clarifying the effectiveness of HRV-BF in sustaining the stability of regulatory systems under conditions of intensive physical load.

Introduction

One of the issues in the literature that warrants particular attention concerns changes in the functional state of boxers during the preparatory training period1. Young boxers encounter numerous challenges throughout this stage, including high physical demands associated with the development of strength, speed-strength qualities, endurance, and the ability to sustain high-intensity performance during multi-round bouts. These demands are compounded by cognitive requirements, such as maintaining attentional focus, making rapid tactical decisions in dynamically changing contexts, and effectively managing stress responses that directly influence both competitive outcomes and the long-term dynamics of motor skill acquisition2. Neurobiological studies have demonstrated that repetitive head impacts may lead to functional and microstructural deterioration in male athletes, underscoring the vulnerability of their nervous system under conditions of intensive training3. Moreover, conditioned motor responses rely on prior learning, in which the perception of unconsciously presented movement features is combined with motor processes responsible for executing rapid reactions4.

At the same time, the psychological component plays a decisive role. Evidence indicates that the bidirectional relationship between conditional self-evaluation and stress factors shapes stress responses, with stressors exerting a stronger influence on individuals with higher conditional self-evaluation compared to those with lower levels5. In response to these challenges, sports science increasingly relies on advanced technological tools that enable precise monitoring and targeted modulation of athletes’ physiological and psychophysiological variables.

Among such innovations is biofeedback (hereafter BF), which in the context of boxing training functions not only as a diagnostic tool but also as an active mechanism for regulating adaptive reserves and optimizing the training process6. Its implementation allows coaches and athletes to design variable training scenarios with an emphasis on emotional self-regulation, which directly contributes to the somatovegetative support of competitive performance7. This approach is expected to facilitate the integration of cardiovascular, respiratory, and neuromuscular regulatory indicators into a comprehensive functional profile of the athlete.

Despite its potential, the application of biofeedback (BF) in youth boxing remains insufficiently explored. In light of this gap, the aim of the present study was to develop and test a novel targeted training method designed to improve the functional state of young boxers through the use of heart rate variability biofeedback (HRV-BF). The following hypotheses were formulated:

H_a: The use of HRV-BF will enhance cognitive indicators, specifically attention and working memory, in young boxers compared with the control group.

H_b: The HRV-BF intervention will reduce physiological stress levels, as assessed by cardiac and galvanic skin measures, relative to the control group.

H_c: HRV-BF will contribute to improvements in neurodynamic indicators, such as sensorimotor reaction speed and strength endurance, in comparison with traditional training methods.

Literature review

The existing literature posits that athlete’s professional experience correlates with the perceptual-cognitive skill level. More specifically, expert athletes were reported to have better general anticipation skills with real and simulated stimuli, and better cognitive functions with regards to attention compared to novices8. Combat sports require that athletes execute rapid motor reactions9. It is assumed that rapid motor reactions are primed by the perception of non-consciously represented movement features embedded into the movement sequences of sports opponents. This form of perceptual identification of visual movement stimuli is said to be based on a common coding of perceptual and action processes4. Trained athletes were reported to have faster reaction times compared to novices, which enables them to make more successful hits10. Some researchers found that simple reaction time, even rate reaction time, high rate reaction time, and times for discrimination and inhibition decline significantly with age11.

Integrating self-regulation into models of off-time pubertal maturation allows for coordination between processes that precede and follow pubertal development12. A study of three distinct attention functions (orienting, alerting, and executive) revealed that attentional functioning and putting performance are somewhat related. At the same time, relationships with orientation and executive attention were found to be the strongest among those which received external focus instructions13. This finding confirms that individual differences in attentional abilities are predictive of new skills performance under an external focus of attention. A strong relationship was found between extroversion and source memory and between extroversion and destination memory14. An athlete’s working memory capacity is argued to be positively related to their dual-task motor performance when the cognitive load is low15. Overall, developing both complex and simple motor skills can contribute positively to the physical and technical abilities of athletes16. Equally important is the context of neurodynamic assessment. For instance, Kulis et al17 focused on the kinematic analysis of movements in elite athletes engaged in sports dance and demonstrated that maximum angular velocities of thoracic spine rotation during dynamic movements were closely associated with judges’ evaluations of technical performance quality. Within the framework of the present study, neurodynamic tests—particularly the assessment of simple sensorimotor reaction and strength parameters—can be regarded as a simplified yet conceptually aligned strategy for the quantitative evaluation of motor functions, comparable to kinematic analysis.

The latest studies show a growing interest in the use of biofeedback as a multi-level tool for regulating the functional state of athletes18. The heart rate variability (HRV) biofeedback is considered to be an effective tool for improving an athlete’s ability to regulate heart rate and breathing rate, which is important in sports that require a high degree of stress resistance and adaptability19. Depending on the intensity of training, adolescent boxers can experience certain fluctuations in the amount of functional resources, but biofeedback can help correct their psychophysiological state20. The empirical studies show that HRV-BF training can speed up the mobilization of energy substrates and neurovegetative stabilization21–23. In the context of biofeedback (BF) application, it is important to highlight studies addressing acute physiological adaptations. For example, Martone et al.24 demonstrated that dynamic protocols combined with core stabilization exercises produced significant improvements in jump performance, dynamic balance, and agility among athletes approximately 15 years of age. The observed effects—including increases in peak relative power and enhanced balance of lower-limb muscle groups—confirm the sensitivity of the young body to functional training stimuli during a critical developmental period. These findings conceptually resonate with the BF approach, which is aimed at optimizing regulatory as well as cognitive–physiological mechanisms. At the same time, there are not enough comprehensive programs for martial arts, which necessitates further research aimed at integrating biofeedback into the training process of teenage boxers.

The methodological justification of training load can be achieved through the integration of normative benchmarks reported in the study by Tounsi et al.25 The established reference values for anthropometric characteristics and physical fitness indicators of elite young athletes, stratified by biological maturity—particularly jump performance, speed-related parameters, and endurance—allow for more accurate prescription of training intensity for adolescent boxers while minimizing the risk of overload during peak growth phases, when the athlete’s body is especially vulnerable to injuries and functional imbalances. Contemporary standards in training load methodology strengthen the rationale for selected protocols in research with adolescent athletes. For instance, a systematic review of the effects of blood flow restriction (BFR) resistance training in older adults has demonstrated that controlled mechanical restriction during resistance exercise effectively enhances muscle strength, increases muscle cross-sectional area, and stimulates protein synthesis biomarkers26. Although the sample comprised older adults, the underlying principles of progressive loading, training frequency (three sessions per week), and intervention duration (approximately 11 weeks) can be adapted for young athletes, ensuring systematic and safe training stimuli.

Equally important is the delineation of training load monitoring principles through the integration of mechanical, neuromuscular, and cardio-physiological indicators. Evidence shows that running gait variability, measured using the phase coordination index, reflects adaptation to training load when neuromuscular activity is stressed under prolonged running protocols27. Simultaneous monitoring of heart rate, kinematic, and kinetic parameters provides a comprehensive assessment of the physiological response to load. Thus, the phase coordination index may serve as a sensitive tool for evaluating the impact of training programs on movement coordination and the balance between performance and fatigue. For the training of young boxers and other athletes, this implies the possibility of precisely regulating training volume and intensity, preventing overload, and optimizing motor skill development, thereby ensuring safe and effective adaptation to systematic training.

To establish a foundation for interpreting athletes’ physiological stress indicators and to deepen the interpretation of cognitive outcomes, it is useful to refer to existing empirical evidence. Kotarska et al.28 demonstrated that students with higher levels of physical activity were more likely to employ situational stress-management strategies, whereas less active respondents predominantly relied on emotional reactions or sought social support. Transferring these findings to the context of intensive training loads in young boxers suggests that not only physiological stress responses but also their impact on performance may be modulated by prevailing cognitive-behavioral strategies.

Furthermore, the integration of conceptual insights from studies examining the interplay between physical activity, sleep, and cognitive functioning has shown that regular physical activity is associated not only with improved academic achievement but also indirectly influences it through enhancements in working memory and attentional processes29. Among young boxers, the observed cognitive improvements may be explained by the fact that the combination of intensive training loads and recovery processes—including sleep—creates optimal conditions for neuroplastic adaptations that reinforce cognitive-motor integration mechanisms.

Engaging with this line of research enriches the theoretical foundation and enables the interpretation of the obtained results not as isolated indicators, but as components of a more complex system of interrelated factors.

Materials and methods

Study design

The first stage of the study (November-December 2024) involved reviewing scientific and methodological literature related to the research problem. The second stage (January-March 2025) was to gather psychophysiological and neurodynamic information from the participating boxers. Finally, the third stage of the study (April-May 2025) involved developing the preparatory-phase training methodology for improving the functional status of boxers aged 15 to 16 years. Participants were divided into two groups. The HRV-BF intervention group (n = 25) engaged in biofeedback practice; the training protocol (breathing and emotional regulation exercises) involved 2 to 3 sessions of 15 to 30 min per week. The control group (n = 25) received traditional training.

Participants

The research took place in the sport complex “Almaz” in Chelyabinsk. A total of 50 male athletes (junior group and candidate masters of sports (CMMs)), aged 15 to 16 (15.6 ± 0.5) years, with 3 to 5 years of training experience participated in the study. The study strictly followed the recommendations of the Declaration of Helsinki and national laws regulating human research involving minors. Before the intervention began, the teenage athletes were informed about the goals, methods and potential risks of the intervention and verbally communicated their consent to participate in the study. Their parents and legal guardians granted approval for their children to participate by voluntary signing an informed consent form. Confidentiality of personal data of the participants was strictly observed: All results were recorded and coded to prevent researchers from identifying the athletes while analyzing the data. The participants were given the opportunity to withdraw at any time without any consequences.

Data collection

The functional status of the athletes was assessed in terms of psychological (attention, working memory, and stress level), neurodynamic (simple sensorimotor reaction, maximal voluntary strength, and strength endurance of wrist flexor muscles) and neurovegetative (tension index (RSTI) and reactivity of regulatory systems (RSR)) indicators. The tests were carried out using NS-PsychoTest software (LLC Neurosoft, Ivanovo), which includes psychological and psychophysiological test batteries designed for assessing the functional status in athletes30.

Here, attention was assessed with Schulte-Platonov tables. The ability to withstand stress was measured using the Lüscher’s test. The working memory (WM) and simple sensorimotor reaction (SSR) tests were conducted as described in the study by Levshin et al.31 The maximal voluntary strength (MSt) and strength endurance (SE) of the wrist flexor muscles were measured using a dynamometer32. Additional markers of stress included heart rate (HR) and galvanic skin response (GSR), the values of which were obtained using photoplethysmographic (PPG) sensors and a device for skin conductance measurement.

Procedures

Participants were allocated to experimental and control groups using a random number generator. To reduce the risk of systematic bias and enhance the internal validity of the study, block randomization with a fixed block size was employed, ensuring balanced group sizes throughout the recruitment process. In addition, stratification by key variables—namely, training experience and baseline functional status—was applied to minimize the likelihood of random imbalances that could lead to spurious detection of effects (Type I error). Athletes in the experimental group were assigned to the heart rate variability (HRV) biofeedback (BF) intervention, which included 2 to 3 sessions per week lasting between 15 and 30 min. At the beginning of each session, participants were asked to wear the HR and SC response sensors (ProComp Infiniti, Thought Technology Ltd.) through which the objective physiological signals were recorded. After all the necessary sensors were attached and equipment calibrated, athletes were briefly instructed on how to correctly perform breathing exercises and how to regulate their emotional state. The main part of the training involved a series of slow-paced breathing exercises with visual and auditory feedback aimed at optimizing heart rate oscillations. During the sessions, the focus was on enhancing HRV, maintaining a calm emotional state, reducing stress levels and increasing concentration. Each BF training session consists of several 5 to 7 min long cycles with breaks between activity for assessing changes in the physiological status and adjusting the emotional self-regulation strategy. The results in the form of graphs and indices were displayed on a display screen.

Athletes in the control group followed a traditional training program that includes 2–3 sessions without biofeedback information per week, each lasting 45 to 60 min. The training exercises are aimed at enhancing endurance, strength, coordination, and boxing technical skills. The training loads administrated in the two groups were identical in terms of volume and intensity, allowing researchers to isolate the effect of biofeedback technologies on the functional state of athletes. All training sessions were held in specially equipped halls under the supervision of qualified trainers and specialists in sports physiology. Physiological parameters were measured before, during, and after training to ensure that adaptive changes are taken into consideration and adjust the training process accordingly.

Data analysis

The initial psychophysiological and neurodynamic equivalence of the two groups was tested using data from the Student’s t-test for independent samples. Differences in pre and post-intervention measures, as well as intergroup differences, were analyzed using the one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Prior to the application of the t-test and ANOVA, the normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. To confirm the appropriateness of employing parametric tests, the assumption of homogeneity of variances was further examined using Levene’s test. The significance threshold was set 0.05. The magnitude of the intervention’s effect was assessed by calculating the Cohen’s d; a value of 0.2 represents a small effect size, 0.5 a medium effect size, and 0.8 a large effect size.

Methodology for improving the functional state of boxers

Based on data from the initial measurements, the preparatory phase of the training program should include sensorimotor exercises. The maximal voluntary strength and strength endurance were tested for the right and left hands, but given the small value of skewness, it was decided to average the strength of both hands. Based on theoretical information, wrist strength depends on the level of arousal, which was revealed to be low in the sensorimotor reaction test.

To improve the functional state of teenage boxers in the preparatory period, a circuit-based physical activity regimen was developed. The training program consists of two phases: (1) increasing the absolute and explosive strength of the muscles as well as fatigue resistance, and (2) enhancing functional abilities. The exercise stations used in the first phase of the circuit training include simulators, weights, and exercises that involve training with your own weight (e.g., pull-ups on a horizontal bar). This phase lasts three weeks and consists of six training sessions. Athletes were instructed to complete a designated number of repetitions; there was no specific time limit on how long athletes could use the stations, but the total set fell within the time frame of a single round. The weights used in the workout stations were selected differentially. The recovery period between stations is one minute. The first circuit workout ends with a relaxation phase and a series of boxing-related tasks, the length of which is equal to that of a single round. After the first circuit, athletes were given a 2-minute break. The circuit workout routine includes three circuits in the first week of training, and four circuits starting from the second week of the training period.

Stations were modified by setting up time boundaries halfway through the first phase of training. Athletes were instructed to perform each exercise for 1.5 min, with 1-minute rest in between stations. Boxers were allowed to tailor the number of repetitions in each set in accordance with the instructions provided by their trainers. The HR threshold was 140–160 beats per min. If the HR was higher by more than 35–40 beats, the training load was reduced. After completing the first circuit, athletes received tasks for relaxation, followed by average-paced shadow boxing drills. Participants were given 2 to 3 min to recover between circuits.

The second phase of the circuit training with two sessions per week is to build functional strength. The exercise stations used in this phase are boxing equipment, light weights, skipping ropes, etc. The exercise duration is 1.5–2 min but is reduced to 1 min halfway into the second phase of training. The intensity level of training is high (170–180 beats per min). Athletes were instructed to focus on technical execution. Their pulse was measured immediately after completing the exercises and after the recovery period. If the recovery was incomplete (less than 40 − 35 beats per min), then some adjustments to the activity regimen were made. Once the duration of the rest period was reduced, more intensity was added to the workout. The recovery time remained unchanged.

Discussion

This study shows that incorporating HRV-BF into the preparatory period of boxing training leads to significant improvements in attention, working memory and stress tolerance when compared to conventional training. These findings align with a previous study where athletes with a poor (50%) and average (40%) ability to concentrate attention experienced substantial improvement after a psychological intervention, with the ratings of good (35%), very good (20%) and excellent (20%)33. One should keep in mind, however, that these two studies use different methodologies for collecting data; hence, one must exercise caution when comparing the findings.

The current study demonstrated that even though both groups showed changes in all neurodynamic variables by the end of the preparatory period, the HRV-BF group exhibited greater improvements. This trend coincides with findings from the previous study where greater experience in boxing correlated with significantly faster reaction time and specific differences in the motor response of the performing arm depending on the stimulus/target position34. For the present study, however, athletes were not divided into separate groups based on the length of their experience in boxing.

The physical preparation framework used in this study to improve the functional status of teenage boxers incorporates the circuit method. Hussein Ali proposed a similar method for enhancing some kinetic abilities and the physiological state of athletes that involves a set of cross-training exercises35.

Integrating HRV-BF into the training process of boxers aged 15–16 years in the preparatory period resulted in a significant reduction of physiological stress and psychophysiological optimization. This trend partially coincides with the results of another study, which found that regular use of HRV-BF leads to significant improvements in emotional self-regulation in athletes who engage in high-intensity sports36. The SC and HR decreases in the HRV-BF group demonstrate convergence with previous observations about the role of HRV-BF in the treatment of stress symptoms and neurovegetative stabilization37.

BF protocols were reported to have pronounced effects on the cognitive sphere, while somatic responses were more variable38. In the present study, however, the most significant changes were detected with respect to physiological markers, while cognitive-neurodynamic parameters remained comparable with the controls. This finding suggests heterogeneity in adaptive responses during puberty. The observed effect is likely due to increasing functional integration of regulatory circuits of the cardiorespiratory system coupled with a decrease in the activity of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis under the influence of targeted training program. Such protocols form stable patterns of neurovegetative self-regulation due to inhibitory influences descending from the prefrontal cortex on the vegetative nuclei of the brainstem39. Therefore, further research involving participants of varying age and experience is needed to verify the results of this study.

It has additionally been demonstrated that prior execution of specific plyometric exercises can elicit significant improvements in performance following a training session, highlighting the importance of preparatory activity for optimizing post-training adaptation40. Similarly, our data indicate that the use of HRV-BF in combination with breathing and emotional regulation exercises significantly enhances cognitive performance and reduces physiological stress in boxers. A close relationship between physical adaptations and adolescents’ subjective perception of their own capabilities has also been confirmed41. Our findings align with this observation, as improvements in functional stability and self-regulation may strengthen athletes’ sense of control and confidence, thereby supporting both physical and psychological development.

Notably, improvements in motor skills and postural stability have been documented in high-level athletes through sensorimotor warm-ups, underscoring the importance of an integrated training approach that incorporates both sensorimotor and proprioceptive exercises42. In this context, we found that HRV-BF, when combined with neurodynamic exercises, may play a similar role for boxers by optimizing not only neurodynamic indicators but also the regulation of the autonomic nervous system.

Discussion

In a study with 25 participants per group, there exists a potential risk of insufficient statistical power to detect significant effects, particularly with respect to neurodynamic variables, where nonsignificant results were observed. To address this issue, a post hoc power analysis was conducted using the G*Power software. The calculations indicated that for cognitive variables, such as working memory (Cohen’s d = 2.12) and attention (d = 3.85), statistical power exceeded the recommended threshold of 0.80. By contrast, for neurodynamic variables, such as maximal voluntary strength (d = 0.14) and strength endurance (d = 0.12), power was below 0.30–0.35. This suggests that, given the relatively small sample size, moderate effects in physical and neurodynamic outcomes may have remained undetected; therefore, the results for these variables should be interpreted with caution.

Moreover, the absence of subgroup analyses by training experience means that potential differences in responses to HRV-biofeedback training between more and less experienced athletes remain unexplored, potentially obscuring nuances in the method’s effectiveness across different skill levels. The relatively short observation period further limits the capacity to evaluate the long-term effects of the intervention, including the stability of cognitive, neurodynamic, and neurovegetative changes. All of these factors must be considered when interpreting the findings and in the design of future studies, as they influence both the reliability and ecological validity of the conclusions, as well as the extent to which the observed effects can be generalized to other age groups or training conditions.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that incorporating HRV-BF into the training process of boxers in the preparatory period yielded positive changes in the key neurodynamic and psychophysiological indicators. The stress level assessment with HR and SC data made it possible to objectively detect vegetative and emotional shifts throughout training. The findings suggest that the BF technology helps athletes develop specific self-regulation patterns, providing a higher level of functional adaptation during the period of intense training. A pronounced decrease in physiological stress observed in the HRV-BF group coupled with improvement in the functional level of the system indicates that HRV-BF is a promising tool for increasing stress resistance and cognitive fitness of young boxers.

The present findings can be applied across various educational contexts, ranging from extracurricular regimens to individualized programs. The biofeedback methods and specially developed breathing and emotional regulation exercises can be integrated into original physical education programs to ensure physical preparedness, instill emotional self-regulation skills, and develop social adaptation competencies. Such interventions—namely, targeted programs employing biofeedback or specific preparatory conditioning exercises—affect competitive performance in combat sports through several interrelated pathways. They enhance athletes’ functional capacities, including cognitive, neurodynamic, and neurovegetative indicators, thereby facilitating more effective adaptation to intensive training and reducing the risk of functional overload. They also improve psychological self-regulation, enabling athletes to restore concentration more rapidly, manage emotional stress, and optimize strategic decision-making during bouts. In addition, they create conditions for specific pre-conditioning of the neuromuscular systems, which increases movement speed and precision, augments strike power, and improves the efficiency of technical combinations in competitive situations.

A promising direction for further research is an in-depth study of integrative regulatory mechanisms that form during the BF intervention with insights from specialists in related disciplines, including sports physiology, psychology and medicine. This information will allow taking a more differentiated approach to boxing program design.
