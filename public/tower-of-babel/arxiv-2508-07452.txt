Stackelberg Coupling of Online Representation Learning and Reinforcement Learning
Fernando Martinez, Tao Li, Yingdong Lu, Juntao Chen

Abstract
Integrated, end-to-end learning of representations and policies remains a cornerstone of deep reinforcement learning (RL). However, to address the challenge of learning effective features from a sparse reward signal, recent trends have shifted towards adding complex auxiliary objectives or fully decoupling the two processes, often at the cost of increased design complexity. This work proposes an alternative to both decoupling and naive end-to-end learning, arguing that performance can be significantly improved by structuring the interaction between distinct perception and control networks with a principled, game-theoretic dynamic. We formalize this dynamic by introducing the Stackelberg Coupled Representation and Reinforcement Learning (SCORER) framework, which models the interaction between perception and control as a Stackelberg game. The perception network (leader) strategically learns features to benefit the control network (follower), whose own objective is to minimize its Bellman error. We approximate the game’s equilibrium with a practical two-timescale algorithm. Applied to standard DQN variants on benchmark tasks, SCORER improves sample efficiency and final performance. Our results show that performance gains can be achieved through principled algorithmic design of the perception-control dynamic, without requiring complex auxiliary objectives or architectures.

Introduction

The introduction of deep reinforcement learning (RL) marked a significant milestone, exemplified by algorithms capable of achieving human-level control in complex tasks like Atari games directly from pixel inputs
mnih2015human
. This success popularized the end-to-end paradigm, where representation learning and policy optimization are performed jointly within a single deep neural network, driven primarily by the RL objective function. Building on these initial successes, the integrated learning approach rapidly evolved, enabling deep RL systems to solve intricate strategy games
vinyals2019grandmaster
;
berner2019dota
, mastered complex robotic manipulation tasks
schulman2017proximalpolicyoptimizationalgorithms
;
ze2023h
, and showed promise in areas such as autonomous driving and resource management
kiran2021deep
. The main appeal of this paradigm derives from its conceptual simplicity and the potential for features and policies to co-adapt, promising benefits such as stabilized learning and improved sample efficiency.

However, relying solely on the often sparse or delayed reward signal to shape internal representations can be challenging
pmlr-v139-stooke21a
, which further hinders RL performance within the current task. Recognizing this, a significant body of work has focused on improving representation learning within the deep RL framework. One prominent direction involves augmenting the primary RL objective with auxiliary losses derived from supervised, unsupervised, and self-supervised learning. These tasks provide additional learning signals, incentivizing the network to capture structural information about the environment, such as predicting future states, reconstructing observations, or ensuring temporal consistency
jaderberg2017reinforcement
;
schwarzerdata
;
pmlr-v119-laskin20a
. These methods often retain the end-to-end structure but enrich the gradient signal for the shared encoder network.

More recently, inspired by the success of self-supervised learning in computer vision
grill2020bootstrap
;
chen2020simple
, a particular trend has emerged exploring the separation of representation learning from the direct reward-driven reinforcement learning objective. Some approaches explicitly separate these processes, aiming to learn powerful representations independently using self-supervised learning (e.g., contrastive learning
pmlr-v139-stooke21a
) that may run concurrently with, but are distinct from, the RL policy learner. While methods focusing on enhanced or decoupled representation learning have demonstrated strong empirical results
pmlr-v139-stooke21a
;
fujimoto2023sale
, they often introduce significant architectural or training complexities. These can include the need for carefully designed auxiliary tasks, management of multiple interacting loss functions, or separate pre-training phases, potentially increasing manual design effort and overall computational demands. Furthermore, decoupling might forgo the potential benefits of tight, dynamic co-adaptation between representation and policy, specifically tailored for the control task at hand.

This work revisits the integrated learning paradigm but proposes a more structured and principled interaction between representation learning and reinforcement learning. We posit that the limitations of naive end-to-end learning can be addressed not only by adding external signals or by complete decoupling, but also by fundamentally rethinking the
internal dynamics
of how representations and policies co-evolve (illustrated in Figure
1
). If the representation learner can strategically anticipate the needs of the policy learner, it might lead to more effective and efficient overall learning without the overhead of fully separate systems.

To this end, we introduce the Stackelberg Coupled Representation and Reinforcement Learning (SCORER) framework. Drawing inspiration from Stackelberg games in economics and game theory
Lambertini_2018
, SCORER models the interaction between representation learning (the
Perception
network) and reinforcement learning (the
Control
network) as a hierarchical game between two strategic agents with aligned but distinct goals. We designate the perception process as the leader. Its objective is to learn features that are maximally beneficial for the control process. The leader makes its decision (i.e., updates its parameters) anticipating the optimal response of the follower—the control process—which aims to minimize its own objective (e.g., Mean Squared Bellman Error) given the current representation. The integrated learning process is characterized by the associated Stackelberg equilibrium, for which we devise a two-timescale gradient descent algorithm to approximate (Section
4.2
). Our code is open-sourced at
https://anonymous.4open.science/r/SCORER-530
. In summary,
our contributions are three-fold
: 1) We introduce the
SCORER framework
, a new game-theoretic formulation for integrated perception and control learning in RL. 2) We develop a practical
two-timescale algorithm
to approximate the Stackelberg equilibrium, enabling anticipatory representation learning 3) Through
extensive experiments
, we demonstrate SCORER’s power to improve sample efficiency and performance of baseline value-based RL algorithms on benchmark tasks.

Preliminary

Reinforcement Learning and Q-Learning.
We consider an agent interacting with an environment formulated as a Markov Decision Process (MDP), defined by the tuple
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},P,R,\gamma)
. Here,
\mathcal{S}
is the state space,
\mathcal{A}
is the action space,
P(s^{\prime}|s,a)
is the state transition probability,
R(s,a)
is the reward, and
\gamma\in[0,1)
is the discount factor. The agent’s goal is to learn a policy
\pi(a|s)
that maps states to actions (or distributions over actions) to maximize the expected sum of discounted future rewards from a given state
s_{t}
:
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{i}r_{t+i}|s_{t}\right]
, where
r_{t+i}
is the reward received
i
steps after time
t
.

In value-based Reinforcement Learning, the optimal action-value function
Q^{*}(s,a)
represents the expected return from taking action
a
in state
s
and following the optimal policy thereafter. It satisfies the Bellman optimality equation:
Q^{*}(s,a)=\mathbb{E}_{s^{\prime}}[R(s,a)+\gamma\max_{a^{\prime}}Q^{*}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})]
. Deep Q-Networks (DQN) approximate
Q^{*}
using neural networks trained by minimizing the Mean Squared Bellman Error:
\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{(s,a,r,s^{\prime})\sim\mathcal{D}}[(r+\gamma\max_{a^{\prime}}Q_{\theta_{\text{target}}}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})-Q_{\theta}(s,a))^{2}]
.

Stackelberg Game.
A Stackelberg game is a hierarchical game involving two types of players: a
leader
and one or more
followers

osborne2004introduction
. The leader moves first, choosing an action
a_{L}
from its action set
\mathcal{A}_{L}
. The follower, equipped with the full information on the leader’s action
a_{L}
, chooses its own action
a_{F}
from its action set
\mathcal{A}_{F}
to maximize its own utility
u_{F}(a_{L},a_{F})
, given
a_{L}
. The basic equilibrium relationship for the Stackelberg game is reflected by the following bi-level optimization problem:
\max_{a_{L}}U_{L}(a_{L},a_{F}^{*}(a_{L})),\text{ s.t. }a_{F}^{*}(a_{L})\in\arg\max_{a_{F}}U_{F}(a_{L},a_{F})
, with
U_{L}(a_{L},a_{F})
being the utility of the leader.

The defining characteristic is the leader’s ability to anticipate and influence the follower’s decision by committing to a strategy first. Recognizing this dependence, the leader strategically guides the follower toward decisions that are advantageous to both of them with shared or closely related utilities. This observation inspires our proposed SCORER framework introduced in the subsequent.

4
Stackelberg Coupled Representation and Reinforcement Learning

In this section, we introduce the Stackelberg Coupled Representation and Reinforcement Learning (SCORER) framework. SCORER formalizes the interplay between representation learning and reinforcement learning within an integrated, end-to-end architecture by modeling their interaction as a hierarchical Stackelberg game. This perspective allows us to design an algorithmic structure where the representation learning process (the
Perception
network) explicitly anticipates and supports the downstream reinforcement learning task (the
Control
network).

The key intuition behind SCORER is recognizing the inherent asymmetry between representation learning and policy optimization in deep RL. While these processes are typically coupled through a single end-to-end objective, they serve distinct purposes: perception captures the environmental structure, while policy optimization exploits this structure for reward maximization
bellemare2019geometric
. This suggests a natural hierarchy where high-quality representations should serve as a foundation for effective policy learning, rather than emerging solely as an implicit byproduct of a single, monolithic objective
schwarzerdata
;
pmlr-v139-stooke21a
. Viewing this as a Stackelberg game, SCORER allows the perception network (leader) to strategically adapt its encodings based on the anticipated needs of the control network (follower).

4.1
The Stackelberg Game Formulation

We propose modeling the intertwined processes of learning representations and learning control policies as a two-player Stackelberg game, where players have distinct but closely related objectives and act hierarchically. These processes are embodied by distinct neural networks: an encoder network
f_{\phi}
for perception, parameterized by
\phi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and a Q-network
Q_{\theta}
for control, parameterized by
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. While the proposed SCORER framework provides principled integration of representation and reinforcement learning applicable to generic RL algorithms, we limit our attention to deep Q-learning (DQN)
mnih2015human
and its variants in this work as an exemplary illustration.

The Leader: Perception Network.
The
Perception
process assumes the role of the Stackelberg leader. Its function is to learn an encoder that maps raw observations
s
to a latent representation
z=f_{\phi}(s)
. The fundamental goal of the leader is to generate representations
z
that are maximally beneficial for the follower’s subsequent task. The definition of “beneficial” is encapsulated in the leader’s objective function,
\mathcal{L}_{\text{leader}}
. While many choices for
\mathcal{L}_{\text{leader}}
are possible within the SCORER framework (e.g., promoting specific geometric properties in
z
, or directly minimizing the follower’s loss), the central idea is that this objective evaluates the quality of the representation
\phi
based on its utility to the follower. For instance,
\mathcal{L}_{\text{leader}}
could be formulated to promote stability in the follower’s learning by minimizing the variance of the follower’s learning signal (e.g., the variance of Mean Squared Bellman Error in Appendix
D
), or it could directly aim to minimize the magnitude of the follower’s learning signal itself (e.g. the Mean Squared Bellman Error in the preliminary and in Equation (
2
)), thereby encouraging representations that allow the follower to reach its objective more easily. Regardless of the specific formulation, the leader strategically selects its parameters
\phi
by optimizing
\mathcal{L}_{\text{leader}}
while anticipating the follower’s reaction. Formally, the leader aims to solve:

\displaystyle\min_{\phi}\ \mathcal{L}_{\text{leader}}(f_{\phi},Q_{\theta^{*}}),

(1)

where
\theta^{*}
represents the parameters the follower would ideally converge to, or take a step towards, given the leader’s choice of
\phi
. This anticipatory optimization is the defining characteristic of the leader’s role in the Stackelberg game: it chooses its representation strategy
\phi
by considering the downstream consequences for the follower (see Equation (
2
)), as measured by
\mathcal{L}_{\text{leader}}
.

The Follower: Control Network.
The control network
Q_{\theta}
acts as the Stackelberg follower. Its objective is to learn an optimal action-value function
Q_{\theta}(z,a)
, mapping the leader-provided state representation
z=f_{\phi}(s)
and action
a
to the expected cumulative discounted future reward. Given the representation
z
(with
\phi
treated as fixed during the follower’s update), the follower seeks parameters
\theta
that minimize its own loss function,
\mathcal{L}_{\text{follower}}
. Denote by
\mathcal{D}
the replay buffer with
Y
being the Bellman target value. The follower’s optimization drives
\theta
towards accurate value estimation within the feature space defined by
f_{\phi}
. For the value-based methods we focus on (DQN and its variants
mnih2015human
;
van2016deep
;
wang2016dueling
), this loss is typically the Mean Squared Bellman Error (MSBE):

\theta^{*}(\phi)\in\arg\min_{\theta}\ \mathcal{L}_{\text{follower}}(f_{\phi},Q_{\theta})\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{(s,a,r,s^{\prime})\sim\mathcal{D}}\Big[(Y-Q_{\theta}(f_{\phi}(s),a))^{2}\Big].

(2)

The essence of SCORER lies in establishing a specific dynamic where the leader actively shapes the representation space, anticipating its effect on the follower’s learning objective, and the follower adapts its policy within the space provided by the leader. Figure
1
provides an illustration of SCORER and its internal dynamics.

4.2
Approximating the Stackelberg Equilibrium via Two-Timescale Gradient Descent

The Stackelberg game described in Section
4.1
translates to a bi-level optimization problem with the lower-level given by Equation (
3b
) and the upper-level by Equation (
3a
). The leader aims to optimize its objective
\mathcal{L}_{\text{leader}}
by choosing its parameters
\phi
(which define the encoder
f_{\phi}
), anticipating the follower’s optimal response
\theta^{*}(\phi)
:

\displaystyle\min_{\phi}\quad

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{leader}}(f_{\phi},Q_{\theta^{*}(\phi)})

(3a)

subject to

\displaystyle\theta^{*}(\phi)\in\arg\min_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\text{follower}}(f_{\phi},Q_{\theta}).

(3b)

Solving such bi-level problems directly in high-dimensional, non-convex settings typical of deep reinforcement learning is challenging, with the primary difficulty arising from computing the gradient of the leader’s objective with respect to its parameters
\phi
. Applying the chain rule and the implicit function theorem to
\mathcal{L}_{\text{leader}}(f_{\phi},Q_{\theta^{*}(\phi)})
yields

\displaystyle\nabla_{\phi}\mathcal{L}_{\text{leader}}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\text{leader}}}{\partial f_{\phi}}\nabla_{\phi}f_{\phi}+\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\text{leader}}}{\partial Q_{\theta^{*}(\phi)}}\nabla_{\phi}Q_{\theta^{*}(\phi)}.

(4)

The term
\nabla_{\phi}Q_{\theta^{*}(\phi)}
represents the implicit derivative of the follower’s optimal parameters with respect to the leader’s parameters. Computing this term often requires differentiating through the follower’s optimization process (e.g., using implicit function theorems or unrolling the follower’s updates
zhou23proxbilevel
), which can be computationally prohibitive and numerically unstable.

Even though recent advancements have developed a variety of first-order Hessian(Jacobian)-free strategies rooted in Danskin-type results
danskintype
in strictly competitive Stackelberg games
tao2024meta
, in dynamic barrier from constrained optimization
bome22first
, and in Lagrangian multiplier method
kwon23primal-dual
. Most relevant to our two-timescale algorithm is
hong23twotime
, where the authors also studied a two-timescale stochastic algorithm to estimate the Hessian and Jacobian. Since the focus of this work is on the game-theoretic coupling between representation and reinforcement learning, we do not dive deep into the sophisticated machinery and cumbersome technical assumptions for Hessian-free computation in bi-level optimization. Instead, we consider a simple
two-timescale first-order gradient descent
algorithm where the follower learns at a faster scale than the leader.

Rather than fully solving the inner optimization for
\theta^{*}(\phi)
at each step or computing the complex implicit derivative
\nabla_{\phi}\theta^{*}(\phi)
, we perform alternating gradient descent steps on
\mathcal{L}_{\text{leader}}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\text{follower}}
with interdependent learning rates and update frequencies. The perception network (leader, parameters
\phi
) and the control network (follower, parameters
\theta
) are distinct, allowing for separate optimization.

The follower updates its parameters
\theta
more frequently, performing gradient descent on its objective
\mathcal{L}_{\text{follower}}
(see Equation (
2
)), treating the leader’s current representation
f_{\phi}(s)
as fixed. This is achieved by detaching
f_{\phi}(s)
from the computation graph when calculating
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\text{follower}}
and learning rate
\alpha_{\theta}
:

\displaystyle\theta_{k+1}\leftarrow\theta_{k}-\alpha_{\theta}\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\text{follower}}(\theta_{k},\overline{f_{\phi_{k}}(s)}),

(5)

where
\overline{\cdot}
denotes the operation that treats its argument as a constant for gradient computation,
k
is the follower’s update step index, and
t
is the leader’s update step index (as
\phi
updates slower).

The leader, in turn, updates parameters
\phi
less frequently. Crucially, to approximate the anticipation of
\theta^{*}(\phi_{k})
, the leader’s update uses the follower’s parameters
\theta_{k}
(or
\theta_{\text{bu}}
, parameters before the current follower’s batch of updates), the result of the follower adapting to
\phi_{k}
. The leader’s gradient step effectively ignores the
\nabla_{\phi}\theta^{*}(\phi)
term from Equation (
4
), simplifying the update (with rate (
\alpha_{\phi}
) to:

\displaystyle\phi_{k+1}\leftarrow\phi_{k}-\alpha_{\phi}\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\text{leader}}}{\partial f_{\phi_{k}}}\nabla_{\phi}f_{\phi_{k}}+\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\text{leader}}}{\partial Q_{\theta_{k}}}\frac{\partial Q_{\theta_{k}}}{\partial f_{\phi_{k}}}\nabla_{\phi}f_{\phi_{k}}\right)\approx\phi_{k}-\alpha_{\phi}\nabla_{\phi}\mathcal{L}_{\text{leader}}(f_{\phi_{k}},Q_{\overline{\theta_{k}}}).

(6)

In practice, the leader’s loss
\mathcal{L}_{\text{leader}}
is computed using the current perception parameters
\phi_{k}
(for
f_{\phi_{k}}
) and the control network
Q
evaluated with its recent parameters
\theta_{k}
(denoted
\theta_{\text{bu}}
in our algorithm, representing parameters "before update" from the follower’s perspective). Gradients are then taken with respect to
\phi_{k}
only, effectively treating
\theta_{k}
as constant parameters for the
Q_{\theta_{k}}
evaluation during this step. The use of
\theta_{k}
(or
\theta_{\text{bu}}
) as fixed parameters for
Q
during the computation of
\nabla_{\phi}\mathcal{L}_{\text{leader}}
signifies that the leader optimizes its representation
f_{\phi_{k}}
based on an anticipated, static response from the follower characterized by
\theta_{k}
. This simplification, combined with the timescale separation where the follower performs multiple updates for each leader update (controlled by
K_{\text{leader}}
), allows the system to approximate the Stackelberg equilibrium. Standard target networks
mnih2015human
are also employed for
\phi_{\text{target}}
and
\theta_{\text{target}}
to further stabilize training.

The overall approach, where the leader effectively optimizes based on the follower’s recent state rather than its fully converged optimal response to the current
\phi
, is a common and practical technique in approximating bi-level optimization and has theoretical grounding under certain conditions related to timescale separation
pmlr-v119-fiez20a
;
petrulionyte2024functional
.

acting as the follower, the framework can, in principle, be integrated with other RL paradigms, such as actor-critic methods. The underlying algorithm (Algorithm

) remains largely agnostic to these specific choices.

Conclusion

This work revisited the challenge of effectively integrating representation learning with reinforcement learning. We proposed SCORER, a framework that models the interaction between a perception network (leader) and a control network (follower) as a Stackelberg game. This game-theoretic perspective allows the perception network to learn representations by anticipating the utility for the downstream control task, which is approximated using a practical two-timescale gradient descent algorithm. SCORER consistently demonstrated improvements in sample efficiency and/or stabilized returns compared to standard baselines and a non-anticipatory coupled variant.

Despite these promising results, our future work aims to address the following limitations. The current study primarily focused on value-based methods in relatively discrete, low-dimensional state and action spaces (MinAtar and classic control). The theoretical underpinnings of the two-timescale approximation for the specific Stackelberg game in deep RL, while drawing on existing literature for bi-level optimization, could be further formalized. Additionally, the choice of the leader’s objective function,
\mathcal{L}_{\text{leader}}
, remains an important design consideration; while we explored TD-based metrics, the optimal formulation for
\mathcal{L}_{\text{leader}}
across diverse tasks is an open question.

In addition to extending SCORER to actor-critic algorithms and more complex tasks, further performance gains may be unlocked by investigating more sophisticated Stackelberg equilibrium approximations, potentially with improved estimations of the follower’s response
\theta^{*}(\phi)
, or by exploring a wider array of leader objectives derived from principles like information theory or other self-supervised signals, all framed within the crucial Stackelberg anticipation. A deeper theoretical analysis of SCORER’s convergence and stability properties would also be valuable. We believe SCORER provides a principled and flexible foundation for advancing the co-design of perception and control in integrated deep RL systems, paving the way for more intelligent and efficient learning agents.

The performance and applicability of SCORER in more complex, continuous control environments or with policy gradient methods remain important areas for future investigation.

Appendix D
Objective Functions for Perception and Control Networks

This section provides the explicit formulations for the objective functions used by the control network (follower) and the perception network (leader) within the SCORER framework, as evaluated in our experiments.

D.1
Control Network (Follower) Objective

As detailed in Section
4.1
(Equation (
2
)), the control network
Q_{\theta}
acts as the Stackelberg follower. Its objective is to learn an optimal action-value function by minimizing the Mean Squared Bellman Error (MSBE). Given a batch of transitions
B=\{(s_{j},a_{j},r_{j},s^{\prime}_{j},d_{j})\}_{j=1}^{N_{\text{batch}}}
sampled from the replay buffer
\mathcal{D}
, the follower’s loss,
\mathcal{L}_{\text{follower}}
, is:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{follower}}(\theta,\phi)=\frac{1}{N_{\text{batch}}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\text{batch}}}\left(Y_{j}-Q_{\theta}(f_{\phi}(s_{j}),a_{j})\right)^{2}.

(7)

Here,
f_{\phi}(s_{j})
is the state representation provided by the perception network (leader), and
Q_{\theta}(f_{\phi}(s_{j}),a_{j})
is the Q-value estimate from the control network for the state-action pair
(s_{j},a_{j})
. The target value
Y_{j}
for each transition
j
is computed as:

\displaystyle Y_{j}=r_{j}+\gamma(1-d_{j})\max_{a^{\prime}}Q_{\theta_{\text{target}}}(f_{\phi_{\text{target}}}(s^{\prime}_{j}),a^{\prime}),

(8)

where
Q_{\theta_{\text{target}}}
and
f_{\phi_{\text{target}}}
are the target networks for the control and perception components, respectively. For Double DQN variants, the target
Y_{j}
is computed by first selecting the optimal action
a^{*}_{j,\text{online}}
uses the online control network
Q_{\theta}
:

\displaystyle a^{*}_{j,\text{online}}=\arg\max{a^{\prime}}Q_{\theta}(f_{\phi_{\text{target}}}(s^{\prime}_{j}),a^{\prime}).

Then this action’s value is estimated using the target network
Q_{\theta_{\text{target}}}
:

\displaystyle Y_{j}=r_{j}+\gamma(1-d_{j})Q_{\theta_{target}}(f_{\phi_{target}}(s^{\prime}j),a^{*}_{j,\text{online}}).

The perception network parameters
\phi
(specifically
\overline{f_{\phi}(s_{j})}
as used in Equation (
5
)) are treated as fixed inputs during the optimization of
\mathcal{L}_{\text{follower}}
with respect to
\theta
.

D.2
Perception Network (Leader) Objectives

The perception network
f_{\phi}
acts as the Stackelberg leader, and its objective
\mathcal{L}_{\text{leader}}
is designed to produce representations that facilitate the follower’s learning. In our experiments, we primarily investigated two formulations for
\mathcal{L}_{\text{leader}}
, both computed using the control network parameters
\theta_{\text{bu}}
(parameters before the follower’s current update, see Algorithm
1
Line 10) to embody anticipation:

D.2.1
Minimizing Follower’s Mean Squared Bellman Error (SCORER MSBE)

One direct strategy for the leader is to learn representations that help the follower minimize its own objective. Here, the leader’s loss is identical in form to the follower’s MSBE (Equation (
7
)), but the gradients are taken with respect to the perception parameters
\phi
, anticipating the follower’s state via
\theta_{\text{bu}}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{leader}}^{\text{MSBE}}(\phi,\theta_{\text{bu}})=\frac{1}{N_{\text{batch}}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\text{batch}}}\left(Y_{j}-Q_{\theta_{\text{bu}}}(f_{\phi}(s_{j}),a_{j})\right)^{2}.

(9)

Here,
Y_{j}
is the same Bellman target defined in Equation (
8
) (using target networks
Q_{\theta_{\text{target}}},f_{\phi_{\text{target}}}
), but the Q-values
Q_{\theta_{\text{bu}}}(f_{\phi}(s_{j}),a_{j})
are computed using the (fixed) pre-update follower parameters
\theta_{\text{bu}}
and the current perception network output
f_{\phi}(s_{j})
. The optimization is
\min_{\phi}\mathcal{L}_{\text{leader}}^{\text{MSBE}}
.

D.2.2
Minimizing Bellman Error Variance with Representation Norm Penalty (SCORER Var+RN)

Another strategy for the leader is to promote stability in the follower’s learning signal by minimizing the variance of the Bellman errors, coupled with a penalty to encourage well-scaled representations. For a batch
B
, let
\delta_{j}(\phi,\theta_{\text{bu}})=Y_{j}-Q_{\theta_{\text{bu}}}(f_{\phi}(s_{j}),a_{j})
be the Bellman error for transition
j
, where
Y_{j}
is again the target from Equation (
8
). The leader’s objective is then:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{leader}}^{\text{Var+RN}}(\phi,\theta_{\text{bu}})=\text{Var}_{j\in B}[\delta_{j}(\phi,\theta_{\text{bu}})]+\lambda_{RN}\left(C_{\text{norm}}-\frac{1}{N_{\text{batch}}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\text{batch}}}\|f_{\phi}(s_{j})\|^{2}\right)^{2},

(10)

where
\text{Var}_{j\in B}[\delta_{j}]=\frac{1}{N_{\text{batch}}}\sum_{j\in B}\delta_{j}^{2}-\left(\frac{1}{N_{\text{batch}}}\sum_{j\in B}\delta_{j}\right)^{2}
is the sample variance of the Bellman errors over the batch. The second term is a representation normalization penalty, where
\|f_{\phi}(s_{j})\|^{2}
is the squared L2 norm of the representation for state
s_{j}
,
C_{norm}
is a target norm value (e.g., 1.0 in our experiments), and
\lambda_{RN}
is a weighting coefficient (e.g., 0.1 in our experiments). This objective induces representations that lead to low-variance Bellman targets for the follower while keeping a consistent scale. The optimization is
\min_{\phi}\mathcal{L}_{\text{\text{leader}}}^{\text{Var+RN}}
.

Appendix E
Statistical Significance Calculations

Throughout this work, reported performance metrics aim to provide a robust understanding of algorithm behavior across multiple independent trials. This appendix details the methods used for calculating and presenting these statistics.

E.1
Learning Curves and Confidence Intervals

The learning curves presented show the mean total reward achieved at various training timesteps, averaged over independent runs each initiated with a different random seed. The shaded regions accompanying these mean curves represent a 95% confidence interval (CI). This CI is calculated assuming a normal distribution of the mean returns at each timestep, using the standard error of the mean across all the seeds:

\displaystyle\text{CI}=\text{Mean}\pm 1.96\cdot\frac{\text{Standard Deviation}}{\sqrt{\text{Number of Seeds}}}.

(11)

E.2
Interquartile Mean (IQM) for Final Performance

To illustrate a robust measure of maximum performance that is less sensitive to outliers than a simple mean, particularly across a limited number of seeds, we report the Interquartile Mean (IQM). For each experimental run, we identify the highest average episode return achieved during training. The IQM is then calculated over these maximum returns from the 10 seeds by first sorting the 10 values, removing the bottom 25% (i.e., the lowest 2 or 3 values) and the top 25% (i.e., the highest 2 or 3 values), and then computing the mean of the remaining central 50% of the values. The confidence intervals reported alongside IQM values in the tables are also 95% CIs, calculated using the standard error of these experiments.

E.3
Statistical Significance Testing (Welch’s t-test)

In Table
1
, we highlight those statistically significant performance differences within each base algorithm family (e.g., DQN and its SCORER variants) for each environment. To determine this, we first identify the variant with the highest Interquartile Mean (IQM) in each group. Then, other variants are compared to this top performer using an independent two-sample Welch’s t-test, which is robust to unequal variances as it does not assume they are equal between the groups being compared. Performances are considered
not statistically worse
(and highlighted accordingly in green) if the p-value from the Welch’s t-test comparing their set of of maximum returns to those of the top performer is greater than or equal to a significance level
\alpha=0.05
.

Appendix H
Analysis of Learned Representation Properties

The following metrics, adopted from recent work on investigating RL representations
2
2

2

The property definitions and analysis methodology are inspired by Wang et al., “Investigating the properties of neural network representations in reinforcement learning,”
Artificial Intelligence
, 2024.
, were computed periodically during training (every
10^{5}
time steps) to characterize the learned representations. For these metrics, we used a fixed dataset of
N_{\text{samples}}=1000
transitions
(s_{j},s^{\prime}_{j})
collected by executing a random policy in the Breakout-MinAtar environment. For each agent variant (Baseline DQN, Coupled Non-Stackelberg, SCORER), the relevant representation vectors
\phi(s_{j})
(denoted
\mathbf{z}_{j}
) and associated Q-values
V(s_{j})=\max_{a}Q(f_{\phi}(s_{j}),a)
were extracted using the current network parameters. Let
\mathbf{z}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the
d
-dimensional representation of state
s_{j}
. For a fair comparison when analyzing the Baseline DQN, its
representations
were taken from an intermediate layer of its Q-network analogous in depth to SCORER’s perception output (specifically, the output of the second hidden layer, see Appendix
C
and our code).

Complexity Reduction:
Quantifies how much the representation simplifies the value function learning. Let
d_{V,ij}=|V(\mathbf{z}_{i})-V(\mathbf{z}_{j})|
and
d_{S,ij}=\|\mathbf{z}_{i}-\mathbf{z}_{j}\|_{2}
. We compute
L_{\text{rep}}=\text{\text{avg}}_{i\neq j}(d_{V,ij}/(d_{S,ij}+\epsilon_{\text{div}}))
, where
\epsilon_{\text{div}}=10^{-8}
is a small constant.
L_{\text{max}}
is tracked as the maximum
L_{\text{rep}}
observed so far across all time steps and agents. Complexity Reduction is then
1-(L_{\text{rep}}/(L_{max}+\epsilon_{\text{div}}))
. Higher values indicate greater complexity reduction.

Dynamics Awareness:
This measures if representations of temporally successive states are closer than representations of random state pairs. For each
\mathbf{z}_{j}
(from
s_{j}
) and its successor representation
\mathbf{z}^{\prime}_{j}
(from
s^{\prime}_{j}
), we also sample a random representation
\mathbf{z}_{\text{rand},j}
from the set
\{\mathbf{z}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{\text{samples}}}
. Dynamics Awareness is
\frac{\text{avg}_{j}(\|\mathbf{z}_{j}-\mathbf{z}_{\text{rand},j}\|_{2}-\|\mathbf{z}_{j}-\mathbf{z}^{\prime}_{j}\|_{2})}{(\text{avg}_{j}\|\mathbf{z}_{j}-\mathbf{z}_{\text{rand},j}\|_{2}+\epsilon_{\text{div}})}
. Higher values are better.

Diversity:
Measures if states with different values have different representations. It is
1-\text{Specialization}
, where Specialization is
\text{avg}_{i\neq j}\min(1,(d_{V,ij}/\text{max }d_{V})/(d_{S,ij}/\text{max }d_{S}+\epsilon))
, with
\epsilon=10^{-6}
. Max values are taken over the current sample batch. Higher diversity is generally preferred.

Orthogonality:
Measures the average pairwise orthogonality of representation vectors in the batch. It is calculated as
1-\frac{2}{N_{\text{samples}}(N_{\text{samples}}-1)}\sum_{i<j}(\frac{\mathbf{z}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{z}_{j}}{\|\mathbf{z}_{i}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{z}_{j}\|_{2}+\epsilon_{\text{div}}})^{2}
. Values closer to 1 indicate higher orthogonality.

Sparsity:
The proportion of near-zero activations in the representation vectors. Calculated as the mean fraction of elements in
\mathbf{z}_{j}
whose absolute value is less than a small threshold
\epsilon_{\text{sparsity}}=10^{-10}
. Higher values indicate sparser representations.
