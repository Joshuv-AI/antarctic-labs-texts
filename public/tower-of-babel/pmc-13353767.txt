Simulating the Impacts of Deep Geothermal Development on Shallow Hydrothermal Resources in a Rocky Mountain Rift Valley
Authors: Michael J Rush, Daniel Birdsell, Lauren Foster, Renee Reichenbacher, Parker Rehmus, Tom Armstrong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNumerical modeling has been widely used to assess the feasibility of geothermal energy development at sites across the world, but modeling applications simulating the potential impacts on shallow hydrothermal resources and surface water are relatively scarce. In this study, we apply the MODFLOW 6 groundwater energy (GWE) code to simulate fully coupled groundwater flow and heat transport in a Rocky Mountain rift valley. The site features a moderate temperature hydrothermal system with steeply dipping normal faults, fractures, and shear zones that convey upwelling geothermal water to the shallow subsurface and facilitate interaction between deep geothermal pumping and the shallow subsurface. Following calibration against a set of publicly available well water levels, streamflow observations, well and spring water temperatures, and thermal gradients, the model is used to simulate the impacts of deep geothermal development on surface water supplies and shallow hydrothermal resources, including a hot spring system. The model simulates significant hydrologic and thermal impacts of deep geothermal pumping on the shallow hydrothermal system, including large changes in groundwater levels (−1.2 to +3.1 m), temperatures (−5.6°C to +8.7°C), and groundwater flow to springs (−10.7% to +15.4%). Depletion of tributary groundwater is simulated in three scenarios (0.8‐10.7% of the geothermal extraction rate), demonstrating that deep geothermal pumping can infringe upon surface water rights. Results broadly demonstrate that in rift valley systems, geologic structures conveying upwelling geothermal water can lead to surficial thermal and hydrologic impacts in response to deep geothermal pumping, highlighting the need for regulatory frameworks that integrate geothermal energy and water resources.

Introduction

Geothermal development offers many social and ecological benefits by increasing access to local, renewable, clean, and reliable energy (Shortall et al. 2015) as well as creating jobs and revenue (Tester et al. 2021). However, sustainable use of geothermal energy requires that impacts of geothermal development are effectively managed, including potential conflicts with recreation and tourism due to depletion of geothermal upwelling to natural features such as hot springs and geysers, groundwater contamination that can occur when boreholes connect water supply aquifers with contaminated zones, induced seismicity that may occur as a result of reinjection, as well as noise pollution associated with exploration, drilling, and power plant operation (Shortall et al. 2015; Tester et al. 2021).

While interference between geothermal power development and surface features such as hot springs and geysers has been reported at sites in New Zealand (Glover et al. 2000) and the Philippines (Bolaños and Parrilla 2000), few studies have quantitatively assessed the impacts of deep geothermal development on surficial hydrothermal resources. White and Hunt (2005) used a combination of simple models, including a chloride mixing model and a one‐dimensional Darcy flow model, to interpret the cessation of spring flow and reductions in chloride concentrations that were observed at Geyser Valley, Wairakei, New Zealand following geothermal development. However, no prior study to our knowledge has employed a three‐dimensional, coupled thermo‐hydrologic model to assess potential impacts on surficial hydrothermal resources prior to deep geothermal development.

Geothermal systems can be broadly divided into conduction‐dominated reservoirs (Enhanced Geothermal Systems, EGS, or Hot Dry Rock) and conventional hydrothermal reservoirs. Conventional hydrothermal systems can then be characterized by enthalpy and fluid phase (Ciriaco et al. 2020). The focus of this paper is on “low enthalpy” systems, in which the reservoir fluid is liquid water that does not undergo a phase change. Some of the relevant applications include direct use, aquifer thermal energy storage, and electricity production using binary cycle power plants, the application considered in this study.

In the context of geothermal energy development, numerical modeling efforts typically focus on thermal resource characterization (e.g., Dempsey et al. 2011; Farkhutdinov et al. 2016), technical aspects of reservoir management and system optimization (e.g., Howle et al. 2003; Enedy and Butler 2010; Willems and Nick 2019; Daniilidis et al. 2020; Yu et al. 2021; Birdsell et al. 2024), and geothermal economics and investment risk, for example, Budisulistyo et al. (2017), with the technical models informing the economic models (Wachowicz‐Pyzik et al. 2015). While it is generally accepted that numerical reservoir models should be able to reproduce heat flows associated with surface expressions like hot springs, seeps, and fumaroles (Ciriaco et al. 2020), numerical reservoir models have traditionally not been used to predict the impacts of geothermal development on shallow hydrothermal resources such as hot springs and surface water supplies.

Existing thermo‐hydrologic modeling approaches (Dempsey et al. 2011; O'Sullivan and O'Sullivan 2016; Scheidler et al. 2026) typically employ prescribed temperature and/or hydraulic head conditions at the land surface, which prevents the prediction of transient water level or temperature responses in the shallow subsurface to pumping. To simulate connections between deep geothermal pumping and surficial hydrothermal resources, prescribed heat flux (e.g., Jupp and Schultz 2000) and prescribed water flux conditions are required.

The need for hydraulic, thermal, mechanical, and chemical process representation in geothermal reservoir simulators depends on the nature of the geothermal development. For example, in Enhanced Geothermal Systems that employ high pressure injection to create fracture networks (Lu 2018), geomechanical modeling is generally required to understand the temporal evolution of rock permeability in response to fluid injection. In contrast, conventional geothermal applications may require treatment of only the thermal and hydraulic components of the system, under the assumption of constant rock permeability, especially during the early phases of project development (Mindel et al. 2021).

A wide variety of numerical simulators are available for conventional geothermal applications that require coupled representation of subsurface thermal and hydraulic flow processes, including PFLOTRAN (Lichtner et al. 2015), FEHM (Zyvoloski 2007), TOUGH3 (Jung et al. 2017), SHEMAT (Keller et al. 2020), and MODFLOW 6 (Langevin et al. 2017; Langevin et al. 2022), among many others. Whereas PFLOTRAN, FEHM, TOUGH3, and SHEMAT offer rigorous treatment of thermodynamics and track enthalpy across phase changes, MODFLOW 6 is a promising candidate for low enthalpy geothermal systems in which simulating interactions with regional aquifers and surface water features is important (Zhao et al. 2022; Mahmoud et al. 2024). Although the MODFLOW suite of finite‐difference codes is the most widely used platform for groundwater flow and transport simulations, applications of MODFLOW to geothermal systems are relatively limited, despite recent integration of simulation capabilities and major improvements in ease of use.

Early applications of MODFLOW to geothermal systems often relied on coupling MODFLOW‐2000 and MT3DMS to simulate aquifer thermal responses to borehole heat exchanger (BHE) systems (Alberti et al. 2012; Angelotti et al. 2014). Subsequent efforts utilized MODFLOW‐USG, including the Connected Linear Network (CLN) and Drain Return Flow (DRT) packages, to improve computational efficiency (Antelmi et al. 2021; Barbieri et al. 2022). Early approaches often neglected the thermal dependence of fluid density and viscosity; however, comparative studies against SHEMAT demonstrated that ignoring such relationships can lead to errors in temperature and well efficiency, particularly outside narrow temperature ranges (Hecht‐Méndez et al. 2010; Fossoul et al. 2011; Zeghici et al. 2015). While such approaches may be suitable in special conditions such as narrow temperature ranges or at steady state, the thermal dependence of fluid density and viscosity is a key relationship in the context of geothermal problems, and the state of the art has evolved sufficiently that fully coupled simulations can be performed with minimal computational time and effort.

In recent years, the MODFLOW 6 team at the U.S. Geological Survey (USGS) has integrated the disparate capabilities of previous versions of MODFLOW, including the Newton solver introduced in MODFLOW‐NWT (Niswonger et al. 2011), the unstructured grid capabilities of MODFLOW‐USG (Panday et al. 2013), the solute transport capabilities of MT3DMS (Zheng and Wang 1999), and the coupled, variable‐density flow and transport capabilities of SEAWAT (Langevin et al. 2008) into a unified MODFLOW 6 framework for simulation of variable‐density saturated flow and solute transport, along with a set of Python tools that enable transparent and reproducible model pre‐ and post‐processing and an Application Programming Interface (Langevin et al. 2017; Hughes et al. 2022; Langevin et al. 2022; Hughes et al. 2024). To facilitate the application of MODFLOW 6 solute transport capabilities to heat transport, Morway et al. (2025) have introduced a new groundwater energy (GWE) simulator in MODFLOW 6 that avoids the need for parameter equivalents when applying solute transport to heat transport problems. SEAWAT v4, from which the governing equations of MODFLOW GWE are derived, has been evaluated alongside a suite of other geothermal simulator candidates by Mindel et al. (2021) and was demonstrated to perform reliably well alongside other geothermal simulators, even those with much more sophisticated treatment of thermodynamics (e.g., PFLOTRAN and TOUGH3). Critically, MODFLOW 6 GWE can represent the temperature dependence of fluid properties through the Buoyancy (BUY) and Viscosity (VSC) packages. While MODFLOW 6 GWE cannot track enthalpy and latent heat requirements across phase changes, it is a suitable candidate for geothermal applications in the moderate temperature, low enthalpy regime without vapor phase. The assumption of instantaneous equilibrium between groundwater and the rock matrix limits the extent to which heat flow and storage can be numerically distinguished between those domains. Further details regarding the provenance of MODFLOW GWE governing equations and benchmarking against other geothermal codes are provided in Text S1.

In this study, we develop a three‐dimensional, coupled thermo‐hydrologic numerical model in MODFLOW 6 that simulates the influence of deep geothermal pumping associated with a binary‐cycle power plant on surface water and shallow hydrothermal resources including a hot spring. Unlike previous approaches that employ prescribed head and temperature conditions on the land surface boundary, the model is designed to predict the response of shallow water levels, temperatures, groundwater flow to springs, and streamflow to geothermal pumping using the Streamflow Routing (SFR) package and prescribed heat and water flux conditions imposed on the land surface boundary. We apply the model to a representative Rocky Mountain rift valley featuring a low enthalpy hydrothermal system occupying a complex network of steeply dipping faults and fractures that convey upwelling geothermal water to the shallow subsurface. The model is calibrated in steady state against static water levels, long‐term streamflow averages, well and spring temperatures, and thermal gradient observations. Four geothermal extraction and injection scenarios are used to simulate the effects of deep geothermal pumping on shallow hydrothermal resources over a transient period of 100 years. The influence of hydraulic flow properties is evaluated through a sensitivity analysis, and conceptual model uncertainty is explored through two alternate model configurations. Our study represents the first application of MODFLOW 6 GWE to a complex geothermal system.

Field Site Description

Study Area

The Upper Arkansas valley is an ideal site for simulating connections between deep and shallow hydrothermal systems due to the occurrence of several geothermal springs along the eastern flank of the Sawatch Range, including Poncha Springs, Cottonwood Hot Springs, and Mt. Princeton Hot Springs (MPHS; the focus of this study), as well as recent interest in deep geothermal development: the binary cycle geothermal system considered in this study is based on a project that was proposed by Mt. Princeton Geothermal (MPG) in 2024. Geology

Colorado's Upper Arkansas Valley is a representative rift valley that typifies the syn‐ to post‐Oligocene extensional tectonics common throughout the Basin and Range geologic province. Near Chalk Creek (Figures 1 and 2), the valley features a deep (~2000 m thick) basin fill aquifer (Tdu, Tertiary Dry Union) overlain by a relatively thin mantle of Quaternary Alluvium (<100 m thick). The valley is bounded by Tertiary Igneous Intrusive rocks (Tiu, Mt. Princeton Granite) to the west and Precambrian rocks to the east and is underlain by Precambrian basement rock at a depth of approximately 2000 m. The valley features normal faults on both the west and east sides of the basin that dip away from the mountain fronts, as well as a dextral strike‐slip fault that occurs at a right‐lateral offset in the mountain front and is considered a brittle reactivation of a pre‐Laramide ductile shear zone termed the Chalk Creek Shear Zone (CCSZ). The CCSZ is collocated with both the geothermal development site and MPHS and conveys upwelling geothermal water to MPHS (Richards et al. 2010), thereby facilitating potential interaction between deep geothermal power operations and the shallow hot springs system. Additional geologic background information can be found in Text S2.

Climate and Hydrology

The Upper Arkansas Valley is characterized by a semiarid climate with warm, dry summers and cold winters. Precipitation arrives primarily as snow during the winter and spring in the mountains surrounding the basin, including the Sawatch Range to the west and the Mosquito Range to the east, and snowfall rates increase with elevation. Moderate summertime precipitation occurs as the result of regional monsoonal patterns and localized convective thunderstorms that form as warm, moisture‐laden air rises from the surrounding basins and collides with steep topography. Snowmelt runoff contributes to high spring stream discharges that recharge the valley aquifers along the mountain front; farther from the mountain front, streams tend to serve as groundwater discharge locations and drain the valley aquifers (Watts 2005; Watts et al. 2014). Chalk Creek, a second‐order stream, flows generally west‐to‐east from the mountains on the edge of the basin toward the valley bottom, where it joins the Arkansas River that drains the basin (Figure 1).

Principal Groundwater Aquifers

Three primary groundwater aquifer systems are considered in this study: alluvial, basin fill, and mountain block aquifers. These systems align with the Quaternary Alluvium, Tertiary Dry Union, and Tertiary Igneous Intrusives, respectively, of Barkmann et al. (2017) (refer to Figures 1 and 2). While these aquifer systems generally extend throughout the Upper Arkansas valley, the following definitions for each aquifer system and sources of recharge are specific to the study area. Mountain block recharge is defined as lateral groundwater flow from mountain aquifers to basin aquifers, whereas mountain front recharge refers to mountain runoff that infiltrates along the mountain front through sediments of ephemeral and intermittent stream channels. Deep (>200‐m depth) regions of the basin fill and mountain block aquifers, which are expected to exhibit much lower permeability than the primary shallow regions of the aquifer, are termed “deep basin fill” and “deep mountain block,” respectively.

The alluvial aquifer consists of shallow groundwater occurring in unconsolidated alluvial outwash deposits and varies in thickness between 4 and 60 m. The aquifer receives recharge as stream leakage from Chalk Creek and the Arkansas River, mountain front recharge along the lower slopes of Mt. Princeton and Mt. Antero, flow from upgradient alluvial sediments, and surficial recharge. The aquifer discharges into the Arkansas River and lower reaches of Chalk Creek, and the general direction of flow is west–southwest to east–northeast.

The basin fill aquifer consists of relatively deep groundwater occurring in sedimentary deposits of the Tertiary Dry Union (Tdu), which comprises interbedded alluvial and fluvial deposits with a wide range of grain sizes, including coarse‐grained facies composed of gravels, cobbles, and coarse sands, as well as fine‐grained facies composed of silty and clayey fine sands that are expected to provide localized confinement (Barkmann et al. 2017). The Tertiary Dry Union generally increases in thickness to the north, with depth to Precambrian basement rock thought to occur at approximately 2000 m (6560 ft) in the vicinity of the geothermal development site. The aquifer receives recharge as mountain block recharge from the mountain block aquifer to the west, flow from the overlying alluvial aquifer, and, where the basin fill aquifer is exposed at land surface, a small amount of surficial recharge and stream leakage from Chalk Creek. The aquifer discharges primarily to the alluvial aquifer near the Arkansas River, and the general direction of flow is west–southwest to east–northeast.

The mountain block aquifer consists of groundwater moving through the uppermost weathered and fractured Tertiary Igneous Intrusives (Tiu, Mt. Princeton Granite), talus along Mt. Princeton and Mt. Antero's alpine slopes, and deep networks of faults and fractures. While the Mt. Princeton Granite is generally a low‐permeability rock, connected groundwater flow occurs through the shallow weathered saprolite and mobile regolith that overly the intact granite, as well as through connected fracture networks. The CCSZ on the southeastern edge of Mt. Princeton is a well‐known permeable fracture zone that transmits downwelling recharge and upwelling geothermal water (Colorado School of Mines 2009; Richards et al. 2010). The aquifer receives significant volumes of recharge originating as snowmelt runoff in the winter and spring and transmits flow to the alluvial and basin fill aquifers to the east. As the shallow flow system occurs across complex terrain, the direction of groundwater flow is highly variable and dependent on topography: generally, flow is to the south and east from Mt. Princeton and to the north and east from Mt. Antero.

Geothermal Heat Sources and Structural Controls on Geothermal Upwelling

In the MPHS area, two distinct heat sources are hypothesized to occur, including a western source that underlies the mountain front and an eastern source that underlies the basin. It is not known whether the two sources truly represent distinct structural zones, or whether they originate due to the same underlying crustal thinning processes associated with the rift valley. While some early studies provided support for the existence of an eastern heat source beneath the basin (Arestad 1977; Romero and Fawcett 1978), most research of the 2000s focused primarily on delineating a western source (Colorado School of Mines 2007, 2008, 2009, 2010; Dimick 2007; Richards et al. 2010) recharged by snowmelt runoff from the west, until a 2012 magnetotelluric survey (Henderson 2024; Henderson et al. 2024) renewed interest in the existence of an eastern source, recharged by deep percolation of Arkansas River water.

Geophysical and geochemical surveys during 2007‐2012 and associated interpretations coalesced on a conceptual model for the MPHS area involving a geothermal reservoir at depth along the mountain front that contributes to upwelling geothermal water through the east–west CCSZ (Colorado School of Mines 2007, 2008, 2009, 2010; Dimick 2007; Henderson et al. 2009; Richards et al. 2010; Morgan 2013; Henderson 2024). Additional background regarding those surveys and interpretations can be found in Text S3.

Methods

In this study, we employ a combination of geologic framework modeling and groundwater flow and heat transport modeling to simulate the impacts of deep geothermal development on surface water supplies and other users of shallow hydrothermal resources. The geologic framework model (GFM), constructed using Leapfrog software (Seequent Limited 2020), is used to assign parameter zones and properties to the MODFLOW 6 thermo‐hydrologic flow model (THFM). The primary focus of the study is the development and application of the THFM; development of the Leapfrog‐based GFM is described in Text S4.

THFM Development

A Thermo‐Hydrologic Flow Model (THFM) describing the movement of groundwater and heat is used to simulate the influence of deep geothermal pumping on shallow subsurface water levels, water temperatures, water flow rates, and heat flow rates. The THFM, implemented with the MODFLOW 6 GWF and GWE simulators, accounts for the coupling of fluid density and viscosity with temperature, which governs buoyancy‐driven upwelling and temperature‐dependent hydraulic conductivity. In the context of heat transport, the overall goal of the THFM is to characterize advective transport of heat with groundwater, which occurs at much shorter timescales than conductive heat migration in hydrothermal systems and constitutes the primary mechanism by which deep geothermal development could impact the shallow hot springs system.

THFM Domain

The THFM domain is taken as the study boundary that was defined in the Study Area section, which includes the confluence of Chalk Creek with the Arkansas River, is oriented with respect to the general direction of shallow groundwater flow, extends north and south to surface water divides associated with the Chalk Creek watershed, and is centered on MPHS and the geothermal development site (Figure 1). The domain is discretized using a 100‐m structured grid with 20 vertical layers (see Figures S1 and S2); layers are generally 100 m thick, aside from the two topmost layers that are structured to accommodate variable alluvial aquifer thickness.

As the overall goal of the THFM is to characterize advective transport of heat with groundwater, all model cells that correspond to Precambrian rocks are deactivated because they are not expected to allow advective heat transport due to extremely low permeability and porosity. Although heat conduction occurs through the Precambrian rocks, this process is very slow compared to advective heat transport and does not play a significant role in controlling the impacts of deep geothermal pumping on shallow geothermal resources.

The model temporal discretization consists of a spinup period comprising two stress periods that represent pre‐geothermal development conditions, followed by a 100‐year transient stress period with 1‐year timesteps that represent the period following installation of a binary cycle geothermal system by imposing geothermal extraction and injection on the model. Additional details regarding the model domain, discretization, and spinup strategy can be found in Text S5.

THFM Hydrologic Boundary Conditions

Surficial and mountain front recharge are represented with the MODFLOW 6 Recharge (RCH) package. Surficial and mountain front recharge occur primarily as the result of snowmelt runoff in the high‐elevation regions bounding the valley; accordingly, recharge zones (see Figure S3) are constructed based on regions associated with intermittent and ephemeral stream channels delineated in the National Hydrography Dataset (USGS 2024), with many small zones in the higher elevation areas (18 zones) and fewer large zones in the lower elevation areas (five zones). Recharge rates are varied during calibration, with the high‐elevation zones allowed to vary up to 1.0 m·year−1, and the lower‐elevation zones limited to 10 cm·year−1.

Mountain block recharge, which occurs in the alluvial aquifer from upgradient alluvial sediments along Chalk Creek, as well as the mountain block aquifer along the western boundaries of the model, is represented with the MODFLOW 6 General Head Boundary (GHB) package (see Figures S4 and S5). GHB elevations are assigned through extrapolation of static water level observations, and GHB conductances are varied during calibration, with a low upper bound (0.02 m2·year−1) used to prevent mountain block recharge from dominating other sources of recharge such as stream leakage, mountain front recharge, and surficial recharge. For model scenarios, the calibrated GHB is replaced with an equivalent MODFLOW 6 WEL package to ensure that the GHB cannot serve as an unrealistic source or sink of water.

Groundwater–surface water interaction is simulated using the MODFLOW 6 Streamflow Routing (SFR) package, implemented for Chalk Creek and the Arkansas River, which primarily incise the alluvial aquifer (layer 1, see Figure S4) and, in several reaches, the basin fill aquifer (layer 2, see Figure S5). The SFR package is constructed with the SFRmaker tool (Leaf et al. 2021) and the NHDPlusHR dataset (USGS 2024), with streambed elevations sampled from a 1‐m digital elevation model. The SFR package simulates exchanges between the groundwater and surface water systems according to the head difference between stream water and groundwater, at a rate governed by the streambed hydraulic conductivity. Water is routed downstream based on Manning's equation for a rectangular channel.

THFM Thermal Boundary Conditions

A prescribed heat flux condition (Energy Source Loading Package; ESL) is applied to the alluvial deposits associated with the shallow hydrothermal system where temperature predictions are important (see Figure S6). In areas of the model that are not of interest for predictions of temperature evolution due to geothermal pumping, prescribed temperature conditions (Constant Temperature package; CTP) are applied (see Figures S7 and S8). Additional details regarding the thermal boundary conditions applied at the ground surface can be found in Text S6.

Prescribed energy flux conditions are imposed on the bottom layer of the model to represent heat fluxes associated with the two hypothesized heat sources (see Figure S9), with the strength of the primary sources varied during calibration (400‐1000 mW·m−2). The extent of the western source is assumed to coincide with the CCSZ at depth following Richards et al. (2010), Morgan (2013), Dimick (2007), and Colorado School of Mines (2009), and the eastern extent is varied through a geometrical parameter. The eastern source area is defined following the interpretations presented in Henderson (2024) and Henderson et al. (2024). Wherever the two primary heat sources do not exist, background geothermal heat fluxes are imposed on the bottom layer of the model, with heat source intensity varied during calibration (40‐400 mW·m−2). Zero‐heat‐flux conditions are imposed on all the lateral boundaries of the model.

In summary, the simulated temperature of the shallow hydrothermal system is determined based on the balance between the warming effects of upwelling geothermal water and upward conduction of heat, and the cooling effects of incoming recharge (RCH and GHB packages) and conduction to the atmosphere (ESL package), subject to the heat storage and transfer properties of subsurface materials (EST and CND packages).

Relationships between water temperature and fluid properties (density and viscosity) are represented through the MODFLOW 6 Buoyancy (BUY) and Viscosity (VSC) packages. The Buoyancy (BUY) package is populated with a linear relationship between water temperature and density (between 1000 kg·m−3 at 4°C and 958.65 kg·m−3 at 100°C), and the Viscosity (VSC) package is populated with the non‐linear viscosity formulation and package defaults.

THFM Calibration

The overall goal of the calibration is to achieve a close match to a hydrologic targets (static water levels and long‐term streamflow discharge averages) and thermal targets (thermal gradients, hot well and hot spring temperatures) as shown in Figure 3. A realistic simulated water budget was enforced through physically based limits on model inflows (maximum recharge rate of 1 m·year−1; low upper bound on GHB conductance, 0.02 m2·year−1) and direct comparisons between outflows and observations (SFR streamflow discharge compared to gage flows). Higher weights have been applied to the thermal targets to ensure that the abundance of groundwater elevation targets does not dominate the calibration. Additional description of the calibration targets can be found in Text S7.

Locations of hydrologic and thermal targets including stream gages, wells with static water level observations screened in the alluvial aquifer (model layer 1) and basin fill aquifers (model layer 2), thermal gradient boreholes, wells with temperature observations, and hot springs.

Calibration was conducted on the pre‐development steady state period using PEST_HP software (Doherty and Hunt 2010; Doherty 2015). Material properties, including hydraulic conductivity, porosity, and thermal conductivity, are assigned zonally, whereas boundary parameters, including heat source intensities and geometries, recharge rates for low‐ and high‐elevation zones (see Figure S3), and streambed hydraulic conductivities are assigned to the relevant boundaries (see Figures S4 and S5). A single streambed hydraulic conductivity value is prescribed to the Arkansas River and six parameters are used for Chalk Creek to represent reaches that are expected to exhibit distinct aquifer interactions, following Watts et al. (2014). Four GHB conductances are used for the mountain block aquifer GHB, and one conductance is used for the alluvial aquifer GHB (refer to Figures S4 and S5). Additional discussion of calibrated parameters and zones can be found in Text S8.

The CCSZ is represented as a permeable flow pathway, and the calibrated hydraulic conductivity is permitted to vary up to 300 ft day−1 (though the final calibrated value is substantially lower, 42 ft day−1, see Table S1). The CCSZ is modeled as a porous medium rather than as a discrete fracture network, which would require advanced tools (Hyman et al. 2015; Birdsell et al. 2018) that do not offer the same groundwater–surface water capabilities as MODFLOW 6.

SFR inflow multipliers are applied to the long‐term average gage discharge to estimate the upstream inflow for each stream, with the Arkansas River treated as a gaining stream (multiplier 0.8‐1.0) and Chalk Creek treated as net losing across the study area (multiplier 1.0‐1.2), which ensures that model predictions of groundwater discharge to Chalk Creek are conservative. Through this inflow parameterization scheme, good performance of the surface water calibration is guaranteed, as long as a realistic quantity of water enters the model as surficial recharge, mountain front recharge, and mountain block recharge.

Hydraulic storage properties were fixed at relatively high values to ensure that simulated impacts of geothermal pumping are conservative. Geothermal wells are permitted to locally draw from or contribute to groundwater storage, ensuring that they do not unduly draw water from boundaries (e.g., surface water). Specific yield was assigned following Barkmann et al. (2017): 0.34 for the alluvial aquifer and 0.21 for the basin fill and mountain block aquifers. Specific storage was fixed at 0.0001 m−1.

Geothermal Pumping Scenarios

Following model calibration, four geothermal pumping scenarios are used to explore the effects of deep geothermal development on shallow surface water and hydrothermal resources (Figure 4). An extraction well is sited at the geothermal development site, and an injection well is sited to the north, south, east, or west, leading to four injection scenarios. Both extraction and injection wells pump from a depth interval of 1000‐1900 m below land surface following Henderson et al. (2024), except for Injection West, in which the injection interval is adjusted to 1000‐1500 m to avoid the lower‐permeability Mt. Princeton Granite (Tiu, Tertiary Igneous Intrusives) in the deeper 1500‐1900 m interval. Injecting into the deep Mt. Princeton Granite is not feasible in practice and would not achieve the intended effect of recharging the geothermal system. The same quantity of water is injected, only over a smaller depth interval. Injection well sites are oriented with respect to the general direction of flow rather than precise cardinal directions so that the influence of the injection well site can be analyzed in the context of broader groundwater flow. Additional details regarding the implementation of geothermal pumping can be found in Text S9.

Injection well locations for four geothermal pumping scenarios, Chalk Creek Shear Zone from Richards et al. (2010), and major stream channels from NHDPlusHR (USGS 2024).

Well spacing is set to 1300 m and a pumping rate of 1500 gal per minute is assigned to both extraction and injection wells based on a review of hydrothermal systems with binary cycle power plants (Sonnelitter et al. 2000; Howle et al. 2003; Diaz et al. 2016). Note that binary cycle power plants tend to target low enthalpy, liquid systems, and therefore the flow rates are larger, per kilowatt of electricity generation, than in higher enthalpy systems containing both vapor and liquid (Diaz et al. 2016). In these scenarios, all extracted water is reinjected, leading to no net loss of water in the system; however, impacts to groundwater and surface water systems can still occur due to the spacing between extraction and injection wells.

Uncertainty in the model scenario results due to uncertainty in hydraulic flow properties is characterized by comparing results with the calibrated parameter set to results with simulations that include factor‐of‐1.5 perturbations to the hydraulic conductivity value for the key hydrogeologic unit (deep basin fill) that controls the influence of deep geothermal development on shallow hydrothermal resources. This simplified approach identifies the relative influence of key hydraulic flow property values on model scenario results.

Exploration of Conceptual Model Uncertainty

Building on the parameter sensitivity approach outlined in the previous section, two model configurations that represent alternate conceptualizations and parameterizations of the deep subsurface are used to explore predictive uncertainty that may arise due to an incomplete conceptual understanding of deep geologic structures. The alternate model configurations include (a) explicit representation of the Sawatch Fault breccia zones (see Figure S10) and (b) shallower Precambrian basement rock (see Figure S11). Both represent departures from the baseline model and were therefore recalibrated.

One source of uncertainty in the deep subsurface is the extent to which the Sawatch Fault breccia zones control vertical and lateral migration of heat and water. The breccia of the Sawatch faults could, in principle, support upwelling geothermal water; however, the lack of surficial expressions (e.g., hot springs, seeps) along the mountain front suggests that the shallow fault breccia zones are not sufficiently permeable to allow for connected flow of geothermal water to the shallow subsurface. As very little is known about the permeability and thermal characteristics of the deep breccia zones, the baseline model setup does not treat them separately; rather, model cells corresponding to the breccia are assigned based on their proximity to either the adjacent deep basin fill or mountain block. The influence of the Sawatch Fault breccia is explored through an alternate model configuration in which hydrologic and thermal parameters are explicitly prescribed to the breccia and calibrated alongside the remaining parameters. The same parameter values (thermal conductivity, porosity, and hydraulic conductivity in three dimensions) are prescribed to the North and South Sawatch Fault breccia zones (see Figure S10).

Depth to low‐permeability Precambrian basement rock is expected to influence interactions between geothermal pumping and the faults and fractures leading to the shallow subsurface, and is uncertain due to the inversion process associated with geophysical survey interpretation. As discussed in the Principal Groundwater Aquifers section, depth to basement is thought to occur at approximately 2000 m (6560 ft) in the vicinity of the geothermal development site, based on interpretations of a magnetotelluric survey conducted in 2012 (Henderson 2024; Henderson et al. 2024). When these interpretations, which likely represent the maximum depth to basement, are excluded from the GFM (see Text S4 for additional details), interpolation of the remaining geophysical survey interpretations and cross sections place depth to basement at approximately 1700 m (5580 ft).

The influence of depth to basement is explored through an alternate model configuration that employs a GFM with shallower depth to basement (~1700 m compared to ~2000 m in the baseline assessment). In this setup, the extraction and injection intervals are reduced by 300 m under the assumption that drilling geothermal wells into the Precambrian basement would not be economical, leading to greater pumping from the 1000 to 1600 m interval such that the extraction rate is consistent with the other model setups. For the Injection South scenario, the injection interval is further reduced by 200 m to avoid the Precambrian basement, which generally slopes upward from north to south. Similarly, the deep subsurface heat sources that are prescribed at the top of basement rock are elevated by approximately 300 m.

Calibration Performance

Water level calibration performance (see Figures S12 and S13) is very good (Pearson Correlation 0.97; Spearman Correlation 0.97; Nash‐Sutcliffe Coefficient 0.94; and Scaled RMSE 5.9%), with alluvial wells generally exhibiting lower residuals than the basin fill wells. Water levels in a hot well adjacent to the MPHS are matched very well, along with the surrounding alluvium wells. Figure S13 shows a group of wells with higher residuals (model overestimates water level) alongside many wells that are matched well; such discrepancies reflect the extended period (1956‐2015) over which the static water level observations are taken and the unique hydrologic conditions during which the water level observation was taken. As the goal of the model is to characterize pre‐development steady state conditions, such tradeoffs are expected. For basin fill wells, there are several outliers corresponding to instances in which the model significantly overestimates water levels (by >200 ft) for water level observations between 7800 and 8200 ft, due to steep topography (such as the Chalk Cliffs area and mountain front areas) that cannot be replicated in a 100‐m grid. Ultimately, the steady‐state period provides a very good representation of the spatial variability in water levels across both the alluvial and basin fill aquifer systems for pre‐development conditions.

Simulated streamflow (see Figure S14) matches observations almost exactly (−0.08% error for Chalk Creek and <0.0% error for the Arkansas River). Compared to the long‐term variability in streamflow, these differences are negligible. In the model, both waterways serve as discharge locations for the groundwater system; the Arkansas River is net gaining, whereas Chalk Creek has gaining and losing reaches along its path, with no overall net gain or loss. The excellent performance of both the streamflow calibration (see Figure S14) and the groundwater level calibration (see Figures S12 and S13) provides a strong foundation for subsequent groundwater–surface water interaction analyses.

The temperature calibration (see Figure S15) is very strong (Pearson Correlation 0.95; Spearman Correlation 0.9), particularly for hot springs and hot wells in the range 35‐75°C, although the model slightly underestimates temperatures exceeding 75°C. Figures S15 and S17 show two outliers corresponding to instances in which the model overestimates temperatures—one for a cold well and the other for a hot spring. The cold well suggests that cold stream leakage from Chalk Creek may be underestimated along the southwestern extent of the MPHS area. The hot spring (~44°C) is located among a group of other hot springs whose temperatures are matched very well (60‐75°C), suggesting that the lower‐temperature spring is influenced locally by mixing with cold recharge or Chalk Creek stream leakage to a greater extent than is represented in the model. Overall, the temperature calibration provides confidence that the model realistically simulates hot springs temperatures in the MPHS area.

The thermal gradient calibration (see Figure S16) is generally acceptable (Pearson Correlation 0.84; Spearman Correlation 0.52), with two clear groups of thermal gradients: one group of many low thermal gradients (10‐50°C per km) and another of five higher thermal gradients (50‐160°C per 100 km). Several of these high gradient targets are slightly underestimated by the model (by 20‐55°C per km). One of these occurs to the south of Chalk Creek, whereas the other two are north of MPHS along the mountain front, suggesting that the northern and southern extent of the deep geothermal heat source may be slightly underestimated, or that the exclusion of deep Precambrian rocks leads to an underestimation of conduction to the north and south of MPHS. However, two other thermal gradients to the north of MPHS are matched well, alongside a third that is overestimated, suggesting a greater degree of heterogeneity in subsurface thermal properties than is represented in the model calibration. Ultimately, the model captures the key spatial trends and matches thermal gradients well in the vicinity of the CCSZ, which is important for understanding the impacts of deep geothermal pumping on MPHS.

As expected, the hydrologic calibration outperformed the thermal calibration due to the relative ease of simulating groundwater flow compared to heat transport as well as thermal simplifications in the GWE framework (assumption of instantaneous equilibrium between groundwater and the rock matrix). Improvements could likely be achieved through additional thermal conductivity and porosity zones along the mountain front, or by relaxing the bounds on the prescribed upstream flow rate for Chalk Creek, which would permit the groundwater system to discharge more water to Chalk Creek. However, as it is currently parameterized, the model meets the objectives of providing a reasonable representation of coupled thermal and hydrologic flows with a small number of parameters in a manner that honors the conceptual model.

Influence of Geothermal Pumping on Groundwater Budget

Before geothermal development, mountain block, mountain front, and surficial recharge is balanced by discharge to streams (Figure 5). Following geothermal development, extraction and injection are imposed on the system. Although all extracted water is reinjected, changes in the other components of the groundwater budget occur due to the large spacing between the wells (1300 m) and the high pumping rates required for a binary cycle system (1500 gal per minute). Specifically, discharge to Chalk Creek is reduced in the North, East, and South Injection scenarios (i.e., baseflow depletion), whereas discharge to Chalk Creek increases in the Injection West scenario.

These differences can be understood through the positions of the geothermal pumping wells with respect to the orientation of the CCSZ that connects the deep and shallow subsurface (refer to Figure 4), as well as the calibrated hydraulic conductivity of the deep basin fill sediments, which is higher in the N‐S direction than the E‐W direction (see Table S1). The hydraulic influence of geothermal pumping for the top of the pumping interval is presented in Figures S19 to S22. In the Injection East and North scenarios (see Figures S19 and S20), the injection wells are sited on the far side of the extraction well from the western reaches of Chalk Creek that are exposed to the influence of the CCSZ. Even though the injection wells provide water, the extraction well still exerts pressure on the CCSZ, reducing discharge to Chalk Creek.

In the Injection North, South, and East scenarios, extraction pulls water from incompressible storage and draws down the aquifer, whereas injection contributes water to compressible storage and expands the aquifer. Conversely, in the Injection West scenario, extraction pulls water from compressible storage and compresses the aquifer, and injection contributes water to incompressible storage and augments the aquifer. Overall, Figure 5 illustrates that the large well spacing and high pumping rates required for a binary cycle geothermal plant, combined with subsurface structures like the CCSZ that connect the deep and shallow subsurface, can lead to depletion of baseflow.

Baseflow Depletion

Baseflow depletion (Figure 6) is calculated as the change in groundwater discharge to streams following geothermal development, expressed as a percent of the geothermal extraction rate. This quantity represents the extent to which geothermal extraction offsets baseflow, a critical metric for water rights administration. Specifically, Colorado statute C.R.S. 37‐90‐103(10.5) defines “non‐tributary” groundwater as “groundwater, … the withdrawal of which will not, within one hundred years of continuous withdrawal, deplete the flow of a natural stream, including a natural stream…at an annual rate greater than one‐tenth of one percent of the annual rate of withdrawal” (State of Colorado 2025). Tributary groundwater is important because it is regulated under the prior appropriation system used for surface water. Figure 6 includes the 0.1% threshold so that results can be understood within the broader context of water rights regulations.

Three scenarios lead to depletion of Chalk Creek tributary groundwater: Injection North (5.3%), South (0.8%), and East (10.7%). The Arkansas River does not experience depletion of tributary groundwater in any scenario due to the lack of permeable structures that connect the deep subsurface to the shallow fluvial sediments of the Arkansas River. No depletion of Chalk Creek tributary groundwater is simulated in the Injection West scenario due to shallow injection that replenishes the shallow subsurface and leads to increased discharge to Chalk Creek.

Like the previous section, the trends in Figure 6 can generally be understood through the positions of wells with respect to the orientation of the CCSZ that connects the deep and shallow subsurface (refer to Figure 4 and Figures S19 to S22) as well as the calibrated hydraulic conductivity of the deep basin fill sediments, which is higher in the N‐S direction than the E‐W direction (Table S1). In most cases (Injection North, East, and South), Chalk Creek is influenced more strongly by extraction than injection. In contrast, in Injection West, the shallower injection leads to replenishment of the shallow system and Chalk Creek, offsetting any potential baseflow depletion caused by deep extraction. Injection South may be expected to behave similarly to Injection West, since in both cases the injection well is closer to Chalk Creek and MPHS than the extraction well. However, the injection well in Injection South is connected to the extraction well by way of a preferential N‐S flow pathway, through which the injection well efficiently supplies the majority of the extracted water and limits the quantity of water that must be pulled from the CCSZ (see Figure S21).

Impacts of Geothermal Pumping on Shallow Geothermal Resources

Geothermal pumping impacts on hot springs water levels, groundwater temperatures, and groundwater flow rates to springs (also referred to as spring flow) are summarized in Figure 7 and Table 1, which present the most severe simulated impact across the springs. “Severe” denotes any deviation from pre‐development conditions, as any change is assumed to be deleterious to the shallow hydrothermal system. Considering changes in spring flow rates is important because, for example, a large reduction in flow rate, for even a small change in temperature, ultimately reduces the overall quantity of hot water produced by the springs. The projected impacts are simulated at an annual timestep, and do not consider seasonal hydrologic or thermal variation; thus, impacts could be more or less severe on a seasonal basis.

At the end of the 100‐year period following the onset of geothermal pumping, MPHS temperature impacts range from −5.6°C (Injection East) to +7.8°C (Injection West), water level impacts range from reductions of 1.2 m (Injection East) to increases of 1.4 m (Injection West), and spring flow rate impacts range from reductions of 10.7% (Injection East) to increases of 15.4% (Injection West). Results are derived by subtracting initial water levels, temperatures, and flow rates from the transient output in each scenario, with uncertainty explored through factor‐of‐1.5 perturbations applied to the deep basin fill hydraulic conductivity.

Like the previous sections, the trends in Figure 7 are governed by well positions with respect to the CCSZ that connects the deep and shallow subsurface (refer to Figure 4 and Figures S19 to S22), and the N‐S preference in the calibrated hydraulic conductivity of the deep basin fill (see Table S1). Scenarios in which the geothermal injection well is located far from MPHS (Injection East and North) lead to significant reductions in groundwater level, temperature, and flow rate because the cone of depression of the extraction well extends toward MPHS by way of the CCSZ and reduces geothermal upwelling to MPHS. Conversely, significant increases in groundwater level, temperature, and flow rate are simulated in the Injection West scenario because the injection well is closer to MPHS than the extraction well and injection occurs at relatively shallow depths compared to the other scenarios (1000‐1500 m) and near mountain front heat sources, leading to enhanced geothermal upwelling to MPHS. In the Injection South scenario, a preferential N‐S flow pathway allows the injection well to efficiently supply the extraction well, balancing the pressures exerted on the CCSZ (see Figure S21) and minimizing shallow impacts.

MPHS impacts in the Injection East scenario demonstrate the greatest sensitivity to the deep basin fill hydraulic conductivity because it is higher in the N‐S direction than the E‐W direction. Accordingly, small increments in this parameter lead to large changes in the N‐S connection between the extraction well and the CCSZ, without increasing the ability of the injection well (to the East) to recharge the geothermal system. For Injection North, increasing N‐S hydraulic conductivity enhances the connection between the extraction well and the CCSZ (similar to Injection East), but also enhances the ability of the injection well (to the north) to recharge the geothermal system, minimizing the net effects of the parameter change. MPHS impacts in Injection West demonstrate lower sensitivity because the injection well is located close to MPHS. MPHS impacts in Injection South show very low sensitivity to deep basin fill hydraulic conductivity because changing the bulk hydraulic conductivity leaves the N‐S preference intact, minimizing any effect of the parameter adjustment on MPHS impacts.

Influence of Conceptual Model Uncertainty on Simulated Impacts

In this section, we present results from two model configurations that represent alternate conceptualizations and parameterizations of the deep subsurface: explicit representation of the Sawatch Fault breccia zones (see Figure S10) and shallower Precambrian basement rock (see Figure S11). Both model configurations achieve a similar hydrologic calibration to the baseline model setup, with marginal differences in the thermal calibration (see Figures S23 to S26). For the breccia model, the temperature calibration improves slightly (Pearson R +0.01) whereas the thermal gradient calibration worsens slightly (Pearson R −0.01; Spearman ρ −0.03). For the shallow basement model, the temperature calibration improves slightly (Pearson's R +0.01; Spearman ρ +0.04) and the thermal gradient calibration improves slightly (Spearman ρ +0.04). Given that these differences are statistically indistinguishable and reflect the inherent non‐uniqueness of the numerical solution, the baseline model remains the most plausible configuration because it honors all the available geophysical survey data (whereas the shallow basement model does not). Nonetheless, these models serve as a robust representation of deep geologic structural uncertainty.

Like the previous section, geothermal pumping impacts on hot springs water levels, groundwater temperatures, and groundwater flow rates to springs (also referred to as spring flow) are summarized in Figure 8 and Table 2, which present the most severe simulated impact across the springs. Simulated baseflow depletion is presented in Figures S27 and S28 (compare to Figure 6).

Overall, the trends in the alternate model setups mirror the baseline results, with increases in groundwater levels, flow rates, and temperatures in the Injection West scenario and decreases in the remaining scenarios (Figure 8 and Table 2). Simulated impacts for the breccia model are generally comparable to or slightly less than the baseline. In the Injection East scenario, for example, simulated changes in temperature (−3.8°C compared to −4.3°C) and flow rate (−7.4% compared to −8.8%) are slightly lower than baseline, whereas water level changes are consistent (−0.9 m for both). Minor exceptions include slightly higher temperature impacts for Injection South (−0.4°C compared to −0.3°C) and water level impacts for Injection West (+1.6 m compared to +1.4 m). Compared to the downgradient deep basin fill, the calibrated breccia permeability is greater in the E‐W and vertical directions but consistent in the N‐S direction (perpendicular to shallow flow). Consequently, simulation of the breccia does not provide the CCSZ any additional connectivity with the geothermal pumping or the heat source to the north, and the slight reductions in shallow impacts can be attributed to greater connectivity in the vertical and E‐W directions that distributes pumping impacts more broadly throughout the system.

For the shallow basement model, simulated impacts are reduced in most cases (Figure 8 and Table 2). In the Injection East scenario, for example, simulated changes in temperature (−1.0°C compared to −4.3°C), water level (−0.3 m compared to −0.9 m), and flow rate (−1.6% compared to −8.8%) are less pronounced. In contrast, the Injection West scenario shows higher temperature impacts (+8.7°C compared to +7.8°C) and water level impacts (+3.1 m compared to +1.4 m). Compared to the baseline model, a shallower basement increases the distance between the CCSZ and geothermal pumping because the CCSZ approaches the pumping interval with increasing depth (see Figure S11) and elevates the western heat source from a depth of ~2000 to ~1700 m, localizing it to the CCSZ and the MPHS area. These changes effectively attenuate connectivity between geothermal pumping, the CCSZ, and the shallow subsurface for scenarios in which the CCSZ is exposed primarily to extraction (North and East). Conversely, for Injection West, in which the CCSZ is exposed primarily to injection, the shallower heat source leads to enhanced upwelling, indicated by the increases in temperature and water level. Changes in spring flow for Injection West (+13.0%) are slightly less than the baseline (+14.7%) due to calibrated parameter differences between the two models.

Trends in the depletion of tributary groundwater for the alternate model setups (see Figures S27 and S28) remain consistent with the baseline (Figure 6). For all three configurations, depletion of Chalk Creek tributary groundwater is simulated in the Injection North, South, and East scenarios (Table 3). Generally, no depletion of Arkansas River tributary groundwater is simulated, except for the Injection North scenario with the shallow basement model, in which baseflow depletion marginally exceeds the threshold (−0.11%). Baseflow depletion rates are slightly lower for the breccia model and shallow basement models compared to the baseline. For example, in the Injection East scenario, baseflow depletion is 10.7% of the geothermal withdrawal rate in the baseline setup, 9.1% in the breccia model, and 3.3% in the shallow basement setup.

Ultimately, the alternate deep hydrogeologic conceptualizations and model setups explored in this section confirm the trends of the baseline model. Both suggest that depth to Precambrian basement rock and, to a lesser extent, the Sawatch Fault breccia zones play an auxiliary role alongside the CCSZ in modulating shallow impacts of deep geothermal pumping. Patterns in hot springs impacts remained consistent across configurations despite differences in magnitudes: specifically, increased upwelling when the CCSZ is exposed to injection and reduced upwelling when the CCSZ is exposed to extraction. Such consistency demonstrates that the orientation of geothermal wells relative to permeable flow pathways is a primary control on shallow impacts. Similarly, depletion of tributary groundwater occurred in the same injection scenarios across all three conceptualizations, providing additional confirmation that deep geothermal pumping can infringe upon surface water rights.

Conclusion

This study provides the first application of MODFLOW 6 GWE to a complex geothermal system. A coupled thermo‐hydrologic model was applied to a representative Rocky Mountain rift valley to assess the impacts of deep geothermal development on shallow hydrothermal resources and surface water. We have demonstrated that in rift valley settings, permeable geologic structures that convey upwelling geothermal water to the shallow subsurface can also serve as pathways for interactions between deep geothermal pumping and shallow hydrothermal resources. Such connections have major implications for groundwater and surface water resources, water rights administration, and regulation of geothermal development in arid regions.

For open‐loop, binary‐cycle systems, deep geothermal pumping can induce baseflow depletion, water table changes, and temperature shifts in shallow geothermal resources such as hot springs. Such impacts are driven by the large well spacing and high pumping rates typically required for binary‐cycle systems, as well as faults and fractures (CCSZ) that connect the deep and shallow subsurface. Simulated hot springs impacts include significant changes in groundwater levels (−1.2 m to +3.1 m), temperatures (−5.6°C to +8.7°C), and groundwater flow rates to springs (−10.7% to +15.4%). A combination of these impacts, such as reductions in both spring flow rates and temperature, would significantly reduce the overall quantity of hot water. For scenarios in which permeable flow pathways were exposed primarily to extraction, reduced upwelling led to a decline in groundwater temperatures, water levels, and flow rates. Conversely, exposure of permeable pathways to injection yielded a corresponding increase in all three metrics.

Regional surface water impacts include depletion of Chalk Creek tributary groundwater in three scenarios (0.8‐10.7% of the geothermal pumping rate). The Arkansas River remains unaffected due to the lack of permeable structures that connect the deep subsurface to the fluvial sediments of the Arkansas River. No depletion of tributary groundwater was simulated in the Injection West scenario due to the proximity of injection to Chalk Creek, and the relatively shallow injection interval required to avoid the underlying low‐permeability granite.

We have provided a simple characterization of uncertainty in model predictions due to uncertainty in (a) geothermal injection well location and (b) hydraulic flow properties, as well as an exploration of conceptual uncertainty that arises due to an incomplete understanding of deep geologic structures. These analyses suggest that beyond the primary control exerted by the CCSZ, depth to Precambrian basement rock and the breccia zones associated with the valley bounding normal faults play a secondary role in modulating shallow impacts of deep geothermal pumping. Additional sources of uncertainty not presented in this study are the spatial extent and intensity of deep subsurface heat sources, which were constrained through a combination of calibration and geophysical survey interpretation. Furthermore, engineering decisions, such as the well spacing and flow rate, were informed by analogous binary‐cycle systems.

MODFLOW 6 GWE proved to be a capable simulator for a low enthalpy geothermal application. Unlike existing modeling approaches that employ Dirichlet conditions (prescribed temperature and prescribed hydraulic head) at the land surface, we have employed Neumann conditions (prescribed water and heat flux) that allow the model to predict the response of surficial water levels, temperatures, and flow rates to geothermal pumping. Furthermore, the Streamflow Routing (SFR) package, a type of head‐dependent boundary condition, was used to simulate hydrologic connections between deep geothermal pumping and surface water, a capability unique to MODFLOW. The framework that we have presented in this study, while limited to a low enthalpy liquid phase application, can be readily extended to multi‐phase geothermal systems by coupling MODFLOW‐6 GWE to one of many platforms with rigorous thermodynamic treatment of phase change and simulation of enthalpy transport.

Geothermal energy is a vital component of future energy portfolios. Adoption of geothermal technologies should be paired with simple groundwater impact assessments that provide confidence that existing cultural, ecological, social, and economic resources are protected. As computational sciences advance in complexity, it is important to prioritize efficient assessment tools that identify key project impacts and challenges early on to isolate the most promising sites, technologies, and strategies, and ultimately facilitate the rapid adoption of renewable energy technologies alongside the protection of groundwater resources.
