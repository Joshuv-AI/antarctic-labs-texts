From force to resorption: mechanotransduction, osteoclasts, prevention
Authors: Fulong Shen, Xiaohui Xia, Yineng Han, Mengfei Yu, Wanghui Ding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Orthodontically induced root resorption (OIRR) is a frequent and often irreversible complication of orthodontic treatment, characterized by the pathological loss of hard root tissue under sustained mechanical loading. The risk and severity of OIRR reflect the interplay between mechanical variables (force magnitude, direction, and duration) and patient-specific biological susceptibility. Osteoclasts and osteoclast-like cells on the root surface are the principal effectors of pathological root loss and act in concert with periodontal ligament cells, cementoblasts, osteocytes, and immune populations to generate a local pro-resorptive microenvironment. Mechanical signals are transduced into biochemical cues that engage canonical osteoclastogenesis pathways (RANK/RANKL/osteoprotegerin, MAPK, nuclear factor-κB), and modulatory processes including epigenetic regulation, autophagy/apoptosis balance, reactive oxygen species dynamics, and extracellular matrix remodeling. Systemic factors (hormonal and metabolic status and genetic predisposition) further modify individual susceptibility and therapeutic responses. This review synthesizes current knowledge on the molecular and cellular cascades linking orthodontic force to osteoclastic activation and root resorption and examines how intercellular communication shapes spatial and temporal patterns of tissue breakdown. We critically appraise emerging diagnostics for the early detection of root resorption and evaluate translational therapeutic strategies, including the local delivery of osteoclast inhibitors and biomaterial-based carriers for gene- and cell-based approaches. These strategies aim to selectively suppress pathological root resorption while preserving the alveolar bone remodeling necessary for efficient tooth movement. Taken together, this review discusses the key cellular and molecular regulatory networks involved in OIRR with osteoclasts as the central focus, and outlines future directions, including multi-omics approaches to resolve cellular subpopulation dynamics and the development of local delivery systems targeting osteoclasts.Graphical Abstract

Introduction

OIRR is the pathological and irreversible loss of cementum and dentin from tooth roots caused by biomechanical forces applied during orthodontic treatment [1]. Epidemiological and imaging studies indicate that a substantial proportion of treated patients exhibit some degree of radiographically detectable root loss, with prevalence estimates in modern series ranging widely (commonly > 60% and up to approximately 90%, depending on the imaging modality and thresholds), whereas moderate to severe resorption affects a smaller but clinically important subset [2]. Maxillary incisors, particularly those subjected to intrusive or retractive vectors, are disproportionately vulnerable to focalized stress in the apical region [3].

Clinically, mild OIRR is often asymptomatic and discovered incidentally; moderate-to-severe resorption may compromise root length and morphology, impair long-term stability and anchorage, increase mobility, and complicate retention [4]. Traditional two-dimensional radiographs provide linear estimations of apical shortening, whereas cone-beam computed tomography enables volumetric quantification and 3D mapping of the lacunae, improving sensitivity and permitting longitudinal monitoring [5]. Despite improvements in appliance design and force protocols, preventive options remain limited. Approaches such as reducing force magnitude, using intermittent force regimens, expanding PDL load distribution, and adjunctive photobiomodulation have shown partial benefits but do not eliminate risk [6]. Accordingly, a mechanistic understanding of osteoclast biology and the periradicular microenvironment under orthodontic stress is essential for the development of targeted root-sparing therapies.

Orthodontic tooth movement relies on physiological alveolar bone remodeling, whereas OIRR represents pathological destruction of the root surface. Alveolar bone remodeling can occur entirely in the absence of root surface tissue loss; conversely, OIRR typically requires a series of root surface-specific preconditions, including injury or apoptosis of cementoblasts or cementocytes, focal disruption of the unmineralized precementum/cementoblast protective layer, exposure of mineralized cementum or dentin, and subsequent recruitment and activation of osteoclast-like/odontoclast-like cells. Thus, not all mechanisms that promote alveolar bone resorption equally promote root resorption; ideally, therapeutic strategies should selectively suppress pathological root surface resorption without completely blocking the bone turnover necessary for tooth movement [7, 8].

Based on the above biological distinction, osteoclasts and osteoclast-like/odontoclast-like cells are the terminal effector cells responsible for the removal of mineralized root surface tissues in OIRR, and their aberrant differentiation, maturation, and activation constitute key steps in the initiation and persistence of root resorption. Compared with root surface-protective or reparative cells such as cementoblasts, the more clinically translatable intervention strategies at present remain focused primarily on suppressing pathological osteoclast activation, because osteoclast-related pathways, including RANKL/RANK/OPG, MAPK, and NF-κB signaling, have been relatively well characterized, and a certain body of interventional experience has already been accumulated in osteoclast-related skeletal disorders such as osteoporosis. By contrast, although cementoblast-mediated root surface repair has biological potential, mature, stable, and clinically actionable strategies for its direct regulation are still lacking. Accordingly, this review focuses on the osteoclast lineage and systematically summarizes its mechanotransduction, differentiation processes, molecular regulatory networks, and intercellular communication mechanisms under orthodontic force, with the aim of identifying clearer, more precise, and more clinically actionable biological targets for the prevention and treatment of pathological root surface resorption in OIRR [9].

Mechanisms underlying OIRR

Mechanical load applied to the teeth is transmitted through orthodontic appliances, including fixed and removable systems, to the periodontal ligament and alveolar bone. Under physiological conditions, low-to-moderate loading typically results in coordinated alveolar bone remodeling without root damage, owing to the relative resistance of cementum to resorption and the maintenance of periodontal ligament homeostasis [10]. OIRR occurs only when root surface-specific protective mechanisms fail. Excessive, focal, or sustained compressive stress can damage cementoblasts and cementocytes, disrupt the precementum layer, and expose the mineralized root surface, thereby permitting attachment of odontoclast/cementoclast-like cells [11].

Compression causes microvascular collapse, local ischemia, and cell injury [9, 12]. Damaged cells release damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), such as HMGB1 and HSP70, which engage pattern-recognition receptors, including TLR4, on resident cells and thereby trigger sterile inflammatory cascades [13]. Early mediators, including prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), IL-6, and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), establish chemotactic gradients that recruit osteoclast precursors [14]. Supportive evidence for this early inflammatory phase is no longer restricted to in vitro systems. Krishnan et al. demonstrated significant HSP70 elevation and increased nuclear DNA damage in pressure-side PDL cells from orthodontically loaded premolars by Western blotting and COMET assay, respectively; Wan Mohamad et al. identified increased HSP1A and S100A9 in gingival crevicular fluid by LC-MS/MS proteomics one month after treatment; and more recent proteomic work further supports the concept that molecular inflammatory changes can precede radiographically detectable injury [15].

Concurrently, stromal and resident cells, including periodontal ligament fibroblasts, osteocytes, and cementoblasts/cementocytes, upregulate receptor activator of nuclear factor-κB ligand (RANKL) and suppress osteoprotegerin (OPG), thereby shifting the local balance toward osteoclast-like differentiation [16]. RANKL-RANK engagement activates NF-κB and MAPK signaling and promotes nuclear autoamplification of NFATc1, which in turn coordinates the expression of osteoclast effector molecules such as TRAP, cathepsin K, and MMP-9 that are required for precursor fusion, ruffled-border formation, and matrix degradation. Once the protective root layer is lost, mature osteoclast-like/odontoclast-like cells can establish sealed resorption lacunae on the root surface, acidify the microenvironment through V-ATPases, and release lysosomal hydrolases to dissolve mineralized dental tissues, thereby producing characteristic Howship’s lacunae [17]. More importantly, dentin sialophosphoprotein (DSPP) and its cleavage products, DPP and DSP, have emerged as among the most root surface-specific biomarkers detectable in gingival crevicular fluid.

Regulatory mechanisms of osteoclast differentiation

Osteoclastogenesis, the conversion of mononuclear precursors into multinucleated, resorptive osteoclasts, is the core cellular event that drives irreversible hard-tissue loss once the root surface has been opened to osteoclast attachment [18]. Contemporary studies have revealed multiple, interlaced regulatory layers, although the strength of evidence supporting each pathway is not uniform, ranging from direct evidence in orthodontic models to broader findings from periodontal and bone biology research (Fig. 1).

Core signaling: RANKL–RANK–OPG and downstream effectors

The tripartite RANKL–RANK–OPG axis constitutes the central molecular switch that governs osteoclast differentiation and maturation. RANKL, produced by osteoblasts, periodontal ligament cells, and activated immune cells, binds its cognate receptor RANK on osteoclast precursors. This engagement triggers intracellular cascades, most notably the NF-κB and MAP kinase pathways, that converge on the master transcription factor NFATc1 (nuclear factor of activated T cells, cytoplasmic 1). Upon dephosphorylation, NFATc1 translocates to the nucleus, cooperates with AP-1 complexes (c-Fos/c-Jun), and drives the transcription of key osteoclast genes (e.g. tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase, cathepsin K, matrix metalloproteinase-9) [19]. Genetic ablation of NFATc1 abolishes osteoclast formation in murine models, whereas its overexpression induces osteoclastogenesis, even in the absence of RANKL. In OIRR lesions, nuclear NFATc1 levels rise two- to three-fold and correlate directly with the number of Howship lacunae. The transcription factors, MITF and PU.1, further modulate precursor proliferation and fusion, reinforcing the differentiation program.

OPG, a soluble decoy receptor, counterbalances this process by sequestering RANKL and preventing its interaction with RANK. Under physiological conditions, the RANKL/OPG ratio is tightly regulated to maintain bone homeostasis. However, excessive orthodontic force and local inflammatory mediators, such as IL-1β and TNF-α, can upregulate RANKL and suppress OPG in periodontal ligament cells, thereby shifting the balance toward resorption [20]. In vitro loading models have confirmed that this ratio changes under different mechanical conditions, but the precise quantitative threshold at which adaptive remodeling transitions to OIRR remains unclear.

Overall, the relevance of this pathway in orthodontics is supported by substantial evidence, although direct clinical intervention evidence specifically targeting OIRR remains limited.

Mechanotransduction: ion channels, integrins and cytoskeletal signaling

Mechanical stimuli in the orthodontic microenvironment

Orthodontic loading generates compressive and tensile stresses, alterations in fluid flow, matrix deformation, and changes in local blood perfusion within the periodontal tissues. These stimuli are heterogeneous in both time and space; intermittent or cyclic loading may elicit cellular responses distinct from those induced by sustained static compression, thereby influencing the balance between adaptive remodeling and pathological root surface damage. At present, most of the available evidence in this area derives from cell culture systems and animal models of orthodontic tooth movement rather than from direct analysis of human OIRR tissues.

Candidate mechanoreceptors: ion channels, integrins, cytoskeleton, and primary cilia

Among candidate mechanoreceptors, TRPV4 has received relatively direct experimental support as a mechanosensitive molecule regulating osteoclast differentiation [16]. In addition, integrins enriched at podosome-like structures or focal adhesion complexes can recruit focal adhesion kinase (FAK) and Src, while actin cytoskeletal remodeling and primary cilia help cells decode the direction, magnitude, and duration of loading [21–23]. Piezo1 has attracted considerable attention in bone and periodontal mechanobiology, and recent studies have established a more direct evidence chain in the orthodontic OIRR context. Zhang et al. emonstrated in a murine orthodontic model that activation of Piezo1 in periodontal ligament cells promoted CXCL12 secretion via IL-6, recruited CXCR4 + inflammatory monocytes that polarized toward M1 macrophages, and directly exacerbated root resorption; moreover, the CXCR4 antagonist AMD3100 attenuated root resorption in vivo, thereby establishing the Piezo1/IL-6/CXCL12/CXCR4 axis as a potentially druggable OIRR cascade [24]. Schröder et al.showed in human periodontal ligament fibroblasts that Piezo1 inhibition with GsMTx4 under compression reduced RANKL expression and osteoclastogenesis, whereas chemical activation with JEDI2 downregulated OPG and induced TNF/IL-6, providing functional evidence in human cells that Piezo1 positively regulates osteoclast formation [25]. In addition, a study reported that compressive force inhibited Piezo1/Wnt signaling through HK2-mediated glycolysis, reduced cementoblast mineralization, and increased the RANKL/OPG ratio, thereby revealing a novel link among metabolic reprogramming, mechanotransduction, and root resorption [26].

Intracellular signaling downstream of mechanosensing

These mechanoreceptors ultimately converge on intracellular Ca2+ signaling, calcineurin/NFATc1 activation, MAPK and NF-κB pathways, as well as mechanoresponsive transcriptional regulators such as YAP/TAZ. In periodontal ligament-related models, compressive loading increases RANKL expression and suppresses OPG, thereby linking mechanosensing to pro-osteoclastogenic signaling [23, 26]. However, the available data still mainly reflect responses in periodontal tissues or alveolar bone rather than within the root surface-specific resorption lesion itself.

Downstream effects on osteoclast/odontoclast-like cell activation and OIRR

Once the protective barrier on the root surface has been disrupted, these signaling events may promote the recruitment, fusion, and activation of osteoclast-like/odontoclast-like cells on the exposed cementum or dentin surface. Mechanotransduction is therefore better viewed as an upstream modulator of OIRR susceptibility rather than as a root resorption-specific pathway per se. Direct evidence establishing a causal link between any single mechanoreceptor and human OIRR remains insufficient.

Epigenetic and microRNA-mediated regulation

Epigenetic modifications fine-tune osteoclast gene expression without altering the DNA sequences. DNA methylation within the promoters of RANKL and NFATc1genes inhibits their transcription, thereby dampening osteoclastogenesis [27]. Histone deacetylases (HDAC1/3/7) compact chromatin to silence key osteoclastogenic loci, while SIRT1, a NAD⁺-dependent deacetylase, activates FoxO transcription factors to upregulate antioxidant defenses (e.g., SOD2) and OPG, restraining excessive resorptive activity [28]. HDAC4/5 in osteoblasts deacetylate Runx2, reduce RANKL output, and boost OPG levels, thereby indirectly suppressing osteoclast activation [29]. At the post-transcriptional level, miR-21 can promote pro-osteoclastic programs, while miR-214, miR-223, and other miRNAs participate in osteoclast-related signaling in a context-dependent manner [30–34].

In recent years, direct functional evidence for miRNAs in human orthodontic patients has expanded substantially. Jiang et al. measured miR-155-5p in gingival crevicular fluid (GCF) from patients with different degrees of root resorption and found that levels in the healthy/mild groups were approximately two-fold higher than those in the severe-resorption group. Dual-luciferase assays confirmed CXCR2 as a direct target, and miR-155-5p mimics reduced TRAP+, carbonic anhydrase II, MMP-9, and cathepsin K, establishing its dual value as both a protective factor and a noninvasive biomarker for OIRR [35]. A 2025 longitudinal GCF miRNA profiling study in adolescent orthodontic patients further revealed stage-specific temporal patterns: miR-21 was upregulated during the early phase of treatment (1–5 weeks), miR-29b increased later in association with RUNX2-related activity, and miR-34a showed inverse correlations with MMP-2, MMP-9, and MMP-14, thereby providing a framework for time-resolved miRNA-based monitoring of OIRR-related tissue responses [36].

Autophagy and apoptosis

Autophagy is a lysosome-mediated degradation pathway that sustains osteoclast bioenergetics and homeostasis by clearing damaged organelles and protein aggregates [37]. Elevated expression of autophagy markers Beclin-1 and LC3-II correlates with enhanced resorptive function, whereas pharmacologic inhibitors (e.g., 3-MA) attenuate osteoclast activity [38]. However, excessive autophagy induced by rapamycin or severe stress can trigger autophagic cell death and terminate cellular resorption. Apoptosis, governed by Bcl-2 family proteins and caspase cascades, precisely controls osteoclast lifespan. Pro-apoptotic Bax/Bak increase mitochondrial outer membrane permeability, releasing cytochrome C and activating caspase-9/−3, whereas antiapoptotic Bcl-2/Bcl-xL maintain cellular integrity. In OIRR, TNF-α may paradoxically promote osteoclast apoptosis via Fas/FasL or CHOP-mediated ER stress, providing an intrinsic termination signal [39]. The regulation of autophagy in cementoblasts has yielded substantial mechanistic advances. Yang et al. demonstrated that 24 h of compressive force at 2.0 g/cm² reduced autophagic flux in cementoblasts, suppressed migration, and increased apoptosis; activation of autophagy with rapamycin partially restored cellular function, thereby identifying autophagy inhibition as a critical node in cementoblast dysfunction [40]. Liu et al. further identified lincRNA-p21 as a force-sensitive epigenetic regulator. Mechanistically, lincRNA-p21 binds FoxO3 and blocks the expression of autophagy-related genes; in vivo knockdown restored autophagy, enhanced cementogenesis, and alleviated root resorption [41]. More recently, Wei reported that ferroptosis represents a novel mechanism of compressive force-induced cementoblast injury: compressive force activated the cGAS-STING pathway, increased p-STING, p-TBK1, and p-IRF3, reduced GPX4, and elevated ROS, Fe²⁺, and MDA levels. In vivo administration of the STING inhibitor H-151 significantly mitigated root resorption, suggesting a new therapeutic target for the prevention and treatment of OIRR [42].

Inflammatory cytokines and oxidative stress

Proinflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-17, potently amplify osteoclastogenesis through NF-κB and MAPK activation, upregulate RANKL in neighboring stromal cells, and suppress OPG [43]. TNF-α also synergizes with RANKL to boost secretion of matrix-degrading enzymes (cathepsin K, MMP-9), accelerating cementum and dentin breakdown [44]. Concurrently, reactive oxygen species (ROS) generated by NADPH oxidases in macrophages and mitochondrial dysfunction serve as secondary messengers. Low ROS levels enhance NFATc1 nuclear translocation, whereas high concentrations induce DNA damage and apoptosis [45]. The transcription factor FOXO1 integrates oxidative and inflammatory signals, binds NFATc1 and p65 promoters to inhibit their transcription, upregulates antioxidant enzymes to quench ROS, and stimulates OPG expression to restore the RANKL/OPG balance [46]. The preclinical use of antioxidants (e.g., N-acetylcysteine) reduces osteoclast numbers and root resorption areas, although clinical validation remains pending [47].

Acidic microenvironment and extracellular matrix (ECM) interactions

Osteoclasts acidify the resorption lacuna via V-ATPase-driven proton secretion, optimally activating cathepsin K for mineral dissolution [48]. Degradation products of the ECM, such as C-terminal telopeptide fragments, act as chemotactic cues for precursor recruitment [49]. The binding of integrin αvβ3 to matrix proteins (bone sialoprotein, vitronectin) polarizes osteoclasts and establishes the sealing zone necessary for focalized resorption [50]. Moreover, ECM stiffness modulates osteoclast differentiation through YAP/TAZ mechanotransduction: substrates with elastic moduli ≥ 20 GPa enhance osteoclast maturation [51, 52]. These concepts are well supported in general bone biology, but the threshold at which ECM-related signals shift from physiological remodeling to pathological root resorption remains poorly defined.

Hormonal and mineral metabolism influences

Endocrine regulators profoundly influence the biology of osteoclasts. Intermittent parathyroid hormone (PTH) spikes drive RANKL expression in osteoblasts, whereas sustained PTH elevation may paradoxically upregulate OPG [53]. Vitamin D merits particular attention. Through vitamin D receptor signaling, calcitriol can act on osteoblast-lineage cells, periodontal ligament-associated cells, and potentially cementoblast behavior; evidence from bone biology and orthodontic models suggests that it may modulate osteoclastogenesis partly through the RANKL/OPG axis and alterations in calcium-phosphate homeostasis [40]. Vitamin D has also been proposed to shape the local inflammatory microenvironment and the mineralization status of the periodontal/root surface niche, thereby influencing the balance between efficient tooth movement and tissue injury. A study genotyped seven SNPs in VDR, GC, CYP27B1, and CYP24A1 and found that carriers of the VDR rs2228570 (FokI) AA genotype showed 21% greater molar root resorption, whereas carriers of the CYP27B1 rs4646536 G allele exhibited 42% less incisor root resorption, suggesting that genomic variation in the vitamin D pathway, rather than circulating vitamin D levels per se, may be a more relevant determinant of OIRR susceptibility [54]. Direct evidence that vitamin D specifically prevents OIRR remains inconsistent; some orthodontic studies even suggest that optimizing vitamin D status may accelerate tooth movement without necessarily reducing root resorption [55, 56]. In addition, Küchler showed in compressed human PDL cells that a physiological concentration of 25(OH)D3 (10 ng/mL) significantly reduced the RANKL/OPG ratio from 2.58 ± 1.16 to 0.96 ± 0.68 (p < 0.05), whereas pharmacological concentrations of calcitriol paradoxically increased RANKL expression, highlighting an important dose-dependent paradox with potential implications for the clinical selection of vitamin D supplementation regimens [57].

Estrogen suppresses RANKL and increases OPG through ERα signaling in osteoblasts, whereas estrogen deficiency shifts this balance toward resorption [58]. Metabolic disorders such as diabetes may further amplify osteoclast activation through ROS-mediated NF-κB signaling [59]. Local mineral signals may also participate: Ca2+ released during hard-tissue resorption can act as a negative feedback signal on the osteoclastic calcium-sensing receptor, whereas local phosphate metabolism may influence osteoclast survival and lacuno-canalicular remodeling [60].

Estrogen, through ERα signaling in osteoblasts, suppresses RANKL and increases OPG; its deficiency (e.g., postmenopause) elevates the RANKL/OPG ratio and exacerbates resorption [61]. Metabolic disorders, such as hyperglycemia and advanced glycation end products in diabetes, amplify osteoclast activation through ROS-mediated NF-κB signaling [20]. Notably, liberated Ca²⁺ from dentin resorption engages the osteoclastic calcium-sensing receptor to attenuate RANKL/NFATc1 signaling, forming a negative feedback loop. In contrast, hypophosphatemia in compressed regions reduces PiT1/2-mediated phosphate uptake, weakens apoptosis cues, and prolongs osteoclast survival [62, 63]. Targeting hormonal axes (e.g., estrogen replacement and vitamin D modulation) or mineral-sensing pathways may therefore offer new avenues to curb pathological root resorption. However, their efficacy in orthodontic contexts requires thorough investigation.

Osteoclasts

Osteoclasts possess intrinsic feedback loops that regulate their resorptive activity. Binding of RANKL to RANK on osteoclast precursors activates the canonical NF-κB and MAPK pathways, driving NFATc1 transcription and nuclear translocation; NFATc1 then upregulates osteoclast-related genes such as cathepsin K and TRAP, and can further amplify its own expression through positive feedback [19]. Mature osteoclasts may also influence neighboring cells through secreted proteases, ATP, and extracellular vesicle-associated signals, although the relevant cargo and their importance in orthodontic OIRR have not been fully characterized and are largely extrapolated from non-orthodontic bone models [20].

Cementoblasts

Cementoblasts maintain the integrity of the mineralized root layer and modulate local osteoclastogenesis. Under orthodontic stress, mechanical strain can accelerate cementoblast senescence, accompanied by increased RANKL expression [64]. In rat models, cementoblasts and cementocytes exhibit force-dependent shifts in their RANKL/OPG ratios, which influence the severity of resorption [65]. Excessive force may also cause cementoblast necrosis, DAMP release, and sterile inflammation, thereby increasing the recruitment of osteoclast-like cells to the otherwise protected root surface. Extracellular vesicles derived from cementoblasts or cementocytes can modulate osteoclastogenesis in vitro, but the balance between their pro-resorptive and inhibitory signals is likely to be context-dependent [66].

Zhou et al. demonstrated in a rat intrusive force model (5 N, 14 days) that cementoblast senescence (p21+ or p16+) was initiated as early as day 3 after force application, establishing senescent cementoblasts as a dominant source of RANKL. Oral administration of dasatinib plus quercetin, as senolytic agents, significantly reduced senescent cells, TRAP+ cells, and root resorption, thereby providing proof-of-concept for directly targeting senescent cells in the prevention and treatment of OIRR [64]. In a study of 90 SD rats, further showed that under high force (50 g), SOST expression in cementocytes exceeded that in osteocytes, the RANKL/OPG ratio increased, and osteoclasts infiltrated the root surface from the bone side, highlighting for the first time the gatekeeper role of cementocytes in force-dependent root protection [65]. Yang et al. found that extracellular vesicles derived from compression-loaded cementoblasts (Comp-EVs) promoted macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype and increased phagocytic activity by two- to three-fold; local injection of Comp-EVs significantly attenuated cementum destruction in a murine root resorption model, suggesting that cementoblast-derived EVs under moderate mechanical stimulation may exert tissue-protective effects through immunomodulatory pathways [67].

However, this endogenous reparative capacity is usually insufficient to restore the original morphology and mechanical function of the root surface in moderate-to-severe OIRR, and current studies still lack effective approaches that have been reliably validated clinically and can purposefully enhance or reconstruct the reparative capacity of cementoblasts [7, 68].

Osteoblasts

Osteoblasts coordinate alveolar bone formation and resorption through paracrine and juxtacrine signals [52]. Under compressive orthodontic loading, osteoblasts on the pressure side upregulate RANKL and downregulate OPG, thereby shifting the local RANKL/OPG balance toward osteoclast activation [69, 70]. The bidirectional EphrinB2-EphB4 axis further couples osteoclast and osteoblast behavior [71]. Under mechanical strain, the effects of osteoblast-derived extracellular vesicles depend on their cargo and the specific context. Notably, miR-34c itself has been reported to promote, rather than inhibit, osteoclast differentiation by targeting LGR4 [72].

Osteocytes

As the principal mechanosensors of bone, osteocytes can influence osteoclastogenesis through RANKL/OPG signaling, apoptosis-related cues, connexin 43 (Cx43) channels, and soluble mediators [73]. In vitro and animal studies suggest that osteocyte apoptosis may spatially couple resorption to damaged regions [74]. Mechanically stimulated osteocytes can also rapidly release PGE2 and ATP through connexin 43 hemichannels [75]. In osteoclastogenesis models, TNT-like structures have been observed and may participate in precursor fusion or intercellular transfer; however, their role in orthodontic OIRR remains to be established [74]. Similarly, extracellular vesicles released by mechanically stimulated osteocytes can modulate osteoblast and osteoclast function in vitro, but direct evidence linking osteocyte EV cargo to orthodontically induced root resorption remains limited [75].

Neural cells

Neuro-osseous-immune interactions add another regulatory dimension to root resorption. Calcitonin gene-related peptide released by sensory nerve endings can inhibit NFATc1 nuclear translocation in osteoclast precursors [76]. Conversely, substance P may amplify NF-κB signaling and potentiate RANKL-driven osteoclastogenesis [77]. The autonomic nervous system also regulates bone remodeling, although its net effects are clearly context-dependent [53]. Mechanosensitive Piezo2 channels on nerve terminals have been proposed to participate in force sensing, but direct evidence linking Piezo2-mediated neural mechanotransduction to OIRR remains limited [78].

Immune cells

Immune cells critically shape the osteoclastic response. M1 macrophages secrete TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, thereby increasing tissue sensitivity to RANKL; HMGB1 released under pressure may further polarize macrophages toward a pro-resorptive phenotype through TLR4/NF-κB signaling [79]. M2 macrophages counterbalance these effects through IL-10 and TGF-β. Compression-induced hypoxia can shift the Th17/Treg balance toward Th17, in which IL-17 favors osteoclastogenesis, whereas Treg cells suppress osteoclast-like differentiation through CTLA-4- and TGF-β-related mechanisms [80, 81]. In addition, neutrophil extracellular traps, mast cell mediators, and immune cell-derived extracellular vesicles have also been proposed to modulate osteoclast-like activity [82–84].

In recent years, several studies have brought immune cell analyses directly into the orthodontic tissue microenvironment. Wang et al. performed single-cell RNA sequencing on murine periodontal tissues during orthodontic tooth movement and identified seven cell lineages and 18 major cell types. A C3ar1 + macrophage subcluster was found on the pressure side, and macrophage-to-osteoclast Ccl/Tnf/Spp1 signaling was significantly enhanced, providing single-cell-resolution evidence for the immune-osteoclast axis in the orthodontic context [85]. Wald et al. showed that γδ T cells, predominantly of the Vγ6 + subtype, are the major source of IL-17 A in the periodontal ligament, and that conditional ablation of γδ T cells markedly reduced orthodontic tooth movement, monocyte/neutrophil recruitment, and RANKL expression [86]. Zhang et al. further demonstrated in a rat model that GCF IL-17 was strongly correlated with the RANKL/OPG ratio (rs = 0.72, p = 0.002) and osteoclast number (rs = 0.84, p < 0.001), supporting IL-17 as a potential noninvasive biomarker of OIRR [87]. Zhao et al. showed through coculture and animal experiments that M1 macrophages impair cementoblast mineralization via exosomal signaling, whereas M2 macrophages enhance it; moreover, a higher M1/M2 ratio and weaker cementoblast mineralization were observed on the pressure side, directly linking immune polarization to root surface-protective function [88].

Periodontal ligament stem cells (PDLSCs)

Under orthodontic loading, PDLSCs sense strain through TRPV4 channels and activate ERK-related inflammatory signaling, leading to increased IL-6, IL-1β, and RANKL expression together with reduced OPG [89, 90]. Mechanical stimulation may also alter H2S-related signaling and influence macrophage polarization, thereby indirectly modulating osteoclastogenesis [91]. PDLSC-derived extracellular vesicles have been reported to regulate osteoclast differentiation, and exosomal circ_0000722 has been shown to enhance osteoclastogenesis in RAW264.7 systems [92, 93].

More recently, PDL-derived exosome research has achieved direct validation in in vivo OIRR models. Wang et al. demonstrated that extracellular vesicles produced by compression-stimulated human PDL cells carry miR-28, which targets RUNX1, as confirmed by dual-luciferase assays, thereby promoting osteoclastogenesis and accelerating OIRR progression in vivo [94]. Huang et al. further showed that compressive force promotes PDLSC exosome production through RAB27B, and that exosomal ANXA3 enhances macrophage internalization and activates ERK phosphorylation to induce osteoclast differentiation. In vivo infusion of PDLSC-derived exosomes promoted tooth movement and increased TRAP+ osteoclasts, whereas the ERK inhibitor U0126 attenuated this effect [95].

Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs)

Orthodontic force can activate STAT3-related pathways in mesenchymal stromal/stem cells, thereby promoting osteogenic differentiation and influencing the broader tissue remodeling environment [96]. MSC-derived extracellular vesicles are context-dependent. Under certain conditions, they may carry anti-resorptive miRNAs, such as miR-124, which has been reported to suppress osteoclast-related inflammatory and resorptive programs, whereas in inflammatory microenvironments they may also deliver miRNAs such as miR-223 that modulate NF-κB-related signaling and may favor pro-resorptive responses [97, 98]. Accordingly, MSC-related regulation is mechanistically attractive, but it has not yet been translated into root surface-specific prevention strategies for OIRR.

In summary, orthodontic force mobilizes a multilayered signaling network involving cytokines, direct cell-cell contact, and emerging vesicle-mediated communication pathways, all of which collectively influence osteoclast behavior and the extent of root resorption. However, not all proposed communication pathways have yet been directly validated in orthodontic OIRR.

Conclusions

OIRR is not driven by a single mechanism but arises from the concerted interplay of genetic regulation, mechanical signals, biochemical mediators, and immune modulation. A deeper understanding of the pathways governing osteoclast differentiation, activity, and lifespan under orthodontic loading is essential for refining force application protocols and minimizing adverse outcomes. To date, most OIRR studies have relied on rodent models or in vitro systems, which cannot fully recapitulate the complex mechanical environment and cellular composition of the human periodontal ligament and root surface. This translational gap limits the clinical applicability of the preclinical findings. Emerging modes of intercellular communication, including exosome release, TNTs, and mitochondrial transfer, have been implicated in OIRR regulation; however, their precise roles and therapeutic potential remain to be elucidated.

Prevention strategies supported by current clinical evidence

In current clinical practice, the prevention of OIRR still relies mainly on several general strategies supported by accumulated clinical experience or small-sample studies. First, force control: relatively high force magnitudes applied continuously to the root surface, together with constant mechanical stimulation, are among the most important modifiable risk factors for OIRR. Current clinical and experimental evidence generally supports the use of light-force or intermittent-force regimens, particularly during high-risk mechanical phases such as intrusion and retraction [99]. Second, control of treatment duration: treatment duration is positively associated with both the incidence and severity of OIRR. Accordingly, once the treatment objectives have been achieved, the active force phase should be terminated in a timely manner and the patient should enter the retention phase as early as feasible. Third, patient risk assessment: by integrating factors such as root morphology (e.g., slender tapered roots, dilacerated roots, or short blunt roots), a history of dental trauma, systemic disease, and genetic susceptibility, patients can be stratified by risk before treatment, and more conservative mechanical protocols can be adopted for high-risk individuals [100, 101]. Fourth, imaging follow-up: CBCT-based volumetric assessment can be used to actively monitor high-risk teeth, such as the maxillary central incisors, at mid-treatment or other critical stages, thereby enabling early identification of patients whose root resorption exceeds the acceptable threshold and timely adjustment of the mechanical protocol [102].

The molecular and cellular mechanisms reviewed above also provide a mechanistic rationale for these general strategies. First, light-force and intermittent-force regimens may reduce ischemia/hypoxia on the compression side, as well as the accumulation of DAMPs and inflammatory mediators, thereby limiting RANKL/OPG imbalance and reducing the sustained activation of osteoclast-like cells on the root surface. Second, controlling treatment duration may help preserve the physiological repair window over time, preventing it from being continuously overridden by ongoing resorption and cellular senescence signals. Third, patient stratification based on risk factors, such as VDR polymorphisms, and systemic inflammatory status, is essentially an indirect clinical recognition of interindividual variability within the dynamic osteoclast regulatory network described above [54].

However, the preventive effects of these general strategies may reduce, but cannot completely eliminate, OIRR. Mechanistic studies centered on osteoclasts suggest that future preventive strategies should build on these general measures and further evolve into a mechanism-based precision intervention framework. To achieve this goal, it is imperative to resolve, at single-cell and spatial resolution, the key osteoclast subpopulations and their regulatory networks in OIRR and, on the basis of clearly defined targets, to develop local delivery systems directed at osteoclasts, so as to selectively suppress pathological root surface resorption without interfering with normal alveolar bone remodeling.

Emerging therapeutic strategies and future directions

Mapping cellular subpopulation dynamics in space and time

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and spatial transcriptomics offer unprecedented resolution to track the emergence, differentiation, and localization of distinct cell subsets during OIRR. For example, in a murine cortical osteotomy model, scRNA-seq identified a CCR2⁺ macrophage subset with dual roles in inflammation initiation and tissue repair [103]. Applying these technologies to orthodontic models could reveal the lineage trajectories of osteoclasts, immune cells, cementoblasts, and other stromal populations, as well as region-specific gene expression patterns at the root surface [85]. Spatial transcriptomics can further assign molecular signatures to precise microanatomical niches, such as compressive versus tensile zones, uncovering the spatial heterogeneity of cytokine gradients, mechanotransductive receptor expression, and cell–cell interaction networks. Delineating different macrophage phenotypes, such as balanced resorption and regeneration, may identify novel cellular targets for mitigating root loss.

Development of targeted therapeutics and local delivery systems

Small-molecule inhibitors and localized drug delivery platforms hold promise for restoring the osteoclast–osteoblast equilibrium without systemic side effects. Activation of the mTORC2/Akt axis in osteocytes and osteoblasts under mechanical stretch promotes autophagy-mediated bone formation, while concurrently suppressing osteoclastogenesis [104]. Likewise, pharmacological activation of Wnt/β-catenin signaling has demonstrated the dual capacity to inhibit osteoclast activity and enhance osteogenesis in multiple bone disease models, providing a rationale for its application in OIRR [105]. In the field of bone regeneration, advanced biomaterials such as biocompatible and degradable hydrogels, nanoparticles, and scaffold constructs can be functionalized with anti-RANKL antibodies or anti-inflammatory agents for sustained local release at the root surface. Biodegradable microneedle patches and in situ-forming hydrogel systems enable precise spatiotemporal delivery of modulators of osteoclast or osteoblast function. For instance, hyaluronic acid–alginate microneedle arrays have been used to administer RANKL to accelerate tooth movement, and similar devices can be engineered to release OPG or RANKL inhibitors directly into the pressure zone to selectively attenuate osteoclast activity [106]. Furthermore, OPG-Fc fusion protein–loaded hydrogels have shown controlled suppression of bone resorption and enhanced regeneration in defect models [107].

By combining mechanobiology insights with cutting-edge omics and biomaterials engineering, future research can systematically unravel OIRR mechanisms at both macroscopic and molecular scales, ultimately achieving the dual objectives of efficient orthodontic movement and maximal root preservation.
