Simultaneous Combination Therapy of Free Anterolateral Thigh Flap and Negative-pressure Wound Therapy With Penrose Drains for Thoracic Dead Space
Authors: Marie Taga, Hiroki Umezawa, Yu Hokazono, Akatsuki Kondo, Hironobu Aoki, Rei Ogawa
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Summary:Open-window thoracostomy is an effective treatment for refractory postpneumonectomy empyema. However, it can leave large thoracic dead space, and bronchopleural fistulas (BPF) are a common major complication. These problems can be treated with myocutaneous flap transfer or negative-pressure wound therapy (NPWT). However, treatment is often difficult and prolonged. Here, we report 2 cases of free anterolateral thigh flap transfer followed immediately by NPWT combined with Penrose drains. Both patients, including 1 with BPF, had favorable postoperative courses and were discharged 28 and 14 days after reconstruction. Dead space infection and BPF recurrence were not observed after discharge. Following free flap transfer with NPWT combined with Penrose drains may efficiently eliminate dead space and accelerate wound healing in a large three-dimensional space.

Postpneumonectomy empyema occurs in 2%–15% of patients. Given its high mortality rates (up to 20%),1 the primary focus is infection control. If initial treatment with antibiotics, chest drainage, and surgical debridement is ineffective, open-window thoracostomy (OWT) is required.2 However, this leaves large thoracic dead space. Although it can be reduced with either muscle flap transfer or negative-pressure wound therapy (NPWT),3 both methods have limitations. Muscle flaps may provide insufficient volume and thus promote dead space infection. Suture failure due to OWT-damaged surrounding skin can also occur.4 Moreover, the ability of NPWT to completely reduce the space is limited.5 In addition, bronchopleural fistulas (BPFs), if present, are unlikely to heal spontaneously and will require bronchoscope-mediated bronchial embolization and pedicled omental filling or myocutaneous flap transfer.4,6,7 The latter approaches can be challenging. Thus, both muscle flap transfer and NPWT can necessitate long treatment and hospitalization durations.

Recently, we induced effective esophagopharyngeal fistula healing by combining NPWT with a Penrose drain in the fistula.8 Here, we report 2 cases whose post-OWT thoracic dead space was effectively treated with free anterolateral thigh (ALT) flap transfer followed by NPWT with Penrose drains.

CASE REPORTS

Case 1

A 68-year-old man with lung cancer underwent resection of the right middle/lower lung lobes and lymph-node dissection. OWT was conducted for empyema on postoperative day 10. The thoracic-dead space was filled 25 days later with a bilateral free ALT flap bearing a vastus lateralis segment. Penrose drains were inserted between recipient and flap tissues (Fig. 1) and poked through holes made in adhesive film, which was then adhered to sponge foam and surrounding skin and subjected to conventional NPWT (V.A.C., KCI USA, Inc., San Antonia, TX). Thus, negative pressure was placed on the Penrose drains (Fig. 2). The anastomosed vein thrombosed shortly after surgery, necessitating prompt reoperation. NPWT with Penrose drains were continued for 12 days. The patient was discharged on postoperative day 28. Dead space did not recur. However, the patient died 3 years post reconstructive surgery due to cancer recurrence.

Schematic depiction of the inset flap and the NPWT system combined with Penrose drains. The objective of this system was to promote the healing of the deep and thin wound and, if present, bronchopleural fistulas. The Penrose drains are placed between the flap and recipient site, and NPWT is then applied over and around the drain. The negative pressure (P) closes the space between the flap and the recipient bed (yellow arrows), causing the flap to adhere to the recipient site and eliminating dead spaces. This reduces the force on sutures and prevents their failure. The negative pressure also induces fluid discharge (F) and encourages granulation (yellow bubbles).

Case 2

A 73-year-old man with metastatic pulmonary tumor developed empyema with BPF after left lower pulmonary lobe resection. OWT was conducted on postoperative day 19. The thoracic dead space (Fig. 3) was filled 170 days later with a unilateral free ALT flap bearing a vastus lateralis segment. NPWT with Penrose drains was applied (Figs. 1, 2) and continued for 8 days. The patient was discharged on postoperative day 14. On postoperative day 22, he was readmitted to hospital due to pneumonia. It was successfully managed with intravenous antibiotics. An old postpneumonectomy hematoma on his rib stump was concomitantly removed. BPF and dead space infection did not recur (Fig. 4). However, the patient died 3 years post reconstructive surgery from recurrent metastastic colorectal cancer.

DISCUSSION

Free muscle flaps are often used to reduce post-OWT thoracic dead space. However, accurate filling can be difficult due to the intricate thoracic structures and the large volume. The remnant dead spaces are prone to abscesses that promote flap dehiscence. The surrounding tissues may also be fragile and prone to suture failure and BPF recurrence. Our study showed that treating post-OWT thoracic dead space with free ALT flap transfer followed by NPWT with Penrose drains yielded good outcomes in 2 cases, including one with BPF. Specifically, postdischarge dead space infections did not occur, and hospital discharge occurred 14 and 28 days postreconstruction. This is earlier than reconstructive surgery using only skin flaps. One of the coauthors of this report previously reported that 8 patients were discharged from the hospital 16 to 195 days (median, 30.5 d) postoperatively when treated with free flaps alone.9 It can also be said that the healing achieved by our method is faster than using NPWT alone. Three reported studies showed an average length of hospital stay of 26,.4 31,6 and 46 days.7 Our results were also shorter compared with NPWT followed by flap grafting, which reported that NPWT required 31 ± 19 days and postreconstruction hospital stay of 21 ± 34 days.5

Thus, combining incisional vacuum-assisted closure with Penrose tubes for treating post-OWT thoracic dead space may effectively promote flap survival and wound healing and prevent postdischarge dead space infection. This may reflect synergistic abilities to reduce dead space, promote fluid drainage and granulation, and encourage flap adherence to the recipient tissue (Fig. 2), thereby reducing suture failure risk. The Penrose drains that we use have numerous fine grooves inside, making them less likely to become clogged even under negative pressure.

The ALT flap is particularly suitable for reconstruction because it imposes minimal postoperative functional disability.10 Moreover, if the lateral thorax recipient vessels are dissected with the 2-team approach, this flap is easy to harvest. The flap also offers long donor vessels and a generous volume that is easily augmented with a vastus lateralis segment or contralateral-ALT flap. Furthermore, the use of free flaps avoids damaging the muscles/vessels that are affected by prior surgery; the ALT flap donor site is also undamaged by previous surgery. Moreover, using free flaps permits flap inset without wasting muscle outside the thoracic cavity, unlike pedicled flaps. The disadvantages of free flap transplantation include dependence on microsurgery and the fact that even bilateral free ALT flaps from lean patients may have insufficient volume. However, bilateral and unilateral ALT flaps were sufficient for cases 1 and 2, respectively.

Case 2 had no postoperative complications, but case 1 developed early venous thrombosis that was rescued by prompt reoperation. This complication did not prolong hospital stay.

Thus, although free flap transfer, NPWT, and Penrose drains can individually promote post-OWT dead space elimination and wound healing, combining them may synergize these individual benefits. Therefore, this approach may provide good outcomes for patients with thoracic dead space after OWT, including shortening their hospital stay. Further prospective studies that take into account factors such as the acute/chronic nature of the empyema, the timing of OWT, and the presence/size of BPF are needed to validate these observations.
