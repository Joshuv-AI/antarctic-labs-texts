Neuromuscular electrical stimulation improves stability and function in chronic ankle instability: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Luoman Hu, Minghua Zhong, Min Jia, Chengfei Gao, Yunxia Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic ankle instability (CAI) commonly develops following lateral ankle sprains and is associated with recurrent sprains, persistent pain, and neuromuscular deficits. While neuromuscular electrical stimulation (NMES) has shown promising results in enhancing muscle strength and neuromuscular control, its specific effects on CAI remain insufficiently investigated.MethodA comprehensive search of electronic databases was conducted to identify randomized controlled trials (RCTs) evaluating NMES interventions in individuals with CAI. Studies were included if they reported outcomes related to instability, balance (both static and dynamic), and functional recovery. Pooled data were analyzed using weighted mean differences (WMDs) or standardized mean differences (SMDs), with heterogeneity assessed using the I² statistic.ResultsSeven RCTs involving 248 participants were included in the analysis. NMES significantly improved self-reported instability (WMD = 2.18; 95% CI: 1.21 to 3.14; p < 0.01), static balance (WMD = − 1.43; 95% CI: − 2.80 to − 0.06; p = 0.04), dynamic balance (SMD = 0.73; 95% CI: 0.40 to 1.06; p < 0.01), and functional recovery (SMD = 0.40; 95% CI: 0.01 to 0.80; p = 0.04) compared to control groups.ConclusionsNMES shows promise as an adjunctive therapy for CAI. However, given the observed heterogeneity and methodological limitations, current evidence warrants cautious interpretation. Further rigorous, sham-controlled trials are essential to validate long-term efficacy.Trial registrationPROSPERO (CRD420251101522).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-026-09824-8.Keywords: Chronic ankle instability, Neuromuscular electrical stimulation, Meta-analysis, Randomized controlled trials

Introduction

Chronic ankle instability (CAI) represents a pervasive and debilitating clinical sequela following acute lateral ankle sprains, affecting up to 40% of individuals within the first year of injury [1, 2]. Characterized by a “vicious cycle” of recurrent giving-way episodes and persistent pain, CAI is no longer viewed merely as a localized orthopedic issue, but rather as a complex syndrome involving peripheral mechanical laxity and central sensorimotor reorganization [3]. If inadequately managed, the cumulative joint trauma associated with CAI significantly accelerates the onset of post-traumatic ankle osteoarthritis, imposing a substantial burden on both individual quality of life and global healthcare systems [4].

Despite the proliferation of traditional rehabilitation strategies, such as balance training and strengthening exercises, a considerable proportion of patients fail to regain pre-injury levels of functional stability [5]. This therapeutic plateau is often attributed to arthrogenic muscle inhibition, a clinical phenomenon where joint injury leads to ongoing neural inhibition, preventing the full voluntary activation of surrounding musculature, particularly the peroneal longus [6]. Addressing this deficit is paramount; however, conventional active exercises may be insufficient to bypass these inhibited neural pathways during the early-to-mid phases of recovery.

Neuromuscular electrical stimulation (NMES) has emerged as a promising adjunctive modality to mitigate these persistent neuro-motor deficits. By delivering transcutaneous electrical impulses to evoke involuntary muscle contractions, NMES bypasses the inhibited voluntary drive, potentially facilitating “top-down” neuroplastic changes and “bottom-up” muscle hypertrophy [7, 8]. While NMES has demonstrated efficacy in post-operative ACL reconstruction [9] and stroke rehabilitation [10], its integration into CAI management remains heterogenous and underutilized. Small-scale randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have reported conflicting results [11], and current clinical guidelines provide only weak recommendations due to the lack of high-level evidence synthesis.

Currently, a definitive clinical consensus regarding the utility of NMES for CAI remains elusive. While prior reviews have broadly assessed general rehabilitation exercises or focused on acute ankle sprains, no quantitative synthesis has specifically isolated the effects of NMES on the chronic instability population. To address this critical gap, this systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to synthesize high-level evidence from RCTs. Distinguishing our approach from studies focusing on isolated laboratory metrics, we specifically prioritized patient-centered clinical outcomes, namely self-reported stability, multi-planar balance control, and overall functional recovery, to provide direct, translatable evidence for refining the rehabilitation paradigm for chronic ankle instability.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in strict adherence to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement [12]. The study protocol was registered in PROSPERO, the online international prospective register of systematic reviews with registration No. CRD420251101522, available at https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/view/CRD420251101522.

Data sources and search strategy

A systematic, multi-database search was executed from database inception through May 31, 2025. We searched PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), and major Chinese databases (CNKI, Wangfang, and VIP). The search architecture utilized a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and comprehensive free-text keywords, including “chronic ankle instability,” “functional ankle instability,” “neuromuscular electrical stimulation,” and “NMES.” Search strings were optimized using Boolean logic (AND/OR). The full search strategies for each database are provided in Supplementary Appendix I. To minimize publication bias, we manually screened the reference lists of included trials and relevant systematic reviews to identify additional eligible studies. No language restrictions were applied.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were selected based on the following PICOS criteria: Studies were included if they met the following conditions: (1) Population: individuals diagnosed with chronic ankle instability, based on clinical symptoms or validated assessment tools; (2) Intervention: neuromuscular electrical stimulation applied to peri-ankle muscles, delivered as a standalone intervention or as a clearly distinguishable treatment arm; (3) Comparison: sham stimulation, no intervention, or conventional physical therapy excluding NMES; (4) Outcomes: Primary outcomes included self-reported instability; secondary outcomes included static and dynamic balance and functional capacity; and (5) Study design: randomized controlled trials.

Exclusion criteria: (1) studies with insufficient data for effect size calculation; and (2) non-peer-reviewed sources such as conference abstracts.

Study selection and data extraction

Literature management was performed using EndNote X9 (Clarivate Analytics, Philadelphia, PA, USA). Two reviewers (YXL and MJ) independently screened titles and abstracts, followed by full-text evaluation. Discrepancies were resolved via consensus or consultation with a third senior reviewer (CFG). A standardized data extraction form was used to collect the following information from each included study: (1) study characteristics (first author, year of publication, study design); (2) participant characteristics (sample size, age, gender); (3) intervention details (targeted muscles, NMES parameters and training protocol); (4) comparison interventions; and (5) outcome measures. Where necessary, corresponding authors were contacted to clarify missing or unclear data.

Risk of bias assessment

The methodological quality of the included randomized controlled trials was evaluated using the updated Cochrane risk-of-bias tool for randomized trials (RoB 2) [13]. Two reviewers (YXL and MJ) independently assessed the risk of bias across five domains: (1) bias arising from the randomization process; (2) bias due to deviations from intended interventions; (3) bias due to missing outcome data; (4) bias in measurement of the outcome; and (5) bias in selection of the reported result. Each domain, as well as the overall risk of bias, was judged as having a ‘low risk’, ‘some concerns’, or ‘high risk’. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer (CFG).

Data synthesis and analysis

Statistical synthesis was executed using Stata version 16.0 (StataCorp). Since all outcomes were continuous, effect sizes were expressed as Weighted Mean Differences (WMDs) for consistent measurement scales or Standardized Mean Differences (SMDs) for varying scales, each with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Heterogeneity among studies was assessed using the I² statistic [14]. An I² value of 50% or below was interpreted as low to moderate heterogeneity, for which a fixed-effects model was applied. When I² exceeded 50%, indicating substantial heterogeneity, a random-effects model was used to pool effect estimates [15]. A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

The initial systematic search yielded 735 records. After removing duplicates and screening titles and abstracts, 589 studies were retrieved for full-text evaluation. Of these, 39 articles were assessed for eligibility, and 7 randomized controlled trials involving 248 participants met all inclusion criteria for the final quantitative synthesis. A detailed list of the excluded full-text articles, along with the specific reasons for exclusion, is provided in Supplementary Appendix II. The study selection process is summarized in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1).

Study selection process depicted using the PRISMA flow diagram

Seven studies [11, 16–21] with 248 individuals were ultimately included in the final analysis. The studies were conducted between 2019 and 2024, with sample sizes ranging from 11 to 25 participants per group. The participants had a balanced gender distribution, with a mean age ranging from 21.9 years to 37.98 years. The interventions in the included studies focused on neuromuscular electrical stimulation, targeting various ankle muscles such as the gastrocnemius, peroneal muscles, abductor hallucis, and flexor digitorum longus. The NMES parameters varied, including frequencies from 20 Hz to 75 Hz, and pulse widths from 200 µs to 700 µs, with intensities set to induce muscle contraction based on individual tolerance or specific parameters defined by the study protocols. The duration of each NMES session varied from 11 min to 30 min, with frequency of application ranging from 3 sessions per week for 4 weeks to 3 sessions per week for 8 weeks. The control interventions varied and included conventional physical therapy, transcutaneous electrical stimulation (TENS), sham NMES.

Outcome measures included assessments of self-reported instability (Cumberland ankle instability tool, CAIT), static balance (center of pressure mean displacement velocity, COP-MV), dynamic balance (Y Balance Test, YBT; Star excursion balance text, SEBT), and functional capacity (Foot and ankle ability measure, FAAM; Foot and ankle disability index, FADI). Risk of bias assessment

The risk of bias assessment using the RoB 2 tool revealed varying methodological quality across the seven included RCTs (Fig. 2). Overall, two trials [16, 21] were rated as having a low risk of bias, as they employed rigorous double-blind designs with sham electrical stimulation, alongside adequate allocation concealment and assessor blinding. Two studies (Needle et al., 2023; Choi et al., 2024) were judged to have some concerns, primarily due to insufficient details regarding allocation concealment or mid-trial deviations from assessor blinding protocols.

Risk of bias assessment of included RCTs

Notably, three studies [17, 19, 20] were categorized as being at high risk of bias. This high-risk judgment was predominantly driven by bias in the measurement of the outcome. Because these trials relied heavily on patient-reported outcome measures such as the CAIT and FADI, the lack of participant and personnel blinding likely introduced significant measurement bias, as subjective responses can be inherently influenced by the knowledge of receiving an active intervention.

Meta analysis results

Effect of NMES on self-reported instability

The effect of NMES on self-reported instability measured with CAIT was evaluated in four studies [16, 17, 19, 21] comprising 157 participants. Because no significant heterogeneity was observed (I² = 25.86%, p = 0.26), a fixed-effects model was applied. The pooled analysis showed a significant improvement in self-reported instability in the NMES group compared to the control group (WMD = 2.18, 95% CI: 1.21 to 3.14, p < 0.01; Fig. 3).

Forest plot of NMES compared to control on self-reported instability in individuals with CAI

Effect of NMES on balance outcomes

The effect of NMES on static balance measured with COP-MV was assessed in two studies [17, 21] with a total of 78 participants. The heterogeneity across studies was significant (I² = 82.08%, p = 0.02), and a random-effects model was applied. The analysis indicated that NMES significantly improved static balance compared to the control group (WMD = -1.43, 95% CI: -2.80 to -0.06, p = 0.04; Fig. 4).

Forest plot of NMES compared to control on static balance in individuals with CAI

For dynamic balance, four studies [11, 16, 19, 20] with 150 participants were included. The observed heterogeneity was moderate (I² = 43.85%, p = 0.15), justifying the use of a fixed-effects model. The pooled analysis revealed a significant improvement in dynamic balance for the NMES group compared to the control group (SMD = 0.73, 95% CI: 0.40 to 1.06, p < 0.01; Fig. 5).

Forest plot of NMES compared to control on dynamic balance in individuals with CAI

Effect of NMES on functional capacity

Finally, the effect of NMES on functional capacity was evaluated in three studies [16, 18, 20] comprising 97 participants. Heterogeneity among studies was negligible (I² = 0.00%, p = 0.63), necessitating the use of a fixed-effects model. The pooled analysis showed a significant improvement in functional capacity in the NMES group compared to the control group (SMD = 0.40, 95% CI: 0.01 to 0.80, p = 0.04; Fig. 6).

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis synthesized evidence from seven RCTs to evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of neuromuscular electrical stimulation in patients with chronic ankle instability. Our findings demonstrated significant improvements in self-reported instability, static and dynamic balance, and functional capacity following NMES intervention compared to control conditions. These results suggest that NMES serves as a beneficial modality within the spectrum of neuromuscular-targeted rehabilitation for CAI.

The improvement in subjective instability observed in this analysis is congruent with the neurophysiological premise that NMES enhances sensorimotor control [22]. NMES facilitates involuntary muscle contractions that assist in restoring the coordination between afferent and efferent pathways, which are frequently disrupted following recurrent ankle sprains [23]. By augmenting the activation of key stabilizing muscles, such as the peroneals, NMES may improve proprioceptive feedback and mitigate the pervasive “giving way” sensation experienced by patients [24].

This is particularly relevant during weight-bearing tasks, aligning with existing literature which suggests that NMES helps recalibrate sensory feedback from the affected joint, thereby restoring patient confidence and reducing perceived instability [25, 26]. However, it is crucial to temper these mechanistic interpretations with the methodological realities of the included trials. Subjective instability was measured using patient-reported outcome measures such as the CAIT. Given the inherent difficulties in blinding participants to electrical stimulation, several included studies were rated at a high risk of performance bias. Consequently, we must explicitly acknowledge that the observed improvements in subjective instability may be partially inflated by participant expectations or placebo effects, rather than stemming solely from the physiological benefits of NMES. Furthermore, while the pooled improvement in CAIT scores (WMD = 2.18) was statistically significant, it falls slightly below the widely accepted minimal clinically important difference threshold of ≥ 3 points for this tool [13]. This suggests that the actual clinical magnitude of subjective improvement may be modest. Therefore, while the mechanistic rationale is sound, the clinical evidence should be viewed as preliminary.

Regarding balance outcomes, both dynamic and static balance exhibited significant improvements following NMES treatment, albeit with notable variation in results. Dynamic balance, which necessitates maintaining stability during active movement [27], revealed substantial gains with a moderate-to-large clinical effect size (SMD = 0.73), indicating that NMES contributes to functional joint stability by engaging proprioceptive feedback loops and enhancing the excitability of stabilizing musculature like the peroneals [28]. This likely assists in modulating joint control mechanisms, improving the ability to stabilize the ankle during more complex, functional movements. These results are consistent with recent studies highlighting that NMES improves motor control and neuromuscular function, particularly in dynamic tasks that challenge joint stability under load [29].

In contrast, while static balance also showed a statistically significant improvement, this finding must be interpreted with extreme caution. The analysis was based on only two studies and exhibited substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 82.08%), with the pooled effect largely driven by a single trial. Because the limited number of studies precluded the use of sensitivity or subgroup analyses to explore the sources of this heterogeneity, confidence in this specific outcome is significantly constrained. From a physiological perspective, this discrepancy between robust dynamic balance and inconsistent static balance may be attributed to the nuances of NMES protocols; static postural control requires a complex integration of neuromuscular adaptations that likely extends beyond the immediate excitatory effects of NMES alone. Therefore, while NMES has the theoretical potential to reduce postural sway in CAI patients [30], the current pooled data is insufficient to confidently confirm this specific clinical benefit, and these initial observations should not be viewed as definitive.

The enhancement in functional capacity further highlights the potential clinical relevance of NMES for the CAI population. Functional recovery is a cornerstone of rehabilitation, reflecting a patient’s ability to perform daily activities and engage in complex physical tasks [31]. Our results indicate a statistically significant, albeit small, effect on functional capacity (SMD = 0.40). It is theorized that NMES may assist in optimizing muscle activation and neuromuscular coordination [32]; however, similar to subjective instability, functional capacity was assessed using self-reported tools (FAAM and FADI) across only three studies, many of which demonstrated moderate to high risks of bias. Without adequate participant blinding, these small improvements are particularly vulnerable to expectancy biases. Thus, the current evidence regarding functional recovery should be framed as preliminary rather than conclusive, highlighting the need for more rigorous, sham-controlled designs in future research to fully confirm whether NMES truly contributes to an improved quality of life and a reduced risk of reinjury [16].

Despite these promising findings, several limitations warrant consideration. First, the total number of eligible trials was relatively small with limited sample sizes, which restricts the statistical power of the pooled estimates and the generalizability of the conclusions. Second, substantial heterogeneity existed across studies regarding both NMES parameters and control conditions. This variability complicates the isolation of specific treatment effects and makes it difficult to pinpoint an optimal NMES protocol. Unfortunately, the limited number of studies statistically precluded the feasibility of subgroup or meta-regression analyses to further explore these potential confounders. Third, given the inherent difficulty in blinding participants to electrical stimulation, several studies were rated at high risk of performance bias. This may have introduced placebo effects or expectancy biases, particularly for self-reported outcomes like the CAIT and FAAM. Fourth, the lack of long-term follow-up data limits conclusions regarding the durability of NMES effects in preventing recurrence. Since the included interventions typically lasted between 4 and 8 weeks, our findings reflect short-term clinical benefits, and sustained efficacy remains to be confirmed. Finally, the small number of included studies prevented a formal assessment of publication bias; thus, the potential overrepresentation of positive results cannot be ruled out.

Conclusions

Current evidence suggests that NMES shows promise as an adjunctive therapy for individuals with CAI. However, given the observed methodological limitations and the substantial heterogeneity across stimulation protocols, these findings warrant cautious interpretation. Further rigorous, sham-controlled trials with larger sample sizes are essential to firmly validate its clinical efficacy and establish long-term durability.
