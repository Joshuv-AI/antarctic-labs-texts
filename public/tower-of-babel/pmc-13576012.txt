Understanding of Key Facts Relevant to Dialysis Care Among Patients Undergoing Hemodialysis
Authors: Richard Sean Lee, Wingel Xue, Mallika L Mendu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPatients with end-stage renal disease who are on hemodialysis (HD) experience high rates of hospitalization and fragmentation in care, which is associated with poor health outcomes and high health care costs. This study characterizes patients' awareness and understanding of the delivery of HD, which can serve as a predictive measure of the care they receive.MethodsThis prospective cross-sectional survey study included 100 participants who were recruited from the inpatient dialysis unit of a single institution that dialyzes acutely hospitalized patients. Patients who had been diagnosed with end-stage renal disease and were on maintenance HD were eligible for the study. Patient awareness and understanding of dialysis care were assessed by a structured questionnaire and verified against the electronic health record.ResultsOn average, the 100 participants answered 2.98 of 5 items correctly (59.6%). Lower educational attainment (high school or less vs college or greater) was associated with a reduced probability of correctly identifying one’s own dry weight (odds ratio [OR], 0.18; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.03–0.75) and primary outpatient nephrologist (OR, 0.26; 95% CI, 0.07–0.97). A primary language other than English predicted a lower probability of correctly identifying dialysis facility (OR, 0.14; 95% CI, 0.02–0.83).DiscussionAmong hospitalized patients with end-stage renal disease receiving acute, inpatient-based dialysis, few participants answered all of the questions about dialysis care correctly, and education level, sex, race, and primary language were associated with specific domains of awareness and understanding gaps.ConclusionsThese findings highlight actionable targets for improving awareness and understanding of care and support broader efforts to improve information transfer and patient engagement to enhance continuity and safety of dialysis care.

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease is a common, complex condition that affects 14% of the US population and can progress to end-stage renal disease (ESRD), which is typically managed with dialysis. 1 In the United States, 68% of ESRD patients receive a form of dialysis (57.9% in-center hemodialysis (HD), 1.6% in-home HD, 8.3% peritoneal dialysis), and 32% are living with a transplant. 2 More than 500,000 patients are currently on dialysis, with Black and Hispanic patients overrepresented among those with ESRD receiving dialysis. 3 Patients with ESRD who are on HD have high rates of health care utilization (1.5 hospitalizations per person-year, with 34% readmitted within 30 days) and higher associated costs ($45 billion in 2022, representing close to 6% of the Medicare budget). 2

High rates of hospitalization among patients receiving HD are thought to be a result of multiple factors, including fragmentation of care and lack of coordination between the hospital and outpatient dialysis units. 4 Mitigating strategies include improving the availability of discharge summaries to dialysis units, 5 transitions of care programs, 6 and optimization of volume. 7 Patient education and engagement is a less studied factor, even though the literature in other chronic illnesses has demonstrated an association with between increased patient education and improved clinical outcomes. 8 One study demonstrated that patient knowledge about dialysis is associated with increased utilization of permanent vascular access for HD. 9

“Patient activation” is a concept that refers to how engaged patients are with their care and has been associated with improved health outcomes. 10 Data related to patient activation among those with ESRD are limited, with 1 study demonstrating low patient activation among patients with ESRD. 11 Patient knowledge and awareness of the disease is a key factor in activation, but research in this area is scant. Cross-sectional surveys in Chinese, 12,13 Japanese, 14 and Finnish 15 populations have demonstrated that there are important knowledge gaps in understanding of dietary restrictions and symptoms associated with complications among patients with ESRD. However, no study to date has examined patients’ awareness and understanding of key, specific facts related to ESRD care in the United States, particularly during acute hospitalization.

This study was thus undertaken to explore and understand the awareness and understanding of key care delivery details among patients with ESRD receiving chronic HD in an inpatient setting. The details investigated included dialysis unit, frequency of dialysis, primary outpatient nephrologist, dry weight, and hypertension medications that could have a direct impact on the quality of care, chance of complications, and readmission. Toward this end, a brief survey study was conducted among patients in an inpatient dialysis unit that dialyzes acutely hospitalized patients in order to examine specific gaps in awareness and understanding as well as potential demographic predictors, which will allow for the development of interventions to improve patient awareness and potentially outcomes. The primary objective of this exploratory study was to characterize the awareness and understanding of critical facts of HD care among patients receiving dialysis to capture accurate, actionable information during the vulnerable time period of an acute admission.

Methods

STUDY DESIGN

This prospective cross-sectional survey study recruited a convenience sample of participants from the inpatient dialysis unit of Brigham and Women’s Hospital, a large academic quaternary care hospital in Boston, Massachusetts. Patients were enrolled between December 1, 2024, and June 30, 2025. This study was conducted in accordance with and approved by the Mass General Brigham Institutional Review Board under protocol number 2024P002656. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their inclusion in the study. All data collected were de-identified to protect participant confidentiality and privacy. Participation was voluntary, and patients were informed that they could withdraw from the study at any time without affecting their medical care.

PARTICIPANTS (PATIENT AND PUBLIC ENGAGEMENT)

This study received approval from the Mass General Brigham Institutional Review Board (IRB 2024P002656). An IRB-approved informational sheet was provided to all participants in this study, and a waiver of consent was granted. The study population comprised patients receiving outpatient maintenance HD at baseline who were admitted to the hospital for any acute illness requiring hospitalization. In the United States, the most common indications for admission for HD patients are noncardiovascular, noninfectious causes (0.6 events per person-year), although cardiovascular causes (0.39 events per person-year) and infection (0.34 events per person-year) are also common indications for hospitalization; the study population in this work had a similar distribution of primary reasons for admission. 2 Eligible patients were 18 years of age or older, had a clinical diagnosis of ESRD, and were receiving scheduled outpatient HD. Patients who did not speak English had an interpreter translate survey questions and patient responses. The recruitment process involved approaching patients in the inpatient dialysis unit who were alert, able to verbally respond for participation, and did not have critical illness, which yielded an 82% response rate. One hundred patients were recruited to participate in this study, and all recruited participants completed the survey (Figure). Patients in the inpatient dialysis unit are consistently asked about the clinical care they receive in their outpatient dialysis unit as part of standard clinical care, likely contributing to the high response rate. All data were de-identified following collection and prior to processing and analysis.

Workflow for patient selection and recruitment.

DATA COLLECTION

The research team designed a questionnaire to collect demographic data and dialysis awareness and understanding based on detailed patient interviews conducted by the Mass General Brigham Springboard Studio of patients transitioning to ESRD and/or receiving dialysis and their perception of gaps in care. The questionnaire included questions related to patient awareness of dialysis care delivery parameters including current dry weight, the name of the primary outpatient dialysis center, the name of the primary outpatient nephrologist, the frequency of dialysis, and the names of blood pressure medications. These 5 elements were chosen because they directly impact acute treatment when patients receive dialysis and pose the greatest risk for fragmentation of care. Each of the fields was a yes/no endorsement of awareness and understanding, followed by a free response if a participant responded “yes” to knowing the information. All questions were conveyed to patients verbally, and responses were recorded by the research team. A survey item was scored as “correct” for a given patient if the patient both endorsed awareness and understanding of that item and provided a response (eg, dry weight or current antihypertensive medications) that matched the information documented in the electronic health record (EHR). If either condition was not met, the item was scored as “incorrect.”

EHR review was utilized to collect demographic data on age, sex, primary language, highest level of education, total household income, and race and ethnicity. Responses were collected as multiple choice fields with stratifications that corresponded to US census categories in all domains except for race and ethnicity, which allowed participants to self-identify. To determine if answers to questions were accurate (“correct”), members of the research team conducted EHR review of the last dialysis unit record. For dry weight, responses were marked correct if the patient’s response was within 1 kg of the last weight documented at a dialysis facility. For outpatient dialysis center, frequency of dialysis, and primary outpatient nephrologist, outpatient dialysis records (which were obtained and integrated during hospitalization within the EHR) and nephrology consult notes were reviewed. The patient’s nephrologist was identified as their primary outpatient nephrologist at their usual dialysis facility. For antihypertensive medications, current admission notes and outpatient dialysis records, as received during any inpatient admission, were reviewed to confirm medications and dosing. Responses were marked correct if patients named all medications correctly. Patients who accurately identified that they were not currently taking any antihypertensive medications were also marked “correct.”

STATISTICAL ANALYSES

Demographic characteristics and responses to survey questions are presented as descriptive statistics; the demographic characteristics collected were age (18–44 years, 45–64 years, 65 years and older), education (some high school or less, high school diploma, college or higher), income (< $50,000, ≥ $50,000+, unknown), race (White, Black, other), primary language (English, other), sex (male, female), ethnicity (not Hispanic, Hispanic), and caregiver status (yes, no). Multivariable logistic regression was used to evaluate the association between demographic characteristics and the probability of a correct response for each domain, as well as the composite outcome (all 5 correct). For each regression, the dependent variable was the binary indicator of correct awareness and understanding; the independent variables were age group, educational attainment, household income, race, primary language, sex, ethnicity, and caregiver status. For variables with few events and nonconvergence, the categories of demographic characteristics were collapsed to promote convergence of the statistical model. Of note, in all statistical analyses involving race, the category “White” included patients who identified as White and not Hispanic. To account for the limited number of events in certain outcomes (and to mitigate the chance of sparse data bias and overfitting inherent in small-sample multivariable modeling), the authors employed Firth’s penalized likelihood regression. This approach provides more stable and less biased estimates than standard maximum likelihood estimation in scenarios with a low events-per-variable ratio. All analyses were performed using R, with the dplyr, tidyr, broom, and readr packages; code reproducibility was ensured through version-controlled scripts. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated using R’s generalized linear model function with a binomial logit link. Model outputs were exponentiated to provide interpretable ORs. For the composite "all 5 correct" analysis, individuals with missing education data were excluded from the regression.

Results

PARTICIPANT CHARACTERISTICS

A total of 100 maintenance HD patients completed the survey (Table 1). The median age was in the range of 45–64 years (42%); the other age categories were 18–44 years (12%) and 65 years or older (46%). Most participants were male (59%), identified as Black (50%) or White (48%), spoke English as their primary language (89%), and identified as not Hispanic. The education levels of the participants were categorized as some high school or less/deferred (21%), high school diploma (46%), and college or higher (33%). The household income of the study participants was most commonly less than $50,000 (44%), whereas 28% had incomes greater than $50,000, and 28% declined to answer or did not know their household income. Nearly two-thirds (64%) reported having a caregiver.

OVERALL SURVEY PERFORMANCE

The mean number of questions answered correctly was 2.98 (out of 5), corresponding to a mean percentage correct of 59.6%. By question, the number of correct responses varied substantially. Patients most often endorsed knowing and correctly answered questions surrounding their frequency of dialysis (98% and 95%, respectively), followed by dialysis facility (94% and 85%, respectively) (Table 2). Questions that patients least endorsed knowing and were least able to answer correctly included those involving their dry weight (75%, and 33%, respectively) and hypertension medications (49% and 29%, respectively). The question regarding the name of the primary outpatient nephrologist was answered by 66% of the participants but was correctly answered by 56% (Table 2). Although the study participants demonstrated varying degrees of success across the survey, they were equally likely to struggle with dry weight and medication accuracy (33% vs 29%, P = .57). Aside from this specific pair, all other differences in domain-specific areas were statistically significant (see Supplementary Appendix 1).

SURVEY QUESTIONS AND CORRECT RESPONSES BY DEMOGRAPHIC AGE GROUP AND SEX

Awareness and understanding regarding dry weight varied across age groups (Table 3), with participants aged 45–64 years demonstrating the highest percentage of correct responses for dry weight (38.10%), followed by those aged 18–44 years (33.33%) and those 65 years and older (28.26%). The younger age group (18–44 years old) had the highest percentages of correct identification of the dialysis facility (91.67%) and recognition of their nephrologist (66.67%), as well as perfect awareness and understanding regarding dialysis frequency (100%) and the highest proportion of correct responses regarding hypertensive medications (58.33%). However, all age groups had low rates of answering all 5 questions correctly, with only 16.67% being correct in both the 45–64 years and 18–44 years age groups and 6.52% being correct in those older than 65 years. Female participants demonstrated higher correct reporting on dry weight (41.46% vs 27.12% for male participants) and medications (39.02% vs 22.03% for male participants), whereas male participants more often answered correctly regarding nephrologist identification (66.10% vs 41.46%). Both sexes had similarly low rates of providing correct answers to all 5 questions (10.17% vs 14.63%).

EDUCATION AND INCOME

Participants with a college degree or higher provided a greater percentage of correct responses regarding identification of their nephrologist (66.67%) and hypertensive medications (27.27%), but a lower percentage of correct responses regarding their dry weight (39.39%) compared to those with a high school degree (36.96%). Achieving all 5 correct responses was rare across educational groups; it was highest among those with college or higher education (18.18%), compared to 13.04% for high school graduates and 0% for those with a high school degree or less. Those with reported incomes of less than $50,000 had a lower percentage of correctly identifying their nephrologist (47.73%), site of dialysis (84.09%), and hypertensive medications (20.45%) compared to those with incomes of $50,000 and greater (67.86%, 89.47%, and 35.71%, respectively). The proportion answering all 5 questions correctly did not exceed 14.3% in any income group.

RACE, ETHNICITY, AND LANGUAGE

White participants had a higher percentage of correct dry weight identification (37.50% vs 30.00% for Black participants), whereas correct identification of the site and nephrologist were notably higher for Black participants (90.00% and 52.00%, respectively) compared to White participants (79.17% and 62.50%, respectively). Participants who identified as Hispanic had similar or higher likelihoods of correct responses in some domains (dry weight, 37.50%; frequency, 100%) compared to individuals who did not identify as Hispanic (32.61% and 94.57%, respectively), and comparable proportions answered all 5 questions correctly (12.50% vs 11.96%). English-speaking participants outperformed those who spoke other languages in most domains, notably with correct dry weight (33.71% vs 27.27%), site (87.64% vs 63.64%), and nephrologist (59.55% vs 27.27%). No participant in the non-English-speaking group answered all 5 questions correctly.

CAREGIVER STATUS

Participants with caregiver support had slightly higher percentages of correct responses regarding dry weight (34.38% vs 30.56%) than those without a caregiver, but there was minimal difference in the rate of answering all 5 questions correctly (12.50% vs 11.11%).

Across all demographic subgroups, the rates of correctly answering all 5 domains were low (< 20%). Variations were found across age, education, income, race, and language, but no subgroup demonstrated consistently high performance across all domains. Female participants had higher correct response rates regarding medications, whereas English speakers and those with higher levels of education generally performed better in several domains.

MULTIVARIABLE REGRESSION ANALYSIS BY AWARENESS AND UNDERSTANDING DOMAIN

In the adjusted analysis, lower education was significantly associated with decreased probability of correctly identifying dry weight and nephrologist (Table 4). Specifically, compared to participants with a college degree or higher, those with a high school education or less had a significantly reduced probability of correctly identifying their dry weight (OR, 0.18; 95% CI, 0.03–0.75; P = .0177) and their nephrologist (OR, 0.26; 95% CI, 0.07–0.97; P = .0444). Being female was significantly associated with a lower probability of correctly identifying one’s nephrologist (OR, 0.31; 95% CI, 0.13–0.73; P = .0069). For correct identification of the facility, a primary language other than English was associated with a lower probability of identifying the dialysis facility compared to those with a primary language of English (OR, 0.14; 95% CI, 0.02–0.83; P = .0307).

Discussion

In this study, the authors found that there were substantial gaps in patients' awareness and understanding of critical facts pertinent to the delivery of dialysis care. Although almost all participants were able to accurately identify their weekly dialysis frequency and their dialysis facility, there were notable gaps in awareness and understanding relevant to high-quality continuity of care. Patients were least accurate in the domains of ability to name their primary outpatient nephrologist (56%), dry weight (33%), and blood pressure medications (29%), which are critical facts relevant to patients’ continuity of dialysis care. Participants also accurately identified all relevant facts with low frequency (< 20%). Because of the exploratory nature of this study and the multiple outcomes assessed, formal adjustments for multiple comparisons were not applied. Consequently, these results should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating, and the reported P values should be viewed as nominal. The statistical modeling in this work was limited by being exploratory in nature and containing few events, but it provides directional guidance. Education level emerged as an important determinant of awareness and understanding in several areas (dry weight and nephrologist), and female sex was associated with a lower probability of correctly identifying the nephrologist, echoing gender-based disparities in health care communication or engagement evinced in prior literature. 16 Notably, language played a prominent role in patients’ ability to identify the dialysis facility. In contrast, no significant demographic predictors emerged for awareness and understanding regarding dialysis frequency or medications. These consistent demographic signals across multiple domains suggest a pattern of inequitable information access, identifying actionable targets for future interventions aimed at standardizing patient awareness and understanding in the HD setting.

Earlier work identifying dialysis patients' awareness and understanding of the facts of their care has been limited; however, the findings reported here are in line with research in the HD population analyzing patient engagement, a measure that combines patient knowledge, confidence, and self-management skills for medical conditions, in those receiving HD. A recent US study surveying patients receiving HD using the Patient Activation Measure found that more than two-thirds of patients receiving HD reported low activation, with higher scores among younger patients, those with higher educational attainment, and female patients. 11

The findings of this study suggest that there is potential for health system–based and targeted interventions to improve patient awareness and understanding, particularly in domains critical for care coordination and clinical outcomes. The association between education and language for key awareness and understanding domains highlights potential areas of intervention. The data reported herein illustrate vulnerabilities that arise in the absence of clinical data interoperability. In most settings, dialysis organizations and health systems have disparate EHRs that are unable to communicate directly, limiting their ability to share critical clinical information in a timely manner. 17 The authors previously advocated for advancements in interoperability for the care of patients with ESRD, proposing solutions such as 1) a central web-based exchange interface, allowing the exchange of predefined clinical data between systems and 2) the adoption of Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resource (FHIR)-based application programming interfaces presenting health care data in a way that is easier for practitioners to share. 18 In particular, dry weight is a clinical domain amenable to EHR data sharing that can impact acute care delivery, length of stay, and readmissions; there are also implications for dialysis treatment adherence, as patient-reported dry weight can be utilized by patients to maintain optimal fluid balance. Similarly, the transfer of information related to hypertension medications can improve blood pressure control before, during, and after dialysis treatments, impacting care delivered in the hospital. Recently, the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services announced an Interoperability Framework as a “call to action for health data networks,” and Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services has specifically referenced the ESRD population as a key constituency that would benefit from interoperability. 19,20 The authors would advocate for this type of broad US health system approach to remedying gaps in awareness and understanding as well as fragmentation of care.

Given identified gaps in awareness and understanding and their association with education and language in particular, interventions targeted at improving patient understanding of dry weight, blood pressure medications, nephrologist name , and site of dialysis care are warranted. One low-cost intervention could be providing patients with standardized dialysis care information cards that contain the site of dialysis, name of their nephrologist, dry weight, and the names and dosages of their blood pressure medications. Ensuring that this information is displayed at an appropriate grade level and is translated appropriately is crucial based on the findings of this work. Researchers in China recently published a report on the use of an eHealth card, an electronic version of health information cards that includes a more comprehensive summary of health conditions, medications, vaccinations, and so on. 21 The findings reported herein also highlight the importance of using interpreters and reinforcing dialysis care facts with patients on a frequent basis, which are strategies that can be adopted by dialysis centers and health systems as best practices. Finally, adequate awareness and understanding of care are critical to ensuring patient activation, associated with clinical outcomes. Further research directions include an analysis of patient awareness and understanding in a larger, multicenter study, as well as pilot studies to determine whether targeted interventions can improve awareness, understanding, engagement and activation.

This study was limited by several factors. First, the exploratory study sample was recruited from a single dialysis facility at a tertiary academic medical center, which may not be representative of the general dialysis patient population. Only patients receiving inpatient dialysis who were admitted for an acute illness were recruited for this study, which may impact the baseline cognitive status of the participants, given the psychological and physical stressors of acute illness. However, for this reason, hospitalized patients represent a critical population to study because of their vulnerability to fragmentation of care. Similarly, patients with ESRD can have numerous comorbidities, also increasing their vulnerability to care gaps. Duration on dialysis could also have a potential impact on the results, and this study was limited in identifying this variable because of inconsistent access to dialysis initiation dates. Items were defined as “correct” based on patients endorsing that they knew the answer to a question and providing a response matching the EHR record; this introduces potential bias in the subgroup comparisons. The survey designed in this study also required unprompted recall, whereas patients in the real world may have access to resources that may assist in the accurate identification of information (for example, prescription bottles or home records). The sample size was relatively modest, limiting the statistical power of this work to detect small differences, and therefore, it serves as an exploratory analysis to guide future studies. The cross-sectional design used in this work precludes an assessment of causality, and self-reported patient awareness and understanding or demographics may be subject to recall or social desirability bias. Lastly, the authors recognize that the EHR reflects a single point in time within a dynamic health care environment; clinical information outlined in the EHR reflects how care is reconciled and delivered for the dialysis population, which is an inherent limitation.

IMPLICATIONS FOR CLINICAL PRACTICE

This exploratory and hypothesis-generating work fills an important gap in the published literature on patients receiving dialysis by characterizing their awareness and understanding of facts immediately pertinent to their ability to receive high-quality care in a US cohort. In this sample of acutely hospitalized patients receiving maintenance HD, many patients were unable to provide EHR-concordant responses to selected dialysis-related questions. Although this study identified substantial associations between certain demographic factors and awareness and understanding in specific domains, these differences did not persist in evaluations of the more stringent outcome of comprehensive awareness and understanding across all 5 domains. These findings underscore the importance of ongoing, multifaceted education approaches to support patients in developing well-rounded awareness and understanding essential for effective self-management and safety in dialysis care.
