Role of gut microbiota in cancer modulation: molecular mechanisms and emerging therapeutic strategies
Authors: Subham Preetam, Jutishna Bora, Sthiti Porna Dutta, Nayan Talukdar, Sarvesh Rustagi, Shailendra Thapliyal, Sumira Malik, Neeraj Choudhary, Dinesh Kumar, Suresh Babu Kondaveeti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe gut microbiota exerts profound effects on host physiology, immunity, and disease susceptibility, with growing evidence implicating its role in cancer initiation, progression, and therapy response. Dysbiosis and microbial-derived metabolites contribute to oncogenesis by modulating epithelial signalling, immune regulation, and systemic inflammation. This review synthesises current knowledge on the crosstalk between the gut microbiome and cancer, spanning molecular mechanisms, site-specific malignancies, and treatment outcomes. We discuss how microbial pathways, including NF-κB, STAT3, Wnt/β-catenin, and inflammasome activation, shape tumour biology, and how microbiota profiles correlate with cancer risk and progression in colorectal, gastric, liver, breast, gynaecological, and pulmonary cancers. Furthermore, we highlight the microbiome’s impact on responses to chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and immunotherapy. Finally, we evaluate therapeutic strategies targeting the microbiota, from conventional dietary and probiotic interventions to cutting-edge approaches such as faecal microbiota transplantation, engineered bacterial strains, and microbiome-based biomarkers. Understanding these complex interactions offers novel opportunities to integrate microbiome science into precision oncology.Graphical Abstract Keywords: Gut microbiota, Cancer, Dysbiosis, Tumor microenvironment, Microbial metabolites, Immune modulation, Oncogenesis

Introduction

The human gastrointestinal tract harbours trillions of microorganisms, bacteria, archaea, fungi, and viruses collectively known as the gut microbiota. This diverse ecosystem is now recognised as an integral component of human biology, with functions that extend far beyond digestion [1, 2]. The microbiota contributes to the breakdown of dietary fibers, production of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), synthesis of essential vitamins, and regulation of bile acid metabolism [3, 4]. Equally important, it educates and shapes the immune system, reinforcing tolerance to commensal microbes while enabling defence against pathogens [1, 3, 5]. Through these multifaceted roles, the gut microbiota exerts a profound influence on systemic physiology, ranging from energy balance to neuroendocrine function [5–8].

Disruptions to this balanced ecosystem, commonly referred to as dysbiosis, have been increasingly associated with chronic disease states, including obesity, metabolic syndrome, inflammatory bowel disease, and cancer [7, 8]. While the traditional view of cancer has emphasised genetic mutations and epigenetic alterations as the primary drivers of oncogenesis, accumulating evidence suggests that microbial factors play equally significant roles in tumour initiation and progression [1]. Gut microbial dysbiosis has been linked to impaired intestinal barrier integrity and chronic inflammation, and many studies report strong associations between dysbiosis and digestive-system cancers. Importantly, most human data are observational, while causality is mainly demonstrated in animal or in vitro models (e.g. fecal microbiota transplants from tumor-bearing mice induce pro-tumorigenic changes in recipients [6, 9].

Several mechanistic pathways have been identified through which the gut microbiota shapes cancer biology. Microbial ligands can interact with pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) such as Toll-like receptors (TLRs) and nucleotide-binding oligomerisation domain (NOD)-like receptors, activating downstream inflammatory cascades including NF-κB and STAT3 signalling [2, 6]. This results in the production of cytokines and growth factors that sustain tumour-promoting inflammation. In parallel, bacterial metabolites exert both protective and harmful effects: while SCFAs [9, 10], such as butyrate, enhance epithelial barrier integrity and regulate immune homeostasis, secondary bile acids and certain genotoxins can induce DNA damage and promote oncogenic mutations. These dual roles highlight the complexity of host–microbe interactions, wherein the same microbial ecosystem can yield opposing outcomes depending on context [9].

Besides bacteria, the gut microbiome also contains fungi (microbiota), which have recently been identified as factors modulating cancers. As in recent comprehensive reviews, authors emphasizes the existence of a fungi-tumor axis [11, 12]. For instance, it has been shown that the fungal species Candida albicans can not only downregulate intestinal immune response but also lead to the development of colon tumors in mouse experiments [11].In addition, gut fungi have been connected with tumors outside the intestine: it was shown that Malassezia spp. which is a normal commensal in the intestine becomes a cancer-causing agent after migrating to the pancreas whereas lung tumor tissues contain Aspergillus sydowii which may be involved in creating an immunosuppressive environment. Furthermore, gut mycobiome patterns are being studied as potential diagnostic markers [11, 12].

Beyond tumour initiation, the gut microbiota also exerts a significant influence on cancer progression and therapeutic response. In preclinical and clinical studies, specific microbial communities have been linked to sensitivity or resistance to chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and particularly immunotherapy with immune checkpoint inhibitors [6, 13]. For instance, beneficial taxa such as Akkermansia muciniphila and Faecalibacterium prausnitzii have been associated with improved immunotherapy outcomes, while pathogenic or dysbiotic communities may blunt therapeutic efficacy. These findings underscore the potential of modulating the microbiome to enhance treatment effectiveness, opening new frontiers in precision oncology. The relationship between gut microbes and cancer is not unidirectional [8–10]. Tumours themselves can remodel the microbiota by altering host immunity, metabolism, and local tissue environments. Moreover, cancer therapies such as antibiotics, cytotoxic drugs, and radiation frequently disrupt microbial communities, which in turn may exacerbate side effects, compromise immunity, and influence patient prognosis [8, 9]. This bidirectional crosstalk suggests that maintaining or restoring a healthy microbiome could be a critical adjunct to cancer care. Therapeutically, microbiome-targeted interventions are rapidly advancing from concept to clinic. Conventional approaches such as diet modification, prebiotics, and probiotics are being complemented by more innovative strategies, including faecal microbiota transplantation (FMT), administration of defined microbial consortia, and even engineered bacteria designed to deliver anticancer agents or immune modulators directly to tumours [2, 8, 9]. Parallel efforts are focused on developing microbial biomarkers to predict cancer risk, monitor disease progression, and guide personalised therapies [14, 15]. Together, these strategies embody a new paradigm in oncology, wherein the microbiome is not merely a passive bystander but an active determinant of therapeutic outcome.

This review synthesises the growing body of literature on the crosstalk between gut microbiota and cancer, emphasising both mechanistic underpinnings and translational opportunities. We begin by outlining the composition and functional roles of the gut microbiome in health, followed by a detailed analysis of how dysbiosis contributes to oncogenesis across multiple organ systems. We then examine evidence linking microbial signatures to clinical outcomes in colorectal, gastric, hepatic, breast, gynaecological, pulmonary, and other malignancies. Special attention is given to the impact of the microbiota on responses to immunotherapy, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy. Finally, we explore the therapeutic landscape, highlighting emerging microbiome-based interventions and discussing future challenges in translating these findings into clinical practice. By integrating mechanistic, clinical, and therapeutic perspectives, this review seeks to provide a comprehensive framework for understanding how the gut microbiome can be harnessed in cancer prevention and treatment.

Gut microbiota in human health

Composition and diversity

The human gut harbours a highly complex and dynamic community of microorganisms, estimated at trillions of cells, that collectively outnumber human somatic and germ cells [1, 2]. Among these, bacteria constitute the dominant kingdom, primarily drawn from the phyla Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes, which together account for more than 90% of the total microbial population. Other important contributors include Actinobacteria, Proteobacteria, Verrucomicrobia, and Fusobacteria, as well as diverse archaea, fungi, and viruses that add functional layers to the ecosystem [9–14]. This taxonomic diversity is mirrored by profound functional specialisation, with microbes encoding a gene repertoire vastly exceeding that of the human genome.

The microbial composition is not uniform throughout the gastrointestinal tract. The stomach and proximal small intestine harbour relatively sparse communities due to acidic pH and rapid flow, while the distal ileum and colon support dense and diverse microbial populations, reaching concentrations of up to 10¹² cells per gram of luminal content. Within individuals, spatial variation is complemented by temporal shifts influenced by diet, circadian rhythms, host metabolism, and immune responses. Nevertheless, the gut microbiota typically achieves a dynamic equilibrium, maintaining functional stability through ecological resilience and redundancy [14, 15].

Inter-individual variability is another hallmark of the gut Microbiota. Each person’s microbial community represents a unique fingerprint, shaped by a complex interplay of host genetics, early-life exposures, diet, geography, antibiotic usage, and lifestyle factors [3, 4]. Despite this variation, a relatively stable core microbiota emerges during adulthood, enriched in functional groups critical for host physiology. These include short-chain fatty acid (SCFA)-producing taxa such as Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, Roseburia, and Eubacterium, which promote intestinal barrier integrity, regulate immune homeostasis, and exert anti-inflammatory effects (Fig. 1) [1, 3, 9]. Diversity within the microbiota is frequently cited as a marker of health. High microbial richness and evenness are associated with metabolic resilience, immune competence, and reduced susceptibility to pathogens. Conversely, reduced diversity, whether due to antibiotics, Westernised dietary patterns, or chronic inflammation, has been linked to obesity, type 2 diabetes, inflammatory bowel disease, and increased cancer risk. Diversity itself, however, must be interpreted with nuance while low richness is generally detrimental, not all taxa are equally beneficial, and the expansion of pathobionts, such as Escherichia coli or Enterococcus faecalis, can occur even in diverse ecosystems [13, 14].

Diet plays a central role in shaping gut microbial composition and function. Diets rich in complex polysaccharides and fiber support SCFA-producing organisms, which generate metabolites such as acetate, propionate, and butyrate that confer anti-inflammatory and anticancer properties [4, 5]. In contrast, high-fat, high-protein, and low-fiber diets characteristic of Westernized populations favor bile-tolerant organisms and potentially pathogenic taxa, increasing the production of secondary bile acids and carcinogenic compounds such as N-nitroso species. Circadian rhythms also modulate microbial abundance and function, reflecting reciprocal interactions between the microbiome and host metabolic cycles. Advances in high-throughput sequencing, shotgun metagenomics, and multi-omics approaches have enabled unprecedented insights into both taxonomic and functional diversity [4, 9–13]. These technologies reveal that microbial function is often more conserved than taxonomy, emphasising the importance of metabolic capacity rather than species identity alone. Large-scale population studies, such as the Human Microbiome Project and international microbiome consortia, continue to refine the definition of a healthy microbiome baseline [1]. Rather than discrete enterotypes, current evidence supports a continuum of community structures shaped by gradients of diet, geography, and host physiology.

A critical challenge in microbiome research is disentangling true causal relationships from associations confounded by host and environmental variables. Factors such as body mass index (BMI), inflammation, medication use, and dietary intake have a profound influence on microbial signatures, necessitating rigorous study design and statistical control [9–13]. Nevertheless, defining the ecological principles and functional attributes of a healthy microbiota provides an essential foundation for understanding its role in disease, including cancer.

Functional roles

The gut Microbiome fulfils a wide spectrum of metabolic, protective, and regulatory functions that are indispensable for host health. A central activity is the fermentation of dietary fibers into short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) such as acetate, propionate, and butyrate [3–8, 16]. These metabolites serve as the primary energy source for colonocytes, reinforce epithelial barrier integrity, and exert systemic effects by modulating immune responses and influencing epigenetic regulation. Notably, butyrate acts as a histone deacetylase inhibitor, altering gene expression patterns in ways that can suppress inflammation and tumorigenesis.

In addition to SCFA production, gut microbes contribute to the synthesis of essential vitamins, including vitamin K and B-complex vitamins and play a pivotal role in bile acid metabolism [8, 13]. Primary bile acids synthesised in the liver are transformed by microbial enzymes into secondary bile acids, which regulate lipid absorption, host metabolism, and immune signalling. While some bile acid derivatives are protective, others may act as carcinogens, underscoring the dual nature of microbial metabolism [3, 4]. The microbiota also participates in xenobiotic metabolism, influencing the pharmacokinetics and toxicity of therapeutic drugs. Certain bacterial enzymes can reverse the inactivation of metabolites, as exemplified by irinotecan-induced toxicity: the active metabolite SN-38 can be regenerated in the gut lumen, leading to severe gastrointestinal side effects. Such findings underscore the microbiome’s crucial role in influencing drug efficacy and safety [3, 4, 8, 16, 17].

Furthermore, microbial metabolism extends to host hormonal regulation. The so-called estrobolome, the collection of microbial genes involved in estrogen metabolism, modulates systemic estrogen levels and has been linked to the risk of hormone-driven cancers such as breast and endometrial malignancies [17, 18]. Through these diverse functions, ranging from nutrient processing to immune and endocrine modulation, the gut microbiota emerges not only as a metabolic organ but also as a powerful determinant of host physiology, disease susceptibility, and therapeutic outcomes.

Microbiota-immune interactions

There is constant immunological cross-talk between gut microbes and the host. Intestinal immune cells sample microbial products via pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) such as Toll-like receptors (TLRs) and NOD-like receptors (NLRs) [19–21]. When it comes to maintaining homeostasis, the host possesses robust regulatory pathways, such as IgA secretion, antimicrobial peptides, and Tregs, which work together to keep inflammation in check and prevent it from escalating. Microbial ligands (LPS, flagellin, peptidoglycan) can activate NF-κB and inflammasome pathways when the epithelial barrier is breached. Commensals play a crucial role in the development of the immune system [21]. For instance, some segmented filamentous bacteria can provoke the formation of Th17 cells. Additionally, Bacteroides fragilis releases polysaccharide A, which plays a crucial role in the generation of T regulatory cells (Tregs). Immune cells such as GPR43 and GPR45 also have G protein–coupled receptors, which interact with short-chain fatty acids, also known as SCFAs. Their role is to initiate an anti-inflammatory response and protect the epithelial barrier (Fig. 2) [19–22]. Balance is key, and with dysbiosis, the damage done can lead to chronic inflammation. more, increasingly, the role of gut microbiota is being discussed, especially as an “immune organ” and the implications that it can have for T cell regulation and cancer immunosurveillance [19–22]. Recently it was found that microbial metabolites could help with checkpoint therapies directly. One example is the finding of Jia et al. in 2024 that indole-3-propionic acid (IPA) produced by Lactobacillus johnsonii (in conjunction with Clostridium sporogenes) significantly enhances the potency of antiPD-1 by encouraging the development of a stem-like program in CD8 T cells [23]. The IPA treatment raised the pool of progenitor-exhausted CD8 T cells and ameliorated the ICB responses not only in melanoma but also in breast and colorectal cancer models [23]. This is a good example of how specific bacterial products might be used as immunotherapy adjuvants.

Gut microbiota and carcinogenesis

Dysbiosis and cancer risk

Gut dysbiosis, defined as a disrupted microbial balance, is linked epidemiologically and mechanistically to cancer risk. Chronic dysbiosis may arise from diet, antibiotics, inflammation or infection [20]. Dysbiotic states often involve a decline in beneficial commensals, such as butyrate producers like Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, while simultaneously seeing an increase in pathobionts, including Fusobacterium nucleatum and Enterotoxigenic B. fragilis (ETBF). Such shifts promote carcinogenesis through multiple routes. For example, butyrate-producing Firmicutes normally inhibit tumour growth by nourishing colon cells and activating regulatory immune pathways. Their depletion removes this brake (Fig. 3) [24–26]. In contrast, pathogens like F. nucleatum can adhere to epithelial cells and invade tumours, where they activate oncogenic signalling [1, 24]. Chronic inflammatory conditions (IBD, cirrhosis) often display dysbiosis, raising cancer risk. Longitudinal epidemiological data suggest that individuals with sustained gut dysbiosis (following infection or chronic antibiotic use) have a higher subsequent incidence of colorectal and liver cancers. Causality is a tricky business, often complicated by various host factors. The latest research underscores the importance of considering factors such as diet, family history, and other available potential health issues to accurately identify microbial risk factors [15, 24].

Microbial metabolites and carcinogenic pathways

Certain microorganisms within the gastrointestinal tract produce a remarkable variety of bioactive metabolites that may exert a tumorigenic effect or may even suppress tumour growth. These include microbes in the gut that transform primary bile acids into secondary bile acids, specifically deoxycholic acid (DCA) and lithocholic acid (LCA) [15, 24]. These hydrophobic bile acids, when present in excess, can cause DNA damage, oxidative stress, and stimulate cellular proliferation. For instance, DCA accumulation induces senescence of hepatic stellate cells (HSC) and release of tumour-promoting factors (e.g. IL-6) in the liver [27]. Excess of secondary bile acids in colorectal tissues is associated with adenoma formation. Moreover, certain gut microbes are known to synthesise ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA), which is a bile acid that is less toxic and has anti-tumour properties.

Microbial decarboxylation of amino acids generates polyamines (e.g., putrescine) and indole derivatives (from tryptophan) that impact cell growth and immunity [7, 13]. Aberrant polyamine metabolism is a hallmark of tumour growth. Some indole derivatives are immunosuppressive and activate AhR. Bacterial genotoxins: Some bacteria in our gut can produce toxins that directly damage DNA. For instance, Escherichia coli bacteria carrying the pks genomic island produce the genotoxin colibactin, which leads to DNA interstrand cross-links and mutational signatures of human CRC. Bacteroides fragilis generates the metalloprotease BFT (fragilysin) that targets E-cadherin, thereby stimulating β-catenin/Wnt signalling and IL-17 synthesis [1]. These metabolite effects tend to converge on host oncogenic pathways. For example, microbial LPS activates TLR4 on intestinal cells, leading to NF-κB and STAT3 signalling, which results in the induction of cyclin D1, COX-2, and anti-apoptotic genes. Secondary bile acids can activate the farnesoid X receptor (FXR) or TGR5 to control NF-κB and β-catenin. Concurrently, loss of SCFA signalling eliminates suppressive signals on NF-κB/IL-6 while enhancing tumorigenesis [3, 4].

Inflammation, immune dysregulation, and tumorigenesis

One of the recurring themes of microbiota cancer cross-talk is inflammation. Dysbiosis causes gut hyperpermeability; microbial products (LPS, flagellin, peptidoglycan) permeate the epithelium and activate innate receptors (TLRs, NLRs) on immune and epithelial cells. For instance, LPS ligation of TLR4 activates the MyD88–NF–κB pathway, inducing pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, TNF-α) and generating a tumour-supportive microenvironment. In colorectal cancer, tumour promotion in chronic colitis models is TLR4- and IL-1β-dependent [25, 26]. In the liver, as mentioned, gut-derived LPS activates Kupffer cells via TLR4, inducing the production of TNF-α and TGF-β, which promote fibrosis and cancer [27].

Inflammasomes (e.g. NLRP3) also sense microbial disturbances. Activation of NLRP3 leads to IL-1β and IL-18 release, which have complex effects: in some contexts (e.g. early tumorigenesis), IL-18 can maintain epithelial barrier and anti-tumour immunity, whereas chronic IL-1β signalling fuels carcinogenesis. For example, in CRC models, NLRP3-stimulated IL-1β signalling enhances NF-κB and COX-2, thereby promoting cancer cell growth and angiogenesis [25, 26]. However, in other contexts, intact inflammasome signalling keeps in balance dysbiosis-driven tumours by sustaining mucosal immunity. One key pathway is STAT3, which is typically activated by cytokines (such as IL-6 and IL-11) and microbial ligands. Chronic STAT3 activation leads to the expression of anti-apoptotic and proliferative genes. For instance, enterotoxigenic B. fragilis induces Th17/IL-17 responses that activate STAT3 in colonic epithelium, leading to tumour growth. Similarly, some microbial metabolites stimulate STAT3 directly by G-protein-coupled receptors. Lastly, dysbiosis skews adaptive immunity. Tumour microbiota can enhance checkpoint molecules like PD-L1, inhibit NK cells (for example, H. pylori inhibits NK NKG2D ligands [28–30], or recruit immunosuppressive cells. For example, in pancreatic cancer models, translocated Enterococcus and E. coli in tumours engage TLR2/4/5 on myeloid cells, reprogramming macrophages to an M2 immunosuppressive phenotype [22]. These immune modulations help cancers escape surveillance (Fig. 3).

Taken together, microbial influences on cancer tend to converge on a few core pathways. Persistent activation of NF-B and STAT3 by microbial signals leads to continuous inflammation and cell survival signaling [31]. Wnt/-catenin signalling is yet another common hub, e.g. F. nucleatum FadA binding E-cadherin and certain bacterial toxins both activate catenin in colonic epithelium [31]. Toll-like receptors (TLRs) that sense bacterial components also induce NF-B mediated inflammation in different tissues. Therefore, even with differences in cancer types (IL-17STAT3 axis in colitis-associated CRC), most of the pro-tumorigenic effects of dysbiosis are channelled through the NF-B/STAT3/-catenin axis [32].

Host microbiome genetic interactions

Host genetics and microbial genetics are interwoven in cancer risk. Human genetic variants (e.g., SNPs in innate immune genes) influence microbiome composition; for example, polymorphisms in TLRs or NOD2 can predispose individuals to dysbiosis in IBD and possibly cancer. Conversely, microbial strain variation affects pathogenicity: a recent GWAS of H. pylori strains identified bacterial gene variants that increase gastric cancer risk [17]. Similarly, host genetic polymorphisms in the autophagy gene may determine how the body handles certain bacteria.

At the genome-wide level, co-evolutionary analysis indicates that tumour mutational profiles are generally concordant with microbiome changes. In one illustration, CRC tumour microbiomes capture the tumour’s burden of mutations, suggesting a feedback between tumour genomics and microbial community [17]. Microbiota also epigenetically reprogram host cells; signals from microbes reprogram DNA methylation and chromatin accessibility in epithelial cells [3, 17]. The microbiome host genomics interaction is thus an active field of research. These interactions make it difficult to infer causation, it is challenging to ascertain whether dysbiosis is a driver or a passenger of tumorigenesis in the absence of longitudinal and mechanistic research.

Importantly, while numerous studies demonstrate strong associations between host genetic variants, microbial composition, and cancer-related phenotypes, establishing direct causality remains challenging [15, 24]. Most human studies rely on cross-sectional or case-control designs that cannot fully disentangle whether microbiome alterations are drivers, modifiers, or consequences of tumorigenesis. Definitive causal inference requires longitudinal cohorts, mechanistic validation in gnotobiotic or genetically engineered models, and controlled microbial perturbation studies [13]. Recognising this distinction is essential to avoid overinterpretation of associative microbiome–host genetic findings in cancer.

Microbiota and certain cancers

Colorectal cancer

CRC is the third most prevalent cancer globally and an archetypal microbiota-induced cancer. CRC incidence has been increasing, particularly in young adults younger than 50 [15, 33–35]. The all-encompassing risk factors are diet (high-fat diet, red meat intake, low intake of fibre), IBD, and genetics (APC, KRAS, mismatch repair genes) (Fig. 4). Dysbiosis is a significant environmental risk factor. More recent high-throughput metagenomic investigations have further tightened CRC microbial signatures. A Nature Medicine report (589 patients, 2024) demonstrated that, when controlling for confounders (inflammation, transit time, BMI), self-reported CRC markers such as F. nucleatum lost significance, but other taxa (Anaerococcus vaginalis, Dialister pneumosintes, Parvimonas micra, Peptostreptococcus anaerobius) remained strongly correlated [15]. This suggests that consistent CRC-associated microbes may differ from earlier claims, underscoring the need to control covariates. Stool tests enriched for CRC (faecal immunochemical tests plus microbial DNA) are an area of active investigation. Interestingly, age-dependent microbiome patterns have emerged. Early-onset CRC (< 50 years) shows a distinct gut and tumour microbiome composition compared to late-onset disease [36]. In early CRC, there are stronger microbe–host interaction networks (reflected in tumour gene expression) than in older patients [36], suggesting that microbiota may play a particularly direct role in younger-onset tumours.

Several gut microbes exert oncogenic effects in CRC. Fusobacterium nucleatum is enriched in most CRC tissues and facilitates tumour growth by binding to epithelial cells (through FadA adhesin) and activating β-catenin signalling and NF-κB [1, 22, 24]. It also generates autophagy through TLR4/MyD88 to inhibit 5-FU-induced apoptosis in tumour cells and induce chemoresistance. Enterotoxigenic B. fragilis (ETBF) secretes BFT toxin, which cleaves E-cadherin, activates Wnt/β-catenin and NF-κB, and triggers IL-17–mediated STAT3 signalling in the colon. Genotoxic E. coli (pks+) secrete colibactin that generates DNA double-strand breaks and a specific mutational signature in colonic cells. Overall, these pathobionts trigger oncogenic pathways (β-catenin, STAT3, NF-κB) and create a pro-inflammatory microenvironment. Conversely, beneficial taxa are usually low in CRC. Butyrate-producing Ruminococcaceae and Lachnospiraceae are generally decreased in CRC patients [37–39]. Butyrate generally suppresses cancer through inducing differentiation and anti-inflammatory Tregs loss thereof contributes to creating a permissive tumour microenvironment [37, 40]. An integrated colon cancer atlas identified a tumour-microbiome risk (MBR) score, dominated by Ruminococcus bromii abundance, that correlated with long survival [40–46]. This butyrate-producer was associated with increased CD103+ dendritic cells and T cell priming in the tumour [40–46]. Combining MBR with an immune signature yielded a mICRoScore predictive of exceptional outcomes. These findings suggest certain microbes may prime an effective anti-tumour immune microenvironment. Indeed, dietary enrichment of R. bromii (via ellagitannin-rich foods, such as castalagin from berries) boosted anti-tumour immunity in mouse models [40–47]. Such diet microbiome immunity interactions are being tested clinically (e.g. Myrciaria dubia powder with ICI in trials). In a recent first-in-human trial, orally administered EcN selectively colonised colorectal tumours. An engineered EcN strain producing salicylate allowed non-invasive tumour detection, while EcN secreting GM-CSF and anti–PD-L1/CTLA-4 nanobodies significantly reduced adenoma burden (~ 50%) in mice [41, 42, 48]. Such approaches illustrate the translational promise of microbiome targeting in CRC.

It is worth noting that not all CRC cohorts show Fusobacterium enrichment and this feature may differ between cohorts. Various factors such as diet, geographic location and the host (age obesity inflammation) determine the microbiota profile. For example, CRC patients with a high BMI had even more Fusobacterium abundances than lean ones [49], which indicated that host metabolism confounds. There are also different Fn subspecies that are carried by different human populations and only some Fn clades are very virulent [50]. Besides this, technical factors (DNA extraction methods, sequencing platforms) and low-biomass effects can also lead to biased results. Therefore, aside from the link between Fn and CRC progression established by many studies, it is still necessary to exercise caution mainly because of batch effects or confounding (diet BMI inflammation) that could potentially account for the inconsistency of some results.

Stomach neoplasms

Gastric cancer is unexpectedly frequent in East Asia and certain Latin American nations. They are predominantly adenocarcinomas, historically classified into two types based on their histological appearance intestinal and diffuse types [51]. Infection with Helicobacter pylori is the most powerful known established risk factor, causing chronic gastritis and predisposition to intestinal-type carcinoma. Around 10% of gastric carcinomas are associated with Epstein–Barr virus (EBV). Other risk factors include a high-salt diet, smoking, and an abnormal gastric microbiome [52].

H. pylori fundamentally reorganises the gastric (and upper gut) microbiota. Infection decreases microbial diversity, particularly that of acidophilic bacteria, while promoting the growth of oral and intestinal microbes. A recent meta-analysis found that H. pylori–positive gastric cancer patients had increased Proteobacteria (including Fusobacterium spp.), Prevotella, and decreased beneficial commensals (e.g. Lactobacillus, Faecalibacterium) [53]. Disease progression was associated with progressive dysbiosis and loss of beneficial microbes [53]. Unexpectedly, Fusobacterium and Bacteroides fragilis proved to be reproducible biomarkers of severe gastric lesions [52, 53]. In contrast, Lactobacillus and Streptococcus are more prevalent in tumour tissue, potentially because of the alkali environment of cancerous stomach. The interaction of H. pylori with other microbes is multifaceted; some non-pylori bacteria may facilitate or suppress carcinogenesis.

H. pylori itself possesses virulence factors (CagA, VacA) that subvert host signalling. CagA is injected into epithelial cells, activating NF-κB and β-catenin signalling and IL-8 release, neutrophil recruitment, and DNA damage. H. pylori infection repeatedly stimulates STAT3 by induction of IL-6. These signalling pathways result in the proliferation of gastric epithelium and eventual neoplastic transformation. In addition, H. pylori inhibits anti-tumour immunity: it decreases NK cell-activating ligands (NKG2D) on gastric cells, impairing NK cytotoxicity [28–30]. There is some evidence that H. pylori co-infection with other dysbiotic taxa Fusobacterium, Peptostreptococcus synergistically increases a pro-carcinogenic niche by additive inflammation. Besides H. pylori, the gastric microbiome can also influence treatment outcomes. For example, gut-lung axis studies reveal that H. pylori-induced gut dysbiosis has the potential to influence systemic immunity. However, specific data on the gut microbiota’s role in gastric cancer therapy response are sparse.

Liver cancer

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is linked to chronic hepatitis B/C infection, alcoholic liver disease, and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) [54]. Cirrhosis (from any cause) underlies the majority of HCC cases. The gut–liver axis is a crucial concept: the portal circulation exposes the liver to gut-derived microbial products. Dysbiosis and increased gut permeability in cirrhosis lead to hepatic inflammation and carcinogenesis [55].

Cirrhotic and HCC patients exhibit characteristic gut dysbiosis. Reviews report increased Firmicutes and Actinobacteria in cirrhosis/HCC [56–59], including genera such as Clostridium, Ruminococcus, Enterococcus, Streptococcus and Lactobacillus, while beneficial taxa decline [56–59]. One striking finding is a > 100-fold expansion of Enterobacter ludwigii in HCC patients [27]. Intriguingly, HCC tumours themselves harbour their own microbiota: studies found enrichment of Proteobacteria, Firmicutes and Fusobacterium within liver tumour tissue [27]. Intratumoral bacteria can induce DNA damage and suppress local immunity, thereby mimicking the effects observed in the gut [60–62].

Gut-derived endotoxins (LPS) and secondary metabolites drive liver tumorigenesis mainly via TLR4/NF-κB signalling (Fig. 4). In mouse models, TLR4 knockout mice resist HCC development despite developing steatosis, underscoring the role of LPS. Liver immune cells (Kupffer cells) respond to translocated LPS by secreting TNF-α, IL-6 and TGF-β, promoting hepatocyte proliferation and fibrosis. Dietary gut metabolites also play a role: excessive gut production of hydrophobic bile acids (e.g., DCA) stimulates HSCs to senesce and release oncogenic growth factors, thereby creating a protumor microenvironment [27]. Conversely, SCFAs have protective roles: for instance, butyrate can activate GPR109a on liver immune cells to enhance CD8 + T cell anti-tumour activity.

Notably, a consortium of Clostridium species has been associated with HCC progression through the metabolism of bile acids, as some Clostridium species transform primary bile acids into secondary bile acids, which are carcinogenic. Other studies note that gut fungi or bacteria may modulate HCC like Candida overgrowth in cirrhosis exacerbates inflammation [27, 54]. However, research is still emerging. Due to the gut–liver axis, HCC risk may be mitigated by microbiome modulation. In fact, clinical data show that Rifaximin (an antibiotic that targets gut bacteria) slows the progression of liver disease. Probiotic trials in cirrhotic patients have shown a reduction in ammonia levels and inflammation, suggesting a decreased risk of HCC. The clinical data corroborate the effects of gut microbiota dysbiosis on liver cancer growth, confirming this close functional link.

Breast and gynaecological cancers

The interplay between microbiota and breast cancer (BC) systems is under investigation. BC is the most common cancer in women worldwide, and its risk factors include ageing, exposure to female hormones, and unhealthy lifestyles [63]. Another important concept is the estrobolome, which is the collection of gut bacteria that metabolise estrogens. Bacteria, such as Bacteroides and Clostridium, express β-glucuronidase that deconjugates estrogens, and thereby increases the enterohepatic circulation of active estrogens. Dysbiosis that increases β-glucuronidase activity could elevate systemic estrogen levels, promoting estrogen receptor–positive (ER+) BC [63].

Indeed, there is evidence of differences in gut microbiota between breast cancer patients and controls. In a multi-cohort analysis, a higher abundance of butyrate-producing Lachnospiraceae such as Ruminococcus and Dorea was linked to better outcomes and lower recurrence of BC, while some other genera Sellimonas, Adlercreutzia, and Rikenellaceae, had associations with ER + BC risk [64]. In a study of HER2 + BC patients, those who responded to trastuzumab had higher fecal Lachnospiraceae and microbial diversity compared to non-responders. Interestingly, a depletion of Lachnospiraceae was linked to recurrence of the disease. Such evidence indicates that some gut commensals might be able to influence the prognosis of breast cancer through immune modulation or estrogen metabolism. The role of gut microbiota with respect to the specific ovary, endometrium, and cervix is still in the formative stages but is innovative [63, 64].

Obesity-related endometrial cancer may be driven by microbiota-mediated inflammation. Some bacteria can deconjugate contraceptive hormones, theoretically affecting hormone balance. Furthermore, vaginal microbiome dysbiosis (for example, decreases in Lactobacillus levels) is known to heighten HPV persistence, thus, indirectly increasing the risk of cervical cancer [65]. Through the entero-mammary pathway, the gut microbiota may also interact with the urogenital microbiota, although this connection still remains uncertain. Mechanistically, gut microbes may affect these cancers through systemic immunity and hormone levels. For instance, there may be higher gut butyrate producers that inhibit inflammation and the proliferation of cancer cells [65]. Systemic immune checkpoints and Tregs, which govern immune tolerance to tumours, may be influenced through a microbial-modified gut. Such theories, however, are scant. To determine the role of the gut microbiota as a potential biomarker or therapeutic target in hormone-driven cancers, larger prospective studies are currently underway.

Lung and extra-intestinal cancers

Non–small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) comprises ~ 85% of lung cancers. Smoking is the major risk, but immune factors are critical in disease progression and therapy response. The gut-lung axis identifies the pathways by which gut dysbiosis influences lung immunity via metabolite and immune cell trafficking and vice versa. Profiling studies have revealed that patients with NSCLC have a distinct gut microbiome compared to healthy controls. For example, one cohort found a decrease in Actinobacteria and Proteobacteria, with an increase in Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes, and an overrepresentation of genera such as Prevotella, Roseburia, and Gemmiger [66]. Antibiotic use is well-documented to impair ICI efficacy in lung cancer and other cancers [67, 68]. Conversely, higher gut diversity and certain bacteria (notably Akkermansia muciniphila and Ruminococcaceae) correlate with better progression-free survival on anti–PD-1 therapy [66]. A recent metagenomic study identified two species-interaction signatures (SIG1/SIG2) predictive of ICI response in NSCLC, highlighting the prognostic value of gut microbiome networks. Furthermore, gut microbiota influence targeted therapy: preclinical work has shown that probiotic supplementation enhances the efficacy of gefitinib (an EGFR inhibitor) in lung cancer models, whereas antibiotics abrogate its effect [69, 70].

Gut microbes also affect cancers at other sites via immune and metabolic pathways. For example, pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) has been shown to have an intra-tumoral microbiome derived from the gut; these bacteria suppress anti-tumour macrophage activity via TLR-mediated signalling [22]. Even melanoma and sarcoma models have been influenced by gut microbes (e.g., castalagin boosting the anti–PD–1 response via R. bromii [40–46]). Experiments are investigating whether donor gut microbiota influence metastasis levels of lung and other types of cancer. In brief, direct metastasis of the gut microbiota to extra-intestinal cancers is uncommon; however, systemic immune modulation by the gut microbiota is a major significant mechanism. This has created the possibility of applying gut-directed therapies (such as diet and FMT) to modulate the course of lung, melanoma, and other cancers.

Cancer treatment and gut microbiota

The gut microbiota has a dramatic influence on the outcomes of cancer therapy. Accumulating evidence suggests that commensal gut bacteria and their metabolites play a critical role in regulating the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), chemotherapy, and radiotherapy. These effects are mediated through coordinated immune modulation and microbial metabolic activity. of human and animal studies suggest that the gut microbiota regulates the efficacy of ICI. In melanoma and lung cancer, responders to PD-1/PD-L1 therapy tend to have higher gut microbial diversity and enrichment of species such as Akkermansia muciniphila, Bifidobacterium longum/pseudocatenulatum, and certain Ruminococcaceae [66]. Mechanistically, these bacteria enhance antigen presentation and T cell priming. For example, A. muciniphila promotes IL-12–dependent CD4+ T cell trafficking into tumours. Notably, faecal microbiota transplant (FMT) trials underscore causality: in refractory melanoma, transferring stool from ICI responders to non-responders has converted some into responders, restoring T cell infiltration and tumour regression [22]. FMT also mitigates ICI-induced colitis. On the molecular level, beneficial microbes may produce inosine and SCFAs that boost Th1 and CTL function [16], while pathobionts may induce Tregs or myeloid-derived suppressor cells.

The microbiota can alter metabolism, the metabolism of chemotherapeutic drugs, and host tolerance. Commensal enzymes (e.g., β-glucuronidase) can reactivate drug metabolites in the gut, thereby exacerbating toxicity (as seen with irinotecan). Conversely, some bacteria inactivate drugs (M. hyorhinis inactivates gemcitabine). A recent systematic review found specific gut bacteria associated with better chemo response in GI cancers for instance, higher levels of Bacteroides fragilis and Roseburia faecis predicted good outcomes [71].

The gut microbiota also affects radiation outcomes. Mice studies indicate that germ-free or antibiotic-treated animals show increased GI toxicity from abdominal radiation, suggesting that commensals can protect the mucosa. On the other hand, new approaches utilise bacteria to enhance the effectiveness of radiotherapy. Genetically engineered microbes are used in the targeting of hypoxic regions of tumours to deliver radiosensitizers. Attenuated Listeria monocytogenes, for instance, loaded with 32P showed tremendous anti-tumour activity in preclinical models [72–74]. The bacterium Salmonella has also been engineered to carry gold nanoparticles, which enhance the impact of X-rays. A probiotic E. coli Nissle engineered to express a tumour-targeting antibody increased DNA double-strand breaks in lung tumours under radiation [75–77]. In all cases, the gut microbiome acts as a hidden organ influencing drug pharmacokinetics, immune activation, and tissue repair. An optimal microbiota can facilitate therapy (enhancing cytotoxic T cell function, reducing inflammation), whereas dysbiosis often impairs it. Current trials are also actively investigating microbiome therapies to complement cancer therapy.

Therapeutic interventions directed against gut microbiota

Considering the influence of the microbiome on cancer, several interventions have been suggested to modify gut bacteria for therapeutic benefits. In this review, we summarise the latest developments in probiotics, prebiotics, FMT, engineered microbes, and other connected strategies.

Probiotics and prebiotics

Instead of trying to generally correct dysbiosis, probiotic and prebiotic interventions are being seen as the means of adjusting individual microbial functions of interest to cancer biology [78]. These interventions have the potential to alter the metabolite production, epithelial barrier integrity and immune signalling pathways, which mediate host tumour interactions at a mechanistic scale. Targeting the selective enrichment of taxa linked to the short-chain fatty acid synthesis and anti-inflammatory functions may be useful in improving physiological resilience in carcinogenesis and therapy, by avoiding wholesale changes in the microbial community [85].

Live bacteria (Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, Saccharomyces) have been utilised to keep the gut healthy. Probiotic supplementation has been tried in cancer to minimise side effects of treatment (chemo/radiation-induced diarrhoea) with variable success. For instance, Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG has been shown to decrease the frequency of radiation-induced enteritis in some studies. However, more systematic evidence is needed.

Synthetic biology has enabled the construction of designer probiotics for targeted therapy. A landmark example (2024) involved engineering E. coli Nissle 1917 to colonise colorectal tumours in mice [48]. These bacteria were designed to (i) produce salicylate, which serves as a urinary biomarker to detect neoplasia, and (ii) secrete immunotherapeutic molecules (GM-CSF, anti–PD-L1 and anti–CTLA-4 nanobodies) directly in the tumour microenvironment [48]. This theranostic approach achieved ~ 50% reduction in adenoma burden. The trial also included a human pilot: healthy donors took EcN before colonoscopy, and labelled bacteria were recovered preferentially from tumours [48]. This demonstrates the feasibility of gut-colonising, engineered microbes to deliver anticancer agents. Many other engineered strains are in preclinical development. For instance, Salmonella typhimurium and attenuated Clostridium have been modified to produce cytotoxic proteins or prodrug-converting enzymes [86–90]. Engineered probiotics secreting immune stimulants (IL-18, IL-15) have increased CD8+ T cell responses in tumours [91–93]. A notable innovation was a probiotic modified to express checkpoint-blockade nanobodies on its surface, enabling localised immune checkpoint therapy [48].

Dietary substrates (fibers, oligosaccharides) that feed beneficial microbes are also therapeutic strategies. High-fiber diets have been associated with improved immunotherapy outcomes, likely by increasing SCFA production. Inulin-type fructans promote Bifidobacterium and Faecalibacterium growth. A recent study found that an insulin-supplemented diet reprogrammed gut–immune interactions and protected against tumour development [17]. Synbiotics (combining probiotics with prebiotics) are being evaluated to mitigate therapy toxicity. For example, a clinical trial (ongoing) is testing a synbiotic formula to prevent chemotherapy-induced hair loss in breast cancer patients (NCT06560385) [94]. Some small studies suggest synbiotics may reduce radiation enteropathy, but more data are needed (Table 2).

Despite their therapeutic promise, engineered probiotics remain at an early translational stage and face several biological and regulatory challenges, including strain stability, controlled colonisation, host-specific efficacy, and long-term safety. Consequently, microbiome-targeted cancer prevention and adjunctive therapy should prioritise the restoration of the underlying ecological balance rather than solely focusing on symptom relief [17, 94]. Addressing root causes of dysbiosis, such as poor dietary quality, chronic inflammation, metabolic dysfunction, and environmental expoipresents a foundational strategy for sustainable microbiota modulation. From a clinical perspective, interventions that reinforce physiological microbial resilience through diet, lifestyle optimisation, and minimally invasive microbiome support may offer broader applicability and lower risk, particularly in preventive and supportive oncology settings.

Natural products as microbiota-modulating agents in cancer care

Dietary and medicinal natural products are gaining growing acceptance as directors of the ecology of gut microbial communities and host-microbe signalling. These compounds, in contrast to single-target pharmacological reagents, are pleiotropic since they change microbial composition, improve epithelial barrier integrity, and change metabolite production [22, 39, 75–77]. Their clinical utility can be widely divided into cancer prevention roles, supportive care and adjunctive therapeutic modulation.

Role in cancer prevention

Preventive Long-term intake of microbiota-accessible nutrients, especially dietary polyphenols, fibres and plant-derived bioactives, facilitates microbial diversity and stimulates the growth of short-chain fatty acid (SCFA)-producing taxa [3, 4]. These metabolites control epithelial homeostasis, inhibit chronic inflammation, and arbitrate oncogenic signalling including the NF- KB and STAT3 signalling. The interactions between diets and microbiota can be used to develop a less favorable metabolic environment, potentially decreasing tumorigenesis because they enhance mucosal integrity and limit genotoxic and inflammatory exposures [9, 95]. Preventive measures thus focus on long term diets instead of short term supplementation.

Role in supportive care during cancer therapy

Natural products can be used to reduce toxicities of therapy through microbiota mediated clearance during active treatment. It has been demonstrated that curcumin, omega- 3 fatty acids, and the use of special phytochemicals can prevent treatment-induced mucosal injury, oxidative stress, and systemic inflammation [96]. Part of the mediation of such effects is restoration of useful commensals and enhanced gut barrier function, which can reduce gastrointestinal complications, metabolic dysregulation, and release of inflammatory cytokines [97]. Under these conditions, natural products are used as complementary measures intended to enhance tolerance to the treatment and maintain physiological stability.

Role as adjunctive microbiota-modulating therapeutics

In addition to prevention and supportive therapy, some natural substances can stimulate responding to therapy by changing the immune-microbiome interactions. In the microbial biotransformation of polyphenols, ginsenosides and other phytochemicals, the resultant metabolites can affect antigen presentation, T-cell activation and immunometacometabolic signalling [98, 99]. These tumour sensitizing effects of these microbiotas might maximize anti-tumour immune tone and minimise pro-tumorigenic inflammation, thereby sensitising tumours to systemic high-therapy, such as chemotherapy and immunotherapy [100]. Notably, these methods can be considered as the complementary, but not substitute methods to common oncologic therapies.

Faecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) and microbial consortia

Microbiota-transfer techniques aim to restore functional microbial networks that promote therapeutic responsiveness, but they present complex clinical applications [81]. The weaknesses in donor composition, host compatibility, and engraftment efficiency indicate that consortia and precision-based application should be carefully standardised. As a result of this line of study, there is a shift in current research, between empiric community replacement and specific microbial formulation which is meant to produce reproducible immunological and metabolic responses, within the oncology setting [82].

Traditional FMT (transfer of donor stool) has gained attention in cancer. Early-phase trials in metastatic melanoma have shown that FMT from PD-1 responders can overcome immunotherapy resistance in some patients [101, 102]. Similar trials are being conducted in other cancers (e.g. bladder cancer) and to ameliorate checkpoint inhibitor colitis [22]. However, variability among donors and safety concerns (pathogen transmission) limit routine use. To address the limitations of FMT (Next-Generation FMT), defined bacterial consortia and next-generation products are being developed. A consortium of 11 commensal strains known to enhance T cell responses (initially identified in mice) has entered human trials as “VE800” (NCT04208958) [103]. MaaT013, a pooled-donor live microbiota with > 1000 species standardised across batches, is in trials combined with CTLA-4/PD-1 blockade in melanoma (NCT04988841) [94]. These next-generation FMTs aim for reproducibility and safety. Preclinically, cocktails of Bifidobacterium/Lactobacillus/Enterococcus are being tested in bladder and lung cancer immunotherapy trials (e.g., NCT05220124) [94].

Although FMT has demonstrated promising outcomes in selected cancer-related contexts, its broader clinical application remains constrained by substantial biological, logistical, and regulatory challenges [94]. Donor selection variability, risks of pathogen transmission, long-term safety uncertainties, and inconsistent engraftment limit its widespread adoption, particularly in immunocompromised patients with oncology. Moreover, inter-individual differences in host genetics, immune status, and baseline microbiota composition contribute to heterogeneous therapeutic responses. Given these limitations, FMT should be viewed as a highly selective intervention rather than a first-line strategy for modulating the microbiome [101, 102]. In many cases, restoring microbial homeostasis through dietary diversification, increased intake of microbiota-accessible fibres, plant-derived bioactives, and lifestyle optimisation may offer safer, more scalable, and physiologically aligned alternatives. Such approaches aim to re-establish ecological balance and functional resilience of the gut microbiota, addressing underlying drivers of dysbiosis rather than transiently replacing microbial communities.

Microbiome-derived biomarkers for cancer

In addition to direct treatments, the microbiome offers prognostic and diagnostic biomarkers. Some microbial signatures in blood or stool are associated with the presence of cancer, a therapeutic response, or both. For CRC, fecal detection of F. nucleatum (and other CRC-associated taxa) can improve early screening sensitivity [104–113]. In pancreatic and lung cancers, circulating bacterial DNA patterns are being explored as non-invasive markers. Microbial diversity metrics and specific taxa (A. muciniphila levels) have been proposed as predictors of ICI response [114]. Recent efforts, such as the colon cancer mICRoScore, combine immune and microbiome data to stratify patient risk [40–46]. As microbiome sequencing becomes more accessible, integrating microbial biomarkers into precision oncology pipelines is an active goal.

Although these are some of the positive developments, microbiome-derived biomarkers are still mostly preclinical and are not currently prepared to be used regularly in oncology [2–4]. Before wide adoption, there are several translational issues, such as the absence of standardized procedures in the collection and sequencing of samples, and interpretation of the data, which are presently restricting the reproducibility of cross-studies [91–115]. Moreover, there is a high inter-individual variability that is caused by diet and medications, lifestyle and geographic factors, and it makes it difficult to determine universal diagnostic thresholds. The majority of the existing literature is cross-sectional, and longitudinal validation of predictive value is yet to be done [85, 95]. Approach to regulation of the microbiome-based diagnostics through regulatory pathways is also not clear especially in relation to reproducibility, quality control and clinical utility. Therefore, although microbiome-informed biomarkers have significant potential to be more precise in oncology, more harmonization, validation, and prospective clinical trials are needed before they can be translated into clinical practice.

Accumulating evidence indicates that the gut microbiome plays a multifaceted role in cancer development and therapeutic response, functioning not merely as a passive bystander but as an active modulator of tumor biology and host immunity. As summarized in Table 1, distinct microbial taxa are recurrently associated with specific cancer types through mechanisms involving chronic inflammation, immune regulation, metabolic reprogramming, and genotoxic stress. However, it is increasingly evident that these associations are context-dependent, shaped by host genetics, diet, tumor microenvironment, and treatment modality. Importantly, emerging interventional studies, outlined in Table 2, demonstrate that targeted modulation of the gut microbiome can influence the efficacy and toxicity of cancer therapies, particularly immune checkpoint inhibitors and chemotherapeutic agents. Despite significant progress, a major challenge lies in distinguishing causal microbial drivers from secondary microbial shifts induced by disease or therapy.

Methodological limitations

Studies of the tumor and blood microbiome are particularly susceptible to external DNA contamination, which can obscure real signals [116]. Therefore, using strict controls and decontamination methods is extremely important. Regarding DNA extraction and primer biases, the choice of extraction kits and 16 S primers can preferentially lyse and amplify specific taxa, thereby introducing compositional biases. Comparing 16 S and shotgun sequencing shows that 16 S analysis is limited to identifying bacteria, usually at the genus level, and doesn’t find rare species. In contrast, shotgun metagenomics offers information about species and their functions, but it requires much more sequencing and is complicated by the presence of host DNA. Durazzi and his team found that 16 S sequencing only provides a partial view of the gut microbiota, especially when compared to the more detailed information from shotgun sequencing [117]. Overfitting is a major problem for many microbiome biomarker models, as these models often use many features, which can exceed the number of samples. To validate the results, meta-analyses and cross-validation are required [116].

Future directions and challenges

The field of microbiota–cancer research is advancing rapidly, yet several hurdles remain before clinical translation can be achieved. A major challenge is establishing causality: although numerous associations between microbial taxa and cancer have been described, mechanistic proof remains limited [15]. Future studies should employ gnotobiotic models, patient-derived organoids, and multi-omics approaches, including metagenomics, metabolomics, and transcriptomics, to define microbial effectors such as metabolites and virulence factors, as well as the host pathways they influence. Technologies like genome-wide CRISPR screens offer novel opportunities to uncover host–microbe interactions. Equally critical is the issue of heterogeneity, as differences in diet, genetics, drug use, and sampling strategies complicate interpretation; thus, rigorous control of confounders, standardised sample collection, DNA extraction, and sequencing protocols, and detailed metadata capture in human cohorts will be essential for reproducibility [22, 66]. Another priority is biomarker development, where integration of microbiome signatures with tumor genomics, immune phenotypes, and clinical parameters could yield predictive models for cancer risk, prognosis, and therapeutic response, as demonstrated in early trials with composite scores such as micro Score, longitudinal monitoring will be necessary to define dynamic biomarkers of treatment efficacy and toxicity, and regulatory pathways for microbial biomarkers must be clarified. On the therapeutic front, next-generation approaches, including engineered probiotics and synthetic microbial consortia, hold great promise, particularly with advances in synthetic biology, such as gene circuits and kill-switches that enhance safety; however, ensuring stable colonisation and consistent efficacy remains a key challenge, alongside regulatory hurdles in bringing live biotherapeutics to the clinic. Safety and ethics also demand careful consideration, since FMT carries risks of pathogen transmission, engineered microbes may exert unforeseen ecological or immunological effects, and issues of donor screening, long-term monitoring, and equitable access must be addressed [40–46]. Finally, the vast, multidimensional datasets generated by microbiome research necessitate advanced computational solutions; machine learning, network analysis, and artificial intelligence are increasingly being applied to identify microbial interaction networks and predict therapeutic outcomes, with early examples in lung cancer underscoring their potential [66]. Taken together, while translation remains in its infancy, the coming years will likely witness integration of microbiome profiling into personalised oncology and the refinement of microbial therapeutics into safe, effective, and accessible interventions, requiring sustained multidisciplinary collaboration across microbiology, oncology, immunology, synthetic biology, and data science.

Nevertheless, patient progress through the clinical trials has been quite changeable. For the purpose of illustration, FMT is an experimental technique in cancer (for ICI resistance reversal) that mainly results in transient engraftment: recipients may return to their original microbiota composition, and some of them even need repeat FMT after a few months [118]. Among other things, a meta-analysis revealed that the clinical response and donor strain colonization were not tightly linked [119]. Probiotics and prebiotics, in other cases, display a degree of benefit that is not very consistent, and that is probably a result of effects specific to one or several strains and of the heterogeneity of the patients. The aforementioned facts explain that it is not simple to adapt microbiome therapies: larger and better-controlled trials are definitely needed, as well as a deep and thorough mechanistic knowledge of the microbiome.

Conclusion

The crosstalk between the gut microbiota and cancer is now recognised as a central factor in tumour initiation, progression, and therapy response. The expanding body of evidence reviewed herein underscores the gut microbiota as a central regulator at the intersection of cancer initiation, progression, and therapeutic response. Rather than acting as an isolated factor, the microbiota integrates genetic susceptibility, environmental exposures, dietary patterns, and immune regulation into a dynamic ecosystem that can either restrain or promote tumorigenesis. Disruption of this balance through modern lifestyle factors, including poor dietary quality, sedentary behaviour, smoking, and chronic inflammation, emerges as a key driver of dysbiosis-associated cancer risk. Advances in microbiome research have enabled the identification of microbial signatures, metabolites, and host–microbe interactions that influence oncogenic signalling, immune surveillance, and treatment efficacy. However, it is increasingly evident that effective microbiome-based oncology strategies must prioritise restoration of ecological and functional balance rather than narrowly targeting individual microbial taxa. Approaches centred on dietary diversification, microbiota-accessible fibres, and plant-derived bioactive compounds offer scalable, low-risk avenues to reinforce microbial resilience and modulate cancer-relevant inflammatory and metabolic pathways. While emerging interventions such as engineered probiotics and fecal microbiota transplantation demonstrate therapeutic promise in selected contexts, their clinical implementation remains constrained by safety, regulatory, and inter-individual variability considerations. Consequently, these modalities should be regarded as adjunctive or situational tools rather than universal solutions.
