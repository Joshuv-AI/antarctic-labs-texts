Early Transplantation After Pretransplant Reduction of TP53‐Altered Clones and Prolonged Dose‐Adjusted Azacitidine Maintenance: A Three‐Case Series
Authors: Fuyuki Yamagata, Kyoko Yoshihara, Yasuhito Nannya, Mami Samori, Saki Takahashi, Tomoko Kumamoto, Yoshiyuki Fujita, Nobuto Utsunomiya, Satoshi Yoshihara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACT TP53‐altered myeloid neoplasms are associated with an extremely high risk of relapse even after allogeneic hematopoietic cell transplantation (allo‐HCT), and the optimal pretransplant and posttransplant strategies remain undefined. We report the cases of three patients with TP53‐altered myeloid neoplasms who achieved durable complete remission after a sequential strategy consisting of limited pretransplant therapy, prompt allo‐HCT after reduction of the TP53‐positive clone, and early dose‐adjusted azacitidine (Aza) maintenance. At presentation, Cases 1 and 2 had TP53 abnormalities detectable by both fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) and sequencing, whereas Case 3 had a TP53 single‐nucleotide variant and an Intron 1 structural variant detectable only by molecular testing. Pretransplant treatment was intentionally limited to 2–3 cycles: three cycles of Aza in Case 1, intensive induction chemotherapy followed by one cycle of Aza in Case 2, and two cycles of venetoclax plus Aza in Case 3. In all cases, serial molecular monitoring demonstrated a reduction of TP53‐altered clones before transplantation, followed by complete disappearance after allo‐HCT. Aza maintenance was initiated approximately 1.5–2 months after transplantation at 30–35 mg/m2 for 5 days and was subsequently escalated according to tolerability. Maintenance was continued for 53, 37, and 27 cycles, respectively. All three patients remain in complete remission more than 4 years after transplantation. These cases highlight two clinically relevant points. First, in TP53‐altered myeloid neoplasms, pretransplant therapy may be best viewed as a bridge to transplantation, and transplantation should be considered promptly once the TP53‐positive clone has decreased, rather than prolonging non‐curative therapy. Second, posttransplant Aza maintenance may be more beneficial when initiated early, individualized according to tolerability and disease status, and continued as long as feasible. In addition, for patients with FISH‐detectable TP53 abnormalities, serial FISH may provide a practical adjunct for pretransplant decision‐making in routine practice. Introduction

TP53‐altered myeloid neoplasms represent one of the most adverse‐risk disease subsets in myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS) and acute myeloid leukemia (AML). Importantly, recent studies have shown that this poor prognosis is driven particularly by multihit or biallelic TP53 alterations, which are characterized by genomic instability, complex karyotype, and markedly inferior survival compared with monoallelic TP53 alterations [1].

Although allogeneic hematopoietic cell transplantation (allo‐HCT) remains the only potentially curative treatment option, several reports suggest that allo‐HCT does not fully overcome this adverse biology. In TP53‐mutated MDS, a recent systematic review and meta‐analysis reported a pooled 3‐year overall survival of only 21% and a relapse rate of 58.9% after allo‐HCT [2].

In clinical practice, TP53‐altered myeloid neoplasms may still show an initial hematologic response to treatment; however, such responses are often short‐lived. In TP53‐mutated MDS treated with hypomethylating agents, initial response rates are not necessarily inferior, but the duration of response is significantly shorter than in TP53 wild‐type disease [3]. This raises a practical therapeutic question: rather than prolonging pretransplant therapy in pursuit of deeper remission, should clinicians proceed to transplantation once the TP53‐positive clone has decreased to a clinically acceptable level?

Azacitidine (Aza) has been investigated as posttransplant maintenance therapy, but its clinical benefit remains unsettled. In the Phase 3 randomized study by Oran et al., Aza maintenance after transplant did not improve relapse‐free survival or overall survival compared with observation, although the study also demonstrated that prospective maintenance in the posttransplant setting was feasible and safe, but challenging [4].

Here, we report three patients with TP53‐altered myeloid neoplasms who achieved durable complete remission after a sequential strategy consisting of limited pretransplant therapy, prompt allo‐HCT after reduction of the TP53‐positive clone, and early dose‐adjusted Aza maintenance. A particular strength of this series is the longitudinal evaluation of TP53‐altered clones before and after transplantation, which provides insight into how clone kinetics may inform treatment timing and posttransplant management.

TP53 alterations were assessed in bone marrow samples by targeted next‐generation sequencing using a previously described targeted‐capture sequencing approach specifically designed for myeloid malignancies [5]. This approach allowed detection of point mutations, structural variants, and copy number alterations, including allelic imbalances, thus enabling assessment of TP53 allelic state (multihit vs. single‐hit) in appropriate cases. Variants were called using predefined quality criteria, including a minimum variant allele frequency threshold of 0.02 [6].

Case 1

A 68‐year‐old man was referred to our department because of pancytopenia and circulating blasts detected during follow‐up for diabetes mellitus and dyslipidemia. At presentation, the peripheral blood counts were as follows: white blood cells 2.39 × 109/L, neutrophils 0.73 × 109/L, circulating blasts 2%, hemoglobin 9.5 g/dL, and platelets 82 × 109/L. Bone marrow examination showed a hypercellular marrow with 6.8% blasts and trilineage dysplasia. He was diagnosed with MDS with increased blasts‐1. Cytogenetic analysis revealed a complex karyotype, and fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) demonstrated TP53 deletion in 91% of cells. Targeted sequencing identified TP53 p.K120E (variant allele frequency [VAF], 0.789) and TET2 p.Y1649X (VAF, 0.423).

After three cycles of Aza, he underwent cord blood transplantation approximately 4 months after the initial hematologic evaluation. Before transplantation, the peripheral blood counts were as follows: white blood cells 2.85 × 109/L, neutrophils 1.61 × 109/L, circulating blasts 0.5%, hemoglobin 11.0 g/dL, and platelets 109 × 109/L. Bone marrow examination at that time showed a normocellular marrow with 3.8% blasts. Neutrophil and platelet engraftment occurred on Days 19 and 26, respectively. Grade I acute skin graft‐versus‐host disease (GVHD) developed and improved with low‐dose prednisolone. Aza maintenance was initiated approximately 1.5 months after transplantation at 35 mg/m2 for 5 days every 4 weeks. The dose was gradually escalated to 50–65 mg/m2 for 5 days according to tolerability. Treatment was occasionally skipped because of pneumonia, but maintenance was continued for 53 cycles and discontinued approximately 58 months after transplantation. More than 5 years after transplantation, the patient remains in complete remission without evidence of relapse. Serial monitoring demonstrated a decrease in TP53 VAF from 0.789 at presentation to 0.352 after three cycles of Aza, with subsequent disappearance after transplantation and sustained negativity thereafter. FISH results showed a similar pattern, decreasing from 91% at presentation to 74% after three cycles and becoming negative after transplantation (Figure 2A).

Case 2

A 45‐year‐old man was admitted emergently because of fever and severe pancytopenia. At presentation, the peripheral blood counts were as follows: white blood cells 1.06 × 109/L, neutrophils 0.21 × 109/L, circulating blasts 5%, hemoglobin 5.2 g/dL, and platelets 21 × 109/L. Bone marrow examination showed a hypocellular marrow with 20.6% blasts and trilineage dysplasia. He was diagnosed with AML, myelodysplasia‐related. Cytogenetic analysis revealed del(5q) and der(7;17). FISH showed TP53 deletion in 90% of cells, and targeted sequencing identified TP53 p.V172D (VAF, 0.675).

Induction chemotherapy with idarubicin and cytarabine was initiated shortly after presentation, and hematologic remission was achieved. One cycle of Aza was subsequently administered. He then underwent HLA‐matched sibling peripheral blood stem cell transplantation approximately 3 months after the initial visit. Before transplantation, the peripheral blood counts were as follows: white blood cells 6.50 × 109/L, neutrophils 4.58 × 109/L, no circulating blasts, hemoglobin 11.8 g/dL, and platelets 177 × 109/L. Bone marrow examination at that time showed a normocellular marrow without excess blasts. Neutrophil and platelet engraftment both occurred on Day 11. Stage I acute gastrointestinal GVHD developed and improved with prednisolone, and no chronic GVHD was observed. Aza maintenance was initiated approximately 1.5 months after transplantation at 30 mg/m2 for 5 days. The dose was escalated to 40, 65, and 70 mg/m2, but was temporarily reduced to 50 mg/m2 because of cytopenia. Thereafter, maintenance at 65 mg/m2 for 5 days was resumed. From approximately 26 months after transplantation, the schedule was extended to one cycle every 3 months. Maintenance therapy was discontinued after 37 cycles at 5 years after transplantation. The patient remains in continuous complete remission. Longitudinal molecular testing showed a marked decrease in the dominant TP53 clone after induction therapy, from VAF of 0.675 at presentation to 0.056, with sustained molecular negativity after transplantation. FISH results also declined from 90% at presentation to 2% after induction and became negative thereafter (Figure 2B).

Case 3

A 58‐year‐old man with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) related to heavy smoking was evaluated because of anemia and thrombocytopenia. At presentation, the peripheral blood counts were as follows: white blood cells 9.91 × 109/L, neutrophils 2.18 × 109/L, circulating blasts 40.5%, hemoglobin 6.8 g/dL, and platelets 49 × 109/L. Bone marrow examination showed a hypercellular marrow with 40.2% blasts and dysplasia in the erythroid and granulocytic lineages. He was diagnosed with AML, myelodysplasia‐related. Cytogenetic analysis showed a complex karyotype. FISH did not detect TP53 deletion. However, targeted sequencing identified TP53 p.K120E (VAF, 0.437) and a structural variant involving TP53 Intron 1 (VAF, 0.239).

Because of underlying COPD, intensive chemotherapy was avoided, and treatment with venetoclax plus Aza was initiated. Hematologic remission was achieved after the first cycle, and a second cycle was administered. He then underwent cord blood transplantation approximately 3 months after the initial hematologic evaluation. Before transplantation, the peripheral blood counts were as follows: white blood cells 1.99 × 109/L, neutrophils 1.01 × 109/L, no circulating blasts, hemoglobin 12.5 g/dL, and platelets 39 × 109/L. Bone marrow examination at that time showed a hypocellular marrow without blasts or overt dysplasia. Neutrophil and platelet engraftment occurred on Days 19 and 26, respectively. Acute skin and gut GVHD improved with low‐dose prednisolone. Aza maintenance was initiated approximately 2 months after transplantation at 45 mg/m2 for 5 days and was subsequently escalated to 60 mg/m2 for 5 days. After 27 cycles, maintenance therapy was discontinued because of severe COVID‐19‐related pneumonia requiring mechanical ventilation. Because of baseline COPD, home oxygen therapy was required thereafter. Nevertheless, the patient remains in complete remission more than 4 years after transplantation and approximately 1.5 years after discontinuation of maintenance therapy. Serial molecular testing showed stepwise reduction of both the TP53 single‐nucleotide variant (SNV) and the TP53 structural variant before transplantation, followed by complete disappearance after transplantation and sustained negativity thereafter (Figure 2C).

Discussion

TP53‐altered myeloid neoplasms represent one of the highest‐risk subgroups of MDS and AML, and this is particularly true for multihit TP53 disease, which is biologically and clinically the most adverse category [1]. Bernard et al. established the major importance of TP53 allelic state in MDS, showing that multihit TP53 disease is associated with genome instability, complex karyotype, and markedly inferior outcomes compared with monoallelic TP53 alterations [1]. In the transplant setting as well, the adverse biology of TP53‐mutated disease is not fully overcome. A systematic review and meta‐analysis in TP53‐mutated MDS found a pooled 3‐year overall survival of only 21% with a relapse rate of 58.9% after allo‐HCT [2]. More recent cohort data also showed that TP53‐altered MDS remained associated with higher relapse incidence and inferior disease‐free survival after allo‐HCT, even though overall survival was not uniformly inferior in all transplant settings [7]. Against this background, the durable long‐term remissions observed in our three patients are clinically notable.

Importantly, Cases 1 and 2 were considered clinically and genetically consistent with multihit TP53 disease, based on the coexistence of TP53 mutation and TP53 deletion detected by molecular and cytogenetic/FISH analyses. In Case 3, a TP53 SNV coexisted with a TP53 Intron 1 structural variant; although allelic phase was not formally determined, this combination is highly suggestive of a biologically biallelic or functionally multihit TP53 state. Thus, all three cases were considered clinically high risk and likely enriched for adverse TP53 biology. However, recent studies suggest that TP53‐mutated disease is biologically heterogeneous and that prognosis may be influenced not only by TP53 allelic state but also by the functional severity of individual TP53 variants [8]. Therefore, we cannot exclude the possibility that variant‐specific TP53 biology contributed, at least in part, to the favorable outcomes observed in our cases.

First, our cases support the concept that, in TP53‐altered myeloid neoplasms—particularly those with likely multihit disease—pretransplant therapy should be used primarily as a bridge to transplantation rather than as prolonged non‐curative treatment. Although TP53‐mutated disease is often regarded as treatment resistant, an initial hematologic and even molecular response is not uncommon. The greater problem is that these responses are often short‐lived. In TP53‐mutated MDS treated with hypomethylating agents, response duration is significantly shorter than in TP53 wild‐type disease [3]. From this perspective, the therapeutic goal before transplantation may not be maximal cytoreduction at all costs, but rather reduction of disease burden and TP53‐positive clone size to a level that permits safe and timely allo‐HCT. This concept is also consistent with recent data showing that, among patients with single‐hit or multihit TP53‐mutated AML/MDS, pretransplant remission status did not clearly translate into major differences in posttransplant overall survival, supporting consideration of transplantation even in the absence of complete remission [9].

In our series, pretransplant treatment was intentionally limited to a short course: three cycles of Aza in Case 1, induction chemotherapy followed by one cycle of Aza in Case 2, and two cycles of venetoclax plus Aza in Case 3. In all three patients, this short bridging strategy was associated with reduction of TP53‐altered clones before transplantation, although the degree of reduction varied among cases. These observations suggest a practical strategy for TP53‐altered disease: once the TP53‐positive clone has decreased to an acceptable level, clinicians should consider proceeding promptly to allo‐HCT rather than prolonging pretransplant therapy in pursuit of a deeper but potentially short‐lived response.

Second, our cases highlight the potential value of serial assessment of TP53‐abnormal clones in determining the timing of transplantation, while also underscoring the limitations of this approach in routine practice. In an ideal setting, repeated next‐generation sequencing would allow real‐time tracking of TP53‐altered clones and better inform treatment decisions. In reality, however, repeated gene panel testing is often difficult to implement in routine care because of cost, turnaround time, and access. Our experience suggests that in selected patients—particularly those in whom TP53 abnormality is identifiable by FISH at baseline—serial FISH assessment may provide a practical surrogate marker of clonal burden. In Cases 1 and 2, serial FISH results closely paralleled the molecular decline of TP53‐abnormal clones before transplantation and remained negative thereafter. Thus, although FISH cannot replace molecular profiling, it may serve as a clinically useful adjunct to guide the transition to transplantation in patients with FISH‐detectable TP53 abnormalities. By contrast, Case 3 also illustrates an important limitation: TP53 abnormality was not detected by FISH but was captured by sequencing as both a TP53 SNV and an Intron 1 structural variant. Accordingly, FISH is not universally informative, but when positive at baseline, it may be a practical monitoring tool in routine clinical care.

Third, our experience suggests that the benefit of posttransplant Aza maintenance may depend less on whether it is simply administered and more on how it is administered. In all three patients, maintenance was initiated relatively early, within approximately 2 months after transplantation, at a low initial dose of 30–35 mg/m2 for 5 days, and then escalated according to hematologic tolerance, infectious complications, and general condition. Case 1 received 53 cycles despite intermittent delays due to pneumonia; Case 2 received 37 cycles with temporary dose reduction for cytopenia followed by re‐escalation; and Case 3 received 27 cycles before discontinuation because of severe COVID‐19‐related pneumonia rather than relapse. These clinical courses suggest that a patient‐tailored strategy—early initiation, careful dose escalation, and flexible long‐term continuation whenever tolerable—may be more important than adherence to a rigid fixed‐dose schedule.

This interpretation may also help explain the discrepancy between the favorable clinical courses observed in our three cases and the negative randomized Phase 3 trial reported by Oran et al. [4]. In that study, Aza maintenance after transplant did not improve relapse‐free survival or overall survival compared with observation [4]. One plausible explanation is not necessarily that posttransplant Aza lacks biological activity, but rather that its efficacy may depend strongly on treatment exposure and feasibility. If maintenance is started but discontinued early in a substantial proportion of patients, or if dose intensity remains low without subsequent escalation, the treatment may fail to exert a clinically meaningful anti‐relapse effect. Our three cases cannot prove this hypothesis, but they support the idea that, in ultra‐high‐risk disease such as likely multihit TP53‐altered MDS/AML, posttransplant Aza may be a rational individualized maintenance strategy when started early, adjusted according to tolerability, and continued over a prolonged period whenever feasible.

Another important point is that the favorable long‐term outcomes in our series likely reflect the combination of two sequentially linked strategies, rather than the effect of maintenance alone. The first was reducing the TP53‐positive clone before transplantation without unnecessarily delaying allo‐HCT. The second was sustaining posttransplant remission with early and flexible Aza administration. The longitudinal molecular data are particularly informative in this regard (Figure 2). In Case 1, TP53 and TET2 VAFs decreased during pretransplant Aza therapy and became undetectable after transplantation. In Case 2, the dominant TP53 clone markedly decreased after induction therapy, and all TP53 abnormalities became undetectable after transplantation. In Case 3, both the TP53 SNV and TP53 structural variant decreased stepwise during venetoclax/Aza therapy and disappeared after transplantation. Together, these findings raise the possibility that pretransplant clonal reduction and posttransplant disease control may be related in some patients. However, this interpretation should be made cautiously, given the very small number of cases, the selected nature of the cohort, and the inter‐case variability in the degree of pretransplant clonal reduction.

This study has several limitations. It is a retrospective description of only three patients without a control group, and the underlying diseases, pretransplant regimens, and transplant sources were not fully uniform. Moreover, this series consists of selected long‐term survivors and may not be representative of the broader population of patients with TP53‐altered myeloid neoplasms undergoing allo‐HCT. We therefore cannot determine the relative contributions of pretransplant therapy, allo‐HCT itself, graft‐versus‐leukemia effects, GVHD, and Aza maintenance to the favorable outcomes observed. In addition, serial molecular monitoring was highly informative in these cases but may not be feasible in all practice settings, and FISH‐based monitoring is applicable only in patients with FISH‐detectable TP53 abnormalities. Nevertheless, we believe that these cases provide clinically relevant lessons. In TP53‐altered myeloid neoplasms, clinicians may consider avoiding unnecessarily prolonged pretransplant therapy once the TP53‐positive clone has decreased, using serial FISH as a practical adjunct when informative, and implementing Aza maintenance with early initiation, careful dose adjustment, and long‐term continuation whenever feasible. In addition, we did not perform formal functional annotation of the individual TP53 variants; therefore, the favorable outcomes observed here may partly reflect the biological behavior of the specific TP53 variants present, in addition to the treatment strategy itself [8].

In conclusion, our three cases suggest that durable remission in TP53‐altered myeloid neoplasms may be facilitated by a sequential strategy of prompt transplantation after initial reduction of TP53‐positive clones, followed by early, dose‐adjusted, and prolonged Aza maintenance. Given the generally dismal outcomes of likely multihit TP53 disease and the limited ability of allo‐HCT alone to overcome this adverse biology, the durable remissions observed in our series are clinically notable and hypothesis‐generating.
