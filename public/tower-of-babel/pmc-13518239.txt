Partial fluidization theory of collective cell migration
Authors: Igor S. Aranson, Lev S. Tsimring
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Collective cell and tissue migration is crucial for various biological processes, including morphogenesis, wound healing, and cancer metastasis. Migrating cell monolayers often exhibit complex viscoelastic responses and coexistence of solid and liquid-like behaviors that are not well understood in the framework of existing theories. Here, we introduce a partial fluidization approach for collective cell migration. We treat the cell monolayer as a single albeit spatially and temporally non-uniform phase bridging two distinct states: a viscous liquid and a soft solid with an elastic response to shear. The continuous transition between these states is controlled by the fluidization order parameter that, in turn, depends on the normalized shear stress. The model successfully captures key experimental observations: solid-like plug flow of cells in a microfluidic channel, spatial coexistence of moving and immobile domains in cell monolayers, and solid body-like rotational flow of cells in a circular chamber. The approach provides insights into the physical mechanisms of cell migration. It can be extended to more realistic situations, such as confluent monolayers on curved, compliant, or degradable surfaces as well as three-dimensional proliferating tissues and chemical signaling.

Introduction

Collective cell migration is essential for many biological processes, from morphogenesis and wound healing to cancer metastasis1–3. It is driven by individual cell motility accompanied by the complex interplay of cell-cell and cell-substrate adhesion, cytoskeleton-based protrusion, and cell deformations, as well as reorganization of the extracellular matrix (ECM)4. To migrate, individual cells polarize and form a cytoskeletal protrusion at the leading edge (lamellipodia)5,6. Furthermore, to generate a propulsion force, the cells persistently create and break adhesive contacts with the substrate7. Besides, cell-to-cell adhesion, viscoelasticity, and substrate mechanical stiffness are pivotal for the formation of confluent tissues, the mesenchymal-epithelial transition, and cell differentiation8,9.

A multitude of mechanical and biochemical factors control the migration of individual cells in confluent tissues10. In addition to biochemical processes such as cell signaling and chemotaxis, there are many pure physical forces and interactions that are involved in the tissue dynamics. Besides self-propulsion and adhesion to the substrate, cells experience viscoelastic response to external forces11,12. Furthermore, interacting cells exhibit local polarity alignment13 and contact inhibition of locomotion14,15. Also, mechanical stresses that emerge due to cell migration may affect the cell polarity and result in cell reorientation along the principal axis direction of the stress tensor (so-called plithotaxis)16. Importantly, confluent cell tissues often manifest coexistence of solid and liquid-like phases17,18. Multiple studies have shown that aging of cell monolayers (the process during which cells develop enduring adhesive contacts) may exhibit some similarities with jamming and rigidity transitions in amorphous solids19–21. In this situation, cellular monolayers display strong spatial heterogeneity of motion, with patches of rapidly moving cells surrounded by large, relatively immobile cell clusters17,19,22,23. The observed coexistence of liquid-like and solid-like domains is challenging to explain entirely by stochastic fluctuations in the vicinity of the jamming transition.

Theoretical approaches to collective cell motility can be roughly separated into two classes: cell-based (where cells are treated as individual entities) and coarse-grained (tissue is described as a nonlinear continuum field). The individual cell-based techniques range from the agent-based simulation20, self-propelled Voronoi (SPV) models24,25 and vertex models22,23,26–31 to multi-cell phase-field approaches32,33. Continuum descriptions of collective cell migration include sharp-interface34, level-set35 (typically in 2D) and phase-field models (in 2D and 3D)36–38. Concurrently, phenomenological active-gel approaches39,40 and active liquid crystal theory41–43 are often used to describe the dynamics of confluent cell monolayers.

Here, motivated by the partial fluidization concept in granular matter44–46, we introduce an alternative approach to collective cell migration. We treat the cell monolayer as a single but non-uniform phase that bridges two distinct states: liquid-like and solid-like. In a purely liquid phase, the cells don’t have strong cell-cell adhesion and exhibit a viscous response to shear. In contrast, in a purely solid phase, cells develop persistent adhesive contacts and respond to shear stress elastically. In a partially fluidized state, cell monolayers should exhibit both viscosity and elasticity, with adhesive contacts forming and breaking continuously. Such viscoelastic media are often described by Maxwell-type constitutive relations that feature a finite relaxation time. The peculiarity of cellular systems, unlike inanimate amorphous solids and polymer suspensions, is that cells dynamically regulate their own viscoelasticity through changes in cell-cell adhesion and motility. In our description, the relaxation time depends on the fluidization order parameter ρ that varies continuously between the value of zero in the purely liquid state and one in the purely solid state. The dynamics of the order parameter is governed by a nonlinear reaction-diffusion-advection equation with the control parameter that depends on the local shear stress and material properties of the cell monolayer. The model parameters can be extracted from the cell-level simulations (e.g., active vertex model28,47,48 or multi-phase field models32) or directly from experiments. We provide some estimates for the relevant parameters in the Methods section. The model exhibits qualitative agreement with experiments, e.g., on the solid-like plug cell flow in the quasi-1D microfluidic channel49, coexistence of moving and immobile domains in cell monolayers17,19,22,23, and spontaneous emergence of rigid body rotation in circular patches50,51. In the broad parameter range, the initially predominantly liquid phase eventually evolves into the mostly solid phase (jamming transition), in agreement with experiments. Our results provide insights into the collective cell migration and can be readily extended to more realistic situations, e.g., curved surfaces52, compliant/degradable substrates53, 3D tissues54, proliferating cells55, and chemical signaling56. Furthermore, our model provides targeted predictions of the responses of cell monolayers to the space/time localized perturbation of (i) cell-to-cell adhesion or (ii) cell self-propulsion in the mostly solid phase. The first “treatment” (suppression of cell-to-cell adhesion) results in transient restoration of activity and a single velocity burst. The second treatment (suppression of self-propulsion) generates long-range stress redistribution and consecutive secondary velocity bursts. These predictions can be tested experimentally.

We postulate that the viscosity coefficient is finite in the liquid phase (when ρ → 0), while it diverges in the solid phase (ρ → 1). To account for this, we assume that the effective viscosity is given by μ/(1 − ρ). Thus, in a liquid state, we have a “normal” Maxwell fluid with a finite relaxation constant μ/μe, so asymptotically the stress is proportional to the rate of strain. In contrast, in a purely solid state, the relaxation time diverges and the stress tensor is proportional to the integral of the strain rate, i.e., the strain itself. In a partially fluidized state (0 < ρ < 1), the relaxation time gradually increases with ρ and diverges when the system locally approaches a purely solid state.

Cell flow in a 1-D channel

Here, we apply our model to a situation where cells migrate along a one-dimensional channel, which was studied experimentally in ref. 49. In this work, a flowing sheet of Madin-Darby canine kidney (MDCK) cells migrated collectively through a narrow channel. The channel width was approximately 100 μm, which is equivalent to about 10–15 cell sizes. Under these conditions, a plug flow of cells with nearly constant cell velocity across the channel and narrow boundary layers with strong shear were observed. Correspondingly, in the middle of the channel, the cell sheet moved like an elastic body, while at the channel edges the cells slid past each other, like in a fluid.

To describe this situation, we assume that cellular flow is in the x-direction in a channel of width L along the y coordinate. Then all variables depend on the coordinate y and time t, and the velocity and polarization have only one component: v = (vx(y, t), 0), P = (Px(y, t) = 1, 0). The stress tensor has only one non-zero component Σxy(y) while Σxx = − Σyy = 0 and pressure p = const. Without loss of generality, we can take pressure p =p0. We used linear and nonlinear stability analysis (Supplementary Note 1) to compute the phase diagram for the one-dimensional system, in the plane of two dimensionless control parameters, the normalized activity strength κF/p0(D/Γ)1/2 and the normalized channel width L(Γ/D)1/2, see Fig. 1d. The analysis is performed in the limit of zero viscous drag coefficient, η = 0. Although this situation does not correspond to the experiment, it allows us to perform an analytical treatment and obtain important insights. The effect of finite η is discussed later. For positive δ0, the diagram bears the signature of the first-order phase transition: there is a finite range of active forces (or channel widths) at which purely liquid and purely solid phases coexist. In this bistable regime, there exist also non-uniform localized solutions with a solid phase in the middle surrounded by the liquid phase near the side walls (see Fig. 1e). These solutions exist between the red and green lines in Fig. 1d. Analytical results for the universal form of the localized solutions are also given in Supplementary Note 1. Two dotted lines with stars in Fig. 1d illustrate the effect of the finite drag coefficient η. Note that the linear stability limit of the solid phase (the red line) is not affected by η because it is calculated for the velocity v = 0. However, increasing η results in shifting the transition line between the liquid state and the partially fluidized states towards larger values of the effective κF, i.e., stabilizing the solid phase. It could be understood as follows: the viscous drag η results in the overall reduction of the flow velocity and the strain rate, which, in turn, will favor the solid phase. Making the parameter δ0 (the adhesion strength) more positive also favors the solid phase.

Cell flows in two-dimensional geometries

Here, we present the numerical results for the fully two-dimensional cellular flows as described by our partial fluidization model. We solved Eqs. (1)-(6) in the square domain of the size L × L with doubly-periodic boundary conditions. For numerical stability, we formulated the Eqs. (1)-(3) in terms of the stream function, which we then solved together with equations Eqs. (4) and (6) using a pseudospectral method based on the Fast Fourier transform (FFT). For more details see Methods for details of our numerical implementation.

We begin with the numerical exploration of the stability of the one-dimensional stationary flow shown in Fig. 1 with respect to 2D perturbations. Periodicity of the stream function ψ implies that the average velocity 〈v〉 = 0, which makes the plug flow, as in Fig. 1, impossible. However, we can get around this limitation by simulating counter-propagating flows in two parallel channels. In order to implement the effective no-slip conditions on the channel walls, we added an extra damping term − θP with sufficiently large θ > 1 to Eq. (6) outside the channels. It forces the polarization P and, correspondingly, the velocity v to be close to zero. This approach produces qualitatively similar results to those obtained with exact no-slip boundary conditions shown in Fig. 1, although minor numerical discrepancies arise due to this approximation. To better compare 1D and 2D algorithms, we introduced the same effective no-slip condition in 1D model, exactly as it was done in 2D model. Then we studied the dynamics in a square domain with periodic boundary conditions starting from small-amplitude random initial conditions for all field variables. Several snapshots from a typical simulation with an intermediate stress susceptibility parameter κ = 50 are shown in Fig. 3 (for a full sequence see Supplementary Movie 4). The evolution of the solid fraction and the velocity is not monotonous. For intermediate κ = 50, in the beginning, the solid fraction rapidly grows and reaches a local maximum. This initial growth is due to small-amplitude random initial conditions that we impose for the velocity. As a result, the shear stress is initially small and cannot oppose the expansion of the solid phase. As soon as the velocity and the corresponding shear stress are established throughout the system, it impedes the growth of the solid phase. However, as time progresses, the strain rate is suppressed in the solid phase. In turn, it leads to a renewed slow expansion of the solid phase. Eventually, the system reaches a steady state in the predominantly solid phase with multiple big islands of solid phase separated by narrow liquid channels. For smaller κ values, the growth of the solid phase is monotonous, and the solid fraction rapidly approaches the steady-state value close to ϕ = 1. For small κ = 0.5, the system rapidly settles in the homogeneous solid phase with ϕ = 1.

For small susceptibility parameter values, κ = 0.5, the mean velocity magnitude vav = 〈∣v∣〉 slowly decays to zero after initial rapid growth. It indicates the onset of jamming that is often observed in confluent cellular monolayers after an initial relaxation63. For intermediate κ = 50, the mean velocity vav does not disappear even though the solid fraction is close to 1. The apparent reason for this is that large solid domains are separated by fluidized gaps that allow the domains to slide past each other. For even larger κ = 100 the mean velocity is also large.

We systematically computed the solid fraction ϕ and the normalized average velocity vav/F in the asymptotic regime for a wide range of κ and F, keeping other parameters fixed (Fig. 4c,d). According to 1D theory (Supplementary Note 1), the dynamics are controlled by the product of the stress susceptibility parameter κ and active force magnitude F. This scaling also appears to apply to the 2D case: the data for different values of F collapse onto single curves when plotted against the effective susceptibility κe = κF. The model exhibits features of a continuous liquid-solid transition: The solid fraction is 1 in the range of small values 0 < κe < κ1 ≈ 2 − 2.5 and is zero for κe > κ2 ≈ 65 − 70. Correspondingly, in the first interval vav/F is near zero, while in the second, vav/F ≈ 1. For intermediate κ1 < κe < κ2 the volume fraction and vav/F change continuously between 0 and 1. This transition correlates well with the three zones in the phase diagram in Fig. 1d for a 1D channel flow.

Moreover, the 1D theory predicts the correct value for the threshold κ1 for the transition to pure solid based on the linear stability of the ρ = 1 state (the red line in Fig. 1d). The 1D theory is developed for a channel flow with no-slip boundary conditions. However, with our stream function-based numerical integration method and periodic boundary conditions, effective no-slip conditions can be realized only in a half-domain. In the other half-domain, there need to be a symmetric channel flow in the opposite direction. It means that the effective length for the comparison with the 1D phase diagram should be half of the length L in a periodic domain. Using Eq. (S8) for the threshold value κ1, we obtain for L/2 = 37.5 and pressure p0 = 25, the value of κ1 ≈ 1.8, which is close to the computed value of κ1 = 2 − 2.5. For the second threshold κ2, the 1D theory predicts for η = 0 a smaller value, κ2 ≈ 10 (the green line in Fig. 1d). As one sees from the 1D phase diagram, Fig. 1d, increasing the viscous drag η expands the region of stability of the mixed state due to the slowing down of the velocity and decreasing the shear stress.

In the homogeneous solid phase (ρ = 1), the average velocity magnitude vav eventually decays to zero, Fig. 4b, the black curve. However, this overall gradual decay is accompanied by localized bursts of activity, which can be compared to stick-slip events in granular media, where the velocity randomly increases in different locations for a short time (see Fig. 5a-c and Supplementary Movie 6). These small bursts of activity are related to the relaxation dynamics of the polarization field, similar to the mean-field frustrated XY model64. The role of the “frustrating” field here is performed by the pressure gradients in Eq. (6) which, in turn, are determined by the distribution of the stress Σ. Note that in the solid phase, the shear stress is large and heterogeneous (Fig. 5d). Further analyzing the statistics of cellular flows in different regimes, we found that in the solid regime (κ = 0.5), the correlation between the velocity and the polarization is almost zero, see Fig. 4e, unlike the liquid (κ = 100) and even in the partially-fluidized regimes (κ = 50), when the velocity and polarization are strongly correlated. We also compared the velocity distributions in solid, liquid, and partially-fluidized phases, Fig. 4f. The velocity distributions for liquid and partially-fluidized phases are similar: both are narrow and centered around the speed of uniform flow v0 = F. However, the velocity distribution in the solid phase (κ = 0.5) is qualitatively different: it is broad and peaks at much smaller velocities. This wide distribution is a manifestation of random intermittent bursts occurring in the solid phase. The broad, non-Gaussian velocity distributions were observed experimentally in ref. 65. While the experimental conditions in ref. 65 likely correspond to our states in the proximity of the jamming, the interpretation of the results in terms of non-canonical q-Gaussian statistics make the comparison challenging.

Such spatially-heterogeneous activity and stress distributions are visually reminiscent of the experimentally-observed behavior of epithelial monolayers near “jamming” or glassy transition19,20,63. However, in our case, the origin of the glassy-like behavior is pure dynamic and deterministic, due to nonlinear coupling between the stress and polarization, rather than stochasticity in the cell-cell adhesion and other cell properties. The emergence of this “deterministic glassy behavior” is one of the key predictions of our work.

Our computational results in Figs. 3 and 5 exhibit strongly heterogeneous stress distributions. This stress heterogeneity could be interpreted as spatial modulations of the elastic modulus like in ref. 18. However, unlike the approach of ref. 18 where two cell populations with different stiffness were introduced, stress heterogeneity in our framework appears within a single cell population due to the feedback between stress and the fluidization order parameter ρ.

Rotational flows in a circular cavity

According to the previous section, a confluent cell sheet in a large square domain experiences jamming in the solid regime that occurs for small F (active force) and κ (low stress susceptibility). However, in smaller circular domains, an active cellular flow may persist even under these conditions in the form of solid body rotation since it does not violate the constitutive relation, Eq. (3), in the solid phase. The only requirement for the formation of such a flow is a “slip” boundary condition on the boundary of a circular domain. An analytical solution for such solid-body rotational flow can be obtained in polar coordinates, assuming that the order parameter ρ = 1 throughout the domain, and velocity and polarization are both purely azimuthal, see Supplementary Note 2. The analytical solution predicts the angular velocity Ω of the solid-body rotation in the form: Ω ≈ 4F/(3ηr0), where r0 is the radius of the circular domain.

To explore this flow regime numerically, we performed simulations in a square domain as before. However, outside a circular region of radius r0, we imposed ρ = 0, P = 0 conditions at all times that should enable the effective slip condition. We started simulations from small random initial conditions for the polarization, velocity, stress, and order parameter. Quickly, the flow becomes circular with a nearly linear azimuthal velocity dependence on the radius that indicates almost solid-body rotation, see Fig. 6a–d and Supplementary Movie 7. The shear strain rate in the bulk is close to zero (Fig. 6c), as expected for the solid body rotating flow. However, as in the previous section, polarization remains non-collinear with the velocity, and the active force FP creates significant heterogeneous stresses inside the cellular flow (Fig. 6b). This means that purely azimuthal rotational flow may be unstable with respect to fluctuations of the polarization. Rotational flows have indeed been observed in experiments with MDCK epithelial cell sheets50. Based on the experimental data, it is hard to tell whether the authors observed solid-body or slipping rotational flow in their experiments, but it would be interesting to further analyze their data to clarify this question.

Monolayer response to localized spatio-temporal stimuli

Our theory is well suited to make predictions about the response of cell monolayers to various perturbations localized in space and time17,66. Specifically, here we make two predictions on the reactions of the monolayer in a square LxL domain to (i) local reduction of cell-cell adhesion and (ii) local stimulation that suppresses cell-surface adhesion and therefore self-propulsion. The first situation can be achieved experimentally, for example, by using cell lines with inducible expression of the master regulator protein RAB5A17 and applying inducer (doxycycline) locally. Since the order parameter ρ describes the fraction of adhesive contacts between cells, in our theory it can be modeled by suppressing the order parameter ρ in a circular domain of radius r0 for a time interval t0. The second situation can be achieved, for example, by shedding UV light on photo-responsive surfaces to reduce cell-substrate adhesion66. We can model it by local suppression of polarization P (i.e., the propulsion force) in a circular domain of radius r0 for a time interval t0. The results are illustrated in Fig. 7 and Supplementary Movies 9 and 10. In the first case, the suppression of cell-cell adhesion results in fluidization of the cell monolayer in the treatment circle, accompanied by a sharp increase of cell velocity, Fig. 7a, reminiscent of the situation observed in ref. 17. However, after the end of treatment, the liquid domain shrinks, and the cell monolayer continues to undergo elastic relaxation; both the maximum and average velocities decay. The second case (suppression of self-propulsion) is more subtle, see Fig. 7b. Discussion

In this work, we proposea partial fluidization theory that describes the collective dynamics of confluent cell monolayers as a viscoelastic flow of an active self-propelled medium poised near the solid-liquid transition. The transition is controlled by the reaction-diffusion equation for the fluidization order parameter ρ which continuously changes between zero in the purely liquid phase and one in the solid phase. In our interpretation, the order parameter reflects the relative density of the adhesive contacts between neighboring cells and controls the relaxation time in the generalized Maxwell model for the cellular medium. Like the partial fluidization approach in the physics of granular matter44, this theory bridges continuum mechanical models of cell tissues with underlying discrete cellular behaviors. This formulation explains how cell tissues may exhibit simultaneous load-bearing elasticity and localized yielding, enabling coordinated migration while preserving mechanical cohesion. Our analytical and computational results reproduce experimentally observed phenomena such as highly heterogeneous cellular flows in liquid and partially fluidized regimes, a gradual slowdown of cellular flows in the solid regime, reminiscent of glassy-like behavior19 and jamming20,63. The latter is accompanied by localized bursts of activity in the bulk of a monolayer. Also in agreement with experiments, we found spontaneous establishment of rotating flows in circular domains that persist even in the solid regime as solid-body rotational flows. Importantly, the theory yields testable predictions: for instance, on the velocity profiles in narrow channels, dynamic velocity heterogeneity, stress distributions, and the responses to spatio-temporal stimulations.

In our model, we did not include the terms −∇ v ⋅ Σ − Σ ⋅ (∇v)T and − (∇ v) ⋅ P that are present in the upper-convective derivatives67,68 to preserve objectivity with respect to the reference frame rotations and translations with a constant rate. The rationale for this choice is the following. Objectivity of physical tensor and vector variables is required when translations and solid-body rotations do not change the internal physical state of the material. In our system, the cellular layer maintains a strong adhesive contact with an immobile substrate. This substrate naturally specifies a preferred reference frame, the flow velocity in the model is always computed with respect to this frame. Therefore, the objective (reference frame independent) stress tensor representation does not apply here. Note that even in the absence of a substrate the form of the objective time derivative is not unique. The right choice depends on how a material property (vector or tensor) is embedded in the flowing medium. The upper-convected derivative is commonly applied to vector or tensor quantities that are advected by the flow and, importantly, are also rotated and stretched by the local shear. One may think, for example, of a polarization vector P as having two endpoints; if these endpoints move with the surrounding fluid elements, the upper-convected derivative DP/Dt − (∇ v) ⋅ P correctly describes the transport of vector P. In contrast, when a vector or tensor field is associated with rigid particles that are transported and rotated by the flow but not stretched, the upper-convected derivative is no longer appropriate. In such cases, a co-rotational (Jaumann) derivative, DP/Dt − Ω ⋅ P, where Ω is the antisymmetric vorticity tensor, is used. In our case of cells migrating on a substrate, cells are not significantly stretched by the flow. Moreover, we argue that they are also not appreciably rotated by the shear flow as long as they maintain strong adhesive contact with the static substrate. To illustrate this point, let’s consider a shear flow of cells in a 1D channel that was studied above. Including either upper-convective or co-rotational derivatives in equations for stress and polarization in our model would lead to the rotation of the cell polarization and stress tensor in the shear layers near the channel walls and the absence of a steady-state 1D channel solution. Meanwhile, experimental observations Ref. 49 show that cells in such channels neither rotate nor stretch but instead primarily slide past one another near the side walls.

Unlike classic models of plasticity and large deformation elasticity69,70, the developed computational framework is well-behaved near transitions between fluid and solid regimes, highly parallel, and computationally efficient. It enables investigating tissue dynamics over the length and time scale not easily accessible by cell-level description, such as vertex models or single-cell phase-field methods. The model parameters can be extracted from the comparison with experimental data or cell-level simulations in relatively small domains. Another advantage of the continuum theory is that, unlike cell-level (e.g., vertex) models which are sensitive to the choice of not-well-quantified microscopic rules30, the presented framework is robust to the variation of parameters and functional dependencies.

Let us also highlight the distinctions with previously developed continuous models of tissue motility. Our approach differs from that of ref. 71, where cell tissues were treated as a two-phase system with cell death and division. Here, we use a single-phase, albeit spatially and temporally non-uniform description of the tissue dynamics. Death/birth processes can also be readily incorporated into our approach.

Another type of coarse-grained continuum modeling for confluent cell tissues was developed in refs. 47,48, inspired by the results of active vertex models and models of dense suspensions. By introducing phenomenological equations for the cell perimeter field, cell orientation tensor, and fluidity parameter, the authors obtained a constitutive strain-stress relation that incorporates tissue stiffening due to weak external strains and plasticity due to larger strains, in agreement with vertex models. However, that model did not include cell polarization, self-propulsion, and emergent complex phenomena described in our work, such as spatio-temporally heterogeneous phases and hysteretic flow transitions.

Our approach can also be readily extended to three dimensions and formulated for open boundaries and proliferating cells, e.g., by introducing an auxiliary phase field that defines the tissue edges38. Other future extensions may include more details of the underlying cell dynamics, such as bistability of cell polarization7,62, plithotaxis16, and chemical signaling (chemotaxis)56. For crucial biological processes such as wound healing and morphogenesis, chemical guidance (chemotaxis) and mechanical guidance often operate simultaneously. It could be included in our framework by adding a reaction-diffusion equation for an additional chemical field and a term in the equation for polarization describing its coupling with the chemical gradient.

Methods

Parameter estimates

Some of the parameters of our model can be estimated from available experimental data. Common cell lines often used in tissue migration experiments are Madin-Darby canine kidney (MDCK) cells; they have a typical size lc ≈ 10−15 μm, and their free migration velocity Vm is approximately 0.5 μm/min. Tissue elasticity μe and viscosity μ in Eq. (3) can be estimated from Ref. 11 that reports the Maxwell relaxation time τ = μe/μ ≈ 70 min. Ref. 72 reports the tissue elastic modulus of the order 10 kPa. The cell protrusion force Fp is of the order 1-2 nN73. Alternatively, the force density (or traction force) is reported as Ftraction ~ 1 nN/μm2= 1 kPa74.

Let us bring Eqs. (1)–(6) in dimensionless form. We will choose a characteristic time scale τ0 as the time required for a cell to migrate one body length, τ0 ≈ lc/Vm ≈ 15−20 min. We will use a cell size lc ≈ 10−15 μm as a characteristic length scale. It yields dimensionless elastic constant μe ≈ 10−15, dimensionless relaxation time τ/τ0 ≈ 4−6, dimensionless viscosity μ ≈ 1 − 3, parameters F, η of the order of one.

Experimental data is lacking for the estimation of other model parameters, such as the order parameter and polarization relaxation rates and the stress susceptibility κ, so we used plausible values for them that produce experimentally observed phenomenology. Fortunately, the model is sufficiently robust, so the phenomenology remains qualitatively the same for a wide variation of these unobserved parameters. We anticipate that the relaxation rates should be comparable with the characteristic time τ0, yielding the OP relaxation rate Γ ~ 1. The value of stress susceptibility κ could be smaller than one, favoring the solid phase. Note that cell-cell adhesion and, therefore, presumably the value of κ can be modulated by certain drugs, e.g., RAB5A protein17.

Numerical implementation

We solved 2D equations (1)–(6) in a doubly periodic square domain using a combination of quasi-spectral and finite difference techniques. Typically, 1024 × 1024 mesh points were used. Results were also checked in a smaller domain of 512 × 512 mesh points. First, we solved Eq. (1) by taking a curl (∇ × ) and finding the vorticity ω = ∇ × v. Then, using the relation that ∇2ψ = − ω, where ψ is the stream function, the velocity was computed as v = z0 × ∇ ψ, where z0 is the unit vector perpendicular to the plane. The pressure p was determined by taking the divergence of Eq. (1) and inverting the Laplace operator. Because we use a pseudo-spectral method, the zero-wavenumber component of the computed pressure field, i.e., the mean pressure 〈p〉, is determined only up to an arbitrary constant. This constant does not affect the flow structure of an incompressible fluid directly, but it affects it indirectly via the constitutive relation Eq. (5) where the full pressure is used. We set 〈p〉 = p0, where p0 denotes the applied pressure and is an important parameter of the model. After that, the velocity gradients were calculated using the central finite differences, and Eq. (3) was solved. Then, Eqs. (4) and (6) were solved by the quasi-spectral split-step method using the Fast Fourier transformation (FFT) for computing exponentials of the diffusion operators.
