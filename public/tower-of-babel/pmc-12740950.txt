A novel microbial dissolution-reprecipitation pathway for bio-sintering of limestone for biocement production
Authors: Raja Murugan, Anant Aishwarya Dubey, Navdeep K. Dhami, Abhijit Mukherjee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn nature, remarkable geological structures, such as stromatolites, thrombolites, and beachrocks, are formed through biocementation, a process involving the successive dissolution and reprecipitation of CaCO3. This research demonstrates mimicking natural cement via bio-sintering of limestone, a process that involves successive dissolution and reprecipitation of limestone facilitated by bacteria under ambient environmental conditions. When the bacterium Acetobacter aceti (ATCC 15973) was introduced into a mixture of ethanol and limestone powder, the pH dropped rapidly, leading to the dissolution of limestone into calcium acetate. After ethanol was fully consumed, the pH gradually increased due to acetate oxidation, causing biocement crystals to precipitate. All reaction rates were measured, and the products characterized through Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy, scanning electron microscopy, quantitative X-ray diffraction, and a particle size analyzer. The detailed characterization indicates that the precipitate is mainly calcite and that the dissolved calcium carbonate sinters microbially. This pathway offers new opportunities in biocementation research by using limestone as a direct calcium source, reducing the overall carbon footprint, and eliminating the need for urea or other chemical additives. The biocement produced shows potential for practical applications in soil stabilization, eco-concrete, and other sustainable construction solutions, providing a foundation for environmentally conscious alternatives in next-generation construction. Introduction

Biocementation is a biologically mediated process in which microorganisms induce the precipitation of calcium carbonate (CaCO3), acting as a natural binder in applications such as soil stabilization (Dubey et al. 2022a, b; Whiffin et al. 2007), self-healing concrete (Kaur et al. 2020), and bio-based construction materials (Achal et al. 2011). However, conventional ureolytic biocementation relies on urea and calcium chloride and consequently poses risks such as ammonia gas production and chloride-related corrosion in concrete (Achal & Mukherjee 2015). Recent studies have also reported that the widely adopted ureolytic pathway of biocementation is environmentally unsustainable, as it relies on urea with high embodied energy and carbon emissions, and generates ammonium by-products that pose significant eutrophication risks (Porter et al. 2021). Therefore, exploring novel mechanisms using naturally available materials for biocement production is important for a cleaner and potentially more scalable alternative (Myhr et al. 2019; Porter et al. 2021; Royne et al. 2019; Shi et al. 2020).

While ureolysis remains the dominant mechanism, non-ureolytic pathways such as those mediated by photosynthetic microorganisms or sulphate-reducing bacteria have shown promise by inducing CaCO3 precipitation through pH shifts or metabolic by-products, without relying on urea (Alvarado-Mata et al. 2025). These alternative systems expand the scope of biocementation toward more environmentally benign processes. Building on this direction, we introduce a novel bio-sintering process for biocement production, where microbial metabolism enables both the dissolution and reprecipitation of CaCO3 using naturally available limestone as the calcium source. Bio-sintering is conceptually distinct from biocementation and biomineralization. While biomineralization refers broadly to all biologically influenced mineral formation, biocementation specifically involves microbial-induced bonding of particles via mineral precipitation. In contrast, bio-sintering, as investigated here, refers to the microbially mediated dissolution and reprecipitation of existing minerals, effectively mimicking natural lithification without the need for high-temperature sintering or chemical additives. This process is driven by the metabolic activity of Acetobacter aceti (ATCC 15973), a gram-negative, obligately aerobic bacterium that can oxidize ethanol to acetic acid through a membrane-bound alcohol and aldehyde dehydrogenase enzyme system. This is followed by the further oxidation or assimilation of acetate via the TCA cycle or glyoxylate shunt (Saeki et al. 2014; Yakushi & Matsushita 2010).

During the process, a series of biochemical reactions occurs in the bio-sintering process via the oxidation of ethanol. Initially, A. aceti oxidizes ethanol (C2H5OH) in the presence of oxygen (O2), producing acetate ions (CH3COO⁻), protons (H⁺), and water (H2O) as illustrated in Eq. 1. The CaCO3 then dissolves in the acidic environment, releasing calcium ions (Ca2+), shown in Eq. 2. The acetate ions are further oxidized by oxygen, forming additional CO2, water, and hydroxide ions (OH⁻), as demonstrated in Eq. 3. The hydroxide ions combine with CO2 to form carbonate ions (CO32−) (Eq. 4), which then react with calcium ions to reprecipitate CaCO3 (Eq. 5). Thus, the overall bio-sintering reaction can be summarized as the oxidation of ethanol leading to the dissolution of CaCO3, followed by acetate oxidation, carbonate ion formation, and the reprecipitation of CaCO3.

Materials and methods

Growth and bio-sintering medium

A. aceti (ATCC 15973) was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection and revived on autoclaved Mannitol-Agar medium containing 5 g/L yeast extract, 3 g/L peptone, 25 g/L mannitol, and 15 g/L agar. Before the bio-sintering experiments, the bacteria were subcultured in a liquid medium with 10 g/L yeast extract. A. aceti is considered biosafe and eco-compatible, as it is a non-pathogenic, food-grade bacterium commonly used in vinegar production.

In a 250-mL flask, 100 mL of bio-sintering medium was prepared containing 1% (w/v) ethanol, 10 g/L limestone powder (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and 1 g/L yeast extract. The yeast extract was autoclaved separately, while ethanol and limestone powder were added afterwards. The overnight-grown A. aceti culture in 10 g/L yeast extract medium had an optical density (OD600) of approximately 2.0 before centrifugation. The culture was centrifuged, and the supernatant was discarded. The resulting cell pellet was resuspended in the bio-sintering medium to achieve an initial OD₆₀₀ of 0.2. To ensure that the observed mineral transformation was biologically mediated, a control experiment using heat-killed A. aceti was also conducted under identical conditions. The bio-sintering experiment was conducted in a shaking flask at 30 °C and 180 rpm.

Bio-sintering process monitoring

The bio-sintering process was monitored over time following the resuspension of bacteria into the bio-sintering medium. At various time intervals during the process, 2 mL of culture medium was collected and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 5 min. The supernatant was then used to measure the concentrations of ethanol, soluble calcium, acetate, and pH. The pellet was analyzed using scanning electron microscopy with energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM-EDS), X-ray diffraction (XRD), Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), and a particle size analyzer. The experiments were performed thrice, independently, to ensure reproducibility. Table 1 shows the experimental monitoring and analysis of the bio-sintering process. Analytical techniques: methods to measure the concentration of ethanol, acetate ions, calcium ions, and pH

The ethanol concentration was determined using the Ethanol Assay Kit (Abcam, ab272531) according to a colorimetric method. Samples were diluted to fall within the standard range of 0 to 170 mM. In a 96-well microplate, 66.66 µL of each sample or standard was mixed with an equal volume of Reagent A and incubated at room temperature for 8 min. To stop the reaction, 66.66 µL of the stopping reagent (Reagent B) was added, bringing the final volume to 200 µL. Absorbance was measured using a microplate reader, and ethanol concentration was calculated from the slope of the standard curve generated by plotting absorbance against known ethanol concentrations.

Acetate concentration was measured using a colorimetric assay with the Acetate Assay Kit (Abcam, ab204719). Samples were diluted to fall within the standard range of 0 to 1 mM, and standard solutions were prepared accordingly. In a 96-well microplate, 50 µL of each sample or standard was mixed with 50 µL of a reagent mix containing acetate assay buffer, acetate enzyme mix, ATP II/ATP, acetate substrate mix, and developer solution in a 21:1:1:1:1 ratio, making a total reaction volume of 100 µL. The mixture was incubated at room temperature for 40 min, protected from light, before measuring absorbance at 450 nm using a microplate reader. A standard curve was generated by plotting absorbance against concentration, and the sample acetate concentration was determined using the slope of the standard plot.

The concentration of soluble Ca2⁺ was determined using the complexometric titration method (American Public Health Association, 2012). To begin, 40 µL of the supernatant was diluted to 10 mL by adding 9.56 mL of water and 400 µL of 1 N NaOH to raise the pH. Subsequently, a few drops of 1% (w/v) hydroxy naphthol blue disodium salt solution were added as an indicator. The titration was carried out using a 1 mM EDTA disodium salt solution, and the endpoint was reached when the colour changed from pink to blue. For calibration, standard solutions of 0 to 2 mM CaCl₂ were prepared and titrated against the 1 mM EDTA solution. The endpoints of these standard solutions were recorded, and a graph was plotted showing the relationship between the CaCl₂ concentration and the volume of 1 mM EDTA required to reach the endpoint. The concentration of the unknown sample was then determined from the slope of the resulting plot. The pH of the supernatant solution was measured using a pH meter (Thermo Scientific, Orion Star A211).

FTIR analysis

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was conducted to analyze the precipitate obtained after the bio-sintering process. For comparison, precursor limestone powder used in the bio-sintering process was also subjected to FTIR analysis. Spectra were recorded using an FT-IR-100 spectrometer (Perkin Elmer) over the wavenumber range of 4000–650 cm⁻1. The resulting spectra were utilized for peak identification.

SEM analysis

Morphological analysis and elemental composition of the dried powder were carried out using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and qualitative energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS). The analysis was conducted with a Variable Pressure Electron Microscope (VP-SEM, Zeiss, EVO 40-XVP, 2008). Samples were mounted on carbon-aluminium tape, and their surfaces were coated with carbon using a carbon evaporative coater (Creissington, 2080 C, 2011). The imaging was performed at a beam intensity of 10 and a voltage of 10 kV, with a working distance of around 15 mm. Both secondary electron (SE) and backscattered electron (BSE) imaging were combined to capture the electron micrographs.

X-ray diffractometry analysis

The samples were resuspended in ethanol and deposited onto low-background sample holders. X-ray diffraction (XRD) data were collected using a Bruker D8 Advance diffractometer equipped with Ni-filtered Cu Kα radiation (40 kV, 40 mA). Measurements were performed over the range of 7–120° 2θ with a step size of 0.015°. Phase identification was conducted using Bruker EVA 5.2 software and the COD (Crystallography Open Database). Phase quantification was performed using the Rietveld refinement method implemented in Topas Academic 7, with crystal structure data retrieved from the COD database.

Particle size analysis

The size distribution of CaCO₃ particles was measured using a Mastersizer (2000S). An appropriate amount of the sample was dispersed in deionized water, which was used as the medium. Measurements were conducted using laser diffraction, and the particle size distribution was recorded. The data were analyzed to obtain D10, D50, and D90 values.

Results

Dissolution and reprecipitation of CaCO3

The bio-sintering process using A. aceti was investigated to evaluate the feasibility of microbially induced dissolution of limestone, followed by cementation of CaCO3 under ambient conditions. To confirm that the process was microbially mediated, a control experiment using heat-killed A. aceti was performed. In this control, no ethanol oxidation or CaCO3 dissolution was observed, indicating that active microbial metabolism is essential for the bio-sintering process. Figure 1a shows the profiles of ethanol, acetate, calcium, and pH over time for this control experiment. To further elucidate the microbial contribution and decouple the microbial oxidation of ethanol from mineral interactions, an additional control experiment was performed without CaCO3 (Fig. 1b). In this system, a steady decline in ethanol concentration was observed, with 90% of the ethanol oxidized within 60 h. A total of 10 g/L of limestone powder (approximately 100 mM CaCO3) was included in the above bio-sintering medium to observe possible dissolution and reprecipitation. A series of contrasting biochemical transformations was observed, labelled as the dissolution phase and the reprecipitation phase (Fig. 1c). During the dissolution phase (0–96 h), ethanol oxidation by A. aceti progressed steadily, with undetectable ethanol concentration by the 96th hour. Simultaneously, the concentration of the acetate rose above 150 mM, and the pH dropped to 5.96. These acidic conditions facilitated the dissolution of limestone powder, as evidenced by the rise in soluble calcium concentration to a peak of 76 ± 4 mM. The calculated ethanol oxidation and acetate production rates were 2.87 ± 0.35 mM/h and 2.33 ± 0.32 mM/h, respectively, while the CaCO3 dissolution rate peaked at 1.23 ± 0.2 mM/h. Following the dissolution phase, the system transitioned into the reprecipitation phase, marked by the consumption of the acetate and an increase in the pH. From 96 to 392 h, acetate was gradually oxidized, driving the pH up to 9.7 and reprecipitating CaCO3 back in the mineral form. This shift resulted in the complete removal of free calcium ions from the solution. The rate of acetate oxidation during this phase was 0.565 ± 0.11 mM/h, while the CaCO3 precipitation rate was 0.23 ± 0.06 mM/h.

Confirmation of reprecipitation of CaCO3 using FTIR

To assess the chemical composition of the biocement produced via bio-sintering, FTIR analysis was performed and compared against precursor limestone powder. As shown in Fig. 2, the FTIR spectra of both the microbially induced biocement and the reference limestone powder exhibit identical peaks at 1398, 871, and 712 cm−1. SEM images illustrate the morphological evolution of CaCO3 crystals

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was used to investigate the morphological evolution of CaCO3 during the bio-sintering process. Before dissolution (Fig. 3a), the CaCO3 crystals exhibited well-defined rhombohedral morphology, characteristic of pure calcite, with smooth, pore-free surfaces. During the dissolution phase (Fig. 3b), substantial surface alterations were evident. The CaCO3 particles showed numerous pores, and surface etching could be attributed to the localized acidic microenvironment generated by the microbial oxidation of ethanol to acetic acid. After the reprecipitation phase (Fig. 3c), the CaCO3 crystals exhibited a transformation into aggregated, spheroidal to ellipsoidal forms with relatively rough, perforated textures. The qualitative EDS spectra of the limestone powder and biocement are provided as supplementary material (Fig. S1). Both spectra confirm the presence of carbon, calcium, and oxygen, indicating that the primary constituent is CaCO3.

XRD confirms that the reprecipitated biocement is calcite

Quantitative X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was performed to investigate the phase composition and crystallographic characteristics of the reprecipitated biocement. The analysis compared the diffraction patterns of biocement samples with those of precursor limestone powder (Fig. 4). The XRD spectra of the biocement showed a high degree of similarity to that of the limestone powder, with both displaying prominent peaks corresponding to the calcite phase. This observation confirms that the primary crystalline phase formed during reprecipitation is calcite, the most thermodynamically stable polymorph of calcium carbonate. Phase quantification indicated that the limestone powder consisted of approximately 95% calcite, with the remaining fraction identified as dolomite [Ca(Mg)(CO3)2]. In contrast, the biocement exhibited altered particle size characteristics, with d(0.1), d(0.5), and d(0.9) values of 8.13 µm, 13.02 µm, and 16.50 µm, respectively. This shift suggests a narrowing of the particle size distribution and an enhancement of size uniformity after bio-sintering. The observed reduction in d(0.9) and increase in d(0.1) indicate that the particle population is being homogenized, which may result from the dissolution of the initial CaCO3 crystals followed by controlled microbial reprecipitation.

Discussion

The dissolution of CaCO3 (Fig. 1c) is consistent with the surface reaction between acetic acid and CaCO3, as reported in microbial acidification-driven carbonate solubilization mechanisms (Fredd & Fogler 1998; Jacobson & Wu 2009). Following this dissolution, reprecipitation aligns with established microbial-induced carbonate precipitation principles, where a pH increase drives Ca2+ and CO32− recombination into solid CaCO3 (Dejong et al. 2010; Murugan et al. 2021), enabling a complete bio-sintering cycle under ambient conditions.

The central role of A. aceti in this bio-sintering process can be attributed to its well-characterized metabolic pathway. The bacterium utilizes membrane-bound pyrroloquinoline quinone (PQQ)-dependent alcohol dehydrogenase (ADH) and aldehyde dehydrogenase (ALDH) to oxidize ethanol to acetate. Once ethanol is exhausted, acetate is further metabolized via acetyl-CoA synthetase (ACS) into acetyl-CoA, feeding into the TCA cycle for energy generation (Sakurai et al. 2012). This acetate oxidation not only provides metabolic energy but also contributes to the alkalinization of the medium, a phenomenon previously associated with the microbial oxidation of negatively charged organic acids (Jacobson & Wu 2009). Taken together, these results highlight the effectiveness of A. aceti in facilitating both the dissolution and precipitation of CaCO3, providing a low-energy, biologically driven approach for biocement production.

In addition to the biological efficacy, this bio-sintering approach offers several application-oriented advantages. The absence of chloride ions eliminates corrosion risks in steel-reinforced structures, while the chemical compatibility with conventional limestone-based materials supports integration into existing construction systems. Moreover, the use of waste-derived ethanol as a carbon source increases sustainability by reducing dependency on high-purity substrates (Narisetty et al. 2023). These features make the system attractive for field applications such as soil stabilization, coastal erosion protection, and bio-brick production.

This study is a proof-of-concept and would need further investigations in several directions. In future, we shall report mechanical strength tests (e.g. compressive or bonding strength) of the carbonate products, which are essential to assess the efficacy of the cement.

The selection of A. aceti is based on its biosafety, aerobic nature, high ethanol oxidation efficiency, and acid tolerance (Gomes et al. 2018). These traits support its use in scalable, safe, and environmentally compatible bio-sintering systems. To further enhance performance and self-sufficiency, future research could explore the use of microbial consortia, where ethanol-producing organisms are paired with A. aceti to establish a closed-loop bio-sintering system. This could reduce input requirements while improving in situ applicability in diverse environments.

Equations 2 and 3 show that one mole of calcium carbonate is reprecipitated while four moles of carbon could be released as CO2 from the dissolution of limestone and oxidation of acetate. Although it can be argued that out of the four moles of CO2 released, three may be considered ‘biogenic’ emissions from acetate during reprecipitation, which could be treated as carbon neutral (Pscherer & Krommes 2024). Nonetheless, with further work, especially on understanding whether acetate is fully oxidized or partly assimilated, there may be an opportunity to reduce carbon emissions (Arai et al. 2016). Moreover, we acknowledge that kinetic data derived from batch flask experiments may not fully reflect the complexity of real-world conditions. Environmental heterogeneity, limited mass transfer, and fluctuating pH in field settings are likely to influence the system’s behaviour. As such, this study provides foundational mechanistic insights. Its pilot-scale validation under site-relevant and application-specific scenarios is underway and will be reported in future.

The mineralogical and morphological evidence support this pathway. FTIR spectra (Fig. 2) show peak positions matching reported CaCO3 values (Hajji et al. 2017; Reig et al. 2002). The close match in peak positions confirms that the mineral phase of the biocement is chemically consistent with that of precursor limestone powder. SEM observations (Fig. 3a) reveal that the morphology of CaCO3 aligns with previously reported structures for synthetic or geological calcite (Boyjoo et al. 2014). Such morphological changes enhance the surface area of the crystals and accessibility for microbial activity, facilitating accelerated dissolution. Figure 3b shows porous, etched crystals attributed to the localized acidic microenvironment generated by the microbial oxidation of ethanol to acetic acid. This acid-mediated dissolution process led to the release of Ca2+ and CO32− ions, as reflected in the porous and degraded crystal structures in Fig. 3c. In turn, these sponge-like features are indicative of microbial mediation during the reprecipitation of CaCO3. The rise in pH due to acetate oxidation by A. aceti promoted supersaturation and subsequent crystal nucleation. The resulting morphology, marked by rounded, aggregated particles, is a well-documented outcome of biologically induced mineralization processes (Khanjani et al. 2021; Rodriguez-Blanco et al. 2011).

The morphological shift from rhombohedral to spheroidal crystal forms suggests that crystal growth dynamics can be tailored by bio-sintering. Factors such as microbial species, medium composition, saturation index, and the presence of ions like magnesium are known to influence crystal morphology during biocementation (Al Imran et al. 2018; Chekroun et al. 2004; Onal Okyay & Frigi Rodrigues 2014). This aspect requires further investigation. Figure 4 shows that the presence of dolomite in both samples may be attributed to the presence of trace amounts of magnesium ions in the starting limestone powder used for the bio-sintering process. Magnesium incorporation into calcium carbonate structures is commonly observed in natural minerals and is known to influence crystal formation and stability (Dwivedi et al. 2016). The microbial species involved, along with the ionic composition of the medium, play a crucial role in determining the resulting CaCO3 polymorph. In this study, A. aceti facilitated the formation of calcite, aligning with previous reports that link microbial activity and solution chemistry to calcite precipitation (Heveran et al. 2019). Moreover, recent microstructural investigations have shown similar calcite morphologies and densification behaviour in bio-cemented matrices, further validating the effectiveness of such microbial approaches (Fang & Achal 2025). The investigated bio-sintering process effectively produces biocement primarily composed of calcite with minor impurities, yielding a material that is structurally and chemically comparable to the precursor limestone powder. The high calcite content suggests the material is suitable for structural and environmental applications where stability and durability are essential (Dhami et al. 2013; Phillips et al. 2013).

The changes in the crystal size distribution in Fig. 5 are consistent with the thermodynamically driven nucleation and crystal growth mechanisms, wherein increased supersaturation during microbial activity can reduce the critical nucleus size, leading to the formation of smaller, more uniform crystals (Oxtoby 1992). The resultant biocement morphology reflects the influence of microbial mediation on precipitation kinetics and crystal development. Previous studies have shown that larger crystals packed with epitaxial growth have higher strength when compared to smaller spheroid crystals (Dubey et al. 2022a, b; Heveran et al. 2019). Therefore, by adjusting environmental conditions during the cementation process, it may be possible to tailor the particle size, which in turn could help shape the physical properties of the final material.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates the successful production of biocement through the bio-sintering process, utilizing A. aceti for microbial dissolution and reprecipitation of CaCO3. The ambient conditions employed in the bio-sintering process, combined with the use of naturally abundant limestone, facilitate reduced energy consumption and present a sustainable alternative for biocementation applications. The biocement produced through this method exhibits similar morphological, phase, and chemical characteristics to limestone powder. Results indicate that it should be possible to tailor the properties of the biocement by controlling the particle size through environmental adjustments in the reprecipitation phase.

Additionally, bio-sintering also opens the possibility of cementing granular materials by utilizing the naturally present native minerals in sand or soil without having to augment synthetic minerals. Such a system would closely emulate nature’s way of construction. Further research is planned in two directions: (1) to optimize the process parameters and improve the scalability and (2) to explore applications of bio-sintering in soil stabilization, coastal protection, and infrastructure development.
