A finite-resources description of a measurement process and its implications for the “Wigner’s Friend” scenario
Fernando de Melo, Gabriel Dias Carvalho, Pedro S. Correia, Paola Concha Obando, Thiago R. de Oliveira, Raúl O. Vallejos

Abstract
Quantum mechanics started out as a theory to describe the smallest scales of energy in Nature. After a hundred years of development it is now routinely employed to describe, among others, quantum computers with thousands of qubits. This tremendous progress turns the debate of foundational questions into a technological imperative. In what follows we introduce a model of a quantum measurement process that consistently includes the impact of having access only to finite resources when describing a macroscopic system, like a measurement apparatus. Leveraging modern tools from equilibration of closed systems and typicality, we show how the measurement collapse can be seen as an effective description of a closed dynamics, of which we do not know all its details. Our model is then exploited to address the “Wigner Friend Scenario”, and we observe that an agreement is reached when both Wigner and his friend acknowledge their finite resources perspective and describe the measurement process accordingly.



Introduction.

Quantum mechanics is one of the most successful theories of science. In the same breadth, it precisely describes the most fundamental constituents of Nature
[
1
]
, and it also enables the development of technologies with tremendous impact on human society
[

Although evermore persuasive, such universality puts a spot in one of the most difficult issues of quantum mechanics, the quantum-to-classical transition or, more specifically, on the quantum measurement problem
[
8
]
. Within the postulates of quantum mechanics there are two types of dynamics: The first type involves the intrinsic, continuous evolution of isolated quantum systems, governed by Schrödinger’s equation. The second type concerns the measurement process, an interaction between a quantum system and a measurement apparatus, which leads to the “collapse” of quantum superpositions and to a measurement outcome. For this second type of process there is no dynamical equation, and the measurement apparatus is assumed classical. The issue is then: if quantum mechanics is universal, why should it resort to a classical apparatus in its very foundations? If it is not universal, when should one use one type of dynamic or another?

Such a conundrum is most vividly illustrated by the so-called “Wigner’s Friend Scenario”
[
9
]
, which is commonly described as follows. Suppose that Wigner runs a lab where a spin is being measured in the
\{\delimiter 69640972+\rangle,\delimiter 69640972-\rangle\}
basis by a friend of his. The measurement apparatus starts in a “ready” state denoted by
\delimiter 696409720\rangle
. Assume that the apparatus is calibrated such that if the spin is prepared in state
\delimiter 69640972+\rangle
(
\delimiter 69640972-\rangle
), after the measurement the apparatus’ pointer will be in the
\delimiter 69640972+1\rangle
(
\delimiter 69640972-1\rangle
) state, with the apparatus’ states classically distinguishable. Now suppose that the spin is prepared in the state
\delimiter 69640972\psi\rangle=(\delimiter 69640972+\rangle+\delimiter 69640972-\rangle)/\sqrt{2}
. In such a case, the postulate of quantum mechanics describing this measurement process entails that fifty percent of the time, the spin and apparatus will end up in the state
\delimiter 69640972+\rangle\otimes\delimiter 69640972+1\rangle

\delimiter 69640972-\rangle\otimes\delimiter 69640972-1\rangle

(|+,+1\rangle\!\langle+,+1|+|-,-1\rangle\!\langle-,-1|)/2

At this point, we turn our attention to the description by Wigner, who is outside of the lab and assumes the universality of quantum mechanics. As such, he describes both the spin and the measurement apparatus (even possibly his friend in the lab) as quantum mechanical systems which undergo unitary dynamics – a description of the measurement process championed by von Neumann
[
10
]
. If the spin and apparatus dynamics seen by the friend is now described by a unitary

U\delimiter 69640972+\rangle\otimes\delimiter 696409720\rangle=\delimiter 69640972+\rangle\otimes\delimiter 69640972+1\rangle

U\left(\frac{\delimiter 69640972+\rangle+\delimiter 69640972-\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}\otimes\delimiter 696409720\rangle\right)=\frac{\delimiter 69640972+,+1\rangle+\delimiter 69640972-,-1\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}
,
which correlates spin and apparatus in the expected way, but does not lead to a mixture of definite outcomes.
In Wigner’s perspective, the state after every run of the measurement process is the entangled state
\delimiter 69640972\phi^{+}\rangle:=(\delimiter 69640972+,+1\rangle+\delimiter 69640972-,-1\rangle)/\sqrt{2}

The fact that Wigner and his friend come to different descriptions is not an issue for most modern interpretations of quantum mechanics
[
11
]
. However, following Deutsch’s argument
[
12
]
, suppose that in each round of the experiment, after receiving from his friend the information that a measurement took place, Wigner measures both the spin and the measurement apparatus with a two-valued observable with associated projectors
\{|\phi^{+}\rangle\!\langle\phi^{+}|,\mathds{1}-|\phi^{+}\rangle\!\langle\phi^{+}|\}

\delimiter 69640972\phi^{+}\rangle
with certainty; while if using the Friend’s description that happens only half of the times. This mismatch of assigned probabilities for a fixed experimental scenario is a legitimate issue for most interpretations of quantum mechanics.

In the above recount of the Wigner’s Friend scenario, it was assumed, as usual, that Wigner does have the capacity to perform a projective measurement in the combined system of a spin and measurement apparatus, even when the latter is a macroscopic system that supposedly behaves classically. At this point most authors mention that performing such a measurement is a major challenge, but they go on to say that it can be done “in principle”.

In Physical sciences, nevertheless, such “in principle” statements cannot disregard the bare fact that only finite resources are available to implement a physical process. One striking example where accounting for finite resources clears up an apparent paradox is the case of Maxwell’s demon. When the finiteness of the demon’s memory is taken into account, one reaches the physically sounding conclusion that no work can be extracted from heat
[
13
]
.

This mindset of thermodynamics can be exploited to shine a light on the measurement process
[
14
,
15
]
, and thus also on the Wigner’s Friend scenario. Such a connection can be made by realizing that the capacity of performing a joint measure in the spin and the measurement apparatus is equivalent to being able to reverse the unitary dynamics characterizing the measurement process

]
. In Wigner’s perspective, if the measurement process is undone, the whole system returns to the initial state
(\delimiter 69640972+\rangle+\delimiter 69640972-\rangle)\otimes\delimiter 696409720\rangle/\sqrt{2}
. While in the Friend’s perspective, half of the time, the system returns to
\delimiter 69640972+\rangle\otimes\delimiter 696409720\rangle
, and in the other half, it goes to
\delimiter 69640972-\rangle\otimes\delimiter 696409720\rangle
. Therefore, by measuring only the spin in the basis
\{(\delimiter 69640972+\rangle+\delimiter 69640972-\rangle)/\sqrt{2},(\delimiter 69640972+\rangle-\delimiter 69640972-\rangle)/\sqrt{2}\}
, which is routinely performed in labs all over the world, one reaches the same dilemma.

This latter narrative of the Wigner’s Friend scenario alludes to the (ir)reversibility of macroscopic systems, a subject much debated in the foundations of statistical physics
[
16
,
17
]

As in there, in what follows, we are going to argue that no paradox is reached in the Wigner’s Friend scenario when a description of the measurement process that uses only finite resources is employed. When a process that can be called a quantum measurement takes place, it is in fact irreversible.

A by now standard way to describe irreversible processes within quantum mechanics is by the open quantum system formalism

A finite-resource model of quantum measurement.

Here, we introduce a general model describing the spin measurement employing only finite resources. For other models see for instance
[

To the spin we associate a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{S}\simeq\mathbb{C}^{2}
, spanned by the vectors
\{\delimiter 69640972+\rangle,\delimiter 69640972-\rangle\}
. The initial state of the spin system will be
\delimiter 69640972\psi\rangle=c_{+}\delimiter 69640972+\rangle+c_{-}\delimiter 69640972-\rangle
, with
c_{+},c_{-}\in\mathbb{C}
such that
|c_{+}|^{2}+|c_{-}|^{2}=1
. We write
\psi=|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|
for its density matrix.

For the apparatus we associate a
d

\mathcal{H}_{A}\simeq\mathbb{C}^{d}
, which we write as a direct sum of three subspaces,
\mathcal{H}_{A}=\mathcal{H}_{+1}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{0}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{-1}
. The first subspace is to be associated with the outcome
+1
, the second with the outcome
0
, and the third with the outcome
-1
. Without loss of generality we assume that
\dim{\mathcal{H}_{+1}}=\dim{\mathcal{H}_{-1}}:=d_{1}
, and define
\dim{\mathcal{H}_{0}}:=d_{0}
, leading thus to
d=2d_{1}+d_{0}
. The space
\mathcal{H}_{0}
relates to the apparatus’ initial value. Since the only information we have is that the apparatus starts in the “
0
” value, its initial state will be taken as the microcanonical ensemble in the corresponding subspace, i.e.,
\mathds{1}_{0}/d_{0}
. The total initial state then reads:
{\varrho}_{0}=\psi\otimes\left(0\oplus\frac{\mathds{1}_{0}}{d_{0}}\oplus 0\right)
.

We can now define the model’s Hamiltonian:

H=|+\rangle\!\langle+|\otimes(H_{[+1,0]}\oplus 0)+|-\rangle\!\langle-|\otimes(0\oplus H_{[0,-1]}),

(1)

where
H_{[+1,0]}
has support only in
\mathcal{H}_{+1}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{0}
, and
H_{[0,-1]}
has support only in
\mathcal{H}_{0}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{-1}
. The rationale behind this Hamiltonian is simple. With the apparatus starting in a state with support only on
\mathcal{H}_{0}
, any component of the spin system along
\delimiter 69640972+\rangle
will induce transitions from
\mathcal{H}_{0}
to
\mathcal{H}_{+1}
and back.
Similarly, any component of the spin system along
\delimiter 69640972-\rangle
will induce transitions between
\mathcal{H}_{0}
and
\mathcal{H}_{-1}
. If
\lim_{d\rightarrow\infty}d_{0}/d_{1}=0
we expect that any initial population in
\mathcal{H}_{0}
will be proportionally transferred to
\mathcal{H}_{+1}
or
\mathcal{H}_{-1}
, and there it will remain for a long time

1

To be able to experimentally prepare the apparatus’ initial state we must require
d_{0}/d_{1}\rightarrow 0
as
\textrm{poly}(1/d)
.
. Additionally, as we are describing the dynamics of a macroscopic system, we do not assume to know
H
with infinite precision
[

. There is always an experimental fluctuation associated with the elements of the Hamiltonian. Thus the very description of the evolved state is not known, and one has to average over the Hamiltonian’s uncertainty. To consistently include this uncertainty, our results will not depend on details of
H
. The only assumption on the Hamiltonian, besides its structure as in (
1

H_{[+1,0]}

i - Measurement Probabilities: Equilibrium value.

Here, we understand the quantum measurement as an effective equilibration process. A given observable is said to be in equilibrium if its expectation value for almost all times does not differ substantially from its equilibrium value. The equilibration of closed complex quantum systems is a much debated subject these days
[

,
30

The key result that we are going to employ is the following one.
Given a time-independent Hamiltonian
H

{\varrho}_{\infty}^{H}
is defined as the time-averaged state
\langle{\varrho}_{t}^{H}\rangle_{\infty}=\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}dt{\varrho}_{t}^{H}

{\varrho}_{t}^{H}:=e^{-{\rm i}Ht}{\varrho}_{0}e^{{\rm i}Ht}
is the evolved state of the system, and

\langle|{\rm tr}(A{\varrho}_{t}^{H})-{\rm tr}(A{\varrho}^{H}_{\infty})|^{2}\rangle_{\infty}\leq\frac{\delimiter 69645069A\delimiter 86422285^{2}}{d^{H}_{\text{eff}}({\varrho}_{0})}.

(2)

In the expression above,

ii - Measurement dynamics: state equilibration.

Now we describe how the measurement dynamics actually takes place.
We are interested in the average equilibration of the total system description, i.e., we want to show that,
for most of the times,
{\varrho}_{t}^{H}
is basically indistinguishable from
{\varrho}_{\infty}^{H}
. Although we are not concerned with the equilibration of a single expectation value, as in the case of probabilities, we grant that measuring an exponentially large set of observables is not feasible for a macroscopic system.

Following
[
33
]
, assume that in trying to distinguish
{\varrho}_{t}
from
{\varrho}_{\infty}^{H}
one has access to a set
\mathcal{M}
of general measurement operators (positive operator valued measure, POVM). Given this set, the distinguishability between the states is defined as
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{M}}({\varrho}_{t}^{H},{\varrho}_{\infty}^{H}):=\max_{M\in\mathcal{M}}\sum_{r}|{\rm tr}(M_{r}{\varrho}_{t}^{H})-{\rm tr}(M_{r}{\varrho}_{\infty}^{H})|/2
, with the summation over the outcomes of a POVM
M\in\mathcal{M}
. As a corollary of bound (
2
), for large dimensions, we obtain (details in
[
39
]
):

\langle\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{M}}({\varrho}_{t}^{H},{\varrho}_{\infty}^{H})\rangle_{\infty}\leq\frac{\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{M})}{4\sqrt{d_{0}+d_{1}}},

(6)

where
\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{M})
is the total number of outcomes for all POVMs in
\mathcal{M}
.
In this way, if
\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{M})
does not grow faster than
\sqrt{d_{0}+d_{1}}

– which typically increases exponentially with the number of particles constituting the apparatus –, the states are effectively indistinguishable, i.e., the system is in equilibrium. Here, again, we see that accounting for resources that do not grow in a unphysical manner, leads to an effective equilibration.

Once more, as we do not assume full knowledge of the Hamiltonian
H
, there is no way to explicitly write
{\varrho}_{t}^{H}
or
{\varrho}_{\infty}^{H}

As we did for the probabilities earlier, now we establish that for macroscopic apparatuses
{\varrho}_{\infty}^{H}
is, for any choice of

\overline{{\varrho}_{\infty}}
– where again the average is performed over the Hamiltonians sampled uniformly from GUE. After
[

Various comments about this description are now in place. First, note that it is a mixture of definite outcomes, as denoted by the classically distinguishable states in the apparatus. This is the major advantage of our model when compared to the traditional decoherence approach. The first term of (

+1

-1

0

Given that the details of the Hamiltonian are not known (and largely not important), and that this is consistently taken into account in our dynamical description, this equilibrium state fully characterizes the measurement completion. Second, as this density matrix simply represents the mixture of classical possibilities, the probabilities can be directly read out from (
9

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\overline{{\varrho}_{\infty}}=|c_{+}|^{2}|+\rangle\!\langle+|\otimes\left(\frac{\mathds{1}_{+1}}{d_{1}}\oplus 0\oplus 0\right)+\\

Notice that when the information about an outcome is known, that does not specify the microscopic state of the apparatus. As such, for each outcome, the corresponding description of the apparatus is the microcanonical ensemble in the corresponding subspace.

Moreover, this effective stationarity is immediately connected to the irreversibility of the measurement process, viewed here as an equilibration process. After the measurement is performed, i.e., one of the classical outcomes actually happens, the corresponding post-measurement quasi-stationary states becomes the best description of the system. In other words, it is not possible to exactly reverse a unknown dynamics. Like in the classical case, as argued by Boltzmann in response to Loschmidt’s challenge

46

, the exact reversible of all microscopic degrees of freedom of a macroscopic system is, in principle, impossible.

Back to Wigner and his friend.
The main point is that if Wigner and his friend are indeed describing a measuring process, then both of them cannot fully account, nor even in principle, for all the apparatus’ degrees of freedom. Within such a perspective, using the collapse postulate or the unitary dynamics postulate, both will reach, for large apparatuses, the same description, namely the one given in Eq. (
10

Conclusions.

In the above, we embraced a description of the measurement process that adequately incorporates the finiteness of resources in its essence. Differently from the approach taken by the decoherence program
[
19
,
21
]
, our model acknowledges that the Hamiltonian describing the dynamics of macroscopic systems cannot be known with certainty, and that the measurement being performed are highly coarse-grained. In a macroscopic system, with
\mathcal{O}(10^{23})
degrees of freedom, a small energy uncertainty of
10^{-10^{22}}
Joules still contains an order of
10^{10^{23}}
energy levels
[
47
]
. Such mind-boggling density of energy levels cannot be dismissed by physical theories aiming to describe macroscopic systems.

As we showed, when the finiteness of resources is incorporated into the description of the measurement process, the universality of quantum mechanics may be regained, as it is possible to describe dynamics in all scales in the same way. In this viewpoint, the collapse postulate turns into a very practical effective description of a complex dynamics for which we do not have access to all its details.
Moreover, this perspective also helps clear up the discussions revolving around the Wigner Friend Scenario, with Wigner and his Friend now reaching the same portrayal. Our finite-resources model of a quantum measurement can be easily applied to other seemingly paradoxical situations, for instance, the extended Wigner’s Friend scenario
[
8
,
48
,
49
]
, and the ’Frauchiger and Renner’ scenario
[
50
]
.

I.1
Evaluation of the effective dimension

In the main-text we employed two results from the field of equilibration of closed quantum systems. The first one concerns the equilibration of an observable property, and it is explicitly written in Eq.(
2
). The second one concerns the equilibration of the state describing the system given a finite set of POVMs. It is a corollary of the bound for observables, and, as described in
[
33
]
, it reads in general as follows:

\langle\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{M}}({\varrho}_{t}^{H},{\varrho}_{\infty}^{H})\rangle_{\infty}\leq\frac{\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{M})}{4\sqrt{d^{H}_{\text{eff}}({\varrho}_{0})}}.

(11)

Both bounds depend on the effective dimension
d^{H}_{\text{eff}}({\varrho}_{0})=1/(\sum_{i}\langle E_{i}|{\varrho}_{0}|E_{i}\rangle^{2})
, with
\{\delimiter 69640972E_{i}\rangle\}
the eigenvectors of
H
.

In order to evaluate the effective dimension for our model, we consider the Hamiltonian
H_{*}
above, and the initial state
{\varrho}_{0}=\psi\otimes(0\oplus\mathds{1}\oplus 0)/d_{0}
. In this case:

\frac{1}{d^{H_{*}}_{\text{eff}}({\varrho}_{0})}=\sum_{m}\langle\phi^{+}_{m}\delimiter 86418188{\varrho}_{0}\delimiter 69640972\phi^{+}_{m}\rangle^{2}+\sum_{m}\langle\phi^{-}_{m}\delimiter 86418188{\varrho}_{0}\delimiter 69640972\phi^{-}_{m}\rangle^{2}.

(12)

Explicitly writing
\delimiter 69640972\phi^{+}_{m}\rangle=\delimiter 69640972+\rangle\otimes(\delimiter 69640972u_{m}^{+}\rangle\oplus\delimiter 69640972z_{m}^{+}\rangle\oplus 0)
, and
\delimiter 69640972\phi^{-}_{m}\rangle=\delimiter 69640972-\rangle\otimes(0\oplus\delimiter 69640972z_{m}^{-}\rangle\oplus\delimiter 69640972b_{m}^{-}\rangle)
, we get

\frac{1}{d^{H_{*}}_{\text{eff}}({\varrho}_{0})}=\frac{|c_{+}|^{4}}{d_{0}^{2}}\sum_{m=1}^{d_{0}+d_{1}}\langle z^{+}_{m}\delimiter 86418188z^{+}_{m}\rangle^{2}+\frac{|c_{-}|^{4}}{d_{0}^{2}}\sum_{m=1}^{d_{0}+d_{1}}\langle z^{-}_{m}\delimiter 86418188z^{-}_{m}\rangle^{2}.

(13)

Note that the vectors
\delimiter 69640972z_{m}^{+}\rangle
and
\delimiter 69640972z_{m}^{-}\rangle
are sub-normalized.

For large apparatus’ dimension, the eigenvectors of
H_{[+1,0]}
and
H_{[0,-1]}
are uniformly distributed. As such, fixing a basis for
\mathbb{C}^{d}
, the amplitude of each component of any eigenvector
\delimiter 69640972E_{m}^{+}\rangle\in\mathcal{H}_{[+1,0]}
will have, on average, absolute value approximately equal to
1/\sqrt{d_{0}+d_{1}}
. The same is true for all eigenvectors
\delimiter 69640972E_{n}^{-}\rangle\in\mathcal{H}_{[0,-1]}
. Within this assumption, we have
\langle z^{+}_{m}\delimiter 86418188z^{+}_{m}\rangle\approx\langle z^{-}_{m}\delimiter 86418188z^{-}_{m}\rangle\approx d_{0}/(d_{0}+d_{1})
. Therefore, for large

\frac{1}{d^{H_{*}}_{\text{eff}}({\varrho}_{0})}\approx\frac{|c_{+}|^{4}+|c_{-}|^{4}}{(d_{0}+d_{1})}\leq\frac{1}{d_{0}+d_{1}},

(14)

where for the last inequality we used that
|c_{+}|^{4}+|c_{-}|^{4}\leq 1
. With the above estimation for the effective dimension we reach results (
3
) and (
6
).

I.2
Equilibrium state: fixed Hamiltonian

For a given Hamiltonian
H
and initial state
{\varrho}_{0}
, the equilibrium state is given by the time-averaged state
{\varrho}_{\infty}^{H}=\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}dte^{-{\rm i}Ht}{\varrho}_{0}e^{{\rm i}Ht}
. If the Hamiltonian has no degenerate levels, nor degenerate gaps, such state can be equivalently obtained by the pinching map
[
32
]
, which simply corresponds to the diagonal elements of the initial state in the Hamiltonian basis.

Therefore, for our model, the equilibrium state for a fixed choice of
H_{*}
and
{\varrho}_{0}
is given by the corresponding pinching map:

{\varrho}_{\infty}^{H}=\sum_{m=1}^{d_{0}+d_{1}}\langle\phi_{m}^{+}\delimiter 86418188{\varrho}_{0}\delimiter 69640972\phi_{m}^{+}\rangle+\sum_{m=1}^{d_{0}+d_{1}}\langle\phi_{m}^{-}\delimiter 86418188{\varrho}_{0}\delimiter 69640972\phi_{m}^{-}\rangle.

Writing
P^{\pm}_{m}=|E_{m}^{\pm}\rangle\!\langle E_{m}^{\pm}|
for the rank-1 energy projector, and explicitly using
{\varrho}_{0}=\psi\otimes(0\oplus\mathds{1}\oplus 0)/d_{0}
, we get:

{\varrho}_{\infty}^{H}=|c_{+}|^{2}|+\rangle\!\langle+|\otimes\sum_{m=1}^{d_{0}+d_{1}}\left[P_{m}^{+}\left(0\oplus\frac{\mathds{1}}{d_{0}}\right)P_{m}^{+}\right]\oplus 0+\\
+|c_{-}|^{2}|-\rangle\!\langle-|\otimes\sum_{m=1}^{d_{0}+d_{1}}0\oplus\left[P_{m}^{-}\left(\frac{\mathds{1}}{d_{0}}\oplus 0\right)P_{m}^{-}\right].

(15)

This expression will be employed below.

Starting with state (

H_{[+1,0]}
and
H_{[0,-1]}
sampled from the GUE ensemble of the state
{\varrho}_{\infty}^{H}
, i.e., we must evaluate

\overline{{\varrho}_{\infty}}=|c_{+}|^{2}|+\rangle\!\langle+|\otimes\sum_{m=1}^{d_{0}+d_{1}}\overline{\left[P_{m}^{+}\left(0\oplus\frac{\mathds{1}}{d_{0}}\right)P_{m}^{+}\right]}\oplus 0+\\

The average over Hamiltonians can be recast as an average over unitary matrices by observing that the eigenvectors associated with different choices of

(16)

I.4
Concentration bound for the equilibrium probabilities: Chebyshev’s inequality

In order to show the concentration of the equilibrium probabilities around their average values we employed the well-known Chebyshev’s inequality:

Lemma 1
.

Let
X
be a random variable with mean
\mu
and non-zero variance
\sigma^{2}
. Then, for all
\delta>0
:

\pr\left(|X-\mu|\geq\delta\right)\leq\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\delta^{2}}.

The random variables of interest are then
\pr(i|H)_{\infty}
, for
i\in\{+1,0,-1\}
, where
H_{[+1,0]}
and
H_{[0,-1]}
are sampled uniformly from the GUE ensemble and form the total Hamiltonian
H
.

To evaluate the average values
\overline{\pr(i|H)_{\infty}}
, given the average typical state, Eq. (
9
), is immediate as
\overline{\pr(i|H)_{\infty}}={\rm tr}(\mathds{1}\otimes\Pi_{i}\overline{{\varrho}_{\infty}})
. This leads directly to the expressions in (
5
).

For the variances we need to evaluate the average of
\pr(i|H)_{\infty}^{2}
. For example, for the outcome
i=+1
such expression reads:

\begin{split}\pr(+1|H_{[+1,0]})_{\infty}^{2}=|c_{+}|^{4}\sum_{m,n=1}^{d_{0}+d_{1}}\langle m^{+}\delimiter 86418188U^{\dagger}(\Pi_{+1}\oplus 0)U\delimiter 69640972m^{+}\rangle\\
\times\langle m^{+}\delimiter 86418188U^{\dagger}(0\oplus\frac{\mathds{1}}{d_{0}})U\delimiter 69640972m^{+}\rangle\langle n^{+}\delimiter 86418188U^{\dagger}(\Pi_{+1}\oplus 0)U\delimiter 69640972n^{+}\rangle\\
\times\langle n^{+}\delimiter 86418188U^{\dagger}(0\oplus\frac{\mathds{1}}{d_{0}})U\delimiter 69640972n^{+}\rangle,\end{split}

where
\{\delimiter 69640972m^{+}\rangle\}
is an orthonormal basis for
\mathcal{H}_{+1}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{0}
, and
U
is the unitary transformation from such basis to the eigen-basis of a given
H_{[+1,0]}
. A similar expression holds for
\pr(-1|H_{[0,-1]})_{\infty}^{2}
.

The evaluation of the average of such expressions can be performed by standard techniques shown, for instance, in
[
42
,
40
]
. Nevertheless, due to the large number of terms, we employed the Random Tensor Network Integrator symbolic library
[
41
]
. With that we obtain:

\sigma_{\pm 1}^{2}=\frac{2|c_{\pm}|^{4}(d_{0}+1)d_{1}(d_{1}+1)}{d_{0}(d_{0}+d_{1})(d_{0}+d_{1}+1)^{2}(d_{0}+d_{1}+2)(d_{0}+d_{1}+3)}.

Given that
\sum_{i}\pr(i|H)_{\infty}=1
, we have that
\sigma_{0}^{2}\leq 2(\sigma_{+1}^{2}+\sigma_{-1}^{2})
, we get

\sigma_{0}^{2}\leq\frac{4(|c_{+1}|^{4}+|c_{-1}|^{4})(d_{0}+1)d_{1}(d_{1}+1)}{d_{0}(d_{0}+d_{1})(d_{0}+d_{1}+1)^{2}(d_{0}+d_{1}+2)(d_{0}+d_{1}+3)}.

Finally, using that
|c_{\pm}|^{4}\leq 1
and
|c_{+}|^{4}+|c_{-}|^{4}\leq 1
, we obtain a state-independent upper bound for the variances:

\begin{split}\sigma_{i}^{2}&\leq\frac{4(d_{0}+1)d_{1}(d_{1}+1)}{d_{0}(d_{0}+d_{1})(d_{0}+d_{1}+1)^{2}(d_{0}+d_{1}+2)(d_{0}+d_{1}+3)},\\
&=\frac{4(d_{0}+1)}{d_{0}d_{1}^{3}}+O\left(\frac{1}{d_{1}^{4}}\right),\end{split}

which leads to the result reported in (
4
).

I.5
Concentration bounds: Levy’s Lemma

The specific form of Levy’s lemma we used can be found, for instance, in
[
44
]
and reads:

Lemma 2
.

Let
f
be a
\eta
-Lipschitz function on
D
-dimensional unitary matrices, with mean over the Haar measure
\overline{f}
. Then,
\forall\delta\geq 0
:

\pr_{U\sim\mu_{\text{Haar}}}(|f(U)-\overline{f}|\geq\delta)\leq 4e^{-\frac{cD\delta^{2}}{\eta^{2}}},

(17)

where
c
is a positive constant, and the Lipschitz constant is defined as
\eta=\sup_{U,V}|f(U)-f(V)|/||U-V||_{2}
.

We want to apply Levy’s lemma to the function
||{\varrho}_{\infty}^{H}-\overline{{\varrho}_{\infty}}||_{2}^{2}
when sampling
H_{[+1,0]}
and
H_{[0,-1]}
from the GUE ensemble. To write such functions as requested in (
17
), we proceed in the same way as explained in the evaluation of the average typical state. It turns out that by exployting the expressions for
{\varrho}_{\infty}^{H}
and
\overline{{\varrho}_{\infty}}
, the only term in
||{\varrho}_{\infty}^{H}-\overline{{\varrho}_{\infty}}||_{2}^{2}={\rm tr}[({\varrho}_{\infty}^{H})^{2}]-2{\rm tr}({\varrho}_{\infty}^{H}\overline{{\varrho}_{\infty}})+{\rm tr}[(\overline{{\varrho}_{\infty}})^{2}]
that depends on the choice of Hamiltonians is
{\rm tr}[({\varrho}_{\infty}^{H})^{2}]
, with the other terms constant. Explicitly evaluating the non-constant term, we can write
{\rm tr}[({\varrho}_{\infty}^{H})^{2}]=f^{+}(U)+f^{-}(U^{\prime})
, where:

f^{+}(U)=|c_{+}|^{4}\sum_{m=1}^{d_{0}+d_{1}}\left[\langle E_{m}^{+}\delimiter 86418188U^{\dagger}\left(0\oplus\frac{\mathds{1}}{d_{0}}\right)U\delimiter 69640972E_{m}^{+}\rangle\right]^{2},\\
f^{-}(U^{\prime})=|c_{-}|^{4}\sum_{m=1}^{d_{0}+d_{1}}\left[\langle E_{m}^{-}\delimiter 86418188{U^{\prime}}^{\dagger}\left(\frac{\mathds{1}}{d_{0}}\oplus 0\right)U^{\prime}\delimiter 69640972E_{m}^{-}\rangle\right]^{2},

with
U
the unitary connecting the eigenvectors of different choices of
H_{[+1,0]}
, and, similarly,
U^{\prime}
connects the eigenvectors of
H_{[0,-1]}
. If we are able to apply Levy’s lemma for each of these functions individually, by the union bound we get an exponential concentration around the mean for
||{\varrho}_{\infty}^{H}-\overline{{\varrho}_{\infty}}||_{2}^{2}
.

Therefore, the only thing we have to show is that the functions
f^{\pm}
are
\eta
-Lipschitz, with appropriate Lipschitz constants. We then consider the Lipschitz continuity of the function
f(U)=\sum_{m=1}^{d_{0}+d_{1}}\left[\langle m\delimiter 86418188U^{\dagger}AU\delimiter 69640972m\rangle\right]^{2}
, with
A
a trace-one Hermitian operator, and
\{\delimiter 69640972m\rangle\}
an orthonormal basis for
\mathbb{C}^{d_{0}+d_{1}}
. Then:

\displaystyle|f(U)-f(V)|

\displaystyle=\Bigg|\sum_{m=1}^{d_{0}+d_{1}}\left[\langle m\delimiter 86418188U^{\dagger}AU\delimiter 69640972m\rangle\right]^{2}-\left[\langle m\delimiter 86418188V^{\dagger}AV\delimiter 69640972m\rangle\right]^{2}\Bigg|\leq\sum_{m=1}^{d_{0}+d_{1}}\Big|\left[\langle m\delimiter 86418188U^{\dagger}AU\delimiter 69640972m\rangle\right]^{2}-\left[\langle m\delimiter 86418188V^{\dagger}AV\delimiter 69640972m\rangle\right]^{2}\Big|;

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m=1}^{d_{0}+d_{1}}\Big|\langle m\delimiter 86418188U^{\dagger}AU\delimiter 69640972m\rangle(\langle m\delimiter 86418188U^{\dagger}AU\delimiter 69640972m\rangle-\langle m\delimiter 86418188V^{\dagger}AV\delimiter 69640972m\rangle)\Big|+\Big|\langle m\delimiter 86418188V^{\dagger}AV\delimiter 69640972m\rangle(\langle m\delimiter 86418188V^{\dagger}AV\delimiter 69640972m\rangle-\langle m\delimiter 86418188U^{\dagger}AU\delimiter 69640972m\rangle)\Big|;

\displaystyle\leq\left[\left(\sum_{m=1}^{d_{0}+d_{1}}\Big|\langle m\delimiter 86418188U^{\dagger}AU\delimiter 69640972m\rangle\Big|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\left(\sum_{m=1}^{d_{0}+d_{1}}\Big|\langle m\delimiter 86418188V^{\dagger}AV\delimiter 69640972m\rangle\Big|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right]\left(\sum_{m=1}^{d_{0}+d_{1}}\Big|\langle m\delimiter 86418188U^{\dagger}AU\delimiter 69640972m\rangle-\langle m\delimiter 86418188V^{\dagger}AV\delimiter 69640972m\rangle\Big|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}};

\displaystyle\leq 2||A||_{2}\left(\sum_{m=1}^{d_{0}+d_{1}}\Big|\langle m\delimiter 86418188U^{\dagger}AU\delimiter 69640972m\rangle-\langle m\delimiter 86418188V^{\dagger}AV\delimiter 69640972m\rangle\Big|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}};

\displaystyle\leq 2||A||_{2}^{2}\left(\sum_{m=1}^{d_{0}+d_{1}}||U|m\rangle\!\langle m|U^{\dagger}-V|m\rangle\!\langle m|V^{\dagger}||_{2}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}};

\displaystyle\leq 2||A||_{2}^{2}\left(\sum_{m=1}^{d_{0}+d_{1}}\left(||U|m\rangle\!\langle m|(U^{\dagger}-V^{\dagger})||_{2}-||(U-V)|m\rangle\!\langle m|V^{\dagger}||_{2}\right)^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}};

\displaystyle\leq 2||A||_{2}^{2}\left(\sum_{m=1}^{d_{0}+d_{1}}\left(||U|m\rangle\!\langle m|||_{2}+|||m\rangle\!\langle m|V^{\dagger}||_{2}\right)^{2}||U-V||^{2}_{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}};

\displaystyle=4\sqrt{d_{0}+d_{1}}||A||_{2}^{2}||U-V||_{2}.

In the above evaluation we made elementary use of triangle inequality, Cauchy-Schwartz inequality, Hölder’s matrix inequality, and also employed the definition of
||\cdot||_{2}
and its sub-additivity property. With all that, and using
||0\oplus\mathds{1}/d_{0}||_{2}=||\mathds{1}/d_{0}\oplus 0||_{2}=d_{0}^{-1/2}
we can established a Lipschitz constant for
{\rm tr}[({\varrho}_{\infty}^{H})^{2}]
equal to
2\sqrt{d_{0}+d_{1}}/d_{0}
. Finally, employing the union bound, we reach the desired result shown in (
8
).

For the bound on the distance we also must show that
\sigma
, in (
6
), vanishes for large apparatuses. This is done as follows. First we write
\sigma=\overline{{\rm tr}[({\varrho}_{\infty}^{H})^{2}]}-{\rm tr}[(\overline{{\varrho}_{\infty}})^{2}]
. The second term in this expression can be directly evaluated from (
9

{\rm tr}[(\overline{{\varrho}_{\infty}})^{2}]=(|c_{+}|^{4}+|c_{-}|^{4})\left[\left(\frac{d_{1}}{d_{0}+d_{1}+1}\right)^{2}\frac{1}{d_{1}}+\right.\\
\left.+\left(\frac{d_{0}+1}{d_{0}+d_{1}+1}\right)^{2}\frac{1}{d_{0}}\right].

(18)

For the first term of
\sigma
we write:

\overline{{\rm tr}[({\varrho}_{\infty}^{H})^{2}]}=\\
|c_{+}|^{4}{\rm tr}\left\{\sum_{m=1}^{d_{0}+d_{1}}\overline{\left[P_{m}^{+}\left(0\oplus\frac{\mathds{1}}{d_{0}}\right)P_{m}^{+}\right]}\left(0\oplus\frac{\mathds{1}}{d_{0}}\right)\right\}+\\
+|c_{-}|^{4}{\rm tr}\left\{\sum_{m=1}^{d_{0}+d_{1}}\overline{\left[P_{m}^{-}\left(\frac{\mathds{1}}{d_{0}}\oplus 0\right)P_{m}^{-}\right]}\left(\frac{\mathds{1}}{d_{0}}\oplus 0\right)\right\}.

Using standard results on Haar integrals
[
40
]
to perform the average, we get:

\overline{{\rm tr}[({\varrho}_{\infty}^{H})^{2}]}=\frac{|c_{+}|^{4}+|c_{-}|^{4}}{d_{0}+d_{1}+1}\left(\frac{1}{d_{0}}+1\right).

(19)

Performing the difference between the above expressions, and using that
|c_{+}|^{4}+|c_{-}|^{4}\leq 1
, we reach the result written in (
6
).
