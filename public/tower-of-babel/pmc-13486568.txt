Canon enables causal inference of downstream genes in single-cell CRISPR studies via instrumental variable analysis
Authors: Peijun Wu, Xiang Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceUnderstanding how genes regulate one another is fundamental to biology, yet existing methods for single-cell CRISPR (sc-CRISPR) screens rely on association-based analyses that cannot establish causality and are prone to high false positive rates. We present Canon, an instrumental variable (IV)-based causal inference framework specifically designed for sc-CRISPR studies, which uses the perturbation status of guide RNAs as IVs to identify causal gene–gene relationships with rigorous type I error control and high statistical power. Applied to real sc-CRISPR datasets, Canon uncovers candidate therapeutic targets with potential relevance for cancer biology and metagenes with distinct biological functions in human lung cancer, demonstrating the transformative potential of sc-CRISPR screening to resolve causal regulatory networks at an unprecedented scale.

Single-cell clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (sc-CRISPR) screening is a suite of innovative genomic technologies developed in recent years, enabling genetic perturbations at the single-cell level using CRISPR. Notable examples of sc-CRISPR technologies include Perturb-seq (1), CROP-seq (2), CRISP-seq (3), and many others (4, 5). These technologies vary in the types of perturbations and the specific single-cell platforms used in combination with CRISPR. Specifically, perturbations can target genes or noncoding regulatory elements (6–8), aiming for complete knockout, activation, or inhibition of the targets, with the ability to perturb either a single gene or multiple genes (9). The associated single-cell platforms include single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) (10), single-cell assays for transposase-accessible chromatin with sequencing (scATAC-seq) (11), cellular indexing of transcriptomes and epitopes by sequencing (CITE-seq) (12), and single-cell imaging (13). In a typical sc-CRISPR experiment, predesigned guide RNAs (gRNAs) are introduced into single cells via viral transduction. Due to variations in transduction efficiency, only a subset of single cells successfully receives gRNAs as determined by sequencing. Once inside the cells, these gRNAs integrate into the genome, inducing targeted genetic perturbations. The effects of these perturbations are then assessed through single-cell platforms, which measure gene expression levels or other omics features across individual cells.

In sc-CRISPR studies, a key analytical task is to identify downstream genes whose expression is influenced by perturbed target genes (7, 14). This can be approached in two distinct ways. The first is a direct approach, which tests the association between the target gene and each candidate downstream gene. This approach, however, is susceptible to false positive signals due to the presence of measured or unmeasured confounders common in omics studies. The second is an indirect approach, also known as differential expression (DE) analysis, that compares gene expression across perturbation groups that can be defined either at the individual-gRNA level or at the target-gene level by pooling cells carrying gRNAs targeting the same gene. The indirect approach encompasses three classes of DE methods: i) methods originally designed for scRNA-seq data and can be adapted for sc-CRISPR [e.g., MAST (15), Wilcoxon test (16), RUV-III-NB (17)]; ii) methods specifically designed for sc-CRISPR [e.g., SCEPTRE (18), MIMOSCA (1)]; and iii) causal DE methods that adjust for unmeasured confounders [e.g., cocoA-diff (19), causarray (20)]. These DE-based methods estimate associations between perturbation status and outcome-gene expression, which is biologically useful for characterizing perturbation-associated responses. However, they do not directly estimate the causal effect of target-gene expression on downstream-gene expression.

Here, we first present an alternative analytic framework for identifying downstream genes potentially causally influenced by perturbed target genes. Specifically, we propose to use the perturbation status of gRNAs in each single cell as instrumental variables (IVs) to infer the potential causal relationship between the gene targeted by CRISPR perturbation (treated as the exposure) and another gene measured by the single-cell platform (treated as the outcome) through IV analysis. IV analysis (21, 22) is a causal inference framework widely applied in observation studies (23) to investigate the causal effect of an exposure variable on an outcome variable. When genetic variants are used as instruments in genetic studies, IV analysis is commonly referred to as Mendelian randomization (MR) (24). Common IV methods include two-stage least squares (2SLS) (25), limited information maximum likelihood (LIML) (26) and Anderson-Rubin (AR) test (25). Similarly, common MR methods include inverse-variance weighting (IVW) (27), weighted median (28) and MR-Egger regression (29). IV analysis can be ideal for sc-CRISPR studies, where both the expression of a perturbed gene and that of a candidate downstream gene may be influenced by unmeasured confounding such as cellular states or cell cycle. Directly testing the association between the perturbed gene and each candidate downstream gene can yield biased causal effect estimates. However, if a guide RNA specifically perturbs the target gene, is independent of these unobserved cellular factors, and influences the downstream gene only through the perturbed gene, then it can serve as a valid IV. Leveraging such instruments, one can identify the causal effect of the perturbed gene on the downstream gene even in the presence of unmeasured confounding. As we demonstrate in this study, IV analysis indeed offers superior type I error control and is much more effective at detecting causal gene relationship compared to both direct and indirect approaches in sc-CRISPR studies. Notably, whereas DE-based methods aim to estimate associations between perturbation status and outcome-gene expression, our IV framework addresses a related but distinct inferential question: It uses gRNA perturbation status as IVs to estimate the causal effect of target-gene expression on the outcome gene.

Unfortunately, a naïve application of existing IV or MR methods to sc-CRISPR studies faces three major challenges. First, almost all existing IV methods are designed for two-sample settings, where the exposure and outcome variables are measured in separate samples. However, sc-CRISPR studies do not fit into this paradigm, as the exposure, outcome genes, and the instrumental variables (gRNAs) are measured within the same single cell, constituting a single sample and leading to potential correlation driven by the shared environment within cells. This shared cellular context introduces intrinsic correlations between gene expressions, which can confound the causal inference and violate the independence assumptions underlying standard IV analyses. Accordingly, sc-CRISPR studies are best modeled using one-sample IV methods rather than two-sample approaches. Second, many gRNAs exhibit weak effects on the exposure gene. When such weak instruments are used in IV analysis, they can lead to biased causal effect estimates as well as diminished statistical power. Third, gRNAs may exhibit off-target effects, influencing the expression of downstream genes through unintended pathways, rather than through the intended target genes. These effects are known as horizontal pleiotropic effects in IV analysis, which further violate the exclusion restriction assumption of IV methods and compromise their validity. Due to these limitations, we observe in this study that existing IV methods show inflated P-values and reduced statistical power, particularly as the correlation between the exposure and outcome genes increases. This issue becomes even more pronounced with smaller gRNA effect sizes, a larger number of gRNAs exhibiting nonzero effects on the exposure gene, or prominent off-target effects.

Therefore, building upon the IV framework, we further present Canon (CAsual relationship identificatioN using ONe sample IV model), a one-sample IV analysis method specifically designed to systematically identify genes potentially causally influenced by perturbed target genes across different sc-CRISPR platforms. Canon examines one gene pair at a time, employs a probabilistic model to automatically select valid IVs among gRNAs, relies on a scalable sampling-based algorithm for calibrated P-values computation, while explicitly accounting for the correlation between exposure and outcome genes inherent to the one-sample design and directly modeling the off-target effects of gRNAs on the outcome genes. As a result, Canon provides effective type I error control and high power across different sc-CRISPR platforms. We illustrate the benefits of Canon through comprehensive and realistic simulations. We also apply Canon to two sc-CRISPR datasets, successfully identifying putative downstream genes with potential therapeutic benefits for cancer treatment.

Results

Simulation.

Canon is described in Methods, with its technical details provided in SI Appendix, Supplementary Text and its method schematic shown in Fig. 1. We conducted extensive simulations to evaluate Canon and compared it with six existing methods across two sets of simulations, using normalized and count data, respectively. Full simulation details are provided in the SI Appendix, Supplementary Note. Briefly, we obtained gRNAs from the TP53 cancer dataset (7) and randomly selected a fraction to display nonzero effects on exposure (i.e., target) gene expression. Among these selected gRNAs, we further assigned a subset to have additional nonzero off-target effects on the outcome (i.e., downstream) gene expression. Additionally, we set the exposure gene to have either zero (null) or nonzero (alternative) effect on the outcome gene and simulated correlated residuals for the exposure and outcome genes. In the simulations, we varied the number of gRNAs displaying nonzero effects on the exposure gene and their effect sizes as measured by the proportion of variance in the exposure gene explained by the gRNAs, the number of gRNAs showing nonzero off-target effects on the outcome gene and their corresponding effect sizes as measured by the proportion of variance in the outcome gene explained by the gRNAs and the strength of expression correlation between the exposure and outcome genes. We explored a total of 24 null simulation scenarios and 28 alternative simulation scenarios across both sets of simulations. With the simulated data, we first compared multiple common IV methods, including 2SLS, random-effect version of inverse-variance weighting (IVW-R), and the weighted median (WM) approach, along with association-based methods including linear regression model (LM), negative binomial regression model (NB), and a standard set of DE methods including Wilcoxon and Wilcoxon_target. Subsequently, we evaluated the performance of Canon in comparison with these methods.

Overview of Canon. Canon is a one-sample IV analysis method specifically tailored to systematically identify genes potentially causally influenced by perturbed target genes in sc-CRISPR studies. Canon examines one gene–gene pair at a time and takes as input the perturbed gene expression vector x, the downstream gene expression vector y, and the guide RNAs (gRNAs) information matrix Z. It employs a probabilistic model to automatically select valid IVs among gRNAs, relies on a scalable sampling-based algorithm for calibrated P-values computation, while explicitly accounting for the correlation between gene–gene pair inherent to the one-sample design and directly modeling the off-target effects of gRNAs on the downstream genes. As a result, Canon provides valuable insights into the causal gene regulatory network and uncovers potential therapeutic targets for cancer treatment.

We first evaluated the ability of different methods in estimating the causal effects, focusing on two critical metrics: bias and precision (Details in the SI Appendix, Supplementary Note). Canon provides the most accurate causal effect estimation compared to other methods across both sets of simulations. Furthermore, IV methods demonstrate substantially better performance than common association-based approaches in estimating causal effects (Fig. 2A), with Canon obtaining unbiased causal effect estimate while outperforming others across different correlation values, number of gRNAs with nonzero effects, and proportion of variance in the exposure gene expression explained by the gRNAs (SI Appendix, Figs. S1–S3) as well as in the second set of simulations with count data (SI Appendix, Figs. S4–S6). The significant increasing bias observed in other methods, particularly under higher correlation scenarios, is likely due to their inability to account for the correlation. Specifically, the same cells were used to fit both the exposure-instrument model and the outcome-instrument model for these methods. Consequently, any unmeasured confounders that jointly influence both the exposure and the outcome induce correlation between the residual errors of the two models, leading to biased causal effect estimates and inflated type I error, particularly when the correlation between exposure and outcome residuals is high. Indeed, using a split-sample approach (Details in the SI Appendix, Supplementary Note), the estimation error in the instrument–exposure associations is independent of the error in the instrument–outcome associations, therefore two-sample IV methods yielded unbiased causal effect estimates (SI Appendix, Fig. S7).

Additionally, we noted that higher proportions of variance explained by the gRNAs (PVEβ = 5%) or fewer gRNAs with nonzero effects (K1 = 5%) helped mitigate the bias, suggesting that these three parameters might interact to influence bias. The latter two simulation parameters are also closely related to the weak instrument bias issue (Details in the SI Appendix, Supplementary Note). Across three proportion of variance (PVEβ = 1%, 2.5%, and 5%, corresponding to F-statistics = 2.2, 4.2, and 7.4), all methods produced unbiased estimates when correlation is zero (SI Appendix, Figs. S1–S3). However, when nonzero correlation is present, Canon outperforms the other methods by achieving lower bias in comparison to other methods, with greater bias reduction observed at lower PVEβ (1%), compared to higher PVEβ (5%) (SI Appendix, Figs. S1 and S3). The number of gRNAs with nonzero effects on the exposure gene expression also influences the estimation accuracy (SI Appendix, Figs. S1–S3). When many gRNAs have nonzero effects on the exposure, ad-hoc selection based on marginal linear models retains only a small subset of the strongest gRNAs, missing many valid instruments with moderate effects. In contrast, Canon assigns nonzero posterior inclusion probabilities to a broader set of valid gRNAs, resulting in more comprehensive instrument selection and more reliable causal inference in the sc-CRISPR context (SI Appendix, Figs. S8 and S9). Consequently, while Canon exhibited a bias of only 0.001, the biases for 2SLS, IVW-R, and WM were 0.032, 0.032, and 0.037, respectively, and LM showed a bias of 0.148 when the correlation is 0.15 (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). Additionally, under the extreme weak instrument setting (0.02% PVEβ), however, all IV methods including LIML showed substantial estimation bias that persisted regardless of sample sizes (SI Appendix, Fig. S10). Importantly, Canon achieved the lowest estimation bias across all simulation scenarios (SI Appendix, Fig. S10). In the simulation scenarios with off-target effects, Canon consistently produced unbiased causal effect estimate with average bias being –0.01 when PVEβ is 2.5% and PVEη is 0.5% across different numbers of gRNAs having off-target effects. However, 2SLS, IVW-R, WM, and LM yielded average bias of 0.025, 0.025, 0.033, and 0.148, respectively, across different numbers of gRNAs having off-target effects (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). In addition to producing unbiased estimates, Canon consistently yielded lower RMSE compared to the other methods across various scenarios (SI Appendix, Figs. S11–S13). Similar findings were observed in the second set of simulations with count data (SI Appendix, Figs. S4–S6 and S14–S16). Canon consistently demonstrated superior performance in both sets of simulations (SI Appendix, Figs. S1–S6 and S11–S16), underscoring its robustness across different scenarios, including the presence of weak instrument bias.

Next, we examined the performance of different methods in type I error control under null simulations. We found that Canon achieves well-calibrated type I error control, while other methods exhibit inflated type I error in the presence of expression correlation between the exposure and outcome genes, with such inflation substantially worsened in non-IV methods. Specially, in the first set of null simulations with normalized data (SI Appendix, Figs. S17–S20), Canon, 2SLS and IVW-R produced well-calibrated P-values under the baseline setting, with genomic control factor (GCF) being 0.93, 1.09, and 0.92, respectively (Fig. 2C). However, the type I error control by 2SLS and IVW-R deteriorated with increasing correlation between the exposure and outcome genes. For instance, when expression correlation is 0.3, 2SLS and IVW-R obtain GCF values of 1.52 and 1.3 while Canon consistently maintains well type I error control with a GCF of 0.95. WM, in contrast, produces slightly conservative type I error control (GCF = 0.92) in the baseline setting (Fig. 2C), but displays inflated type I error with high correlations (GCF = 1.56). Notably, a simple LM is only able to produce calibrated P-values when the correlation strength is zero (GCF = 0.98) and generates heavily inflated P-values as the correlation deviates from zero with an average GCF of 72.2 across different correlations. For both Wilcoxon and Wilcoxon_target, they demonstrated well-calibrated P-values across varying levels of correlation. Similar findings were observed in the second set of simulations with count data (SI Appendix, Figs. S21–S24). NB produces heavily inflated P-values across different correlations with GCF ranging from 1.4 to 72.2 (SI Appendix, Fig. S21) even after adjusting for size factors. This suggests that the similarity of size factors across the same cell population led to significant P-values, and incorporating the size factor through offset was insufficient to eliminate the effect. The distributions of null P-values from different methods remain largely consistent, regardless of the number of gRNAs affecting the exposure (SI Appendix, Figs. S19 and S23), their corresponding effect sizes (SI Appendix, Figs. S18 and S22) and the scenarios with off-target effects (SI Appendix, Figs. S20 and S24) in both sets of simulations. In summary, association-based methods exhibit significant miscalibration, leading to excess false-positive causal gene pair discoveries. In contrast, IV methods provide superior type I error control relative to non-IV approaches, making them advantageous for identifying genes downstream of exposure genes. However, IV methods tend to produce inflated P-values as correlation deviates from zero, a limitation that Canon effectively addresses by demonstrating substantially improved calibration across a range of scenarios.

In the alternative simulations, we examined the power of different methods in detecting nonzero causal effects. Because the same P-value from different methods may correspond to different type I errors, we measured power in the alternative simulations based on a fixed value of false discovery rate (FDR) to ensure fair comparison among methods. In the first set of alternative simulations with normalized data, we found that Canon is consistently more powerful than all the other methods in 12 out of 14 simulation settings (Fig. 3 and SI Appendix, Figs. S25–S29). Specifically, based on an FDR of 0.1, Canon demonstrates an average power of 45% across these 14 simulation settings while the power of 2SLS, IVW-R, WM, and LM is 28%, 26%, 29%, and 0.1%, respectively. Moreover, Canon’s power gain over the other methods increased with increasing correlation strength between exposure and outcome genes (SI Appendix, Fig. S25). For example, Canon achieved an average power gain of 25%, 34%, 51%, and 51% over 2SLS, IVW-R, WM, and LM at the baseline setting. This advantage increased to 26%, 29%, 27%, and 57% at a correlation of 0.5. In addition, although both IVW-R and WM achieved unbiasedness using the sample-splitting strategy, this strategy came at a substantial cost to statistical power: Both two-sample IV methods exhibited nearly zero power under the alternative baseline setting (SI Appendix, Fig. S7). Importantly, the automatic instrument selection procedure in Canon is effective in detecting the nonzero effect gRNAs even when the instrument effects are small. Specifically, when there are a small number of nonzero effect gRNAs with relatively large effects on exposure gene expression, all methods can identify these gRNAs through ad hoc selection procedure (SI Appendix, Fig. S8). Consequently, Canon performs comparably well to the other methods (Fig. 3 and SI Appendix, Fig. S27). However, when there is a large number of nonzero effect gRNAs with relatively small effects on exposure gene expression, ad hoc selection procedure used by the other methods becomes less effective than Canon in selecting the nonzero gRNAs (SI Appendix, Fig. S9). Consequently, Canon is more powerful than the other methods (Fig. 3 and SI Appendix, Fig. S27). Specifically, Canon achieved an average of 2%, 6%, 15%, and 53% power gain than 2SLS, IVW-R, WM, and LM when 30% of gRNAs had nonzero effects, while Canon achieved an average of 33%, 30%, 17%, and 40% power gain than the three methods when 70% of gRNAs had nonzero effects. WM maintained at a similar level of power, presumably due to the fact that 70% of gRNAs have nonzero effects while this method only requires more than 50% of the IVs being valid. Additionally, Canon consistently outperforms other methods across various gRNAs effect sizes (Fig. 3C) and this advantage increases with decreasing gRNAs effect sizes. In the simulation scenarios with off-target effects, Canon still obtained higher power compared to other methods (SI Appendix, Fig. S29). Specifically, when 10% of the gRNAs affecting the exposure gene also had nonzero effects on the outcome gene, Canon, 2SLS, IVW-R, WM, and LM obtained 37%, 0.6%, 0.6%, 22%, and 0% power with FDR of 0.1. Finally, the power of all methods increases with increasing causal effect size except for LM since it fails to distinguish false signals from the true signals under the baseline setting (Fig. 3). In the second set of alternative simulations with count data, Canon again demonstrated superior power in 12 out of 14 simulation settings (SI Appendix, Figs. S30–S34). Similar to the first set of simulations, Canon’s power gain over the other methods increased with greater correlation strength, smaller gRNAs effect sizes and a large number of nonzero effect gRNAs. Both Wilcoxon and Wilcoxon_target achieved zero power across the normalized and count-data simulations under FDR control, likely reflecting the perturbation-association tests had limited ability to distinguish true signals from null associations. Under the extreme weak instrument setting, Canon still outperformed other methods including LIML (SI Appendix, Fig. S35). Overall, as exposure-outcome gene correlation increases, IV methods begin to produce inflated P-values and exhibit reduced power, particularly when the gRNAs effect sizes decrease or the number of gRNAs having nonzero effects increases. However, our proposed method, Canon, successfully addresses these challenges and demonstrates markedly improved calibration and power relative to existing methods in the simulations across different scenarios.

Additional simulations showed that Canon correctly estimates the reverse effect as null, yields well-calibrated PIPs, produces consistent results across different versions and random seeds, and remains robust to reasonable variations in model priors. Moreover, batch effects can be mitigated by including gRNA expression PCs in the model, which also mitigates violations of the IV independence assumption, allowing Canon to achieve the lowest bias among the evaluated methods (SI Appendix, Figs. S36–S43) (details in SI Appendix, Supplementary Note).

KRAS cancer dataset.

We applied Canon to analyze two published datasets (details in Methods). The first dataset we analyzed was generated through 10X Genomics single-cell sequencing on A549 lung cancer cells. The dataset consists of expression measurements of 1,145 genes on 90,262 cells, along with the perturbation status of 98 gRNAs targeted for the exposure gene KRAS on the same set of cells. KRAS is a gene that encodes a protein called K-Ras, which is part of the RAS family of small GTPases. Additionally, KRAS is a well-known oncogene that affects various signaling pathways that contribute to tumor progression and metastasis (30), and when mutated, can lead to uncontrolled cell growth and division.

In this dataset, Canon identified 130 significant genes (SI Appendix, Fig. S44) that are potentially causally influenced by KRAS with Bonferroni correction (P-value threshold = 4.36×10-5). In contrast, 2SLS, IVW-R, WM, LM, NB, and Wilcoxon_target detected 280, 1, 12, 292, 744, and 321 genes, respectively (Fig. 4A). Canon captured a substantial fraction of genes detected by other IV methods, including 103 genes (37%) by 2SLS, 0 genes (0%) by IVW-R, 8 genes (67%) by WM, which are also identified by Canon (Fig. 4B), while only a small fraction of the genes identified by LM (45; 15%), NB (77; 10%), and Wilcoxon_target (31; 10%) are also identified by Canon. In addition, we compared causal effect estimates between the two versions of Canon on KRAS dataset. We observed a positive concordance between the two versions, particularly among significant genes defined by Bonferroni correction threshold (130 genes; Pearson correlation = 0.69), indicating that Canon’s causal effect estimates are not systematically inflated and that winner’s curse has limited practical impact on the results (SI Appendix, Fig. S45). We also found that causal effect estimates remained strongly concordant across different seeds (average correlation: 0.89) with −log10(P-values) showing an average correlation of 0.83 (SI Appendix, Fig. S46). Despite identifying a smaller number of genes, Canon demonstrated a higher likelihood of detecting true putative causal gene relationships compared to other methods. Specifically, we calculated the precision for each method in the power baseline simulations using the same P-value threshold from KRAS dataset (SI Appendix, Fig. S47). In this analysis, we found that Canon has the largest precision value of 0.989 under this threshold while other methods have lower precision values (2SLS: 0.935; IVW-R: 0.929; WM: 0.959; LM: 0.1; Wilcoxon_target: 0.167). This result suggest that Canon offers a more reliable identification of causal gene relationships relative to existing methods. Additionally, Canon uniquely detected 27 genes which have causal interpretation that were not detected by IV methods and these genes are enriched in growth factor activity (SI Appendix, Fig. S48), which is highly relevant to lung cancer. Specifically, aberrant activation of growth factor signaling plays a central role in promoting tumor cell proliferation, angiogenesis, and therapeutic resistance. A few example genes are listed at the end of this section. Moreover, we partitioned the genes with causal interpretation into two sets: Canon-only and other IV methods-only and performed overrepresentation analysis using Hallmark gene sets (Fig. 4C). Canon-only genes were related to WNT/β-catenin, NOTCH, and IL6-JAK-STAT3 pathways which are linked to KRAS signaling. On the other hand, other IV methods-only genes were related to hypoxia and xenobiotic/cholesterol metabolism, which indicate nonspecific stress responses. This pattern suggests that Canon prioritizes KRAS-relevant pathways over generic perturbation signatures. Overall, these results highlight the function of KRAS in modulating genes involved in the tumor progression process, emphasizing the significance of the genes identified by Canon in cancer development and metastasis.

We performed additional analysis to validate the genes identified by Canon. To do so, we obtained three sets of genes that are expected to be regulated by KRAS from Msigdb database (31), including genes in the KRAS signaling pathway, genes in the KRAS regulation pathway, and genes in the PI3K/AKT pathway (details in SI Appendix, Supplementary Note). Although these three gene sets may not encompass all genes targeted by KRAS, they represent the likely sets of biologically important genes to be regulated by KRAS. In this analysis, we used adjusted precision, defined as the observed precision (the proportion of true positives among identified genes) divided by the background rate, where the background rate is the proportion of genes in the gene sets overlapping with the tested candidate gene set relative to the total number of tested genes. Specifically, Canon had the largest adjusted precision (3.2, 5.6, and 2.378), respectively, across three gene sets (Fig. 4D). In addition, Canon achieved consistently better recall compared to other methods (Fig. 4E). More importantly, the genes identified by Canon were more likely to be in each of the three gene sets compared to the genes not identified, with odds ratio being 3.05, 4.57, and 1.93 for the three gene sets, respectively. The odds ratio achieved by Canon was also higher than those by the other methods (Fig. 4F). Specifically, 2SLS obtained odds ratios of 1.33, 2.63, and 1.39 across the three gene sets. IVW-R obtained odds ratios of 0 for all three gene sets because it identified only one gene, which was not contained in these sets. WM also produced odds ratios of 0, 0, and 0. LM showed odds ratios of 0.94, 0.65, and 1.13, consistent with simulation results where LM tends to detect numerous false signals. Although NB and Wilcoxon_target identified more genes, they were more likely to detect false signals, with odds ratios of 0.62, 0.44, and 1.41 for NB and 0.82, 0.97, and 1.14 for Wilcoxon_target. Additionally, Canon also detected more functional genes than other methods among the top rank genes. These observations were also consistent with simulations. Collectively, these results provide support to Canon’s superior detection power.

We categorized the downstream genes identified by Canon into three clusters based on the Silhouette method and performed GO enrichment analysis to characterize their functional roles (Fig. 4G). The first group is enriched for cell death and cytoplasmic translation. These pathways reflect apoptotic signaling and elevated protein synthesis activity frequently dysregulated in cancer. The second group is associated with cell division and spindle organization, suggesting involvement in mitotic progression and proliferative signaling. The third group is related to terms related to wound healing and smooth muscle cell migration, indicating potential roles in tissue remodeling and metastatic behavior. Together, these results highlight that Canon reveals distinct axes of tumor biology including apoptosis, proliferation, and cell motility that are critical for lung cancer progression.

We list a few gene examples having causal interpretation here. One of the genes uniquely identified by Canon is PLAU (Canon P value: 2.2×10-6; 2SLS: 0.048; IVW-R: 0.8; WM: 0.17). PLAU encodes for urokinase-type plasminogen activator (uPA), an enzyme involved in extracellular matrix degradation, which is a critical step in cancer cell invasion and metastasis. PLAU expression is known to be influenced by KRAS mutations (32), which can lead to uncontrolled cell growth and division and contribute to tumor progression and metastasis. The identification of this gene by Canon can be attributed to two key factors. First, among the 20 gRNAs selected through Canon’s automated instrument selection procedure, 16 (80%) were verified as effective gRNAs from the original study (7). In comparison, the univariate instrument selection method selected 35 gRNAs, of which only 23 (65%) were deemed effective (7). Second, in simulations where we counted the proportion of true causative gRNAs among the selected instruments (SI Appendix, Fig. S49), Canon achieved a 100% recovery rate, whereas the univariate approach reached only 89%. These results highlight the advantage of Canon’s automated instrument selection over conventional univariate approaches. The second example gene uniquely identified by Canon is KRT19 (Canon: 3.7×10-15; 2SLS: 0.006; IVW-R: 0.41; WM: 0.88). KRT19 is a cytoskeletal protein often used as a marker of epithelial cells and has been reported to be upregulated in KRAS-driven tumors. Studies have shown a link between mutant KRAS and increased KRT19 expression, particularly in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) (33). This upregulation may involve DNA methylation changes at the KRT19 locus and is observed to correlate with KRAS expression in human PDAC samples (33). KRT19 itself can modulate signaling pathways like Wnt/β-catenin, which are often downstream of KRAS activation (34). Another gene uniquely identified by Canon is DKK1 (Canon: 3.6×10-9; 2SLS: 0.005; IVW-R: 0.53; WM: 0.18). DKK1 is a secreted inhibitor of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway and is also upregulated in KRAS-mutant contexts. Similarly, elevated DKK1 levels have been reported in KRAS-mutated colorectal cancer, and evidence suggests a potential regulatory route between KRAS and DKK1 expression, impacting chemoresistance (35).

Importantly, our analysis revealed that high expression levels of PLAU, KRT19, and DKK1 are significantly associated with worse survival outcomes in lung cancer patients, as demonstrated using external TCGA data (Fig. 4H). The identification of these downstream genes highlights their potential as critical biomarkers for early diagnosis and prognostic evaluation in lung cancer. Details of additional orthogonal biological support for the candidate genes identified by Canon are provided in SI Appendix, Supplementary Note. Collectively, these unique findings underscore the advantage of Canon in uncovering putative causal relationships, which not only enhance our understanding of tumor biology but also open doors for therapeutic target discovery and precision medicine.

Additional analyses indicated minimal confounding issues in the KRAS data, with PC adjustment having negligible impact on the results and MCMC diagnostics showing stable and well-mixed posterior inference (SI Appendix, Figs. S50–S52) (details in SI Appendix, Supplementary Note).

TP53 cancer dataset.

The second dataset we analyzed was a TP53 lung cancer dataset collected through 10X Genomics single-cell sequencing on A549 lung cancer cells. The dataset consists of gene expression measurements for 1,224 genes across 85,778 cells, along with the perturbation status of 99 gRNAs targeted for the exposure gene TP53 on the same set of cells. TP53 gene, also known as Tumor Protein p53, is one of the most well-known tumor suppressor genes. The TP53 gene encodes the p53 protein, a transcription factor that plays a central role in maintaining genomic stability by regulating the cell cycle, promoting DNA repair, initiating apoptosis, and inducing senescence in response to DNA damage.

In these data, Canon identified 253 genes (SI Appendix, Fig. S53) potentially causally affected by TP53 with Bonferroni correction (P-value cutoff = 4.08×10-5). In contrast, 2SLS, IVW-R, WM, LM, NB, and Wilcoxon_target detected 158, 6, 49, 627, 854, and 437 genes, respectively (Fig. 5A). Almost all genes identified by the other IV methods, including 148 genes (94%) by 2SLS, 5 genes (83%) by IVW-R, 49 genes (100%) by WM, were also identified by Canon, while a large faction of the genes identified by LM (181; 29%), NB (216; 25%), and Wilcoxon_target (76; 17%)) were also identified by Canon (Fig. 5B). This uniquely enriched gene set suggests biological functions of TP53 that extend beyond its classical role in apoptosis and DNA damage response. The enrichment in detoxification and cellular oxidant detoxification further supports TP53’s established involvement in oxidative stress response (37). Additionally, the term intrinsic apoptotic signaling pathway in response to DNA damage aligns with TP53’s canonical role in tumor suppression. Interestingly, enrichment in mammary gland epithelial cell differentiation and positive regulation of mitotic sister chromatid separation suggests a broader involvement of TP53 in epithelial tissue regulation and mitotic control. HALLMARK_P53_PATHWAY, a direct target pathway of TP53, were only enriched in Canon-only genes. These results, uniquely captured by Canon, underscore both established and emerging roles of TP53 in cell fate determination and tissue homeostasis.

Discussion

We have presented Canon, a one-sample IV method designed for sc-CRISPR studies that automatically selects suitable instruments from gRNAs, accounts for off-target effects, and identifies downstream genes potentially causally influenced by the perturbed target genes in a likelihood framework. Canon is computationally efficient and scalable to datasets with hundreds of thousands of cells and thousands of genes. In addition, Canon yields calibrated P-values across a wide range of scenarios and improves power of IV analysis over existing approaches. We have illustrated the benefits of Canon through simulations and applications to multiple sc-CRISPR datasets. The genes uniquely detected by Canon facilitate our understanding of the molecular regulatory mechanisms underlying diseases.

While Canon is itself a one-sample IV method, it offers three key advantages over classical one-sample IV methods: i) Adaptive instrument selection with uncertainty quantification: many classical one-sample IV methods rely on naïve, fixed instrument selection, which, when paired with two-stage estimation procedure such as 2SLS and two-stage residual inclusion (2SRI), can introduce weak instrument bias. In contrast, Canon employs a spike-and-slab prior to select valid gRNA instruments within a Bayesian framework that explicitly accounts for uncertainty in the selection process. As demonstrated in our manuscript, this approach enables Canon to effectively identify valid and informative gRNAs while accounting for selection uncertainty, thereby substantially mitigating the weak instrument bias; ii) Explicit modeling of residual correlation: many classical one-sample IV implementations (e.g., 2SLS) do not explicitly model the residual covariance structure between exposure and outcome variable. Instead, they rely on orthogonality conditions for causal effect estimation, which can induce bias when the correlation between exposure and outcome is strong and instruments are weak. Canon addresses this limitation by explicitly modeling the residual correlation structure between exposure and outcome, thereby improving estimation accuracy under these practical but challenging conditions; iii) Relaxation of the exclusion restriction: Canon accounts for potential off-target effects of gRNAs on outcome genes, thereby relaxing the strict exclusion restriction assumption required by IV methods.

Given that Canon and standard DE analysis address related but distinct inferential questions, the choice between them should be guided by the scientific goal. First, Canon estimates the causal effect of target-gene expression on an outcome gene, whereas DE analysis estimates the association between perturbation status and outcome-gene expression. These two approaches differ in their estimands, although in an idealized setting their significance can align—specifically, when i) gRNAs have consistently strong and similar effects on the target gene and ii) the gRNAs have no off-target (horizontal pleiotropic) effects on the outcome gene that are not mediated through the target gene. Second, Canon is particularly advantageous when multiple gRNAs targeting the same gene have potential heterogeneous effects, because it leverages information across gRNAs while adaptively selecting informative and valid instruments. In contrast, DE analyses often aggregate cells across gRNAs targeting the same gene to obtain an overall perturbation-associated response; this implicitly assumes that gRNAs have similar on-target effects. When gRNA efficacies differ, when their effects vary on the target gene, or when some gRNAs have weak or no on-target effect, such aggregation can reduce power and complicate interpretation. Third, Canon is preferable when off-target effects are expected, because it explicitly models direct gRNA effects on the outcome gene, thus providing protection against gRNA effects that bypass the target gene. In contrast, DE analysis can produce spurious associations when off-target effects are present.

Canon is not without limitations. First, Canon models directly on the normalized gene expression data, which ensures its computational efficiency. Modeling normalized data, however, may not be ideal as the raw single-cell data are in the form of sparse counts with a prevalence of zero values and unique mean–variance relationship (40). Consequently, extending Canon to direct model counts, in the generalized linear modeling framework, may have added benefits. However, direct modeling of large-scale count data with more than hundreds of thousands of cells may be computationally challenging, as a negative-binomial model or an overdispersed Poisson model can lead to a failure of convergence in more than 90% of genes in large-scale single-cell data (41). Therefore, generalizing Canon for count modeling while balancing computational efficiency and numerical stability is an important future direction. Second, Canon directly adopts the commonly used perturbation detection method which assigns perturbation identities to cells by thresholding the gRNA UMI counts matrix (dichotomization) and then standardizes each gRNA column to zero mean and unit variance before model fitting. However, this approach may be subject to measurement errors (42), including sequencing depth and batch effects. Although our results indicate that dichotomization has limited practical impact on the results, directly modeling the perturbation identities as a latent variable may provide more beneficial results. However, modeling the perturbation identities as a latent variable introduces important statistical and computational challenges to IV analysis, necessitating careful exploration in future research. In addition, such measurement errors may induce violation of the IV independence assumption. Therefore, modeling perturbation identities as a latent variable could help mitigate this issue. Third, Canon and other IV methods could suffer from bias in high-dimensional settings (e.g., genome-wide sc-CRISPR screening studies), particularly when instruments are weak. Therefore, we recommend the following practical steps: 1) prescreen candidate instruments by testing exposure–instrument associations using a simple linear model, retaining only gRNAs with statistically significant associations for downstream analysis; 2) evaluate instrument strength using metrics such as the F-statistics to ensure that a sufficient number of informative instruments are included. Together, these steps help mitigate weak-instrument bias and improve the stability of IV estimation in high-dimensional screening settings. Fourth, Canon currently models direct/uncorrelated pleiotropy—where gRNAs have off-target effects on the outcome that are independent of their effects on the exposure—but does not explicitly account for correlated pleiotropy, where the magnitude of pleiotropic effects is systematically related to the strength of the instrument–exposure association (e.g., through shared biological pathways). Incorporating such structure would require an additional hierarchical component linking gRNA effects on the exposure to their pleiotropic effects and could represent an important direction for future methodological development.

Methods

Method Overview.

We consider a sc-CRISPR study in which a library of CRISPR guide RNAs (gRNAs) is introduced into a population of single cells to induce genetic perturbations on a predetermined set of genes, commonly known as the target genes. We designate these target genes as exposure genes in the present study. The perturbation status of each gRNA in each single cell is represented by the presence or absence of a unique barcode associated with the gRNA, which is measured through single-cell sequencing. The effects of these gRNA-induced genetic perturbations are assessed through single-cell platforms such as scRNA-seq, which measures gene expression levels across the transcriptome in individual cells. The single-cell platforms capture the expression levels of the exposure genes, potentially influenced by the gRNA-induced genetic perturbations, as well as a set of genes downstream of the exposure genes, which we will designate as outcome genes.

Our goal is to infer the causal effect of exposure genes on outcome genes using IV analysis. To do so, we examine pairs of exposure and outcome genes one at a time. For each gene pair, we denote x as an n-vector of gene expression levels for the exposure gene measured across n cells. We denote y as an n-vector of gene expression levels for the outcome gene measured in the same n cells. Both x and y denote library-size-normalized and log1p-transformed data. We then standardize x and y to have zero mean and unit SD. We further denote Z as a binary n×p perturbation status matrix for p gRNAs in n cells, where each element of Z takes on a value of 0 or 1, representing the perturbation status of the corresponding gRNA in each cell. We scale each column of Z to have zero mean and unit SD. Following (43), we consider a joint modeling framework:[1]x=Zβ+εx,[2]y=Zβα+Zη+εy,

where β is a p-vector of gRNA effect sizes on the exposure gene; η is another p-vector of gRNA off-target effect sizes on the outcome gene; α is a scalar that represents the potential causal effect of the exposure gene on the outcome gene; εx and εy are n-vectors of residual errors. Note that centering of both exposure and outcome gene expression allows us to safely ignore the intercepts in the above two equations. Since both x and y are measured in the same set of single cells, we utilize a bivariate normal distribution to model the potential covariance between the two residual errors in each cell[3]εxiεyi∼N0,Σ,

where εxi is the ith element of εx and εyi is the ith element of εy; Σ is a 2×2 covariance matrix capturing the residual error correlation. To allow for estimation of Σ, we further assume that it follows a conjugate inverse Wishart distribution Σ∼IVW(I2x2,1) a priori, with a 2×2 identity scale matrix and one degree of freedom.

The interpretation of causal relationships in traditional IV analysis relies on three primary assumptions (44): i) relevance assumption: the instruments are associated with the exposure variable; ii) exclusion restriction assumption: the instruments should only influence the outcome through the exposure variable; iii) independence assumption: the instruments are independent of confounding factors. The nature of sc-CRISPR studies likely ensures the validity of the independence assumption. Specifically, sc-CRISPR studies are often conducted on relatively homogeneous cell lines (45), ensuring randomness in the viral transduction step when introducing gRNAs to cells, thus satisfying the independence assumption iii). Additionally, gRNAs are typically designed to target the promoter, enhancer, or exonic regions of the exposure gene. Consequently, if the gRNAs affect the outcome gene, they are likely exerting these effects through the exposure gene and not through other pathways, providing a plausible justification for the exclusion restriction assumption ii). To explicitly model the violation of the exclusion restriction assumption ii), we incorporate η to account for off-target effects, thereby improving the validity of causal interpretation in settings where indirect effects may be present and minimizing the potential of false putative causal relationship detection. However, not all gRNAs may satisfy the relevance assumption i), as some gRNAs may effectively perturb the expression of the exposure gene, while others may exhibit no effect or only weak effects, especially in sc-CRISPR studies focusing on regulatory-element-targeting screens. For example, some sc-CRISPR studies introduce perturbations at a high multiplicity of infection (high-MOI), resulting in multiple perturbations per cell. In such cases, a substantial number of gRNAs may exhibit weak associations with the expression of the exposure gene. Several factors may contribute to this observation. First, when multiple gRNAs target the same gene within a single cell, they may generate varying levels of gene disruption rather than achieving a complete and uniform knockout. Second, the presence of multiple gRNAs increases the likelihood of off-target effects, potentially confounding the intended disruption. Finally, overlapping or competing binding requirements among different gRNAs in the same cell may interfere with their individual efficacy, further reducing the effectiveness of the perturbation. Therefore, some gRNAs may exhibit weak effects, making them unlikely to be the true causal agents influencing the exposure. It is important to select gRNAs that are strongly associated with the exposure gene to serve as valid instruments to ensure the reliability of IV analysis.

Here, we select a set of valid instruments from the entire set of candidate gRNAs by specifying a spike and slab prior (46) on each βj: βj∼πβN0,σβ2+1-πβδ0, where δ0 is the Dirac delta function representing a point mass at zero. That is, with probability πβ, the jth gRNA has a nonzero effect on the exposure gene expression levels, and its effect size follows a normal distribution with mean zero and variance σβ2; while with probability 1-πβ, the jth gRNA has zero effect on the exposure gene expression levels. By explicitly modeling and selecting nonzero β, we can select gRNAs which have a nonzero effect on exposure gene expression to serve as valid instruments, thereby satisfying the relevance assumption and enabling valid causal inference. In addition, the term Zη in Eq. 2 represents the off-target effects. Specifically, we model η only for the subset of gRNAs identified as valid instruments through β using a spike and slab prior, i.e., ηj∼πηN0,ση2+1-πηδ0 if βj≠0, which is recently used in TWAS methods. We make this assumption because valid gRNAs are more likely than nonselected ones to affect the expression of outcome genes through indirect pathways.

The key parameter of interest in the above model is the causal effect α, which represents the causal effect of the perturbed target-gene expression on the candidate downstream-gene expression. Since both the exposure-gene expression x and outcome-gene expression y are normalized and standardized before model fitting, α is estimated on a standardized expression scale. Thus, α is interpreted as the expected change, in SD units, in the downstream-gene expression for a one-standard-deviation increase in the perturbed target-gene expression. A positive α indicates that higher expression of the perturbed target gene causally increases the downstream-gene expression, whereas a negative α indicates that higher expression of the perturbed target gene causally decreases the downstream-gene expression. α should not be interpreted as a fold change or as a presence-versus-absence perturbation effect. Instead, its sign indicates the direction of the inferred target-gene-mediated effect, and its magnitude provides a standardized effect size comparable across candidate downstream genes within the same analysis. Our objective is to estimate α and test the null hypothesis H0:α=0. Following (47, 48), we develop an approximate inference algorithm under the maximum likelihood framework to obtain the test statistics and corresponding P-values for α. Algorithm details are provided in SI Appendix, Supplementary Note. By default, Canon runs 1,000 Gibbs sampling iterations and discards the first 200 iterations as burn-in. Since Canon analyzes each exposure–outcome gene pair independently, users can parallelize analyses across gene pairs. The algorithm is computationally efficient with complexity scales linearly with the number of cells n, or O(n).

Certainly, the causal interpretation of α in the above model is contingent upon the validity of the three IV assumptions, along with other explicit modeling assumptions made in the model, many of which can be challenging to validate in practice. Consequently, IVs analysis conducted in observational studies inevitably offer less compelling evidence of causality in comparison to randomized clinical trials. While we adhere to standard IVs analysis conventions and use the term “causal effect” throughout the paper, our intention is only to emphasize that the α estimate obtained in the instrumental analysis holds more credibility than the effect size estimate obtained from a conventional association-type regression analysis of the outcome on the exposure.

Additionally, motivated by sample-splitting approaches developed for MR analysis, including CFMR (49) and MR-SPLIT (50), we implemented a sample-splitting version of our method to provide formal protection against potential selection-induced bias. Our implementation follows their general split-sample principle, while adapting it to Canon’s Bayesian instrument-selection framework. In this approach, the data are split into two disjoint halves: The first half is used exclusively for IV selection, retaining only gRNAs with posterior inclusion probabilities (PIPs) > 0.5, and the second half is used exclusively for causal effect estimation using the selected instruments. This design ensures that the same data are never used for both IV selection and effect estimation.

We refer to our method as Canon (CAsual relationship identificatioN using ONe sample IV model), which is implemented as an R package, with efficient C/C++ codes linked through Rcpp. Note that an early version of the method was described as part of the first author’s PhD thesis (51). The present manuscript represents the first and only peer-reviewed publication of the method. The software, together with all analysis code for reproducing the results in the present study, are freely available at https://xiangzhou.github.io/software/.

Analyzed Datasets.

We applied Canon and other methods to examine two public sc-CRISPR datasets described below.

TP53 Cancer Dataset.

We obtained the TP53 lung cancer dataset collected on A562 cells from the Gene Expression Omnibus database, with accession number GSE161824 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE161824). The data consist of gene expression measurements for 1,224 genes across 85,778 cells, along with the perturbation status of 99 gRNAs targeted for the exposure gene TP53 on the same set of cells. These 99 gRNAs include frequently reported alleles and negative control alleles, some of which were anticipated to influence the target gene, TP53. We used the file “GSE161824_A549_TP53.processed.matrix.mtx.gz” provided by the authors to obtain the normalized gene expression data. For the analysis, we utilized the normalized gene expression data provided by the original study. Additionally, we subset the raw count matrix to include the same set of genes and cells as the normalized gene expression data to maintain consistency for NB method. Besides the gene expression matrix, we also obtained the file “GSE161824_A549_TP53.variants2cell.csv” to construct the gRNAs binary status matrix for cells in the expression matrix. Following the common procedure (7, 42), for each gRNA in turn, we assigned its binary gRNAs status to be one if its count in the cell is larger than zero and zero otherwise.

KRAS Cancer Dataset.

We followed the same procedure described in the above TP53 lung cancer dataset to obtain and process the KRAS cancer dataset. The data consist of expression measurements of 1,145 genes on 90,262 cells, along with the perturbation status of 98 gRNAs targeted for the exposure gene KRAS on the same set of cells. These 98 gRNAs encompass frequently reported alleles, negative control alleles, where some of these alleles are expected to influence the gene expression of the target gene KRAS. Specifically, we obtained the normalized gene expression matrix from Gene Expression Omnibus with accession number GSE161824 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE161824) provided by the original paper. We extracted the normalized gene expression matrix from the file “GSE161824_A549_KRAS.processed.matrix.mtx” and used the file “GSE161824_A549_KRAS.variants2cell.csv” to construct the gRNAs binary status matrix. The normalization process and the construction process of gRNA matrix are the same as the TP53 cancer dataset. Additionally, we subset the raw count matrix to include the same set of genes and cells as the normalized gene expression data to maintain consistency for NB method.
