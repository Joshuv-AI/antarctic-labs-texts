The status of cell and gene therapy education in medical schools—An international survey
Authors: Edina A. Wappler‐Guzzetta, Alexandra M. Tabacu, Elena Nedelcu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionCell and gene therapy (CGT) has emerged as a distinct medical field with more than 50 “Food and Drug Administration (FDA)”‐approved products treating conditions from leukemia to sickle cell disease. Despite this therapeutic revolution and the field's inherent complexity, the status of CGT education in medical schools remains unknown.Study Designs and MethodsThis study explores the current state of CGT education in medical school curricula internationally and evaluates educators' perspectives on training adequacy and curriculum development needs. An international survey of 504 medical schools across six continents was conducted between September 2023 and April 2024. The 20‐question poll assessed institutional characteristics, CGT educational requirements and structure, faculty resources, curriculum content, and educator perspectives. Descriptive statistics were calculated for all responses.ResultsForty‐six medical schools from 21 countries responded (9.1% response rate). Only 41% (19/46) offered CGT education, predominantly as optional courses (53%) during third and fourth years. Most programs relied on 2–4 faculty with competing responsibilities; only two institutions had dedicated CGT educators. Three schools reported standardized curricula, and none formally assessed student learning. Most educators (56%) considered current CGT education inadequate, and 78% advocated expansion. Nearly two‐thirds (64%) agreed to integrate professionally developed online CGT courses.ConclusionMedical education lags substantially behind the clinical adoption of CGT. The gap threatens future physicians' ability to offer optimal care. Strong educator consensus on inadequacy and interest in standardized resources signals an opportunity for intervention through collaborative curriculum initiatives.

INTRODUCTION

Medical education faces persistent challenges in keeping pace with therapeutic innovation. This gap is particularly apparent in cell and gene therapy (CGT), which has been described as a “fourth pillar of medicine” that complements established therapeutic modalities. 1 The evolution of this field has been remarkable (Figure 1). It began with hematopoietic progenitor cell (HPC) transplantation, first successfully performed in a syngeneic recipient in 1957 2 and an allogeneic recipient in 1968. 3 Over the subsequent decades, HPC transplantation has become the standard of care for numerous hematological and non‐hematological diseases, with more than 700,000 patients treated and 37,000 procedures performed worldwide annually. 4 Forty years after CARs were first described, the FDA approval of tisagenlecleucel (Kymriah) in 2017, the first chimeric antigen receptor T‐cell (CAR‐T) therapy for B‐cell precursor acute lymphoblastic leukemia, 5 , 6 marked the beginning of a new era. CRISPR‐based gene editing has further revolutionized the field, culminating in the 2023 FDA approval of exagamglogene autotemcel (Casgevy) for sickle cell disease. 7 These therapies now treat indications ranging from leukemias and lymphomas to immune deficiencies. 8 More than 50 CGT products have received regulatory approval, with hundreds of clinical centers now delivering these therapies.

The timeline of FDA‐approved cell and gene therapies and their categories: red, hematopoietic cell (HPC) therapy; blue, chimeric antigen receptor T‐cell (CAR‐T) therapy; purple, gene therapy (GT); green, gene‐modified HPC; black, cellular therapy (CT) and other (CT that cannot be categorized as specific).

This transformation has profound implications for medical education beyond specialty care. Primary care physicians encounter patients enrolled in CGT clinical trials, manage post‐transplant complications, and counsel patients about eligibility for novel therapies. Emergency physicians must recognize and evaluate acute CGT‐related toxicities. All physicians need foundational knowledge to navigate this rapidly evolving landscape, yet educational infrastructure has not kept pace with clinical adoption. The academic gap is particularly evident in transfusion medicine curricula, where CGT naturally intersects with established blood banking and cellular therapy content. Previous studies have documented significant deficiencies in transfusion medicine education at the medical student level, 9 with CGT among the least covered topics within this already under‐taught domain. 9 While more than 150 clinical centers worldwide now offer CAR‐T therapy using Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) facilities, 6 CGT's representation in medical school curricula remains largely unexplored. Despite hundreds of clinical centers worldwide offering these treatments, little is known about how medical schools prepare students for this therapeutic paradigm.

This study provides the first comprehensive international survey of CGT education in medical school curricula, addressing three questions: (1) What proportion of medical schools include CGT content? (2) How is this content structured, delivered, and assessed? (3) Do medical educators consider current training adequate, and what opportunities exist for curriculum development?

STUDY DESIGN AND METHODS

Survey design and distribution

A 20‐question survey was developed to assess global CGT education in medical school curricula. For this survey, CGT education was defined as structured instructional content covering HPC transplantation; cellular immunotherapies (e.g., CAR‐T, Natural Killer, and tumor‐infiltrating lymphocyte therapies); gene‐modified cellular products; in vivo gene therapy and gene‐editing technologies; and foundational principles of manufacturing, regulatory oversight, safety, conditioning regimens/chemotherapy, and post‐infusion follow‐up.

Following Kern's curriculum development framework, 10 which emphasizes problem identification and needs assessment, the survey instrument explored five domains: medical school organizational structure, CGT education requirements and structure, faculty resources, curriculum content, and educator perspectives on CGT teaching needs. The survey was created in Qualtrics (Provo, UT, USA). The Institutional Review Board at the University of California, San Francisco Medical Center approved the study protocol and survey instrument (IRB #23‐39847). The complete survey text appears in Supplement A.

Participant recruitment

Educational leaders at 504 medical schools across six continents were identified as potential respondents. Target participants included deans, associate deans for curriculum, academic officers, and faculty involved in CGT education. Contact information was obtained through two methods: (1) purchase of a validated medical education administrator database from Lakeb2b (Beaverton, OR, USA), and (2) direct identification via medical school websites. Survey invitations were distributed electronically from September 2023 to April 2024, with two reminder emails sent at 2‐week intervals. Participation was voluntary and anonymous.

Survey validation and statistical analysis

Content validity was established through expert review and evaluated across the five performance domains: medical school organizational structure, CGT education requirements and structure, faculty resources, curriculum content, and educator perspectives on CGT teaching needs. Pilot testing with three medical educators yielded minor wording changes to improve clarity. Descriptive statistics were calculated for all survey responses. Continuous variables are presented as means or medians, and categorical variables as frequencies and proportions. Denominators vary by question, as not all respondents answered every question. Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corporation, Version 16.39, 2020) was used for data management and descriptive analysis. Given the study's exploratory nature and descriptive focus, inferential testing was not performed.

RESULTS

Response rate and geographic distribution

Of 504 medical schools invited, 46 distinct school officials completed the survey (response rate 9.1%) across 21 countries spanning 6 continents: Africa, Asia, Australia, Europe, North America, and South America. The United States contributed the most responses (n = 19, 41%), followed by Hungary and Turkey (n = 3 each, 7%). Two schools from each of the United Kingdom, Germany, and India responded. Single responses were received from France, Spain, Italy, Greece, Cyprus, Nigeria, Canada, Mexico, Brazil, Australia, New Zealand, Sri Lanka, and South Korea.

Medical school characteristics

Most responding schools (22/46, 48%) offer 4‐year medical education following undergraduate college completion (US model), while 20/46 (43%) provide 6‐year programs following secondary school (European model). Four schools (9%) offer both models or alternative structures.

Availability of CGT education

Nineteen medical schools (41%) reported offering CGT education. These schools were distributed across 12 countries: Brazil, Canada, Cyprus, France, Germany, Hungary, India, Italy, Mexico, New Zealand, Spain, and 7 institutions in the United States. An approximately equal proportion (18/46, 39%) reported not offering CGT education, while 10 schools (22%) indicated uncertainty about whether CGT content was included in their curriculum.

Educational structure and timing

Among the 19 schools offering CGT education, content was predominantly optional (9/17, 53%), with only 5 schools (29%) requiring it as part of the core curriculum. Three schools (18%) did not specify whether content was required or elective. CGT education was mainly provided during the third and fourth years of medical school. Didactic lectures, seminars, and elective courses predominated. Problem‐based learning, clinical clerkships, online courses, and other formats (such as project‐based learning and research opportunities) were rarely used.

Faculty resources

Faculty involvement in CGT teaching varied across institutions. Nearly half of responding schools (8/17, 47%) reported having 2–4 faculty members engaged in CGT education, while 24% (4/17) reported having 6 or more. The remaining schools had either one faculty member or did not specify. Faculty teaching CGT came predominantly from two specialty areas: clinical pathology, transfusion medicine, and hematopathology (16/36, 44%), and adult or pediatric hematology/oncology (15/36, 42%). Other specialties—internal medicine, immunology, and basic science—were rarely involved. Most faculty engaged in CGT education (14/18, 78%) had concurrent clinical service, research, administrative, or other teaching responsibilities. Only two schools reported having faculty dedicated to CGT education.

Curriculum content and standardization

Only three medical schools worldwide reported having a standardized CGT curriculum: one in Canada and two in the United States. One program provided comprehensive curriculum details, including hematopoiesis and basic immunology; cell sources and collection; donor eligibility; cell culture and aseptic processing; cellular therapy product development; assay development and validation; quality systems in cellular therapy laboratories; HPC collection, processing, and transplantation; regulatory T cells; and FDA‐approved cell and gene therapies.

Assessment of student learning

None of the three medical schools with standardized CGT curricula reported formal assessment of student knowledge in this domain.

Perceived adequacy and need for expansion

Most survey participants (20/36, 56%) considered current CGT education inadequate even when offered at their institution. Additionally, 78% of respondents (28/36) believed CGT education should be expanded in medical school curricula. When asked about the appropriate duration for CGT education, most respondents (63%) suggested 2–4 weeks would be adequate. Some proposed that CGT should be reserved for specialty fellowship training when learners would be better positioned to appreciate its clinical complexity.

Specialty training opportunities

Most responding medical schools (26/40, 65%) do not offer advanced specialty training (residency or fellowship) in CGT.

Interest in standardized curriculum

Most respondents (27/42, 64%) indicated they would likely integrate a professionally developed online CGT course into their medical school curriculum if available. The degree of anticipated integration varied.

DISCUSSION

This first international survey reveals substantial gaps between CGT's clinical importance and its representation in medical education. While 41% of surveyed medical schools offer some CGT education, even institutions providing content consider it inadequate; 78% of educators agree that expansion is needed.

The educational gap and its implications

These findings align with previous studies documenting deficiencies in transfusion medicine education 9 but extend concerns to an emerging therapeutic domain with transformative potential. The disconnect between clinical adoption and educational preparation leaves future physicians unprepared to counsel patients, recognize complications, or participate in multidisciplinary care.

Several patterns highlight specific challenges. First, where CGT education exists, it's predominantly optional (53% of programs), suggesting institutional uncertainty about whether this content is essential for all medical students. Second, faculty resources are limited; most programs rely on 2–4 faculty with competing clinical, research, and administrative responsibilities. Only two institutions worldwide reported having dedicated CGT educators. Third, curriculum standardization is nearly absent: only three schools report structured CGT curricula, and none formally assess student learning outcomes.

Study limitations

Several limitations warrant acknowledgment. First, the 9.1% response rate limits generalizability and raises concerns about selection bias. Schools with existing CGT programs or particular interest may be overrepresented, suggesting that 41% of schools offering CGT education in this sample may overestimate global prevalence. Conversely, strong consensus among respondents on the inadequacy of current education (78% favoring expansion) suggests that even institutions engaged with this topic recognize substantial deficiencies. Second, geographic distribution was heavily weighted toward the United States and Europe, limiting insights from other regions. Third, barriers to implementing the CGT curriculum were not systematically assessed, limiting the ability to propose targeted solutions. Fourth, student perspectives on CGT education needs were not included, nor was actual student knowledge assessed. Fifth, the survey did not explore whether CGT content could be incorporated into other courses (immunology, hematology, genetics) beyond dedicated CGT teaching, potentially underestimating total exposure. Finally, whether proximity to CGT clinical programs influenced educational offerings was not assessed.

A model for implementation: The UCSF experience

At the University of California, San Francisco, a three‐credit elective for third‐ and fourth‐year medical students provides one framework for CGT medical education. The course addresses six core competencies: (1) identifying elements of HPC collection, processing, and storage; (2) describing established cell therapy products and their clinical indications; (3) defining basic principles of gene editing and emerging gene‐edited cellular therapies; (4) describing the manufacturing process, indications, toxicities, and mechanisms of action for tumor‐infiltrating lymphocytes, CAR‐T cells, regulatory T cells, and mesenchymal stromal cells; (5) defining fundamental regulatory aspects of cellular therapies; and (6) describing principles of current GMP (cGMP) and quality assurance for cellular therapy production. These objectives are achieved through didactic lectures, laboratory visits to observe CGT manufacturing processes, and student participation in focused projects. However, this curriculum is restricted to the 2‐week timeframe offered for this elective.

Barriers to implementation

While this survey documented the educational gap, potential obstacles to curriculum implementation include competing curricular priorities in already crowded medical school curricula, insufficient dedicated teaching time, a lack of faculty with CGT expertise, limited institutional access to CGT clinical programs for student observation, and uncertainty about appropriate learning objectives for medical students versus specialty trainees. The predominance of optional rather than required courses reflects legitimate questions about educational priorities: Should all medical students understand CGT mechanisms, or only those pursuing relevant specialties? Seventy‐eight percent of surveyed educators support the view that foundational CGT knowledge is increasingly necessary for general medical practice, comparable to a fundamental understanding of immunotherapy or molecular diagnostics. The most critical barrier is likely that the medical school curriculum operates as a zero‐sum game: adding new content necessitates removing or reducing existing material within fixed instructional time. CGT's rapid emergence and high impact exemplify this challenge. These transformative treatments demand curricular attention yet compete with established content at least as standalone modules. Various strategies could address this challenge, such as vertical or horizontal integration, case‐based replacement strategies, competency‐focused reduction, flipped classroom models, or just‐in‐time learning modules. Through such approaches, medical schools could better prepare graduates for a healthcare landscape increasingly defined by precision therapeutics.

Opportunities for intervention

Sixty‐four percent of the surveyed schools indicated they would likely integrate a professionally developed online CGT course, representing a significant opportunity. The development of high‐quality, standardized online educational resources could partially address the barriers posed by limited faculty expertise and time. However, online education alone is insufficient. Optimal CGT training requires practical exposure through laboratory visits, case‐based learning, and interaction with multidisciplinary CGT teams. Professional organizations in transfusion medicine, hematology, oncology, and pathology could advance CGT medical education through several actions: development of standardized learning objectives and competencies; creation of shared educational resources (online modules, case libraries, virtual laboratory tours); faculty development programs to train CGT educators; establishment of visiting student programs at institutions with robust CGT programs; and integration of CGT content into existing board examinations to signal importance.

CONCLUSIONS

CGT has emerged as a transformative treatment for malignancies and genetic diseases, yet medical education lags clinical adoption. This first international survey reveals that fewer than half of medical schools offer CGT content; even when present, 78% of educators consider it inadequate. There is also a strong interest in standardized educational resources (64%) and consensus on the need for expansion, signaling an opportunity for intervention.

The path forward requires multi‐stakeholder engagement: medical schools must prioritize CGT in curriculum design, professional organizations should develop standardized learning resources and faculty development programs, and accrediting bodies might consider CGT competencies in graduation standards. As CGT continues its rapid expansion across medical specialties, closing the education‐practice gap becomes essential to prepare physicians for contemporary medical practice. The question is no longer whether to integrate CGT into medical education, but how quickly this transformation can be accomplished to ensure all graduating physicians possess foundational knowledge in this critical therapeutic domain. These issues exist with graduate medical education as well, and are acknowledged by transfusion medicine and CGT educators. 11

Future research should investigate opportunities to build curriculum along with other CGT educators and clinical specialties and specific barriers to implementing the CGT curriculum. Other studies aim to assess students' attitudes toward CGT, evaluate the effectiveness of different educational approaches, or determine whether the optimal timing and depth of CGT education might be warranted. Multi‐institutional collaboration to develop and validate standardized learning objectives and assessment tools would also advance the field.
