Hybridizable Discontinuous Galerkin Methods for Coupled Poro-Viscoelastic and Thermo-Viscoelastic Systems
Salim Meddahi

Abstract
This article presents a unified mathematical framework for modeling coupled poro-viscoelastic and thermo-viscoelastic phenomena, formulated as a system of first-order in time partial differential equations. The model describes the evolution of solid velocity, elastic and viscous stress tensors, and additional fields related to either fluid pressure or temperature, depending on the physical context. We develop a hybridizable discontinuous Galerkin method for the numerical approximation of this coupled system, providing a high-order, stable discretization that efficiently handles the multiphysics nature of the problem. We establish stability analysis and derive optimal h ​ p hp -error estimates for the semi-discrete formulation. The theoretical convergence rates are validated through comprehensive numerical experiments, demonstrating the method’s accuracy and robustness across various test cases, including wave propagation in heterogeneous media with mixed viscoelastic properties.

1.  Introduction

Porous and thermally responsive viscoelastic media are commonly found in geomechanics, biomechanics, and various engineered systems. Since Biot
[
5
]
and Terzaghi
[
29
]
first quantified the interplay between fluid pressure and solid deformation, poroelasticity has become the cornerstone for modelling soil consolidation, reservoir compaction
[
26
,
16
]
, and geothermal operations
[
18
]
. Incorporating viscoelastic rheology is essential to reproduce secondary consolidation in clays
[
25
]
and stress-relaxation in biological tissues such as cartilage and brain matter
[
24
,
6
,
13
]
. In earthquake modelling and exploration geophysics, thermo-poro-viscoelastic effects govern wave attenuation and slow-mode diffusion
[
9
,
10
,
23
]
. These wide-ranging use cases motivate robust numerical tools capable of accurately capturing coupled poro/thermo-viscoelastic dynamics.

Numerical discretizations for coupled poro- and thermoelastic systems have undergone significant advancement over the past two decades. For dynamic problems, a diverse spectrum of computational approaches has been developed and refined. These include classical methods such as finite difference
[
17
]
, finite element
[
27
]
, boundary element
[
12
]
, finite volume
[
20
]
, and spectral techniques
[
23
]
.

Recent advancements have substantially expanded these computational methods. Among the most significant developments are stabilized mixed methods
[
19
]
and high-order space-time Galerkin formulations
[
3
]
. Advanced polytopal discontinuous Galerkin schemes with adaptive refinement capabilities provide effective solutions for problems involving complex geometries
[
1
,
2
,
7
,
8
]
. Similarly, hybridized discontinuous Galerkin (HDG) methods
[
15
,
22
]
support adaptive refinement while achieving optimal convergence with a reduced number of coupled degrees of freedom.

For thermo-viscoelasticity, Bonetti & Corti
[
7
]
developed a weighted symmetric interior-penalty DG scheme on general polytopal meshes that handles strong heterogeneities robustly. Their displacement/temperature formulation employs the Kelvin-Voigt viscoelasticity model, which combines elastic and viscous responses. However, this model cannot accurately reproduce finite-time stress-relaxation or creep-recovery phenomena essential in many applications. The more general Zener model proves more suitable for practical situations. However, displacement-based formulations of this model lead to hereditary convolution terms that render the problem non-local in time. To overcome this limitation, we adopt the stress-based formulation presented in
[
4
,
21
]
for the linear viscoelastic Zener’s model, which avoids the non-local terms while maintaining the model’s advantages.

The present work develops a high-order hybridizable discontinuous Galerkin method for a multi-field formulation that couples a Zener (standard-linear-solid) rheology with either Biot or Maxwell-Cattaneo transport laws. Following
[
21
]
, we adopt a stress-based formulation and rewrite the problem as a first-order system in time. The primary unknowns in this system are the solid velocity, the elastic and viscous stress tensors, along with either pressure and filtration velocity (in the Biot case) or temperature and heat flux vector field (in the Maxwell-Cattaneo case). The proposed HDG scheme is designed for simplicial meshes with hanging nodes, employing hybrid variables for the solid velocity and the gradient of the transport field—either filtration velocity or heat flux. This choice reduces the size of the globally coupled unknowns to the skeletal degrees of freedom and enables static condensation without parameter tuning. The resulting HDG method exhibits uniform energy stability, achieves optimal
hp
-error estimates, and naturally extends to heterogeneous media containing both purely elastic and viscoelastic subdomains—capabilities that, as far as the authors are aware, have not been reported for Zener-type poro/thermo-viscoelastic problems.

2.  The dynamic and linear poro/thermo-viscoelastic model problem

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded polygonal or polyhedral domain, where
d\in\{2,3\}
denotes the spatial dimension. Consider the following coupled system of partial differential equations on the time interval
(0,T]
, where
T>0
denotes the final time:

(1a)

\displaystyle\rho\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}-\operatorname{\mathbf{div}}\bigl(\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\alpha\psi I_{d}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{f}\quad\text{in }\Omega\times(0,T],

(1b)

\displaystyle\dot{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{E}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{C}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u})\quad\text{in }\Omega\times(0,T],

(1c)

\displaystyle\omega\,\dot{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{V}+\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{V}

\displaystyle=(\mathcal{D}-\mathcal{C})\,\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u})\quad\text{in }\Omega\times(0,T],

(1d)

\displaystyle\chi\,\dot{\boldsymbol{p}}+\beta\,\boldsymbol{p}+\boldsymbol{\nabla}\psi

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0}\quad\text{in }\Omega\times(0,T],

(1e)

\displaystyle s\dot{\psi}+\operatorname{div}\boldsymbol{p}+\alpha\operatorname{div}\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle=g\quad\text{in }\Omega\times(0,T].

Here,
I_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is the identity in the space of real matrices
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}):=\frac{1}{2}\left[\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{u}+(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{u})^{\mathtt{t}}\right]:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\text{sym}}
is the linearized strain tensor, where
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\text{sym}}\coloneq\{\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\mid\boldsymbol{\tau}=\boldsymbol{\tau}^{\mathtt{t}}\}
.

The coupled system (
1
) presents a unified mathematical framework that captures both poro-viscoelastic and thermo-viscoelastic phenomena through a stress-velocity formulation, following the approach of
[
4
,
21
]
. This stress-velocity formulation for the solid eliminates time-domain convolution terms, thereby offering advantages in both theoretical analysis and numerical implementation, see
[
21
]
for more details.

Equation (
1a
) represents the momentum balance for the solid phase. In this equation,
\boldsymbol{u}
denotes the velocity field, and the effective stress tensor
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
follows the Zener viscoelastic model, decomposed as
\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{E}+\omega\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{V}
, where
\omega>0
is the relaxation time. The term
\boldsymbol{f}:\Omega\times[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
accounts for external body forces. The density parameter
\rho
has different interpretations: in thermoelasticity, it represents the solid density
\rho_{S}
, while in poroelasticity, it is the composite density
\rho=\phi\rho_{F}+(1-\phi)\rho_{S}
, where
\phi
is the porosity, and
\rho_{F}
and
\rho_{S}
are the fluid and solid densities, respectively.

The constitutive laws governing the evolution of the elastic and viscous stress components are given by equations (
1b
) and (
1c
). These involve the symmetric and positive definite fourth-order tensors
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{D}
, with the condition that
\mathcal{D}-\mathcal{C}
is also positive definite to ensure energy dissipation. The coupling between the solid mechanics and the secondary field (fluid or thermal) is established through the term
\alpha\,\psi\,I_{d}
in the momentum equation, where
\psi
represents either the fluid pressure or the temperature.

The physical interpretation of equation (
1d
) varies with the context. In poroelasticity, it corresponds to Darcy’s law, describing the filtration velocity
\boldsymbol{p}
of the interstitial fluid, with
\beta=\eta/\kappa
representing the ratio of fluid viscosity to permeability, and
\chi=\rho_{F}/\phi
relating to the effective fluid mass. The coefficient
\alpha\in(0,1]
is the Biot-Willis coefficient, quantifying the coupling between the solid deformation and fluid pressure. Alternatively, in thermoelasticity, equation (
1d
) represents the Maxwell-Vernotte-Cattaneo law for heat conduction, where
\chi=\gamma/\theta
and
\beta=1/\theta
, with
\gamma>0
being the thermal relaxation time,
\theta
the effective thermal conductivity, and
\alpha>0
the thermal stress coefficient.

Finally, equation (
1e
) expresses a linearized conservation principle: mass conservation for the fluid in poroelasticity or energy conservation in thermoelasticity. The parameter
s
represents either the specific storage coefficient or the thermal dilatation effect, while
g
denotes the corresponding source term (fluid injection/extraction or heat generation).

To complete the problem formulation, we specify the initial conditions at time
t=0
:

(2)

\boldsymbol{u}(0)=\boldsymbol{u}^{0},\quad\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{E}(0)=\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{E}^{0},\quad\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{V}(0)=\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{V}^{0},\quad\boldsymbol{p}(0)=\boldsymbol{p}^{0},\quad\text{and}\quad\psi(0)=\psi^{0}\quad\text{in $\Omega$},

where
\boldsymbol{u}^{0},\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{E}^{0},\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{V}^{0},\boldsymbol{p}^{0},\psi^{0}
are given initial data defined on the domain
\Omega
.

Finally, we impose mixed Dirichlet and Neumann boundary conditions. Let
\partial\Omega=\Gamma^{u}_{D}\cup\Gamma^{u}_{N}
and
\partial\Omega=\Gamma^{\psi}_{D}\cup\Gamma^{\psi}_{N}
be two partitions of the boundary
\partial\Omega
into disjoint Dirichlet and Neumann parts, respectively, with the condition that the Dirichlet boundary sets
\Gamma^{u}_{D}
and
\Gamma^{\psi}_{D}
have positive measure. Denoting by
\boldsymbol{n}
the outward unit normal vector on
\partial\Omega
, the boundary conditions are given by:

(3)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0}\quad\text{on $\Gamma^{u}_{D}\times(0,T]$},

\displaystyle\quad(\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\alpha\psi I_{d})\boldsymbol{n}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0}\quad\text{on $\Gamma^{u}_{N}\times(0,T]$},

\displaystyle\psi

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{on $\Gamma^{\psi}_{D}\times(0,T]$},

\displaystyle\quad\boldsymbol{p}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{on $\Gamma^{\psi}_{N}\times(0,T]$}.

3.  The continuous problem

3.1.  Notation and functional spaces

For any
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
and
s\in\mathbb{R}
,
H^{s}(D,\mathbb{R}^{m\times n})
represents the usual Hilbertian Sobolev space of functions with domain
D
and values in
\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
. In the case
m=n=1
, we simply write
H^{s}(D)
. The norm of
H^{s}(D,\mathbb{R}^{m\times n})
is denoted by
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{s,D}
and the corresponding semi-norm is written as
|\cdot|_{s,D}
. We adopt the convention
H^{0}(D,\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}):=L^{2}(D,\mathbb{R}^{m\times n})
and let
(\cdot,\cdot)_{D}
be the inner product in
L^{2}(D,\mathbb{R}^{m\times n})
, i.e.,

(\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\tau})_{D}\coloneq\int_{D}\boldsymbol{\sigma}:\boldsymbol{\tau},\quad\forall\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\tau}\in L^{2}(D,\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}),

where the component-wise inner product of two matrices
\boldsymbol{\sigma}=(\sigma_{ij})
and
\boldsymbol{\tau}=(\tau_{ij})\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
is given by
\boldsymbol{\sigma}:\boldsymbol{\tau}\coloneq\sum_{i,j}\sigma_{ij}\tau_{ij}
.

To establish a suitable framework for formulating the first-order system in time (
1
), we define the pivot Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{H}_{1}\coloneq[L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})]^{2}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}\coloneq[L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\text{sym}})]^{2}\times L^{2}(\Omega)
. We equip
\mathcal{H}_{1}
with the inner product
((\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{p}),(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{q}))_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}\coloneq(\rho\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})_{\Omega}+(\chi\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})_{\Omega}
. Consequently, the corresponding norm is defined as
\lVert(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{q})\rVert^{2}_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}\coloneq((\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{q}),(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{q}))_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}
and it satisfies

(4)

\rho^{-}(\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\rVert^{2}_{0,\Omega}+\lVert\boldsymbol{q}\rVert_{0,\Omega}^{2})\leq\lVert(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{q})\rVert^{2}_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}\leq\rho^{+}(\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\rVert^{2}_{0,\Omega}+\lVert\boldsymbol{q}\rVert_{0,\Omega}^{2})\quad\forall(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{q})\in\mathcal{H}_{1},

with
\rho^{-}=\min\{\rho,\chi\}
and
\rho^{+}=\max\{\rho,\chi\}
.
Additionally, we define
\mathcal{A}\coloneq\mathcal{C}^{-1}
and
\mathcal{G}\coloneq(\mathcal{D}-\mathcal{C})^{-1}
, and equip
\mathcal{H}_{2}
with the inner product given by

((\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{E},\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{V},\psi),(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{E},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{V},\varphi))_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}\coloneq(\mathcal{A}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{E},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{E})_{\Omega}+(\omega\mathcal{G}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{V},\omega\boldsymbol{\tau}_{V})_{\Omega}+(s\psi,\varphi)_{\Omega},

for all
(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{E},\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{V},\psi),(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{E},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{V},\varphi)\in\mathcal{H}_{2}
.
Due to our assumptions on
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{D}
, there exist constants
a^{+}>a^{-}>0
such that the norm
\lVert(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{E},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{V},\varphi)\rVert^{2}_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}\coloneq(\mathcal{A}\boldsymbol{\tau}_{E},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{E})_{\Omega}+(\omega\mathcal{G}\boldsymbol{\tau}_{V},\omega\boldsymbol{\tau}_{V})_{\Omega}+(s\varphi,\varphi)_{\Omega}
satisfies

(5)

a^{-}\left(\lVert\boldsymbol{\tau}_{E}\rVert^{2}_{0,\Omega}+\lVert\boldsymbol{\tau}_{V}\rVert^{2}_{0,\Omega}+\lVert\varphi\rVert^{2}_{0,\Omega}\right)\leq\lVert(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{E},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{V},\varphi)\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}^{2}\leq a^{+}\left(\lVert\boldsymbol{\tau}_{E}\rVert^{2}_{0,\Omega}+\lVert\boldsymbol{\tau}_{V}\rVert^{2}_{0,\Omega}+\lVert\varphi\rVert^{2}_{0,\Omega}\right),

for all
(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{E},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{V},\varphi)\in\mathcal{H}_{2}
.

To enforce the essential boundary conditions (
3
) within the energy space for the solid velocity, we introduce the closed subspace of
H(\operatorname{div},\Omega)\coloneq\{\boldsymbol{v}\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d});\ \operatorname{div}\boldsymbol{v}\in L^{2}(\Omega)\}
given by

H_{N}(\operatorname{div},\Omega):=\{\boldsymbol{v}\in H(\operatorname{div},\Omega);\quad\langle\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{n},q\rangle_{\Gamma}=0\quad\forall q\in H^{1}_{D}(\Omega)\}.

It consists of vector fields in
H(\operatorname{div},\Omega)
with a free normal component on
\Gamma^{\psi}_{N}
. Here,
H^{1}_{D}(\Omega):=\{\varphi\in H^{1}(\Omega);\ \varphi|_{\Gamma^{\psi}_{D}}=0\}
, and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\Gamma}
represents the duality pairing between
H^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(\Gamma)
and
H^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(\Gamma)
. Similarly, we let
H^{1}_{D}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d}):=\{\boldsymbol{v}\in H^{1}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d});\ \boldsymbol{v}|_{\Gamma^{u}_{D}}=\mathbf{0}\}
and define

H_{N}(\operatorname{\mathbf{div}},\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\text{sym}}):=\{\boldsymbol{\tau}\in H(\operatorname{\mathbf{div}},\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\text{sym}});\quad\left\langle\boldsymbol{\tau}\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle_{\Gamma}=0\quad\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in H^{1}_{D}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})\},

as the closed subspace of
H(\operatorname{\mathbf{div}},\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\text{sym}})\coloneq\{\boldsymbol{\tau}\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\text{sym}});\ \operatorname{\mathbf{div}}\boldsymbol{\tau}\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})\}
satisfying a stress–free boundary condition on
\Gamma^{u}_{N}
.

3.2.  Abstract formulation

Collecting the unknowns in
\mathbf{y}(t):=(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{E},\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{V},\psi,\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{p})^{\mathtt{t}}
, system (
1
)-(
2
) rewrites compactly as

(6)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\dot{\mathbf{y}}(t)&=A\mathbf{y}(t)+\mathbf{F}(t)\quad\text{in $\mathcal{H}_{1}\times\mathcal{H}_{2}$},\\
\mathbf{y}(0)&=\mathbf{y}^{0}\end{split}

where
\mathbf{F}(t)\coloneq(\tfrac{1}{\rho}\boldsymbol{f},\mathbf{0},\mathbf{0},\mathbf{0},\tfrac{1}{s}g)
and
\mathbf{y}^{0}\coloneq(\boldsymbol{u}^{0},\boldsymbol{p}^{0},\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{E}^{0},\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{V}^{0},\psi^{0})^{\mathtt{t}}
.

The infinitesimal generator
A
of the strongly continuous semigroup on
\mathcal{H}_{1}\times\mathcal{H}_{2}
associated with the evolution system (
6
) is given by

\displaystyle A\left(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{E},\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{V},\psi\right)

\displaystyle\coloneq\Big(A_{1}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{E},\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{V},\psi)+(0,\tfrac{\beta}{\chi}\boldsymbol{p}),\ A_{2}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{p})+(0,\tfrac{1}{\omega}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{V},0)\Big)

\displaystyle A_{1}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{E},\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{V},\psi)

\displaystyle\coloneq\Big(-\tfrac{1}{\rho}\operatorname{\mathbf{div}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{E}+\omega\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{V}-\alpha\psi\mathrm{I}_{d}),\ \tfrac{1}{\chi}\nabla\psi\Big)

\displaystyle A_{2}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{p})

\displaystyle\coloneq\Big(-\mathcal{C}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}),\ -\tfrac{1}{\omega}(\mathcal{D}-\mathcal{C})\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}),\ \tfrac{1}{s}(\operatorname{div}\boldsymbol{p}+\alpha\operatorname{div}\boldsymbol{u})\Big).

The domain of the operator
A
is the subspace
\mathcal{X}_{1}\times\mathcal{X}_{2}\subset\mathcal{H}_{1}\times\mathcal{H}_{2}
, where

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{1}

\displaystyle\coloneq H_{D}^{1}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})\times H_{N}(\operatorname{div},\Omega)

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{2}

\displaystyle\coloneq\left\{(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{E},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{V},\varphi)\in[L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\text{sym}})]^{2}\times H_{D}^{1}(\Omega);\quad\boldsymbol{\tau}_{E}+\omega\boldsymbol{\tau}_{V}-\alpha\varphi I_{d}\in H_{N}(\operatorname{\mathbf{div}},\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\text{sym}})\right\}.

We endow
\mathcal{X}_{1}
and
\mathcal{X}_{2}
with the inner products

\displaystyle\left((\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{p}),(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{q})\right)_{\mathcal{X}_{1}}\coloneq\left((\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{p}),(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{q})\right)_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}+\left(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}),\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{v})\right)_{\Omega}+\left(\tfrac{1}{s}(\operatorname{div}\boldsymbol{p}+\alpha\operatorname{div}\boldsymbol{u}),\operatorname{div}\boldsymbol{q}+\alpha\operatorname{div}\boldsymbol{v}\right)_{\Omega},

\displaystyle\left((\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{E},\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{V},\psi),(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{E},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{V},\varphi)\right)_{\mathcal{X}_{2}}\coloneq\left((\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{E},\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{V},\psi),(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{E},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{V},\varphi)\right)_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\left(\tfrac{1}{\rho}\operatorname{\mathbf{div}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{E}+\omega\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{V}-\alpha\psi I_{d}),\operatorname{\mathbf{div}}(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{E}+\omega\boldsymbol{\tau}_{V}-\alpha\varphi I_{d})\right)_{\Omega}+\left(\tfrac{1}{\chi}\nabla\psi,\nabla\varphi\right)_{\Omega},

respectively. The completeness of the spaces
\mathcal{X}_{1}
and
\mathcal{X}_{2}
when equipped with the norms
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\mathcal{X}_{1}}
and
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\mathcal{X}_{2}}
, respectively, can be established following the approach of
[
22
, Section 3.2]
.

3.3.  Well-posedness analysis

For a separable Banach space
V
, we denote by
\mathcal{C}^{0}_{[0,T]}(V)
the Banach space of continuous functions
f:[0,T]\to V
. Moreover,
\mathcal{C}^{1}_{[0,T]}(V)
represents the subspace of
\mathcal{C}^{0}_{[0,T]}(V)
consisting of functions
f
with strong derivatives
\frac{df}{dt}
in
\mathcal{C}^{0}_{[0,T]}(V)
.

The Green formulas

(7)

(\boldsymbol{v},\nabla\varphi)_{\Omega}+(\operatorname{div}\boldsymbol{v},\varphi)_{\Omega}=0\quad\text{and}\quad(\boldsymbol{\tau},\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{w}))_{\Omega}+(\operatorname{\mathbf{div}}\boldsymbol{\tau},\boldsymbol{w})_{\Omega}=0,

hold for arbitrary pairs
(\boldsymbol{v},\varphi)\in H_{N}(\operatorname{div},\Omega)\times H^{1}_{D}(\Omega)
and
(\boldsymbol{\tau},\boldsymbol{w})\in H_{N}(\operatorname{\mathbf{div}},\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\text{sym}})\times H^{1}_{D}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})
. They permit us to obtain the identity

(8)

\left(A\left((\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{p}),(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{E},\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{V},\psi)\right),\left((\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{p}),(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{E},\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{V},\psi)\right)\right)_{\mathcal{H}_{1}\times\mathcal{H}_{2}}=(\mathcal{G}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{V},\omega\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{V})_{\Omega}+(\beta\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{p})_{\Omega}\geq 0,

which holds for all
(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{p})\in\mathcal{X}_{1}
and
(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{E},\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{V},\psi)\in\mathcal{X}_{2}
. This identity proves that the operator
A:\mathcal{X}_{1}\times\mathcal{X}_{2}\to\mathcal{H}_{1}\times\mathcal{H}_{2}
is monotone. To establish the well-posedness of the initial boundary value problem (
6
) via the Hille–Yosida theorem, we must prove the surjectivity of
I_{\mathcal{X}_{1}\times\mathcal{X}_{2}}+A:\mathcal{X}_{1}\times\mathcal{X}_{2}\to\mathcal{H}_{1}\times\mathcal{H}_{2}
, where
I_{\mathcal{X}_{1}\times\mathcal{X}_{2}}
denotes the identity operator in
\mathcal{X}_{1}\times\mathcal{X}_{2}
. The proof follows arguments similar to those in
[
22
, Lemma 1]
.

Theorem 1
.

For all
\mathbf{F}\in\mathcal{C}^{1}_{[0,T]}(\mathcal{H}_{1}\times\mathcal{H}_{2})
and

for a detailed proof.

We define the bounded bilinear form
B:\mathcal{X}_{1}\times\mathcal{X}_{2}\to\mathbb{R}
as

B((\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{q}),(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{E},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{V},\varphi))\coloneq(\operatorname{\mathbf{div}}(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{E}+\omega\boldsymbol{\tau}_{V}-\alpha\varphi I_{d}),\boldsymbol{v})_{\Omega}-(\nabla\varphi,\boldsymbol{q})_{\Omega}.

In the following sections, we will develop a finite element discretization method based on the following weak form of (
6
): Find
(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{p})\in\mathcal{C}_{[0,T]}^{1}(\mathcal{H}_{1})\cap\mathcal{C}^{0}_{[0,T]}(\mathcal{X}_{1})
and
(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{E},\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{V},\psi)\in\mathcal{C}^{1}_{[0,T]}(\mathcal{H}_{2})\cap\mathcal{C}^{0}_{[0,T]}(\mathcal{X}_{2})
satisfying

(10)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left((\dot{\boldsymbol{u}},\dot{\boldsymbol{p}}),(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{q})\right)_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}+\left(\beta\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q}\right)_{\Omega}-B((\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{q}),(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{E},\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{V},\psi))&=\left(\boldsymbol{f},\boldsymbol{v}\right)_{\Omega}\\
\left((\dot{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{E},\dot{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{V},\dot{\psi}),(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{E},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{V},\varphi)\right)_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}+\left(\mathcal{G}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{V},\omega\boldsymbol{\tau}_{V}\right)_{\Omega}+B((\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{p}),(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{E},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{V},\varphi))&=\left(g,\varphi\right)_{\Omega},\end{split}

for all
(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{q})\in\mathcal{X}_{1}
and all
(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{E},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{V},\varphi)\in\mathcal{X}_{2}
, and subject to the initial conditions given in (
6
).

4.  The semi-discrete problem

For the sake of simplicity, from now on we assume that
\Gamma^{u}_{D}=\Gamma^{\psi}_{D}=\Gamma
. As a result, the boundary conditions (
3
) become

(11)

\boldsymbol{u}=\mathbf{0}\quad\text{and}\quad\psi=0\quad\text{on $\Gamma\times(0,T]$}.

Let
\mathcal{T}_{h}
denote a shape-regular mesh of the domain
\bar{\Omega}
, consisting of tetrahedra and/or parallelepipeds in three dimensions (
d=3
), or triangles and/or quadrilaterals in two dimensions (
d=2
). The mesh
\mathcal{T}_{h}
may contain hanging nodes. For each element
K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
, we denote its diameter by
h_{K}
, and define the mesh size parameter
h:=\max_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\{h_{K}\}
.

We say that a closed subset
F\subset\overline{\Omega}
is an interior edge/face if it has positive
(d-1)
-dimensional measure and arises as the intersection of two distinct elements, that is,
F=\bar{K}\cap\bar{K}^{\prime}
for some
K,K^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
. Similarly, a closed subset
F\subset\overline{\Omega}
is termed a boundary edge/face if it constitutes both an edge/face of some element
K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
and part of the domain boundary, namely
F=\bar{K}\cap\partial\Omega
. We denote by
\mathcal{F}_{h}^{0}
and
\mathcal{F}_{h}^{\partial}
the collections of interior and boundary edges/faces respectively, and set
\mathcal{F}_{h}=\mathcal{F}_{h}^{0}\cup\mathcal{F}_{h}^{\partial}
.

For any edge/face
F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}
, we denote its diameter by
h_{F}
. The mesh
\mathcal{T}_{h}
is assumed to be locally quasi-uniform with constant
\gamma>0
, meaning that for all mesh sizes
h
and all elements
K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
:

(12)

h_{F}\leq h_{K}\leq\gamma h_{F}\quad\forall F\in\mathcal{F}(K),

where
\mathcal{F}(K)
denotes the set of edges/faces of element
K
. This local quasi-uniformity is invoked for the projection error estimate of Lemma
4
.

4.1.  Broken Sobolev spaces

For any non-negative real number
s\geq 0
, we define the broken Sobolev space with respect to the partition
\mathcal{T}_{h}
of
\bar{\Omega}
as

H^{s}(\mathcal{T}_{h},E):=\{\boldsymbol{v}\in L^{2}(\Omega,E):\quad\boldsymbol{v}|_{K}\in H^{s}(K,E)\quad\forall K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\},\quad\text{for $E\in\{\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\text{sym}}\}$}.

For consistency with earlier notation, we write
H^{0}(\mathcal{T}_{h},E)=L^{2}(\mathcal{T}_{h},E)
and simplify
H^{s}(\mathcal{T}_{h},\mathbb{R})
to
H^{s}(\mathcal{T}_{h})
. We introduce on
L^{2}(\mathcal{T}_{h},E)
, with
E\in\{\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\text{sym}}\}
, the inner product

(\psi,\varphi)_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}:=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}(\psi,\varphi)_{K}\quad\forall\psi,\varphi\in L^{2}(\mathcal{T}_{h},E),

and denote its induced norm by
\lVert\psi\rVert^{2}_{0,\mathcal{T}_{h}}:=(\psi,\psi)_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}
.

Next, we consider the set
\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}:=\{\partial K;\ K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\}
containing all element boundaries. On the space
L^{2}(\partial\mathcal{T}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{d})
of vector-valued functions that are square-integrable on each
\partial K\in\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}
, we define the inner product and its associated norm:

\langle\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}\rangle_{\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}:=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\langle\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}\rangle_{\partial K},\quad\text{and}\quad\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\rVert^{2}_{0,\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}:=\langle\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v}\rangle_{\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}\quad\forall\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}\in L^{2}(\partial\mathcal{T}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{d}),

where
\langle\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}\rangle_{\partial K}:=\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}(K)}\int_{F}\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}
.

Finally, we consider the space
L^{2}(\mathcal{F}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{d}):=\prod_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}}L^{2}(F,\mathbb{R}^{d})
, equipped with the inner product

(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})_{\mathcal{F}_{h}}:=\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}}\int_{F}\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\quad\forall\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{F}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{d}),

and its corresponding norm
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert^{2}_{0,\mathcal{F}_{h}}:=(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v})_{\mathcal{F}_{h}}
.

It is important to keep in mind that functions in
L^{2}(\partial\mathcal{T}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{d})
carry two distinct values on each interior face
F
(one from each adjacent element). In contrast, functions in
L^{2}(\mathcal{F}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{d})
take a unique value on each face
F
.

To formulate the HDG method, we need to introduce product spaces that accommodate both volume and trace variables. For
r>\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
, we define:

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}

\displaystyle\coloneq\left\{\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{q}}=(\boldsymbol{q},\hat{\boldsymbol{q}})\in[H(\operatorname{div},\mathcal{T}_{h})\cap H^{r}(\mathcal{T}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{d})]\times L^{2}(\mathcal{F}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{d})\right\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}

\displaystyle\coloneq\left\{\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{v}}=(\boldsymbol{v},\hat{\boldsymbol{v}})\in H^{1}(\mathcal{T}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{d})\times L^{2}(\mathcal{F}^{0}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{d})\right\},

where
L^{2}(\mathcal{F}^{0}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{d})
denotes the space of vector-valued functions in
L^{2}(\mathcal{F}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{d})
that vanish on boundary faces:

L^{2}(\mathcal{F}^{0}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{d})\coloneq\left\{\boldsymbol{\phi}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{F}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{d})\mid\boldsymbol{\phi}|_{F}=\mathbf{0},\ \forall F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}^{\partial}\right\}.

We define the jumps
{\llbracket{\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{q}}}\rrbracket}=\boldsymbol{q}-\hat{\boldsymbol{q}}
and
{\llbracket{\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{v}}}\rrbracket}=\boldsymbol{v}-\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}
, and equip the spaces
\mathcal{Q}
and
\mathcal{U}
with the semi-norms:

(13)

\left\lvert\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{q}}\right\rvert^{2}_{\mathcal{Q}}=\lVert\operatorname{div}\boldsymbol{q}\rVert^{2}_{0,\mathcal{T}_{h}}+\lVert\tfrac{k+1}{h_{\mathcal{F}}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{\llbracket{\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{q}}}\rrbracket}\rVert^{2}_{0,\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}\quad\text{and}\quad\left\lvert\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\rvert^{2}_{\mathcal{U}}=\lVert\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{v})\rVert^{2}_{0,\mathcal{T}_{h}}+\lVert\tfrac{k+1}{h_{\mathcal{F}}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{\llbracket{\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{v}}}\rrbracket}\rVert^{2}_{0,\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}},

where
h_{\mathcal{F}}\in\mathcal{P}_{0}(\mathcal{F}_{h})
is the piecewise constant function defined by
h_{\mathcal{F}}|_{F}=h_{F}
for all
F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}
.

4.2.  The HDG method

We denote by
\mathcal{P}_{m}(D)
the space of polynomials of degree at most
m\geq 0
on
D
if
D
is a triangle/tetrahedron, and the space of polynomials of degree at most
m
in each variable if
D
is a quadrilateral/parallelepiped. For vector and tensor-valued polynomials, we write
\mathcal{P}_{m}(D,E)
to denote the space of
E
-valued functions whose components belong to
\mathcal{P}_{m}(D)
, where
E
is either
\mathbb{R}^{d}
or
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\text{sym}}
. On the mesh
\mathcal{T}_{h}
, we define the space of piecewise-polynomial functions as

\mathcal{P}_{m}(\mathcal{T}_{h}):=\{v\in L^{2}(\mathcal{T}_{h}):\ v|_{K}\in\mathcal{P}_{m}(K),\ \forall K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\}.

Similarly, on the skeleton
\mathcal{F}_{h}
, we introduce

\mathcal{P}_{m}(\mathcal{F}_{h}):=\{\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{F}_{h}):\ \hat{\boldsymbol{v}}|_{F}\in\mathcal{P}_{m}(F),\ \forall F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}\}.

For vector and tensor-valued piecewise polynomials, we use the notation
\mathcal{P}_{m}(\mathcal{T}_{h},E)
to denote the subspace of
L^{2}(\mathcal{T}_{h},E)
whose components belong to
\mathcal{P}_{m}(\mathcal{T}_{h})
, where
E\in\{\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\text{sym}}\}
. Analogously,
\mathcal{P}_{m}(\mathcal{F}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{d})
represents the subspace of
L^{2}(\mathcal{F}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{d})
with components in
\mathcal{P}_{m}(\mathcal{F}_{h})
.

On element boundaries, we define

\mathcal{P}_{m}(\partial\mathcal{T}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{d}):=\left\{\boldsymbol{v}\in L^{2}(\partial\mathcal{T}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{d});\ \boldsymbol{v}|_{\partial K}\in\mathcal{P}_{m}(\partial K,\mathbb{R}^{d}),\ \forall K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\right\},

where
\mathcal{P}_{m}(\partial K,\mathbb{R}^{d})
is the product space
\prod_{F\in\mathcal{F}(K)}\mathcal{P}_{m}(F,\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

For
k\geq 0
, we introduce the finite-dimensional subspaces of
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}
given by

\mathcal{H}_{1,h}\coloneq\mathcal{P}_{k+1}(\mathcal{T}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\mathcal{P}_{k+1}(\mathcal{T}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{d})\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{H}_{2,h}\coloneq\mathcal{P}_{k}(\mathcal{T}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\text{sym}})\times\mathcal{P}_{k}(\mathcal{T}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\text{sym}})\times\mathcal{P}_{k}(\mathcal{T}_{h}),

respectively. We also define the discrete counterparts of spaces
\mathcal{Q}
and
\mathcal{U}
as:

\mathcal{Q}_{h}\coloneq\mathcal{P}_{k+1}(\mathcal{T}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\mathcal{P}_{k+1}(\mathcal{F}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{d})\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{U}_{h}\coloneq\mathcal{P}_{k+1}(\mathcal{T}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\mathcal{P}_{k+1}(\mathcal{F}^{0}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{d}),

where
\mathcal{P}_{k+1}(\mathcal{F}^{0}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{d})
consists of polynomial functions vanishing on boundary faces:

\mathcal{P}_{k+1}(\mathcal{F}^{0}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{d})\coloneq\{\boldsymbol{\phi}\in\mathcal{P}_{k+1}(\mathcal{F}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{d})\mid\boldsymbol{\phi}|_{F}=\mathbf{0},\ \forall F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}^{\partial}\}.

We propose the following HDG space-discretization method for problem (
10
): find
\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}=(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h})\in\mathcal{C}^{1}_{[0,T]}(\mathcal{U}_{h})
,
\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{p}}_{h}=(\boldsymbol{p}_{h},\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}_{h})\in\mathcal{C}^{1}_{[0,T]}(\mathcal{Q}_{h})
and
(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{E,h},\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{V,h},\psi_{h})\in\mathcal{C}^{1}_{[0,T]}(\mathcal{H}_{2,h})
satisfying

(14)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left((\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h},\dot{\boldsymbol{p}}_{h}),(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{q})\right)_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}&+((\dot{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{E,h},\dot{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{V,h},\dot{\psi}_{h}),(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{E},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{V},\varphi))_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}+(\mathcal{G}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{V,h},\omega\boldsymbol{\tau}_{V})_{\Omega}+(\beta\boldsymbol{p}_{h},\boldsymbol{q})_{\Omega}\\
&+B_{h}((\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{v}},\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{q}}),(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{E,h},\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{V,h},\psi_{h}))-B_{h}((\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h},\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{p}}_{h}),(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{E},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{V},\varphi))\\
&+\langle\tfrac{(k+1)^{2}}{h_{\mathcal{F}}}{\llbracket{\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}}\rrbracket},{\llbracket{\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{v}}}\rrbracket}\rangle_{\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}+\langle\tfrac{(k+1)^{2}}{h_{\mathcal{F}}}{\llbracket{\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{p}}_{h}}\rrbracket},{\llbracket{\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{q}}}\rrbracket}\rangle_{\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}=(\boldsymbol{f},\boldsymbol{v})_{\Omega}+(g,\varphi)_{\Omega},\end{split}

for all
\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{v}}=(\boldsymbol{v},\hat{\boldsymbol{v}})\in\mathcal{U}_{h}
,
\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{q}}=(\boldsymbol{q},\hat{\boldsymbol{q}})\in\mathcal{Q}_{h}
and
(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{E},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{V},\varphi)\in\mathcal{H}_{2,h}
, where the bilinear form

is given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}B_{h}((\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{v}},\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{q}}),(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{E},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{V},\varphi))&:=\left(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{E}+\omega\boldsymbol{\tau}_{V}-\alpha\varphi I_{d},\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{v})\right)_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}-\left(\varphi,\operatorname{div}\boldsymbol{q}\right)_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}\\
&\qquad-\left\langle(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{E}+\omega\boldsymbol{\tau}_{V}-\alpha\varphi I_{d})\boldsymbol{n},{\llbracket{\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{v}}}\rrbracket}\right\rangle_{\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}+\left\langle\varphi\boldsymbol{n},{\llbracket{\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{q}}}\rrbracket}\right\rangle_{\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}.\end{split}

Here,
\boldsymbol{n}\in\mathcal{P}_{0}(\partial\mathcal{T}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{d})
denotes the piecewise constant unit normal vector field on element boundaries, where
\boldsymbol{n}|_{\partial K}=\boldsymbol{n}_{K}
points outward from each element
K
.
We start up problem (
14
) with the initial conditions

(15)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(0)&=\Pi^{k+1}_{\mathcal{T}}\boldsymbol{u}^{0},\quad\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}(0)=\Pi^{k+1}_{\mathcal{F}}(\boldsymbol{u}^{0}|_{\partial\mathcal{F}_{h}}),\quad\boldsymbol{p}_{h}(0)=\Pi^{k+1}_{\mathcal{T}}\boldsymbol{p}^{0},\quad\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}_{h}(0)=\Pi^{k+1}_{\mathcal{F}}(\boldsymbol{p}^{0}|_{\partial\mathcal{F}_{h}}),\\
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{E,h}(0)&=\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}^{k}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{E}^{0},\quad\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{V,h}(0)=\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}^{k}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{V}^{0},\quad\text{and}\quad\psi_{h}(0)=\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}^{k}\psi^{0},\end{split}

where the projections
\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}^{k}
and
\Pi^{k+1}_{\mathcal{F}}
are defined in Appendix

5.  Convergence analysis of the HDG method

5.1.  Well-posedness and consistency

The following result proves that the algebraic-differential system (
14
) admits a unique solution.

Proposition 1
.

Problem (
14
)-(
15
) is well-posed.

Proof.

The system (
14

\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}
and
\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}_{h}
in terms of the volume variables by testing (
14
) with appropriately chosen test functions (specifically, setting volume test functions to zero and using arbitrary trace test functions
\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}\in\mathcal{P}_{k+1}(\mathcal{F}^{0}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\hat{\boldsymbol{q}}\in\mathcal{P}_{k+1}(\mathcal{F}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{d})
). This yields an algebraic system that uniquely determines the traces. Substituting these trace expressions back into (
14
) transforms the DAEs into a well-posed system of ordinary differential equations (ODEs) for the volume variables
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}
,
\boldsymbol{p}_{h}
,
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{E,h}
,
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{V,h}
, and
\psi_{h}
. We refer to
[
22
, Proposition 4]
for a similar detailed construction.
∎

Let us now establish the consistency of the HDG scheme (
14

10

Proposition 2
.

Let
(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{p})\in\mathcal{C}^{1}_{[0,T]}(\mathcal{H}_{1})\cap\mathcal{C}^{0}_{[0,T]}(\mathcal{X}_{1})

be the solution of (
6

and

(16)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\left((\dot{\boldsymbol{u}},\dot{\boldsymbol{p}}),(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},\boldsymbol{q}_{h})\right)_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}+((\dot{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{E},\dot{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{V},\dot{\psi}),(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{E,h},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{V,h},\varphi_{h}))_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}+(\mathcal{G}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{V},\omega\boldsymbol{\tau}_{V,h})_{\Omega}+(\beta\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q}_{h})_{\Omega}\\
&\qquad+B_{h}((\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h},\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{q}}_{h}),(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{E},\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{V},\psi))-B_{h}\bigg(\Big((\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u}|_{\mathcal{F}_{h}}),(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{p}|_{\mathcal{F}_{h}})\Big),(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{E,h},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{V,h},\varphi_{h})\bigg)\\

for all

,

and

across the interelements of

On the other hand, for all

for all

5.2.  Convergence analysis

The convergence analysis of the HDG method (
14
) follows standard procedures. Using the stability of the HDG method and the consistency result (
16
), we prove that the projected errors

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{e}_{u,h}(t)&:=\Pi^{k+1}_{\mathcal{T}}\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h},&\quad\boldsymbol{e}_{p,h}(t)&:=\Pi^{k+1}_{\mathcal{T}}\boldsymbol{p}-\boldsymbol{p}_{h},\\

\boldsymbol{e}_{\sigma_{\bigstar},h}(t)&:=\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}^{k}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\bigstar}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{{}_{\bigstar},h},\ \bigstar\in\{E,V\}&\quad e_{\psi,h}(t)&:=\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}^{k}\psi-\psi_{h},\end{aligned}

can be estimated in terms of the approximation errors

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{\chi}_{u}(t)&:=\boldsymbol{u}-\Pi^{k+1}_{\mathcal{T}}\boldsymbol{u},&\quad\boldsymbol{\chi}_{p}(t)&:=\boldsymbol{p}-\Pi^{k+1}_{\mathcal{T}}\boldsymbol{p},\\
\hat{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{u}(t)&:=\boldsymbol{u}|_{\mathcal{F}_{h}}-\Pi_{\mathcal{F}}^{k+1}(\boldsymbol{u}|_{\mathcal{F}_{h}}),&\quad\hat{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{p}(t)&:=\boldsymbol{p}|_{\mathcal{F}_{h}}-\Pi_{\mathcal{F}}^{k+1}(\boldsymbol{p}|_{\mathcal{F}_{h}}),\\
\boldsymbol{\chi}_{\sigma_{\bigstar}}(t)&:=\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\bigstar}-\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}^{k}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\bigstar},\ \bigstar\in\{E,V\}&\quad\chi_{\psi}(t)&:=\psi-\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}^{k}\psi.\end{aligned}

In the subsequent analysis, to concisely represent both the volume errors and their corresponding trace errors, we introduce the following notation:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{e}}_{u,h}(t)&:=(\boldsymbol{e}_{u,h},\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}_{u,h}),&\quad\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{e}}_{p,h}(t)&:=(\boldsymbol{e}_{p,h},\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}_{p,h}),\\
\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{u}(t)&:=(\boldsymbol{\chi}_{u},\hat{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{u}),&\quad\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{p}(t)&:=(\boldsymbol{\chi}_{p},\hat{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{p}).\end{aligned}

We begin our analysis by the following boundedness property for the bilinear form
B_{h}(\cdot,\cdot)
.

Proposition 3
.

There exists a constant
C>0
independent of
h
and
k
such that

(20)

|B_{h}((\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{v}},\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{q}}),(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{E,h},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{V,h},\varphi_{h}))|\leq C\lVert(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{E,h},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{V,h},\varphi_{h})\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}\lvert(\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{v}},\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{q}})\rvert_{\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{Q}},

for all
(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{E,h},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{V,h},\varphi_{h})\in\mathcal{H}_{2,h}
and
(\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{v}},\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{q}})\in\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{Q}
.

Proof.

Applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and (
5
), we deduce that

\displaystyle|B_{h}((\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{v}},\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{q}}),(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{E},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{V},\varphi))|

\displaystyle\lesssim(\lVert(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{E},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{V},\varphi)\rVert^{2}_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}+\lVert\tfrac{h_{\mathcal{F}}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{k+1}\boldsymbol{\tau}_{E}\rVert^{2}_{0,\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}+\lVert\tfrac{h_{\mathcal{F}}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{k+1}\omega\boldsymbol{\tau}_{V}\rVert^{2}_{0,\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}

(21)

\displaystyle\quad+\lVert\tfrac{h_{\mathcal{F}}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{k+1}\varphi\rVert^{2}_{0,\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}})^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\lvert(\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{v}},\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{q}})\rvert_{\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{Q}},

for all
(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{E},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{V},\varphi)\in\mathcal{H}_{2}
such that
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{E},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{V}\in H^{s}(\mathcal{T}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\text{sym}})
and
\varphi\in H^{s}(\mathcal{T}_{h})
with
s\geq\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
, and for all
(\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{v}},\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{q}})\in\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{Q}
. The result follows by applying the discrete trace inequality (
34
).
∎

We are in a position to establish the following key stability estimate.

Lemma 1
.

Under the conditions of Proposition
2
, there exists a constant
C>0
independent of
h
and
k
such that

(22)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\max_{[0,T]}&\lVert(\boldsymbol{e}_{u,h},\boldsymbol{e}_{p,h})\rVert^{2}_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}+\max_{[0,T]}\lVert(\boldsymbol{e}_{\sigma_{E},h},\boldsymbol{e}_{\sigma_{V},h},e_{\psi,h})\rVert^{2}_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}\\
&\leq C\int_{0}^{T}\Big(\lVert\tfrac{h_{\mathcal{F}}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{k+1}\boldsymbol{\chi}_{\sigma_{E}}\rVert^{2}_{0,\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}+\lVert\tfrac{h_{\mathcal{F}}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{k+1}\boldsymbol{\chi}_{\sigma_{V}}\rVert^{2}_{0,\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}+\lVert\tfrac{h_{\mathcal{F}}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{k+1}\chi_{\psi}\rVert^{2}_{0,\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}+\lvert(\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{u},\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{p})\rvert^{2}_{\mathcal{U}\times\hat{\mathcal{U}}}\Big)\,\text{d}t.\end{split}

Proof.

By virtue of the orthogonality properties

\displaystyle\left((\dot{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{u},\dot{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{p}),(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{q})\right)_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}+(\beta\boldsymbol{\chi}_{p},\boldsymbol{q})_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=0\quad\forall(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{q})\in\mathcal{H}_{1,h},

\displaystyle((\dot{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{\sigma_{E}},\dot{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{\sigma_{V}},\dot{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{\psi}),(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{E},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{V},\varphi))_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}+(\mathcal{G}\boldsymbol{\chi}_{\sigma_{V}},\omega\boldsymbol{\tau}_{V})_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=0\quad\forall(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{E},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{V},\varphi)\in\mathcal{H}_{2,h},

and the consistency result (
16
), we can establish that for all
(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{E},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{V},\varphi)\in\mathcal{H}_{2,h}
and
(\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{v}},\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{q}})\in\mathcal{U}_{h}\times\mathcal{Q}_{h}
:

(23)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\left((\dot{\boldsymbol{e}}_{u,h},\dot{\boldsymbol{e}}_{p,h}),(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{q})\right)_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}+((\dot{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\sigma_{E},h},\dot{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\sigma_{V},h},\dot{e}_{\psi,h}),(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{E},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{V},\varphi))_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}+(\beta\boldsymbol{e}_{p,h},\boldsymbol{q})_{\Omega}+(\mathcal{G}\boldsymbol{e}_{\sigma_{V},h},\omega\boldsymbol{\tau}_{V})_{\Omega}\\
&\qquad+B_{h}((\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{v}},\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{q}}),(\boldsymbol{e}_{\sigma_{E},h},\boldsymbol{e}_{\sigma_{V},h},e_{\psi,h}))-B_{h}((\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{e}}_{u,h},\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{e}}_{p,h}),(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{E},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{V},\varphi))\\
&\qquad+\langle\tfrac{(k+1)^{2}}{h_{\mathcal{F}}}{\llbracket{\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{e}}_{u,h}}\rrbracket},{\llbracket{\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{v}}}\rrbracket}\rangle_{\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}+\langle\tfrac{(k+1)^{2}}{h_{\mathcal{F}}}{\llbracket{\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{e}}_{p,h}}\rrbracket},{\llbracket{\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{q}}}\rrbracket}\rangle_{\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}\\
&=\langle(\boldsymbol{\chi}_{\sigma_{E}}+\omega\boldsymbol{\chi}_{\sigma_{V}}-\alpha\chi_{\psi}I_{d})\boldsymbol{n},{\llbracket{\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{v}}}\rrbracket}\rangle_{\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}-\langle\chi_{\psi}\boldsymbol{n},{\llbracket{\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{q}}}\rrbracket}\rangle_{\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}\\
&\qquad+B_{h}((\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{u},\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{p}),(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{E},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{V},\varphi))-\langle\tfrac{(k+1)^{2}}{h_{\mathcal{F}}}{\llbracket{\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{u}}\rrbracket},{\llbracket{\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{v}}}\rrbracket}\rangle_{\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}-\langle\tfrac{(k+1)^{2}}{h_{\mathcal{F}}}{\llbracket{\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{p}}\rrbracket},{\llbracket{\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{q}}}\rrbracket}\rangle_{\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}.\end{split}

This identity follows from the fact that for all
(\underline{\boldsymbol{v}},\hat{\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}})\in\mathcal{H}_{1,h}\times\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{1,h}
:

B_{h}((\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{v}},\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{q}}),(\boldsymbol{\chi}_{\sigma_{E}},\boldsymbol{\chi}_{\sigma_{V}},\chi_{\psi}))=-\langle(\boldsymbol{\chi}_{\sigma_{E}}+\omega\boldsymbol{\chi}_{\sigma_{V}}-\alpha\chi_{\psi}I_{d})\boldsymbol{n},{\llbracket{\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{v}}}\rrbracket}\rangle_{\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}+\langle\chi_{\psi}\boldsymbol{n},{\llbracket{\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{q}}}\rrbracket}\rangle_{\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}},

which is supported by the polynomial space inclusions:

\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\mathcal{P}_{k+1}(\mathcal{T}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{d}))\subset\mathcal{P}_{k}(\mathcal{T}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\text{sym}})\quad\text{and}\quad\operatorname{div}(\mathcal{P}_{k+1}(\mathcal{T}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{d}))\subset\mathcal{P}_{k}(\mathcal{T}_{h}).

Choosing
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{E}=\boldsymbol{e}_{\sigma_{E},h}
,
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{E}=\boldsymbol{e}_{\sigma_{V},h}
,
\varphi=e_{\psi,h}
, and
(\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{v}},\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{q}})=(\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{e}}_{u,h},\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{e}}_{p,h})
in (
23
) and applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality together with (
20
) yield

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}

\displaystyle\big\{\lVert(\boldsymbol{e}_{u,h},\boldsymbol{e}_{p,h})\rVert^{2}_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}+\lVert(\boldsymbol{e}_{\sigma_{E},h},\boldsymbol{e}_{\sigma_{V},h},e_{\psi,h})\rVert^{2}_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}\big\}+\lVert\beta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\boldsymbol{e}_{p,h}\rVert^{2}_{0,\Omega}+(\omega\mathcal{G}\boldsymbol{e}_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{V},h},\boldsymbol{e}_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{V},h})_{0,\Omega}

\displaystyle\qquad+\lVert\tfrac{k+1}{h_{\mathcal{F}}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{\llbracket{\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{e}}_{u,h}}\rrbracket}\rVert^{2}_{0,\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}+\lVert\tfrac{k+1}{h_{\mathcal{F}}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{\llbracket{\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{e}}_{p,h}}\rrbracket}\rVert^{2}_{0,\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\tfrac{h_{\mathcal{F}}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{k+1}(\boldsymbol{\chi}_{\sigma_{E}}+\omega\boldsymbol{\chi}_{\sigma_{V}}-\alpha\chi_{\psi}I_{d})\boldsymbol{n}\rVert_{0,\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}\lVert\tfrac{k+1}{h_{\mathcal{F}}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{\llbracket{\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{e}}_{u,h}}\rrbracket}\rVert_{0,\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}+\lVert\tfrac{h_{\mathcal{F}}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{k+1}\chi_{\psi}\rVert_{0,\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}\lVert\tfrac{k+1}{h_{\mathcal{F}}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{\llbracket{\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{e}}_{p,h}}\rrbracket}\rVert_{0,\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}

\displaystyle\quad+C\lVert(\boldsymbol{e}_{\sigma_{E},h},\boldsymbol{e}_{\sigma_{V},h},e_{\psi,h})\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}\lvert(\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{u},\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{p})\rvert_{\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{Q}}

\displaystyle\quad+\lVert\tfrac{k+1}{h_{\mathcal{F}}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{\llbracket{\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{u}}\rrbracket}\rVert_{0,\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}\lVert\tfrac{k+1}{h_{\mathcal{F}}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{\llbracket{\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{e}}_{u,h}}\rrbracket}\rVert_{0,\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}+\lVert\tfrac{k+1}{h_{\mathcal{F}}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{\llbracket{\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{p}}\rrbracket}\rVert_{0,\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}\lVert\tfrac{k+1}{h_{\mathcal{F}}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{\llbracket{\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{e}}_{p,h}}\rrbracket}\rVert_{0,\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}.

We notice that, because of assumption (
15
), the projected errors satisfy vanishing initial conditions, namely,
\boldsymbol{e}_{\sigma_{E},h}(0)=\boldsymbol{e}_{\sigma_{V},h}(0)=\mathbf{0}
,
e_{\psi,h}(0)=0
and
\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{e}}_{u,h}(0)=\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{e}}_{p,h}(0)=(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{0})
. Hence, integrating over
t\in(0,T]
and using again the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality we deduce that

\displaystyle\lVert(\boldsymbol{e}_{u,h},\boldsymbol{e}_{p,h})\rVert^{2}_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}+\lVert(\boldsymbol{e}_{\sigma_{E},h},\boldsymbol{e}_{\sigma_{V},h},\boldsymbol{e}_{\psi,h})\rVert^{2}_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}+\int_{0}^{t}\lVert\tfrac{k+1}{h_{\mathcal{F}}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{\llbracket{\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{e}}_{u,h}}\rrbracket}\rVert^{2}_{0,\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}\,\text{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\lVert\tfrac{k+1}{h_{\mathcal{F}}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{\llbracket{\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{e}}_{p,h}}\rrbracket}\rVert^{2}_{0,\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}\,\text{d}s

\displaystyle\lesssim\Big(\int_{0}^{T}(\lVert\tfrac{h_{\mathcal{F}}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{k+1}(\boldsymbol{\chi}_{\sigma_{E}}+\omega\boldsymbol{\chi}_{\sigma_{V}}-\alpha\chi_{\psi}I_{d})\boldsymbol{n}\rVert^{2}_{0,\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}+\lVert\tfrac{h_{\mathcal{F}}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{k+1}\chi_{\psi}\rVert^{2}_{0,\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}+\lVert\tfrac{k+1}{h_{\mathcal{F}}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{\llbracket{\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{u}}\rrbracket}\rVert^{2}_{0,\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\lVert\tfrac{k+1}{h_{\mathcal{F}}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{\llbracket{\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{p}}\rrbracket}\rVert^{2}_{0,\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}})\text{d}t\Big)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\times\Big(\int_{0}^{T}(\lVert\tfrac{k+1}{h_{\mathcal{F}}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{\llbracket{\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{e}}_{u,h}}\rrbracket}\rVert^{2}_{0,\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}+\lVert\tfrac{k+1}{h_{\mathcal{F}}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{\llbracket{\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{e}}_{p,h}}\rrbracket}\rVert^{2}_{0,\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}})\ \text{d}t\Big)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\Big(\int_{0}^{T}\lVert(\boldsymbol{e}_{\sigma_{E},h},\boldsymbol{e}_{\sigma_{V},h},e_{\psi,h})\rVert^{2}_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}\text{d}t\Big)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\Big(\int_{0}^{T}\lvert(\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{u},\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{p})\rvert^{2}_{\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{Q}}\text{d}t\Big)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}},\quad\forall t\in(0,T].

Finally, a simple application of Young’s inequality yields

\displaystyle\max_{[0,T]}\lVert(\boldsymbol{e}_{u,h},\boldsymbol{e}_{p,h})\rVert^{2}_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}+\max_{[0,T]}\lVert(\boldsymbol{e}_{\sigma_{E},h},\boldsymbol{e}_{\sigma_{V},h},e_{\psi,h})\rVert^{2}_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}+\int_{0}^{T}(\lVert\tfrac{k+1}{h_{\mathcal{F}}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{\llbracket{\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{e}}_{u,h}}\rrbracket}\rVert^{2}_{0,\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}+\lVert\tfrac{k+1}{h_{\mathcal{F}}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{\llbracket{\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{e}}_{p,h}}\rrbracket}\rVert^{2}_{0,\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}})\text{d}t

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\lesssim\int_{0}^{T}\Big(\lVert\tfrac{h_{\mathcal{F}}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{k+1}(\boldsymbol{\chi}_{\sigma_{E}}+\omega\boldsymbol{\chi}_{\sigma_{V}}-\alpha\chi_{\psi}I_{d})\rVert^{2}_{0,\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}+\lVert\tfrac{h_{\mathcal{F}}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{k+1}\chi_{\psi}\rVert^{2}_{0,\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}+\lvert(\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{u},\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{p})\rvert^{2}_{\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{Q}}\Big)\,\text{d}t,

and the result follows.
∎

Theorem 2
.

Let
(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{p})\in\mathcal{C}^{1}_{[0,T]}(\mathcal{H}_{1})\cap\mathcal{C}^{0}_{[0,T]}(\mathcal{X}_{1})
and
(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{E},\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{V},\psi)\in\mathcal{C}^{1}_{[0,T]}(\mathcal{H}_{2})\cap\mathcal{C}^{0}_{[0,T]}(\mathcal{X}_{2})
be the solution of (
6
). Assume that
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{E},\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{V}\in\mathcal{C}^{0}_{[0,T]}(H^{1+r}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\text{sym}}))
,
\psi\in\mathcal{C}^{0}_{[0,T]}(H^{1+r}(\Omega))
and
\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{p}\in\mathcal{C}^{0}_{[0,T]}(H^{2+r}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d}))
, with
r\geq 0
. Then, there exists a constant
C>0
independent of
h
and
k
such that

\displaystyle\max_{[0,T]}\lVert(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{p}-\boldsymbol{p}_{h})(t))\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}+\max_{[0,T]}\lVert(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{E}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{E,h},\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{V}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{V,h},\psi-\psi_{h})(t)\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}

\displaystyle\,\leq C\tfrac{h_{K}^{\min\{r,k\}+1}}{(k+1)^{r+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}\Big(\max_{[0,T]}\lVert\boldsymbol{u}\rVert_{2+r,\Omega}+\max_{[0,T]}\lVert\boldsymbol{p}\rVert_{2+r,\Omega}+\max_{[0,T]}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{E}\right\rVert_{1+r,\Omega}+\max_{[0,T]}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{V}\right\rVert_{1+r,\Omega}+\max_{[0,T]}\left\lVert\psi\right\rVert_{1+r,\Omega}\Big).

Proof.

By applying the triangle inequality and the stability estimate (
22
), we obtain:

\displaystyle\max_{[0,T]}\lVert(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{p}-\boldsymbol{p}_{h})(t))\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}+\max_{[0,T]}\lVert(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{E}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{E,h},\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{V}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{V,h},\psi-\psi_{h})(t)\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\max_{[0,T]}\lVert(\boldsymbol{\chi}_{u},\boldsymbol{\chi}_{p})(t)\rVert^{2}_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}+\max_{[0,T]}\lVert(\boldsymbol{\chi}_{\sigma_{E}},\boldsymbol{\chi}_{\sigma_{V}},\chi_{\psi})(t)\rVert^{2}_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}

\displaystyle\quad+\left(\int_{0}^{T}\Big(\lVert\tfrac{h_{\mathcal{F}}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{k+1}\boldsymbol{\chi}_{\sigma_{E}}\rVert^{2}_{0,\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}+\lVert\tfrac{h_{\mathcal{F}}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{k+1}\boldsymbol{\chi}_{\sigma_{V}}\rVert^{2}_{0,\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}+\lVert\tfrac{h_{\mathcal{F}}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{k+1}\chi_{\psi}\rVert^{2}_{0,\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}+\lvert(\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{u},\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{p})\rvert^{2}_{\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{Q}}\Big)\,\text{d}t\right)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}.

The desired result follows directly from the error estimates (
35
) and (
36
).
∎

Remark 1
.

The convergence analysis in Theorem
2
leads to two main observations:

•

The theoretical bound is sub-optimal by a factor
(k+1)^{1/2}
. This slight deterioration arises from the combination of the discrete trace-inverse inequality (
34
) with the standard stabilization weight
(k+1)^{2}/h_{\mathcal{F}}
introduced to ensure stability; together they lead to estimate (
36
).

•

The numerical experiments of Subsection
6.1
indicate optimal convergence rates for
\boldsymbol{u}
and
\boldsymbol{p}
in the
L^{2}
-norms, suggesting a gap between theoretical predictions and practical performance that will be addressed in future work.

7.  Conclusions

This study has developed a single first-order evolution formulation that captures both Zener-type poro-viscoelasticity and thermo-viscoelasticity, proved its well-posedness with semigroup theory, and designed a hybridizable discontinuous Galerkin discretization that remains energy-stable and hp-optimal on general simplicial meshes. Numerical experiments—from manufactured-solution tests to wave-propagation benchmarks in heterogeneous media—match the predicted convergence rates and reproduce the expected
P
-,
S
-, and
T
-waves, confirming both the analysis and the practical efficiency of the scheme. The framework therefore offers a high-order, robust and computationally economical tool for multiphysics simulations in geomechanics, biomechanics and thermoelastic materials, and it provides a foundation for forthcoming work on adaptive hp-refinement and nonlinear rheologies.
