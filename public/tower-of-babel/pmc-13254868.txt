Recent HIV-1 infection and associated factors among newly diagnosed female sex workers in Ethiopia-2019 to 2022
Authors: Minilik Demissie Amogne, Tekalign Deressa, Fethia Kedir, Feyiso Bati, Abnet Assefa, Jaleta Bulti Tura, Ayalew Aklilu, Wudinesh Belete Belihu, Birra Bejiga Bedassa, Saro Abdella, Fekadu Yadeta, Moges Asressie Chanyalew, Mesay Hailu, Getachew Tollera, Sileshi Lulseged, Zenebe Melaku, Gemechu Tadesse
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDetecting sub-population groups that are most at risk for HIV is critical for designing effective intervention. In this study, we aimed to examine the magnitude and associated factors for recent HIV infection among newly diagnosed HIV-positive female sex workers (FSWs) in Ethiopia.MethodsWe analyzed data collected from health facilities involved in the surveillance program from July 2019 to June 2022. HIV case surveillance is an ongoing data collection system from people newly diagnosed with HIV. HIV testing was conducted using the national algorithm, and recency testing done using Asante HIV-1 Rapid Recency Assay. Bivariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were employed to determine associations between demographic and behavioral factors and recent HIV infection. P<.05 was used to declare statistical significance of multivariable logistic regression analyses.ResultsA total of 4852 newly diagnosed HIV positive FSWs, with a median age of 29 years [IQR: 11], were included in the analysis. The proportion of recent HIV infection was 16.4%. Multivariable logistic regression revealed that FSWs aged <30 years, those never married or widowed, those tested in community-based settings, and those with a history of previous HIV testing had higher odds of recent infection. On the other hand, individuals who reported having sexual contact with a known HIV positive person (or an index case) had lower odds of having recent HIV infection.ConclusionThe analysis revealed a high proportion of recent HIV infection among FSWs. These findings provide critical evidence to the HIV program, enabling tailored and effective interventions targeting FSWs at highest risk of HIV infection.Key words: Ethiopia, female sex workers, HIV case surveillance, key population, new diagnosed, recent HIV infection

To gain control over the HIV epidemic, we must identify and epidemiologically characterize every case to capture the remaining chains of transmission. This analysis highlights the specific gaps and necessary actions among Female Sex Workers (FSWs) to effectively curb transmission dynamics.

This study identifies the key factors driving ongoing HIV transmission among Female Sex Workers and shows potential interventions areas to mitigate infection dynamics.

Introduction

Despite achievements in curbing the human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) epidemic, it remains a major global public health concern.1 In 2023, an estimated 1.3 million new HIV infections and 650,000 acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS)-related deaths were reported.1 These statistics represent a failure to meet the global target of keeping both metrics under the 500,000 threshold. Key populations (KPs), including FSWs, along with their sexual partners accounted for 70% of global HIV infections. Furthermore, the risk of acquiring HIV among FSWs was nearly 30 times that of adult women in the general population.2,3

In Ethiopia, FSWs are disproportionately affected by the HIV epidemic. A 2020 study conducted in 16 towns reported a pooled HIV prevalence of 18.7%, among FSWs, compared with 1.2% among females in the general population.4,5 An earlier study in 2013 found a prevalence of 23% among FSWs.6 The persistently high prevalence is linked to risky sexual behavior, low education level, economic hardship, substance use, criminalization, and stigma associated with sex work.5, 6, 7 These social, behavioral, and structural barriers substantially increase vulnerability to HIV acquisition and onward transmission. The 2021 to 2026 Global AIDS Strategy highlights the urgency of addressing such barriers and scaling up evidence-based interventions for KPs to accelerate progress toward ending HIV/AIDS as a public health threat by 2030.8

The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends surveillance of newly diagnosed persons to target interventions toward at highest risk of HIV, particularly in countries approaching epidemic control.9 HIV recency testing enables identification of hotspots and sub-populations with high transmission potential, thereby guiding tailored interventions. Individuals with recent HIV infection- defined as infection acquired within the past 12 months, are more likely to transmit HIV due to high viral loads during acute infection and ongoing risk behaviors.10,11 Identifying sub-population with rapid transmission dynamics is thus critical for maximizing the impact of prevention strategies.3,12 Despite the central role of FSWs in HIV transmission dynamics4,5,9 the proportion of recent HIV infections in this group remains undocumented in Ethiopia.

Ethiopia has implemented HIV case surveillance (CS) and recency testing among newly diagnosed HIV-positive individuals since June 2019. The objectives were to collect data on new HIV diagnoses by person, place, and time, characterizing the epidemic, and guiding the public health response. Thus, the current analysis aimed at determining the magnitude of and factors associated with recent HIV infection among FSWs newly diagnosed with HIV, and their engagement in HIV care and treatment services.

Methods

Study design

This was an analysis of national cross-section surveillance program data. The analysis examined HIV CS data submitted electronically by participating HFs to the Ethiopian Public Health Institute (EPHI) from July 2019 to June 2022.13

Study setting and population

By June 2022, more than 650 HFs across all regions of the country were involved in HIV Case Surveillance (HCS) (Figure 1). Implementation of the program use progressive enrollment of health facilities (HFs), prioritizing those with a high volume of new diagnoses and serving large numbers of people living with HIV (PLHIV). The source population for the surveillance included all newly identified individuals with HIV regardless of sex or age, while recency testing was applied to those aged ≥15 years. The target population for the current analysis is FSWs (newly identified), defined here as participants who reported sex work as their occupation.

Data collection tool and training

Study data were collected using a structured paper-based case report form (CRF) at HIV testing outlets, including voluntary counselling and testing (VCT), outpatient/in-patient departments (OPDs/IPDs), antenatal care (ANC) units, and tuberculosis (TB) clinics. Clinical information (HIV care and treatment, CD4+ T-cell count, opportunistic infections (OIs), and WHO staging was abstracted from patient charts within 2 weeks of diagnosis. Service providers performing HIV testing and completing CRFs received 5 days of training on surveillance objectives, recency testing procedures, research ethics, CRF completion, and data flow.

Laboratory methods

HIV testing was conducted at the point of care (POC) by service providers following the national HIV testing algorithm, which involves three rapid tests: HIV1/2 STAT-PAK (assay one), ABON (assay two), and SD Bioline (assay three). Clients aged ≥15 years and diagnosed with HIV, who provided consent, were classified as recent or long-term infections based on the Asante Rapid Test for Recent Infection (RTRI) (Asante HIV-1 Recency Test, Sedia Biosciences, Oregon, USA).14 The Asante assay is a rapid POC test that simultaneously verifies HIV diagnosis and detects recent infection. The test has sensitivity of 99.1% (95% CI: 98.0–99.6) and specificity of 98.9% (95% CI: 98.0–99.4) for identification of with HIV (14). Tests were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions.15

Data extraction

HCS data were captured using CRFs and entered into the Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) database at the facility level by trained data clerks, and transferred securely to the EPHI server. With permission from EPHI, data for this analysis were extracted from the REDCap database in excel format and imported into Stata/SE version 16 (College Station, TX: StataCorp LLC). Of 51,057 newly diagnosed individuals with HIV, who were reported to the HIV CS system between July 2019 and June 2022, a total of 5387 were FSWs, of which 4852 (90%) had valid HIV recency test results and were included in the analysis. The remaining 535 were excluded: five had inconclusive test results, 31 refused testing, 314 were not tested due to interruptions (COVID-19 or test kits stockout), and 185 had missing information. Variables

The outcome variable of interest is RTRI recent, and the independent variables include sociodemographic clinical and behavioral factors (age, sex, residence, education level, marital status, testing place, modality of testing, sexual contact with index case, injecting drug use, prior history of HIV testing, history of pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) use, pregnancy/breast feeding status, and presence of OIs. Other variables added to describe service utilization include linkage to care and treatment, antiretroviral therapy (ART) initiation, and time to ART initiation.

Data management and analysis

Stata/SE version 16 (College Station, TX: StataCorp LLC) was used for data cleaning and analysis. Baseline characteristics of the study participants were summarized using descriptive statistics (frequencies, proportions, and medians with IQR). Bivariable logistic regression was done to assess association and identify candidate variables for the multivariable regression model. Accordingly, independent variables from the bivariable model with P<.2 were included in a multivariable regression to identify factors that are independently and significantly associated with the outcome variable. We used backward stepwise approach to fit the model, where variables with P-values greater than .05 were excluded from the model step by step until we got all the remaining variables having P<.05. The Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test was used to assess model calibration, and it indicating that the model fit the data well. Results from the final model included adjusted odds ratios, P-values and 95% confidence intervals (CI).

Ethical considerations

The HCS protocol was approved by the Institutional Ethics Review Boards (IRBs) of EPHI (protocol number: EPHI-IRB-127-2018), on 06 October 2018. The HCS Publication Advisory Committee (CPAC) at the EPHI approved the analysis of the data. All providers involved in completing and approving the CRF, and in entering data onto the database signed confidentiality agreement. Participation in the surveillance is voluntary. Verbal consent was taken for risk factor assessment and HIV recency testing from clients aged ≥15 years. Consent was confirmed by the provider’s signature, initials, and date on the data collection form. For age 15 to 18 the ethics committee provided waiver to conduct the data collection considering the sensitivity of HIV.

Results

Sociodemographic and HIV test-related characteristics

The majority, 2466 (51%), of the newly diagnosed FSWs with HIV were younger than 29 years. 2103 (44%), were never married, and 1722 (35%) were divorced or separated. At the time of diagnosis, 4182 (87%) resided in urban areas. More than one-half, 2599 (54%), were tested at community drop-in centers (DICs), followed by health centers, where nearly one-third, 1691 (35%), were tested. Three-quarters, 3547 (75%), were diagnosed through provider-initiated counselling and testing (PICT), while the remainder were diagnosed through voluntary counselling and testing (VCT) (Table 1).

Behavioral characteristics, clinical conditions, and key HIV services uptake among newly diagnosed HIV positive FSWs

Eight hundred and eighteen (18%) of the FSWs with HIV reported sexual contact with known partner with HIV, and 160 3.4% reported recent injecting drug use. Nearly three-quarters, 3496 (74%) had no prior history of HIV testing. Only 23 (2%) reported prior use of pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP). Almost all FSWs, 4812 (99.7%), were linked to HIV care and treatment services, and 4571 (95.2%) initiated ART within 2 weeks of diagnosis. Among those linked to care, 3790 (89%) initiated ART on the same day as diagnosis (Table 2).

Geographic distribution of female sex workers with recent HIV infection

The geographic distribution of recent HIV infections among newly diagnosed FSWs varied widely. Health facilities from 312 woredas (districts) reported individuals with recency testing, and 174 woredas identified at least one recent infection between July 2019 and June 2022. Concentrations of recent infections were most pronounced in the northwestern and central regions of the country (Figure 3). Factors associated with recent HIV infection in female sex workers with HIV

The overall proportion of recent HIV infection was 16.4% (n = 798; 95% CI: 15.4–17.5). Independent predictors of recent infection included age, marital status, facility type, sexual contact with an index/known HIV-positive person, and prior HIV testing history. Compared with FSWs aged ≥40 years, those aged 15 to 19, 20 to 24, and 25 to 29 years were 2.86 (95% CI: 1.94–4.24), 2.06 (95% CI: 1.47–2.90), and 1.49 (95% CI: 1.07–2.07) times more likely to have recent infection, respectively. Never-married and widowed FSWs had higher odds compared with married counterparts (AOR: 1.72; 95% CI: 1.16–2.54 and AOR: 1.91; 95% CI: 1.22–2.98, respectively). Those tested in community settings were more likely to have recent infection than those tested at health centers (AOR: 1.40; 95% CI: 1.16–1.70). Conversely, FSWs reporting sexual contact with an index/known HIV-positive person had significantly lower odds of recent infection (AOR: 0.47; 95% CI: 0.36–0.61). Also, a history of prior HIV testing was associated with increased likelihood of recent infection (AOR: 1.43; 95% CI: 1.19–1.71) (Table 3).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to analyze the magnitude and associated factors of recent HIV infection among newly diagnosed FSWs in Ethiopia. The proportion of recent infection was 16.4%. Significant associations were observed across subgroups defined by age, marital status, having sexual contact with a known PLHIV individuals and history of HIV testing.

The 16.4% proportion of recent HIV infection among newly diagnosed FSWs is high, underscoring their critical role in sustaining active HIV transmission in the community. While no other national-level recency studies in Ethiopia are available for comparison, this estimate exceeds that observed among newly diagnosed women in the general population (11%) reported through the same surveillance program.13 Internationally, our findings align with recent infection estimates among FSWs in China (16.4%) and Zimbabwe (15.7%), despite methodological differences.16,17 For instance, the Chinese study combined HIV progression data with BED-capture enzyme immunoassay (CEIA), while the Zimbabwean study used the LAg avidity assay, yielding 15.7% but dropping to 10.5% when coupled with viral load testing.17 Despite these variations, the convergence of findings highlights the elevated risk of FSWs with recent infection in HIV transmission dynamics and underscores the need for tailored intervention strategies.

We also observed wide geographic variation in recent infections, with higher concentrations in the northwestern and central regions of the country. These patterns may reflect the urban character of these areas and the availability of targeted HIV services through both public facilities and community-based settings.

Younger FSWs face greater vulnerability to HIV infection than their older counterparts due to multiple behavioral, structural, and social factors.18,19 Consistent with this, our analysis showed higher levels of recent infection among those aged <30 years. Possible explanations include low risk perception, exposure to violence, substance use, stigma, and discrimination.19, 20, 21, 22 For example, a study in Gambella, Ethiopia, found that young FSWs showed poor motivation to engage in prevention behaviors.22 Other studies in Ethiopia and Kenya reported that those entering sex work at younger ages were more likely to experience violence and engage in heavy drinking.20,21 Young FSWs also often lack the power to negotiate condom use, and stigma and discrimination restrict access to health services.19 Overall, these factors heighten their vulnerability to recent infections among those in the younger age group. Targeted HIV prevention programs should therefore prioritize youth, with strategies to expand service coverage and address structural barriers.23

Marital status may also influence HIV transmission dynamics. A study in South Africa found that never-married and widowed individuals had approximately twice the risk of HIV infection compared with married individuals.23 Although marital status is more complex in the context of FSWs, our findings similarly showed higher rates of recent infection among widowed and never-married FSWs compared with their married counterparts. This may suggest heightened vulnerability in these groups, while married FSWs may take additional precautions, such as consistent condom use, to protect themselves and their partners.

HIV testing history The high HIV prevalence among frequently tested FSWs likely reflects a self-selection bias, where individuals with heightened risk exposure such as those experiencing violence or engaging in compensated condomless sex, and/or who consume more alcohol and became reluctant on condom use; utilize testing services more regularly. This suggests that frequent testing might be a proxy for sustained high-risk activity within the group

FSWs tested at community DICs were more likely to have recent infection than those tested at health centers. DICs provide safe and supportive spaces for FSWs, offering HIV/sexually transmitted infection (STI) information, prevention services, and linkage to care. As such, FSWs accessed at DICs may have greater risk awareness and earlier health-seeking behavior, contributing to the higher detection of recent infections in this setting. Consistent with this, previous research showed that FSWs visiting DICs demonstrated greater risk awareness and safer sex practices compared with non-attendees.24 DICs are strategically located in hotspots (eg, areas with many bars, hotels, and pensions) and offer behavioral sessions facilitated by peer educators using human-centered design approaches. These features may help explain the higher identification of recent infections in DIC-based testing.

FSWs reporting sexual contact with known individuals with HIV had lower odds of recent infection compared with those without such contact. A study in northern Ethiopia similarly reported lower recent infection among index contacts in the general population.25 This may reflect delays in disclosure or testing among index cases and their contacts or testing only after HIV symptoms appear. However, the mechanisms underlying this disparity require further investigation.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. The Asante RTRI assay without incorporation of viral load testing can overestimate the proportion of recent HIV infections due to its known high false recent rate (FRR). However, this bias is likely to be minimal, as only 2% of FSWs were in WHO stage III/IV at diagnosis. The manufacturer also acknowledges the limitation related to FRR and recommends complementing RTRI results with viral load testing or other recent infection testing algorithms (RITA) components where feasible. Cognizant of this, we clearly acknowledged this limitation and the reader should be cautious during interpretation of the findings.

As self-identified FSWs with HIV were included in our study, underreporting of this key population is possible. Although efforts exist to identify duplicate cases, the lack of a unique identifier system and reluctance to disclose prior HIV-positive status, might have led to misclassification of some repeat diagnoses as new, potentially underestimating recent infection. Furthermore, participating facilities might not be representative of all FSWs nationally, as scale-up prioritized high-burden sites warranting a cautious interpretation of the results and their generalizability.

Conclusion

The magnitude of recent HIV infection among newly diagnosed FSWs in Ethiopia is high. Rates were significantly higher among younger (aged <30 years), never married, and widowed FSWs. Community-based HIV testing services identified a greater proportion of recent infections, underscoring their value in achieving the first 95 of the Joint United Nations Program on HIV/AIDS’ target, enabling timely interruption of transmission chains. History of prior HIV testing was also associated with recent infection, highlighting the importance of widespread and repeated testing to facilitate early diagnosis. Conversely, sexual contact with known HIV-positive individuals was negatively associated with recent infection, suggesting delayed testing behaviors in this group. Further geospatial analyses are warranted to better explain regional variations and guide programmatic responses. Overall, this study demonstrates the value of integrating recency testing into national HIV case surveillance, providing actionable insights to inform tailored interventions for FSWs and contributing to the national epidemic control efforts.
