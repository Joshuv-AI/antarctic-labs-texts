Social network analysis as a technology-enhanced assessment tool for collaborative skills in medical PBL
Authors: Ahmad Hathim Ahmad Azman, Nee Tiong, Mohammad Arif Kamarudin, Zamira Hasanah Zamzuri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeCollaborative communication and teamwork are core competencies in medical education, yet they remain difficult to assess objectively during Problem-Based Learning (PBL). This study evaluates Social Network Analysis (SNA) as a technology-enhanced assessment tool to quantify peer collaboration patterns and identify learners’ engagement profiles. We examined how PBL interaction networks differ across cohorts and whether SNA-derived indicators align with academic achievement.MethodsA comparative SNA was conducted across 17 PBL groups (N = 176; Year 1: 77, Year 2: 99). Directed, weighted ties were constructed by integrating ranked peer nominations with a validated five-item collaboration rubric to generate hybrid edge weights. Cohort-level network properties (density, path length, transitivity, reciprocity, modularity) and node-level centrality measures (in-degree, closeness, betweenness) were computed as indicators of collaborative competence. Between-cohort differences were analysed using non-parametric tests, and alignment between centrality and cumulative grade point average (CGPA) was examined to provide preliminary validity evidence.ResultsYear 2 demonstrated a more cohesive and integrated collaboration structure, reflected by higher density (0.313 vs. 0.277), greater transitivity (0.532 vs. 0.443), and shorter average path lengths (1.72 vs. 2.06). At the individual level, Year 2 students showed significantly higher closeness () and lower betweenness (p = .027), indicating more efficient communication pathways. Across both cohorts, higher in-degree (representing peer-recognized collaborative competence) showed the strongest alignment with CGPA, whereas other centrality measures demonstrated smaller, cohort-dependent effects.ConclusionSNA provides quantifiable, competency-linked indicators of collaboration and communication in PBL, functioning as a novel technology-enhanced assessment tool. Patterns of social prominence and integration were meaningfully associated with academic achievement, suggesting that SNA may support early identification of highly engaged learners and those at risk of marginalization. These findings highlight the potential of SNA to complement existing assessment methods and enhance feedback within competency-based medical education. Further research using longitudinal and multilevel designs is warranted to strengthen the tool’s validity and inform its integration into routine educational assessment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09397-z.Keywords: Social network analysis, Technology-enhanced assessment, Collaborative competence, Problem-based learning, Medical education, Peer evaluation, Learning analytics

Introduction

Medical education has shifted from traditional lectures to active learning approaches [31]. Problem-Based Learning (PBL), introduced at McMaster University in 1969 [21, 34], has consistently been shown to improve learning outcomes [19, 36]. Conducted in small groups of 5–7 students, PBL promotes student-centred learning by fostering collaboration, self-directed inquiry, and the development of clinical reasoning skills [24, 26]. Its emphasis on teamwork and communication has positioned PBL as a core strategy for cultivating key competencies in competency-based medical education (CBME) [11].

Alongside PBL, peer assessment has gained traction as a formative method to provide structured feedback on professional behaviours and teamwork [7, 18]. Effective feedback helps learners recognise gaps and improve as team members [17]. Although tutor ratings, professionalism checklists, and peer evaluations are commonly used, these methods often struggle to capture the complexity of real-time group interactions and can be limited by bias and variable sampling. Consequently, there is interest in more objective, scalable approaches such as learning analytics and e-portfolios, to capture behavioural indicators of collaborative competence [2].

Within PBL, collaborative work is a central construct. Peer interaction parameters such as Attitude and Collaborative Work [9] reflect the quality of group engagement. High-quality interaction, including discussing contradictory information, supports deeper learning [30]. Integrating collaborative learning principles with PBL has demonstrated benefits; for example, Collaborative Problem-Based Learning (CPBL) was shown to enhance students’ collaborative and problem-solving skills [12]. This study therefore focuses on collaborative work within PBL to examine peer assessment and emergent interaction patterns.

Social Network Analysis (SNA) provides a relational lens for understanding how students interact by representing individuals as nodes and their connections as edges. In this study, nodes represent students and edges reflect collaborative interactions. SNA enables the examination of group structure, participation patterns, and relational influences on learning [10]. Its relevance in PBL contexts is well-established, as collaboration and communication are central to effective problem-solving [27, 29].

Previous studies show that social integration is associated with higher academic performance [8], while tightly knit triads may be linked to lower grades [6]. Collectively, findings suggest that students more embedded within their learning networks tend to perform better [8, 27].

In medical education, SNA usage has grown but remains limited. While SNA has yielded insights into collaboration among medical trainees [14], it is still underutilized. Some studies demonstrate that SNA centrality measures can predict performance in online PBL and help identify active or isolated students [29]. However, its adoption as a pedagogical or assessment tool remains low due to analytic complexity, software barriers, and ethical concerns [14]. Faculty development, accessible tools, and strong data governance can help address these challenges [32].

Importantly, most SNA studies in medical education rely on online platforms where digital traces enable automated data capture. Evidence on interaction patterns in face-to-face (F2F) PBL remains scarce, despite F2F discussions being the dominant mode of delivery. To date, no published studies have applied SNA to examine F2F PBL interactions in medical education. This gap limits our ability to generalize findings from online environments to in-person settings, where richer verbal and non-verbal exchanges may produce different network structures. By applying SNA to authentic F2F PBL discussions, this study aims to provide the first empirical evidence on how interaction networks form and function in in-person medical education, thereby offering a more accurate understanding of participation dynamics and their relationship with learning outcomes.

Methodology

Study design

A cross-sectional, between-cohort comparative social network analysis (SNA) of Problem-Based Learning (PBL) groups was conducted in the Doctor of Medicine program at Faculty of Medicine, Universiti Kebangsaan Malaysia (UKM). Data was collected from May – October 2025, towards the end of the term in Year 1 and Year 2.

This study was conceptualized not only as a comparative analysis of PBL interaction networks but also as an evaluation of Social Network Analysis (SNA) as a technology-enhanced assessment tool for collaborative competence. SNA was used to generate quantifiable indicators of teamwork, communication, peer recognition, and participatory engagement where competencies aligned with contemporary competency-based medical education (CBME). The methodological choices described below therefore operationalization SNA metrics as assessment outputs, allowing examination of their psychometric properties and their alignment with academic performance.

Ethical approval

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Universiti Kebangsaan Malaysia Research Ethics Committee (JEP-2025-234). Participation was voluntary, and informed consent was obtained electronically from all participants prior to data collection

Participants

In this study, the sampling frame consisted of all Year 1 and Year 2 medical students enrolled in the 2025 academic session. These students are naturally organized into predefined PBL groups, with each functioning as an intact instructional unit. Thus, individual students were not sampled independently of one another. Instead, recruitment and sampling were carried out at the level of PBL groups. This constitutes a form of cluster sampling, where each PBL group is treated as a cluster, and all members within selected clusters are invited to participate. Given that all PBL groups within each cohort were approached, the design aligns most closely with single-stage cluster sampling with full cluster inclusion.

The rationale for this approach is twofold. First, PBL groups are pedagogically structured to maintain stable membership for the duration of the semester, meaning students’ learning interactions are inherently nested within groups and not statistically independent. Second, because Social Network Analysis (SNA) requires complete relational data, valid network-level analyses can only be performed when all members of a cluster provide responses, thereby necessitating group-level recruitment rather than individual-level sampling.

To ensure methodological rigour, clustering was explicitly considered during both design and analysis. During recruitment, all PBL groups from both cohorts were invited, thus avoiding selection bias arising from heterogeneous group characteristics (e.g., facilitator style, group size, communication dynamics). During analysis, intra-cluster correlation (ICC), anticipated at approximately based on comparable educational cluster designs [16], was acknowledged in interpreting precision estimates and the nested nature of the data. This approach ensures that both the sampling strategy and subsequent analytical procedures appropriately reflect the clustered educational context in which the data were generated.

Measures

Demographics and academic outcome

Students’ academic achievement was the cumulative grade point average (CGPA) at the end of the 2024/2025 term, obtained from faculty records together with age and ethnicity, and linked to survey responses using de-identified IDs. Following the standard set by the university, the CGPA cut-off point of 3.00 is used to identify poor performers.

Peer evaluation

The peer evaluation component employed a five-item collaboration scale adapted for Problem-Based Learning (PBL) from [9] and previously validated in Malay by [1]. Items were rated on a 4-point Likert scale; total scores per ratee ranged from 5 to 20, with higher scores indicating better observed collaborative behaviour. This scale formed part of a broader questionnaire that also included a peer nomination section developed specifically for this study (see Supplementary File 1).

Peer nominations

Within each PBL group, each student nominated and ranked their top three peers (and could include the group supervisor) with whom they most frequently or meaningfully interacted during the session. Ranks were converted to points (Top-1=3, Top-2=2, Top-3=1; otherwise 0). Facilitator nodes (identified in green in the sociogram) appear in the network only when students actively nominated them for collaborative interactions. The absence of a supervisor node in certain PBL sociograms reflects a passive facilitation style where the tutor did not directly engage in measurable information exchange with the students.

Questionnaire administration

Surveys and consent were administered via Google Forms, distributed through the programme’s WhatsApp channels. One submission per student was enforced via authenticated university accounts. Self-nominations were disabled.

Network construction

We modelled directed, weighted graphs at the group level. A directed edge from A to B reflects A’s evaluation and nomination of B. Maximum scaling was applied to both collaboration score and rank weights by dividing each value by the highest score within the group. This method is selected to preserve the proportional meaning of participation without forcing the lowest values to zero. In PBL, even low-contributing students will exhibit some level of engagement, and reducing these values to zero would misrepresent their involvement. Thus, max-normalisation provides a more accurate representation of the relative participation.

Collaboration score normalisation

For each rater–ratee dyad within a PBL group, the collaboration score [5,20] was normalised using maximum scaling, in which each score is divided by the highest observed score within the group. This procedure anchors the maximum to 1 while preserving proportional differences, yielding normalised values in the range (0,1].

Rank weight normalisation

Rank weight scores were normalised similarly using maximum scaling, producing values in [0,1] because the minimum possible raw score is 0.

Operationalization of the SNA-based assessment framework

The weighted, directed network constructed for each PBL group formed the basis of an assessment framework in which hybrid edge weights represented the strength and quality of collaborative interactions. By integrating peer-evaluated collaboration scores with ranked interaction nominations, the resulting network structure captured both behavioural (collaboration quality) and relational (interaction prominence) dimensions of teamwork. Centrality measures derived from these weighted networks thus acted as assessment outputs, providing each student with quantifiable indicators of their collaborative engagement and social positioning within the group.

Mapping SNA metrics to competency domains

To align SNA outputs with assessment purposes, each network metric was mapped to relevant CBME-aligned competency domains. In-degree was interpreted as an indicator of peer-recognised collaborative reliability and communication effectiveness. Closeness centrality was conceptualised as an indicator of accessibility and integration within group discourse, reflecting efficiency in collaborative information flow. Betweenness centrality was treated as a proxy for leadership or brokerage behaviours within teams. These mappings allow SNA outputs to serve as measurable indicators of collaborative competence within PBL.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive and between-cohort comparisons

Group-level structural metrics (density, average path length, transitivity, reciprocity, and modularity) were computed for each PBL group and compared between the Year 1 and Year 2 cohorts using Mann–Whitney U tests. The Mann–Whitney U test is a non-parametric alternative to the independent-samples t-test that does not assume normality and is suitable for comparing distributions when sample sizes are small or when metric values are skewed, as is typical with network-level indices. At the node level, the distributions of centrality measures and academic performance (CGPA) were examined descriptively for each cohort to illustrate variation in social prominence and its potential alignment with academic outcomes.

Validity evidence

To establish preliminary validity evidence for the SNA-based assessment tool, two forms of validity were examined. Internal structure validity was supported through reliability analysis of the collaboration scale (Cronbach’s ), indicating consistency of peer evaluation inputs used in network construction. Criterion-related validity was explored by examining associations between centrality measures and academic achievement (CGPA). Positive alignment between SNA indicators and CGPA was interpreted as evidence that the tool captures meaningful aspects of collaborative competence relevant to academic performance.

Setting

The Medical Faculty of Universiti Kebangsaan Malaysia has implemented the Problem-Based Learning (PBL) system since 2005. During the preclinical years (Year 1 and Year 2), students participate in 2 to 4 PBL sessions within each study module. Each PBL session consists of 8 to 10 students and is conducted in two phases. In the first discussion, students focus on patient-centred approaches, which include analysing the patient information sheet and formulating relevant Learning Issues (LIs). The second discussion serves as a follow-up, where students reconvene to share findings from their independent study, address the previously identified Learning Issues, and collaboratively develop a concept map synthesising the knowledge gained across both discussions. The PBL process emphasises active and collaborative learning. Students are encouraged to share information, engage in constructive discussions, and integrate both basic medical sciences and clinical reasoning. These discussions typically cover history-taking, physical examination, laboratory and radiological findings, and key principles of patient management, ensuring that students gradually bridge preclinical knowledge with clinical acumen.

Results

Demographic characteristics and academic distribution

Overall participation rates were high. In Year 1, 179 of 205 eligible students responded (87.32%), and in Year 2, 160 of 182 students responded (87.91%). However, because SNA requires full group participation, only clusters with complete data could be retained. Consequently, from the Year 1 cohort, only seven fully completed PBL groups comprising 72 students were utilised for network analysis. Similarly, from the Year 2 cohort, only ten complete PBL groups comprising 94 students were included. This selective use of complete clusters ensures the integrity of computed network metrics, as missing nodes or edges would otherwise distort topological properties such as density, centrality, or modularity. For analyses not requiring complete networks such as reliability estimation of the peer-evaluation instrument, all available responses including those from incomplete clusters, were retained to maximize precision.

As shown in Table 2, a total of 17 PBL groups were analysed, comprising 72 Year 1 and 94 Year 2 students aged between 20 and 23 years old. Across all sessions, a total of 482 interactions were captured, forming directed, weighted networks where ties were represented by the hybrid score of collaborative score and rank, reflecting engagement between participants during the PBL sessions.

Node-level measures

As this network was constructed as a directed network, both in-degree and out-degree centralities can theoretically be measured to examine the interconnectivity of the PBL networks. However, out-degree was not included in the present analysis because the questionnaire required each student to nominate only their top three peers. This fixed nomination limit constrains out-degree values to a maximum of three, thereby restricting variability and interpretive value. In contrast, in-degree remains unconstrained and serves as a meaningful indicator of peer recognition, popularity, or perceived collaboration within the network.

In addition to in-degree, two other centrality measures were examined: betweenness and closeness. Betweenness centrality captures the extent to which a student functions as a bridge connecting otherwise separate subgroups, reflecting the potential for information or influence to flow through them. Closeness centrality represents how efficiently a student can reach all others in the network, indicating their accessibility or integration within the group. Together, these measures provide complementary insights into how students occupy different structural roles within their PBL networks, whether as well-connected collaborators, key intermediaries, or efficiently positioned participants within cohesive clusters.

Analysis of important nodes

To identify key individuals within each PBL subgroup, three centrality measures were examined: in-degree, closeness, and betweenness. In-degree reflects the number of incoming nominations a student receives from peers, indicating recognition, perceived competence, or collaborative reliability. Closeness measures how efficiently a student can reach others in the network, representing accessibility and integration within the group. Betweenness captures how often a student lies on the shortest paths between others, identifying individuals who bridge subgroups and facilitate information flow.

For each PBL subgroup, the top three students were identified based on each centrality metric to highlight those most recognised (in-degree), best connected (closeness), and most influential as bridges (betweenness). These top nodes were further examined in relation to academic performance (CGPA) to explore whether structurally prominent students also demonstrated stronger academic outcomes.

This indicates that being highly nominated by peers did not correspond with academic performance in this cohort. Similarly, for closeness centrality, no significant difference in CGPA was observed between those in the top three and their peers (Median = 3.11 vs. 3.15; ). The effect size was negligible (), suggesting that efficient positioning within the network was not associated with higher academic achievement. A similar lack of association was observed for betweenness centrality (Median = 3.11 vs. 3.15; ), indicating that acting as a “broker” between subgroups did not translate into a detectable academic advantage.

These findings suggest that network prominence and academic performance are weakly related, albeit not significantly. As shown in Fig. 5, the boxplots illustrate heavy overlapping distributions of CGPA across all three metrics. While measures reflecting structural accessibility (closeness) and brokerage (betweenness) show mild positive trends with CGPA, in-degree was not associated with higher achievement. This indicates that being well-connected or bridging peers may offer small academic benefits, whereas popularity alone does not necessarily predict performance.

The visual and statistical findings demonstrate that different centrality measures capture distinct dimensions of social prominence within PBL groups. In-degree reflects peer recognition, closeness highlights network integration and accessibility, and betweenness emphasises brokerage and bridging roles. The overlap in node sizes across categories underscores that social prominence and academic achievement are related yet distinct constructs, suggesting that active participation and connectedness may support, but do not fully determine, academic success in PBL settings.

However, this difference did not reach statistical significance (), despite a small to moderate effect size (). Adhering strictly to significance thresholds, this result indicates that being more efficiently connected within the network did not confer a reliable academic advantage.

Similarly, for betweenness centrality, the top-three students had a higher median CGPA (3.36) compared to others (3.06), but the difference was not significant (). Consequently, the data do not statistically confirm that acting as information brokers or bridges between peers offers an academic benefit in this cohort.

Taken together, the Year 2 analyses show that in-degree centrality was the only measure significantly associated with academic performance. While closeness and betweenness exhibited small to moderate effect sizes, their non-significant p-values dictate that they cannot be interpreted as providing distinct academic advantages. These results indicate that being recognised by peers, rather than simply being well-connected or strategically positioned, was the sole network metric reliably related to higher academic achievement in the second-year PBL networks.

Year 2 - network visualisation

The analysis of the Year 2 network plots reveals a strong replication of the social–academic dynamics observed in the previous year, underscoring the stability of these patterns within the Problem-Based Learning (PBL) environment. Across all three network visualisations, the top three central students in each subgroup (orange nodes) were again consistently among those with the largest node sizes, reflecting the highest academic performance (CGPA). This continuity suggests that students who occupy socially influential or structurally prominent positions within their groups tend to maintain superior academic standing over time.

Similarly, in Fig. 10, the in-degree network, students who were most frequently nominated by their peers continued to occupy the central regions of their respective networks. Their larger node sizes indicate that high-performing students were also the most socially recognised, consistent with the Year 2 statistical results showing a significant difference in CGPA between top and non-top students. This finding reinforces the notion that being trusted, consulted, or valued by others is not only a marker of social prominence but also positively associated with academic success.

Network visualisation by in-degree (year 2)

The closeness network in Fig. 11 depicts students who were most efficiently positioned to reach all others within their group. The top three closeness nodes (orange) were typically situated in the middle layers of their networks, bridging peripheral and central members. These individuals displayed slightly higher CGPA scores (Median = 3.37) compared to non-top students (Median = 3.06), though this difference was not statistically significant. This trend nonetheless suggests that students who maintain efficient communication channels and occupy integrative positions within their groups tend to perform modestly better academically, possibly due to more effective participation and access to collective knowledge.

Network visualisation by closeness (year 2)

In the betweenness network, Fig. 12 shows the top three students were again found in intermediary positions, connecting otherwise loosely linked peers or subclusters. These students demonstrated marginally higher academic performance (Median = 3.36 vs. 3.06), although the difference did not reach significance. The pattern mirrors the Year 1 findings, indicating that brokerage roles may yield small but consistent academic benefits through greater exposure to diverse perspectives and collaborative interactions.

Network visualisation by betweenness (year 2)

Across all panels, underperforming students (triangular nodes) consistently appeared at the periphery of their networks, characterized by smaller node sizes and fewer connections. Their structural isolation mirrors their lower academic performance, reinforcing the interpretation that social marginalization within the PBL group corresponds with reduced learning engagement or limited peer support. Supervisors (green nodes) once again remained peripheral, typically connecting to the student network through a single central, high-achieving student. This repeated configuration suggests that supervisors serve primarily as external facilitators, while central student leaders act as intermediaries linking faculty guidance with peer collaboration.

The Year 2 visual and statistical findings provide evidence that social prominence and academic performance are persistently intertwined in PBL settings. While in-degree centrality was significantly associated with CGPA, both closeness and betweenness exhibited positive but non-significant trends. The consistency of these patterns across years indicates that the relationship between academic achievement and social influence is stable, predictable, and structurally embedded within the PBL learning environment. Socially central students, whether through popularity, accessibility, or brokerage, tend to maintain stronger academic outcomes, underscoring the importance of collaborative integration and peer engagement in supporting sustained academic excellence.

Discussion

Social network analysis (SNA) has become a substantive methodological and conceptual lens for probing how peer ties, positionality, and group structure relate to learning, performance, and well-being in educational settings, including medical education [15, 23, 28]. This study shows that peer-learning structures in PBL differ measurably across cohorts and that social positioning can relate to academic performance. Relative to Year 1, the Year 2 networks were denser, exhibited shorter average path lengths and higher transitivity, alongside lower reciprocity and modularity. At the node level, Year 2 displayed significantly higher closeness and lower betweenness, indicating fewer brokerage bottlenecks and more direct access to peers.

However, a closer examination of the network structures reveals that even within highly cohesive PBL groups, underperforming students remain present. This suggests that strong social ties and a compact network do not automatically translate to effective cognitive scaffolding. Because our current comparative analysis yielded limited significant associations between bridging metrics (closeness and betweenness) and academic performance, structural data alone may not fully explain this phenomenon. It remains unclear from the network topologies whether these dense groups fail to support all members due to interactions lacking academic rigour, or because high and low achievers form isolated subgroups within the broader architecture. To identify the separation between low and high achievers, complex behavioural and cognitive dynamics that drive peer scaffolding are better explored by qualitative approaches. Within the current quantitative scope, importantly, only in Year 2 did peer recognition (in-degree) associate significantly with CGPA. These patterns suggest that in cohorts with greater exposure to PBL, those most recognised by peers are more closely aligned with higher achievement, reinforcing the need for facilitators to actively use these recognition metrics to identify and intervene with isolated students.

These findings also highlight the potential of SNA to function as a technology-enhanced assessment tool for capturing collaborative competence in PBL. Collaborative communication, peer engagement, and distributed participation are recognised components of professional behaviours in competency-based medical education (CBME), yet they remain difficult to assess using conventional tutor observations or checklists. By quantifying students’ interactional positions and patterns, SNA provides objective, scalable indicators that can complement existing assessment methods and offer deeper insight into how students participate in group learning.

The difference between the broker-dependent architecture in Year 1 and the more cohesive, clustered structure in Year 2 is consistent with cross-sectional variations in shared norms. Early PBL networks often depend on a few intermediaries to connect otherwise separate subgroups. With more exposure to the curriculum, clearer expectations promote triadic closure and redundant pathways, distributing information flow more broadly. These patterns align with prior work showing that homophily, clustering, and formal grouping practices systematically shape group cohesion and internal density [5, 15, 23]. The observed differences in transitivity, modularity, and reciprocity between Year 1 and Year 2 fit this comparative model: interactions in the later cohort are less dyadic and more embedded in overlapping clusters where multiple peers jointly construct solutions, reducing reliance on any single bridge.

The Shared Mental Model (SMM) theory proposes that understanding how members work towards a common goal, within an environment characterised by the knowledge structure of each team member, builds the foundation of effective teamwork [22]. Early in the PBL experience, when these foundational group frameworks are still forming, students may depend on a small number of peers to organise, summarise, or clarify ideas, resulting in a network with higher betweenness and modularity. However, as students gain experience through repeated negotiation and sharing of strategies, the higher transitivity and reduced reliance on broker roles seen in Year 2 closely reflects SMM, where students can more accurately predict the needs and behaviour of their team members and work more effectively [3]. From an assessment perspective, the structural differences observed across cohorts suggest that SNA metrics can reflect variations in teamwork and collaborative behaviours. As shared mental models differ, the resulting network patterns provide behaviourally grounded evidence of how students coordinate, engage, and contribute, dimensions that align with core professional competencies such as communication, teamwork, and collaborative problem-solving.

In the Year 2 PBL groups, inbound nominations plausibly capture a reputation signal. Being perceived as competent, reliable, or helpful draws questions and collaboration requests. Those interactions constitute usable social capital: recognised students explain, justify, and scaffold reasoning more often, shaping group sense-making while reinforcing their own understanding and ultimately enhancing their own academic performance [4, 20]. Students who perform well may naturally be recognised and nominated by their peers, just as recognition may also support stronger performance. These processes can form a positive loop and reinforce one another: being seen as a capable collaborator leads to more opportunities to explain and contribute, which in turn deepens understanding and can further improve academic outcomes. This aligns with findings by [13, 35], and [33] that peer acceptance and social position show a positive relationship with students’ academic performance. The strong association between in-degree and academic performance also provides preliminary criterion-related validity evidence for using peer-recognition based SNA indicators as part of an assessment framework. Because in-degree integrates multiple dimensions of perceived competence, such as reliability, clarity of explanation, and helpfulness, it may function as a meaningful, behaviour-anchored indicator of collaborative professionalism.

By contrast, the negligible, non-significant effects for closeness and betweenness must be interpreted with caution. While social-capital theory posits that students positioned centrally or acting as bridges may gain exposure to diverse perspectives [15, 20, 28], our statistical findings indicate this did not confer a reliable academic advantage. When networks are highly dense, many students enjoy short, redundant paths to peers, and the marginal benefit of being the most efficiently connected diminishes. Brokerage can bring access to diverse information in fragmented networks, but it also imposes coordination costs in time-bounded tutorials. In our setting, peer recognition (in-degree) appears to be the dominant, lower-friction channel linking social structure to achievement.

In practice, the ability of SNA to differentiate between forms of social prominence, such as recognition, access, and brokerage, offers educators a multidimensional lens for assessing collaborative skills. These indicators may help identify students who are highly engaged, those who dominate discussions, or those who are socially peripheral and potentially at risk of under-participation. Embedding SNA-informed feedback into PBL facilitation could therefore support more equitable participation and guide targeted developmental interventions. In sum, learning in PBL is structurally embedded. In denser networks, peer recognition aligns more strongly with achievement, while peripheral positions flag risk. Making facilitation network-aware by diagnosing isolation, distributing roles, and engineering equitable participation offers a practical route to better learning and fairer outcomes in medical education. Taken together, these insights illustrate how SNA can extend beyond descriptive analysis to serve as an innovative assessment modality. By transforming relational data into quantifiable indicators of collaborative behaviour, SNA offers a structured, technology-enhanced approach to evaluating key competencies in PBL.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study relied on self-reported peer nominations, which may be influenced by social desirability or existing friendship ties. The fixed “top three” nomination design also constrained the range of out-degree values and may have obscured subtler forms of outgoing influence; future work could examine alternative tie-construction strategies (e.g., top-five, uncapped nominations, or collaboration-score-only edges) to assess robustness. Furthermore, as a proof-of-concept, this study utilised a fixed 50:50 contribution ratio to combine ranked nominations and collaboration scores for edge weighting. While this served as a practical initial reference point, future research should perform systematic sensitivity analyses to establish the optimal weighting ratio, potentially employing robust techniques such as Area Under the Curve (AUC) evaluations to maximise the predictive validity of the network models. Second, observations were nested within PBL groups, meaning that data points were not independent; network-aware analytical approaches such as cluster-robust inference or permutation-based methods would strengthen future analyses.

Third, it is important to contextualise the statistical findings within the exploratory nature of this proof-of-concept study. To broadly assess the utility of Social Network Analysis in face-to-face PBL settings, we conducted multiple statistical comparisons across various node-level and network-level metrics. We opted not to apply family-wise error rate corrections, such as the Holm-Bonferroni method, as strict adjustments in early-stage exploratory research can overly penalise the data and obscure potentially meaningful signals. However, this approach inherently increases the risk of Type I errors (false positives). Consequently, the statistically significant differences reported in this study should be interpreted with caution. They serve as preliminary indicators of network dynamics rather than definitive conclusions, highlighting the need for future confirmatory studies to validate these initial observations.

Fourth, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference: it is not possible to determine whether social prominence leads to stronger academic performance or whether high-achieving students become more recognised over time. This study uses a cross-sectional design, whereas a longitudinal or dynamic network model would allow researchers to test how recognition and achievement co-evolve across semesters within the same cohort.

Finally, the study was conducted in a single pre-clinical cohort per year at one institution, which may limit transferability. Given that this study involved distinct cohorts for Year 1 and Year 2, future research should aim to track the same cohort of students as they advance to Year 2 to achieve a consistent comparative analysis. Multi-site replication and mixed-methods designs (e.g., tutor observations, reflective journals) would help clarify the mechanisms underlying these network patterns and support adaptation to other curricular cultures and settings.

Practical implications

While the application of SNA in medical education is still in its infancy, peer assessment remains a cornerstone of the curriculum. To objectively evaluate these collaborative skills, SNA can be utilised during continuous assessments not only to recognise high-performing students who facilitate group cohesion but also to identify socially isolated peers early in the academic year.

For successful routine implementation, several practical frameworks must be established. First, educators require automated or integrated data collection tools to streamline the generation of network metrics without adding administrative burden. Second, faculty development and training are essential to equip facilitators and mentors with the skills to accurately interpret network sociograms.

Finally, strict ethical safeguards must be implemented; peer nomination data should be anonymised where appropriate and used primarily for formative feedback and targeted student support, rather than as a punitive grading metric. Beyond educator-led assessment, sharing anonymised network sociograms with the cohort could provide a unique avenue for student self-assessment. By visualising group dynamics, students can engage in social referencing, allowing them to reflect on their own collaborative engagement compared to their peers. This awareness may serve as a metacognitive trigger, prompting students to adapt their learning strategies and improve their academic outcomes

Conclusion

This study provides empirical evidence that Social Network Analysis can serve as a meaningful, technology-enhanced assessment tool for evaluating collaborative competence in face-to-face PBL environments. Comparing students from Year 1 to Year 2, Year 2 interaction networks are more cohesive and recognition-based, and peer-recognized collaborative behaviour (in-degree) emerged as a significant indicator of academic achievement. These findings reinforce the notion that learning in PBL is socially situated and that students’ contributions to group discourse are integral to their educational development. By transforming relational data into quantifiable indicators, SNA offers an innovative method for assessing teamwork, communication, and professional behaviours—competencies that are essential for modern medical practice but difficult to measure using traditional approaches. Incorporating SNA into assessment systems can help educators identify isolated or struggling learners, promote equitable participation, and provide targeted formative feedback to enhance group functioning. As competency-based frameworks continue to guide medical education, SNA represents a promising and scalable addition to the toolkit for evaluating collaborative skills and supporting high-quality, patient-centred training.
