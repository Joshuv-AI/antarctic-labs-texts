Ductile fracture prediction in thin-walled structures through a novel damage model
Authors: Sunghoon Choi, Taehyun Park, Heuisoo Kim, Byeunggun Nam, Byoungwoo Ye, Dongchoul Kim
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Damage models have significantly advanced predictions of ductile fractures in large, thin-walled structures like automobiles, ships, and aircraft. However, accurately predicting these fractures remains challenging due to variations in strain localization, ranging from biaxial compression to tension. This study introduces a specialized damage model for shell elements, utilizing data from shear, uniaxial, and plane tension tests. Our model notably improves failure displacement prediction under shear stress by 50 % compared to the digital image correlation technique. Additionally, by employing ratios from necking to failure displacements observed in tension tests, we regularized the fracture locus for 3.0 mm shell elements, encompassing the spectrum from biaxial compression to tension. Our proposed damage model offers enhanced accuracy in predicting the occurrence, shape, and force-displacement characteristics of ductile fractures in simulated three-point bending tests, compared to the Walters model. This advancement holds promise for improving the precision of ductile fracture predictions in practical engineering applications.

Introduction

In the automotive industry, there is a rising demand for the precise prediction of ductile metal fractures using numerical methods [1]. To meet this need, damage models have been developed, employing three-dimensional solid elements to predict ductile material fractures. Gurson et al. established yield criteria and flow rules for ductile materials, considering factors such as the influence of hydrostatic stress on plastic yield and spherical void growth through simplified physical damage models [2]. Tvergaard and Needleman investigated necking and failure in round tensile test specimens using a damage model, observing void development leading to macroscopic crack formation and cup-cone fractures [3]. Johnson and Cook studied fracture characteristics under dynamic loading conditions in three different metals, considering the effects of strain rate, temperature, and pressure using a cumulative-damage fracture model [4]. Researchers extended the Gurson model to account for non-spherical void growth and coalescence in elastoplastic solids [5,6]. Bao and Wierzbicki explored stress triaxiality's influence on ductile fracture initiation, revealing shear-dominated fractures at negative triaxialities and void growth-dominated fractures at positive triaxialities [7,8]. They introduced a new three-dimensional fracture locus, considering hydrostatic pressure and the third deviatoric stress invariant, to refine their damage model [9]. Similarly, a damage evolution model based on Continuum Damage Mechanics (CDM) was introduced, incorporating hydrostatic pressure, stress triaxiality, and the third invariant of the deviatoric stress tensor to enhance fracture onset prediction accuracy compared to classical damage models [10]. Other studies extended the Gurson model, incorporating damage growth under shear-dominated stress states and arbitrary ellipsoidal voids [11,12]. An improved GTN (Gurson-Tvergaard-Needleman) model was proposed, introducing two distinct damage parameters—volumetric damage for spherical void growth and shear damage for elongated void growth—coupled in the yield function, for accurate fracture prediction under shear loading conditions [13]. Furthermore, Baltic et al. developed a damage model that combines metal plasticity and continuum ductile damage to predict strain localization and various failure mechanisms, effectively capturing different ductile fracture modes observed in experiments [14]. However, using solid elements in these damage models with a fine mesh smaller than the plate's thickness is impractical and inefficient for thin-walled structures with high aspect ratios, like crush boxes in automotive safety systems. Moreover, severe bending deformation in thin-walled structures challenges solid element accuracy, requiring multiple layers for accurate prediction of bending in the thickness direction [15,16].

Damage models employing shell elements for membrane deformation have been introduced to address limitations of solid elements. These models, developed through Marciniak-Kuczynsk analysis [[17], [18], [19]], focus on predicting ductile fractures solely in the uniaxial to biaxial tension range where membrane deformation occurs. However, actual failures of large thin-walled structures often involve both severe bending and membrane deformation [20,21]. Specifically, bending due to axial compression leads to non-proportional loading encompassing regions from biaxial compression to uniaxial tension [21]. Efforts have been made to develop damage models that consider these varied stress states. Some models aimed to prevent ductile fractures from biaxial compression to shear regions due to the absence of fractures under uniaxial compression [22]. Others addressed the shear to uniaxial tension regions, assuming failure strain equivalence [23]. Attempts using modified Mohr-Coulomb models and adjusted damage models for specific shell element sizes were made to predict ductile fracture in non-proportional loading [24]. Additionally, some models have utilized fracture forming limit curves to develop damage models with shell elements covering various stress states [25,26]. However, these damage models typically consider only fine shell element sizes, smaller than 0.5 mm mesh sizes. For large thin-walled structures, this size is too small to model large structures effectively due to computational power limitations.

Models that reduce the fracture locus with changing element size have been developed to consider strain localization as the element size increases from the region of uniaxial tension to biaxial tension [27]. However, these models are suitable only for uniaxial stress states because they use a forming limit diagram to reduce the fracture locus, and this diagram is only defined in the region of uniaxial tension to biaxial tension. When considering shear stress states, failure strain tends to be independent of mesh size due to the absence of strain localization [28]. Conversely, in biaxial tension stress states, failure strain reduction tends to be dependent on mesh size, owing to material-dependent strain localization [29]. Recent models attempted to characterize the effect of triaxiality on mesh size dependency using forming limit diagrams, yet limited to the region of uniaxial to biaxial tension [[30], [31], [32]]. These models require experiments across various triaxialities within this tension range to determine mesh size dependency [31,32]. In summary, an accurate prediction of ductile fracture in large thin-walled structures necessitates a damage model accounting for shell elements of varying thicknesses and mesh sizes. A precisely developed fracture locus is vital to address stress state regions beyond uniaxial to biaxial tension, encompassing biaxial compression to shear, shear to uniaxial tension and uniaxial tension to biaxial tension.

In this study, we present a comprehensive damage model for shell elements covering various thicknesses and mesh sizes, addressing stress states from biaxial compression to shear, shear to uniaxial tension, and uniaxial tension to biaxial tension. Our model accurately predicts ductile fractures in large thin-walled structures subjected to severe bending and membrane deformations, accounting for strain localization variations in regions from biaxial compression to biaxial tension. It efficiently develops a fracture locus considering the full spectrum of triaxialities, particularly in regions from biaxial compression to biaxial tension. Additionally, by using an extended force-displacement curve that accurately detects significant force drops—indicative of failure displacement—we can automatically identify the parameters of our proposed damage model, eliminating the need for a time-consuming and labor-intensive calibration process. Moreover, our damage model is specifically designed for large shell elements that surpass typical specimen sizes, allowing for the precise development of a fracture locus without further calibration. This is crucial, as identifying a regularization factor for the fracture locus of shell elements is impractical when their size substantially exceeds that of typical specimens. Validation of this proposed damage model was conducted using a two-component test on A steel (600 MPa grade dual-phase steel) and B steel (1200 MPa grade high-strength steel). Additionally, we assessed improvements by comparing our developed model with the Walters model using a three-point bending test.

Setup of finite element model for identification of damage model parameters

In this study, LS-DYNA/Explicit served as the finite element solver for simulating the uniaxial, plane, and shear tension tests, as well as the three-point bending test. The finite element models for these tests were constructed based on specimen geometries outlined in Fig. 1, Fig. 3, along with boundary conditions resembling those described in experiments in Section 2. The simulations of the uniaxial, plane, and shear tension tests employed 0.5 mm fully integrated shell elements, which are based on the Reissner-Mindlin kinematic assumption. This assumption implies 5 degrees of freedom (DOF) in the local coordinate system, yielding globally 6 DOF, with 2 × 2 integration points within the shell plane. A quarter model was used for the uniaxial and plane tension tests, with symmetry conditions set by restricting the movement of nodes in the symmetry plane. Loading was simulated using velocity-controlled conditions, matching the experimental velocity of 1 mm/min. The shear tension test utilized a half-model, with boundary conditions and loading conditions identical to those in the uniaxial and plane tests.

As detailed in Section 2.2, the hat-shaped specimen was subjected to uniaxial loading in the three-point bending test machine. For the numerical representation of this test, the finite element model encompassed two primary segments: the deformable specimen part and the rigid part featuring the impactor and jig. To simulate the impactor and jig of the three-point bending test, as depicted in Fig. 3, three rigid body plates were defined using a 3 mm fully integrated shell element, consistent with the simulation model of the specimen tests. These plates contacted the hat plate's top surface via a reference line at the hat plate center, establishing a general contact relationship to prevent penetration between the impactor and the hat plate. The base plate was constrained in the direction opposite to the impactor loading, along the reference line at each jig's center. The movement of impactor was controlled at a 60 mm/min displacement velocity to replicate experimental settings. Additionally, a coefficient of friction of 0.1 was applied between the impactor, hat plate, base plate, and jig to account for sliding contact between lubricated steel surfaces. In the deformable part, the hat plate and base plate were defined using 3 mm fully integrated shell elements, which is identical with simulation model of specimen tests, to enhance computational efficiency and connected by eight octagonal solid elements to simulate spot welding.

Development of damage model for large shell element

Development of fracture locus for shell element in biaxial compression to biaxial tension region

To develop a reliable damage model for shell elements, establishing a fracture locus is crucial. This locus calculates how damage evolves based on stress-related failure strain, as shown in Eq (20). Initially, we applied a fracture locus to these shell elements, derived from failure strain measurements using Digital Image Correlation (DIC). The results indicated relatively low errors in failure displacement, approximately 26 % in the uniaxial tests and 3 % in the plane tests for A steel, as seen in Fig. 4(a) and b. However, the error in predicting failure displacement significantly increased to 54 % in the shear test, as depicted in Fig. 4(c). For B steel, the error was notably higher in the shear test at 63 %, while the errors in the plane and uniaxial tests were 9 % and 6 %, respectively as shown in Fig. 5(a) and c. These discrepancies indicate an underestimation of failure displacement, especially in the shear tension tests for both A steel and B steel, with errors exceeding 54 %. The errors in the plane and uniaxial tension tests were attributed to the sensitivity of the DIC technique to node size, which is crucial for accurate strain measurement [38]. Specifically, for A steel, which fractures at higher strains than B steel, DIC tends to overestimate failure strains due to the requirement for smaller node sizes to precisely measure high strains. Additionally, the higher maximum forces in the uniaxial and plane test simulations are due to the limitations of the shell elements in calculating the necking in the thickness direction. To improve the accuracy of the fracture locus across regions spanning from biaxial compression to biaxial tension, we adopted a hybrid numerical-experimental approach. This involved integrating the Digital Image Correlation (DIC) technique with inverse analysis [39]. Using LS-OPT software (as shown in Fig. 6), we initiated an optimization procedure. This included setting initial values and defining the upper and lower boundaries for the damage model parameters, which encompassed failure strains in uniaxial, plane, and shear stress states, as well as the damage exponent and fading factor. The initial values for failure strains in these stress states were derived from the DIC technique. We set the upper boundary of failure strains for uniaxial and shear stress states to 5.0 and the lower boundary to 0.1. For the plane stress state, we applied a ratio relative to the uniaxial stress state due to the Hosford-Coulomb model consistently yielding a lower failure strain than the uniaxial stress state. Therefore, the upper and lower boundaries for plane stress failure strains were established at 0.8 and 0.1, respectively. For the damage exponent and fading factor, initial values, upper, and lower boundaries were set to unity, 5.0, and 0.5, respectively. Subsequently, using the D-optimal design method, we generated 10 distinctive combinations of damage model parameters for simulations of plane, shear, and uniaxial tension tests, totaling 30 simulations per iteration. A crucial step in this process was constructing a fracture locus using the Hosford-Coulomb model. This was based on failure strains in these stress states for the selected parameter combinations, as depicted in the steps between ‘sampling’ and ‘build metamodels’ in Fig. 6. MATLAB software was utilized to solve nonlinear equations (Eqns. (3), (4), (5), (6)) in order to determine parameters a, b, and c of the Hosford-Coulomb model, taking into account the failure strains in the stress states. This numerically derived fracture locus was then integrated into the finite element models of shell elements. Post the 30 simulations, we assessed the correlation between force-displacement curves from simulations and experimental tests of plane, shear, and uniaxial tension. Employing the mean square error method facilitated this correlation evaluation, summing the force differences between simulation and experiment. We then selected the combination of damage model parameters with the lowest mean square error from the initial set of 10 parameter combinations. If the error values met the termination criteria, the calibration process concluded. However, if not, we utilized the response surface methodology combined with the mean square error method to select a new combination of damage model parameters. This iterative process was repeated until the termination criteria was met, resulting in the optimization of damage model parameters.Fig. 6Flowchart depicting the calibration process for the damage model. Despite using a hybrid numerical-experimental method with LS-OPT optimization software, the failure displacement errors in simulations of A steel remained notably high—approximately 133 % for plane tests, 20 % for uniaxial tests, and 103 % for shear tests, as shown in Fig. 7. These errors surpassed those of the fracture locus derived from the DIC technique by 107 % in plane tests, 17 % in uniaxial tests, and 49 % in shear tests. In the case of B steel, the simulations exhibited failure displacement errors of approximately 189 % for plane tests, 47 % for uniaxial test, and 10 % for shear tests, as indicated in Fig. 8. Compared to the DIC-derived fracture locus, the errors in B steel increased by 180 % for plane tests and 41 % for uniaxial tests. This discrepancy stems from the limitations in measuring failure displacement experimentally. In such measurements, failure displacement is determined at the end of the force-displacement curve, typically identified by a discontinuous drop in force to zero. However, this method may not accurately capture the force and displacement at the moment of sudden ductile fractures in high-strength steel specimens, such as A steel and B steel. Conversely, in simulations, force and displacement can be continuously calculated from the onset of necking to the point where the force reaches zero. Therefore, this difference in measuring failure displacement, especially in scenarios involving high-strength steels, presents challenges in using the mean square error method to evaluate the correlation between simulations and experiments. Conclusion

This study presents a comprehensive damage model for large shell elements targeting metal alloys such as A steel (600 MPa grade dual-phase steel) and B steel (1200 MPa grade high-strength steel) evaluated through uniaxial, plane, and shear tension tests. The key findings of our research are as follows.1.Inaccuracy in DIC technique: The research uncovered an inherent inaccuracy in fracture loci derived solely from failure strain measurements using the Digital Image Correlation (DIC) technique, particularly in shear stress states, resulting in a 41 % underestimation in plastic strain compared to the actual shear strain of unity.2.Correction for shear strain: To overcome this limitation and enhance the fracture locus's accuracy in shear stress states, we introduced an equation to correct the shear strain as measured by DIC, integrated into a novel hybrid experimental-numerical identification method. This approach significantly improved failure displacement predictions by 52 % for A steel and 50 % for B steel in shear tension tests.3.Development of damage model for large shell element: We developed a damage model for large shell elements with varying mesh sizes and thicknesses by regularizing the fracture locus within the biaxial compression to biaxial tension range, grounded in uniaxial plane and shear specimen tests.4.Improved failure displacement predictions: Utilizing the necking-to-failure displacement ratio for calculating complexities in strain localization significantly improved failure displacement predictions by 43 % for A steel and 74 % for B steel in three-point bending tests using notched component specimens.5.Superior accuracy in predicting ductile fracture: Our proposed damage model exhibited superior accuracy in predicting ductile fracture occurrence and shapes in simulated three-point bending tests using 3.0 mm shell elements, surpassing the Walters model's performance. In three-point bending tests involving welded component specimens of B steel, the proposed damage model demonstrated a 14 % improvement in the coefficient of determination for the force-displacement curve compared to the Walters model.
