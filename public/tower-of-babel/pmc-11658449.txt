Whole slide image based prognosis prediction in rectal cancer using unsupervised artificial intelligence
Authors: Xuezhi Zhou, Jing Dai, Yizhan Lu, Qingqing Zhao, Yong Liu, Chang Wang, Zongya Zhao, Chong Wang, Zhixian Gao, Yi Yu, Yandong Zhao, Wuteng Cao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRectal cancer is a common cancer worldwide and lacks effective prognostic markers. The development of prognostic markers by computational pathology methods has attracted increasing attention. This paper aims to construct a prognostic signature from whole slide images for predicting progression-free survival (PFS) of rectal cancer through an unsupervised artificial intelligence algorithm.MethodsA total of 238 patients with rectal cancer from two datasets were collected for the development and validation of the prognostic signature. A tumor detection model was built by transfer learning. Then, on the basis of the tumor patches recognized by the tumor detection model, a convolutional autoencoder model was built for decoding the tumor patches into deep latent features. Next, on the basis of the deep latent features, the tumor patches were divided into different clusters. The cluster number and other hyperparameters were optimized by a nested cross-validation method. The percentage of each cluster from the patient’s tumor patches, which is hereafter called PCF, was calculated for prognostic signature construction. The prognostic signature was constructed by Cox proportional hazard regression with L2 regularization. Finally, bioinformatic analysis was performed to explore the underlying biological mechanisms of the PCFs.ResultsThe accuracy of the tumor detection model in distinguishing tumor patches from non-tumor patches achieved 99.3%. The optimal cluster number was determined to be 9. Therfore, 9 PCFs were calculated to construct the prognostic signature. The prognostic signature achieved a concordance index of 0.701 in the validation cohort. The Kaplan-Meier survival curves showed the prognostic signature had good risk stratification ability. Through the bioinformatic analysis, several PCF-associated genes were identified. These genes were enriched in various gene ontology terms.ConclusionThe developed prognostic signature can effectively predict PFS in patients with rectal cancer and exploration of the underlying biological mechanisms may help to promote its clinical translation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-024-13292-5.

Introduction

Rectal cancer is a common malignancy worldwide, posing significant challenges to healthcare systems and necessitating effective prognostic strategies to guide clinical decision-making and improve patient outcomes [1]. Prognostic models serve as invaluable tools for predicting disease progression, recurrence, and overall survival, thereby aiding clinicians in tailoring treatment strategies to individual patients [2]. However, the accuracy and reliability of existing prognostic models for rectal cancer remain suboptimal, primarily due to limitations associated with traditional pathological biomarkers and the inherent heterogeneity of the disease [3].

Conventional pathological biomarkers, such as tumor stage, grade, lymph node involvement, and histological subtype, have long been the cornerstone of rectal cancer prognostication [4]. While these markers provide valuable insights into disease severity and aggressiveness, their predictive ability is often limited by several factors. Firstly, these biomarkers are inherently subjective, relying on manual interpretation and prone to interobserver variability, which can lead to inconsistencies in prognostic assessments [5]. Moreover, these markers fail to capture the full spectrum of tumor biology and behavior, as rectal cancer is characterized by extensive molecular and phenotypic heterogeneity [6].

Recent advancements in computational pathology have revolutionized cancer diagnostics and prognostics, offering novel approaches to overcome the limitations of conventional biomarkers [7]. Deep learning has emerged as a powerful tool for analyzing complex histopathological images and extracting meaningful insights from large-scale datasets [8]. Deep learning algorithms can autonomously learn and identify intricate patterns and features within histological slides that may elude conventional pathological examination, thereby providing valuable prognostic information [9, 10]. For example, Kather et al. trained a deep learning model to identify nine tissue types of the tumor microenvironment in whole slide images (WSIs) of colorectal cancer patients and developed an imaging biomarker for prognosis prediction [11]. Other end-to-end prognostic models have also been developed on the basis of WSIs using multiple instance learning methods [12, 13]. To create a deep learning based prognostic model based on WSIs, the images must be cropped into multiple patches. Previous studies have attempted to learn prognostic features directly from patches by supervised deep learning [14, 15] or to learn from transfer learning features [12, 16, 17]. Thus, aggregating patch-level features into patient-level features is crucial to the predictive accuracy. However, at present, there is no stable and reliable feature aggregation scheme for prognosis prediction models.

Unsupervised methods offer a compelling avenue for exploring the complex histopathological features of rectal cancer. By autonomously identifying latent structures and patterns within image datasets, unsupervised algorithms can unveil novel biomarkers and phenotypic signatures associated with disease prognosis, without the need for annotated data [18, 19]. This data-driven approach has the potential to uncover previously unrecognized prognostic factors and refine existing models, ultimately improving the accuracy and reliability of prognosis predictions in rectal cancer [20]. Despite the considerable promise of deep learning-based prognostic models, the interpretability of deep learning algorithms is the critical consideration that remains to be addressed, particularly in the context of medical decision-making, where clinicians require insight into model predictions and underlying biological mechanisms [21].

Our study aims to develop a novel prognostic model for rectal cancer using deep learning-based unsupervised methods. By harnessing the power of artificial intelligence techniques to analyze WSIs and uncover latent prognostic factors, we seek to enhance the accuracy and reliability of prognosis predictions in rectal cancer. Moreover, by investigating the interpretation of latent prognostic factors via bioinformatics analysis, we aspire to pave the way for personalized treatment strategies and improve patient outcomes in rectal cancer management. Materials and methods

Data acquisition

This retrospective study was approved by the ethics committee of the Sixth Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (SAHSYU), and waived the informed consent requirement. A total of 238 patients with rectal cancer were enrolled, of which 147 patients were collected from The Cancer Genome Atlas rectal adenocarcinoma (TCGA-READ) project and 91 patients were collected from SAHSYU. Progression-free survival (PFS) information, clinical variables including age, sex, pathological T-stage and pathological N-stage, and hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) stained WSIs of the enrolled patients are available. Gene expression data from TCGA-READ cohort measured by RNA sequencing data were also collected for bioinformatic analysis. The WSIs were cut into non-overlapped patches with a size of 224 × 224 pixel2 at 20 × (0.5 μm/pixel) magnification for further analysis. Color normalization was performed using “Macenko” method from the “staintools” library in Python to reduce undesirable staining deviation [22]. The “Macenko” method was based on color deconvolution and principal component analysis (PCA). This method analyzes the color distribution in images to extract the main dye components and standardizes them to predefined reference standards, thereby achieving color consistency across different images.

Tumor detection

A tumor patch detection model was developed using the public NCT-CRC-HE-100 K dataset and validated in the public NCT-CRC-HE-7 K dataset. These two datasets were developed by Kather et al. [11] and contain 100,000 and 7,000 patches cut from H&E-stained WSIs of patients with colorectal cancer, respectively. These patches were annotated into 9 classes including adipose (ADI), background (BACK), debris (DEB), lymphocyte (LYM), mucus (MUC), smooth muscle (MUS), normal colonic mucosa (NORM), cancer-associated stroma (STR), and tumor epithelium (TUM). Kather et al. demonstrated that training a VGG19 model [23] using transfer learning strategy achieved the best performance in the two datasets. Thus, we retrained the tumor patch detection model using VGG19 architecture by Tensorflow (v 2.7.0) in NCT-CRC-HE-100 K dataset. The hyperparameters of the training process were same as Kather described [11].

Unsupervised clustering by convolutional autoencoder

A convolutional autoencoder (CAE) was developed from the tumor patches of the TCGA-READ dataset by Tensorflow (v 2.7.0). The CAE consists of two parts, an encoder and a decoder. The encoder consists of BatchNormalization, MaxPooling2D and Conv2D layers, and the decoder consists of UpSampling2D, BatchNormalization and Conv2D layers as depicted in Fig. 2. The CAE was trained with hyperparameters as follows: Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.0001, the number of epochs was set as 30 with early stopping strategy, batch size was 24, loss function was the mean squared error (MSE). The latent deep feature, which was extracted from the last layer of the encoder by average pooling, was used for unsupervised K-means clustering [24]. Firstly, TCGA-READ cohort was randomly divided into a training cohort and an internal validation cohort at a ratio of 7:3. The SAHSYU cohort served as an external validation cohort. Then, we performed the clustering by using the mini-batch K-means method which is an enhanced version of the K-means method for handling large amounts of data. Specifically, we fitted the mini-batch K-means model using the patches of the training cohort to obtain the cluster centers. Then, we assigned each patch of the validation cohorts to the nearest cluster center obtained in the training cohort. Importantly, during the clustering process, we clustered the tumor patches from all samples of the training cohort together, rather than clustering each sample individually. Therefore, the assigned clusters in the validation cohorts were consistent with those in the training cohort. The cluster number was initially set to a list of 2, 3, 4, …, 20, and the best cluster number was determined by 3-fold cross-validation when developed the prognostic signature as described below.

In summary, a nested 3-fold cross-validation was performed to determine the optimal cluster number and the L2 regularization coefficient. An outer cross-validation procedure was performed to provide a performance estimation that can be used to select the optimal cluster number. In each fold of the outer cross-validation, the L2 regularization coefficient of the model was tuned independently to maximize the inner cross-validation estimate generalization performance. After the optimal cluster number was determined, a 3-fold cross-validation was reperformed in the training cohort to select the optimal L2 regularization coefficient. Finally, a prognostic model with the optimal cluster number and L2 regularization coefficient was developed in the training cohort and validated in the internal validation cohort and external validation cohort. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis with log-rank test was performed to validate the predictive ability of prognostic signature. The optimal cutoff value of the prognostic signature was determined by X-tile software [27] in the training cohort to allocate patients into high-risk and low-risk groups, and the same cutoff value was used in the internal validation and external validation cohorts.

In addition, another prognostic model was also developed directly using the latent deep feature. Firstly, the patch-level features were aggregated into patient-level features using average pooling operation. Then, a Cox proportional hazard regression model with L1 regularization was developed using the patient-level features in the training cohort and validated in the two validation cohorts. This modeling process was similar to the study of Sun et al. [20].

Bioinformatics analysis

Because we only have access to the level 3 data of TCGA, the obtained mRNA expression level read counts had been normalized by using three commonly used methods: fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM), upper quartile normalized FPKM (FPKM-UQ), and transcripts per million (TPM). The more detailed mRNA quantification analysis pipeline can be found in the link (https://docs.gdc.cancer.gov/Data/Bioinformatics_Pipelines/Expression_mRNA_Pipeline/). We selected to use the FPKM-UQ data for further analysis, because of that FPKM-UQ is a modified FPKM calculation in which the protein coding gene in the 75th percentile position is substituted for the sequencing quantity. FPKM-UQ can provide a more stable value than including the noisier genes at the extremes. Then, we renamed the identifiers of probes in the downloaded FPKM-UQ data to gene symbols according to the “genecode_v22_annotation_gene_probeMap” document downloaded from TCGA. Spearman correlation analysis was performed to identify the genes significantly associated with each of the PCFs in the training cohort and internal validation cohort, respectively. P-value < 0.05 in the Spearman correlation analysis was considered as significant association. The genes significantly associated with the PCF in both the training cohort and internal validation cohort can be considered as stable PCF associated genes. The stable PCF-associated genes were subsequently enriched to find significant pathways using R package “clusterProfiler” by querying Gene Ontology (GO) annotation database. False discovery rate (FDR)-adjusted P < 0.05 indicated significant enrichment. The significantly enriched biologic functions were used to annotate the PCF. At last, linear regression with least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) was performed to predict the PCF using the gene expression data of the stable PCF associated genes in the training cohort. LASSO is also called L1 regularization, which can be used as an embedded feaure selection method. A 3-fold cross-validation was performed in the training cohort to select the optimal L1 regularization coefficient. The genes selected by LASSO can be considered as potential driver genes for the PCF. These linear regression models with LASSO established for predicting all PCFs were also validated in the internal validation cohort.

Unsupervised clustering by convolutional autoencoder

We randomly selected 99% of the tumor patches from TCGA-READ cohort to train the CAE model and used the rest 1% tumor patches for performance validation and early stopping. From the MSE curves (Fig. 3C) of the CAE model in the training and validation epochs, the CAE was well trained after 25 epochs. Then we tested the CAE model in SAHSYU cohort and got MSE of 0.0024, indicating a well tumor patches reconstruction performance. Finally, through 3-fold nested cross-validation in the training cohort, the optimal cluster number was determined to be 9, as shown in Fig. 3D.

Development and validation of prognostic signature

The distributions of the 9 PCFs in the TCGA-READ cohort and SAHSYU cohort are depicted in Fig. 3E-F. All PCFs presented a long-tail distribution state, that is, patients with smaller PCF values account for a larger proportion. In the two cohorts, the correlation between different PCFs is relatively stable. For example, there is a significant negative correlation between the PCF4 and PCF5 in both the TCGA-READ and SAHSYU cohorts. The optimal L2 regularization coefficient for prognostic signature development was determined to be 0.8594487, as shown in Fig. 3G. The prognostic signature (model a) achieved a c-index of 0.748, 0.719 and 0.701 in the training cohort, internal validation cohort and external validation cohort, respectively. For comparison, the model developed directly using the latent deep feature (model b) presented worse performance, with a c-index of 0.675, 0.612 and 0.630 in the training cohort, internal validation cohort and external validation cohort, respectively (Table S1). The cutoff value for risk stratification was set as −0.07398763. The Kaplan-Meier survival curves (Fig. 4A-C) revealed that in all cohorts the high-risk group presented significant shorter PFS time than that of the low risk group, which also indicated the developed prognostic signature had good risk stratification ability. Figure 4D-E displayed the WSIs of two patients with different PFS. The tumor cluster maps and bar graphs revealed obvious difference in the distributions of PCFs between the two patients.

Bioinformatics analysis

By performing correlation analysis in the training and internal validation cohorts, we obtained a significantly associated gene set for each of the PCFs. The GO enrichment analysis revealed that the PCF-associated genes showed various of enrichment patterns of GO terms. For example, 2447 genes (Fig. 5A) were found associated with PCF0 and enriched in a series of GO terms including “RNA splicing”, “proteasomal protein catabolic process”, “cytoplasmic translation”, “U2-type spliceosomal complex”, “ribonucleoprotein complex binding” and so on (Fig. 5B). Moreover, we identified 37 potential driver genes (Fig. 5C) that can well predict PCF0 in the training and internal validation cohorts (Fig. 5D-E). More detailed results for the other PCFs were described in the Appendix.

Discussion

In the past decade, machine learning methods including radiomics and deep learning have promoted the study of imaging markers for prognosis prediction [28, 29]. This study is an extension of the research field of prognosis prediction in rectal cancer. In this study, we proposed the prognostic signature based on WSIs of rectal cancer using unsupervised deep learning. The results showed the developed prognostic signature was a significant prognostic factor for patients with rectal cancer. Patients with high signature values tend to progress or die early after surgery.

Pathological images, as the gold standard for rectal cancer diagnosis, play a pivotal role in prognosis prediction. Recent advancements in computational pathology, leveraging computer vision and artificial intelligence techniques, have shown great potential in enhancing the accuracy and efficiency of prognosis prediction. Most of these studies developed prognostic models using the transfer learning strategy. They extracted patch-level features from pretrained models such as VGG-16 network [30], ResNet-18 [31] and Xception [32]. Our previous study also explored the prognostic value of different pre-trained feature extractors [17], but the result showed that not every feature extractor could exhibit good performance. The feature extractor without fine-tuning on WSI tasks may be one of the factors affecting performance.

One of the innovations of this study is the use of a CAE as feature extractor. We proposed a hypothesis that the CAE trained on the tumor patches of the rectal cancer more easily characterizes the tumor than the pretrained feature extractors without fine-tuning. Wang et al. [33] trained a CAE as a feature extractor for prognosis prediction in ovarian cancer and achieved satisfactory results, which also supports our hypothesis. The prognostic model developed with the CAE features in our study achieved a c-index of 0.630 in the external validation cohort, which demonstrated the prognostic value of the CAE features. However, the c-index of 0.630 was a little low. The feature aggregation method may be the key to affecting the performance of the model, after all, average pooling is too rough. Recent studies have shown that attention-based feature aggregation for WSI analysis is a promising approach [28, 34, 35]. Different weight coefficients are assigned to each patch based on the attention mechanism, so the heterogeneity between individuals can be better highlighted when transforming patch-level features to patient-level features. In the future, we will attempt to use this feature aggregation method, hoping to improve the results, hoping to improve the prediction.

Another innovation of this study is the use of unsupervised clustering for tumor heterogeneity characterization. We firstly used K-means to create 9 cluster phenotypes for the tumor patches. Furthermore, we calculated the proportion of different cluster phenotypes on each WSI to quantify the heterogeneity among individuals. Compared with average pooling for patch-level features aggregation, the PCFs we calculated were more efficient for prognosis prediction. This cluster-based quantification of tumor heterogeneity has also been used in previous studies. For example, Takashina et al. developed a model that can predict lymph node metastasis of T1 colorectal cancer from WSIs using the unsupervised clustering method [36]. Liu et al. used an unsupervised clustering method to generated 47 histomorphologic phenotype clusters (HPCs) from WSIs, and developed a Cox regression model with L2 regularization to investigate the associations between the HPCs and overall survival in colon cancer [37]. Both of the above studies determined the cluster number before establishing prognostic models. Our study considered the cluster number as a hyperparameter that can be determined by cross-validation during the model establishment. Therefore, the cluster number we determined are more closely related to prognosis.

Despite achieving good performance, the interpretability of prognostic model still poses a challenge for its clinical application. To address this issue, we tried to explore of the biological associations of our model by using biomolecular information from the TCGA dataset. Some studies have attempted to uncover the biological meaning of their proposed prognostic model and obtained promising results. For example, Sun et al. [38] and Yan et al. [39] have identified some driving pathways and key genes for the radiomic or deep learning signatures in glioblastoma. Zhao et al. [40] also explored the underlying biological interpretability for prognosis associated radiomic features in clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) using radiogenomics. Moreover, they developed and validated a well performed multigene signature for prognosis prediction using the radiomics associated genes, which further demonstrated these genes may be involved in key biological processes that contribute to ccRCC metastasis [40]. In our study, we screened a set of significantly associated genes for each PCF, and performed gene set enrichment analysis for the associated gene sets. Furthermore, we identified a set of potential driver genes that can well fit the PCF distribution. We believe that changes in gene expression result in different imaging phenotypes. The discovery of the relationship between imaging phenotypes and genetic changes will help to explore the potential molecular mechanisms more economically in the future, after all, testing large amounts of genetic data directly is expensive.

This study also has some limitations. Firstly, compared to TCGA-READ cohort, the relatively short follow-up time may lead to large validation bias in the external validation cohort. Secondly, owning to the high heterogeneity of WSIs, it is necessary to further expand the sample size to achieve more accurate prediction accuracy for the prognostic model. Thirdly, although we explored the associations between cluster phenotypes and genetic changes in TCGA dataset, the associations should be validated in other cohorts. Finally, transfer learning for prognosis prediction based on digital pathology foundation model is worth studying.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we employed unsupervised approaches to identify some cluster phenotypes on WSIs, which can effectively predict PFS for rectal cancer patients. The potential biological mechanisms of these cluster phenotypes were primarily explored, whereas these results should be further validated.
