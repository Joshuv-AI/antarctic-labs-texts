Complex dorsal metacarpophalangeal joint dislocation in a 15-year-old boy
Authors: Rebecca Z Xu, Behdad Ravarian, Stephanie Mun Kim, Ryan Paul
Published: 2026

A 15-year-old boy presented to the emergency department with a painful, deformed index finger after a sports-related finger hyperextension injury. Radiographs showed a dorsal dislocation of his index metacarpophalangeal (MCP) joint (Figure 1). Multiple attempts at closed reduction using direct longitudinal traction failed, and a hand surgeon was consulted. Surgery was required to reduce the joint and confirmed a complex MCP joint dislocation with trapped soft tissues that prevented reduction, known as a Kaplan lesion. Intraoperatively, the volar plate was seen to be interpositioned dorsal to the metacarpal head, and the head of the metacarpal was buttonholed in the volar palm fascia, with the flexor tendons trapped ulnarly and lumbricals radially. Postoperative radiographs confirmed concentric reduction of the MCP joint (Appendix 1, available at www.cmaj.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cmaj.251814/tab-related-content).

(A) Anterior–posterior and (B) lateral radiographic images of the left index finger of a 15-year-old boy showing dorsal dislocation at the metacarpophalangeal joint. The proximal phalanx (white arrow) is not articulating with the metacarpal head (asterisk).

Overall, MCP joint dislocations are rare (fewer than 1 per 100 000 people/year) and represent a small proportion of finger dislocations; the index finger is most commonly affected.1,2 These dislocations usually occur following a fall on an outstretched hand leading to forced hyperextension of the fingers or thumb; they may be missed if they are subtle or if the appropriate 3-view radiographs are not obtained. Most dorsal MCP dislocations can be treated nonoperatively with closed reduction.1–3 The recommended technique to reduce the proximal phalanx over the metacarpal head is to flex the wrist and interphalangeal joints to relax volar soft tissues, and apply dorsal-to-volar pressure to the base of the proximal phalanx (Appendix 1). It is important to avoid direct longitudinal traction, which can pull the volar plate into the joint, incarcerate the interposed tissue, and convert a simple dislocation into a complex, irreducible one with damaged cartilage.1,3 Post-reduction radiographs should confirm a concentric, stable joint. The finger should be immobilized in a dorsal blocking splint with the MCP joints flexed to 90°, and the patient should be followed up by a hand specialist.1,2 Most patients with simple MCP dislocation treated this way recover near-normal motion and function. Complex cases with volar plate entrapment often require surgery, and patients are at risk for stiffness, pain, or degenerative arthritis.1,2
