Coaching experiences, interest, and preferences among early career researchers
Authors: Lucy O. Alejandro, Monica Kowalczyk, Julia Chael, Valerie G. Press, Rachel K. Wolfson, Vineet M. Arora, Anna Volerman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCoaching is an innovative approach to career development in the biomedical sciences, distinct from mentorship and advising. Past research suggests coaching can improve the retention and success of early career researchers (ECRs), especially those underrepresented. Despite potential benefits of coaching, little is known about ECRs’ perceptions of coaching. This study assessed ECRs’ experiences, interests, and preferences in coaching and examined differences between demographic and professional groups.MethodsAs part of a national cross-sectional study in fall 2021 to query strategies to support ECRs after the COVID-19 pandemic, the authors surveyed ECRs who held National Institutes of Health (NIH) F32 or K-level grants in 2020. Descriptive statistics, thematic analyses, chi-square tests, and logistic regressions summarized data and characterized differences by demographic and professional characteristics.ResultsOf 4,440 eligible ECRs, 1,587 ECRs (35.7%) completed the survey, and 1,481 (33.4%) met inclusion criteria for this study. About one in six respondents (16%) previously participated in coaching. Respondents described diverse coaching experiences that varied in effectiveness. About half reported interest in future coaching (52.4%). Respondents who identify as gender and sexual minority (GSM) and heterosexual females reported greater coaching interest than heterosexual males (60.5% vs. 56.5% vs. 43.3%, p < 0.001), which remained significant in adjusted models (GSM: OR 2.03, 95% CI [1.34, 3.07]; heterosexual females: OR 1.67, 95% CI [1.32, 2.12]). Underrepresented racial/ethnic respondents were more interested in coaching than White and Asian respondents (60.0% vs. 51.9%, p = 0.03), though this finding did not remain significant in adjusted models. Most respondents identified certain topics as important for coaching: project management (66.4%), team management (64.8%), leadership (62.7%), grant writing (55.2%), and financial management (50.3%). Topic importance varied by demographic and professional characteristics.ConclusionThis study demonstrated interest in coaching among ECRs, especially sex/gender/sexual and racial/ethnic minorities. Tailored coaching may be well-received among ECRs and could contribute to efforts supporting a diverse biomedical workforce.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-026-10036-w.

Background

Academic institutions and professional organizations must consider how to best support the professional development of diverse researchers [1, 2], as past studies demonstrate that diverse teams enhance the innovation, novelty, frequency, and significance of scientific research [3]. Coaching may be a key strategy for professional development. While commonly utilized in corporate settings, coaching is an emerging approach in the biomedical sciences [4]. Coaching supports individuals in achieving their fullest potential through reflection and self-actualization. In this model, trained coaches help clients gain insights into their assumptions and identify specific actions to overcome challenges and achieve goals [5]. Unlike mentorship or advising, in which more experienced individuals or experts direct clients’ activity [6], coaching is client-driven. Thus, coaches only require coaching expertise and do not necessarily require domain expertise that matches the client [7]. Coaches are also in a unique position to support researchers without introducing role tensions or conflicts of interest that may arise with mentors, who are often superiors in one’s field and thus can impact one’s future career opportunities [4]. Thus, coaching may fill gaps that are unfilled by mentorship or advising alone.

Prior studies in medicine, public health, and business have demonstrated that coaching reduces burnout and stress [8], builds resilience [9], promotes skill development, as well as improves self-awareness, motivation, and capacity to take action [10, 11]. In the biomedical sciences specifically, past research suggests that coaching can help increase the retention and success of underrepresented graduate students, research trainees, and early career researchers (ECRs) [12–16]. The early career stage is foundational for later research success, promotion, and overall career trajectory. Yet, disparities in representation, funding, and institutional support are documented among ECRs, including by sex, gender identity, sexual orientation, and race/ethnicity [1, 17–21]. These early career disparities may explain why women and people of color are underrepresented among National Institutes of Health (NIH)-funded researchers [22–27]. Thus, ECRs are an important sub-population for intervention given the disparities seen at that stage and their position as future leaders in biomedicine [28].

Despite growing data about the effectiveness of coaching in the biomedical sciences, little is known about ECRs’ perceptions of coaching. It is important for educators and leaders to listen to and incorporate the perspectives of ECRs when planning future coaching programs to ensure such programs are wanted, relevant, effective, and researcher-centered. To that end, this study conceptualized ECR’s perceptions in terms of prior coaching experiences, reported interest in future coaching, and preferences for future coaching. Additionally, given the hypothesized link between ECR identity and professional needs, this study explored differences in interest and preferences among ECRs by demographic and professional characteristics. Understanding the needs and preferences for coaching among ECRs can help inform future implementation of coaching programs to support the diversity of the biomedical workforce.

Methods

This national cross-sectional study surveyed ECRs to evaluate career impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic and strategies needed to support career growth. We defined ECRs as individuals with active F32 (postdoctoral fellowship) and K-level (career development) awards from the NIH in 2020 [29]. We obtained awardee names and email addresses using publicly available data. The Institutional Review Board deemed this study exempt.

Survey

A team with expertise in survey methodology, workforce inequities, and research career trajectories designed an anonymous 49-question survey intended to assess impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic on ECRs and areas for intervention (full survey available in Alejandro et al.) [30]. We sent survey invitations to all 4,440 ECRs between September and December 2021 via REDCap, with up to five email reminders over two months. The introduction to the survey included the purpose of the study, anonymous nature of the data, and plan to share results in aggregate. Individuals consented to participation by advancing to the survey questions. Respondents received $20 electronic gift cards.

This manuscript focuses on a distinct survey section with seven questions about coaching. At the start of the section, we defined coaching to ensure a shared understanding: “a form of development in which an experienced person, called a coach, supports an individual in working to achieve goals, develop individual skill sets, and maximize potential.” [31].

First, we assessed past experiences with coaching with a binary (yes/no) question: “Have you previously participated in coaching?”. An open-ended question then prompted respondents to share information about past coaching: “Tell us about the coaching experience. We welcome information about structure (length, frequency, one-on-one or group), topics covered, benefits, challenges, and other relevant details”. Next, we assessed coaching interest with the question, “How likely would you be to take part in a coaching program?” on a five-point Likert scale (very unlikely/unlikely/neutral/likely/very likely). For individuals indicating coaching interest (likely or very likely), we assessed coaching preferences using a series of multiple-choice questions regarding coaching type (one-on-one, small group of researchers from one institution with one coach, small group of researchers with same research topic of methods (different institutions) with one coach, mixed one-on-one and small group, other, no preference), modality (in-person, virtual, no preference), and frequency (once a week, every other week, once a month, quarterly, or other). Finally, a multiple-response question asked all respondents about the most important topics for coaching (grant writing, project management, financial management, team management, personality assessments, leadership, imposter syndrome, networking, other). The survey questions collected demographic information (sex/gender identity, sexual orientation, race, ethnicity) and professional characteristics (graduation year with highest professional degree, degree, research field, award type, academic rank).

Data analysis

We used graduation year with highest professional degree as a proxy variable for age. We operationalized sex/gender identity and sexual orientation as heterosexual female, heterosexual male, and gender and sexual minority (GSM; non-binary and/or non-heterosexual). We classified race/ethnicity as non-Hispanic White; non-Hispanic Black or African American; non-Hispanic Asian; Hispanic, Latino, or Spanish origin; and multiracial or other race. Underrepresented racial/ethnic groups included non-White and non-Asian groups, in alignment with a 2019 National Science Foundation report [32]. We operationalized degree as PhD, MD/DO/MBBS (or equivalent), MD/PhD, or other. We classified research field as laboratory, clinical, epidemiology/public health, or other. We classified award type as K or F. Finally, we categorized academic rank as associate professor/professor, assistant professor, instructor, and none/other. We re-coded coaching interest as a binary variable: interested (very likely or likely to participate) versus not interested (neutral, unlikely, or very unlikely). All other questions were analyzed without changes.

Descriptive statistics describe respondents’ demographic and professional characteristics, coaching experience, coaching interest, and coaching preferences. Chi-square tests compared coaching interest and preferred coaching topics by demographic and professional characteristics, including sex/gender identity and sexual orientation, race/ethnicity, year of highest degree, degree, research field, award type, and academic rank. Logistic regressions assessed whether differences in coaching interest persisted after adjusting for sex/gender identity and sexual orientation, race/ethnicity, degree, research field, award type, and academic rank. Significance was defined as p < 0.05. Analyses used SAS software 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, North Carolina).

For open-ended questions, we analyzed responses about past coaching experiences using grounded theory, comparing similarities between them as they arose organically from participant responses [33]. Initially, two researchers (LA, JC) reviewed all open-ended responses and proposed a coding framework with themes and sub-themes. The classification of participant responses was discussed iteratively among the research team (LA, JC, MK, AV) to refine and eliminate potential discrepancies. Once the coding framework was developed, a researcher (LA) then applied the framework across all responses. This coding was reviewed with the research team to ensure agreement. It was also validated by peer debriefing, including with individuals with lived experience as biomedical researchers and co-authors who have expertise in professional development in the biomedical workforce (VA, VGP, AV, RW).

Results

Demographic and professional characteristics

Of 4,440 eligible ECRs, 35.7% (n = 1587) responded to the survey, and 33.4% (n = 1481) met the inclusion criteria for this study (Table 1). Over half were heterosexual females (58.1%, n = 860), and approximately 8.4% (n = 124) identified as GSM. The majority identified as White (66.1%, n = 979), followed by Asian (16.3%, n = 241). Underrepresented ECRs by race/ethnicity comprised 14.8% (n = 220) of respondents.

Respondents most commonly graduated between 2011 and 2015 (39.9%, n = 591). Most were K awardees (83.1%, n = 1230), PhD degree-holders (60.3%, n = 893), and assistant professors (60.2%, n = 891). Comparable numbers of respondents worked in laboratory research (37.0%, n = 548) and clinical research (34.2%, n = 506), while 365 (24.7%) worked in epidemiology/public health.

Respondents shared coaching occurred weekly, biweekly, or monthly. They included one-on-one and group formats. The modalities included in-person or virtual programming. Coaching varied in topic focus. Respondents described professional-oriented topics, including writing grants and attaining career goals. Some described personal-oriented topics, such as managing emotions, stress, and burnout. For example, one respondent was coached in “techniques to try and manage our emotions and uncertainties surrounding the [COVID-19] pandemic.” Some respondents participated in coaching centered on specific demographic groups, such as women or underrepresented racial/ethnic minorities. One respondent said, “We discussed work-life balance, managing conflict, and other topics that affect women in faculty roles.” Another respondent shared, “We talk about the transition from postdoc to faculty and challenges facing URM [underrepresented minority] scientists.”

Respondents shared that coaching varied in effectiveness. Some described coaching as helpful. One respondent stated, “I found it incredibly beneficial. It helped increase my confidence, define my values, and ensure I was spending my time in a way aligned with them.” Other ECRs shared that coaching was not helpful, expressing dissatisfaction. A respondent shared, “I tried a few sessions but it wasn’t for me and I didn’t feel like I got anything out of it so I discontinued.” Others expressed ambivalent outcomes, with one respondent stating, “It has been semi-helpful but not specific for academics.”

Respondents detailed challenges to effective coaching. Some respondents stated that coaching was poorly executed. For example, a respondent stated, “[coaching] was too basic. Not directive enough.” Another respondent shared, “It was taught by an administrator who really had no idea what we were going through.” Respondents also described how coaching offered no clear solutions in light of greater barriers. For example, one respondent stated that coaching “foster[ed] a sense of community but ultimately was a little bit depressing… because there were not a lot of constructive solutions to systemic problems.” Finally, logistical barriers like physical and time constraints led to a lack of accessibility and sustainability. For example, one respondent wrote, “clinical responsibilities meant I missed a couple sessions.”

Coaching interest

Among all respondents, 52.4% (n = 776) indicated they were interested in future coaching (Fig. 1).

Coaching interest varied by demographic and professional characteristics (Fig. 1; Supplemental Digital Appendix Table A and B). GSM respondents, followed by heterosexual females, were more interested in future coaching than heterosexual males (60.5% vs. 56.5% vs. 43.3%, p < 0.001), which remained significant after adjusting for race/ethnicity, research field, award type, and academic rank (GSM: OR 2.03, 95% CI [1.34, 3.07]; heterosexual females: OR 1.67; 95% CI [1.32, 2.12]). Underrepresented racial/ethnic respondents were more interested in future coaching than White and Asian respondents (60.0% vs. 51.9%, p = 0.03), though this finding did not remain significant in adjusted models (OR 1.34, 95% CI [0.99, 1.81]).

Respondents in epidemiology/public health research and clinical research were more interested in future coaching than those in laboratory research or other types of research (58.6% vs. 54.9% vs. 46.5% vs. 46.8%, p = 0.002); however, this finding did not remain significant in adjusted models (epidemiology/public health: OR 1.27, 95% CI [0.93, 1.73]; clinical: OR 1.03, 95% CI [0.77, 1.37]; other: OR 0.80, 95% CI [0.46, 1.41]). Respondents with K awards also reported greater coaching interest than those with F awards (54.4% vs. 42.6%, p < 0.001); however, it did not remain significant in adjusted models (OR 0.84, 95% CI [0.57, 1.25]). Respondents of higher academic rank were more interested in future coaching than those with lower rank, with associate professors/professors and assistant professors being most interested in coaching, followed by instructors and respondents with other/no academic rank (55.3% vs. 55.2% vs. 51.8% vs. 44.3%, p = 0.007). In adjusted models, these results were no longer significant (assistant professor OR 0.93, 95% CI [0.65, 1.33], instructor: OR 0.79, 95% CI [0.45, 1.39], none/other OR 0.69, 95% CI [0.42, 1.14]). Coaching interest did not vary by graduation year or degree in unadjusted and adjusted models (p > 0.05).

Coaching preferences

Among respondents interested in future coaching (n = 776), the most preferred coaching formats were one-on-one (39.3%, n = 305) as well as a mix of one-on-one and small group (36.5%, n = 283) (Supplemental Digital Appendix Table C). In terms of coaching modality, 37.0% of respondents preferred virtual (n = 287) and 22.8% preferred in-person (n = 177), while 40.2% had no preference (n = 312). Finally, respondents most preferred coaching that occurs monthly (42.3%, n = 328) and every other week (34.2%, n = 265).

When asked to identify the most important topics for future coaching (Fig. 2), the majority of respondents identified project management (66.4%, n = 983), team management (64.8%, n = 960), leadership (62.7%, n = 929), grant writing (55.2%, n = 818), and financial management (50.3%, n = 745). Less than half of respondents identified the following topics as important: networking (46.2%, n = 684), imposter syndrome (44.5%, n = 659), and personality assessments (25.9%, n = 384).

Topics considered important for future coaching varied by demographics (Supplemental Digital Appendix Table D). Heterosexual females in this study were most likely to report imposter syndrome as an important topic, followed by GSM respondents and heterosexual males (51.4% vs. 46.8% vs. 32.2%, p < 0.001). The same pattern held for the topic of leadership (65.8% vs. 58.9% vs. 57.8%, p = 0.01). GSM respondents were most likely to report project management as important, followed by heterosexual females and then heterosexual males (71.8% vs. 69.1% vs. 61.1%, p = 0.01). Topic preferences did not differ based on race/ethnicity (p > 0.05).

Respondents with varying professional characteristics identified different coaching topics as important (Supplemental Digital Appendix Table D). Respondents with earlier degrees (before 2011, 2011–2015) were more likely to identify leadership as important than those with later degrees (2016–2021) (64.9% vs. 65.3% vs. 57.7%, p = 0.02). In contrast, those with later degrees (2016–2021) were more likely to identify grant writing as an important topic than those with earlier degrees (2011–2015, before 2011) (60.8% vs. 55.3% vs. 49.1%, p = 0.002). K awardees in this study were more likely than F awardees to report financial management (52.2% vs. 41.0%, p = 0.001) and team management (67.1% vs. 53.8%, p < 0.001) as important. F awardees were more likely than K awardees to identify grant writing as important (64.5% vs. 53.3%, p = 0.001). The importance of topics like networking, project management, imposter syndrome, and leadership were significantly different depending on respondents’ academic rank. The importance of grant writing, financial management, networking, personality assessments, and leadership were significantly different by degree. Finally, the importance of grant writing, networking, and financial management differed significantly by research field.

Discussion

This national cross-sectional study is the first, to our knowledge, to report past coaching experiences as well as characterize coaching interest and preferences among ECRs. About one in six respondents had previous coaching experiences. Over half reported interest in future coaching, with greater interest among underrepresented respondents based on sex/gender identity/sexual orientation and race/ethnicity. Further, respondents with different demographic and professional characteristics deemed different coaching topics as important. Thus, unique personal and professional identities may lead ECRs to seek different programming. This study not only highlights a demand for coaching among ECRs as of 18 months after the COVID-19 pandemic but also the importance of tailored approaches that meet individuals’ unique needs.

In this study, heterosexual females and GSM researchers had greater coaching interest than heterosexual males, including in both unadjusted and adjusted models. This finding aligns with past studies demonstrating heightened demand for professional development among women and LGBTQ individuals. For instance, a systematic review found that women in academic medicine, especially those in male-dominated fields, perceived mentorship as valuable for their careers more than men and desired female mentors who advised on personal issues and work-life balance [34]. While there is limited research on LGBTQ researchers’ perceptions of professional development, LGBTQ advocacy organizations call for greater professional development such as mentorship, programming, and outreach [35]. This demand for professional development may be a consequence of the structural barriers preventing women and LGBTQ individuals from advancing professionally and reaching their fullest potential [36, 37]. Notably, our study found that female and GSM respondents considered imposter syndrome and leadership as important coaching topics more often than men. Imposter syndrome is a common challenge for many researchers in the early career stage [38], and women and LGBTQ individuals are particularly susceptible due to factors like societal biases, microaggressions, and lack of representation [39, 40]. Two randomized control trials found that women physician trainees who received coaching significantly decreased imposter syndrome [41, 42]. Similarly, coaching may be one tool to empower women and LGBTQ ECRs to overcome these challenges on an individual level, with potential effects on their retention and success in the biomedical workforce [43].

Underrepresented racial/ethnic respondents had greater coaching interest than White and Asian respondents in the unadjusted model, though this finding was no longer significant in adjusted models. Greater coaching interest among underrepresented racial/ethnic groups aligns with past literature advocating for practices that improve minority researchers’ self-efficacy and persistence in research careers, including the need for mentors, allies, and institutional support [44]. Coaching specifically has been found to improve the perception of academic career success among underrepresented science faculty, with participants citing the benefits of having coaches as role models and supplements to mentors [15]. The lack of significance after adjusting for sex/gender identity and sexual orientation, academic rank, award type, and research field may be because racial/ethnic minorities tend to have lower academic ranks [45], represent a higher proportion of F awardees than K awardees [46], or work in epidemiology/public health (rather than laboratory or clinical research) [25]. Future interventions should consider how coaching can best be tailored to the needs of racial/ethnic minority individuals [47, 48].

In this study, ECRs with diverse professional characteristics reported differing coaching interest and preferences. Based on unadjusted models, more professionally advanced ECRs (i.e., K awards, higher academic rank) displayed greater coaching interest, which may be due to the growing demands and stressors as ECRs progress toward the threshold of research independence. Respondents also offered differing priorities for coaching topics. Financial and team management were more important to K awardees, whereas topics like grant writing emerged as more important to F awardees. Older ECRs (having earlier degrees) were more interested in coaching focused on leadership, whereas younger ECRs (having later degrees) were more interested in grant writing. ECRs of lower academic rank were more interested in coaching focused on networking. These findings reflect the diversity of researchers’ needs, even within the early career stage. To optimize coaching effectiveness for ECRs with diverse backgrounds and varied professional needs, greater implementation and rigorous evaluation of programs are needed to understand how to balance feasibility with tailored approaches.

This study has limitations. Coaching is an emerging model in academia, especially in the biomedical sciences [49]. Our survey provided a definition of coaching for shared understanding, but coaching can still overlap or be confused with mentorship or advising. As a result, this may have skewed responses, overrepresenting how many respondents have had coaching experiences. Compared to known demographics of NIH Early Stage Investigators, our study’s respondents underrepresented Asian individuals, accurately represented Black and Hispanic individuals, and overrepresented women [50]. Further, the survey did not include questions regarding disability status and disadvantaged backgrounds [51], which may have impacted participants’ previous coaching experiences, needs for support, or interests in different programs or particular structures. Finally, the survey response rate was 35.7%, and respondents may have stronger feelings or more time available than non-respondents.

Conclusion

In summary, we observed that just over half of ECRs in this study are interested in future coaching, with greater interest among traditionally underrepresented groups. Research shows coaching has potential in supporting the career development of a diverse biomedical workforce. However, greater implementation and standardized evaluation of coaching are needed to understand best practices and efficacy to create programs that support ECRs widely and meet specific demands of those most vulnerable. Future research should explore the potential impacts of integrating coaching into formal support structures for professional development within institutions to help understand how it can support long-term retention and representation of diverse biomedical researchers.
