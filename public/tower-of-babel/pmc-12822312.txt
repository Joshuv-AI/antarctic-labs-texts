Healthy work environment from the perspective of Jordanian ICU nurses: a descriptive cross-sectional study using the AACN assessment tool
Authors: Enas AlZrigat, Basema Nofal, Muhammad W Darawad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrevious research has emphasized the significant influence of workplace conditions in healthcare settings on nurses’ caregiving practices and patient outcomes. However, there remains a gap in understanding these dynamics, particularly within the context of intensive care units.PurposeTo assess the perception of Jordanian ICU nurses regarding their work environment using the American Association of Critical-Care Nurses-Healthy Work Environment Assessment Tool (AACN-HWET).MethodsEmploying a quantitative, descriptive, and correlational cross-sectional design, the study sampled 150 registered nurses from four Jordanian hospitals in Amman. Participants completed the Healthy Work Environment Assessment Tool (HWEAT), with statistical analyses including descriptive statistics, independent t-tests, Pearson’s correlation coefficient, and one-way ANOVA.ResultsOf the 190 questionnaires distributed, 150 were completed (79% response rate). Participants had a mean age of 30.9 years, were mostly male (56.7%), married (62%), and had an average of 6.1 years of ICU experience. Analysis revealed a mean healthy work environment score of 3.09, reflecting a positive perception of the work environment among Jordanian ICU nurses. However, overall HWEAT mean was 3.09, categorized as ‘good’; Skilled Communication was lowest (M = 3.29, SD = 0.74) and True Collaboration highest (M = 3.22, SD = 0.96). Group differences by marital status, perceived resource adequacy, and hospital type were observed in unadjusted tests.ConclusionThe findings highlight the need for targeted policies and ongoing training programs that strengthen leadership, communication, and resource management—practical strategies that hospital management can implement to enhance ICU nurse satisfaction and ultimately improve patient safety and care quality.Keywords: Healthy work environment; Workplace health; Nurses, intensive care unit; Jordan

Introduction

In the dynamic field of healthcare, the quality of the work environment plays a crucial role in ensuring the well-being of both patients and healthcare professionals [1]. The World Health Organization (WHO) defines a healthy work environment (HWE) as “one where workers and managers collaborate to continually improve the health, safety, and wellbeing of all workers and, by doing this, sustain the productivity of the business” [2]. Despite this ideal, certain hospital settings—particularly intensive care units (ICUs)—face considerable challenges. These include heightened stress associated with end-of-life care, ethical conflicts in clinical decisions, ongoing exposure to human suffering, care inequities, ineffective communication between nurses and patients, and demanding expectations from patients’ families [3–5]. Moreover, ICU nurses are expected to consistently enhance and broaden their competencies to deliver high-quality patient care [6, 7].

In response to these challenges, the American Association of Critical-Care Nurses (AACN) introduced the Healthy Work Environment Assessment Tool (HWEAT), designed specifically for intensive care unit (ICU) settings [8]. This tool is grounded in six core standards that are vital for fostering a healthy work environment: skilled communication, genuine collaboration, effective decision-making, adequate staffing, meaningful recognition, and authentic leadership.

The ICU work environment presents substantial occupational challenges for nursing staff. According to a recent meta-analysis of 18 cross-sectional studies encompassing 23,140 ICU nurses from 23 diverse countries, approximately 27.7% (95% CI: 21.6%–34.3%) reported an intention to leave their positions [9].

Establishing a healthy work environment (HWE) for ICU nurses offers a range of advantages, including lower burnout rates facilitated by supportive leadership and manageable workloads [10, 11], which in turn help maintain job satisfaction and overall well-being [12]. Furthermore, improved patient safety is achieved through effective communication and strict adherence to safety protocols, thereby reducing the likelihood of errors in critical care settings [13, 14].

Higher nurse retention rates contribute to the continuity of patient care, while strong teamwork and the application of evidence-based practices lead to improved outcomes and reduced hospital stays [11, 15]. Access to resources that support stress management strengthens ICU nurses’ resilience and enhances their job satisfaction [16, 17]. When healthcare organizations prioritize staff well-being and maintain low turnover rates, they cultivate a positive reputation, making them more attractive as workplaces [18].

An unhealthy ICU work environment can have detrimental effects on both nurses and the quality of patient care [19], contributing factors such as elevated stress levels [20], insufficient staffing [21], and a lack of organizational support often lead to nurse burnout [22], adversely impacting their mental health and job satisfaction [23]. These challenges, combined with increased turnover rates [24], lead to staffing shortages that compromise the continuity and quality of patient care [25, 26].

Patient safety can be jeopardized when nurses are overworked and stressed, increasing the likelihood of errors that may lead to adverse events and suboptimal outcomes [27]. Additional risks to patient safety include medical errors [19], missed nursing care [28], challenges associated with advanced technology [29], and inadequate care delivery [8]. The overall quality of care may decline as nurses face difficulties in delivering thorough care, especially in environments marked by poor communication and low morale [30].

Although research on HWE is expanding, much of the existing literature primarily highlights the benefits and significance of HWEs. To bridge existing gaps, a deeper understanding of the specific challenges and dynamics that define HWEs within the ICU context is essential. Moreover, most current studies tend to concentrate on general hospital wards, overlooking the distinct nature of intensive care units. Therefore, the ICU setting warrants a more focused and specialized investigation.

The ICU work environment is shaped by multiple factors, such as the critical nature of patients’ conditions, the demand for rapid decision-making, and the essential role of teamwork among nurses—elements that define the unique character of intensive care units [8]. However, current research reveals a lack of in-depth exploration into how these specific factors influence the development and maintenance of a healthy work environment within the ICU setting. In addition, there is a noticeable gap in research exploring ICU nurses’ perspectives and experiences related to healthy work environments. As a vital component of patient care, the nursing profession plays a significant role in shaping the work environment. Gaining insight into how ICU nurses perceive and respond to the various factors influencing an HWE is crucial for developing targeted strategies aimed at improving both job satisfaction and patient outcomes. The AACN Healthy Work Environment framework plays a vital role in promoting patient safety, increasing satisfaction, lowering burnout rates, and supporting the financial viability of healthcare institutions [1, 8]. This study is particularly valuable as it offers an in-depth assessment of the ICU environment in Jordan, examining key elements outlined by the AACN—namely skilled communication, true collaboration, adequate staffing, meaningful recognition, and authentic leadership [8].

Recognizing these factors is essential for enhancing the work environment through targeted training, education, and new strategies that cater to the specific needs of ICU nurses in Jordanian healthcare settings. This research seeks to explore the current work environment and identify demographic differences in nurses’ perceptions. These insights are critical for designing focused interventions that improve work conditions, nurse well-being, and the quality of patient care in Jordanian ICUs. Therefore, this study evaluates the perceptions of ICU nurses in Jordan using the AACN Healthy Work Environment Assessment Tool. Specifically, two primary questions will be addressed:

How do Jordanian ICU nurses perceive their current work environment?

How do Jordanian ICU nurses perceive their current work environment?

Are there differences in Jordanian ICU nurses’ scores on HWE based on their demographics?

Methods

Design and setting

The present study employed a quantitative descriptive cross-sectional research design. Jordan’s healthcare framework classifies hospitals into four sectors: university, private, public, and military. This study selected four hospitals, all conveniently located in Amman, the capital of Jordan, to represent the three healthcare sectors in Jordan: public, university, and private. Among these, three hospitals are accredited by the Joint Commission International (JCI), reflecting compliance with internationally recognized standards for quality and patient safety, while the fourth hospital operates under national Jordanian standards. Selection criteria for target hospitals primarily considered their size being the largest in Amman, and that they offer various types of ICU services, including Medical ICU (MICU), Surgical ICU (SICU), Cardiac Care Unit (CCU), and mixed ICU. The data collection for this study was conducted over a period of three months, from August to October 2023.

Sampling

The study targeted all registered nurses actively engaged in providing direct patient care within the ICU setting in Jordan. Inclusion criteria required participants to possess at least a bachelor’s degree, have direct involvement in ICU nursing care, and possess a minimum of six months of experience to minimize the impact of external influences. However, nurses in administrative roles were excluded due to their indirect involvement in patient care.

Participants were recruited through convenience sampling, and sample size determination was done using G*Power software. Utilizing a Pearson correlation coefficient test with a significance level of 0.05, effect size of 0.30, and statistical power of 0.95, the initial sample size was 111 participants. An additional 20% (n = 22) was added, resulting in a total suggested sample size of 133 participants. However, the calculation was for a secondary correlation aim and that group comparison power may be lower and therefore a larger sample size was targeted.

Instrument

Demographic characteristics

The demographic questionnaire encompassed a range of socio-demographic factors, including gender, education level, age, marital status, income satisfaction, and years of experience in the ICU. Additionally, the study examined hospital variables such as bed capacity, ICU type, nurse-patient ratio, and perception of resource sufficiency.

Healthy work environment assessment tool (HWEAT)

The AACN [8] devised a questionnaire primarily to evaluate HWE within ICUs. Subsequently, AACN [8] outlined that this instrument identified areas necessitating improvement rather than pinpointing specific concerns. The final instrument structured six standards, each with three components, giving a total of 18 elements. Respondents were instructed to provide their assessment using a 5-point Likert scale, where responses ranged from “Strongly Disagree” (1) to “Strongly Agree” (5). Subsequent to data collection, mean scores were utilized to categorize outcomes into three tiers: poor (1.00–2.99), good (3.00–3.99), and excellent (4.00–5.00) [8].

The Healthy Work Environment Assessment Tool (HWEAT) has demonstrated strong psychometric properties across multiple studies. Connor et al. [31] reported a test-retest reliability ranging from 0.50 to 0.68 and a high content validity score of 96.63% among a diverse sample of medical professionals. In a Canadian French translation, the tool showed an internal consistency of 0.89, supporting the use of item averages [28]. In our study, the HWEAT demonstrated excellent internal consistency reliability, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.95 for the overall instrument. Subscale reliability coefficients ranged from 0.77 for skilled communication to 0.96 for authentic leadership, indicating strong consistency across all subscales.

Prior to its use, official permission was obtained from the AACN. Although the HWEAT is a previously validated instrument, a full local validation was not conducted due to the study’s focus and scope. Also, the original English version was utilized even though English is not Jordan’s primary language, as it serves as the medium of instruction in nursing schools and is commonly used in hospitals for communication and documentation. Nurses are therefore expected to understand written English. However, to ensure content validity and contextual relevance within the Jordanian ICU setting, the tool was reviewed by a panel of three PhD-prepared critical care experts who recommended minor adjustments to enhance clarity and appropriateness. Additionally, a pilot study with 15 participants (10% of the total sample, not included in the final analysis) was carried out to assess internal consistency, feasibility, readability, and the clarity of inclusion and exclusion criteria. The feedback indicated that the questionnaire was well understood and required no further modifications. The pilot study also yielded a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.95, indicating excellent internal consistency reliability and supporting the tool’s suitability for use in the local context.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for the study was granted by the Institutional Review Board at the University of Jordan (73/2023) and the relevant participating hospitals. Participant recruitment was conducted across different shifts by one of the researchers (EA), ensuring anonymity and the security of collected data. The research team followed the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki throughout the study. Informed consent was obtained as an implied consent, as indicated on the first page of the questionnaire. To protect confidentiality, no personal identifiers were collected, and all data were anonymized. Finally, they were notified of their right to withdraw at any time without penalty. Access to research data was restricted to the researcher, and all electronic files were securely stored using password protection.

Data collection

Following ethical approval, one of the researchers (EA) commenced data collection in designated hospitals (approximately 3–4 visits per site to obtain the desired sample size). They coordinated with nursing managers and ICU head nurses to clarify study objectives and streamline procedures. Consent for participation was obtained through a cover letter, outlining the study’s purpose and benefits, with questionnaire completion indicating agreement. Eligible nurses were provided questionnaires with adequate time for completion and submission, which was on-duty for most of the subjects.

Statistical analysis

After collecting the questionnaires, the data underwent coding and screening before being prepared for analysis using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS, version 25). Thorough screening and cleaning processes were implemented to address missing scores and outlier values prior to analysis. Normality of the data was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and inspection of Q–Q plots, while variance homogeneity was evaluated with Levene’s test. When equal variances were not assumed, Welch’s t-test was applied, resulting in fractional degrees of freedom. The statistical analyses, tailored to the research questions, encompassed descriptive statistics (means, standard deviation), independent sample t-test, analysis of variance (ANOVA), and Pearson correlation coefficient test.

Results

Description of sample characteristics

Table 1 outlines the demographic profile of the individuals involved in the study. From a total of 190 questionnaires distributed, 150 were completed, yielding a response rate of 79%. Participants were sourced from various types of hospitals—educational, public, and private—with each contributing an equal proportion of 50 individuals (33.3%). On average, participants were around 30.9 years old (SD = 5.9), and males accounted for 56.7% of the respondents. The majority of participants (62%) reported being married.

Jordanian ICU nurses’ perspectives on their current work environment

To tackle the initial inquiry, a descriptive analysis was performed (refer to Table 2). The average assessment for the current workplace ambiance stood at 3.09 (SD = 0.79) on a scale of 1 to 5, implying a positive rating of 61.8%. Generally, ICU nurses in Jordan exhibited a favorable outlook on their work environment. True collaboration garnered the highest average rating (M = 3.22, SD = 0.96), indicating a good evaluation. However, skilled communication obtained the lowest mean score (M = 3.29, SD = 0.74), signaling a less positive perception. A deeper examination revealed that frequent communication received the highest average rating, while the availability of sufficient staff for ensuring patient safety garnered the lowest rating.

Regarding the standards of HWE, skilled communication exhibited notable variances based on income satisfaction (t = 2.299; df = 148; p = 0.02), marital status (t = 2.408; df = 146; p = 0.017), resource sufficiency (t = 2.696; df = 148; p = 0.008), and different hospital types (F = 9.862; df = 2; p = 0.000). A detailed examination unveiled that single nurses (M = 2.98, SD = 0.66), those dissatisfied with their income (M = 2.98, SD = 0.64), perceiving adequate resources (M = 2.95, SD = 0.73), and working in private (M = 3.00, SD = 0.72) and public hospitals (M = 2.96, SD = 0.61) perceived a higher level of communication skills compared to their counterparts.

Similarly, true collaboration exhibited variations based on gender (t = 2.112; df = 148; p = 0.036), nurse-to-patient ratio (t = 2.011; df = 147.967; p = 0.046), adequacy of resources (t = 3.360; df = 148; p = 0.001), and hospital types (F = 3.950; df = 2; p = 0.02). Analysis revealed that female nurses (M = 3.41, SD = 0.96), nurses with a 1:3 patient ratio (M = 3.40, SD = 0.73), participants perceiving sufficient resources (M = 3.46, SD = 0.98), and nurses in public hospitals (M = 3.42, SD = 0.76) perceived higher levels of collaboration in their work environment.

Variations in effective decision-making were observed based on gender (t = 2.117; df = 148; p = 0.036), income satisfaction (t = 2.166; df = 148; p = 0.032), resource adequacy (t = 2.128; df = 148; p = 0.035), and hospital types (F = 10.968; df = 2; p = 0.000). Female nurses (M = 3.39, SD = 0.96), those dissatisfied with their income (M = 3.43, SD = 0.83), participants perceiving adequate resources (M = 3.35, SD = 0.97), and nurses in public hospitals (M = 3.47, SD = 0.76) demonstrated higher levels of effective decision-making.

Significant disparities were evident regarding staffing adequacy, based on various factors including gender (t = 2.421; df = 148; p = 0.017), marital status (t = 3.069; df = 148; p = 0.003), ICU bed capacity (t = 2.873, df = 148, p = 0.005), resource sufficiency (t = 1.998.; df = 147.858; p = 0.048), and hospital types (F = 14.268; df = 2; p = 0.000). Female nurses (M = 3.19, SD = 0.98), unmarried nurses (M = 3.28, SD = 1.05), those in units with fewer than 15 beds (M = 3.11, SD = 0.95), nurses perceiving adequate staffing (M = 3.15, SD = 1.13), those acknowledging resource adequacy (M = 3.11, SD = 1.11), and nurses in private hospitals (M = 3.46, SD = 0.96) held more positive perceptions regarding staffing appropriateness.

Additionally, significant statistical variances were noted for perceived meaningful recognition, contingent on marital status (t = 2.510; df = 148; p = 0.013), level of education (t = 2.388; df = 13.249; p = 0.033), and hospital types (F = 9.718; df = 2; p = 0.000). Nurses who were unmarried (M = 3.33, SD = 1.01), holding a master’s or doctorate degree (M = 3.53, SD = 0.59), and employed in private hospitals (M = 3.33, SD = 1.05) reported higher levels of perceived meaningful recognition.

Moreover, authentic leadership significantly varied based on marital status (t = 2.100; df = 148; p = 0.037), number of ICU beds (t = 2.067; df = 148; p = 0.040), perceived resource sufficiency (t = 3.018; df = 148; p = 0.003), and hospital types (F = 12.145; df = 2; p = 0.000). Nurses who were unmarried (M = 3.38, SD = 1.02), working in units with fewer than 15 beds (M = 3.27, SD = 0.96), perceiving adequate resources (M = 3.38, SD = 1.03), and employed in private hospitals (M = 3.52, SD = 0.97) exhibited the most favorable perspectives on authentic leadership. Lastly, the Pearson correlation coefficient (r) indicated no statistically significant correlation between continuous demographic characteristics (age and years of ICU experience) with HWE total and subscales.

Discussion

Although extensive research has examined the impact of work environments on outcomes for both patients and nurses, there remains a significant gap in understanding ICU nurses’ perspectives on their workplaces. This study sought to address this gap by exploring the views of ICU nurses working in various healthcare facilities across Jordan. The findings revealed that, overall, ICU nurses in Jordan rated their work environment positively, with an average score of 3.09 out of 5, consistent with previous studies [23, 31]. Most nurses reported favorable views, particularly regarding genuine collaboration, which received the highest average rating. However, areas such as skilled communication were identified as needing improvement, with lower mean scores suggesting that participants held less favorable views on this aspect. This highlights a potential area for targeted interventions aimed at enhancing communication strategies within ICU settings.

Differences in perceived work environments between Jordan and other countries are likely influenced by a range of contextual and systemic factors, including healthcare infrastructure, organizational culture, resource allocation, staffing policies, and socioeconomic conditions. For example, a study conducted in Saudi Arabia reported a slightly higher overall HWEAT score of 3.55 [32]. Although aspects such as appropriate staffing and skilled communication were generally viewed positively, these areas still received the lowest scores among all HWEAT standards in that setting, signaling ongoing challenges.

Numerous studies emphasize that components such as skilled communication and staffing are recurrent areas requiring improvement globally. Factors like heavy workloads, rigid organizational structures, communication barriers, and staff shortages exacerbate stress, burnout, and dissatisfaction among nurses, hindering effective teamwork and patient safety [7, 28]. The extent to which these issues are perceived as problematic can vary depending on systemic differences such as healthcare funding, policy frameworks, and hospital management practices; that shape organizational priorities and resource availability. For instance, resource constraints and less mature healthcare systems in Jordan may hinder efforts to improve staffing and communication, impacting nurses’ perceptions relative to other settings with more developed systems.

Interestingly, authentic leadership and true collaboration received the highest mean scores in the Saudi context [32], highlighting cultural differences in perceptions of leadership and teamwork. Cultural dimensions such as respect for authority, collectivism, and cultural competence significantly influence nurses’ perceptions of leadership and recognition. Literature on multicultural nursing teams indicates that nurse managers who demonstrate cultural competence and adopt supportive, transformational leadership styles are better able to positively impact nurse outcomes and perceptions [33]. These findings underscore the importance of leadership attributes that recognize and respect diverse cultural backgrounds, thereby facilitating more effective communication, recognition, and engagement within heterogeneous healthcare teams [34].

Recognizing these contextual and systemic influences underscores the importance of developing tailored interventions that address each country’s unique healthcare infrastructure, policies, and cultural norms. Strategies effective in one setting may not directly translate to another, making it essential for future research to explicitly explore these variables to understand the root causes of perception differences. Such nuanced understanding can inform targeted, context-specific improvements rather than assuming the applicability of strategies across diverse healthcare environments.

Additionally, the findings of this study provide valuable insights into the factors influencing Jordanian ICU nurses’ perceptions of their work environment. Marital status emerged as a significant factor in shaping these perceptions. Single nurses tended to view their work environment more positively and reported higher levels of perceived skilled communication, adequate staffing, meaningful recognition, and authentic leadership compared to married nurses. Several factors may explain the more favorable perceptions among unmarried nurses. Their increased flexibility and autonomy are likely to contribute to a greater sense of control over their duties and schedules. On the other hand, increased family responsibilities and associated stressors diminish nurses’ perceptions of their work environment and social support, which can negatively impact their job satisfaction [35]. Recognizing the unique needs of both single and married nurses enables the development of tailored strategies to enhance overall job satisfaction and productivity. For example, offering flexible scheduling options and providing support services, such as childcare, can help married nurses better balance their work and family responsibilities.

The availability of resources plays a crucial role in shaping how Jordanian ICU nurses perceive their work environment [36, 37]. Nurses who reported having adequate resources generally held more positive views of their workplace and expressed greater satisfaction with various factors, such as communication, teamwork, decision-making, staffing levels, and leadership authenticity. This finding is consistent with previous research linking insufficient medical supplies and equipment to increased staff stress and reduced care quality [38, 39]. Resource shortages are often caused by financial limitations, poor resource management, and high demand for healthcare services. Addressing this issue requires a strategic approach that aligns with the hospital’s objectives, ensuring that resource allocation effectively supports these goals. Further research is needed to gain a deeper understanding of this challenge and develop strategies to improve resource availability and management within healthcare settings.

The type of hospital plays a significant role in shaping the perceptions of Jordanian nurses regarding their work environment. Nurses working in private and public hospitals generally reported more favorable views of their work environment compared to those employed in educational hospitals. Furthermore, they demonstrated higher levels of positivity across all dimensions measured by the HWEAT compared to their counterparts in educational hospitals. These findings are consistent with Balsanelli and Cunha [40], who identified similar work environment conditions in private and public ICUs in southern Brazil. On the other hand, Pires et al. [41] found a more positive professional practice environment in private hospitals compared to public hospitals. One possible explanation is that private hospitals in Jordan may foster better work environments due to factors such as financial stability, a strong commitment to quality care, and investments in staff education and development. Further research is necessary, particularly in educational hospitals, to explore the reasons behind the differing perceptions across various hospital types. Additionally, continuous monitoring, evaluation, and adjustments based on feedback are essential for ensuring ongoing improvements.

Certain demographic factors appear to influence specific aspects of the work environment, rather than shaping its overall perception. For instance, gender has an impact on areas like true collaboration, effective decision-making, and staffing adequacy, with female nurses typically reporting more positive views in these domains. This trend could be attributed to their strong communication skills, empathy, and emotional intelligence. To address this, it may be beneficial to introduce diversity and inclusion training aimed at reducing gender biases and promoting an inclusive atmosphere, ensuring that all nurses feel valued and empowered to contribute meaningfully.

The research also suggests that income satisfaction may influence nurses’ perceptions of skilled communication and effective decision-making. Interestingly, nurses who reported dissatisfaction with their income tended to rate their communication and decision-making abilities more favorably. While the reasons for this are not fully clear, one possible explanation—though not directly examined in this study—is that personal perceptions of professional strengths may help mitigate the negative effects of financial dissatisfaction. To support nurses facing financial stress, institutions might consider employee assistance programs, open communication channels for feedback, and recognition initiatives. Addressing income-related concerns within a supportive workplace culture can contribute to enhanced job satisfaction and overall well-being.

Ensuring a healthy work environment is vital for delivering high-quality care to critically ill patients. To achieve this, it is essential to implement effective practices, policies, and research initiatives. Practically, addressing nurse workload to prevent burnout, fostering inter-professional collaboration, and offering training in time management and communication are key strategies. From a policy perspective, healthcare facilities should focus on proper resource allocation, maintaining appropriate nurse-patient ratios, and establishing ongoing education programs. Moreover, additional research, particularly within ICU settings, is necessary to gain a deeper understanding of the hospital work environment. This includes qualitative studies aimed at enhancing nurse competence and overall job satisfaction.

Strengths and limitations of the study

This study provides an in-depth examination of the hospital work environment in ICU settings, emphasizing the challenges faced by nurses and its practical relevance. It utilized the validated Healthy Work Environment Assessment Tool (HWEAT); however, no specific validation was conducted among Jordanian ICU nurses, which we acknowledge as a limitation. To address this, we referred to similar studies and sought expert feedback to ensure the tool’s relevance, though further empirical validation is still needed. Moreover, the study offers a broad comparative overview without a detailed contextual analysis, which may limit the understanding of specific organizational influences; thus, future research should include context-specific investigations.

Additionally, relying solely on a quantitative approach limited the ability to capture the depth and complexity of nurses’ perceptions, increasing the risk of self-selection bias, whereas qualitative methods or a mixed-methods design could have provided richer insights through exploring deeper meanings behind perceptions. Also, the authors acknowledge the multiplicity issue (multiple testing) and address that confirmatory follow-up studies are needed. The study also focused only on nurses’ perspectives, excluding other ICU professionals whose input could have offered a more comprehensive view of team dynamics. Furthermore, the use of convenience sampling due to time and funding constraints and the exclusive focus on Jordanian ICU nurses working in hospitals located in one city (Amman) may affect the generalizability of the findings and introduce potential selection bias. Given the urban setting, the results may not fully reflect the experiences of ICU nurses in rural or less-resourced areas, where organizational structures, staffing patterns, and work environment challenges may differ. Future research should aim to include a more geographically diverse sample to enhance representativeness. Finally. This study relied on self-reported data, which may be subject to self-reporting and social desirability biases, potentially influencing the accuracy and objectivity of the responses.

Implications for practice and policy

To enhance ICU nurse satisfaction and promote patient safety, hospital management should prioritize creating a supportive work environment by investing in authentic leadership development and skilled communication training. Addressing resource shortages through strategic allocation and improving staffing levels can alleviate nurse stress and prevent burnout. Furthermore, tailoring support systems—such as flexible scheduling and childcare services—to meet the needs of diverse staff demographics can improve work-life balance and job satisfaction. Promoting a culture of collaboration, inclusion, and recognition across all hospital types will contribute to a healthier workplace and better care outcomes in ICU settings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides descriptive insights into Jordanian ICU nurses’ perceptions of their work environment using the AACN-HWET framework. While the overall outlook was generally positive, areas such as meaningful recognition and staffing were noted as lower-scoring dimensions. These findings highlight aspects of the work environment that may warrant further attention in future research. Exploratory group differences observed in this study offer a foundation for more in-depth investigations to better understand factors influencing nurses’ perceptions and experiences.
