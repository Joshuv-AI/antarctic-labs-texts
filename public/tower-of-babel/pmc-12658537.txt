Quality of life in patients with chronic kidney disease in low- and middle-income countries: protocol for a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Alimzhan Muxunov, Dina Kalinina, Abduzhappar Gaipov, Antonio Sarria-Santamera
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroductionChronic kidney disease (CKD) affects over 850 million people globally, with nearly 80% residing in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Despite this high prevalence, there is limited understanding of health-related quality of life (HRQOL) among patients with CKD in these resource-constrained settings. This systematic review and meta-analysis aims to quantify HRQOL scores across CKD stages and treatment modalities in LMICs.Methods and analysisWe will conduct a comprehensive search of electronic databases including PubMed, Medline, Scopus and Web of Science, for observational studies published from January 2000 onwards in English or Russian. Eligible studies will include adult patients (≥18 years) with CKD stages 1–5, those on dialysis or kidney transplant recipients in LMICs. Two independent reviewers will screen studies, extract data and assess methodological quality using the Joanna Briggs Institute critical appraisal checklist. We will perform random-effects meta-analyses to pool HRQOL scores, stratified by CKD treatment groups. Heterogeneity will be assessed using I² statistics, with subgroup analyses and meta-regression conducted to explore potential sources of heterogeneity. The primary outcome will be pooled estimates of HRQOL scores across different CKD stages and treatment modalities in LMICs. Secondary outcomes will include subgroup analyses by income classification, geographical region and CKD stage depending on the availability of the data.Ethics and disseminationEthical approval is not required for this study. Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is an increasing global health concern affecting more than 850 million people, almost 80% of whom live in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs).1 2 This disproportionately high prevalence is explained by the significant dual burden of infectious and non-communicable diseases imposed on the effects of poverty and environmental hazards in LMICs.3 Moreover, limited public awareness and weaknesses in healthcare systems (scarcity of healthcare professionals, lack of resources and infrastructure) lead to the late diagnosis of the disease in most of the patients.4 5 Late diagnosis is in turn associated with increased risk of cardiovascular events and mortality, and patients with end-stage renal disease require urgent start of renal replacement therapy (RRT), which includes dialysis and kidney transplantation.6 7 However, patients in LMICs often do not have access to RRT.3 For instance, peritoneal dialysis services were available in over 90% of high-income countries, compared with just 32% of low-income countries.8 Medications for the treatment of earlier stages of CKD are often not provided for free, and patients are forced to pay out-of-pocket, facing catastrophic health expenditures.9 These challenges, compounded by the poor understanding of CKD in LMICs, underscore the urgent need for comprehensive research on this disease in resource-limited settings. Understanding quality of life level among patients with СKD is crucial because this information can further be used as a critical patient outcome in clinical trials, economic evaluations, assessment of health technologies and many other types of research.

Health-related quality of life (HRQOL) is defined as ‘a multidomain concept that represents the patient’s general perception of the effect of illness and treatment on physical, psychological and social aspects of life’.10 Metrics developed to evaluate HRQOL often include the assessment of people’s physical, psychological and social functioning. A systematic review and meta-analysis conducted by Fletcher et al revealed the significant symptom burden among patients with CKD, with patients receiving dialysis therapy experiencing the worst quality of life.11 However, most of the studies included in this review were conducted in HICs. Although studies from LMICs were also included, the results of meta-analysis still represent the generalised level of quality of life among patients with CKD, whereas for patients from LMICs these figures may be greatly overestimated. Another systematic review conducted by Mullerpatan et al examined HRQOL among patients with CKD in LMICs; however, the authors did not perform meta-analyses and did not provide pooled estimates of HRQOL in LMICs.12

Therefore, critical gaps in understanding the pooled estimates of HRQOL tailored to LMICs still exist. By conducting a comprehensive meta-analysis, we aim to evaluate HRQOL of patients with CKD across disease stages and treatment modalities in LMICs. These pooled estimates can then be used to develop targeted interventions, allocate resources effectively and formulate evidence-based health policies to improve CKD care in these regions.

This research protocol adheres to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Protocols (PRISMA-P) 2015 guidelines13; the completed checklist is available in online supplemental file 1. It was registered with PROSPERO (CRD420251016382).

Search strategy

A comprehensive literature search of peer-reviewed articles written in English or Russian will be conducted in the following electronic databases: PubMed, Medline, Scopus and Web of Science. Additionally, we will conduct a search through Google Scholar and reference lists of included studies for additional eligible studies. Searches will cover studies published between 1 January 2000 and 28 March 2025, with final searches completed by 31 March 2025. A detailed search strategy will be developed in consultation with an expert librarian. The search will use MeSH terms related to CKD and related terminology, HRQOL and LMICs. The full PubMed search strategy and keyword list are provided in online supplemental file 2, while the full list of searched LMICs is presented separately in online supplemental file 3. The search strategy will be adapted to each database. All identified citations will be imported into Zotero, where all duplicates will be removed. Two independent reviewers will screen titles and abstracts of all retrieved citations against the eligibility criteria using Rayaan software. Full texts of potentially eligible studies will then be obtained and independently assessed by two reviewers. Any disagreements during title, abstract and full text screening will be resolved through discussion with a third reviewer. The selection process will be documented using a PRISMA flow diagram.

Data collection process

Data extraction will be performed by two independent reviewers using a form in Microsoft Excel. A pilot calibration exercise will be conducted on a random sample of five studies to ensure consistency between reviewers. Disagreements will be resolved through discussion by a third reviewer. When necessary, we will contact study authors for missing or unclear information. The following data will be extracted: study characteristics (first author, year conducted, country of origin, study design, inclusion/exclusion criteria and sample size), population characteristics (age, gender, CKD stage, estimated glomerular filtration rate, comorbidity indices), instruments used for HRQOL assessment and study outcomes (mean scores with SD for overall and domain-specific HRQOL measures, factors associated with HRQOL).

Risk of bias assessment

The methodological quality of all included studies will be assessed independently by two reviewers using the Joanna Briggs Institute critical appraisal tools appropriate to each study design. Any disagreements will be resolved through discussion with a third reviewer.

Data analysis

Qualitative synthesis

We will provide a narrative synthesis of the findings from the included studies. Tables and figures will be used to summarise and present the findings.

Quantitative synthesis

Mean HRQOL scores (and SD) will be pooled separately for each instrument and domain-specific subscale. A random-effects model using the restricted maximum likelihood method will be used as the primary approach, given the expected heterogeneity among studies. The heterogeneity of the studies will be calculated using I2 statistics with an acceptable threshold of 60%. Significant heterogeneity (I²>60%) will be explored through subgroup analyses and meta-regression using study-level covariates such as mean age, gender distribution and comorbidity burden. Subgroup analyses and meta-regression will be undertaken only when ≥10 studies contribute to a synthesis. The results will be stratified by CKD treatment groups: (1) CKD stages 1–5 not on RRT, (2) patients receiving dialysis and (3) kidney transplant recipients. Subgroup analyses by income classification (low, lower-middle, upper-middle), by geographical region and by CKD stage (if patients are already classified or the study provides test data) will be conducted if there are three or more studies within subgroups.

To assess publication bias, we will conduct visual inspection of funnel plots for asymmetry and Egger’s test for analyses that include ≥10 studies. We will conduct sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of our findings by excluding studies with high risk of bias and using fixed-effect versus random-effects statistical models. All statistical analyses will be performed using Stata V.16.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, Texas, USA). Results will be reported following the PRISMA guidelines. We will present forest plots for the main outcomes and subgroup analyses.

Because this review synthesises cross-sectional HRQOL data rather than intervention effects, a formal certainty assessment will not be conducted. Instead, overall confidence in the evidence will be discussed narratively in relation to study quality and consistency of findings.

Patient-reported outcome measures

Several assessment tools are available for the assessment of HRQOL of patients with CKD. The 36-Item Short Form Survey (SF-36) is a generic health measure that is used to assess eight domains of health, including limitations in physical, social and usual role activities, pain, mental health, vitality and general health perceptions. Short Form Health Survey (SF-12) instrument is a shorter version of SF-36, which provides composite scores for physical component summary and mental component summary.14 Kidney Disease Quality of Life Short Form (KDQOL-SF) instrument combines the generic SF-36 instrument with 43 kidney disease-specific items.15 KDQOL-36 is a shorter version of KDQOL-SF, which consists of SF-12 instrument and 24 items specific to kidney disease. Other commonly used instrument is the EuroQoL-5D by EuroQol, aimed to measure five dimensions of health: mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort and anxiety/depression.16

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in this research’s design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans as the review synthesises secondary published data only.

Ethics and dissemination

Since this study involves a systematic review and meta-analysis of existing published data, formal ethical approval is not required. However, all included studies will be assessed for ethical compliance. Results will be submitted to a peer-reviewed, open-access journal and presented at scientific conferences. All tools (search strategies, data extraction forms) will be made available as supplementary materials.
