Topological dynamics for the endograph metric I: Equivalences with other metrics


Abstract
Given a dynamical system ( X , f ) (X,f) we investigate several topological dynamical properties for its Zadeh extension ( ℱ ⁡ ( X ) , f ^ ) (\mathcal{F}(X),\hat{f}) endowed with the endograph metric d E d_{E} . In particular, we prove that for topological 𝒜 \mathcal{A} -transitivity, topological ( ℓ , 𝒜 ) (\ell,\mathcal{A}) -recurrence, Devaney chaos, and for the specification property, the endograph metric behaves similarly to the supremum metric d ∞ d_{\infty} , the Skorokhod metric d 0 d_{0} and the sendograph metric d S d_{S} . Our results not only resolve certain open questions in the existing literature, but also yield completely new outcomes in terms of point- 𝒜 \mathcal{A} -transitivity.

2
General background and a key lemma

In this section we introduce the fuzzy results that we need in the sequel: first, we recall the definition of
\mathcal{F}(X)
and
\hat{f}
for a given dynamical system
(X,f)
; second, we recall the definitions of the different metrics that we use along the paper; and third, we prove the key lemma that allows us to develop the results stated at the Introduction (see Lemma
2.4
below). From now on let
\mathbb{N}
be the set of strictly positive integers, set
\mathbb{N}_{0}:=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
, and let us denote the unit interval
[0,1]
with the symbol
\mathbb{I}
.

2.1
The space of normal fuzzy sets
\mathcal{F}(X)

A
fuzzy set
on a topological space
X
is a function
u:X\longrightarrow\mathbb{I}
, where the value
u(x)\in\mathbb{I}
denotes the degree of membership of the point
x
in
u
. For each fuzzy set
u
, its
\alpha
-level
is and will be denoted by

u_{\alpha}:=\{x\in X\ ;\ u(x)\geq\alpha\}\ \text{ for each }\alpha\in\ ]0,1]\quad\text{ and }\quad u_{0}:=\overline{\{x\in X\ ;\ u(x)>0\}}.

We will denote by
\mathcal{F}(X)
the
hyperspace of normal fuzzy sets
on
X
, i.e. the space formed by the fuzzy sets
u
that are upper-semicontinuous functions and such that
u_{0}
is compact and
u_{1}
is non-empty. For each compact subset
K\subset X
we will denote by
\chi_{K}:X\longrightarrow\mathbb{I}
the
characteristic function on
K
, which belongs to
\mathcal{F}(X)
. Note also that the function
u:=\max_{1\leq j\leq N}(\alpha_{j}\cdot\chi_{K_{j}})
belongs to
\mathcal{F}(X)
for each pair of finite sequences of values
0<\alpha_{1}<\alpha_{2}<...<\alpha_{N}=1
and compact subsets
K_{1},...,K_{N}\subset X
, and that for such a fuzzy set
u
we have the equality

u_{\alpha}=\bigcup\{K_{j}\ ;\ 1\leq j\leq N\text{ with }\alpha_{j}\geq\alpha\}\quad\text{ for each }\alpha\in\mathbb{I}.

A kind of converse is also true, when
X
is a metric space, as we recall in Lemma
2.3
below.

It follows from
[
28
, Propositions 3.1 and 4.9]
that every continuous map
f:X\longrightarrow X
acting on a Hausdorff topological space
X
induces a well-defined map
\hat{f}:\mathcal{F}(X)\longrightarrow\mathcal{F}(X)
, called the
Zadeh extension
(sometimes referred as the
fuzzification
) of
f
. We have that
\hat{f}
maps each normal fuzzy set
u\in\mathcal{F}(X)
to the following normal fuzzy set:

\hat{f}(u):X\longrightarrow\mathbb{I}\quad\text{ where }\quad\left[\hat{f}(u)\right](x):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcc}\sup\{u(y)\ ;\ y\in f^{-1}(\{x\})\},&\text{ if }f^{-1}(\{x\})\neq\varnothing,\\[5.0pt]
0,&\text{ if }f^{-1}(\{x\})=\varnothing.\end{array}\right.

Moreover, when
X
is a metric space then we have the following well-known properties of the Zadeh extension
\hat{f}
, which we will use without citing them (see
[
28
,
29
,
43
]
for details):

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
f:X\longrightarrow X
be a continuous map acting on a metric space
(X,d)
,
u\in\mathcal{F}(X)
,
\alpha\in\mathbb{I}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, and let
K
be a compact subset of
X
. Then:

(a)

\left[\hat{f}(u)\right]_{\alpha}=f(u_{\alpha})
, i.e. the
\alpha
-level of the
\hat{f}
-image coincides with the
f
-image of the
\alpha
-level;

(b)

\left(\hat{f}\right)^{n}=\widehat{f^{n}}
, i.e. the composition of
\hat{f}
with itself
n
times coincides with the fuzzification of
f^{n}
;

(c)

\hat{f}\left(\chi_{K}\right)=\chi_{f(K)}
, i.e. the fuzzification
\hat{f}
is an extension of the original map
f
.

To talk about the continuity of
\hat{f}:\mathcal{F}(X)\longrightarrow\mathcal{F}(X)
we will endow
\mathcal{F}(X)
with the several fuzzy metrics mentioned in the Introduction (see Subsection
2.2
). However, these fuzzy metrics are defined in terms of the so-called
Hausdorff distance
, so let us quickly recall here its precise definition. Given a metric space
(X,d)
we will denote by
\mathcal{B}_{d}(x,\varepsilon)\subset X
the open
d
-ball centred at
x\in X
and of radius
\varepsilon>0
. Moreover, we will consider the spaces of sets

\mathcal{C}(X):=\left\{C\subset X\ ;\ C\text{ is a non-empty closed set}\right\},

\mathcal{K}(X):=\left\{K\subset X\ ;\ K\text{ is a non-empty compact set}\right\},

and given two sets
C_{1},C_{2}\in\mathcal{C}(X)
the
Hausdorff distance
between them is denoted and defined as

d_{H}(C_{1},C_{2}):=\max\left\{\sup_{x_{1}\in C_{1}}d(x_{1},C_{2}),\sup_{x_{2}\in C_{2}}d(x_{2},C_{1})\right\}.

This value may be infinite if either
C_{1}
or
C_{2}
are not compact. However, in
\mathcal{K}(X)
we have that the map
d_{H}:\mathcal{K}(X)\times\mathcal{K}(X)\longrightarrow[0,+\infty[
defined as before is a metric, called the
Hausdorff metric
, and every continuous map
f:X\longrightarrow X
induces a
d_{H}
-continuos map
\overline{f}:\mathcal{K}(X)\longrightarrow\mathcal{K}(X)
, defined as
\overline{f}(K):=f(K)=\{f(x)\ ;\ x\in K\}
for each
K\in\mathcal{K}(X)
. This will be an extremely useful in-between system when comparing
(X,f)
and
(\mathcal{F}(X),\hat{f})
. It is also well-known that the topology induced in the space
\mathcal{K}(X)
by
d_{H}
coincides with the so-called
Vietoris topology
, but we will not delve into this further here. We will denote by
\mathcal{B}_{H}(K,\varepsilon)\subset\mathcal{K}(X)
the open
d_{H}
-ball centred at
K\in\mathcal{K}(X)
and of radius
\varepsilon>0
.

Given
C\in\mathcal{C}(X)
and
\varepsilon\geq 0
we will write
C+\varepsilon:=\{x\in X\ ;\ d(x,c)\leq\varepsilon\text{ for some }c\in C\}
. The following are well-known (but very useful) facts:

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space,
\varepsilon\geq 0
, and let
C_{1},C_{2},C_{3},C_{4}\in\mathcal{C}(X)
. Then:

(a)

We have that
d_{H}(C_{1},C_{2})\leq\varepsilon
if and only if
C_{1}\subset C_{2}+\varepsilon
and
C_{2}\subset C_{1}+\varepsilon
.

(b)

We always have that
d_{H}(C_{1}\cup C_{2},C_{3}\cup C_{4})\leq\max\{d_{H}(C_{1},C_{3}),d_{H}(C_{2},C_{4})\}
.

We refer the reader to
[
25
]
for a detailed study of
\mathcal{C}(X)
and
\mathcal{K}(X)
, and we conclude this subsection with another useful lemma regarding fuzzy normal sets and the Hausdorff metric (see
[
28
,
29
,
41
]
):

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space. Given any set
u\in\mathcal{F}(X)
and
\varepsilon>0
there exist numbers
0=\alpha_{0}<\alpha_{1}<\alpha_{2}<...<\alpha_{N}=1
such that
d_{H}(u_{\alpha},u_{\alpha_{j+1}})<\varepsilon
for all
\alpha\in]\alpha_{j},\alpha_{j+1}]
and
0\leq j\leq N-1
. In particular, since
d_{H}(u_{\alpha},u_{\alpha_{1}})<\varepsilon
for every
\alpha\in\ ]0,\alpha_{1}]
, we also have that
d_{H}(u_{0},u_{\alpha_{1}})\leq\varepsilon
.

2.2
The supremum, Skorokhod, sendograph and endograph metrics on
\mathcal{F}(X)

In this subsection we introduce the different fuzzy metrics that we consider along the paper. From now on we let
(X,d)
be a metric space, and we will define the
supremum
,
Skorokhod
,
sendograph
and
endograph
metrics by using the original metric
d
of
X
, but also the notion of Hausdorff distance.

Following
[
29
]
we start by the
supremum metric

d_{\infty}:\mathcal{F}(X)\times\mathcal{F}(X)\longrightarrow[0,+\infty[
, which is defined for each pair
u,v\in\mathcal{F}(X)
as

d_{\infty}(u,v):=\sup_{\alpha\in\mathbb{I}}d_{H}(u_{\alpha},v_{\alpha}),

where
d_{H}
denotes the Hausdorff metric on
\mathcal{K}(X)
. We will denote by
\mathcal{B}_{\infty}(u,\varepsilon)
the open
d_{\infty}
-ball centred at
u\in\mathcal{F}(X)
and of radius
\varepsilon>0
, and as mentioned in the Introduction we will denote by
\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(X)
the metric space
(\mathcal{F}(X),d_{\infty})
and by
\tau_{\infty}
the topology induced on
\mathcal{F}(X)
by
d_{\infty}
. This metric has also been called the
level-wise metric
in
[
33
]
(see also the references included there), and it is well-known that the metric space
\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(X)
is non-separable whenever
X
has more than one point (see
[
27
, Proposition 4.5]
).

In the second place we have the
Skorokhod metric

d_{0}:\mathcal{F}(X)\times\mathcal{F}(X)\longrightarrow[0,+\infty[
, which is defined for each pair
u,v\in\mathcal{F}(X)
as

d_{0}(u,v):=\inf\left\{\varepsilon>0\ ;\ \text{there is }\xi\in\mathcal{T}\text{ with }\sup_{\alpha\in\mathbb{I}}|\xi(\alpha)-\alpha|\leq\varepsilon\text{ and }d_{\infty}(u,\xi\circ v)\leq\varepsilon\right\},

where

\mathcal{T}:=\left\{\xi:\mathbb{I}\longrightarrow\mathbb{I}\ ;\ \xi\text{ is a strictly increasing homeomorphism of }\mathbb{I}\right\}.

We will denote by
\mathcal{B}_{0}(u,\varepsilon)
the open
d_{0}
-ball centred at
u\in\mathcal{F}(X)
and of radius
\varepsilon>0
, and we will denote by
\mathcal{F}_{0}(X)
the metric space
(\mathcal{F}(X),d_{0})
and by
\tau_{0}
the topology induced on
\mathcal{F}(X)
by
d_{0}
. This metric was introduced, for the case in which
(X,d)
is an arbitrary metric space, in the 2020 paper
[
28
]
. The relations between
d_{0}
and other metrics in this rather general context have recently been studied in
[
22
]
. Since the identity self-map of
\mathbb{I}
belongs to
\mathcal{T}
, it is not hard to check that
d_{0}(u,v)\leq d_{\infty}(u,v)
and also that
d_{0}(u,\chi_{K})=d_{\infty}(u,\chi_{K})
for every
u,v\in\mathcal{F}(X)
and
K\in\mathcal{K}(X)
.

To introduce the
endograph
and
sendograph metrics
we need to consider an auxiliary metric
\overline{d}
on the product space
X\times\mathbb{I}
as follows:

\overline{d}\left((x,\alpha),(y,\beta)\right):=\max\left\{d(x,y),|\alpha-\beta|\right\}\quad\text{ for each pair }(x,\alpha),(y,\beta)\in X\times\mathbb{I}.

Given now any fuzzy set
u\in\mathcal{F}(X)
, the
endograph of
u
is defined as the following set

\operatorname{end}(u):=\left\{(x,\alpha)\in X\times\mathbb{I}\ ;\ u(x)\geq\alpha\right\},

and the
sendograph of
u
is defined as
\operatorname{send}(u):=\operatorname{end}(u)\cap(u_{0}\times\mathbb{I})
. Then, for each pair
u,v\in\mathcal{F}(X)
the
endograph metric

d_{E}
on
\mathcal{F}(X)
is the Hausdorff distance
\overline{d}_{H}
on
\mathcal{C}(X\times\mathbb{I})
from
\operatorname{end}(u)
to
\operatorname{end}(v)
, and the
sendograph metric

d_{S}
on
\mathcal{F}(X)
is the Hausdorff metric
\overline{d}_{H}
on
\mathcal{K}(X\times\mathbb{I})
between the non-empty compact subsets
\operatorname{send}(u)
and
\operatorname{send}(v)
. We will denote by
\mathcal{B}_{E}(u,\varepsilon)
and
\mathcal{B}_{S}(u,\varepsilon)
the open balls centred at
u\in\mathcal{F}(X)
and of radius
\varepsilon>0
for each of the metrics
d_{E}
and
d_{S}
respectively. We also use the notation
\mathcal{F}_{E}(X)
and
\mathcal{F}_{S}(X)
instead of
(\mathcal{F}(X),d_{E})
and
(\mathcal{F}(X),d_{S})
, and the symbols
\tau_{E}
and
\tau_{S}
for the respective topologies induced on
\mathcal{F}(X)
by
d_{E}
and
d_{S}
. Although
d_{E}
is defined as a distance of closed but not necessarily compact sets on
X\times\mathbb{I}
, the fact that the supports of the fuzzy sets
u,v\in\mathcal{F}(X)
are compact shows that
d_{E}(u,v)
is finite and hence that
d_{E}
is indeed a well-defined metric. Moreover, using Proposition
2.2
one can check that
d_{E}(u,v)\leq d_{S}(u,v)\leq d_{\infty}(u,v)
for every
u,v\in\mathcal{F}(X)
.

The above metrics come from the general theory of Spaces of Fuzzy Sets, each of them has its own role and importance, and there are many references showing this fact (see
[
44
,
9
,
32
,
28
]
for the historical development of the Skorokhod metric, and see
[
12
,
24
,
16
]
for the many applications of the endograph and sendograph metrics and their relation with the notion of
\Gamma
-convergence). The study of the relationships between these metrics has been an important topic addressed in the literature, and it is by now well-known that
d_{E}(u,v)\leq d_{S}(u,v)\leq d_{0}(u,v)\leq d_{\infty}(u,v)
for all
u,v\in\mathcal{F}(X)
, being
d_{S}(u,v)\leq d_{0}(u,v)
the only non-trivial inequality (see
[
26
, Proposition 2.7]
). Thus, we have and we will repeatedly use the following inclusion of topologies
\tau_{E}\subset\tau_{S}\subset\tau_{0}\subset\tau_{\infty}
. Moreover, given a continuous map
f:X\longrightarrow X
on a metric space
(X,d)
, the continuity of the Zadeh extension
\hat{f}:(\mathcal{F}(X),\rho)\longrightarrow(\mathcal{F}(X),\rho)
for every
\rho\in\{d_{\infty},d_{0},d_{S},d_{E}\}
is also well-known (see
[
28
]
and
[
33
]
).

2.3
A key fact regarding the endograph metric

Given a property
\mathcal{P}
from those considered in
[
2
,
4
,
29
,
41
]
, the way used there to prove the equivalence of such a property
\mathcal{P}
between the systems
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
,
(\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(X),\hat{f})
and
(\mathcal{F}_{0}(X),\hat{f})
is the following:

–

Step 1
. One shows that if
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
has property
\mathcal{P}
, then
(\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(X),\hat{f})
has property
\mathcal{P}
.

–

Step 2
. The inclusion
\tau_{0}\subset\tau_{\infty}
trivially implies that also
(\mathcal{F}_{0}(X),\hat{f})
has property
\mathcal{P}
.

–

Step 3
. One comes back from
(\mathcal{F}_{0}(X),\hat{f})
to
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
completing the circle.

The main argument in
Step 3
is that given any
K\in\mathcal{K}(X)
, any
\varepsilon>0
, and a set
u\in\mathcal{B}_{0}(\chi_{K},\varepsilon)
fulfilling some kind of
\mathcal{P}
-condition with respect to
\hat{f}
, then for each
\alpha\in\mathbb{I}
we have that

and that
u_{\alpha}
also fulfills the same
\mathcal{P}
-condition as

but with respect to
\overline{f}
.

For the sendograph and endograph metrics we have that
Step 2
will follow from the inclusions
\tau_{E}\subset\tau_{S}\subset\tau_{0}
. For
Step 3
, the following lemma is the key to get (

∗

‣
1
), all the improvements stated in the Introduction, and to solve Questions
1.1
and
1.2
in a rather general way:

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space, and assume that the sets
K\in\mathcal{K}(X)
and
u\in\mathcal{F}(X)
fulfill that
\delta:=d_{E}(\chi_{K},u)<\tfrac{1}{2}
. Then
d_{H}(K,u_{\alpha})\leq\delta
for every
\alpha\in\ ]\delta,1-\delta]
.

Proof.

Given any arbitrary but fixed
\alpha\in\ ]\delta,1-\delta]
we must show that
d_{H}(K,u_{\alpha})\leq\delta
or, equivalently, that we have the inclusions
K\subset u_{\alpha}+\delta
and
u_{\alpha}\subset K+\delta
(see Proposition
2.2
).

We start by showing
K\subset u_{\alpha}+\delta
. Indeed, since
d_{E}(\chi_{K},u)=\delta
, by Proposition
2.2
we have that

\operatorname{end}(\chi_{K})\subset\operatorname{end}(u)+\delta.

Hence, given
x\in K
we have that
(x,1)\in\operatorname{end}(\chi_{K})
and that there exists
(y,\beta)\in\operatorname{end}(u)
for which

\overline{d}\left((x,1),(y,\beta)\right)=\max\{d(x,y),|1-\beta|\}\leq\delta.

The latter implies that
u(y)\geq\beta\geq 1-\delta\geq\alpha
so that
y\in u_{\alpha}
. We deduce that
x\in\{y\}+\delta\subset u_{\alpha}+\delta
, and by the arbitrariness of
x\in K
we get that
K\subset u_{\alpha}+\delta
as we had to show.

Let us now check that
u_{\alpha}\subset K+\delta
. Indeed, and again since
d_{E}(\chi_{K},u)=\delta
, we also have that

\operatorname{end}(u)\subset\operatorname{end}(\chi_{K})+\delta.

Hence, given
x\in u_{\alpha}
there exists some
(y,\beta)\in\operatorname{end}(\chi_{K})
such that

\overline{d}\left((x,\alpha),(y,\beta)\right)=\max\{d(x,y),|\alpha-\beta|\}\leq\delta.

Then
\beta\in[\alpha-\delta,\alpha+\delta]\subset\ ]0,1]
, and since
\chi_{K}(y)\geq\beta>0
we have that
\chi_{K}(y)=1
and
y\in K
. We deduce that
x\in\{y\}+\delta\subset K+\delta
, and by the arbitrariness of
x\in u_{\alpha}
we get that
u_{\alpha}\subset K+\delta
.
∎

Transitivity, recurrence and Furstenberg families

In this section we study both transitivity and recurrence-kind properties from the Furstenberg families point of view. We start by improving the main transitivity-kind results from

]
and

]

in the positive (see Remark

-transitivity.

From now on, given any dynamical system

and any positive integer

where

of

3.1
Transitivity-kind properties

Following
[
20
]
, the main transitivity-kind notions studied in Topological Dynamics are transitivity itself, and the mixing and weak-mixing properties. We recall that a dynamical system
(X,f)
is called:

–

topologically transitive
(or just
transitive
) if given any pair of non-empty open subsets
U,V\subset X
there exists some (and hence infinitely many)
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
f^{n}(U)\cap V\neq\varnothing
;

–

topologically mixing
(or just
mixing
) if given any pair of non-empty open subsets
U,V\subset X
there exists some
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
f^{n}(U)\cap V\neq\varnothing
for every
n\geq n_{0}
;

–

topologically weakly-mixing
(or just
weakly-mixing
) if the
2
-fold direct product system
(X^{2},f_{(2)})
is topologically transitive.

The notion of
mixing
implies that of
weak-mixing
, which in its turn implies that of
transitivity
. Also, by a well-known result of Furstenberg we have that, once
(X,f)
is weakly-mixing, then
(X^{N},f_{(N)})
is transitive for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
(see
[
20
, Theorem 1.51]
). In addition, if
(X,f)
is weakly-mixing, then either
X
is a singleton (a fixed point), or
(X,d)
has no isolated points (see
[
20
, Exercise 1.2.3]
). These properties have recently been studied from the point of view of Furstenberg families and
\mathcal{A}
-transitivity:

–

a collection of sets
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
is called a
Furstenberg family
(or just a
family
) if it is hereditarily upward but
\varnothing\notin\mathcal{A}
, i.e. if given
A\in\mathcal{A}
and
B\subset\mathbb{N}_{0}
with
A\subset B
then
B\in\mathcal{A}
but
\mathcal{A}\neq\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
;

–

a dynamical system
(X,f)
is called
topologically
\mathcal{A}
-transitive
(or just
\mathcal{A}
-transitive
), for a family
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
, if given any pair of non-empty open subsets
U,V\subset X
the
return set from
U
to
V
, which will be denoted by
\mathcal{N}_{f}(U,V):=\{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\ ;\ f^{n}(U)\cap V\neq\varnothing\}
, belongs to the family
\mathcal{A}
.

Note that: the notion of
transitivity
coincides with that of
\mathcal{A}_{\infty}
-transitivity
for the family
\mathcal{A}_{\infty}
formed by the infinite subsets of
\mathbb{N}_{0}
; the notion of
mixing
coincides with that of
\mathcal{A}_{cf}
-transitivity
for the family
\mathcal{A}_{cf}
of co-finite sets; and it is well-known but non-trivial that the property of
weak-mixing
coincides with that of
\mathcal{A}_{th}
-transitivity
for the family
\mathcal{A}_{th}
formed by the so-called
thick sets
(i.e. the sets
A\subset\mathbb{N}_{0}
such that for every
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
there is
n\in A
with
[\![n,n+\ell]\!]\subset A
), see for instance
[
20
, Theorem 1.54]
.

We can now address our first main result, which improves
[
29
, Theorem 3 and Proposition 7]
since we add the equivalence regarding the system
(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{f})
, but also improves

, Theorem 2]
since we only assume that
\mathcal{A}
is any Furstenberg family and not necessarily a filter (see Remark

below):

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
be a Furstenberg family. Then, given a continuous map
f:X\longrightarrow X
acting on a metric space

, the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

(X,f)
is weakly-mixing and topologically
\mathcal{A}
-transitive;

(ii)

(X^{N},f_{(N)})
is topologically
\mathcal{A}
-transitive for every
N\in\mathbb{N}
;

(iii)

(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
is topologically
\mathcal{A}
-transitive;

(iv)

(\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(X),\hat{f})
is topologically
\mathcal{A}
-transitive;

(v)

(\mathcal{F}_{0}(X),\hat{f})
is topologically
\mathcal{A}
-transitive;

(vi)

(\mathcal{F}_{S}(X),\hat{f})
is topologically
\mathcal{A}
-transitive;

(vii)

(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{f})
is topologically
\mathcal{A}
-transitive.

Along the proof of Theorem

we repeatedly use that:
any of the statements

and

and

(i)
\Leftrightarrow

–

If any of the statements

\mathcal{A}^{\prime}:=\{A\subset\mathbb{N}_{0}\ ;\ \mathcal{N}_{f}(U,V)\subset A\text{ for some pair of non-empty open subsets }U,V\subset X\}.

It is not hard to check that

Remark 3.2
.

The reader should note that:

(1)

A positive answer to both Questions
1.1
and
1.2
follows now from Theorem
3.1
if we consider the family
\mathcal{A}_{\infty}
formed by the infinite subsets of
\mathbb{N}_{0}
. As a consequence we have that Theorem
3.1
significantly improves
[
29
, Theorem 3]
but also
[
29
, Proposition 7]
. Alternatively:

(2)

The fact that “
if
(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{f})
is transitive, then
(X,f)
is weakly-mixing
” can be directly proved using
[
20
, Proposition 1.53]
, which states that:
a dynamical system
(X,f)
is weakly-mixing if, and only if, for any pair of non-empty open subsets
U,V\subset X
we have that
\mathcal{N}_{f}(U,U)\cap\mathcal{N}_{f}(U,V)\neq\varnothing
. Indeed, to check that
(X,f)
is weakly-mixing let
x,y\in X
and
\varepsilon>0
be arbitrary but fixed, and let us prove that
\mathcal{N}_{f}(\mathcal{B}_{d}(x,\varepsilon),\mathcal{B}_{d}(x,\varepsilon))\cap\mathcal{N}_{f}(\mathcal{B}_{d}(x,\varepsilon),\mathcal{B}_{d}(y,\varepsilon))\neq\varnothing
, where
\mathcal{B}_{d}(x,\varepsilon)
and
\mathcal{B}_{d}(y,\varepsilon)
are the open
d
-balls of radius
\varepsilon
centered at
x
and
y
respectively. Assuming that
(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{f})
is transitive there are
u\in\mathcal{B}_{E}(\chi_{\{x\}},\varepsilon)
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\hat{f}^{n}(u)\in\mathcal{B}_{E}(\chi_{\{x,y\}},\varepsilon)
. Finally, if
\varepsilon
is small enough one can apply Lemma
2.4
exactly as in Theorem
3.1
to obtain the existence of points
z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathcal{B}_{d}(x,\varepsilon)
fulfilling that
f^{n}(z_{1})\in\mathcal{B}_{d}(x,\varepsilon)
and
f^{n}(z_{2})\in\mathcal{B}_{d}(y,\varepsilon)
, which completes the proof.

(3)

In statement (i) of Theorem
3.1
we can not drop the assumption “
(X,f)
is weakly-mixing
” unless the family
\mathcal{A}
is a
filter
, i.e. unless we have the condition
A\cap B\in\mathcal{A}
for every
A,B\in\mathcal{A}
. Indeed, the following example is well-known in Topological Dynamics:

–

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}
. The system
(\mathbb{T},f_{\alpha})
, defined on
\mathbb{T}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}\ ;\ |z|=1\}
as
f_{\alpha}(z):=ze^{i\pi\alpha}
, is transitive and even
\mathcal{A}_{syn}
-transitive
for the family
\mathcal{A}_{syn}
formed by the
syndetic sets
(i.e. the sets
A\subset\mathbb{N}_{0}
admitting some
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
such that
A\cap[\![n,n+\ell]\!]\neq\varnothing
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
). However, it is not hard to check that
(\mathbb{T},f_{\alpha})
is not weakly-mixing (see
[
20
, Example 1.43]
).

This example directly shows, although it is not hard to check, that the family
\mathcal{A}_{syn}
is not a filter.

Let us also mention that once a system
(X,f)
is weakly-mixing and
\mathcal{A}
-transitive then we have that the respective family
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
, considered in the proof of Theorem
3.1
and defined as

\mathcal{A}^{\prime}:=\{A\subset\mathbb{N}_{0}\ ;\ \mathcal{N}_{f}(U,V)\subset A\text{ for some pair of non-empty open subsets }U,V\subset X\},

is a subfamily of
\mathcal{A}
fulfilling that

is a filter (such a filter condition follows from

, Lemma 1.50]
applied to the weakly-mixing property). Hence, if a dynamical system

is weakly-mixing and

is indeed

3.2
Recurrence-kind properties

Following
[
2
]
, and having already introduced the concept of Furstenberg family, let us recall the recurrence-kind properties that we consider in this paper. A dynamical system
(X,f)
is called:

–

topologically recurrent
(or just
recurrent
) if given any non-empty open subset
U\subset X
there exists some (and hence infinitely many)
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
f^{n}(U)\cap U\neq\varnothing
;

–

topologically multiply recurrent
(or just
multiply recurrent
) if given any positive integer
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
and any non-empty open subset
U\subset X
there exists some (and hence infinitely many)
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\bigcap_{0\leq j\leq\ell}f^{-jn}(U)=U\cap f^{-n}(U)\cap f^{-2n}(U)\cap\cdots\cap f^{-\ell n}(U)\neq\varnothing;

–

topologically
\mathcal{A}
-recurrent
(or just
\mathcal{A}
-recurrent
), for a Furstenberg family
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
, if given any non-empty open subset
U\subset X
we have that the return set
\mathcal{N}_{f}(U,U)=\{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\ ;\ f^{n}(U)\cap U\neq\varnothing\}
, which from now on will be simply denoted by
\mathcal{N}_{f}(U):=\mathcal{N}_{f}(U,U)
, belongs to the family
\mathcal{A}
.

These properties have recently been studied (see for instance
[
13
,
34
]
and
[
1
]
). Note that, even though the notion of
topological recurrence
is implied by that of
multiple recurrence
and also by that of
topological
\mathcal{A}
-recurrence
for any family
\mathcal{A}
, there is no clear relation between
multiple recurrence
and
topological
\mathcal{A}
-recurrence
as argued in
[
2
, Remark 2.1]
. For that reason, and in order to give a unified treatment to the previous properties, we will use the following notion recently introduced in
[
2
]
:

–

a dynamical system
(X,f)
is called
topologically
(\ell,\mathcal{A})
-recurrent
(or just
(\ell,\mathcal{A})
-recurrent
), for a positive integer
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
and a Furstenberg family
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{N})
, if given any non-empty open subset
U\subset X
we have that the
\ell
-return set from
U
to itself
, which will be denoted by

\mathcal{N}_{f}^{\ell}(U):=\left\{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\ ;\ \bigcap_{0\leq j\leq\ell}f^{-jn}(U)\neq\varnothing\right\},

belongs to the family
\mathcal{A}
.

Our next results improves
[
2
, Theorem 4.1]
by adding the sendograph and endograph equivalences:

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
be a positive integer and let
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
be a Furstenberg family. Then, given a continuous map
f:X\longrightarrow X
on a metric space
(X,d)
, the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

(X^{N},f_{(N)})
is topologically
(\ell,\mathcal{A})
-recurrent for every
N\in\mathbb{N}
;

(ii)

(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
is topologically
(\ell,\mathcal{A})
-recurrent;

(iii)

(\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(X),\hat{f})
is topologically
(\ell,\mathcal{A})
-recurrent;

(iv)

(\mathcal{F}_{0}(X),\hat{f})
is topologically
(\ell,\mathcal{A})
-recurrent;

(v)

(\mathcal{F}_{S}(X),\hat{f})
is topologically
(\ell,\mathcal{A})
-recurrent;

(vi)

(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{f})
is topologically
(\ell,\mathcal{A})
-recurrent.

As a consequence, these equivalences hold if we replace “topological
(\ell,\mathcal{A})
-recurrence” with any of the particular properties “topological recurrence”, “multiple recurrence” or “topological
\mathcal{A}
-recurrence”.

Note that the last sentence of Theorem
3.3
follows because:
topological recurrence
coincides with
topological
(1,\mathcal{A}_{\infty})
-recurrence
;
multiple recurrence
coincides with being
topologically
(\ell,\mathcal{A}_{\infty})
-recurrent
for every
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
; and
topological
\mathcal{A}
-recurrence
coincides with
topological
(1,\mathcal{A})
-recurrence
.

Proof of Theorem
3.3
.

(i)
\Leftrightarrow
(ii)
\Leftrightarrow
(iii)
\Leftrightarrow
(iv): This was proved in
[
2
, Theorem 4.1]
.

(iv)
\Rightarrow
(v)
\Rightarrow
(vi): This trivially follows from the inclusions
\tau_{E}\subset\tau_{S}\subset\tau_{0}
.

(vi)
\Rightarrow
(ii): Given any set
K\in\mathcal{K}(X)
and any
\varepsilon>0
we must show that the return set

A:=\mathcal{N}_{\overline{f}}^{\ell}(\mathcal{B}_{H}(K,\varepsilon))

belongs to
\mathcal{A}
. Without loss of generality we will assume that
0<\varepsilon\leq\frac{1}{2}
. Since
(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{f})
is assumed to be topologically
(\ell,\mathcal{A})
-recurrent we have that

B:=\mathcal{N}_{\hat{f}}^{\ell}\left(\mathcal{B}_{E}(\chi_{K},\varepsilon)\right)\in\mathcal{A}.

Thus, given any arbitrary but fixed

\delta:=\max\left\{d_{E}(\chi_{K},\hat{f}^{jn}(u))\ ;\ 0\leq j\leq\ell\right\}<\varepsilon\leq\tfrac{1}{2},

. The arbitrariness of

Remark 3.4
.

As it happens in Theorem
3.1
and we mention in part (3) of Remark
3.2
, if the Furstenberg family
\mathcal{A}
considered in the statement of Theorem
3.3
is not a filter then we cannot weaken the assumption “
(X^{N},f_{(N)})
is topologically
(\ell,\mathcal{A})
-recurrent for every
N\in\mathbb{N}
”. Indeed, it has recently been showed in
[
18
, Theorem 3.2]
that given any
N\in\mathbb{N}
there exists a dynamical system
(X,f)
fulfilling that
(X^{N},f_{(N)})
is topologically recurrent (and even multiply recurrent), but also fulfilling that the system
(X^{N+1},f_{(N+1)})
is not topologically recurrent (neither multiply recurrent).

3.3
The case of complete metric spaces

Following
[
2
,
29
]
, if given a system
(X,f)
we assume that
(X,d)
is a
complete
metric space, then we can improve Theorems
3.1
and
3.3
in terms of
point-transitivity
and
point-recurrence
by using some concrete
Baire category arguments
. In this subsection we also consider the generalizations of these properties in terms of Furstenberg families, namely
point-
\mathcal{A}
-transitivity
and
point-
\mathcal{A}
-recurrence
, obtaining some completely new results (see Lemma
3.6
, Theorem
3.7
and Corollary
3.9
below).

Let us start by recalling the basic definitions used in
[
2
,
29
]
: given a dynamical system
(X,f)
and a point
x\in X
we will denote its
f
-orbit
by

\mathcal{O}_{f}(x):=\{f^{n}(x)\ ;\ n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\},

and given any subset
U\subset X
we will denote the
return set from
x
to
U
by

\mathcal{N}_{f}(x,U):=\{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\ ;\ f^{n}(x)\in U\}.

Following
[
2
]
and
[
29
]
we will say that a point
x\in X
is:

–

transitive for
f
if the
f
-orbit of
x
is dense in the space
X
, i.e.
X=\overline{\mathcal{O}_{f}(x)}
; equivalently, if for every non-empty open subset
U\subset X
the return set
\mathcal{N}_{f}(x,U)
is non-empty. We will denote by
\text{Tran}(f)
the
set of transitive points for
f
, and
(X,f)
is called
point-transitive
if the set
\text{Tran}(f)
is non-empty.

–

recurrent for
f
if
x
belongs to the closure of its forward orbit, i.e.
x\in\overline{\mathcal{O}_{f}(f(x))}
; equivalently, if for every neighbourhood
U
of
x
the return set
\mathcal{N}_{f}(x,U)
is an infinite set. We will denote by
\text{Rec}(f)
the
set of recurrent points for
f
, and
(X,f)
is called
point-recurrent
if the set
\text{Rec}(f)
is dense in
X
.

–

\mathcal{AP}
-recurrent for
f
if for every neighbourhood
U
of
x
the return set
\mathcal{N}_{f}(x,U)
contains arbitrarily long arithmetic progressions, i.e. if for each positive integer
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
there exist two numbers
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
such that
\{n_{0}+jn\ ;\ 0\leq j\leq\ell\}\subset\mathcal{N}_{f}(x,U)
. We will denote by
\mathcal{AP}\text{Rec}(f)
the
set of
\mathcal{AP}
-recurrent points for
f
, and
(X,f)
is called
point-
\mathcal{AP}
-recurrent
if
\mathcal{AP}\text{Rec}(f)
is dense in
X
.

Let us recall: firstly, that every
point-recurrent
dynamical system is
topologically recurrent
, that
point-
\mathcal{AP}
-recurrence
implies
multiply recurrence
, and that the converse statements hold for completely metrizable spaces (see
[
13
, Proposition 2.1]
and
[
34
, Lemma 4.8]
); and secondly, that
point-transitivity
implies
topological transitivity
on metric spaces without isolated points, and that the converse holds on separable completely metrizable spaces (see
[
20
, Theorem 1.16]
). Moreover, one can show with not too much difficulty that for any metric space
(X,d)
, the associated fuzzy metric space
\mathcal{F}_{E}(X)
is either a singleton (precisely when
X
is a singleton) or has no isolated points, regardless of whether
(X,d)
itself has isolated points (a proof of this fact will appear in
[
39
]
).

We are now ready to improve both
[
2
, Corollary 5.1]
and
[
29
, Theorem 4]
:

Corollary 3.5
.

Let
f:X\longrightarrow X
be a continuous map on a complete metric space
(X,d)
. Hence:

(a)

The following statements are equivalent:

(i)

(X^{N},f_{(N)})
is point-recurrent (resp. point-
\mathcal{AP}
-recurrent) for every
N\in\mathbb{N}
;

(ii)

(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
is point-recurrent (resp. point-
\mathcal{AP}
-recurrent);

(iii)

(\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(X),\hat{f})
is point-recurrent (resp. point-
\mathcal{AP}
-recurrent);

(iv)

(\mathcal{F}_{0}(X),\hat{f})
is point-recurrent (resp. point-
\mathcal{AP}
-recurrent);

(v)

(\mathcal{F}_{S}(X),\hat{f})
is point-recurrent (resp. point-
\mathcal{AP}
-recurrent);

(vi)

(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{f})
is point-recurrent (resp. point-
\mathcal{AP}
-recurrent).

(b)

If
(X,d)
is also separable, then the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

(X^{2},f_{(2)})
is point-transitive;

(ii)

(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
is point-transitive;

(iii)

(\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(X),\hat{f})
is topologically transitive;

(iv)

(\mathcal{F}_{0}(X),\hat{f})

(v)

(\mathcal{F}_{S}(X),\hat{f})

(vi)

Proof.

(i)
\Leftrightarrow
(ii)
\Leftrightarrow
(iii)
\Leftrightarrow
(iv): Part (a) was proved in
[
2
, Corollary 5.1]
and (b) in
[

, Theorem 4]
.

(iv)
\Rightarrow
(v)
\Rightarrow
(vi): This trivially follows from the inclusions
\tau_{E}\subset\tau_{S}\subset\tau_{0}
.

(vi)
\Rightarrow
(i): If
(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{f})
is point-recurrent, point-
\mathcal{AP}
-recurrent or point-transitive respectively, then
(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{f})
is topologically recurrent, multiply recurrent or topologically transitive respectively (for topological transitivity we used that
\mathcal{F}_{E}(X)
is either a singleton or has no isolated points). Thus, Theorem
3.3
in case (a) and Theorem
3.1
in case (b), for the family
\mathcal{A}_{\infty}
formed by the infinite subsets of
\mathbb{N}_{0}
, imply that
(X^{N},f_{(N)})
is topologically recurrent, multiply recurrent or topologically transitive respectively, for every
N\in\mathbb{N}
. Statement (i) in part (a) follows now from the completeness assumption on

by using
[
13
, Proposition 2.1]
or
[

, Lemma 4.8]
respectively. Statement (i) in part (b) follows from the separability and completeness assumptions on

by using
[
20
, Theorem 1.16]
.∎

In view of Corollary
3.5
one may wonder if the notions of
point-transitivity
and
point-recurrence
can also be studied from the Furstenberg families point of view. Indeed, following
[
8
]
, given a Furstenberg family
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
and a dynamical system
(X,f)
we will say that a point
x\in X
is:

–

\mathcal{A}
-transitive for
f
if
\mathcal{N}_{f}(x,U)\in\mathcal{A}
for every non-empty open subset
U\subset X
. We denote by
\mathcal{A}\text{Tran}(f)
the
set of
\mathcal{A}
-transitive points for
f
, and
(X,f)
is called
point-
\mathcal{A}
-transitive
if
\mathcal{A}\text{Tran}(f)
is non-empty.

–

\mathcal{A}
-recurrent for
f
if
\mathcal{N}_{f}(x,U)\in\mathcal{A}
for every neighbourhood
U
of
x
. We denote by
\mathcal{A}\text{Rec}(f)
the
set of
\mathcal{A}
-recurrent points for
f
, and
(X,f)
is called
point-
\mathcal{A}
-recurrent
if
\mathcal{A}\text{Rec}(f)
is dense in
X
.

To finish the section we aim to derive a “point-wise version” of Theorem
3.1
. This kind of question seems to be completely new in the framework of “
comparing the dynamical properties of
(X,f)
with those of
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
and
(\mathcal{F}(X),\hat{f})
”. The main difficulty here will be that for a general Furstenberg family
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
there is no Baire category argument to obtain an
\mathcal{A}
-transitive point, even under the assumption of very strong topological transitivity properties such as topological mixing. To avoid this issue we will consider a particular class of families (see Theorem
3.7
below). Let us first prove the following general lemma, whose statement (b) significantly improves
[
29
, Proposition 9]
:

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
be a Furstenberg family. Then, given a continuous map
f:X\longrightarrow X
on a (not necessarily separable, neither complete) metric space
(X,d)
, the following statements hold:

(a)

If
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
is point-
\mathcal{A}
-transitive, then
(X,f)
is point-
\mathcal{A}
-transitive.

(b)

If
(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{f})
is point-
\mathcal{A}
-transitive, then
(X,f)
is point-
\mathcal{A}
-transitive.

(c)

If
(X,f)
is point-
\mathcal{A}
-transitive, then
(X^{N},f_{(N)})
is point-
\mathcal{A}
-recurrent for every
N\in\mathbb{N}
.

(d)

If
(X^{N},f_{(N)})
is point-
\mathcal{A}
-recurrent for every
N\in\mathbb{N}
, then
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
is point-
\mathcal{A}
-recurrent.

(e)

If
(X^{N},f_{(N)})
is point-
\mathcal{A}
-recurrent for every
N\in\mathbb{N}
, then
(\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(X),\hat{f})
is point-
\mathcal{A}
-recurrent.

Proof.

(a): Let
K\in\mathcal{K}(X)
be an
\mathcal{A}
-transitive point for the map
\overline{f}
and pick any point
x\in K
. We claim that
x\in\mathcal{A}\text{Tran}(f)
. Indeed, fixed any
y\in X
and
\varepsilon>0
, since
K\in\mathcal{A}\text{Tran}(\overline{f})
we have that

\mathcal{N}_{\overline{f}}(K,\mathcal{B}_{H}(\{y\},\varepsilon))\in\mathcal{A}.

Hence, given any
n\in\mathcal{N}_{\overline{f}}(K,\mathcal{B}_{H}(\{y\},\varepsilon))
we have that
f^{n}(x)\in f^{n}(K)=\overline{f}^{n}(K)\subset\{y\}+\delta_{n}
for some
0<\delta_{n}<\varepsilon
, so that
d(f^{n}(x),y)\leq\delta_{n}<\varepsilon
. We deduce that
\mathcal{N}_{\overline{f}}(K,\mathcal{B}_{H}(\{y\},\varepsilon))\subset\mathcal{N}_{f}(x,\mathcal{B}_{d}(y,\varepsilon))
, which implies that
\mathcal{N}_{f}(x,\mathcal{B}_{d}(y,\varepsilon))\in\mathcal{A}
. The arbitrariness of
y\in X
and
\varepsilon>0
proves the claim.

(b): Let
u\in\mathcal{F}(X)
be an
\mathcal{A}
-transitive point for
\hat{f}
with respect to
d_{E}
and pick any point
x\in u_{1}
. We claim that
x\in\mathcal{A}\text{Tran}(f)
. Indeed, fixed any point
y\in X
and any positive value
0<\varepsilon\leq 1
, since
u
is
\mathcal{A}
-transitive we have that

\mathcal{N}_{\hat{f}}(u,\mathcal{B}_{E}(\chi_{\{y\}},\varepsilon))\in\mathcal{A}.

Hence, given any arbitrary but fixed
n\in\mathcal{N}_{\hat{f}}(u,\mathcal{B}_{E}(\chi_{\{y\}},\varepsilon))
we have that
\delta_{n}:=d_{E}(\hat{f}^{n}(u),\chi_{\{y\}})<\varepsilon
. The latter implies that
\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}^{n}(u))\subset\operatorname{end}(\chi_{\{y\}})+\delta_{n}
. In particular, since
[\hat{f}^{n}(u)]_{1}=f^{n}(u_{1})
, we have that
(f^{n}(x),1)\in\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}^{n}(u))
and hence there exist some
(z,\alpha)\in\operatorname{end}(\chi_{\{y\}})
such that

\overline{d}_{H}\left((f^{n}(x),1),(z,\alpha)\right):=\max\left\{d(f^{n}(x),z),|1-\alpha|\right\}\leq\delta_{n}<\varepsilon\leq 1.

We deduce that
\chi_{\{y\}}(z)\geq\alpha>0
, so that
z=y
,
d(f^{n}(x),y)<\varepsilon
and hence
n\in\mathcal{N}_{f}(x,\mathcal{B}_{d}(y,\varepsilon))
. The arbitrariness of
n\in\mathcal{N}_{\hat{f}}(u,\mathcal{B}_{E}(\chi_{\{y\}},\varepsilon))
shows that
\mathcal{N}_{\hat{f}}(u,\mathcal{B}_{E}(\chi_{\{y\}},\varepsilon))\subset\mathcal{N}_{f}(x,\mathcal{B}_{d}(y,\varepsilon))
and hence that
\mathcal{N}_{f}(x,\mathcal{B}_{d}(y,\varepsilon))\in\mathcal{A}
. The arbitrariness of
y\in X
and
0<\varepsilon\leq 1
proves the claim.

(c): This is a well-known general dynamical systems property that has recently and independently been proved in
[
11
, Proposition 3.8]
and
[
19
, Proposition 4.3 and Remark 4.4]
. Let us mention that in the references
[
11
,
19
]
the notion of “point-
\mathcal{A}
-transitivity” is called “
\mathcal{A}
-hypercyclicity” and the notion of “point-
\mathcal{A}
-recurrence” is simply called “
\mathcal{A}
-recurrence” for each Furstenberg family
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
.

To prove statements (d) and (e) we will use that:
for any
K\in\mathcal{K}(X)
and
\varepsilon>0
there exist points
y_{1},...,y_{N}\in K
such that every finite set
L:=\{z_{1},z_{2},...,z_{N}\}
with
d(y_{j},z_{j})<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
for all
1\leq j\leq N
fulfills that
d_{H}(K,L)<\varepsilon
. This fact is probably folklore, but the reader can easily check it by using the compactness condition of
K
together with statement (a) of Proposition
2.2
.

(d): Given
K\in\mathcal{K}(X)
and
\varepsilon>0
let
y_{1},...,y_{N}\in K
be such that every
L:=\{z_{1},z_{2},...,z_{N}\}
with
d(y_{j},z_{j})<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
for all
1\leq j\leq N
fulfills that
d_{H}(K,L)<\varepsilon
. Using the point-
\mathcal{A}
-recurrence of
(X^{N},f_{(N)})
we can choose a point

(x_{1},...,x_{N})\in\mathcal{B}_{d}(y_{1},\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2})\times\cdots\times\mathcal{B}_{d}(y_{N},\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2})\cap\mathcal{A}\text{Rec}(f_{(N)}).

We omit the routine verification that
\{x_{1},...,x_{N}\}\in\mathcal{B}_{H}(K,\varepsilon)\cap\mathcal{A}\text{Rec}(\overline{f})
, which implies that the set
\mathcal{A}\text{Rec}(\overline{f})
is dense in
\mathcal{K}(X)
by the arbitrariness of
K\in\mathcal{K}(X)
and
\varepsilon>0
.

(e): Given any normal fuzzy set
u\in\mathcal{F}(X)
and
\varepsilon>0
we can use Lemma
2.3
to obtain positive numbers
0=\alpha_{0}<\alpha_{1}<\alpha_{2}<...<\alpha_{N}=1
such that
d_{H}(u_{\alpha},u_{\alpha_{j+1}})<\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}
for each
\alpha\in\ ]\alpha_{j},\alpha_{j+1}]
with
0\leq j\leq N-1
. Hence, for each
1\leq j\leq N
, one can find points
y_{1}^{j},...,y_{k_{j}}^{j}\in u_{\alpha_{j}}
such that every finite set
L:=\{z_{1},z_{2},...,z_{k_{j}}\}
with
d(y_{l}^{j},z_{l})<\frac{\varepsilon}{4}
for all
1\leq l\leq k_{j}
fulfills that
d_{H}(u_{\alpha_{j}},L)<\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}
. Using the point-
\mathcal{A}
-recurrence of
(X^{M},f_{(M)})
for
M=\sum_{j=1}^{N}k_{j}
we can choose a point

(x_{1}^{1},...,x_{k_{1}}^{1},...,x_{1}^{j},...,x_{k_{j}}^{j},...,x_{1}^{N},...,x_{k_{N}}^{N})\in\prod_{l=1}^{k_{1}}\mathcal{B}_{d}(y_{l}^{1},\tfrac{\varepsilon}{4})\times\cdots\times\prod_{l=1}^{k_{j}}\mathcal{B}_{d}(y_{l}^{j},\tfrac{\varepsilon}{4})\times\cdots\times\prod_{l=1}^{k_{N}}\mathcal{B}_{d}(y_{l}^{N},\tfrac{\varepsilon}{4})

but also fulfilling that

(x_{1}^{1},...,x_{k_{1}}^{1},...,x_{1}^{j},...,x_{k_{j}}^{j},...,x_{1}^{N},...,x_{k_{N}}^{N})\cap\mathcal{A}\text{Rec}(f_{(M)}).

Considering
K_{j}:=\{x_{1}^{j},...,x_{k_{j}}^{j}\}
for each
1\leq j\leq N
and letting
v:=\max_{1\leq j\leq N}(\alpha_{j}\cdot\chi_{K_{j}})
it follows that
v
is a normal fuzzy set. Arguing now as in
[
2
, Theorem 4.1 and Remark 5.2]
, which essentially uses statement (b) of Proposition
2.2
, one can check that
v\in\mathcal{B}_{\infty}(u,\varepsilon)\cap\mathcal{A}\text{Rec}(\hat{f})
. This finally implies that the set
\mathcal{A}\text{Rec}(\hat{f})
is dense in
\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(X)
by the arbitrariness of
u\in\mathcal{F}(X)
and
\varepsilon>0
.
∎

We are about to obtain a “point-wise version” of Theorem
3.1
but with a slight restriction on the Furstenberg families allowed. In particular, following
[
11
, Definition 3.3]
but also
[
15
,
23
,
35
]
:

–

given a Furstenberg family
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
we define
b\mathcal{A}
, called the
block family associated to
\mathcal{A}
, in the following way: a set
B\subset\mathbb{N}_{0}
belongs to
b\mathcal{A}
if there exists some
A_{B}\in\mathcal{A}
such that for each finite subset
F\subset A_{B}
there is some
n_{F}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
for which
F+n_{F}:=\{m+n_{F}\ ;\ m\in F\}\subset B
.

–

a Furstenberg family
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
is called a
block family
if we have the equality
b\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}
.

Roughly speaking, the block family
b\mathcal{A}
associated to
\mathcal{A}
is the collection of sets that contain every finite block
F
from a fixed set of the original family
\mathcal{A}
, but possibly translated by
n_{F}
. Some examples and basic properties, such as the inclusion
\mathcal{A}\subset b\mathcal{A}
or the equality
b(b\mathcal{A})=b\mathcal{A}
for every family
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
, are exposed in
[
11
, Section 3]
. The next crucial fact is also essentially proved there:

–

[
11
, Proposition 3.6]
:
Let
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
be a block Furstenberg family. If a dynamical system
(X,f)
is both point-transitive and point-
\mathcal{A}
-recurrent, then
(X,f)
is point-
\mathcal{A}
-transitive.

For the shake of completeness, and since this result was originally proved for linear dynamical systems, we include here the main argument of its proof: if
(X,f)
is both point-transitive and point-
\mathcal{A}
-recurrent, then we claim that every transitive point is indeed
\mathcal{A}
-transitive. In fact, given any point
x\in\text{Tran}(f)
and any non-empty open subset
U\subset X
we can pick an
\mathcal{A}
-recurrent point
y\in U\cap\mathcal{A}\text{Rec}(f)
so that
\mathcal{N}_{f}(y,U)\in\mathcal{A}
. Fixed now any finite subset
F\subset\mathcal{N}_{f}(y,U)
we have that
V_{F}:=\bigcap_{m\in F}f^{-m}(U)
is an open subset of
X
containing
y
, so that there exists
n_{F}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
for which
f^{n_{F}}(x)\in V_{F}
and hence
F+n_{F}\subset\mathcal{N}_{f}(x,U)
. We deduce that
\mathcal{N}_{f}(x,U)\in b\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}
and hence that
x\in\mathcal{A}\text{Tran}(f)
. By Lemma
3.6
we can now obtain the already announced (and as far as we know completely new) result:

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
be a block Furstenberg family. Given a continuous map
f:X\longrightarrow X
on a separable complete metric space
(X,d)
, the following are equivalent:

(i)

(X,f)
is weakly-mixing and point-
\mathcal{A}
-transitive;

(ii)

(X^{N},f_{(N)})
is point-
\mathcal{A}
-transitive for every
N\in\mathbb{N}
;

(iii)

(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
is point-
\mathcal{A}
-transitive;

(iv)

(\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(X),\hat{f})
is topologically transitive and point-
\mathcal{A}
-recurrent;

(v)

(\mathcal{F}_{0}(X),\hat{f})
is point-
\mathcal{A}
-transitive;

(vi)

(\mathcal{F}_{S}(X),\hat{f})
is point-
\mathcal{A}
-transitive;

(vii)

(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{f})
is point-
\mathcal{A}
-transitive.

Proof.

(i)
\Leftrightarrow
(ii): The implication (i)
\Rightarrow
(ii) follows from the separability and completeness of
(X,d)
together with statement (c) of Lemma
3.6
and
[
11
, Proposition 3.6]
, while (ii)
\Rightarrow
(i) is trivial.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(iii),(iv): These implications follow from statements (c), (d) and (e) of Lemma
3.6
together with part (b) of Corollary
3.5
and
[
11
, Proposition 3.6]
.

(iv)
\Rightarrow
(v)
\Rightarrow
(vi)
\Rightarrow
(vii): This follows from part (b) of Corollary
3.5
together with the topology inclusions
\tau_{E}\subset\tau_{S}\subset\tau_{0}\subset\tau_{\infty}
and
[
11
, Proposition 3.6]
.

(iii),(vii)
\Rightarrow
(i): These implications follow from statements (a) and (b) of Lemma
3.6
together with Theorem
3.1
for the family
\mathcal{A}_{\infty}
formed by the infinite subsets of
\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
∎

Remark 3.8
.

Let us mention some examples of block families and applications of Theorem
3.7
:

(1)

The family
\mathcal{A}_{\neq\varnothing}
formed by all non-empty subsets of
\mathbb{N}_{0}
and also the already mentioned family
\mathcal{A}_{\infty}
formed by the infinite subsets of
\mathbb{N}_{0}
are easily checked to be block families. It is worth noticing that
point-
\mathcal{A}_{\neq\varnothing}
-transitivity
is exactly equal to the usual notion of
point-transitivity
. Moreover, if a metric space
(X,d)
is a singleton or has no isolated points, then
point-
\mathcal{A}_{\neq\varnothing}
-transitivity
also coincides with the notion of
point-
\mathcal{A}_{\infty}
-transitivity
for every dynamical system
(X,f)
. Thus, since
(X,d)
is either a singleton or has no isolated points whenever
(X,f)
is weakly-mixing, the version of Theorem
3.7
for any of the families
\mathcal{A}_{\neq\varnothing}
or
\mathcal{A}_{\infty}
was already proved in part (b) of Corollary
3.5
.

(2)

The family
\mathcal{AP}
formed by the sets that contain arbitrarily long arithmetic progressions (i.e. the sets
A\subset\mathbb{N}_{0}
such that given
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
there exist
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
with
\{n_{0}+jn\ ;\ 0\leq j\leq\ell\}\subset A
) is also easily checked to be a block family. Note that along this paper we have already used the notion of
point-
\mathcal{AP}
-recurrence
in Corollary
3.5
. Indeed, by
[
11
, Proposition 3.6]
we have that the version of Theorem
3.7
for the family
\mathcal{AP}
is a consequence of both parts (a) and (b) of Corollary
3.5
.

(3)

The family of positive upper Banach density sets
\overline{\mathcal{BD}}:=\{A\subset\mathbb{N}_{0}\ ;\ \overline{\operatorname{Bd}}(A)>0\}
, where

\overline{\operatorname{Bd}}(A):=\limsup_{N\to\infty}\left(\max_{m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\frac{\#(A\cap[m,m+N])}{N+1}\right)\quad\text{ for each }A\subset\mathbb{N}_{0},

is also easily checked to be a block family (see
[
11
, Example 3.5]
for further details). The version of Theorem
3.7
for the family
\overline{\mathcal{BD}}
is, as far as we know, a completely new result in the literature. The
\overline{\mathcal{BD}}
-transitive points
have been called
points with reiteratively dense orbit
but also
reiteratively hypercyclic points
(see for instance
[
10
,
17
,
19
,
37
,
40
]
). We consider worth recalling here that there are
topologically mixing
systems that are not
point-
\overline{\mathcal{BD}}
-transitive
, i.e. there is no general Baire category argument to show the existence of a
\overline{\mathcal{BD}}
-transitive point (see
[
8
, Theorem 13]
).

Using Remark
3.8
, we end this section by showing an application of Theorem
3.7
in the context of
linear dynamical systems
, where we let
f=T
be a
continuous linear operator
acting on a usually
infinite-dimensional Fréchet space

X
(i.e. a completely metrizable locally convex vector space). In this setting it is well-known that
point-
\overline{\mathcal{BD}}
-transitivity
(which has been called
reiterative hypercyclicity
) implies the notion of weak-mixing (see
[
8
, Proposition 4]
). Thus, by Theorem
3.7
we get the following:

Corollary 3.9
.

Let
T:X\longrightarrow X
be a continuous linear operator acting on a Fréchet space
X
. Then, the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

(X,T)
is point-
\overline{\mathcal{BD}}
-transitive (i.e. reiteratively hypercyclic);

(ii)

(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{T})
is point-
\overline{\mathcal{BD}}
-transitive (i.e. reiteratively hypercyclic);

(iii)

(\mathcal{F}_{0}(X),\hat{T})
is point-
\overline{\mathcal{BD}}
-transitive (i.e. reiteratively hypercyclic);

(iv)

(\mathcal{F}_{S}(X),\hat{T})
is point-
\overline{\mathcal{BD}}
-transitive (i.e. reiteratively hypercyclic);

(v)

(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{T})
is point-
\overline{\mathcal{BD}}
-transitive (i.e. reiteratively hypercyclic).

We do not know if Theorem
3.7
holds for non-block families. Indeed, for the highly studied family of positive lower density sets
\underline{\mathcal{D}}:=\{A\subset\mathbb{N}_{0}\ ;\ \underline{\operatorname{dens}}(A)>0\}
, where

\underline{\operatorname{dens}}(A):=\liminf_{N\to\infty}\frac{\#(A\cap[0,N])}{N+1}\quad\text{ for each }A\subset\mathbb{N}_{0},

it is not even known if
point-
\underline{\mathcal{D}}
-transitivity
for a linear system
(X,T)
implies or not the respective
point-
\underline{\mathcal{D}}
-transitivity
for
(X^{2},T_{(2)})
. This would be a first crucial step to get a similar result to that of Corollary
3.9
for
point-
\underline{\mathcal{D}}
-transitivity
(also called
frequent hypercyclicity
in the literature, see
[
6
,
20
,
38
]
).

4
Devaney chaos and the specification property

In this section we focus on Devaney chaos and the specification property, which were considered in the references
[
41
]
and
[
4
]
for fuzzy dynamical systems as described in this paper. In this section, contrary to what we have done in the previous part of this paper, we do not obtain new results. In fact, we only extend the already existent results to the case of the sendograph and endograph metrics.

4.1
Dense sets of periodic points and Devaney chaos

Although there is no unified concept of chaos, the notion of Devaney chaos is one of the most usual definitions from those considered in the literature. Given a dynamical system
(X,f)
:

–

a point
x\in X
is called
periodic for
f
if there is some
p\in\mathbb{N}
such that
f^{p}(x)=x
. The minimum positive integer
p\in\mathbb{N}
fulfilling that
f^{p}(x)=x
is called the
period of
x
, and we will denote by
\operatorname{Per}(f)
the
set of periodic points for
f
.

–

we say that
(X,f)
is
Devaney chaotic
if it is a topologically transitive system and also the set of periodic points
\operatorname{Per}(f)
is dense in the space
X
.

It was noticed in
[
41
, Lemma 1]
that, given a dynamical system
(X,f)
, then a compact set
K\in\mathcal{K}(X)
is periodic for
\overline{f}
with period
p\in\mathbb{N}
if and only if the fuzzy set
\chi_{K}\in\mathcal{F}(X)
is periodic for
\hat{f}
with the same period
p\in\mathbb{N}
. Using our Lemma
2.4
we can now extend
[
41
, Proposition 2]
:

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
f:X\longrightarrow X
be a continuous map on a metric space
(X,d)
. Then, the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

the set of periodic points
\operatorname{Per}(\overline{f})
is dense in
(\mathcal{K}(X),d_{H})
;

(ii)

the set of periodic points
\operatorname{Per}(\hat{f})
is dense in
\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(X)
;

(iii)

the set of periodic points
\operatorname{Per}(\hat{f})
is dense in
\mathcal{F}_{0}(X)
;

(iv)

the set of periodic points
\operatorname{Per}(\hat{f})
is dense in
\mathcal{F}_{S}(X)
;

(v)

the set of periodic points
\operatorname{Per}(\hat{f})
is dense in
\mathcal{F}_{E}(X)
.

Proof.

(i)
\Leftrightarrow
(ii)
\Leftrightarrow
(iii): This was proved in
[
41
, Proposition 2]
.

(iii)
\Rightarrow
(iv)
\Rightarrow
(v): This trivially follows from the inclusions
\tau_{E}\subset\tau_{S}\subset\tau_{0}
.

(v)
\Rightarrow
(i): Given any set
K\in\mathcal{K}(X)
and any
\varepsilon>0
we must show that
\mathcal{B}_{H}(K,\varepsilon)\cap\operatorname{Per}(\overline{f})\neq\varnothing
. Without loss of generality we will assume that
0<\varepsilon\leq\frac{1}{2}
. Using that
\operatorname{Per}(\hat{f})
is dense in
\mathcal{F}_{E}(X)
we can find some fuzzy set
u\in\mathcal{F}(X)
such that

u\in\mathcal{B}_{E}(\chi_{K},\varepsilon)\cap\operatorname{Per}(\hat{f}).

Letting
\delta:=d_{E}(\chi_{K},u)<\varepsilon\leq\frac{1}{2}
and picking any
\alpha\in\ ]\delta,1-\delta]
, Lemma
2.4
tells us that
d_{H}\left(K,u_{\alpha}\right)<\varepsilon
. Moreover, since
u\in\operatorname{Per}(\hat{f})
there exists some
p\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\hat{f}^{p}(u)=u
, which implies that

\overline{f}^{p}(u_{\alpha})=f^{p}(u_{\alpha})=[\hat{f}^{p}(u)]_{\alpha}=u_{\alpha}.

We conclude that
u_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{B}_{H}(K,\varepsilon)\cap\operatorname{Per}(\overline{f})
, finishing the proof.
∎

From the definition of Devaney chaos, using Theorem
3.1
for the family
\mathcal{A}_{\infty}
together with Lemma
4.1
and the original results
[
41
, Corollary 1 and Theorem 1]
we get that:

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
f:X\longrightarrow X
be a continuous map on a metric space
(X,d)
. Then, the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
is Devaney chaotic;

(ii)

(\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(X),\hat{f})
is Devaney chaotic;

(iii)

(\mathcal{F}_{0}(X),\hat{f})
is Devaney chaotic;

(iv)

(\mathcal{F}_{S}(X),\hat{f})
is Devaney chaotic;

(v)

(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{f})
is Devaney chaotic.

Moreover, if
f:X\longrightarrow X
is a continuous linear operator and
X
is a Fréchet space, then the previous statements are equivalent to:

(vi)

(X,f)
is Devaney chaotic.

As mentioned in
[
41
, Section 2]
we must recall that statement (vi) of Theorem
4.2
is not equivalent to, nor weaker or stronger than, the other statements of Theorem
4.2
outside the linear setting. The reader is also referred to
[
21
, Remark 13 and Theorem 14]
for further details.

4.2
The specification property

The specification property is a strong dynamical behaviour consisting in the possibility of approximate arbitrary pieces of orbits by segments of a single (usually periodic) orbit. Although there are several versions of the specification property, following
[
4
]
we will only be interested in the strongest, namely, the periodic one (see
[
4
,
21
]
and the references cited there for further details):

–

we say that a dynamical system
(X,f)
on a metric space
(X,d)

has the specification property
if: for any
\varepsilon>0
we can find a positive integer
N_{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{N}
such that, for any integer
s\geq 2
, any finite sequence of points
\{y_{1},...,y_{s}\}\subset X
, and any sequence of integers
0=i_{1}\leq j_{1}<i_{2}\leq j_{2}<\cdots<i_{s}\leq j_{s}
fulfilling that
i_{r+1}-j_{r}\geq N_{\varepsilon}
for all
1\leq r<s
, there exists a point
x\in X
for which

d(f^{j}(x),f^{j}(y_{r}))<\varepsilon\text{ for every }1\leq r\leq s\text{ and }i_{r}\leq j\leq j_{r},\quad\text{ and }\quad f^{N_{\varepsilon}+j_{s}}(x)=x.

This property may seem quite technical but it is satisfied by many significant dynamical systems in the literature, with shift-like systems being a prominent example. Bauer and Sigmund proved that

–

[
5
, Proposition 4]
:
when
f:X\longrightarrow X
is a continuous map on a
compact
metric space
(X,d)
, if the dynamical system
(X,f)
has the specification property then so does extended system
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
;

while the converse is not true, since in
[
21
]
it was proved that

–

[
21
, Theorem 14]
:
there exists a dynamical system
(X,f)
on a
compact
metric space
(X,d)
such that
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
has the specification property while
(X,f)
has not the specification property.

We briefly discuss why
compactness
can be dropped from
[
5
, Proposition 4]
in Corollary
4.4
below, where following
[
4
]
we also give some sufficient conditions on the space
X
and the map
f
to get the specification property equivalence between the systems
(X,f)
and
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
. However, let us start by using our Lemma
2.4
to extend
[
4
, Theorem 2]
for the sendograph and endograph metrics:

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
f:X\longrightarrow X
be a continuous map acting on a metric space
(X,d)
. Then, the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
has the specification property;

(ii)

(\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(X),\hat{f})
has the specification property;

(iii)

(\mathcal{F}_{0}(X),\hat{f})
has the specification property;

(iv)

(\mathcal{F}_{S}(X),\hat{f})
has the specification property;

(v)

(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{f})
has the specification property.

Proof.

(i)
\Leftrightarrow
(ii)
\Leftrightarrow
(iii): This was proved in
[
41
, Theorem 2]
.

(iii)
\Rightarrow
(iv)
\Rightarrow
(v): This trivially follows from the inclusions
\tau_{E}\subset\tau_{S}\subset\tau_{0}
or, more precisely, from the well-known inequalities
d_{E}(u,v)\leq d_{S}(u,v)\leq d_{0}(u,v)\leq d_{\infty}(u,v)
, which hold for all
u,v\in\mathcal{F}(X)
.

(v)
\Rightarrow
(i): By hypothesis
(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{f})
has the specification property. Then, for any
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\hat{N}_{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for any integer
s\geq 2
, any finite family of fuzzy sets
\{u^{1},...,u^{s}\}\subset\mathcal{F}(X)
, and any sequence of integers
0=i_{1}\leq j_{1}<i_{2}\leq j_{2}<\cdots<i_{s}\leq j_{s}
with
i_{r+1}-j_{r}\geq\hat{N}_{\varepsilon}
for all
1\leq r<s
, there exists a point
v\in\mathcal{F}(X)
for which

d_{E}(\hat{f}^{j}(v),\hat{f}^{j}(u^{r}))<\varepsilon\text{ for every }1\leq r\leq s\text{ and }i_{r}\leq j\leq j_{r},\quad\text{ and }\quad\hat{f}^{\hat{N}_{\varepsilon}+j_{s}}(v)=v.

(4.1)

Let us check that this implies the specification property for
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
. In fact, given
\varepsilon>0
, which can be assumed to fulfill that
0<\varepsilon\leq\tfrac{1}{2}
without loss of generality, set
\overline{N}_{\varepsilon}=\hat{N}_{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{N}
. Now, consider an integer
s\geq 2
, a finite family of non-empty compact sets
\{K_{1},...,K_{s}\}\subset\mathcal{K}(X)
and an arbitrary but fixed sequence of integers
0=i_{1}\leq j_{1}<i_{2}\leq j_{2}<\cdots<i_{s}\leq j_{s}
with
i_{r+1}-j_{r}\geq\overline{N}_{\varepsilon}=\hat{N}_{\varepsilon}
for all
1\leq r<s
. Considering now the characteristic function
u^{r}:=\chi_{K_{r}}
for each
1\leq r\leq s
, and applying the specification property to the set
\{u^{1},...,u^{s}\}\subset\mathcal{F}(X)
for the sequence of integers fixed above, we know that there exists
v\in\mathcal{F}(X)
satisfying (
4.1
). Let

\delta:=\max\{d_{E}(\hat{f}^{j}(v),\hat{f}^{j}(u^{r}))\ ;\ 1\leq r\leq s\text{ and }i_{r}\leq j\leq j_{r}\}<\varepsilon\leq\tfrac{1}{2}

and fix any
\alpha\in\ ]\delta,1-\delta]
. Since
\hat{f}^{j}(u^{r})=\hat{f}^{j}(\chi_{K_{r}})=\chi_{f^{j}(K_{r})}
for every
1\leq r\leq s
and
i_{r}\leq j\leq j_{r}
, an application of Lemma
2.4
shows that

d_{H}(\overline{f}^{j}(v_{\alpha}),\overline{f}^{j}(K_{r}))=d_{H}([\hat{f}^{j}(v)]_{\alpha},f^{j}(K_{r}))<\varepsilon\quad\text{ for every }1\leq r\leq s\text{ and }i_{r}\leq j\leq j_{r}.

Moreover, since
\hat{f}^{\overline{N}_{\varepsilon}+j_{s}}(v)=\hat{f}^{\hat{N}_{\varepsilon}+j_{s}}(v)=v
we also have that

\overline{f}^{\overline{N}_{\varepsilon}+j_{s}}(v_{\alpha})=f^{\overline{N}_{\varepsilon}+j_{s}}(v_{\alpha})=[\hat{f}^{\overline{N}_{\varepsilon}+j_{s}}(v)]_{\alpha}=v_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{K}(X).

We conclude that
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
has the specification property.
∎

From Theorem
4.3
we obtain the following result improving
[
4
, Corollary 1 and Theorem 3]
:

Corollary 4.4
.

Let
f:X\longrightarrow X
be a continuous map acting on a metric space
(X,d)
. Hence:

(a)

If the dynamical system
(X,f)
has the specification property, then so do all the extended dynamical systems
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
,
(\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(X),\hat{f})
,
(\mathcal{F}_{0}(X),\hat{f})
,
(\mathcal{F}_{S}(X),\hat{f})
, and
(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{f})
.

(b)

If
f:=T\arrowvert_{X}
, where
T:E\longrightarrow E
is a continuous linear operator acting on a Fréchet space
E
and
X\subset E
is a convex
T
-invariant compact subset, then the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

(X,f)
has the specification property;

(ii)

(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
has the specification property;

(iii)

(\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(X),\hat{f})
has the specification property;

(iv)

(\mathcal{F}_{0}(X),\hat{f})
has the specification property;

(v)

(\mathcal{F}_{S}(X),\hat{f})
has the specification property;

(vi)

(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{f})
has the specification property.

Proof.

(a): The fact that the dynamical system
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
has the specification property was proved in
[
5
, Theorem 2 and Proposition 4]
for the particular case in which
(X,d)
is a compact metric space. However, their proof can be easily adapted to general metric spaces by properly using the continuity of the map
\overline{f}
rather than relying on its uniform continuity, which is only available in the compact case. The cases of
(\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(X),\hat{f})
,
(\mathcal{F}_{0}(X),\hat{f})
,
(\mathcal{F}_{S}(X),\hat{f})
and
(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{f})
follow from Theorem
4.3
.

(b): This follows from Theorem
4.3
together with
[
4
, Theorem 3]
. See also
[
7
]
.
∎

5
Conclusions

In this paper, we have examined the interaction between several properties of a discrete dynamical system
(X,f)
and its extension
(\mathcal{F}(X),\hat{f})
on the space of normal fuzzy sets
\mathcal{F}(X)
and with respect to the supremum, Skorokhod, sendograph and endograph metrics. In particular, using Lemma
2.4
we have extended the main results from
[
2
,
4
,
29
,
41
]
. Actually, we have improved the already existing equivalences for the notions of
transitivity
,
recurrence
,
Devaney chaos
and the
specification property
, by adding the systems
(\mathcal{F}_{S}(X),\hat{f})
and
(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{f})
, and for separable complete metric spaces we have also obtained some completely new results in terms of
point-
\mathcal{A}
-transitivity
. Several questions and comments arise naturally from the theory developed in this paper as we comment in the next lines.

A first natural question is why we consider the space
\mathcal{F}(X)
of normal fuzzy sets. This question is relevant because many papers have studied the dynamics of the fuzzification of a system
(X,f)
, but taking alternative spaces of fuzzy sets. For instance, if for a metric space
(X,d)
we set

\mathbb{F}(X):=\left\{u:X\longrightarrow[0,1]\ ;\ u\text{ is upper-semicontinuous and }u_{0}\text{ is compact}\right\},

then the spaces of fuzzy sets

\mathcal{F}^{=\lambda}(X):=\left\{u\in\mathbb{F}(X)\ ;\ \max\{u(x)\ ;\ x\in X\}=\lambda\right\}

\mathcal{F}^{\geq\lambda}(X):=\left\{u\in\mathbb{F}(X)\ ;\ u(x)\geq\lambda\text{ for some }x\in X\right\}

were considered in
[
33
,
45
]
for different values
\lambda\in\ ]0,1]
. However, it is not hard to check that the space
\mathcal{F}^{=\lambda}(X)
is homeomorphic to the space of normal fuzzy sets
\mathcal{F}(X)
considered in this paper for every
\lambda\in\ ]0,1]
, and one of the main results in the theory is that the system
(\mathcal{F}^{\geq\lambda}(X),\hat{f})
can not be transitive for
\lambda<1
. These considerations led us to adopt the framework used in
[
2
,
4
,
26
,
27
,
28
,
29
,
41
]
.

A second natural question concerns the dynamical properties studied in
[
4
,
26
,
27
,
28
,
41
]
, mentioned at the Introduction, but not addressed in this paper. Actually, from the list of dynamical properties mentioned at the beginning of this paper, we have only focused on those properties for which the endograph metric
d_{E}
behaves exactly equal to the supremum, Skorokhod and sendograph metrics: we have already obtained some results for the
expansive
,
sensitive
and
contractive
properties, for
Li-Yorke
and
distributional chaos
, and for the
shadowing property
(among other dynamical notions), but the endograph metric
d_{E}
behaves in a extremely radical way for these notions. Our results concerning these dynamical properties will appear in the forthcoming work
[
39
]
.

Finally, regarding possible future research directions, one may study the dynamical properties of the system
(\mathcal{F}(X),\hat{f})
when
\mathcal{F}(X)
is endowed with other metrics (see
[
22
]
