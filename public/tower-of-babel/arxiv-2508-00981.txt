Cosmological Zoom-In Simulation of Odd Radio Circles as Merger-Driven Shocks in Galaxy Groups
Anna Ivleva, Ludwig M. Böss, Klaus Dolag, Bärbel S. Koribalski, Ildar Khabibullin

Abstract
Context. A new class of distinct radio objects has been recently discovered, which are now commonly referred to as Odd Radio Circles (ORCs). The origin of these features is yet unclear because their peculiar properties are a challenge for our current understanding of astrophysical sources for diffuse radio emission. Aims. We test the feasibility and limits of major mergers in galaxy groups as a possible formation channel for ORCs. Methods. By modeling the assembly of a massive galaxy group with a final virial mass of M 200 ∼ 10 13 ​ M ⊙ M_{200}\sim 10^{13}\,\mathrm{M}_{\odot} in a magnetohydrodynamic zoom-in simulation with on-the-fly cosmic ray treatment, we are able to derive the X-ray and radio properties of the system self-consistently and compare them to observations. Results. We show that the X-ray properties for the simulated system are agreeing with characteristics of observed galaxy groups in the regarded mass range, legitimating the comparison between the radio properties of the simulated halo and those of observed ORCs. A major merger between two galaxies in the simulation is triggering a series of strong shocks in the circumgalactic medium, which in unison are forming a ring if the line of sight is perpendicular to the merger axis. The shock is rapidly expanding in radial direction and quickly reaches the virial radius of the halo. This formation channel can hence readily explain the morphology and large extent of ORCs. However, the inferred radio luminosity of these features is lower than for observed counterparts, while the degree of polarization seems to be systematically overpredicted by the simulation. Conclusions. Fossil cosmic ray populations from AGN and stellar feedback might be necessary to explain the full extent of the radio properties of ORCs, since diffusive shock acceleration was the only source term for non-thermal electrons considered in this work.

Introduction

The current advent of next-generation radio telescopes is delivering a number of interesting surprises to the astrophysics community. Their unprecedented depth and frequency coverage continues to reveal stunning details in various extraterrestrial settings – from galactic sources such as supernova remnants
(
Weiler et al. 1986
;
Dokara et al. 2021
;
Ball et al. 2023
;
Do et al. 2024
, e.g.)
over to the plentiful extragalactic zoo of objects like star forming disc galaxies
(
Martinsson et al. 2016
;
Krause et al. 2018
;
Stein et al. 2023
;
Heesen et al. 2024
, e.g.)
, AGN-hosting galaxies
(
McKean et al. 2021
;
Venturi et al. 2022
;
Koribalski et al. 2024a
, e.g.)
, as well as halos and radio relics of galaxy clusters
(
van Weeren et al. 2010
;
Rajpurohit et al. 2020
;
Brüggen et al. 2021
;
Venturi et al. 2022
;
Cassano et al. 2023
;
Koribalski et al. 2024c
, e.g.)
. However, it seems that yet another kind of distinct source needs to be added to the list of possible radio objects. As their name suggests, Odd Radio Circles (ORCs) are peculiar radio features with edge-brightened ring- or shell-like morphology
(
Norris et al. 2021
;
Koribalski et al. 2021
;
Koribalski et al. 2024b
)
. The spectral indices measured in these features, that can lie between
\alpha\approx 2
and
0.6
(assuming that the emission spectrum follows
I_{\nu}=\nu^{-\alpha}
), suggest that these regions are filled with shock-accelerated cosmic rays (CRs), emitting via synchrotron in radio. When found at low galactic latitudes
(
Koribalski et al. 2024c
, e.g.,)
it can be difficult to identify a host galaxy, leaving the possibility that these radio rings are supernova remnants in or near our Galaxy
(
Filipović et al. 2022
;
Omar 2022a
;
Sarbadhicary et al. 2023
)
. Bonafide ORCs, however, have a central early-type galaxy, strongly suggesting they are of extragalactic origin
(
Koribalski et al. 2021
;
Norris et al. 2022
;
Norris et al. 2025
)
. Since ORCs lack a counterpart in any other wavelength regime, distance and size measurements of ORCs rely on the redshifts of their putative host galaxies. Judging by the elliptical galaxies visible in optical that are situated near the geometrical centers of ORCs, these objects lie at distances of
z=0.05-0.6
and often have diameters of several hundred kpc, while the stellar masses of the central galaxies lie around
M_{\ast}=10^{11}\,\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
. This means that ORCs have enormous sizes compared to their central host, as their extent can even exceed the virial radius of the host system.

Since the nature of ORCs is still under debate, one needs to be careful in controlling the sample of considered objects, in order to ensure an appropriate comparison. The following set of conditions seems to works best as a common denominator: the ORC has to a) display a ring or shell-like morphology with b) at least one elliptical galaxy in its center, where c) the size of the radio ring is large, i.e. comparable to the virial radius of the central galaxy (group). While the neighbouring ORCs 2 & 3 from the original discovery paper by
Norris et al. 2021
– who first reported such radio morphologies and have introduced the term ORC – are likely the lobes of a re-started radio galaxy where one lobe has ring morphology, there is a growing list of different ORCs whose origin is yet unclear. It is important to note, that the observed morphologies can depend on the resolution and frequency of the observation facility. While an object may look diffuse in one image, the characteristic ring emission for ORCs can reveal itself in surveys with higher resolution and sensitivity. The following observed objects are falling under the criteria stated above: ORCs 1 & 4
(
Norris et al. 2021
)
, ORC 5
(
Koribalski et al. 2021
)
, ORC 6
(
Dolag et al. 2023
;
Koribalski et al. 2025
)
,
ORC J2223–4834

(
Gupta et al. 2022
)
, SAURON
(
Lochner et al. 2023
)
, Physalis
(
Koribalski et al. 2024b
)
, Cloverleaf
(
Bulbul et al. 2024
)
,
ORC J1027–4422

(
Koribalski et al. 2024c
)
and
ORC J0219–0505

(
Norris et al. 2025
)
. There are several ongoing searches for ORCs and similar systems as for example the study by
Gupta et al. 2025
.

A variety of extragalactic formation scenarios for ORCs have been proposed. Several works have addressed the scenario of a termination shock due to galactic outflows during starburst events
(
Norris et al. 2021
;
Norris et al. 2022
;
Coil et al. 2024
)
. Then there is the possibility that a black hole is responsible for these features, either via binary mergers of supermassive black holes
(
Koribalski et al. 2021
;
Norris et al. 2022
)
, tidal disruption of stars by black holes
(
Omar 2022b
)
, as well as AGN jets, where the ejected radio lobes can adopt a circular appearance under certain projections and physical conditions
(
Nolting et al. 2023
;
Fujita et al. 2024
;
Lin & Yang 2024
)
. In this regard,
Shabala et al. 2024
have pointed out that the radio power of a mere AGN jet would decline too quickly to explain the properties of ORCs, and hence concluded that an additional shock running through preexisting AGN bubbles would be necessary in this scenario. Finally, there is the shock-driven formation channel that was first proposed by
Dolag et al. 2023
and
Koribalski et al. 2024b
. Based on cosmological simulations, the suggested concept was that merger-accelerated shocks – i.e. merger shocks of coalescence events in massive galaxies and groups that run into outer accretion shocks – can produce features that very closely resemble ORCs. Such processes have been studied for a while now in relation to galaxy clusters, where merger shocks are most likely source of for radio relics
(
Vazza et al. 2009
;
Ha et al. 2018
;
Zhang et al. 2019
, e.g.)
. But in the case of clusters, these features always lie well within the boundaries of the respective halo. Hence, ORCs could be unprecedented evidence for merger shocks in galaxy and group halos, where their extent is pushing beyond the boundaries of the virial radius. However, the simulations by
Dolag et al. 2023
predicted radio luminosities that were too low compared to observations, concluding that the relatively low simulated halo mass of
10^{12}\,\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
could be a major issue, since the total energy budget is underestimated. This problem was also pointed out by
Yamasaki et al. 2024
, who explored the feasibility of pure accretion shocks as the source for ORCs analytically, concluding that more complex simulations with a larger halo mass of at least
10^{13}\,\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
are required, since these are the estimated halo masses of the ORC host galaxies.

In the present work, we are addressing the necessity of such a study. We present a first of its kind zoom-in simulation of a galaxy merger in high resolution, where the final group mass is corresponding to observed halos in ORCs. We performed a non-radiative magneto-hydrodynamic simulation with an on-the-fly treatment of spectral CR electrons. First, we summarize the numerical details of the simulation by stating the important features of the code and initial condition in
Section

2
. In
Section

3
, we give a detailed presentation of the simulation results. We outline the time evolution of the merger event and then describe the halo’s X-ray and radio properties, providing inferred mock observation images. Hereby, a particular focus lies on the polarization of the simulated ORC. Similarities and differences with observations are discussed in
Section

4
, before we summarize our results in
Section

5
.

The simulation was carried out with the cosmological code
OpenGadget3
, which is utilizing a smoothed particle magnetohydrodynamics (SPMHD) scheme to model gas dynamics and a Tree-PM solver for the gravity (release paper in prep.). Its infrastructure is based on its predecessor Gadget2
(
Springel 2005
)
but employs major improvements in the gravity solver
(
Ragagnin et al. 2016
)
and SPH implementation
(
Beck et al. 2016b
)
, where also a revised time step criterion is ensuring the stability of the code around strong shocks in a simulation
(
Pakmor et al. 2012
)
. We use the Wendland C4 function as the kernel to reconstruct the local hydrodynamic properties of the simulated fluid based on the position and smoothing length of the simulated particles
(
Dehnen & Aly 2012
;
Donnert et al. 2013
)
. The code includes both a physically motivated conductivity
(
Jubelgas et al. 2004
;
Dolag et al. 2004
;
Petkova & Springel 2009
)
, as well as an artificial conduction and time-dependent viscosity scheme
(
Price 2012
;
Beck et al. 2016b
)
, which provides numerical convergence when compared to other hydrodynamic discretization methods.

The code resolves the magnetohydrodynamic equations in Lagrangian form
(
Dolag et al. 1999
;
Dolag & Stasyszyn 2009
)
in the non-ideal regime, i.e. with non-vanishing resistivity
(
Bonafede et al. 2011
)
. Non-zero
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B}
contributions of the magnetic field
\mathbf{B}
, that can emerge due to numerical truncation errors, are suppressed by the hyperbolic divergence cleaning method described by
Tricco et al. 2016
, where the implementation into
OpenGadget3
will be described by Steinwandel & Price (in prep.). We run the code with an on-the-fly shock finder
(
Beck et al. 2016a
)
, that is returning the shock’s hydrodynamic quantities, as well as as the Alfvénic Mach number and shock obliquity
(
Böss et al. 2023b
)
. Finally, we include cosmic ray electrons with the module CRESCENDO, which is a Fokker-Planck solver for spectral cosmic rays
(
Böss et al. 2023b
, CRs, )
. The subgrid model computes and evolves the distribution function of CRs for each resolution element (i.e. SPH particle) at each time step in the simulation. The source for CRs is diffusive shock acceleration (DSA) – which therefore heavily relies on the output of the built-in shock finder – and applies shock obliquity dependent acceleration efficiencies
(
Pais et al. 2018
;
Kang 2024
)
. These injected populations can then further evolve in time via adiabatic losses as well as radiative losses, which are inverse Compton scattering and synchrotron emission for CR electrons
(
Böss et al. 2023a
, for the computation of the synchrotron emissivity, c.f. Appendix 2 by)
. The present simulation was performed in a non-radiative regime, which does not include star formation, but only resolves the dark matter and gas evolution.

3.4
Emission spectrum and total radio power

According to the formation model we are analyzing in this paper, the radio feature that we identify as an ORC is a merger shock around a massive galaxy or galaxy group, that is triggered along the axis of a major merger. In this scenario, the total radio luminosity would be the highest shortly after the merger event, since that would be the time when the shock can inject many CR electron populations efficiently into the relatively dense medium at small radii. If we assume that CRs get accelerated from the thermal pool according to DSA, the powerlaw index
\gamma
of the injected CR’s energy spectrum will depend only on the Mach number
\mathcal{M}
. Rewriting
\gamma
in terms of the spectral index
\alpha
of the emission spectrum yields:

\alpha=\frac{\mathcal{M}^{2}+3}{2\mathcal{M}^{2}-2}.

(1)

In the limit of a strong shock (
\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\infty
), the spectral index will be
\alpha\rightarrow 1/2
, while weaker shocks will generate steeper spectra (
\alpha>1/2
). After the initial central merger, the shock will travel radially outwards with decreasing effective Mach numbers, producing increasingly steep spectra at the front.

This leads to a more rapid energy loss of CR electrons at higher energy bins, which is reflected in the steeper emission spectrum at higher frequencies, since the contribution to the spectral emission of a charged particle in a magnetic field is closely peaked around an energy dependent maximum

3.5
Polarization

Synchrotron radiation of electrons will be polarized with a polarization angle
\chi
, i.e. the angle between the electric field vector of the associated radiation, and a chosen unit vector in the sky plane, which is defined to be orthogonal to the line of sight. Since the polarization angle is depending only on local properties, the so called
polarization fraction

\pi_{\nu}
will be relatively high in case of a singular, relatively small emitting cosmic ray cloud where the local magnetic field is approximately uniform. But as the number of contributing electrons along the line of sight is increasing, the fractional polarization of the observed radiation will consequently decrease because the direction of the magnetic field is not homogeneous. Therefore, a polarization analysis of radio observations can be a powerful tool in order to dissect the physical processes relevant in ORCs. Since our simulation is self-consistently evolving cosmic ray electrons, we can provide polarization information of the simulated ORC and compare it with observed counterparts for the first time.

The polarization state of electromagnetic radiation at a given frequency
\nu
is commonly described by the four Stokes parameters
I_{\nu},Q_{\nu},U_{\nu}
and
V_{\nu}
, because they can be inferred directly from observed quantities. Generally, synchrotron radiation is elliptically polarized. But in the limit of ultra-relativistic electrons, the light cone of radiation – which is defined as the solid angle around the velocity vector in which an observer is receiving a significant signal – becomes sufficiently narrow. In this case, the observer will receive only
?
flashes
?
of synchrotron radiation when the electron is moving almost exactly towards the observer. The associated electric field vector is hence only drawing a line in the sky plane (instead of an ellipse), which is equivalent to linear polarization
(
Ginzburg 1979
;
Longair 2011
)
. In this limit,
V_{\nu}
is vanishing and only three Stokes parameters remain. Assuming the line of sight is along the
z
-direction,
I_{\nu}
is defined as the total intensity received by the observer integrated along the line of sight:

I_{\nu}=\int j_{\nu}(\mathbf{x})\,dz,

(2)

where
j_{\nu}(\mathbf{x})
is the synchrotron emissivity at a given frequency
\nu
and position
\mathbf{x}=(x,y,z)
. Meanwhile
Q_{\nu}
and
U_{\nu}
, which are actually carrying the polarization information, are defined by

\displaystyle Q_{\nu}

\displaystyle=\int j_{\nu,\rm pol}(\mathbf{x})\cos 2\chi\,dz

(3)

\displaystyle U_{\nu}

\displaystyle=\int j_{\nu,\rm pol}(\mathbf{x})\sin 2\chi\,dz,

(4)

where
j_{\nu,\rm pol}
is the polarized synchrotron emissivity. Note that the polarization angle
\chi
will be subject to Faraday rotation along the line of sight. The polarization angle therefore needs to be adjusted at each contributing particle along the line of sight according to the traveled distance
dl
by

\chi\longrightarrow\chi+\lambda^{2}\text{RM}\quad\text{with}\quad\text{RM}\propto\int n_{e}B_{z}\,dl,

(5)

where
\lambda
is the observed wavelength, RM the rotation measure,
n_{e}
the local electron number density and
B_{z}
the component of the magnetic field vector
\mathbf{B}=(B_{x},B_{y},B_{z})
along the line of sight. The polarization angle is depending only on the magnetic field components in the sky plane and is given by

\cos 2\chi=\frac{B_{x}^{2}-B_{y}^{2}}{B_{x}^{2}+B_{y}^{2}}.

(6)

Finally, the polarization fraction
\pi_{\nu}
can be obtained via

(7)

The subscript 0 is indicating here that the Stokes parameters from

were integrated over the whole energy range in this case. Shock indicators

The built-in shock finder in the code
OpenGadget3
is based on a geometrical on-the-fly scheme, that utilizes pressure gradients to construct the shock normal, along which then the Rankine-Hugoniot jump conditions are evaluated. After masking cold flows and shear flows based on threshold pressure and velocity jumps, the algorithm is finally inferring shock quantities such as the compression ratio and Mach number from approximated hydrodynamic quantities up- and downstream of the shock
(
Beck et al. 2016a
)
. As mentioned in
Section

3.5
, the accuracy of the shock finder is strongly dependent on the resolution of the simulation. Specifically, the size of the local smoothing kernel of the respective SPH particle is defining the scale on which shocks are resolved. In our case, the mean smoothing scale of the simulated ORC is on the order of
10\text{\,}\mathrm{kpc}
at
t=$73\text{\,}\mathrm{Myr}$
, i.e. when the size and morphology matches best to observed counterparts. For reference, this is twice as big as the smoothing kernel imitating the resolution limit of ASKAP at
\nu=$944\text{\,}\mathrm{MHz}$
, indicated by the small circle in the lower left corner of the polarization map in
Fig.

5
. This is the reason for the appearance of the spot-like features in the inferred mock radio images – in particular in the polarization features – which are encompassing the extent of the SPH particles in these regions.

In order to illustrate the actual extent and width of the shock region, it is instructive to compare the results with an alternative way to determine the shock position. In particular, gaussian filtering of pressure maps has proven to be quite efficient in revealing pressure jumps that indicate a shock in the medium
(
Dolag et al. 2005b
;
Ragagnin et al. 2017
;
Sommer et al. 2024
, see also)
. Naturally, this approach is only feasible during post processing of the simulation, since it requires a 2D projection in the correct plane and visual inspection. Nevertheless it is a convenient tool to double check the conclusion of the shock finder. The process is the following: starting from a pressure map
\mathcal{X}(x,y)
, we smooth the image by applying a 2D gaussian kernel function
G(x,y)\propto\exp[-(x^{2}+y^{2})/(2\sigma^{2})]
on each pixel of the original image and obtain a blurred variant
\tilde{\mathcal{X}}(x,y)
. Sharp edges in the original map will still be present, but are naturally attenuated in the new image. If we now subtract the two images from each other, features spanning over a larger scale than the smoothing kernel
\sigma
will vanish and only sharp features remain. Lastly, the contribution of each pixel is normalized by the local value of the blurred image, simply to see each edge feature in the resulting map equally strong, no matter the pressure contrast. The final shock map will be therefore given by

\mathcal{X}_{\rm fin}(x,y)=\frac{\mathcal{X}(x,y)-\tilde{\mathcal{X}}(x,y)}{\tilde{\mathcal{X}}(x,y)}.

(9)

Fig.

6
we present such a shock map of the system which was generated by the method explained above. The time and scale of the simulated ORC is again the same as in the polarization map in
Fig.

5
. The upper panel is showing the integrated surface density of the gas, while the bottom panel is displaying a shock map in the same scale. The pressure at each pixel was determined via the Compton-
y
parameter, which can be used as a proxy for the integrated electron pressure along the line of sight. Finally, we used a gaussian smoothing kernel length of
\sigma=5
pixels, which is corresponding to approximately
6\text{\,}\mathrm{kpc}
in this case. The shock map in
Fig.

6
is revealing the clear edges in pressure, displaying its strikingly circular geometry which was not as clear in the output of the shock finder.

4.1
Comparison to observations and Dolag et al. (2023)

The effectiveness of galaxy mergers in producing circular radio features matching ORCs was first shown in a pilot study by
Dolag et al. 2023
, where the authors have conducted cosmological zoom-in simulations of a Milky-Way-like halo with a mass of approximately
10^{12}\,\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
. Since the simulations were performed in a pure hydrodynamics fashion without magnetic fields, they needed to estimate the radio brightness during postprocessing. That was realized by adding CRs to shock regions, where their amount was chosen such that the integrated CR energy was a fixed fraction of the dissipated shock energy, based on the shock finder output at a single timestep. Finally by assuming a constant and homogeneous magnetic field, they could infer the spectral radio flux based on synchrotron emission. Although the morphology and size of ORCs could be reproduced, these simulations predicted radio powers that were far too low compared to what is measured in ORCs. However it was unclear which assumptions specifically have led to this deficiency. In this work, we are closing two main shortcomings of the study, namely by simulating i) a massive enough galaxy group (
M\sim 10^{13}\,\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
), comparable to what is found in observed counterparts and ii) using a magneto-hydrodynamic scheme with self-consistent, on-the-fly treatment of spectral CRs.

As discussed in
Section

3.3
, it is necessary to proceed with caution when evaluating the absolute value of the magnetic field in a cosmological simulation. It tends to be underestimated, because technically one needs extremely high resolution in order to resolve the turbulent dynamo process in the intergalactic medium, which is the main driver in amplifying the magnetic field
(
Vazza et al. 2014
;
Vazza et al. 2018
;
Böss et al. 2024
;
Steinwandel et al. 2024
, e.g.)
. Since the radio properties of the system are based on synchrotron emission, this can significantly alter the final result. To obtain more realistic predictions, it is therefore appropriate to compare the simulated magnetic field strength with analytic predictions for the system. In
Fig.

7
, we show the time-dependent radio luminosity due to synchrotron emission at
150\text{\,}\mathrm{MHz}
for the simulated ORC. The three vertical lines represent the three times from the visualization in
Fig.

1
, which are each about
70\text{\,}\mathrm{Myr}
apart, starting from shortly before the merger. The colored regions represent three different models for the magnetic field strength, where the lower end is the prediction by the respective model and the upper end is representing a boost by a factor of 10. The orange area is the luminosity profile determined by the simulated magnetic field strength
B_{\rm sim}
, while the other two were generated by assuming a balance between i) magnetic field pressure and thermal pressure (
B_{\beta}
, blue) and ii) magnetic field pressure and turbulent pressure (

\displaystyle B_{\beta}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{8\pi P_{\rm th}}{\beta}}

(10)

In order to keep the models overall realistic, the value of the plasma-

But even taking the boost of stronger field models into account, we are still not reaching the luminosity of observed counterparts indicated by the various dashed-dotted lines in

4.2
Potential role of AGN and stellar feedback

Assuming that there is no other essential process producing significant radio emission other than synchrotron emission, this lack of total radio luminosity would then mean that either there are not enough relativistic CR electrons present, or/and the local magnetic field strength is too low. Since the maximum Mach number of the shock, when the simulated ORC is also the brightest, is already relatively large (
\mathcal{M}\approx 5
, c.f.
Section

3.4
), a stronger shock would not remedy the CR deficiency, because their acceleration efficiency via DSA would not increase substantially. A stronger magnetic field would in principle also give rise to stronger synchrotron emission. The problem is, that several processes are currently underrepresented by our magnetic field models, which are presumed to contribute to the state of the magnetic field in the medium, e.g. Bell amplification in shocks or magnetization of the halo by highly magnetized or laminar outflows. To test the effect of stronger magnetic fields in our simulation, we would need to increase the field strength across the whole system in order to stay self consistent. But this would push the field strength in the central regions of the galaxies beyond actually observed values, and is therefore not an option. Additionally, we need to consider the relative importance of fossil CR populations in the halo, which would be present in case of prior injection events of CRs into the CGM of the merging galaxies. Typically, enough time would have passed such that these old populations would have cooled down to mildly relativistic energies – meaning that they would not be radio-bright anymore – but still not enough time for those populations to join the thermal pool of electrons. While a strong shock is necessary to accelerate charged particles from the Maxwellian distribution, a relatively weak shock would already suffice to push such fossil CRs back to relativistic energies again, effectively boosting the total radio brightness of the whole system
(
Caprioli et al. 2018
;
Kang 2018
, e.g.)
.

Apart from merger or accretion shocks, stellar outflows and AGN feedback are the other main sources for these fossil populations and it can be assumed that they should play a role in the two galaxies in our simulation. But since it was performed in a non-radiative fashion, meaning that it did not include star formation, it is not possible to make definite statements about the actual contribution of stars and black holes at this point. A reasonable assumption would be that they would raise the amount of relativistic electrons by a factor of 10, which is reflected by the upper end of the inferred luminosity profiles of the simulation in
Fig.

7
. But evidently, that would not suffice to reach the observed values. Nevertheless, the fractional polarization is actually also pointing towards the importance of fossil CRs in this system. The simulation produces values mainly between
20-40\%
(c.f.
Section

3.5
), while measurements of ORC 1 report lower values mostly between
10-20\%
in the interior and reaching
\sim
30% at most in the ring
(
Norris et al. 2022
)
. If there are significant amounts of old CR populations present, one would certainly increase the number of emission centers along the line of sight compared to the case where electrons were accelerated from the thermal pool only. But since also the direction of the magnetic field vector is varying along the line of sight – and the polarization angle is depending on that direction – this would naturally diversify the distribution of polarization angles present in the total emission. Therefore, the total polarization fraction along a given line of sight would decrease in the case of additional fossil CRs populations, which is necessary to get into agreement with observations. Thus, in order to give a concluding answer to the question whether fossil CR populations are actually the missing key to explain all radio features of ORCs, one would need to perform the simulation again with star formation and proper feedback implementation including self-consistent CR treatment, which is a challenge to current state-of-the-art cosmological codes and we will refer to in future work.

5
Summary and conclusion

In this article, we have presented a first of its kind zoom-in simulation of a galaxy group performed in a magnetohydronamic setup with spectral cosmic ray treatment on-the-fly. The scientific aim was to test the limits of a strong contender for a formation channel for Odd Radio Circles (ORCs) – a new class of radio objects whose origin is still a puzzle. The idea is that during a major merger event between two galaxies, the immense energy dissipation can release several strong bow shocks (i.e. merger shocks). If the projection is close to the perpendicular direction to the merger axis, these shocks can form a giant ring, which closely resembles the edge-brightened radio features that are now identified as ORCs in observations.

This simulation incorporates two major improvements compared to the pilot study by
Dolag et al. 2023
, who introduced this formation path: it is i) resolving a 10 times more massive halo with a final virial mass of about
10^{13}\,\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
, which is now in agreement with the group mass measured in the center of ORCs and ii) determining CR population and local magnetic field direction self-consistently, rather than adding them in postprocessing. We showed that the simulation reproduces the X-ray surface brightness of a galaxy group in that mass range and demonstrated that the peculiar offset between the X-ray and radio-bright centers observed in ORC
Physalis

(
Koribalski et al. 2024b
)
can be explained by the difference in gas density at the centers of the merging galaxies (
Section

3.2
). By providing mock observations of the radio surface brightness and spectral index based on working frequencies of LOFAR, GMRT and ASKAP, we demonstrated that the morphology and size agrees well with observations (
Section

3.3
). The emission spectrum of the radio shells is indicating a sonic Mach number of
\mathcal{M}\approx 4.8
, which is comparable to strong shock events in massive galaxy clusters (
Section

3.4
). The simulation produces a fractional polarization degree of
20-40\%
across the radio ring (
Section

3.5
). The pressure excess map provided in
Section

3.6
reveals the sharp shock boundaries responsible for the radio features.

Compared to observations, the simulation is underpredicting the total radio luminosity and overestimating its polarization degree (
Section

4
). Both issues would be alleviated by factoring in additional sources for relativistic cosmic rays apart from DSA, which is the only acceleration mechanism in the present simulation. Such mechanisms would be the injection of cosmic rays via stellar or AGN feedback, which could significantly contribute to the total energy budget and boost the radio brightness when reaccelerated by the merger shock. We will implement a description of these fossil cosmic rays in our future work and investigate whether they could be the missing ingredient for explaining the observed radio power of ORCs.
