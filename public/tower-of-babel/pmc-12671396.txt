Understanding pandemics through molecular gas dynamics
Authors: Yao-Yu Guan, Zhi-Hui Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhile the COVID-19 pandemic is over, the road ahead is still clouded by concern about new variants and other similar infectious diseases. Human society, as an inherently complex system, is inextricably linked to the dynamics of respiratory infectious diseases from the interplay of individual behaviors, social interactions, and public policies. However, comprehending and predicting large-scale pandemic evolution based on fundamental individual behavior models remains a big challenge. In this study, we analogize the spread of respiratory infectious diseases to the nonequilibrium chemical reaction in a molecular gas, another complex system. Concepts and methodologies from molecular gas dynamics are extended to elucidate the pandemic. Individuals at distinct infection stages are treated as moving molecules of different species that undergo collisions and reactions. The velocity and collision cross-section are set according to real-world scenarios. Additionally, the viral load in human body is analogized to molecular vibrational energy level which affects the chemical reaction rate. Consequently, we introduce a specific nonequilibrium compartmental model incorporating a time-varying transmission rate, drawing upon the nonequilibrium gas dynamics. By employing the Direct Simulation Monte Carlo method, we directly derive key epidemiological metrics, including the secondary infection number, generation interval, and reproduction number. Furthermore, an initial exploration of the interplay between infection and individual behavior displays how the disease spread mitigates when the mobility of patients is reduced. This novel analogy highlights the generalized similarity between distinct complex systems and opens a new avenue for applying advanced concepts and methods from molecular gas dynamics to the pandemic study.

The COVID-19 pandemic highlighted the urgent need to understand respiratory infectious disease dynamics in complex human societies, attracting attention from diverse fields, including fluid dynamics. We analogize pandemic spread to nonequilibrium chemical reactions in a molecular gas, successfully building a novel framework for infectious disease modeling through cross-disciplinary knowledge transfer and integration. Within this framework, we not only establish a conversion relationship between constant and time-varying transmission rates but also derive a series of key parameters for disease transmission. Furthermore, our findings indicate a limit to the effectiveness of patients’ mobility decrease. This analogy reveals universal similarities across different complex systems and provides novel insights for future public health policy formulation.

Introduction

The emergence of COVID-19 in December 2019 rapidly led to the infection of billions and claim millions of lives, underscoring the urgent need for effective global health measures (1). In response, researchers have pursued various models to forecast the spread of infections and guide public health policies. The most representative and popular models are the Susceptible–Infected–Removed (SIR) model (2) and its further developed variations, and in theses compartmental models, individuals are divide into different categories based on their roles in transmission (3, 4). Furthermore, the network models (5–7) that include social relations or migration patterns, have been developed to analyze intercity transmissions of COVID-19. Recently, data-driven approaches such as neural network (8) and machine learning methods (9) have demonstrated strong predictive capabilities by accounting for a multitude of contributing factors (10). There are also higher-fidelity agent-based models (ABMs) (11–14) which operate with literal inputs and minimal assumptions but can require massive computational resources such that the simulation throughput becomes a challenge (15). These models have achieved numerous results in the field of pandemic forecast, but still, there remains a degree of uncertainty and deviation due to the intrinsic complexity of infectious disease spread in human societies (16). Therefore, it could be meaningful and also interesting to draw on concepts and methods of other analogous complex systems from a similarity-based perspective. In this study, we compare the spread of infectious diseases with the nonequilibrium reaction process in a molecular gas, aiming to understand the fundamental nonequilibrium characteristics of pandemics and to develop a corresponding prediction model that incorporates generalized principles of molecular gas dynamics—one of special branch of fluid mechanics.

Early in the 1760s, Daniel Bernoulli, a pioneer in mathematics, physics, and fluid dynamics, proposed the first mathematical model in epidemiology (17), marking a transformative moment in the field (18). Nowadays, from the viral invasion of cells (19) to the impact of climate change on COVID transmission (20, 21), fluid dynamics underpins nearly every scale of epidemiological phenomena (22, 23). As the ancient aphorism by Heraclitus states, “everything flows”—and so too does disease. Pathogens flow in gas or water, while human beings and animals, as disease carriers, flow in various natural and artificial environments like roads and buildings. Figure 1 shows a comparison between the transmission of COVID and the reaction–diffusion of gaseous molecules. From a microscopic viewpoint, the spread of respiratory infectious diseases can be deconstructed as processes of infection and migration of individuals. When an infected individual comes into contact with a susceptible one, the virus spreads through breathing, coughing, and sneezing (24). This naturally gives rise to the idea of modeling individuals as particles and infection events as binary chemical reactions (25, 26) under the law of mass action (27). A landmark compartmental model based on mass-action mixing was developed by Kermack and McKendrick (28) in 1927 to describe the epidemic process, upon which numerous subsequent models have flourished.

Individuals, possessing specific mass and velocity, move in random mode throughout the city. The pedestrian speed distribution conforms to the Maxwell velocity distribution under low population density by Henderson’s surveys (29), and this model has been adopted by various studies (30–32) on pedestrian kinematics. When pedestrian density is low, individuals must maintain social distances much greater than their physical dimensions to avoid collisions. The critical factor distinguishing between diluted and dense crowds lies in whether people move actively or passively (33). It is thus reasonable to model urban populations as molecules in a dilute gas. For example, both the per capita areas in Beijing (34) and New York (15) are on the order of 100m2, compared to the average individual social area of 3m2. It is evident that even in such densely populated cities, the population remains dilute. More precisely, a dimensionless dilution criterion (Di) of a system can be defined as the ratio of the average spacing (δ) between “molecules” to the effective diameter (d) of “molecules.” Therefore, Di=O(10) for populations in typical cities, which is remarkably close to the counterpart in atmospheric air under standard sea-level condition where δ≈3nm, d≈0.4nm and Di=δ/d=O(10).

On the left panel of Fig. 1, mobility is depicted wherein individuals in a 2D area engage in collisions with each other based on specific collision cross-sections. Beyond collision kinetics, the discourse on reaction dynamics holds significance. The process in the central panel of Fig. 1 portrays the infection process, wherein a susceptible individual (beige disk) becomes infected after a “collision” with an infected individual (red disk). In practice, diseases transmission is contingent not solely upon the interaction between infected and susceptible individuals but also on the viral load within the patient. Analogous to chemical reactions at the molecular level, the successful transmission of infection parallels the concept that, unless the activation energy barrier is overcome, the chemical reaction will not occur (35). Molecules in higher energy states can excite internal degrees of freedom (rotation and vibration), and the internal energy, particularly the vibrational energy without loss of generality, could contribute to overcoming the activation energy barrier and affect the chemical reaction rate (36). The influence of vibrational energy on chemical reactions mirrors the impact of viral load on the infection process, underscoring an intuitionistic analogy between these two phenomena. Additionally, when the viral load is low, symptoms may not appear, and this is referred to as the incubation period. Similarly, in the high-temperature gas dynamics, the concept of the incubation period is also employed to elucidate the characteristic time of molecular dissociation (37), with the dissociation rate depending on both temperatures characterizing the translational and vibrational modes of molecules. We term the process of continuous variation in viral load within the patient’s body as a relaxation process. This relaxation of viral load introduces nonequilibrium aspects into the transmission dynamics of infectious diseases, as depicted on the right side of Fig. 1, where deepening color represents increasing viral load and, consequently, rising infectivity. By integrating kinetics and reaction dynamics, an analogy emerges between thermochemical nonequilibrium reaction in molecular gas and the spread of infectious diseases. In addition, tracking each individual’s viral load also facilitates the modeling of individual behavioral feedback effects (38–41), which is currently one of the most concerned topics in pandemic studies.

Our results are organized into across three sections. First, we introduce a nonequilibrium model to incorporate continuously varying individual viral load into the compartmental model framework. Subsequently, employing the Direct Simulation Monte Carlo (DSMC) method (42), the most popular approach in the field of rarefied gas dynamics, we delve into the analysis of secondary infection numbers, generation intervals, and reproduction number under different transmission rates. Additionally, we apply our method to a typical scenario involving the individual behavioral feedback, i.e. an individual movement speed exhibits a decrease due to an increase of the “body mass” with the augmentation of viral load, which could access the effects of movement restrictions due both to the patient’s willingness and government control measures. In every part, the generalized similarity between the present epidemiological problem and thermochemical nonequilibrium processes in a molecular gas is discussed. Finally, we summarize our key findings in the discussion section.

Results

Nonequilibrium compartmental model

The proliferation of the virus within the human body leads to an increase in infectivity (denoted by P) (43, 44). But over time, the antibodies gradually eliminate the virus, resulting in a continuous decline in both viral load and infectivity (45, 46). Obviously, when studying pandemic characteristics at the population level, whether it is necessary to account for the temporal variation of individual infectivity—rather than simply taking its average—depends on both the population size and the time scale of interest. To delineate this process, we extend the classical compartmental model into a nonequilibrium compartmental (NEC) model, drawing inspiration from vibrational excitation in molecular gas dynamics, which is also formally analogous to the age-structured models in epidemiology (47, 48).

Before delving into the specific details of the model, it is necessary to further clarify the concept of nonequilibrium. The Damköhler number Da, defined as the ratio of macroscopic flow time to the microscopic relaxation time of thermal or chemical process, is a key dimensionless parameter used to characterize nonequilibrium degree of the flow. These model equations can be solved either deterministically by using the finite difference method or probabilistic via DSMC method, and the latter could provide much richer information like the individual-level variability that are often obscured in deterministic methods.

The DSMC method was proposed by Graeme Bird (42, 67) in the 1960s for modeling the rarefied gas flows. As a molecular simulation tool, it has been used in a wide variety of disciplines (68) from aerodynamics of spacecrafts to heat transfer in microdevices. Ryosuke Yano (69) might be the first to apply the DSMC method to the modeling of infectious disease transmission. Pacheco et al. (15) also conducted a simulation study of COVID-19 transmission in New York City, marking the first time the DSMC method was used to simulate real infectious disease transmission processes. In DSMC, a single simulation particle could statistically represent a great number of real molecules or individuals, making it more efficient than the classical ABMs (13, 14) which are more akin to molecular dynamics (MD) method. This conclusion could also be specifically verified by the curves shown in the inset of Fig. 3B. It is demonstrated that a large Da corresponds to a nearly constant β (also P) whose value is close to its average. Basically, this performance results from the fact that in the large Da case, infected individuals fits a relatively stable distribution in the infection age domain, and consequently, the mean infection age τ¯, as well as the effective infectivity, can remain almost unchanged throughout most of the epidemic’s progression.

In contrast, when Da is small, the infection age structure changes continuously, and thus the gradually growing τ¯ can traverse from the left (τ¯<T) to the right (τ¯>T) side of the peak of P(τ) curve. Correspondingly, β first increases and then decreases, exhibiting a sharp, nonmonotonic variation similar in form to the bell-shaped P(τ) curve, as shown by the blue curve for Da=2.5 in the inset. This pronounced fluctuation dominates a significant portion of the transmission process and exemplifies the impact of nonequilibrium dynamics on pandemic evolution.

Furthermore, without major modifications, the present NEC model can be extended to incorporate additional influence factors, such as the presence of antibodies from vaccine- and/or infection-induced immune responses. In fact, the stochastic fluctuations observed in DSMC results are also projections of the real world events to some extent. Since the DSMC method is more convenient than the differential equations for tracking individual-level information and detecting diverse factors, we will primarily employ the DSMC method in the subsequent sections. We will explore the effects of different viral properties (characterized by T and α) and individual mobility on the pandemic features, such as the distribution of secondary infections and the generation interval.

Secondary infection

The new infections caused by each infected individual are termed secondary infections. The time-varying infectivity discussed above is mainly controlled by two parameters: the characteristic time scale T and the correlation coefficient α. This notion is not unfounded, as the RNA virus like SARS-CoV-2 is prone to mutations during self-replication, leading to changes in T and α (71–73). For different variants, obtaining the value of T from medical data is straightforward, but determining α remains challenging. Nevertheless, we can still compute α within possible ranges. Typical values for the Alpha, Delta, and Omicron variants correspond to T=210, 136, and 106 h, respectively. Combination of α and T can be used to represent different COVID variants or even other infectious diseases, and in a broader context, it also has certain predictive significance for potentially emerging variants.

Generally speaking, the distribution characteristics of secondary infections vary across different time slices as the susceptible individuals s declines gradually during the infection process, and a statistical analysis can also be performed over selected time windows (74, 75) or over the entire course of the pandemic. We conducted simulations of the three typical virus variants of SARS-CoV-2, and Fig. 4 shows the distribution of secondary infections at different time-points, with a time window of 1 day. The horizontal axis represents the number of individuals infected over the course of illness, while the vertical axis depicts the corresponding probability. The mean of the probability distribution function (Fsi) is the effective reproduction number Re. Figure 4A and D shows the distributions during the early stages of the pandemic, corresponding to the cases of α=0.002 and α=0.004, respectively. Despite incorporating a time-dependent β, the simulation results still fit well with a Poisson distribution with a mean equals to Re, which is in accordance with previous studies (76). It is worth noting that the three curves in Fig. 4B almost overlap, which is attributed to the negative feedback of the reproductive number. Generation interval

The generation interval (GI) refers to the time interval between the infection of an individual and the infection of his or her infector (77–79). It is commonly used to define the transmission timescale, i.e. a combination of both a host’s infectiousness profile since time of infection and the timing of social contacts between this primary case and potential infectees. The DSMC method enables detailed tracking of individual-level information and direct calculation of GI distribution, showing an evident superiority over the classical equation-based compartmental models. Generally, depending on whether one adopts the perspective of the infector or the infectee, two types of GI formulation are commonly used: the forward GI (who infected whom) and the backward GI (who was infected by whom) (80). In this section, we focus on the forward GI, denoted as Tgi, and record the infection-related information of each individual upon their recovery to infer their past infection dynamics (see SI Appendix Fig. S3).

As depicted in Fig. 6A, a consistent pattern in the evolution of mean GI, T¯gi is observed across three distinct viral variants, each characterized by different recovery periods. Specifically, the mean GI initially decreases due to competition among infected individuals, followed by a subsequent increase resulted from a shrinking susceptible population which makes new infections less probable. It is important to note that the late-stage increase in GI does not accurately reflect the underlying epidemic dynamics, because the disease has largely subsided by this stage, and GI values are derived from a limited number of remaining cases. Additionally, the zero point on the x-axis does not represent the onset of the epidemic, but rather the time when a statistically significant number of recoveries has been recorded. This cutoff point varies for each variant, and in the case of Alpha variant, for instance, it corresponds to the 42nd day after the appearance of the patient-zero. The same convention applies to Fig. 6C. Basically, a longer recovery period TR correlates with an extended T¯gi, and the temporal evolution of T¯gi is strongly influenced by the Da number. A comparative analysis of the Alpha, Delta, and Omicron variants reveals distinct dynamical behaviors. When deliberately setting the same value of α for all variants, the Alpha variant which has the largest TR and the corresponding smallest Da exhibits the most pronounced fluctuations. The significant nonequilibrium effect leads to increased variability in γ, which directly affects the GI. The yellow dotted line in Fig. 6A represents the T¯gi evolution under an equivalent constant β assumption for Omicron, corrected using (9), showing a greater volatility and a significant deviation from the time-varying result. This discrepancy underscores again that assuming constant infectivity, a common practice in many epidemiological studies, may lead to systematic bias and potential misjudgment of the epidemics.

Patients’ mobility decrease

As viral load increases within an infected individual, symptom severity typically escalates, resulting in a decreased mobility and thus a lower contact rate, as seen in the significant drop in daily interactions during the Hong Kong outbreak (83). Here, we examine the effect of this individual behavioral feedback by likening it to the two-temperature model commonly used in the high-temperature gas dynamics.

In the thermochemical nonequilibrium gases, the activated vibrational mode absorbs energy from the translational mode, which presentatively increases the vibrational temperature θv and at the same time decreases the translational temperature θt. A higher θv will promote the dissociation reaction while a lower θt will suppress the reaction. To capture this counteracting effect, Park (37) recommended the weighted temperature θtξθv1−ξ to refine the Arrhenius equation for the dissociation reaction rates of high-temperature gases, with the power index ξ being around 0.5. As we have considered the viral load in an individual’s body analogous to the vibrational energy of a diatomic molecule, a qualitatively similar feedback phenomenon could be observed in infectious disease transmission, despite that the contact between two individuals does not strictly obey conservation laws of mass, momentum, and energy.

Imagine that an infected individual becomes ill, feeling a “heavy” body accompanied by a diminished willingness and capacity for movement. Of course, healthy individuals tend to consciously avoid contact with visibly symptomatic ones. In essence, an increase of viral load will generally result in a reduce in mobility and contact frequency. Therefore, complex transmission models must incorporate a mechanism that adaptively reduces infected individuals’ mobility based on disease progression. Here, we intuitively and tentatively model the behavioral feedback by increasing the mass of the infected individuals as a function of viral load

Subsequently, we focus on the variations in peak infection proportion and transmission rate with respect to parameter φ, which may partly reflect the effectiveness of some pandemic control policy. As shown in Fig. 7C, the peak infection proportion decreases as φ increases. The exponential relationship between peak infection proportion and φ stems from (12), and the specific trend is determined through data fitting. As φ increases, the peak infection proportion decreases from 0.58 to 0.31, but then plateaus, implying that simply limiting patient mobility during the infection period is insufficient to eliminate disease transmission. In scenarios with a relatively lower basic reproduction number, β is approximately linearly correlated with the peak infection proportion (48). This relationship persists in our model, despite it incorporates time-varying transmission rates and mobility-dependent behavioral feedback, as shown in the inset of Fig. 7C.

As an initial exploration of the DSMC method in modeling the real-world scenarios, it still provides insights into the influence of individual behavioral responses on transmission dynamics. From a practical perspective, a slight reduction in patient mobility can significantly lower the infection peak, making it a highly efficient nonpharmaceutical intervention. However, further tightening of mobility yields diminishing returns in controlling disease spread.

Discussion

As the shadow of the COVID-19 pandemic gradually recedes, many uncertainties remain, and our understanding of transmission dynamics of suchlike infectious diseases is still far from complete. By drawing an insightful analogy between two typical complex systems, this study explores the extension of concepts and models in molecular gas dynamics into the pandemic modeling. This interdisciplinary approach provides a unique perspective on the spread of infectious diseases and could aid future efforts in pandemic prevention, preparedness, and response.

While the transmission process of infectious diseases has been well known similar to the chemical reaction of molecules under the mass action law, the proliferation and annihilation of the virus inside a patient’s body was thought to resemble the excitation and de-exciation of vibrational energy of a diatomic molecule. The viral load affects the transmission rate, just like that the degree of vibrational excitation affects the chemical reaction rate in a high-temperature gas. It was observed that for some highly contagious diseases, the time scale of the viral load variation is nonnegligible compared to that of the pandemic wave, suggesting that nonequilibrium effects could play a role.

A specific nonequilibrium compartmental model was proposed from the perspective of generalized analogy, to address the issue of time-varying infectivity. Correspondingly, a Damköhler number Da was introduced as the dimensionless nonequilibrium criterion, and a correction formula based on Da was obtained to approximately measure the nonequilibrium effects on the transmission dynamics. In the nonequilibrium situations (Da∼O(1)), it was found the traditional models employing a constant infectivity could yield distorted predictions for several detailed aspects of epidemic progression, which may misinform intervention strategies.

Moreover, the DSMC method in the field of rarefied gas dynamics was found particularly suitable for studying diseases transmission. It could simulate the stochastic nature in real world, and meanwhile shows advantages in modeling the contacts and tracking detailed infection history. This method was employed to give a preliminary analysis of some fundamental parameters including the secondary infection, generation interval and reproductive number, demonstrating its practical relevance in better understanding pandemics. For example, the analysis on the secondary infections distribution indicated a mutual inference relationship between viral virulence and proliferation period of different variants. A modeling of the individual behavior feedback, namely reducing the mobility of infected individuals, was also demonstrated as an example, and its analogy to the two-temperature model in high-temperature gas dynamics was discussed.

Admittedly, this study is a conceptual exploration which focuses mainly on establishing a specific framework based on generalized similarity. Nevertheless, this interdisciplinary viewpoint opens the door to a wide range of future investigations, for example, medical treatment analogous to a catalysis reaction, the metapopulations analogous to a heterogeneous gas mixture, the nonuniform spatial propagation of pandemic wave, the immunization strategy and especially, various individual behavior feedback models. This approach aims at enriching the understanding of disease spread and pandemic evolution and to showcase the potential of interdisciplinary approaches in predicting future pandemics and informing more effective interventions.
