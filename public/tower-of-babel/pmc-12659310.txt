The effect of acetyl tributyl citrate on coronary heart disease: a comprehensive computational analysis
Authors: Xu Ma, Yingying Liu, Zhen Hua, Feng Jiang, Shuxia Shi, Kaile Wang, Jie Yu, Lei Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRecent research suggests a link between acetyl tributyl citrate (ATBC) exposure and an increased risk of coronary heart disease (CHD).ObjectiveThis study investigated the molecular mechanisms underlying ATBC’s potential role in CHD pathogenesis.MethodsUsing “Acetyl tributyl citrate” as a search term, relevant targets were retrieved from the ChEMBL database. The standard simplified molecular input line entry system (SMILES) notation of ATBC was submitted to the SwissTargetPrediction database. All the targets obtained were compiled to create a target database for ATBC. Functional enrichment analysis and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) were performed to explore the potential pathogenic mechanisms of ATBC. The GSE66360 dataset was used as the training dataset, while GSE48060 and GSE60993 served as validation datasets. A total of 107 combinations of eleven machine learning algorithms, including Random Forest (RF), Elastic Net (Enet), support vector machine (SVM), least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression, Ridge regression, gradient boosting with component-wise linear model (glmBoost), partial least squares regression for generalized linear model (plsRglm), linear discriminant analysis (LDA), extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), Naive Bayes, and stepwise generalized linear model (Stepglm), were applied to identify the model with the highest area under the curve (AUC) as the best diagnostic model. Additionally, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were used to identify key hub genes. Single-cell transcriptomic data were employed to locate these hub genes, while molecular docking further validated the binding capacity between ATBC and its hub targets. This included converting the ligand to 3D format, performing molecular docking, and calculating the binding affinity and hydrogen bond formation between the molecules. The binding site with the lowest predicted binding affinity was selected for visualization.ResultBy integrating ATBC targets with CHD core modules, we identified genes associated with ATBC-induced CHD. Using the RF algorithm, we constructed the optimal diagnostic model and identified key hub genes, including MMP9, NLRP3, and PLAU. These genes were closely associated with glucose and lipid metabolism disorders, induction of estrogen resistance, and vascular inflammation. Furthermore, NLRP3 was predominantly expressed in monocytes, while PLAU showed higher expression in fibroblasts and endothelial cells. The molecular docking results indicated that the calculated predicted binding affinities were all less than or equal to -5.0 kcal/mol. This confirmed the binding affinities of ATBC with MMP9 and PLAU, and supported their involvement in the pathogenesis of coronary heart disease.ConclusionOur study predicted ATBC’s potential mechanisms in CHD progression and identified key hub genes, notably MMP9, NLRP3, and PLAU. These findings provide novel molecular targets for future research and highlight the potential health risks of ATBC in everyday applications.

Introduction

Plasticizers are a class of additives commonly used in plastics and other materials to improve their flexibility, plasticity, and workability. They work by reducing intermolecular forces, which increases the distance between polymer chains. This action effectively enhances the flow and molding properties of the material, facilitating manufacturing. With the advancement of industrialization and the increasing demand for plastic products, the use of plasticizers has skyrocketed, leading to serious environmental issues and problems related to endocrine disruption and reproductive toxicity [1, 2]. Phthalates, a specific type of plasticizer, are widely used in plastic manufacturing to enhance flexibility and durability. However, these compounds pose serious ecological and health risks as they can leach into the environment during their production, usage, and disposal [3, 4]. In response, many countries have introduced stricter regulations on plasticizers and are actively promoting the development of more environmentally friendly alternatives.

Network toxicology, a paradigm in toxicology research, aims to shift focus from individual toxicants to comprehensive biological network models that integrate diverse data sources [5]. By simultaneously analyzing complex toxicological pathways and molecular interactions, this approach provides a comprehensive understanding of how toxicants affect biological systems and thus facilitates the identification of characteristic toxic signatures and mechanisms [6]. Advancements in bioinformatics, particularly the integration of data across various gene expression profiles, protein-protein interactions, and metabolic pathways, have further accelerated toxicological research [7]. In parallel, the molecular docking technique, which elucidates the interactions between ligands and target proteins at potential binding sites, further validates the findings from network toxicology [8]. Together, these methodologies have significantly enhanced our understanding of chemical toxicity [9, 10].

Acetyl tributyl citrate (ATBC) is a next-generation plasticizer increasingly adopted as a safer alternative to phthalates, particularly in food packaging, pharmaceuticals, and personal care products. The European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) defines the tolerable daily intake (TDI) for ATBC as 1.0 mg/kg based on a comprehensive assessment [11, 12]. Its safety profile has led to regulatory approvals, such as the authorization by the European Union (EU) for its unrestricted use in food-contact plastics. While ATBC is generally regarded as safe at lower concentrations [13], its high mobility and potential to migrate from plastics into food, blood, air, and skin surfaces raise concerns about its environmental and health impact [14]. Studies have detected ATBC in common food items such as grains, rice, and bread, highlighting the potential route through which human exposure and bioaccumulation occur [15]. Its lipophilic nature facilitates its absorption into lipid-rich tissues. Moreover, its application in medical devices such as infusion tubes, blood bags, and stents [16] may result in exposure levels exceeding safe thresholds. Therefore, a thorough evaluation of the molecular mechanisms underlying the health risks posed by ATBC is urgently needed.

Despite its widespread acceptance as a safe plasticizer, emerging evidence indicates that zebrafish exposed to ATBC at concentrations of 3, 30, and 300 µg/L may experience endocrine disruption [17, 18]. Furthermore, studies in mice have linked ATBC exposure to lipid accumulation, hepatic steatosis, and exacerbated metabolic disorders, even at doses considered safe for humans [19, 20]. Moreover, its pronounced antiestrogenic activity [21] further raises concerns about its impact on vascular health and potential role in the pathogenesis of coronary heart disease (CHD). To address these concerns, the current study integrated network toxicology, bioinformatics, machine learning, and molecular docking techniques to investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying ATBC-induced CHD. Figure 1 illustrates the workflow of this study. Our findings provide critical mechanistic insights into the toxicological profile of ATBC and offer a scientific basis for future safety assessments. This study advocates for more stringent regulatory policy for safeguarding public health.

Methods

Collection of ATBC targets

The structure and standard Simplified Molecular Input Line Entry System (SMILES) notation of ATBC (PubChem CID: 6505) were corrected and standardized using the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) [22]. The term “Acetyl tributyl citrate” was used as a search keyword, and “Homo sapiens” was set as the filter to retrieve potential targets for ATBC from the ChEMBL database (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/chembl/) [23]. Subsequently, the standardized ATBC SMILES were submitted to the Swiss Target Prediction database (http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/) [24] for target prediction. The results from both the ChEMBL and Swiss Target Prediction databases were compiled, duplicates were removed [25, 26], and the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org/) was used to obtain the UniProt IDs for the predicted targets [27], thus establishing an ATBC target library.

Functional enrichment analysis

To elucidate the potential mechanisms of ATBC-induced disease, functional annotation of selected ATBC targets was performed. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was used to categorize the potential functions of these targets into Molecular Function (MF), Cellular Component (CC), and Biological Process (BP) categories. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis was performed using the KEGG database (https://www.genome.jp/kegg/) [28] to annotate the pathways in which these genes are involved, where a q-value < 0.01 is considered a key metabolic and signaling pathway. We used the clusterProfiler package for gene name conversion and performed GO enrichment analysis using enrichGO and KEGG enrichment analysis using enrichKEGG.

Collection and preprocessing of disease data

Acute myocardial infarction (AMI), a clinical manifestation of CHD, was used as a proxy to investigate the fundamental mechanisms underlying the development of CHD. Three AMI transcriptome datasets were obtained from the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) [29]. Among these, GSE66360 was designated as the training dataset, comprising 49 AMI samples and 50 controls [30], while GSE48060 [31] and GSE60993 [32] served as the test datasets, containing 31 AMI samples and 21 controls, and 17 AMI samples and 7 controls, respectively. Both GSE66360 and GSE48060 used GPL570 for platform annotation, while GSE60993 used GPL6884 for platform annotation. The platform annotation files were used to convert probe IDs to gene symbols. The GEO expression data was normalized using the normalizeBetweenArrays function from the limma package to ensure comparable distributions across samples and reduce technical variability. Batch effects, caused by differences between datasets or experimental conditions, were corrected using the removeBatchEffect function from the sva package, which removes unwanted variation based on batch information and preserves true biological signals.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis for identifying disease targets

Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) is a powerful computational tool designed to identify biologically relevant co-expression modules and intricate gene regulatory networks [33]. In this study, the WGCNA R package was employed to analyze the GSE66360 dataset and identified genes significantly associated with CHD. To ensure data quality, genes with a standard deviation greater than 0.5 were selected, and the goodSamplesGenes function was used to filter out genes that did not meet the established criteria. This pre-processing step enabled the construction of a scale-free gene co-expression network. Subsequently, we used the pickSoftThreshold function to determine the optimal soft-thresholding power based on the scale-free topology criterion (R² >0.8), which is crucial for constructing a robust adjacency matrix. The resulting adjacency matrix was transformed into a topological overlap matrix (TOM) to evaluate the strength of gene-gene interactions. We employed the dynamic tree cut algorithm to classify genes into distinct modules, with a minimum module size of 20 genes. For each identified module, we calculated the correlations among feature genes and established a merging threshold of 0.25. The mergeCloseModules function was used to merge similar modules, thereby enhancing the biological interpretability of the results. We used the plotDendroAndColors, TOMplot, and plotEigengeneNetworks functions to visualize the results of the WGCNA analysis. Finally, the identified gene modules and their interaction were visualized to explore their functional roles in the pathogenesis of CHD.

Construction of the protein-protein interaction (PPI) network

The intersecting genes from the potential targets of ATBC-induced CHD were submitted to the STRING 12.0 database (https://cn.string-db.org/) [34] to construct a PPI network. The organism was set to “Homo sapiens,” and the “minimum required interaction score” was set to “Medium Confidence >0.4”. This ensured the inclusion of active proteins associated with the target genes. The interaction network was imported into Cytoscape version 3.7.2 and we performed network analysis. The size and color of nodes were adjusted based on the degree and combined score; the larger the values, the darker the color and the larger the size.

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

GSEA is a computational method designed to evaluate the coordinated expression changes of predefined gene sets, providing insights into the overall activity of biological pathways [35]. To validate the role of key metabolic and signaling pathways in CHD, GSEA was conducted using the clusterProfiler R package, with the c2.cp.kegg.v7.2.symbols.gmt gene sets. To identify statistically significant pathways, we applied a false discovery rate (FDR) threshold of < 0.05. This approach enables us to systematically evaluate the enrichment of specific pathways and their potential roles within the biological contexts of immune and inflammatory responses, lipid metabolism, endocrine resistance, and estrogen secretion.

Machine learning for gene selection

To develop a robust diagnostic model, we used GSE66360 as the training dataset because it has the largest sample size among the candidate datasets. GSE48060 and GSE60993 were used as validation datasets. We employed 11 ensemble machine learning algorithms, including Random Forest (RF), Elastic Net (Enet), support vector machine (SVM), least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression, Ridge regression, gradient boosting with component-wise linear model (glmBoost), partial least squares regression for generalized linear model (plsRglm), linear discriminant analysis (LDA), extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), Naive Bayes, and stepwise generalized linear model (Stepglm). A total of 107 combinations of these algorithms were used to identify genes associated with ATBC-induced CHD. The best regularization parameters for Enet, LASSO, and Ridge were selected using 10-fold cross-validation, while LDA, glmBoost, and plsRglm also employed cross-validation to enhance their generalization capabilities. SVM and Stepglm incorporated flexibility and feature selection during model implementation. RF was optimized with respect to the number of trees (ntree = 1000) and node size (nodesize = 5), while leveraging feature importance and proximity information to improve predictive performance. glmBoost utilized 10-fold cross-validation to identify the optimal number of trees, whereas XGBoost employed 5-fold cross-validation to determine the best number of iterations, while also tuning tree depth and learning rate to enhance model generalization. The CalPredictScore function computes predicted scores based on the fitted model, generating response probabilities for various model types. The PredictClass function generates predicted classes from the computed probabilities. A comprehensive evaluation of model performance ensured the generalizability of all machine learning algorithms tested. We calculated and ranked the area under the curve (AUC) values for each of the 107 combinations of machine learning algorithms to determine the optimal model. Ultimately, we retained only the genes from this best-performing model.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis

ROC curve analysis was performed to assess the diagnostic utility of hub genes. Diagnostic performance was quantified by calculating the AUC, where higher values demonstrated stronger predictive power.

Collection and processing of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data in CHD

In this study, single-cell transcriptomic data were retrieved from the publicly available GEO database (Dataset GSE180678), specifically from one AMI sample [36], Using the Seurat R package, the expression matrix was converted into a sparse matrix to create a standardized Seurat object for downstream analyses. Quality control filters included: total RNA count >500, number of detected genes between 100 and 5000, mitochondrial gene content < 20%, and red blood cell gene proportion < 1%. The NormalizeData, FindVariableFeatures, and ScaleData functions were applied for data standardization and normalization. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was used to reduce data dimensionality. Finally, we used the Cell Marker 2.0 database (http://117.50.127.228/CellMarker/) [37] to classify cells in each cluster, identifying distinct cell types based on their marker genes.

Molecular docking

The selected hub genes were designated as target proteins for molecular docking analysis. Protein receptor files for the selected hub genes were downloaded from the RCSB Protein Data Bank database (https://www.rcsb.org/) [38]. Processing steps using PyMOL 2.5.0 software included removal of water molecules and heteroatoms, and the addition of hydrogen atoms. ATBC ligand files were retrieved from the PubChem database and converted to 3D format using Chem3D 22.0.0 software (Revvity Signals, Waltham, MA, USA). Using AutoDock Vina software for molecular docking, the docking box size was set to a cube of 40Å, with a grid spacing of 1Å, and the predicted binding affinities between ATBC and the target protein were calculated. The results were visualized using PyMOL software.

Results

ATBC targets selection and functional annotation

A total of 382 putative ATBC targets were identified by aggregating and deduplicating the predicted targets from the SwissTargetPrediction and ChEMBL databases. We explored the functional roles of the identified ATBC targets through GO and KEGG enrichment analyses (Fig. 2A, B). GO analysis indicated that the enriched biological processes were primarily associated with G protein-coupled receptor signaling, chemical synaptic transmission, and proteolysis. Enriched cellular components included the plasma membrane, presynaptic membrane, and postsynaptic membrane, while molecular functions were linked to enzyme binding, G protein-coupled serotonin receptor activity, and endopeptidase activity (q-value < 0.01). KEGG enrichment analysis revealed associations with various cancer pathways, the endocrine resistance pathway, the AGE-RAGE signaling pathway in diabetic complications, and lipid metabolism and atherosclerosis (q-value < 0.01). Based on prior literature, four key pathways were selected for further analysis: the AGE-RAGE signaling pathway, lipid metabolism and atherosclerosis, the endocrine resistance pathway, and the estrogen signaling pathway [19, 39, 40].

ATBC-induced CHD gene screening

We performed WGCNA on the GSE66360 gene set to identify key modules associated with CHD regulation. To ensure proper gene connectivity and achieve a scale-free network distribution (Fig. 3A), a soft-thresholding power of β = 14 was selected, resulting in the division of the dataset into 23 gene modules (r2 = 0.81) (Fig. 3B). Among these, the yellow module exhibited the highest correlation with CHD (correlation coefficient = 0.64, P = 1 × 10− 12) and comprised 757 genes (Fig. 3C). GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed to elucidate the biological significance of this module (Fig. 3D-E). Multiple immune- and inflammation-related pathways were found to be enriched, suggesting that the genes in the yellow module are involved in the regulation of immune and inflammatory responses in the context of CHD. Overlapping genes between the yellow module and the ATBC-related targets were identified as potential targets modulated by ATBC in CHD (Fig. 3F). A PPI network was then constructed from these overlapping targets, which consisted of 20 nodes and 47 edges (Fig. 3G).

Validation of pathway roles

We validated the roles of the four key pathways—the AGE-RAGE signaling pathway, lipid metabolism and atherosclerosis, the endocrine resistance pathway, and the estrogen signaling pathway—in CHD by conducting GSEA on the GSE66360 gene set. The analysis revealed significant enrichment of the AGE-RAGE signaling pathway, lipid metabolism and atherosclerosis, and the estrogen signaling pathway (FDR < 0.05; NES > 1), while the endocrine resistance pathway had an FDR of 0.092 and an NES of 1.41. These results suggest that the first three pathways play important roles in ATBC-induced CHD (Fig. 4A-D).

Hub gene screening and diagnostic efficacy verification

A total of 107 combinations of 11 machine learning algorithms were developed to identify key genes associated with CHD induced by ATBC (Fig. 5A). Based on the models’ AUC values, we selected the RF algorithm for its superior performance (Fig. 5B). Subsequently, using the training set, we plotted the expression levels of the eight core genes (Fig. 5C) and their corresponding ROC curves in both the AMI and control groups to evaluate diagnostic performance. Based on ROC curve metrics (Fig. 6A-C), MMP9, PLAU, and NLRP3 were identified as hub genes due to their significant contributions to the predictive models (training set, AUC > 0.8; testing set, AUC > 0.7).

Validation of hub genes at the scRNA-seq level

Following cell-level quality control (Fig. 7A), we partitioned all cells into 10 distinct clusters by applying the UMAP clustering algorithm (Fig. 7C). Based on the expression levels of established cell-type-specific markers across the clusters (Fig. 7B), we identified five specific cell types: smooth muscle cells, monocytes, NK cells, fibroblasts, and endothelial cells (Fig. 7D). To further investigate the expression patterns of the hub genes, we generated expression profiles of these genes across the cell types (Fig. 7E). The results showed that NLRP3 was predominantly expressed in monocytes, while PLAU exhibited higher expression in fibroblasts and endothelial cells. However, no significant expression pattern was observed for MMP9. This might be attributed to the absence of certain immune cell types in the single-cell dataset, such as neutrophils and T cells, which could have contributed to the detection of this gene.

Molecular docking validation

We obtained the X-ray diffraction structures of MMP9 (PDB ID: 1ITV; Resolution: 1.95 Å), NLRP3 (PDB ID: 3QF2; Resolution: 1.70 Å), and PLAU (PDB ID: 1C5W; Resolution: 1.94 Å) from the PDB database. Molecular docking was performed to assess the interactions between the hub genes (MMP9, NLRP3, and PLAU) and ATBC. We selected the site with the lowest predicted binding affinity and complemented this by presenting the binding site in the context of relevant literature [41, 42] (Fig. 8). Although the predicted binding affinity between NLRP3 and ATBC was − 5.2 kcal/mol, no hydrogen bond interactions were observed with ATBC. The molecular docking results revealed the following predicted binding affinities: MMP9 exhibited − 5.5 kcal/mol, and PLAU − 5.0 kcal/mol. All predicted binding affinities were less than or equal to -5.0 kcal/mol, indicating potential interactions between the hub gene and ATBC [43]. These results suggest that ATBC may be involved in CHD pathogenesis; however, further experimental validation is necessary to confirm its biological relevance.

Discussion

ATBC is an emerging plasticizer increasingly used in plastic products due to its advantageous properties. Although ATBC is generally recognized as safe, recent studies have raised concerns regarding its potential health risks, particularly its endocrine-disrupting and antiestrogenic effects. Due to the high migration characteristics of ATBC, some studies have reported migration levels as high as 79.8 mg/kg [44], significantly exceeding the TDI of 1.0 mg/kg defined by the EFSA [45]. Even at what is considered a safe dose, ATBC can still pose risks to cardiovascular health [18, 20]. These findings highlight the need for a thorough investigation into the impact of ATBC on CHD. This study aimed to elucidate the mechanisms through which ATBC contributes to CHD pathogenesis using an integrative approach that combined network toxicology, bioinformatics, machine learning, and molecular docking techniques. Our network toxicology analysis identified 24 core targets associated with ATBC-induced CHD, which, when validated using bioinformatics and machine learning approaches, confirmed MMP9, NLRP3, and PLAU as central hub genes (p < 0.05). Furthermore, pathway enrichment analysis revealed the involvement of the AGE-RAGE signaling pathway (implicated in diabetic complications), lipid metabolism, atherosclerosis, and the estrogen signaling pathway in these processes. Molecular docking simulations supported the plausibility of interactions between ATBC and these hub targets.

The accumulation of ATBC can trigger endocrine disruption and interfere with glucose and lipid metabolism [39]. Our pathway analysis revealed ATBC’s potential to modulate key pathways including the AGE-RAGE signaling pathway and lipid metabolism, both of which are critical contributors to CHD. Elevated levels of lipids, such as cholesterol and triglycerides, lead to low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) deposition in blood vessels, forming atherosclerotic plaques, a well-known risk factor for CHD [46]. Similarly, in patients with diabetes, AGEs activate endogenous immune cells through the RAGE receptor, which promotes NLRP3 and MMP9 expression [47], leading to chronic inflammation and cellular dysfunction that accelerates the progression of CHD [48]. Furthermore, metabolic dysregulation-induced lipid overload further promotes the expression of inflammatory factors [49]. These findings indicate that ATBC interferes in glucose and lipid metabolism likely elevating the expression of inflammatory factors, which further exacerbate atherosclerosis and CHD development.

Estrogen is recognized for its cardiovascular protective effects, particularly in premenopausal women. However, ATBC exhibits estrogen-antagonistic properties, which may significantly impair vascular health [21]. In our study, pathway analysis revealed that the estrogen signaling pathway plays a pivotal role in modulating inflammatory responses and lipid metabolism, which are crucial for protecting against CHD. Estrogen raises the levels of high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) while lowering LDL-C and triglycerides (TG), thereby improving lipid profiles [50]. Additionally, estrogen regulates inflammatory responses within the body by decreasing inflammatory factor expression levels, which reduces the infiltration of inflammatory cells during atherosclerosis and preserves endothelial cell function, ultimately lowering the risk of cardiovascular diseases [51].

Our analysis identified MMP9, a key enzyme involved in extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, as a central hub gene in ATBC-induced CHD. MMP9 plays a critical role in atherosclerosis and cardiovascular diseases through its enzymatic role in ECM remodeling and degradation [52]. During atherosclerosis development, MMP9 activity increases in the damaged vascular endothelium, where it disrupts structural integrity and impairs endothelial function. This facilitates inflammatory cell infiltration and exacerbates inflammation, accelerating atherosclerosis progression [53]. Excessive MMP9 expression has been associated with plaque instability, increasing the risk of cardiovascular events such as myocardial infarction and stroke [54]. Elevated plasma levels of MMP9 have also been correlated with higher cardiovascular mortality among patients with CHD, suggesting its potential as a prognostic biomarker for CHD risk [55]. Our identification of MMP9 as a central hub gene in ATBC-induced CHD suggests that ATBC exposure may exacerbate MMP9-driven inflammation and matrix remodeling, further destabilizing plaques and increasing the risk of acute cardiovascular events. Thus, targeting MMP9 may represent a promising approach to mitigating CHD progression, particularly in individuals heavily exposed to emerging plasticizers such as ATBC. However, due to the inclusion of only a single dataset of single-cell transcriptomics, we overlooked critical immune cell populations such as neutrophils and T cells, resulting in the absence of MMP9 expression within the scRNA-seq dataset. Including these cell types may significantly enhance our understanding of the role of MMP9 in the biological processes investigated in our study.

The PLAU gene encodes urokinase-type plasminogen activator (uPA), an enzyme essential for converting plasminogen to plasmin, which is a critical step in fibrinolysis. PLAU has been implicated in the progression of atherosclerotic plaques and serves as an early indicator of stable coronary artery disease (SCAD) transitioning to AMI [56]. In our study, PLAU was identified as a central hub gene in ATBC-induced CHD, suggesting that ATBC may influence PLAU activity and contribute to thrombus formation and atherosclerosis progression. Farris et al. demonstrated that macrophage-derived uPA promotes CHD progression [57]. In contrast, our study revealed that PLAU expression was only observed in endothelial cells and fibroblasts. This suggests that PLAU in these cell types plays a similar role in advancing CHD progression. These findings suggest that ATBC exposure likely exacerbates PLAU-mediated mechanisms, accelerating the pathological progression of CHD. The association between PLAU and atherosclerosis severity highlights its potential as both a biomarker and therapeutic target in ATBC-related CHD.

NLRP3 encodes the NLRP3 inflammasome, a tightly regulated macromolecular protein complex that senses cellular damage and amplifies inflammatory responses through caspase-1 activation [58]. This mechanism plays a critical role in the initiation and progression of CHD. Damaged vascular endothelial cells activate NF-κB-mediated transcription, leading to NLRP3 inflammasome assembly [59]. This process exacerbates vascular wall inflammation and contributes to plaque instability. Our single-cell analysis revealed that circulating monocytes specifically express NLRP3 components, promoting IL-1β release and further amplifying systemic inflammation [60]. NLRP3 is a putative target of ATBC. While our molecular docking studies did not indicate hydrogen bond interactions whether it has other non-bonded interactions (e.g., hydrophobic contacts, van der Waals interactions) remains unclear. This requires further in-depth investigation.

This study employed a comprehensive approach to explore the potential molecular mechanisms by which ATBC promotes the pathogenesis of CHD and to establish its relevance in patients with this condition. However, the primary limitation of this study is that our findings are primarily predictive, and extensive experimental validation is required to confirm their reliability. We suggest that future studies should have focused on two key areas: first, identification of pathogenic concentration and dose-response assessments of ATBC to determine its threshold effects in different individuals and stages of the disease; second, human cardiovascular cell lines and primary endothelial cells should be used for in vitro experiments to elucidate the mechanisms underlying pathogenic effects of ATBC on cell signaling and overall function.

Conclusion

In this study, we explored the potential mechanism by which ATBC, a commonly used plasticizer, contributes to the development of CHD. Through an integrative approach using network toxicology, bioinformatics, machine learning, and molecular docking techniques, we predicted key targets and pathways involved in the promotion of CHD by ATBC. MMP9, NLRP3, and PLAU were found to be central hub genes, with molecular docking analysis confirming strong binding affinities between these targets and ATBC. Pathway enrichment analysis highlighted the involvement of the AGE-RAGE and estrogen signaling pathways, both of which play significant roles in metabolic dysregulation and atherosclerosis progression. Furthermore, our findings suggest that ATBC promotes CHD through disruption of glucose and lipid metabolism, and exerts antagonistic effects on estrogen signaling, leading to increased inflammation and endothelial dysfunction. Overall, while this study predicts the potential risks and mechanisms of ATBC in CHD using computational methods, which is of certain research importance, it lacks experimental evidence and real-world exposure-response data. Therefore, future research should focus on conducting further validation through experimental studies.
