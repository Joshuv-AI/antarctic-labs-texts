Intersectionality, #MeToo, and the Mainstream Media: The New York Times’ Problematic Framing of the Blasey Ford/Kavanaugh and Reade/Biden Sexual Assault Allegations
Authors: Alvaro Oleart, Noah Anton Schmitt
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis article asks: How did The New York Times’ opinion section frame the sexual assault allegations of Christine Blasey Ford against Brett Kavanaugh compared to Tara Reade's against Joe Biden? Through a framing analysis, it compares The New York Times' opinion coverage of Blasey Ford's allegations in 2018 with Reade's allegations in 2020, both of which took place in the context of the #MeToo movement. We show that the first case frames sexual violence as a systematic problem and empowers survivors of sexual violence, but nonetheless lacks an intersectional dimension. In contrast, the framing of Reade's allegation is entirely about Biden's political prospects, mobilizes sexist frames to dismiss the allegations, and legitimizes a discourse that inhibits future survivors of sexual violence from being heard. We conclude that the comparison suggests The New York Times was more motivated by partisanship than solidarity with the #MeToo movement in its opinion coverage of both cases.Keywords: #MeToo, The New York Times, framing, feminism, intersectionality

Introduction: Blasey Ford, Reade, and the #MeToo Movement

For many people in the United States, and particularly women, 2017 is marked by two societal moments that stand in stark contrast to one another. On one side is the inauguration of what would turn out to be Donald Trump's first term in office. Trump's election came, amongst others, on the back of numerous sexual assault allegations against him (Padilla, 2024), as well as his on-tape bragging about sexual assault (Fahrenthold, 2016). On the other side stands the #MeToo movement. “Me too” as an idea was coined by Tarana Burke, who created an initiative in 2006 to call attention to Women of Color who survived sexual abuse, assault, and exploitation (Boyd & McEwan, 2022). However, the catchphrase garnered broad public attention when the actress Alyssa Milano tweeted #MeToo, which went viral amidst thousands of women sharing their experiences of sexual violence on Twitter. Given the re-election of Trump in 2024 and the continued repression of reproductive (Center for Reproductive Rights, 2025) and transgender rights (The White House, 2025), among more than could be named here, this was in retrospect a short-lived moment of hope for a systematic challenge of the patriarchal status quo. Nonetheless, the #MeToo movement's central dimension was to problematize the prevalence of sexual violence and systematic abuse of power by men toward women in society. Its greatest accomplishment is arguably that it shifted the discourse around sexual violence toward believing survivors and thus empowered many women to be heard. Notwithstanding these promises, however, scholars also point out important pitfalls of the discourse around #MeToo. Particularly problematic is that the movement lacked a systematic and intersectional approach to sexual violence, as this actively excluded substantial groups of people from being heard (see, e.g., Curry, 2019; Evans, 2018; Hoerl, 2019; Leung & Williams, 2019; Serano, 2013).

In the wake of the #MeToo movement, as well as Trump's presidency, Christine Blasey Ford and Tara Reade both accused prominent political figures of sexual assault committed decades earlier. Blasey Ford alleged in 2018 that then-Supreme Court Justice nominee Brett Kavanaugh sexually assaulted her at a house party in 1982 when they were in high school (Washington Post, 2018). Two years later, in 2020, Reade came forward with allegations that then-presidential candidate Joe Biden touched her inappropriately when she had been a staff assistant for then-Senator Biden's office in 1993. Later on, Reade asserted further details and accused him of sexual assault (Halper, 2020). We choose not to reproduce the accounts provided by Blasey Ford and Reade of the alleged sexual assault in further detail, due to their graphic, disturbing, and potentially re-traumatizing nature. More specific information on the allegations is provided in Blasey Ford's testimony before the Senate Judiciary Committee (The Washington Post, 2018, September 27), as well as the initial article that publicized Reade's allegations (Riquelmy, 2019) and a podcast appearance by Reade (Halper, 2020).

The Blasey Ford/Kavanaugh hearings have already received scholarly attention from a communication perspective (see AbiNader et al., 2021; Blumell & Mulupi, 2020; Boling & Moscowitz, 2021; de Saint Felix & Corrigan, 2022; Gilmore, 2019; Pollino, 2020; Wright et al., 2022). Nonetheless, the framing of Blasey Ford's allegations has not yet been taken as a point of reference for later cases, such as Reade/Biden. While the aforementioned case has been mentioned in some scholarly work (see, e.g., Mitra & Conner, 2021; Serisier, 2022), it has not been the focus of a systematic analysis. Doing so in the present article promises to continue to trace how the discursive zeitgeist of #MeToo has evolved (Hoerl, 2019). Accordingly, this article asks: How did The New York Times’ opinion section frame the sexual assault allegations of Christine Blasey Ford against Brett Kavanaugh compared to Tara Reade's against Joe Biden?

To that end, the remainder of the article is structured as follows. First, we introduce the theory that underpins the idea of framing through an intersectional feminist lens. Second, we delineate the data set, methodology, and framing codes used in the empirical analysis. Next, we present our findings by first outlining the The New York Times’ (NYT) framing of Blasey Ford's 2018 sexual assault allegations against Kavanaugh and then moving on to Reade's 2020 sexual assault allegations against Biden. Finally, we argue that the Blasey Ford case is exemplary for the #MeToo discourse in both senses of the word. That is to say, it pointed to systematic abuses of power, enabled many other survivors to be heard, and simultaneously missed the opportunity to be intersectional. In contrast, the framing of Reade's allegations against Biden did not even take on the promising parts of the discourse. Here, the NYT used sexist frames to discredit Reade for partisan reasons and legitimized a discourse that inhibits future survivors of sexual violence from being heard. We conclude that it is a worrisome sign for the #MeToo movement overall that this sort of framing was exhibited by the NYT, given how instrumental it was in bringing the #MeToo discourse to the mainstream.

Theory: Framing Through an Intersectional Feminist Lens

News media discourse is a central space that reflects and shapes power relations, primarily through its capacity to frame how to make sense of the world. Pan and Kosicki (2005, p. 177) define framing as an “interpretive framework for thinking about a political object.” Importantly, as framing is primarily about “selection and salience” (Entman, 1993, p. 52), media actors are always central. In consequence, the prospects of any social movement are intimately related to shaping media actors’ way of interpreting social reality, and more broadly, mainstream news media's framing (Benford & Snow, 2000). Framing issues in the public sphere is inherently ideological, as powerful actors contribute to the way in which readers make sense of the world. Discourses and frames are ideological in the sense that they are “representations of practices formed from particular perspectives in the interest of maintaining unequal power relations and dominance” (Lazar, 2007, p. 146). In consequence, discourses encourage certain meanings and social orders over others.

We undertake the analysis through a feminist lens (Hardin & Whiteside, 2010). It is feminist insofar as the article scrutinizes how “gendered assumptions and hegemonic power relations are discursively produced, sustained, negotiated, and challenged in different contexts and communities” (Lazar, 2007, p. 142). In accordance, the approach cannot be merely feminist, but must also be intersectional. As originally conceptualized by Crenshaw (1989), intersectionality emphasizes that the discrimination against Black women in the workplace could not be adequately addressed within a legal framework that treats discrimination based on race and discrimination based on gender (or class) independently from one another. Such single-issue frameworks miss the double discrimination that, for example, Black women face as being both Black and women. One reason why the Black and White feminist movements of the 20th century in the United States struggled to connect is precisely the failure to find common ground between the different, but concurrent and intersecting experiences of oppression (Breines, 2007). As argued by the renowned Black feminist Lorde (2007, p. 138), “there is no thing as a single-issue struggle because we do not live single-issue lives.” Therefore, it is precisely the intersections of oppressive structures that should be the focus point.

Angela Davis (2020, p. 30) underlines this point when she asks: “How does our view of gender violence change if we look at it from the vantage point of Black women or Indigenous women, or working-class and poor women of all racial backgrounds?” Such de-centering of middle-class White women is as essential to feminist analysis as it is to any movement that aims to achieve transformational and emancipatory social and political change. Perhaps an even more expansive view of liberation is expressed by Womanism. Centered on the experiences and practices of Women of Color, it likewise insists on intersectionality, but adds spirituality, culture, care and community, as the pathways to collective liberation (see e.g., Brewer, 2020; Collins, 1996; Rodgers, 2016). Burke's initial “me too” is a good example of resisting the present conditions of oppression and building better lives through care, and community. Not the least because there has historically not been a supportive environment for Black women survivors of sexual violence in the United States (Slatton & Richard, 2020).

To operationalize the framing analysis through an intersectional feminist lens, we draw on the intellectual tradition of Cuklanz (1996, 2000). That means analyzing specific cases, such as the Anita Hill and Clarence Thomas hearings, to draw inferences about discursive shifts around the coverage of sexual violence (allegations). Cuklanz shows that while some progress has been made in advancing feminist perspectives, much of today's framing of sexual violence in mainstream media remains anchored on men's stories and masculinity. This also holds for news coverage in the context of #MeToo (Cuklanz, 2020). Not only that, but despite its Black feminist origins, the #MeToo movement largely missed a systematic and intersectional dimension (Hoerl, 2019). That is to say, it did not operate in a way that addressed the connections between sexual violence and other types of systemic oppression. This matters because not all survivors of sexual violence are seen in the same way. Members of social groups such as Black women (Collins, 2004) or sex workers (DiBennardo, 2018) have struggled to be recognized in the public space. Instead of considering these intersections, the #MeToo movement has tended to individualize sexual violence and center the experiences of White, famous, and wealthy cisgender women (see, e.g., Curry, 2019; Evans, 2018; Leung & Williams, 2019; Serano, 2013).

In line with this, Boyd and McEwan (2022, p. 3462) argue that the #MeToo movement has co-opted Burke's initiative and cultivated an “inclusive illusion.” In the name of “all women,” the focus of #MeToo has actually been on White women and as a result marginalizes the communities to whom “me too” was originally dedicated. In opposition to this, intersectional feminism makes the case for collective liberation from different but related struggles. Oppression is not individual but stems from structural dynamics that require one to make sense of them in conjunction. As argued by hooks (2000, p. 9), for “any movement to resist the co-optation of feminist struggle must begin by introducing a different feminist perspective—a new theory one that is not informed by the ideology of liberal individualism.” Thus, the critical task is henceforth not to take feminist discourse at face value when it appears in the NYT's framing. It is to be vigilant, dissect the particular frames used, and bring to the surface the power relations embedded in them—be they related to gender, sexuality, race, or class.

Methodology

Data and Methodology

The empirical focus of this analysis is the NYT, due to its exceptional ability to promote specific interpretative frameworks, both as the leading liberal-leaning mainstream news outlet (Hall, 2013; Usher, 2020) and as one of the highest-circulation daily newspapers in the United States (Naseer & St. Aubin, 2023). Moreover, as a news outlet and an opinion leader, the NYT played a crucial role in the rise of the #MeToo movement (Kantor & Twohey, 2019). It was the first major newspaper to report on the accusations against Harvey Weinstein (Santora & Baker, 2015), and its magazine named key figures of the movement as “person of the year” in 2017 (Zacharek et al., n.d.). Based upon this proximity to the #MeToo movement, we take it to be insightful to focus exclusively on the NYT.

What is more, our analysis looks to the opinion section of the NYT to detail the particular frames that emanated from it. While scholarship often focuses on how the news sections of media outlets frame concrete issues (D'Angelo, 2018), the opinion section remains relevant for shaping public opinion (see, e.g., Boling & Moscowitz, 2021; Ha, 2017; Izadi & Saghaye-Biria, 2007). It sets the interpretive boundaries within which public debate is reasonable and thus considered acceptable. For example, the controversy around an Op-Ed by Senator Tom Cotton (2020) published by the NYT and the subsequent resignation of, among others, the editorial page editor James Bennet, presents an explicit case where the boundaries of that domain were negotiated. Unlike news reporting, which generally at least strives for objectivity, opinion pieces explicitly present a subjective point of view on an issue. They take a stance, detail not the “truth” of what happened per se, but advocate for one particular interpretation, reasoning, and conclusion to be drawn. As such, the NYT opinion section acts as a curated space in which positions are not only presented, but legitimized and actively endorsed.

On top of that, the NYT actively seeks out opinion pieces that differ from the editorial line. In their Op-Eds guidelines, the NYT writes:

If you read the editorials, you know that they present a pretty consistent liberal point of view. There are lots of other ways of looking at the world, to the left and right of that position, and we are particularly interested in presenting those points of view. (Hall, 2013)

Hence, not everything that the NYT chooses to publish in its opinion section reflects the actual opinion of the NYT. Nonetheless, the NYT does choose to publish certain texts over others and thus decides whether an opinion is to be given a public platform or not. This is important to keep in mind, considering the different types of texts that make up the opinion section. Editorials, already mentioned briefly above, represent the opinion of the NYT editorial board. The board itself is made up of a group of NYT opinion columnists and editors, and as such it is the NYT's “institutional voice” on opinion matters (Bennet, 2020). Opinion columns, on the other hand, present the opinions of the respective columnist. That is, NYT staff writers, who publish regularly and are tasked with “guiding readers through the events of the moment” (Tumin, 2017). Aside from the staff, guest writers may submit essays to the NYT independently or be purposely invited to do so. Last, the NYT also publishes letters to the editor in its opinion section, which respond to previous opinion pieces.

The concrete data set for our analysis consists of opinion pieces ranging from the first opinion pieces published discussing the particular case until the dominant framing of the allegations was conclusively established. This point marks the moment in the discursive contest where the opinions culminate in stable and merely reiterated positions on the issue, as well as an overall reduced number of Op-Eds published. In the case of Blasey Ford, the analyzed Op-Eds range from September 16 to September 28, 2018. This was the day after Blasey Ford testified publicly before the Senate Judiciary Committee in the context of Kavanaugh's confirmation hearings. In the case of Reade/Biden, the analyzed Op-Eds range from April 13 to May 18 in 2020, after which the NYT published no more opinion pieces discussing the allegations. In terms of total opinion pieces analyzed, there are 71 in the case of Blasey Ford/Kavanaugh and 17 in the case of Reade/Biden. As such, the total number of opinion pieces analyzed thus exceeds the sample size from Boling and Moscowitz (2021), which compared opinion coverage of the Blasey Ford/Kavanaugh hearings with the Hill/Thomas hearings. Despite the comparatively low sample size in the case of Reade/Biden, the comparison remains relevant because the lack of coverage may already be considered part of the overall power relations at play.

As Table 1 shows, about half of the articles were published by NYT opinion columnists, next a substantive number of guest pieces, then letters to the editor, and last editorials. A detailed sociological analysis of the makeup of the writers and editors behind the opinion pieces is outside the scope of this study (see Boling & Moscowitz, 2021, for a review of the literature on this topic). Nonetheless, there is ample evidence that mainstream news outlets such as the NYT remain largely White institutions that orient their work toward the audience that can afford to pay for news: namely, wealthy, white, and liberal audiences (Usher 2021). One reason for this is the limited access People of Color have historically had to the elite academic pipelines, employment opportunities, and professional networks of mainstream news outlets (Hardaway et al., 2022; Johnson, 2017). The question of who has a voice in the opinion section of major news outlets such as the NYT is not only about diversity and representation as such, but also about epistemic justice. It impacts whose knowledge, voices, perspectives, and opinions are given a platform to shape mainstream discourses. For example, Boling and Moscowitz (2021) argue that an increased presence of women and People of Color in the opinion section explains in part why the discourse in the Blasey Ford/Kavanaugh case improved in comparison to the coverage of Anita Hill's allegations of sexual harassment against then-Supreme Court Justice nominee Clarence Thomas in 1991.

We selected the two cases based on two analytical dimensions. First, in both cases, the allegations of sexual violence were directed against political actors who were candidates for high-scale political office with clear partisan affiliations. On the one hand, Kavanaugh was nominated by Trump and needed the support of the Republican Party to pass the confirmation process in the Senate. On the other hand, Biden was at the time a Democratic presidential candidate and needed to gather the support to win the 2020 primaries and, later, the Presidential election. Second, they took place at different points in time, which promises insights into how the #MeToo discourse developed. While the Blasey Ford/Kavanaugh case occurred early on in the movement and attained broad media coverage, the Reade/Biden case took place later on and only received limited media attention. Besides this, the cases display several similarities, which allow for a comparative analysis. For instance, both women accused powerful men decades after the alleged assault. They both have some contemporaneous evidence, as the women had told their stories to family and friends in the past before publicizing them. Furthermore, there is some degree of uncertainty resulting from the ambiguities in the details recounted over time in both cases. Importantly, we do not claim that the two cases are perfectly comparable but rather that they are sufficiently similar for our analysis. We consider the relevance of contextual differences in the discussion section later on.

We coded the data set inductively, by using the software NVivo and creating framing codes in successive rounds of coding. To ensure inter-reliability between the two authors, the articles were coded by each author, respectively. There were no substantial disagreements, only minor discrepancies in the coding of a few sections, which mostly related to the particular subframe to be applied. Discrepancies were resolved by revising the articles in question together. Finally, once coded, the authors distinguish between body and headlines (comprising title and subtitle) in each article. This is important as headlines foreground a particular discursive theme and subsequently guide the reader's interpretation of the text (Sun & Cheung, 2022). Consequently, we conceptualize the “dominant frame (s)” in each article as corresponding to those frames that appear both in the headlines and the body. This allows us to display the results in a clear and concise manner, without compromising the analytical nuance of our coding process. If a frame appears in the body of the article but not in the headlines or vice versa, it is not considered a “dominant frame.” Additionally, if multiple frames appear simultaneously in the body and headline, the article is coded as referencing multiple “dominant frames.”

Framing Codes: #Metoo, Credibility, Horse Race, and Partisanship

We identified four frames that pervaded the NYT's reporting: #metoo, credibility, horse race, and partisanship. Most frames encompass two subframes that capture the nuances of how it was employed over time. The defining characteristics of our framing codes are briefly outlined below (for exemplary quotes for each frame and subframe, see the Appendix).

First, the #metoo frame captures discourses centered on linking the allegations of sexual harassment and assault to wide-ranging social phenomena. Generally, the frame is employed to discuss the position of women in society at large rather than the details of the individual cases themselves. In addition, this frame encompasses the sexist system and #MeTooFar subframes. The former frames the allegations, as well as the political responses to them, as manifestations of structural injustices that perpetually disadvantage women. In contrast, the #MeTooFar frame emphasizes that the #MeToo movement has effectively equated mere accusations with convictions in public opinion.

Second, the credibility frame refers to discussions about whether the allegations, the accuser/accused themselves are believable or not. This frame foregrounds questions of knowledge. As a result, “truth-finding” is at the discursive center stage, which manifests in expressions of support or skepticism toward the given “evidence.” Hence, epistemic terms such as corroboration, certainty, testimony, and trust indicate this frame. The accuser credibility and candidate credibility subframes denote whose credibility is discussed. To that end, statements framing either person's credibility in a positive or negative light fall under either the accuser or candidate-focused frame. This enables us to illustrate on whom the burden of proof is placed.

Third, the horse race frame assigns particular attention to formal political processes such as candidacies, elections, and negotiations, as well as their corresponding viability of success. Consequently, the allegations are not referenced for their own sake but concern their impact on the political games. When the allegations are related to the confirmation prospects of Biden or Kavanaugh, then the frame is further denoted under candidacy horse race. In line with this, general electoral campaigns (e.g., senate majorities and presidential elections) references are also encompassed under the frame. The procedural horse race subframe, on the other hand, captures discussions around the appropriate procedure to administer the allegations and their role in formal political processes.

Fourth, the partisanship frame prioritizes allegiance to a certain political position over the substance of the allegations. This manifests most notably along two dimensions. First, the accusers themselves are framed as partisan. Hence, the motivation for their allegations is not the sexual assault perpetrated by the respective candidates but rather the aim to impair a political adversary. Second, the response to the allegations is framed as informed by the political interests of Democrats or Republicans.

Analysis: The New York Times' Opinion Coverage of Blasey Ford/Kavanaugh and Reade/Biden

“An X-ray View of the Rotten Foundations of Elite Male Power”: Blasey Ford accusation against Kavanaugh as an Exemplary #MeToo Moment

As illustrated in Figure 1, the initial reaction of the NYT opinion section to the sexual assault accusations made by Blasey Ford is driven by how they will affect the nomination process of Kavanaugh. Nonetheless, from the beginning there are signs of the credibility frame. Then, the discourse progressively turns toward becoming increasingly feminist along the lines of the sexist system frame. The predominance of the sexist system and (high) credibility frames for Blasey Ford throughout the second half of the discourse until the hearings is especially striking. Aside from that, the horse race frame is also prevalent, particularly at the end, which is the day after Blasey Ford had testified before the Senate Judiciary Committee.

Distribution of Dominant Frames by Reporting Days in the Blasey Ford/Kavanaugh Case.

Notably, the first Op-Ed on the subject was entitled “Delay the Vote—for Kavanaugh, for His Accuser, and for the Court” (Lat, 2018) and written by a guest writer who is a legal professional. This frame continues with other pieces published by the NYT early on, which mainly touched upon the (high) credibility of Blasey Ford, and how the accusations ought to impact the candidacy of Kavanaugh. Thus, from the start, the allegations are given substantial credence. Prominent NYT columnists and the NYT editorial board frame the accusations from a (high) credibility perspective in combination with the horse race frame. At the beginning of the discourse, there is little trace of the #metoo frame, even though in most cases Blasey Ford is perceived to be credible. Early on Anita Hill also published a piece as a guest writer. In it, Hill (2018) advocates for a hearing process in the Senate that does better than it did in her allegations of sexual harassment against then-Supreme Court Justice nominee Clarence Thomas. Due to the similarities between the cases, a recurring point of reference for NYT columnists is the comparison with Hill. Curiously, however, race does not become a prominent point of discussion in these references. Hill herself, moreover, does not speak about the role that the intersections of race- and gender-based oppression played in her case.

As the coverage continues, an increasing number of pieces emphasize that the Blasey Ford allegations are part of a structural phenomenon of sexual violence against women and center the hurdles survivors face in speaking out. As a result, the #metoo and credibility frames continue to gain prevalence in this phase. The first piece in which this framing appears prominently is an editorial board piece entitled “Why America Needs to Hear Brett Kavanaugh's Accuser.” The editorial states: “If anyone is still wondering why women don’t speak up about sexual assault sooner, or at all, you have your answer” (Editorial Board, 2018b). Interestingly, the NYT opinion section published several pieces that comprise letters from readers, which also widely reproduce the #metoo frame (and the sexist system subframe) combined with the credibility frame. For instance, a reader argued that the “backlash against Christine Blasey Ford, Judge Kavanaugh's accuser, is exactly why women have kept silent about their attacks and attackers” (Opinion Letters, 2018a). Another letter reads:

I have been re-triggered by the attacks on Dr. Blasey's character and credibility. I have no doubt that this has been the case for millions of other survivors of sexual assault as well. Is it any wonder that so many of us wait years before we tell, or even take this trauma to our graves? (Opinion Letters, 2018b)

This feminist discourse provided room for other victims of sexual violence to share their stories. Another example of this is a guest piece written by Padma Lakshmi, a popular television host, entitled “I Was Raped at 16 and I Kept Silent” (2018). However, these revelations by Lakshmi are simultaneously indicative of #MeToo being covered in terms of “celebrity news,” and the space for being heard in public was focused on public personalities, rather than on the structural inequalities where gender, race, and class intersect.

Among the NYT columnists, Michelle Goldberg was the most outspoken in not only expressing support for Blasey Ford but also highlighting the sexist system that silences women. In an Op-Ed entitled “Boys Will Be Supreme Court Justices,” Goldberg argues that “If the Kavanaugh nomination goes forward, it's because Trump and his allies believe that a certain class of men accused of sexual assault deserve impunity” (Goldberg, 2018a). In another piece, the columnist argues the following:

this scandal has given us an X-ray view of the rotten foundations of elite male power. Despite Donald Trump's populist posturing, there are few people more obsessed with Ivy League credentials. Kavanaugh's nomination shows how sick the cultures that produce those credentials—and thus our ruling class—can be. (2018b)

Here, gender and class are discussed in conjunction with one another. Goldberg points not only to “male power” but to the “ruling class” as the root causes of a culture permeated by sexual violence. Besides Goldberg's piece, Kimberly Crenshaw (2018)—who assisted Anita Hill's legal team in 1991—published a guest piece to argue that “black feminist frameworks have been doing the hard work of building the social justice movements that race-only or gender-only frames cannot,” which could have spearheaded a more intersectional discourse. However, besides Crenshaw's intervention, it is the absence of race as a discursive category that is notable, and class is alluded to in some cases, but not explicitly addressed.

For example, one could have asked how Blasey Ford's allegations would have been received by the public if they had come from a Black woman, as Crenshaw does (Heck, 2019). Put differently, Blasey Ford's Whiteness and how this influenced her perceived credibility is not thematized in the discussion of her allegations. Furthermore, Blasey Ford was repeatedly referred to as “Dr. Blasey” (Opinion Letters, 2018b) or “Dr. Ford” (Editorial Board, 2018c). Likewise, when her background as a “psychology professor” (Goldberg, 2018b) is highlighted, it not only bolsters her credibility as a witness aware of the effects of trauma on memory but also emphasizes her class. In this sense, the framing focused on the allegations’ credibility implicitly relies on Blasey Ford's status as a White professor. This status represents both an important marker for race and class.

Nonetheless, the NYT opinion section widely reproduced the idea that what happened to Blasey Ford is, far from being an anomaly, the norm in a patriarchal society that fosters gender discrimination and sexual violence. Women are afraid to denounce these acts precisely because of how entitled men react, here illustrated by Kavanaugh and his supporters. Partisanship frames are employed insofar as Republicans are presented as protectors of a sexual abuser (“Sexual abuse isn't partisan,” Editorial Board, 2018a). The wide presence of the credibility and #metoo frames is not uncontested. Most notably, NYT columnist Bret Stephens used the political opportunity to critique the #MeToo movement. He not only questioned the credibility of Blasey Ford but the movement itself. Stephens maintained the ensuing:

If the price of this revolution is the subordination of ordinary fairness to abstract justice, the elevation of rumor over fact, the further debasement of journalism, the devaluation of the rights of the accused, and the complete toxification of public service, it will be a price too high. (Stephens, 2018)

However, the anti-MeToo discourse in the context of the Blasey Ford allegations remains marginal. Reflective thereof is the editorial board's piece, which captures the predominant theme of the discourse through the title “Women Are Watching” (Editorial Board, 2018d). In this way, before and after the hearing, the discourse overwhelmingly reproduced the credibility frame in an essentially positive way toward Blasey Ford. In addition, her credibility was connected to the #metoo frame and its sexist system subframe.

“Weakening the Voices of Future Survivors is Worth it”: Reade's Accusations Hurt Biden's Chances Against Trump

In contrast to the previous case, the discourse around Reade's sexual assault allegations against Biden takes on a different form. Figure 2 shows that the horse race and credibility frames (deployed negatively toward Reade) dominate. Especially the horse race frame pervades the NYT opinion section and primarily portrays Reade as a nuisance to Biden's presidential bid. Furthermore, the partisanship frame is much more present, especially in the peak coverage period. Along these lines, Reade's allegations are mostly framed as a political weapon mobilized by Republicans. In some cases, this is linked to the #MeTooFar subframe, by discrediting Reade's allegations and cautioning against the #MeToo movement as a whole.

Distribution of Dominant Narratives by Reporting Days in the Reade/Biden Case.

For instance, Goldberg (2020) argued that Reade's allegations are merely “used to troll” the #MeToo movement, and goes on to say

[i]t would be easier to know what to do with Reade's accusation that Biden sexually assaulted her if her tale were more solid, or if it were less. […] Reade seems almost engineered in a lab to inspire skepticism in mainstream Democrats, both because her story keeps changing and because of her bizarre public worship of President Vladimir Putin of Russia.

While it may be reasonable to consider partisanship as a motivation for false accusations, the choice to foreground such a frame from the start is problematic. As opposed to the benevolent first text published on Blasey Ford, Reade's allegations are framed as a mere “tale” from the start. In addition to that, a similar frame continues along the lines of #MeTooFar, as NYT opinion columnist Maureen Dowd (2020) made the following argument:

I could not have been more thrilled when #MeToo ripped away the curtain on the murky transgressions and diminishments that women had endured in the droit du seigneur era. But as with any revolution, there was some over-correction.

Despite the negative rhetoric toward Reade by NYT columnists, the editorial board (2020) called for an investigation because “Americans deserve to know more about a sexual assault accusation against the likely Democratic Party nominee.” This is the only instance where the procedural horse race frame appears. It represents a framing that takes the allegations seriously and poses the question of how to evaluate the case in the best possible way. There were other exceptions where the horse race frame was used to argue against Biden's political ambitions. One such example is NYT columnist Elizabeth Bruenig (2020), who called on the Democratic Party to consider a “Plan B.” She takes Reade's allegations to be credible, and argues:

To preserve the strides made on behalf of victims of sexual assault in the era of #MeToo, and to maximize their chances in November, Democrats need to begin formulating an alternative strategy for 2020—one that does not include Mr. Biden.

However, neither this sympathetic attitude toward Reade's allegations nor the #metoo frame gains relevance in the discourse from this point onward. While Reade's working-class background is frequently mentioned, it is not connected to the challenges she, or women like her, might face in speaking up against sexual violence. Furthermore, the framing emphasizes a sharp distinction between Hill and Blasey Ford as “poised, professional women” (Dowd, 2020) on one hand, and Reade on the other. This distinction in social status is used by the columnists to frame Reade as being unreliable, and hence misses the mark on any sort of feminist discourse, let alone an intersectional one. For instance, they repeatedly suggest that there may be hidden motivations, such as (Republican) partisanship, that drive these allegations. Not only that, but in a letter to the editor this framing goes as far as to speculate about the clothes that Reade might have been wearing at the time to argue that the allegations could not be true:

In 1993, a congressional staffer most likely would have been wearing something like a knee length tailored skirt and pantyhose. It would be extremely difficult for someone to reach below the tight skirt, then bring his hand up to the waist and then down into the pantyhose. The fact that this supposedly occurred in a public corridor makes the story even more unlikely. (Opinion Letters, 2020b)

In this way, Reade's allegation does not move beyond her concrete case. While we witnessed a systemic approach in the Blasey Ford case, Reade is treated as an isolated case and scarcely related to broader societal dynamics. As such, the discourse in the NYT opinion section was narrowed down to the specific details of the case and the political consequences for the man in question.

In other readers’ letters, the framing also targets Reade as a threat to Biden's presidential bid, rather than expressing the kind of support Blasey Ford had received. Reacting to the NYT editorial board calling for an investigation, the NYT opinion section published two readers’ letters that argued the following:

can you handle being a hypocrite if it means getting Mr. Trump out of the White House? I say, yes, I am happy to be a hypocrite. Please join me. (Opinion Letters, 2020a)

I don’t want an investigation. I want a coronation of Joe Biden. […] I don’t want justice, whatever that may be. I want a win, the removal of Donald Trump from office, and Mr. Biden is our best chance. (Opinion Letters, 2020b)

While the political horse race was important from the beginning, now Biden's campaign begins to take center stage. There are no more calls for a “Plan B” (Bruenig, 2020) to win the horse race, but all bets are on Biden's campaign. As such, Reade's allegations are being viewed through the lens of endangering Biden's success. Moreover, the candidate credibility frame is absent, as there is little scrutiny over Biden's response to the allegations. As a result, Reade is depicted as a political pawn, not a woman who was possibly sexually assaulted by her superior at the time, a powerful U.S. Senator.

However, the possibility of Reade's credibility is acknowledged in passing, as an investigation is presented as a “risk” for Biden's candidacy, thus voicing the sentiment that the allegations could be true. This discourse continued to thrive with NYT opinion columnist Linda Hirshman (2020), who wrote a piece entitled “I Believe Tara Reade. I’m Voting for Joe Biden Anyway.” There, she argued that Democrats should

Suck it up and make the utilitarian bargain […]. Compared with the good Mr. Biden can do, the cost of dismissing Tara Reade—and, worse, weakening the voices of future survivors—is worth it.

Notably, at this point in the discourse, the question of Reade's credibility does not matter anymore—the framing has shifted the arena in its entirety. Reade is completely dismissed (or silenced) in the face of Biden's possible success against Trump. While at the beginning the opinion section writers scrutinized the viability of the allegations, the horse race and partisanship frames completely dominate at this point. Consequently, the framing of Reade's allegations culminates in providing the following answer to the title of the first opinion piece published, “What to Do With Tara Reade's Allegation Against Joe Biden?” (Goldberg, 2020): they should be ignored, no matter their merit or the negative impact on the feminist movement as a whole.

Discussion: The Promises and Pitfalls of the #MeToo Movement Over Time

Despite the similarities of the two cases, the analysis shows a substantial discrepancy between the framing of the two sexual assault allegations. Nonetheless, there are also overlaps in the coverage. This is particularly the case with respect to the horse race frame: in both cases, the political race in which Kavanaugh and Biden were engaged remains important. However, in the case of Blasey Ford and Kavanaugh, the scrutiny is not only put on Blasey Ford's story but also on Kavanaugh and the sexist system. In contrast, in Reade's case, the focus shifts toward scrutinizing the accuser rather than the accused or the systematic power asymmetries involved. There is little to no scrutiny of Biden's credibility, and all the evaluative weight is placed on Reade. As such, she is characterized as a partisan accuser with little credibility. Moreover, there are only four references to the #metoo frame in the articles related to Reade, and all of them are employed in terms of #MeTooFar—hence actually critical of the #MeToo movement.

The framing of Blasey Ford's allegations is exemplary of the #MeToo discourse in both senses of the word: it illustrates the promises of the movement and how a concrete case is used to open an empowering window for many other women to tell their stories. Still, the framing is also exemplary of #MeToo's limitations, as it doesn't extend to other forms of discrimination and remains focused on Blasey Ford, without questioning how her position as a relatively privileged White woman influenced her ability to be heard. In other words, the lack of an intersectional perspective is a missed opportunity to connect Ford's experience to the broader power relations in which sexualized violence takes place, such as its racialized nature. After all, “me too” was about Black women initially. As the Blasey Ford/Kavanaugh case is likely to remain a social and political reference point (Blumell & Mulupi, 2022), building a nuanced understanding of the corresponding communicative practices is essential. Hence, our critical reading of the framing joins the existing scholarship that follows this endeavor.

Our findings are in line with Boling and Moscowitz (2021), who underscore the empowering nature of the discourse around Blasey Ford, wherein many other survivors were heard, but also criticize that merely 11% of the total Op-Eds in the NYT, The Washington Post, and The Wall Street Journal mention race. Race as a social marker is often only made explicit in relation to People of Color, which strengthens the conception of Whiteness as the “norm” (Morris, 2016). As such, these joint findings further support previous research, which concluded that the NYT practiced White feminism in their coverage of #MeToo (Evans, 2018). Moreover, AbiNader et al. (2021) argue that the political horserace still dominated newspaper headlines in the case of Blasey Ford. This echoes Cuklanz's criticism that much of the #MeToo discourse remains attached to the accused men and doesn't provide a “clear picture of the [potential] victim's point of view” (2020, p. 268). While our analysis confirms that the horserace frame remained important, the attention given to sexual violence concerning a sexist system in relation to Blasey Ford's case is nonetheless remarkable.

Furthermore, the Blasey Ford/Kavanaugh case contrasts with the framing of Reade's allegations against Biden. Here, the discourse is all about the prospects of Biden's candidacy and doesn’t even take on the empowering side of #MeToo. Instead, the NYT opinion section dismisses Reade's credibility by using frames that do not take the possibility of sexual violence seriously, suggest ulterior motives for her allegations, and normalize purposely ignoring them altogether. In consequence, the NYT finds itself dangerously close to how political commentators at Fox News used the “sexual violence as a political tactic” frame to discredit Blasey Ford's allegations (Pollino, 2020, p. 78). Moreover, Wright et al. (2022) argue that whereas #MeToo had been a nonpartisan issue before, it was throughout the Blasey Ford/Kavanaugh hearings that it changed to a question of partisanship. Democratic members of the U.S. Congress now framed the allegations along the #metoo and accuser credibility frames, while Republicans framed them as partisan and thus politically motivated. Zooming out to look at the discourse beyond the framing of the NYT in the opinion sections shows that Blasey Ford's allegations were framed along partisan lines, even though partisanship itself isn't a prevalent frame. It just so happened that the Democratic partisan framing coincided with an empowering discourse.

In the case of Reade/Biden, however, the tables turn. As such, the framing of Reade's allegations against Biden casts a shadow of doubt back on empowering discourse around Blasey Ford. Seen in this light, one has to wonder whether the framing of Blasey Ford was already more motivated by partisanship than the #MeToo movement itself. If this is the case, then the opinion coverage of the two cases in the NYT is not conflicting at all, but coherent along partisan lines. Contrary to the stance of the editorial board (2018a) then, sexual abuse (allegations) are very much partisan. In other words, the foundation for the empowering discourse on Blasey Ford was built on sand, not rock. Its ensuing disintegration in Reade/Biden's case is the logical consequence. Our analysis suggests that this problematic framing reflects the conditional and instrumental nature of media support for survivors of sexual violence.

Notwithstanding this, the fact that the NYT was not just campaigning against any Republican candidate, but against Trump is relevant context to consider. There is no question that it was correct to argue that women's rights were better protected in a Biden presidency, as opposed to a second Trump administration. We do not aim to put this in question. Instead, what we do put up for discussion is whether it follows that the political ends legitimize the discursive means to get there. It is legitimately questionable whether the NYT was right in making the “utilitarian bargain” in supporting Biden and discrediting Reade at the cost of “weakening the voices of future survivors” (Hirshman, 2020). It is one thing not to believe Reade's allegations; it is another to suggest that her allegations are motivated by sympathies to Vladimir Putin or to speculate about the clothes she might have been wearing at the time, as if that was relevant. Dismissing Reade on these terms reanimates sexist scripts that cast doubts toward survivors of sexual violence (see, e.g., Banet-Weiser & Claire Higgins, 2023). To be sure, we are not saying that the NYT should have reproduced Reade's allegations without scrutiny. What we are saying is that the particular frames that the NYT put forward in its opinion section to discredit and devalue Reade vis-à-vis Biden are highly problematic—irrespective of the truthfulness of the allegations.

What are the implications of these findings for the evolution of the #MeToo discourse? Blasey Ford's allegations took place not long after #MeToo had gone viral and thus at a point where the movement significantly shaped the public discussion of sexual violence and gendered power abuse. The situation is different when Reade enters the public space, as #MeToo was much less salient in 2020 than in 2018. The fact that Reade's allegations were received in the public sphere with such a sharp contrast to Blasey Ford's indicates a diminished social power of the #MeToo movement. That is so because it wasn't able to set the discursive agenda in the same way it did before. It seems reasonable to suggest that the NYT opinion section would not have put for ward the same sexist frames in 2018. Given how instrumental it was in bringing #MeToo to the mainstream, it is worrisome for the state of the #MeToo movement that it was the NYT that presented Reade's allegations against Biden in this manner. Unfortunately, rather than empowering survivors of sexual violence, the discourse gave way to partisan motives that superseded the idea of #MeToo. This, in turn, reinforced existing oppressive structures rather than fostering feminist voices. So while the presentation of diverse opinions in the NYT opinion section is desirable, not all opinions are equal. There are lines to be drawn, and where these lines are to be drawn is constantly negotiated. To put it simply: “[a]nything can be an Op-Ed” (Hall, 2013), but not everything should be. Our modest hope is that, if nothing else, this article at least convinces the reader of the need to scrutinize the discourses around ambiguous and uncomfortable sexual violence allegations. Even if, or perhaps especially when, they are politically inconvenient.
