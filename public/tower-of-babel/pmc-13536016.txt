Factors associated with the effectiveness and safety of Janus kinase inhibitors in rheumatoid arthritis: an 8-year observational study from the JAGUAR Registry
Authors: Cristina Martinez-Molina, Beatriz Frade-Sosa, Cesar Diaz-Torné, Raimon Sanmarti, Hèctor Corominas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo identify factors associated with the effectiveness and safety of Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitors in patients with rheumatoid arthritis (RA), as reflected by treatment retention.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study used data from the JAGUAR Registry, a multicentre RA registry of JAK inhibitor treatments. At 1 and 4 years, reasons and rates of treatment discontinuation were assessed. Treatment retention was evaluated using Cox regression and Kaplan-Meier curves. Factors associated with treatment retention were analysed for the discontinuation reasons of lack of effectiveness and adverse drug reactions (ADRs).ResultsA total of 435 treatment episodes with JAK inhibitors (tofacitinib: 118, baricitinib: 181, upadacitinib: 80, filgotinib: 56) were included. At 1 year, discontinuation rates were: 23.0% due to lack of effectiveness, 17.0% due to ADRs and 2.3% due to other reasons. At 4 years, the corresponding discontinuation rates were: 32.9%, 19.5% and 5.5%, respectively. Chronic concomitant glucocorticoid (GC) treatment was associated with JAK inhibitor discontinuation due to lack of effectiveness (p=0.007). Discontinuation rates due to lack of effectiveness were higher in patients requiring GCs for >6 months versus ≤6 months (29.1% vs 18.8% at 1 year; 50.5% vs 32.5% at 4 years). Age was associated with JAK inhibitor discontinuation due to ADRs (p=0.003). Discontinuation rates due to ADRs were higher in patients aged ≥65 years versus <65 years (25.6% vs 14.5% at 1 year; 35.3% vs 17.4% at 4 years).ConclusionsIn RA, chronic concomitant GC treatment and patient age are associated with JAK inhibitor discontinuation due to lack of effectiveness and ADRs, respectively.

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic immune-mediated inflammatory disease characterised by persistent synovitis, progressive joint damage and a substantial impact on physical function and quality of life.1 Despite the availability of conventional synthetic disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (csDMARDs) and biologic DMARDs (bDMARDs), treatment failure remains common in clinical practice, as a substantial proportion of patients fail to achieve sustained remission or low disease activity or discontinue treatment due to safety concerns.

Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitors2–5 represent the most recent therapeutic class incorporated into the treatment armamentarium for RA, providing an oral targeted option for patients with an inadequate response to csDMARDs and in many cases bDMARDs.6 Although their efficacy has been consistently demonstrated in randomised controlled trials (RCTs),7–14 evaluating their treatment response in routine clinical practice remains challenging.15 16 Compared with trial populations, patients treated in real-world settings are typically older, have a higher comorbidity burden and are more heavily pretreated, factors that may influence both the effectiveness and safety of JAK inhibitors.17 18

In this context, treatment retention has emerged as a pragmatic composite outcome reflecting the balance between treatment effectiveness and safety in real-world practice. Observational studies and national registries have reported JAK inhibitor treatment retention in RA and suggested associations with factors such as age, prior bDMARD exposure and comorbidities. However, findings across cohorts are heterogeneous, follow-up is often limited and few studies have examined treatment discontinuation separately according to lack of effectiveness and adverse drug reactions (ADRs).19–21

Therefore, the aim of the present study was to identify factors associated with the effectiveness and safety of JAK inhibitors in RA, using treatment retention as a composite outcome in a large, multicentre real-world registry. By analysing treatment discontinuation separately due to lack of effectiveness and ADRs, we sought to generate clinically relevant evidence to support treatment selection and risk stratification in routine clinical practice.

Methods

Study design and data source

This retrospective cohort study used data from the JAGUAR Registry, including treatment episodes for RA with tofacitinib, baricitinib, upadacitinib and filgotinib between September 2017 and January 2026. Only treatment episodes with available follow-up data during the study period were included.

The JAGUAR Registry is a multicentre database in Spain that enrols patients with RA who fulfil the 2010 American College of Rheumatology/European League Against Rheumatism (EULAR) classification criteria22 and are treated with JAK inhibitors in routine clinical practice, at the physician’s discretion, in accordance with the EULAR recommendations.6 The purpose of this registry is to evaluate the effectiveness and safety of these treatments using real-world data prospectively collected since 2017, when the first JAK inhibitor was approved in Spain. Given the treatment-centred design of the registry, each course of treatment was considered an independent treatment episode. Consequently, patients who received more than one JAK inhibitor could contribute multiple entries, each corresponding to a distinct JAK inhibitor exposure. Data were collected at JAK inhibitor treatment initiation, at 3 months, at 6 months and every 6 months thereafter during outpatient visits.

Assessments

Treatment retention was measured as the time from treatment initiation to discontinuation. Treatment discontinuation reasons were categorised as: (1) lack of effectiveness (including primary and secondary non-response), (2) ADRs and (3) other reasons. Primary non-response was defined as the absence of a clinical response within the first 6 months of treatment, whereas secondary non-response was defined as the loss of response after an initial period of effectiveness.23 ADRs associated with JAK inhibitors were identified using the Naranjo ADR Probability Scale.24 The frequency of ADRs associated with JAK inhibitor treatment discontinuation was categorised according to standard regulatory definitions as follows: very common (≥1/10), common (≥1/100 to <1/10), uncommon (≥1/1000 to <1/100), rare (≥1/10 000 to <1/1000) and very rare (<1/10 000).

The potential factors associated with JAK inhibitor retention that were analysed included: patient age, female sex, RA disease duration, seropositivity (rheumatoid factor (RF), anti-cyclic citrullinated peptides (anti-CCP)), type of JAK inhibitor (non-selective JAK inhibitors: tofacitinib and baricitinib; JAK1-selective inhibitors: upadacitinib and filgotinib), Clinical Disease Activity Index (CDAI) at initiation of JAK inhibitor treatment, number of prior RA treatments (csDMARDs, bDMARDs, JAK inhibitors), type of prior bDMARDs (tumour necrosis factor (TNF) inhibitor, interleukin (IL) 6 inhibitor, abatacept, rituximab, anakinra), use of concomitant RA treatments (csDMARDs, glucocorticoids (GCs)), presence of comorbidity and polypharmacy (five or more medications). Patient age was categorised into two groups: <65 years and 65 years or older, reflecting the higher prevalence of RA in adults of advanced age.25 RA disease activity was assessed using the CDAI, which is recommended to minimise bias in effectiveness studies of therapies that markedly influence acute-phase reactants, such as JAK inhibitors.26 Chronic GC treatment was defined as GC use for >6 months, as described by EULAR,27 and was assessed after initiation of JAK inhibitor treatment.

Statistical analyses

Ordinal and continuous variables are presented as medians and IQRs, and categorical variables as counts (n) and percentages (%).

Characteristics across JAK inhibitor groups were compared using the Kruskal-Wallis test (for ordinal or continuous variables) and the Fisher’s exact test (for categorical variables).

Treatment retention was evaluated using Cox proportional hazards regression models with robust variance estimates clustered by patient. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were also used to assess treatment retention. Bivariate and multivariable Cox regression analyses were performed to identify factors associated with JAK inhibitor treatment discontinuation. Discontinuations due to (1) lack of effectiveness and (2) ADRs were analysed as separate endpoints, while other causes of discontinuation were treated as censored observations. Candidate covariates were prespecified based on clinical relevance and evidence from the literature. These covariates were then evaluated in bivariate Cox regression analyses, and those with a p value <0.100 were included in the multivariable model. For categorical covariates, the number of treatment episodes, discontinuation events, total person-years of follow-up and incidence rates (per 100 person-years) were calculated. Kaplan-Meier survival curves for JAK inhibitor retention were examined according to factors associated with each discontinuation reason, and comparisons were performed using the log-rank test. Treatment discontinuation reasons and rates were assessed at 1 and 4 years of JAK inhibitor treatment, reflecting early and long-term treatment retention within the 8-year observational follow-up. The 4-year time point was selected to assess long-term retention while ensuring an adequate sample size for analysis.

All variables included in the study were complete; therefore, no imputation procedures were required. All statistical analyses were conducted using Stata V.12 (StataCorp, College Station, Texas, USA). Statistical significance was set at p value <0.050.

Results

Study population and JAK inhibitor treatments

The study included 435 JAK inhibitor treatment episodes from the JAGUAR Registry, corresponding to 298 patients with RA. These comprised 118 tofacitinib, 181 baricitinib, 80 upadacitinib and 56 filgotinib treatments. Baseline characteristics of the 298 patients are presented in table 1, while characteristics of the 435 JAK inhibitor treatment episodes are shown in table 2. No differences were observed among JAK inhibitor groups in terms of CDAI at baseline, concomitant RA treatment (csDMARDs and GCs) or prior use of csDMARDs. Among the four JAK inhibitor groups, prior use of bDMARDs (particularly TNF inhibitors) and prior use of JAK inhibitors were higher in the upadacitinib and filgotinib groups. The use of antihypertensives and lipid-lowering agents was higher in the tofacitinib and baricitinib groups.

Treatment discontinuation: reasons and rates

At 1 year of JAK inhibitor treatment, discontinuation rates were: 23.0% due to lack of effectiveness, 17.0% due to ADRs and 2.3% due to other reasons. At 4 years, the corresponding discontinuation rates were: 32.9%, 19.5% and 5.5%, respectively.

ADRs associated with treatment discontinuation

Abdominal pain (4.6%) was the most frequent ADR associated with JAK inhibitor treatment discontinuation, followed by headache (3.9%), nausea and vomiting (3.2%) and fatigue (3.0%). Diarrhoea (2.1%), upper respiratory tract infection (1.8%), urinary tract infection (1.8%), herpes infection (1.6%), rash (1.4%), hypertension (1.4%) and alopecia (1.1%) were also common. Less frequent events were classified as uncommon and included paresthesia (0.9%), neutropaenia (0.7%), peripheral oedema (0.7%), cellulitis (0.5%), diverticulitis (0.5%), acne (0.5%), anaemia (0.5%), dyspnoea (0.5%), weight gain (0.5%) and leucopenia (0.2%).

Factors associated with treatment discontinuation due to lack of effectiveness

Regarding JAK inhibitor discontinuation due to lack of effectiveness (table 3), bivariate Cox regression analyses showed that a greater number of previous bDMARDs and JAK inhibitors, prior use of TNF and IL-6 inhibitors, chronic concomitant GC treatment and higher baseline disease activity were potentially associated with an increased risk of treatment discontinuation, whereas patient age was potentially associated with a lower risk. In multivariable Cox regression analysis, chronic concomitant GC treatment was identified as a factor associated with JAK inhibitor discontinuation due to lack of effectiveness (adjusted HR (aHR): 1.69; p=0.007; table 3).

Factors associated with treatment discontinuation due to ADRs

With respect to JAK inhibitor discontinuation due to ADRs (table 4), bivariate Cox regression analyses showed that patient age at initiation of treatment, a greater number of previous csDMARDs and bDMARDs, prior use of rituximab and abatacept, and polypharmacy were potentially associated with an increased risk of treatment discontinuation. In multivariable Cox regression analysis, patient age at JAK inhibitor initiation was identified as a factor associated with treatment discontinuation due to ADRs (aHR: 1.03; p=0.003; table 4).

Treatment retention according to GC use

For concomitant GC use, significant differences were observed in JAK inhibitor discontinuation due to lack of effectiveness. Patients receiving GCs for >6 months (chronic concomitant GC treatment) showed lower JAK inhibitor retention rates than those requiring GCs for 6 months or less (p=0.005; figure 1a). Discontinuation rates due to lack of effectiveness among patients requiring GCs for >6 months were 29.1% and 50.5% at 1 and 4 years of JAK inhibitor treatment, respectively, compared with 18.8% and 32.5% in those receiving GCs for 6 months or less (figure 1a). In contrast, no differences were observed in discontinuation rates due to ADRs according to GC use (p=0.424; figure 1b).

JAK inhibitor retention by concomitant GC use. JAK inhibitor retention in patients requiring GCs for 6 months or less versus chronic concomitant GC treatment (GC >6 months), considering treatment discontinuation due to (a) lack of effectiveness and (b) ADRs.

Treatment retention according to patient age

In terms of patient age at treatment initiation, no differences were observed in JAK inhibitor discontinuation rates due to lack of effectiveness between patients aged <65 years and those aged 65 years or older (p=0.107; figure 2a). Conversely, significant differences were observed in treatment discontinuation due to ADRs: patients aged 65 years or older showed lower JAK inhibitor retention rates than those aged <65 years (p<0.001; figure 2b). Treatment discontinuation rates due to ADRs in patients aged 65 years or older were 25.6% and 35.3% at 1 and 4 years of JAK inhibitor treatment, respectively, compared with 14.5% and 17.4% in those aged <65 years (figure 2b).

Discussion

This study identified factors associated with JAK inhibitor effectiveness and safety in patients with RA by evaluating treatment retention in a large, multicentre real-world registry. Few studies in the literature have specifically examined the reasons for discontinuing JAK inhibitor treatment in RA patients.19–21 Identifying factors associated with different clinical responses may anticipate treatment discontinuation, predict therapeutic responses and personalise clinical follow-up.6

Regarding concomitant GC use, prior observational studies suggest that chronic concomitant GC treatment is associated with refractory disease28 29 and an increased risk of discontinuation of JAK inhibitors and bDMARDs.30 Similarly, in our cohort, chronic concomitant GC treatment was identified as an independent factor associated with JAK inhibitor discontinuation due to lack of effectiveness, whereas it was not associated with an increased risk of discontinuation due to ADRs. These findings support the interpretation that chronic concomitant GC treatment may reflect more severe or difficult-to-control disease rather than directly influencing the effectiveness of these small molecules. In this context, the need for sustained GC treatment may serve as a clinical marker identifying patients at higher risk of JAK inhibitor failure due to lack of effectiveness.

Patient age was another factor associated with treatment discontinuation of tofacitinib, baricitinib, upadacitinib and filgotinib in our study. Regulatory agencies such as the European Medicines Agency31 and international recommendations such as EULAR6 advise careful consideration when prescribing JAK inhibitors to patients aged 65 years or older, largely following the safety signals reported in the ORAL Surveillance trial.32 Results from subsequent observational studies and registry analyses have shown heterogeneous findings regarding the magnitude of these risks in routine clinical practice.33–36 These discrepancies highlight the need for additional data from RCTs and real-world cohorts to clarify the impact of individual risk factors during treatment with specific JAK inhibitors. In our cohort, older patients had a higher probability of discontinuing treatment due to ADRs, whereas age was not associated with discontinuation due to lack of effectiveness. These findings are consistent with previous studies reporting comparable effectiveness of JAK inhibitors across age groups19 21 37–39 but a higher probability of treatment discontinuation due to safety concerns in older patients.19 21

Baseline disease activity has also been proposed as a factor associated with JAK inhibitor treatment response in RA. Higher CDAI at JAK inhibitor initiation has been associated with a lower probability of achieving remission or low disease activity at 6 months of treatment.40 41 However, CDAI at baseline was not independently associated with JAK inhibitor discontinuation due to lack of effectiveness21 41 or ADRs,21 which is consistent with our cohort results.

Another factor to consider is the presence of comorbidities. Although comorbidity burden was highly prevalent in our cohort, it was not associated with JAK inhibitor discontinuation. This finding may reflect the careful selection and monitoring of patients receiving targeted therapies in specialised rheumatology centres, where comorbid conditions are actively considered when initiating treatment.

In reference to polypharmacy, it is highly prevalent among patients with chronic inflammatory diseases42 and particularly common among older individuals with RA.42 43 It has been associated with increased risk of drug–drug interactions, non-adherence, morbidity, hospitalisations, mortality and healthcare costs.42 43 Nevertheless, polypharmacy was not identified as a factor associated with JAK inhibitor discontinuation in our study. This observation may reflect the role of multidisciplinary care, including hospital pharmacy services, in optimising medication safety, treatment adherence and monitoring potential interactions in routine clinical practice.

In terms of sex, the available evidence is limited; however, current studies suggest no significant differences in JAK inhibitor retention between female and male patients,20 21 nor in terms of treatment effectiveness21 41 44 or safety.17 21 Similarly, in our present study, female sex was not associated with an increased risk of JAK inhibitor discontinuation due to lack of effectiveness or ADRs.

Previous treatment exposure is another factor frequently associated with treatment retention in RA. Patients with a higher number of prior bDMARDs often represent a population with more refractory disease. In our analysis, although prior biologic exposure and previous use of specific mechanisms of action were associated with treatment discontinuation in bivariate analyses, these associations were not maintained in the multivariable model. This finding, consistent with previous evidence,20 21 suggests that treatment history may primarily reflect the underlying severity and refractoriness of the disease rather than directly determining JAK inhibitor retention.

Similarly, previous exposure to JAK inhibitors was not independently associated with treatment discontinuation in our cohort. Recent registry studies have suggested that cycling between JAK inhibitors may still provide clinical benefit in selected patients, and treatment response appears to be independent of the reason for discontinuation of the previous JAK inhibitor.20 45 46

With regard to csDMARDs, neither previous exposure nor concomitant csDMARD treatment was associated with JAK inhibitor discontinuation in our study. This observation is consistent with the increasing use of JAK inhibitors as effective monotherapy options in clinical practice, although combination therapy with methotrexate may still be considered in selected patients.6

Serological status, including RF and anti-CCP positivity, has traditionally been associated with disease severity in RA. However, in our study, seropositivity was not associated with JAK inhibitor discontinuation due to lack of effectiveness or ADRs. These findings are consistent with previous real-world studies and RCTs suggesting that the effectiveness and safety of JAK inhibitors may be largely independent of autoantibody status.20 21 41 47

Concerning disease duration, it was neither associated with JAK inhibitor discontinuation in the results of our cohort nor in previously published evidence.19–21 While shorter disease duration has been linked to improved treatment responses in early RA, JAK inhibitors are frequently used in patients with long-standing disease and multiple previous treatment exposures,6 where other factors such as disease refractoriness may play a more relevant role.

Overall, treatment retention of JAK inhibitors in our cohort was moderate over long-term follow-up. Drug retention in real-world studies reflects a complex balance between treatment effectiveness, safety, patient preferences and clinical decision-making. Importantly, analysing the specific reasons for treatment discontinuation provides additional insights beyond overall treatment retention and may help better characterise different patient profiles associated with treatment outcomes.

From a clinical perspective, the identification of factors associated with specific causes of treatment discontinuation may help guide treatment decisions and patient monitoring strategies. Chronic concomitant GC treatment may identify patients with more refractory disease who may require closer monitoring or alternative therapeutic approaches, whereas older age may signal a higher risk of treatment discontinuation related to safety concerns.

This study has several limitations. First, as a registry-based observational and retrospective study, the findings should be interpreted within the inherent limitations of this design, reflecting associations in a real-world cohort with limited generalisability and no causal inference; therefore, the results should be considered hypothesis-generating. Second, treatment allocation was not randomised and may have been influenced by clinical characteristics not fully captured in the registry. Third, treatment patterns may have evolved over time as new JAK inhibitors became available during the study period. Fourth, although lack of effectiveness and ADRs were analysed using cause-specific Cox models, alternative competing risks approaches may provide complementary information by addressing different but complementary analytical objectives. Finally, differences in the number of treatments across tofacitinib, baricitinib, upadacitinib and filgotinib, due to their staggered introduction into clinical practice, precluded analyses according to the specific JAK inhibitor.

Despite these limitations, the study also has important strengths. It is based on a large multicentre real-world registry with long-term follow-up, allowing the evaluation of treatment retention across different JAK inhibitors used in routine clinical practice. In addition, analysing discontinuation according to specific reasons provides clinically relevant insights into the determinants of treatment outcomes.

Conclusions

In patients with RA treated with JAK inhibitors, chronic GC requirement during JAK inhibitor treatment is associated with an increased risk of discontinuation due to lack of effectiveness, whereas patient age is associated with a higher risk of discontinuation due to ADRs. These findings highlight the importance of considering patient characteristics when selecting and monitoring JAK inhibitor treatment in routine clinical practice.
