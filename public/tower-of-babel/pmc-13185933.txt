Quantitative mapping of geological samples by electron microprobe using mean atomic number (MAN) background corrections: method application, data processing and visualization
Authors: Clémentine Hamelin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractElectron probe microanalysis (EPMA) and wavelength dispersive spectroscopy (WDS) are ubiquitous tools for chemical analysis of geological samples. Analysis type is often confined to quantitative but spatially-limited (∼0.5-2+ µm diameter) spot analysis, or qualitative but spatially-resolved mapping. This contribution outlines a method that combines the quantitative aspect of spot analyses with the spatial resolution of EPMA mapping and associated data processing and visualization workflows. The acquisition and processing of spatially-resolved, quantitative chemical maps of geological samples allows for systematic characterization of mineral composition and compositional variations across rock textures, determination of local or effective bulk rock compositions, and further application of texturally-informed thermobarometric calculations.•Application of the mean atomic number (MAN) background-correction routine for EPMA mapping yields efficient and accurate quantitative chemical maps of geological samples.•Data filtering workflows based on mineralogical identification and stoichiometry constraints yield meaningful mineral composition data.•Free and open-source geographic information systems (GIS) software provides a tool for data-visualization strategies to explore the multi-dimensionality of quantitative EPMA map data.Keywords: Electron probe microanalysis (EPMA), Mean atomic background (MAN) background, Metamorphic petrology, Quantitative mapping

Background

Electron probe microanalysis (EPMA) is a ubiquitous petrological tool to characterize the chemical composition (major and trace elements) of minerals in rocks. This method relies on the excitation of a sample by a beam of high-energy electrons, and detection of X-ray intensities for elements of interest via wavelength dispersive spectrometry (WDS). EPMA analyses are typically carried out either as spot analyses or mapping along a grid of points. Spot analyses are limited to small analytical volumes (∼<5 µm3) but yield quantitative chemical data through analytical standardization and matrix correction routines. Mapping provides spatially resolved elemental distribution data across mapped phases, but its simplest and most common application (i.e., mapping of X-ray intensities) is qualitative in nature as the conversion of X-ray intensities to elemental abundances (e.g., wt.% or ppm) is non-trivial due to matrix effects of the different phases.

The primary hurdles of achieving quantitative maps are twofold: (1) achieving reasonable analytical mapping durations while reaching high-enough X-ray throughput for reliable counting statistics, and (2) quantifying background intensities (continuum X-rays) – with the latter being much more challenging to address. Fully transforming map pixels into quantitative analyses requires quantifying background continuum X-rays. For spot analyses, this is typically done through 2- or multi-point background measurements and interpolation, standardization, and matrix correction (ZAF or phi-rho-z, abbreviated herein as Phi(ρz)) routines. Measuring 2-point background intensities for each pixel of a mapped region containing several hundred thousand to millions of pixels is unrealistic and would lead to untenable analytical time budgets, among other issues outlined in Donovan et al. [2]. Data processing routines that attempt to convert raw X-ray intensity maps to quantitative maps using a calibration-curve approach have been developed (e.g., [3,4]) but are not based on the current understanding of theoretical X-ray physics [2]. A more rigorous approach to background corrections is to use Mean Atomic Number (MAN, or Z¯) background corrections, which involves measuring the X-ray peak intensity of a given element in materials that do not contain the element of interest to determine true background X-ray intensities for that material’s given Z¯. By measuring a selection of reference materials spanning the range of Z¯ of unknown materials, we can produce Z¯ vs. background intensity calibration curves that can be applied to unknown analyses for background corrections. This MAN background-correction routine is carried out for all relevant elements and used for quantification, instead of measuring continuum X-ray intensities on either side of the X-ray peak of interest. This approach lends itself to mapping because background values can be determined for all analyzed elements at each pixel location and subtracted from measured on-peak intensities. MAN background-corrected intensity values can then be quantified through the usual standardization and matrix correction routines.

This approach is particularly valuable for petrological and material science applications, where spatially-resolved quantitative analyses are used to assess mineral composition data quality, discard mixed analyses captured at grain boundaries, and systematically evaluate variations in material and phase compositions across sample textural domains. For texturally complex and often heterogeneous or chemically zoned phases in natural rocks, accessing the systematic chemical variability of mineral phases is critical to the interpretation of the larger, tectonic-scale significance of these chemical differences.

Method details

1. Instrument and Analytical Settings

Analyses described in this contribution were performed on a JEOL JXA-8530FPlus electron microprobe at the University of Minnesota – Twin Cities, in the department of Earth and Environmental Sciences. This instrument is equipped with four (4) tunable WDS detectors each housing 2-4 diffracting crystals. Samples under investigation in this study are eclogites, high-pressure metamorphic rocks composed primarily of pyroxene, feldspar, garnet, and amphiboles. Elements Si, Ti, Al, Fe, Mg, Mn, Ca and Na were mapped and distributed into two mapping passes on the 4 spectrometers available as shown in Table 1. Elements typically analyzed for major rock-forming minerals include K and Cr, but these were omitted due to spectrometer configuration restrictions and low abundance of these elements in the samples of interest [5,6].

This study focuses on symplectite textures, fine-grained intergrowths of two or more minerals in a rock that may display grain sizes on the order of a few microns in width. Analytical conditions were chosen based on (1) the analytical interaction volume required to spatially resolve the minerals of interest in the maps (i.e., avoiding mixed analyses at grain boundaries), and (2) on the suite of elements needed to chemically characterize the phases of interest. To model beam interaction volumes, Monte Carlo simulations modeling electron trajectories in solids were performed for different accelerating voltages using Casino (v2.51) [7]. Simulations were run for 10 and 15 keV accelerating voltages on matrices for feldspar and diopsidic clinopyroxene (the finest grained material found in the samples of interest) to determine the most appropriate accelerating voltage to use to resolve ∼1-2 µm lamellae of these minerals. Simulations at 10 keV yielded interaction volumes ∼0.5-1 µm3, compared to ∼1-2 µm3 for a 15 keV accelerating voltage. Comparative simulations at 10 keV and 15 keV showing the effect of accelerating voltage on interaction volume are shown in Fig. 1. An accelerating voltage of 10 keV was chosen as analytically appropriate to maximize counts (optimal accelerating voltage between 2-3 times that of the highest energy line of interest, here Fe kα ∼6.4 keV) while minimizing the interaction volume to the ∼0.5 µm required to resolve the smallest features of interest in the mapped area).

MAN calibration curves are corrected for continuum absorption, and we use an alternative fractional method, the modified Z Fraction introduced by Donovan et al. [8], with uses atomic (vs. mass) fractions instead of mass fraction averages Z¯ shown in the equation above, and introduces an exponent to account for nuclear screening as a function of Z-value. MAN calibration curves were produced for each element using Phi(ρz) absorption correction of Armstrong [9] and Packwood and Brown [10] (Fig. 2). X-ray intensity data was also collected on two natural reference materials (NMNH 143965 Kakanui Hornblende; Royal Ontario Museum Collection M36875 Frank Smith Kimberlite Pyrope, Table 2) that do not make good candidates for MAN background calibrations as they contain many of the elements of interest for our unknowns, and were therefore used as check or secondary standards to assess the performance of the MAN background calibration. These secondary standards were quantified using the reference materials analyzed as primary standards and yielded accurate analyses relative to published values and are presented and discussed in the Method Validation section.

3. Map data collection in ProbeImage or JEOL software

Map data was collected in the JEOL software, which required converting JEOL map files to ProbeImage files through an addition step, though maps may also be collected directly in ProbeImage. Technical steps to setup maps in ProbeImage can be found in the ProbeImage User's Guide and Reference Manual under ‘Map acquisition in Probe Image (https://probesoftware.com/download/ProbeImage.pdf).

Four variably retrogressed eclogite samples were mapped. For each sample, one region of interest was identified. All X-ray intensity maps were collected using a beam current of 500 nA and dwell time of 10 milliseconds for each spot during map acquisition. The short dwell times are compensated by the use of large diffracting crystals (e.g., LIFL), and a relatively high beam current, 10-15 times higher than would be used for a standard spot analysis. Mapping step size was determined for each sample based on symplectite coarseness and targeted spatial resolution, and for each distinct sample, x-step = y-step was used to get equidimensional pixels. The size of the mapped region and pixel step-sizes used for each map is provided in Table 3.

4. Data processing in CalcImage

Mapped data was processed in CalcImage (part of the Probe For EPMA software package). Technical steps for processing of EPMA X-ray intensity maps into quantitative analysis can be found in ProbeImage User's Guide and Reference Manual, under ‘Map quantification in CalcImage’ (https://probesoftware.com/download/ProbeImage.pdf).

Map processing includes: (1) application of MAN background corrections to each pixel in the mapped region, (2) standardization using appropriate standards listed in Table 4, (3) Phi(ρz) or ZAF matrix corrections (here, we used the Phi(ρz) absorption correction of Armstrong/Packwood-Brown 1981 MAS [[9], [10]], the stopping power correction of Love-Scott, the mean ionization potential correction of Berger-Seltzer, the Reed/JTA with M-line fluorescence correction and JTA intensity model, and the Phi(ρz) equation, backscatter corrections and backscatter corrections of Love-Scott). 5. Post-processing data filtering in Excel

A master spreadsheet containing the raw data output file (.DAT) contents was created in Microsoft Excel for further data filtering to assign mineral IDs to each pixel. Two filtering passes were performed based on oxide wt.% and cation atoms per formula unit (apfu) on relevant O-basis calculations for relevant phases. Prior to filtering, minerals present and to be assigned during filtering were identified using petrographic observations in plane- and cross-polarize light. Then, a table containing mineral end-member, formulas and their composition was built to acquire a sense of wt.% oxide and stoichiometric constraints on mineral compositions for filtering (Full datasets available in [11]).

Next, two filtering operations were performed on the map master spreadsheet. First, a coarse filtering pass was used to assign each pixel of the maps to either a phase (mineral) according to appropriate filtering parameters for that phase (Table 5), or pixels that did not fit into any of the relevant phase bins and either correspond to spurious pixels or mixed analyses to be discarded prior to any interpretation, or phases not included in the filtering workflow, and labeled as ‘NA’. Next, a fine filtering pass was used to subsample pixels that fell within a broadly accepted range of mineral compositions and stoichiometric constraints to be considered reliable and publishable. Filtering parameters for coarse and fine filtering passes for the minerals identified in these samples and additional relevant end-member calculations (e.g., common garnet end-members Xalm, Xprp, Xsps, Xgrs; plagioclase end-members Xan and Xab) or stoichiometric constraints and formulas used for their calculation are provided in Table 5.

Additional data processing was carried out for amphiboles for Fe2+/Fe3+ recalculation, crystallographic site cation assignment, and amphibole classification according to Hawthorne et al. [12]. The spreadsheet of Locock [13] was used for recalculation, and the following recalculation options were used: orthorhombic = FALSE (0); use initial M3+/ΣM = FALSE; estimate OH=2-2CTi = TRUE (1); Require initial H2O + = FALSE (0). All “Manual choice of normalization procedure(s)” options were set to FALSE (0). This leads to the program choosing the normalization schemes that yield the smallest maximum deviations from each of the four criteria for Fe3+ recalculation. This reduces bias associated with ‘picking and choosing’ one or more criteria for Fe3+ estimation, and makes this batch-processing approach accommodating of the wide range of amphibole compositions that may be present in the analyzed samples. However, individual users may modulate this approach to their own samples and geological constraints. The schemes used for Fe3+ recalculation are reported in the final maps data amphibole composition spreadsheet.

The original spreadsheet of Locock [13] allows up to 200 recalculations per analysis batch. To process the large number of amphibole analyses obtained from each map (∼2,000-33,000 analyses per map), we expanded the Locock [13] spreadsheet to allow for up to 10,000 recalculations at a time. This speeds up the processing of pixel spot compositions by reducing the number of batches to 1-4 without building an Excel spreadsheet that would operate too slowly, or crash excessively due to overwhelming computer memory/RAM use. Recalculated amphibole spreadsheets are included in the raw supplemental data spreadsheets.

6. Data visualization in QGIS

The quantitative EPMA maps have many data attributes associated with each (x,y) spatial coordinate: wt.% oxides, wt.% totals, mineral ID, atoms per formula unit, end-member calculations for relevant phases, etc. As such, visualizing this multi-dimensional data cube in its spatial context is beyond the application scope of software products like Excel. For rapid data visualization, we developed a workflow enabling visualization of this data cube using Geographic Information System (GIS) software QGIS [14], a free and open-source GIS program that operates on Mac and Windows machines, and allows for easy collaboration across users and research teams. We describe the data import, data visualization and data segmentation/subsampling workflows below.

2. Comparative quantitative map vs. quantitative spot analyses

Spot analyses were collected using conventional 2-point background on the mapped samples. Spot data was collected at 10 keV accelerating voltage, 30 nA beam current using a beam diameter of 1µm. On-peak count times of 10s were used for Si, Ti, Al, Fe, Mn, Ca (with high-low peak count times of 5s + 5s = 10s total) and on-peak count times of 15s were used for Mg and Na (with high-low peak count times of 7.5s + 7.5s = 15s total). The Phi(pz) corrections of Armstrong-LoveScott were used for quantification. Primary standards used to standardize maps were matched for quantification of the spot analyses. Individual spot analysis data is available in Hamelin [11]. Data clouds for individual phases in each map were plotted and compared to quantitative spot analyses to ensure that map pixel data was consistent with conventional spot analyses. Example plots for different minerals analyzed in the samples of interest are represented Fig. 4. All quantitative spot analyses for feldspar, garnet and pyroxene show consistency in accuracy between spot analyses and pixel analyses. The (time x beam current) factor for spot analyses is 10s x 30 nA = 300 s·nA; for mapped pixels, this factor is 0.01s (10 ms) x 500 nA = 5 s·nA. These results show that even though the uncertainty on individual pixel analyses is greater than for individual spot analyses due to the two orders of magnitude difference in (time x beam current) factor between spot and pixel analyses, the pixel analyses are accurate despite being less precise. For example, the Err.% on Si wt.% spot analyses is on the order of 0.04 wt.% compared to ∼2.5% for mapped pixels, though comparative spot and corresponding map pixels yield the same wt.% Si. This tradeoff in precision is necessary to reach reasonable analytical map times, yet we remain confident that the absolute wt.% values for pixel compositions are accurate. In addition, 99% confidence detection limits and analytical sensitivity on mapped pixel analyses are within reasonable values for quantitative analyses (Table 7).

Limitations

Software limitations: this method can currently only be carried out in electron microprobe labs that operate using the Probe For EPMA software package suite, which fully implements the MAN background correction method. Currently, there is no MAN background implementation routines readily available for users in factory software packages included in the acquisition of microprobes (e.g., JEOL or Cameca software) and its implementation would require significant back-end software updates from electron microprobe manufacturers, and/or significant and labor-intensive procedure development from lab managers. PF-EPMA is used in many microprobe labs, but does require additional costs for upfront purchase of the software license.

Hardware and equipment limitations: electron microprobes use wavelength dispersive spectroscopy (WDS) to count X-ray intensities. Each spectrometer (typically, 4-5) is set for measurement of a single X-ray energy line (i.e., 1 WDS detector = 1 element measured at a time). Each spectrometer is equipped with 2-4 diffracting crystals (e.g., TAP, PET, LiF, LDE, etc.) mounted on a revolving turret allowing for a wide range of crystal combination and analyzable elements. The spectrometer setup and diffracting crystal availability is unique to each lab, and therefore each individual mapping session must be tuned to the spectrometer/crystal setup and analytical goals. For example, the electron microprobe used in this study is equipped with 4 spectrometers, enabling mapping of up to 4, 8, 12, etc. maps. To fully characterize the composition of major rock-forming minerals, a typical analytical setup includes the ‘big 10’ element suite (Si, Ti, Al, Mg, Fe, Mn, Ca, Na, K, Cr). However, mapping all 10 elements would have required 3 mapping passes, adding anywhere between 4-8 hours to each mapped sample. We elected to map only 8 elements, leaving out K and Cr with knowledge that the minerals of interest and rocks under study contained negligible amounts of these elements (see whole rock geochemical data presented in [5,6]). However, mapping rocks of different compositions such as metapelites would require analysis of K to fully characterize mica compositions. The element selection strategy above would therefore not have been used, and use of a microprobe equipped with 5 spectrometers would have been more ideal.

Another limitation revolved around the crystal availability and distribution on the available spectrometers. For the analysis to be successful, a spectrometer/crystal setup and distribution must be possible for the rocks, minerals and elements of interest in order to measure all elements required to fully characterize the compositions of minerals under study. Missing key elements will lead to (1) low analytical totals, and (2) poor matrix correction routines resulting in added inaccuracy in the final quantitative analysis.

In addition, we will note that access to large diffracting crystals (TAPL, PETL, LiFL) such as those used in this study yield X-ray count rates that are 2-3 times higher compared to the use of their regular counterparts, and allows for shorter pixel dwell times to reduce overall analytical time for each map without degraded counting statistics. Using non-large crystals would have either reduced the quality of our analysis (higher detection limits, higher % error on individual quantified pixel wt.%) or significantly increased the mapping time required for each sample (e.g., 16-24 hours instead of 8 hours per mapped area). Though these large crystals are becoming more common on newver instruments, many EPMA labs are only equipped with non-large crystals.

Finally, the exact reproduction of this method is likely not achieved unless mapping similar rocks (metabasalts) on the same instrument (JEOL JXA8530FPlus at the University of Minnesota). Each analytical session (samples, spectrometer and diffracting crystals options and setup, elements of interest) needs to be customized to fit the instrument, time available, and analytical goals (e.g., mapping major vs. trace elements) of the session with the assistance of the lab manager. Here, we describe a general workflow for implementation of MAN background corrected mapping, associated data processing and visualization workflows, and their applications to analysis of metamorphic (or igneous) rocks.
