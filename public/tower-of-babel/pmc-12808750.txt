Macretina: a dataset, to support deep learning assisted retinopathy of prematurity diagnosis
Authors: Urvesh Trivedi, Abhishek Srivastava, Pratik Mahajan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Retinopathy of Prematurity (ROP) is a vision-threatening retinal disease found in premature babies, where early diagnosis is very important to prevent irreversible vision loss. In recent years, several studies have been conducted on the development of reliable AI-based screening systems. However, due to the lack of well-annotated public datasets most of them have been limited to experimental research, using single-central datasets. In this study, we introduce Macretina, a comprehensive and expert-annotated dataset curated from 1432 retinal fundus images of 112 premature babies collected at Macretina Hospital, Indore, India. These images were captured using the 3nethra Neo wide-field retinal imaging system, commonly used for retinopathy of prematurity (ROP) screening. The dataset is specially designed to support AI-based automated ROP diagnosis and is organized into three subsets, each addressing a distinct pathologically relevant retinal feature for ROP screening. The three subsets are: Macretina-Ridge which supports binary classification for ridge/demarcation line detection, Macretina-OD which supports object detection for optic disc localization, and Macretina-BV which supports semantic segmentation for blood vessel analysis. We also evaluated the utility of each subset using standard Deep Convolutional Neural Networks (DCNNs), and the experiments achieved promising results across Classification, Object Detection, and Segmentation tasks. Our dataset captures a wide range of disease severity and imaging variations, making it well-suited for developing clinically relevant and generalizable AI models.

The condition of ROP occurs due to the growth of abnormal blood vessels in the retina. ROP is sometimes mild and naturally cured without any treatment, but in severe cases, it leads to irreversible visual impairment or blindness. Thus, ROP-screening is very important in all premature babies, as disease severity can vary widely, and early detection is essential to determine the need for timely treatment. In middle-income countries such as India, where over 64% of the population live in rural areas2, the timely diagnosis of ROP is significantly affected by the lack of essential resources and medical expertise. The International Classification of Retinopathy of Prematurity (ICROP) provides standard guidelines for the detection and classification of the disease into different stages and zones to measure its severity3. The Zone level classification determines the extent of vascular growth in the retina, by dividing it into three concentric areas (Zone-1, Zone-2, and Zone-3) centered around the optic disc (as shown in Figure 2). Zone-1 ROP represents the most severe form of the disease, as it affects the innermost and most critical part of the retina. The Stage level ROP classification is based on specific retinal findings and classifies the disease into five stages. In Stage-1 (Figure 1a), a thin white demarcation line formed between vascular and avascular regions of the retina. In Stage-2 (Figure 1b), the demarcation line becomes thick and called ridge. In Stage-3 (Figure 1c), abnormal blood vessels (extraretinal neovascularization) begin to grow near the ridge, giving it a ragged appearance. In Stage-4 (Figure 1d), partial retinal detachment begins at the ridge and may extend towards the fovea. In Stage-5 (Figure 1e), the retina becomes completely detached, leading to severe vision impairment or blindness. In a recent update, a more severe form of the disease was introduced called Aggressive ROP (A-ROP) (Figure 1f), a rapidly progressing form of ROP commonly seen in Zone-1 or posterior Zone-2, characterized by severe vascular abnormalities without the typical ridge formation. Babies with A-ROP require urgent treatment as it can quickly lead to total retinal detachment.Figure 2Classification of retinopathy of prematurity (ROP) into Zone-1,2 and 3, as defined by ICROP standards.

In recent studies, the introduction of artificial intelligence (AI) in automated ROP screening has given promising results in neonatal care by improving both research and clinical practice. These AI-based screening systems assist ophthalmologists in identifying key retinal features such as abnormal blood vessels growth, optic disc location, and demarcation line/ridge, which are essential for proper ROP diagnosis and severity analysis. However, an impeding factor here is the lack of well-annotated public datasets for AI-based ROP diagnosis4–7, which is crucial to develop reliable AI models for accurate diagnosis. This paper presents Macretina12, an expertly-annotated, well-structured and publicly available (on Figshare) ROP dataset specially designed to support research in AI-based ROP diagnosis. Our dataset contains retinal fundus images of premature babies captured using 3nethra Neo wide-field retinal imaging system13, commonly used in India for neonatal eye examinations. It is designed in a way that can support three key aspects of AI-based ROP diagnosis: Segmentation, Classification, and Object Detection - used to find different retinal features related to ROP. Additionally, to check its relativeness with other public datasets on ROP, we evaluated the dataset using pre-trained Deep Convolutional Neural Networks (DCNNs) for three different tasks. A complete overview of the proposed methodology is presented in Figure 3. With this, our study aims to support the development of AI-based ROP screening system by providing a well-annotated ROP dataset for research on retinopathy of prematurity (ROP).

Related Work

Retinopathy of Prematurity (ROP) is one of the primary causes of childhood blindness, therefore early detection is very important for timely treatment. Fundus imaging is widely used for ROP screening because it provides a clear view of the retinal structures. In recent years, artificial intelligence (AI) has shown great potential in developing automated systems that can assist ophthalmologists in diagnosing disease using retinal fundus images14. Many studies have explored deep learning-based approaches for automated ROP diagnosis, using convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and transformer-based models for Classification, Segmentation, or Object Detection for severity assessment. Although some studies have achieved very good results, most of them rely on private datasets, which limits their applicability in the real world. The performance and robustness of the deep learning models can be improved if trained on multi-center datasets that include data from diverse populations15. However, to the best of our knowledge there are very few publicly available ROP datasets (presented in Table 1) to support such research. We collected 1432 retinal fundus images from 112 premature babies from the Macretina Hospital, Indore, India18. These images (of size 2040 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document} 2040 pixels) were captured using the 3nethra Neo wide-field retinal imaging system with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$120^{\circ }$$\end{document} field of view (Forus Healthcare, Bangalore, India). On average, 4 to 12 images were taken (from both eyes) per baby during regular retinal screening of premature babies at Macretina Hospital for ROP diagnosis. Before imaging, pupil dilation was done using diluted phenylephrine and tropicamide, given three times at 10-minute intervals. Feeding was also stopped after the final dose to meet optimal imaging conditions. All personal details about the babies were kept private by the hospital during the dataset collection process. The collected fundus images covered different retinal views, such as posterior, temporal, superior, inferior, and nasal. However, we selected mainly temporal and posterior view retinal images, as they provide the most critical information about disease progression.

One of the authors, Pratik Mahajan, a senior ophthalmologist at Macretina Eye Hospital with over 15 years of experience in retinal surgeries and pediatric ophthalmology, guided the annotation process along with one ROP expert (with 8+ years of experience), one trained junior ophthalmologist, and one digital designer. Minor disagreements and confusions between the graders during the annotation process were resolved by the senior ophthalmologist with his clinical expertise. Inter-grader agreement between the senior ROP expert and the ROP expert was computed after the final annotation step (with Cohen’s Kappa value of 0.89). However, the possibility of intra-grader variability cannot be ruled out, as no external ROP expert was involved in the annotation process. To gain a deep understanding of ROP pathology, we reviewed existing AI-based research in ROP screening in order to identify the key gaps and challenges present in recent studies. We also had detailed discussions with ROP experts to understand their clinical needs to prepare the dataset accordingly. As part of the process, the authors learned about Aggressive ROP (A-ROP), which is increasingly reported in India19,20. We also gained practical exposure to different ROP stages (Stages 1–5) and learned why temporal and posterior views are essential in ROP diagnosis. Thus, the annotation process was not only about marking the pathological features but also served as a valuable learning experience for the authors.

Before annotation, all the collected images were filtered through an initial quality screening to ensure that only diagnostically relevant images were included in the study. This screening was performed by a trained grader under the guidance of the senior ROP expert. During this process, images that were out-of-focus, blurry, obscured by artifacts (reflections, eyelid or eyelash obstruction), or not relevant to ROP diagnosis (peripheral images without retinal structures) were excluded. After removing such noisy and irrelevant images (303), a total of 1,129 images were used for the labelling and annotation process.During this annotation process, an additional 156 images were excluded by the ROP experts. These included images showing prominent medullary nerve fibers near the optic disc, images that did not clearly represent pathological features due to poor illumination, duplicate images identified during labeling, images with laser scars (due to laser therapy) where the optic disc was not visible, and images showing partial or complete retinal detachment (Stage 4 and Stage 5 ROP). We did not remove images containing laser scars in which the optic disc was clearly visible, as such images were useful for developing the Macretina-OD subset. Of the remaining images, 250 images showed the presence of ridge or demarcation line (Stage 1, 2, and 3 ROP cases), 248 images corresponded to Aggressive ROP (A-ROP) cases, and 215 images were of normal healthy retina. Additionally, 260 images were not included in the normal category (normal healthy retina) but were instead used for optic disc (OD) annotation, as the OD was clearly visible while the blood vessels were poorly illuminated. In total, 500 images with a visible optic disc were later used to prepare the Macretina-OD subset. A summary of dataset preparation workflow for the Macretina dataset is provided in Table 2.

Since, recent studies on AI based ROP diagnosis are mainly focused on detecting retinal structures critical for ROP diagnosis–such as the optic disc, blood vessels, and the demarcation line/ridge–which are essential for identifying ROP zones and stages. Therefore,images showing partial or complete retinal detachment (Stage 4 and Stage 5 cases) were excluded. These cases were limited in number and not the main focus of our work. Moreover, these advanced stages involve significant retinal detachment, often reducing the visibility of key retinal structures, which makes them less suitable for AI-based ROP diagnosis. Hence, the Macretina dataset was specifically prepared to support the detection of pathological features in early-stage ROP cases.

The Macretina-Ridge dataset is designed for binary classification, which can be helpful for training deep learning models to detect the presence of a ridge or demarcation line in retinal fundus images. Identifying this feature at the right time is crucial to monitor ROP staging, as it can progress to a more severe disease state. It is one of the earliest and most significant indicators of disease progression, therefore its accurate detection is very important for early diagnosis and treatment. Sometimes it is difficult to find ridge or demarcation line in the retinal images of premature babies. Unlike other retinal structures, the appearance of a ridge or demarcation line varies depending on factors such as image quality, severity of the disease, and the retinal development stage. In some cases, it appears as a well-defined elevated structure, while in others, it is thin, blurry, and hardly visible. Additionally, imaging conditions–such as poor illumination, motion artefacts, and reflections from the vitreous (clear gel that fills the space between the lens and the retina) can hide the ridge, which makes manual labelling difficult.

To prepare accurate annotations, each image in the Macretina-Ridge dataset was manually labelled by a team of three graders. The initial labelling was performed by the ROP expert and the trained junior ophthalmologist, and the annotations were subsequently verified and refined by the senior ROP expert. Their clinical expertise was essential for distinguishing true ridge formations from imaging artifacts, ensuring that only diagnostically relevant features were marked. Their clinical expertise was required in distinguishing true ridge formations from imaging artifacts, to make sure that only clinically significant features were marked. This sub-dataset includes 465 images, with 250 images showing a ridge or demarcation line and 215 images representing normal retinal structures. By providing expert-labeled images for ridge/demarcation line detection, the Macretina-Ridge dataset serves as a valuable resource for developing AI models that can detect early signs of ROP progression. Such models can support ophthalmologists by differentiating normal and pathological retinal structures, improving early-stage ROP diagnosis, and helping in timely medical intervention for affected infants.Figure 4Manually annotated bounding box around the optic disc (OD).

The Macretina-OD dataset is designed for optic disc (OD) detection, a crucial component in zone-based ROP analysis. It contains 500 retinal fundus images, each with a manually annotated rectangular bounding box around the optic disc as shown in Figure 4. The annotations were created by a digital designer and trained junior ophthalmologist, under the supervision of the senior ROP expert, who performed the final verification and approval. The bounding boxes were created using Roboflow21 (Roboflow Inc., USA), an online computer vision platform that provides an intuitive interface for image annotation, dataset management, and model training support. These annotations serve as ground truth for training Object Detection models to accurately locate the optic disc in unseen retinal images. Annotating the optic disc in infant retinal images is more challenging than in adults. In adults, the optic disc is larger, well-defined, and has clearly visible boundaries. In contrast, infant retinal images often show a smaller, blurry, and less distinct optic disc due to an underdeveloped retina and lower image contrast. Imaging conditions such as uneven illumination and a limited field of view further reduce its visibility. The small size and unclear boundaries of the optic disc in neonatal eyes make it difficult to draw precise bounding boxes for accurate localization. However, it is important to accurately compute the center and diameter of the optic disc for zone-based analysis.

To overcome these challenges, the Macretina-OD dataset includes accurate bounding box annotations, so that the extracted coordinates can be effectively used to compute the center and diameter of the optic disc which can be further used for zone-based ROP classification. In creation of this sub-dataset, mostly posterior (central) view retinal images were included, as they provide the clear and complete view of the optic disc. Since the coordinates of optic disc serves as a key reference for assessing disease progression, this sub-dataset provides valuable data for AI models that assist in ROP severity grading. By providing well-labeled posterior view images, the Macretina-OD dataset supports the development of AI models that can help clinicians in zonally classifying ROP cases to check disease severity and progression.Figure 5Neo Images (a,b,c) with their BV-Masks (e,f,g) from Macretina-BV dataset.

The Macretina-BV dataset is designed for blood vessel segmentation, which plays a very important role in ROP severity analysis. Since abnormal vascular development is a key indicator of disease progression, accurate segmentation is essential for AI-assisted ROP diagnosis. This sub-dataset includes 38 high-quality retinal fundus images, each paired with a manually created blood vessel mask as shown in Figure 5. These ground truths (blood vessel mask) were manually created by a digital designer using Figma Desktop App22 (version-124.6.5; Figma Inc., San Francisco, CA, USA) with the help of two ROP experts and one trained ophthalmologist. We used Figma because it supports precise pixel-level editing and overlay capabilities. Due to the high cost and time-consuming nature of manual vessel annotation, only 38 images were selected for ground truth preparation in this sub-dataset. Despite its limited size, the Macretina-BV dataset serves as a valuable resource for training and evaluating deep learning models for medical image segmentation in automated ROP severity analysis.

To ensure the dataset covers complex and severe vascular abnormalities, we primarily included retinal images with Aggressive ROP (A-ROP) cases. Since it is characterized by the rapid growth of widened (dilated) and wavy (tortuous) blood vessels, each segmentation mask was precisely validated by ROP experts to accurately capture the growth of those dilated, tortuous, and abnormal blood vessels. The Macretina-BV dataset provides segmentation masks for Aggressive ROP (A-ROP) cases, which are absent in existing ROP blood vessel segmentation datasets, making it a clinically valuable resource for training AI models to handle challenging real-world cases.

Experiments and Results

To check the usefulness of the Macretina dataset, we evaluated well-known Deep Convolutional Neural Networks (DCNNs) for Classification, Object Detection, and Segmentation tasks. All models used in the following experiments were initially trained on public datasets and later tested on the Macretina dataset to measure its applicability in AI-based ROP diagnosis. To improve image quality and enhance model performance, simple pre-processing steps were applied before training the models in the following order:(1) The green channel was extracted from the original RGB images, as it provides better contrast for retinal structures, especially blood vessels and ridge formations. (2) Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization (CLAHE)23 with ClipLimit 2.0 and tileGridSize of (8,8) was applied to the green channel to enhance local contrast while preventing overamplification of noise. (3) The CLAHE-enhanced green channel was then merged back with the original image to retain important structural details with improved contrast. (4) Finally, all images were resized according to the specific input dimensions required by each DCNN model to ensure compatibility with different deep-learning architectures.

To increase the generalization capacity of the models and reduce overfitting, we applied the basic data augmentation technique to all the training sets. We used horizontal and vertical flips, as they preserve the anatomical structure of the retina while increasing variability of the dataset. We did not use complex filtering-based augmentation techniques since the dataset consists of retinal fundus images of premature babies, which contains fine and delicate features. Overprocessing such medical images could affect the visibility of clinically relevant features, which are essential for a reliable diagnosis.

We fine-tuned each network on existing public ROP datasets1110 and evaluated on the Macretina-Ridge dataset to classify images into two categories: Ridge and No-Ridge. The optic disc plays a crucial role in zone-based ROP classification, making its accurate localization essential for automated ROP diagnosis. Zone-based analysis helps to determine how far the abnormal blood vessels have developed from the optic disc. The experimental results demonstrate the potential of the Macretina dataset in supporting AI-based ROP diagnosis through three critical tasks: ridge detection, optic disc detection, and blood vessel segmentation. In ridge detection, DenseNet201 achieved the highest accuracy (91.50%), while MobileNet-V2 achieved the best recall (98.00%), although performance could be further improved with larger datasets or different experimental settings. For optic disc detection, YOLOv5 achieved outstanding performance (mAP@0.5: 0.994,mAP@0.5:0.95: 0.80, recall: 0.993), giving accurate localization of optic disc boundaries, which is crucial for zone-based severity analysis. In blood vessel segmentation, models achieved high SSIM scores (0.9981 for U-Net and 0.9983 for AG U-Net) and accuracy above 96.00%. However, Dice coefficients (0.4442 for U-Net and 0.4571 for AG U-Net) were lower than reported on other public ROP - blood vessel datasets in the literature. This drop in model performance is mainly due to the complexity of the Macretina-BV dataset, as it includes detailed and dense vessel masks to represent aggressive ROP (A-ROP) cases.

Discussion

The availability of well-annotated public ROP datasets is essential to develop reliable AI-based ROP screening systems. However, existing public ROP datasets have significant limitations. Some datasets only provide ground truth annotations for a single pathological feature, which makes them ineffective for complete ROP diagnosis and severity analysis8–10, while datasets covering annotations for multiple pathological features are not sufficient to effectively train deep learning models11.In addition, certain datasets provides only image-level disease labels without pixel-level or lesion-level annotations10,16. The proposed Macretina dataset offers multi-task annotations across three clinically important features–ridge, optic disc, and retinal blood vessel -within a single dataset. This makes it more suitable for comprehensive ROP diagnosis by supporting the development of AI models that cover different pathological features for accurate disease assessment. Additionally, our dataset includes severe A-ROP cases, which are underrepresented in existing public ROP datasets, thereby improving its clinical relevance. A key strength of the Macretina dataset lies in its expert-verified and high-quality annotations. Each subset of the dataset was prepared by two domain experts under the guidance of a senior ROP expert, ensuring accurate and clinically meaningful labeling. The images captured using the 3nethra Neo imaging system in our dataset represent data from neonatal screening in low- and middle-income settings, supporting the development of robust AI-based ROP screening systems that can be useful in such regions. The consistent performance of models across different architectures in the experiments suggests that the dataset is well-suited for transfer learning to enhance model generalization capacity in supporting AI-based ROP diagnosis through three critical tasks. However, the our dataset also has a few limitations. Images with chorioretinal scars and advanced-stage ROP (Stages 4 and 5) were excluded, which limits its applicability for those specific conditions. The relatively small number of segmentation masks in the Macretina-BV subset is another limitation, as it restricts large-scale model training, though it still provides valuable reference data for evaluating segmentation performance in complex A-ROP cases. Overall, the Macretina dataset addresses the gaps in existing public datasets by providing a diverse, clinically verified, and multi-task resource to support research in AI-based ROP diagnosis.

Conclusion

In this work, we introduce Macretina, a comprehensive dataset designed to support research in AI-based diagnosis of Retinopathy of Prematurity (ROP). A key strength of the Macretina dataset is its expert-verified annotations. All ground truths were created under the guidance of senior ROP experts, helping to reduce labeling errors and improve data reliability. The dataset includes a wide range of images with varying levels of disease severity, retinal features, and imaging conditions, making it robust and well-suited for supporting the development of AI-based automated ROP diagnosis systems for real-world clinical use.

Future Work

In future work, We aim to include multi-center image data from different imaging systems to enhance the generalizability of the dataset. we also plan to expand the segmentation subset by adding more manually annotated vessel masks for A-ROP cases. Since the current version of the Macretina dataset is not large enough to train AI models from scratch, we plan to increase its size in future releases so that it can be effectively used to develop and validate deep learning models. We also plan to explore the integration of AI-based interpretable ROP diagnostic modules into clinical workflows and study their potential economic impact in real-world screening. As demographic factors such as sex and ethnicity can influence diagnostic accuracy, we will consider including such information in future versions of this dataset to ensure fair and unbiased outcomes.
