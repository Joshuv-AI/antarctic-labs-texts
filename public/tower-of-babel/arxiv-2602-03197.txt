Exploring the Role of Tracing in AI-Supported Planning for Algorithmic Reasoning
Yoshee Jain, Heejin Do, Zihan Wu, April Yi Wang

Abstract
AI-powered planning tools show promise in supporting programming learners by enabling early, formative feedback on their thinking processes prior to coding. To date, however, most AI-supported planning tools rely on students’ natural-language explanations, using LLMs to interpret learners’ descriptions of their algorithmic intent. Prior to the emergence of LLM-based systems, CS education research extensively studied trace-based planning in pen-and-paper settings, demonstrating that reasoning through stepwise execution with explicit state transitions helps learners build and refine mental models of program behavior. Despite its potential, little is known about how tracing interacts with AI-mediated feedback and whether integrating tracing into AI-supported planning tools leads to different learning processes or interaction dynamics compared to natural-language-based planning alone. We study how requiring learners to produce explicit execution traces with an AI-supported planning tool affects their algorithmic reasoning. In a between-subjects study with 20 students, tracing shifted learners away from code-like, line-by-line descriptions toward more goal-driven reasoning about program behavior. Moreover, it led to more consistent partially correct solutions, although final coding performance remained comparable across conditions. Notably, tracing did not significantly affect the quality or reliability of LLM-generated feedback. These findings reveal tradeoffs in combining tracing with AI-supported planning and inform design guidelines for integrating natural language, tracing, and coding to support iterative reasoning throughout the programming process.

Related Work

Planning in Programming Education
. Planning is a widely studied construct in programming education due to its potential to scaffold learners’ reasoning during computing-related problem solving
[
29
]
. Prior work shows that requiring learners to articulate algorithms before engaging in syntax-based implementation shifts attention from surface-level code features to underlying structural representations of problems

[

[

.
In AI-supported learning environments, planning representations not only support learners’ reasoning, but also serve as the primary interface through which AI systems interpret learners’ intent.

A common classroom practice that operationalizes planning is Explain-in-Plain-English (EiPE) questions
[
22
]
, which require learners to describe their intended solution in natural language prior to coding. EiPE has been associated with improved conceptual understanding, alignment with programming proficiency, and the ability to surface misconceptions
[
22
]
. However, natural-language descriptions are frequently ambiguous, or inconsistent, complicating translation into executable code and introducing additional implementation burden
[
33
]
.

Separately, prior research shows that pen-and-paper tracing before coding supports planning by encouraging learners to simulate execution and reason about intermediate program states
[
16
,
7
]
, which may bridge the gap between natural-language planning and code writing.
However, prior work has examined tracing primarily as a cognitive scaffold for supporting learners’ reasoning in non-AI-mediated settings.
In contrast, in AI-supported planning environments, tracing may also function as a structured interface for AI systems to interpret and evaluate learners’ reasoning.
Little to no work has investigated how this dual role of tracing affects learners’ reasoning processes, interaction dynamics, or the quality of automated feedback.

AI-Supported Feedback and Evaluation.

While EiPE has demonstrated instructional value, its evaluation often requires substantial manual grading effort, placing additional workload demands on instructional staff
[
2
]
. Recent advances in NLP and LLMs have led to automated approaches for evaluating natural-language program descriptions. For example, Smith et al. proposed generating executable code from student explanations and running test cases to approximate correctness
[

]
, while other systems explored rubric-based LLM grading or hybrid evaluation pipelines
[

,

. However, student-written natural language responses frequently contain imprecise terminology, omitted assumptions, and inconsistent levels of abstraction
[
10
,
17
,
24

. As a result, LLMs must infer unstated reasoning steps, which can produce variable or misaligned feedback. Such ambiguity introduces reliability concerns and may negatively affect both learning outcomes and learner confidence
[

,

,
26

. Incorporating explicit state transitions via code tracing alongside natural language descriptions may provide more constrained and machine-interpretable representations of student intent. However, the effects of including program execution traces on AI-mediated feedback remains underexplored in existing work.

Study Procedure.

The study (approved by the institutional IRB) consisted of four main stages that lasted 60 minutes: (1) a guided practice phase (up to 10 minutes), (2) the planning phase using natural language- or trace-based planning (15 minutes), (3) a coding phase (10 minutes), and (4) a reflective semi-structured interview (up to 10 minutes; Figure

Participants first completed a brief onboarding, including informed consent and a demographics questionnaire, followed by a worked example
[
1

21
]
from introductory programming demonstrating the planning representation for their assigned condition.
After a short practice task using the AI-supported planning interface, participants iteratively refined their algorithm for the greedy task, followed by a coding test in Python, during which they could reference their plan. The tasks were identical across conditions. Finally, participants completed a set of 7-point Likert-scale items assessing perceived learning outcomes, cognitive load (adapted from
[
14
]
), and interface usability
[
15
]
, culminating in a semi-structured interview to probe their learning experiences, feedback on LLM explanations, and the system’s overall benefits and limitations.
Each participant was compensated with a $25 USD Amazon gift card for their time.

Data Collection and Analysis.

We collected multiple sources of data, including (1) all planning submissions produced during the study and the corresponding LLM-generated feedback, (2) participants’ final code submissions, (3) responses to the demographics questionnaire and post-task Likert-scale surveys (learning outcomes, cognitive load, and usability), and (4) transcripts of the semi-structured interviews.

4
Results

In this section, we report the key findings from our study. Participants in the trace condition exhibited a shift in planning characteristics, including fewer steps and reduced reliance on code-adjacent constructs. Coding submissions from students in the trace-based planning condition showed patterns of iterative improvement and more partially correct intermediate solutions; however, these differences were not statistically significant. No statistically significant differences were observed in final coding performance, perceived learning outcomes, or the quality and reliability of AI-generated feedback.

4.1
Effects on Learners’ Planning and Reasoning Processes

We first examine how tracing influences learners’ planning representations and reasoning processes, and how these changes influence code outcomes.

Plan Representation Across Conditions
.
To gain insights about linguistic and structural differences between conditions, we examined step count and references to control-flow structures. Plans in the trace-based planning condition contained significantly fewer steps (
p=0.026
) and use of explicit control-flow constructs (
p=0.018
) than those in the natural language-based condition (Figure
3
; a,b).
However, total word count did not differ significantly between conditions, indicating comparable overall plan length. This pattern indicates a shift toward more compact, less code-adjacent planning representations rather than simply shorter descriptions.

A deeper qualitative analysis of the plans revealed consistent representational differences across conditions. Plans in the trace condition usually put together multiple operations within a single step, whereas baseline plans separated actions into finer-grained, sequential steps that closely mirrored code structure. Additionally, trace plans more frequently included explanatory justifications, whereas baseline plans listed procedural actions without elaboration.

Semantic alignment analyses between the plan representation and code submission further clarifies these differences (Figure
3
; e). The plans from the natural-language planning condition had a mean alignment of M = 0.955 (MD = 0.953) while plans from the trace-based condition exhibited M = 0.941 (MD = 0.942). These differences were not significant.

Together with the qualitative analysis, this reduced alignment indicates that trace-based planning may shift learners from code-proximal descriptions toward more conceptual reasoning during planning.

Learning Perceptions
. As seen in Figure

Qualitative analysis of students’ interview responses revealed deeper insights into student experiences and perceptions.

Several students explained that tracing enabled them to iteratively test small examples (T01, T05), identify emerging patterns (T08), and surface edge cases earlier (T02, T05, T07), rather than relying on language descriptions. They thus described trace-based planning as beneficial for how they approached and practiced problem-solving, rather than for learning new domain concepts (T05).
In contrast to the quantitative confidence measures, participants added that validating their reasoning before coding lead to increased self-efficacy during the implementation phase (T05, T09).
Students added that separating planning and coding made implementation feel
“less daunting”
(T05) yet constructing explicit execution traces was described as tedious (T08).
Students reacted positively to the system interface, describing it as
“very intuitive for supporting code tracing.”

Nine of ten participants reported finding the trace-based planning workflow useful. Participants suggested that the workflow could be useful for preparing for technical interviews, as it encourages

4.2
Effects on AI Feedback

To examine whether trace-based planning altered LLM feedback, we annotated the steps marked as incorrect in student submissions by the LLM and its justifications. Step-level accuracy was coded using a binary scale (0 or 1), while the accompanying explanations were evaluated on a three-point scale (0, 0.5, 1) to capture partial correctness.

Mean step-level correctness was
0.74
(SD =
0.44

0.80
(SD =
0.41

0.66

p=.485

Despite these results, surprisingly, students in both trace- and natural language -based planning conditions indicated that the AI-guidance was one of the most useful features of the learning tool. Qualitative analysis of student’s feedback revealed that while the elicited feedback was
“useful, it could be improved”
. A few participants added that the feedback was
“vague”
and
“ambiguous”
, saying that while the system identified errors in the their solutions, it did not clearly explain why their reasoning was incorrect or how to meaningfully revise their solution (T05, T06, T08, N01).

Participants reported reduced trust in the AI feedback when the system appeared to contradict itself, such as revising or flagging steps it had previously marked as correct (T06). In addition, students expressed that although the tool was designed to allow flexibility in the level of detail provided in the algorithm descriptions, the LLM often encouraged more verbose and highly detailed explanations (T07, N04, N05).

5
Discussion

In this section, we synthesize our findings and derive design implications for supporting learners’ during algorithmic programming. Our analyses indicated that incorporating tracing during AI-supported planning may shift learners from line-by-line, code-like planning representation to more goal-driven descriptions of program behavior containing reasoning explanations. However, trace-based planning was associated primarily with differences in students’ procedural behaviors during planning, and we did not observe differences in final performance. These procedural differences reflect shifts in students’ underlying cognition
[
6
,
12
]
. In the age of LLMs, where code generation is increasingly automated
[
5
]
, humans play a central role in reasoning and judgment, qualities that LLMs do not reliably exhibit as of now
[
19
]
. By providing insight into students’ cognitive reasoning processes, our work informs the design of learning tools that support structured thinking and principled problem solving rather than implementation, aligning educational efforts with the evolving needs of an AI-driven workforce.

5.1
Code-Like Reasoning to Goal-Driven Plan Representation

Our findings from Section
4.1
revealed that trace-based plans contained fewer steps with comparable overall plan word counts, suggesting that students tend to group actions into higher-level subgoals
[
4
,
18
]
. Organizing plans into meaningful chunks may enhance conceptual clarity
[
12
]
and facilitate knowledge transfer
[
18
]
by making the underlying structure of the algorithm explicit rather than focusing on isolated actions. Thus, by prompting learners to articulate goal driven steps, tracing may nudge them toward more expert-like representations of algorithmic problems. Additionally, writing out step-by-step execution of variables alongside articulated subgoals creates structured opportunities for self-explanation
[
34
]
. By working through concrete examples, learners can evaluate how their algorithms behave across different test cases, supporting sensemaking between intended plan behavior and actual execution outcomes. Notably, one participant expressed that, tracing helps
“explain [your algorithm] to yourself”
(T05).

While such goal-driven representations may reduce the cognitive burden of programming by redistributing effort across distinct planning and implementation stages
[
30
]
, it may increase the difficulty of the translation process during coding, as was observed in our results, where the natural-language plans exhibited higher similarity to the code submissions than the trace-based plans.

Although differences were not statistically significant, descriptive trends in Figure
4
suggest that trace-based planning may result in more consistent incremental refinements across submissions, whereas baseline participants exhibited more variable shifts in solution structure. One possible explanation is that in trace-based planning, students organize solutions into explicit subgoals, allowing them to revise individual components without altering the overall structure. In contrast, when planning representations more closely mirror code structure, even small revisions may propagate more broadly through the implementation, just as in code, producing larger semantic shifts in consecutive submissions.
It is also possible that trace-based planning supports more reflective revision processes, as learners externalize and evaluate their reasoning before modifying their solution. By contrast, code-like planning representations may encourage exploratory structural edits that resemble trial-and-adjust strategies, leading to more variable semantic shifts across submissions.

Although we hypothesized that incorporating execution traces would provide more constrained, machine-interpretable representations, we did not observe measurable improvements in LLM feedback accuracy or explanation quality. This could be because, the move away from code-like descriptions toward conceptual explanations increases interpretive flexibility. When learners describe intent at the goal-level, LLMs must infer how those goals map onto specific algorithmic operations, potentially reintroducing ambiguity despite the presence of explicit trace information. In addition, our qualitative analysis further revealed that the LLM frequently validated forward-iterative strategies as correct, while prompting students who employed backward search or recursive approaches to revise their solutions. This pattern suggests a tendency for the model to prefer a dominant solution rather than accommodate diverse, valid algorithmic strategies.

5.2
Design Guidelines

From qualitative analysis of participants’ feedback, we derive design guidelines organized around three dimensions: (1) planning representation, (2) programming process or workflow, and (3) scaffolded feedback to inform the development of AI-powered learning tools that support students during programming.

DG1: Support flexible representations that adapt to learners’ needs.

Students emphasized that the system should allow them to define the number and type of variables in ways that reflect their intended solution strategy rather than modifying their reasoning to fit a predefined model solution (T02, T03). Participants reported that tracing every execution step felt unnecessary and preferred selectively tracing steps where additional clarification was needed (T08). Participants also expressed interest in examining multiple input cases to evaluate algorithmic behavior across scenarios (T10).

DG2: Incorporate an integrated workflow where natural language, trace, and code co-evolve across programming stages.

Participants pointed to introducing an integrated workflow where they can switch between natural language planning, trace execution, and code writing at any point rather than functioning as strictly separate stages. One student wanted to revise their trace during coding to simulate the new corner cases that surfaced (T01). Participants also speculated shifting their overall approach while implementing their solution in code and expressed a desire to get AI feedback on these revised strategies.

DG3: Encourage learner’s autonomy for decisions about amount of scaffolding provided.

Participants noted that scaffolding needs vary with learners’ prior knowledge and familiarity with the concept. One participant suggested beginning with a template-based planning representation
[
29
,
8
]
to provide heavy support during the initial stages of problem solving (T08), that fades
[
13
]
as they gain proficiency. Similarly, another student proposed letting learners choose the granularity of AI feedback (T05), suggesting more directive guidance for beginners and minimal intervention for advanced learners seeking challenge.

6
Limitations and Future Work

As is common in mixed-methods studies, a key limitation of this work is the relatively small sample size (
N=20
), which limited statistical power to detect subtle differences between conditions. Moreover, this study was designed as a single-session investigation to understand how trace-based planning influences learners’ immediate reasoning processes and behavior. Larger-scale longitudinal studies can examine how these representational and procedural changes develop over time and whether they translate into delayed learning gains.
In addition, we relied on learners’ self-reported measures of understanding and confidence that gave us insight into perceived learning experiences. However, self-reported measures may not always align with empirical outcomes
[
9
]
and objective performance measures are needed to more rigorously evaluate how trace-based planning influences the development of algorithmic reasoning over time.
