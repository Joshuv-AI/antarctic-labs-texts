Interpreting surgical outcomes during the COVID-19 pandemic: methodological considerations—authors’ reply
Authors: Laura Teixeira Castro, Cynthia de Carvalho Fischer, Aneel Bhangu, Dmitri Nepogodiev
Published: 2026

We thank Dr. De Luca for his comments on our article.1,2 We agree that there are limitations to our analysis. Although we have adjusted for country and patient level factors, there may be residual confounding relating to hospital resourcing, which may be higher in better prepared hospitals. Given that it is not possible to randomise hospital preparedness, our study design was the best possible methodology for exploring its relationship to postoperative mortality, accepting the inherent limitations of observational research.

We agree that the Surgical Preparedness Index (SPI) would benefit from further validation. Whilst our dataset included patients from 1589 hospitals across 102 countries, participating hospitals self-selected, so our data may not be fully generalisable. Validation at a national-level would help health systems to better understand how preparedness impacts clinical outcomes.

Although contemporaneous SPI assessment data were used for most hospitals, to maximise data completeness, older SPI measurements were used for some hospitals where contemporaneous data were not available. In those instances, it is possible that SPI scores may have changed over time. However, few hospitals improved their preparedness over the study period,2 so a temporal mismatch between exposure and outcome measurement is unlikely to have biased our results. We encourage health systems to continue to prospectively track SPI to better understand shifts in preparedness over time.

Despite these limitations, the SPI remains the best available metric to measure hospital preparedness and guide initiatives to strengthen it. Further research to better characterise and improve hospital preparedness is dependent on ongoing prioritisation and funding by governments.
