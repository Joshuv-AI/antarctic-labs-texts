On some mathematical problems for open quantum systems with varying particle number
Benedikt M. Reible, Luigi Delle Site

Abstract
. We derive the effective Hamiltonian H − μ ​ N H-\mu N for open quantum systems with varying particle number from first principles within the framework of non-relativistic quantum statistical mechanics. We prove that under physically motivated assumptions regarding the size of the system and the range of the interaction, this form of the Hamiltonian is unique up to a constant. Our argument relies firstly on establishing a rigorous version of the surface-to-volume ratio approximation, which is routinely used in an empirical form in statistical mechanics, and secondly on showing that the Hilbert space for systems with varying particle number must be isomorphic to Fock space. Together, these findings provide a rigorous mathematical justification for the standard grand canonical formalism employed in statistical physics.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Prolegomenon

Realistic quantum many-body systems are never truly isolated but rather exchange energy and possibly matter with their surroundings. Due to their practical relevance, for example, in the development of modern quantum technological devices, the theory of open quantum systems is a cornerstone of present-day theoretical and applied research
[
3
,
60
,
70
]
. While there are different elaborate mathematical frameworks in which to study these systems, e.g., the theory of operator algebras
[
25
]
or Markovian quantum master equations
[
45
]
, numerous challenges regarding their theoretical description remain, especially for systems with varying particle number
[
18
]
. A deeper mathematical understanding of these systems is, however, not only desirable from a purely mathematical point of view, but it can also help, at first instance, to improve current state-of-the-art simulation techniques, which, in turn, can lead to new directions for the experimental realization of quantum systems for technology
[
22
]
.

For an open quantum system with varying particle number, it is commonly accepted that the system’s Hilbert space is given by Fock space and that its Hamiltonian
H
should be extended by the term
-\mu N
, with
\mu
the chemical potential and
N
the particle number operator, resulting in the
effective Hamiltonian

(1)

H-\mu N\ .

In the physics literature, this operator usually appears implicitly in the derivation of the grand canonical density and, based on this, is defined to be the appropriate Hamiltonian for the open system (see, e.g., Ref.
[
43
, p. 29]
); the operator (
1
) itself, however, is not derived from first principles. The use of an effective Hamiltonian of the form (
1
) can be traced back at least to a conjecture by Bogoliubov in the field of condensed matter physics
[
7
]
, where it has been used ever since; see, for example, Ref.
[
73
, p. 314]
and references therein. Also in mathematical physics, it has become common practice to analyze functional-analytic properties of the operator (
1
) and to build physical models with its help; see, e.g., Ref.
[
8
, passim]
.

Recently, a semi-empirical derivation of
Eq.

1
was proposed, obtaining the effective Hamiltonian for an open system by tracing out the degrees of freedom of the reservoir from the von Neumann equation of the combined system
[
23
]
. While this study put forward important ideas (on which we will actually build our analysis), the approach also relied on some empirical assertions and formal manipulations. The goal of the present article is to provide a fully rigorous derivation of the effective Hamiltonian (
1
) from first principles — starting from the total Hamiltonian of an open system coupled to a reservoir, introducing basic physical assumptions, and finally showing how the chemical potential
\mu
emerges. In addition, we will argue that the form of the effective Hamiltonian is unique up to additive constants. This will bridge the gap between studies concerned with the mathematical foundations of quantum mechanics, i.e., those dealing with general Hamiltonians and their properties, and studies focusing on specific mathematical problems for grand canonical systems, where the Hamiltonian (
1
) is taken for granted.

1.2.
Main Results

To derive an expression like (
1
), we will have to analyze the general mathematical structure of an open quantum system with varying particle number in a broader context and establish some preparatory propositions.

Our first result (
Proposition

3.4
) provides a mathematically rigorous foundation for the so-called surface-to-volume ratio approximation. The latter is commonly used in statistical mechanics when deriving the canonical or grand canonical density operator; it consists of neglecting the interaction term
H_{\mathrm{int}}
in the total Hamiltonian
H_{\mathsf{S}}\otimes\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Id}_{\mathsf{R}}+\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Id}_{\mathsf{S}}\otimes H_{\mathsf{R}}+H_{\mathrm{int}}
for a system–reservoir composite. Deferring the details to
Section

3
, we will show that:

(I)

Under some physically justified assumptions on the system–reservoir interaction and the relative size of the open system compared to the reservoir, it holds that
\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[H_{\mathsf{S}}\otimes\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Id}_{\mathsf{R}}+\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Id}_{\mathsf{S}}\otimes H_{\mathsf{R}}+H_{\mathrm{int}}]\approx\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[H_{\mathsf{S}}\otimes\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Id}_{\mathsf{R}}+\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Id}_{\mathsf{S}}\otimes H_{\mathsf{R}}]
, up to higher-order terms in the effective interaction distance.

The proof of this assertion is geometric in nature, relying on certain results from convex geometry, and it highlights that a negligible surface-to-volume ratio is indeed required for the validity of the approximation.

A varying particle number will become relevant for our second result (
Proposition

4.2
), which is independent of
(I)
. It provides a physically motivated and mathematically rigorous argument for the necessity of Fock space when describing quantum systems with the aforementioned characteristic:

(II)

If there exists a total particle number operator with physically reasonable properties (pure point spectrum and
n
-particle eigenspaces), then the kinematical structure of the system is given in terms of Fock space.

Our proof of this statement in
Section

4
relies on standard methods from spectral theory as well as a recent, very general result on direct sums in
W^{\ast}
-categories. As discussed in detail in
4.3

1
, assertion
(II)
should be seen as complementing similar results in the literature on the Fock representation.

Based on the previous two results
(I)
and
(II)
, we will be able to prove our main result (
Theorem

5.1
) regarding the effective Hamiltonian in
Section

5
and show that the form (
1
) of this operator, in the specific physical setting considered, is unique up to a constant:

(III)

If the surface-to-volume ratio approximation can be applied and the particle number is varying, then
H_{\mathsf{S}}\otimes\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Id}_{\mathsf{R}}+\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Id}_{\mathsf{S}}\otimes H_{\mathsf{R}}\approx(H_{\mathsf{S}}-\mu N)\otimes\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Id}_{\mathsf{R}}
, up to higher-order terms in the particle number of the open system.

1.3.
Outline of the paper

After introducing the general mathematical setup and the basic physical assumptions in
Section

2
, we first discuss some preliminary observations on computing expectation values of unbounded operators with respect to mixed quantum states (density operators). In
Section

3
, we introduce specific assumptions on the system–reservoir interaction, establish an auxiliary geometric result concerning quantitative bounds for the interaction volume, and then prove the validity of the surface-to-volume ratio approximation. Next,
Section

4
introduces the assumption of a varying particle number in terms of the existence of a particle number operator, and we show that this necessarily leads to Fock space. Finally, in
Section

5
we synthesize these results in order to derive the effective Hamiltonian (
1
). As a corollary, we show that from this Hamiltonian one obtains an equivalent of the hierarchy of von Neumann equations derived in Ref.
[
23
]
.

Remarks 2.1
.

(1)

Starting with the total system

(2)

Assuming that the open system is confined to a bounded region is necessary in order to ensure that it is thermodynamically stable at positive temperature

Interactions

The interaction between particles within the open system as well as between particles from the open system and the reservoir is of two-body type, meaning that for all pairs

Example 2.3

In the standard mathematical model of non-relativistic quantum mechanics

2.3.
Density operators

Let
\mathscr{S}(\mathcal{H})
denote the set of all density operators on some Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, that is, the set of all positive trace-class operators
\rho\in\mathscr{B}_{1}(\mathcal{H})
satisfying
\tr_{\mathcal{H}}(\rho)=1
. It is well-known (see, e.g., Ref.
[
6
, Thm. 26.9]
) that
\rho\in\mathscr{S}(\mathcal{H})
if and only if there exist (i) a sequence
(\alpha_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq[0,1]
with
\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\alpha_{n}=1
and (ii) an orthonormal basis
(e_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
(ONB for short) such that

\rho=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{n}P_{e_{n}}\ ,

where
P_{e_{n}}\coloneqq\mathinner{|{e_{n}}\rangle}\!\mathinner{\langle{e_{n}}|}
denotes the projection onto the one-dimensional subspace spanned by the vector
e_{n}
, that is, the operator
P_{e_{n}}\xi\coloneqq\langle{e_{n},\xi}\rangle e_{n}
(
\xi\in\mathcal{H}
).

While for bounded operators
A\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{H})
the expression
\tr(A\rho)
is always well-defined since
\mathscr{B}_{1}(\mathcal{H})
is a two-sided ideal in
\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{H})
, this need not be the case for unbounded
T\in\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{H})
due to domain issues. In the literature, there are different necessary and sufficient conditions ensuring that
T\rho\in\mathscr{B}_{1}(\mathcal{H})
; see, for example, Refs.
[
47
, Prop. 11.27]
and
[
48
, Prop. 4.59]
. In this paper, we will use a more direct approach, inspired by Ref.
[
42
, Eq. (3.1.26)]
, to make sense of expressions of the form
\tr(T\rho)
for unbounded
T
.

Definition 2.4

(Compatible density operators)
.

Let
T\in\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{H})
be self-adjoint. Define

\mathscr{S}(\mathcal{H},T)\coloneqq\left\{\rho\in\mathscr{S}(\mathcal{H})\ :\ \parbox{240.00037pt}{\centering$\exists\,(e_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathrm{dom}(T)\ \exists\,(\alpha_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq[0,1]:$ \\
(i) $(e_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ is an ONB for $\mathcal{H}$ and $\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\alpha_{n}=1$; \\
(ii) $\rho=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\alpha_{n}P_{e_{n}}$; (iii) $\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\alpha_{n}|{\langle{e_{n},Te_{n}}\rangle}|<+\infty$\@add@centering}\right\}

and call the elements of this set
T
-compatible density operators
. Furthermore, for any
\rho\in\mathscr{S}(\mathcal{H},T)
define the quantity
\tr(T\rho)
by

(7)

\tr(T\rho)\coloneqq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{n}\langle{e_{n},Te_{n}}\rangle\ .

Note that since the series on the right-hand side of (
7
) converges absolutely by assumption,
\tr(T\rho)
is a real number, interpreted as the expectation value of
T
in the mixed state
\rho
. We will, therefore, also use the notation

\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[T]\coloneqq\tr(T\rho)\ .

In
Appendix

T
-compatible density operators is meaningful by establishing that the value of
\tr(T\rho)
does not depend on the representation of
\rho\in\mathscr{S}(\mathcal{H},T)
, and that for suitable
T
there holds
\mathscr{S}(\mathcal{H},T)\neq\varnothing
.

Denote by
\tr_{\mathsf{R}}:\mathscr{B}_{1}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathsf{S}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\mathsf{R}})\to\mathscr{B}_{1}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathsf{S}})
the partial trace operation on
\mathcal{H}_{\mathsf{T}}

, that is, for every trace-class operator

is the unique trace-class operator on

which satisfies for all

[
5
]
,
[
6

\tr_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathsf{S}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\mathsf{R}}}\bigl((A\otimes\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Id}_{\mathsf{R}})\rho\bigr)=\tr_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathsf{S}}}\bigl(A\tr_{\mathsf{R}}(\rho)\bigr)\ .

Below, we will need this identity also for unbounded operators. With the help of

Lemma 2.5

Let

be self-adjoint and
\rho\in\mathscr{S}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathsf{S}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\mathsf{R}},T\otimes\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Id}_{\mathsf{R}})

3.
Surface-to-volume ratio approximation

When discussing the division of a large system into two subsystems in statistical mechanics, the interaction between these subsystems (that is, the operator
H_{\mathrm{int}}
in our case) is usually neglected based on the reason that it represents surface effects, which are negligible compared to the bulk energies of the subsystems; see, for example, Ref.
[
38
, pp. 131, 149]
as well as Ref.
[
63
, pp. 38, 50]
. This is expressed mathematically as

(11)

H_{\mathsf{T}}\approx H_{\mathsf{S}}+H_{\mathsf{R}}\ ,

with the comment that the operator
H_{\mathrm{int}}
, while necessary for the equilibration of the two subsystems
\mathsf{S}
and
\mathsf{R}
, is
“negligibly small”
compared to the operators
H_{\mathsf{S}}
and
H_{\mathsf{R}}

[
63
, p. 50]
;
Eq.

11
is known as the
surface-to-volume ratio approximation

[
38
, p. 131]
.

Since the involved operators
H_{\mathsf{S}}
,
H_{\mathsf{R}}
, and
H_{\mathrm{int}}
are unbounded, in general, one cannot simply compare their norms to determine whether
H_{\mathrm{int}}
is small compared to
H_{\mathsf{S}}
and
H_{\mathsf{R}}
. Therefore, in the following, we shall make the approximation (
11
) mathematically precise in a framework that can handle unbounded operators, and we will see in what specific sense
H_{\mathrm{int}}
can be neglected compared to
H_{\mathsf{S}}
and
H_{\mathsf{R}}
. Our proof will also show that a negligibly small surface-to-volume ratio is indeed crucial for the validity of the approximation.

3.1.
Short-range interactions

As a first step towards a rigorous version of (

3.2.
Volume of the interaction corridor

The main technical ingredient for the surface-to-volume ratio approximation is the following lemma, which provides an estimate for the volume of the interaction corridor
\varGamma_{2\delta}(\varOmega_{\mathsf{S}})
in terms of the surface area of the boundary
\partial\varOmega_{\mathsf{S}}
and the interaction distance
\delta
; its proof relies on established results in convex geometry that are summarized in
Appendix

B
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Assume that
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a bounded convex domain. Then the volume of the corridor
\varGamma_{2\delta}(K)
is bounded above and below by

(13)

\delta\cdot\mathrm{area}(\partial K)+\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2})\leq\mathrm{vol}\bigl(\varGamma_{2\delta}(K)\bigr)\leq 2\delta\cdot\mathrm{area}(\partial K)+\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2})\quad\text{for}\quad\delta\to 0\ .

Proof.

First, note that using Definition
B.1
of the tubular
\delta
-neighborhood
K_{\delta}
of the set
K
, we may express
(\partial K)_{\delta}^{+}
equivalently as
(\partial K)_{\delta}^{+}=K_{\delta}\setminus K
. Since the outer and inner tubular neighborhoods of
K
are disjoint, it follows from additivity of the Lebesgue measure that

(14)

\mathrm{vol}\bigl(\varGamma_{2\delta}(K)\bigr)=\mathrm{vol}\bigl((\partial K)_{\delta}^{+}\cup(\partial K)_{\delta}^{-}\bigr)=\mathrm{vol}\bigl(K_{\delta}\setminus K\bigr)+\mathrm{vol}\bigl((\partial K)_{\delta}^{-}\bigr)\ .

We shall consider both of the terms separately. For the first one, observe that since
K\subseteq K_{\delta}
and
\mathrm{vol}(K)<+\infty
, we have
\mathrm{vol}(K_{\delta}\setminus K)=\mathrm{vol}(K_{\delta})-\mathrm{vol}(K)
. Thus, we can apply the Minkowski-Steiner formula (
Theorem

B.3
in
Appendix

B
) to obtain

(15)

\mathrm{vol}\bigl(K_{\delta}\setminus K\bigr)=\mathrm{area}(\partial K)\,\delta+\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2})\quad\text{for}\quad\delta\to 0\ .

For the second term, note that by definition, one has
(\partial K)_{\delta}^{-}\subseteq\{{x\in K\,:\,\mathrm{dist}(x,\partial K)\leq\delta}\}
. Therefore, monotonicity of the Lebesgue measure and
Lemma

B.6
imply

\mathrm{vol}\bigl((\partial K)_{\delta}^{-}\bigr)\leq\mathrm{vol}\bigl(\{{x\in K\,:\,\mathrm{dist}(x,\partial K)\leq\delta}\}\bigr)\leq\mathrm{area}(\partial K)\,\delta\ .

Combining the last three equations proves the upper bound. For the lower bound, it suffices to note that
\mathrm{vol}(\varGamma_{2\delta}(K))\geq\mathrm{vol}(K_{\delta}\setminus K)
by
Eq.

14
, hence the estimate follows from (
15
).
∎

4.
Varying particle number and Fock space

In the next step, we want to analyze some immediate consequences of the additional physical feature that the total particle number in the open system
\mathsf{S}
is varying. Throughout the literature, it is usually taken for granted that in this case the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{\mathsf{S}}
is equal to Fock space
[
28
,
15
]
; see, for example, the textbooks
[
35
,
61
]
which are among the most rigorous in their field. In the following, we will provide a conceptually simple proof, relying on a technically involved result, of the fact that under physically well-motivated assumptions, the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{\mathsf{S}}
must indeed be isomorphic to Fock space, i.e., the kinematical structure of
\mathsf{S}
is necessarily given in terms of Fock space.

4.1.
Particle number operator

To start, we will pose another key physical assumption, which implements the feature of a varying particle number in terms of a number operator with specific properties.

Assumption A6

(Varying particle number)
.

Suppose that the open system
\mathsf{S}
consists of identical particles, let
\mathcal{H}
denote the one-particle Hilbert space of this species, and assume that the total particle number
N_{\mathsf{S}}\in\mathbb{N}
of
\mathsf{S}
is varying. Then we pose that

A6.1.

\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{\mathsf{S}}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
with isometric embedding. (We set
\mathcal{H}^{\otimes 0}\coloneqq\mathbb{C}
.)

According to the standard description of quantum mechanics, the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}\coloneqq\bigotimes^{n}\mathcal{H}
is the state space for an
n
-particle system (recall
Remark

2.2
in this context), hence the space
\mathcal{H}_{\mathsf{S}}
must contain all these to correctly describe all possible states with different particle numbers. The embedding should be isometric in order to guarantee that a pure state (that is, a normalized vector) in
\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}
is also a pure state in
\mathcal{H}_{\mathsf{S}}
. Second, we pose:

A6.2.

There exists a self-adjoint operator
\mathscr{N}\in\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathsf{S}})
such that (i)
\sigma(\mathscr{N})=\sigma_{\mathrm{p}}(\mathscr{N})=\mathbb{N}_{0}
and (ii)
\mathrm{Eig}(\mathscr{N},n)=\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

Since the particle number is not merely a parameter anymore but a physical observable, there should be a self-adjoint operator representing this observable whose spectral values are precisely the different possible particle numbers and which yields the number
n
on the
n
-particle subspace
\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}
.

Remark 4.1
.

In ordinary, non-relativistic quantum matter, the particle number operator is typically defined in terms of creation and annihilation operators, and the latter are used to generate and remove
excitations
of physical particles whose number within the open system is fixed, but not to exchange the particles themselves with the reservoir. A truly varying particle number usually appears in quantum field theory, where the creation and annihilation of elementary particles due to vacuum fluctuations is described. In light of these two situations mostly treated in the literature, it must be emphasized that we treat existing physical particles, which constitute actual matter and not merely excitations, that can enter and exit the open system, but are not created or destroyed as in quantum field theory. For a more detailed discussion of the difference between the exchange of excitations and the exchange of physical particles in the context of the Lindblad equation, we refer to Ref.
[
53
]
.

Based on the physically motivated
A6
, we obtain the following result; its proof relies on a very general characterization of infinite direct sums of Hilbert spaces in terms of a universal property
[
30
, Thm. 5.1]
, which is recalled in
Appendix

C
.

Proposition 4.2

(Necessity of Fock space)
.

Assume that the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{\mathsf{S}}
satisfies the assumptions
A6.1
and
A6.2
. Then it follows that

(22)

\mathcal{H}_{\mathsf{S}}=\bigoplus_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}\ .

Proof.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, let
i_{n}:\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{H}_{\mathsf{S}}
,
\xi\mapsto i_{n}(\xi)
, denote the inclusion mapping, which is a well-defined isometry according to
A6.1
, that is,
\|{i_{n}(\xi)}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathsf{S}}}=\|{\xi}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}}
. Using polarization, one obtains
\langle{i_{n}(\xi),i_{n}(\eta)}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathsf{S}}}=\langle{\xi,\eta}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}}
for all
\xi,\eta\in\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}
, and this implies
i_{n}^{\ast}i_{n}=\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Id}_{\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}}

[
14
, Prop. II.2.17]
. Moreover, for
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
with
n\neq m
and
\xi\in\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}
,
\eta\in\mathcal{H}^{\otimes m}
, we have

\big\langle{\xi,i_{n}^{\ast}i_{m}(\eta)}\big\rangle_{\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}}=\big\langle{i_{n}(\xi),i_{m}(\eta)}\big\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathsf{S}}}=0

because
\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}=\mathrm{Eig}(\mathscr{N},n)\perp\mathrm{Eig}(\mathscr{N},m)=\mathcal{H}^{\otimes m}
by
A6.2
, since the eigenspaces of a self-adjoint operator are pairwise orthogonal
[
62
, Lem. 3.4]
. This implies that
i_{n}^{\ast}i_{m}=0
for
n\neq m
because
\xi,\eta
were arbitrary; hence, in total, we have for all
n,m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
:

(23)

i_{n}^{\ast}i_{m}=\delta_{nm}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Id}_{\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}}\ .

Define the operators
P_{n}\coloneqq i_{n}i_{n}^{\ast}\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathsf{S}})
,
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, and note that
P_{n}^{\ast}=P_{n}
and
P_{n}^{2}=P_{n}
by (
23
), showing that the
P_{n}
are orthogonal projections. Let
\xi\in\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}
be arbitrary. It holds that
P_{n}i_{n}(\xi)=i_{n}i_{n}^{\ast}i_{n}(\xi)=i_{n}(\xi)
, so
i_{n}(\xi)\in\mathrm{ran}(P_{n})
which implies
\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}\subseteq\mathrm{ran}(P_{n})
in
\mathcal{H}_{\mathsf{S}}
. Next, let
\varPhi\in(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n})^{\perp}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{\mathsf{S}}
, i.e.,
\langle{\varPhi,i_{n}(\xi)}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathsf{S}}}=0
for all
\xi\in\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}
. If
\varPsi\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathsf{S}}
, it follows that

\big\langle{\varPsi,P_{n}\varPhi}\big\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathsf{S}}}=\big\langle{i_{n}i_{n}^{\ast}(\varPsi),\varPhi}\big\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathsf{S}}}=0\ ,

and thus, since
\varPsi
was arbitrary,
\varPhi\in\ker(P_{n})
which implies
(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n})^{\perp}\subseteq\ker(P_{n})
. As
P_{n}
is an orthogonal projection,
\ker(P_{n})=\mathrm{ran}(P_{n})^{\perp}
and hence
(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n})^{\perp}\subseteq\mathrm{ran}(P_{n})^{\perp}
. This implies that if
\varPsi\in\mathrm{ran}(P_{n})
, then
\varPsi\perp\varPhi
for all
\varPhi\in(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n})^{\perp}
and so
\varPsi\in(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n})^{\perp\perp}=\overline{\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}}=\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}
since
\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}
is a closed subspace of
\mathcal{H}_{\mathsf{S}}
(as this space is complete and
i_{n}
is an isometry). We conclude that
\mathrm{ran}(P_{n})\subseteq\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}
; hence, we have equality
\mathrm{ran}(P_{n})=\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}
. Therefore,
P_{n}
must be the unique orthogonal projection onto the closed subspace
\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{\mathsf{S}}
.

Let
E_{\mathscr{N}}
denote the spectral measure of the self-adjoint operator
\mathscr{N}
. It holds that
E_{\mathscr{N}}(\{{n}\})
,
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, is the orthogonal projection in
\mathcal{H}_{\mathsf{S}}
onto the space
\mathrm{Eig}(\mathscr{N},n)=\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}

[
62
, Prop. 5.10]
, and thus the previous result implies that
E_{\mathscr{N}}(\{{n}\})=P_{n}=i_{n}i_{n}^{\ast}
. From countable additivity of the spectral measure, we can now derive the following identity:

(24)

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}i_{n}i_{n}^{\ast}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}E_{\mathscr{N}}\bigl(\{{n}\}\bigr)=E_{\mathscr{N}}\biggl(\,\bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty}\{{n}\}\biggr)=E_{\mathscr{N}}\bigl(\sigma(\mathscr{N})\bigr)=\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Id}_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathsf{S}}}\ .

In light of
Eqs.

23
and
24
, we may invoke the universal property of the Hilbert space direct sum, see
Theorem

C.1
in
Appendix

C
, to conclude that
\mathcal{H}_{S}
must be the direct sum of the spaces
\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}
, which proves the claim.
∎

Remarks 4.3
.

(1)

Qualitatively, the assertion of
Proposition

4.2
may be summarized as follows: if there exists a total particle number operator on
\mathcal{H}_{\mathsf{S}}
, then
\mathcal{H}_{\mathsf{S}}
has to be the Fock space. In this context, it must be noted that the connection between the existence of a number operator and Fock space has been studied intensively in the literature on representations of the canonical (anti-)commutation relations
[
12
,
13
,
16
,
20
,
21
,
29
,
34
,
72
]
(references in alphabetical order); see, in particular, Ref.
[
12
, pp. 23–25]
for a summary of the various results and also the textbook accounts
[
8
, pp. 30 ff.]
and
[
67
, pp. 21 f.]
. In essence, these studies showed that an operator of the form
\mathscr{N}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}a_{k}^{\ast}a_{k}
, where
a_{k}^{\ast}
and
a_{k}
are the creation and annihilation operators, respectively, exists only in a direct sum of Fock representations of the canonical (anti-)commutation relations. (Different mathematical interpretations of the convergence of the series
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}a_{k}^{\ast}a_{k}
of unbounded operators as well as alternative definitions of a number operator were investigated in Refs.
[
12
,
13
,
20
]
.)

The novelty of
Proposition

4.2
compared to the list of references given above, aside from the one already discussed in
Remark

4.1
, is that it did neither require the abstract mathematical framework of algebraic quantum field theory nor a mathematically involved definition of the number operator in terms of creation and annihilation operators; in fact, the characterization of a total particle number operator as in
A6.2
is mathematically simple but also physically very well motivated. Moreover, relying on a category-theoretic result for the characterization of the infinite direct sum of Hilbert spaces (cf.
Appendix

C
) connects the physical assumptions
A6.1
and
A6.2
with the mathematically fundamental universal property of the direct sum.

(2)

In recent years, there has been increased interest in the physical feature of a varying particle number in the framework of classical mechanics; see, for example, Refs.
[
18
,
19
,
24
,
40
]
and references therein. Hence, it is worthwhile to discuss
Proposition

4.2
in this context. Since there is no canonical notion of a classical Fock space, one has to model the configuration space of a classical system with varying particle number in terms of a hierarchy of hyperplanes of configuration spaces with fixed particle number. These hyperplanes are connected by dynamical transition probabilities (free energy), which are determined based on statistical mechanics considerations. Thus,
Proposition

4.2
shows that in quantum mechanics the mathematical framework for systems with varying particle number is more compact, a fact that is also reflected by
Corollary

5.3
below. However, it must be noted that Ref.
[

]
put forward the proposal to use a Fock space formalism in a classical framework, suitably adapted to the physical situation, to model particle-based reaction–diffusion processes.

.

of

is varying and bounded above by

, then the Hilbert space of

is given by the finite direct sum

\mathcal{H}_{\mathsf{S}}=\bigoplus_{n=0}^{M}\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}\ .

Proof.

Under the additional assumption on
N_{\mathsf{S}}
, it follows that
A6.1
and
A6.2
must be modified by replacing the set
\mathbb{N}_{0}
with
\{{0,1,\dotsc,M}\}
. The proof then proceeds as above by invoking

from

with index set

.

changes due to the transition from the

Recall the general definition of the Hilbert space direct sum, provided in

34
in

, and for every
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
let
H_{\mathsf{S},n}\in\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n})

\mathsf{S}
, that is,
H_{\mathsf{S},n}
is given by

5
and
6
, with

replaced by

. The appropriate Hamiltonian for

\mathscr{H}\in\mathscr{L}\bigl(\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{H})\bigr)
defined on the domain

\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{H})\coloneqq\left\{{\bigl(\psi^{(n)}\bigr)_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{H})\ :\ \psi^{(n)}\in\mathrm{dom}(H_{\mathsf{S},n})\ \text{and}\ \sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\big\|{H_{\mathsf{S},n}\psi^{(n)}}\big\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}}^{2}<+\infty}\right\}\ ,

and it acts on an element
\varPsi=(\psi^{(n)})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\in\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{H})
by
\mathscr{H}\varPsi\coloneqq(H_{\mathsf{S},n}\psi^{(n)})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}

\mathscr{N}=\bigoplus_{n=0}^{\infty}\bigl(n\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Id}_{\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}}\bigr)\ .

mediating the system–reservoir interaction does not play a role anymore due to

\mathscr{S}_{\mathrm{adm}}^{(2)}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathsf{T}})\coloneqq\mathscr{S}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathsf{T}},\mathscr{H}\otimes\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Id}_{\mathsf{R}})\cap\mathscr{S}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathsf{T}},\mathscr{N}\otimes\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Id}_{\mathsf{R}})\cap\mathscr{S}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathsf{T}},\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Id}_{\mathsf{S}}\otimes H_{\mathsf{R}})\ .

5.
The effective Hamiltonian

We have established a rigorous version of the surface-to-volume ratio approximation (
Proposition

3.4
) as well as the necessity of Fock space for systems with varying physical particle number (
Proposition

4.2
). Based on these results, we can now derive the effective Hamiltonian for the open system
\mathsf{S}
and argue that, given the necessary assumptions and relevant approximations, it is unique up to a constant.

5.1.
Main result

In the sequel, we will build on ideas put forward in Ref.
[
23
]
, where the same effective Hamiltonian was derived based on semi-empirical assertions, but we will follow a different, more rigorous approach; see
5.2
below for a detailed discussion of the differences of the two approaches.

Theorem 5.1

(Effective Hamiltonian)
.

Suppose that the surface-to-volume ratio approximation has been applied to the total system
\mathsf{T}
, and that the open system
\mathsf{S}
satisfies
A6
. Set
\varepsilon\coloneqq N_{\mathsf{S}}/N
. Then there exists a constant
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
such that

(25)

H_{\mathsf{T}}=(\mathscr{H}-\mu\mathscr{N})\otimes\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Id}_{\mathsf{R}}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2})\ .

The operator
\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}\coloneqq\mathscr{H}-\mu\mathscr{N}\in\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathsf{S}})
is called the
effective Hamiltonian
for the open system
\mathsf{S}
, and it holds that this form of the open system Hamiltonian is unique up to additive constants.

Proof.

Let
\rho\in\mathscr{S}_{\mathrm{adm}}^{(2)}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathsf{T}})
be an admissible density operator for the total system and set
E_{\mathsf{T}}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[H_{\mathsf{T}}]
. According to the surface-to-volume ratio approximation (
17
), we have

(26)

\displaystyle\begin{split}E_{\mathsf{T}}&\approx\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[\mathscr{H}\otimes\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Id}_{\mathsf{R}}+\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Id}_{\mathsf{S}}\otimes H_{\mathsf{R}}]\\
&=\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[\mathscr{H}\otimes\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Id}_{\mathsf{R}}]+\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Id}_{\mathsf{S}}\otimes H_{\mathsf{R}}]=\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{\mathsf{S}}}[\mathscr{H}]+\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{\mathsf{R}}}[H_{\mathsf{R}}]\end{split}

up to terms of order
\delta^{4}
, where
\rho_{\mathsf{R}}\coloneqq\tr_{\mathsf{S}}(\rho)
denotes the partial trace of the state
\rho
with respect to the open system and
Lemma

2.5
was used in the last step.

Let
E_{\mathsf{R}}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{\mathsf{R}}}[H_{\mathsf{R}}]
be the expected energy of the reservoir. The central physical observation for our proof, which was the key modeling assumption in Ref.
[
23
, p. 5]
, is the following:
E_{\mathsf{R}}
is a function of the number of particles
N_{\mathsf{R}}
of the reservoir, given by

Remarks 5.2
.

(1)

The crucial element in the proof of
Theorem

5.1
is to consider
E_{\mathsf{R}}=\tr_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathsf{R}}}(H_{\mathsf{R}}\rho_{\mathsf{R}})
as a function of
N-N_{\mathsf{S}}
, and then to treat
N_{\mathsf{S}}
as a small perturbation of the much larger
N
. This permits the expansion of
E_{\mathsf{R}}
in a Taylor series, which effectively corresponds to a coarse graining of the microscopic model of the reservoir, as only a first-order response of
\mathsf{R}
to changes in the particle number of
\mathsf{S}
is kept in the resulting expression. This is the reason that in the final result (
25
) for the Hamiltonian
H_{\mathsf{T}}
of the total system, the operator
H_{\mathsf{R}}
does not appear anymore; the influence of the reservoir on the system is contained in the effective Hamiltonian
\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}=\mathscr{H}-\mu\mathscr{N}
through the chemical potential
\mu
due to
Eq.

28
.

(2)

In order to emphasize the range of validity of the expression (
25
), we will recapitulate the necessary assumptions that were invoked during its derivation. First, we implicitly used (i) the assumption of varying particle number and its consequence,
Proposition

4.2
, to situate ourselves in Fock space. Next, we employed (ii) the surface-to-volume ratio approximation,
Eq.

17
, to rewrite
\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[H_{\mathsf{T}}]
in terms of the operator
H_{\mathsf{S}}\otimes\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Id}_{\mathsf{R}}+\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Id}_{\mathsf{S}}\otimes H_{\mathsf{R}}
. Thereafter, to arrive at the intermediate result (
29
), we had to assume that (iii) the open system is macroscopically large, yet significantly smaller than the reservoir, and that (iv) the systems
\mathsf{S}
and
\mathsf{R}
have reached thermodynamic equilibrium. Without any of these four assumptions,
Eq.

25
is no longer valid. In fact, step (ii) is the reason why we could introduce the chemical potential in the first place. Without this approximation,
\mu
cannot be defined as in
Eq.

28
because it is no longer a constant (in particular,
\mu_{\mathsf{S}}\neq\mu_{\mathsf{R}}
) since if the open system is too small, then the exchange of particles with the reservoir drastically changes the physics of
\mathsf{S}
(in particular,
\mu_{\mathsf{S}}
changes), and, thus, higher-order terms in the expansion (
27
) become relevant; for example, the second-order term involves the change of the chemical potential with respect to the number of particles:

\left.\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{2}E_{\mathsf{R}}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}N_{\mathsf{R}}^{2}}\right|_{N_{\mathsf{R}}=N}=\left.\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu_{\mathsf{R}}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}N_{\mathsf{R}}}\right|_{N_{\mathsf{R}}=N}\ .

More generally, one can show that if the interaction
H_{\mathrm{int}}
becomes important for the physics of the system, then one cannot avoid higher-order terms for
\mu_{\mathsf{S}}

[
46
]
. Assumption (iii), in connection with the approximation (ii), entails that the reservoir is large enough so that a change of the number of particles in
\mathsf{S}
does not affect the physics of
\mathsf{R}
; in other words, the reservoir behaves as if it had a fixed particle number (see Ref.
[
23
]
for a thorough discussion of this point). Finally, regarding assumption (iv), it is important to point out that if the two systems were not in equilibrium, then
\mu
would not be the chemical potential of
\mathsf{S}
but only of
\mathsf{R}
, and, again, higher-order terms in the expansion (
27
) would become relevant.

(3)

Let us now discuss the difference of our present approach to derive the effective Hamiltonian (
25
) compared to the previous study, Ref.
[
23
]
. In the latter, a Hamiltonian of the form
H_{\mathsf{S},n}-n\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Id}
was extracted from a hierarchy of von Neumann equations for
n
-particle density operators
\rho_{n}
, namely
\mathrm{i}\hbar\dot{\rho}_{n}=[H_{\mathsf{S},n}-n\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Id},\rho_{n}]
for
n\in\mathbb{N}

[
23
, Eq. (6)]
. To obtain this hierarchy, the framework of bounded operators was used, the surface-to-volume ratio approximation (in its heuristic physical form) and the Born-Markov approximation were employed, the reservoir degrees of freedom were traced out of the von Neumann equation for the total system, and, finally, an expansion like (
27
) was performed.

Thus, the advantages of the new approach are the following: It does not require the von Neumann equation to derive a Hamiltonian but rather stays on the operatorial level; the involved operators are not assumed to be bounded, which increases generality; the effective Hamiltonian is derived in Fock space, which, according to
Proposition

4.2
, is the natural setting for systems with varying particle number and which also avoids dealing with a hierarchy of equations; and, finally, by relying on
Proposition

3.4
and using an expansion in the form (
27
), the approximation leading to (
25
) is more controlled. We will show below in
Corollary

5.3
that our result also reproduces the von Neumann equation found in Ref.
[
23
]
.

(4)

Lastly, we will comment on how
Theorem

5.1
complements the standard derivation of the grand canonical density operator in statistical mechanics (as found, for example, in Ref.
[
63
, Sect. 2.7.5]
). In the latter, the Hamiltonian
\mathscr{H}-\mu\mathscr{N}
appears in the exponent of the density operator after the reservoir has been traced out and an expansion in the variable

similar to (
27
) has been performed. In light of this, our result shows that this operator appearing in the grand canonical density is, in the specific physical regime staked out by the assumptions and approximations, the natural and only Hamiltonian that describes the open system. Moreover, as the following result illustrates, the grand canonical density operator can be recovered from the effective Hamiltonian as well.

5.2.
Grand-canonical von Neumann equation

As an application of
Theorem

5.1
, we show that the effective Hamiltonian (
25
) leads to a von Neumann eqauation which, in similar form, was derived in Ref.
[
23
]
.

Corollary 5.3
.

In the setting of
Theorem

5.1
, suppose that the
Born-Markov approximation
can be applied, i.e., for all
t\geq 0
the time-dependent density operator
\rho(t)
of the total system may be written as
\rho(t)=\rho_{1}(t)\otimes\rho_{2}(0)
for
\rho_{1}(t)\in\mathscr{S}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathsf{S}})
and
\rho_{2}(0)\in\mathscr{S}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathsf{R}})
. Then the time evolution of
\rho_{1}(t)
is governed by the following von Neumann equation:

\mathrm{i}\hbar\,\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho_{1}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}=\bigl[\mathscr{H}-\mu\mathscr{N},\rho_{1}\bigr]+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2})\ .

In particular, if
\mathrm{e}^{-\beta(\mathscr{H}-\mu\mathscr{N})}\in\mathscr{B}_{1}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathsf{S}})
, then a stationary solution of this equation is given by the well-known grand canonical density operator

\rho_{\mathrm{GC}}=\frac{1}{Z_{\mathrm{GC}}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\beta(\mathscr{H}-\mu\mathscr{N})}\quad\text{with}\quad Z_{\mathrm{GC}}=\tr_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathsf{S}}}\bigl(\mathrm{e}^{-\beta(\mathscr{H}-\mu\mathscr{N})}\bigr)\ .

Proof.

Let
\xi\in\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}})\cap\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}\rho_{1}(t))
for all
t\geq 0
and
\eta\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathsf{R}}
be arbitrary. Since
H_{\mathsf{T}}=\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}\otimes\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Id}_{\mathsf{R}}
(up to higher-order terms) by
Theorem

5.1
, it follows that
\xi\otimes\eta\in\mathrm{dom}(H_{\mathsf{T}})
and also
\rho(t)(\xi\otimes\eta)=\rho_{1}(t)\xi\otimes\rho_{2}(0)\eta\in\mathrm{dom}(H_{\mathsf{T}})
. Thus, the following von Neumann equation holds true for the total system
\mathsf{T}
:

(30)

\mathrm{i}\hbar\,\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho(t)}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}\,(\xi\otimes\eta)=\bigl[H_{\mathsf{T}},\rho(t)\bigr](\xi\otimes\eta)+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2})\ .

For every
m\in\mathbb{N}
let
(H_{\mathsf{T}})_{m}\coloneqq(\mathop{}\!\mathbf{1}_{[-m,m]}\cdot\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Id})(H_{\mathsf{T}})
, that is,
(H_{\mathsf{T}})_{m}=\int_{[-m,m]\cap\sigma(H_{\mathsf{T}})}\lambda\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}E_{H_{\mathsf{T}}}(\lambda)
, where
E_{H_{\mathsf{T}}}
is the spectral measure of the self-adjoint operator
H_{\mathsf{T}}
; the latter is given by
E_{H_{\mathsf{T}}}=E_{\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}}\otimes\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Id}_{\mathsf{R}}
because
H_{\mathsf{T}}=\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}\otimes\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Id}_{\mathsf{R}}

[
2
, Thm. 3.5 (ii)]
. Since the function
\mathop{}\!\mathbf{1}_{[-m,m]}\cdot\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Id}
is bounded, the previous identity implies that
(H_{\mathsf{T}})_{m}=(\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}})_{m}\otimes\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Id}_{\mathsf{R}}
, see
Lemma

D.1
in
Appendix

D
. Note that
(\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}})_{m}
is a bounded operator, and that
\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}\psi=\lim_{m\to+\infty}(\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}})_{m}\psi
for all
\psi\in\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}})
(analogous statements hold for
H_{\mathsf{T}}
)
[
47
, Prop. 11.5]
. Thus, we may rewrite the commutator on the right-hand side of
Eq.

30
as

\displaystyle\bigl[H_{\mathsf{T}},\rho(t)\bigr](\xi\otimes\eta)

\displaystyle=H_{\mathsf{T}}\rho(t)(\xi\otimes\eta)-\rho(t)H_{\mathsf{T}}(\xi\otimes\eta)

\displaystyle=\lim_{m\to+\infty}\Bigl((H_{\mathsf{T}})_{m}\rho(t)(\xi\otimes\eta)-\rho(t)(H_{\mathsf{T}})_{m}(\xi\otimes\eta)\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\lim_{m\to+\infty}\Bigl((\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}})_{m}\rho_{1}(t)\xi\otimes\rho_{2}(0)\eta-\rho_{1}(t)(\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}})_{m}\xi\otimes\rho_{2}(0)\eta\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\lim_{m\to+\infty}\Bigl(\bigl((\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}})_{m}\rho_{1}(t)\xi-\rho_{1}(t)(\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}})_{m}\xi\bigr)\otimes\rho_{2}(0)\eta\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\bigl[\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}},\rho_{1}(t)\bigr]\xi\otimes\rho_{2}(0)\eta\ .

Note that the algebraic manipulations of the tensor product in lines three and four were possible because the involved operators are bounded, and that in the last step continuity of the tensor product was used. With this, the von Neumann equation (
30
) takes the form

\mathrm{i}\hbar\left(\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho_{1}(t)}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}\,\xi\otimes\rho_{2}(0)\eta\right)=\bigl[\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}},\rho_{1}(t)\bigr]\xi\otimes\rho_{2}(0)\eta+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2})\ .

Thus, since
\eta\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathsf{R}}
was arbitrary and
\rho_{2}(0)\neq 0
, it follows that for all
\xi\in\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}})\cap\mathrm{dom}(\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}\rho_{1}(t))
and
t\geq 0
:

\mathrm{i}\hbar\,\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho_{1}(t)}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}\,\xi=\bigl[\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}},\rho_{1}(t)\bigr]\xi+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2})\ .

If
\rho_{1}
is stationary (
\dot{\rho}_{1}=0
), we obtain
\bigl[(\mathscr{H}-\mu\mathscr{N}),\rho_{1}\bigr]=0
(up to terms of order
\varepsilon^{2}
), which leads to
\rho_{1}=\rho_{\mathrm{GC}}
by following the same derivation as for the canonical ensemble, however, with the effective Hamiltonian
\mathscr{H}-\mu\mathscr{N}
instead of just
H_{\mathsf{S}}
.
∎

Remarks 5.4
.

(1)

The validity of the Born-Markov approximation relies (i) on a negligible interaction between the open system and the reservoir, which is formalized by the surface-to-volume ratio approximation, and (ii) on using a large reservoir that is not influenced by the exchange of particles with the open system, which is contained in our Assumption
A1.1
, suggesting that the reservoir’s state can be assumed constant in time. This approximation is standard in the theory of open quantum systems
[
9
,
44
,
70
]
(see, however, Refs.
[
58
, Sect. 5.2.7]
and
[
59
, Sect. 2.3.3]
for critical discussions), and it also fits well into the setting of
Theorem

5.1
, cf.
5.2

2
above. Moreover, the Born-Markov approximation was a key modeling assumption in Ref.
[
23
]
for deriving the hierarchy of von Neumann equations from which the effective Hamiltonian was extracted, as discussed in
5.2

3
.

(2)

In a recent paper, we investigated the Lindblad equation for systems with varying particle number
[
53
]
. Using the results of Ref.
[
23
]
, we found that the system Hamiltonian should be replaced by the effective Hamiltonian
\mathscr{H}-\mu\mathscr{N}
in order to produce a consistent Lindblad equation for such systems. Since
Theorem

5.1
justifies the conclusions of Ref.
[
23
]
on a more rigorous level, by extension, the foundation of the findings of Ref.
[
53
]
is also strengthened.

6.
Conclusion

We have analyzed the mathematical model for open quantum systems with varying particle number and rigorously shown that the effective Hamiltonian describing such systems, under suitable assumptions and approximations, is given by the well-known expression
\mathscr{H}-\mu\mathscr{N}
. Thus, our findings justify an empirical hypothesis used in many areas of physics that goes back at least to Bogoliubov’s work in the 1950s
[
7
]
. To obtain the effective Hamiltonian, we have formulated the surface-to-volume ratio approximation in a precise manner, which entails that if the open system is large enough in a certain sense, then the interaction with the reservoir can be neglected. As a key mathematical tool for establishing this approximation as well as deriving the effective Hamiltonian, we have introduced the notion of density operators compatible with unbounded observables (in the sense of computing expectation values), and we have shown that this notion leads to a satisfying framework, generalizing, for example, a partial trace formula to unbounded operators. Moreover, we have provided a new, physically motivated, and mathematically simple yet rigorous argument that for systems with varying particle number, the Hilbert space must have the structure of Fock space.

In summary, our findings justify some widely used tools of the trade of statistical mechanics in a rigorous mathematical way and, therefore, put the theory of open quantum systems with varying particle number on a more solid theoretical footing. Furthermore, our results,
a fortiori
, substantiate what has already been done in applications of this theory to the field of quantum technologies, where it was shown, for example, that the chemical potential (and hence the feature of a varying particle number) controls the accessible quantum states of certain simple model systems
[
54
]
. Therefore, our work contributes to future-oriented mathematical physics that seeks to base developments at the forefront of physics and technology on mathematically solid foundations.

A.1.
Basic Properties

be self-adjoint. In the following, we will prove some auxiliary results about the set of

\mathscr{S}(\mathcal{H},T)=\left\{\rho\in\mathscr{S}(\mathcal{H})\ :\ \parbox{240.00037pt}{\centering$\exists\,(e_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathrm{dom}(T)\ \exists\,(\alpha_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq[0,1]:$ \\
(i) $(e_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ is an ONB for $\mathcal{H}$ and $\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\alpha_{n}=1$; \\
(ii) $\rho=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\alpha_{n}P_{e_{n}}$; (iii) $\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\alpha_{n}|{\langle{e_{n},Te_{n}}\rangle}|<+\infty$\@add@centering}\right\}

and the corresponding notion of expectation value

Lemma A.1
.

For
T\in\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{H})
self-adjoint and
\rho\in\mathscr{S}(\mathcal{H},T)
, the value of
\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[T]
does not depend on the choice of the sequences
(e_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(\alpha_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in the representation
\rho=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\alpha_{n}P_{e_{n}}
.

Proof.

Let
(f_{m})_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathrm{dom}(T)
be another orthonormal basis for
\mathcal{H}
and
(\beta_{m})_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq[0,1]
be a sequence with
\sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\beta_{m}=1
such that
\rho=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\beta_{m}P_{f_{m}}
and
\sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\beta_{m}|{\langle{f_{m},Tf_{m}}\rangle}|<+\infty
. Using completeness of the basis
(f_{m})_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
and self-adjointness of
T
, it follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{n}\langle{e_{n},Te_{n}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{n}\langle{e_{n},f_{m}}\rangle\langle{f_{m},Te_{n}}\rangle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{n}\langle{e_{n},f_{m}}\rangle\langle{Tf_{m},e_{n}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\Big\langle{Tf_{m},{\textstyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}}\alpha_{n}\langle{e_{n},f_{m}}\rangle\,e_{n}}\Big\rangle=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\langle{Tf_{m},\rho f_{m}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\beta_{m}\langle{f_{m},Tf_{m}}\rangle\ .

We note that in the second line, it was possible to interchange the two series because the sum over
m
is finite: it holds that
\alpha_{n}\langle{e_{n},f_{m}}\rangle=\langle{\rho e_{n},f_{m}}\rangle=\langle{e_{n},\rho f_{m}}\rangle=\beta_{m}\langle{e_{n},f_{m}}\rangle
by assumption, hence
(\alpha_{n}-\beta_{m})\langle{e_{n},f_{m}}\rangle=0
for all

. Thus, whenever
\langle{e_{n},f_{m}}\rangle\neq 0
we must have
\alpha_{n}=\beta_{m}
, so the sum
\sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\alpha_{n}\langle{e_{n},f_{m}}\rangle\langle{Tf_{m},e_{n}}\rangle
actually extends only over the set
I_{n}\coloneqq\{{m\in\mathbb{N}:\beta_{m}=\alpha_{n}}\}
. As there are at most finitely many
m
for which
\beta_{m}=\alpha_{n}
(otherwise,
\sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\beta_{m}
would not converge), it follows that
I_{n}
is finite.
∎

Lemma A.2
.

Let
T\in\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{H})
be self-adjoint and assume that for all
\beta>0
,
\mathrm{e}^{-\beta T}\in\mathscr{B}_{1}(\mathcal{H})
is a trace-class operator. Then
\mathscr{S}(\mathcal{H},T)\neq\varnothing
.

Proof.

First, recall the well-known fact that
\mathrm{e}^{-\beta T}
being trace-class implies that
T
is lower semi-bounded
[
2
, Prop. 1.46]
, and that
T
must have a purely discrete spectrum
[
47
, p. 597]
, i.e.,
\sigma(T)
consists of isolated eigenvalues only and all eigenspaces are finite-dimensional (see also Ref.
[
4
, Prop. 2.2]
for an explicit proof of these two assertions). Let
c\in\mathbb{R}
be a lower bound for
T
and define the operator
\widetilde{T}\coloneqq T-c\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Id}_{\mathcal{H}}
. It is evident that
\widetilde{T}
is positive and self-adjoint; one can show that
\widetilde{T}
also has a purely discrete spectrum
[
66
, p. 210]
. Therefore, upon replacing
T
by
\widetilde{T}
, we may assume, without loss of generality, that
T
is positive.

Since
T
has a purely discrete spectrum, there exists a sequence
(\gamma_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq[0,+\infty)
and an orthonormal basis
(e_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of
\mathcal{H}
such that
\gamma_{n}\to+\infty
as
n\to+\infty
and
Te_{n}=\gamma_{n}e_{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}

[

Appendix B
Background from convex geometry

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
be arbitrary. Denote by
B^{d}\coloneqq\{{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,:\,|{x}|<1}\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
the
d
-dimensional Euclidean unit ball centered at the origin, and let
\omega_{d}\coloneqq\mathrm{vol}(B^{d})
be its volume. For two sets
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, their Minkowski linear combination is defined as

\alpha A+\beta B\coloneqq\big\{{\alpha x+\beta y\,:\,x\in A,\,y\in B}\big\}\ .

It holds that
A+B=\bigcup_{a\in A}(a+B)=\bigcup_{b\in B}(A+b)

[
39
, p. 5]
. Moreover, it is evident that for all
\varepsilon>0
and
a\in A
the set
\varepsilon B^{d}
is given by the ball
B(0,\varepsilon)
of radius
\varepsilon
, and the set
a+\varepsilon B^{d}=\{{a+x\,:\,|{x}|<\varepsilon}\}
is equal to the
\varepsilon
-ball
B(a,\varepsilon)
centered at
a
.

Definition B.1
.

Let
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be any set and
\varepsilon>0
be a positive number. The
tubular
\varepsilon
-neighborhood
(or:
\varepsilon
-thickening) of
A
is defined to be the set

A_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq\big\{{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,:\,\mathrm{dist}(x,A)<\varepsilon}\big\}\ ,

where
\mathrm{dist}(x,A)\coloneqq\inf_{a\in A}|{x-a}|
is the distance between the point
x
and the set
A
. That is,
A_{\varepsilon}
consists of all those points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
whose distance to
A
is smaller than
\varepsilon
.

Remark B.2
.

It is straightforward to show that the
\varepsilon
-neighborhood of
A
can also be written as
A_{\varepsilon}=\bigcup_{a\in A}B(a,\varepsilon)
. Therefore, it readily follows that the Minkowski sum
A+\varepsilon B^{d}
is equal to the
\varepsilon
-neighborhood of
A
:

A+\varepsilon B^{d}=\bigcup_{a\in A}(a+\varepsilon B^{d})=\bigcup_{a\in A}B(a,\varepsilon)=A_{\varepsilon}\ .

A cornerstone in convex geometry is the
Minkowski-Steiner formula
, which expresses the volume of the set
A+\varepsilon B^{d}=A_{\varepsilon}
, where
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is convex and
\varepsilon>0
, as a polynomial in the parameter
\varepsilon
. The proof of this result can be found, e.g., in Refs.
[
11
, p. 141]
,
[
33
, Thm. 6.6]
, and
[
39
, Thm. 3.10]
(see also Ref.
[
69
]
and references therein).

Theorem B.3

(Minkowski-Steiner)
.

Let
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be an open, bounded, convex set and
\varepsilon>0
be arbitrary. Then there are numbers
W_{2}(A),\dotsc,W_{d-1}(A)\geq 0
such that

(32)

\mathrm{vol}\bigl(A+\varepsilon B^{d}\bigr)=\mathrm{vol}(A)+\mathrm{area}(\partial A)\,\varepsilon+\sum_{j=2}^{d-1}\binom{d}{j}W_{j}(A)\,\varepsilon^{j}+\omega_{d}\,\varepsilon^{d}\ .

Remarks B.4
.

(1)

The coefficients
W_{j}(A)
in the above polynomial expansion are called
quermassintegrals
of the set
A
. They are defined for all
j\in\{{0,1,\dotsc,d}\}
by

(33)

W_{j}(A)\coloneqq V\bigl(\,\underbrace{A,\dotsc,A}_{d-j},\,\underbrace{B^{d},\dotsc,B^{d}}_{j}\,\bigr)\ ,

with
V(A,\dotsc,A,B^{d},\dotsc,B^{d})
being the
mixed volume
of the convex sets
A,\dotsc,A,B^{d},\dotsc,B^{d}

[
33
, Thm. 6.5]
. The function
V
is symmetric, linear, continuous with respect to the Hausdorff metric, and non-decreasing in each argument
[
33
, Sec. 6.3]
,
[
39
, Thm. 3.9]
.

(2)

In writing
Eq.

32
, we have used the following two identities: (i)
W_{0}(A)=\mathrm{vol}(A)
is the volume of
A
, and (ii)
W_{1}(A)=\frac{1}{d}\,\mathrm{area}(\partial A)
is proportional to the surface area of the boundary of
A

[
11
, p. 139]
,
[
27
, Thm. 3.2.35]
,
[
39
, Thm. 3.9 (a)]
,
[
69
]
.

Example B.5
.

Consider the specific case
d=3
. Aside from the three terms that are already stated explicitly in the Minkowski-Steiner formula (
32
), the remaining sum only contributes the
j=2
term. The corresponding quermassintegral is given by
[
39
, Eq. (3.16)]

W_{2}(K)=V(K,B_{3},B_{3})=\frac{1}{3}\,\int_{S^{2}}h_{K}\ ,

where
h_{K}(u)\coloneqq\sup_{x\in K}\langle{u,x}\rangle
,
u\in S^{2}
, is the support function of the convex set
K
,
S^{2}
denotes the 2-sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, and integration is with respect to the two-dimensional Hausdorff measure. With this identity, the Minkowski-Steiner formula reduces to

\mathrm{vol}(K_{\varepsilon})=\mathrm{vol}(K)+\mathrm{area}(\partial K)\,\varepsilon+\int_{S^{2}}h_{K}\,\varepsilon^{2}+\omega_{3}\,\varepsilon^{3}\ .

Suppose that
0\in K
. Then
\sup_{x\in K}\|{x}\|\leq\mathrm{diam}(K)
, where
\mathrm{diam}(K)\coloneqq\sup_{x,y\in K}\|{x-y}\|
denotes the diameter of
K
. This implies
h_{K}(u)\leq\mathrm{diam}(K)
for all
u\in S^{2}
, using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. Since
\mathrm{area}(S^{2})=3\omega_{3}
, it follows that

\mathrm{vol}(K_{\varepsilon}\setminus K)\leq\mathrm{area}(\partial K)\,\varepsilon+3\omega_{3}\,\mathrm{diam}(K)\,\varepsilon^{2}+\omega_{3}\,\varepsilon^{3}\ .

The second result that we need is an estimate of the volume of the inner tubular neighborhood of a set; it is taken from Ref.
[
26
, Lem. 2.2]
(see also Ref.
[
32
, Rem. 5.7]
).

Lemma B.6

(
[
26
, Lem. 2.2]
)
.

Let
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be open, bounded, and convex. For all
\varepsilon>0
,

\mathrm{vol}\bigl(\{{x\in A\,:\,\mathrm{dist}(x,\partial A)\leq\varepsilon}\}\bigr)\leq\mathrm{area}(\partial A)\,\varepsilon\ .

Appendix C
Characterization of the direct sum of Hilbert spaces

Let
I
be an at most countable index set, and for every
i\in I
let
(\mathcal{H}_{i},\langle{\cdot,\cdot}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{i}})
be a Hilbert space. Consider the following subset of the direct product
\prod_{i\in I}\mathcal{H}_{i}
:

(34)

\bigoplus_{i\in I}\mathcal{H}_{i}\coloneqq\left\{{\varPsi=\bigl(\psi^{(i)}\bigr)_{i\in I}\in\prod_{i\in I}\mathcal{H}_{i}\ :\ \sum_{i\in I}\big\|{\psi^{(i)}}\big\|_{\mathcal{H}_{i}}^{2}<+\infty}\right\}\ .

It is straightforward to show that
\bigoplus_{i\in I}\mathcal{H}_{i}
is a linear subspace of
\prod_{i\in I}\mathcal{H}_{i}
, with algebraic operations defined component-wise. On this subspace, define an inner product
\langle{\cdot,\cdot}\rangle_{\oplus}
by

\langle{\varPsi,\varPhi}\rangle_{\oplus}\coloneqq\sum_{i\in I}\Big\langle{\psi^{(i)},\phi^{(i)}}\Big\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{i}}\ .

The Cauchy-Schwarz and Hölder inequality show that
\langle{\cdot,\cdot}\rangle_{\oplus}
is well-defined. Moreover, one can show by standard arguments that
\bigoplus_{i\in I}\mathcal{H}_{i}
is complete with respect to the norm

\|{\varPsi}\|_{\oplus}^{2}\coloneqq\sum_{i\in I}\big\|{\psi^{(i)}}\big\|_{\mathcal{H}_{i}}^{2}

induced by the inner product
[
6
, Thm. 18.1]
. Therefore,
\bigl(\bigoplus_{i\in I}\mathcal{H}_{i},\langle{\cdot,\cdot}\rangle_{\oplus}\bigr)
is a Hilbert space called the
Hilbert space direct sum
of the family
(\mathcal{H}_{i})_{i\in I}
.

In the recent study Ref.
[
30
]
, the authors define in a very general, category-theoretic setting the concept of infinite direct sums for arbitrary
C^{\ast}
-categories in terms of a universal property
[
30
, Def. 4.2]
. Furthermore, they show that in a
W^{\ast}
-category, the existence of a direct sum as defined by them is equivalent to two other, more concrete assertions
[
30
, Thm. 5.1]
, one of which is the original definition of an infinite direct sum in
W^{\ast}
-categories that was first given in Ref.
[
31
, p. 100]
(see also Ref.
[
30
, p. 363]
). Below, we state the main result of Ref.
[
30
]
for the particular
W^{\ast}
-category of Hilbert spaces with bounded linear operators as morphisms. We emphasize that the original result
[
30
, Thm. 5.1]
is much more general than the version provided here, and that we only state one of the two equivalent characterizations of a direct sum that were given in
[
30
, Thm. 5.1]
, namely the one that goes back to Ref.
[
31
, p. 100]
; for the needs of the present paper, this special case is indeed sufficient and it avoids technical details from category theory.

Theorem C.1

(
[
30
, Thm. 5.1, (a)
\Leftrightarrow
(c)]
)
.

Let
(\mathcal{H}_{i})_{i\in I}
be a family of Hilbert spaces and
\mathcal{H}
be another Hilbert space. The following assertions are equivalent:

(i)

\mathcal{H}
is the direct sum
\bigoplus_{i\in I}\mathcal{H}_{i}
;

(ii)

there exists a family of bounded linear operators
(A_{i}:\mathcal{H}_{i}\to\mathcal{H})_{i\in I}
such that

A_{i}^{\ast}A_{j}=\delta_{ij}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Id}_{\mathcal{H}_{i}}\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{i\in I}A_{i}A_{i}^{\ast}=\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Id}_{\mathcal{H}}\ .

Appendix D
Functional calculus for tensor product operators

In the following, we prove a formula for the functional calculus of tensor product operators that is used in the proof of
Corollary

5.3
.

Lemma D.1
.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}
be Hilbert spaces,
T\in\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{H}_{1})
be a self-adjoint operator on
\mathcal{H}_{1}
, and
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
be a bounded function. Then

f(T\otimes\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Id}_{\mathcal{H}_{2}})=f(T)\otimes\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Id}_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}\ .

Proof.

The two key ingredients for the proof are the facts that (i) the spectral measure
E_{T\otimes\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Id}_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}}
of the self-adjoint operator
T\otimes\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Id}_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}
is given by
E_{T\otimes\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Id}_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}}(B)=E_{T}(B)\otimes\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Id}_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}
for all Borel subsets
B\subseteq\mathbb{R}

[
2
, Thm. 3.5 (ii)]
(where
E_{T}
is the spectral measure of
T
), and that (ii) the spectrum of
T\otimes\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Id}_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}
is given by
\sigma(T\otimes\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Id}_{\mathcal{H}_{2}})=\sigma(T)

[
2
, Thm. 3.6]
.

Let
\xi\in\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\eta\in\mathcal{H}_{2}
be arbitrary. From the standard properties of the bounded functional calculus, it follows that

\displaystyle\big\langle{\xi\otimes\eta,f(T\otimes\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Id}_{\mathcal{H}_{2}})(\xi\otimes\eta)}\big\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{\sigma(T\otimes\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Id}_{\mathcal{H}_{2}})}f(\lambda)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\big\langle{\xi\otimes\eta,E_{T\otimes\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Id}_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}}(\lambda)(\xi\otimes\eta)}\big\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{\sigma(T\otimes\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Id}_{\mathcal{H}_{2}})}f(\lambda)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\big\langle{\xi\otimes\eta,(E_{T}(\lambda)\otimes\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Id}_{\mathcal{H}_{2}})(\xi\otimes\eta)}\big\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{\sigma(T)}f(\lambda)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\langle{\xi,E_{T}(\lambda)\xi}\rangle\cdot\langle{\eta,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Id}_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}\eta}\rangle

\displaystyle=\big\langle{\xi\otimes\eta,(f(T)\otimes\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Id}_{\mathcal{H}_{2}})(\xi\otimes\eta)}\big\rangle\ .

Since the linear span of elementary tensors
\xi\otimes\eta
(that is, the algebraic tensor product
\mathcal{H}_{1}\odot\mathcal{H}_{2}
) lies dense in
\mathcal{H}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{2}
, we conclude that
f(T\otimes\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Id}_{\mathcal{H}_{2}})=f(T)\otimes\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Id}_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}
.
∎

Remark D.2
.

In the setting of the lemma, suppose that
f
is an
E_{T}
-almost everywhere finite function and that
\xi\in\mathrm{dom}\bigl(f(T)\bigr)
. Then the above proof shows that
\xi\otimes\eta\in\mathrm{dom}\bigl(f(T\otimes\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Id}_{\mathcal{H}_{2}})\bigr)
and
f(T\otimes\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Id}_{\mathcal{H}_{2}})=f(T)\otimes\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Id}_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}
on
\mathrm{dom}\bigl(f(T)\otimes\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Id}_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}\bigr)
because
\mathrm{dom}\bigl(f(T)\bigr)\odot\mathcal{H}_{2}
is dense in the latter. Therefore,
f(T)\otimes\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Id}_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}\subseteq f(T\otimes\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Id}_{\mathcal{H}_{2}})
.
