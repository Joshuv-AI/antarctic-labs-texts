Inflammation-specific DNA origami nanodevice for delivery of siRNAs to treat ulcerative colitis
Authors: Tianci Zhang, Ruoqing Li, Zhenghao Wang, Ye Zhou, Yaojia Zhou, Xiaoting Chen, Cui Peng, Yi Jiang, Nanwei Tong, Wei Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSmall interfering RNAs (siRNAs) have emerged as versatile therapeutic agents for ulcerative colitis, and approaches to optimize their assembly, delivery, and on-demand release are therefore needed. Here, we present a method for the precise assembly and accurate dosing of two types of siRNAs on an enzyme-triggerable DNA origami nanodevice. We design a DNA duplex structure as an inflammation-responsive switch and demonstrate that the nanodevice accumulates autonomously and predominantly in the intestine and selectively exposes siRNAs through a translocated enzyme in inflammatory cells. We find that this strategy can effectively inhibit multiple pathogenic genes and pathways in inflamed cells, while remaining inert in healthy cells. We show that when administered to mice with DSS-induced colitis, the nanodevice increases survival rates up to 100% and exhibits robust therapeutic efficacy and biosafety. This study demonstrates that the nanodevice can serve as a switchable delivery platform for siRNA specifically targeted to the intestine.Subject terms: Nanobiotechnology, DNA and RNA, Inflammatory bowel disease, Drug delivery

Introduction

Ulcerative colitis (UC), one type of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), is a multifactorial-mediated chronic and recurrent gastrointestinal disease, and it is difficult to cure currently1,2. RNA interference (RNAi)-based therapeutics have emerged as an attractive and alternative strategy for combating UC since small interfering RNA (siRNA) can specifically and precisely pair with mRNA to regulate multiple pathogenic genes and pathways3–5. However, these therapeutics heavily rely on delivery vectors due to the difficulty of therapeutic RNA to enter cells efficiently and function on its own6,7. Lipid or polymer-based nanoparticles have been developed for targeted delivery of siRNA, but they mainly accumulate in liver or renal tissues and lack efficient transportation to the inflamed intestine6,8. Off-target delivery to non-intestinal tissues may cause unwanted immune responses in healthy tissues after intravenous administration9. Achieving selective exposure of therapeutic RNAs at inflamed sites while maintaining their inactivity at healthy tissues poses another significant challenge. Thus, an intestine-targeting, inflammatory cell-responsive delivery platform that can minimize off-target and overdose effects for UC is highly desired.

DNA nanotechnology is capable to fabricate dynamically intelligent nanodevice through the integration of the structurally well-defined DNA origami with stimuli-responsive elements10–12. The addressability of such DNA nanodevice enables them to precisely manipulate biomolecules in a site-specific manner, while their dynamic reconfigurability allows for the protection and on-demand release of the biomolecules at targeted sites13–16. Previous studies have demonstrated that the DNA nanodevice can respond to many specific stimuli, such as enzymes, pH levels, and small molecules, for drug release, and have shown robust treatment efficacy for many diseases17–20. For example, a tubular DNA nanodevice containing disulfide bonds has been reported to enable the opening and release of siRNA in response to intracellular glutathione (GSH) in tumors for cancer therapy16. Those intelligently designed, stimuli-responsive DNA nanodevices represent a promising strategy for optimizing the assembly, delivery, and exposure of siRNA to combat UC. However, the distinct hyperinflammatory microenvironment in ulcerative colitis fundamentally differs from tumor or other disease microenvironments, and an intestine-targeting, inflammation-responsive DNA nanodevice for effective UC therapy is thus urgently needed.

Apurinic/apyrimidinic endonuclease 1 (APE1), a multifunctional enzyme that exerts DNA repair function by cleaving apurinic/apyrimidinic (AP) sites21,22, is exclusively located at the nucleus in normal cells, but translocates to the cytoplasm when cells transition into an inflammatory state23,24. Such distinct distribution of APE1 from normal cells to inflamed cells offers a possibility to fabricate an inflammation-responsive DNA nanodevice for UC treatment.

In this work, we design an inflammation-responsive DNA duplex structure (named AP locker) that contains AP sites and can be cleaved into two duplex DNA strands by the APE1. Utilizing the distinct distribution of APE1 from normal cells to inflamed cells, we develop an enzyme-triggerable DNA nanodevice through assembling the AP locker with siRNA-loaded rectangular DNA origami. This nanodevice autonomously targets the intestine and normally hides therapeutic siRNAs but selectively exposes them in inflamed cells, which results in the effective inhibition of multiple pathogenic genes and pathways in inflamed cells while remaining inert in normal cells. Due to such an outstanding ability, the nanodevice exhibits excellent therapeutic efficacy and biosafety in UC treatment.

Results

Design and characterization of the nanodevice

The nanodevice is a DNA origami-based, inflammatory cell-responsive delivery system designed for the protection of siRNAs during circulation and for effective exposure at inflamed cells (Fig. 1a and b). A rectangular DNA origami nanosheet (Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Data 1), with dimensions of 100 × 70 × 2 nm3, extends two types of capture strands (Supplementary Data 1) at predesignated locations, enabling the TNF-α siRNA (siTNF-α) and the integrin α4 siRNA (siInte α4) to anchor on its surface through base pairing. The nanosheet, loaded with different numbers of siTNF-α (5, 10, 20, named siTNF-α@nanosheet) and siInte α4 (5, 10, 20, named siInte α4@nanosheet) strands, exhibited a corresponding lagging migration rate with the increasing siRNA loading numbers in the agarose gel electrophoresis (AGE) assay (Fig. 1c and d). The fluorescence intensity of these two loading siRNAs also increased with their increasing loading numbers. The average quantitative numbers of siTNF-α and siInte α4 strands loaded onto the nanosheet were approximately 5, 10, and 20, which were measured by a fluorescence method (Supplementary Fig. 2), and were close to their designed numbers (5, 10, 20). These results demonstrate that both siTNF-α and siInte α4 (Supplementary Data 1) can be precisely loaded onto the nanosheet in a controllable number and ratio by designing the capture strands. A DNA duplex structure, named AP locker (Supplementary Data 1), is designed to curl the siRNA-loaded DNA nanosheet (siNanosheet) into a tubular nanodevice, where the AP locker contains an AP site and can be cleaved into two DNA duplexes by APE1. Thus, the nanodevice can normally hide siTNF-α and siInte α4 within its cavity but change its conformation and selectively expose them in inflamed cells due to the translocation of APE1. The AGE analysis showed the migration rate of the nanodevice was faster than that of the nanosheet (Supplementary Fig. 3), and the fluorescence signals of siTNF-α, siInte α4, and the nanosheet overlapped well (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Fig. 4), indicating the successful formation of the nanodevice. Atomic force microscopy (AFM) showed that the nanosheet and siNanosheet, presented a planar rectangular morphology, while the nanodevice exhibited a three-dimensional tubular shape (Fig. 1f). The height of the nanodevice was about 4 nm, which was twice as high as that of the nanosheet (Fig. 1g). This may be due to the fact that during AFM imaging, the tubular structure of the nanodevice was compressed by the AFM probe, leading to structural collapse and the formation of a two-layer structure. These data collectively confirm the successful preparation of tubular DNA nanodevice as prescribed. Such tubular structure of the nanodevice serves to shield the siRNAs from exposure and protect them during circulation within the bloodstream.

The enzyme-triggerable unlocking by APE1

In our design, the AP locker is used as the “lock” and APE1 is used as the “key”. The relation between the “lock” and “key” was investigated with a fluorescence-quencher pair (Fig. 2a). The AP locker was constructed from three DNA strands: linker3 (in magenta), linker4, and linker5. The linker3 strand harbored an AP site. The 3’ end of linker4 was modified with a black hole quencher2 (BHQ2) molecule, while the 5’ end of linker5 was modified with a cyanine 3 (Cy3) molecule. The Cy3 fluorescence in the AP locker was initially quenched by the BHQ2. This quenching was attributed to the structural integrity of the AP locker, which maintained a short distance (<10 nm) between the Cy3 molecule and the BHQ2 molecule. After adding APE1, the fluorescence gradually increased, attributed to the separation of Cy3 and BHQ2, further confirming that APE1 can hydrolyze the AP site. The AGE analysis further revealed that the AP locker could be cleaved into two DNA duplex strands by APE1, which is in line with our design (Fig. 2b). This “unlocking” process of the nanodevice was also investigated by the Cy3-BHQ2 pair. The quenching and recovering of Cy3 fluorescence in the nanodevice could also be observed, revealing that APE1 could efficiently unlock the nanodevice (Fig. 2c). The AGE analysis also showed that the migration rate of the nanodevice became slower after APE1 (5 U/mL) addition (Fig. 2d). This may be due to the conformation change of the nanodevice from a tubular shape into a planar shape, resulting in the increase of the migration resistance. The AFM further confirmed this result, showing that the tubular nanodevice had been transformed into planar rectangular nanosheets and the height of the nanodevice decreased from 4 nm to 2 nm after the APE1 addition (Fig. 2e). The unlocked process of the nanodevice at cell level was further explored (Fig. 2f). Firstly, RAW264.7 cells were induced into an inflamed state with 500 ng/mL LPS for 12 h, and we found that APE1 was exclusively localized in the nucleus of normal cells, but translocated into the cytoplasm in LPS-treated cells (Fig. 2g). Next, the Cy5 labeled nanodevice was locked with Cy3-BHQ2 modified AP locker and incubated with normal or LPS-pretreated cells, respectively. The fluorescence imaging revealed that Cy5 fluorescence signals could be observed in both normal cells and LPS-treated cells, exhibiting comparable intensities (Fig. 2h). This suggests that the uptake levels of the nanodevice were similar in normal cells and LPS-treated cells. However, a strong Cy3 fluorescence signal from the nanodevice could be observed in LPS-treated cells, while the signal in normal cells was negligible. Furthermore, the Cy3 and Cy5 could overlay very well in LPS-treated cells. These results suggest that most of the nanodevice could be unlocked in inflamed cells, while maintaining its intact structure in normal cells. Together, these results demonstrate that the nanodevice can be efficiently responsive to APE1 and selectively unlocked in inflamed cells.

The nanodevice exhibits robust gene silencing within inflammatory cells

Given that the DNA origami nanostructures exhibit effective cellular uptake due to their size and shape16,25,26, we examined the ability of the nanodevice to deliver siRNA and its gene-silencing function at the cellular level. To visualize the cellular uptake of the nanodevice, we modified siTNF-α and siInte α4 with Cy3 and Cy7 respectively. Caco-2 cells were incubated with free siRNA siTNF&siInte (the mixture of 200 nM siTNF-α and 200 nM siInte α4) or 10 nM of the nanodevice (contained the 200 nM siTNF-α and 200 nM siInte α4) for 6 h. The fluorescence imaging showed that both the Cy3 and Cy7 fluorescence in the siTNF&siInte group could hardly be observed, but showed strong signals in the nanodevice group (Fig. 3a). This result reveals that the efficiency of siRNA cellular uptake is extremely low, likely because its negative charge prevents cells from effectively absorbing them. The nanodevice, due to its compact structure, shape, and nanoscale size, could effectively deliver siTNF-α and siInte α4 into the cytoplasm of Caco-2 cells via scavenger receptor-mediated endocytosis pathway26 or caveolin-mediated endocytosis pathway (Supplementary Fig. 5). The caveolae-mediated endocytosis generates receptor-associated endosomes capable of trafficking to non-lysosomal organelles27, thereby evading canonical degradative pathways. Though some intracellular nanodevices overlapped with lysosomes after 4 hours of incubation, at least 33.47% of the nanodevices remained outside lysosomes (Supplementary Fig. 6). These results provide a possibility for the nanodevice to effectively silence genes. To verify this, we co-incubated the nanodevice with or without LPS-pretreated RAW264.7 cells for 12 h and then detected the expression levels of TNF-α and integrin α4. RT-qPCR demonstrated that the nanodevice effectively achieved gene silencing in LPS-treated cells, as evidenced by significant decreases in the mRNA levels of both TNF-α and integrin α4 in LPS-treated cells (Fig. 3b). Interestingly, the nanodevice presented negligible gene silencing in normal cells compared to LPS-pretreated group. One possible reason is that the locked nanodevice in normal cells may have shielded the siRNAs, whereas the opened nanodevice in inflamed cells exposed the siRNAs and may induce the formation of RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC) on its surface (Supplementary Fig. 7), thereby releasing the antisense nucleotides to reduce target gene expression. These results indicated that the nanodevice exhibited activity in inflamed cells while remaining inert in normal cells. We further examined the protein expression level, and after cells co-incubation with nanodevice for 36 hours, the western blot (WB) results revealed that free siRNA siTNF&siInte exhibited negligible gene silencing effect, which may be attributed to its low cellular uptake efficiency. However, the nanodevice effectively reduced protein levels of TNF-α and integrin α4 (Fig. 3c), and the magnitude of this reduction was comparable to that achieved through liposome-mediated transfection of siTNF&siInte (Supplementary Fig. 8). The reduction of TNF-α leads to a decrease in its binding to the membrane receptor (TNFR), which in turn inhibits a cascade of signaling pathways, notably including the NF-κB pathway (Fig. 3d). This inhibition subsequently results in the downregulation of the expression of inflammatory cytokines28. WB and RT-qPCR revealed that the nanodevice effectively inhibited the phosphorylation of IκB, p65 (Fig. 3e) and the expression of downstream inflammatory factors, including IL-1β and IL-6 (Fig. 3f and Supplementary Fig. 9), indicating the nanodevice can effectively inhibit the activation of NF-κB pathway and inflammation.

In addition, the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in the intestine has been shown to induce cellular damage and is implicated in the pathogenesis of UC29,30. Conversely, the elimination of ROS can alleviate the symptoms of UC31–34. Interestingly, DNA is sensitive to various ROS and DNA-based nanostructures have been utilizing as potent ROS scavenger35–37. The Fenton reaction and fluorescence imaging revealed that our DNA origami-based nanodevice exhibited an effective ability to scavenge ROS (Supplementary Fig. 10), which holds promise for alleviating oxidative stress in UC. Furthermore, the nanodevice presented minimal cytotoxicity in both macrophage RAW264.7 cells and intestinal epithelial Caco-2 cells, exhibiting good biocompatibility (Supplementary Fig. 11). Together, these results show that the nanodevice exhibits effective cellular uptake, and can remarkably inhibit inflammation and scavenge ROS, which is essential for its use in UC treatment.

Biodistribution of the nanodevice

The selective accumulation of the nanodevice in inflamed intestine, while limiting its distribution in others healthy tissues, is pivotal for ensuring its efficacy and biosafety. To visualize the in vivo biodistribution of the nanodevice, we modified the staple strands with Cy5.5 and then intravenously injected the nanodevice (contained 200 nM Cy5.5) into healthy mice or colitis mice (Fig. 4a). The equal volume of Cy5.5-staple strands (200 nM) were used as a control. The in vivo time-resolved fluorescence imaging was utilized to measure Cy5.5 fluorescence in the anaesthetized state at the indicated times (0.5, 1, 8, and 24 hours) post-injection. The Cy5.5 fluorescence signals in all three groups were predominantly located at the abdomen (Fig. 4b). Both normal and colitis mice treated with Cy5.5-nanodevice showed sustained high fluorescence intensity in the abdomen for 24 hours, while the fluorescence in normal mice treated with Cy5.5-staple strands significantly decreased over time, basically disappearing at 24 hours (Fig. 4b and c). The ex vivo fluorescence imaging analysis further reveal that the fluorescence intensity in intestine was much stronger than that of other organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, and pancreas) at 24 hours post-injection (Fig. 4d). Elevating the dosage of the nanodevice could augment its accumulation within the intestine (Supplementary Fig. 12 and Supplementary Fig. 13). These results demonstrate that the nanodevice selectively accumulates in both healthy and inflamed intestines while sparing other organs. To further verify this, two different dyes (Alexa647 and Cy5) were utilized to label the nanodevice, demonstrating that the intestinal accumulation observed in the experiments does not stem from artifacts introduced by these dyes themselves (Supplementary Fig. 14). Imaging the biodistribution of three differently sized and shaped DNA origami nanostructures (rectangular, triangular, and tubular) further showed that the tubular one accumulated slightly more in the intestine (Supplementary Fig. 15). Previous studies have observed that the DNA origami nanostructures (DONs) mainly accumulate in the liver, kidney or inflamed joints18,35,37,38. These may be caused by different labeling techniques, imaging approaches, and the specific positioning methods adopted for the mice. The combination of both the in vivo and ex vivo imaging for evaluating the biodistribution of DONs is highly necessary. Crucially, it is imperative to incorporate the intestine as one of the major organs during ex vivo imaging to prevent the omission of relevant signals (Supplementary Fig. 16).

Furthermore, the fluorescence imaging of the colonic tissue sections showed that the fluorescence signals of the Cy5.5-nanodevice were located within intestinal cells, and the signal intensity in colitis mice was stronger than in normal mice, suggesting that the nanodevice can be taken up by intestinal cells (Fig. 4e). The main organs, including heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, pancreas, and intestine, were collected for sectioning and ex vivo fluorescence imaging. The strongest fluorescence can be observed in the intestinal section (Fig. 4f), again confirming that the nanodevice primarily accumulated in the intestine. We reasoned that both the geometric properties (the shape and size) and the metabolic pathway of enterohepatic circulation via the liver and gallbladder had enhanced the retention of the DNA nanodevice in the intestine39,40. Furthermore, to visualize the in situ distribution of siRNA with intestinal tissue, Cy3-labeled siTNF-α and Cy5-labeled siInte α4 were co-loaded into the nanodevice and intravenously administered to mice with colitis. The fluorescence imaging confirmed that the nanodevice could effectively deliver these two siRNAs into the intestine (Supplementary Fig. 17). Together, these data demonstrate that the nanodevice not only selectively accumulates within the intestine but also exhibits a preference for being taken up by inflamed intestinal cells, thereby ensuring its efficacy and biosafety.

The robust therapeutic efficacy and biosafety of the nanodevice in vivo

The selective accumulation in the intestine of the nanodevice holds a promising potential for effectively combating UC. Therefore, we further evaluated the in vivo therapeutic efficacy of the nanodevice against DSS-induced colitis. The mouse colitis model was established by administering 3% DSS and the immunofluorescence analysis showed that APE1 was predominantly localized in the nucleus within normal intestinal tissues, while it exhibited nuclear export in inflamed intestinal tissues (Supplementary Fig. 18). Thus, the nanodevice could be unlocked at inflamed intestinal tissues through APE1 while maintaining its inactivity at healthy tissues. The majority of DNA nanodevices exhibited good stability when incubated with non-inactivated fetal bovine serum (10%) and were capable of preserving their structural integrity within murine blood (Supplementary Fig. 19), thereby presenting the potential to shield siRNA throughout its circulation in the bloodstream. Next, we intravenously injected PBS, siTNF&siInte, the nanotube (without loading siTNF&siInte), and the nanodevice into the mice on the 1st and 4th days of modeling (Fig. 5a). The nanodevice exhibited outstanding protective effects on colitis mice, with a 100% survival rate while the mice treated with PBS, siTNF&siInte, and the nanotube exhibited differing levels of mortality (Fig. 5b). Consistently, the nanodevice could significantly reverse weight loss (no weight loss) and colon shorting of the colitis mice compared to other three groups, where the colon length of the nanodevice group recovered to 83% of those in the normal group (Fig. 5c-e). At the experimental endpoint, DAI score in the nanodevice-treated group was significantly lower compared to the DSS-induced model group (Fig. 5f). Specifically, the morphology of the colon tissue in the nanodevice-treated group was nearly indistinguishable from that of the normal group. The intestinal epithelial cells and crypt structures were almost intact, with lowest inflammatory infiltration (Fig. 5g). The histological score was utilized to evaluate the overall tissue injury, and we found that the nanodevice significantly ameliorated the DSS-induced injury when compared to the PBS, siRNA and nanotube group (Fig. 5h). These data demonstrate that the nanodevice exhibits robust therapeutic efficacy on colitis mice.

Interestingly, nanotube treatment slightly reduced the mortality rate, improved weight loss, prevented colon shortening, and lowered histological scores of mice, attributing this to the ROS scavenging by DNA origami-based structures. The examination of the oxidative stress markers in the colon revealed that both the nanodevice and nanotube decreased malondialdehyde (MDA) and MPO levels (Supplementary Fig. 20), demonstrating that the DNA origami-based structures can protect intestinal tissues from oxidative stress in colitis mice. Furthermore, the nanotube showed negligible immune response (Supplementary Fig. 21). The nanodevice exhibited good in vivo biosafety, with no observed toxicity to the liver or kidneys, and no abnormalities in serum biochemical indices (Supplementary Fig. 22). Together, these results demonstrate that the nanodevice exhibits excellent therapeutic efficacy and biosafety in the mice UC model.

The nanodevice inhibits intestinal inflammation and homing of T cells

The siRNA-based therapy can reduce target gene expression via RISC-mediated mRNA degradation6. Therefore, we further explored the target genes silencing efficiency of nanodevice in vivo and the underlying mechanism of alleviating UC. RT-qPCR of colonic tissue showed that the nanodevice significantly reduced the expression of TNF-α and integrin α4 (Fig. 6a), which may be attributed to the outstanding ability of the nanodevice for targeted delivery of siTNF&siInte into intestine and selectively releasing them through translocated APE1 in inflamed intestine tissues. Immunofluorescence staining was conducted to assess the protein expression levels of TNF-α and integrin α4 in the colon, revealing that nanodevice treatment markedly reduced their expression (Fig. 6b). More specifically, CD3 and integrin α4 double staining indicated that the nanodevice treatment significantly reduced the expression of integrin α4 in T cells (Supplementary Fig. 23). These results demonstrate that the nanodevice can effectively silence the genes of both TNF-α and integrin α4 and subsequently downregulate their protein expression.

The downregulation of TNF-α effectively inhibits the recruitment of intestinal macrophages to inflammatory sites. Similarly, the downregulation of integrin α4 results in a diminished binding to the ligand MAdCAM-1, which in turn impedes the migration of T cells into the intestine41, subsequently reducing the infiltration of immune cells and expression of inflammatory factors (Fig. 6c). The immunofluorescence staining of F4/80 and CD3 showed that nanodevice treatment significantly reduced the recruitment of intestinal macrophages and the homing of T cells (Fig. 6d–f). Similarly, MPO activity, an indicator of neutrophil infiltration, was significantly reduced by nanodevice (Supplementary Fig. 20). Furthermore, the nanodevice significantly inhibited the phosphorylation of p65/IκB (Supplementary Fig. 24) and the expression of the downstream inflammatory cytokine IL-1β and IL-6 in colon tissues (Fig. 6g, h).

The infiltration of inflammatory cells and the subsequent release of inflammatory factors will lead to apoptosis and necrosis of intestinal epithelial cells (IECs), ultimately disrupting the intestinal barrier and exacerbating intestinal inflammation. We assessed IECs apoptosis and intestinal barrier through immunofluorescence staining for the apoptosis marker cleaved-caspase 3 and the tight junction protein ZO-1. As expected, nanodevice treatment obviously suppressed apoptosis of IECs and restored the intestinal barrier (Fig. 6i and Fig. 6j). WB of colonic tissue corroborated this, demonstrating a restoration of tight junction proteins ZO-1 and occludin, as well as a reduction of cleaved-caspase 3 and its downstream substrate cleaved-PARP in the nanodevice group (Fig. 6k and Supplementary Fig. 25). Overall, these results suggest that the nanodevice can effectively silence TNF-α and integrin α4 in vivo, thereby inhibiting intestinal inflammation, preventing T-cell intestinal homing, reducing apoptosis, and restoring the intestinal barrier, ultimately achieving efficient treatment of UC.

Discussion

Dynamic DNA origami allows the protection of biomolecules during circulation and effective exposure of them at targeted sites, serving as a promising foundational element for the development of intelligent biomedical nanodevices when integrated with therapeutic RNA. Here, we report a DNA origami-based nanodevice that normally shields siRNAs but selectively exposes them through translocated APE1 in inflamed sites. The addressability of DNA origami allows the nanodevice to accurately load two types of siRNAs within its cavity, in a precisely controllable number and ratio, thereby enabling combination therapy with siRNAs in UC. The dynamic reconfigurability of this nanodevice enables it to protect the siRNAs in the bloodstream and expose them at intestinal inflamed sites.

Though many targeting nanovectors have been developed for siRNA delivery in UC treatment, the off-target effect is inevitable. The off-targeted nanovectors enhance the cellular internalization of siRNA in healthy tissues, resulting in more adverse immune reactions compared to siTNF&siInte. Our strategy aims to achieve selective exposure of therapeutic RNAs at intestinal inflamed sites while maintaining their inactivity at healthy tissues. The distinct distribution of APE1 from normal cells to inflamed cells enabled us to develop such an inflammation-responsive nanodevice. This nanodevice predominantly accumulates in the intestine and autonomously inhibits multiple pathogenic genes, which significantly improves therapeutic efficacy for UC and reduces side effects. The mechanism underlying the predominant intestinal accumulation of DNA nanodevice requires further elucidation to provide mechanistic insights for developing optimized intestine-targeted DNA origami-based delivery vectors. Our results demonstrate that the smart DNA nanodevice is a promising nanovector to optimize the assembly, delivery, and exposure of siRNAs.

Methods

Preparation of capture-DNA origami nanosheet

All DNA oligonucleotides strands and siRNA strands were purchased from BBI Life Sciences Corporation. To synthesize the capture-DNA origami nanosheet, a mixture was prepared containing 100 nM of capture strands, 10 nM of M13 strand, and 50 nM of staple strands in a 1 × TAE-Mg2+ buffer solution (comprising 40 mM Tris, 20 mM acetic acid, 2 mM EDTA, and 12.5 mM magnesium acetate, pH = 8.0). This mixture was then annealed by gradually reducing the temperature from 95 °C to 4 °C (Supplementary Data 1). Subsequently, a PEG buffer (composed of 15% PEG8000, 5 mM Tris, 1 mM EDTA, and 505 mM NaCl) was added to the mixture in equal volumes. The combined solution was centrifuged at 10,000 g for 15 min. Following the removal of the supernatant, the precipitate was resuspended in 1 × TAE-Mg2+ buffer and subjected to shaking at 40 °C and 400 rpm for a duration of 8 h. The mass concentration (Cmass) of the obtained capture-DNA origami nanosheet was determined using the NanoDrop one (Thermo Scientific). The final molar concentration of the nanoseet can be calculated through a formula of experience (Cmolar = Cmass/4.478 nM).

The assemble of the siTNF-α and the siInte α4 with capture-nanosheet

Both the siTNF-α and the siInte α4 strands consist of 21-nucleotide (nt) bases that can hybridize with the capture strands on the nanosheet. Thus, to load the siTNF-α and the siInte α4 strands onto the nanosheet, 2 µM of the siTNF-α strand and 2 µM of the siInte α4 strand were mixed with 10 nM purified capture-nanosheet at 37 °C for 1 h. After that, the excess siTNF-α and siInte α4 strands were removed using a PEG buffer method mentioned above. The final concentration was calculated using the experience formula mentioned above.

The loading number of the siTNF-α and the siInte α4

The siTNF-α strand was modified with FAM and the siInte α4 strand was modified with Cy5. After obtained siRNA loaded nanosheet (siNanosheet), excess siTNF-α and siInte α4 strands were removed using a PEG buffer method. The concentrations of siTNF-α and siInte α4 were quantified by fluorescent spectrometry. The NanoDrop one (Thermo Scientific) was utilized to quantify the concentration of nanosheet (Cnanosheet = Cmass/4.478 nM). The loading number of siTNF-α or siInte α4 was defined as n = CsiTNF-α/Cnanosheet or CsiInte α4/Cnanosheet.

Synthesis of the nanodevice

A DNA duplex structure (Supplementary Data 1), named AP locker, is designed to curl the siRNA-loaded DNA nanosheet (siNanosheet) into a tubular nanodevice. In brief, 100 nM AP locker strands were mixed with 10 nM siNanosheet in a 1 × TAE-Mg2+ buffer solution and then annealed from 60 °C to 36 °C (Supplementary Data 1). The excess AP locker strands were removed using a PEG buffer method mentioned above. The mass concentration (Cmass) of the obtained DNA nanodevice was determined using the NanoDrop one (Thermo Scientific). The final molar concentration of the nanodevice can be calculated through a formula of experience (Cmolar = Cmass/4.478 nM).

Synthesis of the triangular DNA origami

The triangular DNA origami nanosheets were fabricated via an annealing process, employing 10 nM of the M13 strand and 50 nM of pre-designed staple strands (Supplementary Data 1) in a 1 × TAE-Mg2+ buffer solution, with a temperature gradient cooling from 95 °C to 4 °C. Subsequently, a PEG buffer was added to the mixture in equal volumes. The combined solution was centrifuged at 10,000 g for 15 min. Following the removal of the supernatant, the precipitate was resuspended in 1 × TAE-Mg2+ buffer and subjected to shaking at 40 °C and 400 rpm for a duration of 8 hours.

The AFM imaging

In brief, 5 µL sample (2 nM) was dropped onto the mica surface for 5 minutes. Before scanning in tapping mode, we added 50 µL of 1 × TAE-Mg2+ buffer. The scanning was done using a J scanner on the Multi-mode Nanoscope IIIa AFM (Vecco/Digital Instruments), equipped with a silicon nitride cantilever with a sharp pyramidal tip (OMCL-TR400PSA, Olympus).

Agarose Gel Electrophoresis

In brief, agarose gel electrophoresis (AGE) was conducted using a GelRed stained 1% agarose gel in 1 × TAE buffer at room temperature for approximately 1–1.5 h. After that the gel was subjected to imaging and analysis.

The enzyme-triggerable unlocking by APE1

The unlocking of AP locker: The linker4 was modified with BHQ2 and the linker5 was modified with Cy3. The fluorescence of Cy3 within the AP locker was initially quenched by the BHQ2. 500 nM of Cy3-BHQ2-AP locker was mixed with 5 U/mL APE1(New England Biolabs, #M0282S), then the fluorescence intensity of Cy3 was monitored using a microplate reader (BioTek, SYNERGY H1) at 37 °C.

The unlocking of nanodevice: The 10 nM of nanodevice formed by Cy3-BHQ2-AP locker was mixed with 5 U/mL APE1, then the fluorescence intensity of Cy3 was monitored using a microplate reader (BioTek, SYNERGY H1) at 37 °C.

Mice

Male C57BL/6 J mice at 7-8 weeks were purchased from SiPeiFu Biological Company (Beijing, China). Mice were housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions with a controlled 12 h light/12 h dark cycle, temperature of 23 °C ± 2 °C, and relative humidity of 50 ± 10%. Animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Sichuan University (Approval Number: 20240227012).

Mice UC model and treatment

Mice were provided with 3% DSS for 7 days to induce UC model. Mice were randomly divided into five groups. Normal group received sterile water, and the other four groups were given DSS. On the first and fourth days, PBS, siRNA (200 nM), Nanotube (10 nM), and Nanodevice (10 nM) were injected through the tail vein, respectively. The mortality rate, body weight, and fecal consistency of the mice were recorded daily. On the 8th day, the mice were euthanized, colon was collected and stored for further analysis.

Disease activity index

The disease activity index (DAI) was employed to assess the grade and extent of intestinal inflammation based on the previous report42. The DAI score was calculated as the mean value of three parameters: weight loss, stool consistency, and rectal bleeding.

Histological score

About 0.5 cm colonic tissues of from the distal rectum were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and alcohol dehydration before embedded in paraffin. 5 μm tissue sections were performed H&E staining for histological assessment. Histological score was calculated as previously described43.

Immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence

Tissue sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated. After being treated with 3% hydrogen peroxide, the slices underwent antigen retrieval and blocking with 5% BSA, and then were incubated overnight at 4 °C with TNF-α (CST, #11948, USA), integrin α4 (CST, #8440, USA), CD3 (Abcam, #ab16669, UK), F4/80 (Abcam, #ab300421, UK) and APE1 (Abcam, #ab189474, UK) antibodies respectively. Secondary antibodies incubation and fluorescence assays were performed according to the IHC Detection Kit manual (Zen BioScience, #18002, China).

For the immunofluorescence of APE1, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 minutes, followed by permeabilization and blocking. Then incubated with APE1 antibody, and FITC-labeled secondary antibody.

In vivo biosafety assessments

To evaluate the biosafety via tail vein injection of nanodevice in mice. Mice were intravenously administered 200 μL of 10 nM nanodevice or PBS twice weekly for four consecutive weeks. Tissues and serum were collected on day 30. Throughout the experimental period, body weights were monitored longitudinally. At the endpoint, heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney tissues along with plasma samples were harvested for histopathological analysis and blood biochemical parameter evaluation, respectively. The serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), creatinine and urea levels were detected by automatic biochemical analyzer according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

In vivo biodistribution of nanodevice and siRNA in situ detection

To evaluate the biodistribution and retention of nanodevice in mice, Cy5.5-labeled nanodevice (10 nM) and ssDNA (200 nM) were intravenously injected to mice in normal and DSS groups. After 0.5 h, 1 h, 8 h and 24 h, the fluorescence in the abdomen of mice was detected by small animal in vivo imaging system (Spectral Instruments Imaging, USA). After 24 hours, the mice were euthanized, and removed heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, pancreas, and intestine was imaged by small animal in vivo imaging system, and the results were statistically analyzed. ‌Further freeze-section these major organs, then quantify the fluorescence distribution within the tissue sections.

To detect the siRNA in situ, Cy3 labeled siTNF-α, Cy5-labeled siInte α4 were loaded into the nanodevice. Administration of 10 nM nanodevices to colitis mice by the tail vein equivalent to injecting 200 nM siTNF&siInte. Four hours after injection, the intestine was collected for sectioning and fluorescence imaging.

Stability analysis of the nanodevice

The nanodevice was labeled with Cy3 or Cy3-BHQ2. The fluorescence of Cy3 was quenched by BHQ2 when the nanodevice kept locked state. The 2 nM nanodevice and 100 μL of mouse blood were co-incubated at 37 °C for indicated time. Cy3 fluorescence signals were detected using a microplate reader (BioTek, SYNERGY H1) at 10, 30, 60, and 120 min.

MPO and MDA analysis

The colonic tissue was homogenized in cold PBS, myeloperoxidase (MPO) activity and malondialdehyde (MDA) levels was measured by assay kit (Nanjing Jiancheng and Beyotime, China).

Cell culture

Human colon cancer Caco-2 cells (HTB-37) and mouse macrophage RAW264.7(TIB-71) were all purchased from American Type Culture Collection. RAW264.7 and Caco-2 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 and DMEM medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin and streptomycin, and maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

Cell uptake and siRNA lysosome escape

Caco-2 cells (2 × 105) were seeded on 35 mm confocal dish, and were treat with Cy3-labeled siTNFα, Cy7-labeled siInte α4 (200 nM), nanodevice (10 nM) for 6 h. Cells were washed with PBS and stained with Hoechst. To study the endocytosis pathway of the nanodevice, Caco-2 cells pretreated for 1 h either 5 mM methyl-β-cyclodextrin (MβCD) or 500 mM sucrose (Sur), followed by 6 h incubation with Cy5 labeled nanodevice. Cells were subsequently stained with 2 μg/mL CellMask™ Green plasma membrane stain for 5 min and counterstained with Hoechst for nuclear visualization.

To study the siRNA escape from lysosome, the Caco-2 cells were seeded on 35 mm confocal dish with 2 × 104 cells. The cells were incubated with Cy3-labeled free siTNF-α (200 nM) or Cy3-siRNA with nanodevice (10 nM) for 4 h, and then the lysosome was stained with 50 nM LysoTracker for 20 min. The intracellular fluorescence was detected by a fluorescence microscope (Leica Thunder Imager DMI8, Germany).

siRNA unwinding assay

Caco-2 cell lysates were extracted by freeze-thaw and subsequently incubated with double-stranded siRNA37 for 2 h at 37 °C for AGE analysis. The siΤΝF-α antisense was modified with BHQ2, and siΤΝF-α linker1-sense was modified with Cy3. The fluorescence kinetic of the siΤΝF-α duplex unwinded by Ago2 (0.1, 0.25, 0.5 µg) was monitored by a microplate reader (BioTek, SYNERGY H1) for 60 min at 37 °C.

RT-qPCR

Total RNA extraction from colonic tissues and cells by Trizol reagent (Aidlab Biotechnologies, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RT-qPCR was performed using cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (Yeasen Biotechnology, China) for reverse transcription and an SsoAdvanced Universal SYBR Green (Bio-Rad, USA)-based real-time PCR detection system. Primers sequences were list in Supplementary Data 1.

Western blot

Cells were lysed using RIPA buffer, and protein concentrations were determined by BCA assay. Proteins were then separated by 10% SDS-PAGE, followed by transfer to PVDF membrane and blocking with 5% skim milk. The membrane was incubated with primary antibody TNF-α, integrin α4, ZO-1 (Proteintech, #21773-1-AP, China), Occludin (Abclonal, #A24601, China), Iκb-α/p-Iκb-α (Proteintech, #10268-1-AP, #82349-1-RR, China), NF-κB-p65(Proteintech, #10745-1-AP, China) NF-κB-p-p65(Immunoway, #YP0191, USA), cleaved-caspase3 (CST, #9664, USA), PARP (CST, #9532, USA), β- actin (Zen BioScience, #250132, China) overnight at 4 °C, and incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody for 1 hour. Finally, the blots were visualized using ECL kit (Bio-Rad, USA).

Free radicals scavenging assay

Hydroxyl radicals (•OH) were generated through the Fenton reaction by mixing hydrogen peroxide (H2O2, 3 mM) with ferrous chloride (FeCl2, 1 mM) in ultrapure water at room temperature for 30 min. The reaction mixture was then incubated with either: (1) ultrapure water (control), (2) M13 (10 nM), (3) Nanotube (5 nM), or (4) Nanodevice (5 nM) for 30 min at room temperature. Following incubation, methylenebenzidine (MB, 30 μM) was added as a •OH-specific probe and to react for 2 h. The •OH-scavenging capacity was determined by measuring the absorbance of MB at 666 nm using a spectrophotometer, following established protocols44.

Intracellular ROS detection

5 × 104 Caco-2 cells were seeded into 24-well plate, then treated with nanotube (10 nm) for 2 h. Caco-2 cells were additionally treated with ROSup for 1 h. After incubation with DCFH-DA probe (Beyotime, Cat. No. S0033S) for 20 min, the intracellular fluorescence was detected by a fluorescence microscope (Leica Thunder Imager DMI8, Germany).

CCK8 assay

5 × 103 Caco-2 and RAW264.7 cells were seeded into 96-well plate. Cells were treated with different concentrations of nanodevices (0, 2.5, 5, 10,15 nm) for 24 h, followed by incubation with CCK8 reagent for 1 hour. The Cell viability was measured at a wavelength of 450 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek, SYNERGY H1, USA) at 37 °C.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) derived from a minimum of three independent replicates. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software, with one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) employed for multiple group comparisons and two-tailed Student’s t-test applied for pairwise comparisons. Details of the statistical methods used for each dataset are provided in the figure legends. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
