Assessing lightweight foamed concrete multi-property with alkaline, calcium, and iron-rich cement kiln dust variants
Authors: Md Azree Othuman Mydin, Roshartini Omar, Khairunisa Muthusamy, Blessen Skariah Thomas, Mohd Mustafa Al Bakri Abdullah, Paul O. Awoyera, Olaolu George Fadugba
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cement production accounts for nearly 8% of global CO₂ emissions, driving the search for sustainable alternatives in concrete manufacturing. Lightweight foamed concrete (LFC) offers benefits in insulation and reduced structural weight but relies heavily on ordinary Portland cement (OPC). This study evaluates the incorporation of three cement kiln dust (CKD) variants, alkaline-rich (AR), calcium-rich (CR), and iron-rich (IR), as 40% partial replacements for OPC in LFC with a target density of 1000 kg/m³. The experimental program included fresh-state tests (slump flow, setting time, density), mechanical tests (compressive, flexural, and splitting tensile strength at 7, 14, 28, 56, 90, and 180 days; modulus of elasticity), transport properties (water absorption, permeable porosity), thermal properties (conductivity, diffusivity, specific heat), and microstructural analysis (SEM at 200× magnification). All tests followed relevant BS EN and ASTM standards. Three replicate specimens were tested per mix per age; error bars in figures represent standard deviation. CR-CKD LFC achieved the highest mechanical performance: compressive strength of 4.32 ± 0.21 MPa at 180 days (18% higher than control), flexural strength of 0.92 ± 0.05 MPa (20% higher), and splitting tensile strength of 0.64 ± 0.03 MPa (23% higher). Water absorption decreased from 21.50% (control) to 19.90%, and porosity from 43.80% to 42.21%. AR-CKD showed inferior performance due to high alkali content (Na₂O + K₂O = 16.32%), which increased porosity (45.45%) and reduced strength. IR-CKD gave moderate improvements but did not outperform CR-CKD. Thermal properties were not enhanced by any CKD variant. These findings are applicable to non-structural LFC components such as partition walls, floor toppings, insulation panels, and void filling, where moderate strength and reduced weight are required.

Introduction

Concrete is a vital construction material worldwide; however, its widespread use poses significant environmental issues since cement production accounts for nearly 8% of global CO₂ emissions1. As infrastructure demands increase, there is growing interest in alternative building materials such as lightweight foamed concrete (LFC), which offers reduced weight, improved insulation, and environmental sustainability2,3. LFC is commonly used for void filling, insulating walls, and thermal barriers, particularly in high-rise structures where mass is critical4.

A major challenge in producing LFC is its reliance on conventional cement, which consumes natural resources and generates high CO₂ emissions. Over one-third of LFC comprises cement as a binder5. Consequently, supplementary cementitious materials (SCMs) are increasingly used to partially replace ordinary Portland cement (OPC). Cement kiln dust (CKD), a fine powder generated during clinker production—has physical and chemical characteristics similar to OPC and can exhibit both pozzolanic and cementitious properties6–8.

When combined with CKD, LFC represents an attractive alternative to traditional mixtures9. However, the impact of CKD on LFC remains insufficiently studied, particularly for CKDs with varying chemical compositions. Different CKD forms, alkaline-rich, calcium-rich, and iron-rich—may affect the workability, durability, and mechanical properties of LFC differently10,11. Alkaline-rich CKD contains high levels of sodium and potassium oxides, which can influence setting time and spreadability. Calcium-rich CKD is known to enhance early-age and long-term mechanical properties. Iron-rich CKD, though less extensively investigated, may affect thermal properties and high-temperature resistance12.

Previous studies have investigated CKD in conventional concrete with promising results. Hasan et al.13 showed that CKD can enhance compressive strength, particularly in low-density mixes. CKD also exhibits pozzolanic properties, reacting with lime and water to form strength-contributing compounds. Additionally, using CKD addresses waste disposal problems, as cement plants generate large quantities of CKD that typically require expensive disposal14.

Most prior research on CKD has focused on traditional concrete rather than LFC. Furthermore, there is a lack of information regarding the effects of alkaline, calcium, and iron contents of CKD on LFC properties. This study specifically investigates the influence of different CKD variants on multiple properties of LFC, aiming to determine the optimal CKD variant that yields the best balance of characteristics.

The chemical composition of CKD is critical for evaluating its effectiveness as a partial cement replacement. Alkaline-rich CKD (high Na₂O and K₂O) may promote C–S–H gel formation but could also cause alkali-silica reaction (ASR) with certain aggregates15. Calcium-rich CKD enhances early strength and long-term stability because calcium oxide reacts with water to form dense cementitious materials16. Iron-rich CKD, though less studied, may improve thermal stability and durability under high-temperature conditions17.

Table 1 summarises previous studies incorporating CKD in concrete applications. Many studies have demonstrated positive contributions of CKD. High-calcium CKD improved compressive strength and toughness, while alkaline CKD improved workability and early strength11. However, the use of CKD in LFC, especially with different chemical compositions, has not been well characterised. The inclusion of CKD is believed to enhance compressive strength and durability while maintaining low density6.

Despite the potential of CKD as a supplementary cementitious material, the influence of its chemical composition (alkaline, calcium, or iron-rich) on the properties of lightweight foamed concrete (LFC) has not been systematically studied. This research addresses this gap by investigating the effects of three CKD variants—alkaline-rich (AR), calcium-rich (CR), and iron-rich (IR). As partial replacements for OPC in LFC. The study evaluates fresh-state properties (workability, setting time, density), mechanical properties (compressive, flexural, tensile strength, modulus of elasticity), transport properties (water absorption, porosity), thermal properties (conductivity, diffusivity, specific heat), and microstructure (SEM). The findings provide guidance on selecting CKD variants for LFC applications and support the use of industrial by-products to reduce cement consumption and environmental impact.

Materials and methods

The study investigated the effect of incorporation and possible application of three types of CKD, explicitly alkaline-rich (AR-CKD), calcium-rich (CR-CKD) and iron-rich (IR-CKD) as SCMs to OPC for producing LFC. Details of the raw materials used in this study, LFC mix design and methods for production of stable LFC will be further described in the subsequent sub-sections.

Raw materials

The raw materials used in this work for LFC production were OPC, three different types of CKD (AR, CR and IR), fine aggregate, clean water and foaming agent. OPC, CEM I grade 52.5, was provided by Lafarge Malaysia. OPC was prepared according to BS EN 197-126. The three varieties of CKD were sourced from local vendors in Malaysia. The physical appearance of CKD and OPC is shown in Fig. 1. Figure 2 presents the particle size distribution for cement and CKD contents. CKD variants were obtained from cement plants specialising in production of environmentally friendly by-products in the cement manufacturing process. Hume cement was the source of alkaline-rich (high pH) CKD. Tasek Corp Berhad supplied the calcium-rich CKD with a relatively higher amount of calcium oxide while Cahya Mata Sarawak (CMS) was the source for an iron-rich CKD which had a high content of iron oxide. Each of these variants has been specifically chosen for its unique chemical structure that improves different elements of LFC performance. The chemical compositions of OPC and CKD are given in Table 2. From Table 2; Fig. 2, it can be observed that the chemical compositions of CKD types are quite different from OPC in respect to the values related to main elements such as CaO, SiO2 and Na2O percentages. CKD (alkaline) contains large amounts of sodium oxide and potassium oxide, which could affect the properties of LFC such as setting time and spreadability. The calcium-based CKD, which is about equivalent in its content of CaO to the OPC, is likely the most similar to the effectiveness it may bind. The CKD with high iron content, however, has higher levels of Fe2O3 and %LOI that may modify its colorimetry appearance and strength characteristic. These alterations of chemical composition will affect property of LFC. The physical and mechanical properties of OPC and CKD are presented in Table 3. CKD has usually a smaller specific surface area than OPC due to its composition and fineness of the particles. Higher cementitious content calcium-rich CKD could have similar specific surface area to OPC. With iron-rich CKD, the particles are slightly larger and have an inferior surface area compared to OPC. Furthermore, since the CKD rich in alkalis (high Na2O and K2O content) is likely to be associated with slightly higher final set than OPC. Due to its high CaO content, calcium-rich CKD would probably set in longer time closer to that of OPC, while iron-rich CKD may even have the quickest setting because of strong effect of iron oxides and silicates. Calcium-based CKD is similar to OPC in terms of compressive and flexural strength; however, alkaline-rich and iron-rich CKDs may have considerable deviations in the setting time, strength development as well as density. These disparities highlight the potential of using CKDs as a replacement for OPC, especially in economic and sustainable LFC production.

Fine aggregate was acquired from a local supplier Tinta Mass Trading Sdn. Bhd., exhibiting a specific gravity of 2.61 under saturated surface-dry conditions. Figure 3 displays the sieve analysis results of the fine aggregate which is in accordance with the ASTM C33-0327. Clean tap water was utilized during mixing and curing, in agreement with the specifications of BS En 100828. The protein-based foaming agent in the mixture generated a foam density of 70 ± 5 kg/m³ when combined with water at a 1:30 ratio.

Mix design

The target density for LFC was set at 1000 kg/m³. This density balances structural requirements, workability, and self-weight, and is commonly used for insulation, floor fill, and semi-load bearing walls.

Four LFC mixtures were prepared: a control mix (C) with no CKD, and three mixes with 40% OPC replaced by alkaline-rich (AR), calcium-rich (CR), and iron-rich (IR) CKD. A 40% replacement level was selected for this study. This is higher than the typical optimal CKD dosage reported in the literature (typically 5–20%18,21,22). The higher replacement level was chosen to test the upper limit of CKD utilisation in LFC and to maximise the environmental benefit of reducing OPC content. The sand-to-cement ratio was fixed at 1:1.5 and the water-to-cement ratio at 0.47 to ensure adequate workability. Table 4 shows the LFC mix design.

LFC mixing process

Once all raw materials were measured and prepared, the protein-based foam density and the flow rate were determined. The stable foam was produced using PM-2 foam generator machine. Firstly, fine aggregate was added into the drum mixer, along with 15% of the clean water, and then OPC with CKD were slowly incorporated. Throughout the mixing activity, everything was blended for around six minutes to make sure consistent blend. Following that, the rest of the water was gradually included until the mix reached its ideal form. Next, a typical one-liter cup with the base mix in it was measured to determine the mortar density. Subsequently, a specific amount of foam was incorporated to the mixture to achieve the desired density. After the stable foam had been fully mixed, the density of the LFC was measured. To cure the LFC samples, a sealed curing method was used. The samples were wrapped in plastic wrap and the procedure persisted up until the day of testing. Each experimental test was conducted on three replicate specimens, and the reported values represent the mean results. Error bars presented in the figures indicate the standard deviation of the measured values.

Fresh properties evaluation

Fresh state properties were assessed to ensure practical workability. In this study, Slump flow of different mixes was obtained with the flow table test method in accordance with ASTM C230-9729. Furthermore, the initial and final setting times of LFC blends were also determined following BS196-330. The densities of such varied mixtures were then determined in compliance with the BS 12350-631.

Mechanical properties assessment

Understanding the mechanical behaviour of LFC is necessary to understand its strength, toughness and suitability in different circumstances. LFC has a lower density than normal concrete due to air pores induced by the foaming agent, and its mechanical properties exhibit great variations according to the mix design, type of foaming agent, and curing condition compared with standard concrete. The compressive strength of the cube specimens with a dimension size of 100 mm x 100 mm x 100 mm was tested at the ages of 7, 14, 28, 56, 90 and180 days. The compressive test was carried out according to BS12390-332. After that, flexural test was carried out in accordance with BS12390-533 using prismatic beam (100 × 100 × 500 mm). A split tensile strength test was also conducted with disc-shaped specimens 100 and 200 mm in diameter. The procedure was according to BS12390-634. The LFC modulus of elasticity was also calculated according to BS En 12390–1335 with the standard cylindrical specimens of diameter 100 mm and height 200 mm. Three LFC specimens per age were tested to find out the average strength properties.

Transport properties evaluation

In particular, the transport properties of LFC are highly important since it will determine the durability, performance and long-term sustainability in practical application. Due to its light weight and permeable nature, LFC has different transport properties compared with the normal strength concrete, which may also lead to different performance under various environmental and loading conditions. In this research, water absorption and permeable porosity of different LFC mixes were determined. Water absorption test was carried out using LFC with specimens of 75 mm diameter and height 100 mm according to the method described in BS1881-12236. Then, a permeable porosity test was conducted in the methodology of Cabrera-Lynsdale37. The procedure used FC cylinders of 45 mm diameter and 50 mm height.

Thermal properties assessment

Understanding of thermal properties ensures that the material performs properly in its intended uses, which include construction, insulation materials and green buildings. In this study, Guarded Hot Plate (HGP) test method was selected to evaluate the LFC thermal conductivity, thermal diffusivity and specific heat of different blends. The method recommended by ASTM C17738 was used. For the experiment, LFC samples were made 25 mm × 25 mm × 8 mm.

Microstructural analysis

Microstructural investigation is indispensable for full understanding of the composition, properties and performance of LFC. These studies provide valuable information about the internal pore structure and network of LFC, thereby improving its design, performance and durability. LFC samples were examined using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) at approximately 200× magnification, following ISO 16,70039. This study was conducted on 12 mm×12 mm×12 mm rectangular samples.

Results and discussion

Fresh properties

Workability (slump flow)

Figure 4 shows the slump flow of LFC mixtures. The control mix (C) achieved 248 mm. The control mix showed adequate slump flow, as expected for OPC. The fine particle size and intense reactivity of OPC with water typically yield a good slump flow, establishing a balance between ease of application and adequate cohesiveness to prevent segregation. With the addition of AR, CR and IR, the slump flow of LFC mixtures affected in different way. Incorporation of AR reduced the slump flow to 246 mm. AR-CKD exhibits decreased reactivity in comparison to OPC. This decreases the fluidity of the LFC paste, probably leading to a denser mixture and hence decreasing the slump flow. AR-CKD raised the air void content within the mixture. An increase in foam and air spaces often reduces slump flow due to increased friction and structural intricacy.

Similar reductions in slump flow with alkaline CKD have been reported40. Replacing OPC with CR-CKD has an opposing effect to that of AR-CKD. AR-CKD contains significant amounts of soluble alkalis (Na₂O and K₂O, see Table 2). These alkalis increase the pH of the pore solution, which can accelerate the early hydration of OPC and promote the formation of C–(N)–S–H (sodium-substituted calcium silicate hydrate)9,14,23. However, this does not indicate that AR-CKD itself is highly reactive. Rather, the alkalis act as chemical activators for OPC hydration. CR-CKD helps in reducing the voids and would enable a paste, which is more uniform, and thus could improve workability. Higher flow provides smoother placement and minimizes the chance of voids or honeycombs in the LFC. This could be an advantage for non-structural uses where ease of installation and finishing is more important.

IR-CKD has relatively low reactivity compared with AR and CR CKDs. It may not influence much on slump flow either way but can slightly reduce the slump fl. ow, since its hydration activity is higher than that of CR-CKD. The measured slump flow was 247 mm. The slight reduction on workability will be within acceptable values for many applications, but to reach the desired flowability adjustment of water content or additives may be necessary. Therefore, understanding the impact of these constituents on LFC’s slump flow can contribute to optimizing the mix design for a specific application where foam strength and stiffness, along with thermal properties will be preserved while ensuring that adequate workability for easy casting and finishing is maintained.

Setting time

The initial and final setting times for various LFC blends with the control mixture (C) is shown in Fig. 5, which indicated an initial setting time of 129 min and a final setting time of 145 min. The delays were higher when the AR-CKD was blended, with an initial and final setting time of 145 min and 274 min, respectively. CR-CKD blend had the lowest retardation, having initial and final setting times of 121 and 226 min, respectively, whereas IR-CKD reported a delayed hardening time of 140 min for initial setting and 262 min for final setting. The variation could be attributed to the lower reactivity of CKD as compared to OPC and with increase in alkali content in AR-CKD delaying the hydration rates and consequently the initial and final setting times. Delayed setting with alkaline-rich CKD is consistent with previous observations21. AR-CKD significantly delayed both initial and final setting times (145 min and 274 min, respectively) compared to the control (129 min and 145 min). This retardation is attributed to the high alkali content of AR-CKD (Na₂O + K₂O = 16.32%, Table 2). Three mechanisms explain this effect49,55,56:

These effects are most pronounced during the first 24–48 h. Once the alkali sulfates are gradually consumed or diluted, hydration resumes. In contrast, CR-CKD contains much lower alkali content (Na₂O + K₂O = 1.46%) and showed faster setting (initial 121 min, final 226 min). This confirms that the retardation observed in AR-CKD is specifically due to alkalis, not to CKD substitution in general.

This indicates that CKD, particularly AR-CKD being of higher alkalinity and relatively more chemically inert in nature is retarding hydration and causing a slowing down of setting. The reduced reactivity of CKD and its narrower particle size distribution increase the water demand thus prolonging setting times. The identified delays clearly demonstrate the need for careful tuning of LFC mix design and curing procedures, particularly in mass applications where rapid release of formwork and adequate early strength gain is important41.

Density

Figure 6 represents the dry density of different LFC mixes, highlighting the influence of incorporation of different CKD types. The dry density value of the control mix (C) was 1002 kg/m³ and provided a reference for comparison. The lowest dry density was 975 kg/m³ for the AR-CKD mix, followed by the value of 980 kg/m³ for IR-CKD mix. The dry density of the CR-CKD mix was 1005 kg/m³, slightly higher than that of control. The reduction in dry density of the CKD-hybrid mixtures is attributed to the lower specific gravity of CKD as compared to OPC. The OPC is partly replaced by CKD, and the total weight of the mixture is reduced so that its density decreases. The AR-CKD blend showed the lowest decrease of dry unit weight, denoting that CKD rich in alkalinity can have a reduced effect on reducing total weight of mixture which is likely due to the light and porous particles42. The result is consistent with recently mentioned as above that CKD lowers the density of a cementitious material, therefore causing to lightweight in mortar25. Despite the reduction in dry density, such lightweight properties could be advantageous for parts requiring reduced weight of material, and particularly structural components benefiting from enhanced thermal insulation.

Mechanical properties

Compressive strength

Figure 7 shows the development of compressive strength in various LFCs blends after curing for 7, 14, 28, 56, 90 and 180 days. Compressive strength of control mix (C) increased continuously and reached a peak value of 2.12 MPa at 7 days and attained the maximum value of 3.65 MPa at180 days with an increase by 72%. Strength increased steadily with curing age. AR-CKD blend exhibited lower strength gain, gaining strength up to 3.47 MPa at the age of 180 days. This retarding effect at high alkali concentrations (Na₂O + K₂O = 16.32%) differs from the accelerating effect observed in alkali-activated systems with lower alkali dosages14. The difference arises because high concentrations of soluble alkalis form alkali sulfates that precipitate on cement grains and suppress C–S–H nucleation49,55. On the other hand, mix CR-CKD, showed the greatest improvement of strength with a value of 4.32 MPa at 180 days and this was an increase by 87% from original strength (2.31 MPa) at 7 days. This improvement is consistent with the known effect of higher calcium content on hydration43. Mix IR-CKD achieved the highest increase as well at 3.61 MPa at 180th days which is 79% increased from that of at 7th day (2.02 MPa). IR-CKD imparts strength, but less so than calcium, as a result of iron’s very low contribution to hydration. The results indicate that the LFC mixtures with CKD improve compressive strength; CR-CKD was found to be effective. Such mixes, especially those with CR- CKD are suitable for lightweight uses in non-structure such as construction of partition walls, floor toppings and insulation materials where moderate compressive strength is sufficient and lesser weight is the requirement.

The total CaO content of CR-CKD (64.15%) is only marginally higher than that of OPC (62.78%). However, the form of calcium differs significantly. In OPC, calcium is primarily bound in alite (C₃S) and belite (C₂S), which dissolve slowly over time. In CR-CKD, a substantial portion of calcium exists as free lime (f-CaO), as indicated by its higher loss on ignition (LOI = 14.45% vs. 2.77% for OPC) and different setting behaviour (Table 3). Free lime reacts almost instantaneously with water to form Ca(OH)₂, releasing heat and raising the pH of the pore solution15,49.

When 40% of OPC is replaced with CR-CKD, there is an indication that the following mechanisms contribute to improved strength:

The rapid release of Ca²⁺ and OH⁻ from free lime increases the ionic strength of the pore solution, which accelerates the dissolution of C₃S and C₂S from the remaining OPC51.

The additional Ca²⁺ shifts the equilibrium towards C–S–H precipitation, increasing the rate and extent of C–S–H formation43.

Fine CR-CKD particles provide additional surfaces for C–S–H nucleation, leading to a denser microstructure.

The net effect is that the OPC–CR–CKD combination hydrates more effectively than OPC alone, resulting in higher strength despite the substitution of 40% of the OPC. This is a synergistic effect, not simply a linear combination of properties.

In contrast, AR-CKD contains high levels of soluble alkalis (Na₂O + K₂O = 16.32%). These alkalis increase pH but also introduce Na⁺ and K⁺ ions, which can disrupt C–S–H structure and lead to the formation of weaker C–(N)–S–H or C–(K)–S–H gels14,23. Additionally, high alkalis can cause delayed ettringite formation and increased porosity, explaining the lower strength.

Flexural strength

The flexural strengths result of various LFC mixes at 7, 14, 28, 56, 90 and 180 days curing times are shown in Fig. 8. The flexural strength for the control mix (C) increased with time, from 0.43 MPa at 7-day to 0.77 MPa at 180-day of age (an increase of 81%). This steady increase reflects a normal growth in flexural strength of conventional cement-based systems where it strengthens with the accumulation and interaction of hydrates. Mixed AR-CKD showed a slight improvement in flexural strength from 0.41 MPa at 7 days to 0.72 MPa at 180 days, or a gain of 76%. The slight development of flexural strength in AR was probably due to the presence of soluble alkalis in CKD, which would inhibit the formation of vital hydration products including C-S-H. Such interference can hinder the growth of a dense uniform structure leading to flexural load capacity, particularly at the early cure points. Mix CR-CKD showed the highest increase of flexural strength, soaring from 0.49 MPa at 7 days to 0.92 MPa at 180-day (87%). The flexural strength of Mix CR-CKD is increased because the highest amount of calcium provided by CKD enhance C-S-H gel to form a more compact and durable structure. This results in an improved transfer of load and crack deflection in bending, benefiting the flexural strength. Finally, Mix IR-CKD showed a marked increase in flexural strength starting from 0.42 MPa at the age of 7-day and reaching 0.75 MPa at the age of 180-day corresponding to an increase of 77 per cent. Iron-rich CKD provides moderate strength benefits but less than calcium-rich CKD44. Tendencies observed in these blends illustrate the influence of CKD content on flexural properties of LFC. CR-CKD significantly improves the material stiffness against bending so that it is more suitable for application requiring higher flexural strength like structural members (e.g., beams, and slabs). On the other hand, AR-CKD may be more appropriate for non-structural use since low flexural strength is tolerable. The results obtained indicate that LFC with CR-CKD may be useful in building projects requiring both reduced weight and improved flexural characteristics.

Splitting tensile strength

Figure 9 presents the findings of splitting tensile strength for different LFC combinations across curing durations of 7, 14, 28, 56, 90, and 180 days. A consistent tendency noted in all mixes is a gradual enhancement in splitting tensile strength over time, signifying that hydration persists in promoting a more robust interaction among particles. In control mix (C), the splitting tensile strength rose from 0.29 MPa at 7 days to 0.52 MPa at 180 days, indicating an increase of 80%. Mix AR-CKD exhibited a marginally reduced rate of tensile strength development relative to control mix, commencing at 0.27 MPa at 7 days and attaining 0.490 MPa at 180-day (an 81% augmentation). Although the ultimate tensile strength of AR-CKD is similar to that of the control mix by 180-day, the gradual early-age increase implies that the alkali constituents in the CKD may at first impede the development of tensile-strengthening compounds, such as C-S-H gel, thereby postponing the material’s reaction to tensile forces.

The CR-CKD had the peak splitting tensile strength improvement, rising from 0.34 MPa at 7-day to 0.64 MPa at 180-day, reflecting an 88% enhancement. Khanna45reported that the improved performance of mix CR-CKD on splitting tensile strength was promoted by the higher content of calcium in CKD, which helps facilitate precipitation and enhance denser C-S-H gel. This gel greatly enhances the resistance to splitting tensile stress in the material, which makes this blend particularly suitable in applications that require high tensile strength, like structural components carrying a tensile load. Lastly, IR-CKD experienced slightly higher tensile strength which was from 0.29 MPa at 7-day to 0.52 MPa at 180-day, an increase of 79%, showing a better performance over time. Iron content in CKD may add to the general strength of the material but also appear to have a less pronounced effect on tensile strength than CR-CKD. According to Salleh et al46., this can be due to the limited role of iron in promoting better formation hydration end-products increasing tension resistance, resulting with similar to control mix (C) results. In summary, CR-CKD is an excellent option for those applications which require a better tensile-strength, especially structural members under pulling stress or cracking environments. On the other hand, AR-CKD mixes could also be beneficial in situations where moderate tensile strength can be tolerated, and other considerations (availability of material or cost) are favoured.

Elastic modulus

The result of the modulus of elasticity shown on Fig. 10 indicates that a stiffness modification is developed gradually in numerous LFC mixtures. The modulus of the control mix increased from 0.76 GPa at 7-day to 180-day value of 1.31 GPa, and this is a consistent improvement by approximately 73%. It achieves increasing stiffness with hydration, and the improved interparticle adhesion is thereby obtained. Modulus continually increasing past this curing-age indicates that the control mix behaviour is typical, and follows the conventional trends described for normal cement hydration without any significant premature retardation or acceleration. Growth of mix AR-CKD was similar though marginally lower with 0.73 GPa reaching 1.29 GPa at 180-day (76% growth). AR-CKD showed lower modulus at early ages (0.73 GPa at 7 days vs. 0.76 GPa for control). However, by 180 days, the modulus reached 1.29 GPa, approaching the control value of 1.31 GPa. This pattern suggests that the negative effect of alkalis on stiffness diminishes over time. Two mechanisms explain this. Continued formation of C–S–H gel beyond 28 days partially compensates for early retardation49,56. This allows the modulus of AR-CKD to approach the control value at later ages. As a result, long-term curing reduces the performance gap between AR-CKD and the control mix.

The Mix CR-CKD had the largest increase in modulus from 0.84 GPa at 7-day to 1.60 GPa at 180-day (90% increment). The increased calcium content in CKD should probably cause more efficient particle cross-linking as well as better initial bonding, which is responsible for higher stiffness development. An enhanced performance can be attributed to the additional reactive calcium sources in the CKD, thus accelerating the establishment of stronger solid bonds between reacting particles and providing a mortar with a much more rigid matrix. Consequently, the CR-CKD mixture presents a higher value of stiffness and better long-term modulus increase compared to other mixtures used, making it more suitable for applications in need of high structural integrity and deformation-regaining. This is consistent with the results by Mohamed et al48.. The Mix IR-CKD showed a relatively less development, from 0.74 GPa at 7 days to 1.33 GPa at 180-day (80%). Iron content in IR-CKD decreases overall stiffness but to a lesser extent than calcium because iron does not significantly enhance early bonding and interlocking of the material. IR-CKD might promote to grow up strength-enhancing mineral phases but contribution to the stiffness appears less than calcium. As a result, mix IR-CKD presents an continually-gaining modulus, which does not reach to the level of performances reached by mix CR-CKD, but it still guarantees a satisfactory growth of overall stiffness.

Finally, the modulus of elasticity results revealed that the stiffness of LFC is significantly affected by the chemical composition of CKD. Thus, the CR-CKD composite presents with better stiffness due to improved bond efficiency and accelerated stiffening rate. Mix AR-CKD has reduced early-age stiffness, presumably due to alkali interference, although ultimately attains a modulus almost identical to that of OPC. Mix IR-CKD offers a considerable enhancement, yet its effect is rather modest. The findings indicate that Mix CR-CKD is optimal for applications necessitating high stiffness, such as structural components, whereas Mix AR-CKD and IR-CKD are more appropriate for non-structural purposes where modest stiffness is permissible and alternative materials are more economical.

Transport properties

Water absorption

Water absorption of various mixtures studied in the present work are shown in Figure 11. The results reveal variations in water absorption between the four combinations, which is an important factor for determining the durability and resistance to moisture entering the material. Control mix attained a water absorption of 21.50%. Water absorption reflects the naturally porous character of construction material and its receptivity to water, such as developing of capillary pores during hydration. The slight water absorption that was observed is as expected, since OPC-based LFC normally balances between strength and water retained, making it suitable for common building purposes. The AR-CKD showed an increase of water absorption (23.10%) with its mix. The higher water absorption for AR-CKD is a result of the higher CKD alkalinity which could increase permeability. The alkali presented in the CKD could affect the hydration process and thus could lead to an increased or more linked pores volume-space in LFC. This in turn results in an increased water absorbance. Some of these alkaline compounds have hydrophilic characteristics and can produce pores or capillary channels; they could promote material’s water absorption, leading to water uptake50.

With the minimum water absorption at 19.90%, however, the mixture CR-CKD showed the least effect of water adsorption. The reduced water absorption was due to the higher calcium content of CKD, thus enabling a denser microstructural structure formation following hydration. CR-CKD accelerates the hydration of cement and promotes a more dense, less porous composite51. The result shows that the reduction in the number of pores and channels penetrating the material will lead to minimizing water absorption and enhancing its durability. This makes mix CR-CKD ideal for constructions that required moisture resistance, particularly in exterior applications or humid climates. IR-CKD blend had water absorption capacity of 22.70%. The water absorption by mix IR-CKD is greater than that of mix CR-CKD but less than to that of mix AR-CKD. This suggests that although IR-CKD further facilitates pore modification, it is not as effective as CR-CKD in reducing water adsorption. The iron oxide in the CKD might contribute to a partially-dense structure that is not equivalent proportion of the CR-CKD mix. Thus, despite the relatively lower water absorption of mix IR-CKD, it is still more porous than mix CR-CKD because iron less affects the morphology.

The water absorption test results also suggest that the variant of CKD used in LFC blends has a considerable impact on the moisture resistance of this material. Mix CR-CKD exhibits the lowest water absorption, indicating better resistance to penetration by moisture and would thus be suitable where resistance to localized wetting is needed. AR-CKD water absorption capacity is high and may not be well-suited in the marginally porous circumstances. The Mix IR-CKD has lower water absorption and no resistance to moisture, a property that is unique to Mix CR-CKD. The results have highlighted the importance of CKD composition when contributing for water absorption and durability properties of LFC, with CR-CKD being the most effective for improve water resistance.

Permeable porosity

Figure 12shows the trends of porosity for different LFC combinations. Porosity is fundamental to the durability of LFC and its resistance to environmental factors as it directly influences the water retention and stability of LFC. The porosity of the control mix was 43.80%, as in the usual cement mixes. The porosity of the control mix could be attributed to the inherent development of capillary pores by age other than by any mixture. It could affect the long-term durability of the LFC, particularly for cases in a moist environment. Mix AR-CKD was found to offer the highest porosity (45.45%). The increased porosity is attributed to the fact that the alkali components in the CKD halted the hydration process therefore created a larger number of voids, thereby increasing permeability of this mix. This will cause it to be more susceptible to water penetration making it less durable overall and usable in moist conditions. In contrast, the CR-CKD blend recorded the lowest porosity with 42.21%. The reduced porosity in CR-CKD is due to better hydration with a higher content of calcium leading to denser, closely packed structure52. These resulted in improved durability, reduced permeability and enhanced resistance to water penetration of the mix CR-CKD which makes it suitable for high durable requirement applications. The porosity of the IR-CKD was 44.57%. It is marginally higher than the control mix but lower than that of mix AR-CKD. The low porosity in mix IR-CKD suggests that iron does not excessively suppress the voids formation compared to CR-CKD though it has an impact on hydration. This results in such a balanced composition that the toughness and porosity are within rational limits53. The porosities results summarized the significant effect of CKD composition on the structural features of LFC. CR-CKD, with low porosity content has maximum performance in two ranking criteria which is hardness and water protection. Mix AR-CKD, a highly porous CKD may not be an appropriate option in environments that require low permeability, whereas mix IR-CKD would serve as a moderate alternative offering intermediate porosity.

Thermal properties

Thermal conductivity

Figure 13 presents the thermal conductivities of different LFC mixes. The thermal conductivities of both control mix and CR-CKD were measured to be 0.31 W/mK, indicating that the addition of CR-CKD barely affected the thermal behaviour in comparison with the case of the control mix. This means that the density and morphology of the LFC are largely preserved, and that its thermal conductivity falls within the normal range for control mix. For Mix AR-CKD even though it had a slightly improved thermal conductivity (0.29 W/mK), that is ascribed the raised porosity due to the weakening role of alkali in CKD leading to more voids which could be treated as thermal insulation47.

The thermal conductivity of Mix IR-CKD was 0.30 W/mK, which means that the heat conduction of the material is hardly affected by iron. The results show that with the addition of CKD particularly AR-CKD, thermal conductivity could be slightly improved because of higher porosity, however, there is no immense change among all mixtures and these increases are unimportant; indeed, a mixture AR-CKD has highly similar transfer thermal property effects.

Thermal diffusivity

Figure 14 shows the thermal diffusivity of different LFC mixes. The control mix and CR-CKD mix both gave 0.31 mm²/s, indicating that CR-CKD did not significantly affect heat diffusion rate. AR-CKD gave 0.29 mm²/s, slightly lower than the control, which may be due to its higher porosity reducing heat transfer. IR-CKD gave 0.30 mm²/s, close to the control. All values are within the typical range for lightweight foamed concrete (0.2–1.0 mm²/s). The uniformity of the thermal diffusivity can be caused by the similar density and morphology of the two blends, which means that heat is spread evenly (similar transfer distance) in both samples. On the other hand, it was noticed that the thermal diffusivity of mix AR-CKD slightly improved to 0.29 mm²/s in comparison with control mix and apparently this difference is attributed to a more porous capacity of AR-CKD which affects materials’ thermal diffusivity47. With the increased porosity of mix AR-CKD there are more voids and hence less heat is being transferred thus inhibiting diffusitivity50. The thermal diffusivity of mix IR-CKD was 0.30 mm²/s which was similar to the control mix, indicating that the presence of IR-CKD does not appreciably influence heat diffusion rate in LFC. In summary, the results reveal that AR-CKD slightly enhances thermal diffusivity, due to a more porous structure; however, irrespective of composition differences between them are negligible as far as their influence on thermal diffusion is concerned.

Specific heat capacity

The specific heat capacity of several LFC mixtures is shown in Figure 15. Control mix achieved a specific heat capacity of 1107 J/kg·K, which is typical for standard cementitious materials. This value reflects the thermal storage ability of the LFC, and its moderate magnitude is related with those in typical LFC materials where a good combination of solid components such as cement and water provides well sustainable heat storage capability. The specific heat capacity of mix AR-CKD was slightly higher (1129 J/kg·K), possibly due to the CKD with alkali-rich composition. The higher alkali content could contribute to the better thermal storage characteristic of LFC by modifying the heat response of its cement paste and increasing its net thermal mass47. The value of CR-CKD mix was lower (1068 J/kg·K) as a result of the accelerated hydration characteristics and denser microstructure induced by CR-CKD. The more porous or chemically active the nature of such a structure, however, the less its ability to trap heat might be. The specific heat capacity of Mix IR-CKD was 1112 J/kg·K, exhibiting a trend comparable to that of Mix AR-CKD. The iron presence probably affects the microstructure, resulting in a marginal enhancement of heat retention relative to the control mix. The specific heat capacity results prove that the CKD composition affects the thermal energy retained of the LFC mixtures; alkaline-rich and iron-rich CKD marginally enhance heat retention capacity, whereas calcium-rich CKD results in a slight decrease in specific heat capacity due to its more rigid form51.

Microstructural analysis

SEM assessment

Figure 16 shows SEM images of the four LFC mixtures at approximately 200× magnification. EDS analysis was not performed; therefore, the following descriptions are limited to observable morphology and pore structure.

Control mix (Fig. 16a): The control mix shows an irregular pore structure with voids of varying sizes, ranging from small micropores to larger macropores. The pore network appears interconnected, and the matrix has a loosely packed appearance. Similar pore structures have been reported in other LFC studies54,55. Some crystalline-like features are visible on the pore walls, but their composition cannot be determined from the image alone. The overall structure is characteristic of lightweight foamed concrete, with porosity distributed throughout the matrix.

AR-CKD (Fig. 16b): The AR-CKD mix displays a more fragmented pore structure compared to the control. Voids appear larger and more irregularly distributed. Some angular particles are scattered across the surface, and the matrix seems less uniform overall. The pore network appears less interconnected than in the control mix, with several isolated macropores visible.

CR-CKD (Fig. 16c): The CR-CKD mix shows a distinctly more uniform and denser pore structure. Voids are more evenly distributed in size and location, and the matrix appears more compact compared to both the control and AR-CKD samples. The pore walls look smoother, suggesting better particle packing. The overall microstructure appears more organised, with fewer large, irregular voids.

IR-CKD (Fig. 16d): The IR-CKD mix exhibits a pore structure that falls between that of CR-CKD and AR-CKD. Voids are present but relatively well-defined, and the matrix appears moderately compact. Some larger pores remain, but the overall structure is more organised than in AR-CKD. The pore walls show moderate roughness, and the void distribution is less uniform than in CR-CKD.

Conclusion

This study investigated the effect of replacing 40% of OPC with three CKD variants (alkaline-rich AR-CKD, calcium-rich CR-CKD, and iron-rich IR-CKD) on the properties of lightweight foamed concrete (LFC). The main findings are as follows:

i.CR-CKD provided the best overall performance among the three variants. It improved workability (slump flow 250 mm vs. 248 mm for control), mechanical strength (compressive + 18%, flexural + 20%, tensile + 23% at 180 days), and microstructure (lower porosity, more uniform pore distribution). However, thermal properties were not enhanced.ii.CR-CKD LFC achieved compressive strength of 4.32 MPa at 180 days (87% increase from 7 days), flexural strength of 0.92 MPa, and splitting tensile strength of 0.64 MPa. The modulus of elasticity increased by 12% compared to the control.iii.CR-CKD reduced water absorption (19.90% vs. 21.50% for control) and porosity (42.21% vs. 43.80% for control), indicating a denser microstructure with more uniform pore distribution.iv.AR-CKD showed inferior performance due to its high alkali content (Na₂O + K₂O = 16.32%), which interfered with hydration, leading to higher porosity (45.45%) and lower strength. IR-CKD gave moderate improvements but did not outperform CR-CKD.v.No CKD variant improved thermal performance. CR-CKD showed thermal conductivity (0.31 W/m·K) and thermal diffusivity (0.31 mm²/s) identical to the control, but specific heat capacity (1068 J/kg·K) was slightly lower than the control (1107 J/kg·K). AR-CKD and IR-CKD showed minor variations with no practical advantage.vi.This study did not include direct characterisation of hydration products (XRD, TG/DSC) or elemental analysis (EDS) to support SEM imaging. Only one replacement level (40%) was tested. The optimum replacement level for each CKD variant was not determined. Future work should incorporate these techniques and systematically vary CKD content to identify optimal dosages.
