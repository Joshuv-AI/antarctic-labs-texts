Impact of Clinicopathological and Institutional Factors on the Clinical Courses of Patients With Localized Prostate Cancer Initially Managed by Active Surveillance: An Analysis of Nationwide Real‐World Data From Japan
Authors: Mizuki Onozawa, Masaki Shiota, Taketo Kawai, Shinichi Sakamoto, Yoshiyuki Yamamoto, Satoru Taguchi, Yasuhide Kitagawa, Tohru Nakagawa, Shiro Hinotsu, Jun Miyazaki, Haruki Kume, Japan Study Group of Prostate Cancer (J‐CaP)
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesTo investigate active surveillance (AS) continuation rates and associated clinical and institutional factors in Japanese patients with localized prostate cancer.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 989 patients with localized prostate cancer from 92 institutions who initiated AS between 2016 and 2018. Institutions were categorized into three groups based on AS uptake for low‐risk disease. Discontinuation triggers and subsequent treatments were examined.ResultsMedian age was 70 years; 66.2%, 26.0%, and 7.8% of patients had low‐, intermediate‐, and high‐risk disease, respectively. Over a median follow‐up of 3.4 years, 47.0% discontinued AS (median AS continuation time: 3.0 years; 5‐year AS continuation rate: 33.7%). Multivariable analysis showed that ≥ 2 comorbidities, ≥ 3 positive biopsy cores, and T2b–c stage were associated with restricted mean continuation time differences (RMCTDs) of −0.6, −0.6, and −0.8 years, respectively (all p < 0.05). Conversely, intermediate‐ and high‐AS uptake institutions had significantly longer adjusted RMCTD (1.0 and 1.1 years, respectively; both p < 0.001). Triggers for discontinuation were more often multifaceted in high‐AS uptake institutions (39.1%) compared with low‐ or intermediate‐AS uptake institutions (16.2% and 15.1%, respectively). After discontinuation, 36.5%, 39.2%, and 24.3% of patients received prostatectomy, radiotherapy, and hormonal therapy, respectively. With advancing age, the proportion of patients whose AS was discontinued due to PSA elevation increased, as did the rate of subsequent hormonal therapy.ConclusionsIn Japanese real‐world practice, AS continuation was limited. Beyond clinicopathological factors, institutional strategies for localized prostate cancer played a significant role in treatment choices following AS.

Introduction

In recent years, approximately 90 000 new prostate cancer cases have been diagnosed annually in Japan, making it the most frequently diagnosed cancer among men in the country [1]. Of these, 70%–75% are diagnosed with localized disease [1, 2], for which standard treatment options include radical prostatectomy, radiotherapy, and active surveillance (AS), with treatment selection determined primarily by patient and tumor factors, as well as by patient and clinician preferences [3].

Over the past two decades, the role of AS in the management of localized prostate cancer has evolved significantly in Japan. An early 2000s observational study reported that only 4%–5% of patients with T1c–T2 disease were managed with watchful waiting (WW), and AS was not yet listed as a treatment option [4, 5]. By 2010, an observational study indicated that AS/WW was selected in 8.3% of localized cases—21%, 5.2%, and 2.1% among low‐, intermediate‐, and high‐risk patients, respectively [2, 6]. A subsequent study covering patients diagnosed between 2016 and 2018 demonstrated an increased selection rate of AS/WW, reaching 11% overall—41%, 8.2%, and 2.8% among low‐, intermediate‐, and high‐risk groups, respectively [2]. This rise reflects growing recognition of AS as a primary management option for low‐risk prostate cancer in Japan, and current treatment patterns are now approaching those observed in the United States [7].

The original concept of AS aimed to minimize overtreatment in patients with favorable‐risk disease while ensuring safety through structured monitoring, including regular prostate‐specific antigen (PSA) testing, scheduled biopsies, digital rectal examinations, and symptom assessments [7]. However, as AS has become more widely adopted, its implementation in clinical practice appears increasingly variable. Monitoring protocols are often inconsistent, and the distinction between AS and WW has become increasingly blurred [7]. A 2014 study of Japanese urologists' clinical practices reported that 29.4% of practitioners conducted AS without a standardized follow‐up protocol, and approximately half either did not perform repeat biopsies or performed them only when deemed necessary [8].

These inconsistencies may influence the continuation and long‐term success of AS in real‐world settings. This study aimed to investigate patterns of AS continuation in Japan and to identify factors associated with AS discontinuation, with particular attention to institutional factors that may impact both AS uptake and continuation.

Methods

Patient Population

The data source for this study was a registry established by the Japan Study Group of Prostate Cancer (J‐CaP), which enrolled patients diagnosed with prostate cancer between 2016 and 2018 across Japan. This registry was designed to investigate patient demographics, tumor characteristics, treatment selection, and clinical outcomes in a real‐world setting in Japan. This observational study was approved by the institutional review board at each participating institute.

In Japan, first‐line treatment selection is typically guided by well‐established clinical guidelines, including those from the Japanese Urological Association (JUA) [3]. According to the 2016 guideline, patients with prostate‐specific antigen (PSA) ≤ 10 ng/mL, ≤ 2 positive biopsy cores, Gleason score ≤ 6, and PSA density < 0.2 or < 0.15 were considered primary candidates for AS [3]; however, the indications for AS gradually expanded during this period. For this study, we selected from the database patients with T1c‐2N0M0 prostate cancer whose first‐line treatment was AS, using data with a cutoff date of December 31, 2024. Patients who withdrew from the study or were diagnosed without a prostate biopsy were excluded. Thus, this study comprised 989 patients as the main cohort (Figure S1).

Variables Considered in This Study

This study considered patient, tumor, and institutional factors. Patient factors included age at diagnosis, ECOG performance status, and comorbidities; the number of comorbidities (heart disease, cerebrovascular disease, diabetes, and other cancers) was calculated for each patient in the cohort. Tumor factors included PSA level at diagnosis, International Society of Urological Pathology (ISUP) Gleason grade group at biopsy, T stage as defined by the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) 7th edition, and D'Amico risk classification.

Classification of Institutions According to AS Uptake Status

In this study, institutions were classified based on their respective AS uptake rates, which were then incorporated into the analysis as a variable. This institutional AS uptake was used as an observable proxy summarizing broader, unobservable facility‐level factors, such as treatment philosophy or culture, rather than as an independent causal variable. The AS uptake rate for each institution was calculated using data from 1488 low‐risk cases with 1 or 2 positive biopsy cores, where the primary treatment was prostatectomy, radiotherapy, hormonal therapy, or AS. A small portion of WW cases was included solely when calculating institutional AS uptake due to database constraints. The patient selection process is detailed in Figure S1. Institutional AS uptake rates were categorized into tertiles (low: ≤ 33.3%, intermediate: 33.4%–66.7%, high: > 66.7%) to ensure an almost equal distribution of institutions across categories (Figure S2). This indicator was applied to institutions with ≥ 10 patients (Table S1). To minimize potential misclassification of the institutional AS uptake status, sensitivity analyses were performed using thresholds of ≥ 5 and ≥ 15 patients per institution. We also performed a sensitivity analysis excluding patients from unclassified institutions.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes of this study were the duration of AS and the AS continuation rate, both overall and within specified subgroups. Patient, tumor, and institutional factors associated with AS discontinuation were also evaluated in the primary outcomes. Secondary outcomes included the triggers for discontinuation and subsequent treatments. Final vital status was assessed in an exploratory analysis.

Statistical Methods

Continuous variables were presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs), while categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Differences in continuous variables among groups were tested with the Kruskal–Wallis test. The association between two categorical variables was examined using the chi‐squared test with Monte Carlo simulation (10 000 replicates). Rates of AS continuation and overall survival were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method. For the AS continuation rate, discontinuation of AS or initiation of subsequent treatment, whichever occurred earlier, was considered an event. Patients without these events were censored at the last known date of AS continuation. Time from prostate biopsy to the event or censoring was used for the analyses. To compare continuation status among groups, we employed restricted mean survival time (RMST) analysis, as the proportional hazards assumption for the Cox proportional hazards model was violated. The truncation time was set at 5 years, which was determined based on the minimum of the maximum observed follow‐up times across the groups. Differences in adjusted restricted mean continuation time (RMCT) between subgroups were assessed by the Wald test. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.5.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Statistical tests were two‐sided, and a p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Active Surveillance Continuation Status in Subgroups and Analysis of Factors Associated With Continuation

As shown in Figure 1 and Table 2, while the continuation rates varied significantly across groups for several variables, the 5‐year continuation rate remained in the 30% range or lower across almost all groups for nearly every variable. Multivariable RMST regression analysis revealed the following factors that were significantly associated with a shorter duration of AS (Figure 2): a comorbidity count of ≥ 2 (adjusted RMCT difference [RMCTD]: −0.6 years [95% CI, −1.2 to −0.1], p = 0.019); ≥ 3 positive biopsy cores (adjusted RMCTD: −0.6 years [95% CI, −1.0 to −0.1], p = 0.010); and T2b‐c stage (adjusted RMCTD: −0.8 years [95% CI, −1.5 to −0.1], p = 0.019). Conversely, compared with low‐AS uptake institutions, intermediate‐ and high‐AS uptake institutions demonstrated significantly longer continuation durations (adjusted RMCTD: 1.0 years [95% CI, 0.4–1.6] and 1.1 years [95% CI, 0.5–1.7], respectively; both p < 0.001). Sensitivity analysis, designed to minimize potential misclassification of the institutional AS uptake status, yielded largely consistent results (Figure S1A,B). Sensitivity analysis excluding unclassified institutions showed consistent results: median continuation was 3.0 years [95% CI, 2.6–3.6], and 3‐ and 5‐year rates were 50.4% [95% CI, 46.3–54.8] and 34.5% [95% CI, 30.4–39.3].

Triggers for Discontinuation of Active Surveillance

Triggers for discontinuation were identified in 345 of the 413 cases in which AS was discontinued (Table 3). Of these, 264 (76.5%) had a single trigger, and 81 (23.5%) had multiple triggers (details unspecified). Multiple triggers were more common in institutions with high‐AS uptake (39.1%) than in those with low or intermediate‐AS uptake (15.1%–16.2%). The most common single triggers for discontinuation were histological deterioration (94 cases) and PSA elevation (90 cases). The remaining 76 cases were based on the patient's choice or the physician's recommendation. PSA elevation was the predominant trigger in older patients (14 out of 21 patients ≥ 80 years of age).

Subsequent Treatment After Discontinuation of Active Surveillance

Of the 296 patients whose subsequent treatment was identified, prostatectomy, radiotherapy (including cases with neoadjuvant or concomitant hormonal therapy), and hormonal therapy were selected in 108 (36.5%), 116 (39.2%), and 72 (24.3%) patients, respectively (Table 4). Prostatectomy was more frequently performed in patients under 75 years of age (38.8%–47.1%) and in those whose trigger for discontinuation was histological deterioration alone (50.6%). Radiotherapy was widely preferred in patients aged < 80 years (37.5%–42.1%). In contrast, hormonal therapy was predominantly selected for patients aged ≥ 80 years (78.9%). In patients whose only trigger for discontinuation was PSA elevation, hormonal therapy was selected in more than half of cases (55.7%).

Survival Outcomes

During a median follow‐up of 5.2 years (95% CI, 4.6–5.5), 22 patients (2.5%) died. Twenty‐one of these deaths were attributed to causes unrelated to prostate cancer, while one patient died of prostate cancer. The 5‐year overall survival rate was 97.4% (95% CI, 96.1–98.7).

Discussion

In this observational study involving 92 Japanese institutions, we investigated the clinical trajectories of 989 patients with localized prostate cancer diagnosed between 2016 and 2018, all of whom were initially managed by AS. A notable finding was the relatively short median duration of AS, at approximately 3 years.

Continuation rates in AS studies show considerable variability. In Western countries, rates typically range from 50% to 75% over 5 years [9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16]. In contrast, Japanese studies have reported lower rates. The PRIAS‐Japan study reported a 5‐year continuation rate of 45% [17], while another multi‐institutional prospective Japanese study (2002–2003) showed a rate of 28% [18]. In this study, the 5‐year continuation rate was 33.7%, intermediate between these two Japanese studies. These findings suggest a distinct pattern of AS management in Japan compared with Western countries.

Several factors influence AS discontinuation, including patient‐related factors, tumor characteristics at diagnosis and during follow‐up, and follow‐up protocols. Tumor‐related factors, including PSA levels, T stages, and Gleason scores, positive biopsy cores, are reported to be associated with transitioning from AS to active treatment [13, 15, 18, 19]. Regarding patient‐related factors, younger age and higher levels of comorbidity have been linked to discontinuation [13, 15, 20]. Likewise, our study found that a comorbidity count of ≥ 2, ≥ 3 positive biopsy cores, and T2b‐c stage were associated with significantly shorter AS duration (adjusted RMCTD: −0.6, −0.6, and −0.8 years, respectively; Figure 2). In our study, older patients, those with worse performance status, and those with higher comorbidity counts were more likely to discontinue AS due to PSA elevation and to shift toward hormonal therapy. This suggests that these patients received simple observation rather than protocol‐directed strict AS management. Historically in Japan, primary hormonal therapy was widely utilized before concerns regarding its adverse effects were fully recognized. Conversely, a substantial proportion of patients with favorable features terminated AS due to patient preference or physician recommendation, whereas some patients with unfavorable features avoided strict surveillance and active treatment. These complex management patterns and diversity in real‐world practice complicate the interpretation of differences in AS continuation rates across clinical factors.

Institutional factors, such as physician specialty and institution type, are also considered to impact treatment discontinuation [15, 21]. In this study, we found that institutional AS uptake status was significantly associated with continuation status: patients from institutions with low‐AS uptake had shorter continuation periods than those from intermediate‐ or high‐AS uptake institutions (median, 1.4, 3.6, and 3.1 years, respectively; Table 2). As patient choice or physician recommendation was the main reason for discontinuation in institutions with low‐AS uptake, institutional policy likely influenced both initial treatment selection and AS discontinuation. However, this association may partly reflect shared upstream factors and unmeasured facility‐level confounders, such as clinician experience or counseling protocols. Regardless of these potential factors, it is worth noting that AS continuation rates in Japan remain low, even in intermediate‐ and high‐AS uptake institutions, as shown in Table 2.

Several less apparent factors may underlie these observable factors, including the characteristics of the Japanese healthcare system and cultural context. First, Japan's universal health insurance system minimizes patients' out‐of‐pocket expenses, reducing hesitation to initiate treatment. Second, there is no specific incentive for healthcare providers during AS, which may result in less encouragement for long‐term monitoring. Third, the historically high utilization of hormonal therapy for localized prostate cancer in Japan [4, 5, 6] could contribute to a preference for early treatment, as reflected in the high rate of hormonal therapy use (24.3%) following AS discontinuation.

AS can impose a psychological burden, with 30% of patients developing anxiety within the first year [22]. This distress can lead to surveillance discontinuation, as 5%–30% of patients opt for radical treatment due to anxiety, despite having no evidence of disease progression [23, 24]. Supportive care is therefore crucial for adherence to AS [24]. However, these findings are mainly from Western studies, with limited Japanese data. Research indicates that Japanese individuals may have higher levels of neuroticism, which correlate with anxiety [25, 26]. Supportive care approaches have rarely been implemented in Japan. Under these circumstances, patients may be more likely to seek treatment, even without disease progression. Further research is needed on the impact of psychological factors on discontinuation in Japan.

Several strategies should be considered to extend AS duration. Enhancing patient–physician communication can reduce anxiety by emphasizing evidence supporting the safety of AS in suitable cases. For patients with voiding symptoms, medications for benign prostatic hyperplasia, such as 5α‐reductase inhibitors or chlormadinone acetate, may help maintain quality of life and prevent premature transition to active treatment [27, 28]. Additionally, less invasive, accurate monitoring tools are needed to replace or reduce the need for repeat prostate biopsies and thereby improve patient compliance and extend surveillance periods. Finally, as reimbursement and policy interventions, incentives to extend AS duration are warranted for physicians and institutions.

This study has several limitations. As a retrospective analysis using a database not designed for AS research, it lacks key data, including prostate volume, PSA density (PSAD), MRI findings, monitoring schedules, timing and criteria for repeat biopsy, results of repeat biopsy, and discontinuation criteria. The database also lacks information on institutional characteristics, such as the availability of curative treatments. While the main cohort included only patients who started AS, when calculating the institutional AS uptake rates, a small proportion of WW cases could not be separated due to database constraints. Some patients likely transitioned from AS to WW during follow‐up, but these secondary were not captured in our database. Some confidence intervals were relatively wide due to the limited number of patients in certain categories. Our data were collected from 2016 to 2018, which may not fully reflect current clinical practice. Despite these limitations, the study reflects real‐world practices across Japan, using data from 92 institutions of varying types and policies, and provides insights into AS in clinical settings.

In conclusion, our findings suggest that most patients with localized prostate cancer in Japan initially managed with AS eventually transition to deferred treatments. Although the inclusion of patients with unfavorable tumor characteristics and low‐AS uptake could partly be responsible for the short continuation of AS, various other less apparent factors may also contribute to the shorter duration of surveillance among Japanese patients. Given Japan's aging population [29], the incidence of new prostate cancer cases—and consequently, the number of candidates for AS—is anticipated to increase. In this context, adopting a “less active” surveillance model—for example, less intensive monitoring and fewer repeat biopsies—could become a practical approach, particularly for elderly patients, for whom the distinction between AS and WW can become ambiguous. Further research is essential to optimize management during AS, balancing the prevention of overtreatment with effective disease control.
