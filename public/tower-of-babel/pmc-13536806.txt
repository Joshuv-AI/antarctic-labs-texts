Association between the prevalence of overweight/obesity and dietary intake among mothers and children in urban and rural Malawi
Authors: Ai Yasudomi, Aubrey Jerome Mankhaka-Banda, Mayamiko Munthali Khomba, Miki Miyoshi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChildhood and adult obesity are emerging public health concerns in sub-Saharan Africa, driven by rapid nutrition transition and changing food environments. To prevent the rising rate of childhood obesity and overweight/obesity among women in sub-Saharan Africa, and to understand factors influencing food consumption in this population, this study aimed to examine the associations between overweight/obesity and dietary intake among children and mothers in urban and rural Malawi.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted in Lilongwe City (an urban area) and Mzimba District North (a rural area) from November to December 2022. Anthropometric measurements were taken for children aged 2.0–4.9 years and their mothers. Questionnaire interviews were conducted with mothers of children. The dietary survey consisted of a 24-hour recall and a food frequency questionnaire (FFQ). Participants' mothers were classified as overweight/obese (body mass index: BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2) or normal weight (18.5≦BMI < 25 kg/m2). Associations between overweight/obesity and dietary factors (nutrient intake, food consumption, FFQ, Minimum dietary diversity for women [MDD-W], Minimum dietary diversity 6–23 months [MDD], and questionnaire variables) were assessed using chi-square tests, independent t-tests, and multivariable logistic regression analyses adjusted for age, education, and income.ResultsOf the 229 mother–child pairs that participated, the data of 200 pairs were included in the final analysis. Consequently, 37.5% of mothers (urban 34.0% and rural 40.2%) were classified as overweight/obesity (BMI ≥ 25), and 4.5% of children (urban 8.0% and rural 1.8%) were classified as obese (weight-for-height Z-score: WHZ ≥ 3). Compared with rural children, urban children consumed greater amounts of energy, protein, fat, "meat, poultry, and fish", as well as "other vegetables", and experienced lower levels of nutrient inadequacy. Multivariable logistic regression analyses showed that several dietary factors were independently associated with maternal overweight/obesity in both urban and rural areas. In urban areas, maternal egg intake (adjusted odds ratio [AOR]: 4.72; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.57–14.19) and children’s fat intake (AOR: 8.73; 95% CI: 1.79–42.57) were positively associated with maternal overweight/obesity, whereas meat and fish intake was negatively associated with overweight/obesity (AOR: 0.28; 95% CI: 0.09–0.83). In rural areas, increased income (AOR: 7.10; 95% CI: 2.37–21.24) and children’s carbohydrates intake (AOR: 4.05; 95% CI: 1.25–13.08) were positively associated with overweight/obesity. Meanwhile, higher dietary diversity among children (MDD ≥ 5) was negatively associated with maternal overweight/obesity (AOR: 0.40; 95% CI: 0.18–0.89).ConclusionsThe dietary factors associated with maternal overweight/obesity differed between urban and rural areas. Tailored nutrition education and interventions may therefore be needed throughout the life course for urban and rural populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s41182-026-01044-z.

Introduction

Non-communicable diseases (NCDs) are the leading cause of death worldwide, accounting for 75% of all fatalities [1, 2]. Approximately 77% of NCD-related deaths occur in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Obesity is one of the major risk factors for NCDs. Similar trends in increasing obesity prevalence can be observed worldwide, particularly in LMICs, where the largest population of obese individuals resides [3]. Many LMICs are undergoing rapid modernization, urbanization, and economic development, accompanied by changing food environments [4]. These changes have increased wealth and led to a “nutrition transition” characterized by a shift toward high-energy-density diets and a decline in energy expenditure [5, 6]. In these LMICs, however, undernutrition remains a major public concern, and many countries are facing the “double burden of malnutrition”, which is characterized by the coexistence of undernutrition, overweight, obesity, and diet-related NCDs [7].

Obesity often begins in childhood, making early prevention essential. Childhood obesity is increasing worldwide, including in low- and middle-income countries [8]. Approximately 85% of children under five years of age with overweight or obesity live in LMICs [9], and the prevalence continues to rise in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) [10]. Malawi, one of the poorest countries in SSA, is no exception. The prevalence of obesity (BMI > 30 kg/m2) among women in Malawi is projected to rise from 7.3% in 2010 to 13.8% in 2030 [11, 12]. To the best of our knowledge, although national prevalence estimates for children under five are unavailable in Malawi, projections for 5–19-year-olds suggest that obesity prevalence is expected to rise by 2040 [13]. In Malawi, the prevalence of stunting, wasting, and being overweight among children under five years of age in 2015–2016 was 37.4%, 3.0%, and 5.0%, respectively, indicating the presence of a double burden of malnutrition [14].

Children with obesity are more likely to remain obese in adulthood, thereby increasing the risk of NCDs [15]. Exposure to early-life undernutrition, followed by later-life overweight, increases this risk [16]. Childhood is considered a critical period for intervention, as preventive efforts at this stage may reduce future risk factors for NCDs, including hypertension, smoking, type 2 diabetes, physical inactivity, and obesity [15]. Therefore, it is important to identify dietary factors associated with childhood obesity to develop effective prevention strategies, particularly given the substantial current and future disease burden attributable to malnutrition among women of reproductive age and children during the first two years of life, including the critical window of opportunity during the first 1000 days of life [17].

However, few studies have examined the relationship between dietary intake and obesity in Malawi. One previous study reported that, despite the predominance of maize-based diets in Malawi, mother–child pairs with an overweight child consumed a higher percent of energy from carbohydrates and a lower percent of energy from fat than pairs with a child of normal weight. Furthermore, these mothers had a higher grain intake [18]. Studies of adults in sub-Saharan Africa have also reported associations between obesity and high intakes of nutrient-dense foods, high-fat and high-protein diets, processed foods and cereal-based diets [19], excessive consumption of fast and fried foods, and low fruit and vegetable consumption [20]. A systematic review and meta-analysis of preschool children in sub-Saharan Africa identified sugary food consumption as one of the factors associated with overweight/obesity [21]. In Malawi, previous studies have shown that the prevalence of overweight and obesity, hypertension, and diabetes is higher in urban areas than in rural areas [22], possibly reflecting differences in dietary intake between urban and rural areas. However, to our knowledge, no previous research has examined the association between obesity and dietary intake among mothers and children in Malawi across both urban and rural areas.

Therefore, in order to prevent rising rates of childhood obesity and overweight/obesity in women in Malawi and to clarify dietary factors in both urban and rural areas, this study aimed to identify the association between childhood obesity and overweight/obesity among mothers as well as the dietary factors involved, in urban and rural Malawi. We formulated the following research questions. (1) What is the prevalence of childhood obesity and maternal overweight/obesity in urban and rural Malawi? (2) Are childhood obesity and maternal overweight/obesity associated with dietary factors after adjusting for other factors, and do these associations differ between urban and rural areas? (3) What strategies may be effective in preventing childhood obesity and maternal overweight/obesity in Malawi?

Methods

Study location

This study employed a cross-sectional design in Lilongwe City (as an urban area) and Mzimba District North (as a rural area) in Malawi. These areas were selected because the study focused on overweight/obesity and the double burden of malnutrition. Lilongwe is the capital city (population 989,318) in the Central Region of Malawi and one of the districts with the highest rates of overweight and obesity (BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2) among women and Mzimba (population 914,088) has one of the highest prevalence rates of obesity among women in the country and one of the highest prevalence of wasting among children under five years age in the Northern Region in the latest Demographic and Health Survey 2015–16 [14].

Study population

Mother–child pairs were recruited from selected health centers in the study areas. In the urban area, participants were recruited from the U-5 Clinic of Bwaila District Hospital in Lilongwe district. In the rural area, participants were recruited from the Mzimba North District Hospital Health Centre (Kaweche and Mpherembe, under the traditional authority (TA) of Mtwalo and Mpherembe) in Mzimba district. Eligible participants were mothers who attended child health check-ups or routine immunization services with children aged 2.0–4.9 years during the dry season (November–December 2022). In Malawi, maternal and child health services provide free routine health check-ups and immunizations for eligible children [23]. Pregnant women and mothers whose children were younger than two years or older than five years were excluded from the study. Pregnant women were excluded because pregnancy substantially alters dietary intake, nutritional requirements, and body weight [24]. Children younger than two years were excluded because breastfeeding remains an important source of nutrition during this period, and their feeding practices and nutritional requirements differ considerably from those of preschool-aged children [25]. Children aged five years and older were also excluded to maintain a homogeneous study population of preschool-aged children. A total of 229 mother–child pairs (101 urban and 128 rural) were included in the study.

Anthropometric measurements

The height, weight, and blood pressure of the mothers were measured, while the height, weight and mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC) of the children were measured. Standing height was measured to the nearest 0.1 cm for both mothers and children using a portable stadiometer (Seca Leicester Portable Height Measure (Seca, UK)). Body weight was measured to the nearest 0.1 kg with participants wearing light clothing and being barefoot using a digital scale (Seca877, 200 kg × 100 g (SECA, Japan)). All anthropometric measurements were taken twice, and the mean value was used for analysis. Blood pressure was measured in mothers using an automated sphygmomanometer (OMRON HEM-7281T). Children’s MUAC was measured using a color-coded insertion tape to assess nutritional status.

Diet assessment

All data were collected in the participants’ local language. Dietary intake was assessed using a qualitative 24-hour dietary recall method [26]. First, mothers were asked to recall all foods and beverages consumed during the previous 24 hours, including meals, sauces, snacks, and drinks. During the second stage, they were asked to provide detailed information on the ingredients and composition of all foods consumed. The interviews were conducted by a trained dietitian, and household utensils of various sizes (e.g., plates and spoons) were used to assist with the estimation of portion sizes. Nutrient intake was calculated using the Malawi food composition table [27]. For food items not included in the Malawi database, nutrient composition data were obtained from the Tanzania Food Composition Table [28] or the USDA FoodData Central database [29]. Portion sizes were estimated using the information on portion sizes included in FoodFinder, a food composition database developed by the South African Medical Research Council [30]. Nutrient and food group intakes were adjusted for total energy intake using the residual method [31] which reduces variation attributable to differences in body size and energy requirements. The adequacy of nutrient intake was assessed using the cut-off method previously [32, 33]. The nutrient requirements established for Malawi were used for mothers [34], whereas the recommended nutrient intake values from Zambia were applied for children [35]. As Malawi currently lacks nationally established dietary reference values for children, dietary adequacy was evaluated using the Zambian recommendations. Zambia was considered an appropriate reference country due to its geographical proximity, similar dietary patterns, food availability, agroecological conditions, and nutrition-related challenges [35]. Reference values for each nutrient are presented in Supplementary Table 1.

Questionnaire survey

Structured questionnaires were administered to mothers in the local language. Information was collected on household socio-demographic characteristics, including household size, number of siblings, maternal educational attainment, source of drinking water, type of toilet facility, experience of food shortages, family history of hypertension, diabetes, or cancer, household expenditure on food in a typical week, monthly household income, and the mode and travel time to the nearest market or food shop. Information on health-related characteristics was also collected, including child birth weight, recent illness history (diarrhea, fever, and cough in the previous two weeks), and maternal reproductive history (number of pregnancies and deliveries). Body size preferences were assessed using a set of seven adult female and seven child body silhouette drawings [36, 37]. Mothers were asked to identify their perceived current body size, their preferred body size, and their preferred body size for their child. In addition, quantitative food frequency questionnaires (QFFQs), adapted from a previous study conducted in South Africa [38] were administered to assess the habitual dietary intake of both mothers and children.

Statistical analysis

The variables included in the analysis are summarized in Supplementary Table 2. Maternal body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg)/height (m)2. Mothers were classified as overweight/obese (BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2) or of normal weight (18.5 ≦ BMI < 25 kg/m2) [34]. Children’s nutritional status was assessed using three anthropometric indices: height-for-age Z-score (HAZ), weight-for-age Z-score (WAZ), and weight-for-height Z-score (WHZ). A cut-off of point of -2SD was used to distinguish between normal children and those who were stunted (HAZ < -2), underweight (WAZ < -2), or wasted (WHZ < -2) [39]. Those with a (WHZ ≥ 3) were classified as obese [8]. HAZ, WAZ and WHZ were calculated using WHO Anthro software [40]. Children with a MUAC below 13.5 cm were classified as undernourished.

Data were analyzed separately for urban and rural areas and are presented according to maternal weight status (normal weight vs. overweight/obesity). Descriptive statistics are presented as means and standard deviations (SDs). Univariate analyses were conducted to examine the association between overweight/obesity and socio-demographic characteristics, nutrient intake, food consumption, food frequency questionnaire responses, Minimum dietary diversity for women (MDD-W) [41], and Minimum dietary diversity 6–23 months (MDD) [42]. Although dietary diversity scores were calculated using the MDD-W for mothers and the MDD for children, food group intake for both mothers and children was classified according to the MDD-W food group classification to enable a more detailed assessment of food group intake. The MDD-W food groups included (1) grains, white roots and tubers, and plantains, (2) pluses (beans, peas and lentils), (3) nuts and seeds, (4) milk and milk products, (5) meat, poultry and fish, (6) eggs, (7) dark green leafy vegetables, (8) other vitamin A-rich fruits and vegetables, (9) other vegetable, and (10) other fruits. The MDD food groups included (1) breast milk, (2) grains, roots, tubers and plantains, (3) pulses (beans, peas, lentils), nuts and seeds, (4) dairy products (milk, infant formula, yogurt, cheese), (5) flesh foods (meat, fish, poultry, organ meats), (6) eggs, (7) vitamin A-rich fruits and vegetables, and (8) other fruits and vegetables. Associations between categorical variables were assessed using the chi-square (χ2) test, while differences in continuous variables were examined using independent t-tests. Nutrient and food group intakes were log-transformed when distributions deviated from normality, and back-transformed values are presented in the tables. Variables associated with overweight/obesity in the univariate analyses (p < 0.05) were considered for inclusion in multivariable logistic regression models. Multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to examine the association between overweight/obesity and dietary factors, adjusting for age, education, and income. These covariates were selected a priori based on previous studies [43–45]. Continuous variables were categorized into quartiles based on their distributions. Variance inflation factor scores for any variable in any model (range: 1.00–1.74) were within acceptable limits (< 10) [46], suggesting that multicollinearity was not an issue. As the prevalence of overweight/obesity exceeded 10%, the odds ratios may have overestimated the relative risks and were therefore interpreted as measures of association. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS statistics (version 26, IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 219 mother–child pairs (94 urban and 125 rural) completed the questionnaire. Data from nineteen mother–child pairs (6 urban and 13 rural) were excluded from subsequent analysis, with the data of 7 BMI < 18.5 (1 urban and 6 rural), with the data of 3 missing data on BMI (1 urban and 2 rural), with the data of 7 mothers aged 50 years or older and missing data on age (3 urban and 4 rural), with the data of 2 extreme energy intakes (exceeding mean ± 3SD) (1 urban and 1 rural). Thus, the final sample included data from 200 pairs: 88 urban and 112 rural.

Anthropometric and sociodemographic characteristics

Table 1 summarizes the demographic characteristics of the study participants. Participant characteristics were compared between the urban (Lilongwe) and rural (Mzimba North) study areas. 37.5% of mothers (urban 34.1% and rural 40.2%) were classified as overweight/obesity (BMI ≥ 25), 4.5% (urban 8.0% and rural 1.8%) of children were classified as obese (WHZ ≥ 3), 46.0% of children (urban 51.1% and rural 42.0%) were classified as stunting (HAZ ≤ − 2), 6.0% of children (urban 10.2% and rural 2.7%) were classified as wasting (WHZ ≤ − 2), and 9.0% of children (urban 17.0% and rural 2.7%) were classified as undernourished (MUAC < 13.5 cm). No significant differences in maternal demographic characteristics were observed between urban and rural areas. However, children in urban areas had significantly higher prevalence rates of obesity (p = 0.045), wasting (p = 0.026), and undernourished (p = 0.000) than children in rural areas. Dietary intake

Frequency of food intake

Supplementary Tables 3 and 4 show the univariate associations between BMI status (normal BMI vs. overweight/obesity) and the frequency of food intake. In urban areas, no significant differences in the frequency of food intake were observed between mothers with a normal BMI and those with overweight/obesity. In contrast, in rural areas, mothers with overweight/obesity consumed nsima (Malawi's staple food: an unfermented, maize-based porridge that is molded into patties) (p = 0.029), beans (p = 0.038), and fried fish (p = 0.048) more frequently. However, normal BMI mothers consumed mutton more frequently (p = 0.049).

Number of food groups consumed

Table 3 presents the univariate associations between BMI status (normal BMI vs. overweight/obesity) and maternal MDD-W score (≥ 5), child MDD score (≥ 5), and the consumption of individual food groups, assessed using the chi-squared test. In urban areas a higher proportion of mothers with normal BMI consumed meat, poultry, and fish than mothers with overweight/obesity (p = 0.048). Conversely, mothers with overweight/obesity had a higher proportion of egg consumption (p = 0.022). In rural areas, children of mothers with a normal BMI were more likely to achieve an MDD score ≥ 5 (p = 0.014) and to consume flesh foods (meat, fish, poultry, organ meats) (p = 0.017) than children of mothers with overweight/obesity. Nutrient and food group intake

Energy-adjusted nutrient and food group intakes according to area of residence and maternal BMI status are presented in Table 4. Among children, those living in urban areas had higher energy and protein intakes, a higher percentage of energy from protein and fat, and higher intakes of “meat, poultry, and fish” and “other vegetables” than those living in rural areas. Discussion

This cross-sectional analysis of mother–child pairs in urban and rural Malawi revealed that several dietary and sociodemographic factors were associated with maternal overweight/obesity. The prevalence of maternal overweight/obesity was 34.0% in urban areas and 40.2% in rural areas, while childhood obesity was observed in 8.0% and 1.8% of children, respectively. Although several dietary and sociodemographic factors were associated with maternal overweight/obesity, the associated factors differed between urban and rural settings. These findings suggest that the determinants of maternal overweight/obesity vary according to area of residence and obesity prevention strategies.

In the present study, mothers with overweight/obesity were older than mothers with normal BMI, consistent with other studies in Malawi [18]. The prevalence of maternal overweight/ obesity in urban areas was consistent with previous national estimates [14], whereas the higher prevalence observed in the rural study area may reflect ongoing socioeconomic changes and the nutrition transition in rural Malawi [6]. These findings suggest that overweight/obesity is becoming an increasingly important public health concern in rural as well as urban communities. Childhood obesity was more prevalent in urban than in rural areas. This finding is consistent with national projections indicating an increasing burden of childhood overweight and obesity in Malawi [13] and may reflect the ongoing nutrition transition in urban settings [47]. Importantly, to our knowledge, this is the first study in SSA to examine the associations between maternal overweight/obesity and dietary intake among mother–child pairs in both urban and rural settings, providing novel evidence that determinants of overweight/obesity differ according to area of residence.

Factors positively associated with overweight/obesity

Our findings are partly consistent with previous studies conducted in SSA, which reported that older women from wealthier households were more likely to be obese in Malawi [48, 49]. Previous studies have also reported a positive association between higher household income and obesity in urban areas. The present study suggests that higher household income may also be associated with obesity in rural areas [50]. A family history of hypertension, obesity, diabetes, or stroke has been associated with a higher BMI, elevated blood pressure, adverse lipid profiles, increased uric acid levels, and a greater risk of obesity and hyperlipidemia [51], which is consistent with our findings. Previous studies in Malawi have reported a higher prevalence of obesity among women with higher levels of education and income [14]. However, educational attainment was not associated with overweight/obesity in either urban or rural areas in the present study. This discrepancy may reflect the complex interaction between education and household wealth. Previous studies have suggested that women with lower educational attainment may be particularly vulnerable to the effects of increasing household wealth on overweight and obesity [45, 52]. To our knowledge, no previous studies have directly examined the association between egg intake and obesity; however, cultural beliefs discouraging egg consumption during pregnancy have been reported in Malawi and other SSA countries [53, 54]. Such beliefs may partly explain the lower egg intake observed in rural areas compared to urban areas. Alternatively, the association may be influenced by cooking methods and accompanying foods. Previous studies have shown that the nutritional value of food is affected by how it is prepared [55], and eating lots of fried food has been linked to an increased risk of being overweight or obese among adults [56]. In the present study, FFQ data indicated that eggs were commonly consumed fried or scrambled. Therefore, the amount of cooking oil used may partly explain the observed association between egg intake and overweight/obesity.

Factors negatively associated with overweight/obesity

To our knowledge, previous studies have not reported an association between rice consumption and a lower odd of overweight/obesity. In the present study, mothers with a normal BMI in rural areas consumed fewer carbohydrates than mothers with overweight/obesity. Food price is a major determinant of food choice in Malawi, and rice is generally more expensive than maize, the staple food [57]. Consequently, rice consumption may reflect a dietary pattern characterized by lower overall carbohydrate intake. Previous studies have reported an inverse association between BMI and dietary protein intake. Higher protein intake may reduce the risk of being overweight or obese among women of reproductive age [58, 59]. Therefore, greater consumption of protein-rich foods may contribute to improved dietary quality and a healthier weight status [60]. A study conducted in South Africa reported that pregnant women who consumed eggs one to three times per week had approximately 50% lower odds of being obese than those who consumed eggs less frequently [43]. Furthermore, maternal dietary habits are known to influence the eating behaviors of young children [61, 62]. Therefore, children who achieve adequate dietary diversity may be more likely to have mothers with healthier dietary patterns. As dietary diversity is considered an important component of a healthy diet, the observed association between children's dietary diversity and maternal overweight/obesity may reflect shared household eating practices [63, 64].

Children

In the present study, children living in urban areas consumed greater amounts of energy, protein, fat, “meat, poultry, and fish”, as well as “other vegetables”, than children living in rural areas. These findings are consistent with previous studies reporting higher consumption of “meat, poultry, and fish” in urban areas of Malawi [18]. The prevalence of overweight/obesity was higher among children in urban than in rural areas. Although this study was not designed to identify the determinants of childhood overweight/obesity, the higher intake of energy-dense foods, including “meat, poultry, and fish”, may be associated with this urban–rural difference. In addition, children in urban areas may have consumed a greater proportion of their energy from carbohydrates. At the same time, this study identified a high prevalence of stunting and inadequate energy intake among children. Previous studies have shown that undernutrition during infancy and early childhood may increase the risk of obesity and NCDs later in life [16]. Furthermore, the rural analysis demonstrated a positive association between a family history of NCDs and maternal overweight/obesity, which is characteristic of the double burden of malnutrition observed in many low- and middle-income countries. Addressing this challenge will require community-specific interventions and guidance on appropriate dietary intake across the life course.

The WHO identifies four key principles of a healthy diet: adequacy, balance, moderation, and diversity [63]. Our findings suggest that dietary patterns associated with overweight/obesity differ between urban and rural areas in Malawi. Therefore, nutrition interventions should be tailored to the local context. In urban areas, these interventions may focus on promoting balanced and diverse diets, as well as preventing excessive fat and carbohydrate intake. In rural areas, interventions may focus on reducing excessive carbohydrate intake while encouraging adequate dietary quantity and diversity.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Firstly, measurement error may have occurred as dietary intake was assessed using a single 24-hour dietary recall, which may not accurately reflect habitual dietary intake. Intake distributions derived from a single-day assessment tend to be wider than usual intake distributions, potentially resulting in an overestimation of the proportion of individuals with intakes below the estimated requirement [65]. In addition, the nutrient intakes reported in this study were lower than those reported in previous studies using FFQ [18]. The 24-hour dietary recall method has been reported to underestimate intake [66, 67], and additional errors may have arisen from estimation of portion-size during data collection. As underestimation of dietary intake increases with BMI [66], the observed associations between dietary intake and overweight/obesity may have been attenuated or biased towards the null. Consequently, some true associations may not have been detected. Secondly, selection bias may have occurred because participants were recruited at health centers. In Malawi, essential healthcare services for children are provided free of charge through the public health system, which likely reduced direct financial barriers to participation. However, participation may still have been influenced by parental health-seeking behavior, accessibility of health facilities, and socioeconomic circumstances [68]. Thirdly, as this study used a cross-sectional design, causal relationships cannot be inferred. Finally, seasonal variation should be considered when interpreting the findings. Data collection was conducted during the dry season, when dietary intake and food choices may differ from the rainy season. Therefore, the findings may not fully reflect dietary patterns throughout the year. Despite these limitations, this study provides valuable evidence on dietary intake and overweight/obesity among mother–child pairs in Malawi. Given the limited availability of dietary intake data in Malawi, these findings could inform future health and nutrition policy development and intervention planning.

Conclusion

This study found that child undernutrition, including stunting and wasting, coexisted with maternal overweight/obesity in both urban and rural areas of Malawi. This highlights the presence of the double burden of malnutrition. Maternal overweight/obesity was associated with several dietary factors, and these associations differed between urban and rural settings. In urban areas, maternal overweight/obesity was positively associated with the intake of carbohydrate, egg, fat, and "other vitamin A-rich fruits and vegetables", and negatively associated with the intake of “meat, poultry, and fish”. In rural areas, maternal overweight/obesity was positively associated with carbohydrate intake, but negatively associated with rice, egg, fat and "other vitamin A-rich fruits and vegetables” intake, as well as dietary diversity. These findings suggest the need for life-course nutrition education and interventions tailored to the specific contexts of urban and rural areas throughout the life course. Such approaches may help to address maternal and childhood overweight/obesity and the double burden of malnutrition in Malawi. Further longitudinal and nationally representative studies are needed to confirm these associations and improve our understanding of them in Malawi. Overall, this study provides valuable evidence on dietary factors and maternal overweight/obesity in Malawi and highlighting the importance of context-specific nutrition strategies and the promotion of appropriate dietary practices throughout the life course.
