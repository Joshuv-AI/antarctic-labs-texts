Post-traumatic trigeminal neuropathy following third molar extraction due to retromolar canal injury: a case report
Authors: Pavithra Manohar, Samarjit Dey, Anuvindha JS, Jitendra Chawla
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The inferior alveolar nerve block (IANB) is the most frequently used mandibular nerve block in oral surgery; however, it has a reported failure rate of 15%–45%. The presence of the retromolar canal (RMC) and nerve, an anatomical variation in the mandibular retromolar region, can cause IANB failure. Although its occurrence is variable, the canal has gained increasing clinical relevance because of its vulnerability during oral and maxillofacial surgical procedures. Its presence may result in anesthetic failure during IANB and, less commonly, may act as a conduit for spreading infection or tumor metastasis. Although several studies have examined the prevalence and anatomical variations of the RMC, literature specifically addressing the traumatic neuropathy from injury to this area during third-molar extraction is lacking. This case report details post-traumatic trigeminal neuropathy following injury to the RMC during third-molar extraction. The case findings contribute to the limited clinical evidence, emphasize the importance of preoperative identification of anatomical variations, and underscore the need for meticulous intraoperative management to minimize the risk of iatrogenic neuropathic complications.

INTRODUCTION

The inferior alveolar nerve block (IANB) is the most frequently used mandibular nerve block in oral surgery. It anesthetizes the mandibular teeth, body and inferior portion of the mandible, mucous membrane anterior to the mental foramen, and lingual soft tissues and periosteum. However, it has a reported failure rate of 15%–45% in routine practice. The reasons for failure include anatomical variations, technical errors, and pathological factors that lower tissue pH. Among these anatomical variations, the presence of the retromolar canal (RMC) and nerve (RMN) is one cause of IANB failure [1]. The RMC, an anatomical variation in the mandibular retromolar region, houses thin myelinated nerve fibers, small venules, and arterioles enclosed within a collagen bundle and adipose tissue [2].

The RMN, a branch traversing the RMC, provides supplementary sensory innervation to the mandibular molars, posterior alveolar process, retromolar fossa mucosa, and adjacent buccal mucosa. It may occasionally extend to the buccinator and temporalis muscles, demonstrating overlapping innervation with the long buccal and inferior alveolar nerves. Although its occurrence is variable, the canal has gained increasing clinical relevance because of its vulnerability during oral and maxillofacial surgical procedures, including third molar extractions, posterior mandibular implant placement, and sagittal split osteotomies [3].

The presence of the RMC carries significant clinical implications. These include local anesthetic failure due to the overlapping innervation of the inferior alveolar nerve (IAN) and RMN, a phenomenon supported by recent studies demonstrating that supplemental infiltration in the RMC region enhances the success of IANB anesthesia for mandibular molars [4]. Further implications are postoperative complications such as hemorrhage, neurosensory disturbances, and, less commonly, the spread of odontogenic infection. An example of the latter is the Chompret–L’Hirondel abscess, which typically migrates anteriorly along the external oblique ridge toward the first molar–premolar region following the path of least resistance. However, in rare instances, the RMC may provide an alternative anatomical corridor for infection spread, thereby underscoring its potential though limited clinical relevance [5]. Finally, in rare instances, the canal can facilitate tumor metastasis due to the anatomical continuity it provides with the foramen [6].

The present report describes a case of traumatic neuropathy resulting from injury to the RMN during third molar extraction. This case contributes to the limited clinical literature, highlights the importance of preoperative recognition of anatomical variations, and emphasizes the need for meticulous intraoperative management to minimize the risk of iatrogenic neuropathic complications.

CASE REPORT

A 33-year-old man presented with a burning sensation in the lower posterior gingival region that had persisted for 2 months. The discomfort was gradual in onset, chronic in nature, and localized to the right retromolar region (Fig. 1). The patient described continuous baseline discomfort interspersed with episodes of pain lasting seconds to minutes. These episodes were preceded by a tingling sensation that gradually intensified into a burning pain, which he described as hot, raw, irritating and typically confined to a localized area. Symptoms were exacerbated by consuming hot or cold foods, had no identifiable relieving factors, and did not radiate or cause sleep disturbance.

The patient’s dental history revealed a transalveolar extraction of a horizontally impacted right mandibular third molar 6 months earlier at our institution. Although the immediate postoperative course was uneventful, the patient developed a tingling sensation at the surgical site approximately 15 days after surgery, which gradually progressed to persistent burning pain. The patient also recalled an episode of excessive intraoperative bleeding during the procedure.

On clinical examination, the extraction socket was healed. The mucosa over the surgical site was normal with no signs of inflammation. Periodontal evaluation revealed no pocket formation distal to the second molar. On palpation of the right retromolar region, the patient reported dysesthesia, while a cold stimulus applied with a cotton-tipped applicator moistened with ethyl chloride elicited hyperesthesia. Comparison with the contralateral side revealed normal responses. Vitality testing of all mandibular teeth demonstrated symmetrical bilateral responses, thereby excluding odontogenic pain as a possible cause. Given the allodynia to cold stimulation and the dysesthesia, neuropathic pain was suspected.

To confirm the neuropathic origin, a peripheral diagnostic nerve block was performed. Initially, local infiltration at the third molar region did not relieve the pain; however, an IANB resulted in complete pain resolution. This finding indicated that the pain originated from a branch of the IAN rather than the buccal nerve.

The preoperative cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) revealed RMC. Approximately 15 days after the extraction, the patient developed a tingling sensation around the surgical site, which gradually progressed to a persistent burning pain lasting approximately 2 months. The temporal association between the symptom onset and the extraction, along with the characteristic clinical features and confirmatory findings from the diagnostic nerve block, strongly supported the diagnosis of neuropathic pain secondary to traumatic injury involving the RMN and RMC. A diagnostic flowchart for neuropathic pain was formulated in this study (Fig. 2).

The patient was counselled about their condition and informed that surgical intervention at this stage would not be beneficial and might exacerbate the pain. Pharmacological management was initiated with oral gabapentin 100 mg. At follow-up visits at 3 and 6 months, the patient reported a progressive reduction in pain intensity and burning sensation, resulting in significant overall symptomatic improvement.

DISCUSSION

The International Association for the Study of Pain (IASP) defines neuropathy as a disturbance of nerve function or a pathological change within a nerve. Neuropathy resulting directly from trauma is termed traumatic neuropathy.

The pathophysiology of traumatic neuropathy can be attributed to peripheral and central sensitizations. Following nerve injury, inflammatory mediators—such as bradykinin, prostaglandins, serotonin, adenosine triphosphate, and cytokines—are released at the injury site, activating nociceptors. This release leads to a lowered activation threshold and heightened neuronal excitability, which clinically manifests as hyperalgesia—a hallmark of peripheral sensitization. In parallel, central sensitization occurs within the dorsal horn of the spinal cord and higher pain pathways, resulting in an amplification of pain signals and persistence of pain perception even without continued peripheral stimulation [7].

This report describes traumatic neuropathy resulting from injury to the RMN during third molar extraction. First described by Schejtman et al. in 1967, the RMC is a bifid variation of the mandibular canal that courses anterosuperiorly within the bone to terminate in the retromolar fossa [68]. The RMC opens as one or more foramina, which is anatomically bounded posteriorly by the anterior border of the mandibular ramus and anteriorly by the third molar region [68]. Its origin is hypothesized to result from the failure of primitive canals to fuse during the prenatal developmental period. Several authors, including Ossenberg, Patil, Zhang, and von Arx, have proposed classifications for the RMC (Fig. 3) [69].

In this case, the RMC was classified as Type B according to the Von Arx classification on CBCT evaluation. The canal was 1.3 mm in diameter and 12.3 mm in height, and was located 5.5 mm distal to the third molar socket and 6.3 mm from the buccal cortex (Fig. 4). These values are consistent with published morphometric data, which emphasizes that careful preoperative identification of the RMC using CBCT can enable precise surgical planning and help prevent potential complications.

Previous reports describe traumatic neuropathy due to RMC injury. Kikuta et al. described a case of paresthesia in the vestibular mucosa of the mandibular molar region after surgical removal of an impacted third molar, with sensorineural deficits persisting at the 12-month postoperative follow-up [10]. Singh reported a case of hypoesthesia involving the buccal sulcus and gingiva extending from the retromolar to the anterior region, resulting from injury to the RMN during the extraction of an impacted mandibular third molar [11].

In this case, the patient responded to pharmacological management; however, several authors have proposed different treatment strategies for post-traumatic trigeminal neuropathy, ranging from early surgical to pharmacological interventions.

Surgical management is advocated within 24–48 hours of injury to prevent irreversible nerve damage, particularly in cases where a causative factor, such as a dental implant encroaching on the nerve, is identified. Zuniga and Renton further note that surgical decompression or repair within 90 days of injury is associated with improved outcomes [12].

Pharmacological management plays a key role as an immediate and long-term therapeutic approach. Corticosteroids such as prednisolone or dexamethasone, administered in tapering doses, are commonly used in the acute phase to control inflammation and reduce secondary nerve injury [13]. For chronic neuropathic pain, agents such as gabapentin, pregabalin, and tricyclic antidepressants remain the mainstay of treatment, owing to their efficacy in modulating neuropathic pain pathways [7]. Topical approaches, including local anesthetic application and capsaicin patches, have also demonstrated effectiveness in providing regional pain relief.

Recently, botulinum toxin injections have emerged as an adjunctive therapy for refractory neuropathic pain, owing to their ability to inhibit nociceptive receptor activity and reduce peripheral and central sensitization [14].

RMC prevalence ranges between 8.8% and 75.4% [9], and more than 50% of the affected patients may continue to experience permanent symptoms of neuropathic pain despite various management strategies. These observations emphasize the need for a thorough preoperative assessment, including careful clinical evaluation and appropriate imaging tailored to individual risk. Panoramic radiography remains the initial modality, whereas CBCT is recommended when anatomical variations or a close relationship between the mandibular canal and third molar roots are suspected. Ogawa et al. (2016) reported accessory branches such as the RMC in up to 28% of cases, underscoring the importance of preoperative identification to prevent neurovascular injury [15].

A precise surgical technique, characterized by gentle tissue handling, adequate flap design, and controlled bone removal, especially in the retromolar region, minimizes trauma and postoperative neuropathic complications. Vigilant postoperative follow-up, including early detection of sensory disturbances and timely intervention, further reduces the risk of chronic neuropathy. Given the high prevalence of RMCs, clinicians should document the findings, inform patients of the possible postoperative sequelae, and maintain appropriate medicolegal records. Collectively, these measures help reduce morbidity and enhance patient safety [7].
