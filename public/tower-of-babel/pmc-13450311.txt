TRIM29-dependent AKR1B10 degradation ameliorates MASH
Authors: Zhengcai Ma, Juan Li, Xiaoduo Li, Jianyu Zhu, Chunyong Sun, Jinyin Zhang, Zhipeng Yang, Jifei Liu, Yuan Zhou, Zhenwei Gui, Jingwei Li, Hongmei Wang, Dan Shi, Qiang Song, Xuegang Li, Rongkun Tao, Hang Ma, Xiaoli Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Metabolic dysfunction–associated steatohepatitis (MASH) is a prevalent and life-threatening liver disease with limited effective therapies, necessitating the urgent identification of previously unidentified molecular targets and effective pharmacologic agents. This study identifies aldo-keto reductase family 1 member B10 (AKR1B10) as the MASH hallmark, showing elevated expression in patient tissues and serum, correlating strongly with disease severity. Genetic ablation of Akr1b10 in mice significantly attenuated high-fat/high-cholesterol (HFHC) diet–induced steatosis, inflammation, and fibrosis. Mechanistically, we found that tripartite motif protein 29 (TRIM29) functions as an E3 ubiquitin ligase that directly binds AKR1B10, promoting its ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation, which ameliorates MASH progressions. Furthermore, the natural compound coptisine (COP) allosterically facilitated TRIM29-AKR1B10 interaction, accelerating AKR1B10 degradation and showing improved efficacy in MASH mice. Moreover, Trim29 overexpression enhances COP-mediated symptoms alleviation. Our findings delineate the TRIM29/AKR1B10 axis as a druggable signaling pathway and identify COP as a promising therapeutic candidate for MASH.

INTRODUCTION

Metabolic dysfunction–associated steatohepatitis (MASH) is a severe and progressive form of chronic liver disease that develops within the spectrum of metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) and poses a significant and growing global health challenge due to its strong associations with obesity and type 2 diabetes epidemics (1, 2). The core pathogenesis of MASH is characterized by hepatic lipid accumulation, lobular inflammation, and hepatocyte ballooning degeneration, which collectively drive the progression of liver fibrosis (2). Without effective intervention, MASH can advance through progressive fibrosis stages to cirrhosis and significantly increasing the risk of developing hepatocellular carcinoma and liver-related mortality (3). Although therapeutic approaches in clinical trials are emerging (4–8), including the recent approval of the thyroid hormone receptor β (THRβ) agonist resmetirom (9), the therapeutic efficacy of these candidates remains limited by the disease’s complex pathogenesis and potential safety concerns. Therefore, elucidating MASH pathogenesis and identifying novel therapeutic targets are essential for developing more effective treatments.

Aldo-keto reductase family 1 member B10 (AKR1B10) (10, 11) has emerged as a critical mediator in the pathogenesis of MASH. While initially recognized as a biomarker for early hepatocellular carcinoma (12), AKR1B10 is consistently up-regulated in human MASLD datasets and shows a strong correlation with disease severity (13, 14). Multiomic profiling studies identified AKR1B10 as a core component of a high-risk steatohepatitis signature, and its inhibition ameliorates steatosis, inflammation, and fibrosis in preclinical models (15, 16). These findings position AKR1B10 as a promising therapeutic target for MASH. Nevertheless, the regulatory mechanisms governing AKR1B10 ubiquitination and degradation remain poorly understood. It is well established that dysfunction of the ubiquitination pathway contributes to metabolic diseases like MASH (17, 18). For instance, certain tripartite motif (TRIM) family proteins, including TRIM16, have been shown to mitigate MASH by down-regulating phospho-TAK1 via ubiquitination (19–21). However, no specific TRIM protein has been reported to directly regulate AKR1B10 degradation.

In this study, we identified TRIM29 (22) as a key regulator of AKR1B10 stability in MASH, demonstrating that TRIM29 directly interacts with AKR1B10 and promotes its ubiquitin-mediated degradation. Hepatic TRIM29 overexpression significantly attenuated MASH progression in mice through the reduction of AKR1B10 protein levels, resulting in decreased hepatic lipid accumulation and fibrosis. Furthermore, we showed that the natural compound coptisine (COP), which shares structural homology with berberine (BBR), functions as an allosteric modulator that enhances the TRIM29-AKR1B10 interaction. This enhanced binding accelerates AKR1B10 degradation, leading to significant anti-MASH effects in vivo. These findings establish the TRIM29/AKR1B10 axis as promising therapeutic target for MASH treatment.

RESULTS

AKR1B10 mediates MASH progression through its ubiquitination

We initially assessed AKR1B10 expression during the progression of clinical liver disease. Immunohistochemical analysis of patient liver tissues revealed significantly elevated AKR1B10 levels correlating with increasing severity of inflammation and fibrosis, with the highest expression observed in hepatocellular carcinoma samples compared to controls (e.g., liver cysts) (Fig. 1A). In addition, clinical assessments and pathological scoring of hepatic pathological sections were performed using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining to ensure the experimental reliability (fig. S1A). Consistent with tissue findings, serum AKR1B10 levels were markedly higher in patients across various stages of MASLD compared to healthy individuals (Fig. 1B and tables S1 and S2).

To directly evaluate the AKR1B10’s role in MASH pathogenesis, we used homozygous Akr1b10(−/−) and heterozygous (Akr1b10(+/−) knockout (KO) mice fed a high-fat (fig. S1, B and C), high-cholesterol (HFHC) diet (Fig. 1C). In the absence of significant differences in diet and drinking water intake (fig. S1, D and E), Akr1b10(−/−) mice exhibited significantly attenuated weight gain, epididymal white adipose tissue (eWAT), and reduced liver weight gain compared to wild-type (WT) and heterozygous mice on the HFHC diet (Fig. 1, D to G). This protective effect extended to metabolic and liver injury markers: Serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and total cholesterol (TC) levels were significantly lower in Akr1b10(−/−) HFHC mice than in WT or heterozygous HFHC groups (Fig. 1, H to J). Furthermore, Akr1b10(−/−) HFHC mice displayed improved glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity, comparable to WT mice on a normal diet (ND; fig. S1, F and G).

Histopathological analysis further confirmed the functional improvements. Liver sections from Akr1b10(−/−) HFHC mice showed substantially fewer lipid vacuoles (H&E staining) and reduced lipid accumulation (Oil Red O staining) compared to WT and heterozygous HFHC mice (Fig. 1, K and O). Fibrosis, assessed by Masson’s trichrome and Sirius Red staining, was also significantly diminished in the Akr1b10(−/−) HFHC group (Fig. 1, L, P, and Q). In addition, immunofluorescence for macrophage markers CD11b and F4/80 demonstrated markedly reduced hepatic inflammation in Akr1b10(−/−) HFHC mice relative to controls (Fig. 1, L, M, R, and S).

Given prior evidence linking proteasome inhibition to AKR1B10 accumulation in cancer cells (23), we examined its turnover mechanism in MASH. Western blot analysis revealed significantly decreased ubiquitination levels alongside markedly increased AKR1B10 protein levels in liver tissues from HFHC-fed mice compared to controls (Fig. 1T). Parallel in vitro experiments in palmitic acid/oleic acid (PAOA)–treated HepG2 cells recapitulated these findings: AKR1B10 protein was up-regulated, and this increase was further amplified by the proteasome inhibitor MG132, but not by the lysosome inhibitor chloroquine (CQ) (Fig. 1U). These results collectively demonstrate that AKR1B10 protein accumulation in MASH is primarily mediated by proteasome-mediated degradation.

TRIM29 is an E3 ligase that ubiquitinated AKR1B10

To identify proteins regulating AKR1B10 ubiquitination, we performed coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP) coupled with liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC-MS) using human embryonic kidney (HEK) 293T cells overexpressing 3×Flag-tagged AKR1B10. SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) analysis of the Co-IP eluates confirmed successful isolation of 3×Flag-AKR1B10, validating the experimental approach (Fig. 2, A and B). Given the established role of the proteasome in AKR1B10 degradation, we specifically focused on E3 ubiquitin ligases within the Co-IP/MS dataset. Intersecting proteins with ≥5 unique peptides against an E3 ligase database yielded three candidates: RACK1, TRIM29, and PARP1 (Fig. 2C and table S3). Subsequent Co-IP validation in HEK293T cells indicated binding of AKR1B10 to TRIM29 and PARP1, but not RACK1 (Fig. 2D).

To confirm physiological relevance, we assessed interactions with endogenous proteins in HepG2 cells. Co-IP clearly demonstrated AKR1B10 binding to endogenous TRIM29 (Fig. 2D), while interactions with endogenous PARP1 and RACK1 were negligible (fig. S2, A and B). Furthermore, we ruled out potential involvement of deubiquitinating enzymes by showing no significant interaction between AKR1B10 and USP10 (fig. S2C). On the basis of its consistent interaction and well-characterized E3 ligase function, we selected TRIM29 for further mechanistic investigation.

We next tested whether TRIM29 directly regulates AKR1B10 protein levels. Co-overexpression of TRIM29 with AKR1B10 in both HEK293T and HepG2 cells resulted in a significant decrease in AKR1B10 protein abundance compared to control vector coexpression (Fig. 2, E and F). This down-regulation was substantially reversed upon treatment with the proteasome inhibitor MG132 (Fig. 2, E and F), indicating that TRIM29 promotes AKR1B10 degradation via the proteasomal pathway.

To definitively establish the TRIM29’s regulatory role, we used complementary gain-of-function and loss-of-function approaches. Specific knockdown of endogenous TRIM29 in HepG2 cells using short hairpin RNA (shRNA) led to a significant increase in AKR1B10 protein levels (Fig. 2G). Conversely, gradient overexpression of TRIM29 in HEK293T cells produced a corresponding, dose-dependent decrease in AKR1B10 protein abundance (Fig. 2H). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that TRIM29 is a critical negative regulator of AKR1B10 protein stability and strongly supports its role as the E3 ubiquitin ligase responsible for targeting AKR1B10 for proteasomal degradation.

TRIM29 directly interacts with AKR1B10 in cell culture

To verify the direct interaction between TRIM29 and AKR1B10, we cotransfected HEK293T cells with Myc-TRIM29 and Flag-AKR1B10 expression vectors and performed reciprocal Co-IPs. Pull-down with an anti-Myc antibody recovered Flag-AKR1B10, while pull-down with an anti-Flag antibody enriched Myc-TRIM29, demonstrating their bidirectional interaction in vitro (Fig. 3, A and B). Repeating this experiment in HepG2 cells confirmed that exogenous Myc-TRIM29 and Flag-AKR1B10 each coprecipitate their endogenous counterpart (Fig. 3, C and D), indicating that their association also occurs in a hepatic context.

To rule out the need for additional mammalian cofactors, we carried out in vitro pull-downs with bacterially expressed proteins. Glutathione S-transferase (GST)–AKR1B10, but not GST alone, selectively retained endogenous TRIM29 from HEK293T lysates (Fig. 3E), and reciprocally, in the detection with Myc as the control, His-AKR1B10 demonstrated a strong binding affinity with Myc-TRIM29. (Fig. 3F). Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) further quantified their affinity, yielding a dissociation constant (Kd) of 7.45 × 10−7 M (Fig. 3G), consistent with high-affinity binding.

To visualize their interaction in living cells, we implemented a SNAP-based bimolecular fluorescence complementation assay (24, 25). After testing four tag orientations, we found that pairing the N-terminal SNAP fragment fused to AKR1B10 with the C-terminal SNAP fragment fused to TRIM29 produced a robust fluorescent signal, whereas other combinations did not (figs. S2D and 3H). Immunofluorescence confirmed that this reconstituted fluorescence colocalized predominantly in the cytoplasm (Fig. 3I). In summary, these reciprocal Co-IPs in two cell lines, in vitro pull-downs of purified proteins, quantitative SPR measurements, and live-cell BiFC imaging provide convergent evidence that TRIM29 and AKR1B10 engage in a direct, high-affinity interaction.

TRIM29 interacts with the key residues K169/E308 of AKR1B10

To explore the interaction between TRIM29 and AKR1B10, we first used the AlphaFold Server to predict the binding effects between the two proteins. The predicted binding energy of −5.5 kcal/mol suggested strong interaction. Key hydrogen bonds and salt bridges were identified between residues K426, D431, and R434 of TRIM29 and residues K169 and E308 of AKR1B10 (Fig. 4A), indicating that these electrostatic interactions are critical for stabilizing the complex. On the basis of these predictions, we truncated AKR1B10 into three domains (1 to 105 amino acids, 106 to 210 amino acids, and 211 to 316 amino acids) corresponding to the main interaction regions (Fig. 4B).

Next, we used the GST pull-down assay to assess the interaction between TRIM29 and the truncated AKR1B10 domains. The results showed that the 106 to 210 and 211 to 316 domains captured significantly more TRIM29 than the 1 to 105 domain, with the 106 to 210 domain capturing the most TRIM29 (Fig. 4C). This suggests that residue K169 within the 106– to 210–amino acid domain is the primary contributor to TRIM29 binding, while E308 in the 211– to 316–amino acid domain plays a secondary role. To further confirm these findings, we used the BiFC system in cells. The strongest fluorescence signal was observed with the 106– to 316–amino acid construct (encompassing both K169 and E308), followed by the 106– to 210–amino acid domain; the 211– to 316–amino acid and 1– to 105–amino acid domains showed weaker signals (Fig. 4D). In addition, point mutations in K169A and E308A significantly reduced fluorescence intensity, further validating the necessity of these residues for the TRIM29-AKR1B10 interaction (Fig. 4, D and E).

To determine the specific ubiquitination linkage involved in TRIM29-mediated degradation of AKR1B10, we conducted Co-IP experiments using a panel of ubiquitin mutants with lysine-to-arginine substitutions (K6R, K11R, K27R, K29R, K33R, K48R, and K63R). The results indicated that the K33R mutation markedly impaired AKR1B10 ubiquitination (Fig. 4, F and G), identifying ubiquitin K33 as the primary site for TRIM29-directed ubiquitination of AKR1B10. Thus, we confirmed that the 106 to 210 and 211 to 316 domains of AKR1B10 interact effectively with TRIM29, with residues K169 and E308 serving as key interaction sites, and identified ubiquitin K33 as the site involved in TRIM29-mediated AKR1B10 ubiquitination.

TRIM29 overexpression promotes AKR1B10 degradation and mitigates MASH process

To further validate the effect of TRIM29-mediated degradation of AKR1B10 in alleviating MASH, we delivered adeno-associated virus 8 (AAV8)–Trim29 via tail vein injection at weeks 8, 12, and 20 of HFHC feeding and continued modeling until weeks 26 (Fig. 5, A and B). The AAV8 system mediates robust targeted overexpression in mouse liver, while no obvious leaky expression was observed in other organs, as has been widely reported in previous studies (26, 27). Robust overexpression of TRIM29 in the liver (Fig. 5, C and D, and table S10) led to a marked reduction in hepatic AKR1B10 protein levels, as confirmed by Western blot (Fig. 5E). No significant differences in food or water intake were observed among the groups (fig. S3, A and B). Compared to AAV8–green fluorescent protein (GFP) controls, AAV8-Trim29–treated mice exhibited significantly lower body, eWAT, and liver weights after 26 weeks, although the liver-to-body weight ratio remained comparable between groups (Fig. 5, F to H). Notably, liver size and gross morphology were substantially improved in the TRIM29-overexpressing group (Fig. 5H and fig. S3C). Serum biomarkers reflected reduced hepatic injury: ALT and AST levels were decreased in the Trim29 group (Fig. 5, J and K), and TC was significantly lowered (Fig. 5L), while insulin resistance remained comparable between groups (fig. S3, D and E). Histological analysis confirmed that Trim29 overexpression attenuated key MASH features, including hepatic lipid accumulation, fibrosis, and inflammation (Fig. 5, M to R).

To delineate downstream pathways affected by AKR1B10 down-regulation, we combined transcriptomic and proteomic analyses of liver tissue from WT mice on ND, WT on HFHC, and Akr1b10(−/−) mice on HFHC, with quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) confirming the transcriptome data’s reliability (∼91%; fig. S3, F and G). Principal components analysis, partial least squares–discriminant analysis, and correlation analysis results of protein-transcript association sets revealed favorable intersample similarity (fig. S3, H to J), supporting the feasibility of subsequent integrative omic analyses. Quadrant analysis of differentially expressed proteins and genes identified significantly altered elements at both protein and transcript levels (Fig. 5S). Differential proteomic and transcriptomic analyses were performed with the criteria of P value < 0.05 and fold changes > 1.5 and > 1.2, respectively. Multiomic Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathway enrichment analysis of the proteome and transcriptome in the KO-HFHC/HFHC group demonstrated significant perturbations in the nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) and phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)–AKT signaling pathways (Fig. 5T). Notably, HFHC-fed WT mice exhibited significant activation of the NF-κB pathway and suppression of the PI3K-AKT pathway, whereas these alterations were substantially blunted in HFHC-fed Akr1b10(−/−) mice, which showed reactivation of PI3K-AKT signaling (Fig. 5U), consistent with prior reports (28–31).

Collectively, hepatic overexpression of TRIM29 down-regulates AKR1B10 protein levels, leading to modulation of the PI3K/AKT/NF-κB axis. This mechanistic pathway underlies the observed amelioration of HFHC-induced hepatic steatosis, inflammation, liver injury, and fibrosis, thereby attenuating MASH progression.

COP facilitates TRIM29-dependent AKR1B10 ubiquitination and degradation

Previous studies have shown that BBR ameliorates MASH by binding to AKR1B10 (32). Our previous research has further demonstrated that the extracts of Rhizoma Coptidis (which contains both COP and BBR) improve metabolic diseases such as non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) and obesity (33–35). Given that COP and BBR share a common parent nucleus [fig. S4A, (i)], we hypothesized that COP might similarly exert therapeutic effects by promoting AKR1B10 ubiquitination.

COP is the second most abundant alkaloid in Rhizoma Coptidis (no. 201103), after BBR. It exhibits green fluorescence under 488-nm excitation, with intensity increasing proportionally to concentration [fig. S4A, (ii) and (iii)], and has reported anti-inflammatory (36, 37) and anticancer potentials (38–40). We therefore investigated whether COP alleviates MASH by enhancing TRIM29-mediated ubiquitination and degradation of AKR1B10.

In HepG2 and Huh7 cells, the median inhibitory concentration values for COP were determined to be 150 and 135 μM, respectively (fig. S4B). After PAOA-induced lipid loading, increasing doses of COP (35, 75, and 150 μM) caused dose-dependent reductions in both intracellular AKR1B10 protein levels, while TRIM29 levels remained unchanged (Fig. 6A and fig. S4, C and D). A cycloheximide chase assay showed that COP accelerated AKR1B10 degradation compared to control (Fig. 6B), whereas AKR1B10 mRNA did not decrease with COP treatment in either cell line (Fig. 6, C and D), indicating regulation at the posttranslational level. The COP-induced reduction of AKR1B10 was reversed by the proteasome inhibitor MG132 but not by the lysosome inhibitor CQ (Fig. 6, E and F), supporting proteasome-dependent degradation.

Real-time imaging with N-terminal mCherry-tagged AKR1B10 revealed progressive loss of fluorescence over 24 hours of COP treatment, consistent with enhanced protein turnover (Fig. 6G). Immunofluorescence colocalization showed that COP increased overlap among COP, AKR1B10, and the proteasome marker PSMD2 (Fig. 6H). Furthermore, COP enhanced colocalization of AKR1B10 with TRIM29, and knockdown of endogenous TRIM29 (shTRIM29) markedly reduced COP’s ability to down-regulate AKR1B10 (Fig. 6, I and J), indicating TRIM29 dependence.

To determine whether COP acts directly on AKR1B10, molecular docking and dynamics simulations were performed (Fig. 6K and fig. S4, C and D), indicating that COP binds strongly to AKR1B10 than to TRIM29, with binding energy of −6.8 kcal/mol versus −3.64 kcal/mol (fig. S4E). Key interacting residues were ASN-161, CYS-299, and GLN-184 (Fig. 6L), which coincide with sites implicated in NADPH (form of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate) binding and catalytic function, suggesting that COP perturbs core structural features of AKR1B10. Circular dichroism confirmed conformational changes in AKR1B10 upon COP binding (Fig. 6M). Isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) yielded a dissociation constant of 1.78 × 10−6 M, indicating high affinity binding (Fig. 6N). Cellular thermal shift assay and drug affinity responsive target stability further validated altered AKR1B10 stability after COP binding (fig. S4, F to I). Last, in vitro Co-IP using Flag-AKR1B10 from HEK293T cells and purified His-TRIM29 showed that COP augmented TRIM29 pull-down by AKR1B10 (Fig. 6O), demonstrating that COP augmented the interaction between TRIM29 and AKR1B10. Together, these data indicate that COP targets AKR1B10 directly, induces conformational and stability changes, and promotes its TRIM29-dependent ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation.

Overexpression of TRIM29 enhances COP-mediated improvement in MASH

To study the COP’s therapeutic effect in MASH, we compared it to the AKR1B10 inhibitor oleanolic acid (OA) (41, 42) and the positive control obeticholic acid (OCA) (43). After MASH modeling, COP was administered for 8 weeks (fig. S5A). Compared to the model group, COP-treated mice had a significant reduction in body weight, outperforming OCA; OA largely maintained pretreatment weight but was still better than the model group (fig. S5B), and no significant differences were observed in food and water intake (fig. S5, C and D). Similar trends appeared in liver weight, eWAT, and liver-to-body ratios (fig. S5, E to G). Serum ALT, AST, and TC were all significantly lower in the COP group versus the model group (fig. S5, H to J), indicating alleviated liver injury and improved lipid metabolism. Consistent with cellular-level findings, the hepatic mRNA expression of AKR1B10 was significantly up-regulated in mice following COP intervention, including both the OA and OCA groups (fig. S5K). However, immunohistochemistry revealed that COP reduced hepatic AKR1B10 protein expression more strongly than OA or OCA (fig. S5, L and M). Pathologically, COP markedly suppressed inflammation and lipid accumulation (fig. S5, N to P) and inhibited fibrosis progression, as evidenced by Masson and Sirius Red staining. Its effect on fibrosis was comparable to OCA in Masson staining and superior to both OA and OCA in Sirius Red staining (fig. S5, Q to T). Inflammatory markers CD11b and F4/80 were also reduced with COP treatment, reflecting slowed inflammation progression (fig. S5, U to X). Together, these data show that COP provides dose-dependent improvements in body/liver weight, liver injury markers, steatosis, inflammation, and fibrosis.

Given prior evidence that Trim29 overexpression alleviates MASH, we next tested whether COP could enhance the benefit of hepatic Trim29 overexpression. Six-week-old male mice received tail vein injections of AAV8-Trim29 at weeks 8, 12, and 20 to induce hepatic Trim29 overexpression, continued on an HFHC diet until week 16, and then began oral COP administration from week 17 (Fig. 7A). Effective liver expression of Trim29 was confirmed, and Western blot analysis also demonstrated significant overexpression (Fig. 7, B and C). In the absence of significant differences in food and water intake (fig. S6, A and B), mice receiving AAV8-Trim29 + COP showed greater reductions in body weight, eWAT, and liver size, along with a significantly decreased organ-to-body weight ratio, compared to AAV8-GFP + COP controls (Fig. 7, D to G, and fig. Correspondingly, liver injury was attenuated: ALT levels dropped significantly in the AAV8-Trim29 + COP group versus control (Fig. 7H). However, no significant difference was observed in AST levels (Fig. 7I), and serum TC was also reduced (Fig. 7J), while blood glucose remained unchanged (fig. S6, D and E). H&E and Oil Red O staining assessment showed improved regulation of inflammation and lipid accumulation in the livers of HFHC-fed mice with combined treatment (Fig. 7, K and L), and fibrosis was significantly suppressed as shown by Masson and Sirius Red staining (Fig. 7, M and N). Immunofluorescence of CD11b and F4/80 confirmed that inflammation was further mitigated in the AAV8-Trim29 + COP group (Fig. 7, O and P).

In summary, COP alone delivers substantial therapeutic benefit in MASH, reducing body/liver weight, liver injury, steatosis, inflammation, fibrosis, and AKR1B10 expression more effectively than OA or OCA. Combining COP with hepatic Trim29 overexpression produces improved effects, yielding deeper suppression of AKR1B10, greater amelioration of metabolic and histopathological hallmarks, and stronger anti-inflammatory and antifibrotic outcomes. These in vivo results reinforce the in vitro finding that COP enhances TRIM29-mediated ubiquitination and degradation of AKR1B10 to improve MASH.

DISCUSSION

MASH is a complex liver disease posing an increasing global health challenge with a pressing unmet need for more effective and safer treatment options. Although Rezdiffra was approved in 2024 for managing F2-F3 fibrosis in MASH (44), lifestyle changes and pharmacological treatments remain the primary clinical approach (45, 46). Further research is still needed to identify effective pharmacotherapies and treatment strategies for MASH. Our study identifies the TRIM29/AKR1B10 axis as a previously unrecognized regulatory pathway, advancing the understanding of MASH pathogenesis and revealing new therapeutic opportunities. This axis centers on ubiquitin-mediated degradation of AKR1B10, a protein previously linked to lipid metabolism, inflammation, and fibrosis but now established as a dynamic pathogenic driver rather than a passive biomarker.

We demonstrate that AKR1B10 serves as a significant contributor to MASH progression, not merely as an up-regulated biomarker in serum and liver samples from patients with MASLD/MASH. Genetic ablation of Akr1b10 in mice attenuated HFHC diet–induced steatosis, inflammation, and fibrosis, confirming its pathogenic role. Integrated multiomic analyses revealed that Akr1b10 deletion primarily affects hepatic energy–related metabolic and inflammatory pathways, including lipid metabolism, NF-κB, PI3K/AKT, retinol metabolism, and tumor necrosis factor signaling. This aligns with reports that AKR1B10 can stabilize acetyl–coenzyme A carboxylase (47), a rate-limiting enzyme in lipogenesis, indicating that its role in MASH is multifaceted, affecting both inflammatory (48) and fatty acid metabolic pathway (49).

Our investigation further established that AKR1B10 protein stability is regulated via ubiquitination by TRIM29. Through multiple approaches in vitro and in cell cultures, we identified TRIM29 as the primary E3 ubiquitin ligase responsible for targeting AKR1B10 for proteasomal degradation (the effective interaction between the 106 to 210 and 211 to 316 structural domains of AKR1B10 and TRIM29, in which K169 and E308 play a major role, is mediated by the ubiquitin K33 site). Prior studies showed that TRIM29 plays critical roles in various cancers (50) and innate immune response–related disorders (51) by regulating the PTEN/AKT/mammalian target of rapamycin (52) and TRIM29/IGF2BP1/PD-L1 axes (ubiquitination regulation) (53), and our findings reveal its novel function in metabolic liver disease MASH. This introduces protein degradation as a crucial regulatory layer in MASH, shifting focus from transcriptional control to posttranslational mechanisms.

The translational potential of targeting the TRIM29/AKR1B10 axis may be influenced by genetic variation in the TRIM29 gene. Now, literature on TRIM29 single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) is sparse. The most documented SNP, rs2444240, located upstream of the gene, has been associated with variations in corneal curvature (54), but its impact on liver function or TRIM29 expression remains unexplored. A compelling meta-analysis has revealed a notable ethnic disparity: High TRIM29 expression predicts poor prognosis in Asian cancer populations but a favorable outcome in Caucasians (55). This suggests that underlying genetic variations, potentially including uncharacterized SNPs, could significantly modulate the TRIM29’s functional role, including the degradation of AKR1B10. Therefore, future clinical investigations into this pathway should include genetic stratification to understand potential interindividual differences in treatment response.

We found that the natural compound COP functions as a molecular enhancer of this pathway by allosterically promoting the TRIM29-AKR1B10 interaction. These findings are consistent with previous reports that BBR, which shares a common structural scaffold with COP, ameliorates MASLD progression through AKR1B10 targeting (32). While COP has demonstrated diverse biological activities including anti-inflammatory (36, 56), antitumor (57), and antibacterial properties (58), its clinical application faces challenges such as poor oral bioavailability. Future work should focus on developing advanced delivery systems, including lipid nanoparticles or polymer-based carriers, to improve pharmacokinetic profiles. In addition, rigorous preclinical toxicology studies are essential to ensure safety and rule out off-target effects.

Our demonstration that AAV8-mediated TRIM29 overexpression alleviates MASH pathology suggests potential genetic therapeutic approaches. This strategy could be adapted to deliver other beneficial genes, while the COP-induced enhancement of natural protein degradation represents a promising pharmacological paradigm. Future research should focus on developing more potent small-molecule enhancers through structure-based drug design and exploring combination therapies targeting complementary pathways.

Collectively, our work defines the TRIM29/AKR1B10 ubiquitination axis as a critical regulatory mechanism in MASH and identifies COP as a promising therapeutic candidate that acts by enhancing this natural degradation pathway. By connecting an E3 ligase to the stability of a key metabolic driver, we provide a mechanistic framework for understanding MASH pathogenesis. These findings highlight the therapeutic potential of targeting protein degradation machinery, moving beyond simple inhibition to promoting clearance of pathogenic proteins. Future efforts aimed at optimizing delivery systems for COP-like compounds, coupled with deeper investigation of this axis’s genetic and molecular nuances, hold significant promises for advancing MASH treatment.

Liver samples of human donors

All human liver samples were deidentified and obtained from adult donors diagnosed with MASLD; hepatic cyst samples served as controls. The study cohort included hepatic cyst (n = 8), moderate interface inflammation and fibrosis (n = 8), severe interface inflammation and fibrosis (n = 8), and hepatocellular carcinoma (n = 8). Liver samples from patients with steatotic liver disease caused by excessive alcohol consumption (both male and female), viral infection (including hepatitis B and C virus), or drug abuse were excluded.

All procedures involving human donors were conducted in accordance with the ethical principles for medical research involving human subjects and the Declaration of Helsinki [64th World Medical Association (WMA) General Assembly]. The requirement for informed consent was waived by the Ethics Committee of Chongqing University Three Gorges Hospital because this study involved the use of deidentified residual human liver tissues obtained from a tissue bank, and the research posed no more than minimal risk to the subjects.

Mouse strains

Akr1b10 KO mice [C57BL/6N background; National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) ID: 67861] were generated using complete gene KO technology based on CRISPR and CRISPR-Cas9. Heterozygous Akr1b10(+/−) male and female mice were obtained from Cyagen Biosciences (Suzhou, China). These mice were further bred in-house, and genotypes were determined by PCR (primer sequences are listed in table S4) to obtain Akr1b10(+/−) and Akr1b10(−/−) male and female offspring. All experiments were conducted using mice at 6 weeks of age.

MASH animal model

Six-week-old mice were randomly assigned to either an ND (18% protein, 4% fat, and 78% carbohydrate; Botaihongda, Beijing, China) or an HFHC diet [20% protein, 60% fat, 20% carbohydrate, and 1% cholesterol (w/w); Shuyu Biotechnology, Shanghai, China]. After 1 week of acclimatization, mice were continuously fed their respective diets for 16 to 18 weeks to induce the MASH phenotype. For experimental grouping, mice were randomly allocated to groups with six to eight mice per group. The animals were maintained under controlled temperature conditions (22° ± 2°C) and filtered air conditions, with a 12-hour light/12-hour dark cycle. They had ad libitum access to food and water. At the conclusion of the designated feeding period, the mice were euthanized, and a series of samples was collected for further analysis. These samples included blood and liver tissue. Portions of the liver were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, while the remaining tissues were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C for subsequent analyses.

AAV-mediated hepatic overexpression and drug treatment

An AAV8-based viral system was used to achieve liver-targeted overexpression of Trim29, with an AAV8-TBG-EGFP vector serving as the control (catalog no. 1: AAV8-TBG>Kozak-Mouse Trim29 CDS [NM-023655.2]/3×Flag/EGFP; catalog no. 2: AAV8-TBG-EGFP). Following initiation of HFHC modeling, mice received tail vein injections at weeks 8, 12, and 20 [viral dose ≥1.8 × 1011 genome copies (GC) per mouse per injection], totaling three injections in total. This ensured each mouse received a cumulative viral dose of no less than 5 × 1011 GC, thereby guaranteeing stable expression of Trim29. At week 16 of modeling, mice were further treated with COP by oral gavage.

To evaluate the effects of COP dosage on MASH progression, mice fed an ND and gavaged with saline served as controls. HFHC-fed mice were treated with either low-dose COP (50 mg/kg) or high-dose COP (100 mg/kg) by daily gavage. OCA (0.75 mg/kg) was used as a positive control, and OA (75 mg/kg) was used as an AKR1B10 inhibitor. Drug administration continued for 8 weeks. At the end of treatment, mice were humanely euthanized in accordance with animal ethics guidelines, and samples including blood, organs, and tissues were collected for further analysis.

RNA sequencing

Three groups of mouse liver tissues (ND, HFHC, and KO-HFHC; n = 3) were processed using TRIzol reagent in combination with oligo(dT). Total RNA was enriched for mRNA with magnetic beads. The enriched mRNA (featuring polyadenylate tails) was fragmented in a fragmentation buffer and reverse-transcribed into double-stranded cDNA using random N6 primers. The synthesized double-stranded cDNA then underwent end repair and 5′ phosphorylation to create blunt ends and form a protruding “A” base at the 3′ end, followed by ligation with bubble-shaped adapters having protruding “T” bases. Ligation products were amplified via PCR using specific primers to construct the library. After quality control, the library was sequenced on the DNBSEQ platform with 150-bp paired-end reads (PE150) by ShenZhen BGI Genomics Co. Ltd.

Proteomics

Three groups of mouse liver tissues (CHOW/ND, HFHC, and KO-HFHC; n = 5) were retrieved from the frozen storage and placed on ice. The samples were suspended in a protein lysis buffer (8 M urea and 1% SDS) supplemented with appropriate protease inhibitors to suppress protease activity. Subsequently, the samples were processed three times in a high-throughput tissue grinder, with each processing lasting for 180 s. Then, cryogenic ultrasonic treatment was carried out on the samples. The supernatant was collected through low-temperature centrifugation, and the protein concentration was measured using the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) method (BCA Protein Assay Kit from Thermo Fisher Scientific). After protein quantification, SDS-PAGE electrophoresis was conducted.

After digesting the sample proteins with solutions such as TEAB, TCEP, IAM, and trypsin, the peptide segments were dried with a vacuum pump. Then, the digested peptide segments were redissolved in 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid, desalted with Hydrophilic–Lipophilic Balance (HLB), and dried with a vacuum concentrator. Last, the peptide segments were quantified by ultraviolet absorbance values using a NANO DROP ONE (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Peptides were analyzed using a VanquishNeo coupled to a timsTOF Ultra2 mass spectrometer (Bruker, Germany) at Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). A Micro Pillar Array Columns (uPAC) High Throughput column (75 μm by 5.5 cm, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was used with solvent A [water, 2% acetonitrile (ACN), and 0.1% formic acid] and solvent B (water, 80% ACN, and 0.1% formic acid). The chromatographic run time was 8 min. Data acquisition was controlled by Compass HyStar software (Bruker, Germany). The mass spectrometer operated in DIA mode, positive ion polarity, with an ion source voltage of 1.6 kV and a scan range of 300 to 1500 mass/charge ratio.

Serum biochemical analysis

Serum ALT and AST levels were measured using an automated biochemical analyzer (HITA 7600).

Cell culture and treatment

HEK293T (item no. CL-0001), HepG2 (item no. CL-0103), and Huh7 (item no. CL-0120) cells were obtained and used as previously described in our earlier studies (33). Cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (Gibco, lot: 6124197) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, streptomycin (100 μg/ml), and penicillin (100 U/ml), under standard culture conditions (37°C, 5% CO2).

To mimic hepatocellular steatosis in vitro, cells were incubated with culture medium containing palmitic acid (PA) and oleic acid at indicated concentrations. L02 cells were treated with PA/oleic acid at 0.2/0.4 mM, and HepG2 cells were treated with PA/OA at 0.5/1 mM, using 0.5% fatty acid–free bovine serum albumin (BSA) as control. Cells were further incubated with COP for 12 hours. Following treatment with either dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) or COP for 12 hours, Oil Red O staining was performed.

TRIM29-based BiFC analysis

To evaluate TRIM29-mediated BiFC activity, multiple fusion protein expression systems were constructed (25): nSNAP-AKR1B10 (NS-AKR1B10) with cSNAP-TRIM29 (CS-TRIM29); nSNAP-AKR1B10 with TRIM29-cSNAP (TRIM29-CS); AKR1B10-nSNAP (AKR1B10-NS) with cSNAP-TRIM29; AKR1B10-nSNAP with TRIM29-cSNAP; nSNAP-AKR1B10 with cSNAP; nSNAP with TRIM29-cSNAP; and nSNAP with cSNAP (1:1). A total of 4 μg of plasmid DNA was cotransfected into HEK293T cells cultured in 35-mm glass-bottom dishes. After 24 hours, cells were incubated with 1 ml of Hoechst 33342 and 0.5 μM SNAP-Cell 647-SiR (New England Biolabs) at 37°C for 30 min, washed three times with PBS, and prepared for imaging.

On the basis of screening results (Fig. 3H and fig. S2D), the nSNAP-AKR1B10 and cSNAP-TRIM29 pair was selected for detecting the interaction between truncated AKR1B10 domains and TRIM29. Imaging was performed using an ultrahigh-speed, super-resolution real-time dynamic imaging system (SpinSR, Olympus Corporation, Japan).

DNA cloning

Full-length human AKR1B10 and TRIM29 cDNAs were amplified by PCR and subcloned into pcDNA3.1-3×Flag and pcDNA3.1-Myc plasmids (Addgene, Watertown, MA, USA) to generate pcDNA3.1-3×Flag-AKR1B10 and pcDNA3.1-Myc-TRIM29 overexpression vectors. Hemagglutinin-tagged WT ubiquitin expression plasmids were derived from the pCMV backbone. Ubiquitin site-mutant plasmids (K6, K11, K27, K29, K33, K48, and K63) were purchased from Miaoling Biotechnology (Wuhan, China). The coding sequence of SNAP was commercially synthesized and inserted into the pcDNA3.1/Hygro(+) vector to generate pcDNA3.1-SNAP. On the basis of this construct, pcDNA3.1-nSNAP and pcDNA3.1-cSNAP plasmids were generated by inverse PCR to express the N- or C-terminal SNAP fragments, respectively. AKR1B10 was cloned into either the N- or C-terminal site of nSNAP, generating pcDNA3.1-nSNAP-AKR1B10 and pcDNA3.1-AKR1B10-nSNAP. Similarly, TRIM29 was cloned into either the N- or C-terminal site of cSNAP, yielding pcDNA3.1-cSNAP-TRIM29 and pcDNA3.1-TRIM29-cSNAP. These constructs were generated by inverse PCR amplification followed by homologous recombination, with primer sequences listed in table S5.

After selecting the optimal BiFC pair (pcDNA3.1-nSNAP-AKR1B10 and pcDNA3.1-cSNAP-TRIM29), truncated and site-directed mutant constructs of AKR1B10 were designed on the basis of AlphaFold3-predicted interaction domains: pcDNA3.1-nSNAP-AKR1B10(1-105), pcDNA3.1-nSNAP-AKR1B10(106-210), pcDNA3.1-nSNAP-AKR1B10(211-316), pcDNA3.1-nSNAP-AKR1B10(106-316), pcDNA3.1-nSNAP-AKR1B10(106-210/K169A), and pcDNA3.1-nSNAP-AKR1B10(211-316/E308A). All constructs were generated by inverse PCR (table S6). For bacterial expressions, AKR1B10 and TRIM29 cDNAs were cloned into the pET28a vector (Addgene) to generate pET28a-His-AKR1B10 and pET28a-His-TRIM29 expression plasmids. To obtain GST-tagged constructs, the GST coding sequence was amplified by PCR and inserted into the linearized pET28a-His-AKR1B10 backbone, yielding pET28a-GST-AKR1B10. Truncated GST-AKR1B10 variants (1 to 105, 106 to 210, and 211 to 316) were generated using truncation-specific primers (table S7). The pcDNA3.1-mCherry-AKR1B10 construct was generated from pcDNA3.1-3×Flag-AKR1B10 by linearizing the plasmid via inverse PCR, followed by homologous recombination with the mCherry sequence amplified from the pCMV-mCherry-MYO1B(pig)-Neo vector (purchased from Miaoling Biotechnology), resulting in pcDNA3.1-mCherry-AKR1B10 (table S8). For RNA interference, shRNA constructs targeting TRIM29 were generated using the pLV3-U6-shRNA-CopGFP-Puro backbone. shRNA oligonucleotides were commercially synthesized (table S9) and ligated into the vector to generate corresponding knockdown plasmids.

LC-MS analysis

HEK293T cells were transfected with pcDNA3.1-3×Flag-AKR1B10 for transient overexpression. After 24 hours, cells were lysed, and supernatants were collected. Protein A magnetic beads (MedChemExpress) were incubated with Flag antibody and washed with phosphate buffered saline with Tween-20 (PBST) to remove unbound antibody, followed by incubation with protein lysates at 4°C for 4 to 6 h on a rotator. Beads were washed at least five times with PBST to eliminate nonspecific binding, and bound proteins were eluted by boiling in 1× loading buffer at 100°C. Protein samples were subjected to LC–tandem mass spectrometry analysis using a Q Exactive HF mass spectrometer coupled to an UltiMate 3000 nano-HPLC system. Data acquisition and analysis were performed by Mohe (Shanghai) Biotechnology Co. Ltd. (project ID: MH20230405).

Coimmunoprecipitation

HEK293T cells were transfected with pcDNA3.1-3×Flag-AKR1B10, pcDNA3.1-Myc-TRIM29, or both plasmids. After 24 hours, cells were lysed in immunoprecipitation (IP) lysis buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors, and lysates were cleared by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 10 min. Supernatants were incubated with Protein A magnetic beads (MCE) prebound to Flag antibody for 4 hours at 4°C. Beads were subsequently incubated with protein lysates on a rotator at 4°C for 4 to 6 hours, washed at least five times with PBST to remove nonspecific binding, and eluted by boiling in 1× loading buffer at 100°C for 10 min. Immunoprecipitates were collected using a magnetic separator and subjected to downstream analysis.

Recombinant protein expression and purification

Recombinant protein expression was performed in Escherichia coli BL21(DE3) using the pET28a vector under the T7 promoter system. Expression plasmids were transformed into BL21(DE3) cells, plated, and grown overnight. Single colonies were picked for large-scale culture, and protein expression was induced with 0.5 mM isopropyl-β-d-thiogalactopyranoside at an optical density at 600 nm of 0.5 to 0.6, followed by overnight incubation at 18°C. Bacterial cultures were harvested by centrifugation, resuspended in lysis buffer, and disrupted by sonication (160 W, 3 s on/7 s off cycles, total of 30 min). Lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C, and supernatants were filtered through 0.45-μm membranes for subsequent purification.

Protein purification was performed using Nickel-Nitrilotriacetic acid (Ni-NTA) Beads 6FF (Smart-Lifesciences). Columns were preequilibrated with lysis buffer, and supernatants were loaded twice to maximize binding. After washing with 30 and 70 mM imidazole to remove nonspecific proteins, target proteins were eluted with 250 mM imidazole and analyzed by SDS-PAGE. Desalting was performed with Prepacked Desalting Columns (Smart-Lifesciences). Columns were pretreated with ultrapure water and equilibrated with the appropriate buffer. Eluted proteins were loaded onto the column, and the first 5 ml of flow-through fractions were collected for analysis and storage.

GST pull-down assay

GST and GST-AKR1B10 fusion proteins were expressed in E. coli BL21(DE3) and purified from bacterial lysates after cell lysis and centrifugation. Equal amounts of GST or GST-AKR1B10 proteins were incubated with glutathione beads at 4°C on a rotator for 4 hours. Beads were pelleted by centrifugation at 4000 rpm for 5 min, resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and washed at least six times until no protein was detected in the wash fractions.

Subsequently, 400 μl of HEK293T cell lysates was added to the beads preloaded with GST or GST-AKR1B10, and the mixtures were incubated at 4°C on a rotator for 6 to 8 hours. Beads were washed five times with PBS, and bound proteins were eluted by boiling in 1× loading buffer at 100°C for 10 min. Supernatants were collected by centrifugation at 4000 rpm for 5 min and subjected to further analysis.

AlphaFold3-based protein interaction prediction

Protein-protein interaction between TRIM29 (AlphaFold ID: AF-Q14134-F1) and AKR1B10 [Protein Data Bank (PDB) ID: 1ZUA] was predicted using the AlphaFold Server. Protein sequences were retrieved from the UniProt database and submitted to the AlphaFold Server for automated modeling. Predicted structures were downloaded from the AlphaFold database, and the top-ranked complex was subjected to energy minimization with AMBER18 using the ff14SB force field. Binding energy was subsequently evaluated with PRODIGY (https://wenmr.science.uu.nl/prodigy/), and the most favorable interaction mode was visualized using PyMOL v2.5.3.

SPR analysis

The dynamic interaction between AKR1B10 and TRIM29 was evaluated by SPR using a Nicoya Lifesciences instrument (Waterloo, Canada) to determine the dissociation constant (Kd). A COOH sensor chip (Nicoya Lifesciences) was installed and conditioned with PBS buffer (pH 7.4) at a constant flow rate of 150 μl/min. The system was flushed with 80% isopropanol to prevent bubble formation, followed by chip pretreatment with 10 mM HCl for 60 s. The chip surface was then activated with N-hydroxysuccinimide and 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl) carbodiimide hydrochloride. AKR1B10 protein was immobilized on the chip at a flow rate of 20 μl/min for 5 min, after which serially increasing concentrations of TRIM29 protein were injected at the same flow rate. Binding data were recorded and analyzed to evaluate the affinity between AKR1B10 and TRIM29.

Oil Red O staining

Cells

Cells were washed twice with PBS after removal of culture medium and fixed with Oil Red O fixative for 20 to 30 min. After discarding the fixative, cells were rinsed twice with distilled water and incubated with 60% isopropanol for 20 to 30 s. Freshly prepared Oil Red O working solution was then added for 10 to 20 min. The stained cells were briefly differentiated with 60% isopropanol for 10 to 20 s until intercellular boundaries were clearly visible, followed by two to five washes with distilled water until excess dye was removed. Nuclei were counterstained with Mayer’s hematoxylin for 1 to 2 min, rinsed two to five times with water, and incubated with Oil Red O buffer for 1 min for bluing. Last, cells were covered with distilled water and visualized under a microscope.

Tissues

The Oil Red O working solution was prepared by mixing saturated Oil Red O stock with distilled water at a 3:2 ratio, equilibrated at room temperature for 5 to 10 min, and filtered before use. Tissue sections were fixed with formalin-calcium fixative for 10 min, washed thoroughly with distilled water, and incubated with 60% isopropanol for 5 to 10 s. Sections were then stained with filtered Oil Red O solution for 10 to 20 min, followed by rapid differentiation with 60% isopropanol for 5 to 10 s until interstitial structures were clearly visible. Staining was terminated by rinsing with distilled water. Nuclei were counterstained with Mayer’s hematoxylin for 3 to 5 min, rinsed with distilled water, and blued under running tap water for 10 min. Sections were mounted with glycerol gelatin and examined by microscopy.

H&E staining

Paraffin-embedded tissue sections were deparaffinized with xylene for 10 min twice, followed by rehydration through a graded ethanol series: 100% ethanol for 5 min twice, 90% ethanol for 5 min, and 75% ethanol for 5 min and then rinsed in running water overnight. Sections were stained with hematoxylin for 3 to 5 min and washed in tap water. Differentiation was performed in hematoxylin differentiation solution for 2 to 5 s, followed by thorough rinsing in running water. Bluing was achieved by immersing sections in bluing reagent for 2 to 5 s and rinsing again in running water. For eosin staining, sections were dehydrated through 85 and 95% ethanol (5 min each) and incubated in alcoholic eosin solution for 5 min. Sections were then dehydrated in absolute ethanol twice (5 min each). For clearing, sections were incubated in fresh absolute ethanol for 5 min, followed by xylene for 5 min twice. Last, sections were mounted with neutral balsam and examined under a light microscope.

Masson’s trichrome staining

Paraffin-embedded tissue sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated to water. Sections were immersed in Masson solution A overnight, rinsed in running water, and then stained for 1 min in an equal mixture of Masson solutions B and C, followed by a brief differentiation and wash in water. Sections were subsequently stained with Masson solution D for 6 min, rinsed in running water, and then incubated in Masson solution E for 1 min. Without washing, sections were immediately transferred into Masson solution F for 2 to 30 s, differentiated in 1% acetic acid, and dehydrated with absolute ethanol. Last, sections were incubated in fresh absolute ethanol for 5 min, cleared in xylene for 5 min, and mounted with neutral balsam for microscopic examination.

Sirius Red staining

Paraffin-embedded tissue sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated to water. Sections were briefly blotted to remove excess moisture and stained in modified Sirius Red solution A for 2 min, followed by rinsing in running water. They were then immersed in modified Sirius Red solution B for 30 min. Subsequently, sections were rapidly dehydrated through four changes of absolute ethanol (3 to 5 s each), cleared in fresh xylene for 1 min, and lastly mounted with neutral balsam for microscopic examination.

Immunohistochemistry

Paraffin-embedded tissue sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated to water. Antigen retrieval was performed under optimized conditions according to tissue type, ensuring that the buffer did not evaporate excessively and that sections were not allowed to dry. After natural cooling, slides were washed three times in PBS (pH 7.4) on a decolorizing shaker (5 min each). To quench endogenous peroxidase activity, sections were incubated with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 25 min at room temperature in the dark, followed by three PBS washes (5 min each). Tissue areas were encircled with a hydrophobic barrier pen, and 3% BSA was applied to cover the sections for 30 min at room temperature for blocking. After removing the blocking solution, sections were incubated with primary antibody diluted 1:200 in PBS overnight at 4°C in a humidified chamber. Following incubation, slides were washed three times with PBS (5 min each) and then incubated with horseradish peroxidase–conjugated secondary antibody (species specific) for 2 to 3 hours at room temperature. For chromogenic detection, slides were washed three times with PBS and incubated with freshly prepared DAB solution, with reaction time monitored microscopically until a brown-yellow signal was observed. The reaction was stopped by rinsing in running water. Nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin for ∼3 min, followed by differentiation in hematoxylin differentiation solution for a few seconds, rinsing in running water, and bluing. Last, sections were dehydrated sequentially in 75% ethanol (5 min), 85% ethanol (5 min), absolute ethanol I (5 min), absolute ethanol II (5 min), n-butanol (5 min), and xylene I (5 min). After brief air drying, sections were mounted with neutral resin and examined under a bright-field microscope.

Immunofluorescence

Paraffin-embedded sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated, followed by antigen retrieval. After natural cooling, slides were washed three times in PBS (pH 7.4) on a shaker (5 min each). Tissue areas were encircled with a hydrophobic barrier pen, and sections were blocked with BSA for 30 min at room temperature. After washing three times with PBS, sections were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C in a humidified chamber. The following day, sections were washed three times with PBS and incubated with appropriate fluorescent secondary antibodies for 2 to 3 hours at room temperature in the dark. Slides were then washed three additional times in PBS, incubated with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) solution for 20 min in the dark, washed again in PBS (three times, 5 min each), and treated with autofluorescence quencher (solution B) for 5 min, followed by rinsing in running water for 10 min. Last, sections were mounted using an antifade mounting medium. Confocal microscopy was performed [ultrahigh speed and super-resolution real-time dynamic imaging system (SpinSR, Olympus Corporation, Japan)]. Imaging parameters were set as follows: DAPI channel, excitation of 330 to 380 nm and emission of 420 nm (blue nuclei); fluorescein isothiocyanate/488 channel, excitation of 465 to 495 nm and emission of 515 to 555 nm (green signal); Cyanine 3 (Cy3) channel, excitation of 510 to 560 nm and emission of 590 nm (red signal).

Molecular docking

The structure of COP (PubChem CID: 72322) was downloaded from the PubChem database and converted from SDF to Mol2 format using Chem3D software. The crystal structure of AKR1B10 was obtained from the Research Collaboratory for Structural Bioinformatics (RCSB) PDB (PDB ID: 1ZUA). Molecular docking was performed using AutoDock Vina, and docking results were visualized and analyzed with PyMOL.

Molecular dynamics simulation

The docked COP-AKR1B10 complex was used as the initial structure for all-atom molecular dynamics (MD) simulations performed with AMBER18. Root mean square deviation and root mean square fluctuation values of the complex were calculated using gmx rms and gmx rmsf. The binding free energy between AKR1B10 and COP was estimated by the MM/GBSA method, based on 45 to 50 ns of MD trajectories.

Cellular thermal shift assay

HepG2 cells were harvested, centrifuged, and resuspended in PBS (pH 7.0). Cells were lysed in buffer containing 2% NP-40, 1% protease inhibitor, and 1% phosphatase inhibitor, followed by three freeze-thaw cycles in liquid nitrogen. Cell lysates were incubated with increasing concentrations of COP (0, 3, 5, 10, 20, 40, 75, and 150 μM) for 30 min at room temperature, with 0.1% DMSO as control. Samples were then heated in a water bath at 56°C for 5 min. Alternatively, lysates incubated with selected COP concentrations were subjected to a thermal gradient (37° to 72°C, 5 min) using a PCR thermocycler. Supernatants were collected by centrifugation, and target protein stability was assessed by Western blot.

Drug affinity responsive target stability

HepG2 cells were washed with PBS, lysed in IP lysis buffer supplemented with 1% phosphatase and protease inhibitors, and incubated on ice for 1 hour. Lysates were clarified by centrifugation, and supernatants were incubated at room temperature for 30 min with increasing concentrations of pronase E (enzyme:protein ratio 1:100; stock concentration, 1.8 μg/μl; dilution range, 1:25,600 to 1:800). Alternatively, after determining the appropriate enzyme concentration, lysates were incubated with serial concentrations of COP (0, 3, 5, 10, 20, 40, 75, and 150 μM) for 30 min at room temperature. Supernatants were collected by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 10 min and analyzed by Western blot assess target protein stability.

Circular dichroism spectroscopy

Purified AKR1B10 protein (0.2 mg) was incubated with COP (50 μM) at room temperature for 30 min. A 100-μl aliquot of the mixture was loaded into a quartz cuvette, and circular dichroism spectra were recorded using a J-810 spectropolarimeter (JASCO, Japan). Measurements were performed over a wavelength range of 260 to 310 nm with a 1-nm bandwidth and 2-s integration time per point. Each spectrum was scanned three times at a rate of 100 nm/min, and baseline correction was applied by subtracting the solvent spectrum.

Isothermal titration calorimetry

Isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) experiments were performed at 25°C using purified protein in PBS buffer at a concentration of 0.01 mM. COP stock solution (0.5 mM) was prepared in dialysis buffer. For titration, 300 μl of protein solution was loaded into the sample cell and titrated with 50 μl of injections of COP solution. Titrations of COP into AKR1B10 protein were performed as the experimental group, while titrations of COP into PBS served as the blank control. Data were fitted using the independent binding model to calculate dissociation constant (Kd), stoichiometry (n), enthalpy change (ΔH), and entropy change (ΔS). Measurements were carried out on a NANO ITC instrument (TA Instruments, USA), and thermodynamic parameters were analyzed using Origin 7.0 software.
