Measuring the homogeneity scale using the peculiar velocity field
Tamara M. Davis

Abstract
We propose an innovative definition of the scale at which the Universe becomes homogeneous based on measurements of velocities rather than densities. When using the matter density field, one has to choose an arbitrary scale (e.g. within 1% of the average density) to define the transition to homogeneity. Furthermore, the resulting homogeneity scale is strongly degenerate with the galaxy bias. By contrast, peculiar velocities (PV) allow us to define an unambiguous scale of homogeneity, namely the distance at which the velocities between pairs of galaxies change from being on-average correlated to anti-correlated. Physically, this relates to when the motion of pairs of galaxies is influenced by the matter density between them, rather than beyond. The disadvantage is that peculiar velocities are more difficult to measure than positions, resulting in smaller samples with larger uncertainties. Nevertheless, we illustrate the potential of this approach using the peculiar velocity correlation functions obtained from the Sloan Digital Sky Survey PV catalog, finding an homogeneity scale of R H ≈ 133 ​ + 28 − 52 ​ Mpc / h R_{H}\approx 133\begin{subarray}{c}+28\\ -52\end{subarray}\,\rm{Mpc/h} . Finally, we show that more precise measurements are within reach of upcoming peculiar velocity surveys, and highlight this homogeneity scale’s potential use as a standard ruler within the standard cosmological model.

Introduction

The cosmological principle posits that the Universe, on sufficiently large scales, is statistically homogeneous and isotropic. Observations broadly support this foundational assumption of the standard cosmological model, yet determining the scales at which deviations from homogeneity may bias cosmological inference remains a subtle and nuanced task.

A number of tests of cosmic homogeneity have been conducted using the three-dimensional distribution of galaxies. One such test is based on the consideration that in an homogeneous distribution, the number of galaxies contained within a sphere of radius
r
scales proportionally with the sphere’s volume. The radius
R_{H}
at which this proportionality is reached (within a specified tolerance) provides an empirical characterisation of the transition to homogeneity

, and it has been shown that the homogeneity scale itself, if measured at different redshifts, can be used as a standard ruler in a given cosmological model
[

4

.
However, these analyses also revealed that the transition scale measured from a set of galaxies is degenerate with the galaxy bias
b
(describing the proportionality between the tracer and the underlying dark matter density field), thus inferring
R_{H}
of the matter field from galaxies requires assuming both a cosmological and galaxy bias model
[
38
]
.

In this work, we present a new method to measure the transition-to-homogeneity scale using peculiar velocity data. At scales where linear cosmological perturbation theory applies, PVs offer two key advantages: they provide an unbiased tracer of the underlying matter distribution, and they are especially sensitive to the largest-scale modes. Moreover, the recent evidence for anomalously large bulk flows in the local Universe

provides strong motivation to explore novel tests of the cosmological principle using PVs as a complementary probe. This is particularly timely in view of upcoming surveys such as the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI) PV and the 4MOST Hemispheric Surveys (4HS), as well as the increasing interest in whether backreaction effects from inhomogeneities may help alleviate several current cosmological tensions

The core idea of our approach is that an inhomogeneous density field induces characteristic correlations in the peculiar motions of distant tracers. For example, the gravitational field of a central mass generates anti-correlated motions between galaxy pairs positioned on opposite sides of it, and correlated motions for pairs within the same hemisphere.
We propose to identify the homogeneity scale as the one at which,
on average
, the motion of tracers within spheres of radius
R_{H}
becomes anti-correlated. More specifically, we identify it with the radius at which the average correlation between the parallel projections of the peculiar velocities of the tracers along their separation vector vanishes. To illustrate the potential of this approach, we apply it to the measurements of the PV correlation function obtained in
[

for the Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS) PV catalog
[
20

Velocity correlation function

If the velocity field is linear, homogeneous, isotropic and irrotational, the correlation between the velocity components
\left(i,j\right)
of two tracers at positions

\displaystyle\langle v_{i}(\vec{r}^{A}),v_{j}(\vec{r}^{B})\rangle=\Psi_{ij}(r)\;,

(2.1)

where we have introduced the velocity tensor

\Psi_{ij}=\left[\Psi_{\parallel}(r)-\Psi_{\perp}(r)\right]\hat{r}^{A}_{i}\hat{r}^{B}_{j}+\Psi_{\perp}(r)\delta_{ij}\;,

(2.2)

where
r
is the distance between
A
and
B
,
\vec{r}^{A}
and
\vec{r}^{B}
their positions, and the
\Psi_{||,\perp}

In linear perturbation theory, these depend only on the variance of the density field and the growth rate of structure, with the functional dependence in Fourier space reading simply:

\Psi_{\parallel}(r)=\frac{H^{2}a^{2}f^{2}}{2\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dkP(k)\left[j_{0}(kr)-2\frac{j_{1}(kr)}{kr}\right]\;,\qquad\quad\Psi_{\perp}(r)=\frac{H^{2}a^{2}f^{2}}{2\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dkP(k)\frac{j_{1}(kr)}{kr}\;.

(2.3)

Since in practice one can only measure the projection of the velocity field along the line of sight
u
, it is useful to define the following functions

\langle\Psi_{1}(r)\rangle=\frac{\sum u_{A}u_{B}\cos{\theta_{AB}}}{\sum\cos^{2}\theta_{AB}}\qquad\quad\langle\Psi_{2}(r)\rangle=\frac{\sum u_{A}u_{B}\cos{\theta_{A}}\cos{\theta_{B}}}{\sum\cos{\theta_{AB}}\cos{\theta_{A}}\cos{\theta_{B}}}\;,

(2.4)

with the sums taken over pairs of galaxies in the
r
separation bin, and where the
\theta

\cos\theta_{i}=\hat{r}\cdot\hat{r}_{i}
,
\cos\theta_{AB}=\hat{r}_{A}\cdot\hat{r}_{B}
). Ref.
[
17
]
derives the following transformations between
\Psi_{1,2}
and
\Psi_{\parallel,\perp}

\langle\Psi_{1}(r)\rangle=\mathcal{A}(r)\Psi_{\parallel}(r)+\left[1-\mathcal{A}(r)\right]\Psi_{\perp}(r)\;,\qquad\quad\langle\Psi_{2}(r)\rangle=\mathcal{B}(r)\Psi_{\parallel}(r)+\left[1-\mathcal{A}(r)\right]\Psi_{\perp}(r)\;,

(2.5)

where the functions
A
and
B
depend on the survey geometry

\mathcal{A}=\frac{\sum w_{A}w_{B}\cos\theta_{AB}}{\sum w_{A}w_{B}\cos^{2}\theta_{AB}}\;\qquad\quad\mathcal{B}=\frac{\sum w_{A}w_{B}\cos\theta_{A}\cos\theta_{B}}{\sum w_{A}w_{B}\cos\theta_{AB}\cos\theta_{A}\cos\theta_{B}}\;,

(2.6)

where the weights

account for the distance-dependent error on individual velocity measurements (
w\propto(n_{g}P_{v}+\sigma^{2})^{-1}
), see for example Refs.
[

]

Correlation dimension

Let us briefly review how the homogeneity scale can be defined from counts in spheres. A good estimate of the number of neighboring tracers in a sphere of radius
R
centered on any galaxy is
[
33
]
:

N(R)=4\pi\bar{\rho}\int_{0}^{R}d\bar{r}\left[1+\xi(\bar{r})\right]\bar{r}^{2}\;,

(2.7)

where
\bar{\rho}
is the mean density,
\xi(r)
the real space two point correlation function of the density field, and whose volume average is simply

\mathcal{N}(R)=\frac{3}{4\pi R^{3}}N(R)\;.

(2.8)

From the above we can define the correlation dimension

D_{2}(R)=3+\frac{d\ln\mathcal{N}(R)}{d\ln R}\;,

(2.9)

where the derivative, for a homogeneous distribution, should vanish since
N(R)\propto R^{3}
. Perfect homogeneity is of course reached only asymptotically, and it is customary (and somewhat arbitrary) to define the homogeneity scale as the one where the correlation dimension crosses the threshold
D_{2}(R_{H})=2.97
, corresponding to
1\%
deviation from homogeneity.

The “bulk in spheres”

Let us now define an analogy of the “count in spheres” for the velocity field. A sensible choice is to compute the average on a sphere of radius
R
of what
[
17
]
refers to as “total velocity”

\mathcal{B}_{R}=\frac{3}{R^{3}}\int_{0}^{R}dr\left(\Psi_{\parallel}(r)+2\Psi_{\perp}(r)\right)r^{2}\;,

(2.10)

which is particularly interesting because of its connection with bulk flow measurements. Indeed, assuming Gaussian density fluctuations, the velocity field is a gaussian random variate with zero mean and variance that can be calculated from the velocity power spectrum
P_{vv}

W(r)=\begin{cases}\frac{3}{4\pi R^{3}}\;\mathrm{if}\;r<R\\
0\qquad\mathrm{if}\;r>R\end{cases}\;,

(2.11)

one obtains

To move forward, let us notice that on linear scales, if the velocity field is sourced by a homogeneous gaussian random density field (generating a scalar only gravitational potential), the parallel and perpendicular components of the velocity correlation functions are not independent
[
17
]

\Psi_{\parallel}(r)=\frac{d}{dr}\left(r\Psi_{\perp}(r)\right)\;.

(2.17)

Simple integration by parts allow us to write

\int_{0}^{R}r^{2}\Psi_{\parallel}(r)=\int_{0}^{R}r^{2}\frac{d}{dr}(r\Psi_{\perp}(r))=R^{3}\Psi_{\perp}(R)-\int_{0}^{R}2r^{2}\Psi_{\perp}(r)\;,

(2.18)

and conclude, by inserting this expression into Eq. (
2.10
), that a suitable expectation is
\bar{\mathcal{B}}_{R}=3\Psi_{\perp}(R)\;
(a bar indicates that this is the total velocity one would obtain
if
the velocity field is irrotational and sourced by a scalar random density field, and may not be equal to what one would measure if the Universe is inhomogeneous, anisotropic or does not obey these assumptions). We can also define an analogous statistic
\mathcal{S}
to the correlation dimension
D_{2}
for
\mathcal{B}_{R}
:

\mathcal{S}(R)=\frac{d}{dR}\left(R\mathcal{B}_{R}\right)\;,

(2.19)

where we included a factor of
R
in the parenthesis because, under the same assumptions as in Eq. (
2.17
), one can show that
\bar{S}=\Psi_{\parallel}
. The identification of the first zero of
\mathcal{S}
with the homogeneity scale has therefore a straightforward physical interpretation: the zero crossing of
\Psi_{\parallel}
signals that no external potential gradient is sourcing coherent flows thorough the sphere (see Fig.
1
for a diagrammatic representation).

Being left with two statistics and a theoretical prediction relating them in a homogeneous and isotropic universe, we are now in the position of defining an homogeneity scale exploiting the different behaviour of the averaged
\Psi_{\perp}(R)
within a sphere of radius
R
. If its radius is smaller than the typical size of linear fluctuations of the density field, the gravitational potential outside the sphere will induce positive correlation between velocities inside, resulting in some degree of collective motion towards an external attractor. On the other hand, when the size of the sphere is large enough for the density fluctuations inside and outside it to be of the same order, the probability that two galaxies move towards a common attractor within the sphere becomes higher. These two regimes correspond to a different evolution of
\mathcal{B}_{R}
at large and small radii. We identify
R_{H}
as the scale at which the slope of the function
\mathcal{B}_{R}
changes. The latter is evident in Fig.

and its derivative
\mathcal{S}

4
Discussion

This paper advocates for a new physically motivated definition of homogeneity scale based on PV measurements. As described in Sec.
3
, the transition to homogeneity is identified with the scale
R_{H}
at which the average parallel components of the peculiar velocities of pairs of galaxies transition from correlated to anti-correlated, corresponding to the zero crossing of
\Psi_{\parallel}(R_{H})=0
.
This definition has a few key advantages: like the correlation dimension
D_{2}
(see Eq.(
2.9
)) used in many previous works and measured from galaxy positions, it does not require the definition of a mean density, which has the additional perk of making it agnostic to the value of
H_{0}
. Unlike
D_{2}
and the mean scaled counts,
\mathcal{N}_{R}
, however, it is insensitive to and hence not degenerate with the tracer’s galaxy bias.

Let us now compare the properties of the homogeneity scale definition given in this work to that evaluated from a
1\%
threshold in the correlation dimension. To distinguish the two, let us indicate the former as
R_{v}
and the latter as
R_{\rho}
. It is well known that
R_{\rho}
evolves with the redshift
[
31
,
4
]
, as one would naively expect from the redshift dependence of the correlation function
\xi(r)
in Eq. (
2.7
). Interestingly enough, however, the zero crossing of
\mathcal{S}(R)
occurs at roughly the same scale at all redshifts. This can be easily understood by looking at Eqs. (
2.17
),(
2.19
) and the theoretical estimator for
\Psi_{\parallel}
in Eq. (
2.3
). Indeed, in the linear regime, redshift evolution can contribute only a global scaling of the amplitude of the power spectrum
P(k)
and the prefactors
H^{2}f^{2}a^{2}
. This overall normalization cannot significantly change the location of the peak in
R\mathcal{B}_{R}
, but only its amplitude, as clearly shown in the left panel of Fig.
2
. We therefore speculate that, once measured and hence calibrated at some redshift, the scale of the zero crossing of
\mathcal{S}(R)
also functions as a standard ruler.

The above considerations suggest that it is hence not possible to find a simple mapping between the two definitions
R_{v}
and
R_{\rho}
. In other words, we cannot just redefine the threshold used for
D_{2}
