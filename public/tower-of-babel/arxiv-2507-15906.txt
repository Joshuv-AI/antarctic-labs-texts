Towards Reliable, Uncertainty-Aware Alignment
Debangshu Banerjee, Kintan Saha, Aditya Gopalan

Abstract
Alignment of large language models (LLMs) typically involves training a reward model on preference data, followed by policy optimization with respect to the reward model. However, optimizing policies with respect to a single reward model estimate can render it vulnerable to inaccuracies in the reward model. We empirically study the variability of reward model training on open-source benchmarks. We observe that independently trained reward models on the same preference dataset can exhibit substantial disagreement, highlighting the instability of current alignment strategies. Employing a theoretical model, we demonstrate that variability in reward model estimation can cause overfitting, leading to the risk of performance degradation. To mitigate this risk, we propose a variance-aware policy optimization framework for preference-based alignment. The key ingredient of the framework is a new policy regularizer that incorporates reward model variance estimates. We show that variance-aware policy optimization provably reduces the risk of outputting a worse policy than the default. Experiments across diverse LLM and reward model configurations confirm that our approach yields more stable and robust alignment than the standard (variance-unaware) pipeline.

Challenges of Reward Model Reliability

Reward models in RLHF can be inherently noisy, posing challenges for robust alignment. As our experiments show, ten independently-trained, identically configured reward models assign varying scores to the same prompt-response pair (Figure
1
). This variability stems from three key factors. First, reward models are trained on far less data, typically hundreds of thousands of preference pairs, than LLMs, limiting generalization and introducing statistical noise. Second, stochastic optimization techniques, such as mini-batch gradient descent with few training epochs
(
Stiennon et al. 2020
;
Meta 2024
)
, further increase randomness across trained instances. Lastly, in the case where absolute reward scores, instead of relative preferences, are directly elicited from a population (of humans or AI agents) for downstream usage in reward model training or policy optimization, score variability naturally arises due to diversity of opinions and biases. These factors imply that reward models are not reliable oracles, and overfitting to their noisy outputs may lead to alignment with spurious artifacts rather than true human intent.

Contributions

This work makes three primary contributions. First, we empirically demonstrate inconsistencies across identically configured reward models on open-source datasets, revealing inherent uncertainty in reward modeling (Appendix
B
). Second, we introduce a mathematical model of uncertain rewards and propose a conservative, variance-aware policy optimization framework that provably reduces risk under uncertainty (Section
2
). Finally, we present theoretical guarantees and numerical evidence showing that our approach mitigates policy overfitting and stabilizes reward outcomes (Section
3
).

Notations:

\mathcal{Y}

. A reward model assigns a scalar value to each prompt–response pair, i.e.,
\widehat{R}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
. We model a large language model (LLM) as a policy
\pi
that defines a probability distribution over responses given a prompt, i.e.,
\pi(\cdot\mid x)\in\mathcal{P}({\mathcal{Y}})
. We denote by
\rho_{\mathcal{X}}
the distribution over prompts. With a slight abuse of notation, we treat the policy
\pi
as an induced joint distribution over
(x,y)

\widehat{R}^{\top}\pi

d

\Sigma

\|d\|_{\Sigma}:=\sqrt{d^{\top}\Sigma d}
, where
\Sigma
is a positive semi-definite matrix.

Assumption 2.1

(
Noisy Reward Model
)
.

We assume that
\widehat{R}
is a noisy observation of the true reward function
r^{*}
. For any
(x,y)
, the observed reward
\widehat{R}(x,y)
is modeled as a Gaussian perturbation of
r^{*}(x,y)
:

\widehat{R}(x,y)=r^{*}(x,y)+\mathcal{N}\big(0,\sigma^{2}(x,y)\big),

where
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}(x,y))
is a Gaussian random variable with mean zero and variance
\sigma^{2}(x,y)
. The values
\widehat{R}(x,y)
are assumed to be independent across different
(x,y)
.

This implies that
\widehat{R}\sim\mathcal{N}(r^{*},\Sigma)
, where
\Sigma
is a diagonal matrix with entries
\sigma^{2}(x,y)
. Let
\pi_{0}
be a reference policy (e.g., from pretraining), and define
d=\pi-\pi_{0}
as the pointwise difference between the current and reference policies. Then, the inner product
\widehat{R}^{\top}d
is normally distributed with mean
r^{*\top}d
and variance
d^{\top}\Sigma d
.

Lower Bound on the True Objective Function:

The following theorem provides a high-probability lower bound on the optimization objective that incorporates the uncertainty in the reward estimates. The proof is provided in Appendix
A
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Under the reward observation model in Assumption
2.1
, for any
\beta>0
, the following inequality holds with probability at least
1-\exp\left(-\frac{\left|\mathcal{X}\right|\left|\mathcal{Y}\right|}{\beta^{2}}\right)
:

\sup_{d\in\left[-2,2\right]^{\left|\mathcal{X}\right|\left|\mathcal{Y}\right|}}\left(\widehat{R}^{\top}d-\beta\|d\|_{\Sigma}\right)\leqslant\sup_{d\in\left[-2,2\right]^{\left|\mathcal{X}\right|\left|\mathcal{Y}\right|}}r^{*\top}d.

The above result implies that the optimization problem on the left-hand side serves as a high-probability lower bound for the true reward maximization problem, which depends on the unobservable function
r^{*}
. Since only noisy estimates
r^{*}
are available through
\widehat{R}
, we propose the following surrogate optimization objective:

\displaystyle\sup_{d\in\left[-2,2\right]^{\left|\mathcal{X}\right|\left|\mathcal{Y}\right|}}\widehat{R}^{\top}d-\beta\|d\|_{\Sigma}.

(1)

This leads to the following constrained optimization problem:

\max_{\pi}\widehat{R}^{\top}\pi\quad\text{subject to}\quad(\pi-\pi_{0})^{\top}\Sigma(\pi-\pi_{0})\leqslant\varepsilon,

for some
\varepsilon>0
. The weighted quadratic constraint penalizes deviations from the reference policy more heavily for prompt–response pairs with higher uncertainty in the reward model, thereby incorporating variance-awareness into the optimization.

Remark 2.3
.

Variants of the constrained optimization problem above have appeared in prior literature. For example, the unconstrained version reduces to the vanilla policy gradient method
(
Sutton et al. 1999
)
. In RLHF, the PPO algorithm
(
Schulman et al. 2017
)
imposes a KL-divergence constraint to regularize updates, though the choice of distance metric is flexible
(
Ziegler et al. 2019
)
. An
\ell_{2}
approximation to the KL constraint leads to an unweighted constraint of the form
\|\pi-\pi_{0}\|_{2}^{2}\leqslant\varepsilon
. Similarly, the Trust Region Policy Optimization (TRPO) algorithm
(
Schulman 2015
)
uses a Fisher-weighted trust region constraint derived from the natural policy gradient.

Theoretical Analysis:

We compare the performance of the variance-aware LLM alignment methodology with its variance-unaware counterpart to evaluate how incorporating reward estimate uncertainty affects policy robustness and effectiveness—particularly in scenarios with noisy reward signals. We consider two policies,
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
, derived from different constrained optimization formulations.

Let
\pi_{1}
denote the “vanilla” policy obtained by solving the unweighted
\ell_{2}
-constrained problem:

\pi_{1}=\arg\max_{\pi}\,\pi^{\top}\widehat{R}\quad\text{subject to}\quad\|\pi-\pi_{0}\|_{2}^{2}\leqslant\varepsilon.

(2)

Let
\pi_{2}
denote a variance-aware policy that is obtained by solving the covariance-weighted
\ell_{2}
-constrained problem:

\pi_{2}=\arg\max_{\pi}\,\pi^{\top}\widehat{R}\quad\text{subject to}\quad\|\pi-\pi_{0}\|_{\Sigma}^{2}\leqslant\tilde{\varepsilon}.

(3)

To ensure a fair comparison between the policies resulting from the two policy optimization formulations, we set
\tilde{\varepsilon}=\lambda_{\min}(\Sigma)\cdot\varepsilon
. This scaling ensures that the largest axis of the ellipsoid in the variance-aware constraint (
3
) is aligned with the radius of the
\ell_{2}
ball in the vanilla constraint (
2
). We evaluate the expected true rewards
\pi^{\top}r^{*}
for both methods and compare them to the reference policy reward
\pi_{0}^{\top}r^{*}
. We show that the variance-aware policy is less likely to underperform relative to the reference policy than the vanilla policy, thereby demonstrating its robustness under uncertainty.

Theorem 2.4
.

Consider the vanilla policy
\pi_{1}
and the variance-aware policy
\pi_{2}
as defined in Equations (
2
) and (
3
), respectively. Let
\tilde{\varepsilon}=\lambda_{\min}(\Sigma)\cdot\varepsilon
so that the feasible set of the variance-aware optimization is no larger than that of the variance-unaware one. Then, the following inequality holds:

\mathbb{P}\left(\pi_{2}^{\top}r^{*}\leqslant\pi_{0}^{\top}r^{*}\right)\leqslant\mathbb{P}\left(\pi_{1}^{\top}r^{*}\leqslant\pi_{0}^{\top}r^{*}\right).

Remark 2.5
.

Theorem
2.4
highlights a risk–reward trade-off. While the vanilla policy
\pi_{1}
may yield higher rewards when
\widehat{R}
closely approximates
r^{*}
, it is riskier because it disregards the uncertainty in reward estimates. By contrast, the variance-aware policy
\pi_{2}
explicitly accounts for variance and reduces the probability of underperforming relative to the reference policy
\pi_{0}
. The proof is provided in Appendix
A
.

Remark 2.6
.

The variance-aware policy is closely related to the notion of the reward-to-variability ratio, known in finance as the Sharpe Ratio
(
Sharpe 1966
)
, which balances expected return against risk.

Theorem 2.7
.

Consider the following constrained optimization problem:

\max_{\pi}\;\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\rho_{\mathcal{X}},\,y\sim\pi(\cdot|x)}\left[\widehat{R}(x,y)\right]\quad\text{subject to}\quad\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\rho_{\mathcal{X}},\,y\sim\pi(\cdot|x)}\left[\sigma^{2}(x,y)\ln\frac{\pi(y|x)}{\pi_{0}(y|x)}\right]\leqslant\varepsilon,

where
\widehat{R}(x,y)
and
\sigma^{2}(x,y)
are the estimated reward and its variance. The optimal policy has the form:

\pi^{*}(y|x)\propto\pi_{0}(y|x)\exp\left(\frac{\widehat{R}(x,y)}{\beta\sigma^{2}(x,y)}\right),

for some
\beta>0
. Thus, the optimal policy assigns higher probability to actions with a high reward-to-variance ratio, analogous to the Sharpe Ratio in portfolio theory.

Appendix B
Variability in Reward Models

B.1
Ensemble Reward Models: Finetuning and Observations

In this section, we describe the process of reward modeling using the
Gemma-2b-it
model
(
Team et al. 2024b
)
, an instruction-tuned variant of the foundational
Gemma-2b
. Our methodology leverages an ensemble of 10 independently trained reward models to estimate the variance in rewards assigned to identical prompt–response pairs. This ensemble-based approach enables a more principled estimation of uncertainty in reward predictions and facilitates the analysis of model variability, even among models trained on the same data.

The sections that follow detail our methodology for training the ensemble of reward models, and key observations drawn from the ensemble’s performance on multiple benchmark tasks.

Dataset

To train our reward models, we use an open-source preference dataset introduced by
Dong et al. 2024
, available on HuggingFace
3
3

3

huggingface.co/weqweasdas/preference_dataset_mix2
. This curated dataset comprises approximately 50,000 labeled preference pairs and is constructed by merging several widely-used open datasets. The constituent datasets include
HH-RLHF

(
Bai et al. 2022a
)
,
SHP

(
Ethayarajh et al. 2022
)
,
HelpSteer

(
Wang et al. 2023
)
,
PKU-SafeRLHF

(
Ji et al. 2024
)
,
UltraFeedback

(
Cui et al. 2023
)
,
UltraInteract

(
Yuan et al. 2024
)
,
Distilabel-Capybara

(
Daniele 2023
)
, and
Distilabel-Orca3

(
Lian et al. 2023
)
.

To ensure data quality, the combined dataset is preprocessed to remove approximately 10% of low-quality samples. The resulting dataset contains prompt–response preference pairs, where each prompt is associated with one preferred and one rejected response. These pairwise labels serve as the ground truth for training the ensemble reward models.

The diversity and breadth of the included datasets make this collection well-suited for training robust reward models that generalize across a range of domains and tasks. For further details on dataset construction and preprocessing, we refer the reader to
Dong et al. 2024
.

Methodology

We use the
Gemma-2b-it
model
(
Gemma 2024
)
as the backbone for our reward models. As an instruction-tuned variant of the foundational
Gemma-2b
, it is well-suited for reward modeling tasks due to its alignment with human instructions. The full model, including a scalar reward head, occupies approximately 9.34 GB on disk. Since we employ an ensemble of 10 independently parameterized reward models, the total storage requirement scales to roughly 90 GB.

To accelerate training and improve memory efficiency, we adopt the following methodology:

•

Initial Training:
We begin by training a single instance of the full
Gemma-2b-it
model with a scalar reward head—a linear layer of size
2048\times 1
—on the preference dataset. We apply early stopping once the loss reaches
0.3
to balance model capacity and mitigate overfitting.

•

Parallel Reward Heads:
After partial training, we attach nine additional reward heads in parallel to the original, following the setup of
Zhang et al. 2024b
. Each head has identical dimensions (
2048\times 1
), resulting in a 10-dimensional output vector in a single forward pass—one scalar reward per ensemble member.

•

Freezing the Foundation Model:
To reduce computational costs, we freeze the pre-trained base of
Gemma-2b-it
and train only the reward heads. This setup simulates training 10 independent reward models in parallel while sharing the same foundation. We employ an additive loss function:

\text{loss}=\sum_{i=1}^{10}\ell(\theta_{i}),

where each
\theta_{i}
denotes the parameters of the
i
-th reward head. This ensures each head is trained independently, while retaining efficiency.

By freezing the foundational layers and isolating training to the reward heads, we significantly reduce both computational and storage overhead. The final ensemble model remains compact, occupying approximately 9.34 GB on disk, with only 20,480 trainable parameters across all reward heads.

Methodology

We fine-tune
GPT-2

Radford et al. 2019
,
Qwen2.5-0.5B

Yang et al. 2024
, and
Mistral-7B

Jiang et al. 2023
using uncertainty-aware reward modeling. Specifically,
GPT-2
is paired with a custom scalar reward model as described in Appendix
B
, while
Qwen2.5-0.5B
and
Mistral-7B
are aligned using
Prompted
reward models constructed from
Gemini-1.5-flash
,
Gemini-2.0-flash
, and
Deepseek-V3
.

We introduce a variance-aware fine-tuning framework that explicitly incorporates uncertainty estimates from the reward model into the policy optimization process. Our approach is compared against the standard PPO baseline across two classes of reward uncertainty:

•

Prompted
: The reward model is queried via a structured natural language prompt (Appendix
C
), returning an interval-valued reward
[a,b]
for a given prompt-response pair. The reward is assumed to be uniformly distributed over this interval.

•

Ensemble
: The reward model consists of an ensemble of independently trained scalar-valued reward heads as described in Appendix
B
. For each input, the ensemble returns a list of scalar rewards
\{r_{1},\dots,r_{10}\}
. The sample mean and sample variance are used as the reward estimate and uncertainty measure, respectively.

For each reward model configuration, we fine-tune a total of
N=80
policies using two methods:

•

Vanilla PPO
: Optimizes the policy using sampled scalar rewards without accounting for reward variance.

•

Variance-Aware PPO
: Incorporates reward variance into the PPO objective to regularize updates more cautiously under uncertainty.

The corresponding optimization objectives are:

\mathcal{L}_{\textsc{VA}}(\theta)=\sum_{x,y}\left[\widehat{r}(x,y)-\sigma^{2}(x,y)\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}{\pi_{0}(y|x)}\right]

(5)

\mathcal{L}_{\textsc{Vanilla}}(\theta)=\sum_{x,y}\left[\widehat{r}(x,y)-\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}{\pi_{0}(y|x)}\right]

(6)

and proceeds as follows:

\{r_{1},\dots,r_{10}\}

Limitations

While our variance‐aware RLHF framework addresses an important gap in existing alignment methods, several limitations merit discussion:

Lack of a Universal Definition of ‘Alignment’

While this work is notionally situated in the area of methods for alignment of AI models, we must bear in mind that the objective of ‘aligning’ AI models to ‘human values’ lacks a formal and concrete definition. The diversity of human preferences, judgements, biases, intuition and aspirations may very well render the search for a satisfactory definition of alignment impossible. We work only within the narrow technical framework of alignment for AI models that is in force today, based on reducing the measurement of alignment to ideal scalar reward scores, and maximizing these rewards in the hope that they result in well-aligned systems.

Quality of Variance Estimates

Our approach relies on reasonably accurate per–prompt–response variance estimates from the reward model. In the “prompted” setting we assume a uniform distribution over the returned interval, and in the “ensemble” setting we use the empirical sample variance. If these estimates are poorly calibrated (e.g. heavy‐tailed errors, systematic bias across prompts), the variance penalty may under‐ or over‐correct, potentially harming performance. Integrating more sophisticated uncertainty quantification (e.g. Bayesian ensembling, temperature‐scaled intervals) is left to future work.

Computational Overhead

Training with an ensemble of reward heads (or multiple forward passes) incurs nontrivial compute and memory costs relative to single‐model RLHF. Although we mitigate this via shared backbones and parallel heads, scaling to very large reward models (e.g. 30B+ parameters) or budget‐constrained settings may be challenging. Exploring low‐cost approximations of the variance penalty (e.g. moment‐matching or distilled variance estimators) is an important direction.

Task and Domain Generality

Our experiments focus on synthetic interval‐based rewards and open‐source preference datasets (e.g. RewardBenchmark’s Chat and Safety tasks). It remains to be seen how variance‐aware fine‐tuning performs on large‐scale, proprietary RLHF setups (e.g. summarization, code generation, or multi‐turn dialogue) and in settings where human feedback is collected online.

Risk Metric Scope

We define “risk” as the probability of underperforming a reference policy under the true reward model. Other risk measures—such as Conditional Value at Risk (CVaR), worst‐case regret, or percentile‐based bounds—may capture different aspects of policy robustness. Extending our theoretical framework to these alternative metrics could broaden applicability.

Hyperparameter Sensitivity

The strength of the variance penalty (
\beta
in Equation (
5
)) must be chosen carefully to balance exploration and conservatism. While we align
\beta
with a target KL divergence in our experiments, more automated schemes for selecting this hyperparameter (e.g. via validation‐set variance calibration) could improve ease of use.

In summary, our work highlights the importance of accounting for reward‐model uncertainty in RLHF, but practical deployment will require addressing variance‐estimation quality, computational cost, and evaluation on a broader range of tasks and risk criteria.
