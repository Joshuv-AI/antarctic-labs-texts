Nanozymes for Bone Regeneration: Mechanistic Insights into Immune and Metabolic Microenvironment Modulation
Authors: Yue Wang, Meiyuan Song, Shumeng Chen, Yang He, Shuxuan Chen, Zhe Li, Miao Xu, Qiang Zhang, Yunfeng Rui, Jianlin Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNanozymes, a class of nanomaterials with intrinsic enzyme-mimetic properties, have emerged as promising therapeutic platforms for bone regeneration due to their low cost, high stability, and multifunctionality. This review provides mechanistic insights and translational perspectives on how nanozymes act as microenvironmental “remodelers” to enhance bone repair. Representative systems—including cerium oxide (CeO2), manganese-based (Mn3O4/MnO2), and Prussian blue (PBNPs)—exert therapeutic effects by scavenging ROS, promoting M2 macrophage polarization, restoring mitochondrial function, suppressing the NLRP3/NF-κB signaling axis, and clearing deleterious metabolites such as lactate. We systematically discuss immune regulation, metabolic intervention, and their synergistic crosstalk, highlighting their capacity for spatiotemporal coordination of angiogenesis and antimicrobial defense. Finally, we outline future directions, including enhancing catalytic specificity, improving in vivo stability, addressing biosafety and pharmacokinetics, developing intelligent nanozyme designs, and establishing clinically relevant evaluation models to accelerate translation into effective bone therapies.Keywords: nanozymes, bone regeneration, immunity, metabolism, microenvironment

Introduction

Osteoporosis, bone tumors, and other bone diseases affect millions of individuals worldwide. The resulting bone defects, infections, and tissue damage severely impair quality of life and pose major challenges for bone repair and regenerative therapy.1,2 Bone regeneration is the process of reconstructing and restoring the normal structure and function of skeletal tissue,3 and is highly dependent on the local “immune–metabolic microenvironment”—a dynamic and spatially organized niche composed of immune cells, bone-lineage cells, vascular cells, inflammatory mediators, metabolites, redox signals, and extracellular matrix (ECM) cues. Within this niche, immune responses and metabolic reprogramming are reciprocally coupled: immune cells, particularly macrophages, regulate osteogenesis, osteoclastogenesis, angiogenesis, and matrix remodeling through cytokine secretion and phenotype switching, whereas metabolic states such as glycolysis, oxidative phosphorylation, lipid metabolism, and mitochondrial function in turn determine immune-cell activation and regenerative outcomes. Therefore, the homeostasis of this microenvironment is maintained through coordinated crosstalk among M1-like/M2-like macrophage polarization, redox balance, energy metabolism, and bone-cell activity.4,5 Numerous bone disorders are associated with chronic inflammation and oxidative stress, which can disrupt this homeostatic balance and lead to pathological alterations including metabolic dysregulation and cellular dysfunction. If these issues are not effectively addressed, bone regeneration may be impaired.6,7

Current therapeutic strategies for bone diseases range from systemic pharmacological interventions to localized surgical reconstruction. For osteosarcoma, neoadjuvant chemotherapy combined with wide surgical resection remains a major clinical strategy; however, postoperative management still faces challenges related to residual tumor recurrence and reconstruction of large bone defects.8,9 For osteoporotic conditions, systemic anti-resorptive agents such as bisphosphonates are widely used to reduce fracture risk and improve bone strength, yet they have limited capacity to directly reverse established local bone defects or restore the impaired regenerative microenvironment.10 In structural reconstruction, autologous bone grafting remains a widely adopted approach because of its osteogenic, osteoinductive, and osteoconductive properties. Advances in tissue engineering have further enabled personalized repair strategies, including anatomically matched autologous cartilage-bone constructs for temporomandibular joint regeneration and tissue-engineered autologous grafts for facial bone reconstruction.11,12 In parallel, exosome-based chitosan scaffolds, stochastic and gyroid scaffolds for osseointegration and bone regeneration, and functionally graded scaffolds for tendon-bone interface repair have been developed to address specific bone repair scenarios.13–15

Despite these advances, the routine clinical translation of current strategies remains constrained by several persistent limitations. Microfracture, while technically simple, is mainly suitable for superficial bone defects and fails to adequately address complex or deep bone injuries. Autologous bone grafts provide excellent osteogenic activity, but their application is limited by donor-site morbidity, limited graft availability, and the risk of complications.16 Moreover, existing hydrogel scaffold repair materials face restrictions in clinical application due to slow curing, insufficient mechanical strength, residual toxicity, and potential damage to healthy tissues.17 More importantly, most current strategies fail to fully restore the diseased bone microenvironment marked by oxidative stress, inflammation, metabolic imbalance, infection, and impaired angiogenesis.

Nanozymes are nanomaterials with enzyme-mimetic catalytic activities. They can mimic the catalytic functions of natural enzymes, such as catalase (CAT) and superoxide dismutase (SOD).18 Specifically, nanozymes are nanomaterials that exhibit intrinsic enzyme-like catalytic properties, which are governed by their surface electronic structure and thermodynamic characteristics. At the physicochemical level, this mimicry arises from specific surface atomic arrangements and redox potentials, which lower activation energy barriers and facilitate efficient catalytic reactions.19 This surface-driven mechanism not only defines their enzymatic function but also underpins their exceptional structural stability. Unlike natural enzymes, whose catalytic efficacy relies on precise three-dimensional folding and can be easily lost through denaturation in acidic (pH < 5) or hyperthermic environments typical of bone defect regions, enzyme-mimetic metal and metal oxide nanozymes (eg, Co3O4, Fe3O4, Cu(OH)2) exhibit remarkable structural robustness.20–22 Standardized characterization protocols have confirmed that these nanozymes maintain catalytic integrity across a broad pH range (3–12) and at temperatures up to 90°C.23 Such environmental tolerance is crucial for scavenging the abundant reactive oxygen species (ROS) generated by osteoclasts and infiltrating neutrophils at sites of bone injury, whereas natural enzymes undergo rapid inactivation under these conditions.24

Nanozymes have consequently emerged as a promising frontier in bone regenerative medicine, although their orthopedic application remains in the early translational stage, supported primarily by preclinical models.25 Notably, the potential of nanozymes to intervene in oxidative stress, chronic inflammation, and mitochondrial dysfunction has been identified as a significant advancement, offering a potential solution to the limitations often associated with conventional monomodal therapies.26,27 Numerous studies have shown that nanozymes can provide enhanced therapeutic strategies for bone injury repair by participating in immune regulation, correcting metabolic disorders, and reshaping the local microenvironment.28,29 However, reported benefits should be interpreted alongside unresolved limitations, including activity loss in complex physiological environments, possible ion release or long-term retention, and incomplete pharmacokinetic and biosafety evidence.

Several recent reviews have summarized the applications of nanozymes in bone regeneration and repair; however, most of them have primarily focused on ROS regulation, specific catalytic activities, material platforms, or particular nanozyme systems (Table 1). In contrast, the present review adopts an immune-metabolic microenvironment-oriented perspective. Rather than simply categorizing nanozymes according to material composition or enzymatic activity, we systematically analyze how nanozymes regulate immune inflammation, restore metabolic homeostasis, promote angiogenesis and osteogenesis, and improve antibacterial defense during bone repair. In particular, we emphasize the mechanistic crosstalk among oxidative stress, inflammatory cytokines, metabolic dysregulation, impaired vascularization, infection, and bone regeneration. Therefore, this review aims to provide mechanistic insights and practical guidance for the rational design of nanozyme-based therapeutic strategies for various bone diseases, as outlined in Figure 1.

Immune-Metabolic Microenvironment Imbalance: Pathological Basis and Intervention Targets for Bone Diseases

The immune and metabolic activities of the body are interconnected through the microenvironment at the site of injury, and this interaction jointly influences the occurrence and progression of bone diseases. During the early stages of various bone diseases, locally accumulated ROS can activate pathways such as Rac1/NF-κB, initiating an inflammatory cascade.35 Excessive ROS serve as critical signals that activate macrophages, promoting their polarization to the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype. Activated M1 macrophages subsequently release substantial amounts of ROS and inflammatory cytokines, such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β), forming a positive feedback loop that amplifies the inflammatory response.36 Furthermore, studies have demonstrated that the inflammatory factors released by M1 macrophages impair the normal physiological functions of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs), inhibiting their osteogenic differentiation and hindering the process of bone mineralization.37 If ROS are not cleared in a timely manner by the antioxidant system (eg, SOD and CAT), they can activate the NLRP3 inflammasome within cells.38 Upon assembly, this inflammasome activates caspase-1, which cleaves gasdermin D (GSDMD) to form pores in the cell membrane, inducing cell death, and releases mature inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-1β.39 Therefore, in inflammatory bone diseases, the redox balance in chondrocytes is often disrupted, resulting in mitochondrial dysfunction and mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) leakage. This process activates innate immune pathways, such as the cGAS-STING-NF-κB pathway, which inhibits the expression of protective ECM genes and promotes the production of matrix-degrading enzymes, leading to direct damage to the ECM.40

Inflammation induced by ROS not only disrupts the function of bone cells and the ECM but, in severe cases, also interferes with the energy metabolism homeostasis of cells. Studies indicate that local accumulation of ROS and persistent inflammatory signals can promote the degradation of glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1)—which has osteogenic protective effects—by upregulating the secretion of dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4) by osteoclasts.41 This degradation leads to increased blood glucose levels and exacerbates energy supply issues in bone tissue. The resulting glucose metabolic disorder is often intertwined with systemic metabolic imbalances. For instance, insulin resistance can result in the abnormal secretion of adipokines, characterized by elevated leptin levels and decreased adiponectin levels. These factors not only regulate energy metabolism but also act as key mediators in the interaction between adipose tissue and bone. By affecting the RANKL/OPG signaling pathway and other related pathways, these adipokines directly regulate the dynamic balance between bone resorption and formation, ultimately inhibiting bone regeneration.42,43 More importantly, disorders in glycolipid metabolism tend to exacerbate oxidative stress and chronic low-grade inflammation. Such inflammatory signals further impair insulin sensitivity and disrupt adipokine homeostasis, leading to progressive bone loss, degradation of bone microstructure, and disorders in bone regeneration. Collectively, these processes promote the occurrence and progression of bone metabolic diseases, such as diabetic osteoporosis.44

Additionally, ROS accumulation induced by inflammation and metabolic disorders can cause oxidative stress damage—including lipid peroxidation, protein denaturation, and DNA damage—in vascular endothelial cells. The accumulation of ROS downregulates the expression of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and other key pro-angiogenic factors. Simultaneously, continuously activated pro-inflammatory macrophages can directly inhibit the proliferation, migration, and lumen formation of endothelial cells through signaling pathways involving Sema4D and TNF.45,46 Moreover, studies indicate that epidermal growth factor-like 6 (EGFL6) derived from osteoblasts plays a critical coupling role in the bone repair process. A persistent or excessive inflammatory microenvironment may lead to the loss of EGFL6 expression from osteoblasts by inhibiting their differentiation and function, thereby weakening the activation of the BMP-Smad/MAPK pathway and resulting in the decoupling of angiogenesis and osteogenesis. Consequently, a reduction in the abundance of type H blood vessels and Runx2-positive osteoblasts is observed in the bone defect area.47 More critically, during the pathological progression of osteoporotic bone defects, ROS and inflammatory factors can also impair nerve regeneration and axon growth, leading to decreased secretion of calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) and other neuropeptides. As a neural signaling molecule, CGRP plays a crucial role in promoting migration of vascular endothelial cells and lumen formation during normal bone healing. Therefore, when sensory nerve function is compromised and neuropeptide secretion is insufficient, angiogenesis is severely affected.48 Such angiogenic disorders have been shown to directly lead to local microcirculation failure, creating a pathological microenvironment characterized by hypoxia and nutritional deficiency. Such conditions render BMSCs and endothelial progenitor cells (EPCs) unable to effectively recruit, survive, and function.49 Additionally, degradation of the vascular system interrupts critical signaling of the “endothelial-osteogenic axis,” preventing endothelial cells from activating the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs via paracrine mechanisms (eg, Notch, VEGFA, BMP2 signals).50 Ultimately, this exacerbates the imbalance of bone metabolism under pathological conditions such as osteoporosis, leading to weakened biomineralization, sparse separation of new bone trabeculae, and delayed bone regeneration.

Moreover, the inflammatory cascade and changes in the immune microenvironment induced by bacterial infections can exacerbate the destruction of the bone microenvironment in tumor bone metastasis and directly interfere with bone regeneration and healing.51 When the balance of the “immune-metabolic microenvironment” in the body is disrupted by bacterial infections, the pathological process can sharply deteriorate.52 Studies show that bacteria can directly activate and regulate immune cell functions. For example, Escherichia coli (E. coli) can activate neutrophils to release neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) and can promote bone resorption by upregulating osteoclast-related genes such as CSF1R, ACP5, and CTSK. This severely hinders bone regeneration and tissue repair.53 Simultaneously, bacteria can induce macrophages to hyperpolarize to the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype, leading to the release of large amounts of ROS, which further disrupt the bone immune microenvironment. Additionally, toxins produced by pathogens, including TSST-1 and PSMS from Staphylococcus aureus, activate osteoclasts, exacerbating bone resorption and inhibiting the differentiation and function of osteoblasts. This not only results in insufficient bone formation and delayed healing but also potentially leads to osteolysis.54 These processes are often accompanied by a continuous inflammatory response, with numerous inflammatory factors released (eg, TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6) that inhibit osteoblast function and enhance osteoclast activity, breaking the physiological balance of bone remodeling.55 Furthermore, the impact of bacterial infection extends to the level of stem cells. Pathogens can increase the expression of ACSL4 by activating the Toll-like receptor signaling pathway in BMSCs, subsequently upregulating the transcription factor IRF7 and promoting its phosphorylation. These changes drive ferroptosis in BMSCs, characterized by lipid peroxide accumulation, ferrous iron overload, and mitochondrial damage, ultimately leading to the loss of stem cells with osteogenic differentiation potential and reducing the cellular reservoir necessary for repair.56 To resist immune clearance and antibiotics, bacteria often adhere to the surfaces of implants or necrotic tissues and secrete an ECM composed of proteins and polysaccharides that encapsulates them, thereby forming a complex biofilm. This biofilm not only enhances bacterial resistance to environmental and chemical stressors but also fosters chronic persistent infections, further damaging the bone immune microenvironment and hindering the healing of infectious bone defects.57

In conclusion, both endogenous metabolic disorders and exogenous infections lead to a vicious cycle characterized by overactivation of immune responses, disturbances in energy metabolism, and inhibition of cellular function. This cycle undermines the cellular foundation required for bone regeneration and disrupts the supporting microenvironment essential for maintaining the regeneration process. Consequently, it serves as a common pathological basis for various bone diseases. This understanding offers a new perspective for a more in-depth exploration of the pathogenesis of bone diseases and suggests potential directions for developing targeted intervention strategies.

Modulating Immune Inflammation: From ROS Scavenging to Reprogramming Macrophage Phenotypes

In the early stages of bone injury, the local microenvironment typically exhibits an inflammatory state. A substantial number of pro-inflammatory factors, such as TNF-α and IL-1β, along with ROS secreted by cells, lead to decreased osteoblast activity and promote bone resorption, thereby hindering bone regeneration.69,70 Recent studies have demonstrated that nanozymes can interfere with this process at molecular, cellular, and signaling network levels.

Mechanistically, nanozymes interfere with the inflammatory cascade at molecular, cellular, and signaling network levels. ROS are not only cytotoxic but also serve as second messengers that drive excessive activation of immune cells.71 With the in-depth exploration of nanozymes, researchers have uncovered various methods for delivering these agents to immune cells. At the molecular level, nanozymes can mimic the activities of natural antioxidant enzymes, such as SOD and CAT, effectively eliminating intracellular ROS and inhibiting ROS-mediated activation of pro-inflammatory pathways.72,73 For example, ROS-responsive niobium carbide (Nb2C) MX enzyme nano-coatings, designed by Li et al can target macrophages at lesion sites through surface-modified phosphatidylserine in the ROS-rich osteolytic microenvironment. With the aid of polyethyleneimine, they can escape lysosomal degradation and enter the cytoplasm to exhibit various antioxidant enzyme activities, including SOD, CAT, and glutathione peroxidase (GPX). This action effectively removes excess ROS, blocks ROS-activated NF-κB/MAPK signaling pathways, and inhibits osteoclast differentiation and bone resorption activity.74 Another ROS-responsive composite micelle system based on ceria nanozymes (HA-PMs@Ce) enables targeted drug delivery in highly oxidative microenvironments through ROS-sensitive borate bonds. It also enhances accumulation at inflammatory sites by utilizing HA-coated CD44 targeting. The released CeOx nanozymes mimic SOD and CAT activities via Ce3+/Ce4+ redox cycles, converting ·O2− and H2O2 into harmless O2 and H2O. This process promotes macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype, downregulates receptor activator of nuclear factor kappa-Β ligand (RANKL) expression, inhibits osteoclast activity, and reduces bone resorption.75

In addition to activating inflammatory pathways, excessive ROS also activate the NOD-like receptor family pyrin domain-containing 3 (NLRP3). Once activated, NLRP3 catalyzes the release of IL-1β as a signaling molecule, which in turn stimulates immune cells to produce elevated levels of RANKL. This further exacerbates bone resorption under the synergistic influence of inflammatory factors, ultimately leading to continuous bone mass loss.76 To address this issue, a recent study on a metal-organic framework (MOF)-based nanozyme, specifically ZIF-8 encapsulated molybdenum (ZIF@Mo-TA), found that this nanozyme inhibits the activity of NADPH oxidase (NOX) by releasing Zn2+, thereby reducing intracellular ROS production and inhibiting the activation of ROS-dependent NLRP3 inflammasomes.77 Similarly, defect-type RuO2 nanospheres (d-RuO2) have been shown to decrease ROS levels and inhibit NLRP3 oligomerization and inflammasome assembly through their enzyme-like antioxidant activity. This process effectively blocks the activation of downstream caspase-1, reduces maturation and release of pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β and IL-18, and ultimately alleviates inflammatory bone erosion.78

Building upon the effective control of inflammation initiation, nanozymes can further target key signaling networks within cells, regulating critical pathways such as nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), which drive chronic inflammation and osteoclast differentiation.79 Ye et al highlighted in their research on Prussian blue nanozymes (HPBZS) that these nanozymes significantly inhibit osteoclast differentiation. Their findings demonstrated that HPBZS reduced the phosphorylation levels of IκBα and p65 by scavenging ROS, while also inhibiting the phosphorylation of ERK, JNK, and p38. The inactivation of these key signaling molecules blocked downstream signaling through the NF-κB and MAPK pathways, subsequently downregulating the expression of osteoclast-specific transcription factors c-Fos and NFATc1, which inhibited osteoclast differentiation and bone resorption.80 Other studies have shown that zinc cerium layered double hydroxide (ZnCe LDO) nanozymes can regulate macrophage polarization by simultaneously inhibiting the activation of NF-κB and MAPK signaling pathways. The results indicated that these nanozymes significantly reduced the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α and IL-1β, while increasing the secretion levels of Arg-1, IL-10, and other repair-related factors. This modulation facilitates the transformation of macrophages from the M1 phenotype to the M2 phenotype, thereby establishing an anti-inflammatory microenvironment conducive to bone repair.81

Additionally, certain nanozymes exhibit the potential to regulate complex inflammatory signaling pathways. For instance, layered double hydroxide (LDH) nanozymes possess SOD activity that effectively clears ROS and alleviates oxidative stress. Moreover, LDH shows a strong binding affinity for double-stranded DNA (dsDNA), primarily relying on van der Waals forces to adsorb cell-free DNA (cfDNA), thereby blocking the activation of the cGAS-STING pathway. The data indicated that LDH and its complex loaded with the STING inhibitor C176 (LDH/C) significantly inhibited the phosphorylation of downstream tank-binding kinase 1 (TBK1) and interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3) (p-TBK1 and p-IRF3), which suppressed the expression of type I interferon, TNF-α, IL-6, and other key inflammatory factors. Simultaneously, LDH/C inhibited the nuclear translocation of NF-κB, further reducing the inflammatory response and promoting bone regeneration and homeostasis recovery.82

Furthermore, nanozymes can reprogram the phenotype of macrophages from the M1 type to the M2 type by removing ROS and co-regulating the STING/NF-κB and other key inflammatory signaling pathways.83 M1 macrophages are characterized by a high expression of inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) and the secretion of pro-inflammatory factors such as IL-1β and TNF-α, which not only sustain and exacerbate the inflammatory response but also inhibit the osteogenic differentiation of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs).84 In contrast, M2 macrophages exhibit high expression of CD206 and secrete anti-inflammatory repair factors, including IL-10, that effectively mitigate excessive inflammation, activate osteogenic genes (such as BMP-2 and osteocalcin), and directly promote bone regeneration and repair.85

Studies have shown that cerium oxide (CeO2) nanozymes, with their reversible redox pair of Ce3+ and Ce4+, can mimic the antioxidant activity of SOD and CAT. These nanozymes effectively clear ROS and reactive nitrogen species (RNS), thereby maintaining intracellular redox homeostasis. Consequently, they inhibit the expression of M1-related markers (such as iNOS and CD86) and reduce the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6), promoting the transformation of macrophages into the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype.86 Similarly, Mn4 nanozymes directly downregulate the expression of the key M1 marker iNOS and reduce the secretion of pro-inflammatory factors through the redox activity of their multivalent manganese centers (Mn2+/Mn3+/Mn4+).87

The regulatory mechanism of nanozyme biological activity relies not only on their inherent properties but is also significantly influenced by external microenvironmental factors. For instance, cerium/manganese-doped hydroxyapatite (Ce/MnHAP) nanozymes can eliminate ROS and respond to localized pH changes and near-infrared photothermal stimulation to release bioactive ions such as Ce3+ and Mn2+. These ions work synergistically with ROS clearance to significantly promote macrophage polarization from the pro-inflammatory M1 type to the anti-inflammatory M2 type, thereby restoring immune homeostasis and inhibiting the inflammatory response.88

In-depth studies have revealed that M2 macrophages not only play a crucial role in alleviating inflammation but also secrete key active factors, such as bone morphogenetic protein-2 (BMP-2) and platelet-derived growth factor BB (PDGF-BB), which induce angiogenesis and establish a “vascular-osteogenic” coupling that supports regenerative repair.89,90 Tang et al demonstrated in their study that cerium oxide nanozymes (CeO2) significantly inhibit the activation of the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, which is closely associated with M1 phenotypic polarization in macrophages. At the metabolic checkpoint governing the transition from glycolysis to mitochondrial TCA cycling, macrophage polarization is closely linked to pyruvate fate and mitochondrial oxidative metabolism. Pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages mainly rely on aerobic glycolysis, in which pyruvate is preferentially converted into lactate rather than acetyl-CoA for TCA cycle entry. This metabolic bias is partly regulated by the PDK/PDH axis and PDH-targeting inflammatory mediators, because PDK-mediated PDH inhibition and nitric oxide-dependent PDH impairment can restrict pyruvate oxidation, thereby limiting pyruvate flux into the TCA cycle and sustaining the glycolysis-dependent M1 phenotype.91,92 Meanwhile, mitochondrial dysfunction and SDH-related metabolic interruption promote succinate accumulation, which stabilizes HIF-1α and amplifies glycolytic and pro-inflammatory gene expression, including IL-1β.93,94 The ACOD1/IRG1-derived itaconate-SDH axis also acts as an endogenous immunometabolic feedback checkpoint that modulates SDH activity and restrains excessive inflammatory activation.95 Therefore, nanozymes may interfere with these glycolysis-to-TCA checkpoints by scavenging excessive ROS/RNS, reducing PI3K-Akt/mTOR/HIF-1α-driven glycolytic activation, preserving mitochondrial function, restoring PDH-dependent pyruvate entry into the TCA cycle, and normalizing succinate- or itaconate-related inflammatory feedback. Through these coordinated effects, nanozymes can shift macrophages from a glycolysis-dominant M1 state toward a TCA/OXPHOS-supported M2 phenotype, thereby linking immunomodulation with metabolic reprogramming during bone repair. This metabolic shift further provides the basis for the pro-regenerative secretory profile of M2 macrophages. Consistent with this metabolic and phenotypic transition, M2 macrophages secrete angiogenic factors, including PDGF-BB and VEGF, as well as osteogenic factors such as BMP-2. These factors promote the migration, lumen formation, and angiogenesis of human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs), while also stimulating the proliferation, differentiation, and mineralization of osteoblast precursor cells (MC3T3-E1).96

Recent research has demonstrated that certain nanozymes actively promote tissue repair. For instance, the acrylic acid group-modified M-Mn3O4 nanozyme can moderately activate endogenous antioxidant pathways, such as the nuclear factor E2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) pathway. While reducing oxidative stress and inflammation, this system significantly enhances the expression of osteogenic genes in BMSCs (such as ALP, Runx2, col1, BMP2, etc.) in vitro and exhibits excellent bone regeneration capabilities in rat calvarial defect models.97 In contrast, other nanozymes, such as mesoporous manganese cobalt oxide (MnCoO) catalase mimetics, facilitate the shift of immune responses from a pro-inflammatory dominant state to a pro-repair dominant state by enhancing the expression of the anti-inflammatory factor IL-10 and reducing the release of key pro-inflammatory factors.98

In conclusion, at the mechanistic level, nanozymes gradually promote the transformation of the immune response from inhibiting inflammation to promoting regeneration by clearing the trigger factor ROS, inhibiting the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, blocking key pro-inflammatory signal transduction pathways such as NF-κB/MAPK, and reprogramming the phenotype of macrophages. Collectively, these actions lay a crucial foundation for maintaining immune homeostasis and supporting bone tissue regeneration. However, the immunomodulatory effects of nanozymes on macrophage polarization exhibit a dose-dependent complexity often overlooked. Low-to-moderate concentrations typically promote M2 polarization, whereas higher concentrations may paradoxically induce pro-inflammatory responses via excessive ROS generation from peroxidase-like activities—a biphasic, hormetic-like effect documented for several metal-based nanozymes.99,100 Moreover, contradictory findings exist regarding subcellular requirements: some studies show that cytosolic localization is essential for ROS scavenging and M2 polarization, while others suggest lysosomal accumulation is needed for sustained anti-inflammatory effects.100 These discrepancies highlight an urgent need for standardized evaluation protocols across different cell types and disease models.

Restoring Metabolic Homeostasis: From Scavenging Metabolic Waste to Rebuilding Mitochondrial Function

Mechanistically, nanozymes intervene in metabolic disorders by targeting three interconnected levels: metabolic waste clearance, mitochondrial function repair, and metabolic signaling pathway regulation. Under conditions such as diabetes, osteoporosis, and aging, the local microenvironment of MSCs often is characterized by metabolic dysregulation. This microenvironment is characterized by dyslipidemia, energy supply disorders, and local acidosis, which inhibit the osteogenic differentiation of MSCs and impair bone regeneration.101 Recent studies have shown that nanozymes can help regulate material and energy metabolism in cells. By aiding metabolic reprogramming and repairing mitochondrial function, these nanozymes support MSC differentiation and enhance bone regeneration.102 At the level of metabolic waste clearance, nanozymes can help regulate material and energy metabolism in cells.

Imbalances in metabolism, particularly issues with polyamine metabolism and lipid accumulation, disrupt the balance between bone and fat cell differentiation in MSCs.103 Nanozymes can promote osteoblast differentiation by regulating relevant metabolic pathways. For example, cerium oxide nanozymes (CNPs), which scavenge ROS, ameliorate oxidative stress in high-fat environments. This leads to increased expression of the polyamine transporter ATP13A3, raising levels of polyamines like putrescine in the cells and restoring metabolic balance. This process activates osteogenic genes such as Runt-related transcription factor 2 (Runx2), bone morphogenetic protein 2 (BMP2), and type I collagen (COL1A1), enhances alkaline phosphatase activity, and promotes the formation of mineralized nodules, reversing the inhibition of MSC differentiation in high-fat conditions.103 Additionally, nanozymes like Mg-ZIF target lipid metabolism directly, clearing ROS and upregulating key lipid metabolism genes, such as ELOVL2 and FADS1, thereby restoring lipid balance and guiding MSCs toward becoming osteoblasts.104

The high-glucose environment is also harmful to bone regeneration. Elevated glucose levels can cause chronic inflammation, weaken immune responses, and create a toxic environment, inhibiting osteoblast activity and new blood vessel formation, causing local hypoxia and hindering healing.105 To address this, nanozymes that catalyze glucose metabolism have emerged as a new strategy for treating diabetic bone disease. Research shows that manganese dioxide nanosheets (MnO2NFs) exhibit glucose oxidase (GOD)-like activity, allowing them to oxidize high glucose levels into gluconic acid while producing H2O2 to reduce glucose toxicity. MnO2NFs also show peroxidase (POD)-like activity, breaking down H2O2 into ·OH and O2, which lessens oxidative damage and improves local oxygen levels.106 Similarly, lactic acid accumulation can create an unfavorable hypoxic environment for bone cells. The MnO2-lactate oxidase system has two functions: it oxidizes lactic acid and breaks down H2O2. This system removes lactic acid and generates oxygen, reversing the “acid hypoxia” state and creating a neutral, oxygen-rich environment that supports regeneration.107

Notably, beyond catalytic clearance of glucose-, lactate-, and H2O2-related metabolic stress, metal-containing nanozymes may also directly regulate osteogenic differentiation signaling in BMSCs through bioactive ions generated during degradation, ion exchange, or surface redox remodeling. For manganese-based nanozymes, released Mn2+ can function as an osteogenic signaling modulator rather than merely a catalytic component. MnO2-containing scaffolds have been reported to upregulate TGF-β/Smad signaling in human BMSCs, thereby enhancing osteogenic differentiation.108 In addition, baicalein-coordinated Mn2+ nanoparticles restore osteogenic differentiation under oxidative stress by activating PI3K-Akt and MAPK signaling while suppressing ROS accumulation and ferroptosis, leading to increased ALP activity, mineralization, and osteogenic marker expression.109 Similarly, cerium-containing systems may influence BMSC fate through released cerium species and/or surface Ce3+/Ce4+ redox states. Ce3+-delivering hydrogel microspheres promote BMSC proliferation and osteogenic differentiation through Wnt/β-catenin signaling, whereas CeO2 coatings with different Ce3+/Ce4+ ratios regulate BMSC osteogenic behavior through SMAD-dependent BMP signaling, with downstream activation of Runx2 and increased ALP, OCN, and mineral deposition.110,111 Beyond these classical pathways, ceria nanoparticles can enhance endochondral ossification-based bone repair by activating DHX15 and downstream p38 MAPK signaling, thereby promoting hypertrophic differentiation of BMSCs.112 Recent evidence also suggests that cerium ions may reduce intracellular ROS and shift mesenchymal stromal cell differentiation from adipogenesis toward osteogenesis, although the detailed signaling mechanism requires further clarification.113 These findings indicate that metal-containing nanozymes promote bone regeneration through both catalytic metabolic remodeling and direct ion-mediated activation of osteogenic signaling in BMSCs, while these effects remain dependent on ion dose, valence state, and release kinetics.

In severe cases, the abnormal build-up of metabolic waste can damage the ECM, hindering bone regeneration. Nanozymes can meet this challenge with their regulatory capabilities.114 For instance, a high-glucose environment inhibits lysyl oxidase (LOX) activity, leading to insufficient collagen cross-linking, lower bone quality, and impaired regeneration. Research shows that specially designed lysyl oxidase nanozymes (LON) can compensate for LOX deficiency in high-glucose conditions. These nanozymes facilitate the linking of soluble collagen fibers secreted by osteoblasts. After cross-linking, the collagen fibers exhibit enhanced mechanical properties, providing a better structure for mineral deposits and promoting mineralized nodule formation.115

At the level of mitochondrial function repair, removing harmful metabolites creates a favorable environment for bone cell survival. However, normal metabolism requires regulating mitochondrial function.116 Under pathological conditions, dysfunction of the mitochondrial electron transport chain (ETC) leads to excess ROS and impaired ATP synthesis, worsening inflammation and causing bone loss.117 In a ligation-induced periodontitis mouse model, researchers found that mitochondrial complex biomimetic nanozymes (MCBN) can target and enter the mitochondria of inflammatory macrophages. These nanozymes significantly lower ROS and ATP production by absorbing excess electrons and regulating the electron transport chain. As a result, inflammation decreases, alveolar bone damage reduces, and bone regeneration is promoted.118 These findings indicate that improving mitochondrial function and maintaining energy metabolism through nanozymes are key strategies for enhancing bone regeneration under pathological conditions.

For example, selenium-doped carbon quantum dots (SeYQDs) not only act as antioxidants by eliminating excess ROS but also enhance the expression of protective mitochondrial proteins such as SIRT3. By repairing mitochondrial membrane potential and reducing depolarization, they optimize oxidative phosphorylation and improve the electron transport chain’s efficiency, restoring energy metabolism in cells.119 In complex environments with chronic inflammation and low oxygen levels, such as osteoarthritis, hydrogen-releasing nanozymes like Se-HMPB@AB@COS continuously release hydrogen in response to acidity. Coupled with their SOD-like activity, these nanozymes effectively remove hydroxyl radicals and superoxide anions (·O2−) in mitochondria, reducing oxidative damage. This system maintains mitochondrial membrane stability, promotes ATP production, and enhances mitochondrial recycling by activating the PINK1-Parkin-LC3B pathway, which helps renew energy metabolism units.120

At the level of metabolic signaling pathway regulation, in addition to providing energy, nanozymes can directly regulate bone metabolism signaling pathways, stimulate alkaline phosphatase activity, and promote mineralized nodule formation by increasing Runx2 and other key osteogenic transcription factors.121 For example, LiMn2O4 nanozymes can activate the AMPK signaling pathway, enhancing the expression of downstream osteogenic genes like osteocalcin (OCN), osteopontin (OPN), and type I collagen (Col I). This helps MSCs differentiate into osteoblasts and boosts their mineralization capacity.122 Furthermore, Au@Pt nanozymes loaded onto GMAP composite microspheres activate the p38 MAPK pathway, increasing Runx2 expression, promoting MSC osteogenic differentiation, and raising OCN levels, while also enhancing MSC paracrine function. This leads to TGF-β secretion, which drives M2 macrophage polarization to reduce inflammation, and VEGF release to promote new blood vessel formation, achieving comprehensive regulation of bone regeneration.123 Prussian blue nanozymes (PBNPs) have dual regulatory effects. They block the RANKL-induced processes that lead to osteoclast differentiation while inhibiting osteoclast gene expression and can activate Nrf2, a key antioxidant protein, increasing downstream antioxidant enzyme production and enhancing cellular metabolism.124

Studies based on metabolism analysis further demonstrate the regulatory role of nanozymes in metabolic networks. Manganese dioxide nanozyme (MnO2@HPM) repairs the metabolic disorder caused by inflammatory stress in stem cells by increasing mitochondrial membrane potential, reducing damage and electron leakage, reversing early mitochondrial apoptosis, and boosting key metabolites like 2-oxoglutarate and citric acid in the TCA cycle.125 This enhances energy metabolism, promotes ATP production, and raises levels of energy-related metabolites like creatine and gluconic acid. It also alleviates amino acid depletion, supporting protein synthesis and cell growth. Additionally, it boosts antioxidant production and strengthens cellular defense against oxidative stress. Ultimately, by regulating key pathways—including the arginine and proline metabolism, the pentose phosphate pathway, and central carbon metabolism—it helps restore the osteogenic capacity of stem cells, providing the energy and building blocks needed for bone regeneration.125

In summary, at the mechanistic level, nanozymes can target crucial points in cellular metabolism, facilitating responses that range from removing harmful waste to repairing mitochondria and activating the osteogenic program. This restoration of metabolic balance under pathological conditions provides the energy and materials necessary for osteogenic differentiation, representing a new therapeutic approach for bone regeneration in complex metabolic diseases. Despite these advances, significant methodological limitations constrain current metabolic studies. Most rely on oversimplified in vitro models that poorly recapitulate chronic bone diseases. Few studies quantitatively assess nanozyme degradation products or long-term metabolic fate, despite concerns that released metal ions may disrupt endogenous pathways.126 Furthermore, metabolomic analyses frequently report pathway alterations without mechanistic validation, leaving causal relationships ambiguous. Therefore, addressing these gaps will require more physiologically relevant models, comprehensive biodegradation profiling, and causal validation.

Reshaping the Extracellular Microenvironment: Synergy of Redox Balance, Vascularization, and Antibacterial Properties

Bone regeneration is not merely a process of cell replacement; it involves complex tissue repair mechanisms.127 The success of bone regeneration is influenced by the extracellular microenvironment, which includes factors such as redox state, pH, oxygen concentration, microbial community, and the properties of the ECM.128,129 Persistent oxidative stress from infections and tumors, along with abnormal ECM structure, hampers bone regeneration.130,131 Nanozymes can help correct microenvironmental imbalances, reshaping the extracellular space to enhance regeneration.

Accumulated ROS at injury sites cause oxidative stress that inhibits osteocyte function. Nanozymes can restore the local redox balance by exerting antioxidant effects. For instance, manganese oxide nanoparticles (MONPs) can convert manganese ions across different valence states to mimic natural antioxidant enzymes like SOD, efficiently eliminating O2−. In vitro studies show they can reduce ROS levels induced by TLR2 agonists in bone marrow-derived macrophages, thereby lessening tissue oxidative damage.132 Cerium oxide nanozymes (CeO2NPs) utilize both SOD and CAT activities to eliminate ROS and activate the Nrf2–HO-1 antioxidant pathway, enhancing the body’s own antioxidant defenses. In vivo experiments confirm that this synergistic antioxidant mechanism effectively lowers ROS levels in inflammatory environments, protecting BMSCs and osteoblasts from oxidative damage and restoring their proliferation and differentiation potential.133

Moreover, redox imbalance can damage vascular endothelial cells, downregulating angiogenic factors like VEGF and hindering new blood vessel formation.134,135 Angiogenesis is crucial for delivering O2 and nutrients to regenerating bone tissues.136 The ZnO-CuS nanozyme effectively removes specific ROS such as ·OH and singlet oxygen (1O2), alleviating oxidative stress on vascular cells. Lowered ROS levels favor macrophage polarization to the M2 type, promoting pro-angiogenic factor secretion like VEGF. ZnO-CuS also enhances the migration and tube formation of HUVECs while increasing vascular markers such as CD31, thereby improving the stability of the vascular network.137 Additionally, the Mn3O4 nanozyme promotes vascular regeneration by removing ROS and inducing the shift of macrophages from the pro-inflammatory M1 to the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, supplying necessary oxygen, nutrients, and transport for stem cells in bone repair.138 Research also shows that endothelial cells can secrete osteogenic factors that work together with sustained osteogenic peptides like P24 to create a microenvironment that supports BMSC osteogenic differentiation. With the synergy of immune regulation, angiogenesis, and osteogenesis, this process significantly speeds up the healing of bone defects.138

If oxidative stress is not controlled, it can weaken local tissue defenses and create risks for bacterial infections.139 Once bacteria breach defenses, they trigger persistent inflammation, erode the bone matrix, hinder osteoblast differentiation, and activate osteoclasts. This chain of events delays or blocks bone regeneration.140,141 Recent studies have shown that developing enzyme-active nanomaterials for antibacterial purposes holds promise. These materials transform harmful substances into agents that can sterilize without causing antibiotic resistance.142,143 Qiu et al found that ROS can help eliminate bacteria. They designed CFO@MoS2 nanozymes that generate ROS for synergistic antibacterial effects and promote bone regeneration. Under ultrasound activation, these nanozymes produce ROS that damage bacterial membranes, DNA, and proteins, resulting in bacterial death. Ultrasound also reduces Cu2+ to more toxic Cu⁺, which binds to bacterial lipid-acylated enzymes, leading to aggregation and inactivation, while disrupting Fe-S cluster proteins and the tricarboxylic acid cycle, causing metabolic and energy failure, ultimately killing bacteria.144

When infection complicates the microenvironment, nanozymes can provide precise chemodynamic therapy. Research by Song et al shows that high H2O2 levels from bacterial infection can activate the POD activity of a Mn monometallic nanozyme (Mn/HSAE), converting H2O2 into highly toxic ·OH and O2−. Mn/HSAE also possesses strong acoustic-thermal conversion efficiency, making it suitable for sonodynamic therapy (SDT). This CDT/SDT strategy effectively kills common pathogens such as Staphylococcus aureus and E. coli via oxidative stress.145 Another study found that gold nanoparticles (Au NPs) loaded onto the ZIF-8 framework with glucose oxidase-like activity can generate gluconic acid and H2O2 upon near-infrared irradiation, utilizing elevated glucose levels. This creates an acidic environment that promotes ZIF-8 degradation and releases Zn2+. H2O2, combined with photothermal therapy, disrupts bacterial structure and eliminates pathogens that disturb homeostasis.146 A critical paradox in nanozyme-mediated antibacterial therapy remains unresolved: the dual role of ROS in bacterial killing versus tissue repair.147,148 While ROS-generating nanozymes can effectively damage bacterial membranes, proteins, nucleic acids, and biofilm matrices, excessive or poorly localized ROS may also injure osteoprogenitor cells, impair angiogenesis, and delay tissue healing.148 Current designs still lack precise spatiotemporal control over ROS flux to achieve bactericidal efficacy while sparing host tissues. Moreover, the long-term safety of persistent catalytic activity has rarely been systematically assessed in bone infection models, raising concerns that chronic low-level oxidative stress may impose cumulative burdens on regenerating tissues, even when acute cytotoxicity is not apparent.147 Stimuli-responsive systems that generate ROS during the infection phase and subsequently switch toward ROS scavenging or catalytic inactivation after bacterial clearance are promising but remain underexplored. Therefore, precisely balancing antibacterial ROS generation with subsequent tissue-protective ROS regulation is essential for converting an infected and inflammatory defect site into a regenerative microenvironment. Once infection is effectively controlled, persistent inflammatory and osteolytic stimuli can be reduced, enabling cell infiltration, nutrient delivery, and angiogenesis, while reactivating osteogenic programs such as Runx2 and OCN expression to restore BMSC osteogenic differentiation and restart bone regeneration.149

In summary, nanozymes remodel the extracellular microenvironment progressively by correcting redox imbalances, supporting vascularization for nutrient delivery, and eliminating destructive factors like bacterial infections. Together, these actions form a comprehensive system that protects against external damage and optimizes the internal environment, ensuring successful bone regeneration.

Element-Specific Regulatory Mechanisms of Nanozymes in the Bone Regenerative Microenvironment

Different elemental compositions endow nanozymes with distinct catalytic behaviors and biological roles in bone regeneration. Therefore, element-specific analysis can help clarify how different nanozyme systems should be selected for specific pathological tasks, including antioxidation, immunomodulation, metabolic correction, antibacterial therapy, angiogenesis, and osteogenesis.

Manganese-based nanozymes (MBNs) are among the most widely studied systems for bone regeneration because of their multivalent redox states and tunable multienzyme-mimetic activities. Mn3O4 nanozymes have been mainly tested for ROS scavenging, M1-to-M2 macrophage polarization, angiogenesis, and osteogenic support, thereby linking immune regulation with vascularized bone repair.138 MnO2-based nanozymes predominantly exert CAT-like activity by decomposing H2O2 into O2 and H2O, which helps relieve oxidative stress and improve local oxygen availability. In addition, Zn-doped MnO2 nanocoatings have been shown to enhance catalase-mimetic activity, protect pre-osteoblasts against H2O2-induced oxidative injury, and support osteogenic differentiation and mineralization.108,150 MnO2 can also be integrated with lactate oxidase to consume accumulated lactate and generate oxygen, thereby reversing the acidic and hypoxic microenvironment that suppresses bone repair. In infected bone defects, Mn single-atom nanozymes, such as Mn/HSAE, combine catalytic ROS generation with sonodynamic effects to eliminate bacteria and restore a regenerative microenvironment.145 Bimetallic CuMn-ZIF nanozymes further expand the role of Mn-based systems by simultaneously scavenging H2O2 and superoxide anions, suppressing PI3K-AKT-mTOR signaling, and restoring lysosomal-autophagic flux in osteoarthritic microenvironments.151 Collectively, Mn-based nanozymes contribute to bone regeneration through composition-dependent ROS/O2 regulation, lactate metabolism remodeling, immune regulation, antibacterial activity, angiogenesis, and osteogenic support.

Cerium-based nanozymes, especially CeO2 nanozymes, are characterized by reversible Ce3+/Ce4+ redox cycling, which provides SOD-like and CAT-like catalytic activities and enables dynamic regulation of redox homeostasis. In high-fat microenvironments, CeO2 nanozymes regulate the ATP13A3-polyamine axis, restore polyamine homeostasis, and activate osteogenic genes such as Runx2, BMP2, and COL1A1, suggesting their role in correcting obesity-related metabolic inhibition of osteogenesis.103 In radiation-induced bone injury, surface Ce3+-enriched CeO2 nanozymes mitigate bone loss and structural damage by scavenging ROS, reducing DNA damage, regulating adaptive stress responses, and supporting bone progenitor cell function.1 Thus, Ce-based nanozymes are particularly suitable for bone regeneration disorders associated with oxidative stress, metabolic dysregulation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and radiation-related tissue damage.

Iron-based nanozymes represent another versatile platform for bone repair, especially in oxidative stress-related bone loss, osteoclast-mediated bone destruction, and infection-associated defects. Hollow Prussian blue nanozymes (HPBZs) and Prussian blue nanoparticles (PBNPs) suppress ROS-mediated MAPK/NF-κB signaling, inhibit osteoclastogenesis, reduce bone resorption, and preserve bone homeostasis under osteoporotic conditions.80,152 Functional modification further broadens their biological roles. For example, copper-doped Prussian blue nanozymes have recently been reported as osteogenic nanozymes for diabetic bone regeneration, combining ROS scavenging, anti-inflammatory activity, and Cu-related activation of PI3K-Akt/RUNX2-associated osteogenic differentiation.153,154 Fe-doped carbon dots exhibit POD-like antibacterial activity and integrate catalytic ROS generation with mild photothermal effects under NIR irradiation, providing an antibacterial nanozyme strategy that may be further adapted for infection-complicated bone repair.155 Fe3O4@GO magnetic nanocomposites further demonstrate how iron-based nanozyme-like systems can combine ROS regulation, magnetic responsiveness, and osteogenic stimulation to protect MSCs from oxidative injury and promote osteogenic differentiation.156

Other element-specific nanozymes have also been explored for specialized regulation of the bone regenerative microenvironment. ZnCe-LDO nanozymes combine zinc- and cerium-related bioactivities to reprogram inflammatory macrophages toward the M2 phenotype while supporting osteogenesis and angiogenesis.81 Defect-engineered RuO2 nanospheres suppress ROS-mediated NLRP3 inflammasome activation and downstream caspase-1 signaling, thereby alleviating inflammation, cartilage degeneration, and abnormal bone remodeling in osteoarthritis models.78 Hollow RuO2 nanospheres also attenuate osteoclast differentiation and bone loss induced by metal wear particles, supporting the potential of ruthenium-based nanozymes in aseptic osteolysis.38 In addition, bimetallic and doped nanozymes, such as Cu-Mn single-atom systems, demonstrate that elemental synergy can enhance catalytic activity, reduce ROS-mediated oxidative damage, promote macrophage reprogramming, and improve tissue-protective outcomes.157

Taken together, nanozyme-mediated bone regeneration is not merely a general antioxidant process. Mn-based nanozymes are particularly useful for ROS/O2/lactate regulation, immunomodulation, antibacterial therapy, and angiogenesis; Ce-based nanozymes are suited for redox homeostasis, metabolic reprogramming, mitochondrial protection, and radiation-related bone injury; Fe-based nanozymes are advantageous for osteoclast inhibition, antibacterial therapy, magnetic responsiveness, and osteogenic support; and Ru-, Zn-, and bimetallic nanozymes provide specialized functions such as inflammasome inhibition, ion-mediated immunoregulation, and synergistic catalytic enhancement. These findings suggest that future nanozyme design should be guided by rational elemental selection and compositional engineering to match the pathological requirements of different bone diseases.

Design and Application of Nanozymes for Bone Disease Treatment

Bone regeneration involves the spatiotemporal coordination of multiple biological processes such as immunity, metabolism, and angiogenesis in time and space. A single-function nanozyme is unable to cope with the complex pathological microenvironment. Synergistic Antioxidant and Immunomodulatory Multilevel Design Strategy

The high concentration of ROS in areas of bone injury, necrosis, and defects induces apoptosis in bone cells and inhibits their osteogenic differentiation while also acting as a danger signal that activates and recruits immune cells, leading to chronic inflammation.162,163 Activated immune cells, such as M1 macrophages and neutrophils, produce large amounts of ROS, worsening oxidative stress and tissue damage.164,165 Thus, simply eliminating ROS or applying anti-inflammatory measures is insufficient to completely reverse the pathological process. To achieve sustained immune microenvironment remodeling, nanozyme systems must be engineered at multiple levels, including active-center construction, ligand coordination, surface modification, local retention, controlled release, and immune-cell targeting.

To address the complex pathological state of inflammatory bone diseases, characterized by intertwined oxidative stress and immune dysregulation, researchers have developed an integrated strategy combining drugs and nanozymes. By coordinating active metal ions with anti-inflammatory natural drugs, they constructed a nanozyme drug system that merges enzymatic functions with drug release capabilities.33 For instance, zinc-ligand B coordination nanozymes (Zn-LB NPs) use the coordination ability of Zn2+ to self-assemble with polyphenolic anti-inflammatory ligand B, forming a stable nanostructure.166,167 This system provides efficient delivery of hydrophobic drugs and achieves a clearance rate of over 60% for free radicals. It also effectively eliminates ROS within macrophages, promoting their transformation from the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype to the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, interrupting the inflammatory cycle.168 Similarly, cerium-zoledronic acid nanoparticles (CZ NPs) combine cerium, which displays SOD and CAT activities, with the bone-targeting drug zoledronic acid (ZOL). ZOL possesses anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory functions and has a high affinity for calcium ions and hydroxyapatite, enhancing the ability of CZ NPs to target bone defects.169,170

Building on functional integration, researchers have further optimized the structural topology of the nanozymes themselves to enhance their drug-carrying capacity and disease-targeting ability. Hollow manganese oxide nanoparticles (H-MnO2) exemplify this. H-MnO2 gains CAT and SOD activities due to manganese and has a hollow structure that serves as a drug reservoir, enabling ROS-responsive release under high drug loads.158

Despite an optimized structure, the high surface activity of nanozymes can lead to biological toxicity.171 To address this, surface hydrophilic modification has become a necessary safety strategy. Li et al modified cerium oxide nanoparticles (CNPs) with polyacrylic acid (PAA) to create PAA-modified cerium oxide nanozymes (PCNPs).172 The PAA layer provides stable hydration and spatial hindrance, allowing CNPs to disperse uniformly in biological fluids while isolating their active sites from direct contact with cells. This design helps PCNPs maintain their ROS clearance ability while reducing toxicity.173 Findings show that treatment with PCNPs significantly increases the bone volume fraction (BV/TV) in disease models, confirming that this modification addresses the limitations of unmodified CNPs.172 Similarly, manganese-based nanozymes coated with polydopamine (PDA) exhibit significant benefits. The PDA layer minimizes direct contact with cell membranes and proteins, avoiding non-specific damage. Its hydrophilic surface also reduces non-specific serum protein adsorption, helping evade immune system recognition. Moreover, PDA can degrade into endogenous dopamine metabolites, reducing potential risks associated with long-term material retention.31

Even with good biocompatibility, free nanozymes can be washed away or diluted by local tissue fluid or rapidly cleared by blood flow after injection, leading to low drug concentration at the lesion site and a short duration of action.171,174 Therefore, loading nanozymes onto biocompatible carrier materials for long-term retention and controlled release has become a critical focus in this field. Hydrogels, with their three-dimensional hydrophilic network and effective sustained release capabilities, show significant promise as a delivery platform for nanozymes.175,176

Research has confirmed that encapsulating MgMn-Layered Double Hydroxide (MgMn-LDH) nanozymes in hydrogels retains them in the bone defect area and enhances their catalytic efficiency by slowing substrate diffusion. Data indicate this composite achieves a clearance rate of 85.8% to 95.3% for various free radicals while maintaining stable catalytic activity for 15 days.159 Similarly, researchers encapsulated dual-valent ruthenium nanozymes (RuNZs) with cascading catalytic ability within gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) hydrogels to develop the MEBTHS system. This design provides sustained antioxidant functions in animal models, significantly reducing intracellular ROS levels and promoting bone defect repair and regeneration.177

In addition to organic hydrogels, inorganic scaffold structures can serve as effective delivery systems for nanozymes. The Ce@MSNs composite system, made by encapsulating cerium oxide within mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSNs), addresses aggregation and activity loss of cerium oxide while maintaining a high catalytic surface area.178 Notably, soluble silicon ions released by the MSN framework during degradation stimulate the proliferation and differentiation of osteoblasts.179 Evidence shows that Ce@MSNs enhance alkaline phosphatase activity and stimulate calcium nodule formation, even without exogenous osteogenic inducers.178

After achieving effective, low-toxicity, and long-lasting delivery of nanozymes, researchers have focused on actively regulating the bone immune microenvironment.180 Neutrophils, the first immune cells recruited after injury, can cause significant tissue damage due to excessive activation during NETosis.181,182 Inspired by traditional Chinese medicine processes, Yuan et al designed iron-doped carbon dots (MM-CDs) nanozymes that preferentially target over-activated neutrophils at the lesion site through surface charge and ligand recognition. By inhibiting NETosis, they transform neutrophils into the reparative N2 phenotype, creating an anti-inflammatory environment that promotes bone repair.160

In addition to regulating neutrophils, progress has been made in precisely regulating macrophage phenotypes.183 Yang et al designed copper-manganese bimetallic single-atom nanozymes (Cu-Mn NE) to counteract the deleterious effects of pro-inflammatory M1-type macrophages, which worsen bone destruction, while promoting the regenerative functions of anti-inflammatory M2-type macrophages. Manganese serves as the active center of SOD, efficiently eliminating ROS and providing a low oxidative stress environment for macrophages. Copper, as a cofactor for lysyl oxidase, aids in stabilizing chondrocyte matrices. This nanozyme effectively drives macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype and activates the cartilage synthesis pathway, fostering an environment conducive to bone regeneration and remodeling.157,184,185

In summary, modern antioxidant–immunomodulatory nanozyme design has evolved from simple ROS clearance toward a multi-level engineering framework involving active-center construction, ligand coordination, surface modification, carrier-mediated retention, scaffold integration, and immune-cell targeting. These strategies enable nanozymes to maintain catalytic activity in complex inflammatory environments, reduce potential biological toxicity, improve local retention, and actively reshape immune-cell behavior. Therefore, the therapeutic value of antioxidant nanozymes depends not only on their intrinsic ROS-scavenging capacity, but also on whether their structural and delivery designs can support durable, localized, and stage-appropriate immune regulation during bone regeneration.

Pathology-Adaptive Nanozyme Design for Metabolic Bone Microenvironments

Metabolic disorders and oxidative stress often interconnect and worsen one another during bone defect repair, creating a vicious cycle that impedes tissue regeneration.186 For example, in bone regeneration disorders caused by a high-fat environment, excessive free fatty acids can result in mitochondrial dysfunction and increased production of ROS, hindering the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs.187 Traditional pharmacological methods, such as bisphosphonates, mainly inhibit bone resorption but do not directly resolve the underlying imbalance between ROS and local metabolic dysfunction. Therefore, recent nanozyme design has shifted toward intelligent response systems that can sense pathological metabolic cues, maintain catalytic activity in metabolically stressed microenvironments, and coordinate metabolic regulation with osteogenic repair.188 Instead of functioning merely as ROS-scavenging agents, these systems are increasingly designed to combine controlled delivery, cascade catalytic reactions, and mitochondrial protection, thereby providing a material-engineering basis for metabolic reprogramming during bone regeneration.

Regarding lipid metabolism disorders, researchers have focused on regulating lipid synthesis and desaturation to restore lipid balance. For instance, the Mg-ZIF nanozyme system has been shown to upregulate genes involved in the synthesis and desaturation of long-chain fatty acids, reducing abnormal lipid accumulation and maintaining lipid metabolism within cells.104 However, effective metabolic regulation needs to align with the specific characteristics of the pathological microenvironment for precise intervention. To tackle this challenge, Li et al developed an intelligent hydrogel system using cerium dioxide nanoparticles (CNPs). This system responds to high levels of matrix metalloproteinase-2 (MMP-2) and elevated ROS in local bone defect areas, allowing controlled release of CNPs. These released CNPs efficiently eliminate ROS and initiate the metabolic program needed to restore intracellular polyamine homeostasis. This strategy provides more sustainable metabolic regulation than traditional exogenous polyamine supplementation by utilizing the controlled release of the hydrogel.103

Similarly, abnormal glucose metabolism is a major contributor to bone regeneration disorders, primarily manifesting as a cycle of “high glucose - oxidative stress - chronic inflammation.” In response, Zhang et al created a hybrid nanozyme called BPQD@MOF, which combines glucose metabolism regulation with multi-enzyme antioxidant properties. By integrating SOD and CAT activities from black phosphorus quantum dots (BPQD), this system adapts to use local glucose, eliminate ROS, and generate O2 in situ, disrupting the metabolic disorder cycle. In a diabetic rat model, a hydrogel-modified titanium scaffold carrying this nanozyme significantly improved new bone volume and mineralization density.189

To create coordinated interventions across various pathological processes, a cascade catalytic strategy was integrated into the nanozyme system design. For example, the cascade nanozyme system combines glucose oxidase (GOx), cerium nanozyme, and zoledronic acid (ZA), using glucose as a trigger for GOx.190 The ROS generated activates the antioxidant function of the cerium nanozyme and produces gluconic acid to lower the local pH, promoting gel degradation and release of ZA. Experimental results show that ZA inhibits osteoclast metabolism, indirectly fostering osteogenesis. Notably, the therapeutic effects of ZCG activate specifically in the diabetic microenvironment, minimizing potential side effects on normal tissues.190

Another significant aspect of metabolic regulation is the clearance and transformation of harmful metabolic products. For instance, lactic acid accumulation in bone defect areas intensifies acidosis and negatively affects osteoblast function through feedback mechanisms.191 While lactate oxidase can convert lactate, its by-product, H2O2, can worsen oxidative stress.192 To overcome this issue, Shen and his team designed a synergistic system combining lactate oxidase with manganese dioxide (MnO2) nanozymes. This system promotes lactate conversion and utilizes MnO2’s CAT-like activity to decompose H2O2 into harmless H2O and O2. This design creates a positive feedback loop for metabolic regulation and microenvironment improvement.107

However, simply eliminating metabolic products or regulating pathways is not enough to reverse cellular function decline in the metabolic bone disease microenvironment. Research indicates that mitochondria, as vital energy metabolism centers, are significant sources of ROS and key players in aging and the decline in osteogenic capacity.193,194 Therefore, beyond waste elimination, restoring mitochondrial homeostasis and reconstructing energy metabolism networks are essential strategies to address the challenges in bone regeneration related to metabolic bone diseases.151

Based on this understanding, Gu et al used Prussian blue nanozymes (PBNPs) to influence mitochondrial dynamics in a diabetic bone microenvironment. Their experiments showed that PBNPs effectively reversed mitochondrial fragmentation caused by high glucose and lipid levels by modulating relevant genes (OPA1, MFN2, and DRP1), restoring mitochondrial membrane potential.195 This method enhanced mitochondrial health in terms of gene expression, morphology, and function.

The effectiveness of this strategy was further confirmed in studies that improved single-atom nanozymes. The Fe2-NCs dual-atom nanozyme, developed from a previous single-atom Fe nanozyme (Fe1-NCs), effectively inhibited NOX4 expression by leveraging metal cooperativity, thereby decreasing mitochondrial ROS generation. Additionally, it significantly enhanced mitochondrial respiratory chain complex IV function, restored ATP synthesis, and showed improved efficacy in both mitochondrial protection and energy metabolism reconstruction.196

For metabolic bone diseases requiring long-term treatment, such as osteoporosis, achieving both metabolic regulation and mitochondrial function repair during continuous therapy is a critical focus in nanozyme design.197 Hu et al, considering the osteoporosis microenvironment’s characteristics and the mechanisms behind mitochondrial dysfunction, designed a cerium/strontium (Ce/Sr) dual-metal bifunctional metal-organic framework (MOF) coating.198 Cerium provides intrinsic antioxidant enzyme activity by mimicking SOD and CAT catalytic cycles. Meanwhile, strontium enhances the body’s antioxidant capacity and promotes mitochondrial fusion, thereby improving cellular function.198,199 This dual-metal approach integrates antioxidant defense and mitochondrial dynamics at the material level.

However, in cases where the imbalance between osteogenesis and osteolysis is more pronounced, such as traction-induced osteogenesis, the design of nanozymes needs to be expanded to regulate bone cell metabolism directly.200 To address prolonged treatment cycles, inadequate mineralization, and high recurrence rates in traditional traction osteogenesis therapy, researchers developed a pH-responsive nanozyme with a core-shell structure called CuMnCO3@BSA. This design responds adaptively to changes in the bone dissolution microenvironment at various stages, maintaining a neutral pH with a ROS burst during early inflammation and becoming locally acidic during late osteoclast activity. Experimental results reveal that this nanozyme preferentially releases manganese ions (Mn2+) in neutral environments to inhibit inflammation, while it rapidly releases copper ions (Cu2+) in acidic conditions—specifically at lesion sites—to induce osteoclast apoptosis. By simultaneously targeting inflammation and bone resorption, this self-adaptive nanoplatform demonstrates efficient synergistic treatment with significant clinical potential.201

In complex pathological environments characterized by inflammation and oxidative stress, developing a bone regeneration platform that offers biological protection and active repair functions is a significant research focus.202 Wei et al combined iron oxide (Fe3O4) nanoparticles with POD-like activity and graphene oxide (GO) to create the Fe3O4@GO magnetic nanozyme system. Experimental results show that GO effectively captures ROS produced during Fe3O4 catalysis, reducing oxidative stress damage to BMSCs. Additionally, GO’s high specific surface area and negative charge enhance its ability to load bone morphogenetic protein 2 (BMP2). Under the influence of a static magnetic field, this system significantly promotes osteogenic differentiation.156

In summary, metabolic regulation-oriented nanozyme design should be tailored to the specific pathological features of bone disease, including lipid imbalance, polyamine disruption, hyperglycemia, lactate accumulation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and osteogenesis–osteolysis imbalance. By integrating stimuli-responsive release, cascade catalytic logic, mitochondrial protection, ion-mediated regulation, and multimodal bioactive cues, these systems transform metabolic stress signals into therapeutic triggers. This design concept provides a more precise and durable approach for reconstructing metabolic homeostasis and promoting efficient bone regeneration under complex pathological conditions.

Temporally Controlled Design Strategy to Address the Dual Challenges of Infection and Vascularization

Traditional treatment strategies for infectious bone defects often fail to simultaneously address the distinct needs during the infection and repair stages. During the infection stage, potent antibacterial agents are crucial for eliminating pathogens, while the repair stage demands angiogenesis and osteogenic activity to rebuild bone tissue. Additionally, excessive ROS production can exacerbate tissue damage.203,204 Nanozymes, with their unique catalytic activity, controllable multifunctionality, and excellent biocompatibility, offer new opportunities to tackle both infection and vascularization challenges.205

A recent study began by optimizing the intrinsic catalytic activity of nanozymes, providing a foundation for multi-functional integration. For example, iron-doped carbon dot nanozymes (Fe-CDs) exhibited POD-like activity due to the coordinated introduction of iron ions.155 Under near-infrared light (808 nm) irradiation, Fe-CDs not only disrupted bacterial structure through a mild photothermal effect (below 45°C) but also catalyzed ROS production, effectively killing Staphylococcus aureus and E. coli. In vivo models indicated that the localized temperature increase stimulated fibroblast proliferation, angiogenesis, and collagen deposition.206 Thus, optimizing nanozyme catalytic activity serves as a crucial starting point for multi-functional integration.

Next, controlling the morphology of nanozymes can further influence their surface chemical state.207 Researchers designed various cerium oxide nanozyme coatings and compared their performance in promoting angiogenesis and antibacterial properties. Among these, nanocones (NC) excelled in removing superoxide free radicals owing to their high Ce3+ content. In contrast, nano-diploids (NP) showed advantages in CAT activity and in promoting direct bone formation due to higher Ce4+ content. However, there is still potential for enhancing the specific surface areas of both morphologies. Conversely, nanoflowers (NF) exhibited a complex flower-like structure and a balanced Ce4+/Ce3+ ratio, demonstrating optimal repair performance.96 This indicates that morphological engineering is vital for coordinating the multifunctionality of nanozymes.

Despite progress, traditional nanozyme design faces a challenge: while antibacterial action typically requires ROS production, anti-inflammatory and repair activities necessitate ROS removal.148 Recent research developed PtCuTe nanosheets that ensure effective sterilization through a non-ROS-dependent physical disruption mechanism, while also possessing strong ROS-clearing capabilities to protect tissues. This innovative approach successfully resolves the contradiction, promoting angiogenesis, guiding macrophages to polarize towards the pro-repair M2 phenotype, and enhancing the migratory capacity of repair cells. Ultimately, it establishes a “vascular-immune-repair” feedback loop within the damaged microenvironment.208 This advancement represents a significant shift from merely adding functions to the synergistic integration of system functions, providing valuable insights for future research.

To achieve precise temperature control, researchers integrated nanozymes into an intelligent responsive delivery system. Using MoS2@PDA-L-arginine (MSPA) as an example, this system improves dispersibility through polydopamine modification and includes L-arginine as a nitric oxide (NO) precursor. Upon irradiation that generates ROS, it facilitates the on-demand release of NO. When integrated into a multi-responsive cryogel that reacts to pH, temperature, and light, the system captures bacteria through positive electrification in acidic environments. Furthermore, under near-infrared irradiation, it synergistically clears biofilms through photothermal, photodynamic, and gas therapies. The continuous release of NO and reduction of oxidative stress jointly promote angiogenesis, transitioning from “anti-infection” to “promoting healing”.209–211 This intelligent design allows nanozymes to switch their functional modes based on microenvironmental changes, better meeting the needs of both the infection and repair phases during bone healing.

Additionally, researchers developed a nanozyme system with stage-adaptive capabilities. The pH/NIR dual-responsive hydrogel (CMSs@Gel) utilizes the acidic environment during the infection phase to activate enzymatic activity and produce ROS for sterilization. After controlling the infection, the system automatically shifts to catalase mode to remove residual ROS.212 Similarly, the W-GA-Van@Zn nanozyme harnesses the pH-responsive feature of the metal-porphyrin bond to rapidly release Zn2+ for sterilization during the infection stage. It then slowly releases Zn2+ to promote tissue repair in the neutral recovery phase, while also achieving bacterial targeting through vancomycin modification. Its antioxidant activity quickly eliminates harmful ROS generated during the photothermal process.213 These designs effectively sense changes in the pathological microenvironment, seamlessly switching between antibacterial and repair functions, facilitating a shift from an infection-controlled microenvironment to a regeneration-permissive microenvironment for angiogenesis and osteogenic differentiation.

As nanozyme designs coordinate antibacterial and repair-promoting functions over time, they must also address the critical issue of “oxidative damage during the electro-stimulated process of bone regeneration.” Although traditional piezoelectric materials like BaTiO3 and ZnO can promote bone regeneration through electrical stimulation, their wide bandgap results in excessive ROS generation under ultrasonic excitation, worsening oxidative stress.214,215 The platinum-modified graphene dihydropyrrole (GDYO@Pt) employs a Schottky heterojunction formed by narrow bandgap GDYO and platinum to precisely direct energy from the piezoelectric effect to hydrogen production reactions. This design not only prevents ROS generation but also actively removes toxic hydroxyl radicals through the production of hydrogen, concurrently achieving “electrical stimulation” and “antioxidation”.216 Consequently, this approach creates a safe regenerative environment for vascular and bone cells, providing a novel dimension for integrating multifunctional nanozymes.

To comprehensively regulate the microenvironment in bone defects, nanozymes can be integrated into functionalized scaffolds to form a multi-level collaborative system. For instance, the manganese mononuclear nanozyme (Mn/HSAE) functionalized bioceramic scaffold synergizes chemical kinetics and photodynamic therapy to eliminate bacteria and alleviate inflammation while establishing a stable microenvironment that supports bone conduction and induction functions. This integration boosts osteogenic marker expression and enhances new bone formation.145 Additionally, the composite platform of gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) and metal-polyphenol networks (MPN) responsively releases BMP-2 and Sr2+ in inflammatory acidic environments. Here, Sr2+ functions as a dual regulator of bone metabolism, promoting osteogenesis and inhibiting osteoclastogenesis, while the AuNPs enhance anti-inflammatory and vascular functions, remodeling the bone defect microenvironment.217–219 Moreover, the innovative Fe3O4 nanozyme, modified with vancomycin for targeted delivery, is encapsulated in an aldehyde-activated hyaluronic acid hydrogel. This design enables site-specific release at the infection site, facilitated by hyaluronidase secreted by bacteria. Concurrently, vancomycin targeting allows the nanozyme to penetrate biofilms and adhere to bacterial surfaces, significantly improving ROS utilization efficiency.220

In conclusion, researchers are progressively developing nanozyme-based regulatory systems that autonomously adapt to dynamic changes in the microenvironment during both the infection and repair stages, addressing the challenge of balancing antibacterial and regenerative functions in repairing infectious bone defects. Research has achieved timely coordination of antibacterial and angiogenic effects through catalytic activity optimization, morphological regulation, non-ROS-dependent antibacterial mechanisms, and intelligent response system integration. Importantly, the oxidative damage issue during electrical stimulation has been resolved through piezoelectric transfer regulation, expanding the application boundaries of nanozymes in creating multi-level collaborative regenerative microenvironments. Therefore, the design of nanozymes should prioritize precise alignment with pathological evolution patterns in both spatial and temporal dimensions. By coupling the intrinsic properties of materials with system integration, a dynamic balance and coordinated progress of antibacterial activity, angiogenesis, and bone regeneration can be achieved.

Challenges and Prospects: From Basic Research to Clinical Translation

Although nanozymes show significant potential in treating bone diseases, they face several challenges in transition from basic research to clinical application.221 The first challenge involves the inherent conflict among catalytic activity, structural stability, and biological safety, making it difficult to achieve an optimal balance.222,223 While catalytic activity is the core feature of nanozymes, excessively high activity may compromise structural stability and biocompatibility in complex biological environments.224,225 For example, nanostructures with high active surface areas, such as nanoflowers, may undergo aggregation, dissolution, or surface passivation under physiological conditions, leading to a significant reduction in activity. Moreover, enhancing catalytic efficiency by introducing metal elements, such as Cu or Mn, may increase the metabolic burden of metal ions and raise toxicity concerns if these elements remain localized for extended periods.226 Therefore, nanozyme design should not only pursue stronger catalytic activity but also consider whether such activity can be maintained safely and reproducibly in vivo. In this context, single-atom enzymes provide a promising direction for next-generation nanozyme optimization, because their atomically dispersed catalytic centers and tunable coordination environments may improve catalytic specificity, clarify structure–activity relationships, and reduce variability in complex biological microenvironments.227

From a clinical translation perspective, biosafety and pharmacokinetics represent two closely connected barriers. After administration, nanozymes may undergo protein corona formation, aggregation, degradation, ion release, or surface passivation, which can alter their biological identity, biodistribution, bone retention, circulation half-life, immune recognition, and clearance route. Therefore, future studies should move beyond short-term cytocompatibility assays and include systematic evaluation of long-term in vivo toxicity, immunogenicity, hemocompatibility, absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and degradation products. These analyses are particularly important for metal-containing nanozymes, because prolonged tissue retention may lead to gradual ion release and chronic local toxicity.67,68,228 In bone tissue, this issue becomes more complex. The highly mineralized ECM, relatively low vascular permeability, and slow bone remodeling kinetics may restrict nanoparticle penetration, diffusion, degradation, and clearance, especially in poorly vascularized deep bone regions.229,230 Because nanomaterial distribution is strongly affected by particle size, morphology, surface charge, hydrophilicity, protein corona formation, and biological barriers, quantitative pharmacokinetic analysis should be combined with radiolabeling, elemental mapping, and multimodal imaging to track the long-term biodistribution, degradation, and clearance of nanozymes in vivo.231,232

In the dynamic process of bone defect healing, existing targeted strategies—like bone targeting via zoledronic acid (ZOL) and cell targeting based on surface charge—exhibit low efficiency and are susceptible to off-target effects.233 Potential solutions include multiligand cooperative targeting and biomimetic strategies, such as cell membrane coating, which may enhance lesion recognition, immune evasion, and local retention.234,235 However, these approaches still need to adapt to the highly dynamic pathological microenvironment during bone healing. Many “intelligent response” systems only respond to a single signal and cannot accurately match the complex processes involving inflammation, repair, and remodeling. This limitation complicates the precise timing of drug release, which hinders the achievement of optimal therapeutic outcomes.236,237 To overcome this limitation, multi-stimuli-responsive systems and temporally controlled release strategies should be developed to coordinate nanozyme activity with different stages of bone repair. Nevertheless, truly spatiotemporally programmable nanozyme systems remain difficult to realize because pathological signals are heterogeneous, transient, and disease-stage dependent.

Another important issue is that current nanozyme-related studies tend to emphasize therapeutic benefits, whereas contradictory findings, limitations, and adverse outcomes remain insufficiently discussed. For example, metal-based nanozymes may exhibit excellent catalytic performance but also carry risks of metal ion release, tissue bioaccumulation, and incomplete clearance.68,238 In addition, excessive ROS scavenging may interfere with physiological redox signaling, which is essential for osteogenic differentiation, osteoclast activity, angiogenesis, and bone remodeling. The catalytic activity of nanozymes may also be attenuated or altered in vivo by protein corona formation, surface passivation, pH fluctuation, ion exchange, and dynamic changes in the inflammatory or metabolic microenvironment.239 These factors may partly explain why some nanozyme systems show strong activity in vitro but inconsistent efficacy in vivo. Thus, future studies should establish more balanced evaluation frameworks, including standardized catalytic assays, long-term toxicity assessment, batch-to-batch consistency analysis, and cross-material performance comparison.

A critical challenge is the lack of clinically relevant models to validate existing research findings.240 Most studies are limited to cell or small animal models, which differ significantly from humans regarding immune responses, metabolic pathways, bone size, mechanical loading, vascular architecture, and bone healing kinetics. Consequently, the antioxidant, osteogenic, and antibacterial effects observed in these models cannot be directly extrapolated to clinical settings.241,242 To improve translational reliability, future studies should incorporate large-animal models, such as sheep, pigs, or rabbits, with weight-bearing bone defects, infectious bone defects, osteoporotic bone defects, or diabetic bone defects.243 These models should be combined with longitudinal imaging, histological evaluation, blood chemistry, organ toxicity analysis, immune profiling, and long-term material fate tracking. Moreover, nanozymes may be more realistically translated as components of combined bone-regeneration strategies rather than as standalone therapies. For example, nanozyme-functionalized scaffolds can provide both structural support and active microenvironmental regulation; nanozyme-mediated ROS regulation may be integrated with stem-cell-based bone regeneration; controlled nanozyme delivery may support osteogenic-factor-based strategies such as BMP-2-mediated repair; and combination with anti-resorptive drugs may enable simultaneous inhibition of osteoclast activity and promotion of osteogenesis.60,62,64,66

Clinical translation is also constrained by regulatory and manufacturing challenges. For nanozyme-based products, market approval will require full life-cycle risk assessment, reproducible physicochemical characterization, and validated quality-control standards, including particle size distribution, morphology, surface chemistry, valence state, catalytic activity, degradation behavior, sterility, and endotoxin levels. Scalable synthesis under good manufacturing practice-compatible conditions, batch-to-batch consistency, storage stability, sterilization compatibility, and potency assays linking catalytic properties with biological outcomes are also necessary. Because many nanozyme systems integrate catalytic nanomaterials, metal ions, drugs, hydrogels, or scaffolds, their regulatory classification may overlap with drugs, medical devices, biologics, or combination products, making clinical translation more complex. Therefore, techno-economic analysis, raw material sourcing, production cost, environmental safety, and post-market monitoring should be considered at an early stage of development.67,244

Despite these challenges, the prospects for nanozymes in treating bone diseases remain promising. Overcoming current bottlenecks requires integrating multiple disciplines and utilizing advanced tools like machine learning and computational materials science to simulate and predict catalytic pathways, metabolic fate, and interactions between nanozymes and biological molecules in complex environments. This approach will facilitate rational design and optimization. Additionally, it is essential to deepen the understanding of the dynamic interaction mechanisms between materials and the biological microenvironment. Future nanozyme systems should be designed with improved targeting, controllable degradation, predictable pharmacokinetic behavior, standardized safety profiles, and regulatory feasibility. Such advancements will promote the clinical translation of nanozymes, offering new solutions for bone defects, osteoporosis, and other related bone diseases.

Conclusion

Bone regeneration is highly dependent on the dynamic balance of the immune-metabolic microenvironment. The central message of this review is that bone repair should not be viewed merely as the restoration of mineralized tissue, but as a coordinated process of microenvironmental reconstruction involving immune regulation, metabolic homeostasis, vascular support, infection control, and osteogenic remodeling. From this perspective, the immune-metabolic microenvironment represents both a pathological bottleneck and a key therapeutic target for complex bone diseases.

In recent years, nanozymes have emerged as promising catalytic platforms for bone regeneration. Their significance lies not only in their ability to scavenge ROS or suppress inflammation, but also in their capacity to integrate multiple regulatory functions within a unified therapeutic framework. By linking catalytic activity with immune modulation, metabolic correction, mitochondrial protection, angiogenesis, antibacterial action, and scaffold-assisted repair, nanozymes provide a strategy for transforming pathological microenvironmental cues into regenerative signals. This represents a conceptual shift from passive microenvironmental protection toward active, spatiotemporally coordinated microenvironmental remodeling.

Despite their broad application potential, nanozyme-based therapies for bone diseases remain largely at the preclinical stage. Future development should move beyond proof-of-concept efficacy and focus on mechanism-guided, disease-specific, and clinically oriented nanozyme systems. In particular, nanozymes may achieve greater translational value as components of integrated regenerative platforms rather than as standalone agents. A more balanced evidence base, including standardized assays, adverse-event reporting, and comparison with current clinical standards, will be essential for judging their true translational value. Overall, the future of nanozyme-assisted bone regeneration will depend on the development of intelligent, multifunctional, and clinically translatable systems capable of dynamically reshaping the immune-metabolic microenvironment throughout the entire process of bone repair.
