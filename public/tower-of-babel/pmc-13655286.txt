Risk Stratification or Disease Staging? Re-Evaluating the Evidence Base for Neoplastic Gallbladder Polyp Management
Authors: Huiting Liu, Xue Liu
Published: 2026

We read with considerable interest the recently published meta-analysis by Lee et al. [1] on ultrasound (US) features associated with neoplastic gallbladder polyps. As a foundational component of the 2025 Korean Society of Abdominal Radiology (KSAR) guideline development process [2], this work has had a substantial influence on shaping clinical practice. However, we wish to raise two fundamental concerns regarding the methodological foundation and clinical framework underlying these recommendations.

Firstly, the pooled sensitivity for size ≥10 mm (0.79) may be substantially inflated due to partial verification bias. Twenty-seven of the thirty included studies were retrospective. In such designs, clinical decision-making creates a powerful selection mechanism: polyps ≥10 mm are preferentially channeled toward cholecystectomy and pathological confirmation, whereas smaller lesions are overwhelmingly managed conservatively, with clinical stability interpreted as benignity. Neoplastic polyps <10 mm that were left unresected are thereby lost to the false-negative pool, and therefore remain invisible to the meta-analytic denominator. This systematically reduces the apparent false-negative rate and inflates sensitivity. The pooled specificity of 0.62 reflects the inherent non-specificity of the 10-mm threshold, as benign polyps ≥10 mm are frequently resected and thus are fully ascertained as false positives. However, given that without universal pathological verification or malignant-outcome tracking, the true false-negative rate among conservatively managed small polyps is unknowable, the sensitivity estimate is particularly vulnerable. Furthermore, 40% of studies were rated as having a high or unclear risk of bias in the QUADAS-2 [3] reference-standard domain, which can be ascribed to the inadequately described blinding between the index test and reference standard, as well as the fact that “pathology plus clinical follow-up” introduces outcome misclassification when neoplastic progression occurs beyond the observation window. Moreover, in the absence of correction for verification bias, using established methods such as those proposed by Begg and Greenes [4] and extended to the meta-analyses conducted by de Groot et al. [5], the claim that size ≥10 mm is the most sensitive screening feature remains methodologically uncertain.

Secondly, gallbladder wall thickening (GBWT) appears to be mischaracterized as a “risk-predicting” rather than “disease-staging” marker. According to epidemiological convention, screening markers for early disease detection typically show high sensitivity (to minimize false negatives), whereas staging or confirmation markers are characterized by high specificity (to rule in advanced disease) [6]. The pooled profile of GBWT (sensitivity 0.42 and specificity 0.91) aligns precisely with the latter pattern. The sensitivity of 0.42 indicates that 58% of neoplastic polyps occur without GBWT, further undermining its utility as a universal screening tool. In non-East Asian studies, GBWT specificity has been reported to reach 97.6%, thereby indicating that GBWT is documented almost exclusively when focal wall invasion or advanced disease has already been suspected. If GBWT is predominantly indicative of established local invasion rather than predictive of future neoplastic potential, then its inclusion, along with size ≥10 mm as an equally weighted “concerning imaging feature,” conflates two fundamentally different clinical tasks: early detection and the confirmation of advanced disease. Although methodologically convenient for algorithmic simplicity, the decision specified in the KSAR guidelines [2] to weight these features equally obscures their distinct positions along the disease time axis.

These two issues reveal a common flaw in the current “feature-list” approach to gallbladder polyp management, namely, that one marker (size ≥10 mm) may be inflated by selection bias, whereas another (GBWT) may be clinically mispositioned. Misclassifying a staging marker as a screening tool risks generating unnecessary urgency for advanced lesions, whilst simultaneously diluting surveillance resources for small polyps that actually harbor early neoplastic change. We suggest that future meta-analyses and guideline updates should 1) apply verification-bias correction methods when synthesizing retrospective cohorts, 2) require standardized definitions for morphological features such as GBWT, and 3) stratify US features by their clinical function. Rather than treating all statistically significant associations as interchangeable components of an undifferentiated risk-scoring algorithm, this approach would help distinguish screening markers (high sensitivity, intended for early detection) from staging or confirmation markers (high specificity, intended to identify established or locally advanced disease).

We believe that these considerations are essential prerequisites for translating meta-analytic associations into population-level management algorithms.
