Association between physical activity based on wearables and self-reported health status among adolescents: NHANES 2011–2014
Authors: Yan Gao, Qiming Zhang, Yiguo Wang, Yu Gao, Yang Xu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPhysical activity (PA) was strongly associated with health status, with fewer related studies in adolescents (12–19 years). Most current studies using questionnaires to assess PA levels were not objective enough. So, this study used a wearable device to assess PA levels quantitatively and focused on the association between PA levels and self-reported 4 health status among adolescents.MethodsData were obtained from adolescents (2241) with both PA and health status assessments from two cycles of NHANES, 2011–2014, using a wearable accelerometer-based device to assess PA levels quantitatively, MIMS-units as the metric, averaging over all valid days of wear (MIMS-units average). There were 4 health statuses, including (1) whether or not flu, pneumonia, or ear infection, (2) whether or not stomach or intestinal illness, (3) whether or not head cold or chest cold, and (4) general health condition, from the past 30 days self-reported. Weighted multiple logistic regression was used to analyze the association between the 4 health statuses and the MIMS-units average and MIMS-units average quartiles respectively. Subgroup analyses were also conducted on age, sex, BMI, and race.ResultsAfter controlling confounding factors, there was no significant correlation between the MIMS-units average and the four physical health conditions in Table 3 model 3. While MIMS-units average quartiles results showed that compared to lower PA levels (Q1), higher PA levels (Q4) were linked to a lower incidence of “flu”(OR = 0.94, 95% CI [0.91, 0.99], P < 0.001). Furthermore, Q3 was linked to a decrease in the incidence of “head cold” (OR = 0.91, 95% CI [0.85, 0.96], P < 0.001), while Q2 showed a decrease in “stomach illness” (OR = 0.95, 95% CI [0.92, 0.99], P = 0.003) in Table 3 model 3. Subgroup analyses showed that higher PA levels significantly reduced “flu” risk among 12–15 year-olds, girls, obese, and Non-Hispanic White; decreased “head cold” risk among 16–19 year-olds, boys, obese, and Non-Hispanic White; significantly lower risk of “stomach illness” in girls.ConclusionsEnhancing PA levels can help prevent flu, head cold, and stomach or intestinal illness in US adolescents. Further studies are needed to determine whether adolescents in other regions have similar results, and high-quality cohort studies are needed to further validate the causal association.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-20363-6.Keywords: Wearables, Physical activity, Self-reported health status, Adolescents, NHANES

Introduction

In 2008, the Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans recommended that adolescents aged 6 to 17 engage in at least 60 min of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity daily [1]. However, about 80% of this group was physically inactive [1]. The definition of adolescents varies among countries, and the WHO has extended the definition of adolescents to include individuals up to 19 years old. (https://www.who.int/zh/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/adolescents-health-risks-and-solutions). Physically active adolescents, in comparison to their inactive peers, exhibit higher levels of cardiorespiratory fitness, stronger muscles and bones, and lower body fat [1]. Additionally, they may experience reduced symptoms of anxiety and depression [1]. Research has established a robust connection between physical activity levels and various health outcomes, including obesity, cardiovascular health [2], type 2 diabetes [3, 4], colon cancer, and breast cancer [3]. Physical inactivity caused 9% of premature deaths [3]. Engaging in regular physical activity has been shown to enhance immune function and reduce the risk of developing numerous diseases [5].

Most current studies were questionnaire-based self-report to assess PA intensity, which suffered from the following limitations: (1) views on physical activity intensity varied from one subject to another, (2) time spent in activity quantified by recall was markedly subjective, and (3) adolescents might have had their physical activity data provided by proxies such as their parents, etc. that may be inaccurate. To obtain more objective PA data, the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) introduced the wearable-based Physical Activity Monitor (PAM) in 2003–2006 and 2011–2014, with the 2003–2006 PAM placed on an elasticized fabric belt and worn on the right hip, while the 2011–2014 PAM was placed on a mesh wristband (like a watch) and worn on the non-dominant hand. PAM was a wearable-based accelerometer device with optional evaluation metrics of monitor-independent motion summary units (MIMS-units) derived from raw triaxial acceleration data. Jone et al. have fully described the MIMS-units algorithm and made the algorithm code publicly available at: http://mhealthgroup.org/data-mims-unit [6]. MIMS is an open-source summary that provides a high-density acceleration measurement, which is generated using signal processing techniques to analyze normal human motion [6]. The raw data is organized into one-minute intervals for easier analysis [7]. Signal processing techniques involve several steps: coordinating raw signals to eliminate differences between instruments (such as differences in sampling rates), applying band-pass filtering (0.2–5.0 Hz) to exclude non-human motion, and aggregating signals to condense data, making visualization and summarization easier [6]. Previous study has been done to confirm that MIMS-units percentiles as a new method for characterizing total activity in a U.S. youth population [8]. The higher values of MIMS-units indicated a higher level of physical activity.

Currently, few studies are focusing on the association between PA and physical health status using this novel and objective indicator for assessing PA, especially in adolescents. And we also want to know whether there are differences in the distribution of PA levels across different health statuses, and which diseases are more likely to be prevented by higher levels of PA.

Methods

Study population

We specifically selected the 2011–2014 NHANES cycle because it not only provided PA level data based on wearables but also included crucial health status information for adolescents, which is absent in the 2003–2006 cycle. NHANES was a major program of the Centers for Disease Control (CDC) and Prevention’s National Center for Health Statistics, about 5,000 people annually as a nationally representative sample for health and nutritional screening among US adults and children. Sampling methods and weights can be obtained online at https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/data/series/sr_02/sr02_162.pdf, and the survey data was approved by the NCHS Ethics Review Board and available on the internet (https://wwwn.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/Default.aspx). Adolescents were aged 12–19 years old, and the included population needed to meet the following three criteria at the same time: PAM worn on the non-dominant hand, no missing values for the 4 health statuses variables, and contain at least 3 days validated PAM parameters in 7 consecutive wearing days. Finally, 2241 participants were included in the study, and the screening process is shown in Fig. 1.

The screening process for population inclusion

Wearable-based physical activity measurement

Each participant wore an accelerometer at 80 HZ per second (ActiGraph GT3X, ActiGraph Corporation, Pensacola, FL), which was placed on a mesh wristband (like a watch) and worn on the non-dominant hand. PAM is water resistant to 1 m for up to 30 min and the rechargeable battery provides power for 10 days between charges and participants wearing PAM for 7 consecutive days will be paid $40 [9].

PAM acceleration obtained on the x-, y-, and z-axes every minute were summarized into MIMS-units. MIMS triaxial values were obtained from PAXMTSD in the PAXDAY_G and PAXDAY_H files [10, 11]. We screened participants who wore PAM from days 2 to 8 and had at least three 24-h data sets. We excluded data from day 1 when participants began wearing PAM, and day 9, when it was removed, due to the lack of complete 24-h data. Then we calculated the average of MIMS-units per participant on valid days, which was the major variable in our study.

The 4 health status values

Current health status was obtained from the NHANES 2011–2014 questionnaire HSQ, which focused on the participant’s health status over the past 30 days, including general health status (HSD010), did he or she have a head cold or chest cold? (HSQ500), did he or she have stomach or intestinal illness? (HSQ510), did he or she have flu, pneumonia, or ear infection? (HSQ520). In the HSD010 questionnaire, health status is categorized as follows: “Positive” for answers of “Excellent,” “Very Good,” or “Good”; “Neutral” for “Fair”; and “Negative” for “Poor”. In questionnaires HSQ500, HSQ510, and HSQ520 who answered “Yes” were defined as symptomatic, while those who answered “No” were classified as non-symptomatic. Additionally, participants who answered “Don’t know” were identified as “not available (NA)”.

Covariates

Age, sex, race, education, and family income to poverty ratios (PIR) from demographic data and height, weight, and body mass index (BMI) from examination data were included as covariates. Age was categorized into two groups, 12–15 years and 16–19 years. Sex was divided into boys and girls. Race was categorized into Mexican American, Non-Hispanic White, Non-Hispanic Black, Other Hispanic, and Other/multiracial (including Non-Hispanic Asian and Other Race—Including Multi-Racial) 5 groups. Education included grades 4–6, grades 7–9, grades 10–12, high school diploma/general educational development certificate or equivalent, as well as young people with lower educational levels. PIR was categorized as low (0–1.3), middle (> 1.3–3.5), and high (> 3.5–5). Height (cm) and weight (kg) were both continuous variables. The categorization of BMI is extracted directly from the variable BMDBMIC (in the BMX file), not from our calculations.

Statistical analysis

In our study, 4-year weights were calculated and weighted analyses were used to better represent the 2011–2014 U.S. adolescents. Table 1 described each of the 4 health statuses distribution in different populations, where continuous variables were expressed as median/ lower and upper quartiles of the interquartile range, categorical variables were expressed as frequencies/weighted percentages, and Wilcoxon rank sum test (continuous variables) and chi-squared test (categorical variables) were used to compare the differences between symptomatic and non-symptomatic groups. Then we divided the MIMS units average into quartiles. Subsequently, we employed the chi-square test to analyze differences in the distribution of the four specific health statuses across these quartiles. Weighted univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were conducted between the MIMS-units average and the four health statuses, as well as the MIMS-units average quartiles, with the odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) as effect indicators for evaluating the association between MIMS-units average (or MIMS-units average quartiles) and the 4 health statuses. In the model, we included covariates that were statistically significant in “General health condition”. Model 1, unadjusted. Model 2 adjusted for age, sex, race, and PIR. Model 3 was further adjusted for BMI and education based on Model 2. In Model 3, subgroup analyses were conducted to examine the association between MIMS-units average quartiles and the four health statuses across various ages, sex, body mass index, and racial groups. We also assessed interactions to clarify whether the relationship between MIMS-units average quartiles and the four health conditions was influenced by age, sex, BMI, and race, to determine the robustness of the analyses. Data processing and analysis were performed using R (version 4.3.2), along with Zstats v1.0 (www.zstats.net), with P < 0.05 indicating statistical significance.

Results

The analysis was based on data that can be accessed in Supplement 1. A total of 2,241 adolescents responded to the 4 health status questionnaires, within the past 30 days, 69 had “Flu, pneumonia, or ear infection” (Hereafter abbreviated as “flu”, accounting for 3.2%), 331 had “Head cold or chest cold” (Hereafter abbreviated as “head cold”, account for 16%), 113 had “Stomach or intestinal illness” (Hereafter abbreviated as “Stomach”, account for 6%), and 19 were “Negative general health status” (account for 0.6%). The distribution of each physical health status among different populations was shown in Table 1, where the statistically significant factors in the general health condition groups were sex, weight, BMI, race, and PIR, P-values were all < 0.05, and those were the covariates that we included in the multiple logistic regression model. The more detailed results of Table 1 are shown in Supplementary 2. In Table 1, with “flu” and “head cold”, symptomatic adolescents had significantly lower MIMS-units average than non-symptomatic ones, P = 0.002 and P = 0.047, respectively. The chi-square values were P = 0.005 for “flu” and P = 0.016 for “head cold,” indicating that the distribution of “flu” and “head cold” was significantly different among all four MIMS-unit average quartiles (Table 2), and the means and standard deviations for each MIMS-unit average quartile were presented in Table 2. After controlling confounding factors, there was no significant correlation between the MIMS-units average and the four physical health conditions in Table 3 model 3. While MIMS-units average quartiles results showed that compared to lower PA levels (Q1), higher PA levels (Q4) were linked to a lower incidence of “flu”(OR = 0.94, 95% CI [0.91, 0.99], P < 0.001). Furthermore, Q3 was linked to a decrease in the incidence of “head cold” (OR = 0.91, 95% CI [0.85, 0.96], P < 0.001), while Q2 showed a decrease in “stomach illness” (OR = 0.95, 95% CI [0.92, 0.99], P = 0.003) in Table 3 model 3. Table 4 illustrated the effects of MIMS-units average quartiles on four health statuses in Model 3. This analysis was based on the findings from Table 3 and considers various populations grouped by age, sex, BMI, and race. 1) Q4’s impact on “flu” and “general health condition” compared to Q1; 2) Q3’s effect on “head cold” compared to Q1; 3) Q2’s influence on “Stomach” compared to Q1. The bold P-value indicated that compared to lower PA levels (Q1), higher PA levels (Q4) significantly reduced the risk of “flu” in 12–15 years old, girls, the obese, and Non-Hispanic White populations. Q3 significantly reduced the risk of “head cold” in 16–19 years old, girls, the obese and Non-Hispanic White populations, while Q2 significantly reduced the risk of “Stomach” issues in girls. The more detailed results are found in Supplementary 2. All P for interaction > 0.05 indicated that the effect of MIMS-units average quartiles on the four health states will not be affected by age, sex, BMI, and race, the results were robust.

Discussion

We found differences in the distribution of MIMS-units average quartiles between adolescents with “flu” and “head cold” (P = 0.005, P = 0.016, respectively, Table 2). Compared to lower levels of PA (Q1), Q4 had a significant negative correlation with “flu”, Q3 with “head cold”, and Q2 with “stomach illness” (Table 3). The results of the subgroup analyses further showed that higher levels of PA in specific populations still significantly reduced the risk of “the flu” (higher-level PA in adolescents aged 12–15 years, girls, obese, and Non-Hispanic White helped prevent “flu”), “head cold”, and “stomach illness”. Considering Tables 2 and 3, our study found that an average of MIMS-units for PA in adolescents was associated with lower risks of illness: 11959.36 (Q2) for “stomach illness”, 14726.79 (Q3) for “head cold”, and 18365.65 for “flu”.

Berendsen, B.A. et al. demonstrated a high correlation between accelerometer intensity (ActiGraphGT3X) and walking speed with higher user-friendliness scores [12]. There are currently several metrics based on accelerometer measurements of total PA, including steps per day (steps/d), total activity counts per day (TAC/d), physical activity energy expenditure (PAEE, kcal/kg/day), moderate-to-vigorous intensity physical activity (MVPA, min/d), and monitor independent movement summary units (MIMS-units/day) [13]. MIMS-units as a novel objective indicator metric for assessing PA can capture normal human movement, and its sensitivity to subtle wrist movements during sedentary behavior was high [6]. Several studies have been conducted using MIMS-units as an indicator for assessing PA [14, 15]. The ActiGraph GT3X, a mobile and sensitive device for objectively assessing PA levels, was introduced to NHANES in 2003–2006 and 2011–2014 and has been widely studied, with representative results [14, 16]. MIMS, Euclidean Norm Minus One (ENMO) [17], Mean Amplitude Deviation (MAD) [18], and Activity Intensity (AI) [19] are all open-source metrics derived from acceleration data. Marta Karas et al. [7] conducted a study to compare different accelerometer-based measures of PA. They analyzed data from 655 participants wearing the ActiGraph GT9 X in the Baltimore Longitudinal Study of Aging. The researchers compared activity counts (AC) in minutes to four metrics: MIMS, ENMO, MAD, and AI [7]. The results showed that AC had the highest correlation with MIMS at 0.988, the lowest mean absolute percentage error with MIMS at 2.5, and the highest accuracy in predicting sedentary minutes with MIMS at 0.981 [7].

Adolescents encounter various health issues, such as respiratory infections, gastrointestinal diseases, and anxiety, all of which significantly affect their well-being and quality of life. These health issues hinder daily activities and academic performance while also placing a heavy economic burden on families and healthcare systems. Research showed that health issues during adolescence can result in long-term negative effects [20]. “stomach illness”, “head cold” and “flu” are common illnesses that cannot be ignored in adolescents, seriously affecting their daily lives and studies. Chronic gastrointestinal diseases can cause malnutrition, hinder growth and development, and contribute to mental health issues like anxiety and depression. Additionally, insufficient physical activity can weaken gastrointestinal motility, resulting in functional dyspepsia, constipation, and abdominal pain [21]. A higher level of PA was negatively associated with “stomach or intestinal disease”, with a likely mechanism by which PA reduced the risk of intestinal disease involving the diversity of the gut microbiota [22]. Frequent colds can lead to school absenteeism and negatively impact academic performance in adolescents [23]. Furthermore, the influenza virus is highly contagious and can result in severe complications such as pneumonia and encephalitis. “Flu” and “head cold” are both respiratory infections caused by bacterial or viral infections. Evidence shows that PA reduces the risk of communicable diseases such as bacteria and viruses [24, 25], PA can enhance the body’s immune response to bacterial and viral antigens, slow down immune system aging [5], as well as reduce flu and pneumonia morbidity and mortality [22], fortunately, our study provides evidence to support these results. Engaging in physical activity is a simple and cost-effective part of everyday life. Encouraging adolescents to be more active will greatly benefit everyone, the state, and society. Published research showed that an increased level of physical activity was linked to an improved self-reported physical health status in adolescents [26], in which physical activity level was assessed based on questionnaires. Physical activity promotes growth and development in adolescents and helps reduce chronic and some communicable disease risks, as a cheap “medication” to prevent disease. We quantitatively assessed physical activity levels through a wearable acceleration device, which has higher accuracy than self-reported assessments, and our findings may to some extent enhance the awareness of physical activity among adolescents and their guardians.

However, a few limitations exist in the current study. Firstly, the cross-sectional study was not sufficiently clear on causality, and the possibility of bidirectional causality may exist, e.g., flu patients may have lower physical activity levels due to weakness. Secondly, the selection of confounders in our study was focused on demographics, with other confounders (e.g., dietary habits, genetics, etc.) associated with adolescents being more difficult to obtain. Thirdly, adolescents representative of the U.S. were selected, and further research is needed to determine whether there are similar findings in other areas. Finally, MIMS-units is a relatively new concept and with different studies using different methods and criteria, no direct evidence has been found for the conversion of MIMS Units to moderate-to-vigorous physical activity. This study collected data for the MIMS units using the ActiGraph GT9 X, however, it is uncertain if other acceleration devices would produce similar results.

In conclusion, the results indicated that a high level of PA was associated with a lower incidence of influenza, head cold, and stomach illnesses, among US adolescents. The MIMS-units is a novel indicator for measuring PA levels and requires further validation by both clinicians and wearable technicians.
