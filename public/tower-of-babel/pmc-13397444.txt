Decoding Chemical Resonance in π‑Conjugated Systems
Authors: Dariusz W. Szczepanik, Pawel A. Wieczorkiewicz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Understanding the electronic structure of (anti)­aromatic poly- and macrocyclic π-conjugated systems remains a central challenge in contemporary chemistry, given their importance in materials science, molecular electronics, photophysics, and catalysis. While magnetically induced ring currents and NMR-based descriptors have long served as key indicators of (anti)­aromatic character, the extent to which such magnetic observables reflect the full ground-state π-bonding architecture becomes less transparent in large and topologically complex systems. Here we introduce a wavefunction-based analytical protocol the π-Bond Delocalization Function (BDFπ) which maps the spatial organization of localized and delocalized π-bonding directly from the ground-state one-electron density matrix. Rather than relying on predefined resonance models or orbital localization schemes, the method extracts bonding topology from the complete occupied π-space. This enables a direct comparison between the global π-electron distribution and the more selective subset of frontier-orbital excitations that dominate magnetic response phenomena. Applied to representative poly- and macrocyclic systems frequently discussed in the context of global (anti)­aromaticity, BDFπ reveals that magnetic ring currents and bonding delocalization need not coincide quantitatively in extended conjugated frameworks. Magnetic descriptors predominantly probe perturbative response channels, whereas the underlying resonance structure reflects the collective organization of the entire occupied π-manifold. By explicitly relating these two perspectives, the present framework provides a coherent and chemically transparent account of how resonance topology and experimentally observable magnetic behavior are connected and where they may diverge in large conjugated architectures.

Introduction

The great promise of quantum chemistry is that the full structure of a moleculeits geometry, bonding, and even reactivityis encoded in its wavefunction. Yet, the degree to which this information can be translated into chemically intuitive bonding patterns remains uneven. The quantum-mechanical description of chemical bonding in π-conjugated (anti)­aromatic systems is particularly demanding for chemists accustomed to classical bonding models such as Lewis structures or resonance hybrids of the Kekulé or Clar type. − Despite decades of theoretical development, a conceptual gap persists between the formally rigorous language of quantum chemistry and the chemically intuitive representation of bonding patterns in systems featuring (anti)­aromatic (de)­stabilization. Numerous approaches have therefore been proposed to bridge this interpretative divide, seeking to reconstruct chemically meaningful delocalized bonding motifs directly from the calculated wavefunction or electron density in its unperturbed form. − In parallel, magnetic response-based techniques (including those based on induced current densities) − have been widely applied to probe the (anti)­aromatic character of molecules. ,

However, as emphasized throughout this work, it is important to distinguish between the intrinsic electronic structure of a molecule in its ground state and the behavior it exhibits under the influence of external perturbations. Within the standard approximations of molecular electronic structure theory (e.g., the Born–Oppenheimer and mean-field frameworks), the ground-state wavefunction defines the full spatial distribution and correlation of electrons across bonding domains, encoding chemically relevant information ranging from reactivity patterns to polarizability and magnetic response. Yet, the extent to which different parts of this electronic structure contribute to an observable response depends strongly on the energy scale and nature of the applied perturbation. In simple physical terms, the electrons that are most weakly bound and therefore easiest to ionize are also those most readily polarized by an external field. In the one-electron framework, this selectivity of response is reflected in the dominance of low-energy virtual excitations involving frontier orbitals. For example, in a π-conjugated annulene-type ring exposed to a weak external magnetic field, the induced diatropic or paratropic current does not arise from a collective, coherent motion of all (4n + 2)­π- or 4nπ -electrons. Rather, it is primarily governed by a limited set of low-energy occupied-to-virtual excitations within the π-manifold. As shown by Steiner and Fowler, − symmetry-based selection rules identify frontier π-orbitals (typically those near the HOMO–LUMO gap) as the dominant contributors to such ring currents. Magnetic-response-based indicators (such as ring current strength) therefore reflect a specific, perturbation-driven channel of the electronic structureone that is highly sensitive to symmetry and frontier-orbital topology. While these observables provide valuable insight into magnetic properties, they do not directly report on the full redistribution of electron density across all bonding domains that defines the underlying π-resonance structure.

To our knowledge, currently available theoretical frameworks do not provide a direct and computationally practical way to extract chemically interpretable π-resonance structures solely from the electron density or wavefunction. In particular, no existing approach enables the identification through inspection of a single, unified representation of the dominant resonance patterns governing the distribution of π-electrons in a conjugated system. Herein, we introduce the π-Bond Delocalization Function (BDFπ), a density-based analysis protocol designed to address this interpretative challenge. Building upon the bond-orbital projection formalism, , BDFπ operates at the level of chemically meaningful bonding units and provides a decomposition of the total one-electron density into localized and delocalized bonding contributions, in the spirit of the original density-fragmentation scheme proposed by Szczepanik. Rather than invoking heuristic resonance constructions, BDFπ quantifies the extent to which individual π-bonds retain localized (olefinic) character or participate in extended delocalization across the molecular framework. The resulting maps offer a chemically transparent visualization of the bonding pattern, highlighting regions dominated by localized double bonds, by distributed π-delocalization, or by more extended aromatic circuits.

By providing a direct connection between the ground-state electron density and the associated bonding motifs without requiring extensive orbital manipulation, BDFπ offers a practical and conceptually consistent tool for analyzing π-resonance structures in poly- and macrocyclic systems. The interpretative utility of BDFπ is illustrated below through applications to structurally diverse π-conjugated frameworks.

Theoretical Framework

In modern electronic structure theory, most qualitative discussions of molecular bonding and reactivity rely on the one-electron approximation, in which the complex, time-independent many-body Schrödinger equation is replaced by a set of effective one-electron equations. , Whether solved in the Hartree–Fock (HF) framework or within Kohn–Sham density functional theory (DFT), these equations yield a spectrum of one-electron wavefunctions known as molecular (spin-)­orbitals (MOs). − In the simple case of diatomic molecules, MOs can often be directly interpreted as bonding, antibonding, or nonbonding orbitals based on their nodal structure. In polyatomic systems, however, individual occupied MOs typically exhibit complex nodal patterns and extend over multiple atomic centers. The resulting electron density is obtained as a sum of orbital density contributions, and its spatial distribution reflects the cumulative bonding and antibonding character encoded in the occupied orbital manifold. In conjugated systems, certain orbitals display density distributions that extend over several adjacent bonds, giving rise to multicenter π-delocalization.

Identifying which parts of the ground-state electron density correspond to localized bonding patterns and which reflect extended delocalization is not straightforward in practice. To address this issue, over a decade ago, Szczepanik and coworkers introduced the Bond-Orbital Projection (BOP) technique, a mathematical framework that enables the extraction of bonding information directly from the one-electron density matrix. This is achieved through a cascade of projections and similarity transformations between canonical MOs and nonorthogonal two- and three-center bond orbitals, ultimately leading to a decomposition of the total electron density (ED) into chemically interpretable components. Specifically, the electron density is partitioned into two complementary density layers (Figure a): the Electron Density of Localized Bonds (EDLB), describing electrons that can be uniquely associated with localized bonding orbitals or lone pairs, and the Electron Density of Delocalized Bonds (EDDB), capturing electron density shared across adjacent or conjugated bonding domains. ,

a) Additive decomposition of the π-electron density (EDπ) in indene into EDDBπ and EDLBπ. b) Definition of the π-Bond Delocalization Function (BDFπ). c) Resonance bonding structures of indene in the representation of Clar and Kekulé forms.

Building upon this decomposition, we introduce the π-Bond Delocalization Function (BDFπ), defined as a differential π-density map constructed from the difference between the delocalized and localized π-bonding components (Figure b): 1 BDFπ=EDDBπ−EDLBπ=2×EDDBπ−EDπ

The BDFπ map provides a compact representation of the balance between localized and delocalized π-bonding contributions within the molecular framework. Positive values of BDFπ identify regions where the delocalized π-density (EDDBπ) exceeds the localized component, indicating participation of a given bond in extended conjugation. Negative values correspond to regions dominated by localized (olefinic) π-bond character, where EDLBπ prevails. Values close to zero may reflect either a significant contribution of ionic resonance forms or a near-compensation between localized and delocalized π-bonding contributions, as commonly observed in systems with competing bonding motifs, such as benzenoid and quinoid arrangements or migrating π-circuits.

Conceptually, BDFπ serves as a resonance-oriented bonding descriptor: it distinguishes between bonds that retain predominantly localized double-bond character and those that contribute to more extended π-delocalization across the molecular scaffold. Importantly, this information is obtained directly from the ground-state electron density matrix, without relying on predefined resonance structures, heuristic bonding assignments, or the adoption of a particular orbital representation. Since the one-electron density matrix remains invariant under unitary transformations of the occupied molecular orbitals, the resulting description of localized and delocalized bonding patterns is not contingent upon arbitrary orbital localization procedures.

Finally, it should be stressed that BDFπ is not designed as another scalar aromaticity index. Rather, it is a real-space representation of the balance between localized and delocalized π-bonding components. Scalar descriptors are useful when a single numerical measure is desired, whereas BDFπ addresses a complementary question: where in the molecule localized and delocalized bonding contributions dominate, compensate, or reorganize. The underlying EDDB component of this framework has already been systematically compared with established electronic aromaticity descriptors.

Computational Details

Geometry optimizations were performed using density functional theory in Gaussian 16, primarily at the CAM-B3LYP/6-31G­(d,p) level. − The only exception was the porphyrin nanoring, for which the ωB97X-D functional was used to provide a more reliable description of long-range exchange effects in this large π-extended system. All optimized structures, except for the most computationally demanding nanoring models, were confirmed as minima by vibrational frequency analysis.

The EDπ, EDDBπ, EDLBπ, and BDFπ scalar fields were generated from the corresponding first-order density matrices using the RunEDDB workflow, with NBO 7.0 and Multiwfn used for auxiliary wavefunction and grid manipulations. , The BDFπ function was obtained as the difference between the delocalized and localized π-bonding components, and the resulting cube files were visualized as isosurfaces. Full computational details, input files, and workflow examples are provided in the Supporting Information.

To assess the sensitivity of the conclusions to the level of theory, selected systems were additionally examined by comparing BDFπ maps generated from B3LYP and CAM-B3LYP density matrices and by evaluating relative energies using higher-level methods, including ωB97M(2)/def2-mTZVPP and, where computationally feasible, DLPNO-CCSD­(T)/CBS extrapolations. ,,, These tests support the use of long-range-corrected functionals for the present π-extended systems and show that the qualitative bonding patterns discussed below are not artifacts of a single computational setup.

Results and Discussion

Below we illustrate the interpretative and predictive utility of BDFπ through four structurally and electronically distinct systems that highlight the interplay between local and global π-delocalization at different length scales ranging from biradical polycyclic motifs, through macroheterocycles exhibiting dynamic sextet redistribution, to nanoscale conjugated rings commonly discussed in the context of global (anti)­aromaticity.

Breaking One Bond to Gain Two Sextets: S-ID

The fused indacene dimer s-indaceno­[2,1-a]-s-indacene (s-ID) was recently synthesized and characterized in its open-shell singlet (OSS) ground state. Based on magnetic response indicators, including anisotropy of the induced-current density (AICD), the authors described s-ID as globally antiaromatic in its closed-shell singlet (CSS) configuration and locally antiaromatic in its OSS ground state (Figure a). These classifications are well supported by the reported magnetic analyses. It is worth noting that the OSS state of s-ID corresponds to a high-purity diradical. Analysis of natural orbital occupation numbers (spinless density matrix) yields occupations of approximately 2.0e, 1.1e, and 0.9e for the three highest occupied natural orbitals, with the remaining virtual occupations close to zero. This occupation pattern is consistent with a nearly pure diradical configuration and suggests that strong multireference effects are not dominant in this case.

From the perspective of ground-state bonding, it is instructive to examine how the full 22π-electron density is distributed across the molecular framework in both electronic states. In light of the Fowler–Steiner selection rules, − the dominant ring-current response arises primarily from frontier-orbital contributions ((Figure b) and Figure S3). In the CSS state, the paratropic current is largely associated with a narrow window of the highest occupied π-orbitals, whereas in the OSS state the redistribution of spin density modifies this frontier manifold and alters the relative weight of the corresponding current-carrying channels. The remaining occupied π-orbitals, while less prominent in the magnetic response, still define the underlying bonding topology of the system.

Inspection of the full π-electron density and spin distribution already hints at distinct bonding scenarios in the two electronic states. However, such qualitative analysis depends sensitively on the chosen isovalue and does not directly quantify the balance between localized and delocalized π-bonding contributions. The BDFπ analysis therefore provides a more systematic and nonarbitrary description of the bonding topology: it shows not only where the π-electron density is located, but also whether a given region behaves more like a localized double bond or as part of a delocalized resonance pathway. We first consider the CSS state of s-ID, which lies 0.59 eV above the OSS state. The BDFπ surface indicates a largely localized π-bonding pattern, with only moderate delocalization in the central region (Figure c). This behavior can be rationalized by the π-topology of s-ID: the fused five-membered termini perturb the six-membered rings and reduce the number of low-energy Kekulé-type resonance arrangements available to the system. In chemical terms, the molecule cannot simultaneously accommodate two independent aromatic sextets without disrupting the bonding pattern imposed by the fused framework. In this configuration, the dominant contributors involve polarized (ionic) resonance forms (Figure d and Figure S1), enabling partial redistribution of π-electron density and the formation of a single migrating π-sextet that alternates between the two indacene fragments.

In regions where BDFπ approaches zero or changes sign, the apparent attenuation or “disappearance” of formal π-bonds should not be read as a loss of bonding interactions. Rather, it indicates that localized and delocalized π-bonding components nearly compensate each other, as expected when charge-resonance effects and ionic resonance forms become important. Thus, a near-zero BDFπ value is often chemically informative: it marks a region where the bonding pattern is neither purely olefinic nor fully delocalized, but is governed by competition between these two limiting descriptions. In this sense, such regions may be viewed as chemically sensitive parts of the molecule, where polarization, excitation, or structural perturbation can tip the balance toward either a more olefinic or a more aromatic bonding pattern. Simultaneous stabilization of sextets in both fragments is therefore topologically restricted, limiting the overall aromatic stabilization. The corresponding BDFπ pattern reflects this constrained delocalization superimposed on largely localized double bonds. Notably, the outermost π-bonds retain pronounced olefinic character, indicating limited participation in extended conjugation.

In the OSS state, the bonding picture changes qualitatively. Homolytic cleavage of a central π-bond effectively decouples the two indacene units, removing the topological constraint that prevented simultaneous Clar stabilization in the CSS configuration. The BDFπ map now reveals two pronounced delocalization domains corresponding to independently stabilized π-sextets within each indacene fragment (Figure c). The unpaired-electron density is localized predominantly in the five-membered rings (pink/blue isosurfaces), consistent with resonance structures that combine Clar sextets with π-conjugated radical centers (Figure d). In this sense, the OSS configuration accommodates a bonding arrangement that maximizes the number of disjoint aromatic sextets, in line with Clar’s rule.

A simple energetic estimate illustrates this balance. The cost of breaking a conjugated π-bond can be approximated as about 55 kcal/mol (by analogy with conjugated systems such as butadiene), whereas the formation of two Clar sextets provides an estimated stabilization of approximately 68 kcal/mol (2 × 34 kcal/mol). The resulting net gain of roughly 13 kcal/mol closely matches the computed OSS-CSS energy difference of 0.59 eV (ca. 13.6 kcal/mol). While this numerical agreement should not be overinterpreted, it supports a resonance-based rationale for the enhanced stability of the OSS state. Consistent trends are also observed in the bond-length alternation analysis (Figure S2).

From a broader viewpoint, the BDFπ analysis complements the magnetic classification by explicitly revealing how the π-electron density reorganizes between localized and sextet-stabilized arrangements under topological constraints. Rather than replacing magnetic descriptors, this perspective adds structural detail to the bonding patterns underlying the observed response. In s-ID, the OSS state emerges as the configuration that allows optimal redistribution of π-electron density into two stabilized sextets, providing a chemically intuitive explanation of its ground-state character.

Fully π-Conjugated Multicyclic Macrocycle

As a further illustration of the BDFπ approach, we consider the fully π-conjugated macrocycle 3TMC, recently synthesized by Ren et al. This architecturally sophisticated system, composed of three fused thiophene-based cycles, was electrochemically oxidized and spectroscopically characterized in both its neutral (CSS) and dicationic states (OSS). Based primarily on magnetically induced current-density analyses, the authors interpreted the electronic structure of 3TMC and its oxidized forms using the Hückel rule and Vogel’s heteroatom guideline. In this framework, the observed evolution of diatropic and paratropic current patterns upon oxidation was taken as evidence of a transition from a globally antiaromatic neutral state to a globally aromatic dication.

The BDFπ analysis (Figure and Figures S4–S11) provides a complementary perspective focused on the underlying resonance bonding structure. In its neutral state, 3TMC adopts a bonding pattern consistent with a Clar-like arrangement in which three disjoint π-sextets are stabilized within the thiophene subunits (Figure ). Although Clar’s rule was originally formulated for benzenoid hydrocarbons, its topological principle extends naturally to heteroaromatic systems, where π-sextet stabilization arises from combined bond and charge delocalization. While the aromatic stabilization energy per thiophene ring is lower than in benzenoids, the tendency to form disjoint sextets remains a dominant organizing principle of the π-bonding network. This preference can be rationalized by the greater energetic efficiency of localized sextet stabilization in smaller rings compared to extended annulenoid circuits. In simpler chemical terms, the π-system first uses the most efficient local stabilization channels before it can reorganize into a larger, macrocyclic delocalization pathway.

Formal resonance forms associated with 0 and 10+ oxidation states of 3TMC and BDFπ maps of 3TMC at oxidation states 0, 2+, 4+, 6+, 8+, and 10+.

The BDFπ isosurface for neutral 3TMC reveals locally stabilized thiophenoid π-sextets (BDFπ > 0) together with olefinic regions (BDFπ < 0), in agreement with this Clar-type topology. Upon oxidation to the +2 state, the distribution of π-delocalization changes, with enhanced delocalization emerging along two of the 24-membered peripheral circuits, while the third retains a more localized character at the outer bithiophene segment. Correspondingly, the number of distinct thiophenoid sextet domains decreases from 12 to 8.

At higher oxidation states, progressive charge removal promotes further redistribution of π-electron density along the macrocyclic periphery. In the hypothetical 3TMC10+ state, the BDFπ map indicates a largely uniform π-delocalization across the conjugated framework, with no clearly defined localized thiophenoid sextets. A more quantitative EDDB analysis (Figure S11) suggests that complete uniformity of delocalization may still not be strictly achieved; nevertheless, the bonding pattern becomes increasingly global in character as oxidation proceeds. Thus, even at high oxidation, the relevant question is not whether the molecule is simply aromatic or antiaromatic as a whole, but how far the π-bonding pattern has progressed from local sextet stabilization toward macrocyclic coherence.

It is important to emphasize that such highly charged multicyclic systems represent a distinct bonding regime compared to neutral archetypal aromatics such as benzene. In heavily oxidized states, extended π-delocalization is strongly influenced by charge redistribution across the molecular scaffold. The BDFπ analysis therefore highlights that the nature of π-delocalization in these macrocycles evolves continuously with the oxidation state, and that magnetic classifications and bonding-topology analyses may emphasize different but complementary aspects of this evolution.

Aza-Supernaphthalene

Among the π-conjugated macrocycles reported in recent literature, oxidized aza-supernaphthalene (Figure ) provides an instructive example of how extended conjugated frameworks accommodate both global charge redistribution and localized aromatic stabilization. This macrocyclic analogue of naphthalene, synthesized by Chi and coworkers, exhibits a remarkably persistent pattern of discrete Clar-type π-sextets even under progressive oxidation.

Clar structure of the neutral aza-supernaphthalene and BDFπ maps of aza-supernaphthalene across successive oxidation states (0, 2+, 4+, 6+, 8+, 10+), illustrating the gradual erosion of fixed π-sextets and the emergence of migrating π-sextet domains and charge delocalization.

In its neutral form, aza-supernaphthalene supports nine well-defined Clar π-sextets (corresponding to an estimated cumulative stabilization of approximately 9 × 34 = 306 kcal/mol), together with two peripheral quinonoid domains. Upon oxidation to the dicationic closed-shell singlet state, the bonding framework reorganizes while largely preserving eight π-sextets, with four central rings engaging more actively in charge-assisted π-delocalization. However, the open-shell singlet configuration is calculated to be approximately 10 kcal/mol lower in energy than the closed-shell form. Importantly, this OSS state retains to a large extent the sextet-based resonance organization of the neutral system, with unpaired electrons localized primarily in peripheral quinonoid units and only modest perturbation of the outer benzenoid domains.

Further oxidation leads to a gradual evolution of the bonding topology. In the 4+ state, seven π-sextets remain clearly identifiable, while several benzenoid domains assume an intermediate character, accommodating increased charge delocalization. In the 6+ state, six Clar sextets persist as dominant structural motifs, whereas four rings exhibit enhanced flexibility of π-bond and charge distribution, reflected by near-zero BDFπ values in those regions. Thus, near-zero BDFπ regions are not empty or chemically inactive areas; they mark parts of the molecule where the bonding pattern becomes flexible and can switch between more localized and more delocalized resonance descriptions. Only at higher oxidation levels (8+ and 10+) does the redistribution of positive charge become sufficiently extensive to promote a more uniform delocalization pattern across the macrocyclic framework.

Taken together, this progression reveals a pronounced tendency of the system to preserve localized Clar-type resonance domains up to relatively high oxidation states. The structural framework appears to favor maintaining discrete aromatic islands, even as global charge delocalization increases. This behavior contrasts with systems such as 3TMC10+, where oxidation more readily leads to extended delocalization along the macrocyclic periphery. In aza-supernaphthalene, local aromatic stabilization within benzenoid units remains a dominant organizing principle over a broad redox window.

The magnetic response analysis reported in the original study classifies the successive oxidation states (0, 2+, 4+, 6+) as nonaromatic, aromatic, antiaromatic, and aromatic, respectively, based on shielding patterns and current-density considerations. The BDFπ results do not contradict these observations; rather, they offer an additional bonding-topology perspective. While magnetic criteria probe the field-induced response dominated by frontier-orbital pathways, the BDFπ maps highlight how the full π-electron density organizes into localized and partially delocalized domains in the absence of external perturbation. Viewed together, these analyses suggest that magnetic classifications and resonance-based bonding descriptions may emphasize different structural features of the same electronic framework, particularly in large and multiply oxidized macrocycles.

Porphyrin Nanoring

While many polycyclic systems organize their π-electrons through localized Clar-type sextets, extended nanoscale architectures introduce a qualitatively different structural question: can multiple aromatic subunits couple in such a way as to establish a continuous delocalized pathway around an entire macrocycle? In porphyrin nanorings, individual 18π-electron porphyrin units (here 18π does not necessarily imply a strict “4n + 2” count in the Hückel sense) serve as prearomatized building blocks. Global delocalization, if present, must therefore arise from an effective resonance pathway that connects the aromatic porphyrin units around the nanoring. In other words, the key question is not whether formal double bonds can be drawn along the perimeter, but whether the bonding pattern actually supports a continuous resonance circuit spanning the whole macrocycle.

The nanoscale manifestation of aromaticity has been investigated in depth by Anderson and coworkers through an elegant series of experimental and computational studies. − These works introduced porphyrin nanorings of increasing size, linked via butadiyne bridges, and examined across multiple oxidation states. Magnetic response descriptors, including NICS(0)iso values and current-density analyses, were combined with formal π-electron counting arguments to classify particular oxidation states as globally aromatic or antiaromatic. For example, the 6+ oxidation state of the 12-porphyrin macrocycle c-P12­[b12] was described as globally aromatic, while the 8+ state was assigned antiaromatic character (Figure b), with the strongest diatropic response observed for the former.

a) BDFπ map for the neutral porphyrin nanoring c-P12­[b12]; b) BDFπ and NICS(0)iso maps for c-P12­[b12] across synthesized cationic forms.

These interpretations are supported by carefully executed NMR experiments and state-of-the-art electronic-structure calculations. At the same time, the reported aromatic stabilization energies (ASEs), typically on the order of 1–2 kcal/mol, indicate that the energetic signature of global delocalization in such extended systems is subtle. For comparison, even simple conjugated systems such as butadiene exhibit resonance stabilization of several kilocalories per mole, − and classical annulenes display significantly larger energetic effects. In large and conformationally flexible macrocycles, energetic contributions arising from strain, torsion, solvation, and thermal motion may therefore compete with or partially mask the intrinsic electronic stabilization associated with global conjugation.

The BDFπ analysis of c-P12­[b12] considered here without the central spoke scaffold and after full structural relaxation provides a complementary bonding-topology perspective (Figure ). In the neutral state, the nanoring consists of twelve locally aromatic porphyrin units connected by butadiyne linkers. The BDFπ map reveals well-defined delocalization within each porphyrin core, while the linker segments retain predominantly olefinic character. This description is fully consistent with the experimentally observed predominance of locally aromatic units.

Upon oxidation to the 6+ state, the first experimentally isolated and fully characterized oxidized form beyond the neutral system, the BDFπ maps indicate enhanced delocalization along several linker segments, accompanied by partial redistribution of π-electron density toward the macrocyclic periphery. Nevertheless, certain butadiyne bridges maintain significant localized character, interrupting complete resonance continuity around the ring. Even in the most highly oxidized experimentally characterized states (up to 12+), the delocalization pattern remains structured rather than fully uniform, suggesting that geometric and orbital-alignment constraints influence the extent to which porphyrin units can couple into a single global π-system.

Magnetic response descriptors and BDFπ maps thus illuminate different, yet equally meaningful, aspects of the same electronic structure. While magnetic criteria probe the field-induced response dominated by frontier-orbital transitions, BDFπ reveals how the full π-electron density organizes into localized and partially delocalized bonding domains in the absence of external perturbation. In extended nanorings, these perspectives need not coincide quantitatively  nor should they be expected to. Indeed, one of the central motivations behind the porphyrin nanoring studies by Anderson and coworkers has been to explore the limits of long-range π-coherence in large conjugated architectures, particularly in the context of charge and energy transport. From this viewpoint, magnetically induced global currents provide a sensitive probe of whether a subset of frontier π-electrons can circulate coherently along an extended pathway. Such field-induced π-coherence is highly relevant for understanding transport phenomena and emergent collective behavior in nanoscale systems. At the same time, the BDFπ analysis shows that this dynamic coherence does not necessarily imply uniform bond delocalization across the entire π-framework. Even when magnetically active electrons participate in global circulation, the majority of the π-electron density may remain organized into locally stabilized bonding domains governed by Clar-type or related resonance principles. In this sense, magnetic response and ground-state bond organization describe complementary dimensions of molecular electronic structure: the former emphasizes response pathways of energetically accessible electrons, while the latter captures the dominant bonding topology that determines thermodynamic stabilization.

Rather than being contradictory, these views together offer a more complete chemical picture. Magnetic descriptors inform us about transport-relevant electronic pathways and field-induced coherence, whereas BDFπ provides insight into the underlying resonance architecture that shapes stability and reactivity. For the rational design of large conjugated systems where both thermodynamic robustness and functional response properties are essential, integrating these complementary perspectives may be particularly valuable.

Conclusions

The resonance structure of π-conjugated systems remains one of the most conceptually rich, yet experimentally indirect, aspects of chemical bonding theory. Measurable properties such as magnetic response provide valuable and highly sensitive probes of molecular behavior, particularly with respect to frontier-orbital transitions and field-induced current pathways. At the same time, these observables reflect a specific perturbative channel of the electronic structure and do not necessarily fully capture the underlying organization of π-electron density that governs thermodynamic stabilization and bonding topology.

In this work, we have introduced the π-Bond Delocalization Function (BDFπ), a wavefunction-based framework that maps the distribution of localized and delocalized π-bonding contributions directly from the ground-state electron density matrix. By analyzing how the occupied molecular orbitals collectively shape bonding domains across a conjugated framework, BDFπ provides a chemically transparent representation of resonance structures without invoking predefined resonance formulas or orbital localization schemes. The method allows one to identify dominant bonding motifs and to follow their evolution across different oxidation states and topologies.

Across a diverse set of systems, from polycyclic diradical motifs governed by Clar-type stabilization, through oxidation-driven reorganization in macrocycles, to porphyrin nanorings at the nanoscale, a consistent picture emerges. The extent and pattern of π-delocalization are determined not solely by formal electron count, but by the interplay of molecular topology, energetic balance, and the availability of stabilizing bonding arrangements. In many cases, Clar-type organization of π-sextets remains a central stabilizing principle even when global magnetic response suggests extended current pathways. Stated more simply, BDFπ helps to distinguish between where a current may flow under an external magnetic field and where the ground-state π-electron density actually forms stabilizing bonding patterns. This distinction is particularly important in large conjugated molecules, where local resonance stabilization and global magnetic response may point to different, but complementary, aspects of the same electronic structure.

Importantly, BDFπ does not aim to reproduce magnetic observables directly; rather, it analyzes the same underlying electronic structure that gives rise to those observables. When used alongside experimentally validated electronic-structure methods, it offers complementary insight into bonding topology that is not directly accessible through perturbative response measurements such as NMR. Magnetic descriptors and density-based resonance analysis thus illuminate different, but interconnected, dimensions of molecular electronic structure.

We anticipate that integrating these complementary perspectives will facilitate a more nuanced understanding of aromaticity in extended π-systems, particularly in regimes where transport properties, thermodynamic stability, and bonding architecture do not align in a simple one-to-one fashion. In this sense, BDFπ provides a framework not for replacing existing criteria, but for enriching the chemical interpretation of resonance and delocalization in complex conjugated molecules.
