Effects of planting density and foliar fertilization on the phyllosphere microbial community of dual-purpose soybean
Authors: Qingbiao Xu, Mingyue Huang, Qingfu Huang, Ping Xiong, Mengnan Lu, Caihuan Deng, Qiongmei Song, Yuanyan Meng, Dan Wu, Liuxing Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Soybean (Glycine max (L.) Merr.) is a dual-purpose crop (grain and forage) with considerable economic and nutritional value in modern agriculture. Despite extensive research on soybean cultivation, the synergistic effects of planting density and foliar fertilization on the phyllosphere microbial community structure and its functional diversity remain poorly understood, particularly in dual-purpose soybean production. This study was conducted as a factorial experiment combining three growing seasons (2022, 2023 and 2024), two planting densities (high: 22 × 104 plants ha− 1; low: 10 × 104 plants ha− 1), four fertilization regimes (LCK, no fertilizer; LWF, standard basal fertilizer; LFN, basal + foliar N; LFP, basal + foliar P) and two maturity stages (R4, pod 3/4-inch long at one of the four uppermost main-stem nodes; R8: 95% of pods exhibiting mature pod color) on the phyllosphere microbial community and dual-purpose (grain and forage) soybean yield. The results showed that the fresh matter yield of the high-density treatment significantly increased by 29.7% compared with that of the low-density treatment (P < 0.05), and the grain yield increased by 20.2% (P > 0.05). Compared with the no fertilizer control, LWF (standard basal fertilizer) was the most effective fertilization treatment under high-density conditions, with its harvest index increasing significantly by 14.6%. The phyllosphere phosphorus content at the R8 stage (3.19 mg g− 1) was lower than that at the R4 stage (3.88 mg g− 1). Low-density planting increased the relative abundance of pathogens such as Alternaria and Fusariella. In contrast, high-density planting enhanced the relative abundances of potentially beneficial bacteria—including Sphingomonas, Quadrisphaera, Methylobacterium–Methylorubrum, and Pseudomonas— and decreased the relative abundance of the pathogen Pantoea. Considering the dual-purpose (grain and forage) yield, plant health performance, and the risk of late-season leaf phosphorus deficiency, we recommend implementing relatively high-density planting combined with foliar phosphorus fertilization in production systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07773-3.

Introduction

Recently, the rapid growth of the global population and accelerated development of animal husbandry have heightened interest in dual-purpose (grain-forage) crops that can produce both human food and livestock feed from a single variety [1]. Soybeans are nutritionally rich, with crude protein contents of 35–40% [2] and crude fiber contents of 13.7–16.5% [3]. In Europe, soybean acreage keeps expanding: output alone jumped more than 3.55-fold from 2008 to 2017 (7.6 × 105 t − 2.7 × 106 t) [4]. In 2020, soybean production in China reached 1.96 × 107 t, while imports totaled 1.003 × 108 t [5]. Cultivating diversified soybean varieties tailored to multifaceted production needs (grain or feed) can greatly enhance their utility and marketability [6]. Stems and leaves remain viable for livestock feed production, helping producers mitigate losses and sustain their livelihoods. However, current research on the production performance of dual-purpose soybean remains relatively limited, with a lack of systematic data support and practical guidance. This has, to some extent, constrained its promotion and application in grain–forage dual-use systems. In actual production, soybean yield (grain and biomass) and quality are regulated by multiple factors, including genetic characteristics [7], and agronomic management practices [8]. Among these factors, planting density [9] and fertilizer type [10] are key variables influencing crop yield. Reasonably controlling planting density and optimizing fertilizer combinations can enhance plant biomass and yield components while also regulating the phyllosphere microenvironment. For instance, biofertilizers positively and indirectly affect yield and quality by altering leaf microbiota [11]. This regulation can reshape the structure and functional diversity of phyllosphere microbial communities. Furthermore, studies have shown that plant density affects the structure of both epiphytic and endophytic fungal communities on the leaf surface [12]. However, the synergistic mechanisms between these microbial communities (phyllosphere microorganisms) and their host plants remain underexplored.

The aboveground parts of terrestrial plants are colonized by diverse microbes forming the phyllosphere microbiota [13]. The phyllosphere microbiota is a key constituent of the plant-associated microbial ecosystem, fulfilling essential ecological functions in modulating host development and sustaining plant fitness. Specifically, the microbial community—comprising archaea, bacteria, fungi, cyanobacteria, and viruses—that inhabits the phyllosphere (stem and leaf surfaces) [14, 15] promotes nutrient uptake, enhances pathogen defense, and improves plant adaptation to abiotic stresses [15, 16]. Inoculation with Sphingomonas spp. greatly improves soybean growth performance, as evidenced by increased shoot elongation and biomass accumulation [17]. Bacillus spp. enhance soybean growth and confer disease resistance against Rhizoctonia solani and Fusarium oxysporum by activating key defense-related enzymes, including phenylalanine ammonia-lyase, peroxidase, and polyphenol oxidase [18]. The positive effects of beneficial phyllosphere microorganisms on crop production are unequivocal. Previous studies have indicated that plant growth-promoting bacterial application to foliage improves the growth and nutritional status of maize (Zea mays) [19], while the foliar application of Pseudomonas stimulates strawberry (Fragaria × ananassa) growth, thereby increasing yield [20]. Nevertheless, the effects of agricultural practices, such as fertilization, on phyllosphere microbial communities remain poorly understood. Studies have shown that foliar fertilization alters the rhizosphere and phyllosphere microbiome of citrus, thereby increasing beneficial bacteria, decreasing pathogens, and substantially improving overall fruit yield [21]. By directionally regulating the structure of the leaf microbial community, the colonization of beneficial microbes in the phyllosphere and production performance can be enhanced. In this context, exploring the regulation of the phyllosphere microbial community is essential for maintaining plant health and improving crop yield.

Despite extensive research on increasing grain yield in crops, systematic investigations into whole-plant biomass optimization in dual-purpose crops remain limited. Previous studies have confirmed that optimized fertilization and adjusted planting density improve soil microorganisms and substantially increase crop yield [22–25]. However, research on phyllosphere microbial communities is rarely addressed, particularly regarding the potential impacts of the synergistic interaction between foliar fertilization and planting density on species composition, phyllosphere microorganism abundance, and crop yield in dual-purpose soybean crops.

Based on the above research background, we conducted a three-year field experiment (2022–2024) to investigate the interactive effects of fertilization method and planting density on soybean phyllosphere microbial communities and on grain and forage yields. Furthermore, we monitored the structural and nutritional changes in the leaves during critical maturity stages (R4: a pod 3/4 inch (2 cm) long at one of the four uppermost nodes on the main stem and R8: 95% of pods exhibiting mature pod color) [26] to offer theoretical foundations and technical parameters for the scientific cultivation of dual-purpose soybeans. Consequently, the following hypotheses were proposed for this study: (1) in contrast to low density, high density would increase the fresh and dry matter of the entire soybean plant and grain yield; (2) under high-density conditions, phyllosphere microbial diversity would decrease, whereas the number of beneficial microbes would increase; and (3) the standard basal fertilizer + foliar phosphorus fertilizer (LFP) and standard basal fertilizer + foliar nitrogen fertilizer (LFN) treatments would have a higher relative abundance of beneficial phyllosphere microorganisms and increased soybean yield than those observed for the no fertilization throughout the growth period (LCK) treatment.

Materials and methods

Site description

The experimental site was set up in the experimental field of Zhaotong University (Zhaoyang District, Zhaotong City, Yunnan Province; 27°36′ N, 103°74′ E; 1,985 m altitude). Data from the Zhaotong Meteorological Bureau indicate an average annual temperature of 12.3 °C and annual total rainfall of 682 mm over the past 20 years. The total rainfall and average temperature during the crop growth periods of 2022 (season 1), 2023 (season 2), and 2024 (season 3) were 512.7 mm and 18.8 °C, 483.5 mm and 20 °C, and 601.7 mm and 20.1 °C, respectively (Figure S1). Initially, the experimental field exhibited the following parameters: soil pH, 6.43; organic matter content, 20.53 g kg− 1; alkali-hydrolyzable nitrogen, 25.01 mg kg− 1; available phosphorus, 42.16 mg kg− 1; and available potassium, 57.71 mg kg− 1. After harvesting the wheat, soybeans were planted, followed by a continued rotation with wheat.

Experimental design and crop management

The experiment was structured as a randomized block design incorporating four factors: three years (2022, 2023, and 2024) × four fertilization types [LCK, standard basal fertilizer (LWF), LFN, and LFP] × two planting densities [22 × 104 plants hm− 2 (High) and 10 × 104 plants hm− 2 (Low: Locally recommended seeding rate)] × two maturity stages (R4 and R8).

LCK: no fertilizers were applied throughout the entire growth period; LWF: a standard compound fertilizer (urea, with 46% nitrogen content) was applied solely at the soybean (Dian Dou No. 7, Yunnan Huitong Seed Industry Co., Ltd.) seedling emergence stage (when 50% of the plants emerged) at a rate of 60 kg hm⁻2; LFN: fertilization at the seedling emergence stage was the same as that of LWF, while foliar nitrogen fertilizer was sprayed at the flowering stage (when 50% of the plants were flowering, approximately 45 days after sowing) at a rate of 12 L hm− 2; LFP: fertilization at the seedling emergence stage was the same as that of LWF, while foliar phosphorus fertilizer (monopotassium phosphate, with P2O5 ≥ 52% and K2O ≥ 34%) was sprayed at the flowering stage at a rate of 15 kg hm− 2, dissolved and sprayed. Two planting densities were set: a low density of 10 × 104 plants hm− 2, row spacing of 40 cm, and plant spacing of 20 cm; a high density of 22 × 104 plants hm− 2, row spacing of 30 cm, and plant spacing of 10 cm. The land was plowed 15 d prior to sowing (to a depth of 25 cm), crop residues were removed, and the land was leveled. Manual cultivation was performed with seeds broadcast on the soil surface and subsequently covered with 3 cm of soil. Thinning was performed 10 d post-emergence to retain healthy plants. Each treatment consisted of three randomly arranged replicate plots, each with an area of 12 m2 (3 × 4 m). A 0.5-m-wide buffer strip was established between each plot to prevent interference between the different treatments. Soybeans were manually sown from May to June each year and harvested in September of the same year (Table 1). Foliar fertilizer spraying was conducted on windless and sunny evenings via a backpack electric sprayer (model 3WBD-16, with a droplet size of 80–120 μm). During the entire growth period, the irrigation volume and schedule were consistented across all treatments, and weeds were manually removed without applying herbicides or pesticides.

Agricultural management

Sample collection

Samples were collected at the pod-setting and maturity stages of the soybeans. On sunny mornings devoid of dew, healthy plants were randomly chosen from each plot, and the third to fifth fully expanded trifoliate leaves (free from disease spots and mechanical damage) were collected from the middle section of the plants. The leaves were categorized into two parts: the first part, approximately 50 g of fresh leaves, was immediately placed into a sealed bag for assessing water retention, stomatal density, epicuticular wax content, and contact angle; the second part was transferred to a −20 ℃ freezer for analyzing leaf physicochemical properties. At the maturity stage, healthy plants were randomly selected from each plot under sterile conditions. The third to fifth fully expanded trifoliate leaves (excluding areas with leaf margin damage) were excised using sterilized scissors, placed in a foam box, and transported to the laboratory for storage in liquid nitrogen prior to microbial diversity analysis. On sunny mornings, an LI-6800 portable photosynthesis system (LI-COR, Lincoln, Nebraska, USA) was used to quantify the net photosynthetic rate (A), transpiration rate (E), intercellular CO2 concentration (Ci), and stomatal conductance of water vapor (GSW) in mature leaves (from the same position) of soybeans. At the maturity stage, following sample collection, all plants within each plot were harvested and threshed. The 100-grain weight and fresh matter yield per unit area were subsequently determined. Following fresh weight measurement, a portion of the plants was dried at 70 ℃ for 48 h to determine the dry matter content. These data were then used in combination with fresh weight data to calculate the dry matter yield.

Measurement methods

Structural and physiological characteristics

Photosynthetic parameters were assessed utilizing the LI-6800 portable photosynthesis system (taking care to avoid the leaf veins during measurements) with the following settings [27]: flow rate, 500 µmol s− 1; relative humidity, 50%; CO2 concentration, 400 ppm; rotation speed, 10,000 rpm; and light intensity, 1,500 µmol m− 2 s− 1. The water retention capacity of the leaves was determined based on the dry weight and fresh weight [28]. Stomatal density on the adaxial and abaxial leaf surfaces was measured through impressions made with transparent nail polish [29]. Leaf wax content was determined using an analytical balance [30], with minor modifications. Briefly, leaves of a similar mass were weighed, immersed in a small amount of chloroform for 30 s, and subsequently removed. The wax content was determined using an analytical balance after the complete evaporation of chloroform. The contact angles of the leaves were measured using an OCA20 contact angle meter (Dataphysics, Germany). Specifically, the leaf was cut into small strips measuring 0.4 cm × 5 cm, avoiding the leaf veins, and then fixed on a glass slide using double-sided tape to ensure a flat orientation. The upper and lower leaf surface contact angles were measured using the sessile drop method [31].

Chemical properties

Soluble protein content was determined using Coomassie Brilliant Blue G-250 staining solution [32]. The total phenolic content was assessed via the Folin–Ciocalteu reagent method [33]. The reducing sugar content was determined using the 3,5-dinitrosalicylic acid (DNS) method [34]. The soluble sugar content was measured according to the method recommended by [35]. Free amino acid content was determined via the ninhydrin method by measuring the absorbance of the sample at 570 nm [36]. Inorganic phosphate content was quantified using the molybdenum blue colorimetric method [37]. All above indices were measured with a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Jingsheng Analytical Instruments Co., Ltd., Chengdu, China).

Bacterial and fungal diversity

Determining microbial abundance: high throughput sequencing was used to analyze the bacterial and fungal communities. Genomic DNA was extracted from each sample (with three replicates) following the instructions of the E.Z.N.A.® palnt DNA kit (Omega Bio-tek, Norcross, GA, U.S.). After extraction, a TBS-380 fluorometer was used to measure the DNA concentration to ensure that the sample quantity met the requirements for subsequent experiments. Meanwhile, the DNA purity was assessed using a NaNoDrop Op2000 (Thermo Scientific, USA), while the DNA integrity was verified through 1% agarose gel electrophoresis to exclude degraded or contaminated samples. For bacteria, the V3–V4 regions of the 16 S rRNA genes were amplified via polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using the DNA extracted as template and specific bacterial primers of 338 F and 806R (F: 5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCA-3′; R: 5′- GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′). For fungi, the ITS1 regions were amplified by PCR using the DNA extracted as template and specific fungal primers of ITS1F and ITS2 (F: 5′-CTTGGTCATTTAGAGGAAGTAA-3′ R: 5′-GCTGCGTTCTTCATCGATGC-3′) [38]. The amplification was conducted using a PCR instrument (ABI GeneAmp® 9700, Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). The reaction system comprised 25 µL, containing 1–10 ng of template DNA (2 µL volume, with a maximum total of 50 ng to prevent inhibitory effects), 12.5 µL of high-fidelity PCR Mix, and the remaining volume supplemented with nuclease-free water. The amplification program was set as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 25–30 cycles of amplification (denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 55 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 45 s), with a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min and storage at 4 °C. The number of cycles was optimized to inhibit chimera formation. After amplification, the product quality was assessed using 2% agarose gel electrophoresis. The target bands were excised and purified using an AxyPrep DNA Gel Extraction Kit and further purified post-elution with Tris-HCl. Based on the initial electrophoresis quantification results, the PCR products were quantified using the QuantiFluor™ -ST Blue Fluorescence Quantitation System (Promega Corporation, WI, USA). After equalizing the samples, a paired-end (PE) library was constructed using a NEXTFLEX Rapid DNA-Seq Kit. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina platform (PE150 sequencing mode) (Illumina, San Diego, USA), and the resulting data were analyzed with bioinformatics software to resolve fungal diversity at the genus level.

Statistical analysis

One-factor analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Duncan’s multiple comparison was used to evaluate leaf physicochemical properties and structural characteristics with SPSS (IBM SPSS Statistics 26; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Figures were generated using Origin 2024 (OriginLab) and GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software). Additionally, R language (version 3.3.1) was used for statistical analysis and figure creation to analyze the microbial community. Using the Ribosomal Database Project (release 11.5, http://rdp.cme.msu.edu/) classifier, sequences were dereplicated (singletons discarded) and clustered into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) at a 97% sequence-identity threshold; chimeras were removed on-the-fly to yield representative OTU sequences. Fungal and bacterial OTUs were validated against the UNITE (release 8, https://unite.ut.ee/) and SILVA (release 138, https://www.arb-silva.de/) databases, respectively. Shared and unique OTUs among samples were enumerated and visualised with Venn diagrams. Functional profiles were predicted with PICRUSt2 (v2.2.0, https://github.com/picrust/picrust2). Microbial abundance and alpha-diversity indices were calculated with Mothur (v1.30.2); group comparisons were performed by t-test and rarefaction curves were used to infer community richness. For significance testing, multi-group comparisons were conducted with one-way ANOVA, while pairwise comparisons employed the Wilcoxon rank-sum test; both were corrected for false-discovery rate. NST analysis—a robust, null-model-based tool for quantifying ecological stochasticity—was applied under the unified neutral framework: an NST index > 50% indicates predominantly stochastic assembly, whereas < 50% indicates deterministic assembly, after accounting for abiotic filtering, competition, environmental noise and spatial scale. Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) was performed with the “vegan” package in R (v3.3.1). Co-occurrence networks were visualised with Cytoscape (v3.7.1, http://cytoscape.org/) to illustrate species coexistence across environmental samples. The relationships between the samples and species were visualized using Circos-0.67–7.67 (http://circos.ca/). Subsequently, figures were created using the R language (version 3.3.1) to analyze the correlation between the relative abundance of microbial species and environmental factors. Only microbial genera were analyzed in this study. The model employed was as follows:

Results

Effects of different treatments on soybean photosynthetic parameters and yield

Planting density had no significant effects on A, E, Ci, or GSW (P > 0.05; Fig. 1A–D). However, under low-density conditions, the fertilization type significantly influenced A, E, and GSW (P < 0.05; Fig. 1A, B, D). Even though significant differences were not observed in the photosynthetic parameters among LCK, LFN, and LFP, they were significantly lower than those of LWF (except for Ci) (P < 0.05). Specifically, the A, E, and GSW of the LCK treatment were significantly lower (42.8%, 36.6%, and 41.5%, respectively) than those of the LWF treatment (P < 0.05). Compared with LWF, the A, E, and GSW values in the LFN treatment were significantly reduced by 57.1%, 52.4%, and 57.2%, respectively (P < 0.05), while those in the LFP treatment were significantly reduced by 64.7%, 56.8%, and 60.5%, respectively (P < 0.05). In high-density planting, although fertilization did not significantly affect photosynthetic parameters (P > 0.05), foliar fertilization indicated a trend to improve these parameters compared to LCK and LWF.

The fertilization type significantly influenced fresh matter yield, dry matter yield, grain yield, and 100-seed weight (P < 0.01) (Fig. 1E–H). Compared with LCK, fertilization significantly increased the fresh matter yield, dry matter yield, grain yield, and 100-seed weight (P < 0.05). Even though significant differences were not observed between the LWF and LFP treatments (P > 0.05), both were higher than those in the CK and LFN treatments. Planting density significantly affected fresh matter yield (P < 0.05) (Fig. 1E), while it did not significantly impact dry matter yield, grain yield, or 100-seed weight (P > 0.05). However, high-density planting demonstrated a trend of increasing yield. Specifically, compared with those values observed for low-density planting, high-density planting significantly increased fresh matter yield by 29.7% (P < 0.05), dry matter yield by 20.2%, and grain yield by 20.2% (P > 0.05). Compared with LCK, the harvest indices of LWF, LFN, and LFP increased significantly by 14.6%, 10.4%, and 10.4%, respectively (P < 0.01) (Figure S2). However, fertilization did not influence the harvest index under low-density conditions (P > 0.05).

Effects of different treatments on soybean leaf physiological traits and structure

The interaction between year and planting density significantly affected the contact angle of the abaxial leaf surface (P < 0.05) (Table 2). The interactions between fertilization and year and between fertilization and maturity stage significantly affected the contact angle of the adaxial surface (P < 0.05). Specifically, the moisture retention capacity in 2024 was significantly lower (54.5% and 47.8%, respectively) than that observed for 2022 and 2023 (P < 0.01). In addition, the wax content, abaxial surface contact angle, and adaxial and abaxial surface stomatal densities in 2024 were higher (52.5%, 1.6%, 57.6%, and 37.6%, respectively) than those observed for 2023 (Table 2). Compared with the R4, water retention and adaxial and abaxial surface stomatal densities at the R8 surged significantly by 59.4%, 46.1%, and 11.6%, respectively (P < 0.01). The wax content and adaxial surface contact angle at R8 were significantly lower (64.6% and 1.2%, respectively) than those observed at R4 (P < 0.01) (Table 2). Fertilization significantly influenced the upper surface contact angle (P < 0.05). Although significant differences were not observed between the LWF and LFN treatments and LCK treatment, the adaxial surface contact angle in the LFP treatment increased by 0.8% relative to that observed in the LCK treatment. Planting density did not significantly influence any of the physiological indices (P > 0.05).

Effects of different treatments on the fungal community in the soybean phyllosphere

In the fungal communities, low- and high-density treatments yielded 255 and 254 OTUs, respectively, including 71 and 70 unique OTUs, with 184 OTUs shared between the two densities (Fig. 2A). At both high and low densities, the relative abundances of five genera, namely Dioszegia, Symmetrospora, Sampaiozyma, Cladosporium, and Hannaella, were relatively high. However, the overall genera relative abundance was similar between high and low densities (Fig. 2B). Additionally, the relative abundances of Plenodomus and Neodidymella increased with higher planting density, In contrast, the relative abundances of Phaeosphaeria, Filobasidium, and Alternaria decreased with increasing planting density. The significance analysis of genera relative abundance indicated that Bensingtonia, Lithohypha, Genolevuria, and Neovaginatispora were significantly enriched at high densities compared to low densities (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2C). Conversely, Fusariella and Cercospora were more abundant at low densities (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2C). Among the environmental variables (Fig. 2D), soluble sugars exhibited a highly significant positive correlation with grain number, dry matter yield, fresh matter yield, grain yield, and total phenol content (P < 0.05). Conversely, soluble sugars were significantly negatively correlated with leaf area and 100-grain weight (P < 0.05). Total phenol content showed a significant positive correlation with dry matter, fresh matter, and grain yields (P < 0.05). Principal components 1 and 2 accounted for 26.64% and 19.62% of the variance in the contribution rates, respectively (Fig. 2E). The 95% confidence interval exhibited a high overlap between low and high densities. Paraophiobolus exhibited significant positive correlations with various environmental factors (Fig. 2F), particularly yield, fresh matter yield, dry matter yield, and grain number (P < 0.05). Erythrobasidium showed a similar trend, with significant positive correlations with fresh and dry matter yields (P < 0.05). Plenodomus was significantly positively correlated with A (P < 0.05); Longispora was significantly positively correlated with grain yield (P < 0.05); Tilletiopsis was significantly positively correlated with grain number (P < 0.05); Stagonosporopsis was significantly positively correlated with Ci (P < 0.05). Bullera showed a highly significant negative correlation with pod number (P < 0.01). The NST index of the fungal community was significantly lower in the low-density treatment than that in the high-density treatment (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2G).

Effects of different treatments on the health-related microbial communities in soybeans

In contrast to low density, high density increased the relative abundances of the potentially beneficial fungi Vishniacozyma, Buckleyzyma, Dioszegia, Sphingomonas, Quadrisphaera, Methylobacterium-Methylorubrum, Chryseobacterium, and Massilia (Table 4). Conversely, low density decreased the relative abundances of the harmful fungi Didymella, Cladosporium, and Plenodomus. Compared to the LCK treatment, fertilization increased the relative abundances of the potentially beneficial fungi Erythrobasidium and Vishniacozyma but increased the relative abundances of Didymella, Stagonosporopsis, and Fusarium. In addition, fertilization increased the relative abundances of Deinococcus and Bacillus, especially with foliar phosphorus fertilization. Compared to the LCK treatment, the LFN and LWF treatments decreased the relative abundance of the pathogen Pantoea, whereas the LFP treatment increased the relative abundance of this pathogen.

Discussion

Hydrophobicity–gas exchange trade-offs shaping soybean yield

Plants typically alter their morphology in response to environmental stressors [72]. Wax is essential for inferring tolerance to both abiotic and biotic stresses. This mechanism aids plants in resisting ultraviolet radiation and fungal invasion, minimizing nonstomatal water loss, and maintaining mechanical strength [73]. In this study, the leaf wax content of soybeans in 2024 (45.3 mg g− 1 DM) was significantly higher than that in 2023 (29.7 mg g− 1 DM). This can be attributed to higher temperatures and rainfall in July–September 2024 (Supplementary Fig. 1). In addition, previous studies have reported that water deficiency increases the total wax content of crop seedlings [74]. This mechanism reduces water loss [75].

Higher temperatures often drive transpirational water loss. The leaf contact angle increases with higher leaf wax content, while a greater stomatal density correlates with an elevated contact angle [76]. This partly explains the higher stomatal density and contact angle in the leaves observed in 2024 in this study. Under conditions of low environmental humidity, plants may reduce the stomatal aperture to minimize transpirational water loss [77]. However, normal photosynthesis requires stomata for gas exchange; therefore, the plant must increase stomatal density to compensate for the adverse effects of the reduced stomatal aperture. Although an increase in stomatal density has been reported to coincide with a decrease in stomatal aperture [78], conclusive evidence linking this phenomenon to higher temperatures is currently lacking. Notably, dense surface wax layers and protruding epidermal cells increase hydrophobicity, while the contact angle rises as leaf water content decreases [76]. These dynamics support the relationship between decreased water retention and increased wax content and contact angle in this study.

Previous studies conducted at two planting densities (1.8 × 105 and 2.7 × 105 plants ha− 1) found that the higher density significantly increased canopy light interception and dry-matter accumulation during soybean growth, thereby enhancing soybean productivity [79]. In this study, the photosynthetic characteristics of soybeans did not show any significant improvement or decline owing to increased planting density. This contradicts the findings of previous studies [79]. This may be attributed to the differences in soybean varieties and field management practices. Under higher plant density conditions, the photosynthetic pigment content of crops decreases while the net photosynthetic rate per plant is reduced. However, it significantly enhances canopy light interception [80], thereby increasing yield. In this study, the high-density treatment led to a significantly greater yield compared to the low-density treatment (P < 0.05) (Fig. 1), This is presumably associated with improved light- and water-use efficiencies. Previous studies have shown that increasing planting density reduces the number of effective branches per plant [81], potentially decreasing the number of lower leaves, maintaining relatively low leaf overlap, and preventing significant deterioration of the internal light environment within the canopy. In addition, plant population cover may reduce soil water evaporation and improve water-use efficiency. Therefore, one reason high-density planting increases yield is the improvement in light-energy-use and water-use efficiencies.

Typically, plant growth and development are reliant on nitrogen absorption and assimilation [82]. Nitrogen fertilizer (urea) serves as a basal fertilizer, fulfilling the basic nutritional needs for plant growth. Foliar fertilizers are directly absorbed by the leaves and facilitate the immediate transport of nutrients to the tissues and organs of crops [83]. They can rapidly replenish key nutrients and optimize plant physiological metabolism, thereby increasing the yield. In this study, all foliar-fertilizer treatments produced significantly higher yields than LCK (P < 0.05); however, no significant difference in yield was detected between LFP and LWF (P >0.05). This may explain the ability of rhizobia in soybean roots to fix nitrogen. Soybeans form a symbiotic relationship with Bradyrhizobium Japonicum in the root nodules [84] and facilitate nitrogen acquisition for growth via biological nitrogen fixation in the roots [79]. Therefore, soybeans may not require large amounts of nitrogen. In addition, nitrogen fertilizer application decreases biological nitrogen fixation by approximately 70% relative to the unfertilized control group [85]. This indicates that nitrogen fertilizer use can suppress biological nitrogen fixation. Research has shown that adequate phosphorus fertilizer enhances nodule formation in leguminous plants and promotes nitrogen absorption in roots [86]. The capacity of soybeans to fix nitrogen via root nodules may reduce their reliance on nitrogen fertilizer. Additionally, the yield exhibits greater response to phosphorus than to nitrogen. This mechanistically rationalizes the observed superiority of foliar phosphate fertilization over foliar nitrogen fertilization in enhancing yield in this study. This indicates that various foliar fertilizers exhibit distinct effects on nitrogen-fixing bacteria and subsequently affect the yield [87, 88]. However, foliar fertilizers can induce osmotic stress and lead to partial stomatal closure to minimize water loss, thereby limiting CO2 absorption and reducing photosynthesis. In this study, foliar fertilizers reduced the photosynthetic characteristics of soybeans at low densities. Conversely, when plant nutrient competition is fierce at high plant density, foliar fertilizers can effectively address the nutritional needs of plant growth in a timely manner, thereby increasing photosynthesis. A larger contact angle indicates reduced wettability of the leaf surface to liquid droplets [89]. This, in turn, reduces the colonization of pathogenic microorganisms and is extremely important for yield formation. These results indicate that, in terms of yield optimization, although there was no significant difference between the LWF and LFP treatments, both were significantly superior to the LFN treatment (Fig. 1).

Stomata serve as pathways for plants to exchange water and carbon dioxide with the environment [90] and plays a vital role in plant photosynthesis. Studies have shown that water deficiency can increase stomatal density in plants [91]. In this study, the stomatal density of R8 leaves exceeded that of R4 leaves, potentially attributable to reduced rainfall and a relatively arid environment at the R8 stage. Similarly, relevant studies have demonstrated that rice leaves exhibit higher stomatal density in the later growth stages [92]. Cuticular wax is synthesized from various substances [93]. During the maturation of crop grains, the translocation of photosynthetic products from leaves to grains [94] can decrease wax synthesis. As R8 soybean leaves gradually age, the enzyme activity associated with wax synthesis in the leaves may decline and result in decreased wax production. Previous research has indicated that newly developed leaves generally possess a denser epicuticular wax layer than aging leaves [89]. Additionally, research has found that the contact angle of aging leaves is markedly reduced [95]. In this study, the significant decrease in wax content and contact angle at the R8 stage relative to that at the R4 stage was consistent with previous research findings. Notably, the water retention capacity of R8 soybean leaves was significantly higher than that of R4, likely because of the relatively dry external environment and increased leaf surface wettability resulting from the reduced wax content at this stage. In arid environments, some plants must maintain leaf moisture to minimize water loss and limit gas exchange [95]. Furthermore, some studies have shown that leaf wetness contributes to leaf hydration via foliar water uptake [96] and subsequently improves leaf water retention. Overall, the observed variations in stomatal density, wax content, and contact angle size in leaves represent protective mechanisms for plants in response to environmental stress, which are primarily aimed at creating optimal conditions for growth and yield accumulation.

Canopy density and foliar nutrition recruit beneficial phyllosphere fungi to support yield

Typically, plant phyllosphere microorganisms are critical for maintaining plant health, promoting growth, preventing diseases, and facilitating environmental stress adaptation [97, 98]. Beneficial phyllosphere microorganisms can inhibit pathogen growth and colonization by competing for nutrients and space. For instance, Sphingomonas strains can inhibit the plant pathogen P. syringae through direct competition for glucose, fructose, and sucrose [97]. Other microorganisms produce various metabolites, antibiotics, and hormones [99] that may inhibit the survival of harmful microorganisms. The diversity of phyllosphere microorganisms is essential for maintaining the ecological balance within the plant phyllosphere. They can inhibit the over-proliferation of pathogens and maintain plant health via competitive and antagonistic effects. At the genus level, this study found that the relative abundances of the fungi Dioszegia and Hannaella were similar under both high- and low-density planting. This may be linked to plant self-health maintenance. Previous reports have classified Dioszegia [40] and Hannaella [45] as beneficial microbes. When plants face adverse external conditions, they actively recruit such beneficial communities to strengthen their tolerance to environmental stresses [100]. Particularly nder dense planting, dense canopy cover can create a warm, humid micro-environment that favors pathogen proliferation; consequently, plants appear to increase the abundance of protective fungi to counteract the negative impacts of these potential pathogens.

In this study, the two most abundant fungi under high-density planting conditions were the beneficial fungi Dioszegia [40] and Hannaella [45]. This may represent a survival mechanism by which soybean plants recruit beneficial fungi to alleviate the negative impacts of harmful microorganisms and thereby maintain their own health. For instance, pulcherriminic acid released by Metschnikowia pulcherrima suppresses the growth of Botrytis cinerea [101], while polymers and secondary metabolites produced by Aureobasidium pullulans have been shown to inhibit the spread of both Alternaria alternata and B. cinerea [43]. Conversely, harmful fungi, including Cladosporium [39], Alternaria [39], and Fusariella [99], were enriched under low-density planting conditions in this study. This may be attributed to the sparse distribution and dry leaf surfaces, which hinder microbial colonization and reduce microbial diversity. However, under low-density conditions, ventilation between plants is relatively improved, allowing certain airborne pathogens to rapidly proliferate and colonize leaf surfaces in the early stage. This occupation of ecological niches creates a competitive disadvantage for other microorganisms in nutrient competition and hinders their survival [97]. Research has indicated that plant leaf nutrients, including amino acids, can leak out from the inside [98]. Phyllospheric microorganisms require nutrients and moisture for survival on leaf surfaces. When plants are densely planted, plant coverage may create a warm and humid leaf surface, thereby allowing more microorganisms to survive; however, this also intensifies nutrient competition. Conversely, when plant density is low, a dry leaf surface environment diminishes microbial colonization and reduces competition for survival. In this study, under high-density conditions, the microbial community was positively correlated with several environmental factors. Under low-density conditions, almost no correlations were observed between the microbial community and environmental factors. Under high-density conditions, the microbial community did not significantly correlate with environmental factors, such as pod number, leaf area, dry matter yield, and grain yield. However, it exhibited a highly significant positive correlation with fresh matter yield and grain number. This can be attributed to certain beneficial microorganisms promoting the healthy growth of soybean plants and increasing yield. In general, Vishniacozyma inhibits fungal pathogen growth by producing volatile compounds [43]. Previous reports have shown that Erythrobasidium possesses antifungal or growth-promoting activities [40, 41] that are beneficial to plants. However, in this study, Erythrobasidium was negatively correlated with both fresh and dry matter yields (Fig. 2F). This was potential preference for senescing or stressed tissues, or its involvement in microbial interactions that compete with other beneficial microorganisms under specific environmental conditions, ultimately affecting plant biomass accumulation. Symmetrospora was significantly negatively correlated with wax content and pod number. Research has shown that the relative abundance of Symmetrospora increases in diseased tobacco leaves [102], highlighting its potential role as a pathogen. The cuticle prevents pathogen infection and water loss [103], rendering the leaf surface dry and unsuitable for microbial survival. This function may explain the negative correlation between Symmetrospora and cuticle content. Stomata are essential for plant defense and serve as important entry points for colonizing endophytic plant pathogens [104]. In this study, adaxial surface stomatal density showed an extremely significant positive correlation with Tricellula and Phaeosphaeria (P < 0.05). Abaxial surface stomatal density exhibited an extremely significant positive correlation with Pseudopithomyces (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2F). This may be linked to stomatal function, which regulates gas exchange and water transpiration [105]. Stomata enable water loss via transpiration [106] increase humidity in the surrounding area, and creates a favorable environment for the survival of microorganisms. These findings suggest that foliar fertilizers not only modify the physical and physiological properties of the plant surface, but may also enhance microbial colonization and improve the microbe-plant interaction environment, thereby playing an indirect yet critical role in increasing crop yield. On the other hand, foliar fertilization can enhance nutrient utilization efficiency and reduce the adverse effects of stress on crops [107]. Meanwhile, studies have shown that under stress, beneficial microorganisms can stimulate plants to produce recruitment signals and regulate the allocation of energy between growth and defense [100]. Additionally, beneficial fungi colonization on leaves can promote healthy plant growth. For example, previous studies evaluated the potential beneficial fungi Trichoderma asperellum T36 and Trichoderma harzianum Td50b by foliar spraying chlamydospore suspensions on Momordica charantia and found that the treatment increased yield by 25.33% to 53.07% [108]. Therefore, beneficial phyllosphere fungi can increase plant yield, as evidenced by the increased soybean yield resulting from fertilization, particularly the higher yield observed with the foliar phosphorus fertilizer treatment compared with the LCK treatment in this study.

Canopy density and foliar nutrition reshape phyllosphere bacteria and yield

Similar to fungi, the phyllosphere is colonized by numerous beneficial and harmful bacteria. The diversity of leaf-surface bacteria is equally crucial for maintaining healthy plant growth and yield formation. The diversity of non-pathogenic microorganisms on leaves may provide a protective role against plant diseases [98]. Microbial diversity may maintain the phyllosphere microecosystem balance and inhibit the formation of either dominant harmful or dominant beneficial microbial communities. The most abundant fungi in this study were Sphingomonas, Quadrisphaera, Methylobacterium-Methylorubrum, and Pseudomonas, all of which were more abundant under high-density planting conditions than under low-density planting conditions. Conversely, Pantoea and Aureimonas were more abundant under low-density conditions. Under high-density planting conditions, the compact canopy of the plant community dissipates heat slowly and maintains warmth [109], thereby providing a favorable environment for microbial survival. In this study, the most abundant genera, including Sphingomonas [17] and Methylobacterium-Methylorubrum [110], were beneficial bacteria, which represented a potential preventive measure for soybeans in response to environmental stress.

In this study, Pseudomonas exhibited a significant positive correlation with pod number (P < 0.05) and significantly negative correlation with abaxial surface stomatal density (P < 0.05). The correlations of these bacteria and microorganisms with leaf structure and nutrition are inseparable. Stomatal density and aperture, wax content, and contact angle all affect leaf microbial colonization. For example, the warm and humid environment around stomata resulting from transpiration creates a favorable habitat for microorganisms. Conversely, wax content accumulation can adversely affect microbial colonization in leaf tissues and on the leaf surface [77, 103]. In this study, Roseomonas exhibited a significant negative correlation with the water retention capacity (P < 0.05) and a positive and significant correlation with Microbacterium (P < 0.05). This may be attributed to different water utilities by plants and the varying water requirements of microorganisms. Water is essential for the photosynthetic process as it ensures the accumulation of photosynthetic products [111]. Studies have reported that leaf nutrients can be leached from plant leaves by rain, dew, and mist [112], potentially providing nutrients for microbial survival. Therefore, the observed positive correlation between the water retention capacity and Microbacterium in this study may be attributed to water retention improving both water storage and plant utilization.

In terms of fertilization, the most abundant genera were Sphingomonas, Pantoea, Quadrisphaera, and Methylobacterium-Methylorubrum. Microorganisms were enriched in different fertilization treatments: Sphingomonas, Quadrisphaera, and Aureimonas had higher relative abundances in the LFN treatment; Pantoea was the most abundant in relative terms in the LFP treatment; and Exiguobacterium was enriched in the LCK treatment. This indicates that various foliar fertilizers influence the abundance of phyllosphere microorganisms, likely because of their effects on leaf structure and canopy microclimate in soybean plants. Alterations in canopy size, density, and structure can affect the canopy microclimate [113]. For example, the foliar application of nitrogen fertilizer during the reproductive stage enhances photosynthetic activity in leguminous plants, facilitating the assimilation and translocation of nutrients to sink organs [114]. In contrast, plants enhance their defense by recruiting beneficial microorganisms by secreting specific nutrients via their leaves [98]. Although harmful microorganisms are detrimental to plant growth and yield formation, under conditions of ample water, nutrients, and light with vigorous growth, a small number of these microorganisms in the phyllosphere may not be the primary factor suppressing yield. Consequently, certain harmful microorganisms exhibit enhanced growth on high-yielding plants, indicating a positive correlation with yield. Thus, crop yield is influenced not only by water [115] and fertilization [116] but may also be modulated by the abundance of phyllosphere microbial communities.

Generally, highly complex microbial networks can resist biotic and abiotic disturbances in the host, thereby maintaining plant health [117]. Increased microbial diversity in the phyllosphere enhances competition for nutrients and promotes mutual inhibition among microorganisms, subsequently promoting plant growth [97]. This limits the proliferation of harmful microorganisms and mitigates excessive consumption of nutrients and interspecific competition owing to numerous beneficial microorganisms, thereby aiding plants in conserving leaf surface nutrients. In this microenvironment, plants may allocate fewer nutrients to defend against harmful microorganisms and support beneficial microorganisms, instead prioritizing growth and metabolism, thereby enhancing nutrient accumulation within the plant. Under these conditions, numerous photosynthetic products are transported and stored in seeds to enhance yield. Therefore, in production, the relationship among microorganisms, plant growth, and yield must be acknowledged. Improving plant phyllosphere microbial diversity through agronomic measures and maintaining the phyllosphere microenvironmental balance is essential for reducing the nutritional investment of plants in response to external environmental disturbances. This approach enables a greater allocation toward reproductive growth, thereby increasing yield.

Conclusions

Considering the dual-purpose nature of soybeans for both grain and fodder production, the findings suggest that high-density planting and foliar phosphorus fertilization are the most effective strategies for maximizing productivity. Future research should focus on elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying the interactions between phyllosphere microbial communities and host plant nutrition, as well as the regulatory networks through which these interactions enhance the yield of dual-purpose soybean under different agronomic management conditions.
