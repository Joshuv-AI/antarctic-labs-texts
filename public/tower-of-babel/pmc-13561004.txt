Optimised carbohydrate dietary intervention for improving metabolic health in adults with prediabetes: study protocol for a multicentre randomised controlled trial
Authors: Ruiqi Zhang, Yuwei Lu, Nan Zhang, Dan Liu, Qian Wu, Jingyi Guo, Hua Zhao, Qian Li, Jingyi Yang, Yan Pang, Ying Zhang, Piao Kang, Qinyi Wang, Qingqing Zheng, Silin Liu, Hongfeng Mao, Xiaoxue Long, Xiaojing Gao, Rong Zeng, Mingliang Zhang, Qichen Fang, Weiping Jia, Yueqiong Ni, Huating Li

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionDietary modification is an important strategy for the prevention of type 2 diabetes, with fibre intake recognised as a key factor in promoting metabolic health. Resistant starch (RS), a type of fermentable dietary fibre, has emerged as a promising therapeutic approach due to its association with weight loss, enhanced insulin sensitivity and improved glucose metabolism. As nutritional research progresses, the focus has shifted from individual nutrients to overall dietary patterns. Based on this concept, this study aims to investigate the metabolic effects of a dietary pattern that optimises carbohydrate structure by increasing RS intake and reducing rapidly digestible carbohydrate in adults with pre-diabetes.Methods and analysisThis is a multicentre, randomised, parallel-controlled clinical trial, which will enrol 250 adults diagnosed with pre-diabetes. Eligible participants will undergo randomisation to receive either a guideline-based conventional diet or an optimised carbohydrate diet (OCD). In the OCD intervention, daily RS intake will be increased to approximately 40 g, with fibre intake rising to 20–40 g per 1000 kcal. At the same time, the intake of rapidly digestible starch and free sugars will be reduced. The intervention will last for 6 months, followed by a 3-month post-intervention follow-up period. The primary outcome is the change in postprandial glycaemic response, assessed using the incremental area under the curve (iAUC) for plasma glucose during the oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT). Secondary outcomes include the proportion of participants achieving remission to normoglycaemia or progressing to type 2 diabetes, changes in other glucose- and lipid-related metabolic parameters and changes in lifestyle-related behavioural factors. Exploratory outcomes will include changes in appetite-related hormones, circulating cytokines, immune function and multi-omics profiles.Ethics and disseminationThis study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Sixth People’s Hospital Affiliated to Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine (Approval No. 2025-122; Protocol V.1.0, 20250811) and the Ethics Committee of Shenzhen Center for Chronic Disease Control (Approval No. SZCCC-2024-011-01-PJ) and registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry. Findings from this study will be disseminated in peer-reviewed journal publications.Trial registration numberChiCTR2500113583.Keywords: DIABETES & ENDOCRINOLOGY, NUTRITION & DIETETICS, Insulin Resistance

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes (T2D) is a highly prevalent metabolic disorder closely associated with a range of complications leading to considerable premature mortality1 2 and constituting a major contributor to the global health burden. Pre-diabetes, characterised by impaired fasting glucose, impaired glucose tolerance (IGT) or their combination, represents an intermediate stage in the progression to T2D, offering a critical window for early intervention.3–5 Globally, approximately 1 in 10 adults is affected by pre-diabetes,6 with annual progression rates to T2D ranging from 5% to 10%.7 8 Glycaemic dysregulation at this stage is potentially reversible, and restoration of normoglycaemia has been shown to reduce the risk of progression to T2D.4 9–13 Consequently, timely intervention in individuals with pre-diabetes may alter disease trajectories and alleviate the burden of subsequent management.14

Lifestyle modification, particularly dietary intervention, remains the cornerstone of metabolic management in individuals with pre-diabetes.15 Compared with pharmacological treatment, it is associated with lower risk, fewer adverse effects and greater cost-effectiveness.16 17 Dietary fibre intake, as a key dietary component, has been consistently associated with improved metabolic health and glycaemic regulation.18 19 Evidence from prospective cohort studies and systematic reviews indicates that higher dietary fibre intake is associated not only with improved glycaemic control among individuals with pre-diabetes but also with a lower incidence of T2D.20

Resistant starch (RS), a fermentable form of dietary fibre, escapes digestion in the small intestine, resulting in a lower postprandial glycaemic response.21–23 It is subsequently fermented by the gut microbiota in the colon to produce beneficial metabolites, including short-chain fatty acids,24 which have been implicated in the regulation of energy metabolism and inflammatory responses.25 26 Experimental and clinical evidence in individuals with metabolic disorders has linked RS intake to reductions in hepatic fat accumulation and visceral adiposity, as well as improvements in glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity assessed using the hyperinsulinaemic-euglycaemic clamp.27 28 As impaired glucose regulation, insulin resistance and abnormal fat distribution are important metabolic abnormalities associated with pre-diabetes, RS may represent a biologically plausible dietary component for pre-diabetes management.

Despite the recognised metabolic benefits of RS, its effects can be influenced by the overall dietary context and by interactions with other dietary components,28 as nutrients do not act in isolation but through synergistic, antagonistic and additive effects.29 Focusing on single nutrients may oversimplify the complex relationship between diet and health.30 In contrast, dietary patterns better capture the combined effects of foods and nutrients and are more applicable in real-world settings.31 Therefore, the metabolic benefits associated with RS may be better understood and more effectively translated into clinical practice within the context of an overall dietary pattern.

Recently, an optimised carbohydrate diet (OCD) has been proposed as a dietary pattern that increases RS intake within the framework of a balanced diet.32 Beyond increasing the intake of RS as a beneficial carbohydrate component, this dietary pattern also reduces the intake of low-quality carbohydrates, thereby optimising the overall carbohydrate composition. Rapidly digestible carbohydrates, including rapidly digestible starch (RDS) and free sugars, are more likely to induce postprandial glycaemic excursions.33 Long-term exposure to such acute metabolic disturbances may cumulatively impair metabolic regulation, thereby contributing to the development of insulin resistance and increasing the risk of progression from pre-diabetes to T2D.34 On this basis, OCD may represent a novel dietary intervention pattern for improving glycaemic management in pre-diabetes. This study will therefore assess its effects on glycaemic control among adults with pre-diabetes, and further examine its potential role in promoting remission of pre-diabetes and reducing the risk of progression to T2D.

Pre-diabetes is characterised not only by dysregulated glucose metabolism but also by a range of metabolic, lifestyle-related behavioural35 36 and immunological alterations.37 Accordingly, this study aims to evaluate the multidimensional effects of the OCD intervention beyond glycaemic control, encompassing these broader domains. Additionally, multi-omics analysis will be conducted to characterise biological signatures induced by the intervention and to elucidate the potential mechanisms underlying the effects of the OCD in individuals with pre-diabetes.

Methods and analysis

Study design

This study is a multicentre, randomised, open-label, parallel-group controlled clinical trial with blinded outcome assessment conducted in individuals with pre-diabetes. Participants will be randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to receive either an OCD intervention or a conventional dietary intervention for pre-diabetes. The OCD is designed based on adjustments in the composition and proportional structure of carbohydrates, whereas the control group will follow a dietary pattern established in accordance with current dietary guidelines for pre-diabetes. The dietary intervention will last for 6 months, followed by a 3-month post-intervention follow-up, resulting in a total study duration of 9 months.

This study protocol is reported in accordance with the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) guidelines (see online supplemental file 1).38 Participants will attend a total of 10 study visits (V0–V9) throughout the study period, as shown in figure 1. This trial was registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry in December 2025 (registration number: ChiCTR2500113583). Participant recruitment is scheduled to commence in August 2026, and the study is expected to be completed by February 2028.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public will not be involved in the design, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Study settings

This trial will be conducted at four hospitals in China, including Shanghai Sixth People’s Hospital Affiliated to Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine, Peking University Shenzhen Hospital, the Second Affiliated Hospital of the Chinese University of Hong Kong, Shenzhen and People’s Hospital of Longhua, Shenzhen.

Participants will be recruited through advertisements displayed in physical health examination centres and outpatient areas at each clinical trial centre. Interested individuals will undergo prescreening via telephone or an online questionnaire. Those meeting the prespecified criteria will be invited to attend a screening visit. Written informed consent will be obtained prior to any study procedures, following provision of detailed information regarding study objectives, potential benefits and risks (see online supplemental file 2). At screening, demographic characteristics and clinical assessments will be obtained by study physicians, including anthropometric measurements, biochemical indices and lifestyle-related behavioural information. On meeting the eligibility criteria, participants will undergo a run-in period. Following successful completion of this phase, they will proceed to the first visit and randomisation.

Run-in period

Participants meeting the eligibility criteria are provided with standardised dietary education and management in accordance with the Dietary Guidelines for Chinese Residents (2022)39 and Intervention for adults with pre-diabetes: a Chinese expert consensus (2023).40 Individual daily energy requirements are calculated using the formula: ((height (cm) - 105) kg * 25 kcal/kg). Macronutrient composition is standardised, with carbohydrates prescribed to account for 50–60% of total energy intake, fat for 25–30% and protein for 15–20%. Total daily energy intake is distributed across three meals, with approximately 25–30% allocated to breakfast, 30%–40% to lunch and 30%–35% to dinner. Daily meal plans are delivered via a customised mobile application.

Interventions

During the intervention phase, individual energy prescriptions and overall macronutrient composition will be determined according to a standardised formula, with meal distribution controlled accordingly, consistent with the run-in period. Participants in both intervention groups will be regularly provided by the study centres with customised carbohydrate-based staple foods to ensure that daily intakes of RS and other carbohydrates meet the levels specified in the study design. Except for differences in carbohydrate composition, all other nutrients and food components are identical between the two dietary groups. To reduce dietary monotony and improve adherence, non-overlapping 28-day rotating menus will be used. Daily dietary intake and meal timing will be recorded via the customised study application, allowing ongoing monitoring of dietary adherence.

Optimised carbohydrate diet

During the 6-month intervention, participants will follow an OCD pattern characterised by increased intake of RS to enhance dietary fibre consumption, together with reduced intake of RDS and free sugars. The nutritional composition of the carbohydrate-based staple foods and the corresponding dietary specifications have been described previously.32

In this dietary pattern, RS accounts for approximately 15–20% of total daily starch intake (approximately 40 g/day), slowly digestible starch (SDS) for 5% and RDS for 75–80%. Total dietary fibre intake is maintained at 20–40 g per 1000 kcal, and free sugar intake is restricted to <5% of total energy intake in accordance with WHO recommendations.41

Conventional diet for pre-diabetes

During the 6-month conventional diet (CD) intervention, participants will receive dietary management in accordance with established dietary guidelines for pre-diabetes. Given the substantial overlap between dietary recommendations for pre-diabetes and diabetes, the overall dietary framework is consistent with that used in previous studies based on diabetes guidelines.

Within this dietary pattern, RS accounts for approximately 5% of total daily starch intake, SDS for 5% and RDS for approximately 90%. In accordance with recommendations from the American Diabetes Association,42 dietary fibre intake is prescribed at 14 g per 1000 kcal, and free sugar intake is restricted to <5% of total energy intake.

Post-intervention follow-up

Following completion of the 6-month intervention period, all participants will enter a 3-month post-intervention follow-up phase. Participants will discontinue receiving the customised carbohydrate-based staple foods and revert to the diet established during the run-in phase, while continuing to record their dietary intake. They will also be advised to maintain usual physical activity levels and to avoid initiating additional intensive behavioural changes. At the end of this phase (month 9), glycaemic outcomes will be reassessed, including the degree of glycaemic improvement, progression to type 2 diabetes and remission of pre-diabetes, in order to evaluate the maintenance of intervention effects in the absence of active dietary intervention.

Medication and exercise management

Given that the target population comprises individuals with pre-diabetes, individuals receiving glucose-lowering pharmacotherapy or other medications known to substantially affect glucose metabolism, such as systemic glucocorticoids, will be excluded at enrolment. Individuals with unstable medication regimens or a foreseeable need for relevant treatment modification during the intervention will also be excluded. During the intervention, participants will be instructed not to initiate pharmacological treatments that may substantially influence glycaemic control, lipid metabolism or body weight unless clinically required. Participants’ metabolic status will therefore be monitored at predefined intervals. If relevant medication is initiated, its indication, name, dose, initiation date and duration will be prospectively documented. Participants who require glucose-lowering pharmacotherapy will discontinue the assigned dietary intervention. All data collected before discontinuation will be retained, and participants will be analysed according to their originally assigned groups under the intention-to-treat principle. Participants will additionally be instructed to avoid the non-essential use of antibiotics, prebiotics, probiotics and other products that may substantially affect the gut microbiota during the trial. Any clinically necessary use of these products will be prospectively documented.

Exercise, as another key lifestyle intervention for pre-diabetes alongside dietary modification, will be required to maintain at a consistent level throughout the intervention period. Participants’ physical activity levels before, during and after the intervention will be assessed using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire.43

Adherence

Dietary adherence will be evaluated using a multimodal assessment framework integrating daily dietary records collected via a mobile application, dietary questionnaires administered at scheduled visits and face-to-face interviews conducted by trained dietitians. During the intervention, participants will upload pre- and post-meal photographs for each meal through the mobile application; these images will be used to estimate actual food consumption and derive nutrient intake. The study team will quantify adherence by calculating the proportion of meals meeting protocol-defined requirements based on uploaded dietary records and by assessing whether macronutrient intake targets are achieved, including predefined carbohydrate distribution, dietary fibre and RS intake and limits on free sugar consumption. To obtain objective evidence of adherence, carbohydrate-based staple foods for all three daily meals will be provided by each study centre at prespecified intervals during both the OCD and CD interventions, and unused carbohydrate-based staple foods and empty packaging will be collected at the end of each distribution period and cross-checked against dispensing records to estimate actual consumption. Automated reminder functions embedded within the study application will support timely dietary logging, while dietitians and study physicians will regularly review submitted records and provide individualised feedback to reinforce adherence to the intervention protocol. By integrating these complementary digital and in-person data sources, dietary adherence will be comprehensively assessed for each participant.

Outcomes

Primary outcomes

The primary outcome will be the change in the incremental area under the curve (iAUC) for plasma glucose during the OGTT.

Secondary outcomes

Metabolic parameters

Metabolic-related outcomes will encompass a range of glycaemic, anthropometric and biochemical parameters. These will include: (1) clinical metabolic outcomes, including pre-diabetes remission status assessed at the 3-month post-intervention follow-up and the proportion of participants progressing to T2D during the study period; (2) glycaemic parameters assessed by continuous glucose monitoring (CGM), such as time in range, time above range, time below range and mean glucose levels; (3) laboratory-based indicators of glucose metabolism, including HbA1c, fasting insulin, C-peptide concentrations and other indices derived from the OGTT; (4) anthropometric and body composition measures, including waist, hip and thigh circumferences, body weight and body composition assessed by bioelectrical impedance analysis; (5) lipid profile, comprising standard serum lipid parameters and (6) other clinical biochemical indicators, such as markers of liver and renal function, vitamins and trace elements.

Behavioural parameters

Behavioural outcomes will include several behavioural and functional domains. These will include: (1) sleep quality, assessed using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) questionnaire and complementary assessment methods; (2) appetite, evaluated using a visual analogue scale (VAS) and other relevant validated questionnaires; (3) food preference, assessed by scales and functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), which examine brain responses to food-related stimuli and (4) bowel habits, assessed using the Bristol Stool Scale and related instruments.

Exploratory outcomes

Exploratory outcomes will comprise a series of mechanistic assessments aimed at elucidating the potential biological pathways underlying the OCD intervention. These will include: (1) appetite-related hormonal responses, assessed through changes in circulating levels of key appetite-regulating hormones, including ghrelin, peptide YY (PYY) and glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1); (2) metabolic and inflammation-related cytokines, including fibroblast growth factor 21, adiponectin, fatty acid-binding protein 4 and lipocalin 2 and (3) multi-omics profiling, encompassing metagenomics, metabolomics, genomics, lipidomics, metatranscriptomics, immunomics and proteomics, to enable an integrated characterisation of metabolic and immunological pathways potentially modulated by the OCD intervention. For immunomics, immune function will be assessed through the isolation and analysis of peripheral blood mononuclear cells. Within the proteomics component, proteins implicated in insulin signalling and insulin resistance will be assessed. In addition, targeted measurement of selected signalling proteins in plasma-derived neuronal extracellular vesicles (NDEVs) will be performed as an exploratory peripheral indicator related to brain insulin sensitivity.44

Measures

All assessments and respective time points are listed in online supplemental table 1.

Anthropometric and body composition measurement

Participants will be instructed to abstain from food intake after 22:00 on the evening prior to the study visit. Following a fasting period of at least 10 hours, anthropometric assessments, including height, body weight, waist, hip and thigh circumferences, will be conducted by trained healthcare professionals using standardised measurement protocols, as previously reported.32 Blood pressure and heart rate will be measured under standardised resting conditions using calibrated sphygmomanometers, following procedures consistent with those described in the prior protocol.32 Body composition will be assessed using bioelectrical impedance analysis in accordance with the same standardised procedures.32

Continuous glucose monitoring

The CGM device will be affixed to the participants’ upper arms by research personnel who have received standardised training. CGM will be performed using the SIBIONICS system. Each CGM device enables CGM for up to 14 days per application, during which interstitial glucose data are continuously recorded and transmitted via the manufacturer’s mobile application.

Oral glucose tolerance test and appetite assessment

After an overnight fast of at least 10 hours, venous blood samples will be collected for assessment of fasting metabolic parameters, including plasma glucose, serum insulin and serum C-peptide concentrations. Participants will then consume a standard 75 g oral glucose solution within 5 min. Venous blood samples will be obtained at prespecified post-load time points to quantify postprandial metabolic responses, including plasma glucose, serum insulin and serum C-peptide levels. Subjective appetite-related sensations, such as hunger and satiety, will be assessed immediately before and after the OGTT using a 100 mm VAS.

Questionnaire assessment

At prespecified study visits, participants will complete a series of validated self-administered questionnaires. These instruments will include measures of sleep quality (PSQI) and bowel habits (Bristol Stool Scale). The collected data will be used to characterise sleep patterns and gastrointestinal function.

Food cue reactivity task and functional magnetic resonance imaging

Participants will complete a food preference questionnaire as part of the food cue reactivity task. Resting-state and task-based fMRI will be conducted in collaboration with psychiatrists to evaluate post-intervention changes in neural responses. Resting-state fMRI will be used to characterise baseline neural activity, while task-based fMRI will incorporate an appetite preference task using food picture stimuli to examine neural responses to stimuli related to food.45 Prior to scanning, participants will be required to fast for at least 6 hours. Before image acquisition, trained research staff will provide standardised prescan practice to familiarise participants with the experimental procedures. Task identifiers will be assigned prior to scanning, and visual display and response devices within the MRI system will be checked to ensure proper function. During scanning, participants will lie supine, with head movement minimised using vacuum cushions and restraints.

Blood and stool sample collection and storage

Venous blood samples will be obtained under fasting conditions and at multiple post-load time points following administration of a standard oral glucose challenge (75 g glucose solution). Blood samples will be processed promptly by centrifugation at 4°C to separate serum and plasma. Routine biochemical measurements, including plasma glucose, serum insulin and serum C-peptide, will be determined immediately using an automated clinical chemistry analyser. Serum samples designated for extended biomarker analyses will be aliquoted and stored at −80°C until batch analysis. Concentrations of metabolic and inflammatory cytokines will be quantified using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays or other validated analytical techniques in accordance with manufacturers’ instructions. For immunological assessments, peripheral blood mononuclear cells will be isolated from whole blood collected in ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid anticoagulant tubes. In addition, NDEVs will be isolated from plasma using established immunoaffinity-based approaches and analysed for insulin signalling-related proteins using validated immunoassays or proteomic techniques.46

Participants will be asked to provide stool samples using commercially available collection kits containing a DNA stabilising agent and sterile containers. Samples should be delivered to the study site as soon as possible after collection. If immediate transport is not feasible, samples may be temporarily stored in a household freezer at approximately −20°C and transferred under frozen conditions within 24 hours. On receipt, all stool samples will be stored at −80°C in an ultra-low temperature freezer until further analysis.

Adverse events

Adverse events (AEs) are defined as any unfavourable or unintended medical occurrences experienced by a participant during the trial period, regardless of causal relationship to the intervention. AEs may present as symptoms, clinical signs or abnormal findings on clinical investigations. Serious adverse events (SAEs) are events that result in death, are life-threatening, require hospitalisation or prolongation of hospitalisation, cause significant disability or impairment of work capacity or result in congenital anomalies.

All AEs, including SAEs, will be recorded in the electronic case report form (eCRF) and followed until resolution, return to baseline or confirmation of non-association with the intervention. All SAEs will be reported within 24 hours to the sponsor, ethics committee and relevant regulatory authorities. If continued participation is deemed inappropriate for participant safety, the intervention will be discontinued and appropriate medical care provided.

Withdrawal

Participants can withdraw at any time if they wish to do so without penalty. Participants who voluntarily discontinue the trial will be invited to take part in an interview to explore factors contributing to early withdrawal. Additionally, participants will be withdrawn if glucose-lowering pharmacotherapy is initiated or if safety concerns arise.

Stoppage rules

This trial will be stopped if three or more SAEs occur within any 30-day period in the same intervention group or if the participant withdrawal rate exceeds 20%.

Sample size

The sample size was calculated using PASS statistical software (V.21.0.3) based on the expected intervention effect. The assumptions were informed by an unpublished 4-month pilot study involving eight participants with pre-diabetes. Based on the pilot findings, the between-group difference in the mean relative reduction of iAUC after the 6-month intervention was assumed to be 10 percentage points, with a SD of 25 percentage points. With a two-sided significance level of 0.05 and a statistical power of 80%, the estimated minimum sample size was 200 participants. Allowing for an anticipated 20% loss to follow-up, this study plans to recruit 250 participants.

Allocation

Eligible participants will be assigned in a 1:1 ratio to the two dietary intervention arms. Randomisation will be stratified by clinical centre and sex and implemented using a web-based central allocation system. To minimise between-centre variability, a competitive enrolment strategy will be applied. The randomisation sequence will be generated by an independent data management team using a validated algorithm. Allocation concealment will be ensured, as study personnel will not have access to group assignments prior to randomisation. All randomisation data will be stored in a secure database for audit and quality control.

Blinding

Owing to unavoidable differences in the appearance and palatability of the two dietary interventions, participant blinding could not be fully achieved. To minimise potential bias, all carbohydrate-based staple foods will be provided in identical packaging, and participants will not be informed of the dietary group corresponding to the foods they receive. Investigators responsible for clinical outcome assessment, data management and statistical analyses will remain blinded to group allocation, and participants will not have access to glucose data generated by their CGM devices during the intervention period.

Data collection and management

Participant data will be collected using eCRFs completed by trained study personnel. All personal identifiers will be removed, and participants will be identified only by unique study identification numbers. Access to study data will be restricted to authorised investigators. Data will be stored in a centralised database with role-based access control and encryption, in compliance with relevant data protection and privacy regulations. Biological samples will be securely stored in the research biobank of Shanghai Jiao Tong University Affiliated Sixth People’s Hospital and labelled solely with study identification numbers.

Statistical analysis

All efficacy analyses will follow the intention-to-treat principle. Clinical data will be analysed using SAS software (V.9.4), while multi-omics analyses will be conducted using R software (V.4.3.0 or higher). Missing values will be addressed through multiple imputation methods. Continuous variables will be described using means with SD or means with 95% CIs when normally distributed and medians with IQRs otherwise. Categorical variables will be summarised as frequencies and percentages. Distributional assumptions will be examined using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. All hypothesis testing will be two-sided, with statistical significance defined as p<0.05.

Baseline variable differences between groups and the within-group variable changes before and after the intervention will be evaluated using mixed-effects modelling approaches, accounting for the effects of clinical trial centre. To assess intervention effects over time, mixed-effects models will be applied with treatment group, visit (categorical) and treatment-by-visit interaction specified as fixed effects and participant and clinical trial centre included as random effects. Corresponding baseline values will be incorporated as covariates to account for initial differences. Outcome variables measured only at baseline and post-intervention will be analysed using analysis of covariance with adjustment for baseline values and centre.

For multi-omics data, within-group changes will be examined using repeated-measures analysis of variance, paired t tests, Wilcoxon signed-rank tests or other appropriate statistical methods. Comparisons between intervention groups will be conducted using unpaired parametric or non-parametric tests, MaAsLin347 or other suitable analytical frameworks. The relationships among omics features, as well as between omics data and clinical variables, will be explored using Spearman correlation, partial Spearman correlation or other adequate analyses. Adjusted analyses will consider age, sex, baseline BMI and study centre as prespecified covariates. Depending on the time points and samples included, treatment group and visit will additionally be considered to account for intervention-related and temporal variation.

Monitoring

An independent Data Monitoring Committee (DMC) will be established to oversee the conduct of the study. In the event that a participant experiences an SAE during the trial, the DMC, in consultation with the attending physician and principal investigator, will determine whether the participant should continue or discontinue the intervention and the event will be reported to the relevant ethics committee. In cases of medical emergencies occurring at a clinical site, study staff will provide all necessary supportive medical care under the direction of the principal investigator or designated personnel.

Ethics and dissemination

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine Affiliated Sixth People’s Hospital (Approval No. 2025-122) and the Ethics Committee of Shenzhen Center for Chronic Disease Control (Approval No. SZCCC-2024-011-01-PJ). The study was registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry. Written informed consent will be obtained from all participants prior to study participation. Any protocol amendments will be submitted to the Ethics Committees. The principal investigator will have access to the final trial dataset, and any data sharing will require approval from the principal investigator and completion of a data use agreement. Dissemination of study findings will include peer-reviewed publications, conference presentations and media reports.

Discussion

This trial is designed to evaluate the effects of a recently proposed dietary strategy focused on optimising carbohydrate composition on glycaemic management in individuals with pre-diabetes. After the 6-month intervention period, the OCD will be compared with a guideline-based CD with respect to glycaemic and other metabolic outcomes. A subsequent follow-up phase will assess whether the metabolic benefits achieved during the intervention are sustained after discontinuation of the dietary regimen, thereby providing insight into the longer-term effects of OCD on glycaemic control.

Evidence from randomised controlled trials indicates that those who achieved normoglycaemia through lifestyle intervention had an approximately 56% lower risk of developing diabetes over the subsequent 10 years than those with persistent pre-diabetes.9 This highlights the importance of developing effective dietary strategies to promote pre-diabetes remission. Such remission extends beyond short-term glycaemic improvement and may involve broader metabolic remodelling, including favourable fat redistribution, insulin sensitivity and β-cell function.5 48 49 As a fermentable prebiotic, RS may influence this process partly through reshaping the gut microbiota and modulating microbial metabolites.22 27 28 However, several previous trials evaluating RS supplementation alone have reported modest or inconsistent metabolic benefits, potentially because the background diet, which can substantially influence microbial composition, was not comprehensively controlled.50–52

A potential advantage of the OCD is that it does not treat RS as an isolated supplement. Instead, it integrates increased RS intake with reductions in RDS and free sugars, thereby improving overall carbohydrate quality within a nutritionally balanced dietary pattern. By simultaneously optimising multiple components of carbohydrate intake while preserving carbohydrate-based staple foods, OCD may promote more coherent interactions between diet, the gut microbiota and host metabolism and may be more acceptable and sustainable than restrictive dietary strategies. This trial will therefore determine whether this integrated approach to carbohydrate-quality optimisation provides clinically meaningful and sustainable metabolic benefits in individuals with pre-diabetes.

Moreover, the multicentre randomised controlled design of the study and relatively long intervention and follow-up periods will enable a comprehensive evaluation of the effects and sustainability of the OCD. A further strength of this study is its multidimensional and multimodal assessment framework. In addition to metabolic endpoints, validated questionnaires, rating scales and fMRI will be integrated to assess intervention-related changes in sleep, appetite and food preferences. Biological samples collected during the trial will also be subjected to multi-omics analyses to characterise the biological signatures induced by the intervention and explore the potential mechanisms underlying its metabolic and behavioural effects. This comprehensive approach will enable the effects of the intervention to be characterised beyond conventional metabolic outcomes and provide complementary clinical and molecular evidence regarding the response of pre-diabetes to the OCD.

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. First, participants cannot be blinded because the appearance, texture and palatability of the staple foods cannot be fully matched between the OCD and a conventional dietary intervention for pre-diabetes. Awareness of the assigned diet may influence participants’ dietary behaviour, adherence or reporting, potentially introducing performance and information bias. To mitigate these risks, study foods will be provided in identical packaging, participants will not be explicitly informed which dietary pattern is hypothesised to be superior, and they will not have access to real-time CGM data during the intervention. Outcome assessors, data managers and statisticians will also remain blinded to group allocation. Second, the assessment of dietary adherence relies partly on participant-reported information, which may introduce information bias. To maximise the reliability of adherence assessment, multiple complementary methods will be used, including pre- and post-meal photographs uploaded through the study application, dietary questionnaires, staple-food distribution records, returned uneaten study foods and empty packages and regular interviews.

Taken together, the study will provide evidence not only on the efficacy of OCD for glycaemic control and pre-diabetes remission, but also on its sustainability, broader metabolic and behavioural effects and potential underlying mechanisms. It is hoped that OCD may offer a feasible and sustainable dietary option for individuals with pre-diabetes and broaden the range of practical lifestyle strategies available for the early prevention of type 2 diabetes.
