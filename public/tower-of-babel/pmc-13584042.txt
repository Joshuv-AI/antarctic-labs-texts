Drift-tolerant artificial olfaction via spatiotemporal encoding of gas diffusion for VOC discrimination
Authors: Shu He, Bingxue Cao, Xinyi Zhang, Yabin Liu, Yongheng Zhang, Haowen Guo, Xingchun Zhai, Min Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummarySpatiotemporal encoding constructs drift-tolerant artificial-olfaction systems using a single sensing material. Here, we present a protocol for discriminating complex volatile organic compounds (VOCs) via this approach. We describe steps for hydrothermally synthesizing a porous CuP@G chemoresistive ink and laser-fabricating programmed micro-architectures. We then detail procedures for micro-cavity device assembly, spatiotemporal signal acquisition, and machine-learning interpretation.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Zhai et al.1Subject areas: Physics, Chemistry, Material sciences

Before you begin

VOCs serve as critical latent fingerprints for medical diagnostics, environmental safety, and industrial monitoring. While traditional electronic noses (e-noses) offer biomimetic alternatives to bulky instruments, their reliance on a “chemical diversity” paradigm2,3,4 and heterogeneous sensor arrays inevitably leads to non-synchronous baseline drift, necessitating frequent recalibration.5,6 To bypass these system-level bottlenecks, this protocol details a time-space-resolved (TSR) physical encoding strategy for artificial olfaction. By utilizing a single hierarchically porous sensing material (e.g., the copper(II)-coordinated polydopamine/reduced graphene oxide, CuP@G ink), this approach derives gas selectivity from deterministic physical micro-architectures, converting gas diffusion dynamics into reproducible spatiotemporal fingerprints. We outline step-by-step execution pathways for four progressively evolved diffusion architectures (Figure 1). Users can select a standalone platform or a targeted combination based on specific analytical goals.

Structural evolution of the TSR encoding architectures

(A) OHSA based on sequential folded layers.

(B) CISS utilizing planar integration.

(C) SCM device featuring vertically stacked layers.

(D) RHC e-nose enabling multidimensional spatiotemporal mapping.

The Origami Hierarchical Sensor Array (OHSA)7 utilizes an ultra-low-cost, folded paper framework and baseline P/G ink for sequential diffusion delays, ideal for decoupling physical interferences and basic chiral recognition. To overcome mass-transfer resistance in denser integrations, the advanced frameworks mandate the porous CuP@G ink. The Chromatography-Inspired Single-Sensor (CISS)8 employs progressive polymer thickness gradients (e.g., PDMS) to translate gas-solid partition coefficients into explicit 1D temporal delays, effectively decoupling VOC polarity. The Spatiotemporal Chromatography-Mimicking (SCM)9 architecture uses vertically stacked gas-permeable layers to capture 3D spatial concentration gradients for resolving complex mixtures. Finally, the Radial-Hierarchical Chromatomimetic (RHC)1 system integrates vertical sequential delays with horizontal radial diffusion via symmetric electrode arrays, extracting decoupled, multidimensional spatial descriptors (Sc, Sp, Sc/Sp) to provide maximum resolution for highly complex matrices.

This protocol describes the steps to utilize these architectures to discriminate standard VOCs, resolve isomers and enantiomers, and evaluate complex real-world matrices, such as assessing heated tobacco geographic origins and quality grades for point-of-care testing. Execution requires foundational knowledge in nanomaterial synthesis alongside proficiency in Python-based data analysis, specifically multivariate statistics (PCA, LDA) and deep learning architectures (ResNet-34, MTL-CNN) for decoding high-dimensional spatiotemporal matrices.

Innovation

While conventional e-nose systems rely on chemically diverse sensor arrays prone to non-synchronous baseline drift, this protocol details a time–space–resolved encoding workflow that derives gas selectivity from structural design using a single sensing material. To execute this drift-free strategy across diverse analytical needs, the protocol standardizes the independent fabrication of four progressively evolved platforms: OHSA written with a baseline reference P/G ink, alongside CISS, SCM, and RHC microchambers laser-engraved onto polymers and loaded with a hydrothermally synthesized porous aggregate CuP@G ink. Operationally, these customized microchambers modulate laminar flow fields (>0.1 m/s) and boundary-layer gas diffusion kinetics, converting VOC adsorption into predictable, highly reproducible spatiotemporal delay signals. Finally, the workflow couples this physical gas encoding with ResNet-34 and MTL-CNN integrated with SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP). This automated computational decoding resolves the typical “black-box” ambiguity of electronic olfaction, enabling direct predictive classification of complex matrices from raw resistance signals.

This final step delineates the computational workflow for translating raw spatiotemporal signals into precise recognition results. The data processing logic follows a task-oriented dual-track approach. Initially, all raw sensor signals are standardized into ΔR/R0 (%). For fundamental performance evaluation involving pure VOCs and specific binary mixtures, dimensionality reduction algorithms—primarily PCA—are utilized to extract features and visualize distinct spatiotemporal fingerprints. Choose the appropriate analytical pathway based on your device architecture for complex real-world predictive classification: follow Step 13 if you are analyzing data from the OHSA; follow Step 14 if you are processing CISS e-nose data, which deploys ResNet-34 for image-like feature extraction; or follow Step 15 if you are analyzing data from the SCM or RHC platforms, as both architectures utilize an MTL-CNN framework for simultaneous multi-task classification.

Expected outcomes

Successful execution of this protocol provides quantitative benchmarking standards across four interconnected tiers. At the material synthesis level, the resulting CuP@G ink characteristically exhibits a high specific surface area (∼50.09 m2/g) enriched with active mixed-valence copper heterojunctions. Hydrodynamically, the pre-optimized microchambers deterministically sustain uniform laminar flow fields (>0.1 m/s) and rapid concentration equilibration (∼4.5 s), converting ambient gas scattering into predictable transport kinetics. During routine gas sensing, these integrated modules consistently afford drift-tolerant baseline stability (<± 0.5% drift), low-ppb detection limits (e.g., 8.48 ppb), and robust signal repeatability over a 180-day operational lifespan. At the computational terminus, multidimensional spatiotemporal decoding via ResNet-34 and MTL-CNN architectures unambiguously resolves challenging structural isomers and chiral enantiomers, predicting complex real-world matrices with a classification accuracy exceeding 92%. To guide replication, these outcomes delineate mandatory bench-top verification thresholds from literature-derived performance ceilings.

Limitations

This protocol establishes a generalized sensing framework by substituting chemical diversity with programmable encoding. While this paradigm intrinsically resolves the non-synchronous drift and heavy calibration burdens of traditional multi-sensor arrays, several boundary conditions dictate its deployment. Foremost, spatiotemporal encoding relies entirely on reversible gas-solid interactions. Irreversible chemisorption fundamentally disrupts the encoded transport kinetics, restricting the optimal analyte scope strictly to species undergoing physisorption or weak, reversible coordination. Compounding this chemical constraint is the cross-sensitivity to reactive inorganic gases. While optimized for VOC discrimination, the chemoresistive inks possess active sites intrinsically responsive to strong redox-active species (e.g., NH3, NO2). Such reactive inorganic gases act as severe chemical interferents that obscure the encoded spatiotemporal delays, mandating appropriate pre-filtration mechanisms for deployment in highly contaminated environments. Additionally, like most adsorption-based sensors, the demonstrated materials exhibit ambient humidity susceptibility. Because water vapor modulates both the baseline (R0) and response amplitude (ΔR/R0), practical deployment strictly necessitates hardware-level humidity compensation or the integration of broad humidity variance into the training datasets.10,11,12

Beyond fundamental sensing mechanisms, the system architecture also imposes specific operational constraints. Inherently, obtaining a complete spatiotemporal “fingerprint” introduces kinetic latency due to the progressive diffusion of gas through structural barriers, rendering the approach unsuitable for millisecond-level, instantaneous process analysis. Furthermore, translating these theoretical diffusion models into physical devices demands stringent manufacturing tolerances. High-precision micro-architectures (e.g., laser-ablated radial arrays) require strict consistency control to ensure reproducible spatial descriptors. Finally, at the computational terminus, there is a heavy algorithmic dependence. Extracting actionable features from high-dimensional spatiotemporal matrices necessitates advanced machine learning. While yielding high discriminative power, this reliance demands supplementary explainable AI frameworks (e.g., SHAP) to prevent “black-box” models and ensure rigorous physicochemical interpretability.13,14

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Loss of spatiotemporal resolution and negligible precipitate yield during the hydrothermal synthesis, resulting in multi-channels exhibiting identical response profiles (related to Step 3e(ii)).

Potential solution

This issue typically arises when the hierarchical porous structure fails to form due to severe GO aggregation or improper pH levels. Ensure that the NaOH addition accurately reaches the target pH (∼4.25) and verify the temperature calibration of the hydrothermal oven. If the problem persists, re-synthesize the ink. It is mandatory to visually and quantitatively verify the 3D porosity and specific surface area via SEM and BET characterizations before proceeding to device assembly.

Problem 2

Discontinuous high-resistance ink lanes and poor sensing repeatability among intra-array sensors in the OHSA structure (related to Step 4d).

Potential solution

This variation is primarily caused by the “coffee-ring” effect during ink drop-casting or uneven writing, leading to an uneven spatial distribution of the sensing material. For paper substrates, re-write the ink layer 2–3 times to guarantee a continuous thickness.

Problem 3

Discontinuous LIG electrodes, poor spatial resolution, or extremely high baseline resistance following laser ablation of the GLIGIE array (related to Step 5d).

Potential solution

These structural defects are generally caused by a laser focus offset due to uneven substrate surfaces or laser “over-burning” that induces topological collapse. Thoroughly flatten the polyimide substrate using a vacuum chuck or double-sided tape. Strictly re-calibrate the laser Z-axis focal length specifically for the target plane. Optimize the laser power and scanning speed by performing a parameter sweep on the substrate margins to confirm continuous porous morphology before fabricating the main array.

Problem 4

Structural defects or discontinuous electrodes during the engraving of the LIGPIE array on PF paper (related to Step 6a).

Potential solution

Similar to the laser ablation issues encountered in Step 5d (problem 3), this is often due to improper laser focus or parameter settings. Thoroughly flatten the paper substrate and meticulously re-calibrate the laser Z-axis. Adjust laser power and speed on a test area to avoid over-burning the porous paper structure.

Problem 5

Poor spatial resolution or electrode discontinuity when fabricating the radial PLIGIE array (related to Step 7a).

Potential solution

As described in problems 3 and 4, laser ablation defects arise from focus offset or unoptimized laser parameters. Ensure the PF paper substrate is completely flat, calibrate the Z-axis focal length specifically for the target plane, and conduct parameter sweeps to maintain a continuous porous morphology for the complex radial 5-electrode pattern.

Problem 6

Poor sensing repeatability among intra-array sensors in the CISS architecture (related to Step 9a).

Potential solution

This variation is primarily caused by the “coffee-ring” effect during ink drop-casting, which leads to uneven spatial distribution of the sensing material and subsequently affects the PDMS overlayer. For planar architectures, ensure the drying oven is pre-heated to exactly 80°C and transfer the chip into the oven within 30 seconds of deposition to rapidly evaporate the solvent.

Problem 7

Irreversible baseline drift and significantly reduced VOC discrimination capability during routine testing (related to Step 12b).

Potential solution

This phenomenon indicates cumulative deep structural poisoning resulting from continuous exposure to heavy, high-boiling-point aerosols. Since macromolecular chemisorption is practically irreversible, the poisoned sensor must be discarded. For future measurements, strictly limit the continuous exposure time to complex matrices and extend the pure nitrogen purging intervals between testing cycles to completely eliminate internal carryover.

Problem 8

The ResNet-34 deep-learning model exhibits exceptionally high training accuracy but fails completely during blind testing (related to Step 14i).

Potential solution

This signifies severe “shortcut learning” (overfitting), where the neural network erroneously anchors on absolute concentration-dependent amplitudes rather than the physically encoded diffusion patterns. To rectify this, revise your feature extraction algorithms. Apply strict signal normalization to the raw spatiotemporal matrices and force the network to prioritize intensity-invariant features (e.g., dynamic slopes and time delays), effectively blinding the model to trivial gas concentration fluctuations.

Problem 9

Erratic spatial descriptor (Sc/Sp) ratios and distorted multidimensional mapping in the RHC architecture (related to Step 15a(iv)).

Potential solution

This deviation stems from the physical disruption of radial symmetry. An interlayer misalignment of > 100 μm during vertical stacking will severely skew the isotropic gas diffusion pathways. Discard the misaligned sensor module. Re-assemble the structural layers utilizing a high-precision micromanipulator or predefined alignment markers to strictly confine concentric deviations to under 50 μm.

Problem 10

The MTL-CNN model shows high training accuracy but performs poorly on the testing set during simultaneous multi-task classification (related to Step 15f(iv)).

Potential solution

Similar to the “shortcut learning” issue described in problem 8, the model may be overfitting to trivial absolute concentration fluctuations instead of learning the true physical diffusion patterns. Apply strict signal normalization (ΔR/R0) to the raw data matrices and ensure the network prioritizes intensity-invariant dynamic delays.15,16
