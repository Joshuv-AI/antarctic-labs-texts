Kinematics of Stellar Streams from Globular Clusters Depend on Black Hole Retention and Star Mass: A Selection Effect for Dark Matter Inference CMC ( Rodriguez et al. 2021 ) , COSMIC ( Breivik et al. 2020a ) , Gala ( Price-Whelan et al. 2020 ) , Astropy ( Astropy Collaboration et al. 2013 ) , matplotlib ( Hunter 2007 ) , SciPy ( Virtanen et al. 2020 ) , NumPy ( Harris et al. 2020 ) , pandas ( Reback et al. 2022 ) .
Newlin C. Weatherford, Ana Bonaca

Abstract
Velocity dispersion ( σ \sigma ) in stellar streams from globular clusters (GCs) is sensitive to heating by Galactic substructure, including dark matter (DM) subhalos. Recent studies have compared σ \sigma in observed and modeled streams to probe DM properties, but have relied on stream models that neglect strong encounters, black holes (BHs), and mass segregation in GCs. Such phenomena may inflate stream σ \sigma or introduce selection effects—e.g., a σ \sigma that depends on star mass ( m m ). We investigate this prospect using Monte Carlo N N -body simulations of GCs under static Galactic tides to generate mock streams with realistic mass and velocity distributions. We find σ \sigma correlates with m m , especially after core collapse (the GC’s observable increase in central density upon ejecting its BHs), rising from 1.2 1.2 – 2.2 ​ km ​ s − 1 2.2\,{\rm km\,s}^{-1} between m = 0.3 m=0.3 – 0.8 ​ M ⊙ 0.8\,M_{\odot} , with typical kinematic cuts on stream membership. Similar in magnitude to heating by Galactic substructure, this enhancement occurs because the GC’s loss of BHs allows its most-massive stars to occupy its dense center, raising their likelihood of strong ejection via binary interactions and adding broad, exponential wings to the stream’s velocity distribution. Streams’ kinematics thus probe properties (density, BH retention) of their progenitor GCs. Our results also imply observations of streams from some GCs, especially those not subject to highly episodic mass loss, may select for higher σ \sigma than predicted by models neglecting σ \sigma ’s m m -dependence. This would cause observed σ \sigma in streams—already on the low side of expectations for cold DM—to further favor alternatives such as warm, ultralight, or self-interacting DM.



I
Introduction

Galactic archeologists have detected abundant Milky Way (MW) substructure, including
{>}120
stellar streams
(
Helmi 2020
;
Mateu 2023
;
Bonaca & Price-Whelan 2025
)
. These strands of comoving stars are debris from dwarf galaxies accreted and tidally disrupted by the MW, and from dissolving globular clusters (GCs) born in those dwarfs (ex situ) or in the MW itself
(
Bonaca et al. 2021
, in situ;)
. 20 streams (1/6) have been firmly traced to surviving GCs—some even connecting directly to a GC as
tidal tails

(
Piatti & Carballo-Bello 2020
)
—while many more are likely from GCs that have recently dissolved
(
Bonaca & Price-Whelan 2025
)
.

As kinematically cold structures sensitive to perturbative heating, streams are excellent probes of the MW’s mass profile, substructure, and assembly history
(
Koposov et al. 2010
;
Bonaca et al. 2014
;
Küpper et al. 2015
;
Bovy et al. 2016
;
Bonaca & Hogg 2018
)
. They also provide clues to the nature of dark matter (DM). Canonical cold DM cosmology predicts clumpy DM halos
(
Klypin et al. 1999
;
Moore et al. 1999
)
with more numerous and centrally dense (cuspy) subhalos, on average, than warm DM
(
Bode et al. 2001
;
Lovell et al. 2014
)
, ultralight DM
(
Hu et al. 2000
;
Hui et al. 2017
)
, or self-interacting DM
(
Spergel & Steinhardt 2000
;
Tulin & Yu 2018
, which also yields more diverse subhalo densities;)
. So galaxy models with cold DM predict frequent encounters with subhalos strongly perturb streams, yielding streams with higher width and internal velocity dispersion
\sigma

(
Johnston et al. 2002
;
Ibata et al. 2002
;
Mayer et al. 2002
;
Carlberg 2009
)
—and more numerous and pronounced irregularities, such as gaps
(
Siegal-Gaskins & Valluri 2008
;
Carlberg et al. 2011
;
Yoon et al. 2011
)
—than most alternative DM models.

Since these first studies, most efforts to use streams to probe DM focus on
morphology
—gaps, kinks, and spurs in stream density profiles left by passing subhalos
(
Carlberg 2012
;
Carlberg et al. 2012
;
Carlberg & Grillmair 2013
;
Carlberg 2013
;
Carlberg 2016
;
Ibata et al. 2016
;
Helmi & Koppelman 2016
;
Erkal et al. 2016
;
Bovy et al. 2017
;
Bonaca et al. 2019
;
Carlberg 2020
;
Banik et al. 2021
;
Menker & Benson 2024
;
Adams et al. 2024
;
Zhang et al. 2025
;
Nibauer et al. 2025
)
. Broad surveys with full kinematic detail (especially radial velocities) remain limited, so it is unsurprising that fewer studies explore how such encounters affect stream
kinematics

(
Carlberg 2015
;
Carlberg & Grillmair 2016
;
Carlberg & Agler 2023
;
Carlberg et al. 2024a
;
Carlberg et al. 2024b
;
Carlberg 2025
)
. Yet these works show
\sigma
is a powerful metric for DM inference. Heating by subhalos widens the velocity distribution in GC streams, adding, to a dominant cold component (
\sigma\sim 1
–
2\,{\rm km\,s}^{-1}
), exponential wings whose
\sigma
is sensitive to the DM model
(
Carlberg et al. 2024b
,
6\,{\rm km\,s}^{-1}
for cold DM and
3
–
4\,{\rm km\,s}^{-1}
for 5.5–7 keV warm DM;)
. Subhalo heating is thus one of several possible explanations for predominately cold streams enveloped by a diffuse warm “cocoon"
(
Malhan et al. 2019
;
Valluri et al. 2025
;
Carlberg 2025
, e.g., GD-1;)
.

Stream
\sigma
is doubly useful for GCs born ex situ, which experience tidal stripping in their parent halo prior to accretion by the MW
(
Carlberg 2018
)
.
\sigma
then probes the form of the parent, too; models by
Malhan et al. 2021a
;
Malhan et al. 2022
show GCs born ex situ in cuspy halos produce streams with higher
\sigma
(
3
–
10\,{\rm km\,s}^{-1}
, depending on halo mass
M_{h}
) than GCs born in cored halos (
1
–
4\,{\rm km\,s}^{-1}
) or in situ (
{\lesssim}1\,{\rm km\,s}^{-1}
). They note many streams from GCs have
\sigma\lesssim 3\,{\rm km\,s}^{-1}
, ruling out origins in cuspy DM halos with
M_{h}\gtrsim 10^{8}\,M_{\odot}
. This point
(
Gialluca et al. 2021
;
Grillmair 2025
, see also)
applies to Fjörm, Gjöll, and Sylgr (from the GCs M68, NGC 3201, and NGC 5024, respectively), GD-1 and Phlegethon (unknown progenitors), and NGC 0288’s stream. As these streams’ orbits indicate ex-situ origins
(
Bonaca et al. 2021
)
, their low
\sigma
is in soft tension with cuspy cold DM, especially given the expectation of further heating by subhalos after accretion.

The low
\sigma
of many streams and the relatively small (few-
{\rm km\,s}^{-1}
) impacts of different DM models or parent halos mean that other effects capable of shifting
\sigma
by
{\gtrsim}0.5\,{\rm km\,s}^{-1}
are relevant when using
\sigma
to infer the properties of DM or parent halos. These include heating by baryonic MW substructures, spurious enhancement of
\sigma
from unresolved binaries or star properties (see Section
IV
), and internal GC dynamics.

Myriad dynamics drive escape from GCs
(
Weatherford et al. 2023
, for a thorough review, see)
, but most stars reach the requisite energy via two-body relaxation
(
Binney & Tremaine 2008
, the slow random-walk in energy from many weak encounters with other stars;)
, or via strengthening external tides during perigalacticon
(
Baumgardt & Makino 2003
)
or passage by/through some other perturber
(
Ostriker et al. 1972
, e.g., the Galactic disk;)
. Each passage temporarily shrinks the GC’s tidal boundary, lowering the threshold energy for escape, and tidally shocks stars with orbital periods longer than the passage. This mostly strips the GC’s outermost stars, but the lower escape energy aids ejection from its core, too. Also, since relaxation is fastest in the core, most stars in GCs not subject to highly fluctuating tides first obtain enough energy to escape while passing through it
(
Spitzer & Shapiro 1972
;
Weatherford et al. 2023
)
. Once unbound, they take about a relaxation time (
{\sim}100\,{\rm Myr}
) to fully escape
(
Weatherford et al. 2024
)
, bouncing about inside the tidal equipotential surfaces enclosing the GC until eventually exiting via openings centered on the L1/L2 Lagrange points (in the approximate case of the circular restricted three-body problem). Since relaxation and gently fluctuating tides only weakly unbind stars, these openings are very narrow for most escapers, tightly collimating them into a stream with
\sigma\lesssim 2\,{\rm km\,s}^{-1}
.

Relaxation is not the only phenomenon in GCs relevant to streams, but its effects are dominant and predictable. Hence most of the above studies of stream kinematics neglect collisional dynamics in the GC. Even those that use direct
N
-body codes to partially model these dynamics
(
Malhan et al. 2021a
;
Malhan et al. 2022
;
Carlberg et al. 2024a
;
Carlberg et al. 2024b
;
Carlberg 2025
)
hasten computation by using softening lengths 1–2
\,{\rm pc}
and assuming all stars have equal mass. They thus neglect some essential drivers of GC evolution: strong encounters (which occur on scales
{\ll}1\,{\rm pc}
), mass segregation, and stellar-mass black holes (BHs). While most impactful in the core, these factors may significantly affect some streams. Strong encounters in the core can eject up to
{\sim}10\%
of escapers from dense GCs, their higher energies leading to less-collimated escape and a wider, hotter subcomponent in the stream
(
Weatherford et al. 2024
)
. And the BHs that form in a GC are essential heat sources supporting the core against collapse
(
Merritt et al. 2004
;
Mackey et al. 2007
;
Mackey et al. 2008
;
Breen & Heggie 2013
;
Kremer et al. 2019
;
Kremer et al. 2020
, e.g.,)
. After mass-segregating to the center, they pair up into binaries that undergo strong encounters with other BHs and stars, hardening the binaries and accelerating the bodies involved. These encounters eject BHs from the core; those that do not escape the GC entirely resegregate back to the center, passing more energy to surrounding stars. This greatly enhances stream density if it expands the GC’s half-mass radius too close to its tidal boundary, potentially explaining the Pal 5 stream’s high density
(
Gieles et al. 2021
;
Gieles & Gnedin 2023
;
Roberts et al. 2024
)
.

Relevant selection effects may also arise from these dynamics. In particular,
\sigma
in streams may depend on star mass
m
, as it does inside GCs
(
Heggie & Hut 2003
, typically anticorrelating with
m
locally, but not globally;)
. There are at least two competing reasons to expect this. Since GCs are mass-segregated, the lowest-
m
stars tend to orbit the most energetically with the furthest apocenters, easing escape. So low-
m
stars escape earliest, on average, and model streams exhibit slight
m
gradients, especially close to dissolution of the highly mass-segregated core
(
Webb & Bovy 2022
)
. It is thus tempting to imagine that the higher energy of low-
m
stars, when stacked with the energy applied by the final ejection kick, would cause
\sigma
in the stream to anticorrelate with
m
. But the notion of a single discrete kick only applies to strong encounters, while most ejecta (from relaxation and tides) barely drift across the tidal boundary, regardless of
m
. This leaves little prospect of a significant
m
–
\sigma
anticorrelation in the stream.
\sigma
may instead
correlate
with
m
, as the highest-
m
stars, having mass-segregated deepest into the core, would be ejected the most often by the strong encounters there. As we shall show later, this possibility turns out to be correct.

Stream observations are often highly magnitude-limited, so any dependence of
\sigma
on
m
, and thereby luminosity, may bias the observationally inferred
\sigma
—and any DM inference based on it. This would be especially relevant to the distant, low-eccentricity streams furthest from the Galactic center—the very streams best suited to probing the MW’s own DM halo
Sandford et al. 2017
—and to extragalactic streams, essential to probing DM halos more broadly
(
Pearson et al. 2019
)
. This motivates investigation of any potential
m
dependence in the kinematics of streams from dense star clusters. To do so we simulate GCs and their streams with all relevant collisional dynamics, stellar evolution, and a realistic stellar mass function and stream velocity distribution.

Numerical Models

II.1
Globular Cluster Simulations

To reliably investigate the prospect of any
m
dependence in a GC stream’s velocity distribution, we must fully simulate the GC’s internal dynamics, including strong encounters that eject stars at high speeds. We do so using our publicly available
Cluster Monte Carlo
code
(
Rodriguez et al. 2022
,
CMC
;)
. Developed over 25 years,
CMC
is a state-of-the-art implementation of the orbit-averaged Monte Carlo method
(
Hénon 1971a
;
Hénon 1971b
)
and can simulate typical MWGCs in
{\sim}10^{3}
–
10^{4}
times less computing time than a direct
N
-body approach while still accurately capturing the internal dynamics relevant to escape. This includes the evaporative (low-speed) escape mechanisms most relevant to stream formation: two-body relaxation
(
Joshi et al. 2000
;
Pattabiraman et al. 2013
)
coupled with the Galactic tide
(
Joshi et al. 2001
;
Chatterjee et al. 2010
)
.
CMC
also features many high-speed ejection mechanisms, such as strong binary-mediated interactions, three-body binary formation
(
Morscher et al. 2013
;
Morscher et al. 2015
, i.e., from three singles;)
, gravitational-wave-driven mergers
(
Rodriguez et al. 2018a
;
Rodriguez et al. 2018b
)
, and supernova kicks during stellar evolution
(
Breivik et al. 2020b
, with
COSMIC
;)
. We focus on the the first three of these mechanisms—the most relevant to high-speed ejection from evolved GCs—but for more thorough descriptions of
CMC
and its dynamics relevant to escape, see
Rodriguez et al. 2022
and
Weatherford et al. 2023
, respectively. While
CMC
allows physical collisions, we do not consider asymmetric mass loss in such events that may eject the remnant at high speed. Nor do we consider high-speed ejection by a central intermediate-mass BH, which may be present in some GCs.

For simplicity and consistency with earlier models by
Kremer et al. 2020
and
Weatherford et al. 2023
;
Weatherford et al. 2024
, we run a grid of twelve GC simulations to an age of
14\,{\rm Gyr}
—three statistically independent realizations each of four models designed to represent archetypal core-collapsed and non-core-collapsed GCs (explained shortly). These models differ only in their initial virial radius,
r_{v}=(0.5,2)\,{\rm pc}
, and binary fraction for massive stars,
f_{b,h}=f_{b}(m>15\,M_{\odot})=(5\%,50\%)
; the binary fraction at lower masses is a flat
5\%
, typical of MWGCs at present
Milone et al. 2012
. We chose to vary
f_{b,h}
thinking
f_{b,h}=50\%
—more in accord with observations of young star clusters
(
Sana et al. 2009
;
Sana et al. 2012
;
Moe & Di Stefano 2017
)
—may enhance strong ejections. Yet there turned out to be no significant enhancement at the ages typical of MWGCs.

The more important parameter is
r_{v}
. The higher initial density in the simulations with
r_{v}=0.5\,{\rm pc}
leads to faster dynamics; by an age corresponding to the present (
{\approx}12\,{\rm Gyr}
), they eject almost all of their central BH population. The loss of heating from BH dynamics allows the GC’s core to contract, forming a cuspy inner surface density profile characteristic of an observably
core-collapsed
state
(
Trager et al. 1995
,
{\approx}20\%
of surviving MWGCs;)
. But the simulations born with
r_{v}=2\,{\rm pc}
are initially puffy enough to retain a substantial fraction of their BH populations all the way to the present, preserving a flat inner surface brightness profile characteristic of a
non-core-collapsed
state (
{\approx}80\%
of MWGCs).

All other initial conditions are chosen to be roughly average among MWGCs
(
Kremer et al. 2020
)
. Specifically, they have an initial total number of particles (single stars plus binaries)
N_{0}=8\times 10^{5}
, Galactocentric distance
R_{g}=8\,{\rm kpc}
, and metallicity
Z=0.1\,Z_{\odot}
. Initial positions and velocities of stars draw from a
King 1962
profile with concentration
W_{0}=5
. Stellar masses (primary mass, in the case of a binary) draw from the canonical
Kroupa 2001
stellar initial mass function from
0.08
–
150\,M_{\odot}
while secondary masses, assigned randomly to
N_{0}\times f_{b}
stars, draw uniformly in mass ratio from
q\in[0.08,1]

(
Duquennoy & Mayor 1991
)
. Binary orbital periods draw from a distribution flat in log scale from near contact to the hard/soft boundary
(
Duquennoy & Mayor 1991
)
and eccentricities are thermal
(
Heggie 1975
)
. We assume all neutron stars born in core-collapse (electron-capture) supernovae receive natal kicks drawn from a Maxwellian with dispersion
\sigma=265\,{\rm km\,s}^{-1}
(
20\,{\rm km\,s}^{-1}
), ejecting most of them from the GC at birth. Natal kicks for BHs share the neutron stars’ core-collapse kick distribution, but reduced in magnitude by the fraction of the stellar envelope’s mass that falls back onto the BH via the prescription from
Hobbs et al. 2005
—see also
Fryer et al. 2012
. This ejects
{\approx}1/2

Weatherford et al. 2024
or references therein.

II.2
Mock Stellar Streams

We follow the methods of
Weatherford et al. 2024
to model stellar streams from the
CMC
simulations, so consult that work for further detail. In short,
CMC
removes bodies from the simulation the moment they acquire enough energy to
potentially
escape
1
1

1

In fact,
CMC
removes a body one it has a bit
more
energy than needed to escape. Developed by
Giersz et al. 2008
and tuned to best match escape rates of direct
N
-body models, this feature roughly accounts for ongoing two-body scattering on the way out of the GC. Such encounters make escape slightly harder by scattering some unbound bodies back onto bound orbits
(
King 1959
;
Baumgardt 2001
)
, so those that
do
escape are preferentially the ones scattered to even higher energy. But this nuance has little impact on the stream
(
Weatherford et al. 2024
)
.
—usually from within the GC’s core, where relaxation is fastest. Since
CMC
’s dynamics are spherically symmetric, we then randomly and isotropically project the body’s position (strictly radial) and velocity (radial and tangential components) into full six-dimensional phase-space coordinates. We then use the Galactic dynamics code
Gala

(
Price-Whelan 2017
)
to evolve the trajectories of these escaping bodies in the rotating frame of the combined effective potential of the GC and MW. Since rigorous testing of
CMC
for eccentric GC orbits is still in progress, we assume the GC circularly orbits the Galactic center with orbital speed
v_{c}=220\,{\rm km\,s}^{-1}

(
Binney & Tremaine 2008
, e.g.,)
. For consistency with
CMC
’s default escape criterion, we fix the MW potential in
Gala
’s trajectory integration to be static, spherical, and logarithmic—
\phi_{g}(R)=v_{c}^{2}\ln(R)
—while the GC potential is allowed to evolve, updated every megayear from analytic fits to the GC potential in each
CMC
snapshot. As the GC loses mass
M_{c}
, its tidal radius
r_{t}
slowly shrinks with time
t
:

r_{t}(t)=\left[\frac{GM_{c}(t)R_{g}^{2}}{2v_{c}^{2}}\right]^{1/3}.

(1)

Conceptually, the above procedure reduces to shutting off collisional dynamics for any body in the GC once it becomes unbound and likely to escape (cross beyond
r_{t}
) within about the GC’s half-mass relaxation time, or
{\sim}100\,{\rm Myr}
. This decoupling from
CMC
’s spherically symmetric collisional dynamics is necessary to allow the truly asymmetric tidal field to channel escapers into realistic stellar streams.

III
Results

We now examine our streams’ kinematics, excluding compact objects (assumed unresolved). We also exclude binaries (
{\approx}4\%
of the stream for either
r_{v}
) to avoid, for now, considering their internal motions, effective observed mass, or selection effects. However, we share some details of the binaries in Section
IV.4
due to interest in how much unresolved binaries may bias observationally inferred radial velocities.

Our streams’ velocity distributions are
anisotropic
, so we analyze them via several metrics. This Section focuses on the distribution and dispersion
\sigma_{v}
of the absolute relative velocity between each stream star and the GC,
v=\lvert\vec{v_{\star}}-\vec{v_{c}}\rvert=\lvert(v_{\star,R},v_{\star,\phi}-v_{c},v_{\star,Z})\rvert
, using the Galactocentric cylindrical coordinates defined above. But we also report the distributions of
v
’s individual components
(v_{R},v_{\phi},v_{Z})
, and corresponding dispersions (
\sigma_{R},\sigma_{\phi},\sigma_{Z})
, in the Appendix. Due to the anisotropy,
\sigma_{v}^{2}<\sigma_{R}^{2}+\sigma_{\phi}^{2}+\sigma_{Z}^{2}
, and we find
\sigma_{R}>(\sigma_{\phi},\sigma_{Z})
by a constant
{\approx}1.0\,{\rm km\,s}^{-1}
for all
m
. Even so, all trends reported in this Section for (
v
,
\sigma_{v}
) also apply
qualitatively
to
(v_{R},v_{\phi},v_{Z})
and (
\sigma_{R},\sigma_{\phi},\sigma_{Z})
in the Appendix.
Hereafter, when we use
\sigma
with no subscript, we mean the velocity dispersion in streams more generally (any definition, theoretical or observed).

III.1
The Velocity Distribution and Escape Mechanisms

As described earlier, most stars escaping from GCs are unbound by two-body relaxation (2BR) or fluctuating tides. Since our GCs circularly orbit in a smooth Galactic potential, the tides do not fluctuate and 2BR dominates. There are also many types of strong encounters in a GC’s core that eject stars more vigorously, contributing a high-
v
tail to the stream’s velocity distribution. The strong ejection mechanisms most relevant to our evolved GCs are binary–single and binary–binary (BS/BB) interactions, and three-body binary formation (3BBF)—a close encounter between three singles that produces a binary, accelerating the leftover single and the new binary’s center-of-mass. But the 3BBF rate,
\Gamma\propto n^{3}m^{5}\sigma_{c}^{-9}
, is far more sensitive to star mass
m
and local velocity dispersion
\sigma_{c}
than the local number density
n
. So ejections via 3BBF stall as a GC ages and loses its BHs (massive and slow), despite the core then collapsing to a denser state
(
Weatherford et al. 2023
)
. The dependence on
m
and
\sigma_{c}
overwhelm the
n
dependence here, as BHs have
m\sim 20
times higher than, and
\sigma_{c}\sim 1/2
, the mean for stars in the core.

III.2
Fitting the Velocity Distributions

To quantify the
v
distribution and aid comparisons to our data, we apply a least-squares fit to all curves in Figure
4
, for robustness based only on stars with
v\leq 40\,{\rm km\,s}^{-1}
(or
v\leq 100\,{\rm km\,s}^{-1}
for ejecta from BS/BB or 3BBF in the left column). These fits appear as thin black lines atop every curve. We find 2BR’s contribution to
v
is well fit by a Maxwellian distribution plus a one- or two-component exponential tail
2
2

2

Our exponential tail for 2BR does not necessarily reflect the true dynamics of relaxation since our classification of escape mechanisms in
CMC
cannot distinguish whether stars were kicked to sufficient energy for escape by
CMC
’s actual 2BR algorithm or (much more rarely) by purely
ad hoc
dumping of excess energy to neighboring stars to conserve global energy
(
Stodolkiewicz 1982
;
Rodriguez et al. 2022
)
. Furthermore, the
Chandrasekhar 1942
theory of relaxation underpinning
CMC
’s 2BR algorithm applies only to the cumulative effect of many weak encounters, so does not consider strong two-body encounters, which might otherwise be expected to contribute a small exponential tail.
CMC
’s 2BR algorithm may lead to a qualitatively similar tail by accident, but not by design.
; i.e., the probability density function for
v

We now discuss the impact of the

IV.1
Comparison to Other Velocity Dispersion Enhancers

As described in Section
I
, recent studies have also modeled the impacts on stream kinematics from heating by DM subhalos, which broaden the stream velocity distribution. This heating yields a dominant cold component (Gaussian
\sigma\sim 1
–
2\,{\rm km\,s}^{-1}
) with broad exponential wings whose dispersion is sensitive to the DM model
(
Carlberg et al. 2024b
,
6\,{\rm km\,s}^{-1}
for cold DM and
3
–
4\,{\rm km\,s}^{-1}
for 5.5–7 keV warm DM;)
. These wings are qualitatively similar to the exponential tails contributed by strong encounters (not considered in the above study), motivating a direct comparison.

In Figure
7
, we compare our stream velocity distributions (black curves) to those predicted by
Carlberg et al. 2024b
—their Figure 10—in substructured cosmological simulations under several DM assumptions (colored curves). To compare on even footing, we now show the probability distribution (normalized to a peak of one) for the Galactocentric radial velocity
v_{R}
of stream stars. We find that while the exponential tails due to strong encounters are typically
{\approx}1.5
orders of magnitude lower than those expected under cold DM cosmology, they have comparable prominence to the tails under warm DM cosmology when limiting to stars
m\gtrsim 0.8\,M_{\odot}
. So while the assumed Galactic potential (especially substructure) in each study is quite different, Figure
7
suggests that strong encounters and the
m
–
\sigma
correlation may be relevant to DM inference, especially for streams from core-collapsed GCs. Observers should at least expect
some
high-
v
outliers in streams due to strong ejections alone.

Keep in mind that many other effects enhance stream
\sigma
, and heating from DM subhalos (or an ex-situ origin) is one of the strongest. The
m
–
\sigma
correlation from strong encounters may inflate inferred
\sigma
as much as some of these other effects, even if subhalo heating dominates overall. The other effects inflating
\sigma
in streams include heating by baryonic MW substructures—e.g., giant molecular clouds
(
Amorisco et al. 2016
)
, the MW’s bar, spiral arms, and disk
(
Hattori et al. 2016
;
Pearson et al. 2019
;
Banik & Bovy 2019
;
Carlberg & Agler 2023
)
, infalling MW satellites
(
Garavito-Camargo et al. 2019
)
, and other GCs
(
Doke & Hattori 2022
;
Ferrone et al. 2025
)
. Stream
\sigma
based on radial velocities can also be spuriously inflated by the internal motions of unresolved binaries
(
Li et al. 2019
;
Conroy et al. 2019
)
and gravitational redshift’s dependence on star mass and radius
(
Loeb 2014
)
, though the expected enhancement from each is
{\lesssim}0.5\,{\rm km\,s}^{-1}
.

IV.2
Comparison to Observed Stream Velocity Dispersions

As noted in Section
I
, many observed streams determined to have originated from GCs have low
\sigma
; see Table
2
. With few exceptions, these streams have
\sigma\lesssim 3\,{\rm km\,s}^{-1}
, typically higher than we predict from internal GC dynamics alone, but not as high as predicted for GCs originating in cuspy DM halos or subject to heating by cold DM subhalos
(
Malhan et al. 2021a
;
Malhan et al. 2021b
;
Carlberg et al. 2024b
;
Carlberg 2025
, e.g.,)
. Further accounting for the other factors above that also enhance observed
\sigma
, including potentially an
m
–
\sigma
correlation in some streams, would only exacerbate this mild tension between observed
\sigma
and predictions for cold DM.

Membership selection for many GC streams is limited to stars not much below the main-sequence (MS) turnoff—see Table
2
’s last column—i.e., to stars of above-average mass. Such streams are potentially susceptible to the
m
–
\sigma
correlation we find at such masses in streams from core-collapsed GCs. However, none of the six tabulated streams with a specific known GC progenitor are core-collapsed
(
Trager et al. 1995
)
, suggesting negligible
m
dependence in their measured
\sigma
. The other tabulated streams have no known progenitor, but the
m
–
\sigma
correlation could affect the inferred
\sigma
of some since
{\approx}20\%
of GCs in the MW are core-collapsed
(
Trager et al. 1995
)
, including two with stellar streams: M30 and NGC 6397
(
Bonaca et al. 2021
)
. Other GCs with streams, such as M2, M3, M92, NGC 1851, and NGC 5824 have borderline cuspy surface brightness profiles and are highly mass-segregated
(
Weatherford et al. 2020
)
, indicating low BH retention and significant progression towards core collapse. So the streams from these GCs may be more likely to exhibit an
m
–
\sigma
correlation from strong encounters.

Many streams also likely originate from fully dissolved GCs, but it is hard to predict what fraction of these underwent core collapse. Whether the GC’s lifetime increases or decreases with cluster density depends on how
tidally filling
the GC is—i.e., on the ratio of the GC’s half-mass radius to its tidal radius. Central heating from BH binaries cause diffuse, low-
N
GCs to overflow their tidal boundary, dissolving faster with lower density
(
Gieles et al. 2021
;
Gieles & Gnedin 2023
;
Roberts et al. 2024
)
. In this case, the GCs retain robust BH populations until dissolution, and never undergo core collapse. But GCs of roughly typical density in the MW or higher, especially those subject to weak tides (i.e., at high Galactocentric distances), dissolve faster with higher density due to their faster relaxation
(
Baumgardt 2001
;
Binney & Tremaine 2008
, e.g.,)
; see also some additional discussion in Section 6.3 of
Weatherford et al. 2024
. In this case, the GCs can eject their BHs well before dissolution, giving rise to the observed core-collapsed GCs in the MW. But the large uncertainties on the initial properties of dissolved GCs, and the several factors at play (masses, central densities, tidal radii), make it challenging to predict the fraction of dissolved GCs that underwent core collapse and potentially produced streams with robust
m
–
\sigma
correlations. So for now we simply note the low
\sigma
of many GC streams, with membership limited to near the MS turnoff or above, makes strong encounter ejecta potentially relevant to DM inference based on
\sigma
.

IV.3
Fluctuating Tides and Direct
N
-body Comparison

By assuming the GC circularly orbits the Galactic center in a smooth underlying potential, our mock streams do not account for fluctuating tides from passage near MW substructure, e.g. the Galactic center. Such passages primarily enhance stripping of the GC’s outermost stars, preferentially adding low-energy escapers to the stream and thereby reducing the fraction of stream members ejected by strong encounters
(
Panithanpaisal et al. 2025
)
. This could conceivably wash out the
m
–
\sigma
correlation from dynamics in the GC’s core. But due to mass segregation, fluctuating tides mostly strip a GC’s lowest-
m
stars. So fluctuating tides may have little influence on
\sigma
at
m
above average in a GC (
{\approx}0.5\,M_{\odot}
, i.e., readily observable
m
), preserving the
m
–
\sigma

N
-body models due to their more rigorous accounting for Galactic tides. We thus examined kinematics in a few snapshots of streams from direct
N
-body models of the nearest core-collapsed GC, NGC 6397, that used the GC’s true eccentric orbit—kindly provided by
Arnold & Baumgardt 2025
. Yet modeling differences made the comparison inconclusive, so we only describe it briefly and leave more careful testing to future work.

In summary, both the provided direct
N
-body snapshots, and some from
CMC

(
Rui et al. 2021
)
, exquisitely match NGC 6397’s observed surface brightness and velocity dispersion profiles (SBPs and VDPs). This inspired us to check if
\sigma
appears similar in each set of stream models. Promisingly, they both exhibit similar
\sigma\approx 1.5\,{\rm km\,s}^{-1}
for
m\lesssim 0.5\,M_{\odot}
, hinting that fluctuating tides from an eccentric GC orbit do not dramatically alter stream kinematics.

Unlike our models, the direct
N
-body snapshots did not exhibit an
m
–
\sigma
correlation for
m>0.5\,M_{\odot}
. This does not necessarily contradict our results, however. Due to
\sigma
’s noisiness (Figure
6

N
-body models lacked primordial binaries and used different initial conditions tuned to result in a match to NGC 6397’s SBP and VDP after only
4\,{\rm Gyr}
versus NGC 6397’s true age,
13\,{\rm Gyr}
(also the rough age of the well-fitting
CMC
snapshots). For example, they began with a stellar mass function similar to that observed in NGC 6397 today, much flatter than the standard
Kroupa 2001
initial mass function in
CMC
. As expected for such differences, the well-fitting direct
N
-body snapshots were less mass-segregated than those from
CMC
. This, and the lack of primordial binaries

IV.4
Unresolved Binaries

Although we excluded binaries from our analysis in Section
III
, we share a few details about them here due to interest in the properties of unresolved binaries in streams and their impact on
\sigma
from radial velocity measurements
(
Li et al. 2019
, e.g.,)
. The fraction of stars in our streams with binary companions is
{\approx}4.5\%
, dropping to
{\approx}3.5\%
if limiting to primary star masses
\gtrsim 0.7\,M_{\odot}
, due to mass segregation and preferential escape of low-mass stars. These fractions depend negligibly on GC age or
r_{v}
, likely since
CMC
only fully considers hard binaries
(
Heggie 1975
)
, which are not easily disrupted by increases in GC density. They also change negligibly with initial
f_{b,h}
(due to rarity of stars with
m>15\,M_{\odot}
), or if we consider only binaries with
v\leq 20\,{\rm km\,s}^{-1}
. These values are not especially predictive, though, as they mainly reflect
CMC
’s default
5\%
initial hard binary fraction, albeit reasonable for MWGCs of typical density
(
Milone et al. 2012
)
.

Regarding the impact on
\sigma
from unresolved binaries, note that those with internal orbital speeds
v_{\rm orb}\gg\sigma
are often easily filtered out as outliers during stream membership selection (depending on inclination to the line-of-sight). Even for
r_{v}=2\,{\rm pc}
, 85% of our escaped binaries have semi-major axes
{\lesssim}0.3\,{\rm au}

(
Weatherford et al. 2023
, Figure 6 of)
, implying
v_{\rm orb}{\gtrsim}50\,{\rm km\,s}^{-1}
and easy filtration, especially if informed by model stream velocity distributions such as presented here. Wider binaries with lower
v_{\rm orb}
pose a larger threat of slipping through the filter and artificially inflating
\sigma
. Both the destruction and formation rates of wide binaries in GCs are very high, with pairs of stars constantly being perturbed into and out of bound states, so there are always some wide binaries present despite their typically short lifespans
(
Heggie 1975
;
Goodman & Hut 1993
)
. While survival long enough to enter a stream is most likely in GCs of lower mass or density than considered here, or during the GC’s final dissolution phase
(
Kouwenhoven et al. 2010
;
Moeckel & Clarke 2011
, e.g.,)
, the impact on
\sigma
certainly merits future investigation.

A second interesting trend in our streams at ages typical of MWGCs is that the fraction of stars with a compact object companion increases roughly exponentially with
m
over the range
m\in[0.1,0.8]\,M_{\odot}
. The fraction rises from
{\approx}0.003\%
–
0.8\%
(
r_{v}=0.5\,{\rm pc}
) or from
{\approx}0.01\%
–
0.3\%
(
r_{v}=2\,{\rm pc}
). The trend with
m
reflects the preference of resonant BS/BB encounters to pair the two highest-
m
bodies in the interaction
(
Heggie 1975
)
. The steeper trend with lower
r_{v}
reflects the higher rate of BS/BB encounters, giving more chances to pair massive bodies together. We direct readers interested in more detailed properties of our GC’s binary ejecta (e.g., star types, semi-major axes, eccentricites, and mass ratios) to Section 4.4 and Figures 5–6 of
Weatherford et al. 2023
.

V
Summary and Future Work

We explored the impact of internal dynamics in GCs on the kinematics of their stellar streams using Monte Carlo
N
-body simulations under a static Galactic tide. Full simulation of strong encounters, mass segregation, and BH populations in the GC’s core enabled us to generate
mock streams with realistic internal mass and velocity distributions. Our simulation initial conditions, specifically their initial
r_{v}=(0.5,2)\,{\rm pc}
, produce clusters representative of typical core-collapsed and non-core-collapsed MWGCs. We analyzed the kinematics of stream stars from each of these archetypes based on several velocity metrics: in Section
III
, the stars’ absolute velocities relative to the GC’s orbital velocity in Galactocentric cylindrical coordinates—
v=\lvert\vec{v_{\star}}-(0,v_{c},0)\rvert
with dispersion
\sigma_{v}
—and in the Appendix, the absolute value of each of
v
’s components: (
v_{R},v_{\phi},v_{Z})
with dispersions
(\sigma_{R},\sigma_{\phi},\sigma_{Z})
.

In the GCs born less dense (
r_{v}=2\,{\rm pc}
), prolonged retention of a central BH population and corresponding dynamical heating prevents the onset of core collapse by the
9
–
13\,{\rm Gyr}
age range typical of MWGCs, quenching mass segregation among luminous stars
(
Alessandrini et al. 2016
;
Peuten et al. 2016
;
Webb et al. 2017
;
Weatherford et al. 2018
;
Weatherford et al. 2020
)
. In the streams from these GCs,
\sigma
increases somewhat with star mass
m\in[0.1,0.8]\,M_{\odot}
, rising from
\sigma_{v}\approx 1.2
–
2.6\,{\rm km\,s}^{-1}
—or
1.2
–
1.6\,{\rm km\,s}^{-1}
when counting only stars with
(v_{R},v_{\phi},v_{Z})\leq 20\,{\rm km\,s}^{-1}
, mimicking kinematic membership cuts in stream observations. But faster loss of BHs in the denser GCs (
r_{v}=0.5\,{\rm pc}
) allows the core to collapse by
9
–
13\,{\rm Gyr}
and the highest-
m
stars to segregate deeper into the core. The resulting stronger correlation between
m
and likelihood of ejection by strong encounters yields a steeper
m
–
\sigma
correlation after core collapse, increasing from
\sigma_{v}\approx 1.2
–
4.0\,{\rm km\,s}^{-1}
, or
1.2
–
2.2\,{\rm km\,s}^{-1}
when limiting to stars with
(v_{R},v_{\phi},v_{Z})\leq 20\,{\rm km\,s}^{-1}
. The ejecta from strong encounters also produce robust exponential tails in the stream’s velocity distribution, qualitatively similar to the contribution of warm cocoons surrounding streams like GD-1.

In our streams,
\sigma
at high
m
fluctuates greatly depending on when the stream is “observed" and which specific ejecta are identified as members. This effect is a result of small sample size in the number of high-
m
ejecta from strong encounters rather than the number of high-
m
ejecta in the stream overall. The 5th–95th percentile range of these fluctuations span
\sigma_{v}\in[1.3,7.3]\,{\rm km\,s}^{-1}
for stars with
m\geq 0.7\,M_{\odot}
after core collapse in the denser GCs, but reduce to
\sigma_{v}\in[1.1,3.5]\,{\rm km\,s}^{-1}
when counting only stars with
(v_{R},v_{\phi},v_{Z})\leq 20\,{\rm km\,s}^{-1}
. It is thus possible that observations of streams from GCs limited to stars near and above the MS turnoff may sometimes measure
\sigma\gtrsim 3\,{\rm km\,s}^{-1}
, even in the absence of any heating from DM subhalos or other MW substructure, any preprocessing in a parent halo, or any spurious enhancement from, e.g., unresolved binaries. This large scatter also implies the overall
\sigma
measured in any
individual
stream is a somewhat imprecise measure of heating applied to that stream by MW substructure, or of properties of the stream’s progenitor cluster or parent halo, though
\sigma
can still rule out causes of especially large
\sigma
, e.g., origins in massive cuspy parent halos. The overall
\sigma
’s sensitivity to high-
v
outliers also motivates future studies to report full velocity distributions—either in the form of raw data or parameters of mixture models fitted to the data, including the distribution’s tail. Indeed, the high-
v
tail is the portion of the velocity distribution most useful for DM inference
(
Carlberg et al. 2024b
;
Carlberg 2025
, e.g.,)
.

Our findings imply observations of streams from dense, highly mass-segregated GCs—e.g., those that are BH-depleted and core-collapsed—may be biased to somewhat higher
\sigma
than would be predicted by typical stream models neglecting
\sigma
’s
m
-dependence. Accounting for such a bias would mildly reduce observationally inferred
\sigma
in streams—perhaps
{\approx}1/3
of GC streams if restricting to changes in
\sigma
of
{\gtrsim}0.3\,{\rm km\,s}^{-1}
when using typical kinematic membership cuts. This would exacerbate tension between the already low
\sigma
in many streams and expectations of significant heating by cold DM subhalos, especially in light of further enhancements to
\sigma
expected from stream formation in a parent halo prior to accretion by the MW, heating from many baryonic MW substructures, and artificially enhanced
\sigma
from unresolved binaries, contamination by background stars, or the dependence of gravitational redshift on star mass and radius. An evolving MW potential
(
Panithanpaisal et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
can also further exacerbate any tail in the velocity distribution from strong encounters. An
m
–
\sigma
correlation in streams would thus cause stream observations to further favor alternatives to cold DM, such as warm, ultralight, or self-interacting DM.

An important caveat to our conclusions is that we limited our analysis to GCs on circular orbits in the MW. GCs subject to strongly fluctuating tides would have enhanced tidal stripping from the GC’s halo, which could conceivably wash out the
m
–
\sigma
correlation from dynamics in the GC’s core. But since the tidal stripping would primarily affect the GC’s lowest-
m
stars, fluctuating tides may have little influence on
\sigma
at
m
above average in a GC (
{\approx}0.5\,M_{\odot}
, i.e., readily observable
m
), preserving the
m
–
\sigma
correlation. We intend to explore such cases in future work, further examine the impact of unresolved binaries, and search for an
m
–
\sigma
relation in observed streams. Regarding the latter, we have performed an initial search, finding suggestive evidence for an
m
–
\sigma
correlation in the nearest GC stream (from the core-collapsed GC NGC 6397). But we defer concrete analysis to future work due to a desire for higher sample purity and concern that the correlation also appeared in the stream’s background.
