Changes in Health‐Related Quality of Life After Metabolic and Bariatric Surgery: A Prospective Single‐Center Cohort Study
Authors: Camilla Thorbøll Wæhrens, Ingrid Poulsen, Thomas Kallemose, Bodil Just Christensen, Inge Marie Poulsen, Julie Jacoby Petersen, Dorthe Greve Jørgensen, Carsten Dirksen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveObesity impairs health‐related quality‐of‐life (HRQoL). Substantial weight loss following metabolic and bariatric surgery improves HRQoL, but there is a need for larger long‐term studies. This study aimed to examine changes in HRQoL at 2 and 5 years, comparing outcomes between Roux‐en‐Y gastric bypass (RYGB) and sleeve gastrectomy (SG), and to evaluate associations with pre‐operative patient characteristics.MethodsHRQoL was assessed using the Short Form‐36 (SF‐36) questionnaire before surgery and at 2‐ and 5‐year follow‐up. Physical and Mental Component Scores (PCS and MCS) were calculated, and associations between baseline characteristics and changes in HRQoL were analyzed.ResultsOf 2031 patients, 1285 completed at least one postoperative SF‐36. PCS improved significantly at 2 years (mean 9.4, 95% CI 8.7–10.0) and was maintained at 5 years. MCS showed an improvement at 2 years (1.5, 95% CI 0.8–2.2) but not at 5 years. No major differences were found between RYGB and SG, except for lower MCS at 5 years in SG patients (5.2, p = 0.009). Preoperative type 2 diabetes and osteoarthritis were associated with improved HRQoL, whereas psychiatric and gastrointestinal disorders predicted poorer results.ConclusionsMetabolic and bariatric surgery leads to sustained improvements in physical HRQoL, but the response is influenced by preoperative comorbidity.Keywords: health‐related quality of life, obesity care, obesity treatment, Roux‐en‐Y gastric bypass, Short Form‐36, sleeve gastrectomy

Introduction

Individuals living with obesity experience reduced health‐related quality of life (HRQoL), primarily driven by physical limitations [1, 2]. Many also suffer from social stigmatization, eating disorders, depression, and anxiety, conditions that are also directly linked to impaired HRQoL [3, 4, 5, 6].

In Denmark, RYGB was implemented into the public healthcare system in the early 2000s together with the now obsolete gastric banding procedure, whereas SG was first introduced around 2010. SG gained popularity rapidly, and by 2015, SG surpassed RYGB as the most frequently performed procedure [7]. Access to metabolic and bariatric surgery in Denmark follows national guidelines issued by the Danish health authorities. Until 2017, the eligibility criteria were a body mass index (BMI) ≥ 40 kg/m2, or a BMI of 35–39,9 kg/m2 in the presence of one or more specific obesity‐related conditions (type 2 diabetes [T2D], severe hypertension [HT], obstructive sleep apnea [OSA], infertility due to polycystic ovary syndrome [PCOS], or osteoarthrosis [OA] of the lower extremities), and a documented history of unsuccessful long‐term non‐surgical weight loss management [8]. The guidelines were updated in 2017 with two major changes: (1) patients with a BMI ≥ 40 kg/m2 must now have obesity‐related conditions that substantially impact their health or ability to work, and (2) patients must quit smoking at least 6 weeks before surgery [8]. Both before and after 2017, the guidelines required patients to lose 8% of their body weight prior to surgery.

Following metabolic and bariatric surgery, HRQoL improves early in the postoperative period during rapid weight loss, most consistently in the physical domains, as demonstrated in systematic reviews [1, 9, 10, 11]. Improvements typically peak 1–2 years postoperatively, followed by a gradual decline in the following years, although benefits seem to persist at long‐term follow‐up of 10–20 years [9, 10, 12]. However, the existing evidence remains limited, as many studies are small (< 100 individuals undergoing metabolic and bariatric surgery), have relatively short follow‐up durations (≤ 2 years), and use heterogenous instruments to measure HRQoL [9, 13]. Furthermore, only a few studies have compared changes in HRQoL between different surgical procedures or examined the association between preoperative patient characteristics and postoperative changes in HRQoL after metabolic and bariatric surgery [9, 12, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18]. Building on this, previous research suggested that variability in HRQoL outcomes might have been influenced by psychological factors and patient perceptions, while a long‐term study indicated that preoperative mental disorders could continue to affect patient‐reported outcomes over time [1, 19]. Taken together, these observations underscore the need for extended follow‐up and a comprehensive, patient‐centered approach to HRQoL assessment.

The present study examined changes in HRQoL after metabolic and bariatric surgery with up to 5 years of follow‐up in a large prospective single‐center cohort, and tested the hypotheses that (1) postoperative changes in HRQoL differ between RYGB and SG and (2) postoperative changes in HRQoL are associated with certain preoperative patient characteristics such as age, sex, preoperative BMI, body weight, and the presence of obesity‐related comorbidities as well as postoperative anthropometric changes at 2‐year follow‐up.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Participants

In this longitudinal, prospective single‐center cohort study, adults (age > 18 years) who underwent metabolic and bariatric surgery (limited to RYGB and SG) between September 2006 and September 2023 at Copenhagen University Hospital, Hvidovre (Hvidovre, Denmark) were included. The hospital is the only public center for metabolic and bariatric surgery in the Capital Region of Denmark with a mean of 116 primary procedures (excluding revisional surgeries) per year in the study period (Figure 1).

Number of primary Roux‐en‐Y gastric bypass (dark gray) and sleeve gastrectomy (light gray) procedures per year from September 2006 to September 2023 (both inclusive) at Copenhagen University Hospital—Hvidovre.

Data Collection

Since September 2006, the SF‐36 questionnaire has been routinely distributed to all patients scheduled for metabolic and bariatric surgery at the hospital. Patients were asked to fill in the questionnaire upon their first contact with the medical team (first outpatient visit) and at follow‐up visits 1 and 2 years after surgery. At 5‐year follow‐up, the questionnaire was sent to all patients by mail together with a pre‐paid return envelope, except for those patients with address protection, those who had moved out of Denmark, or those who had died. To allow as many patients as possible to complete the 2‐ and 5‐year follow‐up questionnaires, December 31st 2021 and December 31st 2018 were used as cut‐off dates, respectively.

Together with the SF‐36 questionnaire, the following preoperative data was collected: date of surgery, age at time of surgery, sex, preoperative total body weight (i.e., prior to the mandatory 8%‐preoperative weight loss), height, BMI, tobacco‐use, and presence of specific obesity‐related diseases including T2D, HT, OSA, PCOS, and OA of the lower extremities, as well as selected additional comorbidities categorized into the following groups: psychiatric disorders, chronic pain syndrome, and gastrointestinal illness including prior non‐bariatric gastrointestinal surgery (see Supporting Information S1: Supplementary material S1).

Anthropometric data (weight and waist circumference) at 2‐year follow‐up were extracted from the Danish Quality Registry for Treatment of Severe Obesity, which is a national public database that collects data on all patients undergoing metabolic and bariatric surgery in Denmark [7].

Short Form‐36

SF‐36 is a widely used patient‐reported outcome measure designed to assess HRQoL in individuals with different kinds of medical conditions [20]. SF‐36 is a generic (i.e., non‐disease specific) instrument consisting of 36 questions covering eight domains: physical functioning, role limitations due to physical health, bodily pain, general health perceptions, vitality/energy, social functioning, role limitations due to emotional problems, and mental health [20]. Based on the eight domains, two scores are calculated: the Physical component score (PCS) and Mental component score (MCS). The PCS covers the following domains: physical functioning, role limitations due to physical health, bodily pain, and general health. The MCS covers the domains, vitality/energy, social functioning, role limitations due to emotional problems, and mental health. Each scale has a total score that ranges from 0 to 100 points (higher scores indicate better health status) [20]. In studies using the SF‐36 to assess HRQoL, changes are interpreted in relation to clinically meaningful thresholds, defined as > 3.8 points for the PCS and > 4.6 points for the MCS [21]. The SF‐36 questionnaire was translated to Danish and validated in 1993 under the abbreviation IQOLA SF‐36 Danish [22]. Danish version 1.1 was used.

Statistical Analysis

The statistical analysis was carried out in accordance with a prespecified statistical analysis plan (see Supporting Information S1: Supplementary material S2). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR) and categorical variables as percentages. The time intervals of 0–2 years and 2–5 years were chosen to represent the early postoperative period characterized by weight loss and regular outpatient follow‐up visits at the hospital, and the later period after discharge from the clinic, during which the primary goal is weight maintenance. Changes in SF‐36 PCS and MCS from preoperatively to 2 years post‐surgery (0–2 years) and from 2 to 5 years postoperatively (2–5), in the entire cohort and within surgical groups, were analyzed using one‐sample t‐tests. If test assumptions were not fulfilled, the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test was used instead. Comparison of changes in SF‐36 between surgical groups was done using a linear regression model that was adjusted for age, sex, diabetes status and year of surgery. Associations between changes in SF‐36 scores during the 0–2 and 2–5 year postoperative periods and preoperative patient characteristics were also analyzed using linear regression models adjusted for age and sex. Similar models were fitted with postoperative anthropometric changes instead of patient characteristics; possible confounders for these models were evaluated by comparing exposure estimates from the unadjusted model to the adjusted model for each possible confounder. If inclusion of a covariate changed the exposure estimate by more than 5%, the variable was considered a confounder and included in the final model. Possible confounders evaluated were; age, sex, surgery year, surgical groups, smoking, hypertension, joint pain, obstructive sleep apnea, and polycystic ovary syndrome. If model assumptions were not fulfilled, 95% confidence intervals (CI) and p‐values were estimated by bootstrapping. Normality assumptions were evaluated by QQ‐plot and histograms, and linearity and homogeneity of variance were evaluated by residual plots. All statistical analysis were performed using R 4.3.2 [23].

Ethical Consideration

According to Danish legislation, registry‐based studies that exclusively use anonymized data are not required to obtain approval from the Danish National Committee on Health Research Ethics. In accordance with the regulations of the Capital Region of Denmark, the study was registered in the Region's central research project database “Privacy” (P‐2023‐288) and approved by the Region's Research Law Department.

Results

From September 2006 to September 2023, 2157 patients underwent RYGB or SG at Copenhagen University Hospital Hvidovre, of whom 2031 (94.2%) completed the SF‐36 questionnaire preoperatively, and 1285 (63.3%) completed the questionnaire at least once postoperatively (at the 2‐ and/or 5‐year follow‐up). A detailed patient flowchart is shown in Figure 2.

Patient flowchart. RYGB, Roux‐en‐Y gastric bypass; SF‐36, Short Form‐36; SG, Sleeve gastrectomy.

Patients who completed the questionnaire before surgery were on average 43.7 years of age (SD 10.1), had a median total body weight of 133 kg (IQR 119: 150), a median BMI of 45.7 kg/m2 (IQR 41.7: 50.3), and a median waist circumference of 128 (IQR 120: 139); 72.8% were females. The prevalence of obesity‐related diseases and other comorbidities were T2D 39.5%, HT 39.6%, OSA 18.3%, PCOS 11.1% (females only), OA 13.1%, chronic pain syndrome 1.7%, psychiatric disorders 11.4%, and gastrointestinal illness 2.7%. Tobacco‐use was reported in 18.2% of patients. Preoperative characteristics for patients who also completed 2‐year and/or 5‐year follow‐up did not differ substantially, except for minor differences in the prevalence of T2D, OSA, and tobacco‐use (see Supporting Information S1: Supplementary material S3). At the 2‐year follow‐up, median weight loss was 42 kg (IQR 32: 53) and waist circumference decreased by 29 (IQR 22: 37).

Changes in HRQoL After Metabolic and Bariatric Surgery

Unadjusted SF‐36 PCS and MCS before and after metabolic and bariatric surgery are shown in Table 1 and Figure 3. From a median preoperative PCS of 41.0 points. A marked improvement was observed within the first postoperative year, after which the score remained stable until year 2, resulting in a mean increase of 9.4 points (95% CI 8.7: 10.0, p < 0.001) from 0 to 2 years. From 2 to 5 years, the PCS declined slightly (median change −1.9 points [IQR: −6.9 to 0.9, p < 0.001]) but remained significantly higher at 5 years compared with the preoperative level (median 4.5 points [IQR: −1.8 to 11.0, p < 0.001]). From a median preoperative MCS of 52.4 points, a modest improvement was observed at 2 years after surgery, although substantially smaller than that seen for PCS (mean increase 1.5 points [95% CI: 0.8–2.2, p < 0.001]). This was followed by a decline from 2 to 5 years, similar to the pattern observed for PCS (median −1.0 points [IQR: −6.9 to 2.9, p < 0.001]) resulting in no statistically significant difference at 5 years compared to baseline (median −0.3 points [IQR: −7.1: 5.6, p = 0.062]).

As shown in Figure 1, the relative use of RYGB and SG differed substantially across the study period, with RYGB being the predominant procedure until 2014, after which the two procedures were performed in nearly equal numbers. Adjusted estimates of the differences in SF‐36 PCS and MCS between the two surgical procedures controlling for age, sex, diabetes status, and year of surgery are shown in Table 2. Association Between Postoperative Changes in HRQoL and Preoperative Patient Characteristics

Associations between postoperative changes in SF‐36 PCS and preoperative patient characteristics are shown in Table 3. Higher body weight before surgery was associated with a small but statistically significant greater improvement in SF‐36 PCS from 0 to 2 years, but not with changes from 2 to 5 years or from 0 to 5 years. Patients with preoperative T2D or OA had, on average, a 2.9–4.5 point greater improvement in PCS from 0 to 2 years after surgery, and this difference was maintained at 5 years. In contrast, patients with a history of gastrointestinal illness experienced an improvement in the early postoperative period (0–2 years) that was nearly four points lower than those without such a history. Preoperative psychiatric disorders were the only characteristic associated with a significantly poorer outcome from 2 to 5 years postoperatively. Associations between preoperative patient characteristics and changes in SF‐36 MCS were all nonsignificant (see Supporting Information S1: Supplementary material S5).

Association Between Postoperative Changes in HRQoL and Anthropometric Measures

Changes in body weight and waist circumference from 0 to 2 years were negatively associated with changes in both SF‐36 PCS and MCS during the same postoperative period (for 10 kg weight loss: PCS 0.94 [95% CI: 0.49; 1.40], p < 0.001 and MCS 0.84 [95% CI: 0.36; 1.32], p < 0.001; for 10 cm reduction in waist circumference: PCS: 1.60 [95% CI: 1.00; 2.20], p < 0.001 and MCS 1.26 [95% CI: 0.60; 1.91], p < 0.001).

Discussion

The main finding of this study is that self‐reported physical QoL (SF‐36 PCS) showed a clinically meaningful improvement after metabolic and bariatric surgery (RYGB and SG) and remained improved for at least 5 years after surgery, despite a modest decline from year 2 to 5. In comparison, only minor improvements in self‐reported mental QoL (SF‐36 MCS), which were unlikely to be clinically meaningful, were observed within the first 2 years after surgery and were followed by a similar decline from 2 to 5 years, as seen for physical QoL. After adjustment for preoperative patient characteristics and year of surgery, RYGB and SG had comparable effects on QoL except for a difference in self‐reported mental QoL late after surgery favoring RYGB. Preoperative T2D and OA were associated with a markedly larger improvement in physical QoL after metabolic and bariatric surgery, whereas a history of gastrointestinal disorders was associated with a poorer outcome during the early postoperative period (0–2 years) and preoperative psychiatric disorders were associated with a poorer outcome during the late (2–5 years) postoperative phase. Greater weight loss and reductions in waist circumference were associated with greater improvements in both physical and mental QoL during the 0–2 years postoperative period. Additionally, the pre‐operative median PCS and MCS in the present bariatric cohort were substantially lower compared with those in a representative Danish sample (n = 8342) of adults aged 25–70 years collected in 2005 (PCS: 41.0 vs. 54.15 points; MCS 52.4 vs. 57.2) [24].

Overall, the findings are in line with previous systematic reviews and, interestingly, one review reported that physical QoL seems to remain stable beyond 5 years after surgery [10, 11, 12]. This suggests that the improvements in SF‐36 PCS observed at 5 years in the present study may represent a persistent improvement. These long‐term improvements in physical QoL, including increases of > 10 points in SF‐36 PCS at 2 years, may seem even more remarkable when compared to the more modest improvements reported during treatment with modern GLP‐1 receptor agonists. For example, 68 weeks of treatment with semaglutide 2.4 mg once weekly resulted in an improvement in SF‐36 PCS of only 2.21 points, which is below the commonly accepted threshold for a clinically meaningful change, despite a 14.85% reduction in body weight [25]. The limited improvements in SF‐36 MCS, which seem to decline over time, are consistent with findings from a previous review [12]. However, this outcome may differ between men and women, as suggested by results from the Longitudinal Assessment of Metabolic and bariatric surgery (LABS) consortium [26, 27].

The present results extend previously reported changes in QoL from the Danish Quality Registry for Treatment of Severe Obesity [7], which included all individuals (n = 16,053) undergoing metabolic and bariatric surgery in Denmark between 2008 and 2021 [7]. That analysis was based on the disease‐specific, but less comprehensive, Moorehead‐Ardelt QoL questionnaire and was limited to the two first years after surgery [7, 28]. The present study found marked improvements in QoL at the 1‐year follow‐up, followed by a slight decline at 2 years, with the most pronounced improvements observed in the “physical activity level,” “social contacts,” and “self‐esteem” domains. Notably, no differences were found between RYGB and SG. Correspondingly, no differences in HRQoL changes between surgical procedures were observed within the first 2 years after surgery. However, at the 5‐year follow‐up, SG was associated with a significantly poorer mental QoL outcome (SF‐36 MCS 0–5 years), a finding that has not been previously reported. However, differences in the effect of the two procedures at the 5‐year follow‐up should be interpreted with caution as they may reflect the later introduction of SG at Copenhagen University Hospital Hvidovre, Denmark (see Supporting Information S1: Supplementary material S6) and consequently less experience with this procedure compared to RYGB. Additional support for similar overall QoL outcomes after RYGB and SG has recently been provided by the large randomized controlled trial By‐Band‐Sleeve, where 1351 individuals were randomly assigned to RYGB, SG, or laparoscopic gastric banding [2, 29, 30]. At the 3‐year postoperative follow‐up, improvements in a range of QoL measures were reported, with no significant differences observed between RYGB and SG, despite significantly greater weight loss after RYGB (6.8 kg). Interestingly, based on the SF‐12, a shorter 12‐item questionnaire alternative to the SF‐36, PCS increased significantly more after both RYGB and SG compared to gastric banding, whereas no differences between the three procedures were observed for MCS [29].

In a large prospective cohort study from Sweden based on data from the Scandinavian Obesity Surgery Register, including nearly 7000 patients undergoing primary RYGB with 5 years of follow‐up, postoperative improvements in HRQoL were associated with the degree of weight loss [17]. However, preoperative use of antidepressant medication or the occurrence of postoperative complications were negatively associated with changes in HRQoL. This negative influence of pre‐existing depression is consistent with observations that the presence of preoperative psychiatric disorders seems to be associated with poorer outcomes during the late postoperative period (years 2–5). Similar observations were reported in a prospective cohort study including 420 Dutch patients undergoing metabolic and bariatric surgery, with follow‐up at 1, 2, and 3 years postoperatively [16]. The findings showed that a lack of improvement in physical QoL, measured using the Short Form 12 (a shorter version of the SF‐36), was associated with a higher preoperative prevalence of depressive symptoms. In addition, poorer mental QoL trajectories were associated with depressive and anxiety symptoms, as well as neuroticism, insecure attachment, and maladaptive coping [16]. Notably, the study also found that self‐esteem, extraversion, and conscientiousness were associated with more favorable mental QoL trajectories [16]. Together, these findings suggest that patients with psychiatric disorders may be at increased risk of poorer long‐term outcomes following metabolic and bariatric surgery, especially after specialized follow‐up care is no longer available. These observations may therefore have implications for future post‐bariatric clinical management. This emphasizes that bariatric care must be adapted to address both psychological and mental health outcomes and requires an individualized approach for patients with previous psychiatric illness [2, 30]. This indicates a need for novel post‐bariatric care regimes targeting these vulnerable patient groups, developed through a co‐design process involving patient representatives and rigorously tested with relevant clinical outcomes prior to clinical implementation.

Another interesting finding of the study is the positive association between preoperative T2D and OA and changes in physical QoL. Greater improvements in QoL among patients with preoperative T2D may be linked to the well‐established remission of hyperglycemia following metabolic and bariatric surgery often occurring despite cessation of antiglycaemic medications [31, 32]. Similar findings were also reported in the Swedish cohort mentioned earlier [17]. In a smaller study (n = 94) with certain methodological limitations, based on the disease‐specific Moorehead‐Ardelt QoL questionnaire, hypertension was the only preoperative patient characteristic associated with better QoL outcomes after metabolic and bariatric surgery, whereas no significant associations were seen for T2D or OA [15, 28].

The present study included a prospective, unselected, and relatively large cohort of patients undergoing metabolic and bariatric surgery at a specialized public bariatric center over a 17‐year period, with 2‐ and 5‐year postoperative follow‐up. As in most of the other studies within this field, follow‐up was incomplete, with only 63% of patients completing the SF‐36 questionnaire at least once postoperatively. In comparison, in the Danish nationwide registry study, only 33.4% of the 16,053 patients completed the 2‐year follow‐up, and among these, QoL scores were available only for 60%–66% [33]. In the Swedish prospective cohort, HRQoL data were available in 6998 of 28,293 patients (24.7%) who underwent primary gastric bypass during the study period [17]. Therefore, a strength of the dataset is that complete preoperative data including SF‐36 were available in 94.2% (> 2000) of all participants undergoing surgery during the study period. Furthermore, only slightly higher preoperative SF‐36 PCS and MCS were observed in patients who completed the 5‐year follow‐up compared to those with only preoperative data (Table 1), with no major differences in preoperative (baseline) patient characteristics (see Supporting Information S1: Supplementary material S3). Another important limitation is the lack of prospective data on the status of obesity‐related diseases, which impedes us from analyzing whether improvements in these diseases contributed to the observed improvements in HRQoL, as perhaps suggested by the associations between preoperative T2D and OA and better outcomes. It is also important to note that SF‐36 was used, a generic questionnaire that has been validated in individuals with obesity but is not specific to the experiences of patients undergoing metabolic and bariatric surgery [34]. However, the development of validated bariatric‐specific PROM instruments is still ongoing [34, 35]. When the current study was initiated, there was no consensus on the use of these instruments in this cohort. Another option would have been the obesity‐specific Moorehead‐Ardelt instrument, which was used during the same period as part of the Danish Quality Registry for Treatment of Severe Obesity and yielded somewhat similar results [28, 33]. However, both questionnaires lack assessment of important obesity‐related domains such as stigmatization and self‐esteem [36, 37].

Conclusion

The present study finds that physical and mental QoL improved during the first 2 years after metabolic and bariatric surgery; however, only the improvement in physical QoL reached a clinically meaningful change. Additionally, the improvement diminished somewhat between 2 and 5 years after surgery; however, at 5 years, physical QoL remained markedly better than pre‐operative levels. No differences were seen between RYGB and SG, except for a poorer mental QoL outcome at 5 years after SG surgery. Greater improvements in physical QoL were seen in patients with preoperatively higher body weight, type 2 diabetes, or lower‐extremity osteoarthritis, whereas prior gastrointestinal and psychiatric disorders were associated with a poorer postoperative QoL outcomes. Taken together, these findings confirm that patients experience meaningful improvements in self‐reported physical QoL following metabolic and bariatric surgery, but also point to certain preoperative characteristics, for example, psychiatric disorders, that might be important for providing an individualized post‐bariatric care.
