Mechanistic exploration of royal jelly production in caged honey bees (Apis mellifera)
Authors: Zhi-Wei Peng, Yi-Ting Hung, Ming-Cheng Wu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the impact of bee pollen nutrition on the royal jelly production of honey bees (Apis mellifera). Results demonstrate that pollen diet significantly impacts hypopharyngeal gland (HPG) development and the expression of genes associated with royal jelly biosynthesis. Bees fed Brassica napus pollen exhibited superior HPG development, and increased mrjp1 expression (encoding a key royal jelly protein). While the cyp450 6AS8 gene expression (encoding a key enzyme in 10-HDA biosynthesis) was increased by pollen consumption, no distinct expression patterns were observed among the different pollen types tested. An in vitro bee cage platform for royal jelly production has been established to further understand the mechanisms behind royal jelly production in bees. The experiment demonstrated a positive correlation between the number of worker bees and the total yield of royal jelly per cage. However, when the number of worker bees is low, the amount of royal jelly each individual worker bee needs to produce increases. In conclusion, these findings enhance our understanding of the role of bee pollen nutrition in royal jelly production. Furthermore, the results from this in vitro bee cage platform suggest that the number of worker bees is a critical factor in royal jelly production, and that bees may possess a controllable mechanism for regulating royal jelly secretion.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-82094-3.

Introduction

Royal jelly, a highly nutritious food for bee larvae and the queen bee in a bee colony, is also a bee product that has been consumed by humans for centuries due to its purported health benefits1–4. The pharmaceutical properties of royal jelly have been extensively studied in both animal models and humans2,5–7. While the mechanisms of action remain under investigation, royal jelly has attracted considerable interest from researchers worldwide8,9. The increasing demand, driven by an aging population, has intensified the focus on royal jelly quality. Major royal jelly protein 1 (MRJP1) and 10-hydroxy-2-decenoic acid (10-HDA) are the most abundant protein and fatty acid, respectively, in royal jelly, and both serve as critical quality markers10.

The paired hypopharyngeal gland (HPG) and mandibular gland, both located in the heads of honey bees (Apis mellifera), are involved in the production of royal jelly11,12. The HPG comprises numerous tiny, interconnected glandular units known as acini. These acini consist of secretory cells that synthesize and secrete royal jelly, which is transported via the glandular lumen and collecting duct to the mouthparts12,13. HPG activity is age-dependent, with peak activity typically occurring between the 6th and 12th days after the emergence of worker bees14–18. A comprehensive proteome study revealed that highly activated protein and energy metabolism in the HPG are responsible for royal jelly production19. Moreover, dietary factors, including bee pollen and protein supplements, significantly affect the developmental size and activity of the HPG17,18,20,21.

The mandibular glands are located at the base of each mandible22. These glands are primarily involved in synthesizing and secreting lipid-based compounds that contribute to the composition of royal jelly, including the most abundant fatty acid, 10-HDA23. Through a comparative transcriptomic analysis of the mandibular glands between worker and queen bees, several CYP450 genes have been identified to be involved in the putative three-step biosynthesis pathway of 10-HDA, that is, in fatty acid hydroxylation, fatty acid chain shortening, and modification of the end hydroxy group24,25. Among these, CYP450 6AS8 was confirmed as a key enzyme in 10-HDA biosynthesis through RNAi knockdown experiments26, is believed to catalyze fatty acid hydroxylation in this pathway24. Dietary factors, such as high-protein diets and zinc supplementation, have been shown to enhance mandibular gland development27,28.

Taiwan’s tropical and subtropical climate fosters a diverse array of nectariferous plants. Beekeepers in Taiwan manage approximately 160,000 A. mellifera colonies, producing at least 700 tons of royal jelly annually, as per records provided by the Taiwan Beekeeping Association. Five predominant bee pollen types annually collected by local beekeepers include rape (Brassica napus, Bn), beggartick (Bidens pilosa var. radiata, Bp), tea tree (Camellia sinensis, Cs), nutgall tree (Rhus chinensis var. roxburghii, Rc), and corn (Zea mays, Zm)29. Given the prevalence of royal jelly production alongside bee pollen harvesting, and our understanding of these bee pollen types’ nutritional composition, this study investigated the influence of bee pollen diets on HPG development in newly emerged bees. We further examined the impact of these diets on the expression of mrjp1 and cyp450 6AS8, genes encoding key proteins involved in royal jelly biosynthesis. While royal jelly production is closely tied to bee nutrition, studying this relationship within a colony is challenging due to numerous confounding factors, including diet composition, worker bee population, and environmental conditions (humidity levels and pheromones). To isolate the effects of bee pollen on royal jelly quality and quantity, we aim to establish an in vitro royal jelly production system. By confining bees in a controlled laboratory environment, we can effectively mitigate the influence of colony-level factors. These works lay the groundwork for a comprehensive understanding of bee nutrition and royal jelly production.

Results

Bee pollen consumption of caged bees

To evaluate bee pollen preference, 30 newly emerged bees were confined in cages containing a nest foundation, one of six bee pollen types (Bn, Rc, Cs, Bp, Zm, or a pollen mix), and a 50% sucrose solution. Cumulative bee pollen consumption was recorded over six days. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 1A, the total bee pollen consumption did not vary significantly among the different bee pollen types (ANOVA, F = 2.511, df1 = 5, df2 = 18, P = 0.0682). However, after correcting for evaporative loss, Rc had the highest average consumption (2.0 g), followed by Bn (1.8 g), Cs (1.6 g), Bp and mixed pollen (1.4 g each), and Zm (1.0 g). On an individual basis, bees consumed an average of 10.2, 11.0, 8.9, 7.6, 5.5, and 7.5 mg of the respective pollen types per day (ANOVA, F = 2.511, df1 = 5, df2 = 18, P = 0.0682) (Supplementary Fig. 1B). Notably, bee pollen consumption in Rc was twice that of Zm, this difference was not statistically significant, likely due to the high variability in bee pollen consumption among individual bees.

Effects of bee pollen diets on HPG development

The bee pollen diets significantly influenced HPG development and acini volume after a 6-day feeding period. As presented in Fig. 1, bees fed Bn, Rc, Cs, or pollen mix exhibited well-developed HPGs, whereas those fed Bp, Zm pollen or protein-free diet displayed smaller glands. HPG acini volume was maximal in bees fed Bn pollen (median = 938.5 mm³, mean = 1010.8 mm³), nearly double that of bees fed Rc pollen (median = 398.0 mm³, mean = 598.6 mm³) or the mixed pollen diet (median = 323.1 mm³, mean = 565.0 mm³) (P < 0.001, general linear hypothesis testing (GLHT) post-hoc comparisons) (Fig. 2, Supplementary Table 2.). The Cs diet supported intermediate HPG acini development (median = 214.7 mm³, mean = 339.8 mm³), while Bp and Zm diets resulted in minimal development (median = 162.3 mm³ and 118.8 mm³, respectively, and mean = 202.1 and 147.9 mm³, respectively), comparable to the protein-free control (P = 0.086 and 0.848, GLHT post-hoc comparisons) (Fig. 2, Supplementary Table 2).

Effect of bee pollen diets on mrjp1 and cyp450 6AS8 gene expression

Several studies have investigated the expression of stable reference genes in honey bees30–33. However, none have investigated suitable reference genes for quantifying adult gene expression following the consumption of different bee pollen diets. In the present study, we selected six candidate genes, namely rpL27A, actin, rpS18, rpL32, α-tubulin, and ef1a, which were used as housekeeping genes in a previous study involving A. mellifera33. We evaluated the stability of the expression of these reference genes in the heads of 7-day-old worker bees after they were fed seven diets (six pollen diets and one protein-free diet). Our analysis revealed that the expression levels of all six reference genes in the bees’ heads exhibited different profiles in response to the various diets (Supplementary Fig. 2). Of the reference genes evaluated, rpL27A and rpL32 demonstrated the most stable expression, as assessed by minimal variation in Ct value. In contrast, the remaining four reference genes exhibited greater Ct value fluctuations. Given its consistent expression and appropriate Ct value in bee head samples, rpL27A was selected as the normalization gene.

We analyzed the mRNA expression of the mrjp1 and cyp450 6AS8 genes in the heads of newly emerged honey bees that were continually fed six different pollen diets for 6 days. The gene expression in the head was normalized using the reference gene rpL27A and compared with the ΔCt value, which was obtained from the bees fed the pollen mix diet. After caged bees consumed different diets for 6 days, the relative expression level of the mrjp1 gene exhibited wide variation among the bees. Bn pollen triggers the highest gene expression with a log2 relative expression value of 5.7-fold, followed by Rc pollen (2.5-fold). In comparison to the pollen mix group, bees fed Cs pollen showed a moderate decrease in gene expression (-1.6-fold), while Bp and Zm pollen led to a decrease in gene expression with a log2 relative expression value of -5.9 and − 7.2-fold, respectively, suggesting a potential protein deficiency impact on mrjp1 expression. However, these differences were not statistically significant (ANOVA, F = 2.839, df1 = 4, df2 = 15, P = 0.0618) (Fig. 3A).

Regarding the cyp450 6AS8 gene, all pollen types led to a moderate increase in expression compared to the pollen mix group (Fig. 3B). Among the pollen types, Bn shows an upregulation with a log2 relative expression value of 1.8-fold, followed by Rc (1.6-fold), Cs (1.6-fold), and Zm (1.5-fold). Bp exhibited the least upregulation with log2 relative expression values of 0.7-fold. However, these differences were not statistically significant (Kruskal-Wallis test, P = 0.9554, Fig. 3B).

Royal jelly production using caged bees

Our findings indicate that honey bee HPGs fully develop within seven days of emergence when bees are fed Bn, Rc, Cs, or pollen mix diets. To induce queen rearing, a cage containing 100 newly emerged bees that were fed Bn pollen was established. On the 7th day, five queen cells containing 1-day-old larvae were introduced into the cages (Fig. 4A). However, none of the queen cells were accepted. To address this, we manipulated several factors including worker bee number (100–500), presence of larval pheromones (a small frame with 100 3-day-old larvae was introduced into the cage on day 6), and queen cell introduction timing (the 7th -9th day after worker emergence). Despite these attempts, worker bees consistently rejected queen cells. These results suggest a potential secretion-based regulatory mechanism for royal jelly production, whereby worker bees, although possessing fully developed HPGs, are unable to secrete royal jelly even after prolonged pollen consumption. Consequently, we were unable to establish an in vitro royal jelly production system and, therefore, could not examine the impact of pollen-induced mrjp1 expression and cyp450 6AS8 expression on royal jelly quality, specifically MRJP1 and 10-HDA content.

Unexpectedly, during repeated trials, we observed worker bees, randomly collected from larval frames, spontaneously secreting royal jelly into queen cells within the cage system (Fig. 4B). This serendipitous finding prompted an investigation into the correlation between worker bee numbers and royal jelly production. Notably, royal jelly secretion occurred exclusively in cages containing a frame of at least 100 3-day-old larvae (each being more than or equal to 3-day-old).

As illustrated in Fig. 5A, introducing 100, 200, and 300 worker bees to cages resulted in queen cell acceptance rates of 62.7%, 64.7%, and 72.0%, respectively. Although a trend towards increased acceptance with higher worker numbers was observed, this difference was not statistically significant (Kruskal-Wallis test, P = 0.4522). However, a significant positive correlation between worker count and total royal jelly production was evident (Fig. 5B). Cages with 300 workers yielded approximately 1.7 g of royal jelly across five queen cells, significantly exceeding the 1.1 g and 1.4 g produced by cages with 100 and 200 workers, respectively (ANOVA, P < 0.001, F = 21.11, df1 = 2, df2 = 39; Fig. 5B). When normalized to worker number, individual royal jelly production decreased with increasing worker population. Bees in cages with 100, 200, and 300 workers produced approximately 10.9, 6.9, and 5.9 mg of royal jelly per bee, respectively (ANOVA, P < 0.001, F = 31.38, df1 = 2, df2 = 39; Fig. 5C).

Discussion

While statistical analysis did not reveal significant differences in total bee pollen consumption among the six pollen types (Bn, Rc, Cs, Bp, Zm, and pollen mix), a clear preference for specific pollen sources emerged. Our previous study revealed that in a group of 50 bees, each bee consumed an average of 5.1 mg of polyfloral bee pollen per day during the first 9 days after emergence34. Similarly, other studies have indicated that bees can consume approximately 5 mg of bee pollen per day18,35. In contrast, our study found that bees consumed more than 5 mg of bee pollen per day when fed Bn, Rc, Cs, Bp, or the pollen mix. Notably, Bn and Rc pollen were particularly favored, with consumption exceeding 10 mg per bee daily. Conversely, Zm pollen was the least preferred.

Honey bee health, including immune function and parasite resistance, is positively correlated with a diverse pollen diet17,36. Bee pollen preferences are likely influenced by physicochemical properties, volatile organic compounds, or phagostimulants37–40. Additionally, honey bees demonstrate a preference for freshly stored pollen over older hive-stored pollen41,42, potentially due to differences in chemical signals or aroma.

In this study, five types of bee pollen were harvested at different times of the year due to different plant blooming times. Dry bee pollen was utilized for this study due to several reasons. First, it allows for precise measurement and control over the amount of pollen provided to bees, ensuring consistency across different experimental groups and replicates. Second, drying pollen helps to preserve its nutritional and chemical properties, preventing degradation or spoilage that can occur with fresh pollen. This ensures that the bees receive a consistent diet throughout the experiment. In our previous study on tea tree bee pollen, we also found that the 45 °C drying process only decreased total phenolic content by about 8% with no effect on other macronutrient or total flavonoid content43. Third, dry bee pollen can be stored for longer periods compared to fresh pollen, making it easier to conduct experiments over extended durations or repeat experiments with the same pollen source.

We have not yet identified the factors that influence bees’ apparent preference for Bn and Rc bee pollen. Currently, we know only that Bn and Rc pollens have higher protein and total phenolic content than Bp and Zm pollens29,44. Whether this factor influences the observed pollen preferences is unclear. Additionally, bees may prefer different types of pollen in different seasons, as the flowering plants vary throughout the year45. Since this study was conducted only in the spring, it may not accurately reflect the bees’ pollen preferences across all seasons. Further research is needed to elucidate the role of volatile compounds or phagostimulants in these preferences.

While we focused on bees’ pollen preferences, which can vary due to differences in the physicochemical properties of bee pollen, other factors may have also influenced our results. Notably, the ability of bees to digest different pollens can vary considerably, impacting nutrient utilization and potentially influencing their physiology and experimental outcomes46,47. This variation in digestibility stems from two key factors: (1) Pollen structure: The exine, the tough outer layer of pollen, varies in composition and thickness among plant species, affecting how easily bees can access nutrients48. (2) Gut microbiota: Newly emerged worker bees are virtually germ-free, and our study’s rearing conditions (caged and kept in clean cages) limited their ability to obtain a typical microbiome. This, combined with the fact that different pollens carry distinct microbial communities that can shape the gut microbiota, likely affected pollen digestion efficiency49–51. Even if bees prefer a particular pollen, their ability to effectively digest it is crucial for determining its nutritional value. Furthermore, our study used dried pollen, which may differ from fresh pollen in scent, texture, and nutrient availability, potentially influencing both bee preferences and pollen processing.

Many studies have indicated that protein content plays a crucial role in HPG development18,20,21. Degrandi-Hoffmann et al. (2010) demonstrated that both bee pollen and protein supplements significantly stimulated HPG development compared to a protein-free diet, establishing a direct link between dietary protein and HPG growth21. Peng et al. (2012) further supported this by showing that protein-supplemented pollen substitutes enhanced HPG development in nurse bees. However, they noted that HPG development is not solely determined by protein consumption; it is also affected by factors such as nonessential amino acids and varying ingredient concentrations20. Omar et al. (2017) compared the impact of different pollen types on HPG development. Pollens from Asparagus sp. and Castanea sp. promoted HPG growth more effectively than those from Helianthus sp. and Sinapis sp. This correlated with higher essential amino acid content in Asparagus sp. and Castanea sp. pollens (8.71 and 6.93 g/100 g dry matter, respectively) compared to Helianthus sp. and Sinapis sp. (5.27 and 8.5 g/100 g dry matter)18. Although Sinapis sp. pollen was rich in essential amino acids, it failed to stimulate HPG growth, suggesting that factors beyond amino acid content, such as lipid or vitamin availability, influence HPG development18.

Bn, Rc, and Cs pollen exhibited higher protein (27.2, 27.0, and 29.9 g/100 g dry matter, respectively) and essential amino acid (10.81, 10.35, and 11.96 g/100 g dry matter, respectively) contents compared to Bp and Zm pollen (16.4 and 17.2 g/100 g dry matter for protein, and 6.66 and 6.17 g/100 g dry matter for essential amino acids)29. These nutritional profiles align with the observed HPG development patterns in this study. Notably, Bn pollen, despite similar protein and essential amino acid content to Rc and Cs pollens, elicited a stronger stimulatory effect on HPG growth. This observation supports the suggestion made by Omar et al. (2017) regarding the presence of additional limiting factors that affect HPG development beyond protein and amino acid content18.

This study represents the influence of bee pollen on mrjp1 and cyp450 6AS8 gene expression in bees. We observed a correlation between mrjp1 expression patterns and HPG development across different pollen diets. These findings suggest a link between mrjp1 expression and HPG development, with higher mrjp1 levels associated with more developed HPGs. The specific components within bee pollen responsible for modulating mrjp1 expression warrant further investigation.

Previous research demonstrated that CYP450 6AS8, expressed in the mandibular gland, is involved in 10-HDA biosynthesis26. In this study, we analyzed the expression pattern of cyp450 6AS8 in the entire bee head rather than solely the mandibular gland after the bees consumed different bee pollens. The expression pattern of cyp450 6AS8 in the bee heads differed from that of mrjp1, showing a consistent induction by bee pollen but no distinct expression pattern among the bee pollen types tested. However, in reality, bees fed with some high-nutrient bee pollens produce royal jelly with increased 10-HDA content52,53. Pattamayutanon et al. (2018) found that bee colonies fed pollens from coffee (Coffea arabica) and bitter bush (Eupatorium odoratum) produced royal jelly with higher 10-HDA content53, and Ma et al. (2022) noted that bee colonies produced royal jelly with higher 10-HDA content during tea tree blooming52. If CYP450 6AS8 were solely responsible for hydroxylating fatty acids in the biosynthesis of 10-HDA, we would expect its expression to vary depending on the pollen type consumed by the bees. However, this is not the case, as we observed no distinct expression patterns among the different pollen types tested. This suggests that CYP450 6AS8 may have additional functions beyond 10-HDA biosynthesis or that other factors influence 10-HDA production. Further research is necessary to elucidate the complex interplay between bee pollen, gene expression, and royal jelly composition.

This study successfully enabled worker bees to produce royal jelly in a laboratory-based bee cage platform in the presence of a larval brood comb. This result corroborates the findings of Huang et al. (1989) and Wegener et al. (2009), which emphasize the critical role of bee larval broods in stimulating HPG development54,55. This also leads us to believe that larval brood pheromones likely play a crucial role in this platform. Previous research has linked beta-ocimene to increased foraging activity and brood ester pheromone to HPG development in bee colonies56–59. Corby-Harris et al. (2022) further confirmed that beta-ocimene and brood ester pheromone can enhance pollen-dependent processes such as HPG growth and mrjp1 expression60. We further examined the impact of larval pheromones, namely beta-ocimene, on royal jelly production in the bee cage. As expected, the workers can secrete royal jelly into queen cells in bee cages when larval broods are replaced with beta-ocimene (Supplementary Fig. 3). The royal jelly yield is comparable to that of the bee cage containing larval brood. By utilizing this controlled environment to investigate factors influencing royal jelly production, we can gain more insights into the underlying mechanisms of worker bee behavior. Ongoing investigations focus on identifying factors that regulate royal jelly secretion by worker bees reared in cages post-pupation.

Methods

Honey bees

The bee colonies were housed at the bee farm of National Chung Hsing University. Each bee colony in this experiment was headed by a 2–3 month-old queen and consisted of seven frames: one with eggs, two with larvae, three with pupae, and one with foods. All queens originated from the same mother colony. The bee colonies were inspected at least once a week. During periods of food shortage, such as summer and winter, the colonies received scheduled feedings of sugar solution and artificial bee bread.

Bee pollens

Beekeepers harvested bee pollen using pollen traps during the plant’s blooming time29. The harvested bee pollen was immediately frozen at − 20 °C. When the producer accumulates about 200–300 kg of bee pollen in the freezer, they will send it to the company Yong Shyang Apiculture Co., Ltd for further processing. There, after bee pollen was dried at 45 °C for 24 h, it was sorted by color using the machine, and the pollen of the same color was pooled. These pooled samples were sent to the bee Lab for further assessment of purity and botanical origin via light microscopy (Axio Scope A1, Zeiss, China). Purity analysis involved calculating the percentage of the dominant pollen species in 1 g of each sample, with all samples exceeding 98% purity. Plant species were first identified based on palynological keys61, with further examination of sequenced rbcL and trnH-psbA marker genes29. Finally, samples were sieved (0.5-mm stainless steel mesh), repackaged, and frozen at − 20 °C for subsequent analysis. In this study, five types of dried bee pollen were used, namely rape (Brassica napus, Bn), beggartick (Bidens pilosa var. radiata, Bp), tea tree (Camellia sinensis, Cs), nutgall tree (Rhus chinensis var. roxburghii, Rc), and corn (Zea mays, Zm) pollen. These bee pollen samples were harvested and processed between 2021 and 2022.

Effect of bee pollen on caged bees

The experiments were conducted in quadruplicate during the spring season of 2022 (Feb-Apr), primarily using four healthy bee colonies (each with seven frames). For each replicate, one of the sealed brood frames was selected from each of the four bee colonies at 5 pm and maintained in a dark insect growth chamber at a temperature of 33 °C ± 1 °C and a relative humidity of 60% ± 10%. The following morning at 9 am, 200 newly emerged bees were randomly collected from each of these four frames. These bees were then mixed together and randomly assigned to the bee cages (10 × 10 × 10 cm3), with 30 newly emerged bees per cage for assays34.

Seven dietary treatments were designed: five individual pollen, one mixed-pollen (comprising 20% of each of the five types of pollen), and one protein-free diet (only supplemented with 50% sucrose solution). A pollen patty was prepared by mixing 10 g of dry bee pollen with 3 mL of 50% sucrose solution. All treatment groups were provided with a syrup feeder containing 50% sucrose solution. The diets, which included the bee pollen patty and 50% sucrose solution, were replaced every 3 days.

Bee mortality remained under 5% per cage throughout the experiment. On day 7, bees were anesthetized with CO2. For each experimental replicate, five bees per cage were randomly selected for head dissection and HPG acini size measurement. Another five bees per cage were dissected for head RNA purification to study gene expression.

Measurement of the HPG acini

To dissect the HPGs, the heads of the bees were opened dorsally by using a pair of tweezers. The paired HPGs were carefully removed from the heads by using tweezers and were placed onto a microscope slide in a droplet of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; pH 7.4). The HPGs on the slide were analyzed using an ocular micrometer (Nikon MBM 12100, Taichung, Taiwan) under a stereomicroscope coupled to a camera (Tekfar Science, Taichung, Taiwan). To assess the developmental status of the HPG, we measured the length and width of each oval acinus within a cluster of 10 adjacent acini for each bee. In each experimental replicate, five bees per cage were randomly chosen for head dissection and HPG acini size measurement. As we conducted four independent biological replicates for this experiment, the total number of acini measured per treatment group was 200 (For each bee pollen diet treatment; 10 adjacent acini per bee x 5 bees per cage x 4 replicate cages).

The volume of each acinus was calculated using the following formula18:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\text{V}=\frac{4}{3}{\uppi\:}{\left(\frac{\text{a}+\text{b}}{4}\right)}^{3}$$\end{document}

where V is the volume of an acinus, a is the maximum length, b is the maximum width of the acini, and π = 3.14.

RNA extraction

Five heads of 7-day-old caged worker bees of each experimental replicate were collected and immediately ground in 1 mL of TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) to homogenize and extract total RNA from the bee heads. Then, high-quality total RNA was extracted using a PureLink RNA minikit (Invitrogen) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. To eliminate any residual DNA contamination from the RNA samples, we further treated them with a TURBO DNA-free kit (Invitrogen) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Next, a Qubit fluorometer (Invitrogen) was used to determine RNA quantity. Four replicate samples were collected. The RNA samples were subsequently stored at − 80 °C.

Gene expression profile obtained using quantitative reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

For the reverse transcription step, we used 1 µg of total RNA and the iScript cDNA synthesis kit (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). The gene-specific primers are listed in Supplementary Table 1. The primer for the cyp450 6AS8 gene was designed using the online programs Primer3Plus and Primer-BLAST to ensure primer specificity. Each quantitative PCR reaction was set up in a 96-well microtiter plate with 10 µL of 2× iQ SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad), 2.5 µL of 1.6 µM of each gene-specific primer, and 5 µL of diluted cDNA in a final volume of 20 µL. PCR was performed using a CFX connect detection system (Bio-Rad). The cycling program comprised an initial step completed at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles at 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C (depending on the Tm value of primers) for 30 s. A melting curve analysis of the final amplified product was performed using continuous readings over increasing temperatures from 55 °C to 95 °C to ensure amplification specificity. The qPCR data were collected using Bio-Rad CFX Maestro Software and normalized to the reference gene rpL27A30. The relative gene expression data were analyzed using the 2−ΔΔCT method62. The 2−ΔΔCt values were further subjected to a log2 transformation. The mrjp1 gene expression was analyzed using four independent biological replicates, each with three technical replicates. The cyp450 6AS8 gene expression, on the other hand, was analyzed using three independent biological replicates, also with three technical replicates each (RNA samples from one of the four independent biological replicates were depleted due to an operational error, resulting in the analysis of cyp450 6AS8 gene expression being conducted with only three replicates.).

Royal jelly production in bee cages

A royal jelly production platform was established using cages (15 × 10 × 15 cm3) containing 100 to 300 worker bees, provided with rape pollen patties and 50% sucrose solution. To assess royal jelly production, 1-day-old larvae were carefully grafted from worker bee frames into the commercial plastic queen-rearing cups. Five cups, serving as artificial queen cells, were then introduced into each cage. These cells were then monitored for acceptance by the nurse bees. After 3 days, the number of queen cups that were successfully adopted (acceptance rate) was recorded. The royal jelly present in each accepted queen cup was then carefully harvested and weighed to determine the yield of royal jelly per cage. This process was repeated 15, 17, and 10 times for the cages with 100, 200, and 300 worker bees, respectively.

Statistics

Data on HPG acini development were visualized using violin plots. Royal jelly yield in caged bees was presented using box plots, where the whiskers extend to the minimum and maximum values, and the box encompasses the interquartile range (IQR), containing the middle 50% of the data. The top and bottom edges of the box represent the upper quartile (Q3) and lower quartile (Q1), respectively.

The impact of dietary treatments on HPG acini volume was assessed using generalized linear mixed models (GLMM) with individual bees nested within replicate as a random factor, followed by general linear hypothesis testing (GLHT) post-hoc comparisons. The log2 relative gene expression of mrjp1 and cyp450 6AS8 across pollen treatments were analyzed using ANOVA with Tukey post-hoc tests and the Kruskal-Wallis test, respectively. For royal jelly production in caged bees, queen cell acceptance rates were compared using the Kruskal-Wallis test, and royal jelly yield was analyzed using ANOVA with Tukey post-hoc tests. Data normality and homoscedasticity were verified using Shapiro-Wilk, Kolmogorov-Smirnov, and Levene’s tests63,64. Statistical analyses were performed in R using various packages and functions: ggplot function in” ggplot2” package65, lmer function in “lme4” package66, glht function in “multcomp” package67, leveneTest function in “car” package68, and aov, Kruskal.test, TukeyHSD functions in “stats” package in R statistical software version 4.3.2 69.
