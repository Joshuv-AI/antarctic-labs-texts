Long-term surgical outcomes of combined Ahmed glaucoma valve implantation and phacoemulsification with intraocular lens implantation for patients with glaucoma secondary to iridocorneal endothelial syndrome
Authors: Yunxiao Sun, Xiaoming Duan, Yuxin Fang, Xin Tang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo assess the long-term outcomes of patients with glaucoma secondary to iridocorneal endothelial syndrome (GS-ICE) who underwent Ahmed glaucoma valve (AGV) implantation combined with phacoemulsification and intraocular lens (IOL) implantation surgery.MethodsIn this non-comparative retrospective case series study, twelve patients with uncontrolled GS-ICE and cataract underwent Ahmed aqueous shunt combined with phacoemulsification and IOL implantation surgery at Beijing Tongren Eye Center between June 2014 and June 2022. Main medical records included best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), intraocular pressure (IOP), number of antiglaucoma medications (AGM), corneal status and further surgical interventions. Surgical success was defined as a postoperative IOP ≥ 6mmHg and ≤ 21mmHg without (complete success) or with/without (qualified success) topical AGM and vision of at least light perception during the follow-up period.ResultsTwelve eyes of twelve patients were reviewed. Preoperative IOP was decreased from a mean of 38.5 ± 6.7 mmHg on 3.3 ± 0.9 AGM to a mean of 16.3 ± 4.3 mmHg (P<0.001) on 0.6 ± 1.1 medications (P<0.001) at the last follow-up (47.0 ± 24.7 months). Eight eyes (66.7%) had stable vision or at least one line improvement than that before surgery. Eight eyes (66.7%) achieved qualified success and six eyes (50.0%) achieved complete success after surgery. The surgical procedure had a qualified survival of 91.7% at 1 year, 82.5% at 3 years, 72.2% at 5 years, and 48.1% at 7 years.ConclusionAGV implantation combined with phacoemulsification and IOL implantation seems to be an effective method in IOP control and vision maintain for patients with GS-ICE. The combined surgical procedure may be a reliable option for those GS-ICE patients with coexisting cataract.Keywords: Glaucoma secondary to ICE syndrome, Ahmed glaucoma valve, Phacoemulsification and intraocular lens implantation

Background

Iridocorneal endothelial (ICE) syndrome is a rare ocular disorder characterized by corneal endothelial abnormalities, anterior-chamber angle changes, iris alterations, and secondary glaucoma [1]. The disease is typically a unilateral condition and more frequent in females than in males. This syndrome can be subdivided into Chandler syndrome (CS), progressive iris atrophy (PIA), and Cogan-Reese syndrome (CRS) according mainly to iris changes. More precisely, the subtypes may be considered as different presentations of the same disorder course because of the basic pathology lying in the abnormal corneal endothelial cell layer. These abnormal cells can lead to corneal edema and possess the ability to migrate across the trabecular meshwork to the iris surface. Contraction of the endothelial cell layer can result in iris alterations, peripheral anterior synechiae (PAS), progressive closure of the anterior chamber angle, trabecular meshwork obstruction, then leading to an increased intraocular pressure (IOP) and angle-closure glaucoma.

It is reported that the prevalence of glaucoma secondary to ICE syndrome (GS-ICE) ranges from 46–82%.1 Glaucomatous optic neuropathy and corneal edema are the 2 main causes of vision decline and loss in these patients. Most cases will undergo repeated surgical treatment to lower IOP. Persistent high IOP and multiple surgical stimulation also make more patients develop cataract early. Which make GS-ICE one of the refractory glaucoma.

Pharmacotherapy is usually ineffective. Filtering procedures of trabeculectomy are often successful in the short term but often fails, probably due to the continued growth of endothelial membrane over the internal ostium and bleb fibrosis because of chronic persistent inflammation [2–4]. Recent studies have shown that Ahmed glaucoma valve (AGV) implantation is an effective way for the treatment of GS-ICE [5, 6]. Only a few cases failed due to iris or ICE membrane obstruction of the tube ostium, especially when the drainage tube in anterior chamber is short or close to the iris surface [7]. There are also some patients with stable IOP control, but cataract surgery is performed in the later stage due to visual loss caused by lens opacity.

When cataract and glaucoma are present in the same patient, combined phacoemulsification and anti-glaucoma surgery may be the best surgical treatment for such cases. The combined procedure may reduce the risks of repeated surgery, such as bleeding, infection, corneal endothelial injury, damage to optic nerve caused by excessive fluctuation of IOP, etc. Previous study have confirmed that combined procedure is a more cost-effective protocol for IOP lowering in glaucoma patients with concomitant cataract, during the 2-year follow-up period [8].

However, combined procedure in GS-ICE patients with coexisting cataract has not been reported so far. The discovery of nearly 5% incidence of late endophthalmitis and blebitis after trabeculectomy with antifibrotic agents offers a new rationale for the application of glaucoma drainage implants in the combined surgery [9]. The objective of this study was to investigate the safety and efficacy of combined AGV implantation and phacoemulsification with intraocular lens (IOL) implantation surgery as a means of IOP reduction and visual recovery in GS-ICE.

Materials and methods

Participants and ophthalmic examination

This is a retrospective, non-comparative case series study recruiting patients with GS-ICE and coexisting cataract who underwent combined AGV implantation and phacoemulsification with IOL implantation surgery from June 2014 to June 2022 at Beijing Tongren Eye Center.

The diagnosis of ICE syndrome is based on a ‘‘hammered-silver’’ appearance of corneal endothelium, ‘‘ICE cells’’ on specular microscopy, iris nodules, iris atrophy and PAS [10]. Patients were divided into CS, PIA, and CRS according to their phenotype. All enrolled patients underwent a comprehensive examination data collection, including age, gender, type of ICE, previous surgery, preoperation details, best corrected visual acuity (BCVA), IOP, number of antiglaucoma medications (AGM), corneal status and postoperation details (BCVA, IOP, number of AGM, corneal status, subsequent surgical treatment, duration of follow-up).

All subjects provided written informed consent for the procedure. And this study was approved by the Ethics Committees of Beijing Tongren Eye Center and complied with the principle of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Surgical procedures

All operations were performed by the same glaucoma specialist (Xin Tang).

The preferred site for surgery in these patients was the superotemporal quadrant. After preparing a fornix-based conjunctival flap in this quadrant, a half-thickness rectangular limbus-based scleral flap with an area of 4 × 5 mm was made. According to the surgeon’s estimate of each patient’s individual condition, MMC of 0.4 mg/ml was placed under the conjunctiva flap for 5–6 min and then rinsed with balanced saline solution.

Cataract phacoemulsification combined with IOL implantation was performed using the standard clear corneal phacoemulsification technique. Carbachol shrinks the pupil. Intermittent suturing of corneal incisions with 10 − 0 nylon sutures was performed in some cases.

AGV (Model FP7: New World Medical Inc, Rancho Cucamonga, CA) was initialized by pushing balanced saline solution via 29-gauge insulin syringe. Then fix it at 8 mm from the limbus with polypropylene 5 − 0 suture. Trim the implant tube to a suitable length, and then insert it into the anterior chamber filled with viscoelastics through a 23-gauge needle track. The drainage tube was covered with the scleral flap and fixed with 10 − 0 nylon suture. The Tenon’s capsule and conjunctiva were re-approximated to the limbus with 10 − 0 nylon suture. Dexamethasone and antibiotics were administered subconjunctival at the end of the procedure.

Success criteria for surgery

The criteria for surgery success were defined as a postoperative IOP ≥ 6mmHg and ≤ 21mmHg without (complete success) or with/without (qualified success) topical AGM and vision of at least light perception during the follow-up period. Failure was considered when the IOP after medical therapy was > 21 mmHg or if further surgery was needed, or when visual acuity declined to no light perception (NLP).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software version 22.0 (SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). The normal distribution of data was tested using Shapiro-Wilk test. Data were presented as mean values ± standard deviation, and categorical variables were described as proportions. Paired t-test was used to compare preoperative and postoperative data. Kaplan-Meier survival curves analysis was used to assess the success rate in IOP control of combined procedure in GS-ICE patients with coexisting cataract. p < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

The baseline clinical characteristics of AGV implantation combined with phacoemulsification and IOL implantation surgery for patients with GS-ICE and coexisting cataract are summarized in Table 1. Twelve patients include 4 males (33.3%) and 8 females (66.7%). The mean age of the subjects at baseline was 47.3 ± 11.3 years. The clinical phenotype of ICE were 7 eyes with CS (58.3%), 1 eyes with CRS (8.3%), and 4 eyes with PIA (33.3%). 11 eyes (91.7%) had a history of glaucoma surgery. Among them, 10 eyes underwent trabeculectomy, 2 eyes underwent repeat trabeculectomy, 1 eye underwent post-trabeculectomy trans-scleral cyclophotocoagulation (TSCPC), and 1 eye underwent 360-degree trabeculotomy. The preoperative BCVA in logMAR ranged from 0.3 to hand movement (HM). The mean preoperative IOP was 38.5 ± 6.7 mmHg. The mean preoperative number of AGMs was 3.3 ± 0.9. And 7 eyes (58.3%) had clear corneas at baseline.

The postoperative results of the combined surgery are shown in Table 2. The median follow-up duration was 47.0 ± 24.7 months. At the last follow-up visit, The BCVA in logMAR ranged from 0.1 to no light perception (NLP). Among them, 8 eyes (66.7%) had stable vision or at least one line improvement than that before surgery. The average IOP decreased to 16.3 ± 4.3 mmHg, which was significantly lower than the baseline (P<0.001). The number of AGMs reduced to 0.6 ± 1.1 (P<0.001). There were 5 eyes (41.7%) with clear corneas at the last follow-up, and in two of these eyes, the cornea became clear from edema after postoperative IOP control. After AGV implantation combined with phacoemulsification and IOL implantation surgery, follow-up interventions were performed in 5 eyes (41.7%) due to the uncontrolled IOP. 3 eyes (25.0%) received additional glaucoma surgeries, 2 of them underwent needle revision followed by TSCPC and 1 underwent needle revision followed by penetrating canaloplasty. And the other 2 eyes required topical AGMs to control IOP.

Discussion

GS-ICE is a refractory glaucoma that cannot be successfully treated using conventional medical therapy and needs glaucoma surgery to control IOP.

Primary trabeculectomy in GS-ICE has a moderate surgical success rate compared with most other glaucoma types. Laganowski et al. reported a 63% survival at 1 year, 44% at 2 years, and 23% at 5 years after trabeculectomy without antifibrotics in a series of 20 patients [11]. Long-term success of trabeculectomy in refractory glaucoma has increased after using antifibrotic agents. Chandran et al. reported a 82% qualified survival at 1 year, 71% at 3 years and 60% at 5 years after trabeculectomy using mitomycin-C (MMC) in a series of 15 patients [4]. It is generally accepted that progressive development of ICE syndrome with abnormal membranes and synechiae obstructing the internal filtration sites and an aggressive inflammatory response lead to early fibrosis and failure of the filtering bleb [11]. Although the neodymium: YAG laser can unblock the internal ostium, early fibrosis of the bleb also affects aqueous humor drainage.

The importance of AGV implantation in the treatment of GS-ICE has become increasingly prominent. The success rate of AGV in GS-ICE reported in recent literature was 50.5–94.4%.5,6 Bayu G. Gebremichael et al. reported that 18 eyes were followed up for 20.6 months, and the rate of AGV success was 92.3%, 66.1%, and 50.5% at 1, 3, and 4 years. And another study with 18 participants, showed that a success rate of 94.4% at 1 year, 88.1% at 2 years, and 73.5% at 3 years during the 42.0 months of follow-up. Different success criteria and follow-up time, patients’ heterogeneity and different perioperative management may contribute to the variability in success rates. In contrast to trabeculectomy, the drainage tube is less easily obstructed by the membrane formed by ICE cells during AGV implantation, that may improve the success rate of the drainage device.

Refractory glaucoma patients are often accompanied by varying degrees of lens opacity under the action of long-term high IOP. For some ICE patients, besides glaucomatous optic neuropathy, lens opacity and corneal edema also affect visual function. Therefore, in addition to the surgical treatment of glaucoma to reduce IOP, it is also necessary to treat cataract to improve postoperative visual acuity. In the follow-up of ICE patients after drainage valve implantation, the incidence of cataract can reach 50% [6].

Studies have found that combined cataract surgery not only improves visual acuity, but also widens the anterior chamber angle. In a study to explore the long-term effect of mild cataract extraction on the morphology of anterior chamber in patients with suspected primary angle closure. Lens extraction performed better in terms of relieving anterior chamber crowding and enlarging drainage angle during up to 2-year follow-up [12]. Similar results have been found in previous reports on the influence of lens extraction on the morphology of anterior segment [13–16]. The above studies show that lens extraction can widen the anterior chamber angle by removing the thick lens to alleviate anterior segment crowding, and relieving the anterior rotation of ciliary body to provide more space for iris to fall back.

Phacoemulsification combined with glaucoma drainage implant surgery has a good clinical effect and low complication rate for patients with refractory glaucoma and cataract [17]. As far as we know, this is the first study to examine the long-term safety and efficacy of combined AGV implantation and phacoemulsification with IOL implantation surgery as a means of IOP reduction and visual recovery in GS-ICE with coexisting cataract.

Among the cases we included, CS was the most common subtype of ICE syndrome, which was consistent with the following studies. Bo Feng et al. reported that among the 4 failed eyes, 2 had CS, and 2 had PIA [10]. Zhen Mao et al. found in their cases that of the 4 eyes with failed AGV implantation, 2 eyes had CS, 1 eye had PIA and 1 eye had CRS [5]. The above results suggest that CS may be a more aggressive variant of ICE in Chinese population.

In present series, all enrolled patients had cataract due to previous multiple intraocular operations and aging. At the last follow-up after the combined operation. Eight eyes (66.7%) had stable vision or at least one line improvement than that before surgery. The more concrete result was that10 eyes (83.3%) had vision of at least light perception, 5 eyes (41.7%) had a better vision than that before surgery and 3 eyes (25.0%) have the same vision as before. Compared with the preoperative baseline IOP, the average postoperative IOP decreased significantly to 16.3 ± 4.3 mmHg. There were 5 eyes (41.7%) with clear corneas at the last follow-up, and in two of these eyes, the cornea became clear from edema after postoperative IOP control. Therefore, based on the above results, the visual rehabilitation was mainly attributed to cataract phacoemulsification and IOL implantation, followed by good control of IOP after AGV implantation.

The combined surgical procedure in our study had a qualified survival of 91.7% at 1 year, 82.5% at 3 years, 72.2% at 5 years, and 48.1% at 7 years. The long-term success rate of combined surgery is higher than that of glaucoma drainage valve implantation alone in previous studies [5–7, 18]. The widening of the anterior chamber angle after lens removal may reduce the pulling effect of the abnormal iris on the drainage tube or slow down the rate of abnormal membrane migration blocking the ostium. In addition, the injury to the corneal endothelium caused by subsequent cataract surgery is also avoided.

At last follow-up of case 2 in our study, we found that the corneal endothelium was decompensated and, despite the deeper anterior chamber, the superior temporal drainage tube was still partially blocked by the iris migration (Fig. 2). Previous study have compared the effects of AGV tube placement in ciliary sulcus or anterior chamber on the endothelial cell density and morphology during the follow-up. It turns out that tube implantation in ciliary sulcus may be the first choice to reduce the loss of endothelial cell in patients with IOL [19]. In addition, placing the tube in ciliary sulcus may help prevent the abnormal membrane from obstructing the tube. Therefore, cataract surgery combined with AGV fixated in sulcus may be a better option for GS-ICE patients. However, more comparative cases are needed to confirm this in the future.

In the follow-up 3 eyes (25.0%) received additional glaucoma surgeries, 2 of them underwent needle revision followed by TSCPC and 1 underwent needle revision followed by penetrating canaloplasty. TSCPC has been shown to be a temporizing option for patients with refractory glaucoma whose IOP remains poorly controlled after multiple anti-glaucoma operations [20]. Study concerning the effectiveness of penetrating canaloplasty in the treatment of GS-ICE has been reported. In a series of 29 cases with successful penetrating canaloplasty, 24(82.8%) gained qualified success and 22(75.9%) gained qualified success at 12 months [21]. Similarly, in case 5, penetrating canaloplasty was performed again after the failure of the combined surgery, and the IOP was maintained at the normal level during the follow-up.

Our study has several limitations. Firstly, this is a retrospective study with small sample, and a prospective, large sample, randomized study is needed to demonstrate whether combined surgery would be a first option for GS-ICE. Secondly, the structure of the anterior chamber angle should be measured before and after surgery to evaluate the effect of the angle widening on the drainage tube after cataract surgery. Thirdly, the present results may not be extrapolated to other ethnic populations because only native Han Chinese cases were enrolled in this study.

In conclusion, AGV implantation combined with phacoemulsification and IOL implantation seems to be an effective method in IOP control and vision maintain for patients with GS-ICE at a long follow-up. The combined surgical procedure may be a reliable option for those GS-ICE patients with coexisting cataract.
