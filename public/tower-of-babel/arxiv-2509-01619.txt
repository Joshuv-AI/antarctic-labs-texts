Throttling Web Agents Using Reasoning Gates
Abhinav Kumar, Jaechul Roh, Ali Naseh, Eugene Bagdasarian

Abstract
AI web agents use Internet resources at far greater speed, scale, and complexity—changing how users and services interact. Deployed maliciously or erroneously, these agents could overload content providers. At the same time, web agents can bypass CAPTCHAs and other defenses by mimicking user behavior or flood authentication systems with fake accounts. Yet providers must protect their services and content from denial-of-service attacks and scraping by web agents. In this paper, we design a framework that imposes tunable costs on agents before providing access to resources; we call this Web Agent Throttling . We start by formalizing Throttling Gates as challenges issued to an agent that are asymmetric, scalable, robust, and compatible with any agent. Focusing on a common component—the language model—we require the agent to solve reasoning puzzles, thereby incurring excessive token-generation costs. However, we find that using existing puzzles, e.g., coding or math, as throttling gates fails to satisfy our properties. To address this, we introduce rebus-based Reasoning Gates , synthetic text puzzles that require multi–hop reasoning over world knowledge (thereby throttling an agent’s model). We design a scalable generation and verification protocol for such reasoning gates. Our framework achieves computational asymmetry, i.e., the response-generation cost is 9.2 × {\times} higher than the generation cost for SOTA models. We further deploy reasoning gates on a custom website and Model Context Protocol (MCP) servers and evaluate with real-world web agents. Finally, we discuss the limitations and environmental impact of real-world deployment of our framework.

3
Web Agent Throttling Problem

As discussed in Section
2
, authentication and attestation alone are not sufficient to prevent DoS attacks or large-scale unauthorized web scraping. Therefore, we advocate for an additional mechanism that can operate alongside these existing techniques, cascading current access-rate control to ensure resilience against malicious or excessive agent activity. We call this mechanism
web agent throttling
, whose primary goal is to provide content providers with mechanisms that allow them to control the rate of automated access requests, thereby imposing cost or delay on high-volume activity while preserving access for legitimate users and benign automation. While some simple techniques such as fixed time delays can be considered throttling approaches, these methods are not suitable in practice, as discussed in Section
2
. Instead, we introduce
throttling gates
—points at which agents must solve one or more puzzles before being granted access. In the following subsections, we first define throttling gates and then identify the key properties that an effective mechanism must satisfy.

3.1
Problem Definition

We consider an agent
\mathcal{A}
requesting resource
\rho
from a service provider
\mathcal{S}
. To regulate access,
\mathcal{S}
places
\mathcal{A}
at a
throttling gate
. A gate presents one or more puzzles that
\mathcal{A}
must solve before proceeding. A gate may consist of a single puzzle or a sequence of puzzles (i.e., an interactive session). For each puzzle
c
with solution
z
, the agent produces a response
r
. Access is granted if the response satisfies the correctness criteria defined by the provider—this may require exact equality (
r=z
) or partial correctness according to a specified threshold. These puzzles are generated by a puzzle generation algorithm
\mathcal{G}
, which produces a set of challenges
C_{b}
that can be served to agents. The design goal is to ensure that gates remain inexpensive for
\mathcal{S}
to generate and verify, while requiring significantly higher effort for
\mathcal{A}
to solve.

3.2
Threat Model

We assume an adversary who controls one or more automated AI agents equipped with at least an LM, giving them the minimum capability needed to attempt solving throttling puzzles. The adversary knows the overall design of the throttling gate, but does not have access to random seeds of the throttling mechanism and cannot directly modify or manipulate the gate construction process. Their primary goal is either to orchestrate a DoS attack against the target service provider by overwhelming it with large volumes of requests, or to conduct large-scale unauthorized scraping of the provider’s content.

3.3
Throttling Criteria

We now outline the essential properties that throttling challenges must satisfy to impose meaningful cost on automated agents while remaining efficient and accessible.

Computational Asymmetry (A).

An effective throttling gate should create a strong imbalance between the ease of challenge generation and verification versus the difficulty of solving it. This asymmetry ensures that content providers incur only negligible costs to issue and verify challenges, while automated agents are forced to expend substantially more resources to solve them, making large-scale abuse economically and computationally inefficient:

•

A1: Generation should be cheap.
The process of generating reasoning problems should be fast and efficient, imposing minimal computational cost on the service provider and should allow for large scale generation.

•

A2: Verification should be constant-time.
The correctness of submitted answers should be verifiable instantly, adding no noticeable verifier end latency during the interactions.

•

A3: Solution should be expensive.
Solving the problem should require effort and cost from the agent, making it more expensive to send large-scale requests.

Scalability (S).

Since malicious web agents operate at massive scale, an effective throttling mechanism must be able to handle billions of requests without creating bottlenecks or imposing prohibitive costs on providers. To this end, scalability requires:

•

S1: Billions of different problems.
The reasoning challenges should be easy to generate and highly diverse, enabling the service provider to issue unique problems per request and reduce the chance of memorization or reuse.

•

S2: Low operational costs.
The entire challenge pipeline—including generation, storage, selection, and serving—must remain lightweight and fast, ensuring that problem handling at inference time does not introduce overhead or bottlenecks.

Robustness to Adversaries (R).

A throttling mechanism is only effective if adversaries are forced to pay its intended computational cost. In practice, attackers will try either to bypass challenges cheaply or to exploit the defense itself. Thus, robustness requires that challenges cannot be trivially circumvented and do not create new vulnerabilities for the service provider:

•

R1: Resistance to cheap shortcuts.
Challenges should not be easily solvable using off-the-shelf models, simple web searches, or other inexpensive tools. They must also be designed so that outsourcing to low-paid human workers is impractical, due to the difficulty, diversity, and range of potential problems.

•

R2: Server-side attacks.
While challenges should impose significant cost on agents, they must not require complex server-side execution that opens additional vulnerabilities. For example, code execution or containerized verification can expose software exploits, while relying on LM-based judges risks prompt-injection attacks or overthinking attacks
[
53
]
, where adversaries deliberately trigger excessive computation on the server.

Compatibility (C).

Throttling challenges must consider the diversity of deployment scenarios and agent capabilities. Without compatibility, throttling mechanisms risk either excluding benign agents or unfairly penalizing low-resource agents built on smaller LMs. Therefore:

•

C1: Adjustable complexity.
The throttling gate should allow content providers to tune challenge difficulty, ensuring that benign agents can reliably pass while only high-volume adversarial activity incurs significant cost. This adaptability prevents weaker but legitimate agents from being excluded.

•

C2: Support for text-only, low-resource agents.
Challenges should be designed for agents that operate solely on textual inputs, since many low-capability agents do not support additional modalities or tools. Restricting puzzles to language-based formats avoids excluding such agents, reduces deployment complexity, and ensures that compatibility does not depend on extra software or multimodal capabilities.

The properties outlined above capture the necessary conditions for an effective throttling gate. They are not, however, sufficient on their own: additional design choices may be required to ensure security and practicality in deployment. Moreover, these properties are not binary. A given approach may exhibit partial robustness, scalability, or compatibility, but still fall short of achieving the level of asymmetry needed to meaningfully throttle automated agents. In the next section, we examine existing throttling strategies and show that they fail to satisfy even these necessary conditions, motivating the need for a new design that explicitly incorporates them.

Throttling Strategies

Before introducing our proposed reasoning-gate mechanism, it is important to understand why both naive countermeasures and existing challenge-based approaches fail to effectively throttle modern LM-powered web agents. We evaluate them against the four key properties outlined in Section
3.3
and illustrated in Table
1

4.1
Classic Gates

CAPTCHAs.
Although, CAPTCHAs can be broken as discussed in Section
2
, could be used as throttling gates to slow down the agent.
From a computational asymmetry (A) perspective, they are cheap to generate (A1) and easy to verify (A2), while also taking time to solve (A3).
Furthermore, CAPTCHAs can be easily generated in large numbers (S1) with relatively low operational cost (S2)
[
91
]
. However, their diversity is limited to a fixed set of puzzle types, which are vulnerable to memorization (R1), including training models on existing CAPTCHA, using human farms and other tools
[
99
,
27
,
111
,
70
]
. CAPTCHAs do not create new attack surfaces for the server (R2) as verification is easy. Finally, while CAPTCHAs allow some tuning of difficulty (C1), their dependence on non-textual modalities undermines accessibility for text-only agents, e.g. using API or MCP tools (C2). Overall, while easy to deploy, CAPTCHAs are not robust to bypasses and not compatible with text-only agents.

Proof-of-Work (PoW).
PoW puzzles were designed to impose cost on clients
[
6
,
9
]
, but they misalign with the needs of throttling web agents. These challenges are cheap to generate (A1) and trivial to verify (A2), and expensive to solve (A3)
[
12
]
. With respect to scalability (S), PoW challenges, like hash inversion, can be easily generated and distributed (S1) and efficiently deployed (S2).
However, they do not guarantee uniformly expensive solutions, as adversaries with access to GPUs or botnets can solve them cheaply at scale (R1)
[
2
,
36
,
6
]
. Deployment of PoW puzzles does not introduce immediately new attack vectors to compromise the server (R2).
Finally, PoW can be tuned to match heterogeneous agent capabilities (C1), but are not suitable for agents that cannot run specialized PoW software (C2)
[
39
]
.

4.2
Towards Reasoning Gates

As discussed in Section
4.1
, existing throttling approaches fail to achieve the desired computational imbalance: they are either too easy for modern agents to bypass using increasingly powerful language models, or they rely on VLMs and thus assume agents can process images, which excludes many text-only agents. To impose meaningful overhead, we need puzzles that are inherently more challenging and cannot be solved through simple tricks or shortcuts. Since the targeted agents themselves rely on LMs, the natural direction is to design puzzles that force them into costly reasoning. Multi-step inference and symbolic composition require generating many additional tokens, which directly increases both the latency and financial cost of solving each challenge.

We propose
reasoning gates
as a promising solution. Reasoning gates are throttling gates instantiated with reasoning-based puzzles that demand multi-hop inference and symbolic composition, tasks that remain challenging even for state-of-the-art models. By leveraging the costliness of reasoning token generation, reasoning gates address the shortcomings of existing throttling mechanisms and naturally create the desired computational asymmetry. The remaining question, then, is how to identify and construct suitable reasoning problem sets for use within these gates, which we discuss next in Section
4.3
.

4.3
Existing Reasoning Problem Sets

To develop effective throttling mechanisms for LM-based web agents, we assess whether existing challenge formats satisfy the key properties outlined in Section
3.3
. While logic, coding, and scientific benchmark test reasoning, they often fail to resist solver shortcuts, scale dynamically, or support real-world deployment. This section analyzes these limitations against existing benchmarks and motivates the need for a new scalable and robust challenge generation framework:

Solver-based Problems.
Solver-based tasks, such as logic puzzles and symbolic reasoning
[
73
,
30
]
can be used as challenges to the agent. However, generating these problems is cheap (A1), e.g., requires only sampling ground truth parameters, and verification of correctness is straightforward (A2) while solution is expensive (A3)
[
57
]
. For scalability (S), some diversity can be achieved through logic templates (S1), and operational overhead to generate and verify these puzzles by the server is low (S2). However, solver-based problems are fragile: adversaries can “cut corners” by integrating off-the-shelf solvers (R1), though these puzzles do not introduce significant new server-side attack surfaces (R2). Finally, such problems can be tuned in difficulty by adjusting logical depth (C1), and they are compatible with text-only and low-resource agents (C2).

Coding-based Problems.
Programming challenges introduce diversity and open-endedness, but they exhibit several limitations when evaluated against throttling properties. Generating tasks is expensive because it requires curating and mutating functional code snippets at scale (A1), and verification is costly since it demands sandboxed execution frameworks that guard against malicious behavior (A2)
[
5
]
. Solving coding problems will result in high token count, making solution expensive (A3).
With respect to scalability (S), coding problems can be created in many variations (S1), but reliably generating solvable, diverse tasks without overlap is difficult and verification overhead scales poorly (S2). On robustness (R), adversaries can exploit tool integration and code memorization
[
17
]
to shortcut solving (R1), while sandboxing itself introduces attack surfaces (R2) since verification frameworks must execute untrusted code. Finally, coding problems can be adjusted in difficulty (C1) by changing task complexity, and text-only and low-resource agents can still generate solution code (C2)
[
84
,
95
,
14
]
.

Advanced Sciences Problems.
Scientific and mathematical challenges
[
32
,
90
,
40
,
43
]
demonstrate strong reasoning requirements but remain impractical as throttling mechanisms. Generation is extremely costly because problems must be hand-curated by domain experts (A1), verification is non-trivial and often requires detailed grading rubrics (A2), and while solving is expensive for LMs (A3), the asymmetry is undermined by their static, limited pool. In addition, diversity is limited (S1) and operational costs are prohibitive as it requires contributions from experts across different fields (S2)
[
76
]
. In terms of robustness (R), they are more resistant to shortcuts (R1) since no simple off-the-shelf solver can easily bypass them, but their static nature makes them vulnerable to memorization and fine-tuning exploits which is an open research problem
[
48
,
106
,
104
,
100
]
, while verification complexity risks new attack surfaces for the server (R2). Finally, difficulty can be tuned by selecting between undergraduate- and Olympiad-level problems (C1), and could be made accessible for text-only and low-resource agents (C2).

4.4
rRG: Rebus-Based Reasoning Gates

As discussed before, conventional reasoning challenges (e.g., math and coding) suffer from these limitations because they are constraint-heavy and have a larger set of rules, making it difficult to satisfy the strict formal requirements. As a result, generation typically requires manual curation to ensure constraints aren’t violated, and the tasks are often less robust given the availability of classical solution methods. We find soft reasoning challenges do not suffer from the same disadvantages.

Soft Reasoning Challenges.

Instead of relying on scientific or mathematical challenges, recent benchmarks also assess a language model’s ability to infer implicit details from a described world or environment. These challenges are called soft-reasoning challenges because the problems may not directly state the rules or restrictions that govern the world
[
109
,
88
]
. Although they are scalable (LMs can synthesize fictional settings and leverage existing literature),prior work suggests that LM’s with varying capabilities do not perform differently on these reasoning tasks
[
4
]
, making reliable difficulty control challenging.

Rebus-based Challenges.
To achieve scalability comparable to soft-reasoning tasks while retaining the difficulty control of conventional reasoning, we use an idea from rebuses
[
37
]
, where the task is to guess a word where every letter is constructed through a visual puzzle, and design textual challenges—rebus-based reasoning gates (rRG). We pick a random word and for each letter we generate a question about a fact from a diverse set of domains, e.g., computer science, psychology etc. Since the clues are domain-based, the model’s performance should improve with increased reasoning
[
78
,
72
]
.
Individual clues require no manual validation and rely only on factual or conceptual knowledge, yet they can be composed into multi-hop reasoning challenges
[
96
]
, turning simple facts into a structured inference task. Because text-rebuses follow simple rules—assemble an English word from domain-specific clues—stronger reasoning models can better spot inconsistencies and, with backtracking
[
63
]
, iteratively correct errors, that could improve performance.

Since we aim to generate text-based multi-hop reasoning challenges with each hint rooted in factual clues LMs become a strong and feasible candidate as they are trained on vast amounts of information from different domains. Further, LMs have been shown to have strong capabilities to generate challenges with different reasoning requirements and complexities
[
74
]
. Table
2
shows examples of rRGs.

5
Reasoning Gates Framework

To make the framework feasible for the service provider
\mathcal{S}
, challenges issued by
\mathcal{S}
and generated via algorithm
\mathcal{G}
are produced offline, so that the latency is minimal during real-time interaction between
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{A}
. As illustrated in Figure
2
,
\mathcal{G}
builds the offline generated challenge bank
C_{b}
in two steps. Step A constructs a set of
high-quality
examples
C_{e}
to be used as in-context learning (ICL) examples for large-scale generation via many-shot learning
[
3
]
. These examples are created through interactions with multiple models across difficulty levels, yielding examples that capture the desired complexity for each level (higher difficulty implies greater required reasoning ability). Step B uses the
C_{e}
to generate the bank
C_{b}
, that
\mathcal{S}
will sample from during the online phase, populating
C_{b}
with challenges at varying difficulty levels using a large set of words and domains as input.

The generation can be formulated as:

\displaystyle\begin{split}C_{b}=\mathcal{G}([d_{1},...,d_{m}],[w_{1},...,w_{m}])\end{split}

where
[d_{1},...,d_{m}]
is the bank of knowledge domains or subject matters and
[w_{1},...,w_{m}]
.

\mathcal{S}
, as illustrated in Figure
1
, uses algorithm
\mathcal{T}
to throttle agents by sampling batch of challenge-solution pairs (
c,z
) from
C_{b}
for each round, until the rejection or access criteria is met.

The
\mathcal{S}-\mathcal{A}
interaction phase can be formulated as:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\text{Access Permission}=\mathcal{T}(C_{b},l,T_{num},T_{min})\end{split}

where
T_{num}
is the total number of challenges to be issued and
T_{min}
is the minimum number of challenges
\mathcal{A}
needs to solve to get access.

\mathcal{G}
(offline phase) and Algorithm

(online phase).

5.3
Evaluating the Desired Properties

This section explains how our framework satisfies the desired properties discussed in Section
3.3
.

Computational Asymmetry.
Gates are generated with smaller models (e.g., o3-mini or GPT-4o) rather than state-of-the-art models such as OpenAI o3 or Anthropic Claude 3.7, or with reasoning models under strict reasoning-token budgets (e.g., Gemini 2.5 with
\leq 100
tokens). We evaluate different models in Section
6
. Moreover, even when the same model is used as both generator and responder, generation consumes fewer tokens than solving. We hypothesize that drawing on randomly sampled words, domains, and patterns shrinks the generator’s search space, whereas responding requires multi-step reasoning over a larger space. This design satisfies
A1
and
A3
. Since each solution is a single string, we verify in constant time by precomputing solution hashes offline and checking equality online, satisfying
A2
.

Scalability.
The generation pipeline shown in Figure
2
allows for automated large-scale challenge generation in an offline phase, satisfying condition
S1
. The generation can be done using existing LM APIs for cheap and the challenges are stored as strings, making the storage and retrieval of the challenges simple and lightweight. The online challenge-response phase relies on retrieval of text-based challenges and string matching, avoiding bottlenecks in the online phase and satisfying property
S2
.

Robustness to Adversaries.

Because of the challenges’ complexity, longer target words, domain diversity, time limits, and symbolic-reasoning requirements, these tasks are difficult for gig workers or off-the-shelf solvers (see Table
3
). An adversary cannot reduce cost by intentionally answering incorrectly; wrong answers simply deny access, enforcing the cost of a successful bypass and satisfying
R1
. Since each solution is a single string, verification reduces to straightforward string matching, avoiding third-party verification frameworks that may introduce vulnerabilities, thus satisfying
R2
.

Compatibility.

The reasoning gates framework generates problems with different difficulty levels,
\mathcal{S}
can adjust the difficulty based on desired throttling goal. Our evaluation in Section
6.2
shows that the framework is successful in generating challenges of different difficulty levels, satisfying properties
C1
. Since the entire framework generates text-rebuses, hence providing textual challenges and expecting a word (string) as a response, the framework supports text-only web agents satisfying property
C2
.

5.4
Deployment of Reasoning Gates

Web Interface.
Since web agents are capable of navigating web services through user interfaces, one way of implementing Reasoning Gates is through directly prompting the agent through the interface, like CAPTCHA, to solve the challenge before accessing the resource. This makes the process simpler and doesn’t require the agent to support additional tools for resource access but can incur an additional delay in solving the challenge.

MCP, API-Only Servers, and Background Processes.

Reasoning Gate can be implemented to secure web-service API endpoints using MCP
[
7
]
. The service can present a challenge prior to content delivery and, upon success, issue a token that authorizes subsequent access for the remainder of the session. Alternatively, the challenge may be dispatched to the agent for background solving, similar to Cloudflare’s Turnstile
[
18
]
.

Cascading with Authentication and Attestation.

Reasoning Gates can be implemented alongside attestation services to improve user accessibility, especially for the users who do not have access to agents and are running legacy systems
[
10
,
19
]
. The user support can also be improved through integration of user authentication services such as Multi-Factor-Authentication (MFA)
[
71
]
, allowing them to bypass reasoning gates. Integrating reasoning gates with other authentication mechanisms can also reduce the number of challenges required to get access and also allows the service provider to trust the user, making the service access easier.

Benign Users and Web Agents.

We assume that a benign user or web agent can support at least one of the following capabilities. For legacy users without agents, authentication and attestation grant access without requiring them to solve agent-oriented challenges. For web agents, requests fall into two categories: (1) interface-based navigation, handled by the framework’s web-UI support, and (2) API access, handled via the framework’s MCP integration.

6
Experiments

To evaluate our rebus-based Reasoning Gates against the criteria in Section
3.3
, we begin by examining
asymmetry
, comparing the token and compute cost of challenge generation with that of response generation in a single interaction session. We then assess model performance and analyze
scalability
by measuring how model choice affects the complexity and quality of generated problems, followed by quantifying hallucination rates and the incidence of unsolvable items during generation. Next, we conduct a user study to test whether a
gig-worker shortcut
can bypass the system. Finally, we validate
difficulty control
by testing models with different reasoning capabilities and by comparing against other reasoning benchmarks.

6.1
Experimental Setup

Models.
We evaluate our framework using multiple reasoning (OpenAI o3 and o3-mini, DeepSeek R1, Claude 3.7 Sonnet) and non-reasoning models (OpenAI GPT-4o and Google Gemma 3-27B, Claude 3.5 and Gemini 2.0-Flash). We utilize o3-mini and Gemini 2.5-Flash, with a thinking budget of 500 tokens, as our primary challenge generator models.

Dataset.

We assess the performance of our framework’s challenge generation capabilities by comparing our generated challenges with MuSR
[
88
]
and FlawedFiction
[
4
]
benchmark, both designed to assess multi-step soft reasoning in narrative contexts.

We use two reasoning tasks from MuSR dataset:
Murder Mysteries
(250 samples) and
Object Placement
(250). Murder Mystery requires the LM to find the criminal from the list of suspects. These challenges assess the models capability to reason through social concepts and understand motives. Object Placement requires the LM to determine the position of a requested object, testing its spatial reasoning skills. FlawedFiction assesses models reasoning capabilities through story comprehension. This benchmark takes existing stories and rewrites them to introduce a single continuity error; each story sample contains exactly one error.

Evaluation Metrics.
We use accuracy on generated samples and existing benchmarks to assess the efficacy of the framework in generating challenges of different complexities.
For the cost metric we count number of generated output tokens. We use it to measure the cost of generated challenges and the cost incurred on the agent to respond to them.
We choose tokens over monetary cost as cost per millions token do not reflect the actual cost of generation of challenges. It is often shaped by service provider’s commercial priorities such as market acquisition strategies. Furthermore, using output tokens also makes it easier to compare open-source and closed-source models.

Comparison with Challenges in Existing Benchmarks.

In Table
5
, we compare Reasoning Gates (mixed difficulty challenges) with existing reasoning benchmarks on two properties: token consumption and the accuracy gap between reasoning and non-reasoning models. Reasoning Gate induces higher token usage than all other benchmarks for both reasoning models (up to 300 tokens) and non-reasoning models (up to 4.8k tokens), indicating that our challenges enable more effective throttling. We also observe that Reasoning Gates yield the largest gap in average accuracy between reasoning and non-reasoning models, whereas Murder Mystery and Object Placement datasets make reasoning models perform worse than non-reasoning models.

We hypothesize that the higher token consumption arises because, unlike reasoning challenges in other benchmarks, Reasoning Gates require the model to draw on multiple domains, whereas those challenges typically lie within a single domain. Because each clue can have many possible solutions, the language model must rely on context to identify the correct answer. As discussed earlier, we also hypothesize that the larger accuracy gap occurs because the final solution in Reasoning Gate must be an English word, making it easier for reasoning models to detect mistakes and backtrack.

6.3
Adaptive Adversaries.

In Table
6
, we study the behavior of an adaptive adversary that employs many-shot ICL and fine-tuning, two popular task-specific learning strategies, to improve performance on the gates and decrease cost. Across all models, many-shot ICL improves performance by 3–5%. Fine-tuning, by contrast, shows greater variance in success rates: for Llama and Gemma it performs worse than many-shot ICL, and for GPT-4o it even underperforms the baseline. We do not see any noticeable trend in number of tokens when comparing baseline output to fine-tuned models. We speculate that this occurs because fine-tuning may cause the adversary to overfit to characteristics of cached problems, yielding little improvement over the baseline. For larger models such as GPT-4o, this overfitting can potentially degrade generalization ability.

Discussion and Limitations

Availability of Resources for Deployment.

Although Reasoning Gates provide strong asymmetry, the operational cost is nontrivial for small-scale resource providers, concentrating viable deployment among larger organizations. As with reCAPTCHA
[
77
]
and Turnstile
[
18
]
, we anticipate that firms with sufficient resources (e.g., LM Providers) will generate and maintain challenge banks and expose them as a service.

Energy Consumption and Environmental Impact.

The resource and energy demands of LMs have already led to serious environmental concerns
[
29
]
. We acknowledge that our framework increases LM consumption on both the client and provider sides. However, by analogy to proof-of-useful-work
[
8
]
, one could design useful Reasoning Gates in which generation and response compute is repurposed for real-world computational tasks.

Limitations.
While major firms may have the resources to deploy the framework, current generation costs are higher than desired, reducing the incentive for large-scale deployment. We present preliminary results against adaptive attackers, but the impact of high-resource adversaries remains open. Finally, at scale we must study how difficulty settings affect latency and user experience across diverse users. We will address these challenges in future work.

8
Conclusion

The upcoming wave of LM-powered web agents will overload critical online
infrastructure, and traditional CAPTCHA-style defenses are no longer
sufficient. We formalized the web agent throttling problem, analyzed why
existing challenge problems fall short, and introduced the
Reasoning
Gates
framework—automatically generated rebus puzzles that create
compute-cost asymmetric problems to prevent DoS attacks while assessing an
agent’s capabilities. Our experiments demonstrate these gates can throttle
reasoning LMs including real web agents. We conclude by outlining the practical
opportunities and remaining challenges in deploying LM-generated throttling
mechanisms at scale.

Appendix A
Ethical Considerations

Our research studies “Reasoning Gates” for throttling LM web agents—lightweight, model-agnostic challenges that add user-side effort. The aim is to help operators reduce abusive automation while preserving access for legitimate users and tools. Stakeholders include end users (who may experience friction), service operators and content owners, agent developers/providers, and the broader public. Indirect stakeholders include accessibility and assistive-tech users, for whom we support verified-human bypasses, as well as researchers and policymakers who rely on transparent reporting. Evaluations ran on a custom website and MCP endpoints with real agents plus a small volunteer study; we did not probe or disrupt third-party services, and we logged only aggregate timing/accuracy without PII.

We recognize dual-use risk: gates could be misused to exclude benign automation or to unfairly block access of legacy users. To reduce harm, we use adjustable difficulty and pass criteria and cascade it with authentication and attestation to provide human-bypass options. For reproducibility without enabling misuse, we release specifications and evaluation code, alongside a precomputed challenge bank. We believe the benefits—clarifying trade-offs and guiding safer deployments—outweigh the potential harm that could be caused by the contributions in this paper.

Prover Prompt

You are a puzzle solving agent who will be given puzzles "gate" and you will solve the puzzle to the best of your abilities.
