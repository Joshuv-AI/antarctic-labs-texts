Embedded military medical support in high-risk police operations: Analysis of 242 operations
Authors: Simon-Pierre Corcostegui, Paul Coutillard, Julien Galant, Gabriel de la Bigne, Steven Lovi, Oscar Thabouillot, Clément Derkenne
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:The National Gendarmerie Intervention Group (GIGN) is the French National Gendarmerie’s tactical unit, operating in high-risk environments involving armed suspects, hostage situations, or counterterrorism. Since 1984, the GIGN has relied on an original medical support model integrating a military physician-nurse team within its tactical elements. The study aimed to describe this support within the specific context of a physician-led prehospital emergency medical system.METHODS:We conducted a retrospective, descriptive, observational study of all GIGN operations identified from a prospective registry and performed in the French homeland between July 12, 2018, and October 12, 2022. The primary outcome was the occurrence of at least one casualty, defined as receiving a medical evaluation during an operation. Operational characteristics, medical organization, casualty severity, and access times to medical resources were analyzed. Univariate analyses were performed to identify factors associated with casualty occurrence.RESULTS:Among 242 analyzed operations, at least one casualty occurred in 21% of missions (n=50), with a total of 90 casualties managed. Law enforcement missions were associated with a significantly higher risk of casualties compared with judicial missions (39% vs. 15%; OR: 3.64, 95% CI: 1.82–7.23). Armed or suspected armed adversaries were present in 79% of operations, with explosives linked to the highest risk. Median theoretical access times were 15 minutes (IQR, 9–22) to a physician-staffed prehospital emergency unit and 40 minutes (IQR, 22–63) to a trauma center, with 22% of operations located more than 1 hour away. Prehospital transfusion was potentially available in 66% of operations, mostly delivered within 60 minutes.CONCLUSIONS:GIGN operations are conducted in environments with substantial exposure to severe trauma. Its embedded support model enables early advanced medical care targeting preventable causes of death and plays a central role in operational planning, coordination with civilian emergency services, and casualty management. The National Gendarmerie Intervention Group (GIGN) is the French National Gendarmerie's (FNG) dedicated crisis response, tactical and counterterrorism unit. It is deployed in support of local gendarmerie units when human, technical, or tactical capacities are exceeded. The GIGN possesses highly specialized and limited capabilities, including sniper operations, explosive breaching, high-speed driving, airborne insertion, combat diving, unmanned aerial systems, canine teams, and crisis negotiation. The unit can be rapidly deployed throughout mainland France and overseas territories by air, helicopter, or ground transport. At all times, two alert sections are maintained: a first-response unit capable of deploying within 30 minutes (A1 detachment) and a second-response unit deployable within 2 hours (A2 detachment).

GIGN operations are conducted under either administrative police authority (A1) or judicial authority (A2). Law enforcement missions (A1) are initiated in response to acute, high-violence situations such as hostage-taking, barricaded suspects, or counterterrorism incidents. These operations are characterized by extreme time pressure, limited preparation, minimal reconnaissance, and uncertain psychological assessment of the suspect, often relying on negotiation under degraded conditions. During these missions, operators wear heavy NIJ Level IV ballistic personal protective equipment, including helmets, visors, and heavy body armor. In contrast, judicial operations (A2) follow prolonged investigative work, allowing for extensive planning based on intelligence collection, detailed profiling of suspects, and comprehensive threat assessment. These missions result in the arrest of high-risk criminal targets, including organized crime figures, narcotics traffickers, and violent offenders. In this context, operators work in civilian clothing to preserve discretion, wearing only light ballistic vests.

Since its creation in 1974, the GIGN has lost 21 operators, including four during operational missions. As a military unit, it has benefited from dedicated medical support since 1984. This support is provided by military emergency physicians and nurses from the French Military Health Service (FMHS). Each alert section is reinforced by a military physician-nurse team that deploys alongside operators during missions. These military medical personnel are selected through a competitive process and trained by the GIGN in tactical intervention techniques.1 They have also been deployed in overseas military operations and receive FMHS-specific training in combat casualty care and war-related trauma management.2 During GIGN operations, the medical team integrates into the civilian prehospital emergency care system while remaining embedded within the tactical unit. In 1986, the GIGN conducted its first medical management of a severely injured operator who sustained a gunshot wound to the cervical region. Despite the long-standing existence of this unique medical support model, no study has previously analyzed the organization and functioning of the GIGN’s integrated medical system. To our knowledge, the GIGN remains the only unit worldwide to systematically embed a physician-nurse medical team within its assault element.

The present study aimed to describe the organization, resources, and operational integration of the GIGN medical support system within the specific context of a military medical team embedded in a civilian physician-led prehospital emergency medical system. By analyzing operational missions conducted in the homeland, we sought to assess the frequency of medical involvement, characterize the occurrence and severity of casualties, and evaluate factors associated with injury during GIGN operations.

METHODS

Study Design and Setting

We performed a retrospective, descriptive, cross-sectional observational study to characterize the medical support provided during GIGN alert operations (A1 and A2 detachments) conducted on French national territory between July 12, 2018, and October 12, 2022. All operations were exhaustively identified using a prospective registry of standardized medical evaluation and intervention forms completed by the deployed medical teams. This study was reported according to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines. The completed STROBE checklist is provided as Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/TA/F442.

GIGN Medical Support

Each alert section is reinforced by an emergency physician-nurse team capable of managing life-threatening emergencies, including orotracheal intubation, thoracostomy, surgical cricothyrotomy, multiparameter monitoring, intravenous thrombolysis, vasoactive drug administration, procedural sedation analgesia, administration of antifibrinolytics, and lyophilized plasma3 transfusion. The team operates from an unmarked civilian vehicle equipped with audible and visual warning devices but without patient transport capability. Equipped with geolocation and communication systems, NIJ Level IV ballistic personal protective equipment, and weapons, these providers are the only medical personnel authorized to operate within hot zones during gendarmerie operations. Depending on the operational context, hot zones may extend from a few meters to several kilometers (fugitive pursuit operations).

For any individual requiring medical assessment, the physician is required to complete a standardized casualty care record. Following each mission, the physician-nurse team systematically completes a cross-checked medical evaluation and intervention report

French Prehospital Emergency Medical System

The French civilian prehospital emergency medical system is based on a physician-led model combining the Service d’Aide Médicale Urgente (SAMU)4 and fire brigade emergency services. Depending on the level of medical emergency assessed during initial call triage, the SAMU may dispatch basic life support ambulances staffed by firefighters, physician-staffed mobile emergency and resuscitation units (SMUR), or, in some regions, protocol-driven nurse-staffed response units. These civilian resources are authorized to operate only within secured areas during gendarmerie operations. Patients are categorized as absolute emergency (A.E., life-threatening condition requiring medicosurgical care within 1 h) or relative emergency (R.E., requiring care within 6 h). Medical dispatch subsequently triages the patient to an emergency department, a critical care unit, or, for patients with severe trauma, directly to the regional trauma center (TC).5,6

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

All GIGN operations conducted on national territory were included, as well as missions canceled after completion of the operational briefing. Operations lacking specification of mission type, missions conducted abroad or overseas, and training or simulation missions were excluded.

Data Collection and Variables

Medical evaluation and intervention forms, initially paper-based and later digitalized, were entered into a dedicated database using a standardized and exhaustive process. The primary outcome was the occurrence of at least one casualty during an operation. A casualty was defined as any individual—civilian or law enforcement officer—receiving a medical evaluation during a GIGN mission.

Collected variables included administrative data (geographic location and coordinates), operational characteristics (mission type, presence of weapons based on intelligence), medical organization (presence of civilian emergency services, reinforcements requested, availability of prehospital transfusion), casualty characteristics (number, severity, status), and postmission assessment of briefing accuracy compared with actual operational conditions.

This study has been approved by our local institutional review board under number 20230420.

Statistical Analysis

A descriptive analysis was performed. Qualitative variables are reported as counts and percentages, and quantitative variables as means with standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges, as appropriate. Univariate analyses compared operational and medical characteristics according to the occurrence of casualties. Fisher exact tests were used for qualitative variables, and Mann-Whitney U tests for quantitative variables. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs were calculated to assess associations. Predictive performance indicators were calculated for selected clinically relevant binary variables. A two-sided p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were conducted using Excel, R, and OSGeoLive softwares (version 4.4.2). Theoretical access times to medical resources were calculated using Google Maps under standardized conditions.

RESULTS

Study Population and Casualty Occurrence

A total of 249 medical intervention forms were extracted from the registry. Four forms were excluded due to the missing specification of mission type, and five corresponded to missions conducted abroad. Consequently, 242 GIGN operations were included in the analysis. At least one casualty occurred in 21% of operations (n=50), with a median of one casualty per operation (IQR, 1–7). Overall, 90 casualties were managed during GIGN operations between 2018 and 2022. Operational Context

Among the analyzed operations, 72.7% (n=176) were conducted under judicial authority, whereas 25.6% (n=62) were law enforcement missions. Law enforcement operations were associated with a significantly higher risk of casualty occurrence (OR: 3.64; 95% CI, 1.82–7.23). Among the six A.E. identified, four occurred during law enforcement missions involving barricaded suspects, and two occurred during judicial missions related to motor vehicle crashes during open-environment arrests. Presence and Type of Weapons

Targets were confirmed or suspected to be armed in 79% of operations (n=192). Weapon status was not documented in 6% of operations (n=15). The most frequently encountered weapons were handguns (44%; n=107), followed by rifles (25%; n=60), edged weapons (21%; n=50), grenades (9.5%; n=23), and explosives (5%; n=13). Confirmed or suspected weapon presence was reported in 90% of operations with casualties (n=45), compared with 74.6% of operations without casualties (n=147) (p<0.05). This was associated with an increased risk of casualty occurrence (OR: 3.27; 95% CI: 0.95–11.17). The proportion of operations resulting in casualties varied according to weapon type. Explosives were associated with the highest casualty rate (30.8%; 4 of 13), followed by grenades (26.1%; 6 of 23), rifles (25.0%; 15 of 60), and handguns (22.4%; 24 of 107). In contrast, edged weapons, although frequently encountered, were associated with a lower casualty rate (12.0%; 6 of 50).

DISCUSSION

Operational Context and Casualty Occurrence

Our findings highlight that GIGN operations are predominantly conducted in high-risk environments, characterized by frequent exposure to armed adversaries, with firearms present or suspected in nearly 80% of missions. This level of risk is consistent with the graduated deployment doctrine of the FNG, whereby tactical units are engaged only when the threat exceeds the capabilities of conventional forces. The GIGN’s operational doctrine, which emphasizes preservation of human life,7 often necessitates close-contact interventions, exposing operators to substantial physical risk. Consequently, the injury patterns and operational hazards encountered by GIGN operators are comparable to those described in military special operations forces.8 Firearms are responsible for a substantial proportion of law enforcement line-of-duty deaths (68%) in the United States,9 however, proportionally, the use of firearms against suspects is rare (approximately 0.4% of police operations), with a very low rate of gunshot injuries (around 0.1%).10 Similarly, the injury rate among SWAT operators during operations is ~3%, with most injuries being musculoskeletal in nature and resulting from trauma.11 In contrast, the 242 GIGN operations analyzed in this study resulted in 52 injured individuals and 19 fatalities, all A.E. involving civilian casualties. By comparison, nationwide data over the study period from the General Inspectorate of the FNG reported 111 casualties (62 injured and 49 fatalities) associated with gendarmerie firearm use, across 381 firearm-use incidents.12

Organization of Medical Support for the GIGN

These findings support the relevance of maintaining a dedicated medical capability tailored to the specific risk profile of GIGN operations. Internationally, tactical medical support models vary widely. In the United States, SWAT medical support often relies on prepositioned civilian ambulances11 or paramedics,13,14 and the role of physicians remains debated.15 Other European models include prepositioned ambulances (Finland),16,17 specialized paramedic tactical response units (Germany), or mixed paramedic–anesthesiologist teams (Denmark).18 In France, police intervention units (RAID and BRI-PP) are accompanied by a civilian emergency physician without a dedicated nurse.19 Recent evidence, however, suggests that physician-led prehospital teams provide added value in the management of severe trauma.20 Strategies such as direct police transport have demonstrated benefit primarily in dense urban environments21 and may not be applicable to the GIGN’s operational context: the FNG operates primarily in rural jurisdictions. Moreover, civilian emergency services are not authorized to operate within hot zones,22 and first-arriving gendarmes may lack the equipment necessary for casualty extraction.23 Consequently, the GIGN medical team is frequently the first, and sometimes only, medical resource capable of intervening during the “therapeutic vaccum,”22,24 the early and most lethal phase of injury. The presence of an embedded medical team alongside operators allows adherence to the FMHS combat casualty care doctrine:2 life-saving interventions within the first 10 minutes, followed by advanced medical care within 1 hour and access to definitive surgical management within 2 hours. As a result, French military personnel benefit from the same level of medical care whether deployed in the homeland or during overseas operations. Beyond doctrinal considerations, this medical support directly addresses the specific risks encountered by the GIGN, namely penetrating trauma and its associated preventable causes of death, including massive hemorrhage, airway obstruction, and tension pneumothorax.2,8,22 In a London cohort of severely injured trauma patients,25 deaths occurred within a median of 13 minutes for hemorrhage and 11 minutes for tension pneumothorax. Although operators are trained to perform limited tactical combat casualty care interventions,2,26 such as tourniquet application and chest sealing, the presence of an embedded medical team maximizes survival by enabling early management of hemorrhagic shock,22,27 including hemorrhage control, vasopressor support, tranexamic acid administration, and aggressive prevention of hypothermia. The deployed military emergency physician is also capable of performing a thoracostomy or surgical cricothyrotomy. More complex vascular hemostasis procedures, thoracotomy, or the management of junctional hemorrhages would require the presence of a military surgeon. At present, the potential role of a forward surgical team remains under discussion.

Link With Civilian Health Care Facilities

Early access to transfusion-based resuscitation represents a key component of a ground zero damage control strategy.27–29 In our cohort, transfusion of red blood cells provided by the SAMU was available within 30 minutes in only 51% of missions, compared with 69% reported in a recent French survey.30 This discrepancy reflects persistent deployment challenges related to strict national regulations31 and the rural operational environment. Prehospital red blood cells transfusion availability is lower in rural settings, with 73% availability in rural physician-staffed emergency units compared with 94% in urban units.30 The military identity of the GIGN medical team thus illustrates, within the national territory, the FMHS’s role in disseminating transfusion-based resuscitation as close as possible to the war-injured patient.32,33 The recent introduction in France of pharmaceutical-grade freeze-dried plasma34 is expected to combine FHMS expertise3,35 with regulatory compliance.31

The GIGN medical support system relies on a dual structure: organically integrated military medical teams and close coordination with territorially responsible civilian emergency services (SAMU, basic life support ambulances staffed by firefighters, SMUR).4 Civilian resources were already present on site in more than one-third of operations, reflecting anticipatory dispatch decisions by medical regulators based on initial intelligence. The presence of SMUR was strongly associated with casualty occurrence, underscoring the inherent violence risk of GIGN deployments and the necessity of early medical anticipation. Delays in evacuation are a crucial issue in the management of severe trauma, particularly in rural areas: they account for an independent factor in trauma preventable deaths in the French trauma networks.36–38 The mortality increases by 4% for every 10 minutes spent at the scene of the incident,39 mainly due to uncontrollable bleeding.36–38,40 Nearly 60% of severe trauma patients in France die in the prehospital setting.41 With a median road access time of 40 minutes to the nearest TC, and more than one-fifth of operations located beyond a 1-hour radius, the feasibility of rapid evacuation is limited. Added to the median arrival time of 15 minutes for the first SMUR, the theoretical time to access the TC increases to 55 minutes, not including the time spent providing medical care on site. It should be noted that the median time in France for SMUR care before arrival at the TC is 65 minutes for blunt trauma and 81 minutes for penetrating trauma.42

Beyond clinical expertise, embedding a medical team within tactical units enhances situational awareness and understanding of the operational environment.1,24 The justified request for helicopter-staffed medical prehospital teams reflects the anticipatory and planning role of the embedded physician, who integrates operational intelligence with regional trauma networks' knowledge to optimize evacuation pathways.43 Despite this, helicopter evacuation was infrequently requested, even when TC access times exceeded 1 hour. Given the demonstrated mortality benefit of helicopter transport in physician-led trauma systems,44 especially in mountainous areas, broader utilization may be warranted. In our study, medical risk assessment performed during predeployment briefings demonstrated high sensitivity and negative predictive value for identifying casualty-producing operations. Law enforcement missions were associated with a significantly higher risk of casualties than judicial operations. Although they accounted for only one-quarter of all missions, they generated nearly 40% of casualty-producing interventions. Similar trends have been reported in the United States, where although judicial warrants represent the majority of SWAT deployments (75%), barricaded suspects and hostage situations, numerically fewer (20%) account for a disproportionate share of armed confrontations (60%).45 These data collectively reflect a higher operational risk profile for time-critical, unplanned missions, consistent with our observations: casualty risk is higher during open-environment arrests and operations involving barricaded suspects, providing actionable insights for future operational planning. Finally, preparation for single-casualty scenarios also enhances readiness for rare but catastrophic multicasualty events.26

Limitations

Several limitations must be acknowledged. The retrospective nature of the registry and its availability only since 2018 limit temporal depth and statistical power. Exclusion of overseas territories may underestimate ballistic trauma exposure46 but avoids confounding related to markedly different trauma system structures.6 The absence of precise timelines between injury occurrence and medical intervention limits causal inference, particularly regarding damage control interventions. The constraints associated with the secrecy of judicial investigations precluded detailed reporting of injury patterns, their origin (self-inflicted or resulting from GIGN gunfire) and prehospital treatments provided by SAMU or GIGN medical teams. Although outcome data were not available, the organizational insights provided by this study address a critical gap in the literature on medical anticipation and early advanced care in high-threat law enforcement operations.

CONCLUSIONS

GIGN operations are conducted in high-risk environments exposing both operators and civilians to potentially severe trauma. In this national cohort of 242 operations performed between 2018 and 2022, more than one in five missions resulted in at least one casualty, with significantly higher risk during law enforcement missions. The GIGN’s original integrated medical support model enables early, advanced prehospital care targeting preventable causes of death. Embedded physician-nurse teams constitute a core component of operational planning, supporting command decision-making, anticipating evacuation pathways, and coordinating civilian and military medical resources. This model provides operational autonomy while preserving mission confidentiality. The strength of the physician-nurse dyad lies in its ability to simultaneously deliver clinical care, operational risk assessment, and medical planning, reinforcing its central role in the GIGN’s operational doctrine.
