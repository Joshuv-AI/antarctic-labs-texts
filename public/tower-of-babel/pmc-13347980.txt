Behçet’s disease presenting with intracardiac thrombus and pulmonary thromboembolism in a young Ethiopian male: a case report
Authors: Eskeatnaf Yosef Getachew, Hailemariam Shimelis Gebeyehu, Tadael Tilahun Gebeyehu, Biruk Demisse Ayalew, Nuradin Abdi Ali, Kaleb Assefa Berhane
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionBehçet’s disease (BD) is a rare, chronic, relapsing multisystem vasculitis with diverse manifestations, including mucocutaneous, ocular, neurological, and vascular involvement. Intracardiac thrombus is a rare manifestation of BD and can result in severe complications such as pulmonary thromboembolism (PTE).Case presentationA 26-year-old Ethiopian male with a history of recurrent oral and genital ulcers and a prior episode of deep venous thrombosis (DVT) presented with hemoptysis and exertional dyspnea of two days. Physical examination revealed tachycardia and tachypnea, multiple healed ulcerations on the scrotum and penile shaft. He had an antinuclear antibody (ANA) titer of 1:320. A right ventricular (RV) apical thrombus measuring 2.8 × 2.1 cm on echocardiography and bilateral segmental and sub-segmental pulmonary emboli on computed tomography (CT) pulmonary angiography. The diagnosis of BD was made based on the International Criteria for BD, with a total score of 5. The patient was treated with anticoagulation and immunosuppressive therapy, including heparin, warfarin, prednisolone, and cyclophosphamide, resulting in clinical improvement and resolution of symptoms.ConclusionThis case highlights a rare but serious cardiovascular presentation of BD in a region where it is seldom reported. It underscores the importance of clinical vigilance for BD in young patients with unexplained thrombotic events. Early diagnosis and appropriate immunosuppressive and anticoagulant therapy are essential for improving outcomes.

Introduction

Behçet’s disease (BD), also known as Behçet syndrome, is a rare relapsing–remitting multisystem inflammatory disorder marked by recurrent oral and genital ulcers, uveitis, and systemic vasculitis affecting vessels of all sizes [1, 2]. It may involve the gastrointestinal, neurological, and cardiovascular systems. The etiology remains unclear but likely involves genetic, environmental, infectious, and immunological factors [3].

Behçet’s disease typically affects young adults aged 20–40 and is less common in children. It tends to follow a more severe course in males and younger individuals [2]. Its prevalence varies globally, ranging from 0.1 to 10.3 per 100,000, with higher rates along the Old Silk Road. Turkey has the highest reported prevalence (80–370 per 100,000), with a male predominance, while most other regions show equal sex distribution [3, 4].

There is no definitive diagnostic test for BD; diagnosis is based on the International Criteria for Behçet’s disease (ICBD). This point-based system assigns 1 point each for oral aphthosis, skin manifestations, vascular lesions, and a positive pathergy test, and 2 points each for genital aphthosis and ocular lesions. A total score of 3 or more confirms the diagnosis [2].

Cardiac thrombosis in BD is rare, with fewer than 100 reported cases, often linked to pulmonary embolism (60%). Common symptoms include fever, hemoptysis, and dyspnea, with frequent involvement of the right heart chambers (78%) [5]. Although there are no specific guidelines for the appropriate treatment of cardiac involvement and thrombotic complications of BD, immunosuppressive therapy combined with anticoagulation remains the mainstay of treatment [6].

Here, we report a case of a 26-year-old BD patient with RV thrombosis and PTE who presented with hemoptysis and dyspnea and was managed with immunosuppressive therapy combined with anticoagulation.

Case presentation

A 26-year-old Ethiopian male presented with hemoptysis and shortness of breath on exertion of 2 days duration. Historically, the patient was diagnosed with DVT of the left leg 1 year prior to his current presentation after he presented with left leg swelling and pain. He was initially started on a twice-daily regimen of 15 mg rivaroxaban. After 21 days, the regimen was adjusted to a 20 mg, once daily dose. He also reported that he has been experiencing recurrent, painful, self-limiting oral lesion involving his tongue and buccal mucosa and genital ulcerations for the past 7 years. The patient has no history of joint pain, change in urine color or amount, malar rash, or dry eyes. There was no known family history of autoimmune disease, thrombotic disorders, or similar symptoms. The patient is employed, independent in activities of daily living, and reports no history of smoking, alcohol use, or substance abuse. He has been in a committed, monogamous relationship with his partner for the past 3 years, and she has no history of recurrent oral and genital ulcerations. Nevertheless, he and his partner has been treated for sexually transmitted infections multiple times.

At the time of presentation, he was tachycardic (126 beats/min, regular), tachypneic (24 breaths/min), hypotensive (blood pressure of 80/50) and his saturation was 82% without oxygen support. He had healed ulcerations on buccal mucosa, the scrotum and the penile shaft. Ophthalmologic and other physical examinations were unremarkable.

Laboratory investigations revealed normal complete blood count, renal, and liver function tests. His coagulation profile showed a mildly prolonged prothrombin time of 16.2 seconds and an international normalized ratio (INR) of 1.25. ANA titer was positive at 1:320, while Venereal Disease Research Laboratory and Human Immunodefi ciency Virustests were negative. Herpes simplex virus (HSV) testing was not conducted owing to financial limitations. Echocardiography revealed a 2.8 × 2.1 cm fibroinflammatory mass at the apex of the RV. Chest CT angiography confirmed acute bilateral pulmonary thromboembolism involving the lower descending segmental and sub-segmental arteries. The diagnosis of BD was made based on ICBD, with the patient scoring 5 points (2 points for oral ulcers, 2 points for genital ulcers, and 1 point for large vessel thrombosis).

In light of the diagnosis of BD with acute pulmonary thromboembolism (PTE), rivaroxaban was discontinued. The patient was started on intravenous fluids and therapeutic doses of heparin and a daily regimen of 5 mg warfarin. He was also initiated on 60 mg of prednisolone daily and 500 mg of cyclophosphamide twice monthly. Following treatment, the patient’s oxygen saturation stabilized without supplemental oxygen, and his hemoptysis resolved within a week. His INR reached the target limit, at which point heparin was discontinued, prednisolone was tapered to 10 mg daily, and the patient was discharged after 15 days with significant symptomatic improvement. Echocardiography done 3 months later reported complete lysis of thrombus and the patient is currently on monthly follow-up at our outpatient department (Table 1). He acknowledged that his earlier symptoms had been recurrent but unrecognized as part of a systemic condition. He is currently adherent to follow-up and medications and is optimistic about his recovery.

Discussion

Behçet’s disease was first described in 1937 by Hulusi Behçet in Istanbul, who reported three patients with recurrent oral and genital ulcerations, uveitis, and erythema nodosum. Over time, additional clinical features were recognized and added to the disease spectrum [2]. Despite its recognition elsewhere, BD remains poorly studied in sub-Saharan Africa, and its prevalence in this region is largely unknown.

In Ethiopia, only two cases have been reported to date: one describing a patient with pan-uveitis and oral and genital ulcers, while the other reported a patient with recurrent history of oral and genital ulcers, right eye blindness, chronic cerebral vein thrombosis, fatal gastrointestinal bleeding and aortic aneurysm with dissection [7, 8]. This case highlights a rare vascular and cardiac presentation of BD in a 26-year-old male. The diagnosis was made clinically based on the ICBD. Although the distribution of BD globally is associated with the prevalence of certain Human Leukocyte Antigen (HLA) particularly HLA-B51 [9] reports from East African, West African, and Afro-Caribbean populations suggest that many patients with typical Behçet’s manifestations are HLA-B51 negative [10, 11]. In our case, we were unable to perform HLA-B51 typing and full thrombophilia workup due to resource limitations. Most studies have described normal levels of the anticoagulant factors protein C, protein S and antithrombin in BD. A meta-analysis showed a significantly higher prevalence of antiphospholipid antibodies in patients with BD than in healthy controls, but this was not correlated with an increased risk of thrombosis [12].

The patient also had an elevated ANA titer but no clinical features of systemic lupus erythematosus or other connective tissue or autoimmune diseases, making these diagnoses unlikely. While not common, ANA titers have been found to be elevated in some patients with BD compared to healthy individuals, with a reported prevalence of 8.3% [13].

Cardiovascular involvement in BD, collectively referred to as "angio-Behçet", is potentially life-threatening, occurring in approximately 7–46% of patients, with mortality rates reaching up to 20% [14]. While DVT of the lower extremities is the most common vascular manifestation, thrombosis at other sites including the portal vein, pulmonary arteries, sagittal sinus, and intracardiac chambers has also been documented. PTE has been reported in 3–5% of patients with BD [15], while cardiac thrombus has been observed in fewer than 1.9% of cases [14]. The pathogenesis of vascular involvement is thought to involve endothelial dysfunction, a hypercoagulable state, and inflammation-driven thrombus formation [12]. In our case, transthoracic echocardiography revealed a 2.8 × 2.1 cm fibroinflammatory mass at the apex of the RV, and chest CT angiography confirmed acute bilateral PTE involving the lower segmental and sub-segmental arteries. Notably, the patient had also been diagnosed with and treated for left leg DVT one year prior to this presentation.

Intracardiac thrombosis and PTE commonly occur in younger patients without traditional cardiovascular risk factors. Studies indicate that BD-associated intracardiac thrombi predominantly affect the right heart and may cause severe complications such as superior vena cava syndrome and PTE [5]. RV thrombi are particularly concerning due to their potential to embolize and cause massive pulmonary embolism, significantly increasing morbidity and mortality [16]. In one study, 18.3% of BD patients with thrombosis also exhibited pulmonary vascular involvement [17].

Early recognition and prompt initiation of immunosuppressive with/without anticoagulant therapy are critical for improving outcomes and preventing complications in BD [8]. However, due to its diverse manifestations and lack of specific diagnostic tests, BD is often underdiagnosed or misdiagnosed [18]. In this case, the patient was managed symptomatically for his oral and genital ulcers and later for DVT, without addressing the underlying BD. This delay in diagnosis contributed to disease progression, culminating RV thrombus formation and PTE.

The pathogenesis of vascular involvement is thought to involve endothelial dysfunction, inflammation, and vasculitis-driven thrombus formation rather than a purely hypercoagulable state [12]. The occurrence of thrombosis despite prior rivaroxaban therapy likely reflects the underlying inflammatory vascular pathology of Behçet’s disease rather than true anticoagulation failure, as anticoagulants alone do not address the primary disease mechanism.

There is no standardized treatment protocol for cardiac thrombosis in BD. However, most experts recommend initiating aggressive immunosuppressive therapy typically high-dose corticosteroids combined with cyclophosphamide with or without anticoagulation [5]. The primary goal is thrombus resolution and prevention of recurrence. The use of anticoagulation remains controversial. While anticoagulation is a cornerstone in the management of thrombosis, in BD it poses a significant risk due to the potential for pulmonary arterial aneurysm rupture—one of the leading causes of mortality in these patients. As such, some experts advise against anticoagulation and recommend exclusive use of immunosuppressive agents for managing cardiac thrombi. Surgery may be required for massive or recurrent thrombi, or in cases of cardiac congestion [5, 14]. In our case, combined immunosuppressive and anticoagulant therapy resulted in complete thrombus resolution without adverse events, supporting the benefit of individualized, cautious use of both modalities.

Behçet’s disease has no known cure and carries significant morbidity and mortality. Poor prognosis is associated with male sex and younger age of onset, with common causes of death including ruptured pulmonary or peripheral aneurysms and involvement of the central nervous system or gastrointestinal tract. Annual mortality in BD range from 2 to 4%. However, over 60% of patients achieve remission after the early, active phase of the disease, with a gradual decline in flares and complications [18, 19].

Conclusion

This case highlights a rare but severe vascular complication of BD in a young Ethiopian male. High clinical suspicion remains key to improve recognition and outcomes of BD in underrepresented regions like sub-Saharan Africa.
