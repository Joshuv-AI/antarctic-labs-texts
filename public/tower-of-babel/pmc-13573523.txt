Could creatine supplementation combined with physical rehabilitation accelerate return to play in athletes with patellar tendinopathy?
Authors: Ángela Sánchez-Gómez, Marco Malaguti, Borja Sañudo, Álvaro López-Samanes, Alejandro F. San Juan, Raúl Domínguez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundSeveral dietary supplements have been proposed to positively influence the rehabilitation process following injury. However, to date, no studies have specifically examined the effects of scientifically supported ergogenic aids on recovery outcomes when combined with physical performance interventions. Therefore, the aim of this study was to analyze the ergogenic effects of creatine supplementation on neuromuscular performance, tendon thickness, pain, and body composition in athletes diagnosed with patellar tendinopathy (PT).MethodsUsing a triple-blind experimental design, 20 federated athletes (age: 33.7 ± 9.84 years) with PT were randomly assigned to either a creatine supplementation group (CR; n = 9) or a placebo group (PLA; n = 11). All participants followed a physical rehabilitation intervention that included daily eccentric training combined with stretching exercises, along with one session every 10 days of extracorporeal shock wave therapy (ESWT) and manual therapy targeting the tendon and surrounding muscle tissue. At the end of each eccentric training session, the participants ingested 3 capsules of creatine monohydrate – Creapure® (CR) or sucrose (PLA). At the beginning (PRE), after 4 weeks (MID), and after 8 weeks (POST), the pain level (Victorian Institute for Sports Assessment-Patella, VISA-P), tendon thickness (echography), body composition (bioelectrical impedance system), and neuromuscular performance using a countermovement jump test (CMJ) and a strength test (5-RM) of knee extension in the injured leg.ResultsA significant main effect of time was observed for VISA-P scores (p < 0.001; η2 p = 0.626), indicating a reduction in pain over time in both the creatine (CR) and placebo (PLA) groups. At POST, both groups showed significant improvements compared to PRE (CR: 78.0 points [69.2–86.8] vs. 60.6 points [53.4–67.7]; p = 0.002; PLA: 75.2 points [66.3–84.0] vs. 60.0 points [52.5–67.5]; p = 0.003). However, only the CR group showed a statistically significant improvement in the MID compared to PRE (MID: 70.2 points [60.0–80.5]; p = 0.027). A significant main effect of time was observed for both tendon thickness (p < 0.001; η2 p = 0.629) and the 5-RM leg extension test (p < 0.001; η2 p = 0.739), with both groups showing progressive improvements over time. Nevertheless, as trend toward a statistically significant time·supplementation interaction was observed (p = 0.099; η2p = 0.126), with an increase in performance only in CMJ in MID vs PRE (36.8 cm (33.8–39.7) vs 34.3 cm (30.8–37.7); p = 0.019) and POST vs MID (POST: 37.6 cm (33.5–41.8); p = 0.015).ConclusionsA physical rehabilitation program involving eccentric training, stretching, and ESWT is effective in reducing tendon thickness and increasing muscle strength. However. It seems that the addition of CR induces an earlier reduction of pain and a positive effect on CMJ performance. Therefore, CR could be considered as an ergogenic recovery aid in athletes with PT.

Introduction

The use of dietary supplements is a common nutritional strategy among athletes. In individual sports, the use of dietary supplements among athletes varies, with a prevalence as low as 46.9% in competitive fencers [1] to 100% in rowing [2], while in team sports, the prevalence of consumption is approximately 59.9% in handball [3] and 87.2% in soccer players [4]. Regardless of whether they compete in individual or team sports, no significant differences are observed between female and male athletes in dietary supplement use, with one of the main motivations being the enhancement of sports performance [1–4]. Nowadays, there exists scientific evidence suggesting that dietary supplement intake can be beneficial in the following contexts: (i) management of micronutrient deficiencies; (ii) supplying convenient forms of energy and macronutrients; (iii) offering direct performance benefits; and (iv) contributing to indirect benefits such as supporting intense training regimens [5].

Moreover [6], Tipton (2010) stated that nutrition might play an important role in the rehabilitation from sports injuries. Based on studies analyzing the effects of various dietary supplements during periods of immobilization, it has been suggested that creatine, leucine, and omega-3 fatty acids may help mitigate muscle loss; additionally, creatine may enhance hypertrophy during the rehabilitation process [6]. Moreover, it has been considered that macronutrient manipulation can play an important role in favoring the nitrogen balance during and after injuries that involve immobilization [7]. Recently, a systematic review analyzed the effect of different nutritional strategies in the rehabilitation of musculoskeletal injuries in athletes; however, only 5 of the 18 studies included were identified as randomized controlled trials (RCT) [8]. Specifically, 2 studies have analyzed the effect of different dietary supplements, leucine [9], and glucosamine sulfate [10], in athletes with anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction (ACLR), while another study has examined the effect of vitamin D in wheelchair indoor athletes with a spinal cord injury [11], and two additional studies have focused on athletes with tendinopathies, one of which analyzed the effect of creatine supplementation in swimmers with Achilles tendinopathy [12], and another one the effect of β-hydroxy-β-methylbutyrate (HMB) in athletes with patellar tendinopathy (PT) [13]. Further, the IOC has identified several supplements that may aid in training capacity, recovery, muscle soreness management, and injury recovery. These include creatine monohydrate, HMB, omega-3 fatty acids, vitamins C and D, collagen, and anti-inflammatory supplements such as curcumin and tart cherry [5]. However, the effects of these supplements on athletes remain poorly understood, and the potential benefits they may offer in enhancing athletic performance during injury recovery are still regarded as preliminary and tentative.

Today, player availability is defined as keeping players injury-free and ready to participate in competition [14]. Player ability influences the performance of a team directly [15], and non-ability represents an important economic impact for clubs. For example, an injury such as PT with an incidence of 0.18 per 1,000 h of exposure represents a cost of 9,976 euros for a professional European soccer club [16]. A recent meta-analysis has informed that the prevalence of PT affects 18.3% of the athletic population [17], with higher incidence in sports that involve jumping, acceleration/deceleration, and changes of direction, including team sports such as volleyball (24.8%), basketball (20.8%), or soccer (6.1%) [17]. Different from other sports injuries that include conservative treatment with or without surgery and an immobilization period (i.e. ACLR), the management of the rehabilitation of PT includes strengthening exercises of the quadriceps muscle [18]. Heavy, slow- resistance eccentric exercises have been demonstrated as the most effective treatment to reduce the symptoms related to PT in the long term [18,19]. In addition, a study reported that the inclusion of stretching of the knee flexor and extensor muscles presents more benefits than only an eccentric training program [20]. Moreover, extracorporeal shock wave therapy (ESWT) has an additive effect on the physical exercise treatment of tendon injuries [21].

A challenge for all sports clubs is to prevent injuries and facilitate the return to play for injured athletes. At the same time, certain dietary supplements have been demonstrated to enhance sports performance in healthy athletes [5,22,23], and now a new objective in the field of sports nutrition should be to analyze the potential ergogenic effects of dietary supplements when combined with physical rehabilitation in high-prevalence injuries (i.e. PT) that do not require an immobilization period. One of the selected dietary supplements for enhancing the effectiveness of physical rehabilitation is creatine supplementation (CR). CR increases the muscle creatine content, which could induce metabolic advantages such as faster resynthesis of phosphocreatine and buffer capacity [24]. In fact, a meta-analysis has reported a positive effect of CR on strength and power, as assessed by jumps and muscular endurance [25], and creates a faster recovery after exercise-induced muscle damage [26]. These effects of CR contrasted in the healthy athletic population led us to hypothesize that the implementation of a CR procedure along a physical rehabilitation program for athletes with PT could positively affect the symptomatology of PT, and to improve the recovery of strength and neuromuscular functional test related to return to play in athletes diagnosed with PT. Therefore, the aim of this study was to analyze the effect of CR supplementation on pain, tendon histological properties, body composition, muscle strength, and vertical jump capacity in athletes with PT undergoing a physical rehabilitation program that includes eccentric exercise, stretching, and ESWT.

Methods

Participants

For the recruitment of the participants for this study, a researcher contacted federated sport clubs (i.e. handball, volleyball, basketball, soccer, and athletics) from the city of Córdoba (Spain) between January 2021 and August 2023. After explaining the study procedures and detailing the inclusion and exclusion criteria, male athletes from these teams who had been diagnosed with PT were invited to contact the research team. All the volunteers were diagnosed with PT by a sports medicine physician, and the diagnosis was confirmed by a researcher, who graduated in physiotherapy, using the criteria established by Rio et al. [27]. In addition to the diagnosis of PT, participants fulfilled the following inclusion criteria: (i) age (>18 <49 years); (ii) to be federated in a sport modality that involves high repetitive impact in the knee; (iii) to train more than 3 sessions per week; (iv) not having undergone knee surgery; (v) no analgesic or platelet-rich plasma injections in the past year; (vi) no use of sports performance supplements in the previous year; and (vii) non-smoker status.

All procedures fulfilled the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and were approved by the university Ethics Committee (code: 50/2022). Additionally, this study was registered as a clinical trial (NCT06482619).

Study design

An a priori sample size calculation using G*Power (version 3.1.9.2; University of Düsseldorf; Düsseldorf, Germany), indicated that 7 participants per group were needed [13] with α of 0.05, and power of 0.8 to determine differences between two independent means. This study was designed as a triple-blind, randomized, experimental trial. All participants underwent an 8-week physical rehabilitation program for PT, which included eccentric training, stretching, ESWT, and manual therapy targeting the patellar tendon and surrounding muscle tissue. Assessments were conducted at three time points: baseline (PRE), midpoint (4 weeks, MID), and post-intervention (8 weeks, POST). The outcome measures included body composition, pain, tendon properties, and neuromuscular function. Throughout the intervention, participants were randomly assigned to receive either CR or placebo (PLA) in a blinded manner (Figure 1).

Summarize of the study design.

Randomization

A researcher (R.D.), who did not have any contact with the participants and the physiotherapist who performed the physical rehabilitation intervention, and the assessments (A.S.-G.), randomly divided the participants into the CR or PLA groups in a computerized random list with a 1:1 allocation using the software Research Randomizer (www.randomizer.org).

Blinding

To ensure that participants were blinded, the CR and PLA groups were provided in dark red capsules contained in a personal canister provided by an external researcher (A.F.S.). Therefore, neither the therapist nor the participants knew the content of the supplement. In addition, the researcher who assessed the different tests included the results in a data file which was provided to another researcher (B.S.) who performed the statistical treatment without knowing the assignment of each code.

Interventions

The supplements were provided in capsules of 1 g of red color not transparent (Guinama S.L.U., 0044634, La Pobla de Valbona, Spain), which were prepared with a Capsunorm 2000 semi-automatic manual capsule-filling machine (Miranda del Ebro, Spain). The capsules contained CR (creatine monohydrate, Creapure®, Crown Sport Nutrition, Arnedo, Spain) or PLA (sucrose). The participants ingested 3 capsules daily (3 g/day) immediately after completing the eccentric training session, as this timing and dosage are considered optimal and appropriate for long-term supplementation protocols [28]. In addition, to avoid possible ergogenic effects of caffeine, participants were restricted from ingesting any caffeine dietary sources (e.g. coffee, tea, coke drinks, mate, chocolate, and energy drinks) 24 h before each experimental session.

Physical rehabilitation intervention

The physical rehabilitation intervention lasted 8 weeks and included eccentric training, stretching, ESWT, and manual therapy on the tendon and muscle tissue. Eccentric training was performed using a single-leg squat on a 25% inclined board, designed to increase the load on the knee extensor mechanism [29]. The descent is carried out at low speed and greater than 60° to guarantee maximum traction of the tendon [30]. The participants performed 3 sets of 15 repetitions daily, with 2 min of rest between sets. To evaluate pain during execution, the visual analogue scale (VAS) was used, where the pain scale is represented on a 10 cm line at one end value of 0 (“no pain”) and at the opposite end of 10 (“the worst pain imaginable”) [31]. During the execution of the EE, the pain had to be between 4 and 6, indicating moderate-severe pain. Pain higher than 6 indicates the presence of very intense pain, and pain below 4 indicates mild-moderate pain. In these cases, the load was adjusted by removing or adding 5 kg of ballast [32]. In addition to the EE, the participants performed five minutes of daily quadriceps and hamstring stretching, as evidence supports enhanced benefits with this approach [20]. During the 8-week treatment period, the participants received 5 sessions of manual therapy and ESWT at the physiotherapy clinic (i.e. approximately every 10 days) to reduce tendon stiffness and relieve quadriceps muscle tension in both lower extremities. ESWT treatment was applied with a Storz Duolith SD1 (Storz Medical AG, Tägerwilen, Switzerland, R-SW 15 bar, 1,500 pulses, frequency 10 Hz; R-SW 2 bar, 2,000 pulses, frequency 15 Hz; V-ACTOR 2 bar, and 2,500 pulses, frequency 25 Hz [33].

Outcomes

In this study, parameters related to body composition, pain, tendon histological properties, and neuromuscular performance were assessed.

Body composition

Before starting each experimental assessment session, participants were measured in bare feet and underwear. Height (cm) was measured with a stadiometer (Seca 214, Hamburg, Germany), with the participant facing back, heels together, head in a vertical position, and looking straight ahead. Then, body weight and composition were assessed using a Tanita bioelectrical impedance system (MC-780 MA, Tanita Corporation, Japan). The following variables were collected: body weight (kg), body fat (kg and %), body muscle mass (kg and %), % body water, and body mass index (BMI) (kg/m−2) [34].

Pain

To assess pain, the VISA-P was administered [35]. It is the most widely used patient-reported outcome measure, and it includes symptom severity. The VISA-P is scored out of 100 points, with higher scores indicating better function. It is sensitive for detecting improvements following therapeutic interventions in patients with PT [36].

Tendon thickness

The progression and thickness of the tendons in the injured knee(s) were assessed using ultrasound imaging (GE VENUE 40, Portable Ultrasound, CA, USA), using a linear transducer (Philips L12-3 – Cleveland, OH). The participants were positioned in the supine position with the knee flexed at 30° using a roller placed under the knees, which helped reduce patellar “locking” against the femur that occurs in full extension [37]. Ultrasound gel was applied and evaluated by placing the probe longitudinally; in pathological tendons, it is the only way to determine the distance of the measurement point from the insertion of the tendon [38]. Thus, all subsequent measurements were consistently taken from the same anatomical reference point.

Neuromuscular function

Neuromuscular function assessment was preceded by a standardized warm-up protocol previously described in detail [13]. Then, participants performed a CMJ test that consisted of 3 maximal CMJs performed using the Optojump photocell system (Microgate, Bolzano, Italy). CMJ was initiated from a stationary standing position with body mass evenly distributed over both feet, followed by a descent to ~90° knee flexion, followed by an explosive concentric action to reach maximum height. During the flight phase, participants maintained full extension of the knees and feet, with their hands remaining on the hips throughout the entire movement [39].

After the CMJ test, muscular strength was assessed with a knee extension machine (Selection Leg Extension, Technogym, Cesena, Italy). A 5- repetition maximum test (5-RM) was performed unilaterally in the injured leg/s. Previously, participants executed a specific warm-up, which consisted of a set of 3 repetitions at 50%1RM, 2 repetitions at 60%1RM, and 1 repetition at 80%1RM of the load selected [40].

Statistical analysis

Data were presented as mean and 95% confidence interval (CI). A Shapiro–Wilk ´ s test was used to check that all the variables were adjusted to the normal distribution, while the equality of variances was checked by Levene's test. A t-test for independent groups was performed to confirm that there was no difference in the baseline values of all the variables used in this study and other descriptive parameters (i.e. training sessions weekly, years federated, age, height, number of eccentric and stretching sessions performed during the intervention, and ESWT sessions). To analyze potential differences in body composition, pain, tendon thickness, and neuromuscular function of the lower limbs, a repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA-RM) was conducted, with time (PRE vs. MID vs. POST) as the within-subject factor, supplementation (CR vs. PLA) as the between-subject factor, and their interaction (time × supplementation) was also evaluated. Additionally, the effect size (ES) was calculated using partial eta squared (η2p), considering small (<0.06), medium (from 0.06 to 0.14), and large (>0.14) ES [41]. In addition, a post-hoc Bonferroni test was used in the pairwise comparisons. P < 0.05 was fixed as a statistical significance results. All the statistical treatments were performed using Jamovi software (version 2.3.28).

Results

As shown in Figure 2, 40 male participants were initially enrolled in this study between January 2021 and November 2023. However, after a first meeting with a researcher (A.S.-G.) who solved all the doubts of the participants and certified that they fulfilled all the inclusion criteria, 14 subjects were excluded from the study. In fact, some participants declared to have recently consumed dietary supplements to improve performance (i.e. creatine, β-alanine, sodium bicarbonate...), to have undergone infiltrations of analgesic drugs, or they were not diagnosed with PT based on the criteria established by Rio et al. [27]. Therefore, 13 participants were randomly assigned to each experimental group. Two participants in the PLA group and 4 participants in the CR group did not complete the intervention due to motivational reasons: (i) they did not reach the minimum frequency in terms of adherence to the eccentric training sessions, or (ii) non-adherence to daily supplementation. Therefore, a total of 20 participants (9 in CR and 11 in PLA), and 28 knee injuries (12 in CR and 16 in PLA) were analyzed in this study.

Discussion

The aim of this study was to analyze the effect of CR on pain, tendon properties, body composition, muscle strength, and vertical jump performance in athletes with PT undergoing a physical rehabilitation program. Our findings provide novel insights into this emerging field, suggesting that CR supplementation may enhance certain rehabilitation outcomes, specifically pain reduction, tendon structural adaptations, and neuromuscular performance, when combined with a structured exercise-based intervention. The main findings of this study were: (i) The PLA group did not report any effect on vertical jump ability or body composition; however, an increase in muscle strength of the injured leg and a progressive reduction in tendon thickness over 8 weeks were observed, with pain decreasing after 8 weeks. (ii) Similarly, the CR group did not show any effect on body composition, and it had a similar effect to PLA in the evolution of tendon thickness and muscle strength of the injured leg. However, the addition of creatine to the physical rehabilitation program presents ergogenic recovery effects in the vertical jump ability and a faster pain reduction in athletes with PT. To our knowledge, this is the first study to assess and report improvements in athletes supplemented with CR during the recovery period of PT.

PT is characterized at the histological level by alterations of the tendon, which include an increased tendon thickness, water content, neoangiogenesis, the content of sulfated glycosaminoglycan, and an alteration in the orientation of the collagen fibers [42]. It has been hypothesized that eccentric exercise could influence the tendon properties in athletes with PT because this type of muscle contraction promotes the alignment of collagen fibers in the longitudinal sense of the forces applied in the tendon, generating more resistant fibers, increasing the activity of fibroblasts, and preventing adherence during the healing stage between the tendon and other adjacent tissues [43]. The results of the present study (i.e. large ES in the reduction of the tendon thickness in CR and PLA) confirm this positive effect of the physical rehabilitation program based on eccentric exercise on the tendon properties. These results are clinically relevant because echography techniques and magnetic resonance are the gold standard methods for clinical examination and evaluation in PT [44–46]. Moreover, these results are in agreement with those published in a systematic review that analyzed the influences of eccentric training on imaging outcomes of the patellar tendon, reporting moderate evidence in the reduction of tendon thickness [47]. It is possible that the characteristics of the physical rehabilitation program contribute to the improvement in the tendon properties, as the participants did not perform eccentric exercises at the same intensity. In this way, based on the recommendations of Pardo et al. [32], the external load was adjusted in every session depending on the pain perception (VAS). During physical rehabilitation programs, training load modulation can be needed to induce an enhancement in the tendon properties because the tendon is a viscoelastic tissue, and it is more strained with heavier contractions [48]. Therefore, it is possible that an eccentric contraction without any additional external load induces lower tendon strain than isometric or concentric contractions, and that a progressive increase in the external load may provide the optimal stimulus for tendon adaptation.

On the other side, it is surprising that, despite the widespread inclusion of eccentric exercises in physical rehabilitation programs for treating PT, only a few studies have investigated the effect of these interventions on knee-extension strength [49]. A second important finding of this study was that an eccentric training program in athletes with PT is effective to increase the muscular strength assessed by a 5-RM leg extension test in the injured leg. This result agrees with those from previous studies in sports people with PT using the same exercise protocol (i.e. single declined squat), which reported an increased strength in isokinetic leg extension after 12 weeks [50] or an increase in 5-RM bilateral leg extension test after 4 weeks [13]. Moreover, in a different study with a similar physical rehabilitation program, it was observed a statistically significant progressive increase in strength (i.e. bilateral knee extension 5-RM test), over 4 and 8 weeks [51].

Pain analyzed by the VISA-P scale is one of the instruments most frequently used for the evaluation of the clinical evolution in athletes with PT [52]. The reduced VISA-P score in the PLA is in agreement with the current scientific evidence. A systematic review has concluded that eccentric exercise is effective in reducing VISA-P scores in the long term (>6 weeks), but not in short-term interventions [18]. Nevertheless, the novel result of this study is that the addition of CR in a physical rehabilitation program is effective for reducing pain earlier, as demonstrated by the statistically significant progressive reduction in the VISA-P score starting at 4 weeks in the CR group.

Further, pain has been demonstrated to affect jumping biomechanics, independently by other factors [53]. Seeley et al. (2021) demonstrated, in healthy subjects, that the induction of knee pain by the injection of saline solution into the infrapatellar fat pad decreased several jump biomechanical parameters, such as the vertical ground reaction force, knee flexion angle, and internal knee extension moment. In a very similar protocol for inducing knee pain [54], Park et al. (2015) previously demonstrated that knee pain alters the activation of several muscles, including the gastrocnemius, vastus medialis, medial hamstrings, and gluteus medius, during landing and jumping at various intensities. In the present study, after 4 weeks of treatment (MID), the combination of CR supplementation and the rehabilitation protocol reduced pain and increased strength, resulting in improved CMJ performance. In contrast, the PLA group, which also followed the rehabilitation protocol, only experienced pain reduction after 8 weeks (POST), with no improvement in CMJ performance.

Although, it has not been reported any positive effect of CR in acute procedures (<1 week) on vertical jump ability [55], in agreement with our results, long-term studies have reported an enhancement in jump capability in athletes [56,57]. Analyzing the effects of CR in an acute strength training session, Izquierdo et al. [58] found that CR significantly increased the mean power along a set of 10 repetitions with a load of 70% 1-RM in the back squat with a reduced fatigue level (assessed as a reduction in the magnitude of the explosive power of the lower limbs). In another research, Furtado et al. [59] demonstrated a statistically significant increase in the number of repetitions along a resistance training session that consisted of 4 sets until failure with a load of 70% 1-RM in the bench press exercise. Similarly, Mills et al. [60] reported an improvement in the maximal number of repetitions when combining leg press and chest press exercises at 50% 1-RM. Regarding a session focused on plyometric contractions (10 sets of 10 block jumps, a specific jump for volleyball players), it was reported decreased to a lesser extent jump performance along the session [61]. The increased work capacity with a concomitant reduced fatigue levels along a strength training session after CR [58–61] could be explained by a quickly restauration along the rest periods of a strength sessions. This is because the restoration kinetics of phosphocreatine are nearly complete within the first 3 minutes of recovery [62], and the bioavailability of creatine at the intramuscular level could positively impact this energy replenishment [63]. Regarding physiological recovery after a strength training session, the analysis of the kinetics of creatin kinase (CK) is frequently used because it is a validated diagnostic tool for detecting muscle damage [64]. At this level, it has been checked that CR attenuated CK levels increase at the 24-h and 48-h post-exercise of a session that combined weightlifting (half squat) and plyometric (vertical jump) exercises [65].

Based on previous studies, it is possible that the participants of CR presented a higher energetic bioavailability (phosphocreatine) during the training sessions and reduced the muscle damage related to the eccentric sessions. In fact, CR led the participants of the intervention group to tolerate a higher training volume and an increased recovery rate [24], which induced a higher overreaching. Additionally, a possible attenuation in the CK post-exercise could mitigate the pain in the CR group. Moreover, CR can bind the heads of the phospholipid, inducing a stabilization of the membrane phospholipid bilayer, decreasing membrane fluidity and producing a more ordered state in the membrane [66], which reduces the progressive accumulation of neutrophils and acute inflammation induced by muscle damage [67]. This reduced pro-inflammatory response serves as a stimulus for decreasing PGE2 synthesis, which is associated with the sensitization of type III and IV pain afferent nerves, ultimately contributing to pain [68]. Therefore, an increased training load with a reduced pain induced by the eccentric training session might explain the faster effect of the intervention of reducing pain in CR, such as the selective increase of jumping ability in CR.

As expected, based on previous studies [13,50,51], eccentric training in both groups induced an increased muscular strength assessed in the 5-RM test. However, an increase in CMJ performance was observed only in the CR group. This is because an increase in maximal strength does not necessarily correspond to an improvement in CMJ performance [69] because the enhancement should be adapted to the technique of the jump [70]. In this way, an increase in the depth during the CMJ improves the height reached along the jump by increasing the time for developing joint torques [70]. It is plausible that the reduction of muscle damage and less pro-inflammatory response in the CR group led the participants of this group to increase the depth of the knee flexion owing to the reduced pain observed, and benefit from the increase in muscular strength experienced. On the other hand, the higher pain levels in the PLA group may have prevented participants from fully applying their strength improvements during the CMJ execution, as they likely avoided generating higher forces during take-off and greater vertical ground reaction forces during landing, which are typically associated with a higher CMJ performance [71].

Strength and limitations

The most important novelty of this study is to play a pioneering role in the study of CR as an ergogenic aid in athletes with PT. The results of this study confirm findings previously reported as a positive effect of rehabilitation programs that involve eccentric training, stretching, and ESWT to increase strength, and reduce tendon thickness and pain within 8 weeks. However, this study is the first to report the absence of significant changes in body composition along the rehabilitation process. Nevertheless, the most important findings of this study are the following: (i) CR, in addition to an increased muscular strength, includes an enhancement in CMJ since the fourth week; and (ii) the increase in CMJ was accompanied by a reduction in pain in the CR group.

All these results have practical implications for the treatment of PT. Specifically, the CMJ test appears to be a more sensitive indicator for assessing the effectiveness of a rehabilitation program in athletes with PT, providing valuable insights into an athlete's potential return to competition. This is especially true when improvements in CMJ performance are accompanied by a reduction in pain. In contrast, strength assessments may be less reliable, as increases in strength do not necessarily translate to improvements in sport-specific actions, such as vertical jump performance.

This study has several limitations that should be considered. Firstly, future studies should analyze the kinematics of the CMJ for detecting the differences in the pattern of the jump that is related to the increase in performance. Additionally, while strength was assessed using dynamic contraction, isokinetic strength testing is considered the gold standard. Although the results of this study suggest that creatine supplementation (CR) can contribute to a faster return to competition when combined with a rehabilitation program involving eccentric training, stretching, and ESWT, it is crucial to further investigate the recurrence rate and long-term effects of this intervention, both on pain and neuromuscular performance. Finally, it is important to consider that the risk factors and mechanisms leading to injury vary across different tendinopathies, and consequently, the treatment approaches differ as well. Therefore, the effects observed in PT should be replicated in other tendinopathies (e.g. Achilles tendinopathy) before extending the benefits of CR to other types of tendinopathies.

Conclusions

This is the first study that analyses the effects of a dietary supplement considered as an ergogenic aid based on scientific evidence [5] on neuromuscular performance, histological properties, pain, and body composition in competitive athletes diagnosed by PT. The results of this study are clinically relevant and contribute to expanding the understanding of the ergogenic properties of CR in the field of sports injury rehabilitation, especially for PT. The CR group experienced an accelerated reduction of pain and the enhancement of neuromuscular properties, as demonstrated by the CMJ performance, a highly reproducible test for assessing return-to-play readiness. Unlike in the PLA group, the improvement in muscle strength observed during the physical rehabilitation program was not related to an increase in the applied force in the vertical jump, but it might be due to a reduced effect on the reduction of pain that affects CMJ technique.
