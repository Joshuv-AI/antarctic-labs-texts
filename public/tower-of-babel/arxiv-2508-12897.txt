FuSaR: A Fuzzification-Based Method for LRM Safety-Reasoning Balance
Jianhao Chen, Mayi Xu, Xiaohu Li, Yongqi Li, Xiangyu Zhang, Jianjie Huang, Tieyun Qian

Abstract
Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) have demonstrated impressive performance across various tasks due to their powerful reasoning capabilities. However, their safety performance remains a significant concern. In this paper, we explore the reasons behind the vulnerability of LRMs. Based on this, we propose a novel method to improve the safety of LLMs without sacrificing their reasoning capability. Specifically, we exploit the competition between LRM’s reasoning ability and safety ability, and achieve jailbreak by improving LRM’s reasoning performance to reduce its safety performance. We then introduce an alignment strategy based on Fu zzification to balance Sa fety -R easoning ( FuSaR ), by detoxifying the harmful reasoning process, where both the dangerous entities and the dangerous procedures in the reasoning steps are hidden. FuSaR successfully mitigates safety risks while preserving core reasoning information. We validate this strategy through alignment experiments on several open-source LRMs using detoxified reasoning data. The results compared with existing baselines conclusively show that FuSaR is an efficient alignment strategy to simultaneously enhance both the reasoning capability and safety of LRMs. Warning: This paper contains some harmful text examples.

Introduction

The emergence of large language models (LLMs) has revolutionized many fields such as natural language processing and even complex problem solving
Yao et al. 2024
. On this basis, the recently emerged large reasoning models (LRMs) are more focused on processing tasks that require deep and structured reasoning
Xu et al. 2025
;
Deng et al. 2025
. LRMs, represented by the OpenAI-o1
Jaech et al. 2024
and DeepSeek-R1 series
Zhang et al. 2025
, have demonstrated their outstanding ability to generate long chains of reasoning to solve complex problems, making them increasingly important in applications such as code assistance and scientific discovery
Chen et al. 2024
;
Lightman et al. 2023
. Unlike LLMs, which directly respond to user queries, LRMs’ output consists of two parts: reasoning and response
Li et al. 2025
. LRMs generate structured chains of thought
Wei et al. 2022
by adopting a
<think>…</think>
reasoning part format before producing the final response. This allows them to follow a structured thought process and generate long reasoning traces with multiple intermediate steps when answering complex questions, even in the absence of advanced prompting strategies
Qu et al. 2025
.

Figure 1:
Overview of the Jailbreak Method of LRMs.

While structured reasoning processes improve LRMs’ capability, they also bring more safety risks
Arrieta et al. 2025
. When facing malicious questions, LRMs’ reasoning phase can easily produce unsafe behaviors, even though the final response part is safe
Zhou et al. 2025
. In addition, due to the powerful reasoning ability of LRMs, their dangerous reasoning chain often contains more details for guidance, which makes the safety risk of LRMs much higher than that of LLMs
Jiang et al. 2025
.

Previous studies on LLMs’ jailbreak
Zou et al. 2023a
;
Shen et al. 2024
;
Mehrotra et al. 2024
attribute the reasons for LLMs’ jailbreak to the target competition and generalization mismatch
Wei et al. 2023a
, where the target competition refers to the competition between the LLMs’ instruction-following target and the safety target. In light of this, we assume that the LRMs’ jailbreak can also be attributed to the specific target competition. We delve into the generation process of LRMs and speculate that there is a reasoning target that causes LRMs to compromise safety for competition, as shown in Figure
1
(a). To verify this conjecture, we collect 40 instructions and rewrite them to get more concrete entities. The reasoning outputs of LRMs for these two types of questions are shown in Figure
1
(b).

As can be seen from Figure
1
(b), the length of the reasoning chain of the rewritten questions, the number of conjunctions, and the number of entities all increase. This shows that more concrete questions will give the model’s preference to the reasoning target. Based on this, we further design a jailbreak attack method which rewrites the malicious question to be more concrete. By doing this, the reasoning target when LRMs respond is strengthened, and thus LRMs prioritize reasoning goals rather than safety requirements and finally output unsafe content, as shown in Figure
1
(c).

Through the above analysis, we can confirm that the reasoning process of LRMs will bring additional safety risks. The typical way to enhance safety is to fine-tune LRMs using safety alignment data. However, previous studies
Huang et al. 2025
;
Wang et al. 2025b
have revealed that simple secure alignment data will cause great damage to the model’s reasoning ability. Then, the next question worth studying is:
how to enhance safety without reducing the reasoning ability of LRMs?

To solve this problem, we design a detoxification strategy FuSaR for harmful reasoning to obtain high-quality alignment data. FuSaR is inspired by the anonymization approach in data desensitization
Murthy et al. 2019
, which achieves privacy protection through suppression, generalization, swapping, masking, and distortion. We also obfuscate harmful inferences, but instead of hiding structured fields such as names or addresses, we
abstract the dangerous details of the reasoning process
reduce the danger of the reasoning process while retaining the reasoning information, which helps to preserve the model’s reasoning ability after fine-tuning.

In summary, our contributions are as follows:

•

To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to investigate how to balance reasoning and safety performance in LRMs.

•

We propose a jailbreak method based on the competition between reasoning and safety in LRMs, and introduce FuSaR, a fuzzification-based detoxification strategy that enhances safety while preserving reasoning ability.

•

Experimental results on several commonly used open source LRMs show that our approach performs well in balancing reasoning ability and safety, which improves the safety of LRMs without harming their reasoning abilities.

2
Related Work

In this section, we will briefly introduce the progress of LRM’s risk and defense methods.

2.1
Safety Risk of LRMs

Many studies have pointed out that the safety performance of LRMs is still seriously insufficient
Wang et al. 2025a
. In the attack test of DeepSeek-R1 using the HarmBench dataset, the authors claim that the attack success rate(ASR) is as high as 100%
Kassianik and Karbasi 2025
. In the prompt injection attack test
Zhou et al. 2025
, the attack success rate of the reasoning model under direct attack is almost twice that of other models, and the experimental results show that the thinking process of the reasoning model is less safe than the final answer. In subsequent studies
Jiang et al. 2025
, the researchers evaluate 12 LRMs, and the results on the StrongReject and WildJailbreak datasets clearly show the current safety risks of LRMs.

2.2
Safety Alignment of LRMs

To ensure that LLM can serve society safely and reliably and in accordance with human values, safety alignment has become a crucial research area
Dong et al. 2024
. Its core goal is to make the behavior of LLM consistent with human intentions and expectations and to avoid generating harmful, false, or biased content
Uuk et al. 2024
. Instruction fine-tuning is a simple and effective method
Ouyang et al. 2022
that fine-tunes the model to better follow safety specifications by constructing a dataset containing high-quality instructions and safe outputs
Ethayarajh et al. 2022
;
Wei et al. 2023b
.

The output of LRMs contains more structured thought processes than LLMs, so the datasets previously used for LLMs safety fine-tuning cannot be applied to the alignment of LRMs
Cui and Zuo 2025
. SafeChain
Jiang et al. 2025
proposes the first dataset that can be used for LRMs for safety alignment training. The STAR-1
Wang et al. 2025b
dataset replaces the reasoning process of dangerous instructions with the analysis of reasons for rejecting the instruction. But this reasoning process ignores the analysis of the original malicious input itself, resulting in information loss, and the model cannot fully understand the complex logic and potential intentions within.
The safety-tax
Huang et al. 2025
work points out that the safety performance of the model and the reasoning performance are in a trade-off relationship. After safety alignment, the safety of the LRMs is improved, but the reasoning ability is also damaged. In this study, we propose a safety alignment method to improve the safety of LRMs without damaging their reasoning performance.

3
Method

In this section, we will introduce our jailbreak attack method and safety alignment strategy for LRMs. First, we will introduce the method of implementing the jailbreak attack through concrete rewriting, and then explain the detoxification process of harmful reasoning.

3.1
Jailbreak Attack Method in the Reasoning Phase

Given that concretization strengthens reasoning constraints, our proposed jailbreak method for LRMs (unlike traditional LLM jailbreaks) doesn’t need complex prompts. We simply rewrite malicious questions to be more concrete, which induces LRMs to produce harmful output. The concrete rewriting process is shown in Figure
1
(c).

(1) Intent Identification: Based on the original malicious question, LLM accurately determines its hidden malicious intent and understands the real needs behind the question, thereby providing a clear direction for subsequent rewriting.

(2) Association: Using the identified malicious intent, associate the intent with things that can achieve the goal. Through these associations, we can build a richer problem context, expand the context of the problem, and enhance the LRM’s tendency to generate more inferences.

(3) Rewriting: Rewriting the question based on the original question and the associated things. The goal of rewriting is to make the question more specific, involve more details, and look legal and harmless in form, so as to induce the model to generate output containing sensitive information.

We provide prompts for these concrete processes in the Appendix.

3.2
Detoxification of Harmful Reasoning

In this section, we will introduce our proposed alignment method FuSaR, which is based on fuzzing harmful content. The output of LRM consists of reasoning text
R
and response text
C
, which can be expressed as follows:

A(Q)=R+C,

(1)

where
A(Q)
represents the output of LRMs corresponding to a query
Q
.

Ideally, when facing malicious questions, the model should identify their risks and reject them directly. But in reality, this method is difficult to guarantee the reasoning performance of the model. Therefore, we chose a compromise: we allow the model to “think first, then reject”, that is, it can first reason about user queries and then reject them later. As shown in Figure
2
, in order to ensure the safety of the reasoning process, we adopt a fuzzy processing strategy (FuSaR) to convert harmful content in the reasoning process into safe and effective expressions, while trying to retain the logical structure and intention of the original reasoning. The reasoning after detoxification is composed of the following:

R^{{}^{\prime}}=\text{Fuzz}(R),

(2)

where
\text{Fuzz}(R)
represents the reasoning process after fuzzification.

Fuzzy Processing

The harmful reasoning output of LRM can be divided into two categories: procedural reasoning that provides specific and actionable details and logical reasoning that analyzes and demonstrates improper ideas. Examples are as shown in Figure
3
:

For
Procedural Reasoning
, we process it as follows:

•

Entity fuzzification.
Using LLM’s powerful few shot capability, we provide several replacement examples and let LLM first extract harmful entities from the input content based on the examples and replace them with higher-level concepts.

•

Numerical fuzzification.
Replace key parameters with textual expressions that do not contain specific information.

•

Operation chain truncation.
Simplify the operation process and reduce the exposure of sensitive operation details. Provide only the key thinking steps and the direct results of each step.

For
Logical Reasoning
, we process it as follows:

•

Entity fuzzification.
The content of harmful logical reasoning generally includes the bullied entity, using higher-level concepts to abstract the bullied object and does not reflect the specific object.

•

Concept deconstruction.
For misleading or inaccurate descriptions, correct or clarify concepts that deviate from factual accuracy and responsible representation.

For both types of reasoning, we will reorganize the language based on the replaced fuzzed-content to keep the overall language fluent and logical, while maintaining the original meaning of the text to the greatest extent possible while ensuring safety. Our principles are as follows:

•

Three Keeps: Keep the logical chain, Keep scientific accuracy, Keep semantic coherence.

•

Two Eliminations: Eliminate hazardous operating details, Eliminate offensive or objectionable expressions.

Through the fuzzification processing, the safety of the reasoning process can be significantly improved while retaining the reasoning information as much as possible, and the secure reasoning process
\text{Fuzz}(R)
is obtained. In this way, the process of “think first” is completed on the basis of safety. For “then reject”, we borrow from the work of STAR-1
Wang et al. 2025b
, first using the referee model to classify malicious inputs, and then generating reasons for refusing to answer the question based on its category, thereby obtaining the content of “Reject”. However, this method may mandatorily cause all queries to need to be classified first, resulting in a waste of resources, so we combine the reasoning part and the output part unique to LRMs, and we use the output part as “Reject”.

Finally, for the data of the response part, since the instructed LLM has good safety performance and gives reject reasons in its response, we input the malicious instructions into the instructed LLM(Qwen3-235B-A22B
Yang et al. 2025
) and use its response as the response part. When generating this part of the content, the prefix instruction of the safety prompt will be added to further improve the safety of the content.

Therefore, the complete data structure is as follows:

A(Q)_{\text{safe}}=R^{{}^{\prime}}+\text{Reject}(Q),

(3)

where
Reject(Q)
represents the secure response generated using an instructed LLM. We also provide prompts for fuzzification processes in the Appendix.

4

Experiment

4.1
Experimental Setups

Training

In order to construct a training set for the LRM jailbreak defense, we first apply the rewriting method proposed in this paper to transform the questions in the HarmfulQA
Bhardwaj and Poria 2023
dataset to obtain new questions. Then, we generate the corresponding benign reasoning paths based on this, and the Instruct model generates the corresponding benign replies. We also remove all possible malicious reasoning paths and malicious replies, and finally complete the construction of the training set, with a final number of 1025. In order to verify the effectiveness of the safety algorithm proposed in this paper, we fine-tune five LRMs using the STAR-1
Wang et al. 2025b
training strategy, and the training is conducted on 8 NVIDIA A800 (40G) GPUs. The key hyperparameters used are: learning rate 1
\times e^{-5}
, total batch size: 128, and maximum sequence length: 8192.

Safety Evaluation

To validate the safety performance of LRMs, we use the following representative datasets:
AdvBench

Chen et al. 2022
, a benchmark comprising 520 prompts for diverse harmful behaviors designed to elicit harmful model responses. We also rewrite the original harmful questions based on this dataset. The rewritten instructions can stimulate the model’s reasoning ability to counter its safety and achieve jailbreak.

Jiang
Jiang et al. 2025
has tested several safety assessment tools: RS-Match
Zou et al. 2023b
, OpenAIMod
Kivlichan et al. 2024
, HarmBenchEval
Mazeika et al. 2024
and Llama-Guard
Inan et al. 2023
. The results showed that Llama-Guard was superior in terms of accuracy, so we also chose Llama-Guard as a safety evaluation tool.

Reasoning Evaluation

To demonstrate that LRMs retain high reasoning capabilities, we test their ability to solve scientific problems using the challenge and easy subsets of the
ARC

Clark et al. 2018
. For the scientific questions in ARC, since it is a multiple-choice question dataset with only reference answers, we only calculate their accuracy.

Models for Evaluation

For the jailbreak attack in the reasoning phase of the reasoning-type large language model, we choose 6 DeepSeek series models
DeepSeek-AI et al. 2025
: DeepSeek, DeepSeek-R1, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-14B, and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-32B for experiments. In the safety alignment experiment, we select the latter 5 models for experiments.

Baselines

In order to verify the effect of FuSaR, we choose
SafeChain

Jiang et al. 2025
,
Safety Tax

Huang et al. 2025
and
STAR-1k

Wang et al. 2025b
as baselines.

5.2
Safety Performance on Safety-Finetuned LRM during Reasoning

We use the obtained safety alignment dataset to fine-tune several open-source DeepSeek-R1-Distilled series LRMs and conduct safety tests. Consistent with the experiments in the jailbreak attack, we also have tested the model’s ability to protect against original malicious problems and rewritten malicious problems.

Performance of FuSaR vs. Baselines

Table
2
summarizes the safety performance of LRMs fine-tuned with FuSaR and various baselines, evaluated on both the original Advbench and the challenging Advbench-rewrite datasets. Our method can demonstrate considerable effectiveness in reducing the ASR across evaluated models compared to their pre-fine-tuning performance (as shown in Table
1
). Compared with the pre-fine-tuning, FuSaR significantly improves the safety performance of the model, and ASR is greatly reduced.

In the DirectRefusal training data, the reasoning process and responses to harmful queries contain explicit rejection instructions, and their reasoning data has only one sentence: “I should not answer this question!”. As a result, the fine-tuned model exhibits very robust safety performance, demonstrating a near-complete refusal of harmful responses on test datasets (near 0.00% on Advbench across most models), and often giving no response. However, as their author mentioned, and we will verify below, this method will greatly reduce the reasoning ability of LRM, so it is normal that our method is not as safe as it.

As for STAR-1, its training data are carefully selected using two distinct filtering criteria and add mandatory rejection instructions (such as “I’m sorry, but I can’t assist with that request.”) in the response stage
Wang et al. 2025b
, resulting in the model fine-tuned with STAR-1 data often only outputting simple rejection instructions in the outputs. Therefore, from the results, similar to DirectRefusal, the ASRs of each model in the reasoning phase and response phase are close to 0%, especially in the response phase.

SafeChain
Jiang et al. 2025
is similar to our approach in that it does not include explicit rejection instructions in the dataset. Instead, it uses LLM to generate safe data based on harmful instructions in the public dataset. Therefore, FuSaR only needs to be compared with SafeChain for safety, rather than with STAR-1 and DirectRefusal, because it is unfair to compare safety with them.

From the experimental results, we can see that FuSaR has lower ASR than SafeChain in almost all models and is only slightly lower in Qwen-7B. For instance, on R1-Qwen-32B, FuSaR’s Reasoning ASR is 19.42% (Origin) and 37.69% (Rewrite) compared to SafeChain’s 44.23% (Origin) and 67.88% (Rewrite). For Response ASR on the same model, FuSaR achieves 0.19% (Origin) and 14.81% (Rewrite) versus SafeChain’s 7.88% (Origin) and 35.00% (Rewrite). These results demonstrate FuSaR’s superior safety performance.

5.3
Reasoning Performance on Safety-Finetuned LRM

A crucial aspect of safety alignment for LRMs is minimizing the degradation of their core capabilities, particularly complex reasoning
Zhao et al. 2025
. In order to verify the impact of safety fine-tuning on the model’s reasoning ability, we also tested the changes in the model’s reasoning ability after fine-tuning. In order to more comprehensively reflect the performance changes on reasoning problems of different difficulties, we conducted tests on ARC-Easy and ARC-Challenge, two scientific problem datasets of different difficulty levels. We considered the accuracy of the output results to measure the model’s reasoning ability. The results are shown in Table
3
.

Performance of FuSaR on Reasoning Benchmarks

As can be seen from Table
3
, the Base models without safety fine-tuning (e.g., R1-Qwen-32B Base has an accuracy > 96% on ARC-easy/challenge) show strong reasoning capabilities. After safety fine-tuning with our method, the reasoning capabilities of the models are effectively maintained. For example, after fine-tuning with our method, the R1-Qwen-32B model has an accuracy of >94% on ARC-easy/challenge. These results are even slightly improved or maintained at a similar level compared to the Base model, indicating that our method does not significantly sacrifice the reasoning capabilities of the model while enhancing safety.

Reasoning Performance of FuSaR vs. Baselines

Compared with existing baselines, our approach shows competitive or even better performance in reasoning ability. In particular, the difference is particularly significant compared with DirectRefusal. DirectRefusal’s performance on harmless problems that require reasoning is significantly reduced (e.g., after DirectRefusal fine-tuning, the accuracy of R1-Qwen-32B dropped from >95% to ˜60% on ARC-Easy/Challenge).

In contrast, STAR-1 and SafeChain methods show different performances in reasoning ability. Our approach can achieve comparable or even better reasoning performance than them in most cases. This further demonstrates the effectiveness of our fuzzification-based safety fine-tuning strategy in balancing safety and reasoning ability.

5.4
Ablation Study

To confirm the effectiveness of our FuSaR method, we conducte targeted ablation experiments. Specifically, we fine-tuned the LRMs using only the Response portion of our training data, excluding the Reasoning part. As shown in Table
4
and Table
5
, ablation results clearly show that fine-tuning with only the Response data was insufficient for the model to achieve the performance levels seen with FuSaR, thus demonstrating FuSaR’s effectiveness on developing LRMs’ safety and reasoning ability.

Ablation Study on Safety

Our ablation study evaluates the impact of our attack method on model performance in both Reasoning and Response phases, comparing it against original and rewritten instructions across models (Table
4
). The results consistently demonstrate that our attack method significantly increases the ASR, effectively breaks the balance between LRM reasoning and safety, and achieves jailbreak. Furthermore, the FuSaR-aligned model performs better than the
w/o-reasoning
ablation model in the face of malicious instruction attacks, and the
w/o-reasoning
model is better than the model without safety alignment, collectively highlighting the effectiveness and criticality of our rewriting strategy.

Ablation Study on Reasoning

In the ablation experiment results on reasoning ability (Table
5
), it is found that the accuracy of the

Conclusion

We investigated the safety vulnerabilities of LRMs, particularly their susceptibility to jailbreak attacks caused by the inherent conflict between reasoning and safety. Our empirical findings show that adversarial inputs, designed to stimulate LRM reasoning, significantly increase the ASR. In response, we developed FuSaR, an innovative detoxification strategy. FuSaR mitigates risks during the reasoning process by fuzzing harmful content while preserving essential reasoning logic. FuSaR is the first strategy proven to simultaneously enhance LRM security and reasoning capabilities. By fine-tuning FuSaR, we can boost LRM safety against malicious instructions without compromising core reasoning, effectively balancing these crucial aspects. Our work offers valuable insights into LRM safety and provides a roadmap for developing more secure and reliable reasoning models.
