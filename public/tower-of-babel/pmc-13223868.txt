Dietary Artemisia annua leaf powder modulates intestinal response and cecal microbiota of broiler chickens during peak Eimeria infection
Authors: Ishwari Gyawali, Milan Kumar Sharma, Seshidhar Gudidoddi, Doyun Goo, Daniel Junpyo Lee, Deependra Paneru, Younghoon Kim, Woo Kyun Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A study was conducted to evaluate the effects of dietary Artemisia annua leaf powder supplementation on growth performance, intestinal health, and cecal microbiota composition in broiler chickens challenged with mixed Eimeria species. A total of 390 one-day-old Cobb 500 chicks were allocated to 5 treatments with 6 replicates of 13 birds each: a non-challenged control (NC), a challenged control (CC), and three challenged groups supplemented with A. annua leaf powder at 0.15, 0.30, and 0.45 %. Birds in the challenged treatments were orally inoculated on day 14 with 10,000 E. maxima, 10,000 E. tenella, and 25,000 E. acervulina oocysts. Growth performance was evaluated from 0 to 6 dpi, and lesion scores, oocyst shedding, intestinal morphology, gene expression, and cecal microbiota were evaluated at 6 dpi. Eimeria challenge significantly reduced body weight gain and feed intake (P < 0.001) and increased feed conversion ratio from 0 to 6 dpi (P = 0.003). Supplementation with 0.15 % A. annua improved body weight gain and feed intake (P < 0.05), whereas higher inclusion levels showed no additional benefit. Intestinal permeability and lesion scores increased in challenged birds (P < 0.001), with a tendency for reduced cecal lesions at higher A. annua levels (linear, P = 0.06). Tight junction gene expression was largely unaffected; however, MUC-2 mRNA expression was significantly downregulated in challenged groups (P < 0.001) and increased linearly with increasing dietary Artemisia annua level (P = 0.02). Cytokine analysis showed reduced IL-1β expression in the 0.15 % A. annua group compared with CC (P = 0.002). Microbial analysis showed no significant differences in α-diversity, whereas β-diversity differed among treatments (PERMANOVA unweighted P = 0.001; weighted P = 0.004). A. annua was associated with increased Bacteroidota and reduced Pseudomonadota abundance, while being correlated with decreased Enterobacteriaceae and enrichment of fermentative taxa such as Lachnospiraceae, Rikenellaceae, Oscillospiraceae, and Bacteroidaceae. Dietary A. annua partially reduced Eimeria-induced intestinal damage and improved gut microbial balance.

Introduction

Poultry coccidiosis, caused by the protozoan parasites of the genus Eimeria, is a prevalent intestinal disease in broilers, resulting in estimated annual global economic losses of up to US$ 13 billion (Blake et al., 2020). This disease weakens the integrity of the intestines and increases intestinal lesions, causing inflammation and oxidative stress that lead to a reduction in feed intake, resulting in a significant decline in growth performance and even increased mortality in affected birds (Blake et al., 2020; Choi et al., 2023; Graham et al., 2023; Teng et al., 2020). Beyond reducing productivity, coccidiosis also makes chickens more vulnerable to other infections, particularly necrotic enteritis caused by Clostridium perfringens which further adds to the economic losses and health challenges faced by poultry producers (Goo et al., 2023; Mathis et al., 2025). Because poultry production is a global enterprise which plays an important role in meeting global demand for meat production, this disease continues to pose a persistent threat to both food security and animal welfare. The infection is commonly acquired through the ingestion of sporulated oocysts from contaminated litter or feed (Mesa-Pineda et al., 2021). Eimeria species like E. acervulina, E. maxima, and E. tenella infect chickens, causing subclinical infections in broilers, leading to reduced performance, feed efficiency, and affecting gut health (Choi et al., 2023; Graham et al., 2023; Mesa-Pineda et al., 2021; Teng et al., 2023). The life cycle of Eimeria involves both intracellular and extracellular stages within the host intestine, during which epithelial cells are destroyed, triggering inflammation and oxidative stress and disrupting the mucosal barrier (Chen et al., 2025; Graham et al., 2023; Teng et al., 2020; Tompkins et al., 2023).

Conventional control of coccidiosis relied on prophylactic or therapeutic use of chemical anticoccidials, such as ionophores and synthetic compounds (Noack et al., 2019). However, continuous drug use has led to the emergence of resistant Eimeria strains and concerns regarding drug residues in poultry products, raising significant food safety and consumer health issues (Cunha et al., 2020; Gao et al., 2024; Martins et al., 2022). Increased restrictions on antibiotic growth promoters and anticoccidial drugs in many countries have intensified the need for alternative control strategies that are safe, sustainable, and economically feasible.

Phytogenic feed additives, a broad range of plant-based products, have become a widely tested and popular natural method for managing coccidiosis in poultry, providing an effective alternative to traditional antibiotic-based strategies (Abdelli et al., 2021; El-Shall et al., 2022). They are valued for their antimicrobial, antioxidant, and immunomodulatory properties, as well as their natural, non-toxic, and residue-free characteristics, making them a promising alternative to antibiotics in animal production (Ayalew et al., 2022; Mandey and Sompie, 2021). Phytogenics not only help reduce pathogen colonization but also promote intestinal integrity and nutrient digestibility by stimulating enzyme secretion and modulating gut microbiota (Abd El-Hack et al., 2022; Oni and Oke, 2025). Their multi-target activity makes them particularly suitable for managing complex diseases such as coccidiosis, where both the parasite and host responses play critical roles in pathology.

Among these, Artemisia annua, also known as sweet wormwood, a perennial herb has received significant attention for its potential to mitigate coccidiosis due to its bioactive compounds, including artemisinin, essential oils, and flavonoids, which possess antimicrobial, antioxidant, and anti-inflammatory properties (Chebbac et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2015; Mohammed et al., 2022). Traditionally, Artemisia annua has been used in herbal medicine to treat malaria caused by Plasmodium and other parasitic diseases. Its effectiveness comes mainly from artemisinin, a compound with strong antiparasitic activity (Elfawal et al., 2012; Wang et al., 2015, 2019). This has led researchers to explore its potential against Eimeria species, which is an intracellular parasite belonging to the same family, Apicomplexa. A. annua has demonstrated promising effects in reducing coccidiosis in broiler chickens, with studies reporting reduced oocyst shedding, lower lesion scores, and improved feed conversion ratio (Coroian et al., 2022). The efficacy of the plant is primarily attributed to its bioactive compound artemisinin, which exhibits strong antiparasitic activity by altering oocyst wall formation and inducing oxidative stress in parasites (Elfawal et al., 2012; Maodaa et al., 2024; Sharma et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2015, 2019).While various administration methods have been studied, including leaf powder supplementation and essential oil administration, the optimal dosage for A. annua leaves in Eimeria-infected broilers remain to be fully established (Coroian et al., 2022; Pop et al., 2015a). Therefore, the aim of the current study was to evaluate the effect of different dosages of Artemisia annua leaf powder on broilers infected with Eimeria spp. By comparing growth performance parameters, lesion scores, and oocyst shedding across different dosages, the current study would provide evidence-based insight on the use of this natural feed additive as an alternative to conventional anticoccidials.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics statement

All experimental procedures were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of Georgia (Protocol No. A2023 12-012). The experiment was conducted at the Poultry Research Center, University of Georgia, Athens, GA.

Animals, housing, experimental design, and diets

A total of 390 one-day-old male Cobb 500 broiler chicks were obtained from a local hatchery (Cleveland, GA). Upon arrival, birds were individually weighed and randomly assigned to five treatments with six replicates per treatment and 13 birds per replicate in a completely randomized design. The treatments consisted of: (1) a non-challenged control (NC); (2) a challenged control (CC); and (3-5) challenged groups fed diets supplemented with Artemisia annua (AA) leaf powder at 0.15 % (CC + 0.15 % AA), 0.30 % (CC + 0.30 % AA), or 0.45 % (CC + 0.45 % AA). Birds were housed in wire-floored battery cages in an environmentally controlled room and had ad libitum access to feed and water throughout the experiment.

Experimental diets were provided from d 0 to 25. Starter and grower diets were fed from d 0 to 14 (Table 1) and from d 15 to 25 (Table 2), respectively. Diets were formulated to meet or exceed the nutrient requirements recommended in the Cobb 500 broiler management guide (Cobb-Vantress, 2022). On d 14, birds in all challenged groups (CC and AA-supplemented groups) were orally inoculated with a mixed Eimeria spp. inoculum containing 10,000 E. maxima, 10,000 E. tenella, and 25,000 E. acervulina sporulated oocysts per bird (Teng et al., 2020). Growth performance and sample collection

Body weight and feed intake were recorded on d 7, 14, 20 (6 dpi), and 25 (11 dpi). Body weight gain (BWG), feed intake (FI), and feed conversion ratio (FCR) were calculated for each cage. On d 20 (6 dpi), one bird per replicate cage was randomly selected and euthanized for sample collection (Shi et al., 2024). Samples of jejunum tissues and cecal contents were collected, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C until further analysis. Fresh fecal samples were collected from each cage on 6 dpi for determination of oocyst shedding.

Gut permeability and lesion score

Intestinal permeability was assessed using a fluorescein isothiocyanate-dextran (FITC-d) assay. On 5 dpi (d 19), one bird per replicate cage was orally gavaged with 1 mL FITC-d solution (2.2 mg/mL; molecular weight 4,000; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) prepared in PBS under dark conditions (Sharma et al., 2022). After 2 h, birds were euthanized, and blood samples were collected. Blood samples were centrifuged at 1,500 × g for 10 min using an Eppendorf 5430R centrifuge (Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany) to obtain serum. A standard curve was generated using serial dilutions of FITC-d solution prepared in PBS, and serum from non-gavaged birds was used as a blank to account for background fluorescence. Serum samples (100 μL) were transferred undiluted into 96-well black plates, and fluorescence was measured at 485 nm excitation and 525 nm emission using a SpectraMax M5 microplate reader (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA). FITC-d concentrations were determined using the standard curve.

Intestinal lesions were evaluated on 6 dpi (d 20). Lesion scores for E. acervulina, E. maxima, and E. tenella were assessed in the duodenum, mid-intestine, and ceca, respectively, using the 0-4 scoring system described by (Johnson and Reid, 1970).

Oocyst shedding

Fecal oocyst shedding was determined on 6 dpi following previously described procedures with minor modifications (Goo et al., 2023). Clean trays were placed under each cage one day prior to collection. Approximately 100 g of fresh excreta were collected from each cage and thoroughly homogenized. A 5 g subsample was mixed with 40 mL distilled water and allowed to stand overnight at room temperature to soften fecal material. The mixture was vortexed and diluted 10-fold with saturated NaCl solution. The suspension was loaded into a McMaster counting chamber (Jorgensen Laboratories, Loveland, CO, USA) to enumerate E. acervulina, E. maxima, and E. tenella oocysts. Oocyst counts were expressed as log10 oocysts per gram (OPG) of feces.

Intestinal morphology

On 6 dpi (d 20), approximately 2-cm segments from the mid-duodenum, mid-jejunum, and mid-ileum were collected from one bird per replicate. Samples were gently flushed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and fixed in 10 % neutral-buffered formalin (Tejeda and Kim, 2021). Tissues were processed using standard histological procedures, embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin. Villus height (VH) and crypt depth (CD) were measured using a BZ-X810 microscope (Keyence, Osaka, Japan). The villus height to crypt depth ratio (VH:CD) was subsequently calculated.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Jejunum samples stored at −80°C were used for RNA extraction. Tissues were homogenized using a bead beater (BioSpec Products, Bartlesville, OK) in QIAzol lysis reagent (Qiagen, Valencia, CA) with 1-mm silica beads. Total RNA was extracted according to the manufacturer’s protocol and quantified using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). RNA purity was evaluated based on the A260/A280 ratio. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from 1 μg of total RNA using a High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA) (Yadav et al., 2022).

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed using a QuantStudio 3 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA) with iTaq Universal SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA). Each 10 μL reaction contained 5 μL SYBR Green Supermix, 2 μL diluted cDNA, 0.25 μL each of forward and reverse primers, and 2.5 μL nuclease-free water. The thermal cycling conditions consisted of an initial denaturation at 95°C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 15 s and 60°C for 60 s. A melt curve analysis was performed to confirm amplification specificity. Gene expression levels were normalized to the housekeeping gene glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) and relative expression was calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCt method. Short-chain fatty acid analysis

Cecal contents were collected on 6 dpi (d 20) from the same birds used for intestinal sampling. Immediately after collection, samples were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C until analysis. Short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) concentrations were determined following previously described procedures with minor modifications (Lourenco et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2023). Briefly, approximately 1 g of cecal content was homogenized with 3 mL of distilled water for 1 min. The homogenate was centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min, and 1 mL of the resulting supernatant was mixed with 0.2 mL of 25 % (w/v) metaphosphoric acid to precipitate proteins and stabilize SCFAs. The mixture was stored at −20°C overnight, thawed, and centrifuged again at 10,000 × g for 10 min to remove residual particulate matter. Subsequently, 800 µL of the clarified supernatant was combined with 1,600 µL of ethyl acetate, vortex-mixed for 10 s, and allowed to stand for 5 min to permit phase separation. The upper organic phase containing SCFAs was transferred to screw-cap glass vials for gas chromatography analysis.

SCFA concentrations were analyzed using a gas chromatograph (GC-2010 Plus, Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan) equipped with an autosampler (AOC-20i, Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan) and a flame ionization detector (FID). Separation of SCFAs was achieved using a Zebron ZB-FFAP capillary column (30 m × 0.32 mm internal diameter × 0.25 µm film thickness; Phenomenex, Torrance, CA). The injection volume was 1 µL, and helium was used as carrier gas. The oven temperature was initially set at 110°C and gradually increased to 200°C over 6 min. The detector temperature was maintained at 350°C.

Individual SCFAs, including acetate, propionate, butyrate, isobutyrate, valerate, and isovalerate, were identified based on their retention times and quantified by comparing the peak areas with those obtained from external standard solutions of known concentrations. The concentrations of SCFAs were expressed as µmol per gram of cecal content.

Cecal microbiota analysis

Cecal contents were collected on 6 dpi (d 20) from the same birds used for intestinal sampling. Samples were immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C until further analysis. Approximately 100 mg of cecal content from each sample was used for microbial DNA extraction using the QIAamp DNA Stool Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The concentration and purity of the extracted DNA were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). Extracted DNA samples were then submitted to LC Sciences (Houston, TX) for 16S rRNA gene sequencing.

The V4 hypervariable region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified using the universal primers 515F and 806R. Sequencing data were processed using the DADA2 plugin within QIIME2, which performs quality filtering, denoising, and merging of paired-end reads to generate amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). Downstream analyses were conducted within the same QIIME2 environment, including calculation of alpha and beta diversity metrics and taxonomic profiling (Goo et al., 2025).

Taxonomic classification was performed using a Naïve Bayes classifier trained on the SILVA reference database (release 138), trimmed to the V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene. Alpha diversity was evaluated using Chao1 richness and Shannon diversity indices. Beta diversity was assessed using unweighted and weighted UniFrac distance metrics, and results were visualized using principal coordinate analysis (PCoA). Taxonomic composition of the cecal microbiota was summarized at the phylum and family levels and visualized using relative abundance bar plots.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using JMP Pro 16.0 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC). Data were tested for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test prior to analysis. The experimental unit was the replicate cage. Growth performance, intestinal morphology, gene expression, and SCFA concentrations were analyzed using one-way ANOVA, with dietary treatment as the fixed effect. When significant treatment effects were detected, means were separated using Tukey's honestly significant difference (HSD) test. Orthogonal polynomial contrasts were performed to evaluate the linear and quadratic effects of increasing AA supplementation among challenged treatments. Lesion scores and alpha diversity indices were analyzed using the nonparametric Kruskal-Wallis test, followed by Steel-Dwass multiple comparison tests. Differences in microbial beta diversity among treatments were assessed using permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA). Results are presented as means ± SEM, and statistical significance was declared at P < 0.05, whereas 0.05 ≤ P ≤ 0.10 was considered a statistical trend (Ko et al., 2025; Teng et al., 2021)

Discussion

The present study evaluated the potential of dietary Artemisia annua leaf powder supplementation to mitigate the effects of mixed Eimeria infection in broiler chickens by examining growth performance, intestinal integrity, immune responses, and cecal microbial communities. The results showed that Eimeria infection impaired growth performance and intestinal health, increasing gut permeability, and intestinal lesions in the present study. Dietary AA supplementation produced modest improvements in gut health indicators and microbial characteristics; however, these effects were not sufficient to fully restore growth performance under the conditions of the present study.

During the pre-challenge period (0 −14 d), increasing dietary AA inclusion resulted in a linear reduction in body weight gain and feed intake. Similar reductions in feed intake have been reported with certain herbal additives and plant-derived compounds, which may alter diet palatability or contain bioactive secondary metabolites such as terpenoids, flavonoids, and essential oils that can influence feeding behavior (El-Rayes et al., 2025). Although AA contains several biologically active compounds with antimicrobial and antioxidant properties, these compounds may also affect feed acceptance when included at higher levels (El-Rayes et al., 2025; Mamdouh et al., 2024; Michalczuk et al., 2024; Obianwuna et al., 2024). However, in the present study, FCR during the pre-challenge period was not significantly affected by increasing dosage of AA, suggesting that nutrient utilization efficiency was maintained despite the reduction in feed intake. Overall, these results are broadly consistent with (Hady and Zaki, 2012), who reported no significant effect of AA supplementation on BWG, FI, or FCR prior to Eimeria infection, indicating neither growth-promoting nor adverse effects of AA supplementation under normal, unchallenged conditions. However, the linear contrasts in the present study suggest a mild suppressive effect of higher AA doses on FI and BWG during the early pre-challenge period, which was not observed by Hady and Zaki (2012), and may be attributed to differences in AA inclusion levels and dietary formulation.

Following challenge, Eimeria significantly reduced growth performance, as evidenced by lower BWG and FI across all infected groups, consistent with infection-induced anorexia and impaired nutrient absorption (Choi et al., 2023; Freitas et al., 2023). Following infection, 0.15 % AA improved BWG and FI in a quadratic manner during 0–6 dpi, the period when disease severity was greatest. This suggests that a low AA dose can partially counteract the growth-depressive effects of coccidiosis by promoting feed intake and body weight recovery. Similar protective effects have been previously reported, where AA supplementation mitigated coccidial damage and improved performance (Allen et al., 1997; Hady and Zaki, 2012). In contrast, higher AA doses (0.30–0.45 %) did not further improve performance, likely due to reduced palatability (Sharma et al., 2024), noting that 0.5 % AA decreased feed intake and body weight in laying hens, possibly because of its aroma or bitter taste. Therefore, moderate inclusion levels may provide sufficient bioactive compounds (artemisinin, flavonoids) to support immune function and limit intestinal injury, whereas higher concentrations can suppress intake and impair growth (Pop et al., 2015b).

Eimeria infection increased gut permeability, as shown by elevated serum FITC-d at 5 dpi, and caused severe intestinal lesions by 6 dpi, leading to reduced growth performance in the present study. Although AA supplementation did not significantly restore gut permeability at this stage, increasing inclusion levels resulted in a linear reduction in cecal lesion scores and E. tenella oocyst shedding, with 0.45 % AA producing the lowest cecal damage and parasite output. Lesions in the duodenum and jejunum remained largely unaffected, likely reflecting the distinct infection kinetics and cecal preference of E. tenella, which develops later and more intensely than E. acervulina or E. maxima (Sun et al., 2024). The cecum’s slower digesta transit and anaerobic environment may also favor greater local retention of AA phytochemicals, enhancing their anti-E. tenella activity. Collectively, these findings indicate that while AA may not rapidly restore intestinal barrier integrity, it effectively limits parasite replication and tissue injury in the cecum, supporting partial recovery of gut function and performance during infection. Mechanistically, artemisinin, the principal bioactive compound in AA, has been reported to disrupt parasite development by impairing oocyst wall formation and sporulation, while simultaneously enhancing host defense through apoptosis of infected cells and suppression of NF-κB mediated inflammation in the ceca (Del Cacho et al., 2010; Fatemi et al., 2015; Jiao et al., 2018; Sharma et al., 2024). Although these mechanisms were not directly examined in the present study, they may partly explain the reduced lesion scores and oocyst shedding observed with higher AA inclusion.

Intestinal villi and microvilli expand the absorptive surface, while crypts support epithelial renewal (Castro et al., 2020). The villus height, crypt depth, and their ratio (VH:CD) are key indicators of intestinal health and nutrient absorption efficiency (Cui et al., 2024; Forte et al., 2018; Gyawali et al., 2022). In the current study, the quadratic improvement in crypt depth with 0.15 % AA supplementation, particularly in the jejunum, suggests that moderate AA doses may promote epithelial regeneration and mucosal recovery during the acute phase of infection. Although no significant differences were observed overall, but numerical increased villus height and reduced crypt depth in the present study aligns with the findings of (Cui et al., 2024) who reported greater jejunal villus height in broilers supplemented with AA. Differences in the form and bioavailability of artemisinin, such as purified artemisinin versus that released from AA leaves, may account for variations across studies.

The immune and inflammatory responses observed in the current study further support the mechanisms underlying epithelial regeneration, mucosal recovery, and improved intestinal barrier function. The strong upregulation of IL-1β in the challenged controls, which was effectively reduced by 0.15 % AA, reflects the anti-inflammatory potential of artemisinin through suppression of NF-κB–mediated cytokine cascades (Wang et al., 2017). The unchanged IFN-γ and IL-10 levels suggest a pathway-specific modulation, where AA selectively attenuates early IL-1β–driven inflammation without broad immunosuppression. Sampling at 6 dpi likely captured the acute inflammatory phase when IL-1β peaks, while IFN-γ and IL-10 responses may occur later or require different dosing (Laurent et al., 2001).

Mixed Eimeria infection also impaired epithelial barrier integrity, as shown by reduced expression of JAM-2 and MUC-2, while Occludin and ZO-1 remained unchanged. This pattern indicates an early disturbance in mucin production and cell–cell adhesion rather than complete tight junction breakdown. It has been reported minimal changes in Occludin, ZO-2, or JAM-2 at 6 dpi under E. maxima challenge (Teng et al., 2020). Likewise, (Sharma et al., 2024) found that MUC-2 was significantly downregulated under mixed Eimeria challenge, whereas ZO-2 expression remained stable, and AA supplementation did not restore MUC-2 levels. These consistent observations suggest that early infection primarily targets mucin and adhesion molecules, while tight junction genes are relatively resistant at this stage. Minor discrepancies among the studies may be attributed to variations in dose, bird strain, or sampling time. In the present study, AA supplementation did not significantly alter these early barrier markers, possibly due to limited duration or suboptimal dose. Future research should optimize treatment timing and examine a broader range of junctional proteins to better define AA’s role in maintaining epithelial integrity.

At 6 dpi, cecal short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) concentrations were not significantly affected by infection or AA supplementation in the present study. Acetate remained the predominant SCFA, followed by butyrate and propionate, which aligns with typical profiles reported in poultry ceca (Liu et al., 2021). The unchanged SCFA levels indicate that Eimeria infection at this stage did not markedly disrupt microbial fermentation, and AA exerted limited influence on fermentation end-products. This contrasts with studies showing that Eimeria can reduce saccharolytic SCFAs (acetate and butyrate) and shift metabolism toward proteolytic fermentation (Lin and Olukosi, 2021). The stability observed here suggests that the cecal microbiota maintained functional resilience despite infection, likely due to microbial compensation or moderate disease severity. The absence of an SCFA response to AA implies that its early effects occur mainly through modulation of microbial composition rather than direct enhancement of fermentation; prolonged feeding or higher doses may be necessary to promote SCFA production.

Consistent with this interpretation, alpha diversity (Chao1, Shannon indices) was not significantly influenced by Eimeria or AA at 6 dpi, suggesting minimal short-term effects on microbial richness and evenness. Similar outcomes were reported by (Su et al., 2024), while (Graham et al., 2023) found reduced alpha diversity indexes (Shannon diversity and ASV counts) in more severe infections, indicating that diversity changes depend on infection intensity and sampling time. Although overall diversity remained stable, beta-diversity analysis revealed that AA-supplemented groups differed in microbial community composition from the control, suggesting that AA influenced microbial community structure rather than total richness, likely through shifts in the abundance of specific taxa.

The cecal microbiota were dominated by Bacillota (Firmicutes), Bacteroidota, Pseudomonadota, Actinomycetota, and Cyanobacteriota, consistent with previous findings in (Gyawali et al., 2022; Goo et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025). Among these, Bacillota remained predominant, underscoring its essential role in fiber fermentation and SCFA production (Goo et al., 2024; Hung et al., 2022). Bacteroidota ranked second and slightly increased under Eimeria challenge, while AA supplementation, particularly at 0.30 % further elevated its abundance. This increase is considered beneficial, as members of Bacteroidota contribute to fiber degradation, acetate and propionate formation, nutrient digestion, and immune regulation (Han et al., 2023; Nireeksha et al., 2025; Yadav and Jha, 2019). These findings are in agreement with the reports that Artemisia-based diets enhance Bacteroidetes abundance in the gut (Cui et al., 2024; Hu et al., 2024).

Conversely, Pseudomonadota (Proteobacteria), which includes opportunistic pathogens such as Escherichia, Salmonella, and Yersinia (Rizzatti et al., 2017)increased markedly in the challenged control group, indicating infection-induced dysbiosis. Overrepresentation of Proteobacteria is often associated with inflammation and compromised intestinal barrier function (Hu et al., 2024; Lobionda et al., 2019; Rizzatti et al., 2017). Notably, AA supplementation decreased Proteobacteria abundance, reaching its lowest level at 0.45 %, even below that of the non-challenged control, suggesting antimicrobial and microbiota-stabilizing effects of Artemisia-derived phytochemicals.

At the family level, Eimeria infection suppressed beneficial fermentative taxa such as Ruminococcaceae and Lachnospiraceae, key anaerobes responsible for fermenting structural carbohydrates and producing SCFAs (de Oliveira Moraes et al., 2023), while promoting stress-associated families including Rikenellaceae and Barnesiellaceae. These changes align with the reports that coccidial infection reduces butyrate-producing bacteria (de Oliveira Moraes et al., 2023). AA supplementation, particularly at 0.45 %, partially reversed these alterations by enriching Lachnospiraceae, Oscillospiraceae, and Bacteroidaceae while reducing Enterobacteriaceae. Restoration of these fermentative, SCFA-producing taxa likely supports epithelial integrity and immune regulation, contributing to improved gut health.

Overall, Artemisia annua supplementation mitigated Eimeria-induced dysbiosis by suppressing opportunistic Proteobacteria and enriching beneficial fermentative bacteria. These compositional shifts suggest enhanced microbial stability and metabolic resilience, which, together with the reduction in cecal damage and oocyst shedding, may help maintain intestinal integrity and protect against secondary infections during coccidial challenge.

Conclusion

In conclusion, Eimeria challenge significantly impaired growth performance, intestinal morphology, gut barrier integrity, and immune response in broilers. Dietary A. annua supplementation showed dose dependent effects, where 0.15 % AA reduced intestinal inflammation reflected by lower IL-1β expression and improved crypt depth, while higher doses (0.30 and 0.45 % AA) linearly reduced cecal E. tenella oocyst shedding, tended to decrease cecal lesion scores, and upregulated MUC-2 expression. Despite no significant effects on gut permeability, villus height, or SCFA concentrations, AA altered cecal microbial community composition. These findings suggest that A. annua can partially mitigate the negative impact of coccidiosis in broilers in a dose-dependent manner, and further research is needed to optimize its dosage and formulation for practical poultry production.
