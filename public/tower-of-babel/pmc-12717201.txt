Wheel-Mounted Inertial Datasets
Authors: Dusan Nemec, Gal Versano, Vojtech Simak, Michal Gregor, Itai Savin, Juraj Kekelak, Itzik Klein
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
A wheel-mounted inertial sensor mitigates inertial drift more effectively than an inertial sensor mounted on the vehicle chassis. Although their usage is increasing, there is no publicly available dataset for wheel-mounted inertial sensors. To fill this gap, this work presents the wheel-mounted inertial (WMI) dataset. WMI was recorded using two platforms: an omni-directional robot equipped with 5 IMUs, and a passenger car equipped with 9 IMUs. Each platform features IMUs mounted on every wheel. In total 64.04 minutes of recordings for each IMU (490 minutes for all IMUs) were made with associated ground truth trajectory. This versatile dataset will help develop model-based and data-driven approaches with wheel mounted inertial sensors.

Background & Summary

This work presents the wheel-mounted inertial (WMI) datasets, made publicly available in the Figshare v7 repository1 and also in our GitHub repository WID Repository (https://github.com/ansfl/WID/tree/main). As well as providing large, and reliable inertial datasets for academic and industrial communities, WMID will enable the development, implementation, and evaluation of different navigation technologies, including data-driven approaches.

In this section we provide a background and motivation for this work, followed by a scientific formulation for wheel-mounted inertial sensing, and lastly a summary of this paper.

Background

The inertial measurement unit (IMU) is a critical sensor that measures motion and orientation. At its core, an IMU contains three accelerometers and three gyroscopes, each positioned along mutually orthogonal axes. This arrangement allows the accelerometers to measure specific force along each axis, while the gyroscopes measure angular velocity about each axis. These measurements are all taken with respect to an inertial frame of reference2,3. Recent advancements in Micro-Electro-Mechanical Systems (MEMS) technology have revolutionized IMU design. MEMS-based IMUs are distinguished by their compact size, low power consumption, and affordability. The significant advantage of these compact and cost-effective MEMS IMUs lies in their ability to be deployed in applications that were previously impractical or impossible. This has profoundly impacted fields ranging from consumer electronics to robotics and healthcare4.

One such application is in mobile robot navigation. The localization and navigation of a mobile robot is the process of estimating the robot’s position and determining its orientation. This setup is commonly addressed in the literature. For example, a 2019 paper published by Shen et al.5 studies integrated navigation systems, which include IMUs, for unmanned ground vehicles (UGVs). The test platform is a modified passenger car fitted with a navigation system which consists of a global navigation satellite system (GNSS) receiver, a MEMS-IMU, an odometer, and a light detection and ranging (LiDAR) unit. A real time kinematic GNSS/strapdown inertial navigation system (RTK-GNSS/SINS) provided the self-driving platform with a reference trajectory. Rudyk et al.6 focuses on the gyroscope as the most critical and sensitive components of SINSs in the positioning of mobile robots. Recently, a model-based mobile robot pure inertial navigation (MoRPI) approach7 and its neural network robust version8 demonstrated high-performance pure inertial navigation. To this end, they require the mobile robot to maneuver in a snake-like slithering motion to encourage nonlinear behaviour and increase the inertial signal to noise ratio.

Inertial sensors are not limited to being rigidly affixed on top of mobile robots or on their chasses. One of the key locations is mounting the inertial sensors on the wheels in a setup known as wheel-mounted IMU. While proposing a dead-reckoning system for wheeled robots, Niu et al.9 lists two major advantages of mounting an IMU to the center of a non-steering wheel. First, to replace the traditional odometer thereby mitigating the error drift of the INS. Second, with the rotation of the wheel, the constant bias error of the inertial sensor can be cancelled to some extent. In a subsequent paper by Niu et al.10, the research team proposes various configurations of wheel-mounted and body-mounted IMUs to improve the robustness of their dead-reckoning system for the wheeled robots. Mezentsev and Collin11 likewise points out that physical rotation of the sensor can be used to remove the constant part of the gyro errors and reduce low-frequency noise, noting that such rotation conveniently exists in the wheels of ground vehicles. Wu et al.12 also cites rotation modulation as a reason for researching a wheel-INS system. This paper focuses on a single, low-cost wheel-mounted IMU. Configurations of multiple low-cost IMUs have particular use in GNSS challenging environments, according to Moussa et al.13. In addition to addressing the use of an inertial navigation system (INS) during outages of GNSS signal, they also discuss the benefits of IMUs mounted on two wheels. The paper explains that a differential wheel odometry based on the IMUs mounted on the rear wheels is proposed to estimate the vehicle’s change of heading.

In parallel to the advances in wheel mounted INS, machine learning and deep learning algorithms have been used to enhance the accuracy and robustness of inertial sensor and related fusion algorithms Cohen and Klein14 and by Chen and Pan15. For example, when studying pedestrian navigation, Tlio is a deep neural network for regressing 3D position displacement and its uncertainty, while Vertzberger and Klein16 provides an attitude estimation for pedestrians. Similarly, LLIO is a lighter version of Tlio, aimed for real-time usage on low power end devices. In the field of drone navigation, VIO-DualProNet is an adaptive learning-based technique designed to estimate inertial process noise covariance of a drone and improve visual-inertial odometry fusion accordingly. Brossard et al.17 presents a deep learning framework aimed at denoising gyroscope readings for open-loop attitude estimation for drones. Buchanan et al.18 proposed a learning-based method for estimating IMU bias factors in pedestrian and quadruped robot visual-inertial factor graph problems. For mobile robots, DeepLIO is a learning-based method for LiDAR-inertial odomtery of ground robots. Brossard et al.19 used a deep neural network for enhancing wheel odometer and IMU fusion of wheeled robots. Learning-based (or data-driven) methods are aptly named as such models rely on datasets to learn patterns and characteristics in the data. Therefore, these datasets should cover diverse scenarios, capturing various environmental conditions, motion dynamics, and sensor configurations. A well-designed dataset helps researchers thoroughly train models and validate their performance, improve their methods, and enhance navigation performance. Thus, datasets are a critical part of any learning algorithm. There are inertial datasets in various domains, ranging from aerial to maritime platforms. Existing datasets which are publicly available include the RONIN dataset20, which contains more than 40 hours of IMU data from 100 human subjects, with ground-truth 3D trajectories under natural human motion. The RIDI21 dataset is a wide and versatile inertial dataset for pedestrian navigation. The authors of the OXIOD22 collected 158 sequences of handheld inertial data, covering a distance of over 42 km. The inertial ADVIO23 dataset contains 23 separate recordings captured in six different locations with a total length of 4.47 kilometers, and a total duration of 1 hour and 8 minutes. The Kitti24 dataset is a popular benchmark dataset in autonomous vehicles navigation, containing 6 hours of traffic scenarios with a sensor suite including inertial sensors, cameras, and GNSS. The IO-VNBD25 is a diverse inertial dataset collected using a car, with more than 58 hours and 4,400 traveled kilometers of data. A popular dataset for inertial navigation research on drones is the Euroc26 dataset, consisting of drone recordings in various motions and scenarios. The UZH-FPV Drone Racing dataset27 is a publicly available dataset with camera and inertial recordings. It is designed for studying visual-inertial odometry state estimation in high-speed aggressive drone racing scenarios. It consists of over 27 sequences, with more than 10 km of flight distance, captured on a first-person-view racing quadrotor flown by an expert pilot. The autonomous platforms inertial dataset28 includes data recordings from multiple platform types, containing more than 805 minutes of data from quadrotors, underwater vehicles, land vehicles, and boats. The multiple and gyro-free inertial datasets were recorded using a passenger car and a mobile robot. The recordings focused on multiple IMU (MIMU) configurations of varying size and contain 45 hours of inertial data and corresponding ground truth trajectories.

The datasets described above are extensive and cover a large variety of platforms and sensor types. However, they do not include wheel-mounted IMU data which limits the development in this field. To fill this gap, this paper presents a large and versatile wheel-mounted IMU dataset (WMID). The dataset consists of inertial recordings made on two different wheeled platforms: a passenger car mounted with nine IMUs and a mobile robot with five IMUs. On the larger passenger car, there are two IMUs mounted on each wheel, and a single IMU affixed to the roof of the car. On the smaller mobile robot, there is a single IMU mounted to each wheel, and a single IMU affixed to the top of the chassis. A diverse set of trajectories and dynamics were recorded. In total, our dataset contains 73.75 minutes of recordings for each IMU: 52.3 minutes from the passenger car and 21.4 minutes from the mobile robot. Thus, considering all 14 IMUs, the dataset contains of 578 minutes of inertial readings.

This versatile dataset will help develop model-based and data-driven approaches to wheel-mounted inertial sensors. The novelty of this dataset stands out due to its unprecedented scope and unique configuration of inertial sensors. This makes it a significant contribution to the development of wheel-mounted IMU theory and research. Specifically, we’ve deployed two IMUs at each wheel of the passenger car and one IMU at each wheel of the mobile robot. This provides an incredibly rich source of data for understanding individual wheel dynamics, which are often overlooked or simplified in traditional motion tracking. Further, the inclusion of an IMU on the roof of the car and on the chassis of the robot offers crucial contextual data. Lastly, by collecting data from both a large passenger car and a small mobile robot, the dataset captures a wide range of scales, speeds, and dynamic behaviours.

The WMID dataset opens new research directions by providing inertial data captured directly from land vehicles’ wheels. It supports the development of wheel inertial systems, detecting wheel slip, modeling surface interaction, wheel motion analysis, and building low-cost inertial navigation pipe-lines suitable for GNSS-denied environments.

Summary

This paper presents the WMI dataset containing inertial measurements (acceleration, angular velocity) from all four wheels and the body of a ground vehicle. Two types of vehicles have been used - a passenger car with Ackerman geometry (equipped with 9 IMUs) and an omnidirectional robot with Meccanum wheels (equipped with 5 IMUs. In total 64.04 minutes of recording for each IMU was made (490 minutes for all IMUs) with its associated ground truth trajectory. This dataset allows development of novel approaches based on multi-modal sensor fusion e. g. it allows to compute the velocity vector of the robot using forward kinematics, inertial localization by integration of linear acceleration, detection of wheel skid, real-time calibration of sensors, and their combinations. The richness of the dataset allows usage and development of machine learning techniques for wheel-mounted IMUs.

The MRU-P incorporates a licensed GNSS real-time kinematic (RTK) TerraStar-C Pro system34. Passenger car: To solve the equations presented in the Scientific Formulation of the paper, we require at least one IMU on each wheel of the car. In order to record angular speed at different points on the wheel, as well as to record the different linear accelerations, we devised a configuration of two IMUs on each wheel: one sitting on the center of the wheel, and one placed radially from the center at 16 cm, as seen in Fig. 2a. The configuration of wheel-mounted DOT IMUs is assembled on a modified hubcap. The center DOT is directly adhered to the center of the hubcap. The radial DOT is adhered to a 3D printed component of our own design, which is in turn secured with zip-ties to the desired spot on the hubcap. Doing so ensures that the two DOTs share the same orientation, and are properly spaced on the wheel. The entire hubcap assembly is then affixed to each wheel of the car, as seen in Fig. 2b. The sensors on the roof were secured to the car by being affixed to the inside of a perforated crate with adhesive and/or zip-ties, as seen in Fig. 3a. Recording Protocol

This section describes the recording protocol used in each data collection experiment for the car and for the mobile-robot. First, we will explain the rationale behind selecting this specific protocol, and then provide a detailed account of the protocol itself. In each car experiment, we utilized two types of sensors: the MRU-P and the Xsens Dot IMU. The Xsens Dot sensors were connected to two smartphone applications. The first application was linked to five dots—four positioned on the left-side wheels and one on the roof—while the second application was connected to four dots, all placed on the right-side wheels. By using these applications, we were able to organize and our recordings across multiple trials. We used the Inertial-Labs GUI on a separate computer to start the recordings for the MRU-P. Although both the MRU-P inertial sensors and the GNSS-RTK were operated within the same application, they ran at different frequencies: the MRU-P inertial sensors at 100 Hz and the GNSS-RTK at 5 Hz. Technical Validation

The quality of the dataset was ensured through careful recording protocols and post-processing checks as described in the Recording Protocol Section. In the car experiments, each trial started and ended in short stationary phases. These periods were later used to confirm that all Xsense-DOT IMU and GNSS sensors were synchronized and stable when the vehicle was not moving. For the mobile robot, synchronization of the wheel IMUs, body IMU, and GNSS-RTK was achieved using an onboard NTP server, and timestamps were checked to verify that no drift occurred between modules.

All files were examined to ensure completeness and consistency. It was confirmed that each folder contained the expected CSV files. It was also confirmed that the number of samples matched the trial duration, and that no missing timestamps or corrupted records existed.

Sensor values were checked for errors and outliers by applying appropriate thresholds. Accelerations beyond ±200 m/s2 and angular rates above ±2000°/s were removed (per sensor specifications). GNSS quality was validated by checking a fixed status before the beginning of each trial. Trials with poor GNSS reception or GNSS outages were excluded. In the car recordings, GNSS trajectories were also compared with satellite maps to confirm alignment with the road between Park Hacarmel and HaTa’asiya Street. At the end of each trajectory we confirmed that the signals from the different wheels and the body IMU were consistent with each other.
