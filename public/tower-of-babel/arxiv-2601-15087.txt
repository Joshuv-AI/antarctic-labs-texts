Critical and multicritical Lee-Yang fixed points in the local potential approximation
Dario Benedetti, Fanny Eustachon, Omar Zanusso

Abstract
The multicritical generalizations of the Lee-Yang universality class arise as renormalization-group fixed points of scalar field theories with complex i ​ φ 2 ​ n + 1 i\varphi^{2n+1} interaction, n ∈ ℕ n\in\mathbb{N} , just below their upper critical dimension. It has been recently conjectured that their continuation to two dimensions corresponds to the non-unitary conformal minimal models ℳ ⁡ ( 2 , 2 ​ n + 3 ) \mathcal{M}(2,2n+3) . Motivated by that, we revisit the functional renormalization group approach to complex 𝒫 ​ 𝒯 \mathcal{P}\mathcal{T} -symmetric scalar field theories in the Local Potential Approximation, without or with wavefunction renormalization (LPA and LPA’ respectively), aiming to explore the fate of the i ​ φ 2 ​ n + 1 i\varphi^{2n+1} theories from their upper critical dimension to two dimensions. The i ​ φ 2 ​ n + 1 i\varphi^{2n+1} fixed points are identified using a perturbative expansion of the functional fixed-point equation near their upper critical dimensions, and they are followed to lower dimensions by numerical integration of the full equation. A peculiar feature of the complex 𝒫 ​ 𝒯 \mathcal{P}\mathcal{T} -symmetric potentials is that the fixed points are characterized by real but negative anomalous dimensions η \eta , and in low dimension d d , this can lead to a change of sign of the scaling dimensions Δ = ( d − 2 + η ) / 2 \Delta=(d-2+\eta)/2 , thus requiring a novel analysis of the analytical properties of the functional fixed-point equations. We are able to follow the Lee-Yang universality class ( n = 1 n=1 ) down to two dimensions, and numerically determine the scaling dimension of the fundamental field as a function of d d . On the other hand, within the LPA’, multicritical Lee-Yang fixed points with n > 1 n>1 cannot be continued to d = 2 d=2 due to the existence of unexpected non-perturbative fixed points that annihilate with the i ​ φ 2 ​ n + 1 i\varphi^{2n+1} fixed points.

Introduction and summary

Unitarity is a fundamental axiom of quantum field theory (QFT), but it plays a lesser role in applications to critical phenomena, for which the relevant QFTs are defined on Euclidean space rather than Minkowski spacetime.
In such setting, unitarity is equivalent to reflection positivity, but in general there is no fundamental reason to impose the latter on a classical statistical system, other than reducing the complexity of the task of classifying universality classes, or for invoking useful results that rely on unitarity.
In fact, many interesting universality classes are described by nonunitary conformal field theories (CFTs). For example, percolation, self-avoiding walks, and critical systems with quenched disorder in two dimensions are associated to logarithmic CFTs with vanishing central charge

Flow equations in the complex local potential approximation

The idea behind Functional Renormalization Group (FRG) is to study the variation of the effective average action
\Gamma_{k}[\phi]
with respect to the RG time
t=\ln k
.
The effective action is defined by the Legendre transform of the logarithm of the partition function, which is modified by adding an infrared cutoff function
R_{k}(q)
. The latter implies that only the modes roughly between a UV cutoff
\Lambda
and the IR scale
k
are integrated out, thus generalizing Wilson’s momentum shell integration, which is recovered for sharp cutoffs and infinitesimal
\Lambda-k
. In the limit
k\rightarrow 0
all fluctuations below the UV cutoff are integrated, thus recovering the full effective action.
The resulting RG flow equation for
\Gamma_{k}[\phi]
takes the form of an exact functional differential equation, called the Wetterich equation
[
34
]

k\partial_{k}\Gamma_{k}[\phi]=\frac{1}{2}\Tr\left[\frac{k\partial_{k}R_{k}}{\Gamma_{k}^{(2)}[\phi]+R_{k}}\right].

(2.1)

While this equation is not generally exactly solvable, several non-perturbative approximations allow to characterized the RG flow. These usually take the form of a truncated ansatz for the effective action, in which we fix some type of functional form for
\Gamma_{k}[\phi]
and discard anything generated by the right-hand side that does not fit to the ansatz.
Among them, we will consider the derivative expansion. It consists in expanding the effective action in powers of derivatives, and truncate it to a given order, while keeping a functional dependence over the field.
The crudest approximation is known as local potential approximation (LPA)

\Gamma_{k}[\phi]=\int{\rm d}^{d}x\,\left[\frac{1}{2}Z_{k}(\partial\phi)^{2}+V_{k}(\phi)\right],

(2.2)

where we keep a full potential plus a minimal two-derivative term.
We refer to the above (
2.2
) as the LPA when
Z_{k}=1
, that is when no wave-function renormalization is taken into account and the anomalous dimension
\eta=-(k\partial_{k}Z_{k})/Z_{k}
identically vanishes;
and as the LPA’ when
Z_{k}\neq 1
.

Under the ansatz (
2.2
), the Wetterich equation can be projected on the potential part by evaluating the flow (
2.1
) on a constant field configuration
\phi(x)=\phi
, which leads to the flow equation

k\partial_{k}V_{k}(\phi)=\frac{1}{2}\int\frac{{\rm d}^{d}q}{\left(2\pi\right)^{d}}\,G_{k}(q^{2})\,k\partial_{k}R_{k}(q^{2}),

(2.3)

where
G_{k}(q^{2})
denotes the Green function in Euclidean momentum space

G_{k}(q^{2})=\left(\Gamma^{(2)}_{k,q,-q}+R_{k}(q)\right)^{-1}=\left(Z_{k}q^{2}+V_{k}^{\prime\prime}(\phi)+R_{k}(q^{2})\vphantom{Gamma^{(2)}_{k,q,-q}}\right)^{-1}.

(2.4)

The anomalous dimension is instead obtained by projecting on the
{O}\left(p^{2}\right)
part of the flow of the second derivative of the effective action (
2.2
).

\displaystyle v(\varphi)-\Delta\,\varphi\,v^{\prime}(\varphi)=\left(1-\frac{\eta}{d+2}\right)\frac{c_{d}}{1+v^{\prime\prime}(\varphi)},\qquad\Delta=\frac{d-2+\eta}{2},

The ordinary differential equation (

However, most of the solutions either lead to an unstable potential, or they
develop a

, due to the nonlinearity of the equation. Because such singularity prevents from taking the limit

(from the definition of dimensionless variables, small

,

]

Previous analyses show

In the case of a complex potential

v(\varphi)=u(\varphi)+ih(\varphi)

(2.9)

the fixed-point equation (
2.7
) splits into a coupled system for the real and imaginary parts (for the sake of readability, from here on we will generally omit the argument
\varphi

\begin{cases}d\,u-\Delta\,\varphi\,u^{\prime}&=c_{d}\left(1-\frac{\eta}{d+2}\right)\frac{1+u^{\prime\prime}}{\left(1+u^{\prime\prime}\right)^{2}+\left(h^{\prime\prime}\right)^{2}},\\

(2.10)

As anticipated in the introduction, we impose invariance of the complex potential under an antilinear
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}
-symmetry,
v^{*}(-\varphi)=v(\varphi)
, which is expected to ensure that the spectrum and critical exponents are real (see footnote
1

Thus, the real and imaginary parts must be taken to be even and odd functions of
\varphi
, respectively:
u(\varphi)=u(-\varphi)
,
h(\varphi)=-h(-\varphi)
.
Under these additional assumptions, the coupled system (
2.10
) is endowed with initial conditions parametrized by two real parameters
(\lambda_{2},g)
chosen to correspond to the
\varphi^{2}
and
i\varphi^{3}
couplings, respectively:

u^{\prime}(0)=0,\quad u^{\prime\prime}(0)=\lambda_{2}\in\mathbb{R},\quad h(0)=0,\quad h^{\prime\prime\prime}(0)=g\in\mathbb{R}\,.

(2.11)

Notice that with these initial conditions, (
2.10
) immediately fixes to zero all the odd derivatives of
u(\varphi)
and all the even derivatives of
h(\varphi)
, at
\varphi=0
.

As mentioned below equation (
2.5
), in the case of the complex potential the anomalous dimension (
2.7
) can be evaluated at the origin, and thus it is fully determined by the initial conditions

\eta=-c_{d}\frac{g^{2}}{(1+\lambda_{2})^{4}}\,.

(2.12)

Therefore, (
2.10
) is solved in the LPA’ scheme by plugging back (
2.12
) into the differential equations.
In the LPA scheme, we set instead
\eta=0
in the fixed point equation, but we will still use (
2.12
) to give an estimate of the scaling dimension at the fixed point in the LPA. Reiterating such procedure – i.e. plug back the fixed-point value of
\eta
in the LPA equations, search again for fixed points, evaluate the new
\eta
, and so on – leads to results equivalent to the LPA’, when the iteration converges.
Notice that the LPA and LPA’ differential equations only depend explicitly on
g
through
g^{2}
, hence the solutions are invariant under the transformation
g\to-g
,
h\to-h
.

The resulting coupled differential equations are nonlinear in both the LPA and the LPA’ schemes and in general do not have known analytical solutions.
Several strategies can be employed to circumvent the problem such as employing perturbative techniques, expanding the potentials in a polynomial truncation, solving analytically in the large-
\varphi
limit or solving numerically while keeping the potential fully functional.
In the following sections, we will present a combination of different methods with the goal of better understanding
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}
-symmetric single scalar universality classes and clarifying the limitations of the LPA and LPA’.

The shift simply ensures that Gaussian solutions of

Assuming that we are close to such Gaussian regime, we may expand the nonlinear parts of Eqs. (

\displaystyle k\partial_{k}\tilde{v}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle-d\,\tilde{v}(\varphi)+\frac{d-2+\eta}{2}\,\varphi\,\tilde{v}^{\prime}(\varphi)-X_{1}\tilde{v}^{\prime\prime}(\varphi)+X_{2}\tilde{v}^{\prime\prime}(\varphi)^{2}-X_{3}\tilde{v}^{\prime\prime}(\varphi)^{3}+\cdots,

(3.4)

\displaystyle\eta

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\left(Y_{0}-Y_{1}\tilde{v}^{\prime\prime}(\varphi_{0})+\cdots\right)\tilde{v}^{\prime\prime\prime}(\varphi_{0})^{2},

In a regime close to the Gaussian fixed point it is natural to choose

The coefficients

For the cutoff chosen in this paper, the constants are

\displaystyle X_{l}(d,\eta)=\left(1-\frac{\eta}{d+2}\right)c_{d},\qquad Y_{l}(d,\eta)=\frac{(l+1)(l+2)(l+3)}{6}c_{d}\,.

For an optimized cutoff, the

Most of the considerations of this section are independent of the choice of the cutoff,
as long as the first few coefficients in (

Other cutoffs that have been used in the literature can be shown to also verify the positivity of the first few coefficients. The solutions will of course be scheme dependent.

We now assume the more stringent requirement that

This is certainly possible perturbatively and, given that for a solution near the Gaussian one we expect

Then we can perform the rescaling

\displaystyle\frac{2d}{d-2+\eta}w(x)-xw^{\prime}(x)+\frac{1}{2}w^{\prime\prime}(x)-F_{2}w^{\prime\prime}(x)^{2}+F_{3}w^{\prime\prime}(x)^{3}+\cdots,

(3.8)

As a result of the shift, eq. (

Equation (

The Gaussian fixed point is

As discussed in Ref.

(3.12)

for some undetermined constant

The use of Hermite polynomials suggests us to introduce a natural inner product for polynomially bounded functions

\left(f,g\right)\equiv\int{\rm d}x\,{\rm e}^{-x^{2}}f(x)\,g(x)\,.

(3.13)

We can then use inner product to project our equations on the natural basis of polynomials
[
42
]

\lVert f\rVert\equiv\left(f,f\right)
to give meaning to the smallness condition.
The smallness requirement is satisfied only approaching critical dimensions such that
d\to d_{n}=\frac{2n}{n-2}
for polynomial solutions. Recall also that
H_{n}(x)=(2x)^{n}+\cdots
at large values of

. Furthermore, the Gaussian fixed point is recovered for

.

In this paper we are interested in odd solutions that are not bounded from below.
We perform the replacement

, which reveals the critical dimensions of the odd models

The

\epsilon

\eta

\epsilon

The Lee-Yang solution.

Notice that we solve the equation for fixed coefficients

In order to solve the equation for

The computation requires the integral of three Hermite polynomials, whose coefficients must sum to an even number and satisfy a triangular inequality. As a consequence,

For general

This can be argued in general, because in

Properties of the general multicritical cases.

There are also some crucial observations that relate to the
\epsilon

We can show that the solutions fail for some large

First we show the general structure of the solutions in

dimensions: equation (

\displaystyle w(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\epsilon^{1/2}\lambda_{n}H_{2n+1}(x)+\epsilon\lambda_{n}^{2}\sum_{p=0}^{2n-1}\alpha_{2p}H_{2p}(x)+O(\epsilon^{3/2})\,,

(3.22)

which is similar to the Polchinski’s case given in eq. (

due to the integration of Hermite polynomials:
0\leq p\leq 2n-1
.
7
7

7

Notice that the bound is more stringent for the Wetterich equation, because for the Polchinski equation it is

.

To see where perturbation theory fails, recall that
H_{2n+1}(x)\sim x^{2n+1}
for large
x
, in which case
we can compare the leading and subleading contributions to see when they are of the same order in terms of
\epsilon
and
x
.
Using the odd part of the potential, the
\epsilon^{3/2}
correction to eq. (
A.20
) can be shown to include the highest polynomial
H_{6n-1}(x)
, so the subleading corrections are of the same order as

when
\epsilon^{3/2}H_{6n-1}(x)\sim\epsilon^{1/2}H_{2n+1}(x)
, resulting in

\epsilon\,x^{4n-2}\sim 1
. Thus for any finite value of
\epsilon
any nonperturbative solution must be complemented by the appropriate large field analysis, which we touch upon in the next section.
We also notice that the polynomial at order
\epsilon
grows as
x^{4n-2}
, i.e. with one power less than the polynomial at order
\epsilon^{1/2}
. Therefore, when
x
approaches the breaking point of the perturbative series,
x\sim(\frac{1}{\epsilon})^{\frac{1}{4n-2}}
, the odd part scales like
(\frac{1}{\epsilon})^{\frac{2n+1}{4n-2}-\frac{1}{2}}=(\frac{1}{\epsilon})^{\frac{1}{2n-1}}
, while the even part scales like
(\frac{1}{\epsilon})^{\frac{4n-2}{4n-2}-1}=1
, and thus the latter is subleading.
8
8

8

Whether this behavior is reflected order by order in perturbation theory it is not obvious, as the analysis requires bounds that come from the integrals of several Hermite polynomials. It would be interesting to verify it in more generality.

Hence later on this will provide further support to our claim that in the large-field the real part of the potential can be neglected.

Lastly, we notice that even if the critical dimensions

approach

in the limit

, the expansions do not converge (not even weakly) when

approaches two, i.e.
\epsilon=\epsilon_{n}\equiv d_{2n+1}-2\sim\frac{2}{n}
. Neglecting
\eta
for a moment, the rescaling

is not analytic when we approach

, and the entire construction cannot be appropriately set up. In fact, the radius of validity of the expansion
x\sim(\frac{1}{\epsilon_{n}})^{\frac{1}{4n-2}}
becomes of order one as
n\to\infty
, meaning that the perturbative solutions breaks down very soon in
x
, and we inevitably require a nonperturbative framework.
This actually happens in general when the scaling dimension of
\varphi
,
\Delta=\frac{d-2+\eta}{2}
, approaches zero.
In the case of even models, this problem is mitigated by the fact that
\eta>0

as long as
d>2
, so
\Delta>0
above
d=2
. However, in the case of the odd models, we have
\eta<0

and we expect that by lowering
d
the value of
\Delta
will approach zero and become negative already at some
d>2
. This problem will manifest and be addressed numerically in section
5
.

To close this section, let us mention that the method of Ref.
[
42
]
can also be used to compute the spectrum of critical solutions. In appendix
A
we give an example of such computation for both even and odd models using the Polchinski equation, which gives relatively more compact final results.

4
Analytic properties of the fixed-point equations

Before moving beyond the perturbative domain and performing numerical integrations of the equations (
2.10
), it is useful to figure out as much as possible of their analytic properties, such as the structure of singularities and asymptotic expansions of the solutions, in order to have sufficient elements for interpreting the numerical results.
The asymptotic analysis is also motivated by the results of section
3
, where we saw that at finite
\epsilon
the perturbative solution breaks down beyond some finite value of
\varphi
, and thus we need a separate analysis of the large-
\varphi
behaviour of solutions.

We start by rewriting the equations in a simplified form that highlights their dependence on only one parameter.
Assuming
\eta\neq d+2
, that is
\Delta\neq d
, we divide (
2.10
) by
d
and do the following rescaling of field and potential:

\varphi=\left(\frac{c_{d}\left(1-\frac{\eta}{d+2}\right)}{d}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,\bar{\varphi},\qquad v(\varphi)=\frac{c_{d}\left(1-\frac{\eta}{d+2}\right)}{d}\,\bar{v}(\bar{\varphi}).

(4.1)

The equations simplify to

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\bar{u}-\gamma\,\bar{\varphi}\,\bar{u}^{\prime}=\,\frac{1+\bar{u}^{\prime\prime}}{\left(1+\bar{u}^{\prime\prime}\right)^{2}+\left(\bar{h}^{\prime\prime}\right)^{2}}\\
\bar{h}-\gamma\,\bar{\varphi}\,\bar{h}^{\prime}=-\,\frac{\bar{h}^{\prime\prime}}{\left(1+\bar{u}^{\prime\prime}\right)^{2}+\left(\bar{h}^{\prime\prime}\right)^{2}}\end{array}\right.

(4.2)

where we defined
\gamma=\Delta/d
.
The initial conditions will be given in the same form as before, namely

\bar{u}^{\prime}(0)=0,\quad\bar{u}^{\prime\prime}(0)=\lambda_{2}\in\mathbb{R},\quad\bar{h}(0)=0,\quad\bar{h}^{\prime\prime\prime}(0)=\bar{g}\in\mathbb{R}.

(4.3)

However, notice that while the second derivative of the potential is invariant under the rescaling, and hence
\lambda_{2}
is the same as before, the third derivative is not invariant, hence
\bar{g}
differs from the
g
in (
2.11
) by a multiplicative factor:

\bar{g}=\left(\frac{c_{d}(1-\frac{\eta}{d+2})}{d}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,g.

(4.4)

In the rescaled variables, the only difference between LPA and LPA’ is that in the former
\gamma
is only a function of
d
, while in the latter it is a function of
d
and
\bar{g}
.
One slight complication brought in by the rescaling is in the expression of
\eta
in terms of the new initial conditions (
4.3
), because (
2.12
) becomes a second order equation for
\eta
,

\eta=-\frac{d(d+2)}{d+2-\eta}\frac{\bar{g}^{2}}{(1+\lambda_{2})^{4}},

(4.5)

whose solutions are

\eta_{\pm}=\frac{d+2}{2}\left(1\pm\sqrt{1+\frac{4\,d\,\bar{g}^{2}}{(d+2)(1+\lambda_{2})^{4}}}\right),

(4.6)

and of which we should pick the one that goes to zero at
\bar{g}=0
, that is
\eta_{-}
, which is also the negative one.
However, in this section we will be mostly using
\gamma
as a free parameter, thus making no distinction between LPA and LPA’, and we will only use this expression for
\eta
at the end for the
\Delta=0
case.

In the numerical analysis of section
5
we will make the further approximation of discarding the real part.
Besides the obvious practical reason of simplifying the analytical and numerical study, we are motivated also by the observation made at the end of section
3
that for the multicritical Lee-Yang models the real part is subleading at large
\varphi
.
Therefore, we will be dealing with a single equation, that we write explicitly for convenience, dropping from now on the bar on the rescaled variables:

{h}-\gamma\,{\varphi}\,{h}^{\prime}=-\,\frac{{h}^{\prime\prime}}{1+({h}^{\prime\prime})^{2}},

(4.7)

with initial conditions
{h}(0)=0
,
{h}^{\prime\prime\prime}(0)={g}\in\mathbb{R}
.
For simplicity, and for direct comparison to the next section, from now on we will consider only the single decoupled equation (
4.7
), and only occasionally comment about the full system (
4.2
).

Let us note some general facts that will be used later on. First, evaluating (
4.7
) at
{\varphi}=0
and using
{h}(0)=0
, we find
{h}^{\prime\prime}(0)=0
. This pattern in fact goes on and all the even derivatives of the potential vanish at the origin, as demanded by
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}
symmetry.

Second, taking one derivative of (
4.7
), and solving for
{h}^{\prime\prime\prime}({\varphi})
, we find

{h}^{\prime\prime\prime}=\frac{((1-\gamma){h}^{\prime}-\gamma\,{\varphi}\,{h}^{\prime\prime})(1+({h}^{\prime\prime})^{2})^{2}}{({h}^{\prime\prime})^{2}-1}.

(4.8)

Evaluated at
{\varphi}=0
, and using
{h}(0)={h}^{\prime\prime}(0)=0
, this fixes also
{h}^{\prime}(0)=-{g}/(1-\gamma)
.

The equation (
4.7
), or the system (
4.2
), can be studied analytically in the neighbourhood of some given point
{\varphi}\sim{\varphi}_{0}
.
Near an ordinary point, such as the origin
{\varphi}_{0}=0
, the solution is smooth and we can develop the solution as a power series in
({\varphi}-{\varphi}_{0})
, essentially by evaluating higher derivatives from (
4.8
).
The analysis of singular points, such as moveable singularities or the point at infinity, is more involved, and we discuss it below, together with the special case
\gamma=0
, for which an analytic (albeit implicit) solution of (
4.7
) is possible.

4.1
Moveable singularities

As a general fact, nonlinear ODEs may lead to movable singularities, i.e. singularities whose location depends on the initial conditions.
Let us check whether the LPA/LPA’ equations have any such singularities, and of what type.

We first notice that for real
{h}({\varphi})
the RHS of (
4.7
) takes values in
[-1/2,1/2]
, and hence so must do also the LHS.
The boundaries of such range are reached for
{h}^{\prime\prime}=\pm 1
, and we might expect that generic solutions develop singularities at these points.
We will confirm this below.

Assume that a singularity is located at
{\varphi}={\varphi}_{s}
, with
0<{\varphi}_{s}<\infty
(by
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}
-symmetry the same singularity is found also at negative
{\varphi}
), and that for
{\varphi}\lesssim{\varphi}_{s}
the potential behaves as

{h}({\varphi})\sim K({\varphi}_{s}-{\varphi})^{\alpha}(1+O({\varphi}-{\varphi}_{s}))+\text{analytic}\;,

(4.9)

for some constant
K\in\mathbb{R}
, and an exponent
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
that is not a positive integer.
We distinguish two case:

1.

\alpha<2
, in which case
{h}^{\prime\prime}({\varphi})
diverges for
{\varphi}\to{\varphi}_{s}
;

2.

\alpha>2
, in which case
{h}^{\prime\prime}({\varphi}_{s})<\infty
, but some higher derivative diverges.

The
\alpha<2
case.

Let us consider first the
\Delta\neq 0
case, i.e.
\gamma\neq 0
in equation (
4.7
).
Using the ansatz (
4.9
) and matching LHS and RHS of (
4.7
) at leading order in
|{\varphi}-{\varphi}_{s}|
, imposes that

\alpha=\frac{3}{2}\;.

(4.10)

Somewhat surprisingly, the leading singular behavior is independent of
\gamma
as long as it is not zero.

If instead
\Delta=0
(
\gamma=0
), the matching leads to
\alpha=1
, which is inconsistent with the assumption that
\alpha
is not integer.
In this case, we find that the ansatz (
4.9
) should be modified to include a logarithmic behaviour:

{h}({\varphi})\sim K({\varphi}_{s}-{\varphi})\ln|{\varphi}_{s}-{\varphi}|+\ldots\;.

(4.11)

The
\alpha>2
case.

For
\alpha>2
, the second derivative is finite at the singular point,
{h}^{\prime\prime}({\varphi}_{s})<\infty
, but some higher derivative diverges.
Using (
4.8
), we see that if
(1-\gamma){h}^{\prime}({\varphi}_{s})-\gamma\,{\varphi}_{s}\,{h}^{\prime\prime}({\varphi}_{s})\neq 0
, then
{h}^{\prime\prime\prime}({\varphi}_{s})
diverges when
{h}^{\prime\prime}({\varphi}_{s})=\pm 1
, that is the point at which the RHS of (
4.7
) reaches the value
\pm 1/2
, i.e. the boundary of its range for real
{h}({\varphi})
.
In this case we must have
2<\alpha<3
. Plugging the ansatz
{h}^{\prime\prime}({\varphi})\sim\pm 1+\alpha(\alpha-1)K({\varphi}_{s}-{\varphi})^{\alpha-2}
back into (
4.8
) and matching powers, we find

\alpha=\frac{5}{2}.

(4.12)

It is easy to see that no other singular solutions with
\alpha>3
are possible: taking further derivatives of (
4.8
) we find that
{h}^{(n)}
is always a fraction involving products of
{h}^{(n^{\prime})}
with
n^{\prime}<n
in the numerator and powers of
({h}^{\prime\prime})^{2}-1
in the denominator, hence either
{h}^{\prime\prime\prime}({\varphi}_{s})
is singular or all of the
{h}^{(n)}({\varphi}_{s})
for
n\geq 0
are regular.

The two cases above can also be understood by first solving (
4.7
) for
{h}^{\prime\prime}
.
For real
{h}
, the denominator
1+({h}^{\prime\prime})^{2}
is strictly positive, hence (
4.7
) reduces to a quadratic equation in
{h}^{\prime\prime}
, and we thus have two solutions:

{h}^{\prime\prime}=\frac{-1\pm\sqrt{1-4\,({h}-\gamma\,{\varphi}\,{h}^{\prime})^{2}}}{2\,({h}-\gamma\,{\varphi}\,{h}^{\prime})}\equiv F_{\pm}({h},{h}^{\prime}).

(4.13)

This can only be divergent (
\alpha<2
case above) at points where
{h}-\gamma\,{\varphi}\,{h}^{\prime}=0
, if we choose
F_{-}({h},{h}^{\prime})
. However, such choice of sign is incompatible with the initial conditions (
4.3
).
On the other hand, the solution for
F_{+}({h},{h}^{\prime})
, which is compatible with the initial conditions, can only become non-analytic at points where the argument of the square root (i.e. the discriminant) vanishes, and then we are in the
\alpha>2
case above.
Therefore, we conclude that for the initial value problem given by (
4.7
) and (
4.3
) the generic singular behaviour is the one with
\alpha>2
.
In particular, if

1-4\,({h}-\gamma\,{\varphi}\,{h}^{\prime})^{2}\sim A({\varphi}_{s}-{\varphi}),\qquad\text{for }{\varphi}\to{\varphi}_{s}^{-},

(4.14)

then by taking one derivative of (
4.13
) we find that
{h}^{\prime\prime\prime}
has a
({\varphi}_{s}-{\varphi})^{-1/2}
singularity, in agreement with (
4.12
).

Lastly, we notice that if the approach to zero of the discriminant is quadratic,

1-4\,({h}-\gamma\,{\varphi}\,{h}^{\prime})^{2}\sim A({\varphi}_{s}-{\varphi})^{2},\qquad\text{for }{\varphi}\to{\varphi}_{s},

(4.15)

then
{h}^{\prime\prime\prime}
has a finite limit for
{\varphi}\to{\varphi}_{s}
, but discontinuous. Indeed in this case
{h}^{\prime\prime}
develops a
|{\varphi}_{s}-{\varphi}|
cusp.
However, the solution can be extended to a regular one if for
{\varphi}>{\varphi}_{s}
we switch to the solution for the
F_{-}({h},{h}^{\prime})
branch. If and when such continuation leads to a global solution, rather than to a singularity for
{h}^{\prime\prime}
(case (
4.10
)) at a larger value of
{\varphi}
, is a hard question to answer analytically, but it will become clear in the following that if a global solution exists for
\gamma>0
, then it must cross the singularity at
{h}^{\prime\prime}=\pm 1
, and the one just described is the only possibility for that to happen.

4.2
Large-field asymptotic behaviour at
\Delta\neq 0

We now turn to the asymptotic behaviour at large
|{\varphi}|
, for
\gamma\neq 0
.
Assume that a solution of the equations (
4.7
), with initial conditions at either the origin or some finite (possibly large) value of
{\varphi}
, reaches infinite
{\varphi}
.
We then try to solve (
4.7
) in the limit of
{\varphi}\to+\infty
(the behavior at
{\varphi}\to-\infty
can be obtained by symmetry).
We leave the details of this analysis to Appendix
B
, where we also discuss the full system (
4.2
), and we summarize here only the relevant behaviour for the numerical solutions studied in section

Excluding the case
\gamma\geq 1/2
, i.e.
\Delta\geq d/2
, which we will never meet, and unphysical solutions with real part of the potential unbounded from below, which are only found for initial conditions with
\lambda_{2}<-1
, the asymptotic behaviour of possible solutions is of the following form:.

•

For
0<\gamma<\frac{1}{2}
, i.e.
0<\Delta<\frac{d}{2}
, we have either the isolated Gaussian solution

{h}=0,

(4.16)

or a nontrivial solution with asymptotic bahaviour

{h}({\varphi})\sim B{\varphi}^{\frac{1}{\gamma}}-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2B(1-\gamma)^{2}}{\varphi}^{2-\frac{1}{\gamma}}+{O}\left({\varphi}^{4-\frac{2}{\gamma}}\right),

(4.17)

for some arbitrary parameter
B
.
The fact that we have only one free parameter, despite the equation being second order, is a signal that any global solution will likely be unstable under small perturbations of the initial conditions at the origin, that is, global solutions are likely isolated.
9
9

9

See example 4 in section 4.3 of
[

]
for a clear example of this phenomenon.

We also notice that since
1/\gamma>2
, then
{h}^{\prime\prime}\sim{\varphi}^{\frac{1}{\gamma}-2}\to\infty
for
{\varphi}\to\infty
. Given that initial conditions imply
{h}^{\prime\prime}(0)=0
, any global solution in this range of

must cross the singularity at
{h}^{\prime\prime}=\pm 1
, as anticipated above.

•

For
\gamma<0
, i.e.
\Delta<0
:

\displaystyle{h}({\varphi})\sim b_{1}{\varphi}^{\frac{1}{\gamma}}\left(1+{O}\left({\varphi}^{-2}\right)\right)+b_{2}\,e^{\frac{\gamma}{2}{\varphi}^{2}}\,{\varphi}^{-\frac{1}{\gamma}-1}\left(1+{O}\left({\varphi}^{-2},e^{\gamma{\varphi}^{2}}\,{\varphi}^{-\frac{2}{\gamma}-2}\right)\right),

(4.18)

for some arbitrary parameter

and

.
Notice that in this case we have two free parameters, hence a continuum family of global solutions is to be expected, at least in some range of

.

The case

is special, and will be discussed below.

4.3
Representation of the fixed-point equation as a dynamical system

We have seen that writing the fixed-point ODE as in (
4.13
) requires a choice of branch.
We can instead avoid choosing a branch if we take one derivative of (
4.7
), and solve for
{h}^{\prime\prime\prime}
, which appears linearly. This leads to (
4.8
), which can also be recast as a non-autonomous dynamical system, if we define
{h}^{\prime}({\varphi})=x({\varphi})
,
{h}^{\prime\prime}({\varphi})=y({\varphi})
, thus obtaining:

\displaystyle

\displaystyle x^{\prime}=y

(4.19)

\displaystyle y^{\prime}=\frac{((1-\gamma)x-\gamma\,{\varphi}\,y)(1+y^{2})^{2}}{y^{2}-1}.

The system (
4.19
) is non-autonomous because of the explicit dependence on
{\varphi}
, which plays the role of time.
Some snapshot of the vector field at different values of
\gamma
and
{\varphi}
are shown in figures
1
and
2
.
Although this rewriting of our ODE does not help in solving it analytically, it allows us to deduce a number of useful properties.

Nullclines and trivial fixed point.

First of all, we identify the nullclines, that is, the curves where either
x^{\prime}=0
or
y^{\prime}=0
.
These are given by the equations
y=0
and

(1-\gamma)x-\gamma\,{\varphi}\,y=0,

(4.20)

respectively.
The former coincides with the locus of initial conditions
{h}^{\prime\prime}(0)=0
,
{h}^{\prime}(0)=-{g}/(1-\gamma)
.
The latter is the locus of points where the derivative of the discriminant in (
4.13
) vanishes.

Fixed points of a dynamical system are obtained when all the nullclines intersect.
The dynamical system (
4.19
) has only one fixed point at the origin
x=y=0
, that we recognize as the Gaussian theory in the FRG interpretation of the system.

Stability of the origin.

We notice from the figures
1
and
2
that the stability (not to be confused with RG stability) of the origin depends on the sign of
\gamma
.
More precisely, from the linearization of (
4.19
), we obtain the stability eigenvalues

\vartheta_{\pm}=\frac{\gamma{\varphi}\pm\sqrt{(\gamma{\varphi})^{2}-4(1-\gamma)}}{2}.

(4.21)

At large
{\varphi}
(or
(\gamma{\varphi})^{2}>4(1-\gamma)
), the eigenvalues are real, one of them grows in absolute value,
\vartheta_{+}\sim\gamma{\varphi}
, and the other approaches zero,
\vartheta_{-}\sim\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma{\varphi}}
. The eigenvector associated to the latter approaches the nullcline (
4.20
).
Taking into account the nonautonomous nature of the system, one finds that these eigenvalues, rather than leading to usual exponential behaviour of eigenperturbations in autonomous systems, lead to the asymptotic behaviours
\exp(\frac{\gamma}{2}{\varphi}^{2})
and
{\varphi}^{\frac{1}{\gamma}-1}
, consistently with (
4.18
) and the discussion around (
B.7
).
For
\gamma<0
these behaviours are consistent with the linearization of the system, and thus the origin is stable, with a spiralling behaviour that disappears for sufficiently large
{\varphi}
.

The large-time stability of the origin at
\gamma<0
means that in this case there exist a continuum of global solutions to the LPA equation that asymptote to zero for large field values, in agreement with (
4.18
) and the counting argument below it. Starting at small
{\varphi}
, in a neighbourhood of the origin, orbits in the
\{x,y\}
plane wind around the origin a number of times, and every time they cross the
x\equiv{h}^{\prime}=0
axis the potential
{h}
has a minimumum or maximum. As
{\varphi}
increases, the spiralling behaviour disappears and
{h}
goes to zero monotonically.
This will be confirmed from the numerical integrations of section
5
.

Notice that even at
\gamma<0
the origin stops being an attractor if we start the flow with initial condition sufficiently far in the
x
variable: beyond some critical value for the initial condition of
x
, the flow ends on the
y=\pm 1
singularity.

Singularities.

The dynamical system (
4.19
) has obvious singularities at
y=\pm 1
.
In general these are the singularities for
{h}^{\prime\prime\prime}
discussed around (
4.12
): at positive
\gamma
, for any initial conditions inside the strip
y\in(-1,1)
, except of course the origin, they are reached at a finite value of
{\varphi}
. For negative
\gamma
, the same happens only for initial conditions outside the basin of attraction of the origin, that at
y=0
corresponds to a finite interval in
x
.

Separatrices and global solutions.

In order to further understand the properties of the dynamical system (
4.19
), and its possible global solutions, it is important to consider also its separatrices. Since the system is non-autonomous, separatrices in the vector field at fixed time are not necessarily true separatrices of the full time-dependent system, in particular they are not invariant curves. Nevertheless, they help in qualitatively understanding some of the behaviour that we observe in numerical integrations.

Consider first the case
\gamma>0
. It turns out that as we change the initial condition
x(0)=x_{0}
,
y(0)=0
, we can jump from solutions that hit the singularity at
y=-1
to solutions that hit the singularity at
y=+1
, with a corresponding jump in the value of
{\varphi}_{s}
. The solution at the jump is what we call a separatrix, and at frozen time it might look like the one pictured in red in the first diagram of figure
1
.

Going back to the behaviour in equation (
4.15
), we claim that this happens for solutions that at
\varphi=\varphi_{s}
lie precisely on a separatrix of this sort.
As can be seen in figure
1
, the separatrix in red is one of the only two curves (at fixed
{\varphi}
) on which the arrows preserve their orientation on the two sides of the
y=-1
singularity (the other one, not shown in figure
1
, has arrows in the opposite direction, and it intersects the red separatrix on the
y=-1
line). Since
y^{2}-1
in the denominator in the
y
-component of (
4.19
) changes sign across the singularity, the sign of
y^{\prime}
can only remain the same if also the numerator changes sign, meaning that it has a first-order zero at
{\varphi}_{s}
. Since the numerator of the
y
-component of (
4.19
) is proportional to the derivative of the discriminant
1-4\,({h}-\gamma\,{\varphi}\,{h}^{\prime})^{2}
, and the discriminant itself vanishes at the singularity, it follows that (
4.15
) must hold when the separatrix reaches the singularity.
In other words, the separatrix intersects the locus of singularities at the same point as the nullcline (
4.20
) does, as indeed can be seen in the plots in figures
1
and
2
.

As we mentioned above, the important next question is whether the continuation of such solution to the
|y|>1
region leads to a global or singular solution.
From the asymptotic analysis, we know that a global solution at
0<\gamma<1/2
must behave like in (
4.17
), and thus it will satisfy

(1-\gamma){h}^{\prime}({\varphi})-\gamma{\varphi}{h}^{\prime\prime}({\varphi})\sim{O}\left({\varphi}^{1-\frac{1}{\gamma}}\right),

(4.22)

that is, it must approach the nullcline.
Stream plots suggest that this is happening, but we do not have an analytic proof, nor a satisfactory understanding of how this behaviour is associated to one or multiple initial conditions.

For
\gamma<0
, we can similarly identify a separatrix with a curve that smoothly hits the singularity at
|y|=1
, and then escapes to
|y|>1
. The main difference is that in this case rather than separating different singular behaviours, it separates global from singular solutions: see the discussion above, about the stability of the origin in the
\gamma<0
case.

4.4
Vanishing
\Delta

At
\gamma=0
, i.e.
\Delta=0
, the equations (
4.2
) change drastically, because the first derivative term drops out.

While the solution (
B.3
) is still valid (and still unphysical), it is easy to see that all other solutions are affected drastically. In particular, the RHS cannot go to zero for
{\varphi}\to\infty
, because this requires either
{u}^{\prime\prime}
or
{h}^{\prime\prime}
to blow up, which leads to a contradiction with the LHS, which would instead require both
{u}
and
{h}
going to zero.
Also the GFP solution is still valid, but its linear perturbations have periodic solutions.
These cannot really be considered small with respect to the
{v}=1
, but we also have no reason to discard them, as they are not in obvious contradiction with the equations.
In fact, periodic solutions are present in this case, as we will now show.

Purely imaginary potential.

In order to simplify the analysis, let us at first discard the real part of the potential.
The equation (
4.7
) reduces to

{h}=-\,\frac{{h}^{\prime\prime}}{1+\left({h}^{\prime\prime}\right)^{2}},

(4.23)

which can be algebraically solved for
{h}^{\prime\prime}
. Of the two roots, we choose the one that admits the initial condition
{h}(0)=0
:

{h}^{\prime\prime}=\frac{-1+\sqrt{1-4\,{h}^{2}}}{2\,{h}}=-\frac{d}{d\,{h}}U({h}).

(4.24)

This equation has the form of a Newton equation, with Newtonian potential

U({h})=\frac{1}{2}\left(\ln\left(1+\sqrt{1-4\,{h}^{2}}\right)-\sqrt{1-4\,{h}^{2}}\right),

(4.25)

which is real, bounded and convex for
|{h}|\leq 1/2
(see figure
3
).

Figure 3:
The Newtonian potential of equation (
4.25
). Outside of the plotted range,
U({h})
becomes complex.

As in the general case, we choose initial condition
{h}(0)=0
,
{h}^{\prime}(0)=-{g}
(notice that by equation (
4.24
) we have
{h}^{\prime\prime\prime}(0)=-{h}^{\prime}(0)
).
Introducing the conserved energy

E=\frac{{h}^{\prime}({\varphi})^{2}}{2}+U({h}({\varphi}))=\frac{{g}^{2}}{2}+U(0)=\frac{{g}^{2}+\ln 2-1}{2}\geq\frac{\ln 2-1}{2},

(4.26)

the equation is solved in implicit form as

{\varphi}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\int_{0}^{{h}}\frac{d\,z}{\sqrt{E-U(z)}}.

(4.27)

The minimal energy
E=(\ln 2-1)/2
corresponds to the stationary point at the bottom of the Newton potential.
This has
{h}(0)={h}^{\prime}(0)=0
, and thus we recognize it to be the GFP at
d=2
(because
{g}=0
and
\Delta=0
imply
d=2
).

For
(\ln 2-1)/2<E<0
, i.e. for
0<{g}^{2}<1-\ln 2
, the solutions are periodic with period

T=\sqrt{2}\int_{{h}_{1}}^{{h}_{2}}\frac{d\,z}{\sqrt{E-U(z)}},

(4.28)

where
{h}_{1}
and
{h}_{2}
are the roots of the equation
U({h})=E
. Periodic solutions exist and are regular for all
{\varphi}\in\mathbb{R}
, hence they are all acceptable fixed point solutions.
Therefore, in the LPA at
d=2
, where
\Delta=0
for any
{g}
, the GFP is not an isolated fixed point.
In the LPA’, the
\gamma=0
analysis is not sufficient, because for fixed
d
, we have
\Delta=0
only at one value of
{g}^{2}
.

For
E>0
,
{h}({\varphi})
reaches the value
\pm 1/2
with finite velocity (i.e. finite
{h}^{\prime}({\varphi})
), hence it is pushed towards the complex domain. Moreover,
{h}^{\prime\prime\prime}({\varphi})
diverges when
{\varphi}
is such that
{h}({\varphi})^{2}=1/4
and
{h}^{\prime}({\varphi})\neq 0
, as it is seen by deriving once equation (
4.24
).
This behaviour is precisely that of a moveable singularity with finite
{h}^{\prime\prime}
, discussed around equation (
4.12
).
We conclude that none of the solutions with
E>0
is a global solution.
Notice also that the singularity is always reached in finite “time”: in fact, since
-U(z)>0
for
z\in[-1/2,1/2]
, for
E>0
we have

{\varphi}_{s}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\int_{0}^{1/2}\frac{d\,z}{\sqrt{E-U(z)}}<\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\int_{0}^{1/2}\frac{d\,z}{\sqrt{-U(z)}}\simeq 1.3132.

(4.29)

The situation at
E=0
requires some extra care. The points
{h}({\varphi})=\pm 1/2
, are reached with vanishing
{h}^{\prime}
and thus finite
{h}^{\prime\prime\prime}
.
Expanding (
4.27
) at
E=0
near
{h}=1/2
, and plugging it into (
4.24
), we find

{h}^{\prime\prime}({\varphi})\simeq-1+\sqrt{2}|{\varphi}_{s}-{\varphi}|.

(4.30)

When continued to
{\varphi}>{\varphi}_{s}
, this leads to a periodic global solution of class
C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, with discontinuous third derivative
{h}^{\prime\prime\prime}
.

Alternatively, we could do the continuation by means of the other branch:

{h}^{\prime\prime}=\begin{cases}\frac{-1+\sqrt{1-4\,{h}^{2}}}{2\,{h}}&\;\text{for }\,{\varphi}<{\varphi}_{s}\\
\frac{-1-\sqrt{1-4\,{h}^{2}}}{2\,{h}}&\;\text{for }\,{\varphi}>{\varphi}_{s},\end{cases}

(4.31)

leading to

{h}^{\prime\prime}({\varphi})\simeq-1+\sqrt{2}({\varphi}_{s}-{\varphi}),\quad\text{for }{\varphi}\simeq{\varphi}_{s}.,

(4.32)

and continuous derivatives.
This can be understood also by using the dynamical system representation (
4.19
), which bypasses the need to choose a branch, and whose stream plot is shown in figure

.
The red curves in the figure denote the separatrices, and from their plot it is clear that the periodic orbit obtained by joining the two red trajectories in the

, we replace the Newton potential by

U({h})=\frac{1}{2}\left(\ln\left(1-\sqrt{1-4\,{h}^{2}}\right)+\sqrt{1-4\,{h}^{2}}\right),

(4.33)

but we can still apply the formula (

{h}=0^{+}

This can also be understood from (

Dimension at which
\Delta=0
.

With some simple assumption, we can straightforwardly give an upper bound for the dimension
d_{0}
at which
\Delta=0
for the fixed point of the
i{\varphi}^{3}
theory, without using the FRG.
We expect that the anomalous dimension of such theory is negative for all
d<6
, and in particular we know that at
d=2
it coincides with the scaling dimension predicted by the
M(2,5)
minimal model
[
4
]
, namely
\eta|_{d=2}=\Delta|_{d=2}=-4/5
.
Assuming that
\eta
(and thus
\Delta
) depends monotonically in
d
, and thus that the 2d result provides a lower bound for the anomalous dimension of the Lee-Yang model for
2\leq d<6
, then
d_{0}>2
and we must have

-\frac{4}{5}<\eta|_{d=d_{0}}=2-d_{0}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad d_{0}<2.8.

(4.34)

A more precise prediction for
d_{0}
, within the (purely-imaginary) LPA’ approximation of the FRG, can be obtained from the following reasoning, for which we anticipate some of the ideas behind the methods of section
5
.

We know that when integrating the ODE (
4.7
) starting from the origin, most of the solutions end up at a singularity of the type discussed in section
4.1
. Only at some isolated point
{g}=g_{*}(d)
we expect to find a global solution.
At
d=6-\epsilon
, with
\epsilon\ll 1
, one finds only one nontrivial global solution, that we can interpret as the fixed point corresponding to the Lee-Yang universality class of the
i{\varphi}^{3}
theory, so let us denote the corresponding initial condition by
{g}_{\rm LY}(d)
.
At small
\epsilon
, we find that at such fixed point the scaling dimension is in the range
0<\Delta<\frac{d}{2}
, i.e. we are in the case with asymptotic behaviour (
4.17
).
Following such solution to lower
d
,
\Delta
decreases and at some value
d_{0}>2
it reaches
\Delta=0
, i.e. we end in the
\gamma=0
case discussed above.
Further decreasing the dimension to
2\leq d<d_{0}
, we find
\Delta<0
, corresponding to the asymptotic behaviour (
4.18
).

On the other hand, given that
\Delta=(d-2+\eta(d,g))/2
, for any
d
there exists a value
{g}={g}_{0}(d)
at which
\Delta=0
. By definition,
{g}_{\rm LY}(d)={g}_{0}(d)
only at
d=d_{0}
.
Therefore, if at
d>d_{0}
we only find a global solution at
{g}={g}_{\rm LY}(d)
, and not also at
{g}={g}_{0}(d)
, then the solution at
{g}={g}_{0}(d)
, with
d>d_{0}
, must correspond to
E>0
(see (
4.26
) and discussion below that).
We thus conclude that if
E
at
{g}={g}_{0}(d)
varies continuously with
d
, at
d_{0}
we must have
E=0
.
By the definition (
4.26
), this is equivalent to
{g}_{0}(d_{0})=\pm\sqrt{1-\ln 2}
. Using (
2.12
), which in the rescaled variables that we use in this section becomes
\eta=-\frac{d(d+2)}{2(d-\Delta)}{g}^{2}
, we find that solving
\Delta=0
for
d_{0}
gives

d_{0}=2\frac{3-\ln 2}{1+\ln 2}\simeq 2.7249.

(4.35)

As we will see in section
5
, this prediction matches beautifully with the results from the numerical integration.

5
Numerical solutions

A functional fixed-point equation such as (
2.7
) is standardly solved using one of the following two methods:

•

Polynomial truncation
: the functional potential is further expanded in a polynomial
v(\varphi)=\sum_{n}\lambda_{n}\varphi^{n}
truncated at some finite order
n\leq N
. Then, the equation (
2.7
) translates into an algebraic linear system equivalent to the system of beta functions
k\partial_{k}\lambda_{n}=\beta(\lambda_{n})=0
for each coupling. While the system is now easily solvable analytically or numerically, as the order of truncation rises, the order of the polynomial equations for the beta functions increases alongside, and a number of spurious solutions appeares. It thus becomes harder to identify the physical solution or vary the dimension.

•

Numerical shooting
: the potential is kept fully functional and the differential equation is solved by integrating numerically starting from various initial conditions until either it reaches a movable singularity or it becomes clear that we have reached a large-
\varphi
asymptotic regime and we thus have a global solution.
As explained before, global solutions are in general unstable, in the sense that they should appear as isolated points, or occasionally they exist for some interval of the initial condition parameters.
Isolated global solutions in general require an infinite fine tuning of the initial conditions, therefore their search relies on a graphical method. The location of the singularity is plotted against the initial conditions, and the global fixed point solutions are identified by “spikes” or discontinuities in the plot, which later we refer to as

6
Summary and discussion

We have presented a detailed study of the fixed-point equation for the local potential approximation of the Wetterich equation in the case of a
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}
-symmetric complex potential for a single scalar field.
Our main motivation was the development of nonperturbative methods for multicritical Lee-Yang models, especially in view of the conjectural status of their link to minimal models in two dimensions
[
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
]
.
And on the technical level, the FRG approach to complex potentials had remained so far largely unexplored (with few exceptions, e.g. Refs.
[
13
,
27
]
), thus providing in itself motivation for their further investigation.
The main lessons from our analysis are:

•

The perturbative construction of solutions, given in section
3
, has shown that weakly coupled fixed points appear at
d=d_{2n+1}-\epsilon=\frac{4n+2}{2n-1}-\epsilon
, as for
i\varphi^{2n+1}
theories in standard perturbation theory
[
32
]
. The coefficients of the series expansion in
\epsilon
of the effective potential
v(\varphi)
are expressed as linear combinations of Hermite polynomials in
\varphi
, and, because of their growth, the expansion breaks down when
\varphi\sim(\frac{1}{\epsilon})^{\frac{1}{4n-2}}
.
For larger values of
\varphi
, the asymptotic analysis of section
4
shows that the leading large-field behaviour is
v(\varphi)\sim\varphi^{d/\Delta_{\varphi}}
, which is a noninteger power for
\epsilon\neq 0
, thus explaining why the polynomial expansion cannot hold at large
\varphi
. Nevertheless, the perturbative results serve as a guidance in identifying universality classes in the numerical analysis, and thus even at finite
\epsilon
we will refer to such universality classes as
i\varphi^{2n+1}
fixed points.

•

The detailed analysis of singularities and asymptotic expansion in section
4
has shown that the properties of the fixed-points equations change drastically when
\Delta
changes sign: in particular, while at
\Delta>0
we expect only isolated global solutions, at
\Delta\leq 0
a continuum of global solutions is possible. This fact affects crucially the LPA’ of the
i\varphi^{2n+1}
theories, that have
\Delta_{\varphi}=\Delta|_{g=g_{*}}>0
near the upper critical dimension
d_{2n+1}
, but are expected to have
\Delta_{\varphi}<0
at
d=2
, based on their conjectured CFT correspondence.

•

The numerical work presented in section
5
has allowed us to verify the appearance of the
i\varphi^{2n+1}
fixed points below their respective upper critical dimension
d_{2n+1}
, and to continue them to the nonperturbative domain of lower dimensions.
For the Lee-Yang universality class
n=1
, we found that
\Delta_{\varphi}
becomes zero at
d=d_{0}\simeq 2.7249
, in agreement with the analytic prediction from section
4
, and to be compared with truncated bootstrap result
d_{0}\simeq 2.6199

[
26
]
and the two-sided Padé result
d_{0}\simeq 2.65
from the five-loop perturbative expansion
[
29
]
.
18
18

18

Private communication by Igor Klebanov.

Estimates for
d_{0}
of
2.649(25)
and
2.655(35)
have also been
established using the six-loop perturbative approach of
[
23
]
,
where the values were derived from the exponents
\sigma
and

\Delta_{\phi}
, respectively.
19
19

19

Private communication by John A. Gracey.

For
d<d_{0}
, we found a continuous interval of initial conditions leading to global solutions, again in agreement with the anaysis of section
4
. Identifying the Lee-Yang universality class with the end point of such interval, we were able to follow it all the way to
d=2
, finding a value of
\Delta_{\varphi}
with a relative error between
2.6\%
and
7\%
compared to the exact value from
\mathcal{M}(2,5)
.

•

Unexpectedly, at
d<d_{0}
, we discovered the appearance of an infinite sequence of new nonperturbative fixed points, that we denoted “
i\varphi^{2n+1}_{\text{bis}}
”, branching off from the
i\varphi^{3}
one, and annihilating one-by-one with the perturbative
i\varphi^{2n+1}
fixed points with
n>1
, at some dimension
2<d<\min(d_{2n+1},d_{0})
.
As a consequence, we could not continue the multicritical Lee-Yang universality classes down to
d=2
and we thus could not compare them to the minimal models
\mathcal{M}(2,2n+3)
or
\mathcal{M}(2,4n+1)
.

There are many possible directions for improving our analysis, but they all require substantial effort, so we leave them to future work. However, we stress that there are at least two strong motivations for pursuing these studies: the first is to aim at a quantitative improvement of our predictions, in order to be able to provide a reliable benchmark for other approaches; the second is to clarify the status of the nonperturbative fixed points found within the LPA’.

The easiest improvement of our analysis would be to change or generalize our choice of cutoff, in order to test the dependence of our results on such choices, and to exploit that for optimization purposes.
In fact, it is well known that while critical exponents (scaling dimensions) should be universal, i.e. cutoff independent, approximations such as the LPA lead to a cutoff dependence of the results.
Optimization then amounts to tuning the cutoff to minimize the dependence of critical exponents on its variations.
This approach is known as
principle of minimal sensitivity

[
58
]
, and it has been successfully implemented in the derivative expansion
[
59
,
60
,
61
]
.

The next obvious improvement would be to enlarge our truncation space, that is, to go beyond our LPA/LPA’.
First, it would be interesting to include also the real part of the potential. As we explained, truncating it to a polynomial destabilizes the fixed point equation for the imaginary part, due to the wrong large-field behaviour of the polynomials. Therefore, one should either employ a polynomial expansion for both real and imaginary parts, as done already in
[
13
]
, or treat both functionally, thus looking for global solutions of the coupled system of ODE (
2.10
).
Second, it would be important to move to the next order in the derivative expansion, thus promoting
Z_{k}\to Z_{k}(\varphi)
in (
2.2
)
[
62
,
50
]
.
Other possibilities to go beyond the LPA/LPA’ include the BMW scheme
[
63
]
, and the LPA”
[
64
]
.

The improvements suggested above would certainly answer the question on whether the predictions for Lee-Yang in low dimensions, e.g. for
\Delta_{\varphi}
and
\Delta_{\varphi^{3}}
, could be improved.
In particular it would be nice to be able to get better results for the scaling dimensions of
\varphi^{m}
, which is of course the ultimate goal of any method.
However, the most pressing question raised by our work is without doubt the one about the nonperturbative fixed points.

Are the “
i\varphi^{2n+1}_{\text{bis}}
” fixed points, and their annihilation with the
i\varphi^{2n+1}
ones, a spurious result of the LPA’, or are they a genuine nonperturbative feature brought to light by the FRG?
The second option would probably be the most exciting, as it would be a nice case of truly nonperturbative physics, unforeseeable with perturbative methods, and it would force us to completely rethink about the Ginzburg-Landau description of the minimal models
\mathcal{M}(2,2n+3)
.
However, at present we lack any interpretation for the fact that an infinite series of fixed points seems to emerge from the Lee-Yang CFT just below
d=d_{0}
, i.e. the dimension at which it has
\Delta_{\varphi}=0
.
The mechanism could have similarities to how the
\phi_{1,3}
-perturbed unitary minimal models emerge from the restricted sine-Gordon model
[
65
,
66
]
(see also
[
67
]
for a clear exposition), but in noninteger dimension, and without the power of 2d CFTs and integrability, it is hard to make sense of it.
About the annihilation, it should also be said that since we are dealing with a complex potential from the start, it is not obvious that complex solutions for
h(\varphi)
should be discarded: what they would really imply, in the full system including both
u(\varphi)
and
h(\varphi)
is that what we mean by real and imaginary part gets mixed up. However, given the transformation properties of
u(\varphi)
and
h(\varphi)
under
\varphi\to-\varphi
, this would also imply an explicit breaking of
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}
invariance when
d<d_{0}
, which currently seems at odds with the properties of
\mathcal{M}(2,2n+3)
.

Given the puzzles above, right now the most convincing answer is that the “
i\varphi^{2n+1}_{\text{bis}}
” fixed points and the related annihilation are probably spurious.
There is actually a simple argument for why we might expect that in the pure-imaginary LPA’ it is impossible to follow the
i\varphi^{2n+1}
fixed points all the way down to
d=2
, and that this problem might be lifted by a larger truncation of the derivative expansion.
The argument is the following. Denoting
\Delta_{\varphi,n}=(d-2+\eta_{n})/2
the scaling dimension of
\varphi
at the
i\varphi^{2n+1}
fixed point, if the latter should correspond in
d=2
to the nonunitary minimal model
\mathcal{M}(2,2n+3)
or
\mathcal{M}(2,4n+1)
, with the identification
\varphi\sim\phi_{1,2}
, then we must have
\Delta_{\varphi,n}|_{d=2}<\Delta_{\varphi,1}|_{d=2}
, for
n>1
.
On the other hand, in the LPA’ the anomalous dimension decreases monotonically with
g
(we have
\eta\propto-g^{2}
in the unrescaled variables, see (
2.12
)).
20
20

20

Note that this statement is independent of the choice of cutoff, as can be seen from (
2.5
): in the complex potential case, the
V_{k}^{(3)}(0)^{2}
in front always produces a
-g^{2}
factor, while at
\phi=0
the integral over
q
is
g
-independent and negative, thus canceling the explicit minus in front.
Since the
i\varphi^{2n+1}
fixed points, with corresponding initial condition
g^{*}_{n}
, pop out of the Gaussian one at
d=d_{2n+1}-\epsilon
with
g^{*}_{n}\sim\sqrt{\epsilon}
, then if
n>1
, at such dimension the
i\varphi^{3}
fixed point
g^{*}_{1}
has already moved farther away at larger values, i.e.
g^{*}_{1}\gg g^{*}_{n}
. As a consequence, we have
\Delta_{\varphi,n}|_{d_{2n+1}-\epsilon}>\Delta_{\varphi,1}|_{d_{2n+1}-\epsilon}
, for
n>1
and
\epsilon\ll 1
.
Therefore, at some dimension
d
such that
2<d<d_{2n+1}
, we must have
g^{*}_{n}=g^{*}_{1}
, i.e. the
i\varphi^{2n+1}
fixed points with
n>1
should cross the
i\varphi^{3}
fixed point (and in fact also cross all the other model with
n^{\prime}<n
).
Such crossings are clearly problematic and cannot occur without harm.
Notice that in the LPA, where we do not see nonperturbative fixed points or annihilations, we can push all the multicritical fixed points to
d\to 2
, but indeed with wrong scaling dimensions, as the fixed points are always ordered on the
g
axis with decreasing
n
, as can be seen in Fig.
6
, and thus we have
\Delta_{\varphi,n}>\Delta_{\varphi,1}
even at
d=2
.

The problem described above could be circumvented within an approximation in which
\eta
depends on more than one parameter. For example, keeping also the real part of the potential, we also have the
\lambda_{2}
parameter, see (
2.12
). And in a two- or higher-dimensional space of parameters the
i\varphi^{2n+1}
fixed points could avoid each other even when their
\Delta_{\varphi,n}
cross each other.

Nevertheless, although the argument above explains why we might expect problems for the
i\varphi^{2n+1}
fixed points in the LPA’, it does not explain the origin of the nonperturbative
i\varphi^{2n+1}_{\text{bis}}
fixed points, and why the annihilation occurs with these new fixed points, rather than for example pairwise among the
i\varphi^{2n+1}
ones, when they should cross each other.
Only further study will clarify this issue.

Appendix A
\epsilon
-expansion of local potential equations

A procedure by O’Dwyer and Osborn can be applied to find the
\epsilon
-expansion of multicritical model of local potential approximations
[
42
]
.

A.1
Polchinski equation

It is simpler to describe the procedure using Polchinski’s equation,
21
21

21

Polchinski’s equation is generally presented as the RG flow with respect to an ultraviolet scale
\Lambda
as opposed to the infrared scale
k
of FRG. However, the methods of this appendix translate to the FRG case replacing
\Lambda\partial_{\Lambda}v(\varphi)=0
with
k\partial_{k}v(\varphi)=0
straightforwardly. To the leading order of the derivative expansion any dependence on the cutoff function can be factored out by appropriate rescalings
[
68
]
.

in which case the RG flow of the local potential is

-\Lambda\partial_{\Lambda}v(\varphi)=-dv(\varphi)+\frac{d-2}{2}\varphi v^{\prime}(\varphi)-v^{\prime\prime}(\varphi)+v^{\prime}(\varphi)^{2}\,,

(A.1)

and fixed points are solutions of
\Lambda\partial_{\Lambda}v(\varphi)=0
as usual.
In the derivation of eq. (
A.1
) the potential
v(\varphi)
is rescaled in such a way that the cutoff dependence is factored out completely.
We limit the first analysis of this appendix to the local potential case in which
\eta=0

for clarity of the presentation, but all the results can be extended to include the anomalous dimension (see also Ref.
[
42
]
and section
A.5
below), as we have done for the case of the odd models that are considered in this paper in section
3
.

We perform the rescaling
x=\frac{1}{2}(d-2)^{1/2}\varphi

and define
w(x)=v(\varphi)
, so that the fixed point equation is equivalent to

\frac{2d}{d-2}w(x)-xw^{\prime}(x)+\frac{1}{2}w^{\prime\prime}(x)-\frac{1}{2}w^{\prime}(x)^{2}=0\,.

(A.2)

Assuming a regime in which a solution
w(x)
is small, i.e. a solution that is sufficiently close to the Gaussian one, the linearized equation becomes

\frac{2d}{d-2}w(x)-xw^{\prime}(x)+\frac{1}{2}w^{\prime\prime}(x)\approx 0\,,

(A.3)

which is identical to the FRG counterpart given in eq. (
3.11
) of the main text. The polynomially bounded solutions are Hermite polynomials, which we repeat here for convenience,

w(x)\approx\lambda\,H_{n}(x)\,,\qquad\qquad n=\frac{2d}{d-2}\,,

(A.4)

for some undetermined constant
\lambda
and
n
a positive integer. This shows that the requirement is satisfied only at critical dimensions such that
d=d_{n}=\frac{n}{n-2}
. The parameter
\lambda
could be though of as the coupling of some multicritical model
\varphi^{n}
, because at large
x
we have
H_{n}(x)\sim x^{n}
, which is marginally relevant below
d_{n}
. However, rather than finding its beta function and fixed point as a function of
d
, the strategy that we adopt is to solve (
A.2
) using an
\epsilon
-expansion below
d_{n}
and solving the couplings iteratively.

A.2
Even models

If we require that
w(x)
in eq. (
A.4
) is bounded from below, we must also enforce that
\lambda>0
and that
n\to 2n
is even (
n\geq 1
). The
\epsilon
-expansion can then be set up below the (upper) critical dimensions
d_{2n}=\frac{n}{n-1}
, i.e. for
d=d_{2n}-\epsilon
, and
the nonlinear equation for
w(x)
is solved iteratively using the inner product

\left(f,g\right)=\int{\rm d}x\,{\rm e}^{-x^{2}}f(x)g(x)\,,

which was introduced also in the main text in (
3.13
) and is such that the Hermite polynomials form an orthogonal basis
\left(H_{p},H_{q}\right)=2^{p}\sqrt{\pi}p!\delta_{p,q}
.

We start with the leading-order ansatz

w(x)=\epsilon\lambda_{n}H_{2n}(x)+O(\epsilon^{2})\,,

(A.5)

which by definition solves eq. (
A.2
) at the leading order in
\epsilon
when
d=d_{2n}-\epsilon
. In order to fix the coefficient
\lambda_{n}
we consider the order
\epsilon^{2}
of eq. (
A.2
), which includes a contribution coming from expanding the coefficient
2d/(d-2)
at the leading order in
\epsilon
and a contribution coming from the nonlinear term
w^{\prime}(x)^{2}
. Together they are

2\left(n^{2}-2n+1\right)\lambda_{n}H_{2n}(x)-16n^{2}\lambda_{n}^{2}H_{2n-1}(x){}^{2}\,,

(A.6)

where we have used the fact that
H_{2n}^{\prime}(x)=4nH_{2n-1}(x)
.
Now we make use of the inner product, i.e. we project the above equation on
H_{2n}(x)

as

\int{\rm d}x\,{\rm e}^{-x^{2}}H_{2n}(x)\left\{2\left(n^{2}-2n+1\right)\lambda_{n}H_{2n}(x)-16n^{2}\lambda_{n}^{2}H_{2n-1}(x){}^{2}\right\}=0\,.

(A.7)

After integration, the first term is proportional to
\lambda_{n}
up to an
n
-dependent coefficient. The second term requires knowledge of the integral of three Hermite polynomials, which is known in general (the coefficients satisfy a triangular inequality). For the purpose of this paper we need integrals of up to four Hermite polynomials, so it may be convenient to make repeated use of

H_{p}(x)H_{q}(x)=\sum_{r=0}^{{\rm min}(p,q)}r!\binom{p}{r}\binom{q}{r}H_{p+q-2r}(x)\,.

(A.8)

Applying this formula to the product
H_{2n-1}(x){}^{2}
and integrating over
x
, only the coefficient such that
r=n-1
gives a contribution. We find the unique nontrivial solution for (
A.7
)

\lambda_{n}=\frac{(n-1)^{2}n!^{3}}{2^{n}n(2n)!^{2}}\,.

(A.9)

Notice that the constant
\lambda_{n}
is greater than zero for
n\geq 1
, so the coefficient of the leading Hermite polynomial is positive, i.e.
\epsilon\,\lambda_{n}\geq 0
, only if
\epsilon>0
, implying that the
d_{2n}
s are actually
upper
critical dimensions.
Now we expand the ansatz to include subleading corrections and we do it in such a way that the solution remains symmetric

w(x)=\epsilon\lambda_{n}H_{2n}(x)+\epsilon^{2}\lambda_{n}\sum_{p\geq 0}\alpha_{2p,n}H_{2p}(x)\,.

(A.10)

The normalization involving one power of
\lambda_{n}
in the subleading terms
is chosen for convenience because it gives equations that are homogeneous in
\lambda_{n}
, which then factors out in the equations determining the coefficients
\alpha_{2p,n}
.
The subleading corrections do not influence the determination of
\lambda_{n}
as long as we consider the case
p\neq n
. We can thus insert the ansatz in eq. (
A.2
)
and at order
\epsilon^{2}
we find that in addition to the terms (
A.6
) there are
also

\lambda_{n}\sum_{p\neq n}\left[4n\alpha_{2p,n}H_{2p}(x)+8p(2p-1-x)\alpha_{2p,n}H_{2p-2}(x)\right]\,.

(A.11)

where the instance of the variable
x
can be replaced by the polynomial
H_{1}(x)=2x
.
Now we again use the inner product to project the sum of (
A.6
) and (
A.11
) but this time on a general
H_{2p}(x)
with
p\neq n
. The projection has contributions from both equations, but the requirement that
p\neq n
ensures that the subleading terms do not affect the original determination of
\lambda_{n}
.
Due to the triangular inequality on the integral of three polynomials, the nonlinear term of the Polchinski equation contributes only if
p
is bound to
0\leq p\leq 2n-1
.
The solution to the equation is

\alpha_{2p,n}=\frac{(n-1)^{2}(n!)^{3}2^{n-p-1}(2n-p)}{n(p!)^{2}(n-p)(2n-p)!}\,,

(A.12)

and analytic continuation of this formula verifies that
\alpha_{2p,n}=0
if
p\geq 2n
.
Finally, the correction to the leading term, that is, the
\alpha_{2n,n}
contribution, can be obtained by including the case
p=n
and solving eq. (
A.2
) to order
\epsilon^{3}

\alpha_{2n,n}=\frac{n-1}{2}-\frac{(2n)!\lambda_{n}}{(n-1)^{2}}\sum_{p\neq n}\frac{2^{p+1}p(2p)!}{(p!)^{2}(2n-p)!}\alpha_{2p,n}\,,

(A.13)

which, together with the previous solutions, confirms the result reported in Ref.
[
42
]
.
The final solution is

w(x)=\epsilon\lambda_{n}H_{2n}(x)+\epsilon^{2}\lambda_{n}\sum_{p\geq 0}^{2n-1}\alpha_{2p,n}H_{2p}(x)+O(\epsilon^{3})\,,

(A.14)

with the coefficients given in Eqs. (
A.9
), (
A.12
) and (
A.13
). The potential
v(\varphi)
can be obtained easily by substituting
x=\frac{1}{2}(d-2)^{1/2}\varphi=(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\sqrt{n-1}}-\frac{\sqrt{n-1}\epsilon}{4\sqrt{2}})\varphi+O(\epsilon^{2})
and expanding once more in
\epsilon
.

We can estimate the convergence of the expansion as a function of
x
by comparing the leading contribution versus the strongest subleading one.
The highest value of the number
p
is
2n-1
, so the dominating subleading term is
H_{4n-2}(x)
, and the comparison gives

\epsilon H_{2n}\sim\epsilon^{2}H_{4n-2}
. At large
x
we have that
H_{p}(x)\propto x^{p}
, which means that the expansion fails when
\epsilon x^{2n-2}\sim 1
. By fixing
\epsilon
to a small but finite value this conforms with the notion that the solution enters a nonperturbative large-field regime and the expansion in terms of Hermite polynomials is no longer valid.
Notice also that, because of our rescaling
\varphi\propto\Delta^{-1/2}x
, where
\Delta
is the dimension of the operator
\varphi
, so in any limit in which
\Delta\to 0
(e.g., when
d\to 2
in the LPA) a finite
\varphi
requires a large
x
, which inevitably brings the equation in its nonperturbative regime.

The analysis of fluctuations around the given fixed point
w(x)
solution can be set up as a Hamiltonian problem. Let
v(\varphi)\to v(\varphi)+{\rm e}^{-\theta t}\delta v(\varphi)
in eq. (
A.1
), where
\delta v(\varphi)
is a deformations of the fixed point solution with critical exponent
\theta
(
t=-\log\Lambda
instead of
t=\log k
because
\Lambda
is an ultraviolet scale). After the same rescaling of the field
\varphi
, the fluctuation obeys the linear equation

{\cal L}\,\delta w\equiv\hat{\theta}\delta w(x)-\left(x+w^{\prime}(x)\right)\delta w^{\prime}(x)+\frac{1}{2}\delta w^{\prime\prime}(x)=0\,,

(A.15)

where

\hat{\theta}=\frac{2(d-\theta)}{d-2}
(the notation in the same as Ref.
[
42
]
).
This is a Hamiltonian problem

{\cal H}\delta w=\hat{\theta}\delta w\,,\qquad\qquad{\cal H}\equiv-\frac{1}{2}\frac{{\rm d}^{2}}{{\rm d}x^{2}}+\left(x+w^{\prime}(x)\right)\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}x}\,,

(A.16)

with eigenvalue
\hat{\theta}.

In the limit
\epsilon\to 0
the solution is such that
w(x)\to 0
, then the requirement that fluctuations are also polynomially bounded gives a spectrum bounded from below with leading contributions

\delta w(x)\approx H_{r}(x)
and

\hat{\theta}\approx r\in\mathbb{N}
, which is the spectrum of fluctuations of the Gaussian fixed point. Notice that
r
is a natural number (not necessarily even) such that both even and odd deformations are included.
At the leading nontrivial order in
\epsilon
, we parametrize

\delta w(x)=H_{r}(x)+O(\epsilon)\,,\qquad\qquad\hat{\theta}_{r}=r+\epsilon\,\hat{\theta}_{r}^{(1)}+O(\epsilon^{2})\,.

(A.17)

Obviously the spectrum is not degenerate as long as
\epsilon
is sufficiently small.
In order to determine
\hat{\theta}_{r}^{(1)}
, we project
\left(H_{r},{\cal L}\delta w\right)=0
and solve
the order
\epsilon
equation.
The result is expressed in general as

\hat{\theta}_{r}^{(1)}=\frac{2(n-1)^{2}k!n!}{(2n)!(r-n)!}\,,

(A.18)

and it is easy to verify that
\hat{\theta}_{r}^{(1)}=0
when
r<n
by analytic continuation.
Further corrections to the eigenfunctions would require standard quantum mechanical perturbation theory, but as long as we are interested in the leading order corrections to the critical exponents, then linear perturbation theory is sufficient. For the exponent
\theta_{r}
we find, inverting the expression of
\hat{\theta}_{r}
in terms of
\theta_{r}

and expanding
d
,

\theta_{r}=\frac{r-2n}{1-n}+\epsilon\left(\frac{r}{2}-1-\frac{\hat{\theta}_{r}^{(1)}}{n-1}\right)+O(\epsilon^{2})=\frac{r-2n}{1-n}+\epsilon\left(\frac{r}{2}-1-\frac{2(n-1)r!n!}{(2n)!(r-n)!}\right)+O(\epsilon^{2})\,,

(A.19)

for
r<2n
.
Further results and a discussion on the universal character of the above results can be found in Ref.
[
42
]
.

A.3
Odd models

Now we build on the method of Ref.
[
42

by relaxing the requirement that the solutions in eq. (

As such, we can include the cases in which

is odd, i.e.
n\to 2n+1
. This reveals the critical dimensions of the odd models
d_{2n+1}=\frac{4n+2}{2n-1}
. The expansion
d=d_{2n+1}-\epsilon

shares some similarities with the even case, but also some key differences in the form of the expansion and the order in which equations are solved that must be taken into account carefully.

To understand the structure of the expansion first, we first take

w(x)\approx cH_{2n+1}(x)
in eq. (
A.2
)
and project the result onto
H_{2n+1}(x)
itself using the Hermite norm (
3.13
).
The term
w^{\prime}(x)^{2}\propto H_{2n}(x)^{2}
is an even polynomial, so it does not contribute to the projected equation and we would assume that
c=0
is the only solution. However, the fact that
w^{\prime}(x)^{2}
is even implies that a nonzero
c
generates even subleading terms proportional to
c^{2}
, which, if taken into account, contribute to
w^{\prime}(x)^{2}
with odd polynomials proportional to
c^{3}
.
The net result is that
c
satisfies a cubic equation which, schematically, is of the form
-\epsilon c+c^{3}=0
with two nontrivial complex conjugate roots proportional to
\pm i\sqrt{\epsilon}
as long as
\epsilon>0
.
This suggests that the proper parameter for the expansion of the fixed point solutions is actually
\sqrt{\epsilon}
, rather than
\epsilon
, in the case of the odd models. This also agrees with the expectations coming from perturbation theory and dimensional regularization
[
32
]
.

In view of the preliminary considerations, we take the ansatz

w(x)=\sqrt{\epsilon}\lambda_{n}H_{2n+1}(x)+\epsilon\lambda_{n}^{2}\sum_{q=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{q,n}H_{q}(x)+O(\epsilon^{3/2})\,,

(A.20)

where the power
\lambda_{n}^{2}
in front of the subleading term is chosen to make the equations for the subleading corrections homogeneous in analogy to the even case.
By construction, the ansatz solves eq. (
A.3
)
with
d=d_{2n-1}-\epsilon

at the leading order in
\epsilon^{1/2}
, but
\lambda_{n}
is still arbitrary.
In order to solve the equation at order
\epsilon
we perform the projection
on
H_{p}
for arbitrary

, but

turns out to be even for the reasons discussed above. The computation requires the integral of three Hermite polynomials, thus

is also bound to satisfy a triangular inequality.
Solving
\left(H_{2p},\Lambda\partial_{\Lambda}v\right)=0
, we find

\alpha_{2p,n}=\frac{2^{2n-p+1}(2n+1)^{2}(2n)!^{2}}{(2n-2p+1)p!^{2}}\,,

(A.21)

and holds for
0\leq p\leq 2n
, which is also confirmed by analytic continuation, while
\alpha_{2p+1,n}=0
.

Notice that

A solution for

Appendix B
Large-field analysis of the fixed point equations at
\Delta\neq 0

The asymptotic behaviour of
\tilde{u}
and
\tilde{h}
at large
\tilde{\varphi}
can be classified according to the possible behaviours of the non-linear RHS of (
4.2
), namely:

1.

diverging RHS (unbounded potential);

2.

constant RHS (Gaussian family);

3.

vanishing RHS (non-trivial potential).

We do not consider the possibility that the RHS (or the potential itself) has no limit for
\tilde{\varphi}\to+\infty
, or in other words that it settles into an oscillatory regime, because we have found no hints of such behaviour at
\Delta\neq 0
.
One practical explanation is that any periodic behaviour of terms involving
\tilde{v}(\tilde{\varphi})
and
\tilde{v}^{\prime\prime}(\tilde{\varphi})
will in general be spoiled by the
\tilde{\varphi}\,\tilde{v}^{\prime}(\tilde{\varphi})
term.

Note that the behaviour of the RHS is mostly dominated by the denominator common to both ODEs (
4.2
).
Thus, in general the RHS of the two ODEs present the same behaviour at large
\tilde{\varphi}
, although the behavior of
\tilde{u}
and
\tilde{h}
might differ, as we will see.

B.1
Diverging RHS

Because of the different powers in the numerator and denominator, the RHS of (
4.2
) can only diverge in the large
\tilde{\varphi}
limit when the square norm of the shifted potential vanishes
\left|1+\tilde{v}^{\prime\prime}\right|^{2}=(1+\tilde{u}^{\prime\prime})^{2}+(\tilde{h}^{\prime\prime})^{2}\rightarrow 0
, or in other words, when
\tilde{u}^{\prime\prime}\rightarrow A
and
\tilde{h}^{\prime\prime}\rightarrow B
, with
A
,
B\in\mathbb{R}
such that
(1+A)^{2}+B^{2}=0
.
The unique solution is given by
A=-1
and
B=0
.

The leading order in the large
\tilde{\varphi}
limit of the potential corresponding to this behaviour is

\tilde{u}(\tilde{\varphi})\sim-\frac{1}{2}\tilde{\varphi}^{2},\quad\tilde{h}(\tilde{\varphi})\sim B_{1}\tilde{\varphi},\ B_{1}\in\mathbb{R}.

(B.1)

The subleading behaviour can be obtained by inverting the LHS and RHS of (
4.2
)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\frac{\left(\tilde{h}^{\prime\prime}\right)^{2}+\left(1+\tilde{u}^{\prime\prime}\right)^{2}}{1+\tilde{u}^{\prime\prime}}=\,\frac{1}{u-\gamma\,\tilde{\varphi}\,\tilde{u}^{\prime}}\\
\frac{\left(\tilde{h}^{\prime\prime}\right)^{2}+\left(1+\tilde{u}^{\prime\prime}\right)^{2}}{\tilde{h}^{\prime\prime}}=-\,\frac{1}{h-\gamma\,\tilde{\varphi}\,\tilde{h}^{\prime}}\\
\end{array}\right.

(B.2)

and plugging back the leading behaviour.
The general solution is then given by

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\tilde{u}(\tilde{\varphi})=-\frac{1}{2}\tilde{\varphi}^{2}+A_{1}\,\tilde{\varphi}+\frac{2}{1-2\gamma}\ln\left(\tilde{\varphi}\right)+A_{2}+{O}\left(\frac{1}{\tilde{\varphi}}\right)\\
\tilde{h}(\tilde{\varphi})=B_{1}\tilde{\varphi}+B_{2}+{O}\left(\frac{1}{\tilde{\varphi}}\right).\end{array}\right.

(B.3)

The asymptotic solution is determined by four free parameters
A_{1},A_{2},B_{1},B_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
as expected for two second-order ODEs.

Since the real part of the potential is unbounded from below, these solutions are unphysical.

B.2
Constant RHS

We now consider the case of the RHS tending to a nonvanishing constant. Because of the different powers in the numerator and denominator, we must have

(1+\tilde{u}^{\prime\prime})^{2}+(\tilde{h}^{\prime\prime})^{2}\rightarrow\,
constant, i.e.
\tilde{u}^{\prime\prime}\rightarrow A
and
\tilde{h}^{\prime\prime}\rightarrow B
, with
A,B\in\mathbb{R}
such that
(1+A)^{2}+B^{2}=\ \text{const}\neq 0
.
Then, expanding the RHS at large
\tilde{\varphi}
and keeping the leading behaviour, we find

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\tilde{u}-\gamma\,\tilde{\varphi}\,\tilde{u}^{\prime}\simeq\,\frac{1+A}{\left(1+A\right)^{2}+B^{2}}\\
\tilde{h}-\gamma\,\tilde{\varphi}\,\tilde{h}^{\prime}\simeq-\,\frac{B}{\left(1+A\right)^{2}+B^{2}},\end{array}\right.

(B.4)

with general solutions

\tilde{u}(\tilde{\varphi})=a\,\tilde{\varphi}^{\frac{1}{\gamma}}+\frac{1+A}{\left(1+A\right)^{2}+B^{2}}+o(1),\quad\tilde{h}(\tilde{\varphi})=b\,\tilde{\varphi}^{\frac{1}{\gamma}}-\frac{B}{\left(1+A\right)^{2}+B^{2}}+o(1),

(B.5)

for some parameters
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
.
However, these solutions are consistent with the assumed behaviour of the second derivatives if and only if one of the following situation is realized:

1.

\gamma=1/2
, and
a=A
and
b=B
;

2.

0<\gamma<1/2
, and
a=b=A=B=0
;

3.

\gamma>1/2
or
\gamma<0
, and
A=B=0
, but
a
and
b
arbitrary.

The first case corresponds to
\Delta=d/2
, and we will not consider it further because it does not occur in the models we are interested in.

In the other two cases we always have
A=B=0
, hence we can write

\tilde{u}(\tilde{\varphi})=1+\delta\tilde{u}(\tilde{\varphi}),\quad\tilde{h}(\tilde{\varphi})=\delta\tilde{h}(\tilde{\varphi}),\qquad|\delta\tilde{u}^{\prime\prime}|,\,|\delta\tilde{h}^{\prime\prime}|\ll 1.

(B.6)

In fact, we notice immediately that
\delta\tilde{u}=\delta\tilde{h}=0
is an exact solution: the Gaussian fixed point (GFP), with constant potential
V(\varphi)=1
.

24

24

Notice that a vanishing potential is not a solution because in the way the Wetterich equation is derived the measure is normalized with respect to the unmodified quadratic part (i.e. without
R_{k}
).

We can check whether the GFP solution is an isolated global solution by substituting (
B.6
) in (
4.2
) and expanding the RHS in
\delta\tilde{u}^{\prime\prime}
and
\delta\tilde{h}^{\prime\prime}
.
At linear order, the resulting equations are

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\delta\tilde{u}-\gamma\,\tilde{\varphi}\,\delta\tilde{u}^{\prime}=-\delta\tilde{u}^{\prime\prime}\\
\delta\tilde{h}-\gamma\,\tilde{\varphi}\,\delta\tilde{h}^{\prime}=-\delta\tilde{h}^{\prime\prime}.\end{array}\right.

(B.7)

These are solved by linear combinations of
{}_{1}F_{1}(-1/2\gamma,1/2,\tilde{\varphi}^{2}\gamma/2)
and
\tilde{\varphi}\,{}_{1}F_{1}((\gamma-1)/2\gamma,3/2,\tilde{\varphi}^{2}\gamma/2)
, whose asymptotic behaviour for
\tilde{\varphi}\to\infty
is a linear combination of
\tilde{\varphi}^{-1-1/\gamma}\exp(\tilde{\varphi}^{2}\gamma/2)
and
\tilde{\varphi}^{1/\gamma}
.
Their fate depends on the value of
\gamma
:

•

For or
0<\gamma<1/2
, both solutions are in contradiction with the hypothesis that
|\delta\tilde{u}^{\prime\prime}|
and
|\delta\tilde{h}^{\prime\prime}|
are small at large
\tilde{\varphi}
, hence they must be discarded and thus we conclude that the GFP is an isolated global solution.
In this case, if we want to find nontrivial global solutions, we need to change working hypothesis, as we will do in the next subsection.

•

For
\gamma>1/2
, the solutions are either exponentially growing, and thus to be discarded, or they are of the type in (
B.5
), with
A=B=0
. We thus find a 2-parameter family of solutions, with parameters
a
and
b
in (
B.5
).

•

For
\gamma<0
, the exponential solutions are decaying ones, hence they are allowed.
We thus find a 4-parameter family of solutions, with parameters
a
and
b
in (
B.5
), plus two parameters for the exponential corrections.

B.3
Vanishing RHS

The last case we consider is when both RHS of (
4.2
) vanish, which happens if and only if
(1+\tilde{u}^{\prime\prime})^{2}+(\tilde{h}^{\prime\prime})^{2}\rightarrow\infty
. We can further decompose this case in three sub-cases:

•

both
\tilde{u}^{\prime\prime}
and
\tilde{h}^{\prime\prime}
diverge, leading to a complex asymptotic solution,

•

only
\tilde{u}^{\prime\prime}
diverges, leading to a purely real asymptotic solution, and

•

only
\tilde{h}^{\prime\prime}
diverges, leading to a purely imaginary leading behaviour but complex subleading contributions.

For all cases, given a vanishing RHS, the two ODEs (
4.2
) decouple at leading order in
\tilde{\varphi}
and they reduce to the classical scaling equation (LHS equal zero), which is solved by

\tilde{u}(\tilde{\varphi})\sim A\tilde{\varphi}^{\frac{1}{\gamma}},\quad\tilde{h}(\tilde{\varphi})\sim B\tilde{\varphi}^{\frac{1}{\gamma}}.

(B.8)

The assumption that at least one of the second derivatives of the potential diverges implies that

\frac{1}{\gamma}>2\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad d>2\Delta>0\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad 2-d<\eta<2.

(B.9)

Otherwise, for
-\infty<\frac{1}{\gamma}\leq 2
, we end up again in the previous case of asymptotically constant RHS.

To compute the subleading contributions in the large
\tilde{\varphi}
limit up to some order
n
, we consider the two parameters ansatz:

\tilde{u}(\tilde{\varphi})=A\tilde{\varphi}^{\frac{1}{\gamma}}+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{k}a_{j,k}(A,B)\tilde{\varphi}^{2j-\frac{1}{\gamma}k},\quad\tilde{h}(\tilde{\varphi})=B\tilde{\varphi}^{\frac{1}{\gamma}}+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{k}b_{j,k}(A,B)\tilde{\varphi}^{2j-\frac{1}{\gamma}k}.

(B.10)

The coupled ODEs can be solved order per order by feeding iteratively the RHS with the ansatz, keeping the leading behaviour in
\tilde{\varphi}
and solving for the coefficients
a_{j,k}
and
b_{j,k}
.

It can be checked via a linearization test
[
41
]
, that we are not missing other free parameters in the solutions.

For the linear perturbations we can consider the exponential ansatz

\delta\tilde{u}(\tilde{\varphi})=Ae^{S_{u}(\tilde{\varphi})}
,
\delta\tilde{h}(\tilde{\varphi})=Be^{S_{h}(\tilde{\varphi})}

and using the method of dominant balance, it can then be shown the linearized equations lead to the contradiction

and

\tilde{u}^{\prime\prime}\rightarrow\infty

\tilde{h}^{\prime\prime}\rightarrow\infty

In this case, the potential is fully complex, with a non-trivial contribution for both the real and imaginary parts:

\displaystyle\tilde{u}(\tilde{\varphi})=A\tilde{\varphi}^{\frac{1}{\gamma}}+\frac{A\gamma^{2}}{2(A^{2}+B^{2})(1-\gamma)^{2}}\tilde{\varphi}^{2-\frac{1}{\gamma}}-\frac{\left(A^{2}-B^{2}\right)\gamma^{4}}{(A^{2}+B^{2})^{2}(1-\gamma)^{2}(3-4\gamma)}\tilde{\varphi}^{4-2\frac{1}{\gamma}}+\dots{\\
}\tilde{h}(\tilde{\varphi})=B\tilde{\varphi}^{\frac{1}{\gamma}}-\frac{B\gamma^{2}}{2(A^{2}+B^{2})(1-\gamma)^{2}}\tilde{\varphi}^{2-\frac{1}{\gamma}}+\frac{2AB\gamma^{4}}{(A^{2}+B^{2})^{2}(1-\gamma)^{2}(3-4\gamma)}\tilde{\varphi}^{4-2\frac{1}{\gamma}}+\dots{\\

Notice that the real part is even under the

transformation

, while the imaginary part is odd. There is no symmetry in
A
instead, because the equations (

) depend on the combination
1+u^{\prime\prime}
.

•

\tilde{u}^{\prime\prime}\rightarrow\infty
,
\tilde{h}^{\prime\prime}\rightarrow 0

The condition

,
\tilde{h}^{\prime\prime}\rightarrow c<\infty
, is inconsistent with the second equation in (

), unless

.
At leading order, we can then ignore

Plugging this back in the second equation in (

\tilde{u}^{\prime\prime}\rightarrow 0

Proceeding similarly to the previous case, we now find a solution that corresponds to setting

Appendix C
Quadratic truncation of the real part

With a purely imaginary potential, the real part of the coupled flow equation (
2.10
) is violated even for small values of
\phi
.
We can try to improve approximation of the coupled system by including a non-trivial real even part to the potential.
As a first improvement, we can introduce a quadratic polynomial for the real part:

u(\phi)=\lambda_{0}+\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{2}\,\varphi^{2}.

(C.1)

Following the usual approximation scheme of polynomial truncations of the FRG, we replace this ansatz in the flow equation, expand it in powers of
\varphi
, and truncate such expansion to quadratic order, effectively discarding the generation of higher-order terms.

Then, the real part of (
2.10
) is cast into fixed-point equations for the beta functions of the real couplings, and after imposing the initial conditions (
2.11
)

\displaystyle\beta_{\lambda_{0}}

\displaystyle=-d\lambda_{0}+\frac{c_{d}}{1+\lambda_{2}}\left(1-\frac{\eta}{d+2}\right)=0,

(C.2)

\displaystyle\beta_{\lambda_{2}}

\displaystyle=\left(2\Delta-d\right)\lambda_{2}-2c_{d}\frac{g^{2}}{(1+\lambda_{2})^{3}}\left(1-\frac{\eta}{d+2}\right)=0.

For simplicity, the above system of equations is then solved for
g
, keeping the coupling
\lambda_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
as our free parameter.
In the LPA,
\eta=0
, and the solutions reduce to:

\lambda_{0}=\frac{c_{d}}{d(1+\lambda_{2})},\quad g=\sqrt{-\frac{\lambda_{2}(1+\lambda_{2})^{3}}{c_{d}}},\quad-1<\lambda_{2}\leq 0,

(C.3)

while in the LPA’,

\displaystyle\lambda_{0}

\displaystyle=c_{d}\,\frac{2+\lambda_{2}+\sqrt{\frac{2(2+\lambda_{2})^{2}+d(2+3\lambda_{2})^{2}}{2+d}}}{4d(1+\lambda_{2})^{2}},\quad\eta=-c_{d}\frac{g^{2}}{(1+\lambda_{2})^{4}},\quad-1<\lambda_{2}\leq 0

(C.4)

\displaystyle g

\displaystyle=\sqrt{-\frac{(1+\lambda_{2})^{3}}{c_{d}}\left((2+d)(2+3\lambda_{2})-\sqrt{2+d}\sqrt{2(2+\lambda_{2})^{2}+d(2+3\lambda_{2})^{3}}\right)}.

Note that (
C.2
) is a second order polynomial equation in
g^{2}
, resulting in spurious solutions.
We lift the ambiguity by choosing the solution consistent with the GFP in
d=6
.
The condition on the parameter
\lambda_{2}
ensues from the reality of
g
.

In the LPA’, the full scaling dimension is negative for

\Delta\leq 0\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad-1<\lambda_{2}\leq-\frac{8-4d}{6-5d},\ d\geq 2.

(C.5)

Noteworthily, the imaginary part of the differential equation (
2.10
)

d\,h-\Delta\,\varphi\,h^{\prime}=-c_{d}\left(1-\frac{\eta}{d+2}\right)\frac{h^{\prime\prime}}{(1+\lambda_{2})^{2}+\left(h^{\prime\prime}\right)^{2}},\quad\eta=-c_{d}\frac{g^{2}}{(1+\lambda^{2})^{4}},

(C.6)

is only modified by a constant term in the denominator on the right-hand side and can be mapped to (
) through the rescaling:

\varphi\rightarrow\tilde{\varphi}=(1+\lambda_{2})\,\varphi,\quad h(\varphi)\rightarrow\tilde{h}(\tilde{\varphi})=(1+\lambda_{2})\,h(\varphi).

(C.7)

Therefore, we conclude that fixed-point equation for
h(\varphi)
with a quadratic truncation of the real part is equivalent to the one with real part set to zero.

Higher-order truncations of the real part lead to a similar mixed system of beta functions for the
\lambda_{2n}
couplings and beta functional for
h(\varphi)
, and they cannot be reduced to the pure-imaginary case by a rescaling.
However, as explained in section
5
, they lead to difficulties in the search of global solutions for
h(\varphi)
, hence we do not consider them.
