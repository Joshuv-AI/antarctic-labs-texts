INHBA promotes chemoresistance in pancreatic cancer by enhancing CTPS1 stability and mediating pyrimidine metabolism
Authors: Xinghao Sun, Zikai Wei, Xingkun Liao, Dijie Zheng, Futang Li, Liwen Chen, Yanyu Gong, Suye Ran, Xiaozhao Han, Zhiwei He, Chao Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGemcitabine is the primary chemotherapeutic agent used as a first-line treatment for pancreatic cancer (PC), underscoring its pivotal role in cancer treatment. This drug induces apoptosis through multiple mechanisms, with the disruption of cellular pyrimidine metabolism being a crucial mode of action. However, gemcitabine resistance significantly limits the long-term efficacy of PC treatments. Our study aimed to identify potential targets for gemcitabine sensitization.MethodsWe identified a gene set associated with pyrimidine metabolism through cluster analysis of The Cancer Genome Atlas Pancreatic Adenocarcinoma database. Intersecting this set of differentially upregulated genes in PC with normal tissues revealed inhibin βA (INHBA) as a gene related to pyrimidine metabolism. Quantitative reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction, western blotting, and immunohistochemistry analyses confirmed INHBA overexpression in PC cells. INHBA’s effects of INHBA on cell proliferation and chemoresistance in PC cells were validated in vitro using a colony counting kit-8 assay, drug sensitivity analysis, EdU incorporation, flow cytometry, and colony formation assay. The proliferation and chemoresistance promotion by INHBA in PC cells was validated in vivo using a mouse xenograft model. Downstream targets of INHBA were identified using immunoprecipitation mass spectrometry.ResultsINHBA, a pyrimidine metabolism-associated gene, was highly expressed in PC, and its expression correlated with gemcitabine resistance. INHBA interacts with cytidine triphosphate synthase 1 (CTPS1) and competitively inhibits SMAD Specific E3 Ubiquitin Protein Ligase 1 mediated ubiquitination, enhancing CTPS1 stability and promoting pyrimidine metabolism.ConclusionINHBA promotes gemcitabine resistance in PC by stabilizing CTPS1 and enhancing pyrimidine metabolism, making it a potential target for chemosensitization in PC.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12935-025-03984-8.

Introduction

Pancreatic cancer (PC) is a prevalent malignant neoplasm of the gastrointestinal tract, and its incidence has been steadily increasing. Owing to the highly invasive nature of PC, the 5-year survival rate remains below 10% [1]. Radical resection remains the most effective and only curative option for patients with resectable and borderline resectable PC [2]. However, approximately 80% of patients with PC are diagnosed at an advanced stage with distant metastasis, rendering them ineligible for surgery [3]. Adjuvant and systemic chemotherapy are the primary treatment approaches for patients with advanced-stage disease. The use of gemcitabine as a first-line treatment for PC is usually limited by the development of resistance [4–6]. Thus, overcoming gemcitabine resistance in patients with PC is a critical clinical challenge.

Studies have shown that the resistance of PC cells to gemcitabine involves multiple mechanisms, including alterations in antiapoptotic pathways, enhanced DNA repair mechanisms, and abnormalities in nucleotide metabolism. Recent studies have indicated that reprogramming of pyrimidine metabolism also contributes to the development of gemcitabine resistance [7, 8]. Gemcitabine is converted into its active forms, 2’,2’-difluorodeoxycytidine diphosphate (dFdCDP) and 2’,2’-difluorodeoxycytidine triphosphate (dFdCTP). dFdCDP inhibits ribonucleotide reductase, whereas dFdCTP competes with deoxycytidine triphosphate (dCTP) for integration into DNA. This integration inhibits DNA synthesis and triggers apoptosis in the host cell. In gemcitabine-resistant PC cells, enhanced de novo pyrimidine biosynthesis enriches the dCTP pool and diminishes dFdCTP competitive binding, thereby mitigating its inhibitory effect on DNA synthesis. Cytidine triphosphate synthase 1 (CTPS1) is a crucial enzyme involved in pyrimidine metabolism that is responsible for the synthesis of cytidine triphosphate (CTP), a pyrimidine nucleotide. CTPS1 helps maintain an adequate dCTP pool, contributing to gemcitabine resistance [9]. Therefore, further investigation into the regulatory mechanisms of CTPS1 in pyrimidine metabolism may provide new insights into overcoming chemotherapy resistance.

Inhibin βA (INHBA) is a subunit of activin and inhibin A and a member of the transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) superfamily [10]. It is involved in various biological processes, including cell proliferation, differentiation, immune regulation, and embryonic development [11]. In most cases, INHBA forms activin A by combining with the INHBB subunit [12]. Furthermore, it activates the downstream Smad2/Smad3 signaling pathway by binding to serine/threonine kinase receptors, thereby regulating cell growth and differentiation [13]. Although increasing evidence suggests that high INHBA expression is associated with chemoresistance in various malignancies, its role in PC remains unexplored [14–18]. Therefore, investigating the underlying mechanisms of INHBA could foster the development of innovative therapeutic approaches for the management of PC.

In this study, we explored the crucial role of INHBA in PC chemoresistance, aiming to elucidate the regulatory mechanisms by which INHBA promotes chemoresistance in PC and provide a potential therapeutic target for PC treatment.

Materials and methods

Bioinformatics analysis

Consistency clustering of TCGA-PAAD was performed using the R packages “limma” and “ConsensusClusterPlus.” The association between clustering and overall survival (OS) was analyzed using the R package “survival.” The results were visualized as heatmaps and Kaplan-Meier (KM) curves using the R packages “pheatmap,” “survival,” and “survminer.” Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the two clusters were identified using the “limma” package, with the criteria of |log fold change (FC)| >1 and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05. The angiogenesis-related gene set was extracted from the gene expression data to calculate the Angiogenesis Score, which was then normalized using the “limma” package. Immune cell infiltration scores were calculated using the TIDE scoring method, and the “limma” package was used to evaluate the differences in immune cell infiltration and immune evasion potential between the two subtypes. The ssGSEA (single-sample gene set enrichment analysis) was used to predict immune cell infiltration in the samples. The enrichment scores were calculated using the GSVA package in R, resulting in the ssGSEA Predicts Immune Cell Score.

Sample collection

A total of 34 PC patients from the Affiliated Hospital of Guizhou Medical University were enrolled, and tumor tissues along with matched adjacent normal tissues were collected. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before tissue collection.

Cell culture

Five cell lines (AsPC-1, BxPC-3, PANC-1, MIA PaCa-2, SW1990) and normal human pancreatic ductal epithelial cells (hTERT-HPNE) were co-cultured. All cells were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences. For hTERT-HPNE, PANC-1, MIA PaCa-2, and SW1990 cells, the culture conditions included DMEM (Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gemini). AsPC-1 and BxPC-3 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 (Gibco) with 10% FBS. All cells were grown in a Humidified environment at 37 °C and 5% CO2.

Stabilized cell line and plasmid construction and transfection

The lentiviruses included the negative control lentiviral vector (Lv-NC), INHBA overexpression lentivirus (Lv-INHBA), and INHBA knockdown lentiviruses (sh-NC, sh-INHBA #1, sh-INHBA #2), all of which were purchased from Shanghai GeneChem Co., Ltd. INHBA (si-NC, si-INHBA #1, si-INHBA #2, si-INHBA#3), SMURF1 (si-SMURF1#1, si-SMURF1#2), and CTPS1 (OE-CTPS1, si-CTPS1#1, si-CTPS1#2) were purchased from Guangzhou RiboBio Co., Ltd. The Myc-SMURF1, HA-CTPS1, Flag-INHBA plasmids, and ubiquitin (HA-Ub) overexpression plasmids (HA-WT, K48, K63) were purchased from Shanghai GeneChem Co., Ltd. Plasmid transfection was performed using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, USA) and Opti-MEM (Gibco, USA). The Sh/Si-RNA sequences and plasmid sequences used are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Antibodies and reagents

In the study, various antibodies were used, including those for Western blot (WB), immunohistochemistry (IHC), immunofluorescence (IF), and immunoprecipitation (IP): INHBA (17524-1-AP, Proteintech), γ-H2AX (10856-1-AP, Proteintech), GAPDH (10494-1-AP, Proteintech), CTPS1 (15914-1-AP, Proteintech), Flag, HA (66006–2-Ig, Proteintech), MYC (60003–2-Ig, Proteintech), SMURF1 (55175-1-AP, Proteintech), MDM2 (27883-1-AP, Proteintech), SYVN1 (13473-1-AP, Proteintech), and Ub (10201-2-AP, Proteintech). The secondary antibodies used included mouse anti-IgG (SA00009-1, Proteintech) and rabbit anti-IgG (SA00013-4, Proteintech). The chemical reagents used in the experiments included CHX (MCE, USA), MG132 (MCE, USA), and DAPI (Solarbio, China).

Immunohistochemistry and multiple fluorescence immunohistochemistry

The tissue samples were first fixed and dehydrated, followed by paraffin embedding. During sectioning, the thickness was set to 4 microns. Subsequently, deparaffinization was performed using xylene, and hydration was carried out through an ethanol gradient. Antigen retrieval was performed using EDTA (P0085, Beyotime). Subsequently, blocking was performed using hydrogen peroxide blocking reagent (P0081, Beyotime). The primary antibody was incubated overnight at 4 °C, followed by washing with PBS. The samples were then incubated with the secondary antibody at room temperature for 1 h. Imaging was performed using standard protocols, with hematoxylin counterstaining and DAB staining. Immunohistochemical (IHC) scoring was independently performed by two pathologists, based on the staining intensity and the percentage of positive cells. The scoring criteria for staining intensity were as follows: no staining (0 points), weak staining (1 point), moderate staining (2 points), and strong staining (3 points. The scoring criteria for the percentage of positive cells were as follows: <1% (0 points), 1–25% (1 point), 26–50% (2 points), 51–75% (3 points), and > 75% (4 points). The final score was obtained by multiplying the staining intensity score by the percentage of positive cells. A final score below 7 was classified as low expression, while a score of 7 or higher was classified as high expression. The procedure for multiplex fluorescence immunohistochemistry (IHC) is similar to that of conventional IHC, with the addition of incubation steps for the staining solutions. After antibody staining, DAPI was used to stain the cell nuclei, and the slides were mounted with an anti-fade reagent to preserve the fluorescence. Between each step, washing was performed using TBST. Finally, the fluorescence intensity of the samples was observed using a laser scanning confocal microscope. To stratify patients by INHBA expression levels, we determined an optimal cutoff value using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. The threshold with the maximum Youden index was identified as 7, and thus patients were categorized into high INHBA expression (IHC score ≥ 7) and low expression (IHC score < 7) groups for survival analysis.

Hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining

The pancreatic cancerpatient samples obtained from the Affiliated Hospital of Guizhou Medical University were fixed, dehydrated, and paraffin-embedded, followed by sectioning. Subsequently, the sections underwent deparaffinization and rehydration, followed by hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. After staining, the sections were dehydrated, cleared with xylene, and mounted with neutral resin. Finally, the samples were photographed and recorded under an inverted microscope.

RNA extraction and qRT‑PCR experiments

Total RNA was extracted from PC tissues and cells using the RNA-Quick Purification Kit (RN001, ES Science). Subsequently, the RNA concentration and purity were measured using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer, ensuring that the A260/A280 ratio was within the range of 1.8 to 2.0. Next, RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the PrimeScript RT Master Mix kit (RR036A, Takara). Finally, the mRNA expression levels were analyzed using SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (HY-K0501A, MedChemExpress) and a real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR instrument. The corresponding primer sequences are provided in Supplementary Table 2.

Western blot assay

Cells were lysed on ice for 20 min in RIPA buffer (R0010, Solarbio) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Afterwards, the samples were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected. The protein concentration was determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (PC0020, Solarbio). Proteins (40 µg) were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to a PVDF membrane. The membrane was blocked with 5% non-fat milk at room temperature for 2 h. The primary antibody was incubated overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with the secondary antibody at room temperature for 2 h. The immunoreactive bands were detected using the enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) kit (MA0186-1, MeilunBio).

EdU proliferation assay

PC cells (5 × 10⁴ cells/well) were seeded into a 24-well plate and cultured for one day. Then, 10 µM EdU solution (C0075S, Beyotime) was added and the cells were incubated for 2 h. Afterward, the cells were fixed with 4% formaldehyde at room temperature for 30 min. Following Click reaction and DAPI staining, the cells were observed and recorded using a fluorescence microscope.

Cell proliferation assay - CCK8 assay

The cells were seeded into a 96-well plate and allowed to adhere. At 0, 24, 48, and 72 h, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution (MA0218, MeilunBio) was added to each well, and the cells were incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader.

Clone formation experiment

PC cells (1,000 cells/well) were seeded into a 6-well plate and cultured for 14 days. Afterward, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde solution, followed by staining with 0.3% crystal violet. After washing and drying, the cells were photographed and counted.

Transwell experiments

The PC cells were seeded in the upper chamber of a 24-well plate at a concentration of 3 × 10⁴ cells per well and starved in serum-free medium for 12 h. The lower chamber was filled with 10% FBS DMEM medium. After 72 h of incubation at 37 °C, the migrated PC cells were fixed, stained with crystal violet, and observed and counted using an inverted microscope. For the invasion assay, 60 µL of Matrigel was added to the Transwell chambers, with the remaining procedures identical to those of the migration assay.

Wound healing experiment

The PC cells were cultured in a 6-well plate until the cell density reached approximately 100%. A sterile pipette tip was used to create uniform scratches, ensuring consistent width. The cells were then washed with PBS to remove cell debris and cultured in serum-free medium. At specified time intervals, the cells were observed and photographed under an inverted microscope.

γ-H2AX staining

The cells were first fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, followed by three washes with PBS buffer. Then, the cells were blocked with immunostaining blocking solution at room temperature for 20 min. Then, γ-H2AX antibody (C2035S, Biotronik) was added and incubated overnight at 4 °C. After incubation, the cells were washed three times with PBS, and then incubated with anti-rabbit 488 antibody (C2035S, Beyotime) for 60 min. Subsequently, the cells were washed twice with PBS, each for 5 min, and stained with DAPI (C0065, Solarbio) for 5 min. Finally, the cells were washed three times with PBS and observed under a fluorescence microscope for imaging.

ELISA assay

The experiment was performed using ELISA kits (abx156753-96tests, Abpexa; 27113-IBL, I8L). In a 96-well plate, 100 µL of cell culture medium or serum samples were added to each well. The plate was then incubated overnight at 4 °C or for 2.5 h at room temperature. Next, biotinylated antibodies were added and incubated with the samples for 1 h. After incubation, the plate was washed 4 times with washing buffer to remove any unbound antibodies. Then, Streptavidin-HRP solution was added, and the plate was incubated for an additional 45 min. After the incubation, the plate was washed 4 times with washing buffer. Subsequently, TMB one-step substrate solution was added to each well, and the plate was incubated for 30 min. After incubation, stop solution was added to terminate the reaction. Finally, the absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader, and the readings were normalized at 620 nm to obtain the final results.

Ubiquitination assay

Cells were cultured in a 6-well plate, and when they reached 70%–80% confluency, plasmid transfection was performed. The plasmids used for transfection included Myc-SMURF1 overexpression plasmid, HA-CTPS1 overexpression plasmid, Flag-INHBA overexpression plasmid, and ubiquitin (HA-Ub) overexpression plasmids. The HA-Ub plasmids include the wild-type (HA-WT) and specific constructs (HA-K48, HA-K63) designed to detect K48- and K63-linked ubiquitination. After 8 h of transfection, the culture medium was replaced, and the next day, 20 µM MG132 was added to continue culturing for 6 h. Afterward, cells were detached using trypsinization, and the protein-antibody complex was extracted using immunoprecipitation (Co-IP). Finally, the ubiquitination levels of the relevant proteins were detected using Western blotting.

Cell cycle analysis by flow cytometry

Cell cycle distribution was assessed using the Cell Cycle and Apoptosis Kit (C1052, Beyotime) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells cultured in 6-well plates were digested with trypsin, collected into centrifuge tubes, and washed twice with PBS. The cells were then fixed overnight at 4 °C in pre-cooled 75% ethanol to preserve cellular DNA integrity. Following fixation, cells were centrifuged, washed with PBS, and incubated with RNase A (CA1510, Solarbio) at 37 °C for 30 min to degrade RNA, preventing interference with DNA staining. Cells were subsequently stained with propidium iodide (PI) for 30 min in the dark at 4 °C to allow selective DNA binding. The cell cycle distribution (G0/G1, S, and G2/M phases) was analyzed using a flow cytometer, and data were processed using ModFit LT software.

Cell apoptosis analysis by flow cytometry

Cell apoptosis was evaluated using the Apoptosis Detection Kit (C1062S, Beyotime) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. PC cells were seeded in 6-well plates and subjected to the indicated treatments. After treatment, cells were harvested, washed twice with PBS, and resuspended in Annexin V binding buffer. Apoptotic cells were identified by staining with Annexin V-FITC and 7-AAD for 15 min at room temperature in the dark. Flow cytometry was performed using a FACSCelesta™ multicolor flow cytometer (Becton Dickinson, Franklin Lakes, NJ) to distinguish early apoptotic (Annexin V⁺/7-AAD⁻) and late apoptotic/necrotic (Annexin V⁺/7-AAD⁺) cell populations.For apoptosis quantification based on DNA fragmentation, cells were fixed with 75% ethanol at 4 °C overnight, centrifuged, washed three times with PBS, and incubated with RNase A (CA1510, Solarbio) at 37 °C for 30 min to eliminate RNA contamination. Cells were then stained with propidium iodide (PI) for 30 min at 4 °C in the dark, and the apoptotic sub-G1 population was analyzed using a flow cytometer, with data processing in ModFit LT software.

Subcutaneous tumor formation in nude mice

Six-week-old female BALB/c nude mice (purchased from the Animal Research Center of Guizhou Medical University, China) were randomly divided into four groups: DMSO (Vector, Sh-INHBA#1, Sh-INHBA#2), Gemcitabine (25 mg/kg i. p.) + DMSO (Vector, Sh-INHBA#1, Sh-INHBA#2), DMSO (Vector, OE-INHBA), and Gemcitabine (25 mg/kg i. p.) + DMSO (Vector, OE-INHBA). 2 × 10⁶ stable INHBA-transfected PANC-1 and MIA PaCa-2 cells were subcutaneously injected into the right flank of the mice. Tumor size was measured weekly, and after continuous feeding for 5 weeks, the mice were euthanized by overdose of isoflurane anesthesia to minimize animal suffering. The excised tumors were subsequently used for further immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis. As depicted in the provided schematic, tumor growth monitoring was conducted systematically. All animal studies were approved by the Ethical Committee for Laboratory Animals of Guizhou Medical University. The methods used in the animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the relevant guidelines.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analysis and graph generation were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 10, GraphPad) and R (version 4.4.0). For skewed distribution data, the median (interquartile range) was used. For normally distributed data, the mean (standard deviation) was used. The comparison between two groups was performed using the Student’s t-test (two-tailed), and multiple group comparisons were conducted using analysis of variance (ANOVA). Survival analysis was performed using the Kaplan-Meier method, and multivariate analysis was conducted using the Cox proportional hazards model to explore the relationship between INHBA expression and survival time in PC patients. A P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Establishment of liver metastasis model

To investigate the role of INHBA in liver metastasis of pancreatic cancer under gemcitabine treatment, we established an in vivo metastasis model using BALB/c nude mice. PANC-1 or MIA PaCa-2 cells with stable INHBA knockdown or overexpression were cultured and resuspended in serum-free medium. A total of 1 × 10⁶ cells were injected into the spleen of 6–8-week-old male BALB/c nude mice. This method allows tumor cells to enter the portal circulation and colonize the liver, mimicking the hematogenous route of liver metastasis. Starting one day after cell injection, gemcitabine (25 mg/kg) was administered intraperitoneally every other day for two weeks. Four weeks later, the mice were sacrificed, and their livers were harvested for gross imaging and quantification of visible surface metastatic nodules.

dCTP rescue assay

To determine whether exogenous dCTP supplementation could rescue the increased gemcitabine sensitivity caused by INHBA knockdown, we performed a series of functional assays. Pancreatic cancer cells (PANC-1 or MIA PaCa-2) stably expressing control or INHBA-targeting siRNA were seeded into 24-well plates at appropriate density and allowed to adhere for 24 h. Cells were then treated with gemcitabine (10 µM), either alone or in combination with exogenous dCTP (100 µM, freshly prepared in sterile PBS), for 48 h. After treatment, cells were subjected to multiple analyses, including CCK-8 assay to evaluate cell viability and calculate IC50 values, EdU incorporation assay to assess DNA synthesis, flow cytometry to analyze cell cycle progression, and γ-H2AX immunofluorescence staining to measure DNA damage levels. Additionally, Transwell invasion and wound healing assays were conducted to evaluate changes in cell invasion and migration capacities.

Results

INHBA is a pyrimidine metabolism-related gene in PC

To identify potential key genes involved in pancreatic cancer proliferation and drug resistance, consensus clustering analysis of The Cancer Genome Atlas-Pancreatic Adenocarcinoma samples was performed using pyrimidine metabolism-related genes, with K = 2 being the optimal cluster number (Supplementary File 1a and b). Consensus clustering was then applied to divide the cohorts into groups C1 and C2 (Fig. 1A). C1 showed higher enrichment of pyrimidine metabolism (Fig. 1B) and longer survival times (Fig. 1C) than C2. PRG (Pyrimidine metabolism-related differentially expressed genes) between C1 and C2 were identified using criteria of |log2 fold change (FC)| >1 and P < 0.05 (Fig. 1D). By intersecting five gene sets from the TCGA + GTEx, GSE15471, GSE28735, GSE183795, and PRG databases, 14 relevant genes were identified. Previous studies and Kaplan–Meier (KM) curve analyses were used to confirm INHBA as the target (Fig. 1E; Supplementary File 1c–p). INHBA mRNA levels were higher in 179 PC tissue samples than in 171 normal pancreatic tissue samples, as analyzed using the GEPIA database (Fig. 1F). KM curves, generated using the KM-plotter online tool, showed that high INHBA expression was associated with shorter overall survival (Fig. 1G). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis of 179 patients with PC revealed that INHBA levels had a high predictive value for pathological grading, with an area under the curve of 0.692 and a 95% confidence interval (CI) of 0.579–0.804 (Fig. 1H). Single-sample Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (ssGSEA) indicated that patients with high INHBA expression had significantly higher angiogenesis scores, potentially affecting tumor metastasis and nutrient supply and causing tumor recurrence (Fig. 1I). Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) scoring revealed poorer responses to immunotherapy in patients with high INHBA expression (Fig. 1J). Similarly, ssGSEA was used to predict the differences in immune cell infiltration between patients with high and low INHBA expression, suggesting a potential mechanism of INHBA-mediated resistance (Fig. 1K).

INHBA is highly expressed in PC and promotes chemoresistance

To validate INHBA upregulation in PC, we collected 34 pairs of PC and adjacent normal tissue samples. INHBA mRNA levels were significantly higher in the 30 PC tissues (Fig. 2A). Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining and western blotting confirmed elevated INHBA protein levels in PC tumor tissues (Fig. 2B and C, respectively). A nomogram integrating INHBA expression and clinical variables was constructed to predict patient outcomes (Fig. 2D). INHBA levels were significantly lower in normal pancreatic cells than in the five PC cell lines (AsPC-1, BxPC-3, PANC-1, MIA PaCa-2, and SW1990). Quantitative reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) and western blotting revealed that PANC-1 cells had the highest INHBA expression, whereas MIA PaCa-2 cells had the lowest expression in PC Cell lines. INHBA protein expression was positively correlated with gemcitabine IC50 (R2 = 0.77; P < 0.05), prompting the selection of PANC-1 and MIA PaCa-2 cells for further experiments (Fig. 2E–G). In chemotherapy-treated PC tissues, hematoxylin and eosin staining and IHC revealed higher INHBA scores in the resistant samples (Fig. 2H). In the cohort from the Affiliated Hospital of Guizhou Medical University, high INHBA expression was associated with a poor prognosis (Fig. 2I). ROC curve analysis revealed that INHBA could predict chemotherapy resistance (Fig. 2J). These findings suggest that INHBA is associated with chemoresistance in PC.

Effect of INHBA on proliferation and chemoresistance in PC cells in vitro

To test the hypothesis that INHBA promotes chemotherapy resistance in PC, we selected the PANC-1 and MIA PaCa-2 cell lines, which exhibit high and low INHBA expression levels, respectively. We established stable INHBA overexpression, knockdown, and control cell lines (Supplementary File 2a and b). Additionally, we assessed the effect of INHBA on PC invasion and metastasis (Supplementary File 2c and d). We generated a chemotherapy cell model using gemcitabine (10 µM), which is a standard PC chemotherapeutic agent. In PC cells, downregulation of INHBA enhanced sensitivity to gemcitabine, whereas INHBA overexpression had the opposite effect (Fig. 3A). We compared two groups: one treated with gemcitabine and the other without gemcitabine. In colony formation and EdU staining assays, downregulation of INHBA reduced PC cell proliferation in the absence of gemcitabine and further decreased it upon gemcitabine (10 µM) treatment. INHBA overexpression increased cell proliferation in both groups, with a more pronounced increase observed following gemcitabine (10 µM) treatment (Fig. 3B–E).

Finally, using γ-H2AX as a DNA damage marker, we found that INHBA downregulation enhanced the DNA-damaging effect of gemcitabine in PC cells, whereas its overexpression diminished this effect (Fig. 4E). This indicates that INHBA plays a crucial role in modulating the DNA damage response, and that overexpression of INHBA compromises gemcitabine efficacy in PC cells.

INHBA affects proliferation and chemoresistance in a PC xenograft model in vivo

To verify whether INHBA also affects the chemoresistance of PC cells in vivo, we used PANC-1 and MIA PaCa-2 cells stably transfected with sh-INHBA#1, sh-INHBA#2, or OE-INHBA via lentiviral transfection for subcutaneous tumor formation in nude mice (Fig. 5A). Our results demonstrated that INHBA influenced PC cell proliferation, but its predominant role was in modulating gemcitabine resistance. In vivo, tumors derived from INHBA-knockdown cells exhibited significantly smaller volumes and slower growth rates compared to control groups, and these effects became even more pronounced upon gemcitabine (25 mg/kg, i.p.) treatment (Fig. 5B and C). Conversely, INHBA overexpression led to larger tumor volumes and accelerated growth, especially in the gemcitabine-treated group (25 mg/kg, i.p.), clearly suggesting enhanced drug resistance (Fig. 5B and C). IHC staining further revealed that expression levels of proliferation markers INHBA, Ki67, and PCNA were markedly decreased in INHBA-knockdown tumors treated with gemcitabine(25 mg/kg, i.p.), whereas these markers exhibited substantially increased expression in tumors derived from INHBA-overexpressing cells following gemcitabine treatment (Fig. 5D and E). Furthermore, to investigate the metastatic potential regulated by INHBA in pancreatic cancer, we established an in vivo liver metastasis model by intrasplenic injection in nude mice. The results showed that INHBA knockdown significantly suppressed liver metastasis formation, while INHBA overexpression promoted metastatic spread (Supplementary File 7c–d). These findings indicate that although INHBA has some effect on cell proliferation, its more critical function lies in enhancing gemcitabine resistance and promoting invasion and metastasis.

INHBA regulates the stability of the CTPS1 protein

Silver staining and mass spectrometry were performed to identify potential INHBA-interacting proteins in PC cells (Fig. 6A–C). Intersecting the relevant proteins identified with those involved in pyrimidine metabolism revealed a potential interaction between INHBA and CTPS1 (Fig. 6D). Subsequent co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) assays confirmed the interaction between INHBA and CTPS1 (Fig. 6E). We further validated this result using qRT-PCR, which showed no significant change in CTPS1 expression at the transcriptional level in INHBA-knockdown PC cell lines (Fig. 6F). Immunofluorescence analysis of tissues and cells revealed that INHBA and CTPS1 were predominantly colocalized in the cytoplasm (Fig. 6G), providing spatial evidence for their interactions. As no correlation was observed between INHBA and CTPS1 at the transcriptional level, we investigated the relationship between INHBA and CTPS1 at the protein level. Western blotting of INHBA-knockdown PC cells revealed decreased CTPS1 expression (Fig. 6H). Considering that ubiquitination is a key mechanism of protein degradation, we conducted protein stability studies. In the presence of the proteasome inhibitor MG132, the decrease in CTPS1 protein expression caused by INHBA knockdown was reversed (Fig. 6I). This suggests that the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway is involved in INHBA-mediated CTPS1 regulation in PC cells. Notably, in cycloheximide-treated PC cells, downregulating INHBA shortened the half-life of CTPS1, whereas overexpressing INHBA prolonged CTPS1’s half-life (Fig. 6J and K). These results suggest that INHBA may exert its effects by interfering with CTPS1 protein degradation, thereby stabilizing CTPS1.

INHBA competes with SMURF1 for binding to CTPS1 and inhibits its ubiquitination and degradation

To investigate the effect of INHBA on CTPS1 and its impact on ubiquitination, we performed a ubiquitination assay. CTPS1 ubiquitination was increased in INHBA-knockdown PC cells and decreased in INHBA-overexpressing PC cells (Fig. 7A). Using the UbiBrowser online platform, we identified 20 E3 ligases associated with CTPS1 ubiquitination (Fig. 7B). Furthermore, we selected the three most highly expressed E3 ligases (SYN1, MDM2, and SMURF1) for co-IP assays and confirmed that SMURF1 interacts with CTPS1 (Fig. 7C; Supplementary File 3a and b). We transfected cells with small interfering RNA plasmids targeting SMURF1 (Supplementary File 3c) and found that CTPS1 ubiquitination was significantly reduced in SMURF1-knockdown PC cells (Fig. 7D). These results indicate that SMURF1 and INHBA exert opposing effects on CTPS1 expression.

We transfected PC cells with HA-Ub plasmids encoding K48 and K63 ubiquitin chains and found that CTPS1 predominantly underwent K48-linked ubiquitination (Fig. 7E). Exogenous IP assays further confirmed that SMURF1 enhanced the K48-linked ubiquitination of CTPS1 (Fig. 7F). IP assays revealed that exogenous SMURF1 promoted K48-mediated ubiquitination and degradation of CTPS1 in wild-type PC cells, with a more pronounced effect in INHBA-knockdown PC cells. Conversely, INHBA overexpression reversed SMURF1-induced K48-mediated ubiquitination and degradation of CTPS1 (Fig. 7G). These findings suggest that SMURF1 disrupts the stabilizing effect of INHBA on CTPS1 through competitive inhibition, thereby enhancing K48-mediated ubiquitination and degradation of CTPS1.

Using the UniProt online platform, we generated truncated INHBA and CTPS1 constructs based on their functional domains (Fig. 7H). We found that INHBA directly interacts with CTPS1 through its C-terminal domain (△C; 1-310 aa) and the truncated D1 or D3 CTPS1 domains (300–554 aa) (Fig. 7I). As CTPS1 plays a critical role in de novo pyrimidine biosynthesis, we conducted an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay on subcutaneous tumors using mouse xenograft models. In subcutaneous tumors with INHBA knockdown, UTP and CTP synthesis increased and decreased, respectively (Fig. 7J and K). In contrast, in subcutaneous tumors overexpressing INHBA, UTP and CTP synthesis decreased and increased, respectively; this trend became more pronounced after gemcitabine treatment (Supplementary File 3f–k). Importantly, exogenous supplementation with dCTP in INHBA-deficient cells partially rescued the increased gemcitabine sensitivity, reduced DNA damage levels, and restored proliferative and invasive capacities, as demonstrated by IC50, γ-H2AX, EdU, flow cytometry, (Supplementary File 5 and 6). These findings suggest that INHBA competitively binds to CTPS1, inhibiting SMURF1-mediated ubiquitination and degradation of CTPS1, which promotes CTP production and increases the dCTP pool. This larger dCTP pool mitigated the cytotoxic effects of the gemcitabine metabolite dFdCTP.

INHBA regulates the malignancy of PC cells by competitively binding to CTPS1 with SMURF1

To determine whether CTPS1 plays a crucial role in INHBA-mediated PC, Si-CTPS1 and OE-CTPS1 were introduced into OE-INHBA and Sh-INHBA PC cells, respectively. Furthermore, we transfected small interfering RNA plasmids targeting CTPS1 and a plasmid for CTPS1 overexpression (Supplementary Files 3 d and e). CTPS1 overexpression in INHBA knockdown cells reversed the gemcitabine sensitivity induced by INHBA knockdown. Similarly, CTPS1 knockdown in INHBA-overexpressing cells reversed the reduction in gemcitabine sensitivity caused by INHBA overexpression (Fig. 8A). In addition, colony formation and EdU assays revealed that CTPS1 overexpression reversed the decreased proliferative capacity of PC cells induced by INHBA knockdown, regardless of gemcitabine treatment. Conversely, CTPS1 knockdown reversed the increase in the proliferative capacity of PC cells caused by INHBA overexpression (Fig. 8B and C). Moreover, flow cytometry analysis showed that CTPS1 overexpression significantly reversed the apoptosis and cell cycle arrest induced by INHBA knockdown in gemcitabine-treated PC cells (10 µM). CTPS1 knockdown also significantly reversed the reduced apoptosis and enhanced cell cycle proliferation caused by INHBA overexpression in PC cells treated with gemcitabine (10 µM) (Fig. 8D and E). Therefore, INHBA may regulate CTPS1 as a potential target for mediating chemoresistance.

Discussion

This study revealed that INHBA is a potential therapeutic target that promotes proliferation and gemcitabine resistance in PC. Our findings revealed that hyperactivated INHBA promotes unregulated pyrimidine biosynthesis by competitively binding to CTPS1 and inhibiting its ubiquitin-mediated degradation by SMURF1. This mechanism alleviates gemcitabine-induced replication stress, thereby reducing gemcitabine sensitivity. This discovery offers a novel therapeutic strategy to overcome gemcitabine resistance and improve the prognosis of patients with PC.

Gemcitabine resistance in PC is a complex process involving multiple factors, including nucleoside transporters, nucleotidases, the tumor microenvironment, epithelial-mesenchymal transition, and microRNA (miRNA) regulation [19, 20]. Previous studies have reported that TRIM21 is a promising driver mediator, with gene knockout inhibiting tumor proliferation and gemcitabine resistance [14]. The ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme UBE2T interacts with the ubiquitin ligase RING1, facilitating p53 ubiquitination and degradation. This process upregulates the rate-limiting pyrimidine metabolism enzymes RRM1 and RRM2, alleviates gemcitabine-induced replication stress, and contributes to gemcitabine resistance in PC [21]. These findings provide new insights into enhancing the efficacy of chemotherapy for PC and offer promising strategies for overcoming drug resistance.

INHBA, a key ligand in the TGF-β signaling pathway, plays a critical role in PC. Elevated INHBA expression is closely associated with tumor progression, increased invasiveness, and poor prognosis in PC [18, 22, 23]. By blocking the TGF-β signaling pathway, INHBA reduces cyclin D1 expression and causes cell cycle arrest. Similarly, aberrant expression of this gene has been associated with immune imbalance within the tumor microenvironment, influencing tumor progression and the efficacy of immunotherapy [15, 24]. Furthermore, INHBA expression levels may serve as a biomarker for predicting the prognosis and therapeutic response in patients with PC, making it a promising therapeutic target [25]. Our study revealed that INHBA was upregulated in PC tissues and cell lines, and various in vitro and in vivo experiments showed that INHBA strongly drives the malignant progression of PC, including proliferation and chemoresistance. INHBA’s role extends beyond regulating the TGF-β signaling pathway and encompasses the modulation of pyrimidine metabolism, representing a novel and critical mechanism underlying PC chemoresistance.

Pyrimidine metabolism and replication stress play central roles in gemcitabine resistance in PC [21, 26]. In the pyrimidine synthesis pathway, the rate-limiting enzymes CTPS1 and CTPS2 are crucial for synthesizing purine and pyrimidine nucleotides [27–29]. Cancer cells may enhance pyrimidine metabolism by upregulating CTPS1 activity, ensuring sufficient dCTP levels to support DNA replication and RNA synthesis [30, 31]. This upregulation can influence the signaling pathways associated with chemoresistance and promote DNA damage repair, bypassing the inhibitory effects of gemcitabine and contributing to the development of resistance. In this study, we used mass spectrometry to identify proteins that interact with INHBA, focusing on CTPS1, because of its intersection with the rate-limiting enzymes involved in pyrimidine metabolism. Our findings suggest that CTPS1 plays a significant role in PC chemoresistance.

Ubiquitin diversity lies in the ability of ubiquitin molecules to bind target proteins at various sites and form different types of ubiquitin chains. K48-linked ubiquitination is closely associated with the proteasomal degradation pathway, which causes the degradation of target proteins. In contrast, K63-linked ubiquitination regulates processes such as the DNA damage response and repair [32]. Our study revealed that INHBA may stabilize CTPS1 by inhibiting its ubiquitination. SMURF1 overexpression significantly increased K48-linked CTPS1 ubiquitination, which was suppressed by INHBA co-expression.

Although this study provides novel insights into the role of INHBA in mediating gemcitabine resistance through CTPS1 stabilization and pyrimidine metabolism, it has certain limitations. First, the clinical specimens analyzed in this study were primarily derived from a single medical center, which may introduce regional or population-specific biases. Future validation using multicenter cohorts with diverse ethnic backgrounds could strengthen the generalizability of our findings. Second, although INHBA has been proposed as a potential therapeutic target, no drug development or preclinical drug trials have been conducted, and there is a lack of validation of direct intervention effects. However, these limitations do not diminish the robustness of our conclusions but highlight opportunities for future translational research to bridge these gaps.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we demonstrated that INHBA binds to CTPS1, competitively inhibiting the interaction between SMURF1 and CTPS1, thereby suppressing the degradation of CTPS1 mediated by K48-linked ubiquitination.By stabilizing CTPS1-mediated pyrimidine biosynthesis and alleviating replication stress, INHBA neutralizes the cytotoxic effects of chemotherapeutic agents, leading to chemoresistance. The combination of gemcitabine and INHBA knockdown significantly inhibited PC cell proliferation and promoted apoptosis. In a subcutaneous tumor mouse model of PC, this combination therapy significantly suppressed tumor growth. This study provides a new perspective on the mechanisms of chemoresistance in PC and offers potential targets for the development of novel therapeutic strategies.
