The Charlson comorbidity index may serve as a good predictor of intensive care unit mortality in patients with necrotizing fasciitis
Authors: Min-I Su, Wei-Ru Chiou, En-Jui Liu, Chung-Yi Wang, Yu-Chiao Tsai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) is a well-established predictor of mortality across various clinical conditions, but its prognostic value in patients admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) for necrotizing fasciitis (NF) remains unclear. This study aimed to evaluate the prognostic value of the CCI for ICU and in-hospital mortality in patients with NF. This retrospective cohort study analyzed data from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV, version 2.2) database from 2008 to 2019. NF was identified using ICD-9 and ICD-10 codes 728.86 and M72.6, respectively. Cox proportional hazards models were used to assess the associations of the CCI and other clinical variables with ICU and in-hospital mortality. Among 139 adult patients admitted to the ICU for NF, the ICU mortality rate was 11.51%, and the mean CCI was 4.81 ± 2.86. Receiver operating characteristic curve analysis identified CCI > 5 as the optimal cutoff for predicting ICU and in-hospital mortality. At this cutoff, sensitivity and specificity were 81.3% and 65.9% for ICU mortality and 78.3% and 68.1% for in-hospital mortality, respectively. Multivariable analysis showed that the CCI remained independently associated with both mortality outcomes. A CCI > 5 was associated with adjusted hazard ratios of 8.089 (95% CI 1.507–43.402, P = .02) for ICU mortality and 5.993 (95% CI 1.737–20.669, P = .005) for in-hospital mortality. The CCI is a reliable predictor of both ICU and in-hospital mortality in patients with NF.

1. Introduction

Necrotizing fasciitis (NF) is associated with high morbidity and mortality rates.[1–3] Treatment typically includes repeated aggressive surgical debridement, antibiotics, and advanced supportive care, with most patients requiring intensive care unit (ICU) admission.[4–6] Several studies have suggested age, vital signs, laboratory data, and underlying systemic diseases as useful predictors of mortality in patients with NF.[7–10]

The Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) was introduced in 1987 to encompass various underlying systemic diseases for mortality prediction.[11] It has been applied across various patient populations, including older adults, hospitalized patients, patients with trauma, surgical patients, patients seen in the emergency department, and patients with cancer. It has also been used for specific diseases, such as heart failure, systemic lupus erythematosus, and COVID-19. Several studies have demonstrated that the CCI may predict ICU mortality.[12–14]

In 2021, Asai et al reported that the CCI, when used in conjunction with the quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA), could effectively reflect the severity and prognosis of NF.[15] Similarly, in 2022, Nagata et al reported an association between the CCI and limb amputation or death from NF.[16] However, neither study specifically evaluated ICU mortality. Therefore, we aimed to investigate the association between the CCI and ICU mortality in patients with NF using the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) database.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Data source

This retrospective cohort study used data from the MIMIC-IV version 2.2 database from 2008 to 2019.[17,18] The MIMIC-IV is a publicly accessible real-world clinical database maintained by Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center in Boston, MA, USA. It comprises comprehensive records of more than 200,000 emergency department admissions and more than 60,000 ICU stays. Access to the database was granted under record ID 42188048, and the code for data extraction is available on GitHub at: https://github.com/MIT-LCP/mimic-iv. The collection of patient information and creation of the research resource were reviewed by the Institutional Review Board at the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center, which granted a waiver of informed consent and approved the data sharing initiative.

2.2. Study population

This study focused exclusively on adult patients aged ≥18 years who were admitted to the ICU because of NF. The diagnosis of NF was determined based on the International Classification of Diseases, ninth revision (ICD-9) code 728.86 and the ICD-10 code M72.6. For patients with multiple ICU admissions, only the first ICU stay was included in the analysis. ICU stays of <24 hours were excluded (Fig. 1).

Flowchart for patient selection and inclusion. ICU = intensive care unit.

2.3. Outcomes and variables

The primary outcomes were ICU and in-hospital mortality. The variables included the patient baseline demographic and clinical characteristics that were recorded in the ICU. Vital signs, including blood pressure, temperature, and oxygen saturation, were recorded throughout the entire ICU stay. Laboratory tests that were previously reported to be associated with NF mortality were also included (Table 1). The CCI, Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) and Logistic Organ Dysfunction Score (LODS) scores assessed within the first 24 h of ICU admission were also included. Variables with >20% missing values were excluded; those with ≤20% missing data were handled using multiple imputation with the “mice” package in R.[19,20]

2.4. Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation and categorical variables were expressed as numbers and percentages (%). Cox proportional hazards models were used to assess the associations between the covariates and outcomes, and the results were presented as hazard ratios with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were employed to determine the optimal cutoff values for the CCI. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated to compare the ICU and in-hospital mortality rates between the different groups, with differences assessed using the log-rank test. All statistical analyses were conducted using R (version 4.2.3) and SPSS (version 22). A threshold of P < .05 (two-sided) was considered statistically significant.

3. Results

3.1. Study population and baseline characteristics

Among 73,181 ICU admissions during the study period, 217 patients with necrotizing fasciitis were identified. After excluding 18 patients with an ICU stay of <24 hours and 60 repeated ICU admissions, 139 patients were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). Their baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The study cohort had a mean age of 58.67 ± 15.71 years, and 47.48% (n = 66) were male. The mean CCI of the cohort was 4.81 ± 2.86. Regarding initial disease severity, the mean SOFA score was 7.68 ± 4.49, and the mean LODS score was 6.55 ± 3.64. The mean ICU and hospital lengths of stay were 6.51 ± 5.96 and 20.37 ± 13.26 days, respectively. The ICU mortality rate was 11.51% (n = 16), and the overall in-hospital mortality rate was 16.55% (n = 23).

3.2. Optimal cutoff value of CCI and survival analysis

Receiver operating characteristic curve analysis identified a CCI > 5 as the optimal cutoff for predicting ICU and in-hospital mortality (Fig. 2). The CCI had an area under the curve of 0.852 (95% CI, 0.752–0.951) for ICU mortality and 0.836 (95% CI, 0.741–0.932) for in-hospital mortality. A CCI > 5 had a sensitivity of 81.3% and specificity of 65.9% for ICU mortality and a sensitivity of 78.3% and specificity of 68.1% for in-hospital mortality. Based on this cutoff, the cohort was stratified into 2 groups: CCI ≤ 5 (n = 84) and CCI > 5 (n = 55) (Table 2). Compared with patients with a CCI ≤ 5, those with a CCI > 5 were significantly older and had higher creatinine levels and LODS scores. Crucially, the CCI > 5 group experienced a significantly higher ICU mortality rate (23.64% vs 3.57%, P = .001) and in-hospital mortality rate (32.73% vs 5.95%, P < .001). Kaplan–Meier analysis also demonstrated significantly higher ICU and in-hospital mortality among patients with a CCI > 5 (both log-rank P < .001; Figure 3).

3.3. Predictors of ICU and in-hospital mortality

In univariable Cox regression analysis, the CCI, evaluated both continuously and categorically, was significantly associated with ICU and in-hospital mortality. Other variables significantly associated with both mortality outcomes included age, temperature, platelet count, alanine aminotransferase, Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), SOFA score, and LODS score (Table 3). Multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were subsequently performed to assess independent associations with mortality. In the continuous CCI model, the CCI remained independently associated with both ICU mortality (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR], 1.239; 95% CI, 1.001–1.534; P = .049) and in-hospital mortality (aHR, 1.209; 95% CI, 1.031–1.418; P = .02). Most importantly, when analyzed using the optimal cutoff in the categorical model, a CCI > 5 emerged as a strong independent prognostic factor. It conferred an approximately 8-fold higher hazard of ICU mortality (aHR 8.089; 95% CI 1.507–43.402, P = .02) and a 6-fold higher hazard of in-hospital mortality (aHR 5.993; 95% CI 1.737–20.669, P = .005). Older age was also independently associated with both mortality outcomes in both multivariable models.

4. Discussion

Despite advances in medical care and intensive care management, necrotizing fasciitis continues to be associated with substantial mortality, with reported mortality rates ranging from 9.9% to 31.7%.[1–3] In our current study, the observed in-hospital mortality rate of 16.6% aligns with these contemporary epidemiological findings. Importantly, we demonstrated that the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) is a robust and independent predictor of both ICU and in-hospital mortality. This association remained significant after adjustment for other clinical variables in multivariable Cox regression models, whether the CCI was analyzed as a continuous variable or using a cutoff of >5. Specifically, a CCI > 5 was associated with an approximately eightfold and sixfold higher hazard of ICU and in-hospital mortality, respectively. These findings suggest that comorbidity burden, as reflected by the CCI, may provide useful prognostic information in critically ill patients with necrotizing fasciitis.

Previous studies have attempted to utilize various laboratory parameters to predict NF mortality.[7–10] However, relying solely on laboratory data can present challenges for clinicians, particularly when specific test results are missing or delayed during the initial assessment. Conversely, the CCI provides a remarkable clinical advantage due to its immediate accessibility. It can be rapidly calculated based on a patient’s medical history upon their arrival at the emergency department, without the need to wait for further laboratory results. Pathophysiologically, the CCI reflects the cumulative burden of underlying chronic conditions, which inherently compromise the host’s immune response and physiological reserve, thereby worsening the prognosis of severe infections like NF. In our study, the association between the CCI and mortality remained significant after adjustment for clinical variables, including systolic blood pressure, respiratory rate, and GCS. These findings suggest that the CCI may provide prognostic information complementary to acute physiologic measures in critically ill patients with NF.

To date, limited literature has explored the prognostic value of the CCI in patients with NF. In 2021, Asai et al reported that the combination of the CCI and qSOFA score was associated with a poor prognosis in patients with NF; however, their study included only 27 patients and did not perform multivariable analysis.[15] Our study extends these findings by demonstrating that the association between CCI and mortality remained significant after adjustment for individual components of the qSOFA score, including systolic blood pressure, respiratory rate, and GCS. Subsequently, in 2022, Nagata et al reported an association between a CCI ≥ 3 and a composite outcome of limb amputation or death in patients with extremity NF.[16] However, a CCI ≥ 3 was not significantly associated with mortality alone, possibly because of the small number of deaths (4 of 56 patients) and the use of a composite outcome. To the best of our knowledge, the present study is the first to specifically evaluate the CCI for predicting ICU mortality in patients with NF. Furthermore, it remains the only study to employ multivariable Cox regression analysis, robustly confirming the CCI as an independent prognostic factor for both ICU and in-hospital mortality.

Our study has several limitations. First, the retrospective design and reliance on the MIMIC-IV database may have resulted in missed or misclassified cases due to coding inaccuracies. Second, we utilized multiple imputation to address missing data, which, while statistically acceptable, relies on the assumption that data are missing at random. Third, although we performed multivariable analysis to adjust for known covariates, residual confounding from unmeasured variables cannot be entirely ruled out. Finally, the relatively small number of mortality events limited the precision of some effect estimates. Therefore, future large-scale, prospective, multicenter studies across different geographical regions are warranted to validate the optimal cutoff of the CCI and its clinical utility in predicting ICU and in-hospital mortality among patients with NF.

5. Conclusions

The CCI was a practical and promising tool to predict ICU mortality, as well as in-hospital mortality, in patients with NF.
