Assessment of postoperative foveal displacement after vitrectomy and epiretinal membrane peeling using ImageJ software
Authors: Ecem Önder Tokuç, Sevim Ayça Seyyar, Fatih Bilgehan Kaplan, Ece Başaran Emengen, Isıl Kutlutürk Karagöz, V Levent Karabaş
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo analyze foveal displacement after macular surgery for idiopathic epiretinal membrane (iERM) using ImageJ software from spectral domain optical tomography (SD-OCT) infrared fundus photographs.MethodsThis retrospective study included total of 75 eyes of 74 patients from the Kocaeli University School of Medicine. The iERM was divided into four stages according to the OCT-based ectopic inner retinal layers (EIFL) staging scheme. SD-OCT infrared fundus photographs were acquired with a Spectralis OCT instrument. The preoperative and postoperative positions of the fovea and the four choroidal landmarks outside the fovea were manually marked from these infrared fundus photographs and entered into ImageJ software, and the displacement of the fovea in the horizontal, vertical, and total planes was measured.ResultsA predominant nasal shift was noted in the majority of foveal positions. When examining the EIFL staging of iERM through structural SD-OCT, the magnitude of foveal displacement is most pronounced in Stage 4 (530 ± 440 μm), succeeded by Stage 3 (310 ± 210 μm), and subsequently Stage 2 (230 ± 140 μm) during the final postoperative visit. At the last visit, 80% of eyes had a visual gain of at least 10-letters, and foveal displacement along the y-axis was found to be significantly higher in these patients compared to those with a visual gain of less than 10-letters (p = 0.034).ConclusionIn patients with higher visual gain after iERM peeling, the fovea tends to shift more along the Y-axis, suggesting that the amount of vertical foveal displacement is a more predictive factor for postoperative visual gain than the horizontal axis.Keywords: Epiretinal membrane, Foveal displacement, ImageJ software

Introduction

Idiopathic epiretinal membrane (iERM) is a relatively common vitreoretinal disease characterized by abnormal fibrocellular proliferation that causes dysfunction of the fovea by microstructural retinal changes and inner retinal displacement of the macula [1, 2]. Symptoms of iERM are highly correlated with membrane thickness, degree of contracture, and foveal displacement. Govetto et al. [3] have recently described an optical coherence tomography (OCT) - based staging system that characterizes the displacement and reorganization of the inner retinal layers, termed ectopic inner retinal layers (EIFL). In patients with symptomatic ERM, pars plana vitrectomy (PPV) combined with iERM removal, with or without additional internal limiting membrane (ILM) peeling, has been shown to restore retinal structure and enhance visual acuity [4].

Foveal displacement is a well-known phenomenon that occurs following retinal reattachment surgery, after macular surgery for iERM, or secondary to iERM due to tractional forces [5–8]. Reproducible and easily accessible tools are needed in patients to examine the change in foveal displacement in the long term after iERM peeling according to iERM stage. Spectral domain-OCT (SD-OCT) is routinely used to visualize retinal pathologies, allowing easy acquisition of high-quality, cross-sectional and face-to-face images. Changes in retinal blood vessel topography in eyes with iERM have previously been evaluated using infrared fundus imaging [9]. ImageJ software, developed at the NIH and providing different software packages for image analysis, provides several different features such as marking and superimposing alignment and measuring changes in preop-postop SD-OCT infrared photographs [10].

This study investigates the sequential movement of the fovea after PPV for iERM using SD-OCT and ImageJ software and the relationships between iERM staging with the EIFL staging system and foveal movement, central macular thickness (CMT), and best corrected visual acuity (BCVA).

Methods

Study population

In this study, which was designed as a retrospective archive scan, patients who underwent PPV, membrane peeling, and ILM removal due to iERM between January 2016 and October 2019 and whose regular follow-up examination records were reached for at least 1 year after surgery were included in the study. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Kocaeli University School of Medicine and was conducted according to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. In addition, all patients or their legal representatives provided written informed consent to participate and have their medical information used in the study at their presentation, following the routine protocol of Kocaeli University.

The indication for surgery for patients with iERM is decreased BCVA, intolerable metamorphopsia, or diplopia. Preoperative and postoperative ophthalmological examination notes at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months for all participants whose age, gender, and lens status were recorded were extracted from electronic records. BCVA measurements, detailed biomicroscopic examinations of the anterior and posterior segments, intraocular pressure (IOP) measurements, infrared fundus photographs obtained by SD-OCT (Spectralis HRA + OCT; Heidelberg Engineering, Heidelberg, Germany), and macula measurements were analyzed. Baseline and follow-up imaging was performed with SD-OCT, using horizontal cross-sections in which automatic real-time averaging was applied with 100 frames to obtain clearer and more reliable images. Patients with significant media opacity that precluded high-quality imaging were excluded from the analysis. The BCVA determined using the ETDRS chart was converted to the logarithm of the minimum resolution angle (logMAR).

Patients with secondary ERM related to other ocular conditions (such as diabetic retinopathy, retinal vein occlusion, uveitis, trauma, or previous retinal surgery) were excluded. Those with significant media opacity, including advanced cataract, that prevented high-quality OCT imaging were also not eligible. Similarly, patients with insufficient follow-up (< 12 months), poor-quality OCT scans, or other major ocular comorbidities that could confound the visual or anatomical outcomes were excluded from the analysis. In addition, eyes with residual intraocular gas at any follow-up visit were excluded to ensure reliable images. Patients with refractive errors greater than ± 6.00 diopters were also excluded. Patients who had retinal cystic changes, lamellar holes associated with epiretinal proliferation, or retinoschisis were also excluded from analysis.

Surgical procedures

All surgical procedures were performed by a single surgeon (LK) using Resight non-contact wide-angle imaging system (Carl Zeiss Meditec, Germany) and OS4 device (Oertli Instrumente AG, Berneck, Switzerland). All patients underwent standard transconjunctival 3-port PPV with iERM peeling using 23 gauge instruments under local anesthesia. The surgical procedure included core vitrectomy, posterior hyaloid detachment if not yet detached (if necessary induced by injection of triamcinolone acetonide), peripheral vitreous shaving, followed by tangential and circular peeling of the iERM visualized with the help of bright blue G (see ILM, Alcimihia, Italy) using intraocular forceps (Eckardt End Gripping Forceps, DORC International, Zuidland, The Netherlands). In the majority of cases, additional ILM peeling was also performed. Combined phacoemulsification and intraocular lens implantation were at the discretion of the surgeon. Unless there was an iatrogenic tear or peripheral retinal degeneration, tamponade was not used.

Determination of foveal location, measurement of central macular thickness, and determination of ectopic inner retinal layer stage

SD-OCT scanning was performed by keeping patients focused on the target, covering the fovea region with the best vision. The determination of foveal location was accomplished through the utilization of infrared images in SD-OCT. By conducting a horizontal section at the point of maximal thickness within the outer nuclear layer based on SD-OCT images, the foveal location was subsequently demarcated on the corresponding infrared image (Fig. 1). Central macular thickness (CMT) was automatically measured within central 1 mm of presumed fovea. Finally, the iERM was divided into four stages according to the OCT-based EIFL staging scheme [3]. Continuous EIFL was defined as the presence of a continuous hypo- or hyper-reflective band, extending from the inner nuclear layer and inner plexiform layer across the foveal region.

The foveal position was discerned using SD-OCT, which permits the direct alignment of OCT projection maps with the infrared image. Subsequently, the foveal location was indicated on the corresponding infrared image

Foveal displacement measurement with ImageJ software

By means of SD-OCT, OCT projection maps are directly recorded on the infrared image. The preoperative and postoperative position of the fovea was compared with ImageJ software using these infrared fundus photographs (Fig. 2). The preoperative and postoperative positions of the fovea and the four choroidal landmarks outside the fovea were manually selected from these infrared fundus photographs, as previously described by Tung et al.11 When selecting the choroidal markings, those that are dark or bright in appearance compared to the surrounding background were selected. These photos were entered into ImageJ software. The software superimposed the choroidal points marked in the two photographs. Euclidean vectors (X, Y) were recorded from the preoperative position to the postoperative position of the fovea, and the amount of displacement between the preoperative fovea and the postoperative fovea was recorded vertically, horizontally and total. The total displacement was calculated as the Euclidean distance between the pre- and postoperative foveal center coordinates, as illustrated in Fig. 2. Both SD-OCT and ImageJ measurements were conducted by two separate observers (EOT, EBE), ensuring reliability. To assess interobserver reliability, foveal displacement measurements obtained by the two observers were compared using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). If any discrepancies arose, measurements were repeated for accuracy.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 20.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Data distribution was assessed with the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test and histograms. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables as percentages. Visual acuity was converted to logMAR for analysis. Changes in SD-OCT parameters and BCVA were evaluated with one-way ANOVA adjusted by Bonferroni correction. For two-group comparisons, independent t-test or Mann-Whitney U test was applied according to data distribution, while the Kruskal-Wallis test was used for more than two groups. Categorical data were analyzed with Chi-square, Pearson Chi-square, or Fisher’s exact test. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 75 eyes from 74 patients were included in the study. Before surgery, 28% (21) of the eyes were pseudophakic. Combined cataract surgery with vitrectomy was performed in 56% (42) of the eyes. After surgery, 84% (63) of the eyes were pseudophakic. In conjunction with epiretinal membrane peeling, internal limiting membrane (ILM) peeling was performed in 84% (63) of the eyes. At the end of the surgery, tamponade was unnecessary for 78.7% of the eyes (59), 12% (9) of the eyes were treated with SF6 gas, and 9.3% (7) received C3F8 gas. Statistically significant BCVA loss was observed in the first month compared to baseline (p < 0.05) (Table 1). However, significant BCVA improvements were observed from the third month onward. Significant visual gains began in the first-year visit when only preoperative pseudophakic patients were evaluated (p < 0.05). Changes in CMT were significant in all patients and preoperative pseudophakic patients compared to the baseline, starting from the first month and at all visits (p < 0.05).

Patients were grouped based on ectopic inner retinal layer staging using their baseline OCT images. The number of eyes in each stage was as follows: Stage 2 had ten eyes (13.3%), Stage 3 had 45 eyes (60%), and Stage 4 had 20 eyes (26.7%). Changes in BCVA, CMT, and foveal displacement were examined across visits based on the stages. Although BCVA values showed statistically significant differences between EIFL groups at all visits, no significant differences were found in visual gains obtained from baseline to the final visit or from the first month to the final visit (Table 2). There was a statistically significant difference in CMT among groups at the initial visit (p < 0.001), but this difference disappeared after surgery. Nonetheless, the most significant change in CMT was observed within the Stage 4 EIFL group when compared to the baseline (p < 0.001).

Interobserver agreement for foveal displacement measurements was high, with an ICC of 0.86 (95% CI: 0.81–0.90). Total foveal displacement values differed between groups at all visits (Table 3). In stage 4, foveal displacement along the X-axis exhibited a statistically significant increase compared to the other groups starting from the sixth month onwards. The amount of foveal displacement along the Y-axis in stage 4 was also significantly higher in the third month compared to stage 3. Foveal displacement along the X-axis was significantly higher in stage 3 than in stage 2, but only at the postoperative third month; no significant difference was observed between the groups in the Y-axis. When stage 4 and stage 2 were compared, the amount of foveal displacement in the Y-axis was statistically significantly greater at stage 4, but this difference was observed only at the last visit. Additionally, no significant difference in the amount of foveal displacement in the X and Y axes was observed between stages 2 and 3 at other follow-up visits. At the end of follow-ups, it was observed that there was at least a 10-letter visual gain in 80% of the eyes. In patients with at least a 10-letter visual gain, it was found that the foveal displacement along the y-axis was significantly higher compared to those without (p = 0.034). However, no significant difference was detected between the two groups regarding the displacement along the x-axis and total displacement (p = 0.327 and p = 0.255, respectively).

Discussion

In the present study, the effect of macular surgery on foveal displacement was evaluated in patients with iERM staged according to the EIFL chart using SD-OCT infrared fundus photographs and ImageJ software. At the end of follow-ups, it was observed that there was at least a 10-letter visual gain in 80% of the eyes. In patients with at least a 10-letter visual gain, it was found that the foveal displacement along the y-axis was significantly higher compared to those without (p = 0.034). However, no significant difference was detected between the two groups regarding the displacement along the x-axis and total displacement (p = 0.327 and p = 0.255, respectively). The results of our study suggest that the amount of vertical foveal displacement in patients with iERM is a more predictive factor for postoperative visual gain compared to the horizontal axis.

Previous reports have adopted different landmarks to assess foveal displacement following iERM peeling. Ishida et al. [6] evaluated the distance from the optic disc edge to certain temporal and nasal vessel crossings, whereas Ichikawa et al. [12] calculated ratios between horizontal and vertical axes intersecting the fovea and adjacent vasculature. Lo et al. [13] instead referred to the optic disc along with nearby superior and inferior vessels. These methods, however, have drawbacks: the first two approaches provide only relative rather than absolute displacement values, and the last one may be unreliable since retinal vessels themselves can shift after surgery. More recently, Tung et al. [11] introduced the use of choroidal vessels as landmarks. Because the choroidal layer is structurally independent from the iERM, this reference is less likely to move in parallel with the membrane. Consequently, adopting choroidal vessels as fixed points enables a more consistent and accurate estimation of foveal displacement in both horizontal and vertical directions.

Using ImageJ software, a modern image processing platform that is easily accessible and has a low learning curve, certain reference points can be marked on the selected photographs, and the photographs can be aligned and superimposed according to these reference marks. Afterwards, the bottom photo can be blurred, and the desired distances between the two photos can be measured. Tung et al. [11] compared the preoperative and postoperative position of the fovea using a software program they designed in Python programming language. This study used imageJ software to measure foveal displacement. Our results confirm the findings of Tung et al. [11] and Dikmetaş et al. [14] measured the distance between the preop-postop position of the fovea according to the number of scans in the vertical plane and the distance between the scans. They measured the distance in the horizontal plane with a caliper tool by digitally covering the preoperative foveal line in the postoperative scan. The advantage of this study over the first of the two studies mentioned above is the use of easily accessible Imagej software, and the advantage over the second one is that the choroidal vessels are referenced. As many previous studies have emphasized, it is known that there is foveal displacement after macular surgery [6, 11–14].

In parallel with our study, two recent publications have emerged that investigate the degree of foveal displacement subsequent to epiretinal membrane surgery. In the investigation conducted by Dikmetaş et al. [14], which involved the evaluation of 28 eyes subjected to iERM peeling, instances of displacement were observed in the following directions: upwards in 14 eyes (50%), downwards in 10 eyes (36%), nasally in six eyes (21%), and temporally in six eyes (21%). In the study conducted by Tung et al. [13] involving 85 eyes, it was observed that the foveal region predominantly exhibited nasally-directed displacement in eyes with iERM peeling. Furthermore, poorer initial visual acuity and thicker central foveal thickness were both associated with a greater degree of foveal displacement. Likewise, within our study, nasal foveal displacement exhibited greater across all EIFL groups. Our observations also indicated that advanced stages, such as stage 4 EIFL, are associated with a more pronounced displacement of the fovea. Nevertheless, when comparing the baseline with the last visit, no statistically significant difference was observed among the EIFL groups in terms of letter gain.

The principal methodological difference that sets our study apart from earlier investigations is the choice of choroidal vessels as the reference landmarks for measurement, combined with the use of ImageJ software for analysis. Unlike Tung and colleagues [13], who utilized a specifically designed custom-built program for this purpose, we adopted the same anatomical reference framework but implemented it through ImageJ, which is widely available, simple to operate, and reproducible in routine practice. By doing so, we aimed to provide a more standardized and practical approach that can easily be replicated in everyday clinical settings. This methodological choice not only enhances reproducibility across different centers but also improves the feasibility of validating the results with commonly accessible tools, making the findings more generalizable to broader clinical environments.

On the other hand, the observed association between vertical (Y-axis) foveal displacement and visual improvement is noteworthy. The exact underlying mechanism cannot be fully explained; however, it may be speculated that vertical movement has a greater impact on the remodeling of foveal architecture and alignment of the photoreceptor layers. For this reason, it could contribute more to visual recovery than horizontal shifts. This observation, while speculative, raises an interesting hypothesis that warrants further testing in larger, prospective studies with longer follow-up. Clarifying this relationship may contribute to a better understanding of the structural-functional correlation in patients undergoing iERM surgery.

The strengths of this study are the long-term follow-up, the relatively large number of patients, and the accurate measurement of pre- and post-operative positions of the fovea with ImageJ software, an easily accessible and usable software. The most important limitation of the study is its retrospective design. In addition, there is no homogeneous patient distribution in EIFL stages and no patients in EIFL 1 stage. Other limitations were the manual identification of the fovea location in SD-OCT and the inability of the scan line to always scan the most central part of the fovea due to foveal morphological changes on the iERM side. Moreover, the effect of gas tamponades on foveal displacement could not be evaluated due to the small number of cases. In addition, outer retinal layer integrity was not assessed, and metamorphopsia was not evaluated. Also, ocular magnification correction was not used, but patients with refractive errors greater than ± 6.00 diopters were excluded to reduce this effect.

Conclusion

In more advanced stages of iERM, surgical removal of the traction over the fovea results in a more significant foveal displacement compared to early stages. It has been observed that patients with higher visual gains tend to have more displacement along the Y-axis. Considering the foveal displacement during preoperative evaluation may aid in predicting the potential visual improvements achievable through surgery. For this reason, measuring the position of the fovea by taking the correct reference points with an easy-to-use standard software will benefit clinicians.
