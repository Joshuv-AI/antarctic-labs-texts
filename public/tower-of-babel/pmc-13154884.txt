Bee derived Aspergillus oryzae as a novel reservoir for selective anticancer metabolites with integrated bioprocess optimization and multi cell line evaluation
Authors: Hamada A. Zina, Mohamed H. Kalaba, Abdelghany S. Shaban, Ahmed A. Elrefaey, Hesham M. Mahdy, Abdullah Haikal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCancer is a major global health challenge, driving the need for new therapeutic agents. This study explored the anticancer potential of microorganisms isolated from healthy bee workers collected from 35 sites in three Egyptian governorates to identify novel bioactive metabolites.ResultsA total of 113 microbial isolates (35 fungal and 78 bacterial) were obtained and screened. Thirty-two selected isolates were fermented, and their cytotoxicity against MCF-7 breast cancer cells was assessed using the MTT assay. Eight isolates showed strong anticancer activity, reducing cell viability below 30%. Among them, isolate Tm2 demonstrated the highest selectivity and potency across multiple cancer cell lines (Caco-2, MCF-7, HepG-2, A549), with IC50 values between 30.63 and 113.39 µg/ml, and minimal toxicity toward normal cells (IC50 > 976 µg/ml; selectivity index = 18.21). Morphological and molecular analyses identified Tm2 as Aspergillus oryzae. Optimization of fermentation parameters via Plackett-Burman and Box-Behnken designs maximized extract yield to 1.86 g/100 ml under specific conditions (yeast extract 1.5 g/L, pH 7.6, 25 °C). Chemical profiling using GC-MS and UPLC-MS/MS detected 65 bioactive compounds, mainly fatty acids such as oleic, hexadecanoic, and linoleic acids. Live-dead staining showed dose-dependent cytotoxic effects, while flow cytometry revealed induction of apoptosis through multiple pathways in cancer cells treated with the extract.ConclusionsAspergillus oryzae isolated from bee microbiota exhibits potent and selective anticancer activity mediated by a complex mixture of bioactive fatty acids. These results highlight its potential as a novel source for developing effective anticancer therapies with low toxicity to normal cells.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-05027-2.

Introduction

Cancer represents one of the most formidable health challenges of our time, accounting for approximately 10 million deaths annually worldwide [1]. Despite significant advances in treatment modalities, cancer remains the second leading cause of mortality globally, with an estimated 19.3 million new cases diagnosed in 2020 and projections suggesting this number will rise to 28.4 million by 2040. This alarming trajectory underscores the urgent need for innovative therapeutic approaches beyond conventional treatments [2].

Scientific efforts to combat cancer have evolved substantially over recent decades, transitioning from non-specific cytotoxic agents to targeted therapies and immunomodulatory approaches [3]. While these advancements have improved survival rates for certain cancer types, significant challenges persist, including treatment resistance, severe side effects, and prohibitive costs limiting accessibility in resource-constrained settings [4] Current chemotherapeutic agents often fail to discriminate between cancerous and normal cells, resulting in substantial toxicity to healthy tissues and compromised patient quality of life [5].

These limitations reveal critical research gaps in developing treatments that specifically target cancer cells while sparing normal tissues [6]. The ideal anticancer agent would demonstrate high selectivity toward malignant cells, minimal toxicity to healthy tissues, limited potential for resistance development, and cost-effective production [7]. This challenging profile has directed scientific attention toward natural sources as reservoirs of bioactive compounds with unique mechanisms of action and potentially superior selectivity profiles.

Natural products have historically represented a rich source of anticancer compounds, with approximately 60% of approved anticancer drugs between 1981 and 2019 being natural products or their derivatives [7]. Microorganisms, in particular, have emerged as promising sources of bioactive metabolites, having evolved complex chemical arsenals to compete and survive in diverse ecological niches [8]. The microbial world represents a largely untapped reservoir of chemical diversity, with estimates suggesting that less than 1% of microbial species have been cultured and characterized [9].

Among microbial sources, fungi have demonstrated exceptional potential as producers of anticancer compounds, with numerous clinically important agents derived from fungal metabolism, including taxol, podophyllotoxin derivatives, and camptothecins [10]. Filamentous fungi, particularly those belonging to the Aspergillus genus, are prolific producers of secondary metabolites with diverse bioactivities [11]. Aspergillus oryzae, traditionally used in food fermentation across Asia, has recently gained attention for producing bioactive compounds with therapeutic potential [12].

The exploration of unique ecological niches for novel microbial strains represents a promising strategy to discover previously uncharacterized bioactive compounds [13]. Honeybees and their associated microbiome constitute one such distinctive ecosystem, harboring microorganisms adapted to this specialized environment [14]. The honeybee microbiome has evolved under selective pressures of antimicrobial compounds present in honey and propolis, potentially leading to the development of unique secondary metabolite profiles [13]. Despite this promising potential, the anticancer properties of bee-associated fungi remain largely unexplored, representing a significant opportunity for novel drug discovery.

This research addresses these gaps by investigating the anticancer potential of Aspergillus oryzae isolated from honeybees. The study aims to evaluate the selective cytotoxicity of A. oryzae extracts against multiple cancer cell lines, identify the bioactive compounds responsible for the observed activity, and elucidate potential mechanisms of action. By exploring this unique ecological niche, this research seeks to contribute to the growing arsenal of natural anticancer agents with superior selectivity profiles, potentially advancing the development of more effective and less toxic cancer therapies.

Materials and methods

Sample collections

A systematic collection of bee specimens was conducted from 10 apiaries across three governorates in Egypt: 5 from Dakahleya, 1 from Ismaelia, and 4 from Menoufia. This selection aimed to capture a broad and representative range of bee populations. To maintain sample integrity, adult worker bees were stored in sterile, specially designed containers that prevented contamination and provided a stable environment. The containers were kept at a controlled temperature of 20 °C. This temperature range is optimal for bee survival, minimizing stress and preserving the microbial communities within the [15].

Isolation and purification of microorganisms from healthy bee workers

According to the protocols reported by [16, 17], bee workers underwent a four-step surface sterilization process to remove external contaminants. This included the following sequences: immersion in 2% sodium hypochlorite for 3 min, 70% ethanol for 60 s, a 5% NaCl solution for 60 s, and finally, rinsing with sterile distilled water. After surface sterilization, three bees were crushed on the surface of each sterile agar plate to release internal microorganisms. For fungi, potato dextrose agar (PDA) and Chloramphenicol Rose Bengal agar were used while nutrient agar was used for bacteria. All types of media were purchased from (Merck, Germany). These media enabled the growth of various microorganisms linked to honeybees. Bacteria were incubated at 37 °C for 48 h and fungi at 25 °C for 7 days. Bacterial colonies were picked up and purified through repeated streaking. In contrast, the emerged hyphal tips were picked up using a sterile needle and reinoculated on the same isolation medium for purification of fungi, after that the pure and morphologically different cultures were maintained on agar slants of the same isolation medium for further analysis.

Fermentation and extraction of the microbial crude extracts

In 100 ml Erlenmeyer flasks, 20 ml of potato dextrose broth, and nutrient broth were inoculated with 6 discs (6 mm in diameter) of 7-day-old fungal culture and 24-hour bacterial culture, respectively. The flasks were incubated under shaking conditions (120 rpm) following the same parameters used during the isolation step. After incubation, the fermentation cultures were filtered through a cotton layer to remove spores and mycelium. The filtrate was centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 20 min to separate planktonic cells. The clear filtrate was adjusted to pH 7.0 to extract bioactive metabolites. Equal volumes of the filtrate and 100% ethyl acetate (Sigma Aldrich, USA) were mixed thoroughly [18]. The mixture was shaken for 1 h and then settled in a separation funnel, forming distinct aqueous and organic layers. The organic layer was collected and evaporated to dryness under reduced pressure at 40–45 °C using a rotary evaporator (Heidolph VV2001, Germany), resulting in deep red residues, representing the crude extract which were then stored at -20 °C for further use.

Preliminary screening of microbial isolates for anticancer activity and selection of the most potent microbial isolates

Anticancer activity of microbial crude extracts was evaluated against MCF-7 cells using the MTT assay as described by [19, 20]. MCF-7 cells were cultured in 96-well plates with RPMI-164 medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 1mM sodium pyruvate, 2mM L-glutamine, and antibiotics (100 IU/ml penicillin, 100 µg/ml streptomycin) at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for 24 h to form complete monolayers. After washing the confluent monolayers twice, microbial extracts were applied at 500 µg/ml in RPMI medium with 2% serum, with three wells per extract. Plates were incubated at 37 °C in 5% CO2 for 24 h and examined for toxicity. MTT solution (20 µl) was added to each well, mixed at 150 rpm for 5 min, and incubated for 4 h at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. Following preliminary screening, the most potent isolates underwent secondary evaluation against four cancer cell lines MCF-7 (breast adenocarcinoma), HepG-2 (hepatocellular carcinoma), A549 (non-small cell lung carcinoma), and Caco-2 (colorectal adenocarcinoma), and two normal cell lines WI-38 (human lung fibroblast cell line) and HFB-4 (human normal fibroblast cell line) to assess cancer-selective activity. All cells were obtained from ATCC (USA) and maintained in DMEM (Lonza, USA) with 10% FBS (Gibco, USA) at 37 °C and 5% CO₂. Using the same MTT protocol, microbial extracts were tested at serial two-fold dilutions starting from 1000 µg/ml. After calculation of the cell viability % as previously mentioned, the IC50 of each treatment was calculated against each cell line and the most effective isolate was selected based on the best selectivity ratio (effectiveness against cancer vs. toxicity to normal cells).

Characterization of the most potent isolate

The fungal isolate was characterized morphologically and genetically in accordance with standardized procedures described by [20, 21]. Cultivation was performed on multiple media types, including Potato Dextrose Agar (PDA) and Czapek Yeast Autolysate Agar (CYA) (HiMedia, India), incubated at 25 °C for 7 days. Following incubation, colony features such as color, reverse pigmentation, texture, and overall appearance were recorded. To evaluate acid and base production, the isolate was also grown on Creatine Sucrose Agar (CREA) (HiMedia, India). For microscopic analysis, 7-day-old PDA cultures were examined under light microscope (Optika, Italy) using 400–600 magnification power to identify structures like sporangiophores, sporangia, and sporangiospores.

Genetic identification followed the same procedures of [15]. DNA extraction was done using the Gene JET Genomic DNA Purification Kit. The internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region of rDNA was amplified by PCR using a BIO-RAD T100 thermal cycler with the forward primer 5’-GACTCCTTGGTCCGTGTT-3’ and the reverse primer 5’-TGAAATTGTTGAAAGGGAA-3’. The resulting PCR product was purified using the Gene JET PCR Purification Kit, then sequenced using an ABI Prism 3730XL DNA Analyzer. The obtained nucleotide sequence was assigned a GenBank accession number and compared to existing ITS rDNA sequences using the BLAST tool to generate phylogenetic information.

Experimental design methodology

The optimization process was carried out in two sequential steps using a statistical approach according to [18]. Initially, the Plackett–Burman Design (PBD) was employed to screen and identify the key factors significantly affecting the production of bioactive metabolites by the fungal isolate, as indicated by an increase in the crude extract yield. In the second step, the Box–Behnken Design (BBD) was applied to determine the interactions among these factors and to establish their optimal levels for maximum metabolite production. Minitab 18 soft wear was utilized for both the setup of the experiments and the investigation of the data obtained from each design. The experiments were replicated twice, and the response was measured as the mean value.

Plackett-Burman screening design

For the Plackett-Burman screening design, we prepared a series of 16 experimental flasks according to a predefined matrix. Each 500 ml Erlenmeyer flask contained 100 ml of culture medium with varying compositions based on the design matrix. The medium components included dextrose (ranging from 10 to 30 g/L), potato infusion (100–300 ml/L), and yeast extract (0.5–1.5 g/L). The pH of each medium was adjusted using 1 M HCl or 1 M NaOH to achieve the target values (5.0–8.0) before sterilization. After sterilization at 121 °C for 15 min, the flasks were cooled to room temperature and inoculated with spore suspensions of the most potent fungus isolated from honeybee. The inoculum size (age = 3 days) varied between 5.0 and 10.0% according to the experimental design. The inoculated flasks were incubated at different temperatures (25–35 °C) for varying incubation periods (10–20 day). Agitation conditions ranged from static (0 rpm) to shaking (100 rpm) depending on the experimental run. Four center point replications were prepared with identical medium compositions and culture conditions to assess experimental error. After the designated incubation period, the fungal biomass was separated from the culture broth through filtration using Whatman No. 1 filter paper. The filtered extract was centrifuged and subjected to the extraction procedure as previously mentioned to obtain the crude extract, after that, the crude extract content obtained from each run was weighed and expressed in gram/ 100 ml. Box-Behnken response surface methodology design

Based on the screening results, we conducted a Box-Behnken design which represented by fifteen experimental flasks (500 ml Erlenmeyer flask contained 100 ml of the prepared medium according to the design order) with varying levels (-1, 0, + 1) of the significant factors, while the non-significant factors were adjusted as conditions at the value at which the crude extract productivity of the fungus increased. The inoculated flasks were incubated statically at the specified condition according to the design matrix. Three center point replicates with identical medium composition and culture conditions were included to estimate pure experimental error. After the incubation period, the fungal cultures were harvested by filtration through Whatman No. 1 filter paper. The cell-free filtrate was collected and subjected to extract recovery through appropriate downstream processing methods. The crude extract of each run was weighed to determine the yield in g/100 ml, which served as the response variable for statistical analysis. where Y is the response variable representing the weight of the crude extract (g/100 mL), β₀ is the intercept, βi and βii represent the coefficients for the linear and quadratic effects, respectively, and βiⱼ are the interaction coefficients between variables Xi and Xⱼ. These independent variables influence the response Y. To assess the statistical reliability of the model, Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) was performed. The model’s significance was validated using Fisher’s F-test and its corresponding p-value. Additionally, the model’s predictive accuracy was evaluated using the coefficient of determination (R²) and the adjusted R².

Validation of optimization

The validity of the statistical optimization model was experimentally verified by cultivating the fungal isolate in an optimized medium across five flasks, using the optimal variable levels identified through the Box–Behnken design. Following the incubation period, the crude extract was collected from each flask, and its weight was measured and reported as the mean value and compared with the predicted value via BBD.

Characterization of the crude extract

The preparation of each sample was carried out by refluxing with 100 ml of absolute methanol and 5 ml sulphuric acid for 2 h, extracting with ether and drying the ethereal layer over anhydrous sodium sulfate followed by evaporation of ether to give residue, kept for GC-MS analysis [22].

Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC/MS) analysis

Thermoscintific’s Trace GC-ISQ mass spectrometer, which has an A3000 autosampler and a TG-5MS capillary column with a 30 m length, 0.25 mm i.d., and 0.25 m film thickness, was used for the GC/MS analysis. The temperature was scheduled in a gradient mode (10 °C/minute) between 50 and 280 °C. Source temperature: 200 °C; interface temperature: 220 °C; injector temperature: 220 °C. Mass spectrometer adjusted in EI mode at 70 eV. One µL of diluted material was injected in splitless mode with a 50–600 amu mass scan. Helium was employed as the carrier gas (1 mL/min). A combination of fatty acid methyl esters (C5-C20) was immediately fed into the GC injector under the aforesaid temperature program to compute the retention index of each molecule for the identification of the components based on their retention indices. All component retention indices were calculated using Van Den Dool’s technique [23]. A comparison of the component’s mass spectral fragmentation patterns and base peak with those found in the literature or in the mass spectral databases NIST and ChemStation data system further corroborated the identity of the components [24, 25].

Conditions of UPLC analysis

Components were determined by UPLC XEVO TQD triple quadruple instrument Waters Corporation, Milford, MA01757, U.S.A. The chromatographic system was comprised of a Waters Acquity QSM pump, a LC-2040 autosampler, and degasser in addition to Waters Acquity CM detector. The dimensions of Waters Acquity UPLC BEH C18 column that was used for the chromatographic separation were: 50 mm (length), 2.1 mm (internal diameter) and 1.7 μm (particle size). The operation of the column was at a flow rate of 0.2 mL/min and the system was thermostated at 30 °C.

Mobile phase was composed of two phases. Phase A was composed of water + 0.1% formic acid, while phase B consisted of acetonitrile + 0.1% formic acid. Elution was a gradient one and its program was as following: 0.0–2.0 min, 10% B; 2.0–5.0 min, 30% B; 5.0–15.0 min, 70% B; 22.0 min, 90% B; 22.0–25.0 min, 90% B; 26.0 min, 100% B; 26.0–29.0 min, 100% B; 30.0 min, 10% B; followed by 4 min to re-equilibrate the column.

Conditions of ESI-MS

The analysis was carried out using a triple quadrupole (TQD) mass spectrometer coupled to electrospray ionization (ESI) source.

ESI in negative mode was operated at the following conditions: the capillary and cone voltages were set at 3 kV and 30 V, respectively. The ion source temperature was set at 150 °C and the pressure of the nitrogen gas (nebulizer) was adjusted at 35 psi. The temperatures of drying and sheath gas (N2) were 440 and 350 °C, respectively. The flow rates of the drying and sheath gas were adjusted at 900 and 50 L/h, respectively.

Cytotoxic assessment of the selected fungal extract on cancer cell lines

The investigation of the selected fungal extract’s anticancer potential employed sophisticated imaging and analytical approaches through two complementary methodologies:

Live/dead cell viability visualization using confocal microscopy

Cancer cell lines (A549, Caco-2, MCF-7, and HepG-2) were accurately cultured under optimized physiological conditions (37 °C, 95% humidity, 5% CO2) in DMEM medium enriched with essential growth factors and 10% fetal bovine serum. To achieve ideal optical resolution for confocal imaging, cells were precisely seeded onto specialized glass-bottom dishes at a calculated density of 1 × 105 cells/ml. The experimental design incorporated treatment with the fungal extract at concentrations started with 1000 µg/ml and twofold diluted until reached to 31.25 µg/ml, alongside untreated controls, followed by a 24-hour incubation period to allow cellular responses to develop fully. The viability assessment protocol employed a dual-fluorochrome approach utilizing Acridine Orange (AO) and Propidium Iodide (PI) with a 15-minute incubation period. This strategic combination exploits distinct membrane permeability characteristics: AO penetrates all cellular membranes to stain nucleic acids (appearing green in viable cells), while PI selectively enters cells with compromised membrane integrity (appearing red in non-viable cells). Following the staining procedure, excess fluorophores were removed through a triple washing sequence with phosphate-buffered saline to maximize signal-to-noise ratio for optimal imaging outcomes, as previously validated by [26].

Apoptosis analysis via flow cytometry

To elucidate the mechanistic underpinnings of the observed cytotoxicity, apoptosis was evaluated using a BioVision Annexin V-FITC Apoptosis Detection Kit, which identifies early apoptosis by detecting phosphatidylserine movement from the inner to outer cell membrane. Following the method reported by [15], the protocol was applied to both control cells and cells treated with IC50 concentrations of fungal crude extracts. Briefly, Cell samples (1–5 × 105) were harvested by centrifugation (300–400 × g for 5 minutes) after treatment. The resulting cell pellet was then suspended in 500 µL of 1X Binding Buffer, followed by the addition of 5 µL Annexin V-FITC and optionally 5 µL propidium iodide (50 µg/mL). This mixture was then incubated in darkness at room temperature for 5 minutes. For adherent cell types, the protocol was modified to include gentle trypsinization and washing with serum-containing media before staining. Flow cytometry analysis was conducted using 488 nm excitation, with detection at 530 nm for Annexin V-FITC (FL1) and PI detection through the phycoerythrin channel (FL2). For microscopic examination, stained cells were either mounted on glass slides with coverslips or, for cells grown directly on coverslips, inverted onto slides after staining. An optional 2% formaldehyde fixation step could be performed post-Annexin V-FITC binding to reduce nonspecific binding. Under fluorescence microscopy with dual FITC/rhodamine filters, apoptotic cells showed green membrane fluorescence, while necrotic cells displayed red nuclear staining combined with green membrane fluorescence.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate and results expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analyses were conducted using appropriate software packages with significance set at p < 0.05. Cell Viability and Cytotoxicity Assays: IC50 values were calculated using non-linear regression analysis with four-parameter logistic curve fitting. Selectivity indices (SI) were determined as the ratio of IC50 values between normal cells (WI-38, HFB-4) and cancer cell lines. One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test was employed to compare IC50 values across different cell lines and fungal extracts. Flow Cytometric Apoptosis Analysis: Annexin V-FITC/PI staining data were analyzed using flow cytometry software with quadrant gating to distinguish viable, early apoptotic, late apoptotic, and necrotic cell populations. Fold-change calculations were performed by dividing treated values by corresponding control values. Statistical significance between control and treated groups was assessed using unpaired Student’s t-test. Plackett-Burman Screening: An 8-factor, 16-run Plackett-Burman design was employed to screen significant variables affecting extract production using Minitab 18. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed to evaluate factor significance.

Fermentation, extraction, and preliminary screening of microorganisms producing anticancer bioactive metabolites

A total of 35 fungal and 78 bacterial isolates were collected from 10 distinct sampling sites. Morphological characteristics of all 113 isolates are presented in Supplementary Figure S1. Based on morphological similarities, 32 isolates (21 fungal and 11 bacterial isolates) were selected for fermentation under shaking conditions to enhance the production of bioactive metabolites. After the preparation of crude extract of these isolates to estimate their anticancer effect, the MTT assay was conducted using the MCF-7 cancer cell line and the results revealed varying levels of cytotoxicity. Several isolates, including Sh-16, Ta-2 low, Sk-1, Is-2, As-2, Tm-1, and Sh-15, displayed cell viability above 70%, indicating lower anticancer activity. In contrast, isolates such as Sk-6 low, Aw-5 low, and As-8 showed moderate viability, ranging between 30% and 70%. Importantly, eight isolates (Sk-2, Aw-5 low, Sa-8, Tm-2, Sh-14, Mn-4 low, As-6, and Sk-6 upper) marked with asterisks in Fig. 1. Demonstrated significant cytotoxic effects, reducing cell viability to below 30%. These isolates were selected for further investigation due to their strong anticancer potential.

Selection of the most potent isolate based on dose-response curves and selectivity index

The dose-response curves in Fig. 2 illustrate the effects of various fungal extracts on both cancer and normal cell lines across concentrations from 0 to 1000 µg/ml. Against cancer cell lines, most extracts demonstrated rapid decreases in cell viability at relatively low concentrations. For Caco-2 cells (Fig. 2A), Sa8, Tm2, and Sh14 showed particularly potent cytotoxic effects, with all extracts ultimately reducing viability to 10–20% at higher concentrations. Similar cytotoxic patterns were observed in MCF-7 cells (Fig. 2B), though with varying potencies; Tm2 and Sa8 again exhibited distinctive efficacy at low concentrations, while MN4 and Aw5 required higher doses to achieve comparable results. HepG-2 cancer cells (Fig. 2C) displayed consistent sharp cytotoxicity curves across most extracts, with viability reduced to below 20% at concentrations of 200 µg/ml or less. A549 cancer cells (Fig. 2D) showed similar trends, with effective extracts causing rapid viability decreases at lower concentrations.

In contrast, normal cell lines exhibited notably different responses. WI-38 normal cells (Fig. 2E) showed more varied responses, with some extracts producing gradual, dose-dependent decreases in viability while others like As6 and Tm2 maintained cell viability above 70% even at high concentrations (500 µg/ml). HFB-4 normal cells (Fig. 2F) demonstrated the most distinct contrast between extract effects on cancer versus normal cells, with Tm2 extract maintaining approximately 80% viability at 400 µg/ml and showing minimal reduction until much higher concentrations, while most other extracts induced viability declines at lower concentrations.

The IC50 values presented in Table 2 correspond directly with the graphical trends observed in Fig. 2 and provide quantitative confirmation of extract potency and selectivity. Sa8 exhibited the most potent activity against cancer cell lines, particularly HepG-2 (IC50 = 3.37 µg/ml), while maintaining relatively high IC50 values against normal cell lines (WI-38: 517.71 µg/ml; HFB-4: 423.05 µg/ml), resulting in a high selectivity index of 16.63. This selective cytotoxicity is clearly visualized in the steep initial decline in cancer cell viability curves compared to the gradual decline in normal cell curves. Tm2 demonstrated consistent potency across all cancer cell lines with IC50 values ranging from 30.63 µg/ml (HepG-2) to 113.39 µg/ml (MCF-7), while showing minimal effects on normal cells until very high concentrations (IC50 values: WI-38: 976.02 µg/ml; HFB-4: 1173.38 µg/ml), yielding the highest selectivity index of 18.21. The remarkable separation between cancer and normal cell response curves for Tm2 in Fig. 1 visually confirms this exceptional selectivity. The remaining extracts demonstrated varied efficacy and selectivity profiles. Sh14 showed moderate potency against cancer cells, particularly HepG-2 (IC50 = 33.31 µg/ml), but affected normal cells at lower concentrations than Tm2 and Sa8, yielding a selectivity index of 1.95. As6 exhibited moderate anticancer activity with gradual dose-dependent curves and modest selectivity (SI = 2.53). Sk6, MN4, and Aw5 presented less favorable profiles with minimal separation between cancer and normal cell response curves, reflected in their low selectivity indices (0.61, 0.71, and 0.90, respectively). Sk2 showed minimal differentiation between cancer and normal cell effects (SI = 1.07), evidenced by similar curve profiles across all cell lines. All of these data identifying Tm2 as the most promising extracts due to their potent anticancer activity with minimal effects on normal cells. So, this isolate was selected for identification and further studies.

Morphological and molecular characterization of most potent isolate

The selected fungal strain exhibited rapid growth characteristics, reaching 4.0 to 5.0 cm in diameter within 5 days when cultured at 28 °C. The colony morphology varied distinctly across different culture media as shown in Fig. 3 (A-C). On Potato Dextrose Agar (PDA) (Fig. 3A), colonies displayed a sandy texture with a yellowish-green pigmentation. Growth on Czapek Yeast Agar (CYA) (Fig. 3B) resulted in faint green colonies that appeared less condensed compared to those on PDA. When cultivated on Creatine Sucrose Agar (CREA) (Fig. 3C), the isolate demonstrated weak acid production, evidenced by a dull yellow color conversion of the medium. Microscopic examination revealed distinctive morphological features characteristic of Aspergillus species, as illustrated in Fig. 3 (D-H). The conidiophores were substantial, measuring 6.0 to 9.0 μm in diameter with lengths exceeding 1.0 mm. These conidiophores terminated in globose to sub-globose vesicles measuring 17.0 to 35.0 μm in diameter (Fig. 3E, F). The vesicles bore a single series of sterigmata that covered more than half of the vesicular surface. These sterigmata produced chains of spherical, smooth-walled conidia measuring 3.0 to 8.0 μm in size (Fig. 3G, H). The conidial head structure was observed to be loosely columnar in arrangement. Phylogenetic analysis based on DNA sequencing of a 385 bp fragment (Fig. 3I) positioned the isolate within the Aspergillus clade. The phylogenetic tree demonstrates that the isolate clustered closely with Aspergillus oryzae NRRL 447 and several other Aspergillus species including A. subflavus, A. pseudotamarii, and A. parasiticus strains. This molecular evidence, combined with the morphological characteristics observed, particularly the colony appearance, conidial structures, and weak acid production on CREA (characteristic of A. oryzae), strongly suggests that the isolate is Aspergillus oryzae. The isolate has been assigned the accession number PP907817 based on its DNA sequence analysis.

Plackett-Burman screening design

The screening matrix follows a Plackett-Burman design, which efficiently evaluates multiple factors with minimal experimental runs. This design allowed us to screen 8 factors (dextrose, potato infusion, pH, agitation, temperature, inoculum size, incubation time, and yeast extract) using only 16 runs. This design includes 4 center points to estimate pure error and check for curvature in the response surface. The orthogonal nature of this design enables us to estimate main effects independently, although two-factor interactions are partially confounded. Response values range from 0.45 to 1.25 (g/100 ml), providing sufficient variation to detect significant effects Table 3. The statistical analysis revealed three key factors that significantly influence the response (fungal extract production g/100 ml). The Pareto chart clearly displays these relationships, with bars extending beyond the significance threshold (2.36) representing statistically significant effects (Fig. 4A). Yeast extract concentration has the strongest impact, with a standardized effect approximately three times larger than any other variable. Increasing yeast extract dramatically improves extract yield. pH level represents the second most influential factor. Higher pH values within our tested range (5.0–8.0) notably enhance extract production. Temperature emerged as the third significant factor, with higher temperatures in the 25–35 °C range positively affecting yield. Agitation nearly reached statistical significance (p = 0.054) and showed a negative effect on production. The remaining factors - dextrose, potato infusion, inoculum size, and incubation time - did not significantly affect extract production (Fig. 4B). The model demonstrates excellent fit, with 98.19% of variation explained by these factors. The ANOVA confirms our model’s high statistical significance (p = 0.000), with an excellent fit (R²=98.19%, adjusted R²=96.13%). Three factors significantly affect extract production: Yeast extract: Extremely significant (p = 0.000, F = 318.00), pH: Highly significant (p = 0.001, F = 36.23), Temperature: Significant (p = 0.011, F = 11.78). Agitation approaches significance (p = 0.054), while dextrose, potato infusion, inoculum size, and incubation time show no significant effects. The non-significant lack-of-fit (p = 0.255) validates our model structure, and the high predicted R² (88.69%) suggests good predictive capability for new observations Table 4.

Response surface methodology

The Box-Behnken experimental design consists of 15 runs exploring three critical factors: yeast extract (0.5–1.5 g/L), pH (5.0–8.0), and temperature (25–35 °C) and includes three center point replicates to estimate pure experimental error while the other nutritional and environmental factors including dextrose, potato infusion, agitation, inoculum size, and incubation time (did not significantly affect extract production) remain at these levels 30 g/l, 100 ml/l, 0 rpm, 5% and 10 days, respectively. The measured response ranges from 0.50 to 1.70 g/100 ml, with the highest yield observed at high yeast extract (1.5 g/L), moderate pH (6.5), and low temperature (25 °C) Table 5. The ANOVA table provides statistical validation of the model. The model shows extremely high significance (p = 0.000) with an F-value of 65.31. All three linear terms—yeast extract, pH, and temperature are highly significant (p = 0.000) with F-values of 166.67, 135.00, and 135.00 respectively. The quadratic terms are also significant (p = 0.001), with pH2 showing the strongest quadratic effect (F = 76.92, p = 0.000), followed by yeast extract2 (F = 27.69, p = 0.003) and temperature2 (F = 12.31, p = 0.017). Among the interaction terms, yeast extract-temperature demonstrates the strongest effect (F = 30.00, p = 0.003), followed by pH-temperature (F = 13.33, p = 0.015), while yeast extract-pH is not statistically significant (p = 0.127). The data reveal that lack-of-fit (p = 0.456) is non-significant, R² =99.16%, adjusted R² =97.64% and predicted R² =90.37% Table 6. The Main Effects Plot (Fig. 5A) illustrates the individual impact of each factor. Yeast extract shows a positive relationship with extract production, reaching maximum yield around 1.5 g/L. pH demonstrates a similar positive trend, peaking near 8.0. Temperature, however, exhibits a negative quadratic relationship, with optimal production around 30 °C declining sharply as temperature approaches 35 °C. The Contour Plots (Fig. 5B) reveal important interactions between factors. The pH-Yeast extract plot shows highest production (> 1.50 g/100 ml) occurring at high values of both factors. The Temperature-Yeast extract contour indicates optimal production occurs at high yeast extract levels combined with temperatures around 27–32 °C. The Temperature-pH plot confirms that mid-range temperatures combined with higher pH values yield the best results. The optimization plot (Fig. 5C) and the regression equation confirms these findings, predicting maximum extract production (g/100 ml) at optimal conditions: yeast extract = 1.50 g/L, pH = 7.6, and temperature = 25 °C. The individual response curves illustrate how each factor contributes to maximizing production.

Statistical model validation

Using the unique capabilities of Response Surface Methodology (RSM), including point prediction and contour plot generation, the optimal combination of the three fermentation factors for maximizing fungal extract production was identified. Validation experiments were conducted in static flasks under the predicted optimal conditions. The results showed a strong agreement between the predicted and observed values, confirming the reliability of the RSM model. The close match between the predicted yield (1.81 g/100 ml) and the actual experimental result (1.86 ± 0.06 g/100 ml) further demonstrated the model’s accuracy (Fig. 6).

Chromatographic analyses of the Aspergillus oryzae extract

GC-MS analysis

The GC-MS analysis of the Aspergillus oryzae extract revealed a complex mixture of bioactive compounds, which were separated and identified based on their retention times (Rt), retention index (RI), and mass spectra. The chromatogram, a graphical representation of the GC-MS data, displayed multiple peaks, each corresponding to a distinct component in the sample. The retention time, measured in minutes, represents the time it takes for each component to travel through the GC column and reach the detector. Gas chromatography mass spectrometry of Aspergillus oryzae revealed presence of sixty-five compounds. Fatty acids and fatty acid esters represent a high percentage of the identified compounds. Hexadecanoic acid, oleic acid, and octadecanoic acid (stearic acid) are the principal components of fatty acid, whereas methyl hexadecanoate, methyl oleate, and methyl stearate are the principal components of fatty acid esters (Table 7; Fig. 7). The peak area, which represents the relative concentration of each compound in the sample, indicates that oleic acid is the most abundant compound in the extract, followed by octadecanoic acid and hexadecanoic acid.

UPLC-MS/MS analysis

UPLC-MS/MS confirmed the presence of fatty acids in Aspergillus oryzae through identification of linoleic acid and oleic acid. It also showed a high percentage of organic acid like malic acid and citric acid (Table 8; Fig. 8).

Cytotoxic effects of Aspergillus oryzae extract on multiple cancer cell lines using live-dead staining analysis

Confocal laser microscopy with live-dead staining revealed distinct dose-dependent cytotoxic effects of Aspergillus oryzae extract across four different cancer cell lines: A549 (Fig. 9), Caco-2 (Fig. 10), MCF-7 (Fig. 11), and HepG-2 (Fig. 12), with green fluorescence indicating viable cells and red fluorescence signifying compromised membrane integrity and cell death. In control conditions (0 µg/mL), all cell lines displayed predominant green fluorescence with intact cell membranes and characteristic morphologies: A549 cells formed clustered, rounded structures; Caco-2 cells appeared as discrete rounded colonies; MCF-7 cells established a confluent monolayer with epithelial morphology; and HepG-2 cells exhibited polygonal shapes with distinct cell-cell contacts, all with clearly delineated nuclear and cytoplasmic structures and no evidence of membrane damage. At 31.25 µg/ml, initial signs of cytotoxicity emerged with varying intensity, where A549 cells demonstrated moderate membrane disruption with emerging yellow-orange signals in some cells. Caco-2 cells exhibited the most pronounced early response with substantial yellow-orange fluorescence indicating membrane permeabilization, MCF-7 cells showed scattered red fluorescent foci against largely intact green cellular backgrounds. HepG-2 cells displayed punctate yellow fluorescence primarily at cell boundaries while maintaining overall structure. When the concentration increased to 62.5 µg/ml, progressive cytotoxicity became evident in all cell lines, with red fluorescence increasing noticeably in A549 and Caco-2 cells indicating enhanced membrane damage, MCF-7 cells displaying increased red fluorescent areas while maintaining cellular architecture, suggesting ongoing membrane disruption processes. HepG-2 cells showing intensified yellow granulation distributed throughout the cytoplasm with some cells exhibiting complete red fluorescence. At 125 µg/ml, marked cytotoxic effects were visible with yellow-orange to red fluorescence dominating in all cell lines, cell density began declining in A549 and HepG-2 cell lines, Caco-2 cells exhibited enlarged, intensely orange-fluorescent structures indicating advanced membrane permeabilization, and MCF-7 cells demonstrated widespread yellow speckled patterns throughout the cytoplasm while preserving some cellular integrity. When exposed to 250 µg/ml, extensive cellular damage was apparent with prominent red fluorescence across all cell lines, where A549 and Caco-2 cells showed substantial reduction in cell numbers with remaining cells displaying primarily red fluorescence, HepG-2 cells exhibited pronounced peripheral yellow-red fluorescence with diminishing green cytoplasmic areas, and MCF-7 cells demonstrated widespread red-yellow fluorescence throughout the field with compromised cellular boundaries. At 500 µg/ml, severe cytotoxicity dominated with intense red fluorescence prevalent across all cell lines, cell density decreased significantly in all cell types, cellular fragmentation became readily apparent in A549 and HepG-2 cells, MCF-7 cells maintained some cellular structure but displayed predominantly red fluorescence, and Caco-2 cells showed advanced disintegration with large red fluorescent structures and minimal green fluorescence remaining. Finally, at the highest concentration of 1000 µg/ml, maximum cytotoxic effect was observed in all cell lines with red fluorescence predominating throughout the fields, cell populations were drastically reduced with abundant cellular debris, HepG-2 cells displayed near-complete red fluorescence with minimal intact cellular structures visible, A549 cells showed scattered fragments with intense red signals, MCF-7 cells exhibited diffuse red fluorescence with few discernible cellular boundaries, and Caco-2 cells demonstrated complete membrane disruption with intense red fluorescence and severe population reduction.

Flow cytometric analysis utilizing Annexin V-FITC and propidium iodide (PI) staining revealed distinct apoptotic signatures across four cancer cell lines (A549, HepG-2, MCF-7, and Caco-2) following treatment with Aspergillus oryzae extract at IC50 concentration corresponding to each cell line. Discussion

The honeybee microbiome represents a specialized ecological niche with characteristic conditions that probable select for microorganisms producing specialized metabolites [14]. The considerable microbial diversity (35 fungal and 78 bacterial isolates from 10 sampling sites) suggests that honeybees interact with varied microbial communities based on geographical location and floral sources. Regional variation in microbial composition indicates that local environmental factors influence the honeybee microbiome, with differing fungi-to-bacteria ratios across locations emphasizing the complexity of these ecological relationships [27]. The single-dose screening approach using the MTT assay efficiently identified potential microorganisms having anticancer activity, with eight isolates (27% of those tested) showing significant activity by reducing cell viability below 30% against MCF-7 breast cancer cell line. The MCF-7 breast cancer cell line was chosen as a screening model because breast cancer is the most frequently diagnosed cancer and the second leading cause of cancer-related deaths among women globally. Additionally, MCF-7 cells exhibit moderate resistance to standard chemotherapeutic agents and possess well-defined molecular pathways [28]. The selection of isolates with significant cytotoxic activity against this relatively resistant cell line may indicate to potential discovery of novel compounds that could help overcome treatment resistance in breast cancer and other cancer types.

The anticancer screening study reveals significant variation in the cytotoxic effects of eight fungal extracts against cancer cell lines compared to normal cells. Among the tested isolates, Tm2 emerged as the most promising candidate with a remarkably high selectivity index (SI) of 18.21, followed by Sa8 with an SI of 16.63. These high SI values indicate the ability of these extracts to selectively target cancer cells while minimizing effects on normal cells. The selectivity index, calculated as the ratio of IC50 values for normal cells to cancer cells, serves as a crucial parameter for evaluating the therapeutic potential of anticancer agents. According to established criteria, compounds with SI values greater than 2 are considered active antiproliferative agents, while those below 2 suggest potential toxicity toward normal cells [29]. The dose-response curves provide visual confirmation of these selective cytotoxicity patterns. The steep initial decline observed in cancer cell viability curves compared to the gradual decline in normal cell curves for both Tm2 and Sa8 extracts clearly demonstrates their selective anticancer properties. This remarkable separation between cancer and normal cell response curves, particularly evident for Tm2, visually confirms its exceptional selectivity and supports its potential for further development as an anticancer agent. When comparing our results with previous studies, the SI values obtained for Tm2 and Sa8 are particularly noteworthy. A recent study on endophytic fungi from Terminalia catappa reported that Fusarium oxyporum extract N97 showed an IC50 of 33.35 µg/ml against HeLa cells with an SI of 8.01 [30]. While this SI value is considered promising for anticancer development, it is substantially lower than the values we observed for Tm2 and Sa8. This comparison highlights the superior selectivity of Tm2 and Sa8 extracts. Similarly, Trichoderma reesei TV221 demonstrated cytotoxic activity against various cancer cell lines with SI values greater than 3, which, although acceptable, remain significantly lower than our fungal [31]. It is interesting to note that some plant extracts have achieved even higher selectivity indices. For instance, an extract from the leaves of Tetraclinis articulata showed a marked cytotoxicity (IC50 = 0.37 ± 0.03 µg/mL) and selectivity (selectivity index = 378.3) against lung cancer cells [32]. However, such exceptionally high SI values are rare, and our fungal extracts with SI values above 15 still represent highly promising candidates for drug development.

The moderate to low selectivity indices observed for the remaining extracts in our study provide important insights into the structural-activity relationships of fungal metabolites. Sh14 showed moderate potency with an SI of 1.95, while As6 exhibited an SI of 2.53. The extracts Sk6, MN4, and Aw5 presented unfavorable profiles with SI values below 1 (0.61, 0.71, and 0.90, respectively), indicating minimal differentiation between effects on cancer and normal cells. These promising comparisons highlight that Tm2 extract represent potentially significant advances in fungal-derived anticancer agents, warranting further chemical characterization and mechanistic studies to identify the active compounds and their modes of action.

The characterization of this fungal isolate demonstrates the critical importance of integrating multiple identification approaches in modern mycology. The observed rapid growth rate of 4.0–5.0 cm within 5 days at 28 °C aligns with typical Aspergillus oryzae characteristics, as this species is known for its vigorous growth under optimal conditions [33]. The distinct morphological variations observed across different culture media provide valuable diagnostic information that cannot be obtained from a single medium approach. The sandy-textured, yellowish-green colonies on PDA distinguish this isolate from closely related species such as A. flavus, which typically produces olive-green colonies with different textural characteristics [34]. The faint green appearance on CYA and particularly the weak acid production on CREA serves as crucial differential features. Recent comparative studies have established that A. oryzae characteristically shows minimal acid production on CREA, while A. flavus and A. parasiticus demonstrate stronger acidification, making this a reliable distinguishing characteristic [35]. This multi-media approach follows current best practices in Aspergillus taxonomy, as morphological features can vary significantly depending on nutritional and environmental conditions [36].

The microscopic features further support the identification, with conidiophore dimensions (6.0–9.0 μm diameter, > 1.0 mm length) and vesicle characteristics (17.0–35.0 μm, globose to sub-globose) falling within the documented range for A. oryzae. The uniseriate arrangement of sterigmata and the production of smooth-walled conidia in chains are particularly diagnostic, as A. flavus typically produces rougher conidia that are less organized in chains [37]. The loosely columnar conidial head structure observed is another characteristic feature that helps differentiate A. oryzae from other section Flavi members.

The molecular phylogenetic analysis provides essential confirmation of the morphological observations. The clustering of our isolate with A. oryzae NRRL 447 and its phylogenetic relationship with A. subflavus, A. pseudotamarii, and A. parasiticus reflects the well-documented complexity within section Flavi [38]. The concordance between our morphological observations and molecular data strengthens the confidence in species assignment, particularly important for distinguishing A. oryzae from the aflatoxin-producing A. flavus, a distinction with significant implications for food safety and industrial applications. So, our strain is designated as A. oryzae Tm2 and has been deposited with accession number PP907817. Recent studies have highlighted the potent anticancer properties of metabolites produced by Aspergillus oryzae. One notable compound, heptelidic acid (HA), a sesquiterpene lactone derived from probiotic A. oryzae, has demonstrated selective cytotoxicity against pancreatic cancer and melanoma cells both in vitro and in vivo [39]. In addition to heptelidic acid, A. oryzae has been identified as a promising source of the chemotherapeutic agent taxol. An endophytic strain isolated from Tarenna asiatica was shown to produce taxol at concentrations around 95 µg/L. The fungal-derived taxol exhibited potent cytotoxic effects against multiple human cancer cell lines, including lung (NCI-H460).

Our investigation employed a systematic two-stage approach to optimize extract production from Aspergillus oryzae isolated from honeybee. The initial Plackett-Burman screening design successfully identified significant factors affecting the fungal extract production, while the subsequent Box-Behnken response surface methodology (RSM) optimized these factors to maximize yield. The PB screening model demonstrated excellent statistical validity (p value = 0.0), with a high R-squared value (98.19%) and adjusted R-squared (96.13%), indicating the model explained nearly all variation in extract production. The non-significant lack-of-fit (p = 0.255) further confirmed the model’s appropriateness [40]. PB screening phase effectively distinguished between significant and negligible factors, allowing us to focus subsequent optimization efforts efficiently. The Box-Behnken optimization model showed even strong statistical validity, with an exceptional R-squared value (99.16%) and adjusted R-squared (97.64%). The high predicted R-squared (90.37%) demonstrated excellent predictive capability for new observations. The non-significant lack-of-fit (p = 0.456) validated that our second-order polynomial model adequately described the response surface [41]. The optimization model predicts maximum extract production (1.816 mg/100 ml) at yeast extract = 1.50 g/L, pH = 7.6334, and temperature = 25 °C, representing a 3.6-fold increase over the lowest yield observed in our experimental matrix. After validation test, the close agreement between actual optimal response (1.86 g/100 ml) and the software-predicted value (1.81 g/100 ml) further confirms model accuracy. In evaluating potential factors influencing extract production, the screening design assessed eight variables: dextrose, potato infusion, pH, agitation, temperature, inoculum size, incubation time, and yeast extract. Among them, the analysis clearly identified three statistically significant factors: yeast extract (p < 0.001), pH (p = 0.001), and temperature (p = 0.011). Agitation approached non significance (p = 0.054) with a negative effect, while dextrose, potato infusion, inoculum size, and incubation time showed minimal influence on extract production, so they adjusted as fixed conditions in the RSM design. The dominance of yeast extract as the most influential factor aligns with its role as a primary nitrogen source, providing essential amino acids, vitamins, and trace elements that support fungal growth and metabolism [42, 43]. The pronounced effect of pH reflects its fundamental role in regulating enzyme activity and cell membrane function in A. oryzae [44]. Temperature’s significant impact was expected given its influence on metabolic rates and enzyme kinetics [45]. The optimization model revealed important insights about factor interactions. The significant yeast extract-temperature interaction (p = 0.003) indicates that temperature’s effect depends on yeast extract concentration, with higher yeast extract levels requiring lower temperatures for optimal production. Similarly, the significant pH-temperature interaction (p = 0.015) suggests that pH effects are temperature-dependent, with higher pH values more beneficial at lower temperatures. When comparing our findings with previous research on A. oryzae optimization, we observed both alignments and divergences. Several studies have identified nitrogen source (particularly yeast extract) as a critical factor for A. oryzae metabolism and secondary metabolite production [46, 47]. The optimal yeast extract concentration we identified (1.5 g/L) falls within the range reported in previous studies (0.5–3.0 g/L) [48, 49]. Most conventional A. oryzae strains are optimized for growth and enzyme production in mildly acidic environments, with optimal pH values generally reported between 4.0 and 6.5 [44], however other study reported that this fungus exhibited high growth and xylanase production at pH 7.5, the value which similar to our result [50]. This difference may reflect the unique adaptation of strain to its original habitat. Honeybee digestive tracts and hive environments typically maintain pH levels closer to neutrality (pH 6.5–7.5), potentially selecting for strains that thrive under these conditions [51]. The brood area of Apis mellifera hives is actively thermoregulated by worker bees to remain within 32–35 °C, which is crucial for larval development [52]. However, regions outside the brood area, particularly during colder seasons or nights, can drop significantly below this temperature [53]. If A. oryzae strains inhabit or are introduced to such environments, selective pressures could favor those capable of sustaining metabolic activity and enzyme production at these cooler ambient temperatures. Furthermore, A. oryzae is well-known for its environmental plasticity and has been observed to adapt to various temperature ranges during traditional fermentations and in natural settings [45]. So, our strain may have undergone physiological adjustments or genetic adaptations allowing it to retain high productivity at 25 °C. Unlike some previous studies which reported the importance of aeration and agitation for the growth A. oryzae [54, 55], we found agitation to have a nearly significant negative effect. This suggests our A. oryzae strain may prefer microaerophilic conditions, possibly reflecting adaptation to the relatively oxygen-limited environments within honeybee digestive tracts or certain hive microenvironments [56].

The chromatographic analysis employed complementary GC-MS and UPLC-MS/MS techniques, providing robust validation of identified compounds [57]. The GC-MS analysis revealed 65 distinct compounds, with fatty acids and their esters comprising the majority of metabolites. This finding aligns well with UPLC-MS/MS results, which confirmed linoleic and oleic acids, demonstrating excellent inter-method reliability. The complementary nature of these techniques is valuable, as GC-MS excels in separating volatile compounds, while UPLC-MS/MS provides superior sensitivity for polar metabolites [58]. The predominance of fatty acids in the A. oryzae extract is noteworthy, with oleic acid (22.78% peak area) being the most abundant compound, followed by octadecanoic acid (7.17%) and hexadecanoic acid (5.11%). This fatty acid profile is consistent with previous reports on Aspergillus species [59]. Oleic acid exhibits significant anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and antimicrobial properties [60]. The presence of linoleic acid, confirmed by both analytical methods, is particularly significant as this essential omega-6 fatty acid demonstrates potent anticancer activities and plays crucial roles in cellular membrane integrity [61]. The fatty acid profile observed shows remarkable consistency with previous investigations on A. oryzae. Recent pangenome analysis revealed similar oleic acid dominance in A. oryzae extracts from different substrates [59].

The UPLC-MS/MS analysis revealed substantial concentrations of malic acid and citric acid. These organic acids possess important biological activities, with malic acid exhibiting antimicrobial properties and citric acid demonstrating antioxidant activity and metal-chelating properties [62, 63]. The organic acid composition, particularly citric and malic acids, aligns with the known metabolic capabilities of A. oryzae, where these acids are produced through central metabolic pathways [64]. Organic acids are known for their antimicrobial effects against various pathogenic bacteria, including Escherichia coli and Salmonella species [65]. The identification of 7-hydroxyflavone and isofraxidin is particularly significant. These compounds exhibit potent antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antimicrobial activities [66]. Phenolic compounds are well-documented for their diverse therapeutic properties, including anticancer, cardioprotective, and neuroprotective effects [67]. The presence of these phenolic compounds suggests that the extraction methodology was effective in preserving bioactive metabolites. The detection of stigmast-5-en-3-ol (β-sitosterol) indicates the presence of important bioactive terpenoids with cholesterol-lowering, anti-inflammatory, and immunomodulatory activities [33]. Recent studies have confirmed that A. oryzae produces various secondary metabolites, including terpenoids, coumarins, and oxylipins, which exhibit significant biological activities [33]. The complete array of bioactive compounds identified potentially explains the anticancer activity with an exceptional selectivity index observed in our cytotoxicity screening (SI = 18.21). The potent anticancer activity of the A. oryzae extract can be mechanistically linked to its rich chemical profile. The major fatty acids identified oleic, linoleic, and hexadecanoic acids are known to disrupt cancer cell membrane integrity and induce apoptosis through mitochondrial dysfunction and ROS generation [68], aligning with our live/dead staining and Annexin V results. Furthermore, phenolic compounds like 7-Hydroxyflavone are established pro-apoptotic agents that can modulate key signaling pathways such as PI3K/AKT [69]. The presence of citric and malic acids may contribute to intracellular acidification, a condition known to potentiate cytotoxic effects in cancer cells [70, 71]. It is likely that the exceptional selectivity and multi-pathway apoptosis induction observed are not attributable to a single metabolite but arise from the concerted or synergistic action of this complex mixture, a well-documented phenomenon in natural product pharmacology [72, 73], targeting cancer cells through several parallel mechanisms.

The present study demonstrates clear dose-dependent cytotoxic effects of Aspergillus oryzae extract across multiple cancer cell lines using live-dead staining analysis. The dual-fluorochrome approach utilizing acridine orange and propidium iodide successfully discriminated between viable and compromised cells, providing mechanistic insights into the cytotoxic process [74]. The progressive transition from green (viable) to yellow-orange (early membrane damage) to red (complete membrane compromise) fluorescence indicates a gradual membrane permeabilization process rather than acute necrotic cell death [26] Among the tested cell lines, Caco-2 cells exhibited the most pronounced early cytotoxic response at 31.25 µg/mL, manifesting substantial yellow-orange fluorescence indicative of membrane permeabilization. This heightened sensitivity may be attributed to the unique membrane characteristics of Caco-2 cells, which naturally develop tight junctions and polarized membrane properties during differentiation [75]. The enhanced membrane permeability observed in Caco-2 cells is consistent with their documented vulnerability to membrane-disrupting agents and oxidative stress [75]. The observation of punctate yellow fluorescence at cell boundaries in HepG-2 cells at intermediate concentrations (62.5 µg/mL) suggests localized membrane instability, possibly indicating mitochondrial membrane potential disruption preceding complete cell death [76]. This finding aligns with established mechanisms of fungal metabolite-induced cytotoxicity, where oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction precede membrane permeabilization [77, 78]. The distinct morphological responses observed across cell lines reflect intrinsic cellular characteristics and resistance mechanisms. A549 cells maintained their characteristic clustered, rounded structures until higher concentrations, signifying enhanced resistance to membrane disruption compared to other cell lines. This resistance pattern is consistent with the known metabolic flexibility of A549 lung cancer cells, which exhibit robust antioxidant defense systems and efficient stress response mechanisms [79, 80]. MCF-7 cells demonstrated unique behavior by maintaining cellular architecture while exhibiting widespread cytoplasmic fluorescence changes, indicating selective membrane permeability that preserves structural integrity. This pattern proposes that MCF-7 cells may activate specific survival pathways that maintain cytoskeletal organization despite membrane compromise, consistent with their documented resistance to apoptotic stimuli [81, 82]. The present study corroborates the general cytotoxic potential of Aspergillus oryzae extract against cancer cell lines, consistent with the broader understanding of fungal metabolites as anticancer agents [83, 84].

The apoptotic effects of Aspergillus oryzae extract on various human cancer cell lines, namely A549 (lung adenocarcinoma), HepG-2 (hepatocellular carcinoma), MCF-7 (breast adenocarcinoma), and Caco2 (colorectal adenocarcinoma) were investigated. Flow cytometric analysis using Annexin V-FITC and propidium iodide (PI) staining revealed distinct apoptotic signatures across these cell lines, indicating the extract’s potential as an anticancer agent. The control populations consistently showed high viability (> 97%), establishing reliable baselines for comparison. Treatment of A549 cells with Aspergillus oryzae extract at IC50 concentration resulted in a dramatic redistribution of cells towards death pathways. The early apoptotic population (Annexin V+/PI-) significantly increased from 0.44% to 19.02%, representing a remarkable 43-fold elevation. Late apoptotic events (Annexin V+/PI+) also rose substantially from 0.26% to 6.39% (24.6-fold increase), while necrotic death (Annexin V-/PI+) showed a more modest increase from 1.23% to 2.6% (2.1-fold increase). Overall, total cell death in A549 cells increased 14.5-fold, from 1.93% in control to 28.01% in treated cells. This pronounced induction of early apoptosis suggests that the Aspergillus oryzae extract primarily triggers programmed cell death in A549 cells through intrinsic or extrinsic apoptotic pathways. Previous studies have similarly reported the ability of fungal extracts, including those from Aspergillus species, to induce apoptosis in lung cancer cells. For instance, a marine-derived Aspergillus oryzae NGM91 extract was shown to induce oxidative stress-mediated DNA damage and activate apoptotic pathways via BCL-2 downregulation and BAX/P53/Caspase-3 upregulation in PC3 cells, which are also highly sensitive to apoptosis induction [84]. Another study demonstrated that Aspergillus fumigatus culture filtrates-initiated apoptosis in A549 cells, characterized by cell shrinkage and membrane blebbing [85]. These findings align with the observed significant increase in early apoptotic events in A549 cells, reinforcing the pro-apoptotic potential of Aspergillus extracts against lung cancer. Similar to A549 cells, Caco-2 cells treated with Aspergillus oryzae extract exhibited a dramatic shift towards apoptosis. The early apoptotic population increased significantly from 0.41% to 19.71% (48-fold elevation), indicating a strong pro-apoptotic effect. Late apoptotic events also increased from 0.26% to 4.49% (17.3-fold increase), and necrotic cell death showed a moderate rise from 1.35% to 2.91% (2.2-fold increase). The total apoptotic events in Caco-2 cells increased 13.4-fold, from 2.02% in control to 27.11% in treated cells. The substantial increase in early apoptosis in Caco-2 cells is consistent with reports on other natural compounds and fungal extracts inducing apoptosis in colorectal cancer cells. For example, a marine Aspergillus oryzae NGM91 extract also demonstrated cytotoxic activity against Caco-2 cells, although specific apoptotic pathway details were not elaborated in the abstract [84]. This suggests a general susceptibility of Caco-2 cells to Aspergillus-derived compounds, with apoptosis being a key mechanism of action. The response of MCF-7 breast adenocarcinoma cells to Aspergillus oryzae extract treatment presented a unique pattern compared to A549 and Caco-2 cells. While early apoptotic events increased (from 0.51% to 8.41%, 16.5-fold) and late apoptotic populations rose (from 0.22% to 4.39%, 20-fold), MCF-7 cells notably demonstrated a dramatic elevation in necrotic death, increasing from 1.23% to 9.77% (7.9-fold increase). This represents the highest necrotic component among all tested cell lines, signifying a more balanced distribution across multiple death pathways, including a significant necrotic component. This divergence from the early-apoptosis-dominant profiles seen in A549 and Caco-2 cells highlights cell-line specific responses to the extract. Previous research on Aspergillus oryzae has shown its anticancer properties against MCF-7 cells, with an IC50 value reported for l-glutaminase isolated from A. oryzae [86]. While the specific apoptotic/necrotic distribution was not detailed, the general anticancer effect supports the current findings. Another study on Aspergillus oryzae fermented Camellia japonica extracts also showed increased apoptotic cells stained with annexin V/PI in FaDu cells, a head and neck cancer cell line, indicating a general pro-apoptotic effect of A. oryzae fermentation products [87]. The observed necrotic component in MCF-7 cells could be attributed to higher concentrations of the extract or specific cellular susceptibilities, necessitating further investigation into the precise mechanisms. HepG-2 liver cancer cells exhibited a striking and distinctive death signature, fundamentally differing from other tested cell lines. While early apoptotic events increased modestly (from 0.58% to 3.63%, 6.3-fold), late apoptotic populations surged dramatically from 0.11% to 19.17%, an extraordinary 174-fold increase. This represents the highest fold-change across all cell death parameters in any tested line, indicating a remarkable shift towards late-stage apoptosis. Necrotic death also increased moderately from 1.49% to 3.62% (2.4-fold). The total cell death percentage reached 26.42%, representing a 12.1-fold increase from control. The predominant late apoptosis in HepG-2cells is a vital finding. Studies on other natural compounds have shown similar effects on HepG-2 cells. For instance, ethyl acetate subfractions (EASs) from ethanol extracts of oats fermented by Rhizopus oryzae 3.2751 were shown to induce apoptosis in HepG-2 cells in a dose-dependent manner [66]. While direct comparisons with Aspergillus oryzae on HepG-2 cells with detailed flow cytometry profiles are less common in recent literature, the observed strong induction of late apoptosis suggests a potent and specific mechanism of action in these cells. This could involve mitochondrial dysfunction leading to a delayed but ultimately significant apoptotic response. The findings of this study generally align with the growing body of literature supporting the anticancer potential of Aspergillus oryzae and other fungal extracts. Many studies report the ability of these extracts to induce apoptosis in various cancer cell lines, often through diverse mechanisms. For example, the significant induction of early apoptosis in A549 and Caco-2 cells is consistent with the general pro-apoptotic activity reported for Aspergillus species [84, 87]. The unique response of MCF-7 cells, with a notable necrotic component, underscores the importance of cell line-specific investigations. While some studies on Aspergillus oryzae have reported anticancer effects on MCF-7 cells [86], the specific balance between apoptosis and necrosis can vary depending on the extract’s composition and concentration. The most striking finding, the dramatic induction of late apoptosis in HepG-2 cells, suggests a potent and potentially novel mechanism of action that warrants further exploration. This specific apoptotic profile in HepG-2 cells, with such a high fold-change in late apoptosis, appears to be a distinctive feature of the Aspergillus oryzae extract used in this study, differentiating it from some other reported effects where early apoptosis might be more dominant. The varied responses across cell lines emphasize the complex interplay between the extract’s bioactive compounds and the specific cellular pathways involved in different cancer types. Building on these phenotypic results, future work will employ molecular docking and dynamics simulations to model the interactions of key identified metabolites—such as oleic acid (22.78%), linoleic acid, and 7-hydroxyflavone—with relevant cancer targets. This computational approach will help generate testable hypotheses to precisely define the binding mechanisms that may underlie the observed cancer cell selectivity.

Conclusion

This study explores the anticancer potential of microorganisms isolated from Egyptian bee workers, ultimately identifying Aspergillus oryzae as a highly promising therapeutic candidate. Through systematic screening of 113 microbial isolates from three governorates, we discovered that A. oryzae extract demonstrates exceptional selectivity against cancer cells while sparing normal cells, with a remarkable selectivity index of 18.21. The study employed advanced optimization techniques to maximize bioactive compound production, achieving optimal fermentation conditions that yielded 1.86 g of the crude extract /100 ml of the fermentation medium. Chemical characterization revealed 65 bioactive compounds, primarily fatty acids known for their therapeutic properties. Comprehensive cytotoxicity testing across multiple cancer cell lines confirmed potent anticancer activity, with IC50 values as low as 30.63 µg/ml against liver cancer cells. Mechanistic studies using flow cytometry and live-dead staining revealed that the extract induces cancer cell death through apoptotic pathways, with different cancer types showing distinct death signatures. The study demonstrates that bee-associated fungi represent an untapped reservoir of anticancer compounds, offering a sustainable and novel approach to cancer therapy development with significant potential for pharmaceutical applications.
