Electrochemical charge-storage behavior of olive-stone-derived rGO anchored with Co–Ni ferrites
Authors: Mohammed Alshammari, Nasser Alqahtani, Fahad M. Albaqi, Manal Khered, Nawaf Bin Darwish, Fehaid M. Alsubaie, Raja Alotaibi, Reda S. Salama, Ahmad Aladeem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Biomass-derived carbon materials provide sustainable platforms for electrochemical energy-storage electrodes. In this study, graphene oxide was synthesized from olive stones and combined with mixed cobalt–nickel ferrites, CoxNi1−xFe2O4 (x = 0.25, 0.50, and 0.75), through an in situ co-precipitation route followed by thermal treatment. The treatment partially reduced GO to rGO and enabled the anchoring of spinel ferrite nanoparticles on the carbon framework. XRD, FTIR, BET, SEM, EDX, TEM, and XPS analyses confirmed the formation of rGO-supported Co–Ni ferrites with controlled Co/Ni ratios, mesoporous texture, and nanoscale ferrite domains. Electrochemical measurements in 3.0 M KOH showed that the Ni-rich Co0.25Ni0.75Fe2O4/rGO electrode delivered a specific capacitance of 697 F g−1 at 1.0 A g−1 and retained 93.5% of its initial capacitance after 10 000 cycles. EIS analysis showed lower charge-transfer resistance for this composition, indicating improved electron-transfer kinetics and electrode–electrolyte interfacial behavior. The improved charge-storage response is associated with the combined contribution of Ni/Co redox activity, reduced crystallite size, mesoporosity, and conductive rGO pathways. A symmetric two-electrode device assembled from Co0.25Ni0.75Fe2O4/rGO operated within 0–1.2 V and delivered 264 F g−1. These results show how Co/Ni compositional tuning and biomass-derived rGO support affect pseudocapacitive charge storage in alkaline electrolyte.

Introduction

The rapid expansion of industrial activities and population growth has led to the generation of enormous amounts of agricultural, industrial, and municipal waste, posing serious environmental and economic challenges worldwide.1 Over the past few years, increasing research interest has focused on transforming waste materials into value-added products, particularly functional materials for energy, environmental, and catalytic applications.2,3 Agricultural wastes like coconut shell, sugarcane bagasse, rice husk, fruit peels, and olive residues are rich in carbon and inorganic components, making them promising precursors for advanced materials.4–7 The transformation of such wastes into carbon-based nanomaterials, metal oxides, and hybrid composites not only reduces landfill disposal and pollution but also supports sustainable development and circular economy principles.8,9 Among these wastes, olive stones are produced in substantial quantities as a by-product of the olive oil industry, especially in Mediterranean regions, yet remain underutilized despite their high carbon content and low cost. Consequently, transforming biomass waste into high-value carbon nanostructures, especially graphene-based materials, has become a promising approach for meeting advanced material requirements while supporting environmental sustainability.10

Graphene oxide (GO) is widely recognized as a highly versatile two-dimensional carbon material because of its distinctive structure, adjustable electronic characteristics, large surface area, and rich oxygen-containing functional groups.11,12 These properties make GO a promising material for diverse applications, including energy storage, sensors, catalysis, water treatment, and biomedicine.13–15 GO is typically synthesized by chemically oxidizing graphite through approaches such as the Hummers, modified Hummers, and improved oxidation methods, which incorporate carbonyl, epoxy, hydroxyl, and carboxyl functional groups onto the graphene sheets.16–18 These functional groups enhance hydrophilicity and provide active sites for further chemical modification. However, conventional GO synthesis typically relies on graphite derived from non-renewable sources and involves harsh chemicals, raising concerns regarding sustainability and environmental impact.19 As a result, increasing research efforts are focused on developing biomass-derived graphene and graphene oxide materials using renewable carbon sources while maintaining desirable structural and electrochemical properties.20 Biomass-derived rGO should be understood as a reduced graphene oxide material obtained from renewable carbonaceous precursors rather than from conventional graphite or commercial carbon sources. Thus, its main distinction from conventional rGO is not a different carbon allotrope, but the precursor-dependent structure and chemistry produced during carbonization, oxidation, and thermal reduction. Biomass-derived carbon precursors can introduce defective, wrinkled, and oxygen-functionalized carbon frameworks that may assist nanoparticle anchoring, electrolyte accessibility, and interfacial contact in hybrid electrodes. In comparison, commercial activated carbon is widely used in supercapacitors because of its porous structure, low cost, and strong electric-double-layer charge-storage behavior; however, its capacitance is mainly governed by ion adsorption at the electrode/electrolyte interface and often requires coupling with redox-active components to improve pseudocapacitive contribution. Therefore, using olive-stone-derived rGO as a support for mixed Co–Ni ferrites provides both a sustainability advantage through agricultural waste valorization and a functional platform for dispersing ferrite nanoparticles within a conductive carbon framework.

To overcome the limitations of pristine GO, extensive efforts have been devoted to its modification with polymers, metal oxides, sulfides, phosphides, and conducting materials.21–23 Such modifications can significantly enhance charge transport, mechanical stability, and electrochemical activity. Among inorganic modifying agents, transition metal ferrites have gained considerable attention because of their affordability, chemical robustness, variable oxidation states, and composition-dependent properties. Ferrites with the general formula MFe2O4, where M represents Co, Ni, Mn, Zn, or other metal ions, possess spinel-type structures and have been extensively studied for use in catalysis, gas sensing, magnetic materials, adsorption processes, and energy storage applications.24–26 Cobalt and nickel ferrites are especially promising because of their rich faradaic redox chemistry and favorable electrical properties.27,28 Furthermore, mixed cobalt–nickel ferrites allow compositional tuning, enabling optimization of electrochemical behavior, conductivity, and structural stability for targeted applications.29 When integrated with conductive carbon frameworks such as graphene oxide, ferrites can display synergistic interactions that enhance their suitability for electrochemical energy storage applications.

The integration of graphene oxide with ferrite nanoparticles has become an efficient approach for designing advanced electrode materials for electrochemical energy storage. In these hybrid structures, GO offers a conductive framework with a large surface area that promotes ion adsorption, whereas ferrite nanoparticles provide pseudocapacitive faradaic activity.30,31 The strong interfacial coupling between GO sheets and ferrite nanoparticles can reduce particle agglomeration, facilitate electrolyte penetration, and promote faster charge-transfer processes.32,33 Several studies have shown that GO/ferrite-based composites deliver higher specific capacitance, better rate performance, and improved cycling durability than their separate components.32,33 Cobalt–nickel ferrite/graphene-based electrodes have shown promising performance in alkaline electrolytes due to redox activity and efficient electron transport pathways. Despite these advances, the use of biomass-derived GO combined with compositionally tuned ferrites for supercapacitor applications remains relatively unexplored.

In this study, we present a sustainable route for producing graphene oxide from olive stones as a renewable biomass-derived carbon precursor and its subsequent anchoring with mixed cobalt–nickel ferrites (CoxNi1−xFe2O4, x = 0.25, 0.50, and 0.75). The hybrid nanocomposites were synthesized through an in situ co-precipitation route followed by thermal treatment. This process promoted the partial conversion of GO into rGO and enabled the homogeneous anchoring of ferrite nanoparticles onto the rGO surface. The structural, morphological, textural, and chemical composition features of the prepared materials were comprehensively examined using XRD, FTIR, BET, SEM, TEM, EDX, and XPS analyses. The electrochemical behavior of the prepared materials was assessed as supercapacitor electrodes in 3.0 M KOH using both three-electrode and two-electrode cell configurations. The effect of the Co/Ni ratio on rate performance, specific capacitance, and cycling durability was also investigated. This work demonstrates the promise of olive stone-derived GO/ferrite composites as sustainable and efficient electrode materials for advanced supercapacitor applications. From a scale-up perspective, this synthesis strategy has practical potential because it uses olive-stone waste as an abundant and low-cost biomass precursor, while the Co–Ni ferrite phase is introduced through a solution-based in situ co-precipitation process followed by thermal treatment. These steps are relatively simple compared with template-assisted or highly specialized nanofabrication routes and may be adapted to larger batch preparation. However, practical scale-up still requires optimization of acid consumption, washing-water use, waste treatment, thermal energy demand, biomass batch-to-batch consistency, reproducible electrode coating, and validation in larger-area devices.

Experimental

Materials

Olive stones were collected from local olive oil processing residues and used as the biomass-derived carbon precursor. Cobalt nitrate hexahydrate (≥99.0%, Sigma-Aldrich), nickel nitrate hexahydrate (≥98.0%, Sigma-Aldrich), and iron nitrate nonahydrate (≥98.0%, Sigma-Aldrich) were used as metal precursors for ferrite synthesis. Potassium permanganate (≥99.0%, Merck), sulfuric acid (98%, Merck), hydrogen peroxide (30%, Merck), hydrochloric acid (37%, Merck), sodium hydroxide (≥98.0%, Merck), and potassium hydroxide (≥99.0%, Merck) were used during oxidation, washing, pH adjustment, and electrochemical testing. All chemicals were of analytical grade and used as received without further purification. Deionized water was used throughout all synthesis and washing procedures. Nickel foam with an approximate thickness of 1.6 mm and porosity of 95% was used as the current collector for electrode fabrication.

Preparation of GO from olive stones

GO was prepared from olive stones through carbonization followed by chemical oxidation using an improved Hummers-type method. Initially, olive stones were rinsed thoroughly with deionized water to remove adhering surface impurities and then dried at 100 °C for 24 h. The dried biomass was milled into a fine powder, after which 20.0 g of the resulting powder was carbonized in a tubular furnace under a nitrogen atmosphere with a gas flow rate of approximately 100 mL min−1. The furnace temperature was raised to 800 °C at a heating rate of 5 °C min−1 and maintained for 2 h, followed by natural cooling to room temperature. The obtained biochar was purified by treatment with 200 mL of 1.0 M HCl (0.200 mol) under continuous stirring for 2 h to eliminate inorganic residues. The acid-treated product was then washed several times with deionized water until the washing solution reached a near-neutral pH and dried overnight at 80 °C. For the oxidation stage, 3.00 g of the purified olive stone-derived graphitic carbon was gradually dispersed into a mixed acid solution containing concentrated H2SO4 (360 mL, 98%, 6.61 mol) and H3PO4 (40 mL, 85%, 0.585 mol) under continuous stirring in an ice bath. Thereafter, KMnO4 (18.0 g, 0.114 mol) was added slowly in portions while maintaining the reaction temperature below 10 °C. The reaction mixture was heated to 50 °C and stirred for 12 h to achieve complete oxidation. After cooling to room temperature, the oxidation reaction was terminated by carefully pouring the mixture over ice containing deionized water, followed by the slow addition of H2O2 solution (30 mL, 30%, 0.294 mol). This step produced a yellow-brown suspension, indicating the reduction of residual oxidizing species. The resulting product was separated by centrifugation and washed successively with 1.0 M HCl and deionized water until a neutral pH was reached. The purified material was ultrasonicated for 1 h to promote exfoliation and obtain GO sheets. The obtained GO was then dried at low temperature (≤60 °C) or stored as a stable aqueous dispersion for further use. Preparation of CoxNi1−xFe2O4/rGO

CoxNi1−xFe2O4 nanoparticles were synthesized and simultaneously anchored onto graphene oxide sheets via an in situ co-precipitation method. For a typical synthesis, GO (100 mg) was first dispersed in 100 mL of DI water through ultrasonication for 1 h to form a homogeneous and stable suspension. Separately, iron nitrate nonahydrate (2 mmol), together with the required molar proportions of cobalt nitrate hexahydrate and nickel nitrate hexahydrate, was dissolved in deionized water. The prepared metal precursor solutions were then introduced dropwise into the GO suspension under vigorous stirring. The Co : Ni molar ratios were adjusted to 0.25 : 0.75, 0.50 : 0.50, and 0.75 : 0.25 to obtain Co0.25Ni0.75Fe2O4/rGO, Co0.5Ni0.5Fe2O4/rGO, and Co0.75Ni0.25Fe2O4/rGO, respectively. The pH of the reaction mixture was adjusted to about 10 by the gradual addition of 2.0 M NaOH under continuous stirring at 80 °C, and the suspension was kept under these conditions for 2 h to promote ferrite formation. After cooling, the obtained precipitate was collected and washed several times with deionized water and ethanol, followed by drying at 80 °C. The dried products were then calcined at 500 °C for 3 h, which promoted the thermal conversion of GO into reduced graphene oxide (rGO) and produced the final CoxNi1−xFe2O4/rGO nanocomposites. A control graphene oxide sample was thermally treated under the same conditions at 500 °C for 3 h without adding any metal precursors, producing thermally reduced graphene oxide (rGO). For clarity, the synthesized samples are denoted as follows: thermally reduced graphene oxide (rGO); Co0.25Ni0.75Fe2O4/rGO (CNF-25); Co0.5Ni0.5Fe2O4/rGO (CNF-50); and Co0.75Ni0.25Fe2O4/rGO (CNF-75). Material characterization

The prepared GO, rGO, and CoxNi1−xFe2O4/rGO nanocomposites were comprehensively examined to evaluate their structural, surface, morphological, and chemical characteristics. FTIR spectroscopy was carried out using a PerkinElmer FTIR spectrometer over the wavenumber range of 400–4000 cm−1 to determine the surface functional groups and verify the presence of oxygenated groups, as well as metal–oxygen vibration bands. The crystal structure and phase composition of the synthesized samples were investigated by XRD using an XRD-7000 diffractometer (Shimadzu, Japan) with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) over a 2θ range of 10–80°. The microstructural characteristics and surface morphology were examined using SEM (JEOL JSM-6510LV) and TEM (JEOL JEM-2100). These analyses provided detailed insight into particle size, nanoparticle dispersion, and the interfacial contact between the ferrite particles and the rGO framework. For SEM measurements, the samples were fixed onto conductive carbon tape and coated with a thin gold layer, whereas TEM samples were prepared by dispersing the materials in ethanol and then dropping the suspension onto copper grids. Elemental composition and distribution were confirmed by EDX analysis coupled with the SEM system. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo Scientific ESCALAB 250Xi) was used to analyze the surface elemental composition and oxidation states of the samples. The pore characteristics and specific surface area were evaluated through N2 adsorption–desorption analysis at 77 K using a BELSORP-mini II analyzer.

Electrochemical measurements

Electrochemical measurements in the three-electrode configuration were completed using a Metrohm Autolab Potentiostat/Galvanostat system operated with NOVA software. The working electrode was fabricated by mixing the active material, either CoxNi1−xFe2O4/rGO, rGO, or GO, with acetylene black and polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) at a weight ratio of 80 : 10 : 10, where acetylene black acted as the conductive additive and PVDF served as the binder. The mixture was dispersed in N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP) to form a homogeneous slurry, which was uniformly coated onto pre-cleaned nickel foam substrates with an active area of 1 × 1 cm2. The active material loading was determined by weighing each nickel foam substrate before and after slurry coating and drying. For the 1 × 1 cm2 nickel foam electrodes, the active material loading was approximately 0.5745 mg cm−2 for GO, 0.5974 mg cm−2 for rGO, 0.5487 mg cm−2 for CNF-25, 0.6045 mg cm−2 for CNF-50, and 0.5912 mg cm−2 for CNF-75. The mass used in the capacitance calculation refers only to the deposited active electrode material and does not include the mass of the nickel foam current collector. Electrochemical measurements were performed at room temperature in 3.0 M KOH aqueous electrolyte using a three-electrode configuration, where the prepared electrode, platinum wire, and Ag/AgCl electrode were used as the working, counter, and reference electrodes, respectively. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) tests were recorded within a potential window of 0.0–-0.7 V at scan rates of 5, 10, 30, 50, and 100 mV s−1 to investigate the charge-storage mechanism and redox behavior. Galvanostatic charge–discharge (GCD) measurements were carried out at current densities of 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, 3.0, and 5.0 A g−1 to evaluate the capacitive performance. Results and discussion

X-ray diffraction pattern (XRD)

The crystalline structure and phase composition of GO, rGO, and CoxNi1−xFe2O4/rGO nanocomposites were examined by X-ray diffraction analysis and displayed in Fig. 1. The XRD pattern of GO exhibits a distinct diffraction peak at 2θ = 9.2°, which is assigned to the (001) reflection of graphene oxide. This peak confirms the successful oxidation of the olive-stone-derived graphitic carbon and the expansion of the interlayer distance. Based on Bragg's law and assuming Cu Kα radiation with λ = 1.5406 Å, the corresponding d-spacing is approximately 0.960 nm. The enlarged interlayer spacing is attributed to the incorporation of oxygen-containing functional groups and possible intercalated water molecules between the oxidized carbon sheets. After thermal treatment at 500 °C, the GO peak at 9.2° disappears and is replaced by a broad diffraction hump centered at 2θ = 21.5°, corresponding to the poorly ordered (002) reflection of rGO. This broad feature indicates the partial restoration of graphitic stacking after reduction while confirming that considerable structural disorder remains. The broad rGO-related hump is also observed in the CNF-25, CNF-50, and CNF-75 composites, together with the characteristic diffraction peaks of the Co–Ni ferrite phase, confirming the successful formation of the CoxNi1−xFe2O4/rGO nanocomposites. The broad nature of this hump suggests partially restored graphitic domains with disordered stacking and few-layer characteristics typical of reduced graphene oxide (rGO). In the CoxNi1−xFe2O4/rGO nanocomposites, additional distinct diffraction peaks appear at approximately 2θ ≈ 28.4°, 33.1°, 35.8°, 38.1°, 40.5°, and 44.7°, confirming the formation of the crystalline spinel ferrite phase. Among these, the peak at 28.4° is the most intense reflection, indicating a preferred growth orientation of the ferrite crystallites. The systematic appearance of these reflections confirms the successful nucleation and growth of Co–Ni ferrite nanoparticles on the rGO sheets.24,34 Furthermore, a clear increase in peak intensity is observed as the cobalt content increases from CNF-25 to CNF-75. This enhancement in diffraction intensity suggests improved crystallinity and higher structural ordering in Co-rich samples. Cobalt substitution is known to promote lattice stabilization and enhance crystal growth due to its slightly different ionic radius and stronger metal–oxygen bonding compared to nickel, which facilitates improved crystallite development during calcination. The absence of extra diffraction peaks confirms the high phase purity of the synthesized composites and indicates that no secondary oxide phases were formed during the synthesis process.

The average crystallite size of the ferrite nanoparticles was estimated using the Scherrer equation35 applied to the most intense reflection peak (28.4°) and was found to be in the nanometer range, as summarized in Table 1. The calculated crystallite sizes reveal a gradual increase with increasing cobalt content, following the trend: CNF-25 < CNF-50 < CNF-75. The progressive increase in crystallite size can be attributed to the effect of Co incorporation on the growth and ordering of the spinel ferrite lattice during calcination. As the Co fraction increases, Co2+ ions progressively replace Ni2+ ions in the CoxNi1−xFe2O4 lattice. The difference between Co2+ and Ni2+ in ionic size, site preference, and metal–oxygen bonding environment can modify nucleation and grain-growth behavior, leading to improved crystallinity and larger coherent diffraction domains. In Co-rich samples, enhanced lattice ordering and possible grain coalescence during thermal treatment reduce peak broadening, which is reflected in the larger crystallite size calculated from the Scherrer equation.36 The larger crystallite size in CNF-75 suggests that cobalt incorporation promotes crystal growth during thermal treatment, likely due to enhanced diffusion kinetics and stronger Co–O bonding interactions within the spinel lattice. In contrast, the Ni-rich CNF-25 sample exhibits relatively smaller crystallites, which implies a higher density of grain boundaries and more exposed electrochemically active sites. Smaller crystallite size is particularly advantageous for supercapacitor applications, as it shortens ion diffusion pathways, increases surface-to-volume ratio, and enhances electrolyte accessibility. Therefore, although Co-rich samples demonstrate improved crystallinity, the smaller crystallite size of CNF-25 contributes significantly to its electrochemical performance by providing a larger active surface area and facilitating faster redox kinetics.

FT-IR spectroscopy

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was employed to investigate the functional groups, chemical bonding, and interfacial interactions in GO, rGO, and CoxNi1−xFe2O4/rGO nanocomposites, as shown in Fig. 2. The spectrum of GO displays a broad absorption band centered at 3400 cm−1, which is attributed to O–H stretching vibrations originating from hydroxyl groups and adsorbed water molecules.37,38 A distinct band at 1880 cm−1 is observed and can be assigned to overtone or combination vibrations associated with carbonyl functionalities in oxidized graphene structures.39 The absorption band appearing at 1407 cm−1 corresponds to C–OH bending vibrations and/or symmetric stretching of carboxylate groups, confirming the oxidation of the carbon framework.40 In addition, the peak at 1177 cm−1 is assigned to C–O–C stretching vibrations of epoxy groups, while the band at 919 cm−1 is associated with epoxy deformation or out-of-plane C–H bending vibrations. The peak at 762 cm−1 arises from aromatic C–H out-of-plane bending, indicating the presence of graphitic domains within the carbon matrix.41

FTIR spectra of (a) GO, (b) rGO, (c) CNF-25, (d) CNF-50, and (e) CNF-75 composites.

After thermal reduction, the spectrum of rGO retains the same characteristic bands; however, a noticeable decrease in the intensity of oxygen-containing functional groups is observed, particularly for the hydroxyl and epoxy-related vibrations. This reduction in band intensity confirms partial removal of oxygen functionalities and restoration of the conjugated sp2 carbon network, indicating successful conversion of GO into reduced graphene oxide while preserving the graphene backbone structure.42,43 For the CoxNi1−xFe2O4/rGO nanocomposites, the characteristic carbon-related bands remain present, demonstrating that the graphene framework is maintained after ferrite anchoring. Importantly, two additional absorption bands emerge in the low-wavenumber region. The band located at 615 cm−1 is attributed to metal–oxygen stretching vibrations at tetrahedral sites of the spinel lattice. The second band appearing at 495 cm−1 corresponds to metal–oxygen vibrations at octahedral sites. These bands arise from Fe–O, Co–O, and Ni–O bonds and are characteristic of the cubic spinel ferrite structure. Their presence confirms the successful formation of Co–Ni ferrite nanoparticles and their integration with the rGO sheets.44,45 The observed vibrations originate from Fe–O, Co–O, and Ni–O bonds and confirm the successful formation of the Co–Ni ferrite phase.

Analysis of textural properties

The textural properties of GO, rGO, and CoxNi1−xFe2O4/rGO nanocomposites were investigated by nitrogen adsorption–desorption measurements, and the corresponding BET isotherms are shown in Fig. 3. All samples exhibit type IV adsorption–desorption isotherms with an H3 hysteresis loop, indicating the presence of mesoporous structures with slit-shaped pores, which are characteristic of layered graphene-based materials. The GO sample shows a specific surface area of 196.3 m2 g−1, reflecting partial exfoliation and the presence of oxygen-containing functional groups that promote interlayer spacing. After thermal reduction, the surface area increases markedly to 352.2 m2 g−1 for rGO, confirming effective removal of oxygen functionalities and enhanced exfoliation of graphene sheets. This substantial increase demonstrates the successful conversion of biomass-derived GO into highly porous rGO. Upon incorporation of CoxNi1−xFe2O4 nanoparticles, a gradual decrease in BET surface area is observed for the composite samples, with values of 334.1, 305.5, and 297.0 m2 g−1 for CNF-25, CNF-50, and CNF-75, respectively, as listed in Table 1. This reduction is attributed to partial coverage of the rGO surface by ferrite nanoparticles, pore blocking effects, and the increased mass density of the hybrid materials. Despite this decrease, the composites retain a relatively high surface area and preserve their mesoporous nature, which is favorable for electrolyte ion diffusion.

Nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherms and pore size distribution (inset) of the investigated samples: (a) GO, (b) rGO, (c) CNF-25, (d) CNF-50, and (e) CNF-75 composites.

The pore size distribution of GO, rGO, and CoxNi1−xFe2O4/rGO nanocomposites was further analyzed using the BJH method and was displayed in Fig. 3 (inset). The GO sample exhibits an average pore diameter of 8.6 nm, which is mainly attributed to the interlayer spacing created by abundant oxygen-containing functional groups and partial exfoliation of graphene sheets. After thermal reduction, the average pore diameter decreases to 7.2 nm for rGO, indicating partial restacking of graphene layers accompanied by the formation of slit-shaped mesopores between wrinkled sheets. Upon incorporation of CoxNi1−xFe2O4 nanoparticles, a gradual reduction in pore diameter is observed, with values of 6.6, 6.8, and 6.9 nm for CNF-25, CNF-50, and CNF-75, respectively, as listed in Table 1. This progressive decrease can be attributed to partial pore filling and narrowing caused by the anchoring of ferrite nanoparticles and nanoneedles on the rGO surface. Despite the reduction in pore diameter, all composite samples remain within the mesoporous range (2–50 nm), which is highly favorable for electrolyte ion diffusion and charge transport. Notably, CNF-25 sample retains relatively larger mesopores among the ferrite-loaded composites, facilitating efficient ion accessibility and contributing to electrochemical performance.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was used to investigate the morphology and microstructure of rGO and CoxNi1−xFe2O4/rGO nanocomposites, as shown in Fig. 4. The TEM image of rGO shows thin, wrinkled, and semi-transparent sheets, indicating the formation of few-layer rGO. The folded edges and contrast variation suggest partial restacking of graphene layers after thermal reduction. In the CoxNi1−xFe2O4/rGO composites, the ferrite phase is uniformly distributed on the rGO surface without noticeable agglomeration, indicating strong interfacial interaction between the ferrite nanoparticles and the carbon matrix. The ferrite nanoparticles exhibit a hierarchical flower-like morphology composed of radially oriented nanoneedles, with an average length of approximately 10 nm for CNF-25 sample. A gradual increase in nanoneedle length and image contrast is observed with increasing cobalt content for CNF-50 and CNF-75, which can be attributed to cobalt-induced anisotropic crystal growth, higher electron density, and increased mass thickness of the ferrite nanostructures. The intimate contact between the nanoneedles and rGO sheets facilitates efficient electron transport, while the hierarchical architecture provides abundant exposed active sites, short ion diffusion pathways, and interconnected open channels. Although Co-rich samples exhibit larger and denser nanostructures, the Ni-rich CNF-25 composite maintains a more open and finely dispersed morphology, which correlates well with its higher surface area, smaller crystallite size, and excellent electrochemical performance.

SEM images and EDX analysis

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was employed to examine the surface morphology, particle distribution, and overall microstructural features of rGO and CoxNi1−xFe2O4/rGO nanocomposites at the microscale as displayed in Fig. 5. The rGO sample in Fig. 5a exhibits folded, crumpled, and wrinkled sheet-like structures. This observation is consistent with the TEM image in Fig. 4, which shows thin and folded rGO sheets. This observation is consistent with the TEM image in Fig. 4, which shows thin and folded rGO sheets. Following the incorporation of CoxNi1−xFe2O4, the CNF-25, CNF-50, and CNF-75 samples exhibit rough surfaces composed of aggregated nanoparticles distributed over the carbon framework, supporting the formation of ferrite/rGO hybrid structures. The apparent bright and dark contrast in the CNF composite images does not represent two actual colours or allow direct phase identification. Because the images were acquired using a secondary-electron detector, the grayscale contrast is influenced mainly by surface topography, particle elevation, edge effects, orientation, shadowing, and possible local charging. Therefore, the brighter and darker regions cannot be assigned exclusively to the ferrite and rGO phases based on SEM contrast alone. The elemental composition and distribution of the Co–Ni ferrite phase on the rGO support are supported more reliably by EDX analysis. CNF-25 shows relatively fine and uniformly distributed particles with visible interparticle spaces. CNF-50 and CNF-75 exhibit more pronounced aggregation and denser particle assemblies, consistent with the gradual increase in particle size observed by TEM.

Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) was utilized to analyze the elemental composition and spatial distribution of the constituent elements in the CoxNi1−xFe2O4/rGO nanocomposites as displayed in Fig. 6. The EDX spectra confirm the presence of carbon (C), oxygen (O), cobalt (Co), nickel (Ni), and iron (Fe). No extraneous elemental signals were detected within the EDX detection limit, supporting the effective removal of residual precursors during washing and calcination. Quantitative EDX analysis reveals that the experimentally determined atomic ratios of Co, Ni, and Fe are in close agreement with the theoretical stoichiometric compositions of CoxNi(1−x)Fe2O4 for CNF-25 (Co0.25Ni0.75Fe2O4), CNF-50 (Co0.5Ni0.5Fe2O4), and CNF-75 (Co0.75Ni0.25Fe2O4), confirming precise control over the Co/Ni ratio during synthesis.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was employed to investigate the surface elemental composition and chemical states of the optimized CNF-25 (Co0.25Ni0.75Fe2O4/rGO) nanocomposite as displayed in Fig. 7. The XPS survey spectrum confirms the presence of carbon (C 1s), oxygen (O 1s), iron (Fe 2p), cobalt (Co 2p), and nickel (Ni 2p), with no detectable impurity elements, indicating high surface purity of the synthesized material. The dominant carbon signal originates from the rGO framework, while oxygen arises from both residual oxygen-containing functional groups on rGO and metal–oxygen bonds in the ferrite phase. The clear detection of Fe, Co, and Ni signals confirms the successful anchoring of cobalt–nickel ferrite nanoparticles on the rGO surface. The relative intensities of the metal peaks follow the expected trend based on the designed Co/Ni ratio in the CNF-25 composition, demonstrating effective control over the elemental incorporation during synthesis.

The high-resolution Fe 2p XPS spectrum of the CNF-25 nanocomposite is shown in Fig. 7d and exhibits well-defined features characteristic of iron in a spinel ferrite environment. The spectrum displays a prominent Fe 2p3/2 peak centered at 711.4 eV, which can be deconvoluted into two components attributed to Fe3+ species within the spinel lattice. The corresponding Fe 2p1/2 peak appears at 724.3 eV, confirming the spin–orbit splitting characteristic of iron oxides.50 In addition, a distinct satellite peak is observed at approximately 718.7 eV, which is a signature feature of Fe3+ ions and provides strong evidence for the trivalent oxidation state of iron in the Co0.25Ni0.75Fe2O4 structure. The absence of low-binding-energy features associated with metallic iron or Fe2+-dominated phases indicates that iron is predominantly present as Fe3+ and is fully incorporated into the spinel ferrite lattice. The high-resolution Co 2p XPS spectrum of the CNF-25 nanocomposite is presented in Fig. 7e and exhibits characteristic features associated with cobalt species in a spinel ferrite structure. The spectrum shows a prominent Co 2p3/2 main peak centered at 780.7 eV, which can be deconvoluted into two components corresponding to cobalt ions occupying different crystallographic sites. The peak at 779.6 eV is assigned to Co2+ ions located at octahedral sites, while the component at 782.2 eV corresponds to Co2+ ions at tetrahedral sites within the spinel lattice. The corresponding Co 2p1/2 peak appears at 795.4 eV, with similar site-dependent contributions.51 In addition, distinct satellite peaks are observed at 786.4 eV and 803.1 eV, which are characteristic shake-up features of Co2+ species and confirm the absence of metallic cobalt. The presence of well-defined satellite structures and the clear splitting of Co 2p peaks provide strong evidence that cobalt exists predominantly in the divalent oxidation state and is successfully incorporated into the spinel ferrite framework. The high-resolution Ni 2p XPS spectrum of the CNF-25 nanocomposite is shown in Fig. 7f and exhibits characteristic features of nickel incorporated into a spinel ferrite structure. The spectrum displays a dominant Ni 2p3/2 peak centered at 855.4 eV, which is attributed to Ni2+ species in different local coordination environments within the ferrite lattice.52 The corresponding Ni 2p1/2 peak appears at 872.9 eV, confirming the expected spin–orbit splitting of nickel. In addition, well-defined satellite peaks are observed at 862.2 eV and 879.7 eV, which are characteristic shake-up features of Ni2+ ions and further confirm the divalent oxidation state of nickel. The absence of peaks associated with metallic nickel or Ni3+-dominated phases indicates that nickel is stably incorporated into the Co0.25Ni0.75Fe2O4 spinel structure.

Electrochemical application

The electrochemical charge-storage behavior of rGO and CoxNi1−xFe2O4/rGO nanocomposite electrodes was initially investigated by cyclic voltammetry (CV) using a three-electrode configuration in 3.0 M KOH electrolyte, as presented in Fig. 8. The CV curves were recorded within a potential window of 0.0–0.7 V at scan rates of 5, 10, 30, 50, and 100 mV s−1. The rGO electrode shows a nearly rectangular CV profile with a relatively low current response and no pronounced redox peaks, indicating that its charge-storage behavior is mainly governed by electric double-layer capacitance (EDLC). In contrast, all CoxNi1−xFe2O4/rGO composite electrodes exhibit clear anodic and cathodic redox features, confirming the contribution of faradaic pseudocapacitive reactions from the Co–Ni ferrite phase. The dual redox response can be attributed to the coupled valence transitions of Ni and Co active sites in alkaline electrolyte, mainly Ni2+/Ni3+ and Co2+/Co3+ redox processes. In simplified form, these surface reactions can be expressed as M–O + OH− ⇌ M–OOH + e−, where M represents Ni or Co surface sites. Additional Co-based oxidation may also occur through CoOOH + OH− ⇌ CoO2 + H2O + e−. It should be clarified that the electrons generated during these redox reactions are not transferred into the electrolyte. The KOH electrolyte mainly supplies OH− ions and provides ionic conductivity for charge balance. During the anodic scan, OH− ions interact with electroactive Ni and Co surface sites, while the released electrons are transported from the ferrite domains through the conductive rGO framework to the nickel foam current collector and then to the external circuit. During the reverse cathodic scan, electrons return through the nickel foam/rGO pathway and reduce the higher-valence Ni/Co species back to their lower-valence states. Therefore, the observed current response originates mainly from the CNF composites, while KOH enables OH− transport and interfacial redox reactions. This assignment is supported by the rGO electrode, which was tested in the same 3.0 M KOH electrolyte and did not show comparable redox peaks. Although the current response is moderate, the systematic appearance of redox peaks in the CNF composites, together with the non-linear GCD profiles and the lower charge-transfer resistance observed from EIS, confirms their pseudocapacitive behavior. Among the investigated compositions, CNF-25 exhibits the largest enclosed CV area and the highest current response at all scan rates, indicating more efficient charge storage. This enhanced behavior is attributed to the Ni-rich composition, smaller crystallite size, higher surface area, ion-accessible mesoporosity, and conductive rGO pathways, which collectively improve OH− diffusion, electron transport, and utilization of redox-active sites. The CV area decreases gradually for CNF-50 and CNF-75, suggesting reduced electrochemically accessible active sites with increasing cobalt content, consistent with the larger crystallite size, denser morphology, and lower surface area of the Co-rich samples.

Cyclic voltammetry (CV) curves of the investigated electrodes: (a) GO, (b) rGO, (c) CNF-25, (d) CNF-50, and (e) CNF-75 composites recorded at different scan rates (5–100 mV s−1).

The charge–discharge behavior of rGO and CoxNi1−xFe2O4/rGO electrodes was further evaluated by galvanostatic charge–discharge (GCD) measurements using a three-electrode configuration in 3.0 M KOH electrolyte, as shown in Fig. 9. The GCD curves were recorded within a potential window of 0.0–0.7 V at various current densities of 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, 3.0, and 5.0 A g−1. The rGO electrode exhibits nearly linear and symmetric charge–discharge profiles with relatively short discharge times, characteristic of electric double-layer capacitive behavior. In contrast, all CoxNi1−xFe2O4/rGO composite electrodes display non-linear GCD curves with clear voltage plateaus during both charging and discharging processes, confirming the dominance of faradaic pseudocapacitive reactions associated with transition-metal redox processes. Among the samples investigated, the Ni-rich CNF-25 electrode delivers the longest discharge time at all current densities, indicating higher charge storage capability. At a current density of 1.0 A g−1, the calculated specific capacitance values reach 697 F g−1, 597 F g−1, and 526 F g−1 for CNF-25, CNF-50, and CNF-75, respectively, demonstrating a clear dependence of capacitance on the Co/Ni ratio. The higher capacitance of CNF-25 is attributed to the enhanced redox activity of Ni species, smaller crystallite size, higher surface area, and more open pore structure, which collectively facilitate efficient ion diffusion and utilization of active sites. With increasing current density, all composite electrodes exhibit a gradual decrease in specific capacitance due to limited ion diffusion and increased polarization effects; however, the CNF-25 electrode maintains a higher capacitance retention compared to the other samples, reflecting its higher rate capability. These results confirm that optimizing the Co/Ni composition plays a critical role in enhancing the pseudocapacitive performance and rate behavior of CoxNi1−xFe2O4/rGO electrodes.

Galvanostatic charge–discharge (GCD) curves of the investigated electrodes: (a) GO, (b) rGO, (c) CNF-25, (d) CNF-50, and (e) CNF-75 composites recorded at different current densities (1.0–5.0 A g−1).

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was carried out to investigate the charge-transfer resistance, ion diffusion behavior, and interfacial properties of rGO and CoxNi1−xFe2O4/rGO electrodes in 3.0 M KOH using a three-electrode configuration, as shown in Fig. 10a. The Nyquist plots of all samples exhibit a depressed semicircle in the high-to-medium frequency region followed by an inclined straight line in the low-frequency region, indicating non-ideal interfacial charge transfer and diffusion-controlled ion transport, respectively. The diameter of the semicircle is associated with the apparent charge-transfer resistance (Rct) of the faradaic reactions at the electrode–electrolyte interface. Based on the approximate semicircle diameters, the apparent Rct values were estimated as 18.8 Ω for GO, 12.8 Ω for rGO, 9.9 Ω for CNF-50, 9.1 Ω for CNF-75, and 6.6 Ω for CNF-25. These approximate values indicate a progressive improvement in interfacial charge-transfer kinetics after the incorporation of the Co–Ni ferrite phase, with CNF-25 showing the lowest apparent resistance. The intercept of the Nyquist plot with the real axis (Z′) at high frequency corresponds to the equivalent series resistance (Rs), which includes the electrolyte resistance, intrinsic resistance of the active material, and contact resistance at the electrode-current collector interface. The high-frequency intercepts of the Nyquist plots appear close to the origin, indicating relatively low equivalent series resistance, which may be attributed to the high ionic conductivity of 3.0 M KOH and the good electrical contact provided by the nickel foam current collector. However, precise numerical Rs values were not reported because the intercepts could not be extracted reliably from the displayed curves without sufficiently resolved high-frequency data or equivalent-circuit fitting. The discussion therefore focuses on the comparative apparent semicircle diameters. CNF-25 shows the smallest apparent semicircle, indicating the lowest comparative charge-transfer resistance and faster interfacial faradaic kinetics. The reduced apparent Rct of CNF-25 indicates faster interfacial electron transfer during faradaic reactions in the alkaline electrolyte. The enhanced capacitance of CNF-25 can be explained by the coupled contribution of composition, structure, and charge-transfer kinetics. First, the Ni-rich composition provides a larger number of electrochemically active Ni sites, which participate in reversible Ni2+/Ni3+ redox transitions and increase the pseudocapacitive contribution. Second, Co species also contribute through Co2+/Co3+ redox activity and support electronic interaction within the mixed spinel ferrite framework. These Ni and Co redox processes operate cooperatively during charge–discharge, producing a stronger faradaic response than that obtained from the carbon framework alone. Third, the smaller crystallite size and mesoporous architecture of CNF-25 shorten ion-diffusion pathways and improve OH− accessibility to surface-active sites. Fourth, the rGO framework provides conductive pathways between ferrite domains and the nickel foam current collector, reducing polarization and facilitating electron transport. Therefore, the improved capacitance of CNF-25 is attributed to coupled Ni/Co redox activity, ion-accessible mesoporosity, smaller crystallite size, conductive rGO pathways, and lower charge-transfer resistance.

(a) Nyquist plots in 3.0 M KOH electrolyte and (b) cycling stability of the electrodes over 10 000 charge–discharge cycles at a constant current density (3.0 A g−1) for GO, rGO, CNF-25, CNF-50, and CNF-75 electrodes.

Long-term cycling stability is a critical parameter for evaluating the practical applicability of supercapacitor electrode materials. The cycling performance of GO, rGO and CoxNi1−xFe2O4/rGO electrodes was investigated using repeated galvanostatic charge–discharge measurements over 10 000 cycles at a constant current density of 3.0 A g−1 in 3.0 M KOH electrolyte, as illustrated in Fig. 10b. Among the composite samples, CNF-25 electrode demonstrates the best cycling durability, retaining 93.5% of its initial capacitance after 10 000 cycles. In comparison, CNF-50 retains 87.5%, while CNF-75 shows a slightly lower retention of 90.7% after the same number of cycles. The excellent cycling stability of CNF-25 can be attributed to its smaller crystallite size, more open porous structure, and strong interfacial interaction between the ferrite nanostructures and the rGO framework, which effectively buffers the volume changes associated with repeated faradaic redox reactions. In contrast, the relatively lower stability observed for CNF-50 and CNF-75 is likely due to their denser morphology and larger particle size, which can induce localized stress and partial structural degradation during long-term cycling. Overall, the high capacitance retention of all composite electrodes confirms their good structural integrity and electrochemical reversibility, with CNF-25 exhibiting the most promising long-term stability for practical supercapacitor applications.

To evaluate the practical applicability of the optimized material, a symmetric two-electrode supercapacitor device was assembled using CNF-25 as both positive and negative electrodes, and its electrochemical performance was systematically investigated in 3.0 M KOH electrolyte, as illustrated in Fig. 11. The cyclic voltammetry (CV) curves of the symmetric device recorded within a potential window of 0–1.2 V (Fig. 11a) exhibit quasi-rectangular shapes with noticeable redox humps, indicating a combined contribution of electric double-layer capacitance from rGO and pseudocapacitance arising from reversible redox reactions of Ni- and Co-based ferrite species. The preservation of CV shape at increasing scan rates demonstrates good electrochemical reversibility and efficient charge transport within the full-cell configuration. Galvanostatic charge–discharge (GCD) curves of the symmetric device (Fig. 11b) show nearly symmetric charge and discharge profiles with a small internal resistance drop, confirming low internal resistance and high coulombic efficiency. Based on the discharge curves, the CNF-25 symmetric supercapacitor delivers a specific capacitance of 264 F g−1, demonstrating that a substantial portion of the capacitance observed in the three-electrode configuration is retained under realistic device conditions.

Electrochemical performance of the CNF-25/rGO-based symmetric supercapacitor assembled from two identical electrodes: (a) cyclic voltammetry (CV) curves within 0.0–1.2 V at scan rates of 5–100 mV s−1; (b) galvanostatic charge–discharge (GCD) curves at different current densities; (c) Ragone plot derived from the GCD measurements; and (d) cycling stability over 10 000 charge–discharge cycles at 5 A g−1.

The Ragone plot of the symmetric CNF-25/rGO//CNF-25/rGO supercapacitor, derived from the galvanostatic charge–discharge data, is presented in Fig. 11c. As expected, the plot demonstrates an inverse relationship between energy density and power density, where the energy density gradually decreases with increasing power density due to shortened discharge times and kinetic limitations at higher current densities. At a low discharge current density of 1.0 A g−1, the symmetric device delivers a high specific capacitance of 264 F g−1, calculated based on the total mass of active material in both electrodes. Correspondingly, the device achieves a maximum energy density of 52.83 Wh kg−1 at a power density of 600 W kg−1 within a stable operating voltage window of 0–1.2 V. As the discharge current density increases, both the discharge time and specific capacitance decrease gradually, while the power density increases. For instance, at a current density of 1.5 A g−1, the device delivers an energy density of 46.5 Wh kg−1 at a power density of 900 W kg−1, whereas at 3.0 A g−1, the energy density decreases to 21.51 Wh kg−1 with a corresponding power density of 1800 W kg−1. At the highest tested current density of 5.0 A g−1, the device still maintains an energy density of 18.33 Wh kg−1 while delivering a high-power density of 3000 W kg−1, highlighting its excellent rate capability. These results confirm that the assembled symmetric supercapacitor maintains competitive energy output even under high power delivery conditions. Overall, the Ragone plot confirms the strong potential of the CNF-25/rGO-based symmetric supercapacitor for practical energy storage applications requiring both high energy density and rapid power delivery. The cycling stability of the symmetric CNF-25/rGO//CNF-25/rGO device was further evaluated under repeated GCD cycling, as shown in Fig. 11d. The device was tested at a current density of 5 A g−1 for 10 000 charge–discharge cycles in 3.0 M KOH electrolyte. The initial device capacitance was 91.7 F g−1 and decreased slightly to 86.7 F g−1 after 10 000 cycles, corresponding to 94.5% capacitance retention. This high retention confirms good device-level durability and indicates stable charge-storage behavior under practical two-electrode conditions. The stable performance can be attributed to the strong integration between the Co–Ni ferrite nanoparticles and the rGO framework, which helps preserve conductive pathways and maintain electrode integrity during repeated charge–discharge cycling.

Conclusion

In summary, a sustainable and high-performance supercapacitor electrode material was successfully developed by synthesizing graphene oxide from olive stone biomass and integrating it with mixed cobalt–nickel ferrite nanoparticles. The CoxNi1−xFe2O4/rGO nanocomposites were prepared via an in situ co-precipitation approach, resulting in uniform dispersion of spinel ferrite nanostructures on the conductive rGO framework. Structural and morphological analyses confirmed the formation of phase-pure ferrites with nanoscale crystallite size, hierarchical nanoneedle morphology, and preserved mesoporosity, while surface characterization revealed strong interfacial coupling between ferrite nanoparticles and rGO. Electrochemical evaluation in a three-electrode system using 3.0 M KOH demonstrated that CNF-25 electrode exhibited excellent pseudocapacitive behavior, delivering a high specific capacitance of 697 F g−1 at 1.0 A g−1, excellent rate capability, and high cycling stability with 93.5% capacitance retention after 10 000 cycles. As well as, the symmetric device also retained 94.5% of its initial capacitance after 10 000 cycles at 5 A g−1, confirming good device-level cycling durability. EIS analysis showed a lower charge-transfer resistance for CNF-25, while the high-frequency intercepts indicated low and comparable equivalent series resistance among the electrodes. In a symmetric two-electrode configuration, the CNF-25/rGO//CNF-25/rGO device operated stably within a 0–1.2 V voltage window and achieved a maximum energy density of 52.83 Wh kg−1 at a power density of 600 W kg−1, while maintaining 18.33 Wh kg−1 at 3000 W kg−1, as evidenced by Ragone analysis. Overall, the use of olive-stone waste and solution-based ferrite anchoring suggests promising scale-up potential, although further optimization of process efficiency, electrode loading, and larger-area device fabrication is required before practical implementation. Future work will also include dedicated layer-number analysis of the biomass-derived rGO support using HRTEM edge imaging or AFM thickness measurements to further clarify the structure–performance relationship.
