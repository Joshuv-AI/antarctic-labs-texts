Optimal Motion Planning for Two Square Robots in a Rectilinear Environment
Pankaj K. Agarwal, Mark de Berg, Benjamin Holmgren, Alex Steiger, Martijn Struijs

Abstract
Let 𝒲 ⊂ ℝ 2 \mathcal{W}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2} be a rectilinear polygonal environment (that is, a rectilinear polygon potentially with holes) with a total of n n vertices, and let A , B A,B be two robots, each modeled as an axis-aligned unit square, that can move rectilinearly inside 𝒲 \mathcal{W} . The goal is to compute a collision-free motion plan 𝝅 \boldsymbol{\pi} , that is, a motion plan that continuously moves A A from s A s_{A} to t A t_{A} and B B from s B s_{B} to t B t_{B} so that A A and B B remain inside 𝒲 \mathcal{W} and do not collide with each other during the motion. We study two variants of this problem which are focused additionally on the optimality of 𝝅 \boldsymbol{\pi} , and obtain the following results. • Min-Sum : Here the goal is to compute a motion plan that minimizes the sum of the lengths of the paths of the robots. We present an O ⁡ ( n 4 ​ log ⁡ n ) O(n^{4}\log{n}) -time algorithm for computing an optimal solution to the min-sum problem. This is the first polynomial-time algorithm to compute an optimal, collision-free motion of two robots amid obstacles in a planar polygonal environment. • Min-Makespan : Here the robots can move with at most unit speed, and the goal is to compute a motion plan that minimizes the maximum time taken by a robot to reach its target location. We prove that the min-makespan variant is NP-hard.

Introduction

Autonomous multi-robot systems are being increasingly used for a wide range of
tasks such as logistics in industry, precision
agriculture, exploration of confined and cluttered
environments,
search and rescue operations, and visual inspection of areas of interest.
These applications have led to extensive work on designing efficient algorithms for computing
high-quality–motion plans in a structured or an unstructured environment for a system of robots;
see e.g.
[
7
,
47
]
for recent surveys.
Several criteria have been proposed to measure the quality of a motion plan, including
the total length of the robot paths,
the make-span (i.e., the maximum time taken by a robot to complete its motion), or
by the utility of the plan (i.e., how well it performs the underlying task).
Already for two simple robots, such as unit squares or disks, translating in a planar polygonal environment,
little is known about computing an optimal motion plan.
Although polynomial-time algorithms are known for
computing a collision-free motion plan of two simple robots
[
8
,
37
]
, no polynomial-time algorithm is known even for computing a plan such that the sum (or the maximum) of the path lengths of
the two robots is minimized, nor is the problem known to be NP-hard. In this paper we study the
problem of computing an optimal motion plan for two simple robots, each modeled as a unit square.

T

Both variants of the optimal motion planning problem are interesting in their own right.
The first minimizes the total work done by the robots, while the second minimizes
the total time taken until both robots have reached their destination.

Related work.

It is beyond the scope of this paper to review the known results on
motion-planning algorithms; for a review of key relevant results,
we refer the reader to recent books and surveys on
the topic
[
22
,
23
,

.
We mention here only a small sample of results—ones that
are most closely related to the problem at hand.

When there is a single translating square robot, or more generally when there is a single
convex polygonal translating robot with a constant number of vertices, the problem is
equivalent—through C-space formulation—to moving a point robot amid polygonal
obstacles with
O(n)
vertices, and it can be solved in
O(n\log n)

time
[
14
,
25
,
46
]
.
The problem of computing
the shortest path for a point robot amid polyhedral obstacles in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}

is NP-hard
[
13
]
, and fast
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximation algorithms are known
[
15
,
5
]
.

Computing a feasible (not necessarily optimal) plan for a team of translating
unit square robots in a polygonal environment
is
PSPACE
-hard
[
40
]
;
see
[
11
,
12
,
24
,
26
,
43
,

,

for related intractability results.
Notwithstanding a rich literature
[

36
,
38
,
44
,
45
]
on multi-robot motion planning
in both continuous and discrete setting—robots move on a graph in the
latter setting—little
is known about algorithms computing plans with provable quality guarantees.
Kirkpatrick and Liu
[
29
]
presented an efficient algorithm
for computing the min-sum plan for unit disks in this setting and showed that an optimal plan always consists of at most six segments of
straight lines and circular arcs. Recently, similar results was derived for the cases of two
unit squares
[
21
,
35
]
and two centrally-symmetric convex polygons
[
30
]
.
Recently, there have been a few results for more than two robots in a setting without obstacles:
Deligkas
et al.

[
18
]
and Eiben
et al.

[
20

consider the problem on graphs minimizing the makespan in

et al.

[
27

minimize the number of “serial” or “parallel” moves of the robots by proving various structural properties of an optimal plan.

Approximation algorithms for minimizing the total path length are known
for unit-disk robots, assuming a certain minimum separation between
the source and target positions as well as from the
obstacles
[
3
,
39
,
42
]
.
The separation assumption makes the problem considerably easier:
a feasible plan always exists, and one can first compute an optimal path for each robot
independently (ignoring the other robots) and then locally modify the paths so that
the robots do not collide with each other during their motion.
For computing a plan that minimizes the makespan for a set of unit disks (or squares)
in the plane without obstacles, an
O(1)
-approximation algorithm was proposed

[

Recently, Agarwal

[

Their algorithm computes an

They leave it as an open problem whether an optimal plan in this case

We conclude our discussion by mentioning a few other lines of work on multi-robot

techniques in robotics is based on sampling

This paradigm has been used for multi-robot motion planning as well,

[

See also
[

Finally, another major line of work on optimizing multi-robot motion plans

In this setting the robots are often referred to as

Our contributions.

Our main result is that the min-sum motion planning problem for
unit square robots translating in a 2D polygonal environment is in
P
while the min-makespan problem in the same setting is
NP
-hard, as
stated in the two following theorems.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\mathcal{W}
be a closed rectilinear polygonal environment with
n
vertices, let

A,B
be two axis-parallel unit-square robots translating inside
\mathcal{W}
,
and let
\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{t}
be source and target configurations of
A,B
.
We can compute an optimal min-sum motion plan
\boldsymbol{\pi}
from
\boldsymbol{s}
to
\boldsymbol{t}
under
the
\ell_{1}
-metric, or determine that no feasible motion exists, in
O(n^{4}\log n)
time.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\mathcal{W}
be a closed rectilinear polygonal environment with
n
vertices, let

A,B
be two axis-parallel unit-square robots translating inside
\mathcal{W}
,
let
\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{t}
be source and target configurations of
A,B
,
and let
T_{\max}
be a given maximum time.
It is
NP
-hard to determine whether there is a
feasible
(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{t})
-plan
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}
such that the maximum speed of each robot is
at most 1 and the makespan of
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}
is at most
T_{\max}
.

We prove Theorem
1.2
by a reduction from the

Partition
problem, which is to decide whether a given set
Y
of integers
admits a partition into subsets

Y_{1},Y_{2}
such that
\sum_{p\in Y_{1}}p=\sum_{q\in Y_{2}}q
.
The idea of the reduction is to build a workspace
\mathcal{W}
that consists
of gadgets
\mathcal{W}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{W}_{m}
, each corresponding to an element of
Y
and to choose a parameter
T_{\max}\geqslant 0
,
so that both robots must pass through every gadget and there is
a plan with makespan at most
T_{\max}
if and only if there is a valid partition of
Y
. The reduction is described in detail in Section
5
.

Our main technical contribution is a polynomial-time exact algorithm for
Min-Sum
,
as stated in Theorem
1.1
.
The crucial step towards designing this algorithm is to prove the existence of
an optimal
canonical-grid plan
,
in which the path followed by each robot lies on an
O(n)\times O(n)

non-uniform predefined grid; see Theorem
2.1
.

For a single robot, the existence of such a plan is well known and easy to prove
[
34
]
:
since an optimal path in the
\ell_{1}
-metric is rectilinear,
one can push the segments of an optimal path in such a way that
each segment is either incident to the source or target position,
or a part of the segment contains an edge of the free space
\mathscr{F}
.
Importantly, pushing the path can be done without increasing its length.
Thus there is an optimal path on the grid defined by the horizontal
and vertical lines through the source and target positions and the lines
containing the edges of the free space
\mathscr{F}
.

For a pair of robots, the existence of an optimal plan on a suitably
defined grid seems intuitive as well. Indeed, consider an optimal plan

\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{t})
and imagine trying to push a segment of
\pi_{A}
in the same
manner as in the single-robot case. It may happen that we fail to push
the segment onto the grid because robot
B
is blocking it.
The hope is that we can continue to push the segment of
\pi_{A}

and push the appropriate segment of
\pi_{B}
in the same direction—essentially

A
is pushing
B
out of the way—until
B

hits
\partial\mathcal{W}
. Thus, we have pushed a segment of
\pi_{B}

onto a line containing an edge of
\mathscr{F}
and the segment of
\pi_{A}

onto a line at distance 1 from that line. Making this idea work
is highly nontrivial though. For instance, we must choose the
direction into which we push a specific segment of
\pi_{A}
in such a way
that
\|\pi_{A}\|
does not increase, but this
may force us to push
\pi_{B}
in such a way that
\|\pi_{B}\|
increases.
Hence, we have to choose the pushing directions carefully.
This is further complicated by the fact that
B
may be on
different sides of a segment of
\pi_{A}
at different moments in time.
Another major complication is that pushing (a segment of) one robot
onto a line at distance
i
from an edge of
\mathscr{F}
, may
cause the other robot to end up on a line at distance
i+1
from this edge.
If we are not careful, this can cause a cascading effect
and the final grid will not have the desired size.

To overcome these issues, we first prove several structural properties of an optimal plan.
Using these properies, we combine local pushing
operations with more global re-routing operations such
that the transformed paths end up on lines at distance 0, 1, or 2
from the edges of
\mathscr{F}
. These operations may increase the length
of one of the paths, but we will argue that when this happens
the length of the other path decreases by the same amount.
Thus we end up with a plan on a grid of size
O(n)\times O(n)

and that is still optimal.

Using the existence of an optimal canonical-grid plan,
we show that we can construct a
4
-dimensional weighted grid graph
\mathscr{G}=(\mathscr{V},\mathscr{E})
,
with
\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{t}\in\mathscr{V}
, of size
O(n^{4})
, in
\boldsymbol{F}

such that a shortest
(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{t}
)-path in
\mathscr{G}
corresponds to a min-sum
(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{t})
-plan.

A Polynomial-Time Algorithm for
Min-Sum

This section describes a polynomial-time algorithm for
Min-Sum
. We first define a

canonical-grid plan
in Section
2.1
—an
(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{t})
-plan where both robots move on a
predefined
O(n)\times O(n)
nonuniform grid—and claim that for any reachable
pair
\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{t}\in\boldsymbol{F}
, there exists an optimal
(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{t})
-plan that is a
canonical-grid plan. In Section
2.2
we give a high-level overview
of the proof of this claim, which proceeds by transforming an optimal plan
to a canonical-grid plan whose cost is no more than that of the original plan.
A detailed proof of the claim is deferred to Section
3
.
Finally, we describe our algorithm in Section
2.3
.

2.1
Canonical-grid plans

Rectilinear, decoupled plans.

Since
\mathcal{W}
is a rectilinear environment and
A,B
are squares, it is easily
seen that
\boldsymbol{F}
is a polyhedral region. Hence, if there is a feasible plan,
then there is a piecewise linear
optimal plan, that is, an optimal plan whose image
is a polygonal chain in
\mathcal{W}\times\mathcal{W}

[
4
]
.
We thus focus on piecewise-linear plans.
We refer to the vertices of such a plan as
breakpoints
.
For a path
\boldsymbol{\pi}=(\pi_{A},\pi_{B})
,
the breakpoints of
\pi_{A}
and
\pi_{B}
are the projections of the breakpoints of
\pi
.
Note that the two segments incident on a breakpoint of
\pi_{A}
(or
\pi_{B}
) may be collinear.

An
(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{t})
-plan
\boldsymbol{\pi}
is called
decoupled
if only one robot
moves at any given time; the other robot is then
parked
at some
point in
\mathscr{F}
. A decoupled plan can be represented as a sequence of

moves
, with each move specifying the parking location of one robot
and the motion of the other robot. By definition of breakpoints,
each parking spot of
A
(resp.
B
) is a breakpoint of
\pi_{A}
(resp.
\pi_{B}
).
Agarwal et al.
[
4
]
have shown that for any pair
\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{t}
of
reachable configurations, there is an optimal
(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{t})
-plan that is decoupled.
Since only one robot moves at a time, the parked robot can be considered
as an obstacle during the move. Thus, the moving robot moves from one position
(the start of the move) to another position (the end of the move) in
a rectilinear environment with obstacles (the original obstacles
plus the parked robot). This can always be done in an optimal
manner—that is, in a manner that minimizes the length
of the motion—with a rectilinear path.
Hence, we can ensure that each robot follows a rectilinear path in
\mathscr{F}
in each move.
We refer to such a plan as a
rectilinear
plan. The breakpoints of
\pi_{A}

and
\pi_{B}
in a rectilinear plan
\boldsymbol{\pi}=(\pi_{A},\pi_{B})

are the parking spots of
A
and
B
and the points at which they switch between horizontal and vertical segments.
We thus conclude the following.

Proposition 2.1
.

For any reachable pair of configurations
\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{t}\in\boldsymbol{F}
, there is an
optimal
(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{t})
-plan that is rectilinear and decoupled.

The canonical grid.

For an integer
i\geqslant 0
, we define an
i
-line
to be a horizontal
or vertical line
\ell
that lies at distance
i
from an edge of
\partial\mathscr{F}

parallel to
\ell
, or that lies at distance
i
from one of the points
s_{A},s_{B},t_{A},t_{B}
.
Note that
0
-lines support an edge of
\partial\mathscr{F}
or pass through
one of the points
s_{A},s_{B},t_{A},t_{B}
. Let
L_{i}
denote the set of all
i
-lines,
and set
L_{\leqslant i}:=\bigcup_{j=0}^{i}L_{j}
. We will be mostly interested in
L_{\leqslant 2}
.
The
arrangement

[
6
]
of
L_{\leqslant 2}
, denoted by
\mathscr{A}(L_{\leqslant 2})
, is the
subdivision of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
induced by
L_{\leqslant 2}
.
Since each edge of
\mathscr{F}
is contained in a
0
-line, each face of
\mathscr{A}(L_{\leqslant 2})
is
a rectangle that is either contained in
\mathscr{F}
or disjoint from
\mathscr{F}
.
The vertices and edges of
\mathscr{A}(L_{\leqslant 2})
form a planar grid graph.
Let
G=(V,E)
denote the subgraph of this graph that lies in
\mathscr{F}
,
that is,
V
and
E
are the sets of vertices and edges, respectively,
of
\mathscr{A}(L_{\leqslant 2})
that lie in
\mathscr{F}
. Note that
|V|=O(n^{2})
and
|E|=O(n^{2})
.
We refer to
G
as a
grid
, to a vertex of
V
as a
grid point
,
to an edge of
E
as a
grid edge
,
and to a line of
L_{\leqslant 2}
as a
grid line
.
We call a rectilinear, decoupled
(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{t})
-plan
\boldsymbol{\pi}=(\pi_{A},\pi_{B})

a
canonical-grid plan
if each breakpoint of
\pi_{A}
and
\pi_{B}

is a grid point. Thus each segment of the path is the union of a number
of consecutive collinear grid edges.
By definition, each parking spot of
A
or
B
in a canonical-grid plan is a grid point.
Our main technical result for the
Min-Sum
problem is the following theorem.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
\mathcal{W}
be a closed rectilinear polygonal environment with
n
vertices, let

A,B
be two axis-parallel unit-square robots translating inside
\mathcal{W}
,
and let
\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{t}
be source and target configurations of
A,B
.
There is an optimal
(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{t})
-plan that is a canonical-grid plan.

2.2
Converting an optimal plan to a canonical-grid plan

Next we give a high-level overview of how we convert an optimal
(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{t})
-plan

\boldsymbol{\pi}=(\pi_{A},\pi_{B})
to an optimal

By Proposition

We now describe how to convert

into a canonical-grid plan without increasing its cost.
We accomplish this conversion in two phases: the first phase modifies the plan

The first phase.

We define a

contains a bad horizontal segment

into a new plan

such that

•

(P1)
Feasibility:
The new plan
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}
is feasible.

•

(P2)
Optimality:
The cost of the plan does not increase,
that is,
\|\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}\|\leqslant\|\boldsymbol{\pi}\|
.

•

(P3)
Progress:
The plan
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}
has at least one fewer bad horizontal segment
than
\boldsymbol{\pi}
.

•

(P4)
Vertical alignment:
Any vertical segment of
\pi^{*}_{A}

is collinear with a vertical segment of
\pi_{A}
or
\pi_{B}
,
or it is contained in a vertical grid line; and
the same holds for any vertical segment of
\pi_{B}^{*}
.

•

(P5)
Alternation:

\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}
is an alternating, rectilinear, decoupled plan.

The
Progress
property guarantees that
by applying the procedure finitely many times, starting with the optimal plan
\boldsymbol{\pi}
,
we obtain a plan without bad horizontal segments. The
Feasibility
and
Optimality

properties imply that
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}
is an optimal
(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{t})
-plan.
Furthermore, the
Alternation
property implies that
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}

is a rectilinear, decoupled, alternating plan. Hence, the solution resulting
from one iteration satisfies the precondition for the next iteration,
so we can indeed apply the procedure iteratively.

After eliminating all bad horizontal segments in the first phase,
we eliminate the bad vertical segments in the second phase. The
Vertical Alignment

property, which in the second phase applies to horizontal segments, guarantees that
the horizontal segments are not moved off the grid in the second phase.
So, all segments of
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}
lie on grid lines, and since
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}
is alternating, its breakpoints therefore lie on grid points.
Hence, at the end of the second phase, we obtain an optimal, canonical-grid plan.

Our transformation of the original plan

2.3
Computing an optimal canonical-grid plan

With Theorem
2.1
at hand, we are now ready to describe our algorithm.

The configuration graph.

Let
G=(V,E)
be the canonical grid defined in Section
2.1
.
We construct an edge-weighted
configuration graph

\mathscr{G}=(\mathscr{V},\mathscr{E})

with weight function
w:\mathscr{E}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}
whose nodes correspond to
free configurations
\boldsymbol{u}\in\boldsymbol{F}
. More precisely,

\mathscr{V}:=\{\boldsymbol{u}=(u_{A},u_{B})\in V\times V:\|u_{A}-u_{B}\|\geqslant 1\}.

By construction,
\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{t}\in\mathscr{V}
.
The set
\mathscr{E}
of edges of the configuration graph is defined as

\mathscr{E}:=\Big\{(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})\in\mathscr{V}\times\mathscr{V}:\left(u_{A}=v_{A}\mbox{ and }(u_{B},v_{B})\in E\right)\mbox{ or }\left(u_{B}=v_{B}\mbox{ and }(u_{A},v_{A})\in E\right)\Big\}.

An edge
\left((p,u_{B}),(p,v_{B})\right)\in\mathscr{E}
corresponds a move in which
robot
A
is parked at
p
while robot
B
moves from
u_{B}
to an adjacent grid point
v_{B}
.
Similarly, an edge
\left((u_{A},q),(v_{A},q)\right)\in\mathscr{E}
corresponds to a move in which

A
moves from a grid point
u_{A}
to an adjacent grid point
v_{A}
while
B
is parked at
q
.
(Lemma
2.1
below states that such moves are feasible.)
The weight of an edge
(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})\in\mathscr{E}
is defined to be
w(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}):=\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1}
,
which is the
\ell_{1}
-distance between
\boldsymbol{u}
and
\boldsymbol{v}
in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
.

The algorithm.

We first compute
\mathscr{F}
in
O(n\log{n})
time in a standard manner
[
10
, Chapter 13]
.
Next, we compute
L_{\leqslant 2}
in additional
O(n)
time and then compute the grid graph

G
in
O(n^{2}\log{n})
time using a sweep-line algorithm. After computing
G
, we compute
the configuration graph
\mathscr{G}
in
O(n^{4})
time. Finally, we compute a shortest path from

\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathscr{V}
to
\boldsymbol{t}\in\mathscr{V}
in
\mathscr{G}
in
O(n^{4}\log{n})
time using Dijkstra’s algorithm.
If we find that no path from
\boldsymbol{s}
to
\boldsymbol{t}
exists, we report the instance to be infeasible,
otherwise we return the
(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{t})
-plan corresponding to the shortest path in
\mathscr{G}
.
The correctness of the algorithm follows from Lemma
2.2
.
The total run time is
O(n^{4}\log{n})
. This completes the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

2.4
Correctness of the Algorithm

The correctness of the algorithm
follows from the following key properties of
\mathscr{G}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

For every edge
(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})\in\mathscr{E}
, the segment
\boldsymbol{u}\boldsymbol{v}
lies in
\boldsymbol{F}
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, assume that
(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})=\left((p,u_{B}),(p,v_{B})\right)

and that
u_{B}v_{B}
is a vertical edge of
G
. Note that
(p,u_{B})\in\mathscr{V}
and
(p,v_{B})\in\mathscr{V}
.
Hence, the only way for the move corresponding to
\boldsymbol{u}\boldsymbol{v}
to be infeasible,
is if
B
would collide with
A
(which is parked at the grid point
p
)
after leaving
u_{B}
and before arriving at
v_{B}
.
Since
p
conflicts with a point in the interior of
u_{B}v_{B}
and not with
the points
u_{B}
or
v_{B}
themselves, the horizontal grid line through
p

must intersect the interior of the vertical segment
u_{B}v_{B}
.
But this would contradict that
u_{B}
and
v_{B}
are adjacent grid points.
Thus
\boldsymbol{u}\boldsymbol{v}\subset\boldsymbol{F}
.
∎

Lemma 2.2
.

Assuming
\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{t}\in\boldsymbol{F}
are reachable, a shortest path in
\mathscr{G}
from
\boldsymbol{s}
to
\boldsymbol{t}

corresponds to an optimal, canonical-grid
(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{t})
-plan.

Proof.

Denote the cost of a path
\boldsymbol{\pi}
in the configuration graph
\mathscr{G}

by
w(\boldsymbol{\pi}):=\sum_{e\in\boldsymbol{\pi}}w(e)
.
Let
\boldsymbol{\pi}
be a shortest path in
\mathscr{G}
from
\boldsymbol{s}
to
\boldsymbol{t}
.
By Lemma
2.1
we have

\boldsymbol{\pi}\subset\boldsymbol{F}
, and so
\boldsymbol{\pi}
is a feasible
(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{t})
-plan. Furthermore,

w(\boldsymbol{\pi})=\|\boldsymbol{\pi}\|
by definition. To bound the cost of
\boldsymbol{\pi}
, let
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}=(\pi_{A}^{*},\pi_{B}^{*})

be an optimal
(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{t})
-plan. By Theorem
2.1
, we can assume that
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}
is a
decoupled, canonical-grid plan. Therefore
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}
can be decoupled into a sequence of moves
in which one robot is parked at a grid point and the other moves along
a segment of a grid line. Suppose
A
is parked at
p
and
B
moves along a grid segment
e=u_{B}v_{B}
. Then

(p,u_{B}),(p,v_{B})\in\mathscr{V}
and the move from
(p,u_{B})
to
(p,v_{B})
can be represented as a sequence

(p,q_{0}),(p,q_{1}),...,(p,q_{k})
with
q_{0}=u_{B}
and

such that each

is an edge of
G
. Hence, the move from
(p,u_{B})
to
(p,v_{B})

is represented by a path in
\mathscr{G}
from
(p,u_{B})

to
(p,v_{B})
. Repeating the argument for all moves of
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}
,
we see that the entire plan
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}
is represented by a path in
\mathscr{G}
.
We conclude that
\boldsymbol{\pi}
, which is the shortest path in
\mathscr{G}
, cannot be
longer than
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}
. Thus,
\boldsymbol{\pi}
is an optimal
(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{t})
-plan.
∎

Note that

Since we assumed that

Now consider a shortest
pq
-path
\pi
and suppose for a contradiction that

is not

as a function

Let

{\pi(\lambda)}_{x}={\pi(\mu)}_{x}

.
If the vertical segment

Then

Therefore

On the other hand,

Lemma 3.3
.

Let

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
[\nu_{1},\nu_{2}]
be an unsafe interval. For

and

Proof.

Let

There are three cases for

to be an endpoint of an unsafe interval.

The first case is that
\pi_{B}
enters

at time

.

, and if

Furthermore,

The third case is that

Consider an unsafe interval
[\nu_{1},\nu_{2}]

If

-separated.
An important type of unsafe intervals are so-called swap intervals, as defined next.

Definition 2
.

A
swap interval
is an
x
-separated unsafe interval such that the
x
-orders
at
\boldsymbol{\pi}(\nu_{1})
and
\boldsymbol{\pi}(\nu_{2})
are different.

Roughly speaking, the role of unsafe intervals is as follows.
If a time
\lambda\in[\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}]
does not lie in an
unsafe interval, translating
\pi_{A}(\lambda)
vertically within

does not cause the point to conflict with
\pi_{B}(\lambda)
because
\boldsymbol{\pi}(\lambda)
is
x
-separated.
If an unsafe interval is not
x
-separated,
it turns out that we can modify
\pi_{B}
to avoid conflicts
after translating
\pi_{A}(\lambda)
vertically without increasing the length of
\pi_{B}
.
If a swap interval
I

is
x
-separated and the two endpoints have the same
x
-order
then we show in Lemma
3.8
that one can re-parametrize the plan so that

is not an unsafe
interval and we can do surgery as above.
So the challenging case is when

is
a swap interval. In this case, the surgery is considerably more involved, but
we will keep the situation under control by proving some
desirable properties of unsafe and swap intervals.
To prove the existence of an optimal plan with these properties,
we modify a given optimal plan in a controlled manner, as defined
below.

Definition 3
.

Consider an interval
[\lambda,\mu]
. We call
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}[\lambda,\mu]
a

compliant modification
of
\boldsymbol{\pi}[\lambda,\mu]
if the

Feasibility
(P1),
Optimality
(P2),
Vertical alignment
(P4)
and
Alternation
(P5) properties hold, and the following
No Regress
property:
each bad segment of
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}
is already present in
\boldsymbol{\pi}
or
it is a segment of
\pi^{*}_{A}[\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}]
.

The fact that a compliant modification has the
No Regress
property instead
of the
Progress
property is not problematic, because when handling a bad segment

of the modified plan
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}
,
we will actually get rid of all bad segments of
\pi^{*}_{A}[\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}]
.

A special type of compliant modification is a
compliant re-parametrization
.
Here
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}[\lambda,\mu]
is a re-parametrization of
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}[\lambda,\mu]
, that is,
the paths traced by
A
and
B
remain the same but the
parametrization of
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}
is different from
\boldsymbol{\pi}

and thus the breakpoints and parking spots in
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}
may change. Note that
a re-parametrization will never introduce a collision of a robot with an obstacle.
Thus, to check the
Feasibility
property, we only need to ensure that the robots do not collide with each other.
Also note that
Optimality
is automatically satisfied in a re-parametrization.
We can ensure the
Alternation

property by adding zero-length segments at breakpoints, where
necessary.
The remaining properties,
Vertical alignment
and
No Regress
,
are only violated if these zero-length segments are unaligned.
To verify that such violations do not happen,
it suffices to prove that
the parking spots in a compliant re-parametrization

are of one of the following types:

(T1):

grid points,

(T2):

vertices of
\pi_{A}[\lambda,\mu]
or
\pi_{B}[\lambda,\mu]
, or

(T3):

intersection points of horizontal (resp. vertical) grid lines with vertical (resp. horizontal) segments of

\pi_{A}[\lambda,\mu]
or
\pi_{B}[\lambda,\mu]
.

Note that compliant modification is composable: if
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\prime}

\boldsymbol{\pi}

\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\prime\prime}

\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\prime}

\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\prime\prime}
is a compliant modification of
\boldsymbol{\pi}

with respect to the original path.

The

{\pi_{X}^{*}(\mu)}_{x}:={\pi_{X}(\mu)}_{x}\mbox{ for all $\mu$}\hskip 14.22636pt\mbox{ and }\hskip 14.22636pt{\pi_{X}^{*}(\mu)}_{y}:=\begin{cases}y^{*}&\mbox{ if $\mu\in I$},\\

4.2

\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}

We will now prove the optimality of
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}

change the
x

\lambda

\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}

increase because of the ghost segments added to remove discontinuities created
by the push operations. Roughly speaking, we show that we are always in
one of the following three cases.

•

\textsc{Push}(X,I,y)
does not create a discontinuity at either endpoint of
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}_{X}[I]

and, hence, no ghost segment is added; or

•

\textsc{Push}(X,I,y)
does not increase the cost of
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}[I]

that is,
\|\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}[I]\|\leqslant\|\boldsymbol{\pi}[I]\|
where the lengths of the ghost segments are included; or

•

\textsc{Push}(X,I,y)
increases the cost of
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}[I]
, but
\|\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}[I]\|-\|\boldsymbol{\pi}[I]\|

(which is the cost increase) is compensated by a cost decrease resulting from another push operation.

We need the following basic properties of the push operation.

Lemma 4.10
.

Consider the operation

In this section we prove Theorem

,
which states that when the objective is to minimize the makespan

problem for two square robots becomes hard.

Our reduction is from

Let

\mbox{\textcent}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*})\leqslant 11m-\tfrac{1}{2}

\Longleftarrow

\Longrightarrow

We now distinguish two cases.

•

Case I:
G_{A}\cap G_{B}=\emptyset
.

Assume without loss of generality that

\sum_{g_{i}\in G_{A}}y_{i}\leqslant\sum_{g_{i}\in G_{B}}y_{i}
. Then

\sum_{g_{i}\in G_{A}}y_{i}\leqslant\tfrac{1}{2}\cdot\left(\sum_{g_{i}\in G_{A}}y_{i}+\sum_{g_{i}\in G_{B}}y_{i}\right)\leqslant\tfrac{1}{2}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{m}y_{i}=\tfrac{1}{2}.

only

\mbox{\textcent}(\boldsymbol{\pi})\geqslant\sum_{g_{i}\in G_{A}}(11-y_{i})+\sum_{g_{i}\not\in G_{A}}11=11m-\sum_{g_{i}\in G_{A}}y_{i}>11m-\tfrac{1}{2}.

be a gate such that

g_{i}
before

be the first time at which the left edge of

is contained

can have fully crossed

6
Conclusion

We presented an
O(n^{4}\log{n})
-time algorithm to compute a plan of minimum total length for two unit
squares in a rectilinear environment; this is the first polynomial
algorithm for 2-robot optimal motion planning in a polygonal environment. In contrast,
we showed that minimizing the makespan in the same setting is weakly
NP
-hard. We conclude
with some open problems:
