Abstract
Removing overlaps is a central task in domains such as scheduling, visibility, and map labelling. This task can be modelled using graphs, where overlap removals correspond to enforcing a certain sparsity constraint on the graph structure. We continue the study of the problem Geometric Graph Edit Distance , where the aim is to minimise the total cost of editing a geometric intersection graph to obtain a graph contained in a specific graph class. For us, the edit operation is the movement of objects, and the cost is the movement distance. We present an algorithm for rendering the intersection graph of a set of unit circular arcs (i) edgeless, (ii) acyclic, and (iii) k k -clique-free in O ⁡ ( n ​ log ⁡ n ) O(n\log n) time, where n n is the number of arcs. We also show that the problem remains strongly 𝖭𝖯 \mathsf{NP} -hard on unweighted interval graphs, solving an open problem of [Honorato-Droguett et al., WADS 2025]. We complement this result by showing that the problem is strongly 𝖭𝖯 \mathsf{NP} -hard on tuples of d d -balls and d d -cubes, for any d ≥ 2 d\geq 2 . Finally, we present an 𝖷𝖯 \mathsf{XP} algorithm (parameterised by the number of maximal cliques) for rendering the intersection graph of a set of weighted unit intervals edgeless.



1
Introduction

Optimally displacing geometric objects to reduce overlaps arises in several domains including visibility, scheduling, and interference.
In scheduling, start times of jobs on a machine are shifted (either delay or advance) to minimise the average deviation from the original schedule.
In map labelling, labels are relocated to avoid overlaps while keeping the modification cost small and ensuring visual clarity.
In interference, overlapping ranges of sensors or antennas must be reduced in order to improve connectivity while preserving specific global network properties.
For a specific graph class
\Pi
, consider the following class of problems.
Let
\mathcal{S}
be a finite collection of (possibly weighted) geometric objects in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and let
G(\mathcal{S})
be the intersection graph of
\mathcal{S}
.
We want to move the objects of
\mathcal{S}
to obtain a new collection
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
such that (i)
G(\mathcal{S}^{\prime})
is in
\Pi
and (ii) the sum of the (weighted) distances of the objects in
\mathcal{S}
is minimised.
This problem was introduced in
[
17
,
18
]
under the name
Geometric Graph Edit Distance
for
\Pi
(
GGED
(
\Pi
) for short), as a way to address graph modification from a geometric perspective, following previous work
[
10
,
11
,
12
]
.
Through the choice of
\Pi
,
GGED
models diverse problems, such as overlap removal and enhancing connectivity.

The works
[
17
,
18
]
present several polynomial-time algorithms for
GGED
for obtaining dense and sparse graphs.
A complete graph can be optimally obtained in linear time given (i) weighted intervals and (ii) unit squares.
For
n
unweighted unit intervals, edgeless, acyclic, and
k
-clique-free graphs and graphs containing a
k
-clique can be obtained in
O(n\log n)
time.
In contrast, when generalised to arbitrary lengths and weights, the problem becomes strongly
\mathsf{NP}
-hard for obtaining the same sparse graphs.
For the minimax variant, it is also strongly
\mathsf{NP}
-hard to obtain edgeless graphs given
n
weighted unit disks.
Although these results cover a range of
GGED
cases on intervals, the instances in which either the length or the weight is arbitrary while the other is unitary remain open.

Given the above context, our motivation is to address unsolved cases of
GGED
and extending known algorithms to generalisations of intervals.
The problem is tractable on intervals with unitary length and weight, but becomes strongly
\mathsf{NP}
-hard
when both are arbitrary.
It is therefore pertinent to analyse cases when only one restriction is relaxed.
We also study its parameterised complexity, aligning with recent work on geometric graph modification.
While
[
18
]
focused on one-dimensional objects, we extend the study to higher dimensions, providing hardness results and structural insights for open cases.
On the application side,
GGED
appears as an abstract model for tasks such as scheduling and map labelling, where the goal is to minimise the overall distortion required to remove overlaps while preserving the original structure.
We employ geometric intersection graphs to capture these applications within a unified model.

Related Work

We briefly summarise results on geometric graph modification and the main domains where overlap removal is found.

Applications of Overlap Removal.

Scheduling is central in computer science and operations research.
Given a set of time lapses (jobs or tasks), the goal is to shift the lapses without overlaps under given constraints.
A fundamental example is minimising the total weighted completion time of
n
given jobs on a single machine, solvable in
O(n\log n)

[
15
]
but strongly
\mathsf{NP}

[

]

Greedy approximation algorithms have since been proposed for hard cases
[
14
]
.
Another variation is minimising the maximum completion time under precedence constraints, solvable in
O(n^{2})
time in
[
20
]
.
The literature on scheduling is vast and an extensive review is beyond our scope; we refer to surveys and textbooks
[
24

26
,
1
]
.

Other problems in optimising a parameter under non-overlap constraints appear in the literature.
In boundary labelling
[
2
]
, the labels must be disjointly placed on the boundary of a given figure and connected to features inside the image with curves of minimum length.
The boundary has been considered for rectangles
[
2
]
, L-shapes
[
19
]
and circles
[
3
]
.
Other displacement problems arise in barrier coverage
[
5
]
, interference
[
9
]
, visibility
[
23
]
and point dispersion
[
22
]
.

These examples illustrate that overlap removal is a recurring task across several domains, where the common difficulty lies in achieving disjointness while optimising a specific cost function.
This motivates a general framework for overlap removal, which we aim to achieve through
Geometric Graph Edit Distance
.

(
\Pi_{\mathrm{edgeless}}

This section is about

(
\Pi_{\mathrm{edgeless}}
) on weighted unit intervals.
We present an

n

m\in O(n)

An
n

(

We first show that

(
\Pi_{\mathrm{edgeless}}

Lemma 5

Given an
n

\mathcal{I}
of unit intervals and a weight vector
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{>0}

Proof.

Let
D

and
\lVert\mathbf{w}D\rVert_{1}
is
minimum among all such vectors

. If there is a

such

, we are done. Otherwise, let

L=\{i\in[n]\colon d_{i}<0\}
, and let

. Note that

.
Let

be the sum of the weights of the

moves to the left and right, respectively.
Assume, without loss of generality, that

. Let

under the word-RAM model.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we have continued the study of
Geometric Graph Edit Distance
(
GGED
) on generalised interval graphs and higher-dimensional geometric objects.
We have first proved strong
\mathsf{NP}
-hardness for
GGED
(
\Pi_{\mathrm{edgeless}}
) on tuples of unweighted intervals, resolving an open question from
[
18
]
, and we have extended the reduction to
d
-cubes and
d
-balls for
d\geq 2
.
Next, we have generalised the
O(n\log n)
-time algorithm for unweighted unit intervals of
[
18
]
to unweighted unit circular arcs.
We have presented an
O(n^{4})
-time algorithm to solve
GGED
(
\Pi_{\mathrm{edgeless}}
) on weighted unit intervals when intervals pairwise intersect, and an
\mathsf{XP}
algorithm parameterised by the number of maximal cliques.
Finally, we have shown that the non-parameterised problem is in
\mathsf{NP}
for weighted unit intervals.

Open Problems and Further Research.

The main open question is whether the
GGED
(
\Pi_{\mathrm{edgeless}}
) remains
\mathsf{NP}
-hard on weighted unit intervals.
Because intervals cannot be distinguished by their length, we cannot adapt the straightforward reduction used in
Section

3.1
.
On the other hand, arbitrary weights prevent an exploitable property by a polynomial-time algorithm.
Solving this problem would help significantly to settle the intractability border of
GGED
(
\Pi_{\mathrm{edgeless}}
).

Restricted instances where lengths are proportional to weights are also noteworthy.
This models scenarios where the movement cost scales with the size (e.g., scheduling when processing time equals weight
[
16
]
).
Such restrictions complicate reductions such as the one for
Theorem

3.1
, since heavy intervals cannot be simulated using a number of short intervals.
At the same time, they may allow properties such as order-preservation
[
18
]
or convex-cost formulations that can be exploited by polynomial-time algorithms.
Another promising restriction assumes that 2-dimensional objects
are given with ordering constraints concerning one
dimension
[
23
]
.

Considering other geometric transformations (scaling or rotation) and parameterisations of strongly
\mathsf{NP}
-hard cases also remains open.
Parameters such as the number of objects moved, maximal cliques, or distinct lengths/weights can be considered.
Our observations from
Section

5
may help design a parameterised algorithm when both length and weight are arbitrary.
Finally, heuristics and approximation algorithms are also natural extensions for hard cases.

Proof.

We show the running time of testing all possible sets of slots.
For an arbitrary
i\in[k]
, the running time is given by:

\displaystyle N_{\mathrm{clique}}\cdot N_{\mathrm{fixed}}\cdot N_{\mathrm{case}}\cdot(N_{\mathrm{slot}})^{3},\text{such that}

\displaystyle N_{\mathrm{clique}}=\binom{k}{i},\quad N_{\mathrm{fixed}}=\Bigl(\frac{n}{k}\Bigr)^{i},

\displaystyle N_{\mathrm{case}}=(2(n-i)+1)^{i}\text{ and }N_{\mathrm{slot}}=2i(n-i).

The above meaning of each component is as follows.
The value
N_{\mathrm{clique}}
is the number of choosing
i

k
maximal cliques.
The value
N_{\mathrm{fixed}}
is the maximum number of possible combinations of
i
fixed intervals, given by selecting one interval from each maximal clique (assuming the worst case where
|I_{1}|=\cdots=|I_{k}|=n/k
).

n-i

N_{\mathrm{case}}
represents the number of cases in the worst case (all gaps admit exactly
2(n-i)
slots).

N_{\mathrm{slot}}
.

Theorem

5.1
at most
N_{\mathrm{clique}}\cdot N_{\mathrm{fixed}}\cdot N_{\mathrm{case}}

(N_{\mathrm{slot}})^{3}
in the worst case.

1

k
, the total running time of the algorithm is as follows:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}N_{\mathrm{clique}}\cdot N_{\mathrm{fixed}}\cdot N_{\mathrm{case}}\cdot(N_{\mathrm{slot}})^{3}=
