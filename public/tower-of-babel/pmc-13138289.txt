Extracranial-intracranial (EC-IC) bypass has long been established as a critical microsurgical strategy in the management of complex cerebrovascular lesions that are not amenable to direct repair or endovascular treatment. Since its original description by Yaşargil in the 1960s.1 EC-IC revascularization has evolved from low-flow superficial temporal artery (STA)–middle cerebral artery (MCA) bypass for moyamoya disease and ischemic syndromes to high-flow bypass using interposition grafts, most commonly the saphenous vein (SV) or radial artery (RA). These high-flow conduits are essential in the treatment of giant or complex aneurysms, skull base tumors encasing the carotid or middle cerebral arteries, and selected cases of ischemic cerebrovascular disease requiring robust augmentation of cerebral blood flow.

EC-IC bypass surgery is currently indicated in three different clinical scenarios: flow replacement in the management of complex cerebral aneurysms not amenable to endovascular treatment,1 cerebral blood flow augmentation in cases of ischemia associated with poor collateral circulation and reduced cerebrovascular reserve,2 and management of thrombotic complications following endovascular procedures,3 such as the deployment of flow diverters (FDs).

Multiple surgical techniques and graft options have been described for EC-IC bypass,4 including the use of the STA, RA, and SV.5,6 In this report, we describe, for the first time, the use of the internal mammary artery (IMA) harvested via a mini-thoracoscopic approach as a vascular graft for EC-IC cerebral bypass. This novel technique may provide an alternative conduit with favorable anatomical and physiological properties, particularly in selected complex cerebrovascular cases.

Illustrative Case

A 48-year-old woman presented with recurrent episodes of limb-shaking transient ischemic attacks, characterized by brief, involuntary movements of the left upper extremity precipitated by standing and relieved by recumbency. These episodes were consistent with hemodynamic instability secondary to cerebral hypoperfusion.

Her past medical history was notable for protein S deficiency and an unruptured aneurysm of the right internal carotid artery (ICA) at the posterior communicating artery segment, initially managed with FD stent placement. Despite a technically successful deployment, thrombosis of the FD occurred, necessitating a second endovascular attempt with repeat FD placement. Unfortunately, thrombosis recurred, further compromising arterial patency and resulting in progressive hemodynamic insufficiency.

Neuroimaging demonstrated persistent compromise of the right ICA circulation with poor distal perfusion. The patient’s limb-shaking transient ischemic attacks, refractory to optimal medical therapy, including dual antiplatelet agents and anticoagulation tailored to her thrombophilic state, indicated a severe reduction in cerebrovascular reserve. Given the failure of endovascular therapy and the presence of a prothrombotic disorder, she was deemed a candidate for EC-IC bypass as a definitive revascularization strategy.

Preoperative angiography showed that the STA was thinned and unsuitable as a donor, whereas the internal maxillary artery had adequate caliber. Because of protein S deficiency, the patient had a history of lower limb venous thrombosis and superficial varicose veins, ruling out the SVs as vascular grafts. Furthermore, the Allen test and subsequent angiography showed that blood flow in both hands depended on the RAs, precluding their harvest. After consultation with the cardiac surgery team, the left IMA was selected as the graft. Intraoperatively, the IMA was harvested via a minimally invasive thoracoscopic approach and mobilized to the cranial field (Fig. 1). It was then anastomosed to the M2 segment of the MCA, providing a pressure gradient–responsive conduit to restore distal cerebral perfusion (Fig. 2). Postoperatively, limb-shaking episodes resolved completely, and perfusion imaging demonstrated improved hemodynamic parameters (Fig. 3).

Discussion

Despite their wide adoption, conventional grafts such as the SV and RA carry inherent limitations. On the one hand, vein grafts may be prone to mismatch in caliber, kinking, intimal hyperplasia, and long-term atherosclerotic degeneration.7 On the other hand, arterial grafts, while superior in patency, require additional dissection, are at risk for vasospasm, and may not always be readily available depending on patient-specific anatomy or comorbidities. These limitations have spurred interest in alternative conduits that could combine the advantages of durability, high-flow capacity, and long-term patency with minimal harvest morbidity.

For a long time, the IMA has been used as a graft in cardiac revascularization. Compared with the SV, it avoids valvular interference and long-term luminal remodeling, offers a more compatible caliber, and exhibits pulsatile behavior that more closely resembles that of native cerebral arteries. In contrast to the RA, the IMA is less prone to vasospasm and may be more readily harvested in patients with compromised upper extremity perfusion or contraindications to forearm dissection.8 These characteristics collectively suggest that the IMA could serve as an optimal conduit in complex scenarios, such as in patients with thrombophilia, failed endovascular interventions, or contraindications to traditional grafts, where high-capacitance flow and low vasoreactivity are paramount.

The IMA represents an excellent conduit. In coronary artery bypass grafting, it has demonstrated superior long-term patency compared with both SV and RA grafts.9 The IMA is resistant to atherosclerosis, offers consistent caliber, and can deliver sustained high-flow volumes, making it an attractive candidate for cerebral revascularization.10 However, its use in neurosurgery has remained virtually unexplored. Traditionally, the IMA has been harvested through a median sternotomy, a surgical approach that provides adequate exposure of the vessel. However, the surgical trauma often results in delayed postoperative recovery and wound infections.11 In our case, the IMA was successfully harvested using a video-assisted endoscopic approach (Video 1), with no associated complications, and recovery was observed within 24 hours postoperatively.12,13

The EC-IC bypass has been previously demonstrated as a feasible option using the RA as the main graft;14 however, harvesting the trunk depends on adequate blood supply to the hand, preferably from the nondominant arm, via the cubital artery. The primary reason for performing the described technique in this case was an unviable harvest from either of the patient’s arms.

To our knowledge, no prior reports have described the direct use of the IMA for high-flow EC-IC bypass to the MCA. While we have limited knowledge of the characteristics that might make one graft better than another, the first reports15 show similar long-term permeability between IMA and RA (96% and 92%, respectively) at 7 years of follow-up, with no significant difference in patency.16 This is likely due to its histological characteristics; its media is thinner and less muscular than other arteries and veins, producing a higher basal and stimulated rate of nitric oxide with lower propensity for spasm, and the nonfenestrated internal elastic lamina makes it less susceptible to intimal hyperplasia and atherosclerosis.17,18 While this case represents a promising proof of concept, several considerations warrant further study. Quantitative hemodynamic assessment using intraoperative flowmetry and serial angiographic or perfusion imaging would help characterize the graft’s long-term adaptation to intracranial flow dynamics. Multicenter or registry-based comparisons between IMA, RA, and venous conduits could further define the ideal indications for each graft type. Additionally, histological or molecular analyses may elucidate the mechanisms underlying the IMA’s superior endothelial stability and resistance to intimal hyperplasia.

Based on our experience and the existing literature, we propose a pragmatic decision-making framework for graft selection in high-flow EC-IC bypass surgery (Fig. 4). First-line conduits include the STA for low-flow augmentation and the RA or SV for high-flow bypass, depending on caliber match, flow demand, and limb perfusion status. The IMA should be considered a third-line or rescue conduit, reserved for highly selected cases in which conventional grafts are unavailable or contraindicated. Such scenarios include inadequate STA caliber, bilateral RA dependence, compromised upper extremity perfusion, venous graft intolerance, prior graft failure, or systemic conditions predisposing to thrombosis or vasospasm. In these contexts, the IMA provides a biologically favorable arterial conduit that supports high-capacitance cerebral blood flow.

Proposed decision-making algorithm for graft selection in EC-IC bypass.

Standard conduits, including the RA and SV, should be considered first based on flow requirements, caliber compatibility, and donor site suitability. The IMA is reserved as a third-line or rescue option in highly selected cases when conventional grafts are unavailable or contraindicated.

Several vascular and systemic conditions may predispose patients to require alternative high-flow bypass strategies beyond standard conduits. These include inherited or acquired thrombophilias, radiation-induced vasculopathy, connective tissue disorders, complex skull base tumors with arterial encasement, chronic carotid occlusion with exhausted collateral pathways, and failed or thrombosed endovascular reconstructions. In such settings, arterial grafts with low vasoreactivity and favorable endothelial characteristics may be particularly advantageous.

Recent evidence suggests that aneurysm healing following flow diversion not only is a hemodynamic phenomenon but also is strongly influenced by endothelial and molecular factors.

Impaired endothelialization, altered nitric oxide signaling, inflammatory dysregulation, and prothrombotic states have been implicated in delayed or failed aneurysm occlusion after flow-diverting stent placement. As highlighted in recent molecular studies,19 these biological variables may partially explain device failure despite technically adequate deployment. In patients with unfavorable vascular biology, surgical revascularization remains a critical alternative to restore cerebral perfusion and mitigate ischemic risk.

Observations

This case highlights the challenges of revascularization in the setting of thrombophilia and endovascular device failure, as well as the potential of the IMA as a novel conduit for EC-IC bypass within the hemodynamic framework of pressure gradients. In this report, we describe what we believe is the first documented case of a high-flow bypass using the IMA anastomosed to the MCA. We provide a detailed technical account of the surgical approach, highlighting the nuances of IMA harvest through a minimally invasive thoracoscopy, graft mobilization into the cranial field, and microsurgical anastomosis. Furthermore, we discuss the theoretical advantages of this conduit, its potential role in expanding the EC-IC revascularization armamentarium, and its implications for future practice in complex cerebrovascular surgery.

Lessons

EC-IC bypass remains indispensable for cerebral revascularization. To achieve optimal outcomes and long-term patency, graft selection and bypass strategy must be planned meticulously. Early graft patency failure has been attributed to several factors, primarily intimal desquamation of the graft wall, with loss of protective fibrinolytic activity and exposure of underlying thrombogenic collagen fibers, as well as high-caliber mismatch, resulting in slow graft flow and eventual thrombosis. Mechanical trauma is another potential trigger for graft failure; therefore, it is essential to minimize it during graft harvest and anastomosis. The introduction of the left IMA as an alternative graft represents a physiologically and technically meaningful innovation in cerebral revascularization.

In this case, the left IMA was harvested by a cardiothoracic surgeon using a minimally invasive thoracoscopic approach, which constitutes a significant procedural advance. This technique preserves chest wall integrity, reduces postoperative pain, and allows early mobilization. These advantages are particularly relevant for patients requiring subsequent neurosurgical positioning and prolonged cranial procedures. Bypass patency and adaptability to the intracranial microenvironment were confirmed intraoperatively with Doppler assessment and postoperatively with perfusion CT. The absence of harvest-related complications further supports its feasibility within a multidisciplinary operative framework.

This case underscores the importance of individualized graft selection in cerebral revascularization. While the IMA may rarely be required, its availability as a tertiary option expands the surgical armamentarium for complex cases in which standard conduits are not feasible. Awareness of this alternative may prove valuable in carefully selected patients with failed endovascular therapy or limited donor vessel options.
