Are LLMs Reliable Translators of Logical Reasoning Across Lexically Diversified Contexts?
Qingchuan Li, Jiatong Li, Zirui Liu, Mingyue Cheng, Yuting Zeng, Qi Liu, Tongxuan Liu

Abstract
Neuro-symbolic approaches combining large language models (LLMs) with solvers excels in logical reasoning problems need long reasoning chains. In this paradigm, LLMs serve as translators, converting natural language reasoning problems into formal logic formulas. Then reliable symbolic solvers return correct solutions. Despite their success, we find that LLMs, as translators, struggle to handle lexical diversification, a common linguistic phenomenon, indicating that LLMs as logic translators are unreliable in real-world scenarios. Moreover, existing logical reasoning benchmarks lack lexical diversity, failing to challenge LLMs’ ability to translate such text and thus obscuring this issue. In this work, we propose SCALe, a benchmark designed to address this significant gap through logic-invariant lexical diversification . By using LLMs to transform original benchmark datasets into lexically diversified but logically equivalent versions, we evaluate LLMs’ ability to consistently map diverse expressions to uniform logical symbols on these new datasets. Experiments using SCALe further confirm that current LLMs exhibit deficiencies in this capability. Building directly on the deficiencies identified through our benchmark, we propose a new method, MenTaL, to address this limitation. This method guides LLMs to first construct a table unifying diverse expressions before performing translation. Applying MenTaL through in-context learning and supervised fine-tuning (SFT) significantly improves the performance of LLM translators on lexically diversified text. Our code is now available at https://github.com/wufeiwuwoshihua/LexicalDiver

Our work effectively fills this gap in the domain of logical reasoning datasets.

Our SCALe approach is built upon a novel concept—logic-invariant lexical diversification—which serves as a significant addition to the application of adversarial techniques in the domain of logical reasoning.

Neurosymbolic Reasoning with Large Language Models.
Previous works

SCALe: A Benchmark for Semantic Consistency Mapping

3.1
Logic-invariant Lexical Diversification

We will first introduce the method of Logic-invariant Lexical Diversification, and the verification of its logic-invariant property will be discussed in the next section. To facilitate the introduction of our method, we first provide the definition of Synonym Set in natural language logical reasoning tasks:

Definition 1 (Synonym Set).

Denote the set that encompasses all the vocabularies in the world as
\mathcal{V}
.Then,
\mathcal{V}
can be partitioned into
\mathcal{V}_{1},\mathcal{V}_{2},\cdots,\mathcal{V}_{N}
, where
\mathcal{V}_{i}
represents the
i
-th synonym set. It satisfies the conditions that
\mathcal{V}_{i}\cap\mathcal{V}_{j}=\emptyset
and
\mathcal{V}_{1}\cup\mathcal{V}_{2}\cup\cdots\cup\mathcal{V}_{N}=\mathcal{V}
.

Then, we provide the formalized definition of the problem. For any given problem
q
and a list
\mathcal{E}=[e_{1},\ldots,e_{m}]
of tokens to be replaced—where
\mathcal{E}
contains all the words in
q
that need to be substituted—the goal is to return a rewritten version of the list and the original problem, denoted as
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
and
q^{\prime}
. For example, we aim to replace all words denoting coldness in
q
, with this synonym set denoted as
\mathcal{V}_{i}
. At this point,
\mathcal{E}=[\text{cold},\text{cold},\text{cold}]
, meaning all words in
q
belonging to
\mathcal{V}_{i}
are "cold" and appear three times. After rewriting, we obtain
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}=[\text{cold},\text{freezing},\text{chilly}]
. By replacing
\mathcal{E}
with
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
, we can derive diversified problem
q^{\prime}
.

The goal of Logic-invariant Lexical Diversification is to enrich the variety of expressions in the text while preserving its logical structure. The key challenge of this task lies in using as diverse a vocabulary as possible without altering the logic. Logical text consists of multiple sentences, with logic embedded both within sentence structures and in the alignment of logical concepts across sentences. Therefore, in order to preserve the internal structure of the original sentences and maintain the correspondence of logical concepts between sentences, we propose a lexical-level rewriting algorithm. Formally, our algorithm returns a replacement set
\mathcal{R}
for each synonym set
\mathcal{V}_{i}
, and uses the expressions in
\mathcal{R}
to replace the elements in
\mathcal{E}
at their respective positions in
q
, ensuring that the final result satisfies
|\mathcal{R}^{*}|>|\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}\{e_{i}\}|
, i.e., the lexical diversity after replacement is greater than the initial value. The full diversification procedure is shown in Algorithm

. The most critical part of the algorithm is generating the replacement set
\mathcal{R}
. We’ll introduce two strategies used in

Lexical Diversification Procedure

Dynamic Synonymic Diversification.

Dynamic Synonymic Diversification searches for more diverse expressions from the synonym sets
\mathcal{V}_{i}
, adds them to the replacement set
\mathcal{R}
, and combines them with the existing expressions in
\mathcal{E}
, such that
|\mathcal{R}|>|\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}\{e_{i}\in\mathcal{V}_{i}\}|
. This process is facilitated by LLMs. Drawing inspiration from established writing manuals and style guides
williams2014lessons
;
bucholtz2015elements
;
roget2023thesaurus
, we summarize three key synonymic rewriting techniques:
lexical synonymy
,
third-person descriptive phrasing
, and
part-of-speech conversion
. These techniques effectively guide LLMs to better perform the task. Finally, we use a different LLM to verify and refine the newly generated text
q^{\prime}
after replacement, ensuring that the process does not introduce semantic drift. This is a generalizable approach that can be applied to nearly any dataset.

Curated Synonymic Diversification.

Curated Synonymic Diversification draws on ideas from the automatic generation of logical reasoning datasets, where the logical structure does not depend on surface semantics. Specifically, if
\mathcal{E}_{i}
represents a logical entity, replacing
\mathcal{E}_{i}
with
\mathcal{R}\subseteq\mathcal{V}_{j},i\neq j
does not alter the logical structure. For example, in the problem "Bob is cold, if someone is cold, then they are nice," replacing the two occurrences of "cold" with "warm" does not change the logical structure. In logical reasoning,
\text{Cold}(x)
and
\text{Warm}(x)
exist merely as logical symbols, and their surface semantics are irrelevant. Leveraging this, as long as the expressions in
\mathcal{E}
exist as a complete logical concept in
q
—i.e., where the logical concept is expressed by a single word—we can replace expressions in
\mathcal{E}
belonging to the synonym set
\mathcal{V}_{i}
with
\mathcal{R}\subseteq\mathcal{V}_{j},i\neq j
. This may cause a change in semantics but will not alter the logical structure. The advantage of this approach is that we can pre-construct multiple high-quality synonym subsets
\mathcal{R}_{1}^{\text{prepared}},\mathcal{R}_{2}^{\text{prepared}},\dots,\mathcal{R}_{3}^{\text{prepared}}
, which can be directly used as
\mathcal{R}
in the algorithm based on part-of-speech. This method effectively avoids semantic drift that may occur when large language models (LLMs) automatically generate synonyms. However, this approach is only applicable in cases where lexical-level replacements do not change the logical structure, i.e., in
q
where logical concepts are expressed by single words.

MenTaL: a Mental Representation Guided Translation Framework

4.1
Methodology

To address the issue observed in SCALe experiments where LLMs fail to map identical semantic concepts to a unified symbol during logical translation tasks, we draw inspiration from the theory of "mental representation" in cognitive science. This theory posits that humans map the external world to abstract concepts through internal symbols, representing multiple referents with the same sense within a structured "language of thought"
carey2009our
;
fodor1975language
;
pinker2007stuff
;
frege1892sense
.

Building on this idea, we propose MenTaL, a method that instructs LLMs to emulate human mental representation processes by explicitly constructing a mapping table before performing formal logical translation. This table lists expressions referring to the same concept and abstracts them into a unified symbol for representation. Formally, MenTaL guides LLMs to identify all
e_{j}\in\mathcal{V}_{i}
in
q
, constructing a subset
\bigcup\{e_{j}\}
of the synonym set
\mathcal{V}_{i}
in
q
. Concurrently, for each synonym set
\mathcal{V}_{i}
, a symbol
s_{i}
is assigned. An entry in the mental representation table
T
is the mapping from
\bigcup\{e_{j}\}
to
s_{i}
. By preconstructing the mental representation table, MenTaL guides LLMs to unify diversified expressions in
q
into a set of symbols. Using these symbols in subsequent formal logical translation effectively prevents the erroneous translation of diversified expressions into different logical symbols.

For datasets with relatively straightforward concept identification, such as ProntoQA, simply prompting the LLM to spontaneously generate a mental representation table yields strong results. However, for datasets with logical concepts embedded in complex sentence structures, such as FOLIO and ProverQA, we developed a set of rules grounded in logic and cognitive science to guide the construction and maintenance of the mental representation table. This method, denoted as MenTaL
∗
, is detailed in the Appendix B.4.

5
Conclusion

Lexical diversity is a common linguistic phenomenon, yet existing logical reasoning benchmarks do not require models to handle such text. In this work, we propose SCALe, which aims to perform lexical diversification on text while preserving the original problem logic, thereby evaluating the model’s ability to handle such text in logical reasoning. Through SCALe, we find that in the current paradigm of using LLMs as translators combined with solvers for logical problems, LLM translators exhibit a significant drop in accuracy when faced with lexically diversified text. More detailed response analysis further reveals the shortcomings of LLMs in handling diversified text. Our work fills a critical gap in logical reasoning benchmarks and, in doing so, exposes the deficiencies of LLMs as logical translators. The ultimate goal of this study is not only to highlight LLMs’ shortcomings but also to enhance their capabilities. Based on findings from SCALe’s experimental phase, we propose a new approach, MenTaL. Through in-context learning and SFT, MenTaL guides LLMs to mimic human mental representation tables, significantly improving their accuracy in translating diversified text. We believe this work is a crucial step toward making the translation process in logical reasoning more reliable and advancing the performance of logical reasoning methods.

Limitations.

The first limitation is that lexical diversification lacks sufficient control. The curated strategy is limited to simple problems, while the dynamic strategy, though more versatile, relies on LLMs, whose complexity makes it hard to generate high-quality diversified text without altering logic. The second limitation is the focus on logical reasoning. The challenge of translating concepts into unified symbols exists broadly in LLM-tool interactions, an important LLM agent issue
wang2024gta
. SCALe’s methods could apply to other domains, but this was not explored. Future work will develop more controllable diversification methods for reliable, high-quality text generation and extend SCALe and MenTaL to broader external tool domains to identify and address large-scale issues.

Appendix B
Details of Methodology

B.1
Techniques in Dynamic Synonymic Diversification

In Dynamic Synonymic Diversification, we propose three techniques to assist large language models (LLMs) in diversifying expressions that appear repetitively. These techniques are described as follows:

1.

Synonym Substitution:
This technique involves replacing a given expression with semantically similar words or phrases. By leveraging lexical or contextual synonymy, the model can maintain the original meaning while varying surface-level expressions, thereby enhancing lexical diversity without compromising semantic consistency.

2.

Third-Person Reformulation:
Instead of employing direct references (such as first- or second-person pronouns), this approach reformulates the expression using descriptive or third-person language. This shift in referential perspective allows the model to generate more varied and contextually rich output, especially in narrative or expository contexts.

3.

Part-of-Speech Transformation:
This method generates semantically equivalent expressions by altering the part of speech of key lexical items. For instance, verbs may be transformed into corresponding nouns (e.g.,
“decide”

\rightarrow

“decision”
), or adjectives into adverbs, enabling syntactic variation while preserving the core semantic content.

B.4
Rules Employed in MenTaL
∗

For tasks in which logical structures are difficult to identify, large language models (LLMs) often struggle to generate a fully accurate mental representation table at the outset. This limitation poses challenges to the direct application of the original MenTaL method. To address this issue, we introduce
MenTaL
∗
, a variant specifically designed for more challenging datasets. MenTaL
∗
enables LLMs to
dynamically maintain
the mental representation table throughout the formal logic translation process, rather than requiring the complete table to be produced before translation begins.

Grounded in principles from logic and cognitive science, we define the following rules to guide the maintenance of the table:

•

Rule 1:
If the semantic meaning of an entity is exactly the same as an existing entry in the table, reuse the corresponding predicate to maintain consistency.

•

Rule 2:
If the semantic meaning of an entity differs from all existing entries and there is no inclusion relationship, a new row should be added to the table to record the new concept.

•

Rule 3:
If the semantic meaning of one entity includes that of another, use the more specific entity (e.g., replace
PopularShow
denoting “A is a popular show” with
Show
denoting “A is a show”).

•

Rule X:
Whenever a table entry is replaced, all associated “Occurrence Sentence” translations must be updated to reflect the revised predicate.

•

Rule Y:
After each logical parsing step, a table consistency check must be performed to ensure that all entities are aligned with the current state of the table.

Rule 1
serves as the foundation for reusing identical concepts and aligns with the core goal of maintaining a consistent mental representation table.
Rule 2
ensures that novel concepts are incorporated in a timely manner.
Rule 3
emphasizes the use of the most specific concepts available, as general concepts are often compositional in nature. For example, in logical language,
PopularShow(x)
meaning “
x
is a popular show” can be decomposed into
Show(x)
\wedge
Popular(x)
. The use of more specific concepts improves the precision of logical reasoning.

Rules X
and
Y
ensure the structural integrity of the table through systematic updating and verification. Although some redundancy is introduced to simplify LLM comprehension,
Rule X
enforces retranslation of affected content to apply updated entries, while
Rule Y
mandates validation after every parsing step to ensure consistency between the table and the logical representation.

By following these rules, MenTaL
∗
enables LLMs to effectively construct and maintain mental representation tables even in complex logic translation scenarios, thereby extending the utility of the MenTaL framework to more challenging tasks. Examples are shown in Appendix
I.4
.

Following the experimental design of humans, we tested LLMs on the Origin and SCALe diversified versions of questions at the natural language level. Unlike the formal logic translation process, given the sufficiently rich natural language corpus in the training process, the current best-performing LLMs can, like humans, correctly identify and categorize different expressions of the same concept when processing natural language questions. If lexical diversification that preserves logical equivalence is achieved, the accuracy of LLM responses should theoretically remain largely unchanged. Therefore, observing the change in LLMs’ accuracy between the two versions of natural language questions—if the accuracy difference is minimal—would provide strong evidence supporting that SCALe’s diversification does not alter the underlying logic.

Figure
5
illustrates the experimental results of one of the most advanced reasoning LLMs, DeepSeek-R1, answering questions from the five datasets at the natural language level. Referencing evaluation methodologies
[
59
]

Q_{origin}
denote the set of questions correctly answered by the LLM in the Origin datasets,
Q_{SCALe}
denote the set of correctly answered questions in the SCALe datasets, and
n
represent the number of questions in the test set. ACC@Consist is defined as:

ACC@Consist=\frac{|Q_{origin}\cap Q_{SCALe}|}{n}

Additionally, the LLM’s performance on the Deduction dataset, which involves complex numerical computations, was inherently weaker, impacting the results to some extent. The experimental results indicate that the accuracy of LLMs in answering natural language questions does not significantly change due to SCALe diversification, further proving that SCALe’s diversification does not alter the logic of the original questions.

Datasets:

FOLIO
: FOLIO is an expert-constructed open-domain dataset characterized by its logical complexity and diversity. It is used for natural language reasoning involving first-order logic and is built to ensure logical rigor. We use the full validation set in our experiments, which contains 130 examples.

ProofWriter

The attributes in the problems are represented with single-word tokens, most of which are invented and not present in natural English. We randomly sample 200 examples from ProntoQA for our experiments.

ProverQA

Appendix F
Broader Impacts

This work addresses a core challenge in using LLMs for formal logic reasoning: accurately translating lexically diverse natural language into consistent logical forms. We introduce the SCALe benchmark and the MenTaL framework to enhance LLM robustness and fidelity in logic-centric tasks, especially those requiring semantic consistency despite surface variation. This supports more reliable neuro-symbolic systems, with implications for theorem proving, formal verification, and logic-based education.

Improved translation into formal logic can enable more precise, interpretable, and verifiable reasoning agents. Our open-source release of SCALe and MenTaL offers tools for rigorous benchmarking, promoting transparency and progress in logic-oriented AI research.

However, stronger logic translation capabilities bring risks. They could be misused to produce manipulative arguments with a facade of rigor, particularly in legal or regulatory contexts. Over-reliance on LLM-generated logic without validation may also introduce subtle errors. Furthermore, the resource demands for training such models may centralize access among well-funded institutions.

To address these risks, we advocate for verification mechanisms, human oversight, and interpretability tools in logic-based LLM applications. Future efforts should prioritize efficient, accessible implementations to ensure responsible and equitable deployment of enhanced logical reasoning.
