Annexin A2: the feasibility of being a therapeutic target associated with cancer metastasis and drug resistance in cancer microenvironment
Authors: Song Weijie
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAt present, there is still a lack of effective treatment strategies for cancer metastasis and drug resistance, so finding effective biomarkers is particularly important. AnnexinA2 (ANXA2), a vital membrane protein, critically influences cancer progression, tumor invasion, and tumor microenvironment modulation. To assess the possible application of ANXA2 as a therapeutic target against cancer cell metastasis and drug resistance to chemotherapeutic drugs in the tumor microenvironment, we elucidated the functionality of ANXA2 in stromal cells, angiogenic vascular cells, and infiltrated immune cells that mediate metastasis and drug resistance, as well as its potential as a therapeutic target. ANXA2 shows a high expression level in many tissues, and its expression level is even higher in several tumors and their microenvironments. ANXA2 is a crucial regulator of many factors and may serve as a target against drug-resistant cancers.Keywords: Annexin A2, Tumor microenvironment, Therapeutic, Drug-resistant

Introduction

Cancer is a primary lethal disease that threatens human health, leading to global health concerns, and researchers have sought innovative approaches to combat this disease [1]. Cancer entails a multifaceted and evolving process characterized by interactions between cells and the extracellular matrix (ECM), fostering tumor cell proliferation, drug resistance, and metastasis. This review offers an updated overview of the pivotal roles undertaken by various components within the tumor microenvironment (TME) which contains cells of different classes, including fibroblasts, immune-related cells, and endothelial cells as well as extracellular components like extracellular matrix (ECM), hormones, growth factors, and cytokines which interacts with ANXA2 [2, 3]. Better knowledge of tumor biology can be gained by focusing on the various cells that participate in TME formation during tumorigenesis [4, 5]. The TME plays a crucial role in several aspects of tumorigenesis. For example, the TME generates the tumor vascular system and is highly involved in metastasis progression [6]. In recent years, the response of TME to treatment has become a hot topic of research. Previous studies have shown that altering the tumor matrix can increase the efficacy of current anticancer therapies and provide ample opportunities to develop new therapeutic targets [7, 8]. In the last two decades, researchers have discovered many oncogenes and anti-oncogenes. These genes regulate the signaling pathways involved in cancer cell proliferation or apoptosis, which has further led to the development of several antitumor drugs and therapies. Several studies have confirmed that ANXA2 interacts with these signaling pathways [9, 10].

ANXA2 is a calcium-mediated phospholipid-binding protein that belongs to the annexin family. ANXA2 is widely expressed in various tissues and organs of mammals (Fig. 1). ANXA2 stimulates metastasis, invasion, and proliferation in several types of cancers include breast cancer, pancreatic cancer, and non-small cell lung cancer, hepatoma, and gliomas [11–16]. More recently, an increased focus is being placed on the role of ANXA2 in regulating the expression of cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) and infiltrating immune-related cells and in the collaborative interaction of endothelial cells with the TME. The environment mediated drug resistance arises from ongoing communication between tumor cells and their surrounding stroma. Although the role of the tumor microenvironment (TME) during tumorigenesis has been extensively reviewed elsewhere recently [17], the present review describes the ANXA2 network in the TME and elucidates whether ANXA2 could possibly be used as a target for treating cancers.

Stromal cell types in the TME

It is widely acknowledged that cancer exhibits key hallmarks, including genomic instability and mutations, uncontrolled growth, evasion of growth suppressors, resistance to cell death, heightened inflammation, enhanced metabolism, and the capacity to promote angiogenesis, invasion, and metastasis [4, 18]. It is widely recognized that tumors consist not only of tumor cells but also encompass recruited stromal cells and the non-cellular component, the ECM. Stromal cells and the ECM play active roles during tumorigenesis, initially exerting anti-tumorigenic effects in the early stages and transitioning to pro-tumorigenic roles over time, thereby contributing to the acquisition of specific cancer hallmarks [5, 8, 19, 20]. Therefore, the study and comprehension of cancer and tumorigenesis now encompass not only tumor cells but also stromal cells and the ECM, collectively constituting the tumor microenvironment [6, 21–27]. It has been proposed that components of stromal cells are associated with cancer hallmarks, such as the impact of endothelial cells on tumor angiogenesis. Related review has been proposed for interpreting the relationship between cancer cells and the TME [19]. The review by Hanahan and Weinberg [18] describes how stromal cells contribute to cancer hallmarks, wherein the authors classified the general components of the TME matrix as follows: infiltrating immune cells, CAFs, and angiogenic vascular cells [28].

Role of ANXA2 in angiogenic vascular cells

It is known that ECM degradation is promoted by plasmin, followed by cancer cell migration and invasion and tumor neoangiogenesis [29–31]. Given its critical role in plasmin formation, ANXA2 is overexpressed in human breast cancer MDA-MB -231 cells, but minimally expressed in non-invasive MCF-7 cells, indicating the association of ANXA2 with an aggressive phenotype [32]. Another study showed that ANXA2 expression is probably correlated with plasmin overproduction and regula -tion of neoangiogenesis in the TME [33]. These findings were also confirmed by an Anxa2 gene knockout mice experiment, in which the knockout mice showed reduced neoangiogenesis [34]. Collectively, the abovementioned findings show that ANXA2 participates in neoangiogenesis [35]. As the cell surface receptor for tissue plasmin -ogen activator (tPA) and plasminogen, ANXA2 facilitates the production of plasmin. The ANXA2 heterotetramer consists of two copies of A2 and two copies of S100A10/p11. It promotes fibrinolytic activity on the surface of vascular endothelial cells by assembling plasminogen and tissue plasminogen activator (tPA), thereby accelerating the production of plasmin [36, 37]. Heterotetramer ANXA2 also plays a crucial role in degrading the ECM required for angiogenesis, tumor expansion, and invasion [38]. Anti-ANXA2 antibodies can inhibit breast cancer cell invasion and migration [32] as well as prevent angiogenesis [33].

In addition, ECM degradation releases vascular endothelial growth factor, a matrix-binding growth factor, and the inactive pre-matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), and they trigger the “angiogenesis switch” during their participation in tissue remodeling [39–43] (Fig. 2). This finding reveals that pre-MMPs perform important functions in regulating and processing the functional, active forms [44–47]. Therefore, activated MMPs, particularly MMP-2 as well as MMP-9, stimulate angiogenesis and promote remodeling of the tissue by degrading the endothelial cell basement membrane, ECM, connective tissue, and epithelial cells [39–44, 48]. The MMP-based process of tissue degradation is considered a classical model of neoangiogenesis and invasion of tumor cells [30, 47, 49, 50]. The MMP-9/2 activated form is implicated in angiogenesis and breast cancer cell invasion [41, 51–54].

ANXA2 binds to plasminogen (PLG) and tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) on tumor cell (TC) and endothelial cell (EC) surface. This process transforms PLG in the tumor microenvironment into active plasmin. Basement membrane (BM) degradation by plasmin generation leads to the emergence of activated forms of pro-MMPs and pro-VEGF, which promote the biological actions of EC, such as invasion, proliferation, migration, and subsequent neoangiogenesis. The newly generated blood vessels promote TC circulation, and the circulating TC then bind to PLG and tPA to degrade the ECM (adapted from [29])

Endothelial cells binding to ANXA2 complex form the inner lining of blood vessels, and they function as the main boundary between the organ vasculature and the surrounding tissues [55]. They regulate the transport of small molecules and metabolites through the endothelial barrier and maintain intravascular homeostasis by secreting factors that control local inflammation, thrombosis, and fibrinolysis. In their resting state, endothelial surfaces act as anticoagulants, while in an activated state, they become procoagulant and proinflammatory, capturing platelets and leukocytes. The ANXA2-S100A10 complex in breast cancer cells promotes neoangiogenesis by binding to vascular endothelial cells. Reducing ANXA2 expression in glioblastoma cells delays tumor progression by slowing angiogenesis, invasion, apoptosis, and proliferation [11].

ANXA2's role in angiogenesis and glioma invasion is linked to EGFR and HIF-1 expression [56–58]. Experimental models show ANXA2 interacting with vascular endothelial cadherin and F-actin, regulating the Robo4-paxillin-ARF6 pathway to protect against transendothelial permeability. As a coreceptor of tPA and plasminogen, ANXA2 aids in plasmin production and may regulate the dynamics of actin expression [34]. Inhibiting ANXA2 with the LCKLSL hexapeptide reduces plasmin production. Post-cerebrovascular injury, ANXA2maintains endothelial cell compact -ness, suggesting potential therapeutic targets for protecting the blood–brain barrier [59, 60].

In summary, ANXA2 is crucial in regulating angiogenesis and presents a potential target for inhibiting tumor angiogenesis. (Fig. 2) [34, 61].

Role of ANXA2 in stromal cells

Stroma cells secrete cell factors that influence cancer progression. Therefore, the knowledge of the mechanisms responsible for controlling cancer development by stromal cell signals may guide the production of novel therapeutic drugs against cancer. ANXA2 plays a significant role in cancer progression through its interactions with the stroma cells. In the presence of Collagen-I, the main constituent of the stroma cells, ANXA2 undergoes post-translational phosphorylation at Tyr24. This phosphory -lation promotes the translocation of ANXA2 to the cell surface, where it can interact with various stroma components. Mass spectrometry analysis revealed that ANXA2 binds to several stroma-related proteins, with fibronectin being a central interactor. This interaction is significant because the degradation of fibronectin by plasmin enhances tumor cell migration and invasion. Furthermore, the regulation of ANXA2 by Collagen-I underscores its role in the dynamic reciprocity between cancer cells and the stroma, contributing to the aggressive behavior of tumors [62, 63].

In some studies, the metastatic properties of stromal cell signals were assessed to determine whether they perform critical functions in the microenvironment of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC). It was observed that Src and insulin-like growth factor type 1 receptor (IGF-1R) phosphorylated the premetastatic molecule ANXA2 in response to the stromal cell signaling molecules hepatocyte growth factor (HGF) and IGF-1, respectively. The expression of IGF-1 was additionally regulated by the Sonic hedgehog (Shh) pathway. Shh and HGF exhibited heterogeneous expression within the pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) stroma, and inhibiting both pathways significantly reduced ANXA2 phosphorylation, as well as the proliferation and metastasis of PDAC cells [64].

In another study, Jung et al. found that Anxa2 knockout mice showed fewer hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) in the bone marrow and low expression levels of CXCR4 and CXCR7, thus indicating inherent cellular defects. Studies on transplantation of wild-type bone marrow into Anxa2 knockout mice also confirmed this finding. CXCL12 binds directly to ANXA2 and facilitates its presentation to HSCs [65]. However, the interaction between CXCL12 and ANXA2 can lead to proteolytic cleavage, which is mediated by proteases that are induced by granulocyte–macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF). This process results in the mobilization of stem or progenitor cells. Taken together, these observations confirm that ANXA2 substantially controls the anchoring of CXCL12 to translocate HSCs to the tumor niche [65].

D’Souza et al. reported that multiple myeloma (MM) is incurable because the marrow microenvironment contributes to B-cell malignancy. Marrow stromal cells existing in the marrow microenvironment perform the crucial function of facilitating the growth, homing, and lodging of MM cells by stimulating complex signaling pathways in stromal or MM cells. Thus, ANXA2 is considered an emerging factor produced by stromal cells and osteoclasts, and it participates in the formation of osteoclasts and homing of HSCs and prostate cancer (PCa) cells to the bone marrow. ANXA2 can function as a receptor on the cell surface, interacting with ligands such as plasminogen and tissue-type plasminogen activator (tPA). This interaction is important for processes like fibrinolysis and tissue remodeling. ANXA2 expressed by stromal cells enables the lodging of PCa cells through the ANXA2 receptor on these cells. Moreover, it was observed that the ANXA2 receptor expressed on MM cells enables these cells to adhere to stromal cells; the growth response of MM cells was also enhanced byte OCL-derived ANXA2 receptor. Finally, ANXA2 stimulates the AKT and ERK1/2 pathways to increase the growth of MM cells [66].

Ahmad Salameh et al. demonstrated that ANXA2 and prohibitin-1(PHB) interact in adipocytes. Mice lacking ANXA2 had normal white adipose tissue vascularization, adipogenesis, and glucose metabolism, but atrophy of white adipose tissue due to decreased fatty acid uptake by white adipose tissue endothelial cells and adipocytes. The researchers also demonstrated that ANXA2 and PHB form a complex with the fatty acid transporter CD36, which correlates with the biodistribution of long-chain fatty acids, thus providing new ideas for metabolic diseases and cancer prevention [67, 68].

The role of ANXA2 in immune cell stimulation and infiltration

Plasmin is a potential signaling molecule, especially in human monocytes, and it induces a proinflammatory response. Some research studies included the ANXA2 receptor, as a plasmin receptor, in the list of receptors expressed on the surface of human monocytes. The ANXA2 receptor is, however, cleaved into its subunits by plasmin; this step triggers the signaling process in monocytes and causes the heterotetramer on the cell surface to dissociate. Initially, an analog of ANXA2 could mimic the plasmin activation process of tyrosine kinases JAK1 and STAT3, followed by increased expression of monocyte/macrophage chemoattractant protein-1, a major chemokine secreted from monocytes and macrophages [69]. Laumonnier et al. also demonstrated that the ANXA2 heterotetramer signaling receptor can be activated by plasmin through proteolysis [70]. Lei et al. conducted a preclinical study and highlighted how ANXA2 functions in bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis, which could enable to improve the use of bleomycin to treat cancers in clinical settings as well as provide insights into the pathological mechanisms of pulmonary fibrosis [71].

The decreased density of macrophages in the tumor impairs tumor growth. Furthermore, chlorophosphonate-mediated macrophage depletion also caused an identical reduction in tumor size in wild-type mice. Previous studies have reported that soluble ANXA2 tetramers stimulate mouse monocyte- and human monocyte -derived macrophages. This stimulation requires the presence of Toll-like receptor 4. The tetramer also regulates cytokine production in macrophages [72, 73]. Therefore, the macrophage-based invasion of tumors may be partly due to the signaling transduction of the soluble ANXA2 tetramer.

In humans, T cells form the first protective barrier through-cell receptor (TCR)-mediated recognition of stressed cells. Romain Marlin et al. demonstrated that the exposure of tumor cells to various stress stimuli led to the recognition of tumor cells by a Vγ8Vδ3 TCR. ANXA2is expressed in tumor cells under stress conditions according to the levels of reactive oxygen species. Purified ANXA2 also stimulates growth of a T-cell subset among peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMNCs) and other ANXA2-specific T-cell clones acquired from PBMNCs [74].

Mechanisms of ANXA2-mediated metastasis and resistance to anticancer therapy

Multiple interactions between the TME and tumor cells cause some tumor cells to escape apoptosis as well as exhibit resistance to anticancer drugs. Notably, drug resistance mediated by the environment is generally transient and occurs only when the tumor cells come interacted with the relating microenvironment. Following their exclusion from the microenvironment, the tumor cells again show drug sensitivity [60]. Overexpression of lncRNA MUF accelerates epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and enhances malignant tumor capacity. The mechanism underlying this phenomenon involves lncRNA MUF binding to ANXA2, followed by EMT induction and activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway [75].

ANXA2 knockout in glioma cells delayed tumor progression and reduced the size of the tumor through various ways, such as a decrease in invasion, angiogenesis, and proliferation and an increase in tumor cell apoptosis. Haiyan Zhai et al. showed that ANXA2 knockdown reduced the migration of glioma cells in vitro but lowered the activity of plasmin bound to the membrane. Furthermore, in the same study, rodent brains were injected with glioma cells, and the progression of glioma was monitored. The results revealed that ANXA2 expression level in the human glioma samples were associated with malignancy degree [11].

This precedent study suggested that ANXA2 analog in combination with PD-1 antibodies could be an alternative choice for PDAC therapy. After ANXA2 knockout in a PDAC transgenic mouse (Kras and p53 mutants with Cre alleles targeted to the pancreas), human PDAC progressed from pre-malignancy to metastatic disease, thus verifying ANXA2 promotion of in vivo metastases [76]. In addition to its interaction with PAK1, ANXA2 also regulated Sema3D secretion in PDAC cells, and Sema3D was found to be associated with plexin D1, a coreceptor in PDAC cells. Supporting this hypothesis, neither impaired the invasive and metastatic potentials of PDAC cells in mice [15].

The interaction between ANXA2 and CXCL12 in the TME is key point for the metastasis phenotype and may affect the potential efficacy of chemotherapy [77]. G-Rg5 and G-Rk1 have been confirmed to inhibit the growth of tumor cells through targeting ANXA2 and the NF-κB pathway. Thus, these molecules could be emergent natural compounds for application in cancer therapies. In summary, the mechanisms underlying the promotion of cancer progression by ANXA2 include EMT; stimulation of cell migration, proliferation, and invasion; resistance to various treatments; and cancer stem cell production [78].

Dr Chaudhary et al. demonstrated ANXA2–EGFR interaction on tumor cell surface, which critically affects the proliferation and migration of cancer cells by regulating EGFR function. The anti-ANXA2 antibody blocks ANXA2’s function on the cell membrane and suppresses EGF-induced tyrosine phosphorylation and EGFR internalization through blockage of its homodimer. Moreover, during EGF induction and growth, the inclusion of ANXA2 antibodies remarkably suppressed the EGFR-correlation PI3K/AKT and Raf/MEK/ERK downstream pathways, leading to decreased migration and proliferation of cancer cells [79]. The phosphorylation of ANXA2 at Tyr23 is essential for the invasion and metastasis of multidrug-resistant cells of breast cancer. Rack1 participates in enhancing the invasiveness and metastatic potential of drug-resistant cells of breast cancer by altering the phosphorylation level of ANXA2. We also confirmed that Rack1 functions as a signaling hub to mediate Src–ANXA2 interaction, thus facilitating the phosphorylation of ANXA2 by Src kinase [80]. The results revealed that high levels of ANXA2 phosphorylation are related with the upregulated levels of Src in the TME in most solid cancers [81].

The abovementioned studies offer deep insights into the molecular features of cancer proliferation by ANXA2-related mechanisms and further highlight the excellent potential to overcome the drug resistance of many types of cancers showing high expression levels of ANXA2 by formulating novel therapies targeted toward ANXA2.

ANXA2’s role in cancer stem cell

Human embryonic stem cells (hESCs) are a potential source of fetal antigens [82]. To identify novel fetal targets, researchers used a panel of monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) generated with intact hESCs as immunogens [83]. This method allows for the targeting of naturally occurring conformational epitopes, such as those involving glycosylation in post-translational modifications [84]. The reports from this group include monoclonal antibodies directed against well-known fetal antigens like the epithelial cell adhesion molecule (EpCAM, CD326) and podocalyxin-like protein-1 (PODXL-1) [85].

Herein, some study report 2448, a mouse IgG1 derived from the same panel of monoclonal antibodies. In addition to its capability to selectively target undifferentiated hESCs [83], 2448 has been found to connect to the membrane of breast cancer cells and epithelial ovarian. The antigen target of 2448 has been identified as a glycosylated epitope on annexin A2 membrane protein. Importantly, in vitro assays and murine xenotransplantation models, chimeric 2448 (ch2448) demonstrates cancer cell killing through both antibody–drug conjugate (ADC) and antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity (ADCC) pathways.

In Ma Sai’s study, the researchers implemented a miRNA microarray in CD133 + liver cancer stem cells (LCSCs) and discovered a downregulation of miR-101 expression. Enhanced expression of miR-101 was found to suppress the metastatic and tumorigenic potential of LCSCs. Crucially, the study identified ANXA2 as a novel and significant target of miR-101, playing a pivotal role in LCSCs. ANXA2 was shown to be essential in accelerating the cell cycle and enhancing the migratory and invasive capabilities of LCSCs. Elevated levels of ANXA2 led to increased activation of extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK), which subsequently regulated SOX2 and cell cycle-related kinases. Additionally, ERK phosphorylation inhibited the expression of early growth response 1 (EGR1), thereby suppressing the transcription of miR-101, creating a feedback loop that promotes tumorigenic properties [86].

Cancer stem cells (CSCs) have been well-documented to play a crucial role in driving tumor dormancy and resistance to therapy. However, most therapeutic approaches fail to effectively target these regenerative populations. In another study, the researchers report the development and evaluation of a monoclonal antibody, mAb150, which targets the metastasis-associated antigen ANXA2 by recognizing an N-terminal epitope. Treatment with mAb150 was shown to enhance the re-entry of CSCs into the cell cycle, thereby disrupting tumor dormancy and promoting the targeted elimination of CSCs. This effect was validated through both in vitro and in vivo experiments. The findings underscore the critical role of AnxA2 in CSC biology and highlight the potential of mAb150 as a therapeutic strategy to overcome tumor dormancy and resistance, leading to more effective cancer treatments [87].

Early detection of HCC is challenging due to the lack of reliable biomarkers. A clinical trial aimed to evaluate the diagnostic role of Annexin A2 (ANXA2) and follistatin as serum biomarkers in HCC patients. The study demonstrated that the combined detection of Annexin A2 and AFP significantly improved diagnostic efficiency, with 98% specificity, LR + 41, and 97.6% PPV. Although the combined detection of follistatin and AFP provided 92% specificity, its sensitivity was relatively low (50%). Serum ANXA2 is a promising biomarker for HCC, especially when measured alongside AFP. Follistatin, while unable to distinguish HCC from chronic liver disease on its own, enhances the specificity of HCC diagnosis when combined with AFP [20].

Therapeutic targeting of AXAN2

ANXA2 exhibits upregulation in various cancer types. Its pivotal role in tumor progression is underscored, encompassing critical functions such as cell survival, proliferation, migration, invasion, and metastasis. Furthermore, aberrant ANXA2 expression is intricately linked to the development of multidrug resistance, posing challenges in cancer therapeutics [78, 88–93]. ANXA2 recognizes the binding site of bleomycin in pulmonary fibrosis, leading to resistance to bleomycin therapy [92]. Some studies have further demonstrated that Glu139 (E139) in ANXA2 is essential for reducing fibrosis induced by bleomycin. If this site mutates, bleomycin is prevented from binding to ANXA2 and activates EB transcription factor (TFEB) to regulate autophagy in lung epithelial cells. TFEB mediated autophagy enhances flux, thereby suppressing epithelial cell apoptosis and proliferation, and enhancing the efficacy of bleomycin therapy for pulmonary fibrosis in murine models. This finding also designates ANXA2 as a promising target for bleomycin treatment. Natural compounds are expected to be used for ANXA2 targeted therapy. Saponins Rg5 and Rk1 have similar structures and were found to specifically bind to ANXA2, and inhibit NF-Kβ through the interaction between two saponins and ANXA2, downregulation of apoptotic protein inhibitors, activation of caspase and promotion of apoptosis [94]. Researchers purified chickpea plant agglutinin and tested its anti-tumor effect. It has been confirmed that this plant lectin could indeed inhibit tumor cell proliferation, promote migration and apoptosis, and inhibit EGFR mediated signaling by blocking the binding of ANXA2 and galactin [95]. Matrine, a plant alkaloid extracted from China, has anti-tumor activity by directly targeting ANXA2 [96]. Daniela et al. suggest that there is indeed a functional link between ANXA2 and tumor cell adhesion, migration and engraftment in vivo, which is not yet clear. In addition, this study identifies new peptide motifs that bind to ANXA2 and inhibit it, which could be used as therapeutic candidates and could potentially be translated into clinical applications [97].

There is a growing focus on investigating therapeutic approaches targeting ANXA2 in cancer management. A biochemical analysis showed that treatment with ANXA2 monoclonal antibodies significantly inhibited tPA transformation in the TME, thereby interrupting plasmin production and consequently preventing MMP-9 and MMP-2 activation. These findings illustrate that ANXA2 has a crucial role in the invasiveness of breast cancer cells by regulating the proteolytic pathway in the TME. Thus, ANXA2 could be used as a new target for developing drugs to treat invasive breast cancer [29] (Table 1).

Annexin based biomarker in cancer therapy

The measurement of ANXA2 levels has gained concern for its prognostic and diagnostic value in cancer therapy. It is noteworthy that ANXA2 has been documented to be upregulated in NSCLC [13], HCC [98], serous ovarian cancer [99], epithelial ovarian carcinoma [100], urothelial carcinoma [101], breast cancer [102, 103], and NPC [104], correlating with poor prognosis. Meta-analysis involving 2321 patients with various cancers confirmed that elevated ANXA2 expression is associated with overall survival (OS) (Hazard Ratio [HR] 1.56; p < 0.001) and disease-free survival (DFS) (HR 1.47; p < 0.001) [105].

In another aspect, ANXA2 serves as a diagnostic factor for cancer screening. Elevated ANXA2 serum levels have been assessed in peripheral blood in HCC [98, 106], gastric cancer [107], and OSCC [108]. Besides being detectable in the serum of cancer patients, excessive ANXA2 expression is also reported to be associated with a high risk of metastasis and recurrence [109]. It indicates that ANXA2 may represent a diagnostic and emerging prognostic biomarker in cancer treatment. Investigators have identified a probe set, Tz6/10, and demonstrated the role of these probes in cancer diagnosis or treatment by markering different ANXA2-expressed cancer cell lines [110].

ANXA2 emerges as a promising biomarker for the detection of invasive breast cancer, especially in estrogen receptor-negative cases. ANXA2 was notably involved in cell motility and was secreted at higher levels in invasive triple-negative breast carcinoma cells (Hs578T and MDA-MB-231), correlating with their invasive phenotypes. Clinically, ANXA2 levels were significantly higher in plasma samples and breast cancer tissues from patients compared to normal groups, reinforcing the relevance of the in vitro findings [111]. ANXA2 is involved in several tumorigenic processes, including proliferation, migration, invasion, and metastasis. Elevated levels of ANXA2 in serum correlate with higher tumor grades and poorer survival rates in breast cancer patients. Moreover, the phosphorylation of ANXA2 at Tyr23 enhances its secretion from TNBC cells, making it a crucial step for its role as a secretory biomarker. The diagnostic value of serum ANXA2 is notably high in TNBC, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.951, demonstrating its potential as a diagnostic and prognostic marker [112]. ANXA2 also serves as a crucial biomarker for cancer prognosis and immune infiltration, showing significant upregulation in various cancers, including liver hepatocellular carcinoma and pancreatic adenocarcinoma. ANXA2 plays an important role in immune cell infiltration, DNA methylation, tumor mutation burden, and microsatellite instability, indicating its role in tumorigenesis and immune regulation. This suggests that ANXA2 could be a promising target for cancer immunotherapy and a valuable prognostic marker [113].

The expression of ANXA2 in exosomes is associated with enhanced tumorigenic activities, including angiogenesis, metastasis, and invasion. ANXA2 is secreted and transported in exosomes through lipid raft-mediated endocytosis and exosomal transport mechanisms. Studies have shown that the presence of ANXA2 in exosomes isolated from serum samples of breast cancer patients correlates with the aggressive phenotype of the cancer. This protein promotes cancer cell motility and invasiveness by facilitating the reorganization of the actin cytoskeleton and formation of cellular protrusions like filopodia. Furthermore, ANXA2 in exosomes is implicated in the modulation of the tumor microenvironment, contributing to the establishment of pre-metastatic niches and enhancing the cancer cells' ability to colonize distant organs. Thus, ANXA2's presence in exosomes underscores its potential as a biomarker for cancer diagnosis and prognosis, as well as a target for therapeutic interventions [114].

Conclusion

ANXA2 remarkably influences the adhesion, angiogenesis, and migration and proliferation of cancer cells and is a crucial molecule in the TME and tumor cells, including tumor cell metastasis [115, 116]. New lines of evidence suggest that ANXA2 affects critically the tPA system [73, 117–121]. ANXA2–tPA interaction mediates plasminogen to plasmin conversion, which contributes to degradation of the ECM and results in angiogenesis; this process is essential for enhancing the migration and invasiveness of cancer cells [29, 122]. The role of ANXA2 in regulating the relationship between overexpression and proteolytic cleavage in different malignancies has also been confirmed. The relevance of ANXA2 in relation to MMPs has been evaluated in tumor progression. The obtained findings provide a possible explanation regarding the differences between the outcomes of previous studies in demonstrating the complex and heterogeneous environment of PDAC stromal cells and the signaling pathways involved in the process[123, 124]. It is essential to reveal the complexity of stromal cell signals in PDAC to determine whether ANXA2 phosphorylation inhibition in premetastatic stromal signals can be an effective approach for cancer treatment that provides survival benefits[125, 126]. In the study of the role of adipocyte and endothelial cell CD36 in the promotion of tumor growth and chemoresistance produced by adipose tissue-derived low-lipoic acid fatty acids, a bi-directional cellular LCFA transport mechanism regulated by CD36 S-acylation and its interactions with PHB and ANX2 is proposed, demonstrating the potential of ANXA2 as a therapeutic target for cancer [67].

Current studies have focused on ANXA2. A close association between macrophages and cancer cells is required to promote tumor growth and metastasis. These findings reveal a new therapeutic approach to control tumor progression by targeting tumor-associated macrophages. ANXA2 is translocated into the nucleus, and its phosphorylated form prevents DNA damage due to radiotoxicity. Chemotherapeutic drugs assemble to ANXA2 with enlargeosomes exocytosis, thus decreasing the chemotoxicity level in cancer cells [70, 127–132]. Furthermore, ANXA2–Sema3D interaction triggers immunosuppression that occurs due to the escape of immune cells from the TME. These research findings provide greater insights into the molecular pathways of cancer progression and offer excellent guidance for improving the efficacy of targeted therapies against NPC and other types of cancers showing high expression levels of ANXA2 [94, 99, 133–138]. Further research is required to explore the correlation between ANXA2 and its binding proteins, to clarify how ANXA2 is critical in promoting metastasis and invasion of tumor cells, and to provide more details on whether these mechanisms will allow the development of novel drugs to inhibit cancer progression and drug resistance.

Future direction

Future research on ANXA2 should focus on elucidating the mechanisms underlying its role in cancer progression and its potential as a therapeutic target. Current studies have highlighted ANXA2’s involvement in various tumorigenic processes, including cell migration, invasion, and angiogenesis, primarily through its interactions with the extracellular matrix and immune modulation. Future directions should include exploring ANXA2’s specific signaling pathways and its interactions with other proteins at the molecular level. Additionally, developing targeted therapies that inhibit ANXA2’s function could provide new avenues for cancer treatment. Integrating bioinformatics and experimental approaches will enhance our understanding of ANXA2’s prognostic value across different cancer types, facilitating its use as a biomarker for early diagnosis and personalized therapy.
