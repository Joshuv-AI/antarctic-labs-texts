Abstract
. Bojańczyk, Pilipczuk, and Grohe [LICS ’18] proved that for graphs of bounded linear clique-width, clique-decompositions of bounded width can be produced by a CMSO transduction. We show that in the case of tournaments, a first-order transduction suffices. This implies that the logics CMSO and existential MSO are equivalent over bounded linear clique-width tournaments.



1.
Introduction

Bojańczyk and Pilipczuk proved the following conjecture of Courcelle:

Theorem 1.1

(
[
4
,
2
]
)
.

For any
k
, there is a monadic second-order (
MSO
) transduction
\Phi
from graphs to tree-decompositions of graphs such that, on every input graph
G
, the
following holds:

(1)

If
G
is a graph of tree-width at most
k
, then
\Phi
non-deterministically outputs a tree-decomposition of
G
of width at most
k
, and

(2)

every output of
\Phi
is a tree-decomposition of width at most
k
.

This can be understood as saying that tree-decomposition can be constructed
not in a usual algorithmic sense, but through a process described by
MSO
logic.
The motivation for this result is to prove the converse of Courcelle’s theorem:
for graphs of tree-width
k
, if
\mathcal{P}
is a property that can be tested by a tree automaton running on tree decompositions of width
k
, then
\mathcal{P}
is definable by an
MSO
formula.

The proof of
Theorem

1.1
proceeds in two main steps:

(1)

First, one considers bounded
path-width
graphs.
Path decompositions are seen as words in a semigroup,
to which can be applied the Factorisation Forest Theorem of Simon
[
13
]
.
This reduces the problem to path decomposition with a crucial regularity property—idempotence—which notably ensures the existence of paths without excessive detours.

(2)

In the general case, one constructs
a tree decomposition whose bags have bounded path-width, and are in a sense maximal.
Careful connectivity considerations, involving path systems with low congestion,
allow to MSO transduce this decomposition, reducing the problem to the previous path-width case.

A natural generalisation of
Theorem

1.1
is to consider dense graphs,
replacing tree-width by clique-width.
The same authors and Grohe generalised step (
1
) to this setting,
replacing path-width by its dense equivalent
linear clique-width
,
and using the slightly stronger logic
CMSO
, i.e.
MSO
with counting modulo 2.
Their proof is once again crucially based on Simon’s theorem.

Theorem 1.2

(
[
3
]
)
.

For any
k
, there is a
CMSO
transduction
\Phi
from graphs to clique-decompositions, and
k^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
for any input graph
G
,

(1)

If
G
has linear clique-width at most
k
, then
\Phi
non-deterministically outputs a clique-decomposition of
G
of width at most
k^{\prime}
,

(2)

every output of
\Phi
is a clique-decomposition of width at most
k^{\prime}
.

Pushing this generalisation to graphs of bounded clique-width however remains an open problem:
the second step of the proof of
Theorem

1.1
seems strongly tied to the behaviour of paths in tree decompositions,
hence much harder to generalise to dense graphs.
The proof of
Theorem

1.2
is already quite involved,
requiring to define a dense equivalent of connectivity.

We propose considering these questions for a restricted class of graphs,
namely
tournaments
, i.e. directed graphs in which between every pair of vertices
x,y
,
exactly one of the edges
x\to y
or
y\to x
exists.
In this setting, we strengthen
Theorem

1.2
in two ways:

•

We only use first-order (
FO
) logic, which is much weaker than
MSO
.

•

Our transduction always produces
linear
decompositions,
whereas the output decompositions sometimes need to be tree-like
in
Theorem

1.2
and step (
1
) of
Theorem

1.1
.

That is, we prove the following.

Theorem 1.3
.

For any
k
, one can compute an
FO
transduction
\Phi
from graphs to linear clique-decompositions, and
k^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
, such that on any input graph
T
,

(1)

if
T
is a tournament of linear clique-width at most
k
, then
\Phi
non-deterministically outputs a linear clique-decomposition of
T
of width at most
k^{\prime}
, and

(2)

every output of
\Phi
is a linear clique-decomposition of width at most
k^{\prime}
.

The proof of
Theorem

1.3
is also significantly simpler than
Theorem

1.2
.
The key difference is that we do not refer to any notion of connectivity.
Oversimplifying greatly, in the path-width case,
the relative ordering of vertices in the path decomposition
is determined by queries such as ‘is there a small separator between
x
and
y
’.
For tournaments, our queries are rather ‘is
x\to y
an edge’
or ‘is there a vertex
z
with edges
x\to z\to y
’.
The latter, unlike the former, can be expressed using first-order logic.
Simon’s theorem remains a key tool in our proof.

Theorem

1.2
, together with Courcelle’s Backwards Translation Theorem
[
6
, Theorem 1.40]
,
implies that for bounded linear clique-width graphs,
a property
\mathcal{P}
is
CMSO
definable if and only if it is testable by a finite automaton running on linear clique decompositions.
In the case of tournaments, our
FO
transduction implies that the same holds with

existential
MSO

(
EMSO
) instead of
CMSO
.
Therefore, by going back and forth through automata on linear clique decompositions, we obtain the following.

Theorem 1.4
.

For any
k
and any
CMSO
sentence
\varphi
on tournaments,
there is an
EMSO
formula
\psi
such that
\varphi
and
\psi

are equivalent for tournaments of linear clique-width at most
k
.

Let us conclude by discussing potential generalisations of our results to (non-linear) clique-width, and limits to them.

Theorem

1.3
cannot directly generalise to bounded clique-width tournaments:
A Ramsey-like argument of Mikołaj Bojańczyk (personal communication) shows that
for
\mathcal{T}
the class of tournaments obtained by iterated lexicographic products of directed triangles
(which has clique-width 3),
no
FO
transduction can produce a clique decomposition of bounded width for all tournaments in
\mathcal{T}
.
On the other hand, it is simple to do so for the class
\mathcal{T}
with a transduction in the logic
FO
with counting modulo 2
(
FO
+
C
).
To our knowledge, it is possible that
Theorem

1.3
generalises to clique-width if we allow
FO
+
C
transductions.

A major part of our proof focuses on constructing a vertex ordering with low cut-rank, as a first step towards a linear clique decomposition.
We believe that asking to transduce
any
total vertex ordering is an interesting intermediate question.
For which tournament classes
\mathcal{C}
and logics
\mathcal{L}
is there an
\mathcal{L}
-transduction that produces a total vertex ordering on any given
T\in\mathcal{C}
?
Bojańczyk’s example once again implies that this is not possible for bounded clique-width tournaments and the logic
FO
, but
FO
+
C
could be sufficient.
Note that from a bounded width clique-decomposition of a tournament, one can always transduce a linear vertex ordering, corresponding to some left-to-right ordering on the leaves of the decomposition tree.

Let us point out that in the context of e.g.
Theorem

,
one needs to consider a generalisation of clique decompositions

These high degree nodes make it impossible to transduce a total vertex ordering from the decomposition tree.
In the case of tournaments however, they cannot exist.

Thus transducing a total ordering is an easier question than transducing clique-decompositions.

Structure of the paper

Section

presents standard definitions and results on clique-width and

In

, we introduce an infinite monoid

2.
Preliminaries

We denote by
[n]
the interval of integers
\{1,\dots,n\}
.

A
tournament

T=(V,E)
is a directed graph with for each pair
u\neq v
of vertices in
V
, exactly one of the two possible directed edges
(u,v)
or
(v,u)
in
E
.
We also write
V(T)\coloneqq V
and
E(T)\coloneqq E
for the vertex and edge sets of
T
,
and denote an oriented edge
(u,v)\in E(T)
as
u\to v
to emphasise its orientation.
A
bipartite tournament

B=(L,R,E)
is defined similarly restricting to edges between the two vertex sets
L
and
R
: the edge set
E
consists of
exactly one of
u\to v
or
v\to u
for each pair of vertices
u\in L
and
v\in R
.

In a tournament
T
, two disjoint subsets of vertices
A,B\subset V(T)
are called
homogenous
if the edges between them are either all oriented
from
A
to
B
, i.e.
a\to b
is an edge for all
a\in A
and
b\in B
, or inversely all from
B
to
A
.

2.1.
Linear NLC-width and rank-width

There are several graph complexity measures that are equivalent to (linear) clique-width. For our purposes, we will use two of them instead of linear
clique-width, namely
linear NLC-width

[
14
]
and
linear rank-width

[
1
]
.

For the definition of linear NLC-width, we restrict ourselves to tournaments, and refer to
[
14
]
for the definition on all graphs.
NLC-width and clique-width (and their linear variants) are very similar in definitions, and differ by a factor of at most 2, see for instance
[
7
,
8
,
9
]
for the proof of the equivalences.

A
k
-labelled tournament
is a tuple
T=(V,E,\lambda)
where
(V,E)
is a tournament equipped with a labelling map
\lambda:V\to[k]
.
We also often call
\lambda(v)
the
colour
of the vertex
v
; however
\lambda
is not a proper colouring.

Definition 2.1

(Linear NLC-width)
.

The class
\mathcal{T}_{k}
consists of the
k
-labelled tournaments obtained by iteratively applying the following two operations, starting from the empty tournament:

vertex addition:

Given a
k
-labelled tournament
T=(V,E,\lambda)
, a subset
X\subseteq[k]
, and a colour
c\in[k]
,
add a new vertex
v
to
V
, add for each
u\in V
the edge
u\to v
when
\lambda(u)\in X
, and
u\leftarrow v
otherwise, and finally extend
\lambda
to
v
as
\lambda(v)=c
.

relabelling:

Given a
k
-labelled tournament
T=(V,E,\lambda)
and a function
\rho:[k]\to[k]
, replace the labelling
\lambda
by
\rho\circ\lambda
.

The least
k
such that
T\in\mathcal{T}_{k}
is called the
linear NLC-width
of
T
, denoted by
\lnlcw(T)
.

A sequence of vertex-addition and relabelling operations witnessing that
\lnlcw(T)\leqslant k
is called a
linear NLC decomposition of width
k
of
T
, or simply linear decomposition.
Given a linear decomposition
D
of
T
, the order in which vertices are added in
D
defines a linear ordering
<
of
V(T)
.
When
D
is a linear decomposition of width
k
, we say that
<
is a
linear decomposition ordering of width
k
.
By extension, we define the
relative NLC-width
of a linear ordering
<
of
V
as the smallest
k
such that
<
is a linear decomposition ordering of width
k
.

Let us now define
linear rank-width
,
which will be more convenient to bound the width of the output of our transductions.
We again restrict the definition to tournaments, but refer to for instance
[
1
,
12
]
for more information on (linear) rank-width.
The adjacency matrix of a tournament
T
is the boolean matrix whose rows and columns are indexed by
V(T)
, where the entry at row
x
and column
y
is
1
only if
(x,y)\in E(T)
.

Definition 2.2

(Linear rank-width)
.

In a tournament
T
, consider a bi-partition
V(T)=X\uplus Y
of the vertices.
The
rank
of this bi-partition, denoted by
\rk_{T}(X;Y)
, is the rank (over the binary field
\mathbb{F}_{2}
) of the adjacency matrix of
X
versus
Y
, i.e. the
adjacency matrix restricted to rows indexed by vertices in
X
and columns indexed by vertices in
Y
.
If
x_{1}<\dots<x_{n}
is a linear ordering of
V(T)
, the
cut-rank
of
<
is defined as

\max_{i}\rk\left(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{i}\};\{x_{i+1},\dots,x_{n}\}\right).

The
linear rank-width
of
T
, denoted by
\lrw(T)
, is the minimum cut-rank over all linear orderings of
V(T)
.

Rank-width and clique-width (or NLC-width) are famously equivalent up to an exponential bound
[
11
]
.
The proof in fact relates the different widths of any fixed ordering.
Since we need this slightly more precise result, we state it below and provide a proof in the case of
tournaments, however it holds for all graphs.

Lemma 2.3
.

For any linear ordering
<
of a tournament
T
, if
\nlcw
and
\rw
denote, respectively, the relative NLC-width and cut-rank of
<
in
T
, then

\rw\leqslant\nlcw\leqslant 2^{\rw}+1.

Proof.

Enumerate
V(T)
as
v_{1}<\dots<v_{n}
.

First assume that
<
is a linear NLC decomposition ordering of width
k
.
In the corresponding linear decomposition, call
\lambda_{i}:\{v_{1},\dots,v_{i}\}\to[k]
the labelling obtained just after the addition of
v_{i}
.
Then for any
j\leqslant i
and
\ell>i
, the direction of the edge
v_{j}\to v_{\ell}
or
v_{j}\leftarrow v_{\ell}
only depends on
v_{\ell}
and the label
\lambda_{i}(v_{j})
.
It follows that the adjacency matrix of
\{v_{1},\dots,v_{i}\}
versus
\{v_{i+1},\dots,v_{n}\}
has at most
k
distinct rows corresponding to the
k
possible labels in
\lambda_{i}
, hence its rank is at most
k
.
This proves that
<
has cut-rank at most
k
.

Conversely, assume that
<
has cut-rank at most
k
.
For each
i\in[n]
, define the partition
\mathcal{P}_{i}
of
\{v_{1},\dots,v_{i}\}
into neighbourhood classes:
two vertices are in the same class when they have exactly the same neighbours in
\{v_{i+1},\dots,v_{n}\}
.
The assumption that
\rk\left(\{v_{1},\dots,v_{i}\};\{v_{i+1},\dots,v_{n}\}\right)\leqslant k
implies that
\mathcal{P}_{i}
contains at most
2^{k}
parts.
For each
i
, choose a labelling map
\lambda_{i}:\{v_{1},\dots,v_{i}\}\to[2^{k}]
such that
\mathcal{P}_{i}=\textstyle\left\{\lambda_{i}^{-1}(c)\ :\ c\in[2^{k}]\right\}
.
From the definition, it is clear that each part of
\mathcal{P}_{i}
is contained within a part of
\mathcal{P}_{i+1}
.
This implies that the restriction of
\lambda_{i+1}
to
\{v_{1},\dots,v_{i}\}
can be written as
\rho_{i}\circ\lambda_{i}
for some
\rho_{i}:[2^{k}]\to[2^{k}]
.

Suppose we have already constructed a linear NLC-decomposition for
T[\{v_{1},\dots,v_{i}\}]
of width
2^{k}+1
with labelling
\lambda_{i}
and insertion ordering
<
.
Note that the colour
2^{k}+1
is unused in
\lambda_{i}
.
For any
j\leqslant i
, the direction of the edge between
v_{j}
and
v_{i+1}

only depends on the part of
\mathcal{P}_{i}
containing
v_{j}
, hence only on the label
\lambda_{i}(v_{j})
.
Thus adding
v_{i+1}
with the desired edges is a valid vertex addition operation.
We assign the last colour
2^{k}+1
to
v_{i+1}
at this step.
Then, to obtain the labelling
\lambda_{i+1}
we apply the relabelling map
\rho_{i}
, extended by mapping
2^{k}+1
to the desired colour for
v_{i+1}
.
Repeating these two steps for each
i
yields a linear NLC decomposition of width
2^{k}+1
, where vertices are added in the order given by
<
,
thus proving that
<
is a linear NLC decomposition ordering of width at most
2^{k}+1
.
∎

The following lemma will be used to bound the cut-rank of the ordering obtained in
Theorem

1.3
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
<
be a linear ordering of the vertices of a tournament
T
and let

X_{1}<\dots<X_{\ell}
be a partition of
V(T)
into intervals of
<
such that, for each
1\leq i\leq\ell
,

(1)

\rk(X_{1}\cup\dots X_{i};X_{i+1}\cup\dots X_{\ell})\leqslant k
, and

(2)

the linear ordering
<
restricted to the sub-tournament
T[X_{i}]
has cut-rank at most
k^{\prime}
.

Then
<
has cut-rank at most
2k+k^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Consider a bi-partition
V(T)=Y\uplus Z
with
Y
and
Z
prefix and suffix respectively of the ordering
<
.
Call
X_{i}
the last interval intersected by
Y
, and define

\displaystyle Y^{\prime}

\displaystyle\coloneqq Y\cap X_{i}

\displaystyle Y^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle\coloneqq X_{1}\cup\dots\cup X_{i-1},\quad\text{and symmetrically}

\displaystyle Z^{\prime}

\displaystyle\coloneqq Z\cap X_{i}

\displaystyle Z^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle\coloneqq X_{i+1}\cup\dots\cup X_{\ell}.

Note that
Y=Y^{\prime}\cup Y^{\prime\prime}
and
Z=Z^{\prime}\cup Z^{\prime\prime}
.
By assumption,
\rk(Y^{\prime\prime}\cup X_{i};Z^{\prime\prime})
and
\rk(Y^{\prime\prime};Z^{\prime\prime}\cup X_{i})
are at most
k
.
Also,
Y^{\prime},Z^{\prime}
is a prefix–suffix bi-partition of
X_{i}
, hence
\rk(Y^{\prime};Z^{\prime})\leqslant k^{\prime}
.
Thus

\displaystyle\rk(Y;Z)

\displaystyle\leqslant\rk(Y^{\prime};Z^{\prime})+\rk(Y;Z^{\prime\prime})+\rk(Y^{\prime\prime};Z)

\displaystyle\leqslant\rk(Y^{\prime};Z^{\prime})+\rk(Y^{\prime\prime}\cup X_{i};Z^{\prime\prime})+\rk(Y^{\prime\prime};Z\cup X_{i})

\displaystyle\leqslant k^{\prime}+2k.\qed

2.2.
First-order logic, interpretations, and transductions

A
relational signature

\Gamma
is a finite set of
relation symbols

R\in\Gamma
each with an
arity

\arity(R)\in\mathbb{N}
.
A
relational structure

S
over the signature
\Gamma
, or
\Gamma
-structure for short,
consists of a
universe
or
vertex set

V(S)
, and for each symbol
R\in\Gamma
with arity
r=\arity(R)
, a valuation of the symbol as a relation
R^{S}\subseteq(V(S))^{r}
.
For instance, directed simple graphs with loops are exactly
\{E\}
-structures, where
E
is a binary symbol (meaning
\arity(E)=2
) representing directed
edges. The transduction in
Theorem

1.3
will take as inputs
\{E\}
-structures.

FO
formulas over the signature
\Gamma
are formulas which can be evaluated in a
\Gamma
-structure.
They are defined inductively as follows:

atomic formulas:

For each symbol
R\in\Gamma
with arity
r
, there is a predicate
R(x_{1},\dots,x_{r})

which checks that the vertices represented by the variables
x_{1},\dots,x_{r}
form an
r
-tuple in the relation
R
.
There is also an equality predicate
x=y
, checking that the variables
x,y
represent the same vertex.

combinators:

FO
formulas
\varphi,\psi
can be combined with the usual boolean operations
\neg\varphi
,
\varphi\wedge\psi
,
\varphi\vee\psi
.

quantifiers:

FO
formulas allow quantification over the vertices of the structure
\exists x,\,\varphi
and
\forall x,\,\varphi
, where
\varphi
is itself an
FO
formula and
x
is a variable.

When a formula
\varphi
has free variables
x_{1},\dots,x_{r}
(i.e. variables appearing without any binding quantifier), we write them as parameters
\varphi(x_{1},\dots,x_{r})
.
Given a
\Gamma
-structure
S
and vertices
v_{1},\dots,v_{r}\in V(S)
, one can define whether
\varphi(x_{1},\dots,x_{r})
is satisfied by
S
when evaluating
x_{i}
as
v_{i}
in the obvious way;
this is denoted as
S\models\varphi(v_{1},\dots,v_{r})
.

Let us now define
FO
transductions
. We follow
[
4
]
for our terminology. An
FO
transduction
from a signature
\Gamma
to a signature
\Delta
is a non-deterministic (one-to-many) map from
\Gamma
-structures to
\Delta
-structures, consisting of any sequence of the following operations.

interpretation:

An interpretation
\Phi
from signature
\Gamma
to
\Delta
is described by a formula
\varphi_{R}(x_{1},\dots,x_{r})
over
\Gamma
for each symbol
R\in\Delta
with arity
r
.
It deterministically maps a
\Gamma
-structure
S
to the
\Delta
-structure
\Phi(S)

with same vertices and with relations

R^{\Phi(S)}=\textstyle\left\{(v_{1},\dots,v_{r})\ :\ S\models\varphi_{R}(v_{1},\dots,v_{r})\right\}.

colouring:

This operation enables non-determinism by extending the signature
\Gamma
with unary relations (i.e. with arity 1).
For unary relation symbols
C_{1},\dots,C_{c}
not present in
\Gamma
,
colouring a
\Gamma
-structure
S
by
C_{1},\dots,C_{c}
outputs the set of all
(\Gamma\cup\{C_{1},\dots,C_{c}\})
-structures
S^{+}

which coincide with
S
on
\Gamma
(meaning
V(S^{+})=V(S)
and
R^{S^{+}}=R^{S}
for all
R\in\Gamma
).

filtering:

The colouring operations outputs all possible colourings, but we may only be interested in some of them.
The filtering operation allows to remove unwanted structures.
For a sentence
\varphi
over
\Gamma
, given a
\Gamma
-structure
S
, the operation of filtering by
\varphi
outputs
S
itself when
S\models\varphi
, and outputs nothing otherwise.

universe restriction:

The restriction operation is described by a single unary formula
\varphi(x)
, and deterministically maps
\Gamma
-structures to
\Gamma
-structures by deleting any vertex
v
which does not satisfy
\varphi(v)
.

k
-copying:

Given a
\Gamma
-structure
S
, this operation outputs the structure consisting of
k
disjoint copies of
S
,
together with a new relation
C
of arity
k
interpreted as the set of tuples
(v_{1},\dots,v_{k})
for each
v\in V(S)
where
v_{i}
denotes the
i
th copy of
v
.

Each of the previous operations
f
has input and output signatures
\Gamma
and
\Delta
,
and maps a
\Gamma
-structure
S
to a set
f(S)
of
\Delta
-structures.
For deterministic operations (interpretations, universe restriction, copying),
f(S)
is a singleton,
for colouring it is a non-empty set, and for filtering it is either
\{S\}
or
\varnothing
.
When
\mathcal{C}
is a set of
\Gamma
-structures, we write
f(\mathcal{C})=\bigcup_{S\in\mathcal{C}}f(S)
.
Thus, these operations map sets of
\Gamma
-structures to sets of
\Delta
-structures, allowing to compose them when the signatures match.

It is well known that any
FO
transduction can be equivalently expressed using only one instance of each operation, in the following order:
copying, colouring, filtering, interpretation, and universe restriction.
This follows from the Backwards Translation Theorem
[
6
]
, which roughly says that, for any interpretation
\Phi
from
\Gamma
to
\Delta
and formula
\psi
over
\Delta
,
there is a formula
\psi\circ\Phi
over
\Gamma
such that
S\models\psi\circ\Phi
if and only if
\Phi(S)\models\psi
,
obtained from
\psi
by replacing a predicate
R\in\Delta
by the formula
\varphi_{R}
defining it in
\Phi
.

An
FO
transduction is said to be
non-copying
if it does not use the copying operation, and is called
deterministic
if it does not use colouring.
Given signatures
\Gamma\subseteq\Delta
, a
\Gamma
-structure
S
and a
\Delta
-structure
T
,
we say that
S
is a
reduct
of
T
, and
T
an
extension
of
S
, if
V(S)=V(T)
, and
R^{S}=R^{T}
for all symbols
R\in\Gamma
.
That is,
T
is exactly the structure
S
with some additional relations added for the symbols in
\Delta\setminus\Gamma
.
A transduction
\Phi:\Gamma\to\Delta
is an
extension transduction

if for any
\Gamma
-structure
S
, any
T\in\Phi(S)
is an extension of
S
.

Let us finally recall the Parallel Application Lemma, which allows to apply in parallel a same transduction to disjoint substructures of a given structure.
Let
\Gamma
be a signature and
S_{1},\dots,S_{n}
be disjoint
\Gamma
-structures.
Define the
disjoint union
of
S_{1},\dots,S_{n}
, denoted by
\bigsqcup_{i=1}^{n}S_{i}
,
as the following structure over the vocabulary
\Gamma\cup\{\thicksim\}

where
\thicksim
is a new binary relation symbol:

•

the universe of
\bigsqcup_{i=1}^{n}S_{i}

is the disjoint union of the universes of the
S_{i}
s;

•

for each relation symbol from
\Gamma
,
its interpretation in
\bigsqcup_{i=1}^{n}S_{i}

is the union of the interpretations in each of the
S_{i}
s;

•

the interpretation of
\thicksim
in
\bigsqcup_{i=1}^{n}S_{i}

is the set of pairs of elements that originate from the same
S_{i}
.

Lemma 2.5

(Parallel Application Lemma
[
3
]
)
.

For any
FO
-transduction
\tau:\Sigma\to\Gamma
, there is a second
FO
-transduction
\hat{\tau}:(\Sigma\cup\{\thicksim\})\to(\Gamma\cup\{\thicksim\})
such that
for any input sequence
{I}_{1},\dots,{I}_{n}
of
\Sigma
-structures and
\mathcal{I}\coloneqq\bigsqcup_{i=1}^{n}I_{i}
,
the outputs
\mathcal{O}\in\hat{\tau}(\mathcal{I})
are exactly the structures of the form

\mathcal{O}=\bigsqcup_{i=1}^{n}{O}_{i}
with
O_{i}\in\tau(I_{i})
for all
i\in[n]
.

2.3.
Example: transducing cut-width orderings

To illustrate the previous notions and introduce some conventions, let us prove a very simple case of
Theorem

1.3
.
For a tournament
T
and an ordering
v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{n}
of the vertices, a
cut
of this ordering is a partition into two intervals
\{v_{1},\dots,v_{i}\}
and
\{v_{i+1},\dots,v_{n}\}
.
The
width
of this cut is the number of edges oriented backwards
u\leftarrow v
where
u\in\{v_{1},\dots,v_{i}\}
and
v\in\{v_{i+1},\dots,v_{n}\}
.
The
cut-width
of this ordering is the maximum width of its cuts. The
cut-width
of
T
is the minimum cut-width over all linear orderings of its vertices.

For any
k
, we want a transduction
\Phi
which, given a tournament
T
, produces orderings of cut-width
k
, if any.
The signature of
\Phi
should be
\{E\}\to\{E,<\}
(the symbols
E,<
being both binary):
the transduction is given a tournament
(V,E)
, and outputs an
ordered tournament

(V,E,<)
, where
<
should be the cut-width ordering.
Since we expect the vertex and edge sets to remain the same in the output, we also ask
\Phi
to be an
extension transduction
.

Proposition 2.6
.

For any
k
, one can compute an extension
FO
-transduction
\Phi:\{E\}\to\{E,<\}
such that for any tournament
T=(V,E)
,

\Phi(T)=\{(V,E,<):\text{$<$ is a cut-width\penalty\ $k$ ordering of\penalty\ $T$}\}.

Proof.

The transduction
\Phi
is the composition of two steps:
first an extension transduction
\tau:\{E\}\to\{E,<\}
which produces
at least
the desired set of ordered tournaments,
i.e.
(V,E,<)\in\tau(V,E)
for any cut-width
k
ordering
<
,
followed by a filtering transduction to remove any undesired structure.

Let us start with the filtering step.
Over the signature
\{E,<\}
, with
E
supposedly being the edge relation of a tournament and
<
a linear ordering, there are simple
FO
formulas (depending on
k
) expressing the following conditions:

•

the relation
<
is interpreted as a linear ordering, and

•

for each vertex
v
, there are at most
k
distinct edges
x_{i}\leftarrow y_{i}
in
E
with
x_{i}\leqslant v<y_{i}
,
i.e. the cut
\{w:w\leqslant v\},\{w:w>v\}
has width at most
k
.

The filtering transduction simply checks that both are satisfied.

Let us now turn our attention to the extension transduction that will output all linear orderings of cut-width at most
k
.
Let
T
be a tournament and
v_{1}<\dots<v_{n}
a linear ordering of cut-width at most
k
of
T
.
First, if there are
2k+1
vertices
z_{1},\dots,z_{2k+1}
, each satisfying
x\to z_{i}\to y
, then
x<y
must hold.
Indeed, at most
k
out-neighbours of
x
can be before
x
in
<
,
and symmetrically at most
k
in-neighbours of
y
are before
y
, leaving at least one vertex satisfying
x<z_{i}<y
.
This criteria is expressed in
FO
as

\varphi(x,y)\coloneqq\exists z_{1},z_{2},\dots,z_{2k+1},\ \bigwedge_{i=1}^{2k+1}E(x,z_{i})\wedge E(z_{i},y).

We cannot expect
\varphi(x,y)
by itself to describe a total ordering of
T
, but it is not too far from doing so:
\varphi(v_{i},v_{j})
holds whenever
j-i>4k+1
.
Indeed, of the
4k+1
vertices
v_{i+1},\dots,v_{j-1}
, at most
k
can be in-neighbours of
v_{i}
, and at most
k
out-neighbours of
v_{j}
.
This leaves
2k+1
of them satisfying
v_{i}\to v_{\ell}\to v_{j}
, hence
\varphi(v_{i},v_{j})
holds.

The previous two facts imply that two cut-width
k
orderings of the same tournament cannot be very different: they agree on all but
(4k+1)n
of the pairs of vertices.
There remains some choice in the ordering of the remaining pairs, which is why we need non-determinism.

We now begin the construction of our transduction itself.
The first step is to non-deterministically colour
T
with
8k+5
colours.
We assume that this colouring assigns to
v_{i}
the colour
i
modulo
8k+5
, and show that this specific choice gives
<
as output.
For this specific colouring, if vertices
v_{i},v_{j}
are at distance at most
4k+2
, one can immediately tell whether
v_{i}<v_{j}
or
v_{j}<v_{i}
simply by looking at their colours. This can be encoded by a simple, but tedious
FO
formula
\gamma(x,y)
.
We then apply the following interpretation to obtain
<
: given vertices
u,v
,

(1)

if
\varphi(u,v)
holds, then
u<v
and symmetrically if
\varphi(v,u)
holds, then
u>v
, and

(2)

otherwise,
u,v
must be at distance at most
4k+2
(in the successor relation of
<
), and we test whether
u<v
by using their colours.

This description of
<
is encoded by the following formula:

\psi_{<}(x,y)=\varphi(x,y)\lor(\lnot\varphi(y,x)\land\gamma(x,y)).

Since we wish to preserve the edge relation
E
(in order to check that each linear ordering has cut-width at most
k
), we add a second ‘identity’ formula
\psi_{E}(x,y)=E(x,y)
.
The formulas
\psi_{E},\psi_{<}
define an interpretation from signature
\{E,C_{1},\dots,C_{8k+5}\}
to
\{E,<\}
.

In the end, the transduction consists of first colouring with
C_{1},\dots,C_{8k+5}
, then interpreting by
(\psi_{E},\psi_{<})
, and finally checking (with the filtering operation) that
<
indeed has cut-width at most
k
.
∎

3.
Linear decompositions and semigroup theory

This section presents linear decompositions as constructed from pieces called
bags
,
which can be composed, giving them a monoid structure.
In this context, we will describe a linear decomposition as a sequence of small bags.
We then introduce Simon’s Factorisation Forest Theorem,
which will be used to obtain factorisations of such sequences, subject to some regularity conditions.

While all the notions presented here can be defined for directed graphs of small NLC-width, we restrict the definitions
to tournaments to simplify notions and notations. We refer to
[
3
]
for a similar monoid for undirected graphs of small clique-width, which trivially extends to directed graphs.

3.2.
Types

We next define some finite abstraction of bags, which we call
type of a bag
.
The first step is to define types for
vertices
inside a bag.

Consider a bag
B
and an internal vertex
v\in V(B)
.
The type of
v
describes its interactions with bags which may be multiplied to the left or right of
B
.
Specifically,
\type_{B}(v)
consists of the following information:

(1)

the colour
\lambda_{B}(v)
, and

(2)

the direction of edges
i\to v
or
i\leftarrow v
for each input vertex
i\in[k]
.

We use
\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\dots
to denote vertex types.
When the order
k
of the bag is fixed, there are only
k\cdot 2^{k}
distinct vertex types.

Remark that the type of a given vertex
v
may change when multiplying bags:
given bags
A,B,C
and
v\in V(B)
, in general
\type_{B}(v)
may differ from
\type_{ABC}(v)
.
However,
\type_{ABC}(v)
only depends on
\type_{B}(v)
and the recolouring functions
\rho_{A},\rho_{C}
:

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
k
be a positive integer. For every two recolouring functions
g:[k]\to[k]
and
h:[k]\to[k]
, there is a function

f_{g,h}
from vertex types to vertex types such that, for any three bags
A,B,C
of order
k
and any
v\in V(B)
,

\type_{ABC}(v)=f_{\rho_{A},\rho_{C}}(\type_{B}(v)).

Proof.

Applying the definition of product of bags, we have the following:
The colour of
v
in the product
ABC
is
\lambda_{ABC}(v)=\rho_{C}(\lambda_{B}(v))
.
For an input vertex
i\in[K]
, there is an edge
i\to v
in
E^{\partial}(ABC)
if and only if
\rho_{A}(i)\to v
is an edge in
E^{\partial}(B)
.
The above defines
\type_{ABC}(v)
, and only depends on
\type_{B}(v)
,
\rho_{A}
, and
\rho_{C}
.
∎

In particular, this means that if vertices
u,v\in V(B)
have the same type
\type_{B}(u)=\type_{B}(v)
in
B
, then they also have the same type
\type_{ABC}(u)=\type_{ABC}(v)
in
ABC
.

Next, we define the type of a bag.
If
B
is a bag and
\alpha
is a vertex type, we denote by
\alpha(B)\coloneqq\textstyle\left\{v\in V(B)\ :\ \type_{B}(v)=\alpha\right\}
the set of vertices with type
\alpha
in
B
.
The type
\alpha
is
inhabited
in
B
if
\alpha(B)
is non-empty.
The type of
B
, denoted by
\type(B)
, now consists of the following information:

(1)

the recolouring function
\rho_{B}:[k]\to[k]
,

(2)

the set of inhabited vertex types in
B
, and

(3)

for each pair of inhabited types
\alpha,\beta
,
whether
\alpha(B)
and
\beta(B)
are homogeneous or not in the internal tournament of
B
,
and when they are, the direction of edges
\alpha(B)\to\beta(B)
or
\alpha(B)\leftarrow\beta(B)
.

We use
\sigma,\tau,\dots
to denote types of bags.
For fixed order
k
, the number of bag types is some constant
2^{2^{O(k)}}
.

A routine check using crucially
Lemma

3.2
shows that for any bags

of order
k
,

\type(B\cdot B^{\prime})
only depends on
\type(B)
and
\type(B^{\prime})
.
This implicitly defines a monoid structure on the set of bag types,
which is a quotient of the monoid of bags.
Equivalently, the map
\type
from bags to types is a monoid homomorphism. We denote by
M_{k}
this monoid of types of bags of order
k
. We summarise all these
properties in the following.

Lemma 3.3
.

For any
k
, the set
M_{k}
of types has size bounded by
2^{2^{O(k)}}
,
and there is an associative operation
\tilde{\cdot}
such that

\type(B_{1}\cdot B_{2})=\type(B_{1})\tilde{\cdot}\type(B_{2}).

Proof.

The number of recolouring functions is
k!
.
There are
k\cdot 2^{k}
possible vertex types, hence
2^{k\cdot 2^{k}}
possibilities for which vertex types are inhabited,
and
3^{(k\cdot 2^{k})^{2}}
choices for the information on homogeneity and edge directions between vertex types.
Thus, the size of
M_{k}
is bounded by
2^{2^{O(k)}}
.

Let us now prove the existence of the product
\tilde{\cdot}
for
M_{k}
.
That is, we need to show that
\type(B_{1}\cdot B_{2})
depends only on
\type(B_{1})
and
\type(B_{2})
.
First, the recolouring function of
B_{1}\cdot B_{2}
is by definition the composition
\rho_{B_{2}}\circ\rho_{B_{1}}
.
Next, by

3.2
, there is a map
f_{1}
on vertex types, depending only on
\rho_{B_{2}}
, such that
\type_{B_{1}B_{2}}(v)=f_{1}(\type_{B_{1}}(v))
for all internal vertices

of
B_{1}
;
and a similar map

for
B_{2}
depending only on
\rho_{B_{1}}
.
Then a vertex type
\alpha
is inhabited in

if and only if
some type in

or
f_{2}^{-1}(\alpha)
is inhabited in
B_{1}
or
B_{2}
respectively.

Finally, consider two vertex types
\alpha,\beta
, for which we want to test homogeneity in
B_{1}\cdot B_{2}
.
For
\alpha_{1},\beta_{1}
inhabited in
B_{1}
with

,
we know from

\alpha_{1}(B_{1})
and
\beta_{1}(B_{1})
.
The same holds for types in
B_{2}
mapping to

through

.
Next, if
\alpha_{1},\beta_{2}
are inhabited in

and

respectively, and
f_{1}(\alpha_{1})=\alpha
,

,
then the direction of edges between
\alpha_{1}(B_{1})
and
\beta_{2}(B_{2})
(which are always homogeneous) is entirely determined by
\alpha_{1}
and
\beta_{2}
themselves.
The same holds when swapping the roles of
\alpha
and
\beta
.
To test whether
\alpha
and
\beta
are homogeneous, it suffices to consider all pairs of inhabited vertex types from combinations of
B_{1},B_{2}
as above, and check that the edge direction is either always from
\alpha
to
\beta
, or vice versa.

We have then shown that one can compute, in a deterministic way, the type of
B_{1}\cdot B_{2}

from the types of
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
, i.e. there is an operation
\tilde{\cdot}
such that
\type(B_{1}\cdot B_{2})=\type(B_{1})\tilde{\cdot}\type(B_{2})
.
Because
\cdot
is associative,
\tilde{\cdot}
must also be associative.
∎

3.3.
Simon’s Factorisation Forest Theorem

Given a linear decomposition
B=B_{1}B_{2}\dots B_{n}
,
Simon’s theorem gives a
factorisation forest
,
which is a more structured way to construct
B
from
B_{1},\dots,B_{n}
,
subject to some restrictions relative to a finite semigroup
S
of our choice—in our case the monoid of types.

The general setting of Simon’s theorem is the following.
Consider a (usually infinite) semigroup
\Sigma
and a generating set
A\subset\Sigma
,
a finite semigroup
S
, and a homomorphism
\varphi:\Sigma\to S
.
In our case,
\Sigma
are the bags,
A
the atomic bags,
S
the types, and
\varphi=\type
.
Given some
w\in\Sigma
, Simon’s theorem is interested in factorising
w
in one of two ways:

binary factorisation:

w=w_{1}\cdot w_{2}
for
w_{1},w_{2}\in\Sigma
with no further restriction.

idempotent factorisation:

w=w_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot w_{n}
, where the number
n
of factors is unrestricted,
but the factors must all map to the same
e=\varphi(w_{i})
in
S
,
which furthermore must be idempotent, meaning
e\cdot e=e
.

Simon’s theorem states that any
w\in\Sigma
can be reduced down to generators in
A

by applying the previous two operations nested only up to depth bounded by a linear function of
|{S}|
.
This process is described as a
factorisation forest
for
w
, see
Fig.

4
for an illustration.

Let us introduce some terminology allowing to count binary and idempotent factorisations separately.
A factorisation forest has depth
(p,q)
if the nesting depth of idempotent operations is at most
p
,
and that of binary operations is at most
q
. Formally:

•

Generators
a\in A
have a factorisation forest of depth
(0,0)
.

•

If
w_{1},w_{2}
have factorisation forests of depth
(p_{1},q_{1})
and
(p_{2},q_{2})
respectively,
then
w_{1}\cdot w_{2}
has a factorisation forest of depth

(\max\{p_{1},p_{2}\},\max\{q_{1},q_{2}\}+1).

•

If
w_{1},\dots,w_{n}\in\Sigma
satisfy
\varphi(w_{i})=e
for some idempotent
e\in S
and all
1\leq i\leq n
,
and there are
p,q\in\mathbb{N}
such that each
w_{i}
has a factorisation forest of depth
(p_{i},q_{i})
,
then
w_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot w_{n}
has a factorisation forest of depth
(\max\{p_{1},\dots,p_{n}\}+1,\max\{q_{1},\dots,q_{n}\})
.

Since this definition depends on the choice of semigroup
S
and morphism
\varphi
,
we will call it a
factorisation forest over
\varphi
to disambiguate.

Simon’s theorem can now be precisely stated as follows.

Theorem 3.4

(Simon’s Factorisation Forest Theorem,
[

,

]
)
.

Consider a semigroup
\Sigma
generated by
A\subset\Sigma
, and a homomorphism
\varphi:\Sigma\to S
to a finite semigroup.
Then all
w\in\Sigma
have factorisation forests over
\varphi
of depth at most
(|{S}|,2|{S}|)
.

The original proof by Simon
[
13
]
shows that there are factorisation forests of total depth
9|{S}|
.
Kufleitner
[

]
improved this bound to
3|{S}|
,
and the proof in fact uses only
|{S}|
idempotent factorisations and
2|{S}|

In the case of bags, we will apply
Theorem

3.4
to the monoid
\mathfrak{C}_{K}
with atomic bags as generators,
and the homomorphism
\type:\mathfrak{C}_{K}\to M_{K}
.
Since we will only consider factorisation forests over the

we will omit the ‘over

3.4.
Technical lemma

The proof of our main result proceeds by induction over the depth of a given factorisation forest.
At some point during this induction, we may need to restrict the bag considered to a subset of vertices.
In general, this does not preserve the factorisation depth. Indeed, deleting vertices in a bag may change its type,
and what was an idempotent factorisation may thus stop being one.
This section describes a sufficient condition to control the depth of factorisation forests when deleting vertices.

Given a bag
B
and a subset
X\subseteq V(G)
of internal vertices,
we denote by
B[X]
the bag obtained by deleting any internal vertex of
B
outside
X
, and restricting the edges and colouring map of
B
to
X
.
If
\Gamma
is a set of vertex types, then we denote by

\Gamma(B)=\bigcup_{\alpha\in\Gamma}\alpha(B)
the set of vertices with type in
\Gamma
,
and we say that
\Gamma(B)
is a
union of (vertex) types
.
When deleting vertices from bags, we will only delete unions of types,
which is sufficient to control the factorisation depth.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
k
be a positive integer and let
B
be a bag in
\mathfrak{C}_{k}
.
If
B
has a factorisation forest of depth
(p,q)
and
X\subset V(B)
is some union of types,
then
B[X]
has a factorisation forest of depth
(p,q+2p)
.

Before proving
Lemma

3.5
, let us make two very simple observations.
The first follows directly from the definition of types.

Remark 3.6
.

If
\Gamma
is a set of vertex types, and
B_{1},B_{2}
are two bags with the same type,
then
B_{1}[\Gamma(B_{1})]
has the same type as
B_{2}[\Gamma(B_{2})]
.

Secondly, recall that the type of a vertex
x
changes depending on the bag containing
x
considered:
thus in general
\Gamma(B)\neq\Gamma(ABC)\cap V(B)
.
Nonetheless,
Lemma

3.2
shows that for bags
A,B,C
and
x\in V(B)
,

\type_{ABC}(x)
only depends on
\type_{B}(x)
,
\type(A)
, and
\type(C)
.
Thus,

Remark 3.7
.

For any bags
A,B,C
and set of vertex types
\Gamma
, there exist
\Gamma^{\prime}
such that

\Gamma(ABC)\cap V(B)=\Gamma^{\prime}(B)
.
Furthermore,
\Gamma^{\prime}
only depends on
\Gamma
,
\type(A)
, and
\type(C)
.

Proof of
Lemma

3.5
.

We prove the result by induction on the factorisation forest of
B
with depth
(p,q)
.
When
B
is atomic, the result is trivial.
In the binary case, assume that
B=B_{1}\cdot B_{2}
where
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
have each a factorisation forest of depth at most
(p,q-1)
.
By
Remark

3.7
,
X_{i}\coloneqq X\cap V(B_{i})
is also a union of types in
B_{i}
for
i\in\{1,2\}
.
Hence, by induction we find factorisation forests of depth
(p,q+2p-1)
for
B_{1}[X_{1}]
and
B_{2}[X_{2}]
,
which combine into one of depth
(p,q+2p)
for
B[X]
.

Consider now the idempotent case
B=B_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot B_{n}
,
where all bags
B_{i}
for
i\in[n]
have idempotent type
\tau
,
and have factorisation forests of depth at most
(p-1,q)
.
Call
\Gamma
the set of vertex types defining
X
, i.e.
X=\Gamma(B)
,
and for each
i\in[n]
, define
X_{i}\coloneqq X\cap V(B_{i})
.

By
Remark

3.7
, there is a set of vertex types
\Gamma^{\prime}
depending only on
\Gamma
and
\tau
,
such that for all bags
A_{1},A_{2},A_{3}
of type
\tau
we have

(1)

\Gamma(A_{1}A_{2}A_{3})\cap V(A_{2})=\Gamma^{\prime}(A_{2}).

Then, by
Remark

3.6
, there is a bag type
\tau^{\prime}
, depending again only on
\Gamma
and
\tau
,
such that the bag
A_{2}[\Gamma^{\prime}(A_{2})]
has type
\tau^{\prime}
.

Claim 3.8
.

For
2\leqslant i\leqslant n-1
, the restriction
B_{i}[X_{i}]
has type
\tau^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Immediate by choice of
\tau^{\prime}
when applying the previous remarks with
A_{1}=B_{1}\dots B_{i-1}
,
A_{2}=B_{i}
, and
A_{3}=B_{i+1}\dots B_{n}
.

\blacksquare

Claim 3.9
.

The type
\tau^{\prime}
is idempotent.

Proof.

Consider this time four arbitrary bags
A_{1},\dots,A_{4}
, each of type
\tau
.
Denote
Y=\Gamma(A_{1}A_{2}A_{3}A_{4})
, and for each
i\in\{1,2,3,4\}
, let
Y_{i}\coloneqq Y\cap V(A_{i})
.

Grouping them as
A_{1}A_{2}(A_{3}A_{4})
and applying (
1
), we have

Y_{2}=\Gamma(A_{1}A_{2}A_{3}A_{4})\cap V(A_{2})=\Gamma^{\prime}(A_{2}).

By instead grouping the four bags as
(A_{1}A_{2})A_{3}A_{4}
, or
A_{1}(A_{2}A_{3})A_{4}
,
we similarly find that
Y_{3}=\Gamma^{\prime}(A_{3})
and
Y_{2}\cup Y_{3}=\Gamma^{\prime}(A_{2}A_{3})
.
Then, by choice of
\tau^{\prime}
, the bags
A_{2}[Y_{2}]
,
A_{3}[Y_{3}]
, and
(A_{2}A_{3})[Y_{2}\cup Y_{3}]
all have type
\tau^{\prime}
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\tau^{\prime}\tilde{\cdot}\tau^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\type(A_{2}[Y_{2}])\tilde{\cdot}\type(A_{3}[Y_{3}])

\displaystyle=\type((A_{2}A_{3})[Y_{2}\cup Y_{3}]))=\tau^{\prime}.

Thus
\tau^{\prime}
is idempotent.

\blacksquare

To conclude, we apply the induction hypothesis to each
B_{i}[X_{i}]
for
i\in[n]
, yielding factorisation forests of depth
(q-1,p+2q-2)
.
By
3.8
and
3.9
, each term in the product
B_{2}[X_{2}]\cdot\ldots\cdot B_{n-1}[X_{n-1}]
has type
\tau^{\prime}
, which is idempotent.
This is thus an idempotent factorisation of depth
(q,p+2q-2)
.
With two additional binary factorisations to add
B_{1}[X_{1}]
and
B_{n}[X_{n}]
,
we obtain a factorisation forest of depth
(q,p+2q)
for
B_{1}[X_{1}]\cdot\ldots\cdot B_{n}[X_{n}]=(B_{1}\dots B_{n})[X]
.
∎

4.
Transducing an ordering

In this section, we show that orderings of bounded cut-rank can be
FO
transduced.
Throughout the section, we work with (non-deterministic) extension transductions
\Phi
with signature
\{E\}\to\{E,<\}
, where
E,<
are binary relational symbols.
Explicitly, such a transduction takes as input a (directed) graph
G=(V,E)
, and non-deterministically outputs structures
(V,E,<)
,
i.e. the same graph
(V,E)
plus some binary relation
<
expected to be a linear ordering.

Theorem 4.1
.

For any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, one can compute an extension
FO
transduction
\Phi_{k}
with signature
\{E\}\to\{E,<\}
,
and
k^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

(1)

for any graph
G=(V,E)
, every structure in
\Phi_{k}(G)
is
(V,E,<)
for some linear ordering
<
of
G
with cut-rank at most
k^{\prime}
, and

(2)

if
T
is a tournament with linear NLC-width at most
k
, then
\Phi_{k}(T)
contains at least one such structure.

The proof will follow the same line as in
[
4
,
3
]
and will be by induction on the height of the Simon’s
Factorisation Forest of the words generating bags in
\mathfrak{C}_{k}
.
We first show in
Section

4.1
that the first condition of
Theorem

4.1
is easy to ensure.
The main induction is explained in
Section

4.2
and then we proceed to the proof of the main technical lemma in
Section

4.3
.

4.1.
Checking the cut-rank of an ordering

We first handle the first condition of
Theorem

4.1
:
ensuring that the output of
\Phi_{k}
only contains orderings of cut-rank at most
k^{\prime}
.

Consider a bi-partition
V(G)=X\uplus Y
of the vertices of a graph
G
.
For
x\in X
, denote by
N_{Y}(x)=\{y\in Y:(x,y)\in E(G)\}
its neighbourhood in
Y
.
By definition,
\rk(X;Y)\leqslant k
if and only if there are at most
k
vertices
x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\in X
such that
for any
x^{\prime}\in X
, there is some
B\subseteq[k]
satisfying
N_{Y}(x^{\prime})=\bigoplus_{b\in B}N_{Y}(b)
,
where
\oplus
denotes sum modulo 2 (or symmetric difference) of sets.
If
X
is given as a unary predicate, it is routine using this characterisation to write an
FO
formula with
k+1
quantifiers asserting that
\rk(X;Y)\leqslant k
.

Consider now an ordering
x_{1}<\dots<x_{n}
of
V(G)
.
Replacing the condition
x\in X
by
x\leqslant x_{i}
in the previous argument
yields a formula
\varphi(x_{i})
checking that
\rk(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{i}\};\{x_{i+1},\dots,x_{n}\})\leqslant k
.
Then, the formula
\forall x_{i}.\ \varphi(x_{i})
checks that the ordering
<
has cut-rank at most
k
.
Given any extension transduction
\Phi
with signature
\{E\}\to\{E,<\}
,
one can add to
\Phi
a filtering step which checks that (1) the relation
<
is indeed a linear ordering,
and (2) this ordering has cut-rank at most
k^{\prime}
, using the previously described formula.
This ensures that
\Phi
satisfies the first condition of
Theorem

4.1
,
and if
\Phi(T)
did contain
(V,E,<)
for some ordering
<
of cut-rank at most
k^{\prime}
, then it will still be there after filtering.

Henceforth, we only focus on the second condition of
Theorem

4.1
:
building an extension transduction
\Phi_{k}
such that on any
T
of linear NLC-width
k
, the output
\Phi_{k}(T)
contains at least one ordering of cut-rank at most
k^{\prime}
.

4.2.
Main induction

By
Lemma

3.1
, any tournament
T
with linear NLC-width
k
is also the internal tournament of some bag
B
in
\mathfrak{C}_{k}
.
Furthermore, by
Theorem

3.4
, this bag
B
has factorisation depth at most
(|M_{k}|,2|M_{k}|)
,
where
M_{k}
is the monoid of types of arity
k
, whose size is a function of
k
only.
Thus,
Theorem

4.1
follows from the next statement and the arguments of
Section

4.1
.

Lemma 4.2
.

For any
k,p,q\in\mathbb{N}
, one can compute
f(k,p,q)\in\mathbb{N}
and
an extension
FO
transduction
\Phi_{(p,q)}^{k}:\{E\}\to\{E,<\}
such that
if
T=(V,E)
is the internal tournament of a bag
B\in\mathfrak{C}_{k}
of factorisation depth at most
(p,q)
,
then there is some ordering
<
of
T
with cut-rank at most
f(p,q,k)
such that
(V,E,<)\in\Phi_{(p,q)}^{k}(T)
.

We once again insist that the output
\Phi_{(p,q)}^{k}(T)
needs to contain some linear ordering of
T
with the required cut-rank,
but may also contain other structures where
<
is interpreted arbitrarily (possibly not even as a linear ordering).
Thus we will show that if non-deterministic steps follow the ‘right’ choices,
then the transduction produces the desired ordering, while ignoring anything resulting from a ‘wrong’ choice.
We phrase this as the non-deterministic step
guessing
the desired colouring.

Let us begin the proof of
Lemma

4.2
, by induction on the pair
(p,q)
ordered lexicographically.
Choose the bound
f(k,p,q)
to satisfy:

\displaystyle f(k,0,0)

\displaystyle=k,

\displaystyle f(k,p,q)

\displaystyle\geqslant f(k,p,q-1)+k

if
q>0
, and

\displaystyle f(k,p,q)

\displaystyle\geqslant f(k,p-1,2p+q)+2k\cdot(2^{k}+1)

if
p>0
.

Consider a bag
B\in\mathfrak{C}_{K}
whose internal tournament is
T
, and with factorisation depth
(p,q)
.

In the base case
p=q=0
, the bag
B
is atomic, and the result is trivial.
Assume now that
p>0
or
q>0
, so that the Simon’s factorisation of
B

has either a binary or an idempotent operation at the root.
The transduction
\Phi_{(p,q)}^{k}
starts by guessing which of these two cases occurs (this can be simulated using a colouring step),
and will continue with a different transduction for each.

Binary case

Assume
B=B_{1}\cdot B_{2}
where
B_{1},B_{2}
have factorisations of depth at most
(p,q-1)
.
We use a non-deterministic colouring to guess the bipartition
V(B_{1}),V(B_{2})
.
Then, one can apply to
T[V(B_{i})]
the transduction
\Phi_{(p,q-1)}^{k}
obtained by induction, by restricting each quantifier to
V(B_{i})
.
Thus, we obtain linear orderings
<_{1},<_{2}
of cut-rank at most
f(k,p,q-1)
for
T[V(B_{1})]
and
T[V(B_{2})]
respectively.

Define
<
on
T
by
V(B_{1})<V(B_{2})
, where
<
coincides with
<_{i}
inside
V(B_{i})
.
One can transduce
<
from
<_{1},<_{2}
and the bipartition
V(B_{1}),V(B_{2})
.
Since we are dealing with bags of arity
k
, we have
\rk(V(B_{1});V(B_{2}))\leqslant k
.
Since
<_{i}
has cut-rank at most
f(k,p,q-1)
in
T[V(B_{i})]
for
i\in\{1,2\}
,
this implies that
<
has cut-rank at most
f(k,p,q-1)+k\leq f(k,p,q)
.

Idempotent case

Assume now we are given an idempotent factorisation,
that is
B=B_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot B_{n}
where
\type(B_{i})=\tau
for all
1\leq i\leq n
, with
\tau
idempotent,
and each
B_{i}
has factorisation depth at most
(p-1,q)
.

Consider the quasi-ordering
\preceq
of
V(B)
defined by
V(B_{1})\preceq\dots\preceq V(B_{n})
,
and call
\sim
the equivalence relation whose classes are
V(B_{1}),\dots,V(B_{n})
.
Suppose in a first time that we are given
\preceq
(transducing it will be the core of this proof).
Using parallel application (
Lemma

2.5
), this allows to apply
\Phi_{(p-1,q)}^{k}
to each
B_{i}
simultaneously,
yielding a relation
<
which inside each
B_{i}
is interpreted as a linear ordering of cut-rank at most
f(k,p-1,q)
.

Define
<^{\prime}
to be the linear ordering which coincides with
<
inside each bag
B_{i}
, and with
\preceq
between the bags.
Clearly
<^{\prime}
can be transduced from
<
and
\preceq
, and
Lemma

2.4
gives a bound on the cut-rank of
<^{\prime}
.

We are thus only left with the core problem of transducing the quasi-ordering
\preceq
.
Unfortunately, it is not always possible to transduce exactly
\preceq
.
For instance
B
might have two strongly connected components
X_{1},X_{2}
, each intersecting all
B_{j}
.
Then, we might be able to transduce
\preceq
inside each
X_{i}
,
but it will be impossible to transduce
\preceq
between
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
.
In this case, we need to reorganise the decomposition of
B

by first separating
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
, and then decomposing each of them.
The precise statement we prove is thus the following.

Lemma 4.3
.

Given
k
, one can compute an extension
FO

\Psi_{k}
with signature
\{E\}\to\{E,\preceq\}
satisfying the following.
If
T=(V,E)
is the internal tournament of a bag
B\in\mathfrak{C}_{k}
with factorisation depth at most
(p,q)
,
then there is a quasi-ordering
\preceq
of

with equivalence classes
X_{1}\preceq\dots\preceq X_{m}

such that

(1)

for each
i\in[m]

is the internal tournament of a bag in
\mathfrak{C}_{k}
having factorisation depth at most
(p-1,q+2p)
,

(2)

for each

\rk(X_{1}\cup\dots\cup X_{i};\ X_{i+1}\cup\dots\cup X_{m})\leqslant k\cdot(2^{k}+1),

(3)

and
(V,E,\preceq)\in\Psi_{(p,q)}^{k}(T)
.

Assuming
Lemma

4.3
, we conclude as follows.
From the quasi-ordering
\preceq
, it is simple to transduce the equivalence relation
\sim

.
Recall that we do induction on

ordered lexicographically,
hence we can assume the transduction

is already defined.
Using parallel application (
Lemma

2.5
), this transduction can be applied to each

in parallel.
This yields a relation

which inside each

is a linear ordering with cut-rank at most

.
Define
<^{\prime}
to coincide with
<
inside each
X_{i}
and with
\preceq
between the different
X_{i}
,
which is easily transduced from
<,\preceq
.
Using
Lemma

2.4
, and condition
2
of
Lemma

4.3
,
we obtain that
<^{\prime}
has cut-rank at most

f(k,p-1,q+2p)+2k\cdot(2^{k}+1)\leqslant f(k,p,q).

This concludes the idempotent case, and thus the proof of
Lemma

4.2
.

4.3.
Proof of Lemma
4.3

For this entire section, we fix some
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
and consider a factorisation
B=B_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot B_{n}
,
where all
B_{i}
have the same idempotent type
\tau
, and have factorisation depth at most
(p-1,q)
.
We also call
T=(V,E)
the internal tournament of
B
.

Recall that
Lemma

4.3
asks for a transduction depending only on
k
.
As the very first step of this transduction, one may use non-deterministic colouring to guess the type
\tau

(for which there are only finitely many possibilities for a fixed
k
, by
Lemma

3.3
).
We may thus instead allow the transductions constructed in this section to depend on both
k
and
\tau

(but not
B
, the factorisation, or
p,q
).

For a vertex
x\in V(B_{i})
, we call
\idx(x)=i
the
index
of
x
.
Our goal is to answer (with an
FO
transduction) the question: given
x,y
, is
\idx(x)\leqslant\idx(y)
?
To be formal, for a subset
X
of
V(B)
, we say that a transduction
\Phi

orders
X
according to
\preceq

if
\Phi
is an extension
FO
transduction, and there is some
(V,E,\preceq^{\prime})\in\Phi(T)
such that
\preceq
and
\preceq^{\prime}
coincide on
X
.

Using the non-determinism of transductions, one may show that it suffices to consider vertices whose indices differ by at least some constant.
That is, say that
\Phi

approximately
orders
X
according to
\preceq

if once again
\Phi
is an extension
FO
transduction, and there is
(V,E,\preceq^{\prime})\in\Phi(T)
such that
\preceq,\preceq^{\prime}

coincide for any pair
x,y\in X
that satisfies
|\idx(x)-\idx(y)|\geqslant 2
.
When
X
hits all bags of the factorisation, if we can approximately order
X
, then we can also order it:

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
X
be a subset of vertices containing at least one vertex for each possible index.
For any
FO
transduction
\Phi
, there is a second transduction
\Psi
such that
if
\Phi
approximately orders
X
according to
\preceq
, then
\Psi
orders
X
according to
\preceq
.

Proof.

Using non-deterministic colouring,
\Psi
guesses the index of every vertex modulo 5.
Let
x
and
y
be two vertices in
X
with indices
i
and
j
respectively.
If
i
and
j
differ by at least 2 modulo 5, then we can by assumption test whether
x\preceq y
using
\Phi
.
Assume thus that
j
is one of
\{i-1,i,i+1\}
modulo 5.
Then, there exists
k\in\mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z}
at distance at least 2 modulo 5 from both
i
and
j
.

If there exists
z\in X
with index
k
modulo 5 such that
x\preceq z
and
z\preceq y
(which can again be tested with
\Phi
),
then
x\preceq y
by transitivity, and similarly if
y\preceq z\preceq x
.
Let us thus assume that any
z\in X
with index
k
modulo 5
is either before both
x
and
y
in
\preceq
, or after both of them.

For any
t
, pick
z_{t}\in X
with index
5t+k
(as long as it is in the interval
[n]
),
which exists by assumption.
Then there must be some
s
such that
z_{t}\preceq x,y
when
t\leqslant s
and
z_{t}\succeq x,y
when
t>s

(with possibly
s=0
or
s=n
).
Thus
i
and
j
are between
5s+k
and
5(s+1)+k
, and are equal to neither of them since
i,j
are not
k
modulo 5.
It follows that that the ordering of
i,j
only depends on their values modulo
5
, which we have guessed.
∎

Let us call
\rho
the recolouring function of the bags
B_{i}
.
It is the same for all bags since it is part of the type
\tau
,
and it is idempotent (
\rho\circ\rho=\rho
).
Consider any vertex of
B
, say
x\in V(B_{i})
.
In the bag
B_{i}
,
x
is given some colour
\lambda_{B_{i}}(x)
,
which we call its
initial colour

\lambda_{init}(x)
.
Then, as we multiply by
B_{i+1}
, the colour of
x
changes to
\rho(\lambda_{init}(x))
.
Continuing with
B_{i+2}
will again recolour
x
by applying
\rho
,
but since
\rho
is idempotent, this does nothing.
Thus, for any
j>i
, the colour of
x
in
B_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot B_{j}
is
\rho(\lambda_{init}(x))
.
We call this its
final colour
and denote it by
\lambda_{fin}(x)
.

Recall that to define the type
\tau
of the bags
B_{i}
,
we first defined the vertex type
\type_{B_{i}}(x)
of
x
inside the bag
B_{i}
,
which indicates its colour
\lambda_{B_{i}}(x)
, i.e. the initial colour,
and the direction of edges between
x
and input vertices in
B_{i}
.
In what follows, the type of a vertex
x
will always be understood
relative to the bag
B_{i}
containing it, and we shorten it to
\type(x)
.
Using a non-deterministic colouring, we can guess the types of all vertices in
B
,
and we assume this colouring to be given in all subsequent lemmas.

Lemma 4.5
.

Given vertices
x,y
with
\idx(x)+2\leqslant\idx(y)
,
the direction of the edge between
x
and
y
only depends on
\type(x),\type(y)
, and
\rho
.

Proof.

Say
\idx(x)=i
and
\idx(y)=j
.
By definition of the product of bags, the direction of the edge between
x
and
y

is the same as that of the edge between
c
and
y
in
B_{j}
, where
c
is the colour of
x
in
B_{i}\cdot\ldots\cdot B_{j-1}
.
Since
j\geqslant i+2
, the colour
c
is
\lambda_{fin}(x)
, which only depends on
\type(x)
and
\rho
.
The direction of the edge between
c
and
y
in
B_{j}
is then given by
\type(y)
.
∎

With this in hand, we can already order the vertices of a given type
\alpha
.
Denote by
V_{\alpha}=\bigcup_{i}\alpha(B_{i})
the set of vertices of type
\alpha
.
Note that if
V_{\alpha}
is non-empty, then it intersects all bags
B_{1},\dots,B_{n}
.
Indeed whether or not
B_{i}
contains a vertex of type
\alpha
is indicated by the bag type
\tau
.

Lemma 4.6
.

For any type
\alpha
, there is an
FO
transduction depending only on
k,\rho,\alpha
that orders
V_{\alpha}
according to
\preceq
.

Proof.

By
Lemma

4.4
, it is sufficient to approximately order
V_{\alpha}
.
According to
Lemma

4.5
, for any
x,y
of type
\alpha
satisfying
\idx(x)+2\leqslant\idx(y)
,
either there always is the edge
x\to y
, or always
x\leftarrow y
,
and this direction depends only on
\alpha,\rho
.
Either way, one can order
x,y
with indices differing by at least 2
simply by looking at the direction of the edge between them.
∎

Thus we can order each type independently, and the issue is now to combine these orderings.
Denote by
\Gamma
the set of inhabited vertex types in
B_{1},\dots,B_{n}
.
Recall that subsets of vertices
X,Y
are called
homogeneous

if either all edges are oriented from
X
to
Y
, or all from
Y
to
X
.
We define the
synchronisation graph

G^{\mathsf{syn}}
with vertices
\Gamma
,
in which
\alpha,\beta
are adjacent if and only if
V_{\alpha}
and
V_{\beta}
are
not
homogeneous.

We will show that when
G^{\mathsf{syn}}
is connected, one can transduce the quasi-ordering
\preceq

corresponding to the original decomposition
B_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot B_{n}
.
Then, we will handle the case where
G^{\mathsf{syn}}
is disconnected by slightly modifying this decomposition.
We start with only two types that are non-homogeneous.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
\alpha,\beta\in\Gamma
be types such that
V_{\alpha},V_{\beta}
are non-homogeneous.
Then
V_{\alpha}\cup V_{\beta}
can be ordered according to
\preceq
by an
FO
transduction depending only on
k,\tau,\alpha,\beta
.

Proof.

Consider
x\in V_{\alpha}
and
y\in V_{\beta}
which we are trying to order.
Using
Lemma

4.4
, we can assume that their indices differ by at least 2.
We first consider the orientation of the edge between
x
and
y
.
By
Lemma

4.5
, it only depends on the types
\alpha,\beta

and on which of
x
and
y
has the smaller index.
It is simple to check that this leaves only four possibilities:

•

Either the edge is always oriented from the smaller index to the larger one,
or symmetrically from the larger to the smaller.
These are the simple cases: as in
Lemma

4.6
,
we immediately obtain the ordering of
x
and
y
from the edge between them.

•

Otherwise, the edge is always oriented from
x
to
y
, i.e. from the type
\alpha
to the type
\beta
, or vice versa.

Let us thus assume that we are in the second case, and
without loss of generality, for
\type(x)=\alpha
and
\type(y)=\beta
,
the edge is always oriented as
x\to y
, unless their indices differ by less than 2.
On the other hand, since
V_{\alpha}
and
V_{\beta}
are not homogeneous,
there must exist an edge
u\leftarrow v
with
\type(u)=\alpha
and
\type(v)=\beta
.
We call such an edge from
V_{\beta}
to
V_{\alpha}
a
backward edge
.

Call
V_{i,\alpha}=\alpha(B_{i})
the set of vertices with type
\alpha
in
B_{i}
.
Recall that the type of
B_{i}
indicates whether or not
V_{i,\alpha}
and
V_{i,\beta}
are homogenous,
and, when they are, the direction of edges between them.
Thus this information does not depend on
i
, since all
B_{i}
have the same type.
Similarly, using the definition of types and product of bags, one may check that
the direction of edges between
V_{i,\alpha}
and
V_{i+1,\beta}
(which are always homogeneous)
does not depend on
i
, and idem with
V_{i-1,\beta}
.

It follows that if the backward edge
u\leftarrow v
is for instance
between
u\in V_{k,\alpha}
and
v\in V_{k+1,\beta}
,
then there is also a backward edge between
V_{i,\alpha}
and
V_{i+1,\beta}
for all
i
.
In all cases, we obtain that for all
i
, there is a backward edge between
V_{i,\alpha}

and one of
V_{i-1,\beta},V_{i,\beta},V_{i+1,\beta}
.

We are now able to order
x
and
y
as follows. Say that
x\in V_{i,\alpha}
.
Using
Lemma

4.6
, we can identify the set
V_{i,\alpha}
.
Then, we can find some backward edge
u^{\prime}\leftarrow v^{\prime}
with
u^{\prime}\in V_{i,\alpha}
and
v^{\prime}
of type
\beta
.
By the previous arguments, such a backward edge must exist, and necessarily
\idx(v^{\prime})\in\{i-1,i,i+1\}
.
Finally, we compare
v^{\prime}
and
y
which are both of type
\beta
using
Lemma

4.6
.
Since the index of
y
is by assumption less than
i-1
or more than
i+1
,
it does not matter that the index of
v^{\prime}
is only known up to plus or minus 1.
∎

Once we can order vertices of two non-homogeneous types, it is simple to propagate the ordering to a connected component of
G^{\mathsf{syn}}
.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
\Theta\subset\Gamma
be a connected component of the synchronisation graph
G
,
and denote
V_{\Theta}=\bigcup_{\alpha\in\Theta}V_{\alpha}
.
Then
V_{\Theta}
can be ordered according to
\preceq
by an
FO
transduction depending only on
k,\tau,\Theta
.

Proof.

Say, without loss of generality, we want to compare vertices
x
and
y
of types
\alpha
and
\beta
, respectively, where
\alpha
and
\beta
belong to the same connected component
\Theta
. As
\alpha,\beta\in\Theta
, there exists a path

P:\alpha=\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{h}=\beta

connecting them in the synchronisation graph.

For each pair
\gamma_{i},\gamma_{i+1}
,
Lemma

4.7
gives a transduction that orders
V_{\gamma_{i}}\cup V_{\gamma_{i+1}}
.
Combining all these transductions, we may assume we have access to a relation
\preceq_{i}

which coincides with
\preceq
on any pair
x,y
with
x\in V_{\gamma_{i}}
and
y\in V_{\gamma_{i+1}}
, or vice versa.
Then we can also define
x\sim_{i}y
as
x\preceq_{i}y\land y\preceq_{i}x
to check that
x,y
are in the same bag under the same assumption on types.
Recall also that we assume to have already guessed the type of each vertex with colouring,
and let us write
\gamma_{i}(x)
for the predicate checking that
x
has type
\gamma_{i}
.

Now we can order
x\in V_{\alpha}
and
y\in V_{\beta}
by the formula:

\displaystyle\exists x_{1},\dots,x_{h-1},\quad

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}(x_{1})\land\dots\land\gamma_{h-1}(x_{h-1})

\displaystyle\land\

\displaystyle x\sim_{0}x_{1}\land x_{1}\sim_{1}x_{2}\land\dots\land x_{h-2}\sim_{h-2}x_{h-1}

\displaystyle\land\

\displaystyle x_{h-1}\preceq_{h-1}y.

Indeed, since each type
\gamma_{i}
is inhabited, and thus intersects every type,
there must be some
x_{i}
of type
\gamma_{i}
in the same bag as
x
.
The first line of the formula checks that
x_{1},\dots,x_{h-1}
have the correct types,
the second line checks that they are in the same bag as
x
, and the last line compares
x_{h-1}
and
y
,
which is equivalent to comparing
x
and
y
.
∎

When
G^{\mathsf{syn}}
is connected,
Lemma

4.8
concludes the proof of
Lemma

4.3
.
Note in that case that the given factorisation
B=B_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot B_{n}
is not modified,
i.e. the partition
\{X_{1},\dots,X_{m}\}
in the conclusion of
Lemma

4.3
is exactly
\{B_{1},\dots,B_{n}\}
,
and conditions (
1
) and (
2
) of the statement are trivial.

Assume finally that
G^{\mathsf{syn}}
has several connected components that we enumerate as
\Theta_{1},\dots,\Theta_{r}
.
We then reorganise the decomposition of
B
.
For
i\in[n]
and
t\in[r]
, call
X_{t,i}
the set of internal vertices of
B_{i}
with types in
\Theta_{t}
.
By
Lemma

4.8
, for each
t\in[r]
, the set
V_{\Theta_{t}}
can be ordered according to
\preceq
with a transduction.
Let
\preceq^{\prime}
be the quasi-ordering on the partition

\{X_{t,i}\mid i\in[n],t\in[r]\}
obtained by ordering first by type and then by bag, i.e.

X_{1,1}\preceq^{\prime}\dots\preceq^{\prime}X_{1,n}\preceq^{\prime}\dots\preceq^{\prime}X_{r,1}\preceq^{\prime}\dots\preceq^{\prime}X_{r,n}.

This quasi-ordering
\preceq^{\prime}
has small cut-rank:

Lemma 4.9
.

For any
x\in V(T)
,

\rk_{T}(\{y:y\preceq^{\prime}x\});\{y:y\succ^{\prime}x\})\leqslant k\cdot(2^{k}+1).

Proof.

Any bipartition as in the statement can be written as
(X,Y)

with
X=\big(\bigcup_{t^{\prime}<t}V_{\Theta_{t^{\prime}}}\cup\bigcup_{j\leq i}X_{t,j}\big)

and
Y=\big(\bigcup_{t^{\prime}>t}V_{\Theta_{t^{\prime}}}\cup\bigcup_{j>i}X_{t,j}\big)

for some
t\in[r]
and
i\in[n]
.
Recall that the total number of vertex-types (for bags of order
k
) is bounded by
k\cdot 2^{k}
.
By definition of the synchronisation graph, between
\bigcup_{t^{\prime}<t}V_{\Theta_{t^{\prime}}}
and
\bigcup_{t^{\prime}\geqslant t}V_{\Theta_{t^{\prime}}}
,
the direction of edges only depend on the types of vertices.
This implies

(2)

\displaystyle\rk_{T}\left(\bigcup_{t^{\prime}<t}V_{\Theta_{t^{\prime}}};\big(\bigcup_{t^{\prime}>t}V_{\Theta_{t^{\prime}}}\cup\bigcup_{j>i}X_{t,j}\big)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{k\cdot 2^{k}}{2},

and similarly

(3)

\displaystyle\rk_{T}\left(\bigcup_{j\leq i}X_{t,j};\bigcup_{t^{\prime}>t}V_{\Theta_{t^{\prime}}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{k\cdot 2^{k}}{2}.

On the other hand, since the bags have order
k
, any prefix/suffix bipartition in the ordering of bags has rank at most
k

(see
Lemmas

2.3
and
3.1
). Thus

(4)

\rk_{T}\left(\bigcup_{j\leq i}X_{t,j};\bigcup_{j>i}X_{t,j}\right)\leq k.

The three inequalities directly combine to prove
\rk(X;Y)\leqslant k\cdot(2^{k}+1)
.
∎

We can now complete the proof of
Lemma

4.3
, by checking the three conditions of the statement.

(1)

Firstly, each
T[X_{t,i}]
is the internal tournament of a bag in
\mathfrak{C}_{k}
of factorisation depth at most
(p-1,q+2p)
.
Indeed, this follows from
Lemma

3.5
since
T[X_{t,i}]
is the restriction of
T[V(B_{i})]
to some union of types,
and
B_{i}
is a bag in
\mathfrak{C}_{k}
and has factorisation depth at most
(p-1,q)
by assumption.

(2)

By
Lemma

4.9
, the quasi-ordering

X_{1,1}\preceq^{\prime}\dots\preceq^{\prime}X_{1,n}\preceq^{\prime}\dots\preceq^{\prime}X_{r,1}\preceq^{\prime}\dots\preceq^{\prime}X_{r,n}.

has cut-rank at most
k\cdot(2^{k}+1)
.

(3)

Finally, there is an extension
FO
transduction
\Psi_{k}
depending only on
k
such that
for
T=(V,E)
the internal tournament of
B
, we have
(V,E,\preceq^{\prime})\in\Psi_{k}(T)
.
Indeed, from
Lemma

4.8
, we can obtain the restriction of
\preceq^{\prime}
to any
V_{\Theta_{t}}
by a transduction depending only on
k,\tau,\Theta
.
By first guessing the type of each vertex, ordering by types between the different sets
V_{\Theta_{1}},\dots,V_{\Theta_{r}}
,
and by the previous transductions inside each
V_{\Theta_{t}}
, we obtain the desired quasi-ordering
\preceq^{\prime}
.

This transduction depends on
k
, the type
\tau
of bags, and the synchronisation graph
G^{\mathsf{syn}}
.
But, as argued at the start of
Section

4.3
, there are only finitely many possible choices for
\tau
and for
G^{\mathsf{syn}}
when
k
is fixed,
thus the transduction
\Psi_{k}
may first guess
\tau
and
G^{\mathsf{syn}}
, and then proceed as above.

This completes the proof of
Lemma

4.3
, and with it
Theorem

4.1
.

5.
Definable decompositions and definable properties

Theorem

4.1
proved that in tournaments of bounded linear clique-width, one can
FO
-transduce orderings of bounded cut-rank.
We now show in
Section

5.1
that from this ordering, one can also transduce a linear clique decomposition, proving our main result,
Theorem

1.3
.
Then, in
Section

5.2
, we combine this with some of the central ideas from Courcelle’s work
[
6
]

to obtain that the logics
MSO
and
EMSO
are equivalent over bounded linear clique-width tournaments (
Theorem

1.4
).

5.1.
FO-transducing linear decompositions

Let us first explain how we represent a linear decomposition as a relational structure.
We choose a representation based on the monoid
\mathfrak{C}_{k}
from
Section

3
,
but it would be simple to obtain the same results with a representation based on the operations defining linear clique-width or linear NLC-width.

We use the following standard encoding of words as relational structures:
for a finite alphabet
A
, a word
w_{1},\dots,w_{n}
in
A^{*}
is represented by the ordered structure with
vertex set
\{1,\dots,n\}
, one binary relation
<
for the natural ordering on
\{1,\dots,n\}
,
and for each letter
a\in A
, a unary relation
a(x)
such that
a(i)
holds if and only if
w_{i}=a
.
We call this the
unary representation
of the word
w_{1},\dots,w_{n}
.

Now for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\mathfrak{A}_{k}
denote the set of atomic bags of order
k
(up to isomorphism).
Recall that
\mathfrak{A}_{k}
by definition generates the monoid
\mathfrak{C}_{k}
,
and that linear NLC decompositions are equivalent, in the sense of
Lemma

3.1

to words
B_{1},\dots,B_{n}
with each
B_{i}\in\mathfrak{A}_{k}
.
We then represent the linear decomposition
B_{1},\dots,B_{n}
as a word over the alphabet
\mathfrak{A}_{k}
, in unary representation.

Firstly, from a linear decomposition, one can reconstruct the corresponding graph with a transduction.

Lemma 5.1
.

For any
k
, one can compute an
FO
transduction
\Phi_{k}
which given a linear decomposition
B_{1},\dots,B_{n}
with
B_{i}\in\mathfrak{A}_{k}
, outputs the underlying graph of
B_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot B_{n}
.

Lemma

5.1
is usually considered for
MSO
transductions, where it is a very easy result.
With FO transductions instead, it is a folklore application of Simon’s factorisation.
Specifically, we will need the following fact.

Consider a finite semigroup
S
, and a word
w_{1},\dots,w_{n}
with letters in
S
.
The
binary representation
of
w_{1},\dots,w_{n}
is the relation structure with
again vertices
\{1,\dots,n\}
and the natural ordering
<
as a binary relation,
and additionally, for each
a\in S
a
binary
relation
a(x,y)

such that
a(i,j)
holds if and only if
i\leqslant j
and
w_{i}\cdot\ldots\cdot w_{j}=a
.
The following result is a variant of
[
5
, Lemma 3]
.

Lemma 5.2
.

For any finite semigroup
S
, one can compute an
FO
transduction
\Psi_{S}
that,
for any word
w=w_{1},\dots,w_{n}
in
S^{*}
, given the unary representation of
w
, outputs its binary representation.

Sketch of proof.

Colcombet
[
5
]
defines an encoding of factorisation forests called
splits
.
Roughly, if
T
is a factorisation forest for
w
(i.e. a tree whose leaves are
1,\dots,n
in this order),
the split encoding
T
is the map
s:[n]\to\mathbb{N}
where
s(i)
is the minimal depth of a node
t\in T
such that
i
is the left-most descendent of
t
.
When considering factorisation forests of height at most
h\coloneqq 3|S|
given by
Theorem

3.4
,
the split is thus a map
s:[n]\to[h]
.
It can thus be added to the unary representation of
w
as
h
additional unary relations
s_{d}
, where
s_{d}(i)
holds if and only if
s(i)=d
.

Crucially, since the split
s
is encoded by a bounded number of unary relations,
an
FO
transduction can guess
s
with a non-deterministic colouring.

Now the statement of
[
5
, Lemma 3]
is precisely equivalent to:
for any finite semigroup
S
, there is an
FO
interpretation which
given any word
w
in
S^{*}
in unary representation together with a split of
w
of bounded height,
outputs the binary representation of
w
.

To obtain our statement, the transduction
\Psi_{S}
proceeds as follows:

(1)

Guess a split of bounded height for
w
using non-deterministic colouring.

(2)

With this split, apply
[
5
, Lemma 3]
to obtain the binary representation of
w
.

(3)

Using filtering, check that the binary representation is correct.
That is, check that it is correct for each letter
w_{i}
,
i.e. that

holds in the input unary representation if and only if
a(i,i)
holds in the output binary representation,
and that it is consistent with product,
i.e. that for
i<j
,
a(i,j)
holds if and only if
b(i,j-1)
and
c(j,j)
hold for some
a=b\cdot c
in
S
.

When step 1 guesses a valid split, then step 2 outputs the desired binary representation,
and step 3 ensures that only the valid binary representation can be produced by the transduction.
∎

Proof of
Lemma

5.1
.

Consider a linear decomposition
B_{1},\dots,B_{n}
given by its unary representation, with
B_{i}\in\mathfrak{A}_{k}
.
By definition, each bag
B_{i}
has a single internal vertex
v_{i}
, or none at all.
Let
\rho_{i}
be the recolouring map of
B_{i}
, which is in the semigroup of maps
[k]\to[k]
.
By
Lemma

5.2
, we may assume that we are also given
\rho_{1},\dots,\rho_{n}
in binary representation,
i.e. for each map
\nu:[k]\to[k]
, there is a binary predicate
\nu(i,j)
that holds if and only if
\nu=\rho_{j}\circ\dots\circ\rho_{i}
.

Now given
i<j
corresponding to vertices
v_{i},v_{j}
, we want to test whether or not
v_{i}v_{j}
is an edge.
The different relations in the input structure give access to
(1)
B_{i}
, and in particular the colour
c
of
v_{i}
in
B_{i}
,
(2) the recolouring map
\rho_{j-1}\circ\dots\circ\rho_{i+1}
(thanks to
Lemma

5.2
),
and (3)
B_{j}
, and in particular the colours in
B_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot B_{j-1}
to which

is connected.
These three pieces of information determine whether or not
v_{i}v_{j}
is an edge,
and each of them is bounded, hence one can test adjacency by writing the corresponding finite lookup table as an
FO
formula.

Finally, it only remains to delete the vertices

corresponding to bags
B_{i}
that do not contain any vertex.
Again this is trivially expressible by an
FO
formula querying the unary relations that encode the word
B_{1},\dots,B_{n}
.
∎

Theorem 5.3

For any

\Phi_{k}
and

such that

(1)

for any graph

,

to obtain an ordering

with cut-rank at most some

function of

By

, there is a linear decomposition

5.2.
Application to MSO-definable properties

Applications of transductions between graphs and tree- or clique-decompositions in Courcelle’s work
typically involve the
Backwards Translation Theorem

[
6
, Theorem 1.40]
,
which states that for any
MSO
transduction
\Phi
with signature
\Gamma\to\Delta
,
and any
MSO
formula
\psi
on
\Delta
, there is an
MSO
formula
\psi\circ\Phi
such that

G\models\psi\circ\Phi\quad\iff\quad\exists H\in\Phi(G),\ H\models\psi.

We will use a similar statement to prove
Theorem

1.4
from
Theorem

1.3
.

Backwards translation is easily adapted to
CMSO
formulas and transductions,
as well as
FO
formulas and
deterministic

FO
transductions.
On the other hand,
FO
formulas are not sufficiently expressive to handle the non-deterministic colouring operation.
To this end, one should instead use
existential
MSO

(
EMSO
) formulas, that is formulas of the form

\exists X_{1},\dots,X_{n},\ \psi,

where the variables
X_{i}
range over subsets of vertices,
and
\psi
is an
FO
formula that may use the predicates
x\in X_{i}
.
The proof remains essentially the same as in the
MSO
case.

Lemma 5.4

(Backwards Translation for
FO
Transductions)
.

For any
FO
transduction
\Phi
with signature
\Gamma\to\Delta
,
and any
EMSO
formula
\psi
on
\Delta
, there is an
EMSO
formula
\psi\circ\Phi
such that

G\models\psi\circ\Phi\quad\iff\quad\exists H\in\Phi(G),\ H\models\psi.

Sketch of proof.

It suffices to prove the result when taking
\Phi
to be each of the five operations constituting
FO
transductions independently.

interpretation:

If
\Phi
is an interpretation
\Gamma\to\Delta
,
define
\psi\circ\Phi
by replacing each occurrence of a relation
R(x_{1},\dots,x_{r})
in
\psi
for
R\in\Delta

by the corresponding formula
\varphi_{R}(x_{1},\dots,x_{r})
from
\Phi
.

colouring:

If
\Phi
is a non-deterministic colouring adding unary predicates
C_{1},\dots,C_{c}
,
then define
\psi\circ\Phi
as

\exists X_{1},\dots,X_{n},\ \psi,

after replacing
C_{i}(x)
with
x\in X_{i}
inside
\psi
.

filtering:

If
\Phi
is a filtering that checks that the sentence
\varphi
holds,
then define
\psi\circ\Phi
as
\psi\land\varphi
.

universe restriction:

If
\Phi
restricts the vertex set to those
x
satisfying
\varphi(x)
,
then define
\psi\circ\Phi
by replacing first-order quantifiers in
\psi
as follows:

\displaystyle\exists x.\ \theta(x)\quad

\displaystyle\text{becomes}\quad\exists x.\ \varphi(x)\land\theta(x)

and

\displaystyle\forall x.\ \theta(x)\quad

\displaystyle\text{becomes}\quad\forall x.\ \varphi(x)\rightarrow\theta(x)

The leading
EMSO
quantifiers
\exists X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
need not be modified.

copying:

When
\Phi
copies each vertex
v
into
(v,1),\dots,(v,k)
,
defining
\psi\circ\Phi
involves simulating a quantifier
\exists(v,i)
over copies of vertices
by a quantification
\exists v
, and a case disjunction over
i
.
As this case is far more tedious than the others, and none of our transductions use copying, we do not detail it further.
∎

The last tool we need to prove
Theorem

1.4
is the following folklore variant of Büchi–Elgot–Trakhtenbrot theorem.

Theorem 5.5

(Büchi–Elgot–Trakhtenbrot)
.

For any finite alphabet
A

L\subseteq A^{*}
, the following are equivalent:

(1)

L
is a regular language,

(2)

L
is defined by an
MSO
sentence,

(3)

L
is defined by an
CMSO
sentence,

(4)

L
is defined by an
EMSO
sentence.

See
1.4

Proof.

Take the transduction
\Phi_{k}
and
k^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
given by
Theorem

5.3
.
Applying backwards translation for
CMSO
to the transduction from linear decompositions to graphs given by
Lemma

5.1
,
we obtain a
CMSO
formula
\varphi^{\prime}
on linear decompositions of width at most
k^{\prime}
,
such that for any linear decomposition
D
with width at most
k^{\prime}
of a tournament
T
,
we have

if and only if
T\models\varphi
.
Since linear decompositions are words,
Theorem

5.5
gives that
\varphi^{\prime}
is equivalent to some

.

Now apply backwards translation for
FO
(

) to

given by

.
This yields an

such that

holds for some

.

Consider

.
Assume that

, and consider a linear decomposition

Then the choice of

∎
