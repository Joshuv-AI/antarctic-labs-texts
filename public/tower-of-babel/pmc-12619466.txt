Helicopter EMS for scene response to head-injured patients: systematic review & meta-analysis
Authors: Christie L Fritz, Carlo L Rosen, Caroline E Thomas, Andy J Kim, James Price, Owen Hibberd, Sam Galvagno, David W Schoenfeld, Stephen H Thomas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHelicopter EMS (HEMS) is an important component of prehospital trauma scene response care worldwide, including for traumatic brain injury (TBI), a major cause of mortality in injured patients. Our objective was to perform a meta-analysis (MA) of trauma HEMS scene responses to patients with severe head injury to determine whether air medical response is associated with improved survival.MethodsA broad and systematic search of the literature was conducted from the years 1970–2024. We included studies with the outcome of mortality in HEMS vs. the control of ground EMS (GEMS) in trauma scene transports (adult or pediatric) with severe TBI as defined by Glasgow Coma Score (GCS) < 9 or Head Abbreviated Injury Score (AISHead) ≥ 3. A random effects restricted maximum likelihood MA was conducted, with post-analysis evaluation for bias.ResultsOf 21 HEMS outcomes studies evaluating TBI, 15 were eligible for MA, and effect estimates were HEMS-favorable in 13 and statistically significant in 9. The null hypothesis of no HEMS association with TBI survival was rejected (p < .01) for both the GCS < 9 and the AISHead3+ groups. Heterogeneity measures supported generation of a pooled effect estimate for the GCS < 9 group: HEMS survival OR 1.37 (95% CI 1.23–1.53, I2 0%) but not for the AISHead3+ group (for which HEMS had statistically significant association with improved survival in six of eight studies, but to different degrees with resulting I2 of 93%). There were no signs of small-study (publication) or other substantial bias, with overall evaluation of moderate to low risk of bias.ConclusionsThe available evidence suggests a survival benefit associated with HEMS scene response for patients with severe head injuries as defined by GCS < 9. For this group, at the median mortality from all studies (24%), HEMS scene response to TBI saves one life for every 19 transports (95% CI for number needed to treat, 15–28).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12873-025-01392-9.Keywords: Helicopter emergency medical service, Traumatic brain injury, Mortality, Prehospital, Scene, Trauma

Introduction

From its origins in military conflicts to its application in modern trauma systems, helicopter emergency medical services (HEMS) play a vital role in both delivery of expert medical care to the scene of injury as well as expeditious transport to definitive trauma care. Despite acknowledged limitations with the relevant evidence base, available data suggest that HEMS can offer benefits attributable to a combination of clinical care and time savings [1, 2].

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality [3]. The only randomized controlled HEMS trial to date, Australia’s Head Injury Retrieval Trial (HIRT) [3] had to be terminated early since ground EMS (GEMS) ambulances were non-compliant with treatment assignments. As a result, HIRT’s precise planning and methodological execution were largely negated. Thus, questions pertaining to the comparative effectiveness of HEMS must be evaluated using non-randomized study data.

There are few if any systematic evaluations of survival benefit associated with HEMS response to TBI. Existing meta-analysis (MA) [4] of physician-crewed EMS response to head injury scenes has been non-definitive. More importantly, studies of prehospital physician deployment to TBI must be interpreted with caution, because European GEMS units often include physicians whereas USA HEMS units are usually not staffed with physicians [5].

HEMS is thought to mediate improved outcomes in TBI patients based on a combination of crew expertise, decreased hospital time, and the fact that HEMS are an integral part of many developed trauma systems. HEMS is also resource-intensive, expensive, and associated with potential safety risks when utilized imprudently. Hence, the aim of this MA was to assess the comparative effectiveness of HEMS vs. GEMS in patients with severe TBI. We hypothesized that HEMS would be associated with a survival benefit for patients with a severe TBI, as defined by a Glasgow Coma Score (GCS) < 9 or Head Abbreviated Injury Score (AISHead) of 3 or higher.

Methods

Review framework

This systematic review with evidentiary quality rating (using Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation; GRADE) [6] was conducted in accordance with recommendations of the Cochrane Collaboration [7]. The structure follows the Preferred reporting items for systematic review and meta-analysis (PRISMA) version 2020 [8]. The PRISMA checklist is provided in the Supplement.

Bias was assessed using Cochrane’s Risk of bias in non-randomized studies of interventions (ROBINS-I) [9] with GRADE co-implementation recommendations [6]. Bias in the RCT was assessed using the Risk of Bias version 2 (RoB2) tool [10]. Bias plotting for ROBINS-I and RoB2 employed McGuinness and Higgins’ tool (https://mcguinlu.shinyapps.io/robvis/) [11]. The review was registered with the University of York’s International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO #648823).

PICO structure: participants – intervention – comparator – outcome

Studies were eligible if a comparison was made for adults or children with severe TBI who were transported by either HEMS or GEMS following a scene response. While the focus of this MA was scene transport, like previous MAs [12] that have attempted to address this topic, studies that included some interfacility transfers (less than half of the study population) were included. A pre-planned sensitivity analysis was performed to assess the effect of inclusion of interfacility transfers, though the final MA data only include one such study [13].

In order to reduce clinical heterogeneity and focus the MA on higher-acuity patients (i.e. participants), two severe TBI groups were defined a priori, one based on clinical severity (GCS < 9) and one based purely on anatomical (AISHead 3+) severity [12]. Advantages and disadvantages with each of the two grouping approaches were acknowledged; thus, study planning called for separate MA evaluations for any HEMS associated survival benefits. HEMS outcomes studies assessing TBI using other definitions were eligible for discussion in the systematic review but were not for inclusion in MA calculations.

The predictor of interest (i.e., intervention) was HEMS response to the trauma scene. HEMS was defined as a scene response of a helicopter for management of a patient with severe TBI. Subsequent helicopter transport of the patient was the usual course, but was not required for the patient to be classified as receiving the intervention. This definition was established due to the previously reported occasional (e.g. Australia [3], Netherlands [13]) helicopter use to transport medical teams to trauma scenes with subsequent ground vehicle transport to trauma centers.

Any study design was eligible for consideration for inclusion, as long as the methods including reporting of a comparative estimate for HEMS vs. GEMS outcomes association (i.e. comparator). This MA excluded studies that did not report (or provide raw data enabling calculation of) a HEMS vs. GEMS effect estimate with 95% confidence interval (CI).

The outcome of interest evaluated in this MA was in-hospital survival (i.e., outcome). This outcome was chosen because in-hospital mortality is a single, consignable, and patient-centered outcome. Pre-planned sensitivity analyses were devised to evaluate studies that reported the outcome at different time intervals (i.e. one-month survival). Outcomes other than mortality (e.g. differing definitions of neurological and functional outcomes [14]) were recorded for analysis in future studies.

Information sources & search strategy

A two-part approach was used to constitute the evidentiary set for this MA. First, all HEMS outcomes studies – all reports since 1970 that executed comparison of HEMS vs. GEMS for patient outcomes in any diagnosis –were identified, and from that group, only TBI studies were selected. The primary data source for all candidate studies was the Helicopter Outcomes Assessment Research Database (HOARD). The freely available HOARD (www.cctcore.net) was initially constituted in 2023 and encompasses civilian HEMS vs. GEMS outcomes studies published since 1970 [15]. The database has been maintained with annual updates in 2024 [16] and January 2025 [17] and covers HEMS outcomes studies from 1970 through December 2024. For additional information on HOARD its construct, please see supplement.

Data collection and management

Study data were retrieved from databases in the Research Information Systems (RIS) format and imported into a reference manager (EndNote v21, Clarivate PLC, London, UK). Basic data on candidate studies were entered into the HOARD spreadsheet (Excel v16.95, Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA) by two authors (for the most recent January 2025 update, authors CET and SHT) with each entry cross-checked by both. In the event clarifications were needed, that study’s contact author was emailed.

A single simple endpoint of vital status for in-hospital or one-month time frame yielded relatively few areas of data entry subjectivity. One such area was the frequent use of propensity scoring (PS) methods to adjust for confounding in HEMS studies. When studies estimated effects as odds ratio (OR) generated by classical logistic regression and by PS methods, we considered the PS-model OR as a more conservative effect estimate. We performed sensitivity analyses to determine whether overall MA conclusions changed with replacement of the PS results compared to those obtained with standard multivariable regression techniques.

Analysis

Preliminary ROBINS-I and RoB2 ratings were generated by two of this review’s authors (JP and OH), with GRADE ratings generated by three others (CT, SG, and ST). These ratings were used as a basis from which consensus was reached among all study authors.

Stata (Stata 18.5MP, Stata Corp., College Station, TX) was used for all analysis, reporting, and graphing. Significance was defined as p < .05, and precision was reported using 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Study planning called for recording of studies’ n and results for HEMS’ mortality association, using the OR as the main effect measure. MA was performed using the random-effects (RE) maximum likelihood (REML) approach. RE methods were chosen due to known clinical heterogeneity in multiple arenas (e.g. patient case mix, logistics, prehospital crew configurations and practice scopes). Preplanned analysis aimed at evaluating results sensitivity to model selection called for repeating the MA calculations using three alternative RE approaches (empirical Bayes estimator, DerSimonian-Laird, and Sidak-Jonkman) [18].

Publication bias and other forms of small-study bias were assessed using funnel plotting and Galbraith plots (which were also employed to identify outliers). In keeping with Cochrane recommendations [19], we also assessed for signs of publication bias by calculating fixed-effect (FE) results and comparing those with the results from RE modeling. Leave-one-out MA was used to evaluate for studies with high leverage. Prespecified cumulative MA focused on possible changes of the effect estimate over time (i.e. later publication year). With the caveat that such approaches are most reliable when overall study N is at least ten, we further explored bias and heterogeneity using prediction intervals (to convey information about dispersion of effect sizes) and Egger’s test (to test null hypothesis of no small-study effects) [18–20].

One well-known issue requiring a prespecified approach was that of study overlap. Previous reviews [1, 21] of the HEMS trauma outcomes literature have noted the potential problem posed by multiple reports using the same (or largely the same) data. The problem is usually related to use of large national (or international) datasets such as the American College of Surgeons National Trauma Data Bank (NTDB), the German Trauma Society’s Trauma Registry (TRDGU), or the Japan Trauma Data Bank (JTDB). The issue is compounded by the fact that local or regional data reported in individual studies (e.g. from San Diego [22] or from the Pennsylvania state trauma registry [14, 23]) are highly likely to analyze cases that are also included in the NTDB.

Since reporting a MA with substantial case overlap can suggest inappropriately high precision, we established an a priori plan to address case overlap. Due to inability to know which cases were shared between studies, we were not able to use decoupling approaches or generalized-weight meta-estimation [24]. Instead, we excluded any studies for which 100% of cases were subsumed within other (included) studies. For studies with partial overlap, we highlighted potential overlap causes (e.g. study year, patient age). We then performed sensitivity analyses to determine the degree to which results were affected by excluding case-overlap studies.

The MA protocol called for inclusion of HEMS TBI outcomes studies that accrued patients based on one of two classifications: GCS < 9 or AISHead 3+. These were the only two categories for which pooled effect estimation was planned. Other studies using different patient accrual (eligibility) criteria had results recorded but these ORs and 95% CIs were only plotted as preliminary, hypothesis-generating findings.

We anticipated that mortality in the TBI population would be sufficiently high to violate the rare-disease assumption; ORs would not be suitable for interpretation as approximation of risk ratio. Given the non-intuitive nature of the OR, we followed recommendations of MA experts [18] to use calculations [25] converting OR to the more intuitive metric of number needed to treat (NNT); NNT was calculated at various baseline mortality rates.

Results

Study identification and characteristics

The PRISMA diagram shown in Fig. 1 demonstrates the derivation of the final 21 studies included in this MA [3, 14, 22, 23, 26–42]. The final set included 12 studies from the USA, six from Europe (after a seventh [43] was discarded as 100% subsumed in an included study), two from Japan (after a third [44] was discarded as 100% subsumed), and one from Australia. The European study [43] that was discarded for case overlap was published in German. All remaining studies in the MA were in English.

The other studies were reviews of trauma databases that were regional or national in scope. Many studies did not explicitly note inclusion or exclusion based on mechanism of injury, but four [3, 26, 31, 35] stated their cases were blunt trauma only. Age ranges of study patients varied widely, and differing definitions were used to delineate pediatric cases (thus preventing subgroup analysis). All studies assessed HEMS outcome vs. same-setting GEMS controls. None of the studies used standardized prediction tools such as the Trauma & Injury Severity Score (TRISS) [45] to constitute their GEMS control group.

Adjustment for confounding by acuity was executed in nearly all studies using multivariable logistic regression. The two oldest studies (Baxt [28] and Celli [32]) employed a stratified analysis approach, and DeJongh [33] executed a case-control matching methodology. PS adjustment for confounding patterns was used by eight studies [22, 23, 29, 30, 34, 36, 37, 40] but separate PS and non-PS model results were reported only in four [30, 36, 37, 40].

While most studies used logistic regression, methods for modeling differed. For example, in two studies highly restrictive univariable p cutoffs (0.01 in Ota [38] and 0.05 in Pakkanen [39]) were used to dictate variables’ eligibility for inclusion in multivariable logistic regression; such a rigorous approach risks residual confounding. Another area of methodological heterogeneity was execution of postestimation assessments such as model calibration and discrimination. Postestimation was irregularly included in results. As examples, Davis [22] reported on calibration and Andruszkow [27] and Hosomi [36] reported on discrimination.

Regarding missing data, studies handled the issue differently (as outlined in detail in the ROBINS-I table in the Supplement). Some studies [22, 27, 36, 38] used complete case analysis whereas others employed some variation of imputation [23, 30, 31, 35, 37].

For one report, Ota’s analysis of isolated TBI with AISHead of 3+ [38], a judgment was made to retain the study despite their dataset’s including a small number (< 5% in both HEMS and GEMS groups) of interfacility transports. Since this was the only study in the MA calculations set that included any interfacility transports, effects of its exclusion did not require sensitivity analysis beyond the leave-one-out analysis reported for all studies.

Bias assessment

All but one study were observational (non-randomized), and the sole RCT was terminated early due in part to substantial non-compliance. The overall risk of bias was above the “low risk of bias” level [9]. ROBINS-I and RoB2 were thus used to inform judgments as to whether level of bias was moderate, serious, or critical [9, 10]. 16 studies were found to have moderate risk of bias, one serious and three with critical overall risk of bias.

Detailed multi-stage ROBINS-I and RoB2 table results are provided in the Supplement. Since most of the studies in this MA had similar registry-centered methodology, the MA’s studies largely shared the same bias issues. We report here some highlights of the ROBINS-I and RoB2 tables.

The design of this MA did limit some forms of bias risk. Since the intervention (HEMS or GEMS) and the outcome (mortality) were both objective and fixed, misclassification was not an issue of concern. The mortality endpoint was usually reported for the MA’s predefined preferred time intervals of either one-month or in-hospital time frames. Three studies [28, 32, 39] reported mortality at six months post-injury, and one study [35] reported 90-day mortality.

One source of bias of unknown direction is individual study accrual of patients who had on-scene arrest. Exclusion of such cases, which is the usual approach in HEMS outcomes studies, has potential for bias in favor of either HEMS or GEMS [29, 39, 46]. Moribund patients may be more likely to be sent by HEMS, since they are higher in acuity, or they may be more likely to be sent by GEMS since chest compressions may be perceived as more easily performed in ground vehicles. Additionally, HEMS physicians may be more likely to pursue advanced resuscitative attempts but they may also be more likely to declare patients dead on scene [29, 39].

Nearly all studies in this MA appeared to exclude patients who were in arrest at the scene. One report [23] did execute a sensitivity analysis, and found that its results (OR in favor of HEMS, 1.61 with 95% CI 1.16–2.22) were not changed by inclusion vs. exclusion of on-scene arrests. Another report found that a posteriori exclusion of the cases in which HEMS physicians made an on-scene death declaration (a declaration GEMS could not make) resulted in a change from statistically non-significant to statistically significant findings in favor of HEMS response [39].

Acuity stratification was not judged to have been a likely source of important bias. GCS and AISHead are both commonly used and familiar scoring methods. GCS’ many potential cutoffs (the score runs from 3 to 15) present potential subjectivity in selection of an accrual criterion, but the GCS < 9 definition for severe TBI has been used for over four decades [28, 47] and is endorsed by recent investigators [23] who point out its preferability to other GCS cutoffs. The GCS < 9 cutoff is commonly encountered in EMS systems in many countries (e.g. Austria [48], Netherlands [35], USA [22]).

Figures 2 and 3 depict the risk of bias as tabulated by ROBINS-I and RoB2 approaches. The Garner study [3] is evaluated using RoB2 and the other studies were assessed using ROBINS-I. It is noted that while the Bulger study [31] had been initially reported as a trial, the HEMS-relevant study results were reported as a secondary analysis and there was no randomization of cases to HEMS.

In assigning bias risk we followed the algorithms provided by the ROBINS-I and RoB2 citations [9–11]. We generally tried to err towards more conservative ratings, in order to prevent overestimation of the validity of the HEMS non-randomized data.

For the observational studies, issues with confounding were the most important concerns. Additional limitations were posed by the problem of missing data.

For the RCT, the deviations from intended interventions and the missing outcome data were the primary concerns. High rates of non-compliance translated into a high risk of bias. The study’s authors noted that the directionality of bias could not be known definitively; there were high non-compliance rates in both the HEMS-assigned group (14%) and the GEMS-assigned group (21%). The RCT’s discussion included detailed analysis based on both intent-to-treat (ITT) and as-treated assessments, with the Australians concluding that on balance their findings suggested a HEMS benefit [3].

With regard to the HIRT RCT, this MA reports the ITT results, but it is noted that the as-treated results were more HEMS-favorable, reaching statistical significance in the RCT’s original TBI definition of GCS < 9 (HEMS survival OR 1.84, 95% CI 1.21–2.81). As-treated results were also more HEMS favorable, but did not reach significance, in the RCT’s modified TBI category of AISHead 3+ (OR 1.42, 95% CI 0.90–2.25).

This review’s interpretation of the overall bias data is that the observational studies were of reasonable design but still subject to inherent biases of non-RCT designs. Furthermore, although well-designed, the RCT was subject to substantial bias due to execution issues that were not under control of the investigators. MA data were further analyzed with additional advanced diagnostics, including both random and fixed effects models as well as trim-fill analyses and Galbraith plotting (Supplement 7). The overall conclusion was that the MA data were indeed subject to bias, and that any associations identified would be less reliable than similar findings from RCTs.

HEMS effect on survival: meta-analysis and forest plotting

The systematic review discussion encompasses the 21 studies in the HEMS TBI review set. These 21 studies, which include studies using TBI definitions other than the a priori groupings of GCS < 9 or AISHead 3+, are presented (without overall pooled effect estimation) in Appendix Fig. 1. That appendix figure portrays 26 effect estimates, since some studies presented multiple sets of results. One study [42] reported results for GCS < 8 but included supplementary raw data allowing calculation of HEMS survival OR for GCS < 9. Two other studies [3, 35] each reported separate findings for GCS < 9 and AISHead 3 + groups. Finally, two NTDB analyses [30, 37] each reported results for transports stratified by Level 1 or Level 2 receiving trauma centers (with no overall effect estimate).

Of 26 total sets of effect estimates from the 21 studies, point estimates were HEMS-favorable (OR > 1.0) in 24 cases with statistical significance reached in 16. The two non-favorable HEMS survival point estimates arose from the Bulger [31] ROC analysis TBI subset (OR 0.91, 95% CI 0.63-1.32) and from Garner’s [3] ITT analysis of AISHead 3+ (OR 0.95, 95% CI 0.60–1.51).

Of the 21 studies addressing association between HEMS scene response and TBI survival, 17 reported effect estimates using the a priori definitions this MA used for severe TBI. These 17 study findings comprised the analysis set for our MA modeling. The GCS < 9 TBI definition was reported by nine studies [3, 23, 28, 29, 31, 34, 35, 39, 42] and the AISHead 3 + definition was reported by eight studies [3, 14, 22, 26, 27, 35, 36, 38]. Included in the previous counts are two studies [3, 35] reporting using both the GCS < 9 and the AISHead 3 + definitions. Analyses for both predefined TBI subgroups (GCS < 9 and for AISHead 3+) rejected the null hypothesis of no HEMS association with survival (p < .01 for both). Low heterogeneity for the GCS < 9 group supported reporting of a pooled effect estimate for HEMS’ association with improved survival in this group. High heterogeneity for the AISHead group precluded generation of a pooled effect estimate for HEMS scene response in this group.

Based on the median overall mortality (of 24.2%) in the nine studies reporting GCS < 9 results, the HEMS survival OR 1.37 (95% CI 1.23–1.53) translated into a NNT of 19 (95% CI 15–28). One additional survivor is achieved for every 19 GCS < 9 cases transported by HEMS rather than GEMS. Further results on translating the pooled effect estimate to NNT at different baseline mortalities is presented in the Supplement.

The high heterogeneity (I2 93%) for the AISHead group precluded generation of a pooled effect estimate for this group. This heterogeneity also precluded generation of an overall effect estimate covering both TBI groups (GCS < 9 and AISHead 3+).

The fact that heterogeneity precluded calculation of an overall pooled effect estimate limited the effects of case overlap between studies in the GCS < 9 and AISHead 3 + sets. Overlap issues were thus restricted to three major points. First, it should be emphasized that there was high overlap between the GCS < 9 and AISHead group results for the two studies (Frankema [35] and Garner [3]) that provided results for each of these groups. Second, there was a relatively minor level of NTDB-based overlap in the GCS < 9 group, involving studies of Bulger [31], Chen [23], and Englum [34]. Third, in the AISHead group the high degree of inclusion of Ota’s [38] JTDB cases (isolated severe TBI) in Hosomi’s larger JTDB series (isolated and non-isolated TBI) should offset the visual impact of precision resulting from these two studies’ similar effect estimates. These and other case overlap issues are explored in detail in the Supplement in sensitivity analyses demonstrating no substantive changes in effect estimates based on excluding affected studies.

Given the heterogeneity within the AISHead 3 + group, and the resulting decision to not report an overall pooled effect estimate, further MA steps (e.g. evaluation for small-study bias) were executed separately for the GCS < 9 and AISHead 3 + groups seen in GRADE table in Fig. 5 below.

Discussion

This systematic review and MA assessed the comparative effectiveness of HEMS vs. GEMS scene response for patients with severe TBI. Using established definitions for severe TBI, a statistically significant and non-biased estimate of a beneficial association of HEMS with improved survival was found (OR 1.37; 95% CI, 15–28; NNT 19).

When examining the results using the clinical (GCS < 9) definition for inclusion, the effect estimates in the anatomical (AISHead 3+) group showed expected directionality but unexpected imprecision. Although there was an overall statistically significant association favoring HEMS with AISHead 3 + identified cases, heterogeneity was high. Since statistical findings (e.g. no substantial small-study bias) did not appear to explain the observed heterogeneity, we conclude that clinical heterogeneity accounted for the high I2 for the AISHead 3 + group. A few AISHead 3 + outlier studies reported lower HEMS effect estimates [27, 36, 38], and mortality rates were lower (13.9–14.6%) compared to the other AISHead 3 + studies that reported a stronger beneficial effect and higher mortality (40%). Hence, for the AISHead 3 + studies that were included in this MA, the association seemed to vary widely in different settings. Potential mechanisms likely involve advanced interventions such as endotracheal intubation or transfusion of blood products, which have been found to be more likely with HEMS response [23, 26]. As has been the case with previous HEMS MA interpretations [46], we believe that available data may be used to support conclusions regarding only presence of (not the mechanisms or explanations for) HEMS association with survival.

Our MA did not accrue sufficient AISHead 3 + studies to perform meta-regression to further examine associations between HEMS effects and individual study characteristics such as mortality, crew capabilities, or triage criteria [18, 20].

For prehospital system planners and others responsible for triage decisions, our MA findings for GCS < 9 provide a practical benefit that would not be applicable for the AISHead 3 + group. Practicality lies in the fact that, while GCS < 9 is known to have suboptimal accuracy in identifying severe TBI [3], it can be reliably calculated at the time of triage decision-making. Those responsible for optimizing judicious HEMS deployment will likely find utility in our finding of a statistically robust estimator for HEMS benefit in a group (GCS < 9) that can be quickly identified in the field.

We acknowledge related reviews focusing on either HEMS deployment for trauma (not limited to TBI) [1, 4, 46, 49–51] or trauma scene physician deployment (not limited to HEMS) [4, 5, 52]. These analyses generally (but not invariably) suggest HEMS benefit, but have not specifically addressed the question of HEMS use in TBI. Hence, the current study represents new knowledge regarding the HEMS literature pertaining specifically to the use of HEMS for patients with severe TBI.

The calculations in this MA were restricted to the a priori group definitions for severe TBI: GCS < 9 and AISHead 3+. Findings regarding the other TBI groups were informative in that they were generally consistent with the main findings. HEMS effect estimates favored HEMS in both of the higher-acuity groups (GCS < 8 and AISHead 4+) and also in the group comprising studies of all TBI (i.e. without restriction to particular severity). Furthermore, similarity of findings for Level 1 and Level 2 trauma centers in the Bekelis [30] and Missios [37] studies (which assessed all TBI) suggest HEMS effects may be similar in the highest two levels of receiving hospital trauma accreditation.

The Bekelis and Missios studies represented one group’s assessment of TBI patients across the age spectrum (< 16 in one study [37], 16 + in the other [30]). Similarly favorable effect estimates in the two studies were indicative of a broadly similar HEMS effect in all age groups. Englum’s [34] pediatric study of GCS < 9 cases assessed patients up to age 18, finding a HEMS-favorable effect estimate (1.40) essentially the same as the overall effect estimate (1.37) for all GCS < 9 studies. While we did not have sufficient data to definitively evaluate the HEMS association with TBI survival across different age categories, the available results did not suggest a substantial age-dependent difference in HEMS effects.

In addition to age, receiving trauma center level, and crew configuration, logistics represents an additional factor potentially related to HEMS putative survival effect. Since Baxt’s seminal JAMA HEMS study from 1983 [53], it has been known that HEMS “time-savings” benefit is measured not by total prehospital time, but by time saved in getting advanced care to patients [23, 54]. We did not specifically adjust for total prehospital time, since studies rarely report the key time-savings variable of expedited advanced care arrival to the patient and since total prehospital time was often longer for HEMS cases [26]. The absence of detailed logistics evaluation was not viewed by us as a major limitation, since rapid delivery of advanced care is a mediator (not a confounder) of HEMS’ effects in TBI. Furthermore, HEMS utility has been demonstrated for TBI even when HEMS is associated with longer on-scene times [23] or when multivariable modeling adjusts for prehospital time [26].

Limitations

Regardless of methodology, observational HEMS scene trauma mortality analyses are at high risk for residual confounding and selection bias. In fact, the same issues were behind the non-compliance with the Australian RCT: HEMS is usually the preferred method to transport patients perceived to be higher-acuity. Acuity-related bias cannot be eliminated. Although sensitivity analyses and PS modeling can mitigate bias, even advanced methods cannot fully eliminate confounding and selection bias [55].

For most other studies in this review, there was insufficient information to allow reliable ascertainment of the potential bias due to missingness. With regard to mechanism of missingness, we concur with the judgments in previous HEMS MA reports [46] that the missing data mechanism cannot be confidently classified as “missing at random” (MAR). This judgment has ramifications that include risk of bias related to missing data, even in the setting of strategies such as multiple imputation (MI).

We provide GRADE ratings as preliminary conclusions, given the relatively poor certainty associated with application of the methodology to studies that were virtually all observational, although moderate confidence in the effect estimate is reported for a HEMS benefit in patients with a GCS < 9 (Supplement 8) [6]. A number-needed-to-treat (NNT) analysis is also reported based on baseline estimated mortality (Appendix Table 7). GRADE certainties in the range of those identified by us in this MA have been found in previous HEMS reviews [46, 56].

GRADE’s patient-focused methods would be aptly applied to non-mortality endpoints such as functional outcome using the Glasgow Outcomes Score (GOS). While complexity and length restrictions precluded our MA from focusing on functional outcome in this review, we note that outcomes assessment by either GOS or discharge destination (e.g. home, nursing care facility) was reported in five studies. Statistically significant HEMS benefit for functional outcomes was identified by Wang [14] and Davis [22]. Hosomi [36] and Pakkanen [39] found non-significant effect estimates in favor of HEMS, whereas Garner [3] found a non-significant estimate in GEMS’ favor.

In addition to methodologic shortcomings previously discussed, this MA has additional limitations. The attempt to identify all HEMS outcomes studies addressing TBI was thorough, but it is possible that relevant studies were not identified. For those studies that were accrued into the review and MA, overall quality (even in the RCT) was not sufficiently high to support definitive results.

Another limitation in our work is the lack of control for crew configuration, though heterogeneity was thoroughly assessed and reported. Prior work has attempted to identify the independent association of crew configuration (i.e., prehospital physicians vs. paramedics) in HEMS [3–5, 13, 38, 39]. The results of these studies have been conflicting and indicate the need for more research in this area. Any benefit of HEMS is likely to be multifactorial, and due to some combination of time and distance, the ability of HEMS crews to perform lifesaving interventions, and the role of HEMS is a developed trauma system.

With regard to statistical limitations, some of the analyses we report may have been subject to suboptimal accuracy due to either low study N (e.g. Egger testing) or high heterogeneity (e.g. trim-fill assessment for the AISHead 3 + group). Generalization of results is limited by identification of high heterogeneity in the AISHead 3 + group, which precluded calculation of a pooled effect estimate for either the AISHead 3 + group or the overall group (of AISHead 3 + and GCS < 9). Future analyses choosing to focus on study accrual using the AISHead category will need to take further steps (e.g. stratification of results by AISHead level) to reduce heterogeneity and enable pooled effect estimation.

Conclusion

HEMS is associated with an improvement in in-hospital mortality when used for patients with severe TBI. Using a clinically derived definition (GCS < 9), findings that were robust to evaluation for bias suggested a 37% improvement in the odds for in-hospital survival. This odds reduction corresponds to approximately one life saved for every 19 HEMS deployments for severe TBI. When severe TBI was defined using anatomical terms (AISHead 3+) there was a statistically significant rejection of the null hypothesis of no effect but the range of favorable HEMS associations in the evidence base was too broad to support a common effect estimate.
