Abstract
We present an analysis of high-resolution mid-infrared observations at 25 and 37 µm of the Sagittarius C Complex in the Central Molecular Zone, based on data from the SOFIA/FORCAST Galactic Center Legacy Survey. Enabled by the high bright-source limit of the FORCAST instrument, we perform a map-level dust temperature and optical depth analysis with a focus on the Sgr C H II region, which has an average dust temperature of 61 61 K and average 37 µm optical depth of 0.05 0.05 . We find that the Sgr C H II region contains several high-density dust emission ridges, with lengths of up to several parsecs. Noting prior evidence for nonthermal radio emission from these density ridges, we postulate that there is an enhancement of relativistic electrons within them, possibly attributable to diffusive shock acceleration induced by the wind of a known nearby Wolf-Rayet (WR) star impacting the density ridges and the ambient gas in the surrounding photo-dissociation region. Additionally, the tangential magnetic field in the outskirts of the Sgr C H II region may serve to confine the electrons within this region. We examined the heating effect of the WR star by calculating its heating profile and performing a spectral energy distribution modelling of the H II region. We found an integrated MIR luminosity of ( 1.40 ± 0.19 ) × 10 6 ​ L ⊙ (1.40\pm 0.19)\times 10^{6}L_{\odot} , which implies that presently unidentified massive stars must be present in the H II region in addition to the WR star. We also present a brief analysis of adjacent regions, such as a mid-infrared/radio source denoted “Source C” and the G359.43+0.02 young stellar object cluster near the northern end of the prominent Sgr C non-thermal radio filament.

Introduction

The central half kiloparsec of the Milky Way disk is known as the Central Molecular Zone (CMZ) for its large molecular cloud mass, high cloud densities, strong magnetic fields, and dynamically complex behaviour, but has a star formation rate significantly lower than expected in such extreme environments
(see
Morris & Serabyn 1996
;

;

(

;
Lu et al. 2019a
)

∘
(
\sim

As such, Sgr C is a valuable laboratory to study the interactions between these astrophysical components, therefore contributing to our understanding of the CMZ as a whole.

Historically, Sgr C has received less attention than its more luminous and star-bursting counterpart, Sgr B2, at the opposite end of the CMZ. Very recently, however, a series of studies of Sgr C has been conducted with data from the Stratospheric Observatory for Infrared Astronomy (SOFIA) and the

II
Observations

The SOFIA/FORCAST Galactic Center Legacy Survey is a targeted survey designed to image MIR-bright regions of the inner 200 pc of the Milky Way Galaxy at wavelengths of 25 and 37 µm. The full width at half maximum (FWHM) of the 25 µm observations is
\sim
2.3″ and the measured point source sensitivity is 0.75 mJy. At 37 µm, the FWHM is
\sim
3.4″ and the measured point source sensitivity is 1.5 Jy. Further description of the survey can be found in
Hankins et al. 2020
. In this work, we also include later observations that were not part of the original Legacy Survey carried out in SOFIA cycle 7, but were carried out following a similar observing strategy as part of SOFIA cycle 9. These observations and subsequent data reduction are described in
Cotera et al. 2024
.

However, in our initial analysis, we found a small background offset between the data collected in cycles 7 and 9. The issue of background matching is a commonly encountered problem in SOFIA/FORCAST images when the source emission is more extensive than the native instrument field of view. This effect can be corrected by supplying a DC offset to individual frames to bring the background to a comparable level in regions of overlap between frames. The correction process is described in both
Hankins et al. 2020
and
Cotera et al. 2024
.

Observations of the Sgr C region also suffer from an imaging artifact known as the “bowl” effect
(
Lau et al. 2013
, e.g., see)
. This is a well-known artifact of the FORCAST instrument that appears near extended, bright sources, where adjacent regions within the frame can be over-subtracted. This likely contributes to the above-mentioned background matching issue. Several bright regions of the survey exhibit this effect and are discussed further in
Hankins et al. 2020
.

With the need to adjust background levels in individual frames, it became necessary to rebuild the survey mosaics. The steps for constructing the mosaics presented in this work largely follow from the process detailed in
Cotera et al. 2024
. Data processing for Level 3 data products uses the software package
Redux
, and the procedure is described in
(
Clarke et al. 2015
)
. The processed data can be downloaded from the NASA/IPAC Infrared Science Archive
2
2

2

https://irsa.ipac.caltech.edu/frontpage/
(IRSA). Background matching was performed to improve uniformity between individual frames.

We used the
Reproject
package in Python
(
Robitaille et al. 2023
)
to create the survey mosaics. This process included steps to compute an optimal world coordinate system (WCS) for the final image based on the final data coverage, and made use of the reproject_exact() function which conserves flux by using a spherical polygon intersection method
3
3

3

https://reproject.readthedocs.io/en/stable/api/reproject.reproject_exact.html#reproject.reproject_exact
. Prior to co-adding the data to create the final mosaic, additional data checks were performed to ensure consistency across the individual observations, similar to the process described in
Hankins et al. 2020
.

III.2
MIR Analysis

The images obtained with FORCAST at 25 and 37 µm are dominated by dust heated by the interstellar radiation field
(
Hankins et al. 2020

III.2.1
Extinction & Signal-to-noise Correction

In order to draw meaningful physical inferences from the luminosities of objects in the GC, we must first correct for the substantial foreground extinction
(
Weingartner & Draine 2001
;
Nishiyama et al. 2008
;
Fritz et al. 2011
)
. To make such corrections, we utilize the dust extinction map reported by
Simpson 2018
, derived as the optical depth at 9.8 µm (denoted
\tau_{9.8}
) from
Spitzer
/IRS observations in the CMZ. However, given that the
Spitzer
/IRS observations did not cover the entire CMZ, we adopt an interpolated version of the
\tau_{9.8}
map made using the nearest neighbours algorithm (provided by J. P. Simpson; private communication). We convolve the
\tau_{9.8}
map using a 2D Gaussian kernel with standard deviation of 12″ (or a FWHM of 28″) to smooth the sharp boundaries between different observation patches. We then adopt the extinction curve from
Chiar & Tielens 2006
to extrapolate the 9.8 µm optical depth to FORCAST-observed wavelengths, giving
\tau_{25.2}/\tau_{9.8}=0.41
and
\tau_{37.1}/\tau_{9.8}=0.28
.

Due to the spatial resolution difference at the two FORCAST wavelengths, we convolve both FORCAST images to a lower 5″ resolution using a 2D Gaussian kernel before computing the dust temperature. The FWHM of the said Gaussian kernel is computed as

{\rm FWHM}_{G,\lambda}=\sqrt{W_{\rm Target}^{2}-W_{\lambda}^{2}},

(1)

where
W_{\rm Target}=5
″ is the resolution of the final convolved maps and
W_{\lambda}
is the resolution of the FORCAST images at wavelength
\lambda
.

Since the survey data were obtained over the span of a few years, the frame-to-frame noise properties of the FORCAST images vary non-trivially due to differences in integration time and observing conditions. As such, we cannot assume a uniform SNR throughout the mosaicked images. Therefore, we assigned appropriate SNR cutoffs to individual frames prior to mosaicking. This method provided the optimal trade-off between excluding any pixel below the locally measured
3\sigma
level while preserving emission in frames that might have been cut off if a uniform SNR cutoff were adopted based on data with the worst SNR. Although some information contained in diffuse emission is lost, a higher SNR cutoff enables an accurate calculation of the physical properties of luminous MIR regions. We adopt the mosaic processed in this fashion throughout the rest of Section
III
.

III.2.2
Dust temperature map

F_{\nu}\propto\nu^{\beta}B_{\lambda}
, such that the flux
F_{\nu}
measured at frequency
\nu
follows a blackbody
B_{\nu}
weighted by a frequency-dependent power-law with index
\beta

\frac{F_{25}}{F_{37}}=\left(\frac{\lambda_{37}}{\lambda_{25}}\right)^{7}\frac{{\rm exp}\left(\frac{hc}{\lambda_{37}k_{\rm B}T_{\rm d}}\right)-1}{{\rm exp}\left(\frac{hc}{\lambda_{25}k_{\rm B}T_{\rm d}}\right)-1}

(2)

in wavelength space, where
F_{\lambda}
is the extinction-corrected flux at one of the FORCAST-observed wavelengths
\lambda
denoted by the subscript,
h
is the Planck constant,
k_{B}
is the Boltzmann constant, and
c
is the speed of light. The top left panel of Figure

III.2.3
Optical Depth map

An optical depth at these wavelengths can be derived in concert with the computed dust temperature. Following the assumption that the dust is optically thin, which is widely adopted in studying the CMZ
(
Hankins et al. 2019
, e.g.,)
, the optical depth at a given wavelength
\lambda
at each pixel is approximated as

\tau_{\lambda}=\frac{F_{\lambda}}{\Omega_{p}B_{\lambda}(T_{d})},

(3)

where
\Omega_{p}
is the solid angle subtended by a single FORCAST pixel. All of these quantities are taken per pixel. We show the global optical depth map calculated at 37 µm in the top right panel of Figure
3
. We find that the highest optical depth globally has values of
\sim
0.1, supporting the thin dust approximation. Regions with high MIR luminosity (e.g., the Sgr C H

region) are found to have a typical optical depth of
\sim 0.1-0.01
, whereas compact sources in the G359.43+0.02 YSO cluster and to the east of the H
II
region are found to have relatively low optical depth of
<0.01
, in accordance with the observation that they are stellar or young stellar objects situated in low-density ISM. The G359.44-0.10 EGO has the highest optical depth of
>0.1
, again confirming its high cloud density from previous studies
(
Kendrew et al. 2013
;
Crowe et al. 2025
)
, which is ideal for massive star formation.

III.2.4
Temperature Profile Modelling

Recently,
Geballe et al. 2019
and
Clark et al. 2021
reported two spectroscopically-determined late-type, dusty, carbon-sequence Wolf-Rayet stars (hereafter denoted WCL stars, based on this spectroscopic determination) in the Sgr C complex. Both of the stars are confirmed by
Oka et al. 2019
to be in the CMZ, with one of them suggestively located in projection at the centre of the Sgr C H
II
region (cross symbol in the bottom panels of Figure
3
). It is therefore of great interest to study their effect on the surrounding ISM. Between these two WCL stars, the one located outside the H
II
region has no observable association with other physical features across all wavelengths. Therefore, we dedicate the subsequent discussion almost exclusively to the WCL star at the projected centre of the H
II
region (2MASS label J17443734-2927557), which we hereafter denote as the central WCL star unless otherwise noted.

Here, we estimate the heating effect of the central WCL star on the surrounding ISM and evaluate whether this star can be a primary source of heating. We adopt the model from
Hankins et al. 2019
for calculating the heating profile of the ISM. This method provides an estimate of the maximum temperature of the dust at a given distance from a compact heating source, which in our case is the central WCL star. The modelled temperature profile can then be compared with the dust temperature map from Section
III.2.2
to infer the astrophysical properties of the ISM. In our calculation, we assume the dust grains are in thermal equilibrium with the incident and unextinguished radiation field emanating from the stellar source having luminosity
L_{\star}
. The dust temperature as a function of distance
l
from the source is computed as

T_{d}(l)=\left(\frac{L_{\star}}{16\pi\sigma l^{2}}\frac{Q_{\rm UV}}{Q_{d}^{\prime}}\right)^{1/(\beta+4)},

(4)

where
\sigma
is the Stefan-Boltzmann constant, and
\beta
is the dust emissivity exponent, and we adopt the same value,
\beta=2
, as was used for our dust temperature calculation. The
Q_{\rm UV}
factor is the grain absorption cross section averaged over the spectrum of the incident radiation field, expressed as

Q_{\rm UV}=\frac{\int d\nu B_{\nu}(T)Q_{\rm abs}(\nu)}{\int d\nu B_{\nu}(T)},

(5)

where
Q_{\rm abs}(\nu)
is the frequency-dependent cross-section of the grain.
Q_{d}^{\prime}
is the temperature-independent dust emission efficiency. We adopt
Q_{d}^{\prime}=1.0\times 10^{-6}(a/0.1~{\rm\mu m})
from
Draine 2011
as the expression for graphite grains, since the ISM environment around the WCL star is likely carbon-rich. Furthermore, we adopt a characteristic grain radius of
a=0.1
µm, which is consistent with the previously determined dust grain properties in the GC H
II
regions
(
Lau et al. 2016
;
Hankins et al. 2017
;
Hankins et al. 2019
)
. To account for the distance projection effect along the line-of-sight, we multiply the projected distance by
\sqrt{2}
to obtain a more likely measure of the true distance,
l
. We have also attempted assuming a smaller grain size,
a=0.01
µm, but the temperature profiles are much broader in that case and provide a poorer fit to the observed dust temperature map.

The contours in the lower left panel of Figure
3
display the calculated radial dust temperature profile around the central WCL star, assuming its luminosity to be
10^{39}
erg/s. We observe that the modelled profile shows higher temperatures than the observed in regions close to the source (within the 80 and 100 K contours), as the observed temperature there has a maximum of
\sim 70
K. However, further from the source in the annulus between the 60 and 80 K contours, the observed temperature is somewhat higher than the calculated temperature profile. We also note that the observed temperature in the southern portion of the
60-80
K annulus is much higher than that in the northern portion. The optical depth map in the lower right panel of Figure
3
shows the location of the X-shaped dust ridges to the immediate north of the central WCL star (magenta cross), and a relatively uniform region south of it. Therefore, the elevated density in the dust ridges to the north may cause a higher rate of ISM cooling relative to the heating by the star, assuming the dust is collisionally coupled to the gas. It is also possible that the winds of the WCL star could be slowed down by the high-density ISM, thus inducing the formation of higher-density ridges.

Curiously, we are able to locate the central WCL star as a compact source in
Spitzer
/IRAC and 2MASS images, but found no counterparts in the FORCAST source catalogue. We conclude that the high temperature of the dust that has formed in the winds of the WCL star makes its SED peak at much shorter wavelengths than those probed by FORCAST, and the Rayleigh-Jeans tail of the SED is therefore below the FORCAST sensitivity limit.

(
Geballe et al. 2019
;
Clark et al. 2021
)
. This star is located immediately south of the density ridge distributed along
b=-0.09
∘

7

We suggest that the stellar wind of the WCL star could be responsible for compressing and forming the dense ridge to the north and for creating the cavity to the south, where the wind is relatively unimpeded. Moreover, if the density ridges are the impact front where the high-velocity wind from the central WCL star hits and compresses the ISM, it is possible that the local electrons can accelerate in the ridges via diffusive shock acceleration
(
Drury 1983
;
Rosner & Bodo 1996
;
Malkov & Drury 2001
, see)
. If enough electrons are accelerated to relativistic speeds, then they could contribute synchrotron emission to the existing thermal emission from the ridges, and thereby increase the spectral index.

To investigate whether there might be direct interactions between these density ridges, we have checked whether any of the intersection sites have higher radio brightness than the sum of the brightnesses of the overlapping density ridges, which can be due to their collisional shocks. However, none of the intersection sites showed such an excess, so we conclude that these ridges are probably located at different line-of-sight positions.

IV.2.2
Total IR Luminosity

To constrain the stellar population residing inside the Sgr C H
II
region, we perform an SED analysis using the FORCAST data together with other IR surveys, to estimate the total IR luminosity of the H
II
region and thereby constrain the stellar population in this region.

We use a wide range of images, including the images from
Spitzer
/IRAC at 3.6, 4.5, 5.8, and 8 µm
(
Stolovy et al. 2006
)
, ISOGAL at 15 µm
(
Omont et al. 2003
;
Schuller et al. 2006
)
, FORCAST at 25 and 37 µm
(
Hankins et al. 2020
;
Cotera et al. 2024
)
, and the
Herschel
/PACS Hi-GAL survey at 70 and 160 µm
(
Molinari et al. 2010
;
Molinari et al. 2016
)
. To correct for dust extinction at the short-wavelength end, we again apply the interpolated
\tau_{9.8}
extinction map (provided by J. Simpson; private communication) to images with wavelengths shorter than 37 µm. Following the extinction law of
Chiar & Tielens 2006
, we adopt
\tau_{8}/\tau_{9.8}=0.2969,\tau_{15}/\tau_{9.8}=0.336
as conversion factors for
Spitzer
/IRAC and ISOGAL images, respectively. The conversion factors for FORCAST wavelengths are stated in Section
III.2.2
. Given that extinction at 70 µm is uncertain and that it is of less importance in the FIR regime, we do not attempt to apply an extinction correction to the 70 and 160 µm images. To ensure a uniform image resolution, we convolve all included images to match the lowest resolution of the Hi-GAL 70 µm image, 12″.

For this calculation, we perform the photometry computation using the
Photutils
package
(
Bradley et al. 2024
)
with an aperture centred at
l=359.434

∘
,
b=-0.09
and having a 0.21
∘
radius (cyan circle in Figure
8
). Given the unique and complex environment of Sgr C, we perform careful background subtraction at each wavelength using standard photometry techniques. Namely, we select an annulus that is relatively noise-dominated compared to the H
II
region, calculate the median flux per pixel within that annulus, and then subtract that value from each pixel within the central aperture of the H
II
region. We use an annulus with a 0.3
∘
outer radius and a 0.25
∘
inner radius. However, due to the variable noise properties of individual FORCAST frames, for FORCAST only, we use a much smaller annulus with 0.25
∘
outer radius and a 0.21
∘
inner radius to restrict background estimation within each frame. Additionally, for the
Spitzer
/IRAC 3.6 and 4.5 images, we replace pixels that are higher than a
3\sigma
limit from the mean with the median flux density value within the aperture, such that we avoid the contamination from the Rayleigh-Jeans emission from the abundant stellar sources.

We use the
DustEM
package
(
Compiègne et al. 2013
)
to model the extracted fluxes.
DustEM
is equipped with the capability to model very small transiently-heated grains, including PAHs, which allows for the most inclusive and complete luminosity estimation at wavelengths shorter than 8 µm. As defined by
Compiègne et al. 2011
, this model includes four grain species. We list them in terms of their mass fractions
4
4

4

A

M_{H}
.

,

V
Conclusions

This paper presents an analysis of MIR-bright regions in the Sgr C complex using the FORCAST Galactic Center Survey data in combination with imaging data from other observatories, and taking advantage of the absence of bright source saturation with FORCAST. The study focuses largely on the Sgr C H
II
region, while covering other adjacent MIR-bright sources such as the G359.43+0.02 YSO cluster and Source C. We summarize our findings with the following:

•

We find that dust in the MIR bright regions has a dust temperature range of
50-90
K. The Sgr C H
II
region has a peak temperature of approximately
70
K near the central WCL star, and gradually decreases to
\sim
55 K towards its periphery, especially towards the west.

•

The typical 37 µm optical depth of the Sgr C H
II
region is around
10^{-2}
. We find several density ridges that have an elevated optical depth of
\sim 10^{-1}
in the central portion of the H
II
region. Additionally, the western portion of the H
II
region exhibits higher optical depth than the central and eastern portions. The optical depth map does not exhibit any obvious correlation with the dust temperature map.

•

We also survey other intriguing MIR bright sources in the Sgr C region. The G359.44-0.10 EGO has the lowest temperature (
\lesssim 50
K) and highest optical depth (
\gtrsim 0.5
) in the Sgr C region, confirming previous reports that they are dense clouds that favour star formation
(
Kendrew et al. 2013
;
Crowe et al. 2025
)
. The sources found in the G359.43+0.02 YSO cluster, on the periphery of the H
II
region, and along the Galactic plane are mostly stellar sources with
Spitzer
/IRAC counterparts. They have higher temperatures and lower optical depths than the H
II
region. As for Source C, we suggest that it is likely a low-density region of ionized gas, possibly due to the compact source in the high temperature “head” with a
Spitzer
/IRAC counterpart source.

•

We find multiple dust emission ridges inside the Sgr C H
II
region with counterparts in the radio continuum, which is also reported by
Bally et al. 2025
using JWST-NIRCam images. The dust ridges have an elevated optical depth compared to their surrounding area, but do not exhibit significant temperature variation from their surroundings. We postulate that these dust ridges are enhanced density ridges of the ionized gas and embedded dust caused by the winds of the stellar population within the region, shocking the ambient ionized gas. Many compact and extended MIR sources are found as local flux peaks along these ridges. We suggest that the wind from the central WCL star may accelerate the free electrons in the ridges via diffusive shock, creating nonthermal emission in the ridges as suggested by the radio spectral index map reported by
Bally et al. 2025
.

•

On the periphery of the Sgr C H
II
region, we found that one of the densest dust ridges immediately borders the PDR signified by the [C
II
] shell
(
Riquelme-Vásquez et al. 2025, submitted
)
. Together with the observation that there is a magnetic field largely tangential to the surface of the H
II
region
(
Zhao et al. 2025
)
, we postulate that such a magnetic field can confine the non-thermal synchrotron radiation within the boundary of the H
II
region, subject to further confirmation of the radio spectral index.

•

From fitting a
DustEM
SED model, we find the total MIR luminosity of the Sgr C H
II
region to be
(1.40\pm 0.19)\times 10^{6}L_{\odot}
and a dust mass of
5.4\pm 0.7M_{\odot}
. The central WCL star, albeit contributing to the high MIR luminosity of the region, is unlikely to be the sole producer of a luminosity of this order of magnitude. Our result implies the presence of additional massive stars within the H
II
region.

Past and ongoing star formation is clearly central to the phenomenology of the Sgr C H
II
region and its surroundings, but a few important parts of the story remain to be elucidated. Apart from the two known WCL stars in the region, we can infer that more – so far unidentified – massive stars remain to be found to account for the total luminosity. The suggestion by
Riquelme-Vásquez et al. 2025, submitted
that it might be necessary to invoke a past supernova to account for the expansion of the [C
II
] shell around the H
II
region could reveal the fate of at least one massive star. And finally, the protostellar objects in the EGO herald the next phase in the evolution of this key region lying at the collision site of an expanding H
II
region and a molecular cloud, or in a larger scale, the shocked interaction zone of the Central Molecular Zone and the Galactic-bar-induced inflow from the Galaxy
(
Tress et al. 2020
, e.g.,)
.
