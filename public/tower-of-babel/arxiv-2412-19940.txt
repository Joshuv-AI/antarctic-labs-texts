Chemotaxis and Reactions in Anomalous Diffusion Dynamics
De Andrade, Crystianne L., and Kiselev, Alexander A

Abstract
. Chemotaxis and reactions are fundamental processes in biology, often intricately intertwined. Chemotaxis, in particular, can be crucial in maintaining and accelerating a reaction. In this work, we extend the investigation initiated by [ 17 ] by examining the impact of chemotactic attraction on reproduction and other processes in the context of anomalous diffusion of gamete densities. For that, we consider a partial differential equation, with a single density function, that includes advection, chemotaxis, absorbing reaction, and diffusion, incorporating the fractional Laplacian Λ α \Lambda^{\alpha} . The inclusion of the fractional Laplacian is motivated by experimental evidence supporting the efficacy of anomalous diffusion models, particularly in scenarios with sparse targets. The fractional Laplacian accommodates the nonlocal nature of superdiffusion processes, providing a more accurate representation than traditional diffusion models. Our proposed model represents a step forward in refining mathematical descriptions of cellular behaviors influenced by chemotactic cues.

1 Introduction

Chemotaxis is the process through which cells convert chemical signals into motion behavior
[
1
,
24
]
.
The mathematical modeling of chemotaxis in cellular systems traces back to the works of
[
15
]
and
[
23
]
, with the Keller-Segel model standing out as the most extensively studied one.
This is a system of partial differential equations describing the chemically induced movement of cells with density
\rho=\rho(x,t)
towards increasing concentrations of a chemical substance with density
c=c(x,t)
, i. e.,
\rho
moves along the gradient of the chemical:

(1.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}\rho=\Delta\rho-\chi\nabla\cdot\left(\rho\nabla c\right)&x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},&\quad t>0,\\
&\tau\partial_{t}c=\kappa\Delta c+\zeta\rho-\gamma c\qquad&x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},&\quad t>0,\\
&\rho(t=0)=\rho_{0},\quad c(t=0)=c_{0}\qquad&x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.&\;\end{aligned}\right.

This system incorporates, in the right-hand side of the first equation, the diffusion of the cells, representing a random component of motion, and a chemotactic flux of advective type, where
\chi
stands for the chemotactic sensitivity.
The second equation accounts for the diffusion, production, and consumption (or degradation) of the chemical signal, known as the chemoattractant, as it is emitted by the cells, diffused in the environment, and degraded at a rate proportional to the local concentration.
As the biological background suggests, the model ensures non-negative solutions for cell density and chemical concentration under non-negative initial conditions.
Moreover, as this model does not incorporate birth or death processes, describing exclusively cell motion, the total cell population, represented by the
L^{1}
norm of
\rho
, is conserved over time:
\|\rho_{0}\|_{L^{1}}=\|\rho(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{1}}

[
1
,
6
,
24
]
.

The parabolic-elliptic Keller-Segel model arises from the assumption that the production and diffusion of the chemical occur much faster than other time scales in the problem
\left(\kappa\sim\zeta\gg 1\right)
. Additionally, with the observational time scale being much shorter than the speed at which the chemical signal
c
degrades,
\gamma
can be set equal to zero. Under these conditions, the second equation of the Keller-Segel system, (
1.1
), simplifies to the elliptic form:
-\Delta c(x,t)=\rho(x,t)
.

This simplification results in a single equation, since the gradient of the chemical concentration can be expressed as function of
\rho

[
2
,
17
,
16
]
. That is,

\nabla c(x,t)=\nabla\left(-\Delta\right)^{-1}\rho(x,t)=-\frac{\Gamma\left((d+2)/2\right)}{\pi^{d/2}d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{x-y}{|x-y|^{d}}\rho(y,t)\mathrm{~d}y.

Thus, the classical parabolic-elliptic Keller-Segel model takes the following form

(1.2)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}\rho=\Delta\rho-\chi\nabla\cdot\left(\rho\nabla\left(-\Delta\right)^{-1}\rho\right),&x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},&\quad t>0,\\
&\rho(x,0)=\rho_{0}(x)\qquad&x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.&\;\end{aligned}\right.

Several variations of the Keller-Segel model have been proposed to explore chemotaxis in conjunction with other phenomena, such as biological reactions.
In that context,
[
17
]
investigated the role of chemotaxis in enhancing biological reactions, focusing on coral broadcast spawning, a fertilization strategy adopted by various benthic invertebrates (sea urchins, anemones, corals). In this process, males and females release sperm and egg gametes into the surrounding flow, and the chemotaxis appears as the eggs release a chemical that attracts sperm.

The mathematical model analyzed by
[
17
]
is a modification of (
1.2
), including advection and absorbing reaction, in which the approximation to one equation was based on the assumption that the chemical diffusion is much faster than the gamete densities diffusion.
Another simplification in this model is the assumption that sperm and egg gametes have identical densities, leading to a single density function
\rho\geq 0
. With these assumptions, the model is represented by the following equation:

(1.3)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}\rho+u\cdot\nabla\rho=\Delta\rho-\chi\nabla\cdot\left(\rho\nabla\left(-\Delta\right)^{-1}\rho\right)-\epsilon\rho^{q},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\quad t>0,\\
&\rho(x,0)=\rho_{0}(x),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\quad d\geq 2,\end{aligned}\right.

where
u=u(x,t)
is a given vector field modeling the ambient ocean flow (
u
is divergence-free, regular, and prescribed, independent of
\rho
),
and the term
\left(-\epsilon\rho^{q}\right)
models the reaction (fertilization), with
the parameter
\epsilon

They pointed out that the value of
\epsilon
is small due to the fact that an egg gets fertilized only if a sperm attaches to a certain limited area on its surface.
The model does not track the production of reaction – fertilized eggs.

In this work, we extend the investigation initiated in
[
17

by examining the impact of chemotactic attraction on reproduction processes in the context of anomalous diffusion of gamete densities.
To this purpose, we analyze the behavior of the total fraction of unfertilized eggs over time

In this equation,

is the fractional Laplacian, which can be defined in Fourier variables as

(1.6)

\widehat{(\Lambda^{\alpha}f)}(\xi)=\widehat{\left[\left(-\Delta\right)^{\alpha/2}f\right]}(\xi)=|\xi|^{\alpha}\widehat{f}(\xi),\;\;\forall\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\text{and }\;\alpha\in(0,2],

for a sufficiently regular function

For

\Lambda^{\alpha}f(x)=c_{d,\alpha}P.V.\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{f(x)-f(y)}{|x-y|^{d+\alpha}}\mathrm{~d}y,

where

Note that, in standard diffusion, the use of the Laplacian (
\Lambda^{2}=-\Delta
) and the first-order time derivative (
\partial_{t}
) results in a Gaussian process for the particle motion. At the microscopic level, this leads to the mean squared displacement (MSD)
2
2

2

The MSD is a measure of the deviation of the position of a particle concerning a reference position over time.
of a particle being a linear function of time:
\left\langle x^{2}\right\rangle\sim t
.
In contrast, anomalous diffusion is characterized by an MSD that increases nonlinearly; it grows either slower (subdiffusion) or faster (superdiffusion) for large times than that in a Gaussian process. At the macroscopic level, such processes are adequately described by fractional differential equations, which involve fractional derivatives in time and/or space.
Specifically, superdiffusion processes are modeled using equations with fractional derivative in space.
In that case, the standard Laplace operator is replaced by the fractional one, which is nonlocal, since it is an integrodifferential operator
[
20
,
12
,
14
]
.

In the chemotaxis phenomena, many experiments have shown that anomalous diffusion models (superdiffusion type of behavior) can offer a superior fit to experimental data, especially when chemoattractants, food, or other targets are sparse or rare in the environment.
At a microscopic level, superdiffusion processes can be described by Lévy flights or Lévy walk, where the length of particle jumps follows some heavy-tailed distribution, reflecting the long-range interactions among particles, which means that large jumps dominate the more common smaller ones. This non-negligible probability for long positional jumps in a biological context translates into cells/organisms persisting in a single direction of motion for a substantially longer time than in typical random walks
[
9
,
10
,
12
]
.

Nonetheless, while chemotaxis is well-established within the framework of classical diffusion as a mechanism that enhances and sustains reaction processes (e.g.,
[
17
]
), its influence on anomalous diffusion remains largely unexplored.
It is worth highlighting that anomalous diffusion processes characterized by a fractional diffusion exponent
\alpha<2
introduce significant challenges, such as nonlocal effects and weaker dissipative properties compared to classical diffusion
\alpha=2
, which makes the system more susceptible to blow-ups.
This research aims to bridge the gap in understanding how nonlocality and fractional dynamics influence the behavior of chemotactic systems. By extending classical models to include anomalous diffusion, it provides a novel framework to study the interplay between chemotaxis and anomalous transport, with particular emphasis on analyzing the key quantity
m(t)
, through which we can access the fraction that reacts (or the amount of egg gametes that have been successfully fertilized),
\displaystyle m(0)-\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}m(t)
.

&\leq C\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(1+|x-y|^{\beta}\right)\left(1+|y|^{\beta}\right)|\psi(x-y)|\left(|\varphi(y)|+|\nabla\varphi(y)|\right)\mathrm{~d}y\mathrm{~d}x\\
&\leq C\|(1+|\cdot|^{\beta})\psi\|_{L^{1}}\|\varphi\|_{M_{\beta}}\\
&\leq C\left\|\left(1+\sum_{i=1}^{d}|x_{i}|^{\beta}\right)\psi\right\|_{L^{1}}\|\varphi\|_{M_{\beta}}\\
&\leq C\left(\|\psi\|_{L^{1}}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\|x_{i}^{\beta}\psi\|_{L^{1}}\right)\|\varphi\|_{M_{\beta}}.\end{split}

Now, set
\psi=K^{\alpha}_{t}
. From (
3.4
), as
\beta<\alpha
, we obtain

\begin{split}\|K^{\alpha}_{t}*\varphi\|_{M_{\beta}}&\leq C\left(\|K^{\alpha}_{t}\|_{L^{1}}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\|x_{i}^{\beta}K^{\alpha}_{t}\|_{L^{1}}\right)\|\varphi\|_{M_{\beta}}\leq C(1+t^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}})\|\varphi\|_{M_{\beta}}.\end{split}

Next, set
\psi=\nabla K^{\alpha}_{t}
. Again from (
3.4
), as
\beta<\alpha+1
, we find that

\begin{split}\|\nabla K^{\alpha}_{t}*\varphi\|_{M_{\beta}}&\leq C\left(\|\nabla K^{\alpha}_{t}\|_{L^{1}}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\|x_{i}^{\beta}\nabla K^{\alpha}_{t}\|_{L^{1}}\right)\|\varphi\|_{M_{\beta}}\leq C(t^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}+t^{\frac{\beta-1}{\alpha}})\|\varphi\|_{M_{\beta}}.\end{split}

Finally, observe that (
3.7
) is a direct consequence of the preceding calculations and the definition of the norm
\|\,\cdot\,\|_{K_{s,\beta}}
.

Remark 12
.

The constraint on the value of
\beta
is rooted in the characteristic behavior of non-Gaussian Lévy
\alpha
-stable
(0<\alpha<2)
semigroups. These semigroups exhibit decay solely at an algebraic rate of
|x|^{-d-\alpha}
as
|x|\rightarrow\infty

\left(|K^{\alpha}(x)|\leq C(1+|x|)^{-d-\alpha}\right)
. Note that the divergence from this pattern in the case of the Gaussian kernel (

), which undergoes exponential decay.

Lemma 13
.

Let
q
and
s
be positive integers such that
s>d/2+1
, and
\beta\geq 0
. Consider

(3.8)

\displaystyle\|f^{q}-g^{q}\|_{H^{s}}\leq C\left(\|f\|_{H^{s}}^{q-1}+\|g\|_{H^{s}}^{q-1}\right)\|f-g\|_{H^{s}},

(3.9)

\displaystyle\|f^{q}-g^{q}\|_{M_{\beta}}\leq C\left(\|f\|_{H^{s}}^{q-1}+\|g\|_{H^{s}}^{q-1}\right)\|f-g\|_{M_{\beta}},

(3.10)

\displaystyle\|f\nabla\Delta^{-1}f-g\nabla\Delta^{-1}g\|_{H^{s}}\leq C\left(\|f\|_{H^{s}}+\|f\|_{M_{\beta}}+\|g\|_{H^{s}}\right)\left(\|f-g\|_{M_{\beta}}+\|f-g\|_{H^{s}}\right),

(3.11)

\displaystyle\|f\nabla\Delta^{-1}f-g\nabla\Delta^{-1}g\|_{M_{\beta}}\leq C\left(\|f\|_{H^{s}}+\|f\|_{M_{\beta}}+\|g\|_{H^{s}}+\|g\|_{M_{\beta}}\right)\left(\|f-g\|_{M_{\beta}}+\|f-g\|_{H^{s}}\right).

Consequently,

(3.12)

\displaystyle\|f^{q}-g^{q}\|_{K_{s,\beta}}\leq C\left(\|f\|_{K_{s,\beta}}^{q-1}+\|g\|_{K_{s,\beta}}^{q-1}\right)\|f-g\|_{K_{s,\beta}},

(3.13)

\displaystyle\|f\nabla\Delta^{-1}f-g\nabla\Delta^{-1}g\|_{K_{s,\beta}}\leq C\left(\|f\|_{K_{s,\beta}}+\|g\|_{K_{s,\beta}}\right)\|f-g\|_{K_{s,\beta}}.

Proof.

Estimate (

and the fact that

is an algebra when

For (

\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|\nabla\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C\|\varphi\|_{H^{s}}

\|f^{q}-g^{q}\|_{M_{\beta}}\leq\left(\|f^{q-1}+\cdots+g^{q-1}\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|\nabla\left(f^{q-1}+\cdots+g^{q-1}\right)\|_{L^{\infty}}\right)\|f-g\|_{M_{\beta}}.

To establish (

), we start by writing

(3.14)

Now, note that, for any
\varphi\in H^{s}

\|\nabla\Delta^{-1}\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C\displaystyle\underset{\hskip 8.19447ptx\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\operatorname{ess\sup}}\left(\int_{B_{1}(x)}\frac{|\varphi(y)|}{|x-y|^{d-1}}\mathrm{~d}y+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{1}(x)}\frac{|\varphi(y)|}{|x-y|^{d-1}}\mathrm{~d}y\right),

\|\nabla\Delta^{-1}\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C\left(\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|\varphi\|_{L^{1}}\right),

\|\nabla\Delta^{-1}\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C\left(\|\varphi\|_{H^{s}}+\|\varphi\|_{M_{\beta}}\right).

Next, since
s

\|\varphi\|_{H^{s}}=\left(\sum_{|\zeta|\leq s}\|\partial^{\zeta}\varphi\|_{2}^{2}\right)^{1/2}
, we have for
\varphi
,
\psi\in H^{s}

\|\psi\nabla\Delta^{-1}\varphi\|_{H^{s}}\leq\sum_{|\zeta|\leq s}\|\partial^{\zeta}\psi\nabla\Delta^{-1}\varphi\|_{L^{2}}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\zeta+\gamma|\leq s\\
|\gamma|=1\end{subarray}}\|\partial^{\zeta}\left(\psi\partial^{\gamma}\left(\nabla\Delta^{-1}\varphi\right)\right)\|_{L^{2}}=I_{1}+I_{2}.

Using (

I_{1}\leq\sum_{|\zeta|\leq s}\|\partial^{\zeta}\psi\|_{L^{2}}\|\nabla\Delta^{-1}\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\sum_{|\zeta|\leq s}C\|\partial^{\zeta}\psi\|_{L^{2}}\left(\|\varphi\|_{H^{s}}+\|\varphi\|_{M_{\beta}}\right).

By the Sobolev embedding theorem and

Then, from (

\begin{split}\|f\nabla\Delta^{-1}f-g\nabla\Delta^{-1}g\|_{H^{s}}&\leq C\left(\|f\|_{H^{s}}+\|f\|_{M_{\beta}}\right)\|f-g\|_{H^{s}}+C\left(\|f-g\|_{H^{s}}+\|f-g\|_{M_{\beta}}\right)\|g\|_{H^{s}}\\
&\leq C\left(\|f\|_{H^{s}}+\|f\|_{M_{\beta}}+\|g\|_{H^{s}}\right)\left(\|f-g\|_{M_{\beta}}+\|f-g\|_{H^{s}}\right).\end{split}

For (
3.11

(3.18)

\begin{split}\nabla\cdot\left(f\nabla\Delta^{-1}f-g\nabla\Delta^{-1}g\right)&=\left(\nabla f\cdot\nabla\Delta^{-1}f-\nabla g\cdot\nabla\Delta^{-1}g\right)+\left(f^{2}-g^{2}\right)\\

&\quad\leq C\left(\|f\|_{H^{s}}+\|f\|_{M_{\beta}}+\|g\|_{H^{s}}+\|g\|_{M_{\beta}}\right)\left(\|f-g\|_{M_{\beta}}+\|f-g\|_{H^{s}}\right).\end{split}

Finally, note that (

4 Global Existence of smooth solutions

To establish the global existence of smooth solutions, our initial step involves the construction of a local solution in the Banach space
X_{s,\beta}^{T}\equiv C\left(K_{s,\beta},[0,T]\right)
, where
T>0
is chosen sufficiently small. To this purpose, from (
3.1
), let us denote

B_{t}(\rho)\equiv\int_{0}^{t}K^{\alpha}_{t-r}*\left(-\nabla\cdot(u\rho)-\epsilon\rho^{q}+\chi\nabla\cdot\left(\rho\nabla\Delta^{-1}\rho\right)\right)\mathrm{d}r.

Lemma 14
.

Assume
u\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,\infty))
satisfies
\nabla\cdot u=0
. Let
s
and
q
be positive integers, where
s>d/2+1
, and let
\alpha
and
\beta
be such that
\alpha\in(1,2]
, and
0\leq\beta<\alpha
. Then, for any
f,g\in X_{s,\beta}^{T}
, we have

\|B_{T}(f)-B_{T}(g)\|_{X_{s,\beta}^{T}}\leq\Theta\|f-g\|_{X_{s,\beta}^{T}},

where, for
T\leq 1
, we have

\Theta\leq C(d,q,\beta,\epsilon,\chi)\max_{0\leq t\leq T}\left(\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{C^{s}}+\|f(\cdot,t)\|_{K_{s,\beta}}^{q-1}+\|g(\cdot,t)\|_{K_{s,\beta}}^{q-1}+\|f(\cdot,t)\|_{K_{s,\beta}}+\|g(\cdot,t)\|_{K_{s,\beta}}\right)T^{\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha}}.

Proof.

Consider

B_{t}(f)-B_{t}(g)=\int_{0}^{t}K^{\alpha}_{t-r}*\left(\nabla(u(f-g))-\epsilon\left(f^{q}-g^{q}\right)+\chi\nabla\cdot\left(f\nabla\Delta^{-1}f-g\nabla\Delta^{-1}g\right)\right)\mathrm{d}r.

From
Lemmas

11
and
13
, and the fact that
\alpha>1
, we obtain

\begin{split}\|B_{t}(f)-B_{t}(g)\|_{K_{s,\beta}}&\leq C\int_{0}^{t}\left[\left((t-r)^{-1/\alpha}+(t-r)^{(\beta-1)/\alpha}\right)\left(\|u\|_{C^{s}}+\|f\|_{K_{s,\beta}}+\|g\|_{K_{s,\beta}}\right)\right.\\
&\hskip 51.21504pt\left.+\left(1+(t-r)^{\beta/\alpha}\right)\left(\|f\|_{K_{s,\beta}}^{q-1}+\|g\|_{K_{s,\beta}}^{q-1}\right)\right]\|f-g\|_{K_{s,\beta}}\mathrm{d}r\\
&\leq C\left[\left(t^{\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha}}+t^{\frac{\alpha-1+\beta}{\alpha}}\right)\max_{0\leq r\leq t}\left(\|u\|_{C^{s}}+\|f\|_{K_{s,\beta}}+\|g\|_{K_{s,\beta}}\right)\right.\\
&\hskip 51.21504pt\left.+\left(t+t^{\frac{\beta+\alpha}{\alpha}}\right)\max_{0\leq r\leq t}\left(\|f\|_{K_{s,\beta}}^{q-1}+\|g\|_{K_{s,\beta}}^{q-1}\right)\right]\max_{0\leq r\leq t}\|f-g\|_{K_{s,\beta}},\end{split}

and considering
T\leq 1
we can neglect the higher powers of
T
, yielding the estimate for
\Theta
.

\Box

In a standard way, the existence of a local solution is implied by
Lemma

14
through the contraction mapping principle. This leads us to formulate the following theorem.

Theorem 15
.

Consider positive integers
q
and
s
such that
s>d/2+1
. Let
\alpha\in(1,2]
,
0\leq\beta<\alpha
, and assume
u\in C^{\infty}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,\infty)\right)
with
\nabla\cdot u=0
. Additionally, suppose
\rho_{0}\in K_{s,\beta}
. Then, there exists
T=T\left(q,d,u,s,\epsilon,\chi,\alpha,\|\rho_{0}\|_{K_{s,\beta}}\right)
such that equation (
1.5
) has a unique mild solution
\rho\in X_{s,\beta}^{T}
satisfying
\rho(x,0)=\rho_{0}(x)
.

Remark 16
.

Higher regularity of the solution in space and time – implying
\rho\in C\left(H^{m},(0,T]\right)
for every
m>0
– follows from
Theorem

15
and parabolic regularity estimates applied iteratively
[
11
,
7
]
.

Remark 17
.

Note that, from
Lemma

14
, the value of
\Theta
depends on the
H^{s}
and
M_{\beta}
norms of functions in
X_{\beta,s}^{T}
. Therefore, under the conditions of
Theorem

15
, if there is a control over the growth of the
H^{s}
and
M_{\beta}
norms of the solution, the time existence of the solution can be extended through iterative applications of local results. Hence, to prove global existence of smooth solutions, we can establish a global a priori estimate on
\|\rho(\cdot,t)\|_{H^{s}}
and
\|\rho(\cdot,t)\|_{M_{\beta}}
, and then the local solution can be extended globally to
X_{\beta,s}^{T}
with arbitrary
T
.

To prove the bounds on
M_{\beta}
and
H^{s}
norms of the solution, we first establish control of the
L^{\infty}
norm.

Lemma 18
.

Assume that
\rho
is the local mild solution guaranteed by
Theorem

15
, and
q>2
. Then,

(4.1)

\|\rho(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq N_{0}\equiv\max\left((\chi/\epsilon)^{\frac{1}{q-2}},\|\rho_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}}\right)

for all
0\leq t\leq T
.

Proof.

For the sake of completeness, we present the same proof found in
[
17
, Lemma 5.6]
, this time addressing the fractional Laplacian.

Suppose that the statement is false and that there exist
N_{1}>N_{0}
and
0<t_{1}\leq T
such that
\left\|\rho\left(x,t_{1}\right)\right\|_{L^{\infty}}=N_{1}
is attained for the first time, i. e.,
\left|\rho\left(x,t\right)\right|\leq\left.N_{1}\right.
holds for every
x
and
0\leq t\leq t_{1}
.

In this case, there exists
x_{0}
such that
\rho\left(x_{0},t_{1}\right)=N_{1}
. Indeed, let
x_{k}
be a sequence such that
\rho\left(x_{k},t_{1}\right)\rightarrow N_{1}
as
k\rightarrow\infty
. If
x_{k}
has finite accumulation points, one of them can be labeled
x_{0}
and, by continuity,
\rho\left(x_{0},t_{1}\right)=N_{1}
.
Otherwise, if
x_{k}\rightarrow\infty
, we can consider a subsequence and assume the unit balls around
x_{k}
,
B_{1}\left(x_{k}\right)
, are disjoint; then, using a version of Poincare inequality (e.g.,
[
28
]
), we see that
\|\rho-\bar{\rho}\|_{L^{\infty}\left(B_{1}\left(x_{k}\right)\right)}^{2}\leq C\|\rho\|_{H^{s}\left(B_{1}\left(x_{k}\right)\right)}^{2}
and, since
\sum_{k}\|\rho\|_{H^{s}\left(B_{1}\left(x_{k}\right)\right)}^{2}\leq C\left(t_{1}\right)<\infty
,

\bar{\rho}_{k}\equiv\frac{1}{\left|B_{1}\left(x_{k}\right)\right|}\int_{B_{1}\left(x_{k}\right)}\rho dx\xrightarrow{k\rightarrow\infty}N_{1},

which is impossible as
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\rho(x)|\left(1+|x|^{\beta}\right)dx\leq C\left(t_{1}\right)
.

Then, considering
x_{0}
such that
\rho\left(x_{0},t_{1}\right)=N_{1}
is a maximum (the case of a minimum follows an analogous procedure), we obtain

\Lambda^{\alpha}\rho(x_{0},t_{1})=c_{d,\alpha}P.V.\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\rho(x_{0},t_{1})-\rho(y,t_{1})}{|x_{0}-y|^{\alpha+d}}\mathrm{~d}y\geq 0\;(\leq 0)\quad\text{ for all }y\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

implying

\displaystyle\left.\partial_{t}\rho\left(x_{0},t\right)\right|_{t=t_{1}}=

\displaystyle(u\cdot\nabla)\rho\left(x_{0},t_{1}\right)-\Lambda^{\alpha}\rho\left(x_{0},t_{1}\right)+\chi\nabla\rho\left(x_{0},t_{1}\right)\cdot\nabla\Delta^{-1}\rho\left(x_{0},t_{1}\right)

\displaystyle+\chi\rho\left(x_{0},t_{1}\right)^{2}-\epsilon\rho\left(x_{0},t_{1}\right)^{q}\leq\rho\left(x_{0},t_{1}\right)^{2}\left(\chi-\epsilon\rho\left(x_{0},t_{1}\right)^{q-2}\right).

Therefore, by the assumption on
N_{1}
, we have
\partial_{t}\rho\left(x_{0},t_{1}\right)<0
, which contradicts our choice of
t_{1}
.

Next, we prove an upper bound on the growth of the
M_{\beta}
norm of the solution.

Lemma 19
.

Assume that
\rho
is the local mild solution guaranteed by
Theorem

15
. Then, on the interval of existence, we have the following bound for the growth of the
M_{\beta}
norm of the solution

(4.2)

\|\rho(\cdot,t)\|_{M_{\beta}}\leq C\left(1+t^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}}\right)\|\rho_{0}\|_{M_{\beta}}\exp\left(C\int_{0}^{t}\left[\epsilon\left(1+\left(t-r\right)^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}}\right)\|\rho(\cdot,r)\|_{L^{\infty}}^{q-1}\right.\right.\\
\left.{\color[rgb]{1,1,1}\int_{0}^{t}}\left.+\left(\left(t-r\right)^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}+\left(t-r\right)^{\frac{\beta-1}{\alpha}}\right)\left(\|u(\cdot,r)\|_{C^{1}}+\|\rho(\cdot,r)\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|\rho(\cdot,r)\|_{L^{1}}\right)\right]\mathrm{d}r\right).

Proof.

Note that the following estimates hold:

(4.3)

\displaystyle\|u\rho\|_{M_{\beta}}

\displaystyle\leq\|u\|_{C^{1}}\|\rho\|_{M_{\beta}},

\displaystyle\|\rho^{q}\|_{M_{\beta}}

\displaystyle\leq C\|\rho\|_{L^{\infty}}^{q-1}\|\rho\|_{M_{\beta}},

\displaystyle\|\rho\nabla\Delta^{-1}\rho\|_{M_{\beta}}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\|\rho\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|\rho\|_{L^{1}}\right)\|\rho\|_{M_{\beta}},

\begin{split}\|\rho\nabla\Delta^{-1}\rho\|_{M_{\beta}}&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left[|\nabla\Delta^{-1}\rho|(|\rho(x)|+|\nabla\rho(x)|)+|\rho(x)|^{2}\right]\left(1+|x|^{\beta}\right)\mathrm{~d}x\\
&\leq\left(\|\nabla\Delta^{-1}\rho\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|\rho\|_{L^{\infty}}\right)\|\rho\|_{M_{\beta}}\end{split}

and estimate (
3.15
).

Now, employing estimates (
3.5
) and (
4.3
) to (
3.1
), we obtain

(4.4)

\|\rho(\cdot,t)\|_{M_{\beta}}\leq C\left(1+t^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}}\right)\|\rho_{0}\|_{M_{\beta}}+C\int_{0}^{t}\left[\epsilon\left(1+\left(t-r\right)^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}}\right)\|\rho(\cdot,r)\|_{L^{\infty}}^{q-1}\right.\\
\left.+\left(\left(t-r\right)^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}+\left(t-r\right)^{\frac{\beta-1}{\alpha}}\right)\left(\|u(\cdot,r)\|_{C^{1}}+\|\rho(\cdot,r)\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|\rho(\cdot,r)\|_{L^{1}}\right)\right]\|\rho(\cdot,r)\|_{M_{\beta}}\mathrm{d}r.

Then, applying Gronwall’s inequality to (
4.4
), noticing that
(1+t^{\beta/\alpha})
is a non-decreasing function on

, we obtain (
4.2
).

Now we prove uniform in time bounds on the
H^{s}
norm of the solution.

Lemma 20
.

Let
\rho
be the local mild solution whose existence is guaranteed by
Theorem

15
and suppose
\|\rho(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{\infty}}
does not exceed
N_{0}
for all
0\leq t\leq T
. Then, for
s
even, we have

\|\rho(\cdot,t)\|_{H^{s}}\leq\max\left(\|\rho_{0}\|_{H^{s}},C\left(u,d,q,s,\chi,\epsilon,N_{0}\right)\right).

Proof.

Here we follow the same steps as in
[
17
, Lemma 5.8]
. Applying
\Delta^{s/2}
to (
1.5
), multiplying by
\Delta^{s/2}\rho(x,t)
, and integrating, we find

(4.5)

\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\|\rho\|_{\dot{H}^{s}}^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Delta^{s/2}\left[(u\cdot\nabla)\rho\right]\left(\Delta^{s/2}\rho\right)\mathrm{~d}x-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\Delta^{(s+\alpha)/2}\rho\right)\left(\Delta^{s/2}\rho\right)\mathrm{~d}x\\
-\epsilon\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\Delta^{s/2}\rho^{q}\right)\left(\Delta^{s/2}\rho\right)\mathrm{~d}x+\chi\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left[\nabla\cdot\Delta^{s/2}\left(\rho\nabla\Delta^{-1}\rho\right)\right]\left(\Delta^{s/2}\rho\right)\mathrm{~d}x.

Now, using the fact that
\nabla\cdot u=0
, for the first integral on the right-hand side of (
4.5
), we obtain

\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Delta^{s/2}\left[(u\cdot\nabla)\rho\right]\left(\Delta^{s/2}\rho\right)\mathrm{~d}x\right|\leq C\|u\|_{C^{s}}\|\rho\|_{\dot{H}^{s}}^{2}.

For the second one, we see that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\Delta^{(s+\alpha)/2}\rho\right)\left(\Delta^{s/2}\rho\right)\mathrm{~d}x=\|\rho\|_{\dot{H}^{s+\frac{\alpha}{2}}}^{2}.

The third integral can be written as a sum of a finite number of terms of the form
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}D^{s}\rho\;\prod_{i=1}^{q}D^{s_{i}}\rho\mathrm{d}x
,
s_{1}+\cdots+s_{q}=s
,
s_{i}\geq 0
, where
D^{l}
denotes any partial derivative operator of the
l\text{th}
order. By Hölder’s inequality, we have

\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}D^{s}\rho\prod_{i=1}^{q}D^{s_{i}}\rho\mathrm{~d}x\right|\leq\|D^{s}\rho\|_{L^{2}}\prod_{i=1}^{q}\|D^{s_{i}}\rho\|_{p_{i}},

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{q}1/p_{i}=1/2
. Then, taking
p_{i}=2s/s_{i}
and using Gagliardo-Nirenberg inequality, we obtain

(4.6)

\|D^{s_{i}}\rho\|_{L^{2s/s_{i}}}\leq C\|\rho\|_{L^{\infty}}^{1-\frac{s_{i}}{s}}\|D^{s}\rho\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{s_{i}}{s}},

and hence

\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Delta^{s/2}\rho^{q}\Delta^{s/2}\rho\mathrm{~d}x\right|\leq C\|\rho\|_{L^{\infty}}^{q-1}\|\rho\|_{\dot{H}^{s}}^{2}.

Additionally, to express the fourth integral, we can use a sum of a finite number of terms of the form
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}D^{s}\rho D^{k}\rho D^{s+2-k}\Delta^{-1}\rho\mathrm{~d}x
, where
k=0,\ldots,s
. The only term one gets from the direct differentiation that does not appear to be of this form is
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Delta^{s/2}\rho\nabla\Delta^{s/2}\rho\nabla\Delta^{-1}\rho\mathrm{~d}x
. However, we find that this term is equal to
-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho\left|\Delta^{s/2}\rho\right|^{2}\mathrm{~d}x
through integration by parts.
Now,

\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}D^{s}\rho D^{k}\rho D^{s+2-k}\Delta^{-1}\rho\mathrm{~d}x\right|\leq C\|D^{s}\rho\|_{L^{2}}\|D^{k}\rho\|_{L^{p_{1}}}\|D^{s-k}\rho\|_{L^{p_{2}}},

1/p_{1}+1/p_{2}=1/2
,
p_{2}<\infty
, where we used boundedness of Riesz transforms on
L^{p_{2}}
,
p_{2}<\infty
. Then, setting
p_{1}=\frac{2s}{k}
,
p_{2}=\frac{2s}{s-k}
, and using Gagliardo-Nirenberg inequality (
4.6
) with
s_{i}=

k,s-k
, we get

\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}D^{s}\rho D^{k}\rho D^{s+2-k}\Delta^{-1}\rho\mathrm{~d}x\right|\leq C\|\rho\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\rho\|_{\dot{H}^{s}}^{2}.

Thus, putting all the estimates into (
4.5
), we find that

(4.7)

Note that, as

-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\rho^{q-1}\Lambda^{\alpha}\rho\mathrm{~d}x\leq-\frac{2}{q}\|\Lambda^{\alpha/2}\rho^{q/2}\|_{L^{2}}^{2},

and using integration by parts, we find

(5.16)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\rho^{q-1}\nabla\cdot\left(\rho\nabla\Delta^{-1}\rho\right)\mathrm{~d}x=-(q-1)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\rho^{q-1}\nabla\rho\cdot\nabla\Delta^{-1}\rho\mathrm{~d}x=\frac{q-1}{q}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\rho^{q+1}\mathrm{~d}x.

Furthermore, from interpolation inequality for
L^{p}
norms, we see that

(5.17)

\begin{split}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\rho^{q+1}\mathrm{~d}x=\|\rho^{q/2}\|_{L^{2+\frac{2}{q}}}^{\frac{2(q+1)}{q}}&\leq C(q,\alpha)\|\rho^{q/2}\|_{L^{\frac{4}{2-\alpha}}}^{\frac{4q}{2q-2+\alpha}}\|\rho^{q/2}\|_{L^{\frac{2}{q}}}^{\frac{2}{q}\left(\frac{\alpha q-2+\alpha}{2q-2+\alpha}\right)}.\end{split}

Now, employing Hardy-Littlewood-Sobolev fractional integration theorem, which states that, for
0<\upsilon<d
,
\|\Lambda^{-\upsilon}f\|_{L^{r}}\leq C\|f\|_{L^{p}}
, where
C
is a constant depending only on
p
, and
p
and
r
satisfy
\frac{1}{r}=\frac{1}{p}-\frac{\upsilon}{d}
with
1<p<r<\infty
, we can derive that

(5.18)

\|\rho^{q/2}\|_{L^{\frac{4}{2-\alpha}}}\leq C\|\Lambda^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\rho^{q/2}\|_{L^{2}}.

Moreover, by standard interpolation inequality for
L^{p}
norms, we obtain

(5.19)

\|\rho^{q/2}\|_{L^{\frac{4}{2-\alpha}}}^{\frac{2}{q}}=\|\rho\|_{L^{\frac{2q}{2-\alpha}}}\leq\|\rho\|_{L^{1}}^{\frac{2-\alpha}{2q}}\|\rho\|_{L^{\infty}}^{\frac{2q-2+\alpha}{2q}}.

Then, from (
5.18
) and (
5.19
), we establish that

\|\rho^{q/2}\|_{L^{\frac{4}{2-\alpha}}}^{\frac{4q}{2q-2+\alpha}}=\|\rho^{q/2}\|_{L^{\frac{4}{2-\alpha}}}^{2}\|\rho^{q/2}\|_{L^{\frac{4}{2-\alpha}}}^{\frac{2(2-\alpha)}{2q-2+\alpha}}\leq C\|\Lambda^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\rho^{q/2}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\|\rho\|_{L^{1}}^{\frac{(2-\alpha)^{2}}{2(2q-2+\alpha)}}\|\rho\|_{L^{\infty}}^{\frac{2-\alpha}{2}}.

Applying this to (
5.17
), we can write

\begin{split}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\rho^{q+1}\mathrm{~d}x&\leq C(q,\alpha)\|\Lambda^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\rho^{q/2}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\|\rho\|_{L^{1}}^{\frac{(2-\alpha)^{2}}{2(2q-2+\alpha)}}\|\rho\|_{L^{\infty}}^{\frac{2-\alpha}{2}}\|\rho^{q/2}\|_{L^{\frac{2}{q}}}^{\frac{2}{q}\left(\frac{\alpha q-2+\alpha}{2q-2+\alpha}\right)}\\
&\leq C(q,\alpha)\|\Lambda^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\rho^{q/2}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\|\rho\|_{L^{1}}^{\frac{(2-\alpha)^{2}}{2(2q-2+\alpha)}+\frac{\alpha q-2+\alpha}{2q-2+\alpha}}\|\rho\|_{L^{\infty}}^{\frac{2-\alpha}{2}}.\end{split}

Thus, from (
5.15
), (
5.16
), and the inequality above, we obtain

\begin{split}&q\left(-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\rho^{q-1}\Lambda^{\alpha}\rho\mathrm{d}x+\chi\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\rho^{q-1}\nabla\cdot\left(\rho\nabla\Delta^{-1}\rho\right)\mathrm{d}x\right)\leq\\
&\hskip 142.26378pt\|\Lambda^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\rho^{q/2}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\left(C(q,\alpha)\chi\|\rho\|_{L^{1}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}\left(1+\frac{2}{2q-2+\alpha}\right)}\|\rho\|_{L^{\infty}}^{\frac{2-\alpha}{2}}-2\right).\end{split}

Therefore, we can rewrite (
5.14
) as

(5.20)

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\rho^{q}\mathrm{d}x\leq N_{0}^{\frac{2-\alpha}{2}}\|\Lambda^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\rho^{q/2}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\left(C(q,\alpha)\chi\|\rho\|_{L^{1}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}\left(1+\frac{2}{2q-2+\alpha}\right)}-2N_{0}^{-\frac{2-\alpha}{2}}\right)-q\epsilon\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\rho^{2q-1}\mathrm{d}x,

where
N_{0}
is a uniform in time upper bound of
\|\rho(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{\infty}}
, i. e.,
\|\rho\|_{L^{\infty}\left(\mathbb{R}_{+};L^{\infty}\right)}:=\underset{t\in[0,\infty)}{\operatorname{ess}\sup}\|\rho(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq N_{0}
.

Note that
N_{0}=\max\left((\chi/\epsilon)^{\frac{1}{q-2}},\|\rho_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}}\right)<\infty
according to
Lemma

18
, and
\|\rho(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{1}}
is non-increasing in time.
Now supposing that, at some time

,
C(q,\alpha)\chi\|\rho\|_{L^{1}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}\left(1+\frac{2}{2q-2+\alpha}\right)}
drops below
2N_{0}^{-\frac{2-\alpha}{2}}

(5.21)

C(q,\alpha)\chi\|\rho(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{1}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}\left(1+\frac{2}{2q-2+\alpha}\right)}<2N_{0}^{-\frac{2-\alpha}{2}},\qquad\forall\ t>t_{0}.

Then, for all later times, we get

(5.22)

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\rho^{q}\mathrm{d}x\leq-N_{0}^{\frac{2-\alpha}{2}}\|\Lambda^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\rho^{q/2}\|_{L^{2}}^{2},

and, again from interpolation inequality for
L^{p}
norms, we obtain

\|\rho^{q/2}\|_{L^{2}}\leq C(q,\alpha)\|\rho^{q/2}\|_{L^{\frac{4}{2-\alpha}}}^{\frac{2(q-1)}{2q-2+\alpha}}\|\rho^{q/2}\|_{L^{\frac{2}{q}}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2q-2+\alpha}}.

Hence, from this and (
5.18
), we have

\|\rho^{q/2}\|_{L^{2}}^{1+\frac{\alpha}{2(q-1)}}\leq C(q,\alpha)\|\Lambda^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\rho^{q/2}\|_{L^{2}}\|\rho^{q/2}\|_{L^{2/q}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2(q-1)}},

which, applied to (
5.22
), leads to

(5.23)

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\rho^{q}\mathrm{~d}x\leq-C(q,\alpha)\ N_{0}^{\frac{2-\alpha}{2}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\rho^{q}\mathrm{~d}x\right)^{1+\frac{\alpha}{2}\left(\frac{1}{q-1}\right)}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\rho\mathrm{~d}x\right)^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}\left(\frac{q}{q-1}\right)}.

Then, using the fact that
\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\rho(x,t)\mathrm{~d}x
is monotone decreasing and introducing

, we can represent (

z^{\prime}(t)\leq-C(q,\alpha)\ N_{0}^{\frac{2-\alpha}{2}}z(t)^{1+\frac{\alpha}{2}\left(\frac{1}{q-1}\right)}\|\rho(\cdot,t_{*})\|_{L^{1}}^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}\left(\frac{q}{q-1}\right)}

for

.
Solving this differential inequality, we find

z(t)\leq\left(\frac{C(q,\alpha)\ N_{0}^{\frac{2-\alpha}{2}}(t-t_{*})}{\|\rho(\cdot,t_{*})\|_{L^{1}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}\left(\frac{q}{q-1}\right)}}+\frac{1}{\|\rho(\cdot,t_{*})\|_{L^{q}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}\left(\frac{q}{q-1}\right)}}\right)^{-\frac{2}{\alpha}\left(q-1\right)},

implying

(5.24)

\|\rho(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{q}}^{q}\leq\min\left(\|\rho(\cdot,t_{*})\|_{L^{q}}^{q},C(q,\alpha)\ N_{0}^{-\frac{2-\alpha}{\alpha}(q-1)}(t-t_{*})^{-\frac{2}{\alpha}\left(q-1\right)}\|\rho(\cdot,t_{*})\|_{L^{1}}^{q}\right).

Next, setting
p=q
and

as in the proof of

26
, we can certify that

(5.25)

\frac{\|\rho(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{q}}}{\|\rho(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{1}}}\leq\frac{\|\rho(\cdot,t_{0})\|_{L^{q}}}{\|\rho(\cdot,t_{0})\|_{L^{1}}}.

Indeed, with chemotaxis, an inequality equivalent to (
5.9
) can be deduced from (
5.20
).
This leads to an inequality equivalent to (

q^{-1}\|\rho(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{1}}\|\Lambda^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\rho^{q/2}(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\left(C(q,\alpha)\chi\|\rho(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{1}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}\left(1+\frac{2}{2q-2+\alpha}\right)}\|\rho(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{\infty}}^{\frac{2-\alpha}{2}}-2\right)\\
+\epsilon\left(\|\rho(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{q}}^{2q}-\|\rho(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho^{2q-1}\mathrm{~d}x\right),

which, based on assumption (

.

Now, for every
\tau>0
and

\displaystyle\int_{t_{*}}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho(x,t)^{q}\mathrm{~d}x\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{t_{*}}^{t_{*}+\tau}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho(x,t)^{q}\mathrm{~d}x\mathrm{d}t+\int_{t_{*}+\tau}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho(x,t)^{q}\mathrm{~d}x\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq C(q,\alpha)

where we use (
5.24

(5.26)

\|\rho(\cdot,t_{*})\|_{L^{1}}-\|\rho(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{1}}\leq C(q,\alpha)\epsilon\|\rho(\cdot,t_{*})\|_{L^{1}}^{q}\left(\frac{\|\rho(\cdot,t_{0})\|_{L^{\infty}}^{q-1}}{\|\rho(\cdot,t_{0})\|_{L^{1}}^{q-1}}\tau+N_{0}^{-\frac{2-\alpha}{\alpha}(q-1)}\tau^{\frac{2+\alpha-2q}{\alpha}}\right)

for all
t>t_{*}>t_{0}
, and
\tau>0
.
Subsequently, choosing

to minimize expression (

), we find, for every

, that

\|\rho(\cdot,t_{*})\|_{L^{1}}-\|\rho(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{1}}\leq C(q,\alpha)\epsilon N_{0}^{-\frac{2-\alpha}{2}}\|\rho(\cdot,t_{*})\|_{L^{1}}^{q}\left(\frac{\|\rho(\cdot,t_{0})\|_{L^{\infty}}}{\|\rho(\cdot,t_{0})\|_{L^{1}}}\right)^{\frac{2(q-1)-\alpha}{2}}.

(5.27)

\inf_{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\rho(x,t)\mathrm{~d}x\geq\min\left(\frac{1}{2}\|\rho(\cdot,t_{0})\|_{L^{1}},C(q,\alpha)\ N_{0}^{\frac{2-\alpha}{2\left(q-1\right)}}\epsilon^{-\frac{1}{q-1}}\left(\frac{\|\rho(\cdot,t_{0})\|_{L^{1}}}{\|\rho(\cdot,t_{0})\|_{L^{\infty}}}\right)^{\frac{q-(1+\alpha/2)}{q-1}}\right).

5.27

18
,

N_{0}^{\frac{2-\alpha}{2\left(q-1\right)}}\|\rho(\cdot,t_{0})\|_{L^{\infty}}^{-\frac{q-(1+\alpha/2)}{q-1}}\geq N_{0}^{-\frac{q-2}{q-1}}>0.

Additionally, if there exists

.

Upper bound for the total fraction of unfertilized eggs

For the classical Keller-Segel equation (equation (
1.5
) with
\alpha=2
and
\epsilon=u=0
) a virial argument has been employed to demonstrate the existence of blowing-up solutions. This argument involves studying the evolution of the second-order moment
m_{2}(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x-x_{0}|^{2}\rho(x,t)\mathrm{d}x
,
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Assuming the regularity of the solution,
one can derive a differential inequality of the form

(5.28)

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}m_{2}(t)\leq f(m(t),m_{2}(t)).

It can be shown that for
d\geq 2
and large (in some sense) initial data, this inequality leads to negative second moment of the solution, leading to a contradiction since the initial data and hence the solution are positive.
This implies that the solution must lose regularity before the time
m_{2}
would turn negative
(see e.g.
[
24
,
19
,
4
]
).

For the classical Keller-Segel equation, since
m(t)
remains constant over time (
m(t)=m_{0}
), for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the function
f
in (
5.28
) satisfies:

(5.29)

f(m_{0},m_{2}(t))=\begin{cases}\displaystyle 4m_{0}\left(1-\frac{\chi}{8\pi}m_{0}\right),&d=2,\\
\displaystyle 2m_{0}\left(-1+C\frac{\chi m_{2}(t)}{(\chi m_{0})^{\frac{d}{d-2}}}\right),&d>2.\end{cases}

Thus, for
d=2
, if
m_{0}>8\pi/\chi
,
m_{2}(t)
would become negative in finite time, which is impossible since
\rho
is nonnegative.
Similarly, for
d>2
, if
\chi m_{2}(0)
is sufficiently small compared to
(\chi m_{0})^{\frac{d}{d-2}}
,
m_{2}(t)
decreases for all
t
, leading to a contradiction.

[
17
]
applied this idea to establish an upper bound for
m(t)
in equation (
1.5
) with
\alpha=2
and
d=2,
and integer
q>2
. They observed that when the reaction is present, solutions will remain globally regular. But now the analog
of (
5.28
) implies that
m(t)
must decay and quickly become small (at least if
\chi
is large), or otherwise the second moment will turn negative. An interesting feature of their result is that the upper bound on
m(t)
as well as the time scale
when it is achieved are independent of
\epsilon.
This is the “shadow" of the singularity formation that takes place if
\epsilon=0:

even if
\epsilon
is very small, the dynamics will concentrate significant mass in a small region and the size of
\rho
there
will drive the reaction.

The case
0<\alpha<2
makes this argument inapplicable, as the second moment of a typical solution to an evolution equation with fractional Laplacian cannot be finite.
[
19
]
pointed out that in this case the weight function
|x|^{2}
makes the linear term too strong to be controlled by the nonlinear part.
To overcome this limitation and prove the existence of blowing-up solutions for a nonlocal Keller-Segel equation (equation (
1.5
) with
1<\alpha<2
and
\epsilon=u=0
),
[
4
]
extended the classical method by studying moments of lower order
\gamma\in(1,2)
:

(5.30)

m_{\gamma}(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x-x_{0}|^{\gamma}\rho(x,t)\mathrm{d}x,\quad x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

They emphasized that, for
\alpha<2
, the existence of higher-order moments
m_{\gamma}
with
\gamma\geq\alpha
cannot be expected. Even for the linear equation
\partial_{t}v+\left(-\Delta\right)^{\alpha/2}v=0
, the fundamental solution
p_{\alpha}(x,t)
behaves like
p_{\alpha}(x,t)\sim\left(t^{d/\alpha}+|x|^{d+\alpha}/t\right)^{-1}
, forcing moments with
\gamma\geq\alpha
to be infinite.

[
4
]
then demonstrated the existence of blowing-up solutions by showing the finite-time extinction of the function (setting
x_{0}=0
for simplicity)

(5.31)

w(t)\equiv\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(x)\rho(x,t)\mathrm{d}x,

where
\varphi(x)
is a smooth nonnegative weight function on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
defined as

(5.32)

\varphi(x)\equiv\left(1+|x|^{2}\right)^{\gamma/2}-1,

with
\gamma\in(1,2]
.

[
4
]
highlighted that the quantity
w
is essentially equivalent to the moment
m_{\gamma}
of order
\gamma
of the solution, as for every
\varepsilon>0
, a suitably chosen
C(\varepsilon)>0
ensures for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
that

(5.33)

\varphi(x)\leq|x|^{\gamma}\leq\varepsilon+C(\varepsilon)\varphi(x).

Drawing inspiration from the work of
[
17
]
and
[
4
]
, we analyze the ODE associated to the evolution of
w(t)
to
provide an upper bound for
m(t)
(
Theorem

33
) that remains unchanged despite variations in the coupling of the reaction term.
As
Lemma

18
assures that the balance between chemotaxis and the reaction term (as far as
q>2
) leads to a smooth decaying solution, global regularity of the solutions is ensured, giving rise to
Theorem

21
.
Consequently, the quantity
m_{\gamma}(t)
cannot vanish, even for initial condition
\|\rho_{0}\|_{L^{1}}
sufficiently large and well-concentrated around a point (
m_{\gamma}(0)
small enough), in contrast to what occurs when
\epsilon=0
, for
u=0
(see
[
4
,
3
]
for details). It is important to point out that, differently from
[
17
]
, our discussions are explicitly based on the sign of
\mathrm{d}w/\mathrm{d}t
for the initial condition
(m_{0},w_{0})
.
To start, consider the two following lemmas from
[
4
]
:

Lemma 28
.

[
4

Let
\alpha\in(1,2)

\gamma\in(1,\alpha)
, and

\left(-\Delta\right)^{\alpha/2}\varphi\in L^{\infty}

[

For every

Lemma 31
.

Let
\alpha\in(1,2)
,
\gamma\in(1,\alpha)
, and
\varphi
be defined as in (

). Then, the inequality

(5.35)

\left|\frac{\nabla\varphi(x)}{\gamma}\right|^{\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}}\leq\frac{2}{\gamma}\varphi(x)

Proof.

Note that
\nabla\varphi(x)=\gamma\left(1+|x|^{2}\right)^{\frac{\gamma}{2}-1}x
and both
|\nabla\varphi(x)|^{\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}}
and
\varphi(x)
are radially symmetric functions. Then, the functions
F,\;G:\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash\{0\}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

F(x)=\frac{|\gamma^{-1}\nabla\varphi(x)|^{\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}}}{\varphi(x)},\quad\text{ and }\quad G(x)=\frac{|\gamma^{-1}\nabla\varphi(x)||x|}{\varphi(x)}

are equivalent, respectively, to the functions
f,\;g:\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,

f(x)=\frac{\left[\left(1+x^{2}\right)^{\frac{\gamma}{2}-1}x\right]^{\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}}}{\left(1+x^{2}\right)^{\gamma/2}-1},\quad\text{ and }\quad g(x)=\frac{\left(1+x^{2}\right)^{\frac{\gamma}{2}-1}x^{2}}{\left(1+x^{2}\right)^{\gamma/2}-1}.

We observe that
f(x)<g(x)
. Indeed, this inequality can be expressed as

\left[\left(1+x^{2}\right)^{\frac{\gamma}{2}-1}x\right]^{\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}}<\left(1+x^{2}\right)^{\frac{\gamma}{2}-1}x^{2},

which simplifies to
\left(1+x^{2}\right)^{1/2}>x
,
a statement valid for all
x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
.
Further,

g^{\prime}(x)=\dfrac{2x\left(1+x^{2}\right)^{\frac{{\gamma}-4}{2}}\left(\left(1+x^{2}\right)^{\frac{{\gamma}}{2}}-\frac{\gamma}{2}x^{2}-1\right)}{\left(\left(1+x^{2}\right)^{\frac{{\gamma}}{2}}-1\right)^{2}}<0\quad\forall\;x>0,

since

\begin{split}\left(1+x^{2}\right)^{\frac{{\gamma}}{2}}-\frac{\gamma}{2}x^{2}-1&=\left(1+x^{2}\right)\left(\left(1+x^{2}\right)^{\frac{\gamma}{2}-1}-\frac{\gamma}{2}\right)+\left(\frac{\gamma}{2}-1\right)<0\quad\forall\;x>0,\end{split}

implying
g(x)\leq\lim_{x\rightarrow 0}g(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
. Then, as

\lim_{x\rightarrow 0}g(x)=\lim_{x\rightarrow 0}\left(\frac{|x|^{2}}{\left(1+|x|^{2}\right)^{\frac{\gamma}{2}}-1}\right)\overset{\underset{\text{L'H}}{}}{=}\lim_{x\rightarrow 0}\left(\frac{2x}{\gamma x\left(1+|x|^{2}\right)^{\frac{{\gamma}}{2}-1}}\right)=\frac{2}{\gamma},

we have
f(x)\leq\frac{2}{\gamma}
, concluding the proof.

Proposition 32
.

Let
q
and
s
be integers such that
q
,
s>2
,
d=2
,
\alpha\in(1,2)
,
\beta
satisfy
0\leq\beta<\alpha
, and
\gamma\in(1,\beta]
. Assume
u\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,\infty))
is divergence free, and
\rho
solves (
1.5
) with
\rho_{0}\geq 0\in K_{s,\beta}
. Then the time derivative of
w(t)
, defined in (
5.31
), can be expressed as

(5.36)

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}w(t)\leq 2\delta_{(u)}\chi^{-\mu}w(t)+f_{(u)}(m,w),

where
\delta_{(u)}
equals zero when
u=0
and one otherwise, and

(5.37)

f_{(u)}(m,w)=m(t)\left(C_{1}+\chi^{\mu(\gamma-1)}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}^{\gamma}-\chi C_{2}m(t)^{\frac{2}{\gamma}}\left(m(t)+2w(t)\right)^{-\frac{2-\gamma}{\gamma}}\right),

with
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
being functions of
\gamma
, and
\mu\geq 0
a parameter to be chosen.

Proof.

By differentiating equation (
5.31
), we obtain

(5.38)

\hskip 42.67912pt\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}w=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\varphi(x)(u\cdot\nabla)\rho\mathrm{~d}x-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\varphi(x)\left(-\Delta\right)^{\alpha/2}\rho\mathrm{~d}x+\\
\chi\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\varphi(x)\nabla\cdot\left(\rho\nabla\Delta^{-1}\rho\right)\mathrm{~d}x-\epsilon\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\varphi(x)\rho^{q}\mathrm{~d}x.\hskip 42.67912pt

Note that, due to
Theorem

21
, all the upcoming integrations by parts are justified for all
t\geq 0
.

As
\nabla\cdot u=0
, we have

(5.39)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\varphi(x)(u\cdot\nabla)\rho\mathrm{~d}x=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\nabla\varphi(x)\cdot u\rho\mathrm{~d}x.

Then, assuming that
u
is an arbitrary smooth divergence-free vector field, and applying Young’s inequality and
Lemma

31
, we see that

\begin{split}\left|\frac{\nabla\varphi(x)}{\gamma}\right|\left|u\right|&\leq\chi^{-\mu}\left|\frac{\nabla\varphi(x)}{\gamma}\right|^{\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}}+\chi^{\mu(\gamma-1)}\left((\gamma-1)^{\gamma-1}\gamma^{-\gamma}\right)\left|u\right|^{\gamma}\\
&\leq\frac{2}{\gamma}\chi^{-\mu}\varphi(x)+\frac{\chi^{\mu(\gamma-1)}}{\gamma}\left|u\right|^{\gamma},\end{split}

with
\mu>0
to be chosen later.
Therefore, we can rewrite (
5.39
) as

\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\nabla\varphi(x)\cdot u\rho\mathrm{~d}x\right|\leq 2\chi^{-\mu}w(t)+\chi^{\mu(\gamma-1)}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}^{\gamma}m(t).

Next, from
Lemma

28
, we find that

-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left(-\Delta\right)^{\alpha/2}\rho(x,t)\varphi(x)\mathrm{~d}x=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\rho(x,t)\left(-\Delta\right)^{\alpha/2}\varphi(x)\mathrm{~d}x\leq\|\left(-\Delta\right)^{\alpha/2}\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\rho(x,t)\mathrm{~d}x.

For the chemotaxis term, we obtain

(5.40)

\begin{split}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\varphi(x)\nabla\cdot\left(\rho\nabla\Delta^{-1}\rho\right)\mathrm{~d}x&=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\nabla\varphi(x)\cdot\left(\rho\nabla\Delta^{-1}\rho\right)\mathrm{~d}x\\
&=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}}\nabla\varphi(x)\cdot\frac{x-y}{|x-y|^{2}}\rho(x,t)\rho(y,t)\mathrm{~d}y\mathrm{~d}x\\
&=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left(\nabla\varphi(x)-\nabla\varphi(y)\right)\cdot\left(x-y\right)\frac{\rho(x,t)\rho(y,t)}{|x-y|^{2}}\mathrm{~d}y\mathrm{~d}x,\end{split}

where in the last step we used symmetrization in
x,y
.
Now, observe that

\begin{split}m^{2}&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}}\rho(x,t)\rho(y,t)\mathrm{~d}x\mathrm{~d}y\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}}\rho(x,t)\rho(y,t)\frac{1}{\left(1+|x|^{2-\gamma}+|y|^{2-\gamma}\right)^{\gamma/2}}\left(1+|x|^{2-\gamma}+|y|^{2-\gamma}\right)^{\gamma/2}\mathrm{~d}x\mathrm{~d}y\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left(\frac{\rho(x,t)\rho(y,t)}{1+|x|^{2-\gamma}+|y|^{2-\gamma}}\right)^{\gamma/2}\left(\rho(x,t)\rho(y,t)\right)^{\frac{2-\gamma}{2}}\left(1+|x|^{2-\gamma}+|y|^{2-\gamma}\right)^{\gamma/2}\mathrm{~d}x\mathrm{~d}y.\end{split}

Thus, by applying the Hölder’s inequality, we obtain

(5.41)

\begin{split}m^{2}&\leq\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{\rho(x,t)\rho(y,t)}{1+|x|^{2-\gamma}+|y|^{2-\gamma}}\mathrm{~d}x\mathrm{~d}y\right)^{\frac{\gamma}{2}}\\
&\hskip 113.81102pt\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}}\rho(x,t)\rho(y,t)\left(1+|x|^{2-\gamma}+|y|^{2-\gamma}\right)^{\frac{\gamma}{2-\gamma}}\mathrm{~d}x\mathrm{~d}y\right)^{\frac{2-\gamma}{2}}.\end{split}

From the properties of convex functions, as
\gamma/(2-\gamma)>1
, and utilizing the inequality
|x|^{\gamma}\leq 1+\varphi(x)
, we have

\left(1+|x|^{2-\gamma}+|y|^{2-\gamma}\right)^{\frac{\gamma}{2-\gamma}}\leq C\left(1+|x|^{\gamma}+|y|^{\gamma}\right)\leq C\left(1+\varphi(x)+\varphi(y)\right),

for
C
a function of
\gamma
.
This implies that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}}\rho(x,t)\rho(y,t)\left(1+|x|^{2-\gamma}+|y|^{2-\gamma}\right)^{\frac{\gamma}{2-\gamma}}\mathrm{~d}x\mathrm{~d}y\leq C\left[m^{2}+2m\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\varphi(x)\rho(x,t)\mathrm{~d}x\right)\right].

Thus, back to (
5.41
), we have

(5.42)

\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{\rho(x,t)\rho(y,t)}{1+|x|^{2-\gamma}+|y|^{2-\gamma}}\mathrm{~d}x\mathrm{~d}y\right)^{\frac{\gamma}{2}}\geq C^{-\frac{2-\gamma}{2}}\frac{m^{2}}{\left(m^{2}+2mw(t)\right)^{\frac{2-\gamma}{2}}},

and, considering
Lemma

29
, we see that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{\rho(x,t)\rho(y,t)}{1+|x|^{2-\gamma}+|y|^{2-\gamma}}\mathrm{~d}x\mathrm{~d}y\leq\frac{1}{K}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}}(\nabla\varphi(x)-\nabla\varphi(y))\cdot(x-y)\frac{\rho(x,t)\rho(y,t)}{|x-y|^{2}}\mathrm{~d}x\mathrm{~d}y.

Hence, from (
5.42
),

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}}(\nabla\varphi(x)-\nabla\varphi(y))\cdot(x-y)\frac{\rho(x,t)\rho(y,t)}{|x-y|^{2}}\mathrm{~d}x\mathrm{~d}y\geq KC^{-\frac{2-\gamma}{\gamma}}\frac{m^{\frac{4}{\gamma}}}{\left(m^{2}+2mw(t)\right)^{\frac{2-\gamma}{\gamma}}}.

Therefore, the chemotaxis term (
5.40
) can be estimated as

\begin{split}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\varphi(x)\nabla\cdot\left(\rho\nabla\Delta^{-1}\rho\right)\mathrm{~d}x&\leq-C_{2}\frac{m(t)^{\frac{4}{\gamma}}}{\left(m(t)^{2}+2m(t)w(t)\right)^{\frac{2-\gamma}{\gamma}}}\\
&\leq-C_{2}m(t)^{1+\frac{2}{\gamma}}\left(m(t)+2w(t)\right)^{-\frac{2-\gamma}{\gamma}}\end{split}

allowing us to rewrite (
5.38
) as

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}w(t)\leq m(t)\left(C_{1}+\chi^{\mu(\gamma-1)}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}^{\gamma}-\chi C_{2}m(t)^{\frac{2}{\gamma}}\left(m(t)+2w(t)\right)^{-\frac{2-\gamma}{\gamma}}\right)+2\chi^{-\mu}w(t)-\epsilon\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\varphi(x)\rho^{q}\mathrm{~d}x,

which can be simplified to (
5.36
).

\Box

Theorem 33
.

Let
q
and
s
be integers such that
q
,
s>2
,
d=2
,
\alpha\in(1,2)
,
\beta
satisfy
0\leq\beta<\alpha
, and
\gamma\in(1,\beta]
. Suppose
u\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,\infty))
is divergence free,
and
\rho
solves (
1.5
) with
\rho_{0}\geq 0\in K_{s,\beta}
. Then, for all
\tau>0
, we have

(5.43)

\|\rho(\cdot,\tau)\|_{L^{1}}<\max\left\{\psi^{\frac{\gamma}{2+\gamma}}w_{0}^{\frac{2}{2+\gamma}},\;\psi\right\},

where

(5.44)

\psi(\chi,u,\tau)=\frac{C_{1}+\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}^{\gamma}}{\chi C_{2}}\left(\frac{\Theta(\tau)}{C_{1}+\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}^{\gamma}}+1\right),\quad\quad\Theta(\tau)=\begin{cases}\tau^{-1}&\text{ if }u=0\\
2\left(1-e^{-2\tau}\right)^{-1}&\text{ if }u\neq 0,\end{cases}

w_{0}=w(0)
, and
C_{1}
,
C_{2}
are functions of
\gamma
.

Remark 34
.

Here
C_{2}
is obtained through the product of
3^{-\frac{2-\gamma}{\gamma}}
and the constant
C_{2}
featured in (
5.37
).

Remark 35
.

Notice that when
u=0
, if
w_{0}<\frac{C_{1}}{\chi C_{2}}\left(\frac{1}{C_{1}\tau}+1\right)
, the level
\|\rho(\cdot,\tau)\|_{L^{1}}\sim\chi^{-1}
will be attained in at most
\tau\sim 1
.
Otherwise, if this condition is not met, the level
\|\rho(\cdot,\tau)\|_{L^{1}}\sim\chi^{-1}
will be reached in at most
\tau\sim\chi^{\frac{2}{\gamma}}
,
while the level
\sim\chi^{\frac{\gamma}{2+\gamma}}

\left(1/3<\gamma/(2+\gamma)<1/2\right)
in at most
\tau\sim 1
.
Moreover, for
u\neq 0
, if
w_{0}<\frac{C_{1}}{\chi C_{2}}\left(\frac{1}{C_{1}\tau}+1\right)
, on the time scale
\tau\sim 1
the level
\|\rho(\cdot,\tau)\|_{L^{1}}
is
\sim\chi^{-1}
, and otherwise, the level
\|\rho(\cdot,\tau)\|_{L^{1}}
is
\sim\chi^{\frac{\gamma}{2+\gamma}}
.

Proof.

We begin by observing that, since
\rho_{0}\in K_{s,\beta}
and
\gamma\leq\beta
, the integral
w_{0}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\varphi(x)\rho_{0}(x)\mathrm{~d}x
is finite.
Moreover, from the ordinary differential inequality provided by (
5.36
), we obtain

(5.45)

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(e^{-2\delta_{(u)}\chi^{-\mu}t}w(t)\right)\leq e^{-2\delta_{(u)}\chi^{-\mu}t}f_{(u)}(m,w),

where
f_{(u)}
and
\delta_{(u)}
are defined as in
Proposition

32
.
Proceeding, we split the proof into two cases.

\bullet

Case 1
(u=0)
:

Setting
u=0
in (
5.45
) yields the differential inequality

(5.46)

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}w(t)\leq f(m,w),

where
f(m,w)\equiv f_{(u=0)}(m,w)
.

Now, suppose the initial condition
(m_{0},w_{0})
satisfies
f\left(m_{0},w_{0}\right)<0
. Then, by the continuity of
f
in
m
and the continuity of
m(t)
, there exists an open neighborhood
U_{1}
of
t=0
such that, for all
t\in U_{1}
,

\left|f\left(m(t),w_{0}\right)-f\left(m_{0},w_{0}\right)\right|<-\frac{1}{2}f\left(m_{0},w_{0}\right),

implying that
f\left(m(t),w_{0}\right)<f\left(m_{0},w_{0}\right)/2
in
U_{1}
.
Furthermore, by the continuity of
f
in both
m
and
w
, and of
(m,w)
with respect to
t
, there exists an open neighborhood
U_{2}
of
t=0
such that
f\left(m(t),w(t)\right)<0
for all
t\in U_{2}
.
Choosing
\tau\in U_{1}\cap U_{2}
, we ensure
f(m(t),w(t))<0
for all
t\in[0,\tau]
and
f(m(\tau),w_{0})<0
.

Since
f(m(t),w(t))<0
on
[0,\tau]
, from (
5.46
),
w(t)
decreases and, hence,
w_{0}\geq w(t)
on this interval. Moreover, as
m(t)
is monotonically decreasing,
m(\tau)\leq m(t)
for every
t\in[0,\tau]
.
Consequently,
f(m(t),w(t))\leq f(m(\tau),w_{0})
for all
t\in[0,\tau]
.

To clarify this assertion, consider the partial derivatives of
f(m,w)
:

(5.47)

\displaystyle\partial_{m}f(m,w)=\frac{f(m,w)}{m}-C_{2}{\chi}m^{\frac{2}{{\gamma}}}\left(m+2w\right)^{-\frac{2}{\gamma}}\left(m+\frac{4w}{\gamma}\right),

(5.48)

\displaystyle\partial_{w}f(m,w)=2\gamma^{-1}C_{2}{\chi}m^{\frac{2}{{\gamma}}+1}\left(2-{\gamma}\right)\left(m+2w\right)^{-\frac{2}{{\gamma}}}.

Note that
\partial_{w}f(m,w)>0
and, as
f(m,w)<0
holds for all
t\in[0,\tau]
,
\partial_{m}f(m,w)<0
over this interval. The signs of these derivatives reveal that
f
decreases as
w
decreases, and, over the interval
[0,\tau]
,
f
increases as
m
decreases. Thus, the inequalities
w_{0}\geq w(t)
and
m(\tau)\leq m(t)
for every
t\in[0,\tau]
imply
f(m(t),w(t))\leq f(m(\tau),w_{0})
.
In a more direct argument, we can compare
-f(m(t),w(t))
and
-f(m(\tau),w_{0})
for
t\in[0,\tau]
using the inequalities
-f(m(t),w(t))>0
,
w_{0}\geq w(t)
and
m(\tau)\leq m(t)
in this interval:

\begin{split}-f\left(m(t),w(t)\right)&=m(t)\left(\chi C_{2}m(t)\left(1+\frac{2w(t)}{m(t)}\right)^{-\frac{2-\gamma}{\gamma}}-C_{1}\right)\\
&\geq m(\tau)\left(\chi C_{2}m(\tau)\left(1+\frac{2w_{0}}{m(\tau)}\right)^{-\frac{2-\gamma}{\gamma}}-C_{1}\right)=-f\left(m(\tau),w_{0}\right).\end{split}

Next, upon integrating equation (
5.46
), we derive the following estimate:

w_{0}-w(\tau)\geq-\int_{0}^{\tau}f\left(m(\tau),w_{0}\right)\mathrm{d}t=-\tau f\left(m(\tau),w_{0}\right).

Thus, as
w(\tau)
cannot vanish, we must have

-\tau f\left(m(\tau),w_{0}\right)<w_{0}
, that is,

(5.49)

\tau m(\tau)\left[\chi C_{2}m(\tau)^{\frac{2}{\gamma}}\left(m(\tau)+2w_{0}\right)^{-\frac{2-\gamma}{\gamma}}-C_{1}\right]<w_{0},

which can be written as

m(\tau)^{1+\frac{2}{\gamma}}<\frac{C_{1}}{\chi C_{2}}\left(\frac{w_{0}}{C_{1}\tau}+m(\tau)\right)\left(m(\tau)+2w_{0}\right)^{\frac{2-\gamma}{\gamma}}.

From this, we obtain, if
m(\tau)<w_{0}
,

\begin{split}m(\tau)&<\left[\frac{C_{1}}{\chi C_{2}}\left(\frac{1}{C_{1}\tau}+1\right)\right]^{\frac{\gamma}{2+\gamma}}w_{0}^{\frac{\gamma}{2+\gamma}}\left(3w_{0}\right)^{\frac{2-\gamma}{2+\gamma}}=\left[\frac{C_{1}}{\chi C_{2}}\left(\frac{1}{C_{1}\tau}+1\right)\right]^{\frac{\gamma}{2+\gamma}}w_{0}^{\frac{2}{2+\gamma}},\end{split}

and, if
m(\tau)\geq w_{0}
,

\begin{split}m(\tau)^{1+\frac{2}{\gamma}}&<\left[\frac{C_{1}}{\chi C_{2}}\left(\frac{1}{C_{1}\tau}+1\right)\right]m(\tau)\left(3m(\tau)\right)^{\frac{2-\gamma}{\gamma}}=\left[\frac{C_{1}}{\chi C_{2}}\left(\frac{1}{C_{1}\tau}+1\right)\right]m(\tau)^{\frac{2}{\gamma}},\end{split}

where we incorporated
3^{-\frac{2-\gamma}{\gamma}}
into
C_{2}
. Therefore,

m(\tau)<\frac{C_{1}}{\chi C_{2}}\left(\frac{1}{C_{1}\tau}+1\right).

Note that, if the initial assumption,
f(m_{0},w_{0})<0
, is not met, the result still follows from
m(t)\leq m_{0}
for all
t>0
. Indeed,
f(m_{0},w_{0})\geq 0
is equivalent to
m_{0}^{1+\frac{2}{\gamma}}\leq\frac{C_{1}}{\chi C_{2}}m_{0}\left(m_{0}+2w_{0}\right)^{\frac{2-\gamma}{\gamma}}
. Then, as seen in the previous calculations, incorporating
3^{-\frac{2-\gamma}{\gamma}}
into
C_{2}
, if
m_{0}<w_{0}
, we deduce that
m_{0}<\left(\frac{C_{1}}{\chi C_{2}}\right)^{\frac{\gamma}{2+\gamma}}w_{0}^{\frac{2}{2+\gamma}}
; otherwise, we obtain
m_{0}\leq\frac{C_{1}}{\chi C_{2}}
.

\bullet

Case 2
(u\neq 0)
:

Assuming the initial condition
(m_{0},w_{0})
satisfies
f_{(u)}\left(m_{0},w_{0}\right)<0
, as proved in
case 1
, there exists
\tau>0
such that
f_{(u)}\left(m(t),w(t)\right)<0

t\in[0,\tau]
, and
f_{(u)}\left(m(\tau),w_{0}\right)<0
.
Additionally,
f_{(u)}(m(t),w(t))\leq f_{(u)}(m(\tau),w_{0})
in this interval, since the partial derivatives of
f_{(u)}
with respect to
m
and
w
are expressed, respectively, by equations (
5.47
) and (
5.48
), with
C_{1}
replaced by

Subsequently, as
\delta_{(u)}=1
in
(
5.45
), the time integral of the right-hand side of (
5.45
) over the interval
[0,\tau]
can be upper-bounded by

(5.50)

\int_{0}^{\tau}e^{-2\chi^{-\mu}t}m(\tau)\left[C_{1}+\chi^{\mu(\gamma-1)}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}^{\gamma}-\chi C_{2}m(\tau)^{\frac{2}{\gamma}}\left(m(\tau)+2w_{0}\right)^{-\frac{2-\gamma}{\gamma}}\right]\mathrm{~d}t=\\

(5.51)

m(\tau)^{1+\frac{2}{\gamma}}<\left(\frac{C_{1}+\chi^{\mu(\gamma-1)}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}^{\gamma}}{\chi C_{2}}\right)\left(\frac{2w_{0}}{\left(C_{1}+\chi^{\mu(\gamma-1)}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}^{\gamma}\right)\left(1-e^{-2\chi^{-\mu}\tau}\right)\chi^{\mu}}+m(\tau)\right)\left(m(\tau)+2w_{0}\right)^{\frac{2-\gamma}{\gamma}}.

We can see that the optimal choice for the parameter

.
