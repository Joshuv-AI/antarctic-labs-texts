The integrable Volterra system in the case of infinitely many species, either countable or uncountable.


Abstract
In the present paper we derive a further extension of the results contained in two recent articles, both published in Open Communications in Mathematical Physics, where it was shown that the integrable version of the N − N- species Volterra model, introduced by V. Volterra in 1937, is in fact maximally superintegrable. Here we point out that the superintegrability property applies as well to the case of infinitely many competing species, either countable or uncountable. Analytical and numerical results are given.

2
The case of a finite number N of species

The equations for the integrable
N
-species Volterra system have been introduced by V.Volterra in
[
25
]
, and thoroughly investigated in
[
17
]
,
[
18
]
:

\frac{dN_{r}}{dt}=\epsilon_{r}N_{r}+\sum_{s\neq r=1}^{N}A_{rs}N_{r}N_{s}\quad\quad r=1,\ldots,N.

(2.1)

In the equation (
2.1
),
\epsilon_{r}
are the natural growth coefficients of each species and
A_{rs}
are interaction coefficients between species
r
and species
s
that account for the effects of encountering between two individuals. In the integrable
N-
species case, the
N\times N
interaction matrix
A
has the form
[
18
]
:

A_{rs}=\epsilon_{r}\epsilon_{s}(B_{r}-B_{s}),

(2.2)

where all the
\epsilon_{r}
and the
B_{r}
are real non-zero numbers, and moreover
B_{r}\neq B_{s}
if
r\neq s
. The matrix
A
has always rank
2
, so
{\mathcal{I}}
, the Image of
A
, is
2
-dimensional and
{\mathcal{K}}
, its Kernel, is
(N-2)
-dimensional.
Moreover
{\mathcal{I}}
and
{\mathcal{K}}
are two orthogonal subspaces with respect to the inner product
(u,v)\equiv\sum_{j}u_{j}v_{j}
and the
N
-dimensional Euclidean space
{\mathbb{E}}_{N}
can be written as the
direct sum of them:

{\mathbb{E}}_{N}={\mathcal{I}}\oplus{\mathcal{K}}.

Also, it has been shown in
[
18
]
that
{\mathcal{I}}
is the linear span of two vectors, which are linearly independent because the coefficients
B_{r}
are all distinct, namely
\epsilon
and
\eta
, of components
\epsilon_{r}
and
\eta_{r}=B_{r}\epsilon_{r}
. The Kernel
{\mathcal{K}}
is naturally defined as the
(N-2)
-dimensional subspace of

\mathbb{E}_{N}
orthogonal to
{\mathcal{I}}
and is such that its generic vector
k
fulfils the linear conditions

(k,\epsilon)=(k,\eta)=0.

(2.3)

On the other hand, the affine variety of equilibrium configurations
{\mathcal{E}}
is the set of elements
z
fulfilling

(z,\epsilon)=0,~(z,\eta)=1.

(2.4)

It is convenient to rewrite the evolution equations (
2.1
) In terms of the logarithmic variables defined by
y_{j}:=\log N_{j}
, getting:

\dot{y}_{j}=\epsilon_{j}+\sum_{l=1}^{N}A_{jl}\exp(y_{l})=\epsilon_{j}+(\eta_{j}\sum_{l=1}^{N}\epsilon_{l}-\epsilon_{j}\sum_{l=1}^{N}\eta_{l})\exp(y_{l})

(2.5)

It is possible to represent any vector
\in{\mathbb{E}}_{N}
as a linear combination of a vector
\in{\mathcal{I}}
and a vector
\in{\mathcal{K}}
as follows:

y_{n}=P\epsilon_{n}+Q\eta_{n}+k_{n},

(2.6)

where in (
2.6
)
k_{n}
is a generic element
k\in{\mathcal{K}}
.
1
1

1

To be precise, with the term
k_{n}
we mean
\sum_{k=1}^{N-2}R_{k}\tau_{m}^{(k)}
, i.e. the decomposition of
k_{n}
along a given orthonormal basis. We adopt here this shorter symbol for easiness of notation.

The evolution equations (
2.5
) take the form:

\dot{P}\epsilon_{n}+\dot{Q}\eta_{n}+\dot{k}_{n}=\epsilon_{n}+\eta_{n}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\epsilon_{k}\exp(y_{k})-\epsilon_{n}\sum_{=1}^{N}\eta_{k}\exp(y_{k})

(2.7)

entailing:

\displaystyle\dot{P}=1-\sum_{n=1}^{N}\eta_{n}\exp(P\epsilon_{n}+Q\eta_{n}+k_{n}),

(2.8)

\displaystyle\dot{Q}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\epsilon_{n}\exp(P\epsilon_{n}+Q\eta_{n}+k_{n}),

(2.9)

\displaystyle\dot{k}_{n}=0,\quad n=1...N-2

(2.10)

we can rewrite the Hamilton equations in the canonical form:

\dot{P}=-\frac{\partial{\mathcal{H}}}{\partial Q};~~\dot{Q}=\frac{\partial{\mathcal{H}}}{\partial P}

(2.11)

with the Hamiltonian explicitly given by:

{\mathcal{H}}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\exp(P\epsilon_{n}+Q\eta_{n}+k_{n})-Q.

(2.12)

For other properties of this dynamical system, like its Poisson structure, the corresponding associated
N-2
Casimirs, existence of equilibrium positions, nature of the orbits and some numerical examples we refer the reader to
[
18
]
. In the next Section we are going to extend the above construction to the infinite chain, i.e. we let the numerosity of the species to be infinite.

3
About the discrete infinite case

3.1
General outlook

In the discrete infinite case we extend the involved summation to the whole discrete line, taking care of convergence properties. Here we have to point out that, on one hand, we can look at the operator
iA
as a self-adjoint separable Hilbert-Schmidt operator
[
11
]
mapping the Hilbert space
\ell^{2}
of square summable sequences into itself, thanks to the the inner product
(x,y)\doteq\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}x_{n}y_{n}
. As such, its spectrum consists of two opposite real eigenvalues, say
\pm\omega
, and a null eigenvalue of infinite multiplicity, the Hilbert space
\ell^{2}
being spanned by the two orthonormal eigenvectors associated to
\pm\omega
and by the orthogonal subspace Ker
(A)
of codimension 2. On the other hand,
A
acts nonlinearly on the space of sequences, according to the law
\dot{y}=A\exp(y)
, mapping
\ell^{\infty}
into
\ell^{2}
. To be on the safe side, from now on we will assume that
\epsilon
belongs to
\ell^{2}
, while
\eta
and
\kappa
belong to the dense open subset
S^{\infty}\subset\ell^{2}
consisting of bounded sequences
s_{n}
that vanish at
\infty
faster than any power of
1/n
, i.e. such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}n^{p}s_{n}=0,\forall p
; this condition is quite natural for
\eta
, whose elements are defined by
\eta_{n}=B_{n}\epsilon_{n}
, and simply amounts to require that
B_{n}
itself shares the same properties; its extension to
\kappa
ensures that the whole quantity
Q\eta_{n}+\kappa_{n}
enjoys the same regularity properties. Anyway, the possibility for
\eta
and
\kappa
to belong to other spaces will be also discussed in the next Sections.

The solution procedure is much the same as the one holding for finite
N
. We report the main formulas. The image of the summation operator

(Af)_{n}\doteq\sum_{m\in{\mathbb{Z}}}(\epsilon_{m}\eta_{n}-\epsilon_{n}\eta_{m})f_{m}

is the linear span of
\{\epsilon_{n}\},\{\eta_{n}\}
, to be considered as
\ell^{2}
sequences; as previously mentioned,
f_{n}=\exp(y_{n})
belong to
\ell^{\infty}
. In terms of the logarithmic variable
y_{n}
, the integrable Volterra system takes the following form:

\dot{y}_{n}=\epsilon_{n}+\sum_{m\in{\mathbb{Z}}}(\epsilon_{m}\eta_{n}-\eta_{m}\epsilon_{n})\exp(y_{m}).

(3.1)

The sequence
y_{n}
can be projected on the two complementary subspaces
{\mathcal{I}}=ImA
and
{\mathcal{K}}=KerA
in the form:

y_{n}=P\epsilon_{n}+Q\eta_{n}+\kappa_{n}

(3.2)

where
\kappa_{n}
just depends on the initial conditions, belonging to the infinite dimensional subspace
{\mathcal{K}}
of codimension
2
defined by the linear equations:

\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}k_{n}\epsilon_{n}=0,\quad\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\kappa_{n}\eta_{n}=0.

(3.3)

Since
\epsilon_{n},\eta_{n}
and
\kappa_{n}
both converge to zero as
|n|\to\infty
, the numerosity
N_{n}\to 1
as
|n|\to\infty
. The equations (
3.1
) split in the following
3
equations:

\displaystyle\dot{P}=1-\sum_{m\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\eta_{m}\exp(P\epsilon_{m}+Q\eta_{m}+\kappa_{m})

(3.4)

\displaystyle\dot{Q}=\sum_{m\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\epsilon_{m}\exp(P\epsilon_{m}+Q\eta_{m}+\kappa_{m})

(3.5)

\displaystyle\dot{\kappa}_{n}=0

(3.6)

As stated before the sequences
\eta_{n},\kappa_{n}
are assumed to vanish at infinity faster than any power of
1/n
. As we shall see, in view of some concrete examples that we will consider, one can also just assume that
\eta_{n},\kappa_{n}
vanish at infinity exponentially or faster.

The equilibrium position, if any, is a pair
P^{0},Q^{0}
such that:

\displaystyle 0=1-\sum_{m\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\eta_{m}\exp(P^{0}\epsilon_{m}+Q^{0}\eta_{m}+\kappa_{m})

(3.7)

\displaystyle 0=\sum_{m\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\epsilon_{m}\exp(P^{0}\epsilon_{m}+Q^{0}\eta_{m}+\kappa_{m})

(3.8)

The sequence
z_{n}\doteq\exp(P^{0}\epsilon_{n}+Q^{0}\eta_{n}+k_{n})\doteq z_{n}(P^{0},Q^{0})
is the
equilibrium sequence
. Under the previous conditions imposed to
\epsilon_{n},~\eta_{n},~k_{n}
the equilibrium sequence will belong to
\ell^{\infty}
.

Should we follow the alternative way of the spectral decomposition of the operator
A
and apply the Hilbert-Schmidt machinery, as we did in
[
17
]
and
[
18
]
for the case of finite species, we had to assume that both the sequences
\epsilon_{n}
and
\eta_{n}
belong to
\ell^{2}
.
We would have to look at the roots of the secular equation:

\lambda_{\pm}v^{\pm}(n)=\alpha^{\pm}\eta_{n}-\beta^{\pm}\epsilon_{n}

(3.9)

As a matter of fact, the nonzero eigenvalues read:

\lambda_{\pm}\doteq\pm i\omega=\pm i{\sqrt{|\epsilon|^{2}|\eta|^{2}-(\epsilon,\eta)^{2}}}

(3.10)

where we used the shorthand notations:

|f|^{2}=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}f_{n}^{2};\quad(f,g)=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}f_{n}g_{n}

Notice that
\omega
, i.e. the expression under the square root, is strictly positive because of Cauchy-Schwartz inequality. The equations for the eigenfunctions are given by:

\displaystyle|\eta|^{2}\alpha-(\eta,\epsilon)\beta=\lambda\beta

(3.11)

\displaystyle(\epsilon,\eta)\alpha-|\epsilon|^{2}\beta=\lambda\alpha

(3.12)

By solving for
\alpha,\beta
the system (
3.11
) and (
3.12
) in terms of variables
\epsilon,\eta
we get for the eigenfunctions

v_{n}^{\pm}\doteq u_{n}\pm iw_{n}
the following expressions:

u_{n}=\frac{\epsilon_{n}}{|\epsilon|},\quad w_{n}=\frac{1}{|\epsilon|\omega}\left((\epsilon,\eta)\epsilon_{n}-|\epsilon|^{2}\eta_{n}\right)

(3.13)

The sequences
u_{n},w_{n}
are
\ell^{2}
sequences with norm
1
and are mutually orthogonal, namely
\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}u_{n}w_{n}=0
. The interaction matrix
A_{n,m}
now takes the form

A_{n,m}=i\omega(w_{m}u_{n}-w_{n}u_{m}).

(3.14)

The projection of
y_{n}
, equivalent to (
3.2
), now reads

y_{n}=pu_{n}-qw_{n}+\kappa_{n},

(3.15)

whereas the equations of motion (
3.1
) reduce to

\displaystyle\dot{p}=|\epsilon|+\omega\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}w_{m}\exp(pu_{m}-qw_{m}+\kappa_{m}),

(3.16)

\displaystyle\dot{q}=-\omega\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}u_{m}\exp(pu_{m}-qw_{m}+\kappa_{m}),

(3.17)

\displaystyle\dot{k}_{m}=0,

(3.18)

The corresponding Hamiltonian is then given by

\mathcal{H}=\omega\left(-|\epsilon|q+\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\exp(pu_{m}-qw_{m}+\kappa_{m})\right)

(3.19)

In the next Section we will show how it is possible to give some very general expressions for the equations of motion in the infinite discrete case. This is somewhat surprising since in the case of finite species we have not been able to achieve such general results.
By looking at equations (
3.3
), in order to be operative it is natural to assume that the sequences
\epsilon_{n}
and
\eta_{n}
have an opposite parity with respect to the Kernel sequence
\kappa_{n}
. So, in the following two subsections, we will consider the case of a Kernel given by an even sequence (and hence
\eta_{n}
and
\epsilon_{n}
odd sequences), and the case of a Kernel given by an odd sequence (and hence
\eta_{n}
and
\epsilon_{n}
even sequences). The conditions to get the convergence will be specified in the corresponding subsections.

3.2
The case of an even Kernel

We define the following sequences for
\epsilon_{n}
,
\eta_{n}
and
\kappa_{n}
:

\begin{split}&\epsilon_{n}=\frac{\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}}{\sqrt{2N}},\quad|\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}|^{2}=1\quad\tilde{\epsilon}_{-n}=-\tilde{\epsilon}_{n},\\
&\eta_{n}=-\eta_{-n},\\
&\kappa_{n}=\kappa_{-n}\end{split}

(3.20)

In the previous it is assumed that
\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}
has compact support in
[-N,N]
, so it is an odd function of
n
assuming the values
\pm 1
on the interval
[-N,N]
. The sequence
\eta_{n}
is odd also, whereas
\kappa_{n}
is even since it must be orthogonal to the sequences
\epsilon_{n}
and
\eta_{n}
. Notice that
\epsilon_{n}
is normalized in
\ell^{2}
to 1, and the same normalization can be chosen for
\eta_{n}
. As previously mentioned, both
\eta_{n}
and
\kappa_{n}
are assumed to belong to the dense open subset
S^{\infty}
of
\ell^{2}
consisting in rapidly decreasing sequences.
We start with the equation for
\dot{Q}
:

\dot{Q}=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\epsilon_{n}\exp(P\epsilon_{n}+Q\eta_{n}+\kappa_{n}).

(3.21)

In order to deal with equation (
3.21
), we make use of the following

Proposition 3.1

Let
s_{n}
be a sequence in
S^{\infty}
and consider the following expression:

S(P,x,N)=\sum_{n=-N}^{N}\epsilon_{n}\exp(P\epsilon_{n})f(xs_{n}),

(3.22)

where
f(x)
is a continuous function of
x
and
\epsilon_{n}
is given in (
3.20
). Then, for any finite values of
x
one has

\lim_{N\to\infty}S(P,x,N)=Pf(0).

(3.23)

Proof
: by taking the Taylor expansion of the exponential we get

S(P,x,N)=\sum_{n=-N}^{N}\epsilon_{n}(1+P\epsilon_{n}+\frac{P^{2}}{2}\epsilon_{n}^{2}+\ldots)f(xs_{n}).

(3.24)

Let us analyze the term proportional to
\epsilon_{n}^{2}
. It is given by

P\sum_{n=-N}^{N}\epsilon_{n}^{2}f(xs_{n})=P\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{n=-N}^{N}f(xs_{n}).

(3.25)

The previous is the arithmetic mean of
f(xs_{n})
, so we can use the Cauchy’s limit theorem on the arithmetic mean of sequences: since
s_{n}
converges to
0
as
n\to\infty
and
f(x)
is continuous we get in the limit
N\to\infty

\lim_{N\to\infty}P\sum_{n=-N}^{N}\epsilon_{n}^{2}f(xs_{n})=\lim_{N\to\infty}P\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{n=-N}^{N}f(xs_{n})=Pf(0).

(3.26)

It follows that all the powers of
\epsilon
greater than
2
does not give a contribution in the Taylor series expansion of
\exp(P\epsilon_{n})
in (
3.22
).

Let us consider the contribution from the term linear in
\epsilon_{n}
.

\sum_{n=-N}^{N}\epsilon_{n}f(xs_{n})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2N}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}\left(f(xs_{n})-f(xs_{-n})\right)

(3.27)

Notice that, since
s_{n}
is a sequence in
S^{\infty}

f(xs_{n})
is a bounded sequence and in the limit
n\to\infty
(and hence
N\to\infty
) it converges to
f(0)
(the sequence
f(xs_{n})-f(0)
is bounded and approaches zero). The above sum, by adding and subtracting
f(0)
, can be rewritten as

\sum_{n=-N}^{N}\epsilon_{n}f(xs_{n})=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\epsilon_{n}\left(f(xs_{n})-f(0)\right)-\sum_{n=1}^{N}\epsilon_{n}\left(f(xs_{-n})-f(0)\right)

(3.28)

and both the addenda converge to zero in the limit
N\to\infty
. For example for the first addendum on the right hand side, if
f(x)
is Lipschitz at
x=0
, i.e. if
|f(xs_{n})-f(0)|\leq C|xs_{n}|
, one has

|\frac{1}{\sqrt{2N}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}\left(f(xs_{n})-f(0)\right)|\leq\frac{C|x|}{\sqrt{2N}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}|s_{n}|

(3.29)

and, since
s_{n}\in S^{\infty}
the sum is finite and in the limit
N\to\infty
the linear term in
\epsilon_{n}
in the Taylor series expansion of
\exp(P\epsilon_{n})
in (
3.22
) vanishes.
\square

The fact that
\epsilon_{n}
is an odd sequence is crucial in the vanishing of the linear term in
\epsilon_{n}
. As a result of Proposition (
3.1
) we immediately get the following

Corollary 3.2

The equation of motion (
3.21
) reduces, in the limit
N\to\infty
to

\dot{Q}=P.

(3.30)

Indeed, it is sufficient to take
f(xs_{n})=\exp(Q\eta_{n}+\kappa_{n})
since both
\eta_{n}
and
\kappa_{n}
belong to
S^{\infty}
.

Now we look at the equation for
\dot{P}
. One has

\dot{P}=1-\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\eta_{n}\exp(P\epsilon_{n}+Q\eta_{n}+\kappa_{n}).

(3.31)

In order to deal with the sum, we introduce the following

Proposition 3.3

Let
\eta_{n}
and
s_{n}
be two sequences in
S^{\infty}
and consider the following expression:

T(P,x,N)=\sum_{n=-N}^{N}\eta_{n}\exp(P\epsilon_{n})f(xs_{n}),

(3.32)

where
f(x)
is a continuous function of
x
and
\epsilon_{n}
is given in (
3.20
). Then, for any finite values of
x
one has

\lim_{N\to\infty}T(P,x,N)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\eta_{n}f(xs_{n})

(3.33)

Proof
: Indeed, if one consider the sum
\sum_{n=-N}^{N}\eta_{n}f(xs_{n})
, it is convergent in the limit
N\to\infty
. For large
n
the terms
s_{n}\to 0
so
f(xs_{n})\to f(0)
. Being continuous,
f(x)
is locally bounded around
x=0
so we can find an
M
such that
|f(xs_{n})|<M
for all values of
n
. It follows the convergence of the sum
\sum_{n=-N}^{N}\eta_{n}f(xs_{n})
from the
S^{\infty}
convergence of
\eta_{n}
. If one considers the Taylor expansion of
\exp(P\epsilon_{n})
in the sum (
3.32
), only the constant term survives in the limit
N\to\infty
and we get (
3.33
).
\square

By using Proposition (
3.3
) we get for the expression (
3.31
)

\dot{P}=1-\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\eta_{n}\exp(Q\eta_{n}+\kappa_{n}).

(3.34)

The previous expression can be written in a different form by using the fact that
\eta_{n}
is an odd function and
\kappa_{n}
even. We get

\begin{split}&\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\eta_{n}\exp(Q\eta_{n}+\kappa_{n})=\sum_{n=1}\eta_{n}\exp(Q\eta_{n}+\kappa_{n})-\sum_{n=1}\eta_{n}\exp(-Q\eta_{n}+\kappa_{n})=\\
&=2\sum_{n=1}\eta_{n}\exp(\kappa_{n})\sinh(Q\eta_{n})=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\eta_{n}\exp(\kappa_{n})\sinh(Q\eta_{n})\end{split}

(3.35)

giving

\dot{P}=1-\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\eta_{n}\exp(\kappa_{n})\sinh(Q\eta_{n}).

(3.36)

The corresponding Hamiltonian reads

\mathcal{H}=\frac{P^{2}}{2}-Q+\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\exp(\kappa_{n})\left(\cosh(Q\eta_{n})-1\right).

(3.37)

Notice that we subtracted the constant term
-1
to each term of the sequence in the Hamiltonian in order to ensure the convergence.

3.3
The case of an odd Kernel

Now we specify the sequences
\epsilon_{n}
,
\eta_{n}
and
\kappa_{n}
as:

\begin{split}&\epsilon_{n}=\frac{\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}}{\sqrt{2N}},\quad|\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}|^{2}=1\quad\tilde{\epsilon}_{-n}=\tilde{\epsilon}_{n},\\
&\eta_{n}=\eta_{-n},\\
&\kappa_{n}=-\kappa_{-n}\end{split}

(3.38)

In the previous again it is assumed that
\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}
has compact support in
[-N,N]
. We further assume that
2
2

2

It is known
[
2
]
that if the values of
\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}
are chosen randomly, then
\sup_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}}|\sum_{n=0}^{N}\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}e^{\textrm{i}n\theta}|=O(\sqrt{N\log(N)})
with probability 1. Other interesting sequences that can be considered are the automatic sequences, like the Rudin-Shapiro sequence, for which one has
\sum_{n=0}^{N}\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}=O(\sqrt{N})
(see also
[
2
]
).

\sum_{n=-N}^{N}\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}=o(\sqrt{N}),\quad\textrm{i.e.}\quad\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{n=-N}^{N}\epsilon_{n}=0.

(3.39)

This condition will ensure the convergence of the formulae for the equations of motion as we will see.

Again, we will give two Propositions in order to obtain the equations of motion.

Proposition 3.4

Let
s_{n}
be a sequence in
S^{\infty}
and consider the following expression:

M(P,x,N)=\sum_{n=-N}^{N}\epsilon_{n}\exp(P\epsilon_{n})f(xs_{n}),

(3.40)

where
f(x)
is a continuous function of
x
and
\epsilon_{n}
is given in (
3.38
) and satisfies (
3.39
). Then, for any finite values of
x
one has

\lim_{N\to\infty}M(P,x,N)=Pf(0).

(3.41)

Proof
: we take the Taylor series of the exponential in (
3.40
) one has

M(P,x,N)=\sum_{n=-N}^{N}\epsilon_{n}\left(1+P\epsilon_{n}+\frac{P^{2}}{2}\epsilon_{n}^{2}+\ldots\right)f(xs_{n}),

(3.42)

and analyze firstly the term proportional to
\epsilon_{n}^{2}
:

P\sum_{n=-N}^{N}\epsilon_{n}^{2}f(xs_{n})=\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{n=-N}^{N}f(xs_{n}).

(3.43)

From the Cauchy’s limit theorem on the arithmetic mean of sequences we see that this term converges to
Pf(0)
. It follows that all the powers of
\epsilon
greater than
2
does not give a contribution in the Taylor series expansion of
\exp(P\epsilon_{n})
in (
3.40
). It remains only the contribution from the term linear in
\epsilon_{n}
. One has

\sum_{n=-N}^{N}\epsilon_{n}f(xs_{n})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2N}}\sum_{n=-N}^{N}\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}f(xs_{n}).

(3.44)

Due to the condition (
3.39
), since
f(xs_{n})
is bounded, the sum on the right hand side is
o(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}})
and it follows that the contribution from the term linear in
\epsilon
in (
3.40
) vanishes
\square
.

From the Proposition (
3.4
) one has the following

Corollary 3.5

The equation of motion for
Q
, i.e.

\dot{Q}=\sum_{n=-N}^{N}\epsilon_{n}\exp(P\epsilon_{n}+Q\eta_{n}+\kappa_{n})

(3.45)

reduces, in the limit
N\to\infty
, to

\dot{Q}=P.

(3.46)

Let us now look at the equation of motion for
P
. We notice that Proposition (
3.3
) is still valid if
\epsilon_{n}
is as given in (
3.20
), the proof being the same. So we can directly write

\dot{P}=1-\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\eta_{n}\exp(Q\eta_{n}+\kappa_{n}).

(3.47)

Now
\eta_{n}
is even and
\kappa_{n}
is odd and since the interval of summation is symmetric only the even portion of
\exp(\kappa_{n})
survives, giving

\dot{P}=1-\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\eta_{n}\exp(Q\eta_{n})\cosh(\kappa_{n}).

(3.48)

The corresponding Hamiltonian is given by

\mathcal{H}=\frac{P^{2}}{2}-Q+\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\exp(Q\eta_{n})\left(\cosh(\kappa_{n})-1\right).

(3.49)

At this point it is also possible to take a flow that is a combination of the Hamiltonian containing the even Kernel (
3.37
), say
\mathcal{H}_{e}
, and the Hamiltonian containing the odd Kernel, say
\mathcal{H}_{o}
:

\alpha\mathcal{H}_{e}+(1-\alpha)\mathcal{H}_{o}=\frac{P^{2}}{2}-Q+\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(\alpha\exp(Q\eta^{o}+\kappa^{e}_{n})+(1-\alpha)\exp(Q\eta^{e}+\kappa^{o}_{n})-1\right).

(3.50)

where the superscripts on the sequences remind if they are odd or even. Let us now give a Remark about the equilibrium positions.

Remark 3.6

The equations of motion, both for an odd Kernel and for an even one, are given by

\dot{Q}=P,\quad\dot{P}=1-\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\eta_{n}\exp(Q\eta_{n}+\kappa_{n}),

(3.51)

where the sequences
\epsilon_{n}
and
\eta_{n}
have opposite parity with respect to
\kappa_{n}
. The above system has always just one equilibrium position, say
(Q,P)=(Q_{0},0)
if the sequence
\eta_{n}
has not a definite sign. The motion is bounded and the equilibrium position is a center.

Indeed the function defining
\dot{P}
in (
3.51
) is a decreasing function of
Q
as it follows immediately by taking its derivative with respect to
Q
. Further, for
Q\to-\infty
,
\dot{P}\to+\infty
(we assume that there will be always at least one
\eta_{n}
negative), whereas for
Q\to+\infty
,
\dot{P}\to-\infty
(we assume that there will be always at least one
\eta_{n}
positive), and one has just one zero. Further, the motion must be bounded. Notice that the equilibrium point is stable but not asymptotically stable: this easily follows from the linearization of the system (
3.51
) around the equilibrium point, that is a center. The fact that the motion is bounded follows from a global analysis of the orbits in the plane
(Q,P)
. Indeed, as can be seen from Figure (
1
), where a generic curve defined by
\dot{P}=0
and the line
\dot{Q}=P=0
have been drawn, the plane
(Q,P)
is divided in four regions. Since the signs of
\dot{Q}
and
\dot{P}
are defined in each regions as in Figure (
1
), it follows that if a solution
(Q(t),P(t))
starts in one of these regions at time
t=0
, then at a later time it must exit that region. Take for example the region we labelled with I: in that region
P(t)
is a monotonically decreasing function of time and
Q(t)
monotonically increasing. The orbit starting at
(Q(0),P(0))
in region I must move towards the
Q
axis. It cannot approach asymptotically the
Q
-axis for
t\to\infty
since the system is Hamiltonian and the energy must be preserved. So if one assumes that the orbit does not exit from this region it must reach a finite limit
(Q,P)=(\tilde{Q},\tilde{P})
, i.e.
(\tilde{Q},\tilde{P})
is an asymptotically stable equilibrium position. But this equilibrium position cannot be equal to
(Q_{0},0)
since
(Q_{0},0)
is not asymptotically stable. So we have a contradiction and the orbit must exit from region
I
. The same holds true for the other regions and the motion is bounded.

From another point of view we can say that the system (
3.51
) is Hamiltonian with the Hamiltonian given by

\mathcal{H}=T+V=\frac{P^{2}}{2}-Q+\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(\exp(Q\eta_{n}+\kappa_{n})-1\right).

(3.52)

The potential
V
has a global minimum in
Q_{0}
, as can be easily seen by taking its first and second derivative. Also it approaches
+\infty
for
Q\to\pm\infty
and is an infinite potential well.

(red) and

(black) dividing the plane
(Q,P)
in four regions, each having a definite sign for
\dot{Q}
and
\dot{P}
.

4
About the continuous case

4.1
General outlook

In the continuum limit, the dynamical system

\dot{y}_{j}=\epsilon_{j}+\sum_{jk}A_{jk}\exp(y_{k})

(4.1)

is replaced by the integro-differential equation for the variable
y(x,t)
:

\frac{\partial y}{\partial t}=\epsilon(x)-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx^{\prime}(\epsilon(x)\eta(x^{\prime})-\eta(x)\epsilon(x^{\prime}))\exp[y(x^{\prime})]

(4.2)

Clearly, the right hand side is a linear combination of two functions,
\epsilon(x)
and
\eta(x)
. Since
\eta(x)=B(x)\epsilon(x)
, the two functions will be linearly independent if
B^{\prime}(x)\neq 0
(almost everywhere).
Further conditions that will come out in the following are:

1)There must be a finite or countably infinite set of points where
\epsilon(x)
changes its sign;

2) both
\epsilon(x)
and
\eta(x)
must be square-summable on the whole real line.

We recall the inclusion relations for the spaces
L^{1},L^{2},L^{\infty}
. If the functions are defined on a set of finite Lebesgue measure we have
L^{1}\subset L^{2}\subset L^{\infty}
. In the case of a support of infinite measure. like for instance a semi-line or the whole real line, the inclusion relations are reversed:
L^{\infty}\subset L^{2}\subset L^{1}
.

Even in this case, for the
existence
of the equations of motion one needs that the argument of the exponential be uniformly bounded on the whole real line, while
\epsilon(x),\eta(x)
must be absolutely integrable, and then square integrable, on the whole real line. Of course these requirements are "a fortiori" satisfied by
S^{\infty}
functions.

It is then natural to look at
y(x,t)
as a linear combination of
\epsilon(x),\eta(x)
with time-dependent coefficients, that we will call again
P
and
Q
, plus a term
\kappa(x)
orthogonal to
\epsilon(x)
and
\eta(x)
:

y(x,t)=P(t)\epsilon(x)+Q(t)\eta(x)+\kappa(x)

(4.3)

Accordingly, we will have:

\displaystyle\dot{P}=1-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\eta(x)\exp[P\epsilon(x)+Q\eta(x)+\kappa(x)]

(4.4)

\displaystyle\dot{Q}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\epsilon(x)\exp[P\epsilon(x)+Q\eta(x)+\kappa(x)]

(4.5)

Equations (
4.4
,
4.5
) can be written in canonical form, namely

\displaystyle\dot{P}=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial Q}

(4.6)

\displaystyle\dot{Q}=\frac{\partial H}{\partial P}

(4.7)

H
being the Hamiltonian:

H=-Q+\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\exp(P\epsilon(x)+Q\eta(x)+\kappa)

(4.8)

As in the infinite discrete case, the Hamiltonian (
4.8
) does not seem to exist, even if
\kappa(x),~\epsilon(x),~\eta(x)
are uniformly bounded and rapidly decreasing functions. However, we remark that, analogously to the infinite discrete case, if the exponential is uniformly bounded on the whole real line, then the improper integral can be made convergent by just subtracting to the integrand its asymptotic limit, i.e.
1
.

We look now at the spectral decomposition of the integral operator
A
, whose action on a given function
f(x)
is given by:

(Af)(x):=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx^{\prime}[\epsilon(x)\eta(x^{\prime})-\eta(x)\epsilon(x^{\prime})]f(x^{\prime})

A
is a skew-symmetric Hilbert-Schmidt operator since its kernel is square summable if so are the functions
\epsilon(x)
and
\eta(x)
); its kernel is in fact the kernel of the Poisson Bracket between two functionals
F[y],G[y]
:

\{F,G\}:=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx^{\prime}\frac{\delta F}{\delta y(x)}A(x.x^{\prime})\frac{\delta G}{\delta y(x^{\prime})}

We can apply to
iA
all the theorems holding for compact self-adjoint operators
[
11
]
on a Hilbert space. In particular, it has no residual spectrum, its eigenvectors, including those associated with its kernel, form a complete (orthonormal) set in
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
, its discrete spectrum is either finite or given by a decreasing sequence accumulating to
0
, and its norm is a point of the spectrum. Indeed, we have more, because the kernel
A(x,x^{\prime})
is a
separable kernel of rank 2
, and consequently the operator
A
has only two nonzero complex conjugated eigenvalues.

It is remarkable, though probably obvious, that the results for eigenvalues and eigenfunctions holding in the discrete case migrate with no essential modifications to the continuum case.

Namely, the eigenvalue equation reads:

\psi^{\pm}(x,\omega)=(1/\lambda_{\pm})(\eta(x)\alpha_{\pm}-\epsilon(x)\beta_{\pm}))

(4.9)

The nonzero eigenvalues are given by:

\lambda_{\pm}\doteq\pm i\omega=\pm i{\sqrt{|\epsilon|^{2}|\eta|^{2}-(\epsilon,\eta)^{2}}}

(4.10)

where we used the shorthand notations:

|f|^{2}\doteq\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx|f(x)|^{2},\quad(f,g)\doteq\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dxf(x)g(x).

Notice that
\omega
, i.e. the expression under the square root, is strictly positive because of Cauchy-Schwartz inequality.

The coefficients defining the eigenfunctions have to satisfy the homogeneous system:

\displaystyle|\eta|^{2}\alpha-(\eta,\epsilon)\beta=\lambda\beta

(4.11)

\displaystyle(\epsilon,\eta)\alpha-|\epsilon|^{2}\beta=\lambda\alpha

(4.12)

Once solved for
\alpha,\beta
the system (
4.11
), (
4.12
), in terms of variables
\epsilon,\eta
we get for the eigenfunctions

\psi_{\pm}(x.\omega)\doteq u(x,\omega)\pm iv(x,\omega)
the following expressions:

\displaystyle u(x,\omega)=\epsilon(x)/|\epsilon|

(4.13)

\displaystyle w(x,\omega)=(\epsilon(x)(\epsilon,\eta)-\eta(x)|\epsilon|^{2})/(\omega|\epsilon|)

(4.14)

As in the finite
N
case,
u(x,\omega)
and
w(x,\omega)
have norm one and are mutually orthogonal. Accordingly., the kernel
A(x,x^{\prime})
of the integral operator
A
can be written in the form:

A(x,x^{\prime})=i\omega(u(x,\omega)w(x^{\prime},\omega)-u(x^{\prime},\omega)w(x,\omega))

(4.15)

Now, let us assume that the system enjoys an equilibrium configuration, namely that there exist at least one point
P^{0},Q^{0}
in the
P,Q
-plane such that
the function
z(x)\doteq\exp[P^{0}\epsilon(x)+Q^{0}\eta(x)+\kappa(x)]
fulfills the properties
(z,\epsilon)=0
,
(z,\eta)=1
, and ask whether there exist small oscillations around that equilibrium configuration.
To this aim, we set
P=P^{0}+\delta_{1},\quad Q=Q^{0}+\delta_{2}
, and consider a first order expansion of the exponential, setting

\exp[P\epsilon(x)+Q\eta(x)+\kappa(x)]\simeq z(x)(1+\delta_{1}\epsilon(x)+\delta_{2}\eta(x))

Accordingly, we get the following linear system of differential equations for the unknowns
\delta_{1},\delta_{2}
:

\displaystyle\dot{\delta}_{1}=-{\cal A}\delta_{1}-{\cal B}\delta_{2},

(4.16)

\displaystyle\dot{\delta}_{2}={\cal C}\delta_{1}+{\cal A}\delta_{2}.

(4.17)

In the previous we have denoted:

\displaystyle{\cal A}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dxz(x)\epsilon(x)\eta(x)

(4.18)

\displaystyle{\cal B}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dxz(x)\eta(x)^{2}

(4.19)

\displaystyle{\cal C}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dxz(x)\epsilon(x)^{2}

(4.20)

We notice that
z(x)
can be interpreted as a weight function in the previous integrals.

The nature of the system (
4.16
-
4.17
) is fixed by the solution of the secular equation:

\lambda^{2}-{\cal A}^{2}+{\cal B}{\cal C}=0

(4.21)

It easy to see that

-{\cal A}^{2}+{\cal B}{\cal C}=(1/2)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx^{\prime}z(x)z(x^{\prime})[\epsilon(x)\eta(x^{\prime})-\eta(x)\epsilon(x^{\prime})]^{2}

(4.22)

where (
4.22
) is the trace of the square of the matrix
M

M=\begin{pmatrix}-{\cal A}&-{\cal}B\\
{\cal C}&{\cal A}\end{pmatrix}

(4.23)

hence, the linearized motion will be periodic around the centrum
P^{0},Q^{0}
with period

\omega={\sqrt{-{\cal A}^{2}+{\cal B}{\cal C}}}

.

provided
z(x)
exists as a positive real function a.e. on
{\mathbb{R}}
.

In the next Section, in parallel with the infinite discrete case, we will give some general expressions for the equations of motion. Again, in order to be operative, we assume that the functions
\epsilon(x)
and
\eta(x)
have an opposite parity with respect to the function
\kappa(x)
. In the following subsections we will consider the case of a Kernel given by an even function, and hence
\epsilon(x)
and
\eta(x)
odd, and the case of a Kernel given by an odd function, and hence
\epsilon(x)
and
\eta(x)
even.

4.2
The case of an even Kernel

Let us specify the functions
\epsilon
and
\eta
as:

\epsilon(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2L}}\tilde{\epsilon}(x),\quad\eta(x)=-\eta(-x)

(4.24)

where
\tilde{\epsilon}(x)^{2}=1
for
x\in[-L,L]
,
\epsilon(x)=0
for
x\notin[-L,L]
,
\epsilon(x)
odd and
\eta(x)
odd. We assume that both
\epsilon
and
\eta
are square integrable and normalized to 1. As for the Kernel
\kappa(x)
, it is represented by square integrable even functions, compactly defined in an interval or defined on the whole real line. Let us start from the equation for
\dot{Q}
. We have

\dot{Q}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\epsilon(x)\exp[P\epsilon(x)+Q\eta(x)+\kappa(x)]

(4.25)

We give the following

Proposition 4.1

Let
s(\xi)
be a bounded rapidly decreasing function and consider the following expression:

S(P,x,L)=\int_{-L}^{L}\epsilon(\xi)\exp(P\epsilon(\xi))f(xs(\xi))d\xi,

(4.26)

where
f(x)
is a continuous function of
x
and
\epsilon(\xi)
is given in (
4.24
). Then, for any finite values of
x
one has

\lim_{L\to\infty}S(P,x,L)=Pf(0).

(4.27)

Proof
: by taking the Taylor expansion of the exponential we get

S(P,x,L)=\int_{-L}^{L}\epsilon(\xi)(1+P\epsilon(\xi)+\frac{P^{2}}{2}\epsilon(\xi)+\ldots)f(xs(\xi))d\xi.

(4.28)

The term proportional to
\epsilon^{2}
is given by

P\int_{-L}^{L}\epsilon(\xi)^{2}f(xs(\xi))d\xi=\frac{P}{2L}\int_{-L}^{L}f(xs(\xi))d\xi

(4.29)

Consider the following expression

F(x,L)=\frac{1}{2L}\int_{-L}^{L}\left(f(xs(\xi))-f(0)\right)d\xi

(4.30)

If the integral is finite for
L\to\infty
, then in the limit
L\to\infty
one has
\lim_{L\to\infty}F(x,L)=0
, hence

\lim_{L\to\infty}\frac{1}{2L}\int_{-L}^{L}f(xs(\xi))d\xi=f(0).

(4.31)

If the integral diverges for
L\to\infty
, by the L’Hôspital rule one has

\lim_{L\to\infty}\frac{1}{2L}\int_{-L}^{L}\left(f(xs(\xi))-f(0)\right)d\xi=\lim_{L\to\infty}\frac{f(xs(L))+f(xs(-L))-2f(0)}{2}=0,

(4.32)

where in the last equation we use the fact that
s(\xi)\in S^{\infty}
.. Put in another way, in the Taylor series of the integral of
f(xs(\xi))
(
4.29
) around
x=0
, only the first term
f(0)
gives a finite contribution, all the others approaches zero in the limit
L\to\infty
.

From the previous result it follows that all the powers of
\epsilon
greater than two doesn’t give a contribution in the limit
L\to\infty
.

Let us look at the term linear in
\epsilon
in (
4.26
). One has

\int_{-L}^{L}\epsilon(\xi)f(xs(\xi))d\xi=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2L}}\int_{0}^{L}\tilde{\epsilon}\left(f(xs(\xi))-f(xs(-\xi))\right)d\xi

(4.33)

By adding and subtracting
f(0)
the previous integral can be written as

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2L}}\int_{0}^{L}\tilde{\epsilon}\Big(f(xs(\xi))-f(0)\Big)d\xi-\int_{0}^{L}\tilde{\epsilon}\Big(f(xs(-\xi))-f(0)\Big)d\xi

(4.34)

If
f(x)
is Lipschitz at
x=0
then one has, for some constant
C
,
|f(xs(\xi))-f(0)|\leq C|xs(\xi)|

\begin{split}&|\int_{-L}^{L}\epsilon(\xi)f(xs(\xi))d\xi|\leq\frac{C|x|}{\sqrt{2L}}\int_{0}^{L}\left(|s(\xi)-s(-\xi)|\right)d\xi\end{split}

(4.35)

and since
s(\xi)
is an
S^{\infty}
function the integral is bounded for any
L
and so the left hand side approaches zero in the limit
L\to\infty

\square
.

Corollary 4.2

The equation of motion (
4.25
) reduces, in the limit

, to

\dot{Q}=P.

(4.36)

Indeed, it is sufficient to set

).

Let us now look at the equation of motion for

. One has

(4.37)

We use the following Proposition

Proposition 4.3

Let
\eta(\xi)
and
s(\xi)
be two rapidly decreasing functions and consider the following expression:

T(P,x,L)=\int_{-L}^{L}\eta(\xi)\exp(P\epsilon(\xi))f(xs(\xi))d\xi,

(4.38)

where
f(x)
is a continuous function of
x
and
\epsilon(\xi)
is given in (

). Then, for any finite values of

\lim_{L\to\infty}T(P,x,L)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\eta(\xi)f(xs(\xi))d\xi

Proof
: For
\xi>\xi_{0}
, since
s(\xi)\to 0
for
\xi\to\infty
, by continuity one has
|f(xs(\xi))-f(0)|<\delta(x)
or
|f(xs(\xi))|<|f(0)|+\delta(x)

So one has

\begin{split}&|\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\eta(\xi)f(xs(\xi))d\xi|=|\int_{|t|<t_{0}}\eta(\xi)f(xs(\xi))d\xi+\int_{|t|\geq t_{0}}\eta(\xi)f(xs(\xi))d\xi|\leq\\
&\leq\int_{|t|<t_{0}}|\eta(\xi)f(xs(\xi))|d\xi+(|f(0)|+\delta(x))\int_{|t|\geq t_{0}}|\eta(\xi)|d\xi,\end{split}

(4.40)

i.e. the integral
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\eta(\xi)f(xs(\xi))d\xi
is finite since
\eta(\xi)
is in

.
It follows that if one considers the Taylor expansion of

in the integral (

survives in the limit

and we get (
4.39
).
\square

By using Proposition (

) we get the following equations of motion for

\mathcal{H}=\frac{P^{2}}{2}-Q+\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp(\kappa(\xi))\left(\cosh(Q\eta(\xi))-1\right)d\xi

(4.43)

4.3
The case of an odd Kernel

Now we specify the functions
\epsilon
and

\epsilon(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2L}}\tilde{\epsilon}(x),\quad\eta(x)=\eta(-x)

(4.44)

where
\tilde{\epsilon}(x)^{2}=1
for

,

for

\lim_{L\to\infty}\int_{-L}^{L}\epsilon(\xi)d\xi=o(\sqrt{L}).

(4.45)

For the equation of motion for

Proposition 4.4

Let
s(\xi)

M(P,x,L)=\int_{-L}^{L}\epsilon(\xi)\exp(P\epsilon(\xi))f(xs(\xi))d\xi,

(4.46)

where

is a continuous function of

and

) and satisfies (

\lim_{L\to\infty}M(P,x,L)=Pf(0).

(4.47)

Proof

M(P,x,L)=\int_{-L}^{L}\epsilon(\xi)\left(1+P\epsilon(\xi)+\frac{P^{2}}{2}\epsilon(\xi)^{2}+\ldots\right)f(xs(\xi))d\xi.

(4.48)

The term proportional to
\epsilon^{2}
is:

P\int_{-L}^{L}\epsilon(\xi)^{2}f(xs(\xi))d\xi=\frac{1}{2L}\int_{-L}^{L}f(xs(\xi))d\xi.

(4.49)

Exactly as for the equation (
4.29
), the previous expressions equals
Pf(0)
. It remains only the term linear in
\epsilon
to look at, since all the powers of
\epsilon
greater than
2
in the Taylor expansion (
4.48
) do not contribute in the limit
L\to\infty
. The linear term in
\epsilon
is

\int_{-L}^{L}\epsilon(\xi)f(xs(\xi))d\xi=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2L}}\int_{-L}^{L}\tilde{\epsilon}(\xi)f(xs(\xi))d\xi.

(4.50)

Due to the condition (
4.45
), since
f(xs(\xi))\to f(0)
as
\xi\to\infty
, the integral on the right hand side is
o(\frac{1}{\sqrt{L}})
and it follows that the contribution from the term linear in
\epsilon
in (
4.46
) vanishes. Indeed the integral (
4.50
) can be written as

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2L}}\int_{-L}^{L}\tilde{\epsilon}(\xi)f(xs(\xi))d\xi=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2L}}\int_{-L}^{L}\tilde{\epsilon}(\xi)\left(f(xs(\xi))-f(0)\right)d\xi+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2L}}\int_{-L}^{L}\tilde{\epsilon}(\xi)f(0)d\xi.

(4.51)

The last integral is equal to zero in the limit
L\to\infty
due to condition (
4.45
). For the other integral, assume that
f(x)
is Lipschitz at
x=0
. Then one has, for some constant
C
,
|f(xs(\xi))-f(0)|\leq C|xs(\xi)|
, giving

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2L}}|\int_{-L}^{L}\tilde{\epsilon}(\xi)\left(f(xs(\xi))-f(0)\right)d\xi|\leq\frac{C|x|}{\sqrt{2L}}\int_{-L}^{L}|s(\xi)|d\xi

(4.52)

and since
s(\xi)
is a rapidly decreasing function the left hand side in (
4.52
) approaches to zero in the limit
L\to\infty

\square
.

The equation of motion for
Q
follows from the previous Proposition by taking
f(xs(\xi))=\exp(Q\eta(\xi)+\kappa(\xi))
. We get

\dot{Q}=P.

(4.53)

For the equation of motion for
P
we can use Proposition (
4.3
), still valid if
\epsilon(\xi)
is given as in (
4.44
). We get

\dot{P}=1-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\eta(\xi)\exp(Q\eta(\xi)+\kappa(\xi))d\xi.

(4.54)

The function
\eta(\xi)
is even and
\kappa(\xi)
is odd, whereas the interval of summation is symmetric. It follows that only the even portion of
\exp(\kappa(\xi))
survives, giving

\dot{P}=1-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\eta(\xi)\exp(Q\eta(\xi))\cosh(\kappa(\xi))d\xi.

(4.55)

The corresponding Hamiltonian is given by

\mathcal{H}=\frac{P^{2}}{2}-Q+\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp(Q\eta(\xi))\left(\cosh(\kappa(\xi))-1\right)d\xi.

(4.56)

As we pointed out for the discrete case, it is possible to take a flow that is a combination of the Hamiltonian containing the even Kernel
\mathcal{H}_{e}
(
4.43
), and the Hamiltonian containing the odd Kernel
\mathcal{H}_{o}
(
4.56

\alpha\mathcal{H}_{e}+(1-\alpha)\mathcal{H}_{o}=\frac{P^{2}}{2}-Q+\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\alpha e^{Q\eta^{o}(\xi)+\kappa^{e}(\xi)}+(1-\alpha)e^{Q\eta^{e}(\xi)+\kappa^{o}(\xi)}-1\right)d\xi.

(4.57)

where the superscripts on the sequences remind if they are odd or even.

The Remarks (

) given for the discrete case can be easily translated to the continuous case.

Remark 4.5

The equations of motion, both for an odd Kernel and on even one, are given by

\dot{Q}=P,\quad\dot{P}=1-\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\eta(\xi)\exp(Q\eta(\xi)+\kappa(\xi))d\xi,

where the functions
\epsilon(x)

\kappa(x)

Again, the function defining

The motion must be bounded for the same reasons given in Remark (

\mathcal{H}=T+V=\frac{P^{2}}{2}-Q+\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\left(\exp(Q\eta(\xi)+\kappa(\xi))-1\right)dx,

the potential

Remark 4.6

If the function
\eta(x)

The previous Remark follows by looking at the properties of the system (

Remark 4.7

5.3
An example for the continuous case

We choose for
\epsilon(x)
the class of odd piecewise constant functions with a compact support in
(-L,L)
. The function
\eta(x)
also is odd, whereas
\kappa(x)
must be even. By requiring the
L^{2}
normalization for
\epsilon
and
\eta
we can write (
a
and
C
are two parameters)

\begin{split}&\epsilon(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2L}}\tilde{\epsilon}(x),\quad\tilde{\epsilon}^{2}=1.\\
&\eta(x)=\frac{a}{\sqrt{1-\exp(-2a^{2}L)}}\exp(-a^{2}|x|)\tilde{\epsilon}(x),\\
&\kappa(x)=C\exp(-a^{2}|x|).\end{split}

(5.26)

Let us look at the equations of motion for
P
(
4.4
) and
Q
(
4.5
). From the general formulae (
4.36
) and (
4.41
) we get

\dot{Q}=P,\quad\dot{P}=1-\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\eta(\xi)\exp(Q\eta(\xi)+\kappa(\xi))d\xi

(5.27)

We take the Taylor series expansion of the exponential in the equation for
\dot{P}
and then use the binomial formula to get

\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\eta(\xi)\exp(Q\eta(\xi)+\kappa(\xi))d\xi=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{j=0}^{n}{n\choose j}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\xi\frac{Q^{j}\eta(\xi)^{j+1}\kappa(\xi)^{n-j}}{n!}

(5.28)

Since
\eta
is an odd function, whereas
\kappa
is even, only when
j
is odd there is a contribution different from zero. So, by taking into account the expressions (
5.26
) and by noticing that
\tilde{\epsilon}^{j+1}=1
for
j
odd we can write:

\sum_{j=0}^{n}{n\choose j}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\xi\frac{Q^{j}\eta(\xi)^{j+1}\kappa(\xi)^{n-j}}{n!}=\sum_{j=0}^{n}{n\choose j}\frac{1-\cos(\pi j)}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\xi\frac{Q^{j}C^{n-j}\alpha^{j+1}e^{-(n+1)\alpha^{2}|\xi|}}{n!}

(5.29)

that is, by performing the integral

\sum_{j=0}^{n}{n\choose j}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\xi\frac{Q^{j}\eta(\xi)^{j+1}\kappa(\xi)^{n-j}}{n!}=\sum_{j=0}^{n}{n\choose j}\frac{\alpha^{j-1}Q^{j}C^{n-j}(1-\cos(\pi j))}{(n+1)!}.

(5.30)

The sum over
j
can be carried out by using the formula for the binomial expansion. The sum in equation (
5.30
) is just given by

\frac{(C+Qa)^{n}}{a(n+1)!}-\frac{(C-Qa)^{n}}{a(n+1)!}.

(5.31)

Going back to equation (
5.28
) we can write

\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\eta(\xi)\exp(Q\eta(\xi)+\kappa(\xi))d\xi=\sum_{n=1}\left(\frac{(C+Qa)^{n}}{a(n+1)!}-\frac{(C-Qa)^{n}}{a(n+1)!}\right),

(5.32)

that can be summed to

\frac{e^{C+aQ}-1}{a(C+aQ)}-\frac{e^{C-aQ}-1}{a(C-aQ)}

(5.33)

giving the following equations of motion

\dot{Q}=P,\quad\dot{P}=1-\frac{e^{C+aQ}-1}{a(C+aQ)}+\frac{e^{C-aQ}-1}{a(C-aQ)}.

(5.34)

The equilibrium position are given by (
P=0,Q=Q_{0})
, where
Q_{0}
is the root of the transcendental equation:

\frac{e^{C+aQ}-1}{a(C+aQ)}+\frac{e^{C-aQ}-1}{a(C-aQ)}=1.

(5.35)

As expected, this equation has always just one real root. For the sake of simplicity, let us just set
a=1
. Then, both the functions
\frac{e^{x}-1}{x}
and
\frac{e^{-x}-1}{x}
are increasing, so that the left hand side of (
5.35
), as a function of
Q
, is decreasing when
a=1
. Also, it decreases from
+\infty
to
-\infty
, crossing the
Q
-line only once. By looking at the properties of the functions
\frac{e^{x}-1}{x}
and
\frac{e^{-x}-1}{x}
it is also possible to give more precise statements about the intervals on the
Q
-line containing the root. For example, if
C\in(0,C^{*})
where
C^{*}=\frac{x^{*}}{2}
and
x^{*}
is the only real root of the equation
\frac{e^{x}-1}{x}=2
(
x^{*}\sim 1.25643
), then
Q_{0}\in(C,x^{*}-C)
. In general, if
C
is large and negative, then
Q_{0}\sim C
, whereas if
C
is positive then
Q_{0}\in(0,x^{0})
, decreasing to
0
when
C
increases. Here
x^{0}
corresponds to the root of (
5.35
) when
C=0
, i.e.
x^{0}\sim 0.93082
.

This equilibrium position is stable and indeed is a center. In fact, the linearization of the dynamical system corresponding to the Hamiltonian (
4.8
) around
Q_{0}
follows by letting
P=\delta p
and
Q=Q_{0}+\delta q
with
p
and
q
satisfying

\dot{q}=p,\quad\dot{p}=G(Q_{0})q,

(5.36)

where
G(Q)
is the derivative of the left hand side of (
5.35
). We have shown however that the left hand side of (
5.35
) is decreasing, so the solution of (
5.36
) is oscillatory.

In this special case, in the limit
L\to\infty
one gets for the quantities in (
4.18
)-(
4.20
):
{\cal A}=0,~{\cal C}=1
, while
{\cal B}
is given by the following more complicated expression:

{\cal B}=1/(C-Q_{0})^{2}[\exp(C-Q_{0})(C-Q_{0}-1)+1]+1/(C+Q_{0})^{2}[\exp(C+Q_{0})(C+Q_{0}-1)+1]

As expected,
{\cal B}
coincides with
F^{\prime}(Q_{0})
(
5.35
), so that the linearized equations yield a harmonic motion.

6
Concluding remarks

In this paper we proposed an extension of the integrable Volterra model to the case of infinitely many species, either countable or uncountable. The corresponding models have been shown to possess the property of being maximally superintegrable. In our opinion, though the generalizations proposed are non trivial, they do not lead to an enrichment of the dynamics of the system. Indeed, within the class of sequences or continuous functions we have considered, in the infinite limit, both discrete and continuous, the behaviour of the system looks even simpler than the one characterizing the case of a finite number of species. We may assert that the maximal superintegrability, already discovered in
[
17
]
, gets in a sense strengthened by the infinite limit, inasmuch as there exists a general formula showing that, in canonical coordinates, the Hamiltonian takes, in way unexpectedly, the classical one body feature
{\mathcal{H}}=\frac{P^{2}}{2}+V(Q)
. Moreover, the potential
V(Q)
supports just one equilibrium position in the general case, around which the motion foliates in a family of closed orbits and represents an infinite potential well. In a given class of special cases one can have open orbits. These results are strongly related to the family of sequences for the growth coefficients
\epsilon_{n}
or the family of growth function
\epsilon(x)
chosen. In principle, a different choice of these coefficients or function can destroy the simple Hamiltonian structure described in this work. The instances here given are, in our opinion, significant for the following reasons: they give, in the infinite limit, both in the discrete and in the continuous case, a finite and specific Hamiltonian model. Also, the uncountable case can be seen as the natural limit of the denumerable one. Further, the sequences
\eta_{n}
and
\kappa_{n}
, or the functions
\eta(x)
and
\kappa(x)
for the continuous case, are essentially arbitrary (apart from the fact of belonging to a suitable summable or integrable space) and the properties of the corresponding Hamiltonian models are universal, in the sense that, fixed the sequences
\eta_{n}
and
\kappa_{n}
or the functions
\eta(x)
and
\kappa(x)
, the behavior of the orbits are fixed (or periodic or open for specific cases). Finally, the connection between the growth coefficients and the automatic sequences, like the Rudin-Shapiro sequence, briefly mentioned in the text, is somehow unexpected and surely deserves much attention in future works. As for the unanswered questions: we have not been able to find an explicit formula for the period of the orbits, apart the linearized case. For a finite number of species we have shown
[
18
]
that, in suitable cases, the period of the linearized system is a very good approximation of the period of the orbits of the non linearized model. We expect that, if the nonlinearity is not too strong, the observation remains true also in our examples, but we didn’t give any quantitative statement about this. Finally, it would be interesting to expand the class of sequences or functions for
\epsilon_{n}
and
\epsilon(x)
here given to other families: as said, the Hamiltonian structure that we obtained may be destroyed and richer models, for example with a greater number of equilibrium positions and different type of behaviors for the orbits, could be found.
