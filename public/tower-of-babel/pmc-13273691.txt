Effects of ultrasound-assisted tumbling on physicochemical properties, myofibrillar protein structure, and protein oxidation during beef curing
Authors: Lei Jin, Jian Zhang, Ruyu Zhang, Lujuan Xing, Min Li, Xianqi Yao, Wangang Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

Introduction

Curing is one of the main techniques in beef processing that can improve the flavor, tenderness, water-holding capacity (WHC), and storage stability of the product [1], [2]. However, in traditional curing processes, the intricate tissue structure of beef restricts the diffusion rate of brine, largely reducing production efficiency [3]. Thus, novel processing methods have been explored to improve the curing efficiency of beef products. Tumbling is an effective curing method that can destroy muscle cell structures and facilitate brine distribution through friction and collisions between the meat and the equipment. Meanwhile, Yin et al. [4] found that the tumbling process enhanced the textural attributes and sensory assessment scores of hams by promoting protein exudation and cellular enzyme activity. Nevertheless, conventional tumbling still suffers from prolonged curing time and the shortcomings of uneven curing [5]. Consequently, there is an urgent need to develop novel methods to complement conventional tumbling brining, facilitating the efficient production of high-quality beef products.

Ultrasound, as an environmentally friendly and efficient physical-assisted processing technology, has been extensively applied in meat research on account of its physical effects, including acoustic cavitation, shear forces, shock waves, and pressure [6]. Sanches et al. [7] found that the ultrasound process reduced protein denaturation temperature and water mobility of cured beef, without affecting lipid oxidation and pH. Alarcon-Rojo et al. [8] reported that ultrasound application facilitated NaCl diffusion and accelerated the curing process. Based on these findings, it is speculated that ultrasound could be an ideal technology for assisting conventional tumbling, collectively shortening curing cycles while enhancing meat quality characteristics. However, studies on the impact of ultrasound-assisted tumbling curing on the quality attributes, protein structure, and oxidative state of beef remain limited.

This study aimed to explore the influences of tumbling treatment combined with various ultrasonic power levels on beef quality, and to evaluate changes in quality parameters including the tumbling yield, color, cooking loss, shear force, and texture properties. Furthermore, the potential mechanisms underlying quality changes in beef during ultrasound-assisted tumbling were elucidated by analyzing low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (LF-NMR), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), myofibrillar fragmentation index, protein carbonyl and sulfhydryl contents, and secondary structure. The results obtained in the present study not only provide new perspectives for the enhancement of beef quality but also offer a theoretical basis for the industrial implementation of ultrasound-assisted tumbling.

Materials and methods

Preparation of samples and brine

Thirty beef knuckles of Simmental cattle (48 h post-mortem, 24 months of age, 725 ± 25 kg body weight, pH 5.55–5.65) were obtained from Huasheng Meat Company Limited (Yangxin, Shandong, China). The raw materials were trimmed of all visible fat and then partitioned into 120 uniform cubes measuring 10 × 10 × 10 cm3. These cubes were randomly allocated into 30 groups, each consisting of four pieces. Salt solution was formulated following the protocol reported by Zhang et al. [9].

Tumbling treatment

Beef samples were subjected to tumbling treatment using laboratory-developed ultrasound-assisted tumbling equipment [9]. The samples were randomly assigned to five tumbling treatment groups, including a single tumbling treatment (ST, conventional tumbling without ultrasound treatment) and four ultrasound-assisted tumbling treatments (UT) operated at ultrasonic powers of 100, 300, 500, and 700 W, respectively. Curing was carried out in a tumbling drum, and the meat-to-brine ratio was maintained at 100:35 (w/v). All tumbling treatments were conducted at 4 °C under a vacuum level of −0.08 MPa. The tumbling durations were 30, 60, 90, 120, 150, and 180 min, respectively.

Physicochemical properties of cured beef

Tumbling yield

After tumbling treatment, the beef samples were collected, and excess surface brine was blotted dry. The tumbling yield of beef samples was calculated using Eq. (1) as described by Zhang et al. [5].(1)Tumbling yield=M1-M0M0where M0 and M1 denote the initial and final mass of beef samples, respectively.

Protein content in the brine

The concentration of protein was quantified employing the protocol of Gornall et al. [10]. Following the tumbling treatment, the brine was collected, clarified to remove suspended meat residues, and subsequently analyzed for protein content.

Low-field nuclear magnetic resonance and magnetic resonance imaging

The water status of beef after tumbling treatment was determined using the LF-NMR analyzer (PQ001-GU, Suzhou, China). The samples were prepared with uniform dimensions of 2 × 1 × 1 cm3 and loaded into NMR tubes following the procedure reported by Santos et al. [11]. The parameters of Carr–Purcell–Meiboom–Gill (CPMG) program were set as follows: sampling bandwidth was 100, number of scans was 4, and number of echoes was 5,000. Then, MRI was observed on the same samples using a repetition time of 600 ms, with four signal averages and two imaging slices.

Color

Color assessment of beef was carried out using a colorimeter (CM-700d, Tokyo, Japan) following the modified protocol established by Zhang et al. [12]. Before data collection, the colorimeter underwent calibration with a standard white plate. Three random positions were selected for measurement on each sample, and the corresponding color parameters were obtained.

Cooking loss

Beef samples were weighed, vacuum-sealed, and heat-treated until their internal temperature attained 72 °C, following the modified protocol reported by Gu et al. [13]. Cooking loss was quantified based on Eq. (2).(2)Cooking loss=W0-W1W0where W0 and W1 denote the sample mass prior to and following cooking, respectively.

Texture analysis

The cooked beef was sectioned into 1 cm3 cubes. Measurements were performed using a texture analyzer (TMS-PRO, Virginia, USA) following the modified procedure of Zou et al. [14]. A P50 cylindrical probe was employed with a 50 % compression strain. The velocity applied during the test was adjusted to 2 mm/s.

Shear force

Uniform rectangular strips (5 × 1 × 1 cm) were obtained from the cooked samples, following the modified protocol of Wang et al. [15]. Subsequently, the beef was sheared perpendicularly using a tenderness meter, and the data (N) were acquired.

Oxidative and structural characterization of myofibrillar proteins

Extraction of myofibrillar proteins

Myofibrillar proteins (MPs) were extracted using buffers prepared based on the previously reported protocol [16]. Briefly, beef samples (50 g) were homogenized with three volumes (w/v) of isolation buffer at 9,000 rpm for three cycles, each consisting of 10 s of homogenization followed by a 10 s interval. After centrifugation, the precipitates were homogenized with three volumes (w/v) of another buffer at 9,000 rpm.

Myofibrillar fragmentation index

The myofibrillar fragmentation index (MFI) was quantified by adhering to the established protocol detailed by Wang et al. [17]. Following the dilution of MPs, the absorbance was recorded at 540 nm using a spectrophotometer (Cary 3500, Agilent, USA), with the MFI subsequently derived by multiplying the obtained value by a conversion factor of 200.

Carbonyl content

Protein carbonyl content was quantified following the approach described by Li et al. [18]. Briefly, the MP samples were combined with 10 mM 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine prepared in phosphate buffer. After vortexing for 60 s, the trichloroacetic acid (20 %) was incorporated to ensure the complete cessation of the reaction. After centrifugation, the obtained protein pellets were rinsed with an ethanol: acetic acid mixture (1:1, v/v). The final residue was re-dissolved in guanidine hydrochloride, followed by measurement of the absorbance at 370 nm.

Sulfhydryl content

In accordance with the procedure documented by Zhang et al. [19], the content of total and reactive sulfhydryl groups was assessed in the samples. For total SH determination, 0.5 mL of the MP solution was combined with tris(hydroxymethyl)aminomethane hydrochloride buffer. Following the addition of 0.1 mL of 10 mM 5,5′-dithiobis-(2-nitrobenzoic acid), the samples were kept in a light-shielded environment and incubated for 25 min. For reactive SH assessment, the MP samples (4 mL) were incubated with 5,5′-dithiobis-(2-nitrobenzoic acid) (0.1 mL) for 1 h. The absorbance was promptly recorded at a wavelength of 412 nm.

Protein secondary structure

The protein secondary structure characteristics of the beef were established following the approach reported by Zheng et al. [20]. The beef was finely ground and homogeneously blended with potassium bromide at a mass ratio of 2:100, followed by the formation of translucent sheets through compression. The prepared sheets were analyzed via a Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) within the 4,000 to 400 cm-1 region.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate, and data were shown as mean ± standard error. One-way ANOVA was adopted for intergroup comparison using SPSS 21.0 software. Statistical significance was predefined at P < 0.05. Furthermore, Origin 2023 (OriginLab Corp., USA) was utilized to construct all graphical illustrations.

Results and discussion

Tumbling yield

Tumbling yield is used to evaluate the capacity of meat samples to absorb brine during the curing process, which is crucial for cost control and product quality enhancement. As shown in Table 1, the tumbling yield was influenced by ultrasonic power, processing time, and their interaction (P < 0.05). With the extension of curing time, both the ST and UT groups showed an upward trend in tumbling yield. Under identical curing times, the tumbling yield of the UT groups (300, 500, and 700 W) was greater than that of the ST group (P < 0.05), verifying that ultrasound-assisted processing enhanced the tumbling yield of beef. Zhang et al. [5] observed a notable enhancement in the tumbling yield of pork after ultrasound treatment for 120 min at 300 and 500 W compared with single tumbling. These phenomena might be due to ultrasound-induced cavitation, which disrupted the compact structure of myofibrils, widened the gaps within the muscle fibers, and formed microchannels at the interfaces, thereby reducing the resistance to brine penetration into muscle tissue [21]. Additionally, ultrasound enhanced the mass transfer rate of sodium chloride, facilitating better binding of chloride ions to myosin side chains. This increased electrostatic repulsion between myofibrils, enlarging the gaps between fibers and improving brine absorption [22]. However, some studies reported that ultrasound had no significant influence on the tumbling yield of pork [12]. This discrepancy may be associated with inherent histological differences between meat species. Compared to pork, beef possesses coarser muscle fibers, a denser myofibrillar network, and a higher degree of connective tissue cross-linking [23]. Consequently, the relatively tender matrix of pork may reach its maximum brine absorption capacity under conventional tumbling. Protein content in the brine

The protein content in the brine is a key parameter for evaluating the dissolution and migration behavior of proteins from meat samples during the curing process. As documented in Table 2, processing time, ultrasonic power, and their interactive effects exerted an influence on the protein content in the brine (P < 0.05). With prolonged curing duration, the protein content in the brine of both the ST and UT groups (100 and 300 W) showed an increased trend, and the highest protein content in the brine (7.54 mg/mL) was observed under the 300 W ultrasound treatment for 180 min. Additionally, at the same tumbling time (120, 150, or 180 min), the UT groups (100, 300, and 500 W) exhibited greater protein content than the ST group (P < 0.05). This revealed that ultrasound-assisted tumbling brining further facilitated the release and solubilization of beef proteins. Comparable observations were noted by McDonnell et al. [24], who confirmed that ultrasound raised protein content in brine during pork curing. These observations might stem from ultrasonic cavitation, which disrupted the microstructure of muscle, enlarged the gaps between myofibrils, and loosened the tissue framework, thereby accelerating the penetration of brine into the internal regions of the beef. Meanwhile, the elevated ionic concentration in the brine enhanced electrostatic repulsion between myofilaments, promoting the extraction of myofibrillar proteins [5]. Nevertheless, with increasing ultrasonic power up to 700 W, the protein content in the brine after prolonged tumbling treatment (150 or 180 min) was lower than that observed in the 300 W and 500 W groups. For the L* value, all treatment groups exhibited an upward trend with extended curing time, indicating increased surface lightness of the meat samples. At tumbling times of 150 or 180 min, UT-300, UT-500, and UT-700 showed greater L* values than the ST group (P < 0.05). Such observations were potentially attributable to the implosion of cavitation bubbles and micro-streaming, which compromised the integrity of muscle fibers and expanded the interstitial spaces among myofibrils. This promoted brine penetration and distribution, increased meat moisture content, enhanced light reflection, and ultimately led to elevated L* values [26].

The a* value of beef initially increased and subsequently declined as the tumbling duration was prolonged. During the early tumbling stage (30, 60, or 90 min), ultrasound treatment (300, 500, and 700 W) enhanced the a* value (P < 0.05), suggesting that ultrasound promoted the conversion of myoglobin to oxymyoglobin, yielding a brighter red color profile. The enhanced redness might be ascribed to the production of hydroxyl radicals with strong reactivity during acoustic cavitation. The resulting oxidative environment promoted a more rapid transition of myoglobin to its oxygenated state in the brine, increasing the redness of the beef [27]. However, when the ultrasonic power was excessively high (700 W) or the brining duration was prolonged (150 or 180 min), the a* value declined (P < 0.05). This decrease was driven by the excessive accumulation of cavitation-induced radicals. Specifically, an overabundance of ROS can directly attack the heme group, accelerating the oxidation of the central iron atom from the ferrous (Fe2+) to the ferric (Fe3+) state, which converts red oxymyoglobin into brown metmyoglobin [28]. In addition, the high pressure and temperature generated by ultrasound disrupted the myoglobin structure in beef and accelerated pigment loss, ultimately leading to a further decline in redness [28].

Low-field nuclear magnetic resonance and magnetic resonance imaging

Fig. 2 illustrates the alterations of water status in beef induced by ultrasound-assisted tumbling treatment. Specifically, T2b, T21, and T22 represent bound water, immobile water, and free water, respectively, while P2b, P21, and P22 denote their corresponding proportions. The T2b values increased with increasing ultrasonic power and treatment time, indicating that ultrasound-assisted tumbling enhanced the mobility of bound water. This result can be rationalized by the cavitation effect, which modified the structure of myofibrillar proteins and transformed part of the bound water from a highly restricted state to a weakly bound state [29]. In addition, with increasing ultrasonic power and prolonged processing time (150 or 180 min), higher T21 values were detected in the UT groups (300, 500, and 700 W) relative to the ST group (P < 0.05), suggesting that ultrasound-assisted tumbling treatment affected the mobility of water within myofibril and protein structures. Furthermore, when processing times were 60, 90, 120, or 150 min, the P21 values of the UT-300, UT-500, and UT-700 were greater than in the ST group (P < 0.05). This was primarily due to ultrasound-induced protein extraction, leading to extracellular water conversion and muscle cell swelling. Under swelling pressure, further aqueous diffusion into the muscle and its lattice structure occurred, followed by conversion into intracellular fluids, which manifested as an elevated immobilized water content [30]. However, opposite trends were observed for T22 and P22 compared with T21 and P21. The T22 and P22 values of the UT-300, UT-500, and UT-700 were lower than those of the ST group (P < 0.05). This indicated that ultrasound-assisted tumbling treatment restricted the mobility of free water and enhanced WHC. Fig. 2G displays the spatial distribution of water in beef samples during brining. Generally, red regions represent high proton signal intensity (high water content), whereas blue regions denote the opposite (low water content). After 180 min of ST treatment, the central region of the samples exhibited distinct orange-yellow signals, indicating incomplete moisture penetration and uneven distribution. In contrast, ultrasound treatment promoted the progressive expansion of high-intensity red signal regions from the sample surface toward the geometric center, demonstrating that the ultrasound-assisted treatment accelerated the penetration of brine into the muscle tissues. Additionally, with prolonged brining time, the uniformity and intensity of the moisture signals in the UT groups increased. At 180 min, samples in the UT groups exhibited a highly uniform and intense red signal distribution, indicating enhanced moisture diffusion. This phenomenon may be associated with ultrasound-induced cavitation, which reduced the mass transfer resistance within muscle tissues and accelerated the inward diffusion of the brine. Consequently, a deeper and more homogeneous moisture distribution was achieved during brining [30].

Cooking loss

As documented in Table 3, processing time, ultrasonic power, and their interactive effect exerted an influence on the cooking loss (P < 0.05). In general, the cooking loss of beef gradually decreased with increasing curing time, and ultrasound treatment further diminished the cooking loss. When the tumbling times were 120, 150, or 180 min, the UT-300, UT-500, and UT-700 groups exhibited reduced cooking loss relative to the ST group (P < 0.05), indicating that moderate ultrasound power helped reduce water loss and enhance the WHC of beef. This phenomenon was primarily attributed to the fact that ultrasound treatment promoted marinade diffusion between muscle fibers, increasing the extraction and dissolution of myosin and actin. These proteins formed a dense gel membrane during heating that tightly enveloped the muscle surface, effectively reducing water loss and minimizing cooking loss [13]. The observed enhancement in water retention aligns with previous research on pork, where ultrasound-assisted brining was found to enhance WHC [5]. However, when the tumbling time reached 180 min, the cooking loss of the UT-700 group increased compared to that of the UT-300 and UT-500 groups (P < 0.05), suggesting that high-intensity ultrasound adversely affected the WHC of beef. Texture analysis

Ultrasound-assisted tumbling affected the textural properties of beef (P < 0.05, Fig. 3). As curing time and ultrasonic power increased, the hardness and chewiness of all samples gradually declined. Compared with the ST group, the UT groups (300, 500, or 700 W) exhibited lower hardness and chewiness after 180 min of brining (P < 0.05). Among these treatments, the UT-700 samples exhibited the lowest hardness and chewiness, with values of 1419.10 N and 590.20 N, respectively. These results revealed that ultrasound-assisted tumbling effectively reduced the hardness and chewiness of beef. Chimenes et al. [32] reported similar findings when investigating the textural properties of ultrasound-treated beef burgers, further confirming the contribution of ultrasound in reducing the hardness and chewiness of meat products. This is attributed to the mechanical effects of ultrasound expanding the gaps between muscle fibers, leading to physical weakening of the tissue structure and cellular damage, promoting endogenous protein hydrolysis [12], [28]. The springiness of samples gradually increased with prolonged curing time and increasing ultrasonic power. Compared with the ST group, the samples in the UT groups (100, 300, 500, and 700 W) exhibited increases of 5.6 %, 9.3 %, 7.4 %, and 5.6 %, respectively, after 180 min of ultrasound-assisted tumbling treatment. This improvement in texture was likely due to enhanced myofibrillar protein content and protein–protein interactions under moderate ultrasound treatment, promoting the establishment of a compact gel structure and improving textural properties. Tenderness and myofibrillar fragmentation index

A gradual reduction in beef shear force was observed with the increase in brining time or ultrasonic power. After 180 min of treatment, the UT groups (300 W, 500 W, and 700 W) exhibited lower shear force than the ST group (P < 0.05, Fig. 4). The shear force decreased by 8.17 %, 10.21 %, and 11.78 % relative to the ST group, respectively. This tenderization effect was attributed to the transient high temperatures and microjets, which promoted brine penetration and enhanced protein hydration, ultimately leading to improved beef tenderness [12], [34]. On the other hand, ultrasound treatment improved beef tenderness by disrupting protein structures and promoting the release of proteolytic enzymes from lysosomes [35]. However, Contreras-Lopez et al. [36] found no significant influence on pork tenderness during ultrasound-assisted curing. Protein oxidation

As illustrated in Table 4, with increased ultrasonic power and prolonged tumbling time, the carbonyl and free sulfhydryl contents of beef samples increased, whereas the total sulfhydryl content decreased (P < 0.05). Under the condition of 700 W ultrasonic power and 180 min treatment duration, the levels of carbonyl groups, total sulfhydryl groups, and free sulfhydryl groups in beef reached their respective maximum values of 0.63, 53.97, and 28.36 nmol/mg. These outcomes demonstrated that ultrasound treatment significantly enhanced the degree of protein oxidation. This observation could be attributed to the acoustic cavitation induced by ultrasound, which generated highly reactive radicals from water molecules [37]. These reactive radicals have been identified as key contributors to protein oxidation by Zhang et al. [38], resulting in the production of carbonyl groups and disulfide bonds. Meanwhile, ultrasound-induced lipid oxidation produced lipid peroxidation products, such as malondialdehyde, which further reacted with proteins to form aldehyde groups, promoting protein oxidation [39]. This observation is in line with the observations made by Delles et al. [40], who demonstrated that lipid oxidation products could bind to actin, forming protein-bound aldehydes during pork protein oxidation. On the other hand, Zou et al. [14] found that disulfide bonds formed through protein oxidation could promote the construction of protein networks, which enhanced the immobile water content and strengthened the WHC of beef. This finding accords with the above results obtained from cooking loss and LF-NMR analyses. This phenomenon could be primarily accounted for by the cavitation effect, which caused myofibrillar proteins to unfold and exposed sulfhydryl groups buried within the protein structure, subsequently facilitating the oxidative conversion of reactive sulfhydryl groups to disulfide bonds [19]. However, Wang et al. [17] observed that excessive protein oxidation led to an increased formation of protein dimers, resulting in enlarged protein aggregate size and aggravated disruption of muscle structure, adversely affecting beef quality. The cooking loss results of this study substantiate this point. Specifically, when beef samples were treated at an ultrasonic power of 700 W for 180 min, the muscle structure disruption caused by excessive oxidation resulted in a cooking loss that was higher than that of the moderate-power groups (UT-300 and UT-500). Therefore, selecting appropriate ultrasound parameters to avoid excessive oxidation is crucial for enhancing beef quality.

Protein secondary structure

As shown in Fig. 5A and B, quantitative analysis of different secondary structural elements was performed via deconvolution and curve fitting of the amide I band. As ultrasonic power and tumbling time increased, the α-helix proportion gradually decreased, while the β-sheet proportion exhibited an increasing trend. Compared with the single tumbling treatment for 90 min, the ultrasound-assisted tumbling treatment for 180 min at 700 W caused a decline in α-helix proportion from 20.35 % to 15.54 % and an increase in β-sheet proportion from 21.88 % to 29.05 % (P < 0.05), whereas no variation was observed in the random coil proportion (P > 0.05). These outcomes showed that the ultrasound-assisted tumbling process caused alterations in the α-helix and β-sheet structures of proteins. This observation could be attributed to acoustic cavitation, which disrupted intramolecular hydrogen bonds within protein molecules that were essential for maintaining α-helical stability. As a result, protein structures transitioned from an ordered state to a disordered state. Consistent with our findings, Zheng et al. [20] demonstrated similar structural modifications in beef myofibrillar proteins after 640 W ultrasound treatment. Mechanistically, this ultrasound-induced disruption of intramolecular hydrogen bonds leads to the partial unfolding of protein conformations and the exposure of reactive moieties, promoting intermolecular interactions [9]. In the present study, the observed significant increase in β-sheet proportion indicates that these unfolded proteins underwent a subsequent ordered rearrangement. During this process, the establishment of new intermolecular hydrogen bonds among peptide chains stabilized the β-sheet conformations [4]. Consequently, this α-helix to β-sheet transition provides a crucial microstructural basis for our macroscopic observations. Correlation analysis

Fig. 6 illustrates the correlation analysis among beef quality attributes, moisture distribution, and myofibrillar protein structural parameters. Overall, cooking loss was positively correlated with shear force, textural properties, P22, total sulfhydryl groups, and α-helix content (P < 0.05), and negatively correlated with tumbling yield, brine protein content, P21, MFI, free sulfhydryl groups, and β-sheet content (P < 0.05). Moderate ultrasound processing contributed to the disruption of muscle fiber structure, promoting marinade penetration and protein solubilization. This process enhanced the WHC of beef, leading to a decrease in hardness and an enhancement of tenderness. Simultaneously, the cavitation effect of ultrasound disrupted the α-helix structure of proteins and promoted protein–protein interactions, which facilitated the establishment of a gel network and a reduction in cooking loss. Conclusion

This study demonstrated that ultrasound-assisted tumbling treatment positively impacted beef quality. The cavitation-generated microjets physically disrupted muscle fibers and increased MFI, directly reducing hardness and enhancing tenderness. Concurrently, the formation of microchannels within muscle tissue accelerated brine penetration and increased tumbling yield. At the molecular level, ultrasound induced partial protein unfolding (α-helix to β-sheet transition) and moderate protein oxidation (evidenced by decreased total sulfhydryls and increased carbonyls). These changes promoted intermolecular disulfide bond formation and the assembly of a dense protein network, which effectively immobilized water molecules (converting free water to immobile water) and significantly improved WHC. Scientifically, this research clarified the profound correlation between cavitation-induced protein structural modification, water migration dynamics, and meat quality. Practically, the identified optimal power range (300–500 W) provided clear guidance for industrial application, enabling shorter curing cycles, more uniform product quality, and reduced production costs, demonstrating broad prospects for meat processing.
