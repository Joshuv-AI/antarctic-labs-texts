Abstract
. Classical filtrations in probability theory formalize the accumulation of information along a linear time axis: the past is unique and the present evolves into an uncertain future. In many contexts, however, information is neither linear nor uniquely determined by a single history. In this paper we propose a geometric model of synthetic filtrations , where the present may be formed by synthesizing multiple possible pasts. To achieve this, we introduce a new category 𝚺 \mathbf{\Sigma} , an extension of the simplex category 𝚫 \mathbf{\Delta} , whose objects encode context-dependent times. Synthetic filtrations are realized as contravariant functors 𝚺 o ​ p → 𝐏𝐫𝐨𝐛 \mathbf{\Sigma}^{op}\to\mathbf{Prob} , where 𝐏𝐫𝐨𝐛 \mathbf{Prob} is the category of probability spaces with null-preserving maps. After seeing a general definition of synthetic filtrations, we define, as a concrete example, Dirichlet filtrations , where probability measures on simplices arise from Dirichlet distributions, highlighting the role of parameter and contextual uncertainty. Finally, we interpret Bayesian updating as a categorical update of a Dirichlet functor, showing how learning fits naturally into the synthetic filtration framework. This work combines categorical probability, simplicial geometry, and Bayesian statistics, and suggests new applications in finance, stochastic modeling, and subjective probability.



1.
Introduction

Filtrations are a cornerstone of probability theory,
providing a formal description of how information accumulates over time.
In the classical framework, time is modeled linearly:
the past forms a unique path leading to the present,
and the future extends from it.
This picture, though powerful, is often too restrictive.
In real-world settings—for example, finance, economics, and decision theory—the present may emerge from multiple competing pasts,
reflecting different contexts, models, or prior beliefs.

In this paper,
we develop a
geometric model of synthetic filtrations
in which time is context-dependent
and the present can be understood as a synthesis of multiple pasts.
The key construction is the category
\mathbf{\Sigma}
,
which extends the simplex category
\mathbf{\Delta}

by incorporating contextual information into the notion of time.
Objects of
\mathbf{\Sigma}
represent
times in context
,
while morphisms encode transitions between them.
This framework generalizes the traditional idea of filtrations and provides a new categorical foundation for studying information flow.

Our contributions are as follows:

•

We review categorical probability theory and generalized filtrations (
\mathcal{T}
-filtrations) as a foundation.

•

We define the category
\mathbf{\Sigma}
of context-dependent time and show how it extends the simplex category.

•

We construct
\mathbf{\Sigma}
-filtrations as contravariant functors
\mathbf{\Sigma}^{op}\to\mathbf{Prob}
.

•

We introduce
Dirichlet filtrations
, where measures on simplices are induced by Dirichlet distributions,
and analyze both parameter uncertainty and contextual uncertainty.

•

We demonstrate that Bayesian updating can be viewed as a transformation of Dirichlet functors,
providing a categorical interpretation of learning.

This geometric perspective unifies ideas from category theory, simplicial geometry, and Bayesian probability.
Beyond its conceptual novelty,
it opens the door to applications in stochastic modeling, subjective probability, and mathematical finance.

3.
Synthetic filtration

The central object of this paper is the notion of a
synthetic filtration
,
which generalizes classical filtrations by incorporating contextual information into the flow of time.
In the standard setting,
a filtration is a contravariant functor

F:{\mathcal{T}}^{op}\to\mathbf{Prob}
,
where
{\mathcal{T}}
is a linearly ordered index set such as
\mathbb{R}_{+}
or
\mathbb{N}
.
This structure presupposes that time unfolds along a single path,
so that the present is determined by a unique past.

In contrast,
our construction replaces the linear index category with a richer category
\mathbf{\Sigma}
,
in which each moment of time is equipped with a
context
.
Objects of
\mathbf{\Sigma}
are pairs
[t]_{c}
consisting of a discrete time

t\in\mathbb{N}

and a context

c\in\Sigma_{0}
,
while morphisms are inherited from the simplex category
\mathbf{\Delta}

subject to compatibility with the chosen context.
Intuitively,
\mathbf{\Sigma}
allows us to encode the idea
that the present state may be reached through multiple possible pasts,
each corresponding to a different contextual path.

A
\mathbf{\Sigma}
-filtration
is then defined as a contravariant functor

X:\mathbf{\Sigma}^{op}\to\mathbf{Prob},

which associates to each time-in-context
[t]_{c}
a probability space and
to each morphism the corresponding null-preserving map.
This categorical formulation provides a geometric model
in which uncertainty arises not only from stochastic variation but also from the multiplicity of contexts.

3.2.
Geometric realization

The construction of geometric realization is essential for our framework
because it provides a concrete topological space on which probability measures can be defined.
While simplicial objects capture combinatorial data about time and context,
their geometric realizations translate this information into shapes (simplices)
that admit natural probability measures,
such as the uniform distribution or, more generally, Dirichlet distributions.
In this way,
geometric realization acts as a bridge between the categorical structure of time (encoded in
\mathbf{\Delta}
or
\mathbf{\Sigma}
)
and the analytic structure of probability theory.
Without this step, filtrations would remain purely combinatorial,
whereas our goal is to endow them with probabilistic and geometric content suitable for modeling uncertainty.

4.
Dirichlet filtration

To illustrate the abstract framework of

\mathbf{\Sigma}
-filtrations with a concrete probabilistic model,
we introduce the notion of a
Dirichlet filtration
.
The guiding idea is simple:
simplices are natural carriers of probability measures,
and the Dirichlet distribution provides a flexible family of such measures.
Just as the Beta distribution plays a central role in one-dimensional Bayesian analysis,
the Dirichlet distribution generalizes this role to higher-dimensional simplices,
serving as the canonical conjugate prior for multinomial models.
By assigning Dirichlet distributions to the simplices
that arise in the geometric realization of

\mathbf{\Sigma}
,
we obtain a filtration that reflects both stochastic variability (through the distributional parameters)
and contextual variability
(through the choice of paths in
\mathbf{\Sigma}
).
This construction allows us to study uncertainty in a dual sense—uncertainty
about future outcomes and
uncertainty about which contextual past has given rise to the present.

4.1.
Dirichlet distribution

This subsection is a review of
Dirichlet distributions on
n
-simplex.
Please refer to

[
Ferguson, 1973
]

for the further details.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let

\lambda_{n}

be the Lebesgue measure on

\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

(1)

\lambda_{n+1}(\Delta_{n})=\frac{1}{n!}
,

(2)

n!\lambda_{n+1}

is the uniform measure on

\Delta_{n}
.

Proof.

For
n\geq 1
,
define functions

u_{n}:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}

inductively by:

u_{1}(t):=t,\quad u_{n+1}(t):=\int_{0}^{t}u_{n}(t-x)\,dx,\quad(t\in[0,1]).

Then, we can easily show that

u_{n}(t)=\frac{t^{n}}{n!},

and so

\lambda_{n+1}(\Delta_{n})=u_{n}(1)=\frac{1}{n!}.

∎

The following definition of the gamma distribution is the version generalized by Ferguson

[
Ferguson, 1973
]
.

Definition 4.2
.

[Gamma distribution]

(1)

The
gamma function

\Gamma(\alpha)

is the function defined by for
\alpha>0
,

(4.1)

\Gamma(\alpha):=\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{\alpha-1}e^{-x}dx.

(2)

We denote by

\Gamma(\alpha,\lambda)

the
gamma distribution
with shape parameter

\alpha\geq 0

and scale parameter

\lambda>0
.
For

\alpha=0
,
this distribution is degenerated at zero, that is,
it becomes the Dirac delta function

\delta(x)
;
for

\alpha>0
,
this distribution has density with respect to Lebesgue measure on the real line

(4.2)

f_{\Gamma(\alpha,\lambda)}(x):=\begin{cases}\frac{\lambda^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}x^{\alpha-1}e^{-\lambda x}&\textrm{if }x>0,\\
0&\textrm{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Adopting the Dirac delta function when

\alpha=0

in Definition
4.2
(2)
is reasonable because
it is the limit distribution of
that of
any random variable

X\sim\Gamma(\alpha,\lambda)

for

\alpha>0
,
whose expectation and variance are

\mathbb{E}[X]=\frac{\alpha}{\lambda}

and

\mathrm{Var}(X)=\frac{\alpha}{\lambda^{2}}
.

Definition 4.3
.

Let

n\geq 1

be an integer.

(1)

\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}:=\{(x_{0},\cdots,x_{n-1})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid x_{i}\geq 0\textrm{ for all $i\in[n-1]$}\},

(2)

\mathbb{R}_{\#}^{n}:=\{(x_{0},\cdots,x_{n-1})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}\mid x_{i}>0\textrm{ for some $i\in[n-1]$}\},

(3)

\mathbb{R}_{++}^{n}:=\{(x_{0},\cdots,x_{n-1})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid x_{i}>0\textrm{ for all $i\in[n-1]$}\}.

Definition 4.4
.

[Multivaliate beta function]

Let

n\geq 1

be an integer,
and

\bm{\alpha}=(\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{n})\in\mathbb{R}_{++}^{n+1}

be a vector.

(1)

For

\mathbf{x}=(x_{0},\cdots,x_{n})\in\Delta_{n}
,

(4.3)

\mathrm{b}_{n}(\mathbf{x},\bm{\alpha}):=\prod_{i=0}^{n}x_{i}^{\alpha_{i}-1}.

(2)

\mathrm{B}_{n}

is the function, called
the multivariate beta function
,
defined by

(4.4)

\mathrm{B}_{n}(\bm{\alpha}):=\frac{\prod_{i=0}^{n}\Gamma(\alpha_{i})}{\Gamma\Big(\sum_{i=0}^{n}\alpha_{i}\Big)}.

The following fact is well-known.

Proposition 4.5
.

For

\bm{\alpha}=(\alpha_{0},\cdots,\alpha_{n})\in\mathbb{R}_{++}^{n+1}
,

(4.5)

\mathrm{B}_{n}(\bm{\alpha})=\int_{\Delta_{n}}\mathrm{b}_{n}(\mathbf{x},\bm{\alpha})\,d\mathbf{x}.

Definition 4.6
.

[Dirichlet distribution]

Let

n\geq 1

be an integer
and
let

\bm{\alpha}=(\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{n})\in\mathbb{R}_{\#}^{n+1}
.
For

j\in[n]
,
let

g_{j}\sim\Gamma(\alpha_{j},1)

be independent random variables.
Then the random variable

\mathbf{f}=(f_{0},\cdots,f_{n})

on the standard
n
-simplex

\Delta_{n}
,
defined by

(4.6)

f_{i}:=\frac{g_{i}}{\sum_{j=0}^{n}g_{j}}\quad(i\in[n]),

is said to have
the Dirichlet distribution
with parameter

\bm{\alpha}
,
and is denoted by

\mathbf{f}\sim\mathcal{D}_{n}(\bm{\alpha}).

Note that, if any

\alpha_{i}=0
,
the corresponding

f_{i}

is degenerate at zero.
However,
if

\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}_{++}^{n+1}
,
it has a probability density function defined by

(4.7)

p_{f}(\mathbf{x})=p_{\mathcal{D}_{n}}(\mathbf{x};\bm{\alpha}):=\frac{\mathrm{b}_{n}(\mathbf{x},\bm{\alpha})}{\mathrm{B}_{n}(\bm{\alpha}).}

Note that

p_{\mathcal{D}n}(x_{0},\dots,x_{n};1,\cdots,1)=n!,

which is the uniform density over

\Delta_{n}
.
Therefore,
the uniform distribution on
\Delta_{n}
is a special case of Dirichlet distribution.

Proposition 4.7
.

Let

f=(f_{0},\cdots,f_{n})\sim\mathcal{D}_{n}(\alpha_{0},\cdots,\alpha_{n})

be a random variable,

0<\ell\leq n
,
and

\{r_{j}\}_{j=0}^{\ell}

be a sequence of integers such that

0\leq r_{0}<r_{1}<\cdots<\cdots r_{\ell-1}<r_{\ell}=n
.
Then, we have

(4.8)

\Big(\sum_{k=0}^{r_{0}}f_{k},\sum_{k=r_{0}+1}^{r_{1}}f_{k},\cdots\sum_{k=r_{\ell-1}+1}^{r_{\ell}}f_{k}\Big)\sim\mathcal{D}_{\ell}\Big(\sum_{k=0}^{r_{0}}\alpha_{k},\sum_{k=r_{0}+1}^{r_{1}}\alpha_{k},\cdots\sum_{k=r_{\ell-1}+1}^{r_{\ell}}\alpha_{k}\Big).

Proof.

This follows directly from the definition of the Dirichlet distribution
and the additive property of the gamma distribution:

f+g\sim\Gamma(a+b,\lambda)

for
two independent random variables

f\sim\Gamma(a,\lambda)

and

g\sim\Gamma(b,\lambda)
.
∎

Corollary 4.8
.

If

f\sim\mathcal{D}_{n}(\bm{\alpha})
,
then
the marginal distribution of

is a
\beta
-distribution:

f_{j}\sim\beta\Big(\alpha_{j},\sum_{i=0}^{n}\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{j}\Big).

Note 4.9
.

Let
n
be a fixed integer.
Consider a random vector

\mathbf{r}=(r_{0},\cdots,c_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n+1}

follows a multinomial distribution,
which has a probability mass function of the form:

(4.9)

p(\mathbf{r})=\binom{N}{r_{0}\cdots r_{n}}q_{0}^{r_{0}}q_{1}^{r_{1}}\cdots q_{n}^{r_{n}}=\binom{N}{r_{0}\cdots r_{n}}\mathrm{b}_{n}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{r}+1),

where

N:=\sum_{i=0}^{n}r_{i}

and

\mathbf{q}:=(q_{0},\cdots,q_{n})
.
Now let us assume that the prior distribution of

\mathbf{q}\in\Delta_{n}

is a Dirichlet distribution, that is, it has of the form:

(4.10)

p(\mathbf{q})=\frac{\mathrm{b}_{n}(\mathbf{q},\bm{\alpha})}{\mathrm{B}_{n}(\bm{\alpha})}.

Here

\bm{\alpha}

is a hyperparameter.

If we sample this random vector

\mathbf{r}
, than we have
a likelihood function
with a parameter

\mathbf{x}\in\Delta_{n}
:

(4.11)

\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{r}\mid\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{x})=\binom{N}{r_{0}\cdots r_{n}}\mathrm{b}_{n}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{r}+1).

Then, since the prior distribution of

\mathbf{q}

is of the form:

(4.12)

\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{x})=\frac{\mathrm{b}_{n}(\mathbf{x},\bm{\alpha})}{\mathrm{B}_{n}(\bm{\alpha})}\;\sim\;\mathcal{D}_{n}(\bm{\alpha}),

we have the following posterior distribution:

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{r}\mid\mathbf{x})\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{x})}{\int_{\Delta_{n}}\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{r}\mid\mathbf{y})\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{y})\,d\mathbf{y}}=\frac{\binom{N}{r_{0}\cdots r_{n}}\mathrm{b}_{n}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{r}+1)\mathrm{b}_{n}(\mathbf{x},\bm{\alpha})/\mathrm{B}_{n}(\bm{\alpha})}{\int_{\Delta_{n}}\binom{N}{r_{0}\cdots r_{n}}\mathrm{b}_{n}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{r}+1)\mathrm{b}_{n}(\mathbf{y},\bm{\alpha})/\mathrm{B}_{n}(\bm{\alpha})\,d\mathbf{y}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{b}_{n}(\mathbf{x},\bm{\alpha}+\mathbf{r})}{\int_{\Delta_{n}}\mathrm{b}_{n}(\mathbf{y},\bm{\alpha}+\mathbf{r})\,d\mathbf{y}}=\frac{\mathrm{b}_{n}(\mathbf{x},\bm{\alpha}+\mathbf{r})}{\mathrm{B}_{n}(\bm{\alpha}+\mathbf{r})}\;\sim\;\mathcal{D}_{n}(\bm{\alpha}+\mathbf{r}).

So,
the Dirichlet distribution is a
conjugate prior
of the multinomial distribution,
which is a well-known fact.

4.3.

In this subsection,

Let

D:\mathbf{\Sigma}\to\mathbf{Diri}

Suppose that

5.
Conclusion

In this work
we proposed a geometric framework for
synthetic filtrations
,
extending the classical notion of filtrations in probability theory.
By introducing the category
\mathbf{\Sigma}
,
which enriches the simplex category
\mathbf{\Delta}
with context-dependent time,
we captured the idea that the present may arise from the synthesis of multiple possible pasts.
This categorical extension provides a natural setting for analyzing uncertainty not only in parameters but also in contexts.

As a concrete realization,
we developed
Dirichlet filtrations
,
where probability measures on simplices are induced by Dirichlet distributions.
This construction highlighted two sources of uncertainty:
parameter uncertainty, which reflects variability within probabilistic models,
and contextual uncertainty, which arises from the multiplicity of possible pasts.
Furthermore,
we showed that Bayesian updating can be formulated as the categorical update of a Dirichlet functor,
offering a new interpretation of learning within our framework.

The theory of synthetic filtrations opens several directions for further research.
One avenue is the extension to continuous time and its relationship with classical stochastic processes
though there need some jumps to find appropriate continuous geometric structures.
Another is the exploration of homological and topological invariants of
\mathbf{\Sigma}
-filtrations,
potentially connecting with homotopy theory.
Finally, applications in finance and economics,
where information often arrives in context-sensitive and non-linear ways, represent a promising field of study.

Overall,
synthetic filtrations provide a unifying geometric and categorical language
for modeling time, uncertainty, and information,
enriching both the theory of probability and its applications.
