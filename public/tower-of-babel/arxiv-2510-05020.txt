Comparing fine-tuning strategies of MACE machine learning force field for modeling Li-ion diffusion in LiF for batteries
Nada Alghamdi, Paolo de Angelis, Pietro Asinari, Eliodoro Chiavazzo

Abstract
Machine learning interatomic potentials (MLIPs) are transforming materials science and engineering by enabling the study of complex phenomena, such as those critical to battery operation. In this work, we benchmark the MACE machine learning model against a well-trained DeePMD potential for predicting interstitial lithium diffusivity in LiF, a key component in the solid electrolyte interphase in Li ion batteries. Our results demonstrate that the MACE-MPA-0 foundational model achieves comparable accuracy to well-trained DeePMD, in predicting key diffusion properties based on molecular dynamics simulation, while requiring minimal or no training data. For instance, the MACE-MPA-0 predicts an activation energy E a E_{a} of 0.22 eV 0.22\text{\,}\mathrm{eV} , the fine-tuned model with only 300 data points predicts E a = E_{a}= 0.20 eV 0.20\text{\,}\mathrm{eV} , both of which show good agreement with the DeePMD model reference value of E a = E_{a}= 0.24 eV 0.24\text{\,}\mathrm{eV} . In this work, we provide a solid test case where fine-tuning approaches – whether using data generated for DeePMD or data produced by the foundational MACE model itself – yield similar robust performance to the DeePMD potential trained with over 40,000 actively learned data, albeit requiring only a fraction of the training data.

Introduction

High-performance energy-storage systems are key technologies for the energy transition.
In particular, Lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) are recognized to play a crucial role in renewable energy storage, grid stability, and low-carbon mobility
[
1
]
[
2
]
[
3
]
.
The performance, lifetime, and safety of LIB cells are governed by processes at the electrode–electrolyte interface, where the solid-electrolyte interphase (SEI) forms
[
4
]
[
5
]
.
The SEI is a reactive passivation layer where multiple chemical reactions occur.
It is highly heterogeneous, comprising many organic and inorganic components.
The SEI extends the electrolyte’s electrochemical window and isolates the anode, enabling proper operation of the LIB
[
4
]
.
However, uncontrolled formation or growth can degrade cell performance, leading to capacity fade and, in extreme cases, thermal runaway
[
6
]
.
Recent experimental evidence showed that LiF-rich SEIs enhance mechanical stability, provide electronic insulation, and reduce interfacial resistance, which translates into improved performance and safety for fluoride-rich battery formulations
[
6
]
.
Therefore, it is important to understand and engineer the SEI for improved battery lifetime, safety, and performance
[
7
]
[
8
]
.

The advent of Machine Learning Interatomic Potentials (MLIPs) has significantly advanced the field of materials modeling
[
9
]
[
10
]
, enabling simulations over longer timescales and larger systems that were previously unaffordable.
Among many applications, it plays a crucial role in reactive atomistic simulations that are essential for studying physical phenomena of relevance for energy conversion processes
[
11
]
.
MLIPs offer a promising solution for addressing complex simulations, where understanding the interplay between components (e.g. the electrode and electrolyte, the SEI) is crucial to ensure battery stability and safety, but it remains inaccessible with traditional experimental methods
[
2
]
[
7
]
, and computationally expensive to perform with traditional
ab-initio
methods
[
12
]
.

The Deep Potential Molecular Dynamics (DeePMD) model
[
13
]
has emerged as an MLIP framework that provides a viable way to go beyond fixed pairwise potentials for dealing with many-body interactions.
In this approach, the atomic environment for each atom
i
is represented as a transformation of the coordinates from the global configuration to a local reference frame centered around the atom
i
.
A cutoff radius
R_{c}
is introduced, within which
N_{c}
neighboring atoms are considered.
Accordingly, a mapping is defined from atomic positions to local vector descriptors
\boldsymbol{D}_{i}
.
Unlike earlier approaches that relied on fixed descriptors, later developments introduced learnable descriptors that are optimized during training.
Those descriptors incorporate not only interatomic distances but also bond angles and chemical species,
enabling improved generalization and higher accuracy
[
14
]
.
Descriptors are made such that translation and rotation transformations are invariant.
In DeePMD, every atomic species has its own sub neural network (NN), permutation symmetry is preserved as the atoms are sorted based on their distances from the central atom and all sub-networks corresponding to a given atomic type share the same parameters.
The total potential energy
E_{tot}
of a given atomic configuration is the sum of the energy contribution
E_{i}
of each atom
i
in the system.
For a system of several atomic species, each atomic species
s
, has its own NN
s
, and every atom
i_{s}
within that species uses the same NN
s
of the species, so the total energy:

E_{tot}=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{atom}}E_{i}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad E_{tot}=\sum_{s=1}^{N_{species}}\sum_{i_{s}}^{N_{atom\in s}}E_{NN_{s}}(\boldsymbol{D}_{i_{s}}),

(1)

where
N_{atom}
is the total number of atoms in the system,
N_{species}
is the total number of atomic species,
N_{atom\in s}
is the number of atoms belonging to species
s
, and
E_{NN_{s}}(\boldsymbol{D}_{i_{s}})
is the energy predicted by the NN
s
associated with species
s
for the atom with descriptor
\boldsymbol{D}_{i_{s}}

[
15
]
[
16
]
.

More recently, Message-Passing-based graph Neural Networks (MPNN) have been developed
[
17
]
.
In MPNN, a material is represented as a graph in which each node corresponds to an atom, and each edge represents a connection between atoms within a specific cutoff radius.
In particular, MACE
[
18
]
, which combines Atomic Cluster Expansion (ACE)
[
19
]
with an equivariant Message-Passing Neural Network (MPNN), has achieved notable success in describing different chemistries
[
20
]
[
21
]
[
22
]
.
In this approach, the features are built using learnable radial functions, spherical harmonics, and learnable embedding of neighboring node features.
Equivariance is preserved through symmetrization with Clebsch–Gordan coefficients.
Subsequently, the higher-order features are generated by a tensor product of these equivariance two-body features.
Symmetrizing by generalized Clebsch-Gordan coefficients maintains correct equivariance and allows for very efficient computation of higher order features as these coefficients are highly sparse and can be pre-computed.
This constitutes the body-ordered basis for message construction, where incorporating higher body-order features in the messages allows the model to capture higher correlation order with fewer message-passing steps.
In addition, the graph representation and the tensor product decomposition lends itself to an efficient implementation on GPU architectures, making the approach highly scalable.
Further implementation details are reported in Ref.
[
18
]
.
The energy of each atom is computed through a hierarchical mapping from the invariant components of the node features to a body-order energy contribution:

E_{i}=E_{i}^{(0)}+E_{i}^{(1)}+\ldots+E_{i}^{(T)},

(2)

where the intermediate contributions
E_{i}^{(t)}
for
t<T
are obtained through linear readout functions of the features, maintaining the interpretation of a successive contribution in the body-order energy expansion.
The last layer
E_{i}^{(T)}
is computed by a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) which can be seen to approximate any remaining higher-order or non-linear contributions.
The total energy of the system is then given by the sum over all atomic contributions

E_{tot}=\sum_{i}E_{i}
.

By pre-training MACE on large chemical spaces, emerging foundational models encapsulate broad knowledge of atomic interactions.
They can later be customized for specific tasks or used directly for fast though less accurate simulations.
In spirit, this is similar to large language models (LLMs), trained on the entire Internet, that serves as general purpose models.
MACE model, MACE-MP-0, trained on the Materials Project trajectory dataset (MPtrj)
[
23
]
, containing over 1.5 million data point, exhibited excellent extrapolation capability to out of distribution domains
[
20
]
.
Several foundational MACE models trained on different datasets have followed,
MACE-MPA-0, which includes 3.5 million new configurations, combines MPtrj with a subset of the Alexandria dataset
[
24
]
.
MACE-OMAT-0, trained using Open Materials 2024 (OMat24) dataset
[
25
]
, containing over 110 million Density Functional Theory (DFT) single-point calculations of inorganic materials, including a wide range of non-equilibrium structures, other foundational models based on different training datasets are continually emerging.

Similar to recent approaches of transfer learning in LLMs and computer vision models
[
26
]
, fine-tuning foundational models has emerged as an efficient strategy for achieving high accuracy while substantially reducing training data requirements.
By leveraging the broad knowledge encoded in a foundation model (pre-trained on extensive datasets spanning diverse materials), fine-tuning enables rapid specialization to specific sub-domains with minimal additional data
[
27
]
.

This study focuses on a benchmark of the foundational MACE-MPA-0 model and its fine-tuned variants for predicting lithium diffusivity in LiF.
We evaluate the activation energy for Li diffusion predicted by by running molecular dynamics simulations using MACE and compare it to the results obtained by a well-trained DeePMD model
[
28
]
, which was trained on a data set consisting of over 40,000 actively learned configurations.
Finally, we compare the diffusivity at 400 and 450 K across several MACE fine-tuned models to get important insights into the optimization of fine-tuning strategies.

2
Method

Molecular Dynamics (MD) simulations were performed using the Large-scale Atomic/Molecular Massively Parallel Simulator (LAMMPS)
[
29
]
, the Atomic Simulation Environment (ASE)
[
30
]
, and MACE machine learning force fields
[
18
]
[
31
]
.
Simulations were carried out in the NVT ensemble with a time step of 1 fs.
A 5×5×5 supercell of the LiF conventional bulk (containing 1000 atoms and measuring 20.42 Å in length) was employed for the diffusion calculations.
Temperature was controlled by the Nosé–Hoover thermostat
[
32
]
[
33
]
with a time constant of
0.1\text{\,}\mathrm{ps}
.
The total simulation time for the activation energy calculations was
9\text{\,}\mathrm{ns}
using four replicas for each temperature.
Furthermore, to compare models’ performance, diffusivity was calculated at 400 K and 450 K for 3 ns, with two independent replicas.
As a preliminary assessment, we evaluated the bulk equilibrium properties.
We find that the bulk equilibrium properties are accurately predicted by MACE (details are provided in Table. S1 in the Supplementary information), consistent with its training on periodic bulk structures from the Materials Project, which covers many Li and F-containing systems
[
20
]
[
23
]
.

2.1
Diffusivity

The diffusivity was found according to the Einstein-Smoluchowski relation:

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\left\langle\left\|\mathbf{r}(t)-\mathbf{r}(0)\right\|^{2}\right\rangle=c_{d}Dt

(3)

where
t
is time,
D
is the diffusion coefficient, and
c_{d}
is a constant that accounts for the system dimensionality; being
c_{d}=6
for three-dimensional diffusion.
For a sufficiently long simulation time, diffusion coefficients are extracted by calculating the slope of the Mean Square Displacement (MSD) versus time for each simulation.
The activation energy for diffusion,
E_{a}
, was then obtained by fitting the temperature dependence of
D
to the Arrhenius like equation:

D=D_{0}\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{E_{a}}{k_{B}T}\right),

(4)

where
D_{0}
is the maximum diffusivity,
k_{B}
is the Boltzmann constant, and
T
is the temperature
[
34
]
.
Note that finding the pre-exponential factor is not trivial by heuristic arguments
[
35
]
. Here we find it through an empirical measurement of
in silico
MD experiments, this has an advantage over other methods as it inherently accounts for entropic effects.

2.2
Fine-tuning with DeePMD data

The MACE-MPA-0 model
[
36
]
served as the starting point for our experiments.
First, we fine-tune it using data generated during the active learning process of DeePMD
[
28
]
, which consists of over 40,000 structures, from which we randomly select a small subset for fine-tuning.
We use datasets of increasing size: 110, 210, 410, 710, and 800 samples.
In the dataset with 800 samples, 100 structures correspond to bulk LiF, while the remaining 700 contain LiF with interstitial Li configurations.
For all other datasets, 10 structures are bulk LiF, and the rest consist of LiF with interstitial Li.
Notably, in the 800-sample case, the 700 interstitial structures are identical to those used in the 710-sample set; hence, the 800-sample dataset includes 90 additional bulk structures compared to the 710-sample dataset.
Additionally, we incorporate varying numbers of Materials Project data—0 (naive fine-tuning), 100, 1000, and 10,000 (multi-head fine-tuning)—into the training.

The weighted sum of Huber loss
[
37

functions for energies, forces, and stresses were used as a training loss. The general form of the Huber loss for a difference
x_{n}-y_{n}
is given by:

\mathcal{L}_{\delta}(x_{n}-y_{n})=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}(x_{n}-y_{n})^{2},&\text{ for }|x_{n}-y_{n}|\leq\delta,\\
\delta\cdot\left(|x_{n}-y_{n}|-\frac{1}{2}\delta\right),&\text{ otherwise }.\end{cases}

(5)

The respective differences
x_{n}-y_{n}
are the error between model predictions and reference values for energies, forces and stresses.
Following the universal loss in MACE
[
20

, Huber deltas
\delta_{E},\ \delta_{F_{0}},\ \delta_{\sigma}
are set to 0.01,
and conditional Huber loss was used for the forces such that the Huber delta
\delta_{F}
adapts based on the magnitude of the force on each atom. The Huber delta decreases as the force component magnitude increases according to:

\delta_{F_{\alpha}}=\begin{cases}\delta_{F_{0}},&F_{\alpha}<$100\text{\,}\mathrm{(}\mathrm{eV}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{\text{Å}}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{atom}\mathrm{)}$\\[6.0pt]
0.7\,\delta_{F_{0}},&$100\text{\,}$\leq F_{\alpha}<$200\text{\,}\mathrm{(}\mathrm{eV}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{\text{Å}}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{atom}\mathrm{)}$\\[6.0pt]
0.4\,\delta_{F_{0}},&$200\text{\,}$\leq F_{\alpha}<$300\text{\,}\mathrm{(}\mathrm{eV}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{\text{Å}}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{atom}\mathrm{)}$\\[6.0pt]
0.1\,\delta_{F_{0}},&F_{\alpha}\geq$300\text{\,}\mathrm{(}\mathrm{eV}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{\text{Å}}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{atom}\mathrm{)}$\end{cases}

(6)

where
F_{\alpha}
represents the force component in each Cartesian direction (
\alpha=x,y,z
)
[
18
]
[
20
]
[
31
]
.
The weights for energy, forces, and stresses losses were set to
(\lambda_{E},\lambda_{F},\lambda_{\sigma})=(1,10,1)
, assigning higher importance to forces as they were harder to learn.

2.3
Active learning

An active learning protocol similar to
[
28
]
[
38
]
was followed.
A committee of four models were trained using the same data, with different random initialization, to quantify model uncertainty and inform decisions regarding additional training data.
Simulations were performed with an NVT ensemble, consistent with previous MD simulations, using 3×3×3 supercell (comprising 216 atoms, 12.25 Å).
Each simulation was carried out for 24 hours, corresponding to approximately 600 ps trajectories.
The mean force error was evaluated
[
14
]
:

\sigma=\max_{i}\left[\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left\|\mathbf{F}_{i}^{k}-\overline{\mathbf{F}}_{i}\right\|^{2}}\right],

(7)

where the size of the committee
N=4
,
\mathbf{F}_{i}^{k}
is the force on
i
-th atom predicted by the
k
-th model, and
\overline{\mathbf{F}}_{i}
is the committee average
[
14
]
[
28
]
.
The structures were sampled according to the uncertainty: 30% of the data are taken from within the 1
\sigma
range, 60% within the 1.5
\sigma
range, and the remaining within the 2
\sigma
range
[
38
]
.

Energy, forces and stresses for new sampled configurations were evaluated with DFT using the Quantum ESPRESSO code
[
39
]
[
40
]
[
41
]
.
Consistent with the methodology of Ref.
[
28
]
, we used the PBE exchange-correlation functional and
Projector Augmented Wave (PAW) pseudopotentials
[
42
]
.
Brillouin-zone sampling was limited to the
\Gamma
-point.
Self-consistent convergence threshold was set to
5\ \times\ 10^{-7}
Ry.
Plane-wave basis with a kinetic energy cutoff of 110 Ry and charge-density cutoff of 440 Ry, were used.
Electronic occupations were treated with Gaussian smearing of 0.005 Ry.
More details about the active learning protocol are provided in the Supplementary Information.

A total of 76 structures are randomly selected: 16 bulk configurations, 4 structures at each temperature: 300, 400, 500, and 600
K
, and 60 interstitial configurations, 10 structures at each temperature: 300, 350, 400, 450, 500, and 600

For the FT1 model, we perform two active learning steps.
In each step, 40 additional structures are selected based on committee disagreement in the interstitial Li in the LiF configuration.
In total, FT1 is trained on 156 structures and validated on equal amount of structures.
For the model FT2, we perform only one active learning step. In this case, the training data includes both interstitial Li (10 structures per temperature) and bulk (7 structures per temperature) configurations from the committee run.
This results in a total of 144 structures used for training and similar amount for validation.

Results and Discussion

3.1
Arrhenius behavior of Li diffusion

As the transport of Li ions is key to battery performance, accurately describing this process provides an essential test for MLIP applied to battery materials.
In the Li-ion diffusion process, a Li ion breaks bonds with its surrounding atoms and forms new ones.
Unlike classical force fields, which use fixed potential forms that does not capture bond breaking and formation, MLIP can simulate such reactive systems.
We therefore first benchmark the MACE-MPA-0 and fine-tuned MACE-MPA-0 models by using the Arrhenius equation (eq.
4
) to derive their predicted activation energies
E_{a}
and maximum diffusivity
D_{0}
, which we compare against the reference values simulated by the DeePMD model
[
28
]
.

The Arrhenius plot for the MACE-MPA-0 model and a fine-tuned model along with the reference DeePMD values
[
28
]
are shown in Fig.
1
.
The fine-tuned model was trained on 200 data points randomly sampled from actively learned DeePMD structure
[
28
]
and 100 pre-training MPtraj
[
23
]
data points, we denote it as ft200-pt100 (ft: fine-tuning, pt: pre-training).

3.2
Fine-tuning with DeePMD data

Following the excellent performance of the MACE models observed in the previous section in predicting Arrhenius properties, we now investigate the effect of different fine-tuning strategies.
This comparison is designed to provide some insights into the training protocol.
The diffusivity of interstitial Li in LiF at 400 K and 450 K was found for several fine-tuned models using 3 ns trajectories.
In Fig.

All models exhibit good performance, with accuracy improving as the amount of fine-tuning data increases, gradually approaching the reference values.

3.3
Fine-tuning with MACE-MPA-0 data

While the previous sections used a dataset carefully constructed for this specific problem via an active learning process with DeePMD potential
[
28
]
, such resources are not always available.
As an alternative approach, we investigate generating training data by sampling configurations from trajectories explored by the foundational model itself.

Summary and conclusions

In this study, we investigated interstitial Li diffusivity in LiF, a key SEI component that plays an important role in battery performance and safety.
Our results demonstrate that both the foundational MACE-MPA-0 model and its fine-tuned variants provide an accurate description of the system.
Training data generated either from configurations explored by a reference accurate model (DeePMD) or by the foundational MACE model itself yield highly efficient and reliable predictions.
The use of the reference model data, however, offers a significant gain in training speed and ease of use.

In particular, the MACE-MPA-0 foundational model predicts an activation energy
E_{A}
of
0.22\pm 0.015\ eV
and a maximum diffusivity
D_{0}
of
2.1\times 10^{-6}\pm 8.4\times 10^{-7}\ cm^{2}/s
.
The fine-tuned model with only 300 data points (denoted: ft200-pt100, 200 data points from actively learned DeePMD generated data and 100 data points from pre-training MPtraj data) predicts
E_{A}=0.20\pm 0.004\ eV
and
D_{0}=2.2\times 10^{-6}\pm 2.5\times 10^{-7}\ cm^{2}/s
.
Both of which show strong agreement with the DeePMD model reference values of
E_{A}=0.24\ eV
and
D_{0}=6.6\times 10^{-6}\ cm^{2}/s
.

The diffusivity at different temperature also showed good agreement with the reference, for example, the diffusivity at
450\text{\,}\mathrm{K}
, for the MACE-MPA-0 model is
D=6.89\times 10^{-9}\pm 1\times 10^{-9}\ cm^{2}/s
, and for the ft200-pt100 fine-tuned model is
D=1.27\times 10^{-8}\pm 1.21\times 10^{-9}\ cm^{2}/s
, while the value found by the DeePMD reference is
D=1.35\times 10^{-8}\ cm^{2}/s
.
Notably, the predicted values agree well with the DeePMD model—trained on over 40,000 data points—despite MACE foundational model achieving comparable accuracy with little to no training.

The availability of highly efficient MLIPs now enables, for the first time, an in-depth analysis of the SEI formation mechanism, a process comprised of numerous components and a highly complex composition and topology, for which an efficient utilization of training data is highly needed.
Furthermore, designing high-quality battery-specialized training datasets as well as developing efficient methods to determine the optimal structure of the training dataset can play a crucial role in rapidly adopting these computational tools to address critical, long-standing challenges in batteries.
