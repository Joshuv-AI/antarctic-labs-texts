Transposition of great arteries with left ventricular outflow tract obstruction undergoing definitive repair beyond the third decade
Authors: Mayank Yadav, Vuribindi Manognitha Reddy, Nita Bharti, Srinivas Thimmasarthi, Parag Gharde
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA 30-year-old male presented with progressive dyspnea, cyanosis, and chest pain due to unrepaired d-transposition of the great arteries with ventricular septal defect (VSD) and severe left ventricular outflow tract obstruction, underwent successful late biventricular repair including VSD closure, neo-aortic mechanical valve implantation, coronary transfer, and LeCompte maneuver. Despite postoperative stroke and complete heart block requiring pacemaker implantation, he was discharged in stable condition with preserved biventricular function. This case supports the feasibility of anatomic repair beyond the third decade.

INTRODUCTION

Complete transposition of the great arteries (TGA) is a congenital cardiac malformation characterized by ventriculoarterial discordance, resulting in parallel systemic and pulmonary circulations and severe cyanosis early in life unless adequate mixing is present. Survival beyond infancy without definitive repair is rare and is usually facilitated by associated lesions such as ventricular septal defect (VSD) and left ventricular outflow tract obstruction (LVOTO), which permit partial physiological compensation.[1]

Presentation of unrepaired TGA in adulthood represents an uncommon and challenging clinical scenario. Long-standing abnormal hemodynamics lead to ventricular remodeling, altered myocardial performance, complex outflow tract anatomy, and variable coronary patterns, all of which complicate assessment of operability and surgical decision-making.[2] Unlike neonatal TGA, adult patients require individualized evaluation using multimodality imaging to assess ventricular suitability, the level and severity of LVOTO, pulmonary vascular resistance, and coronary anatomy before considering late anatomic repair.[3]

We report the case of a 30-year-old patient with d-transposition of the great arteries (D-TGA) associated with a large VSD and valvular-level LVOTO who successfully underwent late biventricular anatomic repair. This case highlights the diagnostic considerations, operative strategy, and technical challenges inherent in managing late-presenting TGA in adulthood.

CASE REPORT

A 30-year-old male (weight: 48 kg, height: 167 cm, and body surface area [BSA]: 1.5 kg/m2) presented with progressive dyspnea over 3 years, worsening on exertion and associated with cyanosis of the lips, hands, and feet, which was partially relieved by rest. He also reported intermittent, nonradiating, dull chest pain. There was no history of syncope or palpitations. Resting oxygen saturation on room air was 75%–80%.

Chest radiography demonstrated situs solitus, levocardia, and absence of cardiomegaly. Electrocardiography (ECG) showed a normal sinus rhythm with left ventricular hypertrophy. Transthoracic echocardiography confirmed D-TGA with atrioventricular (AV) concordance and ventriculoarterial discordance. Additional findings included normal systemic and pulmonary venous drainage, a large inlet VSD with subpulmonic extension, a small additional muscular VSD, right ventricular hypertrophy, posterior and slightly rightward pulmonary artery position relative to the aorta, severe valvular and infundibular pulmonary stenosis with a doming pulmonary valve, right aortic arch, absence of patent ductus arteriosus, and preserved left ventricular systolic function (ejection fraction: 65%). Cardiac computed tomography further delineated the complex spatial relationships of the great arteries and ventricular septal anatomy [Figure 1].

Given the late presentation and to assess operability, cardiac catheterization was performed. Hemodynamic and angiographic evaluation demonstrated double-outlet right ventricle physiology with an adequate inlet VSD extending subpulmonically, aorta remote from the VSD, severe subvalvular pulmonary stenosis, and confluent, adequately sized pulmonary arteries (right pulmonary artery: 22 mm and left pulmonary artery: 18 mm). The aorta was positioned anterior and to the left of the pulmonary artery. Coronary arteries originated from facing sinuses. Mean pulmonary artery pressure was 10 mmHg, indicating the absence of pulmonary vascular disease. One large aortopulmonary collateral supplying the right lung and multiple small collaterals were identified [Table 1].

Perioperative management and surgical technique

After standard preoperative preparation, transcatheter embolization of the large aortopulmonary collateral was performed 1 day before surgery. Intraoperative transesophageal echocardiography confirmed the VSD location, left ventricular routability, and suitability for biventricular repair [Figure 2].

Intraoperativetee images suggesting complex anatomy – malaligned inlet ventricular septal defect with subpulmonic extension and stenotic pulmonary valve

Median sternotomy and right paramedian pericardiotomy were performed. Cardiopulmonary bypass was established using bi-caval cannulation under moderate hypothermia. Dissection of the great vessels revealed ductal tissue, which was ligated and divided. The right and left pulmonary arteries were looped. After application of the aortic cross-clamp, antegrade cold blood cardioplegia was administered.

The ascending aorta was transected proximal to the cannulation site and distal to the coronary ostia. The pulmonary artery was transected below its bifurcation, and both roots were mobilized. Intraoperative inspection confirmed a bicuspid, severely stenotic pulmonary valve and a large inlet VSD with subpulmonic extension. The VSD was closed using a curved Gelweave Dacron patch, with the convexity oriented toward the right ventricle. Coronary buttons were harvested. The pulmonary valve was excised, and the annulus was sized at 17 mm. The effective orifice area of 17 mm CarboMedics bi-leaflet valve was suitable for a 1.5 BSA patient without causing patient prosthetic mismatch; hence, the possibility of enlarging the annulus was not considered. The coronary buttons were reimplanted into the neoaorta above the prosthetic valve. A LeCompte maneuver was performed, followed by reconstruction of the neoaorta with an end-to-end anastomosis to the ascending aorta. The neopulmonary artery was reconstructed using a Gelweave Dacron graft. The patient was weaned gradually from cardiopulmonary bypass without difficulty, and standard closure was performed [Figure 3].

Postoperative course

The patient was hemodynamically stable postoperatively and was extubated on postoperative day (POD) 2. On POD 7, he developed left-sided weakness and altered sensorium, necessitating elective reintubation. ECG was showing AV dissociation, and INR was 1.31. Neuroimaging confirmed a right middle cerebral artery infarct, which was managed conservatively. A tracheostomy was performed on POD 12 to facilitate airway management and neurological rehabilitation. With intensive neurophysiotherapy, neurological function gradually improved, and decannulation was achieved on POD 25.

Persistent AV dissociation required implantation of an endocardial permanent pacemaker on POD 29. After prolonged multidisciplinary rehabilitation, the patient was ambulatory, cognitively coherent, and hemodynamically stable at discharge on POD 41. Postoperative transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated no residual VSD, a mild neoaortic valve gradient, and preserved biventricular systolic function.

DISCUSSION

Late presentation of D-TGA with VSD and LVOTO beyond the third decade of life remains exceptional. Untreated TGA with an intact ventricular septum is associated with extremely poor survival, with a reported 1-year survival of approximately 4%, whereas the presence of VSD and LVOTO permits prolonged survival by allowing balanced pulmonary and systemic circulations and maintaining left ventricular pressure loading.[1] Adults presenting with this physiology therefore represent a highly selected subgroup with favorable anatomic and hemodynamic characteristics.

The present case demonstrates that definitive biventricular anatomic repair can be successfully achieved in selected adult patients when careful assessment confirms operability. Preoperative multimodality imaging and cardiac catheterization were central to decision-making, allowing detailed evaluation of ventricular morphology and function, LVOTO level and severity, pulmonary vascular resistance, coronary anatomy, and VSD routability. The absence of pulmonary vascular disease and preserved left ventricular systolic function supported an anatomic rather than palliative approach.

Comparison with published adult experience

Published experience with late-presenting TGA in adults is limited to isolated case reports and small series. Nevertheless, systematic comparison suggests that outcomes are superior in patients undergoing definitive anatomic repair when biventricular physiology can be restored. Bahrami et al.[4] reported successful anatomic repair in a 55-year-old patient, while Bansal et al.[5] and Mishra et al.[6] described favorable early outcomes following definitive repair in young adults from India. In contrast, patients managed conservatively or without repair demonstrate poor long-term outcomes, including arrhythmias, heart failure, and sudden cardiac death, as illustrated by the 68-year-old untreated patient reported by Fedrigo et al.[7]

Importantly, chronological age alone does not appear to predict outcome. Instead, preserved ventricular function, favorable pulmonary vascular status, and feasible coronary transfer are the key determinants of success. The present case aligns with these observations, achieving early postoperative results comparable to those reported in younger adult cohorts despite presentation beyond the third decade of life [Table 2].

Rationale for anatomic repair over palliative strategies

The choice of definitive anatomic repair over palliative options in this patient was based on objective physiologic and anatomic considerations. The large inlet VSD with subpulmonic extension and severe LVOTO resulted in long-standing left ventricular pressure loading, preserving left ventricular mass and function, an essential prerequisite for systemic ventricular performance after anatomic correction. Cardiac catheterization confirmed low pulmonary artery pressures, excluding advanced pulmonary vascular disease, which would otherwise contraindicate biventricular repair.

Palliative approaches, including systemic–pulmonary shunts or atrial switch procedures, were considered inferior alternatives. These strategies do not correct ventriculoarterial discordance and are associated with late systemic right ventricular failure, progressive cyanosis, arrhythmias, and reduced survival.[89] Single-ventricle palliation was similarly unattractive given the presence of two well-developed ventricles and the known long-term morbidity of Fontan physiology in adults.

Technical considerations and outcomes

Several technical modifications were employed to mitigate risks associated with late anatomic repair. Closure of the VSD using a curved Gelweave Dacron patch with its convexity oriented toward the right ventricle minimized the risk of postoperative LVOT obstruction, a recognized cause of failure in adult repairs. Excision of the severely stenotic bicuspid pulmonary valve and implantation of a mechanical prosthesis[1011] in the neoaortic position ensured durable systemic outflow competence, avoiding early valve degeneration. Coronary transfer, the LeCompte maneuver, and reconstruction of the neopulmonary artery with a Dacron graft restored physiologic ventricular–arterial coupling using principles derived from complex Rastelli, Nikaidoh, and REV procedure.[3]

Although the postoperative course was complicated by right middle cerebral artery infarction and complete heart block requiring permanent pacemaker implantation, these events reflect recognized risks in complex adult congenital cardiac surgery. Despite these complications, the patient achieved favorable early outcomes, including preserved biventricular function, absence of residual VSD, and only a mild neoaortic valve gradient at discharge, supporting the viability of definitive repair in appropriately selected adults.

CONCLUSION

This case illustrates the feasibility of definitive biventricular repair in a 30-year-old with D-TGA, VSD, and LVOTO, achieving physiological correction despite late presentation and complications such as stroke and heart block. Such anatomic repairs, incorporating VSD patch optimization, mechanical neoaortic valve, and LeCompte maneuver, yield stable outcomes superior to palliation in suitable adults.

Long-term success hinges on preoperative multimodality imaging, collateral embolization, and postoperative rehabilitation, supporting biventricular pathways even beyond the third decade.
