Rapid and efficient generation of multigene-knockout THP-1 cells using a simple CRISPR-Cas9 protocol
Authors: Chihiro Ito, Yuki Kinomura, Kensho Kawagoe, Shinobu Fujii, Toshihiro Fujii, Rikiro Fukunaga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
THP-1, a widely used human monocytic leukemia cell line, has been described as one of the hard-to-transfect suspension cell lines. Here, we present a rapid and efficient method for generating multiplex gene knockout cells from THP-1 using a simple co-transfection strategy based on the CRISPR-Cas9 system. In this method, THP-1 cells were transfected with a mixture of pX330-based sgRNA plasmids and a small amount of puromycin-resistance plasmid by electroporation, followed by transient selection with high-dose puromycin. This simple method resulted in efficient simultaneous knockout of multiple genes. For genes important for survival and/or cell proliferation, gene-knockout clones may be isolated by the limiting dilution method immediately after puromycin selection.•A simple co-transfection strategy to knock out multiple genes simultaneously in the human monocytic leukemia cell line, THP-1.•Transient selection with high-dose puromycin appeared to be effective for efficient knockout.•In general, knockout efficiency is satisfactory, and polyclonal knockout cells can be used without a cloning step unless the target gene plays a critical role in cell survival or proliferation.

Background

The CRISPR-Cas technology is a versatile tool for investigating the molecular functions of various genes in both living organisms and cultured cell lines [1]. The CRISPR-Cas-mediated genome editing utilizes double-strand break (DSB)-triggered repair pathways, such as homologous recombination (HR) and non-homologous end joining (NHEJ), with the latter prone to introducing insertions or deletions, leading to functional knockout of target genes. Generating multigene-knockout cells from cultured cell lines is useful for dissecting isotype-specific or redundant functions of individual members in a multigene family or of individual components of multiprotein complexes, and various strategies for multiplex gene editing have been developed [2]. We previously reported a rapid and efficient method for generating multigene-knockout HeLa cells using a simple CRISPR-Cas9 system, in which at least seven genes can be simultaneously knocked out by co-transfection of multiple sgRNA-expressing plasmids [3,4]. In principle, this method can be applied to other cell lines; however, transfection efficiency may be limiting for certain cell types, particularly hematopoietic cell lines. For example, the widely used human monocytic leukemia cell line THP-1 is a hard-to-transfect suspension cell line, and several reports describe protocols for efficient THP-1 transfection by electroporation [5,6], cationic lipids [7], and lentiviral vectors [8]. Recently, two groups reported protocols to efficiently generate single-gene knockout THP-1 cells using lentiviral vectors [9,10]. Lentiviral methods are useful for many applications, including multiplex knockout and library screening, but establishing a non-viral multiplex knockout system that does not integrate into the genome remains challenging.

Here, we provide step-by-step protocols for generating multigene-knockout THP-1 cells by electroporation using multiple targeting plasmids, each expressing a single-guide RNA (sgRNA) and engineered Cas9. To ensure that the transfected cells express all the sgRNA genes, cells are co-transfected with the plasmid mixture and a small amount of the pUREF-EX plasmid by electroporation. Then, cells are selected using an extremely high concentration of puromycin to enrich for cells that incorporate high-copy-number plasmids. Although we previously described a concise procedure for generating multigene-knockout polyclonal cell lines from HeLa cells [3], a detailed protocol and technical tips tailored to THP-1 cells are beneficial when applying this method to other cell types. The protocol is validated with a case of multiple knockouts of seven genes in the Jun/Fos family.

Method details

Materials and reagents

THP-1 cells (ATCC: TIB-202) were maintained in Growth Medium [GM = RPMI-1640 medium (Nacalai Tesque) supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum (Biosera), 50 µM 2-mercaptoethanol, 100 U/mL penicillin G (FujiFilm Wako), and 100 µg/mL streptomycin (FujiFilm Wako)] under a 5% CO2 atmosphere. Puromycin was purchased from InvivoGen. Electroporation was performed using the Neon™ Transfection System Unit MPK5000 (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher) and the Neon™ Transfection System Kit (100 µL; MPK10096) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The rabbit monoclonal antibodies used for Western blot were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology: c-Jun (#9165), JunB (#3753), JunD (#5000), c-Fos (#2250), FosB (#2251), Fra1 (#5281), and Fra2 (#19,967). Horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated swine anti-rabbit immunoglobulin antibody (Agilent Dako, P039901–2) and HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit immunoglobulin antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, #7074) were used as secondary antibodies. HRP-conjugated rabbit anti-β-actin antibody was purchased from MBL Life Science.

Plasmid construction and preparation of the plasmid mixtures

Targeting plasmids for the Cas9- and sgRNA-mediated gene knockout were constructed by inserting each 20-nucleotide target sequence into the BbsI site of the pX330-U6-Chimeric_BB-CBh-hSpCas9 (hereafter, pX330) plasmid [11](Addgene ID: 42,230). The target sequences listed in Table 1 were selected from the target entries of the GeCKOv2 human library [12,13] based on rankings from the CHOPCHOP web tool [14](https://chopchop.cbu.uib.no). For the construction of a targeting plasmid, sense (SN) and antisense (AS) 24- or 25-mer oligonucleotides (Table 2) were annealed to form double-stranded DNA and ligated to the BbsI-digested pX330 plasmid. Correct construction of the plasmids was confirmed by sequencing the insertion site. Western blot analysis

One million THP-1 or THP-1-derived KO cells were incubated in 1 mL of GM containing 0.5% calf serum instead of fetal calf serum at 37 °C for 18 h for serum starvation. Then, cells were left unstimulated (-) or stimulated with 0.5–1.0 µM phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA; Sigma-Aldrich, P8139) or 100 ng/mL lipopolysaccharide (LPS; Sigma-Aldrich, L4391) at 37 °C for 120 min. Cells were washed with 1 mL of ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 1 x Protease Inhibitor Cocktail for Use with Mammalian Cell and Tissue Extracts (Nacalai Tesque, #25,955), suspended in 20 µL of ice -cold PBS containing the same protease inhibitor cocktail. Cells were lysed with 80 µL of 1.25 x Laemmli's SDS sample buffer (1x buffer = 62.5 mM Tris–HCl [pH 6.8], 2% SDS, 10% glycerol, 5% 2-mercaptoethanol, and 0.01% bromophenol blue) with vigorous vortexing, incubated at 85 °C for 30 min to shear genomic DNA, and then at 95 °C for 5 min. Cell lysates were resolved by 12.5% SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and analyzed by Western blotting with the ECL reagent set, Clarity Western ECL Substrate (Bio-Rad Laboratories), using the chemiluminescence imaging system LuminoGraph III (ATTO Corporation). The signal intensities of bands in Western blots were quantified using a CS analyzer 4 (ATTO Corporation).

Protocol 3 (optional): cloning by limiting dilution

The knockout efficiency of the above protocol is generally high enough for applying the polyclonal knockout cells to subsequent experiments. In some cases, however, targeted disruption of a gene important for cell proliferation may slow proliferation of knockout cells, causing them to disappear from the polyclonal cell population after propagation, since the polyclonal cell lines generated as above contain some, if not many, non-knockout cells with a higher growth rate. Technical tips

•Before transfection, optimize electroporation conditions for the cells you use, as they depend critically on the cell type and the electroporation device. We used the Neon Transfection System Unit MPK5000 (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher) for THP-1 cells. According to the manufacturer’s instructions, we determined the electric pulse conditions (1400 V, 20 ms, 2 pulses), cell density (2.0 × 107 cells/mL), and total plasmid amount (10.75 µg) for a 100 µL Neon Tip, by measuring luciferase activity of the cell extract prepared from the THP-1 cells transfected with a firefly luciferase reporter plasmid. We estimated the transfection efficiency under these conditions by transfecting an EGFP-expressing plasmid; about 70% of live cells were GFP-positive 24 h after electroporation.•After transfection, we selected cells using an extremely high concentration of puromycin (10 µg/mL), >10 times the standard concentration. This transient, high-dose drug selection would enrich for cells that incorporate relatively high plasmid copy numbers. Therefore, determine the puromycin concentration for transient selection of the cells you use. We first determined the minimum puromycin concentration required to select THP-1 cells using GM containing 0, 0.1, 0.316, 1.0, 3.16, and 10 µg/mL puromycin. THP-1 cells died almost completely within 48 h in the medium containing 1.0 µg/mL puromycin, whereas they died only partially in the medium containing 0.316 µg/mL puromycin. This indicates that 1.0 µg/mL would be suitable for most experiments to select for puromycin-resistant stable transformants. Next, we tested 1, 5, and 10 µg/mL puromycin in pilot knockout experiments and found that transient selection with 10 µg/mL puromycin effectively generated multigene knockout cells. Method validation

The AP-1 transcription factor is a dimeric complex consisting of two bZIP proteins, the Jun and Fos families of which have been extensively studied [15]. We first attempted to generate single-gene-knockout HeLa cells for each of the seven family members, c-Jun, JunB, JunD, c-Fos, FosB, Fra1, and Fra2, to evaluate the knockout efficiencies of targeting plasmids. We constructed two targeting plasmids, TG1 and TG2, for each of the seven genes (Table 1) and tested their knockout efficiencies in HeLa cells by Western blotting, as described [3]. Expression of the protein from each targeted gene was strongly reduced to scarcely detectable levels in HeLa-derived cell lines, except for cell lines of JunB-TG1, JunB-TG2, FosB-TG2, and Fra2-TG2 (data not shown). Then, we attempted to generate multiplex gene knockout cell lines from THP-1 cells, including c-Jun/JunB/JunD-triple KO (Jun-TKO), c-Fos/FosB/Fra1/Fra2-quadruple KO (Fos-QKO), and c-Jun/JunB/JunD/c-Fos/FosB/Fra1/Fra2-septuple KO (JunFos-SKO), by co-transfecting THP-1 cells with mixtures of the targeting plasmids (Table 3), as illustrated in the Graphical Abstract. For the JunB-KO, we used a combination of the pX330-JunB-TG1 and -TG2 plasmids to attain high KO efficiency. We also generated the sole-survivor (ss)-KO cells, in which only one of the seven Jun/Fos genes remains undisrupted. For example, the JunB/JunD/c-Fos/FosB/Fra1/Fra2-hextuple KO (HKO) cells were designated as c-Jun-ss-HKO cells (Table 3).

THP-1 cells were transfected and puromycin-selected as described in Protocols 1 and 2, and the whole-cell lysates were analyzed by Western blotting (Fig. 1). In the vector control cells (Vector), c-Jun and c-Fos were inducibly expressed by stimulation with PMA, whereas JunB, JunD, FosB, Fra1, and Fra2 showed basal expression and PMA- or LPS-induced upregulation. In contrast, expression of the Jun family members was scarcely detectable in Jun-TKO and JunFos-SKO polyclonal cells. Expression of Fos family members was drastically reduced in Fos-QKO and JunFos-SKO polyclonal cells. No apparent differences in growth rate or cellular morphology were observed among wild-type THP-1, Vector control, Jun-TKO, and c-Fos-ss-HKO cells. However, Fos-QKO, JunFos-SKO, and c-Jun-ss-HKO cells appeared to grow more slowly than the others, and their growth rates gradually recovered after several weeks of continuous passage. This observation suggests that non-knockout cells, initially present in relatively small numbers within these polyclonal KO cell lines, might gradually increase their proportion, indicating that these polyclonal cell lines should not be used for any further experiments. Therefore, we used the method described in Protocol 3 to isolate clones from the Fos-QKO and JunFos-SKO cell lines, and designated them as TQ-series and TS-series clones, respectively. We then screened them by Western blotting to characterize their knockout status. Although the isolated clones showed various patterns of Jun and Fos family protein expression, TQ10, TQ15, and TQ22 clones did not express any Fos family proteins (Fig. 2A and B). In contrast, we were unable to isolate the JunFos-SKO clone (Figs. 2C-2E). Collectively, we obtained three Fos-QKO clones (14%) out of the 21 clones screened, but no JunFos-SKO clones from the 21 clones screened (Figs. 2B and 2E). We somehow managed to maintain the Fos-QKO clones for a few months, but found that they grew more slowly than vector-control cells, were prone to stochastic cell death, and were difficult to handle. These results suggest that the efficiency of simultaneous knockout is insufficient to recover complete seven-gene-KO clones or that the complete loss of all Jun/Fos family members is lethal or severely growth-impaired in THP-1 cells. In conclusion, we optimized the simultaneous multigene knockout protocol for THP-1 cells. THP-1 cells can differentiate into macrophage-like cells upon PMA treatment, acquiring the ability to produce cytokines. Therefore, multiplex gene KO in THP-1 cells would provide opportunities to investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying macrophage differentiation and cytokine production.

Limitations

•In this study, we roughly estimated knockout efficiencies by Western Blotting for the gene products of the targeted genes, but reduced protein expression does not necessarily reflect gene knockout. It could reflect transcriptional downregulation, translational inhibition, antibody epitope disruption without a frameshift, or even off-target effects. Therefore, DNA sequence analysis is required to confirm gene knockout unequivocally. The knockout efficiency of polyclonal cells can be estimated by deep sequencing analyses, such as amplicon sequencing of the sgRNA-target site [3]. For more precise determination of knockout status, isolate clones and perform PCR amplification and Sanger sequencing of the genomic DNA region containing the targeted sites.•In multigene knockout experiments, there is an inevitable trade-off between increasing the number of sgRNAs and the resulting nearly proportional increase in off-target mutations [2]. Therefore, we recommend generating at least two independent knockout cell lines with different target sites for each gene, and confirming that relevant phenotypes are identical between cell lines with the different targets.•Simultaneous induction of multiple DSBs on different chromosomes may cause a translocation, typically a reciprocal translocation [16]. Although translocations were likely rare in HeLa cells [3], they should be considered in multigene knockout experiments and verified after clone isolation, when needed.•We observed that the knockout clones thus obtained from HeLa or THP-1 cells are usually sensitive to puromycin, comparable to the parental cells, suggesting that the transfected DNA is rarely, if ever, integrated into the genome. Depending on the research purpose, however, integration of the introduced DNA into the genome may be problematic, and should therefore be checked after clone isolation, when needed.•As demonstrated by the Jun/Fos-KO experiments, the strategy of using polyclonal KO cell lines cannot be applied to genes important for cell survival or proliferation. For polyclonal knockout cells, we recommend avoiding long-term cultivation and confirming that expression of the targeted genes has not been recovered by the end of the experiments.
