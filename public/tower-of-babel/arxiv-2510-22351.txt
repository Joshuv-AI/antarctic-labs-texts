Design Stability in Adaptive Experiments: Implications for Treatment Effect Estimation
Saikat Sengupta mb2419@isical.ac.in, Koulik Khamaru kk1241@stat.rutgers.edu, Suvrojit Ghosh sg1565@scarletmail.rutgers.edu, Tirthankar Dasgupta tirthankar.dasgupta@rutgers.edu, [0.3cm] Indian Statistical Institute, Kolkata

Abstract
We study the problem of estimating the average treatment effect ( ATE ) under sequentially adaptive treatment assignment mechanisms. In contrast to classical completely randomized designs, we consider a setting in which the probability of assigning treatment to each experimental unit may depend on prior assignments and observed outcomes. Within the potential outcomes framework [ 1 ] , we propose and analyze two natural estimators for the ATE : the inverse propensity weighted (IPW) estimator and an augmented IPW (AIPW) estimator. The cornerstone of our analysis is the concept of design stability , which requires that as the number of units grows, either the assignment probabilities converge, or sample averages of the inverse propensity scores and of the inverse complement propensity scores converge in probability to fixed, non-random limits. Our main results establish central limit theorems for both the IPW and AIPW estimators under design stability and provide explicit expressions for their asymptotic variances. We further propose estimators for these variances, enabling the construction of asymptotically valid confidence intervals. Finally, we illustrate our theoretical results in the context of Wei’s adaptive coin design [ 2 ] and Efron’s biased coin design [ 3 ] , highlighting the applicability of the proposed methods to sequential experimentation with adaptive randomization.



Keywords:

Average treatment effect; Sequential treatment assignment; Design stability; Adaptive designs; IPW estimator; AIPW estimator.

Introduction

Estimating the average treatment effect is a foundational problem in causal inference, especially when evaluating interventions in fields such as healthcare

However, many real-world experiments depart from this idealized setting: units often arrive sequentially, treatment assignments may adapt over time based on previous allocations, observed outcomes, or covariate information, and the study population is finite. Such sequential finite-population setups are common in adaptive clinical trials, online A/B testing, and policy evaluations. In these scenarios, adaptively assigning treatments can lead to complex dependencies among units, causing traditional ATE estimators to become biased or inefficient and undermining the applicability of conventional asymptotic results. Despite its practical importance, this setting remains methodologically less explored.

This paper develops a general framework for ATE estimation and inference under sequential designs in finite populations. Related work

Causal inference in experimental settings is typically framed through two paradigms. The infinite-superpopulation perspective views study units as random draws from an underlying population, with randomness arising from the data-generating process. In contrast, the finite-population or design-based perspective treats the set of units as fixed, with uncertainty introduced solely through the experimental design. This latter view, combined with the potential outcomes formulation, traces back to
[
1
]

[

.

The study of asymptotic normality in causal inference can be traced back to results on simple random sampling. Classical central limit theorems were established by

,

]

[

,

,
22
]
. Further foundational work includes the theory of U-statistics developed by
[
23
]
and the weak convergence results of
[
24

, which laid the groundwork for modern asymptotic theory in survey sampling and experimental design. Because treatment and control groups in randomized experiments correspond to simple random samples from the finite set of experimental units, these sampling-based central limit theorems are directly applicable to the difference-in-means estimator of the average treatment effect. This connection underlies much of the early asymptotic justification in randomization-based causal inference
[
25
,
26
,
27
,

.

In modern applications such as adaptive clinical trials
[
29
]
, online A/B tests
[

]
, and adaptive policy experiments
[

]
, treatment assignments may evolve in response to interim data, violating the independence assumptions of static designs. Such sequential mechanisms introduce dependence across units, requiring martingale‐based CLTs

In spite of the recent explosion of research on design-based finite population inference, to the best of our knowledge, rigorous theory for finite-population central limit theorems under general sequential general sequential Bernoulli assignments remains scarce. Recent explorations on inference of ATE from adaptive designs have been done in a setting where the potential outcomes for each experimental unit are assumed to follow an unknown probability distribution
\mathcal{P}
and the ATE is defined as the difference of expectations of the potential outcomes with respect to
\mathcal{P}

The present work addresses this gap, establishing central limit theorems for IPW and AIPW-inspired estimators in finite populations under broad sequential designs in a purely design-based inferential framework, where the potential outcomes are assumed fixed.

Problem Description

Consider a study with
N
experimental units indexed by
i=1,\ldots,N
. We adopt the potential outcomes framework, introduced by
[
1
]
and later formalized by
[
36
]
. For each unit
i
, the outcome of interest
Y_{i}
is characterized by two potential outcomes:
Y_{i}(0)
under control and
Y_{i}(1)
under treatment. The individual treatment effect is defined as
\tau_{i}=Y_{i}(1)-Y_{i}(0)
, and our target parameter is the average treatment effect (
ATE
), defined as

\displaystyle\bar{\tau}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\tau_{i}\qquad\qquad(\textsc{ATE}).

(1)

Assumptions of homogeneity of unit-level treatment effects
\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{N}
play important roles in finite-population causal inference. For example, the assumption that the
\tau_{i}
’s are the same for
i=1,\ldots,N
, or equivalently,

\displaystyle Y_{i}(1)=Y_{i}(0)+\tau\quad\text{for all }i,

(2)

for some constant
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
is called
additivity
of potential outcomes and is standard in literature
[
14
, Chapter 6]
. Here we introduce the following definition that generalizes the concept of treatment effect homogeneity:

Definition 1

(
Generalized treatment effect homogeneity
)
.

Potential outcomes
(Y_{i}(0),Y_{i}(1))
,
i=1,\ldots,N
are said to satisfy generalized treatment effect homogeneity if

\displaystyle Y_{i}(1)-\overline{Y}_{N}(1)\propto Y_{i}(0)-\overline{Y}_{N}(0)\quad\text{for all }i,

(3)

where
\overline{Y}_{N}(\ell)=\tfrac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}Y_{i}(\ell)
for
\ell\in\{0,1\}
.

It is easy to see that additivity (
2
) implies generalized treatment effect homogeneity (
3
). Another sufficient condition for (
3
) is additivity of potential outcomes on a log-scale, that is,

\displaystyle Y_{i}(1)=cY_{i}(0)\quad\text{for all }i,

(4)

for some constant
c\in\mathbb{R}
. We will see that conditions (
2
)-(
4
) play important roles in the inference problem to be discussed.

In the classical randomized treatment allocation design, a pre-defined constant number
N_{1}
of the

N
units are assigned to treatment, with the subset selected uniformly at random
[
36
]
. Formally, let
\mathbf{K}=(K_{1},K_{2},\ldots,K_{N})^{\text{T}}\in\{0,1\}^{N}
denote the random assignment vector, where
K_{i}=1
if
i
th unit is assigned to the treatment group and
K_{i}=0
otherwise. A simple random sample of size
N_{1}
is chosen from the finite population using the assignment vector
\mathbf{K}
, where
\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{K}=\mathbf{k})=\binom{N}{N_{1}}^{-1}
for all
\mathbf{k}\in\{0,1\}^{N}
satisfying
\mathbf{1}_{N}^{\text{T}}\mathbf{k}=N_{1}
. Given a treatment assignment vector
\mathbf{k}\in\{0,1\}^{N}
, the observed data
\{Y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
are the realized potential outcomes, where each unit’s outcome corresponds to its assigned treatment or control, defined by

\displaystyle Y_{i}=K_{i}Y_{i}(1)+(1-K_{i})Y_{i}(0)\qquad\text{for}\qquad i=1,\ldots,N.

(5)

A natural estimator of the ATE under the randomized treatment assignment described above is the difference in sample means between the treatment and control groups, i.e.

\displaystyle\widehat{\widebar{\tau}}_{\text{avg}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}K_{i}Y_{i}-\frac{1}{N_{0}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(1-K_{i})Y_{i},\qquad(\textsc{difference-in-means estimator})

where
N_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}K_{i}\text{ and }N_{0}=N-N_{1}.
This estimator is unbiased and satisfies a central limit theorem as
N
grows to infinity
[
28
]
. We note that the difference-in-means estimator is a special case of the Horvitz–Thompson type estimator
[
37
]
, also known as the inverse propensity weighted (IPW) estimator, defined as

\displaystyle\widehat{\tau}_{\textsc{IPW}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\{\frac{K_{i}Y_{i}}{p_{i}}-\frac{(1-K_{i})Y_{i}}{1-p_{i}}\right\},

(6)

where the weights
p_{i}=N_{1}/N
for
i=1,\ldots,N
.

In contrast to the classical randomized treatment assignment, we consider in this paper a
sequential treatment assignment
mechanism, in which the probability of assigning the
i
th unit to treatment is adaptive. In other words, for each unit
i\in{1,2,\ldots,N}
, the assignment indicator
K_{i}
, conditional on the past history, follows a Bernoulli distribution with success probability
p_{i}
, which we refer to as the
inclusion probability
. The inclusion probability
p_{i}
is not fixed; rather, it is a measurable function of the prior assignment history and outcomes. Formally, we define a sequence of increasing sigma-fields
\{\mathcal{F}_{i-1}\}_{i\geq 1}
, where
\mathcal{F}_{i-1}=\sigma(K_{1},Y_{1},\ldots,K_{i-1},Y_{i-1})
represents the cumulative information available after assigning treatment or control and observing the outcome of the
(i-1)
th unit. The assignment mechanism is such that

\displaystyle p_{i}\in\mathcal{F}_{i-1}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{P}(K_{i}=1\mid\mathcal{F}_{i-1})=p_{i}.\qquad(\textsc{Sequential treatment assignment})

(7)

In words, the probability of assigning treatment to unit
i
may depend on all previous assignments and observations up to stage
(i-1)

in an arbitrary way
; we call this assignment a
sequential treatment assignment
. As an example, one may choose
p_{i}
to promote relative balance between the numbers of treatment and control assignments. In this case,
p_{i}
can be defined as the complement of the moving average of past assignments:

\displaystyle p_{i}=1-\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}K_{j}}{i-1}\qquad\text{for \;\; $i\geq 2,$ \;\;\;\; and}\qquad p_{1}=\frac{1}{2}.

In this assignment, if the previous units have mostly been assigned treatment, the chance of assigning treatment to the next unit will be lowered and vice versa
[
2
]
.

In this paper, we aim to develop estimators, establish their asymptotics, and provide valid inference for the ATE under the sequential treatment assignment scheme (
7
). We first consider the Horvitz–Thompson type estimator defined in (
6
) as a natural unbiased estimator of the average treatment effect. In our setting, unlike a static completely randomized experiment, the
p_{i}
’s will not be equal and will depend on the past history.

It is well known that IPW estimators suffer from inflated variance when the probabilities approach extremes
[
38
]
. In a model-based setting, this limitation of IPW estimator is mitigated by the AIPW estimator
[
35
]
via model augmentation, offering double robustness. In our setting, where no probability model for the potential outcomes is assumed, we propose the following finite-population model-free version of the AIPW estimator:

\displaystyle\widehat{\tau}_{\textsc{AIPW}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[\left\{\frac{K_{i}(Y_{i}-\widehat{Y}_{i-1}(1))}{p_{i}}+\widehat{Y}_{i-1}(1)\right\}-\left\{\frac{(1-K_{i})(Y_{i}-\widehat{Y}_{i-1}(0))}{1-p_{i}}+\widehat{Y}_{i-1}(0)\right\}\right],

(8)

where
\widehat{Y}_{1}(0)=\widehat{Y}_{1}(1)=0
, and for
i\geq 2

\displaystyle\widehat{Y}_{i-1}(0)=\frac{1}{i-1}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\frac{(1-K_{j})Y_{j}}{1-p_{j}},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\widehat{Y}_{i-1}(1)=\frac{1}{i-1}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\frac{K_{j}Y_{j}}{p_{j}}.

(9)

Note that for large
N
, the weighted average
\widehat{Y}_{N}(\ell)
serves as an intuitive estimator of
\overline{Y}_{N}(\ell)
for
\ell\in\{0,1\}
.
In this sense,
\widehat{\tau}_{\textsc{AIPW}}
is directly motivated from the classical AIPW estimator
[
35
]
. We formalize this intuition in a later theorem on the behavior of the AIPW estimator
\widehat{\tau}_{\textsc{AIPW}}
(see Theorem
4
).

4
Main Results

This section presents our main theoretical contributions. We establish the asymptotic normality of the estimators

\widehat{\tau}_{\textsc{IPW}}
and
\widehat{\tau}_{\textsc{AIPW}}
under the sequential experimental designs in (
7
),
and derive conservative variance estimators that facilitate the construction of asymptotically valid confidence intervals for the average treatment effect
\bar{\tau}
. Our analysis proceeds in two steps: first, we prove central limit theorems for both estimators;
second, we propose conservative estimators of their asymptotic variances.
Together, these results enable the construction of asymptotically valid confidence intervals for
\bar{\tau}
.

The foundation of our analysis rests on a structural condition that we call
design stability
. We consider two notions of design stability (a)
strong stability
, and (b)
weak stability
. Strong design stability requires that the assignment probabilities themselves converge asymptotically, ensuring that the design does not drift in the limit. Weak design stability, however, relaxes this by requiring only that the sample averages of the inverse propensity scores and of the inverse complement propensity scores converge in probability to finite, non-random limits. At a high level, both forms of stability ensure that the cumulative effect of sequential randomization does not induce excessive variability in the long run.

Definition 2

(
Strong design stability
)
.

A sequential design with inclusion probabilities
\{p_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
is said to be strongly stable if there exists a
non-random
scalar
p^{\star}\in(0,1)
such that

\displaystyle p_{i}\xrightarrow{p}p^{\star}.

(10)

Although the notion of strong design stability is intuitive, it is not satisfied by several popular designs. A concrete example is Efron’s biased coin design
[
3
]
, which enforces balance between treatment and control assignments. Fortunately, Definition
2
can be relaxed so that, even if a design is not stable in the strong sense, central limit theorems for the IPW and AIPW estimators may still hold under weaker regularity conditions. This motivates the following weaker notion of stability.

Definition 3

(
Weak design stability
)
.

A sequential design with inclusion probabilities
\{p_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
is said to be weakly stable if there exists
non-random
scalars
p_{1}^{\star},p_{2}^{\star}\in(0,1)
such that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{p_{i}}\xrightarrow{p}\frac{1}{p_{1}^{\star}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{1-p_{i}}\xrightarrow{p}\frac{1}{1-p_{2}^{\star}}.

(11)

The tradeoff between these two stability notions becomes apparent when estimating the asymptotic variance of our estimators to construct confidence intervals for the ATE. While variance estimators can be constructed in a completely data-dependent manner under strong stability, weak stability requires additional restrictions (see Theorems
3
and
6
for more details).

4.1
The IPW estimator

We now turn to the asymptotic behavior of the IPW estimator
\widehat{\tau}_{\textsc{IPW}}
, as defined in (
6
). To derive our main result, we impose a positivity condition on the inclusion probabilities along with uniform boundedness and natural moment conditions on the potential outcomes.

Assumption 1
.

The inclusion probabilities and potential outcomes satisfy the following regularity conditions:

(a)

There exists
\delta\in(0,1)
such that
p_{i}\in[\delta,1-\delta]
for all
i\geq 1
.

(b)

There exists a constant
M>0
such that

\displaystyle|Y_{i}(\ell)|\leq M\quad\text{for all }i\geq 1\text{ and }\ell\in\{0,1\}.

(c)

The following limits exist:

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}Y_{i}(0)^{2}=m^{2}_{0},\quad\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}Y_{i}(1)^{2}=m^{2}_{1},\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}Y_{i}(0)Y_{i}(1)=m_{01},

where
m^{2}_{0},m^{2}_{1}>0
and
m_{01}\in\mathbb{R}
.

The first condition in Assumption
1
ensures that the IPW estimator (
6
) is well defined, the second condition is a uniform bound on the potential outcomes, and the third assumption ensures that the limiting asymptotic variance of
\widehat{\tau}_{\textsc{IPW}}
exists. With this set-up, we have the following guarantees on the asympotic behavior of
\widehat{\tau}_{\textsc{IPW}}
.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose Assumption
1
holds, and the sequential design with inclusion probabilities
\{p_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
is either strongly or weakly stable in the sense of Definition
2
or Definition
3
, respectively. Then the IPW estimator (
6
) satisfies

\sqrt{N}\left(\widehat{\tau}_{\textsc{IPW}}-\bar{\tau}\right)\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}\left(0,\mathrm{V}^{\mathrm{IPW}}\right),

(12)

with asymptotic variance

\displaystyle\mathrm{V}^{\mathrm{IPW}}=\begin{cases}\mathrm{V}^{\text{IPW}}_{\text{strong}}=m^{2}_{0}\dfrac{p^{\star}}{1-p^{\star}}+m^{2}_{1}\dfrac{1-p^{\star}}{p^{\star}}+2m_{01}&\text{(strong design stability)},\\[11.99998pt]
\mathrm{V}^{\text{IPW}}_{\text{weak}}=m^{2}_{0}\dfrac{p_{2}^{\star}}{1-p_{2}^{\star}}+m^{2}_{1}\dfrac{1-p_{1}^{\star}}{p_{1}^{\star}}+2m_{01}&\text{(weak design stability)}.\end{cases}

(13)

Remark 1
.

The proof of Theorem
1
proceeds by rewriting the centered and scaled IPW estimator as a sum of martingale difference terms. This representation allows us to apply the martingale central limit theorem
[
32
, Chapter 3]
. We establish unbiasedness by verifying that each summand has zero conditional mean, and then compute the conditional variance, which converges under both stable and weakly stable designs to the stated asymptotic variance. Finally, we check the Lindeberg condition, ensuring that the contribution of large deviations vanishes. Together, these steps yield asymptotic normality of the IPW estimator with the asymptotic variance given in (
13
). Refer to Supplementary material
7.1
for detailed proof.

Having established the asymptotic normality of the IPW estimator, we next construct confidence intervals for the average treatment effect
\bar{\tau}
. This, in turn, requires estimation of the asymptotic variance
\mathrm{V}^{\mathrm{IPW}}
. First, we consider that the design is strongly stable in the sense of Definition
2
, and assume that
p^{*}
is known (which is the case in our illustrative example on strongly stable designs). To estimate
\mathrm{V}^{\text{IPW}}_{\text{strong}}
in (
13
), we must estimate
m^{2}_{0}
,
m^{2}_{1}
, and
m_{01}
. While obtaining consistent estimators of
m^{2}_{0}
and
m^{2}_{1}
under strong stability is straightforward, the cross-moment term
m_{01}
cannot be estimated from the observed outcomes without additional assumptions, as only one potential outcome is observed for each unit. To address this problem, we apply the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality to obtain
|m_{01}|\leq m_{0}m_{1}
, leading to the conservative variance estimator:

\displaystyle\widehat{V^{\text{IPW}}_{\text{strong}}}=\left(\widehat{m}_{0}\sqrt{\frac{p^{\star}}{1-p^{\star}}}+\widehat{m}_{1}\sqrt{\frac{1-p^{\star}}{p^{\star}}}\right)^{2},

(14)

where
\widehat{m}_{0}
and
\widehat{m}_{1}
are estimators of
m_{0}
and
m_{1}

\displaystyle\widehat{m}^{2}_{0}=\frac{1}{\max\{N_{0},1\}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(1-K_{i})Y_{i}^{2},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\widehat{m}^{2}_{1}=\frac{1}{\max\{N_{1},1\}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}K_{i}Y_{i}^{2},\qquad

(15)

where

For strongly stable designs (Definition

), respectively, is consistent when the potential outcomes are additive on the log-scale; that is, when they satisfy (
4
). Thus, we obtain the following theorem.

Theorem 3
.

For weakly stable designs (Definition
3
), under the sufficient condition (
18
), the estimators
\widetilde{m}_{0}^{2}
and
\widetilde{m}_{1}^{2}
from (
19
) and (
20
) are consistent for
m^{2}_{0}
and
m^{2}_{1}
, respectively.
Furthermore, the variance estimator
\widehat{V^{\text{IPW}}_{\text{weak}}}
given by (
17
) provides a conservative estimate of
V^{\text{IPW}}_{\text{weak}}
,
and is consistent when the potential outcomes are additive on a log scale, that is, satisfy (
4
).

Refer to Supplementary material
7.3
for a proof of the theorem.

Remark 3
.

If, for a weakly stable design, the limiting values
p_{1}^{\star},p_{2}^{\star},
and
\widetilde{p}
are unknown or difficult to compute explicitly, we need to estimate them. Since
p_{i}\in\mathcal{F}_{i-1}
, the inclusion probability is deterministically known to the experimenter given the history. We therefore propose the following intuitive and consistent estimators for
p_{1}^{\star},p_{2}^{\star}
and
\widetilde{p}
:

\widehat{p}_{1}^{\star}=\frac{1}{\tfrac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\tfrac{1}{p_{i}}},\qquad\widehat{p}_{2}^{\star}=1-\frac{1}{\tfrac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\tfrac{1}{1-p_{i}}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\overline{p}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}.

(21)

Substituting the above estimators into (
17
), a conservative estimator of
\mathrm{V}^{\mathrm{IPW}}
under weak stability when
p_{1}^{\star},p_{2}^{\star}
and
\widetilde{p}
are unknown is:

\displaystyle\widehat{V^{\text{IPW}}_{\text{weak}}}=\widetilde{m}_{0}^{2}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{1-p_{i}}-1\right)+\widetilde{m}_{1}^{2}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{p_{i}}-1\right)+2\widetilde{m}_{0}\widetilde{m}_{1},

(22)

where
\widetilde{m}_{0}^{2}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}(1-p_{i})}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(1-K_{i})Y_{i}^{2}
and
\widetilde{m}_{1}^{2}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}K_{i}Y_{i}^{2}.

Since we have constructed conservative variance estimators of
\mathrm{V}^{\mathrm{IPW}}
, for any target level
\alpha\in(0,1)
, asymptotically conservative confidence interval for
\bar{\tau}
, that is, one with coverage at least
(1-\alpha)
can be constructed,

\displaystyle\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\bar{\tau}\in\left[\widehat{\tau}_{\textsc{IPW}}-z_{1-\alpha/2}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{\widehat{V}}{N}},\;\;\widehat{\tau}_{\textsc{IPW}}+z_{1-\alpha/2}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{\widehat{V}}{N}}\right]\right)\geq 1-\alpha,

where
\widehat{V}=\widehat{V^{\text{IPW}}_{\text{strong}}}
or
\widehat{V^{\text{IPW}}_{\text{weak}}}
, and

z_{1-\alpha/2}
denotes the
(1-\alpha/2)
th quantile of the standard normal distribution.
Moreover, if the potential outcomes satisfy the log-additive treatment-effect model (
4
),
the inequality holds with equality, yielding asymptotically exact coverage.

4.2
The AIPW-type estimator

We now analyze the asymptotic behavior of the AIPW estimator
\widehat{\tau}_{\textsc{AIPW}}
, as defined in (
8
).

Assumption 2
.

The inclusion probabilities and potential outcomes satisfy the following regularity conditions:

(a)

There exists
\delta\in(0,1)
such that
p_{i}\in[\delta,1-\delta]
for all
i\geq 1
.

(b)

There exists a constant
M>0
such that

\displaystyle|Y_{i}(\ell)|\leq M\quad\text{for all }i\geq 1\text{ and }\ell\in\{0,1\}.

(c)

The following limits exist for
\ell\in\{0,1\}:

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\widebar{Y}_{N}(\ell)=\widebar{Y}_{\ell},\qquad\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(Y_{i}(\ell)-\widebar{Y}_{N}(\ell)\right)^{2}=\sigma^{2}_{\ell},

\displaystyle\text{and}\qquad\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(Y_{i}(0)-\widebar{Y}_{N}(0)\right)\left(Y_{i}(1)-\widebar{Y}_{N}(1)\right)=\sigma_{01},

where
\sigma^{2}_{0},\sigma^{2}_{1}>0
and
\widebar{Y}_{0},\widebar{Y}_{1},\sigma_{01}\in\mathbb{R}
.

The first two conditions in Assumption
2
are same as that of Assumption
1
. The last condition above ensures that the limiting variance of the AIPW estimator exists. With this set-up, we state the asymptotic behavior of the AIPW estimator
\widehat{\tau}_{\textsc{AIPW}}
.

Theorem 4
.

Suppose Assumption
2
holds, and the sequential design with inclusion probabilities
\{p_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
is either strongly or weakly stable in the sense of Definition
2
or Definition
3
, respectively. Then the AIPW estimator (
8
) satisfies

\sqrt{N}\left(\widehat{\tau}_{\textsc{AIPW}}-\bar{\tau}\right)\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}\left(0,\mathrm{V}^{\mathrm{AIPW}}\right),

(24)

with asymptotic variance

\displaystyle\mathrm{V}^{\mathrm{AIPW}}=\begin{cases}\mathrm{V}^{\text{AIPW}}_{\text{strong}}=\sigma^{2}_{0}\dfrac{p^{\star}}{1-p^{\star}}+\sigma^{2}_{1}\dfrac{1-p^{\star}}{p^{\star}}+2\sigma_{01}&\text{under strong design stability},\\[11.99998pt]
\mathrm{V}^{\text{AIPW}}_{\text{weak}}=\sigma^{2}_{0}\dfrac{p_{2}^{\star}}{1-p_{2}^{\star}}+\sigma^{2}_{1}\dfrac{1-p_{1}^{\star}}{p_{1}^{\star}}+2\sigma_{01}&\text{under weak design stability}.\end{cases}

(25)

Remark 4
.

The proof of Theorem
4
requires a different strategy from that of Theorem
1
, since the AIPW estimator is not directly amenable to martingale central limit theorem. To handle this, we introduce a proxy estimator that is analytically more tractable and can be expressed as sum of a martingale difference sequence, allowing the martingale central limit theorem to establish its asymptotic normality. The key step is then to show that the difference between the proxy and the actual AIPW estimator is asymptotically negligible, using variance bounds and Hájek’s lemma (see Supplementary material
6
). This ensures that the asymptotic distribution of the AIPW estimator coincides with that of the proxy, yielding the stated central limit theorem with variance given in Theorem
4
. See Supplementary material
7.4
for detailed proof of the above theorem.

Remark 5
.

Note that
\mathrm{V}^{\mathrm{AIPW}}\leq\mathrm{V}^{\mathrm{IPW}}
; that is,
\widehat{\tau}_{\textsc{AIPW}}
is more efficient than
\widehat{\tau}_{\textsc{IPW}}
. This fact clearly establishes the superiority of the AIPW estimator over the IPW estimator in finite population design-based inference under the adaptive assignment mechanism defined in (
7
).

We now turn to the problem of estimating the asymptotic variance
\mathrm{V}^{\mathrm{AIPW}}
.
Under strong design stability (Definition
2
) with known
p^{\star}
, estimation of
\mathrm{V}^{\text{AIPW}}_{\text{strong}}
in (
25
) requires estimation of
\sigma^{2}_{0}
,
\sigma^{2}_{1}
and
\sigma_{01}
. As earlier, the covariance term
\sigma_{01}
depends on both potential outcomes for the same unit and therefore cannot be estimated without additional restrictions. Analogous to the estimation of
\mathrm{V}^{\mathrm{IPW}}
in Section
4.1
, we invoke Cauchy-Schwarz inequality to get
|\sigma_{01}|\leq\sigma_{0}\sigma_{1}
, yielding the following conservative estimator of
\mathrm{V}^{\text{AIPW}}_{\text{strong}}
as follows:

\displaystyle\widehat{V^{\text{AIPW}}_{\text{strong}}}=\left(\widehat{\sigma}_{0}\sqrt{\frac{p^{\star}}{1-p^{\star}}}+\widehat{\sigma}_{1}\sqrt{\frac{1-p^{\star}}{p^{\star}}}\right)^{2},

(26)

where
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{0}
and
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{1}
are estimators of
\sigma_{0}
and
\sigma_{1}
that are consistent under strong design stability. We propose the following estimators:

\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\max\{N_{0},1\}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(1-K_{i})\big(Y_{i}-\widehat{Y}_{i-1}(0)\big)^{2},

(27a)

\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\max\{N_{1},1\}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}K_{i}\big(Y_{i}-\widehat{Y}_{i-1}(1)\big)^{2},

(27b)

where
N_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}K_{i}
and
N_{0}=N-N_{1}
.
We set
\widehat{Y}_{1}(0)=\widehat{Y}_{1}(1)=0
, and for
i\geq 2
define

\displaystyle\widehat{Y}_{i-1}(0)=\frac{1}{i-1}\sum_{j<i}\frac{(1-K_{j})Y_{j}}{1-p_{j}},\qquad\widehat{Y}_{i-1}(1)=\frac{1}{i-1}\sum_{j<i}\frac{K_{j}Y_{j}}{p_{j}}.

The variance estimator in (
26
), which incorporates the estimators in (
27
), is consistent when the potential outcomes satisfy the generalized treatment effect homogeneity condition in Definition
1
. The preceding discussion leads to the following theorem.

Theorem 5
.

For strongly stable designs (Definition
2
), the estimators
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{0}
and
\widehat{\sigma}_{1}^{2}
defined in (
27
)
are consistent for
\sigma^{2}_{0}
and
\sigma^{2}_{1}
, respectively.
Furthermore, the variance estimator
\widehat{V^{\text{AIPW}}_{\text{strong}}}
given by (
26
) provides a conservative estimate of
\mathrm{V}^{\text{AIPW}}_{\text{strong}}
,
and is consistent when the potential outcomes satisfy generalized treatment effect homogeneity (
3
).

A proof of this theorem is given in Supplementary material
7.5
.

As noted in Section
4.1
, under weak design stability (Definition
3
) with known
p_{1}^{\star}
and
p_{2}^{\star}
, variance estimation is not straightforward, as additional conditions are required for the consistent estimation of
\sigma^{2}_{0}
and
\sigma^{2}_{1}

As before, under the additional assumption (

) and with known

, the variance components

and

\displaystyle\widetilde{\sigma}^{2}_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N(1-\widetilde{p})}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(1-K_{i})\big(Y_{i}-\widehat{Y}_{i-1}(0)\big)^{2},

(28)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\sigma}^{2}_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N\widetilde{p}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}K_{i}\big(Y_{i}-\widehat{Y}_{i-1}(1)\big)^{2}.

(29)

As discussed previously, the cross-moment term
\sigma_{01}
cannot be estimated without additional assumptions, since it depends on both potential outcomes for all units. We therefore bound it from above using the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality. Consequently, asymptotic variance

\displaystyle\widehat{V^{\text{AIPW}}_{\text{weak}}}=\widetilde{\sigma}_{0}^{2}{\frac{p_{2}^{\star}}{1-p_{2}^{\star}}}+\widetilde{\sigma}_{1}^{2}{\frac{1-p_{1}^{\star}}{p_{1}^{\star}}}+2\widetilde{\sigma}_{0}\widetilde{\sigma}_{1}.

1

Theorem 6
.

For weakly stable designs (Definition
3
), under the sufficient condition (
18

\widetilde{\sigma}_{0}^{2}
and
\widetilde{\sigma}_{1}^{2}
from (
28
) and (
29

\sigma^{2}_{0}
and
\sigma^{2}_{1}
, respectively.
Furthermore, the variance estimator
\widehat{V^{\text{AIPW}}_{\text{weak}}}
given by (
30
) provides a conservative estimate of
\mathrm{V}^{\text{AIPW}}_{\text{weak}}
,
and is consistent when the potential outcomes satisfy generalized treatment effect additivity (
3
).

See Supplementary material
7.6

Remark 6
.

If for a strongly stable design the limiting value

5.1
Wei’s Adaptive Coin Design

We begin with Wei’s adaptive coin design
[
2

m_{k}
and
n_{k}
denote, respectively, the numbers of treatment and control units among the first
k

D_{k}=m_{k}-n_{k},

R_{k}=\frac{D_{k}}{k},

k

Under Wei’s adaptive coin design, the
i

\displaystyle p_{i}=f\left(R_{i-1}\right),

(32)

where
f:[-1,1]\to[0,1]
is a non-increasing function satisfying (i)
f(0)=\frac{1}{2}
and (ii)

f
is continuous at zero. For the estimators
\widehat{\tau}_{\textsc{IPW}}

If
f

\displaystyle p_{i}=\min\big\{\max\{f(R_{i-1}),\delta\},\,1-\delta\big\},

(33)

\delta\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
.

p_{i}\in[\delta,\,1-\delta]
for all
i\geq 1

Intuitively, when the trial is in its early stages, the number of units in each group can differ substantially in relative terms; the design then shifts
p_{i}
away from
\tfrac{1}{2}
to favor the smaller group and reduce imbalance. As the sample size grows, any absolute difference in group sizes becomes small relative to the total number of units, causing
R_{i-1}
to shrink and
p_{i}
to converge to
\tfrac{1}{2}
. The following Lemma, which is a direct consequence of
[
2

1
,
2
,
4
,
5

Lemma 1

Wei’s adaptive coin design (

2

See Supplementary material

\displaystyle=m^{2}_{0}+m^{2}_{1}+2m_{01},

These results align with the theoretical guarantees of variance estimator consistency established in Theorems

and

In general, these results emphasize that although both estimators attain reliable coverage, the AIPW estimator achieves this with noticeably tighter intervals, making it generally more efficient and preferable in practical applications.

5.2
Efron’s Biased Coin Design

Moving beyond Wei’s adaptive coin design, we consider the biased coin design introduced by
[
3
]
which enforces another form of approximate balance between the number of allocations in the treatment and control groups. As in the previous section, let
D_{k}=m_{k}-n_{k}
denote the imbalance between the treatment and control groups after the assignment of the
k
th unit,
where
m_{k}
and
n_{k}
denote the numbers of treatment and control assignments, respectively. Under Efron’s biased coin design, the
i
th unit is assigned to treatment with probability:

\displaystyle p_{i}=\begin{cases}\eta&\text{if }D_{i-1}<0\\
\frac{1}{2}&\text{if }D_{i-1}=0\\
1-\eta&\text{if }D_{i-1}>0\end{cases},

(36)

where
\eta\in[\frac{1}{2},1)
controls the strength of the bias toward balance. In words, a larger value of
\eta
forces faster correction of imbalance.

It is worth noting that
p_{i}
takes the values
\eta
and
1-\eta
infinitely often, and thus Efron’s biased coin design is not strongly stable in the sense of Definition
2
. However, the following lemma establishes weak stability of the design, making Theorems
1
,
3
,
4
, and
6
applicable.

Lemma 2
.

Efron’s biased coin design (
36
) is weakly stable in sense of Definition
3
, with
p_{1}^{\star}=\frac{4\eta^{2}(1-\eta)}{1-4\eta+12\eta^{2}-8\eta^{3}}
and
p_{2}^{\star}=\frac{1-4\eta+8\eta^{2}-4\eta^{3}}{1-4\eta+12\eta^{2}-8\eta^{3}}
. Moreover,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}\xrightarrow{p}\frac{1}{2}.

Remark 7
.

The proof of Lemma
2
proceeds by studying the treatment–control imbalance sequence
\{D_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
. We first establish that
\{D_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
forms an irreducible and positively recurrent Markov chain by applying Foster’s Theorem
[
39
]
. The resulting positive recurrence and irreducibility ensure the existence of a unique stationary distribution, which, together with the mean ergodic theorem, facilitates characterization of the limiting behavior of long-run averages of functions of the assignment probabilities. Details of the proof are provided in Supplementary material
8.2
.

Substituting the values of
p_{1}^{\star}
and
p_{2}^{\star}
from Lemma
2
into Theorems
1
and
4
yields the limiting variances of the IPW and AIPW estimators,
\widehat{\tau}_{\textsc{IPW}}
and
\widehat{\tau}_{\textsc{AIPW}}
, under Efron’s design:

\displaystyle V^{\text{IPW}}_{\text{Efron}}

\displaystyle=\left(m^{2}_{0}+m^{2}_{1}\right)\frac{1-4\eta+8\eta^{2}-4\eta^{3}}{4\eta^{2}(1-\eta)}+2m_{01},

(37a)

\displaystyle V^{\text{AIPW}}_{\text{Efron}}

\displaystyle=\left(\sigma^{2}_{0}+\sigma^{2}_{1}\right)\frac{1-4\eta+8\eta^{2}-4\eta^{3}}{4\eta^{2}(1-\eta)}+2\sigma_{01}.

(37b)

Furthermore, as Lemma
2
shows, under this design
N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}\xrightarrow{p}\tfrac{1}{2}
, satisfying the sufficient condition in (
18
).
This result allows for consistent estimation of
m^{2}_{0}
and
m^{2}_{1}
from (
19
) and (

6
Discussion

We have developed a general theoretical framework for conducting inference on average treatment effects in settings where treatment assignment is sequentially adaptive within a finite population. This framework unifies and extends existing results by accommodating a broad class of adaptive randomization schemes, where assignment probabilities may evolve over time based on past outcomes. Within this setup, we establish central limit theorems (CLTs) for both inverse probability weighted (IPW) and augmented IPW (AIPW) estimators under strong and weak design stability conditions. Although the limiting distributions feature explicit expressions for the asymptotic variances, the fundamental problem of causal inference - not being able to observe the two potential outcomes for each unit - leads to challenges in their estimation. We propose conservative variance estimators that are consistent under different forms of treatment effect homogeneity.

To demonstrate the applicability of our framework, we analyze Wei’s adaptive coin design and Efron’s biased coin design, two classical examples in sequential experimentation. These applications reveal how the general theory accommodates designs that deviate from strong stability (e.g., Efron’s design), thereby illustrating its flexibility and robustness.

From a practical standpoint, our findings provide reassurance that adaptive treatment assignment mechanisms—increasingly popular in modern experimental and clinical trial settings—can be used within a finite population framework without imposing any model on the potential outcome. The research opens up several new research possibilities. Extending the framework to covariate-adaptive designs where assignments depend explicitly on pre-measured covariates
[
40
,
41
]
, would broaden the applicability of the theory. Adaptive treatment assignment mechanisms also provide a natural solution to finding optimal designs in a finite population setting, e.g.,
[
42
]
and the results presented in this paper can provide an inferential framework for such adaptive designs.

7.1
Proof of Theorem
1
(CLT for the IPW estimator)

We begin by expressing the centered and scaled IPW estimator
\sqrt{N}\left(\widehat{\tau}_{\textsc{IPW}}-\bar{\tau}\right)
as a sum of a martingale difference sequence.
This allows us to prove the central limit theorem for
\widehat{\tau}_{\textsc{IPW}}
via an application of the martingale central limit theorem
[
32
, Chapter 3]
.

\displaystyle\sqrt{N}\left(\widehat{\tau}_{\textsc{IPW}}-\bar{\tau}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{K_{i}-p_{i}}{\sqrt{N}}\left(\frac{Y_{i}(0)}{1-p_{i}}+\frac{Y_{i}(1)}{p_{i}}\right)\;=\;\sum_{i=1}^{N}\xi_{i},

where
\xi_{i}=\tfrac{K_{i}-p_{i}}{\sqrt{N}}\left(\tfrac{Y_{i}(0)}{1-p_{i}}+\tfrac{Y_{i}(1)}{p_{i}}\right).

Now,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}{\left[\xi_{i}\,\middle|\,\mathcal{F}_{i-1}\right]}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\left(\frac{Y_{i}(0)}{1-p_{i}}+\frac{Y_{i}(1)}{p_{i}}\right)\mathbb{E}{[K_{i}-p_{i}|\mathcal{F}_{i-1}]}=0,

implying
\{\xi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
are terms of a martingale difference sequence, and that
\widehat{\tau}_{\textsc{IPW}}
is an unbiased estimator for
\bar{\tau}
. Next, we compute the total conditional variance:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}{\left[\xi_{i}^{2}\,\middle|\,\mathcal{F}_{i-1}\right]}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{N}\left(\frac{Y_{i}(0)}{1-p_{i}}+\frac{Y_{i}(1)}{p_{i}}\right)^{2}\mathbb{E}{\left[(K_{i}-p_{i})^{2}\,\middle|\,\mathcal{F}_{i-1}\right]}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{p_{i}}{1-p_{i}}Y_{i}(0)^{2}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1-p_{i}}{p_{i}}Y_{i}(1)^{2}+\frac{2}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}Y_{i}(0)Y_{i}(1).

In order to invoke the martingale central limit theorem
[
32
, Chapter 3]
, we need to ensure that the total conditional variance converges in probability to a constant and that the Lindeberg condition is satisfied.

For Strongly Stable Design:
Under the assumption of strong design stability (Definition
2
), we have
p_{i}\xrightarrow{p}p^{\star}.

Invoking the continuous mapping theorem in conjunction with Assumption
1
(a) yields
\tfrac{p_{i}}{1-p_{i}}\xrightarrow{p}\tfrac{p^{\star}}{1-p^{\star}}.

Therefore, Assumption
1
along with Lemma
9
implies

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{p_{i}}{1-p_{i}}Y_{i}(0)^{2}\xrightarrow{p}m^{2}_{0}\frac{p^{\star}}{1-p^{\star}}.

Similarly,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1-p_{i}}{p_{i}}Y_{i}(1)^{2}\xrightarrow{p}m^{2}_{1}\frac{1-p^{\star}}{p^{\star}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{2}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}Y_{i}(1)Y_{i}(0)\xrightarrow{}2m_{01}.

Overall we have,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[\xi_{i}^{2}\,\middle|\,\mathcal{F}_{i-1}\right]\xrightarrow{p}{m^{2}_{0}\frac{p^{\star}}{1-p^{\star}}+m^{2}_{1}\frac{1-p^{\star}}{p^{\star}}+2m_{01}}.

For Weakly Stable Design:
Under weak design stability (Definition
3
), it follows that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1-p_{i}}{p_{i}}\xrightarrow{p}\frac{1-p_{1}^{\star}}{p_{1}^{\star}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{p_{i}}{1-p_{i}}\xrightarrow{p}\frac{p_{2}^{\star}}{1-p_{2}^{\star}}.

Therefore, Assumption
1
and Lemma
10
give

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[\xi_{i}^{2}\,\middle|\,\mathcal{F}_{i-1}\right]\xrightarrow{p}{m^{2}_{0}\frac{p_{2}^{\star}}{1-p_{2}^{\star}}+m^{2}_{1}\frac{1-p_{1}^{\star}}{p_{1}^{\star}}+2m_{01}}.

Combining the two cases, the asymptotic variance of the IPW estimator is

\displaystyle\mathrm{V}^{\mathrm{IPW}}=\begin{cases}\mathrm{V}^{\text{IPW}}_{\text{strong}}=m^{2}_{0}\dfrac{p^{\star}}{1-p^{\star}}+m^{2}_{1}\dfrac{1-p^{\star}}{p^{\star}}+2m_{01}&\text{(strong design stability)},\\[11.99998pt]
\mathrm{V}^{\text{IPW}}_{\text{weak}}=m^{2}_{0}\dfrac{p_{2}^{\star}}{1-p_{2}^{\star}}+m^{2}_{1}\dfrac{1-p_{1}^{\star}}{p_{1}^{\star}}+2m_{01}&\text{(weak design stability)}.\end{cases}

(40)

Next, from Assumption
1
(a)–(b), the boundedness of
p_{i}
and
Y_{i}
ensures that

\displaystyle|\xi_{i}|=\left|\frac{K_{i}-p_{i}}{\sqrt{N}}\left(\frac{Y_{i}(0)}{1-p_{i}}+\frac{Y_{i}(1)}{p_{i}}\right)\right|=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}|K_{i}-p_{i}|\left|\frac{Y_{i}(0)}{1-p_{i}}+\frac{Y_{i}(1)}{p_{i}}\right|\leq\frac{4M}{\sqrt{N}\delta}.

Fix
\varepsilon>0.
For any
N>\left(\tfrac{4M}{\delta\varepsilon}\right)^{2},
we have
\mathbf{1}_{\{|\xi_{i}|>\varepsilon\}}=0
a.s. Consequently, for such
N,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}{\left[\xi_{i}^{2}\mathbf{1}_{\{|\xi_{i}|>\varepsilon\}}\,\middle|\,\mathcal{F}_{i-1}\right]}=0,

and therefore

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}{\left[\xi_{i}^{2}\mathbf{1}_{\{|\xi_{i}|>\varepsilon\}}\,\middle|\,\mathcal{F}_{i-1}\right]}=0,

which verifies the Lindeberg condition. Putting together the pieces and invoking martingale central limit theorem
[
32
, Chapter 3]
yields
\sqrt{N}\left(\widehat{\tau}_{\textsc{IPW}}-\bar{\tau}\right)\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}\left(0,\mathrm{V}^{\mathrm{IPW}}\right),

with asymptotic variance
\mathrm{V}^{\mathrm{IPW}}
as specified in (
40
).

7.2
Proof of Theorem
2

(Variance estimation of the IPW estimator under strong design stability)

First, we establish the the consistency of

and
\widehat{m}_{1}^{2}

\displaystyle\frac{N_{1}}{N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}K_{i}\xrightarrow{p}p^{\star}.

Under a strongly stable design, since

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(K_{i}-p_{i})\xrightarrow{p}0.

Since

(Assumption

(a)) and Chebyshev’s inequality, it follows that for any fixed

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(K_{i}-p_{i})\right|\geq\varepsilon\right)\leq\frac{1}{N^{2}\varepsilon^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\!\left[(K_{i}-p_{i})^{2}\right]=\frac{1}{N^{2}\varepsilon^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\!\left[p_{i}(1-p_{i})\right]\leq\frac{(1-\delta)^{2}}{N\varepsilon^{2}}\to 0,

implying claim (

Consistency of
\widehat{m}^{2}_{1}
&
\widehat{m}^{2}_{0}

We now establish the consistency of
\widehat{m}^{2}_{1}

To establish the consistency of
\widehat{m}_{1}^{2}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}K_{i}Y_{i}(1)^{2}\xrightarrow{p}p^{\star}m^{2}_{1}.

Note that

\displaystyle\frac{\max\{N_{1},1\}}{N}=\frac{N_{1}}{N}+\frac{1}{N}\,\mathbf{1}_{\{N_{1}=0\}},

where the second term converges to zero as

, and
\tfrac{N_{1}}{N}\xrightarrow{p}p^{\star}
by (

\displaystyle\frac{\max\{N_{1},1\}}{N}\xrightarrow{p}p^{\star}.

(44)

Therefore, once (

) holds, Slutsky’s theorem implies the consistency of
\widehat{m}_{1}^{2}
.

Note that
\tfrac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(K_{i}-p_{i})Y_{i}(1)^{2}
is sum of a martingale difference sequence.
By the boundedness of
p_{i}
and

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(K_{i}-p_{i})Y_{i}(1)^{2}\right|\geq\varepsilon\right)\leq\frac{1}{N^{2}\varepsilon^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[(K_{i}-p_{i})^{2}Y_{i}(1)^{4}\right]\leq\frac{M^{4}(1-\delta)^{2}}{N\varepsilon^{2}}\to 0,

By strong stability of the design (

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}K_{i}Y_{i}(1)^{2}\xrightarrow{p}p^{\star}m^{2}_{1},

proving our claim (

\widehat{m}^{2}_{1}

Bounding the cross-moment term:
By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}Y_{i}(0)Y_{i}(1)\right|\leq\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}Y_{i}(0)^{2}\right)^{1/2}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}Y_{i}(1)^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

\displaystyle|m_{01}|\leq m_{0}m_{1}.

7.4
Proof of Theorem

The proof of this theorem differs from that of Theorem

32
, Chapter 3]
. The crucial step then is to show that the difference between
\widehat{\tau}_{\textsc{AIPW}}
and
\widehat{\psi}_{\textsc{AIPW}}
is asymptotically negligible, in the sense that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{\tau}_{\textsc{AIPW}}-\widehat{\psi}_{\textsc{AIPW}}\right]^{2}}{\mathrm{Var}\left[\widehat{\psi}_{\textsc{AIPW}}\right]}\to 0\text{ as }N\to\infty.

(52)

This allows us to invoke Hájek’s Lemma (see Lemma
6
), which implies that the asymptotic distribution of
\widehat{\tau}_{\textsc{AIPW}}
matches that of
\widehat{\psi}_{\textsc{AIPW}}
, thereby establishing the central limit theorem for
\widehat{\tau}_{\textsc{AIPW}}
.

We start by observing that
\widehat{\psi}_{\textsc{AIPW}}
after proper centering and scaling can be written as a sum of martingale difference sequence.

\displaystyle\sqrt{N}\left(\widehat{\psi}_{\textsc{AIPW}}-\bar{\tau}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{K_{i}-p_{i}}{\sqrt{N}}\left(\frac{Y_{i}(0)-\overline{Y}_{i-1}(0)}{1-p_{i}}+\frac{Y_{i}(1)-\overline{Y}_{i-1}(1)}{p_{i}}\right)\;=\;\sum_{i=1}^{N}\zeta_{i},

where
\zeta_{i}=\tfrac{K_{i}-p_{i}}{\sqrt{N}}\left(\tfrac{Y_{i}(0)-\overline{Y}_{i-1}(0)}{1-p_{i}}+\tfrac{Y_{i}(1)-\overline{Y}_{i-1}(1)}{p_{i}}\right).

Now,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\zeta_{i}\,\middle|\,\mathcal{F}_{i-1}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\left(\frac{Y_{i}(0)-\overline{Y}_{i-1}(0)}{1-p_{i}}+\frac{Y_{i}(1)-\overline{Y}_{i-1}(1)}{p_{i}}\right)\mathbb{E}\left[K_{i}-p_{i}\,\middle|\,\mathcal{F}_{i-1}\right]=0,

implying
\{\zeta_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
are terms of a martingale difference sequence and that
\widehat{\psi}_{\textsc{AIPW}}
is an unbiased estimator for
\bar{\tau}
. The total conditional variance of
\{\zeta_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
is given by

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[\zeta_{i}^{2}\,\middle|\,\mathcal{F}_{i-1}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{N}\left(\frac{Y_{i}(0)-\overline{Y}_{i-1}(0)}{1-p_{i}}+\frac{Y_{i}(1)-\overline{Y}_{i-1}(1)}{p_{i}}\right)^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[(K_{i}-p_{i})^{2}\,\middle|\,\mathcal{F}_{i-1}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{p_{i}}{1-p_{i}}\left(Y_{i}(0)-\overline{Y}_{i-1}(0)\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1-p_{i}}{p_{i}}\left(Y_{i}(1)-\overline{Y}_{i-1}(1)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{2}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(Y_{i}(0)-\overline{Y}_{i-1}(0)\right)\left(Y_{i}(1)-\overline{Y}_{i-1}(1)\right).

Next, we verify that the total conditional variance converges in probability to a constant and that the Lindeberg condition holds.

For Strongly Stable Design:
Under strong design stability (Definition
2
) and Assumption
2
(a), the continuous mapping theorem implies that
\tfrac{p_{i}}{1-p_{i}}\xrightarrow{p}\tfrac{p^{\star}}{1-p^{\star}}.

Therefore, Lemma
3
, together with Lemma
9
and Assumption
2
(a)–(b), implies

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{p_{i}}{1-p_{i}}\left(Y_{i}(0)-\overline{Y}_{i-1}(0)\right)^{2}\xrightarrow{p}\sigma^{2}_{0}\frac{p^{\star}}{1-p^{\star}}.

Similar arguments yield,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1-p_{i}}{p_{i}}\left(Y_{i}(1)-\overline{Y}_{i-1}(1)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{p}\sigma^{2}_{1}\frac{1-p^{\star}}{p^{\star}},

\displaystyle\text{and}\qquad\frac{2}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(Y_{i}(0)-\overline{Y}_{i-1}(0)\right)\left(Y_{i}(1)-\overline{Y}_{i-1}(1)\right)

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{}2\sigma_{01}.

Overall,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[\zeta_{i}^{2}\,\middle|\,\mathcal{F}_{i-1}\right]\xrightarrow{p}\sigma^{2}_{0}\frac{p^{\star}}{1-p^{\star}}+\sigma^{2}_{1}\frac{1-p^{\star}}{p^{\star}}+2\sigma_{01}.

For Weakly Stable Design:
By arguments analogous to the weakly stable case (Definition
3
) in the proof of Theorem
1
, and under the additional assumption (
18
), it follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[\zeta_{i}^{2}\,\middle|\,\mathcal{F}_{i-1}\right]\xrightarrow{p}\sigma^{2}_{0}\frac{p_{2}^{\star}}{1-p_{2}^{\star}}+\sigma^{2}_{1}\frac{1-p_{1}^{\star}}{p_{1}^{\star}}+2\sigma_{01}.

Combining the two cases, the asymptotic variance of the AIPW estimator is

\displaystyle\mathrm{V}^{\mathrm{AIPW}}=\begin{cases}\mathrm{V}^{\text{AIPW}}_{\text{strong}}=\sigma^{2}_{0}\dfrac{p^{\star}}{1-p^{\star}}+\sigma^{2}_{1}\dfrac{1-p^{\star}}{p^{\star}}+2\sigma_{01}&\text{under strong design stability},\\[11.99998pt]
\mathrm{V}^{\text{AIPW}}_{\text{weak}}=\sigma^{2}_{0}\dfrac{p_{2}^{\star}}{1-p_{2}^{\star}}+\sigma^{2}_{1}\dfrac{1-p_{1}^{\star}}{p_{1}^{\star}}+2\sigma_{01}&\text{under weak design stability}.\end{cases}

(53)

Next, note that the boundedness of
p_{i}
(Assumption
2
(a)) and the uniform boundedness of
Y_{i}
(Assumption
2
(b)) imply that

\displaystyle|\zeta_{i}|=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}|K_{i}-p_{i}|\left|\frac{Y_{i}(0)-\overline{Y}_{i-1}(0)}{1-p_{i}}+\frac{Y_{i}(1)-\overline{Y}_{i-1}(1)}{p_{i}}\right|\leq\frac{8M}{\sqrt{N}\delta}.

Fix
\varepsilon>0.
For any
N>\left(\tfrac{8M}{\delta\varepsilon}\right)^{2},
we have
\mathbf{1}_{\{|\zeta_{i}|>\varepsilon\}}=0
a.s. Consequently, for such
N,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}{\left[\zeta_{i}^{2}\mathbf{1}_{\{|\zeta_{i}|>\varepsilon\}}\,\middle|\,\mathcal{F}_{i-1}\right]}=0,

and therefore

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}{\left[\zeta_{i}^{2}\mathbf{1}_{\{|\zeta_{i}|>\varepsilon\}}\,\middle|\,\mathcal{F}_{i-1}\right]}=0,

which verifies the Lindeberg condition. Putting together the pieces and applying the martingale central limit theorem
[
32
, Theorem 3]
yields

\displaystyle\sqrt{N}\left(\widehat{\psi}_{\textsc{AIPW}}-\bar{\tau}\right)\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}\left(0,\mathrm{V}^{\mathrm{AIPW}}\right).

(54)

It now remains to verify the condition (
52
).

Verifying condition (
52
):

Observe that
\widehat{\tau}_{\textsc{AIPW}}-\widehat{\psi}_{\textsc{AIPW}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Delta_{i},

where

\displaystyle\Delta_{i}=\frac{K_{i}-p_{i}}{N}\left(\frac{\widehat{Y}_{i-1}(1)-\overline{Y}_{i-1}(1)}{p_{i}}+\frac{\widehat{Y}_{i-1}(0)-\overline{Y}_{i-1}(0)}{1-p_{i}}\right).

It is easy to verify that
\{\Delta_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
is a martingale difference with respect to filtration
\{\mathcal{F}_{i-1}\}_{i\geq 1}
, and hence

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\widehat{\tau}_{\textsc{AIPW}}-\widehat{\psi}_{\textsc{AIPW}}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\big[\Delta_{i}^{2}\big].

(55)

Using boundedness of
p_{i}
(Assumption
2
(a)),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta_{i}^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta_{i}^{2}\,\middle|\,\mathcal{F}_{i-1}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\mathbb{E}\!\left[p_{i}(1-p_{i})\left(\frac{\widehat{Y}_{i-1}(1)-\overline{Y}_{i-1}(1)}{p_{i}}+\frac{\widehat{Y}_{i-1}(0)-\overline{Y}_{i-1}(0)}{1-p_{i}}\right)^{\!2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(1-\delta)^{2}}{N^{2}}\mathbb{E}\!\left[A_{i}^{2}+2A_{i}B_{i}+B_{i}^{2}\right],

where

\displaystyle A_{i}=\frac{\widehat{Y}_{i-1}(1)-\overline{Y}_{i-1}(1)}{p_{i}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad B_{i}=\frac{\widehat{Y}_{i-1}(0)-\overline{Y}_{i-1}(0)}{1-p_{i}}.

Since
\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{Y_{j}(1)(K_{j}-p_{j})}{p_{j}}\big|\mathcal{F}_{j-1}\right]=0,A_{i}
can be expressed as sum of a martingale difference sequence. Hence, by Assumption
2
(a)-(b),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[A_{i}^{2}\right]=\frac{1}{(i-1)^{2}}\sum_{j<i}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{Y_{j}(1)^{2}(K_{j}-p_{j})^{2}}{p_{i}^{2}p_{j}^{2}}\right]\leq\frac{M^{2}(1-\delta)^{2}}{(i-1)\delta^{4}}.

(56)

Similarly,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[B_{i}^{2}\right]\leq\frac{M^{2}(1-\delta)^{2}}{(i-1)\delta^{4}},\quad\text{and}\quad\left|\mathbb{E}\left[A_{i}B_{i}\right]\right|\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[A_{i}^{2}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[B_{i}^{2}\right]}\leq\frac{M^{2}(1-\delta)^{2}}{(i-1)\delta^{4}}.

(57)

Combining (
55
)-(
57
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\widehat{\tau}_{\textsc{AIPW}}-\widehat{\psi}_{\textsc{AIPW}}\right)^{2}\right]\leq\frac{(1-\delta)^{2}}{N^{2}}\sum_{i<N}\frac{4M^{2}(1-\delta)^{2}}{(i-1)\delta^{4}}=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{\log N}{N^{2}}\right).

(58)

Assumption
2
(a)-(b) ensures that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\left[\widehat{\psi}_{\textsc{AIPW}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\{\frac{p_{i}}{1-p_{i}}\left(Y_{i}(0)-\overline{Y}_{i-1}(0)\right)^{2}+\frac{1-p_{i}}{p_{i}}\left(Y_{i}(1)-\overline{Y}_{i-1}(1)\right)^{2}\right.\right.

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\left.\left.+\,2\left(Y_{i}(0)-\overline{Y}_{i-1}(0)\right)\left(Y_{i}(1)-\overline{Y}_{i-1}(1)\right)\right\}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\frac{4M^{2}(1-\delta)}{\delta}+\frac{4M^{2}\delta}{1-\delta}+8M^{2}\right)=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{1}{N}\right).

(59)

Combining the bounds in (
58
) and (
59
), we conclude that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\widehat{\tau}_{\textsc{AIPW}}-\widehat{\psi}_{\textsc{AIPW}}\right)^{2}\right]}{\mathrm{Var}\left[\widehat{\psi}_{\textsc{AIPW}}\right]}=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{\log N}{N}\right)\to 0\quad\text{as }N\to\infty,

which completes the proof of (
52
). Hence, it follows that
\sqrt{N}\left(\widehat{\tau}_{\textsc{AIPW}}-\bar{\tau}\right)\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}\left(0,\mathrm{V}^{\mathrm{AIPW}}\right),

with asymptotic variance
\mathrm{V}^{\mathrm{AIPW}}
specified in (
53
).

7.5
Proof of Theorem
5

(Variance estimation of the AIPW estimator under strong design stability)

We begin by considering the case in which
p^{\star}
is known.

Consistency of
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{1}
&
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{0}
:
Recalling from (
27
),

\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\max\{N_{1},1\}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}K_{i}\left(Y_{i}(1)-\widehat{Y}_{i-1}(1)\right)^{2},

where
N_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}K_{i}.
Set
\widehat{Y}_{1}(1)=0
and for
i\geq 2
,

\displaystyle\widehat{Y}_{i-1}(1)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{i-1}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\frac{K_{j}Y_{j}(1)}{p_{j}}.

Under strong design stability (Definition
2
), Lemma
5
and Assumption
2
(a)–(b), together with Lemma
9
, imply

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}\left(Y_{i}(1)-\widehat{Y}_{i-1}(1)\right)^{2}\xrightarrow{p}p^{\star}\sigma^{2}_{1}.

Since we have already established in (
44
) that, under strong design stability,
\tfrac{\max\{N_{1},1\}}{N}\xrightarrow{p}p^{\star}
, Slutsky’s theorem implies

\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{1}\xrightarrow{p}\sigma^{2}_{1}.

The proof for
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{0}
is analogous.

Bounding the covariance term:
By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(Y_{i}(0)-\overline{Y}_{i-1}(0)\right)\left(Y_{i}(1)-\overline{Y}_{i-1}(1)\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(Y_{i}(0)-\overline{Y}_{i-1}(0)\right)^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\quad\times\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(Y_{i}(1)-\overline{Y}_{i-1}(1)\right)^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

(60)

Taking limits as
N\to\infty
yields
|\sigma_{01}|\leq\sigma_{0}\sigma_{1}
, and hence

\displaystyle\mathrm{V}^{\text{AIPW}}_{\text{strong}}=\sigma^{2}_{0}\frac{p^{\star}}{1-p^{\star}}+\sigma^{2}_{1}\frac{1-p^{\star}}{p^{\star}}+2\sigma_{01}\leq\left(\sigma_{0}\sqrt{\frac{p^{\star}}{1-p^{\star}}}+\sigma_{1}\sqrt{\frac{1-p^{\star}}{p^{\star}}}\right)^{2}.

(61)

Using the consistency results
\widehat{\sigma}_{0}^{2}\xrightarrow{p}\sigma^{2}_{0}
,
and
\widehat{\sigma}_{1}^{2}\xrightarrow{p}\sigma^{2}_{1}
,
together with nonnegativity of
\widehat{\sigma}_{0}
and
\widehat{\sigma}_{1}

and the continuous mapping theorem,
it follows that
\widehat{\sigma}_{j}\xrightarrow{p}\sigma_{j}
for
j\in\{0,1\}
.
Consequently,
\widehat{V^{\text{AIPW}}_{\text{strong}}}
, as defined in (
26
),
consistently estimates

\left(\sigma_{0}\sqrt{\frac{p^{\star}}{1-p^{\star}}}+\sigma_{1}\sqrt{\frac{1-p^{\star}}{p^{\star}}}\right)^{\!2}
. Therefore,
\widehat{\mathrm{V}^{\text{AIPW}}_{\text{strong}}}
estimates
\mathrm{V}^{\text{AIPW}}_{\text{strong}}
conservatively.

For
\widehat{\mathrm{V}^{\text{AIPW}}_{\text{strong}}}
to be consistent for
\mathrm{V}^{\text{AIPW}}_{\text{strong}}
, equality must hold in inequality (
61
).
This corresponds to the equality case of the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality in (
60
), which implies that

\displaystyle\frac{Y_{i}(1)-\overline{Y}_{i-1}(1)}{Y_{i}(0)-\overline{Y}_{i-1}(0)}=c\quad\text{for all }i,

for some constant
c\in\mathbb{R}
. Consequently, the potential outcomes satisfy generalized treatment effect homogeneity, i.e., equation (
3
).
This completes the proof of the theorem.

If
p^{\star}
is unknown or difficult to compute, the consistent estimator
\widehat{p}^{\star}
defined in (
16
) may be used; see Remark
6
for further details.

8.1
Proof of Lemma
1

Under Wei’s adaptive coin design
[
2
]
, recall from (
32
) that the
i
th unit is assigned to treatment with probability

\displaystyle p_{i}=f\!\left(R_{i-1}\right),

where
R_{i-1}=\tfrac{D_{i-1}}{i-1}
denotes the normalized treatment–control imbalance after
(i-1)
assignments, and
f:[-1,1]\to[0,1]
is a non-increasing function satisfying
f(0)=\tfrac{1}{2}
and continuous at zero. To ensure that the variance estimators
\widehat{\tau}_{\textsc{IPW}}
and
\widehat{\tau}_{\textsc{AIPW}}
are well defined, it is necessary that the assignment probabilities be bounded away from
0
and
1
.
If
f
does not automatically satisfy this condition, we consider its truncated version

\displaystyle p_{i}=\min\!\left\{\max\!\left\{f(R_{i-1}),\,\delta\right\},\,1-\delta\right\},\qquad\delta\in(0,\tfrac{1}{2}],

(64)

which guarantees
p_{i}\in[\delta,\,1-\delta]
for all
i
. By Theorem 1 of
[
2
]
, the assignment probabilities in (
32
) satisfy

\displaystyle p_{i}\xrightarrow{p}\tfrac{1}{2}.

Since the truncation in (
64
) is a continuous transformation, the continuous mapping theorem implies that the truncated inclusion probabilities also converge in probability to
\tfrac{1}{2}
.
Therefore, Wei’s adaptive coin design satisfies strong design stability with limiting inclusion probability
p^{\star}=\tfrac{1}{2}
.

8.2
Proof of Lemma
2

We begin by showing that Efron’s biased coin design
[
3
]
satisfies weak stability.
Suppose a total of
k
units have been assigned to treatment or control.
Let
m_{k}
and
n_{k}
denote, respectively, the number of units assigned to the treatment and control groups, so that
m_{k}+n_{k}=k
.
The corresponding treatment–control imbalance after
k
assignments is given by
D_{k}=m_{k}-n_{k}
.
Under Efron’s biased coin design (
\eta
) the probability of assigning the
(k+1)
th unit to treatment, denoted by
p_{k+1}
is given by

\displaystyle p_{k+1}=\begin{cases}\eta&\text{if }D_{k}<0,\\
\frac{1}{2}&\text{if }D_{k}=0,\\
1-\eta&\text{if }D_{k}>0.\end{cases}

Observe that
\{D_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
is a Markov chain and the state space is
\mathbb{Z}.
Since we can always move from
D_{k}=a
to
D_{k+1}=(a-1)\text{ or }D_{k+1}=(a+1)
in a step, the Markov chain is irreducible. We begin by recalling Foster’s Theorem
[
39
]
, which provides a condition for positive recurrence in Markov chains with a countable state space.

Theorem 7

(
[
39
]
)
.

Consider an irreducible discrete-time Markov chain on a countable state space
S
,
with transition probability matrix
P=(p_{i,j})_{i,j\in S}
,
where
p_{i,j}
denotes the probability of transitioning from state
i
to state
j
. The Markov chain is positive recurrent if and only if there exists a Lyapunov function
V:S\to\mathbb{R}
, such that
V(i)\geq 0
for all
i\in S
, and

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in S}p_{i,j}V(j)

\displaystyle<\infty\quad\text{for }i\in F,

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in S}p_{i,j}V(j)

\displaystyle\leq V(i)-\varepsilon\quad\text{for all }i\notin F,

for some finite set
F\subset S
and strictly positive constant
\varepsilon>0
.

We will now show that
\{D_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
is positive recurrent using the above theorem. Consider the Lyapunov function
V(s)=|s|\text{ for }s\in S=\mathbb{Z}
, which is non-negative for all
s\in S,
and take
F=\{0\}.
For
i\in F\text{ i.e. }i=0,

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in S}p_{0,j}V(j)=p_{0,-1}V(-1)+p_{0,1}V(1)=p_{0,-1}+p_{0,1}=1<\infty.

Now we will consider the case
i\notin F\text{ i.e. }i\neq 0.
If
i>0,

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in S}p_{i,j}V(j)-V(i)=p_{i,{i+1}}V(i+1)+p_{i,{i-1}}V(i-1)-V(i)

\displaystyle=(1-\eta)(i+1)+\eta(i-1)-i=1-2\eta<0.

If
i<0,

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in S}p_{i,j}V(j)-V(i)=p_{i,{i+1}}V(i+1)+p_{i,{i-1}}V(i-1)-V(i)

\displaystyle=\eta(-i-1)+(1-\eta)(-i+1)-(-i)=1-2\eta<0.

Taking
\varepsilon=2\eta-1>0
and noting that
{\{D_{k}\}}_{k\geq 1}
is an irreducible discrete time Markov chain on the countable state space
\mathbb{Z}
, we conclude that the conditions of Foster’s Theorem
7
are satisfied. Therefore,
{\{D_{k}\}}_{k\geq 1}
is positive recurrent. Since
{\{D_{k}\}}_{k\geq 1}
is irreducible, positive recurrent discrete-time Markov chain, it has unique stationary distribution
\boldsymbol{\pi}
, which we have computed in Section
10
. By the mean ergodic theorem,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{p_{i}}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{a.s.}\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\!\left[\frac{1}{p_{i}}\right]

\displaystyle=2\pi(0)+\sum_{d>0}\frac{\pi(d)}{1-\eta}+\sum_{d<0}\frac{\pi(d)}{\eta}

\displaystyle=2\pi(0)+\frac{1-\pi(0)}{2}\left(\frac{1}{1-\eta}+\frac{1}{\eta}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1-4\eta+12\eta^{2}-8\eta^{3}}{4\eta^{2}(1-\eta)}.

A symmetric calculation for
\tfrac{1}{1-p_{i}}
gives,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{1-p_{i}}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{a.s.}\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\!\left[\frac{1}{1-p_{i}}\right]

\displaystyle=2\pi(0)+\sum_{d>0}\frac{\pi(d)}{\eta}+\sum_{d<0}\frac{\pi(d)}{1-\eta}

\displaystyle=2\pi(0)+\frac{1-\pi(0)}{2}\left(\frac{1}{1-\eta}+\frac{1}{\eta}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1-4\eta+12\eta^{2}-8\eta^{3}}{4\eta^{2}(1-\eta)},

implying Efron’s design is weakly stable with
p_{1}^{\star}=\tfrac{4\eta^{2}(1-\eta)}{1-4\eta+12\eta^{2}-8\eta^{3}}
and
p_{2}^{\star}=\tfrac{1-4\eta+8\eta^{2}-4\eta^{3}}{1-4\eta+12\eta^{2}-8\eta^{3}}.

Moreover, mean ergodic theorem also gives,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{a.s.}\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(p_{i}),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}[p_{i}]

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi(0)}{2}+\sum_{d>0}\pi(d)(1-\eta)+\sum_{d<0}\pi(d)\eta

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi(0)}{2}+\sum_{d>0}\pi(d)

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi(0)}{2}+\frac{1-\pi(0)}{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2},

implying Efron’s design satisfies the extra restriction (
18
).

Lemma 3
.

Under Assumption
2
(b)-(c), as

,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(Y_{i}(1)-\overline{Y}_{i-1}(1)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(Y_{i}(1)-\widebar{Y}_{N}(1)\right)^{2}}_{A_{N}}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\widebar{Y}_{N}(1)-\overline{Y}_{i-1}(1)\right)^{2}}_{B_{N}}

\displaystyle\quad+\underbrace{\frac{2}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(Y_{i}(1)-\widebar{Y}_{N}(1)\right)\left(\widebar{Y}_{N}(1)-\overline{Y}_{i-1}(1)\right)}_{C_{N}}.

2
(c),

.

B_{N}\to 0
. Fix
\varepsilon>0
.
By Assumption
2
(c),
\widebar{Y}_{N}(1)\to\widebar{Y}_{1}
, so there exists

Y_{i}(1)

\displaystyle B_{N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\left(\widebar{Y}_{N}(1)-\overline{Y}_{i-1}(1)\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=K+1}^{N}\left(\widebar{Y}_{N}(1)-\overline{Y}_{i-1}(1)\right)^{2}.

Lemma 5
.

Under Assumption
2
(b)-(c), as
N\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(Y_{i}(1)-\widehat{Y}_{i-1}(1)\right)^{2}\to\sigma^{2}_{1}\quad\text{ and }\quad\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(Y_{i}(0)-\widehat{Y}_{i-1}(0)\right)^{2}\to\sigma^{2}_{0}.

Proof.

We first establish the result for the first part; the proof of the second part follows analogously. We first show that

\displaystyle\widehat{Y}_{i-1}(1)-\overline{Y}_{i-1}(1)\xrightarrow{p}0.

(65)

Note that,

\displaystyle\widehat{Y}_{i-1}(1)-\overline{Y}_{i-1}(1)=\frac{1}{i-1}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\frac{(K_{j}-p_{j})Y_{j}(1)}{p_{j}}.

Since
\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{(K_{j}-p_{j})Y_{j}(1)}{p_{j}}\,\middle|\,\mathcal{F}_{j-1}\right]=0
, the summands form a martingale difference sequence. Under Assumption
2
(a)–(b),

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\frac{1}{(i-1)^{2}}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\!\left(\frac{(K_{j}-p_{j})Y_{j}(1)}{p_{j}}\right)^{\!2}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\frac{1}{(i-1)^{2}}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{Y_{j}(1)^{2}p_{j}(1-p_{j})}{p_{j}^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{M^{2}\delta}{(i-1)(1-\delta)}.

Hence, by Chebyshev’s inequality, claim (
65
) follows.

Next, consider

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\!\left[\!\left(Y_{i}(1)-\overline{Y}_{i-1}(1)\right)^{2}-\left(Y_{i}(1)-\widehat{Y}_{i-1}(1)\right)^{2}\!\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\overline{Y}_{i-1}(1)-\widehat{Y}_{i-1}(1)\right)\left(\overline{Y}_{i-1}(1)+\widehat{Y}_{i-1}(1)-2Y_{i}(1)\right).

By boundedness of
Y_{i}(1)
and
p_{i}
from Assumption
2
(a)–(b),

\displaystyle\bigl|\overline{Y}_{i-1}(1)+\widehat{Y}_{i-1}(1)-2Y_{i}(1)\bigr|\leq 3M+\frac{M}{\delta}.

Hence, for any
\varepsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left[\!\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(Y_{i}(1)-\overline{Y}_{i-1}(1)\right)^{2}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(Y_{i}(1)-\widehat{Y}_{i-1}(1)\right)^{2}\right|\geq\varepsilon\!\right]\leq\mathbb{P}\!\left[\!\frac{C}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left|\overline{Y}_{i-1}(1)-\widehat{Y}_{i-1}(1)\right|\geq\varepsilon\!\right],

(66)

where
C=3M+\tfrac{M}{\delta}
.
By Lemma
8
and (
65
),
the upper bound in (
66
) converges to zero as

, giving

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(Y_{i}(1)-\overline{Y}_{i-1}(1)\right)^{2}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(Y_{i}(1)-\widehat{Y}_{i-1}(1)\right)^{2}\xrightarrow{p}0.

(67)

Lemma
3
and (
67
) then imply

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(Y_{i}(1)-\widehat{Y}_{i-1}(1)\right)^{2}\xrightarrow{p}\sigma^{2}_{1}.

(68)

∎

Lemma 6

(Hájek’s Lemma)
.

Let
\{S_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
and
\{T_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
be sequences of random variables, and let
L
be a random variable.
If

\displaystyle\frac{T_{n}-\mathbb{E}(T_{n})}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(T_{n})}}\;\xrightarrow{d}\;L\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{\mathbb{E}\!\left[(T_{n}-S_{n})^{2}\right]}{\mathrm{Var}(T_{n})}\;\to\;0,

(69)

then

\displaystyle\frac{S_{n}-\mathbb{E}(S_{n})}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(S_{n})}}\;\xrightarrow{d}\;L.

Proof.

We first compare the standardized versions of
T_{n}
and
S_{n}
under the variance of
T_{n}
. Observe that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left(\frac{T_{n}-\mathbb{E}(T_{n})}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(T_{n})}}-\frac{S_{n}-\mathbb{E}(S_{n})}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(T_{n})}}\right)^{\!2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}\!\left[\,(T_{n}-S_{n})-\mathbb{E}(T_{n}-S_{n})\,\right]^{\!2}}{\mathrm{Var}(T_{n})}\;\leq\;\frac{\mathbb{E}\!\left[(T_{n}-S_{n})^{2}\right]}{\mathrm{Var}(T_{n})}\;\to\;0.

Hence,

\displaystyle\frac{T_{n}-\mathbb{E}(T_{n})}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(T_{n})}}-\frac{S_{n}-\mathbb{E}(S_{n})}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(T_{n})}}\xrightarrow{p}0,\quad\text{and thus}\quad\frac{S_{n}-\mathbb{E}(S_{n})}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(T_{n})}}\xrightarrow{d}L.

(70)

To replace
\mathrm{Var}(T_{n})
by
\mathrm{Var}(S_{n})
in the denominator, note that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}[(T_{n}-S_{n})^{2}]}{\mathrm{Var}(T_{n})}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\mathrm{Var}(T_{n}-S_{n})}{\mathrm{Var}(T_{n})}=\frac{\mathrm{Var}(T_{n})+\mathrm{Var}(S_{n})-2\mathrm{Cov}(T_{n},S_{n})}{\mathrm{Var}(T_{n})}.

By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,
\mathrm{Cov}(T_{n},S_{n})\leq\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(T_{n})\mathrm{Var}(S_{n})}
, so

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}[(T_{n}-S_{n})^{2}]}{\mathrm{Var}(T_{n})}

\displaystyle\geq\left(1-\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{Var}(S_{n})}{\mathrm{Var}(T_{n})}}\right)^{\!2}.

Since the left-hand side tends to zero by the condition (
69
), it follows that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{Var}(S_{n})}{\mathrm{Var}(T_{n})}\to 1.

(71)

Combining (
70
) and (
71
) with Slutsky’s theorem yields

\frac{S_{n}-\mathbb{E}(S_{n})}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(S_{n})}}\xrightarrow{d}L,

as required.
∎

Proofs of Auxiliary Lemmas

Lemma 7
.

If a sequence
\{x_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
of bounded reals has exactly one limit point
\ell,
then

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}x_{n}=\ell.

Proof.

We argue by contradiction. Suppose
\{x_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
does not converge to
\ell
. Then there exists
\varepsilon>0
and a subsequence
\{x_{n_{k}}\}_{k\geq 1}
such that

\displaystyle|x_{n_{k}}-\ell|>\varepsilon\quad\text{for all }k\in\mathbb{N}.

Since
\{x_{n_{k}}\}_{k\geq 1}
is bounded, the Bolzano–Weierstrass theorem ensures the existence of a further subsequence
\{x_{n_{k_{j}}}\}_{j\geq 1}
converging to some
\ell^{\prime}
, implying
\ell^{\prime}
is a limit point of
\{x_{n}\}
. However, since
|x_{n_{k}}-\ell|>\varepsilon
for all
k
, we must have

\displaystyle|\ell^{\prime}-\ell|\geq\varepsilon>0,

and hence
\ell^{\prime}\neq\ell
. This contradicts the assumption that
\ell
is the unique limit point of
\{x_{n}\}
. Consequently, we conclude that
x_{n}\to\ell
as
n\to\infty
.
∎

Lemma 8
.

Let
\{a_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
be a sequence of bounded random variables with
a_{i}\xrightarrow{p}a^{*}
, then the Cesàro mean converges in probability to the same limit i.e.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\xrightarrow{p}a^{*}.

Proof.

By Markov’s inequality, for any
\varepsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left(\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}-a^{*}\right|>\varepsilon\right)\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}-a^{*}\right|\right]\leq\frac{1}{n\varepsilon}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\!\left[|a_{i}-a^{*}|\right].

(72)

Since
a_{i}\xrightarrow{p}a^{*}
, we have
|a_{i}-a^{*}|\xrightarrow{p}0
. Moreover, boundedness of
\{a_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
implies the existence of an integrable random variable
X
such that
|a_{i}|\leq X
for all
i
. Hence,
|a_{i}-a^{*}|\leq 2X
and
\mathbb{E}[|a_{i}-a^{*}|]<\infty
for every
i
. Now, from
a_{i}\xrightarrow{p}a^{*}
we may extract a subsequence
a_{i_{j}}\xrightarrow{a.s.}a^{*}
. Dominated convergence theorem then yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|a_{i_{j}}-a^{*}|]\to 0.

Thus
0
is the only possible subsequential limit of
\{\mathbb{E}[|a_{i}-a^{*}|]\}_{i\geq 1}
, hence Lemma
7
implies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|a_{i}-a^{*}|]\to 0\quad\text{as }i\to\infty.

By the Cesàro mean theorem,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[|a_{i}-a^{*}|]\to 0.

(73)

Combining (
72
) with (
73
) gives, as
n\to\infty,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left(\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}-a^{*}\right|>\varepsilon\right)\to 0,

and hence the result follows.
∎

Lemma 9
.

Let
\{a_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
be a sequence of bounded random variables with
a_{i}\xrightarrow{p}a^{*}
,
and let
\{b_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
be a sequence of bounded real numbers with

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}\to b^{*}
as
n\to\infty
.
Then the cross-average satisfies

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}b_{i}\xrightarrow{p}a^{*}b^{*}.

Proof.

Decompose

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}b_{i}=\underbrace{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(a_{i}-a^{*})\,b_{i}}_{\mathrm{I}}+\underbrace{\frac{a^{*}}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}}_{\mathrm{II}}.

Since
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}\to b^{*}
, Slutsky’s theorem implies
\mathrm{II}\xrightarrow{p}a^{*}b^{*}
.
Thus, it suffices to show that
\mathrm{I}\xrightarrow{p}0
.
Since
\{b_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
is bounded, there exists
L>0
with
|b_{i}|\leq L
for all
i
. Fix
\varepsilon>0
and choose
\delta<\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2L}
. Decompose
\mathrm{I}
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathrm{I}=\underbrace{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(a_{i}-a^{*})\,b_{i}\,\mathbf{1}_{\{|a_{i}-a^{*}|>\delta\}}}_{\mathrm{A}}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(a_{i}-a^{*})\,b_{i}\,\mathbf{1}_{\{|a_{i}-a^{*}|\leq\delta\}}}_{\mathrm{B}}.

For
\mathrm{A}
, boundedness of
b_{i}
implies

\displaystyle|\mathrm{A}|\leq\frac{L}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|a_{i}-a^{*}|.

Hence, by Markov’s inequality and result (
73
), we have, as
n\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|\mathrm{A}|>\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)\leq\frac{2L}{n\varepsilon}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}|a_{i}-a^{*}|\to 0.

For
\mathrm{B}
, we have
|\mathrm{B}|\leq L\delta<\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}
,
so that
\mathbb{P}\big(|\mathrm{B}|>\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}\big)=0
.
Hence,
\mathbb{P}(|\mathrm{I}|>\varepsilon)\to 0
, i.e.,
\mathrm{I}\xrightarrow{p}0
.
Combining this with the limit of
\mathrm{II}
gives

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}b_{i}\xrightarrow{p}a^{*}b^{*},

as desired.
∎

Lemma 10
.

Let
\{a_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
be a sequence of bounded random variables with

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\xrightarrow{p}a^{*}
, and let
\{b_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
be a sequence of bounded real numbers with

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}\to b^{*}
as
n\to\infty
.
Then the cross-average satisfies

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}b_{i}\xrightarrow{p}a^{*}b^{*}.

Proof.

Following the approach in the proof of Lemma
9
, write

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}b_{i}=\underbrace{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(a_{i}-a^{*})b_{i}}_{\mathrm{I}}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a^{*}b_{i}}_{\mathrm{II}}.

By Slutsky’s theorem,

\mathrm{II}=\frac{a^{*}}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}\xrightarrow{p}a^{*}b^{*}.

It remains to show that
\mathrm{I}\xrightarrow{p}0
. Since
\{b_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
is bounded, say
|b_{i}|\leq L
, we have

|\mathrm{I}|\leq L\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(a_{i}-a^{*})\right|,

and therefore, for any
\varepsilon>0
,

\mathbb{P}(|\mathrm{I}|>\varepsilon)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(a_{i}-a^{*})\right|>\frac{\varepsilon}{L}\right).

Noting that
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\xrightarrow{p}a^{*}
, the right-hand side converges to
0
as
n\to\infty
, establishing
\mathrm{I}\xrightarrow{p}0
.
Hence, the claim follows.
∎

10
Stationary distribution of
\{S_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}

Let
P
be the transition matrix for the discrete-time Markov chain
\{D_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
and
\boldsymbol{\pi}
be the corresponding stationary distribution. The
(i,j)
th entry of
P
,
p_{i,j}
denotes the probability of transitioning from state
i
to state
j
in a single step. Due to the way the setup is defined,
p_{ij}=0
for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
except
j=i-1
or
j=i+1
. The balance equations from
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\text{T}}P=\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\text{T}}
are as follows

\displaystyle\pi(n)=\begin{cases}\eta\pi(n-1)+(1-\eta)\pi(n+1)&\text{if }n\leq-2,\\
\eta\pi(-2)+\frac{1}{2}\pi(0)&\text{if }n=-1,\\
\eta\pi(-1)+\eta\pi(1)&\text{if }n=0,\\
\frac{1}{2}\pi(0)+\eta\pi(2)&\text{if }n=1,\\
(1-\eta)\pi(n-1)+\eta\pi(n+1)&\text{if }n\geq 2.\end{cases}

Solving the above set of equations give

\displaystyle\pi(0)=\frac{2\eta-1}{2\eta},\quad\text{and}\quad\pi(-n)=\pi(n)=\frac{2\eta-1}{4\eta(1-\eta)}\left(\frac{1-\eta}{\eta}\right)^{n}\text{ for }n\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}.
