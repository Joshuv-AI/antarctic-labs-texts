Deep learning enable precision authentication of seasonal and processing signatures in tieguanyin tea
Authors: Chao Zheng, Xiaohe Zhou, Ningning Shao, Jiayi Cheng, Wei Xin, Ying Liu, Junling Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Authenticating specialty tea products remains a critical challenge in premium food markets, yet current analytical approaches are constrained by limited reproducibility and susceptibility to instrumental variation. Here, we present a deep learning framework that transforms liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS) metabolomic data into image representations, enabling robust authentication of tea products under real-world analytical conditions. Profiling 274 Tieguanyin tea samples across seasonal harvests (spring and autumn) and processing methods (light-scented and strong-scented), our approach achieved 90.9% (95% confidence interval [CI]: 80.4%–96.0%) classification accuracy—substantially outperforming conventional multivariate and machine learning methods (sPLS-DA: 85.5%; random forest: 87.3%). Critically, when subjected to chromatographic drift—a pervasive source of analytical irreproducibility—our model maintained 78.2% accuracy while traditional methods degraded to 69.1%. This framework addresses fundamental limitations in untargeted metabolomics, offering a generalizable solution for food authentication that extends beyond tea to broader applications in agricultural product verification and systems biology.

Introduction

Tea (Camellia sinensis), one of the most widely consumed beverages worldwide, poses persistent challenges for flavor authentication in global food systems due to its pronounced chemical complexity and strong geographical dependence. Among oolong teas, Tieguanyin is particularly difficult to authenticate, as it is produced through diverse cultivars and processing practices and widely distributed in international markets. Originating from Anxi County, Fujian Province, Tieguanyin represents one of the most economically and culturally significant oolong teas in China, accounting for a substantial proportion of national oolong tea production. Reliable authentication of Tieguanyin is therefore essential not only for safeguarding product quality and consumer trust but also for maintaining the integrity of regional brands and tea trade systems.

The chemical complexity underlying Tieguanyin’s distinctive flavor profile emerges from dynamic interactions between seasonal variation and post-harvest processing protocols1–3. Spring and autumn harvests develop distinct metabolite signatures under contrasting climatic conditions, with spring teas traditionally prized for delicate taste profiles and autumn harvests valued for pronounced aromatic intensity1. Post-harvest processing further transforms these seasonal signatures through two commercially dominant routes: light-scented (Qingxiang) and strong-scented (Nongxiang) production. Light-scented processing employs minimal oxidation (10–25%) coupled with gentle roasting at 105–115 °C, preserving metabolite diversity and maintaining elevated polyphenol content and free amino acids2. In contrast, strong-scented processing intensifies oxidation (40–70%) and applies high-temperature roasting (>130 °C), catalyzing extensive chemical transformations2,3. Gallated catechins, quercetin glycosides, and theanine decrease substantially, while theaflavins, vitexin derivatives, and kaempferol compounds accumulate. Critically, high-temperature conditions promote Maillard reactions between amino acids and reducing sugars, generating furfural derivatives and driving the formation of polymeric theabrownins4. These thermal transformations fundamentally alter metabolomic profiles, reducing astringency through polyphenol polymerization while enhancing flavor complexity2–4. Despite advances in understanding metabolite variation across seasons or processing methods individually, critical knowledge gaps persist1,5,6. The synergistic effects of seasonal timing and processing intensity on metabolite profiles remain unexplored, while most studies rely on limited sample sizes and single-source materials that may not represent market diversity. Moreover, the absence of standardized processing protocols compounds authentication challenges. Current sensory evaluation methods, though industry standard, suffer from inherent subjectivity and limited reproducibility.

Existing authentication approaches, including stable isotope analysis and multi-element profiling, while effective, require extensive sample preparation and specialized instrumentation7. Liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) based untargeted metabolomics offers comprehensive molecular coverage with high analytical throughput. However, metabolite annotation remains challenging, with typically < 10% of detected features successfully identified, resulting in substantial information loss8. Additionally, analytical variations including retention time (RT) drift and signal intensity fluctuations compromise reproducibility and limit practical deployment8.

Machine learning (ML) has emerged as a transformative approach for pattern recognition in complex, high-dimensional datasets, with rapidly expanding applications in food authentication, origin verification, and quality control7,9–11. While metabolomics has been extensively applied in tea research, ML integration remains nascent, with existing studies typically constrained by small sample sizes that limit classifier performance and generalizability12,13. Deep learning (DL) architectures, particularly convolutional neural networks (CNNs), offer enhanced feature extraction capabilities through hierarchical data representation14,15. Unlike traditional ML approaches, DL models can jointly learn intrinsic regularities and feature representations through end-to-end optimization, achieving superior pattern recognition in complex classification tasks15. Recent advances, including deep-learning-based Pseudo-Mass Spectrometry Imaging (deepPseudoMSI), demonstrate the potential of converting LC-MS data into image formats for enhanced analytical performance16.

Here, we present an integrated framework combining untargeted metabolomics with advanced ML for robust Tieguanyin authentication. We analyzed 274 samples representing four distinct variants—spring and autumn harvests processed via light- or strong-scented methods—to comprehensively map the metabolomic landscape shaped by seasonal and processing factors. Through systematic comparison of chemometric approaches (sPLS-DA), ensemble learning (random forest), and DL architectures, we established optimal authentication strategies addressing current methodological limitations. By implementing chemically informed data augmentation and demonstrating robustness to analytical drift, we overcome key barriers in metabolomic workflows. Our results reveal that processing intensity fundamentally modulates seasonal metabolite signatures, with light-scented methods preserving chemical diversity while strong-scented processing drives convergent thermal transformations. This work establishes a scalable authentication framework with implications extending beyond tea to diverse agricultural products requiring molecular fingerprinting.

Results

Metabolomic profiling reveals orthogonal separation by season and processing

Metabolomic profiling of 274 Tieguanyin samples revealed orthogonally organized chemical landscapes. Principal component analysis separated samples along two independent axes: PC1 (25.3% variance) distinguished processing methods (light-scented vs strong-scented), while PC2 (21.8% variance) differentiated harvest seasons (autumn vs spring) (Fig. 1a). Supervised partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) validated this orthogonal separation pattern (R² = 0.741, Q² = 0.713), with light-scented teas exhibiting greater seasonal metabolomic divergence compared to their strong-scented counterparts (Fig. 1b). Hierarchical clustering of differential metabolites (VIP > 1, p < 0.05) revealed four distinct chemotypes (Fig. 1e). Light-scented processing maintained higher metabolite diversity (Shannon index: Spring_QS, 5.038 ± 0.005; Autumn_QS, 4.954 ± 0.021), preserving seasonal signatures through minimal thermal intervention (Fig. S1). In contrast, strong-scented processing reduced chemical diversity (Shannon index: Spring_NS, 4.927 ± 0.017; Autumn_NS, 4.847 ± 0.033) through thermal reactions above 130 °C, with processing-induced transformations overriding seasonal variations (Fig. S1). Notably, seasonal substrate availability modulated processing outcomes—autumn samples with elevated catechin content generated higher yields of N-ethyl-2-pyrrolidinone-substituted flavan-3-ols (EPSFs), explaining their greater separation in multivariate space. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) identified twelve metabolite modules exhibiting distinct seasonal and processing responses (Fig. 1c–e). Module GM6, comprising EPSFs, formed exclusively in strong-scented teas via theanine-catechin condensation above 130 °C. Conversely, module GM1 triterpene saponins accumulated preferentially in autumn through the mevalonate pathway and remained stable across processing conditions. Competitive metabolic flux manifested as module antagonism—GM3 procyanidins and GM5 galloylated flavans exhibited a strong negative correlation (r = −0.88, p < 10⁻⁸), reflecting divergent channeling through the flavonoid biosynthetic pathway. Module GM4, containing vitexin and non-galloylated catechins, increased with thermal intensity but displayed season-dependent magnitude (Fig. 1c–e), suggesting temperature-threshold mechanisms governing metabolite interconversions.

Sparse PLS-DA (sPLS-DA) enables robust authentication of Tieguanyin types

Given the complexity of the metabolomic landscape comprising 2560 features, we implemented sPLS-DA to achieve robust tea authentication. By incorporating Lasso regularization for automatic feature selection, sPLS-DA effectively addressed the curse of dimensionality that constrained the discriminatory power of standard PLS-DA when limited to 190 annotated metabolites alone17. The optimized model achieved robust group separation using two components that collectively explained 54% of the total variance (Component 1: 45%; Component 2: 9%) (Fig. 2a), with spring harvest samples exhibiting tighter clustering patterns than autumn harvest samples. Cross-validation identified optimal performance at 8 components with a classification error < 10% (Fig. 2b). Balanced error rate and three distance metrics converged at component 3, indicating model stability. ROC (receiver operating characteristic) analysis revealed exceptional discrimination: Spring_QS achieved near-perfect classification (Area Under Curve (AUC) = 0.9986), while autumn variants showed strong performance (Autumn_NS: 0.9378; Autumn_QS: 0.9176) (Fig. 2c). Models using eight components exceeded 0.99 AUC across all categories (Fig. S2). Independent test set validation (n = 55) yielded 85.5% overall accuracy (Fig. 2d), with perfect classification of light-scented samples (Autumn_QS: 13/13; Spring_QS: 15/15). Misclassifications occurred exclusively between strong-scented types across seasons (5 Autumn_NS as Spring_NS; 3 Spring_NS as Autumn_NS), confirming that intensive processing partially masks seasonal signatures. Random forest classification enhances the authentication of Tieguanyin tea variants

ML algorithms, including random forest (RF) and support vector machines (SVM), have been extensively applied to authenticate geographic origins and detect adulteration in various agricultural products18–20. To validate our metabolomic fingerprinting approach through complementary ML methodologies, we implemented an RF classifier—a robust ensemble learning algorithm that constructs multiple uncorrelated decision trees via bootstrap aggregation21. By capturing non-linear feature interactions through this ensemble approach, RF classification achieved 90.5% cross-validation accuracy and 87.3% accuracy on the independent test set (Fig. 3c, d), modestly outperforming sPLS-DA (85.5%). Precision-recall (PR) analysis demonstrated an average precision of 0.96 (Fig. 3a), with light-scented variants achieving perfect classification (AUC = 1.00) and strong-scented samples exhibiting near-perfect discrimination (Spring_NS: AUC = 0.96; Autumn_NS: AUC = 0.97) (Fig. 3b). Both sPLS-DA and RF achieved complete separation of all 28 light-scented samples, confirming algorithmically invariant chemical signatures from minimal processing. CNNs enable drift-resistant authentication of Tieguanyin tea

DL approaches have revolutionized pattern recognition across diverse domains, yet their application to metabolomic authentication remains nascent. To leverage CNNs for metabolomic pattern recognition, we transformed LC-MS data into images, enabling application of computer vision architectures to high-dimensional metabolomic fingerprints (Fig. 4). This approach converts three-dimensional LC-MS data—RTmass-to-charge ratio (m/z), and intensity—into two-dimensional grayscale images where pixel position encodes chemical coordinates, and intensity represents metabolite abundance (Fig. 4a). Critically, this representation preserves spatial relationships between chemically similar metabolites, allowing convolutional filters to learn biologically meaningful features. A key step was chemically informed data augmentation that expanded our training dataset from 219 to 1314 training images (Fig. 4b). Unlike conventional image augmentation, we introduced controlled variations mimicking genuine analytical variability: RT drifts, m/z variations, and intensity fluctuations. To demonstrate superiority over traditional approaches, we simulated RT drift—a pervasive LC-MS challenge—by introducing random RT errors (mean: 20 s, standard deviation: 5 s) to generate an RT drift dataset alongside the original data (Fig. 6a). Feature overlap between datasets was minimal: only 1275 of 2532 features (50.3%) matched after RT perturbation (Fig. 6b), reflecting realistic analytical variation. When trained on the original dataset and tested on RT-shifted data, traditional random forest methods showed severe performance degradation (87.3% to 69.1% accuracy), whereas the DL approach maintained substantially higher robustness (90.9% to 78.2% accuracy) (Fig. 6c, d). These results demonstrate that DL can effectively handle analytical variations that confound conventional metabolomic pipelines.

Discussion

Our study elucidates that the metabolomic identity of Tieguanyin tea is not static but emerges from a dynamic interplay between endogenous seasonal rhythms and exogenous processing interventions. The orthogonal separation observed in our multivariate analysis reveals two distinct control regimes governing metabolite accumulation (Fig. 1). Light-scented processing, with its minimal thermal intervention, functions as a chemical preservation strategy, retaining high metabolic diversity and faithfully reflecting the distinct biosynthetic signatures of spring and autumn harvests (Figs. 1d and S1). In this regime, “nature” dominates “nurture.” Conversely, strong-scented processing imposes a convergent chemical force. The intensive roasting (>130 °C) acts as a metabolic equalizer, driving the extensive degradation of thermally labile compounds and the formation of processing-specific markers (Figs. 1d and S1). Notably, we identified that while thermal processing tends to mask seasonal differences, it does not obliterate them; rather, it amplifies specific precursor-product relationships. The exclusive formation of N-ethyl-2-pyrrolidinone-substituted flavan-3-ols (EPSFs) in strong-scented teas—specifically derived from theanine-catechin condensation—demonstrates how autumn-specific substrate enrichment translates into unique thermal reaction products. This finding underscores that processing is not merely a subtractive step but a generative chemical synthesis whose outcome is strictly gated by the initial seasonal metabolome.

While deciphering these chemical mechanisms is scientifically valuable, translating them into robust authentication tools has historically been hindered by the “curse of dimensionality” and analytical instability. Our comparison of sPLS-DA and RF highlights a critical bottleneck in conventional metabolomics: reliance on peak alignment and feature annotation. Although these methods achieved respectable accuracy (>85%), their performance degraded significantly when seasonal distinctness was blurred by strong processing, or when instrumental drift was introduced. Traditional algorithms treat metabolites as discrete, independent features, failing to capture the global structural correlations inherent in chromatographic data. The superior performance of our DL framework (90.9% accuracy and 0.994 AUC) marks a paradigm shift from manual feature engineering to end-to-end representation learning. By transforming LC-MS data into 2D images, we leverage the spatial inductive bias of CNNs. Unlike tabular methods that discard RT and m/z correlations after alignment, the image-based approach preserves the local spectral topology—where chemically related compounds often elute in proximity. The most improved capability of our framework is its resilience to chromatographic drift. In routine food authentication, RT shifts caused by column aging or solvent fluctuation are inevitable8,18. Our simulation showed that while RF accuracy collapsed to 69.1% under RT drift, the VGG19-based model maintained 78.2% accuracy. We attribute this robustness to the CNN’s ability to learn shift-invariant features22. Much like a vision model recognizes a cat regardless of its position in a photo, our model identifies metabolic fingerprints based on relative pattern intensity and spectral texture, rather than absolute coordinate matching. This tolerance to noise is achieved through our chemically informed data augmentation strategy, suggesting that future metabolomic models should be trained on probabilistic data clouds rather than static peak tables to ensure real-world deployability23. An unexpected but critical methodological insight concerns data resolution. Counterintuitively, the intermediate resolution (224 × 224 or 448 × 448) outperformed high-resolution (1024 × 1024) inputs. This phenomenon reflects the inherent sparsity of untargeted metabolomic data. Excessive resolution in LC-MS images introduces vast empty space (zero-value pixels), which dilutes relevant chemical signals and increases the risk of overfitting to noise. The 224 × 224 binning effectively acts as a logical aggregation step, merging signals from isotopic envelopes and adducts into coherent features. This finding provides a practical guideline for future “Omics-to-Image” applications: resolution should be optimized to feature density, not maximized arbitrarily. Compared with the “gold standard” of spiking internal standards for correcting instrumental variation, our DL framework offers a “label-free” and cost-effective computational alternative. While internal standards are often limited by cost and their inability to correct non-linear shifts across complex chromatograms, the CNN-based model leverages shift-invariance to maintain robustness without specific chemical markers.

Despite these advances, the ‘black box’ nature of DL remains a significant constraint. Although our model achieves high classification accuracy, it lacks the inherent molecular interpretability offered by sPLS-DA and random forest. Future work should integrate Explainable AI (XAI) techniques, such as Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM), to visualize which spectral regions drive the network’s decisions, thereby bridging the gap between predictive power and chemical discovery24,25. Another potential limitation of this study is the relatively small sample size (n = 274) compared to typical large-scale DL applications. In the field of untargeted metabolomics, obtaining thousands of high-quality biological replicates is often constrained by the high cost of authentic samples and instrumental throughput. To mitigate this, we utilized a chemically informed data augmentation protocol to artificially expand the training diversity to 1314 images. This strategy, combined with the use of pre-trained VGG19 through transfer learning, allows the model to achieve high classification accuracy by leveraging low-level features already optimized on massive datasets. Furthermore, while simulated drift demonstrates model resilience, we acknowledge that longitudinal verification—using samples acquired at significantly different time points—remains the “gold standard” for assessing long-term robustness. Due to constraints in sample stability and instrument scheduling, this was not explored in the present work. Future studies will prioritize the collection of multi-batch, longitudinal data to further validate the model’s performance in sustained industrial monitoring settings.

In conclusion, this work establishes a scalable, drift-resistant framework for precision food authentication. By coupling the chemical insights of untargeted metabolomics with the pattern recognition power of computer vision, we overcome the reproducibility crisis often plaguing chromatographic analysis. This approach extends beyond tea to any agricultural commodity where complex metabolic phenotypes define value, offering a generalized solution for protecting geographic indications and verifying process integrity in global food supply chains.

Methods

Metabolomic profiling of tea samples

A detailed sample collection and metabolomic analysis of the Tieguanyin tea samples were published in our previous study1. Briefly, 274 Tieguanyin oolong tea samples (Spring_QS, spring light-scented; Spring_NS, spring strong-scented; Autumn_QS, autumn light-scented; Autumn_NS, autumn strong-scented Tieguanyin.) were collected from Anxi City, Fujian Province, China. The lyophilized ground tea samples were extracted with methanol and prepared for untargeted metabolomic analysis using a UPLC-QToF-MS system (Waters SYNAPT G2Si, Milford, MA, USA) equipped with a photodiode array detector and SYNAPT G2-Si HDMS QToF mass spectrometer. Tea extracts (1 μL) were separated by reverse-phase chromatography on a Waters Acquity UPLC HSS T3 column (100 × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm particle size) with the column oven temperature maintained at 40 °C. The mobile phase consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water (A) and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (B). Gradient elution was programmed as follows: 1–7% B (0–2 min), 7–40% B (2–13 min), 40–60% B (13–17 min), rapid increase to 99% B at 17 min, held at 99% B (17–22 min), followed by 3 min equilibration. Data acquired during the final 8 min of the run were excluded from analysis, as this period corresponded to the column wash and re-equilibration phases, where no target analytes elute. The flow rate was maintained at 0.3 mL min⁻¹. Metabolites were ionized using electrospray ionization in negative mode with MassLynx v4.1 software (Waters). Data acquisition employed MSᴱ continuum mode with ramped collision energy (10–50 eV). Operating parameters included: capillary voltage, 2.0 kV; cone voltage, 40 eV; collision energy, 4 eV; source temperature, 120 °C; desolvation temperature, 450 °C; cone gas flow, 50 L h⁻¹; desolvation gas flow, 800 L h⁻¹; mass range, m/z 50–1200. Quality control samples were analyzed every tenth injection to monitor instrument stability.

Metabolomic data processing and statistical analysis

Raw MSᴱ data were processed using Progenesis QI v2.1 (Waters) for peak alignment, extraction, normalization, and annotation. Metabolite identification was achieved through comparison of accurate masses, MS/MS fragmentation patterns, isotope distributions, and UV spectra against authentic standards, literature data, and public databases including METLIN, HMDB, MassBank, ChemSpider, and ReSpect. Quality control-based normalization corrected for systematic variation. Normalized intensities underwent log10 transformation and auto-scaling (z-score normalization). Principal component analysis (PCA) and PLS-DA were performed using MetaboAnalyst v5.026. sPLS-DA with Lasso regularization was implemented in R using the mixOmics package27. WGCNA was conducted using the WGCNA R package v1.63. The metabolomic datasets were analyzed with the following parameters: minModuleSize = 10, minKMEtoStay = 0.6, mergeCutHeight = 0.05, and minCoreKME = 0.7. Network visualization was performed in Cytoscape v3.7.128,29.

Mass image generation

To enable DL analysis, we transformed LC-MS data into images through systematic binning. Metabolic feature table from Progenesis QI was discretized along both m/z (y-axis, 60–1500 Da) and RT (x-axis, 60–1020 s) dimensions according to specified resolution parameters (224 × 224, 448 × 448, and 1024 × 1024 pixels). For 224 × 224-pixel images, the m/z range was divided into 224 bins, with metabolites sharing similar RTs and mass-to-charge ratios grouped within individual pixels. Within each pixel, constituent data points underwent log10 transformation to correct heteroscedasticity and enhance low-intensity signal contribution. Mean intensities were calculated for each pixel and linearly mapped to grayscale values (0–255), generating monochrome images in PNG format using R’s png() function that preserves spatial relationships between chemically related metabolites.

Data augmentation for the training dataset

We developed a chemically informed augmentation protocol to expand the training dataset while maintaining biological relevance. In alignment with our selection of the 224 × 224 resolution for optimal computational efficiency and model stability, this augmentation was performed exclusively on the 224 × 224 image set. Random perturbations were introduced to RT, m/z, and intensity values following normal distributions centered on expected instrumental variations. Specifically, each of the 219 original training images at 224 × 224 resolution was used to generate five augmented counterparts based on predefined error distributions: (i) RT 5 ± 1 s, m/z 5 ± 1, Intensity 1 ± 0.2; (ii) RT 5 ± 1 s, m/z 10 ± 2; (ii) RT 10 ± 2 s, m/z 5 ± 1; (iv) RT 20 ± 5 s, Intensity 0.5 ± 0.1; and (v) RT 20 ± 5 s, m/z 10 ± 2. This strategy expanded the training dataset to a total of 1314 images, enabling the model to learn invariance to technical noise at the targeted resolution.

RT drift dataset generation and alignment

Distinct from the training augmentation described above, an independent ‘RT Drift Dataset’ was generated using the 55 validation samples to assess the model’s robustness against severe chromatographic shifts. To simulate significant instrumental drift, RT values were perturbed with a specific error distribution (mean = 20 s, SD = 5 s). The original and RT drifted data were aligned according to m/z and RT using the R package masstools30. Briefly, only the features in two metabolic feature tables within the setting cutoff for m/z matching (<10 ppm) and RT matching (<20 s) are considered the same features. If one feature matches multiple features, only the feature with the minimum RT matching error remains.

ML and DL implementation

The metabolomics dataset was partitioned 80:20 for training (219 samples) and validation (55 samples), maintaining equal representation across categories. Model performance was assessed through 5-fold cross-validation, with evaluation metrics including accuracy, precision, recall, and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC-ROC). Random forest classifiers were constructed using the scikit-learn package (v0.24.2) in Python (v3.8.12), with hyperparameters optimized via Bayesian optimization31,32. For DL analysis, we fine-tuned five pre-trained convolutional neural network architectures from PyTorch Image Models (ConvNeXtV2_large, DenseNet201, ResNet101, tf_EfficientNetV2_b3, and VGG19_bn) for hierarchical feature extraction in Python (v3.10.8)33. To determine the optimal model and image resolution, DL architectures were comparatively evaluated at three resolutions (224 × 224, 448 × 448, and 1024 × 1024 pixels) using 5-fold cross-validation. The final model was trained on 1314 augmented images (219 × 6) using an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 GPU (24 GB memory, 2520 MHz). The Adam optimizer was employed with an initial learning rate of 0.0001, a decay factor of 0.98, and a batch size of 256. Training was conducted for up to 200 epochs or until convergence.
