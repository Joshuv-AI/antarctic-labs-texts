Association between CYP2C19 gene polymorphisms and analgesic response to meperidine in emergency department patients with renal colic: a prospective observational study
Authors: Sena Baykara Sayili, Aylin Koseler, Bulent Erdur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRenal colic is a urologic emergency that presents with characteristic severe pain and is mostly seen in emergency services. The preferred medications are NSAIDs, paracetamol and opioids, but considering the severity of pain, opioid analgesics should be advised. The aim of this study is to determine the relationship between meperidine efficacy and CYP2C19 gene polymorphisms.MethodsThis prospective observational study was conducted between 01.07.2017 and 01.10.2018 in the Pamukkale University Faculty of Medicine Emergency Department. Our study subjects included 203 patients with renal colic and 102 individuals presenting with abdominal pain but not diagnosed with renal colic were included only for genetic frequency analysis; this group was not included in the intervention safety/efficacy analyses. 203 patients were given 100 mg of meperidine and observed 60 min later, and vital signs, laboratory values, etc., were recorded.ResultsThe age and sex distributions were similar between the case and control groups. There was no statistically significant difference between the sexes in visual analog scale (VAS) scores at any time (0.min–15.min–30.min–60.min). The normal-activity/rapid metabolizer groups had significantly lower 30- and 60-min VAS scores than did the moderate-activity groups. The percentages of VAS decreases at 30 and 60 min from baseline were significantly greater in the normal-activity rapid metabolizer group than in the moderate-activity group. This study revealed that the efficacy of the drug was not affected by age, sex or stone location. The VAS score decreased significantly at all time intervals. Additionally, the decrease in the VAS score between 0 and 15 min after drug administration was similar in each genotype group, but after 30 min, it was remarkable for the CYP2C19 1/1 genotype. During follow-up, 11 patients had drug-related adverse events (most commonly nausea and dizziness). No serious events occurred, and no additional interventions or hospitalizations were required.ConclusionsThis study suggested that the effectiveness of meperidine in treating renal colic may be influenced by the genetic polymorphism of CYP2C19 in the patient. The pain response was markedly reduced in individuals with the CYP2C19 *1/*1 genotype. The age, sex, and stone localization groups were seemingly comparable with respect to the efficacy of meperidine. It is advisable to consider the genetic profile in individualized treatment approaches, with the aim of increasing drug efficacy and optimizing dose adjustments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12894-026-02217-y.Keywords: Cytochrome P-450 CYP2C19, Meperidine, Renal colic, Polymorphism, Emergency medicine

Background

Renal colic is a common and painful urological emergency that often requires rapid and effective analgesia to control symptoms [1]. Renal colic involves acute obstruction of the urinary tract, leading to increased intraluminal pressure, ureteral spasm, and severe pain [1]. The prevalence of kidney stone disease is increasing globally, contributing to a significant healthcare burden and frequent emergency department visits [2–5]. In Turkey, kidney stone epidemiology has regional and gender-based variations, notably impacting quality of life and healthcare resources. In Turkey, the prevalence of stone disease is assumed to be 14% [6]. The prevalence of having renal colic at least once in their lives until the age of 70 years is reported to be 12% in men and 5% in women [7, 8].

Effective pain management is essential for reducing morbidity and improving patient outcomes. The initial therapeutic approach for most patients presenting with renal colic is NSAIDs, whereas opioids serve as an essential alternative for cases where NSAIDs are contraindicated or demonstrate inadequate therapeutic response [1, 9–11]. This approach maintains adherence to evidence-based clinical guidelines while accounting for the necessity of individualized patient care on the basis of specific clinical circumstances and risk stratification [3, 10, 12–14].

Meperidine is a synthetic opioid analgesic used for the management of pain. Meperidine provides significant pain relief in renal colic and studies show that it is as effective as dexketoprofen trometamol in the short term and more effective than paracetamol and tramadol [15, 16]. It provides rapid pain relief response in renal colic, but is less preferred due to higher side effect rates and publications supporting that it is less effective compared to NSAIDs [17, 18]. Meperidine also carries risks of addiction, respiratory depression and accumulation of the toxic metabolite normeperidine in repeated doses [19]. Its metabolism, genetic variability, and clinical indications are important for understanding its safety and effectiveness [2, 20–22]. The primary metabolic pathway of meperidine occurs within the liver and is facilitated by cytochrome P450 enzymes [21, 23–25]. Genetic variations, particularly those affecting CYP2C19 and CYP2B6, have been demonstrated to influence the rate of meperidine metabolism and the potential for the induction of neurotoxic side effects [23]. Meperidine undergoes N-demethylation in the liver, mainly by the enzymes CYP2B6, CYP3A4, and CYP2C19, to form normeperidine, a neurotoxic metabolite [22, 24, 25].

CYP2C19, a vital gene within the cytochrome P450 family, encodes the enzyme cytochrome P450 2C19, which plays a pivotal role in drug metabolism and biotransformation processes. Located on chromosome 10, this gene is highly genetically polymorphic, resulting in various allelic variants that significantly influence the pharmacokinetics of numerous therapeutic agents. Among its variants, the most notable are *CYP2C19* *1* (wild type), *CYP2C19* *2* (resulting in poor metabolism), *CYP2C19* *3* (poor metabolism), and *CYP2C19* *17* (associated with ultrarapid metabolism). The prevalence of these polymorphisms varies across different populations, leading to implications for individualized medicine and drug therapy optimization [23, 24, 26–29].

CYP2C19 has 7 mutant alleles identified thus far. The normal activity allele is CYP2C19*1 (wild type). The mutant alleles causing reduced or absent activity are CYP2C19*2 (m1), CYP2C19*3 (m2), CYP2C19*4 (m3), CYP2C19*5 (m4), CYP2C19*6 (m5), CYP2C19*7 (m6) and CYP2C19*8 (m7) [30–35].

To compensate for the variability in drug response, drug metabolism phenotypes are determined on the basis of genetic test results, and drug dosages are adjusted if necessary [13, 36, 37]. In some instances, a divergence may emerge between the anticipated and observed phenotypes (phenoconversion) due to drug‒drug interactions precipitated by concomitant medications [26].

The relationship between CYP2C19 gene polymorphisms and drug metabolism has been investigated worldwide, especially in the clopidogrel, proton pump inhibitor and antiepileptic drug groups, and these studies are still ongoing [38–43]. However, no studies have evaluated the genetic relationship with meperidine in our literature search.

The aim of this study was to determine the relationship between meperidine efficacy and CYP2C19 gene polymorphisms.

Materials and methods

Study design and settings

This prospective observational study was conducted at Pamukkale University Faculty of Medicine, Department of Emergency Medicine, between 01.07.2017 and 01.10.2018. A total of 203 patients who presented with renal colic and 102 patients with abdominal pain unrelated to renal colic composed the control group.

The diagnosis of renal colic was established by supervising senior resident based on the clinical presentation (renal-type pain, costovertebral angle tenderness, evidence of hematuria, etc.) and confirmed with imaging (ultrasonography and/or non-contrast CT) as clinically indicated.

Each patient was administered 100 mg of meperidine intravenously. Meperidine was diluted in 150 mL 0.9% saline and administered as a 30-minute intravenous infusion. Peripheral blood was drawn during infusion into sterile 2 mL K3EDTA tubes. Rescue analgesia (if VAS ≥ 40 mm after 30 min: ketorolac 30 mg IV or morphine 2–4 mg IV titrated) and antiemetic therapy (ondansetron 4 mg IV for nausea/vomiting) were prespecified. The 0, 15, 30, and 60 min VAS pain scores of the patients were recorded. Sociodemographic characteristics, laboratory values, and physiological characteristics (SPO2, blood pressure, etc.) were recorded. The form was completed by a senior emergency medicine resident trained in the protocol, VAS assessment, and data recording. Vital signs were monitored by ED nurses. Patients received brief instructions and, under the researcher’s supervision, recorded their own VAS scores. The study form used for case follow-up has been included as a supplementary file. This study was designed and reported in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines to ensure transparency and completeness in reporting observational research.

Ethics

This study was approved by Pamukkale University, Non-Invasive Clinical Research Ethics Committee (24.05.2017-60116787-020/33092). Written and verbally informed consent was obtained from each individual.

Sample, study group

The sample size was calculated via OpenEpi with the following parameters: 80% power (1-β) and 95% confidence interval, cases/control ratio: 2, with the prediction of 30% polymorphism in the patient group receiving meperidine treatment and 15% in the control group. The sample size was 196 cases and 98 controls. However, it was targeted to reach 200 cases/100 controls, considering the losses that may occur during data collection.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criterion for the patient group was individuals aged 18 years and older who presented to the emergency department with renal colic pain and agreed to participate in the study. The control group consisted of adults aged 18 years and older who presented to the emergency department with abdominal pain and consented to participate; those whose current presentation was not related to renal colic were included in the control group. The exclusion criteria included those who had used analgesics within the last six hours, patients with chronic renal failure, liver cirrhosis, neoplastic pain, or hypersensitivity to meperidine or any of its ingredients. Additional exclusions included pregnant individuals, patients taking a monoamine oxidase (MAO) inhibitor, patients who had taken one of these agents in the past 14 days, illiterate patients, those who rated their pain as 50 mm or less on the 100 mm visual analog scale (VAS) upon admission, and those who declined to participate in the study. During follow-up, 11 patients had drug-related adverse events (most commonly nausea and dizziness).No serious events occurred, and no additional interventions or hospitalizations were required. They were not excluded from the study. The patient selection process and group assignment for the study are summarized in Fig. 1.

Genotyping procedure

Peripheral blood samples (3 mL) were collected in EDTA tubes from all participants. Genomic DNA was extracted using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. DNA purity and concentration were determined spectrophotometrically (NanoDrop 2000, Thermo Scientific, USA).

The CYP2C19 genotypes (*1, *2, *3) were determined by polymerase chain reaction–restriction fragment length polymorphism (PCR-RFLP) analysis. PCR amplification was performed in a total reaction volume of 25 µL containing 100 ng of template DNA, 0.5 µM of each primer, 200 µM dNTPs, 1.5 mM MgCl₂, and 1 U of Taq DNA polymerase (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The PCR products were digested with appropriate restriction enzymes (SmaI for *2 and BamHI for *3 alleles) and electrophoresed on a 2% agarose gel stained with ethidium bromide. Genotype identification was based on the resulting band patterns visualized under UV light.

For quality assurance, 10% of samples were reanalyzed by Sanger sequencing (ABI 3500 Genetic Analyzer, Applied Biosystems, USA) to confirm the PCR-RFLP results, yielding 100% concordance.

Data sources of the research

The dependent variable of the study was pain severity, which was evaluated using a 100 mm Visual Analog Scale (VAS) at 0, 15, 30, and 60 min after meperidine administration. The VAS ranged from 0 mm (no pain) to 100 mm (worst imaginable pain) and was used to measure the intensity of renal colic pain in patients admitted to the emergency department. Changes in VAS scores across these time points (absolute values and percentage reductions from baseline) represented the primary measure of drug efficacy.

The independent variables were defined as demographic variables (gender, age), stone size (mm), and genetic factors (CYP2C19 genotype and phenotype).

In addition, clinical variables were also recorded as accompanying variables (initial vital signs and occurrence of adverse events). Furthermore, patient file numbers, laboratory findings, etc. were also recorded on the same form.

The diagnosis of renal colic and the anatomical stone location were confirmed by ultrasonography and/or non-contrast CT when clinically indicated. Stones were classified as renal, proximal/mid/distal ureter, ureteropelvic junction, ureterovesical junction, or bladder. Stone size was measured in millimeters along the longest axis on imaging, and all results were verified by the radiology department.

SpO2 monitoring, automatic sphygmomanometry (blood pressure), and rhythm monitoring (rate and rhythm) were provided during the procedure, and all other medications used during the study were also recorded. The body temperature of the patients at the time of admission was measured under the armpit. Oxygen saturation and blood pressure were measured.

Pain scores were evaluated and recorded at 0, 15, 30 and 60 min. In addition, throughout the study, heart rate (beats/min), systolic blood pressure (mmHg), diastolic blood pressure (mmHg), respiratory rate (breath/min), body temperature (C°) and O2 saturation (SPO2) were recorded at 0, 15, 30 and 60 min. In addition, possible side effects were recorded in the data form. Treatment was applied if necessary for side effects. All the information was recorded in the created work form.

The CYP2C19 1/1 genotype is defined as a normal activity-rapid metabolizer, the 1/2 and 1/3 genotypes are defined as moderately active (poor metabolizers), the 2/2 genotype is defined as a low-grade activity-slow metabolizer, and the 1/17 and 17/17 genotypes are defined as very fast active (ultrarapid metabolizers) [26, 27, 41–43].

Statistical analysis

The IBM Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) for Windows version 21.0 (IBM SPSS Corp.; Armonk, NY, USA) was used for data evaluation and analysis. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies (n) and percentages (%), and numeric variables are presented as the mean ± standard deviation and median (quartile 1–quartile 3) value. The suitability of the data for a normal distribution was examined via visual (histogram), descriptive (coefficient of variation, skewness, kurtosis) and analytical methods (Kolmogorov‒Smirnoff test). The chi-square test was applied for the comparison of categorical variables. Independent samples t tests and Mann‒Whitney U tests were used for comparisons of continuous variables between two independent groups. The Kruskal‒Wallis test was used to compare continuous variables between more than two independent groups. The Friedmann test was used to compare continuous variables between more than two dependent groups. When a statistically significant difference was detected in comparisons involving two or more groups, post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed and adjusted p values were reported. Spearman correlation analysis was used to identify relationships between continuous variables. Additionally, partial correlation analysis was performed to examine the relationship between stone size and VAS scores while controlling for the degree of hydroureteronephrosis as a covariate. The statistical significance level was set at p < 0.05.

No missing data was encountered in this study. Due to the non-normal distribution of the data, multivariable regression analyses for confounding control could not be performed. However, partial correlation analysis was performed to examine the relationship between stone size and VAS scores, controlling hydroureteronephrosis as a covariate.

Discussion

In this study, the relationship between meperidine efficacy and CYP2C19 gene polymorphisms was investigated and It is aimed to determine the current clinical relevance of meperidine use in emergency settings for severe acute pain, such as renal colic. The present study found that the efficacy of meperidine in patients with renal colic pain is significantly influenced by CYP2C19 gene polymorphisms. Although previous studies, including those by Ramirez et al. and Murray et al., have shown an association between CYP2C19 gene polymorphisms and rapid metabolic conversion of meperidine to its active metabolite, normeperidine, there are no studies evaluating genetic polymorphisms together with clinical analgesic response [23, 24, 27]. Despite the restrictions on meperidine use due to concerns about its neurotoxic metabolite, normeperidine, this gap in the literature motivated the present investigation. The findings of the present study corroborate those of earlier studies. The present study found that the efficacy of meperidine in patients with renal colic pain is significantly influenced by CYP2C19 gene polymorphisms. Pain relief was more significant in patients with the CYP2C19 1/1 genotype (normal activity-rapid metabolizer), particularly after 30 and 60 min of drug administration. These findings suggest that genetic variability plays a critical role in the metabolism and analgesic efficacy of meperidine.

Despite the extensive research conducted on the relationship between CYP2C19 gene polymorphisms and various drugs, particularly clopidogrel, there is a notable gap in studies focused on meperidine. Many articles have investigated the impact of the CYP2C19 polymorphism on proton pump inhibitors, sodium valproate, antidepressants (including amitriptyline, clomipramine, doxepin, imipramine, and trimipramine), and particular antidiabetic medications such as gliclazide and tolperisone [44–52]. However, the specific interaction between the CYP2C19 gene polymorphism and meperidine remains underexplored, highlighting the need for further research in this area.

In this study, the 1/1 genotype of CYP2C19 was observed in 68.96%, the 1/2 genotype of CYP2C19 was observed in 28.08%, and the 1/3 genotype was observed in 2.96%. The rapidly metabolized types 1/17 and 17/17 were not detected in the patients included in this study.

In a meta-analysis of studies worldwide, the CYP2C19*2 and CYP2C19*3 alleles were more common in East Asians (30.26% and 6.89%, respectively), and a twofold higher frequency of these alleles was found in Native populations from Oceania (61.30% and 14.42%, respectively; P < 0. 001, in all cases), the ultrafast metabolizer type, the CYP2C19*17 genotype, was 42- and 24-fold more common in Mediterranean-South Europeans and Middle Easterns than in East Asians [48]. In the context of the CYP2C19 metabolic phenotype, poor metabolizers are more common among Asians than among Europeans [53]. Similar genotype distributions have been reported in other studies conducted in Türkiye. In the study by Aynacıoğlu et al., the CYP2C19 allele frequencies of CYP2C19*1, CYP2C19*2, and CYP2C19*3 were 87.5%, 12.1%, and 0.4%, respectively [54]. Sabirli et al. reported that the CYP2C19*1/1 genotype was identified in 132 patients (66%), the CYP2C19*1/2 genotype was identified in 62 patients (31%), and the CYP2C19*2/2 genotype was identified in 6 patients (3%) [54, 55].

The present investigation revealed that the CYP2C19*2 allele is linked to variations in meperidine metabolism within the Turkish population. These variations have the potential to impact the effectiveness and safety of the drug. Importantly, there is a paucity of research in this area, particularly in the context of Turkey, where no studies have yet evaluated the association between meperidine and the CYP2C19 gene polymorphism. Other publications in the literature did not evaluate the association of meperidine with CYP2C19 subtypes but aimed to determine the relative contributions of each P450 enzyme to the N-demethylation of meperidine. CYP2C19 subtypes were not evaluated in these publications. In this study, an association was found between the CYP2C19*2 polymorphism and meperidine metabolism, which was correlated with previous pharmacogenetic studies. Ramirez et al. reported that CYP2C19, together with CYP2B6 and CYP3A4, is responsible for the N-demethylation of meperidine in human liver microsomes [27]. Murray et al. highlighted the impact of cytochrome P450 variation on the formation of the neurotoxic metabolite normeperidine, revealing the clinical importance of polymorphisms [24]. Although meperidine is metabolized primarily via CYP2B6, CYP3A4, and CYP2C19 through N-demethylation to form normeperidine, the observed greater analgesic response in rapid metabolizers (CYP2C19 *1/*1) may not be explained exclusively by metabolic rate. Analgesic efficacy depends on multiple factors including individual pain perception, coordinated enzymatic activity (CYP3A4, CYP2B6), and potential central effects of normeperidine [22, 24, 25]. Therefore, the greater VAS reduction in rapid metabolizers likely reflects a multifactorial interaction of pharmacogenetic and physiological factors rather than a single enzyme pathway. Overall, the results suggest that genetic variability in opioid metabolism demonstrates a complex and integrated effect on clinical analgesic outcomes.

Clinical parameters show varying degrees of correlation with renal colic pain severity and treatment outcomes. Most of the studies rate the pain score via the visual analog scale (VAS) [56–59]. Higher pain scores are often associated with first episodes of renal colic, solitary kidneys, pyuria, elevated creatinine, severe hydronephrosis, and stones located in the renal pelvis [57]. Some studies also reported that high anxiety levels and a greater degree of hydronephrosis are linked to more severe pain, suggesting that both physiological and psychological factors can influence pain perception [56]. However, other studies have indicated that pain severity does not reliably predict stone size, stone location, hydronephrosis severity, or the need for hospitalization or rescue intervention, limiting its utility as a prognostic tool for treatment outcomes [58–60]. Additional clinical factors, such as age, underlying diseases, fever, chills, nausea, vomiting, and abnormal ultrasound findings, are more predictive of the need for invasive management or recurrence [61]. Overall, while certain clinical and laboratory parameters correlate with pain severity, pain scores alone are not sufficient to predict stone characteristics or treatment outcomes, and a comprehensive assessment remains essential for optimal management [56–58, 60, 61]. In this study, there were no significant differences observed between allele type groups in terms of age, sex, or stone location. These findings provide a basis for a more direct assessment of the pharmacogenetic impact on the meperidine response.

The present study contributes to the literature by focusing on meperidine, for which pharmacogenetic data are limited, and by demonstrating that demographic and clinical confounders did not affect the results. This improves the broad applicability and practical significance of the findings, particularly in the context of acute pain management for renal colic patients. In this study, only CYP2C19 polymorphisms were evaluated, and other genetic and environmental factors influencing meperidine metabolism were not assessed. The study was conducted at a single center, which may limit its applicability to broader populations. The association between the presence of the CYP2C19 1/2 allele and altered meperidine metabolism indicates the potential value of pharmacogenetic testing in clinical practice.

Abouir et al. reported that drug‒drug interactions can further affect the activity of CYP2C19, making it more difficult to predict drug responses, even in individuals who have been genomically tested [26]. A meta-analysis by Milosavljević et al. confirmed the clinical importance of CYP2C19 and CYP2D6 metabolism in the pharmacokinetics and efficacy of centrally acting drugs, including opioids. Another study from China examined the prevalence and clinical significance of CYP2C19 polymorphisms in patients receiving pharmacotherapy, highlighting the necessity of personalized treatment strategies [38]. Together, these findings support the importance of pharmacogenetic testing to optimize analgesic therapy, especially in populations with a high prevalence of relevant genetic variants. More pharmacogenetic research links specific polymorphisms to the pharmacologic effects of specific opioid analgesics, and clinicians will continue to improve their understanding of how to prescribe these medications more effectively [37, 62]. In the context of these findings, the clinical significance of pharmacogenetic variability in renal colic management merits further emphasis. The European Ubiquitous Pharmacogenomics (U-PGx) clinical implementation project has provided considerable evidence that pharmacogenetically guided therapy results in fewer clinically significant adverse drug reactions compared with standard treatment approaches [63]. This finding highlights the potential benefit of incorporating genetic information into acute care management. Although point-of-care genetic testing in emergency departments is currently limited by time, cost, and resource constraints, the feasibility of these approaches is rapidly improving [64]. The integration of pharmacogenomic data into electronic health records and national biobank databases suggests that individualized therapeutic decision-making could soon become both rapid and efficient, even in time-sensitive emergency situations [63, 64]. Increased utilization of pre-emptive genetic testing may therefore improve the safety and effectiveness of analgesic therapy for renal colic.

Limitations of the study

This study was conducted at a single center, which may introduce selection bias. In addition, excluding patients with certain comorbidities or those taking interacting medications may have reduced variability in the study population. Furthermore, as patients were able to view their previous VAS scores during repeated assessments, anchoring and recall bias may have influenced the accuracy of reported pain reduction outcomes. Moreover, we acknowledge the absence of pharmacokinetic measurements, the short follow-up duration (only 60 min), and the limited generalizability of findings given that meperidine is not universally used. In addition, as abdominal CT was not performed in all patients, stone size and location data were based on ultrasonography in a subset of cases. Due to the non-parametric distribution of the data, multivariable regression analyses could not be performed, and the small sample size in certain genotype subgroups (particularly the 1/3 genotype, n = 6) limits the precision of estimates and the statistical power of subgroup comparisons.

Despite these limitations, this study provides valuable insights into the role of CYP2C19 polymorphisms in opioid metabolism and pain management.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that the efficacy of meperidine in managing renal colic varies according to individual genetic polymorphisms. Among the patient population, 68.96% were identified as rapid metabolizers of the CYP2C19 enzyme, whereas 31.03% were moderate metabolizers. Pain reduction was significantly greater in individuals with the CYP2C19 *1/*1 genotype (wild type). Although the analgesic effect of meperidine was consistent across all time intervals, no significant differences were observed at specific intervals. However, while the initial response (0–15 min) was similar across genotypes, a divergence emerged after the 15-minute mark, with normal metabolizers showing superior pain relief.

The efficacy of meperidine was not influenced by age, sex, or the anatomical location of the stone, and only a modest correlation was observed with stone size. These findings highlight the importance of genetic polymorphisms, particularly CYP2C19 genotypes, in determining the clinical response to meperidine.

Future studies should include larger, more diverse populations and assess long-term outcomes. Randomized controlled trials are also needed to confirm these findings and evaluate the clinical utility of genetic testing in guiding pain management. Integrating genetic profiling into personalized treatment protocols could enhance drug efficacy and optimize dosage adjustments, ultimately improving patient outcomes.
