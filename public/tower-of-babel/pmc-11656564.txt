Aspergillomas from Gastrointestinal Tract: a case report and review of the literature
Authors: Hui Meng, Fang He, Xiao hong Lin, Xianrang Yan, Xiaolong She, Lijuan Lu, Xuetao Yu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractGastrointestinal (GI) aspergillosis is a rare and fatal complication in immunocompromised patients. We present the case of a 72-year-old patient with unexplained recurrent pancytopenia who presented with fever, constipation, abdominal distention, and jaundice. Imaging revealed a mass in the ileocecal junction, and biopsy revealed Aspergillus hyphae infiltrating the bowel wall. He had no evidence of pulmonary, sinus, or central nervous system Aspergillus infection. After 1 month of antifungal treatment with oral isavuconazole, he recovered well and was discharged. The clinical manifestations of GI aspergillosis are non-specific. This case highlights the importance of increasing the awareness regarding GI aspergillosis in high-risk patients without pulmonary involvement.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-024-10278-3.Keywords: Case report, Gastrointestinal aspergillosis, Pancytopenia, Mass, Isavuconazole

Introduction

Invasive aspergillosis (IA) is a life-threatening opportunistic infection in immunocompromised patients, particularly those with prolonged neutropenia and received high-dose corticosteroids or immunosuppressive therapy [1, 2]. Aspergillus species are often inhaled or ingested as airborne spores; therefore, IA typically involves the respiratory tract (including the lungs and sinuses). However, these species may infect other organs by hematogenous spread. Gastrointestinal (GI, stomach to rectum) aspergillosis is a seldom but fatal extrapulmonary IA (mortality rate of 37%) [3] that is usually observed as a part of disseminated infection. Primary GI aspergillosis is rare, even in immunocompromised hosts [4]. Owing to the poor specificity of symptoms and imaging, the diagnosis of GI aspergillosis infrequently occurs before autopsy [5]. Hemorrhage, ulcer, perforation, and necrotic lesions are common pathological lesions, while pseudotumour is rare [6–9].

Herein, we report describes the presentation, diagnosis and management of a patient with primary GI aspergillomas, which is pathologically characterized by rare pseudotumour. We performed a systematic review of the literature as well. Especially high-risk patients, we suggested early screening for endoscopy and early aggressive antifungal therapy to avoid potential surgery.

Materials and methods

A systematic literature review was performed from inception to July 2024, using PubMed database. The following search terms: “aspergillosis”, “invasive aspergillosis”, “disseminated aspergillosis” and “gastrointestinal”, “intestinal”. 42 articles were identified, patient characteristics are summarized in Table 1.

Presentation of the case

A 72-year-old male with unexplained pancytopenia was admitted to the Department of Hematology in May 2022. He had a history of diabetes and chronic kidney disease. Pancytopenia was first noted 11 months prior when he was admitted to another hospital due to fever. The laboratory data revealed a white cell count (WCC) of 0.23/mm3, hemoglobin (Hgb) of 8.3 g/dL, and platelet count (Plt) of 81,000/mm3. During hospitalization, twice bone marrow biopsies were performed, both of which showed hypoplasia, which was suspected to be aplastic anemia. After antibiotic therapy, the patient’s condition improved, and he was discharged.

The patient was readmitted in a similar condition after 3 months. On admission, his general condition was poor, and he complaining of pyrexia (temperature, 39.0 °C), shivers, fatigue, tachycardia, shortness of breath, and abdominal distension. The laboratory tests revealed a WCC of 0.46/mm3, an absolute neutrophil count of 0.01/mm3, an Hgb level of 9.3 g/dL, a Plt of 125,000/mm3, a hypersensitive C-reactive protein (hCRP) level of 189 mg/L (range, 0–10.0 mg/L), and a procalcitonin (PCT) level of 4.06 ng/mL (range, 0–0.05 ng/mL). Initially, broad-spectrum antibiotics (meropenem, moxifloxacin, and linezolid) were administered. Despite 2 weeks of antimicrobial therapy, the patient had persistent fever and respiratory distress.

Subsequently, he was referred to the intensive care unit (ICU) because of clinical deterioration caused by severe sepsis. Thoracic computed tomography (CT) revealed aggravation of pneumonia, and mechanical ventilation was initiated upon ICU admission. Micafungin was added because of an increase in the level of (1,3) ß-D-glucan (657.2 pg/ml, range: 0–60 pg/ml) following its assay. Unfortunately, he developed carbapenem-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa (CRAP)-induced parotid abscess, bloodstream infection, and cholecystitis, as confirmed by serial cultures. Although systemic symptoms improved after 1 week of levofloxacin therapy, his GI symptoms (distension, constipation, and jaundice) remained overt. Point-of-care ultrasonography suggested cholecystitis with common bile duct dilatation. Advanced investigations, including abdominal contrast-enhanced CT (CECT), were performed to evaluate the etiology of the biliary dilatation; however, a contrast-enhanced mass was unexpectedly found in the ileocecal junction on day 23 (Fig. 1). Subsequently, colonoscopy and biopsy were performed to determine the etiology of the mass (Fig. 2). On day 30, microscopy of the biopsy specimen and the PSA strain revealed chronic inflammation with lymphocyte infiltration and invasion of numerous septate hyphae into the mucosa, compatible with Aspergillus (Fig. 3). Repeat chest CT scan revealed the absence of features of invasive aspergillosis. Moreover, the next-generation sequencing of bronchoalveolar lavage fluid investigations demonstrated no evidence of Aspergillus infection in the lung. The serum galactomannan (GM) antigen level was 9.82 µg/L(range 0–0.25 µg/L). Based on the clinical images and histopathological findings, the patient was diagnosed with primary GI aspergillosis.

As there were no serious abdominal complications, the patient was managed conservatively. After the diagnosis, voriconazole was added to initiate treatment. As the patient presented with intermittent fever, persistent GI symptoms (abdominal distension, constipation, and jaundice), and concurrent hepatic impairment, the antifungal agent was modified to oral isavuconazole (200 mg/day, q8h) on day 33. The patient’s WCC and Plt increased gradually to the normal range, accompanied by GI symptom improvement after 1 week of isavuconazole treatment. After 3 weeks of oral isavuconazole therapy, repeated abdominal CECT and colonoscopy revealed a significant reduction in the lesion (Sup Figs. 1–2). Moreover, histopathological analysis of the specimen revealed the absence of Aspergillus hyphae (Sup Fig. 3). The patient was discharged after 1 month of antifungal treatment; he had no relapse and lived independently after 12 months of follow-up.

Review of the literature

The review of the literature reveals 42 reports contained 98 patients of GI aspergillosis, most of them are case reports or case series. Data are summarized in Table 1. The mean age of patients was 46 years (range 0.3–88). There were 54 males (mean age: 43 years) and 44 females (mean age: 49 years), respectively. 94 patients had immunodeficiency disorder, the common underlying diseases included: varies hematologic malignancy (n = 62, 65.9%), solid tumor (n = 7, 7.4%), organ transplantation (n = 4, 4.3%). They were receiving immunosuppressive agents for either prophylaxis for or treatment. Other predisposing disease that may the development of IA included: primary immunodeficiency syndromes, AIDS (acquired immune deficiency syndrome), diabetes, septic shock, COVID-19, liver failure, alcoholic and malnutrition, ulcerative colitis, systemic sclerosis, COPD, IABP and ECMO.

There were 67 reported deaths (mortality, 68.4%), including 41 patients diagnosed by autopsy (28.6%). Another 57 patients were diagnosed before death, of which 38 patients were diagnosed by postoperative pathology (38.8%), followed by endoscopy (n = 18, 18.4%) and another by repeated fecal culture (n = 1). GM antigen testing was performed in 30 patients, include positive results in 25 (83.3%). The differences in outcomes between surgical and antifungal treatment are shown in Fig. 4.

Among these, 40 patients had lesion in small intestine (41.2%), 31 in the large intestine (31.9%), 30 in the stomach (31%), and 14 in the esophagus (14.4%). These lesions may be limited in the segmental intestinal or disseminated in multiple tracts. Isolated GI aspergillosis occurred in 34 of 98 patients (34.7%), while in disseminated cases, 54 patients were complicated by lungs (55.1%), followed by the central nervous system (n = 13, 13.3%).

Atypical GI mucositis was the main clinical manifestation, included abdominal pain in 54 patients, diarrhea in 47, GI hemorrhage in 32, vomit in 21, abdominal distension in 19, constipation or occlusion in 12. The imaging of abdomen was performed in 52 patients, among which the thickening of GI wall was the most common abnormalities (n = 26), followed by GI dilatation (n = 12) and perforation (n = 9). Morphologically, there showed mixed lesions, including ulcers (n = 41), necrosis (n = 14), perforations (n = 11), mass (n = 8), mucosal flecks (n = 7) and pseudomembranous (n = 6), which may be single or multiple.

Discussion

IA mainly involves the lower respiratory tract and sinuses. Other sites of infection include the central nervous system, eyes, skin, kidneys, heart, liver, and GI tract [10, 11]. Most GI aspergillosis cases have been reported to be disseminated cases. Eggiman et al. [12] reviewed retrospective studies showing an incidence of 2%, of which only 0.7% were diagnosed with a primary GI tract infection. However, the true incidence may be underestimated. Another retrospective study from an autopsy case series reported that the incidence had increased to 17% (2–53%) [12].

Notably, it is difficult for Aspergillus spores to survive or obtain a favorable environment for colonization in the GI mucosa under physiological conditions. Unfortunately, when injury to the mucosal barrier or ostomy occurs in immunocompromised patients, the GI tract may be a portal of entry for Aspergillus [6, 13, 14]. We think that mucosal problems due to repeated infection and long-term antibiotic exposure may be potential risk of GI aspergillosis in our case. Due to differences in studied populations, the most common site of GI Aspergillus infection remains controversial [3, 4]. Nevertheless, the small intestine was the most involved organ in lower GI tract infections [3, 12, 15]. This is consistent with our retrospective findings.

Many cases of GI aspergillosis are usually ignored because their clinical presentations are similar to those of non-specific enterocolitis, such as abdominal pain, diarrhea, GI hemorrhage, and abdominal distention [3, 15]. Notably, Aspergillus spp. can invade submucosal vessels [16], consequently becoming complicated by hemorrhage, perforation, abscess, and ileus [6, 15, 17]. In clinical practice, these symptoms are not singular manifestations as they are commonly overshadowed or mixed with varying systemic symptoms, which increases the difficulty of identification.

Despite poor specificity, abdominal CT should be suggested in high-risk patients. Especially, radiological findings reveal distention with a segmental thickened of GI wall with inflammation around. These images features may be a non-specific warning sign of GI aspergillosis, highlighting the necessity for further screening in immunocompromised patients, particularly in those with compatible clinical manifestations and positive serum GM antigen test findings.

Overall mortality rate of IA was 68.4% in our retrospective review, which was similar to the retrospective results of P. Eggimann et al. [12] This result may have been an overestimate because diagnosed by autopsy were also included, whose cause of death could have been multifactorial. The review result show surgical patients had a higher mortality rate, which may be associated with more severe complications in GI aspergillosis, such as perforation, severe bleeding, and intestinal obstruction. Given the high mortality rate associated with GI aspergillosis, aggressive endoscopic biopsy, and prophylaxis therapy should be considered for these patients to reduce delayed diagnosis and treatment. There is no consensus regarding the management, the selection of antifungal agents must be based on the clinical condition of the patient and the local epidemiology [18]. The IDSA guidelines [19] recommend voriconazole as the first-line treatment for IA; however, recently, it has been demonstrated that isavuconazole is as effective as voriconazole and is better tolerated [20]. Surgical intervention plays a key role in controlling complications [15]. In addition, another benefit of surgical procedures is the ability to provide diagnostic aid for cases with obscure diagnoses.

In this case, the patient’s main symptoms included moderate abdominal distension and constipation. His pathological changes were necrotic pseudotumour, which was a rare granulomatous enteritis and was easily misdiagnosed as a tumoral lesion. Although GI aspergillosis-related aspergillomas is rare but possible, and may be complicated by mechanical bowel obstruction and granulomatous gastroenteritis [7, 8, 21]. The patient expressed gratitude for the timely diagnosis, which enabled avoidance of potential surgical risks. In addition, pancytopenia may be a potential clinical manifestation rather than a cause because the patient’s pancytopenia gradually recovered after treatment with isavuconazole, and did not relapse during follow-up. However, the real causal relationship between the two is unclear. Given the recurrence of pancytopenia before antifungal therapy and a prolonged debilitating condition, we speculated that the patient may have had a chronic low-burden infection of GI aspergillosis. Similar to the Splendore-Hoeppli phenomenon in which fungal components are coated with immune complexes that inhibit the effects of the fungus [22].

Conclusion

The diagnosis of GI aspergillosis is difficult owing to the poor specificity of symptoms and imaging, especially for aspergillomas. An optimal therapy has not yet been established, and isavuconazole can be considered the preferred drug.
