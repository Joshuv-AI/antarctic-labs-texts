Ethiopia's first-ever Marburg virus disease outbreak: Implications for health security in Jordan in the context of labor migration
Authors: Ala'a B Al-Tammemi, Tareq L Mukattash
Published: 2025

Marburg virus disease (MVD) is widely recognized as one of the most lethal infectious diseases known. It is a rare but highly devastating zoonotic viral haemorrhagic fever caused by Marburg virus (MARV) and Ravn virus, two closely related members of the Filoviridae family. This viral family also includes Ebola virus. MARV was originally identified during two simultaneous outbreaks in 1967 in Germany and Belgrade [1,2].

The natural reservoir of MARV is the Egyptian fruit bat Rousettus aegyptiacus. These bats can carry the virus without obvious disease, and several human outbreaks have been traced to caves and mines harboring bat colonies. Spillover typically occurs through exposure to bat guano, secretions, or contaminated fruit in these settings, followed by human-to-human transmission through direct contact with blood and other body fluids (such as vomitus, stool, urine, saliva, sweat, breast milk, and semen) or via contaminated fomites, including bedding and medical equipment [2,3]. Nosocomial transmission and unsafe burial practices amplify MVD outbreaks as well.

The incubation of MARV ranges from 2 to 21 days. Clinically, MVD can be categorized into three stages of disease progression, namely generalization phase (days 1–4), early organ phase (days 5–13) and late organ/recuperative phase (days 13+) [1,2,4]. Survivors may endure a prolonged convalescence marked by fatigue, myalgia, arthralgia, neurocognitive complaints, and sometimes uveitis or orchitis. Like Ebola survivors, Marburg survivors may harbor the virus in immune-privileged sites such as ocular fluid or semen for weeks to months, necessitating follow-up, counselling, and interim sexual precaution advice.

Definitive diagnosis of MVD relies on molecular diagnostics. Specimens require high biosafety handling, with inactivation and testing performed under stringent laboratory protocols with sample referral to higher-containment laboratories is routine in many settings. At present there is no licensed specific antiviral therapy or vaccine for MVD, although promising candidates are under development. Therefore, treatment is essentially supportive and centred on meticulous clinical and critical care. The case fatality rate (CFR) has been reported to range from about 24% to 90%, depending on the specific outbreak context, particularly the timeliness and quality of clinical management and the effectiveness of early case detection [2].

The ongoing outbreak in Ethiopia, reported in mid-November 2025, represents the country's first recognized Marburg event and may have significant regional and international implications. According to the WHO, by November 20, 2025, a total of six laboratory-confirmed MARV infections, three of whom have died (CFR 50 % among confirmed cases) [5]. Additionally, three patients with epidemiological links (probable cases) have died and samples could not be taken before death. Also, more than 200 contacts are under active follow-up.

Historically, after MARV first identification in 1967, many countries in Africa have experienced subsequent MVD outbreaks including the 1998–2000 in the Democratic Republic of the Congo, linked to a gold mine, which caused 154 cases and 128 deaths (CFR 83 %), and the 2004–2005 outbreak in Uige, Angola, which remains the largest documented Marburg outbreak, with 252 cases and 227 deaths (CFR 90.1 %). Following that, many African countries have experienced MVD outbreaks including Uganda (2012 and 2017), Ghana (2022), Equatorial Guinea and Tanzania (2023), Rwanda (2024), and Tanzania (early 2025) [1,2].

For Jordan, which hosts migrant workers from Ethiopia as part of its labor force, the ongoing MVD outbreak in Ethiopia necessitates careful but measured preparedness and response planning. The risk of importation is real but not extreme as MARV does not transmit before symptom onset, and severely ill patients are unlikely to travel, but mildly symptomatic or incubating travelers might still cross borders. Accordingly, Jordan's preparedness should therefore focus on strengthening detection and response capacity.

A logical starting point is a rapid risk assessment that considers direct flights and transit routes between Ethiopia and Jordan, the size and geographic distribution of migrant workers in the country, and the readiness of the health system, including isolation capacity, PPE stocks, and laboratory arrangements. Surveillance measures can be enhanced at both health facilities and points of entry (POE) with implementing syndromic screening algorithms for febrile illness with hemorrhagic features in travelers arriving from or transiting recent MVD-affected countries. Hospitals and primary healthcare centers should receive updated case definitions for suspected and probable MVD, and clinicians should be reminded to consider hemorrhagic fevers in patients with compatible symptoms and recent travel from affected areas.

In high-risk scenarios, such as sustained MARV transmission in Ethiopia combined with substantial labor migration flows to Jordan, implementing time-limited quarantine (21 days) for newly arriving migrant workers as a precautionary measure consistent with the known incubation period of MVD should be considered. During quarantine, individuals should undergo daily screening for early MVD manifestations, supported by clearly mapped referral pathways to designated treatment centers if symptoms develop. Concurrently, risk communication strategies should incorporate culturally and linguistically appropriate educational materials for travelers and migrant workers from Ethiopia and other countries with documented MVD outbreaks, explaining disease characteristics, modes of transmission, key warning symptoms, the critical importance of early care-seeking, and recommended preventive behaviors during travel. Employers of migrant workers should receive explicit guidance on how to respond when workers become ill, including facilitating rapid access to healthcare while safeguarding confidentiality, and avoiding any practices that might deter symptom reporting.
