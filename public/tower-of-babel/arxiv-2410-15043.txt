1.
Introduction and statement of the results

Let
\mu
be a fixed distribution in
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Under what conditions on
\mu
is the convolution
operator

c_{\mu}:f\rightarrow f*\mu

surjective as a map from
\mathcal{E}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
to
\mathcal{E}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, or from
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
to
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
? In general,
c_{\mu}
is not injective on
\mathcal{E}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
(take
\zeta
to be a zero of the Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}\mu
of
\mu
and observe that if
f=e^{i\langle\zeta,\cdot\rangle}
, then
c_{\mu}(f)=0
); it can be nevertheless surjective, as for instance when
c_{\mu}
is a constant coefficient differential operator, as proved by Ehrepnpreis
[
17
, Theorem 10]
.
This problem was also studied by Malgrange
[
26
]
for the aforementioned spaces of functions and distributions, when
\mu=D\delta_{0}
, or more generally when
\mu=\sum_{j}D_{j}\delta_{x_{j}}
where
D
and the
D_{j}
are constant coefficient differential operators, and
\{x_{j}\}
is a finite set of points in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. (Here
\delta_{x}
is the Delta distribution at
x
.)

In order to present some conditions which are equivalent to the surjectivity of
c_{\mu}
, we need first a definition:

Definition
1.1
.

We say that a function
u:\mathbb{C}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is slowly decreasing if there are positive constants
A
,
B
,
C
, and
D
such that

\sup\{|u(\zeta)|:\,\zeta\in\mathbb{C}^{n},\,\|\zeta-\xi\|\leq A\,\log(2+\|\xi)\|\}\geq B\,(C+\|\xi\|)^{-D}

for all
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Then, we have the following characterization on the surjectivity of convolution operators.

Theorem
1.2
.

(
[
18
]
, Section 2.) Let
\mu\in\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Then the following are equivalent:

(a)

The Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}\mu
is slowly decreasing.

(b)

The convolution operator
c_{\mu}
maps
\mathcal{E}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
onto
\mathcal{E}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

(c)

The convolution operator
c_{\mu}
maps
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
onto
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

(d)

There is a distribution
S\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that
S*\mu=\delta_{0}
.

(e)

The linear map
f*\mu\mapsto f
is continuous from
c_{\mu}(\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
to
\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Recently, this question has been a subject of investigation beyond the Euclidean setting, and more precisely on hyperbolic spaces, or more generally on arbitrary rank noncompact symmetric spaces
G/K
, see
[
9
]
and
[
19
]
. Owing to the underlying algebraic structure of these Riemannian manifolds, one can still define convolution operators, as well as a Fourier–type transform (the so-called spherical transform, or more generally, the Helgason–Fourier transform). Then, the surjectivity of
c_{\mu}=\ast\mu
on the space of smooth functions on
G/K
, where
\mu\in\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(G/K)
is bi-
K
-invariant, can be completely characterized in terms of the spherical transform of
\mu
. Applications include the surjectivity of certain mean value operators
[
9
]
, the proof of existence of fundamental solutions to invariant differential operators on
G/K
, first proved by Helgason in 1964
[
21
]
, or existence and uniqueness of a wave having three given snapshots at three different times on Euclidean space, noncompact symmetric spaces or the sphere
[
10
]
.

A harmonic
NA
group is a solvable Lie group with a canonical left Riemannian structure. It is well-known that as Riemannian manifolds, they contain noncompact Riemannian symmetric spaces of rank one
[
3
]
, but the latter form only a small subclass of these manifolds. Indeed, most of them are not symmetric, thus providing
numerous counterexamples to the Lichnerowicz conjecture
[
14
]
. It is natural, therefore, to consider the problem of surjectivity for convolution operators on harmonic
NA
groups, as well as interesting, since the concept of a radial function/distribution is not connected with any group action, and there is no group
K
acting transitively
on the distance spheres in
NA
. A challenge occurring from this fact is that the explicit form of the so-called
“angular Laplacian”
is not yet available, as emphasized in
[
7
,
8
]
. As a result, neither the full Paley–Wiener result for functions, which is essential to this work, is available; the state of the art on Paley–Wiener type results concerns
\mathfrak{v}
–radial functions, see Section
2
for details. Our main result is the following.

Theorem
1.3
.

Let
\mu
be a radial compactly supported distribution on a harmonic
NA
group. Then the right convolution operator
c_{\mu}:f\mapsto f*\mu
maps the space of smooth
\mathfrak{v}
-radial functions
\mathcal{E}^{\mathfrak{v}}(NA)
onto
\mathcal{E}^{\mathfrak{v}}(NA)
if and only if the spherical Fourier transform
\widetilde{\mu}(\lambda)
,
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
, is slowly decreasing.

Let us comment on the result above. The direction
“surjectivity implies slow decrease”
holds even for the whole space
\mathcal{E}(NA)
, without restricting to the subspace of
\mathfrak{v}
-radial functions, see Section
5
. For the other direction, this technical restriction is a consequence, as already mentioned, of the current state of the art on the Paley–Wiener theorem for compactly supported functions, and thus, of our result for compactly supported distributions in Section
3
.

This paper is organized as follows. After the present introduction in Section
1
and preliminaries in Section
2
, we prove in Section
3
that the slow decrease of the spherical Fourier transform
\widetilde{\mu}(\lambda)
is sufficient to ensure that the convolution operator
c_{\mu}=\,*\mu
is surjective on the space of smooth
\mathfrak{v}
-radial functions. To do so, we prove a Paley–Wiener theorem for compactly supported distributions, which is of independent interest. In Section
4
we prove that the slow decrease of
\widetilde{\mu}
is also necessary for the surjectivity of
c_{\mu}
on
\mathcal{E}^{\mathfrak{v}}(NA)
. Finally, in Section
5
, we obtain as an application the surjectivity of certain averages over spheres. These operators are the analog of mean value operators over translated
K
-orbits of a fixed point on symmetric spaces

,
[
9
]
.

Throughout this paper, the notation

between two positive expressions means that

such that
A\leq CB
. The notation

means that

.
Moreover, we use the following standard spaces on a smooth manifold

equipped with the topology of uniform convergence of all derivatives on every compact subset of

2.
Preliminaries

A harmonic
NA
group, also known as a Damek–Ricci space, is a solvable Lie group, equipped with a left-invariant metric. More precisely,
A\ltimes N
is a semi-direct product of a simply connected Lie group
A\simeq\mathbb{R}
with a Heisenberg type Lie group
N
. For more details, we refer the reader to the lecture notes
[
29
]
, as well as to the articles
[
1
]
,
[
3
]
,
[
11
]
and
[
15
]
. Generalizing hyperbolic
spaces, harmonic
NA
spaces provide a large class of examples of Riemannian
harmonic manifolds which are not symmetric spaces (for harmonic manifolds, see for instance
[
27
]
).
Extending to all harmonic
NA
spaces classical results about hyperbolic geometry and
harmonic analysis (geodesics, spherical functions, Radon
transform) entails new difficulties, because of the lack of the compact group
K
of
symmetric spaces
G/K
. For analogies and differences to symmetric spaces, we refer the reader to the important paper
[
11
]
.

Let us recall the structure of a Heisenberg type Lie group. Suppose that
\mathfrak{n}
is a two-step nilpotent Lie algebra, equipped with an inner product
\langle\;,\;\rangle
. Let us denote by
\mathfrak{z}
the center of
\mathfrak{n}
and by
\mathfrak{v}
the orthogonal complement of
\mathfrak{z}
in
\mathfrak{v}
(so that
[\mathfrak{v},\mathfrak{v}]\subset\mathfrak{z}
), with
k=\text{dim}\mathfrak{z}
and
m=\text{dim}\mathfrak{v}
.

Let
J_{Z}:\mathfrak{v}\rightarrow\mathfrak{v}
be the linear map defined by

\ \langle J_{Z}X,Y\rangle=\langle Z,[X,Y]\rangle,

for
X,Y\in\mathfrak{v},Z\in\mathfrak{z}
.
Then
\mathfrak{v}
is of
Heisenberg type
if the following condition is satisfied:

\ J_{Z}^{2}=-|Z|^{2}I\quad\text{ for every }Z\in\mathfrak{z}.

The corresponding connected Lie group
N
is then called of
Heisenberg type
, and we shall identify
N
with the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{n}
via the exponential map:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{v}\times\mathfrak{z}\rightarrow

\displaystyle N

\displaystyle(X,Z)\mapsto

\displaystyle\exp(X+Z).

In other words, we realize an
H
-type group
N
as
\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}
, for some
m,k\in\mathbb{N}
, via the exponential map. Under this identification
the Haar measure on
N
is the Lebesgue measure
dXdZ
.
Also, multiplication in
N\equiv\mathfrak{n}=\mathfrak{v}\oplus\mathfrak{z}
is given by

\ (X,Z)(X^{\prime},Z^{\prime})=(X+X^{\prime},Z+Z^{\prime}+\frac{1}{2}[X,X^{\prime}]).

Notice that each
J_{Z}
, with
Z\in\mathfrak{z}
a unit vector, induces a complex structure on
\mathfrak{v}
, since
J_{Z}^{2}=-I_{\mathfrak{v}}
. Therefore, we deduce that
m=\text{dim}\mathfrak{v}
is always even.

Consider the group
A=\mathbb{R}_{+}=\{a_{t}=e^{t}:\,t\in\mathbb{R}\}
and let
\mathfrak{a}
be its Lie algebra. Let
H
be a vector in
\mathfrak{a}
, acting on
\mathfrak{v}
with eigenvalue
1/2
, and on
\mathfrak{z}
with eigenvalue
1
; we
extend the inner product on
\mathfrak{n}
to the algebra
\mathfrak{n}\oplus\mathfrak{a}
, by requiring
\mathfrak{n}
and
\mathfrak{a}
to be
orthogonal and
H
to be a unit vector. The product in

N\ltimes\mathbb{R}_{+}
is given by

(2.1)

(X,Z,a)(X^{\prime},Z^{\prime},a^{\prime})=(X+a^{1/2}X^{\prime},Z+aZ^{\prime}+\frac{1}{2}a^{1/2}[X,X^{\prime}],aa^{\prime}),

(notice that
A
normalizes
N
, so
AN=NA
). Then,
S:=NA
is a solvable connected and simply connected Lie group, nonunimodular, with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{s}=\mathfrak{n}\oplus\mathfrak{a}=\mathfrak{v}\oplus\mathfrak{z}\oplus\mathbb{R}
and Lie bracket

\ [(X,Z,l),(X^{\prime},Z^{\prime},l^{\prime})]=(\frac{1}{2}lX^{\prime}-\frac{1}{2}l^{\prime}X,lZ^{\prime}-l^{\prime}Z+[X,X^{\prime}],0).

Let us endow
NA
with the left invariant Riemannian metric which agrees with the inner product on
\mathfrak{s}
at the identity and it is induced by

\ \langle(X,Z,l),(X^{\prime},Z^{\prime},l^{\prime})\rangle=\langle X,X^{\prime}\rangle+\langle Z,Z^{\prime}\rangle+ll^{\prime}

on
\mathfrak{s}
. Define
d
to be the distance induced by this Riemannian
structure. The Riemannian manifold
(NA,d)
is then called a
Damek-Ricci space
.

The associated left-invariant Haar measure on
NA
is given by

a^{-Q}{dX}{dZ}{\frac{da}{a}},

where
Q=\frac{m}{2}+k
is the
homogeneous dimension
of
N
, while, writing
a\in A
as
a=a_{t}=e^{t}
, the measure becomes

e^{-Qt}dXdZdt.

In fact, the only values that

and
k
can take can be seen in the following table,

where

and

are arbitrary integers.

Most Riemannian symmetric spaces
G/K
of noncompact type and rank one
fit into this framework. According to the Iwasawa decomposition
G=NAK
,
they can be realized indeed as
NA=AN
, with
A\simeq\mathbb{R}
. The group

is abelian for real hyperbolic spaces
\mathbb{H}^{N}(\mathbb{R})
and of Heisenberg type in the other cases
G/K=\mathbb{H}^{N}(\mathbb{C})
,
\mathbb{H}^{N}(\mathbb{H})
,
\mathbb{H}^{2}(\mathbb{O})
.
Notice that these classical examples form only a very small subclass of harmonic
NA
groups, as can be seen by checking the dimensions

, which are equal to
(0,N-1)
,
(1,2(N-1))
,
(3,4(N-1))
and
(7,8)
for

Remark
2.1
.

Since questions about surjectivity on real hyperbolic spaces
\mathbb{H}^{N}(\mathbb{R})
have been settled in
[
9
,
19
]
, we prefer to disregard these spaces in the present work.

Similarly to the case of hyperbolic spaces, this general group
S=NA
can be realized as the unit ball in
\mathfrak{s}
,

\ B(\mathfrak{s})=\{(X,Z,l)\in\mathfrak{s}:|X|^{2}+|Z|^{2}+l^{2}<1\},

through a
Cayley type transform
:

\displaystyle C:\quad S\quad

\displaystyle\rightarrow

\displaystyle\quad B(\mathfrak{s})

\displaystyle\quad x=(X,Z,a)\quad

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle\quad x^{\prime}=(X^{\prime},Z^{\prime},l^{\prime}),

where

\begin{cases}X^{\prime}=\{(1+a+\frac{1}{4}|X|^{2})^{2}+|Z|^{2}\}^{-1}\;\{(1+a+\frac{1}{4}|X|^{2})-J_{z}\}X,\\
Z^{\prime}=\{(1+a+\frac{1}{4}|X|^{2})^{2}+|Z|^{2}\}^{-1}\;2Z,\\
l^{\prime}\;=\{(1+a+\frac{1}{4}|X|^{2})^{2}+|Z|^{2}\}^{-1}\;\{(-1+(a+\frac{1}{4}|X|^{2}))^{2}+|Z|^{2}\}.\end{cases}

In the ball model
B(\mathfrak{s})
, the geodesics passing through the origin are the diameters. The geodesic distance to the origin is given by

(2.2)

r=d(x^{\prime},0)=\text{log}\frac{1+|x^{\prime}|}{1-|x^{\prime}|},\qquad\rho=|x^{\prime}|=\sqrt{|X^{\prime}|^{2}+|Z^{\prime}|^{2}+l^{\prime 2}}=\text{tanh}\frac{r}{2}.

The Riemannian volume is

\displaystyle dV

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2^{n}(1-\rho^{2})^{-Q-1}\rho^{n-1}d\rho d\sigma

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2^{m+k}(\text{sinh}\frac{r}{2})^{m+k}(\text{cosh}\frac{r}{2})^{k}drd\sigma,

where
d\sigma
denotes the surface measure on the unit sphere
\partial B(\mathfrak{s})
in
\mathfrak{s}
and
n=\text{dim}NA=m+k+1
,
[
15
, p.230]
,
[
29
, p.72]
.

The volume density in normal coordinates at the origin, and by translation
at any point, is a purely radial function, which means that
S
is a harmonic
manifold. The Lichnerowicz conjecture stated that every simply connected harmonic space is either flat or a rank one symmetric space. By construction, Damek-Ricci spaces are simply connected one-dimensional extensions of Heisenberg-type groups, they have nonpositive sectional curvature (thus, they are non-flat),
[
13
]
, but in general they are not symmetric. As a result, they provide counterexemples to the Lichnerowicz conjecture.

A function on
NA
is called radial if it depends only on the distance to the origin, i.e. if there
exists a function
f_{0}
on
[0,+\infty)
such that
f(x)=f_{0}(d(x,e))
for all
x\in NA
. For distributions, invariant differential
operators etc., radiality is defined by means of an averaging operator over spheres. More precisely, let
R:\mathcal{D}(NA)\rightarrow\mathcal{D}(NA)
be the linear operator defined by

(2.3)

Rf(x)=\int_{\mathbb{S}_{\rho}}f(y)\,d\sigma_{\rho}(y),\quad\rho=d(x,e),

where
d\sigma_{\rho}
is the surface measure induced by the left-invariant Riemannian metric on
the geodesic sphere
\mathbb{S}_{\rho}=\{y\in NA:d(y,e)=\rho\}
, normalized by
\int_{\mathbb{S}_{\rho}}d\sigma_{\rho}(y)=1
. Let

\mathcal{D}(NA)^{\#}
denote the subspace of radial functions in
\mathcal{D}(NA)
. Then
R
is a projection from

\mathcal{D}(NA)
onto
\mathcal{D}(NA)^{\#}
.

The following fundamental properties were established in
[
15
]
:

(i)

Convolution preserves radiality.

(ii)

Convolution is commutative on radial objects. In particular, the radial integrable functions on
NA
form a commutative Banach algebra
L^{1}(NA)^{\#}
under
convolution.

(iii)

The algebra
\mathbb{D}(NA)^{\#}
of invariant differential operators on
NA
which are radial
(i.e. which commute with the averaging operator) is a polynomial algebra with a single generator, the Laplace-Beltrami operator.

2.0.1.
\mathfrak{v}
-radial functions.

The aim of this part is to define

-radial functions, for technical reasons having to do with the fact that the Paley–Wiener Theorem for functions, which is essential to this work, has not been

We say that

if

Following

(\pi f)(X,Z)=\int_{S^{m-1}}f(|X|\omega,Z)\,d\sigma(\omega),

where

Let now

Then for

\displaystyle(f\ast g)(na)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{N}f(n^{\prime}a^{\prime})\,g(a^{\prime-1}n^{\prime-1}na)\,a^{-Q-1}\,dn^{\prime}da^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{e,a^{\prime}}\ast g_{a^{\prime-1},a}(n)\,a^{-Q-1}\,da^{\prime}.

If

NA
one can compute that

\mathbb{Y}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\varepsilon_{\ell}\mathbb{X}_{\ell}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\zeta_{i}\mathbb{X}_{m+i}+\eta\mathbb{X}_{0}.

Let
r=r(X,Z,a)=d((X,Z,a),e)
denote the Riemannian distance of
(X,Z,a)
to the origin. Using (
2.2
) on the ball model, the Cayley transform and the fact that
J_{Z}^{2}X=-|Z|^{2}X
,
|J_{Z}X|=|Z|\,|X|
and that
J_{Z}X
is orthogonal to
X
, one can get (see
[
29
, p.72]
)

\displaystyle\cosh^{2}\left(\frac{r}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{a^{1/2}+a^{-1/2}}{2}+\frac{1}{8}a^{-1/2}|X|^{2}\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{4}a^{-1}|Z|^{2}

(2.5)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}a^{-1}\left\{\left(1+a+\frac{1}{4}|X|^{2}\right)^{2}+|Z|^{2}\right\}.

Then, as proved in
[
6
, Lemma 3.4]
, it holds

(2.6)

|\mathbb{X}_{J}(\cosh r)|\lesssim\cosh r,\quad\forall J\in\{0,1,...,n-1\}^{\nu},\,\forall\nu\in\mathbb{N}.

The following result will be needed later.

Lemma
2.3
.

Let
na\in NA
such that
d(na,e)\leq R
. Then,

e^{-R}\leq a\leq e^{R},

while, writing
n=(X,Z)
, we have

|X|\lesssim e^{R/2},\quad|Z|\lesssim e^{R}.

Proof.

Since it holds
d(a,e)\leq d(na,e)
for all
n\in N
and
a\in A
, having
d(na,e)\leq R
implies by (
2.5
) that

\left(\frac{a^{1/2}+a^{-1/2}}{2}\right)^{2}\leq\cosh^{2}(R/2).

Since the left hand side rewrites as
\cosh^{2}(\log\sqrt{a})
, we get immediately that
e^{-R}\leq a\leq e^{R}
.

Let now
n=(X,Z)
. Then, due to (
2.5
), we have

\frac{1}{8}a^{-1/2}|X|^{2}\leq\cosh\frac{R}{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{1}{4}a^{-1}|Z|^{2}\leq\cosh^{2}\frac{R}{2},

which, using the fact that
a\leq e^{R}
, implies

|X|\lesssim e^{R/2}\text{ and }|Z|\lesssim e^{R}.

∎

2.1.
Fourier analysis

In this section we review Fourier analysis on harmonic
NA
groups, and we present Fourier-type transformations for general functions on
NA
, but mainly discuss the so-called spherical analysis, which concerns radial functions on
NA
.
As already mentioned, unlike the case of Riemannian symmetric spaces, the concept of a radial function is not connected with any group action and the elementary spherical functions are not necessarily matrix entries of irreducible unitary representations.

Let us start by defining the
Poisson kernel

\mathcal{P}
as follows,
[
11
, p.233]
: if
x=na
, then

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(x,n_{0})=\mathcal{P}(na,n_{0}):=P_{a}(n_{0}^{-1}n),

where, if
n=(X,Z)
, we define

(2.7)

\displaystyle P_{a}(n)

\displaystyle=P_{a}(X,Z):=c_{m,k}\,a^{Q}\left(\left(a+\frac{|X|^{2}}{4}\right)^{2}+|Z|^{2}\right)^{-Q}

\displaystyle=a^{-Q}P_{1}(a^{-1}na).

Here,
c_{m,k}=2^{k-1}\pi^{-n/2}\Gamma(n/2)
,
[
3
, Eq. (2.5)]
. Notice that

(2.8)

\int_{N}P_{a}(n)\,dn=1\quad\text{and}\quad P_{a}(n^{-1})=P_{a}(n)\quad\forall a\in A,

see
[
3
, p.410]
for more details.

Definition
2.4
.

(
[
3
, p.410]
)
The Helgason-Fourier transform of a function
f
in
\mathcal{D}(NA)
is given by

(2.9)

\widetilde{f}(\lambda,n_{0})=\int_{NA}f(x)\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(x,n_{0})\,dx,\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{C},\;n_{0}\in N,

where

\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(x,n_{0})=\left[\mathcal{P}(x,n_{0})\right]^{1/2-i\lambda/Q}.

An interesting property of the function
x\mapsto\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(x,n_{0})
is that it is constant on certain hypersurfaces which are analogs of hyperplanes in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and of horospheres in Riemannian symmetric spaces of noncompact type. We need to introduce the notion of the geodesic inversion to illustrate this feature. The geodesic inversion
\sigma:NA\rightarrow NA
is an involutive, measure-preserving diffeomorphism which is explicitly given by

\displaystyle\sigma(X,Z,a)

\displaystyle=\left(\left(e^{t}+\frac{|X|^{2}}{4}\right)^{2}+|Z|^{2}\right)^{-1}\left(\left(-\left(a+\frac{|X|^{2}}{4}\right)+J_{Z}\right)X,-Z,a\right),

see
[
29
]
. For
x=na_{t}\in NA
, let
A(x):=\log a_{t}=t
. Then (see
[
11
, p.233]
but also
[
28
, p.4273]
)

\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(x,n_{0})=e^{(\frac{Q}{2}-i\lambda)\,A(\sigma(n_{0}^{-1}x))}.

It is now clear that
\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(\cdot,n_{0})
is constant on the hypersurfaces
H_{n_{0},a_{t}}=\{n_{0}\sigma(a_{t}n):\,n\in N\}
.

A spherical function
\Phi
on
NA
is a radial eigenfunction of the Laplace-Beltrami operator normalized so that
\Phi(e)=1
. For
\lambda
in
\mathbb{C}
, we denote by
\varphi_{\lambda}
the spherical
function with eigenvalue
-(\lambda^{2}+Q^{2}/4)
. For all
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
, by
[
3
, Proposition 4.2]
, it has the following integral representation

(2.10)

\varphi_{\lambda}(x^{-1}y)=\int_{N}\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(x,n)\mathcal{P}_{-\lambda}(y,n)\,dn,\quad\text{for all}\quad x,y\in NA.

For
\alpha,\beta>-1/2
,
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
and
t\geq 0
, let
\phi_{\lambda}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)
denote the Jacobi function
defined by

\phi_{\lambda}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t):={}_{2}F_{1}\left(\frac{1}{2}(\alpha+\beta+1-i\lambda),\frac{1}{2}(\alpha+\beta+1+i\lambda);\alpha+1;-\sinh^{2}t\right)

where
{}_{2}F_{1}(a,b;c;z)
is the Gaussian hypergeometric function. (For a detailed account on Jacobi functions we refer to
[
25
]
.) It follows from properties of
{}_{2}F_{1}
that

\phi_{\lambda}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)=\phi_{-\lambda}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)
for
\alpha,\beta
and
\lambda
as above.
We recall that for specific parameters
\alpha,\beta
, Jacobi functions coincide with spherical
functions on
NA
, realized as functions on nonnegative real numbers through the polar decomposition of
NA
. More precisely, in
our parametrization, they are related in the following way (
[
3
, p.650]
):

\varphi_{\lambda}(t)=\phi_{2\lambda}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t/2)\quad\text{where}\quad\alpha=(m+k-1)/2,\quad\beta=(k-1)/2.

Definition
2.5
.

(
[
3
, p.408]
.)
The spherical transform of a radial function
f
in
\mathcal{D}(NA)
is given by

(2.11)

\widetilde{f}(\lambda):=\int_{NA}f(x)\varphi_{\lambda}(x)\,dx,\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{C}.

We next define the Abel transform, which maps radial functions
f\in\mathcal{D}(NA)
to even functions
\mathcal{A}f\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R})
according to the rule

\mathcal{A}f(t):=e^{-\frac{Q}{2}t}\int_{N}f(ne^{t})\,dn,

[
1
, p.650]
. The following formula describes a
“projection-slice”
relation between the Fourier and the spherical transform:

(2.12)

\widetilde{f}(\lambda)=(\mathcal{F}\circ\mathcal{A})(\lambda)=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}e^{-i\lambda t}\mathcal{A}f(t)\,dt,\quad f\in\mathcal{D}(NA)\quad\text{radial},

which will be essential to this work. Notice that the dual
\mathcal{A}^{*}
of the Abel transform is given by

\mathcal{A}^{*}f(x)=\mathcal{A}^{*}f(d(x,e))=\mathcal{A}^{*}f(r)=\int_{\mathbb{S}_{r}}e^{\frac{Q}{2}A(y)}\,f(A(y))\,d\sigma_{r}(y).

The dual Abel transform is a topological isomorphism between the space
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})_{\text{even}}
of smooth even functions on the real line and the space
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(NA)^{\#}
of smooth radial functions on
NA
,
[
2
, Theorem 2.1]
, due to the duality relation

\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\mathcal{A}f(r)g(r)\,dr=\int_{NA}f(x)\mathcal{A}^{*}g(x)\,dx.

In order to compare the Helgason-Fourier transform (
2.9
) with the spherical
transform (
2.11
) for radial functions on
NA
, let us recall
[
3
, Propostition 3.1]
, which states that for
f\in\mathcal{D}(NA)
radial, we have

(2.13)

\widetilde{f}(\lambda,n_{0})=\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(e,n_{0})\widetilde{f}(\lambda).

Finally, it is well-known that the convolution in
\mathcal{D}(NA)
is not commutative (unlike in its subset containing radial functions).
However, if the second factor is radial, we have the following result.

Proposition
2.6
.

(
[
3
, Proposition 3.2]
) If
f
,
g
are in
\mathcal{D}(NA)
and
g
is radial, then

\widetilde{(f*g)}(\lambda,n_{0})=\widetilde{f}(\lambda,n_{0})\widetilde{g}(\lambda),\quad\forall\lambda\in\mathbb{C},\;\forall n_{0}\in N.

We now recall the inversion formula for the
Fourier transform.

Theorem
2.7
.

(
[
3
, Theorem 4.4]
) Every function
f
in
\mathcal{D}(NA)
can be written as

(2.14)

f(x)=\frac{c_{m,k}}{4\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\int_{N}\mathcal{P}_{-\lambda}(x,n)\widetilde{f}(\lambda,n)|c(\lambda)|^{-2}\,d\lambda dn,\quad x\in NA,

where
|c(\lambda)|^{-2}
is the Plancherel density.

The Helgason-Fourier transform extends naturally to compactly supported distributions on
NA
: if
T\in\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(NA)
, then
\widetilde{T}
is the function on
\mathbb{C}\times N
given by

\widetilde{T}(\lambda,n_{0}):=T(\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(\cdot,n_{0})).

This definition is often written in integral notation as

(2.15)

\widetilde{T}(\lambda,n_{0})=\int_{NA}\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(x,n_{0})\,dT(x).

In the case that the distribution
T
is radial, we can consider its spherical Fourier transform, given by

(2.16)

\widetilde{T}(\lambda):=T(\varphi_{\lambda})=\int_{NA}\varphi_{\lambda}(x)\,dT(x).

Finally, for
T\in\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(NA)
and
f\in\mathcal{D}(NA)
radial, using arguments similar to those of
[
3
, Proposition 3.2]
, we have

(2.17)

\displaystyle\widetilde{(T*f)}(\lambda,n_{0})=\widetilde{T}(\lambda,n_{0})\widetilde{f}(\lambda).

Here, we used that if
T\in\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(NA)
and
f\in\mathcal{E}(NA)
, then
T*f
is the function in
\mathcal{E}(NA)
well-defined by the expression

(T*f)(x)=\int_{NA}f(y^{-1}x)\,dT(y).

Finally, given a function
F\in\mathcal{E}(\mathbb{R})
, define the function
\overline{F}
on
NA
by
\overline{F}(x):=F(A(x))
,
x\in NA
. For a radial distribution
\mu\in\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(NA)
, define the distribution
\overline{\mu}\in\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
by
\overline{\mu}(F)=\mu(\overline{F})
, for
F\in\mathcal{E}(\mathbb{R})
. Then, we define the Abel transform
\mathcal{A}\mu
of
\mu
by

\mathcal{A}\mu=e^{\frac{Q}{2}\cdot}\,\overline{\mu}.

The projection-slice theorem for radial distributions
\mu\in\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(NA)
now writes

\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{A}\mu)(\lambda)=\widetilde{\mu}(\lambda),\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{C}.

2.2.
Radon transform

Let
f\in\mathcal{D}(NA)
and define its Radon transform by

(2.18)

\widehat{f}(a,n_{0}):=e^{-\frac{Q}{2}\log a}\int_{N}f(n_{0}\sigma(na))\,dn.

(see
[
29
, Eq. (31)]
or
[
28
, Eq. (5.1)]
). Therefore, the map
f\mapsto\widehat{f}
is a continuous linear map from
\mathcal{D}(NA)
into
\mathcal{D}(A\times N)
. Its adjoint (of the first kind) is given by

\displaystyle\widecheck{f}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{N}g(n,A(\sigma(n^{-1}x)))\,\mathcal{P}_{0}(x,n)\,dn=\int_{N}g(n,A(\sigma(n^{-1}x)))\,e^{\frac{Q}{2}A(\sigma(n^{-1}x))}\,dn.

In fact, the following duality relation holds

[
28
, Proposition 5.2]
: if
f
is a measurable function on
NA
and
g
is a measurable function on
N\times\mathbb{R}
, then

\int_{N\times\mathbb{R}}\widehat{f}(e^{\log s},n)g(n,s)\,ds=\int_{NA}f(x)\widecheck{g}(x)\,dx.

Finally, for
f\in\mathcal{D}(NA)
, it holds

(2.19)

\mathcal{F}(\widehat{f}(\cdot,n))(\lambda)=\widetilde{f}(\lambda,n)

for
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
,
n\in N
,
[
28
, Theorem 5.3]
.

Passing to distributions, in order to derive a projection-slice theorem, we first define “restriction” maps
T\mapsto\widehat{T}_{n_{0}}
from
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(NA)
to
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
for each
n_{0}\in N
as follows: fix
n_{0}\in N
. Then for
F\in\mathcal{E}(\mathbb{R})
, consider the function
F^{n_{0}}\in\mathcal{E}(NA)
given by

(2.20)

F^{n_{0}}(x):=F(A(\sigma(n_{0}^{-1}x)))\,e^{\frac{Q}{2}A(\sigma(n_{0}^{-1}x))}.

Furthermore, for
n_{0}\in N
fixed, the function
F^{n_{0}}
is constant on the hyperplanes
H_{n_{0},a_{t}}=\{n_{0}\sigma(a_{t}n):\,n\in N\}
, and the map
F\mapsto F^{n_{0}}
is linear and continuous from
\mathcal{E}(\mathbb{R})
to
\mathcal{E}(NA)
. According to
[
28
, Proposition 5.2]
, the following duality relation holds true:

(2.21)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\widehat{f}(a_{t},n_{0})F(t)\,dt=\int_{NA}f(x)F^{n_{0}}(x)\,dx.

Let
T\in\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(NA)
. We define the map
T\mapsto\widehat{T}_{n_{0}}
to be the adjoint of the map
F\mapsto F^{n_{0}}
:

(2.22)

\widehat{T}_{n_{0}}(F)=T(F^{n_{0}})\quad(F\in\mathcal{E}(\mathbb{R})).

The map
T\mapsto\widehat{T}_{n_{0}}
is therefore a continuous linear map from
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(NA)
to
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
.
The projection-slice theorem for distributions thus reads as follows,

(2.23)

\widetilde{T}(\lambda,n_{0})=\mathcal{F}(\widehat{T}_{n_{0}})(\lambda).

Last, notice that if
T,\mu\in\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(NA)
and
\mu
is in addition radial, then

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}((\widehat{T*\mu})_{n_{0}})(\lambda)=\widetilde{T*\mu}(\lambda,n_{0})=\widetilde{T}(\lambda,n_{0})\widetilde{\mu}(\lambda)=\mathcal{F}(\widehat{T}_{n_{0}})(\lambda)\,\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{A}\mu)(\lambda),

where
\mathcal{A}\mu
is the Abel transform of the radial measure
\mu
, which in turn implies that

(2.24)

(\widehat{T*\mu})_{n_{0}}=\widehat{T}_{n_{0}}*{\mathcal{A}\mu}.

3.
Paley-Wiener type results

In this section we discuss some theorems of Paley-Wiener type, concerning functions and distributions on suitable classes.

3.1.
Paley-Wiener type results for functions.

We first provide some Paley–Wiener type results for functions describing the range of the spherical transform (
2.11
) and the Fourier transform (
2.9
).

Let
\mathcal{D}(NA)^{\#}
denote the space of radial, compactly supported
C^{\infty}
functions on
NA
. Then, we have the following result for the spherical transform,
[
25
, Theorem 2.1]
(see also
[
29
, Théorème 14]
).

Theorem
3.1
.

(
[
25
]
,
[
29
]
)
The spherical transform
u\mapsto\widetilde{u}
is a bijection of
\mathcal{D}(NA)^{\#}
onto the space of entire, even functions
f
of
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
, for which there exists a constant
A_{f}\geq 0
such that for all integers
j\geq 0
,

(3.25)

\sup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\,}e^{-A_{f}|\textrm{Im}\lambda|}(1+|\lambda|)^{j}|f(\lambda)|<\infty.

In addition, the support of
u
is contained in the closed ball
\overline{B}_{R}=\{x\in NA:\;d(x,e)\leq R\}
if and only if
\widetilde{u}
verifies (
3.25
) with
A_{\widetilde{u}}\leq R
.

We now pass to the general case, which concerns compactly supported functions which are not necessarily radial. To this end, let us introduce some terminology.

Definition
3.2
.

For fixed
R>0
, define
\mathcal{H}_{R}(\mathbb{C}\times N)
to be the space of
C^{\infty}
functions
\psi
on
\mathbb{C}\times N
, holomorphic in the first variable and such that for each integer
j\geq 0
,

(3.26)

\sup_{(\lambda,n_{0})\in\mathbb{C}\times N}P_{1}(n_{0})^{-1/2-(\textrm{Im}\lambda/Q)}\,e^{-R|\textrm{Im}\lambda|}\,(1+|\lambda|)^{j}|\,\psi(\lambda,n_{0})|<\infty,

where
P_{1}(n_{0})
is the Poisson kernel defined in (
2.7
).
Moreover, define
\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{C}\times N)
to be the space of all functions
\psi
in
\cup_{R>0}\mathcal{H}_{R}(\mathbb{C}\times N)
satisfying the condition

(3.27)

\int_{N}\mathcal{P}_{-\lambda}(x,n)\psi(\lambda,n)\,dn=\int_{N}\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(x,n)\psi(-\lambda,n)\,dn

for all
x\in NA
.

Then, the corresponding Paley–Wiener theorem for compactly supported
\mathfrak{v}
-radial
functions, related to the Helgason-Fourier transform (
2.9
) is the following.

Theorem
3.3
.

(
[
3
]
,
[
7
]
)
The Fourier transform
f(x)\mapsto\widetilde{f}(\lambda,n_{0})
is a bijection of
\mathcal{D}^{\mathfrak{v}}(NA)
onto the set of holomorphic functions
\mathfrak{v}
-radial in
n_{0}
, satisfying the conditions (
3.26
) and (
3.27
). Moreover,
\widetilde{f}
satisfies (
3.26
) if and only if
f
has support in the closed ball
\overline{B}_{R}=\{x\in NA:\;d(x,e)\leq R\}
.

The fact that
\text{supp}f\subset\overline{B}_{R}
implies that
\widetilde{f}
is holomorphic satisfying the size estimate (
3.26
) was proved in
[
3
, Theorem 4.5]
, without any restrictions on the symmetry of
f
.For the other direction, we refer to the works of Camporesi
[
7
]
,
[
8
]
: first, in
[
7
]
it is proved that if the function

(\lambda,n)\mapsto\psi(\lambda,n)
satisfies a uniform exponential type
R
bound in
\lambda
, together with a suitable
parity condition, and
\psi
is biradial in
n
, then
\psi
is the Helgason transform of a regular function
with support in the geodesic ball of radius
R
. This result is refined in
[
8
]
, relaxing
the biradiality condition in
n
to a
\mathfrak{v}
-radiality condition.

3.2.
Paley-Wiener type theorems for distributions

Our aim is to give a Paley-Wiener result for distributions, related to the Helgason-Fourier transform (
2.15
). To this end, let us introduce some necessary definitions.

Definition
3.4
.

A
C^{\infty}
function
\psi
on
\mathbb{C}\times N
, holomorphic in the first variable, is called a
holomorphic function of uniform exponential type and of slow growth
if there exist constants
R\geq 0
,
M\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
,
C>0
such that

(3.28)

|\psi(\lambda,n_{0})|\leq Ce^{R|\textrm{Im}\lambda|}\,(1+|\lambda|)^{M}\,P_{1}(n_{0})^{1/2+(\textrm{Im}\lambda/Q)},\;\lambda\in\mathbb{C},\;n_{0}\in N.

Given
R\geq 0
, let
\mathcal{K}_{R}(\mathbb{C}\times N)
denote the space of those functions
\psi
satisfying (
3.28
) and let
\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{C}\times N)
be their union over all
R\geq 0
. Finally, we denote by
\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{C}\times N)_{\text{even}}
the space of functions in
\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{C}\times N)
satisfying (
3.27
).

The next lemma, giving estimates of the derivatives of
\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(\cdot,n_{0})
,
n_{0}\in N
, is essential.

Lemma
3.5
.

Fix
R>0
and let
B_{R}=\{na:\,d(na,e)<R\}
. Then, for every multi-index
J
, there is a constant
c=c(R,J)>0
and an integer
M=M(J)
such that for all
na\in B_{R}
and
n_{0}\in N
,

|\mathbb{X}_{J}\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(na,n_{0})|\leq c\,e^{R|\textrm{Im}\lambda|}\,(1+|\lambda|)^{M}\,P_{1}(n_{0})^{1/2+(\textrm{Im}\lambda)/Q}.

Proof.

Recall that by Definition
2.4
,

\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(na,n_{0})=\left[P_{a}(n_{0}^{-1}n)^{-1/Q}\right]^{-Q/2+i\lambda}.

For every multi-index
J\in\{0,...,n-1\}^{\nu}
, one can straightforwardly compute that the derivatives
\mathbb{X}_{J}\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(\cdot,n_{0})
,
n_{0}\in N
, have the following expansion,

(3.29)

\displaystyle\mathbb{X}_{J}\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(na,n_{0})=\sum_{s=1}^{\nu}P_{s}(\lambda)\,\left[P_{a}(n_{0}^{-1}n)^{-1/Q}\right]^{-Q/2+i\lambda-s}\,D_{\nu+1-s}(P_{a}(n_{0}^{-1}n)^{-1/Q}).

Here
P_{s}(\lambda)
is a polynomial in
\lambda
of degree
s
, while for a smooth function
f
on
NA
, we denote

D_{1}f:=\prod_{i=1}^{\nu}\mathbb{X}_{(i)}f,

where
\mathbb{X}_{(i)}
denote the components of
\mathbb{X}_{J}
,

D_{\nu}f:=\mathbb{X}_{J}f,

and
D_{\nu+1-s}f
,
s=2,...,\nu-1
, is a linear combination of products of the form

\mathbb{X}_{I_{1}}f...\mathbb{X}_{I_{s}}f,\quad\mathbb{X}_{I_{r}}\in\{0,,...,n-1\}^{|I_{r}|},\quad r=1,...,s,

with
|I_{1}|+...+|I_{s}|=|J|=\nu
,
|I_{1}|,...,|I_{s}|\geq 1
, but at least one of
|I_{1}|,...,|I_{s}|
is strictly larger than
1
(in other words, at least one of those derivatives is of order higher than one).

Therefore, in view of (
3.29
), it suffices to show that for
d(na,e)<R
, and any
s\in\{1,...\nu\}
, it holds

|P_{s}(\lambda)|\lesssim_{s,R}(1+|\lambda|)^{s},

(3.30)

\left|\left[P_{a}(n_{0}^{-1}n)^{-1/Q}\right]^{-Q/2+i\lambda}\right|=|\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(na,n_{0})|\lesssim_{s,R}e^{R|\textrm{Im}\lambda|}P_{1}(n_{0})^{1/2+(\textrm{Im}\lambda)/Q},

and

(3.31)

\left|\left[P_{a}(n_{0}^{-1}n)^{-1/Q}\right]^{-s}D_{\nu+1-s}(P_{a}(n_{0}^{-1}n)^{-1/Q})\right|\lesssim_{s,R}1,

whence the Lemma follows.
We stress that the implied constants can depend on
s
,
R
and the geometry, but not on
n_{0}
.

The claim for the upper bound of the polynomials
P_{s}(\lambda)
is obvious. Thus, let us now prove (
3.30
).
Recall first by
[
4
, Corollary 3.2]
that

e^{-Qd(na,e)}\leq\frac{P_{a}(n_{0}^{-1}n)}{P_{1}(n_{0}^{-1})}=\frac{\mathcal{P}(na,n_{0})}{\mathcal{P}(e,n_{0})}\leq e^{Qd(na,e)}.

Therefore, taking into account that
d(na,e)\leq R
, we obtain indeed

\displaystyle|\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(na,n_{0})|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(na,n_{0})}{\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(e,n_{0})}\right||\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(e,n_{0})|

\displaystyle=\left|\left(\frac{P_{a}(n_{0}^{-1}n)}{P_{1}(n_{0}^{-1})}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}-i\frac{\lambda}{Q}}\right||\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(e,n_{0})|

(3.32)

\displaystyle\leq e^{QR/2}\,e^{R\,|\textrm{Im}\lambda|}\,P_{1}(n_{0})^{1/2+(\textrm{Im}\lambda)/Q}.

It remains to prove (
3.31
). To do so, let us start with some pointwise estimates of
\mathcal{P}(\cdot,n_{0})^{-1/Q}
. We claim that if
d(na,e)\leq R
and
r=d(n_{0}^{-1}na,e)
, then

(3.33)

e^{-2R}\cosh r\lesssim P_{a}(n_{0}^{-1}n)^{-1/Q}\lesssim\cosh r.

Indeed, to show the right-hand side inequality, it suffices to use the distance formula (
2.5
) with the Poisson kernel formula (
2.7
) to immediately get

P_{a}(n_{0}^{-1}n)^{-1/Q}\lesssim\cosh r.

For the opposite direction, observe first that by Lemma
2.3
we have

\frac{1+a}{a}\leq e^{R}+1\leq 2e^{R}.

Then, since for
\alpha,\beta>0
having

\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\leq M
for some
M\geq 1
, implies
\frac{\alpha+\gamma}{\beta+\gamma}\leq M
for all
\gamma\geq 0,

we get

\frac{\left(1+a+\frac{1}{4}|X|^{2}\right)^{2}}{\left(a+\frac{1}{4}|X|^{2}\right)^{2}}\leq 4e^{2R},

which in turn yields the left-hand side of (
3.33
).

It remains therefore to deal with the derivatives of
\mathcal{P}(\cdot,n_{0})^{-1/Q}
. Observe first that if
x=(X,Z,a)
and
d=d(x,e)
, then (
2.5
) implies that

2\cosh d=a^{-1}\left\{\left(a+\frac{|X|^{2}}{4}\right)^{2}+|Z|^{2}\right\}+a^{-1}\left(1+\frac{|X|^{2}}{2}\right).

Therefore, by (
2.7
), for
n=(X,Z)
and
n_{0}=(X_{0},Z_{0})
, we have that

c_{m,k}^{1/Q}\,P_{a}(n_{0}^{-1}n)^{-1/Q}=-a^{-1}\left(1+\frac{|X-X_{0}|^{2}}{2}\right)+2\cosh r,\quad r=d(n_{0}^{-1}na,e).

It follows that for all
\mu\in\mathbb{N}
, using the notation of (
2.4
), we have

\displaystyle c_{m,k}^{1/Q}\,\mathbb{X}_{0}^{\mu}(P_{a}(n_{0}^{-1}n)^{-1/Q})

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\mu-1}a^{-1}\left(1+\frac{|X-X_{0}|^{2}}{2}\right)+2\mathbb{X}_{0}^{\mu}\cosh r.

For derivatives coming from
\mathfrak{z}
, we compute

\displaystyle c_{m,k}^{1/Q}\,\mathbb{X}_{m+i}(P_{a}(n_{0}^{-1}n)^{-1/Q})

\displaystyle=2\mathbb{X}_{m+i}\cosh r,\quad i=1,...,k,

while for derivatives coming from
\mathfrak{v}
, we have

\displaystyle c_{m,k}^{1/Q}\,\mathbb{X}_{\ell}(P_{a}(n_{0}^{-1}n)^{-1/Q})

\displaystyle=-a^{-1/2}(X_{\ell}-X_{0\ell})+2\mathbb{X}_{\ell}\cosh r,

\displaystyle c_{m,k}^{1/Q}\,\mathbb{X}_{\ell^{\prime}}\mathbb{X}_{\ell}(P_{a}(n_{0}^{-1}n)^{-1/Q})

\displaystyle=-\delta_{\ell^{\prime}\ell}+2\mathbb{X}_{\ell^{\prime}}\mathbb{X}_{\ell}\cosh r,\quad\ell,\ell^{\prime}=1,...,m,

where
\delta_{\ell^{\prime}\ell}=1
, if
\ell^{\prime}=\ell
, and
0
otherwise (here,
X_{\ell}
and
X_{0\ell}
denote the
\ell
coordinates of
X
and
X_{0}
, respectively, in the basis
\{e_{1},...,e_{m}\}
). Also, observe that these derivatives are enough for our purposes, as any higher order derivatives of
\mathcal{P}(\cdot,n_{0})^{-1/Q}
will consist of linear combinations of the terms
a^{-1}\left(1+\frac{|X-X_{0}|^{2}}{2}\right)
and
a^{-1/2}(X_{\ell}-X_{0\ell})
, as well as of derivatives of
\cosh r
, so no additional terms to be controlled will appear.

We are now ready to prove (
3.31
). First, observe first that all derivatives of
\cosh r
can be bounded above by a multiple of
\cosh r
, due to (
2.6
).
Next, by the distance formula (
2.5
), since
r=d(n_{0}^{-1}na,e)
, we obtain

\frac{1}{4}a^{-1}\left(1+\frac{|X-X_{0}|^{2}}{4}\right)^{2}\leq\cosh^{2}\left(\frac{r}{2}\right).

This also yields on the one hand, that

a^{-1}\left(1+\frac{|X-X_{0}|^{2}}{2}\right)\leq a^{-1}\left(1+\frac{|X-X_{0}|^{2}}{4}\right)^{2}\leq 4\cosh^{2}\left(\frac{r}{2}\right),

and on the other hand, that

a^{-1/2}|X_{\ell}-X_{0\ell}|\leq a^{-1/2}|X-X_{0}|\leq a^{-1/2}\left(1+\frac{|X-X_{0}|^{2}}{4}\right)\leq 2\cosh\left(\frac{r}{2}\right).

Altogether, the above computations give the following upper bounds,

(3.34)

|\mathbb{X}_{J^{\prime}}(P_{a}(n_{0}^{-1}n)^{-1/Q})|\lesssim_{J^{\prime}}\cosh r,\qquad r=d(n_{0}^{-1}na,e).

for any
J^{\prime}\in\{0,...,n-1\}^{|J^{\prime}|}
. In other words, the order of the derivative of
P_{a}(n_{0}^{-1}n)^{-1/Q}
does not quantitatively affect the size of the upper bound in (
3.34
). Therefore

(3.35)

D_{\nu+1-s}(P_{a}(n_{0}^{-1}n)^{-1/Q})\lesssim(\cosh r)^{s}.

By (
3.33
) and (
3.35
) we conclude that for any
s\in\{1,...,\nu\}
,

(3.36)

\displaystyle\left|\left[P_{a}(n_{0}^{-1}n)^{-1/Q}\right]^{-s}D_{\nu+1-s}(P_{a}(n_{0}^{-1}n)^{-1/Q})\right|\lesssim e^{2sR}\lesssim_{s,R}1,

that is, we have proved (
3.31
). The proof of the Lemma is now complete.
∎

The following proposition yields a Paley–Wiener theorem for
\mathfrak{v}
-radial compactly supported distributions (the space of which will be denoted by
\mathcal{E}_{\mathfrak{v}}^{\prime}(NA)
) related to the Helgason-Fourier transform (
2.15
). Its analog on symmetric spaces of noncompact type can be found in
[
22
, Corollary 5.9]
. (See also
[
12
]
and
[
16
]
for the Paley–Wiener theorem for distributions on noncompact symmetric spaces.)

Proposition
3.6
.

The Helgason-Fourier transform
T\mapsto\widetilde{T}
is a linear bijection of
\mathcal{E}_{\mathfrak{v}}^{\prime}(NA)
onto the subspace of
\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{C}\times N)_{\text{even}}
functions which are
\mathfrak{v}
-radial in the second variable. Moreover,
\widetilde{T}
satisfies (
3.28
) if and only if
\text{supp}T\subset\overline{B}_{R}
.

Proof.

We follow the approach of
[
22
, Corollary 5.9]
. Let
\eta_{\epsilon}\in\mathcal{D}(NA)
be radial, positive, such that
\int\eta_{\epsilon}=1
and
\text{supp}(\eta_{\epsilon})\subset\overline{B}_{\epsilon}(e)
. Then

\widetilde{\eta_{\epsilon}}(\lambda)=\int_{B_{\epsilon}(e)}\eta_{\epsilon}(x)(\varphi_{\lambda}(x)-1)\,dx+1.

Then,
\widetilde{\eta_{\epsilon}}(\lambda)\rightarrow 1
, as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
, uniformly on compact sets. If
T\in\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(NA)
, then by
(\ref{conv-mult})
we have
\widetilde{T*\eta_{\epsilon}}(\lambda,n_{0})=\widetilde{T}(\lambda,n_{0})\widetilde{\eta_{\epsilon}}(\lambda)
, so for every
n_{0}\in N
,
\lambda\mapsto\widetilde{T}(\lambda,n_{0})
is holomorphic. Further,
T\mapsto\widetilde{T}
is injective and
\widetilde{T}
satisfies (
3.27
). Last,
n_{0}\mapsto\widetilde{T}(\lambda,n_{0})
is
\mathfrak{v}
-radial in
n_{0}
(see the method of
“descend”
to a hyperbolic space,
[
8
, Lemma 3.1]
).

We first show that
\lambda\mapsto\widetilde{T}(\lambda,n_{0})
satisfies (
3.28
) when
\text{supp}T\subset\overline{B}_{R}
. Since
T\in\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(NA)
, by continuity we can find differential operators
D_{i}\in\textbf{D}(N)
,
E_{i}\in\textbf{D}(A)
such that

(3.37)

|T(\zeta)|\leq\sum_{\text{finite}}\sup_{x}|\tau(D_{i})\tau(E_{i})(\zeta)(x)|,\quad\text{for}\quad\zeta\in\mathcal{D}(NA).

Here, we used the notation

(\tau(X_{1}...X_{\ell})f)(na)=\left\{\frac{\partial^{\ell}}{\partial t_{1}...\partial t_{\ell}}f(na(\exp t_{1}X_{1})...(\exp t_{\ell}X_{\ell}))\right\}_{t_{1}=\cdots=t_{\ell}=0}

for
X_{1},...,X_{\ell}\in\mathfrak{n}\oplus\mathfrak{a}.

Consider a function
\Psi\in\mathcal{E}(\mathbb{R})
such that
0\leq\Psi\leq 1
,
\Psi\equiv 1
on
(-\infty,1/2)
and
\Psi\equiv 0
on
[1,\infty)
. Fix
n_{0}\in N
and define

x\mapsto\Psi_{\lambda}(x)=\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(x,n_{0})\,\Psi(|\lambda|(d(x,e)-R)).

Then
\Psi_{\lambda}\in\mathcal{D}(NA)
and
\Psi_{\lambda}(x)=\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(x,n_{0})
in a neighborhood
B_{R^{\prime}}
of
\overline{B}_{R}
. Therefore,
\widetilde{T}(\lambda,n_{0})=T(\Psi_{\lambda})
which we estimate by (
3.37
) with
\zeta=\Psi_{\lambda}
. The support of
\Psi_{\lambda}
equals
d(x,e)\leq R+|\lambda|^{-1}
. Let
|\lambda|\geq 1
. Notice that each
(X,Z)\in\mathfrak{n}
defines a left invariant operator related to a linear combination of the derivatives
\mathbb{X}_{1},...,\mathbb{X}_{n-1}
; in the same way, each
\log a\in\mathfrak{a}
defines a left invariant operator related to a multiple of
\mathbb{X}_{0}
(see Remark
2.2
).
Thus, by Lemma
3.5
, we get that for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
, there is
M=M(i)\in\mathbb{Z}
such that

|\tau(D_{i})\tau(E_{i})(\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(\cdot,n_{0}))(x)|\leq C_{R,i}\,e^{(R+|\lambda|^{-1})|\textrm{Im}\lambda|}\,(1+|\lambda|)^{M}\,P_{1}(n_{0})^{1/2+(\textrm{Im}\lambda)/Q},

for all
x\in NA
such that
d(x,e)\leq R+|\lambda|^{-1}
, while the constant
C_{R,i}
does not depend on
n_{0}
. Also, it is clear that derivatives of
\Psi(|\lambda|(d(\cdot,e)-R))
introduce polynomials in
|\lambda|
. Therefore, since the index
i
runs in a finite subset of
\mathbb{N}
, we conclude that
\widetilde{T}(\lambda,n_{0})
satisfies (
3.28
) for all
|\lambda|\geq 1
, thus for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
.

For the converse direction, let
\psi
be a function on
\mathbb{C}\times N
which is
\mathfrak{v}
-radial in the second variable, and satisfies (
3.28
) and (
3.27
). Then the assignment

f\rightarrow T(f)=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\int_{N}\mathcal{P}_{-\lambda}(e,n)\psi(\lambda,n)\widetilde{f}(-\lambda,n)|c(\lambda)|^{-2}\,dnd\lambda

is a well defined functional on
\mathcal{D}(NA).
To see this, observe on the one hand that for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
there exists
C_{j}>0
such that

|\psi(\lambda,n)||\widetilde{f}(-\lambda,n)||c(\lambda)|^{-2}\leq C_{j}(1+|\lambda|)^{-j}P_{1}(n)^{1/2}

for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and
n\in N
, where we used the bound (
3.28
) for
\psi
, the Paley–Wiener result (
3.26
) for
f
, and the fact that the Plancherel density
|c(\lambda)|^{-2}
has polynomial growth on the real line (see for instance
[
1
, p.653]
). On the other hand, we have

\int_{N}|\mathcal{P}_{-\lambda}(e,n)|P_{1}(n)^{1/2}\,dn=\int_{N}P_{1}(n)\,dn=1,

for
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, by (
2.8
). This shows that
|T(f)|<\infty
for all
f\in\mathcal{D}(NA).

Now, taking into account the upper bounds of
\psi
given in (
3.28
), and those of
\eta_{\epsilon}
given in (
3.25
) (recall that
\eta_{\epsilon}
is radial, supported on
\overline{B}_{\epsilon}
), we get that for every
M\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, there is a constant
c_{M}
such that

|\psi(\lambda,n)\widetilde{\eta_{\epsilon}}(\lambda)|\leq c_{M}(1+|\lambda|)^{-M}\,e^{(R+\epsilon)|\textrm{Im}(\lambda)|}\,P_{1}(n)^{1/2+(\textrm{Im}\lambda/Q)}.

Therefore, since the map
n\mapsto\psi(\lambda,n)\widetilde{\eta}(\lambda)
is
\mathfrak{v}
-radial, Theorem
3.3
implies that
\psi\widetilde{\eta_{\epsilon}}
is the Helgason–Fourier transform of a function
f_{\epsilon}\in\mathcal{D}^{\mathfrak{v}}(NA)
, supported in
B_{R+\epsilon}
. The Plancherel formula (see for instance
[
3
, Theorem 5.1]
), however, implies that this test function considered as a distribution must be
T_{\epsilon}
, with
\text{supp}T_{\epsilon}\subset B_{R+\epsilon}
. But since
\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\widetilde{\eta_{\epsilon}}(\lambda)=1
uniformly on compacts, it follows that
\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}T_{\epsilon}(f)=T(f)
for all
f\in\mathcal{D}(NA)
, so
\text{supp}T\subset B_{R}
. Finally, we verify that the Helgason–Fourier transform of
T
coincides with
\psi
:

\displaystyle\widetilde{T}(\lambda,n)

\displaystyle=\int_{NA}\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(x,n)\,dT(x)=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\int_{NA}\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(x,n)\,dT_{\epsilon}(x)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\int_{NA}\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(x,n)f_{\epsilon}(x)\,dx=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\psi(\lambda,n)\widetilde{\eta_{\epsilon}}(\lambda)=\psi(\lambda,n).

The proof is now complete.
∎

Remark
3.7
.

The Paley–Wiener Theorem for radial distributions implies that the Abel transform is a linear bijection from
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(NA)^{\#}
onto
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})_{\text{even}}
, and in fact we have a commutative diagram of linear bijections:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})_{\text{even}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(NA)^{\#}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{C})_{\text{even}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{F}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{A}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\widetilde{}}

Here
\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{C})_{\text{even}}
is the vector space of slowly increasing even holomorphic functions on
\mathbb{C}
of exponential type.

4.
Slow decrease implies surjectivity

Our aim in this section is to give a sufficient condition for the surjectiviy of convolution operators on the space of smooth functions. More precisely, suppose that
\mu\in\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(NA)
is radial and let
c_{\mu}
be the convolution operator on
\mathcal{E}(NA)
given by
c_{\mu}(f)=f*\mu
. Let us denote be
\mathcal{E}^{\mathfrak{v}}(NA)
and
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}_{\mathfrak{v}}(NA)
the
\mathfrak{v}
-radial subspaces of
\mathcal{E}(NA)
and
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(NA)
, respectively. Then the main result of this section is the following.

Theorem
4.1
.

Let
\mu
be a radial distribution in
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(NA)
whose spherical Fourier transform
\widetilde{\mu}(\lambda)
is a slowly decreasing function on
\mathbb{C}
. Then the convolution operator
c_{\mu}=\,\ast\mu:\mathcal{E}^{\mathfrak{v}}(NA)\rightarrow\mathcal{E}^{\mathfrak{v}}(NA)
is surjective.

Notice that since
\mu
is radial, the fact that
\lambda\mapsto\varphi_{\lambda}
is even combined with (
2.16
), implies that
\widetilde{\mu}
is even. Also, by Proposition
3.6
,
\widetilde{\mu}(\lambda)
is of exponential type and slow growth in
\mathbb{C}
.

Proof.

Assume that
\widetilde{\mu}
is slowly decreasing.
Since
\mu
is radial, the adjoint map to
c_{\mu}:\mathcal{E}(NA)\rightarrow\mathcal{E}(NA)
is

c_{\mu}:\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(NA)\rightarrow\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(NA),\quad T\mapsto T*\mu.

Indeed, the adjoint of
c_{\mu}
would correspond to a convolution operator
c_{\mu^{*}}
with the distribution
\mu^{*}
being the reflection of
\mu
with respect to the reflection
na\mapsto a^{-1}n^{-1}
. In view of radiality, the two distributions coincide.

Therefore, it will be sufficient to show the latter map is injective and has closed range in the strong (and hence weak*) topology on
\mathcal{E}_{\mathfrak{v}}^{\prime}(NA)
. Indeed, the theorem will then follow from the fact that a continuous linear map
\Phi:E\rightarrow F
between two Fréchet spaces is surjective if and only if its adjoint
\Phi^{*}:F^{\prime}\rightarrow E^{\prime}
is injective and has a weak* closed range in
E^{\prime}
.

Let us first show that the map
c_{\mu}
is injective. The set of all
\lambda
for which
\widetilde{\mu}(\lambda)\neq 0
is open and dense in
\mathbb{C}
, and therefore
T*\mu=0
implies that

\widetilde{T}(\lambda,n_{0})\widetilde{\mu}(\lambda)=0,\quad(\lambda,n_{0})\in\mathbb{C}\times N,

from which we obtain
\widetilde{T}(\lambda,n_{0})=0
, hence
T\equiv 0
.

Next, we claim that the range
c_{\mu}(\mathcal{E}_{\mathfrak{v}}^{\prime}(NA))
is equal to

(4.38)

\{T\in\mathcal{E}_{\mathfrak{v}}^{\prime}(NA):\;\widetilde{T}(\lambda,n_{0})/\widetilde{\mu}(\lambda)\text{ is holomorphic in }\lambda\text{ for each }n_{0}\in N\}

and further, that the latter set is a closed subset in
\mathcal{E}_{\mathfrak{v}}^{\prime}(NA)
.

First of all, the fact that the range
c_{\mu}(\mathcal{E}_{\mathfrak{v}}^{\prime}(NA))
is contained in the set (
4.38
) follows by the observation that if
T_{1},T_{2}\in\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(NA)
where
T_{1}
is
\mathfrak{v}
-radial and
T_{2}
is radial, then
T_{1}*T_{2}
is
\mathfrak{v}
-radial and
\widetilde{(T_{1}*T_{2})}(\lambda,n_{0})=\widetilde{T_{1}}(\lambda,n_{0})\widetilde{T_{2}}(\lambda)
.
Suppose now that
T
belongs to the set (
4.38
). Since
\widetilde{T}(\lambda,n_{0})/\widetilde{\mu}(\lambda)
is holomorphic for each fixed
n_{0}
,
[
9
, Proposition A.1]
implies that
\widetilde{T}(\lambda,n_{0})/\widetilde{\mu}(\lambda)
is smooth on
\mathbb{C}\times N
. Also, since for every fixed
n_{0}
the function
\lambda\mapsto P_{1}(n_{0})^{1/2-i(\lambda/Q)}
is also holomorphic and nowhere vanishing, we deduce that the function
P_{1}(n_{0})^{-1/2+i(\lambda/Q)}\widetilde{T}(\lambda,n_{0})/\widetilde{\mu}(\lambda)
is also smooth on
\mathbb{C}\times N
. In addition, by the forward Paley–Wiener Theorem on
NA
, there exist
C,R>0
and
M\in\mathbb{N}
(neither of which depends on
n_{0}
) such that
\widetilde{T}
satisfies the growth condition

|\widetilde{T}(\lambda,n_{0})|\leq Ce^{R|\textrm{Im}\lambda|}\,(1+|\lambda|)^{M}\,P_{1}(n_{0})^{1/2+(\textrm{Im}\lambda/Q)},\;\lambda\in\mathbb{C},

for all
n_{0}\in N.
By
[
9
, Proposition 2.5]
, there exist
C^{\prime},R^{\prime}>0
and
M^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\left|\frac{P_{1}(n_{0})^{-1/2+(i\lambda/Q)}\,\widetilde{T}(\lambda,n_{0})}{\widetilde{\mu}(\lambda)}\right|\leq C^{\prime}(1+|\lambda|)^{M^{\prime}}e^{R^{\prime}|\textrm{Im}\lambda|},\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{C}

for all
n_{0}\in N
. Therefore,
\widetilde{T}(\lambda,n_{0})/\widetilde{\mu}(\lambda)\in\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{C}\times N)
and it remains to show that this quotient also belongs to
\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{C}\times N)_{\text{even}}
. In other words, it remains to show that it verifies
(
3.27
)
.

By assumption,
\widetilde{T}\in\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{C}\times N)
, which means that

\int_{N}\mathcal{P}_{-\lambda}(x,n_{0})\widetilde{T}(\lambda,n_{0})\,dn_{0}=\int_{N}\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(x,n_{0})\widetilde{T}(-\lambda,n_{0})\,dn_{0}.

The fact that
\widetilde{\mu}(\lambda)
is even gives

\int_{N}\mathcal{P}_{-\lambda}(x,n_{0})\frac{\widetilde{T}(\lambda,n_{0})}{\widetilde{\mu}(\lambda)}\,dn_{0}=\int_{N}\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(x,n_{0})\frac{\widetilde{T}(-\lambda,n_{0})}{\widetilde{\mu}(-\lambda)}\,dn_{0}

for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
for which
\widetilde{\mu}(\lambda)\neq 0
. In fact, this relation extends to all
\mathbb{C}
by continuity, the density of
\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}:\,\widetilde{\mu}(\lambda)\neq 0\}
in
\mathbb{C}
, and the fact that the integrands above are uniformly continuous on compact sets.

Having shown that
\widetilde{T}(\lambda,n_{0})/\widetilde{\mu}(\lambda)\in\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{C}\times N)_{\text{even}}
, since
n_{0}\mapsto\widetilde{T}(\lambda,n_{0})/\widetilde{\mu}(\lambda)
is
\mathfrak{v}
-radial in
n_{0}\in N
, the Paley–Wiener result of Proposition
3.6
for distributions implies the existence of a distribution
S\in\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(NA)
such that
\widetilde{S}(\lambda,n_{0})=\widetilde{T}(\lambda,n_{0})/\widetilde{\mu}(\lambda)
. In other words,
S=T*\mu
, proving the range characterization (
4.38
).

Finally we prove that the set in (
4.38
) is a closed subset of
\mathcal{E}_{\mathfrak{v}}^{\prime}(NA)
.
Owing to the intertwining property of the Abel transform discussed in Remark
3.7
, the problem now becomes Euclidean: indeed, let us denote by
\mathcal{A}\mu
the Abel transform of the radial distribution
\mu\in\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(NA)
. Then
\mathcal{A}\mu\in\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
has a slowly decreasing Euclidean Fourier transform on
\mathbb{C}
. Hence by the results of Ehrenpreis and Hörmander (for a unified version of the results, see
[
9
, Theorem 2.3]
), it follows that the convolution operator
v\mapsto v*\mathcal{A}{\mu}
on
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
has closed range
c_{\mathcal{A}{\mu}}(\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}))
.
Then by the characterization (
4.38
), (
2.23
), and (
2.24
), we conclude that

c_{\mu}(\mathcal{E}_{\mathfrak{v}}^{\prime}(NA))=\{T\in\mathcal{E}_{\mathfrak{v}}^{\prime}(NA):\,\widehat{T}_{n_{0}}\in c_{\mathcal{A}{\mu}}(\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}))\text{ for all }n_{0}\in N\}.

For each
n_{0}\in N
, the linear map
T\mapsto\widehat{T}_{n_{0}}
from
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(NA)
to
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
is continuous. Therefore,
c_{\mu}(\mathcal{E}_{\mathfrak{v}}^{\prime}(NA))
is a closed subspace of
\mathcal{E}_{\mathfrak{v}}^{\prime}(NA)
, since
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})*\mathcal{A}{\mu}
is closed in
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
. The proof is now complete.
∎

5.
Surjectivity implies slow decrease

Let
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(NA)^{\#}
denote the space of radial compactly supported distributions in
NA
. For
\mu\in\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(NA)^{\#}
fixed, let
c_{\mu}:\mathcal{E}(NA)\rightarrow\mathcal{E}(NA)
be the convolution operator defined as
c_{\mu}(f)=f*\mu
. In the previous section, we proved that if
\widetilde{\mu}
is slowly decreasing, then
c_{\mu}:\mathcal{E}^{\mathfrak{v}}(NA)\rightarrow\mathcal{E}^{\mathfrak{v}}(NA)
is surjective. In this section, we aim to prove the converse -full- assertion, which we state below.

Theorem
5.1
.

Suppose that
\mu\in\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(NA)^{\#}
. If
c_{\mu}:\mathcal{E}(NA)\rightarrow\mathcal{E}(NA)
is surjective, then
\widetilde{\mu}
is a slowly decreasing function on
\mathbb{C}
.

Once again, the idea is to transfer the analysis to
\mathbb{R}
by means of the Abel transform and then use certain Euclidean results proved in
[
19
]
. To this end, let us introduce the following operator. Let
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
and recall that the dual Abel transform
\mathcal{A}^{*}
was defined by

(5.39)

\mathcal{A}^{*}f(x)=\mathcal{A}^{*}f(d(x,e))=\mathcal{A}^{*}f(r)=\int_{d(e,y)=r}\widetilde{f}(y)\,d\sigma_{r}(y)

where
\widetilde{f}(y)=\widetilde{f}(X,Y,a)=e^{\frac{Q}{2}\log a}f(\log a)
. The map
\mathcal{A}^{*}
is a continuous linear map of Fréchet spaces, and for any
f\in\mathcal{E}(\mathbb{R})
it is clear that
\mathcal{A}^{*}f\in\mathcal{E}(NA)^{\#}
. Furthermore recall that, as already mentioned,
\mathcal{A}^{*}
is a topological isomorphism between
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})_{\text{even}}
and
C^{\infty}(NA)^{\#}
.

Proposition
5.2
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(NA)^{\#}
. Then the following diagram commutes:
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{E}(\mathbb{R})_{\text{even}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{E}(\mathbb{R})_{\text{even}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{E}(NA)^{\#}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{E}(NA)^{\#}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces c_{\mathcal{A}\mu}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{A}^{*}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{A}^{*}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces c_{\mu}}

Proof.

Since all maps in the diagram are continuous, it suffices to prove that the diagram consisting of the adjoint maps is commutative:
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(NA)^{\#}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(NA)^{\#}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})_{\text{even}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})_{\text{even}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces c_{\mu}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{A}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{A}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces c_{\mathcal{A}\mu}}

First, let us justify why the maps in the diagram above are the claimed adjoints of the maps in the statement of Proposition
5.2
. To begin with, as already mentioned, the map
\mathcal{A}^{*}
is the adjoint of
\mathcal{A}
. Next, the adjoint of
c_{\mu}
is itself, as we have proved in Proposition
5.2
. Finally, the adjoint of
c_{\mathcal{A}\mu}
is given by convolution with
(\mathcal{A}\mu)^{\widecheck{}}
, the latter being the reflection of the distribution
\mathcal{A}\mu
with respect to the map
t\mapsto-t
in
\mathbb{R}
. Taking into account that the Abel transform maps radial distributions on
NA
to even distributions on
\mathbb{R}
, we see that
(\mathcal{A}\mu)^{\widecheck{}}
and
\mathcal{A}\mu
coincide. Thus, showing the commutativity of the diagram boils down to proving that

\mathcal{A}T*\mathcal{A}\mu=\mathcal{A}(T*\mu)

for all
T\in\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(NA)^{\#}
. This identity is clearly true, since the Euclidean Fourier transform of both sides is equal to
\widetilde{T}(\lambda)\widetilde{\mu}(\lambda)
, which finishes the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
5.1
.

Suppose that
c_{\mu}:\mathcal{E}(NA)\rightarrow\mathcal{E}(NA)
is surjective. Then for any
\psi\in\mathcal{E}(NA)^{\#}
, there is a
\phi\in\mathcal{E}(NA)
for which
\phi*\mu=\psi
. Observe that this implies that
\phi*\mu=R(\phi*\mu)
, where
R
is the radialization operator in (
2.3
), so by a Fubini argument, it is easy to see that one can replace
\phi
by
R\phi
. Therefore, we may assume that
\phi\in\mathcal{E}(NA)^{\#}
. Thus
c_{\mu}
maps
\mathcal{E}(NA)^{\#}
onto
\mathcal{E}(NA)^{\#}
.

As already mentioned in this section, the linear map
\mathcal{A}^{*}:\mathcal{E}(\mathbb{R})_{\text{even}}\rightarrow\mathcal{E}(NA)^{\#}
is a linear bijection. Proposition
5.2
therefore shows that the convolution operator

c_{\mathcal{A}\mu}:\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})_{\text{even}}\rightarrow\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})_{\text{even}}
is surjective. The problem is now Euclidean, thus we can invoke
[
19
, Theorem 4.1]
, which states, in dimension one, that if a distribution
\Lambda\in\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})_{\text{even}}
is such that the convolution operator
c_{\Lambda}
maps
\mathcal{E}(\mathbb{R})_{\text{even}}
onto
\mathcal{E}(\mathbb{R})_{\text{even}}
, then its Euclidean Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}\Lambda
is slowly decreasing. Therefore, taking
\Lambda=\mathcal{A}\mu
, we conclude that the holomorphic function
\mathcal{F}({\mathcal{A}\mu})=\widetilde{\mu}(\lambda)
is slowly decreasing, which then proves the desired surjectivity of
c_{\mu}
on
\mathcal{E}(NA)
. The proof of Theorem
5.1
is now complete.
∎

6.
Mean value operators

In this section, our aim is to prove -as an application of the results of the previous sections- the surjectivity of certain mean value operators on
\mathcal{E}(NA)
. On symmetric spaces of the noncompact type
G/K
, the authors in
[
9
, Section 9]
consider mean value operators over translated
K
-orbits of a fixed point, and present various surjectivity results for those operators according to the structure of the group
G
. Due to the lack of a stabilizer group
K
on the present setting, we modify the definition of these mean value operators as follows: for suitable functions
f
on
NA
, consider the average on a sphere of radius
t>0
around
x\in NA
given by

\mathcal{M}_{t}f(x):=\int_{d(y,e)=t}f(xy)\,d\sigma_{t}(y)=f*\sigma_{t}(x),

where
\sigma_{t}
is the normalized surface measure on the sphere of radius
t
with center at the identity
e
. The spherical Fourier transform of the radial measure
\sigma_{t}
is

\widetilde{\sigma_{t}}(\lambda)=\varphi_{\lambda}(t).

The main result of this section is the following.

Proposition
6.1
.

For any
t>0
, the mean value operator

\mathcal{M}_{t}:\mathcal{E}^{\mathfrak{v}}(NA)\rightarrow\mathcal{E}^{\mathfrak{v}}(NA)

is surjective.

To prove Proposition
6.1
, let us make some preparatory comments. First of all, in view of Theorem
4.1
and the fact that the spherical transform of the surface measure
\sigma_{t}
is
\varphi_{\lambda}(t)
, it suffices to show that
\lambda\mapsto\varphi_{\lambda}(t)
is slowly decreasing. As already mentioned, spherical functions
\varphi_{\lambda}
on harmonic
NA
groups fall under the scope of Jacobi analysis. We use the following formula by Koornwinder,

(6.40)

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{n-1}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)}{2^{\frac{n-1}{2}}\Gamma\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)}\,(\sinh t)^{n-2}\,(\cosh t)^{\frac{k}{2}}\,\varphi_{\lambda}(t)=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\cos(\lambda s)\,(\cosh t-\cosh s)^{\frac{n-3}{2}}\,{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\frac{k}{2},1-\frac{k}{2};\frac{n-1}{2};\frac{\cosh t-\cosh s}{2\cosh t}\right)ds.

see
[
25
, Eq. (5.57) and (5.60)]
. Let
\Phi_{k}(\lambda)
denote the integral on the right hand side above:

\displaystyle\Phi_{k}(\lambda):=\int_{0}^{t}\cos(\lambda s)\,(\cosh t-\cosh s)^{\frac{n-3}{2}}\,{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\frac{k}{2},1-\frac{k}{2};\frac{n-1}{2};\frac{\cosh t-\cosh s}{2\cosh t}\right)ds.

The function
\Phi_{k}
can be extended to an even holomorphic function, also denoted by
\Phi_{k}
, and clearly it is enough to prove that this function is slowly decreasing. Since the case
k=0
corresponds to the real hyperbolic space, where the surjectivity of
\mathcal{M}_{t}
has been treated in
[
9
]
, we may assume that
k\geq 1
.

Proof of Proposition
6.1
.

We split the proof into two cases, namely when
k
is odd or even.

Assume first that
k
is odd. Then since
m
is always even, it follows that
n=m+k+1
is even. Then the desired slow decrease in
\lambda
has been established in
[
9
, pp.3636–3637]
.

Next, assume that
k
is even. This implies that
n
is odd, and in fact, by the table in the Introduction,
n\geq 7
. If
k=2
, then observe that the hypergeometric function is equal to

{}_{2}F_{1}\left(1,0;\frac{n-1}{2};\frac{\cosh t-\cosh s}{2\cosh t}\right)={}_{2}F_{1}\left(0,1;\frac{n-1}{2};\frac{\cosh t-\cosh s}{2\cosh t}\right)=1,

due to its symmetry in the first two arguments. Writing
n-3=2N\geq 4
, it follows that

\displaystyle\Phi_{k}(\lambda)=\int_{0}^{t}\cos(\lambda s)\,(\cosh t-\cosh s)^{N}\,ds.

Then the desired slow decrease in
\lambda
has been established in
[
9
, pp.3635-3636]
.

It remains to treat the case when
k\geq 4
is even, which in turn implies that
n
is odd: write
n-3=2N\geq 10
. It follows that
1-\frac{k}{2}=-M
, for some
M\in\mathbb{N}
. Then the hypergeometric function

{}_{2}F_{1}\left(-M,1+M;1+N;\frac{\cosh t-\cosh s}{2\cosh t}\right)

reduces to a polynomial

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=0}^{M}(-1)^{\ell}{M\choose\ell}\frac{(1+M)_{\ell}}{(1+N)_{\ell}}\left(\frac{\cosh t-\cosh s}{2\cosh t}\right)^{\ell}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{M}c_{\ell,M}\left(\frac{\cosh t-\cosh s}{2\cosh t}\right)^{\ell},

with
c_{0,M}=1
. (Here, we used the notation
(\nu)_{\ell}=\nu(\nu+1)...(\nu+\ell-1)
.) Thus we need to show that the function

\displaystyle\Phi_{k}(\lambda)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{M}2^{-\ell}\,c_{\ell,M}\,(\cosh t)^{-\ell}\int_{0}^{t}\cos(\lambda s)\,(\cosh t-\cosh s)^{N+\ell}ds

is slowly decreasing. Clearly, this function cannot be identically zero.
Setting

\widetilde{I}_{N+\ell}(\lambda):=\int_{0}^{t}\cos(\lambda s)\,(\cosh t-\cosh s)^{N+\ell}ds,

let us
rewrite

(6.41)

\Phi_{k}(\lambda)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{M}2^{-\ell}\,c_{\ell,M}\,(\cosh t)^{-\ell}\,\widetilde{I}_{N+\ell}(\lambda).

Assuming for a moment that the following asymptotics for
\widetilde{I}_{N+\ell}
hold,

(6.42)

\displaystyle\widetilde{I}_{N+\ell}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=(N+\ell+1)!\,(\sinh t)^{N+\ell}\,\lambda^{-N-\ell-1}\,\sin\left(\lambda t-\frac{(N+\ell)\pi}{2}\right)+\textrm{O}(\lambda^{-N-\ell-\frac{3}{2}})

as
\lambda\rightarrow+\infty
, let us complete the proof: given (
6.42
), it follows that the main term in the sum (
6.41
) amounts to
\ell=0
. In other words,

(6.43)

\Phi_{k}(\lambda)=(N+1)!\,(\sinh t)^{N}\,\lambda^{-N-1}\,\sin\left(\lambda t-\frac{N\pi}{2}\right)+r_{N}(\lambda),

where
r_{N}(\lambda)=\textrm{O}(\lambda^{-N-\frac{3}{2}})
.

For
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
, we define the sets
U_{\xi}
and
V_{\xi}
by

U_{\xi}:=\{\zeta\in\mathbb{C}:\,|\zeta-\xi|<A\log(2+|\xi|)\},\quad V_{\xi}:=U_{\xi}\cap\mathbb{R},

where
A=(N+1)!\,(\sinh t)^{N}.

If necessary, we take the above
\xi_{0}
large enough so that
tV_{\xi}
is an interval of length greater than
2\pi

and
A\log(2+\xi)\leq\xi/2
for
\xi>\xi_{0}
.
Therefore, on the one hand, there is a
\lambda_{0}
in
V_{\xi}
such that
\sin\left(\lambda_{0}t-\frac{N\pi}{2}\right)=1
, which yields

\sup_{\lambda\in V_{\xi}}\left|A\,\lambda^{-N-1}\,\sin\left(\lambda t-\frac{N\pi}{2}\right)\right|\geq A\,\lambda_{0}^{-N-1}.

On the other hand, for
\lambda\in V_{\xi}
we have
\xi/2\leq\xi-A\log(2+\xi)<\lambda<\xi+A\log(2+\xi)\leq 2\xi
,
thus

\displaystyle\sup_{\zeta\in U_{\xi}}|\Phi_{k}(\zeta)|

\displaystyle\geq\sup_{\lambda\in V_{\xi}}|\Phi_{k}(\lambda)|

\displaystyle\geq\sup_{\lambda\in V_{\xi}}\left|A\,\lambda^{-N-1}\,\sin\left(\lambda t-\frac{N\pi}{2}\right)\right|-\sup_{\lambda\in V_{\xi}}|r_{N}(\lambda)|

\displaystyle\geq A\,\lambda_{0}^{-N-1}-C(\lambda+1)^{-N-\frac{3}{2}}

\displaystyle\geq A\,(2\xi)^{-N-1}-C(\xi/2+1)^{-N-\frac{3}{2}}.

When
\xi
is large enough, the first term above dominates, and this completes the proof that
\Phi_{k}
is slowly decreasing.
∎

We finally complete the proof of Proposition
6.1
, proving the asymptotics (
6.42
) for

\widetilde{I}_{\nu}(\lambda):=\int_{0}^{t}\cos(\lambda s)\,(\cosh t-\cosh s)^{\nu}ds,\quad\nu\in\mathbb{N}.

Lemma
6.2
.

Let
\nu\in\mathbb{N}
. Then,

\displaystyle\widetilde{I}_{\nu}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=(\nu+1)!\,(\sinh t)^{\nu}\,\lambda^{-\nu-1}\,\sin\left(\lambda t-\frac{\nu\pi}{2}\right)+\textrm{O}(\lambda^{-\nu-\frac{3}{2}})

as
\lambda\rightarrow+\infty.

Proof.

Let us first recall the definition of Bessel functions of the first kind: By
[
20
, §3.715, Formula 20]
, we have

(6.44)

\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\cos(z\cos\theta)\,(\sin\theta)^{2\mu}d\theta=\sqrt{\pi}\,2^{\mu-1}\,z^{-\mu}\,\Gamma\left(\mu+\frac{1}{2}\right)J_{\mu}(z),

whenever
\text{Re}\mu>-\frac{1}{2}
. In addition, if
\nu\in\mathbb{N}
, then by
[
20
, §8.461, Formula 1]
, we have

\displaystyle J_{\nu+\frac{1}{2}}(z)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi z}}\sin\left(z-\frac{\nu\pi}{2}\right)\sum_{m=0}^{\lfloor\frac{\nu}{2}\rfloor}\frac{(-1)^{m}(\nu+2m)!}{(2m)!(\nu-2m)!}(2z)^{-2m}

\displaystyle+\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi z}}\cos\left(z-\frac{\nu\pi}{2}\right)\sum_{m=0}^{\lfloor\frac{\nu-1}{2}\rfloor}\frac{(-1)^{m}(\nu+2m+1)!}{(2m+1)!(\nu-2m-1)!}(2z)^{-2m-1}.

This implies that as
z>0
grows to infinity, we have

(6.45)

\displaystyle J_{\nu+\frac{1}{2}}(z)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi z}}\sin\left(z-\frac{\nu\pi}{2}\right)+\textrm{O}(z^{-3/2}).

It follows that for fixed
t>0
and
\nu\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\displaystyle I_{\nu}(\lambda):

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\cos(\lambda s)\,(t^{2}-s^{2})^{\nu}\,ds

\displaystyle=t^{2\nu+1}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\cos(\lambda t\cos\theta)\,(\sin\theta)^{2\left(\nu+\frac{1}{2}\right)}\,d\theta

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\pi}\,2^{\nu-\frac{1}{2}}\,t^{2\nu+1}\,(\lambda t)^{-\nu-\frac{1}{2}}\,\Gamma\left(\nu+1\right)J_{\nu+\frac{1}{2}}(\lambda t)

(6.46)

\displaystyle=2^{\nu}\,(\nu+1)!\,t^{\nu}\,\lambda^{-\nu-1}\,\sin\left(\lambda t-\frac{\nu\pi}{2}\right)+\textrm{O}(\lambda^{-\nu-\frac{3}{2}}),\quad\lambda\rightarrow+\infty,

using (
6.44
) and (
6.45
).

We are now ready to compute the asymptotics for
\widetilde{I}_{\nu}
. To this end, we follow the approach of
[
9
, Lemma 10.4]
. Let

f(z)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{\ell}}{(2\ell)!}.

Observe that
\cosh z=f(z^{2})
. Therefore, for any
\nu\in\mathbb{N}
, the function

G_{\nu}(z)=\begin{cases}\left(\frac{f(t^{2})-f(t^{2}-z)}{z}\right)^{\nu}\quad&z\neq 0\\
(f^{\prime}(t^{2}))^{\nu}\quad&z=0\end{cases}

is well-defined and holomorphic near
[0,t^{2}]
, thus it admits a Taylor series expansion

(6.47)

G_{\nu}(z)=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}a_{i,\nu}z^{i}=a_{0,\nu}+z\,g_{\nu}(z),

where the remainder
g_{\nu}(z)
is holomorphic near the line segment
[0,t^{2}]\subseteq\mathbb{C}
and
a_{0,\nu}=(\sinh t/(2t))^{\nu}>0
. Substituting
z=t^{2}-s^{2}
in (
6.47
), we have

\left(\frac{\cosh t-\cosh s}{t^{2}-s^{2}}\right)^{\nu}=a_{0,\nu}+(t^{2}-s^{2})\,g_{\nu}(t^{2}-s^{2}).

Thus

\displaystyle(\cosh t-\cosh s)^{\nu}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\cosh t-\cosh s}{t^{2}-s^{2}}\right)^{\nu}(t^{2}-s^{2})^{\nu}

\displaystyle=a_{0,\nu}(t^{2}-s^{2})^{\nu}+(t^{2}-s^{2})^{\nu+1}\,g_{\nu}(t^{2}-s^{2}).

Therefore,
\widetilde{I}_{\nu}(\lambda)
can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\widetilde{I}_{\nu}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=a_{0,\nu}\int_{0}^{t}\cos(\lambda s)(t^{2}-s^{2})^{\nu}\,ds+\int_{0}^{t}\cos(\lambda s)\,(t^{2}-s^{2})^{\nu+1}\,g_{\nu}(t^{2}-s^{2})\,ds

\displaystyle=a_{0,\nu}\,I_{\nu}(\lambda)+R_{\nu}(\lambda).

The term
I_{\nu}(\lambda)
satisfies the asymptotics (
6.46
) as
\lambda\rightarrow+\infty
. For the remainder term
R_{\nu}(\lambda)
, observe that, owing to the fact that
g_{\nu}
is holomorphic near
[0,t^{2}]
, we may further expand

g_{\nu}(t^{2}-s^{2})=g_{0,\nu}+(t^{2}-s^{2})\,\widetilde{g}_{\nu}(t^{2}-s^{2}),

where
\widetilde{g}_{\nu}
is again holomorphic near
[0,t^{2}]
. This way, we obtain

\displaystyle R_{\nu}(\lambda)=g_{0,\nu}\,I_{\nu+1}(\lambda)+\int_{0}^{t}\cos(\lambda s)\,(t^{2}-s^{2})^{\nu+2}\,\widetilde{g}_{\nu}(t^{2}-s^{2})\,ds,

which is
\textrm{O}(\lambda^{-\nu-2})
: for the first summand above, this follows by (
6.46
), while for the second by
[
9
, Lemma 10.2]
.

Altogether, we conclude that

\widetilde{I}_{\nu}(\lambda)=(\nu+1)!\,(\sinh t)^{\nu}\,\lambda^{-\nu-1}\,\sin\left(\lambda t-\frac{\nu\pi}{2}\right)+\textrm{O}(\lambda^{-\nu-\frac{3}{2}}).

∎
