Symmetry Analysis of the Non-Hermitian Electro-Optic Effect in Crystals
Sylvain Lannebère, Tatiana G. Rappoport, Tiago A. Morgado, Ivo Souza, Mário G. Silveirinha

Abstract
Here, we investigate how crystal symmetry tailors the non-Hermitian electro-optic effect arising from the Berry curvature dipole. Specifically, we demonstrate the critical influence of the material’s point group symmetry and external electric biases in shaping this effect, leading to current-induced optical gain and non-reciprocal optical responses. Through a symmetry-based analysis of the crystallographic point groups, we identify how different symmetries affect the electro-optic response, enabling the engineering of polarization-dependent optical gain without the need for gyrotropic effects. In particular, we demonstrate that the non-Hermitian electro-optic response in a broad class of crystals is characterized by linear dichroic gain. In this type of response, the eigenpolarizations that activate the gain or dissipation are linearly polarized. Depending on the point group symmetry, it is possible to achieve gain (or dissipation) for all eigenpolarizations or to observe polarization-dependent gain and dissipation. Weyl semimetals emerge as promising candidates for realizing significant non-Hermitian electro-optic effects and linear dichroic gain. We further examine practical applications by studying the reflectance of biased materials in setups involving mirrors, demonstrating how optical gain and attenuation can be controlled via symmetry and bias configurations.

Notably, we identify a broad range of point groups that enable linear dichroic gain, where the gain or dissipation is governed by linearly polarized fields.
We remind the reader that nonreciprocity refers to responses that enable asymmetric propagation between two points, whereas non-Hermitian interactions are associated with dissipative or gain-related processes.

Finally, we illustrate the practical implications of the NHEO effect by examining the reflectance of materials in setups involving mirrors. This allows us to evaluate how the incoming wave polarization can trigger either dissipative or gain responses in systems with different symmetries, providing valuable insights into the potential applications of these materials in optical devices and electromagnetic systems.

II
Electro-optic permittivity

The linearized optical response of a generic low-symmetry three-dimensional conductor under a static electric bias
\mathbf{E}_{0}
can be found using the semiclassical Boltzmann transport theory
[
25
,
27
]
. The linear electro-optic response is determined by the BD tensor
\overline{{\bf D}}
,

D_{ij}=-\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int\frac{\partial f^{0}_{\mathbf{k}}}{\partial k_{i}}\Omega_{\mathbf{k},j}d^{3}{k}=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int f^{0}_{\mathbf{k}}\frac{\partial{\Omega_{\mathbf{k},j}}}{\partial k_{i}}d^{3}{k},

(1)

where
f^{0}_{\mathbf{k}}
is the Fermi-Dirac distribution and
\bm{\Omega}_{\mathbf{k}}
is the Berry curvature (with implied band summations). The BD in 3D materials is dimensionless and traceless, so that
\sum_{i}D_{ii}=0
.
The electro-optic conductivity can be expressed as a sum of two terms,
[
27
]
:

\displaystyle\overline{{\bf\bm{\sigma}}}_{\text{EO}}(\omega)=\frac{e^{3}\tau}{\hbar^{2}}\left(-\mathbf{E}_{0}\cdot\overline{{\bf D}}\times\mathbf{1}_{3\times 3}+\frac{1}{1-i\omega\tau}\mathbf{E}_{0}\times\overline{{\bf D}}^{T}\right)

(2)

where
\tau
is the scattering relaxation time, and
\overline{{\bf D}}^{T}
denotes the transpose of the BD tensor. The first term is associated with conservative light-matter interactions, while the second term is associated with non-conservative interactions.

It is convenient to express the optical response in terms of an EO susceptibility (permittivity):

\displaystyle\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}_{\text{EO}}(\omega)=\frac{\overline{{\bf\bm{\sigma}}}_{\text{EO}}}{-i\varepsilon_{0}\omega}

\displaystyle=\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}_{\text{EO}}^{\prime}+i\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}_{\text{EO}}^{\prime\prime}

(3)

where
\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}_{\text{EO}}^{\prime}=\frac{\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}_{\text{EO}}+\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}_{\text{EO}}^{\dagger}}{2}
is the Hermitian part of the susceptibility and
i\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}_{\text{EO}}^{\prime\prime}=\frac{\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}_{\text{EO}}-\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}_{\text{EO}}^{\dagger}}{2}
is the anti-Hermitian part. Here,
{\dagger}
represents the Hermitian-conjugate operation. Both
\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}_{\text{EO}}^{\prime}
and
\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}_{\text{EO}}^{\prime\prime}
are Hermitian tensors with real-valued eigenvalues.
The Hermitian component,
\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}_{\text{EO}}^{\prime}
, contributes to the conservative part of the material response, dictating the wave dispersion, while the anti-Hermitian part,
i\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}_{\text{EO}}^{\prime\prime}
, controls the power exchange between the wave and the medium.

It is useful to decompose the two tensors into real (subscript R) and imaginary (subscript I) parts. For both
\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}_{\text{EO}}^{\prime}
and
\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}_{\text{EO}}^{\prime\prime}
, the real part is a symmetric tensor, while the imaginary part is an antisymmetric tensor. As shown in Appendix
A
, they can be written explicitly in terms of the (vectorial) frequency
\bm{\omega}_{0}=\frac{e^{3}\tau}{\varepsilon_{0}\hbar^{2}}\mathbf{E}_{0}
as follows,

\displaystyle\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}_{\text{EO,R}}^{\prime}=

\displaystyle\frac{-\tau}{2\left[1+(\omega\tau)^{2}\right]}|\bm{\omega}_{0}|\overline{{\bf D}}^{\rm S}_{\bm{\omega}_{0}},

(4a)

\displaystyle\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}_{\text{EO,I}}^{\prime}=

\displaystyle-\left(\frac{1}{\omega}+\frac{1}{2\omega\left[1+(\omega\tau)^{2}\right]}\right)\left(\overline{{\bf D}}^{T}\cdot\bm{\omega}_{0}\right)\times\bm{1}_{3\times 3},

(4b)

\displaystyle\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}_{\text{EO,R}}^{\prime\prime}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\omega\left[1+(\omega\tau)^{2}\right]}|\bm{\omega}_{0}|\overline{{\bf D}}^{\rm S}_{\bm{\omega}_{0}},

(4c)

\displaystyle\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}_{\text{EO,I}}^{\prime\prime}=

\displaystyle\frac{-\tau}{2\left[1+(\omega\tau)^{2}\right]}\left(\overline{{\bf D}}^{T}\cdot\bm{\omega}_{0}\right)\times\bm{1}_{3\times 3}.

(4d)

We defined
\overline{{\bf D}}^{\rm S}_{\bm{\omega}_{0}}
as the real symmetric tensor
\overline{{\bf D}}^{\rm S}_{\bm{\omega}_{0}}\equiv(\bm{\omega}_{0}\times\overline{{\bf D}}^{T}-\overline{{\bf D}}\times\bm{\omega}_{0})/|\bm{\omega}_{0}|
.
It is interesting to note that
\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}_{\text{EO,R}}^{\prime}
and
\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}_{\text{EO,R}}^{\prime\prime}
have the same structure but different frequency dependence. Similarly,
\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}_{\text{EO,I}}^{\prime}
and
\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}_{\text{EO,I}}^{\prime\prime}
also share the same structure and a different frequency variation.

Conversely,
\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}_{\text{EO}}^{\prime\prime}
describes a non-conservative (non-Hermitian) response responsible for optical gain or loss. As discussed in our previous works
[
27
,
25
]
the time-averaged net power exchanged between an electromagnetic wave and a material per unit of volume is

\displaystyle p_{\rm d}=\frac{\omega\varepsilon_{0}}{2}\mathbf{E}_{\omega}^{\ast}\cdot\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}^{\prime\prime}\cdot\mathbf{E}_{\omega}.

(5)

where
i\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}^{\prime\prime}
is the anti-Hermitian part of the full permittivity tensor
\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}
.
Here, it is implicit that the fields have a time-harmonic variation
\mathbf{E}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbf{E}_{\omega}e^{-i\omega t}+\mathbf{E}_{\omega}^{\ast}e^{i\omega t}\right)
.
Thus, the quadratic form
\mathbf{E}_{\omega}^{\ast}\cdot\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}^{\prime\prime}\cdot\mathbf{E}_{\omega}
governs the direction of the irreversible energy flow. For a positive
\mathbf{E}_{\omega}^{\ast}\cdot\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}^{\prime\prime}\cdot\mathbf{E}_{\omega}
the energy of the wave is irreversibly dissipated in the material in the form of heat. Conversely, for a negative
\mathbf{E}_{\omega}^{\ast}\cdot\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}^{\prime\prime}\cdot\mathbf{E}_{\omega}
the medium supplies energy to the wave, corresponding to optical gain. The tensor
\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}_{\text{EO}}^{\prime\prime}
describes the non-conservative interactions that arise from the electro-optic effect. It combines additively with the standard linear (Drude) response contribution of the material
[
27
,
25
]
, which is strictly dissipative.

As previously noted,
\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}_{\text{EO}}^{\prime\prime}
is an Hermitian tensor and thereby has a spectral decomposition of the type

\displaystyle\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}_{\text{EO}}^{\prime\prime}=\sum\limits_{i=1,2,3}{\lambda_{i}{\bf{u}}_{i}\otimes{\bf{u}}_{i}^{*}},

(6)

where
\lambda_{i}
are the real-valued eigenvalues,
{\bf{u}}_{i}
are the eigenvectors normalized as
{\bf{u}}_{i}\cdot{\bf{u}}_{j}^{*}=\delta_{ij}
and
\otimes
represents the tensor product of two vectors. The contribution of the electro-optic response to the dissipated power can be expressed in terms of the eigenvalues as follows:

\displaystyle p_{\rm d,EO}=\frac{\omega\varepsilon_{0}}{2}\sum\limits_{i=1,2,3}{\lambda_{i}\left|{{\bf{E}}_{\omega}\cdot{\bf{u}}_{i}^{*}}\right|^{2}}.

(7)

The sign of
p_{\rm d,EO}
is determined by the sign of the eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}
.
Specifically, when
\lambda_{i}<0
the corresponding eigenpolarization
{\bf{E}}_{\omega}\sim{\bf{u}}_{i}
activates gain in the material, whereas when
\lambda_{i}>0
the eigenpolarization activates dissipation.

From Eq. (
4
), we observe that

\displaystyle\bm{\omega}_{0}\cdot\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}_{\text{EO}}^{\prime\prime}\cdot\bm{\omega}_{0}=0.

(8)

This indicates that
\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}_{\text{EO}}^{\prime\prime}
cannot be a positive definite or negative definite tensor, meaning its eigenvalues cannot all share the same sign. Consequently, the light-matter interactions arising from the electro-optic effect are typically indefinite, meaning that the electro-optic contribution to the power dissipated in the material is governed by an indefinite quadratic form. Thus, the non-Hermitian response can be either active or dissipative, depending on the eigenpolarization.

In this work, we focus on material systems with a symmetry axis and a bias field aligned with that axis. For such systems,
\bm{\omega}_{0}
is itself an eigenvector (
{\bf{u}}_{3}\sim\bm{\omega}_{0}
) and is necessarily associated with a trivial eigenvalue (
\lambda_{3}=0
). Therefore, in this class of platforms, the NHEO response is governed by two orthogonal eigenpolarizations,
{\bf{u}}_{1}
and
{\bf{u}}_{2}
, which lie in the plane perpendicular to the system’s symmetry axis.

Let us now focus on the contribution of the term
i\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}_{\text{EO,I}}^{\prime\prime}
to
p_{\rm d,EO}
. Following Ref.
30
, it can be written explicitly as:

\displaystyle p_{\rm d,EO}^{\rm I}=-\frac{\omega\varepsilon_{0}}{2}\bm{\sigma}\cdot\bm{\Omega}_{\omega}\left|{{\bf{E}}_{\omega}}\right|^{2},

(9)

where
\bm{\sigma}=i\left({{\bf{E}}_{\omega}\times{\bf{E}}_{\omega}^{*}}\right)/\left|{{\bf{E}}_{\omega}}\right|^{2}
is the spin angular momentum of the wave, whereas

\displaystyle\bm{\Omega}_{\omega}=\frac{\tau}{2\left[1+(\omega\tau)^{2}\right]}\overline{{\bf D}}^{T}\cdot\bm{\omega}_{0}

(10)

is by definition the chiral-gain vector. Thus, the light-matter interactions can be either dissipative (
p_{\rm d,EO}^{\rm I}>0
) or active (
p_{\rm d,EO}^{\rm I}<0
), depending on the relative alignment between the spin angular momentum of the wave and the chiral-gain vector. The optimal polarization to unlock the gain is a circular polarization in the plane perpendicular to the chiral-gain vector, whereas the optimal polarization to unlock the dissipative properties of the medium is a circular polarization in the same plane but with opposite handedness
[
30
]
. Accordingly, the two eigenvectors of
i\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}_{\text{EO,I}}^{\prime\prime}
are two circular polarizations in the plane perpendicular to
\bm{\Omega}_{\omega}
. As shown in table
1
,
i\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}_{\text{EO,I}}^{\prime\prime}
also contributes to a nonreciprocal electromagnetic response. Note also that as the gyrotropic and chiral-gain terms have the same mathematical structure, they typically coexist within the same material.

Next, we analyze the contribution of the term
\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}_{\text{EO,R}}^{\prime\prime}
to
p_{\rm d}
. Since
\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}_{\text{EO,R}}^{\prime\prime}
is real-valued, its eigenvectors
{\bf{u}}_{i}
are necessarily real-valued as well. Unlike the chiral-gain case, where non-conservative effects are associated with circular polarizations, the non-conservative effects in this scenario are governed by linear polarizations with trivial spin angular momentum. We refer to this type of gain as “linear dichroic gain” to emphasize that the associated eigenpolarizations are linearly polarized and that the non-conservative effects depend on polarization. As shown in Table
1
, the linear dichroic gain is associated with a reciprocal electromagnetic response.

Later in the article, we present examples where the linear dichroic gain is either indefinite—meaning the gain is activated by a specific linear polarization while dissipation occurs for an orthogonal linear polarization—or semi-definite, where the non-conservative effects (when nontrivial) are exclusively dissipative or active.

In the general case, where
\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}_{\text{EO}}^{\prime\prime}
has both real and imaginary components, the eigenpolarizations that activate gain and dissipation are typically elliptically polarized. Furthermore, the frequency dependence of the real and imaginary parts of
\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}_{\text{EO}}^{\prime\prime}
plays a critical role in determining optical gain. For both
\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}_{\text{EO,R}}^{\prime\prime}
and
\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}_{\text{EO,I}}^{\prime\prime}
, the non-conservative effects become increasingly pronounced as
\omega\to 0
. In this low-frequency limit, the real (symmetric) part,
\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}_{\text{EO,R}}^{\prime\prime}
, dominates due to its
1/\omega
dependence on frequency.
As a result, at lower frequencies, the non-conservative physics is predominantly influenced by linear dichroic gain. However, at higher frequencies, the scaling behavior changes: the linear dichroic gain decreases as
1/\omega^{3}
, while the chiral gain decreases more slowly as
1/\omega^{2}
. This indicates that at higher frequencies, chiral gain becomes the dominant contributor to the optical gain.

In the next section, we will examine the properties of
\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}_{\text{EO}}^{\prime\prime}
across various point-group symmetries of non-centrosymmetric crystals. We will show that the structure of the BD tensor is intrinsically determined by the material’s symmetry. As a result, the symmetry of the material plays a crucial role in shaping the non-conservative light-matter interactions arising from the electro-optic effect.

By decomposing

IV.1
Pure linear dichroic gain: categories B, C and E

One of the most interesting scenarios originates from the BD of the point group

For this group, considering a polar axis directed along

As can be seen in Eq. (

\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}_{\text{EO}}^{\prime\prime}

and along

and
D_{xy}

This leads to two distinct scenarios: in the first, the system exhibits gain for one polarization and loss for the orthogonal polarization (referred to as indefinite gain). In the second scenario, the system exhibits gain (or dissipation) for all polarizations in the
xoy

IV.2
Category D

Next, we analyze the point groups in category D, where the BD is diagonal and satisfies
D_{zz}=-D_{xx}-D_{yy}
.

For the point groups
32
,
422
, and
622
, the BD tensor is further constrained by the condition
D_{xx}=D_{yy}\equiv D_{0}
. From Eq. (
IV
), it follows that these point groups give rise to a pure chiral-gain response, with the chiral gain vector aligned along the material’s symmetry axis (
\bm{\Omega}_{\omega}\sim\hat{\bf{z}}
). As a result, the eigenvectors are frequency-independent and exhibit circular polarization:

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{\pm}\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\pm i\hat{\mathbf{x}}+\hat{\mathbf{y}}\right).

(24)

The corresponding eigenvalues are

\lambda_{\pm}=\pm\frac{\omega_{0z}\tau D_{0}}{1+\tau^{2}\omega^{2}}
.
Additionally,
\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}_{\text{EO}}^{\prime\prime}
is purely imaginary, which significantly enhances the gyrotropic effects. Specifically, for an arbitrarily linearly polarized wave propagating along the
z
-direction, EO-induced gyrotropy enhances the polarization rotation within the material (the kinetic Faraday effect).

For a chiral-gain response, one circular polarization state undergoes amplification, while the other experiences attenuation
[
30
]
. The handedness of the polarization experiencing gain is determined by the orientation of the bias along the principal axis. This property makes chiral gain a powerful tool for achieving controlled polarization dynamics and enhanced chiral selectivity within the material.

The ability to control and generate circularly polarized light using chiral gain opens the door to innovative electromagnetic devices with unique functionalities. These include chiral lasers, polarization-dependent amplifying mirrors, and loss-compensated photonic waveguides
[
28
,
30
,
40
,
41
]
. Moreover, chiral gain may contribute to enhancing the sensitivity of chiral sensing and spectroscopy.

For the
222

D_{xx}\neq D_{yy}
, the nontrivial eigenvalues of
\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}_{\text{EO}}^{\prime\prime}
are given by

\displaystyle\lambda_{\pm}=\pm\frac{\omega_{0z}\sqrt{\tau^{2}\omega^{2}(D_{xx}+D_{yy})^{2}+(D_{xx}-D_{yy})^{2}}}{2\left(\tau^{2}\omega^{3}+\omega\right)},

(25)

indicating that the gain remains indefinite. The corresponding eigenpolarizations are

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{\pm}\sim\pm\frac{(D_{xx}-D_{yy})-i\tau\omega(D_{xx}+D_{yy})}{\sqrt{\tau^{2}\omega^{2}(D_{xx}+D_{yy})^{2}+(D_{xx}-D_{yy})^{2}}}\hat{\mathbf{x}}+\hat{\mathbf{y}}.

(26)

Unlike the previously discussed cases, the eigenpolarizations in this scenario are frequency-dependent and exhibit elliptical polarization. This behavior arises because, for this point group, the NHEO response described by Eq. (
IV
) (with
D_{xx}\neq D_{yy}
and
D_{xy}=D_{yx}=0
) incorporates both chiral-gain and linear dichroic components.

IV.3
Category A

For point groups in category A (specifically groups 3, 4 and 6), the BD is
subject to the constraints
D_{xx}=D_{yy}=-D_{zz}/2\equiv D_{0}
and
D_{yx}=-D_{xy}
(see Table
2
).
For a bias along the polar axis, the nontrivial eigenvalues of
\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}_{\text{EO}}^{\prime\prime}
are

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}=-\frac{\omega_{0z}\left(D_{xy}+D_{0}\tau\omega\right)}{\tau^{2}\omega^{3}+\omega},\quad\lambda_{2}=-\frac{\omega_{0z}\left(D_{xy}-D_{0}\tau\omega\right)}{\tau^{2}\omega^{3}+\omega},

(27)

where negative eigenvalues indicate gain, whereas positive eigenvalues correspond to loss. This implies that, depending on the sign of
\frac{D_{0}\tau\omega+D_{xy}}{D_{0}\tau\omega-D_{xy}}
, the medium can exhibit either a definite or indefinite non-conservative response across all polarizations with electric field in the
xoy
plane.

As the tensor
\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}_{\text{EO}}^{\prime\prime}
contains both linear dichroic and chiral-gain components for this point group, the specific behavior is frequency-dependent. This property enables a transition from an indefinite non-conservative response to a semi-definite (positive or negative) response, where all field polarizations are either amplified or absorbed, depending on the frequency. This transition occurs at the critical frequency
\omega_{c}=\left|D_{xy}/(\tau D_{0})\right|
for which the linear dichroic gain and the chiral-gain have equal strength.
In the example shown by the orange curves in Fig.
2
,
\omega_{c}\tau\approx 0.67
.

At low frequencies (
\omega\tau\rightarrow 0
), the two eigenvalues become degenerate, given by
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}=-\omega_{0z}D_{xy}/\omega
. In this regime, the linear dichroic gain or dissipation component dominates, leading to either uniform gain or absorption across all polarizations. In contrast, for
\omega>\omega_{c}
, the chiral-gain component of the non-conservative response becomes dominant, rendering the response indefinite.

Interestingly, even though
\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}_{\text{EO}}^{\prime\prime}
[see Eq. (
IV
)] has in general both real and imaginary components, its eigenvectors are always circularly polarized when
D_{0}\neq 0
:

\mathbf{E}_{1}\sim\left(i\hat{\mathbf{x}}+\hat{\mathbf{y}}\right)/\sqrt{2}
and
\mathbf{E}_{2}\sim\left(-i\hat{\mathbf{x}}+\hat{\mathbf{y}}\right)/\sqrt{2}
. The justification for this property is that the linear dichroic gain component
\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}_{\text{EO,R}}^{\prime\prime}
is a scalar in the
xoy
plane.
Furthermore, the presence of a chiral-gain response inherently implies that
\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}_{\text{EO}}^{\prime}

induces a nonreciprocal gyrotropic response.

Biases along other crystallographic directions

Up to this point, we have focused exclusively on an electric bias along the
z

For an electric bias along a generic crystallographic direction, the tensor
\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}_{\text{EO}}^{\prime\prime}
typically has both linear gain and chiral-gain components. Thereby, its eigenvectors and eigenvalues are typically frequency dependent with the eigenpolarizations elliptically polarized. Moreover, since the nonreciprocal gyrotropic response is intrinsically linked to the chiral-gain response, it also manifests in most cases.

For a generic bias direction,
most point groups exhibit three non-trivial eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}
. As
\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}_{\text{EO}}^{\prime\prime}
cannot be a definite tensor, either one eigenvalue is positive and the other two are negative, or two eigenvalues are positive and one is negative. An exception occurs for the groups
32
,
422
, and
622
(category D), where one eigenvalue remains zero, and the other two satisfy
\lambda_{1}=-\lambda_{2}
.

For a horizontal bias (relative to the symmetry axis), it can be shown that only the elements
\varepsilon_{xz}^{\prime\prime}
,
\varepsilon_{yz}^{\prime\prime}
,
\varepsilon_{zx}^{\prime\prime}
, and
\varepsilon_{zy}^{\prime\prime}
are nonzero. In particular, the trace of
\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}_{\text{EO}}^{\prime\prime}
is zero, implying that the sum of all eigenvalues must also be zero. Moreover, the determinant of
\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}_{\text{EO}}^{\prime\prime}
is identically zero for this type of matrix structure, meaning that one of its eigenvalues necessarily vanishes, while the remaining two are equal in magnitude but opposite in sign. In general,
\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}_{\text{EO}}^{\prime\prime}
has both chiral-gain and linear-dichroic gain components, resulting in elliptical eigenpolarizations that depend on frequency.

Consequently, for a horizontal bias, the electro-optic response is always characterized by indefinite gain. As before, the gain and loss responses are interchanged when the electric bias is reversed.

It is relevant to note that when the electric bias is aligned with the symmetry axis of the material (as in Sects.

V
Criteria for Optical Gain

So far, we have focused solely on the linear electro-optic response of 3D materials with nontrivial BDs. However, to fully understand the conditions for optical gain, it is important to also consider the material’s response in the absence of an electric bias. In particular, since these materials must exhibit a metallic response with electronic scattering
[
27
]
, we should take into account the linear Drude-type response, which describes the behavior of free electrons. In this context, we assume for simplicity that the material response in the absence of bias is characterized by an isotropic Drude model. This allows us to establish a straightforward criterion for the emergence of optical gain. The permittivity of Drude’s model is given by
\varepsilon_{\text{m}}=\varepsilon_{\text{m}}^{\prime}+i\varepsilon_{\text{m}}^{\prime\prime}
, where the real
\varepsilon_{\text{m}}^{\prime}
and imaginary
\varepsilon_{\text{m}}^{\prime\prime}
parts are given by

\varepsilon_{\text{m}}^{\prime}=1-\frac{\omega_{p}^{2}\tau^{2}}{\omega^{2}\tau^{2}+1},\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \varepsilon_{\text{m}}^{\prime\prime}=\frac{\omega_{p}^{2}\tau}{\omega^{3}\tau^{2}+\omega},

(28)

and
\omega_{p}
is the plasma frequency. The high-frequency dielectric constant was chosen to be equal to one. At low frequencies
\omega\to 0
, the Drude losses tend to
\varepsilon_{\text{m}}^{\prime\prime}\to\omega_{p}^{2}\tau/\omega
and have the same frequency dependence
1/\omega
as the EO susceptibility.

The full permittivity response includes both the Drude term and the linear electro-optic term:

\displaystyle\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}=\varepsilon_{\text{m}}\mathbf{1}_{3\times 3}+\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}_{\text{EO}}=\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}^{\prime}+i\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}^{\prime\prime}

(29)

with
\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}^{\prime}=\varepsilon_{\text{m}}^{\prime}\mathbf{1}_{3\times 3}+\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}_{\text{EO}}^{\prime}
denoting
the Hermitian part of the response while
\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}^{\prime\prime}=\varepsilon_{\text{m}}^{\prime\prime}\mathbf{1}_{3\times 3}+\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}_{\text{EO}}^{\prime\prime}
governs the non-Hermitian part, which determines the exchange of power between the material and the wave [see Eq. (
5
)].

As described in previous sections, the sign of the eigenvalues of
\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}^{\prime\prime}
determines whether the system exhibits gain or loss. The eigenvalues of
\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}^{\prime\prime}
are given by
\lambda_{i}+\varepsilon_{\text{m}}^{\prime\prime}
, where
\lambda_{i}
(
i=1,2,3
) are the eigenvalues of
\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}_{\text{EO}}^{\prime\prime}
. Thus, the threshold for optical gain can be found by solving
\lambda_{i}+\varepsilon_{\text{m}}^{\prime\prime}=0
. The eigenvectors of
\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}^{\prime\prime}
, in turn, are identical to those of
\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}_{\text{EO}}^{\prime\prime}
.

Focusing on a bias along the
z
-axis, we can explicitly write the gain criteria for two sets of interesting symmetries. For category C, gain occurs when
\omega_{0z}D_{xy}>\omega_{p}^{2}\tau
. For category A, the two different eigenvalues provide the conditions
\omega_{0z}\left(D_{0}\tau\omega+D_{xy}\right)>\omega_{p}^{2}\tau
and
\omega_{0z}\left(D_{xy}-D_{0}\tau\omega\right)>\omega_{p}^{2}\tau
. Both conditions can be satisfied simultaneously, and in that case, the two modes provide gain. At low frequencies, where
\omega\tau\to 0
, both conditions reduce to the same equation. In this limit, we can achieve gain for both polarizations when
\omega_{0z}D_{xy}>\omega_{p}^{2}{\tau}
. Although the threshold expressed in terms of
\omega_{0z}
is proportional to
\tau
,
\omega_{0z}
itself is proportional to
\tau E_{0}
. Consequently, the threshold electric bias is independent of
\tau
within the present single-relaxation-time Drude model.

For nanophotonic applications, the most relevant regime is typically the one in which the operating frequency
\omega
is comparable to or larger than the plasma frequency
\omega_{p}
, or more generally the epsilon-near-zero (ENZ) frequency, since the material becomes transparent in this range. Due to this reason, gain effects are most relevant when the bias strength
\omega_{0}
is comparable to
\omega_{p}
. Consequently, systems in which the effective plasma frequency (ENZ point) is in the mid infrared or optical ranges require, in practice, a stronger bias to achieve gain than systems where the ENZ point lies in the terahertz range.

[
3

. These field strengths are well below typical dielectric breakdown thresholds of thin-film and two-dimensional devices
[

VIII
Conclusions

We investigated the non-Hermitian linear electro-optic effect in crystals, focusing on how different point group symmetries influence the resulting optical response.
Through a detailed symmetry analysis of the 32 crystallographic point groups, we have outlined a comprehensive roadmap for engineering non-reciprocal optical responses with gain for specific light polarizations, with potential applications in optical devices.

Our analysis shows that the NHEO response of a material is intricately linked to its point-group symmetry. We have organized the non-centrosymmetric crystal materials into several symmetry-based categories (A, B, C, D, and E) and explored the distinctive optical properties each category exhibits. Materials in categories B, C and E demonstrate linear dichroic gain responses when the electric bias is aligned with the principal axis. Notably, the eigenpolarizations that activate the gain or loss are linearly polarized, and the gain or dissipation response can be reversed by simply changing the direction of the applied static bias. These materials provide a reciprocal gain response free from gyrotropic effects. For certain point groups, it is possible to achieve gain (or dissipation) for all polarizations in the plane perpendicular to the applied bias, while for others, the response remains indefinite.

In contrast, materials belonging to category D exhibit chiral optical gain, where light of one handedness is amplified while light of opposite handedness is absorbed, showcasing the potential for chiral-selective applications. Furthermore, in these materials the electro-optic effect also induces a nonreciprocal gyrotropic response. Additionally, materials in category A typically feature both linear dichroic and chiral-gain components. These materials may undergo a transition that determines which component dominates.

Weyl semimetals emerge as particularly strong candidates for exhibiting prominent NHEO effects due to their large BDs. Specifically, many Weyl semimetals belong to the
mm2
and
4mm
point group symmetries (e.g., NbP, TaAs, NbAs, and MoTe
2
), making them ideal for observing NHEO effects and for tuning linear dichroic optical gain in response to external biases.

We also presented a reflectance analysis that illustrates the practical implications of the NHEO effect. In particular, we demonstrated how wave amplification or attenuation can be achieved using non-Hermitian mirrors, utilizing materials with different symmetries through either linear dichroic gain or chiral-gain. Our analysis provides important insights into how the NHEO effect could be harnessed in practical applications, such as in the design of non-reciprocal optical devices or systems where polarization-dependent optical gain is desirable.

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{-i\omega}\left(-\bm{\omega}_{0}\cdot\overline{{\bf D}}\times\mathbf{1}_{3\times 3}+\frac{1+i\omega\tau}{1+(\omega\tau)^{2}}\bm{\omega}_{0}\times\overline{{\bf D}}^{T}\right)

\displaystyle\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}_{\text{EO}}^{\prime}

Appendix C
Transfer matrix for propagation along
z

Here we derive the transfer (ABCD) matrix that characterizes wave propagation in the considered birefringent non-Hermitian materials.

The Maxwell’s equations in the frequency domain are

\displaystyle\nabla\times\mathbf{E}

\displaystyle=i\omega\mu_{0}\mathbf{H}

(37a)

\displaystyle\nabla\times\mathbf{H}

\displaystyle=-i\omega\varepsilon_{0}\overline{{\bf\varepsilon}}\cdot\mathbf{E}

(37b)

For propagation along the
z
-direction, we have
\nabla=\partial/\partial z\,\hat{\mathbf{z}}
, and these equations can be written in a matrix form as

\displaystyle i\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\mathbf{f}(z)=\overline{{\bf\mathbf{M}}}\cdot\mathbf{f}(z)

(38)

where the four-component state vector
\mathbf{f}
is defined by

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}(z)=\begin{pmatrix}E_{x}(z)&E_{y}(z)&H_{x}(z)&H_{y}(z)\end{pmatrix}^{T}

(39)

with
E_{x}
,
E_{y}
,
H_{x}
,
H_{y}
the
x
and
y
components of the electric and magnetic fields at position
z
. The
4\times 4
matrix
\overline{{\bf\mathbf{M}}}
is given by

\displaystyle\overline{{\bf\mathbf{M}}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0&-\omega\mu_{0}\\
0&0&\omega\mu_{0}&0\\
\omega\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon_{yx}&\omega\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon_{yy}&0&0\\
-\omega\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon_{xx}&-\omega\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon_{xy}&0&0\end{pmatrix}.

(40)

It is implicit that the
z
-direction is a symmetry axis of the system so that
\varepsilon_{xz}=\varepsilon_{yz}=\varepsilon_{zx}=\varepsilon_{zy}=0
.
Similar to a Schrödinger equation, the solution of Eq. (
38
) is

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}(z)=e^{-iz\overline{{\bf\mathbf{M}}}}\cdot\mathbf{f}(0),

(41)

where the exponential
e^{-iz\overline{{\bf\mathbf{M}}}}
is a
4\times 4
matrix that plays the role of a transfer matrix. The matrix exponential can be evaluated analytically (not shown here), or numerically. Thus, the fields at a generic position
z
within the material depend only on the fields calculated at
z=0
.
