A stealth-body Fc engineering platform enables complete effector silencing in antibody and ADC therapeutics
Authors: Suyeon Kim, Migyeong Jo, Munsu Kyung, Woo Hyung Ko, Wonju Lee, Kihwan Chang, Pedro Lee, Sungkuk Cho, Seongin Seomun, Byoung Joon Ko, Tae-Erk Kim, Jungmin Choi, Sang Taek Jung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe development of next-generation antibody therapeutics requires Fc scaffolds that suppress unwanted effector functions while preserving core developability attributes. Existing effector-attenuated Fc backbones, however, retain residual interactions with Fcγ receptors (FcγRs) and C1q, highlighting a persistent design limitation in achieving complete effector silencing without compromising glycosylation, FcRn-mediated persistence, and manufacturability. This is particularly relevant for formats in which Fc effector function is undesirable, including immune-engaging antibodies, CD47 blockers, and antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs). Accordingly, a robust Fc engineering framework enabling complete and transferable effector silencing across antibody modalities is required.ResultsWe developed Stealth-Body (SB) Fc variants, a fully effector-silenced Fc platform that abolishes Fc-mediated immune engagement while maintaining native-like glycosylation, pharmacokinetics, and manufacturability. Using a CHO cell display system with directed evolution guided by cross-species sequence features from goat IgG, we identified human glycosylated IgG1 variants (SB1, SB5, SB6) that abrogated binding to all human FcγRs and C1q, and to all murine and cynomolgus FcγRs. SB Fc retained pH-dependent FcRn binding, antigen recognition, thermostability, and expression, with in vivo persistence comparable to wild-type IgG1. Incorporation of SB Fc into trastuzumab, rituximab, and ADCs abolished ADCC, ADCP, and CDC, while integration into a CD47-targeting IgG–CD40L fusion protein mitigated hematologic toxicity without impairing immune activation.ConclusionsCollectively, SB Fc variants represent a generalizable, effector-null Fc scaffold that enables complete and transferable silencing of Fc-mediated immune functions while preserving key developability attributes. This platform expands the therapeutic window of antibody therapeutics through precise control of Fc activity across diverse modalities.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13036-026-00735-9.Keywords: Effector function silencing, Fc engineering, CHO cell display, Fcγ receptor (FcγR), Complement C1q, Antibody and ADC therapeutics

Background

Therapeutic monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) have become a dominant modality across oncology, autoimmunity, transplantation, and infectious diseases, with clinical performance determined by the coordinated integration of antigen recognition and immune effector activation [1, 2]. The Fc domain mediates interactions with Fc gamma receptors (FcγRs) on innate immune cells, principally the high-affinity FcγRI and the low-affinity FcγRIIa and FcγRIIIa [3, 4], thereby eliciting antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) via FcγRIIIa on NK cells, and antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis (ADCP), via FcγRI and FcγRIIa on monocyte-derived macrophages and dendritic cells [1, 2, 5]. In parallel, C1q binding to the Fc region initiates the classical complement pathway and complement-dependent cytotoxicity (CDC) [6]. From a biological engineering perspective, the Fc region can be viewed as a modular and programmable interface that links target engagement to system-level immune responses, enabling tunable control over therapeutic activity [4].

These effector pathways, although beneficial against pathogenic targets, can narrow the therapeutic window by inducing on-target toxicities. Agonistic antibodies to co-stimulatory receptors such as 4-1BB and CD40 require FcγR-dependent crosslinking, which has been associated with intrahepatic immune activation, severe hepatotoxicity, and early termination of clinical programs [7, 8]. Antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs) bearing cytotoxic payloads can undergo FcγR-mediated uptake by phagocytes, leading to dose-limiting hepatic and renal toxicity [8–12]. Even IgG4-based checkpoint inhibitors, exemplified by PD-1 antibodies, may retain sufficient FcγRI binding to deplete cytotoxic CD8⁺ T cells and blunt antitumor activity [13]. For CD3-targeting T cell engagers, indiscriminate FcγR engagement can amplify cytokine release and bystander cytotoxicity, contributing to life-threatening cytokine release syndrome (CRS); the withdrawal of muromonab (OKT3) and the catastrophic theralizumab (TGN1412) trial highlight the consequences of uncontrolled Fc-driven activation [14–17]. Antibodies incorporating attenuated Fc effector functions have improved safety in autoimmune and neurological indications by limiting off-target inflammation [18–20], yet residual Fc engagement remains problematic in highly immune-sensitive settings, underscoring the need for Fc architectures that are effectively immune-silent while retaining favorable pharmacokinetics and manufacturability [21].

The growing emphasis on Fc engineering is reflected by the fact that 9 of 12 monoclonal antibodies approved by the US Food and Drug Administration in 2023 carried Fc modifications intended to attenuate effector functions [22]. Nevertheless, current approaches involve substantial trade-offs. Fc-deficient formats such as Fab, scFv, and VHH fragments abolish FcγR and C1q binding but lose FcRn-mediated half-life extension and often exhibit suboptimal manufacturability [23]. Aglycosylated antibodies reduce FcγR binding by removing the Asn297 N-glycan, yet frequently display reduced thermal stability and increased aggregation [5, 24]. Subclass switching to IgG2 or IgG4 lowers effector activity but does not fully abrogate FcγR and C1q interactions and may compromise structural integrity [4, 25, 26]. Point-mutated Fc variants, including LALA (L234A/L235A), LALAPG (L234A/L235A/P329G), FES (L234F/L235E/P331S), and SPLE (S228P/L235E) reduce but do not eliminate FcγR and C1q binding [27–30], and subclass substitution and switching have similarly failed to achieve complete effector silencing [27, 31–34]. Even computational approaches such as AlphaFold3 provide limited actionable guidance for Fc engineering because the hinge and CH2 loops are highly flexible and heterogeneous [35, 36]. Moreover, microbial display platforms cannot reproduce mammalian Fc glycosylation, which is crucial for accurate Fc–FcγR and Fc–C1q interactions [37]. Collectively, these limitations reflect the intrinsic complexity of the Fc domain as a conformationally dynamic and glycosylation-dependent system that resists optimization through conventional design strategies. To address this challenge, we established a CHO cell-based mammalian display platform for high-throughput screening of glycosylated Fc variants, enabling the identification of variants that abolish unwanted effector engagement while preserving therapeutic efficacy and developability.

Here, we report fully silenced, glycosylated Fc variants that eliminate detectable Fc-mediated immune effector functions while retaining key properties required for therapeutic translation. We constructed a focused Fc variant library and displayed the variants on Chinese hamster ovary (CHO) cells, which provide human-like Fc glycosylation and represent the dominant platform for antibody manufacturing [38]. Through CHO display-based directed evolution, we isolated Stealth-Body (SB) Fc variants that show no measurable binding to FcγRs or C1q, and we validated their function across multiple antibody backbones and ADCs. In contrast to conventional Fc variants such as LALAPG, which retain residual FcγRI binding, our SB Fc scaffolds abolished ADCC, ADCP, and CDC in all tested contexts while maintaining robust pH-dependent FcRn binding, high expression yields, and thermostability comparable to wild-type antibodies. Collectively, these fully silenced Fc variants establish a versatile platform for next-generation antibody and ADC therapeutics in which immune effector silencing is required to expand the therapeutic index without compromising pharmacologic performance. More broadly, this work establishes a generalizable engineering framework for programmable control of Fc-mediated immune function, enabling the rational design of safer and more effective biologics.

Methods

CHO cell display for screening of a glycosylated Fc variant library

The Flp-FRT recombination system was employed to display glycosylated Fc on the mammalian cell surface and to establish a stable cell line for screening purposes. To display Fc on the cell membrane, the platelet-derived growth factor (PDGFR) transmembrane domain was fused to the Fc C-terminus, and the resulting Fc-PDGFR construct was subcloned into the pcDNA5/FRT plasmid (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), which contains a hygromycin B resistance gene for selection of stable integrants. Co-transfection of the pOG44 plasmid (Thermo Fisher Scientific), which encodes Flp recombinase, was carried out in CHO cells harboring an FRT site. Cells with stable integration of Fc-PDGFR were selected using hygromycin B (500 µg/ml), generating stable cell lines expressing glycosylated Fc on the cell surface.

A focused Fc variant library was generated by introducing random point mutations into four Fc regions implicated in FcγR binding. Fragment A was amplified using primer pairs SYK#18–SYK#23 from an Fc template containing mutations L234A/L235P. Fragment B was amplified with primer pairs SYK#24–SYK#30, Fragment C with primer pairs SYK#31–SYK#34, and Fragment D with primer pairs SYK#35–SYK#41. Fragments were assembled and cloned into the pcDNA5/FRT vector, and the resulting mutagenized Fc library plasmids (pcDNA5-Fc) were co-transfected into CHO cells with pOG44 to establish a stable Fc display library under hygromycin B selection.

High-throughput CHO cell display screening of glycosylated Fc variant libraries

Approximately 1 × 108 CHO cell library cells were labeled with tetrameric hFcγRIIIa-158V-Alexa 647 and Protein A-FITC in 1× PBS/EDTA. Following incubation on ice (30 min) and centrifugation (300 × g, 3 min), the cells were washed twice with 1× PBS/EDTA, passed through a cell strainer to remove clumps, and sorted. Alexa 647-negative, FITC-positive cells were selected using a BD FACSAria™III (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) and recovered by resuspension in Ham’s F-12 media with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). Genomic DNA of recovered cells was prepared using the AccuPrep® Genomic DNA Extraction kit.

ELISA

For the analysis of FcγR binding affinities of purified Fc variants, dimeric human FcγRs (hFcγRI, hFcγRIIa-131H, hFcγRIIa-131R, hFcγRIIb, hFcγRIIIa-158V, and hFcγRIIIa-158F), dimeric mouse FcγRs (mFcγRI, mFcγRIIb, mFcγRIII, and mFcγRIV), or dimeric cynomolgus monkey FcγRs (cFcγRI, cFcγRIIa, cFcγRIIb, and cFcγRIII) were diluted to 4 µg/ml in 0.05 M Na2CO3 (pH 9.6) and immobilized on high-binding, flat-bottom 96-well polystyrene plates (50 µl/well) overnight at 4 °C. After blocking with 4% (w/v) skim milk in PBS for 1 h at room temperature (RT), the plates were washed four times with PBST (PBS containing 0.05% Tween 20). Serially diluted Fc variants (prepared in 1% skim milk in PBS) were then added to the wells and incubated for 1 h at RT. Plates were washed, and HRP-conjugated Protein L (1:5,000 in 1% skim milk in PBS) was added and incubated for 1 h at RT. After washing, the plates were developed with 1-Step™ Ultra TMB-ELISA substrate, the colorimetric reaction was stopped by the addition of 2 M H2SO4. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using an Epoch microplate spectrophotometer. For antigen and human C1q (hC1q) binding ELISAs, antigens or IgG-Fc variants were immobilized, followed by detection using Protein L–HRP or anti-hC1q–HRP (1:400), respectively. To assess pH-dependent FcRn binding, assays were performed at pH 6.0 and 7.4, and bound FcRn–GST was detected using HRP-conjugated anti-GST antibody (1:5,000).

Thermal shift assay

Antibody-Fc variants were diluted to 5 µM in PBS and combined with SYPRO Orange dye (final concentration 200×, diluted from a 5,000× stock) before dispensing into a PCR plate. A control sample containing PBS only was prepared similarly, and all samples were run in triplicate. The plate was sealed with an optically clear sealing film, and fluorescence was monitored using the QuantStudio 3 real-time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA, USA) as the temperature increased from 25 °C to 99.9 °C at 0.03 °C/s. Fluorescence data were analyzed with OriginPro software (OriginLab, Northampton, MA, USA), and melting temperatures (Tm) were determined by fitting the data to a Boltzmann model.

Quantitative Fc–FcγR and Fc–C1q binding analysis by biolayer interferometry

Deionized water was added to each well of a black 96-well plate to hydrate FAB2G biosensors for 10 min. Antibodies, diluted to 75 µg/ml in PBS (pH 7.4), were aliquoted into each well. For measuring binding affinities of all hFcγRs except hFcγRI, hFcγRs-His were serially diluted two-fold from 5,000 nM and added to the wells, with the final well containing only PBS (pH 7.4) as a reference. The biosensors and sample plate were placed into the Octet® R8 (Sartorius, Göttingen, Germany). Binding was measured by sequentially exposing the biosensors to deionized water for 60 s (baseline), antibodies for 300 s (loading), PBS (pH 7.4) for 60 s (baseline), hFcγRs-His for 30 s (association), and PBS (pH 7.4) for 30 s (dissociation). For hFcγRI measurements, kinetic buffer (Sartorius) replaced PBS, and binding measurement began at 500 nM for hFcγRI-His. Dissociation was monitored for 300 s.

Functional evaluation of antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) using human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs)

Target cells (10,000 cells/well) were seeded in RPMI medium with 10% FBS on E-Plates and placed into an xCELLigence RTCA SP instrument (Agilent, Santa Clara, California, USA) to allow for cell adhesion and proliferation. SKBR-3 and SKOV-3 cells were incubated for 24 h, while MDA-MB-453 cells were incubated for 240 h prior to assay setup. PBMCs were washed with PBS, and resuspended in RPMI with 10% FBS. After adding PBMCs and antibodies to the wells, the mixture was incubated at RT for 30 min to allow cells to settle. The E-plate was placed in the RTCA device and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2, monitoring cytotoxicity in real-time with RTCA Software 1.2 (Agilent). Cytotoxicity was calculated as: Cytotoxicity (%) = [{(Cell Index no effector – Cell Index effector) / Cell Index no effector} × 100], where the “effector” well contains target cells, PBMCs, and antibodies, and the “no effector” well contains only target cells.

Live cell imaging

SKBR-3 cells were diluted in RPMI-1640 medium without phenol red, mixed with 2 µM Red CMTPX dye, and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 30 min. Labeled cells were washed with PBS and plated in black plates with RPMI medium (without phenol red) containing 10% FBS and 1× Antibiotic-Antimycotic at a density of 1 × 104 cells/well. Cells were incubated overnight at 37 °C with 5% CO2. The next day, PBMCs were labeled with 0.5 µg/ml Hoechst 33342 in PBS for 5 min at RT, followed by three washes with PBS. PBMCs were then resuspended in RPMI medium (without phenol red) containing 10% FBS and 1× Antibiotic–Antimycotic, and 1 × 105 cells/well were added to each well, along with 60 µg/ml antibody and caspase-3/7 detection reagent. Time-lapse imaging of cell death was performed using a Lionheart FX imaging system (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA) with fluorescence microscopy at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

NK92 cell-mediated antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC)

ADCC activity mediated by NK92 cells was assessed using rituximab-Fc variants with effector-silencing mutations. The target cell line Ramos (CD20-expressing) and the effector cell line NK92 (GFP-hFcγRIIIa-expressing) were harvested, with Ramos cells centrifuged at 300 × g for 3 min and NK92 cells centrifuged at 125 × g for 10 min, washed with PBS, and resuspended. Target cells and effector cells were added to a V-bottom 96-well plate (Corning, 3894) at 20,000 Ramos cells/well and either 100,000 (E:T = 5:1) or 200,000 (E:T = 10:1) GFP-hFcγRIIIa-NK92 cells/well, along with antibody samples. Following incubation at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 3 h, Sytox Red (5 nM) was added and incubated for an additional 15 min at RT. Plates were centrifuged at 200 × g for 5 min, washed twice with PBS, and analyzed by flow cytometry using a BD FACSLyric cytometer. Cytotoxicity was calculated as: Cytotoxicity (%) = [{(dead cell population / total cell population) × 100} – background].

Functional evaluation of complement-dependent cytotoxicity (CDC)

Complete media (RPMI with 10% heat-inactivated FBS) was used, and Daudi cells were immobilized on E-Plates at 40,000 cells/well using 4 µg/ml IMT Assay Anti-CD40 Tethering Kit (Agilent). After approximately 22 h, 50 µl of media was removed, and 10% human complement serum (Sigma-Aldrich) with antibodies diluted in complete media was added. Plates were incubated at RT for 30 min, then placed in the RTCA instrument to monitor cytotoxicity in real-time using RTCA Software 1.2 at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Cytotoxicity was calculated as: Cytotoxicity (%) = [{(Cell Index no Ab – Cell Index effector) / Cell Index no Ab} × 100], where the “effector” well contains target cells, human complements, and antibodies, and the “no Ab” well lacks antibodies.

Direct cytotoxicity assessment of antibody–drug conjugates in human cancer cell lines

Cultured cancer cell lines and normal human keratinocytes were diluted in culture medium and adjusted to the required density, seeded in 80 µl/well in 96-well culture plates, and placed in a CO2 incubator at 5% CO2 and 37 ℃ overnight. Conjugates were prepared by 1:5 serial dilutions to create a 9-point dose-response curve. These dilutions were then added to the wells to give final concentrations of the test articles of 1,000, 200, 40, 8, 1.6, 0.32, 0.064, 0.0128, and 0.0026 nM. After 144 h of incubation in a 5% CO2 incubator at 37 ℃, cell viability was measured with Celltiter-Glo according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cytotoxicity was calculated as: Cytotoxicity (%) = [{(dead cell population / total cell population) × 100} – background].

In vivo pharmacokinetic and safety evaluation

All animal experiments were performed in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Korea University (Approval No. KOREA-2023-0070) and Woojung Bio (Approval No. SN023005). Female C57BL/6 mice (8 weeks old, weighing 18–20 g) and BALB/c mice (6 weeks old, weighing 18–20 g) were purchased from Orient Bio (Seongnam, Korea) and Samtako Bio (Osan, Korea), respectively. Animals were housed in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) facility under a 12h light/dark cycle with free access to food and water. Mice were acclimated for at least one week before starting the procedures. Mice were randomly assigned to experimental groups to minimize potential confounders. Sample sizes were determined based on prior experience and pilot studies to ensure sufficient statistical power for detecting differences in pharmacokinetic and hematological profiles, while adhering to the “Reduction” principle of the 3Rs. No animals or data points were excluded from the analysis. Data analysis was performed by investigators blinded to group allocations.

Pharmacokinetic analysis was performed using female C57BL/6 mice as the animal model. Four (for LALA) or five mice were used per Fc variant, and antibodies were injected intravenously at 2 mg/kg. Blood samples were collected at 1, 24, 168, 312, 504, 672, 840, 1,008, 1,176, and 1,344 h post-injection, and serum was isolated by centrifugation at 1,000 × g for 15 min. The serum samples were stored at -80 °C until further analysis. Antibody concentration in serum was quantified by ELISA, with absorbance values compared to a standard curve. Pharmacokinetic parameters were calculated using the BA Calc2002 program. Data were analyzed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test for multiple comparisons using OriginPro 2015 (OriginLab, Northampton, MA, USA). A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Hematological toxicity was evaluated in female BALB/c mice. A total of five mice were allocated per test article, and antibodies were administered intravenously at 10 mg/kg. Blood samples were collected at 1, 3, 5, and 12 days post-injection from the posterior vena cava and abdominal aorta of surviving animals following anesthetization with isoflurane. For whole blood analysis, samples were collected into tubes containing dipotassium EDTA (EDTA-2K) as an anticoagulant and measured using a Sysmex XN-10 hematology analyzer. For hematological assessments, lymphocytes, neutrophils, monocytes, red blood cells, and platelets were quantified using a Sysmex XN-10 analyzer. Data were analyzed as described above.

Results

A goat IgG-inspired strategy identifies Fc variants with reduced FcγR binding beyond LALA

Among various animal-derived IgG antibodies, goat IgG exhibits minimal cross-reactivity with human Fcγ receptors (hFcγRs), and is therefore widely employed as a secondary antibody in hFcγR binding assays by our group and others [5, 39–41]. Inspired by this unique property, we hypothesized that specific amino acid residues in the hFcγR-binding regions of goat IgG1 and human IgG1, particularly within four key structural areas (the lower hinge, B/C loop, C′/E loop, and F/G loop), critically regulate FcγR engagement. These residues provide a rational basis for engineering Fc variants with silenced effector functions, thereby facilitating the identification of fully effector-silent Fc constructs (Supplementary Fig. 1).

To test this hypothesis, we performed systematic site-directed mutagenesis to introduce amino acid residues that differ between goat IgG1 and human IgG1 at hFcγR-binding interfaces into the human IgG1. This approach yielded 15 human IgG1 variants, which were subsequently expressed and purified in Expi293F cells (Supplementary Fig. 3A). We focused on hFcγRI and hFcγRIIIa-158V as representative activating receptors among the human FcγRs (hFcγRI, hFcγRIIa-131H, hFcγRIIa-131R, hFcγRIIb, hFcγRIIIa-158V, and hFcγRIIIa-158F), all of which modulate immune cell activation or inhibition via ITAM and ITIM motifs. hFcγRI was selected for its high affinity to human IgG1, and hFcγRIIIa-1 V for its critical role in ADCC. Using ELISA, we assessed variant binding to both receptors (Supplementary Fig. 2). Among the variants, eight constructs containing the L235P mutation (L235P (GI1-1), L235P/H268Q (GI1-5), L235P/Y296F/Y300F (GI1-6), L235P/E333V (GI1-7), L235P/H268Q/Y296F/Y300F (GI1-11), L235P/H268Q/E333V (GI1-12), L235P/Y296F/Y300F/E333V (GI1-13), and L235P/H268Q/Y296F/Y300F/E333V (GI1-15)) consistently showed markedly reduced binding to hFcγRI while retaining binding to hFcγRIIIa-158V comparable to wild-type human IgG1 (Supplementary Fig. 3B and C).

Given that the L235P substitution consistently reduced binding to hFcγRI, we selected this mutation as a backbone for further engineering. Aiming to silence binding to both hFcγRI and hFcγRIIIa-158V, we generated an additional variant by combining L235P with L234A, a residue substitution derived from the well-characterized L234A/L235A (LALA) variant [27], yielding the L234A/L235P (LALP) variant (Supplementary Fig. 4A). Although LALP modestly reduced binding across human FcγRs relative to LALA, it retained substantial interaction with hFcγRIIIa-158V, indicating that additional engineering is required to achieve complete FcγR silencing (Supplementary Fig. 4B–G).

CHO cell display enables isolation of stealth-body (SB) variants with abolished hFcγRs and hC1q binding

Residual hFcγR binding affinity poses potential risks, including unintended immune cell destruction [13, 14, 27] and accelerated therapeutic antibody clearance due to IgG uptake by hFcγR-expressing cells [11, 12, 42]. To address these issues and develop fully silenced Fc variants, we implemented a high-throughput screening strategy based on a CHO cell display system [43]. This platform enables the presentation of glycosylated proteins with human-like glycosylation profiles, thereby providing a stringent, physiologically relevant context for identifying Fc variants with fully silenced effector function. First, we established a CHO cell display system using the Flp-FRT recombination system, which enables the targeted integration of genes into specific genomic loci, ensuring stable and consistent expression of inserted genes [44]. Using this system, we integrated both IgG1 wild-type Fc and IgG1 T229L Fc into the CHO cell genome to generate stable cell lines. The IgG1 T229L Fc variant features a mutation in the canonical N-linked glycosylation motif (N-X-S/T) that prevents glycosylation at Asn297, resulting in significantly reduced hFcγR binding [39]. Together with wild-type Fc, these constructs confirmed that Fc glycosylation-dependent interactions are preserved on the CHO surface, validating the system for Fc engineering. We validated the expression and functionality of the displayed Fc regions by measuring their binding to hFcγRs and Protein A. For this, we used Alexa 647-labeled tetrameric hFcγRIIIa-15 to assess hFcγR binding and FITC-labeled Protein A to evaluate Fc expression levels. Robust binding of both probes confirmed proper surface display of glycosylated Fc, establishing the functionality of the CHO display platform (Fig. 1A and Supplementary Fig. 5).

Building on this platform, we constructed a diverse Fc library by introducing NNK codon mutations into four key hFcγR-binding regions of an IgG1 Fc template incorporating the LALP mutation. This strategy allowed for the randomization of amino acids at these critical positions, expanding library diversity and facilitating the identification of Fc variants with abolished hFcγR binding. Stable CHO cell lines displaying these Fc variants on their surface were then established. To drive directed evolution toward Fc variants with diminished hFcγR binding, we subjected the Fc-displaying CHO cells to negative selection by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS). We specifically isolated cells that did not bind to hFcγRIIIa-158V-Alexa 647, effectively enriching for Fc variants with reduced or eliminated hFcγR binding. This screening process yielded three candidate Fc variants (Supplementary Fig. 6). These selected Fc variants were then introduced into trastuzumab to assess their potential within a therapeutic antibody framework (Fig. 1B). ELISA analyses revealed that these variants showed no detectable binding to any human FcγRs or C1q (Fig. 1C–I). Based on their complete loss of effector function, these variants were designated Stealth-Body (SB) variants (SB1, SB5, and SB6).

SB variants confer complete hFcγRs and hC1q silencing while preserving antibody integrity

We conducted a cell-based hFcγR binding assay using HER2-expressing SKOV-3 cells to evaluate the interaction of hFcγRI with tumor cells opsonized by trastuzumab-SB variants. In this assay, SKOV-3 cells were treated with trastuzumab or its SB variants, followed by tetrameric hFcγRI-Alexa 647 and Protein A-FITC to assess hFcγRI binding across a range of probe concentrations (Supplementary Fig. 7). Strikingly, SB variants exhibited markedly reduced hFcγRI binding relative to LALA [27] and L234A/L235A/P329G (LALAPG) [28] controls across all tested concentrations.

To further characterize the hFcγR binding profile, we quantitatively assessed the binding affinity of these Fc variants using biolayer interferometry (BLI) on the Octet platform. Antibodies were loaded onto FAB2G sensors, which are pre-immobilized with a high-affinity ligand specific for the CH1 region of human IgG, thereby capturing the Fab region of the antibody and orienting the Fc region for optimal interaction with hFcγRs (Table 1 and Supplementary Fig. 8). Whereas trastuzumab-LALAPG retained detectable hFcγRI binding, all trastuzumab-SB variants showed complete abrogation of binding to hFcγRI with no detectable interaction with any human FcγR (Fig. 2A–E and Supplementary Fig. 8).

We next used ELISA to examine whether mutations in the lower hinge/upper Fc region of SB variants influenced antigen binding or the pH-dependent human FcRn (hFcRn) binding, which is critical for antibody serum persistence. All SB variants retained antigen-binding affinities equivalent to wild-type trastuzumab (Fig. 2F). At endosomal pH (pH 6.0), SB5 and SB6 displayed hFcRn binding profiles similar to wild-type trastuzumab, while SB1 showed a modestly reduced affinity (Fig. 2G and H). Under neutral pH, no SB variants exhibited detectable hFcRn binding, confirming preserved pH-dependent FcRn interaction. In addition, thermostability analyses revealed that trastuzumab-SB variants exhibited thermostability comparable to that of wild-type Fc and Fc variants containing LALA or LALAPG mutations (Supplementary Fig. 9). Collectively, these data establish that SB variants achieve complete abrogation of hFcγR and hC1q binding, while preserving antigen recognition, pH-dependent hFcRn interaction, and thermostability, supporting their function as fully effector-silent Fc scaffolds.

Additional N-linked glycosylation at the engineered D265N-derived Fc site in SB1

To determine whether the D265N substitution introduces an additional occupied N-linked glycosylation site in the Fc region, we performed released N-glycan profiling and site-specific glycopeptide analysis. The D265N substitution in SB1 generates a canonical N-linked glycosylation sequon, NVS, within the Fc peptide sequence TPEVTCVVVNVSHEDPEVK, whereas wild-type and SB6 do not contain this engineered sequon.

Released N-glycan profiling by HILIC-UPLC showed that wild-type and SB6 exhibited major Fc-type complex N-glycans, including G0F, G1F(1), G1F(2), and G2F, consistent with glycosylation derived predominantly from the conserved Fc N297 site (Supplemental Fig. 10A and C). In contrast, SB1 showed a distinct and more complex N-glycan profile, suggesting the presence of an additional N-glycan population beyond that observed in conventional Fc glycosylation (Supplemental Fig. 10B). Because released glycan analysis does not provide site-specific information, we further performed LC–MS/MS-based glycopeptide mapping.

LC–MS/MS analysis identified an SB1-specific glycopeptide corresponding to the engineered D265N-containing Fc peptide TPEVTCVVVNVSHEDPEVK carrying a Man7 N-glycan (Supplemental Fig. 10D). The representative MS/MS spectrum contained peptide backbone fragment ions as well as glycan-derived fragment ions, confirming both the glycopeptide sequence and glycan composition (Supplemental Fig. 10D). Extracted ion chromatograms generated for the representative precursor ion at m/z 1227.53 showed a clear SB1-specific signal, whereas the corresponding engineered-site glycopeptide signal was not detected in wild-type or SB6 (Supplemental Fig. 10E). These results demonstrate that the D265N substitution in SB1 generates an additional occupied N-linked glycosylation site in the Fc region, in addition to the conserved Fc N297 glycan.

SB variants completely eliminate Fc effector functions across antibody contexts

hFcγR binding affinity is closely linked to Fc-mediated effector functions of antibodies, such as ADCC and ADCP [1, 2, 5, 39, 40, 45]. To determine whether the elimination of hFcγR binding in SB variants effectively abolished these Fc effector functions, we performed in vitro functional assays using trastuzumab antibodies. ADCC was assessed across three HER2-positive cancer cell lines (SKBR-3, SKOV-3, and MDA-MB-453), each with different levels of HER2 expression, using human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) as effector cells. Consistent with partial hFcγR engagement, LALA and LALAPG variants retained measurable ADCC activity, whereas SB variants exhibited cytotoxicity indistinguishable from the isotype control across all tested cell lines (Fig. 3A, B and Supplementary Fig. 11). Notably, LALAPG retained residual cytotoxicity, whereas SB variants showed near-complete loss of ADCC activity. Imaging-based ADCC analyses further confirmed this pattern, demonstrating retained cytotoxicity for LALAPG and minimal activity for SB variants (Fig. 3C–H). Similarly, SB variants exhibited ADCP levels comparable to the isotype control, indicating complete loss of detectable ADCP activity (Supplementary Fig. 12). Collectively, these data demonstrate that SB variants eliminate Fc effector functions, including ADCC and ADCP, in the trastuzumab context as a direct consequence of abolished hFcγR engagement.

To further evaluate whether this effect extends across antibody contexts, we generated a panel of rituximab antibodies incorporating the Fc variants (Supplementary Fig. 13A). Using ELISA, we assessed the binding of rituximab-Fc variants to hFcγRs and hC1q. Consistent with trastuzumab-SB Fc variants, rituximab-SB variants exhibited no detectable binding to any human FcγR or C1q (Supplementary Fig. 13B–H). Next, ADCC assays were performed using Ramos cells, which express CD20 as a target antigen. As expected, LALA and LALAPG variants retained detectable cytotoxicity, whereas the SB variants exhibited minimal cytotoxicity comparable to that of the isotype control. Notably, SB6 displayed significantly lower cytotoxicity than LALAPG, consistent with complete abrogation of ADCC activity in the rituximab format (Fig. 4A and Supplementary Fig. 14). To assess whether CDC activity was also abolished due to the loss of hC1q binding, we conducted real-time CDC assays using Daudi cells and human serum containing complement proteins. Consistent with previous studies [46–48], LALA and LALAPG variants retained cytotoxicity, with LALAPG exhibiting significantly higher CDC activity than the isotype control (Fig. 4B, C, and Supplementary Fig. 15). In contrast, rituximab-SB variants exhibited minimal cytotoxicity comparable to the isotype control, indicating effective loss of CDC activity. These residual effects were attributed to Fab-mediated apoptosis rather than Fc-dependent mechanisms [49, 50]. Together, these findings demonstrate that SB variants robustly and reproducibly eliminate Fc effector functions across therapeutic IgG antibodies targeting distinct antigens.

SB variants abolish FcγRs binding across species and exhibit maintained or enhanced serum persistence in mice

In the pursuit of Fc variants with fully silenced effector functions, achieving complete elimination of FcγR binding across human and relevant preclinical species, such as mice and cynomolgus monkeys, is a key requirement for safe and translatable antibody therapeutics. Such cross-species silencing enhances the translatability of in vivo pharmacology while minimizing Fc-mediated toxicity in preclinical models. To evaluate SB variants across species, we assessed FcγR binding in murine and cynomolgus monkey systems, two widely used preclinical models. SB variants exhibited complete loss of binding to all orthologous FcγRs in both species (Supplementary Fig. 16A–H). This conserved abrogation across species supports the translatability of Fc effector silencing and reduces the risk of unintended immune activation in vivo.

Considering the central role of the Fc domain in controlling IgG serum half-life through FcRn engagement, we next analyzed the impact of SB variants on serum persistence by characterizing their pharmacokinetic profiles in C57BL/6 mice. Trastuzumab-SB variants, which exhibited pH-dependent binding to human FcRn (hFcRn) and mouse FcRn (mFcRn) comparable to wild-type trastuzumab (Fig. 2C and Supplementary Fig. 17), were administered by intravenous injection at 2 mg/kg. SB variants exhibited serum persistence comparable to trastuzumab, LALA, and LALAPG, with a non-significant trend toward prolonged circulation (Supplementary Fig. 18 and Table 2). Notably, SB5 showed significantly increased systemic exposure relative to wild-type and LALA. Together, these data indicate that SB variants maintain, and in some cases enhance, in vivo persistence while preserving fully silenced effector functions, supporting their therapeutic potential.

SB variants decouple FcγR-mediated off-target cytotoxicity from on-target ADC efficacy

To investigate the ability of SB variants to functionally abrogate interactions with FcγRs that drive off-target cytotoxicity, we introduced SB6, a representative SB variant, as the Fc scaffold in antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs) used as a mechanistic proof-of-concept (PoC). Non-targeting cetuximab (anti-EGFR) and targeting gemtuzumab (anti-CD33) antibodies with wild-type Fc or SB6 were conjugated with the microtubule-disrupting payload maleimidocaproyl-valine-citrulline-p-aminobenzyloxycarbonyl-monomethyl auristatin E (mc–VC–PAB–MMAE). The cytotoxic activity of these ADCs was evaluated in vitro using human cancer cell lines known to express hFcγRs. Because these cells do not express EGFR, cytotoxicity observed with cetuximab-based ADCs reflects FcγR-mediated, antigen-independent effects. HEK293 and Jurkat cells, which lack both hFcγRs and relevant target antigens, were included as negative controls.

Strikingly, cetuximab-SB6-MMAE exhibited no detectable cytotoxicity, whereas the corresponding wild-type Fc ADC retained substantial activity, demonstrating effective elimination of hFcγR-mediated off-target killing (Fig. 5A–C). In contrast, targeting gemtuzumab conjugates showed potent cytotoxicity, with the gemtuzumab–SB6–MMAE displaying potency comparable to that of the wild-type Fc ADC (Fig. 5D–F). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the SB6 variant preserves on-target ADC efficacy while eliminating FcγR-driven off-target cytotoxicity, highlighting its utility as an effector-silent Fc scaffold for ADCs and related therapeutic modalities.

SB variant enables immune activation without Fc-mediated toxicity in vivo

Immune modulation has emerged as a next-generation therapeutic strategy capable of inducing durable antitumor responses. Two pathways of particular interest are CD47 blockade, which relieves the “don’t eat me” signal and promotes phagocytic clearance of target cells, and agonistic activation of CD40 on antigen-presenting cells (APCs), thereby promoting T cell priming and activation. Although therapeutic antibodies targeting CD47 or CD40 have advanced into late-stage clinical trials, dose-limiting hematologic and systemic toxicities have hindered their clinical translation. Fc-mediated effector functions are major contributors to these toxicities, underscoring the need for effector-silent Fc architectures in immune-oncology applications.

To evaluate the extent to which an SB variant can mitigate such toxicities, we compared wild-type Fc and SB6 using murine surrogate CD47-targeting IgG–CD40L fusion proteins. Normal BALB/c mice received a single 10 mg/kg dose of either CD47-targeting IgG–CD40L fusion with wild-type Fc or CD47-targeting IgG–CD40L fusion with SB6, followed by hematologic and serum chemistry analyses at defined time points (Fig. 6 and Supplementary Fig. 19). Mice treated with the wild-type Fc fusion exhibited reductions in lymphocytes, red blood cells, and platelets, accompanied by increased neutrophils, monocytes, and splenomegaly, consistent with Fc-driven hematologic perturbation. Notably, at day 1 post-injection, the wild-type Fc group showed acute and severe platelet depletion relative to vehicle, reflecting dose-limiting thrombocytopenia associated with CD47-targeting therapies (Supplementary Fig. 19C). In contrast, SB6-containing fusion proteins preserved platelet counts at day 1, demonstrating a significant improvement relative to the wild-type Fc group. Furthermore, the SB6 group maintained overall hematologic parameters and spleen size comparable to those of vehicle controls and showed enhanced platelet preservation relative to the CD47 agonist control, indicating that the SB6 variant mitigates the severe hematologic toxicities associated with wild-type Fc formats. These findings demonstrate that SB variants enable effective CD47/CD40 pathway engagement while minimizing Fc-mediated hematologic toxicity, supporting their application in immuno-oncology formats requiring potent immune activation with improved safety margins.

Discussion

Growing recognition of the risks associated with residual Fc–FcγR and Fc–C1q interactions has driven a paradigm shift in therapeutic antibody design toward rigorous effector attenuation. In 2023 alone, 9 of 12 monoclonal antibodies approved by the US FDA incorporated Fc variants engineered to reduce effector functions, underscoring the increasing emphasis on safety and precision immunomodulation [22]. These trends highlight a critical unmet need for Fc architectures that fully eliminate immune effector engagement while preserving pharmacokinetics, manufacturability across emerging immunotherapy modalities.

Clinical experience further reinforces this need. Therapeutic antibodies such as muromonab, theralizumab (TGN1412), and magrolimab have demonstrated that incomplete effector silencing can result in severe toxicities, including cytokine release syndrome and hematologic adverse events [16, 17, 51]. Even minimal residual FcγR engagement can drive unintended immune activation [51–54], particularly in immune-engaging or payload-sensitive modalities, such as CD3 bispecific antibodies and antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs), where even minimal FcγR engagement can trigger systemic immune activation or off-target toxicity [12, 14, 15].

Despite extensive engineering efforts, currently available Fc variants remain suboptimal for applications requiring complete effector silencing. The LALA variant, while a key milestone, retains measurable binding to FcγRI, with a dissociation constant exceeding 100 nM, and persistent interaction with FcγRIIIa, particularly in inflammatory or high-avidity contexts, thereby maintaining residual cytotoxicity [27, 46]. The LALAPG variant, developed as a refinement to LALA through incorporation of P329G to further suppress FcγR binding [28], also maintains detectable affinity for FcγRI and measurable effector activity in multiple assays [55, 56]. More broadly, strategies based on limited site-directed mutagenesis or IgG isotype switching have not achieved complete abrogation of FcγR and C1q binding, Even advanced structure-prediction tools such as AlphaFold3 provide limited actionable insight due to the intrinsic flexibility and conformational heterogeneity of key Fc regions [35, 36]. Together, these limitations underscore fundamental constraints in existing Fc engineering strategies [27, 31, 34].

To overcome these challenges, we developed a strategy to generate a truly effector-null Fc variant by leveraging goat IgG-derived sequence features in combination with high-throughput functional selection [5, 39–41, 57]. Goat IgG, which exhibits minimal cross-reactivity with human FcγRs, provided a framework for identifying sequence determinants of reduced receptor engagement. However, sequence substitution alone was insufficient to completely abolish FcγRIIIa binding, indicating that complete effector silencing requires coordinated structural perturbations. To achieve this, we implemented our recently developed CHO cell–based display platform, enabling high-throughput selection of glycosylated Fc variants under native post-translational conditions [43], ultimately yielding SB variants that eliminate detectable binding to all human FcγRs and C1q.

Previously resolved Fc–FcγR and Fc–C1q complex structures provide a framework for interpreting the complete silencing observed in SB variants [58–60]. SB1 (LALPDN), SB5 (LALPPV), and SB6 (LALPPL) disrupt receptor binding through mechanisms analogous to LALA and LALAPG, primarily by perturbing hydrophobic interactions and inducing conformational rearrangements in the upper CH2 region. The proline introduced in the LALP scaffold likely induces local conformational distortion that disrupts the hydrophobic pocket critical for FcγRI interaction, thereby reducing affinity [58]. In parallel, the D265N substitution in SB1 is predicted to weaken hydrogen bonding with FcγRI and electrostatic interactions with other FcγRs while introducing N-glycosylation that increases local rigidity, thereby further suppressing FcγR and C1q engagement (Supplementary Fig. 10) [61]. Residue P329 is central to the “proline sandwich” motif required for FcγR binding and stabilization of the C1q-interacting loop [28]; accordingly, P329V in SB5 may increase loop flexibility, whereas P329L in SB6 likely introduces steric hindrance and loop distortion. Together, these combined mutations (L234A, L235P, D265N, and P329V/L) are proposed to induce coordinated structural perturbations that prevent stable receptor–Fc complex formation, thereby abolishing FcγR and C1q interactions.

Functionally, SB variants eliminated Fc-mediated effector responses across all tested contexts, including ADCC, ADCP, and CDC, and this effect was consistently observed across multiple antibody scaffolds, including trastuzumab and rituximab, underscoring their broad applicability. Even under PBMC co-culture conditions, where avidity-driven FcγRI clustering typically reveals residual cytotoxicity, SB variants remained indistinguishable from isotype controls, whereas LALAPG variants retained substantial ADCC activity. From a biochemical and biophysical standpoint, SB variants preserved pH-dependent FcRn binding, resulting in serum persistence in mice comparable to wild-type IgG1 and superior to that of LALA and LALAPG. SB variants further retained native-like glycosylation, high thermostability, low aggregation propensity, and robust expression, supporting a favorable developability profile for clinical translation.

In preclinical antibody development, murine and cynomolgus monkey models are most commonly used; however, the FcγR binding patterns of human IgG differ substantially from those of these species, limiting the predictive value of animal toxicology and pharmacology studies [26]. By achieving complete silencing across human, murine, and cynomolgus FcγRs, SB variants provide pan-species effector abrogation that enhances the translational relevance of preclinical studies. This cross-species consistency increases confidence that safety and efficacy profiles observed in animal models will translate to human outcomes and supports more predictive pharmacology, combination therapy assessment, and mechanistic studies in immune-oncology and autoimmunity.

Incorporation of an SB variant into a CD47-targeting IgG–CD40L fusion protein further demonstrated its utility in immune cell-engaging formats. In murine models, SB-containing fusion proteins attenuated immune overactivation, reducing lymphopenia, neutrophilia, and monocytosis relative to wild-type Fc, while preventing red blood cell and platelet depletion. These results provide direct evidence that the SB variant can effectively eliminate Fc-mediated toxicity in immune cell–targeting antibodies. Notably, clinical trials of anti-CD47 antibodies have been halted due to anemia and thrombocytopenia arising from Fc-dependent phagocytosis, whereas CD40-targeting antibodies have been developed predominantly as IgG2 or silent IgG2 formats to minimize Fc-mediated cytotoxicity [8, 10, 51, 54]. Within this context, our findings indicate that rigorous Fc silencing can substantially improve the safety of immune cell–engaging therapeutics at the interface of innate and adaptive immunity.

Wilkinson et al. recently reported the STR variant (L234S/L235T/G236R), representing a notable advance in Fc silencing, with robust abrogation of FcγRs and C1q binding across a range of in vitro assays [32]. However, its performance has not yet been validated in vivo in pharmacologic or disease-relevant models, which may limit its qualification as a clinically deployable Fc backbone for complex therapeutic settings. By contrast, SB variants achieved complete elimination of Fc-mediated cytotoxicity in assays using human immune cells and maintained consistent silencing across multiple antibody scaffolds, including trastuzumab, rituximab, and ADCs. This complete effector-null profile enables precise payload delivery in ADCs, preventing systemic toxicity and loss of tumor selectivity arising from even minimal FcγR engagement. Moreover, the ability of SB-containing CD47-targeting IgG–CD40L fusion proteins to mitigate hematologic perturbations in mice provides in vivo validation that SB-based Fc designs can expand the therapeutic window of immune cell-engaging biologics. These findings position Fc silencing, as implemented in SB variants, as a rational design principle for engineering safer immune-modulating biologics with expanded therapeutic indices.

Despite the breadth of this study, several limitations warrant future investigation to support clinical translation. While SB variants exhibited serum persistence equivalent to wild-type IgG1 in mice, validation in human FcRn-transgenic mice or non-human primate models will be required to confirm pharmacokinetic performance under human physiological conditions. High-resolution structural analyses, including X-ray crystallography or cryo-EM, will be essential to define the molecular basis of complete effector silencing and to refine our understanding of Fc–receptor interactions, guiding further optimization. Finally, although SB variants retain native glycosylation and exhibit high sequence homology to human IgG1, systematic evaluation of their immunogenicity, including anti-drug antibody (ADA) responses, remains necessary for clinical translation.

Conclusions

SB variants represent a significant advance in Fc engineering, achieving complete, glycosylation-compatible silencing of effector functions across diverse antibody modalities. By eliminating all detectable FcγR and C1q interactions while maintaining favorable developability, biophysical properties, and pharmacokinetics, SB variants provide a versatile backbone for a wide range of immunotherapeutic applications, including immune checkpoint inhibitors, CD3-engaging bispecific T-cell engagers, CD47-blocking antibodies, ADCs, Fc-cytokine fusions, and tolerogenic biologics. Their ability to integrate complete effector silencing with preserved pharmacokinetic and manufacturability profiles positions SB variants as a modular Fc platform for safer, more precisely controlled immune modulation in next-generation antibody and fusion protein therapeutics.
