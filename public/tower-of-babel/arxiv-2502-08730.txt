New Bounds for Sparse Variational Gaussian Processes
Michalis K. Titsias

Abstract
Sparse variational Gaussian processes (GPs) construct tractable posterior approximations to GP models. At the core of these methods is the assumption that the true posterior distribution over training function values f and inducing variables u is approximated by a variational distribution that incorporates the conditional GP prior p ⁡ ( f | u ) p(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u}) in its factorization. While this assumption is considered as fundamental, we show that for model training we can relax it through the use of a more general variational distribution q ⁡ ( f | u ) q(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u}) that depends on N N extra parameters, where N N is the number of training examples. In GP regression, we can analytically optimize the evidence lower bound over the extra parameters and express a tractable collapsed bound that is tighter than the previous bound. The new bound is also amenable to stochastic optimization and its implementation requires minor modifications to existing sparse GP code. Further, we also describe extensions to non-Gaussian likelihoods. On several datasets we demonstrate that our method can reduce bias when learning the hyperparameters and can lead to better predictive performance.



1
Introduction

Gaussian processes (GPs) are nonparametric models for learning functions using Bayesian learning. Thanks to their flexibility and ability to quantify uncertainty, GPs have found many applications in
machine learning
(
Rasmussen & Williams, 2006
)
, spatial modeling
(
Cressie, 1993
)
, computer experiments
(
O’Hagan, 1978
;
Gramacy, 2020
)
, Bayesian optimization
(
Jones et al., 1998
;
Garnett, 2023
)
, robotics and control
(
Deisenroth & Rasmussen, 2011
)
, unsupervised learning

(
Lawrence, 2005
)
and others.

Despite the numerous applications, GPs suffer from
\mathcal{O}(N^{3})
time cost and
\mathcal{O}(N^{2})
storage where
N
is the number of training examples. This has originated a large body of research on scalable or sparse GP methods
expanded in several decades; see e.g., Chapter 8 in
Rasmussen & Williams (2006)

for an early review and
Heaton et al. (2018)
;
Liu et al. (2020)
;
Leibfried et al. (2022)
for recent treatments.
An important class of methods
bases an approximation on a small set of
M\ll N

inducing points

(
Csato & Opper, 2002
;
Lawrence et al., 2002
;
Seeger et al., 2003
;
Snelson & Ghahramani, 2006
;
Quiñonero-Candela & Rasmussen, 2005
;
Banerjee et al., 2008
;
Finley et al., 2009
;
Titsias, 2009
;
Hensman et al., 2013
;
Bui et al., 2017
;
Burt et al., 2020
)

that reduce the time complexity to
\mathcal{O}(NM^{2})
and
the storage to
\mathcal{O}(NM)
.

Among inducing point methods, the sparse variational Gaussian process (SVGP), introduced for standard regression
(
Titsias, 2009
)
,
applies variational inference to obtain a posterior approximation and selects hyperparameters and inducing points by maximizing an evidence lower bound. Unlike
the prior approximation framework

(
Quiñonero-Candela & Rasmussen, 2005
)
, SVGP
leaves the GP prior unchanged and instead it reduces the cost to
\mathcal{O}(NM^{2})

by imposing a special structure on the variational
distribution. This framework has been extended
to stochastic gradient optimization

(
Hensman et al., 2013
)
and non-Gaussian likelihoods
(
Chai, 2012
;
Hensman et al., 2015
;
Lloyd et al., 2015
;
Dezfouli & Bonilla, 2015
;
Sheth et al., 2015
)
. Also, it has been explained as KL minimization between stochastic processes
(
de G. Matthews et al., 2016
)
.

An important aspect of the SVGP method is that it uses a special form for the variational distribution. It approximates the exact posterior distribution
p(\textbf{f},\textbf{u}|\textbf{y})
over the training function values
f
and the
inducing variables
u

(see
Section

2
for precise definitions)
by a variational distribution of the form
q(\textbf{f},\textbf{u})=p(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})q(\textbf{u})
, where

q(\textbf{u})
is some optimizable distribution over the inducing variables, while

p(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})
is the conditional GP prior.
This special form of the variational approximation seems to be fundamental, and it has been applied also to more complex GP models, such as those with multiple outputs

(
Álvarez et al., 2010
;
Nguyen & Bonilla, 2014
;
Yousefi et al., 2019
)
, uncertain inputs
(
Titsias & Lawrence, 2010
;
Damianou et al., 2016
)
and multiple layers
(
Damianou & Lawrence, 2013
;
Salimbeni & Deisenroth, 2017
)
. However, an open question regarding
the SVGP framework is whether this particular form of variational distribution
is really necessary to obtain scalable computations. The answer we give in this paper is that “it is not”, since at least for training a GP model it can be relaxed.

To this end, we derive new variational bounds for training
sparse GP regression models by replacing

p(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})
in the variational distribution with a more general conditional distribution
q(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})
. This
q(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})
depends on
N
additional parameters (on top of the parameters of
p(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})
), i.e., as many as the training examples, and it is constructed to enable better covariance approximation of the underlying true factor
p(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u},\textbf{y})
. We show how to analytically optimize over the
N
parameters and obtain a better posterior approximation together with a tighter
collapsed evidence lower bound.
The new bound is also amenable to stochastic gradient optimization, and its
simple form suggests that it can be implemented with minor modifications to existing sparse GP code. We also describe extensions of the method to non-Gaussian likelihoods. Furthermore, we point out the
concurrent work of
Bui et al. (2025)
who derived similar sparse GP approximations
and variational training objectives by using the same form for the
q(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})
distribution.

The remainder of the paper is as follows.
Section

2
provides
an overview of GPs and the variational approach to sparse GPs using inducing points.
Section

3

derives the new evidence lower bounds for training.
Section

4
discusses connections with previous works.
Section

5
presents experiments using several datasets showing that the new bounds can reduce underfitting bias and can lead to better predictive performance.
Section

6

concludes with a discussion and suggestions for future work.

2
Background

A GP is a distribution over functions specified by a mean function
m(\bm{x})
and a covariance or kernel function
k(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})
, where the kernel function is parametrized by
\theta
.
By assuming that
m(\bm{x})=0
we denote a GP draw as

f(\bm{x})\sim\mathcal{GP}(0,k(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})).

For a finite set of inputs

\textbf{X}=\{\bm{x}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
the
distribution over the function values
\textbf{f}=\{f_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
(stored as
N\times 1
vector with
f_{n}:=f(\bm{x}_{n})
) is
the multivariate Gaussian
p(\textbf{f})=\mathcal{N}(\textbf{f}|{\bf 0},\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}})
where the
N\times N
covariance
matrix
\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}
has entries
[\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}]_{ij}=k(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}_{j})
.

We consider standard GP regression
where we are given a set of training inputs

X
and corresponding noisy outputs

\textbf{y}=\{y_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
where

y_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
. Conditionally on the latent values
f
, these outputs follow a factorized
Gaussian likelihood,
p(\textbf{y}|\textbf{f})=\prod_{n=1}^{N}\mathcal{N}(y_{n}|f_{n},\sigma^{2})=\mathcal{N}(\textbf{y}|\textbf{f},\sigma^{2}I)
. The joint distribution over outputs
y

and latent values
f
is

p(\textbf{y}|\textbf{f})p(\textbf{f})=\mathcal{N}(\textbf{y}|\textbf{f},\sigma^{2}I)\mathcal{N}(\textbf{f}|{\bf 0},\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}).

(1)

To learn the hyperparameters

(\theta,\sigma^{2})
we can maximize the
log marginal likelihood which is analytically available,

\log p(\textbf{y})=\int p(\textbf{y}|\textbf{f})p(\textbf{f})d\textbf{f}=\log\mathcal{N}(\textbf{y}|{\bf 0},\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}+\sigma^{2}\textbf{I}).

(2)

After training we can perform predictions at test inputs
\textbf{X}_{*}

by first computing the posterior
over the corresponding test function values
\textbf{f}_{*}
:

\displaystyle p(\textbf{f}_{*}|\textbf{y})=\int p(\textbf{f}_{*}|\textbf{f})p(\textbf{f}|\textbf{y})d\textbf{f}=

(3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(\textbf{f}_{*}|\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}_{*}\textbf{f}}(\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}\!+\!\sigma^{2}\textbf{I})^{-1}\textbf{f},\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}_{*}\textbf{f}_{*}}\!\!-\!\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}_{*}\textbf{f}}(\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}\!+\!\sigma^{2}\textbf{I})^{-1}\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}_{*}}\!)

and then writing the predictive density as
p(\textbf{y}_{*}|\textbf{y})=\int\mathcal{N}(\textbf{y}_{*}|\textbf{f}_{*},\sigma^{2}\textbf{I})p(\textbf{f}_{*}|\textbf{y})d\textbf{f}_{*}
, which is the same as the above Gaussian but with
\sigma^{2}\textbf{I}
added to the covariance.

While the log marginal likelihood and predictive density have closed-form
expressions, they require
the inversion of
\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}+\sigma^{2}\textbf{I}
which costs
\mathcal{O}(N^{3})
and it is prohibitive for large datasets.
Next we review methods using inducing
points and particularly the
variational approach
(
Titsias, 2009
)
that our method in
Section

3
improves upon.

2.1
Sparse Variational Gaussian Process (SVGP)

The idea of inducing points
is to base a GP approximation
on a smaller set of
M\ll N
function values; see e.g.,
Csato & Opper (2002)
;
Seeger et al. (2003)
;
Snelson & Ghahramani (2006)
;
Quiñonero-Candela & Rasmussen (2005)
.
Snelson & Ghahramani (2006)

introduced pseudo inputs
by instantiating extra GP function values
\textbf{u}=\{f(\bm{z}_{m})\}_{m=1}^{M}
evaluated at locations
\textbf{Z}=\{\bm{z}_{m}\}_{m=1}^{M}

that can be optimized freely with gradient-based methods. However,
the GP prior modification procedure

(
Quiñonero-Candela & Rasmussen, 2005
;
Snelson & Ghahramani, 2006
)

does not result in a rigorous approximation to the GP model. An alternative variational inference method

(
Titsias, 2009
)
, next referred to as
SVGP
1
1

1

Another common name for this method is Variational Free Energy (VFE); see
Bui et al. (2017)
;
Bauer et al. (2016)
;
Liu et al. (2020)
.
, does not modify the GP prior but instead it augments the model with extra function values
u
:

\displaystyle p(\textbf{y},\textbf{f},\textbf{u})\!=\!p(\textbf{y}|\textbf{f})p(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})p(\textbf{u})\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \text{augmented joint}

(4)

\displaystyle p(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})\!=\!\mathcal{N}(\textbf{f}|\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}\textbf{K}_{\textbf{u}\textbf{u}}^{-1}\textbf{u},\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}\!-\!\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{u}}\textbf{K}_{\textbf{u}\textbf{u}}^{-1}\textbf{K}_{\textbf{u}\textbf{f}})\ \ \ \text{cond. GP}

(5)

\displaystyle p(\textbf{u})\!=\!\mathcal{N}(\textbf{u}|{\bf 0},\textbf{K}_{\textbf{u}\textbf{u}})\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \text{inducing GP prior}

(6)

where
\textbf{K}_{\textbf{u}\textbf{u}}
is the
M\times M
covariance matrix on the
inducing inputs
Z
,

\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{u}}
is the
N\times M
cross covariance between points in
X
and
Z
, while
\textbf{K}_{\textbf{u}\textbf{f}}=\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{u}}^{\top}
. SVGP approximates the exact posterior
p(\textbf{f},\textbf{u}|\textbf{y})
by a variational
distribution
q(\textbf{f},\textbf{u})
through the minimization of
\text{KL}[q(\textbf{f},\textbf{u})||p(\textbf{f},\textbf{u}|\textbf{y})]
. A critical assumption is the following
choice for
q
:

q(\textbf{f},\textbf{u})=p(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})q(\textbf{u}),

(7)

where
q(\textbf{u})
is an optimizable
M
-dimensional variational distribution, while
p(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})
is the same conditional GP prior from
Equation

5

that appears in the joint
in (
4
). The KL minimization is expressed as the maximization of an evidence lower bound (ELBO) on the log marginal likelihood,

\displaystyle\log p(\textbf{y})

\displaystyle\geq\int p(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})q(\textbf{u})\log\frac{p(\textbf{y}|\textbf{f})\cancel{p(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})}p(\textbf{u})}{\cancel{p(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})}q(\textbf{u})}d\textbf{f}d\textbf{u}

\displaystyle=\int q(\textbf{u})\log\frac{\exp\{\int p(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})\log p(\textbf{y}|\textbf{f})d\textbf{f}\}p(\textbf{u})}{q(\textbf{u})}d\textbf{u}.

If we optimize over
q(\textbf{u})
and obtain the optimal choice
q^{*}(\textbf{u})\propto\exp\left\{\int p(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})\log p(\textbf{y}|\textbf{f})d\textbf{f}\right\}p(\textbf{u})
, then we can substitute this
q^{*}(\textbf{u})
in the last line above and express the
so called collapsed bound, having the general form

\log p(\textbf{y})\!\geq\!\mathcal{F}\!=\!\log\!\int\!\exp\left\{\int p(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})\log p(\textbf{y}|\textbf{f})d\textbf{f}\right\}p(\textbf{u})d\textbf{u}

which for the standard GP regression
model takes the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}=\underbrace{\log\mathcal{N}(\textbf{y}|{\bf 0},\textbf{Q}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}+\sigma^{2}\textbf{I})}_{\text{DTC log lik}}-\underbrace{\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\text{tr}\left(\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}-\textbf{Q}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}\right)}_{\text{trace term}},

(8)

where
\textbf{Q}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}=\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{u}}\textbf{K}_{\textbf{u}\textbf{u}}^{-1}\textbf{K}_{\textbf{u}\textbf{f}}
is the M-rank Nystróm matrix.
The first term in the bound is the deterministic training conditional (DTC) log likelihood
(
Seeger et al., 2003
;
Quiñonero-Candela & Rasmussen, 2005
)

while the second is a regularization
term which, since
\text{tr}(\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}-\textbf{Q}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}})\geq 0
, promotes
\textbf{Q}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}
to stay close to
\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}
. The inducing points
Z
can be learned as variational parameters by maximizing the bound jointly with the hyperparameters
(\theta,\sigma^{2})
, which requires

\mathcal{O}(NM^{2})
operations per optimization step.
Hensman et al. (2013)
further reduced the operations to
\mathcal{O}(M^{3})

per optimization step by applying stochastic minibatch training for
maximizing the uncollapsed version of the bound; see
Section

3.2
.

To obtain the form of the GP posterior
over any test function values
\textbf{f}_{*}
we can first write the exact form

p(\textbf{f}_{*}|\textbf{y})=\int p(\textbf{f}_{*}|\textbf{f},\textbf{u})p(\textbf{f},\textbf{u}|\textbf{y})d\textbf{f}d\textbf{u},

(9)

where
p(\textbf{f}_{*}|\textbf{f},\textbf{u})
is the conditional GP of
\textbf{f}_{*}
given
training function values
f
and inducing values
u
while

p(\textbf{f},\textbf{u}|\textbf{y})
is the posterior
over
(\textbf{f},\textbf{u})
written also
as

p(\textbf{f},\textbf{u}|\textbf{y})=p(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u},\textbf{y})p(\textbf{u}|\textbf{y}).

(10)

The SVGP method approximates
p(\textbf{f},\textbf{u}|\textbf{y})
by
q(\textbf{f},\textbf{u})
and therefore by plugging in this
q
into (
9
) we obtain

q(\textbf{f}_{*}|\textbf{y})\!=\!\!\int p(\textbf{f}_{*}|\textbf{f},\textbf{u})p(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})q(\textbf{u})d\textbf{f}d\textbf{u}\!=\!\!\int p(\textbf{f}_{*}|\textbf{u})q(\textbf{u})d\textbf{u},

(11)

where
p(\textbf{f}_{*}|\textbf{u})=\int p(\textbf{f}_{*}|\textbf{f},\textbf{u})p(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})d\textbf{f}
comes from the GP consistency. For completeness, in
Appendix

A

we include further details about SVGP such as a derivation of the collapsed bound and the
Gaussian form
of the optimal

q^{*}(\textbf{u})
.

We conclude this review of SVGP for regression with a couple of remarks that will be useful next.

Remark 2.1
.

The approximation becomes exact when

\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}=\textbf{Q}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}
and the collapsed bound matches the log marginal likelihood in
(
2
). However, to obtain good approximations we may need sufficiently large number of inducing points
(
Burt et al., 2020
)
. Otherwise the bound will cause underfitting.
For instance, as studied by

Bauer et al. (2016)

and
Titsias (2009)

the SVGP bound tends to overestimate the noise variance
\sigma^{2}
.

Remark 2.2
.

SVGP approximates
p(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u},\textbf{y})
in the exact posterior
in (
10
)
by the conditional GP

p(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})
, in the variational posterior in (
7
),
while
q(\textbf{u})
is treated optimally by KL minimization. If

p(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u},\textbf{y})\!=\!p(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})
then
\text{KL}[q(\textbf{f},\textbf{u})||p(\textbf{f},\textbf{u}|\textbf{y})]=0
and the approximation becomes exact, meaning
q(\textbf{f}_{*}|\textbf{y})\!=\!p(\textbf{f}_{*}|\textbf{y})
for any
\textbf{f}_{*}=f_{*}(\textbf{X}_{*})
.

3
Proposed Method: Tighter Bounds

Remark
2.1
suggests that
it would be useful to tighten the collapsed bound
in order to reduce underfitting bias
and match better exact GP training.
Remark
2.2
suggests that one way to tighten the bound is to replace
p(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})
, in the variational approximation in (
7
), with another distribution
that can better approximate

p(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u},\textbf{y})
. Next we develop a method that does this while keeping the cost unchanged.

Let us write the
exact form of
p(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u},\textbf{y})
. By noting that this quantity is the exact posterior over
f
in a GP regression model with joint
p(\textbf{y}|\textbf{f})p(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})

we conclude that this is

p(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u},\textbf{y})=\mathcal{N}\left(\textbf{f}|\textbf{m}(\textbf{y},\textbf{u}),(\widetilde{\textbf{K}}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}^{-1}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\textbf{I})^{-1}\right),

where
\textbf{m}(\textbf{y},\textbf{u})=\mathbb{E}[\textbf{f}|\textbf{u}]+\widetilde{\textbf{K}}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}(\widetilde{\textbf{K}}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}+\sigma^{2}\textbf{I})^{-1}(\textbf{y}-\mathbb{E}[\textbf{f}|\textbf{u}])

with
\mathbb{E}[\textbf{f}|\textbf{u}]=\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{u}}\textbf{K}_{\textbf{u}\textbf{u}}^{-1}\textbf{u}
and
\widetilde{\textbf{K}}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}=\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}-\textbf{Q}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}
. Note that under this notation,

p(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})=\mathcal{N}(\textbf{f}|\mathbb{E}[\textbf{f}|\textbf{u}],\widetilde{\textbf{K}}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}})
. We will construct a new
q(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})

that keeps the same mean
\mathbb{E}[\textbf{f}|\textbf{u}]

as
p(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})
but it replaces
\widetilde{\textbf{K}}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}
with a closer approximation to the
covariance

(\widetilde{\textbf{K}}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}^{-1}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\textbf{I})^{-1}
of
p(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u},\textbf{y})
. We first
write this matrix as

(\widetilde{\textbf{K}}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}^{-1}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\textbf{I})^{-1}=\widetilde{\textbf{K}}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\textbf{I}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\widetilde{\textbf{K}}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}})^{-1}\widetilde{\textbf{K}}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(12)

Then we approximate the inverse

(\textbf{I}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\widetilde{\textbf{K}}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}})^{-1}
by a diagonal matrix
\textbf{V}=\text{diag}(v_{1},\ldots,v_{N})
of
N
variational parameters
v_{i}>0
. In other words, in the initial
q(\textbf{f},\textbf{u})=p(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})q(\textbf{u})
we will replace
p(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})
by

q(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})=\mathcal{N}(\textbf{f}|\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{u}}\textbf{K}_{\textbf{u}\textbf{u}}^{-1}\textbf{u},(\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}-\textbf{Q}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}})^{\frac{1}{2}}\textbf{V}(\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}-\textbf{Q}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}})^{\frac{1}{2}}).

(13)

The ELBO now is written as

\displaystyle\int q(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})q(\textbf{u})\log\frac{p(\textbf{y}|\textbf{f})p(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})p(\textbf{u})}{q(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})q(\textbf{u})}d\textbf{f}d\textbf{u}=

\displaystyle\int\!\!q(\textbf{u})\!\left\{\!\log\frac{e^{\mathbb{E}_{q(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})}[\log p(\textbf{y}|\textbf{f})]}p(\textbf{u})}{q(\textbf{u})}\!-\!\text{KL}[q(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})||p(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})]\!\right\}\!d\textbf{u}

and the challenge is to see whether

\text{KL}[q(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})||p(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})]

and
\mathbb{E}_{q(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})}[\log p(\textbf{y}|\textbf{f})]

are computable in
\mathcal{O}(NM^{2})
time.
We have the following results (proofs are in
Appendix

B
).

Lemma 3.1
.

\text{KL}[q(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})||p(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})]=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(v_{i}-\log v_{i}-1)
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let us denote the diagonal elements of
\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}-\textbf{Q}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}
as

k_{ii}-q_{ii}
for
i=1,\ldots,N
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{q(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})}[\log p(\textbf{y}|\textbf{f})]

\displaystyle\!=\!\log\mathcal{N}(\textbf{y}|\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{u}}\textbf{K}_{\textbf{u}\textbf{u}}^{-1}\textbf{u},\sigma^{2}\textbf{I})-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}v_{i}(k_{ii}-q_{ii}).

(14)

By combining the two lemmas the full bound is written as

\displaystyle\int\!\!q(\textbf{u})\log\frac{\mathcal{N}(\textbf{y}|\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{u}}\textbf{K}_{\textbf{u}\textbf{u}}^{-1}\textbf{u},\sigma^{2}\textbf{I})p(\textbf{u})}{q(\textbf{u})}d\textbf{u}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\{v_{i}\left(1+\frac{k_{ii}-q_{ii}}{\sigma^{2}}\right)-\log v_{i}-1\right\}.

(15)

Proposition 3.3
.

Maximizing the bound in (
15
) with respect to
q(\textbf{u})

and each
v_{i}
results in the
optimal settings
q^{*}(\textbf{u})\propto\mathcal{N}(\textbf{y}|\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{u}}\textbf{K}_{\textbf{u}\textbf{u}}^{-1}\textbf{u},\sigma^{2}\textbf{I})p(\textbf{u})
and

v_{i}^{*}=\left(1+\frac{k_{ii}-q_{ii}}{\sigma^{2}}\right)^{-1}
. By substituting these values
back to (
15
) we obtain

\mathcal{F}_{new}\!=\!\log\mathcal{N}(\textbf{y}|{\bf 0},\textbf{Q}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}+\sigma^{2}\textbf{I})-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log\left(\!1+\frac{k_{ii}-q_{ii}}{\sigma^{2}}\!\right).

(16)

The first term is the
DTC log likelihood as in the original bound in (
8
),
but the regularization term
makes the bound tighter,
i.e.,
\log p(\textbf{y})\geq\mathcal{F}_{new}\geq\mathcal{F}
, due to the inequality
\log(a+1)\leq a
. Also since
\log(a+1)<a
for all
a>0
, if
\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}\neq\textbf{Q}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}

(so there is at least one
k_{ii}-q_{ii}>0
), then
\mathcal{F}_{new}>\mathcal{F}
. This means that
\mathcal{F}_{new}
is strictly better than
\mathcal{F}
unless both bounds match exactly the log marginal likelihood.

Clearly,
\mathcal{F}_{new}
has
\mathcal{O}(NM^{2})
cost and its implementation requires a minor modification to the initial bound. The optimal
q^{*}(\textbf{u})

is the same as in the initial SVGP method, while
an interpretation of the optimal
v_{i}^{*}
values
is the following.

Remark 3.4
.

The diagonal matrix
\textbf{V}^{*}
(with the optimal
v_{i}^{*}
values in its diagonal) is the inverse obtained after zeroing out the off-diagonal elements of
\textbf{I}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}(\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}-\textbf{Q}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}})
,
i.e.,
\textbf{V}^{*}=\text{diag}[\textbf{I}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}(\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}-\textbf{Q}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}})]^{-1}
which approximates

(\textbf{I}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}(\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}-\textbf{Q}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}))^{-1}

in
Equation

12
.
Also note that in the ordering of positive definite matrices it holds
\textbf{V}^{*}\leq\textbf{I}
, from which it follows that
q(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})
has smaller covariance than
p(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})
and more accurately approximates the covariance of
p(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u},\textbf{y})
.
The latter as implied by
Equation

12
,
has also smaller covariance than
p(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})
.

3.1
Predictions

To perform
predictions we will be using
the same predictive posterior
from
Equation

11
, i.e.,

q(\textbf{f}_{*}|\textbf{y})=\int p(\textbf{f}_{*}|\textbf{u})q(\textbf{u})d\textbf{u},

where the optimal
q^{*}(\textbf{u})
(see
Appendix

A
)
is exactly the same as in
the standard SVGP method. The alternative expression (and strictly speaking more appropriate
since our variational approximation is
q(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})q(\textbf{u})
) is given by

q_{high\_cost}(\textbf{f}_{*}|\textbf{y})\!=\!\!\int p(\textbf{f}_{*}|\textbf{f},\textbf{u})q(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})q(\textbf{u})d\textbf{f}d\textbf{u}.

(17)

But this is expensive since it has cost
\mathcal{O}(N^{3})
. The reason is that
\int p(\textbf{f}_{*}|\textbf{f},\textbf{u})q(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})d\textbf{f}
does not simplify anymore since
q(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})

is not the conditional GP, which
means that
p(\textbf{f}_{*}|\textbf{f},\textbf{u})
and
q(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})
are not consistent
under the GP prior.
Nevertheless,

q(\textbf{f}_{*}|\textbf{y})
and
q_{high\_cost}(\textbf{f}_{*}|\textbf{y})
have exactly the same mean, since
q(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})
and
p(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})
have the
same mean, but the tractable
q
will give higher variances than
q_{high\_cost}
.

3.2
Stochastic Minibatch Training

The initial SVGP method
(
Titsias, 2009
)
does the training in a batch mode where all data are used in each optimization step. Stochastic optimization using minibatches was proposed by
Hensman et al. (2013)
.
Here, we apply our new approximation to this stochastic method.

We start from
Equation

15
,
and substitute only the optimal values for each
v_{i}

without using the optimal setting for
q(\textbf{u})
. This results in the uncollapsed
bound

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\biggl\{\mathbb{E}_{q(\textbf{u})}[\log\mathcal{N}(y_{i}|\textbf{k}_{f_{i}\textbf{u}}\textbf{K}_{\textbf{u}\textbf{u}}^{-1}\textbf{u},\sigma^{2})]

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\log\left(1+\frac{k_{ii}-q_{ii}}{\sigma^{2}}\right)\biggr\}-\text{KL}[q(\textbf{u})||p(\textbf{u})],

(18)

where
\textbf{k}_{f_{i}\textbf{u}}
is the
1\times M
vector of all kernel
values between the training input
\bm{x}_{i}
and the inducing inputs
Z
, while
the expectation under
q(\textbf{u})
in the first line is
analytic; see
Hensman et al. (2013)
. The above bound is strictly better than
the previous uncollapsed bound in
Hensman et al. (2013)
,
since
-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}(k_{ii}-q_{ii})\leq-\frac{1}{2}\log\left(1+\frac{k_{ii}-q_{ii}}{\sigma^{2}}\right)
.
Based on the above we can apply stochastic gradient methods to optimize

q(\textbf{u})
and the hyperparameters by subsampling
data minibatches to deal with the sum over the
N
training points, i.e., at each iteration we use the stochastic ELBO:

\displaystyle\frac{N}{|\mathcal{B}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}}\biggl\{\mathbb{E}_{q(\textbf{u})}[\log\mathcal{N}(y_{i}|\textbf{k}_{f_{i}\textbf{u}}\textbf{K}_{\textbf{u}\textbf{u}}^{-1}\textbf{u},\sigma^{2})]

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\log\left(1+\frac{k_{ii}-q_{ii}}{\sigma^{2}}\right)\biggr\}-\text{KL}[q(\textbf{u})||p(\textbf{u})],

(19)

where
\mathcal{B}
denotes a minibatch.

The most common parametrization of
q(\textbf{u})

is
q(\textbf{u})=\mathcal{N}(\textbf{u}|\textbf{m},\textbf{S})
where the mean vector
m
and covariance matrix
S
are
variational parameters. Another popular
parametrization, for instance used as the default in GPflow
(
de G. Matthews et al., 2017
)
, is the whitened
parametrization that we consider in our experiments. For any choice of

q(\textbf{u})
, the new bound is always tighter than its corresponding
previous uncollapsed bound and requires minor modifications to existing
implementations, i.e., to replace the previous term
-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}(k_{ii}-q_{ii})
with
-\frac{1}{2}\log\left(1+\frac{k_{ii}-q_{ii}}{\sigma^{2}}\right)
.

3.3
Non-Gaussian Likelihoods

Consider a factorized likelihood
p(\textbf{y}|\textbf{f})=\prod_{i=1}^{N}p(y_{i}|f_{i})

where
p(y_{i}|f_{i})
is non-Gaussian, e.g., Bernoulli for binary outputs
or Poisson for counts.
In this non-conjugate setting the sparse
variational GP approximation imposes the same form for the variational distribution, i.e.,
q(\textbf{f},\textbf{u})=p(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})q(\textbf{u})

where
p(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})
is the
conditional GP prior. As shown in several works
(
Chai, 2012
;
Hensman et al., 2015
;
Lloyd et al., 2015
;
Dezfouli & Bonilla, 2015
;
Sheth et al., 2015
)
, this leads to the bound

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}_{q(f_{i})}[\log p(y_{i}|f_{i})]-\text{KL}[q(\textbf{u})||p(\textbf{u})],

(20)

where
q(f_{i})=\int p(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})q(\textbf{u})d\textbf{f}_{-i}d\textbf{u}
is the marginal over

f_{i}:=f(\bm{x}_{i})
with respect to the approximate posterior
q(\textbf{f},\textbf{u})
. Given
that
q(\textbf{u})
is Gaussian with mean
m
and covariance

S
,
q(f_{i})
can be computed fast in
\mathcal{O}(M^{2})
time (after precomputing the Cholesky factorization of

\textbf{K}_{\textbf{u}\textbf{u}}
) as follows

q(f_{i})=\mathcal{N}(f_{i}|\textbf{k}_{f_{i}\textbf{u}}\textbf{K}_{\textbf{u}\textbf{u}}^{-1}\textbf{m},k_{ii}-q_{ii}+\textbf{k}_{f_{i}\textbf{u}}\textbf{K}_{\textbf{u}\textbf{u}}^{-1}\textbf{S}\textbf{K}_{\textbf{u}\textbf{u}}^{-1}\textbf{k}_{\textbf{u}f_{i}}).

(21)

For the discussion next it is useful to observe that the efficiency when computing
q(f_{i})
comes from
p(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})
being a conditional GP prior, so
expressing
p(f_{i}|\textbf{u})
is trivial.

Suppose now that we wish to impose the more structured variational
approximation
q(\textbf{f},\textbf{u})=q(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})q(\textbf{u})
where

q(\textbf{u})=\mathcal{N}(\textbf{u}|\textbf{m},\textbf{S})
and
q(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})

is given by

Equation

13
. The bound can be written as

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}_{q(f_{i})}[\log p(y_{i}|f_{i})]-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(v_{i}-\log v_{i}-1)

\displaystyle-\text{KL}[q(\textbf{u})||p(\textbf{u})],

(22)

where we used the fact that

\text{KL}[q(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})||p(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})]
is obtained from
Lemma

3.1
. The above bound
is not computationally efficient since
the marginal
q(f_{i})=\int q(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})q(\textbf{u})d\textbf{f}_{-i}d\textbf{u}

has
\mathcal{O}(N^{3})
cost. This is because
the marginalization
q(f_{i}|\textbf{u})=\int q(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})d\textbf{f}_{-i}
cannot be trivially expressed, due to the complex structure of the covariance

(\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}-\textbf{Q}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}})^{\frac{1}{2}}\textbf{V}(\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}-\textbf{Q}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}})^{\frac{1}{2}}
in
q(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})
. To overcome this, we will use a simplified version of
q(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})
, in which we choose a spherical
\textbf{V}=v\textbf{I}
with
v>0
. Then, things become tractable.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
q(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})=\mathcal{N}(\textbf{f}|\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{u}}\textbf{K}_{\textbf{u}\textbf{u}}^{-1}\textbf{u},v(\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}-\textbf{Q}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}))
for
v>0
. Then (
22
) is computed in
\mathcal{O}(NM^{2})
time as

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}_{q(f_{i})}[\log p(y_{i}|f_{i})]-\frac{N}{2}(v-\log v-1)

\displaystyle-\text{KL}[q(\textbf{u})||p(\textbf{u})],

(23)

where the marginal is
q(f_{i})=\mathcal{N}(f_{i}|\textbf{k}_{f_{i}\textbf{u}}\textbf{K}_{\textbf{u}\textbf{u}}^{-1}\textbf{m},v(k_{ii}-q_{ii})+\textbf{k}_{f_{i}\textbf{u}}\textbf{K}_{\textbf{u}\textbf{u}}^{-1}\textbf{S}\textbf{K}_{\textbf{u}\textbf{u}}^{-1}\textbf{k}_{\textbf{u}f_{i}})
.

The parameter
v
multiplies the term

k_{ii}-q_{ii}
inside the variance of

q(f_{i})
, and it also appears in the regularization term

-\frac{N}{2}(v-\log v-1)
. If
v=1
the bound
in (
23
) reduces to
(
20
), while by
optimizing over
v
it can become a tighter bound.
The optimization
of
v
is done jointly
with the remaining parameters
\textbf{m},\textbf{S},\textbf{Z},\theta
using gradient-based methods. Stochastic gradients can also be used
by subsampling minibatches
to deal with the sum

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}_{q(f_{i})}[\log p(y_{i}|f_{i})]
and reduce the complexity to
\mathcal{O}(M^{3})
.

The above framework can be extended to non-conjugate models having multiple functions, such as multi-class GP classification, by introducing a separate
v
parameter per GP function.
In our experiments, we consider only
single-function non-conjugate GP models and we leave the experimentation with more complex models for future work.

4
Related Work

Several recent works on sparse GPs focus on constructing efficient inducing points, such as works that place inducing points on a grid
(
Wilson & Nickisch, 2015
;
Evans & Nair, 2018
;
Gardner et al., 2018
)
, construct inter-domain Fourier features
(
Lázaro-Gredilla & Figueiras-Vidal, 2009
;
Hensman et al., 2018
)
, provide Bayesian treatments to inducing inputs
(
Rossi et al., 2021
)

or use nearest neighbor
sparsity structures

(
Tran et al., 2021
;
Wu et al., 2022
)
.
There exist also algorithms that allow to increase the number of inducing points using the decoupled method
(
Cheng & Boots, 2017
;
Havasi et al., 2018
)

and the related orthogonally decoupled approaches

(
Salimbeni et al., 2018
;
Shi et al., 2020
;
Sun et al., 2021
;
Tiao et al., 2023
)
.
Our contribution is orthogonal to these previous methods since
we relax the conditional GP
prior
assumption in the posterior variational approximation. This means that our method could be used to improve previous variational sparse GP approaches,
as the ones mentioned above as well as earlier schemes that select inducing points from the training inputs
(
Cao et al., 2013
;
Chai, 2012
;
Schreiter et al., 2016
)
.

Zhu et al. (2023)
proposed
inducing points GP approximations that change the conditional GP
p(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})
in the variational approximation to a modified conditional GP that uses different kernel hyperparameters in its mean vector.
Note that our method differs since
our
q(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})
directly tries to
construct a better approximation to the exact posterior

p(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u},\textbf{y})
, using the extra
V
variational parameters,
without changing the kernel hyperparameters; see
Section

3
. More
importantly, our method has
\mathcal{O}(NM^{2})
cost, while the ELBO in
Zhu et al. (2023)
(see Section 3.1 and Appendix A.1 in their paper) has cubic cost
\mathcal{O}(N^{3})
since it depends
on the inverse of
\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}-\textbf{Q}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}
(denoted as
\tilde{\textbf{K}}_{nn}
in their paper).

Artemev et al. (2021)

derived an upper bound on the log determinant
\log|\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}+\sigma^{2}\textbf{I}|
in the exact GP log marginal likelihood and obtained the following tighter upper bound to the initial trace
regularization term
-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\text{tr}\left(\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}-\textbf{Q}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}\right)
:

-\frac{N}{2}\log\left(1+\frac{\text{tr}(\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}-\textbf{Q}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}})}{N\sigma^{2}}\right).

(24)

Our bound is tighter since
from Jensen’s inequality
it holds
-\frac{N}{2}\log\left(1+\frac{\text{tr}(\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}-\textbf{Q}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}})}{N\sigma^{2}}\right)\leq-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log\left(1+\frac{k_{ii}-q_{ii}}{\sigma^{2}}\right)
. Further, the above regularization term
can be interpreted as a restricted special case of our method, obtained through a
q(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})
from
Equation

13
where the diagonal matrix
V
is constrained to be spherical
\textbf{V}=v\textbf{I}
; see
Section

B.4
. Finally note,
that unlike (
24
)
(where the sum is inside the logarithm) our bound allows to apply stochastic optimization
as described in
Section

3.2
.

Finally,

Bui et al. (2017)

used power expectation
propagation that minimizes
\alpha
-divergence and derived an approximation
to the log marginal likelihood that interpolates between the FITC (
\alpha=1
) log marginal
likelihood
(
Snelson & Ghahramani, 2006
;
Quiñonero-Candela & Rasmussen, 2005
)
and the standard collapsed
variational bound in (
8
)
(
\alpha\rightarrow 0
).
This approximation uses the regularization
term

-\frac{1-\alpha}{2\alpha}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log\left(1+\alpha\frac{k_{ii}-q_{ii}}{\sigma^{2}}\right).

(25)

This is different from
ours since
there is no value of
\alpha

such that the two regularization terms will become equal.
For example, note that for
\alpha\rightarrow 0
,
Equation

25

reduces to
-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\text{tr}\left(\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}-\textbf{Q}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}\right)
as discussed
in
Bui et al. (2017)
.

Poisson Regression

We consider a non-Gaussian likelihood example where the output data are counts modeled by a Poisson likelihood

3.3

Conclusions

We have presented a method that relaxes the conditional GP assumption in the approximate
distribution in sparse variational GPs.
This leads to tighter collapsed and uncollapsed bounds, that maintain the computational cost with the previous bounds and can reduce training underfitting. For future work
an interesting topic is to apply our method to more complex GP models,
such as those with multiple outputs, with uncertain inputs and deep GPs.
For the Bayesian GP-LVM, where the collapsed closed form bound has strong
similarities with the previous GP regression collapsed bound in
Equation

8
, deriving
a new collapsed bound is tractable as described in
Section

B.5
.
Finally, it might be useful to investigate whether theoretical convergence results on sparse GPs

(
Burt et al., 2020
;
Wild et al., 2023
)
, can be improved given the new collapsed lower bound.

Appendix A
Further details about the SVGP method

We give a brief overview of the derivation of the standard collapsed bound
in
Equation

8
.
Some steps of the derivation will also be instructive for proving the
main results of this paper in

Appendix

B
.

Given the variational distribution

q(\textbf{f},\textbf{u})=p(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})q(\textbf{u})
the lower bound is

\displaystyle\log p(\textbf{y})

\displaystyle\geq\int p(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})q(\textbf{u})\log\frac{p(\textbf{y}|\textbf{f})\cancel{p(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})}p(\textbf{u})}{\cancel{p(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})}q(\textbf{u})}d\textbf{f}d\textbf{u}

\displaystyle=\int p(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})q(\textbf{u})\log\frac{p(\textbf{y}|\textbf{f})p(\textbf{u})}{q(\textbf{u})}d\textbf{f}d\textbf{u}

\displaystyle=\int q(\textbf{u})\left\{\int p(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})\log p(\textbf{y}|\textbf{f})d\textbf{f}+\log\frac{p(\textbf{u})}{q(\textbf{u})}\right\}d\textbf{u}

\displaystyle=\int q(\textbf{u})\log\frac{\exp\{\int p(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})\log p(\textbf{y}|\textbf{f})d\textbf{f}\}p(\textbf{u})}{q(\textbf{u})}d\textbf{u}.

(26)

The expectation
\int p(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})\log p(\textbf{y}|\textbf{f})d\textbf{f}
can be computed
as

\displaystyle\int p(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})\log p(\textbf{y}|\textbf{f})d\textbf{f}

\displaystyle=\int p(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})\log p(\textbf{y}|\textbf{f})d\textbf{f}

\displaystyle=\int p(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})\left\{-\frac{N}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^{2})-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\text{tr}\left[\textbf{y}\textbf{y}^{T}-2\textbf{y}\textbf{f}^{T}+\textbf{f}\textbf{f}^{T}\right]\right\}d\textbf{f}

\displaystyle=-\frac{N}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^{2})-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\text{tr}\left[\textbf{y}\textbf{y}^{T}-2\textbf{y}(\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{u}}\textbf{K}_{\textbf{u}\textbf{u}}^{-1}\textbf{u})^{\top}+(\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{u}}\textbf{K}_{\textbf{u}\textbf{u}}^{-1}\textbf{u})(\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{u}}\textbf{K}_{\textbf{u}\textbf{u}}^{-1}\textbf{u})^{\top}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}-\textbf{Q}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\log\left[\mathcal{N}(\textbf{y}|\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{u}}\textbf{K}_{\textbf{u}\textbf{u}}^{-1}\textbf{u},\sigma^{2}\textbf{I})\right]-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\text{tr}(\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}-\textbf{Q}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}).

(27)

where we highlighted with blue
a term in the third line to contrast it
with a similar term when proving
Lemma

3.2
in
Section

B.4
. The ELBO in
Equation

26

is written as

\displaystyle\log p(\textbf{y})

\displaystyle\geq\int q(\textbf{u})\log\frac{\mathcal{N}(\textbf{y}|\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{u}}\textbf{K}_{\textbf{u}\textbf{u}}^{-1}\textbf{u},\sigma^{2}\textbf{I})p(\textbf{u})}{q(\textbf{u})}d\textbf{f}-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\text{tr}(\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}-\textbf{Q}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}).

(28)

By maximizing this bound wrt the distribution
q(\textbf{u})
we obtain the
optimal
q^{*}
:

\displaystyle q^{*}(\textbf{u})

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathcal{N}(\textbf{y}|\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{u}}\textbf{K}_{\textbf{u}\textbf{u}}^{-1}\textbf{u},\sigma^{2}\textbf{I})p(\textbf{u})}{\int\mathcal{N}(\textbf{y}|\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{u}}\textbf{K}_{\textbf{u}\textbf{u}}^{-1}\textbf{u},\sigma^{2}\textbf{I})p(\textbf{u})d\textbf{u}}=\frac{\mathcal{N}(\textbf{y}|\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{u}}\textbf{K}_{\textbf{u}\textbf{u}}^{-1}\textbf{u},\sigma^{2}\textbf{I})p(\textbf{u})}{\mathcal{N}(\textbf{y}|{\bf 0},\textbf{Q}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}+\sigma^{2}\textbf{I})}

(29)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}(\textbf{u}|\sigma^{-2}\textbf{K}_{\textbf{u}\textbf{u}}(\textbf{K}_{\textbf{u}\textbf{u}}+\sigma^{-2}\textbf{K}_{\textbf{u}\textbf{f}}\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{u}})^{-1}\textbf{K}_{\textbf{u}\textbf{f}}\textbf{y},\textbf{K}_{\textbf{u}\textbf{u}}(\textbf{K}_{\textbf{u}\textbf{u}}+\sigma^{-2}\textbf{K}_{\textbf{u}\textbf{f}}\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{u}})^{-1}\textbf{K}_{\textbf{u}\textbf{u}}),

(30)

where the expression in the second line (obtained after some standard
completion of a square procedure) shows that
q^{*}(\textbf{u})
can be computed in
\mathcal{O}(NM^{2})
time. In fact, this optimal
q^{*}(\textbf{u})

is the same as the one obtained by the DTC (also known as projected process) approximation
(
Seeger et al., 2003
;
Quiñonero-Candela & Rasmussen, 2005
)
. By substituting
the expression in (
29
) into the bound in (
28
)
we obtain the well-known formula of the collapsed bound:

\displaystyle\log p(\textbf{y})

\displaystyle\geq\log\mathcal{N}(\textbf{y}|{\bf 0},\textbf{Q}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}+\sigma^{2}\textbf{I})-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\text{tr}(\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}-\textbf{Q}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}).

(31)

Given the Gaussian
form of
q(\textbf{u})=\mathcal{N}(\textbf{u}|\bm{\mu},\textbf{A})
the posterior GP
is given by
q(\textbf{f}_{*})=\int p(\textbf{f}_{*}|\textbf{u})q(\textbf{u})d\textbf{u}
:

\displaystyle q(\textbf{f}_{*})=\mathcal{N}(\textbf{f}_{*}|\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}_{*}\textbf{f}}\textbf{K}_{\textbf{u}\textbf{u}}^{-1}\bm{\mu},\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}_{*}\textbf{f}_{*}}-\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}_{*}\textbf{u}}\textbf{K}_{\textbf{u}\textbf{u}}^{-1}\textbf{K}_{\textbf{u}\textbf{f}_{*}}+\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}_{*}\textbf{u}}\textbf{K}_{\textbf{u}\textbf{u}}^{-1}\textbf{A}\textbf{K}_{\textbf{u}\textbf{u}}^{-1}\textbf{K}_{\textbf{u}\textbf{f}_{*}})

(32)

which further simplifies if we substitute the optimal mean and covariance of
q^{*}(\textbf{u})
:

\displaystyle q(\textbf{f}_{*})=\mathcal{N}(\textbf{f}_{*}|\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}_{*}\textbf{f}}\bm{\Lambda}^{-1}\textbf{K}_{\textbf{u}\textbf{f}}\frac{\textbf{y}}{\sigma^{2}},\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}_{*}\textbf{f}_{*}}-\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}_{*}\textbf{u}}\textbf{K}_{\textbf{u}\textbf{u}}^{-1}\textbf{K}_{\textbf{u}\textbf{f}_{*}}+\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}_{*}\textbf{u}}\bm{\Lambda}^{-1}\textbf{K}_{\textbf{u}\textbf{f}_{*}})

(33)

where
\bm{\Lambda}=\textbf{K}_{\textbf{u}\textbf{u}}+\sigma^{-2}\textbf{K}_{\textbf{u}\textbf{f}}\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{u}}
.

Appendix B
Further details about the proposed bounds

Here, we provide several proofs
regarding the proposed bounds.

B.1
Proof of
Lemma

3.1

q(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})

and
p(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})
are Gaussian distributions having the same mean but different covariance matrices. Thus,
the KL divergence reduces to

\displaystyle\text{KL}[q(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})||p(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left\{\log\frac{|\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}-\textbf{Q}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}|}{|(\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}-\textbf{Q}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}})^{\frac{1}{2}}\textbf{V}(\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}-\textbf{Q}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}})^{\frac{1}{2}}|}-N+\text{tr}\{(\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}-\textbf{Q}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}})^{-1}(\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}-\textbf{Q}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}})^{\frac{1}{2}}\textbf{V}(\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}-\textbf{Q}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}})^{\frac{1}{2}}\}\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left\{-\log|\textbf{V}|-N+\text{tr}\{\textbf{V}\}\right\},

(34)

where all the terms involving

\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}-\textbf{Q}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}
cancel out by using standard properties of the
matrix determinant and trace. Now since
V
is a diagonal matrix (with diagonal elements
v_{i}>0
) we
conclude that

\text{KL}[q(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})||p(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})]=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(v_{i}-\log v_{i}-1)
.

B.2
Proof of
Lemma

3.2

The derivation of
\int q(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})\log p(\textbf{y}|\textbf{f})d\textbf{f}
is
similar to the derivation in
Equation

27

with a small difference highlighted in blue:

\displaystyle\int p(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})

\displaystyle\log p(\textbf{y}|\textbf{f})d\textbf{f}

\displaystyle=-\frac{N}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^{2})-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\text{tr}\left[\textbf{y}\textbf{y}^{T}-2\textbf{y}(\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{u}}\textbf{K}_{\textbf{u}\textbf{u}}^{-1}\textbf{u})^{\top}+(\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{u}}\textbf{K}_{\textbf{u}\textbf{u}}^{-1}\textbf{u})(\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{u}}\textbf{K}_{\textbf{u}\textbf{u}}^{-1}\textbf{u})^{\top}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}(\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}-\textbf{Q}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}})^{\frac{1}{2}}\textbf{V}(\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}-\textbf{Q}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}})^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\log\left[\mathcal{N}(\textbf{y}|\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{u}}\textbf{K}_{\textbf{u}\textbf{u}}^{-1}\textbf{u},\sigma^{2}\textbf{I})\right]-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\text{tr}(\textbf{V}(\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}-\textbf{Q}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}})),

(35)

where we used that
\text{tr}((\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}-\textbf{Q}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}})^{\frac{1}{2}}\textbf{V}(\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}-\textbf{Q}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}})^{\frac{1}{2}})=\text{tr}(\textbf{V}(\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}-\textbf{Q}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}))
. Now since
V
is a diagonal matrix
we have
\text{tr}(\textbf{V}(\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}-\textbf{Q}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}))=\sum_{i=1}^{N}v_{i}(k_{ii}-q_{ii})
which completes the proof.

B.3
Proof of
Equation

16

The ELBO is written as

\log p(\textbf{y})\geq\int q(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})q(\textbf{u})\log\frac{p(\textbf{y}|\textbf{f})p(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})p(\textbf{u})}{q(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})q(\textbf{u})}=\int\!\!q(\textbf{u})\!\left\{\!\log\frac{\exp\{\mathbb{E}_{q(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})}[\log p(\textbf{y}|\textbf{f})]\}p(\textbf{u})}{q(\textbf{u})}\!-\!\text{KL}[q(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})||p(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})]\!\right\}\!d\textbf{u}

and by using the results from
the two lemmas this becomes

\log p(\textbf{y})\geq\int q(\textbf{u})\log\frac{\mathcal{N}(\textbf{y}|\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{u}}\textbf{K}_{\textbf{u}\textbf{u}}^{-1}\textbf{u},\sigma^{2}\textbf{I})p(\textbf{u})}{q(\textbf{u})}d\textbf{u}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\{v_{i}\left(1+\frac{k_{ii}-q_{ii}}{\sigma^{2}}\right)-\log v_{i}-1\right\}.

Clearly, maximizing over

q(\textbf{u})
gives the same optimal distribution as in
Equation

29
, and the first term in the bound is the DTC log likelihood. The second term that depends on the
v_{i}
s is a concave function over these parameters. Thus, by differentiating and setting to zero we obtain the optimal values
v_{i}^{*}=\left(1+\frac{k_{ii}-q_{ii}}{\sigma^{2}}\right)^{-1}
. If we plug these values back into the bound we obtain the new tighter collapsed bound in
Equation

16
.

B.4
Reinterpretation of
Artemev et al. (2021)
’s bound

We consider the following form of
q(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})
:

q(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})=\mathcal{N}(\textbf{f}|\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{u}}\textbf{K}_{\textbf{u}\textbf{u}}^{-1}\textbf{u},v(\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}-\textbf{Q}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}})).

Then,
\text{KL}[q(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})||p(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})]=\frac{N}{2}(v-\log v-1)

and
\mathbb{E}_{q(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})}[\log p(\textbf{y}|\textbf{f})]\\
=\log\mathcal{N}(\textbf{y}|\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{u}}\textbf{K}_{\textbf{u}\textbf{u}}^{-1}\textbf{u},\sigma^{2}\textbf{I})-\frac{v}{2\sigma^{2}}\text{tr}(\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}-\textbf{Q}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}})
and the bound is written as

\log p(\textbf{y})\geq\int\!\!q(\textbf{u})\log\frac{\mathcal{N}(\textbf{y}|\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{u}}\textbf{K}_{\textbf{u}\textbf{u}}^{-1}\textbf{u},\sigma^{2}\textbf{I})p(\textbf{u})}{q(\textbf{u})}d\textbf{u}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{\frac{v}{\sigma^{2}}\text{tr}(\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}-\textbf{Q}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}})+N(v-\log v-1)\right\}.

By maximizing wrt
v
we obtain
v^{*}=\left(1+\frac{\text{tr}(\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}-\textbf{Q}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}})}{N\sigma^{2}}\right)^{-1}
, and by substituting this back into the bound we obtain
Artemev et al. (2021)
’s tighter bound on the initial trace regularization term. Overall this collapsed bound has the form

\log p(\textbf{y})\geq\log\mathcal{N}(\textbf{y}|{\bf 0},\textbf{Q}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}+\sigma^{2}\textbf{I})-\frac{N}{2}\log\left(\ 1+\frac{\text{tr}(\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}-\textbf{Q}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}})}{N\sigma^{2}}\right).

(36)

This collapsed bound is what the method
SGPR-artemev is using in
Section

5.2
. Note that

Artemev et al. (2021)
propose also additional but more expensive bounds for the first DTC log likelihood term that require running conjugate gradients.
We do not consider those in our comparisons (such bounds could be used in all SGPR bounds since all share the same DTC log likelihood term) as they have higher cost.

B.5
Bound for the Bayesian GP-LVM

Due to the strong similarity of the
standard collapsed SVGP bound in
Equation

8
with the collapsed
bound in the Bayesian
GP-LVM
(
Titsias & Lawrence, 2010
)
, applying the new approximation to Bayesian GP-LVM
seems to be simple and we discuss it next.

Given observed data
Y\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times D}

and latent variables

X\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times Q}
we have the latent variable model

p(Y|X)p(X)=\left(\prod_{d=1}^{D}p(\textbf{y}_{d}|X)\right)p(X),

where
p(X)
is a Gaussian prior over the latent variables and

p(\textbf{y}_{d}|X)=\mathcal{N}(\textbf{y}_{d}|\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{f}}(X)+\sigma^{2}\textbf{I})
.
Given a Gaussian variational distribution

q(X)
over the latent variables, the initial form of the bound is

\displaystyle F

\displaystyle=\int q(X)\log p(Y|X)dX-\text{KL}[q(X)||p(X)]

\displaystyle=\sum_{d=1}^{D}\int q(X)\log p(\textbf{y}_{d}|X)dX-\text{KL}[q(X)||p(X)]

\displaystyle=\sum_{d=1}^{D}F_{d}-\text{KL}[q(X)||p(X)],

(37)

where
F_{d}=\int q(X)\log p(\textbf{y}_{d}|X)dX
. The KL part will be a tractable KL between two Gaussians, and thus the difficulty is to approximate
F_{d}
.
Given that

\log p(\textbf{y}_{d}|X)
has the same form with the log marginal likelihood in GP regression, we can lower bound it using inducing variables and exactly the same form of
q(\textbf{f}|\textbf{u})
as we did in the main paper. This gives

\log p(\textbf{y}_{d}|X)\geq\int q(\textbf{u})\log\frac{\mathcal{N}(\textbf{y}_{d}|\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{u}}(X)\textbf{K}_{\textbf{u}\textbf{u}}^{-1}\textbf{u},\sigma^{2}\textbf{I})p(\textbf{u})}{q(\textbf{u})}d\textbf{u}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\{v_{i}\left(1+\frac{k(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}_{i})-q(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}_{i})}{\sigma^{2}}\right)-\log v_{i}-1\right\},

where
\bm{x}_{i}
is the latent variable for the
i
-th data point and
q(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}_{i})=\textbf{k}(x_{i})^{\top}\textbf{K}_{\textbf{u}\textbf{u}}^{-1}\textbf{k}(\bm{x}_{i})=\text{tr}\{\textbf{K}_{\textbf{u}\textbf{u}}^{-1}\textbf{k}(\bm{x}_{i})\textbf{k}(\bm{x}_{i})^{\top}\}
. Note that we write the cross
kernel matrix as

\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{u}}(X)
to emphasize its dependence on the latent variables
X
, while
\textbf{K}_{\textbf{u}\textbf{u}}
does not depend on
X
. Note also that we assume that each
v_{i}
parameter does not depend on
\bm{x}_{i}
and this is crucial to obtain a closed form collapsed bound. Now we follow
the derivation in the initial Bayesian GP-LVM
where we use the
above bound to replace

\log p(\textbf{y}_{d}|X)
in

\int q(X)\log p(\textbf{y}_{d}|X)dX
and do first the expectation
over
X
, and then solve for the optimal
q(\textbf{u})
. This eliminates
q(\textbf{u})
and it gives the bound

\log\int e^{\langle\mathcal{N}(\textbf{y}_{d}|\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{u}}(X)\textbf{K}_{\textbf{u}\textbf{u}}^{-1}\textbf{u},\sigma^{2}\textbf{I})\rangle_{q(X)}}p(\textbf{u})d\textbf{u}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\{v_{i}\left(1+\frac{\langle k(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}_{i})\rangle_{q(\bm{x}_{i})}-\langle q(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}_{i})\rangle_{q(\bm{x}_{i})}}{\sigma^{2}}\right)-\log v_{i}-1\right\}.

where we have used physics notation for expectation, i.e.,

\langle\cdot\rangle
.
For
v_{i}=1
this is the previous collapsed bound used by Bayesian GP-LVM. By maximizing over each
v_{i}
we obtain the new collapsed bound

F_{d}\geq\log\int e^{\langle\mathcal{N}(\textbf{y}_{d}|\textbf{K}_{\textbf{f}\textbf{u}}(X)\textbf{K}_{\textbf{u}\textbf{u}}^{-1}\textbf{u},\sigma^{2}\textbf{I})\rangle_{q(X)}}p(\textbf{u})d\textbf{u}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log\left(1+\frac{\langle k(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}_{i})\rangle_{q(\bm{x}_{i})}-\langle q(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}_{i})\rangle_{q(\bm{x}_{i})}}{\sigma^{2}}\right),

which can be substituted in the overall Bayesian GP-LVM bound above. Again the implementation of the new bound requires a minor modification to existing
Bayesian GP-LVM code.
