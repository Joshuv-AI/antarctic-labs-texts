Probiotics in the Prevention and Treatment of Periodontal Diseases: A Systematic Review
Authors: Nishath Sayed Abdul, Lara Ghassan Odeh, Asma Awadh Alenazi, Jumana Abdullah Alzahrani, Aldanah Tawfiq Almutib, Cristalle Soman
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction:Periodontal diseases (PDs) pose a significant challenge to dental health, leading to a growing interest in probiotics as potential therapeutic and prophylactic agents. Literature evidence has shown conflicting results on the use of probiotics in the management of PDs. Hence, this systematic review was performed to explore the effectiveness of probiotics in both the prevention and treatment of PDs by synthesizing data from relevant studies.Methodology:Various databases were searched using appropriate MeSH keywords as per the PRISMA protocol. Studies were included only if they met certain criteria. Two reviewers independently extracted data variables from the included literature. The risk of bias 2.0 tool was employed to assess the methodological quality of the included studies.Results:In total, 21 studies were considered eligible and included in the review. It was observed that 17 studies reported a statistically significant improvement in both periodontitis and gingivitis among the probiotic group compared to control cohorts. The synthesized evidence from the review suggests that probiotics play a favourable role in both the prevention and treatment of PDs.Conclusion:It also supports the incorporation of probiotics as a potential adjunctive therapy in PD management. However, further research is warranted to explore the specific probiotic strains, dosages, and treatment durations for optimized outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

Periodontal diseases (PDs) affect a large section of the global population.[1] PDs cause inflammation and periodontal component degradation, including alveolar bone, ligament, and cementum.[2] A progressive subgingival microbial dysbiosis is assumed to be the main cause. This dysbiosis is characterized by periodontal pathogenic microorganisms dominating and beneficial mouth bacteria declining.[3] This change in microbial dynamics disrupts the host-microbiota homeostatic balance, causing PDs.[3] Gram-negative and gram-positive bacteria from the tooth surface adhere, proliferate, and colonize the subgingival environment.[4]

Probiotics are sought as supplemental therapies to improve therapy efficacy and prevent illness relapse. Probiotics, often known as “advantageous microorganisms”, improve host health when introduced in appropriate amounts. The hunt for alternate plaque and gingivitis treatments has led dental researchers to probiotics.[5] Probiotics, living microorganisms provided in large amounts to benefit the host, have been considered as supplementary plaque and gingivitis treatment. Probiotic strains are associated with improved gingival indices, gingivitis suppression, and reduced periodontopathogens in subgingival biofilms, saliva, and oral mucosal inflammatory indicators. Probiotics have been studied for PD prevention and treatment, but the results are mixed. Hence, this systematic review was undertaken.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Eligibility criteria

The current review was performed according to the PRISMA guidelines. The population of interest consisted of individuals diagnosed with and treated for PDs. The exposure of interest was the administration of probiotics as an intervention. The comparator group comprised individuals with PDs, who did not receive probiotic intervention or who received a placebo or an alternative treatment. The outcomes of interest included a comprehensive range of clinical parameters associated with PD, such as plaque index, clinical attachment level, bleeding on probing, probing pocket depth, gingival index, and gingival crevicular fluid.

Search strategy

The protocol searched databases of Pubmed/MEDLINE, Scopus, Web of Science, and Cochrane Library for relevant studies. Grey literature, conference abstracts, theses, and unpublished works were excluded.

Data extraction

Two reviewers performed the data extraction independently; any disagreement or non-census was sorted out by discussion. The data extraction table included the following components: study ID, location, sample size of test and control group, probiotic species, follow-up time, and inferences.

Risk of bias assessment

The risk of bias 2.0 tool was utilized for evaluating the bias in the selected studies. The evaluation of bias was conducted independently by qualified reviewers, who are trained in its application.

RESULTS

An extensive search across multiple databases yielded 579 entries, with no additional register records found, indicating thorough coverage. After a detailed review process, duplicate records and irrelevant articles were eliminated, leaving 269 eligible reports for consideration. DISCUSSION

In 17 of 21 studies, probiotics provided effects comparable to conventional therapies. This strongly recommends adding probiotics to PD treatment methods. The studies reviewed consistently suggest that probiotics may play a beneficial role in enhancing oral health, particularly in managing and preventing PDs. This effect is presumably because of the ability of probiotics to modify and stabilize the oral microbiota, thereby reducing pathogenic bacteria and promoting a healthier oral environment. Several mechanisms have been proposed to explain the beneficial effects of probiotics on periodontal health. These include the inhibition of pathogenic bacteria through competitive exclusion, the production of antimicrobial substances, and the modulation of host immune responses. Despite these promising mechanisms, the results across studies remain varied because of differences in study design, probiotic strains used, delivery methods, and the populations studied.

The majority of the studies reviewed used different strains of Lactobacilli and Bifidobacteria, which are among the most common probiotics. These studies have shown improvements in clinical periodontal parameters, such as reductions in plaque indices, gingival inflammation, and depth of periodontal pockets. However, the variability in formulations and concentrations of probiotics used makes it difficult to standardize recommendations for clinical practice.

After studying specific probiotic strains, some showed greater efficacy and positive results across all metrics. A closer study of these species showed that formulations with a specific strain improved clinical outcomes. The mechanisms for this improvement may be many. These species may reduce plaque by direct interactions, including as resistance to colonization, competition for binding sites and nutrition, and antibiotic production that inhibits other oral bacteria. Emerging research suggests that these probiotics may impact the oral microbiota, reducing pathogenic microorganisms. Because an imbalance in the oral microbiota is a critical component in PD, these probiotics may explain the observed improvements in PD-related clinical outcomes. One study found that the subgingival application of a non-colonizing yeast probiotic reduced clinical markers but not plaque buildup. This yeast probiotic’s immunomodulatory activity may explain its low plaque buildup effect, although its non-colonizing nature may explain it.

Probiotic formulations could not reduce plaque buildup in PD patients in a few studies. Our analysis of probiotic therapy duration revealed probiotics’ positive benefits on clinical outcomes. Because of the limited number of studies with treatment durations greater than one to two months, our qualitative analysis could not determine the ideal treatment duration. Research is needed to fully comprehend this. The findings of this review have various limitations that must be addressed when interpreting the conclusions. First, the studies’ heterogeneity may limit them. Geographic locations, probiotic species, follow-up times, and outcomes varied in the trials. The results may vary because of heterogeneity, making it difficult to draw universal conclusions.

CONCLUSION

The synthesis of data from various studies suggests that probiotic interventions may offer certain benefits for improving periodontal health. Specifically, probiotics induced statistically significant improvements in various periodontal parameters. These findings suggest that probiotics have the potential to contribute to the management of PDs, both in terms of preventing their progression and supporting treatment outcomes. The potential for publication bias, the reliance on subjective clinical measures, short follow-up timeframes in some studies, and variability in study quality are notable limitations that must be considered.
