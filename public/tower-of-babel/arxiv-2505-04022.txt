Robust acoustic and elastic full waveform inversion by adaptive Tikhonov-TV regularization Thanks: Submitted to the editors DATE
Kamal Aghazade, Ali Gholami

Abstract
Full Waveform Inversion (FWI) is a powerful wave-based imaging technique, but its inherent ill-posedness and non-convexity make it prone to local minima and poor convergence. Regularization techniques are commonly employed to stabilize FWI by incorporating prior information that enforces structural constraints, such as smooth variations or piecewise-constant behavior. Among them, Tikhonov regularization promotes smoothness, while total variation (TV) regularization preserves sharp boundaries–both widely used in solving ill-posed inverse problems. However, in the context of FWI, we highlight two key shortcomings of these regularization methods. First, subsurface model parameters (P- and S-wave velocities, density) often exhibit complex geological formations with sharp discontinuities separating distinct layers, while parameters within each layer vary smoothly. Neither Tikhonov nor TV regularization alone can effectively constrain such piecewise-smooth structures. Second, and more critically, when the initial model is far from the true model, these regularization assumptions can lead to convergence toward a local minimum. To address these limitations, we propose an adaptive Tikhonov-TV (TT) regularization method that decomposes the model into smooth and blocky components, enabling robust recovery of piecewise-smooth structures. The method is implemented within the Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers (ADMM) framework and incorporates an automated balancing strategy based on robust statistical analysis. Extensive numerical experiments on both acoustic and elastic FWI are conducted using challenging benchmark geological models. The results demonstrate that TT regularization significantly improves convergence and reconstruction accuracy compared to Tikhonov and TV regularization when applied separately. We show that for complex models and remote initial models, both Tikhonov and TV regularization tend to converge to local minima, whereas the TT regularization effectively mitigates cycle skipping through its adaptive combination of the two regularization strategies.

Introduction.

Wave-based imaging techniques aim to infer the spatially varying physical properties of an unknown medium, such as seismic wave velocity and density, from a set of observational data.
This study specifically focuses on subsurface Earth imaging, where the physical properties
are denoted as model and are represented by
\bm{m}\!:=\!\bm{m}(\bm{x})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, with
\bm{x}
denoting the spatial coordinates and
n
the number of discrete model parameters. The estimation of
\bm{m}
is indirect, relying on the analysis of seismic measurements or data
\bm{d}
by solving a parameter identification problem of the form:

\mathcal{F}(\bm{m})=\bm{d},

(1)

where
\mathcal{F}:\mathbb{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{D}
is a known mapping that encapsulates the governing physics of the seismic wave propagation; mapping the model parameters to the observed data recorded at specific receiver locations.
Among various wave-based imaging techniques, full waveform inversion (FWI) has emerged as a high-resolution method, capable of recovering
\bm{m}
with wavelength-scale accuracy
[
58
]
. Initially introduced in the 1980s
[
54
]
, FWI has since evolved into a robust inversion technique with broad applications (see e.g.,
[
56
]
and references therein). It is typically formulated in the time
[
54
]
, frequency
[
45
]
, or complex-frequency domains
[
53
]
within a multiscale data-fitting local optimization framework
[
14
]
, where medium parameters are iteratively refined by minimizing an appropriate discrepancy metric.

1.1
Challenges of FWI: Necessity of regularization.

Before FWI can be regarded as a powerful imaging technique, several challenges must be addressed. Starting from equation
eq.

1
, it can be shown that the problem is at least locally ill-posed
[
39
]
, which means that considering
\mathcal{F}(\bm{m})
as a forward mapping, for any neighborhood of
\bm{m}^{*}
satisfying
\mathcal{F}(\bm{m}^{*})=\bm{d}
, there exists a sequence
\{\bm{m}^{k}\}
such that:

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\|\mathcal{F}(\bm{m}^{*})-\mathcal{F}(\bm{m}^{k})\|\rightarrow 0,~~~~\text{but}~~\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\|\bm{m}^{*}-\bm{m}^{k}\|\nrightarrow 0.

(2)

This indicates that the forward map
\mathcal{F}
is stable: the sequence
\{\bm{m}^{k}\}
produces data that converge to the actual data
\mathcal{F}(\bm{m}^{*})=\bm{d}
, but the inputs
\bm{m}^{k}
do not converge to the true solution
\bm{m}^{*}
. Several factors contribute to this ill-posedness:

I.

Nonlinearity of the forward mapping:
The complex relationship between model parameters and wave propagation creates multiple local minima, making the inversion sensitive to the initial model and prone to cycle skipping when measured and predicted wavefields differ by over half a cycle
[
58
]
.

II.

Limited low-frequency content:
Low-frequency components are essential for mitigating nonlinearity and recovering large-scale variations in
\bm{m}

[
14
]
. However, practical data often lack sufficient low frequencies, thereby impeding the recovery of a smooth background model.

III.

Acquisition limitations and poor illumination:
Seismic sources and receivers are typically deployed only on one side of the medium (e.g., at or near the surface), which primarily captures backscattered rather than transmitted waves. Sparse spatial sampling and incomplete illumination further increase the non-convexity of the objective function
[
57
,
9
]
.

IV.

Multiple scattering effects and reflections:
Waves reflect at discontinuities, generating complex multi-arrival wavefields and increasing the possibility of cycle skipping, especially at intermediate to long offsets and in the presence of high-contrast anomalies such as salt bodies
[
36
,
47
]
.

V.

Assumptions within the modeling process and numerical errors:
The forward model often employs simplifying assumptions (e.g., acoustic vs. elastic, isotropic vs. anisotropic), which lead to model discrepancies. Additionally, numerical errors (e.g., discretization, boundary effects) introduce systematic biases in data matching.

VI.

Presence of noise:
Noise in the data leads to instability, potentially creating false minima or bias as the inversion fits both the true signal and the noise.

VII.

Complexity of multiparameter imaging such as elastic media:
More parameters increase the solution space and ambiguity, as different parameter combinations can produce similar wavefield responses. Information can leak between parameters (crosstalk), and elastic FWI involves complex wave physics, including mode conversions, increasing nonlinearity
[
42
,
38
]
.

These challenges contribute to the uncertainty in FWI resolution, making it an ill-posed and non-convex problem. Addressing these issues requires incorporating additional information about
\bm{m}
through regularization, denoted as
\mathcal{R}(\bm{m})
.
The choice of
\mathcal{R}(\bm{m})
is critical for model recovery, as it biases the solution towards models that possess desirable characteristics or encode prior knowledge about
\bm{m}

[
30
,
31
,
27
,
8
]
.
In this study, we are particularly interested in models that exhibit piecewise smooth behavior, which can be represented as
\bm{m}=(\bm{m}_{1},\bm{m}_{2})
, where:

I.

\bm{m}_{1}
: Encodes discontinuities and rapid variations, capturing sharp boundaries and structural interfaces.

II.

\bm{m}_{2}
: Represents the smoothly varying background component.

Without loss of generality, we present our formulation in the frequency domain. For the sake of brevity, the formulations are presented for a single frequency. However, extending them to multiple frequencies is straightforward and can be achieved by summing over frequencies in the objective function.

1.2
Related works.

We focus on regularization techniques based on
\ell_{2}
-and
\ell_{1}
-norms, widely used in FWI.
Tikhonov regularization in its isotropic form promotes smoothness by penalizing the
\ell_{2}
-norm of the model or its derivatives, thereby suppressing high-frequency variations
[
55
,
8
]
. Its anisotropic variant further refines this approach by incorporating geological tilt information

[
26
]
, improving structural alignment with subsurface features. Despite being straightforward and computationally efficient, Tikhonov regularization tends to oversmooth sharp boundaries and is unsuitable for capturing piecewise constant features.
Sparsity-promoting techniques enforce sparsity in transform domains (e.g., wavelet, curvelet, seislet)
[
41
,
61
,
60
]
using
\ell_{1}
-norm penalties to enhance resolution and suppress noise. However, their effectiveness depends on the transform’s ability to represent geological structures accurately.
The family of TV regularization
[
50
]
, preserves discontinuities by penalizing the
\ell_{1}
-norm of the model gradient. Variants include isotropic
[
21
,
2
]
, anisotropic (with directional operators,
[
46
]
), and asymmetric TV
[
20
]
, offer tailored edge preservation under different geological assumptions. In addition, the use of TV regularization for complex-valued imaging (visco-acoustic FWI in frequency domain) is studied in
[
5
]
. TV regularization typically experiences staircase effects, motivating the use of higher-order TV approaches
[
19
]
.

The previously mentioned
\ell_{1}
-and
\ell_{2}
-based regularization methods are effective for enforcing only a single distinct property of the model; either smoothness (by using Tikhonov) or blockiness (by using TV). However, neither approach is well-suited for reconstructing piecewise smooth models, where discontinuities are embedded within a smoothly varying background
[
27
,
52
,
35
]
.
Instead, promoting solutions that simultaneously exhibit multiple desired properties through
compound regularization can be more effective
[
27
,
4
]
.
One prominent approach within this framework is the infimal convolution method
[
15
]
, which combines different regularization functionals. A well-known example from this category is Total Generalized Variation (TGV)
[
12
,
51
]
, which integrates first- and second-order TV functionals to capture both blocky and smooth features.
An
\ell_{p}
-norm based TGV formulation has been proposed for imaging piecewise smooth models in FWI
[
23
]
. Additionally, a combination of the Shearlet transform with TGV
[
32
]
has been explored in FWI
[
59
]
.
Following
[
34
]
,
[
40
]
integrates zero-order Tikhonov regularization with TV for a better balance between smooth and sharp features. A more suitable functional in this form is based on the Tikhonov-TV (TT) regularization
[
1
,
4
]
, where the main challenge is controlling the trade-off between smoothness and blockiness. Another approach for piecewise smooth imaging is Mumford-Shah regularization
[
37
]
, which approximates an image with smooth regions separated by edges. However, its inherent non-convexity complicates optimization and parameter selection.

1.3
Contributions.

In general, previous regularization methods for piecewise smooth imaging face two primary challenges: (i) selecting appropriate regularization functionals to simultaneously preserve smooth and discontinuous structures, and (ii) determining a proper balance between these terms to ensure accurate model decomposition. To address these issues, we propose the following advancements:

I.

Balanced Tikhonov-TV regularization: Unlike prior approaches such as
[
23
]
and
[
40
]
, which respectively use second-order TV and zero-order Tikhonov regularization to promote smoothness, we adopt second-order Tikhonov regularization to enforce large-scale smoothness and a first-order TV term to capture blocky features. This choice, inspired by
[
27
]
, naturally aligns with the structural characteristics of piecewise smooth models.

II.

Extending TT regularization to multi-parameter elastic FWI:
Previous applications of TT regularization have been limited to acoustic FWI
[
1
,
4
]
. In this work, we extend the methodology to the elastic case, where multiple parameters with distinct structural properties must be reconstructed simultaneously. The central challenge lies in adaptively tuning the balancing parameters required for decomposing different elastic parameters. We address this by introducing an adaptive parameter selection strategy that ensures a robust and consistent performance across all elastic components.

III.

Statistical decomposition with automatic parameter selection:
A key limitation of prior methods is their reliance on manual or heuristic parameter tuning, making it challenging to accurately decompose the model into its smooth and discontinuous components. Our approach builds on
[
25
]
by formulating an adaptive decomposition scheme in the model gradient domain, allowing for robust separation of the smooth and piecewise constant components. The optimal balancing parameter is automatically determined via a statistical anomaly detection method using median absolute deviation, leveraging gradient variations to dynamically adjust the weighting between regularization terms.

The proposed method is rigorously tested in challenging FWI scenarios, demonstrating superior performance in:

I.

Mitigating cycle skipping in FWI for improved seismic imaging.

II.

Resolving salt and subsalt structures with high accuracy.

III.

Reconstructing complex models from ultra-long offset data.

IV.

Ensuring stability against noise, sparse acquisition, and varying balancing parameters ensuring reliable performance for real world conditions.

V.

Imaging multi-parameter elastic media and evaluating the method’s ability to reconstruct distinct parameters with varying structural properties, highlighting its adaptability across diverse geological environments.

Full waveform inversion.

2.1
Forward problem.

Let
n
,
n_{s}
and
n_{r}
denote the number of discrete model parameters, sources, and receivers, respectively.
We assume a set of known source functions in the frequency domain, denote as
\bm{b}_{i}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
, where
i\in\set{1,\dots,n_{s}}
indexes the sources. Given a candidate subsurface model
\bm{m}
, the seismic wavefield,
\bm{u}_{i}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
, can be obtained by solving the following partial differential equation (PDE) with coefficients
\bm{m}
:

\begin{array}[]{cc}\bm{A}(\bm{m})\bm{u}_{i}=\bm{b}_{i},&\text{in}~\Omega\\
~~~~~~~\bm{u}_{i}=0,&~~\text{on}~\partial\Omega\end{array}

(3)

where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a bounded domain with boundary
\partial\Omega
.

\bm{A}(\bm{m})\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
is the complex-valued discretized PDE operator and will be defined precisely for acoustic and elastic media in
section

2.1.1
and
section

2.1.2
, respectively. The observed data
\bm{d}_{i}=\bm{P}\bm{u}_{i}
is obtained by solving
eq.

3
for the wavefield and sampling it at the receiver locations, where the matrix
\bm{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{r}\times n}
is the sampling operator.

2.1.1
Acoustic media.

For two-dimensional (2D) acoustic media, the forward problem is defined by the scalar Helmholtz equation:

\underbrace{[\Delta+\omega^{2}\text{diag}(\bm{m})]}_{\bm{A}(\bm{m})}\bm{u}_{i}=\bm{b}_{i},

(4)

where
\omega
denotes the angular frequency, and
\Delta=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}+\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial z^{2}}
represents a discretized Laplace operator using an optimal 9-point finite-difference stencil, with perfectly matched layer (PML) boundary conditions applied to all sides of the computational domain
[
17
]
.
The medium is parameterized by the squared slowness
\bm{m}:=\bm{V}(\bm{x})^{-2}
, where
\bm{V}(\bm{x})
denotes the P-wave velocity.

2.1.2
Elastic media.

For 2D elastic FWI (EFWI), we consider the isotropic form of the frequency-domain elastic wave equation, defined as

\displaystyle\bm{\rho}\omega^{2}\bm{u}_{i,x}+(\bm{\hat{\lambda}}+2\bm{\hat{\mu}})\partial_{xx}\bm{u}_{i,x}+\bm{\hat{\mu}}\partial_{zz}\bm{u}_{i,x}+(\bm{\hat{\lambda}}+\bm{\hat{\mu}})\partial_{xz}\bm{u}_{i,z}=\bm{b}_{i,x},

(5a)

\displaystyle\bm{\rho}\omega^{2}\bm{u}_{i,z}+(\bm{\hat{\lambda}}+2\bm{\hat{\mu}})\partial_{zz}\bm{u}_{i,z}+\bm{\hat{\mu}}\partial_{xx}\bm{u}_{i,z}+(\bm{\hat{\lambda}+\hat{\mu}})\partial_{xz}\bm{u}_{i,x}=\bm{b}_{i,z},

(5b)

where
\bm{\rho}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times 1}
is mass density,
\bm{\hat{\lambda}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times 1}
and
\bm{\hat{\mu}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times 1}
denote the Lamé parameters,
\bm{u}_{i,x}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times 1}
and
\bm{u}_{i,z}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times 1}
are horizontal and vertical particle displacements, and
\bm{b}_{i,x}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times 1}
,
\bm{b}_{i,z}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times 1}
are the source terms in the respective directions. The P- and
S- wave velocities are defined as

\text{V}_{\text{p}}=\sqrt{\frac{\bm{\hat{\lambda}}+2\bm{\hat{\mu}}}{\bm{\rho}}},~~~\text{V}_{\text{s}}=\sqrt{\frac{\bm{\hat{\mu}}}{\bm{\rho}}}.

Assuming a constant-density medium, we discretize and rewrite the system of equations in
eq.

5
as:

\small\bm{A}(\bm{m})\bm{u}_{i}=\bm{b}_{i},

(6)

where

\noindent\bm{A}(\bm{m})=\begin{bmatrix}\omega^{2}+\text{diag}(\bm{m}_{\text{p}})\partial_{xx}+\text{diag}(\bm{m}_{\text{s}})\partial_{zz}~~~~\text{diag}(\bm{m}_{\text{p}}-\bm{m}_{\text{s}})\partial_{xz}\\
\text{diag}(\bm{m}_{\text{p}}-\bm{m}_{\text{s}})\partial_{xz}~~~~\omega^{2}+\text{diag}(\bm{m}_{\text{p}})\partial_{zz}+\text{diag}(\bm{m}_{\text{s}})\partial_{xx}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{C}^{2n\times 2n},

(7)

denotes the PDE operator, and is discretized using the optimal 9-point finite difference stencil proposed by
[
16
]
, and

\bm{m}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{m}_{\text{p}}:=\text{V}_{\text{p}}^{2}\\
\bm{m}_{\text{s}}:=\text{V}_{\text{s}}^{2}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 1},\quad\bm{u}_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{u}_{i,x}\\
\bm{u}_{i,z}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{C}^{2n\times 1},\quad\bm{b}_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{b}_{i,x}\\
\bm{b}_{i,z}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{C}^{2n\times 1}.

(8)

Wavefield subproblem.

For an initial model

\left(\bm{P}^{\top}\bm{P}+\mu\bm{A}(\bm{m}^{k-1})^{\top}\bm{A}(\bm{m}^{k-1})\right)\bm{u}^{k}_{i}=\bm{P}^{\top}\bm{d}_{i}+\mu\bm{A}(\bm{m}^{k-1})^{\top}\left(\bm{b}_{i}+{\bm{\lambda}}^{k-1}_{i}\right),

\mu\!>\!0
is the penalty parameter,
k
represents the iteration number, and superscript

is the complex-conjugate
(Hermitian) transpose.

2.2.2
Model subproblem.

The update of the model parameters by optimizing the AL function for a given set of wavefields
\set{\bm{u}_{i}}
and multipliers
\set{\bm{\lambda}_{i}}
requires solving the following problem:

\bm{m}^{k}=\argmin_{\bm{m}\in\mathcal{M}}~\mathcal{R}(\bm{m})+\frac{\mu}{2}\|\bm{L}^{k}\bm{m}-\bm{y}^{k}\|_{2}^{2},

(12)

where
\bm{L}^{k}
and

are defined differently for acoustic and elastic media

\bm{L}^{k}=\begin{pmatrix}\omega^{2}\text{diag}(\bm{u}_{1}^{k})\\

\omega^{2}\text{diag}(\bm{u}_{n_{s}}^{k})\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{C}^{(n_{s}n)\times n},~~~~~\bm{y}^{k}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{b}_{1}+\bm{\lambda}_{1}^{k-1}-\Delta\bm{u}_{1}^{k}\\
\vdots\\
\bm{b}_{n_{s}}+\bm{\lambda}_{n_{s}}^{k-1}-\Delta\bm{u}_{n_{s}}^{k}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{s}n\times 1}.

(13)

For elastic formulation, the structure of
\bm{L}^{k}
and
\bm{y}^{k}

\bm{L}^{k}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{L}_{1}^{k}\\
\vdots\\
\bm{L}_{n_{s}}^{k}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{C}^{(2n_{s}n)\times 2n},~~~~~\bm{y}^{k}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{y}_{1}^{k}\\
\vdots\\
\bm{y}_{n_{s}}^{k}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{C}^{2n_{s}n\times 1},

(14)

where

\displaystyle\bm{L}_{i}^{k}=\begin{bmatrix}\text{diag}(\partial_{xx}{\bm{u}}_{i,x}^{k})+\text{diag}(\partial_{xz}{\bm{u}}_{i,z}^{k})&\text{diag}(\partial_{zz}{\bm{u}}_{i,x}^{k})-\text{diag}(\partial_{xz}{\bm{u}}_{i,z}^{k})\\

(15a)

\displaystyle\bm{y}_{i}^{k}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{b}}_{i,x}+\bm{\lambda}_{i,x}^{k-1}-\omega^{2}\bm{u}_{i,x}^{k}\\
{\bm{b}}_{i,z}+\bm{\lambda}_{i,z}^{k-1}-\omega^{2}\bm{u}_{i,z}^{k}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{C}^{2n\times 1}.

(15b)

3
Regularization by smooth-blocky decomposition.

A subsurface model,
\bm{m}
, can be represented using a Fourier basis to define its wavenumber content
[
18
,
7
]
.
FWI relies on constructing the model’s low-wavenumber component first, followed by refining higher-wavenumber details through frequency continuation
[
14
]
.
To present the methodology, we consider a simple 2D Gaussian void model as an example of the subsurface candidate model
\bm{m}
(
fig.

1
).
We assume that
\bm{m}
can be decomposed into two distinct components, each describing specific structural features with characteristic spectral behavior:

I.

A blocky component,
\bm{m}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, capturing sharp boundaries and discontinuities. The gradient of this component follows a non-Gaussian distribution that is mainly represented in the Fourier domain by high-wavenumber coefficients (
fig.

1
).

II.

A smooth background component,
\bm{m}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, representing the smoothly varying structures (or long-wavelength features). It is characterized by Gaussian-distributed second-order derivatives (
fig.

1
).

Accordingly, we assume that the desired medium is piecewise smooth, defined as

\bm{m}={\bm{m}}_{1}+{\bm{m}}_{2}.

(17)

Accurately separating the smooth and blocky components provides significant flexibility in FWI. It enables the stable recovery of the background model through the term
\bm{m}_{2}
, which is crucial for ensuring good convergence of the optimization algorithm in the early iterations. The blocky component is then gradually reconstructed as the solution approaches convergence. This flexibility is achieved through a well-balanced combination of these two terms in the regularization function.

3.1
Tikhonov-TV (TT) regularization.

Following the model decomposition under the piecewise smooth assumption, the smooth component,
\bm{m}_{2}
, and blocky component,
\bm{m}_{1}
, can be effectively constrained using second-order Tikhonov and first-order TV functionals, respectively.
Accordingly, the balanced TT regularization functional can be defined as
[
27
]
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\bm{m})=\|\nabla\bm{m}_{1}\|_{1}+\frac{\beta}{2}\|\nabla^{2}\bm{m}_{2}\|_{2}^{2},~~~~~\text{with}~~\bm{m}=\bm{m}_{1}+\bm{m}_{2},

(18)

where
\beta>0
is a balancing parameter that controls the relative influence of the two regularization terms.
\nabla
and
\nabla^{2}
are first-and second order finite difference operators, respectively (see e.g.,
[
25
]
for explicit definitions).
The behavior of the regularization functional is governed by
\beta
:

1.

For
\beta\rightarrow 0
, the minimization on the TV term dominates.
In this regime, the blocky component
\bm{m}_{1}
tends to a constant field
(minimizing total variation), while the smooth component
\bm{m}_{2}=\bm{m}-\bm{m}_{1}

absorbs essentially all of the signal structure.

2.

For
\beta\rightarrow\infty
, the smooth component is strongly
constrained to lie in the null space of the curvature operator
(i.e., to a constant or affine trend). Consequently, the formulation
asymptotically reduces to TV regularization, where
\bm{m}_{1}
captures
the remaining variability through edge-preserving, piecewise-constant features.

Regularization of the form
eq.

18
was initially introduced in
[
27
]
for signal and image processing applications. More recently,
[
4
]
extended this approach to acoustic FWI within an ADMM framework. Specifically, the model update step in
eq.

12
requires solving the following constrained optimization:

\displaystyle\minimize_{\bm{m}_{1},\bm{m}_{2}}~\|\nabla\bm{m}_{1}\|_{1}+\frac{\beta}{2}\|\nabla^{2}\bm{m}_{2}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\mu}{2}\|\bm{L}[\bm{m}_{1}+\bm{m}_{2}]-\bm{y}\|_{2}^{2},

(19)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}~~~\bm{m}_{1}+\bm{m}_{2}=\bm{m}\in\mathcal{M}.

We have omitted the dependence of
\bm{L}
and
\bm{y}
on the iteration number
k
for simplicity and clarity. Despite the successful application of
eq.

19
, the choice of
\beta
significantly influences the solution, and determining an optimal value is not straightforward. In the following, we introduce an equivalent but straightforward version of the TT functional to address this challenge.

3.1.1
Adaptive TT regularization.

Given the linearity of the gradient operator, the total model gradient can be decomposed as:

\displaystyle\nabla\bm{m}

\displaystyle=\nabla\bm{m}_{1}+\nabla\bm{m}_{2},

(20)

\displaystyle=\bm{g}_{1}+\bm{g}_{2},

where
\bm{g}_{1}=\nabla\bm{m}_{1}
and
\bm{g}_{2}=\nabla\bm{m}_{2}
are auxiliary variables representing the gradients of the blocky and smooth components, respectively. Additionally, using the identity
\nabla^{2}=\overline{\nabla}\nabla
, the constrained optimization problem in
eq.

19
can be reformulated as
[
25
]
:

\minimize_{\bm{m}\in\mathcal{M},\bm{g}_{1},\bm{g}_{2}}\|\bm{g}_{1}\|_{1}+\frac{\beta}{2}\|\overline{\nabla}\bm{g}_{2}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\mu}{2}\|\bm{L}\bm{m}-\bm{y}\|_{2}^{2},~~\text{subject to}~~\nabla\bm{m}=\bm{g}_{1}+\bm{g}_{2},

(21)

which can be solved by ADMM (appearing as an inner loop within the FWI iteration).
The ADMM iteration is defined as

\displaystyle\bm{m}^{k}=\argmin_{\bm{m}}~\mathcal{L}(\bm{m},\bm{g}_{1}^{k-1},\bm{g}_{2}^{k-1},\bm{p}^{k-1},\bm{\nu}_{1}^{k-1},\bm{\nu}_{2}^{k-1}),

(22a)

\displaystyle\bm{g}_{1}^{k}=\argmin_{\bm{g}_{1}}~\mathcal{L}(\bm{m}^{k},\bm{g}_{1},\bm{g}_{2}^{k-1},\bm{p}^{k-1},\bm{\nu}_{1}^{k-1},\bm{\nu}_{2}^{k-1}),

(22b)

\displaystyle\bm{g}_{2}^{k}=\argmin_{\bm{g}_{2}}~\mathcal{L}(\bm{m}^{k},\bm{g}_{1}^{k},\bm{g}_{2},\bm{p}^{k-1},\bm{\nu}_{1}^{k-1},\bm{\nu}_{2}^{k-1}),

(22c)

\displaystyle\bm{p}^{k}=\Pi_{\mathcal{M}}(\bm{m}^{k}-\bm{\nu}_{2}^{k-1}),

(22d)

\displaystyle\bm{\nu}_{1}^{k}=\bm{\nu}_{1}^{k-1}+\bm{g}_{1}^{k}+\bm{g}_{2}^{k}-\nabla\bm{m}^{k},

(22e)

\displaystyle\bm{\nu}_{2}^{k}=\bm{\nu}_{2}^{k-1}+\bm{p}^{k}-\bm{m}^{k},

(22f)

where

\mathcal{L}(\bm{m},\bm{g}_{1},\bm{g}_{2},\bm{p},\bm{\nu}_{1},\bm{\nu}_{2})=\|\bm{g}_{1}\|_{1}+\frac{\beta}{2}\|\overline{\nabla}\bm{g}_{2}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\mu}{2}\|\bm{L}\bm{m}-\bm{y}\|_{2}^{2}+I_{\mathcal{M}}(\bm{p})\\
+\frac{\tau_{1}}{2}\|\nabla\bm{m}-\bm{g}_{1}-\bm{g}_{2}-\bm{\nu}_{1}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\tau_{2}}{2}\|\bm{m}-\bm{p}-\bm{\nu}_{2}\|_{2}^{2}.

(23)

In these formulations
\bm{\nu}_{1}
and
\bm{\nu}_{2}
are Lagrange multipliers, and
\tau_{1},\tau_{2}>0
are the penalty parameters controlling the new constraints enforcement,

I_{\mathcal{M}}(\bm{p})
is the indicator function on
\mathcal{M}
(i.e.,
I_{\mathcal{M}}(\bm{p})=0
for
\bm{p}\in\mathcal{M}
, and
I_{\mathcal{M}}(\bm{p})=\infty
for
\bm{p}\notin\mathcal{M}
).

\Pi_{\mathcal{M}}(\bm{x})=\text{max}(\text{min}(\bm{x},\overline{\bm{x}}),\underline{\bm{x}})
is the Euclidean projection of
\bm{x}
onto the feasible region
\mathcal{M}=\set{\bm{x}}{\underline{\bm{x}}\leq\bm{x}\leq\overline{\bm{x}}}
. In the following, we provide a detailed analysis for solving the subproblems
eq.

22a
–
eq.

22c
.

Subproblem
eq.

22a
.

The minimization subproblem over
\bm{m}
in
eq.

22a
has a closed-form solution given by:

\bm{m}^{k}=\left(\mu\bm{L}^{\top}\bm{L}+{\tau}_{1}\nabla^{\top}\nabla+{\tau}_{2}\bm{I}\right)^{-1}\left(\mu\bm{L}^{\top}\bm{y}+{\tau}_{1}\nabla^{\top}(\bm{g}_{1}^{k-1}+\bm{g}_{2}^{k-1}+\bm{\nu}_{1}^{k-1})+{\tau}_{2}(\bm{p}^{k-1}+\bm{\nu}_{2}^{k-1})\right),

(24)

where
\bm{I}
is the identity matrix.

Subproblem
eq.

22b
.

Regarding the update of
\bm{g}_{1}
, we need to solve the following subproblem at iteration
k
:

\min_{\bm{g}}~\|\bm{g}\|_{1}+\frac{1}{2\gamma}\|\bm{g}-\bm{z}\|_{2}^{2},\quad\text{with}\quad\gamma=\frac{1}{\tau_{1}}\quad\text{and}\quad\bm{z}=\nabla\bm{m}^{\,k}-\bm{g}_{2}^{\,k-1}-\bm{\nu}_{1}^{\,k-1}.

(25)

Although this formulation involves the non-differentiable
\ell_{1}
norm, it admits a closed-form solution given by the proximal operator of the
\ell_{1}
norm
[
28
]
:

\bm{g}_{1}^{k}=\text{prox}_{\gamma\ell_{l}}(\bm{z})=\text{prox}_{\frac{1}{\tau_{1}}\ell_{l}}\left(\nabla\bm{m}^{\,k}-\bm{g}_{2}^{\,k-1}-\bm{\nu}_{1}^{\,k-1}\right),

(26)

where
\text{prox}_{\gamma\ell_{l}}(\bm{z})
is the well-known soft-thresholding operator, defined as

\left[\text{prox}_{\gamma\ell_{l}}(\bm{z})\right]_{i}\;=\;\text{sign}(z_{i})\,\max\bigl(|z_{i}|-\gamma,\,0\bigr).

(27)

This useful property highlights the power of proximal operators in handling non-differentiable optimization problems. To better understand this, consider the Moreau envelope of the
\ell_{1}
norm, defined as

M_{\gamma\ell_{1}}(\bm{z})=\min_{\bm{g}}~\|\bm{g}\|_{1}+\frac{1}{2\gamma}\|\bm{g}-\bm{z}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\|\bm{z}\|_{1}.

(28)

Intuitively,
M_{\gamma\ell_{1}}
can be interpreted as a smooth, regularized version of the
\ell_{1}
norm. It is continuously differentiable, shares the same minimizers as
\|\cdot\|_{1}
, and is therefore much easier to handle in optimization. Importantly, the gradient of the Moreau envelope is
[
43
]

\frac{d}{d\bm{z}}M_{\gamma\ell_{1}}(\bm{z})=\frac{d}{d\bm{z}}\left(\min_{\bm{g}}~\|\bm{g}\|_{1}+\frac{1}{2\gamma}\|\bm{g}-\bm{z}\|_{2}^{2}\right)=\frac{1}{\gamma}\left(\bm{z}-\text{prox}_{\gamma\ell_{l}}(\bm{z})\right).

(29)

Equivalently,

\text{prox}_{\gamma\ell_{l}}(\bm{z})=\bm{z}-\gamma\frac{d}{d\bm{z}}M_{\gamma\ell_{1}}(\bm{z}).

(30)

Thus, the update
\bm{g}_{1}^{k}
via the proximal operator can be viewed as performing a gradient-descent step on the smooth Moreau envelope of the
\ell_{1}
norm, with
\gamma
acting as the step size.

Subproblem
eq.

22c
.

The update of
\bm{g}_{2}
associated with Tikhonov term, is given by:

\bm{g}_{2}^{k}=\left(\bm{I}+\frac{\beta^{k-1}}{\tau_{1}}\overline{\nabla}^{\top}\overline{\nabla}\right)^{-1}\left(\nabla\bm{m}^{k}-\bm{g}_{1}^{k}-\bm{\nu}_{1}^{k-1}\right).

(31)

Automatic balancing parameter selection.

Consider the piecewise smooth model shown in
fig.

1
. The TT functional in
eq.

19
aims to separately recover the model components (
\bm{m}_{1}
,
\bm{m}_{2}
) shown in
figs.

1
and
1
, while the alternative TT functional in
eq.

23
jointly updates
\bm{m}
together with its gradient components,
\bm{g}_{1}\!=\!\nabla\bm{m}_{1}
and
\bm{g}_{2}\!=\!\nabla\bm{m}_{2}
. In both cases, the accuracy of blocky-smooth decomposition is governed by the balancing parameter
\beta
. However, the model’s gradient domain offers a more intuitive and adaptive approach to decomposition.
As shown in
figs.

1
,
1
, and
1
, the overall model gradient
{\bm{g}}\!=\!\nabla{\bm{m}}
(
fig.

1
) can be viewed as a mixture of two distinct components: a non-Gaussian distributed component (sparse part,
\bm{g}_{1}
,
fig.

1
) and a Gaussian distributed component (smooth part,
\bm{g}_{2}
,
fig.

1
).
The parameter
\beta
is determined by treating the nonzero entries of
\bm{g}_{1}
(representing discontinuities) as anomalies in
\bm{g}
, whose magnitudes can be characterized using robust statistics
[
49
]
.
The procedure consists of three main steps:

I.

Robust z-score computation: To identify anomalous components in the model gradient, robust z-score is used. The robust z-score for each component of the model gradient is computed as:

z_{i}=\frac{[\bm{g}]_{i}-\operatorname{median}({\bm{g}})}{\operatorname{MAD}(\bm{g})},

(32)

where
[\bm{g}]_{i}
represents the
i^{\text{th}}
sample of
\bm{g}
, and MAD denotes the median absolute deviation, defined as:

\text{MAD}(\bm{g})=1.4826~\text{median}(\bm{g}-\text{median}(\bm{g})),

(33)

which measures how far the values in
\bm{g}
typically are from the median. Here, the constant scaling factor
1.4826
makes MAD a consistent estimator of the standard deviation under the Gaussian distribution (see e.g,
[
48
]
).

II.

Anomaly detection: In the anomaly detection step, elements satisfying
\{\bm{z}_{i}|~|\bm{z}_{i}|>\tau_{\text{nrm}}\}
are classified as anomalies, where
\tau_{\texttt{nrm}}\in[2.5,4]
is a predefined threshold. Here,
\tau_{\texttt{nrm}}
plays the role of the standard deviation cut-off and helps to determine what is considered as anomaly or outlier in the model’s gradient.
In practice,
\tau_{\text{nrm}}
is chosen in the range
[2.5,4]
depending on e.g., the level of sensitivity in detecting outliers. For example, using a higher threshold (e.g., 3-4) will flag fewer data points, making the detection more conservative, while lower thresholds like
2.5
or even
2
make the detection more sensitive and are useful to catch even subtle outliers.

The non-anomalous (smooth) components of
\bm{g}
, are then extracted by:

\texttt{nrm}(\bm{g})=\set{[\bm{g}]_{i}:\left}{z_{i}}.

(34)

III.

Balancing parameter adaptation: Since
\bm{g}\!=\!\bm{g}(\beta)
and
\bm{g}_{2}=\bm{g}_{2}(\beta)
, the balancing parameter
\beta
is adapted to ensure that the normally distributed elements of
\bm{g}
identified in
eq.

34
match the smooth component of the gradient,
\bm{g}_{2}
, updated in
eq.

22c
. This is formulated as a root-finding problem:

\text{Find}\;\beta^{*}~~~\text{such that}~~\phi(\beta^{*})=0,~~\text{where}~~\phi(\beta)=\|\bm{g}_{2}(\beta)\|_{\infty}-\|\texttt{nrm}(\bm{g}(\beta))\|_{\infty}.

(35)

Starting from an initial value
\beta^{0}
, the optimal
\beta
at iteration
k
is derived through the following fixed-point iteration:

\beta^{k}=\left(\frac{2\|\bm{g}_{2}^{k}\|_{\infty}}{\|\bm{g}_{2}^{k}\|_{\infty}+\|\texttt{nrm}(\bm{g}(\beta))\|_{\infty}}\right)\beta^{k-1}.

(36)

\bm{m}^{k}=\left(\mu\bm{L}^{\top}\bm{L}+{\tau}_{1}\nabla^{\top}\nabla+{\tau}_{2}\bm{I}\right)^{-1}\left(\mu\bm{L}^{\top}\bm{y}+{\tau}_{1}\nabla^{\top}(\bm{g}_{1}^{k-1}+\bm{g}_{2}^{k-1}+\bm{\nu}_{1}^{k-1})+{\tau}_{2}(\bm{p}^{k-1}+\bm{\nu}_{2}^{k-1})\right)

Considerations for elastic FWI.

The AL formulation for elastic media is analyzed in
[
6
]
. In two-parameter elastic FWI, the inner ADMM iteration for TT regularization follows the same principle as in the acoustic case, as summarized in
algorithm

2
. However, due to the joint update of (
\bm{m}_{\text{p}},\bm{m}_{\text{s}}
), each auxiliary and dual variable consists of two components:

\bm{g}_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{g}_{1,p}\\
\bm{g}_{1,s}\end{bmatrix},\quad\bm{g}_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{g}_{2,p}\\
\bm{g}_{2,s}\end{bmatrix},\quad\bm{\nu}_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\nu}_{1,p}\\
\bm{\nu}_{1,s}\end{bmatrix},\quad\bm{\nu}_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\nu}_{2,p}\\
\bm{\nu}_{2,s}\end{bmatrix},\quad\bm{p}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{p}_{p}\\
\bm{p}_{s}\end{bmatrix}.

In multiparameter media, different physical parameters exhibit distinct characteristics, requiring specific tuning for each model. The feasible sets for (
\bm{m}_{p},\bm{m}_{s}
) are defined as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{p}=\set{\bm{m}_{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\underline{\bm{m}}_{p}\leq\bm{m}_{p}\leq\overline{\bm{m}}_{p}},

(37)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{s}=\set{\bm{m}_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\underline{\bm{m}}_{s}\leq\bm{m}_{s}\leq\overline{\bm{m}}_{s}}.

(38)

Moreover, to account for parameter-specific tuning, the diagonal structures of the tuning matrices in line 1 of
algorithm

2
are defined as:

\bm{\Gamma}=\begin{bmatrix}{{\tau}}_{1,p}\bm{I}&\bm{0}\\
\bm{0}&{\tau}_{1,s}\bm{I}\end{bmatrix},\quad\bm{\Upsilon}=\begin{bmatrix}{\tau}_{2,p}\bm{I}&\bm{0}\\
\bm{0}&{\tau}_{2,s}\bm{I}\end{bmatrix}.

(39)

where
\bm{I}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is the identity matrix, and
{\tau}_{1,p},{\tau}_{1,s},{\tau}_{2,p},{\tau}_{2,s}>0
are relative weights assigned to the bound constraints and regularization applied to each parameter class.
The block-diagonal gradient operator (line 1) for the elastic inversion framework is given by:

\mathscr{\bm{D}}=\begin{bmatrix}\nabla&\bm{0}\\
\bm{0}&\nabla\end{bmatrix}.

(40)

Finally, similar to acoustic FWI, to control the balance between regularization terms, separate balancing parameters (
\beta_{p},\beta_{s}
) are determined for each parameter class (lines 7-8).

3.2
Practical considerations.

3.2.1
On the selection of the free parameters.

The strategy for tuning free parameters follows that of
[
2
]
with some modifications to enhance generality and robustness across both acoustic and elastic FWI settings.

The wavefield update in
eq.

11
leverages the robustness of the AL formulation with respect to the penalty parameter
{\mu}
. As demonstrated in
[
3
]
for acoustic FWI and extended in
[
6
]
to elastic FWI, the algorithm performs reliably with a fixed
{\mu}
across all iterations. Consequently, we maintain
{\mu}
as a constant for both problem types.

Regarding the model subproblem for acoustic FWI, the ratios
(\frac{\tau_{1}}{\mu},\frac{\tau_{2}}{\mu})
associated with regularization and bound constraints are selected based on the diagonal of the Hessian matrix,
\bm{L}^{\top}\bm{L}
. Their values decrease over iterations to reduce the impact of regularization and constraints near the convergence point:

\displaystyle\frac{\tau_{1}}{\mu}=\frac{c_{1}}{k}\times\text{max}(|\text{diag}(\bm{L}^{\top}\bm{L})|),\quad 0<c_{1}<1,

(41)

\displaystyle\frac{\tau_{2}}{\mu}=\frac{c_{2}}{k}\times\text{max}(|\text{diag}(\bm{L}^{\top}\bm{L})|),\quad 0<c_{2}<1,

with
c_{1}\geq c_{2}
. For the case of elastic FWI, the ratios
(\frac{\tau_{1,j}}{\mu},\frac{\tau_{2,j}}{\mu})
with
j\in\set{p,s}
indexing the parameter class; are set similar to
eq.

41
. However, for elastic inversion
\bm{L}^{\top}\bm{L}
is a 2 by 2 block diagonal matrix. Consequently, we use the diagonal of the first and second block for the case of P-and S-wave parameters, respectively.
Regarding the subproblems
eq.

26
and
eq.

31
, for each parameter class (
\{p\}
for acoustic and
\{p,s\}
for elastic) we set:

\frac{1}{\tau_{1,j}}=c_{3,j}\times\text{max}\left(\sqrt{\left|\nabla\bm{m}^{k}_{j}-\bm{g}_{2,j}^{k-1}-\bm{\nu}_{1,j}^{k-1}\right|^{2}}\right),\quad 0<c_{3,j}<1,\quad j\in\set{p,s}

where the optimal selection for
c_{3,j}
depends on the properties of the parameter class
j
. Our analysis suggests that starting with a high
c_{3}
value during low frequency inversion and gradually decreasing it as the frequency increases leads to improved results.
Finally, for updating the balancing parameter
\beta
, a predefined threshold distance
\tau_{\text{nrm}}
, is required to separate anomalies in the model gradient. In FWI, the optimal choice of
\tau_{\text{nrm}}
depends on the problem, however, its value typically ranges from 2.5 to 4. Higher
\tau_{\text{nrm}}
promotes smoother model updates, suitable for early iterations with low-frequency data, while lower
\tau_{\text{nrm}}
allows for sharper features as frequency increases.

3.2.2
Implementation considerations.

For all numerical examples the variables are initialized as

I.

The dual variables
\bm{\lambda}
and (
\bm{\nu}_{1},\bm{\nu}_{2}
) are initialized to zero.

II.

The variables
\bm{g}_{1}
,
\bm{g}_{2}
are set to zero at the beginning of the inversion process.

III.

The dual variable
\bm{\lambda}
is reset to zero whenever we change the data frequency for inversion.

We compare the performance of TT regularization with the Tikhonov and TV regularizations, all implemented with the same algorithm described above.
The Tikhonov regularization is achieved by disabling the update of
\bm{g}_{1}
, i.e. keeping
\bm{g}_{1}=\bm{0}
throughout the process. The TV regularization is implemented by skipping
\bm{g}_{2}
to focus solely on the TV term.

Finally, we use the relative model error (RME) defined as

\text{RME}=\frac{\|\bm{m}^{k}-\bm{m}^{*}\|_{2}}{\|\bm{m}^{*}\|_{2}},

where
\bm{m}^{*}
represents the true model to show the convergence and reconstruction accuracy different methods. Computations were conducted on a dual Intel Xeon Platinum 8176 processor system with 56 cores operating at 2.10 GHz.

Conclusions.

We proposed an adaptive Tikhonov-TV regularization technique for acoustic and elastic Full Waveform Inversion (FWI), utilizing the efficacy and simplicity of the alternating direction method of multipliers. The proposed method dynamically balances smoothness and edge preservation by adjusting the influence of Tikhonov and TV regularizations, allowing accurate imaging of subsurface models with integrated structural characteristics. Numerical experiments on acoustic and elastic FWI benchmarks demonstrated the method’s resilience to local minima, accelerated convergence rate, and superior reconstruction quality compared to Tikhonov and TV regularizations when applied separately. The method demonstrated notable efficacy in addressing the challenges of imaging complex geological media, including subsalt imaging and parameter crosstalk mitigation in elastic FWI. The adaptive technique for adjusting the balancing parameter demonstrated robustness against variations in initial parameter selection and maintained stable performance despite sparse acquisition configurations and noisy data. Importantly, the added computational cost of the adaptive strategy remains minimal, making it a practical and adaptable tool for high-resolution seismic imaging.
