Tuberculosis disease and infection screening among migrants in the Netherlands 2019–2023: a national retrospective observational study
Authors: Gerard de Vries, Annemieke Mulder, Ineke Spruijt, Erik Huisman, Henrieke Schimmel, Merel Siebers, Natasha Vrubleuskaya, Marijne Zandbelt-Smits, Rianne van Gageldonk-Lafeber, Susan van den Hof
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundMigrants from tuberculosis (TB) high-incidence countries are at increased risk of TB, therefore, screening for TB is frequently implemented in host countries, but evidence for systematic screening for TB infection (TBI) is limited. We evaluated the national migrant TB/TBI screening programme in the Netherlands to assess TB yield, TBI prevalence, and the subsequent cascade of care.MethodsThis retrospective observational study included all immigrants and asylum seekers screened for TB and TBI in the Netherlands in 2019–2023. We linked data from the national TB client information system to the Netherlands TB Register to assess TB yield (cases detected per 100,000 screened), TBI prevalence, and treatment initiation and completion, by migrant group, country of birth, and age group.Findings149,214 migrants were included (87,511 immigrants; 61,703 asylum seekers). TB at entry was diagnosed in 69 immigrants and 170 asylum seekers, corresponding to yields of 79 per 100,000 (95% CI 62–100) and 276 per 100,000 (237–320), respectively. Among 202 pulmonary TB cases, 129 (63.9%) was bacteriologically confirmed and 147 (72.8%) did not report cough symptoms. TB treatment completion was 92.8% (64/69) among immigrants and 81.2% (138/170) among asylum seekers. TBI screening included 20,378 immigrants. TBI prevalence increased with age, from 0.6% in children <12 years, 4.4% in adolescents 12–17 years, to 12.9% in adults ≥ 18 years. These findings support targeted screening policies as a core component of TB prevention and control in low-incidence settings.FundingThis work was supported by the Ministry of Health, Welfare and Sports (VWS), the Netherlands.Keywords: Evaluation, Migrants, Screening, Tuberculosis, Tuberculosis infection

Research in context

Evidence before this study

Evidence on integral tuberculosis (TB) and TB infection (TBI) screening is lacking. We searched PubMed from inception to 31 December 2025 using the terms tuberculosis, tuberculosis infection, screening, migrants, treatment, preventive treatment, with no language restrictions. Most prior studies focused on detection of TB disease, some more recent on cost-effectiveness of TBI screening, with few reporting on TBI detection or TB preventive treatment completion among migrants in routine national programmes. Limited evidence exists on large-scale, national evaluations of TB and TBI screening, age-stratified yields, and the cascade of preventive treatment.

Added value of this study

This evaluation included nearly 150,000 migrants screened over five years in the Netherlands, integrating TB and TBI data from national registries. We show that TB yield was over three times as high among asylum seekers than immigrants and that 72.8% of pulmonary TB cases did not report cough symptoms, underscoring the limitations of symptom-based detection. TBI prevalence among immigrants increased with age, reaching 12.9% among adults, of whom two-third completed preventive treatment. The low TBI prevalence and limited treatment uptake among children <12 years informed proposed changes in national screening policy.

Implications of all the available evidence

National, targeted TB and TBI screening programmes can effectively identify both TB disease and TB infection among migrant populations at increased risk of TB in low-incidence countries. Age- and migrant group-specific data allow for optimisation of screening strategies, thereby improving the efficiency and impact of preventive interventions. The findings support prioritisation of asylum seekers for TB screening.

Introduction

The natural history of tuberculosis (TB) spans a spectrum from infection to asymptomatic and symptomatic TB disease, and from non-infectious to highly infectious forms.1 These stages, often characterised by prolonged latency, offer opportunities for prevention and early diagnosis, improving individual outcomes and reducing transmission of Mycobacterium tuberculosis. Systematic screening for TB and TB infection (TBI) among populations at increased risk for TB,2 alongside TB preventive treatment (TPT),3 are central components of the World Health Organization (WHO) End TB Strategy aiming to achieve a 90% reduction in TB incidence by 2035 compared with 2015 levels.4

Migrants from TB high-incidence countries are disproportionately affected by TB due to exposure in the country of birth, during migration and in the host country. This risk for TB is further influenced by a range of individual, biological, environmental, and socio-economic determinants.5 Migrants are also at increased risk of extrapulmonary TB, multidrug-resistant TB (MDR-TB), HIV/TB co-infection and poorer treatment outcomes.6 In the European Union/European Economic Area (EU/EEA), 37.6% of individuals notified with TB disease were of foreign origin in 2024.7 This proportion exceeds 80% in several countries, including the Netherlands. Evidence-based TB and TBI screening for migrants at increased risk of TB, delivered in culturally and linguistically appropriate ways with free access to care, is therefore recommended in low-incidence settings to provide good care and limit transmission in the host country.8

Approaches to migrant screening across EU/EEA countries vary in timing, target populations, settings and diagnostic methods.9,10 Programmes are most effective when targeted to migrants from high-incidence countries, achieve high screening coverage, and ensure strong linkage to care.11,12 They should be informed by national epidemiological data, as country-of-origin incidence may not reflect the epidemiology of migrant populations upon arrival in the host country.13 Timely data are essential to guide responsive, evidence-based screening strategies.14

In the Netherlands, a low-incidence country (4.3 cases per 100,000 population notified in 2024) with 81% of TB cases among foreign-born individuals, TB screening is mandated for migrants from high-incidence countries if they intend to stay more than three months in the country, including persons seeking asylum.7 Data on TB, and more recently TBI screening, have been routinely collected and evaluated since 1998 in accordance with the Dutch Population Screening Act and National Plans for TB Control.15,16 These evaluations have informed updates to the list of countries eligible for screening and refinements to screening algorithms for refugees and asylum seekers, (hereafter ‘asylum seekers’) and for other migrants, including labour migrants and international students (hereafter ‘immigrants’).

We aimed to evaluate the current migrant TB/TBI screening strategy in the Netherlands and to explore possible reprioritisation of migrant groups eligible for screening. Therefore, we assessed characteristics of those screened in 2019–2023, the yield of TB and TBI screening, and treatment initiation and outcome.

Methods

Study design

This retrospective observational study used data from all immigrants and asylum seekers enrolled in TB/TBI screening in the Netherlands in 2019–2023, with additional detail for those diagnosed with TB and TBI. TB is a notifiable disease in the Netherlands. Municipal Public Health Services (PHSs) report all TB cases to the National Institute for Public Health and the Environment (RIVM), which maintains the Netherlands TB Register (NTR). Individuals diagnosed with TBI are reported to the NTR by PHSs if consent is provided, as TBI is not a notifiable condition in the Netherlands.

Immigrants from countries with a TB incidence ≥100 per 100,000 population are referred by the Immigration and Naturalisation Services (IND) to PHSs for screening, which must be completed within 3 months of arrival. The PHSs inform the IND that TB screening has taken place, without disclosing results. TB screening is a requirement for the IND to issue a residence permit to the immigrant. For immigrants aged <18 years, screening consists of a TBI test combined with a symptom checklist. Both the tuberculin skin test (TST) and interferon gamma release assays (IGRAs) are used. IGRA testing includes QuantiFERON-TB Gold Plus (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) and T-SPOT. TB (Oxford Immunotec, Oxford, UK) assays. A TST result of ≥10 mm is followed by IGRA for confirmation. TB public heath physicians perform clinical and radiological evaluations to exclude TB disease and discuss options for TPT with individuals diagnosed with TBI. For immigrants aged ≥18 years, screening at entry is conducted using a chest X-ray (CXR). Immigrants from countries with a TB incidence ≥200 per 100,000 population are subsequently invited for half-yearly CXR screening for two years. In 2018, an alternative follow-up screening strategy was introduced, replacing repeated CXRs with a single TBI test. Nine of the 25 PHSs implemented this strategy: one throughout the entire five-year evaluation period and eight during part of the evaluation period (See Supplementary analysis protocol).

Asylum seekers from countries with a TB incidence ≥50 per 100,000 population and their family reunification applicants generally begin their application procedure at one of the two national reception centres and are screened with CXR on the second day. Since July 1, 2023, CXR entry screening for asylum seekers aged <12 years has been replaced by TST combined with a symptom checklist. Asylum seekers aged ≥12 years from countries with TB incidence ≥200 per 100,000 population are invited for half-yearly CXR screening for two years. TBI screening was not offered systematically to asylum seekers ≥12 years.

Results from entry and follow-up screening of immigrants and asylum seekers are recorded in the national TB client information system (iTBC) used by all TB public health departments of the 25 PHSs.

Data collection and management

A dataset from iTBC, including screening results up to April 28, 2024, was obtained. Variables comprised unique identifier, age, sex, country of birth, migrant category (asylum seeker, immigrant), screening test (CXR, TST, IGRA), and test dates and results.

The dataset was linked to NTR data on TB and TBI cases identified through the screening programme (See Supplementary material: data analysis protocol). For TB cases, data included year of diagnosis, disease type (pulmonary, extrapulmonary, or both), cough symptoms, previous TB diagnosis, bacteriological results (smear microscopy and culture), drug susceptibility, and treatment outcome. For TBI, data included management (TPT or half-yearly radiological follow-up for two years), regimen, and preventive treatment outcome.

Data quality checks were undertaken to identify missing cases and validate records, in consultation with PHSs. The dataset was further supplemented with annual Dutch lists of countries whose migrants were eligible for TB screening to assess eligibility at the time of screening.

Definitions and data analysis

TB diagnosis was based on clinical assessment by the treating clinician. Cases were considered bacteriologically confirmed if microscopy showed acid fast bacilli, PCR was positive for M. tuberculosis complex, or culture confirmed M. tuberculosis complex.

For the estimation of TB yield at entry screening, all migrants screened with CXR or TBI testing were included. Yield was defined as the number of TB cases detected per 100,000 individuals screened, and with 95% CI. Yield by country of birth was compared with the WHO-estimated TB incidence for 2021.

CXR findings were classified as: i) suggestive for TB disease; ii) consistent with previous TB; iii) abnormal requiring further examination; iv) other findings, not requiring further examination; and v) normal.

TBI was defined as a positive IGRA result (initial or confirmatory) or a TST induration of ≥15 mm in children aged <5 years, after exclusion of TB disease.

The outcome of TB treatment was classified as cured/completed, lost to follow-up (including individuals who did not start treatment), and not evaluated (treatment continued outside the Netherlands or no consent for outcome reporting was provided). Initiation of TPT was defined as the number and proportion of persons with TBI who started TPT; and TPT completion as the number and proportion reported by the PHS as having completed treatment.

For analysis of TBI screening at entry among immigrants aged <18 years, data from 19 PHSs were included, as these PHSs had systematically screened individuals in this age group for TBI during the evaluation period (See Supplementary material: data analysis protocol). For immigrants aged ≥18 years, data from nine PHSs were included, as these had replaced CXR with TBI testing in follow-up screening during the evaluation period. Proportion of TBI by country of birth were calculated with 95% CIs.

Chi-square tests were used to assess differences in proportions observed.

Ethics approval

Data transfer agreements between PHSs and RIVM permitted periodic evaluation of the migrant screening programme, included linkage of iTBC and NTR data. We received approval from the NTR’s Registration Committee to use the provided pseudonymised dataset which was collected under the legislative authority of the Public Health Act. As this study used de-identified data from the NTR and did not involve any potential risk to persons, it was exempted from the requirement for further ethical approval under the provisions of the Dutch Medical Research Act (WMO), as determined by the Medical Ethical Committee.

Role of the funding source

This work was supported by the Ministry of Health, Welfare and Sports (VWS), the Netherlands. The funder had no role in study design, data collection and analysis, decision to publish, or preparation of the manuscript.

Results

Characteristics of migrants screened

A total of 149,214 migrants with valid screening results (87,511 immigrants and 61,703 asylum seekers) were included (Fig. 1). Immigrants were more often female (46,386/87,511 = 53.0%), whereas asylum seekers were predominantly men (43,113/61,703 = 69.9%) (Table 2). Age distributions differed: most immigrants were aged 18–34 years (58,422/87,511 = 66.8%), compared with 52.0% (32,081/61,703) of asylum seekers. Conversely, 14.6% (8982/61,703) of asylum seekers were aged 12–17 years vs. 3.1% (2753/87,511) of immigrants; many of these asylum seekers were unaccompanied minors.

Countries of birth also differed. The most common countries among immigrants were India (38.7%), South Africa (11.7%), Indonesia (7.0%), the Philippines (5.7%), and Viet Nam (3.2%). Among asylum seekers the main countries were Eritrea (16.1%), Afghanistan (11.6%), Algeria (9.2%), Somalia (7.8%), and Morocco (6.7%).

Abnormal CXR findings requiring action were more frequently observed among asylum seekers than immigrants (2.7% vs. 1.9%; p < 0.001). Among those with CXR findings suggestive for TB, asylum seekers were more likely to be diagnosed with TB than immigrants (23.6% vs. 8.4%; p < 0.001).

TB prevalence at entry screening

TB was identified in 69 immigrants and 170 asylum seekers, corresponding to yields of 79 per 100,000 (95% CI 62–100) and 276 per 100,000 (95% CI 237–320), respectively (Table 2). Yields were higher among asylum seekers across all age groups. Both in immigrants and asylum seekers, the highest yield was observed among those aged 12–17 years and the lowest yield among children <12 years.

Among immigrants, the highest yields were observed in those from Indonesia (18/6179; 291 per 100,000) and the Philippines (12/4986; 241 per 100,000), followed by India (17/33,878; 50 per 100,000) and South Africa (5/10,273; 49 per 100,000). Among asylum seekers, the highest yields were in those from Sierra Leone (6/676; 888 per 100,000), Eritrea (70/9944; 704 per 100,000), Somalia (28/4816; 581 per 100,000), the Gambia (7/1246; 562 per 100,000) and (South) Sudan (10/1926; 519 per 100,000), followed by Ethiopia (8/2910; 275 per 100,000), Afghanistan (9/7175; 125 per 100,000), Algeria (7/5655; 124 per 100,000), and Morocco (5/4143; 121 per 100,000).

Among immigrants, TB prevalence at entry was lower than WHO-estimated TB incidence for all countries with more than five people with TB identified through screening in this study (Fig. 2). In contrast, among asylum seekers, screening prevalence exceeded the WHO estimates for all countries except for Afghanistan.

Characteristics of TB detected through entry screening

Among all 239 migrants (immigrants and asylum seekers) diagnosed with TB through screening, 202 (84.5%) had pulmonary TB (PTB) and 27 (15.5%) extrapulmonary TB (EPTB) (Table 3). Overall, 135 (56.5%) were bacteriologically confirmed (129 [63.9%] of PTB and 37 [16.2%] of EPTB); two cases (1.5%) had MDR-TB. Among all 202 migrants with PTB, 44 (21.8%) had acid-fast bacilli detected on microscopy, with positivity observed more frequently among asylum seekers than immigrants (24.3% vs. 15.5%; p = 0.09).

Cascade of care for TB

All immigrants with TB initiated treatment, while 5 (2.9%) asylum seekers did not. Treatment completion was higher among immigrants than asylum seekers (92.8% [64/69] vs. 81.2% [138/170]; p = 0.01). After excluding individuals without evaluated outcomes (missing data and treatment continued outside the Netherlands), completion increased to 97.0% (64/66) among immigrants and 87.9% (138/157) among asylum seekers (p = 0.01).

TST was used as the initial test in 7302 (76.0%) of those aged <18 years and 10,341 (96.1%) of those aged ≥18 years; IGRA was used in the remainder (Supplementary Tables S1–S3). Among those with TST ≥10 mm, IGRA was positive in 9.4% (30/320) of children aged <12 years, 30.1% (59/196) of those aged 12–17 years, and 48.4% (1264/2609) of those aged ≥18 years.

TBI prevalence increased with age: 0.6% (95% CI 0.5–0.8) in immigrants aged <12 years, 4.4% (95% CI 3.6–5.4) in those aged 12–17 years, and 12.9% (95% CI 12.3–13.6) in those ≥18 years. Among adults, prevalence exceeded 20% in immigrants from several African countries (Kenya, Nigeria, Zimbabwe), whereas rates were lower in those from South Africa (4.9%).

Cascade of care for immigrants with TBI

TPT was initiated in 68.6% (33/48) of children aged <12 years, 85.2% (69/81) of those aged 12–17 years, and 70.3% (720/1024) of adults (Fig. 1). Among those initiating TPT (n = 822), the most common regimen was 3 months of rifampicin and isoniazid (93.2%). Other regimens included 4 months of rifampicin (3.4%), 6 or 9 months of isoniazid (2.1%), 4 months of rifampicin and isoniazid (0.9%), and other regimens (0.5%).

Among those who initiated TPT, treatment completion was 93.9% (31/33), 95.7% (66/69) and 92.1% (663/720) across the three age groups, respectively. When considering both initiation and completion, 64.6% (31/48), 81.5% (66/81) and 64.7% (663/1024) of individuals with TBI in the <12, 12–17, and ≥18 years age groups, respectively, successfully completed treatment.

Discussion

This national evaluation of migrant TB screening in the Netherlands included nearly 150,000 individuals over a five-year period. TB was identified in 69 immigrants and 170 asylum seekers at entry, corresponding to yields of 79 and 276 per 100,000 screened, respectively. Among people diagnosed with PTB, 63.9% were bacteriologically confirmed, and only 27.2% reported couth symptoms, with lower proportions observed among immigrants than asylum seekers (13.8% vs. 32.6%). Treatment completion was higher among immigrants with TB than asylum seekers (92.8% vs. 81.2%).

For the first time in our national evaluations, TBI screening was included, covering over 20,000 immigrants. TBI prevalence varied by age: 0.6% among children aged <12 years, 4.4% among adolescents aged 12–17 years (screened only from countries with TB incidence ≥100 per 100,000), and 12.9% among adults ≥18 years screened (screened only from countries with TB incidence ≥200 per 100,000). In the cascade of care analysis, 64.6%, 81.5% and 64.7% of individuals in these respective age groups both initiated and completed TPT.

Our findings confirm the elevated risk of TB among asylum seekers, often exceeding the WHO estimated incidence in their countries of birth.17, 18, 19 This vulnerability reflects both high TB burden in their home countries and adverse conditions during migration.18 In contrast, TB prevalence among immigrants was lower than WHO-estimated incidence in their countries of birth, likely reflecting the selective migration of more affluent, healthier individuals for work or study. Given the Dutch yield criterion for screening (≥50 cases detected per 100,000 screened), our results support maintaining divergent screening thresholds for immigrants (TB incidence ≥100 per 100,000) and asylum seekers (≥50 per 100,000).

A substantial proportion of people diagnosed with PTB (72.8%) did not report cough, a key symptom in passive case detection. This confirms the low sensitivity of screening based on symptoms only.2 The WHO recently defined the term “asymptomatic TB” as TB disease in individuals without TB-suggestive symptoms at screening.20,21 Community-based prevalence surveys in high-incidence settings show that about half of bacteriologically confirmed PTB cases are asymptomatic.20, 21, 22 Such individuals can contribute substantially to transmission, underscoring the importance of early identification. Our evaluation demonstrates the added value of systematic radiological screening for detecting TB among migrants at increased risk for TB before symptom onset.

Approximately two-thirds (63.9%) of people diagnosed with PTB had bacteriological confirmation, comparable to other studies reporting 64–72%.23 Previous evaluations in the Netherlands reported higher confirmation rates among immigrants (81.2% in 2005–2010 and 69.2% in 2011–2015) and asylum seekers (76.4% in 2011–2015). This suggests a greater reliance on radiology for the TB diagnosis during the current evaluation period, warranting further investigation. The lower TB treatment completion among asylum seekers (81.2%) was mainly due to loss of follow-up, including persons who did not initiate treatment, or who migrated abroad. Tailored care to the needs of people with TB and strengthened cross-border collaboration is essential to ensure continuity of care and monitoring of treatment outcomes.5

The estimated global TBI prevalence is 23.0%, with substantial regional variation.24 One systematic review including more than 17 million screened migrants reported a pooled TBI prevalence of 14.9%.25 However, TBI prevalence estimates across studies are often not directly comparable because of differences in study populations, screening methods, and study periods. We observed a TBI prevalence of 12.9% among adult immigrants from countries with TB incidence ≥200 per 100,000. This relatively low TBI prevalence likely reflects the characteristics of the migrant population, including younger age and higher socioeconomic status among some subgroups, such as labour migrants from India and South Africa, who are often employed in high-skilled occupations in the Netherlands. In addition, the Dutch screening algorithm, which uses an initial TST followed by IGRA confirmation, may have contributed to the lower observed TBI prevalence by reducing the number of false-positive TST results. Furthermore, we would like to note that immunoreactivity may not always reflect viable M. tuberculosis infection.24

TPT initiation and completion were generally high in our study and exceeded rates reported in a systematic review including studies from the USA, the Western Pacific Region (Australia, Japan and South Korea), the European Union, and the United Kingdom, which reported pooled estimates of 69% initiation, 74% treatment completion, and 52% for both initiation and completion.26 Our results likely reflect the well-structured, client-centred public health TB programme in the Netherlands.27 However, TPT uptake was lower among children <12 years, probably due to limited parental acceptance giving preventive treatment to healthy children. Based on the low TB/TBI prevalence and limited uptake in this evaluation, the National TB Guideline Committee recommended exempting immigrants <12 years from mandatory screening, illustrating the importance of epidemiological data to guiding evidence-based policy.

In the Netherlands, TBI screening usually follows a two-step protocol: initial TST followed by IGRA for TST ≥10 mm. In adults, IGRA confirmed infection in approximately 50% of those with a positive TST; confirmation rates were even lower in younger age groups. False-positive TST results in children may be attributable to prior BCG vaccination. In adults, false-positive TST results may be associated with infection by non-tuberculous mycobacteria; however, the high rate of discordant TST and IGRA results warrants further investigation. These findings highlight the need for screening tools with higher specificity and predictive value for recent infection, which could also improve cost-effectiveness of targeted TBI screening.12,28 Emerging TB-antigen skin tests using ESAT-6 and CFP-10 in skin test format, may offer an alternative to improve cost-effectiveness.29

A recent systematic review on TB screening in migrants concluded that the most cost-effective approach is screening for TBI using IGRA, followed by CXR in individuals with a positive test result.30 A Dutch cost-effectiveness study also found that TBI entry screening of immigrants with IGRA yields more quality-adjusted life-years (QALYs) and is cost-effective compared with CXR-based entry screening, provided that TPT completion is high.31 However, the National TB Guideline Committee has not yet recommended discontinuing CXR as part of entry screening for immigrants. For asylum seekers, TBI follow-up screening was more cost-effective than the current CXR-based follow-up strategy and, in most model scenarios, resulted in cost savings.31 Implementation of TBI-based follow-up screening is currently being piloted in the Netherlands, and is expected to have the greatest impact on reducing TB burden among migrants and overall TB incidence in the Netherlands.31

Strengths of this evaluation include the use of systematically collected national TB screening data in a uniform information system and high-quality NTR data, including voluntary TBI registration. Limitations include: inability to determine screening coverage since the actual number of migrants to be screened is not known, missing data on nationality and the use of country of birth as a proxy for nationality, possible under-ascertainment of TB cases due to incomplete iTBC-NTR linkage, and incomplete reporting of TBI due to voluntary registration.

In conclusion, migrant TB screening in the Netherlands effectively identifies people with TB, facilitating early treatment and potentially limiting onward transmission. TBI screening revealed substantial prevalence among older age groups, with high initiation and completion of preventive treatment. This evaluation supports the role of targeted migrant screening as a core component of TB prevention and elimination strategies in low-incidence settings.
