The IDEA3 pilot trial: feasibility, acceptability, and preliminary efficacy of an online adaptation of an evidence-based intervention for campus sexual assault prevention
Authors: Sarah M Peitzmeier, Louise Ashwell, Mackenzie P Adams, Katie Edwards, Charlene Y Senn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEnhanced Assess, Acknowledge, Act (EAAA) is the only intervention shown in a randomized controlled trial to reduce sexual violence victimization against undergraduate women by 50%. EAAA is an in-person, small-group intervention led by two trained peer facilitators over four three-hour sessions. Implementation cost and train-the-trainer complexities have led to limited uptake with relatively few institutions implementing the intervention, despite its efficacy. Adapting EAAA for online delivery would enable use of a centralized facilitator pool, dramatically cutting cost and complexity for institutions who wish to implement it and enabling scale-up of a singularly effective primary prevention intervention. The aim of the current study was to adapt and pilot EAAA for online delivery and assess the feasibility, acceptability, and potential efficacy of the adapted intervention.MethodsUsing the ADAPT-ITT framework for systematic intervention adaptation, we created Internet-Delivered EAAA (IDEA3), which uses an innovative hybrid online/in-person delivery mode whereby pairs of women are together in person, and then facilitators live-facilitate the intervention to a group of up to 8 pairs via videoconferencing. The adaptation process included an initial pilot or “theater” test (n = 8) with direct observation by experts, an expert focus group to offer suggestions on refining the intervention, and a one-armed pilot trial (n = 65). Primary outcomes were feasibility (recruitment and retention), acceptability (participant ratings on post-session surveys and feedback provided in participant focus groups), and preliminary indicators of efficacy (increases in self-defense self-efficacy and ability to detect risk in coercive situations, decreases in rape myth acceptance, as well as reports of using skills learned in the intervention to decrease sexual assault risk).ResultsIDEA3 was delivered successfully with strong recruitment (90% of target) and retention (84%). Participants found the intervention acceptable with high mean enjoyment (8.2 out of 10) and willingness to recommend the training to others (8.5 out of 10). Preliminary indicators of potential efficacy showed that the program significantly increased self-defense self-efficacy and perceived risk of acquaintance rape, while significantly reducing acceptance of rape myths and beliefs about female precipitation of rape.ConclusionsIDEA3 had high feasibility and acceptability, and promising indicators of potential efficacy, comparable to historical data from the in-person EAAA efficacy trial.Trial registrationNCT04797741. Background

Sexual assault on college campuses is a public health problem. While sexual assault can be experienced by students of any gender, undergraduate women (of all sexual identities) make up the largest proportion of sexual assault survivors, with 1 in 4 women experiencing sexual assault during her time in college [1]. Campus sexual assault drives elevated rates of clinically significant depression and anxiety in survivors and contributes to negative educational outcomes [2]. Despite the magnitude of this issue, there are few interventions designed for college settings that have been assessed as effective in reducing perpetration or victimization by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention [3]. While stopping perpetration is the ultimate goal, existing programs targeting cisgender men, who perpetrate over 90% of campus rape, do not yet reliably decrease sexual assault and sometimes have backlash effects [4]. While progress is still being made toward this goal, interventions that focus on bystanders or those at high risk of victimization are necessary, while ensuring that blame continues to be squarely placed on perpetrators [5]. While bystander programs designed for university students of all genders have been shown to increase bystander intention to help and decrease rape myths, sexual assault outcomes are rarely studied and are not found when evaluations are done at the individual level of analysis [6]. The Enhanced Assess, Acknowledge, Act (EAAA) sexual assault resistance program was evaluated in a randomized controlled trial and reduced individual sexual assault victimization by as much as 50% in undergraduate women. EAAA is a 12-hour, in-person, small group-based intervention (≤ 20 women), and is the only intervention shown to have durable reductions in victimization lasting at least two years [7, 8]. It accomplished this while decreasing rape myth acceptance including woman-blaming [8] and self-blame if a sexual assault occurred [9].

EAAA was developed based on a strong evidence foundation (see Rozee & Koss [11] and Senn et al. [10], for a summary) and social psychological theories, including Norris and Nurius’ (1996) cognitive ecological theory explaining the emotional and cognitive barriers that women face when confronted by sexually coercive behavior by a male acquaintance in a social situation that should be safe [10]. EAAA increases women’s abilities to Assess a situation (cognitive appraisal to recognize empirically supported “red flags” typical of campus sexual assault), more quickly Acknowledge that it is a potential assault situation (e.g., overcoming self-doubt that one may be misreading the situation), and Act to resist sexual assault using evidence-based strategies that have been linked to better outcomes, including verbal and physical self-defense (after overcoming psychosocial barriers that may result in a reluctance to defend one’s sexual rights, particularly against someone they know) [11]. The intervention consists of four three-hour modules called Assess, Acknowledge, Act, and Relationships and Sexuality. The last module is a sex-positive sex education module that encourages exploring what sex participants actually do want to have, which bolsters their ability to resist coercion in long-term partnerships [12]. Activities are led by a pair of trained peer facilitators and include facilitated discussions, partnered role plays, watching and responding to videos, brainstorming sessions, small-group discussion of vignettes, and practicing physical and verbal self-defense.

While 92% of public and 75% of private non-profit four-year universities currently offer what they intend to be primary prevention programs for their students [13], the vast majority of these interventions are designed based on legal frameworks focusing on Title IX compliance, rather than public health frameworks, and have not been proven to reduce sexual assault victimization. Despite EAAA demonstrating the largest reductions in campus sexual assault victimization of any RCT-tested intervention [3, 14], few institutions are currently delivering the evidence-based EAAA intervention, due at least in part to the cost of implementing the in-person intervention, which is roughly US$294 per participant in the first year, and $229 in subsequent years, plus the cost and logistical burden of hiring and training a Campus Trainer who then recruits, hires, trains, supervises, and manages a team of peer facilitators on campus. If the university fails to retain its campus trainer (as is common due to high turnover rates in university sexual violence prevention professionals) [15], it would need to hire someone new and send them for training in delivering the intervention, bumping costs up to the first-year cost again. Peer facilitators are usually graduate students; these women can be difficult to hire and retain at colleges with few graduate programs or few graduate programs that last longer than 1–2 years.

To avoid these logistical issues and the complexity of implementing in-person interventions, universities are increasingly turning to online prevention programming to train students at scale: 22% of four-year public institutions use online programming [16]. The most common product (a one-hour online module now called Sexual Assault Prevention for Undergraduates by Vector Solutions; formerly called Haven and owned by EverFi), is used by over 1,300 universities. It was evaluated by the company producing it, using a pretest/posttest design of changes in attitudes and behavioral intention, with no control sites and no measurement of victimization or perpetration [17, 18]. Recent pre-test/post-test studies by independent researchers have found no changes to knowledge and attitudes after taking the intervention [19]. An online program for women called The RealConsent Program® was recently developed and is not widely implemented, but is available from the company Behavioral Science Technologies. RealConsent was shown to reduce frequency and severity of sexual violence victimization among women who experienced some victimization; however, it did not significantly reduce sexual violence victimization for women overall [20]. Other available online programs include Bringing in the Bystander®, which was adapted during COVID for online delivery; however, the original program does not have effects on victimization and no evaluations have been conducted to show that it maintains its efficacy for improving bystander attitudes and behavior in the online setting [21, 22].

Adapting EAAA for online delivery would allow for simplified logistics and lowered cost for delivering this uniquely effective program which could in turn increase reach. An online version of EAAA could be delivered to any campus by a centralized pool of peer facilitators who are hired, trained, and supervised by a central non-profit organization. This implementation model would cut the costs to universities of delivering EAAA by an estimated 30% and remove nearly all of the logistical barriers for the university to implement the intervention. Because the university would not need to hire, train, and retain its own Campus Trainer, costs also become stable and predictable over time. Universities would be responsible only for recruiting students to take the program and providing students with space on campus if their living space is too small. On the participant end, the convenience and accessibility of joining the intervention from their dorm room might increase willingness to participate.

Further, an Internet-delivered EAAA (IDEA3) may be likely to succeed at preventing victimization where other online interventions have failed for three reasons: (1) because IDEA3 would be an adaptation of a theory-based intervention already proven to be effective for reducing victimization [7, 23], (2) IDEA3 involves interactive learning from and with peers and peer facilitators, rather than reading or watching online click-through modules, and interactivity has been shown to lead to better learning outcomes in online settings [24, 25], and (3) the longer 12-hour program allows for meaningful knowledge and skill development and attitude change [26]. Live facilitation also means that survivors have access to support and tailored, warm resource referral in a way that a click-through module cannot provide beyond offering a generic list of resources at the end of the training.

Beyond increased cost-effectiveness and decreased implementation complexity, an online version of EAAA has other potential benefits over an in-person version. Unlike EAAA, some students could take the intervention online before arriving on campus as long as they were able to meet up with one other student in person, addressing the issue that the highest-risk period for sexual assault in the U.S. is within the first six weeks on campus, though risk remains elevated for the first year [27, 28]. Online facilitation allows for centralization of peer facilitators, enhancing fidelity to the intervention at small institutions where facilitators may only offer 1 or 2 sessions per year or where supervision of facilitators is inconsistent, which should improve efficacy of the intervention across sites [29]. Further, since the advent of COVID-19, students became accustomed to virtual meetings and may find this delivery format more appealing than having to travel to an in-person site. Young people are used to building connections with peers and seeking out health and sexuality information online, making online delivery a natural fit for the target population [30]. Finally, this delivery format, if efficacious, would provide opportunities for adaptations for subgroups of students, particularly trans students who are a relatively small proportion of the student body but are at elevated risk [31, 32]. An online format to permit students at one university to attend sessions with students from other universities would make adaptation and the resulting specialized prevention delivery economically and practically feasible.

The aim of the current study was to adapt the existing EAAA intervention for online delivery to groups of students by live facilitators using a systematic adaptation process called Assessment, Decision, Adaptation, Production, Topical experts-Integration, Training, and Testing (ADAPT-ITT) [33]. The primary outcomes were to understand feasibility and acceptability of the online adaptation in order to support and inform a large-scale efficacy trial of the intervention following this study. As an exploratory aim, we also assessed the potential efficacy of the adapted intervention by measuring pre- to post-test changes in six intermediary outcomes that together accounted for 95% of EAAA’s effect on reducing victimization [34].

Methods

Overall study design: the ADAPT-ITT intervention adaptation and testing process

We followed the ADAPT-ITT framework, which uses an eight-step process to systematically modify an existing evidence-based intervention to be effective with a new delivery mode [33], and has been used previously to adapt violence-focused interventions (See Fig. 1) [35, 36].

IDEA3 pilot study design. FG=Focus Group

The first two preparatory steps of the framework (“Assessment” and “Decision”) guide selection of the intervention to be adapted for the population and identify any unique considerations for the new population or modality. This was accomplished through formative work interviewing individuals from the target population and conducting consultations with experts in online learning and self-defense teaching. In step 3 (“Adaptation”), considerations identified in the formative work were used by the first and senior authors to create an initial adapted intervention manual. The goal of this step was to make the minimum necessary adaptations needed for moving the intervention from in-person to online (e.g., in EAAA, a facilitator holds a strike pad for participants to practice some physical self-defense strategies; in IDEA3, where facilitators are remote, partners hold a yoga mat for one another). However, any major adaptations, such as replacing activities entirely, are reserved for after testing of this initial version has been completed and investigators are able to see how well the minimally adapted intervention performed. This ensures that changes to the intervention are made systematically and are only as extensive as necessary, as revealed by actual testing with participants. We tested this initial online version of the intervention using a methodology called “theater testing” with one group of participants. The “theater” of theater testing indicates that a group of experts views a run-through of the program (“Production”) as an audience in order to provide feedback on what they observe (“Topical Experts”). We also had intervention participants provide feedback via post-session surveys and a focus group on their experiences. Feedback from participants and experts was then used to make a revised manual (“Integration of feedback”) and retrain facilitators in the updated intervention (“Training”). Finally, we conducted a pilot test (“Testing”) with four groups of participants, who were also given the opportunity to provide feedback via post-session surveys and focus groups. We iteratively incorporated feedback from each group to revise the manuals to optimize the activities between groups, with a final round of revisions at the end of all four groups to prepare the program for future efficacy testing in an RCT. All study procedures were approved by the University of Michigan Health Sciences and Behavioral Sciences Institutional Review Board and registered on clinicaltrials.gov (NCT04797741) before the trial commenced. Detailed methods for the three major phases of the study (formative work, theater test, and pilot test) are provided below.

Phase 1: formative work

Consultations

We consulted the feminist self-defense expert from Wen-Do Women’s Self Defence (http://www.wendo.ca), who helped shape the Act unit of EAAA, about how to safely and effectively teach self-defense through videoconferencing. When we started the adaptation process in 2019, she was somewhat skeptical of whether it would be possible for facilitators to ensure participant safety when teaching them remotely, but she gained experience teaching online herself during the COVID-19 pandemic and adapted and tested safety processes and systems. We also consulted several experts in online learning, who provided advice on software options for activities. We decided that a pre-intervention interview with a facilitator would be necessary to establish ground rules (e.g., cameras must remain on during the sessions to make sure participants are safe and that they are participating from a private place where no outsiders can observe or overhear the group) and ensure that women had a private and large enough space to participate safely.

Finally, we conducted formative in-depth interviews via Zoom with first-year women students (n = 18) across the US and Canada to ask about the acceptability of and preferences for online sexual assault prevention programs (see Supplementary Material for in-depth interview guide). Questions were included about who they would pick as their partner or if they would want to be matched with a stranger, any challenges they foresaw to being in their own space while participating in the intervention, and ground rules they would want to have in place in the online group in order to feel safe and comfortable discussing sexual assault via Zoom.

Participants were recruited through Facebook and purposively sampled for diversity in race and in type of institution (private/public, four-year/two-year). We recruited until we reached saturation (i.e., no new themes, viewpoints, or substantive feedback were raised in the last several interviews).

Formative study demographics and findings

The median age of the 18 participants was 19 (25th quartile: 18, 75th quartile: 19). All participants were assigned female at birth and identified as women. Twelve participants attended school in the US, and six were attending in Canada. Just over half of the participants were Asian, Asian American, or Pacific Islander (n = 8), followed by White (n = 7), Chicanx, Hispanic, or Latino (n = 2), and Black or African American (n = 1). Two-thirds of the participants were heterosexual or straight, three were bisexual, two were asexual or aromantic, and one was queer. Most (n = 15) were sophomores, and three were freshmen, with seventeen attending four-year public institutions and one attending a two-year college. Two participants identified as disabled, having a disability or impairment, medical condition, chronic pain, or chronic illness. Half of the participants had previously received sexual assault prevention training, and half had not.

These formative interviews confirmed the feasibility of an online adaptation, with women indicating willingness to participate in an online training about the topic and speak to a facilitator in a one-on-one interview beforehand. Most indicated they had a space that would be large and secure enough for the self-defense portion of the training, or that they would be willing to go to a friend’s place for the intervention. All participants said that they had or could borrow a yoga mat for the self-defense practice. Participants were split between wanting to do the intervention with a friend and wanting to be paired with someone new, indicating the importance of offering both options. They were also split between wanting weekday evening times and weekend afternoon times, once again indicating the importance of offering both options to maximize recruitment.

There were concerns that the context of an online Zoom meeting would make discussing sexual assault “awkward,” and participants asked for strong ground rules around confidentiality and taking turns. Challenges anticipated were fitting a 12-hour training into their schedule and “Zoom fatigue” for 3–6 h trainings. Many of the other anticipated concerns and suggestions were already addressed by the existing nature of EAAA, such as building in breaks and incorporating interactive activities to promote attention, and saving the self-defense portion of the intervention for after the participants could get to know their partner. Saturation was reached after 18 interviews. No significant concerns were raised about the feasibility of adapting the program for online delivery.

Intervention design and initial manual adaptation

The formative phase confirmed the potential feasibility of the major proposed adaptations: (1) the intervention is still delivered synchronously by two peer facilitators, but through videoconferencing software Zoom Pro; (2) women videoconference into the intervention group in pairs, rather than as individuals as in the in-person intervention, combining the advantages of having in-person interaction with a peer (comraderie, in-person emotional support, ability to practice self-defense with a partner) and the advantages of online delivery; and (3) women attend a brief one-on-one interview with a facilitator before the first session to fully consent them into the study and check for safety and logistical concerns with full participation in the program (e.g., do they have a private space in which they feel safe and comfortable meeting with their partner and conducting the study activities). We also decided, based on the formative interviews with students, that the four units would always be offered on different days, that is, two units would not be combined on a single weekend day, as is frequently done for the in-person version of the program. This limited sessions to three hours rather than six hours to reduce “Zoom fatigue.”

Phases 2 and 3: theater test and pilot trial

Facilitators

We hired existing EAAA facilitators who had facilitated in-person groups prior to the COVID-19 pandemic, but who had not led groups for at least one year, to become IDEA3 facilitators. Before the theater test, facilitators went through 24 hours of training led by the first and senior author, including significant training and practice on facilitating in the online environment. This was followed by 13 hours spent doing a dress rehearsal of the full 12-hour intervention with feedback.

Participants

Eligible participants for the theater test and pilot trial had to identify as a woman, or as a nonbinary person who answered yes to the question “This workshop is designed for women, including cis and trans women. It uses language like ‘empowering women’ and uses she/her pronouns. Is this language you would identify and feel comfortable with?” Eligible participants also had to be a first-year or second-year undergraduate student, be 18–24 years old, be available for the program times, and consent to being observed and recorded while participating. Participants were recruited at a single large public Midwestern university, primarily via department listservs and targeted messages to freshman women from the University registrar. Information was also posted to Facebook groups for university students and a searchable registry of health-related studies maintained by the university.

Baseline demographics are presented for the theater test and pilot test participants together. Seventy-three participants were enrolled and 61 completed the study (Table 1 presents demographics for both groups). Most participants identified as women, with the remaining 5% identifying as non-binary. Most participants were in their first year. There was considerable diversity in the sample; roughly two-thirds were women of color and just over half identified as heterosexual and nearly a quarter as bisexual. A sizeable minority (roughly 1 in 6) of participants identified as disabled, having a disability or impairment, medical condition, chronic pain, or chronic illness. Most participants reported having been in a romantic or sexual relationship with someone in the past, and three-quarters reported having been in a sexual relationship with a man. The lifetime number of sexual partners was low (mode 0; median 1; range 0 to 11). More than one-third of participants had previously experienced some form of sexual assault. Close to one-third of participants have taken a self-defense class in the past, but few had previously taken a sexual assault prevention intervention.

Baseline participant demographics

Theater test qualitative feedback collection

Three focus groups and an in-depth interview, all led by the first author, were held to collect feedback on the draft intervention. Focus group 1 (FG1) was with the participants who were in the theater test. Focus group 2 (FG2) was with the investigatory team and outside experts, following theater test methodology. While typically investigators and experts watch the theater test in person, given the ease and unobtrusiveness of recording the Zoom sessions, we recorded the sessions to have investigators and experts watch the intervention later. Theater test recordings were viewed by members of the study team heavily involved in developing the revised manual (authors 1, 2, and 3), an investigator uninvolved in developing the revised manual and also less familiar with EAAA (author 4), and an outside expert who trains Campus Trainers for the in-person version of EAAA. These individuals convened for another focus group to discuss their perceptions of and feedback on the intervention. Finally, a focus group (FG3) was conducted with the two facilitators to collect their perceptions of how the theater test had gone. Focus group guides focused on eliciting areas of consensus and disagreement about the effectiveness of each module of the intervention and areas of innovation for how the intervention might be adapted or improved for the online space. For the participant FG, we also asked how participants may have already used the information. To collect further expert feedback, the self-defense module was also viewed by our self-defense consultant. She was interviewed by the first and second authors to provide feedback separately.

Theater test and pilot trial: quantitative feedback collection

Following each intervention session in both the theater test and pilot trial, participants completed a brief survey on Qualtrics with closed- and open-ended questions on acceptability, including dimensions of affective attitude (how the individual feels about the intervention), burden (perceived effort required to participate), and whether they would recommend the intervention to a friend. Because the pedagogical theories [37, 38] underpinning the design of the intervention suggests that women need to respect and identify with the near-peer facilitators, questions were also asked about their perceptions of the facilitators. Participants were asked to complete this survey immediately after each module in order to minimize recall bias.

Post-theater test manual revisions and facilitator retraining

Following the theater test, further revisions were made to the intervention manual based on feedback from the post-session surveys, focus groups, and in-depth interview. This included further training for facilitators on teaching self-defense virtually (e.g., better use of camera angles to allow participants to more clearly see the self-defense moves, using more verbal prompts to keep participants on track with their practice), as well as tweaking the script in places (e.g., participants shared that role-play activities felt particularly awkward to do over Zoom and thus the instructions for the activities were revised). Facilitators went through an additional 24 h of retraining (in addition to standard 2-day refresher self-defense training offered to in-person EAAA facilitators annually) to address the new intervention material and correct fidelity issues.

Pilot test procedures

A pilot trial of the fully adapted intervention was conducted with four groups of 16 women (n = 64). Groups 1 and 2 were run concurrently. Following this first wave, 10 participants were selected from Group 1 and 10 participants were selected from Group 2 at random to participate in two separate focus groups to elicit feedback. FG1 was led by the first author and FG2 was led by the second author. As with the theater test, participants filled out post-session evaluation surveys. Findings from the focus groups, evaluation surveys, and investigators reviewing the recordings were then incorporated into another round of revisions of the manual. After intervention groups 3 and 4 were conducted and data collection with participants was finished, the first author led a focus group with the three facilitators who had been involved in the pilot trial to collect final feedback on feasibility of the intervention and suggestions for revision. After the conclusion of the study, all of this feedback was incorporated in a final round of revisions on the manuals in preparation for a future large-scale randomized controlled trial to test efficacy of the program.

Survey administration

Participants in the theater test and pilot trial took a baseline survey before the first intervention session and a post-test survey in the week following the last intervention session to measure pre-test/post-test changes in indicators of potential program efficacy (see Measures below). All surveys in the theater test and pilot trial were administered via Qualtrics with assigned individualized ID codes to match participant surveys across time periods. Post-session surveys were also administered in Qualtrics.

Participant incentives

For the theater test, participants received $10 for filling out the pre-program survey, $15 for attending each of the four sessions and filling out the brief post-session evaluation (up to $60 total), $20 for filling out the post-test survey, and $30 for attending the focus group discussion.

For the pilot test, we used a $25 raffle for attendees at each session instead of direct incentives for attendance at each session to better mimic real-world implementation conditions, i.e., to understand attendance rates when women are not assured of getting paid for their time. Participants were entered into the raffle if they attended the session and filled out the brief post-session evaluation. Pilot trial participants received $10 for the pre-test survey and $25 for the post-test survey. For those who were invited and chose to attend a focus group, they received an additional $10.

Measures

Measures of acceptability and feasibility

Procedural indicators of feasibility included tracking data on recruitment (percent of target sample size successfully enrolled) and retention (percent of participants who withdrew from the study before and after the first session, and why). Attendance rate was calculated as the percent of participants who attended a session and filled out the post-session evaluation survey out of all participants who were still enrolled in the study for that session (i.e., taking into account participants who withdrew from the study in the denominator).

Participant-reported feasibility and acceptability measures were included in the brief post-session evaluation surveys. Participants were asked questions on a scale from 1 to 10 about enjoyment (“How much did you enjoy today’s session?”, “How much would you recommend today’s session to a friend?”) and perceived learning (“How much did you learn from today’s session?”). Perceived burden was measured with the questions “How difficult was it for you to find time to participate in today’s three-hour session?”, “How difficult was it for you and your partner to find and agree upon a space to participate in today’s session?”, “How difficult was it for you use Zoom and the online activities?”, “How difficult was the material presented to you today to follow and understand?”, and “How difficult did you find it to concentrate during today’s session?” Perceived safety of participating from their home or other mutually agreed upon location with their partner was assessed with “How comfortable and secure did you feel the space was that you completed the training in?” Feedback on the pacing of the material was solicited with the question “We have likely all been in a workshop where there is too much content, and the whole process feels rushed. On the other hand, sometimes a one-hour lecture could have easily been summed up in fifteen minutes. We’re trying to find that ‘sweet spot’, where the information is coming in at a digestible pace. How would you rank the timing/pacing of today’s session?” with answer choices “Way too slow,” “A little slow,” “About right,” “A little fast,” and “Way too fast.” Finally, acceptability of facilitators was measured with “how knowledgeable were your facilitators today?” and “How credible (believable) were your facilitators today?”.

Focus group guides with participants contained many questions on acceptability, including “What was the experience of the workshop like for you generally?, “What did you enjoy most about the experience?”, “What did you like least about the experience?”, “What emotions did you feel while undergoing the program?”, “Learning about and discussing topics related to sexual assault and sexuality can be challenging at times. Were there any challenges you experienced as you were participating?” Questions on feasibility included “What was it like getting together with a partner in person for this online workshop?” and “What were the technical challenges you encountered in participating in the workshop online, if any? Is there anything you can think of that would have made this easier?”.

Measures of potential efficacy

Surveys covered demographics, sexual assault victimization using the Sexual Experiences Survey-Short Form Version [39], and key theorized mediators of intervention efficacy (see below). We could not do a prospective analysis of victimization data in this relatively small pilot study with no longitudinal follow-up. Instead we collected efficacy-related data on the six intermediary outcomes that account for 95% of EAAA’s effect on reducing victimization [34].

A single primary mediator, self-defense self-efficacy, accounted for 45% of EAAA’s intervention effect, and therefore served as our primary efficacy-related outcome and was measured pre- and post- intervention. Self-defense self-efficacy was measured using nine questions assessing women’s confidence in their ability to defend themselves in a variety of situations on a 7-point scale (e.g., “If a man you were with was attempting to get you to have sex with him and you were not interested, how confident are you that you could successfully resist his advances?”, “How successful do you believe you would be in fighting off or otherwise stopping an attempted rape by a stranger?”). Higher scores on this adapted measure [8, 40] indicate greater self-defense self-efficacy or confidence that a woman could act in her own defense.

Post-test surveys included measures of two primary mediators that can only be assessed once (i.e., cannot also be assessed at baseline) because they require the individual to react to an unfolding vignette that they are reading for the first time: (1) earlier detection of risk in a coercive situation (7-point scale that asks participants to rate between 1 ‘not at all likely’ and 7 ‘very likely’ that a date scenario will end positively; high scores earlier detection of risk based on risk cues placed in the scenario); and (2) willingness to use evidence-based direct resistance strategies (i.e., asks participants to rate on a 7-point scale whether they are ‘not at all likely to do’ to ‘very likely to do’ various forceful verbal and physical resistance actions; high scores indicate more willingness). As we had no control group in this pilot trial to compare with on the aforementioned measures, we also included an open-ended question that could be asked at both baseline and post-test that measures knowledge and willingness to use direct resistance in another way [8]. This measure asks participants to describe how they would respond to the hypothetical, “If a man I knew (e.g., a date or acquaintance) tried to force me to have sex with when I didn’t want to, I would…”. We were able to compare changes from pre-test to post-test using this measure. Responses were coded for the mention of successful verbal and physical rape resistance strategies (0 for none, 1 for at least one strategy) and the number of forceful verbal or forceful physical strategies mentioned (frequency). Two research assistants trained in the coding procedures independently coded the responses. Coders were blinded to whether the responses were from the pre-test or the post-test to reduce ascertainment bias. Interrater agreement was 85.6% for verbal strategies and 87.0% for physical strategies. Disagreements were resolved by consensus.

Three secondary mediators, perception of personal risk of acquaintance rape (“What are your chances of being raped by someone you know?” rated on a 5-point scale) [41], rape myth acceptance (17 items adapted to 1–5 scale) [42], and belief in female precipitation of rape (6 items of a 32 item measure, 1–7 scale) [43] were also measured.

We also collected qualitative evidence of efficacy, including reports from focus groups and post-test surveys when women described using the program material they learned to resist sexual coercion. The post-test contained a question, “Have you, since you completed the sexual assault resistance program, had a situation where you believe you AVOIDED sexual coercion or sexual assault by your actions? (e.g., removing yourself from the situation, calling a friend, etc.) with a request for a description of the situation if the answer was yes [44]. The focus group guide had a question, “Have you used any of the information or skills you learned in the workshop yourself or shared anything with others?” and “How, if at all, has the workshop changed how you will approach your time in college?” Additional detail on survey measures or focus group guides is available upon request to the authorship team.

Data analysis

Acceptability and feasibility analyses use data from all participants who provided at least one session evaluation (n = 73). Efficacy analyses used data only from participants who completed the post-test (n = 61). Model-wise deletion was used to handle missing data.

Acceptability

Data from quantitative questions on the post-session surveys were analyzed via simple summary statistics (proportions, means), and open-ended responses were analyzed using pragmatic content analysis. Focus group recordings were listened to repeatedly and analyzed using Framework Analysis, a structured analysis process for qualitative data that works well to generate recommendations for a program in a limited time period, given targeted research questions [45]. We identified exemplar quotes for each of the broad categories that emerged around feasibility (e.g., participant comments about how much they enjoyed the program, logistics of finding time and space to participate), and efficacy (participants describing their use of information learned in the program).

Feasibility

Speed of recruitment, no-show at first session, session attendance, and retention rates were quantified. Intervention session recordings were viewed by investigators to assess fidelity when timing of a session went under or over expected timing or when facilitator supervision meetings revealed changes/alterations. Deviations from the manual were discussed with facilitators to understand why a deviation occurred and if it was due to a fundamental issue with the intervention or an issue with training.

Efficacy indicators

T-tests were used to examine pre-test/post-test changes in efficacy-related outcomes collected at baseline and post-test. Summary statistics were used to describe outcomes that were only collected at the post-test. These findings were qualitatively compared to historical data from the in-person EAAA efficacy trial to understand if similar levels of each construct were observed.

Power calculations

The primary endpoints of this trial were feasibility and acceptability, with the aim of having enough groups to iteratively revise the manuals into a final version for a future large-scale randomized controlled efficacy trial. However, we also wanted to collect preliminary data on potential efficacy of the program, based on our primary efficacy-related outcome of self-defense self-efficacy. In our historical trial data, on a scale from 9 to 63, self-defense self-efficacy increased an average of 9.6 points from pre-test to post-test among EAAA participants, compared to 2.2 points in the control group (see Fig. 2). Based on this, the trial was designed to be able to detect a mean pre-test/post-test increase in self-defense self-efficacy of IDEA3 participants of > 5 points. We made the conservative assumption that IDEA3 might not be quite as effective as EAAA the first time it was tested in this pilot, and powered the study such that if IDEA3 achieved at least 80% of the mean pre-test/post-test increase in self-defense self-efficacy observed in the EAAA group (i.e., a mean increase of 7.7 or more), a sample size of at least 60 would make us 95% confident that the true mean increase in self-defense self-efficacy is greater than 5 points (i.e., lower bound of the confidence interval of the estimated mean increase is greater than 5) with 80% power. We ultimately enrolled 73 individuals out of a target enrollment of 80, of whom 61 completed the study.

Results

Feasibility and acceptability

Recruitment and retention

Seventy six students completed the consent process and consented to participate. Six of the 76 students (7.9%) dropped out before the first session. Reasons given included participants citing their wellbeing or emotional health, which meant they did not feel well enough to participate (n = 2), and not wanting to stay enrolled if their partner for the intervention was dropping out (n = 2). Four of the six students who dropped out were enrolled in the last group of the pilot trial, which took place closer to the end of the semester when exams were approaching and time conflicts and stress may have been heightened.

Ultimately, we obtained 66% of our target enrollment for our theater test (n = 8 out of 12) and enrolled 97% (n = 62 out of 64) for our pilot test and had them show up at the first session.

The theater test took place over the summer when most freshmen were no longer on campus, making recruitment difficult. We developed new recruitment strategies (e.g., partnership with the registrar’s office) for the pilot test.

Of the remaining 70 participants, 6 dropped out after Session 1 (8.6%). The reasons given included changes to participants’ schedules (n = 3), the sense that the material brought up too many unpleasant memories of a prior assault (n = 2), and the time commitment required to attend the sessions (n = 1). The two participants who found the course material emotionally upsetting were provided with in-the-moment support, as well as contacted the following day to assess their emotional state and provide referrals to resources for survivors. Participants were not in active distress but felt that the program was not for them at that time.

Attendance to intervention sessions was high, with 240 out of 262 sessions attended and evaluation surveys were completed for all of these sessions (91.6%). Poor attendance was highest in group 1, where 6 people no-showed for unit 3 and 9 people no-showed for unit 4 out of 16 participants. There was a major snowstorm that prevented the facilitators from being able to hold unit 3 on the originally scheduled date, and we were forced to reschedule units 3 and 4, which caused attendance to drop off.

Quantitative indicators of acceptability

Intervention sessions received high average scores in short post-session evaluation surveys (see ranges, means, and standard deviations in Table 2). On a scale from 1 to 10, questions asking about enjoyment of the session, willingness to recommend to a friend, and how much was learned scored between 8 and 9. Perceived burden in terms of finding a space to use with your partner and difficulty of using the videoconferencing technology was low (M = 1.8-2.0), participants felt safe in the space they had selected (M = 8.4), and the material was considered easy to understand (M = 1.7). About two-thirds of responses said that the pacing of the program was correct, and about one-third said the program was a little slow. There was a moderate amount of difficulty in finding time to participate (M = 3.4) and in concentrating the entire session (M = 3.4). Facilitators received high ratings as being knowledgeable and believable (M = 9.2).

Having an in-person partner to do the workshops with was perceived as integral to fostering camaraderie and making participants feel more comfortable. A common thread was that participants especially enjoyed activities with an interactive element where connections could be made with other group members. Participants reported that, generally, the program did not present many logistical issues in terms of finding space or time to get together with a partner.

Some concerns were raised about being overheard yelling during the self-defense portion, especially when students were participating in their families’ homes or study rooms. While a number of participants felt that elements of the workshops were emotionally challenging, they also described feeling well-supported by facilitators. A number of participants who took the program on weekday evenings reported that they would have preferred to complete the workshops on weekend mornings or afternoons as the sessions left them little time to eat dinner or complete schoolwork. Many stated that three-hour sessions were challenging to fit into their schedules and that it was hard to stay focused for the entire time, particularly on weekday evening sessions when there was little time for dinner or homework.

Fidelity

Particularly in the earlier groups, facilitators had some difficulties completing all activities in the allotted time and had to skip some activities. In some cases, they were able to incorporate the missed material into later sessions. With more experience and with revisions to the manuals, fidelity increased.

Feedback from intervention facilitators

Focus groups with facilitators after the theater test and the pilot test indicated that they thought the intervention was overall effective at achieving its goals, and comparable to their experience with the in-person intervention. Key challenges perceived by facilitators for implementation were that they found it much more stressful to deliver the intervention online than in-person because of the cognitive load created by trying to manage the technology (e.g., creating breakout rooms, setting up apps, sharing links, messaging participants) while simultaneously delivering the usual content of the intervention. While they did competently deliver the intervention with moderately high fidelity, they said they would have struggled to commit to a longer trial given the level of stress they experienced in delivering the intervention. Other key challenges noted were (1) enforcing ground rules given to participants, particularly no cell phone use during the sessions in order to maintain rapport and privacy for all women, and (2) getting all women to participate in small-group breakout activities. They used strategies such as reminding the entire group that everyone should put their cell phones away or should be participating, and directly messaged participants. Participants would often agree but then quickly go back to their previous behavior.

In facilitators’ and investigators’ experience of in-person EAAA delivery, in-person participants have a harder time ignoring the facilitator in the room and there is a greater sense of wanting to do what other people were doing. In the Zoom setting, participants appeared to revert to typical behavior for Zoom classrooms, such as periodic cell phone usage.

Preliminary indicators of efficacy

The IDEA3 program demonstrated preliminary efficacy on key intermediary outcomes that research shows drive reductions in sexual assault. For two mediators (risk perception and willingness to use direct effective resistance), we only had data at a single time point, which in the original RCT of in-person EAAA were compared to a control group. Table 4 shows that mean scores on these measures were comparable to mean scores in the intervention group of the original RCT, with participants indicating a high level of ability to detect risk and to use direct resistance strategies that escalated as the coercion increased in the hypothetical acquaintance dating scenario.

We also assessed changes in participants’ knowledge and willingness to use effective resistance strategies by coding an open-ended question requiring self-generation of strategies. All pre- to post- changes in use of forceful verbal and physical strategies were similar to the original in-person RCT and in the expected direction, though only the increases in number of forceful verbal strategies met the threshold for significance.

There was also data to suggest that people were actively using what they had learned in the program in real life, with one in ten participants reporting on the post-test that they had successfully avoided coercion or assault in the approximately one week that had elapsed since taking the program, with an additional 7% saying they were unsure but may have. Survey and focus group participants described situations in which they had employed key core learning objectives of IDEA3. Broad categories of feedback regarding participant use of program content in real life included: (1) overcoming emotional barriers to recognizing and responding to danger cues, (2) use of forceful verbal self-defense, (3) self-defense self-efficacy, (4) use in bystander or culture shift applications, (5) reductions in women-blaming, (6) increased sexual agency (see Table 3 for exemplar quotes).

Discussion

This one-arm intervention pilot study took an existing evidence-based intervention shown to robustly reduce sexual assault victimization in college women and systematically adapted it for an innovative hybrid online/in-person format, wherein participants have a partner with them in person, but the pairs and the facilitators join together via Zoom. We found high levels of feasibility and acceptability, particularly considering the intervention asked participants to participate in a series of three-hour Zoom meetings on their nights or weekends. Despite some issues with fidelity in the earlier programs as facilitators gained confidence delivering the online intervention, all intermediary outcomes shown to mediate the effect of the intervention on sexual victimization in the in-person EAAA trial showed significant pre-test/post-test differences in the correct direction, including the key mediator of self-defense self-efficacy. This is a positive signal for the potential efficacy of the intervention, which is being systematically studied in a multi-site randomized controlled trial (trial began enrollment in Fall 2023; NCT06058455).

It is notable that in the very brief follow-up period (less than two weeks), more than one in ten individuals said they had used or may have used something they learned in the program during this time to successfully avoid sexual coercion or assault. This speaks to the reality that freshmen and sophomore women were frequently in situations where they perceived risk of sexual coercion, and that they were able to immediately apply what was learned in the intervention to these situations. Feedback from the focus groups also indicated broader dividends beyond individual risk reduction, such as sharing the information with friends and being empowered to challenge sexist comments they had heard. The in-person EAAA program has similar benefits evident across two years of follow-up [46]. Challenging sexist comments and rape culture is an objective in many bystander programs. While bystander intervention is not a specific focus of this program, the skills practiced in IDEA3 around unpacking gender socialization that teaches women to “be nice” or not “make a scene,” overcoming emotional barriers to action, and feeling empowered to resist coercion may also translate into improved positive bystander action [46, 47]. Future research should empirically measure the degree to which sexual assault resistance programs may also increase women’s bystander behavior.

Despite seeing significant changes in key mediators of the intervention’s efficacy to reduce sexual victimization, we did not observe significant pre-test/post-test increases in the percentage of participants who named any forceful verbal and physical resistance strategies when asked how they might respond to sexual coercion. We also did not observe a significant increase in the number of forceful physical strategies mentioned by participants on their post-test compared to their pre-test, though we did observe a significant increase in the number of forceful verbal resistance strategies they mentioned. This could reflect the fact that the Act unit in which participants learn self-defense strategies had the most prominent fidelity issues in the pilot. In at least two of the five groups across the theater test and pilot trial, facilitators ran out of time to finish the content. While they taught and practiced the verbal and physical self-defense strategies, they ran out of time to do the culminating activities at the end of the unit designed to promote recall and creative application of individual resistance strategies to specific scenarios (e.g., describing an assault scenario and asking participants to brainstorm multiple specific strategies for responding). Practice of the self-defense strategies tends to take more time in the online format, as it takes more time for facilitators to ensure their camera angle allows participants to see their demonstration clearly, and it can take more time to call the group back from their individualized practice for safety or to move on to the next move. Application of new skills to different scenarios and encouragement of novel strategies beyond those taught was critical to extending benefits beyond a few months (to two years) as demonstrated in early EAAA development research [8, 12]. Therefore we revised this unit after the pilot trial, in careful consultation with our self-defense expert, to reduce the variations in some physical self-defense moves taught to accommodate the greater time needed for teaching and practicing each move (decrease from 16 unique strategies to 13 strategies). We selected 3 strategies for removal that were variations on other strategies already taught (e.g., elbow strike to the back verus the side, double wrist release versus single wrist release). This has resolved issues with timing in this unit without substantially reducing the types of strategies available to participants. This change may in improve participant recall of the moves they have learned, and hence, efficacy.

Based on this pilot trial, we conducted another round of revisions before finalizing the intervention for efficacy testing in a randomized controlled trial. Based on feedback from facilitators, we introduced a “technology facilitator” to handle technology-related logistics such as setting up breakout rooms and apps, so the facilitators can focus on leading discussions and activities and connecting with participants. This change was designed to improve fidelity to the intervention and the quality of facilitation by reducing the cognitive burden on facilitators. We also reviewed the activity that two participants reported felt triggering to them and analyzed how the online environment may change the feeling of emotional safety during activities. This activity is at the beginning of the second session and has been used effectively with many hundreds of young women in person. While the other participants still responded well to the activity, which has an important role in identifying psychological barriers to resistance, we reshaped the framing of the activity and unit to better prepare participants for what to expect and provide additional support. Finally, we trimmed verbiage from the script to decrease the perception by some participants that the pace was too slow and to reduce time pressure on facilitators, but do not plan to substantively change or drop activities given the overall positive results on feasibility, acceptability, and potential efficacy.

Limitations of the study include that the pilot was carried out only at a single site, which was a large, public university in the American Midwest. Feasibility and acceptability of the intervention may be lower at postsecondary settings that differ, such as two-year colleges or institutions with a large remote or commuter population. This was a single-arm pilot focused on adapting the intervention and testing its feasibility and acceptability, and thus, there was no control group for comparison. A strength of the study is that the sample was notably more diverse than the student body, with only about a third of participants identifying as white, and there was no differential retention of participants of color versus white participants in the study.

However, given the relatively small overall sample size there were only 9 Black or Latinx students who completed the study (plus 7 additional multiracial participants who did not disclose their specific background, but who may have had Black or Latinx heritage). Future efficacy testing should continue to collect acceptability data from participants and examine findings from an intersectional perspective to ensure broad acceptability across diverse undergraduate women.

Developing a synchronous group intervention delivered by facilitators through teleconferencing technologies is a relatively novel approach that is ripe for exploration in the current climate of increased remote and hybrid learning. Since COVID-19 necessitated a period of distance learning, universities have almost universally purchased licenses for Zoom or other platforms, and students are now accustomed to receiving trainings via these platforms, making delivery of IDEA3 through this technology more accessible than ever. The hybrid component of having an in-person partner helps address students’ desire for in-person interaction, as well as the flexibility of remote attendance. Should it prove to be as effective as the in-person version, IDEA3 has the potential to be scaled up and reduce sexual assault among undergraduate women nationally by half using a cost-effective medium. Our adaptation may also serve as a blueprint for other online, synchronous, high-touch health interventions.

Finally, it should be noted that the online modality enables feasible adaptation of EAAA for minority student populations who are at high risk for campus sexual assault, but whose size is too small on an individual campus to support the implementation of an in-person intervention. [48] For instance, colleges who struggle to support in-person EAAA implementation for women, who make up some 50% of the student body, may find it even more difficult to fund a campus trainer and recruit near-peer facilitators to implement a version for transgender students, who make up some 1.5% to 6.6% of the population. [1, 49, 50] IDEA3’s online format makes it possible for a non-profit to train a centralized pool of trans facilitators who can lead groups of trans students who join virtually from multiple campuses. Each institution would only have to pay the per-person cost for as few as two students joining the program. The online format, therefore, makes these adaptations for trans students and other high-risk minority groups such as sexual minority men dramatically more feasible. Removing universities from the implementer role also makes it possible to adapt IDEA3 in the future for non-college populations who may be at high risk of assault.

Conclusions

The results of this pilot trial represent a proof-of-concept for an acceptable, feasible, and potentially efficacious adaptation of an existing evidence-based intervention for hybrid online/in-person delivery, which may reduce sexual violence at scale for undergraduate women and potentially other populations in the future.
