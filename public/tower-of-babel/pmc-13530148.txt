Polyphenols intake and expression of insulin signaling genes in adipose tissue of obese adults: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Farinaz Hosseini Balam, Faraneh Zolfaghari, Farshad Teymoori, Maryam Mousavi, Golaleh Asghari, Emad Yuzbashian, Alireza Khalaj, Mehdi Hedayati, Maryam Zarkesh, Atoosa Saidpour
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Evidence suggests that polyphenol-rich diets enhance insulin sensitivity; however, their molecular mechanisms in human adipose tissue remain incompletely understood. We investigated whether habitual intake of total polyphenols and their major subclasses is associated with the expression of PI3K, Akt, and PTEN, key components of the PI3K/Akt insulin-signaling pathway, in visceral and subcutaneous adipose tissue (VAT and SAT) of adults with obesity, and how these associations relate to markers of insulin sensitivity. In this cross-sectional study, 135 adults with obesity (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2), aged ≥ 18 years, undergoing elective abdominal surgery, provided paired VAT and SAT samples. Dietary polyphenol intake was estimated using a validated food-frequency questionnaire (FFQ), and gene expression was quantified by RT-qPCR. Fasting insulin concentrations were measured, and insulin resistance was estimated using the HOMA-IR index. Higher total polyphenol intake was associated with lower expression of PI3K, Akt, and PTEN, with depot- and subclass-specific patterns. Total polyphenols and stilbenes were inversely related to Akt expression in both VAT and SAT, while flavonoids, particularly anthocyanins and flavanones, showed additional downregulation in SAT. PI3K expression decreased in both depots with higher polyphenol intake, driven primarily by stilbenes in VAT and by flavonoids, anthocyanins, and flavanones in SAT. PTEN expression was reduced in SAT among participants with higher total polyphenol and stilbene intakes, and in VAT with greater hydroxycinnamic acid intake. In adults with obesity, higher dietary polyphenol intake was associated with altered expression of key genes involved in the PI3K/Akt insulin-signaling pathway in adipose tissue. These associations appeared to be depot-specific and varied across polyphenol subclasses, suggesting potential interactions between dietary polyphenols and adipose tissue insulin-signaling-related gene expression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-62587-z.

Introduction

Adipose tissue is a metabolically active organ integral to energy homeostasis, insulin signaling, and inflammation1. White adipose tissue (WAT) and brown adipose tissue (BAT) represent the two principal forms of adipose tissue, differing in morphology and physiological roles. WAT, primarily responsible for energy storage, is further categorized into subcutaneous and visceral adipose tissues (SAT and VAT) based on anatomical location and metabolic activity1. A chronic imbalance between energy intake and expenditure results in excessive fat accumulation within adipose tissue, leading to overweight and obesity. Obesity is a complex, multifactorial disease associated with various non-communicable conditions, including coronary heart disease, hypertension, stroke, certain cancers, type 2 diabetes, and metabolic syndrome2. The pathological expansion of adipose tissue induces the secretion of proinflammatory cytokines, resulting in chronic low-grade inflammation, impaired insulin signaling, and metabolic dysfunction3. Lifestyle and behavioral strategies, including increased physical activity and the consumption of natural bioactive food compounds, can enhance β-cell function and improve insulin sensitivity in adipose tissue, liver, and skeletal muscle4. Numerous studies have indicated that dietary modifications can regulate the expression of genes involved in inflammation and insulin resistance5,6.

Polyphenols, a diverse group of bioactive and water-soluble secondary metabolites found in plant foods, are structurally divided into four primary categories: flavonoids, phenolic acids, stilbenes, and lignans7. Polyphenols have been extensively studied for their diverse bioactive properties, including antioxidant8, anti-inflammatory9, and anticancer effects10,11. Epidemiological and clinical studies suggest that polyphenol-rich dietary patterns, such as the Mediterranean diet12, are associated with improved metabolic health, reduced insulin resistance, and enhanced resolution of inflammation in individuals with obesity. Resveratrol, a polyphenolic stilbenoid, decreases oxidative stress and, consequently, insulin resistance13.

At the molecular level, polyphenols have been implicated in the modulation of key signaling pathways involved in metabolism and inflammation14. The Phosphatidylinositol-3-kinase (PI3K/Akt) pathway is a central regulator of insulin signaling, cellular metabolism, and adipocyte function15. PTEN (phosphatase and tensin homologue) functions as a tumor suppressor and negative regulator of the PI3K/Akt pathway, contributing to metabolic homeostasis by influencing insulin sensitivity and lipid metabolism. Disruption of the PI3K/Akt/PTEN axis is linked to obesity, insulin resistance, and metabolic syndrome15,16.

Studies have demonstrated that polyphenols such as resveratrol and quercetin can influence this pathway, enhancing insulin sensitivity and mitigating inflammation13,17. Specifically, flavonoids have been shown to activate PI3K/Akt signaling, regulate oxidative stress responses, and improve adipocyte function18. Moreover, gene-lifestyle interactions suggest that dietary polyphenols can regulate the expression of metabolic genes, thereby influencing adipose tissue function and systemic metabolic health5.

Given the critical role of polyphenols in targeting metabolic pathways, the present study aimed to investigate their association with PI3K/Akt/PTEN signaling in VAT and SAT. Understanding the molecular interactions between dietary polyphenols and key metabolic regulators may provide insight into novel dietary strategies for managing obesity-related metabolic disorders.

Materials and methods

This cross-sectional study was conducted among adults with obesity (body max index (BMI) ≥ 30 kg/m2) aged 18–80 years who were undergoing elective, non-emergency abdominal surgeries, including umbilical and inguinal hernia repairs, at Mostafa Khomeini and Khatam Al-Anbia Hospitals in Tehran, Iran. We enrolled all participants who had been hospitalized for fewer than three days. Individuals diagnosed with chronic illnesses, such as diabetes or cancer, were excluded from the study. Furthermore, we excluded those who were using lipid-lowering, anti-obesity, or anti-diabetic medications, following any special diets for at least three months, as well as women who were pregnant or lactating.

All participants provided informed consent before enrolling in the study. During elective abdominal surgery, approximately 50–100 mg of subcutaneous and visceral adipose tissue was collected by an experienced general surgeon. Subcutaneous adipose tissue samples were obtained from the periumbilical abdominal region (approximately 5 cm from the umbilicus), and visceral adipose tissue samples were collected from the upper left portion of the omentum (subomental region). Tissue samples were rinsed with sterile saline, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C until further analysis. The research was conducted in accordance with the ethical guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki and our institutional guidelines. Additionally, the study received approval from the ethics committee of the Research Institute for Endocrine Sciences (RIES) at Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences.

Anthropometric and physical activity assessments

Body weight of participants was measured in light clothing using a SECA digital weighing scale (Seca 707; Seca Corporation, Hanover, Maryland) with a precision of 100 g. Height was measured without shoes in a standing position using a fixed tape to the nearest 0.1 cm. Body Mass Index (BMI) was calculated by dividing weight (in kilograms) by the square of height (in meters). To assess physical activity over the past seven days, we utilized the Persian version of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) short form. All measurements were expressed in terms of metabolic equivalent tasks (MET-min/week)19.

Laboratory measurement

Before the surgery, venous blood samples (5 ml) were collected after an overnight fast of 10–12 h. Serum glucose levels were measured using the Enzymatic-Colorimetric method (GOD-PAP; Glucose Oxidase-Phenol), which has a coefficient of variation of 1.0%. Enzymatic methods were also used to measure serum levels of triglycerides, total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein (HDL), were also assessed using enzymatic methods. Fasting serum insulin concentrations (µU/mL) were measured using a ELISA method with commercial kits from Pars Azmoon Co. (Tehran, Iran). The assay sensitivity was [X µU/mL], and he inter-assay and intra-assay coefficients of variation for these tests were less than 0.1% and 0.4%, respectively. Insulin resistance was estimated using the homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR), calculated as HOMA-IR = [fasting insulin (µU/mL) × fasting glucose (mmol/L)]/22.5].

Dietary measurement

Trained interviewers conducted face-to-face assessments of individuals’ usual dietary intake using a valid and reliable 147-item semi-quantitative food frequency questionnaire (FFQ)20,21. Participants were asked about the frequency of their food consumption over the year prior to surgery to estimate portion sizes during the preoperative valuation period to minimize recall bias and patient burden. They reported their intake in terms of daily, weekly, or monthly frequencies, which were then converted to grams. The daily energy and nutrient intake were calculated using the United States Department of Agriculture’s (USDA) Food Composition Table (FCT).

Estimation of polyphenol intake

Dietary polyphenol intake was estimated based on the data obtained from the same 147-item semi-quantitative FFQ described above. Among these, 80 food items identified as sources of polyphenols were used to calculate polyphenol content. Estimation relied on the updated Phenol-Explorer database (https://www.phenol-explorer.eu), which includes information on 501 polyphenols categorized into 6 classes and 31 subclasses, along with data on the effects of food processing on polyphenol levels22.

We calculated the intake of total polyphenols and the main subclasses, including flavonoids, phenolic acids, lignans, and stilbenes. Within flavonoids, the predominant subclasses flavonols, flavanols, flavones, flavanones, and anthocyanidins were considered, as well as hydroxybenzoic and hydroxycinnamic acids within phenolic acids. Total polyphenol values were mainly derived from Folin assay data; when unavailable, subclass values were summed to estimate totals. For flavonoids and phenolic acids, values from chromatography after hydrolysis were applied. For anthocyanins, values analyzed by the pH differential method were incorporated into the flavonoid total. Stilbenes were assessed using chromatographic methods, and lignans by chromatography following hydrolysis.

To account for food preparation, processes such as boiling, frying, and other cooking methods were adjusted using retention factors reported in the Phenol-Explorer database. Finally, polyphenol intake was calculated by multiplying the polyphenol content of each food item by its reported daily consumption.

Measurement of gene expression levels using quantitative real time PCR

Samples were immediately transferred to RNase/DNase-free microtubes, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C. Total RNA was extracted from adipose tissue using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocols. RNA quality was assessed via Nanodrop spectrophotometry (ND-1000, USA), with 260/280 ratios within acceptable limits. Genomic DNA was removed by DNase I treatment prior to cDNA synthesis, which was performed using a BIOFACT kit (South Korea) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. cDNA was stored at − 20 °C until use. GAPDH served as the reference gene23,24. Primers for PI3K, PTEN, Akt, and GAPDH were as previously described25,26.

RT-qPCR was conducted on a Rotor-Gene 6000 (Corbett Research, Australia) using 20 µL reactions containing 10 µL 2× SYBR Green Master Mix (BIOFACT), 1 µL cDNA, 7 µL RNase-free water, and 1 µL each of forward and reverse primers. All reactions were run in duplicate with GAPDH and non-template controls. Cycling conditions were: 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 45 cycles of 95 °C (30s), 60 °C (30 s), and 72 °C (30 s). Gene expression levels were normalized to GAPDH using the 2−ΔΔCt method27. All procedures followed MIQE guidelines28.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using SPSS software, version 26 (IBM SPSS Statistics). A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all tests. The normality of data distribution was assessed using histograms and the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed continuous variables, median (25th–75th percentile interquartile range) for non-normally distributed variables, and percentages (%) for categorical variables.

The required sample size was determined a priori using G*Power 3.1 for our primary hypothesis, an association between dietary polyphenol intake and adipose-tissue expression of insulin-signaling genes (PI3K, Akt, PTEN). We assumed a medium effect size (Cohen’s f2 = 0.15) for linear regression with one main predictor (polyphenol intake) and up to five covariates (age, sex, BMI, physical activity, and energy intake), a two-sided α = 0.05, and 80% power. Under these assumptions, the required sample size is approximately N ≈ 120–130, depending on the number of predictors. Equivalently, for a simple correlation this corresponds to detecting r ≈ 0.24. Allowing for ~ 5–10% non-evaluable samples, we targeted N = 135, which ensures ≥ 80% power to detect effects in the medium range.

Participants were categorized into tertiles based on total polyphenol intake to describe population characteristics. The chi-square test was used to examine the distribution of categorical variables, while linear regression was applied to assess trends in continuous variables across polyphenol intake tertiles (using the median value of each tertile as a continuous predictor).

The associations between dietary polyphenol intake (total and subclasses) and the expression of PI3K, Akt, and PTEN in both VAT and SAT adipose tissues were assessed using multiple linear regression models. Results are reported as unstandardized β coefficients with 95% confidence intervals. In the first model, analyses were adjusted for age and sex; the final model was further adjusted for potential confounders, including BMI, physical activity, total energy intake, and homeostatic model assessment for insulin resistance (HOMA-IR).

To assess the associations between dietary polyphenol intake and serum insulin levels as well as HOMA-IR, scatter plots with fitted regression lines were generated. Additionally, Pearson correlation analyses were conducted in both crude and multivariable-adjusted models (adjusted for age, sex, BMI, physical activity, and total energy intake).

Results

Baseline characteristics of study individuals

The general characteristics, biochemical parameters, and dietary intakes of the 135 participants recruited for this study are summarized across tertiles of total polyphenol intake in Table 1. Participants in the highest tertile of total polyphenol intake were, on average, 7 years older than those in the lowest tertile. They also demonstrated elevated concentrations of HDL, reduced levels of fasting plasma insulin, and decreased HOMA-IR indices. Furthermore, fiber and dietary carbohydrate intakes were higher in the highest tertile of total polyphenol intake, whereas fat intake was inversely lower.

The total polyphenol intake in the highest tertile was 946.9 (± 455.47), and that in the lowest tertile was 402.18 (± 79.43). The most consumed class of polyphenols was phenolic acids (105.23 ± 51.67), and the least consumed were stilbenes (0.02(0.012, 0.037)) and lignans (3.90 ± 2.63). Significant differences were observed across the quartiles of total polyphenols for all polyphenol subclasses, except Flavanols, Total phenolic acids, and hydroxybenzoic acids (Table 1).

Association between dietary total polyphenol intake and PTEN gene expression

Table 2 presents the association between dietary total polyphenol intake and its subclasses with PTEN expression in VAT and SAT. In the fully adjusted model, total flavonoid intake was inversely associated with PTEN gene expression in both VAT (β = − 0.187, 95% CI − 0.358, − 0.015) and SAT (β = − 0.224, 95% CI − 0.387, − 0.061). In addition, higher anthocyanin intake was associated with lower PTEN expression in both VAT (β = − 0.211, 95% CI − 0.379, − 0.043) and SAT (β = − 0.220, 95% CI − 0.381, − 0.060), whereas hydroxycinnamic acid intake was inversely associated with PTEN expression only in VAT (β = − 0.178, 95% CI − 0.349, − 0.007). In SAT, after adjustment for all covariates, lower PTEN expression was also associated with higher intakes of total polyphenols (β = − 0.201, 95% CI − 0.365, − 0.037) and stilbenes (β = − 0.199, 95% CI − 0.363, − 0.036).

Polyphenol intake and insulin sensitivity relationship

Since the relationship between dietary polyphenols and serum insulin levels as well as HOMA-IR could help explain the findings of the present study, we assessed these associations by generating scatter plots and regression lines. Overall, Pearson correlation analyses indicated an inverse but non-significant relationship between total polyphenols and their subclasses with serum insulin and HOMA-IR in both crude and adjusted models (data not shown).

Correlation between polyphenol intake and fiber intake

Scatter plot analysis demonstrated a significant positive association between total polyphenol intake and dietary fiber intake (r = 0.64, P < 0.001), indicating substantial overlap between polyphenol-rich and fiber-rich dietary patterns (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Discussion

The current study examined the association between dietary polyphenol intake (total polyphenols and major subclasses) and PI3K, PTEN, and Akt gene expression in the VAT and SAT of obese participants. Overall, higher polyphenol intake was associated with lower expression of PI3K, PTEN, and Akt, although these associations differed across adipose tissue depots and polyphenol subclasses. The reduced expression of insulin signaling-related genes observed with higher polyphenol intake may reflect altered metabolic regulation or compensatory adaptations associated with insulin-resistant states, rather than direct impairment of insulin signaling. Under insulin-resistant conditions, upregulation of signaling intermediates may occur to maintain metabolic homeostasis. Thus, the lower gene expression observed in participants with higher polyphenol intake may indicate a more efficient metabolic state. However, given the cross-sectional design and the assessment of insulin signaling only at the mRNA level, this interpretation remains a plausible biological hypothesis rather than evidence of a causal mechanism. SAT and VAT differ in metabolic activity, cellular composition, and polyphenol responsiveness29,30, which may explain the stronger, more consistent associations observed in SAT. VAT exhibits higher lipolytic activity, inflammation, and vascularization, which may contribute to chronically activated signaling pathways and greater biological variability, thereby reducing the detectability of polyphenol-associated changes in gene expression (e.g., PI3K/Akt modulation via PTEN). In contrast, SAT is characterized by lower baseline inflammation, better expandability, and enhanced PPARγ/estrogen receptor expression, which may create a more metabolically responsive environment for detecting polyphenol-associated modulation of insulin signaling pathways29. These depot-specific patterns suggest that SAT may be more responsive to the signaling and transcriptional regulatory effects of dietary polyphenols beyond their antioxidant properties29,31.

Participants in the highest tertile of polyphenol intake had lower fasting plasma insulin and HOMA-IR values. However, our correlation analysis did not reveal a significant association between polyphenol intake and serum insulin or HOMA-IR. Nevertheless, pour findings are consistent with those of Aghasi et al., who reported that green cardamom, a polyphenol-rich seed, reduced HOMA-IR in patients with type 2 diabetes32. Similarly, a cross-sectional study of 65 overweight or obese adults without diabetes showed that greater adherence to a polyphenol-rich Mediterranean diet was associated with lower HOMA-IR values12. Several possibilities have been proposed for these findings. Based on previous studies, our observations are consistent with prior evidence suggesting that reduced gene expression may reflect decreased systemic insulin demand rather than pathway inhibition25,33. Previous studies have suggested that, with the onset of hyperinsulinemia, adipose tissue responds through compensatory upregulation of the PI3K/Akt pathway to counteract insulin resistance25. Notably, the reduction in PTEN expression observed in our study may be consistent with the proposed mechanisms described in previous studies, which suggest that reduced PTEN expression represents a beneficial adaptive response that enhances insulin signaling through sustained Akt phosphorylation. This proposed mechanism is consistent with the established role of PTEN as a negative regulator of the PI3K/Akt pathway, where PTEN dephosphorylates PIP3 to PIP2, thereby limiting Akt recruitment to the plasma membrane and its subsequent phosphorylation and activation34. Since PTEN is a key negative regulator of the PI3K/Akt pathway, alterations in its expression may modulate insulin-mediated glucose uptake, adipocyte metabolism, and inflammatory signaling, thereby influencing adipose tissue function33,35. Dysregulation of PTEN expression or activity has been linked to altered adipogenesis, insulin sensitivity, and inflammatory responses in adipose tissue. In adipose tissue, alterations in PTEN function have been associated with obesity and metabolic syndrome36.

Although in the present study we assessed PTEN only at the mRNA level, reduced PTEN expression is typically expected to increase PIP3 availability. This facilitates Akt phosphorylation at the key activation sites Thr308 and Ser473 and, consequently, enhances downstream insulin signaling and glucose uptake. Such an effect could occur even in the presence of lower Akt mRNA expression, because Akt signaling is largely modulated at the post-translational level through changes in its phosphorylation status rather than through alterations in transcript abundance37,38. Nevertheless, mechanistic studies including protein and phosphorylation assessments are needed to clarify the biological mechanisms underlying these associations.

We found a positive correlation between total polyphenol and dietary fiber intake, which is expected, as many polyphenol-rich foods, such as fruits, vegetables, legumes, and whole grains, are also high in fiber. We analyzed gene expression exclusively in adipose tissue. It is possible that insulin pathway gene expression in other tissues, such as skeletal muscle or liver, may exhibit different and potentially more favorable patterns. These differences could contribute to the overall improvement in insulin sensitivity and serum insulin levels observed in our obese population. Research indicates that the synergistic interaction between fiber and polyphenols can modulate the composition of the intestinal microbiota, which plays a crucial role in metabolic health by promoting the growth of beneficial bacterial species39,40. This modulation of the gut microbiota has been associated with improvements in insulin sensitivity through mechanisms including the production of short-chain fatty acids that enhance glucose metabolism and reduce systemic inflammation39,40. Since dietary fiber and polyphenol intake are highly collinear, it is challenging to distinguish their individual effects. This suggests that the beneficial associations observed in this study may not be attributable to dietary polyphenols alone, but rather to the overall nutrient and bioactive compound profile of a plant-based dietary pattern.

Regarding glucose regulation, dietary fiber intake has been shown to delay the digestion and absorption of carbohydrates and other macronutrients in the gastrointestinal tract. This slower absorption rate leads to a reduced postprandial glucose spike, thereby diminishing the demand for insulin secretion41,42. Together, polyphenols and dietary fiber may be associated with favorable glucose homeostasis and metabolic outcomes; however, their independent and combined effects were not specifically evaluated in the present study.

Another consideration is that no statistically significant association was found between calorie intake and polyphenol intake tertiles. However, a downward trend in calorie consumption was observed as polyphenol intake increased. Previous studies have suggested that polyphenol-rich, fiber-containing foods may influence satiety; however, this relationship was not specifically evaluated in the present study43,44.

Furthermore, the insulin-sensitizing properties of polyphenols have been hypothesized to depend on the modulation of oxidative stress and reduction of inflammatory signaling by reducing the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α and IL-6, typically activate PI3K/Akt, promoting inflammatory signaling and metabolic dysregulation45,46. Accumulation of fat in obesity is also associated with chronic low-grade inflammation, which is mediated by proinflammatory immune cells2. This process involves an increase in inflammatory mediators, such as IL-6 and TNF-α, alongside a reduction in adiponectin levels2,3. Consequently, this disrupts energy balance and reduces insulin sensitivity47. The polyphenols may influence redox balance in adipose tissue by scavenging reactive species or by modulating endogenous antioxidant enzyme systems, which could, in turn, be compatible with the reduced expression of components of the insulin signaling pathway, particularly PTEN, observed in VAT in the present study48. In the present study, both depots showed associations between mRNA abundance of insulin signaling molecules and dietary polyphenol intake. Nevertheless, the differences between VAT and SAT are also noteworthy, as visceral fat is more strongly linked to insulin resistance. In our study, the associations between polyphenols and gene expression appeared stronger in SAT, which may reflect differences in the metabolic or inflammatory profile of VAT compared to SAT. Further research is warranted to clarify these tissue-specific effects.

In the mentioned study, certain polyphenol classes were significantly linked to reduced gene expression in the insulin pathway. A possible explanation is that the present study evaluated habitual dietary intake, in which polyphenol subclasses are consumed as part of complex dietary patterns rather than as isolated compounds. In addition, dietary heterogeneity among individuals with obesity may influence the intake and distribution of different polyphenol subclasses, potentially contributing to variability in the observed associations. These findings suggest that habitual dietary polyphenol intake may not be sufficient to induce substantial changes in insulin signaling pathways in individuals with obesity. It is possible that higher levels of dietary polyphenol intake than those typically achieved through the habitual diet may be required to influence insulin signaling, although this hypothesis should be tested in well-designed intervention studies.

The strengths of our study included the use of human subjects and an appropriate sample size. We also estimated polyphenol intake from a food frequency questionnaire, using the Phenol-Explorer database for comprehensive dietary polyphenol information. The findings of this study should be interpreted with consideration of its limitations. The study design was cross-sectional, and a causal relationship could not be inferred. Moreover, due to the incompleteness of the Iranian level, without evaluating protein expression or phosphorylation Food Composition Table (FCT) for some items, the USDA FCT was used for energy and nutrient calculations, which may have led to inaccuracies. Furthermore, gene expression was assessed only at the mRNA status, which is critical for interpreting the functional impact on insulin signaling. Genetic polymorphisms and protein expression of PTEN, PI3K, and Akt, which were not assessed in this study, may also influence gene expression levels and individual metabolic responses. The absence of histological analysis represents another limitation, as it prevents the characterization of adipose tissue morphology and inflammatory status that could have provided valuable molecular findings. Finally, the study included patients admitted for elective abdominal surgery rather than using a random sampling method, and the sample consisted solely of Iranian individuals, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other populations.

Conclusion

This study provides novel insights into the association between dietary polyphenol intake and gene expression in the adipose tissue of individuals with obesity. Higher intake of total polyphenols and specific polyphenol subclasses was associated with differences in the expression of key insulin signaling-related genes, including PI3K, Akt, and PTEN, in both visceral and subcutaneous adipose tissue, with more consistent associations observed in subcutaneous adipose tissue. Lower PTEN expression observed in association with higher polyphenol intake may be consistent with altered regulation of insulin signaling-related pathways in obesity. These findings may be consistent with proposed mechanisms linking dietary polyphenols to insulin signaling–related pathways beyond their antioxidant properties. Further studies incorporating protein-level analyses, including phosphorylation assessments, are needed to clarify the biological relevance of these associations.
