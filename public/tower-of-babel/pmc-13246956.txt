Association of physical activity with motoric cognitive risk syndrome among the middle-aged and older adults in China
Authors: Weiyou Wang, Xiaowei Wei, Minxue Wu, Yandi Fu, Sitian Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMotoric Cognitive Risk Syndrome (MCR) represents a pre-dementia syndrome amenable to intervention, bearing substantial public health significance in China amidst its rapidly aging population. While physical activity (PA) potentially exerts neuroprotective effects, empirical evidence regarding its impact on MCR remains limited. This study endeavors to comprehensively investigate the association between PA and MCR among middle-aged and older adults. Utilizing cross-sectional data from the 2015 China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), we employed binary logistic regression models to examine the PA-MCR association. Restricted Cubic Spline (RCS) analysis was conducted to characterize the dose-response relationship and test for potential non-linearity, complemented by extensive subgroup and interaction analyses to evaluate population heterogeneity. To ensure result robustness, we implemented Propensity Score Matching (PSM) and conducted two additional sensitivity analyses. The study revealed an MCR prevalence of 11.96% among middle-aged and older adults. In the fully adjusted model, high PA levels demonstrated a 44.8% reduction in MCR risk compared to low PA levels (OR = 0.552, 95% CI = 0.418–0.729). RCS analysis indicated a significant linear relationship between PA and MCR (P for nonlinearity = 0.060). Subgroup analyses identified sleep duration as a significant effect modifier in the PA-MCR association (P for interaction < 0.05). Consistent findings were observed in PSM-adjusted results and both sensitivity analyses. This study substantiates a significant association between PA and MCR in middle-aged and older adults, providing crucial theoretical foundations for developing PA-based dementia prevention strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-48806-7.Keywords: Motoric cognitive risk syndrome, Physical activity, Middle-aged and older adults, CHARLS, Propensity score matchingSubject terms: Diseases, Health care, Medical research, Neurology, Risk factors

Introduction

The aging population in China has emerged as a significant societal challenge. In the foreseeable future, although China will soon be overtaken by India as the world’s most populous country, it remains the nation with the largest older adults globally. According to the latest 2020 national census data, individuals aged 65 and above account for nearly 14% of China’s total population, indicating that the country is progressively transitioning into an aging society. This substantial demographic shift has positioned geriatric health concerns as a critical issue within China’s medical and public health domains1. Motoric Cognitive Risk Syndrome (MCR), a pre-dementia condition characterized by subjective cognitive complaints and objectively measured gait speed reduction in the absence of mobility disability or dementia2–4, warrants particular attention. Epidemiological data reveal that the prevalence of MCR among Chinese individuals aged 60 and above stands at 7.3%, with comparative rates of 5.5% in Scotland, 9.9% in Italy, and 4.34% in Brazil5–8. Contemporary research has established MCR as a robust predictor of cognitive impairment and dementia3, demonstrating significant associations with accelerated cognitive decline, increased risk of Alzheimer’s disease and vascular dementia9,10. Furthermore, the synergistic effects of cognitive dysfunction and gait abnormalities in MCR patients substantially elevate fall risk compared to healthy older adults, predisposing them to recurrent falls and subsequent hip fractures11. The research findings have demonstrated that MCR significantly elevate mortality risk in medium and long term12,13. In alignment with modern geriatric medicine’s emphasis on preventive strategies, the early identification and management of modifiable risk factors associated with MCR have become paramount.

Physical activity (PA) is defined as any bodily movement produced by skeletal muscles that results in energy expenditure, encompassing diverse domains including leisure, occupational, educational, and household activities14. This concept not only includes systematic exercises, but also encompasses various activities in daily life, such as walking, cycling, and various leisure and entertainment activities15. Epidemiological studies indicate that approximately 66% of Chinese individuals aged 35 to 75 engage in at least 30 min of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) per day16. However, the prevalence of physical inactivity in China has demonstrated a concerning upward trend, increasing from 17.9% in 2010 to 22.3% in 201817,18. The World Health Organization (WHO) emphasizes the substantial health benefits of PA for older adults, particularly in reducing all-cause mortality, cardiovascular disease mortality, hypertension risk, while enhancing cognitive function and sleep quality. Additionally, PA serves as an effective intervention for fall prevention and mitigation of fall-related injuries19. Despite emerging evidence suggesting a significant association between sedentary behavior and MCR9, the scientific community has paid insufficient attention to the relationship between PA and MCR. Investigating this association may yield valuable insights for promoting healthy aging strategies.

In conclusion, utilizing the 2015 cross-sectional data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), this research aims to comprehensively examine the association between PA and MCR among middle-aged and older adults in China.

Method

Data sources

The study utilized data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), a nationally representative, interdisciplinary survey project jointly conducted by Peking University and Wuhan University. CHARLS employs a Probability Proportional to Size (PPS) sampling methodology, encompassing over 10,000 households across 150 randomly selected counties/districts and 450 rural villages throughout China. This comprehensive survey aims to establish a high-quality micro-level database that captures the demographic, economic, health status, and physical measurement characteristics of Chinese middle-aged and older adults aged 45 years and above20, thereby facilitating in-depth analysis of China’s aging population dynamics. The data collection protocol received ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board of Peking University (IRB00001052—11015), and all participants provided written informed consent prior to survey administration. The dataset and corresponding questionnaires can be downloaded by applying at the official website(http://charls.pku.edu.cn/en).

Ethics

The ethical approval for CHARLS came from the Biomedical Ethics Review Board of Peking University (IRB00001052–11015), and all participants signed written informed consent forms before participating in the survey. This study followed the guidelines of the “Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology” (STROBE), and all procedures were carried out in accordance with local laws, institutional guidelines, and the Declaration of Helsinki.

Sample size calculation

This investigation utilized the cross-sectional data released by CHARLS in 2015, comprising a total of 21,095 participants. The minimum sample size was determined using the standard cross-sectional study formula:(1) N denotes the required sample size;2 p represents the prevalence rate of MCR among the target population;3 q is defined as (1-p);4 Zα/2 is set at 1.96 for a two-tailed test with α = 0.05;5 δ signifies the permissible margin of error, calculated as 0.1p. Given the absence of established prevalence rates for MCR among Chinese individuals aged 45 and above in the existing literature, a conservative estimate of 50% was adopted for sample size calculation21, with the allowable error set at 0.1 of the prevalence rate. Consequently, the minimum required sample size for this study was determined to be 385 participants.

We established the following exclusion criteria:1 absence of PA related data2, missing data on motoric cognitive risk syndrome3, samples lacking covariate data. Ultimately, we included 2,860 middle-aged and older adults aged 45 years and above for analysis. The detailed data cleaning process is illustrated in Fig. 1. The data has been weighted according to the official weights published by CHARLS.

Physical activity assessment

In this investigation, PA was quantified utilizing metabolic equivalent (MET) values derived from the CHARLS questionnaire. In accordance with the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) scoring protocol, PA was operationalized across three primary dimensions: duration, frequency (measured in days), and intensity (categorized as vigorous, moderate, or light) of activities lasting a minimum of 10 min that participants reported engaging in during a typical week. Based on the CHARLS questionnaire framework, participants’ daily activity duration was stratified into five distinct categories (0 min, 10–29 min, 30–119 min, 120–239 min, ≥ 240 min)22, The cumulative weekly PA duration was computed by multiplying the frequency of active days per week by the corresponding daily activity duration. The weekly PA energy expenditure was determined through the formula: MET × daily activity duration (minutes) × number of active days per week. Subsequently, PA intensity was classified into three distinct levels: low physical activity (LPA, < 600 MET-min/week), moderate physical activity (MPA, 600-3,000 MET-min/week), and vigorous physical activity (VPA, > 3,000 MET-min/week)23.

Motoric cognitive risk syndrome

Motoric Cognitive Risk Syndrome (MCR) represents a pre-dementia condition characterized by subjective cognitive impairment and objectively measured slow gait speed. In this study, cognitive impairment was assessed through self-reported responses in the questionnaire. Participants were required to evaluate their memory status by responding to the question “How do you think your memory is?” with the following response options: excellent, very good, good, fair, and poor. Participants who selected “fair” or “poor” were classified as having cognitive impairment. Gait speed was quantitatively measured by recording the average time taken by participants to complete two trials of a 2.5-meter straight walking path under the supervision of an interviewer. Participants were permitted to use walking aids during the assessment. The test was conducted twice, and the mean value was calculated for analysis. The study population was stratified according to age and gender parameters. Age stratification comprised two categories: 45–65 years and ≥ 65 years, while gender was categorized as male and female. The operational definition of slow gait speed was established using statistical methods specific to this cohort. Specifically, slow gait was defined as a gait speed average value ≤ 1.0 standard deviation (SD) within the corresponding age (45–65 years, ≥ 65 years) and gender (male, female) groups. Reference values for appropriate gait speed averages were derived from previous studies, as detailed in Table S1 of the supplementary materials4.

Covariates

To mitigate the potential confounding effects on the research outcomes, covariates were systematically incorporated from four distinct dimensions, informed by prior research experience:1 Basic demographic characteristics, including age, gender, residential location, educational attainment, and marital status;2 Mental health indicators, specifically life satisfaction;3 Lifestyle factors, encompassing sleep duration, alcohol consumption, and smoking status;4 Chronic disease conditions, including hypertension, diabetes, and cardiovascular disease. The operationalization of all variables is delineated in Table S2 of the supplementary materials.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages (n (%)). The chi-square test was employed to assess distributional differences in MCR prevalence. Two logistic regression models were constructed to examine the association between PA and MCR. Model 1 served as an unadjusted baseline, while Model 2 incorporated comprehensive covariate adjustments, including age, gender, residential location, educational level, marital status, life satisfaction, sleep duration, alcohol consumption, smoking status, hypertension, diabetes, and cardiovascular disease. The potential impact of unmeasured confounding factors was quantified through E-value calculations24. Restricted cubic splines (RCS) were implemented to investigate the dose-response relationship between PA and MCR. Subgroup analyses and interaction tests were performed to evaluate potential heterogeneity across different characteristic subgroups. To address multiple comparison issues, P-values were adjusted using the false discovery rate (FDR) method25. Furthermore, propensity score matching (PSM) with a 1:1 nearest neighbor matching algorithm was applied to balance low PA and high PA groups, with the model controlling for the aforementioned covariates to minimize confounding effects. Two sensitivity analyses were conducted to verify result robustness: first, multiple imputation by chained equations was used to handle missing data, thereby preserving sample size and statistical power; second, a restricted analysis excluding patients with hypertension, diabetes, and cardiovascular disease was performed, considering potential PA limitations in these populations.

All statistical analyses were conducted using R 4.3.0. A two-tailed P value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic characteristics of the study population

The study cohort comprised 2,860 participants, with a gender distribution of 1,484 females (51.89%) and 1,376 males (48.11%). The sample included 342 individuals (11.96%) diagnosed with MCR. Regarding residential distribution, 556 participants (19.44%) resided in urban areas. Educational attainment analysis revealed that 841 participants (29.41%) had no formal education, while 2,313 participants (80.87%) were married. In terms of subjective well-being, 1,235 participants (43.18%) reported high life satisfaction, and 1,578 participants (55.17%) maintained an optimal sleep duration of 7–9 h. Behavioral and health status indicators demonstrated that 44.58% of participants consumed alcohol, 42.24% were current smokers, and 30.70%, 7.17%, and 15.63% had been diagnosed with hypertension, diabetes, and cardiovascular disease, respectively. Chi-square analysis revealed statistically significant associations (P < 0.05) between MCR prevalence and educational attainment, smoking status, and hypertension diagnosis. Association analysis between physical activity and motoric cognitive risk syndrome

The present investigation revealed a statistically significant association between PA and MCR through logistic regression analysis, as demonstrated in Table 2. In the initial Model 1 without covariate adjustment, moderate and high levels of PA were associated with 32% and 45% reductions in MCR risk, respectively, compared to low PA levels (MVPA: OR = 0.680, 95% CI = 0.495–0.936; VPA: OR = 0.550, 95% CI = 0.419–0.722). Subsequent adjustment for demographic characteristics, mental health status, lifestyle factors, and chronic disease comorbidities demonstrated that high PA levels were associated with a 44.8% reduction in MCR risk relative to low PA levels (OR = 0.552, 95% CI = 0.418–0.729). Furthermore, we computed an E-value (1.59) to evaluate the potential impact of unmeasured confounding variables. The sensitivity analysis suggested that the primary findings remained robust unless unmeasured confounders with an odds ratio lower than the E-value were present.

Subgroup analysis and interaction analysis

The subgroup analysis demonstrated statistically significant associations between PA and MCR across various demographic and clinical subgroups (P < 0.05). These subgroups encompassed distinct age cohorts, gender categories, life satisfaction levels, alcohol consumption patterns, smoking statuses, and hypertension statuses. Furthermore, significant associations were observed among rural residents, individuals with ≤ 6 years of formal education, married participants, those with nocturnal sleep durations either < 7 h or > 9 h, and non-diabetic or non-cardiac disease patients. The interaction analysis identified sleep duration as a significant effect modifier in the PA-MCR association (P for interaction < 0.05). The comprehensive research findings are graphically presented in Fig. 3.

Subgroup analysis of the association between MCR syndrome and PA intensity based on multiple factors.

Propensity score matching

To mitigate the potential confounding effects of covariate differences on the research outcomes, we performed a secondary analysis employing Propensity Score Matching (PSM) methodology26. Following the exclusion of samples with moderate MET, we implemented a 1:1 nearest neighbor matching algorithm with a random seed fixed at 123 to match participants exhibiting low and high MET levels. The caliper width was established at 0.2, with a standardized mean difference below 0.2 indicating satisfactory inter-group balance. The baseline characteristics post-PSM are delineated in Table S4 of the Supplementary Materials. Subsequently, we conducted a secondary analysis examining the association between PA and MCR using the newly generated dataset, with the findings presented in Table S5. Model 1 demonstrated that, relative to low PA levels, the risk of MCR among middle-aged and older adults with high PA decreased by 53.1% (OR = 0.469, 95% CI = 0.326–0.674). Model 2, which incorporated adjustments for all covariates, revealed a 55.4% reduction in MCR risk among middle-aged and older adults with high PA compared to their low PA counterparts (OR = 0.446, 95% CI = 0.308, 0.648). These findings corroborate the primary research outcomes.

Sensitivity analysis

To further validate the robustness of our findings, this study implemented two additional sensitivity analyses. First, we examined the pattern of missing data and determined that all missing values were random in nature. Consequently, we processed the missing data using the Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE) method. The detailed content is presented in the attached Table S6. Second, we excluded participants with hypertension, diabetes, and cardiovascular diseases. Using these two newly generated datasets, we reconstructed logistic regression models to analyze the association between PA and MCR. The results of these analyses are presented in Supplementary Tables S7 and S8. Both analyses demonstrated consistency with our primary research findings, revealing a statistically significant association between PA and MCR (P < 0.05).

Discussion

Utilizing nationally representative data from the CHARLS, this study represents the first systematic investigation to demonstrate a significant linear correlation between PA and MCR among middle-aged and older adults in China. The prevalence of MCR in this demographic was determined to be 11.96%. Current epidemiological data indicate substantial regional variations in the global prevalence of MCR12,27,28. These disparities may be attributable to divergent assessment protocols for gait speed across different countries. For instance, in a Colombian MCR study, gait speed was evaluated through two trials of 3-meter walks at normal pace from a standing position, whereas the more permissive threshold adopted in China could potentially result in an overestimation of MCR prevalence29. Moreover, these variations might be associated with the synergistic effects of multiple factors within the context of China’s rapidly aging population. Notably, the relatively high prevalence of cardiovascular diseases among Chinese older adults is particularly relevant, as empirical evidence has established significant associations between MCR and both hypertension and stroke30,31.

The observed association between PA and MCR in this investigation aligns with findings from prior studies32,33. The mechanism of neural plasticity potentially serves as a primary pathway through which PA influences MCR34. Current research indicates that there is a significant dose-response relationship between PA and cognitive function. When PA reaches a specific threshold dose, it can significantly reduce the risk of cognitive impairment; while PA levels below this threshold have a limited effect on improving cognitive function35. The main mechanism of action may be that hippocampal volume undergoes an annual reduction of 1–2% during later adulthood. This volumetric decline correlates with progressive increases in cognitive impairment prevalence36, which can be mitigated through aerobic exercise interventions. Moderate-intensity PA has been shown to elevate concentrations of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and other neurotrophic factors, resulting in a 2% increase in hippocampal volume, thereby counteracting age-related degeneration and delaying the progression of neurodegenerative diseases, including Alzheimer’s disease (AD)37. However, the association between lower levels of PA and cognitive function remains limited due to insufficient intensity and duration, which hinders the effective activation of neuroplasticity-related mechanisms. In contrast, higher levels of PA adequately stimulate cerebral metabolism and blood circulation, thereby enhancing neuroprotective effects. Drawing upon existing research and theoretical frameworks, we hypothesize that high-intensity PA may exert positive effects on cognitive function through neural plasticity mechanisms, consequently reducing MCR risk38. Furthermore, previous studies have established depression as an independent risk factor for MCR, with physical exercise as the principal modality of PA demonstrating multidimensional therapeutic efficacy in depression prevention and treatment39,40. As a non-pharmacological intervention, PA ameliorates depressive symptoms through multiple pathways, including hippocampal volume augmentation, cellular proliferation stimulation, and neurotrophic factor circulation enhancement, thereby indirectly mitigating MCR risk.

In the subgroup analysis, we observed a significant association between PA and MCR among specific demographic groups: rural residents, individuals with ≤ 6 years of education, married participants, those with nighttime sleep durations of < 7 h or > 9 h, and individuals without diabetes or heart disease. The significant PA-MCR association among rural residents may be attributed to the complex interplay of environmental-behavioral-social factors41. which can be observed across three primary dimensions. Firstly, urban and rural environments exhibit distinct exposure profiles. Chronic stressors prevalent in urban settings, including noise pollution, air pollution, and high population density, may impair cognitive and motor functions through neuroendocrine mechanisms42. Secondly, urban and rural populations demonstrate differential patterns of physical activity and metabolic compensation. Rural residents typically engage in sustained, moderate-to-high intensity activities related to daily labor (farming and active transportation), which may more effectively stimulate brain-body integration. In contrast, urban residents predominantly participate in fragmented, low-intensity fitness activities. The combination of high-calorie diets and sedentary behaviors among urban populations may counteract PA’s protective effects against MCR through pathways such as insulin resistance and vascular endothelial dysfunction43. Thirdly, disparities in healthcare access and health behaviors between urban and rural populations may influence outcomes. Enhanced medical accessibility in urban areas may facilitate earlier MCR detection and intervention, potentially attenuating the observed PA-MCR association. Conversely, rural areas may experience higher rates of undiagnosed MCR cases due to insufficient screening44. The significant PA-MCR association among individuals with ≤ 6 years of education may reflect education’s positive impact on cognitive function45. The relationship between educational attainment and cognitive ability demonstrates significant influence across the adult lifespan and educational spectrum. Research indicates that education primarily exerts a sustained effect on cognitive function in later life by shaping individual cognitive development trajectories, a mechanism validated across diverse age groups and educational backgrounds46. The significant PA-MCR association among married individuals may be mediated by spousal support and companionship, which can positively influence mental health and cognitive function47. The restricted PA-MCR association to individuals without diabetes or heart disease may relate to the pathological mechanisms of metabolic and cardiovascular diseases48. Individuals without these conditions maintain intact vascular system function, enabling efficient oxygen and nutrient transport to the brain. PA may further enhance cerebral circulation and vascular elasticity, thereby supporting neural metabolism and cognitive function. In contrast, patients with diabetes and heart disease often exhibit vascular pathologies (atherosclerosis and microvascular damage) that may mitigate PA’s beneficial effects on MCR49. Additionally, the long-term use of multiple medications required for disease management may produce cognitive side effects, potentially interfering with the PA-cognition relationship50,51. The association between PA and MCR among individuals with atypical sleep durations (< 7 h or > 9 h) aligns with previous research linking such sleep patterns to various health conditions, including functional disorders, disabilities, hypertension, and cardiovascular diseases, which may indirectly influence the PA-MCR relationship52. Interaction analysis revealed sleep duration’s significant moderating effect on the PA-MCR association, establishing it as a novel risk factor for MCR53. Potential mechanisms include sleep fragmentation, difficulty initiating sleep, and impaired sleep microstructure, which may indirectly affect MCR by compromising the cognitive abilities of older adults.

In conclusion, a statistically significant association has been established between PA and MCR. After controlling for confounding factors, moderate to vigorous physical activity has been demonstrated to exert a positive effect on MCR patient, thereby providing a scientific foundation for its prevention. Furthermore, the research findings underscore the necessity of considering urban-rural disparities and multifactorial influences, including sleep duration, educational attainment, social support systems, and chronic disease profiles, when developing intervention protocols. These insights not only contribute novel perspectives to geriatric health management but also serve as valuable references for public health policy formulation, facilitating the implementation of personalized MCR intervention measures.

Limitations

The present study is subject to several methodological limitations. Primarily, the reliance on self-reported data for key variables introduces potential recall bias and social desirability bias, which may compromise the accuracy and comprehensiveness of the collected information. Second, due to the limitations of the database content, some MCR factors were not fully considered in the covariates (such as genes, environment, etc.). Although the E value of the model shows a small unmeasured effect, if some potentially related covariates (such as genes, diet, etc.) are directly included in the analysis in future studies, the results may be more realistic. Third, Thirdly, this study only employed a cross-sectional study to conduct a preliminary exploration of the association. It cannot definitively establish the causal relationship between PA and MCR. The specific relationship between the two needs to be further demonstrated and explained through cohort studies and other methods. At the same time, a cross-sectional study can only show the current prevalence of MCR among older adults, but it cannot observe the dynamic process of MCR from the emergence of mild symptoms to the progression of the disease. Therefore, the key influencing factors and changing trends during the development of MCR cannot be accurately revealed through this study.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study, utilizing cross-sectional data from the CHARLS, conducted a systematic investigation into the association between PA and MCR among middle-aged and older adults in China. The empirical findings demonstrate that higher-intensity PA is inversely associated with the risk of MCR in the population. Particularly noteworthy is the significant moderating effect of sleep duration on the PA-MCR association. These findings contribute substantial scientific evidence to the development of health intervention strategies targeting middle-aged and older adults in China, indicating that enhancing PA intensity may exert a positive effect on the risk of MCR to a certain extent. This result could hold significant implications for promoting healthy aging. Meanwhile, Based on these findings, we recommend that intervention strategies aimed at reducing MCR risk through PA improvement should incorporate comprehensive consideration of population subgroup characteristics to optimize disease management and enhance patient quality of life.
