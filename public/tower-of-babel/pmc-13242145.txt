Virtual gifting as affective monetization: a meta-analysis of behavioral antecedents in live game streaming platforms
Authors: Siwat Chokpaisan, Charin Suwanwong, Jakkrit Thavorn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractVirtual gifting is an important monetization mechanism in live game streaming, but prior evidence remains fragmented across psychological, social, and platform-oriented explanations. This study conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of empirical research published between 2015 and 2025 to synthesize construct-level antecedents associated with virtual gifting in live game streaming and closely related live streaming contexts. Following PRISMA 2020, eligible studies were identified through a structured database search, screened against predefined criteria, and coded using a standardized extraction procedure. Effect sizes were synthesized using random-effects models, and exploratory subgroup analyses were used to examine potential differences between self-report and secondary behavioral data. The results showed that engagement, attractiveness, utility, social acceptance, hedonic motivation, and awareness of needs were positively associated with virtual gifting, whereas passing time was negatively associated. Parasocial relationships showed a large but statistically unstable pooled association under substantial heterogeneity. Across several antecedent categories, heterogeneity was high, indicating that pooled estimates should be interpreted as average associations across diverse operationalizations, platforms, samples, and data sources. Differences between self-report and behavioral data were observed descriptively, but these subgroup findings should be interpreted cautiously because several subgroups were based on limited numbers of studies. Overall, the findings provide a bounded synthesis of factors associated with virtual gifting and suggest that gifting behavior is linked to activated engagement, streamer-related cues, social acceptance, perceived value, and need awareness, rather than passive viewing motives.Keywords: Virtual Gifting, Live Game Streaming, Platform Economics, Digital Monetization, Meta-Analysis

Introduction

Over the past decade, live game streaming has become a prominent form of digitally mediated social interaction, where viewers engage with streamers and other audience members in real time. Platforms like Twitch, YouTube Live, and Douyu allow for viewers to interact with the streamers and other viewers while also streaming their gameplay [1]. Within these platforms, viewers often gift items to the streamers that the streamers can use to purchase items for their channel or to increase the streamer’s revenue [2].

Virtual gifting is a practice in which users of the live streaming platforms gift items to the streamers on the platforms. Virtual gifting plays a dual role within these platforms; the platforms often utilize virtual gifting as a means of creating revenue for the platforms, and the streamers often utilize virtual gifting as their primary means of earning income from the platforms [3, 4]. Additionally, as described by Chen and Chua [5], the act of virtual gifting can be utilized as a means of understanding the level of engagement that the users have with the streaming platforms and their content. Thus, virtual gifting plays a dual role within these platforms as a means of both creating revenue for the platforms and enabling the streamers to receive that revenue. Though most existing research on live streaming platforms does not focus upon virtual gifting specifically, some studies have recognized its role in contributing to the revenue of those platforms.

The majority of existing research on virtual gifting considers the motivations of the individual gifter, such as the desire to form parasocial relationships with influencers, to become more engaged with the content of those influencers, to interact with others within the virtual platform, or for purely hedonic reasons for gift giving [2, 6–8]. Such research has provided valuable insights into the reasons that individuals purchase virtual gifts from others. However, most of these existing studies have considered each of these antecedents of virtual gifting separately, and without considering how each of these individual factors may relate to one another, particularly in relation to the motivations of individuals to gift as a means of monetization within virtual platforms.

Although prior studies have examined several antecedents of virtual gifting, the evidence remains fragmented across psychological, relational, social, and platform-oriented explanations. For instance, existing studies have focused mainly on different aspects of virtual gifting, which prevents the development of an understanding of the factors that contribute to virtual gifting in general. Furthermore, the lack of studies that relate these factors to the monetization of virtual platforms prevents an understanding of the reasons that such platforms have adopted such systems of virtual gifting as a means of generating income for those platforms.

To address these gaps in the literature, this study conducts a systematic review and meta-analysis of existing empirical studies that investigate virtual gifting within the context of live game streaming and related live streaming platforms. The study intends to synthesize the relationships between each of these antecedent categories and virtual gifting. Additionally, most of the existing studies published on the topic have investigated the relationship between each of these antecedent categories and virtual gifting; thus, these constructs are the units of analysis for this study.

By performing a review of the existing literature on virtual gifting within live game streaming platforms, this research contributes to the existing literature by providing a thorough and systematic review of the literature on the topic, and by discussing the implications of those reviewed studies to the concept of virtual gifting as a form of monetization, as well as to the management of those live game streaming platforms.

Background and conceptual organizing framework

The following section presents a conceptual framework for the antecedents of virtual gifting that have been recognized in previous studies. The framework distinguishes between platform-related, streamer-related, and community-related antecedents of virtual gifting, each of which has been the focus of previous research studies. Furthermore, the framework is constructed in a manner that indicates that the intentions of this research study were not to estimate the statistical interactions between these three different categories of antecedents; rather, the framework was created to help structure the discussion of the constructs that relate to each of these categories within the context of the findings of the meta-analysis. It should be noted that this meta-analysis did not investigate any interactions between these levels of the live streaming platforms (platform, streamer, and community). Rather, the framework established for these three levels of live streaming platforms was utilized in this study to guide the investigation of each of these antecedent categories.

Virtual gifting in a platform–streamer–viewer environment

The concept of virtual gifting in the context of live game streaming can be understood as a system of interactions among users, streamers, and platforms [9]. While most existing research investigates the interactions between streamers and the viewers who gift them, such a perspective considers the interactions among the different components of the virtual gifting system [10]. The system consists of the actions of the platforms, streamers, and users within the live game streaming environment. The actions of these components are both influenced by and have an impact upon one another; for instance, the design decisions of the platforms influence the kinds of interactions that occur among the streamers and users, the actions of the streamers have an impact upon the degree of engagement and gifting by the viewers, and the gifting by users creates revenue for the platforms, which can then influence the features that the platforms make available to the streamers and users [1]. Thus, the system enables the platforms and streamers to gain economic value from the emotional engagement of their audiences.

By viewing virtual gifting within the context of the platform–streamer–viewer triangle, it becomes possible to more fully understand why each of those antecedents, while present in previous studies, should not be considered as individual motivations for virtual gifting within such platforms. Each of these antecedents can be classified according to the different types of mechanisms that each represents.

Platform affordances and viewer activation

At the platform environment, the technology that enables virtual gifting has a certain set of affordances that impact how users engage with and monetize out of the platform. Based on the theories of Uses and Gratifications 2.0, the affordances of a technology platform are actually the features of the platform that enable users to fulfill specific motivations [11]. For platforms like live game streamers, factors like modality, agency, interactivity, and navigability are actually the aspects of the platform that enable users to experience the platform and its content and to monetize for those experiences [12]. Furthermore, other recent research in the area has shown that enabling features like personalization and providing users with a sense of value co-creation with the platform can also contribute to users’ involvement with the platform, which subsequently impacts their decisions to engage in behaviors like virtual gifting [13]. Furthermore, recent work on short video applications has also revealed that the features of the platform, such as personalization, design characteristics, and telepresence, can impact user stickiness on those platforms [14]. These findings support the idea that the features of the platforms create the conditions under which viewers of those platforms become absorbed in the videos, become engaged with the content of those videos, and begin to take part in the actions that are required of those viewers. Thus, the platform itself can be seen as a means of enabling users’ initial motivations to exhibit the behavior of virtual gifting.

More specifically, different affordances are associated with distinct antecedent mechanisms examined in this study. Modality affordances, such as video, animation, sound effects, and visual gift displays, may heighten sensory stimulation in live-streaming environments. Prior studies have discussed arousal as one possible affective response to such environments [15]. However, arousal was not retained as a quantitative antecedent category in the present meta-analysis because the available evidence was insufficient and methodologically inconsistent for reliable pooling. Agency affordances (e.g., following, subscribing, customizing profiles) enhance users’ sense of control and participation, contributing to engagement and perceived utility [16]. Interactivity affordances (e.g., live chat, reactions, and real-time feedback) foster social presence and reciprocity, linking directly to social acceptance, parasocial relationships, and interaction-based engagement [17]. Navigability affordances (e.g., recommendation algorithms, raids, and discovery systems) influence exposure and continuity of participation, reinforcing engagement and awareness of needs over time [18]. Through these mechanisms, platform design does not merely accompany user motivations but actively shapes the conditions under which these antecedents translate into gifting behavior.

In addition, platforms systematically frame virtual gifts as expressions of support or appreciation, rather than as conventional purchases. The interface elements, labels, and cues work to suggest the relational and community aspects of the platform [10]. These aspects relate to the antecedents of the behavior (such as engagement, attractiveness, and acceptance) of the users of the platform. Thus, these interface elements work to associate gifting with those relational and emotional aspects of the platform rather than the transactional aspects of gifting.

Overall, these interface elements indicate how those antecedents of gifting are actually enabled by the social gifting platform. That is, each of those antecedents exists within the interactive environment that was designed into the social gifting platform. This relationship between the antecedents to gifting and the social gifting platform provides a theoretical basis for the findings of the meta-analysis in the context of those monetization processes for the platform.

In the present review, platform-related mechanisms are; therefore, represented only indirectly through the inclusion of antecedent categories like hedonic motivation, engagement, and utility. While these variables are utilized in the meta-analysis as constructs to be evaluated for their relationship to other constructs in the study, each of these constructs also relate to the psychological responses to the various online game streaming platforms.

Streamer-related relational and evaluative cues

Beyond the platform itself, various streamers contribute to virtual gifting through their own behaviors and interactions. Factors related to the streamers that may enhance the receptiveness of the audience include their physical attractiveness, communication style, and authenticity, all of which are correlated with attractiveness-related antecedents of receptiveness [19, 20]. Beyond these factors, other characteristics of the streamers that have been researched include their credibility, their expertise in the topics they discuss, their level of attractiveness, and their level of trustworthiness, all of which have been correlated with the attitudes and behavioral intentions of their audiences [21]. Each of these factors can lead to the streamers gaining the attention and value of their viewers, which in turn impacts the viewers’ decision to interact with the streamers in such a way as to contribute to virtual gifting.

Another factor that relates to the interaction between the streamers and their audience is the interaction between the streamers and their audience members. Factors like the streamer’s real-time communication with their audience members, their responsiveness to the audience, and the ability to form parasocial relationships with their viewers have been shown to lead to higher levels of engagement between those viewers and the streamers themselves [22]. These parasocial relationships are relationships between the viewers and the streamers that are based upon the viewers’ perceptions of the intimacy of those relationships with the streamers [23]. However, these relationships typically develop alongside the other interaction factors discussed within the streamer–audience relationship.

From the perspective of micro-entrepreneurs, streamers provide relational and evaluative cues that may shape viewers’ willingness to support them through virtual gifts [24]. While the process of such conversion is not often directly modeled between these two concepts, there are still certain practices by streamers that increase the likelihood that the audience will contribute to their efforts. For example, thanking the individuals who donate to a streamer, showcasing their recognition of these donors, or providing descriptions of the impact of the donations can all work to increase the sense of social acceptance and engagement that is required from the audience members to donate [25].

Importantly, this framework helps explain differences in the relative influence of antecedents observed in empirical research. Interactional and affective mechanisms—such as engagement and hedonic involvement, may be more immediately activated during live interactions because they are continuously activated during live interactions [8]. In contrast, parasocial relationships tend to operate as a contextual background condition, shaping longer-term attachment but not always directly translating into immediate gifting actions [26]. Attractiveness similarly functions as an enabling condition that enhances attention and receptiveness, rather than as a standalone driver.

Streamer-level mechanisms provide a set of behavioral and relational pathways through which the antecedents synthesized in the meta-analysis are activated and translated into monetization outcomes. This perspective reinforces the role of streamers as active agents within the broader ecosystem while maintaining alignment with the empirical structure of the study.

Streamers are considered as mechanisms within the study through the use of antecedent categories such as attractiveness, parasocial relationships, and awareness of needs. While these categories do not allow for the estimation of the effects of streamers themselves, they help indicate the relationship between viewers and streamers.

Community-related social alignment

Beyond the platform- and streamer-level dynamics, there are also dynamics within the channels themselves that influence virtual gifting. According to social identity theory, the channels within which live streamers host their audiences are social groups, in which the behavior of individuals becomes influenced by the norms and self-categorization processes of those groups [27]. The interactions and experiences that exist within the channels create the norms and processes within which the individuals of those groups are expected to participate; one of those processes includes the practice of virtual gifting.

Furthermore, research into virtual gifting within these channels suggests that the practice is often used as a means of signaling to others within that channel an individual’s membership and affiliation with that group [7]. As a result, virtual gifting becomes a normalized practice within those channels. In some instances, individuals who do not participate in virtual gifting may experience increased motivation to begin doing so in order to indicate their membership in the channel’s community [10]. Related work in the area of virtual gifting indicates that many within digital communities who perform these gifting behaviors exhibit specific characteristics regarding their sense of belonging to those communities and the factors that make those communities appealing to members [28]. Thus, these findings indicate the relevance of social and identity-related factors to virtual gifting.

At the same time, it is important to distinguish between theoretical interpretations of community dynamics and what can be directly inferred from the empirical synthesis conducted in this study. The meta-analysis primarily captures community-related processes through constructs such as social acceptance, which reflect perceived norms and social alignment. However, these measures do not fully isolate community-level mechanisms as a distinct analytical domain. As such, while the findings are consistent with the idea that social environments shape gifting behavior, they should be interpreted as providing indirect evidence of community influence.

Within the present synthesis, the concept of community is represented through the construct of social acceptance and related constructs. These constructs indicate the level of social acceptance by individuals within a community of the behaviors and actions of the individuals in question. Thus, the concept of community is utilized within the present analysis as an interpretative construct.

Conceptual mapping of antecedents to the meta-analytic design

Building on the preceding discussion of the platform–streamer–community perspective, this framework is employed in this study as a conceptual organizing framework rather than as a statistically tested multi-level model. It provides a structure for organizing the antecedent categories synthesized in this study and for interpreting how different types of psychological, relational, and social mechanisms may be associated with virtual gifting.

Within this framework, the platform-related variables are represented by the antecedents that relate to the viewers’ level of involvement and the value they place upon the live streaming platform. Examples of such antecedents include hedonic motivation, engagement, and utility. Each of these antecedents relates to the features of the live streaming platform (such as interactions with the live streamers, the gifts that may be viewed and given, chat functions, and the feedback that viewers receive from the live streamers) and the way that those features can lead to the viewers feeling emotionally involved in the lives of the streamers, enjoying themselves while streaming, or finding the platform to be useful [29]. Each of these variables is analyzed within the context of this meta-analysis regarding the viewers or the constructs of those viewers.

The streamer-related mechanisms are represented by antecedents that reflect the viewers’ streamer-related responses, such as attractiveness, parasocial relationship, and awareness of needs. Attractiveness is associated with the viewers’ positive evaluation of the streamer’s attractiveness. Parasocial relationship indicates that the streamer and viewer have a relationship that is one-sided from the streamer’s perspective, and awareness of needs indicates that the viewer believes that the streamer may benefit from some form of support from the viewers. Each of these antecedents is theoretically likely to be associated with the streamers’ potential to successfully solicit monetary contributions from the viewers [6, 30].

The mechanisms related to community and acceptance are represented primarily by constructs related to social acceptance, such as subjective norms. It is possible that these constructs reflect the viewers’ desire to gain acceptance within the community associated with the live stream in which they participate [22].

Finally, the motivation related to passing time is separate from the other motivations described. Unlike the other motivations described in this study, passing time is associated with lower levels of viewer involvement with the live stream; viewers pass the time while watching live streams without the desire for social interaction or arousal, for instance [31]. Including this motivation as a potential antecedent to virtual gifting will allow researchers to determine whether virtual gifting is mainly associated with those activated motivations or whether it also occurs during the low-involvement passing-of-time motivation to view live streams.

Within this section of the paper, the framework that was established in the background of the study was mapped to the strategy that was employed in performing the meta-analysis to determine the direction and magnitude of the relationships between each of those antecedents and the various outcomes of virtual gifting. Thus, while each of the described categories of platforms, streamers, and communities are discussed within this paper, they are not categories that were established through the performance of this meta-analysis to demonstrate the existence of such a model of virtual gifting as an ecosystem with each of those contributing factors. Instead, the use of such a model was employed as a means of integrating those various antecedents of virtual gifting within the literature with the theoretical and construct-based framework of this meta-analysis.

Methodology

Research design and review protocol

In order to gain an understanding of the antecedents of virtual gifting within the context of live game streaming, a review and meta-analysis of the existing literature on the topic was performed. The nature of the research topic motivated the use of such a research method, as the primary goal of the study was to review the literature and synthesize the findings of the studies regarding virtual gifting behavior within live game streaming. Furthermore, as is explained in more detail in Sect. Methodology, the intention of the literature review was to arrive at a synthesis of the association between each of the proposed antecedents and virtual gifting within live game streaming.

The search and review procedures were performed in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) guidelines [32]. According to these guidelines, there are five main stages to be performed in the conducting of a systematic literature review: performing a search for the relevant literature, screening the titles and abstracts of those articles to determine whether they relate to the research topic, reviewing the full texts of the articles to determine if they satisfy the criteria for the inclusion of the articles in the review, extracting the data from those articles, and performing a synthesis of the results of each of those studies [33].

In addition to these five main stages, a protocol was established for the research study that detailed the database searches that would be performed, the time period during which the studies would be searched for, which studies would be included or excluded from the review, the procedures that would be performed to extract the data from the studies, and the statistical test that would be performed to synthesize the effect sizes of the reviewed studies. Each of these components of the protocol enabled the research study to systematically review the literature on the topic of virtual gifting within live game streaming, and to arrive at a synthesis of the research on that behavior.

Literature search strategy and study selection

Literature search was performed within four databases: Scopus, Emerald Insight, ScienceDirect, and Taylor & Francis Online. These databases were selected because, taken together, they provide broad coverage across the principal fields in which research on virtual gifting is most likely to appear, including business and management, media and communication, digital platforms, and user behavior research. The time limit for the databases was restricted to the years between January 2015 and December 2025. This time period reflects the relatively recent introduction of live streaming platforms. As such, virtual gifting related to live game streaming is a relatively recent phenomenon.

The search strategy for the databases was designed with the concepts to be investigated in mind. For instance, the search focused on literature related to donation, gifting, and virtual gifting. Furthermore, the search was restricted to articles related to live streaming or game streaming to exclude articles related to other types of e-commerce or donation websites. The search query was; therefore, constructed as follows:

(donation OR donate OR gift OR “virtual gift”) AND (“live streaming” OR “game streaming” OR “game live streaming”) AND (follower OR viewer).

This search query was constructed to ensure retrieval of articles related to the main concepts to be investigated. The search was conducted broadly within the databases to maximize the opportunity to find relevant research prior to screening articles for eligibility [34]. Thus, the determination of the relevance of articles related to the research topic occurred during the screening of articles for eligibility, which is described in the PRISMA flow diagram (see Fig. 1). Moreover, the search terms that were employed were the terms most commonly utilized in academic literature regarding live streaming to describe the concept of viewers contributing to their favorite streamers: donation, donate, gift, and virtual gift. These terms were specifically selected to indicate the type of contribution by viewers to streamers and to exclude any studies related to unrelated forms of purchasing. The use of the term virtual gift may differ among live streaming platforms and studies, and it is possible that other terms specific to those platforms (such as tipping, cheering, or paid messages) were not utilized within the search term string. Thus, this search string was used to ensure that the search was based on the terminology chosen for the study, rather than attempting to perform an exhaustive search for all terms that may relate to such contributions within live streaming platforms.

PRISMA diagram

The literature search within these four databases resulted in 3,134 records: 708 from Scopus, 270 from Emerald Insight, 299 from ScienceDirect, and 1,857 from Taylor & Francis Online. Within these databases, 303 duplicates were removed. Thus, 2,831 records were screened for relevance and eligibility. The complete search strategy and the reasons for the removal of studies are represented in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1), as well as in the description of the screening of articles for eligibility.

Screening and eligibility assessment

Following the database search, all of the literature records were exported and screened in stages to determine their relevance to the research question. The database records were first screened by title and abstract. Records determined not to relate to virtual gifting, live streaming, or viewer interactions with the live streamers were eliminated at this stage. The remaining articles were subjected to a full-text review to determine if they met the inclusion criteria for the review.

Studies were included if the following criteria were met. First, the studies had to be empirical research papers (excluding review articles). Second, the studies had to investigate virtual gifting of monetary gifts to streamers, or related concepts to virtual gifting of money to live streamers. Third, the studies had to relate to the concept of live game streaming or interactions between streamers and viewers during such live streams. Finally, the studies had to provide information regarding the methodology and statistics of the research studies, as these would be used to pool and compare the results of each study.

In order to improve the conceptual consistency of the review, studies that used the context of livestreaming in broader ways were only included in the review if the behavior that was studied could be understood as virtual gifting within the context of livestreaming interactions. In other words, the studies had to include some discussion of virtual gifting occurring within the context of livestreaming interactions to be included in the review. Studies that examined unrelated contexts to virtual gifting were therefore excluded from the review. Similarly, studies that only mentioned the use of livestreaming in their study, studies that did not examine virtual gifting as a behavior within the study, or studies whose contexts were too distant from live game streaming to be relevant to the review were also excluded.

Overall, 2,831 articles were screened based on their titles and abstracts; 2,750 of these articles were excluded. Eighty-one articles were then reviewed in their entirety. Of these 81 articles, 66 were excluded; the remaining 15 were included in this research review. Each of these stages ensured that the included studies met the required criteria and would be comparable for conducting a systematic review and meta-analysis of these studies.

Data extraction and coding procedures

The data from the included studies were collected independently by two of the reviewers. Any disagreements between the two reviewers regarding the data collection or coding of the studies were discussed and resolved within the research team. Beyond collecting information about the characteristics of the included studies, the data were collected to preserve the information necessary to perform the planned meta-analysis.

Data collected regarding the studies included information about the authors of each study, the year in which the study was published, the journal in which it was published, and the country of origin of the authors. Information regarding each study that was collected included the size of each study’s sample, the profile of the participants in that study, the age of the participants in each study, the gender of the participants in each study (if reported by the authors), the streaming platform from which the participants streamed (or type of streaming platform used), and the context of the studies in which virtual gifting was examined. Additionally, information was collected regarding the data source from which data were collected, such as whether studies used surveys to collect data from participants versus studies that used another source of data (such as the data provided by a streaming platform).

At the level of the construct, all the variables were coded according to their role within the study. The antecedent variables were grouped into categories according to their conceptual definitions within the literature. Such definitions included concepts like parasocial relationship, attractiveness, utility, social acceptance, engagement, hedonic motivation, passing time, and awareness of needs. Variables with similar meanings within the literature were grouped into the same category only if they were used in other studies with similar meanings for those variables. Variables with different meanings within the studies, even if they appear to be similar in name or meaning, were not grouped into the same category. Furthermore, within each of these groups, the names of the variables were established according to the original study’s definition of the variable. Thus, three considerations guided the establishment of the names of these antecedent variable categories: the meaning of the variable, the motivation behind the variable, and the relationship of the variable to the other variables that were extracted from the studies.

Outcome variables were coded as the outcome of virtual gifting if they represented the actions of the viewers toward the streamers within the studies. Such variables included gifting intention, gifting likelihood, gifting frequency, gift amount, donation behavior, and variables with similar meanings. Variables that were not related to virtual gifting, such as variables related to the general use of live streaming or the viewing of the streamers by the viewers, were not coded as the outcome of virtual gifting unless they were also related to virtual gifting behavior within the original study.

For each study that included statistical information relevant to performing a meta-analysis, that information was extracted from the studies. When multiple effect sizes were reported from the same study, those effect sizes were coded separately during the extraction phase; however, if the effect sizes related to the same antecedents and outcomes within that study alone, those coefficients were combined to calculate a single effect size for that study and those related variables [35]. However, if the extracted coefficients related to different antecedents to the outcomes, those coefficients were not combined, but coded separately as they relate to different concepts.

Quality assessment

The methodological quality of the studies included in this review was appraised using the Appraisal Tool for Cross-Sectional Studies (AXIS) by Downes et al. [36]. Given that the majority of the included studies have utilized an observational, non-experimental, and/or cross-sectional study design (including surveys and observational studies based on social media platforms), the AXIS tool was used to provide a means of evaluating the methodological quality of the studies included in the review.

The AXIS tool contains 20 items that assess the methodological quality of cross-sectional studies. For each of the items within the tool, the experts rate each item as either a “Yes” (1) or a “No/Don’t know” (0). The total score for each study is then converted to a percentage. Following the predefined thresholds used in this review, studies were categorized as high quality when they scored 80% or above, moderate quality when they scored 60–79%, and low quality when they scored below 60%. These thresholds were used as the criteria to classify each study; the AXIS scores and ratings for each study are presented in Table 1.

Two experts independently assessed the quality of the studies included in this review. After each study had been assessed by the two experts, the assessments of each study were compared. Any disagreements between the two experts were discussed and resolved prior to assigning a quality rating to each study. Such a process helped to ensure that the quality assessments were not based upon the judgment of a single expert, but upon the consensus of the experts regarding the quality of each study.

As the studies were of different types, employed different data sources, and employed different research designs, the criteria for assessing each study were applied in a way that considered the type of study that was performed. For studies that were based upon surveys, experts considered factors related to the survey administration, such as the sampling procedure, the validity of the survey instrument, the characteristics of the participants in the study, and the suitability of the statistical analyses used in the study. For studies that used data other than survey data (such as behavioral data collected from users of a platform), experts considered factors related to the use of those alternative data for the research study, such as the source of that data, the variables that were measured using the data, the statistical analyses used, and the potential discussion of any potential bias of those alternative data sources. Thus, the AXIS criteria were applied in a way that ensured a common framework for assessing the quality of the studies included in the review.

Statistical analysis

A quantitative synthesis was conducted through meta-analysis to examine the strength and direction of the associations between the identified antecedents of virtual gifting and virtual gifting behavior. Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r) was used as the common effect size metric because it was the most frequently reported statistic across the included studies. For studies reporting standardized regression coefficients (β) rather than correlation coefficients, β values were converted to approximate Pearson’s correlation coefficients (r) using the procedure proposed by Peterson and Brown [47]. This approach has been commonly applied in meta-analytic research when primary studies report different effect size statistics; however, it should be noted that the conversion provides an approximation and may introduce some estimation bias, particularly when β coefficients are derived from multivariate models with correlated predictors. The conversion of β coefficients to r values was treated as an approximation. Standardized β coefficients are partial coefficients that may depend on the covariates included in the original model, whereas Pearson’s r represents a zero-order association. Therefore, converted coefficients may be affected by model specification, covariate adjustment, multicollinearity, or suppression effects in the primary studies. Thus, effect sizes converted from β coefficients were identified during data extraction and interpreted cautiously.

For studies reporting multiple correlation coefficients for the same antecedent, only one effect size was retained for each meta-analytic model to maintain statistical independence among effect sizes [35]. Prior to pooling, all correlation coefficients were transformed into Fisher’s Z scores to stabilize variance and improve the normality of the sampling distribution. Following the meta-analysis, pooled Fisher’s Z values were converted back into Pearson’s correlation coefficients (r) to facilitate interpretation of the magnitude and direction of the relationships between antecedent variables and virtual gifting behavior. The original directionality of all coefficients was retained throughout the analyses to preserve the theoretical interpretation of the observed associations.

A random-effects model was used to calculate the pooled correlation coefficients for each analysis. A random-effects model was utilized for these calculations because there was expected to be considerable variation between the studies in the variables examined and the methods used to perform the analyses. It would therefore not be appropriate to assume that all of the included studies had any relationship to the same population effect. Thus, the random-effects model was the more appropriate model to utilize in this meta-analysis to arrive at pooled estimates that were meaningful and interpretable across the different studies included in the analysis [48].

The measure of statistical heterogeneity was performed using Cochrane’s Q test and the I² statistic. Cochrane’s Q test assesses whether the heterogeneity between the study effect sizes is due to random variation alone, while the I² statistic estimates the percentage of heterogeneity in the studies [48]. I² values of 25%, 50%, and 75% are interpreted as indicating low, moderate, and high heterogeneity among the studies, respectively. Due to the expected presence of high levels of statistical heterogeneity within this research, any results from these tests are used to both interpret the results of the meta-analysis and to explore the reasons for the heterogeneity within the context of this review.

To investigate the possibility of publication bias and small-study effects, Egger’s regression test was conducted for meta-analytic models with a sufficient number of included studies. Because several antecedent categories were based on only a limited number of studies, the results of Egger’s regression tests were interpreted cautiously, as statistical tests for publication bias may become unstable and unreliable when few studies are available [49]. Funnel plots were not used in the present review because visual interpretation of funnel plot asymmetry is considered unreliable and potentially misleading in meta-analyses with small numbers of studies and substantial heterogeneity. Accordingly, Egger’s regression test was used as the primary statistical indicator of potential publication bias and small-study effects where appropriate.

Moreover, exploratory subgroup analyses were conducted to descriptively examine whether effect sizes differed according to data source. These analyses were treated as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory tests of moderation because several subgroup categories contained only small numbers of studies. Such subgroup analyses were particularly performed for studies that differed in their use of self-report surveys of smaller sample sizes versus the use of secondary data from the records of the large social media platforms. Thus, exploratory subgroup analyses were conducted to descriptively examine whether effect sizes differed according to data source. These analyses were treated as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory tests of moderation because several subgroup categories contained only small numbers of studies.

All statistical analyses were performed using RStudio (version 4.3.1) and the metafor package in R [50]. This package was used to calculate the effect sizes, heterogeneity, sub-group analyses, and to create the graphs published in this manuscript.

Results

Overview of meta-analytic findings

The selection of studies in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 statement led to the inclusion of 15 studies that would be used in the systematic review and meta-analysis. A search of the four databases resulted in the selection of 3,134 records. Of these records, 303 were found to be duplicates. Of the remaining records, 2,750 did not relate to the research studies that would be performed. Of the remaining articles, their full texts were read to determine if they would relate to the research study; 66 did not relate to the topic of review. Thus, 15 articles were determined to be applicable for the systematic review and meta-analysis (Fig. 1). The AXIS appraisal indicated that all the included studies met the threshold for high quality. Table 1 reports the AXIS percentage score and the corresponding quality category for each study.

The main characteristics of the studies that were included in the review are also presented in Table 1. Most of the studies used samples of users from East Asia (particularly China), the United States, and global populations. The sample sizes ranged from 204 participants to more than 2.29 million users of live streaming platforms. The majority of the users in the samples were young adults, though age was not always published in the study reports. The majority of the studies used in these analyses were survey-based, though there were also observational and platform-based studies included within the review.

While many of the studies used different variables to indicate the dependent variable of interest, those variables were only retained for synthesis if they related to viewer support behaviors toward the live streamers on the live game streaming platforms. Although the term “gifting” or “donation” was sometimes used to indicate such a variable, there were also other ways of measuring it. Regardless of the label, if the variable referred to viewer-to-streamer gifting and donating behaviors, it was included in Table 1 and analyzed as a measure of virtual gifting behaviors toward streamers of live game streaming platforms.

All of the studies included in the review were high quality, according to the AXIS study quality appraisal tool. Thus, all of the studies met the quality threshold established for the systematic review and meta-analysis of these studies. Furthermore, some studies published more detailed information on specific aspects of the studies than others; however, all met the threshold for inclusion in the synthesis.

During data extraction, arousal was identified as a theoretically relevant affective construct in the virtual gifting literature. However, it was not retained for quantitative synthesis because the available evidence was insufficient and methodologically inconsistent for reliable pooling. Specifically, the available arousal-related evidence was based on a very limited number of usable effect sizes and differed in how arousal was operationalized. Including this construct in the pooled meta-analysis could have produced an unstable and potentially misleading estimate. For this reason, arousal is discussed only as a theoretically relevant construct in the background, but it is not reported as a pooled antecedent in the meta-analytic results.

Conceptual categorization of antecedents

Due to the use of different terms to describe the same constructs in the primary studies, the extracted predictors from these studies were categorized into eight antecedent categories. Each of these antecedent categories was formed based on the meanings of the constructs and the presumed mechanism through which each construct was thought to influence an individual’s decision to participate in virtual gifting. As presented in Table 2, these eight antecedents were parasocial relationship, attractiveness, utility, social acceptance, engagement, hedonic motivation, passing time, and awareness of needs.

The use of these antecedent categories was formed according to three main rules. First, the variables for each study were categorized according to the specific variables that were referenced within those studies. For instance, some variables may have referenced concepts related to the streamers themselves, the individuals’ experiences, or the relationship between those streamers and the gifter. Second, those variables were grouped according to the motivation behind those concepts referenced in the studies. Finally, the antecedents into which each variable was categorized were based on the concept of the variables themselves, rather than the similarity of the terms used to describe those variables.

Furthermore, these larger categories did not indicate that the constructs referenced within each primary study were identical to one another. For instance, the antecedent of social acceptance included constructs related to the symbolic value of virtual gifting, social self-presentation, feelings of social belonging, and the expectations of others within the gifting community. Antecedents of engagement included constructs related to the level of activity within the virtual chat rooms, the number of danmaku observed while watching the streamers, and other measures of viewer engagement. Thus, Table 2 presents the concept of each antecedent as well as the constructs related to each of those antecedents as they were described within the primary studies.

There were two main purposes to the categorization of the constructs into these antecedent categories. First, the category provided a means of categorizing and discussing the constructs that were otherwise diverse within the primary studies. Second, the use of these categories enabled the development of the meta-analysis according to these antecedent categories.

Meta-analytic effects of antecedents on virtual gifting

The results indicate that six antecedent categories had statistically significant positive associations with virtual gifting, one had a statistically significant negative association, and one did not reach statistical significance. The strength of, and stability of, these relationships, however, varies considerably. For instance, the magnitude of the relationship between some of these variables and the dependent variable shows high levels of heterogeneity. Thus, in reviewing the results that follow, it is important to consider not just the statistical significance of these relationships, but also the plausibility and consistency of those relationships, especially where the number of studies that investigated such relationships was relatively limited. As several of the antecedent categories consisted of both limited studies and considerable heterogeneity within the groups of studies, the results for each of these categories are to be understood as the average results for the various studies rather than the effect that is present within the population as a whole. Thus, each of the results below indicates not just the statistical significance of each of the pooled estimates, but also the strength of those estimates as well.

Platform-enabled engagement and affective antecedents

The first functional grouping included antecedents related to activated participation and immersion within the live-streaming environment, including engagement (Fig. 2) and hedonic motivation (Fig. 3). Engagement demonstrated a statistically significant positive association with virtual gifting (r = 0.32, 95% CI [0.13, 0.48], p = 0.001), while hedonic motivation also showed a positive relationship (r = 0.27, 95% CI [0.07, 0.44], p = 0.007). However, both estimates were characterized by extremely high heterogeneity (engagement: I² = 99.9%; hedonic motivation: I² = 96.7%), indicating substantial variability across studies. Accordingly, these pooled coefficients should not be interpreted as representing a single common effect size, but rather as broad summaries of heterogeneous findings observed across different study contexts, populations, and operationalizations.

Overall, these findings suggest that activated and emotionally engaging forms of participation are positively associated with virtual gifting behavior. Nevertheless, the substantial heterogeneity observed across studies indicates that the strength and consistency of these associations vary considerably depending on study characteristics, measurement approaches, and platform contexts.

Streamer-related relational cues

The second grouping consisted of streamer-related relational factors, specifically viewers’ perceptions of attractiveness (Fig. 4) and parasocial relationship (Fig. 5). The findings indicated that perceived attractiveness was positively associated with virtual gifting (r = 0.26, 95% CI [0.04, 0.45], p = 0.021). Although heterogeneity remained high (I² = 97.6%), the direction of the relationship was relatively consistent across studies, suggesting a comparatively more interpretable association than several other antecedent categories.

By contrast, the parasocial relationship variable produced a descriptively large pooled coefficient (r = 0.73), but the association did not reach statistical significance (95% CI [-0.01, 0.95], p = 0.053). In addition, heterogeneity was extremely high (I² = 99.8%), indicating substantial inconsistency across studies. Given the limited number of studies included and the wide variability in reported effects, this pooled estimate should be interpreted cautiously and not as evidence of a stable or generalizable association between parasocial relationships and virtual gifting behavior.

Overall, these findings suggest that streamer-related relational cues may play a role in virtual gifting behavior, although the stability and interpretability of these relationships vary considerably across constructs and studies.

Social and instrumental motivations

The third grouping comprised antecedents reflecting socially embedded and functionally oriented motivations for gifting, including social acceptance (Fig. 6), utility (Fig. 7), and awareness of needs (Fig. 8). Social acceptance demonstrated a statistically significant positive association with virtual gifting (r = 0.24, 95% CI [0.16, 0.32], p < 0.001). Although heterogeneity remained high (I² = 96.5%), this estimate was comparatively precise and based on the largest number of coded relationships, making it one of the more interpretable pooled effects within the analysis.

Utility also demonstrated a statistically significant positive association (r = 0.28, 95% CI [0.05, 0.47], p = 0.015), suggesting that viewers were more likely to engage in gifting when they perceived content or platform participation as instrumentally valuable. Awareness of needs produced a somewhat larger pooled coefficient (r = 0.40, 95% CI [0.01, 0.69], p = 0.047), indicating that recognition of streamers’ support needs may also be associated with gifting behavior. However, this estimate was derived from only two effect sizes and exhibited extremely high heterogeneity (I² = 97.2%). Overall, these findings suggest that virtual gifting is associated not only with affective engagement but also with socially and instrumentally oriented motivations. Nevertheless, the substantial heterogeneity observed across studies indicates that these pooled effects should be interpreted cautiously and primarily as indicators of possible relational tendencies rather than definitive population-level estimates.

Low-involvement motive

The final grouping examined the role of time passing as an antecedent of virtual gifting (Fig. 9). In contrast to the previously discussed antecedents, time passing reflects relatively low-involvement or boredom-related use of live-streaming platforms. The findings demonstrated a statistically significant negative association between time passing and virtual gifting (r = -0.21, 95% CI [-0.35, -0.06], p = 0.007), suggesting that viewers who primarily used live-streaming platforms to relieve boredom or pass time were less likely to engage in gifting behavior. Although heterogeneity remained substantial (I² = 84.0%), it was comparatively lower than that observed for many of the other pooled effects. This may indicate relatively greater consistency across studies regarding the negative association between low-involvement viewing motives and gifting behavior.

Nevertheless, because heterogeneity remained considerable across most analyses, the pooled estimates throughout this study should be interpreted cautiously and within the broader context of methodological and conceptual diversity across the live-streaming literature.

The subgroup analyses suggested differences in the magnitude of associations between antecedents and virtual gifting across the two types of data sources. For parasocial relationship, the association appeared stronger in studies relying on self-report measures than in studies utilizing behavioral platform data. Overall, these findings highlight the importance of data source when examining the relationship between antecedents and virtual gifting behavior. Self-report studies primarily capture viewers’ subjective explanations regarding why they engage in gifting, although such responses may be influenced by factors such as social desirability, response consistency, or contextual features of the research setting (Durmaz et al., 2020). By contrast, behavioral-data studies assess actual user activities within live-streaming platforms, including gifting transactions, chat participation, badges, and danmaku interactions. However, these measures are constrained by the types of behavioral indicators available within the platforms themselves. Consequently, discrepancies between self-report and behavioral-data studies may reflect differences between viewers’ perceived motivations and their observable gifting behaviors.

Nevertheless, these subgroup findings should be interpreted cautiously. The relatively small number of studies within several subgroups limits the ability to draw definitive conclusions regarding data source as a moderator of the observed relationships. In addition, the self-report constructs and behavioral indicators used across studies were not always conceptually equivalent, even when categorized under the same antecedent domain. Therefore, these subgroup analyses should be considered preliminary indications of the possible influence of data source on observed effect sizes rather than definitive evidence of moderating effects. Further discussion of these issues is provided in the Discussion section.

Discussion

The findings should be interpreted within the methodological boundaries of the synthesis. The meta-analysis estimates construct-level associations between antecedent categories and virtual gifting outcomes, and does not establish causal pathways or cross-level interactions among platform, streamer, and community mechanisms. Therefore, the discussion focuses on patterns of association and theoretical consistency.

Virtual gifting as affective monetization in platform economies

The findings of this research support the idea that virtual gifting within the context of live game streaming is inherently associated with certain player motivations and viewer involvement in the live streaming event. The results indicate that viewer engagement and hedonic motivation to play the games being streamed are positively associated with virtual gifting. In contrast, the motivation of viewers to pass through time while streaming is statistically significantly associated with virtual gifting. Thus, viewers are more likely to participate in virtual gifting if they are activated or involved in the live streaming experience [7, 12, 22].

Moreover, live streaming platforms are built with features that increase viewer engagement during live streaming events, such as live chats, gifting prompts, donations, rankings, and acknowledgments [60]. These features may connect viewers’ engagement and enjoyment with monetized forms of participation, including the gifting of in-platform virtual items and rewards. In this sense, virtual gifting can be interpreted as a form of platform-based monetization. However, this interpretation should be made cautiously because the present meta-analysis shows association mechanisms. The findings indicate that engagement and hedonic motivation are positively associated with virtual gifting, but they do not demonstrate that platform features directly convert viewer affect into gifting behavior. Thus, virtual gifting should be understood as a monetized behavior that is associated with activated and enjoyable forms of participation, while the specific platform mechanisms that shape this process require further investigation [2, 20].

Despite the positive link between motivation to engage with the games being streamed, and their gifting of virtual items, viewers’ motivation to pass through time while streaming showed a statistically significant negative association with virtual gifting. Thus, viewers are less likely to participate in virtual gifting if they simply intend to pass through time on the live streaming platforms. The contrast between these two outcomes supports the idea that live streaming platforms are established to monetize certain moments rather than the relationships between the live streamers and viewers. However, such a statement is only an interpretation of the outcomes of the study. Thus, these findings support the idea that virtual gifting can be a form of affective monetization within live streaming platforms, but other forms of motivation between viewers and streamers are also likely to exist within certain contexts [6, 10, 42].

Streamer-centered value capture

Beyond the platform-level process of monetization of audience reactions discussed above, the findings also suggest that streamers themselves play a role in monetizing their content. The positive relationship between both engagement and attractiveness with virtual gifting indicates that streamers who can exhibit these qualities will experience higher rates of virtual gifting from their audience members.

In recognizing that live game streamers can be considered a business in some respects, each of these streamers may play an important role in the process of monetizing their streams. More specifically, streamers contribute to the monetization of their game streams in that they are the elements of the stream that encourage audience members to gift items to other streamers within those games. Furthermore, the relationship between streamers and the potential for those streamers to be monetized is consistent with the understanding of streaming platforms as overall multi-sided platforms that host different types of interactions between a variety of different participants, which can range from streamers and audience members to the platforms themselves [7, 22]. Thus, the performance of streamers is one of the factors that can lead to the monetization of those streams.

More specifically, the positive relationship between attractiveness and virtual gifting does not necessarily relate to the physical attractiveness of streamers. Instead, attractiveness in the context of live game streams and interactions between streamers and their audiences generally relates to the likability of streamers, their ability to express themselves while streaming, and their potential to garner positive impressions from their audience members [52, 61, 62]. Thus, these findings suggest that it is not the factor of attractiveness alone that determines the likelihood of streamers being monetized for their games, but that these factors contribute to the evaluations of streamers by their audiences, which in turn helps to enable those potential gifting behaviors. The link between engagement and value capture is even more direct. The positive association between engagement and virtual gifting suggests that gifting is more likely to occur in streaming environments characterized by higher viewer interaction and participation. The included studies suggest that streamers can increase engagement with their viewers and chat rooms, which subsequently leads to increases in viewers who exhibit gifting behavior toward those streamers [20, 22]. These findings are additionally consistent with the affordance perspective of virtual gifting, which argues that streamers can only exhibit certain types of interactions within their live streams, and that the affordances for those interactions correlate with gifting behavior [11].

While these findings are interesting and helpful in indicating the relationship between streamers, engagement, and gifting behavior, such conclusions should be made with some caution. These findings only indicate that streamers have attributes that are associated with gifting behavior; they do not indicate that the streamers themselves have any control over that behavior, or that such factors exhibit the same relationship with gifting behavior across all live streaming platforms. Instead, these findings help to suggest that streamers are contributing factors to the capturing of the value of live streaming platforms.

Overall, then, these findings help to indicate that there are different factors related to the streamers themselves that are associated with the occurrence of gifting behavior. Thus, the streamers can be considered as different contributors to the business and economic element of live streaming platforms as a whole.

Boundary conditions

The findings also indicate that the relationships between antecedents and virtual gifting should not be interpreted as uniform across all streaming contexts. Although several antecedents showed positive associations with gifting behavior, the strength and stability of these associations varied considerably across studies. This variation suggests that virtual gifting is shaped by important boundary conditions, particularly the role of parasocial relationships and the data source used to measure gifting-related behavior.

The boundary condition that can be discussed is that of parasocial relationships between the streamers and their viewers. Previous research has mostly utilized the presence of a parasocial relationship between the streamers and their viewers as the main explanation for the viewers’ willingness to contribute to the streamers [6]. However, the current analysis of these relationships demonstrates that while the effect size for the relationship between parasocial relationships and virtual gifting is large, the relationship is statistically unstable. Thus, this relationship may be contextual in that while viewers with parasocial relationships with the streamers may be more receptive to gifting to those streamers, they do not inherently lead to gifting to those creators.

This is also consistent with the temporal nature of live streaming [2, 8]. Parasocial relationships tend to develop over time between individuals who have a relationship that appears to be intimate, yet which does not involve reciprocal interactions between those two individuals. Consequently, while such relationships may lead to an individual being more likely to gift to another individual who is streamed live, those relationships alone may not be sufficient to lead to live streaming gifting behaviors without some other factors that activate those gifting behaviors during live interactions [63].

The second boundary condition relates to the difference between self-report and secondary data. Within the subgroup analyses, factors such as attractiveness, acceptance, and engagement were found to be stronger in studies that utilized self-report data, while factors like parasocial relationships were stronger in studies that utilized secondary behavioral data. These differences are not to be taken as a disproof of the relationship between those factors and viewing behavior; instead, they may reflect the differences between the data types used in those studies. For instance, studies that utilize self-report data are only able to assess the motivations and intentions of the viewers [22, 38]. Thus, when those viewers report both their motivational factors and their gifting intentions, the studies may reflect the coherence of those self-reported motivations rather than their actual gifting behavior.

Secondary data also help to understand the behavior of the gifter within the platforms themselves. The behavioral data, however, are limited to the data that the platforms themselves publish. Such indicators as chat activity, gifts given, badges earned, danmaku activity, or the number of words typed do not necessarily indicate the reasons behind the gifter’s behavior. The actual gifting of digital merchandise by viewers can be influenced by the platforms themselves. For instance, the way in which the platforms implement payment systems, display gifts, implement ranking features for creators, influence the types of gifts that are prompted from the creators, the times at which gifting occurs, and the behaviors of the streamers themselves can all contribute to the gifting behavior of the viewers [4]. Furthermore, digital platforms have been studied in the past in relation to the emotions that they can inspire within viewers. For instance, one study revealed that digital platforms can influence the emotions of users of those platforms, such as causing pleasure or annoyance in users, which subsequently influence their intentions and behaviors to purchase digital merchandise from the platform creators [64]. The ability of digital platforms to influence the emotions of users of those platforms indicates that the differences in the effects sizes within those behavioral datasets may actually be a result of platform-induced emotions influencing the behavior of those individuals. Thus, the differences in the sizes of the effects within those datasets do not automatically indicate the relative strengths of the corresponding psychological mechanisms altogether.

This distinction allows for a deeper interpretation of the findings within each of these subgroups. The self-report data were used to measure the motives behind the virtual gifting, such as the enjoyment that individuals derive from the streamer or the desire for acceptance within the community. The behavioral data were used to measure the actual gifting by users to those selected streamers, based upon their emotions or their interaction with those streamers [7, 8]. In this sense, self-report and behavioral data capture different layers of virtual gifting (the subjective motivational layer and the enacted platform-mediated behavioral layer).

Accordingly, the present findings suggest that data source should be treated as a methodological and theoretical boundary condition in digital behavior research. Survey-based intentions and platform-derived behavioral traces should not be treated as interchangeable evidence of the same process. Instead, differences across data sources should inform how effect sizes are interpreted across contexts. Therefore, differences in effect sizes across data sources should not be interpreted only as heterogeneity to be explained away, but as theoretically meaningful evidence that virtual gifting has different empirical expressions depending on whether it is measured as motivation, intention, or enacted platform behavior. Future studies should integrate self-report and behavioral data to examine when perceived motivations translate into actual gifting and when platform constraints, event timing, or community visibility alter this translation.

Theoretical contributions

This study makes four theoretical contributions. First, it reframes virtual gifting as a form of affective monetization. Second, it offers a conceptual organizing framework that classifies antecedents into platform-related, streamer-related, and community-related domains without treating these domains as a tested multi-level model. Third, it qualifies the role of parasocial relationships by showing that their pooled association with gifting is large but statistically unstable. Fourth, it contributes to digital behavior research by distinguishing between self-reported motivational evidence and platform-derived behavioral evidence.

This study makes a theoretical contribution by reframing the explanation of virtual gifting from a predominantly relational theoretical perspective to one that is centered upon the notion of monetization. The motivations that have been cited in previous research as the reason for players’ gifting of virtual items to their favorite live game streamers include the motivations of enjoyment of the live game stream, the desire for connection with the streamers, and the development of parasocial relationships between the viewers and the streamers of those live games [6, 7]. While these motivations may help to explain why individuals participate in virtual gifting, the findings of this study suggest that those reasons are insufficient to explain virtual gifting within the context of the live game streaming platforms. The findings that viewers who experience an emotional connection to and enjoy the live game streamers tend to exhibit higher rates of virtual gifting, and that viewers who use live games to pass the time tend to exhibit lower rates of virtual gifting, help to contribute to such a reframing of virtual gifting as a process of affective monetization [2, 10].

Beyond reframing the phenomenon of virtual gifting itself, this study makes another contribution to the theoretical literature in recognizing that the relationship between virtual gifting and live game stream platforms can be investigated on multiple levels of that streaming platform. For instance, while platform features are thought to be correlated with virtual gifting (as one of the main theoretical reasons that virtual gifting occurs), other factors associated with the streamers themselves, such as their attractiveness or their engagement with live game viewers, are also positively associated with virtual gifting of those streamers [7, 22]. Thus, this finding indicates that the study offers a conceptual organizing framework that helps classify antecedents into platform-related, streamer-related, and community-related domains. This framework should not be interpreted as a tested multi-level model, but as a way to organize fragmented construct-level evidence; one that recognizes that each of these groups has both a role in enabling and facilitating virtual gifting to occur [11, 38].

A third contribution of this study is the use of the findings to refine explanations of parasocial relationships. As explained in previous studies, the concept of parasocial relationships has often been used to explain the tendency of viewers to support the individuals who stream content on live streaming platforms [6]. However, the findings of this study suggest a different narrative altogether. Because the effect that was found to exist between parasocial relationships and live streaming gifting was only ever present at the individual level but not at the aggregate level, it is more appropriate to describe parasocial relationships as a phenomenon that has some influence upon live streaming gifting behaviors but is not a reliable contributor of such gifting activities to the platforms [2, 8]. Thus, while parasocial relationships may help to create the relationship between the streamers and the viewers, those relationships do not necessarily lead to revenue for the streamers through gifting activities from the viewers.

Finally, another use of the findings of this study is to contribute to the discussion of how parasocial relationships and live streaming gifting can be theoretically explained. Current research generally treats the concepts of motivation and gifting as the same phenomena, whether determined through surveys of the viewers or through the platforms of live streaming. Studies based on survey responses can help to determine the motivations of the viewers in relation to the live streaming content, while the live streaming platforms can reveal the actual behaviors of the viewers in relation to gifting to the streamers on those platforms [22, 38]. The distinction between these two types of research, therefore, helps to separate the discussion of live streaming gifting into psychological discussions of motivations and behaviors of the viewers, and business-oriented discussions of monetization of the revenue from those gifting viewers.

Practical implications

The findings of this research have implications for the actions that those involved in game streaming platforms can undertake to enhance the experiences of both streamers and viewers. These implications are derived from the findings of this research, but are not recommendations that are to be tested within the context of this research. Overall, virtual gifting is associated with viewers who are actively watching and engaging with the games that are being streamed, rather than with those who are passively viewing. Thus, platform operators may benefit from ensuring that the viewing and streaming experience encourages viewers to become more actively involved in the games that are being streamed and to contribute to the games in which they are interested [2, 22].

For those streaming platforms and their operators, these findings suggest that platforms may be more successful if they encourage viewers to become actively engaged in the games that are being streamed. As a result of the positive relationship between virtual gifting and viewer involvement with the streamed games, platforms may desire to incorporate features that encourage such involvement to increase the likelihood that viewers will participate in gifting virtual items to the streamers [60]. These findings also provide recommendations for platform operators regarding methods to determine the success of such platforms in relation to virtual gifting. Because the relationship between self-report data and secondary data from the platforms suggests a divergence between these methods of determining viewer behavior, platform operators should combine self-reported user insights with behavioral analytics, rather than relying exclusively on either source to manage their platforms; instead, behavioral analytics may provide more insightful information regarding virtual gifting within their platforms [38].

Regarding the findings for streamers and content creators, it is evident from the research that elements like personal appeal and performance can be considered resources that are valuable within the existing markets for streaming content. The finding that both attractiveness and engagement are positively associated with gifting indicates that streamers who are perceived as appealing and who maintain active interaction with viewers may be more likely to receive virtual gifts, although these relationships should not be interpreted causally [61]. Furthermore, the fact that the effect size for these variables was relatively high indicates that successful streamers are likely those who can maintain high levels of interaction and engagement with their viewers during their broadcasts (though this relationship was not statistically significant). However, the unstable results for the variable of parasocial relationships indicate that the relationship between streamers and viewers is not directly linked to gifting. Thus, streamers who aim to increase the amount of gifting that they receive are likely better off implementing strategies that increase the interaction between the streamers and their viewers [6].

Beyond the implications for the streamers themselves, however, the research also has implications for platform governance and regulation. Specifically, the findings of this research help to clarify that virtual gifting occurs within the platforms’ designed choices and environments. Thus, while virtual gifting is not inherently problematic, the findings suggest that those platforms should be regulated in relation to the impact that such design can have upon viewers and streamers [2]. Discussions of virtual gifting and its regulation may benefit from focusing on the design of those environments in which gifting occurs. For instance, platforms may consider implementing changes to the environment that relate to topics like spending information, controlling gifting, or creating optional safeguards for streamers [65].

Conclusion and future research directions

This paper conducts a systematic review and meta-analysis of the antecedents of virtual gifting within the context of live game streaming. The review of existing literature indicates that virtual gifting is related to factors involving active engagement and hedonic involvement with the live game streamers, but is not related to factors that relate to the general passing of time. Factors positively associated with virtual gifting include engagement, hedonic motivation, attractiveness, utility, social acceptance, and awareness of needs. These are the factors that relate to activated engagement and hedonic involvement with the live streamers. The variable of passing of time was negatively associated with virtual gifting. However, the relationship between virtual gifting and parasocial relationships was not statistically significant within the existing literature. Thus, virtual gifting is more consistently associated with activated engagement, social meaning, and perceived value than with passive or low-involvement viewing motives.

Based on these findings, future research should focus on several areas. First, despite the use of a framework that conceptualized the antecedents of virtual gifting across platform-, streamer-, and community-related domains, the present analysis did not examine potential interactions among these domains. Most existing studies have evaluated these antecedents independently, whereas future research may benefit from incorporating multi-level, longitudinal, or event-based designs to better understand how different antecedent domains interact in shaping virtual gifting behavior. Second, each antecedent category included in the present meta-analysis was based on a relatively limited number of studies, and several subgroup analyses were conducted using small subsets of effect sizes. Consequently, these subgroup findings should be interpreted cautiously, as they may have been influenced by individual studies, extreme values, or methodological inconsistencies across studies. Future research should therefore seek to replicate and extend these findings using larger and more diverse evidence bases across different platform types, cultures, streamer characteristics, and gifting contexts. Third, future studies should integrate measures of self-reported motivations with indicators of actual gifting behavior in order to better understand potential discrepancies between viewers’ stated intentions and observable platform behaviors. Fourth, future research should further examine the ethical and psychological implications of virtual gifting systems and platform monetization design. Finally, despite efforts to conduct a comprehensive literature search, some relevant studies may not have been identified due to the limited range of search terms employed. Future reviews should therefore utilize broader and more platform-specific terminology related to virtual gifting and monetization practices in live-streaming environments.
