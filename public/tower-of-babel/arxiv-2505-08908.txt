Abstract
Many researchers apply classical statistical decision theory to evaluate treatment choices and learn optimal policies. However, because this framework relies solely on realized outcomes under chosen actions and ignores counterfactuals, it cannot assess the quality of a decision relative to feasible alternatives at the unit level, which is an important requirement in some settings. For example, in pretrial bail decisions, a judge must balance crime prevention upon release against the risk of imposing unnecessary burdens on arrestees. A central challenge in this framework is identification: since only one potential outcome is observed per unit, counterfactual risk is typically not identifiable. We show that, under strong ignorability, counterfactual risk is identifiable if and only if the loss is additive in the potential outcomes. We further demonstrate that additive counterfactual losses can yield treatment recommendations that differ from those based on standard losses when more than two treatment options are available. We show that additive counterfactual losses capture not only decision accuracy but also decision difficulty, whereas standard losses reflect accuracy alone. Finally, we introduce a symbolic linear inverse program that determines whether a given counterfactual loss yields an identifiable risk, without requiring data. Keywords: causal inference, decision theory, counterfactual utility, identification, policy learning, potential outcomes, risk

Introduction

Statistical decision theory, pioneered by
Wald [1950]
,
formulates decision-making as a game against nature. A decision-maker
selects a decision
D=d
in the face of an unknown state of nature

\theta
, and incurs a prespecified
loss
(or negative utility)

\ell(d;\theta)
, which quantifies the consequence of the chosen
decision.
A better decision
corresponds to a lower loss. Given covariates
\bm{X}
, we can define a
decision rule
D=\pi(\bm{X})
that maps observed data to decisions. The
performance of a decision rule is commonly evaluated by its
risk
, that is, the expected loss:

R(\pi;\theta,\ell)\ :=\ \mathbb{E}\left[\ell(\pi(\bm{X});\theta)\right],

where the expectation is taken over the distribution of
\bm{X}
. Since

\theta
is unknown, the true risk is typically unidentifiable. This
motivates the use of optimality criteria such as admissibility,
minimax, minimax-regret, and Bayes risk

[
Wald, 1950
,
Savage, 1972
,
Berger, 1985
]
.
Statistical decision theory also provides a foundational framework for
classical estimation theory
[
Lehmann and Casella, 2006
, e.g.,]
and
hypothesis testing
[
Lehmann et al., 1986
, e.g.,]
.

In a series of influential papers,
Manski [2000]
,
Manski [2004]
,
Manski [2011]
applies this framework to
the problem of treatment choice. In its simplest form, the goal is to
choose a treatment
D
to minimize the expected loss associated with
an outcome of interest
Y
. Because the treatment may influence the
outcome, the loss
\ell(d;Y(d))
depends on the potential outcome

Y(d)
under the chosen treatment
d\in\mathcal{D}
and possibly the
treatment itself, where
\mathcal{D}
denotes the support of decision
D

[
Neyman, 1923
,
Rubin, 1974
]
. As before, we can
define a treatment rule
D=\pi(\bm{X})
based on observed covariates, and
evaluate its performance via the following risk:

R(\pi;\ell)\ =\ \mathbb{E}{\left[\ell(\pi(\bm{X});Y(\pi(\bm{X})))\right]},

(1)

where the expectation is taken over the joint distribution of
\bm{X}

and
Y(\pi(\bm{X}))
. Since only one potential outcome is observed per unit, i.e.,

Y=Y(D)
, the risk is generally unidentifiable unless the treatment
rule coincides with the one used to generate the data. Consequently,
additional assumptions, such as the strong ignorability of treatment
assignment
[
Rosenbaum and Rubin, 1983
]
, are required to identify the
risk and learn optimal treatment rules

[
Manski, 2000
,
Kitagawa and Tetenov, 2018
,
Athey and Wager, 2021
, e.g.,]
.

In this paper, we generalize the standard framework of statistical
decision theory for treatment choice by introducing a broader class of
loss functions. Specifically, we consider counterfactual losses, which
depend on the full set of potential outcomes rather than the outcome
under the selected treatment alone. That is, the loss takes the form
of
\ell(d;\{Y(d^{\prime})\}_{d^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}})
. We refer to this as
a
counterfactual loss
because it evaluates each decision in
light of the alternative outcomes that could have occurred had a
different treatment been chosen. Given this generalized loss, we
define the
counterfactual risk
for measuring the performance of
treatment rule
\pi
as:

R(\pi;\ell)\ =\ \mathbb{E}[\ell(\pi(\bm{X});\{Y(d)\}_{d\in\mathcal{D}})],

(2)

where the expectation is taken over the distribution of covariates

\bm{X}
and all potential outcomes
\{Y(d)\}_{d\in\mathcal{D}}
.

Counterfactual losses raise two foundational questions: coherence and
identification. Coherence asks whether decision making based on
counterfactual losses satisfies basic rationality requirements such as
transitivity. In a companion paper

[
Koch et al., 2026
]
, we
establish an axiomatic foundation for counterfactual loss by showing
that the preferences it induces are coherent and satisfy a
generalization of the von Neumann-–Morgenstern axioms

[
von Neumann and Morgenstern, 1944
]
. We briefly summarize this result in
Section
4
.

In this paper, we take the coherence of counterfactual loss as given
and study its identification. In contrast to the standard risk in
Equation (
1
), even under strong ignorability, the
counterfactual risk in Equation (
2
) is generally
not identifiable, due to its dependence on the joint distribution of
potential outcomes. We derive a structural condition on the
counterfactual loss that enables the identification of counterfactual
risk (Section
3
). Specifically, we consider a
class of counterfactual losses that are
additive
in the
potential outcomes. We show that under strong ignorability of
treatment assignment, additivity is both necessary and sufficient for
identifying counterfactual risk
differences
, and hence for
identifying an optimal policy within a given policy class.
Importantly, our results extend beyond the binary treatment and
outcome cases that have been a focus of the existing literature.

We next examine how restrictive the additivity condition is
(Section
5
). When decisions are binary,
i.e.,
|\mathcal{D}|=2
, we show that every additive counterfactual loss
admits an equivalent standard loss that induces the same ranking over
policies and hence the same optimal decision rule. By contrast, when
the decision problem involves more than two treatment options
(
|\mathcal{D}|>2
), additive counterfactual losses can induce rankings and
optimal policies that cannot be replicated by any standard loss. This
divergence arises because additive counterfactual losses capture both
the
accuracy
and the
difficulty
of a decision,
whereas standard losses reflect accuracy alone. Furthermore, we show
that, under binary outcomes and a monotonicity restriction, every
counterfactual loss is additive.

Finally, we formulate a symbolic linear inverse problem that offers a
practical test for whether a given loss is additive and hence its risk
identifiable (Section
6
). This test does not require
any data, and the solution to this linear inverse problem provides a
closed-form decomposition of counterfactual risk into identifiable
marginal distributions of potential outcomes.

Related Literature.

The application of counterfactual logic to decision-theoretic problems
has appeared in several strands of the literature. One of the most
prominent examples of counterfactual loss is the notion of regret,

\ell(d;\{Y(d^{\prime})\}_{d^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}})=\max_{d^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}}Y(d^{\prime})-Y(d)

[
Savage, 1972
]
. Yet, because of the
aforementioned identification challenge, prior work has mostly focused
on the settings with binary treatment and binary outcome. For example,

Coston et al. [2020]
and
Ben-Michael et al. [2025]

consider the counterfactual loss that is a function of the baseline
potential outcome
Y(0)
alone. A more general binary case has been
studied by
Li and Pearl [2019]
,
Li and Pearl [2022]
,
Richens et al. [2022]
,
Kallus [2022]
,
Mueller and Pearl [2023]
,
Ben-Michael et al. [2024]
,
who address non-identifiability through partial-identification bounds
and by leveraging observational data in some cases. In addition,

Christy and Kowalski [2024]
,
Christy and Kowalski [2026]
circumvent
non-identifiability by evaluating decisions with a finite-sample
maximum-likelihood rule.

The most relevant work in the binary case is
Li and Pearl [2019]
which
provides a sufficient condition for identification. We show that this
condition follows directly from our general identification result and
it is indeed necessary for the identification of counterfactual risk.
However, our result implies that their condition can be relaxed if the
goal is to identify a counterfactual risk difference and can be
expressed as a mathematically equivalent standard loss.

Extensions to non-binary treatments have been studied by

Li and Pearl [2024a]
,
Li and Pearl [2024b]
. Specifically,

Li and Pearl [2024a]
derive partial-identification bounds,
while
Li and Pearl [2024b]
propose a non-constructive algorithm that
numerically tests identifiability using experimental data. In
contrast, we provide a closed-form, analytical characterization of
counterfactual losses that is both necessary and sufficient for the
identification of counterfactual risk. We also extend these results
to the identification of counterfactual risk difference, which
suffices for learning optimal treatment rules. Our identification
results lead to a symbolic linear inverse problem formulation that can
be used, without data, to verify whether a counterfactual risk (or the
difference between any pair of counterfactual risks) is
identifiable. We further show that in the non-binary treatment case,
an identifiable counterfactual loss need not reduce to a standard
loss.

2
Illustrative Examples

Before presenting our general identification analysis in
Section
3
, we use illustrative examples to
highlight the differences between standard and counterfactual losses. We begin with a binary decision example and then consider a trichotomous decision example. Numerical illustrations are provided in Appendix
A
.

2.1
Binary Decisions

Consider a stylized setting, in which a physician must decide whether to
approve (
D=1
) a new medical treatment for a population of at-risk
individuals, or not (
D=0
). The outcome of interest is survival,
denoted by
Y
, where
Y=1
indicates survival and
Y=0

otherwise. For simplicity, we assume no covariates are observed. We introduce standard, counterfactual, and additive counterfactual
losses in the context of this example.

2.1.1
Standard Loss

Suppose that we evaluate the physician’s decision using the standard
loss
\ell^{\textsc{Std}}(D;Y(D))
, which assigns a value to each realized
decision–outcome pair
(D,Y(D))
. Let
c_{1}>0
denote the cost of
providing treatment relative to the cost of no intervention
c_{0}=0
.
We use
\ell_{y}
to denote the loss associated with the realized
outcome
Y(D)=y
. For example, if the treatment is administered and
the patient dies, the loss is given by

\ell^{\textsc{Std}}(1;0)=\ell_{0}+c_{1}
. If no treatment is given and the
patient survives, the loss is
\ell^{\textsc{Std}}(0;1)=\ell_{1}
. Since
survival is preferred, we assume
\ell_{1}<\ell_{0}
.

This yields the standard loss function,

\displaystyle\ell^{\textsc{Std}}(D;Y(D))=\ell_{Y(D)}+c_{D}.

(3)

An optimal decision
d^{\ast}
minimizes the standard risk,

\displaystyle d^{\ast}=\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{d\in\{0,1\}}R(d;\ell^{\textsc{Std}}),

where
R(d;\ell^{\textsc{Std}})=\mathbb{E}{\left[\ell^{\textsc{Std}}(d;Y(d))\right]}
. The classical A/B
test is a special case of this standard loss where

\ell^{\textsc{Std}}(D;Y(D))=-Y(D)
, reducing risk minimization to the
comparison of average survival rates.

2.1.2
Counterfactual Loss

The standard loss
\ell^{\textsc{Std}}
only considers the realized outcome
under the chosen decision. In contrast, the physician may decide to use
a counterfactual loss that depends on both potential outcomes
simultaneously. This naturally leads to the concept of
principal
strata
, which classifies individuals according to their joint
potential outcomes
[
Frangakis and Rubin, 2002
]
:

•

Never survivors

(Y(0),Y(1))=(0,0)
: patients who
would not survive regardless of treatment

•

Responders

(Y(0),Y(1))=(0,1)
: patients who
would survive only if given the treatment

•

Harmed

(Y(0),Y(1))=(1,0)
: patients who would
survive only if not treated

•

Always survivors

(Y(0),Y(1))=(1,1)
: patients
who would survive regardless of treatment

Building on this idea, several authors have considered the following
counterfactual loss
[
Li and Pearl, 2019
,
Ben-Michael et al., 2024
,
Christy and Kowalski, 2024
]
:

\displaystyle\ell^{\textsc{Cf}}(D;Y(0),Y(1))

\displaystyle=(1-Y(0))(1-Y(1))\ell_{0}+(1-Y(0))Y(1)\ell_{D}^{\text{R}}

(4)

\displaystyle+Y(0)(1-Y(1))\ell_{1-D}^{\text{H}}+Y(0)Y(1)\ell_{1}+c_{D}.

This generalizes the standard loss given in
Equation (
3
), which is obtained as a special
case by setting
\ell_{D}^{\text{R}}=\ell_{D}
and
\ell_{1-D}^{\text{H}}=\ell_{1-D}
.

Unlike the standard loss, therefore, the counterfactual loss
incorporates the information about whether the treatment would alter
the outcome. For example, responders and always survivors both incur
the same standard loss
\ell^{\textsc{Std}}(1;1)=\ell_{1}+c_{1}
under the
treatment, but their counterfactual losses may differ (e.g.,

\ell_{1}^{\text{R}}+c_{1}<\ell_{1}+c_{1}
if we reward the correct
decision).
Ben-Michael et al. [2024]
design a
counterfactual loss that reflects a version of the Hippocratic Oath
principle of “Do no harm”
[
Wiens et al., 2019
]
, where killing a
patient is worse than failing to provide life-saving treatment, i.e.,

\ell_{0}^{\text{R}}-\ell_{1}^{\text{R}}<\ell_{0}^{\text{H}}-\ell_{1}^{\text{H}}
.

Counterfactual loss can also reveal heterogeneity that standard loss
may obscure. For example,
Mueller and Pearl [2023]
exhibit a
setting in which men and women have the same standard risk difference,

R(1;\ell^{\textsc{Std}})-R(0;\ell^{\textsc{Std}})
, yet the treatment is safe for
women but harmful for men
[
Kallus, 2022
, see also]
. Deploying
such a policy could therefore be ethically problematic, yet this issue
would not be detected by standard loss (and hence by a classical A/B
test).

Despite its ability to express rich preferences, the main limitation
of counterfactual loss is identifiability since principal strata are
never observed
[
Sarvet and Stensrud, 2023
,
Dawid, 2000
]
. As a
result, the literature primarily focused on partial identification

[
Li and Pearl, 2019
,
Li and Pearl, 2024a
,
Ben-Michael et al., 2024
, e.g.,]
.

We propose an alternative strategy to non-identifiability by
restricting the class of counterfactual losses to those that are
additive in the potential outcomes. We now provide an example of
additive counterfactual loss by extending a counterfactual loss
considered in the literature
[
Coston et al., 2020
,
Ben-Michael et al., 2025
]
that depends on the baseline potential
outcome
Y(0)
alone. Under this formulation, the decision is a false
negative if the physician does not provide the treatment and the
patient dies,
(D,Y(0))=(0,0)
, in which case the loss is
\ell_{0}

since
Y(0)
is the realized outcome in this case. Conversely, a false
positive occurs when the physician provides the treatment even though
the patient would have survived without it,
(D,Y(0))=(1,1)
. In
this case,
Y(0)
is counterfactual, and we denote the associated loss
as
\tilde{\ell}_{1}
. Under this setup,

Ben-Michael et al. [2025]
propose the following counterfactual
classification loss:

\ell(D;Y(0))=(1-D)(1-Y(0))\ell_{0}+D\cdot Y(0)\tilde{\ell}_{1}+c_{D}.

(5)

As illustrated in Table
1
, this counterfactual loss
can be further generalized. First, we may also incorporate losses
based on the potential outcome under treatment, covering cases where

(D,Y(1))=(0,1)
(false negative) or
(D,Y(1))=(1,0)
(false
positive). Second, we may consider rewards (negative losses) for
correct decisions:
(D,Y(0))=(1,0)
and
(D,Y(1))=(1,1)
are
true positives, while
(D,Y(0))=(0,1)
and
(D,Y(1))=(0,0)
are
true negatives. Letting
\tilde{\ell}_{0}
denote the loss incurred when a
patient would have died under the alternative (unchosen) decision, a
more general counterfactual loss can be written as:

\ell^{\textsc{Add}}(D;Y(0),Y(1))=\ell_{Y(D)}+\tilde{\ell}_{Y(1-D)}+c_{D}.

(6)

This formulation is additive in the potential outcomes. While it does
not fully capture stratum-specific interactions, additive
counterfactual loss does account for the notion of counterfactual
regret. For example, if
\tilde{\ell}_{0}<\tilde{\ell}_{1}
, this reflects a
regret for not choosing the decision that would save the patient.

Despite these differences, as shown later, in the binary decision
case, every additive counterfactual loss has an equivalent standard
loss. For example, the additive counterfactual loss in
Equation (
6
) is equivalent to

\ell^{\textsc{Equ}}(D;Y(D))=\ell_{Y(D)}-\tilde{\ell}_{Y(D)}+c_{D}
, because we have

\ell^{\textsc{Add}}(D;Y(0),Y(1))-\ell^{\textsc{Equ}}(D;Y(D))=\tilde{\ell}_{Y(1-D)}+\tilde{\ell}_{Y(D)}=\tilde{\ell}_{Y(0)}+\tilde{\ell}_{Y(1)},

which does not depend on the decision. Hence,

R(d;\ell^{\textsc{Add}})
and
R(d;\ell^{\textsc{Equ}})
induce the same
ordering of treatment rules.

2.2
Trichotomous Decision

We next move beyond binary decisions and illustrate counterfactual additive loss in the case of trichotomous
decisions. We introduce a different stylized example taken from
criminal justice
[
Imai et al., 2023
, see e.g.,]
. Consider a first appearance hearing, where a judge
must decide whether to release an arrestee on their own recognizance
or ROR (
D=0
), impose supervision (e.g., monitoring via an electronic
device) as a condition of release (
D=1
), or detain the arrestee
until trial (
D=2
). As before, let
Y
be the binary outcome of
interest, where
Y=0
indicates a negative outcome (e.g., the arrestee
commits a crime before trial) and
Y=1
represents a positive outcome
(e.g., no new crime). For simplicity, we assume that detention
prevents crime, i.e.,
Y(2)=1
, though this assumption can be
relaxed. We also assume no covariates are observed.

2.2.1
Standard Loss

Suppose a judge seeks to balance the social cost of crime against the costs of supervision and incarceration, which are borne at least in part by arrestees.
Let
\ell_{y}
denote the
loss associated with outcome
Y=y
. Since we would like to avoid
crime, we assume
\ell_{1}<\ell_{0}
. Let
c_{d}
be the cost of decision

d
. The cost of detention is assumed to be greater than that of
supervision, which in turn is larger than the cost of ROR, i.e.,

c_{0}<c_{1}<c_{2}
. The corresponding standard loss is given by,

\ell^{\textsc{Std}}(D;Y(D))=\ell_{Y(D)}+c_{D}.

2.2.2
Counterfactual Loss

This standard loss does not account for two important considerations a
judge must weigh:
harm to the public
and
harm to the
arrestee
. Harm to the public arises when a judge’s decision leads
to a crime, whereas harm to the arrestee occurs when the decision
imposes unnecessary costs through supervision or detention. It is
natural to assume that a judge seeks to minimize both types of
harm. The following counterfactual loss incorporates these two notions of harm,

\displaystyle\ell^{\textsc{Cf}}(D;Y(0),Y(1),Y(2))

(7)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ell_{Y(D)}+c_{D}+\mathds{1}{\left\{D=0\right\}}\,(1-Y(0))Y(1)p_{0}+\mathds{1}{\left\{D=1\right\}}\,Y(0)(1-Y(1))p_{1}+\sum_{k<D}r_{D,k}Y(k),

where
p_{d}
is the additional penalty for causing harm to the public
with decision
D=d
for
d=0,1
(recall that
D=2
is assumed to lead
to no crime), and
r_{d,k}
represents the regret for imposing an
unnecessarily harsh decision
d
when a more lenient decision
k<d

would lead to no crime.

2.2.3
Additive Counterfactual Loss

Since the additional penalty term for the harm to the public depends
on joint potential outcomes, the counterfactual risk based on
Equation (
7
) is not identifiable.
However, the regret term for the harm to the arrestee does not involve
joint potential outcomes and hence can be incorporated into an
additive counterfactual loss in the following manner,

\displaystyle\ell^{\textsc{Add}}(D;Y(0),Y(1),Y(2))

\displaystyle=\ell_{Y(D)}+c_{D}+\sum_{k<D}r_{D,k}Y(k).

(8)

3
Identification under Strong Ignorability

We now formally define counterfactual loss and risk, and introduce the
class of additive counterfactual losses, which depend additively on
the potential outcomes. We show that additivity is both necessary and
sufficient for identification.

3.1
Setup

Consider a setting in which, for a unit with pre-treatment covariates

\bm{X}\in\mathcal{X}
, a decision
D\in\mathcal{D}:=\{0,1,\ldots,K-1\}
is chosen
and an outcome
Y\in\mathcal{Y}:=\{0,1,\ldots,M-1\}
is observed. Here,

\mathcal{X}
is an arbitrary measurable space, and we assume
K,M\geq 2
. Let
Y(d)\in\mathcal{Y}
denote the potential outcome under decision

d\in\mathcal{D}
. The vector of potential outcomes and covariates has an unknown law,

(Y(0),\ldots,Y(K-1),\bm{X})\sim P_{Y(\mathcal{D}),\bm{X}}\in\Delta(\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}}\times\mathcal{X}),

where
\Delta(\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}}\times\mathcal{X})
denotes the set of
all probability measures on
\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}}\times\mathcal{X}
. We view this law as
describing an unknown superpopulation, while treating potential
outcomes as fixed for a given unit

[
Neyman, 1923
,
Rubin, 1974
]
.

The observed data are generated under the realized decision
D
via a

treatment assignment mechanism

e:\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}}\times\mathcal{X}\to\Delta(\mathcal{D})
, where
\Delta(\mathcal{D})
denotes the
set of probability distributions on
\mathcal{D}
. Given

P_{Y(\mathcal{D}),\bm{X}}\in\Delta(\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}}\times\mathcal{X})
, define the induced

joint
law
P
of
(D,Y(0),\ldots,Y(K-1),\bm{X})
as

\displaystyle P(D=k,Y(\mathcal{D})=\bm{y},\bm{X}\in A)=\int_{A}e(k;\bm{y},\bm{x})\cdot P_{Y(\mathcal{D})\mid\bm{X}}(Y(\mathcal{D})=\bm{y}\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x})dP_{\bm{X}}(\bm{X}=\bm{x}),

(9)

for all
k\in\mathcal{D},\bm{y}=(y_{0},\ldots,y_{K-1})\in\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}}
and measurable
A\subseteq\mathcal{X}
, where
Y(\mathcal{D})=(Y(0),\ldots,Y(K-1))
.

We study the identifiability of counterfactual risk under the standard consistency and strong ignorability assumptions
[
Rosenbaum and Rubin, 1983
]
on the joint law
P
.

Assumption 1
(Consistency)

The observed outcome satisfies:

Y=Y(D)
.

Assumption 2
(Strong Ignorability)

The observed decision satisfies:

(a)

Unconfoundedness:
D\mbox{$\perp\!\!\!\perp$}\{Y(d)\}_{d\in\mathcal{D}}\mid\bm{X}
; equivalently,

e(d;\bm{y},\bm{x})=e(d;\bm{x})
for every
d\in\mathcal{D}
,

P_{Y(\mathcal{D}),\bm{X}}
-almost surely.

(b)

Overlap:
e(d;\bm{x})>0
for every
d\in\mathcal{D}
,
P_{\bm{X}}
-almost surely.

Assumption
1
links the observed outcome to the
potential outcomes through the realized decision.
Assumption
2
(a)
implies
that, conditional on covariates, the observed decision
D
is
independent of the potential outcomes while
Assumption
2
(b)
requires that each
decision occurs with positive probability conditional on covariates.

Given
P_{Y(\mathcal{D}),\bm{X}}\in\Delta(\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}}\times\mathcal{X})
, define

\mathcal{E}(P_{Y(\mathcal{D}),\bm{X}})
as the set of assignment mechanisms that
satisfy Assumption
2
with respect to

P_{Y(\mathcal{D}),\bm{X}}
.
Define the following set of all joint laws compatible with our assumptions,

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}:=\{P:P=e\cdot P_{Y(\mathcal{D}),\bm{X}},P_{Y(\mathcal{D}),\bm{X}}\in\Delta(\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}}\times\mathcal{X}),e\in\mathcal{E}(P_{Y(\mathcal{D}),\bm{X}})\},

where
P=e\cdot P_{Y(\mathcal{D}),\bm{X}}
denotes the joint law induced by
(e,P_{Y(\mathcal{D}),\bm{X}})
through Equation (
9
).
Note that these assumptions restrict the assignment mechanism but place no restrictions on the law of the potential outcomes and covariates.

Although the observed data are generated under the realized decision

D
, our aim is to evaluate a given, possibly randomized

policy

\pi:\mathcal{X}\to\Delta(\mathcal{D})
, which may differ from the
observed assignment mechanism
e
. The policy
\pi
induces a
decision
D^{\pi}
according to

D^{\pi}\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x}\sim\pi(\cdot;\bm{x}).

Similar to the assignment mechanism, we assume that any policy
randomization is independent of the unit’s potential outcomes
conditional on the covariates. That is, if
D^{\pi}=g(\bm{X},U)
, where

U\sim\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)
, then

U\mbox{$\perp\!\!\!\perp$}(Y(0),\ldots,Y(K-1))\mid\bm{X}
.

3.2
Counterfactual Loss and Risk

We define counterfactual losses, which depend on the full vector of potential outcomes, and contrast them with standard losses, which depend only on the realized outcome under the chosen decision. A counterfactual loss induces a counterfactual risk that depends on the unknown joint distribution of potential outcomes, whereas a standard loss induces a standard risk that depends only on the observed marginal distribution of the realized outcome.

We define a loss (or negative utility) associated with a decision as a
function of the covariates and all potential outcomes, where a lower
loss value indicates a better decision. We refer to this as the

counterfactual loss
, as it depends on the full vector of
potential outcomes.

Definition 1
(Counterfactual Loss)

A counterfactual loss is a measurable function

\ell:\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
, where

\ell(d;\bm{y},\bm{x})
denotes the loss of
choosing decision
D=d
for a unit with potential
outcomes
Y(\mathcal{D})=\bm{y}

and covariates
\bm{X}=\bm{x}
.

A subclass of counterfactual losses are
standard losses
, which depend only on the realized outcome under the chosen decision, i.e.,
\ell^{\textsc{Std}}(d;\bm{y},\bm{x})=\ell^{\textsc{Std}}(d;y_{d},\bm{x})
.

Since our goal is to study identification uniformly over all
P\in\mathcal{P}
, we impose a boundedness condition on the loss to accommodate the fact that
\mathcal{X}
need not be finite.

Assumption 3
(Bounded Counterfactual Loss)

The counterfactual loss
\ell
satisfies

\|\ell\|_{\infty}=\sup_{d\in\mathcal{D},\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}},\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}}|\ell(d;\bm{y},\bm{x})|<\infty.

Next, we define the
counterfactual risk
, or the expected
counterfactual loss, which serves as the primary estimand of
interest. We also define the
conditional counterfactual risk
,
which conditions on the observed covariates.

Definition 2
(Counterfactual Risk and Conditional Counterfactual Risk)

Given a joint law
P\in\mathcal{P}
and a counterfactual loss
\ell
, the counterfactual risk
R_{P}(\pi;\ell)
of policy
\pi:\mathcal{X}\to\Delta(\mathcal{D})
is given by,

\displaystyle R_{P}(\pi;\ell)

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}_{P^{\pi}}[\ell(D^{\pi};Y(0),\ldots,Y(K-1),\bm{X})]=\mathbb{E}_{P_{\bm{X}}}[R_{P_{Y(\mathcal{D})\mid\bm{X}}}(\pi;\ell)],

where,

\displaystyle R_{P_{Y(\mathcal{D})\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x}}}(\pi;\ell):=\sum_{d\in\mathcal{D}}\sum_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}}}\ell(d;\bm{y},\bm{x})\cdot\pi(d;\bm{x})\cdot P(Y(0)=y_{0},\ldots,Y(K-1)=y_{K-1}\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x}),

is the conditional counterfactual risk defined almost surely with
respect to
P_{\bm{X}}
.

Definition
2
evaluates a fixed policy by its expected counterfactual loss. The expectation is taken with respect to the joint law
(D^{\pi},Y(0),\ldots,Y(K-1),\bm{X})\sim P^{\pi}
where

P^{\pi}(D^{\pi}=d,Y(\mathcal{D})=\bm{y},\bm{X}\in A)=\int_{A}\pi(d;\bm{x})\cdot P_{Y(\mathcal{D})\mid\bm{X}}(Y(\mathcal{D})=\bm{y}\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x})\,dP_{\bm{X}}(\bm{X}=\bm{x}).

The policy
\pi
determines the conditional distribution of the
decision given the covariates, while
P_{Y(\mathcal{D}),\bm{X}}
specifies the
(unknown) joint distribution of potential outcomes and covariates. We
therefore view
P_{Y(\mathcal{D}),\bm{X}}
as the unknown
state of nature

from statistical decision theory
[
Wald, 1950
,
Berger, 1985
,
Manski, 2004
,
Stoye, 2009
, e.g.,]
. Note that
P
and

P^{\pi}
have the same marginal law of potential outcomes and
covariates, i.e.,
P_{Y(\mathcal{D}),\bm{X}}=P^{\pi}_{Y(\mathcal{D}),\bm{X}}
. Similarly,
we refer to the risk induced by a standard loss as a
standard
risk
.

Following the literature
[
Li and Pearl, 2019
,
Coston et al., 2020
,
Mueller and Pearl, 2023
,
Ben-Michael et al., 2024
, e.g.,]
, we treat
\pi
as fixed rather than learned from observational data. If instead the policy was estimated from a sample
\mathcal{S}_{n}=\{(D_{i},Y_{i},\bm{X}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}\sim Q_{P}^{n}
, where

Q_{P}
denotes the observable law induced by
P\in\mathcal{P}
, we would additionally average over the realization of the sample

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{P}(\hat{\pi};\ell)=\mathbb{E}_{Q_{P}^{n}}[R_{P}(\hat{\pi}(\mathcal{S}_{n});\ell)],

(10)

where
\hat{\pi}(\mathcal{S}_{n}):\mathcal{X}\to\Delta(\mathcal{D})
is a

statistical decision rule

[
Berger, 1985
, Section
1.3]
that maps a sample to a policy. We leave
the study of this finite sample counterfactual risk to future work

[see e.g.,
Berger, 1985
,
Manski, 2004
,
Stoye, 2011
,
Kitagawa and Tetenov, 2018
, for this finite sample standard
risk]
.

3.3
Additivity

The main obstacle to identifying counterfactual risk is that under a counterfactual loss the risk may depend on the joint distribution of all potential outcomes, even though only a single realized outcome is observed. To overcome this identification problem we consider a class of loss functions that depends additively on the potential outcomes.

Definition 3
(Additive Counterfactual Loss)

A counterfactual loss is
additive
if it admits the
following decomposition,

\displaystyle\ell^{\textsc{Add}}(d;\bm{y},\bm{x}):=\varpi(\bm{y},\bm{x})+\sum_{k\in\mathcal{D}}\omega_{k}(d,y_{k},\bm{x}),

where
\omega_{k}:\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
\varpi:\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}}\times\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
are bounded, measurable functions. We call
\omega_{k}
the weight function and
\varpi
the
intercept function.

The additive counterfactual loss equals the sum of two terms; the
intercept function does not depend on the decision, while
the weight function does. Moreover, each weight function
\omega_{k}

depends only on the corresponding potential outcome given the
covariate, whereas the intercept function may include all possible
interactions of potential outcomes. Although we cannot identify the
intercept function, we can ignore it when comparing alternative
decision rules because it does not depend on the decision. We
generally refer to the counterfactual risk induced by an additive
counterfactual loss as
additive counterfactual risk
.

We now illustrate Definition
3
by revisiting the examples shown in Section
2
.

Example 1
(Counterfactual Classification
Loss)

The
counterfactual loss function given in
Equation (
5
) is additive in the potential
outcomes with
\omega_{0}(0,0)=\ell_{0}
,

\omega_{0}(1,1)=\tilde{\ell}_{1}+c_{1}
,
\omega_{1}(d,y_{1})=0
for

d,y_{1}\in\{0,1\}
, and
\varpi(\bm{y})=0
. An extension of this
counterfactual loss given in
Equation (
6
) is also additive
with
\omega_{d}(d,y_{d})=\ell_{y_{d}}+c_{d}
,

\omega_{1-d}(d,y_{1-d})=\tilde{\ell}_{y_{1-d}}
for

d,y\in\{0,1\}
, and
\varpi(\bm{y})=0
.

Example 2
(Asymmetric Counterfactual
Loss)

The asymmetric counterfactual loss
given in Equation (
4
) is an example of
non-additive loss whenever
\ell_{0}^{\text{R}}-\ell_{1}^{\text{R}}\neq\ell_{0}^{\text{H}}-\ell_{1}^{\text{H}}

(see Equation (

) in Section
6.2
for details). When

\ell_{0}^{\text{R}}-\ell_{1}^{\text{R}}=\ell_{0}^{\text{H}}-\ell_{1}^{\text{H}}=\kappa
, the loss is
additive with
\omega_{d}(d,y_{d})=\ell_{y_{d}}+c_{d}
,

\omega_{1-d}(d,y_{1-d})=\{\kappa-(\ell_{0}-\ell_{1})\}y_{1-d}
, and

\varpi(\bm{y})=(\ell_{1}^{\text{H}}-\ell_{1})y_{0}(1-y_{1})+(\ell_{1}^{\text{R}}-\ell_{1})(1-y_{0})y_{1}-\{\kappa-(\ell_{0}-\ell_{1})\}y_{0}y_{1}
. Note that
\kappa-(\ell_{0}-\ell_{1})
is the
difference in loss between units for whom the treatment matters and

Example 3
(Trichotomous decision)

The counterfactual loss for individual harm, given in

\omega_{d}(d,y_{d})=\ell_{y_{d}}+c_{d}
,

\omega_{k}(d,y_{k})=r_{d,k}y_{k}\mathds{1}{\left\{k<d\right\}}
for

, and
\varpi(\bm{y})=0
. By
contrast, the extended counterfactual loss given in

p_{0}=p_{1}=0
, in which case it reduces to
Equation (

).

3.4
Nonparametric Identification

We now study when counterfactual risk is identifiable under strong
ignorability. We show that additive losses are sufficient to identify
counterfactual risk differences and are also necessary for uniform
identification for every
P\in\mathcal{P}
.

The fundamental problem of causal inference is that only one potential
outcome is observed for each unit
[
Holland, 1986
]
. Although the
full data are governed by
(D,Y(0),\ldots,Y(K-1),\bm{X})\sim P
for some
P\in\mathcal{P}
,
we observe only
(D,Y,\bm{X})
, where
Y=Y(D)
by
Assumption
1
. For
P\in\mathcal{P}
, let
Q_{P}
denote the
induced observed law

Q_{P}(D=k,Y=y,\bm{X}\in A):=P(D=k,Y(k)=y,\bm{X}\in A).

We adapt the following definition of identification from

Basse and Bojinov [2020]
to our setting. A more
general definition is given in
Lemma
1

(Appendix
B.8.1
).

Definition 4
(Identifiability)

Let
\theta:\mathcal{P}\to\mathbb{R}
be an estimand. We say that
\theta
is
identifiable
if every pair
P_{1},P_{2}\in\mathcal{P}
that induce the same observed law,
Q_{P_{1}}=Q_{P_{2}}
, also have the same value,
\theta(P_{1})=\theta(P_{2})
.

According to Definition
4
, an estimand is identifiable if its value is invariant across all admissible joint distributions that induce the same observable law. In our setting, the counterfactual risk
R_{P}(\pi;\ell)
is identifiable if and only if any two admissible joint laws
P_{1},P_{2}\in\mathcal{P}
, with the same induced observed law
Q_{P_{1}}=Q_{P_{2}}
, have the same counterfactual risk
R_{P_{1}}(\pi;\ell)=R_{P_{2}}(\pi;\ell)
.

Equivalently, by Lemma
1
(Appendix
B.8.1
),
R_{P}(\pi;\ell)
is identifiable if and only if it can be expressed as a function of the observable distribution, i.e.,
R_{P}(\pi;\ell)=h_{\pi,\ell}(Q_{P})
.
Furthermore, as formalized and shown in Lemma
2
(Appendix
B.8.2
), the counterfactual risk
R_{P}(\pi;\ell)
is identifiable with respect to
Q_{P}
if and only if the conditional counterfactual risk
R_{P_{Y(\mathcal{D})\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x}}}(\pi;\ell)
is identifiable with respect to
Q_{P}(D,Y\mid\bm{X})
.

While estimands that depend on the joint distribution of multiple
potential outcomes are generally not identifiable,
Assumption
1
and
2
imply that
the following conditional distribution is identified,

P(Y(k)=y\mid\bm{X})=P(Y=y\mid D=k,\bm{X})=Q_{P}(Y=y\mid D=k,\bm{X}),

for every
k\in\mathcal{D}
and
y\in\mathcal{Y}
. This observation suggests that the additivity assumption given in Definition
3
can facilitate identification.

We first show that for an additive loss, we can identify the
difference
in conditional counterfactual risk between any two
decision rules even though each conditional counterfactual risk is
only identifiable up to an unknown additive constant. In other words,
the conditional counterfactual risk of a policy
\pi
can be
decomposed into an identifiable part and an unidentifiable part, and
the latter does not depend on
\pi
.

Theorem 1
(The Conditional Counterfactual Risk under the Additive Loss)

Fix a joint law
P\in\mathcal{P}
and a policy
\pi:\mathcal{X}\to\Delta(\mathcal{D})
. Suppose that a counterfactual loss function
\ell^{\textsc{Add}}
takes the additive form given in Definition
3
. Then, under Assumptions
1
–
3
, the conditional counterfactual risk can be decomposed as,

\displaystyle R_{P_{Y(\mathcal{D})\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x}}}(\pi;\ell^{\textsc{Add}})=\sum_{d\in\mathcal{D}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{D}}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\omega_{k}(d,y,\bm{x})\cdot\pi(d;\bm{x})\cdot P(Y=y\mid D=k,\bm{X}=\bm{x})+C_{P_{Y(\mathcal{D})\mid\bm{X}}}(\bm{x}).

where

C_{P_{Y(\mathcal{D})\mid\bm{X}}}(\bm{x})=\sum_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}}}\varpi(\bm{y},\bm{x})\cdot P(Y(\mathcal{D})=\bm{y}\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x}),

Thus, the difference of conditional counterfactual risks between any pair of policies can be identified.

The proof is given in
Appendix
B.1
.

Taking the expectation of the expression in Theorem
1
over the distribution of covariates, we find that the counterfactual risk is identified up to an unknown additive constant
\mathbb{E}_{P_{\bm{X}}}[C_{P_{Y(\mathcal{D})\mid\bm{X}}}(\bm{X})]
. We state this result as the following corollary without proof.

Corollary 1
(The Counterfactual Risk under the Additive Loss)

Fix a joint law
P\in\mathcal{P}
and a policy
\pi:\mathcal{X}\to\Delta(\mathcal{D})
. Suppose that a counterfactual loss function
\ell^{\textsc{Add}}
takes the additive form given in Definition
3
. Then, under Assumptions
1
–
3
, the counterfactual risk can be decomposed as,

\displaystyle R_{P}(\pi;\ell^{\textsc{Add}})=\sum_{d\in\mathcal{D}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{D}}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\mathbb{E}_{P_{\bm{X}}}\left[\omega_{k}(d,y,\bm{X})\cdot\pi(d;\bm{X})\cdot P(Y=y\mid D=k,\bm{X})\right]+\mathbb{E}_{P_{\bm{X}}}[C_{P_{Y(\mathcal{D})\mid\bm{X}}}(\bm{X})].

Thus, the difference in counterfactual risk between any pair of
decision rules is identified.

Theorem
1
immediately implies that given
P\in\mathcal{P}
, one can find an optimal policy by minimizing the identifiable term alone,

\displaystyle\pi^{\text{opt}}\ \in

\displaystyle\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{\pi\in\Pi}R_{P}(\pi;\ell^{\textsc{Add}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{\pi\in\Pi}\sum_{d\in\mathcal{D}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{D}}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\mathbb{E}_{P_{\bm{X}}}\left[\omega_{k}(d,y,\bm{X})\cdot\pi(d;\bm{X})\cdot P(Y=y\mid D=k,\bm{X})\right],

where
\Pi
is a class of policies to be considered. Moreover,
if the constant term is zero, then we can exactly identify the
counterfactual risk. This happens, for example, when the intercept
function in the additive counterfactual loss
(Definition
3
) is zero, i.e.,
\varpi(\bm{y},\bm{x})=0

for all
\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}}
and
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}
. Lastly, by placing
assumptions on
\varpi
, we can control the magnitude of

\mathbb{E}_{P_{\bm{X}}}[C_{P_{Y(\mathcal{D})\mid\bm{X}}}(\bm{X})]
, which leads to partial identification results.

A natural question arises as to whether or not the above
identification results can be obtained under a different form of
counterfactual loss function. The next theorem shows that the
additivity of the loss function given in Definition
3

is a necessary and sufficient condition for the identification of
difference in counterfactual risk. In other words, every
counterfactual risk that is identifiable up to an additive constant
must have an additive loss.

Theorem 2
(Additivity as Necessary and Sufficient Condition for Identification)

Fix a counterfactual loss
\ell
. Under
Assumptions
1
–
3
, the risk
difference,
R_{P}(\pi;\ell)-R_{P}(\rho;\ell)
, is identifiable in
the sense of Definition
4
for every pair of
policies
\pi,\rho:\mathcal{X}\to\Delta(\mathcal{D})
if and only if the loss

\ell
admits the additive representation given in
Definition
3
.

The proof is given in Appendix
B.2
and it shows that it suffices to prove Theorem
2
for all pairs of
deterministic policies

\pi_{d}:=d\in\mathcal{D}
.

Finally, as a corollary of this identification result, we show that
the exact identification of the counterfactual risk is possible
if and only if
there exists an equivalent counterfactual loss that
sets the intercept function
\varpi
to zero
(Definition
3
).

Corollary 2
(Necessary and Sufficient Condition for Exact Identification)

The counterfactual risk given in Definition
2
is identifiable under Assumptions
1
–
3
for every policy
\pi:\mathcal{X}\to\Delta(\mathcal{D})
if and only if the loss admits the following form,

\displaystyle\ell^{\textsc{Add}}(d;\bm{y},\bm{x})=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{D}}\omega_{k}(d,y_{k},\bm{x}),

for a weight function
\omega_{k}:\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.
The identification formula is given by the corresponding risk,

\displaystyle R_{P}(\pi;\ell^{\textsc{Add}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{d\in\mathcal{D}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{D}}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\mathbb{E}_{P_{\bm{X}}}\left[\omega_{k}(d,y,\bm{X})\cdot\pi(d;\bm{X})\cdot P(Y=y\mid D=k,\bm{X})\right].

The proof is given in Appendix
B.3
.

Theorem
2
shows that additive counterfactual
losses represent the largest class of counterfactual losses that are
uniformly identifiable for every
P\in\mathcal{P}
under
Assumptions
1
–
3
. In particular,
separability alone is not sufficient for identification; what is
required is
additive
separability in the potential outcomes. The counterfactual
risks associated with this class never depend on the joint
distribution of potential outcomes in decision-relevant
terms. Moreover, this class of counterfactual losses is consistent
with the decision-analytic framework of
Dawid [2000]
, since
the resulting quantity of interest depends only on the marginal
distributions of potential outcomes.

4
Preferences of Counterfactual Loss

The preceding section studied identification of counterfactual risk,
but did not address whether decision making with counterfactual losses
satisfies basic rationality requirements such as transitivity. By
contrast, standard losses are known to induce preferences that are
consistent with the von Neumann–Morgenstern (vNM) axioms

[
von Neumann and Morgenstern, 1944
]
when preferences are defined over the
space of realized outcomes
Y=Y(D)\in\mathcal{Y}
. In a companion paper

[
Koch et al., 2026
]
, we show
that counterfactual losses are also coherent in the vNM sense once
preferences are defined on the generalized space of
potential
outcomes
, where the decision maker may also care about
counterfactual outcomes
\{Y(d)\}_{d\in\mathcal{D}}
. We briefly summarize the
main results here.

Let
\mathcal{Z}:=\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}}\times\mathcal{X}
denote the potential outcome space. As in Section
3.2
, each policy
\pi
and joint law
P\in\mathcal{P}
induce a distribution
P^{\pi}\in\Delta(\mathcal{Z})
. In this section only, we allow for
oracle
policies
\pi:\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}}\times\mathcal{X}\to\Delta(\mathcal{D})
, so that every element of
\Delta(\mathcal{Z})
can be represented as
P^{\pi}
for some
(P,\pi)
. In particular, absent restrictions such as Assumption
2
, the space
\mathcal{P}
can be identified with
\Delta(\mathcal{Z})
.
Given a counterfactual loss
\ell
, define a preference relation on
\Delta(\mathcal{Z})
by,

P^{\pi}\succsim Q^{\rho}\iff R_{P}(\pi;\ell)\leq R_{Q}(\rho;\ell).

This relation compares state–policy pairs across populations, and
includes as a special case comparisons of policies within a fixed
state. In
Koch et al. [2026]
,
we show that these preferences are fully characterized by a
generalization of the vNM axioms to
\Delta(\mathcal{Z})
and, in particular,
satisfy transitivity.

On the same counterfactual outcome space, we also characterize how standard and additive losses restrict preferences. Preferences induced by standard losses correspond to an additional irrelevance axiom with which the decision maker is indifferent to counterfactual outcomes. Additive losses impose a weaker restriction. They allow the decision maker to value all potential outcomes, but require indifference to their joint dependence structure.

This aligns with the identification results above. The identification problem arises because the joint distribution of potential outcomes is unobserved. Additivity is precisely the restriction that eliminates dependence on the unidentified correlation structure while still allowing preferences to depend on counterfactual outcomes.

5
Expressiveness of Additive Counterfactual Loss

Having characterized the identifiable additive class, we ask how restrictive additivity is. If an additive counterfactual loss induces the same policy ranking as a standard loss, then they are not different in a meaningful way. We show that this equivalence holds when decisions are binary, but that additive losses are generally strictly more expressive with three or more decisions. In the latter case, under binary outcomes, we interpret additive counterfactual risk in terms of both accuracy and difficulty, whereas standard risk captures only accuracy. Finally, we give a sufficient monotonicity condition under binary outcomes under which every counterfactual loss admits an additive representation.

5.1
When Does the Additive Counterfactual Loss Differ from the Standard Loss?

We show that when the decision is binary (
K=2
), every additive counterfactual loss has an equivalent standard loss that induces the same ranking over policies and hence the same optimal decision. However, if the decisions are non-binary, additive counterfactual losses are generally strictly more expressive than standard losses, provided at least one counterfactual weight has sufficient interactions between decision and counterfactual potential
outcomes.

Proposition 1
(Additive Counterfactual Risk with Binary Decision)

Suppose that the decision is binary, i.e.,
\mathcal{D}=\{0,1\}
. Suppose that the counterfactual loss
\ell^{\textsc{Add}}
takes the additive form given in Definition
3
and that Assumption
3
holds. Define

\displaystyle\ell^{\textsc{Std}}(d;y_{d},\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\omega_{d}(d,y_{d},\bm{x})-\omega_{d}(1-d,y_{d},\bm{x}),

\displaystyle h(\bm{y},\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\omega_{0}(1,y_{0},\bm{x})+\omega_{1}(0,y_{1},\bm{x})+\varpi(\bm{y},\bm{x}).

Then,

\displaystyle\ell^{\textsc{Add}}(d;\bm{y},\bm{x})=\ell^{\textsc{Std}}(d;y_{d},\bm{x})+h(\bm{y},\bm{x}),

for all
d\in\{0,1\},\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}^{2}
and
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}
. Consequently, for any additive counterfactual loss
\ell^{\textsc{Add}}

there exists a standard loss
\ell^{\textsc{Std}}
such that for every joint law

P\in\mathcal{P}
and every policy
\pi:\mathcal{X}\to\Delta(\mathcal{D})
, the risk
difference,

R_{P}(\pi;\ell^{\textsc{Add}})-R_{P}(\pi;\ell^{\textsc{Std}})=\mathbb{E}_{P_{Y(\mathcal{D}),\bm{X}}}[h(Y(0),Y(1),\bm{X})]
, does not depend on
\pi
. Moreover, the standard loss

\ell^{\textsc{Std}}
is unique for this property up to addition of a bounded
measurable function of covariates only, i.e.,

\ell^{\textsc{Std}}_{c}(d;y_{d},\bm{x})=\ell^{\textsc{Std}}(d;y_{d},\bm{x})+c(\bm{x})
. Finally,
the mapping from additive counterfactual losses to their equivalent
standard losses is surjective but not injective.

The proof is given in Appendix
B.4
. This
proposition implies that when the decision is binary, every additive
counterfactual loss has an equivalent standard loss that induces the
same ordering over policies. As a consequence, the corresponding risks
lead to the same optimal decision rule.

Intuitively, when the decision is binary there is only one comparison:
choosing
d
versus choosing
1-d
, that is, considering

\ell(d;\bm{y},\bm{x})-\ell(1-d;\bm{y},\bm{x})
. For an identifiable loss,
this contrast must be additive in the potential outcomes. Since
1-d

is a deterministic function of
d
, it must be of the form

f_{1}(y_{1},\bm{x})-f_{0}(y_{0},\bm{x})
. But, with only two potential
outcomes, any such additive contrast can be absorbed into a standard
loss contrast, for example by defining

\ell^{\textsc{Std}}(d;y_{d},\bm{x})=f_{d}(y_{d},\bm{x})
. On the other hand, when

K=3
, the relevant comparisons involve a third, unchosen
option. Because a standard loss difference depends on at most two
potential outcomes, it cannot generally capture this additional
dependence.

Perhaps surprisingly, this
result is not limited to binary outcomes. The corresponding standard
loss
\ell^{\textsc{Std}}(d;y_{d},\bm{x})
is given by the difference between the weight
for the realized outcome
Y(D^{\pi})=y_{d}
under the chosen decision

D^{\pi}=d
and the counterfactual weight for the same outcome
Y(D^{\pi})=y_{d}
under the
alternative decision
D^{\pi}=1-d
(up to an additive constant that
only depends on covariates).

Although every additive counterfactual loss has a unique corresponding
standard loss (up to an additive constant) that yields the same policy ranking, the reverse does not hold. That is, any given
standard loss has infinitely many additive counterfactual losses with
the same policy ranking. Each of such counterfactual losses
assigns different values to principal strata. This asymmetry explains
why, even in the binary case, additive counterfactual loss has a
straightforward interpretation based on principal strata.

We now revisit our binary decision examples given in Section
3.3
.

Example 1
(Counterfactual Classification Loss)

The counterfactual loss in Equation (
6
) can be expressed as a standard loss,
\ell^{\textsc{Std}}(d;y_{d})=\ell_{y_{d}}-\tilde{\ell}_{y_{d}}+c_{d}
,
which corresponds to the difference between the realized and
counterfactual losses associated with outcome
y_{d}
with the additional
cost term. Thus, the regret is absorbed into the standard loss. If
\tilde{\ell}_{0}<\tilde{\ell}_{1}
(see Section
2.1.3
), the loss difference between correct and incorrect decisions is amplified, since
(\ell_{0}-\tilde{\ell}_{0})-(\ell_{1}-\tilde{\ell}_{1})>\ell_{0}-\ell_{1}
.

Example 2
(Asymmetric Counterfactual Loss)

When
\ell_{0}^{\text{R}}-\ell_{1}^{\text{R}}=\ell_{0}^{\text{H}}-\ell_{1}^{\text{H}}=\kappa
holds, the loss in Equation (
4
) can be rewritten as a standard loss with

\ell^{\textsc{Std}}(d;y_{d})=\ell_{y_{d}}+c_{d}-\{\kappa-(\ell_{0}-\ell_{1})\}y_{d}\propto\ell^{\text{H}}_{y_{d}}+c_{d}\propto\ell^{\text{R}}_{y_{d}}+c_{d}
. Thus, the loss reduces to the standard loss in
Equation (
3
).

Despite Proposition
1
, additive and
standard losses can still induce different preferences on the
potential outcome space
\Delta(\mathcal{Z})
even in the binary decision case
[
Koch et al., 2026
, Section 4.2]
. The
reason is that, although the risk difference

R_{P}(\pi;\ell^{\textsc{Add}})-R_{P}(\pi;\ell^{\textsc{Std}})=H_{P}
is policy
independent, it can vary with
P\in\mathcal{P}
. The next remark discusses how
this affects common decision criteria.

Remark 1
(Connection to Decision Criteria)

Empirical risk minimization (ERM) is a common learning strategy
where one chooses a policy that minimizes the estimated risk

\hat{R}(\cdot;\ell)
, leading to

\hat{\pi}_{\mathrm{ERM}}\in\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{\pi\in\Pi}\hat{R}(\pi;\ell)
. By Theorem
2
, this approach
requires additivity. Alternatively, one may appeal to standard
decision principles such as the Bayes, minimax, and minimax regret
criteria
[
Berger, 1985
,
Manski, 2004
,
Stoye, 2009
]
. Under the Bayes principle, given an assignment mechanism
e
, one specifies a prior

\xi
over states of nature
P_{Y(\mathcal{D}),\bm{X}}\in\Delta(\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}}\times\mathcal{X})
and chooses

\pi_{\mathrm{Bayes}}\in\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{\pi\in\Pi}\int\mathcal{R}_{e\cdot P_{Y(\mathcal{D}),\bm{X}}}(\pi;\ell)\,\mathrm{d}\xi(P_{Y(\mathcal{D}),\bm{X}})
, where
e\cdot P_{Y(\mathcal{D}),\bm{X}}\in\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{R}_{e\cdot P_{Y(\mathcal{D}),\bm{X}}}
defined as in Equations (
9
) and (
10
). Similarly, the minimax criterion
selects the policy that minimizes worst-case risk,

\pi_{\mathrm{MM}}\in\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{\pi\in\Pi}\sup_{P_{Y(\mathcal{D}),\bm{X}}\in\Delta(\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}}\times\mathcal{X})}\mathcal{R}_{e\cdot P_{Y(\mathcal{D}),\bm{X}}}(\pi;\ell)
. Finally, the minimax
regret criterion chooses the policy that minimizes worst-case regret
relative to the oracle policy,

\pi_{\mathrm{MM-R}}\in\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{\pi\in\Pi}\sup_{P_{Y(\mathcal{D}),\bm{X}}\in\Delta(\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}}\times\mathcal{X})}\left\{\mathcal{R}_{e\cdot P_{Y(\mathcal{D}),\bm{X}}}(\pi;\ell)-\min_{\pi^{\prime}\in\Pi}R_{e\cdot P_{Y(\mathcal{D}),\bm{X}}}(\pi^{\prime};\ell)\right\}.

In the binary case
\mathcal{D}=\{0,1\}
, the decomposition
\ell^{\textsc{Add}}=\ell^{\textsc{Std}}+h
in Proposition
1
with a decision independent function
h
implies that
\ell^{\textsc{Add}}
and
\ell^{\textsc{Std}}
yield the same optimal policy under ERM, Bayes, and minimax regret. This invariance need not hold under minimax as the decision-independent term
h
can shift risks differently across states
P_{Y(\mathcal{D}),\bm{X}}
.

Next, we show that when the decision is non-binary, no standard loss
corresponds directly to the additive counterfactual loss, as long as
the counterfactual weight is not additively separable in decision and
counterfactual outcome.

Proposition 2
(Additive Counterfactual Risk with Non-binary Decision)

Assume that the decision is non-binary, i.e.,
K=|\mathcal{D}|\geq 3
. Suppose that the counterfactual loss
\ell^{\textsc{Add}}(d;\bm{y},\bm{x})

takes the additive form given in Definition
3
and
that Assumption
3
holds.

Then, there exists no standard loss
\ell^{\textsc{Std}}(d;y_{d},\bm{x})
such
that for every
P\in\mathcal{P}
the risk difference

R_{P}(\pi;\ell^{\textsc{Add}})-R_{P}(\pi;\ell^{\textsc{Std}})
, is independent of

\pi:\mathcal{X}\to\Delta(\mathcal{D})
if and only if at least one weight
function
\omega_{k}(d,y,\bm{x})
is not off-diagonal additively
separable in
d\neq k
and
y\in\mathcal{Y}
. That is, there do not
exist bounded measurable functions

f_{k}:\mathcal{D}\setminus\{k\}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
and

g_{k}:\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

\omega_{k}(d,y,\bm{x})=f_{k}(d,\bm{x})+g_{k}(y,\bm{x})
, for all

d\in\mathcal{D}\setminus\{k\},y\in\mathcal{Y}
and
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}
.

If all the weight functions are off-diagonal additively separable,
then an equivalent standard loss exists and is given by,

\ell^{\textsc{Std}}(d;y_{d},\bm{x})=\omega_{d}(d,y_{d},\bm{x})-g_{d}(y_{d},\bm{x})+\sum_{k\in\mathcal{D},k\neq d}f_{k}(d,\bm{x}).

This standard loss is unique up to the addition of a bounded measurable function of the covariates.

The proof is given in Appendix
B.5
.
Proposition
2
shows that in the
non-binary setting, standard losses are inadequate when the
interactions between decision and counterfactual outcomes matter. In
the binary decision case, the weight functions are always additively
separable. Indeed, since
\mathcal{D}\setminus\{k\}=\{1-k\}
is a
singleton for
\mathcal{D}=\{0,1\}
, we can set
f_{k}(1-k,\bm{x})=0
and

g_{k}(y,\bm{x})=\omega_{k}(1-k,y,\bm{x})
to recover
Proposition
1
.

Example 3
(Trichotomous decision)

The additive counterfactual loss given in
Equation (
8
) is not off-diagonal additively
separable. Indeed, the weight function

\omega_{k}(d,y)=\mathds{1}{\left\{d=k\right\}}(\ell_{y}+c_{d})+\mathds{1}{\left\{d\neq k\right\}}r_{d,k}y\mathds{1}{\left\{k<d\right\}}
is not separable in the
decision and outcome for
d\neq k
if and only if
r_{2,1}\neq 0

or
r_{1,0}\neq r_{2,0}
. Thus, no standard loss can take harm to
the arrestee into account.

However,
Propositions
1
and
2

also show that there do exist counterfactual losses depending on all
potential outcomes that do not generalize the decision-making compared
to losses only depending on the observed outcome. We will illustrate
this somehow paradoxical behavior with an example.

Example 4
(Additively Separable Regret)

Consider the following additive counterfactual loss,

\ell(d;\bm{y})=\ell_{d,y_{d}}+\sum_{k\in\mathcal{D},k\neq d}\left(c_{k,d}+\tilde{\ell}_{k,y_{k}}\right),

for
d\in\mathcal{D}
and
\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}}
,
which generalizes Equation (
6
) to
multi-valued decision and outcome settings. The corresponding weight
function is given by

\omega_{k}(d,y)=\mathds{1}{\left\{d=k\right\}}\ell_{d,y}+\mathds{1}{\left\{d\neq k\right\}}\left(c_{k,d}+\tilde{\ell}_{k,y}\right)
, which is off-diagonal
additively separable with
f_{k}(d)=c_{k,d}
and

g_{k}(y)=\tilde{\ell}_{k,y}
. By
Proposition
2
, an equivalent standard
loss is equal to,

\ell^{\textsc{Std}}(d,y_{d})=\ell_{d,y_{d}}-\tilde{\ell}_{d,y_{d}}+\sum_{k\in\mathcal{D},k\neq d}c_{k,d}.

Computing the loss difference yields

\ell(d,\bm{y})-\ell^{\textsc{Std}}(d,y_{d})=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{D}}\tilde{\ell}_{k,y_{k}}
, which does not depend on the decision. Thus, even losses
that assign a different regret value to each unrealized
counterfactual outcome can be represented as a standard loss, so
long as the regret only depends additively on the decision and
counterfactual outcome. By
Proposition
2
, all counterfactual
losses that can be written equivalently as standard losses have this
form.

5.2
Case with Binary Outcome and Multi-valued Decision

Next, we show that when the outcome is binary, additive counterfactual
risk admits a decomposition into
accuracy
and
difficulty

terms. Accuracy captures how well the chosen decision performs on the
realized outcome. Difficulty measures how many counterfactual
alternatives would have produced a successful outcome. Standard losses
reflect accuracy, but cannot capture difficulty.

Consider the case of binary outcome and multi-valued decision, i.e.,

\mathcal{Y}=\{0,1\}
and
K\geq 3
. For notational simplicity, we ignore
covariates. Without loss of generality, assume that
Y=1
is a
desirable outcome while
Y=0
is undesirable. Under this setting, it
is natural to consider the following ordering of weight functions in
the additive counterfactual loss in Definition
3
for
any given decision
d
,

\omega_{d}(d,1)\leq 0\leq\omega_{d}(d,0),\quad\{\omega_{d^{\prime}}(d,0)\}_{d^{\prime}\neq d}\leq 0\leq\{\omega_{d^{\prime}}(d,1)\}_{d^{\prime}\neq d},

(11)

where
\omega_{d}(d,y_{d})
for
y_{d}=0,1
accounts for the
accuracy
of decision, and

\{\omega_{d^{\prime}}(d,y_{d^{\prime}})\}_{d^{\prime}\neq d}
for

y_{d^{\prime}}=0,1
capture its
difficulty
.

We first discuss the accuracy component. The correct “true positive”
decision, i.e.,
(D^{\pi},Y(d))=(d,1)
decreases the
counterfactual loss, which is represented by a negative sign of

\omega_{d}(d,1)
. In contrast, the incorrect “false negative”
decision, i.e.,
(D^{\pi},Y(d))=(d,0)
increases the loss,
which corresponds to a positive sign of
\omega_{d}(d,0)
.

While accuracy is an essential part of the standard decision theory,
difficulty is unique to the counterfactual loss. Avoidance of an
incorrect decision (“true negative”), i.e.,

(D^{\pi},Y(d^{\prime}))=(d,0)
, results in a decrease of the loss,
while failure to make a correct “true positive” decision, i.e.,

(D^{\pi},Y(d^{\prime}))=(d,1)
, increases the loss. Thus, the
counterfactual loss is smaller when fewer alternative decisions would
yield a desirable outcome (making a correct decision is
difficult). The loss is greater when many alternatives would result in
a desirable outcome (making a correct decision is easier). In this
way, the counterfactual loss rewards a consequential decision that
changes the outcome.

Lastly, since each weight function
\omega_{k}(d,y)
depends on
both the decision and the outcome, we can decompose it into a baseline
decision term and an interaction component:

\omega_{k}(d,y)=\omega_{k}(d,0)+\mathds{1}{\left\{y=1\right\}}\left(\omega_{k}(d,1)-\omega_{k}(d,0)\right).

The first term corresponds to the baseline cost of making decision
D^{\pi}=d
on the potential outcome
Y(k)
, while the second term accounts for the interaction between decision and potential outcome.

We formalize the above discussion as the following corollary of
Theorem
1
. Specifically, we show
that the conditional counterfactual risk can be decomposed into four
components; accuracy based on the realized outcome

P^{\pi}(D^{\pi}=d,Y(d)=1\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x})
, difficulty based on
counterfactual outcomes
P^{\pi}(D^{\pi}=d,Y(k)=1\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x})
for

k\neq d
, a baseline decision probability

P^{\pi}(D^{\pi}=d\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x})
, and the intercept function

C_{P_{Y(\mathcal{D})\mid\bm{X}}}(\bm{x})
. Each of these terms has a weight
function based on the specified additive loss function.

Corollary 3
(Accuracy and Difficulty for the Binary Outcome Case)

Assume
\mathcal{Y}=\{0,1\}
. Suppose that the counterfactual loss
\ell^{\textsc{Add}}
takes the additive form given in Definition
3
and that Assumption
3
holds. Then for every joint law
P\in\mathcal{P}
and policy
\pi:\mathcal{X}\to\Delta(\mathcal{D})
, the conditional counterfactual risk can be written as,

\displaystyle R_{P_{Y(\mathcal{D})\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x}}}(\pi;\ell^{\textsc{Add}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{d\in\mathcal{D}}\zeta_{d}(d,\bm{x})P^{\pi}(D^{\pi}=d,Y(d)=1\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x})+\sum_{d\in\mathcal{D}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{D},k\neq d}\zeta_{k}(d,\bm{x})P^{\pi}(D^{\pi}=d,Y(k)=1\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x})

\displaystyle+\sum_{d\in\mathcal{D}}\xi(d,\bm{x})P^{\pi}(D^{\pi}=d\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x})+C_{P_{Y(\mathcal{D})\mid\bm{X}}}(\bm{x}).

where

\displaystyle\zeta_{k}(d,\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\omega_{k}(d,1,\bm{x})-\omega_{k}(d,0,\bm{x}),\qquad\xi(d,\bm{x})=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{D}}\omega_{k}(d,0,\bm{x}),

\displaystyle C_{P_{Y(\mathcal{D})\mid\bm{X}}}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{y}\in\{0,1\}^{K}}\varpi(\bm{y},\bm{x})P(\bm{Y}(\mathcal{D})=\bm{y}\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x}).

The proof is given in Appendix
B.7
. If we choose
the weights such that
Equation (
11
) holds, then we have

\zeta_{d}(d,\bm{x})\leq 0\leq\zeta_{k}(d,\bm{x})
for all
k\neq d
. This implies that the risk decreases with greater accuracy (when
the realized outcome is desirable), and increases with counterfactual
regret (when other decisions also would yield a desirable outcome),
reflecting the difficulty of the decision. The baseline decision term
is a regularizer that penalizes decisions with higher baseline losses.

We next characterize when the additive counterfactual loss differs
from the standard loss in the binary outcome case. This result is a
corollary of Proposition
2
. It shows
that an additive counterfactual loss cannot be represented by a
standard loss if and only if the former includes a decision-dependent
difficulty term.

Corollary 4
(Decision-Dependent Difficulty for the Binary Outcome Case)

Assume
\mathcal{Y}=\{0,1\}
and
K=|\mathcal{D}|\geq 3
. Suppose that the counterfactual loss
\ell^{\textsc{Add}}(d;\bm{y},\bm{x})
takes the additive form given in Definition
3
and that Assumption
3
holds. Then, there exists a standard loss
\ell^{\textsc{Std}}(d;y_{d},\bm{x})
such that for every
P\in\mathcal{P}
the risk difference

R_{P}(\pi;\ell^{\textsc{Add}})-R_{P}(\pi;\ell^{\textsc{Std}})
,
is independent of
\pi:\mathcal{X}\to\Delta(\mathcal{D})
if and only if for each
k\in\mathcal{D}
the slope

\zeta_{k}(d,\bm{x})=\omega_{k}(d,1,\bm{x})-\omega_{k}(d,0,\bm{x})

is independent of
d\in\mathcal{D}\setminus\{k\}
. Under this condition an equivalent standard loss is given by

\ell^{\textsc{Std}}(d;y_{d},\bm{x})=y_{d}[\zeta_{d}(d,\bm{x})-\tilde{\zeta}_{d}(\bm{x})]+\xi(d,\bm{x}),

where
\tilde{\zeta}_{d}(\bm{x}):=\omega_{d}(j,1,\bm{x})-\omega_{d}(j,0,\bm{x})
for any
j\neq d
and

\xi(d,\bm{x})=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{D}}\omega_{k}(d,0,\bm{x})
. This standard loss is unique up to the addition of a bounded measurable function of the covariates.

Thus, a necessary and sufficient condition for an additive
counterfactual loss to differ from a standard loss is to have the
regret weight function
\zeta_{k}(d,\bm{x})
in
Corollary
3
depend on the decision
d
.

5.3
When Is Every Counterfactual Loss
Additive?

In Section
5.1
, we discussed the conditions
under which an additive counterfactual loss is equivalent to a
standard loss. Here, we briefly consider the opposite question. When
can a fully counterfactual loss be written in an additive form? We
give a monotonicity condition under which any counterfactual loss
admits an additive representation in the important case of binary
outcomes. This extends the monotonicity result of
Li and Pearl [2019]

to non-binary decisions. A necessary and sufficient condition is given
later as Corollary
5
. The
following result formalizes the above discussion.

Proposition 3
(Counterfactual Risk with Binary Outcomes under Monotonicity)

Suppose that
Y\in\{0,1\}
and the potential outcomes are monotone
in the decision, i.e.,
Y(0)\leq Y(1)\leq\cdots\leq Y(K-1)
. Then,
every counterfactual loss
\ell
in Definition
1
admits
an additive representation on the monotone support. Specifically,
there exists an additive loss
\ell^{\textsc{Add}}
according to
Definition
3
such that

\ell(d;\bm{y},\bm{x})=\ell^{\textsc{Add}}(d;\bm{y},\bm{x})
, for all
d\in\mathcal{D}
,

\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathrm{mon}}
and
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}
where

\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathrm{mon}}=\{s^{0},\ldots,s^{K}\}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{D}}
is
the maximal possible support of
(Y(0),\ldots,Y(K-1))
under
monotonicity, with
s_{k}^{m}=\mathds{1}{\left\{k\geq m\right\}}
for

k=0,\ldots,K-1
and all
m=0,\ldots,K
. A choice of
weights for
\ell^{\textsc{Add}}
is given by

\omega_{k}(d;y,\bm{x})=\frac{1}{K}\ell(d;s^{K},\bm{x})+y[\ell(d;s^{k},\bm{x})-\ell(d;s^{k+1},\bm{x})].

The proof is given in Appendix
B.6
. The
weights
\omega_{k}(d;y,\bm{x})
in
Proposition
3
assign to
Y(k)
the
incremental change in loss from setting
Y(k)=0
to
Y(k)=1
. We
revisit the examples in Section
2
to illustrate this
result under monotonicity.

Example 2
(Asymmetric Counterfactual Loss)

Consider monotonicity,
Y(0)\leq Y(1)
, implying that no unit is
harmed. Then, the support is given by

\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathrm{mon}}=\{(1,1),(0,1),(0,0)\}
, and the

\ell^{\textsc{Cf}}(d;y_{0},y_{1})=\omega_{0}(d;y_{0})+\omega_{1}(d;y_{1})

\omega_{k}(d;y)=\frac{1}{2}(\ell_{0}+c_{d})+y\{\mathds{1}{\left\{k=0\right\}}(\ell_{1}-\ell_{d}^{\mathrm{R}})+\mathds{1}{\left\{k=1\right\}}(\ell_{d}^{\mathrm{R}}-\ell_{0})\}

6
Symbolic Verification of Additivity

We have established additivity as a necessary and sufficient condition
for identifiability. In this section, we show how to verify this
condition in practice. We formulate identification as a linear system
whose solution determines whether a proposed counterfactual loss is
additive. We then apply the analysis to the binary decision and
outcome case where we derive the resulting identification constraints
in terms of principal strata.

6.1
A Symbolic Algorithm for Identification Verification

Theorem
2
establishes additivity as a
necessary and sufficient condition for identifying counterfactual risk
differences. Building on this result, we propose a symbolic algorithm
that takes a counterfactual loss function as an input and tests
whether the corresponding counterfactual risk is identifiable. The
algorithm also yields the weight and intercept functions from
Definition
3
, leading to a decomposition of
counterfactual risk into observable marginals. The proposed algorithm
further clarifies the constraints that additivity imposes on principal
strata. For notational simplicity, we suppress covariates though our
analysis applies conditional on covariates.

We begin by observing that the additive form in
Definition
3
induces a finite system of linear
equations. Let

\bm{\ell}=\{\ell^{\textsc{Add}}(d;y_{0},\ldots,y_{K-1})\}_{d\in\mathcal{D},\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{KM^{K}}
denote the vector of all loss values,

\bm{w}^{\textsc{Add}}=\{\omega_{k}(d,y),\varpi(\bm{y})\}_{d\in\mathcal{D},k\in\mathcal{D},y\in\mathcal{Y},\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}^{K}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K^{2}M+M^{K}}
denotes the
corresponding vector of weight and intercept values. The additive
structure determines a fixed
\left|\bm{\ell}\right|\times\left|\bm{w}\right|
matrix

\bm{A}^{\textsc{Add}}
of zeros and ones such that, given
\bm{A}^{\textsc{Add}}
, a loss
vector
\bm{\ell}
is additive if and only if the following linear
equation has a solution,

\displaystyle\bm{A}^{\textsc{Add}}\bm{w}^{\textsc{Add}}=\bm{\ell}.

(12)

An explicit example is provided in Appendix
C
. This
leads to the following corollary of
Theorem
2
.

Corollary 5
(Identification Verification of Counterfactual
Risk)

Suppose
Assumptions
1
–
2
hold. Consider a
counterfactual loss
\ell
defined in Definition
1
. The counterfactual risk difference
R_{P}(\pi;\ell)-R_{P}(\rho;\ell)
is identifiable for every pair of policies
\pi,\rho\in\Delta(\mathcal{D})
if and only if the linear system in Equation (
12
) has a solution.

If the support of
Y(\mathcal{D})
lies in a subset
S\subseteq\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}}
, as in Proposition
3
,
then after restricting
\bm{A}^{\textsc{Add}}
and
\bm{\ell}
in Equation (
12
) to rows with
\bm{y}\in S
, Corollary
5
yields necessary and sufficient criteria for identifiability on
S
.

The linear system in Equation (
12
) can be checked
for a solution and solved using a standard linear program solver whose
time complexity is polynomial in
M
but exponential in
K
. Whenever
a solution exists, the linear system directly yields the weight and
intercept functions, and thus a representation of the counterfactual
risk in terms of observable marginals. The solution, however, need not
be unique.

For exact identification, we remove the intercept terms from the
additive representation. This gives the restricted system

\displaystyle\bm{A}^{\textsc{Exa}}\bm{w}^{\textsc{Exa}}=\bm{\ell},

(13)

where

\bm{w}^{\textsc{Exa}}=\{\omega_{k}(d,y)\}_{d\in\mathcal{D},k\in\mathcal{D},y\in\mathcal{Y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K^{2}M}
contains only the weight functions,
\bm{A}^{\textsc{Exa}}
is adjusted
accordingly, and
\bm{\ell}
remains
KM^{K}
dimensional. The next
corollary shows a necessary and sufficient condition for exact
identification.

Corollary 6
(Exact-Identification Verification of the
Counterfactual Risk)

Suppose Assumptions
1
–
2
hold. Consider a
counterfactual loss
\ell
defined in Definition
1
. The counterfactual risk
R_{P}(\pi;\ell)
is identifiable for every policy
\pi\in\Delta(\mathcal{D})
if and only if the linear system in Equation (
13
) has a solution.

Lastly, by further restricting the linear system given in
Equation (
12
) to

\displaystyle\bm{A}^{\textsc{Std}}\bm{w}^{\textsc{Std}}=\bm{\ell},

(14)

where

\bm{w}^{\textsc{Std}}=\{\omega_{d}(d,y)\}_{d\in\mathcal{D},\,y\in\mathcal{Y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{KM}

contains only the diagonal weight functions,
\bm{A}^{\textsc{Std}}
is defined
accordingly, and
\bm{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{KM^{K}}
as before. We obtain a
necessary and sufficient condition under which a counterfactual loss
can be equivalently written as a standard loss.

Li and Pearl [2024b]
propose a
numerical
algorithm for
verification of exact identification given experimental
data. Corollary
5
extends their
result to risk differences, and proposes a
symbolic
verification
algorithm that does not require data and operates directly on a loss
function itself. Our algorithm also yields an explicit decomposition
of counterfactual risk into observable marginals.

6.2
Restrictions on Principal Strata

Corollaries
5
and

Concluding Remarks

In this paper, we generalize the classical statistical decision theory
for treatment choice by introducing counterfactual losses that
depend on all potential outcomes. While standard losses evaluate
decisions based solely on realized outcomes, counterfactual losses
additionally take into account what would have happened under
alternative decisions. Counterfactual losses can account
for ethical and other considerations that are not possible to express
with standard losses.

While counterfactual losses are much more expressive than the
standard losses, a central challenge is the difficulty of
identification. Since only one potential outcome is observed per unit,
counterfactual risks are generally unidentifiable. We show that under
the standard assumption of strong ignorability, a counterfactual loss
leads to an identifiable risk if and only if it is additive in the
potential outcomes. Moreover, we establish that such additive
counterfactual losses do not have an equivalent standard loss
representation so long as decisions are non-binary. Finally, our proposed
symbolic linear inverse formulation provides a practical way to verify whether a given
counterfactual loss is additive.

Our results should allow practitioners to consider a wider class of
loss functions when evaluating decision rules and learning optimal
policies. Promising directions for future research include extending
the framework to continuous decisions and outcomes, relaxing the
strong ignorability assumption in the identification analysis

[
Ben-Michael et al., 2025
, see]
and studying the sampling
risk in Equation (
10
) when policies are learned
from finite data under counterfactual losses.

\displaystyle\quad-\left[\omega_{d}(d,y_{d},\bm{x})-\omega_{d}(1-d,y_{d},\bm{x})+\omega_{0}(1,y_{0},\bm{x})+\omega_{1}(0,y_{1},\bm{x})+\varpi(\bm{y},\bm{x})\right]

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\left[\omega_{1-d}(d,y_{1-d},\bm{x})+\omega_{d}(1-d,y_{d},\bm{x})\right]-\left[\omega_{0}(1,y_{0},\bm{x})+\omega_{1}(0,y_{1},\bm{x})\right]

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle 0.

This proves the decomposition.

Next, we show that
\ell^{\textsc{Std}}
induces the same ranking over policies
as
\ell^{\textsc{Add}}
. Fix
P\in\mathcal{P}
and policy

\pi:\mathcal{X}\to\Delta(\mathcal{D})
, then by Definition
2
, we have,

\displaystyle R_{P}(\pi;\ell^{\textsc{Add}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{P^{\pi}}[\ell^{\textsc{Add}}(D^{\pi};Y(0),Y(1),\bm{X})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{P^{\pi}}[\ell^{\textsc{Std}}(D^{\pi};Y(D^{\pi}),\bm{X})]+\mathbb{E}_{P_{Y(\mathcal{D}),\bm{X}}}[h(Y(0),Y(1),\bm{X})]

\displaystyle=R_{P}(\pi;\ell^{\textsc{Std}})+\mathbb{E}_{P_{Y(\mathcal{D}),\bm{X}}}[h(Y(0),Y(1),\bm{X})].

Note that

\mathbb{E}_{P^{\pi}}[h(Y(0),Y(1),\bm{X})]=\mathbb{E}_{P}[h(Y(0),Y(1),\bm{X})]=\mathbb{E}_{P_{Y(\mathcal{D}),\bm{X}}}[h(Y(0),Y(1),\bm{X})]
as
P
and
P^{\pi}
have the
same marginal law for
(Y(0),Y(1),\bm{X})
. Since the second term is
independent of
\pi
, this proves that the risk difference does not
depend on the policy.

For uniqueness, suppose that for another bounded standard loss

\tilde{\ell}^{\textsc{Std}}
, the risk difference

R_{P}(\pi;\ell^{\textsc{Add}})-R_{P}(\pi;\tilde{\ell}^{\textsc{Std}})
is independent of

\pi
for every
P\in\mathcal{P}
. This implies that for a constant policy

\pi_{d}:=d\in\mathcal{D}
, we have,

\displaystyle\left[R_{P}(\pi_{d};\ell^{\textsc{Add}})-R_{P}(\pi_{d};\tilde{\ell}^{\textsc{Std}})\right]-\left[R_{P}(\pi_{d};\ell^{\textsc{Add}})-R_{P}(\pi_{d};\ell^{\textsc{Std}})\right]

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle R_{P}(\pi_{d};\ell^{\textsc{Std}})-R_{P}(\pi_{d};\tilde{\ell}^{\textsc{Std}})

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{P}[\ell^{\textsc{Std}}(d,Y(d),\bm{X})-\tilde{\ell}^{\textsc{Std}}(d,Y(d),\bm{X})]

does not depend on
d\in\{0,1\}
for every
P
. Hence, the
following equality holds uniformly over all
P\in\mathcal{P}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{P}[\ell^{\textsc{Std}}(0,Y(0),\bm{X})-\tilde{\ell}^{\textsc{Std}}(0,Y(0),\bm{X})]=\mathbb{E}_{P}[\ell^{\textsc{Std}}(1,Y(1),\bm{X})-\tilde{\ell}^{\textsc{Std}}(1,Y(1),\bm{X})].

(B.3)

In particular, the equality holds under any degenerate law that
concentrates on a single value of
(Y(0),Y(1),\bm{X})
. Fix
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}

and
y,y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}
, set
e(d;\bm{x})=1/2
for
d\in\{0,1\}
, and define,

P(D=d,Y(0)=y_{0},Y(1)=y_{1},\bm{X}\in A)=\frac{1}{2}\mathds{1}{\left\{y_{0}=y,y_{1}=y^{\prime}\right\}}\delta_{\bm{x}}(A),

where
A\subseteq\mathcal{X}
and
\delta_{\bm{x}}(\cdot)
is the Dirac measure at
\bm{X}=\bm{x}
.
Then
P\in\mathcal{P}
. Substituting this
P
into
Equation (

) yields,

\displaystyle\ell^{\textsc{Std}}(0,y,\bm{x})-\tilde{\ell}^{\textsc{Std}}(0,y,\bm{x})=\ell^{\textsc{Std}}(1,y^{\prime},\bm{x})-\tilde{\ell}^{\textsc{Std}}(1,y^{\prime},\bm{x}).

(B.4)

Taking
y^{\prime}=y
shows that
\ell^{\textsc{Std}}(d,y,\bm{x})-\tilde{\ell}^{\textsc{Std}}(d,y,\bm{x})

does not depend on
d
. Plugging this back into
Equation (
B.3
) and varying
y,y^{\prime}
implies that the
same difference cannot depend on
y
either. Hence,

\ell^{\textsc{Std}}(d,y,\bm{x})-\tilde{\ell}^{\textsc{Std}}(d,y,\bm{x})
is constant in
(d,y)
.
Therefore, there exists a bounded, measurable function
c:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}

such that

\ell^{\textsc{Std}}(d;y,\bm{x})-\tilde{\ell}^{\textsc{Std}}(d;y,\bm{x})=c(\bm{x})
, which
proves uniqueness.

Finally, the surjectivity of the map from an additive counterfactual
loss to an equivalent standard loss follows immediately from the fact
that any standard loss is a special case of an additive counterfactual
loss. However, the map is not injective. Given any standard loss

\ell^{\textsc{Std}}(d;y_{d},\bm{x})
, fix a bounded measurable function

h:\mathcal{Y}^{2}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
and define

\ell^{\textsc{Add}}(d;\bm{y},\bm{x})=\ell^{\textsc{Std}}(d;y_{d},\bm{x})+h(\bm{y},\bm{x})
. Then,
by the first part of this proposition, this corresponds to an additive
counterfactual loss with the same policy ordering. Since
h
was
arbitrary, there exist infinitely many additive counterfactual losses
that map to the same standard loss. ∎

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=[\phi_{P}(\pi_{d})-\phi_{P}(\pi_{d^{\prime}})]-[\phi_{P^{\prime}}(\pi_{d})-\phi_{P^{\prime}}(\pi_{d^{\prime}})]

B.8.1
Lemma
1

Lemma 1

Let
\mathcal{P}
denote the set of all joint probability distributions under consideration, and let
\mathcal{Q}
be the set of all observable distributions. Let
Q:\mathcal{P}\to\mathcal{Q}
denote a surjective mapping that associates each joint distribution with an observable distribution. Let
\theta(P)
denote the (causal) estimand of interest under distribution
P\in\mathcal{P}
. For a fixed observable distribution
Q_{0}\in\mathcal{Q}
define

\mathcal{S}_{0}=\{P\in\mathcal{P}:Q(P)=Q_{0}\}.

Then, the following statements are equivalent:

(a)

For every
Q_{0}\in\mathcal{Q}
there exists a constant

\theta_{0}
such that
\theta(P)=\theta_{0}
for all
P\in\mathcal{S}_{0}
.

(b)

There exists a function
h
such that
\theta(P)=h(Q(P))
for every
P\in\mathcal{P}
.

Proof.

We first prove that (b) implies (a). Fix any
Q_{0}\in\mathcal{Q}
and let
P\in\mathcal{S}_{0}
such that
Q(P)=Q_{0}
. Note that
\mathcal{S}_{0}
is non-empty by
Q
surjective. Then, by (b), there exists
h
such that

\displaystyle\theta(P)=h(Q(P))=h(Q_{0})=\theta_{0},

for every
P\in\mathcal{S}_{0}
. Because
Q_{0}
was arbitrary, the claim follows.

We next prove that (a) implies (b). Let
Q_{0}\in\mathcal{Q}
. Surjectivity of
Q
ensures
\mathcal{S}_{0}
is non-empty. By (a), there is a constant
\theta_{0}
such that
\theta(P)=\theta_{0}
for all
P\in\mathcal{S}_{0}
. Define
h:\mathcal{Q}\to\mathbb{R}
by setting
h(Q_{0}):=\theta_{0}
for every such
Q_{0}\in\mathcal{Q}
. Now, let
P\in\mathcal{P}
be an arbitrary probability measure and let
Q_{0}=Q(P)
. Then, there exists
\theta_{0}=h(Q_{0})
such that
\theta(P)=\theta_{0}=h(Q_{0})=h(Q(P))
. This proves the claim.
∎

B.8.2
Lemma
2

Lemma 2

Fix a counterfactual loss
\ell
satisfying Assumption
3
and a policy
\pi:\mathcal{X}\to\Delta(\mathcal{D})
. Then the following are equivalent:

(a)

The counterfactual risk
R_{P}(\pi;\ell)
is identifiable from
Q_{P}
according to Definition
4
.

(b)

The conditional counterfactual risk
R_{P_{Y(\mathcal{D})\mid\bm{X}}}(\pi;\ell)
is identifiable from
Q_{P}(D,Y\mid\bm{X})
, i.e., for every pair
P_{1},P_{2}\in\mathcal{P}
with common covariate marginal
P_{1,\bm{X}}=P_{2,\bm{X}}=:\mu
, whenever
Q_{P_{1}}(D,Y\mid\bm{X})=Q_{P_{2}}(D,Y\mid\bm{X})

\mu
-a.s., we also have
R_{P_{1,{Y(\mathcal{D})\mid\bm{X}}}}(\pi,\ell)=R_{P_{2,{Y(\mathcal{D})\mid\bm{X}}}}(\pi,\ell)

\mu
-a.s.

Proof.

We first prove that (a) implies (b). We use proof by contradiction. Suppose that
R_{P_{Y(\mathcal{D})\mid\bm{X}}}(\pi,\ell)
is not identifiable from
Q_{P}(D,Y\mid\bm{X})
. Then there exist two joint laws
P_{1},P_{2}\in\mathcal{P}
with common covariate marginal
P_{1,\bm{X}}=P_{2,\bm{X}}=:\mu
such that the event

E:=\left\{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}:Q_{P_{1}}(D,Y\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x})=Q_{P_{2}}(D,Y\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x})\right\},

has
\mu(E)=1
, and the event

A:=E\cap\left\{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}:R_{P_{1,{Y(\mathcal{D})\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x}}}}(\pi;\ell)-R_{P_{2,{Y(\mathcal{D})\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x}}}}(\pi;\ell)\neq 0\right\}

has
\mu(A)>0
. Note that
A
is measurable, because
\phi(\bm{x}):=R_{P_{1,{Y(\mathcal{D})\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x}}}}(\pi;\ell)-R_{P_{2,{Y(\mathcal{D})\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x}}}}(\pi;\ell)
is measurable as the difference of two measurable functions.

Let
A_{+}=\{\bm{x}\in A:\phi(\bm{x})>0\}
and
A_{-}=\{\bm{x}\in A:\phi(\bm{x})<0\}
, then
A=A_{+}\cup A_{-}
. Since
\mu(A)>0
at least one of
A_{+},A_{-}
has positive
\mu
-measure. Without loss of generality assume
\mu(A_{+})>0
and define the covariate law
\nu(B):=\frac{\mu(B\cap A_{+})}{\mu(A_{+})}
for measurable
B\subseteq\mathcal{X}
. Then
\nu\ll\mu,\nu(A_{+})=1
and
\phi(\bm{x})>0

\nu
-a.s.

Therefore, since
\ell
is bounded by assumption,

\int_{\mathcal{X}}\phi(\bm{x})d\nu(\bm{x})>0.

Define the joint probability measures
P^{\nu}_{i}
for
i=1,2
,

P^{\nu}_{i}(D,Y(\mathcal{D}),\bm{X})=P_{i}(D,Y(\mathcal{D})\mid\bm{X})\cdot\nu(\bm{X}).

Since
P_{i,\bm{X}}^{\nu}=\nu\ll\mu=P_{i,\bm{X}}
,
P_{i}^{\nu}
satisfies Assumption
2
and thus
P_{i}^{\nu}\in\mathcal{P}
for

.
Moreover, since the marginal distribution of
\bm{X}
does not affect the conditional risk, we have

R_{P_{i,{Y(\mathcal{D})\mid\bm{X}}}}(\pi;\ell)=R_{P^{\nu}_{i,{Y(\mathcal{D})\mid\bm{X}}}}(\pi;\ell)\qquad\nu\text{-a.s.}

for
i=1,2
. Hence

\displaystyle R_{P^{\nu}_{1}}(\pi,\ell)-R_{P^{\nu}_{2}}(\pi,\ell)

\displaystyle=\int_{\bm{X}}R_{P^{\nu}_{1,{Y(\mathcal{D})\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x}}}}(\pi;\ell)-R_{P^{\nu}_{2,{Y(\mathcal{D})\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x}}}}(\pi;\ell)d\nu(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\int_{\bm{X}}R_{P_{1,{Y(\mathcal{D})\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x}}}}(\pi;\ell)-R_{P_{2,{Y(\mathcal{D})\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x}}}}(\pi;\ell)d\nu(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\int_{\bm{X}}\phi(\bm{x})d\nu(\bm{x})

\displaystyle>0.

(B.8)

On the other hand, by construction,

Q_{P^{\nu}_{1}}(D,Y,\bm{X})=Q_{P_{1}}(D,Y\mid\bm{X})\cdot\nu(\bm{X})=Q_{P_{2}}(D,Y\mid\bm{X})\cdot\nu(\bm{X})=Q_{P^{\nu}_{2}}(D,Y,\bm{X}).

Since
R_{P}(\pi,\ell)
is identifiable and
Q_{P^{\nu}_{1}}=Q_{P^{\nu}_{2}}
, Definition
4
implies that
R_{P^{\nu}_{1}}(\pi,\ell)
=
R_{P^{\nu}_{2}}(\pi,\ell)
, contradicting Equation (

). Thus,
R_{P_{Y(\mathcal{D})\mid\bm{X}}}(\pi,\ell)
must be identifiable from
Q_{P}(D,Y\mid\bm{X})
.

We next prove that (b) implies (a). Let
P_{1},P_{2}\in\mathcal{P}
with
Q_{P_{1}}=Q_{P_{2}}
. This implies that
P_{1,\bm{X}}=P_{2,\bm{X}}=:\mu

Q_{P_{1}}(D,Y\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x})=Q_{P_{2}}(D,Y\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x})\qquad\mu\text{-a.s.}

Since the conditional risk is identifiable, this implies that

R_{P_{1,{Y(\mathcal{D})\mid\bm{X}}}}(\pi;\ell)=R_{P_{2,{Y(\mathcal{D})\mid\bm{X}}}}(\pi;\ell)\qquad\mu\text{-a.s.}

Therefore

\displaystyle R_{P_{1}}(\pi,\ell)

\displaystyle=\int_{\bm{X}}R_{P_{1,{Y(\mathcal{D})\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x}}}}(\pi;\ell)d\mu(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\int_{\bm{X}}R_{P_{2,{Y(\mathcal{D})\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x}}}}(\pi;\ell)d\mu(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=R_{P_{2}}(\pi,\ell),

which proves (a) by Definition
4
.
∎

B.8.3
Lemma
3

Lemma 3

Associate the space of all joint laws
\Delta(\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}})
of
Y(\mathcal{D}):=(Y(0),\ldots,Y(K-1))
with the simplex
\Delta_{M^{K}-1}=\{\bm{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{M^{K}}:\sum_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}}}p_{\bm{y}}=1\text{ and }p_{\bm{y}}\geq 0\}
. For
\bm{p}\in\Delta_{M^{K}-1}
define the vector of marginal probabilities,

\bm{q}(\bm{p})=\left\{\sum_{\bm{y}_{-k}\in\mathcal{Y}^{K-1}}p_{y,\bm{y}_{-k}}\right\}_{k\in\mathcal{D},y\in\mathcal{Y}},

where
\bm{y}_{-k}=(y_{0},\ldots,y_{k-1},y_{k+1},\ldots,y_{K-1})
, so that
\bm{q}(\bm{p})
collects
\{P(Y(k)=y)\}_{k\in\mathcal{D},\ y\in\mathcal{Y}}
from
\bm{p}=\{P(Y(\mathcal{D})=\bm{y})\}_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}}}

Define the matrix
\bm{C}\in\{0,1\}^{KM\times M^{K}}
with rows indexed by
i(k,y)
and columns indexed by
j(\bm{y})
defined via

C_{i(k,y),j(\bm{y}^{\prime})}=\mathds{1}{\left\{y^{\prime}_{k}=y\right\}}.

Then
\bm{q}(\bm{p})=\bm{C}\bm{p}
for all
\bm{p}\in\Delta_{M^{K}-1}
.
Moreover,

\mathrm{ker}(\bm{C})=\left\{\bm{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{M^{K}}:\sum_{\bm{y}_{-k}\in\mathcal{Y}^{K-1}}v_{y,\bm{y}_{-k}}=0\ \text{for all }k\in\mathcal{D},y\in\mathcal{Y}\right\}.

In particular
\mathbf{1}^{\top}\bm{v}=0
for all
\bm{v}\in\mathrm{ker}(\bm{C})
and
\bm{C}
is surjective from
\Delta(\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}})
onto
(\Delta(\mathcal{Y}))^{\mathcal{D}}
.

Proof.

For every joint probability distribution
P(Y(\mathcal{D}))
, we can express its marginals
\{P(Y(k)=y)\}_{k\in\mathcal{D},y\in\mathcal{Y}}
as,

\displaystyle P(Y(k)=y)=\sum_{\bm{y}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}}}\mathds{1}{\left\{y^{\prime}_{k}=y\right\}}P(Y(\mathcal{D})=\bm{y}^{\prime}).

(B.9)

Define the matrix
\bm{C}\in\{0,1\}^{KM\times M^{K}}
with rows indexed by
i(k,y)
and columns index by
j(\bm{y})
via

\displaystyle C_{i(k,y),j(\bm{y}^{\prime})}=\mathds{1}{\left\{y^{\prime}_{k}=y\right\}}.

Then, by associating
\bm{p}\in\Delta_{M^{K}-1}
with
\{P(Y(\mathcal{D})=\bm{y})\}_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}}}
and
\bm{q}=\bm{q}(\bm{p})
with its marginal
\{P(Y(k)=y)\}_{k\in\mathcal{D},\ y\in\mathcal{Y}}
, Equation (
B.9
) can be written as,

(\bm{C}\bm{p})_{k,y}=\sum_{\bm{y}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}}}\mathds{1}{\left\{y^{\prime}_{k}=y\right\}}p_{\bm{y}^{\prime}}=\sum_{\bm{y}_{-k}\in\mathcal{Y}^{K-1}}p_{y,\bm{y}_{-k}}=q_{k,y},

implying
\bm{q}(\bm{p})=\bm{C}\bm{p}
. Since for any collection of marginal distributions

\{q_{k,y}\}_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\in\Delta(\mathcal{Y})
,
k\in\mathcal{D}
, the product law

p_{\bm{y}}=\prod_{k\in\mathcal{D}}q_{k,y_{k}}

has these marginals, the restriction of
\bm{C}
to

\Delta(\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}})
is surjective onto
(\Delta(\mathcal{Y}))^{\mathcal{D}}
.

We proceed to examine the kernel of the matrix
\bm{C}
. Let
\bm{v}=(v_{\bm{y}})_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M^{K}}
then

(\bm{C}\bm{v})_{k,y}=\sum_{\bm{y}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}}}\mathds{1}{\left\{y^{\prime}_{k}=y\right\}}v_{\bm{y}^{\prime}}=\sum_{\bm{y}_{-k}\in\mathcal{Y}^{K-1}}v_{y,\bm{y}_{-k}}.

Therefore
\bm{C}\bm{v}=0
if and only if all marginals vanish, which proves the claim. Finally, if
\bm{v}\in\mathrm{ker}(\bm{C})
, then for any
k\in\mathcal{D}

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}^{\top}\bm{v}=\sum_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}}}v_{\bm{y}}=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\sum_{\bm{y}_{-k}\in\mathcal{Y}^{K-1}}v_{y,\bm{y}_{-k}}=0.

∎

B.8.4
Lemma
4

Lemma 4

Associate the space of all joint laws
\Delta(\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}})
of
Y(\mathcal{D}):=(Y(0),\ldots,Y(K-1))
with the simplex
\Delta_{M^{K}-1}=\{\bm{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{M^{K}}:\sum_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}}}p_{\bm{y}}=1\text{ and }p_{\bm{y}}\geq 0\}
. Let
\bm{C}
be the marginalization matrix from Lemma
3
and define
\bm{C}(\Delta_{M^{K}-1})=\{\bm{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{KM}:\exists\bm{p}\in\Delta_{M^{K}-1}\text{ such that }\bm{q}=\bm{C}\bm{p}\}
, the set of marginal vectors induced by joint laws on
\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}}
. Let
\bm{a}=(a_{\bm{y}})_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M^{K}}
. Then the following are equivalent:

(a)

There exists a well-defined function
f_{\bm{a}}:\bm{C}(\Delta_{M^{K}-1})\to\mathbb{R}
such that
f_{\bm{a}}(\bm{q})=f_{\bm{a}}(\bm{q}(\bm{p}))=f_{\bm{a}}(\bm{C}\bm{p})=\bm{a}^{\top}\bm{p}
for every
\bm{p}\in\Delta_{M^{K}-1}
.

(b)

For all
\bm{p},\bm{p}^{\prime}\in\Delta_{M^{K}-1}
with
\bm{C}\bm{p}=\bm{C}\bm{p}^{\prime}
we have
\bm{a}^{\top}\bm{p}=\bm{a}^{\top}\bm{p}^{\prime}
.

(c)

\bm{a}\in\mathrm{im}(\bm{C}^{\top})
.

(d)

There exist
w_{k,y}\in\mathbb{R}
,
k\in\mathcal{D},y\in\mathcal{Y}
such that
a_{\bm{y}}=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{D}}w_{k,y_{k}}
for every
\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}}
.

Proof.

We first show that (a) is equivalent to (b). Assume (a) holds for a function
f_{\bm{a}}
and let
\bm{p},\bm{p}^{\prime}\in\Delta_{M^{K}-1}
with
\bm{C}\bm{p}=\bm{C}\bm{p}^{\prime}
. Then

\bm{a}^{\top}\bm{p}=f_{\bm{a}}(\bm{C}\bm{p})=f_{\bm{a}}(\bm{C}\bm{p}^{\prime})=\bm{a}^{\top}\bm{p}^{\prime},

which proves (b). Conversely, let (b) hold then the function
f_{\bm{a}}:\bm{C}(\Delta_{M^{K}-1})\to\mathbb{R}
with
f_{\bm{a}}(\bm{C}\bm{p})=\bm{a}^{\top}\bm{p}
is well-defined as all
\bm{p},\bm{p}^{\prime}\in\Delta_{M^{K}-1}
with
\bm{C}\bm{p}=\bm{C}\bm{p}^{\prime}
map to the same value. This proves (a).

Next we show that (b) implies (c). Assume (c) does not hold such that
\bm{a}\notin\mathrm{im}(\bm{C}^{\top})
. By standard linear algebra
[
Lax, 2007
, Chapter 3, Theorem 5]
,
\mathrm{im}(\bm{C}^{\top})=\mathrm{ker}(\bm{C})^{\perp}
. This implies that
\exists\bm{v}=(v_{\bm{y}})_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}}}\in\text{ker}(\bm{C})\setminus\{\bm{0}\}:\bm{a}^{\top}\bm{v}\neq 0
.
Let
\bm{p}=(p_{\bm{y}})_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}}}\in\mathring{\Delta}_{M^{K}-1}
be in the interior of the simplex with strictly positive entries. Define

\displaystyle\bm{p}^{\prime}=\bm{p}+\alpha\bm{v},\quad\text{with}\ 0<\alpha<\frac{\min_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}}}p_{\bm{y}}}{\max_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}}}\left|v_{\bm{y}}\right|}.

Note that such an
\alpha
exists. Indeed, by assumption,
0<p_{\bm{y}}
for all entries and
\bm{v}
has at least one non-zero entry as
\bm{v}\neq\bm{0}
. Observe,

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}}}p^{\prime}_{\bm{y}}\geq\min_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}}}p_{\bm{y}}-\alpha\max_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}}}\left|v_{\bm{y}}\right|>0,

and,

\displaystyle\sum_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}}}p^{\prime}_{\bm{y}}=\sum_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}}}p_{\bm{y}}+\alpha\sum_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}}}v_{\bm{y}}=\sum_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}}}p_{\bm{y}}=1,

where the second equality follows from
\mathbf{1}^{\top}\bm{v}=0
by
\bm{v}\in\text{ker}(\bm{C})
and Lemma
3
. Thus,
\bm{p}^{\prime}\in\Delta_{M^{K}-1}
represents a law in
\Delta(\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}})
.

Because
\bm{v}\in\text{ker}(\bm{C})
,

\displaystyle\bm{C}\bm{p}^{\prime}=\bm{C}\bigl(\bm{p}+\alpha\bm{v}\bigr)=\bm{C}\bm{p}.

By (b) this implies
\bm{a}^{\top}\bm{p}=\bm{a}^{\top}\bm{p}^{\prime}
and thus,

\displaystyle 0=\bm{a}^{\top}\bm{p}^{\prime}-\bm{a}^{\top}\bm{p}=\bm{a}^{\top}(\bm{p}^{\prime}-\bm{p})=\alpha\bm{a}^{\top}\bm{v}\neq 0,

as
\alpha\neq 0
by construction and
\bm{a}^{\top}\bm{v}\neq 0
by assumption. This yields a contradiction and proves (c).

Next we show that (c) implies (d). If
\bm{a}\in\mathrm{im}(\bm{C}^{\top})
then
a=\bm{C}^{\top}\bm{w}
for some
\bm{w}=(w_{k,y})_{k\in\mathcal{D},y\in\mathcal{Y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{KM}
. By Definition of
\bm{C}
in Lemma
3
this implies,

a_{\bm{y}}=(\bm{C}^{\top}\bm{w})_{\bm{y}}=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{D}}\sum_{y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}}\mathds{1}{\left\{y_{k}=y^{\prime}\right\}}w_{k,y^{\prime}}=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{D}}w_{k,y_{k}},

which proves (d).

Finally, we show that (d) implies (b). Let
\bm{p},\bm{p}^{\prime}\in\Delta_{M^{K}-1}
with
\bm{C}\bm{p}=\bm{C}\bm{p}^{\prime}
. By (d),

\displaystyle\bm{a}^{\top}\bm{p}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}}}a_{\bm{y}}p_{\bm{y}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}}}\left(\sum_{k\in\mathcal{D}}w_{k,y_{k}}\right)p_{\bm{y}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{D}}\sum_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}}}w_{k,y_{k}}p_{\bm{y}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{D}}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}w_{k,y}\sum_{\bm{y}_{-k}\in\mathcal{Y}^{K-1}}p_{y,\bm{y}_{-k}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{D}}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}w_{k,y}(\bm{C}\bm{p})_{k,y}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{D}}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}w_{k,y}(\bm{C}\bm{p}^{\prime})_{k,y}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{D}}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}w_{k,y}\sum_{\bm{y}_{-k}\in\mathcal{Y}^{K-1}}p^{\prime}_{y,\bm{y}_{-k}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{D}}\sum_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}}}w_{k,y_{k}}p^{\prime}_{\bm{y}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}}}\left(\sum_{k\in\mathcal{D}}w_{k,y_{k}}\right)p^{\prime}_{\bm{y}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}}}a_{\bm{y}}p^{\prime}_{\bm{y}}

\displaystyle=\bm{a}^{\top}\bm{p}^{\prime},

which proves (b) and completes the proof.
∎

B.8.5
Lemma
5

Lemma 5

Let
\left|\mathcal{D}\right|\geq 3
and fix
k\in\mathcal{D},\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}
. Consider the weight function
\omega_{k}:\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
. Then the following are equivalent:

(a)

For all
d,d^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}\setminus\{k\}
the difference
\omega_{k}(d,y,\bm{x})-\omega_{k}(d^{\prime},y,\bm{x})
does not depend on
y\in\mathcal{Y}
.

(b)

There exist functions
f_{k}:\mathcal{D}\setminus\{k\}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
and
g_{k}:\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\omega_{k}(d,y,\bm{x})=f_{k}(d,\bm{x})+g_{k}(y,\bm{x})
for all
d\in\mathcal{D}\setminus\{k\}
and
y\in\mathcal{Y}
.

Proof.

We first prove that (a) implies (b). Fix
d_{0}\in\mathcal{D}\setminus\{k\}
and define
f_{k}(d,\bm{x}):=\omega_{k}(d,y,\bm{x})-\omega_{k}(d_{0},y,\bm{x})
for any fixed
y\in\mathcal{Y}
. By assumption,
f_{k}(d,\bm{x})
does not depend on
y
for
d\neq k
. Next, define
g_{k}(y,\bm{x}):=\omega_{k}(d_{0},y,\bm{x})
. Then

\omega_{k}(d,y,\bm{x})=f_{k}(d,\bm{x})+\omega_{k}(d_{0},y,\bm{x})=f_{k}(d,\bm{x})+g_{k}(y,\bm{x}),

for
d\neq k
, which proves the claim.
We next prove that (b) implies (a). If
\omega_{k}(d,y,\bm{x})=f_{k}(d,\bm{x})+g_{k}(y,\bm{x})
for all
d\in\mathcal{D}\setminus\{k\}
,
then for any
d,d^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}\setminus\{k\}
we have

\omega_{k}(d,y,\bm{x})-\omega_{k}(d^{\prime},y,\bm{x})=f_{k}(d,\bm{x})+g_{k}(y,\bm{x})-(f_{k}(d^{\prime},\bm{x})+g_{k}(y,\bm{x}))=f_{k}(d,\bm{x})-f_{k}(d^{\prime},\bm{x}),

which is independent of
y
. This proves the claim.
∎
