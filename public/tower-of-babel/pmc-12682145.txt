Preparation of low molecular weight chitosan through hydroxyl radicals generated via hydrodynamic cavitation
Authors: Andraž Zupanc, Jernej Ortar, David Pahovnik, Nataša Zabukovec Logar, Blaž Stres, Alenka Šmid, Mojca Zupanc, Martin Petkovšek, Ema Žagar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

Introduction

Biomass from the food processing industry is often treated as waste and poses a burden for producers. Although it is rich in bioactive compounds, including polysaccharides, it is frequently incinerated or disposed of in the environment [1]. One such biomass is chitinous waste, the second most abundant biopolymer after cellulose and a major component of marine crustacean shells (e.g. shrimp, crab, lobster), fungi and insect exoskeletons [2,3]. Due to its high crystallinity and extensive hydrogen bonding, chitin is insoluble in common solvents. Therefore, it is partially or completely deacetylated to obtain amino-functionalized chitosan. It is estimated that global production of chitosan will increase from 107 thousand tons in 2020 to 281 thousand tons in 2027 [4]. Chitosan has vast and diverse properties, including antifungal, antibacterial and antioxidant activities, as well as wound-healing, film-forming and fiber-forming abilities. This gives it great potential for applications in various fields such as medicine (e.g., antimicrobial agent, tissue engineering, gene transfer), the food industry (antimicrobial agent, food additive, preservative), agriculture, membrane preparation for desalination and as an agent for waste-water treatment [2,3,[5], [6], [7], [8], [9]].

Despite these interesting properties, broader use of chitosan is limited by its solubility, which is restricted to acidic aqueous conditions where the amine groups are protonated [2,[10], [11], [12]], as well as by its high molecular weight and the resulting high viscosity [13]. Therefore, there is growing interest in producing low molecular weight (LMW) chitosan, which is water-soluble [14], has lower solution viscosity and exhibits better biological activity compared to high molecular weight analogues [12]. For example, LMW chitosan with a molecular weight of 46 kDa has been reported to be a safe functional food ingredient for treating obesity by preventing increases in body weight and adipose tissue weight induced by a long-term high-fat diet in mice [15]. Furthermore, LMW chitosan supplements in the 20–30 kDa range have been shown to promote the growth of weaned piglets, improve intestinal barrier function and inhibit intestinal inflammation [16]. These findings highlight the importance of controlled chitosan degradation to achieve the desired molecular weight ranges while maintaining amino functionality and promoting beneficial biological activities.

The properties of LMW chitosan depend on its molecular weight characteristics and precise chemical structure, highlighting the importance of the technology used to produce it [10,17]. LMW chitosan products can be obtained by degrading the glycosidic bonds (C-O-C) in the chitosan structure through chemical methods (addition of strong acids, H2O2 or NaNO2) [18,19], enzymatic methods (use of chitinases, chitosanases, and proteases) [13,20] or physical methods (e.g. gamma irradiation, cavitation) [3,21,22]. Chemical degradation methods have several disadvantages, such as harsh experimental conditions, high energy consumption due to high reaction temperatures, low selectivity of degradation since hydrolysis of N-acetyl side chains can also occur [23], and cleavage of the saccharide rings, which reduce reaction yield and control over the type of reaction products formed [2,3,24]. In contrast, enzymatic methods offer higher selectivity for glycosidic bond degradation and higher yield, but are impractical, time-consuming and have high production costs, limiting their wider applicability [3,12,24]. Given these drawbacks and the low availability of commercial LMW chitosan [25], new degradation methods that allow control over the molecular weight characteristics and structure of chitosan are highly desirable. Therefore, physical methods have gained attention because they offer several advantages over chemical and enzymatic methods, such as shorter treatment times, better control over degradation processes and final products, operation at ambient temperature, low cost and easy scalability [3,26]. Physical methods [27] that combine chemical and mechanical cleavage of glycosidic bonds to produce LMW chitosan [3,28,29] have received particular attention. One such method is hydrodynamic cavitation (HC) [17,22,26,[30], [31], [32]], a mechano-chemical approach with the potential to replace conventional chemical and enzymatic methods for degrading polysaccharide chains.

HC is a physical phenomenon that occurs when the static pressure within a liquid drops below its vapor pressure, resulting in the formation of small gas–vapor bubbles [33]. A local pressure drop usually occurs due to flow acceleration within constrictions (typically orifices or Venturi) or due to fast-moving objects passing through the liquid (rotor–stator cavitation generators) [34,35]. After reaching their maximum size the bubbles collapse violently, generating extreme conditions in a very small spatio-temporal region and concentrating the released energy. The collapse can generate mechanical effects such as local hot spots [36], local shear forces [37], microstreaming [38], shock waves [39] and microjets [40]. Furthermore, under extreme temperature and pressure conditions, water molecules can dissociate into reactive species (•OH and •H), causing chemical effects [41]. Research has shown that cavitation can facilitate the cleavage of 1,4-glycosidic bonds in polysaccharides through mechanical and/or chemical effects [17,26,30,31,42]. Mechanical effects cause central chain cleavage, resulting in a lower molecular weight of the polymer and a narrower molecular weight distribution, while chemical effects randomly cleave the macromolecular chains, resulting in a lower molecular weight and a broader distribution [30]. In our previous study, we showed that the cleavage of glycosidic bonds in hydroxypropyl methylcellulose occurs through cavitation caused by mechanical and chemical effects and that the degree of glycosidic bonds degradation can be manipulated by experimental conditions [31].

Although HC has already been used for the degradation of chitosan [17,22,26,30,32,43], it has not been combined with hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) for this purpose, except in the degradation of other polymer types [31,44]. Previous work on chitosan has mainly used ultrasonic cavitation instead of HC [11], indicating a significant research gap regarding the possible synergistic effect of HC and H2O2 on chitosan degradation, which has not yet been experimentally demonstrated. Thus, the purpose of the present work is to address this gap by determining the enhancement of chitosan degradation using HC in combination with H2O2 and establishing a correlation between •OH formation from H2O2 due to cavitation and the degradation efficiency of chitosan. Therefore, we systematically investigated the effects of cavitation intensity, H2O2 concentration and sample preparation procedure on chitosan degradation performance. The degree of degradation was monitored by size-exclusion chromatography coupled with a multi-angle light scattering detector and a refractive index detector (SEC/MALS-RI), while changes in chitosan structure were assessed by Fourier transform infrared (FTIR), nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy (1H and 13C NMR) and ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis) spectrophotometry, and further supported by differential scanning calorimetry (DSC), thermogravimetry (TGA) and X-ray diffraction (XRD) analyses. The novelty of this study lies in demonstrating that factors such as cavitation experimental conditions, the presence of an oxidizing agent, the chitosan preparation process, and manipulation of the reaction mixture after HC treatment influence chitosan degradation.

Materials and methods

Materials

The chitosan used in this study supplied by Glentham Life Sciences, has a specified viscosity of 3854 cps (1 % in 1 % acetic acid, 20 °C), degree of deacetylation of 90.6 %, weight average molecular weight (Mw) of 710 kDa and dispersity (Đ) of 1.67 as determined by SEC-MALS-RI. Sodium hydroxide pellets (≥99.0 %) and methanol (LiChrosolv®) suitable for liquid chromatography were obtained from Merck. High purity phenolic compounds including salicylic acid (SA) (≥99 %), 2,3- dihydroxybenzoic acid (2,3-DHBA) (99 %), 2,5-dihydroxybenzoic acid (2,5-DHBA) (98 %) and catechol (≥99 %) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich·H2O2 (30 %) was purchased from Belinka. 1 M HCl and acetic acid (>99.8 %) were supplied by Honeywell Fluka, while trifluoroacetic acid (99 %, extra pure) was obtained from Acros Organics.

Cavitation set-up

The HC was generated in a blow-through cavitation reactor (Fig. 1), first described in detail in [45]. The device consists of two reservoirs (1), each with a volume of 2 L, connected by an experimental test section (2) – with exchangeable orifice plates (3). The pressure difference between the reservoirs drives the liquid through the test section and determines the cavitation conditions formed downstream of the orifice. The number of passes (Np) (1 pass is defined as pushing the entire sample from one reservoir to the other through the constriction) was monitored via Labview software and controlled with a liquid level sensor (4). To regulate the sample temperature, a cooling coil (5) was installed in one of the reservoirs and connected to a DuraChill CA03 cooling device (cooling capacity: 1.28 kW). The temperature was monitored using a PT100 RTD (6). To visualize cavitation downstream of the orifice, the entire test section was made of transparent acrylic glass. The test section had a rectangular flow channel (13 × 13 mm2) to avoid disturbing visualization. The orifice geometry was designed in such a way that the number of holes varied, but the constriction cross-section (Aconstric.) and the ratio between constriction and free flow cross-section (Aconstric.Afreeflow) remained constant.Fig. 1Experimental cavitation reactor with enlarged test section and orifice plate designs. Cavitation characterization

The characterization of cavitation formed downstream of the orifice plates was systematically investigated through visualization and •OH formation. The effects of the number of holes on the orifice plates, the pressure difference between the reservoirs, and the addition of H2O2 on cavitation development and •OH formation were examined. All visualizations and •OH formation measurements were conducted at a sample temperature of 20 °C. High-speed visualization was performed using a Photron FastCam SA-Z high-speed camera at 100,000 fps with a resolution of 640 × 280 pixels in 12-bit monochrome mode. Using high-power LED illumination, the shutter speed was reduced to 1 µs and the aperture was kept half open. The formation of •OH was determined after 800 Np using SA dosimetry, as described in detail elsewhere [46]. Briefly, for the determination of SA products, 1 L of a 300 mg L-1 SA solution was acidified with HCl to a pH below 2.5 prior to the HC experiments. The SA products were analyzed using a high-performance liquid chromatography system (HPLC Agilent Infinity 1260) with a diode array detector and a LiChrospher® 60 RP-Select B column (MZ-Analysentechnik GmbH, Germany). For these analyses, 1 mL of the sample was taken after 0 and 800 Np. To evaluate the contribution of the external oxidant H2O2 to •OH formation, an additional experiment (H5H2O2 in Fig. 3) was performed at 700 kPa.

Experimental design

The original chitosan (C0) used in degradation experiments had a Mw of 710 kDa and a Đ of 1.67. Degradation experiments included control experiments, in which solutions of chitosan and H2O2 were stirred with a magnetic stirrer, and experiments with cavitation under different conditions (Table 1, Suppl. 1). The study was systematically designed to investigate the influence of experimental parameters such as number of holes (1, 3, and 5), pressure difference (300, 500, and 700 kPa), HC time (200, 400, 600 and 800 Np), H2O2 concentration, chitosan preparation procedure and pH of the medium on the effectiveness of chitosan degradation. The pressure difference for the cavitation experiments was selected based on preliminary observations of cavitation activity. A pressure below 300 kPa produced very weak cavitation, whereas 700 kPa represented the upper stable operating limit of our system. An intermediate pressure of 500 kPa was chosen to allow comparison across the pressure range. The volume of chitosan solutions for the control experiments C1-C3 was 333 mL, while 1 L of chitosan in the form of solution, gel or suspension was used for the cavitation experiments T1-T14. The control experiments were carried out for 48 min, which corresponds to 800 Np using the H5 orifice at 700 kPa. Procedures for preparation of chitosan for the degradation experiments

For each experiment, chitosan was freshly prepared as a solution, gel or suspension, at a concentration of 1 g L-1. Chitosan solutions were prepared by dissolving chitosan in aqueous CH3COOH solution with a pH of 4.4 (Table 1: T1 − T7). Chitosan in the form of a gel was prepared from the chitosan solutions by adding NaOH, which induced gelation. NaOH was added in amounts sufficient to adjust the pH of the medium to 6, 7 or 8.5 (Table 1: T8 − T11). Chitosan suspensions were prepared by dispersing original chitosan in MQ water (Suppl. 1: T12 − T14). When investigating the addition of an oxidizing agent, H2O2 was added to the chitosan solution, gel or suspension before starting the experiment.

The extent of chitosan degradation was monitored by measuring the reduction in chitosan molecular weight using SEC coupled with a MALS detector (DAWN HELEOS-II, Wyatt Technology Corporation, USA) and an RI detector (Optilab T-rEX, Wyatt Technology Corporation, USA). Size separation of chitosan was performed at room temperature on an Agilent 1260 HPLC chromatograph (Agilent Technologies, USA) using a TSKgel G5000PWXL-CP analytical column (7.8 x 300 mm, 10 μm) with precolumn (both columns from Tosoh Bioscience, Japan) and 0.2 M CH3COONa solution in MQ water as the mobile phase. The pH of the mobile phase was adjusted to 4.4 with acetic acid. The nominal flow rate of the eluent was 0.5 mL min−1. The concentration of the chitosan solutions was typically 1 mg mL−1 and the injection volume was 100 µL. The input parameter required to determine the molecular weight characteristics of chitosan is the specific refractive index increment (dn/dc = 0.174 mL g−1), which was determined by assuming 100 % mass recovery of the original chitosan from the column. After HC treatment, the filtered chitosan solutions with a pH of 4.4 were injected directly onto the SEC column. The gel samples treated at pH 7 and 8.5 were not completely soluble, so acetic acid was added to adjust the pH to 4.4, which allows chitosan to dissolve at the molecular level. The mass recovery of all degraded chitosan samples was generally above 94 %. The molecular weight characteristics of the samples obtained in experiments C1, C2 and T1 − T10 were also determined by precipitating chitosan from the solutions prior to the SEC-MALS measurements by adjusting the pH to 9 − 10 with NaOH solution. The precipitated chitosan samples were centrifuged, rinsed with deionized water and freeze-dried. Astra 7.3.1 software (Wyatt Technology Corp., USA) was used for data acquisition and evaluation.

The effects of cavitation treatment of chitosan in the presence and absence of H2O2 on structural changes were studied by FTIR, NMR and UV–vis, while the effects of these structural changes on the crystallinity and thermal properties of chitosan were investigated by XRD, DSC and TGA. For this purpose, HC/H2O2-treated chitosan was precipitated from the solutions according to the procedure described in the above paragraph for SEC/MALS-RI measurements of precipitated chitosan samples. FTIR spectra of the chitosan samples were recorded in attenuated total reflectance mode using a Spectrum Two FTIR spectrophotometer (Perkin-Elmer, Waltham, MA, USA). Scanning was performed at a resolution of 4 cm−1 over a wavenumber range of 400–4000 cm−1. 1H and 13C NMR spectra were recorded using a Bruker AVANCE NEO 600 MHz instrument (Bruker Corporation, USA) at room temperature in D2O with added trifluoroacetic acid to dissolve the chitosan. The delay before applying the pulse was 6 s and the acquisition time was 2 s for a total relaxation time of 8 s between each transient. Chemical shifts are expressed in ppm relative to an internal standard (trimethylsilyl)-2,2,3,3-tetradeuteropropionic acid (TMSP-d4). The average degree of deacetylation (DD) was determined by 1H NMR according to the method described by Hirai et al. [47,48] based on the signal intensities of the methyl hydrogens of the acetamido group with the chemical shift of δ 2.1 ppm and H2–H6 of the 2-amino-2-deoxy-D-glucopyranose (GlcN) and 2-acetamido-2-deoxy-D-glucopyranose (GlcNAc) units between δ 4.1–3.0 ppm. UV–Vis spectra of chitosan solutions at a concentration of 1mg mL−1 were recorded on a Nanocolor UV–Vis II spectrophotometer (Macherey-Nagel, Germany). DSC thermograms were recorded using a DSC-1 calorimeter (Mettler Toledo, Switzerland). Samples of approximately 5 mg were sealed in standard 40 µL aluminum pans and subjected to DSC analysis in the range of 25 °C to 400 °C at a heating rate of 10 K min−1 under nitrogen supply. TGA were performed on a TGA-1 instrument (Mettler Toledo, Switzerland) in the range of 25 to 600 °C under nitrogen atmosphere (20 mL min−1) at a heating rate of 10 K min−1. At 600 °C, the atmosphere was switched to oxygen (20 mL min−1) and the samples were further heated to 1000 °C at the same heating rate. The mass of each sample was 5–6 mg. Powder XRD was performed using a PANalytical X’Pert PRO diffractometer (Malvern Panalytical B.V., Almelo, The Netherlands) with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5418 Å). Data were collected in the 3–60° 2θ range with a step size of 0.033° and a measuring time of 500 s per step. The patterns were evaluated using the X’Pert HighScore Plus program package 4.9 (Malvern Panalytical B.V., Almelo, The Netherlands).

Results and discussion

Cavitation characterization

The differences in cavitation dynamics in water under various operating conditions (variation of orifice geometry and pressure difference, Fig. 1) were visually assessed using high-speed photography (Fig. 2). Increasing the pressure difference between the reservoirs (from 300 to 700 kPa) significantly enhances cavitation and causes its development over a larger volume downstream of the channel for all orifice plate designs. As the number of holes increases, the overall length of cavitation decreases at the same pressure difference, as shown in Suppl. 2. At the same time, the size of residual non-cavitating bubbles in the flow increases with higher pressure differences. Their movement clearly demonstrates the complex, highly turbulent three-dimensional flow and serves as an indicator for the propagation of shock waves through the channel. As the shock waves pass through the flow, the compression of these bubbles can be observed as a shadow moving through the fluid [49]. The frequency of the shock waves increases with both rising pressure and the number of holes in the orifices, due to the greater number of collapsing cavitation clouds. Analysis of individual cavitation cloud volumes reveals that the largest clouds (viewed in cross-section) form at H1 with the highest pressure difference. This is attributed to the largest diameter of a single hole, which naturally generates larger cavitation clouds. Comparison of cavitation dynamics at H5 and 700 kPa pressure difference between water and water with added H2O2 (c = 3.0 g L-1) shows clear differences. While cavitation in water is characterized by high-frequency shedding of smaller cavitation structures, water with added H2O2 tends to form larger cavitation clouds, resulting in a lower shedding frequency (Fig. 2, Fig. 3, Fig. 4, Fig. 5, Fig. 6, Δp = 700 kPa). The observed shock waves in water/H2O2 appear more intense, likely due to the formation and collapse of larger cavitation clouds – cavitation clouds are formed in a “more compact bubble cluster”, especially when observing H5. Formation of •OH radicals

High local temperatures generated during the collapse of cavitation bubbles lead to the formation of •OH in the gas phase after homolytic cleavage of H2O molecules [50]. The •OH formed can be indirectly quantified using various dosimetry reactions. SA dosimetry is often the preferred method for determining the amount of •OH in various advanced oxidation processes, including cavitation [[51], [52], [53]]. To enable SA to reach the gas–liquid interface where •OH are located after bubble collapse, the pH of the SA solution is lowered below its pKa of 2.97 before the experiments, ensuring that SA is present in its protonated form. Due to the electrophilic nature of •OH, they attack the electron-rich sites of SA by hydroxylation, resulting in the formation of mainly three degradation products, i.e. 2,3-DHBA, 2,3-DHBA and catechol [46].

Fig. 3 shows the sum of the concentrations of the SA products after 800 Np using different orifice plates and inlet pressures. The results indicate that a lower number of holes favors radical formation at all pressure differences studied and the highest concentration of SA products was achieved at 700 kPa (1.2 µg mL−1). These results are consistent with previous observations showing that increased pressure favors •OH formation [52,53]. Additionally, increasing the number of holes appears to negatively affect •OH yield, regardless of the pressure difference. For the experiment investigating the effect of H2O2 addition on •OH production, the H5 orifice and 700 kPa were chosen, although the H1 orifice appears to favor radical formation (Fig. 3: H5H2O2). These conditions were selected because previous studies on chitosan degradation using HC showed higher effectiveness with orifice plates having more holes [26], which is also consistent with our results showing a decrease in chitosan molecular weight with an increasing number of holes (Table 1: T1-T3). Furthermore, visualization showed a higher number of individual cavitation clouds when H2O2 was added to H5 compared to H1 and H3 (Suppl. 4). This results in a higher frequency of shock waves generated, as discussed in Chapter 3.1, which could indicate that more •OH are generated from H2O2, leading to enhanced chemical effects. Fig. 3, shows that the addition of H2O2 significantly increases the yield of •OH (31.3 ± 5.9 µg mL−1), confirming that H2O2 is decomposed to •OH by cavitation, as also reported in [54].

Chitosan degradation experiments

The experiments on chitosan degradation (Table 1) include control experiments in which H2O2 was added to a chitosan solution (C1 − C3) and then stirred with a magnetic stirrer for 48 min, corresponding to a treatment time of Np 800 at H5 and Δp of 700 kPa. The effect of HC on chitosan degradation was investigated by optimizing the number of holes on the orifice plate (T1-T3) and the pressure difference between the reservoirs (T3-T5). The effect of an external oxidizing agent (H2O2) in combination with HC was studied in experiments T6-T7, while the effect of the preparation procedure on chitosan degradation efficiency was examined in T7-T14 (Table 1, Suppl. 1).

Effect of H2O2 on chitosan degradation (control experiments)

The effect of H2O2 addition on chitosan degradation was evaluated in control experiments where chitosan solutions were treated with H2O2 (Table 1: C1 − C3). Compared to the Mw of the original chitosan (710 kDa), the Mw of H2O2-treated chitosan decreased to 620 kDa and 390 kDa at pH 4.4 and H2O2 concentrations of 0.3 g L-1 and 3.0 g L-1, respectively, and to 420 kDa at pH 6 and H2O2 concentration of 3.0 g L-1. These results show that a higher H2O2 concentration facilitates the oxidative cleavage of 1,4-glycosidic bonds, consistent with previous observations [[55], [56], [57]], while pH has a much smaller effect. The •OH formed by H2O2 decomposition are efficient oxidizing agents that abstract the hydrogen bound to the carbon in the N-acetyl-d-glucosamine and/or d-glucosamine repeating units of chitosan [58]. The resulting carbohydrate radicals then lead to cleavage of the β-1,4-glycosidic bonds by rearrangement, reducing the chitosan molecular weight. The 1H, 13C NMR and FTIR spectra of H2O2-treated chitosan show no additional signals/bands compared to the original chitosan, indicating that the structure of the degraded chitosan is largely preserved (Suppl. 5). The DD determined by 1H NMR is 91 % for the original chitosan (C0), 92 % for the chitosan samples treated with H2O2 at pH 4.4 (C2) and 91 % for the chitosan treated with H2O2 at pH 6 (C3). These results reveal that •OH primarily attack the repeating units of d-glucosamine, while the acetyl groups of the N-acetyl-d-glucosamine repeating units are unaffected, which is consistent with previous studies on the oxidative treatment of chitosan with H2O2, especially at moderate degrees of chitosan degradation, i.e. up to a chitosan molecular weight of a few 10 kDa [55,56,[58], [59], [60], [61]]. The extent of chitosan degradation is difficult to compare with previous reports because the reduction in molecular weight in this study was followed by SEC/MALS-RI, while in literature reports the molecular weight of degradation products was determined either by viscosity [25,58,60] or by relative SEC using polysaccharide standards for column calibration [55,56,59,61]. In addition, the experimental conditions for chitosan degradation in these studies differ from ours in terms of the type of acid used to dissolve the chitosan and its concentration, the H2O2 concentration, the reaction temperature, and/or the initial molecular weight of the chitosan and its concentration in solution.

Effect of cavitation conditions on chitosan degradation

The effect of the number of holes on the orifice plate at a constant pressure difference of 700 kPa on chitosan degradation was investigated in experiments T1-T3 (Table 1). The results in Fig. 4 show that the Mw of HC-treated chitosan decreased from 710 to 550, 530 and 510 kDa as the number of holes increased from 1 to 3 and 5, respectively. Since the reduction in chitosan molecular weight was highest for the H5 orifice (T3), it was selected for further experiments to investigate the effects of pressure difference on chitosan degradation (Table 1: T3-T5). When the pressure was increased from 300 to 500 and 700 kPa, the degradation efficiency of chitosan improved, as evidenced by the decrease in Mw to 640, 570 and 510 kDa, respectively (Fig. 4: T3-T5). These results are a consequence of a larger pressure drop across the entire orifice plate [22,26], resulting in higher velocities, which means that cavitation extends over a larger volume and the number of bubbles is consequently higher, ultimately leading to more intense mechanical and chemical effects [30,43]. The same conclusions can be drawn from the visualization shown in Fig. 2 and discussed in Section 3.1. The higher number of holes and the higher-pressure difference have a positive effect on chitosan degradation, which is consistent with previous results [22,26,30,32,43]. However, when HC alone is used, the reduction in the molecular weight of chitosan is limited to a few hundred kDa. Compared to the original chitosan, the FTIR and NMR spectra of chitosan treated under extreme HC conditions (T3) show no changes in DD (91 %) or structure (Fig. 5). These results are consistent with previous reports [17,22,26,43] and are similar to those for H2O2-treated chitosan (Suppl. 5), again suggesting preservation of the chitosan structure and preferential cleavage of the glycosidic bonds in the d-glucosamine repeating units of chitosan.

Synergistic effect of cavitation and H2O2 addition on chitosan degradation

Next, chitosan solutions at pH 4.4 were treated with an H5 orifice at a pressure of 700 kPa in the presence of H2O2. Prolonged HC treatment (200, 400, 600 and 800 Np; Suppl. 1: T6-T6c) of the chitosan solution with added H2O2 resulted in more efficient polymer degradation, which is in-line with previous results [44]. After 800 Np, the Mw of HC-treated chitosan at pH 4.4 and at 0.3 and 3.0 g L-1 H2O2 reached 360 and 250 kDa, respectively (Table 1: T6, T7), showing in both cases more efficient degradation of chitosan compared to chitosan treated with HC alone under the same cavitation conditions, or with H2O2 alone (Table 1: T3, C1, C2). The observed synergistic effect of cavitation in the presence of an oxidizing agent can be explained by the fact that cavitation facilitates the degradation of H2O2 to •OH, as shown in Fig. 3, which in combination with mechanical forces leads to the cleavage of the chitosan glycosidic bonds. These results are consistent with previous reports in which chitosan was degraded by a combination of HC and NaNO2 [32], by a combination of discrete acoustic cavitation and H2O2 [11], and by acoustic cavitation in combination with H2O2 and ascorbic acid [62]. Compared to the original chitosan, the FTIR and NMR characterization results of HC-treated chitosan samples in the presence of H2O2 show no changes in chemical structure (T7 in Fig. 5), which is consistent with previous studies showing that chitosan retains its structure when a combination of cavitation and H2O2 is used [11,62].

Effect of chitosan preparation procedure for the degradation experiments

The previous experiments were performed on chitosan solution prepared by dissolving chitosan in acidic water at pH 4.4. In the last series of experiments, we studied how the preparation procedure of chitosan for the HC/H2O2 experiments affects chitosan degradation. For this purpose, chitosan was prepared in a two-step procedure (Table 1: T8–T11); first, chitosan was dissolved in acidic water at pH 4.4 (as in previous experiments) and then the pH was adjusted to 6, 7 or 8.5 with NaOH. At pH 6, the chitosan remains dissolved, whereas at pH 7 and 8.5 it forms a gel. The chitosan samples prepared in this way were subjected to HC treatment with an H5 orifice at a pressure difference of 700 kPa and 800 Np in the presence and absence of H2O2. The Mw of HC-treated chitosan at pH 6 in the absence of H2O2 is 470 kDa (T8), which is lower than that of HC-treated chitosan under the same conditions at pH 4.4 (T3: 510 kDa), indicating more efficient chitosan degradation at higher pH. This suggests that the mechanical effects of cavitation may play a greater role in the degradation of chitosan in the associated form or that more •OH are formed during cavitation at higher pH. C3 experiment, where the chitosan solution at pH 6 was treated solely with 3.0 g L-1 H2O2, the Mw of chitosan was reduced to 420 kDa, which is slightly less than that of H2O2-treated chitosan at pH 4.4 (C2: 390 kDa), indicating easier accessibility of chitosan to •OH at pH 4.4, where it is molecularly dissolved. In contrast, the Mw of chitosan decreased to 170, 80 and 30 kDa, respectively, when chitosan solution/gels were treated with HC at pH 6, 7 and 8.5 in the presence of the same amount of H2O2 (3.0 g L-1) (Table 1: T9 − T11, Fig. 4B). This decrease in chitosan molecular weight is significantly greater than for chitosan solution treated at pH 4.4 under the same HC conditions and in the presence of the same amount of H2O2 (T7: 250 kDa). These results suggest that the formation of highly reactive •OH during HC treatment and in the presence of H2O2 is more pronounced in neutral/slightly alkaline medium [11,62], where the chitosan amino groups are present to a greater extent in deprotonated form and may increase the instability of H2O2, leading to accelerated formation of •OH.

To accurately assess the influence of pH, chitosan was also suspended in water and in water at pH 8.5 without prior dissolution at pH 4.4 (Suppl. 1: T12 − T13). The chitosan samples prepared in this way were subjected to HC under the same conditions in the presence of 3.0 g L-1 H2O2. The Mw of chitosan decreased to 370 and 480 kDa, respectively, indicating much less chitosan degradation than in chitosan treated under the same conditions in gel or solution form. This is most likely because chitosan in suspension, due to strong hydrogen-bonding interactions, is inaccessible to •OH except at the surface of the suspended particles. In contrast, chitosan in gel form is much more prone to degradation, since the hydrogen-bond network of chitosan is destroyed during the dissolution step at pH 4.4 before gelation is triggered by adjusting the pH to a higher value.

The influence of pH on chitosan degradation is also evident in the experiment monitoring the time dependence of chitosan degradation at pH 7 and 4.4 after chitosan was treated with HC in the presence of H2O2 at pH 7 (sample from experiment T10). In this experiment, part of the T10 sample was acidified to pH 4.4. Regardless of the medium pH, the molecular weight of chitosan continued to decrease over time (Suppl. 6), indicating persistent •OH activity even after the HC experiment ended. However, the chitosan degradation over time is more efficient when the sample is stored at higher pH, as shown by the reduction in Mw to 14 and 7 kDa for the T10 sample stored for 18 h at pH 4.4 and 7, respectively (Suppl. 6).

Further evidence for the influence of pH on chitosan degradation is that HC– and H2O2-treated samples, especially those treated with HC in combination with H2O2 show significantly lower molecular weight averages after isolation (precipitation by adjusting the pH to 9 − 10, washing the precipitate, and freeze-drying) than samples injected onto the SEC column directly after HC/H2O2 treatment (Table 1: *Mw column). These results support the accelerated degradation of chitosan in neutral/slightly alkaline medium [63] and a higher amount of •OH formed during HC treatment in the presence of H2O2, which is consistent with the results of cavitation characterization (Section 3.1 and Fig. 3). The molecular weight distributions of the isolated chitosan samples are slightly broader (higher dispersity, Đ) than those of chitosan samples injected directly onto the SEC column. This can be attributed to the persistent activity of •OH after the experiment, which randomly cleaved the glycosidic bonds in the chitosan structure [30]. The FTIR, 13C and 1H NMR spectra of HC-treated chitosan in the absence or presence of H2O2 at different pH values show no significant differences from the original polymer, only the resolution in the NMR spectra improved with decreasing Mw, indicating a largely preserved chitosan structure (Fig. 5: T7 and T10) with unchanged DD values (91 %) as determined by 1H NMR.

Since FTIR and NMR results show no significant chemical changes in the backbone of the degraded chitosan samples down to a Mw of 20 kDa, we performed additional analyses (DSC, TGA, XRD and UV–Vis) to assess changes in thermal properties, crystallinity and detailed structural changes of representative chitosan samples due to degradation; i.e·H2O2-treated chitosan at pH 4.4 (C2), HC-treated chitosan at pH 4.4 in the absence (T3) and presence of H2O2 (T7; Fig. 6). DSC, TGA, XRD and UV–Vis analyses were performed on precipitated C2, T3 and T7 chitosan samples, which have Mw values of 350, 470 and 20 kDa, respectively. Their DSC thermograms show an endothermic peak at ∼ 100 °C, due to the loss of adsorbed water and an exothermic peak at 298 − 304 °C due to chitosan decomposition (Fig. 6A). The DSC thermograms are consistent with the TGA curves, which indicate water loss up to 150 °C and the main thermal degradation process at about 293 − 298 °C. The mass loss above 600 °C is due to combustion of the samples as the atmosphere changes from nitrogen to oxygen. A close comparison shows that the TGA curves of HC-only (T3) and H2O2-only (C2) treated chitosan samples are comparable to the original chitosan (C0), while HC/H2O2-treated chitosan (T7) shows slightly faster thermal degradation (higher mass loss in the 196 − 265 °C region; Fig. 6B). The XRD patterns of the original and degraded chitosan samples show similar crystallinity pattern, but the H2O2-treated (C2) and H2O2/HC-treated (T7) samples display a slightly more intense peak at 18° 2θ compared to the other samples, which is attributed to their lower molecular weight averages and a higher concentration of chain end-groups that may influence the stacking of the chitosan chains into ordered structures (Fig. 6C). In contrast to the original chitosan, the UV–Vis absorption spectra of C2, T3 and T7 show a low intensity absorption band between 260 − 330 nm, indicating the presence of small amounts of chromophore groups in the structure of degraded chitosan samples. Since the intensity of this band increases in the order C0 < T3 < C2 < T7, which is consistent with the corresponding decrease in molecular weight, the chromophore groups are likely located at the termini of the chitosan chains as a result of oxidative cleavage of the glycosidic bonds [63]. All these results reveal that degraded chitosan samples retain the backbone of the original chitosan, while oxidative cleavage of the glycosidic bonds by •OH leads to the formation of chromophore groups at the chain ends, however, their concentration is below the detection limit of FTIR and NMR. Compared to previously published studies that report higher temperatures (40 − 60 °C) and longer treatment times (Suppl. 7), this study achieved a comparable reduction in the molecular weight of chitosan while preserving its backbone structure in a shorter time at room temperature, indicating a potentially faster and milder degradation process. Nevertheless, a limitation of the present work is that the detailed composition of the degradation products was not analyzed. For future studies, particularly after reaching low molecular weight fractions (several kDa), detailed chromatographic analyses (e.g., HPLC-MS or HPAC-PAD) would be desirable to identify the exact chemical composition of degradation products and their relative content. Such analyses would further strengthen the findings and provide deeper insights into the degradation mechanism, advancing the understanding established in this study.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that HC combined with H2O2 is a highly effective and controllable method for producing LMW chitosan under mild and environmentally friendly conditions. We confirmed that cavitation intensity, governed by the pressure difference between the inlet and outlet sides of the orifice and optimized orifice plate design, enhances •OH formation and promotes efficient chitosan degradation. The novelty of this study lies in showing that not only the hydrodynamic and oxidative conditions but also factors such as the chitosan sample preparation process and post-treatment handling significantly influence degradation efficiency. Importantly, the method reduces chitosan molecular weight (from 710 kDa to 20–30 kDa) without altering its backbone structure, ensuring preservation of functional properties. Compared with conventional chemical and enzymatic approaches, this mechano-chemical strategy offers a faster, milder, and more sustainable alternative, highlighting its potential for scalable and eco-friendly production of bioactive chitosan.
