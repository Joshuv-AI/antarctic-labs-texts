Exercise-induced hypertrophic preconditioning alleviates myocardial ischemic injury through trained immunity of macrophages
Authors: Jichen Liu, Zhihong Li, Xinnan Wei, Dongxiao Xie, Qi Chen, Kaitong Chen, Cankun Zheng, Mingjue Li, Mingyuan He, Mengjia Shen, Lu Chen, Chiyu Liu, Wangjun Liao, Jianping Bin, Ziyun Guan, Qiancheng Wang, Yulin Liao, Hairuo Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPhysical exercise is known to induce trained immunity and improve function of macrophages, and we recently reported that exercise-induced hypertrophic preconditioning (EHP) could protect pathological hypertrophic heart, but it remains unclear whether EHP can mitigate myocardial ischemic injury. Here we hypothesized that EHP would alleviate acute myocardial ischemic injury through trained immunity of macrophages.MethodsSwimming or treadmill running training was used to induce EHP in C57 mice. After 1-week of exercise termination, mice were subjected to myocardial ischemia/reperfusion (IR). IR injury and inflammatory response and cell energy metabolism reprogramming of bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) were analysed. Moreover, the effects of exercise on myocardial ischemic injury or long-term survival in patients with acute myocardial infarction (AMI) or history of MI were retrospectively analysed.ResultsEHP mice had a significantly smaller infarct size and less apoptotic cardiomyocytes than IR mice without EHP. At 4 weeks after IR, EHP mice had better cardiac function and less myocardial fibrosis. We noted less infiltration and polarization of myocardial M1 macrophages, higher expression of M2 signature anti-inflammatory factors in BMDMs of EHP mice. EHP promoted tricarboxylic acid cycle, mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) and adenosine 5’-monophosphate activated protein kinase (AMPK) phosphorylation, decreased glycolysis and phosphorylation of AKT and mTOR in BMDMs. Importantly, AMPK inhibitor treatment abrogated the cardioprotective effects of EHP, abolishing the improvement in infarct size and apoptosis and reversing the metabolic reprogramming of macrophages. In mice received treadmill running training for 6 weeks, no physiological hypertrophy occurred but myocardial infarct size after IR was significantly reduced. Similarly, in response to AMI, patients with exercise habit had higher left ventricular ejection fraction and lower plasma levels of cardiac troponin T than those sedentary AMI patients. In 2457 patients with self-reported history of MI, patients with regular exercise habit had significantly lower post-MI mortality.ConclusionsEHP protects heart against myocardial ischemic injury through trained immunity of macrophage mediated by energy metabolism reprogramming.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07359-5.

Introduction

Ischemic heart disease (IHD) contributes to about 70% incidence of heart failure. Regular physical exercise has been recommended as a nonpharmacological intervention to improve cardiovascular fitness [1], reduce the risk of IHD and cardiac dysfunction [2, 3]. Athletes who engage in high intensity exercise also have a lower incidence of IHD [4]. Similarly, continuous aerobic exercise in animals also reduces myocardial infarct size and improves cardiac dysfunction after myocardial ischemic/reperfusion (IR) insult [5, 6], while our previous study demonstrated that an exercise-induced antihypertrophic memory exists even after termination of exercise and protects heart from pathological cardiac hypertrophy, a phenomena we called exercise-induced hypertrophic preconditioning (EHP) [7, 8]. Interestingly, high-intensity interval training (HIIT) in human is believed to inhibit cardiac remodelling and improving heart failure [9] a phenomena similar to EHP. However, whether EHP exerts protective effect on myocardial IR injury? If so, what are the underlying mechanisms? Except for athletes, exercise in general population is usually insufficient to induce physiological myocardial hypertrophy (PMH), does EHP can be induced in the absence of PMH? To answer these questions, we designed this study.

Physical exercise can act as an immunostimulant therapy known as “trained immunity [10, 11]. Previous studies have demonstrated that exercise could regulate inflammatory immune response [12], which is believed to play a pivotal role in myocardial IR injury. In the early stage of myocardial IR, immune cells including neutrophils, M1 macrophages, Ly6Chigh monocytes are recruited in heart and secreted pro-inflammatory factors to damage myocardium, while in the late stage, immune cells such as M2 macrophages, Ly6Chigh monocytes, T regulatory (Treg) cells help to repair myocardium. It was reported that exercise could protect heart through regulating inflammatory immune response after myocardial IR injury [13] However, little is known on whether EHP can mitigate IR injury by beneficially regulating inflammatory immune response. We hypothesized that EHP could protect heart from myocardial IR injury through trained immunity of macrophages.

In this study, we focused on the delayed effect rather than the immediate effect of exercise on myocardial ischemic injury in mice with/without PMH history and in patients unlikely to develop PMH.

Methods

The data, methods, and materials in this research were available to other researchers upon reasonable request.

All animal experiments were performed in accordance with guidelines of the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (NIH Publication No. 85 − 23, revised 1996). C57BL/6 male mice (8–10 weeks old and weighing 20–25 g) were provided by the Animal Centre of Southern Medical University. The detailed information for animal number in each group presents in Supplemental Table I. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Nanfang Hospital, Southern Medical University (Guangzhou, China) and all the participating patients agreed to use of their medical records for research purposes.

Exercise protocols

Swimming training. Male and female mice were randomly divided into sedentary (SED) group and swimming group. The swimming protocol was carried out as we previously described [7]. Briefly, mice swam twice a day for 15 min each time at the 1st day, then 15 min was increased to each swimming time every two days until 90 min each time and the 90-minute swimming programs were continued for the next 7 days. Mice swam 5 days a week followed by 2-day rest, and the swimming training was lasted for a total of 21 days. One week after the end of exercise training, all mice underwent myocardial IR injury or a sham operation.

Treadmill running. Male mice subjected to treadmill running group ran 5 days a week as previous described [14]. At the first week, mice ran with gradual speeds for 30 min (8, 11, 15, 18 and 22 cm/s, 4° slope, 6 min each speed). For another 2 or 5 weeks, mice ran for 60 min per day (22 cm/s, 4° slope). One week after the end of exercise training, all mice underwent myocardial IR injury.

Generation of IR model

Mice were anesthetized with ketamine (100 mg/kg) and xylazine (5 mg/kg), intubated, and ventilated with a rodent ventilator (Harvard Apparatus, USA). The left thoracotomy was performed between the 3rd and 4th ribs and the pericardium was stripped to expose the heart. An 8 − 0 silk suture was passed around the left coronary arteries (LCA) approximately 1 mm beneath the edge of left atrial appendage. LCA was ligated with slipknot for myocardial ischemia. The success of myocardial ischemia was judged by ST segment elevation of electrocardiogram (ECG) (Supplemental Figure IA). After 45 min of ischemia, the slipknot was then released and the chest was closed. The mice of sham groups underwent the same procedure except arterial ligation. Animals that suffered permanent myocardial infarction judged by ECG or echocardiography or had been infected or dead before the designated test time point were excluded from the study based on predefined exclusion criteria. The detailed information for animal number in each group is presented in Supplemental Table I.

Assessment of myocardial infarct size

Infarct size was evaluated as described previously [15]. Briefly, the left coronary artery of mice was re-ligated at previous site 24 h later after reperfusion. Evans blue (1%, 300 µl. Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was injected into proximal ascending aorta after clipping distal ascending aorta. The heart was washed and frozen immediately. We cut the cardiac tissue below the ligature into five slices, and then incubated them with 1% 2,3,5-triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TTC; Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 30 min at 37℃ and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 1 h, the slices were photographed and left ventricular (LV) area, area at risk (AAR), and infarct size (IS) were measured using Image J software.

Echocardiography

Cardiac remodelling and function after surgery in mice were assessed by echocardiography with a Vevo 2100 system (Fujifilm Visual Sonics, Canada). Mice were anaesthetized with 3% isoflurane and then placed in a supine position on a warming pad. The inspired concentration of isoflurane was adjusted to maintain the heart at 450–550 beats/min. We recorded the M-mode images in short axis to evaluate LV end-diastolic and systolic diameters (LVEDd, LVESd), LV posterior and anterior wall thickness at diastolic (LVPWd, LVAWd), LV ejection fraction (EF) and LV fractional shortening (LVFS) were calculated [16].

Cardiac function in patients was evaluated using echocardiography with with Sequoia 512 system (Siemens, Germany), including LVEF, left atrial diameter (LAd), LV diameter (LVd), right atrial diameter (RAd), right ventricle diameter (RVd), LVEDd, LVPWd and interventricular septal thickness at diastole (IVSd) were assessed by experienced cardiac sonographers in 24 h after admission to hospital. The LV mass (LVM) was calculated using the Devereux formula: LVM (g) = 0.8 × 10.4×[(IVSd + LVPWd + LVIDd)3-LVIDd3] + 0.6.

Flow cytometry

Cardiac cell suspensions were prepared as previously described [17].Briefly, cardiac tissue below the ligature was digested with 1 mg/mL type II collagenase (Worthington, USA), 100 U/mL elastase (Biochemical Corporation, USA), and 100 U/mL DNase I (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) on a shaker (37 °C, 220 rpm) for 1 h. Subsequently, the cell suspension was passed through a 70 μm cell strainer (Becton Dickinson (BD) Falcon, USA). After erythrocyte lysis with lysing buffer (BD, USA) and centrifugation, single cells were collected and suspended in 1 mL phosphate buffer saline (PBS) supplemented with 1% Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA). Subsequently, single cell suspensions were incubated with a mixture of antibodies (DAPI, anti-CD45, anti-CD11b, anti-Ly6G, anti-Ly6C and anti-MHC-II) for 30 min at room temperature. After washed with PBS, cell samples were measured and quantified by flow cytometry (BD LSRFortessa, USA). The obtained results were presented as the percent or cell number per microgram of heart and analyzed using FlowJo software.

Cell culture

Mouse bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) were harvested and cultured as previously described [18]. In brief, bone marrow cells were isolated from femurs and tibias of mice and passed through a 70 μm cell strainer. After erythrocyte lysis, cell suspensions were cultured in dulbecco’s modified eagle medium (DMEM) supplemented with 20% L929-cell conditioned medium, 10% fetal calf plasma (Gibco, USA), 1% penicillin and streptomycin at 37℃ in 5% CO2. After 7-day culture, the suspended cells were discarded, and the adherent cells (BMDMs) were cultured for further experiments.

TUNEL staining

Mouse heart tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded paraffin and sectioned at a thickness of 4 μm. Sections at papillary muscle level were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and finally washed twice in PBS for 5 min. The remaining steps were performed following the instruction manual of the terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase–mediated deoxyuridinetriphate nick-end labeling (TUNEL) staining kit (Roche, Switzerland). The nuclei were stained with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI). Images were captured using confocal microscopy (Leica, Germany) and quantified using the Image J software.

Western blot

Total proteins were extracted from cardiac apex of mice with a tissue protein extraction reagent (Thermo Scientific, USA) or from BMDMs of mice with RIPA lysis buffer (FDBIO Science, China) containing proteinase and phosphatase inhibitors. The concentration of protein was evaluated with a BCA Protein Assay kit (BestBio, China). Western blotting was performed as described elsewhere [19]. Briefly, proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE electrophoresis and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). The blots were incubated with specific primary antibodies (Supplemental Table II) at 4 °C overnight. After incubation with secondary antibodies conjugated to HRP at room temperature for 1 h, target proteins were detected using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagents and quantified using the Image J software.

Polymerase chain reaction (PCR)

Total RNA was extracted and refined from of BMDMs or cardiac apex of mice by using the RNA isolation system (Omega, USA) and reverse transcribed by using a PrimeScriptTM RT Reagent Kit (TaKaRa, China). Expressions of mRNA of target genes were measured by real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR) with primers listed in Supplemental Table III and a SYBR Premix Ex Taq kit (TaKaRa, China). Quantitation of mRNAs was determined using the ΔΔCt method and presented as the fold change over a control group of β-actin.

Quantification of cardiac troponin T, lactate, succinate and α-ketoglutarate

Blood was obtained from heart of mice and then centrifuged at 300 g for 5 min in tubes containing heparin. Serum cardiac troponin T (cTNT) were measured using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA) (Meimian, China) following manufacturer’s instructions.

Pellets from 1 × 106 BMDMs were resuspended in iced PBS and lysed by ultrasonic cell disruptor (20 kHz, 150 W). The supernatants were collected after centrifugation at 1500 g for 10 min. Intracellular lactate, succinate and α-ketoglutarate (α-KG) were measured using ELISA (Meimian, China) following manufacturer’s instructions.

Inhibition of adenosine 5’-monophosphate activated protein kinase (AMPK)

Following the 3-week swimming training period, male mice proceeded to the drug intervention phase. Mice in the exercise preconditioning + AMPK inhibitor group received daily intraperitoneal injections of the AMPK inhibitor, doxorubicin hydrochloride (TargetMol, Shanghai, China), at a daily dose of 10 mg/kg for 7 consecutive days. Control mice were administered an equivalent volume of saline vehicle at corresponding time points. After the 7-day injection period, all mice were subjected to the myocardial ischemia-reperfusion (IR) injury model.

Patients with AMI

The patients with acute myocardial infarction (AMI) for the first time who had no history of percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) at Nanfang Hospital from 2018 to 2020 were included in this study. The echocardiographic and routine blood examination before PCI were performed. The patients were grouped according to weekly exercise time and exercise intensity collected by phone interviews. The exercise intensity was assessed with the assistance of the Rating of Perceived Exertion (RPE) Borg scale [20] (Supplementary Table IV). Patients in exercise group engaged in aerobic physical activity or an equivalent combination of moderate and vigorous activity and met one of the following criteria: (1) at least 150 min per week of moderate intensity (RPE Borg scale range from 13 to 16), or (2) 75 min per week of vigorous intensity (RPE Borg scale range from 17 to 20). Patients did not meet the above-mentioned criteria were included in sedentary group. Patients with the following comorbidities were excluded: permanent atrial fibrillation, severe ventricular arrhythmia, atrioventricular block, moderate or severe valvular disease, obstructive pulmonary disease, acute systemic disease or fever, severe renal dysfunction and treatment with drugs that improve heart function.

Patients with history of MI from NHANES database

To further validate the influence of exercise habit on outcome of patients with myocardial infarction (MI), we performed data mining from NHANES database. Data from 1999 to 2018 were extracted. Participants without a history of MI or with missing exercise or follow-up data were excluded, resulting in 2,457 included participants with a self-reported MI history (encompassing both first-time and recurrent events) (Supplemental Figure II). Participants were divided into EXE (exercise) group (n = 537) and SED (sedentary) group (n = 1920) based on physical activity criteria, where those engaging in ≥ 150 min/week of moderate-intensity or ≥ 75 min/week of vigorous-intensity exercise were assigned to the EXE group. The effect of exercise habit on post-MI survival was analyzed.

All-cause mortality data was ascertained by linkage to the National Death Index through 31 December 2018. Participants who were not matched to death records were considered alive throughout the follow-up period.

Metabolic equivalent hours (h) (MET·h = MET score × exercise time) as a quantitative index of exercise, which represents the energy expenditure associated with activity above the baseline metabolic levels. MET scores were calculated according to the NHANES Physical Activity Questionnaire [21].

Covariates were obtained through interviews and questionnaires, including age, gender, body mass index (BMI), race, history of heart failure, smoking and diabetes. Aric acid (UA), triglyceride and high-density lipoprotein (HDL) levels were acquired and measured from serum samples. The detailed methods were reported on the official website: https://wwwn.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/continuousnhanes/manuals.aspx?.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SEM for normally distributed continuous variables or median with interquartile range for non-normally distributed variables. For comparisons between two groups of normally distributed data, two-tailed Student’s t-test was employed. One-way or two-way ANOVA with Bonferroni post-hoc test was used for multiple group comparisons of normally distributed data. The Wilcoxon matched pairs signed-rank test was used for comparison of continuous variables with non-normal distribution. The Pearson χ2 test was used to compare categorical variables. Multivariate linear regression used to assess the impact of premorbid regular exercise on myocardial injury. For survival analysis, we performed Kaplan-Meier estimates with log-rank tests for unadjusted comparisons. Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to assess the association between exercise habits and mortality outcomes. We employed a three-tiered adjustment strategy: Model 1 (unadjusted); Model 2 (adjusted for age, sex, and race); Model 3 (further adjusted for BMI, smoking status, diabetes, hypertension, heart failure history, and HDL levels). Missing data were handled with multiple imputation using the R MICE package. P value (two-tailed) < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant. All analyses were conducted by two investigators who were unaware of the treatment group of patients, animals or samples. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 23.0 software or GraphPad Prism 8.0.1.

Results

EHP through swimming mitigates myocardial IR injury in mice

Three-week swimming induced PMH evidenced by the increases of the ratios of heart weight (HW) to body weight (BW) and HW to tibia length (TL) and slight increases of the thickness of the wall (LVPWd and LVAWd). While a week after swimming termination, HW/BW, HW/TL and the thickness of the wall in the EHP group regressed to the level of SED group (Supplemental Figures IB-IF). In response to 45 min myocardial ischemia and 24 h reperfusion (IR), similar ischemic area at risk was noted between EHP and SED group, but the male EHP mice exhibited significantly smaller infarct size (30% ± 3% vs. 49%±2%, P<0. 01) and approximately 50% less apoptotic cardiomyocytes than the SED mice (Fig. 1A and D). After myocardial IR, a higher expression of myocardial B-cell lymphoma 2 (Bcl2) and phosphorylation of extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK), and a lower expression of myocardial cleaved caspase 3 and Bcl2 Associated X (BAX) proteins were found in male EHP group than in SED group after 45 min ischemia and 24 h reperfusion (Fig. 1E and I). Furthermore, lower level of plasma cardiac troponin T (cTNT) was detected in male EHP group than in SED group (151.1 ± 3.939 vs. 199.8 ± 6.851 ng/L, P<0.01) (Fig. 1J). Consistent with the results observed in males, female mice that underwent swim training exhibited a significantly smaller infarct size and regulated the expression and activation of apoptosis-related proteins (Supplemental Figures III). These findings indicate that EHP through swimming exerts a protective effect on myocardial IR injury.

EHP through swimming inhibits cardiac remodelling in post-IR mice

Echocardiography analysis revealed that left ventricular (LV) end-systolic diameter (LVESd) and LV end-diastolic diameter (LVEDd) were significantly smaller, while LV ejection fraction (LVEF) and LV fractional shortening (LVFS) were significantly larger in the EHP mice than in the SED mice (Fig. 2A and E) at 1 to 4 weeks after myocardial IR. Furthermore, the percentage of myocardial fibrosis (old infarct size) at 4 weeks after IR was markedly smaller in the EHP mice than in the SED mice (5.47 ± 0.68% vs. 8.48 ± 1.03%, P<0.051) (Fig. 2F and G).

EHP through swimming improves the profile of myocardial macrophages

To investigate the role of EHP through swimming on immune cell infiltration after IR (ischemia 45 min and reperfusion 24 h), we performed flow cytometry and set gates for analyzing neutrophils (CD45+ CD11b+ ly6G+), ly6C+ monocytes (CD45+ CD11b+ ly6G− ly6C+), pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages (CD45+ CD11b+ ly6G− ly6C− MHC-IIhigh) and anti-inflammatory M2 macrophages (CD45+ CD11b+ ly6G− ly6C− MHC-IIlow) (Fig. 3A). Accumulation of neutrophils and ly6C+ monocytes in myocardium was significantly more in IR mice than in Sham group, while this effect was blocked by EHP through swimming (Fig. 3B and C). The number of myocardial macrophages was similar between SED sham group and SED IR group, while EHP decreased myocardial infiltration of macrophages in both EHP sham and EHP IR groups and no significant difference was noted between the two groups (Fig. 3D). We further analyzed the change of macrophage polarization in each group (Fig. 3E). Compared with SED sham group, there were fewer myocardial M1 macrophages and more myocardial M2 macrophages in SED IR group (Fig. 3F and G), while EHP further decreased M1 macrophages in both sham and IR mice (Fig. 3F), and further increased M2 macrophages in sham mice but not in IR mice (Fig. 3G). The percentage of M1 macrophages in the myocardium was smaller in SED IR group than in SED sham group, EHP significantly decreased M1 macrophage percentage in both sham and IR mice (Fig. 3H). The percentage of M2 macrophages in the myocardium was larger in SED IR group than in SED sham group, EHP significantly increased M2 macrophage percentage in both sham and IR mice (Fig. 3I). These results indicate that EHP through swimming decreases infiltration of M1 macrophages and increases infiltration of M2 macrophages.

EHP through swimming improves energy metabolism of macrophages in IR mice

We assessed the mRNA expression of inflammatory factors in mouse heart and bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs). We found that myocardial mRNA expression of M1 signature pro-inflammatory factors tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interferon-γ (IFN-γ) and immune-related GTPase family M member 1 (Irgm1) was higher, while the expression of M2 macrophages-related ant-inflammatory factors arginase-1 (Arg1), interleukin-10 (IL-10) and macrophage scavenger receptor 1 (MSR-1) was lower in IR mice than in sham group. Similarly, EHP significantly downregulated the mRNA expression of pro-inflammatory factors Irgm1 and IL-6, and upregulated the mRNA expression of anti-inflammatory factors Arg1, IL-10 and MSR-1 (Fig. 4A) in BMDMs (Fig. 4B). These findings suggest that the effect of EHP on myocardial macrophage sub-type change was mediated by function regulation of BMDMs. We further analysed the effect of EHP on metabolism related to glycolysis and tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle in mouse BMDMs that regulating polarization of macrophage. The concentrations of lactate and succinate in BMDMs was significantly lower in EHP IR group than in SED IR group (Fig. 4C-D), while the concentration of α-ketoglutarate (α-KG) was significantly higher in EHP IR group than in SED IR group (Fig. 4E). We further examined energy metabolism related signalling molecules in myocardial macrophages (Fig. 4F-L). The protein expressions of phosphorylation of mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) and serine/threonine kinase (AKT) in macrophages were lower in EHP IR group than in SED IR group (Fig. 4F, H and I), while the expression of phosphorylation of AMPK and mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation complex proteins CIV, CII and CI were higher in EHP IR group than in SED IR group (Fig. 4F, G, J and L). These results demonstrate that EHP through swimming regulates the function of BMDMs and improves energy metabolism of macrophages that may contribute to myocardial macrophage sub-type change.

Inhibition of AMPK abrogates the cardioprotective effects of EHP through swimming

To investigate the mechanistic role of AMPK signaling in exercise-induced cardioprotection, we administered the AMPK inhibitor, doxorubicin hydrochloride, to mice following the EHP protocol. Remarkably, pharmacological inhibition of AMPK largely abolished the beneficial effects of EHP on myocardial IR injury. AMPK inhibitor-treated EHP mice exhibited significantly larger infarct sizes (SED IR vs. EHP IR vs. EHP + doxorubicin, 42.3 ± 3.2% vs. 28.7 ± 2.8% vs., P < 0.01) than EHP IR group (Fig. 5A and B). Meanwhile, AMPK inhibition reversed the anti-apoptotic effects of EHP, as evidenced by increased Bax and cleaved caspase 3/ caspase 3 ratio, and decreased Bcl2 and phosphorylation of ERK in mice heart (p-ERK/ERK ratio: 0.4 ± 0.1 vs. 0.9 ± 0.2 in EHP group, P < 0.01). Furthermore, we found that AMPK inhibition significantly increased macrophage infiltration and promoted M1 macrophage polarization while reducing M2 polarization (Fig. 5H-M). At the molecular level, MPK inhibitor treatment enhanced phosphorylation of mTOR and AKT, while suppressing the expression of mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) complexes in BMDMs (Fig. 5N-T). These results demonstrate that AMPK activation is essential for mediating the cardioprotective effects of exercise preconditioning against IR injury.

EHP without physical myocardial hypertrophy also attenuates myocardial IR injury in mice

To investigate whether EHP without PMH also exerts a protective effect against myocardial IR injury, we used treadmill running to training mice for 3 or 6 weeks. We found that treadmill running for 3 or 6 weeks didn’t induce PMH, as evidenced by no significant differences on HW/BW, HW/TL, LVPWd and LVAWd (Supplemental Figure IV).

Our results revealed a clear duration-dependent effect of exercise training. After termination of running for 1 week, IR (ischemia for 45 min and reperfusion for 24 h) was performed. Mice undergoing 3 weeks of treadmill running demonstrated minimal protection, with no significant improvement in infarct size (46% ± 5% vs. 50% ± 3%, P = 0.8304). However, extending the training to 6 weeks resulted in a significant attenuation of infarct area than SED mice (37% ± 3% vs. 48 ± 3%, P<0.01) (Fig. 6A and B.). We also found that less TUNEL-positive apoptotic cardiomyocytes in EHP IR (6-week running) group than in SED IR group (Fig. 6C and D). Both 3-week and 6-week running reduced serum concentration of troponin T than in SED IR group (181.4 ± 6.466 vs. 150.1 ± 6.122 vs. 204.2 ± 5.174 ng/L, P<0.01) in response to myocardial IR injury (Fig. 6E). At the molecular level, extended exercise duration produced marked benefits in apoptotic regulation. The 6-week exercise group exhibited significantly elevated expression of the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl2 and enhanced phosphorylation of the pro-survival kinase ERK, coupled with reduced levels of the pro-apoptotic proteins cleaved caspase-3 and BAX (P < 0.01 versus SED group). These molecular changes were absent in the 3-week training group (Fig. 6F-J). Echocardiographic assessment revealed that cardiac function, as measured by ejection fraction (EF) and fractional shortening (FS), showed no significant improvement after 3 weeks of training but demonstrated marked enhancement following 6 weeks of exercise (Fig. 6K-O). Similarly, that macrophage infiltration and M1-polarized macrophage presence were unchanged after 3 weeks but significantly decreased after 6 weeks of training, accompanied by a pronounced increase in M2 macrophages (Fig. 6P-U).

Exercise habit contributes to higher post-MI survival in patients with history of MI

To further validate the benefit of exercise, we analysed data from NHANES database. We selected 2457 participants with self-reported history of myocardial infarction (MI). Although the patients in the exercise (EXE) group had higher body mass index (BMI), serum triglyceride level, and proportion of diabetes (See supplementary Table VI), they had significantly lower all-cause mortality and cardiovascular death rate than the SED group (P < 0.001, Fig. 8A and B). Further analysis using a three-tiered Cox regression model confirmed that exercise was associated with reduced risk of both all-cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality even after adjusted for age, gender, body mass index (BMI), race, history of heart failure, smoking, diabetes, and high-density lipoprotein (HDL). Discussion

Although substantial evidence supports the impact of regular physical activity and exercise in primary and secondary prevention of cardiovascular disease, and even single bouts or short-term exercise-induced protection can also attenuate cardiovascular damage in response to prolonged IR injury, a phenomenon termed exercise preconditioning (EP) [22, 23], it remains unclear how long the cardioprotection persists after exercise termination. Previous EP-related studies usually examine the cardioprotection immediately after the exercise termination [24, 25]. Differentially, EHP focus on the cardioprotection after the PMH regression [7, 8]. In this study, we provided new evidence of EHP attenuating myocardial ischemia in both IR mice and patients with AMI and found that regulation of macrophage polarization and inflammatory suppression contributed to the beneficial effect of exercise. Furthermore, we noted that sufficient exercise without PHM still produced a beneficial memory against myocardial ischemia or IR injury.

PMH occurs in physiological settings of pregnancy and exercise, which is completely reversible postpartum or with exercise cessation [7, 8, 26]. It is believed that PMH is cardioprotective against myocardial ischemia and adverse cardiac remodelling [24]. However, less attention focused on whether the cardioprotection still exists after PMH regression. Most of patients, even elite athletes, usually have terminated their regular exercise habit for an extended period when suffering from cardiovascular disease. Whether the protective effects of exercise on cardiovascular system still existed after termination of exercise? Our previous study revealed that exercise-induced antihypertrophic memory in mice could persist for at least 3 weeks even after PMH has regressed [7]. In this study, we further confirmed that EHP exerted beneficial effects on myocardial IR injury in mice manifested by smaller infarct size, less apoptosis of cardiomyocytes and post-infarction fibrosis. Previous studies have tried to identify the molecular features of hypertrophy regression [27], which would be helpful for designing specific therapies to protect heart from harmful damage such as IR injury if beneficial molecular memories could persist after PMH regression.

Trained immunity is a de facto memory for innate immune responses which can occur in both physiological and pathological conditions, leading to long-term functional reprogramming of innate immune cells [28]. Regulation of trained immunity represents an important target for prophylactic and therapeutic approaches in human diseases. In this study, we noted a beneficial trained immunity of macrophages and BMDMs contributed to the EHP-produced alleviation of IR injury. It is well known that IR could cause myocardial sterile inflammation to aggravate the IR injury through producing cytokines and reactive oxygen species [29, 30], while therapies targeting leukocyte activation, infiltration, polarization and extravasation may attenuate the IR injury [17, 31, 32]. In other hand, exercise has been recognized as an immunomodulatory therapy. It was reported that regular exercise or endurance exercise training or low intensity exercise inhibits chronic inflammatory response in patients or animals through increasing M2/M1 macrophage ratio and upregulating anti-inflammatory factors [33–36]. Pre-operative exercise therapy could improve outcomes in patients receiving surgery through driving Kupffer cells toward an anti-inflammatory phenotype with trained immunity via metabolic reprogramming [11]. However, few studies investigated whether EHP exerts cardio-protection on myocardial IR injury through regulating immune-inflammatory response, especially macrophages. In this study, we provided new evidence that the beneficial immunomodulatory effects could persist after the exercise termination or even after PMH regression. We found EHP significantly reduced the myocardial infiltration of macrophages and regulated polarization of macrophage by increasing M2 macrophages and decreasing M1 macrophages, while similar changes were observed in BMDMs.

We noted that the EHP-induced beneficial myocardial macrophage regulation was attributable to the exercise-trained immunity of BMDMs through metabolic reprogramming. Macrophages possess distinct metabolic characteristics that contribute to their functional states [37]. M1 macrophages display enhanced glycolytic metabolism and impaired TCA cycle [38], while M2 macrophages exhibit enhanced fatty acid oxidation (FAO) and mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) [39]. Improving glycolytic metabolism and activating glycolysis-related signalling pathway promotes polarization and pro-inflammatory function of M1 macrophage [40, 41]. In contrast, enhancing OXPHOS and FAO promotes M2 macrophage polarization and secretion of anti-inflammatory factors [42, 43]. Exercise training promotes the lipolysis of triacylglycerol stored in adipose tissue and the delivery of fatty acids to skeletal muscle [44, 45], activates FAO-associated proteins to improve FAO and OXPHOS in skeletal muscle [46, 47]. In this study, we provided further evidence that EHP could induce energy metabolism reprogramming in BMDMs and still exist after termination of exercise through activation of FAO-associated proteins AMPK, and increasing the α-KG/succinate ratio, inhibiting the activation of glycolysis-related AKT-mTOR pathway, and decreasing lactates.

Previous studies demonstrated that AMPK plays pivotal roles in polarization and metabolism of macrophage. Activation of AMPK promotes a shift from pro-inflammatory M1 to anti-inflammatory M2 polarization and reduces infiltration of macrophage in plaque, thus, attenuates atherosclerosis [48, 49]. AMPK activity was found to be associated with HMGCR and ACC, key enzymes in cholesterol and fatty acid synthesis, and further enhances fatty acid oxidation [50, 51]. Furthermore, AMPK plays an important role in exercise metabolism by increasing fatty acid oxidation in skeletal muscle [52]. Our study extends these findings by demonstrating that AMPK is crucial for the metabolic reprogramming of macrophages induced by EHP. Pharmacological inhibition of AMPK abolished the cardioprotective effects of exercise preconditioning and reversed its beneficial modulation of macrophage function, underscoring the indispensable role of AMPK in mediating exercise-induced trained immunity of macrophage.

In addition, we further noted that sufficient exercise without PMH could also produce cardioprotection similar to EHP, which should be more clinically significance, because the intensity of exercise carried out by most people is insufficient to induce PMH. Lennon et al. found without PMH, exercise-induced cardioprotection remained for a short time after cessation of exercise training in Vitro [53]. In Vivo, it is unclear as to whether preconditioning memory can be produced and exists in the heart even after exercise termination. We postulate that a reasonable combination of the type, frequency, intensity, and duration of exercise training is necessary to produce preconditioning memory. As a result, we noted that running training could not induce PMH in mice, while mice made to run for 6 weeks rather than 3 weeks protected against myocardial IR injury at 1 week after exercise termination. We further provided data indicating that patients with regular exercise habits had a smaller myocardial injury and better cardiac function when compared with sedentary behavior patients after the first attack of AMI.

As a primary preventive measure for coronary heart disease, Guidelines recommended the adults should engage in at least 150 min per week of accumulated moderate-intensity physical activity or 75 min per week of vigorous-intensity physical activity. Kim et al. examined the longitudinal associations of exercise frequency with the incidence of MI in a large sample database and reported that moderate levels of sweat-inducing exercise decreased the risk ratio for MI incidence by 21% [54]. However, compared with the influence of exercise on morbidity, fewer clinical investigations focused on whether regular physical exercise attenuates myocardial injury in the presence of MI. Our data added new evidence on this issue.

In contrast to the primary prevention, substantial studies have tried to explore the secondary preventive effects of physical exercise on cardiovascular disease [55]. Exercise-based cardiac rehabilitation has been found to reduce cardiovascular mortality and improve health-related quality of life [56]. Lawler et al. reported that exercise rehabilitation was associated with 26% reduction of all-cause mortality in a meta-analysis of 64 studies and 6111 post-infarction patients [57]. Contractionary, in a RCT with 1813 patients, RAMIT trial reported that cardiac rehabilitation including exercise training had no significant effect on morbidity or mortality [58]. Our analysis of data from NHANES database indicates regular physical exercise behaviour was associated with a 56% reduction of risk for all-cause mortality and a 41% reduction of risk for cardiovascular mortality in patients with history of MI, which should be attributed to both the primary and secondary preventive effects of exercise because an exercise habit usually exists before and after suffering from a disease.

However, our study has several limitations. First, the clinical data were obtained from a single-center retrospective cohort, which may introduce selection bias and limit generalizability. Although we adjusted for multiple confounding factors, residual bias from unmeasured variables such as medication adherence and lifestyle factors cannot be entirely excluded. Second, Physical activity levels were assessed through self-reporting using questionnaires, which are subject to recall bias and may not fully capture the intensity, duration, or consistency of exercise behaviour. More precise metabolic equivalents, such as accelerometry or direct oxygen consumption monitoring, would better capture the actual exercise workload and improve the accuracy of our exercise-related analyses. Furthermore, our study identified trained immunity and metabolic reprogramming of macrophages as a potential mechanism, while the exact molecular pathways and their causal relationship with cardioprotection still warrant further investigation using cell-specific knockout models. Thus, these limitations underscore the need for future multi-center prospective studies incorporating objective activity monitoring and human tissue validation alongside mechanistic investigations.

Conclusion

Taken together, EHP protects heart against myocardial ischemic injury through trained immunity of macrophage mediated by energy metabolism reprogramming.
