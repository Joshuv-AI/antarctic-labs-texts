Public Policies in Historical Context and the Association Between Education and US Older Adults’ Health
Authors: MARK D HAYWARD, MATEO P FARINA
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

On march 20, 2025, president trump signed an executive order that ordered his administration to close the Department of Education and return education authority back to US states. This closure is the cumulation of long‐standing efforts by a contingent of the Republican Party to return authority back to the states for funding and administration of key social safety net and other types of programs for the public good (including education). The shift in authority has also often been accompanied by state preemption laws that reduce local authority, making state policy contexts a key determinant of what people are or will be exposed to throughout the life course. Importantly, these shifts in authority are often accompanied by policies that systematically underfund these public goods, leading to durable inequalities in educational quality and access that have lifelong consequences. Moreover, with increased polarization, the dissimilar policy and funding environments in states have widened, leading to growing inequalities across them. Although there may be arguments for greater experimentation and flexibility for states to address the educational needs of their populations, the retreat of the federal government from legislating, enforcing, and coordinating education policy may have profound consequences for population health and health inequalities in the coming decades, consequences that will be difficult to reverse given the lifelong and cumulative effects of educational attainment and health.

As the policy landscape in the United States has undergone profound changes, the nation has also experienced tectonic shifts in health and mortality, with increased morbidity and shorter life expectancy for the general population. 1 However, these trends mask growing inequalities in the US population. Most notably, education differences have started to grow alongside defederalization and less generous funding for social goods and safety net programs. Since 1990, overall life expectancy among less‐educated Americans stalled and is currently declining, whereas inequality has grown as a result of the increased life expectancy among well‐educated Americans, the only educational group to experience such a gain. 2 , 3 , 4 Inequality in life expectancy has also diverged within education groups: well‐educated Americans have become more homogeneous in their life spans, whereas less‐educated Americans have become much more heterogeneous. 3 In essence, the life spans of less‐educated persons have stagnated or declined and become more uncertain, whereas well‐educated persons have experienced increasing life spans and greater certainty in their life chances. 3 , 5

Connections between education policy and inequalities in health and mortality have received limited attention outside academic circles. Although a growing body of research has documented how shifts in the policy landscape contribute to widening educational differences in life expectancy and shape population health, these insights have largely remained confined to scholarly debates. Recent studies linking US state policy environments to mortality trends—and demonstrating that educational gradients in mortality are sensitive to long‐term policy shifts within states—underscore the importance of explicitly situating associations between education and health within their policy contexts. 6 , 7 , 8 The reversal of Americans’ declining health and ultimately its improvement will necessarily rest on policy contexts that provide opportunities and resources that enable people to garner health advantages.

In this brief Perspective, we consider some of the issues facing policymakers grappling with disturbing US health trends. Some of the issues focus on states’ policies, but we also call attention to the fact that state and federal policies are often intertwined, interacting to exacerbate or ameliorate the inequalities in health experienced by education groups. Often, the inequalities reflect the uncertainties, inadequate resources, and risks faced by less‐educated persons, whereas well‐educated persons appear to be relatively insensitive to the policy changes affecting less‐educated persons. However, we speculate that advanced education is not necessarily the “firewall” that it appears to be and that policies are indeed important in understanding the trends in the health and mortality experiences of well‐educated Americans. Moreover, we acknowledge that threats are looming that could potentially undercut the future health of many well‐educated persons. In essence, we argue that state and federal policies addressing the health concerns of people across the educational distribution are critical to reversing the downward spiral of American mortality and improving the health of all Americans.

Why Is Education So Strongly Associated with Health?

An extensive literature lays out the numerous individual‐level mechanisms by which education confers health advantages. In summary, this literature indicates that education is associated with a host of flexible resources that enable persons to garner health advantages across an array of health outcomes. These resources include valued information about and support for healthy lifestyles and care including technology, access to good jobs and rewards in an information‐based society, access to valuable social networks, sophisticated cognitive skills, and greater sense of control and human agency. 9

Despite the voluminous literature documenting the importance of these fundamental processes, it is also evident that the association between education and health over the life course is highly dynamic, with three core patterns characterizing these changes over the past 40 years. First, the level of educational inequality in mortality has grown dramatically in recent decades. Between 1990 and 2017, for example, the gap in life expectancy between college‐educated persons and persons with less than a college degree more than doubled for all demographic groups (see Figure 1). 3 , 10 Among non‐Hispanic White men, the gap between the college‐educated and the high‐school–educated groups grew from 3.9 to 9.5 years in less than three decades. Among non‐Hispanic White women, the gap grew from 1.3 to 7 years. Growing inequality was driven by both improvements for college‐educated persons and stagnating or declining health for persons with less than a college degree over the course of almost 40 years. By 2010, health stagnation and deterioration were not confined to those with high school educations or less, as all education groups lacking a college degree experienced a decline in life expectancy.

Second, how educational attainment is associated with mortality risk changed in fundamentally important ways in the latter part of the 20th century and into the 21st century. 11 Between 1960 and 1990, the risk of mortality largely looked like a step function. More specifically, the risk of mortality was approximately uniform for persons with less than a high school education; it then dropped significantly with the completion of a high school education but remained constant until the attainment of a college degree, when it dropped again and remained flat with additional years of advanced education. This pattern is indicative of the attainment of educational credentials and the importance of labor market mechanisms driving the association between educational attainment and mortality risk. 12 After 1990, however, the association began to change. The risk of mortality in the lower part of the education distribution remained similar to that observed during the 1960‐1990 period. However, once persons attained a high school degree, the risk declined almost continuously with each additional year of education, with a steepening of the slope beginning after 2000. 11 , 12 These changes coincided with the increasing influence of health behaviors on mortality among highly educated individuals—alongside rising socioeconomic resources for this group—as individuals with less than a college education experienced a parallel erosion of labor market resources. 2 , 11 Smoking rates dropped among well‐educated persons compared to persons with less than a college degree. 13 , 14 Access to and use of medical technology such as the rapid adoption of new drugs, use of digital tools, and increase in health literacy were concentrated among well‐educated persons. 15 , 16 So too were improvements in social risk factors including greater family stability and higher rates of marriage among well‐educated persons. The educational divide in the risks and resources associated with good health thus grew at a ferocious pace in the latter part of the 20th century.

Third, related work on educational differences in life span variability has identified a diverging trend: highly educated persons have become more similar to each other in their mortality risk, whereas persons with less than a college degree have become increasingly diverse in their mortality risks. 3 , 5 The resources that education confers are becoming more highly concentrated among the well‐educated, whereas less‐educated persons face considerable uncertainty in their risk of death. Well‐educated persons have experienced precipitous declines in mortality, postponing death to older ages while also becoming more similar in their risks. Mortality among less‐educated persons has stagnated or risen as uncertainty has grown dramatically. Later in this Perspective, we discuss the possible institutional factors underlying these educational differences in mortality uncertainty.

If the overarching goal is to improve life expectancy and reduce inequality in life chances, several questions can be asked: What are the ways in which this could happen? In speculative terms, is it possible to turn back the clock and reduce inequality to the levels experienced in 1990 or before? What issues should be considered in implementing this goal? In the next section, we offer some suggestions based on the patterns described in the preceding paragraphs. We emphasize “drilling up” rather than “drilling down”—that is, understanding the role of policies rather than examining the changes in the causes of death due to shifting epidemiological environments and technological changes over time. Regardless of these changes, educational groups have diverged in their mortality experiences historically, which guides our attention to the policy contexts in which educationally related risks and resources influence health and mortality.

The US Federal Government, US States, and Improving Americans’ Health

Until roughly 1970, technological and public health advances fueled improvements in the health of the entire population. 9 , 17 , 18 Between 1910 and 1940, rural electrification, the Meat Inspection Act, the discovery of different blood types, the development of vaccines for a variety of common diseases, the discovery of antibiotics, the introduction of refrigeration for home use, the establishment of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, the switch from home to hospital deliveries… all of these factors and more rapidly diffused through the entire population and contributed to dramatic improvements in life expectancy for the majority of Americans. Technological change during this period was also accompanied by significant social changes, most notably the rapid growth in educational attainment along with declines in absolute poverty and the expansion of the contract between the federal government and the American population to improve public safety and economic security (e.g., the establishment of the Tennessee Valley Authority, Social Security, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, and the minimum wage) seemed promising. The post–World War II establishment of the Interstate Transportation System and the passage of the Servicemen's Readjustment Act of 1944 (commonly known as the GI Bill) fueled both population mobility and the expansion of college education. Finally, the Great Society accomplishments of the 1960s (e.g., the Voting Rights Act, the Surgeon General's report on cigarette smoking, the Higher Education Act, the Special Supplemental Nutrition Program for Women, Infants and Children, and Medicare and Medicaid) cemented the contract between the federal government and the American population to enhance well‐being and health for all Americans. As summarized by Hayward and Farina, 9 (p406) “institutional investments in the population were broad, scientific and technological changes were accessible to most people, and prosperity was shared across a large swath of the population.”

Goldin and Katz 19 have argued that the low levels of wage inequality in the earlier part of the 20th century were largely due to the rapid rise of educational attainment in the US population, especially the expansion of high school degree attainment, which exceeded or kept pace with the technological changes underlying the growth in demand for skilled workers. This led to relatively narrow wage inequality. Goldin and Katz 19 noted, however, that, after 1970, technological advances began to outstrip educational improvements, resulting in rapidly rising wages among college‐educated persons compared to those without a college degree. Indeed, wage inequality has risen rapidly for more than 50 years in the United States, mirroring the rise in educational differences in life expectancy. The “race between education and technology,” then, played a central role in determining the trends in economic inequality across the entire 20th century and, indeed, into the 21st century. 19 Technology will also likely continue to exacerbate economic inequality with the increasing rise of automation that will impact jobs across the United States.

The expansion of education throughout the first seven decades of the 20th century was strongly influenced by the need of a growing industrial economy for skilled workers. Already in 1900, 34 states had compulsory education laws, and by 1930, every state required children to complete elementary education. 20 , 21 The booming industrial sector also fueled the rise in secondary education, introducing vocational training in high schools and preparing workers to meet the industrial demand. The federal government was largely a junior partner in bringing about these early changes, although it provided funding for vocational training in high schools. Beginning with the GI Bill and continuing into the 1960s, the federal government began to play a more robust role in the expansion of educational attainment in the population, for example, by expanding funding for low‐income students (Elementary and Secondary Education Act) and enacting the Higher Education Act, which provided financial aid to college students and currently remains the primary source of financial aid for students attending college.

In contrast, whereas the role of the federal government and federal policy in directly influencing the gains in educational attainment of the American population is relatively smaller than the roles of state and local communities, the federal government has played an incredibly important role in influencing the pace of technological change through major investments in technological and scientific research, including investments in both the business sector and the nation's universities, especially sponsored research. We speculate that the role of federal policies in contributing to technological and scientific advances may be especially important for understanding the educational divergence in life expectancy in the latter part of the 20th century. With respect to universities, for example, numerous federal agencies fueled a ferocious expansion of university research enterprises, technological developments, and scientific breakthroughs across a myriad of fields, for example, the development of the internet; the human genome project; vaccines; aerospace technologies; medical technologies such as magnetic resonance imaging; supercomputing; and genetically modified organisms, which dramatically increased agricultural production. A comprehensive list of federal investments is too long for this short Perspective. The role of the federal government in impacting the pace of technological improvements and scientific advances more broadly was profound, such that technology began to expand faster than education in the population. Figure 2 illustrates this phenomenon graphically, showing the growth in federal obligations for research and development from the 1950s until the current period. As the figure highlights, expansions in defense, National Institutes of Health research, and energy were important components of these obligations. The pace of these wide‐ranging technological advances coincided with the rapid rise in economic inequality in the United States and the rapidly growing educational divide in life expectancy. Policies affecting the association between education and health, we argue, are not just education policies. Rather, policies affecting technological change and scientific investments also appear to be important, especially for understanding the health trends experienced by college‐educated adults since 1970.

As described earlier, the life expectancy for persons with less than a college degree has fallen further behind that for college‐educated persons in recent decades, yet the risk of death for less‐educated persons has also become increasingly heterogeneous—and uncertain. Less educated persons have become more likely to be excluded from the technological and scientific advances, relative to well‐educated persons, with less access and less use. Real income has stagnated, there are fewer well‐paying jobs for less‐educated workers, union power has declined, and automation and globalization have fueled job losses and uncertainty. 2 , 23 , 24 Marriage rates have also declined, and divorce rates have increased among this group.

Although less‐educated persons have been increasingly beset by economic and social challenges in recent decades as their life expectancy has stagnated, this group has also become more heterogenous in their mortality risk. This pattern is shown heuristically in Figure 4. A growing body of research supports the idea that an important part of the growing heterogeneity appears to have been the rise of New Federalism in the last few decades of the 20th century, the devolvement of federal social welfare policies to the states, and state preemption laws that fostered political polarization at the state level. Reviews of this work are provided by Montez and Bisesti 7 and by Montez and Grumbach. 25 By the beginning of the 21st century, states had become the battlegrounds for population health.

When considering the role of state policies in mortality trends, one must keep several issues in mind to understand educational differences in both the stagnation and the growing heterogeneity in life expectancy among less‐educated persons. First, the health and mortality of less‐educated persons have been strongly influenced by state policies. 6 , 8 , 26 In contrast, college‐educated persons have remained relatively similar in their health and mortality regardless of where they live. Second, the influences of state policies on health and mortality have usually reflected bundles of policies rather than any one policy per se. 25 For example, states in which policy bundles focused on improvements in the population's opportunities for economic well‐being and health experienced improvements in life expectancy, whereas states implementing policies that created obstacles or ignored residents’ economic and health needs experienced stagnating and declining life expectancy. 7 , 8 , 25 , 27 This led to an explosion of state differences in life expectancy that is still continuing, an explosion pointing to the growing heterogeneity in mortality risk among less‐educated persons at the national level. State policies play a critical role in understanding both the stagnation and decline in life expectancy among less‐educated persons and their growing heterogeneity in mortality risk. Moreover, given the current political environment, it is likely that state policies will continue to play a substantial role going forward.

Discussion

We have argued that it is critical to view the association between education and health within the larger historical context involving institutional forces at multiple levels. Here, the focus has primarily been on policies at the federal and state levels, but we recognize that institutions and policies at other levels such as at the level of the local community also come into play. Indeed, policies across the levels are intertwined in complex ways that define the larger and highly dynamic historical context shaping the association between education and health.

We also specifically focus on mortality in this work precisely because it serves as a comprehensive state that captures the cumulative burden of the many health conditions and social disadvantages that precede it. In this sense, mortality integrates across the full spectrum of morbidity, including disability, chronic disease, and declining self‐rated health, making it a uniquely powerful end point for understanding the long‐run consequences of educational inequality and policy change. That said, the educational gradients across these broader health dimensions are not uniform, and understanding where gaps are more or less pronounced—and why—represents an important frontier for future research. Indeed, some health outcomes may prove more amenable to intervention than mortality itself, offering potential leverage points for policy. For a more comprehensive treatment of educational gradients across these wider health dimensions, we direct readers to the work of Montez, Zajacova, and colleagues, 6 , 8 , 26 , 28 and we see the systematic examination of policy effects across a broader range of health outcomes as a critical direction for future work.

An important lesson from the historical record is that rapid improvements in health combined with low levels of inequality are indeed possible. Bringing this situation about, however, requires recognition of the importance of broad institutional investments in the population that advance well‐being and health broadly and whose benefits are enjoyed not only by socioeconomically advantaged persons. With a few exceptions, such as the works by Avendano and Kawachi 29 and Montez and colleagues, 28 , 30 this conversation is largely silent in the policy world and sidesteps the importance of understanding who the institutional actors to bring about this change could and should be. Indeed, even among those who understand the importance of policy for educational differences in health, the assumption is that the major institutional actors typically operate at the state and local levels. Although this may empirically be the case at the current time, effectively tackling this problem is likely to require a resurgent involvement of federal policies and oversight, especially as states have continued to cut budgets. Bringing the federal government back into the conversation with the states as a lynchpin stakeholder is critical.

Another lesson from the historical record is that the association between education and health is sensitive to policies influencing the race between technology and education. States have played a fundamentally important role in the expansion of education through most of the 20th century that, when combined with technological change, led to relatively low levels of wage inequality and improvements in population health that were shared broadly across social groups. The federal government played a critical role in the expansion of technology in the latter part of the 20th century and, through extensive investments, fueled the expansion of business‐related research and universities’ research enterprises. College education grew rapidly with the advances in technology and scientific knowledge, providing the means for college‐educated persons to reap the health benefits from these advances, benefits that were not shared by people who had lower levels of education. One might think of this as the “dark side” of technological change with respect to health. College‐educated persons not only improved in life expectancy but also increasingly shared similar benefits. Meanwhile, the health of less‐educated persons during the period of rapid technological expansion increasingly depended on the degree to which states’ policies promoted opportunities for economic well‐being and health among less‐educated persons. The race between education and technology has heightened the tension and challenges involved in reducing inequalities in health and mortality, particularly given the pace of technological change when one considers how social groups face different risks of automation and/or have differential access to newer medicines such as glucon‐like peptide‐1 (GLP‐1) agonists. This tension in the 21st century is not likely to go away but may in fact accelerate.

A further threat on the horizon concerns the displacement of workers through automation and AI and its implications for health insurance coverage and, ultimately, population health. Projections suggest that a substantial share of jobs, including some white‐collar positions, will be disrupted by AI in the coming decades, and recent reports indicate that the technology sector has already experienced notable displacement. Although this issue has garnered considerable public attention, the health consequences are likely to fall most heavily on workers without a college degree. Less‐educated workers already disproportionately hold routine service, clerical, and administrative positions that are among the most susceptible to automation, 23 and they are far less likely to hold jobs with employer‐sponsored health insurance or the financial reserves to weather prolonged job displacement. In the United States, where employment remains the primary gateway to health insurance for working‐age adults, job loss translates rapidly into loss of coverage, delayed care, and deteriorating health. College‐educated workers are not immune to these structural shifts and may face growing heterogeneity in their own economic and health outcomes as displacement reaches further into white‐collar occupations, but they retain considerably greater capacity to pivot occupationally and financially. To the extent that technological displacement accelerates job loss and compounds the economic and social precarity already driving the stagnation and decline in life expectancy among less‐educated workers, the educational divide in health is poised to widen further unless policies decouple access to health insurance from employment status or otherwise shore up the labor market position of the most vulnerable workers.

Given the historical contextual forces influencing the association between education and health, what might the future hold? The portion of the American population that is college‐educated has grown at a relatively constant rate for a number of decades. 31 However, the rates of technological and scientific advances have been escalating over the same period. Technological and scientific advancements are outstripping educational improvements, and the gap is growing. Addressing this growing divide is not simply a matter of improving education in the population, given the role of technological advancement in generating the divide. Moreover, the growing divide itself suggests that a college education may not be the firewall that some researchers have suggested in terms of garnering health advantages. In fact, with the rise of college debt and private for‐profit education companies selling degrees and technical diplomas in the latter part of the 20th century, there may be greater heterogeneity in the economic, social, and health outcomes of college degree holders in the near future. Ultimately, the question is how much education is necessary to garner health advantages in an era of rapid technological and scientific change. Will college‐educated persons become more heterogeneous at some point in the future if gradations in educational experience and variations in the economic returns of a college education begin to differentiate the ability of college‐educated persons to garner health advantages? At the same time, it seems evident that state and federal policies promoting opportunities for economic well‐being and health among less‐educated persons will become even more critical at a time when this segment of the population is increasingly stuck and left behind. This is particularly true given the demographic diversity of less‐educated Americans, notably the growing share of Latino Americans, for whom extending education‐specific mortality trends remains an important priority for future research, although one that will require careful attention to data limitations, including inconsistencies in ethnic identification, the so‐called healthy immigrant effect, 32 and return migration patterns that complicate mortality ascertainment.

Several policy domains merit particular attention in addressing these trends. Access to health care remains a fundamental lever. Medicaid expansion under the Affordable Care Act (ACA) represents one of the clearest recent examples of a state‐level policy directly improving health outcomes among less‐educated adults, and the continued patchwork of expansion status across states is itself a structural driver of the growing heterogeneity in mortality risk that we document; this is a direct embodiment of the New Federalism dynamics described in the preceding section. As noted earlier in this discussion, as the automation threat unfolds, decoupling health insurance from employment is an increasingly urgent complement to these access expansions, whether through broader Medicare eligibility, a public option, or strengthened ACA marketplaces. Equitable access to new therapeutics and digital health tools represents an additional and emerging frontier. The recent arrival of GLP‐1 medications, which show transformative effects on the conditions of obesity, cardiovascular disease, and diabetes disproportionately burdening less‐educated Americans, illustrates the stakes: without deliberate Medicaid and Medicare coverage policies and investments in community health infrastructure, advances in medicine will continue to widen rather than narrow the educational divide in health. As with the state policy patterns documented throughout this Perspective, these interventions are most powerful when implemented as coordinated bundles rather than isolated programs.
