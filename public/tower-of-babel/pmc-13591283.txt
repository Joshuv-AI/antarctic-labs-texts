Identification and catalytic optimization of pinene oxidases in paeoniflorin biosynthetic pathway
Authors: Xi-Rui Tan, Xin-Ran Chen, Long-Ji Wang, Tian-Lin Pei, Jiewei Zhu, Wen-Lu Cai, San-Ming Zhu, Zhong-Rong Yan, Yu Kong, Ying Zhang, Qing Zhao, Xiao-Ya Chen, Jian-Xu Li, Shui-Yan Yu, Yong-Hong Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPaeoniflorin is a pharmacologically important cage-like monoterpene glycoside characteristic of Paeonia plants, yet its biosynthetic pathway has remained largely unresolved, hindering sustainable production. Here, we confirmed that paeoniflorin biosynthesis originates from α-pinene and identified 3 novel cytochrome P450 enzymes that catalyze pinene oxidation. CYP71AN126 catalyzes the hydroxylation of α-pinene at positions C4 and C10, followed by further oxidation of the alcohol to a ketone at C4, whereas CYP76A225/226 exclusively catalyze C10 hydroxylation. Virus-induced gene silencing assays demonstrated that silencing CYP71AN126, but not CYP76A225 and CYP76A226, significantly reduced the paeoniflorin content, indicating that C4 hydroxylation plays an important role in paeoniflorin biosynthesis, whereas C10 hydroxylation is not. Through the analysis of natural sequence and activity divergence among CYP71AN126 and CYP76A225/226, combined with protein structure prediction and site-directed mutagenesis, we identified L493 as a critical residue involved in regulating catalytic site specificity and substrate specificity of CYP71AN126. Mutation of L493 reduced or eliminated the formation of undesired C10 hydroxylation side product and enhanced substrate specificity. These findings establish C4 oxidation of α-pinene as the critical committed step in paeoniflorin biosynthesis. Our study lays a foundation for elucidating the complete biosynthetic pathway of paeoniflorin in Paeonia and provides a target for enzyme engineering of CYP71AN126 aimed at the efficient production of paeoniflorin via synthetic biology approaches.

Introduction

Paeoniflorin is a chemotaxonomic marker of the Paeoniaceae family and is a key quality indicator of their medicinal value (Zhang et al. 2019). It has pharmacological effects, including treating atherosclerosis (Yuan et al. 2024), modulating the immune system (Zhang and Wei 2020), reducing inflammation (Sheng et al. 2022), alleviating depression (Hu et al. 2025), preventing thrombosis (Ye et al. 2016), inhibiting tumor growth (Zhang et al. 2018), protecting against intestinal damage (Wen et al. 2019), and safeguarding neural cells (Zhou et al. 2019). Clinically, the pharmacological profile of paeoniflorin makes it a promising candidate drug for aiding recovery of patients with cerebral infarction. Currently, paeoniflorin is mainly extracted from Paeonia plants, which yield low quantities and inefficient separation. Chemical synthesis methods are complex, costly, and environmentally harmful (Chen et al. 2025). Although synthetic biology offers an ideal approach for paeoniflorin production, the incomplete understanding of its biosynthetic pathway limits its application in synthetic biology. Furthermore, as one of the representative compounds with a cage-like structure, the biosynthetic pathway of paeoniflorin not only serves as an excellent system for studying the evolution of specialized metabolites but also represents a key case for understanding the mechanism of cage-like structure formation (Li et al. 2022).

Paeoniflorin, a bicyclic monoterpene glycoside with a pinane skeleton, is conjugated with benzoyl and β-D-pyranoglucosyl groups and has a hemiacetal–acetal structure (Zhang et al. 2022). Paeoniflorin biosynthesis has been proposed to be initiated by the conversion of geranyl pyrophosphate (GPP) to α-pinene, catalyzed by pinene synthase (PIN), and the PIN PlPIN from Paeonia lactiflora has been identified (Ma et al. 2016). It is also worth noting that direct experimental evidence confirming α-pinene as the biosynthetic origin of paeoniflorin remains lacking. Considering the structures of all monoterpenoids with a pinane skeleton in Paeonia plants (Figure S1), we predicted the potential catalytic pathway from pinene to paeoniflorin. α-Pinene first undergoes hydroxylation and carbonylation at C4, followed by hydroxylation at C2 and C9, which culminates in cyclization to form the hemiacetal-acetal structure (Fig. 1). During hemiacetal–acetal moiety formation, the 8-debenzoylpaeoniflorin 8-O-benzoyltransferase (DPBT) (Zhang et al. 2023a) and a glycosyltransferase catalyze the attachment of the benzoyl group and β-D-glucopyranosyl unit to the core skeleton. In the proposed pathway, hydroxylation and carbonylation at C4 in α-pinene are considered fundamental steps in the biosynthesis of paeoniflorin. Although CYP6DE1, which catalyzes C4 hydroxylation of α-pinene, has been reported in mountain pine beetle (Chiu et al. 2019), the enzymes responsible for hydroxylation of α-pinene at C2, C4, and C9 have yet to be identified in plants.

Cytochrome P450 enzymes, which constitute the largest enzyme superfamily in plant metabolism (Renault et al. 2014), have been shown to participate in the biosynthesis of terpenoid compounds (Hamberger and Bak 2013). The CYP725 family plays a role in the paclitaxel biosynthetic pathway (Jiang et al. 2024). The CYP716 family has been implicated in triterpenoid saponin biosynthesis among plant families, including Araliaceae, Fabaceae, and Campanulaceae (Zheng et al. 2019; Li et al. 2025). Genes of the CYP71 and CYP76 families have been reported to participate in monoterpene hydroxylation (Hansen et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2023a, 2023b). For example, CYP71D13/15 in Mentha piperita are responsible for hydroxylation of limonene at the C3 and C6 positions. Many genes in the CYP76 family, such as CYP76B6 in Catharanthus roseus (Collu et al. 2001), CYP76B10 in Swertia mussotii (Wang et al. 2010), and CYP76C1/C2/C4/B6 (Höfer et al. 2014), have geraniol hydroxylase activity. In Vitis vinifera, CYP76F12/F14 have linalool hydroxylase activity and participate in the biosynthesis of wine lactone, a key odorant in wine (Ilc et al. 2017). Therefore, we propose that CYP450s are involved in paeoniflorin biosynthesis, with genes belonging to the CYP71 and CYP76 families serving as key candidate genes catalyzing the hydroxylation of α-pinene.

To date, no conclusive evidence confirms that paeoniflorin biosynthesis originates from α-pinene, and no genes catalyzing hydroxylation of α-pinene have been reported in plants. Here, we first used virus-induced gene silencing (VIGS) to silence PIN in Paeonia, establishing pinene as the precursor. We then identified 3 α-pinene oxidases, CYP71AN126 and CYP76A225/226, from 4 Paeonia cultivars with different paeoniflorin accumulation profiles. Functional VIGS assays demonstrated that silencing CYP71AN126, but not CYP76A225 or CYP76A226, significantly reduced the paeoniflorin content. Furthermore, we identified L493 as a critical residue in CYP71AN126 that is involved in regulating both catalytic site specificity and substrate specificity. This work provides a foundational framework for elucidating the complete paeoniflorin biosynthetic pathway.

Results

Paeoniflorin accumulation exhibits cultivar and organ specificity

Paeoniflorin is widely distributed among the tissues and organs of Paeonia plants, with its contents varying among cultivars. To systematically screen candidate genes involved in paeoniflorin biosynthesis, we measured the paeoniflorin content in different tissues (primary roots, secondary roots, stems, and leaves) of 4 Paeonia cultivars (Fig. 2a and b) using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). The obtained values were compared with a reference standard (Figure S2). We identified cultivars with significant paeoniflorin content differences: high-content cultivars P. lactiflora ‘Bing Gao’ (165.3 mg/g) and P. ostii ‘Lan Furong’ (64.47 mg/g) and low-content cultivars P. lactiflora ‘Belgravia’ (35.2 mg/g) and P. ostii (24.7 mg/g). Simultaneously, paeoniflorin exhibited a distinct tissue-specific distribution, and its content was significantly greater in the roots than in the leaves and stems (Fig. 2c). The multi-tissue transcriptome of these 4 cultivars was constructed to facilitate further research. Significant differences among tissues were observed in the score plot of principal component analysis of the transcriptome (Figure S3).

To further investigate whether the expression levels of genes in the paeoniflorin biosynthetic pathway correlate with paeoniflorin accumulation, we performed VIGS-mediated silencing of PlPIN in P. lactiflora, since no stable genetic transformation system is currently available for Paeonia (Wang et al., 2020). This gene had been previously implicated in paeoniflorin biosynthesis (Ma et al. 2016), and our aim was to determine whether it indeed participates in this pathway in vivo. Two-month-old P. lactiflora seedlings underwent Agrobacterium-mediated vacuum infiltration to specifically degrade the mRNA of PIN, resulting in transcriptional silencing of the target gene. After 1 month, the paeoniflorin content in the leaves was determined using HPLC. There was a significant decrease in the paeoniflorin content to 62.3% in the TRV2-PIN-GFP-treated group compared to the empty vector (EV) control group (Fig. 2e), which was accompanied by a reduction in PIN expression to 45.8% (Fig. 2d). These results demonstrate that PIN contributes to paeoniflorin biosynthesis. Despite our attempts to directly detect α-pinene levels using both solvent extraction and headspace solid-phase microextraction, this compound was not detectable in either treated or control plants. This is consistent with a previous report in which α-pinene was also not detected in P. lactiflora leaves (Zhao et al. 2024). A plausible explanation is that α-pinene, serving as a precursor for multiple terpenoid pathways, is rapidly metabolized by downstream enzymes such as P450s and UGTs, resulting in steady-state concentrations below the detection limit of our analytical methods. DPBT has been previously reported to be definitively involved in paeoniflorin biosynthesis (Zhang et al. 2023a, 2023b). The expression patterns of these 2 genes were consistent with the paeoniflorin content, showing higher levels in roots than in stems and leaves and higher levels in P. lactiflora than in P. ostii (Fig. 2f and g).

Screening and functional characterization of candidate P450s

Using a hidden Markov model (HMM) search of the P. ostii genome (Yuan et al. 2022), we extracted 539 P450 genes. Phylogenetic analysis with Arabidopsis thaliana P450s (Christ et al. 2019) classified them into 39 families (Figure S4). Since α-pinene is a cyclic monoterpene, and genes from the CYP71 and CYP76 families have been extensively reported to participate in the oxidation of monoterpenes (Hansen et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2023a, 2023b), we prioritized genes belonging to these 2 families as candidates for further characterization.

Based on transcriptome data from 4 varieties across different tissues, we visualized the expression profiles of CYP71 and CYP76 family genes using heatmaps. Ten P450 genes exhibiting variety- and tissue-specific expression patterns that parallel paeoniflorin content were selected as final candidates (Fig. 3a). These candidate genes from Paeonia plants were cloned and expressed heterologously in WAT11 yeast. Microsomal fractions were extracted for in vitro enzyme activity assays, with yeast microsomes expressing an EV serving as negative control. Products were detected using gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) and identified by comparing their retention times and mass spectra with standards (Figure S5).

Proteins encoded by Pos. gene11539 from both P. ostii and P. lactiflora catalyzed the conversion of α-pinene (compound 1) to verbenol (compound 2), verbenone (compound 3), and myrtenol (compound 4) and were named PoCYP71AN126 and PlCYP71AN126. Additionally, proteins encoded by Pos. gene67455 and Pos. gene13781 from P. ostii catalyzed the formation of myrtenol from α-pinene. They were named PoCYP76A225 and PoCYP76A226 (Fig. 3b and d). The remaining candidate genes showed no detectable catalytic activity toward α-pinene (Figure S6). Considering the ability of CYP71AN126 to catalyze hydroxylation and carbonylation at C4 in α-pinene, we verified its activity on verbenol. PoCYP71AN126 and PlCYP71AN126 exhibited carbonylation activity at C4 in verbenol, whereas PoCYP76A225/226 showed no activity in verbenol (Fig. 3c). Thus, CYP71AN126 catalyzed hydroxylation at C4 and C10 in α-pinene and carbonylation at C4, and CYP76A225/226 specifically catalyzed hydroxylation at C10 in α-pinene (Fig. 3d and e). The expression patterns of these 3 genes exhibit variety specificity and tissue specificity that are consistent with the paeoniflorin accumulation pattern (Figure S7).

Cytochrome P450 enzymes are typically associated with the membrane systems of cellular organelles such as the endoplasmic reticulum, mitochondria, Golgi apparatus, and vacuoles (Toda et al. 2012). Subcellular localization experiments showed that all 4 genes localized to the endoplasmic reticulum (Figure S8). However, PlPIN has only been reported to be predicted to localize to plastids based on bioinformatic analysis, suggesting that PlPIN and PlCYP71AN126 may reside in different subcellular compartments. To test whether these 2 enzymes can function in a coherent manner in vivo, we co-expressed PlPIN and PlCYP71AN126 in N. benthamiana leaves, rather than directly supplying α-pinene. The results showed that trace amounts of α-pinene, verbenol, verbenone, and myrtenol were successfully detected in the co-expressing leaves (Figure S9), indicating that the 2 enzymes can function in concert when co-expressed in this heterologous system.

In vivo role of CYP71AN126 in paeoniflorin biosynthesis confirmed by VIGS

The C10 position of paeoniflorin and its derivatives lacks a hydroxyl group, and our predicted biosynthetic pathway does not require C10 hydroxylation. Therefore, we hypothesized that myrtenol, the C10 hydroxylation product of α-pinene, does not participate in paeoniflorin biosynthesis. To test this hypothesis, we used VIGS technology to silence CYP71AN126 and CYP76A225 in 2-month-old P. ostii seedlings (Figure S10). There was a sharp contrast between the 2 genes. Silencing CYP71AN126 decreased the paeoniflorin content, with levels falling to 57.2% in P. ostii and 64.9% in P. lactiflora compared to their EV controls, consistent with the significant downregulation of the gene (Fig. 3f to i). Conversely, despite the successful silencing of CYP76A225 (Fig. 3f), no corresponding decrease in paeoniflorin accumulation was observed (Fig. 3g).

To further exclude the possible compensatory effect of CYP76A226, we performed simultaneous silencing of both CYP76A225 and CYP76A226 in 4-month-old P. ostii seedlings using VIGS. The results showed that, despite a significant reduction in the transcript levels of both genes, the paeoniflorin content still showed no significant change (Figure S11). These VIGS results are consistent with our hypothesis. Thus, we have established the important role of CYP71AN126 in paeoniflorin biosynthesis and demonstrated that CYP76A225/226-catalyzed C10 hydroxylation is likely not involved in this pathway.

Phylogenetic relationships of P450s from insects and plants

CYP6DE1, which catalyzes the hydroxylation of pinene to verbenol, has been identified in mountain pine beetle (Chiu et al. 2019). Plants and insects share a variety of secondary metabolites, including monoterpenes, benzoic acid derivatives, and others. This co-occurrence may represent one organism mimicking or interfering with the function of the compound in another organism (Beran et al. 2019). To investigate whether the ability of both the mountain pine beetle and Paeonia plants to convert pinene to verbenol represents a case of convergent evolution or results from horizontal gene transfer from plants, we constructed a phylogenetic tree including members of the CYP71 and CYP76 families, which have been reported to be involved in terpenoid hydroxylation in various plant species, together with members of the CYP6 family (Table S2), which have been reported to be involved in the metabolism of phytotoxins (eg, terpenoids, alkaloids) in insects.

Phylogenetic analysis revealed that PlCYP71AN126 and PoCYP71AN126 clustered within the CYP71 family, whereas CYP76A225 and CYP76A226 belonged to the CYP76 family. The CYP71 family is known to catalyze the hydroxylation of cyclic monoterpenes and sesquiterpenes, and the CYP76 family predominantly hydroxylates acyclic monoterpenes and diterpenes. None of the CYP6 family members, including CYP6DE1, clustered within any plant clade. Moreover, conserved motif structure analysis revealed that almost all members of the plant CYP71 and CYP76 families in the phylogenetic tree contain 10 conserved motifs, whereas insect CYP6 family members possess only 5 motifs. The marked difference in motif architecture between the plant and insect lineages reflects distinct evolutionary trajectories and structural constraints between the 2 kingdoms. Based on these analyses, we propose the hypothesis that verbenol production in mountain pine beetle and Paeonia plants has evolved by convergent evolution rather than by horizontal gene transfer or divergent evolution (Fig. 4a).

Substrate promiscuity analysis of CYP71AN126 and CYP76A225/226

To dissect the functional divergence of CYP71AN126 and CYP76A225/226 within the monoterpenoid metabolic network, we performed a systematic analysis of their substrate promiscuity. We selected other bicyclic monoterpenes structurally similar to α-pinene (β-pinene, α-pinene oxide, borneol), as well as substrates catalyzed by P450s from other clades of the phylogenetic tree (eg, geraniol, limonene, valencene), for in vitro enzyme activity assays. The results showed that CYP76A225 and CYP76A226 have narrow catalytic profiles, only catalyzing the hydroxylation of α-pinene and β-pinene, with no detectable activity toward the other tested substrates. In contrast, CYP71AN126 exhibited a relatively broad substrate recognition capacity, catalyzing the oxidative modification of 5 bicyclic monoterpenoids: α-pinene, β-pinene, verbenol, pinene oxide, and borneol (Fig. 4b to e). Notably, CYP71AN126 is the first plant cytochrome P450 enzyme reported to catalyze the conversion of borneol to camphor (Fig. 4b; Figure S12). Furthermore, none of the 4 candidate genes showed catalytic activity toward the substrates of other clades of the phylogenetic tree (Fig. 4e; Figure S13), indicating that the substrate recognition patterns are relatively conserved within each clade and that pinene oxidation activity may have evolved via neofunctionalization rather than through relaxation of substrate specificity.

Rational engineering of CYP71AN126 to eliminate C10-hydroxylase activity

PlCYP71AN126 catalyzes the oxidation of α-pinene at the C4 and C10 positions, and the side reaction, C10 hydroxylation, produces 33.7% side product of the total products. The product promiscuity of CYP71AN126 may adversely affect paeoniflorin biosynthesis, limiting its further utilization in synthetic biology. To elucidate the detailed catalytic mechanism of CYP71AN126 and eliminate its C10 hydroxylation side activity while retaining its C4 hydroxylation and carbonylation functions, we employed AlphaFold 3 to predict the complex structures of PlCYP71AN126, PoCYP71AN126, and PoCYP76A225/226 bound to the heme cofactor. Subsequently, the substrate α-pinene was docked into this complex (Fig. 5a to c). Despite sharing only 35.1 and 36.0% amino acid sequence identity between PlCYP71AN126 and PoCYP76A225/226 (Figure S14), PlCYP71AN126 exhibited a high degree of overall structural resemblance to PoCYP76A225/226, including in the substrate binding pocket, except in the N-terminal transmembrane helix (RMSD: 0.971 Å/1.031 Å). However, subtle conformational differences were observed in the loop regions at the edge of the catalytic domain (Fig. 5a and b). Natural sequence and activity differences between PlCYP71AN126, PoCYP71AN126, and PoCYP76A225/226 provide an excellent model for determining the molecular mechanisms regulating α-pinene hydroxylation at different positions.

To identify key residues associated with C10 hydroxylation activity, we performed a detailed comparative analysis of the amino acid residues forming the substrate binding pockets of PlCYP71AN126, PoCYP71AN126, PoCYP76A225, and PoCYP76A226. The residues in the substrate binding pockets were highly similar between PlCYP71AN126 and PoCYP71AN126 and between PoCYP76A225 and PoCYP76A226 (Fig. 5d and e). However, PlCYP71AN126 and PoCYP76A225 differed significantly, with 8 distinct residues surrounding the substrate (Fig. 5f). Modifying the key amino acids at these corresponding positions enables the adjustment of characteristics such as the size or charge property of the enzyme's active pocket, altering substrate selectivity and product specificity.

Subsequently, we performed site-directed mutagenesis of these key amino acid residues in PlCYP71AN126 and conducted in vitro experiments using normalized microsomes from yeast expressing PlCYP71AN126 and its mutants to verify the function of these key amino acid residues in PlCYP71AN126 (Figure S15). When using α-pinene as the substrate, V377A and V494A mutants maintained full catalytic activity and showed no significant changes in product preference, but their total product yields decreased to different extents (Fig. 5g; Figure S16a). Moreover, the L493A mutant exhibited a 78.5% reduction in overall substrate conversion (Figure S17), with the yield of the C4 hydroxylation and carbonylation products (compounds 2 and 3) decreasing by 67.7%, while it completely abolished the C10 hydroxylation activity (compound 4). All other mutants, including F123A, exhibited complete loss of catalytic activity (Fig. 5g; Figure S16a). With verbenol as the substrate, V377A, V494A, and L493A mutants maintained C4 carbonylative activity, whereas all other mutants (including F123A) were inactive (Figure S16b).

To elucidate the critical role of residue 493 in controlling the product specificity of CYP71AN126, Leu493 was substituted with amino acids bearing side chains of varying sizes and charges. Substitution with charged (L493D) or polar residues (L493T and L493S) impaired the enzymatic activity, and mutation to phenylalanine (L493F) also significantly decreased the enzymatic activity. Substitution of Leu493 with other hydrophobic amino acid residues (L493A, L493V, and L493I) reduced or abolished the formation of the C10 hydroxylation side product (Fig. 5g; Figure S16c). Specifically, L493A and L493V mutations reduced the yield of the main C4 product by 67.7% and 90.5%, respectively, but both completely eliminated C10 hydroxylation activity. Notably, although the L493I mutation led to a 27.9% reduction in overall substrate conversion (Figure S17), this decrease was mainly attributable to a 75.6% drop in the production of the C10 hydroxylation side product, with no effect on the formation of the main C4 product (Fig. 5g). Consequently, the relative proportion of the desired C4 products in the total product mixture was increased (Fig. 5h). With verbenol as the substrate, L493F, L493V, L493A, and L493I mutants maintained C4 carbonylative activity, whereas all other mutants were inactive (Figure S16d).

Transient expression of PlCYP71AN126 and its mutants PlCYP71AN126 L493A and PlCYP71AN126 L493I in N. benthamiana leaves, followed by feeding with the substrate, α-pinene, resulted in the formation of hydroxylated and carbonylated products, which confirmed their catalytic activity in vivo. GC-MS analysis showed that the C10 hydroxylation side product constituted 68.2% of the total product profile in PlCYP71AN126. In contrast, the L493I mutant produced only 29.7% of the C10 hydroxylation side product, whereas the yield of the main C4 product increased by 65.3% compared to wild type (WT). The L493A mutant reduced the yield of the main C4 product by 33.9% relative to WT, yet no C10 hydroxylation side product was detected (Fig. 5i and j). PlCYP71AN126 and its mutants exhibited distinct product profiles in N. benthamiana (in vivo) compared to yeast (in vitro). Specifically, the proportion of the C10 hydroxylation side product and the C4 carbonylation product was significantly higher in N. benthamiana than in yeast, likely due to the combined effects of multiple factors including the complex cellular environment and endogenous enzymes in tobacco, the reaction duration post-substrate infiltration, and differences in product volatility.

To further evaluate the regulatory role of the key residue L493 in substrate selectivity, we performed a comparative analysis of the substrate promiscuity of the WT, L493I, and L493A mutants using the yeast (in vitro) enzyme activity system. The L493I mutant retained catalytic activity toward β-pinene and borneol but completely lost the ability to oxidize pinene oxide. In contrast, the L493A mutant exhibited an even narrower substrate profile, retaining only weak catalytic activity toward borneol, with no detectable activity toward β-pinene or pinene oxide (Fig. 5k to m). Similar to the WT, L493I and L493A also showed no catalytic activity toward compounds 8 to 12 (Figure S18). These results indicate that, compared with the WT, both the L493I and L493A mutants displayed significantly enhanced substrate specificity, with L493A showing more stringent substrate selectivity (Fig. 5o).

These findings indicate that residue L493 not only serves as a key residue regulating catalytic site specificity but also plays a critical gating role in substrate recognition, establishing it as an important functional residue determining the substrate specificity of CYP71AN126 (Fig. 5n and o). We successfully identified 2 key mutants: L493A, which abolished C10 hydroxylation, and L493I, which reduced C10 hydroxylation. Both mutants exhibited stricter substrate specificity than WT. Furthermore, the L493A and L493I mutants retained catalytic activity toward α-pinene in yeast (in vitro) and in N. benthamiana (in vivo) and exhibited superior product specificity compared to WT, highlighting their great potential for applications in paeoniflorin biosynthesis.

Discussion

Paeoniflorin, a characteristic bioactive compound in the genus Paeonia, exhibits diverse pharmacological activities and significant medicinal value. Efforts have been made to elucidate its biosynthetic pathway. However, due to its complexity and the large genome size of Paeonia plants, paeoniflorin biosynthesis has been only partially elucidated, limiting its efficient bioproduction. By integrating VIGS-based functional validation, heterologous enzymatic characterization, and protein engineering, we established α-pinene as a precursor for paeoniflorin biosynthesis. We then identified CYP71AN126 as the key enzyme catalyzing the early oxidation step and addressed a critical side reaction that compromises pathway efficiency.

Prior work has shown a correlation between PIN transcription and paeoniflorin accumulation, but it offered no definitive evidence for the enzyme's functional role or unequivocal genetic validation in plants of the genus Paeonia (Ma et al. 2016). Our study provides the first reverse genetic evidence demonstrating the involvement of PIN in paeoniflorin biosynthesis (Fig. 2d). The consistency of the expression patterns of PIN and DPBT with paeoniflorin accumulation patterns indicates that our transcriptome data represent an excellent resource for elucidating the biosynthetic pathway of paeoniflorin. Based on this, 4 P450s catalyzing pinene oxidation were identified. The functional divergence among these enzymes is striking. CYP76A225/226 specifically catalyzes the formation of myrtenol, whereas CYP71AN126 catalyzes the formation of myrtenol, verbenol, and verbenone.

These pinene-derived oxides, as aroma compounds, are widely present in plants, such as Teucrium polium (de Barros et al. 2025), Pistacia lentiscus (Ayub et al. 2024), and Aeschynomene indica (Feng et al. 2024). They serve not only as high-value fragrances or food flavoring agents with important pharmacological activities but also as synthetic precursors for various significant compounds (Vidya and Agrawal 2003; Choi et al. 2010; Mrabti et al. 2023). The identification of CYP71AN126 and CYP76A225/226 provides new enzymatic tools for the biosynthesis of verbenol, verbenone, and myrtenol and has significant implications for studying monoterpene oxidative metabolism in other species. Interestingly, a P450 enzyme (CYP6DE1) capable of converting α-pinene to verbenol has also been reported in mountain pine beetle, where it plays a role in detoxification while also producing verbenol as an aggregation pheromone (Chiu et al. 2018, 2019; Xu et al. 2024). Phylogenetic analysis together with conserved motif structure analysis of the insect CYP6 family and the plant CYP71 and CYP76 families led us to propose that verbenol formation in the mountain pine beetle and Paeonia plants likely arose via convergent evolution, rather than by horizontal gene transfer or divergent evolution. This may represent another classic case of chemical convergence between plants and insects. Nevertheless, broader taxon sampling and more stringent phylogenetic approaches will be necessary to substantiate this hypothesis in the future.

Substrate promiscuity analysis revealed marked differences in the catalytic profiles of CYP71AN126 and CYP76A225/226. CYP76A225 and CYP76A226 exhibited highly narrow substrate specificities, catalyzing only the formation of myrtenol, suggesting that they may assume more dedicated metabolic roles in planta. In contrast, CYP71AN126 displayed a relatively broad substrate recognition capacity, capable of catalyzing various bicyclic bridged-ring monoterpenes. Of particular note, CYP71AN126 is the first plant P450 enzyme identified to catalyze the oxidation of borneol to camphor. Camphor, an important medicinal monoterpene ketone, has long been thought to be synthesized primarily by alcohol dehydrogenases or short-chain dehydrogenases/reductases. The discovery of this function not only expands our understanding of the CYP71 family's roles in monoterpene metabolism but also provides a new enzymatic tool for camphor biosynthesis. Furthermore, none of the 4 genes exhibited detectable catalytic activity toward the characteristic substrates of other clades in the phylogenetic tree, indicating that their oxidation activity toward bicyclic bridged-ring monoterpenes was likely acquired through an independent neofunctionalization process, rather than being inherited from a promiscuous ancestral enzyme.

For the paeoniflorin biosynthetic pathway, the in vivo importance of CYP71AN126, as confirmed by VIGS (Fig. 5), clearly establishes C4 oxidation as one of the committed routes for paeoniflorin but functionally excludes the CYP76A225/226-mediated C10 hydroxylation branch. This is consistent with our predicted paeoniflorin biosynthetic pathway. This indicates that the product promiscuity of CYP71AN126 likely limits its application in synthetic biology, as a considerable amount of output is diverted to myrtenol, an undesired side product in paeoniflorin biosynthesis.

Residues in the active center of P450 enzymes can fix substrate orientation via steric hindrance (hydrophobic residues) and electrostatic interactions (polar residues), directing specific C-H bonds toward the heme iron, which indirectly regulates hydroxylation site selectivity (Chen et al. 2022; Jiang et al. 2023). We performed protein structure modeling and molecular docking experiments for PlCYP71AN126, PoCYP71AN126, PoCYP76A225, and PoCYP76A226 based on the natural amino acid variations and activity divergence among them. Of the 8 distinct residues characterized, Leu493 was demonstrated to be pivotal. Mutagenesis experiments revealed that Leu493 substitution with smaller hydrophobic residues (Ala, Val, and Ile) effectively suppressed the C10 hydroxylation side reaction. This suggests that the steric bulk of the residue at position 493 likely contributes to orienting the α-pinene substrate at the active site and preventing it from adopting a conformation conducive to C10 attack. The complete loss of activity upon the introduction of charged or polar residues (L493D/T/S) highlights the necessity of a hydrophobic environment at this position for optimal catalysis, likely by facilitating the binding of the hydrophobic substrate or ensuring proper geometry for catalysis. The L493I mutant significantly reduced the yield of C10 hydroxylation side product without compromising the production of the main C4 products. This demonstrates that fine-tuning the steric properties of the active site selectively disfavors an unwanted reaction pathway. Although the L493A and L493V mutations impaired overall catalytic activity, they also completely eliminated C10 hydroxylation, highlighting a trade-off between selectivity and efficiency. The superiority of L493I and L493A was validated in both microbial and plant chassis.

Substrate promiscuity analysis revealed that both L493I and L493A exhibit stronger substrate specificity than WT. This finding has important implications for enzyme engineering: L493I and L493A not only suppress the undesired C10 hydroxylation side reaction while retaining effective catalytic activity toward the desired C4 position, but also further restrict the enzyme's activity toward non-cognate substrate. These results indicate that residue L493 is a critical residue regulating catalytic site specificity and substrate specificity of CYP71AN126. The presence of L493 may represent a critical evolutionary innovation that maintains the scaffold specificity of CYP71AN126, and it preserves the structural adaptability required for recognizing multiple bicyclic bridged-ring monoterpenes while preventing the erroneous entry of other monoterpenes through the steric gating effect of its side chain.

This study highlights the plasticity and high research value of CYP71AN126 and expands its application potential in paeoniflorin biosynthesis. As a bifunctional gating residue that simultaneously regulates both catalytic site specificity and substrate specificity, L493 provides a clear structure–function target for the rational engineering of CYP71AN126 family members and other plant P450 enzymes. Our work serves as an important reference for elucidating the detailed molecular mechanisms underlying the site-specific hydroxylation of α-pinene and offers valuable insights for engineering strategies aimed at altering both catalytic site specificity and substrate specificity. Moreover, it bridges mechanistic discovery with practical applications in future synthetic biology and enzyme engineering.

Following oxidation at C4 of α-pinene, subsequent hydroxylation at C2 and C9, together with cyclization between C2–C9 and C4–C9, are necessary for forming the characteristic hemiketal-acetal structure. The identification of CYP71AN126 provides a new bait for the screening of additional hydroxylases and cyclases involved in the downstream steps of paeoniflorin biosynthesis. Future work should integrate co-expression analysis, heterologous expression, in planta functional validation, and enzyme engineering to reconstitute these steps one by one.

In summary, this study resolves the long-standing question of how paeoniflorin biosynthesis is initiated from α-pinene. We identified and characterized the key oxidase CYP71AN126, validated its critical role in paeoniflorin biosynthesis in planta, and successfully engineered 2 variants, CYP71AN126L493I and CYP71AN126L493A, which exhibit superior substrate and product specificity compared to WT. These findings provide a robust foundation and critical tools for the reconstitution of the paeoniflorin pathway in a heterologous host, paving the way for its sustainable production via synthetic biology and laying a theoretical foundation for future biotechnology applications to improve the quality of Paeonia plants.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and chemicals

All plant materials were derived from Paeonia plants cultivated and maintained at the Shanghai Chenshan Botanical Garden. Four tissue types (primary roots, secondary roots, leaves, and stems) were collected, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and divided for transcriptome construction and paeoniflorin content analysis.

All chemicals used were of analytical grade. α-Pinene (CAS 7785-26-4) and verbenol (CAS 18881-04-4) were obtained from Weikeqi Biotech (Sichuan, China). Verbenone (CAS 1196-01-6), myrtenol (CAS 19894-97-4), and paeoniflorin (CAS 23180-57-6) were obtained from Yuanye Biotech (Shanghai, China). α-Pinene, verbenol, verbenone, and myrtenol were dissolved in ethanol. Paeoniflorin was dissolved in 70% methanol.

Paeoniflorin extraction

Plant materials were freeze-dried at −50 °C. For each extraction, 50 to 100 mg of freeze-dried tissue was ground into powder in a grinder mill at 60 Hz for 180 s. Two milliliters of 70% methanol (v/v) was added, and the mixture was vortexed at 60 Hz for 180 s. The extract was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatant was collected. Prior to analysis, impurities were removed by centrifuging the supernatant at 12,000 rpm for 10 min. A series of gradient concentrations of paeoniflorin standard solutions were prepared to generate a calibration curve for quantitative analysis. To ensure accurate quantification, sample extracts were diluted appropriately to fall within the linear range of the standard curve.

Paeoniflorin detection

Paeoniflorin analysis was performed using HPLC (Agilent 1260 Infinity II). Chromatographic separation was achieved using a C18 column (ZORBAX StableBond, 250 mm × 4.6 mm, 5 μm). The mobile phase consisted of 0.1% formic acid in ultrapure water (A) and acetonitrile (B), with a flow rate of 0.35 mL/min. The gradient program was as follows: 0 to 3 min, 95% A; 4 min, 76% A; 4 to 7 min, 76% A; 25 min, 73.5% A; 30 min, 5% A; 30 to 32 min, 5% A; 35 min, 95% A; and 35 to 40 min, 95% A. Detection wavelength was set at 230 nm. The injection volume was 10 μL, and the column temperature was maintained at 25 °C.

Genome-wide identification and phylogenetic analysis of CYP450 genes

The HMM profile of the PF00067 domain was employed to identify candidate CYP450 genes within the P. ostii genome using HMMsearch in TBtools-Ⅱ (Chen et al. 2023). Candidate DNA sequences shorter than 1,000 bp were filtered out. Multiple sequence alignment was performed using MEGA X software (Kumar et al. 2018), and a neighbor-joining phylogenetic tree was constructed, incorporating known A. thaliana CYP450 sequences.

Expression of CYP450 recombinant proteins in Saccharomyces cerevisiae and microsome preparation

Candidate CYP450 genes were amplified from cDNA using primers listed in Table S1 and cloned into the expression vector pESC-TRP, generating N-terminal myc-tagged fusion proteins, using the ClonExpress Ultra One Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme, China). Constructed plasmids were transformed into S. cerevisiae WAT11 chemically competent cells (Coolaber, China) using the Frozen-EZ Yeast Transformation II Kit (Zymo Research, United States). Recombinant yeast strains were cultured in synthetic dropout (SD) medium lacking tryptophan and containing 2% glucose at 30 °C for 72 h. Cultures were then transferred to 50 mL of SD + 2% glucose medium in 250 mL flasks and grown to an initial OD600 of 0.6, followed by incubation for an additional 12 h. Cells were harvested by centrifugation, resuspended in 50 mL YPGal medium (1% yeast extract, 2% peptone, 2% galactose) for induction, and incubated for 12 h. Cells were pelleted by centrifugation (4,000 rpm, 10 min, 4 °C), the supernatant discarded, and the pellet was washed once with TE buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 1 mM EDTA) and then resuspended in ice-cold TESB buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 1 mM EDTA, 0.6 M sorbitol, 0.5 mM PMSF, 1 mM DTT). Yeast cells were lysed using a One Shot cell disruptor (Constant Systems, UK) at 18.5 to 19.5 kpsi. The lysate was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C to collect the supernatant. CaCl2 was added to the supernatant to a final concentration of 18 mM, incubated on ice for 20 min, and then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. The resulting pellet, containing yeast microsomes, was resuspended in storage buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 1 mM EDTA, 40% glycerol (v/v)) for enzyme activity assays.

Enzyme assays

Enzyme activity assays were conducted in a 300 μL reaction mixture containing 50 μg microsomal protein, 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 1 mM NADPH, 0.5 mM GSH, and 500 μM substrate. Reactions were incubated at 30 °C for 4 h and then terminated by adding 300 μL ethyl acetate. The mixture was vortexed and centrifuged, and the organic phase was collected. Extraction with ethyl acetate was repeated twice. The combined organic phases were concentrated under a stream of nitrogen gas and analyzed by GC-MS.

GC-MS analysis

Capillary gas chromatography (GC) was performed on a Perkin-Elmer Clarus 680 gas chromatograph coupled to a Perkin-Elmer Clarus 600T mass spectrometer. Separation was achieved on an HP-5 ms column (30 m, 0.25 mm, 0.25 μm; Agilent Technologies) using splitless injection. The injector temperature was 250 °C. The oven temperature program was as follows: 60 °C for 2 min, ramped at 8 °C min−1 to 220 °C, then held at 220 °C for 10 min. Helium was used as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 1 mL min−1. Products were identified by comparison of their retention times and electron ionization mass spectra with those of authentic standards.

Subcellular localization

cDNA sequences of PlCYP71AN126, PoCYP71AN126, PoCYP76A225, and PoCYP76A226 (excluding stop codons) were cloned into the pCAMBIA1300-35S-GFP vector using primers detailed in Table S1. RFP-HDEL, an endoplasmic reticulum (ER)-localized marker protein, served as a positive control. Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101 harboring either the RFP-HDEL plasmid or the target gene plasmid was resuspended in buffer (10 mM MES, pH 5.6, 10 mM MgCl2, 200 μM acetosyringone) to an OD600 of 1.2 to 1.5. Equal volumes of the resuspended bacteria carrying the target gene and RFP-HDEL were mixed and used to infiltrate the abaxial side of leaves from 4-week-old N. benthamiana plants. Leaves were collected 3 d post-infiltration (dpi), and protein subcellular localization was observed using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Leica SP8).

VIGS assay

VIGS was performed in leaves to silence PlPIN, PlCYP71AN126, PoCYP71AN126, PoCYP76A225, and PoCYP76A226 following previously described methods (Xie et al. 2019). Tobacco rattle virus 2 (pTRV2) vectors were constructed using green fluorescent protein (GFP) as a reporter gene. Tobacco rattle virus 1 (pTRV1) vector was used as a helper vector, responsible for expressing the proteins necessary for viral replication and systemic spread. Fragments of PlPIN (316 bp), PlCYP71AN126 (535 bp), PoCYP71AN126 (535 bp), and PoCYP76A225 (506 bp) were individually cloned into the pTRV2 vector, generating the constructs pTRV2-PlPIN, pTRV2-PlCYP71AN126, pTRV2-PoCYP71AN126, and pTRV2-PoCYP76A225. These 4 pTRV2 constructs, the empty pTRV2 vector, and the pTRV1 vector were transformed into A. tumefaciens strain GV3101. Transformed bacteria were cultured at 28 °C with shaking at 220 rpm for 12 h. Bacterial cells were harvested by centrifugation and resuspended in infiltration buffer (10 mM MgCl2, 10 mM MES, 200 μM acetosyringone) to a final OD600 of 1.2 to 1.5. Cultures harboring pTRV1 and individual pTRV2 constructs were mixed at a 1:1 (v/v) ratio after gentle shaking at 28 °C for 3 h. Whole plants were submerged in the infiltration buffer and subjected to vacuum infiltration at 0.08 MPa for 20 min. GFP fluorescence in leaves was observed 7 dpi to verify successful TRV2 infection and systemic spread. Leaves were harvested 4 weeks post-infiltration for quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis and paeoniflorin content determination. For co-silencing of 2 genes, the pTRV1 culture and 2 pTRV2 cultures carrying different target genes were mixed at a 1:1:1 ratio, while all other steps remained unchanged. Due to the growth cycle of the plant material, single silencing of CYP71AN126 and CYP76A225 was performed in 2-month-old seedlings, whereas co-silencing of CYP76A225 and CYP76A226 was carried out in 4-month-old seedlings.

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time reverse transcription PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted using the SteadyPure Plant RNA Extraction Kit (ACCURATE, China). First-strand cDNA was synthesized from 1 μg total RNA using the Evo M-MLV RT Mix Kit with gDNA Clean (ACCURATE, China). Quantitative PCR was performed on a StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, United States) using the SYBR Green Premix Pro Taq HS qPCR Tracking Kit (ACCURATE, China), with 3 technical replicates per gene. Gene-specific primers were designed using Primer Premier 5 software. The actin gene (Pos.gene4533) was used as the reference gene. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method (Schmittgen and Livak 2008).

Structural prediction and molecular docking

This study employed AlphaFold 3 (https://alphafoldserver.com/) to predict the complex structure of PlCYP71AN126, PoCYP71AN126, PoCYP76A225, and PoCYP76A226 proteins and its cofactor heme (Abramson et al. 2024). Subsequently, molecular docking between these proteins and the α-pinene substrate was performed using Autodock Vina software version 1.1.2 (https://github.com/ccsb-scripps/AutoDock-Vina). The potential key amino acid sites were predicted based on the binding positions. Lastly, PyMOL software was utilized to enhance and present the images.

Detection of PlCYP71AN126 single-site mutant protein expression by western blotting

Microsomal proteins were separated by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred onto a polyvinylidene difluoride membrane. The membrane was incubated overnight at 4 °C with mouse monoclonal anti-myc antibody (ABclonal, China) diluted 1:2000. Subsequently, the membrane was incubated with horseradish peroxidase–conjugated rabbit anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody (ABclonal, China) diluted 1:1000 for 1 h at room temperature. Then, the membrane was developed using the ECL Enhanced Plus Kit (ABclonal, China), and target protein bands were visualized using the Image Lab software. Densitometric analysis of western blot bands was performed with ImageJ to normalize and equalize protein loading across samples.

Transient expression of PlCYP71AN126 and its L493 mutants in N. benthamiana

PlCYP71AN126, PlCYP71AN126L493A, and PlCYP71AN126L493I were constructed into plasmid pEAQ-HT-DEST1 using the ClonExpress Ultra One-Step Cloning Kit, with primers detailed in Table S1. The pEAQ-HT EV was used as a control. The A. tumefaciens strain GV3101 containing the constructed plasmid was resuspended in buffer (10 mM MES at pH 5.6, 10 mM MgCl2, 200 mM acetosyringone) to an OD600 of 0.8 and used to infiltrate the underside of 4-week-old N. benthamiana leaves of similar growth status.

Three days post-infiltration, the substrate solution (1 mM α-pinene, 10 mM MgCl2, 10 mM MES, pH 5.6) was injected into the same locations. Leaf samples were collected 16 h after substrate injection, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and ground to a fine powder. Subsequently, 200 mg of the powder was homogenized in 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tubes with 200 μL of ethyl acetate for 16 h. The extracts were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min, and the resulting supernatants were filtered and analyzed by GC-MS as described above.
