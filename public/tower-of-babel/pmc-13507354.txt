Brain regions with gestational age differences mediate cognition in adolescents born very premature
Authors: Dawn Jensen, Teddy Jakim, Qiang Li, Anne Murphy, Debra Bangasser, Vince Calhoun, Jingyu Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBeing born prematurely is known to have wide and diverse effects on neural development, some of which persist into adolescence. Currently, little research exists regarding the mediating and longitudinal effects of these neuronal differences into adolescence. Using ~2100 subjects from the Adolescent Brain and Cognitive Development study, a longitudinal cohort of adolescents (aged 10–14), identified as premature, and matched full-term peers, we conducted a canonical correlation analysis of their grey matter volume and fractional anisotropy. We also performed cross-sectional and longitudinal linear modeling with behavioral and cognitive measures and mediation analyses. Adolescents born more than 9 weeks premature showed reduced GM and increased FA in regions of the brain that are critical to attentional processes, memory, executive function, cognitive control, problem solving, and information processing. These differences mediated the relationship with cognition and persisted across four years of development. Neuronal changes associated with very premature birth are long-lasting and explain in part their lower cognitive functioning.Subject terms: Intelligence, Developmental biology

Introduction

Typical healthy gestation age (GA) is widely considered to be 37–42 weeks, which allows for optimal fetal development1. Babies born before this full-term of gestation are classified as varying levels of premature, with parturition between 32 and 37 weeks considered moderately premature, and before 32 weeks classified as very premature1. Development of myriad physiological, as well as neuronal structural and functional, processes continues right to the end of a full-term pregnancy2. This crucial developmental arc is disturbed by premature birth, possibly incurring long-term downstream consequences, particularly when considering neural development2.

A large body of research, focused primarily on infants, exists documenting the neurodevelopmental and behavioral deficits associated with moderately and very premature births. These deficits include, but are not limited to, disrupted cortical folding patterns of grey matter and connectivity of white matter within the first year of development3, poorer early school performance4, and mild but persistent cognitive and motor delays through age six5.

Recently, there has been interest in understanding what effects of premature birth might persist in adolescence. A small cohort in Sweden (n = 143, 74 moderately premature) found there were GA-dependent reductions in grey and white matter volume in adolescents aged 12–17 years old6. Another study showed that in very premature children, brain growth trajectories still lagged behind their full-term peers at ages seven and thirteen7. A recent study considering cortical thickness and volumetric differences based on GA8 used the Adolescent Brain and Cognitive Development (ABCD) cohort, a large longitudinal study of ~11 k subjects from 21 different sites9. This study focused on adolescents aged 9-10 who were moderately and very premature, finding modest differences in cortical thickness, surface area, and grey matter volume in diverse regions of the brain8. Another study using the same cohort found that maternal pregnancy complications, low birth weight, as well as premature birth, were associated with global altered surface area and cortical thickness10. Previous research has also established that there are socio-emotional as well as cognitive deficits associated with premature birth that seem to persist into adulthood11,12. These behavioral problems can manifest in adolescence as attention problems, anxiety, or poor emotional regulation11. The cognitive domains often impacted by very premature birth include memory, executive function, inhibitory control, and cognitive flexibility12.

While these most recent studies provide evidence that neurodevelopmental effects associated with premature birth seen in infants are still present in adolescence, they fail to elucidate the possible connection between these regional brain differences and the behavioral and cognitive deficits seen in premature subjects11. There are also very few studies that have researched the impacts of GA effects across time. To extend the current understanding of GA-dependent neuronal differences in early adolescence and their relationship to cognitive and behavioral deficits, we have chosen an approach that allows consideration of the developmental relationship between changes in GM volume and FA measures during adolescence. While not in lockstep with one another, there is research demonstrating that maturation-driven decreases in GM volume occur in coordination with increases in FA13,14. To that end, we applied a canonical correlation analysis (CCA), which will allow us to first focus on GA-dependent differences in highly correlated patterns of grey matter and white matter measures. A CCA finds maximally correlated linear combinations of two variables, highlighting data-driven multivariate relationships15. Since both grey matter volume and white matter integrity have been shown to be changing in synchrony, if diametrically, during adolescence13,14, leveraging the shared information between the two modalities would highlight developmentally relevant brain regions to then investigate longitudinally for GA-driven neuronal differences and their role in cognitive and behavioral performance.

We selected a group of adolescents from the ABCD cohort, 9–10 years old at baseline, that included subjects identified as moderately and very premature, as well as a group of full-term controls matched for sex and socio-economic status. These subjects had both grey matter volume (GM) and fractional anisotropy (FA) measures from the Freesurfer cortical and subcortical parcellations, and in the case of FA, additional tract-based parcellations from Atlas Tracts16. These measures were calculated by the ABCD study (total n = 2128) from three timepoints (9–14 yrs old), as well as cognitive and behavioral measures from the child behavioral checklist and the NIH toolbox. To identify the patterns of correlated grey matter volume (GM) and white matter integrity (fractional anisotropy (FA)), we conducted a canonical correlation analysis (CCA), resulting in canonical variates (CVs) of highly correlated regions throughout the brain. CCA is a form of multivariate analysis that finds simultaneous latent features between two modalities of data, here GM and FA, such that the correlations between the modalities are maximized17. This multimodal analysis allowed us to conduct a whole-brain, data-driven investigation by leveraging coherent patterns between GM and FA not possible with a unimodal analysis15. These pairs of latent canonical variates (CVs) are multivariate linear combinations of the original measurements, reflecting each GM and FA region’s contribution to that latent combination18. This allowed us to identify regions driving the highly correlated CVs. Individual participants’ contributions to the GM and FA patterns in each CV are also calculated, which are then used in linear regression models to evaluate associations with GA, behavior, and cognitive performance, followed by mediation analyses to determine if the identified brain regions mediate these relationships. Finally, we performed longitudinal analysis of three timepoints from ages 10–14 using repeated measures linear mixed-effects models. We hypothesize that regional differences in GM and FA related to prematurity will have mediating effects on adolescent cognitive and behavioral measures during adolescence. This comprehensive investigation will further our understanding of the implications of GA-dependent GM and FA differences, their impact on cognition, and their persistence across adolescence.

Results

Adolescents born very prematurely have significant differences in correlated patterns of GM and FA at 10 years old

Eight pairs of significantly correlated CVs, after Bonferroni correction, replicated for this cohort, with the minimum correlation (r) of 0.49 and a maximum correlation (r) of 0.74. Supplementary Materials 1 contains the full details of the CVs. Of the replicated CVs, four showed significant group differences. These differences were seen in CV2, where very premature adolescents contributed more to the correlated patterns in both GM (t = 3.50, p < 0.0005) and FA (t = 5.19, p < 2.25^-7); in CV5, where very premature adolescents contributed more to the FA (t = 3.17, p < 0.002); in CV7, where very premature adolescents contributed more to the patterns of GM (t = 3.38 m p < 0.0007); and in CV8, where moderately premature adolescents contributed less to the patterns of FA (t = -4.48, p < 8.11^-6). CV2 highlighted correlated patterns between regions of GM (the highest 5 contributors) in the left thalamus (DSK), left putamen (DSK), left caudate (DSK), right medial orbitofrontal gyrus (DSK), left anterior cingulate (DSK), and FA (the highest five contributors) in the right superior longitudinal fasiculus (slf) (AT), right superior corticostriate (DSK), left thalamus (DSK), right inferior frontal fasiculus (AT), and left orbitofrontal cortex (DSK). The top five regions of FA in CV5 consisted of the right superior corticostriate (DSK), right insula (DSK), left superior corticostriate (DSK), right anterior cingulate (DSK), and left slf (AT). The top five regions of GM in CV7 were right superior frontal cortex (DSK), left orbitofrontal cortex (DSK), right cerebellar cortex (DSK), right putamen (DSK), and right pars triangularis (DSK). The top five contributors to the FA patterns highlighted in CV8 were right superior corticostriate (DSK), right slf (AT), left slf (AT), left superior corticostriate (DSK), and right anterior cingulate (AT). Brain region-specific GA-dependent differences at 10 years old

Results of the linear regression investigating group differences in GM and FA within the regions that contributed to the correlated patterns in CV2, CV5, CV7, and CV8 were seen primarily in GM after correction for multiple testing. GA-dependent differences in behavior and cognition at 10 years old

Significant group differences were identified in cognitive performance after multiple testing corrections, with moderately premature (t = −4.1, p < 3.2^-5) and very premature (t = -6.9, p < 7.2^-12) adolescents both performing significantly less well than their full-term peers in crystalized cognition. With regard to fluid cognition, only the very premature (t = −6.00, p < 2.3^-9) adolescents performed significantly less well. Figure 3A shows the differences in the baseline performance of the GA groups on the fluid and crystalized cognition metric. There were no significant GA differences found for subjects’ internalizing and externalizing scores. Supplementary Materials 3 contains the full model results.

Brain regions with GA-dependent differences mediate the relationship between GA and cognition at 10 years old

To determine if these group differences in the brain might mediate cognitive performance, we first performed linear regressions to test for significant relationships between these brain regions and fluid and crystalized cognition. Mediation analyses were only performed on brain regions that were significantly linearly associated with cognition. The GM volume in the right cerebellar cortex mediated the relationship between GA and fluid cognition by 11% and crystalized cognition by 15%. The GM volume in the left anterior cingulate mediated the relationship between GA and crystalized cognition by 11%. Figure 3B, C highlights the mediated effects found in both regions.

GA-dependent regional brain differences persist from ages 10–14

Of the four brain regions with significant cross-sectional GA-dependent differences, two showed significant longitudinal differences in the repeated measures mixed-effects models. The GM volume in the left thalamus, very premature adolescents had lower gray matter volume (t = −2.99, p < 0.003), across time. Conversely, in the right anterior cingulate, very premature adolescents had higher FA (t = 3.89, p < 0.0001), than their peers. These GA-dependent differences can be seen in Fig. 2C, D. Complete model details are found in Supplementary Materials 4.

Discussion

Using a multivariate approach to identify brain regions of interest in a large longitudinal adolescent population, our study revealed that differences in GM and FA associated with gestational age are still present in adolescence and can be seen in correlated structural brain regions that highlight cortical and subcortical relationships. Additionally, we established that these regional differences in GM mediate cognitive performance. A longitudinal examination of these regional GM and FA differences showed little change in trajectory across time. Of the adolescents born moderately versus very prematurely, the very premature group showed the most profound differences, suggesting that the largest effects of premature birth impact those born at 32 weeks or earlier.

Previous research using this cohort to investigate the neuronal impacts of gestational age considered cortical thickness and surface area of grey matter8 at baseline, 10 years of age. Our study replicated their cross-sectional results, even though we applied a CCA to initially select ROIs based on correlated patterns rather than a whole-brain investigation. Both studies found that adolescents born very prematurely had lower GM volume than moderately premature and full-term subjects, in particular in the left anterior cingulate, the right cerebellar cortex, and the left thalamus.

While not as extensively studied as GM differences related to GA, there is some evidence that white matter integrity, as measured by FA, also differs with regard to GA. FA is thought to indirectly reflect axon density, myelination, and axonal integrity based on diffusion measures14. Our study saw differences in FA in the right anterior cingulate (DSK). Subjects who were in the very premature group had higher FA than their more moderately premature and full-term peers. A small study that considered 18 white matter tracts in children and adolescents who were born very premature found diverse regions of decreased and increased FA when compared to peers who were born full-term19. Our study only found GA-dependent differences in the anterior cingulate, but it was also focused on FA in regions that were highly correlated with regions of GM at baseline.

Our longitudinal analysis of these regions showed that regardless of whether GM was increasing or decreasing over time during this stage of development, subjects born moderately or very prematurely did not change their trajectory and remained lower in GM than their peers. They neither converged nor diverged, neither worsening nor improving as their development progressed. The longitudinal FA results were also consistent, with subjects maintaining their GA-dependent differences across the four years. This longitudinal analysis, in a large and diverse cohort, supports the findings of the much smaller study of growth trajectories with regard to the persistent GM and FA GA-dependent differences over time7.

Cognitive deficits have been well documented among children and adolescents born moderately and very premature20,21. Our study found the expected lower cognitive performance associated with very premature birth in both fluid and crystalized cognition. A meta-analysis from 2018 investigated which risk factor associated with very premature birth might be responsible for this deficit21. They considered common complications of premature birth that included intraventricular hemorrhage, periventricular leukomalacia, bronchopulmonary dysplasia, necrotizing enterocolotis, sepsis, and postnatal corticosteroid use20. Their analysis found that bronchopulmonary dysplasia explained 65% of the differences in intelligence measures across 71 unique cohorts21. Our mediation results show that the persistent differences in GM in the left anterior cingulate and right cerebellar cortex also explained between 11% and 15%, although differences in FA did not have any mediation effects on cognition.

The regions of GM in our study that had GA-dependent differences played varied and diverse roles in cognition. As a central integrator, the thalamus supports cognition by playing a role in shaping mental representations22. The anterior cingulate plays a role in the cognitive control process23. The cerebellum contributes to important cognitive functions such as executive function, working memory, and problem solving24. Notably, these regions also have synergistic roles in cognition. Research in animals suggests that thalamic-cingulate connectivity regulates cognitive flexibility and information processing25,26. Additionally, the cerebello-thalamo-cortical pathway has been found to be involved in executive function and cognitive control27. Disruptions to these regions or circuits could degrade problem-solving skills, as well as make retention and recall of prior knowledge more difficult.

A comprehensive review from 2016 highlights the socio-emotional deficits found in adolescents born very premature11. We did not detect differences or deficits in behavioral problems, which may be attributable to two factors. Firstly, this study has a much larger number of subjects than most of the previous studies. This could lead to a smaller effect size across a much more heterogeneous cohort, while studies with a smaller number of subjects may have inflated effect sizes28. We also limited our investigation of socio-emotional deficits to the summary scores of internalizing and externalizing behaviors. Future work should investigate more specific behaviors, for example, depression, social anxiety, or oppositional disorders.

Limitations and future directions

There are some limitations to this research. Mediation analyses were limited to baseline measures due to the missingness of second and fourth-year summary cognitive measures. Future studies could consider cognitive measures with more complete continuity. Our study also restricted itself to regions found through the multivariate patterns of highly correlated grey and white matter. An ROI driven analysis, for example, focused on white matter microstructure or thalamic connectivity, might allow a deeper dive into possible drivers of these GA-dependent differences.

Conclusion

Using a combination of cross-sectional, longitudinal, and mediation analyses, our study established that neuronal changes associated with very premature birth persist into and through adolescence. These GA-dependent differences were reduced GM and FA in regions of the brain that are critical to attentional processes, memory, cognitive control, problem solving, and information processing. These differences not only mediated the relationship with cognition but also persisted across four years of development with little change in trajectory. This suggests that neuronal changes associated with very premature birth are long-lasting and explain, in part, their lower cognitive functioning.

Methods

Cohort

The ABCD Study, the largest long-term study of brain development and child health in the United States, has collected data on a wide spectrum of variables to inform our understanding of the environmental, genetic, neurobiological, cognitive, and behavioral factors that affect or associate with healthy brain development9. Recruitment of subjects was carried out at 21 different sites across the United States29. Deliberate effort was made to maximally reflect the demographic and socio-economic distributions within the US population of 9-10 year old children29. We selected 727 adolescents (398 m/329 f) who were born at least four to eight weeks premature (labeled as the moderately premature group) and another 154 subjects (78 m/76 f) who were more than nine weeks premature (labeled as very premature). To construct the control group, we selected another 1,247 adolescents (667 m/580 f) from the remaining full-term cohort for a total of 2128 subjects. The control group was matched to the premature group for sex and socio-economic status. All subjects were from data release 5. Table 1 has the breakdown of subjects across the multiple time points.

Neuroimaging measures

The ABCD Study has collected structural MRI (sMRI), and diffusion MRI (dMRI), across 22 sites with scanning parameters as harmonized as possible with three different scanner types (GE, Philips, and Siemens). GM measures were obtained from T1-weighted collected with the following parameters: slices = 176–256, field of view = 256 × 240–256, TE = 2.8 ms, flip angle = 8°30. FA measures were calculated from dMRI collected with the following parameters: slices = 81, field of view = 240 × 240, TE = 88 ms, flip angle = 90°, diffusion directions = 96, b-values = 300030. These images were then processed using the Multimodal Processing Stream (MMPS), software designed to process large-scale multimodal neuroimaging data30. This pre-processing for the T1-weighted images included gradient distortion correction, intensity normalization, tissue segmentation, and then rigid alignment with a FreeSurfer (v7.1.1) Desikan atlas30. The dMRI images were corrected for eddy current distortion, head motion, and B0 distortions, and then resampled to standard orientation16. Tensor values were calculated from the full shell, using all b values, for microstructural tissue measures that included FA16. GM volume measures for subcortical and cortical regional values were then extracted from the FreeSurfer Desikan atlas for a total of 94 regions, while FA values were extracted from both the Desikan (DSK) atlas for cortical and subcortical regions as well as the AtlasTract (AT) for tract-specific measures for a total of 127 regions16. Image quality ratings were calculated from pial and white matter overestimation, artifacts, and inhomogeneity. Image artifacts were also quantified, as was head motion30. Subjects who had ratings that indicated excessive motion, artifacts, or sub-par image quality were excluded. These quality control and FreeSurfer derivate measure were provided by the ABCD study in the 5.0 Release.

Cognitive and behavioral measures

Two summary cognitive measures, fluid and crystalized cognition, were derived from the battery of NIH Toolbox tests given to subjects. These outcomes spanned cognitive domains such as working memory, executive function, attention, episodic memory, language, and processing speed31. The fluid cognition summary score is calculated from the results of the Dimensional Change Card Sort, Flanker Inhibitory Control and Attention, Picture Sequence Memory (Form A), List Sorting Working Memory, and Pattern Comparison tests. This fluid cognition score is considered to represent capacity information processing in novel situations31,32. The crystalized cognition summary score is calculated from the results of the Picture Vocabulary and Oral Reading Recognition tests, representing acquired knowledge31,32.

Three summary behavioral measures, internalizing, externalizing, and total problems, were derived from the Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL)29. The CBCL is a parent-rating scale in the Achenbach System of Empirically Based Assessment that detects behavioral and emotional problems in children and adolescents33. Questions in the CBCL report correspond to eight empirically based syndrome scales (anxious/depressed, withdrawn/depressed, somatic complaints, social problems, thought problems, attention problems, rule-breaking, and aggressive behavior). Internalizing behavior scores are compiled from the withdrawn, somatic complaints, and anxiety/depressed scores; externalizing behavior scores comprise delinquent and aggressive behavior scores, and total behaviors reflect a summary of the entire CBCL9.

Statistics and Reproducibility

Canonical correlation analysis of GM and FA

A canonical correlation analysis (CCA) on the grey and white matter measurements was applied as a method of data decomposition to find maximally correlated canonical variates, using the metan package in R (version 4.1.2)34. The goal of a CCA is to find the maximally correlated latent variates by projecting two sets of high-dimensional data into linear space18. These datasets, XϵRnxp and YϵRnxp, where n is the number of subjects and p and q represent the number of variables in the datasets X and Y, the CCA will identify projection vectors u and v that solve18 the following: maxu,vu⊤∑xyv=minu,v∣Xu−Yv∣22 subject to ∣∣Xu∣∣22=∥Yv∥22=1, where ΣXY is the covariance matrix of XY. For our analysis, the imaging variables were scaled prior to running the CCA. X represents GM data, Y represents FA data, u and v represent GM and FA region’s contribution to the latent CVs, respectively. Xu represents the subjects’ contributions/loadings to each GM CV, and Yv is the subjects’ loadings to the FA CV. A Bartlett’s test was applied to determine the significance of the canonical correlations and then corrected for the number of CVs. Only CVs with significant correlation values were considered for this analysis.

We implemented a discovery and replication method (n = 2128), where the data was split randomly 50/50, to correct for overinflation18. Canonical weights were first estimated in the discovery sample and applied to the replication sample to compute the canonical scores. Those CVs that replicated were retained for further analysis.

Cross-sectional analyses of baseline measures

GA-dependent differences in the brain -

Replicated CVs were used as the dependent variables (subjects loadings) in the linear mixed effects models using the lme4 package in R (version 4.1.2)35. GA status was the independent variable, sex and age (corrected to account for birth differences36,37) were the fixed effects, and site was the random effect. Brain regions with the top five highest loadings for the CVs that were significantly associated were then included in linear mixed effects models as the dependent variables (subjects’ GM and FA measures) to again test for GA status differences, with sex and age as fixed effects, and site as the random effect (n = 2128). For these and later linear models, the full-term group was designated as the reference group against which the moderately and very premature groups were compared. Bonferroni correction was applied to control for Type I error38. Plots of significant relationships were created using marginal effects calculated using the ggeffects package in R39.

GA-dependent differences in behavior and cognition -

The linear relationship between GA status and baseline behavioral measures (problematic internalizing and externalizing behaviors) and baseline cognitive measures (fluid and crystalized cognition) was tested, with behavioral and cognitive measures as the dependent variable, GA status as the independent variable, and sex and age as fixed effects, and site as the random effect. Brain regions significant for GA status were also tested for their linear relationship with baseline cognitive and behavioral measures (n = 2102). Bonferroni correction was applied to control for Type I error38. Plots of significant relationships were created using marginal effects calculated using the ggeffects package in R39.

GA-brain-cognition mediation analyses -

A mediation analysis was performed using the mediation package in R40 to ascertain how much each brain regions’ GM or FA measures might mediate the relationship between GA status and baseline behavioral/cognitive measures. The cognitive and behavioral measures were the dependent variables, GA status was the independent variable, and brain regions from the CVs identified as having significant linear relationships with GA status and the cognitive/behavioral measures (n = 2102).

Longitudinal analyses

A repeated measures mixed effects model41 was used to test the longitudinal relationship between GA and the brain regions of interest identified in the cross-sectional analysis. Subject’s GM and FA measures from three time points (10, 12, and 14 years of age) were the dependent variables, their GA status was the independent variable, sex and age were included as fixed effects(baseline: n = 2128, 2nd year follow-up: n = 1470, 4th year follow-up: n = 584). Subject intercepts and slopes were included as random effects to account for individual variability as well as repeated measures42. The site was also included as a random effect. Bonferroni correction was applied to control for Type I error38. Plots of significant relationships were created using marginal effects calculated using the ggeffects package in R39.
