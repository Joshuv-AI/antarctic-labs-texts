First molecular diagnosis of the human pathogen Rickettsia raoultii and other spotted fever group rickettsiae in Sudanese ixodid ticks from domestic ruminants
Authors: Nagwa Eisawi, Jabbar Ahmed, Mohammed A Bakheit, Dina A Hassan, Mohammed O Hussien, Abdel Rahim M El Hussein
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRickettsial infections are often neglected and poorly recognized by physicians in many tropical and subtropical regions. Despite a number of recent reports describing rickettsial diseases in new locations and the discovery of new rickettsiae, medical science and research have largely neglected the diagnosis and antimicrobial treatment of rickettsial infections in subtropical and tropical areas; thus, much remains to be discovered. This study aimed to detect and characterize spotted fever group (SFG) rickettsiae in ixodid ticks infesting domestic ruminants in Khartoum State.MethodsPolymerase chain reaction targeting both genes that encode for citrate synthase (gltA) and outer membrane protein (ompA) was performed for the presence of SFG rickettsia followed by sequence and phylogenetic analysis.ResultsOf the 202 ticks examined for the presence of SFG rickettsia, gltA gene was detected in 4 samples (2%). Furthermore, gltA‐positive samples were used to amplify the ompA gene, in which only two samples yielded positive results. Sequence and phylogenetic analysis of the positive samples revealed four different species of SFG rickettsiae: Rickettsia aeschlimannii, Rickettsia rhipicephali, Rickettsia massiliae and Rickettsia raoultii.ConclusionsThese results indicated the presence of SFG rickettsia in Sudanese ticks. This also indicates that humans have an opportunity to acquire these infections. It is important to keep in mind the need for careful consideration of rickettsial infections in individuals with a fever of unknown origin.Keywords: gltA, Khartoum State, ompA, SFG rickettsiae, ticks

INTRODUCTION

Ticks and tick‐borne diseases are important causes of losses to the livestock industry, particularly in the production of cattle and small ruminants in tropical and subtropical areas (Perveen et al., 2021). Tick‐borne diseases are prevalent in Sudan, causing considerable economic losses and hindering the growth of animal resources. Among these diseases, tropical theileriosis, malignant ovine theileriosis, cowdriosis, babesiosis, anaplasmosis and avian spirochaetosis are particularly noteworthy (El Hussein et al., 2004).

Spotted fever group (SFG) rickettsiae are obligatory intracellular gram‐negative bacteria that belong to the genus Rickettsia. They are associated with arthropods, mainly ticks that act as vectors and/or reservoirs (Parola et al., 2005). Ruminant ectoparasites can act as mechanical vectors for selected Rickettsia SFG species. These ectoparasites can harbour the bacteria on their body surfaces and transmit them from infected animals to human or other susceptible hosts during feeding or close contact. In addition, ruminant ectoparasites can contribute to the maintenance and amplification of Rickettsia SFG in certain ecological settings (Radzijevskaja et al., 2018). Rickettsiae are vertically transmitted symbionts of invertebrates, and it has been suggested that they are initially symbionts of invertebrates that secondarily become vertebrate pathogens (Perlman et al., 2006). More than 25 new species or subspecies of rickettsiae have been identified as emerging agents of human tick‐borne diseases (Parola et al., 2013). Rickettsia raoultii is a gram‐negative, coccobacillus‐shaped bacterium. It is an intracellular pathogen transmitted to humans and animals through arthropod vectors, particularly ticks. It is considered an emerging pathogen and has been associated with a febrile illness in humans (Seo et al., 2020).

To date, six tick‐borne rickettsiae pathogens have been reported in humans in Africa (Parola et al., 2013). Rickettsia conorii conorii, the agent of Mediterranean spotted fever transmitted by Rhipicephalus sanguineus (Bitam et al., 2006), is endemic in North Africa and has been reported in South Africa, Kenya and Zimbabwe. Rickettsia conorii caspia, the agent of Astrakhan fever, was detected in R. sanguineus in Chad (Fournier et al., 2003). It has been nearly three decades since Rickettsia africae, the cause of African tick‐bite fever, was found in sub‐Saharan Africa (Kelly et al., 1992). In southern Africa, Amblyomma hebraeum is the identified vector and reservoir of R. africae and has also been detected in Amblyomma variegatum throughout West, Central and Eastern sub‐Saharan Africa and Sudan (Nakao et al., 2015). In addition, R. africae was detected in Amblyomma lepidum in Sudan (Morita et al., 2004). Rickettsia aeschlimannii transmitted by the bites of Hyalomma marginatum marginatum and Hyalomma marginatum rufipes has been described in several African countries, including Morocco, Niger, Mali, Zimbabwe, South Africa, Kenya and Sudan (Morita et al., 2004). Rickettsia massiliae, which was demonstrated to be a human pathogen in 2005, has been detected in several Rhipicephalus ticks parasitizing cattle in the Central African Republic and Mali (Parola et al., 2005). It should be noted that the last four rickettsiae were first identified in ticks before being recognized as human pathogens (Parola et al., 2005). Rickettsia rhipicephali, a rickettsia of unknown pathogenicity, has been detected in ticks of the Rhipicephalus composites group and Rhipicephalus lunulatus (Dupont et al., 1994).

The objective of this study was to detect and characterize SFG rickettsiae in ixodid ticks infesting domestic ruminants in Khartoum State.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study area

This study was carried out in Khartoum State of Sudan. The investigation area is located between latitudes 15.16–16.65 north and longitudes 31.64–32.05 east, in the savannah climatic zone (Figure 1).

Map of Khartoum State showing the locations from where samples were collected.

Tick collection

Adult ticks (n = 202, 111 F and 91 M) were collected from 31 sheep (Ovis aries), 39 camels (Camelus dromedarius) and 162 cattle (Bos taurus) from 25 different areas distributed in the three provinces of Khartoum State after obtaining the owners’ consent. Ticks were collected on either of the longitudinal halves of the body to represent all anatomical parts of the body from head to tail. All visible ticks were collected using fine‐tipped tweezers to grasp the tick as close to the skin surface as possible. The ticks from each animal were preserved in separate vials containing 70% ethanol. The vials were labelled to show the sites and dates of collection as well as the animal numbers. Tick identification and speciation were carried out at the Department of Parasitology, Faculty of Veterinary Medicine, under a dissecting microscope according to the keys of Hoogstraal (1956), Hoogstraal and Kaiser (1959) and Walker et al. (2014).

DNA extraction

Adult ticks were individually processed. The specimens were rinsed with distilled water and left to dry on sterile filter paper. DNA was extracted using a modified simple salting out procedure described by Miller et al. (1988). Briefly, the tick samples were frozen immediately in liquid nitrogen and ground to a fine powder using a mortar and pestle. Samples were resuspended in 1 mL lysis buffer (10 mM Tris–HCl, 400 mM NaCl and 2 mM Na2 EDTA, pH 8.2) and transferred to a 15 mL falcon tube. The cell lysates were digested overnight at 37°C with 0.2 mL of 10% SDS and 50 µL of proteinase K solution (20 mg/mL). After digestion was completed, 0.25 mL of saturated NaCl (approximately 6 M) was added to each tube, shaken vigorously for 15 s, allowed to stand for 10 min at room temperature and then centrifuged at 4000 RCF for 15 min. The precipitated protein pellet was left at the bottom of the tube, and the supernatant containing the DNA was transferred to another 15 mL falcon tube. Two volumes of cold absolute ethanol were added, and the tubes were inverted several times and incubated at −20°C overnight, followed by centrifugation at 4000 RCF for 15 min. The supernatant was discarded, and 1 mL of 70% ethanol was added and centrifuged at 4000 RCF for 15 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the DNA pellet was left to dry for at least 3 h and then suspended in 30–50 µL ddH2O. The DNA concentration and purity were measured using an Eppendorf photometer (Eppendorf).

PCR for detection of Rickettsia spp., cloning and sequencing

The polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was performed as previously described by Regnery et al. (1991) (Table 1). The first PCR with the primer pair RpCS.877p/RpCS.1258n targeted a 381‐bp fragment of the gltA gene, which encodes citrate synthase, a highly conserved enzyme present in all rickettsiae (Roux & Raoult, 2000). The second PCR with the primer pair Rr190.70p/Rr190.602n amplified a 530‐bp fragment of the ompA gene encoding an autotransporter present only in the SFG rickettsiae (Fournier et al., 1998). All amplifications were performed in a T3000 thermocycler (Biometra Analytic Jena) and were carried out in a 25 µL reaction mix using the protocols supplied with the Thermo Scientific Dream Taq Green PCR Master Mix (2×) (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc). The reaction typically contained 2 µL of DNA template, 12.5 µL of DreamTaq Green PCR master mix, 8.5 µL nuclease‐free water and 2 µL of oligonucleotide primer pairs. Sterile water or DNA‐free extraction controls served as negative controls, and Rickettsia rickettsii DNA was used as a positive control. The final PCR products were analysed by 1.0% agarose gel electrophoresis. Initially, all individual 202 DNA extracts were screened for the presence of the gltA gene using the first PCR. Samples positive for gltA PCR were evaluated for amplification of the ompA gene. PCR products from positive samples were cloned using the QIAGEN PCR Cloning Kit and purified using the QIAprep miniprep kit.

Phylogenetic analysis

Sequence homology searches were performed using Basal Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST) in GenBank (www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov.library.vu.edu.au/BLAST/). Alignment and phylogenetic reconstructions were performed using the function ‘build’ of ETE3 3.1.2 (Huerta‐Cepas et al., 2016) as implemented on the GenomeNet (https://www.genome.jp/tools/ete/). The tree was constructed using FastTree v2.1.8 with default parameters (Price et al., 2010).

RESULTS

PCR

The homogenates of 202 individual ticks of different species were examined for the presence of SFG rickettsiae using PCR (Table 2). The gltA gene PCR was positive in four samples (2%) (Figure 2). Furthermore, the gltA‐positive samples were amplified by a second PCR amplification of the ompA gene, in which only two samples yielded positive results (Figure 3). The overall prevalence rate of SFG rickettsiae in ticks infesting domestic ruminants in Khartoum State was 2%. Rickettsia infection was found in 1 out of 4 (25%) Hyalomma rufipes, 1 out of 96 (1.04%) Hyalomma anatolicum and 2 out of 23 (8.7%) R. sanguineus, whereas such infections were not detected in the other five spp. of the collected ticks (Table 2).

Phylogenetic analysis based on partial sequence of gltA gene (four samples) and ompA gene (two samples) indicated that the rickettsia samples (six) from Sudan are highly related to the specific Rickettsia SFG species as follows: R. aeschlimannii (GenBank accession no. KU666468) (Table 3) (Figures 4 and 5).

DISCUSSION

Rickettsial infections are often neglected and poorly recognized by physicians in many tropical and subtropical regions. Indeed, despite a number of recent reports describing rickettsial diseases in new locations and the discovery of new rickettsiae, medical science has focused insufficient efforts in the diagnosis and antimicrobial treatment of rickettsial infections in subtropical and tropical areas. Thus, it is likely that much remains to be discovered (Richard et al., 2006).

In Sudan, Rickettsia species have been recorded in ticks with varying prevalence (Morita et al., 2004; Nakao et al., 2015; Shuaib et al., 2020; Springer et al., 2020). In the present study, the prevalence of Rickettsia species in ticks was 2%, which was lower than that reported by Morita et al. (2004) and Nakao et al. (2015) (up to 30%), Shuaib et al. (2020) (13.7%) and Springer et al. (2020) (5.64%). It is also lower than that reported in countries such as Ethiopia, the Central African Republic, Uganda and Djibouti (Dupont et al., 1994; Mura et al., 2008; Socolovschi et al., 2007). This difference may be attributable to the number of ticks screened in each study; however, tick infection rates can be influenced by many factors, including environmental and vegetation patterns that affect the availability of rickettsia hosts for different tick stages that determine tick population size and infection rates. In addition, ticks can acquire rickettsia infection transstadially, transovarially or by sexual transmission, which also influences infection rate.

In the current study, SFG rickettsiae were detected in H. rufipes, R. sanguineus and H. anatolicum ticks at different percentages, which suggests that there are ample opportunities for humans to acquire these infections. Furthermore, H. rufipes tick, which demonstrated an infection rate (25%) in this study, may feed on humans as an alternative host, as previously reported by Andersson et al. (2018). The number of ticks examined in this study is not sufficient to determine the prevalence of SFG rickettsia in ixodid ticks in Sudan. However, this study demonstrates that some SFG rickettsia species are detected in examined adult ticks in Sudan.

The SFG rickettsia detected in ticks in previous studies in Sudan were the well‐known R. aeschlimannii and R. africae, which were detected in Hyalomma spp. and Amblyomma spp., respectively (Morita et al., 2004), and Rickettsia conorii israelensis and R. massiliae, which were detected in Rhipicephalus camicasi and Rhipicephalus afranicus, respectively (Morita et al., 2004; Springer et al., 2020).

The present study detected R. aeschlimannii, which is recognized as a zoonotic Rickettsia species that causes spotted fever in humans. This finding adds to the information reported by Morita et al. (2004), Shuaib et al. (2020) and Springer et al. (2020) who had already demonstrated the existence of R. aeschlimannii in Hyalomma ticks in Sudan. R. aeschlimanni has also been isolated from Hyalomma ticks in Morocco, Zimbabwe, Egypt and, recently, Kenya (Abdel‐Shafy et al., 2012; Beati et al., 1997; Koka et al., 2017), indicating that Hyalomma ticks are the preferred invertebrate hosts for this rickettsia in a widely dispersed area.

In the current study, gltA, ompA‐based molecular detection enabled the recognition of the existence of R. massiliae in R. sanguineus ticks collected from sheep in Omdurman (Libya – Alssalam Market). This is in line with the findings of Springer et al. (2020), who detected R. massiliae from a single R. afranicus specimen in Sudan, and Chisu et al. (2018), who detected this organism in R. sanguineus ticks in Sardinia Island, Italy.

R. rhipicephali has been detected in several other tick species and regions of the world, including one record of Rhipicephalus annulatus in cattle in the Central African Republic (Dupont et al., 1994). R. rhipicephali has never been associated with human infections, whereas laboratory experiments have shown that Rickettsia spp. are moderately pathogenic to guinea pigs (Burgdorfer et al., 1978; Labruna et al., 2007). Interestingly, it has been suggested that ticks infected with R. rhipicephali may be protected from subsequent infections with pathogenic rickettsiae (Padgett et al., 2016).

The nucleotide sequence of the amplified gltA gene fragment of H. anatolicum was similar to that of R. raoultii (99.48% identity). R. raoultii is a member of the SFG rickettsiae and has been implicated in human cases (Mediannikov et al., 2008). To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report demonstrating molecular proof of R. raoultii in H. anatolicum ticks collected from cattle, as well as the first identification of R. massiliae in R. sanguineus ticks collected from sheep in Sudan. The present study also represents the first report of R. rhipicephali and R. raoultii in Sudanese ticks. Considering the large number of ticks in Sudan (≥70 species) and the variety of wild and domestic animals in the country, it is not surprising to detect more species of SFG rickettsia in Sudan.

CONCLUSION

Finally, the number of ticks examined in this study is not sufficient to determine the prevalence of SFG rickettsia in ixodid ticks in Sudan. However, this study demonstrates that some SFG rickettsia species are detected in examined adult ticks in Sudan. It is important to keep in mind the need for careful consideration of rickettsial infections in individuals with a fever of unknown origin.
