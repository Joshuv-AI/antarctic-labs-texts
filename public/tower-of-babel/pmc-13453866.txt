The 90-day blind spot: A surgical imperative after Kuehn and colleagues
Authors: Zain Khalpey
Published: 2026

The 90-day postoperative risk window and role of wearable defibrillators.

Central Message

Surgeons must own the 90-day postoperative recovery arc; the WCD bridges this vulnerable window while structured registries generate the evidence to guide care.

Every cardiac surgeon knows the feeling. A patient with a low ejection fraction undergoes a technically perfect operation. They are discharged home, but the question lingers: will the ventricle recover before a fatal arrhythmia strikes? For years, we have observed this 90-day window of vulnerability, a period during which guidelines recommend waiting before implanting a permanent defibrillator, hoping for myocardial recovery but accepting a degree of unquantified risk.

The 2022 study by Kuehn and colleagues1 in the European Journal of Cardio-Thoracic Surgery offered the most extensive dataset yet on the use of the wearable cardioverter defibrillator (WCD) in a strictly cardiac surgery population. Their retrospective analysis of 1168 patients from 10 German centers provided a compelling, if not definitive, look into this critical period. Although we must acknowledge the inherent limitations of a nonrandomized registry, the data demand our attention. The study reported that 9.1% of patients experienced ventricular tachyarrhythmias, with 93.2% of these events occurring within the first 3 months. A WCD delivered a successful, life-saving shock in 1.5% of all patients, with a low rate of inappropriate shocks (0.7%) and excellent patient compliance (23.4 h/d). Importantly, the observed wear time compliance aligns with the nationwide German SCD-PROTECT study, which demonstrated a median compliance of 23.5 h/d across 19,598 patients.2

These figures, although provocative, must be interpreted with scientific discipline. The study's retrospective design makes it impossible to definitively separate the effect of the device from the natural history of the patients selected to receive it. Yet, the clinical reality presented is powerful. The WCD provided a safety net that allowed clinicians to do what guidelines recommend: wait. This period of protected observation led to a remarkable outcome. More than one half (50.6%) of patients who did not have a previous implantable cardioverter-defibrillator indication saw their left ventricular ejection fraction improve beyond the implantation threshold, thus avoiding the implantation of a permanent device.1 However, the first 90 postoperative days should not be viewed as a passive waiting period. Surgical correction may initiate reverse remodeling, but the trajectory of recovery is increasingly shaped by early optimization of guideline-directed heart failure therapy. Ensuring this medical management is therefore an essential part of postoperative surgical care. Although it is plausible that this reflects the natural history of myocardial recovery, the WCD served as the essential bridge that made this waiting period safe.

Furthermore, the enthusiastic adoption of this technology must be contextualized by the results of the VEST (Vest Prevention of Early Sudden Death Trial), the only large-scale randomized study of the WCD.3 Although the intention-to-treat analysis of VEST was neutral, a per-protocol analysis demonstrated a significant reduction in total (P < .001) and arrhythmic (P = .001) mortality, highlighting that WCD wear time and duration was the decisive factor.4

Importantly, coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) at the index hospitalization was exceedingly rare in VEST; less than 1% of patients in the device group were postoperative coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG). The baseline characteristics published by Olgin and colleagues4 do not list CABG as a substantive subgroup, confirming that postoperative cardiac surgery patients were effectively not represented in the VEST study. This context underscores why the results from the strictly surgical population in the study by Kuehn and colleagues1 fill a critical evidence gap.

The value of the WCD extends beyond just delivering a shock. It is a powerful data-gathering tool. Every patient wearing the device contributes to a massive, continuous electrocardiogram recording library. The question is not whether we should collect these data—we already are, via the LifeVest Network—but how we can learn from them. Foundational work shows that machine learning can identify subtle electrocardiographic features that track recovery and predict adverse events.5, 6, 7 Applying these techniques to the data stream from our own postoperative patients is the logical next step, moving us from a reactive posture to a predictive one.

Initial commentary on the study by Kuehn and colleagues rightly highlighted its importance. The conversation, however, must now evolve from observation to action. The primary conclusion is not simply that a problem exists, but that we, as surgeons, are uniquely positioned to solve it. We can no longer delegate this 90-day risk to others. It is our responsibility to own the entirety of the recovery arc and identify patients who will benefit from a WCD.

Therefore, the ultimate message from this study is a call for leadership focused on evidence generation that reflects real surgical practice. At present, the United States lacks meaningful data on patients after CABG, during the vulnerable early recovery phase. Data are now emerging in Europe, and particularly in Germany, through studies such as that of Kuehn and colleagues. Together with the ongoing prospective registry of patients with isolated CABG (NCT06570902), which already includes more than one half of all cardiac surgery clinics in Germany, Europe is rapidly building a high-resolution, real-world dataset describing ventricular recovery patterns, and the practical role of the WCD after surgery.

This momentum toward high-quality, prospective, real-world evidence underscores a clear need for comparable US initiatives. Fortunately, the infrastructure for such an effort is emerging. The American Association for Thoracic Surgery Quality Gateway represents a modern registry that uses machine learning algorithms with real-time risk adjustment to improve patient care.8,9 Currently used in a growing number of major US and Canadian centers, the American Association for Thoracic Surgery Quality Gateway captures real-time cardiac surgery data and provides comprehensive risk modeling. Only by leveraging and expanding such structured data efforts can the United States generate the same level of insight and ensure that patients after CABG receive care informed by robust, population-specific evidence. Kuehn and colleagues have defined the problem; the surgical community now has the framework to generate the answer.
