Self-reflection and empathy in undergraduate nursing students: a network analysis
Authors: Weiwei Wu, Caimin Lian, Yaling Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimTo explore the nuanced structural relationship between insightful self-reflection and empathy using network analysis among undergraduate nursing students.BackgroundEmpathy, which is an essential component of humanistic nursing care, should be fostered in nursing students. Self-reflection is considered a precondition for the development of empathy. However, the fine-grained interconnections between the different dimensions of self-reflection and empathy remain unclear.DesignA cross-sectional online survey.MethodsConvenience sampling was used to recruit participants from four nursing schools from January to February 2024. Participants completed the Chinese version of the Jefferson Scale of Empathy-Nursing Student (CJSE-NS) and the Self-Reflection and Insight Scale (SRIS). Network analysis was used to examine the structural relationships between self-reflection and empathy.ResultsA total of 258 valid questionnaires were received. The node perspective taking had the highest centralities and predictability, emerging as the most central node of the network. Exhibiting the highest bridge strength, the node insight served as the bridge node connecting self-reflection and empathy. The strongest positive correlation in the cross-community was between the nodes need for self-reflection and walking in their shoes (weight = 0.142). Network stability and accuracy were confirmed by bootstrapping methods, indicating the robustness of the findings.ConclusionsThis study highlights the intricate connections between self-reflection and empathy among nursing students. Salient central and bridge nodes may inform intervention targets; prospective or experimental studies are needed to test whether targeting these nodes improves empathy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08363-5.Keywords: Self-reflection, Empathy, Network analysis, Nursing students, Bridge networks

Introduction

As a foundational skill in humanistic nursing care and a component of person-centred care, empathy is directly related to the quality of nursing care [1, 2]. Empathy-based nursing can improve patient satisfaction and nurse–patient relationships, ultimately resulting in efficient treatment and better clinical outcomes for patients [3–5]. Additionally, previous studies have found that empathy not only prevents nurse burnout but also influences nursing students’ work engagement, emotional intelligence, sense of value, and professional identity [6–9]. However, a decline in empathy competency levels has been observed among nursing students during their academic training [10, 11]. Empathy is a teachable competence that plays a particularly crucial role in preparing nursing students for their transition into clinical practice and should be prioritized and fostered [12, 13]. Formulating training programs to enhance empathy among nursing students necessitates identifying the potential factors and underlying mechanisms involved in the development of empathy.

Background

Empathy refers to “an ability to sense the client’s private world as if it were your own, but without ever losing the ‘as if’ quality” [14]. Evidence suggests that empathy is a complex psychological construct composed of two main dimensions: affective and cognitive [15]. Affective empathy allows us to experience and share in others’ feelings, while cognitive empathy is the ability to identify and understand the perspectives and suffering of others. Empathy ability enables nurses to accurately identify and respond to patients’ needs with appropriate nursing care based on an understanding of their physical, mental, and emotional suffering [16]. Previous studies have determined that nursing students’ empathy is influenced by various personal characteristics, such as age, sex, and an individual’s work, training, and care experience [10, 12, 17, 18]. Further, given the cognitive nature of empathy, self-conscious traits may influence the development of empathy in nursing students [19]. According to the dual model of empathy, cognitive empathy relies on individuals using their awareness and effort to establish personal theories or assumptions about others’ psychological states [15]. Recognizing and distinguishing their own attitudes and emotions can help nursing students to better understand patients’ feelings. Studies investigating the interplay between emotional and cognitive ability in nurses [20] found that an adequate level of self-awareness promoted high levels of empathy and clinical decision-making in nurses [7, 21]. Lee et al. reported a similar correlation between self-consciousness and empathy among Korean nursing students, suggesting that this relationship also exists in Asian cultures [22]. Such findings point to a potential association between perception and evaluation of the self (i.e., self-reflection) and empathy development in nursing students.

Self-reflection is a form of self-focused attention that refers to the inspection, evaluation, and understanding of one’s thoughts, feelings, and behaviours. Grant et al. conceptualized self-reflection as a three-dimensional model, comprising the need for self-reflection, the engagement in self-reflection and insight [23]. According to theories of metacognition and self-regulation, self-reflection functions as a regulatory process that enables individuals to monitor and evaluate their efficacy, thoughts, and actions, thereby enhancing performance and goal attainment [24]. Within nursing, empirical studies substantiate that increased self-reflection is correlated with superior clinical performance, advanced critical thinking, and, consequently, improved care quality and patient safety [25, 26]. A growing body of literature posits a close, bidirectional association between self-reflection and empathy. On the one hand, self-reflection was found to serve as a prerequisite for the development of empathy [27]. Self-reflection provides the precise ability to recognize one’s own emotions and differentiate them from others’, which is pivotal for developing and effectively applying empathy. This is supported by findings that enhanced self-awareness in nursing students significantly increases their ability to deliver empathy-based patient-centred care [28]. The efficacy of reflective educational interventions in bolstering empathy skills has been consistently demonstrated across diverse contexts, including in studies from China and Iran [29, 30]. A systematic review of 23 studies further corroborates that guided reflection is among the most effective strategies for empathy education [31]. On the other hand, empathy reciprocally strengthens self-reflection and related psychological constructs. Empathy ability was found to be effective in enhancing emotional resilience and self-efficacy, contributing to personal development and positive psychological adjustment [32]. Moreover, educational interventions targeting empathy have been found to promote deeper self-awareness among nursing students, indicating the potential impact of empathy on self-reflection in nurse education [33].

Although studies have investigated the relationship between self-reflection and empathy in broader medical education contexts, including among dental and medical students [34], focused research into this relationship within the specific population of nursing students remains scarce. Nursing students face unique challenges in educational settings, such as empathy fatigue and the self-awareness needed for their professional development [19, 35], warranting a more comprehensive investigation of this group. Additionally, most studies focus on the variables of self-reflection and empathy at the construct level (i.e., via sum scores) based on traditional statistical methods, which ignores the complex interactions among components and within their networks. The potentially nonlinear relationship between self-reflection and empathy has not been fully elucidated. As self-reflection and empathy are complex variables consisting of different dimensions, exploring the interconnections between the components of these two variables can help to develop targeted interventions in nursing education. It is necessary to employ more effective and comprehensive methods to deeply investigate the link between insightful self-reflection and empathy.

Network analysis—an observation-based approach suitable for fine-grained analyses of various complex variables—has been more widely applied in recent years. Different from traditional correlation methods, network analysis assesses the strength and nature of associations between variables, giving the centrality and predictability indices of each potential variable to identify and visualize its importance and role in the network [36]. More importantly, network models allow the identification of central nodes in the network and influential nodes that may bridge potential communities. These central and bridge nodes play a pivotal role in activating or sustaining a network and can be viewed as important targets for developing interventions [37]. Precise interventions for bridge nodes can improve intervention effects through the connections between bridge networks [38]. Network analysis can therefore facilitate an in-depth exploration of the interactions between self-reflection and empathy. This could offer new perspectives for understanding the dynamics of empathy development and optimizing interventions to enhance nursing students’ empathy.

Previous studies have used network analysis to examine the interplay of the components of moral distress [39], cognitive emotion regulation, and professional identity [40] among nursing students. To date, however, no study has explored the interrelationship between the components of self-reflection and empathy in nursing education using network analysis. To address this gap, we constructed a network that combines the components of self-reflection and empathy for Chinese nursing students and explored the influential nodes in the network. This can help nursing educators develop targeted interventions for empathy-based, patient-centred care competence among nursing students.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study employed network analysis to conduct a cross-sectional survey in order to examine the network construction between self-reflection, insight, and empathy among nursing students.

Participants and recruitment

Participant recruitment was conducted via convenience sampling from four nursing schools in Fujian Province, China, from January to February 2024. These schools belong to public universities that offer bachelor’s degrees in nursing in regular four-year undergraduate programs. The inclusion criteria of the participants were third-year undergraduate nursing students as well as those who voluntarily participated. The decision to focus on third-year students was based on two key considerations. First, having completed core coursework, third-year nursing students possessed a more mature understanding of the profession than junior students. Second, they had not yet entered clinical internships, thus avoiding exposure to the highly variable and confounding influences of the clinical setting (e.g., ward culture, instructor style, and high workload), which are difficult to evaluate and control for. The exclusion criteria were nursing students who were unable to correctly complete the questionnaire and those who were on leave during the survey period. Regarding the sample size for network analysis, we used the Powerly package in R developed by Constantin et al. [41]. Specifically, for a network model consisting of 9 nodes with a sensitivity of 0.6, a probability of 0.8, and a density of 0.4, a sample size of 214 was recommended. This study significantly exceeded the recommended minimum number.

Instruments

Sociodemographic variables

We developed a self-administered questionnaire based on the literature [10, 12, 17, 21] to assess sociodemographic characteristics, including sex, age, ethnicity, place of residence, family type, leadership experience, volunteer experience, and the number of clinical nursing observations during school.

Chinese version of the Jefferson scale of Empathy-Nursing student (CJSE-NS)

The Chinese version of the Jefferson Scale of Empathy-Nursing Student (CJSE-NS) was used to evaluate empathy among nursing students [42]. Hojat et al. developed the original Jefferson Scale of Empathy [43]; through rigorous cross-cultural adaptation, Qiu et al. adapted it for the Chinese context in 2009. The scale comprises 20 items across three dimensions: perspective taking (10 items), walking in their shoes (3 items), and compassionate care (8 items). It uses seven-point Likert scales, and the total score ranges from 20 to 140. The first two dimensions are associated with cognitive empathy, while the third is related to affective empathy [44]. The CJSE-NS exhibits strong reliability and validity and has been widely used in studies involving Chinese nursing students. The Cronbach’s alpha for the present study was 0.850, indicating acceptable reliability.

The self-reflection and insight scale (SRIS)

This study used the Self-Reflection and Insight Scale (SRIS) to evaluate the self-reflection and insight of nursing students. This scale was developed by Grant et al. and has been translated and culturally adapted for China [23, 45]. It consists of 20 self-reported items divided across three subscales, including insight (eight items), need for reflection (six items), and engagement in reflection (six items). Each item is assessed using a six-point Likert scale, with scores ranging from 20 to 120. Higher scores suggest more inspection, evaluation, and perception of self-thoughts, beliefs, emotions, and behaviours. The SRIS has been previously used in studies of Chinese nursing students with satisfactory reliability [46]. In our analysis, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.898, indicating adequate reliability.

Ethical considerations and data collection

The study employed an online questionnaire survey for data collection. First, four nursing schools were selected using convenience sampling. The researchers contacted the nursing student manager of each nursing school, distributed the electronic questionnaire, and conducted training sessions on ethics and instructions for completing it. Next, the questionnaire link was distributed to the participants along with detailed explanations of the purpose and method. After obtaining informed consent, we instructed the nursing students to complete and submit the anonymized questionnaire. Standardized submission procedures for online completion time (more than three minutes) and submission standards (completing all the items) were implemented to control data quality. This study was approved by the Medical Research Ethics Committee of Fujian Medical University (no. 2024–180) and adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Data analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS 25.0 and R version 4.4.3. The Shapiro–Wilk test was employed to assess the normality of continuous variables. Non-normally distributed data were transformed through Z-score normalization. The Mann–Whitney U test and Kruskal–Wallis test were used to analyse differences in self-reflection and empathy based on sociodemographic characteristics. The significant characteristic variables in the rank-sum test were considered as covariates and incorporated into the network analysis. Considering the influence of covariates, the network was constructed using a mixed graphical model. Specifically, the mgm and qgraph packages were used to build and visualize weighted networks of nursing students’ self-reflection and empathy. In the networks, each “node” represents a domain of self-reflection or empathy, with each “edge” representing the partial correlation coefficient between node pairs. The thicker the edge, the stronger the correlation between the two nodes. The centrality index of the model—including strength, closeness, betweenness, and expected influence—was employed to measure the importance of each node. A higher value reflects higher node centrality. The predictability of each node was estimated to represent the extent to which the variance of a node can be explained by its neighbouring nodes.

The bootnet package was used to estimate the accuracy of edge weights and the robustness of the centrality indices. First, non-parametric bootstrapping (1500 bootstrapped samples) was performed to estimate the differences in centrality measures, bridge measures, and network edges. Next, the node-dropping sub-setting bootstrap method and the correlation stability coefficient (CS coefficient) were used to examine the stability of centrality, with ≥ 0.5 representing good stability.

Additionally, the networktools package was used to evaluate and calculate the bridge expected influence (BEI) values and bridge strength within the three communities: self-reflection, empathy, and covariates. Bridge strength is the sum of the direct connections between the nodes and all communities, reflecting the ability of the nodes to influence other communities. BEI represents the total of the edge weights of the edges from nodes to other nodes.

Results

Participant characteristics

Of the 478 invited potential participants, 193 did not respond, and one was excluded because of absence due to illness; thus, a total of 284 nursing students participated in the study. After excluding 26 questionnaires with identical responses on all items, 258 valid questionnaires remained, for an effective response rate of 90.85%. Supplementary Material Fig S1 show the flowchart of participants recruitment. As shown in Table 1, the mean age of participants was 21.7 years (standard deviation = 0.79), with ages ranging from 20 to 24. The majority were female (n = 224, 87.1%), of Han ethnicity (n = 234, 90.7%), and from non-one-child families (n = 218, 74.5%). More than half of the participants (58.1%) were from rural areas, and 57.0% had no experience as a student leader. Regarding clinical practice, the majority (79.1%) had more than five clinical observations, and 96.1% had volunteer experience. Supplementary Material Table S1 present the difference in demographic characteristics of the participants on self-reflection and empathy. The rank-sum tests revealed that self-reflection and empathy differed significantly between ethnicity (p < 0.05), student leader experience (p < 0.05), and volunteer experience (p < 0.05).

Network structure

We constructed a combined partial correlation network to evaluate the relationship between self-reflection and empathy. To control for the effect of the covariates on the relationships between nodes, we included the significant demographic variables, including volunteer experience, ethnicity, and leader experience, in the network. Network visualization (Fig. 1) revealed that the nodes within each community were generally positively correlated. Out of a possible 36 edges, 17 within the network were non-zero edges. Among them, seven (41.2%) were within communities and 10 (58.8%) were across communities. In the cross-community edges, node E—perspective taking was observed to be associated with all nodes of the self-reflection community. The strongest positive correlation was observed between node SRN—need for self-reflection and E3—walking in their shoes (weight = 0.142). Within the community edges, the strongest correlation was observed between SRN—need for self-reflection and SRE—engagement in self-reflection (weight = 0.576) in the self-reflection community and between E1—perspective taking and E2—compassionate care (weight = 0.648) in the empathy community. Discussion

To our knowledge, this study is the first to use network analysis to examine how the elements of self-reflection relate to empathy among Chinese nursing students. The results showed that the node perspective taking had the highest centralities and predictability, emerging as a core variable for understanding the network of self-reflection and empathy. Furthermore, the nodes insight and need for self-reflection exhibited the highest bridge strength, highlighting their roles in facilitating cross-community influences within the network. Our findings provide potentially valuable information for developing targeted interventions based on these components to address self-reflection and facilitate empathy in nursing education.

The strongest connection in the empathy network was between the nodes perspective taking and compassionate care. indicating the interactive relationship between cognitive and affective empathy. This aligns with a previous study that found the connection between affective and cognitive empathy might affect levels of empathy processing [47]. Our findings also echo the dual route model of empathy and evidence from neural and behavioural studies, highlighting the multifaceted psychological construction and cognitive-emotional processes of empathy [15]. Specifically, affective empathy represents an automatic response and the primitive beginning of empathy. Cognitive empathy establishes a higher route in the empathy model that may modulate the lower route of empathy. In this study, nursing students’ ability to mentalize patients’ experiences and situations stems from their concern for patients’ feelings. Conversely, when nurses fully understand patients’ mindsets, they are likely better equipped to know patients’ feelings. In addition, Heggestad et al. found that the affective part of empathic capacity was the most salient among nursing students when encountering patients’ situations [48]. However, affective components of empathy may cause nurses to excessively worry about patients, thus increasing anxiety and exhaustion [49, 50]. It is important, then, to optimize the balance between affective and cognitive empathy when considering empathy-related intervention strategies. Nursing educators should guide students on dealing with these challenging emotions and properly using affective empathetic skills.

More importantly, across the network, the node perspective taking had the highest centrality and predictability, suggesting it played an important role in the self-reflection and empathy network. This aligns with a Finnish study that reported a significant association between perspective taking and self-reflection among medical students [34]. It is also consistent with studies in Spain and Australia showing that nurses’ cognitive empathy is positively associated with the ability to be aware of themselves and engage in professional nursing [49, 51]. Perspective taking relies on the involvement of cognitive effort and is believed to be related to the habitual use of adaptive emotion regulation strategies [20]. Nursing students with high perspective-taking ability may have greater resilience against the adverse effects of affective empathy, enabling them to maintain a buffering distance from the emotional experiences of patients, thereby remaining compassionate and supportive without becoming emotionally overwhelmed [47, 49]. In addition, greater cognitive empathy may enable nursing students to maintain cognitive clarity during high-stress situations, facilitating analytical reasoning and critical thinking, which were significantly related to self-reflection [20, 25]. Our findings provide clear and actionable direction for designing targeted interventions in the context of nursing education. Nurse educators should incorporate immersive and experiential learning strategies into the education practice. Providing structured scenarios and high-fidelity-based simulation training may allow students to be immersed and cognitively engaged with care recipients, enabling them to better relate to patients’ feelings and experiences, thereby fostering professional behaviours and self-awareness [12, 28, 52]– [53].

Our findings revealed that the node insight was the most influential node bridging self-reflection and empathy, which aligns with previous studies showing that people with high insight exhibit a broader range of functioning in terms of self-evaluation and affect [54–56]. Studies in the Middle East and Europe also suggested that self-concept is positively associated with professional performance and cognitive processes such as critical thinking in nursing practice [21, 57]. Central to the self-regulatory process, insight relies on a combination of intact self-reflection and cognitive perspective-taking capacities, enabling us to successfully regulate our performance oriented toward achieving specific goals [58]. Therefore, nursing students who possess high insight into their own psychological makeup through self-reflection may have a better ability to comprehensively inspect the state of patients. This cognitive ability enables them to intellectually understand patients’ feelings and needs, thereby fostering empathetic ability and improving nurse–patient relationships in nursing practice [59]. Furthermore, nursing students with a greater capacity for empathy may be better able to distinguish their personal feelings and viewpoints from those of others. This could contribute to a better comprehension of others’ thoughts about themselves and enhance the meta-cognitive capacity to reflect upon one’s own self from the perspectives of others [20]. In recent years, several reflection-based empathetic educational programs have been implemented in nursing education to enhance patient care behaviours and professional development among nursing students [12]. Findings of the current study highlighted the importance of practicing insight in empathy education and the personal growth of students. Nursing educators should incorporate elements of fostering insight, such as mindful training, narrative writing, and guided reflection/debriefing in simulation [29, 31, 49], into their teaching strategies to help students enhance their ability to be aware of and analyse themselves, thereby contributing to personal growth and better empathic ability.

Additionally, the node need for self-reflection was identified as a bridge node with the highest edge weight connected to the node walking in their shoes in the cross-community. This finding is consistent with prior research, which underscored intrinsic motivation for ongoing self-reflection as a key facilitator of effective learning [60]. According to self-determination theory, an increased need for self-reflection helps nursing students better recognize the meaning of self-reflection and supports their persistent engagement in reflective learning practices [61]. This clarified sense of self enhances self-monitoring and self-other differentiation, which in turn deepens the understanding of patients’ inner experiences, thus fostering empathy [62]. Furthermore, educators’ influence has been reported as key in prompting students to initiate self-reflection, while the development of a professional identity serves as a crucial motivator for sustaining this practice throughout the learning journey [60]. Therefore, nursing educators should focus on encouraging students to participate in self-reflection and providing them positive feedback. Linking reflection to the development of a professional identity may help students to make sense of their learning experience and build their confidence in reflective practice. However, it is noteworthy that overly self-focused engagement in reflection can indicate excessive rumination, which could impede the development of insight and the achievement of personal goals [63]. Therefore, nursing educators should guide students toward reflection characterized by depth and purpose, as opposed to an excess of self-focus.

Limitations

Several limitations warrant consideration when interpreting our findings. First, the cross-sectional nature of the data did not allow for the exploration of the temporal dynamics of the network and the causal relationships between self-reflection and empathy. Second, the self-reported survey employed online data collection, potentially introducing self-selection and social desirability bias, particularly for items assessing empathy. Third, because the constructed network examined between-community relationships at the group level, the network structure might not be identical at the item level. Fourth, we convenience sampled from four nursing schools in a single Chinese province, which may have led to sampling bias, restricting the generalizability of the findings to other Chinese nursing schools or international contexts. Additionally, the participants’ focus on third-year undergraduate nursing students may affect the generalizability of findings to students in other years or to nurse interns. Finally, although we included key sociodemographic covariates in the network analysis, this might not have comprehensively captured all potential confounders, such as academic stress, personality traits, and mental health status. Future research should consider using experimental and longitudinal designs to verify the direction of edges and strengthen the reliability of the results. Probability sampling with larger sample sizes and broader representation is needed to enhance external validity. Additionally, the use of more objective behavioural measures of empathy and the assessment of a broader range of potential confounders would improve the robustness of future findings.

Conclusion

This study provides novel insights into the relationship between self-reflection and empathy among nursing students. Perspective taking was the strongest node, highlighting its central role in the network. Insight and need for self-reflection were the critical bridge nodes connecting self-reflection and empathy. These findings offer new insights into nursing education and patient-centred care. From a network perspective, nursing educators should help cultivate students’ perspective-taking ability and guide them to balance affective-cognitive empathic skills in nursing practice. Based on the identified bridge nodes, educators should use strategies that bolster insight skills and encourage suitable engagement in self-reflection, which can strengthen students’ ability to deliver empathic care and foster personal growth.
