Long-term outcomes and treatment response in early-stage invasive lobular carcinoma: insights from a nationwide population-based study☆
Authors: G Nader-Marta, L Ameye, D Martins-Branco, R Salgado, N Van Damme, J Verbeeck, P Aftimos, L Buisseret, M Paesmans, C Molinelli, EL Mayer, M Lambertini, K Van Baelen, C Desmedt, M Piccart, E de Azambuja
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInvasive lobular carcinoma (ILC), the second most common breast cancer subtype, differs from invasive breast carcinoma of no special type (BC-NST) in biology, presentation, and treatment response. Evidence on its prognostic impact remains inconsistent.Patients and methodsOur nationwide, population-based study of stage I-III breast cancer diagnosed in Belgium (2008-2014) compared characteristics, treatment patterns, and overall survival (OS) between ILC and BC-NST.ResultsOf 51 815 eligible patients, 7593 (14.6%) had ILC. Compared with patients with BC-NST, patients with ILC were older (median 62 versus 59 years), more often T3-T4 (16.5% versus 7.4%) or N3 (5.4% versus 2.9%), and less frequently grade 3 (19.3% versus 40.7%; P < 0.0001). ILC was associated with worse unadjusted OS [hazard ratio (HR) 1.13, 95% confidence interval (CI) 1.08-1.18], with 15-year OS of 62.1% (versus 65.9% for BC-NST), but no difference after adjustment [adjusted HR (aHR) 0.97, 95% CI 0.92-1.02]. In subgroup analyses, inferior OS persisted in patients with N3 disease (aHR 1.29, 95% CI 1.07-1.55) and in those treated with neoadjuvant therapy (aHR 1.27, 95% CI 1.06-1.52). Flexible parametric models demonstrated a time-dependent effect, with lower early mortality but higher late mortality in ILC. Patients with ILC had lower odds of response to neoadjuvant therapy overall (odds ratio 0.34, 95% CI 0.27-0.43), consistent across hormone receptor-positive/human epidermal growth factor receptor 2-negative and triple-negative subtypes.ConclusionsThe apparent survival disadvantage of ILC is largely explained by clinicopathologic features, with no independent association with OS. However, ILC demonstrates a time-dependent survival pattern, with lower early but higher late mortality, and worse outcomes in patients with extensive nodal involvement.Key words: breast neoplasms, ductal breast carcinoma, lobular carcinoma, neoadjuvant therapy, prognosis

Introduction

Invasive lobular carcinoma (ILC), which represents approximately 10%-15% of all breast cancer cases, is the second most prevalent histological subtype after invasive breast carcinoma of no special type (BC-NST, previously referred to as invasive ductal carcinoma).1 ILC is distinguished from BC-NST at the molecular, pathological, clinical, and radiological levels, resulting in unique patterns of tumor growth, metastatic spread, and imaging detectability.1 Loss of E-cadherin function is a defining hallmark, driving the classic discohesive, single-file infiltration that underlies many of the distinct clinical and radiological features of ILC.2 These biological and morphological differences have long raised questions regarding whether ILC should be considered, and treated, as a distinct disease entity.

Despite these differences, histological subtype is not routinely incorporated into current treatment selection algorithms or clinical trial designs.3 The prognostic implications of lobular histology remain debated, with prior studies reporting heterogeneous results, ranging from worse long-term survival compared with BC-NST to no meaningful differences after adjustment for stage and other factors.4, 5, 6, 7, 8 Moreover, emerging evidence suggests that recurrence risk in ILC may be time-dependent and modified by baseline risk, challenging the conventional interpretation of survival outcomes across subgroups.9, 10, 11, 12 ILC also appears less responsive to neoadjuvant chemotherapy than BC-NST, particularly in hormone receptor-positive disease.13

However, data on treatment response within less common subtypes, such as human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)-positive and triple-negative ILC, are scarce.14 This reflects the underrepresentation of ILC in clinical trials and the frequent absence of histology-specific reporting or collection of response data.15 This lack of granular, histology-specific evidence is a critical knowledge gap, especially in an era when neoadjuvant approaches are increasingly used to guide surgical decision making and tailor adjuvant therapies based on disease response.3 Without robust, subtype-stratified benchmarks for prognosis and treatment sensitivity, clinicians are left to extrapolate from BC-NST-dominated datasets, which may obscure important histology-driven differences in outcomes.

Population-based datasets with large sample size, long-term follow-up, and representation across clinically relevant subgroups are essential to clarify these uncertainties. To address this gap, we designed a large, contemporary, population-based cohort study to comprehensively compare clinicopathologic characteristics, treatment patterns, long-term survival outcomes, and response to neoadjuvant therapy between ILC and BC-NST.

Patients and methods

Study design and data source

This is a population-based, nationwide retrospective cohort study using data from the Belgian Cancer Registry (BCR). The BCR has covered the entire Belgian population since 2004 and systematically collects information on all newly diagnosed cancers.16 Clinical data are reported by the cancer care programs, while histopathological data are provided by pathology laboratories, including both encoded variables and the full-text pathology reports. These complementary data sources are integrated, and in cases of discrepancies between clinical and pathological information, dedicated quality checks are performed to derive the most accurate classification. All data are subjected to an extensive set of automated and manual validation procedures to ensure accuracy and completeness. Information on patients’ vital status is routinely obtained from the Belgian Crossroads Bank for Social Security, ensuring comprehensive follow-up for survival analyses.17 In addition, BCR data are linked with health insurance claims from the InterMutualistic Agency, which provide detailed information on therapeutic procedures and allow characterization of treatment patterns at the population level.

The study followed the World Medical Association's Code of Ethics (Declaration of Helsinki) for human research and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Institut Jules Bordet (CE3790). The manuscript was reported in accordance with the ESMO Guidance for Reporting Oncology Real-World Evidence (ESMO-GROW) recommendations.18

Study population

Patients diagnosed with stage I-III invasive breast cancer between 1 January 2008 and 31 December 2014, who underwent surgery for their primary tumor were included. The cut-off date for the inclusion time interval was selected to ensure adequate follow-up time for long-term survival analyses. Exclusion criteria comprised the presence of distant metastases at diagnosis, lack of recorded surgery, nonresidence in Belgium at diagnosis, or a history of another (breast or non-breast) invasive cancer. The analysis was restricted to tumors with pure ILC [International Classification of Diseases for Oncology, Third Edition (ICD-O-3) codes 8520 and 8524] or BC-NST (ICD-O-3 codes 8035, 8500, 8501, 8514, 8521, and 8523).19 Cases with mixed ductal and lobular histology, other rare histological subtypes, or histology codes classified ‘unspecified’ were excluded from this analysis. Prespecified treatment patterns of interest included type of surgery (breast-conserving surgery or mastectomy), use of chemotherapy, endocrine therapy (ET), and anti-HER2 therapy within nine months of diagnosis, as well as receipt of adjuvant radiotherapy. For the purpose of this study, neoadjuvant therapy was defined as any systemic treatment administered before surgery, including chemotherapy, ET, or targeted agents. Staging was based on the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) Tumor–Node–Metastasis (TNM) classification (6th edition for 2008-2009; 7th edition for 2010-2014).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) subtype classification

Breast cancer IHC subtypes were defined primarily using a treatment-based surrogate subtype approach applied to the entire cohort. For all patients, a surrogate classification based on systemic therapies initiated within nine months of diagnosis was applied. Patients who initiated ET within this time frame and did not receive anti-HER2 therapy were classified as hormone receptor-positive [estrogen receptor (ER)-positive]/HER2-negative. Those who initiated both ET and anti-HER2 therapy within nine months were classified as ER-positive/HER2-positive, while those who received anti-HER2 therapy without ET during this period were classified as ER-negative/HER2-positive. Patients who received chemotherapy but neither ET nor anti-HER2 therapy within nine months of diagnosis were classified as having triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC). Drugs used for subtype classification are detailed in Supplementary Table S1 (available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.107731). As a sensitivity analysis, a pathology-based classification using ER, progesterone receptor, and HER2 expression status was applied to patients diagnosed in the years 2008 and 2014, the only years for which patient-level receptor data were available.

Statistical considerations

The primary objective of this study was to assess the prognostic value of histological subtype, specifically ILC compared with BC-NST, on overall survival (OS) in patients with early-stage breast cancer. The primary endpoint was OS, defined as the time from breast cancer diagnosis (incidence date) to death from any cause or until the last date known to be alive. Follow-up was complete up to 1 April 2023. All patients were included in the primary analysis. Analyses using the pathology-based classification were prespecified as sensitivity analyses and were restricted to the subset of patients with available biomarker data (incidence years 2008 and 2014). Patients without a date of death were censored at the last available follow-up. In a prespecified secondary analysis restricted to patients who received neoadjuvant systemic therapy, tumor response was classified as pathological complete response (pCR), partial response (PR), stable disease (SD), or progressive disease (PD). Patients were eligible for this analysis if they either (i) had explicit information on pCR, defined as absence of invasive disease in both breast and lymph nodes (ypT0/is and ypN0), or (ii) had complete information on clinical (cT, cN) and pathological (pT, pN) stage. PR was defined as downstaging of the T and/or N category in the absence of any upstaging. SD was defined as no change in T or N stage, and PD as upstaging in T or N after therapy. Patients with incomplete staging information were excluded. For analytic purposes, pCR and PR were considered response, whereas SD and PD were considered nonresponse. For patients receiving neoadjuvant therapy, clinical nodal stage (cN) was used; otherwise, pathological nodal stage (pN) was used. Descriptive statistics summarized patient/tumor characteristics and treatment patterns. Tumor response to neoadjuvant systemic therapy was a prespecified secondary endpoint and was analyzed descriptively and considered exploratory, given the heterogeneity of treatment modalities and the requirement for complete staging data to define response. Comparisons between ILC and BC-NST were performed using chi-square tests for categorical variables and Student’s t-tests or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for continuous variables. OS was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and differences between groups were evaluated with the log-rank test. Univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were applied to assess the association between histological subtype and OS. If the proportional hazards assumption was not satisfied, flexible parametric survival models (Royston–Parmar model) allowing the effect of explanatory variables to vary over time were used. Multivariable models were adjusted for tumor size (T), nodal status (N), age, grade, year of diagnosis, and treatment-based surrogate subtype. Stratified subgroup analyses were used to characterize their modifying effect on outcomes. Logistic regression models were applied to evaluate the association between histology and tumor response to neoadjuvant therapy, with response defined as CR or PR compared with SD or PD. All multivariable and subgroup analyses were conducted using complete-case data; missing values were not imputed, and no missing category was included in the models. Subgroup analyses were prespecified and considered exploratory; no adjustment for multiple comparisons was performed. All statistical tests were two-sided with a significance level of α = 0.05, and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. Analyses were conducted using SAS (version 9.4).

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 51 815 patients diagnosed with stage I-III invasive breast cancer between 2008 and 2014 were included in the study cohort, including 7593 with pure ILC and 44 222 with BC-NST. Further details on the cohort selection process are provided in Supplementary Figure S1 (available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.107731). Median follow-up was 11.6 years overall and was similar between BC-NST (median 11.6 years, interquartile range 9.9-13.4) and ILC (median 11.4 years, interquartile range 9.8-13.2).

Patients with ILC were older at diagnosis than those with BC-NST, with a median age of 62 versus 59 years (Table 1). A higher proportion of patients with ILC were diagnosed at advanced T stage, with 16.5% presenting as T3-T4 compared with 7.4% among patients with BC-NST. Similarly, N3 nodal involvement was more common in ILC (5.4% versus 2.9%). Histologically, ILCs were less likely to be high grade, with only 19.3% classified as grade 3 compared with 40.7% among BC-NST cases. Despite more advanced disease, patients with ILC less often received chemotherapy (33.3% versus 44.8%). Mastectomy was also more frequently performed in patients with ILC than in those with BC-NST (42.7% versus 30.5%). The relative proportion of ILC among all breast cancers appeared to increase over the study period, rising from 14% (956 of 6836) in 2008 to 16% (1232 of 7714) in 2014, whereas the proportion of BC-NST decreased from 86% (5880 of 6836) to 84% (6482 of 7714).

As a sensitivity analysis, we also examined subtype distribution using pathology-based receptor data, available for patients diagnosed in 2008 and 2014 (n = 11 903; 23% of the overall cohort). Across both classification systems, ILC was consistently enriched for the ER-positive/HER2-negative subtype, accounting for over 91% of ILC cases when based on receptor expression, compared with approximately 70% among BC-NST cases (Table 1). Conversely, HER2-positive and TNBC subtypes were substantially less frequent in ILC relative to BC-NST. Using the pathology-based classification as reference, the treatment-based surrogate subtype classification showed strong concordance for ER-positive/HER2-negative tumors, with an absolute difference of 0.9%. In contrast, HER2-positive disease was underestimated by 5.0% and TNBC was overestimated by 6.3% for these two incidence years. When stratified by incidence year, discordance was more pronounced in 2008 (HER2-positive underestimated by 10.2% and TNBC overestimated by 7.2%), while in 2014 the magnitude of misclassification was smaller (HER2-positive underestimated by 5.1% and TNBC overestimated by 5.9%), consistent with greater uptake of HER2 testing and treatment in routine practice (Supplementary Table S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.107731).

OS by histology subtype

In the unadjusted analysis, patients with ILC had significantly worse OS compared with those with BC-NST [hazard ratio (HR) 1.13, 95% CI 1.08–1.18; Figure 1]. The estimated 5-year OS was similar between groups: 89.9% (95% CI 89.3% to 90.6%) for ILC and 89.6% (95% CI 89.3% to 89.9%) for BC-NST, respectively. However, the 10-year OS was lower in patients with ILC: 75.7% (95% CI 74.7% to 76.7%) versus 78.4% (95% CI 78.0% to 78.8%) in BC-NST, respectively, and this gap widened at 15 years, with rates of 62.1% (95% CI 60.15% to 64.2%) versus 65.9% (95% CI 65.1% to 66.7%), respectively (P < 0.0001).

The proportional hazards assumption for histological subtype was not satisfied, indicating a time-dependent effect of histology on survival. Flexible parametric survival models allowing time-varying effects demonstrated that the HR for ILC compared with BC-NST changed over time, with a lower hazard during the early follow-up period and a higher hazard thereafter (HR 0.79 at 1 year, 1.19 at 5 years, and 1.23 at 10 years). Annual hazard rates similarly demonstrated a time-dependent pattern, with the risk of death comparable or lower for ILC during the early follow-up period but higher than for BC-NST after approximately five years, with the largest differences observed between 5 and 10 years after diagnosis (Supplementary Table S3 and Supplementary Figure S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.107731). In multivariable analysis, histological subtype was not independently associated with OS [adjusted HR (aHR) 0.97, 95% CI 0.92-1.02]. Associations between clinicopathologic and treatment-related variables and OS are presented in Supplementary Table S4 (available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.107731).

Subgroup analyses

Subgroup analyses evaluating the association between histological subtype and OS across clinical and pathological subgroups are shown in Figure 2. Across nodal stages, ILC was associated with improved OS in node-negative patients (N0; aHR 0.90, 95% CI 0.83-0.98) and with worse OS in patients with N3 disease (aHR 1.29, 95% CI 1.07-1.55; Figure 3). No independent association was observed in patients with N1 or N2 disease. When stratified by treatment-based surrogate subtype, ILC was associated with higher mortality in univariate analyses across all subtypes. After adjustment, no significant differences in OS were observed between ILC and BC-NST in ER-positive/HER2-negative, ER-positive/HER2-positive, or TNBC. In contrast, in ER-negative/HER2-positive disease, ILC remained independently associated with worse OS (aHR 1.68, 95% CI 1.08-2.64). According to neoadjuvant treatment status, ILC was associated with worse OS in patients who received neoadjuvant therapy (aHR 1.27, 95% CI 1.06-1.52). In a sensitivity analysis excluding patients who received neoadjuvant therapy, results were consistent with the primary multivariable and subgroup analyses (Supplementary Table S5, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.107731).

In sensitivity analyses based on pathology-defined subtypes (n = 11 903; 23% of the cohort), histological subtype was not associated with OS in any subtype (Supplementary Table S6, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.107731).

Tumor response to neoadjuvant therapy

Among the 51 815 patients included in the study cohort, 4991 (9.6%) received neoadjuvant systemic therapy and were considered for the tumor response analysis. Of these, 3210 patients had sufficient data to determine treatment response, including 2893 (64%) of BC-NST cases and 317 (68%) of ILC cases (Supplementary Table S7, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.107731).

When analyzing ILC and BC-NST cases combined, response patterns varied according to baseline disease characteristics and treatment modality, with higher clinical T and N stages generally associated with lower rates of CR and higher response rates observed among patients receiving chemotherapy and anti-HER2 therapy (Supplementary Tables S8 and S9, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.107731). Patients with HER2-positive tumors had the highest likelihood of achieving a CR or PR (70.2% for ER-positive/HER2-positive and 80.3% for ER-negative/HER2-positive), followed by those with TNBC (66.9%), while responses were lowest in the ER-positive/HER2-negative subgroup (42.7%; Supplementary Table S10, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.107731). When examining response categories separately, rates of CR were consistently lower than PR across subgroups, with variation according to baseline stage and treatment modality (Supplementary Tables S8 and S9, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.107731).

In logistic regression analyses, histological subtype was evaluated in relation to tumor response to neoadjuvant therapy. Patients with ILC had significantly lower odds of achieving a response (CR or PR) compared with those with BC-NST [odds ratio (OR) 0.34, 95% CI 0.27-0.43]. When stratifying by subtype, ILC was consistently associated with lower odds of treatment response compared with BC-NST. ILC was associated with reduced response rates compared with BC-NST in both ER-positive/HER2-negative (OR 0.48, 95% CI 0.35-0.66) and TNBC subgroups (OR 0.32, 95% CI 0.19-0.54). In contrast, no statistically significant differences in response rates between histologies were observed among HER2-positive subtypes, regardless of the ER status. Results of logistic regression analyses evaluating the association between histological subtype and treatment response, stratified by IHC subtype, are presented in Table 2. In sensitivity analyses based on pathology-defined subtypes, ILC remained significantly associated with reduced odds of response in the ER-positive/HER2-negative subgroup (OR 0.37, 95% CI 0.21-0.65), while no significant differences were observed in HER2-positive or TNBC subtypes (Supplementary Table S11, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.107731).

Discussion

In this large, nationwide, population-based retrospective cohort study using data from the BCR, we compared the characteristics, prognostic relevance, and treatment response patterns associated with ILC and BC-NST across breast cancer subtypes. The cohort included over 51 000 patients with stage I-III disease, and patients with ILC were characterized by older age at diagnosis, larger tumor size, higher nodal burden, and lower histological grade relative to those with BC-NST. In univariate analysis, ILC was associated with worse long-term OS; however, outcomes were largely comparable between ILC and BC-NST after adjustment. Notably, ILC histology remained an independent adverse prognostic factor in selected subgroups, including individuals with N3 nodal disease and those treated with neoadjuvant therapy.

The distinct clinicopathologic profile of ILC observed in our cohort reinforces and expands upon patterns previously reported in studies. Compared with patients with BC-NST, patients with ILC were older at diagnosis, and their tumors more frequently presented at advanced stages, with a greater proportion of T3-T4 tumors and N3 nodal involvement.4, 5, 6,20,21 Despite these more advanced staging features, ILC was markedly less likely to be high grade, with only 20% of tumors classified as grade 3 compared with over 40% among BC-NST cases.20 ILC tumors were predominantly ER-positive/HER2-negative, a profile observed at a substantially higher frequency than in BC-NST, in keeping with the established luminal-like phenotype of this histological subtype. Importantly, despite having more advanced T and N categories, patients with ILC were less frequently treated with chemotherapy. This finding may reflect longstanding concerns regarding the lack of relative chemosensitivity of ILC and the observation that many patients with this histology derive limited benefit from cytotoxic therapy.22,23 In line with these staging features and the diffuse growth pattern characteristic of ILC, mastectomy was more frequently performed in this subgroup. Finally, we observed a modest but statistically significant increase in the relative frequency of ILC over the study period. Although this was not the primary focus of our analysis, similar patterns have been described in other population-based cohorts and warrant continued attention.24

Building on the baseline differences between histologies, we next examined their association with long-term outcomes. In the univariate analysis, OS curves for ILC and BC-NST were largely superimposed during the initial years, before gradually diverging from approximately year 8 or 9 onward, with outcomes thereafter favoring BC-NST. By 15 years, the absolute difference in OS reached nearly 4%, corresponding to a significantly worse HR for ILC at longer follow-up. This pattern suggests a time-dependent effect of histology on outcomes, in which ILC demonstrates comparable or even favorable short-term prognosis but inferior long-term survival, a finding that has also been reported in other large cohorts.9,10,25 When survival comparisons were adjusted for other prognostic features, the difference in OS between ILC and BC-NST was no longer statistically significant. Although stage likely explains part of the OS difference, a biological role is also possible, as other studies have reported persistent long-term differences even after stage adjustment.25 The propensity for later-stage detection in ILC has been widely attributed to the lower sensitivity of conventional screening modalities, particularly mammography, for this histological subtype. Biologically, ILC is characterized by loss of E-cadherin expression, leading to a discohesive, single-file growth pattern that infiltrates the stroma without forming a distinct mass or eliciting a marked desmoplastic reaction.26 As a result, mammographic findings in ILC are often subtle or absent, manifesting as ill-defined densities, architectural distortion, or even mammographically occult lesions, and there is a lower frequency of microcalcifications compared with BC-NST.27 These features can delay diagnosis and contribute to the more advanced stage at presentation in ILC, highlighting an important unmet need to develop more sensitive early detection strategies for ILC, which may, in turn, help close the gap in long-term survival outcomes.28 In addition, given the extended duration of recurrence risk in ILC, prolonged courses of adjuvant ET may represent another strategy to improve long-term outcomes in this population.29

In the subgroup analyses, significant associations between histology and OS persisted after multivariable adjustment. The association between ILC and worse OS was confined to patients with N3 disease, whereas no significant differences were observed among those with N1-N2 status. A similar pattern was observed in the SUCCESS trials, where patients with N2-N3 ILC experienced worse outcomes than their BC-NST counterparts, whereas no such difference was seen for patients with N0 or N1 disease.11 Consistently, a single-institution analysis with Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) Program validation demonstrated that prognosis in ILC relative to BC-NST differs markedly by nodal status, with better outcomes in node-negative disease but significantly worse long-term outcomes in node-positive disease, especially after prolonged follow-up.12 These findings may reflect challenges in detecting distant metastases at baseline in ILC. Patients with clinical N3 breast cancer are recommended to undergo systemic staging according to international guidelines; however, the infiltrative, discohesive growth pattern of ILC can limit the sensitivity of conventional imaging for identifying metastatic sites.3 Prior work has shown that conventional imaging, including 2-fluoro-2-deoxy-D-glucose–positron emission tomography-computed tomography, may underperform in detecting systemic disease in patients with stage III breast cancer, and particularly in ILC.30 More recently, [18F]-fluoroestradiol–positron emission tomography has been suggested as a complementary approach that may identify additional metastatic sites not detected on standard modalities, although the number of patients with ILC in these studies was limited.31 Overall, these findings suggest that the adverse prognosis associated with ILC in the setting of high nodal burden may stem from a combination of factors, including limitations in staging accuracy and intrinsic biological characteristics such as differential sensitivity to systemic therapies.

Consistent with prior reports, our study confirmed that ILC is associated with lower rates of response to neoadjuvant systemic therapy compared with BC-NST. Although this reduced responsiveness has traditionally been described in ER-positive disease,32 our findings indicate that it also extends to the TNBC subgroup. One potential explanation is the relative enrichment of the luminal androgen receptor subtype within triple-negative ILC, which has been shown to display distinct biology and poor chemosensitivity.33 This is particularly meaningful given that perioperative chemoimmunotherapy is now a standard option for patients with stage II-III TNBC,34 yet its efficacy in lobular histology remains largely unknown. In this context, the increasing emphasis in international guidelines on neoadjuvant therapy highlights the clinical challenge posed by the lower chemosensitivity of ILC, especially given its tendency to present at a higher stage.3 Accurate diagnosis of lobular histology, including recognition of non-classic variants that are more often triple-negative, is also essential to better characterize the biology of this disease and its differences from BC-NST.35 Current treatment recommendations do not differentiate by histological subtype, yet our findings underscore that ILC and BC-NST exhibit distinct patterns of response.3 An additional challenge is that ILC is more often clinically and radiologically underestimated at baseline compared with BC-NST, which may result in underappreciation of the true disease extent and complicate the evaluation of response to neoadjuvant therapy when comparing cTN with ypTN status.36 These findings should be interpreted with caution, as response assessment required complete staging data, included heterogeneous treatment modalities, and relied on changes between clinical and pathological staging, which may be affected by differences in staging accuracy.

The strengths of our study include its large sample size, nationwide scope, and long follow-up period, enabling robust assessment of long-term outcomes across clinically relevant subgroups. The use of a population-based registry with standardized data collection and internal quality checks for histologic coding minimized selection bias and ensured broad representation of real-world practice patterns. Nevertheless, some limitations should be acknowledged. Hormone receptor and HER2 status were not available for all patients, restricting pathology-based subtype classification to specific years. We sought to mitigate this limitation through a sensitivity analysis, which demonstrated high concordance with the treatment-based surrogate subtype approach in the most common ER-positive/HER2-negative subtype. We lacked granular data on comorbidities, specific systemic therapy regimens, sequencing, and duration, including duration of ET, precluding detailed correlation of treatment exposure with outcomes. Nodal staging was based on UICC TNM definitions that changed during the study period and differed by treatment setting (cN versus pN), which may have introduced heterogeneity and potential misclassification. In the multivariable and subgroup analyses, nodal stage was defined according to treatment context, using clinical staging for patients receiving neoadjuvant therapy and pathological staging for those undergoing upfront surgery, to reflect pretreatment disease burden and avoid incorporating treatment response. Given known differences between clinical and pathological staging, some heterogeneity and residual confounding cannot be excluded. Information on recurrence events was unavailable, limiting analyses to OS. Finally, histological classification relied on registry coding, which incorporates data from both oncological care programs and pathology laboratories with validation in case of discrepancies. However, central pathology review was not available, and the possibility of misclassification cannot be fully excluded.

Conclusion

In this large, nationwide cohort with long-term follow-up, ILC was associated with more advanced stage at diagnosis, leading to worse OS compared with BC-NST. These differences, together with lower responsiveness to neoadjuvant systemic therapy across both hormone receptor-positive/HER2-negative and triple-negative subtypes and worse outcomes in patients with advanced nodal disease, underscore the need for histology-specific evidence to inform treatment strategies. Ensuring accurate histological classification and greater representation of ILC in clinical trials will be essential to optimize management for this understudied population.
