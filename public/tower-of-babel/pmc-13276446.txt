Novel Dynamic Foot Abduction Bar for Clubfoot Treatment
Authors: Hannah Seatle, Barbara Harvey, Stacy Robitaille, Maryse Bouchard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBracing tolerance during Ponseti treatment is often challenging. Braces that allow more natural leg movement could improve tolerance and comfort, thereby improving outcomes. This manuscript introduces a novel dynamic clubfoot bar design that enables multiplanar movement. It reports on a prospective, assessor-blinded feasibility study in which 11 children aged 1-3 years with idiopathic clubfoot were randomized to wear the novel bar (experimental arm, n = 7) or their original Mitchell-Ponseti standard bar (control arm, n = 4) for 30 days, followed by 60 days of monitoring. Longer-term studies are needed to evaluate efficacy with and without Plantar Flexion Stop boots. This novel bar may be a promising alternative for children who do not tolerate a standard bar or for walking-age children who require extended wear times (ClinicalTrials.gov, [details omitted for blinded peer review]).Key Concepts(1)The novel dynamic clubfoot bar may enable more natural leg movement and increase patient comfort.(2)The novel bar showed no inferiority to the standard bar during a 1-month trial period; however, longer-term studies are required to evaluate efficacy.Keywords: Clubfoot, Ponseti, Foot abduction brace, Novel design

Introduction

Clubfoot occurs in approximately 1 in 1,000 live births [1]. The standard care for clubfoot is the Ponseti method [2,3]. After serial casting and tenotomy when needed, correction is maintained with a foot abduction brace (FAB), which consists of two ankle-foot orthoses (boots) connected by a shoulder-width bar to keep the feet externally rotated and dorsiflexed [2]. The typical wear schedule for the FAB is 23 h per day for the first 3 months, then at least 12 h at night until age 3-5 years, depending on provider preference [3]. Brace intolerance and inadequate time in the brace have been shown to increase the risk of recurrence, with up to 61% of idiopathic clubfoot patients who relapse reporting noncompliance with the prescribed bracing schedule [[2], [3], [4], [5]].

Most standard bars are straight and static. Prior literature suggests that parent-reported discomfort is a leading cause of noncompliance [5,6]. Alternative FABs that enable greater lower-limb motion include the Dobbs Bar [7] and single-leg-style braces, such as the Cunningham Dynamic Torsional Knee-Ankle-Foot Orthosis (DTKAFO) and Abduction Dorsiflexion Mechanism (ADM) braces. While studies suggest that the independent mobility provided by these alternative brace designs may be directly correlated with improved compliance [8,9], many of these braces remain controversial and underevaluated. For example, the spring-loaded hinges in the Dobbs Bar design can cause intermittent loss of the corrective foot position and provide resistance to inversion (potentially strengthening the already-overactive invertor muscles in clubfoot patients). The long-term impact of this is unknown. Many studies have also linked the absence of a foot abduction bar in some single-leg brace designs to an unacceptably high rate of recurrence [[10], [11], [12]]. High-quality, peer-reviewed literature on these devices is limited and many studies report conflicting results regarding parent satisfaction, bracing compliance, and recurrence rates for each bar type: Table 1 provides a non-exhaustive summary of comparative findings.

Novel brace development

Given the potential for improved compliance with added hip and knee mobility, we have designed a novel dynamic foot abduction bar (NB) for clubfoot bracing that addresses the limitations of existing static and dynamic FABs: this brace aims to enable physiological, independent leg movement while avoiding spring mechanisms and maintaining the optimal foot position at all times during use, with a connecting bar between the limbs.

As shown in Fig. 1, this novel bar prototype is a 3-part device consisting of 2 metal L brackets and a 3D-printed crossbar, connected via sliders and modified ball joint linkages (US patent application no. 18/117,233). These joints allow movement in all 3 planes while limiting rotation in the coronal and axial planes, permitting only outward and upward movement beyond the preset abduction and dorsiflexion angles, thereby ensuring an optimal corrective foot position (Fig. 2). This design promotes independent, physiological hip and knee movement, consistently maintains a corrective foot position, and avoids the use of springs. As with other standard abduction bars, the length of the NB’s crossbar can be adjusted to hold the feet shoulder-width apart. The NB connects to standard clubfoot boots using the same clip mechanism (with permission) as the static Mitchell-Ponseti SB, designed by OrthoPediatrics Specialty Bracing (Massachusetts, USA).

Schematic of a novel dynamic bar highlighting the full range of motion provided.

The current cost to produce each NB prototype is approximately $130 USD, with over 90% of costs attributable to machining the L brackets, 3D printing the sliders and crossbar, and the grooved roller bearings integrated into each slide. With higher production volume and a switch to different manufacturing processes and bearing suppliers, this cost could likely be reduced by approximately 70%.

Initial brace experience

To evaluate this novel device, an assessor-blinded feasibility evaluation was conducted in 11 children aged 1-3 years with idiopathic clubfoot, who were randomized to wear either the dynamic NB or their original SB for 1 month. In addition to assessing the feasibility of conducting randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comparing different clubfoot brace designs, this study evaluated bracing compliance using parent-reported logs and temperature sensors, parental perceptions using surveys, complications and recurrence with a blinded outcomes assessor, and patient range of motion using video analysis. This project was approved by our institution’s research ethics board, and study conduct was reviewed by a data safety monitoring board.

These patients had a well-corrected idiopathic clubfoot (Pirani Score ≤0.5) with no signs of recurrence, were in the nighttime bracing stage without significant brace intolerance, and fit the NB prototype with a boot that allowed temperature sensor insertion. The mean observation period was 100 days, during which the mean time spent in the NB was 32.4 days, followed by their original SB for 2 months (Fig. 3).

Graph demonstrating intervention duration and timeline for each participant. Note that the irregular timelines for the first 3 experimental patients listed were due to breakages in the novel dynamic bar prototype that required an immediate bar change.

Brace Wear and Compliance. Brace wear hours were measured using parent-reported logs and iButton [iButtonLink, Whitewater, Wisconsin (Product Number: DS1921G-F5)] temperature sensors inserted into the sole of one boot and set to record every 45 min. These sensors have been used in other studies evaluating FAB compliance [21]. The iButton temperature readings typically oscillated between ∼23 °C and ∼32 °C, so the cutoff temperature for determining brace wear was set at 27.5 °C (Fig. 4).

Typical sensor output recorded during the study period.

To assess whether parents complied with the prescribed bracing schedule, we calculated a bracing deficit: minimum prescribed wear hours per day – average actual wear hours per day. We allowed a 1-h grace period; more than 1 h was deemed noncompliant. We observed lower compliance with sensor data than with parent logs: the logs reported no cases of noncompliance, whereas the sensors detected noncompliance in 1 out of 4 control patients and 2 out of 7 experimental patients while wearing the SB (noncompliance was not recorded in any experimental patient while wearing the NB). The bracing deficits for these patients averaged approximately 6.0, 5.1, and 1.7 h per day.

An adherence fraction was also calculated: average actual wear hours per day/average prescribed wear hours per day (11.5 of 13.5 h). In the experimental arm only, the average adherence fraction was slightly higher in the NB than in the SB (0.96 vs. 0.97 per logs; 0.78 vs. 0.91 per sensors). Both logs and sensors indicate that 5 out of 7 experimental patients wore the NB for more hours per day than the SB (Fig. 5).

Patient-specific brace wear compared by arm and bar type.

Note that 5 out of 11 families were missing ≥1 week of logbook data because parents forgot to log hours and 3 out of 11 patients had incomplete sensor data. There were also several major discrepancies between the logs and the sensor data, so it is unclear whether the sensor malfunctioned or whether certain parents significantly over-reported their child's brace wear.

Parental Perspective. After using the NB, 7 out of 7 parents in the experimental arm reported that their child was either “more comfortable” or “much more comfortable” in the NB. When asked about their preferred bar, 5 out of 7 chose the NB on Day 30 and 6 out of 7 chose the NB on Day 0 (the other parents were unsure). Responses indicate that parents were no more worried about their child’s foot correction in the NB than in the SB. Parents reported higher satisfaction with their child’s ability to move normally in the NB (4.4 vs 3.4), the NB’s shape (4.3 vs. 3.9), and size (4.6 vs. 3.9) but reported lower satisfaction with the NB’s weight (3.9 vs 4.0). Two out of 7 parents indicated that their child had difficulty returning to their SB after using the NB. Overall feedback on the NB was largely positive, with parents reporting that their child slept better and was happier and more active. Some expressed concerns about the NB’s potential price point, weight, slider sounds, and that the L brackets caught more on blankets. On the final Day 90 survey, 10 out of 11 parents indicated interest in participating in a future study on the NB and no parent reported concerns about participating in the current study.

Video Data. Video from all patients showed differences in mobility between the NB and SB, indicating that several normal developmental gross motor skills and transitions can be achieved in the NB but not in the SB. For example, the Z-sit and half-kneel positions were observed only in children wearing the NB. Clinicians noted less torque at the knee joint in the NB during kneeling, transitions, and W-sit. They also noted that the knees were farther apart in the NB, resulting in more natural movements. More independent leg movement, greater dorsiflexion, and increased toe activation were observed when wearing the NB versus the SB (Fig. 6).

Intermittent ankle plantarflexion beyond neutral was also observed during certain transitions in the NB. Based on this finding, a separate follow-up evaluation was conducted with 9 patients who wore the NB and/or their SB with their regular boots and/or Plantar Flexion Stop (PFS) boots. PFS boots have a rigid extension behind the heel to prevent ankle plantarflexion. During this evaluation, subtle intermittent plantarflexion was again observed with standard boots in the NB and was also noted with standard boots and an SB. Plantarflexion beyond neutral was no longer observed when PFS boots were used with either bar. The clinical impact of this intermittent plantarflexion is unclear but it may suggest that all FAB models consider boot designs, such as the PFS, that minimize plantarflexion.

Adverse Events (AEs). A total of 13 AEs occurred; all were non-serious and mild in severity. Three AEs were omitted from the analysis because the blinded outcomes assessor deemed them “unrelated” to the foot abduction bar (eg, dry, patchy skin over the entire leg). There were 7 AEs in the control arm involving 3 out of 4 patients and 3 AEs in the experimental arm involving 2 out of 7 patients. The most common AE was a pressure sore or redness (2 in control, 2 in experimental). Because many participants received new clubfoot boots on the first day of the study to insert the iButton, most instances of pressure sores or redness were deemed “unlikely to be related” to the bar. One experimental patient developed a stage 1 pressure sore after 1 month of NB use and was deemed “possibly related” to the bar as the patient received only 1 new clubfoot boot and developed bilateral redness (which resolved by the next study visit after using band-aids). Since minor AEs occurred more often in the control arm and no serious AE occurred, it was concluded that wearing the NB for 30 days did not lead to increased AEs or significant immediate recurrence compared to the original SB in this short trial.

Outcome. For these patients, recurrence was defined strictly as a Pirani score >0.5, passive ankle dorsiflexion <10°, and/or a reduction in passive ankle dorsiflexion ≥5° from the previous visit. Under this definition, 2 out of 11 patients developed mild recurrence during the study period, though no intervention for recurrence was recommended in either case. Both cases occurred in the SB arm: one lost 5 degrees of passive ankle dorsiflexion between Day 0 and 90 (but remained within the normal range at 25° past neutral), and another developed mild adduction (Pirani score rated as 0) and a 5-10° decrease in their external thigh-foot angle (eversion range). Notably, the latter patient was one of three whose sensor data indicated they wore the brace much less than prescribed (average deficit >1.5 h/d). A third SB patient had mild dynamic supination at the Day 30 visit (but not at other study visits) and decreased eversion strength noted on Days 30 and 90. One experimental patient was able to fit their finger in the NB’s slot (prompting immediate bar redesign), resulting in minor redness that spontaneously resolved.

Given this short study period, we cannot determine whether the minor changes in the physical exam were related to the bar type alone. A larger-scale trial of longer duration is therefore required to assess the risks and impacts of sustained use on recurrence rates. Recruitment for this study was challenging, largely due to the increased frequency of required clinic visits, and this will likely pose a concern in future studies as well.

Prototype design changes

The NB experienced mechanical failures during our initial trial period, with 5 distinct design issues recorded. As aforementioned, the slider in the original design (V1) had a slot that one child could put a finger into, posing a pinch risk. The sliders were therefore redesigned as solid components in the second prototype (V2). On 3 separate occasions, the ball joint linkages broke, and the ball joint popped out of the socket. This issue occurred once with the V1 prototype and twice with the V2 prototype. The prototypes were subsequently redesigned and the ball joints were reinforced (V3; Fig. 7).

All 3 versions of the NB prototype used during the feasibility study.

V3 was used by all 7 experimental patients in the study. Two patients tore the protective foam end caps on the NB prototype during use. One patient snapped the bar’s crossbar after 35 days of use (this patient was the tallest/heaviest in the study at 105 cm and 19.9 kg). Slight wear was also observed on all brass ball joint insert components after use, which slightly affected the corrective foot position (maximum change <10°). Before widespread clinical use is considered, several design changes are under review.

Summary

A feasibility proof-of-concept study of a novel bar with 3 planes of motion for clubfoot bracing showed no inferiority to the standard Mitchell-Ponseti static bar over a 1-month period. Parents in this study generally preferred the novel bar as they perceived that their child was more comfortable, and video analysis demonstrated more physiologic motor skill execution with the novel bar. In this small cohort over a short study period, we did not identify any significant differences in compliance with brace wear, recurrence rates, or complications of FAB use. Complaints about bar breakages, wear abrasions, weight, and noise should be considered in future redesigns. Longer-term studies are needed to evaluate efficacy with and without PFS boots. This novel bar may be a promising alternative for children who do not tolerate a standard bar or for walking-age children who require extended wear times.
