Beyond the hazard ratio: machine learning and the future of personalized mitral valve repair counseling
Authors: Mark Cordell, Coleen Rizzo, Ilias P Doulamis
Published: 2026

Degenerative mitral regurgitation (MR) remains one of the most common indications for cardiac surgery. In experienced, high-volume centers, mitral valve repair is preferred over replacement, given its association with preserved ventricular geometry, fewer prosthesis-related complications, and improved long-term survival and mortality. However, a central challenge persists: patient-specific prediction of the durability of the repair. Traditional population-level survival models provide aggregate predictions but fall short in guiding individualized, long-term estimates of durability. Malik et al. addressed the adequacy of current analytic tools in the Journal of Thoracic and Cardiovascular Surgery (1). In a 16-year, single surgeon dataset, the authors compare traditional and machine learning survival tools to capture the complexity of long-term mitral valve repair performance and advance discussions about the adequacy of machine learning models to predict repair outcomes.

The Cox proportional hazards (CPH) model dominates surgical outcomes research for good reason. CPH models yield hazard ratios that clinicians can interpret directly and have been stress-tested across diverse clinical settings, such as the Society of Thoracic Surgeons (STS) Adult Cardiac Surgery Database risk calculator. Traditional CPH analysis is best suited for short-term outcomes and assumes proportional hazards over time, linearity between predictors and outcomes, and constant effect size throughout follow-up. These assumptions may be less predictive in longer-term results, since complex biological and biomechanical changes occur over time. In contrast, the random survival forest (RSF) model employed by the authors does not rely on these assumptions. RSF is a non-parametric machine learning method capable of handling nonlinear interactions and time-varying effects, making it potentially better suited for analyzing long-term durability in mitral valve repair.

The study included 444 patients undergoing primary mitral valve repair for degenerative MR between 2008 and 2024, all performed by a single surgeon. The primary endpoint was mitral valve repair failure (MRF), defined as reintervention or development of moderate-to-severe mitral regurgitation or stenosis. Over a median follow-up of 5.2 years, MRF occurred in 13 patients (3%), directly reflecting repair durability rather than composite perioperative outcomes (1,2).

The RSF model demonstrated superior discrimination compared with the penalized Cox model [concordance index (C-index): 0.874 vs. 0.796], with both models showing good long-term calibration (1). However, the models identified different predictors. The Cox model identified post-repair coaptation length as the sole significant factor, with each additional millimeter associated with roughly a 40% reduction in failure risk. In contrast, the RSF model identified both coaptation length and post-repair mean mitral gradient, suggesting a more complex and time-dependent relationship.

This difference becomes clinically relevant when examining long-term outcomes. At 5 years, gradients of 2, 5, and 8 mmHg were associated with similar freedom from failure (~98%). Over time, however, outcomes diverged substantially. At 10 years, freedom from failure was 95%, 93%, and 68% for gradients of 2, 5, and 8 mmHg, respectively, with further separation at 15 years (93%, 90%, and 64%). These findings highlight a late hazard not captured by proportional hazards modeling and caution against extrapolating short-term Cox estimates to long-term durability.

A similar pattern emerged for coaptation length. Freedom from failure at 5, 10, and 15 years was 94%, 74%, and 51% for 6 mm; 98%, 94%, and 91% for 9 mm; and 99%, 98%, and 96% for 12 mm. These results underscore that coaptation length influences late durability, even when early postoperative outcomes appear acceptable.

To better illustrate the clinical implications, the authors modeled six intraoperative scenarios for a representative patient. Even with a favorable coaptation length of 12 mm, a mean gradient of 7 mmHg corresponded to an estimated 10-year failure risk of 37%. When the same coaptation length was paired with a gradient of 2 mmHg, the predicted risk fell to approximately 1%. These findings suggest that coaptation length and gradient influence durability independently, rather than serving as interchangeable markers. Achieving durable repair therefore appears to depend on optimizing both parameters, not relying on a single favorable measurement.

A major barrier to clinical adoption of machine learning models is interpretability. The authors address this by reporting variable importance measures and partial dependence plots to illustrate nonlinear and time-varying effects (3-6). Although Cox models are more straightforward to interpret through hazard ratios, these methods provide useful clinical insight for more complex relationships. Importantly, comparison against a penalized Cox model with glmnet regularization strengthens the finding that the RSF model offers improved discrimination for complex survival analyses.

Several limitations should be noted. The dataset is from a single surgeon at a single institution. That design choice eliminates operative technique as a confounder and standardizes intraoperative echocardiographic practice, but it also anchors the model to one operator’s technical style, one institution’s measurement conventions, and one patient population. Whether the coaptation length and gradient thresholds translate to centers with different ring preferences, surgical volumes, or echocardiographic protocols is unknown. Without a centralized core laboratory for echocardiographic adjudication, measurement variability is a genuine concern, particularly for coaptation length given its technical sensitivity. The 13 MRF events in 444 patients reflect the quality of the surgical program; however, with fewer than 15 outcome events, the low event rate substantially limits model stability and constrains the reliability of variable importance estimates regardless of the statistical methodology employed. Independent, multi-center validation remains a prerequisite for broader clinical deployment.

The authors also outline practical next steps. Training models directly on echocardiographic video, rather than extracted measurements, could capture the full spatial and temporal behavior of the mitral valve and identify predictive features that manual measurements may miss (1). They also propose knowledge-enhanced artificial intelligence (AI), integrating causal evidence from large trials with institution-specific data (7,8). Notably, the accompanying web application allows surgeons to input intraoperative parameters and simulate predicted repair durability, moving this work closer to real-time clinical use (1,9).

These findings are particularly relevant as transcatheter mitral therapies continue to expand. When surgeons can provide patient-specific durability estimates, such as a predicted 10-year failure risk under 2%, shared decision-making may be informed with greater individualized precision than population-level averages allow, pending external validation of these estimates. Machine learning models do not replace surgical judgment but provide time-specific risk estimates that can refine intraoperative decision-making.

Overall, Malik et al. provide an important methodological advance. The random survival forest model improved durability prediction, identified time-dependent signals missed by traditional approaches, and was translated into a practical simulation tool. While external validation remains necessary, this work moves the field toward more individualized mitral valve repair counseling.
