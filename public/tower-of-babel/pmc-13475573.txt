Nuclear RSK1 mediates interferon-γ–induced proinflammatory activation in human primary macrophages and humanized mice
Authors: Keishi Nihira, Yoshihiro Yanagihara, Yuto Nakamura, Sarvesh Chelvanambi, Arda Halu, Lang Ho Lee, Alexander Mojcher, Hideyuki Higashi, Iwao Yamada, Jiao Qiao, Andrew K. Mlynarchik, Jianguo Wang, Julius L. Decano, Elena Aikawa, Vladimir Vrbanac, Sasha A. Singh, Masanori Aikawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Proinflammatory activation of macrophages promotes various inflammatory disorders. The molecular mechanisms underlying macrophage activation, particularly in the context of nuclear translocation of proinflammatory response mediators, remain obscure. We have used a systems approach to explore key regulators of macrophage activation using quantitative proteomics to monitor protein translocation to the nuclei of human primary macrophages elicited with interferon-γ (IFN-γ). Unbiased bioinformatics identified several candidates, including RSK1, a ribosomal protein kinase. Network analysis linked RSK1 with human gene modules for various inflammatory disorders. In vitro mechanistic experiments showed that IFN-γ stimulation promotes RSK1 phosphorylation at Ser380 via JAK signaling, resulting in STAT1 phosphorylation at Ser727, in the nuclei of macrophages. In concert with these results, RSK1 silencing or deficiency hinders IFN-γ–induced secretion of proinflammatory chemokines in human primary macrophages. Furthermore, RSK1 deficiency in human leukocytes altered IFN-γ–induced responses in humanized mice. Our findings provide evidence that RSK1 is a key nuclear shuttling enzyme that mediates proinflammatory activation of macrophages.

INTRODUCTION

Proinflammatory activation of macrophages plays a critical role in the pathogenesis of multiple human diseases (1–4). Macrophages are a heterogeneous population (5). Transcriptomics studies have demonstrated that stimuli, such as interferon-γ (IFN-γ) and lipopolysaccharide (LPS), skew macrophages toward subsets of proinflammatory phenotypes (6, 7) that are classified according to their stimulant conditions, for example, M(IFN-γ) and M(LPS) macrophages (8). Similarly, anti-inflammatory stimuli such as interleukin-4 (IL-4) and IL-13 shift their phenotypes toward the subsets of alternative activation, e.g., M(IL-4) and M(IL-13), which favor the resolution of inflammation (7–11). Nonetheless, M(IFN-γ) and M(LPS) macrophages commonly express high levels of proinflammatory chemokines such as chemokine (C-X-C motif) ligand 2 (CCL2) to recruit immune cells to inflamed sites, permitting either stimulant to be used as an inducer of general proinflammatory signaling. Although emerging evidence suggests a theory that macrophage heterogeneity is multidimensional rather than a conventional M1/M2 polarization paradigm (5), the use of an in vitro model of each phenotype such as M(IFN-γ), where specific cause-effect relationships are known, helps identify specific molecular mechanisms.

Intracellular signaling mechanisms, including the Janus kinase–signal transducer and activator of transcription (JAK-STAT) pathway, mediate IFN-γ–triggered proinflammatory responses. IFN-γ stimulation leads to STAT1 phosphorylation at Tyr701 (phospho-STAT1-Tyr701) by JAK1 and JAK2 in the cytoplasm, promoting nuclear translocation of phospho-STAT1-Tyr701. In the nucleus, phospho-STAT1-Tyr701 then undergoes phosphorylation at Ser727 (phospho-STAT1-Tyr701/Ser727), leading to transactivation of STAT1-target genes to produce proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines in macrophages (12–14). These lines of evidence support the notion that nuclear-targeting molecules regulate the proinflammatory activation of macrophages. However, the mechanisms and roles for nuclear translocation of such potential regulators in the proinflammatory activation of macrophages remain incompletely understood.

This study used IFN-γ, representing proinflammatory instigators, to identify molecular mechanisms contributing to macrophage activation through transcriptional activation of nuclear STAT1. While many studies have used human cell lines or mouse cells in macrophage research, we are aware that responses to stimuli may differ between cancer cells and primary cells and between species. The present study thus used human primary macrophages derived from peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) in a systems approach to identify IFN-γ–induced nuclear translocation of key regulators of macrophage activation. Our holistic target discovery platform has involved proteomics of nuclear translocation, bioinformatics for clustering, and network analysis. We discovered that ribosomal protein S6 kinase 1 (RSK1) is a nuclear shuttling kinase for proinflammatory macrophage activation in response to IFN-γ. IFN-γ–induced RSK1 phosphorylation, in turn, facilitates STAT1 phosphorylation at Ser727 in the nucleus, promoting inflammatory responses. These findings provide insights into regulatory mechanism for inflammatory diseases.

RESULTS

Quantitative nuclear proteomics demonstrates enrichment of nuclear-specific and nuclear shuttling proteins

Translocation of phospho-STAT1-Tyr701 to the nucleus in response to IFN-γ is a critical step toward the STAT1-dependent expression of proinflammatory molecules such as chemokines. This transient increase in nuclear signal (primarily measured using immunoblotting or immunostaining) usually occurs within 60 min after IFN-γ treatment (15, 16). To investigate whether additional proteins are translocated to the nucleus in a similar manner, we performed quantitative nuclear translocation proteomics using human primary macrophages derived from PBMC, elicited with IFN-γ for 1 hour. Nuclear lysates from three different PBMC donors (donors A, B, and C) with five time points of IFN-γ stimulation (0, 10, 20, 30, and 60 min) were digested and labeled using isobaric tandem mass tags (TMTs) followed by mass spectrometry analysis (Fig. 1A). We identified a total of 1086 distinct proteins when considering the combined data from both TMT 10-plex sets. To verify that we enriched for nuclear-prominent proteins, we queried the corresponding gene identifiers against three public datasets: UniProt (17), Uhlén et al. (18), and COMPARTMENTS (19), which, in turn, confirmed that 50.1 to 70.8% of detected proteins are known to be localized to the nucleus or are annotated as being localized to the nucleus and other organelles (fig. S1, A and B). For this latter annotation, we refer to these proteins as nuclear shuttling proteins because they can be found in multiple compartments in the cell, and IFN-γ promoted their accumulation in the nucleus, a process that can be monitored by a kinetics experiment.

RSK1 is an IFN-γ–induced nuclear translocating protein

Our hypothesis was that proteins that undergo nuclear translocation in response to IFN-γ exhibit a discernable increase in abundance at one time point (10, 20, 30, or 60 min) that could decline before the 60-min mark or remain sustained. To classify the protein kinetics profiles, we performed a high-dimensional cluster analysis method (see Materials and Methods) previously published by our group (20). We combined the three donors’ kinetics data into a single input for clustering (see Materials and Methods) that resulted in 41 clusters (fig. S1C). We focused on clusters that would indicate translocation to the nucleus by an increase in abundance at any time point. We further refined our list of proteins whose kinetic trends were similar across all three donors and selected proteins quantified with at least five unique peptides to include profiles with a greater number of quantified observations (21), resulting in 39 proteins from our dataset (fig. S1D). We then cross-checked these proteins with the UniProt-extracted annotations, bringing about nine candidate proteins that we characterized as nuclear shuttling proteins (fig. S1D).

We were particularly interested in RPS6KAI, ribosomal protein S6 kinase alpha-1, also known as RSK1 (Fig. 1B), because it is known to be translocated to the nuclei of HeLa cells in response to growth factor stimulation (22). This translocation leads to phosphorylation of nuclear substrates to regulate transcription of mitogen-responsive genes (22, 23). Given that our nuclear proteomics detected RSK1 translocation to the nucleus in response to IFN-γ, we hypothesized that RSK1 may also phosphorylate proteins involved in transcription regulation during macrophage activation. RSK1, however, is one of the four kinases in this enzyme family—RSK1, RSK2, RSK3, and RSK4 (fig. S2A)—of which RSK2 and RSK3 are also known to translocate to the nucleus in response to growth factor (24, 25). RSK4 is distinct from the other RSK isoforms in that it is predominantly cytosolic and constitutively active (26). Because we only detected RSK1 in our proteomics data (Fig. 1B), it would indicate that the three other kinases were sufficiently lower in abundance that they were not sequenced by the mass spectrometer. According to the data from the Human Protein Atlas database, RSK1 and RSK2 appear to be predominantly expressed among RSK isoforms in macrophages (fig. S2B). We therefore investigated whether RSK1 and RSK2 are expressed in macrophages using immunoblot analysis. Compared to the various amounts of recombinant RSK standards, we could deduce the relative expression levels of RSK1 and RSK2 in unstimulated macrophages. The data revealed that RSK1 expression was nearly five times higher than RSK2 expression in the lysates, suggesting that RSK1 is the predominant isoform among the RSK family in human macrophages (Fig. 1C).

To confirm the results of the RSK1 nuclear translocation kinetics data (Fig. 1B), we performed immunofluorescence staining in human PBMC–derived macrophages to visualize translocation of RSK1 to nuclei. In unstimulated macrophages, RSK1 signal was diffused throughout the cell (Fig. 1D); however, after 30 min of IFN-γ stimulation, intense RSK1 signal was detected in the nuclei (Fig. 1D). In human macrophages deficient in RSK1 using CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing, the RSK1 signal is nearly undetectable, indicating that the anti-RSK1 antibody is suitable for immunofluorescence imaging for intracellular RSK1 protein (fig. S3). Anti-RSK1 immunoblot analysis of nuclear lysates from human macrophages also validated IFN-γ–induced nuclear translocation of RSK1 between 20 and 30 min of one donor and by 10 min for the second (Fig. 1E). Using multiple detection methods, proteomics and immuno-based manner, we confirmed that RSK1 is translocated to the nucleus in response to IFN-γ stimulation. Although the exact timing of this translocation can vary across donors, we consistently observe that it occurs within 60 min of stimulation.

Network analysis links RSK1 to human inflammatory diseases

Proteomics and immunoblot analysis indicate that, of the four RSK enzymes, RSK1 predominates in human primary macrophages. We therefore hypothesized that, if RSK activity contributes to macrophage activation, then it is likely to occur through the most abundant RSK1. Sequence alignment indicates that the four enzymes share 79.7 to 81.0% (>594 sequence identity), where RSK2 and RSK3 are the most similar to each other and RSK4 is least conserved with respect to the other three enzymes (fig. S2A). Divergence in sequence conservation suggests divergence in function, not related to enzyme activity per se (because the active sites are conserved, fig. S2A), but related to signaling pathways and molecular interactions. Based on this assumption, we performed network analysis on each RSK enzyme to determine their likely molecular interactors and potential involvement in a variety of human diseases.

Recent evidence suggests that disease-related proteins tend to localize within the molecular interaction network or the interactome, forming closely interacting subnetworks called disease modules (27). Furthermore, the interactome-based location of a disease determines its pathobiological relationship to other diseases (28, 29). We sought to establish the association of the RSK family of proteins with a variety of diseases associated with macrophage activation such as cardiovascular, autoimmune, and metabolic disorders. Based on the network proximity between the RSK interaction partners and disease modules (see Materials and Methods), we identified that the RSK1-first-neighbor module is significantly close to various autoimmune, cardiovascular, and metabolic diseases (Fig. 2 and fig. S4). Moreover, RSK2 and RSK3 modules share disease associations with RSK1 (Fig. 2). RSK2 and RSK3, however, tend to associate with less human disease gene modules than does RSK1. The RSK4 module shows no significant associations with any of the diseases we tested (Fig. 2 and fig. S4). These results may predict that RSK1 has the most potential impact on human inflammatory diseases among the RSK family of proteins.

IFN-γ triggers RSK1 activation mediated by JAK1/2 signaling

Previous studies showed that an enzymatic activity of RSK1 is regulated by the status of multiple phosphorylation sites (30). As many as five phosphorylation sites, Ser221, Thr359, Ser380, Thr573, and Ser732, have been reported for RSK1 activation in epidermal growth factor (EGF) signaling (fig. S2A). We therefore investigated whether proinflammatory signaling affects the phosphorylation status of RSK1 in PMBC-derived human primary macrophages. We stimulated macrophages with IFN-γ for 30 and 60 min and then immunoprecipitated with anti-RSK1 antibody, followed by immunoblot analysis against these five phosphorylation sites. When compared to the unstimulated macrophages, signal for phospho-RSK1-Ser380 increased between 30 and 60 min of IFN-γ treatment but showed no change to phosphorylation of Ser221 and Ser732 (Fig. 3A). Signals for phosphorylation of Thr359 and Thr573 were too low to perform any comparisons (Fig. 3A). These data imply that RSK1 is activated through Ser380 phosphorylation during proinflammatory activation of macrophages. Moreover, immunofluorescence staining revealed that Ser380 phosphorylation mainly increased in the cytoplasm of macrophages in response to IFN-γ (fig. S5A). To further define whether the JAK signaling regulates RSK1 activation, we treated human macrophages with dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) or a pan-JAK inhibitor, pyridone-6, followed by stimulation with IFN-γ for up to 90 min. With IFN-γ alone, phospho-RSK1-Ser380 signal increased within 30 min (Fig. 3B and fig. S5B). Pyridone-6–mediated inhibition of JAK signaling resulted in a marked suppression of phospho-RSK1-Ser380 signal (Fig. 3B and fig. S5B). In contract, the pan-JAK inhibitor treatment did not influence extracellular signal–regulated kinase 1/2 (ERK1/2) phosphorylation, indicating that the suppression of phospho-RSK1-Ser380 was not mediated by the off-target effects such as ERK1/2 inhibition (Fig. 3C and fig. S5C). Furthermore, we generated human primary macrophages with JAK1 deletion using the CRISPR-Cas9 system, which demonstrated that JAK1 deficiency attenuated signal for phospho-RSK1-Ser380 during IFN-γ–stimulated macrophage activation (Fig. 3D). This response to pyridine-6 or JAK1 deficiency is similar to that of phospho-STAT-Ser727, as confirmed in the corresponding cell lysates (Fig. 3). These results suggest that JAK signaling mediates IFN-γ–induced activation of RSK1 in macrophages.

RSK1 inhibition reduces STAT1 phosphorylation at Ser727 in IFN-γ–stimulated macrophages

Our data have supported that, as is STAT1, RSK1 is a downstream target for JAK signaling (Fig. 3). Given that phosphorylation of STAT1 at Ser727 occurs in the nucleus and is important for its activity (31) and that RSK1 is translocated to the nucleus, we then hypothesized that STAT1 is a substrate for nuclear RSK1. To assess whether RSK1 induces phosphorylation of endogenous STAT1 at Ser727, we transfected human PBMC–derived macrophages with control small interfering RNA (siRNA) or RSK1 siRNA followed by IFN-γ exposure. In the siRNA controls, phospho-STAT1-Tyr701 signal was observed at 10 min, followed by a decrease at 60 min of IFN-γ stimulation, whereas an increase of the basally phospho-STAT1-Ser727 occurred at 10 min and increased further at 60 min (Fig. 4A), consistent with two waves of STAT1 phosphorylation dynamics reported previously (13). RSK1 silencing attenuated the phospho-STAT1-Ser727 signal, but not that of phospho-STAT1-Tyr701 (Fig. 4A), indicating that RSK1 contributes to this second wave of STAT1 phosphorylation. To further validate the role of RSK1 in phosphorylation of STAT1 at Ser727, we deleted the RSK1 gene in human macrophages. Gene editing–mediated abrogation of RSK1 expression also reduced signal for phospho-STAT1-Ser727 upon IFN-γ stimulation (Fig. 4, B and C). Additionally, we used four RSK inhibitors [BI-D1870 (32), LJH685 (33), LJI308 (33), and FMK (34)] to monitor the STAT1 phosphorylation status of Tyr701 and Ser727 at 60 min of IFN-γ stimulation. In human primary macrophages treated with RSK inhibitors or DMSO (control), pharmacologic RSK inhibition with small molecules attenuated signal for phospho-STAT1-Ser727 and produced no change to that of phospho-STAT-Tyr701 (Fig. 4, D to K, and fig. S6A). To further validate RSK-mediated phospho-STAT1-Ser727 in macrophages, we performed immunoprecipitation of the cell lysates with either control immunoglobulin G (IgG) or anti-STAT1-pSer727 for subsequent mass spectrometry analysis (fig. S6B). Postimmunoprecipitation, we recovered less STAT1-pSer727 in the BI-D1870 condition versus IFN-γ (plus DMSO) alone and detected the pSer727 site-specific decrease in signal using parallel reaction monitoring of three electron-transfer/higher-energy collision dissociation (EThcD) fragment ions (y122+, b8+, and c12+) (fig. S6, C and D).

Three independent methods (siRNA-mediated gene silencing, CRISPR-Cas9–based gene editing, and small-molecule–mediated RSK inhibition) support that RSK1 contributes to the levels of phospho-STAT1-Ser727, indicating that RSK1 can induce proinflammatory signaling events through STAT1 phosphorylation in macrophages.

RSK1 promotes secretion of proinflammatory chemokines during macrophage activation

To demonstrate that RSK1 can activate macrophages through STAT1 signaling, we investigated whether RSK1 silencing decreases the transcription of IFN-γ–induced chemokines. Human primary macrophages were treated with control siRNA or RSK1 siRNA followed by IFN-γ small molecule. IFN-γ increased the mRNA expression of the proinflammatory chemokines CXCL9/MIG, CXCL10/IP-10, and CXCL11/I-TAC, and RSK1 silencing decreased their total expression levels throughout the IFN-γ stimulation period, as demonstrated by the area-under-the-curve (AUC) graphs (Fig. 5A). We also monitored the expression of other known IFN-γ–inducible genes such as those encoding transcription factors STAT1 and IRF1 (35, 36); enzymes GBP1, PARP14, and PARP9 (35–37); and membrane proteins TAP1 and FCGR1 (35). RSK1 silencing, however, exerted no effects on any of their mRNA levels (fig. S7). Moreover, to validate the siRNA data, we deleted the RSK1 gene in human macrophages. The loss of RSK1 expression also decreased IFN-γ–induced mRNA levels of the proinflammatory chemokines in macrophages (Fig. 5B). These results indicate that RSK1 may selectively mediate the induction of a certain set of molecules in response to IFN-γ stimulation.

To further assess the role of RSK1 in chemokine induction, we treated human primary macrophages with BI-D1870 followed by stimulation with IFN-γ. Compared to the DMSO condition, BI-D1870 attenuated mRNA levels of proinflammatory chemokines such as CXCL9/MIG, without affecting STAT1 mRNA levels (Fig. 5C and fig. S8). In this context, we verified that BI-D1870 exhibits minimal cytotoxic effects using a lactate dehydrogenase assay (Fig. 5D). This decrease in mRNA expression also resulted in a decrease in secreted chemokine proteins. IFN-γ induced the release of CXCL9/MIG, CXCL10/IP-10, and CXCL11/I-TAC into culture medium of human macrophages, which was abrogated by RSK1 silencing (Fig. 5E and fig. S9). Using human primary macrophages deficient for the STAT1 gene that we established using the CRISPR-Cas9 system, we confirmed that STAT1 deletion reduced the mRNA expression levels of these chemokines in response to IFN-γ, suggesting that the RSK1-STAT1 axis plays a key role in the process of chemokine secretion (fig. S10). Together, these data suggest that RSK1 mediates the increased secretion of major chemokines, including CXCL9/MIG in IFN-γ–triggered macrophage activation.

RSK1 plays a key role in secreting proinflammatory chemokines in vivo in humanized mice

To investigate the contribution of RSK1 to macrophages’ proinflammatory activation in vivo, we examined the responsiveness of RSK1 against IFN-γ stimulation in mice. We first evaluated whether RSK1 silencing suppresses IFN-γ induced phosphorylation of STAT1 at Ser727 and chemokine expressions in mouse primary macrophages. RSK1 silencing did not suppress phosphorylation of STAT1 at Ser727 or expression of chemokines in mouse bone marrow–derived macrophages and peritoneal macrophages upon IFN-γ stimulation (fig. S11). These data implied that RSK1 in mouse macrophages does not have the same roles for macrophage activation as in human macrophages.

Humanized mice are used as a type of animal model, in which human hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) are transplanted into immunocompromised mice (38). Conventional CD34+ humanized mice had a limitation of poorly reconstituting human innate immune cells such as myeloid cells and natural killer (NK) cells because of the lack of cross-reactivity of mouse cytokines (39). We chose NOG-EXL mice as recipient mice, which were genetically altered to express human granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (hGM-CSF) and IL-3 cytokines. NOG-EXL mice that were reported to mimic the human immune cell composition especially improved CD33+ myeloid cell reconstitution (40). We generated RSK1-deficient humanized mice using CRISPR-Cas9 system targeting RSK1 genome region in human HSCs and transplanting the human HSCs into NOG-EXL mice. We then evaluated the inflammatory response of human macrophages against human IFN-γ intraperitoneal injection (Fig. 6A).

To delete the RSK1 gene in human HSCs, we used the CRISPR-Cas9 system with tandem guide RNA (gRNA) strategy. A tandem gRNA–based strategy, which combines two gRNAs mapping at close [40 to 300 base pairs (bp)] genomic proximity, enables to avoid subcloning process and achieves fast and efficient gene silencing on cell pools with a low allelic heterogeneity (41). We compared the knockout score of the RSK1 gene in human HSCs between single guide RNA (sgRNA) and tandem sgRNAs by Sanger sequence after electroporating the ribonucleoprotein (RNP) of Cas9 precomplexed with RSK1 sgRNAs. The tandem sgRNA strategy increased the knockout score of the RSK1 genome in human HSCs (fig. S12). We therefore selected the combination of RSK1 gRNAs (nos. 46839 and 47192) that showed 100% knockout scores in HSCs for the establishment of RSK1-deficient humanized mice.

We engrafted HSCs electroporated with RNP of tandem RSK1 gRNAs with NOG-EXL mice. The proliferation of human leukocytes in RSK1-deficient humanized mice was confirmed at 12 weeks postengraftment by flow cytometry, and the percentage of human leukocytes (hCD45+/mCD45− cells) of the total leukocyte population in peripheral blood was 31.3 and 37.0% for humanized mice engrafted with HSCs treated with control sgRNAs and those with RSK1 sgRNAs, respectively (Fig. 6B). At 15 weeks postengraftment, we injected human IFN-γ intraperitoneally in RSK1-deficient humanized mice. Four hours after IFN-γ injection, the animals were euthanized, and peritoneal cavity was washed with ice-cold Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Human peritoneal macrophages (hCD45+/mCD45−/hmCD11+ cells) were isolated by flow cytometry, and mRNA levels of IFN-γ responsive genes were measured by high-throughput real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR). Furthermore, we isolated human splenic leukocytes with a magnetic isolation kit, pooled the cells, and evaluated a knockout score of the RSK1 gene by Sanger sequence. The fragment deletion of the RSK1 gene was well maintained in RSK1-deficient humanized mice, and the knockout score was 97% (Fig. 6, C and D). RSK1 deletion significantly decreased the mRNA levels for a wide range of inflammatory signals such as CXCL9, RELA, STAT1, IRF5, HLA-DR1, WARS, and SECTM1, whereas RSK1 deletion did not change noninflammatory genes such as GK, LAMP2, and ITGA3 and inflammasome-related genes such as IL-1B, NLRP3, and TNFa in human macrophages isolated by using flowcytometry (Fig. 6, E and F). RSK1-deficient humanized mice and in vitro PBMC-derived macrophages showed both similarities and differences: The reduction in CXCL9 was consistent in both models, whereas, in humanized mice, there were no significant changes in CXCL10 and CXCL11, and STAT1 was reduced (Fig. 6F and fig. S13). Based on the protein-protein interaction (PPI) network, it has been demonstrated that extensive inflammatory signaling pathways are suppressed in macrophages of RSK1-deficient humanized mice (Fig. 6G). From the perspective of the types of the proteins, RSK1 deletion dampens the inflammation by changing the gene expression of multiple different types of proteins such as receptors, epigenetic regulators, secreted proteins, and enzymes (fig. S14). These results suggest that RSK1 plays an important role in the induction of proinflammatory chemokines against IFN-γ stimulation in vivo.

DISCUSSION

This study demonstrates that RSK1 is a key nuclear shuttling enzyme that mediates IFN-γ–induced proinflammatory activation of human macrophages based on the following findings and technological advances: (i) Nine nuclear translocated protein candidates identified by human primary macrophage nuclear proteomics include RSK1; (ii) RSK1 closely associates with multiple human inflammatory diseases, as predicted by network analysis using the PPI databases; (iii) IFN-γ stimulation increases phospho-RSK1 Ser380 via JAK signaling in human primary macrophages; (iv) RSK1 phosphorylation by IFN-γ leads to phosphorylation of STAT1 at Ser727; (v) RSK1 mediates IFN-γ–induced production of proinflammatory chemokines such by macrophages; (vi) CD34+ humanized mice model maintained almost complete RSK1 gene deletion for 15 weeks postengraftment; and (vii) RSK1 deletion by CRISPR-Cas9 system suppressed IFN-γ–induced chemokines elevation in humanized mice. These lines of evidence suggest a concept that RSK1 functions as a key kinase of proinflammatory M(IFN-γ) macrophage activation mediated by the JAK-STAT pathway (see a schematic diagram in fig. S15).

We postulated that IFN-γ–induced nuclear translocation of regulators serves key roles for skewing macrophages to the M(IFN-γ) phenotype, based on several lines of evidence supporting that nucleocytoplasmic shuttling proteins control a variety of cellular responses in the nucleus (42–46). Nuclear shuttling proteins that engage in proinflammatory macrophage activation, however, remain largely unknown. In this study, we aimed to identify a key nuclear shuttling enzyme(s) for M(IFN-γ) macrophage activation. To achieve this, we adopted a mass spectrometry–based proteomics approach using nuclear lysates from human primary macrophages, because quantitative proteomics is suitable to monitor changes in the nuclei of macrophages during proinflammatory activation. As the results of screening, we identified RSK1 as a key nuclear shuttling enzyme for M(IFN-γ) macrophage activation.

The RSK serine/threonine kinase family consists of four isoforms—RSK1, RSK2, RSK3, and RSK4—which regulate various cellular processes such as transcription, translation, cell cycle regulation, and cell survival (30, 47, 48). Although RSK isoforms show a high degree of sequence homology, increasing evidence supports functional differences among RSK isoforms, especially in cancer cells (47, 49). Three RSK isoforms—RSK1, RSK2, and RSK3—function as downstream effectors of the ERK signaling in response to mitogenic stimuli, whereas ERK signaling does not affect RSK4 due to its constitutive activation even in serum-starved cells (26, 50, 51). In several tumor cell types, RSK1 and RSK2 contribute to tumor progression, invasion, and migration (52–54). RSK1 and RSK2 thus serve as promising candidates of molecular targets for cancer therapies (48, 55). In contrast, RSK3 and RSK4 may act as tumor suppressors (56–58). Despite the biological importance of RSK isoforms, few studies have focused on additional RSK-mediated biological processes. In addition, the functionality of RSK-family members may depend on cell types and contexts. Our findings highlight the previously unidentified role of RSK1 in proinflammatory macrophage activation.

As with IFN-γ stimulation, RSK1 is activated in the cytoplasm and subsequently translocates to the nucleus upon exposure to EGF (47). Phosphorylation of RSK1 at Ser221 is essential for its nuclear targeting induced by EGF stimulation (59). Additionally, the phospho-mimetic S221D mutant of RSK1 translocates to the nucleus upon EGF exposure, similar to wild-type RSK1 (59). Our data reveal that Ser221 was constitutively phosphorylated in human primary macrophages even without stimulation, followed by nuclear translocation (Figs. 1 and 3), resembling the behavior of the S221D phospho-mimetic mutant (59). This finding suggests that Ser221 phosphorylation alone is insufficient to cause nuclear translocation. It remains unclear how nuclear targeting of RSK1 is tightly controlled in an IFN-γ–dependent manner in human macrophages. Hence, further studies will be needed to address this question.

Our findings also suggest that RSK1 is responsible for IFN-γ–induced phosphorylation of STAT1 at Ser727 in human primary macrophages. Ser727 phosphorylation is essential for maximal activation of STAT1, contributing to IFN-γ–induced macrophage activation (31, 60). The S727A mutant in which Ser727 mutated to alanine differentially affects STAT1-target genes, suggesting that Ser727 phosphorylation also controls selectivity of STAT1 transactivation (60, 61). In this context, the Ser727 kinases could differentially promote STAT1-target genes as well as induce STAT1 activation in response to IFN-γ. Consistent with this notion, silencing or deleting RSK1 suppressed specific part of the STAT1-target genes including proinflammatory chemokines, e.g., CXCL9, suggesting that RSK1-mediated Ser727 phosphorylation selectively induces transactivation of STAT1-target genes in human macrophages (Fig. 5 and fig. S7). Therefore, Ser727 phosphorylation of a portion of STAT1 may suffice to induce specific target genes of proinflammatory chemokines in human macrophages. In addition, IFN-γ–induced Ser727 phosphorylation requires nuclear translocation of STAT1 (13), suggesting that the Ser727 kinase phosphorylates STAT1 in the nucleus. Concerning our finding that RSK1 translocates to the nucleus in response to IFN-γ, RSK1 appears to phosphorylate STAT1 at Ser727 after their nuclear translocation in proinflammatory activated macrophages.

An approach of delivering gRNA encoding plasmid into the cells achieved a gene deletion efficiency at less than 50% (62). Electroporation of Cas9-gRNA RNP complexes increased the gene deletion efficiency at more than 80% (63). In some cases, they have been reported the gene deletion efficiency greater than 90% with adding of an electroporation enhancer (64). Tandem gRNA approach, which transfects two gRNAs targeting at close genomic proximity (40 to 300 bp), was reported to achieve almost 100% gene deletion in HepG2 cells and primary T cells (41). In this study, we have succeeded in inducing nearly 100% gene deletion efficiency of RSK1 in humanized mice by combining the tandem gRNA approach (41) to the approach delivering Cas9-gRNA RNP complexes with an electroporation enhancer (Fig. 6D). The RSK1 gene deletion was maintained in humanized mice after 15 weeks postengraftment (Fig. 6D). A similar approach also achieved almost 100% gene deletion efficiency of the human leukocytes in mice transplanted the edited human CD34+ cells at 8 weeks postengraftment (65), suggesting that these approaches provide stable results for inducing gene deletions at very high rate and persistently after engrafting into humanized mice. Our approach can be used to evaluate in vivo function of human target genes and is particularly useful in cases such as RSK1, where there are species differences in the downstream reaction pathway.

RSK1 deletion suppressed a wide range of inflammatory signals in RSK1-deficient humanized mice (Fig. 6, E to G). RSK1-deficient humanized mice and in vitro PBMC-derived macrophages showed both similarities and differences: the reduction in CXCL9 was consistent in both models, whereas there were no significant changes in CXCL10 and CXCL11, and STAT1 was reduced in humanized mice (Fig. 6F and fig. S13). Regarding the reasons for differing results between in vitro and in vivo studies, it is important to keep in mind that RSK1 deletion occurred not only in macrophages but also in other CD34+ HSC-derived immune cells in RSK1-deficient humanized mice. We confirmed that the RSK1 gene remained deleted in most of human leukocytes up to 15 weeks posttransplantation (Fig. 6D). This result indicated that RSK1 is genetically ablated in multiple functional human immune lineage cells such as T cells, B cells, NK cells, granulocytes, monocytes, macrophages, and dendritic cells, which are reported to present after human stem cell transplantation in NOG-EXL mice (40, 66–68). Given that many research groups reported on immune cell cross-talk, such as macrophages and T cells (69, 70), our results in the humanized mice that RSK1 deletion altered the expression of several proinflammatory genes including STAT1 under IFN-γ–treated conditions might be influenced by the differences in complex interactions between multiple different compartments of the adaptive and innate immune systems in vitro and in vivo. In addition, it is plausible that RSK1-deficient human HSC-derived lineage cells and the mouse resident cells in the mice could exert reciprocal influences on their cellular phenotypes. Furthermore, accumulating evidence suggests that macrophages exhibit diverse differentiation, activation, and polarization patterns depending on the surrounding microenvironment and show heterogeneous responses against IFN-γ stimulation (5, 71). Considering the distinct microenvironments during the differentiation between in vitro PBMC-derived macrophages and peritoneal macrophages in humanized mice, it is reasonable that their responses to IFN-γ vary. Consequently, we hypothesize that the phenotype of human CD34+ HSC-derived primary peritoneal macrophages in the humanized mice might exhibit variations when compared to those in a pure primary macrophage culture system in vitro.

In summary, this study demonstrates that RSK1 plays as a key kinase that translocates to the nucleus for shifting human primary macrophages toward proinflammatory phenotype. We also present a molecular mechanism by which RSK1 controls transcriptional activity and target selectivity of STAT1 through Ser727 phosphorylation to promote secretion of proinflammatory chemokines in IFN-γ–stimulated macrophages. This insight into the role of RSK1 in proinflammatory activation of macrophages presented in the present study provides a molecular basis for the development of a new therapy for patients with macrophage-mediated inflammatory diseases.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Cell culture of human PBMC–derived primary macrophages

All experiments, except animal experiments, were performed at the Center for Interdisciplinary Cardiovascular Sciences in compliance with the Institutional Review Board of Mass General Brigham Hospital under no. 2012B000079. Human PBMCs were isolated from buffy coat using lymphocyte separation medium (MP Biomedicals) according to the instructions of the manufacturer. PBMCs were incubated in RPMI 1640 without serum for 1 hour, washed with Hanks’ balanced salt solution, and cultured in RPMI 1640 containing 5% human serum (Gemini Bio-Products), penicillin, and streptomycin. After differentiation for 10 days, we used the cells as human PBMC–derived macrophages. Cells were maintained at 37°C in 5% CO2. Cells were treated with IFN-γ (R&D Systems), DMSO (Sigma-Aldrich), BI-D1870 (RSK Inhibitor II; EMD Millipore), LJI308 (MedChemExpress), LJH685 (MedChemExpress), FMK (MedChemExpress), or pyridone-6 (JAK Inhibitor I; EMD Millipore). Cellular toxicity was measured using CyQUANT LDH Cytotoxicity Assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Cell culture of human fetal liver–derived HSCs

Human fetal liver tissues were obtained in accordance with informed consent that was approved by the Institutional Review Board at Mass General Brigham Hospital (no. 2016P001106). Fetal liver sample were mechanically dissociated into small pieces and incubated in Liver Digest Medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 37°C. Mononuclear cells were isolated over a Ficoll gradient (Lymphoprep, STEMCELL Technologies) before separation into CD34+ and flowthrough (CD34−) cells using magnetic beads (CD34 Microbead Kit, Miltenyi Biotec). Fetal liver–derived hCD34+ HSCs were cultured in StemSpan SFEM II medium (STEMCELL Technologies) supplemented with 1% of StemSpan CC110 (STEMCELL Technologies) and 1% of antibiotic-antimycotic (Gibco). Cells were cultured in Nunc EasYFlask 75 cm2 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 37°C in 5% CO2 for 5 days before electroporation. Media were added to the flask on day 3.

Subcellular fractionation

We used the ProteoExtract Subcellular Proteome Extraction Kit (EMD Millipore) to obtain nuclear lysates of human macrophages according to the instructions of the manufacturer. Purity of the fractions was monitored using immunoblot analysis (see below).

Tandem mass tagging (TMT) sample preparation

We stimulated human PBMC–derived macrophages obtained from three donors (donor A, #44383; donor B, #44442; and donor C, #44400) with IFN-γ for 0, 10, 20, 30, or 60 min. Nuclear fractions of each condition were isolated using the ProteoExtract Subcellular Proteome Extraction Kit (EMD Millipore) and proteolysed (Lys-C, Wako Chemicals) using in-solution urea strategy detailed previously (37). Peptides were labeled with TMT 10-plex reagent (Pierce). The reporter ion channels were assigned for two sets of running as follows: for first running, 126 (0 min with IFN-γ, donor A), 127N (10 min with IFN-γ, donor A), 128N (20 min with IFN-γ, donor A), 129N (30 min with IFN-γ, donor A), 130N (60 min with IFN-γ, donor A), 127C (60 min with IFN-γ, donor B), 128C (30 min with IFN-γ, donor B), 129C (20 min with IFN-γ, donor B), 130C (10 min with IFN-γ, donor B), and 131 (0 min with IFN-γ, donor B); for second running, 126 (0 min with IFN-γ, donor A), 127N (10 min with IFN-γ, donor A), 128N (20 min with IFN-γ, donor A), 129N (30 min with IFN-γ, donor A), 130N (60 min with IFN-γ, donor A), 127C (60 min with IFN-γ, donor C), 128C (30 min with IFN-γ, donor C), 129C (20 min with IFN-γ, donor C), 130C (10 min with IFN-γ, donor C), and 131 (0 min with IFN-γ, donor C). The labeled peptides were combined and desalted using Oasis Hlb 1 cc columns (Waters). The peptides were then fractionated into 24 fractions based on their isoelectric focusing point (pH range of 3 to 10) using the OFF-gel system (Agilent). The fractions were dried using a tabletop speed vacuum, cleaned with the Oasis columns, and resuspended in 40 μl of 5% acetonitrile and 0.5% formic acid for subsequent analysis by liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC-MS).

Liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry

TMT studies

The high-resolution/accuracy Q Exactive mass spectrometer fronted with a Nanospray FLEX ion source, coupled to an Easy-nLC1000 HPLC pump (Thermo Fisher Scientific), was used to analyze the TMT peptide samples. The analytical gradient was run at 300 nl/min from 5 to 18% solvent B (acetonitrile/0.1% formic acid) for 120 min, followed by 5 min of 95% solvent B. Solvent A was 0.1% formic acid. The precursor scan was set to 140-K resolution, and the top 10 precursor ions [within a scan range of 380 to 2000 mass/charge ratio (m/z)] were subjected to higher-energy collision-induced dissociation (HCD; collision energy of 30%, isolation width of 3.0 m/z, dynamic exclusion enabled, starting m/z fixed at 120 m/z, and resolution set to 35,000) for peptide sequencing [tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS)].

Anti-STAT1-pSer727 IPs

The STAT1 peptide with phosphorylation at Ser727 was detected on the Orbitrap Fusion Lumos. The gradient flow rate was 300 nl/min from 5 to 21% solvent B (acetonitrile/0.1% formic acid) for 80 min, 21 to 30% solvent B for 10 min, followed by 5 min of 95% solvent B. Solvent A was 0.1% formic acid. The target phosphorylated STAT1 peptide, LQTTDNLLPmsPEEFDEVSR (m10-oxidation, s11-phosphorylation, 806.3575 m/z, z = 3), was subjected to EThcD (calibrated charge–dependent ETD parameters enabled, HCD collision energy of 30%, and resolution set to 500,000) for MS/MS.

LC-MS/MS data analysis

TMT studies

The MS/MS data were queried against the human UniProt database (downloaded on 1 August 2014) using the SEQUEST search algorithm, via the Proteome Discoverer version 2.1 (PD2.1, Thermo Fisher Scientific), using a 10–part-per-million tolerance window in the MS1 search space and a 0.02-Da fragment tolerance window for HCD. Methionine oxidation was set as a variable modification, and carbamidomethylation of cysteine residues and 10-plex TMT tags (Thermo Fisher Scientific) were set as fixed modifications. The peptide false discovery rate (FDR) was calculated using Percolator provided by PD2.1: The FDR was determined on the basis of the number of MS/MS spectral hits when searched against the reverse, decoy human database. Peptides were filtered on the basis of a 1% FDR. Peptides assigned to a given protein group, and not present in any other protein group, were considered as unique. Consequently, each protein group is represented by a single master protein (PD Grouping feature). Master proteins with two or more unique peptides were used for TMT reporter ratio quantification. The normalized reporter ion intensities were exported from the PD2.1 analysis below.

Anti-STAT1-pSer727 IPs

The three most abundant fragment ions of the target peptide (LQTTDNLLPmsPEEFDEVSR - m10-oxidation, s11-phosphorylation, 806.3575 m/z, z = 3) as annotated by SEQUEST (PD2.1) were used for quantification: y122+, 759.79455 m/z; c12+, 1424.64911 m/z; and b8+, 899.48327 m/z. AUC of each fragment was calculated using the Skyline software (https://skyline.gs.washington.edu).

Multiplexed cluster analysis

High-dimensional clustering of the normalized TMT ion intensities was done using our original software XINA (20). Our method is unique from standard clustering approaches in that we combine the kinetics data acquired from multiple datasets (e.g., the two TMT 10-plex experiments) into a single input file for clustering, under the assumption that the sources and extent of variation (response to IFN-γ) across the experiments, the three donors’ kinetics, are similar (20). The value in this multiplexing approach includes a simplified output of a single set of clusters and the ability to monitor the behavior of a single protein across various conditions (in this case, the three donors’ nuclear responses to IFN-γ). In this study, we combined the three independent nuclear translocation datasets: the five–time point kinetics of donor A (the average of the two TMT 10-plex replicate datasets), donor B, and donor C for subsequent clustering. We ran model-based clustering analysis using “mclust” R package, resulting in 41 clusters that explain the variation (donor and IFN-γ response) in the combined data (fig. S1C).

Network analysis

As a proxy of the association between the RSK family of proteins and human diseases, the average shortest network distance between RSK modules and disease-related proteins was measured, where network distance is defined as the non-Euclidean distance measured in terms of the number of edges between two nodes. RSK modules are defined as the subgraphs consisting of the RSK family gene and its first neighbors, i.e., direct interaction partners on the interactome. The average shortest distance D of an RSK module to disease genes is measured by calculating the shortest distance between each RSK module gene s and all genes t of a disease and then averaging over all RSK module genes s such that D=∑sϵSds and ds=∑tϵTdst, where dst is the shortest distance between s and t and S and T are the sets of genes in the RSK-first-neighbor module and disease genes, respectively. To compare the average shortest distance value to random expectation, the average shortest distance of the same number of randomly selected genes to disease genes was calculated for N = 100 realizations. To control for degree (i.e., the number of connections of a gene), the random selection was done in a degree-preserving manner where all genes were binned according to their degree and random genes were selected uniformly at random from their corresponding degree bin. Empirical P values were calculated by pemp=PDr<D, where Dr is the average shortest distance of the randomized instance. The interactome onto which the RSK modules and disease genes were mapped consists of curated physical PPIs with experimental support, including binary interactions, protein complexes, enzyme-coupled reactions, signaling interactions, kinase-substrate pairs, regulatory interactions, and manually curated interactions from literature, as described previously (29). Disease genes were obtained from the DiseaseConnect (http://disease-connect.org) (19) [using entries with evidence from genome-wide association studies and Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (www.omim.org/)] and MalaCards (www.malacards.org/) (73) databases. Cellular localizations of proteins were assessed using the UniProt database (accessed 20 February 2018) using the Subcellular Location annotations. For the identification of nuclear proteins, “Chromosome, centromere, kinetochore,” “Nucleus,” and “Nucleus speckle” were considered as nuclear locations.

For PPI network analysis, the network visualizations were made using Gephi v0.10.1. The interaction networks based on our gene list were defined by STRING v11.5 PPI network. The connected genes are shown with node size linked to the amount of change. The node color indicated the direction of the gene expression changes (orange, increased; green, no change; and blue, decreased). The label color indicated whether it was statistically significant (red) or not (black).

For the isolation of human peritoneal macrophages, the peritoneal lavage cells were counted; blocked with Human TruStain FcX and mouse TruStain FcX antibodies (BioLegend); stained with a cocktail of antibodies of anti-human CD45-AF488 (BioLegend, no. 368536), anti-mouse CD45-AF647 (BioLegend, no. 103124), and anti-human/mouse CD11b-PE (BioLegend, no. 101208) for 20 min; and washed three times with cell staining buffer. Cells were then incubated with Calcein Violet 45 (Invitrogen, no. 65-0854-39) for viability staining, and then human peritoneal macrophages (hCD45+/mCD45−/hmCD11b+) were sorted in a flow sorter, CytoFLEX SRT (Beckman Coulter).

Establishment of RSK1-deficient humanized mice

Humanized mouse experiments were performed in accordance with the approved protocols by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Mass General Brigham Hospital (animal protocol no. 2009 N000136). RSK1-deficient humanized mice were generated by the Human Immune System Mouse Program at Brigham and Women’s Hospital. Twenty hGM-CSF/hIL-3 NOG (NOG-EXL) mice (10 weeks old, female) were purchased from Taconic Biosciences. Animals were housed in Ragon Institute under specific pathogen–free conditions, with controlled 12-hour light/dark cycles and food and water ad libitum. Human fetal liver–derived HSCs were cultured for 5 days (see the “Cell culture of human fetal liver–derived HSCs” section), and the RNP complexes were electroporated with the cells on day 5 (see the “Gene deletion using CRISPR-Cas9” section). The cells were cultured overnight after electroporation. The cells were resuspended with PBS, and 1,000,000 cells in 200 μl of PBS were administered by tail-vein injection into the mice after sublethal irradiation with 2 gray. Engraftment was assessed at 12 weeks postengraftment by determining the ratio (%) of peripheral blood cells expressing human CD45 versus total CD45 expressing cells (see the “Flow cytometry” section).

Human IFN-γ intraperitoneal injection study

Recombinant Human IFN-γ Protein (R&D Systems, no. 285-IF) was resuspended with PBS. One thousand units of IFN-γ in 1 ml of PBS was intraperitoneally injected. Four hours after administration, mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation, and blood was collected from heart puncture. Ice-cold Dulbecco’s PBS (8 ml) was injected into the peritoneal cavity. After shaking the mouse for a minute, peritoneal exudates were collected. Human peritoneal macrophages were isolated by flow cytometry, and the mRNA levels of proinflammatory chemokines were measured by the real-time PCR system.

Spleen was collected and minced to isolate splenic leukocytes. Pooled splenic leukocytes from control humanized mice or RSK1-deficient humanized mice were used for determining the RSK1 gene deletion score by Sanger sequence.
