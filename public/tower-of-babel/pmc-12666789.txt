Beyond dengue and Oropouche: The urgent need for yellow fever preparedness in Cuba
Authors: Roberto Cañete, Junior Vega-Jiménez, Alfonso J Rodriguez-Morales
Published: 2025

The Cuban National Health System is highly structured, prevention-oriented, and emphasizes continuing medical education. One of Cuba's most recognized goals is public health [1].

Recently, the largest documented outbreak of Oropouche in Cuba has been reported [2], underscoring the rising threat of arboviruses in regions burdened by endemic dengue and other vector-borne diseases [2]. We highlighted the critical need to understand the demographic and clinical characteristics of affected patients, as well as the imperative of surveillance, diagnostic accuracy, and case management strategies. Oropouche, like dengue, Zika, and chikungunya viruses, represents an emerging arboviral threat in Latin America with the potential for global dissemination through imported cases, as observed in the United States, Canada, and various European countries.

In the Americas, another regional arboviral threat is yellow fever, with more than 356 confirmed cases in the ongoing 2024/2025 outbreak across seven countries of South America (up to November 17, 2025) (152 deaths, 42.7 %) (https://shiny.paho-phe.org/yellowfever/). Yellow fever is a viral disease transmitted primarily by Aedes aegypti mosquitoes in tropical regions of South America and Africa [3]. This disease is no longer endemic in Cuba. Its eradication in the country illustrates the efficacy of public health initiatives against vector-borne diseases. However, current ecological and social determinants enabling the reemergence and spread of yellow fever and other arboviruses worldwide call for urgent international and national responses [4,5].

Cuba's robust vector surveillance and control programs, integrated with the Ministry of Public Health's general health surveillance system, facilitate early detection of vector presence and behavior, as well as timely identification of new cases of human arboviral diseases. Despite this, the regional emergence and re-emergence of arboviruses pose ongoing challenges to effective disease control.

Like many countries in the Americas, Cuba faces threats from Aedes aegypti-associated arboviruses [5]. The province of Matanzas, located in western Cuba, has experienced repeated dengue outbreaks, demonstrating active circulation of different dengue virus serotypes and the presence of competent vectors. The introduction and expansion of Zika and chikungunya viruses further heighten the risk of yellow fever emergence, given the availability of mosquito vectors.

The persistent presence of the competent vector, mainly Aedes aegypti, poses a serious risk. This eco-epidemiological dynamic increases the likelihood of virus introduction into receptive areas within Cuba by undetected viremic travelers from regions with active transmission. Cuba's role as a transit hub connecting countries where yellow fever is endemic, coupled with significant travel among Cubans residing abroad, further exacerbates the risks of imported cases.

Currently, the World Health Organization (WHO) and Pan American Health Organization (PAHO) recommend vaccination focused on populations living in identified risk areas and rapid outbreak response. Nonetheless, the evolving regional epidemiological landscape and the rapid pace of global trade suggest that reactive strategies alone may be inadequate to protect densely populated, immunologically naive communities. Hence, this communication advocates reassessing the yellow fever risk in Cuba and considering a proactive, progressive vaccination program [6].

Despite the absence of the sylvatic cycle involving non-human primates in Cuba, the risk of establishing an urban Aedes aegypti-human transmission cycle remains a threat, given the high vector infestation in certain localities. The potential impact of yellow fever introduction could be catastrophic, with significant morbidity and mortality, and could compromise the public health system's ability to respond effectively.

The 17D yellow fever vaccine has effectively controlled the disease for almost 90 years and is considered highly safe, with the potential for lifelong immunity. A strategic vaccination campaign prioritizing high-risk groups, such as healthcare personnel, port workers, and travelers to endemic regions, followed by gradual expansion, would be a critical component of primary prevention [4,6].

Concerns about yellow fever emergence in non-endemic areas like Cuba are growing, driven by climate change, mosquito adaptation (e.g., egg resistance to desiccation, winter diapause), and increased global morbidity. Waiting for the first confirmed case before initiating mass vaccination could result in failed containment, given the rapid urban spread potential in a vector-dense environment.

Lessons from Cuba's past demonstrate the effectiveness of preventive over reactive public health approaches. Therefore, we strongly encourage national health authorities to conduct thorough cost-benefit analyses that weigh the costs of preventive vaccination against the human, economic, and social costs of a potential urban yellow fever outbreak in the 21st century.

Globalization, climate change, and social determinants will continue to drive the emergence of new pathogens, some of which are highly contagious and dangerous. Understanding the dynamics of yellow fever epidemics is critical for implementing targeted interventions and reducing disease burden. Currently, Cuba does not require a yellow fever vaccination certificate for travelers arriving from endemic areas with active virus circulation (e.g., Brazil or Colombia); therefore, this poses a significant risk given the ongoing epidemics in South America and the high number of travelers from such countries to the island. However, if a foreigner arrives from risk areas without a yellow fever vaccination, they have the opportunity to receive the vaccination on site. Additionally, the vaccination rate in Cuba is very low, as vaccination is recommended only for travelers on official missions to endemic countries in South America and Africa. When a Cuban arrives from endemic areas without vaccine certification, they are placed under close surveillance for 2 weeks. People arriving without the vaccine can get it. Only 12 % of Cubans are estimated to be vaccinated against yellow fever.

The Cuban health care system has been internationally recognized for its comprehensive successes, particularly its emphasis on primary care and preventive strategies. This comment supports our call for Cuban health authorities to consider implementing a yellow fever vaccination campaign.

In our opinion, this recommendation should not be limited to Cuba but extended to other countries and regions facing similar risks. Proactive vaccination strategies, combined with rigorous surveillance and vector control, can provide a robust defense against the resurgence of yellow fever in the Americas.
