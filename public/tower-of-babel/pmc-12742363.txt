Recurrent Syncope in Patients With Reflex Syncope Treated With Dual‐Chamber Pacemakers: Short‐Term Associated Factors—A Single‐Center Retrospective Study
Authors: Thai Duy Vo, Ngoc Dung Kieu, Le Uyen Phuong Tran, Cao Dat Tran, Tri Thuc Nguyen, Thi Thanh Binh Dao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundDual‐chamber pacemakers equipped with anti‐reflex syncope algorithms are an established therapeutic option for preventing recurrent syncope in selected patients with reflex syncope. However, their efficacy in patients under 40 years old and in non‐type 2B syncope remains uncertain and clinical predictors of recurrent syncope post‐implantation are not well established.ObjectiveTo identify clinical factors associated with early recurrence of syncope in patients with reflex syncope who received dual‐chamber pacemakers with anti‐syncope functionality.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 117 patients (65% female) with reflex syncope confirmed by a positive tilt‐table test. All received dual‐chamber pacemakers. The primary endpoint was syncope recurrence within 6 months.ResultsAmong 117 paced patients, 15 (12.8%) had recurrent syncope by 6 months. In the reduced Cox model, all three prespecified variables independently predicted recurrence: female sex (HR: 5.386; 95% CI: 1.689–17.175; p = 0.004), systolic blood pressure differential between the end of the passive phase and the syncope onset (HR: 1.036; 95% CI: 1.008–1.064; p = 0.011), number of prior syncope episodes (HR: 2.950; 95% CI: 1.565–5.561; p = 0.001). ROC‐based cutoffs supported descriptive separation (e.g., ≥ 2.5 prior episodes; ΔSBP ≥ 87.5 mmHg; asystole ≥ 13.5 s), but continuous coding was used for modeling to avoid information loss.ConclusionWithin 6 months of dual‐chamber pacemaker implantation, recurrent syncope was more likely in female sex, a larger systolic blood pressure differential between the end of the passive phase and syncope onset, and greater pre‐implantation syncope burdens.Keywords: blood pressure drop, dual‐chamber pacemaker, recurrent syncope, reflex syncope, tilt‐table test

Introduction

Syncope is a clinical syndrome characterized by transient loss of consciousness (TLOC) due to temporary cerebral hypoperfusion [1], representing various pathophysiological mechanisms, with reflex syncope being one of the three primary etiological groups according to the 2018 ESC guidelines [1]. Reflex syncope accounts for approximately 1%–3% of all emergency department admissions [2, 3, 4] and has a community prevalence ranging from 20% to 30%, varying among different populations [5, 6].

Recurrent reflex syncope significantly impacts quality of life and poses a risk of injury due to falls. Pacemakers with anti‐syncope algorithms have emerged as a therapeutic option for highly selected patients, demonstrating efficacy in landmark randomized controlled trials such as ISSUE‐3 (2012) [7] and Russo et al. [8]. Consequently, ESC guidelines (2021) [9, 10] provide a class IA recommendation for pacemaker implantation in patients experiencing reflex syncope with documented asystole (type 2B). However, current guidelines specifically recommend pacemaker implantation primarily for patients older than 40 years, whereas younger patients (< 40 years) or those with other forms of reflex syncope (especially mixed types) lack precise guidelines, necessitating individualized clinical decision‐making. Current evidence also suggests that clinical and hemodynamic characteristics, such as sex, burden of prior syncopal episodes, magnitude of blood pressure drop, and duration of asystole during tilt testing—may influence the likelihood of recurrence after pacemaker implantation [11]. Yet these potential predictors have not been systematically evaluated in a real‐world cohort where patients often present with heterogeneous syncope patterns beyond the strict guideline‐defined subgroup. To address this knowledge gap, we examined clinical, electrocardiographic, and tilt‐induced hemodynamic factors associated with syncope recurrence after implantation of dual‐chamber pacemakers in patients with reflex syncope. Our goal was to explore factors related to syncope recurrence and to determine patient populations most likely to benefit from pacemaker therapy.

Methods

Study Design

Predictors of recurrent syncope in patients with reflex syncope implanted with dual‐chamber pacemakers is a single‐center, retrospective cohort study conducted at the Arrhythmia Treatment Department at Cho Ray Hospital. Cho Ray Hospital is a tertiary referral hospital under the central health system in southern Vietnam, with an annual volume of 800–900 cardiac device implantations (including pacemakers, ICDs, and CRTs). Consecutive patients were screened from March 2017 to March 2025, and those meeting the inclusion criteria were followed for 6 months after pacemaker implantation (Figure 1).

Flow diagram showing study participants.

Participants

Eligible participants were adults diagnosed with reflex syncope based on a positive head‐up tilt test (HUTT) using the Italian protocol. Before device implantation, all patients underwent standardized evaluations including 12‐lead ECG, 24‐h Holter monitoring, exercise testing, and transthoracic echocardiography. Pacemaker implantation indications followed ESC 2021 cardiac pacing guideline [9]. Patients with type 2B cardioinhibitory response were considered for pacing. For those with non–type 2B responses, implantation was performed only when objective cardioinhibition was demonstrated (e.g., high‐grade atrioventricular block during syncope with preserved ventricular rate and no asystole > 3 s, junctional escape rhythm temporally associated with loss of consciousness) and after failure of guideline‐directed conservative therapy, including midodrine up to 15 mg/day and structured tilt‐training [1, 7, 10, 12]. Patients with pure vasodepressor physiology were not implanted. Individuals with baseline atrioventricular block or junctional rhythm on Holter monitoring were excluded. Additional exclusions included incomplete clinical data or < 6‐month follow‐up.

Pacemaker Systems and Programming

All patients received dual‐chamber pacemakers from major manufacturers, including St. Jude Medical (Assurity MRI PM2272, Endurity MRI PM2172, Endurity core PM2152), Medtronic (Adapta DR pacing system, Advisa DR MRI SureScan Pacemaker System, Attesta DR MRI SureScan Pacemaker System, Azure XT DR MRI Surescan Pacemaker System), Biotronik (Evia DR‐T, Evity 8 DR‐T), and Boston Scientific (L121, L111, L131, L211, L231). Devices were programmed according to manufacturer recommendations and harmonized with protocols used in prior landmark trials. For rate‐drop response (RDR) algorithms, settings were aligned with ISSUE‐2 and ISSUE‐3 [7]: lower rate 40 bpm for 2 beats or a 20‐bpm drop detected at < 50 bpm within 1 min, with an intervention rate of 90 bpm for 1 min. For closed‐loop stimulation (DDD‐CLS), the basic rate was 50 bpm, maximum CLS rate 120 bpm, medium CLS response, and resting rate control disabled. Conventional DDDR programming used a lower rate limit of 50 bpm, upper tracking rate of 120 bpm, and AV delays of 150/120 ms (paced/sensed), with optional optimization as needed.

Measurements and Study Variables

Baseline data included demographic and anthropometric characteristics, comorbidities, number of prior syncopal episodes, ECG findings, biochemical tests, and echocardiographic parameters. During HUTT, heart rate and blood pressure were measured before tilt, during the passive phase, before pharmacologic provocation, at the moment of syncope, and during recovery.

The systolic blood pressure differential (ΔSBP) was defined as the difference between SBP at the end of the passive phase (minute 20 of upright tilt) and SBP at syncope onset. Reflex syncope type was categorized using the VASIS classification [13], and associated arrhythmias were documented (Figure 2). Patients were stratified as having recurrent syncope if they experienced ≥ 1 episode following pacemaker implantation during the 6‐month follow‐up period.

Systolic blood pressure differential (ΔSBP). SBP, systolic blood pressure.

Follow‐Up and Outcomes

Patients were evaluated at 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, and 6 months after implantation. The primary outcome was recurrent syncope within 6 months. Patients and caregivers were instructed to report any syncopal or presyncopal episodes, and clinical verification was performed during follow‐up visits. Time to first recurrent episode was recorded.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range, as appropriate. Categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages. Group comparisons used Student's t‐test or Mann–Whitney U‐test for continuous data and Pearson's chi‐square or Fisher's exact test for categorical data. To identify predictors of syncope recurrence, univariable analyses were initially performed, followed by multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression. The significance threshold was two‐sided p < 0.05. All analyses were conducted using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Ethics Statement

The study has been approved by the ethics committee on biomedical research at Pham Ngoc Thach University of Medicine according to Official Letter No. 1313/QĐ‐TĐHYKPNT dated April 19, 2024 and The Ethics Committee of Cho Ray Hospital approved this study under approval No. 1699/GCN‐HĐĐĐ dated January 11, 2024. As a retrospective study using de‐identified medical records, informed consent was waived.

Results

Baseline Demographic and Clinical Characteristics

From March 2017 to March 2025, a total of 117 patients diagnosed with reflex syncope who underwent dual‐chamber pacemaker implantation were enrolled and followed for 6 months. The mean age was 47.7 ± 15.2 years, with 65% of patients being female. The average body mass index (BMI) was 22.1 ± 2.8 kg/m2. Common comorbidities included hypertension (21.4%), diabetes mellitus (2.6%), and dyslipidemia (3.4%). The overall mean number of syncopal episodes prior to implantation was 2.4 ± 1.1. Patients with recurrent syncope group had significantly more previous episodes than those in the non‐recurrent group (3.3 ± 0.9 vs. 2.3 ± 1.1; p = 0.001). Routine laboratory evaluations and echocardiographic findings were within normal limits across the cohort.

Indications for pacemaker implantation were primarily a type 2B cardioinhibitory response (66.7%), followed by junctional rhythm (19.6%) and complete atrioventricular block (13.7%), as identified by tilt‐table testing. Regarding anti‐syncopal pacing algorithms, closed loop stimulation (CLS) mode was used in 41.9% of patients, while non‐CLS modes were used in 58.1%, with no significant difference in usage distribution between recurrent and non‐recurrent syncope groups (p > 0.05) (Table 1).

In subgroup analyses, patients with a type 1 vasovagal response demonstrated a statistically significant difference in systolic blood pressure drop between the recurrent and non‐recurrent groups (p = 0.002). This duration was significantly longer in the recurrent syncope group compared to the non‐recurrent group (14.3 ± 2.9 s vs. 8.9 ± 2.5 s; p < 0.001) (Tables 2 and 3).

Discussion

Recurrent Syncope

Among 117 paced patients, 15 (12.8%) had recurrent syncope within 6 months. This 6‐month rate is similar to Velázquez‐Rodríguez et al. [12], and lower than the proportions reported in ISSUE‐3 [7] and SYNPACE [11] (21% and 50%, respectively), consistent with our cohort's larger fraction of type‐2B cardioinhibitory responses (66.7% vs. ~57% in ISSUE‐3 and 52% in SYNPACE). Conversely, our rate exceeds that of more selectively enrolled series such as VASIS [13] and RUSSO [8] (5% and 2%), both of which contained a higher proportion of type‐2B cases (86%–100%). Our study was designed with a pre‐specified 6‐month primary endpoint to capture early clinically relevant recurrences while minimizing loss to follow‐up in a relatively young population. Prior pacing studies shown that most recurrences (and treatment separation) occur early after implantation, with subsequent flattening of Kaplan–Meier curves; multicenter DDD versus no‐therapy [13], SYNPACE [11], and ISSUE‐3 [7] all demonstrate early divergence that persists. We acknowledge cumulative syncope incidence increases with time and late events occur beyond 6–12 months in these trials. Review on recent advances in syncope management by Martone et al. [14] also emphasized that the vasodepressor burden can limit pacing efficacy; the higher prevalence of type‐1 responses in our cohort is compatible with this mechanism, although our data should be regarded as hypothesis‐generating.

Predictors of Recurrent Syncope

Cox proportional hazards regression identified predictors of 6‐month recurrence. To respect events‐per‐variable constraints and avoid collinearity, the multivariable model was prespecified with three complementary predictors: female sex, number of pre‐implantation syncopal episodes (continuous, per episode), and systolic blood pressure drop during tilt (ΔSBP) (continuous, per mmHg). Other candidates (VASIS type 1, VASIS type 2B, asystolic duration during tilt) were assessed only in univariable models (Table 3). ROC thresholds were used solely for descriptive Kaplan–Meier displays (Figure 3); hemodynamic variables entered the Cox model in continuous form to minimize information loss and instability from dichotomization. Proportional hazards assumptions were tested with Schoenfeld residuals and log–log plots and were not violated. In the reduced model, all three predictors remained independently associated with recurrence: female sex HR 5.39 (95% CI 1.69–17.18; p = 0.004), prior syncope burden HR 2.95 per episode (95% CI 1.57–5.56; p = 0.001), and ΔSBP HR 1.036 per mmHg (95% CI 1.008–1.064; p = 0.011) (Table 6). Given the small number of events, estimates remain imprecise and should be viewed as exploratory.

Systolic Blood Pressure Difference

Type 1 reflex syncope is characterized by a mixed cardioinhibitory and vasodepressor response [15], resulting in rapid decompensation [16]. Because pacemakers correct bradycardia or asystole but do not counteract vasodilation, an early, steep fall in systolic blood pressure (SBP) can outpace any rate support and precipitate recurrence despite appropriate device function. In our cohort, type 1 patients showed a larger SBP drop from end‐passive to syncope (ΔSBP) than other subtypes. Importantly, a greater ΔSBP independently predicted recurrence, and the Kaplan–Meier curves separated from about 3 months onward (Figure 4), indicating that a pronounced vasodepressor burden, rather than persistent bradyarrhythmia, is the principal driver of early post‐implant events. Patients with higher systolic blood pressure differential (> 87.5 mmHg) tend to have worse syncope‐free survival, but in this sample the difference is borderline, not statistically significant at the 0.05 level (log‐rank test, p = 0.075).

Kaplan–Meier curves of systolic blood pressure differential and recurrent syncope after 6‐month follow‐up.

These observations are consistent with trials and guidelines emphasizing that pacing is most effective when cardioinhibition predominates, whereas vasodepressor‐dominant phenotypes recur more often despite pacing (e.g., ISSUE‐3; ESC 2018 and ACC/AHA/HRS 2017 syncope guidance have confirmed that type 1 reflex syncope is associated with a higher recurrence rate compared to other subtypes) [1, 6, 7]. Accordingly, type 1 patients merit closer follow‐up and consideration of adjunctive measures (volume or salt, physical counter‐pressure training, medication review, selective midodrine or fludrocortisone), while procedural strategies such as cardioneuroablation have been explored only in highly selected, refractory cases and remain investigational [17]. Overall, our data support ΔSBP as a pragmatic marker of vasodepressor load and pacemaker non‐responsiveness in mixed responses, although these findings are hypothesis‐generating given event counts and should be validated in larger cohorts.

These findings highlight the clinical importance of precise monitoring and careful management of this patient subgroup, aiming to reduce recurrent syncope and enhance overall patient outcomes.

Number of Syncopal Episodes Prior to Intervention

On ROC analysis, the optimal cutoff of 2.5 prior episodes yielded the highest Youden's index (0.445) with AUC 0.762 (p = 0.001), indicating good discrimination for 6‐month recurrence. Consistent with this, in the prespecified multivariable Cox model the number of pre‐implantation syncopal episodes (continuous) remained independently associated with recurrence (HR 2.95 per episode, 95% CI 1.57–5.56; p = 0.001) (Table 6). For illustration only, Kaplan–Meier curves stratified by the ROC cutoff diverged from about 3 months onward, with higher recurrence in patients above the threshold (Figure 3). To minimize information loss and instability, the variable was modeled continuously in Cox; the dichotomous cutoff was used only for descriptive Kaplan–Meier display.

This finding aligns with results reported in the SYNPACE and ISSUE‐3 trials, as well as the large retrospective Danish cohort study of 11 126 patients conducted by Davidsen et al. [18], followed over an 11‐year period (1996–2017). This latter study demonstrated that each additional previous syncopal episode significantly increased the risk of recurrence, with a reported HR of 1.10 (95% CI: 1.08–1.12), emphasizing prior syncope burden as a significant prognostic factor for future recurrence. Clinically, patients with multiple prior episodes likely have a more easily triggered reflex and/or greater vasodepressor susceptibility; thus, earlier structured evaluation and tailored conservative measures may help reduce short‐term recurrences.

Sex and Recurrence

Female sex remained independently associated with 6‐month recurrence despite pacing. This is consistent with prior clinical data: in a post hoc analysis of POST I–II, women had a higher adjusted risk of recurrent vasovagal syncope than men, and a large syncope‐unit registry likewise reported more recurrences and distinct trigger/tilt profiles in females [19, 20].

From a mechanistic perspective, experimental studies demonstrate sex‐related differences in autonomic and baroreflex regulation. Young women exhibit relatively greater vagal influence on heart rate and different sympathetic–vascular coupling than age‐matched men, and baroreflex function is modulated by sex and hormonal status, affecting cardiovagal sensitivity and orthostatic responses [21]. Such differences may predispose women to a mixed cardioinhibitory–vasodepressor reflex pattern. Because pacemaker therapy corrects only the bradycardic/asystolic limb and does not reverse vasodilation [22], a relatively greater vasodepressor burden and distinct autonomic profile in women could plausibly translate into a higher rate of syncope recurrence despite appropriate device function.

Duration of Asystole

In the subgroup with tilt‐induced asystole (predominantly type 2B), ROC analysis identified an optimal cutoff of 13.5 s (AUC 0.922, p = 0.005), indicating that longer asystolic pauses on tilt were strongly associated with 6‐month recurrence. For modeling, asystolic duration was treated as continuous in univariable Cox and was not retained in the prespecified 3‐variable multivariable model (female sex, prior syncope burden, ΔSBP) to avoid collinearity with ΔSBP and overfitting. Mechanistically, pacemakers act after the reflex is triggered; when a prolonged pause coincides with (or follows) a marked vasodepressor response, rate support alone may arrive too late to prevent hypotension and cerebral hypoperfusion, hence recurrence despite appropriate device function. In other words, pacemaker therapy is most effective when blood pressure remains sufficiently high; in cases of prolonged asystole accompanied by delayed cardioinhibition, the substantially reduced blood pressure limits the therapeutic effectiveness of pacing [23].

These findings align with previous studies, emphasizing the complexity of physiological mechanisms underlying reflex syncope [24]. Overall, our data suggest that asystolic duration is a pragmatic risk marker in cardioinhibitory phenotypes, while its independent prognostic value beyond ΔSBP requires confirmation in larger cohorts.

Role of Pacemaker

Our findings add to the evidence that pacing is effective in cardioinhibitory/asystolic reflex syncope, particularly type 2B, and are consistent with ESC syncope guidance recommending pacing when prolonged asystole is documented [10].

In the SPAIN trial, DDD‐CLS pacing significantly reduced syncope burden and prolonged time to first recurrence compared with sham DDI pacing in patients ≥ 40 years with recurrent tilt‐induced cardioinhibitory vasovagal syncope. In BIOSync CLS, patients ≥ 40 years with severe recurrent reflex syncope and tilt‐induced asystole treated with DDD‐CLS pacing had a 1‐year recurrence rate of 16%, compared with 53% in the inactive control arm, with persistent benefit over 2‐year follow‐up. These studies underpin current support for CLS in a rigorously selected, older, asystolic population.

By contrast, our cohort was younger and phenotypically heterogeneous (mixed and vasodepressor forms in addition to cardioinhibitory/asystolic). In patients with a prominent vasodepressor component, any pacing algorithm, including CLS, addresses brady/asystole but cannot reverse vasodilation, likely attenuating observable incremental benefit. Moreover, our CLS vs. non‐CLS comparison involved few patients/events and real‐world programming (non‐protocolized), so it was not powered to detect the effect sizes reported in SPAIN [25] and BIOSync [26].

Regarding age, current ESC syncope guidelines recommend pacing only in highly selected patients, generally ≥ 40 years, with documented asystole after failed conservative therapy, and caution against indiscriminate pacing in younger individuals because of benign prognosis and long‐term device risk. ISSUE‐3, SPAIN and BIOSync CLS all focused on patients ≥ 40 years with documented asystole or cardioinhibitory responses. For patients < 40 years, the 2021 ESC Pacing Guidelines underscore that the Task Force did not issue a recommendation due to insufficient data supporting efficacy in this age group. Interestingly, our series includes a substantial proportion of young patients, but the overall sample size is insufficient for a robust, prespecified age‐stratified analysis with adequate power. However, recurrence did not differ significantly by age (≤ 40 vs. > 40 years, p = 0.265). These observations support age‐related findings as exploratory and require confirmation in adequately powered studies.

Conclusions

Pacemaker therapy reduced recurrent episodes in reflex syncope, with an overall 6‐month recurrence of 12.8% in our cohort. Time‐to‐event analysis identified three independent predictors of recurrence: female sex, greater pre‐implantation syncope burden, and a larger systolic blood pressure differential (ΔSBP) during tilt, in decreasing strength of association. Clinically, these factors delineate a subgroup at higher post‐implant risk in whom closer follow‐up and adjunctive measures (education, hydration/salt, counter‐pressure training, medication review, selective midodrine) should be prioritized. Given the small number of events, estimates are exploratory and warrant confirmation in larger, prospective studies; nonetheless, our findings support early diagnosis and timely intervention, ideally at the first suspected reflex‐syncope episode, to mitigate recurrence and improve outcomes.

Limitations

The current study has several limitations. It is a retrospective cohort study with an unequal number of patients across subgroups, which may have influenced the identification of independent predictors of syncope recurrence. Additionally, unmeasured confounding factors not included in the dataset may have affected the outcomes. The generalizability of the findings is also limited, as data were collected from a single center.

The follow‐up duration was limited to 6 months. As a result, our findings primarily describe early post‐implant outcomes, and we may have underestimated the cumulative incidence of late syncope recurrences. This limitation is particularly relevant in patients with a strong vasodepressor phenotype, in whom late events may still occur despite pacing.

Furthermore, our study did not include a control group for direct comparison between patients with and without pacemaker implantation. Therefore, it is judged that the impact of this part on the main outcome is minimal.
