Enhancing medical student skills in dispute prevention through objective structured clinical examinations: insights from a pilot study
Authors: Ching-Yi Lee, Hung-Yi Lai, Yu-Che Chang, Ming-Ju Hsieh, Chih-Wei Yang, Ching-Hsin Lee, Wu Chen Hui, Mi-Mi Chen, Sze-Yuen Yau
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn the complex and dynamic landscape of healthcare, preventing medical disputes has become a critical aspect of medical practice. Medical disputes often arise from miscommunication, medical errors, or unmet patient expectations, leading to a breakdown in the doctor-patient relationship and potentially resulting in litigation. Despite the growing recognition of the importance of dispute prevention, current medical education often lacks comprehensive training in communication and conflict resolution. This study aims to explore the feasibility and preliminary educational effects of integrating Objective Structured Clinical Examinations (OSCE) into medical education to enhance medical students’ ability to prevent and manage medical disputes.MethodsThis quasi-experimental pilot study involved 24 sixth-year medical students from Chang Gung University, divided into two groups: one received traditional classroom instruction on medical dispute prevention, while the other participated in formative OSCE-based simulation sessions. Both groups later underwent a summative OSCE. Group allocation was based on existing timetable assignments; no randomisation was performed. Data were collected through MCQ assessments, OSCE performance scores, and a semi-structured group interview conducted by an independent researcher, recorded and transcribed verbatim. Quantitative data were analysed using descriptive statistics, paired t-tests, and ANOVA; qualitative data were analysed using thematic analysis.ResultsThe results suggested preliminary improvements in students’ ability to manage and prevent medical disputes following simulation-based familiarisation. The simulation group demonstrated higher scores across all five OSCE communication domains: rapport building (18.4 ± 1.3 vs. 16.2 ± 1.8, p = 0.004), issue identification (17.9 ± 1.2 vs. 15.7 ± 1.9, p = 0.007), active listening (17.5 ± 1.5 vs. 14.8 ± 2.2, p = 0.002), patient-centred language (17.8 ± 1.6 vs. 15.6 ± 2.0, p = 0.006), and respectful discussion of sensitive topics (17.7 ± 1.4 vs. 15.2 ± 2.3, p = 0.003). MCQ scores showed greater gains in the simulation group (pre = 72.5 ± 6.4; post = 85.3 ± 5.8) than in the traditional instruction group (pre = 73.1 ± 7.2; post = 79.4 ± 6.6). Qualitative findings revealed three themes: learning effectiveness, course evaluation, and confidence levels. Students in the simulation group perceived OSCE-based training as more realistic and described increased confidence in handling disputes.ConclusionsThis pilot study provides preliminary evidence that simulation-based familiarisation using formative OSCEs may support the development of medico-legal communication and dispute prevention competencies in senior medical students. As a pilot study with a non-randomised design and small sample size, these findings should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. A key limitation is that the simulation group practised in the identical format as the summative assessment, which may confer a rehearsal advantage independent of genuine learning. Background

In the complex and evolving landscape of modern healthcare, preventing medical disputes has become an integral dimension of maintaining both patient trust and professional integrity [1, 2]. Medical disputes frequently arise from communication failures, unmet expectations, or perceived lapses in care, often culminating in litigation, emotional distress, and defensive clinical behaviors [3, 4]. Beyond their legal and financial consequences, such disputes erode the ethical foundation of the doctor–patient relationship and highlight the moral responsibility of physicians to communicate transparently and empathetically [5, 6].

Despite widespread acknowledgment of communication as a cornerstone of professionalism and patient safety, undergraduate medical curricula continue to prioritize technical proficiency over interpersonal and ethical competence [7, 8]. Conventional medical law instruction often relies on didactic lectures that emphasize theoretical principles rather than cultivating situated moral reasoning and conflict-resolution skills applicable to real clinical encounters. As a result, many graduating students enter residency ill-equipped to manage emotionally charged situations that can escalate into disputes or complaints [9].

Simulation-based learning (SBL) offers a pedagogically robust means to bridge this gap. Rooted in experiential learning theory [10] and situated learning principles [11], SBL enables learners to act, reflect, and adapt within controlled yet realistic clinical scenarios. Within this framework, the Objective Structured Clinical Examination (OSCE) has evolved beyond its original summative assessment role into a powerful formative learning tool that fosters self-reflection, feedback, and skill consolidation [12–15]. When applied to medico-legal or ethically sensitive contexts, formative OSCEs employing standardized-patient simulation can provide a psychologically safe environment for rehearsing difficult conversations, disclosing adverse events, and negotiating patient dissatisfaction—core competencies in dispute prevention and ethical communication [16–18].

This pedagogical perspective reframes the OSCE not merely as an assessment mechanism but as a structured simulation-based educational method for cultivating professionalism, empathy, and conflict-management skills. It aligns with contemporary movements in ethics education that emphasize the integration of moral reasoning with authentic clinical experience rather than isolated theoretical instruction [6, 7]. Accordingly, this pilot study aimed to explore the feasibility and preliminary educational effects of a formative, simulation-based OSCE module on medical students’ competencies in dispute prevention and communication. Specifically, it compared this experiential approach with traditional classroom instruction on medical law and dispute management, examining both performance outcomes and learners’ perceived confidence. Given the pilot nature and design limitations described below, findings should be treated as preliminary and exploratory rather than confirmatory.

Methods

This study employed a quasi-experimental pre–post design to explore the feasibility and preliminary educational effects of an OSCE-based educational intervention in enhancing medical students’ ability to prevent and manage medical disputes. Two groups were compared: one received traditional classroom instruction on medical law and dispute prevention, while the other participated in formative OSCE-based simulation sessions. Both groups were subsequently evaluated using a summative OSCE to assess overall performance.

Traditional instruction comprised two 90-minute didactic lectures delivered by a legal consultant and a senior clinician, covering the same content domains assessed by the MCQ: principles of informed consent, patient rights and communication obligations, identification and management of adverse events, and medico-legal documentation. Total face-time for the traditional instruction group was approximately 180 min, equivalent to the duration of the formative OSCE session. Instruction was delivered in a lecture-theatre format using slides and case vignettes for illustration; no structured debriefing, role-play, or standardized-patient interaction was provided. The same faculty members who designed the MCQ delivered the traditional instruction, but were not involved in developing the OSCE scenarios or facilitating the simulation session.

The study was conducted between March and June 2019 at the College of Medicine, Chang Gung University, in collaboration with the Chang Gung Memorial Hospital Clinical Skills Center, Linkou, Taiwan.

Participants

The study targeted sixth-year medical students enrolled in the clinical clerkship phase at Chang Gung University who had completed the required course in medical law. All eligible students (n = 24) were invited and consented to participate. Because this was a pilot educational intervention aimed at feasibility testing, formal power analysis was not conducted. The total sample size was determined pragmatically, consistent with prior pilot studies evaluating formative OSCE interventions in medical education [12, 13].

Group allocation was based on existing administrative timetable assignments within the clinical clerkship rotation. Students were assigned to the traditional instruction group (n = 12) or the OSCE-simulation group (n = 12) according to their scheduled rotation timetable. No randomisation, allocation concealment, or participant blinding was performed. This is acknowledged as a significant limitation inherent to the convenience design. Demographic and baseline academic characteristics were compared between groups to assess baseline equivalence (Table 1).

Sampling followed a convenience approach, including all students enrolled in the elective module. Inclusion criteria were (1) completion of the medical law course and (2) willingness to participate in both training and evaluation sessions. Exclusion criteria were (1) absence from any OSCE station, (2) incomplete data submission, or (3) prior participation in similar dispute-prevention simulations.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Chang Gung Medical Foundation (IRB No. 201700740B0D001). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and anonymity was maintained using coded identifiers.

Implementation of OSCE sessions

All OSCE sessions were conducted at the Clinical Skills Center. Each student completed all six stations, interacting with trained standardized patients (SPs) for ten minutes per encounter, followed by immediate feedback from faculty examiners. Examiners participated in a calibration workshop before the study to ensure consistent rating and feedback delivery. To minimize inter-rater variability, examiners used the same scoring rubric and engaged in practice scoring sessions before the formal OSCE.

Session structure and timing: All six stations were completed within a single half-day session (approximately three hours, including station rotations and inter-station intervals). Students rotated sequentially through stations in a pre-assigned order, with one student occupying each station at any given time. The formative OSCE session was conducted approximately four weeks prior to the summative assessment.

Learning objectives: Each station was mapped to five specific learning objectives: (1) apply principles of informed consent and disclosure of adverse events; (2) demonstrate empathetic communication with dissatisfied patients; (3) use patient-centred language to de-escalate conflict; (4) identify and accurately acknowledge patients’ expressed concerns; and (5) demonstrate respectful and professional discussion of sensitive medico-legal topics.

Feedback model: Immediate post-station feedback was structured using a modified Pendleton’s rules framework. Students were first invited to self-assess their performance, followed by examiner reinforcement of effective behaviours, identification of areas for development, and a brief action-planning statement. Feedback was delivered by the same faculty examiner who assessed the station, over approximately five minutes per station.

Examiner identity and blinding: Faculty examiners who assessed the formative OSCE stations were the same individuals who subsequently assessed the summative OSCE. No formal blinding of examiners to group allocation was implemented. This represents a potential source of expectation bias, confirmation bias, and performance bias in summative scoring, which is acknowledged as a design limitation.

Instruments and data collection

Data were collected using a combination of quantitative and qualitative instruments designed to assess knowledge acquisition, communication performance, and learner perceptions.

MCQ — developed by clinicians and legal consultants to measure knowledge of medical dispute prevention and related legal principles. The MCQ comprised 20 items, each with four response options, covering: principles of informed consent (5 items), patient rights and communication obligations (5 items), identification and management of adverse events (5 items), and medico-legal documentation (5 items). Items were developed by a panel of two legal consultants and two clinicians, and reviewed for content validity by three independent medical education faculty not involved in the intervention. No formal item analysis or pilot testing was conducted prior to the study, which is acknowledged as a limitation.

Data analysis

Quantitative data from MCQ and OSCE scores were analyzed using SPSS 24.0 for Windows. Descriptive statistics (mean ± SD) summarized performance. Paired t-tests compared pre- and post-intervention MCQ results, and one-way ANOVA examined differences between instructional groups.

Given the small sample size (n = 12 per group), all inferential statistics should be interpreted as descriptive and exploratory rather than confirmatory. No correction for multiple comparisons was applied, which increases the risk of Type I error; this is acknowledged as a limitation. Effect sizes (Cohen’s d) are reported alongside p-values to provide information about the magnitude of observed differences.

Qualitative data from the verbatim interview transcript were analysed using thematic analysis following the six-phase framework described by Braun and Clarke [19]: (1) familiarisation with data, (2) generating initial codes, (3) searching for themes, (4) reviewing themes, (5) defining and naming themes, and (6) producing the final report. Two researchers (CWY and SYY) independently coded the transcript. Codes were compared and discrepancies resolved through discussion. The final thematic structure was reviewed by a third team member (MJH) to enhance credibility. Themes were identified inductively from the data. Individual participant contributions are identified by coded participant numbers (e.g., MS-04) in the Results section.

Results

All 24 eligible sixth-year medical students completed the intervention and post-test assessments (response rate: 100%). The mean age was 24.1 ± 0.7 years, and 54% were female. Baseline demographic and academic characteristics were comparable between the traditional-instruction and OSCE-simulation groups (p > 0.05; Table 1).

Knowledge outcomes (MCQ Performance)

Both groups demonstrated improvement in medical dispute-prevention knowledge after the instructional period. The OSCE-simulation group achieved a significantly greater mean gain in MCQ scores (pre = 72.5 ± 6.4; post = 85.3 ± 5.8; p < 0.001) compared with the traditional-instruction group (pre = 73.1 ± 7.2; post = 79.4 ± 6.6; p = 0.02). Between-group comparison of post-intervention scores confirmed a statistically significant difference in favour of the simulation group (F = 5.42; p = 0.029; Fig. 1).

Across all six OSCE stations, students in the simulation group obtained higher mean checklist scores (84.2 ± 8.1) than those receiving classroom instruction (77.5 ± 9.3, p = 0.018). The highest-scoring domains were rapport building and respectful discussion of sensitive topics, while active listening showed the greatest improvement relative to baseline (Table 2). Qualitative findings

The qualitative data collected through the semi-structured group interview provided insights into the students’ experiences and perceptions of their respective training approaches, revealing three major themes: learning effectiveness, course evaluation, and confidence levels. The single group interview format, in which all 24 participants were interviewed simultaneously, did not permit systematic group-by-group attribution of individual responses; participant codes (MS-01 to MS-24) are used throughout but group membership for each code cannot be disclosed without compromising anonymity within this small cohort. The representative quotes reported below were selected to reflect the breadth of perspectives expressed; students from the traditional instruction group similarly reported gains in awareness of the importance of communication, though they described a relative lack of opportunity to practise these skills in a realistic context.

Learning effectiveness

Students who received OSCE-based simulation consistently reported that this training provided a more realistic and practical learning experience compared to traditional classroom instruction. The interactive nature of the OSCEs, where students engaged directly with standardized patients in simulated dispute scenarios, was highlighted as a key factor in enhancing their learning.

One student noted, “OSCE feels much more like real-life practice. It’s one-on-one, and you don’t have your classmates around, so you’re really pushed to think on your feet about how to handle the situation” (MS-24). Another student said, “Before, we would just have a lecture with some slides, maybe a case study, but with OSCE, I actually had to face a patient and explain things. It made the learning stick much better” (MS-14).

Course evaluation

While the overall feedback was positive, students identified areas for improvement. Several participants noted that scenarios became repetitive, leading to a sense of predictability. One student mentioned, “The scenarios were good, but by the time we got to the last couple, it started to feel a bit formulaic. We knew what was coming and how to respond” (MS-04). This observation is educationally significant: the perceived formulaic quality of later stations is consistent with the interpretation that performance improvements may partly reflect format familiarity and rehearsal rather than deep skill acquisition. Another student suggested, “Some of the cases, like the central venous catheter one, were really tough. Maybe it would be better to start with something easier and build up to that” (MS-03).

Confidence levels

Students reported increased confidence in handling medical disputes. “After going through the OSCEs, I feel a lot more confident. If I am faced with a patient who is upset about something, I now have a better idea of how to communicate and defuse the situation” (MS-17). Another student reflected, “I realized that it’s not just about knowing the right medical answer but also about how you say it and how you listen to the patient’s concerns” (MS-14).

Discussion

This pilot quasi-experimental study provides preliminary evidence that students who underwent formative OSCE-based simulation achieved higher scores on measures of medico-legal communication and dispute prevention compared to those receiving traditional lecture-based instruction. While these findings are consistent with a beneficial effect of simulation-based familiarisation, they should be interpreted with considerable caution in light of the design limitations detailed below.

Practice effect and format familiarity — a critical alternative explanation: The OSCE-simulation group completed six formative stations in the identical format, duration, and assessment structure as the summative OSCE, whereas the traditional instruction group had no prior OSCE exposure. Students themselves acknowledged that by the final formative stations, the format had become ‘formulaic’ and predictable. A substantial body of literature documents that prior exposure to an assessment format produces performance gains independent of content learning — attributable to reduced assessment anxiety, familiarity with task demands, and optimised test-taking strategy rather than genuine skill development [20–22]. This study cannot disentangle authentic communicative skill development from rehearsal advantage, format familiarity, and reduced performance anxiety. Accordingly, claims of OSCE pedagogical superiority are not supported by this design, and all comparative findings should be understood as preliminary observations requiring confirmation in appropriately controlled studies.

From a theoretical perspective, the observed gains in the simulation group can be interpreted through Kolb’s experiential learning theory [10], which conceptualizes learning as a cyclical process involving concrete experience, reflective observation, abstract conceptualization, and active experimentation. However, the same format advantage that may explain performance differences also prevents attribution of gains to the experiential learning mechanism alone.

The findings resonate with the competency-based medical education (CBME) paradigm [23] and with evidence that structured communication training is associated with enhanced patient satisfaction and physician confidence [24]. Qualitative data revealed that students perceived OSCE-based learning as ‘realistic practice,’ consistent with situated learning theory [11], and described the development of adaptive expertise [25].

These findings are consistent with the growing body of literature on simulation-based professionalism and ethics education [7, 14, 15]. Practical challenges include resource-intensive delivery, requiring trained standardized patients, examiner calibration, and infrastructure [26]. Emerging digital solutions offer potential for scalable delivery [15, 27, 28].

Limitations

Several important limitations must be acknowledged. Findings should be treated as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

Non-randomised allocation: Students were allocated based on timetable assignments with no randomisation or concealment. Unmeasured differences in baseline communication skills or motivation cannot be excluded. Without randomisation, causal claims about educational superiority are unsupported.

Assessment format familiarity bias (rehearsal advantage): This is the most fundamental design confound. The simulation group practised in the exact OSCE format used for summative assessment; the traditional instruction group had no prior OSCE exposure. Observed performance differences may reflect rehearsal advantage and format familiarity rather than differential learning. This cannot be corrected post hoc.

Recommendations

Curricular integration

Institutions should consider embedding formative OSCE-based communication modules within longitudinal professionalism and ethics curricula, ensuring control comparators have equivalent format familiarity [29, 30].

Faculty development

Structured preparation for facilitating debriefing and providing feedback is essential [31, 32]. Examiner training should include procedures to minimise performance and expectation bias, including blinding to group allocation where feasible.

Hybrid and digital simulation

Virtual OSCEs and tele-simulation should be explored as adjuncts to face-to-face training [27, 28].

Program evaluation and research

Rigorous randomised studies with adequate sample sizes, blinded examiners, equivalent format exposure for both groups, and individual interview-based qualitative data are needed to isolate the independent pedagogical contribution of OSCE-based dispute-prevention training.

Conclusion

This pilot study provides preliminary evidence suggesting that formative OSCE-based simulation may support the development of medico-legal communication and dispute prevention competencies among senior medical students. However, the non-randomised design, rehearsal advantage, examiner non-blinding, and statistical fragility represent fundamental limitations that prevent causal conclusions about pedagogical superiority. The study contributes initial descriptive data and learner perceptions to inform the design of future, more rigorously controlled investigations.
