Effects of exercise interventions in hypoxic environment on cardiovascular function and maximal power output in obese or overweight individuals: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Ruinan Liu, Zehui Wen, Pengpeng Gou, Dong Han
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo compare the effects of normobaric hypoxic exercise versus normoxic exercise on improving cardiopulmonary and cardiovascular function and maximal power output in overweight and obese individuals, thereby providing evidence-based support for exercise prescription and public health interventions.MethodsFollowing the PRISMA guidelines, randomized controlled trials examining the effects of exercise interventions under hypoxic conditions on cardiopulmonary and cardiovascular function or exercise capacity in individuals with overweight or obesity were retrieved from databases comprising PubMed, the Cochrane Library, Ovid, Web of Science, Embase, and ClinicalTrials.gov. The search period extended from the inception of the databases to February 15, 2025. Stata was used to assess publication bias, heterogeneity, and data synthesis and generate funnel plots and forest plots. The DerSimonian–Laird random-effects model was applied for the meta-analysis. The I² statistic was used to quantify heterogeneity and subgroup, and sensitivity analyses were conducted to explore potential sources of heterogeneity and influencing factors.ResultsA total of 19 randomized controlled trials with 523 participants were included. A meta-analysis revealed that hypoxic training significantly enhanced maximal oxygen uptake (VO2max) and maximal power output (MPO) compared to normoxic training. The standardized mean differences (SMDs) were 0.42 (95% CI: 0.16–0.67, 95% PI: -0.10, 0.94, P < 0.01, I2 = 15.4%) for VO2max and 0.43 (95% CI: 0.01–0.85, 95% PI: -0.80,1.67, P < 0.05, I2 = 56.0%) for MPO. The corresponding weighted mean differences were 191.88 mL/min for VO2max (95% CI: 75.66–308.10; 95% PI: 31.68–352.08; P = 0.001) and 14.53 W for MPO (95% CI: 1.40–27.66; 95% PI: -3.51–32.57; P = 0.030). Subgroup analyses revealed that VO2max (SMD = 0.498, 95% CI: 0.077–0.919, P = 0.020, I2 = 0%) and MPO (SMD = 0.431, 95% CI: 0.012–0.851, P = 0.044, I2 = 0%) were significantly improved compared with the control group when the intervention duration exceeded 8 weeks. Additionally, significant improvements in VO2max were observed under the following conditions: fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2) ≧ 15% (SMD = 0.420, 95% CI: 0.057–0.784, P = 0.024, I2 = 12.1%), training frequency of more than three sessions per week (SMD = 0.534, 95% CI: 0.002–1.065, P = 0.049, I2 = 0%), high-intensity protocols (SMD = 0.532, 95% CI: 0.108–0.956, P = 0.014, I2 = 0%), session duration of less than 45 min (SMD = 0.420, 95% CI: 0.047–0.793, P = 0.027, I2 = 0%), and high-intensity interval training (HIIT) as the intervention (SMD = 0.532, 95% CI: 0.108–0.956, P = 0.014, I2 = 0%). Female participants showed significantly greater improvements in VO2max compared to the control group (SMD = 0.571, 95% CI: 0.128–1.014, P = 0.012, I2 = 0%).ConclusionsHypoxic exercise interventions effectively improved VO2max and MPO in individuals with overweight or obesity, with greater VO2max gains observed in women. Exercise interventions in hypoxic environments are recommended to last for longer than 8 weeks, with no more than three sessions per week of HIIT lasting less than 45 min.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-025-05271-w.

Introduction

The global prevalence of obesity has continued to rise. In 2021, approximately 2.11 billion adults were classified as overweight or obese, and this is projected to reach 3.8 billion by 2050 [1]. According to the World Health Organization’s body mass index (BMI)-based classification, overweight and obesity severity can be categorized as follows: (1) BMI ≥ 25 kg/m² (overweight); (2) BMI ≥ 30 and < 35 kg/m² (obese), BMI ≥ 35 and < 40 kg/m² (severely obese), and BMI ≥ 40 kg/m² (morbidly obese) [2]. Overweight and obesity significantly elevate the risk of developing various chronic diseases, particularly cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) such as atherosclerosis, hypertension, coronary heart disease, and heart failure [3–5]. Overweight and obese individuals often experience decreased cardiopulmonary function and excessive visceral fat accumulation, both of which are independent risk factors for CVDs [6, 7]. Being overweight or obese not only affects individuals’ athletic performance and physical function but may also impair their daily functional abilities [8, 9], thereby further reducing their quality of life and life expectancy [10–12]. Obesity-related complications lead to approximately 5 million deaths annually [13], and healthcare expenditures related to obesity prevention and treatment are projected to reach 3 trillion USD per year by 2030, placing severe strain on global healthcare systems and emerging as a critical challenge in global public health [14].

To date, no measures have successfully curbed the rising prevalence of overweight and obesity among adults. Without immediate and effective interventions, rates of overweight and obesity will continue to increase globally. Exercise intervention has been widely recognized as an essential strategy, alongside medication and dietary control, for improving health outcomes in overweight and obese populations [15, 16]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that exercise intervention effectively reduces BMI, enhances cardiorespiratory endurance, improves cardiovascular function, and promotes beneficial metabolic adaptations, thereby lowering the incidence and mortality risk of CVDs [17–19]. Cardiorespiratory fitness, typically measured by maximal oxygen uptake (VO₂max), inversely correlates with the risk of CVDs, obesity, and metabolic syndrome. Thus, increased VO₂max generally signifies a reduced risk of these conditions and improved overall health [20, 21]. Additionally, a higher resting heart rate significantly elevates the risk of cardiovascular and all-cause mortality [20–23]. Moreover, maximum heart rate (HRmax), a critical indicator reflecting cardiac reserve capacity and cardiovascular adaptability, signals poor cardiovascular health when abnormally low or high [24, 25]. Therefore, cardiovascular indicators such as VO₂max, blood pressure, resting heart rate (RHR), HRmax, and maximal power output (MPO) are crucial for assessing health risks in individuals with obesity.

Hypoxic training, an emerging form of exercise intervention under conditions of reduced oxygen availability, has recently progressively expanded from competitive sports into health promotion. Traditional hypoxic training was predominantly conducted at high altitudes to enhance aerobic metabolism and endurance performance. However, modern training technologies enable the simulation of low-oxygen environments at sea level by regulating inhaled oxygen concentration, a method termed normobaric hypoxic training [26]. Commonly applied fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2) values range from approximately 18.3% to 14.4%, which correspond to estimated altitudes of 1,000 to 3,000 m above sea level under normobaric conditions [27]. Natural and artificially induced hypoxia elicit similar physiological adaptations, including increased secretion of erythropoietin (EPO), elevated hemoglobin concentrations, improved vascular endothelial function, and enhanced aerobic metabolic efficiency [28–30]. These collectively result in improved cardiopulmonary fitness and athletic performance [26]. Related meta-analyses have indicated that normobaric hypoxic training can also effectively reduce body fat percentage in individuals with overweight or obesity, and the effects of hypoxic and normoxic training on lipid and glucose metabolism in individuals with overweight or obesity are the same [31]. Additionally, hypoxic training has significantly enhanced VO2max, cardiorespiratory fitness, and exercise performance in both healthy individuals and athletes [32–34]. However, considering the potential risks in individuals with overweight or obesity, such as heightened cardiovascular strain and dyspnea resulting from reduced oxygen availability [35], caution should be exercised when implementing hypoxic training in this population.

Systematic investigations regarding hypoxic training in overweight and obese populations remain relatively scarce, and its effectiveness has yet to reach a consensus. Preliminary research has explored the potential benefits of hypoxic training on body composition, metabolic parameters, and select cardiopulmonary indicators in this demographic. However, existing studies are limited by small sample sizes, inconsistent intervention parameters, and insufficiently comprehensive measures, impeding the formulation of clear and systematic conclusions. Specifically, comprehensive evidence remains lacking regarding crucial cardiopulmonary and cardiovascular indicators—such as VO₂max, blood pressure, RHR, and HRmax—as well as performance-related metrics, including MPO. Therefore, a systematic review and meta-analysis is necessary to synthesize current findings and compare the intervention effects of normobaric hypoxic exercise with those of normoxic exercise in improving cardiovascular fitness and MPO in overweight and obese populations. This comparison aims to provide evidence-based support for the development of exercise prescriptions and public health interventions in this area.

Methods

This study adhered to the guidelines and standards of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) [36] and was registered under number CRD42024598357.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria

(1) Only randomized controlled trials (RCTs) published in English were included. (2) The participants were overweight or obese individuals who were healthy and free of other diseases. (3) Participants were classified based on BMI: overweight (BMI ≥ 25 kg/m²) and obesity categories comprising obese (BMI ≥ 30 and < 35 kg/m²), severely obese (BMI ≥ 35 and < 40 kg/m²), and morbidly obese (BMI ≥ 40 kg/m²). If BMI was not provided in the study, participants were included based on a percentage of body fat (BF%; ≥30% for women and ≥ 25% for men). (4) The intervention types comprised aerobic exercise (AE; e.g., running, walking, cycling, yoga), resistance training (RT; e.g., free-weight training, machine-based resistance, elastic band resistance, bodyweight resistance, weightlifting), combined aerobic and resistance exercise (COM), interval training (IT; repeated bouts of relatively hard work interspersed with recovery periods of easier work or rest), and high-intensity interval training (HIIT; >6 metabolic equivalent of task, > 65% VO2max, > 77% HRmax, > 14/20 rating of perceived exertion, > 7/10 rating of perceived exertion). (5) Studies were included if they reported at least one cardiovascular function indicator (VO₂max, DBP, SBP, or heart rate [HR]) or a physical performance indicator (MPO) measured both before and after the intervention.

Exclusion criteria

(1) Theses, dissertations, conference proceedings, or abstracts without accessible full text. (2) Studies without extractable outcome data. (3) Duplicate publications. (4) Studies not reporting cardiovascular function or physical performance outcomes. (5) Animal experiments.

Literature search strategy

Relevant RCTs examining the effects of hypoxic exercise interventions on cardiovascular function and physical performance in overweight or obese populations were identified through comprehensive searches of the PubMed, Cochrane Library, Ovid, Web of Science, Embase and ClinicalTrials.gov. Databases were searched from their inception to February 15, 2025. The search strategy involved a combination of subject headings and free-text terms. Additionally, the reference lists of included articles were manually screened to identify further relevant studies. The search terms included “hypoxia,” “altitude,” “overweight,” “obesity,” “exercise,” “blood flow restriction therapy,” “endurance training,” “high-intensity interval training,” “jogging,” “marathon running,” “martial arts,” “movement,” “plyometric exercise,” “resistance training,” “running,” “sports,” “stair climbing,” “walking,” “yoga,” “randomized controlled trial,” and “RCT” (as shown in Supplementary material, Appendix S1). Only studies published in English were considered for inclusion in this review. This language restriction was applied to ensure accurate interpretation of study details, methodological quality, and outcome data.

Literature screening and data extraction

Two independent researchers conducted the literature screening and data extraction, followed by cross-verification. Discrepancies were resolved through consultation with a third reviewer. Efforts were made to contact authors to supplement missing data. Initially, titles and abstracts were reviewed to exclude irrelevant studies, followed by full-text screening to determine eligibility for inclusion. The data extracted primarily were the first author, year of publication, participants’ characteristics (sample size, sex, age, and BMI per group), intervention details (exercise type, duration, and frequency), and outcome measures.

Risk of bias assessment

Two independent reviewers assessed the risk of bias in the included studies using the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool for RCTs [37]. Any disagreements were resolved through arbitration by a third reviewer.

Certainty of evidence

The Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) methodology was used to evaluate the certainty of the evidence [38]. In the GRADE assessment, each research variable (e.g., SBP, DBP, RHR, HRmax, VO2 max, MPO) was treated as an independent outcome, and the evidence for each outcome was graded separately. The GRADE rating process considered five domains: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. The quality of evidence was classified into four categories: very low, low, moderate, and high.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses and graphical representations were performed using Stata 18.0 and RevMan 5.4 software. Standardized mean differences (SMDs) and weighted mean differences (WMDs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and 95% prediction intervals (PIs) were extracted from individual studies to calculate pooled effect sizes [39, 40]. Given the typically small sample sizes of included studies, Hedge’s g, corrected for bias using exact formulas, was selected as the measure of effect size. Effect sizes were interpreted as follows: trivial (0–0.2), small (0.2–0.5), moderate (0.5–0.8), and large (>0.8) [41]. Stata was used to assess publication bias, heterogeneity, and data synthesis, as well as to generate funnel plots and forest plots. The DerSimonian–Laird random-effects model was applied for the meta-analysis. Heterogeneity was assessed primarily through the I² statistic [42]. Subgroup and regression analyses were performed to explore sources of heterogeneity based on intervention characteristics (e.g., type, intensity, frequency, duration, and session length), FiO2%, and gender. Funnel plots [43] combined with Egger’s test [44] were used to detect publication bias; a P >0.05 indicated no publication bias. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Results

Literature search and selection process

A total of 7,499 articles were retrieved from the following sources: PubMed (n = 461), the Cochrane Library (n = 257), Ovid (n = 439), Web of Science (n = 1301), Embase (n = 1381), and ClinicalTrials.gov(n = 3663). Two additional articles were identified from other sources. After removing 1,349 duplicate records, 6,030 articles were excluded after reading the title and abstract. The full-text review resulted in the exclusion of 57 articles for the following reasons: no control group (n = 10), not a training intervention (n = 16), no focus on overweight or obesity (n = 13), inconsistent research design (n = 7), and unmatching result indicator (n = 11). Ultimately, 19 RCTs [45–63] were included in the analysis. The study selection process is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Basic characteristics of included studies

Among the 22 studies included in the 19 articles, the experimental group (EG) consisted of 272 participants, and the control group (CG) included 252 participants, totaling 524 individuals. Three studies included only male participants, nine studies involved only female participants, and the remaining studies included both male and female participants. Detailed study characteristics are reported in the Supplementary material, Table S1.

Risk of bias assessment of included studies

The quality of the included studies was evaluated using the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool 2. As shown in Fig. 2, among the 22 studies reported across the 19 included articles, seven studies reported adequate allocation concealment, and 13 reported the implementation of blinding. The remaining studies exhibited some degree of bias, primarily attributable to unclear randomization procedures and a lack of reported blinding during the experimental and measurement processes.

Certainty of evidence

The overall certainty of evidence was assessed using the GRADE tool, and the results are presented in Table S2 in the Supplementary material. According to the GRADE methodology, the certainty level for VO₂max was rated as low, and the certainty levels for the other outcomes were rated as very low.

These low certainty ratings primarily resulted from downgrades related to the risk of bias, inconsistency, and imprecision within the body of evidence. A substantial proportion of the included trials exhibited a high risk of bias, with many failing to adequately conceal treatment allocation. Additionally, several outcomes demonstrated substantial between-study heterogeneity, and the total sample sizes for the pooled analyses were relatively small (with no outcome exceeding 400 participants). Furthermore, the CIs for the pooled estimates were generally wide. Due to this combination of methodological limitations, including high risk of bias, inconsistency, and imprecision, the certainty of evidence for most outcomes was downgraded by multiple levels, ultimately resulting in a final rating of “very low.” The only exception was VO2max, which exhibited relatively consistent findings across studies and less heterogeneity. Therefore, VO2max underwent fewer downgrades and was assigned a slightly higher certainty rating of “low.”

Meta-analysis results

As shown in Fig. 3; Table 1 and Supplementary material Figure S1, the SMDs of VO2max (g = 0.42, 95% CI: [0.16, 0.67], 95% PI: [−0.10,0.94], P < 0.01) and MPO (g = 0.43, 95% CI: [0.01, 0.85], 95% PI: [−0.80,1.67], P < 0.05) were statistically significant. The SMDs of SBP (g = 0.12, 95% CI: [−0.30, 0.53], 95% PI: [−1.46,1.69], P = 0.581), DBP (g = 0.09, 95% CI: [−0.26, 0.44], 95% PI: [−1.18,1.36], P = 0.623), RHR (g = −0.25, 95% CI: [−0.75, 0.25], 95% PI: [−1.67,1.17], P = 0.320), and HRmax (g = 0.34, 95% CI: [−0.12, 0.81], 95% PI: [−1.01,1.70], P = 0.147) were not statistically significant.

The WMDs of VO2max (191.883 mL/min, 95% CI: [75.661, 308.104], 95% PI: [31.68, 352.08], P = 0.001) and MPO (14.529 W, 95% CI: [1.404,27.655], 95% PI: [−3.51, 32.57], P = 0.030) were statistically significant. The WMDs of SBP (1.102 mmHg, 95% CI: [−2.655, 4.858], 95% PI: [−4.13, 6.33], P = 0.565), DBP (0.856 mmHg, 95% CI: [−1.476, 3.188], 95% PI: [−2.39, 4.10], P = 0.472), RHR (−1.902 bpm, 95% CI: [−4.897, 1.093], 95%PI: [−5.98, 2.18], P = 0.213), and HRmax (5.131 bpm, 95% CI: [−1.521, 11.783], 95% PI: [−3.93,14.20], P = 0.131) were not statistically significant.

Sensitivity analysis results

After sequentially excluding studies with high heterogeneity, no significant differences were observed between hypoxic exercise interventions under normobaric conditions and the CG in terms of SBP (g = −0.14, 95% CI: [− 0.40, 0.11], P = 0.27, I² = 27.9%), DBP (g = 0.12, 95% CI: [−0.17, 0.40], P = 0.42, I² = 40.7%), RHR (g = −0.02, 95% CI: [−0.37, 0.33] P = 0.90, I² = 0%), or MPO (g = 0.72, 95% CI: [−0.03, 0.54], P = 0.80, I² = 36.2%; Supplementary material, Table 3. for details). In contrast, a significant difference was found for HRmax (g = 0.52, 95%CI: [0.20, 0.85], P = 0.001, I² = 40.2%).

No single study was found to disproportionately drive the VO₂max or MPO effects.

Subgroup analysis results

The subgroup analysis (as shown in Tables 2., 3., 4 and 5.) suggested that various factors, including hypoxic conditions, training program duration, training frequency, exercise intensity, session length, and intervention modality, influenced participants’ outcomes.

(1) Hypoxic conditions. When the EG’s hypoxic condition was FiO₂ < 15%, participants’ HRmax was higher than in the CG (g = 0.889, 95% CI 0.368–1.411; P = 0.001; I2 = 0%). When FiO₂ was ≥ 15%, participants’ VO₂max was higher in the EG compared to controls (g = 0.420, 95% CI 0.057–0.784; P = 0.024; I2 = 12.1%). (2) Intervention duration. When the intervention program lasted longer than 8 weeks, participants in the EG showed greater improvements in VO₂max (g = 0.498, 95% CI 0.077–0.919; P = 0.020; I2 = 0%) and MPO (g = 0.431, 95% CI 0.012–0.851; P = 0.044; I2 = 0%) compared to the CG. (3) Intervention frequency. When the intervention frequency was ≤ 3 sessions per week in the EG, participants exhibited higher VO₂max (g = 0.392, 95% CI 0.025–0.758; P = 0.036; I2 = 36.9%) and higher HRmax (g = 0.585, 95% CI 0.161–1.010; P = 0.007; I2 = 36.3%) compared to the CG. When the frequency exceeded three sessions per week, participants’ DBP (g = 0.089, 95% CI −0.264–0.441; P = 0.020; I2 = 0%) and VO₂max (g = 0.534, 95% CI 0.002–1.065; P = 0.049; I2 = 0%) improved relative to the CG. (4) Intervention intensity. In high-intensity intervention conditions, the EG demonstrated a higher VO₂max compared to the CG (g = 0.532, 95% CI 0.108–0.956; P = 0.014; I2 = 0%). (5) Intervention time. When the intervention time was less than 45 min, participants’ VO₂max improved significantly in the EG relative to controls (g = 0.420, 95% CI 0.047–0.793; P = 0.027; I2 = 0%). (6) Type of intervention. When the intervention type was HIIT, participants’ VO₂max was higher in the EG than in the CG (g = 0.532, 95% CI 0.108–0.956; P = 0.014; I2 = 0%). (7) Gender: Female participants’ VO₂max was higher in the EG than in the CG (g = 0.571, 95% CI 0.128–1.014; P = 0.012; I2 = 0%).

Subgroup analysis demonstrated that the gender of participants, intervention duration, frequency, time and FiO2% did change the results of the meta-analysis.

Discussion

This study systematically synthesized RCTs examining the effects of hypoxic exercise interventions on cardiovascular function and physical performance in overweight or obese populations. The meta-analysis showed that hypoxic training significantly improved VO₂max and MPO compared to normoxic training. However, no significant differences were observed for SBP, DBP, RHR, or HRmax.

Effects of hypoxic training on cardiovascular function and physical performance in overweight and obese populations

The meta-analysis results demonstrated that hypoxic exercise interventions produced significantly greater improvements in VO₂max and MPO compared to normoxic training in overweight and obese populations. These findings are consistent with previous studies [55, 64]. Notably, a study found that under equal exercise volumes, hypoxic HIIT resulted in twice the VO₂max improvements compared to normoxic HIIT [52]. Similar meta-analyses in athletes have also confirmed that hypoxic exercise provided greater enhancements in VO₂max and MPO compared to normoxic conditions [65, 66]. These findings collectively reinforce the effectiveness of hypoxic training in improving VO₂max and MPO. However, some studies have reported no significant differences. For example, one comparative study involving overweight and obese participants reported similar VO₂max improvements between hypoxic and normoxic HIIT groups, with no significant intergroup differences [57]. Additionally, other meta-analyses have noted that the advantages of hypoxic training may predominantly manifest in improved physical performance rather than weight loss or metabolic improvements [67].

Several mechanisms may underlie the improvements in VO₂max and MPO attributed to hypoxic training. First, hypoxic exercise has been reported to reduce body weight and body fat percentage in obese populations. This suggests that it can alter energy balance and increase metabolic rate, thereby enhancing basal energy expenditure [68, 69]. Second, hypoxic exposure stimulates EPO secretion. This boosts red blood cell production and increases hemoglobin concentrations, thereby enhancing oxygen-carrying capacity, oxygen delivery, and ultimately, aerobic metabolism [70–73]. Otherwise, increased hemoglobin will increase the blood viscosity and may increase the risk of thrombosis. Additionally, hypoxia increases the accumulation of hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α), a key transcription factor regulating hypoxic adaptations. Activation of HIF-1α elevates the expression of downstream genes such as vascular endothelial growth factor, stimulating capillary growth and increasing myoglobin content in skeletal muscle. These adaptations enhance oxygen extraction efficiency, glycolytic enzyme activity, mitochondrial density, and glucose transporter 4 expression, thereby improving glucose utilization and energy metabolism and ultimately increasing VO₂max and MPO [74–76]. Third, hypoxic training induces metabolic adaptations in muscle cells through the upregulation of pathways such as peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-γ coactivator-1α. This promotes mitochondrial biogenesis and improves mitochondrial function, critical for increasing muscle oxygen utilization efficiency and energy production [77].

Hypoxic training also elevates HR, induces peripheral vasodilation, increases arteriolar diameter, and enhances the oxygen affinity of hemoglobin. At the pulmonary level, hypoxic exercise improves alveolar–capillary diffusion capacity [60]. Collectively, these adaptations compensate for reduced oxygen availability by enhancing oxygen delivery and use efficiency, thereby significantly improving VO₂ max and MPO in overweight and obese populations.

In contrast, the current meta-analysis found no significant differences between hypoxic and normoxic exercise interventions in SBP, DBP, RHR, or HRmax. Several factors may contribute to these findings. First, variations in study duration or intervention periods might influence outcomes. Short-term hypoxic exposure transiently elevates blood pressure and HR, which subsequently return to baseline levels, indicating physiological self-regulation in response to hypoxic stress [78]. Thus, inadequate or repetitive exposure might facilitate rapid cardiovascular adaptation, reducing long-term differences between hypoxic and normoxic conditions [79]. Second, differences in exercise intensity and relative workloads may play a role. Studies have suggested that at equivalent HR outputs, absolute workloads are lower under hypoxic conditions, reducing musculoskeletal strain despite similar metabolic demands [80, 81]. Therefore, although relative intensity may appear identical, the absolute physiological stress might be diminished in hypoxic conditions, potentially minimizing cardiovascular differences compared to normoxia [51]. Furthermore, cardiovascular responses to hypoxia exhibit threshold and individual variations. Research indicates that increasing hypoxia severity progressively reduces HRmax [82], implying that despite achieving the same relative HRmax percentages, hypoxic groups experience lower actual HRmax and workloads. Additionally, acute hypoxia induces temporary sympathetic excitation and elevated RHR, which normalize after a few days of adaptation. These fluctuations reflect an automatic regulatory threshold for oxygen saturation changes; when stimuli do not surpass this threshold, additional cardiovascular adaptations may not occur. Many studies included in meta-analyses involve participants with normal baseline blood pressure, whose modest post-intervention reductions may converge with those of normoxic groups, diminishing observable differences [83]. Finally, methodological variations among studies affect the reliability of findings. Differences in intervention frequency, intensity prescription methods, sample sizes, and baseline heterogeneity contribute to potential biases. Intensity prescriptions based on HRmax or VO₂max percentages vary among individuals due to hypoxia-induced reductions in HRmax [67, 82]. Additionally, limited sample sizes and group heterogeneity may decrease statistical power. Collectively, these methodological factors may explain the observed absence of significant differences in cardiovascular indicators (SBP, DBP, RHR, and HRmax) between hypoxic and normoxic exercise interventions in overweight and obese populations.

Effects of various hypoxic exercise protocols on VO₂max, SBP, DBP, RHR, HRmax, and MPO in overweight and obese populations

Subgroup analyses revealed significant differences in the effects of hypoxic exercise interventions with varying durations, intensities, and oxygen concentrations on VO₂max in overweight and obese individuals. When the FiO₂ was greater than 15%, hypoxic exercise significantly improved VO₂max. However, interventions with FiO₂ less than 15% did not yield significant improvements. This outcome may be because the FiO₂ concentration is critical for improving VO₂max; excessively low oxygen levels at higher altitudes may inadvertently reduce exercise intensity, thus offsetting the adaptive benefits of hypoxia. Interestingly, training with FiO₂ concentrations below 15% significantly increased participants’ HRmax, indicating that stronger hypoxic stimuli were more effective in enhancing cardiovascular adaptations and sympathetic responsiveness, thereby elevating HRmax during exercise [84, 85]. Conversely, training at FiO₂ ≥15% did not significantly influence HRmax. These findings confirm that substantial hypoxic stimulation is necessary to evoke pronounced cardiovascular adaptations.

Regarding intervention duration, studies showed significant improvements in VO₂max and MPO among overweight and obese participants when hypoxic exercise lasted more than 8 weeks, whereas shorter durations produced no significant effects. These results suggest a cumulative adaptation effect: the body requires sufficient exposure to hypoxia over extended periods to facilitate substantial physiological adjustments [78], ultimately translating into enhanced physical performance [86]. Supporting evidence suggests that prolonged hypoxic training effectively induces adaptations, including increased cardiac output, higher capillary density, and elevated skeletal muscle myoglobin levels [87–89], all of which require several weeks to months to manifest noticeable effects [32]. In contrast, shorter-duration interventions may only trigger initial physiological adaptations, such as increased blood plasma volume, but fail to significantly enhance muscle metabolic capacity or improve output performance. Consequently, extending the duration of hypoxic training (>8 weeks) is more beneficial for improving VO₂max and MPO, whereas shorter programs are less effective.

This studies have shown that hypoxic exercise significantly improves VO₂max in overweight and obese individuals, regardless of training frequency. This suggests that training approximately three times per week is sufficient to induce aerobic adaptations under low-oxygen conditions, with no additional benefits from higher frequencies of training. This may be because individuals who are overweight or obese often have reduced capillary density and impaired mitochondrial function in their skeletal muscles [90]. This results in inefficient oxygen delivery and utilization, leading to a lower baseline VO₂max and making improvements through exercise interventions more pronounced [91].

Individuals who are overweight or obese have a greater potential for improvement and often experience significant gains through exercise interventions, even with as few as 2–3 sessions of moderate-to-high-intensity training per week. This results in significant enhancements in VO2max. A minor yet substantial decrease in DBP has been observed in instances where the weekly training frequency is more than three sessions. Conversely, no substantial change in DBP is observed when the frequency is ≤ 3 sessions per week. Moreover, existing evidence suggests that an insufficient level of physical activity may compromise the effectiveness of blood pressure reduction [92]. The implementation of regular and consistent exercise training is foundational to achieving a substantial and enduring reduction in blood pressure levels. Conversely, the performance of exercise every week, limited to 1–2 sessions, has been observed to fall short of yielding comparable benefits [93].

One study found that low exercise frequency did not significantly improve vascular function in obese individuals, whereas regular aerobic exercise interventions of ≥ 3 sessions per week led to moderate improvements in vascular function [94]. This suggests that a training frequency of more than three times per week is necessary to improve vascular function and subsequently reduce blood pressure in obese populations. Higher-frequency hypoxic training may be more beneficial for improving blood pressure due to the post-exercise hypotensive effect. This effect is characterized by the induction of vasodilation and sympathetic inhibition, resulting in a decrease in blood pressure for several hours after exercise [95]. High-frequency exercise has been demonstrated to prolong the post-exercise hypotensive effect, enhance endothelial function and vascular resistance, and cumulatively result in a greater reduction in blood pressure [96–99]. It has also been demonstrated to be efficacious in reducing arterial stiffness, thereby enhancing endothelial function [100, 101]. Therefore, training frequencies exceeding three times per week may yield more pronounced enhancements in vascular function, blood volume regulation, and autonomic nervous system balance when compared with low-frequency exercise (2–3 times per week). This may result in a more substantial long-term reduction in DBP.

Significant enhancements in HRmax were observed in the group exercising ≤ 3 times per week, whereas no such effect was found in the higher-frequency group. Excessive frequency of training is ineffective in providing additional benefits; conversely, a moderate frequency (2–3 times per week) is more conducive to heart rate adaptation. Frequent high-intensity hypoxic training has been demonstrated to induce cumulative fatigue and sympathetic overactivation, which may blunt the heart’s positive adaptation to exercise. Conversely, moderate-frequency training facilitates adequate recovery and super-compensation, thereby enhancing the maximal heart rate response [102]. The intensity, duration, and frequency of hypoxic exercise interventions should be balanced to ensure beneficial adaptations rather than counterproductive outcomes. Excessive exercise interventions have been shown to impose a burden on the cardiovascular system. Therefore, rational control of the program is crucial for achieving optimal training outcomes [103]. Additionally, the baseline status of the participants must be considered; this includes individuals with relatively weak cardiopulmonary function, who exhibit a greater capacity to increase their HRmax following hypoxic training [82].

Research has shown that the intensity of training is a crucial factor in improving VO₂max. In comparison with low-intensity exercise, high-intensity training (such as HIIT) has been shown to significantly increase VO2max. In contrast, low-intensity training has been demonstrated to have no significant effect [104]. This may be attributed to the capacity of high-intensity training to markedly enhance VO2max, concomitantly augmenting left ventricular ejection fraction and stroke volume [105]. Conversely, in a hypoxic environment, low-intensity training cannot fully stimulate the heart to increase stroke volume [106, 107] due to an insufficient cardiovascular load. Therefore, its effect on improving maximal cardiac output and VO2max is limited. High-intensity training has been shown to significantly increase VO2max by strengthening the heart’s pumping capacity (resulting in higher stroke volume and cardiac output). In contrast, low-intensity exercise lacks such strong stimuli and thus shows no significant increase in performance.

HIIT has been shown to produce greater improvements in VO₂max in overweight and obese individuals compared with moderate-intensity continuous training [104, 108]. The underlying mechanism may involve HIIT eliciting greater cardiac output and oxygen utilization in skeletal muscle, thereby inducing more pronounced adaptations in stroke volume and capillary density [109]. This may be because HIIT under hypoxic conditions, despite its short duration, provides a sufficient stimulus. The combination of high intensity and short duration enhances cardiovascular function, thereby improving VO₂max [110].

In terms of training duration, improvements in VO₂max were more pronounced in participants whose individual training sessions lasted less than 45 min. This suggests that hypoxia-inducible factors activated by hypoxic conditions can synergistically interact with exercise stimuli [111], thereby enabling improvements in VO₂max even without fully relying on absolute training intensity. This may be because HIIT, although shorter in duration, provides a sufficient stimulus for improvement. In contrast, prolonged sessions exceeding 45 min tend to be of relatively lower intensity, making it difficult to improve VO₂max further.

Participants’ characteristics (e.g., gender) may also influence their response to hypoxic interventions. Subgroup analyses revealed that the EG had a significantly higher VO2max than the CG among female participants. This suggests that overweight or obese women may derive greater aerobic benefit from hypoxic training compared to men. Potential mechanisms may involve sex-specific differences in oxygen delivery, hemoglobin concentration, and hormonal regulation.

In individuals with overweight or obesity, the interaction between hypoxic conditions (e.g., FiO2 concentration) and the intervention of frequency, intensity, and duration, along with individual physiological responses, is complex. Recent studies have demonstrated that integrating artificial intelligence into predictive health models improves diagnostic accuracy, personalizes treatment approaches, and facilitates informed clinical decision-making [112]. Therefore, predictive models incorporating AI provide a practical means to analyze individual cardiopulmonary data, allowing for more tailored exercise prescriptions and thereby enhancing the precision and overall effectiveness of interventions and patient outcomes.

This study has some potential limitations. (1) It included only English-language publications, which may have introduced a risk of language bias by excluding relevant studies published in other languages. Additionally, the gray literature was not searched, which may have further limited the comprehensiveness of the search. (2) Although the discussion offers plausible biological mechanisms for improved VO2max and MPO under hypoxia, these conclusions should be interpreted cautiously due to the relatively small sample sizes of the 19 included studies and the substantial heterogeneity among them. (3) The absence of long-term follow-up is also a limitation. Whether the improvements in VO2max induced by hypoxic training can be maintained in the long term and translated into sustained clinical health benefits, such as a reduced risk of cardiovascular events, remains unclear.

This meta-analysis focused on cardiopulmonary function tests. Other laboratory parameters were not considered. Future research should increase the sample size, measure other laboratory parameters, and follow up for at least 1 year. Additionally, integrating additional indicators is recommended to allow for a thorough evaluation of exercise interventions in hypoxic conditions. This will further elucidate the supplementary benefits that hypoxic conditions provide. Research endeavors must concentrate on optimizing the design of exercise interventions under hypoxic conditions. Additionally, strictly implementing and reporting randomization, blinding, and long-term follow-up procedures is necessary to minimize bias. After this, a comparison of factors such as intervention intensity, duration, frequency, and training cycles should be conducted to examine the differential effects of various exercise intervention regimens on cardiopulmonary function under hypoxic conditions. Concomitantly, given the scarcity of research on the dose–response relationship of exercise intensity under hypoxic conditions, future studies should prioritize determining the optimal training intensity and regimen for hypoxic environments. Furthermore, the feasibility of normobaric hypoxic environments in real-world settings and their accessible application pathways should be further explored, including the use of small hypoxic generators or altitude simulation masks for safe hypoxic training in home environments. These initiatives would enhance the accessibility and sustainability of normobaric hypoxic exercise interventions in daily fitness, reduce implementation costs, and increase public acceptance of these interventions. In-depth mechanistic studies are imperative to elucidate the potential mechanisms by which normobaric hypoxic exercise interventions enhance cardiopulmonary metabolic function. The role of hypoxic exercise interventions in the fields of sports science and clinical medicine will be more precisely defined, providing more evidence-based practical guidance.

Conclusion

Exercise interventions conducted in hypoxic environments are important for improving VO2max and MPO in individuals with overweight or obesity, with improvements in VO2max being particularly significant in female participants. Such individuals should engage in exercise interventions in hypoxic conditions lasting more than 8 weeks. To enhance VO2max, HIIT should be no more than three times per week, with each session lasting less than 45 min. For improving MPO, interventions in hypoxic environments should extend beyond 8 weeks.
