Non-traditional barriers to participation in pharmacologic chronic kidney disease clinical trials
Authors: Gabriel Cojuc-Konigsberg, Alan Braverman-Poyastro, Vianca Anabel Canaviri-Flores, Alejandra De Las Fuentes Cepeda, Briana Rodriguez-Paniagua, Andrea Mizrahi-Drijanski, Alberto Guijosa, Alberto Nordmann-Gomes, Alberto Moscona-Nissan, Ricardo Correa-Rotter, Juan C Ramírez-Sandoval

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Agenda for Sustainable Development agenda prioritizes access to care for non-communicable diseases, including chronic kidney disease (CKD), and sustainable cities and communities. City-level health may impact local research capacity and subsequent participation in randomized controlled trials (RCTs). We identified city-level barriers to CKD RCT participation in the cities from the Global Observatory of Healthy and Sustainable Cities (GOHSC).MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional study of 214 drug CKD RCTs (2003–2023) with participants spanning 146 GOHSC sites. We queried open datasets to retrieve relevant financial, urban, healthcare, and nephrology-related indicators. We designed a CKD research capacity scale incorporating six variables: population-weighted RCTs, sites per sqkm, funding, site span, facility type, RCTs per site. We classified research capacity as absent (0–1 points), insufficient (2–4), fair (6–7), good (7–8), or excellent (9–10) and compared indicators between research capacity categories.ResultsOf the included cities, Odense had the highest population-weighted RCT count (7.01 RCTs per 100,000 population), and Mexico City had the lowest (0.06 RCTs per 100,000 population). Cities with good or excellent research capacity had higher health expenditures, nephrologists, and dialysis centers per million population than cities with absent or insufficient research capacity. In cities with good or excellent research capacity, the most prevalent type of insurance coverage for CKD was public, whereas mixed financing was the most frequent type in cities with absent or insufficient research capacity. Among the identified non-traditional barriers to CKD RCT participation, daily living score, access to public transport, access to open spaces, and access to a fresh food market were higher in cities with good or excellent research capacity, compared to those with lower research capacity scores.ConclusionsWe found differences in CKD research capacity across the cities included in the GOHSC Study Collaboration, which may be affected by financial, socio-demographic, healthcare, and nephrology-related barriers. Background

Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) target 3.4, proposed by the United Nations (UN) in 2015, aims to reduce premature mortality from non-communicable diseases (NCDs). [1] Among NCDs, chronic kidney disease (CKD) is an increasingly prevalent condition, projected to become the fifth leading cause of mortality worldwide by 2040[2, 3]. However, despite their significant healthcare, financial, and quality of life burden, kidney diseases remain relatively under-recognized[2, 4]. Moreover, access to advanced CKD care worldwide is substantially hindered by income, geographic, and socio-demographic disparities[5, 6].

In accordance with SDG Target 3.b, which focuses on supporting research and development of medicines for NCDs [1], prioritizing CKD research access is imperative. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) are the cornerstone of evidence-based guideline development and, in some circumstances, represent the only opportunity for people with CKD to access novel disease-modifying treatments. [7] Nonetheless, considerable gaps exist in RCT participation and research capacity, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), attributed to multiple global, regional, national, and local factors, including financial investment, public policy, and workforce availability. [8, 9] Additionally, often neglected determinants, such as access to transportation, provider trust, and lack of transparency, might influence individual participation in RCTs. [10–12] These barriers, combined with the challenges in obtaining timely quality care for CKD, especially in cases of kidney failure, could also influence RCT enrollment.[13, 14]

In addition to SDG 3, SDG 11 [1]—sustainable cities and communities—holds considerable potential for improving kidney health by mitigating the risk of NCDs and enhancing access to CKD care. [4, 15–17] (Box 1) Despite numerous measurable healthcare and nephrology-specific indicators emerging as standards for decision-making and policy implementation [6, 18], most are broad and overlook local heterogeneity. These local differences are crucial intervention targets to improve kidney care. [19, 20] Notably, the Global Observatory of Healthy and Sustainable City (GOHSC) Indicator Study Collaboration offers a comprehensive dataset of standardized, city-level, spatially designed urban health indicators that serve as a benchmark for policymaking and progress monitoring.[21, 22].

In this study, we aimed to explore previously unidentified potential barriers to CKD RCT participation in the cities included in the GOHSC Indicator Study Collaboration. [21] We hypothesized that various urban, financial, socio-demographic, healthcare, and nephrology-related metrics would directly impact RCT involvement.

Methods

We conducted a cross-sectional study evaluating CKD RCTs published between 2003 and 2023 to assess the financial, urban, socio-demographic, healthcare, and nephrology-related barriers to CKD RCT participation in the 25 cities from the GOHSC Indicator Study Collaboration. [21] We obtained RCT data from our previously published systematic review of MEDLINE (search date October 4, 2023, updated on December 5, 2023), evaluating the global distribution and disparities in pharmacological RCTs in adults with CKD. [9] For the present study, we selected CKD RCTs that included participants from the GOHSC cities. The initial iteration of the GOHSC collaboration included publicly available data for 25 global and diverse cities, which were selected by an international investigator panel, with plans to expand to 1000 cities worldwide. [21, 22] The dataset was the result of a global research initiative that employed geospatial analytical tools to develop indicators aiming to inform policy to develop city-level health and sustainability interventions. [21, 22]

We extracted the following additional variables for each RCT: city name, site count, site names, site postal codes, and site category (primary, secondary, or tertiary care). When site names were unavailable, we used postal codes as a surrogate to discern whether the recruitment site in question differed from the other identified sites.

We obtained indicators from various sources to understand the potential barriers to CKD RCT participation. We extracted the latest available open data city-level variables from the GOHSC [21], national-level healthcare-related metrics from the World Health Organization [18] and World Bank [23], and nephrology-related metrics from the International Society of Nephrology (ISN)-Global Kidney Health Atlas. [6] We categorized countries based on ISN regions [6], World Bank income group [24], and socio-demographic index (SDI) group. [25] Additionally, we calculated city-level kidney care indicators, estimating the number of nephrologists and dialysis centers per city by extrapolating national-level data to the assessed cities. For example, to estimate the number of nephrologists per city, we multiplied the nephrologists per million population [6] by the city’s population. [21] Applying this formula to Mexico City, we multiplied the population (20.8 million) by the country’s nephrologist count (10.45 per million), resulting in an estimated 211.26 nephrologists in the city. Using the information retrieved from RCTs and open data sources, we calculated multiple other indices reflecting access to CKD RCTs and care. Detailed data on the extracted variables and calculations are presented in the supplementary methods.

In addition, we developed a three-point CKD research capacity scale utilizing six variables (Table 1).We applied this scale to the 25 evaluated cities and categorized the results of research capacity as: absent (0–1 points), insufficient (2–4 points), fair/average (5–6 points), good (7–8), or excellent (9–10 points).

This study’s carefully designed pilot CKD research capacity scale incorporates six local city-level factors, each contributing to the overall research capacity. First, the number of CKD RCTs adjusted to the population, a measure of scientific productivity. Second, the number of recruiting sites also adjusted to the population reflects accessibility and infrastructure. Third, the proportion of RCTs not funded by industry provides a subjective evaluation of independent research. Fourth, the proportion of local or national CKD RCTs is an additional indicator of independent capabilities. Fifth, the proportion of participating primary and secondary care facilities represents the strength of a health system vis-à-vis research; and finally, the number of RCTs per site along the years evaluated, a metric encompassing productivity, infrastructure, and the prioritization of research. The set of items to establish a CKD research capacity scale was selected based on relevance and availability of information, following a subjective professional consensus among the authors. Some important domains could not be included due to data limitations: (1) RCT size per center, as data on individual site recruitment were unavailable; (2) RCT recruitment per site; (3) site accessibility (catchment radius); and (4) number of independent investigators. Sites were classified according to RCT reporting, which may have grouped multiple primary care clinics under a single larger health network. We considered national RCTs to be partially representative of independent research capabilities—although we acknowledge that they may not reflect local capacity to the same extent—because a large proportion of national RCTs are driven by local resources and reflect a country’s support for research infrastructure at smaller scales. This score was developed as a pilot and has not been previously used or validated.

We descriptively compared urban, financial, socio-demographic, healthcare, and nephrology-related variables with the CKD research capacity scale categories.

We used frequencies and percentages for categorical variables and medians with ranges or interquartile ranges (IQR) for quantitative variables. Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS 24.0 (IBM, New York, USA), GraphPad Prism 9.5.0 (GraphPad Software, California, USA), and Tableau Software (Salesforce Company, California, USA).

Results

The original search strategy [9] returned 1,366 eligible records, of which we included 214 CKD RCTs with participation from at least one recruiting site from the cities in the GOHSC Indicator Study Collaboration (Fig. 1, Table S1). Of the included RCTs, 206 (96%) reported the number, name, and location of the recruiting sites per city. CKD RCTs have been conducted in 22/25 (88%) of the cities of interest in 176 research sites (S2). The remaining three cities from the GOHSC, Hanoi, Maiduguri, and Vic, have not participated in pharmacological CKD RCTs.

Study selection flowchart. We included 214 randomized controlled trials with participation of at least one site from the cities included in the global observatory of healthy and sustainable cities collaboration

Of the 214 RCTs meeting our inclusion criteria, 65 (30%) were local RCTs (conducted in one city), 46 (22%) national (conducted in two or more cities within the same country), 18 (8%) regional (conducted in two or more countries within the same region), and 85 (40%) global (conducted in two or more regions). Most local RCTs were conducted in a single center (n = 57/65, 88%). Interestingly, Bangkok, Hong Kong, and Seattle performed local multi-center RCTs. The predominant primary source of funding for RCTs was industry (n = 127, 59%), while 44 (21%) were supported by academia, 6 (3%) by government, 29 (13%) had mixed funding, and 8 (4%) did not report the funding source. Of the 146/176 (83%) sites with available information, 8 (6%), 41 (28%), and 97 (66%) were primary, secondary, and tertiary care facilities, respectively (S3).

Bangkok had the overall highest CKD RCT count (n = 41), and Sao Paulo had the highest participating site count (n = 28). When adjusting the RCT count to the overall population, Odense (7.01 RCTs per 100,000 population) and Ghent (5.16 RCTs per 100,000 population) had the highest numbers, while Chennai (0.06 RCTs per 100,000 population) and Mexico City (0.11 RCTs per 100,000 population) had the lowest. This ranking was similar when adjusting the RCT count to the estimated CKD prevalence. [2] Cities like Belfast and Melbourne, despite having a moderate prevalence-population-weighted number of RCTs, showed a high number of RCTs adjusted for the CKD population. Notably, Odense and Ghent also had the highest RCTs per estimated nephrologist (2.77 and 2.45, respectively), CKD stage 5 RCTs per estimated dialysis center (8.26 and 2.72, respectively), and RCTs per site (11.0 and 9.0, respectively), indicators of CKD research capacity and site productivity. In contrast, Cologne had the fewest RCTs per estimated nephrologist (0.10), Sao Paulo had the fewest RCTs per site (0.89), and Baltimore had the fewest CKD stage 5 RCTs per estimated dialysis center among cities with at least one CKD stage 5 RCT (0.03).

According to the CKD research capacity scale, 3 (12%), 2 (8%), 9 (36%), 10 (40%), and 1 (4%) cities were deemed to have absent, insufficient, fair/average, good, and excellent research capacity, respectively (Fig. 2). As expected, when exploring financial differences between cities categorized based on CKD research capacity, we observed that, compared to cities with absent or insufficient research capacity, cities with good or excellent research capacity had a higher health expenditure per capita (median $5680.2 [IQR 2866.4–6780.1] Vs. $173.6 [IQR 83.8–610.6), and a higher domestic general government health expenditure per capita (median $3946.3 [IQR 2234.5–4946.2] Vs. $73.7 [IQR 25.3–305.9]). Socio-demographic indicators, such as literacy and unemployment rates, were similar between groups (Figure S1).

Despite CKD prevalence being similar between categories, certain healthcare and nephrology-related indicators were associated with increased CKD research capacity (Fig. 3). Cities in the good or excellent research capacity categories had more nephrologists (median 23.1 [IQR 21.3–37.4] Vs. 3.09 [IQR 1.8–10.4]) per million population and dialysis centers per also per million population (median 6.6 [IQR 5.9–11.4) Vs. 1.2 [IQR 0.8–3.1]), as well as medical doctors (median 44.4 [IQR 36.3–50.2] Vs. 8.3 [IQR 7.3–24.4]) and researchers in R&D (median 4584 [IQR 3871–6182] Vs. 358 [IQR 260–779]) than those in the absent or insufficient categories.

We calculated several novel city-level nephrology-related indicators to understand local differences in CKD care and research access (Fig. 4, S4). There were considerable differences in many of these indicators when comparing cities based on research capacity (Figure S2). Notably, coverage for CKD care was different between groups. In cities with good or excellent research capacity, the predominant form of CKD care coverage was public for both non-dialysis-dependent CKD (n = 10/11, 91%) and kidney replacement therapy (n = 10/11, 91%), as opposed to cities with absent or insufficient research capacity, where mixed financing was the most frequent type of coverage for both non-dialysis-dependent CKD care (n = 3/5, 60%) and kidney replacement therapy (n = 2/5, 40%).

Since in-person attendance of patients to RCT activities and evaluations is critical for enrollment, we compared urban Global Observatory of Healthy and Sustainable Cities indicators with CKD research capacity (Fig. 5). There was a signal regarding different non-traditional variables related to access and participation in RCTs. Variables such as daily living score, access to regular public transport, access to open spaces, and access to fresh food markets were apparently higher in cities with more research capacity. Meanwhile, except for Hong Kong, population density, walkable neighborhood density, intersection count, and sample point count seemed to be inversely related to research capacity.

Finally, we assessed selected indicators across income groups [24], socio-demographic index categories [25], and ISN regions. [6] As expected, and as presented in Table S5, cities within high-income economies performed better in most indicators than those within middle-income economies. A similar pattern was observed in cities within countries with high or high-middle SDI since the indicators showed a consistently superior difference to those with lower indices.

Discussion

This study evaluated CKD research output and factors related to research capacity in the 25 cities included in the GOHSC. [21] Based on information gathered from more than 200 RCTs and multiple open datasets, we observed that urban, financial, socio-demographic, healthcare, and nephrology-related factors may influence research capacity.

It is well-established that RCTs are prone to biases, including initial sample selection bias, which arises when the sample of participants is not truly of the general population. [26] Our findings underscore the growing emphasis on enhancing infrastructure, knowledge, and competencies necessary to conduct global RCTs, as outlined in the SDG. [27] In nephrology, this trend is no exception, as local, regional, and global collaborative platforms have been created to broaden the scope and impact of RCTs.[28–30]

Measuring the success of newly established research frameworks, a critical aspect in any enterprise, remains challenging. Multiple organizations and researchers have proposed the development of measurable kidney health indicators, in line with other international proposals such as the International Council for Harmonization and the International Organization for Standardization (ISO) 9001 methodology for quality management, ISO 14,155:2020 for the investigation of medical devices, and ISO 37,120:2018 for indicators on city services and quality of life. [6, 31–36] Moreover, the ISN-Global Kidney Health Atlas survey presented astonishing results and gaps in nephrology research capacity across regions and income groups, highlighting the necessity for effective initiatives to empower local researchers and agencies to improve global collaboration. [8] However, despite its remarkable contributions, survey-inherent limitations and heterogeneity between the described groups do not fulfill the extensive basis required to strengthen local research. Therefore, in our study, we attempted to investigate additional factors that could contextualize research capacity using comprehensive, easily accessible indicators and provide a relatively simple, measurable CKD research capacity scale to serve as an initial model for further elaboration. Complementing the previously detailed scale, we calculated a pilot series of useful city-level indicators of CKD care and research capacity based on the GOHSC, ISN-GKHA, and GBD collaborations. [2, 6, 21] These tools offer an integral perspective on two intricately related aspects of SDG 3: access to CKD care and research. Moreover, the CKD research capacity score highlights that reports of large clinical trials should include additional factors that are critical for assessing each center, such as RCT size per site, recruitment metrics, site accessibility, and the number of independent investigators.

Ensuring equitable, timely, and access to kidney health, as urged by the ISNs kidney health for all agenda, is a multilayered process encompassing hurdles at the global, regional, national, city, community, and individual levels. [19, 20] While significant efforts have been made to prioritize CKD care in the public health arena and tackle worldwide disparities [37], local barriers are critical to address this major health problem. Building on previous studies underlining gender-, ethnicity-, region-, and income-based disparities in CKD care and clinical research [5, 9, 38–40], in the present study, adopting a unique approach focusing on the GOHSC, we outlined additional non-traditional local financial, urban, socio-demographic, and health-related obstacles that might deter the conduction and performance of RCT and additionally, may complicate enrollment.

In other NCDs, particularly cancer, several administrative, financial, logistical, infrastructural, and navigational barriers have been identified as hindrances affecting RCT participation. In addition, few RCTs are designed for community-based settings. [41, 42] These difficulties are likely comparable in people with CKD, as portrayed by Crews et al. and Hossein et al., who describe determinants of CKD and its prognosis among vulnerable populations. [14, 43] Moreover, other social determinants, such as marital status, employment, and education level, have been recognized as factors leading to suboptimal kidney health and could be linked to limited RCT enrollment.[13]

Unhealthy and unsustainable cities are also closely related to NCDs, including CKD, and promoting healthy lifestyles might attenuate modifiable risk factors for this condition. [4, 17, 44] In the current epidemiological landscape in which NCDs are becoming progressively prevalent, policies fostering healthy city planning and city-level policies to control the burden of NCDs are essential, and promoting research is a crucial aspect in this regard, particularly in LIC where research is totally absent, and in LMICs, where it is often insufficient. [45–47] As intended by the GOHSC, the development of indicators related to urban health should be accompanied by monitoring and policymaking.[46]

Our study has limitations. Among these, the limited number of cities from the GOHSC with currently available open data is one of them. In addition, the size of the sample restricts our capability to conduct more comprehensive descriptive and statistical analyses to compare cities, yet we must remember that this represents the totality of pharmacologically related RCTs on CKD during almost 20 years. We opted to utilize the GOHSC data rather than other datasets, such as UN-Habitat, because it provides explicit data acquisition and measurement standards while addressing within-city heterogeneity, fundamental considerations when implementing local data-driven interventions. [22, 48] Given that we only included urban areas, our results may not be generalizable to other cities worldwide, as well as rural areas, and future research is required to assess CKD research capacity on a globally representative scale and in underserved areas, which may face additional barriers to research participation. We designed the CKD research capacity scale as a pilot to present a novel idea for exploration and broader development. Therefore, the proposed scale requires external validation—and possible consequential adaptations—employing a more representative sample size. With the upcoming publication of the “GOHSC 1000 Cities Challenge”, further validation could be conducted. [46] Another limitation of our study was the conversion of various national-level indicators to city-level metrics. Since data for many local indicators was unavailable, we calculated them by pondering demographic information. Nonetheless, city- and neighborhood-wide discrepancies may exist, potentially yielding inexact estimates. [22] The lack of city-level indicators emphasizes our previously mentioned points on the need to gather measurable data, as well as the vision of the GOHSC to develop standardized and comprehensive local spatial indicators, as stated by their motto: “What gets measured, gets done.” [47] The exclusive inclusion of drug trials, while excluding behavioral or lifestyle interventions, could indeed underestimate the full scope of CKD research, particularly of that conducted in LMICs. [8] However, we deliberately focused on drug trials because of their major influence on the development of clinical practice guidelines. [49] We acknowledge, nevertheless, that a review with a similar methodological scope should be conducted specifically for non-pharmacologic interventions. In addition, future studies should evaluate research across the full extent of cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic health. Considering the above, the results of this study should be interpreted in the context discussed, and until extended validation is conducted, they should be employed for exploration, as intended by the authors.

Notwithstanding its limitations, the main strength of our study is the description of a novel, distinctive framework for measuring local CKD research capacity based on real-world data, calculated indicators, and information obtained directly from RCTs. Moreover, the insights on financial, socio-demographic, healthcare, and nephrology-related barriers to participation in CKD RCTs are crucial aspects that warrant additional investigation. Further research should be directed at understanding individual, spatial, and system-derived barriers to RCT participation, leading to quality evidence to guide policy implementation.

It will be essential to develop strategies to address the barriers highlighted in this study. Strengthening partnerships between industry, government, and academia may be particularly valuable [50], not only to expand external funding but also to ensure appropriate allocation of resources and infrastructure development to increase access. International initiatives, such as the ISN Clinical Research Program, have shown that capacity-building efforts can support investigator-led studies and workforce development. [51] Moreover, at the city level, collaboration with government officials on urban planning initiatives, such as improving access to public transport, reducing neighborhood congestion, and promoting healthier environments, remains key to addressing barriers and facilitating clinical research participation. Collectively, sustainable solutions will require coordination across global, national, and local levels.

Conclusions

In conclusion, there are considerable differences in CKD research capacity between the cities included in the GOHSC Study Collaboration, which may be affected by financial, socio-demographic, healthcare, and nephrology-related barriers. To improve global kidney health, in line with the 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development, local, regional, and international initiatives and collaborations should address distinctive local variations in relation to availability, requirements, and obstacles to CKD research and care.
