Beyond Positivity: Understanding and Interpreting MOG-IgG Testing in MOGAD
Authors: Jieun Chung, Su-Hyun Kim, Fabienne Brilot, Ho Jin Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe diagnosis of myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein antibody-associated disease (MOGAD) relies heavily on detecting serum MOG-IgG by cell-based assay, but seropositivity alone does not establish the disease. In selected clinical cohorts, the positive predictive value of MOG-IgG ranges from approximately 50%–70% at low titers, depending on the assay and cohort, to near 100% at high titers, and inter-laboratory agreement is markedly lower in the low-positive samples than for clearly positive or negative samples. The difficulty in interpreting low-positive MOG-IgG results largely reflects the interplay of two elements: biological factors (including antigen-related background reactivity and potential cross-reactive immunoreactivity) and methodological variability between assays (including differences in antigen presentation, live versus fixed assay platforms, secondary antibody selection, fluorescence readout, sample timing, and locally validated cutoffs). This ambiguity is unlikely to be resolved by assay optimization alone. The 2023 International MOGAD Panel criteria address these issues by requiring stronger clinical and radiological support when the serological evidence is weaker. Accordingly, MOG-IgG should be interpreted as probabilistic evidence within the clinical and MRI context, rather than as a stand-alone diagnostic marker.Keywords: myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein, MOGAD, MOG-IgG, cell-based assay, diagnostic criteria, low-positive results

INTRODUCTION

Over the past decade, myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein antibody-associated disease (MOGAD) has become established as a distinct inflammatory demyelinating disorder of the central nervous system (CNS), separable from both multiple sclerosis (MS) and aquaporin-4 (AQP4)-IgG-positive neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD).1,2 Recent population-based studies estimate an annual incidence of 1.6–3.4 per million and a prevalence of approximately 20 per million (95% confidence interval 11–34).3,4 MOGAD presents with a broad range of clinical features, including optic neuritis, transverse myelitis, acute disseminated encephalomyelitis, cerebral monofocal or polyfocal deficits, cortical encephalitis, and brainstem or cerebellar syndromes. For diagnosis, the 2023 International MOGAD Panel criteria require three elements: a compatible core clinical demyelinating event, exclusion of alternative diagnoses such as MS, and a positive MOG-IgG test by cell-based assay (CBA).2

Applying these criteria in practice is often challenging because the clinical significance of MOG-IgG seropositivity depends heavily on antibody titer, assay methodology, and clinical phenotype. Although contemporary live CBAs are analytically specific, the positive predictive value (PPV) of MOG-IgG falls sharply at low titers, and low-positive results may occur in patients with alternative diagnoses such as MS, as well as in healthy individuals or otherwise low-probability populations.5,6,7,8,9 A positive MOG-IgG result, taken in isolation, therefore cannot be equated with MOGAD.

Interpretive challenges surrounding MOG-IgG testing arise from the interaction of two factors. First, intrinsic biological properties of the MOG antigen and the anti-MOG antibody repertoire may contribute to low-level background or cross-reactive immunoreactivity. Second, methodological differences in CBA design and interpretation substantially influence assay performance, particularly near the positivity threshold. Recognizing how these biological and methodological factors interact is essential for the appropriate interpretation of MOG-IgG results in clinical practice. We therefore review the biological context for MOG-IgG specificity, the methodological determinants of assay performance, the interpretive framework of the 2023 International MOGAD Panel criteria, and a practical approach to clinical and MRI integration.

BIOLOGICAL CONSIDERATIONS IN LOW-LEVEL MOG-IgG IMMUNOREACTIVITY

MOG protein and the IgV-like fold family

MOG is a quantitatively minor glycoprotein, accounting for approximately 0.05% of total CNS myelin protein. It is localized to the outermost surface of the myelin sheath and the oligodendrocyte membrane.10,11 Structurally, MOG comprises an extracellular N-terminal immunoglobulin variable (IgV)-like domain, a single transmembrane segment, and an intracellular C-terminal portion containing a second hydrophobic region.12,13 The best-characterized MOG-IgG epitopes are conformational, involving residues such as P42 in the CC' loop and H103/S104 in the FG loop, surface-exposed regions of the extracellular IgV-like domain of correctly folded human MOG.14,15,16 This IgV-like fold is one of the most prevalent structural motifs encoded by the human genome,17 and proteins sharing this architecture are widely distributed across immune and non-immune tissues. Moreover, exposed loop regions such as the CC' and FG loops are known to contribute to ligand- or antibody-binding interfaces in several IgV-containing proteins.18,19 Although these mapped MOG-IgG epitopes have not been established as generic IgV-fold epitopes, their position on a broadly represented IgV-like scaffold may provide a possible explanation for low-level background or cross-reactive antibody binding in unselected populations.

Cross-reactivity within the Ig superfamily: butyrophilin as a possible example

The potential for cross-reactivity exists broadly across the Ig superfamily, but the best-characterized example relevant to MOG is butyrophilin (BTN). Originally identified as the major glycoprotein of the bovine milk fat globule membrane, BTN is now recognized as the prototype of an expanded mammalian gene family expressed across immune cells, mucosal epithelia, and lactating mammary tissue.20 Its N-terminal IgV-like domain shares approximately 50% sequence identity with the extracellular IgV-like domain of MOG,13,21 and the two domains also adopt closely matching three-dimensional structures, with even the exposed loops conserved in length.22 In MOG, these loops carry the mapped MOG-IgG epitopes.14,15,16 This structural similarity can influence immune recognition. In the Dark Agouti rat, immunization with native BTN induces a T-cell response that cross-reacts with MOG residues 76–87 and produces CNS inflammation.23 In humans, MOG-reactive antibodies from patients with MS can cross-react with homologous BTN peptide epitopes, and comparable BTN-reactive antibodies have also been detected in healthy individuals. Although these observations derive from Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assays (ELISAs) rather than CBAs,24 BTN may illustrate how a structurally homologous antigen might contribute to low-level reactivity in non-MOGAD populations.

Structural contrast with AQP4-IgG

Analytical specificity is high for both MOG- and AQP4-IgG CBAs. A meta-analysis of detection methods reported a pooled specificity of approximately 99% for AQP4-IgG CBAs,25 and contemporary live MOG-IgG CBAs report comparable clinical specificity.26 The difference lies instead in the low-positive range, where MOG-IgG samples are markedly less concordant across laboratories than corresponding low-positive AQP4-IgG samples.9 One possible contributor to this contrast is the supramolecular organization of the two target antigens. The M23 isoform of AQP4 assembles into orthogonal arrays of particles on the astrocyte surface, generating conformational epitopes that are preferentially formed in the assembled state.27,28,29 These arrays may constrain antibody recognition and thereby contribute to the high diagnostic specificity of AQP4-IgG assays. By contrast, MOG is not known to form an equivalent AQP4-like supramolecular architecture. Binding of patient MOG-IgG often relies on bivalent, avidity-dependent engagement. Experimental data suggest that the second hydrophobic, intracellularly oriented domain of MOG may help position neighboring extracellular MOG domains (ED-MOG) at a distance compatible with bivalent IgG binding, which may also explain why CBAs using highly expressed, full-length MOG (FL-MOG) are better able to detect low-affinity MOG-IgG.30,31,32

Together, these features help explain why some degree of uncertainty may persist for results close to the positivity threshold, despite advances in CBA methodology.

METHODOLOGICAL DETERMINANTS OF MOG-IgG DETECTION

Given this potential biological background, the primary methodological challenge is distinguishing clinically meaningful antibody binding from low-level background signal. Non-cell-based formats do not meet this challenge. In an international comparison, ELISA results did not correlate with CBA findings for human MOG-IgG,9 and the 2023 criteria accordingly restrict diagnostic testing to CBAs.2 While presentation of FL-MOG on a cell membrane is a critical prerequisite,30,31,32 laboratories should carefully optimize the remaining analytical variables to maximize diagnostic precision near this threshold.

Antigen construct and cell-line preparation

Antigen construct

The MOG construct is the first methodological variable to interact directly with the bivalent binding behavior of MOG-IgG. FL-MOG, comprising the extracellular IgV-like domain, the transmembrane segment, and the intracellular C-terminal portion, sits in the membrane at a spacing that permits bivalent IgG engagement.30 The ED-MOG, lacking the transmembrane and intracellular anchors, reproduces this arrangement poorly and substantially reduces detection sensitivity.30 Almost all laboratories use the α1 isoform, which is the standard for diagnostic testing.33 Patient antibodies bind MOG isoforms to differing degrees, but this variation has no established diagnostic significance,33 and non-α1 isoforms remain confined to research use.33,34 Clinically, these construct-related differences mean that a result should be interpreted in light of whether the assay presents native, FL-MOG on the cell surface.

Transient versus stable expression systems

A second key variable is how the construct is introduced into the host cell. Transient transfection delivers the MOG-encoding plasmid into human embryonic kidney 293 (HEK293) cells for each experiment,26 which is flexible but leads to batch-to-batch variability in transfection efficiency and antigen expression. Stably expressing cell lines, including lentivirus-transduced FL-MOG platforms and lines generated using selectable-marker methods (e.g., G418/geneticin resistance) followed by clonal isolation, have been reported to provide more consistent antigen expression across experiments, which may reduce this source of variability.35,36,37,38

Live versus fixed CBA

In live CBAs, the serum is applied to living MOG-expressing cells, preserving the dynamic membrane environment in which MOG is normally expressed. In fixed CBAs, the cells are first fixed with formaldehyde or methanol. Fixed CBAs offer practical advantages, including standardization, longer shelf life, and compatibility with routine clinical laboratory workflows, and the commercial Euroimmun fixed CBA is a widely distributed platform in this category.9

Fixation, however, can influence assay performance in both directions. In a flow cytometry-based comparison using the same MOG-expressing cells before and after fixation, many sera that recognized native MOG showed reduced or lost binding to fixed MOG, despite comparable antigen expression.32,39 This loss of native conformational recognition can produce false negatives: in an international multicenter comparison, four clearly positive samples that all seven live CBAs detected were missed by the fixed commercial CBA, so that, among the 39 samples submitted as clearly positive, concordance across the eight MOG-IgG CBAs was 82% (32/39), compared with 92% (36/39) for the seven live CBAs alone.9 Conversely, fixation can expose cryptic epitopes that are inaccessible on the native membrane and produce false positives: in a separate multicenter comparison, four of five false-positive samples were positive only on the commercial fixed CBA, possibly reflecting fixation-generated cryptic epitopes, although that report did not establish the mechanism.26 More recently, real-world paired testing has shown substantial live-fixed discrepancies, with many MOG-IgG-positive samples detected only by live flow cytometry (CBA-FC) and substantially lower sensitivity for fixed immunofluorescence microscopy (CBA-IF) than for live CBA-FC despite similar specificity.40 These contrasting effects can share a single cause: depending on which epitopes are masked and which are exposed, fixation can compromise either sensitivity or specificity.

These findings support live CBA as the preferred platform where cell-culture facilities are available, while validated fixed CBA remains acceptable under the 2023 criteria. Where such infrastructure is lacking, fixed CBA can be useful, but its results carry the bidirectional error risk described above and should be interpreted with that limitation explicitly in mind.

Fluorescence readout: CBA-IF versus CBA-FC

Within the live CBA category, two readout methods are most commonly used. CBA-IF relies on visual scoring of fluorescence by trained observers, whereas CBA-FC provides automated quantitative measurement of mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) on individual transfected cells.35 Two analytical metrics are used with CBA-FC: ratiometric MFI (rMFI), the MFI ratio between MOG-transfected and non-transfected cells, and delta MFI, the absolute difference between them. In a comparison across three serum dilutions, rMFI at 1:100 gave the highest area under the receiver operating characteristic curve and the best agreement with CBA-IF endpoint titers, whereas delta MFI was more strongly influenced by background binding to non-transfected cells.35 The positivity threshold and the choice of data-analysis method influence detection sensitivity in live flow cytometry.41

Each readout has its place. CBA-IF allows direct visualization of the binding pattern, which can separate specific membrane staining from nonspecific intracellular or background fluorescence, a distinction that may be lost in a purely quantitative readout. CBA-FC offers higher throughput and reduces subjectivity in visual scoring. The two may be best regarded as complementary rather than mutually exclusive, and several laboratories use one for primary screening and the other for confirmation or for resolving ambiguous results (Table 1).

Secondary antibody and isotype/subclass selection

The choice of secondary antibody may affect assay performance. Three options are in use: IgG heavy-and-light (H+L) chain, IgG Fc, and IgG subclass-specific antibodies. The 2023 criteria recommend Fc- or IgG1-specific detection and accept anti-human IgG (H+L) detection when used in externally validated in-house assays.2 An early live CBA using transiently transfected cells raised concerns that anti-human IgG (H+L) detection might produce nonspecific reactivity attributable to IgM.42 However, studies using subsequent stable FL-MOG live CBAs found comparable performance between H+L and subclass-specific detection, with no IgM binding observed,32,36 and Fc-specific detection has performed consistently in independent centers (Table 1).43,44 IgG1-restricted detection maximizes specificity but may miss rare patients with exclusive or predominant MOG-IgG3 seropositivity.45 Overall, no single secondary-antibody strategy is uniformly superior, and performance should be assessed within the complete, validated assay configuration.9,39

Serum dilution and the bidirectional risk of misclassification

Serum dilution remains one of the least standardized aspects of MOG-IgG testing. Laboratories generally use either a fixed-dilution approach, in which each sample is evaluated at a single predefined dilution, or endpoint titration, in which a positive sample is serially diluted until reactivity is no longer detectable. Endpoint titration provides valuable semiquantitative data, but it is labor-intensive and difficult to implement in routine practice. Consequently, many laboratories favor testing at a single fixed dilution for practical reasons. Once a fixed dilution is adopted, the key methodological decision is the choice of dilution, because assay performance depends critically on the dilution selected.

A higher dilution reduces nonspecific background and may improve specificity, but can compromise sensitivity for low-titer MOG-IgG. A lower dilution has the opposite effect: sensitivity improves, but nonspecific and background reactivity rise along with the signal of interest, reducing specificity. In a live-CBA cohort, the PPV for MOGAD was only 15% among patients whose reactivity was confined to 1:20, but increased to 87% among those who remained positive at 1:100, indicating that reactivity detected only at a low dilution is frequently not disease-specific.46

Interestingly, low serum dilution can also produce the opposite form of misclassification, which is a false-negative result despite the presence of a high antibody concentration. Although this phenomenon has not, to our knowledge, been specifically documented for MOG-IgG, a strongly positive sample for AQP4-IgG has been reported as negative when tested undiluted and clearly positive only after dilution.47 Such paradoxical behavior reflects a dilution-dependent unmasking of antibody binding that has been reported across several autoantibody assays, arising from a prozone effect due to antibody excess, serum matrix interference (in which other serum components interfere with antibody binding), or from both.48,49,50,51

These bidirectional risks highlight the limitations of relying on a single fixed dilution. For samples positive at the screening dilution, reflex testing at one or more validated higher dilutions can distinguish low positivity from clear positivity, a distinction that improves identification of true MOGAD among MOG-IgG-positive patients.46 However, this positive-only reflex strategy does not detect samples that are false negative at the initial low dilution because of a potential prozone effect. Therefore, in clinically compelling cases, testing at a higher dilution despite an initially negative result, or parallel testing at two validated dilutions, may be warranted. This approach does not replace full endpoint titration, but a targeted or parallel multi-dilution strategy can reduce both forms of dilution-dependent misclassification, while remaining more feasible than full endpoint titration for routine high-throughput testing. This principle is supported by evidence from other autoantibody assays showing that parallel testing of diluted samples can identify prozone-related false-negative results that would otherwise be missed.52

Inter-laboratory variability: a synthesis

These variables do not act in isolation. In any given laboratory, their combined effect sets the assay’s operating characteristics across the full titer range (Table 1). A blinded international comparison of 11 assays across five centers found that agreement among the seven live CBAs was 92% for samples submitted as clearly positive and 96% across clearly positive and clearly negative samples combined, but only 28% for low-positive samples.9 This pattern, robust agreement at the extremes and poor agreement in the middle, may be an inherent feature of MOG-IgG assays, because the position of the positivity threshold relative to the zone of overlapping MOGAD and non-MOGAD reactivity is itself assay-dependent (Fig. 1). Two consequences follow. A low-positive result from one laboratory cannot be assumed equivalent to a low-positive result from another without knowledge of the respective assay configurations, and no single methodological refinement is likely to resolve the ambiguity in this range. The 2023 criteria therefore address this uncertainty at the level of diagnostic requirements rather than at the level of the assay.

CLINICAL INTERPRETATION UNDER THE 2023 CRITERIA

The tiered diagnostic framework and real-world performance

The 2023 International MOGAD Panel proposed criteria incorporated antibody titer-dependent diagnostic uncertainty into a tiered framework linking the strength of serological evidence to the level of clinical and MRI corroboration required for diagnosis.2 The criteria define three levels of serological evidence: clear positive, low positive, and a third tier comprising positive results without a reported titer and isolated cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) positivity, both of which carry the same additional requirements as a low-positive result (Table 2). The definition of a clear-positive result is assay-dependent: for fixed CBAs, a serum titer of at least 1:100 is considered clear positive, whereas for live CBAs a clear-positive result is defined according to each validated assay, for example at least two doubling dilutions above the assay-specific cutoff or above a validated flow cytometry ratio. A clear-positive result carries substantial diagnostic weight, requiring only a compatible core clinical event and exclusion of alternative diagnoses. Conversely, a low-positive result (a fixed-CBA titer of at least 1:10 but less than 1:100, or a value within the low-positive range of a validated live CBA) requires both AQP4-IgG seronegativity and at least one additional supporting clinical or MRI feature, reflecting the lower specificity of low-titer seropositivity.

Validation studies have generally demonstrated high sensitivity of the criteria, although specificity varies according to patient selection and clinical setting. A UK cohort reported an overall sensitivity of 96.5% and a specificity of 98.9%, although sensitivity was lower in adults (91.9%) than in children (100%), and a Korean cohort likewise reported high sensitivity and specificity in a clinically enriched population,53,54 whereas a Chinese multicenter cohort demonstrated substantially lower specificity, particularly among adults, despite preserved sensitivity, although that estimate should be interpreted cautiously because only nine of 291 patients in the cohort received a final diagnosis other than MOGAD, so the specificity estimates rest on very small numbers.55 A Latin American cohort similarly supported real-world applicability of the criteria.56 These differences likely reflect case mix, referral patterns, adult-pediatric composition, comparator diagnoses, and pre-test probability rather than a simple failure of the criteria themselves. Pediatric cohorts tend to show higher PPV, which may reflect differences in phenotype and pretest probability, as typical pediatric phenotypes such as acute disseminated encephalomyelitis carry a higher prior probability of MOGAD.53,55 Collectively, these studies suggest that the framework performs robustly when applied in appropriately selected clinical contexts, but becomes more challenging in unselected referral populations. Sensitivity itself can also fall when the criteria are applied in routine practice. An Italian multicenter real-life cohort reported one of the lowest sensitivities among validation studies (approximately 88%), which rose to approximately 99% only after expert central re-review of discordant cases, indicating that much of the apparent underperformance reflected inconsistent application of the criteria and limited availability of adequate MRI rather than a deficiency of the criteria themselves.57 In a large international cohort, supporting clinical or MRI features were present in most patients with low-positive or untitered results, so that few genuine cases appeared to fail the criteria.58

The low-positive range

The interval between clear positivity and assay negativity represents the most challenging region for clinical interpretation. In this low-positive range, immunoreactivity can be detected in genuine MOGAD cases as well as in patients with MS, other neurological diseases (e.g., autoimmune encephalitis and, occasionally, AQP4-IgG-positive NMOSD, in which AQP4-IgG is typically strongly positive whereas MOG-IgG is only low positive), or in individuals without neurological disease.6,8,59,60 Methodological variability is also greatest in this range. Beyond assay variability, the PPV of low-positive MOG-IgG depends on both antibody titer and pretest probability. In one large cohort study, PPV rose with titer (51% at 1:20–40, 82% at 1:100, and 100% at 1:1,000 or higher) and, independently, varied with clinical and MRI phenotype: 85% in patients with a high pretest probability versus only 12% in those with a low pretest probability, although these titers were generated by a single live flow-cytometry assay and, as discussed above, may not be directly transferable to other laboratories.5

These limitations become particularly problematic when MOG-IgG testing is applied indiscriminately in low-probability settings. In a Korean referral-center cohort of patients with externally reported MOG-IgG positivity, approximately one-third (18/57, 31.6%) failed to fulfill the 2023 diagnostic criteria on expert re-evaluation, most commonly because of nonspecific symptoms, alternative diagnoses, or absence of supportive features.8

Interpretation within this range therefore requires considerable caution. This practical challenge has led to more detailed guidance on how positive results should be weighed. For instance, the TRUE-MOGAD score weighs the antibody titer together with clinical phenotype, age at onset, supporting MRI features, and the presence or absence of CSF-restricted oligoclonal bands. Once a positive result is obtained, such scoring system can be applied as a supplementary tool downstream of the 2023 criteria.46 Both these tools and the diagnostic criteria converge on the same underlying principle: a low-positive result must never be interpreted in isolation, but rather in the context of the individual patient’s clinical and MRI probability of MOGAD (Fig. 2).

Sample timing and serial assessment

The timing of testing relative to disease activity and treatment exposure is a critical determinant of MOG-IgG detectability. Antibody titers are dynamic, typically increasing during acute attacks and declining after corticosteroid treatment or recovery.32,61,62,63 Consequently, testing performed after high-dose corticosteroid therapy or during prolonged remission may yield false-negative or lower-titer results despite underlying MOGAD. Current diagnostic recommendations therefore emphasize obtaining serum samples during acute attacks and before initiation of immunotherapy whenever possible.2,62 In clinically suspicious cases with initially negative or low-positive results obtained after treatment exposure, repeat testing may improve diagnostic sensitivity and confidence.

Beyond diagnosis, longitudinal MOG-IgG monitoring has also been explored for prognostic stratification. Persistent seropositivity, especially when titers remain high during remission, has been associated with increased relapse risk across several cohorts, whereas seroreversion to negativity is more frequently associated with a monophasic disease course.62,63,64,65,66,67,68 Similar associations have been noted in pediatric populations, where a rapid decline in titers may indicate a lower risk of recurrence.67,68 However, these associations are probabilistic rather than deterministic: some persistently seropositive patients remain relapse-free, whereas relapses may still occur in patients with fluctuating or negative titers.65,67 Accordingly, longitudinal MOG-IgG status should be interpreted as one component of disease assessment rather than as a stand-alone predictor of future disease activity, and treatment escalation or de-escalation should not be based on antibody persistence alone.

CSF testing

MOG-IgG is predominantly a serum-based biomarker, and serum analysis remains the diagnostic standard.2 Reported frequencies of CSF-exclusive positivity vary by more than an order of magnitude, largely because the denominator differs between studies. Among seronegative patients with a MOGAD-compatible phenotype, CSF-restricted MOG-IgG has been reported in approximately 4%–12%, and it accounted for 11% of all patients ultimately diagnosed with MOGAD in one referral cohort.69,70,71 By contrast, in an unselected national referral series it was detected in only 0.7% (7/1,016) of consecutively tested seronegative patients, four of whom had a confirmed alternative diagnosis.72 This gradient mirrors the effect of pretest probability described above for serum testing. CSF-restricted MOG-IgG is not entirely specific to MOGAD and may also occur in other inflammatory conditions, including MS.69,71,72 Accordingly, CSF MOG-IgG testing should not be used as a broad screening tool. It is most defensible as a complementary test in selected patients with a highly compatible clinical-MRI phenotype, negative or equivocal serum testing, and persistent diagnostic uncertainty.

When CSF testing is performed, paired serum-CSF interpretation is preferable to isolated CSF interpretation. Recent studies suggest that intrathecal MOG-IgG synthesis may identify a biologically meaningful subset,70 but the clinical use of CSF testing remains less standardized than serum CBA. A serum-negative/CSF-positive result should therefore be treated similarly to a low-positive or untitered serum result under the 2023 criteria: it requires AQP4-IgG seronegativity, supportive clinical or MRI features, and careful exclusion of alternative diagnoses.2,69,70,71

CONCLUSION

The identification of MOG-IgG by CBA has fundamentally reshaped the classification of inflammatory demyelinating diseases and established MOGAD as a distinct clinical entity. However, real-world experience has also demonstrated that MOG-IgG positivity is not necessarily synonymous with MOGAD, particularly within the low-positive range where diagnostic uncertainty remains substantial.

While methodological standardization and improved assay technologies may further reduce variability, some degree of uncertainty is likely to persist. The 2023 International MOGAD Panel criteria address this limitation by linking the required level of clinical and MRI corroboration to the strength of serological evidence rather than relying on antibody positivity alone.

The clinically useful question is therefore not whether MOG-IgG is present, but whether the strength of the serological evidence is proportional to the clinical and MRI probability of MOGAD (Table 3). Although future advances in assay harmonization and epitope-specific diagnostics may further clarify low-positive results, they will not obviate the need for careful clinical evaluation. For the foreseeable future, MOG-IgG should be utilized as a targeted diagnostic tool that is reported quantitatively when feasible and always interpreted in conjunction with the clinical phenotype, MRI findings, AQP4-IgG status, sample timing, and competing alternative diagnoses.
