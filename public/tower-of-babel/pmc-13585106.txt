Prevalence of Upper Airway Resistance Syndrome in Children with Orofacial Malocclusion in Bengaluru Urban District: An Observational Study
Authors: Simran P Desai, Dhananjaya Gaviappa, Somashekar Ankanahalli Ramu, Lalitha S Jairam, Maanya Vijay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe hallmark signs of upper airway resistance syndrome (UARS) include increased breathing effort, frequent arousals during sleep, and daytime drowsiness without noticeable problems with oxygenation or airflow. It may involve craniofacial traits such as a long face, small mouth, retrognathia, nasal obstruction, narrow throat, and high palate and primarily affects young, thin people who suffer from insomnia. UARS causes fatigue by interfering with sleep. Due to airway obstruction, mouth breathing in children can cause Class II Division 1 malocclusion and a V-shaped maxillary arch.AimTo assess the prevalence of UARS in children with orofacial malocclusion in Bengaluru Urban District.MethodsThe study included a total of 50 participants aged 8–14 years, equally divided into a case group and a control group. (1) UARS was determined using diagnostic tests such as the Apnea-Hypopnea Index (AHI) and by evaluating daytime sleepiness with the Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS). (2) The patient's facial profile was assessed, and all children underwent a dental examination based on canine relationship, presence or absence of posterior crossbite, amount of overjet and overbite. Palatal morphology, neck circumference, and uvula were also assessed.ResultsThe prevalence of UARS was significantly higher in the malocclusion group than in the control group (p < 0.05). Class II malocclusion, constricted maxillary arches, and retrognathic mandibles were significantly associated with increased UARS indicators.ConclusionThe study highlights a significant association between orofacial malocclusion and UARS in children. Early orthodontic screening and interdisciplinary intervention may play a critical role in identifying and managing UARS in pediatric populations.Clinical significanceThis study highlights the need for early detection and management of orofacial malocclusions to prevent UARS, with orthodontic interventions such as rapid maxillary expansion improving airway patency. A multidisciplinary approach involving orthodontists, pediatricians, and otolaryngologists is essential for effective care.How to cite this articleDesai SP, Gaviappa D, Ramu SA et al. Introduction

Because of its effects on neurocognitive development, cardiovascular health, and quality of life, sleep-disordered breathing (SDB) in children, particularly obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) and upper airway resistance syndrome (UARS), has become a major concern in pediatric health care.1,2 Although OSA has been the focus of extensive research, UARS is still frequently overlooked, even though it is linked to disrupted sleep, heightened sympathetic activity, and behavioral problems.3,4 UARS is marked by increased respiratory effort-related arousals without notable airflow restriction or oxygen desaturation, often presenting in a subtle manner that complicates clinical identification.5,6

Craniofacial features such as a narrow upper jaw, a high palatal arch, and a recessed lower jaw have been linked to decreased upper airway patency, potentially resulting in conditions such as UARS and OSA.7,8 Malocclusions, including open bite, crossbite, and Class II skeletal patterns, are frequently seen in mouth-breathing individuals and are associated with airway obstruction and alterations in facial growth.8,9 Studies suggest that early orthodontic interventions targeting these problems may help reduce sleep-related breathing disorders.9,10 Given the scarcity of data from India on this subject, this research aims to investigate the relationship between orofacial malocclusion and UARS in children from Bengaluru's pediatric population. The objectives of the study include identifying UARS using diagnostic tools such as the Apnea-Hypopnea Index (AHI) and the Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS) for children, as well as evaluating malocclusion and mouth breathing through cephalometric and dental assessments.

Methods

The retrospectively study was conducted among pediatric patients undergoing treatment at the Department of Pediatrics, M S Ramaiah Memorial Teaching Hospital (MSRMTH), Bengaluru. The participants included children who reported to the Department of Pediatrics, Ramaiah Medical College, and the Department of Pediatric and Preventive Dentistry, Faculty of Dental Sciences, Ramaiah Memorial Hospital (RMH), Ramaiah University of Applied Sciences (RUAS). The study period was from June 2023 to January 2025.

The study group consisted of 25 children aged 8–14 years who were diagnosed with UARS and were undergoing treatment at MSRMTH. UARS was diagnosed using overnight polysomnography, the AHI, and the ESS for Children and Adolescents (ESS-CHAD). The control group consisted of 25 age- and sex-matched healthy children in the same age range without SDB, UARS, malocclusion, or a history of orthodontic treatment. Both groups underwent orthodontic, clinical, and cephalometric evaluation.

The exclusion criteria for both groups included systemic diseases, genetic syndromes, upper airway infections, a history of orthodontic treatment or adenoidectomy/tonsillectomy, dental anomalies, or missing teeth. The sample size was calculated using G*Power (effect size = 0.82, α = 0.05, power = 80%, confidence interval = 95%) based on Kula et al. 2013, requiring a total of 50 participants, with 25 participants in each group.

Children in the study group underwent evaluation using the AHI, calculated from overnight polysomnography, with respiratory events lasting ≥10 seconds included, and the ESS-CHAD, an eight-item parent-reported questionnaire in which scores >10 indicated excessive daytime sleepiness. Daytime sleepiness was classified as follows: normal (0–9), suggesting no excessive daytime sleepiness; mild (10–12), indicating a slight increase in daytime sleepiness; moderate (13–15), indicating noticeable daytime sleepiness; and severe (≥16), indicating marked excessive daytime sleepiness.

Orofacial malocclusion and mouth breathing were assessed through clinical and orthodontic examination. The facial profile was determined through clinical inspection and cephalometric tracings, with SNA, SNB, and ANB angles measured from standardized lateral cephalograms using the landmarks Sella (S), Nasion (N), Point A, and Point B. Dental examination was performed by a single examiner using a mouth mirror and probe and included classification of canine relationship (Class I–III), detection of posterior crossbite, and measurement of overjet according to the Index of Orthodontic Treatment Need (IOTN) criteria and overbite according to Proffit's method using a calibrated caliper. Any deviation from normal occlusion was recorded as malocclusion. Palatal morphology was visually categorized as U-shaped or V-shaped. Neck circumference was measured at the level of the thyroid cartilage using a flexible tape, and oropharyngeal crowding was graded using the Mallampati classification (Grades I–IV) with the patient's mouth open and tongue protruded. The primary outcomes measured were whether (1) UARS was associated with retrusive or prognathic skeletal patterns and altered facial contour and (2) a larger neck circumference was associated with greater airway obstruction in the study group. UARS was evaluated in relation to craniofacial and dental parameters. UARS and OSA share overlapping features within the spectrum of pediatric SDB. While OSA is graded using the AHI, UARS is characterized by increased respiratory effort-related arousals without significant oxygen desaturation. Both conditions are associated with malocclusion traits, maxillomandibular retrusion, and soft tissue crowding. Evidence suggests that untreated UARS may progress to OSA over time, highlighting the importance of early detection and intervention.3

Statistical analysis was performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) for Windows, Version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, 2013). Descriptive analysis of all explanatory and outcome variables was performed using mean and standard deviation for quantitative variables and frequencies and proportions for categorical variables. Inferential statistics were performed using the independent Student's t-test and the Mann–Whitney U test to compare the mean AHI, daytime sleepiness assessed using the ESS-CHAD, dental parameters, airway parameters, and respiratory test parameters between the two groups. The Chi-squared test was used to compare uvula classification, facial profile, palatal morphology, and neck circumference between the two groups. The level of significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

The study and control groups were demographically comparable, with no statistically significant differences in age (p = 0.24) or sex distribution (p = 0.78). The mean age was 10.68 ± 1.95 years in the study group and 10.00 ± 1.44 years in the control group, with an age range of 8–14 years. Both groups had a nearly equal representation of males and females, ensuring a balanced sample (Table 1).

Sleep-related assessments revealed statistically significant differences between the study and control groups. The mean AHI was markedly higher in the study group (15.50 ± 3.16) than in the control group (1.64 ± 0.42), with a mean difference of 13.85 (p < 0.001). Similarly, the ESS scores were significantly higher in the study group (15.00 ± 2.77) than in the control group (4.88 ± 1.17), with a mean difference of 10.12 (p < 0.001) (Table 2). Only 4% of children in the study group had normal daytime sleepiness, compared to 100% in the control group. In contrast, 96% of the study group showed varying degrees of excessive daytime sleepiness: 16% mild, 40% moderate, and 40% severe, whereas no such findings were observed in the control group. These findings indicate a strong association between UARS and both disrupted sleep and impaired daytime alertness. All children in the UARS group had OSA, with 52% classified as mild and 48% as moderate, whereas no cases were observed in the control group (p < 0.001). Excessive daytime sleepiness was common, affecting 96% of children with UARS, with moderate and severe cases each accounting for 40%, highlighting the significant impact of UARS on sleep quality and daily functioning (Fig. 2).

Within the study group, moderate OSA cases showed reduced SNA and SNB angles and increased ANB angles (p < 0.001), indicating maxillary protrusion and mandibular deficiency as contributors to airway compromise. None of the children with UARS had a straight facial profile, with convex (52%) and concave (48%) profiles predominating, compared to 64% of controls with a straight facial profile (p < 0.001).

Children with UARS showed predominantly Class II (68%) and Class III (32%) canine relationships, compared to 80% with Class I canine relationships in the control group (p < 0.001). Posterior crossbite was more common in children with UARS (52 vs 16%, p = 0.007), along with increased overjet (36 vs 4%) and overbite (40 vs 12%; both p = 0.001), highlighting a strong association between malocclusion and airway resistance (Fig. 3).

Anthropometric and soft tissue analyses revealed significant differences between the groups. Children with UARS had a markedly greater mean neck circumference (43.62 ± 1.08 cm) than the control group (36.65 ± 0.63 cm; p < 0.001), suggesting a role of cervical soft tissue in airway narrowing. Although palatal shape differences were not statistically significant, a higher proportion of children with UARS presented with a V-shaped palate (48 vs 28%), indicative of reduced oral cavity volume. Mallampati grading further highlighted airway compromise, with 80% of children with UARS classified as Grade III or Grade IV compared to none in the control group, in which lower grades predominated (p < 0.001). Collectively, these findings underscore the contribution of soft tissue morphology, particularly oropharyngeal crowding, to the pathophysiology of UARS.

Discussion

This study demonstrates a significant association between UARS and distinct craniofacial, dental, and soft tissue characteristics in children from the Bengaluru Urban District. Children with UARS exhibited significantly higher AHI and ESS scores than healthy controls, reflecting pronounced sleep disruption and excessive daytime sleepiness, consistent with previous findings by Chervin et al.,11 Guilleminault et al.,12 and Marcus et al.2

Dental features were closely linked to UARS, with malocclusion traits such as increased overjet, deep overbite, posterior crossbite, and a higher occurrence of Class II and Class III canine relationships being notably more prevalent in children with UARS than in the control group. These observations align with previous studies indicating that malocclusion contributes to decreased airway patency and SDB in children.12–14 Research by Pliska et al.15 and Festa et al.16 also found a higher prevalence of malocclusion in children with upper airway obstruction, supporting these findings.15,16 Cephalometric analysis in this study showed reduced SNA and SNB angles and increased ANB angles, suggesting maxillomandibular retrusion. Such skeletal discrepancies have previously been identified as risk factors for airway narrowing.4,17 Behlfelt et al.4 identified craniofacial differences in children with enlarged tonsils, whereas Rinchuse and Rinchuse17 highlighted cephalometric indicators of skeletal retrusion contributing to airway compromise.

Soft tissue characteristics further increased the risk of UARS, with larger neck circumference and higher Mallampati grades being significantly more common among children with UARS, indicating oropharyngeal crowding. Similar findings were reported by Iwasaki et al.13 and Huynh et al.,8 who emphasized the combined impact of skeletal and soft tissue factors in SDB. Although differences in palatal morphology were not statistically significant in this study, a higher proportion of children with UARS had a V-shaped palate, which is clinically important because it reduces oral cavity volume and airway space.18 From a treatment standpoint, maxillary expansion has shown promising results. Camacho et al.19 demonstrated in a systematic review that rapid maxillary expansion (RME) enhances airway patency and reduces the severity of SDB. Villa et al.2 confirmed the long-term effectiveness of RME in children with OSA, whereas more recent research by Bazargani et al.20 showed volumetric increases in nasal and nasopharyngeal airway spaces following expansion. However, systematic reviews such as Vale et al.21 highlighted variability in outcomes and the need for long-term randomized studies. These findings emphasize the multifactorial nature of UARS, involving both skeletal and soft tissue contributions. Early interdisciplinary screening involving pediatricians, orthodontists, and sleep specialists is crucial for timely diagnosis. Orthodontic interventions, such as RME and malocclusion correction, may alleviate symptoms and improve airway patency.22,23 Given the cognitive, behavioral, and developmental consequences of untreated pediatric SDB,2,24 early detection and management are essential. This study underscores the important role of dental professionals in the early identification and referral of children at risk for UARS and related SDB conditions.25–30

Limitation

This study has certain limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. The relatively small sample size and recruitment from a limited clinical setting in Bengaluru may restrict the generalizability of the results to broader pediatric populations. The observational, cross-sectional design permits identification of associations between UARS and orofacial characteristics but does not establish causality or determine the temporal relationship between airway dysfunction and craniofacial development. Furthermore, all children in the UARS group were reported to have OSA based on AHI, reflecting the diagnostic overlap between these sleep-disordered breathing conditions and limiting the ability to determine the independent contribution of UARS. The use of parent-reported ESS-CHAD scores may also introduce subjective reporting bias. In addition, airway assessment was primarily based on clinical and two-dimensional cephalometric parameters, without three-dimensional evaluation of airway volume. Future research should therefore involve larger, multicenter, longitudinal studies using standardized diagnostic criteria to clearly differentiate UARS from OSA, along with objective three-dimensional airway assessment and comprehensive evaluation of potential confounding factors. Prospective studies should also evaluate whether early orthodontic interventions, including rapid maxillary expansion, result in sustained improvements in airway function and sleep-related outcomes.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates a significant association between orofacial malocclusion and UARS in children from the Bengaluru Urban District. Structural differences, including variations in cephalometric parameters, malocclusion patterns, facial profiles, neck circumference, and Mallampati grading, were strongly linked to airway compromise. The higher prevalence of OSA and excessive daytime sleepiness in the UARS group underscores the clinical importance of craniofacial assessment in the diagnosis and management of pediatric sleep-related breathing disorders.

Clinical Significance

The results of this study highlight the importance of early detection and treatment of orofacial malocclusions in children to prevent or mitigate UARS. Orthodontic interventions aimed at correcting malocclusions may have the added benefit of improving airway patency during sleep. The interplay between respiratory patterns and craniofacial development necessitates a collaborative approach to diagnosis and management. Orthodontists, pediatricians, and otolaryngologists should work together to assess and address the multifaceted needs of children presenting with malocclusion and potential UARS. Early interventions, such as rapid maxillary expansion, have shown promise in improving nasal airflow and mitigating the progression of SDB conditions.
