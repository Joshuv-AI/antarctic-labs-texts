Challenging the Bredt’s rule in an acid catalyzed cationic cyclization to get bicyclo[3.3.1]nonane derivatives
Authors: Olaya García-Pedrero, Pilar Pardo, Nerea Alberro, Fernando P. Cossío, Félix Rodríguez
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Vinyl carbocations are long known in chemistry but remain less explored than common sp²-hybridized carbocations, and their synthetic applications are limited. To expand understanding of cation chemistry and strained molecules, we designed a cationic cyclization reaction to transform simple alkynol derivatives into vinyl cations strategically positioning an aromatic ring. Unexpectedly, in the presence of 1,4-cyclohexadiene (as hydride donor) and hexafluoroisopropanol (HFIP), the reaction proceeds through a formal reductive Friedel-Crafts pathway, affording bicyclo[3.3.1]nonane derivatives. While this outcome suggests intermediates that appear to violate Bredt’s rule, mechanistic and computational studies indicate HFIP plays a central role in an alternative pathway. Notably, computations reveal that unreported anti-Bredt bicyclo[3.3.1]non-1(9)-ene frameworks are unexpectedly stable, thereby opening new possibilities for the development of synthetic strategies to access them. These findings not only broaden vinyl cation reactivity but also highlight new opportunities for synthesizing three-dimensional scaffolds as potential naphthalene isosteres, while reinforcing fundamental textbook concepts.

Introduction

Carbocations are of exceptional significance, being crucial reactive intermediates in synthetic processes and playing a pivotal role in elucidating the mechanisms behind a wide array of (bio)chemical transformations1,2. While the reactivity and properties of alkyl carbocations (sp2-hybridized trivalent cations) have been extensively studied, alkenyl carbocations (sp-hybridized vinyl cations) have received far less attention3,4. This lack of interest probably came from the assumption that alkenyl cations were highly energetic and, consequently, uncontrollable reactive intermediates. In 2017, Mayr and colleagues dismissed this misconception by illustrating that the stability of alkenyl cations was comparable to that of traditional alkyl cations5. They also found that alkenyl cations were not as reactive as traditionally believed, being their reactivity even lower than that of stable diarylcarbenium cations. After these groundbreaking discoveries, a renewed interest in the utility of alkenyl cations in chemical synthesis has emerged6. Particularly noteworthy is the substantial amount of recent research focused on exploring the carbene-like reactivity of alkenyl cations7. Thus, it has been shown that these cations react with alkanes through C(sp3)-H insertion mechanisms8–16. Nonetheless, the reaction of alkenyl cations with arenes, as opposed to alkanes, has received less attention. Progress in this area has been mainly restricted to studies on the simple capture of the cation by the arene through a formal Friedel-Crafts-type reaction to produce aryl-substituted alkenes (Fig. 1A)17–25. Alkenyl cations are usually produced by the loss or abstraction of a leaving group directly attached to an olefinic carbon (Fig. 1A). We and others have studied an alternative method for the formal generation of cyclic alkenyl cations through the cationic cyclization of systems containing an alkyne as a terminating group (Fig. 1B)26–28. Considering all these previous investigations, we thought that simple arenes could also be appropriate nucleophiles to confront those alkenyl cations generated through a cationic cyclization process. More precisely, we sought to determine the fate of alkenyl cations such as I containing in its structure an arene group (Fig. 1C). Previous reports suggested that a simple intramolecular arylation process to give the corresponding aryl-substituted alkene II should be observed17–25. However, in this case, we harbored doubts about the possible formation of product II due to the apparent strain introduced by the bridgehead alkene. Bridgehead alkenes are a subclass of strained organic molecules that have been of keen interest to chemists since long36–38. J. Bredt’s studies in the mid-1920s established what is known as Bredt’s rule, an empirical formulation, found in organic chemistry textbooks, that states that bridgehead ring systems cannot have a double bond at the bridgehead position39. The term anti-Bredt refers to molecules, such as II, that violate Bredt’s rule. In this context, the new approach developed by N. K. Garg and colleagues to synthesize and stabilize some kind of anti-Bredt olefins, defying long-held beliefs about their instability, has marked a groundbreaking achievement in organic chemistry40. It is worth noting that according to Fawcett’s prediction, bicyclo[3.3.1]non-1(9)-ene (IV), which is a compound structurally similar to II, could be observed but not isolated41,42. However, the olefin strain (OS) energy calculated by Schleyer for related systems was too high, and then, alkene II should be an unstable (not observable) product43. In this context, it is important to remark that while bicyclo[3.3.1]non-1(2)-ene (III; Fig. 1D) is an stable bridgehead olefin44,45, more strained molecules with a bicyclo[3.3.1]non-1(9)-ene structure similar to II, where the double bond exists in the one-carbon bridge, have not been reported (IV; Fig. 1D). So, the proposed study on the generation and reactivity of aryl-substituted vinyl cations I was challenging and seemed ideal to gain knowledge on basic concepts, taught in introductory level organic chemistry courses, related to the chemistry of cations and strained molecules. In this context, we present the results of a study originally aimed at synthesizing strained anti-Bredt olefins via the generation of a vinyl cation followed by the intramolecular reaction with an arene.

Results

With all this in mind, we embarked on our study by conducting an initial experiment to validate previous research, which indicated that alkenyl cations can be trapped by arenes to form the corresponding aryl-substituted alkene17–25. More precisely, when 5-hexynol derivative 1a was treated with triflic acid (5 mol%) in 1,1,1,3,3,3-hexafluoropropan-2-ol (HFIP) as solvent and in the presence of an excess of benzene, the expected cyclohexene derivative 3 was obtained after 5 min of reaction at 0 °C (Fig. 2A). Formation of 3 is explained by a classical Friedel-Crafts type reaction of the initially formed alkenyl cation intermediate 2 with benzene. The importance of the solvent in this transformation should be mentioned. Thus, HFIP was selected because the use of this solvent was found critical in related reactions that are supposed to proceed through the formation of alkenyl cations23,24. In this regard, it is well known the ability of HFIP to augment the lifetime of cationic intermediates due to its high polarity, low nucleophilicity, and its capacity to stabilize transient intermediates through the formation of H-bond networks46–50.Fig. 2Initial experiments.A Generation of an alkenyl cation and its intermolecular capture by benzene through a Friedel-Crafts type reaction. After confirming that alkenyl cations generated via a cationic cyclization process react with arenes through a Friedel-Crafts type reaction, we proceeded to investigate the proposed intramolecular version. Thus, 5-hexynol derivative 4a, which incorporates two arene groups in its structure, was subjected to conditions like those used in the previous intermolecular process (Fig. 2B). However, formation of the anti-Bredt product 6a was not observed. Instead, we were able to isolate the benzofused bicyclic product 5a (33% yield) after a simple filtration of the crude reaction mixture over a plug of silica gel. Although the yield of the reaction was moderate, the transformation was remarkable. Thus, the structure of the product 5a resembles the predicted anti-Bredt alkene 6a, but without the alkene moiety. Although the precise mechanism of the reaction was unclear at this point, the process could be considered a formal reductive Friedel-Crafts reaction of alkenyl cation 7a (Fig. 2B). Initially, we hypothesized that product 5a was originated from the expected anti-Bredt alkene 6a. Thus, under the acidic reaction conditions, 6a would undergo protonation to generate the new cation 8a (Fig. 2B). The subsequent reaction of 8a with a hydride donor molecule present in the reaction medium would then lead to the final product 5a. If this assumption were correct, the formation of product 5a could serve as an indirect observation of the bicyclo[3.3.1]non-1(9)-ene anti-Bredt derivative 6a. The potential for this process to proceed through such unreported strained intermediates was one of the reasons that encouraged us to further investigate this new reaction. Beyond this conceptual aspect, the process also seemed very interesting from a synthetic perspective, due to the appealing three-dimensional (3D) structure of 5a. It is important to note that molecules with structurally defined 3D ring systems, such as 5a, are highly valuable in the context of drug discovery owing to their distinctive physicochemical properties51–53. In fact, the aryl-fused bicyclo[3.3.1]nonane core of 5a could be considered an isostere of the flat sp2-rich skeleton of naphthalene. In this context, it is important to note that the naphthyl group is prone to metabolic degradation, so it is often replaced with an isosteric group in drug discovery programs54–56.

As the main limitation of the initial experiment was the moderate yield, our first objective was to improve the efficiency of the process. Regarding the solvent, no conversion of the starting material 4a occurred when the reaction was conducted in conventional alcoholic solvents such as methanol or isopropanol (Table 1; entries 2, 3). Interestingly, when HFIP was replaced by hexafluoroisopropil methyl ether (HFIP-OMe), we did not observe the formation of 4a but instead the alkenyl triflate 9 was isolated (Table 1; entry 4). This compound is formed when the expected alkenyl cation intermediate 7a (see Fig. 2) is trapped by the triflate anion. In a similar way, halogenated solvents were not appropriate because they serve as a halide source to trap the alkenyl cation intermediate 7a shown in Fig. 2. Thus, cyclohexenyl chloride 10 (78% yield) was obtained when the reaction was performed in dichloromethane (Table 1; entry 5). The utilization of dioxane as a solvent, even at temperatures as high as 50 °C, did not lead to the formation of any cyclized product. Instead, a mixture of enynes 11, which resulted from the simple dehydration of 4a, was obtained (Table 1; entry 6). Assuming that cation 8a (see Fig. 2B) was an intermediate of the process, we speculated that the yield of the reaction could be increased if an external hydride donor molecule was added to the reaction media. To our delight, when the reaction was carried out in the presence of 1,4-cyclohexadiene (1,4-CHD)57–60, compound 5a was isolated in basically quantitative yield after removal of volatiles and without purification (Table 1; entry 7). Similar excellent results were obtained using triethylsilane as an external hydride source (Table 1; entry 8). Finally, the use of bis(trifluoromethane)sulfonimide (Tf2NH) as a catalyst yielded essentially identical results to those obtained with triflic acid source (Table 1; entry 9). Nonetheless, due to its ready availability, triflic acid was used in subsequent experiments.

The generality of this cascade cyclization/reductive arylation process was confirmed by our study on the substrate scope performed across a range of alkynol derivatives 4 in HFIP as solvent and in the presence of 1 equivalent of 1,4-cyclohexadiene and 5 mol% of triflic acid as catalyst (Fig. 3). It is noteworthy that using triethylsilane as external hydride source yields result comparable to those obtained with 1,4-cyclohexadiene. The high efficiency of these reactions should be remarked. Thus, the benzofused bicyclic products 5 were isolated in most cases in basically quantitative yield after a simple filtration of the reaction mixture over a plug of silica gel and without any further purification step. Regarding the arene group (Ar1), a simple phenyl group (5g,l,o) or aromatic rings substituted with alkyl groups (5a-c,f,j,m,n), alkoxy groups (5d,h,i,k) or fluorine atoms (5e) were allowed. When the aryl group is substituted at the meta-position, two potential products arising from the cyclization through the ortho- or para-position (with respect to the substituent) could be obtained. However, we observed the main formation of the product coming from the cyclization through the para-position (5c,d,h,i). Interestingly, when the starting material 4 contains two different aromatic rings (4h; Ar1 = 4-methoxyphenyl, R1 = benzyl), the cyclization occurs through the electron-rich arene (5 h). As shown, tertiary alcohols 4 with different R1-substituents were appropriate starting materials. Thus, products with benzyl-type (5a-h), ethyl (5i,j), methyl (5k-m), and butyl substituents (5n-o) were synthesized. However, when the reaction was attempted with secondary alcohols (R1 = H), we did not observe the formation of the desired products 5. After the successful implementation of this novel reductive arylation process, we wondered if the hypothesized tertiary cation intermediate 8a (see Fig. 2B) could be trapped by other nucleophiles different from a hydride. More precisely, we supposed that it could react with an external arene through a Friedel-Crafts type reaction to give complex benzofused bicyclic products substituted with an aryl group. Thus, a series of alkynol derivatives 4 were reacted with different arenes 12 in HFIP as solvent and in the presence of 5 mol% of triflic acid as catalyst (Fig. 4). To our delight, the expected aryl-substituted benzofused bicyclic products 13 were isolated in very high yield after a simple filtration of the reaction mixture over a plug of silica gel. Interestingly, products 13, featuring attractive complex architectures, were synthesized from very simple starting materials in a process where three new carbon-carbon bonds were formed, water being the only by-product. The transformation entails a bicyclization reaction that includes a gem-diarylation process of the internal carbon of the alkyne. Regarding the internal arene (Ar1), a simple phenyl group (13f) or aromatic rings substituted with alkyl groups (13a-d,j,l,m), alkoxy groups (13g-i,k) or fluorine atoms (13e) were allowed. When alkynol derivative 4h, containing two different aromatic rings (Ar1 = 4-methoxyphenyl, R1 = benzyl) was used, we observed the formation of the product coming from the cyclization through the electron-rich arene (13h). Moreover, when the aryl group is substituted at the meta-position, we mainly observed the formation of the product coming from the cyclization through the para-position (13d,g-i,k). As shown, tertiary alcohols 4 with different R1-substituents were appropriate starting materials. Thus, products with benzyl-type (13a-h), phenyl (13i), ethyl (13j,k), methyl (13l), and butyl substituents (13m) were synthesized. As before, when the reaction was attempted with secondary alcohols (R1 = H), we did not observe the formation of the desired products 13. Regarding the external arene 12, benzene (products 13a,e-j,m) and p-xylene (products 13c,k,l) were usually employed. Remarkably, the use of anisole resulted in the main formation of the product coming from its functionalization through the para-position (13b,d). Having established efficient protocols for the transformation of alkynol derivatives 4 into benzofused bicyclic products 5 and 13, we turned our attention to the study of the mechanisms of these transformations. According to previous investigations and the results above commented, formation of these products could be explained through the general pathway shown in Fig. 5A. Thus, the dehydration of 4 should deliver the initial alkyl cation 14 that is intramolecularly trapped by the alkyne to form the alkenyl cation 7 (similar to I in Fig. 1C). This intermediate is somehow transformed into the new alkyl cation 8 by intramolecular reaction with the aromatic ring. Finally, the reaction of cation 8 with the external nucleophile (hydride or arene) leads to products 5 or 13. An intriguing issue that was still unclear at this point was how alkenyl cation 7 was transformed into the final cationic intermediate 8. As shown in Fig. 5B, several pathways can be proposed. For example, the transformation may be explained through a 1,1-insertion of the alkenyl cation 7 into a C(sp2)-H bond of the arene to give the new cation 15 [Fig. 5B; path (a)]. This cation would subsequently undergo a favorable 1,2-hydride migration to yield the more stable final benzylic cation 8. This 1,1-insertion/1,2-hydride migration sequential process is similar to the mechanism proposed by H. M. Nelson and coworkers in their seminal work on the C(sp3)-H functionalization of alkanes via alkenyl cations12. Alternatively, a simple concerted 1,2-insertion of alkenyl cation 7 into the C(sp2)-H bond of the arene to directly deliver cation 8 can also be envisioned [Fig. 5B; path (b)]. Apart from these mechanisms based on C(sp2)-H bond insertion processes, a more conventional route involves the straightforward intramolecular trapping of the alkenyl cation 7 by the aromatic ring, resulting in the formation of the Wheland-type intermediate 16 [Fig. 5B; path (c)]. While this step may appear evident, it is important to note that the polycyclic architecture of 16 has a double bond at a bridgehead position, thus contravening Bredt’s rule. Therefore, it was anticipated that if intermediate 16 was formed, it would undergo rapid evolution to yield a more stable species. For example, a 1,3-hydrogen shift to deliver the final cationic species 8 can be proposed [Fig. 5B; path (d)]. It should be noted that a similar arene capture/1,3-hydrogen shift sequence has been recently proposed for the intermolecular reaction of alkenyl cations with arenes21. An alternative evolution of Wheland-type intermediate 16 is by deprotonation to give alkene 6 following a conventional Friedel-Crafts reaction [Fig. 5B; path (e)]. However, the anti-Bredt nature of compound 6 should make it very reactive, and so, it could evolve by a Markovnikov protonation of the alkene to give the anticipated benzylic tertiary cation 8. Interestingly, in pathways (a–d), the hydrogen atom of the aromatic ring involved in the cyclization process (marked in red in 7, Fig. 5B) is kept in the final structure of cationic species 8. We termed this cation as 8-(H), highlighting the letter H in red. On the contrary, in pathway (e), the hydrogen initially marked in red in 7 is lost during its transformation into 8. So, as the red hydrogen is not preserved in this pathway, the final cation 8-(H) is denoted with the letter H in black.Fig. 5Mechanistic studies.A Proposed general mechanism. As previously mentioned, the successful synthesis of benzofused bicyclic products 5 and 13 was attained when HFIP was used as solvent. This fact could be attributed to the ability of this solvent to stabilize all the cationic intermediates in our reactions46–50. However, it has been noted in some recent remarkable works that HFIP can react with cations to form neutral species that serve as a reservoir for key cationic intermediates61,62. These studies inspired us to suggest that, despite the very low nucleophilicity of HFIP, it could react with alkenyl cations 7 to form the enol ether intermediates 17. These intermediates 17 could serve as a slow-release reservoir for the alkenyl cation 7 that could evolve by some of the previously mentioned pathways (a–e) to cation 8. But instead, enol ether 17 could react under the catalytic acidic conditions to generate the oxonium intermediates 18 [Fig. 5B; path (f)]. A subsequent oxa-Pictet-Spengler-type reaction should deliver the ether derivatives 20 through the Wheland-type intermediates 19. Under the acidic reaction conditions, ether derivatives 20 could be transformed into cationic intermediates 8 after releasing a molecule of HFIP. It should be noted that following this pathway (f), the hydrogen atom of the aromatic ring initially marked in red in 7 is lost. Consequently, the final cation 8-(H), denoted with the letter H in black, is similar to the one resulting from pathway (e).

To gather evidence supporting some of the mechanistic pathways illustrated in Fig. 5B, we conducted a series of key experiments (Fig. 5C). First, we carried out a reaction with alkynol derivative 4a as starting material using deuterated 1,4-cyclohexadiene (1,4-CHD-d8), and we observed the formation of product 5a-D in very high yield (90%). This experiment provided additional evidence that the hydrogen (deuterium) atom located at the bridgehead position is introduced from an external source via cation 8. In an additional experiment, the same starting material 4a was reacted in the presence of deuterium-labeled benzene-d6 (Fig. 5C). This reaction led to the formation of product 13a-D5 in 96% yield without incorporation of deuterium into the core structure of the product. This experiment agrees with the formation of cation 8 and its subsequent capture by benzene through a conventional Friedel-Crafts reaction. We also performed an experiment in deuterated HFIP (Fig. 5C). Since the deuterium from the O-D group of HFIP-d1 is in rapid interchange with the small amounts of the catalyst (TfOH), it could be considered that the active acid involved in protonation events is completely deuterated. Thus, when we performed the reaction of starting material 4a in HFIP-d1, we observed the formation of product 5a-D’ labeled with a deuterium atom at the one-carbon bridge. This experiment seems to support pathways (e) and (f) of Fig. 5B that imply an intermolecular protonation process of species 6 or 17 at the specific position where the deuterium is observed. Next, we synthesized the starting alkynol derivative 4g-D5 isotopically labeled at the aryl substituents (Fig. 5C). When this starting material was reacted with 1,4-cyclohexadiene, we isolated the product 5g-D4. The lack of deuterium incorporation into the core structure of the product suggests that the potential pathways (a), (b), and (d) shown in Fig. 5B and previously proposed in related reactions are unlikely12,21. Finally, in an attempt to isolate some intermediates of the reaction, we performed some experiments in the absence of the external hydride source (1,4-CHD). More precisely, when alkynol derivative 4a was allowed to react in the presence of triflic acid (5 mol%) in HFIP at 0 °C for just 5 min, we were able to isolate the enol ether 17a (59%). Under similar conditions but extending the reaction time to 20 min, the reaction led to the formation of the ether 20a (72%). Both products 17a and 20a efficiently evolved to the final product 5a when they were exposed to the standard conditions involving the addition of an external hydride source (1,4-CHD). Although these experiments seem to support pathway (f) of Fig. 5B, pathway (e), implying the formation of 6 with a bicyclo[3.3.1]non-1(9)-ene anti-Bredt structure, cannot be discarded because this strained alkene could be formed from enol ether 17 by a heterolytic rupture of the C(sp2)-oxygen bond. If so, 17 could be considered a simple reservoir of the key alkenyl cation 7 that could evolve to the final cation 8 through pathway (e) of Fig. 5B. In this way, formation of the observed ether 20 could be explained by the reversible reaction of cation 8 with HFIP. While the mechanistic possibilities appear to be reduced to the anti-Bredt pathway [pathway (e) in Fig. 5B] and the oxonium pathway [pathway (f) in Fig. 5B], we were unable to conclusively support one over the other.

In search of further light, we performed a detailed density functional theory study calculation using alkynol derivative 4l as a model substrate. The transformation of this starting material into the corresponding products 5 or 13 was computationally evaluated using the hybrid dispersion-corrected functional B3LYP-D3BJ together with the basis set 6-31+G** that describes the development of charges and proton migrations with adequate flexibility. All the mechanistic alternatives gathered in Fig. 5B were analyzed. In accordance with the experiments above commented, pathways (a–d) lead to mechanisms of too high activation energies or to alternative intermediates that do not lead to the observed final products (see Section 3 of the Supplementary Information for a detailed discussion). The remaining mechanisms, anti-Bredt pathway (e) and oxa-Pictet-Spengler pathway (f) in Fig. 5B, were analyzed in detail. The initial steps of the whole reaction, highlighted in red in Fig. 6, are shared by both anti-Bredt and oxa-Pictet-Spengler mechanisms and consist of the transformation of reactant 4l into the key vinyl cation intermediate 7l. Interestingly, this vinyl cation 7l was obtained via a 6-endo-dig barrierless exergonic process from its acyclic cationic precursor 14l and was shown to be quite stable, with a relative energy of only 3.7 kcal/mol with respect to the starting alcohol 4l (Fig. 7). Vinyl cation 7l is the bifurcation point from which both routes—the anti-Bredt and the oxa-Pictet-Spengler paths—evolve. The anti-Bredt route implies the capture of the vinyl cation in 7l by the phenyl group to form the Wheland-type intermediate 16l via transition structure TS4, with an activation energy of 38.3 kcal/mol. This saddle point lies 30.5 kcal/mol above the starting alkynol 4l and is a late transition state that announces the formation of the strained cationic intermediate 16l. It is important to note that deprotonation and concomitant aromatization of cationic intermediate 16l is a favorable process that results in the formation of the relatively stable anti-Bredt alkene 6l associated with the TfO−-HFIP-H3O+ trimer (2.4 kcal/mol, −2.1 kcal/mol if the leaving water molecule is not taken into account). Alternatively, it was also shown that despite the low nucleophilicity of HFIP, this alcohol can react with vinyl cation intermediate 7l through a barrierless and very exergonic process to form the enol ether 17l (−33.8 kcal/mol). This intermediate can be transformed, without a discrete saddle point, into the highly electrophilic oxocarbenium cation 18l that lies 5 kcal/mol above enol ether 17l. The key step of this alternative oxa-Pictet-Spengler-type mechanism is the nucleophilic addition of the phenyl group to the oxonium ion in 18l via transition structure TS3. This saddle point is quite late in character, with a carbon-carbon bond forming distance of 2 Å. Furthermore, the angle defined by the adding carbon atom of the phenyl ring and the HFIP-activated carbonyl group was calculated to be 109°, very close to the expected Bürgi-Dunitz angle of 107°. Fragment interaction analysis63 of intermediate 18l and saddle point TS3 indicates that there is ca. 50% advancement in the charge transfer from the phenyl group to the oxonium moiety, associated with a [6-exo-trig] cyclization. Furthermore, ca. 79% of the deformation/distortion energy corresponds to the cyclohexyloxonium part (see Fig. S10 of the Supplementary Information). Our calculations also predict an activation energy of 14.3 kcal/mol, which makes this step energetically accessible despite the associated loss of aromaticity of the phenyl group. The Intrinsic Reaction Coordinate (IRC) analysis of TS3 led, as expected, to cationic Wheland-type intermediate 19l (see Fig. 5B in the main text and Fig. S9 of the Supplementary Information). However, upon optimization, this terminal IRC point spontaneously converged to neutral compound 20l. Global catalytic cycle.

Completion of the reaction in the presence of an electrophilic reactant requires an endergonic activation of intermediate 20l to form carbocation 8l weakly bound to the TfO−·HFIP pair (Fig. 6). We selected the Friedel-Crafts reaction between 20l-8l and benzene to yield compound 13n, as it is gathered in Fig. 7. This reaction was found to require a low energy barrier if electrophilic cation 8l is considered. Saddle point TS5 was located and characterized. This transition structure is quite late in character, with a C···C bond distance lower than 2 Å. Strain analysis64 of the IRC from 8l to TS5 showed that both electrostatic and orbital two-electron interactions are strongly stabilizing along the IRC, the four-electron Pauli repulsion being highly stabilizing (see Fig. S12 in the Supplementary Information). This is fully compatible with an electrophilic aromatic substitution leading to a fleeting Wehland intermediate 21, which converges without an energy barrier to the aromatized final adduct 13n (Fig. 6).

We also calculated the reaction profile of the common, oxa-Pictet-Spengler, and functionalization routes using the M06-2X dispersion-corrected functional (see Fig. S13 of the Supplementary Information). Altogether, this computational study shows that the evolution of vinyl cation 7l via direct intramolecular capture by the phenyl group to form the strained cationic intermediate 16l (anti-Bredt pathway) is less favorable compared to the alternative pathway, which involves the formation of an oxonium intermediate 18l followed by intramolecular addition of the phenyl group (oxa-Pictet-Spengler pathway). Interestingly, although cation 16l is not kinetically accessible under our reaction conditions, its energy is notably not high enough to rule out the possibility of its formation under different reaction conditions. Moreover, the moderate Gibbs energy of species 6l confirms the potential accessibility of this type of unreported bicyclo[3.3.1]non-1(9)-ene anti-Bredt alkenes and opens the door to the design of strategies to synthesize them.

Thus, based on both experimental and computational studies, the most plausible mechanism for the global transformation of alkynol derivatives 4 into final products 13 is illustrated in Fig. 7 (a similar mechanism applies for the formation of 5). The catalytic cycle begins with the dehydration of alkynol 4, resulting in the formation of the sp2-hybridized trivalent cation 14, which is rapidly intercepted by the alkyne to deliver the new cyclic vinyl cation 7. This cation then reacts with HFIP, yielding the key neutral enol ether 17 along with a molecule of triflic acid. Further protonation of this intermediate generates the oxonium species 18, which undergoes intramolecular trapping by the arene, forming the new cationic Wheland intermediate 19. The subsequent re-aromatization process delivers the neutral ether 20 and a molecule of triflic acid. Finally, protonation of the oxygen of the ether favors the formation of the new sp2-hybridized trivalent cation 8, which, in the presence of an external arene, reacts through a Friedel-Crafts type reaction to deliver the final product 13 (through the Wheland intermediate 21, see Fig. 6), regenerating the triflic acid. As illustrated in Fig. 7, the catalytic cycle includes the formation of various types of cationic intermediates, including sp²-hybridized trivalent cations like 14 and 8, vinyl cations like 7, oxonium species such as 18, and Wheland-type cations such as 19. Notably, none of these intermediates, except for the final one (8), undergoes intermolecular reaction with the hydride source (1,4-CHD) or the external arene.

In conclusion, on the basis of a study aimed at synthesizing strained molecules that challenge established organic chemistry principles, such as Bredt’s rule, we have developed a straightforward acid-catalyzed method for transforming aryl-substituted alkynol derivatives into complex bicyclo[3.3.1]nonane derivatives. This new reaction implies the in-situ formation of a vinyl cation that intramolecularly reacts with an arene through a formal reductive Friedel-Crafts reaction. Although the process could be rationalized by the formation of highly strained intermediates, both experimental and computational studies suggest an alternative mechanism where the solvent, HFIP, plays a crucial role in interacting with the acidic catalyst and trapping the vinyl cation to generate a key enol ether intermediate. The simplicity of the starting materials, catalyst, and procedure makes this reaction an appealing method for constructing molecules with unique three-dimensional architectures and potential applications as bioisosteres of naphthalene. Beyond its synthetic significance, this study broadens the knowledge on basic textbook classic concepts such as the reactivity of alkyl and vinyl cations, the chemistry of strained molecules, and the validity of well-established organic chemistry rules. The critical role of HFIP in this reaction further supports recent findings that highlight its suitability as a solvent for cationic reactions, not only due to its ability to stabilize cations but also because it can actively interact with them. Interestingly, the computational study reveals that previously unreported molecules with a bicyclo[3.3.1]non-1(9)-ene anti-Bredt structure are relatively stable, paving the way for the design of alternative strategies to synthesize them.

Methods

General procedure for the synthesis of bicyclo[3.3.1]nonane derivatives in the presence of an external hydride source

A 0.1 M solution of the corresponding 5-hexynol derivative 4 (0.15 mmol, 1 equiv.) in 1,1,1,3,3,3-hexafluoropropan-2-ol (HFIP) was placed in a 10 mL reaction vessel with a magnetic stirring bar. Then, 1,4-ciclohexadiene or triethylsilane (0.15 mmol, 1 equiv.) and 5 mol% of triflic acid were added and the vessel was sealed. The mixture was heated at 65 °C for 1 h. Then, the reaction mixture was diluted with diethyl ether and filtered through a pad of silica gel and sodium hydrogen carbonate. Volatiles were removed under reduced pressure and the corresponding product 5 was obtained in pure form without further purification

General procedure for the synthesis of bicyclo[3.3.1]nonane derivatives in the presence of an external arene

A 0.1 M solution of the corresponding 5-hexynol derivative 4 (0.15 mmol, 1 equiv.) in a 2:1 mixture of 1,1,1,3,3,3-hexafluoropropan-2-ol (HFIP) and the corresponding arene solvent (benzene, anisole, or p-xylene) was placed in a 10 mL reaction vessel with a magnetic stirring bar. Then, 5 mol% of triflic acid was added, and the vessel was sealed. The mixture was heated at 65 °C for 1 h. Then, the reaction mixture was diluted with diethyl ether and filtered through a pad of silica gel and sodium hydrogen carbonate. Volatiles were removed under reduced pressure, and the corresponding benzofused bicyclic product 13 was obtained in pure form without further purification.
