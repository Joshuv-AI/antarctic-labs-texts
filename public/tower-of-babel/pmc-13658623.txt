Unbound Intrinsic Formation Clearance of 6β‐Hydroxycortisol Is a Useful Biomarker of CYP3A4 Activity during Pregnancy
Authors: Aurora K Authement, Mary F Hebert, Emily E Fay, Nina Isoherranen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPregnancy results in multiple physiological changes that impact drug disposition. For example, hepatic CYP3A activity and renal transporter‐mediated clearance increase in late pregnancy, but the time course of these changes across pregnancy is not known. Endogenous biomarkers offer a minimally invasive approach to characterize pharmacokinetic changes during pregnancy and postpartum. The goal of this study was to determine whether 6β‐OH‐cortisol formation clearance (CLf,OHCOL) can be used as a biomarker of hepatic CYP3A activity during pregnancy. Thus, we measured cortisol, cortisone, and 6β‐OH‐cortisol in plasma and urine from 47 women during late pregnancy and postpartum and calculated the CLf,OHCOL, unbound fraction (fu), unbound intrinsic CLf (CLf,u,int,OHCOL), and renal clearance (CLr). CLf,OHCOL increased 1.3‐fold in late pregnancy while cortisol fu decreased significantly. CLf,u,int,OHCOL increased 1.8‐fold, corresponding to a 2.1‐fold increase in hepatic CYP3A activity. The increase in CLf,u,int,OHCOL was observed in CYP3A5 expressors and in CYP3A5 nonexpressors. In late pregnancy, the increase in CLr,cortisol and CLr,OHCOL exceeded the calculated increase in filtration clearance, suggesting increased transporter‐mediated secretion. The decreased fu of cortisol during pregnancy was captured by a model incorporating increased expression of corticosteroid binding globulin (CBG) and increased binding affinity of cortisol to CBG. These data suggest that CLf,u,int,OHCOL is a robust biomarker of CYP3A activity during pregnancy and could be used in future studies to characterize the time course of changes in CYP3A activity across gestation and postpartum. Further, CLr,cortisol and CLr,OHCOL may be biomarkers of kidney OCT and MATE activity and could be used to evaluate kidney transporter activity during pregnancy.

Study Highlights

WHAT IS THE CURRENT KNOWLEDGE ON THE TOPIC?

CYP3A activity increases during pregnancy, with approximately 2‐fold higher clearance of CYP3A substrates at late pregnancy. However, data from early and mid‐pregnancy are limited, due to the challenges in measuring CYP3A activity reliably at multiple occasions across pregnancy. Endogenous biomarkers such as 6β‐hydroxycortisol have been used to assess CYP3A activity during pregnancy, but the data have been difficult to interpret due to changes in protein binding and CLr of cortisol during pregnancy. Use of other CYP3A biomarkers such as cholesterol and testosterone metabolites during pregnancy is limited by long half‐life or narrow dynamic range of these biomarkers.

WHAT QUESTION DID THIS STUDY ADDRESS?

This study tested whether 6β‐hydroxycortisol CLf,int, can be used as an endogenous biomarker of hepatic CYP3A activity during pregnancy. It further evaluated whether cortisol and its metabolite CLr may provide a biomarker of kidney transporter activity in late pregnancy and postpartum.

WHAT DOES THIS STUDY ADD TO OUR KNOWLEDGE?

This study demonstrates that the CLf,u,int,OHCOL increased 1.8‐fold during late pregnancy, consistent with 2‐fold induction of hepatic CYP3A. By accounting for the changes in protein binding, we were able to isolate the change in hepatic CYP3A activity. Similarly, the kidney secretion clearance of cortisol and 6β‐hydroxycortisol increased about 1.5‐fold during pregnancy.

HOW MIGHT THIS CHANGE CLINICAL PHARMACOLOGY OR TRANSLATIONAL SCIENCE?

The use of CLf,u,int enables assessment of CYP3A activity even when protein binding and renal clearance are altered during pregnancy. This approach can be applied to minimally invasive studies of CYP3A activity during pregnancy. Collectively, these data support the use of endogenous biomarkers to predict changes in drug clearance across pregnancy and inform model‐based dose optimization in pregnant populations.

Cytochrome P450 (CYP) 3A enzymes are important in hepatic drug metabolism. CYP3A4 contributes to almost half of CYP‐mediated elimination of drugs. 1 , 2 CYP3A5 shares substrate specificity with CYP3A4, 3 but due to genetic polymorphisms, only 10–60% of the population express CYP3A5. 4 Even within a genotype group, CYP3A expression and activity show substantial interindividual variability. 3 , 5 CYP3A4 is highly inducible through PXR activation. 6 , 7 CYP3A5 lacks the distal enhancer element required for PXR‐mediated induction 7 and is less inducible than CYP3A4. Basal CYP3A4 and CYP3A5 expression do not appear to be dependent on PXR 8 , 9 but may instead be governed by other shared transcription factors. 10 , 11 Endogenous steroid hormones such as glucocorticoids, 12 , 13 , 14 progesterone, 15 and estradiol 15 have been shown to induce CYP3A4 and CYP3A5 expression in vitro. Hence, the hormonal milieu of pregnancy, characterized by increases in these hormones, may be a key driver of the increase in CYP3A activity during gestation. However, whether endogenous hormones regulate CYP3A4 and CYP3A5 in vivo remains unknown. Differential regulation of CYP3A5 in comparison with CYP3A4 during pregnancy could also have meaningful consequences for drug exposure.

During pregnancy, CYP3A activity increases, resulting in significant changes in pharmacokinetics of CYP3A substrates. For example, the clearance of midazolam and nifedipine is about 2‐fold higher in mid‐to‐late gestation compared with postpartum or nonpregnant state. 16 , 17 , 18 CYP3A4 protein expression was also increased gradually across the three trimesters when measured in small extracellular vesicles (sEV) isolated from plasma of 3 different women per trimester. 19 However, measurement of the urinary dextromethorphan‐to‐3‐hydroxymorphinan ratio, a marker of CYP3A activity, did not reproduce this time course of increase in CYP3A activity across pregnancy. Dextromethorphan‐to‐3‐hydroxymorphinan urinary ratio was affected already at first trimester and stayed constant across pregnancy. 20 As such, the time course of changes in CYP3A activity across the gestational timeline and through the postpartum period is unclear. This is a challenge since induction of CYP3A may lower drug exposure and reduce therapeutic efficacy throughout pregnancy. Yet, measuring changes in CYP3A‐mediated clearance across pregnancy and through postpartum to define the time course and magnitude of changes in CYP3A activity using validated probe substrates is not feasible.

Characterization of the changes in CYP3A4 and CYP3A5 activity across pregnancy would enable simulations and predictions of altered drug exposure across gestation and postpartum. Ultimately such data could inform dosing regimen design for pregnant and postpartum patients. Physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) modeling of midazolam, nifedipine, and indinavir has previously been used to capture the about 2‐fold increase in hepatic CYP3A activity during the third trimester. Sensitivity analyses pointed to hepatic rather than intestinal induction as the primary driver of increased CYP3A‐mediated clearance in pregnancy. 16 , 18 , 21 This is important, as endogenous biomarkers typically measure hepatic and not intestinal CYP3A activity. Hence, data collected of hepatic CYP3A activity using endogenous biomarkers can be incorporated into PBPK models to predict the time course of pharmacokinetic changes across pregnancy and postpartum.

Endogenous biomarkers provide a minimally invasive approach to assess hepatic CYP3A activity in vivo. Several endogenous CYP3A biomarkers have been measured during pregnancy, but the data interpretation is complicated by simultaneous changes in synthesis, protein binding, and renal clearance. The 4β‐hydroxycholesterol‐to‐cholesterol ratio has been evaluated as a CYP3A biomarker in pregnant women, but the observed 1.57‐fold increase in third trimester women is lower than the approximately 2‐fold increase seen with midazolam over the same period. 16 , 17 , 22 This discrepancy likely reflects the long plasma half‐life of cholesterol, limiting its dynamic range and responsiveness to CYP3A change. 23 A short half‐life biomarker is preferable to assess the time course of changes in CYP3A activity.

CYP3A4‐mediated 1β‐hydroxylation of deoxycholic acid has been proposed as a CYP3A biomarker. Although bile acids have been previously measured during pregnancy, 24 their application as a biomarker has yet to be evaluated. As biliary clearance and hepatic and intestinal transport are significant for bile acids, measurement of formation clearance for their CYP3A metabolites is not possible, and the urinary and plasma ratios can be confounded by transporter activity. 23 By contrast, CYP3A‐mediated 6β‐hydroxylation of cortisol (6β‐OH‐cortisol) responds rapidly to changes in CYP3A activity and can track the time course and day‐to‐day changes in CYP3A activity. 25 , 26 In nonpregnant, healthy volunteers, 6β‐OH‐cortisol formation clearance has been shown to correlate with the magnitude of changes in midazolam clearance with different levels of CYP3A inhibition. 26 In addition, 6β‐OH‐cortisol is entirely renally cleared, allowing direct measurement of formation clearance. However, the urinary 6β‐OH‐cortisol‐to‐cortisol ratio was not different between the third trimester and postpartum despite known CYP3A induction, 27 likely because the urinary ratio is confounded by changes in CLr that can occur as a result of changes in plasma protein binding and filtration clearance of transporter activity.

Cortisol and 6β‐OH‐cortisol have been identified as substrates of renal transporters. Hence, their CLr may reflect renal transporter‐mediated secretion. Cortisol has been shown to interact with organic cation transporters (OCT) 2 and OCT3, 28 but the role of OCTs in cortisol CLr is unknown. 6β‐OH‐cortisol is actively secreted by organic anion transporter 3 (OAT3) and multidrug and toxin extrusion transporters (MATE1 and MATE2‐K). 29 , 30 Endogenous OAT1/3 biomarkers suggest that pregnancy increases renal OAT1/3 activity 1.3‐ to 1.6‐fold in the second to third trimester 31 suggesting 6β‐OH‐cortisol CLr is also increased during pregnancy.

We hypothesized that increases in CYP3A activity in late pregnancy can be measured by unbound intrinsic formation clearance of 6β‐OH‐cortisol (CLf,u,int,OHCOL) while formation clearance (CLf) and urinary ratios would be inadequate due to changes in cortisol plasma protein binding and in cortisol and 6β‐OH‐cortisol CLr. To test these hypotheses, we measured CLf,u,int,OHCOL and CLf,u,int,OHCON in late pregnancy and postpartum as endogenous biomarkers of hepatic CYP3A activity. Our findings show the validity of CLf,u,int,OHCOL as a CYP3A biomarker during pregnancy and suggest that CLf,u,int,OHCOL can be used to study the time course of CYP3A activity changes during pregnancy and postpartum. In addition, our data suggest that 6β‐OH‐cortisol and cortisol CLr can be used to measure pregnancy‐mediated changes in OAT3 and OCT2 activity.

METHODS

Clinical study design

The clinical study enrolled healthy, pregnant (singleton pregnancies) women (n = 47) and has been previously described. 32 , 33 The study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT03117660), and the protocol was approved by the University of Washington Institutional Review Board (STUDY00001620). All study participants provided written informed consent. Exclusion criteria included history of obesity (BMI > 30 kg/m2 based on pre‐pregnancy weight), diabetes, kidney disease, or liver disease. Additionally, treatment for mental illness, vitamin A supplementation other than prenatal vitamins, or any current illness involving a fever or cough were exclusionary. The average age of the participants was 32 years (range 25–41 years). Of the participants, 81% (n = 38) were white, 4% (n = 2) were black, and 15% (n = 7) were Asian. Participants were genotyped for CYP3A5 as described in the Supplemental Methods, and 26% (n = 12) were CYP3A5*1/*3 and 75% (n = 35) were CYP3A5*3/*3.

At 25 weeks to 28 weeks and 6 days of gestation and again at least 3 months postpartum (Table S1 ), urine was collected for 4 h and a plasma sample was collected at the midpoint of the collection interval, approximately 2 h after the start of the urine collection. All participants took oral dextromethorphan at the beginning of the urine collection interval. 32 , 33 Blood draws were collected in the morning (Table S1 ), such that cortisol concentrations at the beginning of the urine collection interval were assumed to be near peak morning levels and then decline over the collection interval, as observed in reproductive‐age nonpregnant women. 34

Quantification of cortisol, cortisone, the 6β‐OH‐metabolites, creatinine, CBG, and albumin

Cortisol, cortisone, 6β‐OH‐cortisol, 6β‐OH‐cortisone, and creatinine were quantified in plasma and urine using previously described LC–MS/MS methods with minor modifications. 34 , 35 Details of sample preparation, LC–MS/MS analysis, and method validation are provided in the Supplemental Methods and Tables S2 and S3 . Protein binding of cortisol and cortisone was measured in each sample using rapid equilibrium dialysis (RED) as previously described. 34

Plasma corticosteroid binding globulin (CBG) was quantified as previously described 34 using a sandwich ELISA (Biovendor, Ashville, NC) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Cystatin C concentrations were quantified according to the manufacturer's protocol using the R&D Systems Quantikine ELISA for Human Cystatin C. Albumin concentrations were measured on both study days by the clinical laboratory of the University of Washington (UW) Medical Center.

Pharmacokinetic and statistical analysis

Metabolite‐to‐parent ratios were calculated for total and unbound analyte concentrations. Unbound concentration (Cu) was calculated as the product of unbound fraction in plasma (f u) and the total concentration. Formation clearances (CLf) of 6β‐OH‐metabolites were calculated as the ratio of the amount of metabolite excreted in urine over a 4 h interval (Ae) to the corresponding parent plasma concentration at 2 h (Cmid):

As cortisol is a low extraction ratio compound, the hepatic formation clearance is estimated by the product of unbound fraction and intrinsic clearance (f u•CLint). Hence, unbound intrinsic formation clearance (CLf,u,int) was calculated by dividing CLf by the f u of cortisol or cortisone substrate in plasma:

Renal clearance (CLr) was calculated as the ratio of the amount of the glucocorticoid excreted in urine over the 4 h collection interval to the corresponding plasma concentration of the same glucocorticoid at the midpoint (2 h) of the urine collection interval.

The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) during pregnancy and postpartum was calculated using the CKD‐EPI equation based on measured serum creatinine alone, cystatin C alone, and the combination of serum creatinine and cystatin C. 36 , 37 Filtration clearance (CLfilt) was calculated as the product of f u and the eGFR derived from the combined creatinine and cystatin C approach.

Statistical comparisons between late pregnancy and matched postpartum data were performed using paired t‐tests on log‐transformed data with false discovery rate (FDR) correction for multiple comparisons. Comparisons of CLf,u,int between CYP3A5 genotype groups (*1/*3 vs *3/*3) were performed using unpaired t‐test on log‐transformed data, and a P‐value < 0.017 was considered significant after Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons (α = 0.05/3). All data are reported as geometric means with corresponding geometric 90% confidence intervals. The relationship between CLf,u,int,OHCOL and the urinary 3‐hydroxymorphinan‐to‐dextromethorphan ratio, a previously published CYP3A activity marker measured in the same participants, 33 was assessed using Pearson correlation on log‐transformed data. Analysis scripts and individual participant level data on all the measures are available online (https://github.com/Isoherranen‐Lab/Unbound‐intrinsic‐formation‐clearance‐of‐6b‐hydroxycortisol‐in‐pregnant‐women.git).

Plasma protein binding model (PPBM) for cortisol and cortisone in pregnancy and postpartum

Two previously reported plasma protein binding models (PPBM1 and 2) were fit to the collected CBG, albumin and total and unbound cortisol and cortisone concentration data. Cortisol and cortisone binding constants to CBG and albumin were estimated as described in the Supplemental Methods . 34 PPBM1 describes independent binding of cortisol and cortisone to CBG and albumin, while PPBM2 accounts for simultaneous and competitive binding of both ligands. Both models were initially evaluated using published CBG dissociation constants (Kd) from a healthy volunteer dataset (cortisol‐CBG: 13 nM; cortisone‐CBG: 169 nM) to assess whether existing parameter values could describe the pregnancy data. Given the known alterations in CBG glycosylation during pregnancy, cortisol‐CBG and cortisone‐CBG Kd values were subsequently estimated by fitting both PPBM1 and PPBM2 to the data, while albumin binding affinities were fixed at published values (cortisol–albumin: 172 μM; cortisone–albumin: 519 μM). Model fitting was performed independently for each of the two study days. Model selection between PPBM1 and PPBM2 was based on corrected Akaike Information Criterion (AICc) and fold‐error metrics, with PPBM2 selected as the final model. As PPBM1 is nested within PPBM2, model performance was further confirmed using a likelihood ratio test comparing the two models within each study day. Additional model details, parameterization, and fitting procedures are provided in the Supplemental Methods.

To evaluate the robustness of PPBM2 parameter estimates, a bootstrap resampling was performed as described in detail in Supplemental Methods. In each iteration, 80% of participants (n = 38 of 47) were randomly selected without replacement, generating 10 resampled datasets (Table S4 ). Parameter estimation was repeated for pregnancy and postpartum datasets using the same model and optimization settings. The distribution of parameter estimates across bootstrap runs was used to assess model stability and to derive 95% confidence intervals.

RESULTS

Observed concentrations and plasma protein binding during pregnancy and postpartum

Total cortisol, cortisone, and 6β‐OH‐cortisol concentrations were increased during pregnancy by 2.7‐, 3.3‐, and 2.4‐fold, respectively (P < 0.001; Table 1 ), compared with postpartum. The cortisol, cortisone, and 6β‐OH‐cortisol f u values decreased by 24%, 12%, and 14%, respectively (P < 0.01), during pregnancy in comparison with postpartum. In parallel, concentrations of plasma proteins involved in cortisol and cortisone binding changed significantly during pregnancy. The CBG concentrations increased 1.7‐fold and albumin concentrations decreased 22% during pregnancy relative to postpartum (Table 1 ). Overall, unbound cortisol and 6β‐OH‐cortisol concentrations increased 2.0‐fold and cortisone concentrations increased 2.8‐fold during pregnancy relative to postpartum (P < 0.001). The total plasma cortisone‐to‐cortisol concentration ratio did not change significantly, but the unbound ratio increased 1.4‐fold (P = 0.0055) during pregnancy. The urinary cortisone‐to‐cortisol ratio decreased by 29% (P < 0.001) during pregnancy when compared to postpartum (Table 1 ).

Unbound intrinsic formation clearance as a marker for hepatic CYP3A activity during pregnancy

The CLf of both 6β‐OH‐cortisol and 6β‐OH‐cortisone increased 1.3‐fold during late pregnancy compared with postpartum (Table 2 , Figures 1 and S1 ; P < 0.05). The CLf,u,int, the measure of intrinsic CYP3A activity, increased 1.8‐fold for 6β‐OH‐cortisol and 1.5‐fold for 6β‐OH‐cortisone (P < 0.01) during late pregnancy when compared with postpartum, indicating significant CYP3A induction during pregnancy. The CLf,u,int,OHCOL showed moderate correlation (r = 0.32, P = 0.00182) with the CYP3A marker, urinary 3‐hydroxymorphinan‐to‐dextromethorphan metabolic ratio, previously measured in the same participants 33 (Figure S1 ).

Metabolite‐to‐parent ratios and renal clearance

Neither the 6β‐OH‐cortisol‐to‐cortisol urinary ratio, nor the total or unbound plasma ratios were significantly different between pregnancy and postpartum (Table 2 ). We hypothesized that this lack of change in plasma and urinary ratios is due to increases in CLr of cortisol and 6β‐OH‐cortisol confounding the metabolic ratios as measures of CYP3A activity. The CLr for both cortisol and 6β‐OH‐cortisol increased by 1.5‐fold (P < 0.001) during pregnancy compared with postpartum, while cortisone CLr did not change (Table 3 ). To explore potential mechanisms of the increased CLr of cortisol and 6β‐OH‐cortisol, we estimated CLfilt in the study participants. There is currently no established gold standard for eGFR estimation during pregnancy, 38 , 39 and therefore, multiple markers were employed (Table 3 ). Serum creatinine decreased 30% during pregnancy while cystatin C was unchanged. Depending on the measure used, eGFR increased 1.07‐ to 1.16‐fold during pregnancy. Creatinine clearance increased to a greater extent, 1.5‐fold (P < 0.001). For CLfilt calculations, we used the eGFR derived from the combined creatinine and cystatin C measurements, which increased 1.16‐fold during pregnancy. Using this eGFR, the estimated CLfilt of cortisol decreased modestly (12%) due to the decrease in f u, while the cortisone and 6β‐OH‐cortisol CLfilt were unchanged during pregnancy (Table 3 ). Comparison of the CLfilt and CLr of cortisol and 6β‐OH‐cortisol suggests that the secretion clearance of both increased at least 1.5‐fold during pregnancy while cortisone was not affected.

CBG‐binding affinity of cortisol and cortisone in pregnancy and postpartum

The cortisol and cortisone f u values measured during pregnancy and postpartum differed substantially from those predicted using the PPBM and Kd values derived from healthy nonpregnant women. 34 When the nonpregnant Kd values were applied to predict unbound cortisol concentrations during pregnancy, the model systematically overpredicted unbound concentrations, with an average fold error (AFE) of 2.28 (Figure 2 a ). Prediction of unbound cortisone was better but still showed systematic overprediction, with an AFE of 1.52 during pregnancy (Figure 2 a ). These results indicate that the protein binding environment in pregnancy is different from that in healthy nonpregnant women. When PPBM1 was fit to the data from pregnancy and postpartum study days separately, the estimated Kd values for cortisol‐CBG were lower and cortisone‐CBG were higher in the pregnant and postpartum individuals than in the nonpregnant reference population (Table 4 ). However, PPBM1 fit cortisone data poorly during pregnancy, with an AAFE of 1.77 (Figure 2 c ). When competitive binding of cortisol and cortisone to CBG was accounted for using PPBM2, model fit improved substantially for cortisone, reducing the AAFE from 1.77 to 1.36 during pregnancy and from 1.51 to 1.32 postpartum, with a corresponding improvement in cAIC from −82 to −112 during pregnancy and from −111 to −133 postpartum (Figure 2 e,f ; Table 4 ). This superiority was confirmed by a likelihood ratio test comparing the nested models during pregnancy (χ2(1) = 32, P = 0.000000014) and postpartum (χ2(1) = 24, P = 0.00000087). Using PPBM2, the estimated cortisol‐CBG Kd was 4.2 nM during pregnancy and 6.9 nM postpartum, compared with 13 nM in healthy nonpregnant women. Bootstrap resampling across 10 independent participant subsets confirmed the stability of these estimates (Table S6 ). The 95% confidence intervals for cortisol‐CBG Kd were 4.1–4.6 nM during pregnancy and 6.4–7.0 nM postpartum, and for cortisone‐CBG were 54–63 nM during pregnancy and 78–90 nM postpartum. The non‐overlapping confidence intervals indicate that cortisol and cortisone binding affinities to CBG were significantly tighter during pregnancy than postpartum. When compared to nonpregnant reference population, the Kd values estimated by PPBM2 for both cortisol‐CBG and cortisone‐CBG were lower in the pregnant and postpartum individuals.

DISCUSSION

The time course of hepatic CYP3A induction across gestation and through the postpartum period remains poorly characterized. Minimally invasive biomarker approaches that track these changes, despite the physiological complexity of pregnancy are lacking. Repeated administration of exogenous probe substrates across all trimesters and postpartum is neither feasible nor ethical in pregnant populations limiting opportunities for characterization of the time course of change in CYP3A activity across gestation and postpartum. Endogenous biomarkers offer a low risk minimally invasive alternative that can be measured repeatedly, making longitudinal characterization of CYP3A activity across gestation possible. However, not all endogenous biomarkers are suited for this purpose. An ideal biomarker must be sensitive and dynamic, responding rapidly to changes in enzyme activity, and must allow direct measurement of hepatic activity without confounding uptake or biliary clearance. In the present study, we measured cortisol, cortisone, and their 6β‐hydroxy metabolites simultaneously during late pregnancy and postpartum and calculated CLf,u,int,OHCOL as an index of hepatic CYP3A activity. Our results suggest that CLf,u,int,OHCOL can be applied in future studies to characterize the time course of CYP3A induction across all trimesters of pregnancy, and to assess the time course of return of CYP3A activity postpartum to pre‐pregnancy levels. In the present study, samples were collected 12–24 weeks postpartum covering a wide time window. It is possible that CYP3A activity and CBG concentrations and glycosylation are still returning to pre‐pregnancy levels during this time window. However, as each participant was studied only once postpartum, longitudinal analysis of the return of CYP3A activity is not feasible in this study.

The CLf,u,int,OHCOL increased 1.8‐fold during late pregnancy compared with postpartum (Table S7 ). After accounting for fm,CYP3A4,OHCOL, 26 the 1.8‐fold increase corresponds to a 2.1‐fold increase in hepatic CYP3A activity. This is consistent with the approximately 2‐fold increase in CYP3A‐mediated clearance during late pregnancy estimated based on clearance of midazolam and nifedipine, two CYP3A probe drugs. 16 , 17 , 18 This confirms that CLf,u,int,OHCOL captures the magnitude of hepatic CYP3A induction measured using probe substrates. Further CLf,u,int,OHCON also increased significantly during pregnancy providing a second marker reaction for assessing CYP3A activity. Notably, a similar 1.8‐fold increase in CLf,u,int,OHCOL was observed in both CYP3A5 nonexpressors and CYP3A5 expressors. No significant difference in CLf,u,int between CYP3A5 genotypes on either study day was observed (Table S5 ). The lack of effect of CYP3A5 genotype is likely due to the significantly lower catalytic activity of CYP3A5 toward cortisol hydroxylation when compared to CYP3A4. 40

The apparent formation clearance CLf,OHCOL increased only 1.4‐fold, reflecting the confounding effect of reduced cortisol f u during pregnancy and the critical need to correct biomarker measures for plasma protein binding. The lack of consideration for protein binding changes likely in part explains the inconsistent findings reported with urinary and plasma ratios across prior studies. In addition, urinary ratios are impacted by changes in CLr which can confound the measurements. There are additional limitations for use of other endogenous biomarkers during pregnancy. The 4β‐hydroxycholesterol‐to‐cholesterol ratio showed only a 1.6‐fold increase in the third trimester and, due to its 17‐day half‐life, is unsuitable for tracking longitudinal changes in enzyme activity. 22 , 41 Deoxycholic acid metabolites are confounded by transporter contributions and biliary clearance, making direct measurement of CLf impractical. 23 Based on the overall analysis of CYP3A biomarkers, CLf,u,int,OHCOL uniquely satisfies the criteria for a dynamic, hepatically selective, and directly measurable CYP3A biomarker in pregnancy. However, measurement of CLf,u,int requires additional analytical steps, including determination of the f u and collection of both urine and plasma samples.

Neither plasma nor urinary 6β‐OH‐cortisol‐to‐cortisol ratios changed significantly in this study during pregnancy despite the 1.8‐fold increase in CLf,u,int,OHCOL. By contrast, the 6β‐OH‐cortisone‐to‐cortisone urinary ratio did increase significantly during pregnancy. The urinary ratio is sensitive to changes in plasma protein binding and CLr of the parent and metabolite. The impact of CLr changes on urinary ratios is particularly important to consider during pregnancy as filtration clearance increases together with increased active transport. These changes can affect the metabolite and parent differently resulting in biased or misleading urinary ratios. As such, urinary ratios are insufficient to reliably assess CYP3A activity during pregnancy. The challenges in the use of urinary ratios are also illustrated in the analysis of urinary metabolic ratio of 3‐hydroxymorphinan‐to‐dextromethorphan as CYP3A marker. Only a weak‐to‐moderate correlation was observed between the 3‐hydroxymorphinan‐to‐dextromethorphan urinary ratio and CLf,u,int,OHCOL (Figure S1 ). This can be explained by the impact of divergent changes in 3‐hydroxymorphinan and dextromethorphan Clr during pregnancy and the confounding effect of CYP2D6‐mediated clearance of both compounds resulting in low confidence in the 3‐hydroxymorphinan‐to‐dextromethorphan urinary ratio.

The lack of change in the 6β‐OH‐cortisol‐to‐cortisol urinary ratio is consistent with prior similar observation of no change. 42 Yet, increased urinary 6β‐OH‐cortisol‐to‐cortisol ratios in HIV‐infected pregnant women 43 and increased excretion of urinary 6β‐OH‐cortisol during pregnancy 25 have been reported. The only prior study that determined 6β‐OH‐cortisol CLf and CLr in pregnant women was conducted in a population with acute pyelonephritis. 44 In this context, both CYP3A and OAT3 were likely suppressed by inflammation, limiting the interpretation of those findings in the context of healthy gestational physiology. Further, CLf was not corrected for protein‐binding changes, a significant confounder in a population in which CBG concentrations and glycosylation may be altered.

CYP3A biomarkers have been mainly validated in healthy volunteers. Whether physiological changes in patient populations affect the biomarkers is not well defined. This is a limitation of the current study as well since all pregnant participants were overall healthy and the study population had limited racial and ethnic diversity. For example, obesity or race and ethnicity may impact the measures and limit the generalizability of the findings. Further studies should be conducted to determine whether biomarker findings can be extrapolated to more complex patient populations and to estimating hepatic CYP3A activity in individual patients. In the present study, the biomarker approach was applied to evaluate overall healthy population level changes in CYP3A activity.

The CLr of cortisol and 6β‐OH‐cortisol increased 1.5‐fold during late pregnancy compared with postpartum suggesting an increase in their renal transport. By contrast, cortisone CLr was unchanged. The increase in cortisol and 6β‐OH‐cortisol CLr cannot be explained by increased glomerular filtration alone. GFR has been estimated to increase approximately 1.3‐fold in healthy pregnancy compared with nonpregnant values using inulin dosing. 45 The increases in eGFR estimated here based on creatinine and cystatin C were lower, 1.07‐ to 1.16‐fold during pregnancy compared with postpartum. The lower sensitivity of cystatin C for measuring eGFR during pregnancy is consistent with prior reports. 39 Assuming the maximum 1.3‐fold increase in GFR during pregnancy together with the 24% decrease in cortisol f u would yield a CLfilt ratio of 0.99‐fold. For 6β‐OH‐cortisol, due to the 14% decrease in f u, the CLfilt ratio would be 1.12‐fold. Neither value approaches the observed 1.5‐fold increase in cortisol and 6β‐OH‐cortisol CLr. Hence, the increase in CLr is most likely due to induction of transport‐mediated secretion. The measurement of CLr values in this study has some limitations. A 4‐h urine collection was combined with a single midpoint plasma concentration measurement assuming the midpoint plasma concentration reflects the average plasma concentration across the 4 h urine collection interval. If the plasma concentrations fluctuate during the collection interval, the absolute CLr value may be biased. Hence it is important to ensure the sampling time is consistent, and longer collection intervals and sampling across multiple time points are recommended.

The increase in CLr of cortisol and 6β‐OH‐cortisol is most likely due to increased activity of OCT2 and OAT3 respectively in the kidney during pregnancy. 6β‐OH‐cortisol is an established substrate of kidney OAT3, MATE1 and MATE2‐K. 29 , 30 Cortisol interacts with OCT2 at the basolateral membrane, and steroid hormones including cortisol have been suggested to regulate OCT2 expression. 28 , 46 Creatinine CLr also increased 1.5‐fold during pregnancy, an observation supporting increased OCT2 activity. Creatinine is a recognized endogenous substrate of OCT2 and is considered a tier 2 biomarker of OCT2 activity. 47 The true fold increase in transporter‐mediated secretion may differ from the 1.5‐fold increase as the fraction reabsorbed of cortisol and 6β‐OH‐cortisol may also be altered during pregnancy compared with postpartum. Nevertheless, cortisol and 6β‐OH‐cortisol CLr may provide a valuable biomarker to characterize the time course of changes in renal transporter activity across pregnancy. Further studies are warranted to assess the specificity and sensitivity of cortisol as a kidney transporter biomarker.

In this study, during late pregnancy, total cortisol, cortisone, and 6β‐OH‐cortisol concentrations were significantly higher compared with postpartum, consistent with prior reports of gestational changes in glucocorticoid exposure. 48 , 49 , 50 Despite the large increase in total cortisol, unbound cortisol increased to a lesser extent due to the 24% decrease in cortisol f u driven by increased CBG and changes in cortisol‐CBG Kd. The cortisol‐CBG Kd was lower during pregnancy (4.2 nM) than postpartum (6.9 nM) and in healthy nonpregnant women (13 nM), indicating higher CBG‐binding affinity during pregnancy. This tighter binding is consistent with pregnancy‐associated changes in CBG glycosylation, which alters the conformation of the ligand‐binding pocket and protects CBG from proteolytic cleavage, maintaining a greater proportion of CBG in its high‐affinity form. 51 , 52 , 53

In conclusion, this study shows that CLf,u,int,OHCOL can be used to measure CYP3A activity even when multiple simultaneous physiological changes occur. This study is the first to show that pregnancy‐mediated increase in CYP3A activity occurs similarly in CYP3A5 expressors and nonexpressors. Our study shows that CLf,u,int,OHCOL can be measured using plasma and urine samples without administration of probe drugs to enable characterization of CYP3A activity across pregnancy trimesters and through postpartum. In addition, this study supports parallel development of cortisol and 6β‐OH‐cortisol as markers of renal transporter activity across pregnancy. The lack of significant change in cortisone CLr in late pregnancy together with more modest effects of pregnancy on cortisone protein binding and circadian variation suggest that CLf,u,int,OHCON should be measured in parallel as a CYP3A marker to provide additional confirmation of CYP3A activity. Collectively, these findings establish endogenous biomarker‐based intrinsic formation clearance measurement as a practical approach to characterize enzyme activity in pregnant populations. This is a critical step toward enabling future studies focused on clinical translation and directed at understanding interindividual variation in CYP3A activity and induction during pregnancy.
