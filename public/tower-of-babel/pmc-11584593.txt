Minimally invasive ALL technique using the gracilis in the treatment of chronic ankle instabilities in athletes

Introduction

Chronic instability represents a significant complication for athletes, with an incidence ranging from 15 % to 48 % [1,2]. Although appropriate rehabilitation, including strengthening of the peroneal muscles, proprioceptive training, reduction of swelling, and range-of-motion exercises, often alleviates residual symptoms [2], it sometimes remains insufficient, particularly for athletes involved in sports that place the ankle in varus, such as football, basketball, and dance, necessitating surgical intervention. More than 50 surgical procedures have been described to restore lateral ankle stability, ranging from direct in situ repair of the anterior talofibular ligament (ATFL) and the calcaneofibular ligament (CFL) to augmented reconstructions using autografts or allografts [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5]].

Surgical techniques are classified according to their principle: capsuloligamentous retension, which can be isolated or combined with reinforcement, substitution plasties using a free graft (gracilis, semitendinosus, plantaris, or third peroneal tendon), and stabilizing plasties utilizing the peroneus brevis tendon partially or entirely. The various surgical techniques yield relatively satisfactory early results. However, non-anatomical techniques have shown their limitations in terms of long-term ankle stability [2]. Anatomical reconstructions for lateral ankle instability using tissue augmentation have gained popularity recently [6,7]. Although the modified Broström procedure is often used, it may lack adequate capsular tissue for retensioning [7,8]. Recent studies have also highlighted the importance of reconstructing the calcaneofibular ligament (CFL). Colville and others have noted the problems associated with non-anatomical reconstructions using a peroneus brevis tenodesis [[9], [10], [11]]. Some of these repairs achieve stability by reconstructing or replacing the anterior talofibular ligament (ATFL) and CFL, while others use a tenodesis that restricts hindfoot motion [6]. The kinematic function of the ankle and subtalar joint can be substantially altered depending on the reconstruction method. A gracilis tendon graft is an excellent source of tissue, leaving the semitendinosus tendon intact for potential knee surgery. This autograft is used to reconstruct both the ATFL and CFL, with the graft fixed through drill holes in the talus, calcaneus, and fibula for an anatomical reconstruction of both ligaments [1]. This technique was described by Coughlin et al. using a 10 cm long “L”-shaped incision centered on the posterior aspect of the fibula and extending anteriorly and distally along the lateral aspect of the calcaneus [1].

The aim of this work is to describe the ALL (Ankle Lateral Ligamentoplasty) surgical technique using a tendon transfer of the gracilis muscle tendon for chronic ankle instability in athletes. This minimally invasive method offers several advantages, including the preservation of anatomical and proprioceptive stability elements of the ankle, the anatomical restoration of ruptured lateral collateral ligaments, and the preservation of the semitendinosus tendon for potential knee surgery. However, despite its potential benefits, this technique remains underreported in the scientific literature. We will examine the advantages and limitations of this approach while reviewing the existing literature to provide a comprehensive framework for its effectiveness and applications. This work aims to fill the gap in detailed information about the ALL technique and to offer perspectives for its future use in managing chronic ankle instability in athletes,

This work has been reported in line with the SCARE criteria [12].

Methods and operative technique [13]

Installation

The patient is positioned in a supine position with a bolster under the buttock on the side of the operation. A tourniquet is placed at the base of the thigh. A bar under the sole of the foot allows for stabilizing the knee in 90° flexion during the harvesting of the gracilis tendon. Then, the limb is extended to proceed with the ankle ligamentoplasty.

Harvesting and preparation of the “Gracilis Tendon” graft

The foot is placed on the support bar, with the knee in flexion. An oblique incision is made, centered on the pes anserinus. The gracilis tendon is entirely harvested using a tendon stripper and a harvesting hook, preserving its distal pedicled insertion (Fig. 1). The tendon is then cleaned of any remaining muscle fibers. The required length is approximately 15 cm.

A: Harvesting the gracilis tendon at the knee – B: Graft tubing technique – C: Final appearance of the graft after tubing with Vicryl 1.

The free end of the tendon is tubularized using a continuous suture with Vicryl 1 over approximately 15 cm to facilitate its passage through the tunnels (Fig. 1). Its distal insertion is cut flush with the periosteum. The graft is then calibrated and kept in a moist compress (Fig. 2).

Calibration of the tubed graft with measurement of the diameter.

Preparation of the blind talar tunnel

A 1.5 cm pre-malleolar incision is made between the anterior edge of the lateral malleolus and the talar crest, identified by palpation, with the foot in a varus equinus position. The capsular incision is parallel to the skin incision. The lateral talar crest is exposed at the osteochondral junction and cleared of the capsular and ligamentous attachments of the anterior talofibular ligament bundle. With the ankle at 90°, a 1.6 mm guide wire is inserted in front of the talar crest, directed medially and posteriorly into the body of the talus at a 45° angle, while remaining parallel to the sole to avoid joint penetration (Fig. 3). A blind tunnel 20 mm deep is drilled into the talus using a 6 mm graduated and cannulated drill bit over the guide wire, after placing the soft tissue protector. Through the same incision, the capsular attachments and ligament remnants from the anterior surface of the lateral malleolus, where the anterior bundle of the talofibular ligament inserts, are resected. This area is anatomically flat and triangular in shape with a proximal base.

A: The skin incision for the preparation of the talar tunnel. B: The drilling angle of the tunnel directed medially and posteriorly into the talus at a 45° angle (right).

Preparation of the malleolar tunnels and placement of the guider

A second longitudinal incision, approximately 5 mm in length, is made at the back of the posterior body of the lateral malleolus (Fig. 4A). The peroneal tendon sheath is incised parallel to and the same length as the skin incision. The targeting guide (Fig. 4B) is equipped with two barrels that are positioned on the anterior facet of the lateral malleolus, and a targeting tip placed at the posterior edge of the lateral malleolus, just in front of the peroneal tendons. It is crucial to properly center the guide barrels on the anterior facet of the malleolus to avoid cortical breach during drilling. Two 1.6 mm guide wires are inserted through the converging guide barrels, passing through both cortices of the lateral malleolus. The guide is then removed. It is important to check the proper centering of the wires on the anterior facet of the malleolus, ensuring they are equidistant from the medial and lateral cortices. The lower wire should be located approximately 1 cm above the tip of the fibula. The two converging fibular tunnels are then drilled, typically using 4 mm diameter drill bits. Drill bits of 3.5 mm or 4.5 mm can also be used depending on the size of the graft or the fibula.

A: The longitudinal retro-malleolar incision for creating the two fibular tunnels. B: The guider with two convergent cannulas.

Graft fixation (anterior talofibular bundle) and malleolar passage

The distal end of the gracilis tendon (the widest part of the tendon) is placed at the bottom of the blind talar tunnel and stabilized with a guide wire. With the foot at 90°, fixation is secured using a cannulated PLLA interference screw (FH Orthopedics, Heimsbrunn, France) measuring 6 mm in diameter and 20 mm in length, buried flush with the bone surface (Fig. 5). The proximal end of the gracilis tendon is introduced into the upper fibular tunnel using a suture passer. A curved suture passer is used to pull the tendon through the lower fibular tunnel (Fig. 5).

Fixation of the graft (anterior talofibular bundle) and malleolar passage.

Preparation of the calcaneal tunnel

A 1 cm horizontal incision is made at the site of the distal insertion of the middle bundle of the calcaneofibular ligament, located 1.5 cm below and behind (at a 45° angle) the tip of the fibula, with the ankle at a right angle. The incision should be parallel to and close to the lower edge of the peroneal tendons to avoid damaging the sural nerve. Dissection is done carefully, staying beneath the peroneal tendons until contact with the bone is made. The calcaneal surface is roughened over 1 cm. A1.6 mm transfixing guide wire is inserted from lateral to medial and from dorsal to plantar, avoiding penetration of the tarsal canal. The transfixing calcaneal tunnel is then created using a 5 mm drill bit, with the use of a soft tissue protector.

Graft fixation (calcaneofibular bundle) and calcaneal passage

A curved Kelly clamp is inserted through the calcaneal incision, along the bone and beneath the peroneal tendons, to retrieve the end of the graft at the pre-malleolar incision. The tendon is then passed through the calcaneal tunnel using a straight suture passer (Fig. 6).

Passage of the calcaneofibular graft.

The leg is placed on a wedge, leaving the heel suspended to reduce anterior drawer movement of the ankle. The ankle is held at a right angle without eversion. Gradual tensioning of the tendon, retrieved from the medial side of the heel, is performed using the “sardine key” maneuver. With the foot at 90°, the graft is fixed in the calcaneal tunnel at the lateral entry point using a cannulated screw measuring 6 mm in diameter and 20 mm in length (Fig. 7). The excess tendon graft exiting through the calcaneal and medial openings is then trimmed.

Fixation of the calcaneofibular graft.

Precautions to take during the preparation of the calcaneal tunnel.

Rehabilitation is crucial for ensuring optimal recovery, especially for athletes involved in pivoting sports. During the first three postoperative weeks, a removable walking boot is used, allowing for full and protected weight-bearing. Passive mobilizations are started from the first day to limit joint stiffness, while flexibility and upper limb strengthening exercises are incorporated. From the fourth week, the boot is replaced with a soft ankle brace until the sixth week, focusing on active mobilizations, ankle muscle strengthening, and proprioceptive training. Weight-bearing activities, such as jogging, are introduced starting from the second month, while sports involving pivots and direction changes are gradually reintegrated from the third month. Progress should be closely monitored, with adjustments based on pain tolerance and ankle function, to ensure a safe and effective return to sports activities.

Discussion

The minimally invasive technique using the gracilis tendon for the anatomical reconstruction of the anterior and middle bundles of the lateral ankle ligament offers several advantages over other methods. The primary goal of lateral ankle reconstruction is to stabilize the ankle without compromising hindfoot or ankle mobility [1].

An in situ repair of the anterior talofibular ligament (ATFL) was described by Brostrom and later modified by Gould [7,14]. While generally satisfactory, this method has limitations, particularly in cases of chronic ankle instability, failed previous reconstruction, intense sports activity, or generalized ligamentous laxity [14]. Many reports have added tissue grafts to reinforce this repair. However, a non-augmented Brostrom repair does not address issues of weak or attenuated tissues and insufficiency of the calcaneofibular ligament (CFL), which is often associated with subtalar instability. The frequency of CFL involvement is significantly higher than the 23 % initially reported by Brostrom, ranging between 40 % and 71 % [7,15].

Repairing only the ATFL often proves insufficient to restore ankle stability in athletes. Studies confirm that simultaneous reconstruction of the ATFL and CFL increases the likelihood of postoperative stability of the ankle and subtalar joint [16]. Moreover, an in situ direct repair requires adequate ligamentous tissue, which is not always present, often necessitating augmentation. Barbari et al. and other researchers have reported cases of insufficiency in lateral ligament structures, requiring augmentation for adequate repair [2,9]. However, the stability achieved is largely due to the augmentation with the gracilis tendon, rather than a direct in situ repair of the ATFL [1,17].

Tenodesis procedures, while stabilizing, are associated with a high rate of recurrent instability, requiring revision interventions [6,17]. They also lead to postoperative restrictions in ankle and subtalar movement, as noted by Clanton [18]. In contrast, anatomical reconstruction using the gracilis tendon, without restricting subtalar movement, offers better functionality and a more natural recovery. Studies by Colville and others show that anatomical reconstruction of the ATFL and CFL, without restricting movement, is ideal. This method allows for better range of motion, faster strength recovery, and a significant reduction in pain and instability symptoms [9]. Additionally, the incidence of degenerative arthritis is reduced compared to non-anatomical reconstructions, confirming the superiority of this minimally invasive approach using the gracilis tendon [1,13,15].

Consequently, we agree with Colville and others, who suggested that an ideal ligament reconstruction should anatomically recreate the anterior talofibular and calcaneofibular ligaments without restricting subtalar movement. Paterson et al. reported an anterior talofibular ligament reconstruction using a free semitendinosus graft [19]. They observed no difference in the range of motion of the ankle and subtalar joint compared to the non-operated side. Colville et al., in an anatomical study, demonstrated subtalar motion restriction as well as a substantial change in graft length after all non-anatomical tenodesis procedures [9].

The assessment of knee strength after harvesting the hamstring tendon was conducted by Yasuda et al., who noted a recovery of strength approximately one year after the operation. Pain related to activity in the donor knee resolved three months after surgery [20,21].

Several authors, such as Sammarco, have concluded that the longer the duration of ankle instability, the higher the risk of developing ankle osteoarthritis. Several follow-up reports have noted that non-anatomical ankle reconstruction procedures have a high incidence of radiographic signs of degenerative arthritis of the ankle [22].

Conclusion

Chronic ankle instability is a common and concerning complication in athletes, particularly those involved in sports that place the ankle in varus. While rehabilitation is often beneficial, surgical intervention sometimes becomes necessary. The ALL (Ankle Lateral Ligamentoplasty) technique, using gracilis tendon transfer, presents itself as a promising option. This minimally invasive method aims to preserve the anatomical and proprioceptive structures of the ankle while allowing for anatomical reconstruction of the lateral ligaments, specifically the anterior talofibular ligament (ATFL) and the calcaneofibular ligament (CFL). Results from this technique show effective ankle stabilization without compromising hindfoot mobility, in contrast to tenodesis procedures, which, although stabilizing, restrict movement and increase the risk of recurrent instability. Moreover, anatomical reconstruction with the gracilis tendon facilitates rapid strength recovery and significant reduction in pain and instability symptoms, while decreasing the incidence of degenerative arthritis.

In conclusion, the ALL technique offers a superior surgical alternative for athletes suffering from chronic ankle instability, combining effectiveness with respect for the functional anatomy of the ankle. It warrants further study and adoption in clinical practice to enhance the management of this condition and optimize functional outcomes for athletic patients.
