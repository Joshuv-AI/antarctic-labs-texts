ZNF556 serves as an immunological and prognostic biomarker in colon adenocacinoma
Authors: Cheng Huang, Shidong Feng, Hui Yi, Wenqian Tao, Yue Li, Zhiwei Liao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesColon adenocarcinoma (COAD) is one of the most common and deadliest digestive tract malignancies, but its diagnostic and prognostic markers still need further exploration as targets for risk stratification and treatment response.MethodsCOAD cohorts were retrieved from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) databases. Differences in gene expression were analyzed using relevant R packages, followed by gene function enrichment analysis and Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA). Next, the Kaplan-Meier method and LASSO regression analysis were employed to screen for prognostic signature genes associated with the survival of COAD patients. Furthermore, comprehensive analyses were conducted to assess the role of this signature gene in immunomodulation and genetic mutation. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analyses were performed using the GSE166555 dataset. Additionally, CCK-8, colony formation, wound healing and Transwell assays were used to evaluate the biological functions of the signature gene in COAD cells.ResultsDifferential expression analysis and WGCNA led to the identification of 156 candidate genes, which were primarily enriched in functional pathways including the cell cycle, P53 signaling pathway, and cellular senescence. Subsequently, LASSO regression combined with survival analysis screened out ZNF556 as a characteristic prognostic gene for COAD. The expression level of ZNF556 correlates with the infiltration of plasma cells, mast cells, and monocytes, and is further associated with the expression of multiple immune checkpoint genes. Additionally, gene mutation analysis revealed a significant correlation between mutant TP53 and ZNF556 expression. ScRNA-seq analysis further indicated that, in the context of COAD, ZNF556 may exert a key regulatory role in the differentiation of epithelial cells, thereby facilitating tumor initiation and progression. Furthermore, subsequent experiments confirmed the cancer-promoting effect of ZNF556, whose expression was positively regulated by mutant TP53.ConclusionZNF556 was highly up-regulated in COAD tissues, with high expression correlating with poor prognosis and difference in TIICs. It functions as a diagnostic, immunological and prognostic biomarker for COAD, and a potential therapeutic target.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12885-026-16596-w.

Introduction

Colon adenocarcinoma (COAD) is the fourth most common cancer worldwide, with 1,142,222 new diagnoses and 538,138 patients died from COAD in 2022 [1]. The American Cancer Society further projects that, in the United States alone, there will be approximately 107,320 new cases of colon cancer in 2025, including 54,510 in males and 52,810 in females [2]. Notably, it is worth noting that up to 75–80% of COAD patients are at the operable stage at the time of diagnosis and undergo surgery for therapeutic purposes [3]. However, up to 50% of these patients with initial local diseases will develop metastasis and have a poor prognosis, especially 10–15% of COAD patients are diagnosed with locally advanced stage, defined as invasion of extramural or nodal invasion [4, 5]. Approximately 20% of patients with COAD have metastases at the time of diagnosis [6]. The 5-year survival rate of patients with metastatic COAD varies greatly (10–30%) due to individual differences in response to systemic treatment carried out by a multidisciplinary team [6–8]. To stratify the prognostic risk and treatment response of COAD patients, some markers have been developed, but there are still more key genes to be explored.

The TNM staging system, derived from clinicopathological evaluations of the primary tumor lesion(T), regional lymph node metastasis(N), and distant metastasis(M), has long served as the cornerstone for predicting clinical outcomes in patients with COAD [9]. To further enhance the precision of prognostic prediction, indicators such as vascular invasion [10], tumor deposits [11], and tumor budding [12] were employed for risk stratification of COAD patients. With the deepening of researches, a multitude of key oncogenes and suppressor genes, including BRAF [13], members of the RAS family [14], and phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K) [15], adenomatous polyposis coli (APC) [16], tumor protein (TP53) [17], mismatch repair (MMR) genes [18] that regulate microsatellite instability(MSI), as well as epigenetic alterations [19], were identified to be involved in COAD progression and closely associated with patient prognosis. These discoveries gradually unraveled the mechanistic landscape underlying the initiation and development of COAD. Nevertheless, further exploration of additional key genes and potential pathways is warranted to deepen our understanding of COAD progression mechanisms and refine the prognostic stratification assessment system for patients.

This study was conducted using the COAD cohort from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) portal databases. Candidate genes were first screened via weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) and differential expression analysis, then subjected to least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression to identify prognostic feature genes. Subsequent immune analysis and single-cell sequencing analysis further elaborated on the biological functions and potential mechanisms of these signature genes in COAD.

Materials and methods

Data sources and processing

Count data and clinical information for the TCGA-COAD cohort were retrieved from the TCGA data portal (http://gdc.cancer.gov). A total of 514 COAD samples were obtained, comprising 41 normal and 473 tumor specimens (Supplementary Table S1). Survival information was available for 449 patients. Count data for 898 normal colon specimens were retrieved from the GTEx portal (https://www.gtexportal.org/) for downstream analysis. Three independent public COAD datasets, including GSE138202(eight pairs of tumor and normal tissues), GSE225182(six pairs of tumor and normal tissues), and GSE228012(12 tumor tissues and 11 normal tissues), were downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) database to externally validate the expression pattern of genes.

Batch effects between TCGA and GEO colon cancer datasets were corrected using “ComBat_seq”, a negative binomial model for raw RNA-seq counts. Only genes common to all datasets were retained as unnormalized integer counts. Batches were defined by dataset origin (TCGA or individual GEO series), with cancer subtype or tumor-normal status specified as the biological group (full_mod = TRUE). Empirical Bayes shrinkage was enabled (shrink = TRUE) to stabilize estimates given unequal sample sizes, while dispersion shrinkage remained disabled (shrink.disp = FALSE). Age, sex, and tumor stage were further adjusted via covar_mod.

WGCNA

WGCNA can identify co-expression gene modules and explore associations between gene networks and significant phenotypes, and decipher key genes within the network. In this study, based on the merged dataset gene expression profiles (TCGA-COAD and GTEx), the R package “WGCNA” was used to construct a scale-free co-expression network in order to identify the modules most correlated with COAD. The topological overlap matrix was calculated, gene clustering was performed, and dynamic tree-cut modules were identified. A soft-thresholding power of β = 14 was selected based on the scale-free topology criterion, at which the scale-free topology fit index (R²) reached 0.85. Module-trait associations were evaluated using Pearson’s correlation coefficient. The minimum gene number per module was set to 30, and the module merging height cutoff was set to 0.25. Then, modules were associated with traits and correlations were calculated using thresholds of |R| > 0.5 and P < 0.05. Finally, genes in the core modules were extracted. The module eigengene (ME) is the first principal component of the expression matrix of all genes in a module, representing the overall expression level of the module for each sample. The boxplot was used to compare the distribution of these sample-level ME values between tumor and normal phenotypic groups, where each point in the boxplot represents the ME value of one sample.

LASSO

To reduce background transcription noise before model construction, low-abundance genes with average expression value < 0.5 were pre-filtered from the candidate gene set. The LASSO regression model was developed utilizing the “glmnet” package (version 4.1) in R, configured with a binomial family distribution to accommodate binary classification. This approach incorporated L1 regularization to impose a penalty on the absolute magnitudes of regression coefficients, effectively driving the coefficients of less biologically relevant genes to zero while retaining only the most predictive and robust features. The optimal tuning parameter (λ) was identified through 10-fold cross-validation, selecting the value that minimized binomial deviance as the criterion for model performance. Genes were biting non-zero coefficients in the final optimized model were deemed diagnostically informative and were subsequently incorporated into the diagnostic model construction. For receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, stratified random sampling was applied to split training and testing datasets, preserving the original positive/negative sample proportion consistent with the raw clinical cohort without artificial oversampling or undersampling. The optimal cut-off value was comprehensively determined by multiple metrics including AUC, sensitivity, specificity, and F1-score, rather than relying solely on AUC value.

Identification of differentially expressed genes (DEGs)

Differential expression analysis of COAD samples was performed using the “limma” R packages to compare tumor versus normal tissue, yielding gene-specific P-values and log fold change (logFC) values. For multiple testing correction, the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method was applied. Genes with adjusted P value < 0.05 and |logFC| > 1 were defined as significantly differentially expressed. Volcano plot was then generated using the “ggplot2” R package for visualization. The intersecting set of DEGs identified by all three methods was carried forward for subsequent analyses.

To mitigate spurious differentially expressed genes (DEGs) arising from cross-platform batch heterogeneity and stochastic transcriptional noise within the integrated multi-cohort dataset, a multi-stage quality control and correction pipeline was adopted prior to differential expression testing. First, cross-batch technical variation was eliminated using “ComBat_seq” on raw count matrices. Second, lowly expressed transcripts with negligible average expression were pre-filtered to reduce random background noise. Third, raw P-values from differential testing were adjusted for multiple comparisons via the FDR procedure. Finally, only genes satisfying the dual criteria of |log2FC| > 1 and adjusted P < 0.05 were retained as robust DEGs. This layered analytical framework substantially suppressed false-positive differential signals and enhanced the reproducibility of the resulting upregulated and downregulated gene sets.

Functional enrichment analysis of DEGs

Functional and pathway enrichment analyses were conducted using the “clusterProfiler” R package to perform Gene Ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG). A threshold of P < 0.05 was applied to define statistical significance. GO enrichment analysis was used to uncover enriched biological processes (BP), cellular components (CC), and molecular functions (MF) from comprehensive gene expression data. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed to identify significantly involved pathways and associated functions.

Survival analysis

Univariate Cox regression analysis was performed to identify genes significantly associated with overall survival (OS) in the TCGA-COAD cohort. Genes common to the DEGs set, prognostic markers, and the module most strongly correlated with COAD were intersected to construct the feature set. To refine this selection, Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was conducted on the intersected genes, and those with P < 0.05 were designated as candidate prognostic genes.

Analysis of immune cell infiltration

The tumor immune microenvironment was characterized using the CIBERSORT algorithm (https://cibersortx.stanford.edu/), which estimates the relative abundance of immune cell types based on gene expression profiles. The LM22 signature matrix containing 547 genes was used to deconvolute the proportions of 22 immune cell types. Only samples with CIBERSORT p-value < 0.05 were included for downstream analysis. Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were conducted to compare infiltration differences between ZNF556 high- and low-expression groups. All immune infiltration results represent statistical correlations derived from deconvolution analysis, rather than direct experimental validation of causal relationships.

Genetic mutation landscape

Based on the median expression level of ZNF556 in the TCGA-COAD cohort, tumor samples were stratified into ZNF556 high-expression and low-expression groups. Somatic mutation data in mutation annotation format (MAF) format were processed and analyzed using the “maftools” R package (version 2.18.0). Mutational landscape analysis was performed to characterize and compare single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) profiles between the two groups, including variant classification, variant type distribution, and SNP spectrum visualization. Differential mutation analysis between ZNF556 high-expression and low-expression groups was conducted using Fisher’s exact test with FDR adjustment. Genes with an adjusted p-value < 0.05 and |log2 odds ratio| > 1 were considered significantly differentially mutated. Furthermore, drug-gene interaction analysis was performed using the “maftools” package to identify potential therapeutic agents targeting the mutated genes in ZNF556 high-expression group.

Single-cell RNA sequencing

Single-cell RNA sequencing data from the GSE166555 dataset were downloaded from GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) and processed and analyzed to investigate the expression patterns and cellular distribution of ZNF556. Quality control, normalization, and dimensionality reduction were performed using the Seurat package (version 4.3.0) in R. Low-quality cells were filtered out based on the following criteria: genes detected per cell (< 200 or > 6000), mitochondrial gene percentage (> 20%), and hemoglobin gene expression. Normalization was performed using the “SCTransform” method. The Harmony algorithm was applied to integrate cells across different samples and correct for inter-sample batch effects.

Dimensionality reduction was carried out via principal component analysis (PCA), followed by uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) for visualization. Cell clusters were identified using the “Louvain” algorithm at a resolution of 0.6. We acknowledge that the Leiden algorithm represents an improvement over Louvain in terms of community connectivity. However, Louvain remains a well-established and widely used graph-based clustering method in single-cell RNA-seq analysis and was implemented as part of our predefined analysis workflow. Importantly, the final cell-type annotation in our study was not determined solely by the clustering algorithm. Instead, clusters were further interpreted and annotated based on canonical marker-gene expression, dimensionality reduction visualization, and biological relevance. Thus, Louvain clustering served as an initial graph-based partitioning step, while cell identity assignment was supported by marker-based validation. Cell types were annotated based on well-established marker genes obtained from the CellMarker database and published literature [20].

For pseudotime analysis, the “Monocle3” R package (version 1.3.4) was employed to reconstruct the developmental trajectory of epithelial cells expressing ZNF556. Cells were ordered along pseudotime based on their transcriptional profiles, and branch points were identified to delineate potential lineage differentiation pathways. The expression dynamics of ZNF556 along pseudotime were visualized using smoothed gene expression curves.

Cell lines, lentiviral transfection and reagents

Four Human COAD cells (HCT116, RKO, HT29 and SW620) were purchased from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China) and were cultured under the condition of 37 °C and with 5% CO2 using RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, USA) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco, USA). Among them, HCT116 and RKO were TP53 wild-type colon cancer cell lines, while HT29 and SW620 were TP53-mutant cell lines. All the cell lines were authenticated using short tandem repeat (STR) analysis by Genetic Testing Biotechnology Corporation (Suzhou, China). Lentiviral vectors targeting ZNF556 and empty control vectors were transduced into HCT116 and RKO cells, defined as the shZNF556 group and negative control (NC) group, respectively. Meanwhile, lentiviral vectors for TP53 knockdown and corresponding empty vectors were delivered into HT29 and SW620 cells. All lentivirus packaging services were provided by Shanghai Huyuan Biotechnology. Detailed procedures for lentiviral transfection were provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Western blot analysis

Western blot (WB) analyses were performed in this study. Detailed experimental procedures for both WB were provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Cell viability assay, colony formation assay, transwell assay, wound healing assay

Cell viability assay, colony formation assay, migration and invasion assays, wound healing assay for COAD cells in vitro were studied as the statements in Supplementary Materials.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.2) and GraphPad (version 8.0). Correlation analyses were performed using Pearson’s correlation coefficient. For comparisons between two groups in experimental validation, Student’s t-test was used. Wilcoxon rank-sum test was applied for non-normally distributed data. All tests were two-sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant unless otherwise specified.

Results

Differential gene expression analysis comparing normal and COAD samples

We first obtained colon cancer–related transcriptomic data from the TCGA and GTEx databases, comprising 473 tumor samples and 939 normal samples. Batch effects were corrected across datasets, and T-Distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (T-SNE) revealed a clear distinction between tumor and normal samples (Fig. 1A). Differential expression analysis was performed on the integrated cohort using the “limma” package, comparing COAD tissues with normal controls, which led to the identification of both upregulated and downregulated genes (Fig. 1B). Enrichment analysis of the upregulated genes showed that GO terms were predominantly associated with nuclear division, organelle fission, mitotic nuclear division, chromosomal region, receptor–ligand activity, and cytokine activity (Fig. 1C). KEGG pathway analysis demonstrated significant enrichment in pathways including cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction, chemokine signaling, IL-17 signaling, and the p53 signaling pathway (Fig. 1D). Our study mainly focuses on the oncogenic role of ZNF556 and the activated signaling pathways in colon cancer. Upregulated genes are more closely associated with tumor progression, malignant phenotypes and pathway activation, which align well with our research objectives. In addition, preliminary analysis showed that the functional enrichment of downregulated genes produced scattered results without obvious biological relevance to our core research questions. Therefore, we only performed enrichment analysis for upregulated genes in this work. To identify disease-relevant gene modules, we applied WGCNA, which enables the discovery of regulatory networks based on coordinated gene expression patterns. Hierarchical clustering was conducted on the integrated dataset, and the optimal soft-thresholding power was set to 15 using the “pickSoftThreshold” function (Figs. 1E, F). Seven gene co-expression modules were identified, and the clustering dendrogram of all samples is shown in Fig. 1G.

Key modules identified through WGCNA

Among the seven co-expression modules identified by WGCNA, the midnightblue module exhibited the strongest positive correlation with colon cancer molecular subtypes with a moderate correlation coefficient (r = 0.55, P < 0.05) (Fig. 2A). Although this correlation is moderate in strength, it represents the most significant association among all identified modules. The topological overlap matrix (TOM) demonstrated that genes with similar expression patterns were clustered into the same branches (Fig. 2B). Moreover, compared to other modules, genes within the midnightblue module showed greater differential expression between normal and tumor tissues, with elevated expression in the tumor group (Fig. 2C). Based on these findings, we selected genes from the midnightblue module for enrichment analysis. GO analysis revealed that these genes were predominantly involved in biological processes such as chromosome segregation and mitotic nuclear division, as well as cellular components like the chromosomal region and condensed chromosome, and molecular functions including ATP hydrolysis activity and catalytic activity (Fig. 2D). KEGG pathway enrichment further indicated that these genes were significantly associated with pathways including the cell cycle, cellular senescence, p53 signaling, and DNA replication (Fig. 2E). We then assessed the relationship between module membership (MM) and gene significance (GS). The scatter plot showed a clear positive correlation (Fig. 2F), and genes in the upper right quadrant (MM > 0.7, GS > 0.2) were considered potential hub genes driving module–trait relationships. By intersecting these hub genes with the upregulated differentially expressed genes in COAD, a total of 156 core genes were identified for downstream analyses (Fig. 2G).

LASSO regression analysis was performed to identify the primary feature genes

LASSO regression analysis was performed on 156 core genes to identify those with significant prognostic relevance in COAD. From this analysis, ten key prognostic genes were selected: SPSB3, SNORA31, C20orf144, ZNF556, LRRC8E, OTX1, EPHX4, ARSH, NPFFR1, and SULT2B1 (Figs. 3A, B, and C). Time-dependent ROC curve analysis revealed that ZNF556 exhibited the highest area under the curve (AUC) of 0.991. We acknowledge that this high AUC value is unusual for a single biomarker. This exceptional performance reflects the inherent biological characteristics of ZNF556 in our cohort: the gene shows extremely low expression in normal tissues but strong upregulation in tumor tissues, with nearly no overlap in expression levels between the two groups (Fig. 3D). To validate these findings, independent datasets GSE138202, GSE225182, and GSE228012 were used as external validation cohorts, consistently demonstrating significantly elevated expression of ZNF556 in tumor tissues compared to controls (Figs. 3E, F, and G). Additionally, survival analysis across these genes showed that ZNF556 was the only gene significantly associated with overall survival, with higher expression levels correlating with poorer prognosis (Fig. 3H). These results underscore the potential of ZNF556 as a robust prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target in COAD. As a member of the zinc finger (ZNF) protein family, ZNF556 falls into the largest class of human transcription factors, exerting epigenetic regulatory effects and are widely involved in tumor progression. Accordingly, we selected ZNF556 for in-depth functional and mechanistic investigation.

Analysis of immune cell infiltration in COAD

Next, we investigated the immune microenvironment landscape of cancer. Using the CIBERSORT algorithm, we estimated the relative abundance of immune cell types in tumor tissue (Fig. 4A, B). This analysis reveals both positive and negative correlations, indicating that there may be cooperative or antagonistic interactions between immune subgroups. Further comparison of immune cell composition between ZNF556 high- and low-expression groups revealed that regulatory T cells (Tregs) and activated B cells were significantly enriched in the high-expression group, whereas resting NK cells were markedly decreased (Fig. 4C). Although the absolute differences in cell proportions between groups were not large, these subtle variations might collectively reshape the tumor microenvironment (TME) and exert functional impacts on tumor progression. To explore the immunological relevance of ZNF556, we investigated its relationship with the proportion of specific immune cell types (Fig. 4D). The expression of ZNF556 is negatively correlated with Mast cells activated, Plasma cells, while positively correlated with monocytes. Then, we evaluated the expression levels of cytokines and immune checkpoint molecules between the two groups (Figs. 4E, F). A series of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-6 and TNF-α, were significantly downregulated in the ZNF556 high-expression group, which may reflect the anti-inflammatory state in the TME. In addition, immune checkpoint molecules such as CD27 and CTLA4 were also expressed at lower levels in the high group. These statistically significant changes were mild in degree, yet they might jointly shape the immune landscape of tumor tissues. In summary, our findings highlight the complexity of the TME in COAD, including the immune cell landscape, cytokine and checkpoint molecule expression profiles, and their interactions. These insights may provide molecular basis and potential targets. To evaluate the potential interactions between immune cells, we generated Spearman related heatmaps depicting the relationships between various immune cell populations in the TME (Fig. 4G).

Distinct mutational landscapes and therapeutic implications stratified by ZNF556 expression

Next, we investigated the genomic landscape of tumors stratified by ZNF556 expression. Using whole-exome sequencing data from 390 samples, we performed comprehensive mutation profiling. Through comprehensive mutational analysis, we identified distinct patterns between ZNF556 expression groups. Tumors with high ZNF556 expressions exhibited burden of missense mutations and SNPs. C > T transitions represented the most predominant substitution signature in this cohort. The median tumor mutational burden (TMB) was calculated at 87 mutations per sample in the high-expression group. Recurrently mutated genes were identified, with the top five most frequently altered genes being APC, TTN, TP53, MUC16, SYNE1 (Fig. 5A). Then we presented a quantitative analysis of the most frequently mutated genes, displaying the precise number of somatic mutations identified per gene (Fig. 5B). Comprehensive oncoplot visualization of ZNF556 high-expression group generated with maftools demonstrated recurrent mutations in APC (78%), TP53 (65%), and TTN (42%), with 96.86% samples harboring ≥ 1 alteration (Fig. 5C). Pathway activation quantification revealed WNT (95.6%), RTK-RAS (77.0%), and TP53 (80.3%) as most disrupted (Fig. 5D). Then, we assessed pathologic stage distribution confirmed Stage IIA/IIB predominance using multinomial regression (Fig. 5E) and the stage distribution differences, found that TNM stages (III/IV) pathologic Stage IIB significantly enriched in ZNF556-high tumors (Fisher’s exact test P < 0.05; Fig. 5F).

To identify differential mutations, we calculated odds ratios via logistic regression and discovered TP53 was markedly enriched in high-expressors (OR = 2.237, 95% CI 1.62–3.09; P < 0.001; Fig. 6A). Single-cell level revealed the role of ZNF556 in epithelial cell heterogeneity and developmental trajectory in COAD

To investigate the role of ZNF556 in the TME of COAD, we obtained single-cell RNA sequencing data from the GSE166555 dataset, which included 12 control samples and 13 tumor samples. Based on this dataset, distinct cell types were identified, and gene expression differences among cellular subpopulations were characterized. Using the “Seurat” R package, we performed dimensionality reduction and clustering analyses and identified 14 major cell types based on well-established marker genes (Fig. 7A). For example, CD4⁺ T cells were defined by high expression of IL7R, SPOCK2, TRAC, CD4, and CD40LG; B cells by MS4A1, BANK1, VPREB3, TNFRSF13C, and PAX5; macrophages by C1QC, C1QB, C1QA, MS4A4A, and MSR1; epithelial cells by KRT8, KRT18, ELF3, LGALS4, and CLDN4; and fibroblasts by DCN, LUM, FBLN1, PDGFRA, and MMP2 (Fig. 7C). We next assessed the expression pattern and spatial distribution of ZNF556 across normal and tumor tissues. ZNF556 expression was predominantly enriched in epithelial cells, with notably higher expression observed in tumor-derived epithelial compartments. In epithelial cells derived from tumors, the expression density of ZNF556 is between 0.1 and 0.2, while in epithelial cells derived from normal tissues, the expression density is mostly between 0.06 and 0.09 (Fig. 7B). To further dissect its functional relevance, we isolated epithelial cells characterized by high KRT8 and KRT18 expression and subjected them to batch correction and unsupervised clustering. This analysis revealed seven epithelial subclusters, among which cluster 0 showed prominent ZNF556 expression (Figs. 7D, E). Subsequently, to explore the phenotypic heterogeneity and potential differentiation states of epithelial cells, we reconstructed a developmental trajectory using pseudotime analysis (Fig. 7F). Notably, the trajectory inferred a progressive maturation of epithelial cells from cluster 6 to other states. Among these, cluster 0 was positioned at the terminal end of the developmental trajectory, with ZNF556 predominantly and highly expressed in this cluster. These findings suggest a correlative relationship between ZNF556 expression and epithelial cell differentiation states within the context of COAD. The dynamic expression pattern of ZNF556 along pseudotime indicates its potential involvement in epithelial cell heterogeneity and malignant transformation, though further experimental validation is required to establish causal relationships (Fig. 7G).

Given that cluster 0 was positioned at the terminal end of the pseudotime trajectory and exhibited prominent ZNF556 expression, we conducted differential gene expression analysis between epithelial subclusters, focusing on genes uniquely expressed in cluster 0. Subsequent GO and KEGG enrichment analyses revealed that the differentially expressed genes were significantly associated with ribonucleoprotein complex biogenesis, cytoplasmic translation, cell-substrate junction formation, focal adhesion, and structural components of the ribosome (GO terms). The KEGG pathway analysis further implicated these genes in proteasome activity, protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum, salmonella infection, alzheimer’s disease, and axon guidance, suggesting cluster 0 epithelial cells may be involved in cellular homeostasis and tumorigenic signaling pathways, potentially in association with ZNF556 expression (Fig. 7H).

Silencing ZNF556 inhibited proliferation, clone formation, migration, and invasion of COAD cells

We examined the expression of ZNF556 in vitro. The protein levels of ZNF556 in normal intestinal epithelial cell lines and four COAD cell lines (HCT116, RKO, SW620, and HT29) were detected by WB. ZNF556 expression was elevated in COAD cells, with moderate upregulation observed in TP53 wild-type cells (HCT116 and RKO) and markedly stronger induction in cells (SW620 and HT29) carrying mutant TP53 (Fig. 8A). To further investigate the pro-tumorigenic role of ZNF556 in COAD, we knocked down the ZNF556 gene in RKO and HCT116 cells using lentiviral-mediated RNA interference. Western blotting confirmed a marked decrease in ZNF556 protein levels in both cell lines (Fig. 8B). The effects of ZNF556 knockdown on COAD cell proliferation and growth were assessed through Transwell, CCK-8, wound healing, and colony formation assays. After knockdown of ZNF556, the increase in OD value of colon cells was significantly slowed down, indicating a decrease in cell proliferation activity (Fig. 8C). In the colony formation assay, the number of colonies formed by cells in the shZNF556 group was significantly less than that in the control group (Fig. 8D). We compared the invasion and migration abilities of the two groups of cells through Transwell and wound healing assays. The number of invading and migrating cells transfected with shZNF556 was significantly impaired (Fig. 8E), while the relative width of the scratch in these cells was significantly larger than that in the control group (Fig. 8F).

We further measured E2F1 together with its downstream functional markers including CCNE1, MCM2 and PCNA via WB, and observed consistent downregulation of all these proteins upon ZNF556 knockdown (Fig. 9C). Collectively, these data indicate that mutant TP53 may drive elevated ZNF556 expression, and the oncogenic function of ZNF556 is likely mediated by transcriptional activation of E2F target genes.

Discussion

While the rise of non-invasive diagnostic approaches including imaging and liquid biopsies has heightened public enthusiasm for COAD screening, obtaining tissue via colonoscopy for pathological examination remains the most effective method [21]. On the one hand, diagnostic markers in tissues, particularly those for early detection, hold significant implications for the prognosis of COAD patients. This is underscored by the stark contrast in clinical outcomes between early-stage and advanced-stage patients, with 5-year survival rates of 90% and 14% respectively [22]. Predictive markers, on the other hand, could be utilized for postoperative risk stratification and treatment selection in patients. Numerous studies have validated the utility of single markers such as mRNA, lncRNA, and miRNA in the diagnosis, prognosis, and prediction of COAD. However, multi-gene panel models offer superior aCOADuracy and specificity. Examples include OncotypeDX [23], Coloprint [24], Veridex [25], and GeneFx Colon [26], all of which have demonstrated efficacy in large patient cohorts. Nevertheless, the development of novel biomarkers remains a valuable complement to existing clinicopathological staging systems and risk factor stratification frameworks.

In this study, we systematically identified and validated ZNF556 as a novel aberrantly expressed gene in COAD through multi-dataset bioinformatics screening and in vitro functional verification. ZNF556 exhibited robust tumor-specific expression characteristics, indicating its potential as an auxiliary diagnostic biomarker for COAD. Clinically, elevated ZNF556 expression was closely correlated with unfavorable OS and enriched TP53 mutation, suggesting its promising value for prognostic stratification of COAD patients. Mechanistically, considering that ZNF556 was positively regulated by mutant TP53, ZNF556 expression status might also provide potential reference information for individualized therapeutic strategies targeting the p53 pathway. Nevertheless, the clinical applicability of ZNF556 still requires further validation in large-scale prospective clinical cohorts before its routine clinical translation.

The ZNF protein family represents the largest group of transcription factor proteins, encoding approximately 5% of the human genome and participating prominently in a wide range of biological processes [27]. Based on conformational variations in their finger-like DNA-binding domains, these proteins are categorized into eight subfamilies [28], among which Kruppel-associated box domain zinc finger proteins (KRAB-ZNFs) are the most abundant family of epigenetic suppressors [29]. They exert diverse gene regulatory functions under distinct cellular environments and stimuli, with particularly notable roles in tumor-related processes such as cell differentiation, cellular metabolism, autophagy, apoptosis, and the maintenance of stemness [30, 31]. This constitutes the basis for some ZNFs to be used as tumor diagnostic and predictive markers. In breast cancer, ZNF589 exhibited downregulation, and its low expression correlated with unfavorable OS and relapse-free survival (RFS), particularly in subgroups of advanced stage patients and those negative for hormone receptors [32]. This association arises because ZNF589 expression shows a negative correlation with the cell cycle process at both transcriptional and protein interaction levels [32]. The expression of ZNF471 in renal cancer tissues was significantly lower than in adjacent normal tissues, with its expression showing a negative correlation with the stage and differentiation of renal cancer patients [33]. Currently, experimental research on ZNF556 in tumors remains limited; only two analyses based on public databases confirmed that ZNF556 was part of multi-marker predictive models, which were employed to prognosticate outcomes in patients with COAD [34, 35]. Our research not only confirmed the potential of ZNF556 as a diagnostic and predictive model, but also demonstrated through cell experiments that its overexpression is positively correlated with the proliferation, cloning and migration abilities of COAD cells.

The biological effects exerted by these ZNFs arise from their shared structural features. As a member of the KRAB-ZNF family, ZNF556, like other family members, structurally contains tandem repeats of the C2H2 zinc finger DNA-binding motif and a KRAB domain [36]. This KRAB domain mediates the recruitment of a specific and universal co-inhibitor, namely KRAB-associated protein 1 (KAP1)/tripartite motif-containing 28 (TRIM28) [37]. As sequence- specific DNA-binding proteins, after binding to KAP1/TRIM28, KRAB-ZNFs induce transcriptional silencing of target genes by triggering heterochromatin formation and DNA methylation [38]. Dysregulated expression of KRAB-ZNFs affects the complex formed by KRAB-ZNFs and KAP1/TRIM28, a key transcriptional regulatory complex that is involved in DNA binding as well as the expression of oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes. Accumulating evidence has demonstrated that multiple ZNF members are critically involved in colon tumorigenesis, tumor progression, inflammatory microenvironment remodeling, and signaling pathway regulation. The correlation between low ZNF575 expression and poor prognosis in colorectal cancer (CRC) patients prompted An et al. to validate the function and expression of ZNF575 in CRC through in vivo and in vitro experiments, which demonstrated that ZNF575 silencing induced the proliferation, colony formation, and induced apoptosis of CRC cells in vitro, while ZNF575 also suppressed CRC tumor growth in mice, likely due to its direct action on the TP53 promoter to activate transcription [39]. In contrast, ZNF70 promotes colitis-associated tumorigenesis by activating the NLRP3/IL-1β and STAT3 inflammatory axes [40]. Bioinformatic studies have also revealed that ZNF384 is closely associated with CRC cell cycle progression and DNA repair regulation through ceRNA networks [41]. On the other hand, ZNFs are also regulated by epigenetics. ZNF471 was demonstrated to significantly inhibit the proliferation, migration, and cell cycle progression of renal cancer cells due to abnormal methylation of the promoter CpG island, while promoting their apoptosis, a mechanism achieved by inactivating the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway to suppress the malignant phenotype of renal cancer [33]. The inhibition of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), Wnt and PI3K/AKT signaling pathways by ZNF471 CPG-specific promoter methylation became a key factor in the progression of cervical cancer [42]. A cohort study using immunohistochemistry revealed that the methylation level of ZNF577 was higher in lung adenocarcinoma tissues than in normal tissues, and low mRNA expression of ZNF577 was associated with poor prognosis [43]. In addition, while direct experimental evidence linking ZNF556 to stress-mediated cancer progression is currently lacking, its family member ZNF148 has been confirmed to promote breast cancer progression via the miR-335/SOD2/ROS oxidative stress axis [44]. Given that both are C2H2-type zinc finger transcription factors, we hypothesize that ZNF556 may also participate in stress-mediated tumor progression, which remains to be verified by dedicated stress intervention experiments in future work.

In this study, we further analyzed the impact of ZNF556 on immune cell infiltration in COAD. The results demonstrated that ZNF556 expression was negatively correlated with the infiltration of multiple immune-reservoir-related cells, including plasma cells, mast cells, and CD4 memory T cells. Such correlation implies that the unfavorable prognosis of COAD patients with elevated ZNF556 arises not merely from intrinsic transcriptional disorders of tumor cells but also from ZNF556-mediated remodeling of the TME. This divergent immune landscape indicates that ZNF556 exerts selective regulatory effects on distinct immune subsets. As a transcription factor, high ZNF556 preferentially drives accumulation of immunosuppressive Tregs and activated B cells to build immune tolerance, whereas it limits the infiltration of tumor-suppressive resting NK cells and dampens pro-inflammatory cytokine release. Variable correlation trends between ZNF556 and different immune populations further suggest that ZNF556 potentially modulates functional polarization of TIICs, eventually constructing an immunosuppressive niche facilitating COAD progression. Although there are currently no studies focusing on the immunomodulatory function of ZNF556, extensive research has been conducted on other structurally similar members of its subfamily. For instance, ZNF337 expression was likely to affect immune cell infiltration across pan-cancer, and patients with high ZNF337 expression may exhibit a poorer response to immunotherapy targeting immune checkpoint genes [45]. In study on esophageal cancer, Hong et al. analyzed the regulation of immune function by ZNF family gene expression and identified correlations between the expression of certain ZNFs and the upregulation of tumor immune activity [46]. Similar findings were obtained in research on pancreatic cancer [47]. Despite the widespread recognition of the link between ZNFs expression and immune cell infiltration, current studies have not yet provided in-depth insights into the underlying mechanisms.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. Firstly, as this research relies on multi-database bioinformatics analyses, independent cohort studies are still required to validate the reliability of ZNF556 as a diagnostic and prognostic indicator. Secondly, the biological functions and underlying mechanisms of ZNF556, which underpin the association between its upregulated expression and poor prognosis in COAD patients, need further verification through in vitro and in vivo experiments. Additionally, the regulatory role of ZNF556 in the TME remains to be further elucidated.

Conclusion

ZNF556 was found to be more highly expressed in COAD tissues than in adjacent non-cancerous tissues. High ZNF556 expressions in COAD were significantly correlated with poor prognosis. Furthermore, ZNF556 exhibited a significant association with tumor-infiltrating immune cells. Taken together, ZNF556 serves as a diagnostic, immunological, and prognostic biomarker for COAD patients and has the potential to be a therapeutic target for COAD treatment.
