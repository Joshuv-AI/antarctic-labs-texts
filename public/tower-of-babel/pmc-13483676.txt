A novel description of dynamic organ-specific trajectories in pelvic organ prolapse based on magnetic resonance imaging traces
Authors: Xiaoman Hou, Susu Zhou, Fuqing Feng, Xietong Wang, Aihua Li, Tianhang Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe dynamic motion trajectories of pelvic organs during prolapse remain incompletely characterized. Detailing these trajectories could enhance the understanding and treatment of pelvic organ prolapse (POP). This study aimed to generate and visualize generalized dynamic trajectories for the bladder, uterus, and rectum.MethodsThis observational study enrolled 63 symptomatic patients with Stage III or worse POP (57 with cystocele, 35 with uterine prolapse, and 29 with rectocele). Patients underwent dynamic pelvic MRI scans during three consecutive maximum Valsalva maneuvers. Pelvic organs were segmented by contouring to calculate their centroids. Centroid displacement was tracked with a coordinate system based on the sacrococcygeal-inferior pubic point (SCIPP) line. Trajectories were analyzed using generalized additive mixed models (GAMMs) to generate composite fitting curves, followed by segmented linear regression.ResultsThe fitted trajectories revealed distinct, organ-specific motion patterns. Based on the angles between the centroid motion trails and the SCIPP line, the bladder formed an inverse C-shape (three breakpoints, with slopes decreasing from 99.3° to 63.5°), the uterus exhibited an inverse S-shape (four breakpoints, with slopes fluctuating between 56.3° and 93.3°), and the rectum followed a near-linear path (slopes ranging from 62.3° to 67.0°). Furthermore, measurement approaches utilizing these organ-specific trajectories quantified prolapse severity more accurately than conventional, one-size-fits-all methods.ConclusionsGeneralized POP trajectories are organ-specific, nonlinear, and segmented, challenging the clinical utility of universal reference lines. The heterogeneous motion patterns of these organs highlight the need for organ-specific MRI assessment protocols and tailored surgical strategies to optimize clinical outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12880-026-02483-0.Keywords: Pelvic organ prolapse, Dynamic magnetic resonance imaging, Pelvis, Urogenital system

Introduction

Pelvic organ prolapse (POP) is a condition characterized by the descent of pelvic organs—primarily the bladder, uterus, and rectum—from their normal anatomical positions. Affecting up to 50% of women, particularly those over the age of 50, POP represents a significant public health concern due to its profound impact on quality of life, including symptoms such as pelvic pain, urinary incontinence, and sexual dysfunction, which typically arise from mechanical distortion of pelvic anatomy and compromised support structures [1, 2]. This anatomical pelvic displacement can lead to nerve compression or traction, urethral sphincter dysfunction resulting in stress incontinence, and intimate discomfort or psychological distress during sexual activity. Pelvic organ prolapse quantification (POP-Q) has been widely adopted to assess the final status of descent by physical examinations. However, dynamic trajectories of POP - defined as motion paths of prolapsed organs - remain poorly understood. A comprehensive understanding of these trajectories is essential for improving clinical management, as radiological assessments of the maximum prolapse require a reference line orthogonal to the direction of organ displacement [3], while surgical interventions also account for the direction against the prolapse propensity to suspend organs reversely. However, few studies have described the motion paths of prolapsed organs, and the selection of reference lines for POP assessment remains a topic of debate [3, 4]. These gaps not only hinder the development of standardized diagnostic tools but also compromise the effectiveness and durability of surgical interventions. Consequently, there is a pressing need for detailed profiles of prolapse trajectories.

As the affected organs are located within the pelvic cavity, direct observation of these trajectories is challenging. In this context, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) has emerged as a valuable tool for evaluating and managing POP. While clinical examination remains the cornerstone of POP diagnosis, MRI has become increasingly important for comprehensive assessment. It offers a non-invasive, radiation-free, and dynamic way of visualizing the complex anatomy of the female pelvis. Unlike traditional imaging techniques, dynamic MRI, which captures images during rest, strain, and the Valsalva maneuver, is particularly useful for assessing the motion of prolapsed organs. This technique provides superior visualization of pelvic structures and enables the evaluation of dynamic changes throughout the whole descending trajectory. By allowing real-time assessment of organ descent, dynamic MRI is valuable for characterizing motion features of POP.

This observational study aims to explore trajectories of pelvic organs during repeated maximum Valsalva maneuvers using dynamic pelvic MRI scans. We seek to identify general patterns of descending motion for the bladder, uterus, and rectum. To quantify organs’ displacements, we employ a two-dimensional Cartesian coordinate system, which includes a measurable feature of the moving object and a stable reference system. Motion trajectories are derived from extracted MRI frames, and the paths of the bladder, uterus, and rectum are described using fitting curves. Additionally, we investigate whether performing three maximum Valsalva maneuvers provides a more complete view of prolapse compared to one or two maneuvers.

Materials and methods

This prospective study was designed to investigate cross-sectional trajectories of cystocele, uterine prolapse, and rectocele, with the goal of identifying general patterns from these movements. The study was approved by the institutional review board of the hospital (SWYX: NO.2022 − 434) and written informed consent was obtained from each enrolled subject. For cases, MRI scans were obtained from patients with symptomatic POP who sought treatment at the Department of Gynecology in a whole year (July 2023 to June 2024). Eligibility criteria were consistent with those used in our previous study [5, 6]. Inclusion criteria were as follows: ages ≥ 48years, vaginal deliveries from 1 to 5, BMI from 18 to 32 kg/m2, and any point of Ba/C/Bp ≥ + 1 cm. POP-Q was confirmed by experienced gynecological surgeons in the lithotomy position under the maximum Valsalva maneuver, with the maximum prolapse defined as the greatest value measured using the POP-Q system. Exclusion criteria were that patients with POP-Q less than + 1 cm, or with previous pelvic surgeries (hysterectomy, caesarean section, etc.), or failing to finish Valsalva maneuvers were excluded.

Pelvic MRI was performed using a 3-Tesla scanner (Philips Medical Systems, Netherlands) with a standardized protocol. Scans were conducted in the supine position by experienced radiologists blinded to related clinical records. No premedication was given. Before undergoing an MRI, the patient should have a defecate and urinate if she needed. To improve visualization of the anterior and posterior vaginal walls, 30 ml of ultrasound gel was placed in the vagina. Prior to imaging, subjects were coached on how to perform the maximum Valsalva maneuver. Mid-sagittal pelvic 3D T2-weighted spin-echo sequences (repetition time: 600 ms; echo time: 66 ms; flip angle: 90°; slice thickness: 3 mm; resolution: 384 × 384) were acquired with a protocol in which each subject performed three maximum Valsalva maneuvers continuously during 16 frames. Scans were analyzed using 3D Slicer software (https://www.slicer.org) and AutoCAD (https://www.autodesk.com). Two experienced gynecologists, blinded to the clinical records, independently assessed the mid-sagittal scans and identified the starting and ending frames of each maneuver. To trace motion trajectories, starting, middle, and ending frames were extracted. Given these three frames, the initial, intermediate, and final states of the prolapsed organs were confirmed.

As reference lines for POP assessment in MRI scans vary, we selected the sacrococcygeal-inferior pubic point (SCIPP) line [3], defined by the inferior pubic symphysis and sacrococcygeal junction. This bony structure is fixed and unaffected by changes in body position. A two-dimensional Cartesian coordinate system was established, with the X-axis representing the SCIPP line and the origin at the inferior margin of the pubic symphysis (Fig. 1A). To normalize the scales of different pelvic sizes, the length of the SCIPP line was set to 1 (100%).

The centroid of each organ, suggested as a reliable displacement feature [7, 8] in POP, was chosen as the representative tracking point for analyzing displacement trajectories. This geometric simplification provides a single, objective, and reproducible point that can be consistently defined across all patients, maneuvers, and organ systems. It is calculated automatically from the segmented contour, minimizing observer bias associated with manually identifying specific anatomical landmarks (e.g., the bladder neck of a cystocele), which can vary between different reference lines and observers. This makes it particularly suitable for modeling overall trajectory trends and more importantly, for enabling a direct comparison for all three organs of their descent kinematics, which would be challenging using organ-specific morphological landmarks that are not directly analogous. The bladder, uterus, and rectum were segmented independently by two observers. Based on the outlines segmented, the center of mass was computed automatically in each extracted frame using AutoCAD, and the average centroid coordinate of the two observers’ segmentations was recorded as the final result. Centroids in the first, middle, and last MRI frames were connected to represent the trajectory of each maneuver (Fig. 1B).

The generalized additive mixed model (GAMM) had been validated as a good model to extract a common trend from heterogeneous data [9]. To summarize and then visualize a general feature of a certain organ’s dynamic motions, centroid coordinates of that organ from all patients involved were incorporated into a GAMM and then used to form a fitting consecutive curve conclusively. We constructed and ran independent GAMMs for the bladder, uterus, and rectum, separately. To model the non-linear trajectory of the centroid coordinates (x, y), we fitted GAMMs using the gam() function, specifying a Gaussian distribution for the response variable. From the fitted model, predicted values and their standard errors were calculated. The 95% confidence intervals were derived as the predicted value ± 1.96 × standard error. Additionally, to explore the relationship between diagnostic capacity and the number of Valsalva repetitions, we used t-tests to compare the mean and standard deviation of the linear distances between the centroid of each organ in the first frame and the last frame during three Valsalva maneuvers.

Results

Finally, 63 cases were enrolled and given that POP patients often experienced prolapse of multiple compartments, the distribution of multi-compartments involvement was: 21 with all three compartments, 9 with combined prolapse of anterior and middle compartments, 5 with combined prolapse of anterior and posterior compartments, and 2 with combined prolapse of middle and posterior compartments. MRI scans were collected for 57 cystoceles, 35 uterine prolapses, and 29 rectoceles, defined by clinical POP-Q examination. Demographic characteristics of the subjects are shown in Table 1. The medians of Ba/C/Bp indicated that the enrolled subjects primarily had severe prolapse (Stage III or worse), with an average age of approximately 65 years and 1 to 2 vaginal deliveries. Scatter plots of centroids for each organ in the first, middle, and last frames of three Valsalva maneuvers were depicted simultaneously (Fig. 1C) and then separately (Fig. 1D). The main distribution areas of centroids for the bladder, uterus, and rectum revealed distinct high-density zones during the prolapse process. The bladder’s active area was in close proximity to the pubic symphysis, while the uterus and rectum were located further down and dorsally, particularly the rectum. For individual Valsalva maneuvers, a gradual descending motion along the Y-axis was observed from the first to the last frame, while motion along the X-axis exhibited swaying.

Initial verification was performed to confirm the utility of the protocol implemented in this study. While clinical physical examination typically employs three repeated Valsalva maneuvers to achieve the patient’s maximum prolapse, previous studies [7, 8] performed only a single abdominal straining maneuver during MRI scans. We investigated the centroid position of each organ at the end of each Valsalva maneuver and found that the final centroid position after the third Valsalva maneuver demonstrated significantly greater displacement inferiorly compared to the first and second maneuvers for all three organs (Table 2; Fig. 2). This indicates that our dynamic MRI protocol, incorporating multiple Valsalva samplings, enables better characterization of true maximum prolapse severity.

The general fitting curves of three organs’ trajectories were formed by GAMMs (Fig. 3A, data of fitting curves in details were in Supplementary Material 1). The fitting curves were not straight lines, particularly for the bladder and uterus. The GAMM curve for the bladder formed an inverse C-shape, initially heading dorsally and then turning ventrally to create a prominent curve. The uterine curve appeared as an inverse S-shape. To quantitatively describe these curves, they were segmented by linear regression and modeled as consecutive broken lines with different slopes (Fig. 3B). Segmented linear regression was performed using the SegmentedPackage [10] in R, where the number of segmented slopes was controlled by the minimum interval between breakpoints. The optimal number of breakpoints (ranging from 1 to 99) was determined automatically via the segmented() function, which employs the Bayesian Information Criterion. A constraint was applied requiring the distance (in terms of decline along the y-axis) between any two adjacent breakpoints to be at least one-tenth of the total y-axis range, ensuring that identified segments represented meaningful deviations. For the final model with the optimal number of breakpoints, the slope and intercept of each segment were obtained using the slope() and intercept() function. Properties of slopes were presented in Table 3. The segmented linear regression model for the bladder had three breakpoints, with a large initial slope (99.3°) that gradually decreased (85.2°, 70.4°, and 63.5°). The uterine model had four breakpoints with five slopes, initially decreasing and then increasing (82.3°, 70.7°, 56.3°, 68.1°, and 93.3°). Fitting brokenlines for the rectum had the minimized fluctuation of slopes, which varied from 62.3° to 64.9° and then to 67.0°. All three segmented linear models exhibited excellent goodness of fit, with high adjusted R² values.

To evaluate the utility of three general trajectories derived from our dynamic analysis for assessing pelvic floor prolapse severity, we compared them against the conventional approach (Fig. 4). For each of the three pelvic organ types, ten cases were randomly selected for assessment of maximum prolapse extent by two measurement approaches. The SCIPP line was established as the baseline in both approaches, while one measurement line was the organ-specific trajectory and the other utilized a fixed perpendicular line. A discrepancy was demonstrated between the lowest prolapse points identified by the organ-specific approach or by the perpendicular line. When measuring maximum prolapse extent based on the two approaches, the trajectory-based system yielded statistically significant greater inferior displacement distances compared to the conventional perpendicular-based method (Table 4).

Discussions

While the assessment of POP has traditionally been and remains fundamentally grounded in clinical examination, contemporary research is integrating advanced imaging, computational anatomy, and biomechanics to deepen our understanding of its pathophysiology. This study utilized dynamic MRI to investigate the dynamic motion trajectories of three primary pelvic organs during prolapse, which remain poorly defined. Our findings indicate that none of the three organs demonstrated a simple vertical displacement. Instead, their motion trajectories exhibited organ-specific patterns, with each trajectory divisible into segments characterized by distinct descent slopes.

Organ-specific descent trajectories likely reflect differential biomechanical interactions between each organ and its unique suspensory ligaments and adjacent anatomical structures. The near-vertical initial descent of the bladder and uterus may correspond to the initial failure pattern of apical and level I support structures under raised intra-abdominal pressure. The subsequent ventral rotation of the bladder’s path is consistent with its descent being constrained and guided anteriorly by the pubic symphysis. Regarding the uterine body, as it enters the vaginal canal, its subsequent path is likely shaped by the vaginal structure and axis. Given the close alignment between the slopes of the final two segments of uterine trajectory (68.1° and 93.3°) and the reported average angles of the middle and distal vaginal axis (approximately 72° and 90°, respectively) [11], the vagina appears to act as a final conduit for uterine prolapse (both differences < 4°). In contrast to the bladder and uterus, whose initial trajectories are nearly perpendicular (99.3° and 82.3°) to the SCIPP line, the rectum moves anteriorly as it descends. This feature may be attributed to the characteristics of rectocele, which is closely related to the rectovaginal septum and perineal body. During the maximum Valsalva maneuver, the dilation of the urogenital hiatus and the descent of the perineal body facilitate the forward protrusion of the rectum.

These general trajectories provide a preliminary framework for understanding the common descent patterns of POP, which may help refine conventional “one-size-fits-all” methods in both imaging assessments and surgical approaches. First, universal reference lines may not adequately capture organ-specific variations, which could help explain the inconsistencies reported between MRI measurements and clinical manifestations, with the lowest correlation typically observed for the rectum [12–14]. The fitting curve of the rectum has a horizontal component, which may partially explain its poor compatibility with existing reference lines such as the pubococcygeal line, SCIPP line, and H-line. Second, there are apparent discrepancies in slopes among different sections of a single trajectory. Since none of these organs follow idealized straight paths, the assessment of overall displacements may benefit from a segmented approach, and methods relying on a single reference line may be insufficient. Some current reference lines may be more suitable for specific stages of POP than for the entire displacement. For the bladder and uterus, mild prolapse may be well-adapted to the SCIPP system, which captures the perpendicular downward displacement. The mid-pubis line may help evaluate moderate cystoceles as the bladder moves along the pubic symphysis during this stage. The H-line of the HMO system may be effective for assessing moderate uterine prolapse, as it is usually perpendicular to the axis of the middle-lower vagina. Third, these biomechanical insights may inform hypothesis-driven research into surgical strategies. It is easy to associate that a surgical approach aligned with the natural descent trajectory could theoretically help preserve normal anatomy, maintain the vaginal axis, and provide more physiological support, though this concept remains to be prospectively evaluated. Sacral-uterine suspensions are widely used for apical prolapse restoration [15, 16]. However, based on the trajectory outlined here, it may be hypothesized that anterior-uterine suspensions might more closely parallel the original prolapse paths of the bladder and uterus, and thus represent a concept worth exploring for maintaining the vaginal axis while simultaneously addressing cystoceles. Indeed, laparoscopic lateral suspension has been reported to preserve a near-normal vaginal axis in postoperative patients [17]. Laparoscopic pectopexy and laparoscopic lateral suspension also have been reported to provide additional cystocele correction for apical prolapse combined with cystocele and urinary incontinence [18–20]. Whether these observations are attributable to alignment with natural prolapse trajectories warrants dedicated investigation. The observed differences in organ-specific trajectories may also provide a partial biomechanical explanation for why adjuvant posterior repair performed simultaneously with uterine suspension does not appear to improve surgical success rates [21], given that the middle and posterior compartments seem to follow distinct prolapse pathways. Similarly, these findings generate the hypothesis that reinforcing and elevating the perineal body could mechanically impede the rectum’s descent trajectory, thereby potentially reducing the extent of rectocele. This hypothesis requires further biomechanical and clinical study.

The strengths of our study include addressing a gap in understanding the detailed dynamic motion trajectories during prolapse. This description of general trajectories not only reveals three distinct descending curves but also identifies segmented characteristics across these curves, addressing the limitations of classic one-size-fits-all reference systems and emphasizing the importance of tailored clinical management. Additionally, the landmarks, reference systems, and targets we selected enhance the robustness of our study. We did not use a horizontal reference line because, during the Valsalva maneuver, subjects would flex their hips and rotate their pelvis, which could introduce measurement errors to a constant reference system. The use of the centroid as a tracing point avoids the disadvantages of alternative targets, such as the lowest point, which varies across reference systems, or specific anatomical landmarks, which are influenced by organ deformation easily.

Several limitations of our study must be acknowledged. Due to the high cost and time requirements of dynamic MRI, the sample size in this study is limited, particularly for rectoceles, which indeed have the lowest incidence among the three types. The comparative analysis (Table 4), which features a small sample size (n = 10 per organ), is actually an exploratory attempt to visualize the potential discrepancy between measurement methods. The preliminary nature of this comparison necessitates cautious interpretation regarding statistical power, and its findings require validation in larger, dedicated cohorts. Additionally, all participants are from a single racial background; thus, larger studies involving diverse populations are needed to enhance the generalizability of these findings. Our study employed the organ centroid as a proxy for overall displacement. While robust for analyzing general trajectory patterns, this approach does not capture the complex and heterogeneous morphological changes that occur during prolapse, such as focal wall bulging or the formation of specific herniation patterns (e.g., enterocele). Furthermore, this study utilized a coordinate system based on the SCIPP line, which does not account for changes in pelvic inclination occurring between rest and the Valsalva maneuver, a limitation critically discussed by DeLancey et al. [3]. They have proposed a 3D Pelvic Inclination Correction System (PICS) to address this issue by establishing a coordinate system that corrects for pelvic tilt [22]. Future research should combine objective reference systems, advanced shape analysis, and multiple tracking points to facilitate a more holistic understanding of prolapse characteristics and personalized care strategies.

Conclusion

In summary, POP is a complex pathological process that cannot be described as a simple downward trajectory. Pelvic organs exhibit segmented motion paths with distinct characteristics. Our findings underscore the need for a more nuanced understanding of prolapse to guide patient evaluation and surgical techniques.
