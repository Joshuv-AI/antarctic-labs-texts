Mitochondrial genome assembly and analysis of Cercis chinensis: insights into an economically valuable ornamental species
Authors: Mengyue Wang, Ruifeng Yang, Mingliang Wang, Jinglong Li, Chien-Hsun Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCercis chinensis is a leguminous species with notable ornamental value and considerable economic potential, and is primarily distributed in southern China. Among species in the genus Cercis, only the complete mitochondrial genome of Cercis canadensis has been publicly available. This lack of mitochondrial genomic data constrains our understanding of the evolutionary history, genetic diversity, and environmental adaptability of Cercis chinensis and its closely related species. In this study, we successfully completed the de novo assembly and annotation of Cercis chinensis mitochondrial genome.ResultsThe mitochondrial genome of Cercis chinensis consists of two putative circular chromosomes, totaling 393,546 bp in length with a GC content of 45.29%. A total of 61 genes were annotated, including 39 protein-coding genes (PCGs), 20 transfer RNA (tRNA) genes, and 2 ribosomal RNA (rRNA) genes. Functional analysis predicted 546 C-to-U RNA editing sites, the majority of which result in nonsynonymous amino acid substitutions. A total of 155 repeat elements were identified, comprising simple sequence repeats (SSRs), tandem repeats, and dispersed repeats, highlighting the structural complexity of the mitochondrial genome. Furthermore, 18 mitochondrial plastid DNA transfer events (MTPTs) were detected, encompassing six complete genes, including 5 tRNA genes and 1 rRNA gene, originating from the chloroplast genome. The presence of intact tRNA and rRNA fragments among MTPTs suggests that such transfers may not only add to structural diversity but could also provide functional redundancy or influence RNA processing within the mitochondrial genome. Ka/Ks analysis indicated that most core PCGs are subject to purifying selection, reflecting high functional conservation across species. Notably, atp4 and nad4 exhibited signs of positive selection, suggesting their potential involvement in the adaptive evolution of Cercis chinensis. Phylogenetic analysis based on shared mitochondrial and chloroplast protein-coding genes placed Cercis chinensis in close relation to Bauhinia purpurea, thereby supporting its current taxonomic classification within the Fabaceae.ConclusionThis study provides the first characterization of the mitochondrial genome of Cercis chinensis, detailing its structural organization, functional elements, and evolutionary dynamics. The assembled genome and associated analyses provide valuable insights into the mitochondrial architecture of Cercis chinensis and enrich the genomic resources for the Fabaceae family, enhancing our understanding of mitochondrial genome evolution in legumes and serving as a foundational reference for future research in species identification, molecular breeding, and phylogenetic studies of C. chinensis and related taxa.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07904-w.

Introduction

Cercis chinensis is an endemic leguminous species in China, valued for its ecological, ornamental, medicinal, and economic importance [1]. As the primary center of diversity for the genus Cercis, China hosts over 50% of the known global species, these species are widely distributed across the country, primarily found at elevations of 600–1,900 m along forest edges, valleys, and roadsides [2]. Cercis chinensis is characterized by its graceful tree form, upright trunk, and clusters of pink to pale purplish-red papilionaceous flowers that bloom profusely on leafless branches in early spring, making it a highly attractive ornamental plant [3]. Beyond its ornamental appeal, Cercis chinensis also exhibits important medicinal and industrial potential. Various parts of the plant—including flowers, fruits, roots, and stems—are rich in bioactive compounds such as flavonoids and polysaccharides, which have a range of pharmacological activities [4, 5]. The bark is bitter in taste and neutral in nature, associated with the liver meridian, and is traditionally used to promote blood circulation, detoxification, and diuresis, being prescribed for rheumatic arthralgia, abdominal pain caused by blood stasis, traumatic injuries, and insect or snake bites [6]. Phytochemical analyses have shown that the bark mainly contains flavonoids, particularly quercetin [7–9], and exhibits pharmacological effects including analgesic [10–12], anti-inflammatory [10, 11], and antibacterial activities [6]. The flowers are used to clear heat, cool blood, and detoxify [12], with flavonoids being the principal constituents, including rutin, quercetin, and kaempferol. Recent studies demonstrated that the total flavonoids from Cercis chinensis flowers can effectively scavenge hydroxyl and superoxide anion radicals, thereby producing significant hypoglycemic effects [13–15].

Mitochondria are semi-autonomous organelles responsible for ATP synthesis through oxidative phosphorylation, a function that is highly conserved across eukaryotes [16]. Compared with animals and fungi, plant mitochondrial genomes show distinctive features, including larger genome sizes, complex structural rearrangements, and frequent intra- or inter-genomic recombination [16, 17]. Gene expression in plant mitogenomes is further regulated by cis- and trans-splicing as well as extensive RNA editing, which together pose challenges for genome assembly and annotation [17]. Plant mitochondrial genomes are valuable resources for developing molecular markers and studying evolution. Comparative analyses among related taxa can reveal lineage-specific evolutionary patterns and help resolve phylogenetic relationships [18]. Furthermore, mitochondrial genomic information has important applications in plant genetic engineering and provides insights into evolutionary processes across diverse plant lineages [19].

In recent years, research on Cercis species has expanded considerably, particularly in the areas of taxonomy, physiological ecology, and genetic diversity, with substantial progress achieved at the chloroplast and nuclear genome levels. The mitochondrial genome of Cercis canadensis has been successfully sequenced, providing valuable insights into the structural and evolutionary characteristics of Cercis mitogenomes [20]. However, no complete mitochondrial genome has yet been reported for other Cercis species, especially the Chinese endemic C. chinensis. Critical genomic features such as its overall mitogenome structure, gene content, RNA editing sites, and repeat elements remain unexplored. To address this gap, we assembled and annotated the complete mitochondrial genome of C. chinensis using third-generation Single Molecule Real-Time (SMRT) sequencing. Based on the assembled genome, we conducted a systematic analysis of its gene composition, repeat elements, RNA editing sites, codon usage bias, and selective pressure on protein-coding genes. We further examined syntenic relationships and constructed phylogenetic trees based on shared mitochondrial and chloroplast protein-coding genes to clarify the evolutionary position of C. chinensis within the Fabaceae. Mitochondrial plastid DNA transfers (MTPTs) also were identified to investigate intracellular gene transfer events.

In conclusion, this study reports the first complete mitochondrial genome of Cercis chinensis, which exhibits two circle organization distinct from the single circular structure previously described in Cercis canadensis, thereby revealing structural diversity of mitochondrial genomes among species within the genus Cercis. Phylogenetic analyses based on mitochondrial and chloroplast protein-coding genes further provide clear support for the monophyly of Cercis, offering new evidence for its evolutionary placement within Fabaceae. Comprehensive identification of repetitive sequences and plastid-derived fragments (MTPTs) highlights both similarities and differences in genome composition and suggests potential roles in shaping genome structure and functional evolution. A total of 546 C-to-U RNA editing sites were predicted, and the frequent conversion to leucine, a hydrophobic amino acid, may have important implications for protein structure and function, providing a basis for future functional studies. Selection pressure analysis shows that most genes are under purifying selection, while atp4 and nad4 exhibit signals of positive selection, which may be associated with optimizing energy metabolism efficiency and stress adaptation. Overall, this work not only enriches the mitochondrial genomic resources of legumes but also highlights Cercis as an important model for investigating mitochondrial genome structural diversity, RNA editing patterns, and adaptive evolution, thereby providing a solid foundation for future research in systematics, molecular evolution, and genetic improvement.

Result

Characteristics of the Cercis chinensis mitogenome

Based on HiFi sequencing data, we successfully assembled the mitochondrial genome of Cercis chinensis. The mitogenome consists of two circular contigs measuring 135,372 bp and 258,174 bp in length, respectively, yielding a total genome size of 393,546 bp with a GC content of 45.29% (Fig. 1). This structural divergence from the single circular genome reported for Cercis canadensis suggests that mitochondrial organization within Cercis may be more dynamic than previously recognized, potentially reflecting lineage-specific recombination or repair mechanisms. A total of 61 genes were annotated, including 39 protein-coding genes, 20 transfer RNA (tRNA) genes, and 2 ribosomal RNA (rRNA) genes (rrn5 and rrn18) (Table 1). Among the 39 PCGs, 35 are classified as core mitochondrial genes. These include genes encoding ATP synthase subunits (atp1, atp4, atp6, atp8, and atp9), cytochrome c maturation factors (ccmB, ccmC, ccmFC, and ccmFN), cytochrome c oxidase subunits (cox1, cox2, and cox3), the cytochrome bc1 complex (cob), maturase (matR), a membrane transport protein (mttB), and subunits of NADH dehydrogenase (nad1, nad2, nad3, nad4, nad4L, nad5, nad6, nad7, and nad9). The remaining genes are considered variable and include three large subunit ribosomal proteins (rpl5, rpl10, and rpl16), six small subunit ribosomal proteins (rps1, rps3, rps4, rps10, rps12, and rps14), and two succinate dehydrogenase genes (sdh3 and sdh4). Intron structure analysis revealed that nad4 contains three introns, while nad1, nad2, nad5, and nad7 each harbor four introns. RNA editing analysis

Analysis of codon usage bias in the Cercis chinensis mitogenome

The analysis of codon usage in the Cercis chinensis mitochondrial genome revealed a total of 10,499 codons across all protein-coding genes, including three stop codons: TAA, TAG, and TGA. Notably, C → U RNA editing events were detected at certain start and stop codons, influencing overall codon usage patterns. Relative Synonymous Codon Usage (RSCU) values were calculated to assess codon bias. Among the codons analyzed, 9,347 (87.74%) exhibited RSCU values greater than 1.0, indicating a preference for these codons over synonymous alternatives. The most frequently used codon was TTT, with an RSCU value of 3.68. Conversely, 1,270 codons displayed RSCU values below 1.0, reflecting lower usage frequency; the least used codon was TGC, with an RSCU of 0.52 (Table S2).

Repeats detection

A total of 155 repeat sequences were identified in the mitochondrial genome of Cercis chinensis, including 43 simple sequence repeats (SSRs) (Table S3), 12 tandem repeats (Table S4), and 100 dispersed repeats (Fig. 4, Table S5). The abundance and distribution of repeats, particularly long repeats, may contribute to recombination-mediated structural rearrangements, thereby driving the multipartite organization observed in Cercis chinensis. Among them, mononucleotide repeats were the most abundant, with A/T repeat units accounting for 67.44% of all SSRs, and most repeat lengths ranging from 10 to 16 bp. Dinucleotide and trinucleotide repeats accounted for 13.95% and 11.63%, respectively, with lengths mostly between 12 and 16 bp. The SSRs in the contig2 region were relatively simple, predominantly mononucleotide A/T repeats, with no dinucleotide repeats detected. These SSRs may serve as potential molecular markers for assessing genetic diversity and cultivar identification in C. chinensis [21].Fig. 4Distribution of repeat sequences in the mitochondrial genome. Among the 100 dispersed repeats, 53 were forward repeats (30 in contig1, 23 in contig2), and 47 were palindromic (reverse) repeats (20 in contig1 and 27 in contig2). The longest forward and palindromic repeats were respectively 93 bp and 199 bp. In contig1, forward repeats ranged from 35 to 93 bp, palindromic repeats were mainly between 35 and 199 bp. In contig2, forward repeats ranged from 23 to 51 bp, and palindromic repeats ranged from 23 to 42 bp. A large repeat of 1976 bp was detected in contig1, which constitutes the junction for mitochondrial genome circularization, providing direct evidence for the complete circular structure of the Cercis chinensis mitochondrial genome.

Tandem repeats, defined as consecutively arranged repeat units typically ranging from 1 to 200 bp, are widespread in eukaryotic and some prokaryotic genomes [22]. In the mitochondrial genome of Cercis chinensis, 12 tandem repeats were identified, with seven in contig1 and five in contig2. The repeat unit lengths ranged from 13 to 32 bp, and sequence identity exceeded 71% for all repeats.

DNA transfer between the chloroplast and the mitochondrion

Mitochondrial plastid DNA sequences (MTPTs) are fragments of plastid-derived DNA integrated into the mitochondrial genome [23]. Through BLAST comparison between the chloroplast and mitochondrial genomes of Cercis chinensis, we identified 18 homologous fragments ranging from 28 bp to 3,049 bp in length, collectively totaling 8,602 bp and accounting for 2.18% of the mitochondrial genome. The longer fragments (> 1 kb), including 3,049 bp, 1,332 bp, and 1,107 bp segments, showed sequence identities above 95%, suggesting that they may represent relatively recent transfers. Medium-length fragments (400–900 bp) exhibited lower sequence identities (74–80%), likely representing older transfer events that have accumulated mutations. Several short fragments (tens to a few hundred base pairs) with high sequence identities (90–100%) were detected, most of which corresponded to tRNA, rRNA, or conserved functional regions. Six complete genes were annotated, including 5 tRNA genes (trnW-CCA, trnS-GGA, trnN-GUU, trnM-CAU and trnD-GUC) and 1 rRNA gene (rrn18) (Fig. 5, Table S6). To investigate the potential functionality of these genes, we performed MEME motif analysis on the 1.5 kb upstream regions of the six genes. Several conserved motifs were identified, and subsequent comparison using TomTom against the CIS-BP (Glycine_max) database revealed that some motifs share similarity with known regulatory elements or transcription factors, such as ethylene-responsive TF4 (ERF4) and Dof-type zinc finger (Table S10, S11, S12). These results suggest that, despite originating from chloroplast-to-mitochondria transfer, a subset of MTPT genes may retain regulatory features and potential activity. In addition to tRNA and rRNA genes, several fragments homologous to functional genes (nad1, nad2, nad5, and cox2) were also detected as transferred from the chloroplast to the mitochondrial genome. Analyses of Ka/Ks

The Ka/Ks ratio is widely used to assess selective pressures on PCGs, thereby providing insights into the underlying genetic mechanisms [24]. Ka/Ks ratio greater than 1 indicates positive selection, less than 1 indicates purifying selection, and equal to 1 suggests neutral evolution[25]. We calculated Ka/Ks ratios for 22 core PCGs by comparing Cercis chinensis with 18 other species from the subfamilies Cercidoideae, Detarioideae, and Papilionoideae (Fig. 7, Table S8). PCGs in Cercis chinensis are subject to purifying or neutral selection due to their functional constraints, resulting in Ka/Ks ratios less than or close to 1 [26, 27]. The results revealed that atp9, cox1, cox3, nad4L, nad5, rpl5, and rps12 had Ka/Ks ratios significantly below 1, indicating strong purifying selection, suggesting these genes play critical roles in maintaining mitochondrial function. The genes atp4 and nad4 exhibited Ka/Ks ratios greater than 1, suggesting these genes may have experienced positive selection, potentially reflecting adaptive evolutionary changes in the mitochondrial genome of Cercis chinensis. It should be noted that these analyses were conducted at the subfamily level, which may introduce some saturation at synonymous and nonsynonymous sites over long branches. Nevertheless, the majority of mitochondrial PCGs still exhibit Ka/Ks < 1, indicating purifying selection, and the overall trends are considered reliable.Fig. 7Boxplots of the Ka/Ks ratio of Cercis chinensis. Discussion

Plant mitochondrial genomes are characterized by high structural diversity, polymorphism, and complexity [28]. Although most plant mitogenomes predominantly exhibit circular structures, studies have reported alternative conformations such as H-shaped, Y-shaped, and linear forms, as well as coexistence of linear and circular molecules within a single genome [29] Among Cercis species, Cercis canadensis is currently the only one with a publicly available complete mitochondrial genome, which consists of a single circular chromosome with a total length of 348,530 bp and encodes 35 protein-coding genes, 3 rRNA genes, and 18 tRNA genes [30]. In comparison, the Cercis chinensis mitogenome assembled exhibits a multichromosomal architecture composed of two circular chromosomes with a total length of 393,546 bp. It encodes 61 genes, including 39 protein-coding genes, 20 tRNA genes, and 2 rRNA genes. As a closely related species of Cercis, Bauhinia purpurea possesses a single circular mitochondrial chromosome with a total length of 525,727 bp, encoding 35 protein-coding genes, 16 tRNA genes, and 3 rRNA genes [31]. The observed differences in genome size and gene content among these three species suggest that mitochondrial genome structure and function in congeneric and closely related species may be shaped by lineage specific evolutionary mechanisms, potentially influencing gene expression and energy metabolism. Importantly, the structure of Cercis chinensis should be regarded as putative, as its have not yet been validated by experimental approaches. Future work incorporating such methods will be valuable for further confirming the structural organization of the Cercis chinensis mitochondrial genome.

GC content is an important genomic feature that reflects genome stability and the amino acid composition encoded by the genome [32, 33]. The GC content of the Cercis chinensis mitogenome is 45.3%, which is comparable to that of other Fabaceae species such as Phaseolus vulgaris (45.1%) [34]、Trigonella foenum-graecum (45.3%) [35] and Medicago truncatula (45.4%) [36], suggesting a relatively conserved GC content across the Fabaceae mitochondrial genomes. Despite the stability of GC content, genome size varies considerably among Fabaceae species—a phenomenon that is widespread across angiosperms [26].

Extensive intracellular gene transfer (IGT) and interspecies horizontal gene transfer (HGT) in plant mitochondrial genomes [37] can contribute to the unusual variation in genome size and structure. Although nuclear copies of mitochondrial genes may gradually degrade over time, some transferred genes can regain functionality by acquiring regulatory elements and intracellular targeting signals, allowing their encoded products to be retargeted to mitochondria [38]. The activation and targeting of transferred genes render the mitochondrial copies dispensable, often leading to the loss of mitochondrial genes, this phenomenon was observed for many ribosomal protein and succinate dehydrogenase genes [39]. We analyzed the conservation of multiple mitochondrial genes in Fabaceae [20]. The results showed that rps2, rps7, rps11, and rps13 are widely missing across Fabaceae, while genes such as cox2, rpl2, rps1, rps19, and sdh3 have undergone multiple independent losses in different lineages [20]. In Cercis chinensis, we failed to detect rpl2 and rps19, consistent with their partial loss or pseudogenization in Cercis canadensis, suggesting that the loss of these genes may have occurred early in the evolution of subfamily Cercidoideae.

The loss of the cox2 gene has been reported in the mitochondrial genome of Cercis canadensis, representing a relatively common pattern of mitochondrial gene loss within the Fabaceae family [40]. However, in the Cercis chinensis mitochondrial genome assembled, the cox2 gene remains intact with a typical start codon and open reading frame, suggesting that this gene has not been universally lost within the genus Cercis. This may reflect lineage-specific gene retention events during the evolution of the genus.

RNA editing is a widespread nucleotide modification mechanism in higher plants, primarily occurring in organelles such as mitochondria and chloroplasts. It enriches genetic variation and promotes the diversity of gene products [41]. Among the various types of RNA editing, cytidine-to-uridine (C-to-U) conversion is the most common, though U-to-C editing also occurs in some species [42]. We identified a total of 546 RNA editing sites across 30 PCGs in the mitochondrial genome of Cercis chinensis. All predicted editing events were unidirectional C-to-U conversions, mostly resulting in single-base changes, with some involving two-base changes. Importantly, all RNA editing events led to nonsynonymous codon changes, and 254 sites (46.60% of total editing sites) involved conversion to leucine (Leu), making it the most frequent amino acid substitution. RNA editing can affect protein function by altering the hydrophilicity or hydrophobicity of amino acids within coding regions and also promote RNA splicing in noncoding regions [43, 44]. Hydrophobic amino acids contribute to protein stability, while hydrophilic amino acids aid in proper protein folding [32]. Approximately 60% (322 sites) of the RNA editing events in Cercis chinensis were associated with changes in amino acid hydrophobicity or hydrophilicity. These predicted sites have not yet been validated experimentally, for example by transcriptome sequencing, and their actual functionality and roles remain to be confirmed. But these predictions provide preliminary insights into the potential RNA editing patterns in the mitochondrial genome of Cercis chinensis and lay a foundation for future experimental verification and functional studies. Future experimental investigations will be valuable for gaining a more comprehensive understanding of the biological significance of these RNA editing events in gene expression and mitochondrial function.

Plant mitochondrial genomes are rich in repetitive sequences, which promote frequent recombination events and contribute to increased genomic complexity and diversity in both size and structure [45]. Among these, microsatellites or simple sequence repeats (SSRs) are particularly noteworthy. SSRs are composed of short DNA motifs, typically 1–6 nucleotides in length [46]. Due to their diverse functions, SSRs are widely used in species identification, QTL mapping, marker-assisted breeding, diversity analysis, and studies of evolutionary relationships [46–50]. We identified a total of 155 repeat sequences in the mitochondrial genome of Cercis chinensis, including 43 SSRs, 12 tandem repeat sequences, and 100 dispersed repeat sequences. The abundance of these repeat elements suggests the presence of frequent intermolecular recombination events within the Cercis chinensis mitogenome [51]. Among all SSRs, mononucleotide repeats were the most prevalent, particularly A/T motifs, which accounted for 64.44% of all SSRs. Previous studies have shown that AT base pairs are thermodynamically less stable than GC base pairs, and AT-rich regions are more prone to replication slippage and recombination, which may explains the higher frequency of AT-rich SSRs in organellar genomes [52]. In the mitochondrial genome of Cercis canadensis, 209 repeat units in total were identified. [23]. In comparison, C. chinensis contains 155 repeats, while Bauhinia purpurea has 124 repeats [31]. These differences in repeat abundance may reflect varying frequencies of repeat-mediated recombination or differential loss events among species, which could influence mitochondrial genome stability. The relatively fewer repeats in C. chinensis may indicate a trend toward a more stable mitogenome structure, potentially ensuring consistent mitochondrial function. Although B. purpurea contains fewer repeats, its relatively larger mitochondrial genome size may be attributed to the accumulation of insertion sequences rather than repeat expansion [31, 53, 54].

Codon usage bias is shaped by multiple factors including natural selection, species-specific mutation pressures, and genetic drift [55]. In the Cercis chinensis mitochondrial genome, all PCGs use the standard ATG start codon. Analysis showed that the most abundant amino acids in Cercis chinensis mitochondrial PCGs were phenylalanine (Phe), isoleucine (Ile), and glutamic acid (Glu), while cysteine (Cys) and arginine (Arg) were less frequent. Additionally, the A/T nucleotide bias observed in codon usage mirrors the overall base composition bias of the mitochondrial genome, this phenomenon commonly found across plant mitochondrial genomes [34, 56].

Intracellular gene transfer can occur between different organelles, including chloroplasts, mitochondria, and the nuclear genome [56, 57]. During the long course of plant evolution, gene transfer from the chloroplast to the mitochondrial genome is a frequent event [58]. Identifying intracellular gene transfer events is crucial for the accurate assembly of chloroplast and mitochondrial genomes and understanding the evolutionary dynamics of organellar genomes [57, 59]. Mitochondrial plastid DNA transfers (MTPTs) represent fragments of plastid-origin DNA that have been incorporated into the mitochondrial genome through inter-organellar DNA transfer. Such transfers are frequent in plants and are thought to occur mainly via non-homologous recombination or DNA repair mechanisms following double-strand breaks [60, 61]. Although most MTPTs are noncoding, some retain functional tRNA or rRNA genes and may participate in mitochondrial genome maintenance or expression. The number and size of MTPTs can therefore serve as indicators of the frequency of inter-organellar gene exchange and genome structural plasticity. In C. chinensis, we identified 18 MTPTs totaling 8.6 kb (2.18% of the mitogenome), slightly more than the 5.7 kb (~ 1.6%) in C. canadensis and much higher than the 6 MTPTs (~ 0.65%) in B. purpurea. These differences may reflect distinct rates of mitochondrial DNA repair, recombination, and genome turnover among species. The relatively higher MTPT content in C. chinensis could be associated with its adaptation to more variable subtropical environments, where stronger oxidative stress and metabolic fluctuations promote inter-organellar DNA exchange as part of a dynamic genome maintenance process. In contrast, the lower MTPT content in B. purpurea may reflect a more conserved or stable mitochondrial genome structure, possibly due to reduced DNA transfer activity or different repair mechanisms.

In the transferred fragments of C. chinensis, six fully annotated genes were identified, including 5 tRNA genes (trnW-CCA, trnS-GGA, trnN-GUU, trnM-CAU and trnD-GUC) and 1 rRNA gene (rrn18). Several fragments homologous to functional genes (nad1, nad2, nad5 and cox2) were detected; however, as these sequences are incomplete, they are most likely pseudogenes or non-functional remnants rather than intact functional genes. Although some chloroplast-derived protein-coding genes have lost their functionality, their transfer still contributes to the sequence diversity of the mitochondrial genome and facilitates its structural evolution [23]. To further investigate the potential functionality of the complete tRNA and rRNA genes, we conducted additional analyses. BLAST [62] comparisons showed that these genes are highly conserved across reference genomes, suggesting possible functional retention. Motif analysis using MEME Suite [63] in the 1.5 kb upstream regions revealed several conserved motifs, and TomTom [63] comparisons indicated similarities with known regulatory elements and functional proteins, such as ethylene-responsive TF4 (ERF4) and Dof-type zinc finger, implying the presence of potential regulatory signals. Despite originating from chloroplast-to-mitochondria transfer, some MTPT genes may retain regulatory features and potential activity. Future transcriptomic or functional experiments will help validate their expression and clarify their roles in mitochondrial genome evolution and function.

Mitochondria and chloroplasts are organelles with complete genetic systems [64]. Compared to the nuclear genome, they are typically maternally inherited and possess relatively simple genomic structures, which makes them easier to assemble and analyze. As a result, organellar genomes hold critical research value and application potential in studies of evolutionary history, phylogenetic relationships, taxonomic identification, and genetic diversity of plant species [65]. We conducted a phylogenetic analysis based on the mitochondrial genome of Cercis chinensis, along with 18 previously published mitochondrial genomes from other Fabaceae species and one Euphorbiaceae species as an outgroup. Shared genes from both the mitochondrial and chloroplast genomes were used for the analysis. The resulting phylogenetic tree showed a consistent and well-supported topology within Fabaceae. Within subfamily Cercidoideae, C. chinensis and B. purpurea were the earliest diverging lineages. Subfamilies Caesalpinioideae and Papilionoideae were recovered as monophyletic clades, forming a strongly supported branch prior to further diversification. These results demonstrate that mitochondrial genomes are effective for resolving deep phylogenetic relationships in Fabaceae, although the limited number of complete mitogenomes currently available restricts broader taxonomic coverage and may affect the resolution of some lineages.

By comparing the mitochondrial genome of Cercis chinensis with those of other Fabaceae species, this study also revealed the selective pressures acting on PCGs during evolution. Most genes in the Cercis chinensis mitogenome were found to be under purifying (negative) selection, indicating a high degree of conservation among Fabaceae species. Both atp4 and nad4 showed strong signals of positive selection in the Cercis chinensis mitochondrial genome, suggesting that these two genes may play important roles in the species’ environmental adaptation.

The atp4 gene encodes the b subunit of the F₀ portion of mitochondrial ATP synthase (Complex V), which plays a crucial structural role in coupling proton translocation with ATP synthesis [66, 67]. This subunit forms part of the stator stalk that stabilizes the catalytic F₁ head during rotation, thereby maintaining the efficiency and structural integrity of the entire ATP synthase complex [68]. Given this central role in energy conversion, any adaptive modification in atp4 could directly affect ATP production efficiency and cellular energy balance [69]. As C. chinensis is mainly distributed in southern China, the hot and humid summer conditions may induce stomatal closure and reduce intercellular CO₂ concentrations, the mild and dry winter conditions may limit water and nutrient uptake. Both seasonal scenarios can restrict the supply of photosynthetic assimilates and consequently increase the reliance on mitochondrial respiration for energy compensation [70, 71]. We hypothesize that the positive selection acting on atp4 may enhance ATP synthesis efficiency, ensuring sufficient energy supply for growth and stress-related metabolic processes under adverse conditions. This adaptive trend is similar with findings in high-altitude Rhododendron species, which atp4 has been shown to optimize energy production under low-oxygen stress [72].

In addition, nad4 encodes a key subunit of mitochondrial complex I and can influence the efficiency of electron transport as well as the rate of reactive oxygen species (ROS) production [73]. During the early spring germination stage of Cercis chinensis, the plant may be exposed to low-temperature stress caused by cold spells, whereas in midsummer it can experience photoinhibition triggered by high temperatures and intense irradiance. Both types of stress can impair the mitochondrial electron transport chain, resulting in the excessive accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [74, 75]. Previous studies have demonstrated that defects in nad4 function result in mitochondrial structural and functional impairment in Zea mays, further supporting its crucial role in maintaining the stability and efficiency of complex I [76, 77]. The positive selection observed in nad4 of C. chinensis is likely to contribute to stabilizing complex I and sustaining efficient energy metabolism under fluctuating environmental conditions. Nevertheless, further experimental validation, such as expression profiling and functional assays under stress conditions, will be required to confirm these adaptive hypotheses.

Materials and methods

Plant materials DNA extraction and sequencing

To obtain the mitochondrial genome of Cercis chinensis, fresh leaf samples were collected from Jiangxi Agricultural University (Jiangxi Province, China). The samples were immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at –80°C to preserve the integrity of both RNA and DNA. Mitochondrial DNA was extracted and purified using the cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) method [78]. Whole-genome sequencing was performed using third-generation Single Molecule Real-Time (SMRT) sequencing technology. A PCR-free SMRTbell library was constructed and sequenced on the PacBio Revio platform. After sequencing, adapter sequences and low-quality reads were filtered out. Subreads were extracted from the raw data and processed to generate high-accuracy HiFi (High-Fidelity) reads, which were then used for mitochondrial genome assembly and subsequent analyses.

Mitochondrial genome assembly and annotation

De novo assembly of the Cercis chinensis long-read sequencing data was performed using Flye software (v2.9.5) with default parameters, generating a graphical assembly output in GFA (Graph Fragment Assembly) format [79]. The assembly underwent several rounds of iterative optimization, resulting in a complete genome sequence comprising nuclear, chloroplast, and mitochondrial components.To determine the structural configuration of the mitochondrial genome, the GFA file was visualized using Bandage software (v0.8.1) [80]. Contigs corresponding to the nuclear and chloroplast genomes were manually identified and removed. The resulting mitochondrial genome sequence was subsequently subjected to functional annotation using the online platform PMGA (A Plant Mitochondrial Genome Annotator, http://www.1kmpg.cn/ipmga/) [81].

RNA editing analysis

RNA editing sites in the Cercis chinensis mitochondrial genome were predicted using the online tool PREPACT3 (http://www.prepact.de/) with default parameter settings.

Repeats detection and codon usage analysis

Dispersed repeat sequences were identified using the online tool REPuter [82] (https://bibiserv.cebitec.uni-bielefeld.de/reputer) with the following parameters: minimum repeat size of 8 bp, maximum repeat size of 50 bp. Simple sequence repeats (SSRs) were detected using the MISA web tool [83], with thresholds set to a minimum of 10 repeat units for mononucleotides, 6 for dinucleotides, and 5 for tri-, tetra-, penta-, and hexanucleotides. Tandem repeats were identified using Tandem Repeats Finder (v4.09, https://tandem.bu.edu) with default parameters [84]. Relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) values were calculated based on the protein-coding sequences of the mitochondrial genome using custom Python scripts, and the results were visualized using R.

DNA transfer between the chloroplast and the mitochondrion

The chloroplast genome of Cercis chinensis (GenBank accession number: MZ128523.1) was retrieved from the GenBank database. Sequence similarity between the mitochondrial and chloroplast genomes was analyzed using BLASTN with an E-value threshold of 1e-5 to identify potential chloroplast-derived DNA fragments. The results were visualized using the Circos package integrated in TBtools [85, 86]. To investigate the potential functionality of the complete tRNA and rRNA MTPT genes, multiple analyses were performed. Sequence conservation of these genes was assessed across reference genomes using BLASTN [62]. Motif discovery in the 1.5 kb upstream regions of each gene was performed using MEME Suite (v5.5.8) [63]. The identified motifs were compared against known regulatory sequences using TomTom [63] with the parameters: target motif database: CIS-BP 2.00 Single Species DNA (Glycine_max), default E-value threshold.

Phylogenetic analysis

To determine the phylogenetic position of Cercis chinensis, complete mitochondrial and chloroplast genome sequences from 19 plant species were downloaded from the NCBI Organelle Genome Database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genome/organelle/) (Table S9). Phylogenetic analyses were conducted separately using mitochondrial and chloroplast genomes from a total of 20 species, including Cercis chinensis. The selected taxa primarily represent the subfamilies Cercidoideae, Caesalpinioideae, and Papilionoideae. Of the 19 comparative species, 18 belong to the Fabaceae family, while Balanophora japonica was included as an outgroup.

Shared protein-coding genes across the 20 mitochondrial genomes were identified and used to construct the maximum likelihood (ML) phylogenetic tree. Multiple sequence alignment was performed using MAFFT (v7.256) [87] with the “–auto” option to select the most appropriate alignment strategy, and the aligned sequences were concatenated and trimmed with trimAl (v1.5). Phylogenetic analyses were conducted with IQ-TREE (v2.4.0) [88], in which the best-fit substitution model was automatically selected using ModelFinder (option -m MFP) based on the Bayesian information criterion (BIC). ML trees were inferred with 1,000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates (-B 1000) to assess branch support. The resulting phylogenetic trees were visualized and annotated using FigTree (v1.4.4) and the Interactive Tree Of Life (iTOL) online platform [88].

Analysis of Ka/Ks values

To investigate the natural selection pressures acting during the evolution of Cercis chinensis, 19 species from the subfamilies Cercidoideae, Caesalpinioideae, and Papilionoideae, including C. chinensis, were selected for comparative analysis. A total of 22 shared protein-coding genes (PCGs) were aligned using MAFFT (v7.256) [87]. The ratios of nonsynonymous (Ka) to synonymous (Ks) substitutions (Ka/Ks) were calculated using KaKs_Calculator v2.0 [25]. The resulting values were processed and analyzed in Microsoft Excel 2021, and visualized using custom Python scripts.

Conclusion

In this study, we successfully assembled and annotated the mitochondrial genome of Cercis chinensis, conducted in depth analyses of both the nucleotide and amino acid sequences of the annotated genes. The Cercis chinensis mitogenome comprises two circular chromosomes with a total length of 393,546 bp and a GC content of 45.29%. A total of 61 genes were annotated, including 39 protein-coding genes, 20 transfer RNA (tRNA) genes, and 2 ribosomal RNA (rRNA) genes.

We analyzed RNA editing sites, codon usage bias, and the distribution of repeat sequences. We also investigated intracellular gene transfer (IGT) events between the mitochondrial and chloroplast genomes, assessed the evolutionary pressure on PCGs using Ka/Ks ratios, and conducted phylogenetic analyses based on shared genes from both mitochondrial and chloroplast genomes across Cercis chinensis and 19 other plant species. The phylogenetic results confirmed the close relationship between Cercis chinensis and Bauhinia purpurea within subfamily Cercidoideae.

This study provides valuable genomic resources for Cercis chinensis, offering insights that will facilitate future research on species identification, genetic variation, and evolutionary relationships. Overall, our findings enrich the mitochondrial genome database of the Fabaceae family and lay a solid foundation for further exploration of this species, which holds both ornamental and economic significance.
