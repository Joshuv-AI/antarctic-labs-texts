Serum HMGB3 and PCSK9 Levels as Predictors of Early Treatment Response in Pediatric Mycoplasma pneumoniae Pneumonia: A Single-Center Prospective Cohort Study
Authors: Huan Gao, Yanqin Yu, Dongmei Lyu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo investigate the predictive value of serum high mobility group box 3 (HMGB3) and proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9) levels for poor early treatment response in pediatric Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia (MPP).MethodsA total of 400 pediatric patients with Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia (MPP) and 178 healthy controls were recruited for this single-center prospective cohort study conducted between January 2022 and January 2025. All patients received standardized azithromycin sequential therapy. Based on the criteria for early treatment response at 7 days post-treatment, patients were categorized into the good early treatment response group (n=321) and the poor early treatment response group (n=79). Multivariable logistic regression was performed to identify independent predictors, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was used to evaluate predictive performance, and Spearman correlation analysis was conducted to assess correlations with inflammatory markers.ResultsSerum HMGB3 and PCSK9 levels in children with MPP were significantly higher than those in the healthy control group, and were significantly higher in the poor early treatment response group than in the good early treatment response group (all P<0.05). Multivariable logistic regression analysis showed that HMGB3 (odds ratio [OR]=2.310, P<0.001) and PCSK9 (OR=1.518, P=0.027) were independent predictors of poor early treatment response in MPP. ROC analysis revealed that the areas under the curve (AUC) for HMGB3 and PCSK9 alone were 0.766 and 0.758, respectively, and the AUC for combined detection increased to 0.840. HMGB3 and PCSK9 were weakly positively correlated with interleukin-6 (IL-6), C-reactive protein (CRP), and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α).ConclusionSerum HMGB3 and PCSK9 are independent predictors of poor early treatment response in children with MPP. Combined detection demonstrates superior predictive performance compared with individual markers and may serve as a reference for short-term risk assessment.Keywords: Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia, high mobility group box 3, proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9, early treatment response, biomarkers, children

Introduction

Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia (MPP) in children represents a common etiology of pediatric community-acquired pneumonia (CAP), constituting an acute inflammatory respiratory disease triggered by Mycoplasma pneumoniae infection.1,2 Characterized by persistent fever and irritative dry cough as principal clinical manifestations, this disease has demonstrated a markedly increasing incidence among infants and young children in recent years.3,4 Previous clinical experience indicates that the majority of mild cases achieve favorable outcomes following standard macrolide antibiotic therapy.5 However, with the rapid global dissemination of macrolide-resistant Mycoplasma pneumoniae strains, antimicrobial resistance in pediatric MPP has escalated substantially, resulting in a significant rise in refractory MPP (RMPP) incidence. Severely affected children may develop life-threatening complications including acute respiratory distress syndrome and systemic inflammatory response syndrome.6,7 Although current inflammatory biomarkers and radiological assessment systems have become increasingly sophisticated, approximately 18–25% of children still sustain irreversible pulmonary injuries such as bronchiolitis obliterans due to pathological mechanisms involving dysregulated immune responses and airway remodeling.8 Therefore, identifying specific biomarkers capable of early recognition of high-risk patients and constructing precise prognostic prediction models for MPP hold substantial clinical translational value and scientific significance for optimizing therapeutic strategies and improving long-term quality of life in affected children. Although inflammatory markers such as white blood cell count (WBC) and C-reactive protein (CRP) are frequently used to evaluate disease severity in children with MPP,9,10 they lack disease specificity and fail to reveal the specific immune-pathological mechanisms involved, resulting in limited predictive efficacy when used in isolation.

High-mobility group box 3 (HMGB3) is a non-histone chromosomal protein belonging to the high-mobility group (HMG) family, primarily involved in DNA repair, transcriptional regulation, and cell proliferation.11,12 Recent investigations have revealed its critical role in pulmonary inflammation. For instance, one study demonstrated that HMGB3 functions as a downstream effector molecule of the SNHG16/miR-128-3p axis; its upregulated expression promotes alveolar epithelial cell apoptosis and inflammation, exacerbating acute lung injury in sepsis.13 Additionally, another study reported that exosomal HMGB3 is significantly overexpressed in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid of silicosis patients, potentially promoting macrophage M1 polarization and monocyte infiltration through the STAT3/MAPK/NF-κB/CCR2 multi-pathway signaling axis, thereby contributing to the formation of early inflammatory microenvironments in pulmonary fibrosis and serving as a potential liquid biopsy marker for monitoring inflammatory activity in silicosis.14 These findings collectively indicate that HMGB3 plays a crucial pro-inflammatory role in lung inflammatory injury and repair processes; nevertheless, whether it participates in the pathogenesis of pediatric MPP and correlates with patient early treatment response remains unelucidated, warranting urgent clarification.

Proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9) is a serine protease predominantly secreted by the liver, which degrades low-density lipoprotein receptors (LDLR) in hepatocytes to elevate low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) levels while exerting pro-inflammatory effects through activation of the NF-κB pathway to exacerbate vascular inflammation.15,16 Research has demonstrated that PCSK9 not only elevates cholesterol through LDLR degradation but also directly activates inflammatory pathways and aggravates atherosclerosis, with this pro-inflammatory effect being independent of its impact on LDL receptors.17 Furthermore, studies have shown that PCSK9 is significantly elevated in the blood of patients with Gram-positive or Gram-negative bacterial infections, synchronously reflecting inflammatory intensity alongside CRP.18 Concurrently, PCSK9 levels are markedly increased in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid of smokers, exerting pro-inflammatory effects through induction of pro-inflammatory cytokines and activation of the MAPK p38 pathway, thus serving as a biomarker for pulmonary inflammation.19 In summary, the role of PCSK9 in inflammatory responses has been established; however, its specific involvement in pediatric MPP pathogenesis and its predictive value for early treatment response remain undefined, necessitating further exploration. Compared with conventional markers, both HMGB3 and PCSK9 are directly involved in inflammatory regulation, and their serum levels may more accurately reflect the immune-pathological status of MPP. Combined detection is expected to improve risk identification efficacy.

To date, no study has systematically evaluated the predictive efficacy of HMGB3 and PCSK9 for early treatment response in pediatric MPP. Through prospective cohort design, this study aims to elucidate the association between their serum levels and early treatment response, validate their clinical value as novel prognostic markers, and thereby provide new targets for improving risk stratification and individualized treatment of pediatric MPP.

Materials and Methods

Study Participants

This single-center prospective cohort study enrolled 400 children with confirmed MPP who were admitted to our hospital between January 2022 and January 2025 as the observation cohort, and 178 age-matched healthy children undergoing physical examination were selected as the control group. Sample size was estimated based on the events-per-variable (EPV) principle, requiring at least 10 events per predictor. With an anticipated poor early treatment response rate of approximately 20% and 5–8 candidate predictors considered for the multivariable model, a minimum of 50–80 outcome events and approximately 250–400 total participants were required. Diagnostic, Inclusion, and Exclusion Criteria

The diagnosis of MPP required fulfillment of the following three criteria:20,21 (1) acute respiratory infection symptoms, including fever, cough, wheezing, or tachypnea; (2) chest imaging demonstrating pulmonary infiltrative lesions (bronchopneumonia, segmental/lobar pneumonia, interstitial pneumonia, etc).; and (3) positive MP pathogen detection with serum MP-IgM antibody titer ≥ 1:80 by passive agglutination assay.

Inclusion criteria: (1) fulfillment of MPP diagnostic criteria, and aged 3–14 years; (2) normal growth and development (height and weight within reference ranges for age and gender); (3) disease course ≤7 days and no anti-mycoplasma treatment (macrolides, tetracyclines, or fluoroquinolones) within 48 hours prior to admission, with allowance for symptomatic treatment including antipyretics and expectorants; (4) serum collection completed within 24 hours of admission; (5) informed consent obtained from guardians with signed written consent; and (6) complete clinical data, laboratory examinations, and imaging materials.

Exclusion criteria: (1) active pulmonary infection caused by other pathogens (bacteria, viruses, fungi, tuberculosis, etc.) based on etiological testing and clinical judgment; (2) severe cardiopulmonary insufficiency: New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional class ≥III, or requirement for mechanical ventilation or extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) support; (3) concomitant malignant tumors, primary immunodeficiency diseases, hematological disorders, coagulation dysfunction, or active autoimmune diseases; (4) concomitant bronchial asthma (acute exacerbation within the past year), bronchopulmonary dysplasia, cystic fibrosis, congenital lung malformations, or other chronic pulmonary diseases; (5) systemic corticosteroid use (oral or intravenous, ≥0.5 mg/kg/day prednisone-equivalent dose, continuously for ≥3 days) or immunosuppressants within the past month; (6) previous history of Mycoplasma pneumoniae infection (within the past 6 months); (7) severe hepatic or renal insufficiency: alanine aminotransferase (ALT)/aspartate aminotransferase (AST) >3 times the upper limit of normal, or serum creatinine above the age-specific reference upper limit; and (8) known allergy to study medications or presence of contraindications to the study.

Treatment Protocol

All children received standardized azithromycin sequential therapy with the following protocol: intravenous infusion of 10 mg/kg (maximum 500 mg) once daily for 2 days; after defervescence and clinical stabilization, switched to oral administration of 10 mg/kg (maximum 500 mg) once daily, with a total treatment course of 5 days. Symptomatic supportive treatments were administered concurrently, including oxygen therapy, nebulized bronchodilators, antipyretics, and mucolytics as adjunctive measures.

Clinical Data Collection

Baseline demographic characteristics (age, gender, body weight) and admission laboratory parameters (WBC, neutrophil percentage, CRP, lactate dehydrogenase [LDH], D-dimer, Interleukin-6 [IL-6], Tumor Necrosis Factor-alpha[TNF-α]) were recorded. Intergroup comparisons encompassed age, gender, fever duration, peak body temperature, and Clinical Pulmonary Infection Score (CPIS).22

Laboratory Measurements

Fasting venous blood (5 mL) was collected within 3 hours of admission, centrifuged at 3,000 × g for 15 minutes; serum was then separated into aliquots. Specimens were either analyzed immediately or frozen at −20°C, with care taken to prevent repeated freeze-thaw cycles. Prior to testing, serum was diluted 1:2, and procedures were strictly performed according to kit instructions.

Serum HMGB3, PCSK9, and D-dimer levels were measured using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (EH0806, EH0251, EH20012; Wuhan Fine Biotech, China). LDH and IL-6 (JL12774, JL14113; Shanghai Jianglai Biology, China), and CRP and TNF-α (SHM10103, HM10001; Wuhan Bioswamp Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) were detected using ELISA kits according to manufacturer protocols.

Anti-Mycoplasma pneumoniae antibody titers were quantified via passive agglutination assay, while complete blood counts were performed using the Sysmex XN-1000 automated hematology analyzer.

All specimens were assigned unique random identifiers. Laboratory staff conducting the ELISA assays and verifying the data remained unaware of clinical diagnoses and group allocations. The senior pediatrician and radiologists assessing early treatment response were blinded to the laboratory results. However, owing to the observational nature of the study, both participants and their attending physicians were not blinded.

Early Treatment Response Assessment

After 7 days of treatment, poor early treatment response was determined by a senior pediatrician (blinded to the laboratory results) according to the early treatment response criteria.23 Children were assigned to the good early treatment response group (n=321) when they achieved full resolution of fever, cough, and wheezing; demonstrated ≥50% absorption of pulmonary infiltrates on chest imaging (reviewed by two independent radiologists masked to group allocation); and maintained clinical stability without escalation of treatment. Assignment to the poor early treatment response group (n=79) required fulfillment of any one of the following: (1) persistent fever ≥38°C lasting ≥7 days or worsening respiratory distress; (2) CT showing progressive disease (enlarging areas of consolidation, new-onset atelectasis, pleural effusion, or necrotic lesions); or (3) requirement for intensified therapy. Two researchers independently performed double data entry; a third researcher arbitrated any divergence exceeding 5%.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 software. Missing data were assessed for all variables. No missing data were observed for the outcome or key predictors; therefore, a complete-case analysis was conducted. Categorical variables were described as frequencies (percentages) and compared between groups using χ2-test or Fisher’s exact test. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), and compared using independent samples t-test or Mann–Whitney U-test according to distribution characteristics. Continuous predictors were entered as linear terms into the regression models after confirming the log-linearity assumption. Categorical predictors were treated as dichotomous variables. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was conducted to screen independent predictive factors for poor early treatment response, calculating odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). Internal validation was performed using 1000 bootstrap resamples to calculate optimism-corrected C-statistics and calibration slopes. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were plotted to evaluate the predictive value of serum HMGB3 and PCSK9 alone and in combination, calculating area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, and optimal cutoff values; Delong test was used to compare differences in AUC. Spearman rank correlation analysis was employed to explore correlations between HMGB3, PCSK9, and inflammatory indicators. A two-sided P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Reporting Standards

This study was reported in accordance with the TRIPOD (Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis Or Diagnosis) statement. The completed TRIPOD Checklist is provided in Supplementary Material 1.

Results

Comparison of Baseline Characteristics: MPP Children versus Healthy Controls

A total of 400 children with confirmed MPP were enrolled after applying the eligibility criteria; of these, 321 (80.3%) achieved good early treatment response and 79 (19.7%) had poor early treatment response. In this study, no statistically significant differences were observed between the MPP group (n=400) and healthy control group (n=178) regarding age, gender, or body mass index (BMI) (all P>0.05), indicating comparability between the two groups in terms of basic demographic characteristics. However, the MPP group demonstrated significantly elevated levels of multiple inflammation and tissue injury-related indicators: WBC, neutrophil percentage, and serum LDH were all markedly higher than those in healthy controls (P<0.05; Table 1), suggesting the presence of pronounced systemic inflammatory response and potential tissue damage in MPP children.

Variables Independently Associated with Poor Early Treatment Response in Pediatric MPP

To identify independent predictive factors for poor early treatment response in pediatric MPP, multivariable logistic regression analysis was conducted with clinical outcome (poor=1, good=0) as the dependent variable, and fever duration, peak temperature ≥38.5°C, CPIS score, Mycoplasma pneumoniae antibody titer, LDH, D-dimer, HMGB3, and PCSK9 as independent variables. The variance inflation factor (VIF) for all predictive factors was <2, indicating no multicollinearity in the model.

Results (detailed in Table 3) demonstrated that elevated CPIS score, Mycoplasma pneumoniae antibody titer ≥1:160, elevated LDH, elevated D-dimer, elevated HMGB3, and elevated PCSK9 were all independent predictive factors for poor early treatment response in pediatric MPP. By contrast, duration of fever and peak temperature ≥38.5°C did not reach statistical significance in the multivariable model. The final multivariable model demonstrated acceptable discrimination and calibration upon internal validation using 1000 bootstrap resamples, with an optimism-corrected C-statistic of 0.855 (95% CI: 0.814–0.896) and a calibration slope of 0.918. The full model is specified as follows: Logit(P) = β0 + β1×CPIS + β2×MP_antibody_titer + β3×LDH + β4×D_dimer + β5×HMGB3 + β6×PCSK9, where β0 represents the model intercept and β1–β6 represent the regression coefficients for each predictor (detailed in Table 3). To use the model, substitute an individual patient’s baseline predictor values into the equation to obtain the predicted probability of poor early treatment response. A predicted probability above the optimal cutoff value (Table 4) indicates high risk for poor early treatment response.

Predictive Performance of Admission HMGB3 and PCSK9 for Poor Response

ROC curve analysis was performed to evaluate the predictive value of admission serum HMGB3 and PCSK9 for poor early treatment response in pediatric MPP. Results showed that the AUC for serum HMGB3 and PCSK9 alone in predicting poor early treatment response was 0.766 (95% CI: 0.697–0.836) and 0.758 (95% CI: 0.691–0.826), respectively; the combined detection of both biomarkers significantly improved the AUC to 0.840 (95% CI: 0.787–0.893), demonstrating superior predictive efficacy compared to single markers (Table 4 and Figure 2).

Dynamic Changes in Serum HMGB3/PCSK9 and Inflammatory Indicators

Compared with the healthy control group, serum HMGB3, PCSK9, IL-6, CRP, and TNF-α levels were significantly elevated in MPP children before treatment (all P<0.05); following treatment, the levels of these indicators were significantly decreased compared with pre-treatment values (all P<0.05), indicating that inflammatory response and abnormal elevation of HMGB3 and PCSK9 were effectively controlled with treatment (Figure 3).

Discussion

This study identified significantly elevated serum HMGB3 and PCSK9 levels in children with MPP, which were weakly positively correlated with traditional inflammatory indicators and served as independent predictive factors for poor early treatment response in MPP. The combination of both biomarkers demonstrated superior predictive efficacy, and their levels decreased significantly following treatment, suggesting their potential as candidate serological markers for disease assessment and early treatment response evaluation in MPP, warranting further validation.

In this study, elevated CPIS, Mycoplasma pneumoniae antibody titer ≥1:160, elevated LDH, and elevated D-dimer were identified as independent predictive factors for poor early treatment response in pediatric MPP. The underlying mechanisms may be as follows: CPIS comprehensively reflects the clinical manifestations and radiological severity of pulmonary infection, and its elevation typically indicates expanded pulmonary inflammation and disease progression.22 Mycoplasma pneumoniae antibody titer ≥1:160 suggests robust host immune response to the pathogen, which may be associated with enhanced inflammatory reaction and aggravated immune-mediated injury.24 As an enzyme marker related to cellular damage, elevated LDH commonly indicates pulmonary tissue injury and increased severity of inflammatory response.20,25 Elevated D-dimer reflects hypercoagulable state and activation of the fibrinolytic system, correlating with intensified inflammatory response and increased risk of complications.10,26 Previous studies have also confirmed that LDH and D-dimer levels are closely associated with disease severity and prognosis in pediatric MPP.27 Therefore, these indicators can reflect disease severity and are consequently associated with adverse clinical outcomes. Although duration of fever and peak temperature ≥38.5°C were not retained as independent predictors in the final multivariable model, their inclusion was statistically justified given that all variance inflation factors were < 2, indicating no multicollinearity. The lack of independent significance likely reflects that these non-specific systemic manifestations were overshadowed by more direct pathophysiological indicators (CPIS, HMGB3, PCSK9, LDH, and D-dimer) that capture pulmonary inflammatory injury and disease severity more precisely.

HMGB3 is a high-mobility group nuclear protein belonging to the HMGB family, playing important roles in chromatin structural regulation, cell proliferation, and inflammatory response;28,29 PCSK9 is a serine protease involved in low-density lipoprotein receptor degradation and lipid metabolism regulation, which has recently been demonstrated to participate in various inflammation-related disease processes.30,31 Previous studies have shown that HMGB3 is upregulated in multiple pulmonary inflammatory diseases. For example, in acute lung injury models and lipopolysaccharide-induced pulmonary epithelial cell injury, HMGB3 expression is markedly elevated and can promote inflammatory cytokine release and cell apoptosis, suggesting its important regulatory role in pulmonary inflammatory response and tissue injury.13,32,33 On the other hand, besides its involvement in lipid metabolism, PCSK9 has also been confirmed to be closely related to inflammatory responses: in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), pulmonary fibrosis, and pulmonary arterial hypertension, PCSK9 levels are significantly upregulated and positively correlated with inflammatory cytokine levels and disease severity, promoting pulmonary microvascular endothelial cell apoptosis, inflammatory infiltration, and vascular remodeling, thereby exacerbating pulmonary injury and serving as a key pathogenic factor in the progression of pulmonary inflammatory diseases.19,34,35 However, research regarding HMGB3 and PCSK9 in MPP early treatment response remains relatively limited. The results of this study demonstrated that serum HMGB3 and PCSK9 levels were significantly higher in children with poor early treatment response compared to those with good early treatment response, and both were independent predictive factors for poor early treatment response in MPP. The underlying mechanisms may be attributed to the involvement of HMGB3 and PCSK9 in inflammatory regulation: on one hand, HMGB3 may participate in pulmonary inflammatory response and tissue injury through promoting macrophage activation, inflammatory cytokine release, and inflammatory cell recruitment;14 on the other hand, PCSK9 may be involved in inflammatory responses through activation of inflammatory signaling pathways, enhanced inflammatory cell responses, and microcirculatory injury, potentially contributing to pulmonary inflammation and delayed disease recovery.16,36

In recent years, multiple novel biomarkers have been explored for prognostic evaluation of MPP in children. Ma et al found that CCL26 and CCR3 levels predict prognosis in pediatric MPP patients on azithromycin therapy, with AUCs of 0.709 and 0.751, respectively, and combined detection increased to 0.798.24 A systematic review conducted by Ding and Jiang examined biomarkers associated with diagnostic and prognostic assessment of MPP in children, noting that the development of precise predictive models is essential for assisting clinical decision-making.7 However, the value of HMGB3 and PCSK9 in the prognosis of MPP had not been previously evaluated. The present study found that their serum levels are associated with early treatment response in MPP, providing a novel addition to the biomarker spectrum for MPP in children.

This study has the following limitations: the single-center design may limit the generalizability of the results; future multi-center, large-sample studies are warranted for external validation. This study used 7 days as the assessment endpoint, representing a short-term treatment response evaluation, and was unable to track long-term prognosis (such as bronchiolitis obliterans and pulmonary function recovery); future long-term follow-up studies are needed to validate the long-term predictive value of these biomarkers. In this study, the Spearman correlation coefficients between HMGB3, PCSK9, and inflammatory indicators were only 0.170–0.194, indicating weak positive correlations, suggesting that HMGB3 and PCSK9 may not be core drivers of the inflammatory response; their value as biomarkers lies primarily in independently predicting early treatment response rather than reflecting inflammatory intensity. The observational design precluded establishing a causal relationship between HMGB3 and PCSK9 and MPP prognosis; subsequent animal experiments and mechanistic studies should be conducted to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying their involvement in the pathological progression of MPP. This study did not compare the HMGB3/PCSK9 combined model with conventional clinical models, nor did it quantify the added contribution of the novel markers relative to conventional indicators through net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI); thus, their incremental clinical value remains unclear. Future studies should incorporate conventional clinical models as a reference to validate whether HMGB3/PCSK9 can provide additional risk stratification information beyond the existing assessment system. Furthermore, drug resistance gene testing and dynamic imaging evaluation were not included; future studies may integrate multi-omics technologies to construct precise predictive models and explore the potential therapeutic value of HMGB3/PCSK9-targeted interventions.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that serum HMGB3 and PCSK9 are independent predictors of poor early treatment response in children with MPP. Combined detection exhibits moderate discriminative performance and may serve as a reference for short-term risk assessment.
