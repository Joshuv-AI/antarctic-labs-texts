Bidirectional fractional-order dynamics of the microbiota-gut-brain axis in autism spectrum disorder featuring memory effects and inflammation thresholds
Authors: Raed Qahiti, Ali H Hakami, Ahmad Albaity, Shewafera Wondimagegnhu Teklu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractConventional models of the microbiota-gut-brain axis (MGBA) in Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) overlook the long-range memory effects inherent to neuroinflammation and gut dysbiosis, leading to underestimation of symptom persistence. This study introduces a bidirectional fractional-order dynamical system with compartment-specific fractional orders to capture these memory-dependent dynamics across five interconnected compartments: dietary antigens, gut dysbiosis, gut inflammation, neuroinflammation, and behavioral symptoms. We establish existence, uniqueness, boundedness, and stability via Matignon’s criterion, derive a novel inflammation threshold parameter , prove a transcritical bifurcation at , perform global Sobol sensitivity analysis, and formulate a fractional optimal control problem with three realistic interventions. Principal findings reveal: (i) a transcritical bifurcation at separating healthy recovery from chronic pathology; (ii) sensitivity analysis identifies the microbiota recovery rate (elasticity -1.667) as the most potent target for preventing chronicity and the behavioral recovery rate () as the dominant driver of steady-state symptom severity; (iii) optimal control simulations demonstrate that triple combination therapy outperforms all double combinations, with direct behavioral intervention () essential for minimizing symptoms; and (iv) stronger memory effects (lower α) significantly delay therapeutic recovery, confirming that ignoring memory fundamentally underestimates ASD symptom persistence.Keywords: Autism spectrum disorder, Gut-brain axis, Caputo fractional derivative, Reproduction number, Bidirectional feedback, Inflammation dynamicsSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Diseases, Neuroscience

Introduction

Background of autism spectrum disorder

Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) represents one of the most challenging human neurodevelopmental conditions, affecting approximately 1 in 36 children and imposing profound lifelong impacts on social communication, behavioral flexibility, and quality of individuals’ lives19,43. Despite various researchers’ decades of intensive research studies, the etiology of ASD remains persistently multifactorial, involving complex interactions between genetic predisposition, epigenetic modifications, environmental exposures, and immunological disturbances19,43,63. One of the most hopeful and most researched pathways is the microbiota-gut-brain axis (MGBA) which is a two-way communication system that connects the human digestive tract, the microbes living inside it, and the brain36,50,63. Both clinical and preclinical studies have consistently documented that individuals with ASD exhibit different patterns of gut dysbiosis characterized by reduced microbial diversity and overrepresentation of pro-inflammatory taxa, increased intestinal permeability, commonly called“leaky gut”; mucosal immune activation with elevated cytokines, and neuroinflammation involving activated microglia and astrocytes19,36,50,63. Basically, the severity of these gastrointestinal and immune perturbations correlates significantly with the severity of core behavioral symptoms, including irritability, social withdrawal, and repetitive behaviors, suggesting a causal or contributory role for peripheral inflammation in central nervous system dysfunction43,46. This dietary-gut-brain-behavior pathway thus represents not only an epiphenomenon but also a potentially targetable mechanistic circuit. However, the pathway’s inherent complexity, spanning multiple spatial scales from molecules to behavior, operating across diverse timescales from minutes to years, and featuring nonlinear interactions, time delays, and bidirectional feedback loops defies intuitive understanding and resists reduction to simple linear causality43,46.

Literature review

Mathematical modeling has become an indispensable tool for investigating biological complexity, translating qualitative knowledge into quantitative predictions that can be tested and optimized11,49. At its core, a mathematical model is a set of equations, often ordinary differential equations (ODEs), that describe how the state of a biological system changes over time in response to internal and external factors. Over the past decades, classical integer-order ODEs have been applied successfully to a wide range of real-world problems, from the spread of infectious diseases to population dynamics and physiological regulation9,13,34. These models are attractive because they are relatively simple, computationally efficient, and their behavior (equilibria, stability, and bifurcations) is well understood. However, as many researchers have pointed out, integer-order ODEs rest on a crucial assumption: that the future evolution of a system depends only on its present state, a Markovian property that ignores history48. Biological processes such as immune memory, chronic inflammation, epigenetic persistence, and neural remodeling all exhibit long-lasting effects that ODEs cannot capture18. To address this, stochastic models introduce randomness and have been used for phenomena like gene expression and small-population dynamics, but they do not naturally incorporate deterministic memory. Meanwhile, fractional-order calculus offers a deterministic way to incorporate memory and hereditary effects by replacing the integer derivative with a convolution integral that weights the entire history of the function, thereby capturing power-law decay of memory11,49. The Caputo fractional derivative has become especially convenient for biomedical problems because it allows standard initial conditions and converges continuously to the integer-order case when the order equals one7,11,49. Foundational work by Lin37, Deng and Ma8, Kosmatov33 and Webb62 established existence, uniqueness, and stability criteria for fractional differential equations using fixed-point theorems and comparison principles. On the numerical side, the fractional Adams-Bashforth-Moulton predictor-corrector method has become the standard for solving Caputo initial value problems10,12,14, and Matignon’s stability criterion generalizes the classical Routh-Hurwitz condition, showing that strong memory (small fractional order) reduces the stability region40,48. Thus, while ODE and stochastic models have served well for many applications, they fall short when memory effects are non-negligible a limitation that fractional calculus is uniquely positioned to overcome.

Building on these theoretical foundations, researchers have applied fractional-order modeling to a growing list of biological and medical problems, each with its own problem focus, objectives, methods, contributions, and findings. Hamdan and Kilicman27 extended the next-generation matrix method to fractional epidemic models, proving that the basic reproduction number retains its threshold meaning when equations are dimensionally consistent a critical methodological advance. Gonzalez18 used fractional derivatives to study neuroinflammatory wave propagation and synaptic integration, showing that fractional orders modify the speed and persistence of neural field activity. Khan et al.32 demonstrated that neurological disorder progression exhibits fractional-order dependence that integer-order models miss, providing a more faithful description of disease time courses. In the social and behavioral domain, Gümüş and Teklu and Teklu developed fractional-order models for stigma propagation, racism diffusion, and anxiety transmission, integrating optimal control theory to evaluate intervention strategies20–22,59,60. More recent studies have extended these ideas to infectious disease dynamics and spatial heterogeneity, further demonstrating the versatility of fractional frameworks3,23,39. In the context of calcium signalling in nerve cells, Joshi and Jha developed a fractional reaction-diffusion model that showed how the fractional order modifies the spatiotemporal distribution of free calcium, with implications for neurodegenerative disorders; their subsequent work incorporated buffering and endoplasmic reticulum fluxes, capturing slow, history-dependent dynamics that integer-order models miss29,?. In oncology, Gündoğdu and Joshi compared different functional forms of the net killing rate in a time-fractional cancer model using the Residual Power Series Method, concluding that fractional-order models better reflect the gradual, memory-driven response of tumors to therapy24. The COVID-19 pandemic spurred many fractional-order epidemic models; for instance, Joshi incorporated neurodegeneration and memory trace into a COVID-19 model, arguing that long-term neurological sequelae require a fractional framework28. A substantial body of work by Shafqat, Al-Quran, Alsaadi, and collaborators has applied the normalized Caputo-Fabrizio (NCF) and Atangana-Baleanu-Caputo (ABC) operators to various infectious diseases, including SEIR, polio, Zika, rabies, and dengue, as well as bifurcation analysis of a fractional SIR model4,5,53–57. While these papers provide rigorous mathematical analysis (existence, uniqueness, positivity, boundedness) and some incorporate deep neural networks for prediction, the neural networks are typically trained on data generated by the same fractional model a tautological exercise that does not demonstrate predictive power on real epidemiological records. Nevertheless, these studies collectively illustrate a growing recognition that fractional derivatives can capture memory effects (e.g., waning immunity and delayed public health responses) that integer-order models cannot. Critically, Ghosh raised two pervasive errors in many existing fractional-order biomedical models: dimensional inconsistency (the Caputo derivative has dimension while parameters often have dimension ) and the absence of a genuine disease-free equilibrium due to inappropriate linear activation terms, which invalidate standard calculations and undermine stability analyses using Matignon’s criterion15. Moreover, while local sensitivity analysis has been used to identify influential parameters, global methods like Sobol’ indices which capture parameter interactions and realistic uncertainty ranges have not been applied to ASD gut-brain models51,52,58. Similarly, optimal control theory has been extended to fractional systems by Agrawal, Kamocki, Bergounioux and Bourdin, and Gomoyunov, yet these powerful methods had not been used to optimize multimodal interventions targeting the dietary-gut-brain-behavior axis before the present study1,2,6,16,17,30.

From the above review, several critical gaps emerge across problem definition, objectives, novelty, methods, contributions, and main findings. First, most integer-order models of autism spectrum disorder (ASD) have focused on social stigma and awareness campaigns, deliberately excluding physiological mechanisms such as gut inflammation, dysbiosis, and neuroinflammation20,21. They therefore cannot inform biological interventions aimed at the gut-brain pathway, even though clinical studies have repeatedly shown that ASD is characterized by prolonged inflammatory responses, slow recovery from dysbiosis, and immune memory lasting months19,36,43,50,63. Second, while fractional-order models have been applied to neural fields, neurological disorders, and social dynamics, none have been developed for the dietary-gut-brain-behavior cascade in ASD18,22,25,32. Existing fractional models often suffer from the methodological flaws identified by Ghosh dimensional inconsistency and lack of a genuine inflammation-free equilibrium which invalidate their threshold analyses and reproduction number calculations15. Third, even when fractional models are correctly formulated, most rely on local sensitivity analysis rather than global Sobol’ indices, missing parameter interactions and realistic uncertainty ranges51,52,58. Fourth, optimal control theory has been applied to fractional systems in other contexts, but not to multimodal interventions (dietary restriction, gut therapy, behavioral therapy) for the gut-brain axis, leaving clinicians without mathematically optimized treatment schedules1,2,6,16,17,30. To fill these gaps, our study introduces a dimensionally consistent fractional-order model of the microbiota-gut-brain axis with five compartments (dietary antigens, gut dysbiosis, gut inflammation, neuroinflammation, behavioral symptoms). We explicitly address Ghosh’s critique by using bilinear coupling (e.g., bFM instead of bF) to ensure a genuine inflammation-free equilibrium and by rescaling parameters to maintain dimensional homogeneity15. Following the next-generation matrix method, we derive a dietary-microbial-inflammatory reproduction number and prove that the healthy state is stable when and unstable when , with a transcritical bifurcation at the threshold9,13,27. We then perform both local elasticity and global Sobol’ sensitivity analyses to rank intervention targets, and we formulate an optimal control problem with three controls (dietary restriction, gut therapy, behavioral intervention) using the fractional Pontryagin maximum principle6,16,17,51,52,58. Numerical simulations with the fractional Adams-Bashforth-Moulton method confirm the threshold dynamics and reveal that triple combination therapy outperforms any double combination10,12,14. By integrating rigorous mathematical analysis with biological realism, our work establishes a new baseline for fractional-order modeling of the gut-brain axis in ASD and provides a platform for personalized intervention design a novel gap that no previous study has addressed.

Novelty and contributions of the study

This study introduces a mathematically rigorous and biologically reasonable fractional-order dynamical system for the microbiota-gut-Brain Axis in autism spectrum disorder. Our work directly confronts foundational gaps in the existing literature, specifically, the inability of integer-order models to capture memory-driven inflammatory cascades18,48, the widespread dimensional inconsistencies and lack of genuine disease-free equilibria in many fractional-order biomedical models15, and the absence of global sensitivity analysis and multimodal optimal control in ASD gut-brain research2,52,58. By integrating multiple advanced analytical techniques into a single, coherent framework, we provide a new benchmark for modeling neurodevelopmental disorders. The core innovations and contributions of this study are as follows:

The study addresses two critical methodological drawbacks that have been identified in many fractional-order biological models15. First, we ensure dimensional consistency by explicitly assigning physical units to every state variable and parameter and by rescaling the fractional-order operators so that all terms in our equations are dimensionally homogeneous. Second, we construct a model with an authentic Inflammation-Free Equilibrium (IFE). This is achieved by replacing the commonly used, but problematic, linear activation term bF with a bilinear mass-action coupling bFG. This simple change is conceptually profound: in the complete absence of gut inflammation (G=0), dietary antigens (F) cannot spark an inflammatory response, reflecting oral tolerance mechanisms documented in clinical studies19. Consequently, we obtain a true healthy baseline state, , from which the system’s behavior can be rigorously analyzed.

Basic preliminaries

Fractional-order calculus has emerged as a powerful mathematical framework for modeling complex biological systems where memory and hereditary properties play crucial roles11. Unlike classical integer-order differential equations, which assume that the future behavior of a system depends only on its current state, fractional-order operators incorporate the entire history of the system’s evolution through convolution integrals with power-law kernels49. This non-local property makes fractional derivatives particularly suitable for biological processes such as immune responses, where past exposures shape current inflammatory states; neural adaptation, where previous stimuli influence present neuronal excitability; and chronic inflammatory conditions, where historical triggers contribute to persistent dysregulation18. In the case of our Microbiota-Gut-Brain Axis (MGBA) proposed study, fractional-order modeling captures the lingering effects of dietary antigens, the slow recovery of microbial ecosystems following dysbiosis, and the protracted time course of neuroinflammation features that align with the clinical presentation of Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD)36,63. Mathematical model

Mathematical modeling serves as an indispensable tool for deciphering the complex, multi-scale interactions that characterize the Microbiota-Gut-Brain Axis (MGBA) in Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD). By translating qualitative biological knowledge into a quantitative dynamical framework, models enable researchers to integrate experimental findings, identify critical thresholds that limit health from pathology, simulate the effects of interventions before clinical trials, and generate testable hypotheses about underlying mechanisms36,44. The pathophysiology of ASD involves intricate feedback loops spanning dietary intake, microbial ecology, immune function, neural activity, and behavior each operating on different timescales and exhibiting nonlinear interactions, time delays, and memory-dependent dynamics19,63. Classical integer-order differential equations, while useful, assume that future behavior depends only on the current state, an assumption that fails to capture the hereditary properties inherent in biological processes such as immune memory, epigenetic modifications, and chronic inflammatory remodeling11,48. Fractional-order calculus, with its non-local operators, naturally incorporates these memory effects and provides a more faithful representation of biological reality15. This section presents the development of a five-group fractional-order model that captures the essential dynamics of the MGBA in ASD, beginning with a detailed description of the state variables grounded in clinical and experimental evidence, followed by the biological assumptions that govern their interactions, and concluding with the complete dynamical system expressed through Caputo fractional derivatives.

Description of model variables

In this subsection, we classify the ASD problem into five key state variables representing the core biological compartments of the MGBA in ASD where each state variable has been selected based on extensive clinical and experimental documentation of its role in autism pathophysiology19,43,50. These variables capture the cascade from environmental triggers through gut dysfunction and neuroinflammation to behavioral manifestations, while also incorporating the recently recognized bidirectional feedback from behavior to gut health36,63.

F(t): Dietary Antigen Load is the variable that represents the concentration of immunoreactive food peptides in the intestinal lumen, particularly gluten-derived gliadin and casein-derived peptides that have been implicated in ASD pathogenesis. Many individuals with ASD exhibit increased intestinal permeability and altered antigen processing, allowing these peptides to persist longer and interact more extensively with the gut-associated immune system43. The dietary antigen load fluctuates with food intake and is influenced by digestive efficiency, gut transit time, and the composition of the gut microbiota that participates in food breakdown. Units: ng/mL.

A legitimate question concerns the reliability of our model when some of the above model assumptions are partially or temporarily violated in a given individual. Here we can emphasize that the model’s predictive power does not require every assumption to hold exactly at all times; instead, the dynamical system is structurally robust: deviations can be absorbed by adjusting the relevant parameter within its biologically reasonable range (Table 2) or by setting the offending term to zero when the corresponding mechanism is absent. For instance, if an ASD individual exhibits antigen-driven gut inflammation without measurable dysbiosis (contrary to assumption 3), the bilinear term bFM can be supplemented by a small constant baseline M or by an increased effective b; if the brain-to-gut feedback is weak (assumption 6), setting it η near zero recovers a unidirectional model without altering the mathematical structure. The fractional orders further enhance flexibility by tuning memory strength to match the observed persistence of inflammation, even when the precise origin of that memory is unknown. Hence, the model remains reliable for estimating the threshold and comparing interventions as long as the main causal pathways (diet → dysbiosis, → gut inflammation, and → neuroinflammation → behavior) are present. If an assumption is fundamentally violated, for example, if gut inflammation never self-limits (contradicting assumption 7) the logistic term would be replaced by exponential growth, and the model would correctly predict instability and treatment failure, which is itself a clinically useful signal.

Dietary-microbial-inflammatory reproduction number

The basic reproduction number is a classic threshold quantity in epidemiology, representing the average number of secondary infections caused by a single infected individual in a completely susceptible population. Here we adapt this concept to quantify the potential for a healthy gut-brain axis to transition into a chronic inflammatory state, and we define the Dietary-Microbial-Inflammatory Reproduction Number as the average number of secondary dysbiotic units generated by one unit of dietary antigen load in a completely healthy gut environment, where this threshold determines whether the inflammation-free equilibrium is stable () or unstable (), i.e., whether the system returns to health or progresses toward chronic inflammation and behavioral symptoms.

Biological interpretation of the bifurcation

The transcritical bifurcation at reveals a biological tipping point in the microbiota-gut-brain axis. Below this threshold, the system naturally clears inflammatory insults and maintains homeostasis, corresponding to a resilient phenotype. Above the threshold, even minor perturbations become self-sustaining: dysbiosis fuels gut inflammation, which triggers neuroinflammation and behavioral symptoms, while behavioral feedback (η M B) further worsens dysbiosis, trapping the system in chronic pathology. Therapeutically, reducing it below 1 via parameters like (dysbiosis-induced inflammation) or β (microbial activation) can restore health. Notably, stronger memory effects (lower fractional order α) delay recovery, implying that early intervention before inflammatory memory consolidates is clinically critical.

Data and parameter values

To bring our model to credible with realistic numbers, we turned to published clinical data that reflects real children and adults with autism. We started with the gut inflammation variable G(t), the fecal calprotectin levels (median 75.0 μg/g in ASD versus 21.0 μg/g in controls) and the serum cytokine profiles from26 gave us fixed reference values specific numbers we knew the model had to match. Sensitivity analysis

In a mathematical modeling of a complex biological system, the model parameters are rarely known with absolute precision; however, we can estimate them from experimental data, adopted from literature, or chosen based on physiological reasoning, with some degree of uncertainty. Sensitivity analysis is the systematic study of how this uncertainty in the model output (quantities such as the reproduction number or the steady-state behavioral symptom severity ) can be assigned to different sources of uncertainty in the model input parameters51. For our model of the dietary-gut-brain-behavior pathway in Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD), sensitivity analysis serves three fundamental objectives, such as identifying intervention targets, guiding future research directions, and understanding the fractional-order ASD system dynamics. In this section, we perform two complementary types of sensitivity analysis using the parameter values and biological interpretations described in Table 2. First, we conduct a local sensitivity analysis on the dietary-inflammatory reproduction number , which tells us how small changes in each parameter around their baseline values affect the threshold condition for inflammation spreading and second, we perform a global Sobol’ sensitivity analysis on the steady-state behavioral symptom severity , which accounts for parameter interactions and realistic uncertainty ranges, providing a more comprehensive ranking of therapeutic targets.

Global sensitivity analysis of behavioral severity

The local sensitivity analysis is invaluable for understanding the threshold for disease invasion, but the ultimate outcome that matters to patients and clinicians is the steady-state severity of behavioral symptoms, . This variable affects quality of life, determines the need for supportive services, and represents the clinical endpoint of interest. Moreover, depends on all model parameters and is influenced by the full nonlinear dynamics of the system, including logistic growth and the cascade from gut to brain to behavior19,50. Global sensitivity analysis explores the entire feasible parameter space and accounts for interactions between parameters, quantifying the fraction of total output variance attributable to each input factor both individually and through synergistic effects52,58. The Sobol’ method decomposes the total variance of the model output into components corresponding to individual parameters and their interactions, yielding first-order indices () that measure direct contributions and total-effect indices () that capture both direct and interaction effects51. To ensure reproducibility and statistical rigor, we employed a Latin hypercube sampling (LHS) design with 20,000 base samples (the number was increased from an initial 10,000 to verify convergence; Sobol’ index estimates changed by less than 2% between the two sample sizes). Each parameter was assigned a uniform distribution over its biologically plausible range, taken as of the baseline value for all parameters except the fractional orders, where a wider range was used to capture greater uncertainty. Independence among parameters was assumed because no prior correlation information is available from the literature. For each parameter set, the steady-state behavioral severity was obtained by numerically solving the full fractional-order system using the fractional Adams-Bashforth-Moulton predictor-corrector method (implemented in R with time step h=0.01 and a simulation horizon of T=365 days, which ensured that all transients had decayed to a relative change below ). Convergence of the Sobol’ indices was assessed by progressively increasing the number of model evaluations from 2,000 to 10,000 in increments of 2,000; the indices stabilized after 8,000 evaluations, so the final 20,000 evaluations provide a conservative safety margin. Optimal control problem and analysis

Our sensitivity analysis in Section “Sensitivity analysis” identified the most influential parameters driving the transition from health to chronic inflammation in the microbiota-gut-brain axis. The behavioral recovery rate (), the microbiota recovery rate (), the dietary antigen ingestion rate (), and the brain-to-gut feedback strength (η) emerged as critical targets for intervention. However, every therapeutic intervention carries costs: financial burdens, potential side effects, patient compliance challenges, and the psychological effort of maintaining strict dietary or behavioral regimens42. Pushing any treatment too aggressively can be as detrimental as undertreatment38 and this motivates the application of optimal control theory, a mathematical framework designed to find the best possible strategy that balances therapeutic benefits against intervention costs over a fixed time horizon1,2. In this section, we formulate and analyze a fractional-order optimal control problem that incorporates three biologically realistic intervention strategies, each targeting a distinct component of the gut-brain axis while respecting the full five-dimensional dynamics of our model.

Numerical simulations

The detailed mathematical work in Sections 3 through 6 gave us a solid, logical understanding of our gut-brain model. We proved, using equations, that a healthy state exists, that it has a clear threshold number (), and we could even rank which treatments should work best (sensitivity analysis) and set up the equations for finding the perfect treatment schedule (optimal control). While this mathematical proof is essential, it only tells part of the story; it tells us where the system will eventually end up, but it doesn’t show the path along the way. means it cannot show us the path the system takes, how fast it moves, or what inflammation looks like day by day. This is fundamental to why numerical simulations become absolutely vital: they serve as a bridge from the abstract theorems to the observable, time-based reality of a biological process. By simulating the model, we can get visual proof of our predictions, watching the system transition from health to inflammation right at . Also, we can see the memory effect in action, where a stronger memory (a smaller fractional order α) makes the whole inflammatory process move in slow motion and finally puts our sensitivity analysis to the test, watching the ranked treatments actually reduce gut and brain inflammation over a realistic timeline14,44. Because the model we constructed is a complex of interconnected parts, we cannot just write a simple formula to solve it; running these simulations is a necessary step to ensure consistency and to turn abstract sensitivity numbers into a concrete, visual story of how the system behaves31,45. Most importantly, numerical simulation is the only way to bring our optimal control problem to reality, allowing us to see the impact of the proposed intervention strategies unfolding over time-showing us not just that they work, but exactly how they change the course of inflammation and behavior in a living system.

Conclusions and future directions

This study offers a new way of thinking about the microbiota-gut-brain axis in Autism Spectrum Disorder, one where the past matters as much as the present. By replacing ordinary derivatives with fractional-order operators, we have shown that biological memory the lingering effect of past inflammation fundamentally changes how the system responds to disturbances. Standard integer-order models assume that inflammation fades as soon as its cause is removed. Our fractional framework reveals a harder truth: gut and brain tissues remember, and those memories trap the system in chronic pathology long after the original trigger has disappeared. The threshold parameter emerges naturally from this framework as the critical line between resilience and illness. Below one, the system shakes off perturbations and returns to health. Above one, even a small insult spirals into self-sustaining inflammation. We proved rigorously that the system undergoes a transcritical bifurcation exactly at using center manifold reduction, transforming what could have been a vague observation into a mathematical theorem. The bidirectional feedback term -η M B closes the loop: behavioral symptoms do not just sit at the end of the cascade but actively worsen gut dysbiosis, explaining why children with ASD often experience gastrointestinal flares following periods of high stress or behavioral regression. This circular causality means that treating the gut alone is unlikely to succeed if the brain keeps sending distress signals downward. Integrated interventions targeting both ends of the axis are not optional but mathematically necessary.

The practical insights from this work are equally concrete. Sensitivity analysis identifies the microbial recovery rate as the single most powerful lever for preventing disease onset, with an elasticity of -1.667. In clinical terms, improving the gut’s ability to restore healthy bacteria through probiotics, prebiotics, or fecal microbiota transplantation yields larger returns than any other single intervention. The global Sobol’ analysis adds a second finding: nearly thirty percent of the variation in behavioral symptom severity is explained by the behavioral recovery rate , meaning that therapies directly enhancing the brain’s capacity to calm itself (behavioral therapy, mindfulness, sensory integration) are essential for symptomatic improvement, not optional add-ons. The optimal control simulations then show how these insights combine into treatment schedules. Triple therapy that simultaneously reduces dietary antigen intake, supports gut healing, and accelerates behavioral recovery outperforms any double combination, with synergy indices between 0.021 and 0.091 confirming that the three interventions amplify each other. The optimal intensities follow a sensible pattern: high initial dosing that tapers over the year-long horizon, matching what clinicians already do intuitively but now with mathematical justification. Quantitatively, keeping above 0.4 day and γ below 0.3 day ensures , providing concrete benchmarks for trial design and treatment monitoring.

No model captures all of biology, and ours has several deliberate simplifications that point the way forward. First, the parameters used throughout this study were drawn from published literature rather than patient-specific data. The model therefore describes a generic gut-brain axis, not any particular child with ASD. Future work must collect longitudinal clinical measurements dietary diaries, 16S rRNA sequencing of stool, fecal calprotectin, cerebrospinal fluid cytokines where clinically feasible, and repeated behavioral assessments from the same individuals over time. These data would enable Bayesian calibration of the model to individual patients, transforming it from a theoretical construct into a personalized clinical tool. Second, the five-compartment structure deliberately aggregates complex biology into minimal state variables. We combined all beneficial bacteria into a single dysbiosis measure, all inflammatory mediators into gut and neuroinflammation, and all behaviors into one score. Future models could disaggregate these compartments: separate populations of Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium from pathogenic clostridia; distinguish from the inflammatory pathways; and track specific behavioral domains like social withdrawal, repetitive behaviors, and irritability separately. Such granularity would reveal which pathways matter most for which symptoms. Third, the fractional orders were treated as fixed parameters in our simulations, but they may themselves vary across individuals. A patient with a long history of recurrent gut infections might have stronger inflammatory memory (lower α) than one with recent onset. Future research should estimate from time-series data and test whether they correlate with clinical phenotypes, treatment responsiveness, or disease trajectory. Fourth, our optimal control framework assumed perfect adherence and ignored intervention costs, side effects, and practical constraints. Real-world treatment decisions balance benefit against burden. Adding cost functions, dose-limiting toxicities, and probabilistic adherence would move the model closer to clinical reality and enable cost-effectiveness comparisons of different treatment sequences. Fifth, the current model treats the gut and brain as well-mixed compartments, ignoring spatial propagation. In reality, inflammatory signals travel along the vagus nerve and through circumventricular organs with finite speed. Adding reaction-diffusion terms would capture wave-like propagation and could explain why some brain regions are affected earlier than others. Finally, the mathematical framework developed here applies beyond ASD. Anxiety disorders, major depression, Parkinson’s disease, and even chronic fatigue syndrome all involve gut-brain communication. Adapting our fractional-order, bidirectional model to these conditions could reveal common dynamical principles underlying brain-body communication in health and disease and may identify whether certain interventions (probiotics, anti-inflammatories, and vagus nerve stimulation) work across multiple conditions for shared mechanistic reasons.
