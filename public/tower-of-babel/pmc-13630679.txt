Case Report: Tislelizumab-associated secondary adrenal insufficiency in a patient with lung squamous cell carcinoma
Authors: Zongsheng Tian, Guanghui Zhang, Wenli Kong, Xueliang Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundImmune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have transformed cancer treatment, but immune-related adverse events remain clinically challenging. Tislelizumab is a humanized anti-PD-1 monoclonal antibody widely used in China, and tislelizumab-associated secondary adrenal insufficiency (SAI) remains insufficiently characterized, particularly with regard to how and when it is recognized.Case presentationA 66-year-old man underwent video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery for right lower lobe squamous cell carcinoma (pT1cN1M0, stage IIB), followed by four cycles of carboplatin, paclitaxel liposome and tislelizumab, then 13 cycles of tislelizumab maintenance. His last tislelizumab dose was administered on December 11, 2023. Poor appetite and fatigue first developed in November 2023, accompanied by a blood pressure of 92/57 mmHg. His symptoms improved after supportive treatment, but adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) and cortisol were not measured. He subsequently developed headache, nausea, vomiting, and progressive weight loss of 19 kg. In November 2024, he was admitted with hypotension (75/56 mmHg) and severe hyponatremia (108.9 mmol/L); chest CT and bronchoscopy excluded tumor recurrence, and he improved with supportive care. Symptoms recurred within a week of discharge, and he was readmitted in December 2024 with hypotension (52/37 mmHg) and recurrent hyponatremia. On December 11, 2024, approximately 13 months after the initial symptoms, morning cortisol was <1.00 µg/dL and ACTH was inappropriately low at <5.00 pg/mL, supporting SAI (Naranjo score 6, probable). Pituitary MRI, cosyntropin stimulation testing and comprehensive pituitary hormonal assessment were unavailable; hypophysitis was therefore not confirmed, and the site of hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis injury could not be determined. He improved with intravenous hydrocortisone and fluids and was transitioned to oral prednisone. ACTH and cortisol remained low through July 2025 during replacement, although endogenous HPA-axis recovery could not be reliably assessed. At telephone follow-up in June 2026 no recurrent adrenal crisis was reported.ConclusionsTislelizumab-associated SAI may present with nonspecific symptoms and remain unrecognized until adrenal crisis develops, including months after the last dose. Unexplained fatigue, gastrointestinal symptoms, weight loss, hyponatremia, hypoglycemia, or hypotension should prompt early measurement of paired morning ACTH and cortisol levels. Prompt glucocorticoid replacement and structured endocrine follow-up are essential for patient safety and assessment of potential HPA-axis recovery.

Introduction

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) targeting PD-1/PD-L1 and CTLA-4 have transformed oncology practice (1, 2), yet their use is associated with immune-related adverse events (irAEs) affecting multiple organ systems (3). Endocrine irAEs are clinically important because they can lead to persistent, and sometimes permanent, hormone deficiencies requiring long-term or lifelong hormone replacement, even after discontinuation of ICI therapy (4). Among these, secondary adrenal insufficiency (SAI) associated with ICI-related pituitary-adrenal axis dysfunction is uncommon but potentially life-threatening if not promptly recognized (5).

Tislelizumab is a humanized IgG4 anti-PD-1 monoclonal antibody developed in China and specifically engineered to minimize Fcγ receptor binding on macrophages, thereby reducing Fc-mediated effects that may compromise antitumor T-cell activity. It received its first regulatory approval in China in December 2019 for relapsed or refractory classical Hodgkin lymphoma and has since been approved for multiple hematologic and solid tumor indications (6). Although safety data on tislelizumab have accumulated rapidly, reports specifically addressing tislelizumab-associated adrenal insufficiency remain relatively few and are heterogeneously reported.

Herein, we report a case of SAI following tislelizumab treatment for resected lung squamous cell carcinoma. The patient was diagnosed with SAI during an episode suggestive of adrenal crisis after prior exposure to tislelizumab, and follow-up ACTH and cortisol measurements obtained during glucocorticoid replacement remained low, although endogenous HPA-axis recovery could not be formally assessed. This case highlights the need to consider tislelizumab-associated SAI in patients with current or prior exposure to ICIs and supports structured endocrine surveillance during and after ICI therapy.

Case description

Patient history and clinical course

A 66-year-old male farmer was diagnosed with right lower lobe squamous cell carcinoma in May 2022. He underwent video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) with right middle and lower lobectomy plus lymph node dissection (postoperative stage: pT1cN1M0, stage IIB). Adjuvant therapy comprised four cycles of carboplatin(0.5 g), paclitaxel liposome(210 mg), and tislelizumab (200 mg), followed by 13 cycles of tislelizumab monotherapy maintenance (200 mg every 4 weeks), with the last dose administered on December 11, 2023.PD-L1 expression was not tested before treatment. His medical history was otherwise unremarkable, except for a 40-pack-year smoking history. No history of autoimmune disease was documented. Apart from single-dose dexamethasone 5 mg administered intravenously as premedication with paclitaxel liposome on the day of each of the four combination chemotherapy cycles, he had received no systemic glucocorticoid therapy before the diagnosis of adrenal insufficiency; no glucocorticoid premedication was given during the subsequent 13 cycles of tislelizumab monotherapy maintenance. No systematic irAE-oriented endocrine baseline assessment was performed before tislelizumab initiation; pretreatment ACTH, cortisol, and gonadal hormones were unavailable. Preoperative echocardiography showed no clinically significant abnormalities. Thyroid function was assessed at four time points during and after treatment (Table 1). Isolated borderline abnormalities were observed while on therapy: a marginally low thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) of 0.524 µIU/mL (reference range, 0.55–4.78) in December 2022, a marginally low free thyroxine (FT4) of 11.00 pmol/L (reference range, 11.5–22.7) in August 2023, and a mildly elevated free triiodothyronine (FT3) of 7.15 pmol/L (reference range, 3.5–6.5) in December 2023. On each occasion the remaining thyroid parameters were within the reference range, and none of these abnormalities was accompanied by clinical thyroid dysfunction or required treatment. Thyroid peroxidase antibodies (TPO-Ab) were elevated in December 2022 (300.4 IU/mL; reference range, 0–60) and August 2023 (256.2 IU/mL), and declined to 119.6 IU/mL in December 2023; TPO-Ab was not repeated thereafter. At the first admission in November 2024, FT3, FT4 and TSH were all within the reference range. These findings indicate thyroid autoantibody positivity with minor, transient thyroid-function abnormalities but no overt thyroid dysfunction. Because pretreatment TPO-Ab testing was unavailable, whether the thyroid autoimmunity predated tislelizumab could not be determined.

Clinical presentation and diagnosis

The patient first developed poor appetite and fatigue in November 2023. At that time, his temperature was 36.3 °C, pulse rate 86 beats/min, respiratory rate 21 breaths/min, and blood pressure 92/57 mmHg. His symptoms improved following symptomatic and supportive treatment, but cortisol and ACTH levels were not measured.

On November 16, 2024, the patient was admitted with progressive fatigue, anorexia, nausea and vomiting. His temperature was 36.2 °C, pulse rate 76 beats/min, respiratory rate 20 breaths/min, and blood pressure 75/56 mmHg. He was alert and cooperative, with unlabored breathing, coherent responses, and clear speech. Breath sounds were slightly coarse without wheezes or crackles. Cardiac rhythm was regular without pathological murmurs. The abdomen was soft and non-tender without rebound tenderness, and no lower-extremity edema was observed. Laboratory findings revealed severe hyponatremia (108.9 mmol/L), hypochloremia (78.3 mmol/L), and hypoglycemia (3.38 mmol/L). Given his history of lung cancer and prior immunotherapy, disease progression and treatment-related toxicity were initially considered. Chest CT and bronchoscopic evaluation showed postoperative changes without evidence of tumor recurrence or metastasis. He was treated with antibiotics, fluid resuscitation, and sodium supplementation, with symptomatic improvement, and was discharged on November 24, 2024.

However, his symptoms recurred and worsened within one week after discharge, leading to readmission on December 4, 2024. On readmission, his temperature was 36.2 °C, pulse rate 85 beats/min, respiratory rate 16 breaths/min, and blood pressure 52/37 mmHg, raising concern for adrenal crisis. He was alert but appeared fatigued, and the remainder of the physical examination was essentially unchanged from that documented during the first admission. Repeat laboratory testing showed recurrent hyponatremia, with serum sodium declining from 136.9 mmol/L on readmission to 122.6 mmol/L by December 11. In view of recurrent hypotension, hyponatremia, profound weight loss, and the absence of tumor recurrence, endocrinology consultation was obtained. Morning endocrine testing on December 11 showed cortisol <1.00 µg/dL (reference range, 5-25 µg/dL) and an inappropriately low ACTH level of <5.00 pg/mL (reference range, 0–46 pg/mL), supporting the diagnosis of secondary adrenal insufficiency. Thyroid function, last measured at the first admission on November 16, 2024, had been normal (Table 1). Non-contrast head CT showed no brain metastasis or pituitary macroadenoma. Pituitary MRI was not performed during the acute illness because of the patient’s poor general condition. Key laboratory findings across both admissions and follow-up are presented in Table 1. Diagnostic assessment

The profoundly low morning cortisol level accompanied by an inappropriately low ACTH level strongly supported secondary adrenal insufficiency. Primary adrenal insufficiency was considered unlikely because ACTH was low rather than elevated; in primary adrenal insufficiency, low cortisol is typically accompanied by elevated ACTH (7). Thyroid function measured at the first admission in November 2024 was normal and there was no clinical evidence of thyroid dysfunction at diagnosis, making concomitant thyroid axis involvement unlikely; thyroid hormones were not repeated on December 11, 2024. Chest CT and bronchoscopic evaluation showed no evidence of tumor recurrence or metastasis, and there was no clinical evidence of adrenal or pituitary metastasis. The patient’s only systemic glucocorticoid exposure was single-dose dexamethasone 5 mg given intravenously as chemotherapy premedication on the day of each of the four combination cycles, which had been completed more than two years before diagnosis; such brief, intermittent, low-dose exposure is not expected to produce sustained suppression of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, and the entire year of tislelizumab monotherapy maintenance that followed was glucocorticoid-free. Exogenous glucocorticoid-induced adrenal suppression was therefore considered unlikely. Together with the temporal association with tislelizumab exposure and symptom development during the treatment course, these findings supported tislelizumab-associated secondary adrenal insufficiency as the most likely diagnosis. However, because pituitary MRI, cosyntropin testing, and comprehensive pituitary hormonal assessment were unavailable, hypophysitis could not be confirmed, isolated ACTH deficiency could not be distinguished from broader pituitary dysfunction, and the exact site of HPA-axis injury remained uncertain.

Other treatment-related causes were also reviewed. Carboplatin and paclitaxel were considered less likely direct contributors because adrenal insufficiency is not recognized as a typical adverse reaction in their prescribing information. Causality was assessed using the Naranjo Adverse Drug Reaction Probability Scale, with a score of 6 indicating a “probable” adverse drug reaction (Table 2) (8).

Therapeutic intervention, follow-up, and outcomes

After SAI was diagnosed on December 11, 2024, the patient received fluid resuscitation and intravenous hydrocortisone (50 mg in the morning and 25 mg in the evening), followed by transition to oral prednisone replacement. His blood pressure, serum sodium, and clinical symptoms improved, and he was discharged on oral glucocorticoid replacement. During follow-up, prednisone was adjusted to a maintenance regimen according to clinical status. ACTH and cortisol remained low through July 2025 during glucocorticoid replacement, with normalized electrolytes, however, HPA-axis recovery could not be reliably assessed because sampling was not standardized relative to prednisone dosing and dynamic testing was unavailable. At telephone follow-up in June 2026, no recurrent adrenal crisis was reported, but updated biochemical reassessment was not available.

Discussion

SAI is an uncommon but clinically important endocrine immune-related adverse event associated with ICIs. In this case, the diagnosis was supported by a profoundly low morning cortisol level with an inappropriately low ACTH level, improvement after glucocorticoid replacement, prior exposure to tislelizumab, and the exclusion of tumor recurrence by imaging and bronchoscopic evaluation. Although adrenal insufficiency is a recognized endocrine complication of ICIs, tislelizumab-associated SAI remains insufficiently characterized, particularly with respect to delayed recognition and longitudinal endocrine assessment (5, 9–11).

The mechanism of ICI-associated SAI is thought to involve immune-mediated injury of the pituitary-adrenal axis, presenting as hypophysitis or isolated ACTH deficiency (9, 11), but the two checkpoint pathways appear to differ. For CTLA-4 blockade therapy, pituitary endocrine cells ectopically express CTLA-4, and antibody binding subsequently induces complement-mediated tissue injury (12). No comparable mechanism has been established for PD-1 blockade, although the clinical phenotype differs from that of CTLA-4 blockade. In a comparative series, hypophysitis was less frequent after nivolumab or pembrolizumab than after ipilimumab, pituitary enlargement was uncommon, and the interval to diagnosis was longer (13). Isolated ACTH deficiency can occur after anti-PD-1 therapy even without pituitary enlargement. The low ACTH and cortisol levels in our patient were compatible with this phenotype, although pituitary MRI was unavailable. In a Japanese cohort, pretreatment anti-pituitary antibodies and several HLA alleles (HLA-Cw12, HLA-DR15, HLA-DQ7, and HLA-DPw9) were more frequent in patients who developed ICI-induced isolated ACTH deficiency, suggesting a possible role for host susceptibility (14). Anti-GNAL and anti-ITM2B autoantibodies have also been associated with ICI-related hypophysitis (15). A complementary paraneoplastic mechanism has been proposed in a patient with large-cell neuroendocrine carcinoma, in whom ectopic ACTH expression and lymphocytic infiltration were demonstrated in tumor tissue, together with circulating anti-POMC antibodies recognizing the ACTH25–39 region (16). Tislelizumab is engineered to minimize Fcγ receptor binding on macrophages, but whether this modification affects the risk or pathogenesis of endocrine irAEs remains unknown. In our patient delayed onset and delayed recognition cannot be distinguished, because adrenal function was never assessed during the symptomatic interval. These findings remain preliminary and do not yet support routine antibody or HLA testing, so recognition still depends on paired morning ACTH and cortisol measurement. Future studies should evaluate potential predictive biomarkers to facilitate early detection, prompt treatment, and close monitoring of this potentially life-threatening endocrine irAE.

Table 3 summarizes 12 patients reported in nine previous publications (17–25). Clinical presentation varied across the reported cases, from biochemical abnormalities found during routine screening to adrenal crisis. Blood pressure was not reported in 8 of the 12 patients, making it difficult to compare the severity of hemodynamic involvement. Cortisol deficiency lasting more than 7–8 months was reported in two patients. Apparent biochemical recovery was reported in only one patient, whose cortisol level decreased again after one cycle of tislelizumab rechallenge. Follow-up in most other patients lasted only about 5 weeks to 3 months. In our patient, anorexia, fatigue, and hypotension (92/57 mmHg) first appeared during tislelizumab treatment in November 2023. The symptoms improved after supportive treatment but later recurred, and SAI was diagnosed approximately 13 months after their initial appearance. During the two admissions, the patient developed profound hyponatremia (108.9 mmol/L) and severe hypotension (52/37 mmHg). Biochemical data were available through July 2025, and clinical follow-up continued until June 2026. Adrenal insufficiency was not initially considered. When hypotension was first documented in November 2023 the patient was still receiving tislelizumab, and the accompanying anorexia and fatigue were attributed to treatment; by the November 2024 admission tislelizumab had been stopped eleven months earlier, and the symptoms were attributed to the underlying malignancy. Paired morning ACTH and cortisol were therefore not obtained until December 2024. Compared with previous reports, this case contributes a fully documented recognition pathway together with a more severe presentation and longer follow-up, rather than a new endocrine phenotype. This pattern is consistent with a recent systematic review of ICI-associated adrenal insufficiency, which found that early symptoms are often vague, easily misattributed to cancer-related or treatment-related causes, and associated with delayed hormonal testing and diagnosis (5). Therefore, persistent or clustered nonspecific symptoms in ICI-exposed patients should prompt early morning ACTH and cortisol testing, even when symptoms are initially mild or attributed to malignancy-related causes.

In this patient, septic, cardiogenic, and hypovolemic shock were considered; however, these alternative causes were not supported by the overall clinical course, inflammatory markers, cardiac evaluation, imaging findings, and the rapid clinical improvement after hydrocortisone replacement with supportive treatment. Once ICI-associated SAI is diagnosed, patients require glucocorticoid replacement, education regarding stress-dose glucocorticoids, and structured endocrine follow-up to assess treatment adequacy and potential HPA axis recovery (9).

This case report has several limitations. Pituitary MRI and complete assessment of the remaining pituitary axes were not available; therefore, hypophysitis could not be confirmed, isolated ACTH deficiency could not be distinguished from broader pituitary dysfunction, and the exact site of HPA-axis injury remained uncertain. Cosyntropin stimulation testing was not performed, and follow-up blood sampling was not standardized relative to prednisone administration, precluding reliable assessment of endogenous HPA-axis recovery. As a single case report, causality cannot be definitively established, and the findings should be interpreted in the context of existing literature.

Conclusions

This case highlights tislelizumab-associated secondary adrenal insufficiency as a potentially underrecognized endocrine immune-related adverse event. In patients receiving or previously exposed to tislelizumab, unexplained fatigue, gastrointestinal symptoms, hyponatremia, hypoglycemia, weight loss, or hypotension should prompt early measurement of paired morning ACTH and cortisol levels. Timely glucocorticoid replacement and structured endocrine follow-up are essential to help prevent adrenal crisis, guide long-term management, and assess potential HPA axis recovery.
