Negligence in data recording during institutional birth: exploring health workers’ perspectives on the barriers and enablers of data documentation practices
Authors: Biniam Kefiyalew Taye, Lemma Derseh Gezie, Asmamaw Atnafu, Shegaw Anagaw Mengiste, Jens Kaasbøll, Monika Knudsen Gullslett, Binyam Tilahun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe availability of well-documented records during labor and delivery is essential for monitoring childbirth and ensuring positive outcomes for mothers and newborns. However, recent research in northwestern Ethiopia shows that data recording during these critical periods is inadequately performed. This study explored the barriers and enablers of data recording practices during institutional childbirth in this region.MethodsA descriptive qualitative study was conducted in six health centers in the Wegera district, focusing on three source documents: one facility-level log (the Delivery Register) and two personal records (the Integrated Card and the Women’s Folder). An interview guide was used to collect spoken accounts from 14 health workers. The spoken data was transcribed and analyzed using thematic framework analysis, assisted by ATLAS.ti (version 9.1.3).ResultsThis study identified three primary barriers to recording institutional birth data: organizational, behavioral, and technical. Four key subthemes emerged from the analysis within the organizational barriers category: the workload during childbirth, the lack of supervision of data within personal records, a lack of accountability for recording, and the turnover of trained staff responsible for data management. Behavioral barriers included health workers neglecting data recording and negative attitudes toward documentation. Regarding technical obstacles, the complexity of data recording formats presents challenges, resulting in missing data and errors when transferring data from one source to another. Despite these barriers, the study highlighted enablers, all of which were organizational, including the presence of routine data quality reviews, training, and supervision from the higher officials.ConclusionsThis study examines health workers’ perspectives in northwestern Ethiopia on the barriers and enablers to recording birth data, focusing on women’s personal records. It shows that weak organizational emphasis on client-level documentation and limited accountability for personal records diminishes motivation and contributes to neglect of crucial data. Busy settings, marked by heavy workloads, staff shortages, and competing priorities, further undermine confidence in maintaining accurate records. Interventions should aim to enhance motivation, build staff self-esteem in documenting under pressure, and foster a sense of professional responsibility. Promoting a positive attitude toward detailed record-keeping is also essential.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13690-025-01735-9.Keywords: Childbirth, Barriers, Enablers, Data recording, Data accuracy, Ethiopia, Workload

Background

Childbirth is a critical life event that requires rigorous monitoring to ensure positive outcomes for both mothers and newborn [1]. Appropriate data collection during labor, delivery, and the postpartum period is an essential component of this effort [2]. Research consistently emphasizes the importance of comprehensive documentation at health facilities in reducing maternal and neonatal morbidity and mortality [3–5]. The availability of essential birth-related data enables healthcare providers to adhere to evidence-based guidelines during institutional childbirth, facilitating the early detection and management of life-threatening complications [6, 7]. For instance, well-documented records empower birth attendants to identify deviations from regular labor progress and take timely, appropriate actions [8, 9].

To promote evidence-based maternal and newborn care, many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) have implemented strategies to strengthen their Routine Health Information Systems (RHIS) [10]. RHIS systematically collects, processes, and reports data generated during healthcare service delivery, with institutional childbirth being a key indicator monitored within these frameworks [11]. Improving data recording and reporting is a priority, and the commitment to enhancing institutional birth data documentation is evident. It is reflected in established data quality review toolkits, such as the WHO Data Quality Review (DQR) and the Performance of Routine Information System Management (PRISM), which regularly assess RHIS data quality [12, 13].

In Ethiopia, multiple interventions have been introduced to strengthen data recording practices in public health facilities [14, 15]. The Ministry of Health (MOH), in collaboration with regional health bureaus and local health offices, provides ongoing support and monitoring to ensure optimal data quality and utilization [16, 17]. One of the key initiatives addressing RHIS performance in quality data recording and use is the Capacity Building and Mentorship Program (CBMP) [18, 19], a partnership between the MOH and local universities. Through this program, universities offer technical support, including supervision, mentorship, and training, to enhance data quality and usage at health facilities. In northwestern Ethiopia, the University of Gondar has been involved in supporting two districts within the Central Gondar Zone. Collaborating with the MOH and the Amhara National Regional State, the university aims to improve data recording practices and enhance the quality and utilization of data in healthcare facilities [20, 21].

Despite these efforts, gaps persist in the documentation of institutional births. Some studies conducted in healthcare facilities across the region reveal that recording birth-related data remains inadequate, with some key data elements documented at rates as low as 10% [22]. This situation highlights the need for a deeper understanding of the contextual factors influencing data recording practices in institutional childbirth records.

Therefore, this study aimed to explore the barriers and enablers associated with these practices through qualitative research. By conducting in-depth interviews (IDIs) with frontline healthcare providers in northwestern Ethiopia, the research sought to generate context-specific insights that could inform strategies for improving data recording practices in similar settings.

Methods

Study design and period

This study employed a descriptive qualitative design to explore health workers’ perspectives regarding barriers and enablers related to data recording practices related to institutional birth in health facilities. Qualitative descriptive design is a valuable research approach recognized for its flexibility and potential to produce high-quality findings [23]. Data collection was conducted from July 7 to 16, 2022.

Study settings

The study was conducted in the Wegera district of the Amhara National Regional State, Ethiopia. This district is located 698 km northwest of Ethiopia’s capital, Addis Ababa. According to the Ethiopian Central Statistical Agency (CSA), the population of Wegera district was approximately 278,942 as of 2021 [24, 25]. The district’s health infrastructure includes one primary hospital and eight health centers. The health workforce in the district consists of 678 healthcare providers and 215 support staff [26, 27]. Regarding the volume of deliveries during the period around the data collection, from Hamle 2013 to Miazia 2014 (i.e., July 2021 to April 2022), the National DHIS2 reporting system recorded 1,648 SBAs in Wogera district, averaging about 165 births per month. This amounts to roughly 5.5 deliveries attended daily at each health facility.

Documentation practices

This study examined the barriers and enablers linked to data recording practices concerning institutional birth data, with a specific focus on women’s personal records. It is crucial to recognize that, in healthcare, “data recording” and “data documentation” are closely intertwined yet can have different functions. Within the scope of this study, we used these terms interchangeably to describe the process of capturing and maintaining client health data in line with Ethiopia’s HMIS data recording and reporting procedures [28]. These guidelines set forth standardized national formats for documenting birth data at health facilities in the study areas.

We focused on three primary source documents for capturing institutional birth data. These three primary source documents used for birth data documentation include:

Integrated Card – Records pregnant women’s data throughout antenatal care (ANC), labor, delivery, and postnatal care (PNC). It contains medical history, physical exam results, and clinical data for both mother and newborn. The partograph used to track labor progress is embedded within this card.

Individual Folder – A pouch that holds the integrated card and organizes a woman’s personal records. The front section contains personal identification data, while the inner section captures service summary data.

Delivery Register – A serial logbook listing all women who give birth at the facility. Data in this register is abstracted from the integrated card.

Sampling and study participants

The study was conducted in six of the eight health centers in the Wegera district. Two health centers were excluded due to security concerns in adjacent areas during the study period. The study focused on four key departments within these health centers: the Maternal and Child Health (MCH) unit, the HMIS unit, the office of the health center head, and the Medical Record Unit (MRU).

Study participants included health workers responsible for recording and managing institutional birth data, such as midwives, nurses, Health Information Technologists (HIT), and personnel from the MRU. Participants were recruited using a purposive sampling technique to include individuals with direct experience in birth data documentation, and sampling continued until data saturation was reached. The selection process was facilitated through discussions with facility heads and department coordinators to identify those with the most experience in data management.

Data collection

In-depth interviews (IDIs) were conducted using an interview guide prepared by this study’s authors to gather data for this study (Additional file 1). The guide included semi-structured questions and probes to capture participants’ perspectives on the barriers and enablers in recording practices of institutional birth data. An audio recorder was used during the interviews to capture participants’ spoken words, ensuring data integrity and facilitating subsequent transcription and analysis. All in-depth interviews were conducted in the local language, Amharic.

Field notes were used to record observational data, including identifying commonly missing data elements indicated by study participants during the interview, comparing documentation practices to guidelines, and noting any practical challenges encountered when recording some data fields, as reported by the study participants.

Data analysis

Thematic framework analysis, assisted by ATLAS.ti software (version 9.1.3) [29]was employed to analyze this study’s data following six recommended phases: Data Familiarization, Initial Coding, Theme Construction, Reviewing Themes, Naming and Defining Themes, and Report Production [29, 30]. The analysis used the PRISM framework [31], under which the themes identified were categorized into three primary determinants of RHIS performance.

For data familiarization, each audio recording of the in-depth interviews was transcribed in Amharic and then translated into English. The transcripts were carefully reviewed to ensure a thorough understanding of participants’ perspectives. The transcribed data, stored in Microsoft Word format, were imported into ATLAS.ti for detailed analysis.

Initial coding was performed inductively, assigning codes based on the data [32, 33]. Five transcripts were coded first to develop a preliminary codebook, which was exported in Microsoft Excel format. The codebook contained codes and their descriptions, serving as a reference for coding the remaining nine transcripts. Once all transcripts were coded, related codes were grouped to identify themes, which were iteratively refined and named based on participants’ narratives and existing literature on RHIS [32].

The coding and theme development process remained flexible, allowing for continuous refinement. Some initial themes were condensed into codes, while others evolved into distinct themes. Although the analysis began inductively, it was later aligned with the PRISM framework [31]. In this stage, subcategories derived from the data were mapped onto the three primary PRISM determinants of RHIS performance: behavioral, organizational, and technical.

During the final phase, themes, along with their corresponding codes and participant quotations, were exported from ATLAS.ti to Microsoft Word. Moreover, to explore the relationships between the key themes, network analysis in ATLAS.ti was utilized, and the connections among these identified themes were diagrammatically presented. The final report was structured to align with the study objectives, with themes supported by participant quotations to enhance interpretations.

Barriers and enablers of institutional birth data recording practices

This study identified ten barriers to health facility birth data recording, categorized into organizational, behavioral, and technical groups. Organizational barriers included heavy workload, staff turnover, insufficient supervision, lack of accountability, documentation tied to procedures, and resource limitations. Behavioral barriers were characterized by health workers’ neglect and negative attitudes toward data recording. Technical barriers stemmed from complex birth source document designs and mismatches between data collection tools and service scope. Additionally, the study found six enablers for improving data practices: internal data quality reviews, experience sharing, staff collaboration, presence of training, supportive supervision from higher officials, and supervisor feedback (Table 2).

Barriers

Organizational barriers

This study identified six organizational barriers affecting birth-related data recording at health facilities: excessive workloads, staff turnover, limited supervisory focus, lack of accountability, procedure-linked documentation, and resource constraints. Participants noted that high workloads and staff shortages, especially during night shifts, led to incomplete documentation. Accountability varied, with some providers prioritizing certain data items over others. Trained staff turnover disrupted consistent record-keeping practices, and supervisors often focused on aggregated reports instead of individual records. Documentation was typically linked to specific procedures, resulting in omissions of crucial information, such as vaginal tears and postpartum hemorrhage. Additionally, resource constraints, such as the lack of newborn height scales, hindered data collection.

Workload

Workload was identified as one of the major organizational barriers affecting the documentation of birth data in health facilities. Participants identified several workload-related factors contributing to providers’ failure to record birth data. The findings revealed that the workload stems from an imbalance between the number of staff and the complex demands of health facility birth services, which require the completion of various tasks. This disparity creates pressure on healthcare workers, leading to gaps in proper documentation.

“That’s why I mentioned the lack of attention. Why? Firstly, you become fatigued, especially after performing numerous other tasks. That’s how I perceive it.” (Participant No.6).

Participants noted that heavy workloads, particularly during overnight shifts with limited staff, adversely impacted the quality of documentation. The common practice of assigning a single provider to manage multiple births resulted in task overload and less time for accurate data entry. These structural challenges, including staff shortages and shift demands, were reported to contribute to a situation where proper documentation was frequently deprioritized.

“Gaps may arise in the meantime. For instance, we spent the overnight duty alone here. Yesterday, for example, I was the sole staff member on the overnight shift, with five mothers giving birth. I want to tell you that, unless due to exhaustion or negligence….” (Participant No.13).

Insufficient supervision of personal record data

This study found that providers often neglect to properly document birth data in health facilities due to a lack of supervisory emphasis on detailed data recording. While higher officials conduct regular supportive supervision, participants noted that they focus on verifying aggregated data quality at the facility level. Instead of ensuring the accuracy and completeness of detailed data in primary source documents, supervisors prioritize reviewing the overall facility data, such as monthly reports.

“Most of the time, for instance, if 34 mothers gave birth this month, they [the supervisors] check if all 34 mothers were reported. They [the supervisors] review it in that way. So, the supervisors are concerned about whether we have 34 documented cases and 34 reports. It’s about identifying any gaps between what’s documented and what’s reported.” (Participant No.13).

According to our participant, the facility-based data quality assurance approach has resulted in providers neglecting to document data elements related to facility childbirth in primary source documents. For example, despite widespread omissions in recording specific data elements like newborn height, supervisors have made minimal efforts to rectify these gaps.

“Until now, during our mentoring sessions, no one (mentors) has asked about the height measurement’s data recording concerns.” (Participant No.4).

Turnover of trained staff

This study identified the turnover of trained staff as a barrier to data recording practices in health facility childbirth settings. This issue was highlighted as the foremost concern among various barriers. The participants firmly believe that the expertise of trained professionals is crucial for ensuring high-quality data. For instance, one health center head recounted experiences with data quality gaps resulting from inadequately trained staff, attributing such gaps to staff turnover:

“Others [other barriers] are not as problematic; the biggest thing is that the quality could be compromised while the untrained one is replaced by a trained (trained professional); this is the point we have pinpointed in our review; otherwise, the challenges are the ones I have just mentioned.” (Participant No.1).

Lack of accountability for data

The study uncovered that inadequate accountability for data documentation undermines birth data recording at healthcare facilities. It was found that there is a link between the level of accountability and the recording of data, with healthcare workers prioritizing tasks for which they are held accountable.

Participants noted that providers’ sense of accountability levels for recording data varied, primarily influenced by the availability of documentation guidelines. The guidelines’ emphasis varied across different source documents related to birth records. Health workers tended to meticulously document specific data sections when clear guidelines were provided, while areas lacking explicit guidance were often neglected.

Participants elaborated on the importance of clear guidelines for maintaining better documentation. For instance, our data indicated that health workers exhibited a strong sense of accountability when recording data on the partograph, mainly due to the availability of specific protocols for this section. Healthcare professionals made a concerted effort to accurately record data following the partograph guidelines, prioritizing compliance to enhance patient safety. According to participants, adhering to these guidelines is essential for ensuring client safety and safeguarding providers from accountability in the event of adverse outcomes during institutional childbirth. In contrast, other source documents, such as individual folders, did not evoke the same sense of accountability among providers, mainly due to the lack of clear guidelines for these sections. As a result, some data entries, especially those related to women’s folders, were often viewed as lower priorities.

“Per my observation, this [recording data on the women’s folder] one is a second priority because you need to prioritize the work you will be accountable for.” (Participant No. 6).

Some participants also noted that health workers gave less attention to recording data in the woman’s folder than the delivery register and integrated card, reflecting varying levels of appreciation across different birth service documents.

“It’s a common practice for individuals not to fill out the information on that card within the women’s folder. This may raise the question, why? Well, I don’t find it valuable; recording details right here [within the women’s folder] doesn’t hold significance for me…; since individuals view recording as valueless, they tend to neglect it.” (Participant No. 9).

Procedure-linked documentation

According to this study’s findings, some data elements, like the delivery method (such as vacuum extraction or episiotomy), were only documented if the procedure was performed, leaving such data undocumented if the corresponding procedure did not occur.

“For instance, if we do not perform resuscitation, we do not record it; If resuscitation is conducted, we document it and make the appropriate tick mark. The same goes for episiotomy; if we performed it, we recorded it; if not, we left it. If vacuum extraction is done, it (the format) asks to put a tick mark; we mark it accordingly.” (Participant No. 4).

This study’s findings showed a link between incomplete data and the absence of diagnosis of some women’s health conditions after birth in a health facility. The data showed that vaginal lacerations were often not recorded, which could be attributed to the fact that most women did not experience it, according to some participants in our study.

“…, if there is no complete laceration, we don’t document it; we record it only if the tear is present. In our case, lacerations rarely occur.” (Participant No. 13).

“Laceration would not occur; that is the reason why it is not being recorded.” (Participant No. 10).

In contrast, some participants in the study noted that healthcare professionals frequently neglected to record instances of vaginal tears following childbirth, even when the tears occurred. These participants shared their experiences of identifying inconsistencies between the documented data and the true incidence of vaginal tears. They reported discovering this inconsistency by comparing medical records with the accounts provided by the women, as reported by male midwives:

“Lacerations often occur, and after suturing and discharging the woman, she may return with an infected wound. However, when you check the registers, the documentation is blank.” (Participant No. 9).

The same study participant in this study emphasized that cases of postpartum hemorrhage (PPH) were also frequently left unrecorded, resulting in discrepancies between the number of recorded cases and the actual occurrences.

"The other point is that it (the source document) asks if she[the woman] had PPH…. A PPH; now, each week, by a simple estimate, 10, 9, 20, 30, 40 cases could be, but later, you would find a single PPH case on the register, maybe 3 or 4. It [the data field of the PPH] often remains blank. But, in case a woman comes and you ask her, “Where did you give birth?” she would respond, “At this health center.” “How was it? Did you go to the hospital?” she would say, “Yes, after I got bleeding.” … Are you getting my point? There are many such data items."

Resource constraint

Participants reported that the lack of essential tools contributed to missing data on facility births. Incomplete institutional birth data often arises when specific medical procedures are not performed. For instance, the absence of newborn height data is common due to the unavailability of height scales in health facilities.

“We often lacked materials like the height scale, experiencing frequent losses of the apparatus. The absence of the height apparatus led us to the omission of height measurement as well as its documentation.” (Participant No. 11).

Urine test results are another frequently left undocumented data item during health facility births due to the absence of diagnostic procedures. The participants noted that the recording of these results was infrequent, which they attributed to the reserved nature of urine diagnostics, especially for high-risk women with elevated blood pressure who require urine examination.

“On delivery, as long as we don’t realize that her BP doesn’t increase, we often do not send it for laboratory examination.…, it remains unrecorded.” (Participant No. 9).

Time pressure further contributed to incomplete documentation. Participants noted that many women arrived at the facility late in labor, leaving little time for initial assessments, including standard tests such as urine analysis.

“They [the women] have delays at home, and they often arrive at the health center in a panicked state, not at the early stages of labor; consequently, they usually give birth immediately upon arrival so that there is not enough time to perform a urine examination.” (Participant No. 12).

Behavioral barriers

This study identified two behavioral barriers that negatively affect the documentation of birth data in health facilities: negligence in data recording and negative attitudes toward documentation. Participants mentioned instances where some health workers neglect documentation, viewing it as unimportant or intentionally skipping it. Additionally, repeated exposure to incomplete records led to indifference toward missing data, weakening effective documentation practices.

Health workers’ negligence in data recording

This study identified negligence as a key behavioral barrier to accurately recording institutional birth data. Some participants blamed this issue on a perceived lack of importance given to documentation, as health workers often failed to see its value or potential impact. This lack of recognition resulted in little time and attention being devoted to data recording.

“In my view, it[negligence] is because of not giving sufficient time, filling out things[data] with less emphasis; it[negligence] emerges from not knowing why it [the data] is important, what it [the source documents] is being there [at health facility]; and missing the consequences of not documenting and appropriate documentation.” (Participant No. 10).

“You’re questioning me why? What is the most valuable thing here? Isn’t it the data? Hence, it’s all about the belief that the data is not valuable.” (Participant No. 9).

Additionally, respondents described intentional neglect of documentation, attributing it not to a lack of understanding but to a deliberate choice to ignore necessary recordings. This form of deliberate negligence, separate from misunderstanding the importance of data, was seen by participants as a concerning and persistent trend that undermines the accuracy of institutional birth records.

“We are aware of what deserves to be documented. Despite knowing what should be documented, we have often left data undocumented for many years due to carelessness.” (Participant No. 11).

Unfavorable attitudes toward documentation

In this study, some participants stressed the negative impact of health workers’ unfavorable attitudes on data recording during childbirth in health facilities. Over time, staff believed that leaving certain data unrecorded is acceptable, and incomplete data is not surprising. This perception has been shaped by prolonged exposure to incomplete documentation, leading to the customary omission of health facility birth records data. This practice presents a challenge to maintain birth data documentation; for instance, newborn height is frequently left unrecorded due to the absence of measuring equipment, which has become a common practice among health workers in institutional birth settings.

“One might eventually cease documenting height; not recording it may become a habit. After some time, even if you have the height apparatus, you may persist with the same mindset. It’s essentially a matter of your mindset.” (Participant No. 11).

Technical barriers

A major barrier identified in this study was technical challenges related to data collection tools during childbirth services. Two key issues were noted: the complex design of source documents, which required data to be recorded in multiple locations, leading to errors, and a mismatch between documentation fields and the services provided at health centers.

Source document design’s complexity

This study found that the complexity of institutional birth-related data collection formats negatively impacts documentation practices. Our research revealed that the complexity arises from the fact that health facility birth services are an integral part of the continuum of pregnancy care, spanning from pre-pregnancy to postnatal care. The presence of data cells across multiple source documents throughout the continuum of maternal care, requiring healthcare providers to record the same data item for a woman in various documents, poses challenges for data recording. Participants in the study highlighted specific issues with the design complexity of source documents, such as redundant data items related to diagnostic test results (e.g., HIV testing and urine analysis) found across different sources, including the ANC register, delivery register, partograph, women’s folder, and prescription papers. Our participants cited that such redundancy could lead to errors in data recording.

“The result is recorded in the ANC register for mothers who have already had follow-ups during ANC, meaning we can access it when reviewing the records. Otherwise, we usually don’t document the urine results.” (Participant No.11).

Our analysis revealed two underlying reasons for data errors linked to the complexities of source document design: deliberate data omission to avoid re-recording and errors during data transfer between source documents. In some instances, healthcare providers may omit data during childbirth, believing that previously recorded data from other sources, such as ANC follow-up registers, is sufficient.

“In the case of a mother who has already undergone follow-up [ANC], the result [urine examination result] is documented on the ANC register. I mean, it can be available for us when reviewing it[the urine examination result recorded during the ANC follow-up]. Otherwise, we usually don’t document it[urine results].” (Participant No.11)

The second type of error occurred when providers attempt to transfer data from earlier pregnancy care during ANC to the source documents for birth. One of our participants, a HIT personnel, provided an example of an error in HIV data recording that could occur during this transfer process.

“So, given she[a mother] had been tested during ANC, she[a mother] might not have undergone the test[HIV test] at birth; however, they[health care providers] used to say that they brought that one[the data at ANC follow-up]; as we put the tick mark at the ANC, we bring the previous one[the data at ANC follow-up] and put it in the delivery register, they[health care providers] say. Hence, the fallacy[error] resulted due to this [transferring HIV data from ANC to birth record].” (Participant No.6).

Tool-service mismatch

This study revealed that some data items in health facility births were not documented due to irrelevant data fields included on source documents at health center levels. Participants provided examples of services, such as Cesarean section (CS), listed on source documents even though providers at health centers are not obligated to perform them. These services are expected to be carried out at the hospital level, and consequently, the corresponding data remains unrecorded.

“The one that frequently remained unrecorded is, firstly, the CS; now, we are not mandated to perform it [Cesarian section] …. Other than that [CS], we do not have any remaining unrecorded items. To us, we are being documented all that it[the protocol] has obliged us to do. However, we are not concerned with recording services that pertain to the hospital level.” (Participant No. 4).

Enablers

All enablers identified in this study to improve the recording of birth data in health facilities were organizational. Our analysis revealed six key categories of these enablers. These include the Presence of Internal Data Quality Review, Experience Exchange, Collaboration Among Staff, Presence of Training, Supportive Supervision from higher officials, and Feedback from Supervisors.

Existence of internal data quality review

Participants in this study emphasized the importance of regular internal data quality reviews for maintaining documentation of health facility birth data. These reviews take two main forms: a case team-level discussion among colleagues and a routine review conducted by the data quality review team established at the health center level.

As participants stated, the review at the case team level with colleagues helps identify routine gaps and facilitates in-depth discussions to pinpoint remedial actions for addressing these gaps.

“By the way, we covered various points as we engaged in discussions through a case team. Our better data quality results from regular peer critiques during work reviews, aided by meeting minutes. We thoroughly examine the data, ensuring any discrepancies in the register are promptly addressed and corrected.” (Participant No.10).

Some participants also noted that the presence of a data quality assurance committee at the health facility level, including the MCH coordinator as one of its members, is crucial in ensuring the documentation of health facility birth data.

“… the committee established for the data quality assurance is also here composed of members from OPD, MCH, and others; I mean the PMT [performance monitoring team]…. They [the committee] visit us regularly every month or every two weeks to inform us about our gaps.” (Participant No.11).

Experience exchange

According to the report of this study’s participants, sharing best practices is valuable for enhancing the recording of health facility birth data. Experience sharing may involve exchanging successful techniques for assuring data recording with other healthcare facilities.

“There is something like sharing of experiences. There is the so-called experience sharing; from the other health centers, there is experience sharing. There is something like that; it would be better to share experiences with different health centers.” (Participant No.12).

Other participants recognized the importance of transferring knowledge from formally trained colleagues to untrained providers.

“For instance, consider that someone receives training and returns to address issues at the focal point. If that professional [the trained professional] shares with us[what he/she has trained] to correct our gaps, we can correct them [the gaps in documentation]. Otherwise, if the trained professional doesn’t impart this knowledge to the untrained, the gaps persist and will remain not fixed” (Participant No.11).

Collaboration among the staff

Per our study, some respondents highlighted the significance of collaboration among staff in health facility birth services to ensure data recording. They emphasized that assistance among providers plays a crucial role in managing multiple activities that might shift the focus away from documentation toward service delivery. In such cases, staff can support each other by dividing responsibilities, enabling one to concentrate on service delivery while the other focuses on data recording.

“It is up to me; I have to document what I have worked out in a timely manner. Otherwise, I may need to seek assistance from my colleagues. For instance, while I may be busy having a lot of activities at hand, I suggest making another person handle the timely documentation.” (Participant No.11).

Training

According to this study’s findings, training for providers in health facility birth services is an effective way to improve the documentation of institutional birth data. Our data shows that training can enhance personnel’s awareness of the importance of better documentation.

“To further improve data quality, it is advisable to offer orientation sessions for midwives and raise awareness among providers by bringing them together for regular reviews.” (Participant No.1).

In addition, training can positively influence providers’ attitudes toward data documentation by reversing negative perceptions.

“The factors contributing to poor data quality are linked to differences in perception and the extent of training. Those who have received training may diligently perform their tasks, while those who have not received training might not. A person who hasn’t undergone training may not work, because they believe they are not qualified due to the lack of training.” (Participant No.6).

Supportive supervision from the higher officials

This study identified supportive supervision from higher authorities as an enabler in enhancing health facility birth data recording. Respondents reported that efforts to improve data recording through supportive supervision involved different governmental administrative institutions, including support from regional health bureaus and district health offices.

“Well, regarding facilitators, regular supervision has been a significant help for us. It has motivated us to review data quality monthly and allows us to document the summary sheet appropriately… we have various entities involved; the regional health bureau, the district office, and others who visit us.” (Participant No.13).

In addition to administrative oversight, respondents mentioned the University of Gondar’s support for enhancing data recording.

“We did encounter an issue [data quality gap] a year ago, but since the University of Gondar took over, there is no gap; we have achieved consistency [data consistency]; all the data are being recorded consistently.” (Participant No.4).

Feedback from supervisors

Our study participants highlighted the positive impact of receiving feedback from supervisors and mentors on data recording in healthcare facilities. The feedback is a practical way to convert provider knowledge into action, resulting in improved healthcare facility birth data recording.

“Firstly, if someone is informed about a gap in their[providers] work, they are likely to fix it [the gap]. If they are made aware of the gap, it[feedback] can significantly assist us in improving our processes.” (Participant No.10).

Discussion

This qualitative study examined the barriers and enablers related to data recording practices for institutional births in health centers in northwestern Ethiopia. Among the identified barriers were behavioral factors, including healthcare providers’ neglect of documentation and unfavorable attitudes toward data recording. The findings indicated that these behavioral barriers arise from organizational issues, primarily driven by the workloads during childbirth and a supervisory focus on aggregate data quality assurance that overlooks the quality of individual records. Furthermore, the study found that technical complexities related to data formats contribute to poor documentation, resulting in missing data and transfer errors. Conversely, the study identified enablers that enhance data recording, such as regular internal data quality reviews; and training and supervision provided by higher-level officials.

This study identifies behavioral barriers that affect data recording during institutional births, with negligence emerging as a key issue. Participants reported that such negligence often stemmed from organizational barriers, particularly staff shortages and workload pressures. These challenges were especially pronounced during night shifts, where a single provider was frequently responsible for managing multiple births, making timely and accurate documentation difficult. These findings align with previous research conducted in LMICs, which identified health workforce shortages as an organizational barrier to data quality in RHIS [34–36]. Several studies further report that heavy workloads hinder health workers from dedicating sufficient time to proper data recording [37–40], while others found that adequate staffing was associated with improved RHIS data quality [41]. The convergence of findings across studies reinforces the impact of workforce-related constraints on data quality. This suggests that the Ethiopian government should prioritize investments in training and recruiting healthcare personnel to address staffing challenges related to health facility births, especially in rural facilities, and enhance data recording and quality.

The study also highlights that a lack of accountability for data documentation hampers the recording of birth data in healthcare facilities. Participants noted that this contributed to negligence in data recording, particularly within women’s personal records. Our analysis showed that providers’ sense of accountability varied across different data formats: healthcare providers were more consistent in updating the delivery register than in recording information in integrated cards or women’s folders. For example, healthcare providers frequently omitted the recording of newborn height data from the integrated cards, and several participants described a limited sense of responsibility for completing entries in women’s folders. These findings align with earlier studies reporting lower completeness for critical data elements in personal health records [22], which has important implications for the continuity of maternal care [22, 28].

To our knowledge, specific key performance indicators (KPIs) that monitor the completeness and quality of individual-level data, such as documentation within personal records, are not routinely applied at health facilities or RHIS levels in Ethiopia. While aggregate data quality may be reviewed through HMIS outputs (i.e., routine monthly or quarterly reports), accountability mechanisms for detailed record-keeping at the point of care remain limited. We recommend that future RHIS programs strengthen accountability by embedding such KPIs into supervision tools, performance appraisals, and data quality reviews. Emphasizing accurate and complete documentation across all data formats, not only facility registers, will be essential for ensuring that RHIS data is trusted, actionable, and supportive of evidence-based maternal and newborn care [42–46].

Participants also reported that some institutional birth data elements were often left unrecorded because healthcare providers deemed them irrelevant, particularly when procedures were not performed (e.g., “newborn height,” “episiotomy,” and “blood pressure”), services unavailable at the health center (e.g., “Cesarean section”), or medical conditions were not diagnosed (e.g., “vaginal tear” and “postpartum hemorrhage”). While omitting data may be justifiable in some cases, such as when Cesarean sections are not performed at a facility or newborn height is not measured due to a lack of equipment, the concern arises when clinically significant events are left undocumented despite occurring. For instance, the participants in this study offered conflicting accounts about “vaginal tear” and “postpartum hemorrhage” data. Some claimed these conditions were rare, while others admitted that even when they occurred, they were often left unrecorded. This undermines the core purpose of the RHIS, which is to enable data-informed service delivery [8, 9]. The omission of such data can lead to delayed treatments and preventable complications, including sepsis or maternal death following PPH. For example, systematic reviews from Ethiopia indicate that postpartum hemorrhage and puerperal sepsis occur in approximately 8.2% and 14.8% of facility deliveries, respectively; both of which are recognized as leading causes of maternal mortality in the country [47, 48]. Nevertheless, participants in our study noted that these complications are infrequently documented, suggesting a potential issue of underreporting within the RHIS [49]. Strengthening RHIS guidelines and reinforcing accurate documentation through targeted training are essential steps to ensure that critical maternal and newborn data are consistently captured and used to improve care quality [42, 50, 51].

Technical barriers, particularly those related to the design and fragmentation of source documents, were also found to hinder accurate birth data recording in health facilities. These findings echo prior research that has linked poor form design and use of multiple, uncoordinated formats with low data quality in RHIS [37, 41, 52, 53]. In our study, participants reported challenges with recording diagnostic test results, such as HIV and urine tests, because these data were required in both ANC and delivery registers. This led to omissions and transfer errors. These challenges disrupt data continuity across the maternal care continuum, ultimately weakening the integrity of health information systems. To address this, RHIS stakeholders in Ethiopia should prioritize redesigning data tools to reduce redundancy, improve integration across ANC and delivery records, and facilitate more efficient documentation. Streamlining formats would ease the documentation burden on providers and enhance the accuracy of pregnancy-related records [54].

Our findings recommend that RHIS planners and policymakers reevaluate current data quality approaches by focusing more on the realities faced by health workers at the practice level. While existing interventions in Ethiopia have mainly concentrated on organizational strategies, such as comprehensive training programs, supervision, and structural reforms [18], our results indicate that documentation practices during institutional births are influenced by indicator-specific constraints that these structural efforts may not fully address. This is especially important because interventions aimed at improving one maternal health indicator may not be effective for another. For instance, strategies that work for antenatal care data might be less successful for documenting institutional births, which are often impacted by night-shift workloads and dependence on prior pregnancy records.

Notably, the thematic structure of our findings reveals an apparent asymmetry: while barriers were identified across organizational, behavioral, and technical domains, all enabling factors were found only within the organizational domain. Participants did not report any behavioral or technical enablers. This imbalance suggests that health system responses may focus too heavily on structural inputs, while neglecting efforts to change negative behaviors or technical constraints into enabling conditions. Consequently, key individual- and practice-level drivers of change, such as motivation, documentation-friendly formats, and psychological empowerment, may be underutilized in strengthening RHIS. Recognizing and addressing this gap not only adds a unique dimension to our findings but also highlights missed opportunities for more comprehensive, theory-informed interventions.

Findings from theories proven effective in changing healthcare practices support the need to address individual-level drivers of data quality, including attitudes, social expectations, perceived control, self-efficacy, and beliefs about outcomes [55, 56]. Our findings exemplify this: some health workers viewed documentation, such as recording newborn height, as unnecessary; others were discouraged by weak supervisory norms that failed to check detailed records; and many felt unable to complete documentation during night shifts due to fatigue or workload. These insights underscore the need to move beyond generalized, system-wide approaches and invest in interventions that directly engage individual health professionals. Strengthening health workers’ motivation, self-esteem, and accountability through strategies such as financial or non-financial incentives, personalized feedback, and supportive supervision focused on personal records may offer more practical and meaningful improvements in RHIS data quality [57–61].

Strengths and limitations of the study

This study employed qualitative methods to examine the barriers and enablers related to recording birth data in healthcare facilities. The study’s rigor was ensured through various methodological approaches, such as conducting IDIs with health workers who have direct experience with birth data management in health facilities, prolonged engagement by the principal investigator, and data triangulation between IDI transcripts and field notes [33, 62, 63]. Unlike previous literature, which broadly examined RHIS data quality at the system level, this study specifically focused on the context of institutional childbirth. This targeted approach allows the findings to be uniquely tailored to address the identified barriers, providing actionable insights for enhancing data recording in health facility birth settings.

Despite its strengths, certain limitations must be acknowledged. While the targeted participant selection effectively elicits information-rich lived experiences, it may limit the transferability of the findings beyond institutional births [64, 65]. Future research should include diverse perspectives from healthcare personnel involved in other aspects of maternal and child health services to achieve broader transferability. Additionally, this study focused solely on health centers, excluding other contexts such as hospitals and public health administrative institutions. Expanding future research to encompass health workers’ perspectives from these settings would offer a more comprehensive understanding of the barriers and enablers in data recording across various healthcare system levels.

Conclusions

This study offers a contextually grounded perspective on why essential birth data are often not recorded in health facilities in northwestern Ethiopia. Our findings show that the barriers to poor documentation practices are mainly behavioral, yet closely tied to systemic issues such as heavy workloads, weak supervisory oversight of personal records, and limited accountability structures. These organizational dynamics influence how health workers view the importance of documentation, their confidence in completing it, and whether they believe the data will be valued or used. Improving the quality of institutional birth data, therefore, requires more than structural reforms; it calls for attention to the lived realities of frontline staff. Interventions that strengthen motivation, build self-esteem when documenting under pressure, and foster positive attitudes toward detailed record-keeping are implied by this study. Such strategies are crucial for improving care for mothers and newborns. Future research should explore how individual and system-level factors interact across different levels of the health system.
