High-Deductible Health Plan Enrollment and Buprenorphine Dispensing
Authors: Kao-Ping Chua, Amy S. Bohnert, Rena M. Conti, Pooja Lagisetty, Ushapoorna Nuliyalu, Thuy D. Nguyen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionAmong privately insured adults using buprenorphine, is switching to a high-deductible health plan (HDHP) associated with changes in buprenorphine dispensing or health care visits for opioid use disorder (OUD)?FindingsIn this repeated cross-sectional study of 14 801 patient claims from a national commercial claims database, switching to an HDHP was associated with decreased buprenorphine dispensing but not with changes in OUD-related health care visits. Among patients experiencing above-median deductible increases, switching to an HDHP was associated with decreased buprenorphine dispensing and OUD-related outpatient visits.MeaningResults of this study suggest that patients who switch to an HDHP may experience heightened barriers to buprenorphine use.

Introduction

Buprenorphine is a medication that can reduce opioid-related mortality by 50% among patients with opioid use disorder (OUD).1 Despite this, national data indicate that only 1 in 5 US residents with OUD receive treatment with buprenorphine or any other medication for OUD.2 Moreover, even among patients who receive treatment with buprenorphine, discontinuation is common,3 raising the risk of opioid overdose.4 Addressing underuse of buprenorphine is a crucial clinical and public health goal, as the number of US opioid overdose deaths remains substantial despite recent decreases.5

In 2023, 42% of privately insured US residents younger than 65 years were enrolled in a high-deductible health plan (HDHP) – defined in 2023 as plans with a minimum deductible of $1500 for individual coverage and $3000 for family coverage.6 Several studies suggest that switching to an HDHP is associated with decreased use of high-value services among patients with chronic diseases, as well as with worsened health outcomes in some patients.7,8,9 These findings raise the possibility that switching from a non-HDHP to an HDHP may be associated with decreased buprenorphine dispensing and increases in opioid-related adverse events among privately insured patients using buprenorphine.

To date, this possibility has been incompletely evaluated. In a quasi-experimental study10 of commercial claims from 2007 to 2017, an employer’s offer of an HDHP was not associated with changes in buprenorphine dispensing or the number of OUD-related outpatient visits among patients initiating buprenorphine therapy. However, no study of which we are aware has evaluated the association between switching to an HDHP and these outcomes. Additionally, the association between switching to an HDHP and OUD-related emergency department (ED) visits and hospitalizations, a potential indicator of opioid-related adverse events, remains unclear. Finally, it is unknown whether patients who switch to an HDHP and experience large increases in deductibles have greater changes in buprenorphine dispensing or OUD-related health care visits compared with those who switch to an HDHP and experience small increases in deductibles. One might expect the changes to be greater among patients experiencing a large deductible increase (eg, patients switching from a plan with no deductible to an HDHP) compared with those experiencing a small deductible increase (eg, patients switching from a plan with a moderate deductible to an HDHP), as the former would experience greater exposure to the price of health care after the switch.

This study evaluated whether switching to an HDHP is associated with changes in buprenorphine dispensing and OUD-related outpatient visits, ED visits, and hospitalizations among privately insured adults with pre-existing buprenorphine use. Analyses were conducted both overall and among patients experiencing larger vs smaller increases in deductibles.

Methods

Data Source

We analyzed the 2010 to 2023 Optum Labs Data Warehouse, which reports fully adjudicated claims from more than 300 million commercial and Medicare Advantage enrollees. Only commercially insured patients were included in this analysis. Data elements include a unique identifier for patients, employers, and plans, as well as information on insurance benefit design, including the in-network plan deductible, the amount of co-insurance required for patients, and the annual out-of-pocket maximum. Because data were deidentified, the Institutional Review Board of the University of Michigan Medical School exempted this study from review; informed consent was not required. This manuscript follows the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline for cross-sectional studies.11

Sample

We identified all 2-year spans of continuous enrollment that began on January 1 of a baseline year and extended through December 31 of the subsequent year (follow-up year). Spans were eligible if the baseline year occurred from 2010 through 2022. We excluded spans if patients lacked pharmacy coverage in either the baseline or follow-up year, were less than 18 years old in the baseline year, changed employers between the baseline and follow-up year, switched from an individual to family plan or vice versa, or had multiple employers or plans during either the baseline or follow-up year. Because analyses focused on patients with pre-existing buprenorphine use, we limited analyses to spans in which patients had at least 1 dispensed buprenorphine prescription during the baseline year. We further excluded spans in which patients remained in an HDHP in both years, leaving spans in which patients either stayed in a non-HDHP or switched from a non-HDHP to an HDHP. Finally, we limited to each patient’s earliest span, thus ensuring the unit of analysis was the patient. Patients were not excluded if they had buprenorphine dispensing prior to the baseline year.

HDHPs were identified based on whether the plan’s in-network general deductible was equal to or greater than the statutorily defined minimum for HDHPs (eMethods 1 in Supplement 1). We did not require the presence of a health savings account or health retirement account. The general plan deductible was the amount patients or families must pay out-of-pocket before coverage occurs.

Study Design

We conducted a difference-in-differences analysis. The treatment group included patients who switched from a non-HDHP to an HDHP on January 1 of the follow-up year. The control group included patients who remained in a non-HDHP. Analyses compared within-patient changes in outcomes between the baseline and follow-up years in the treatment group to the corresponding change in the control group.

Outcomes

The first primary outcome was the annual number of days covered by an active buprenorphine prescription. A buprenorphine prescription was considered active from the date of dispensing through the date of dispensing plus days supplied minus 1. If multiple buprenorphine prescriptions were active on a given date, the date only counted once toward the outcome. In the baseline year, only buprenorphine prescriptions dispensed during the year contributed to the count of days with active prescriptions. In the follow-up year, this count included buprenorphine prescriptions dispensed during the year, as well as prescriptions that were dispensed during the baseline year and extended through the follow-up year. We did not include prescriptions for buprenorphine formulations approved to treat pain as opposed to OUD.

The second primary outcome was the annual number of OUD-related outpatient visits, defined as the number of outpatient visits with an International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems 10th Revision diagnosis code for OUD in any position. This outcome was included to explore whether any findings for switching to an HDHP and changes in buprenorphine dispensing may have been associated with changes in the number of outpatient visits in which a patient with OUD might be prescribed buprenorphine. The third primary outcome was the annual number of OUD-related ED visits or hospitalizations, an indicator of opioid-related adverse events. The number of OUD-related ED visits and hospitalizations was expressed per 100 patients owing to the rareness of these events. Measurement of OUD-related use began on January 1 of each year.

Finally, to contextualize results, we calculated mean out-of-pocket spending (sum of deductibles and copays) per dispensed buprenorphine prescription, per OUD-related outpatient visit, and per OUD-related ED visit and hospitalization. We also calculated total annual out-of-pocket spending across all medical and pharmacy claims, a measure of overall out-of-pocket burden. eMethods 2 in Supplement 1 reports additional details on outcome measurement.

Statistical Analysis

We used descriptive statistics to characterize the sample and to assess changes in out-of-pocket spending among the treatment and control groups. To estimate differential changes in primary outcomes, we fitted linear difference-in-differences models (eMethods 3 in Supplement 1). Models included an indicator for treatment group status, the interaction between this indicator and an indicator for follow-up year (coefficient of interest), year fixed effects, and patient fixed effects. Patient fixed effects were included to mitigate potential bias from time-invariant patient-level confounders that may be associated both with the decision to switch to an HDHP and with study outcomes. We used robust standard errors clustered at the patient level. Analyses used 2-sided hypothesis tests with α = .05 and were conducted using Stata version 17.1/MP (StataCorp).

In subgroup analyses, we repeated analyses when limiting the treatment group to patients experiencing a deductible increase exceeding $1250 vs a deductible increase of $1250 or less. The $1250 threshold demarcated the 50th percentile of the deductible increase in the treatment group. The control group was the same as in the main analysis.

The main analysis included patients with buprenorphine dispensing in the baseline year, regardless of the timing of dispensing. If the treatment group were more or less likely than the control group to discontinue buprenorphine before the end of the baseline year, this could lead to a misleading conclusion that there was a true association between switching to an HDHP and the study outcomes when in fact the association was based on another factor. To explore this possibility, we conducted a sensitivity analysis in which the sample was limited to patients with at least 1 buprenorphine prescription dispensed in the last quarter of the baseline year. In a second sensitivity analysis, we limited to patients with a baseline year from 2010 to 2018, thus ensuring the follow-up year occurred before the COVID-19 pandemic.

Results

Sample Characteristics

After sample exclusions (Figure), the sample included 14 801 patients (5382 [36.4%] female; 9419 [63.6%] male; mean [SD] age, 38.4 [12.2] years), including 1587 patients in the treatment group (mean [SD] age, 38.3 [12.3] years) and 13 214 patients in the control group (mean [SD] age, 38.8 [11.6] years). Table 1 reports sample characteristics, including rates of missing data for demographic variables. In both groups, most patients were male, resided in the South, and resided in urban areas. In the treatment group, the mean (SD) in-network general deductible increased from $1462 ($695) to $3163 ($1486) between the baseline and follow-up years compared with $833 ($661) to $884 ($677) in the control group. The first dispensed buprenorphine prescription in the baseline year was more likely to occur in January for the treatment group (946 [59.6%]) compared with the control group (5735 [43.4%]) (eFigure in Supplement 1).

Out-of-Pocket Spending

For each measure assessed, out-of-pocket spending increased in the treatment group and decreased in the control group between the baseline and follow-up years (Table 2). For example, mean (SD) out-of-pocket spending per dispensed buprenorphine prescription increased from $42.0 ($37.2) to $51.8 ($77.3) in the treatment group but decreased from $31.0 ($24.5) to $22.5 ($24.1) in the control group. Mean (SD) out-of-pocket spending per OUD-related outpatient visit increased from $28.6 ($85.5) to $34.1 ($67.3) in the treatment group but decreased from $24.1 ($57.3) to $21.4 ($68.2) in the control group. Mean (SD) annual out-of-pocket spending across all medical and pharmacy claims increased from $2721.0 ($2420.4) to $3273.2 ($3489.2) in the treatment group but decreased from $2531.7 ($3464.4) to $2322.2 ($3191.9) in the control group. Among patients in the treatment group, the magnitudes of the increases in out-of-pocket spending were greater when deductible increases exceeded $1250 compared with when deductible increases were $1250 or less.

Main Results

Table 3 reports unadjusted values of the primary outcomes in the baseline and follow-up year among the treatment and control groups, as well as adjusted difference-in-differences estimates. In the treatment group, the mean (SD) annual number of days with active buprenorphine prescriptions decreased from 214.3 (121.7) to 193.2 (143,5) days, whereas it increased from 182.4 (126.4) days to 190.3 (146.3) days in the control group. Switching to an HDHP was associated with a differential decrease in this outcome (−29.0 days, 95% CI, −35.0 to −22.9 days). The mean (SD) annual number of OUD-related outpatient visits decreased from 5.5 (9.2) to 5.1 (8.9) in the treatment group but increased from 5.3 (9.9) to 5.5 (11.9) in the control group. Switching to an HDHP was not associated with a differential change in this outcome (−0.5 visits, 95% CI, −1.0 to 0.05 visits). The mean (SD) annual number of OUD-related ED visits and hospitalizations per 100 patients decreased from 21.9 (90.7) to 15.8 (68.9) in the treatment group and from 29.5 (87.3) to 19.4 (77.9) in the control group. Switching to an HDHP was not associated with a differential change in this outcome (4.0 events per 100 patients; 95% CI, −0.5 to 8.4 events per 100 patients).

Subgroup Analyses

There were 771 treatment group patients with deductible increases exceeding $1250; 691 (89.6%) were in family plans. When limiting the treatment group to these patients, the magnitude of the differential decrease in the annual number of days with active buprenorphine prescriptions increased (−35.2 days; 95% CI, −43.7 to −26.7). Moreover, switching to an HDHP was associated with a differential decrease in the number of OUD-related outpatient visits (−1.0 visits; 95% CI, −1.8 to −0.3 visits) unlike in the overall analysis. Switching to an HDHP was not associated with a differential change in the annual number of OUD-related ED visits and hospitalizations per 100 patients (5.1 events per 100 patients; 95% CI, −1.1 to 11.4 events per 100 patients).

There were 816 treatment group patients with deductible increases of $1250 or less; 528 were in family plans (64.7%). When limiting the treatment group to these patients, the difference-in-differences estimate for the annual number of days with active buprenorphine prescriptions remained negative (−23.0 days; 95% CI, −31.3 to −14.8). Difference-in-differences estimates for the 2 other primary outcomes were not significant.

Sensitivity Analyses

Among the 1587 patients receiving treatment, 1234 had buprenorphine dispensing in the last quarter of the baseline year (77.8%), and among 13 214 control patients, 10 038 had buprenorphine dispensing in the last quarter of the baseline year (76.0%). When limiting analyses to these patients, findings were similar to the main analysis. The findings were also similar when limiting to prepandemic data (eTables 1-2 in Supplement 1).

Discussion

In this difference-in-differences analysis of a national commercial claims database, switching to an HDHP was associated with decreased buprenorphine dispensing but not with changes in the number of OUD-related outpatient visits, ED visits, or hospitalizations among privately insured adults with pre-existing buprenorphine use. Among patients experiencing above-median deductible increases, switching to an HDHP was associated with a differential decrease in both buprenorphine dispensing and the number of OUD-related outpatient visits.

Many studies have evaluated the association between switching to an HDHP and the use of medications for chronic diseases, with some showing reductions in use and others showing no change.8,12,13,14,15,16,17 The current study adds to this literature with the finding of an association between switching to an HDHP and decreased buprenorphine dispensing. Findings contrast with a prior analysis of commercial claims data, which showed that an employer offer of an HDHP was not associated with changes in buprenorphine dispensing.10 A potential explanation is that the current analysis examined patients who made the switch to an HDHP, not those who were newly offered the option to enroll in an HDHP.

Prior studies have reported seemingly contradictory findings regarding the degree to which buprenorphine dispensing changes as a function of cost-sharing, defined as the amount insurers require patients to pay to fill prescriptions. On the one hand, several groups of authors used commercial claims databases to demonstrate that the amount paid out-of-pocket for buprenorphine prescriptions – an indicator of cost-sharing – is negatively associated with retention in buprenorphine therapy.18,19 On the other hand, other authors used a national pharmacy transaction database to show that the association between cost-sharing and patients’ decisions on whether to fill a buprenorphine prescription was either null or small at most.20,21

These contradictory findings might be reconciled under 2 conditions: (1) high out-of-pocket spending for buprenorphine is an indicator that patients are enrolled in a nongenerous plan that requires high cost-sharing not just for buprenorphine but also for other OUD care, including the outpatient visits during which buprenorphine is prescribed; and (2) use of these outpatient visits is sensitive to cost-sharing. In support of the first condition, the current study found that out-of-pocket spending for buprenorphine and OUD-related outpatient visits both increased among patients who switched to an HDHP, particularly among those experiencing above-median deductible increases. In support of the second condition, the current study found that switching to an HDHP was associated with a differential decrease in both buprenorphine dispensing and in the number of OUD-related outpatient visits among patients experiencing above-median deductible increases. Thus, the finding in prior studies that high out-of-pocket spending for buprenorphine is associated with reduced treatment retention may reflect sensitivity of OUD-related outpatient visits to cost-sharing, as opposed to sensitivity of buprenorphine dispensing to cost-sharing.18,19

In this study, switching to an HDHP was not associated with a differential change in the annual number of OUD-related ED visits and hospitalizations. However, the point estimate (4.0 events per 100 patients) was positive and the lower bound of the 95% CI was −0.5. This finding suggests that a better-powered study may have detected a significant increase.

This study has policy implications. Findings suggest that patients using buprenorphine who switch to an HDHP are at greater risk of experiencing barriers to OUD treatment compared with those who do not switch. To mitigate this risk, employers and private insurers might consider providing first-dollar coverage for buprenorphine and other OUD-related services, meaning that coverage would be provided even if the deductible has not yet been met. This approach has precedent, as the US Internal Revenue Service allows first-dollar coverage of preventive services for chronic conditions in HDHPs.22

Limitations

This study has limitations. First, there were differences in the observed characteristics of patients who did and who did not switch to HDHPs in this study, including differences in the baseline intensity of buprenorphine use and level of deductibles. This raises the possibility that the 2 groups differed on unobserved factors associated with the study’s outcomes. Bias could occur if these differences change to a greater or lesser degree among 1 group of patients compared with the other. For example, if the severity of OUD improved in the treatment group but not the control group, this could result in a differential decrease in buprenorphine dispensing that is unrelated to the switch to an HDHP. Notably, however, the sign of the difference-in-differences estimate for the number of OUD-related ED visits and hospitalizations was positive, which does not suggest that OUD severity improved differentially in the treatment group.

Second, the database lacked information on prescription indication. Thus, analyses could not be restricted to patients using buprenorphine for OUD as opposed to pain. Third, because each patient only contributed data from 2 time points, the parallel trends assumption of difference-in-differences analysis could not be evaluated. Fourth, claims data do not capture cash-pay buprenorphine prescriptions, which may have been more common in the follow-up year among patients in the treatment group. Fifth, findings are limited to patients with existing buprenorphine use. Additional research is needed to assess whether switching to an HDHP is associated with initiation of buprenorphine therapy. Sixth, owing to small sample sizes, analyses could not be limited to patients whose employer forced them to switch into an HDHP. Seventh, small sample sizes also precluded the ability to analyze whether changes differed among patients enrolled in individual vs family plans.

Conclusions

In this study, switching to an HDHP was associated with decreased buprenorphine dispensing among privately insured adults with pre-existing buprenorphine use, as well as with decreased OUD-related outpatient visits among patients experiencing above-median deductible increases. Findings suggest that switching to an HDHP may be associated with heightened barriers to OUD treatment.
