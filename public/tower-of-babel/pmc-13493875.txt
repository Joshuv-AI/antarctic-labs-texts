A data-light composite climate resilience index reveals superior drought resilience of millets in rainfed agroecosystems
Authors: A K Nayak, B S Satpathy, S Pradhan, N M Jeepsa, S Barik, Mithlesh Kumar, D K Panda, M Jothimani, A Sarangi, S K Mishra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRainfed agriculture is highly vulnerable to increasing rainfall variability and drought under climate change, necessitating robust and transferable approaches for assessing crop resilience. The IPCC climate risk and resilience framework conceptualizes resilience as the integrated interaction of exposure, sensitivity, and adaptive capacity. Accordingly, the Composite Crop Resilience Index (CCRI) was developed by integrating exposure resilience under the exposure component, rainfall–yield elasticity-based sensitivity resilience under the sensitivity component, and detrended Yield Stability Index (YSI) under the adaptive capacity component using long-term district-level rainfall and crop yield datasets (1998–2023). Rice exhibited the highest climatic sensitivity, with drought-year yield reductions frequently exceeding 40–60% and high rainfall–yield elasticity across districts, followed by maize. In contrast, millet crops consistently recorded lower drought-induced yield losses, lower rainfall sensitivity, higher yield stability, and consequently higher CCRI values across districts. Sensitivity analysis demonstrated stable resilience rankings under alternative weighting scenarios, confirming the robustness of the CCRI framework. Overall, millet-based systems exhibited substantially greater climatic resilience than rice-based systems under drought-prone rainfed conditions, highlighting their potential for climate adaptation and resilient crop diversification in vulnerable agroecosystems.Keywords: Rainfed agriculture, Composite resilience index, Millets, Drought resilience, Climate adaptationSubject terms: Climate sciences, Environmental sciences, Plant sciences

Introduction

Rainfed farming constitutes 80% of the world’s cropland and produces more than 60% of the world’s cereal grains, generating livelihoods in rural areas while producing food for cities1. Despite its importance, rainfed farming systems remain highly vulnerable to climate variability and drought, particularly in semi-arid and sub-humid tropical regions where crop productivity is strongly dependent on seasonal rainfall. Recent assessments highlight increasing rainfall variability, more frequent droughts, and intensifying hydro-climatic extremes under climate change, posing growing risks to the stability and sustainability of rainfed food systems2–5. Rainfed agriculture in India accounts for nearly 60% of the agricultural area and contributes around 44% of the country’s food requirement and supports nearly 40% of the human population6. Eastern India exemplifies these challenges, where agricultural productivity is closely tied to the southwest monsoon and recurrent droughts continue to undermine crop yields, farmer incomes, and regional food security. Odisha, in particular, represents a typical rainfed agroecosystem characterized by pronounced spatial and temporal rainfall variability, limited irrigation coverage, and persistent exposure to drought risk7.

Historically, cereal-based production systems dominated by rice and, to a lesser extent, maize have been promoted across rainfed regions to meet food security objectives. However, these crops exhibit strong sensitivity to rainfall anomalies and soil moisture stress under rainfed conditions, where adaptive buffering through irrigation is limited or absent8. Numerous empirical studies shows that rice and maize yields display high interannual variability and substantial declines during drought years, reflecting strong rainfall–yield dependence and limited capacity to absorb climatic shocks9. Under projected climate change, the vulnerability of such water-intensive cereal systems is expected to intensify, raising concerns about their long-term sustainability in rainfed landscapes3.

In contrast, millets—often referred to as nutri-cereals—comprise a diverse group of small-grained crops inherently adapted to water-limited and climatically harsh environments10,11. Crops such as sorghum, pearl millet, finger millet, and small millets possess several drought-adaptive traits, including efficient water-use strategies, extensive root systems, heat tolerance, and, in some genotypes, relatively shorter crop duration or flexible phenological responses that facilitate drought escape under moisture-stressed conditions12,13. These physiological and phenological traits enable millets to sustain productivity under low and erratic rainfall, making them particularly suited to rainfed agroecosystems where climatic uncertainty is increasing14.

Within the context of climate change, resilience in agricultural systems is commonly defined as the capacity to anticipate, absorb, adapt to, and recover from climatic stresses while maintaining essential functions and productivity3,14. While a growing body of literature recognizes the superior drought tolerance and stability of millets relative to conventional cereals, most existing studies assess resilience using isolated indicators such as yield trends, drought-year yield losses, or rainfall sensitivity alone15. Such approaches provide valuable insights but remain limited in their ability to capture the multi-dimensional nature of resilience, which encompasses stability, sensitivity, and adaptive capacity simultaneously. Despite renewed policy interest in millets—through initiatives such as the International Year of Millets and national food security programmes—empirical, multi-indicator assessments of crop resilience at regional and sub-regional scales remain scarce. In particular, data-light and transferable frameworks that integrate multiple resilience dimensions using routinely available rainfall and yield data are limited, constraining their application in climate adaptation planning across diverse rainfed regions.

Against this backdrop, the present study develops and applies a data-light, multi-indicator framework to quantify and compare the drought resilience of major cereals and millets across rainfed agroecosystems of Odisha. The study develops a Composite Crop Resilience Index (CCRI) by integrating exposure resilience, rainfall–yield elasticity-based sensitivity resilience, and detrended Yield Stability Index (YSI) representing adaptive capacity across multiple agro-climatic zones. The framework provides an integrated assessment of crop resilience aligned with the IPCC AR6 risk–resilience conceptualization4,5. Unlike previous studies that primarily rely on single indicators of drought impact, the proposed CCRI integrates climatic exposure, crop sensitivity, and production stability within a unified resilience framework at the sub-regional scale. Although focused on eastern India, the analytical framework is designed to be transferable, reproducible, and applicable across diverse rainfed agroecosystems using routinely available rainfall and crop yield datasets.

Results

Rainfall variability and exposure resilience

Long-term monsoon rainfall across the selected rainfed districts of Odisha exhibited substantial spatial variability in both rainfall magnitude and interannual variability. Mean JJAS (June, July, August, September) rainfall ranged from 881.37 mm in Ganjam to 1401.25 mm in Malkangiri, indicating contrasting hydro-climatic conditions across districts (Table 1). Rainfall variability, expressed through coefficient of variation (CV), varied between 16.90% and 21.06%, suggesting generally moderate interannual rainfall fluctuations. Malkangiri exhibited the highest rainfall variability (CV = 21.06%) followed by Kendujhar (20.30%) and Nuapada (19.89%), indicating relatively greater drought exposure and climatic uncertainty in these districts. In contrast, Ganjam (16.9%), Gajapati (17.14%), and Koraput (17.39%) showed comparatively lower rainfall variability, reflecting relatively stable monsoon conditions. Exposure resilience represents the ability of cropping systems to withstand long-term drought exposure and rainfall variability. The exposure resilience (Table 1) showed relatively limited spatial variation, ranging from 0.917 to 0.934, indicating overall moderate to high resilience to rainfall variability across the study districts. Gajapati recorded the highest exposure resilience (0.934), followed by Koraput (0.930) and Ganjam (0.929), whereas comparatively lower resilience was observed in Malkangiri (0.917) and Kendujhar (0.921), primarily due to higher rainfall variability. Overall, districts with higher rainfall variability generally exhibited lower exposure resilience, indicating greater climatic stress on rainfed agricultural systems.

Rainfall–yield elasticity and crop sensitivity

2.2.1 Rainfall-yield Elasticity: Crop yield responses during drought years varied substantially across crops and agro-climatic zones (Table 2). Pre-monsoon rice and wet-season rice consistently exhibited the highest yield reductions frequently exceeding 40% and reaching more than 60% in Sundargarh, indicating strong dependence on seasonal rainfall. Maize also showed considerable yield declines across districts, generally ranging between 28% and 46%, although reductions were comparatively lower than those observed for rice. In contrast, millet crops demonstrated relatively lower drought-induced yield losses across most districts. Sorghum, pearl millet, finger millet, and small millet often recorded yield reductions below 20%, with several districts exhibiting losses below 10%, indicating relatively greater tolerance to rainfall variability and drought stress. Rainfall–yield elasticity exhibited substantial crop-specific and spatial variability across the rainfed districts of Odisha (Fig. 1). Wet-season rice exhibited the highest elasticity values, particularly in Ganjam (27.23), indicating extremely high yield sensitivity to rainfall variability and strong dependence on monsoonal precipitation. Similarly, pre-monsoon rice showed elevated elasticity in Ganjam (21.21), reflecting pronounced vulnerability under rainfall anomalies. Maize demonstrated moderate to high elasticity across several districts, with the highest value observed in Koraput (9.10), suggesting considerable responsiveness of maize yield to interannual rainfall variability in upland agroecosystems. In contrast, sorghum, pearl millet, finger millet, and small millet consistently exhibited low elasticity values, generally remaining below 2.5 within rainfed agroecosystems. Finger millet and small millet consistently exhibited the lowest elasticity values among crops, highlighting their superior drought adaptability and resilience to rainfall uncertainty. Spatially, Ganjam and Koraput exhibited comparatively higher elasticity across major cereals. Conversely, Malkangiri and Kendujhar generally showed lower elasticity values across crops, suggesting relatively stable crop responses to rainfall variability. Extremely high elasticity values observed in rice in Ganjam resulted from disproportionately large yield deviations occurring under relatively small rainfall anomalies, which amplified rainfall–yield responsiveness.

Crop sensitivity resilience

The sensitivity resilience analysis revealed substantial contrasts among crops and districts to withstand rainfall-induced yield fluctuations (Fig. 2). Wet-season rice exhibited the lowest resilience across most districts, with resilience values of 0.00 in Ganjam, Gajapati, Sundargarh, Kendujhar, and Nuapada, while only modest resilience was observed in Koraput (0.28) and Malkangiri (0.10), indicating extremely high climatic sensitivity. Pre-monsoon rice also showed relatively low sensitivity resilience, varying from 0.00 in Malkangiri to 0.56 in Gajapati, with intermediate values in Ganjam (0.22), Koraput (0.29), and Nuapada (0.40). Maize displayed moderate spatial variability in resilience, ranging from 0.00 in Koraput to 0.94 in Sundargarh and 0.89 in Ganjam, suggesting district-dependent adaptation potential. In contrast, traditional millets consistently demonstrated very high sensitivity resilience across all districts. Sorghum recorded resilience values between 0.84 and 1.00, achieving 1.00 in Gajapati, Koraput, and Kendujhar. Pearl millet maintained high resilience ranging from 0.84 to 0.97, while finger millet exhibited exceptionally high resilience values of 0.94–1.00 across all districts. Similarly, small millet showed resilience values between 0.88 and 1.00. Spatially, Koraput and Malkangiri displayed strong resilience performance for millets despite lower resilience in rice and maize, whereas Ganjam showed extreme vulnerability in rice crops but very high resilience in millets. Overall, millet crops exhibited comparatively lower rainfall–yield elasticity, indicating weaker yield responsiveness to rainfall anomalies and greater production stability under drought conditions, whereas rice-based systems showed markedly higher elasticity, reflecting stronger dependence on monsoonal rainfall and greater climatic sensitivity.

Higher sensitivity resilience values indicate lower rainfall sensitivity and greater capacity of crops to withstand rainfall-induced yield fluctuations, and vice versa. Yield stability and adaptive capacity

The Yield Stability Index (YSI) revealed substantial variation in interannual production stability among crops and districts (Fig. 3). Wet-season rice exhibited consistently low YSI values ranging from only 0.08 in Nuapada to 0.25 in Koraput, indicating high interannual yield fluctuations and strong susceptibility to rainfall variability. Pre-monsoon rice also showed relatively poor stability, with YSI values between 0.35 in Sundargarh and 0.52 in Gajapati. In contrast, maize exhibited moderate stability, with YSI values varying from 0.57 in Nuapada to 0.74 in Sundargarh, suggesting comparatively better adaptation under variable rainfall conditions. Millets consistently recorded the highest stability across all districts. Sorghum showed very high YSI values of 0.88–0.98, while pearl millet maintained stable performance with values ranging from 0.82 to 0.92. Finger millet and small millet emerged as the most stable crops reaching a maximum of 0.99 in Koraput and Sundargarh, respectively. Millet crops in districts such as Koraput, Gajapati, and Kendujhar generally exhibited higher YSI values, indicating comparatively stable performance under rainfed conditions, whereas rice-based systems in Ganjam and Nuapada showed relatively lower yield stability. Overall, millet crops maintained consistently higher YSI values, indicating greater interannual yield stability and stronger adaptive capacity under variable rainfall conditions, whereas lower YSI values in rice systems reflected greater temporal instability in productivity.

Composite crop resilience index (CCRI) and multi-dimensional resilience visualization

The Composite Crop Resilience Index (CCRI) revealed clear contrasts in resilience among crops and districts across the rainfed agroecosystems of Odisha (Fig. 4). Wet-season rice recorded the lowest CCRI values, ranging from 0.25 in Nuapada to 0.40 in Koraput, indicating poor resilience under rainfall variability and drought stress. Similarly, pre-monsoon rice showed relatively low resilience, with CCRI values varying between 0.39 in Sundargarh and 0.51 in Koraput. Maize demonstrated moderate resilience within rainfed agroecosystems, with values ranging from 0.52 in Nuapada to 0.65 in Sundargarh. In contrast, traditional millets consistently exhibited high CCRI values across all districts. Sorghum recorded resilience values between 0.80 and 0.91, while pearl millet maintained relatively high CCRI values ranging from 0.74 to 0.87. Finger millet emerged as one of the most resilient crops, with CCRI values consistently exceeding 0.90 and reaching a maximum of 0.98 in Kendujhar. Similarly, small millet showed very high resilience across districts, with CCRI values ranging from 0.92 in Nuapada to 0.98 in Sundargarh. Radar-based visualization further highlighted distinct multidimensional resilience profiles among crops (Fig. 5). Overall, the CCRI integrated exposure resilience, rainfall sensitivity, and adaptive capacity into a unified resilience framework, clearly distinguishing stable dryland crops from highly climate-sensitive cereal systems, with higher CCRI values in millet crops primarily resulting from the combined effects of low rainfall sensitivity and greater yield stability.

District-wise resilience patterns and ranking

District-wise ranking based on mean Composite Crop Resilience Index (CCRI) revealed notable spatial differences in crop resilience across the selected rainfed districts of Odisha (Table 3). District-wise mean CCRI values indicated moderate spatial variability in overall crop resilience across the rainfed districts of Odisha, with Ganjam recording the highest mean CCRI (0.559), followed by Gajapati (0.550) and Nuapada (0.532), suggesting relatively better aggregate resilience performance of cropping systems in these districts. Malkangiri (0.510), Sundargarh (0.505), and Koraput (0.504) exhibited intermediate resilience levels with marginal differences among them, whereas Kendujhar recorded the lowest mean CCRI (0.496), indicating comparatively lower overall resilience. Overall, district-level differences in mean CCRI were comparatively small, indicating that resilience patterns were more strongly governed by crop-specific characteristics than by spatial variability in climatic exposure.

Sensitivity analysis of weighting scenarios

Sensitivity analysis of the Composite Crop Resilience Index (CCRI) under different weighting scenarios was conducted to test the robustness of the proposed framework across alternative weighting assumptions (Fig. 6). Sorghum, pearl millet, finger millet, and small millet consistently exhibited high CCRI values across all weighting scenarios, reflecting strong resilience to rainfall variability and drought stress in rainfed agroecosystems. Sorghum recorded the highest and most stable resilience, with CCRI values ranging from 0.91 to 0.92 across all scenarios, followed closely by small millet (0.89–0.91) and pearl millet (0.90 under all scenarios). Finger millet also maintained relatively high resilience with only marginal variation (0.84–0.86), indicating stable performance irrespective of weighting adjustments. In contrast, wet-season rice and pre-monsoon rice consistently showed lower CCRI values, varying from 0.41 to 0.56 and 0.48–0.61, respectively, highlighting their greater sensitivity to drought exposure and rainfall fluctuations. Maize exhibited intermediate resilience, with CCRI values ranging from 0.68 to 0.75 depending on weighting emphasis. The sensitivity-focused weighting scenario produced comparatively lower CCRI values for rice and maize, suggesting their higher dependence on sensitivity-related components, whereas adaptive-focused and balanced scenarios resulted in modest improvements. Overall, the limited variation in CCRI rankings across weighting combinations demonstrates the methodological stability and robustness of the CCRI framework, while consistently identifying millets as more climate-resilient crops compared to rice-based systems in rainfed regions of Odisha.

The heatmap illustrates the robustness of crop resilience rankings under varying weighting assumptions, including equal, balanced, sensitivity-focused, adaptive-focused, and crop-response-focused scenarios. Sorghum, pearl millet, and small millet consistently maintained high CCRI values across all scenarios, indicating stable resilience performance irrespective of weighting structure. In contrast, wet-season rice and pre-monsoon rice exhibited consistently lower CCRI values, particularly under sensitivity-focused weighting, reflecting strong climatic sensitivity and rainfall dependence.

Statistical comparison of crop and district resilience

Statistical comparison of CCRI across crops revealed highly significant differences in resilience levels among crop groups (ANOVA: F = 102.056, p < 0.001), indicating substantial variability in drought resilience across cropping systems. In contrast, district-wise comparison showed no significant differences in mean CCRI among districts (F = 0.044, p = 0.9996), suggesting that crop type exerted a stronger influence on resilience than geographic location across the selected rainfed districts of Odisha. Tukey’s HSD post-hoc analysis further demonstrated that wet-season rice and pre-monsoon rice consistently exhibited significantly lower CCRI values compared to maize, sorghum, pearl millet, finger millet, and small millet (p < 0.05). Finger millet, small millet, pearl millet, and sorghum formed a statistically similar high-resilience group, with no significant differences among them (p > 0.05), reflecting their stable and drought-resilient performance under rainfed conditions. Similarly, pearl millet and sorghum showed resilience levels comparable to small millet and finger millet, indicating the robust adaptive capacity of millet-based systems. Maize exhibited intermediate resilience, significantly differing from both low-resilience rice systems and high-resilience millet systems. No significant difference was observed between wet-season rice and pre-monsoon rice (p = 0.858), indicating similarly low resilience characteristics in rice-based production systems. District-wise Tukey comparisons further confirmed the absence of statistically significant differences among districts, as all pairwise comparisons were non-significant (p > 0.05). Overall, the results demonstrate that crop choice rather than district-level climatic variation primarily governed resilience patterns, with millets and sorghum emerging as significantly more resilient crops compared to rice-based systems in rainfed Odisha. The absence of significant district-level differences suggests that resilience variability was primarily governed by crop-specific sensitivity and adaptive capacity rather than differences in climatic exposure alone.

Discussion

The present study revealed substantial crop-specific variability in climatic resilience across the rainfed agroecosystems of Odisha within an IPCC AR6-aligned framework integrating exposure resilience, rainfall–yield elasticity-based sensitivity resilience, and adaptive capacity represented by the Yield Stability Index (YSI). The Composite Crop Resilience Index (CCRI), developed using these integrated resilience components, consistently demonstrated the superior resilience of millet-based cropping systems under rainfall-deficit conditions compared with rice-based systems. Finger millet, small millet, sorghum, and pearl millet generally exhibited lower rainfall dependence, greater yield stability, and higher adaptive capacity across districts, resulting in comparatively higher CCRI values. In contrast, wet-season rice and pre-monsoon rice showed greater drought-year yield reductions, higher rainfall–yield elasticity, and lower yield stability, indicating comparatively higher climatic vulnerability under rainfed conditions.

The observed superiority of millet-based systems is consistent with their well-documented physiological and agronomic adaptations to moisture stress. Millets generally possess deeper rooting systems, shorter growing duration, lower transpiration demand, superior water-use efficiency, and greater tolerance to intermittent dry spells and elevated temperatures compared with conventional cereals12,16–18. These adaptive traits enable millet crops to maintain productivity under erratic rainfall and low-input production environments. In the present study, drought-year yield reductions for millet crops were generally below 20% across several districts, whereas wet-season rice frequently recorded yield losses exceeding 40–60%, particularly in Sundargarh and Keonjhar. The relatively lower interannual variability and higher YSI values observed for millet crops further indicate their stronger buffering capacity against climatic fluctuations and production shocks17,18.

The rainfall–yield elasticity analysis provided important insights into climatic sensitivity across cropping systems. Wet-season rice generally exhibited the highest elasticity values across districts, particularly in Ganjam, Koraput, and Nuapada, indicating strong dependence on monsoon rainfall and high vulnerability to rainfall deficits. Pre-monsoon rice also demonstrated considerable climatic sensitivity, while maize showed intermediate rainfall dependence with substantial spatial variability among districts. In contrast, millet crops generally maintained lower elasticity values, reflecting comparatively stable yield responses under variable rainfall conditions. Lower rainfall–yield elasticity implies that millet yields are less responsive to interannual rainfall fluctuations and therefore more resilient under drought stress. Similar findings have been reported in previous studies showing that rainfall variability explains a substantial proportion of yield instability in rainfed rice systems, whereas millets exhibit greater production stability under moisture-limited environments19–21.

The sensitivity resilience analysis reinforced these findings by demonstrating clear contrasts in the ability of crops to withstand rainfall-induced production shocks. Millet crops repeatedly recorded very high sensitivity resilience values across districts, while rice systems exhibited extremely low resilience in several locations. Finger millet and small millet particularly maintained resilience values close to unity across most districts, indicating strong capacity to absorb climatic stress without substantial yield disruption. Sorghum and pearl millet al.so demonstrated high resilience performance, reflecting their strong adaptation to dryland conditions. Conversely, wet-season rice exhibited near-zero resilience across multiple districts, highlighting its pronounced vulnerability to rainfall variability under rainfed cultivation. These results collectively suggest that the resilience advantage of millet-based systems primarily arises from their lower climatic sensitivity and more stable production responses under drought conditions20,21.

The Yield Stability Index further highlighted substantial differences in adaptive capacity among crops. Finger millet, small millet, sorghum, and pearl millet exhibited high YSI values across districts, indicating strong interannual production stability despite climatic variability. In contrast, wet-season rice and pre-monsoon rice showed markedly lower stability, reflecting greater susceptibility to rainfall fluctuations and production instability. The comparatively higher YSI values of millet systems suggest stronger adaptive capacity within the IPCC resilience framework, wherein resilient systems maintain functionality and productivity despite external climatic disturbances4,5. These findings are particularly important in the context of increasing monsoon variability and recurrent drought events across eastern India. The ability of millet crops to maintain stable productivity under rainfall uncertainty highlights their potential role in climate-resilient crop diversification strategies for vulnerable rainfed regions.

The integrated CCRI framework enabled a multidimensional assessment of crop resilience by simultaneously incorporating exposure resilience, rainfall sensitivity, and adaptive capacity. Unlike single-indicator approaches, the CCRI provided a more comprehensive representation of resilience by integrating both climatic stress as well as crop response characteristics. The radar-based resilience visualization further illustrated that millet-based systems maintained broader and more balanced resilience profiles characterized by low rainfall sensitivity and high adaptive capacity, whereas rice systems displayed contracted resilience structures dominated by high climatic sensitivity and unstable productivity. These findings corroborate earlier studies reporting superior drought tolerance, water-use efficiency, and production stability of millets under moisture-stressed rainfed environments18,22,23. Across districts, finger millet and small millet consistently recorded the highest CCRI values, followed by sorghum and pearl millet, whereas wet-season rice mostly exhibited the lowest resilience. Maize demonstrated intermediate resilience with moderate spatial variability.

District-level analysis indicated comparatively smaller spatial differences in mean CCRI than crop-level differences. Although districts such as Malkangiri, Nuapada, and Kendujhar experienced relatively greater rainfall variability and drought exposure, millet crops maintained high resilience even within these climatically vulnerable regions. This finding highlights the inherent adaptability of traditional dryland crops across diverse agro-climatic settings12,13. Districts such as Koraput and Kendujhar are characterized by undulating uplands, shallow lateritic soils, high climatic exposure, and limited supplemental irrigation, which collectively increase climatic sensitivity and reduce buffering capacity during drought years. In contrast, districts such as Gajapati and Ganjam likely benefited from more favourable agro-hydrological conditions and improved production stability under rainfall variability24,25. The absence of statistically significant district-wise differences in CCRI further supports the conclusion that crop-specific physiological and adaptive traits exerted stronger influence on resilience outcomes rather than spatial variation in rainfall exposure. In contrast, the highly significant crop-wise differences identified through ANOVA and Tukey’s HSD analyses confirmed that millet crops were significantly more resilient than rice-based systems under rainfed conditions. The absence of statistically significant district-level differences suggests that crop-specific sensitivity and adaptive capacity exerted stronger influence on resilience outcomes than regional exposure variability alone26,27.

The sensitivity analysis substantially strengthened the robustness of the CCRI framework. Crop resilience rankings remained largely stable across equal-weight, balanced, sensitivity-focused, adaptive-focused, and crop-response-focused weighting scenarios. Sorghum, pearl millet, finger millet, and small millet maintained high CCRI values irrespective of weighting assumptions, whereas wet-season rice and pre-monsoon rice consistently remained the least resilient crops. The limited variation in resilience rankings indicates that the resilience superiority of millet-based systems was structurally robust and not an artifact of subjective weighting choices. Such methodological stability is important for operational applications of resilience assessment frameworks in climate adaptation planning and agricultural decision-support systems.

An important contribution of the present study is the development of a transferable and data-light resilience assessment framework using routinely available rainfall and crop yield datasets. Many existing resilience assessment approaches rely on extensive socio-economic surveys, high-resolution remote sensing datasets, or process-based crop simulation models that are often difficult to implement in data-scarce rainfed regions. In contrast, the proposed CCRI framework relies primarily on long-term rainfall and yield time-series data, thereby improving operational applicability for drought-risk assessment, climate-resilient crop planning, and adaptation prioritization across vulnerable agroecosystems. The framework may be particularly useful for climate-vulnerability assessments in data-scarce rainfed regions of South Asia and Sub-Saharan Africa where long-term rainfall and crop yield datasets are available but advanced monitoring infrastructure is limited.

Despite its strengths, the study has certain limitations. The framework primarily focuses on climatic and production-based dimensions of resilience and does not explicitly incorporate socio-economic conditions, irrigation access, farm-management practices, varietal characteristics, soil fertility dynamics, or institutional adaptive capacity. In addition, rainfall–yield elasticity assumes a simplified linear relationship between rainfall variability and crop productivity, whereas crop responses under severe drought may exhibit non-linear threshold behaviour. District-level yield datasets may also reflect technological improvements, management changes, and policy interventions over time. Future studies may integrate remote sensing observations, groundwater availability, household-level adaptive capacity indicators, and climate projection datasets to develop more comprehensive multidimensional resilience assessments.

Overall, the findings demonstrate that millet-based systems provide a viable pathway for enhancing climate resilience in drought-prone rainfed agriculture. Promotion of resilient millet-based systems may reduce climatic risk, improve production stability, and strengthen adaptive capacity under increasing rainfall variability and drought frequency. The framework may support climate-resilient crop diversification planning, drought adaptation strategies, and resilience-oriented agricultural policy development in vulnerable rainfed agroecosystems.

Methods

Study area

The study was conducted across four agro-climatic zones of Odisha, India, selected to represent a broad gradient of rainfall variability, soil types, and rainfed production environments relevant to cereal and millet cultivation (Fig. 7). The selected districts span the North Eastern Ghats as well as coastal agro-climatic zone (Ganjam and Gajapati), the South Eastern Ghats and Eastern Ghats Highlands (Koraput and Malkangiri), the North Western and North Central Plateau (Sundargarh and Keonjhar), and the Western Undulating Zone (Nuapada). Together, these regions characterized by contrasting monsoon rainfall regimes, drought frequency, and edaphic conditions. Odisha experiences a tropical monsoon climate, with the majority of annual rainfall received during the southwest monsoon season (June–September). The coastal districts are influenced by intense monsoon rainfall and occasional cyclonic events, while the central and western districts are more frequently affected by rainfall deficits, dry spells, and recurrent droughts. This spatial heterogeneity in hydro-climatic conditions provides a representative setting for assessing crop performance and resilience across diverse rainfed agroecosystems.

Cropping systems and datasets

The analysis focused on seven major crops cultivated under predominantly rainfed conditions: pre-monsoon rice, wet-season rice, maize, finger millet, small millet, sorghum, and pearl millet. Rice and maize are widely grown across lowland and medium-land systems, whereas millet crops are primarily cultivated on well-drained hill slopes and tablelands under low-input management28–30. Millets are typically grown in marginal environments characterized by shallow soils, limited irrigation access, and high rainfall variability, making them suitable candidates for resilience assessment under rainfed conditions.

Data sources

District-level crop yield data for the period 1998–2023 were obtained from the Directorate of Agriculture and Farmers’ Empowerment, Government of Odisha, supplemented by other official secondary sources31,32. The dataset includes yields of millet crops (finger millet, small millet, sorghum, and pearl millet), rice (pre-monsoon and wet-season), and maize. Corresponding daily rainfall data for the same period were collected from the Indian Meteorological Department (IMD) and cross-verified with district-level records maintained by the Special Relief Commissioner (SRC), Government of Odisha33. These datasets were used to characterize long-term rainfall variability, drought occurrence, and crop yield response. In the present study, the term “data-light” refers to a resilience assessment framework that relies exclusively on routinely available long-term monthly monsoon rainfall and crop yield time-series data, without requiring high-resolution remote sensing products, process-based crop models, field-scale biophysical measurements, or extensive socio-economic surveys. So, the proposed framework utilizes only two primary datasets: (i) seasonal monsoon rainfall records and (ii) district-level crop yield data. A minimum continuous time series of approximately 15–20 years is sufficient to estimate interannual variability, rainfall–yield elasticity, yield stability, and resilience metrics with reasonable statistical reliability. The data-light nature of the framework enhances its applicability in data-scarce rainfed regions where advanced monitoring infrastructure and high-resolution agricultural datasets are often unavailable.

Drought year identification

Meteorological drought years were identified following the India Meteorological Department (IMD) criterion, wherein seasonal rainfall during the southwest monsoon (June–September) falling below 75% of the long-period average (LPA) is classified as a drought year, corresponding to a rainfall deficit of 25% or more34. Identified drought years were used to evaluate crop yield responses under stress conditions relative to normal rainfall years. IPCC AR6-based crop resilience framework

The crop resilience assessment framework adopted in the present study was developed based on the IPCC climate risk and resilience framework, wherein resilience is conceptualized as the integrated interaction of exposure, sensitivity, and adaptive capacity under climatic stress conditions4. Within rainfed agricultural systems, crop resilience represents the capacity of cropping systems to withstand rainfall variability, sustain productivity under drought stress, and recover from climatic disturbances while maintaining essential production functions. Accordingly, three major resilience dimensions were considered: (i) drought exposure, representing climatic stress associated with monsoon rainfall deficits; (ii) rainfall–yield sensitivity, representing the degree of crop yield response to rainfall variability; and (iii) adaptive capacity, representing the ability of crops to maintain stable productivity under variable climatic conditions. The framework was intentionally designed as a “data-light” resilience assessment approach relying exclusively on long-term district-level rainfall and crop yield datasets, thereby improving applicability in data-scarce rainfed agroecosystems where high-resolution biophysical or socio-economic datasets are often unavailable. The selected indicators collectively captured the major resilience dimensions emphasized in contemporary climate-risk assessment frameworks and enabled comparative evaluation of cereal- and millet-based production systems across diverse agro-climatic regions of Odisha.

Statistical analysis

Differences in resilience indicators among crops and districts were evaluated using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) post-hoc test. Normality and homogeneity of variance assumptions were evaluated prior to statistical analysis. All data processing, statistical analyses, indicator normalization, and computation of resilience metrics, including the Composite Crop Resilience Index (CCRI), were performed using Python-based scientific libraries. The analytical workflow integrated long-term rainfall and crop yield time series, estimation of rainfall–yield elasticity, yield variability, and Yield Stability Index (YSI), followed by Z-score normalization and composite index construction. The implementation ensures analytical transparency, reproducibility, and facilitates transferability of the CCRI framework across data-limited rainfed agroecosystems using routinely available rainfall and crop yield datasets.
