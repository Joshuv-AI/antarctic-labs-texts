Perceptions of integrating cervical cancer screening with HIV and sexual and reproductive health services: results from serial multiple methods studies among health workers and women in Zimbabwe
Authors: Nancy Ruhode, Claudius Madanhire, Mary K Tumushime, Stephano Gudukeya, Nyaradzo D Muhonde, Karin Hatzold, Frances M Cowan, Euphemia L Sibanda
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCervical cancer screening among Zimbabwean women is suboptimal. Integrating cervical cancer screening with HIV and sexual and reproductive health services can improve uptake. To inform optimisation of integrated models, we explored evolution of women’s and health worker views on integration and cervical cancer screening.MethodsOver three phases between Nov-2013 and Mar-2016, we collected serial multiple methods data from female attendees and health workers at four clinics providing integrated cervical cancer screening and HIV and sexual/reproductive health services. Using program data, we examined trends in uptake of cervical cancer screening.ResultsBetween 2013 and 2016, 38,342 women were screened for cervical cancer at three clinics. Program data showed increasing trends in uptake, capping at clinic capacity. Qualitative studies showed that clinic attendees and health workers viewed integration positively across phases because: (i) information on cervical cancer screening was easily disseminated, leading to improved uptake; (ii) co-location of services was convenient. As knowledge increased, demand exceeded supply, requiring operation of strict appointment-based systems. Other facilitators to cervical cancer screening included offer of services for free, personal experience of friends/family who suffered cervical cancer, peer encouragement, spousal/partner support, and having suspicious signs/symptoms. Barriers were more pronounced in earlier phases and largely centred around myths that spread in communities or clinic waiting rooms. Lack of training among health workers impeded implementation.ConclusionsIntegrating cervical cancer screening with HIV and sexual/reproductive health services was feasible and acceptable, with less pronounced barriers as programs matured. Addressing individual and program-level barriers can optimise uptake of services.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12978-025-02190-z.Keywords: Integration, Cervical-cancer-screening, HIV, Sexual and reproductive health

Background

Zimbabwe is among the countries with the highest cervical cancer incidence rates in the world, with 36.7 cases per 100,000 per year; and cervical cancer is the number one cause of cancer-related deaths among women in the country [1]. Women living with HIV are disproportionately affected by cervical cancer, with higher rates of disease progression and poorer outcomes [2–8] Most (80%) cervical cancer patients in Zimbabwe present late for treatment when the disease is advanced [8, 9]. Screening for cervical cancer promotes early detection and prevention of disease progression [10–12].According to the Zimbabwe Demographic and Health Survey (ZDHS) 2015, 79% of women aged between 15 and 49 reported that they have heard about cervical cancer, however only 13% reported to have ever been screened [13]. Major barriers include low awareness of cervical cancer and screening services and limited access to screening and treatment services [14–18].

Integrating cervical cancer prevention services into HIV delivery services has the potential to accelerate ‘screen and treat’ programs and mitigate the cervical cancer burden among vulnerable HIV positive women [19–22]. The Zimbabwe Ministry of Health and Child Care (MoHCC) has prioritised the scale-up of cervical cancer screening by adopting visual inspection with acetic acid (VIA), a practical, affordable, and accessible cervical cancer screening method that can be used at lower level health facilities in low resource settings [23, 24]. MoHCC also works closely with partners, including the United States President’s Emergency Plan for AIDS Relief (PEPFAR), to drive implementation. PEPFAR committed funding to eight countries in sub–Saharan Africa (SSA) to catalyse screening and pre-invasive treatment services for HIV positive women. This enabled the initiation of ‘screen and treat’ programs for cervical cancer screening, where women are screened and treated in a single visit [25–27].

Population Services International Zimbabwe (PSI-Z), one of the Zimbabwe MoHCC implementing partners, integrated cervical cancer screening services with HIV and reproductive health services at their clinics across the country, starting in 2013. HIV testing, treatment and prevention, tuberculosis diagnostic tests and contraception are offered alongside cervical cancer screening. Screening is done using VIA and women who have a positive screening result are immediately treated using cryotherapy for pre-cancerous lesions or referred to gynaecological services if there is evidence of advanced disease. To inform optimisation of the integrated program, we explored health workers’ and clinic attendees’ views on cervical cancer screening and its provision in integrated clinics. We also determined trends in uptake of cervical cancer screening following integration.

Methods

Setting

The study was conducted at four clinics: three out of seven purposively selected PSI-Z clinics (“New Start” Centres) in three cities, namely Harare, Chitungwiza and Bulawayo, and one public clinic in Bulawayo that had a public-private partnership with PSI-Z. PSI-Z sites provide HIV testing and treatment services, and after 2013, these were integrated with sexual and reproductive health services. These include cervical cancer screening, a wide spectrum of family planning services, including implants and inter-uterine device (IUD) insertions, screening, and treatment of sexually transmitted infections (STI), and services for survivors of sexual assault. Women aged 30 and above who visited the clinics seeking any of the services listed above were primarily offered cervical cancer screening. However, women under 30 were offered screening only if they were living with HIV. The public–private partnership site provided general outpatients services including family planning services.

Study design

A serial multiple methods study design was used to explore the evolution of clinic attendees’ and health workers’ perceptions of integrated services, and barriers and facilitators to cervical cancer screening. Data collection was done in three phases to capture changes in views/perceptions: November 2013 to March 2014 (phase 1), November 2014 to March 2015 (phase 2) and July 2015 to March 2016 (phase 3). During each phase of data collection, we explored views corresponding to the preceding months of implementation. Multiple data collection methods were employed among diverse participant groups facilitating triangulation of views and experiences across clinic attendees and health workers by method of data collection. In-depth interviews (IDI) were conducted with female clinic attendees across all three phases and with health workers in phases 1 and 2 at the four clinics. Focus group discussions (FGDs) were conducted with health workers in phase three at the three New Start Clinic sites to discuss findings from all phases. Participant observations were conducted in phase 1 only at all four clinics (Table 1).

Sample and sampling procedures

Women aged 18 years and older, who had accepted or declined the offer for cervical cancer screening, were purposively recruited to understand facilitators and barriers to screening. Women were recruited at the end of their clinic visit, with help from health workers. Health workers who provided integrated services across the different clinics were purposively recruited to include those who provided cervical cancer screening, family planning, and HIV services. Within each phase, for each of health workers and clinic attendees we aimed to recruit 25–40 participants. The actual number of recruited participants was informed by theoretical saturation (when no new themes emerged) [28].

Data collection procedures

Across all three phases, data were collected by a team of eight trained qualitative researchers (2 males, 6 females). Researchers received training on the study protocol, data collection methods, and instruments from experienced qualitative researchers before the onset of the study. In-depth interviews, focus group discussions and participant observations were conducted using topic guides (see in-depth interview guide for clinic attendees, Additional file 1, in-depth interview guide for health workers, Additional file 2, focus group discussion guide, Additional file 3 and participant observations guide, Additional file 4) that elicited participants’ views on cervical cancer screening, experiences of the integrated services, and barriers and facilitators to cervical cancer screening. Discussions were held in participants’ preferred language.

Participant observation guides spelt out the procedures for observation, health workers and client behaviours to observe, as well as relevant processes in the provision of services in an integrated setting, for instance, how clinic attendees were received and how long they would wait to get services.

Topic guides underwent expert review prior to their use in data collection. As part of the data analysis process, the research team held ongoing discussions to review themes emerging from interviews, focus group discussions, and participant observations. These discussions helped ensure that the data collection tools elicited responses relevant to the research questions. Based on these reflections, topic guides were revised on an ongoing basis to ensure that emerging themes were adequately explored.

Clinic attendees were interviewed by female researchers to ensure ability to respond freely. Interviews were conducted in private and lasted 30 to 60 min. FGDs were facilitated by experienced male and female researchers in private rooms within or outside clinic venues. Each FGD lasted 60 to 90 min. Written informed consent was obtained from in-depth interview and FGD participants before procedures were conducted.

Participant observations were conducted over 2–3 consecutive days per clinic, with 3 to 4 h of observation per day. Participant observers were anonymous (PSI-Z managers were aware of the observations but did not know the date of observation nor the identity of observers). Observations were made in waiting areas and other clinic environments that were publicly accessible with observers posing as clinic attendees seeking services, but they did not access the services.

Collection of programme data on uptake of cervical cancer screening

Routine programme data on uptake of cervical cancer screening and uptake of other family planning, sexual reproductive health and HIV services were electronically recorded by PSI-Z on an ongoing basis.

Data handling and analysis

Interviews and focus group discussions were audio recorded, transcribed verbatim and translated into English. Qualitative data analysis began as soon as data collection began: as soon as possible after each interview, FGD or participant observation field notes were written with focus on emerging themes. The research team held weekly data analysis meetings where they reflected on emerging findings and discussed themes that needed to be probed in subsequent data collection episodes. The process continued until no new themes emerged across data collection methods and participant groups. Themes from interviews and FGDs were further developed through thorough reading of transcripts and writing of analytic summaries of each in-depth interview, focus group discussion, or participant observation. Analytic summaries drew similarities and contrasts within and across participants and data collection methodologies (triangulation), with analytic memos written on how some thematic areas were related. Participant views were compared across the three phases to see if these remained the same or changed over time. A team of researchers developed coding frameworks for clinic attendees and health workers for each of the 3 phases, using information from summaries, analytic memos, and team discussions. Coding of transcripts was done in NVIVO 10. Quantitative programme data from 3 PSI-Z sites were analysed descriptively.

Results

Cervical cancer screening uptake

Between August 2013 and October 2016, a total of 38 342 women were screened for cervical cancer across the three New Start Centres. An upward trend in monthly average uptake was evident from phase 1 to 3 across all sites (Fig. 1). At two sites, there were no additional increases after Phase 2, reflecting maximum capacity of these sites, while numbers continued to increase at one site due to increased resources and site capacity.

We analysed data from 26 656 women whose demographic data were recorded. No uptake data were available for the public–private partnership site.

Characteristics of women who took up cervical cancer screening

Characteristics of women who took up cervical cancer screening are summarised in Table 2. Women were aged between 15 and 50 years. While Ministry of Health and Child Care Zimbabwe guidelines recommend cervical cancer screening to women aged 25–49 years [8], PSI-Z primarily offered screening to women aged 30 years and over and to women below 30 who were at higher risk, for example, those living with HIV. The youngest women taking up cervical screening at PSI-Z clinics were aged 15 years. The median age group for all three PSI-Z sites was 35–39 years. More than two-thirds of women (18 837, 71%) were married, 1667 (6%) were single, 2 986 (11%) were separated and 3 059 (12%) were widowed. Results from qualitative studies

Women’s views on integration and cervical cancer screening

Views on integration

Women appreciated the convenience of sexual reproductive health services being integrated with HIV services, reporting that it allowed them to access multiple services, cervical cancer screening included, under one roof on a single visit, thereby saving time and travel expenses. They also appreciated that services were offered free of charge.

“When I came for (HIV) testing I was also offered family planning, and then when I went for family planning that is when I was told that we also test for cancer…You can have all the services here on one day, so it saves time” (IDI 28-year-old woman, screened for cervical cancer).

Service integration appeared to facilitate the acquisition of knowledge on cervical cancer screening, among clinic attendees, including the fact that services were offered for free. Women reported getting information on cervical cancer screening from health workers at multiple service points within the clinic thereby facilitating uptake even among women who were not initially seeking the service. Posters on cervical cancer screening hung at the clinics prompted women to enquire and book for service. The efficiency of health workers’ information-giving sessions was commended by women during on site discussions among themselves:

“The health workers are so good in the way they counsel us before screening…the way they teach you is so understandable and makes you want to go out and tell others in the community” (Participant observation, words of a middle-aged woman).

In-depth interviews revealed that knowledge on cervical cancer screening also spread through informal discussions women held in the waiting areas at the clinics.

“We were just discussing stories as women, sitting in the shade (waiting area) and that is when I heard that here they screen for cervical cancer.” (IDI 27-year-old woman took up cervical cancer screening).

It was evident that discussions on cervical cancer screening also happened in communities and that these discussions encouraged some women to attend for screening.

“…she told me that as women you should have your uterus checked so that you can be diagnosed early, if you delay look at me, I had my uterus removed (IDI 40 -year-old woman, took up cervical cancer screening).

Of note, discussions among women in the waiting area were also a source of misinformation: they were sometimes laden with myths and misconceptions about cervical cancer screening, causing uncertainty, mistrust, and fear of the screening process. During participant observations women were heard giving exaggerated information of the pain and discomfort that was experienced during screening. In these discussions, many women who had just screened likened the pain they experienced during screening to that of labour pain. The speculum was described as a formidable ‘metal dipstick as long as one’s arm’:

“Ah I am dreading this (screening); I will faint before that metal is inserted” (elderly woman, participant observation).

Knowledge and awareness on cervical cancer screening at the integrated services sites improved from phases 1 to 3. Findings from participant observations suggested less pronounced misconceptions about cervical cancer screening in the last quarter of phase 1 through to phases 2 and 3 as more women screened and shared more positive experiences at the clinic waiting areas and presumably in the community. However, knowledge and awareness on cervical cancer screening remained somewhat limited at the public-private-partnership site across all phases as cervical cancer screening services were not offered on site until phase 3 when PSI-Z started offering services once a week on an outreach basis. Though the media particularly radio and television were frequently cited (44 and 51 references respectively) as sources of information on cervical cancer screening, the radio was often perceived as limited in catching the attention of the target audience.

Women who portrayed substantial knowledge about cervical cancer were positive about screening. Knowledge about cervical cancer was acquired at the integrated clinics and through the media, peers, and community discussions. It was evident that women who had been screened portrayed deeper knowledge about cervical cancer compared to women who had not been screened as they gave accurate descriptions of the importance of screening and the procedures involved. It follows that this good knowledge was enhanced by having gone through the procedure, but there was evidence that many women who took up cervical cancer screening had good prior knowledge which informed their decisions to take up cervical cancer screening.

On the other hand, we found that women who had not taken up screening had poor knowledge, with poor understanding of the importance/value of screening, with evidence of several myths and misinformation. For example, there was the view that screening was not necessary for young women as cervical cancer only affected older women; and for some women the absence of symptoms meant one was not at risk, which in their view made it unnecessary to take up screening.

“I am still young; I don’t think I have any chances of getting cancer… it affects our mothers who are grown up.” (IDI 28-year-old married woman; did not take up cervical cancer screening).

A recurrent theme from both clinic attendees’ interviews and participant observations was the fact that cervical cancer screening was viewed as a complex surgical operation that carried a lot of risks, suggesting that the decision to take up cervical cancer screening was a big one. Linked to this was the perception that the cervix is very fragile and should not be tampered with – with fear that ‘tampering’ would cause post-screening complications or even cervical cancer itself.

“…we discussed with others, and they said being checked causes you to have cancer… when you go for the second time to be checked you will find out that you now have cancer…” (IDI 25-year-old woman, did not take up cervical cancer screening).

Fear of a positive cancer diagnosis

The fear of a positive cancer diagnosis was widely reported as a barrier to uptake as this was thought to be stressful.

“I’m afraid that maybe I have it. It’s not that I am afraid to get screened, what is frightening is the thought that it (cervical cancer) may already be on you…and that makes you feel scared” (IDI 32- year-old woman, did not take up cervical cancer screening).

Perception of risk

Some women reported that their own personal experiences motivated them to attend screening, for instance, if they had cared for someone with cervical cancer or knew someone who had suffered or even died from it. This gave women insight into the gravity of disease and emphasized the need for screening and treatment. Observing suspicious symptoms and knowing that this was a possible sign of disease also prompted screening.

“I was feeling some pains like a person who is in labour then I decided to come and get screened to find out (IDI 24- year-old married woman, took up cervical cancer screening).

The importance of spouses

Women reported having discussed cervical cancer screening with their partners who approved and encouraged them to take it up.

“He said it (screening) is right because he wants to…to know so that we can preserve our lives.” (IDI 26 -year-old married woman, took up cervical cancer screening).

Though male partners were mostly supportive of cervical cancer screening, some women reported that their partners had poor knowledge of the procedure with some portraying the views that cervical cancer screening was for sick and elderly women and that if cervical cancer does not run in the family screening was not necessary.

Health workers views on cervical cancer screening service provision

The importance of training

Health workers at the PSI-Z sites appreciated the training they received on cervical cancer screening pre-implementation as this enabled provision of diversified services beyond provision of HIV services. This diversity was a source of motivation. Health workers at the public–private partnership site, where a different model of service provision was implemented, revealed that they had limited knowledge about cervical cancer screening, which was evident from the not- so- detailed explanations of the screening and treatment procedures. Health workers at this clinic reported that they had not been trained in cervical cancer screening service provision until phase 2, and even then, only one nurse was trained. This training gap necessitated referring women who needed screening services to the few centralised public health facilities and PSI-Z static clinic that provided the service in the city.

Views on integrated services

In both focus group discussions and interviews, health workers at the integrated services sites corroborated women’s views that integration improved awareness and access to cervical cancer screening and they proposed recommendations on how to improve the integrated model of cervical cancer screening service provision – mainly calls for expanding the capacity for cervical cancer screening so that more women could be helped.

“…more space and more health workers then it means more women are going to access the service…because there is really a huge demand for this service” (FGD 31-year-old female nurse counsellor).

Additionally, health workers thought the provision of Loop electrosurgical excision procedure (LEEP) in addition to cryotherapy would constitute a more complete package to cater for clients who could not afford to pay for referral LEEP services.

Health workers expressed dissatisfaction with lack of systems to follow up on clients referred for LEEP services to check if they accessed the services they were referred for:

“Yes, the way we cannot track that client and find out finally what happened … maybe you have, you really have interest in that client, but you cannot really follow up the client and see what happened.” (FGD 51-year-old male nurse counsellor).

At the public-private partnership site, health workers felt integration was partial in that women who needed cervical cancer screening were largely referred elsewhere with only a few women accessing services onsite through the PSI-Z once- a -week outreach initiative that started operating in phase 3.

Barriers to cervical cancer screening

Although health workers at the integrated sites said they were confident that they gave clinic attendees correct information on cervical cancer screening, as echoed by clinic attendees themselves, they were concerned about the spread of inaccurate and misleading information through women discussions in waiting areas which they said counteracted the accurate information they gave.

“…clients take what other clients say most of the times and that’s what they go out with so at times we really have to counsel them and really clear the myths and the misconceptions that they will be having”. (FGD 35-year-old female nurse counsellor).

In addition, health workers felt that the invasive nature of the screening process made some women feel uncomfortable thereby evading screening.

“…it’s the procedure that is done on the most sensitive part of the person and they (women) think maybe it’s too much exposure.” (IDI 41- year-old female nurse counsellor).

Demand exceeded supply

Health workers at the integrated sites felt they provided a valuable and highly sought - after service, with demand exceeding supply. Health workers reported feeling overwhelmed by the increased demand for cervical cancer screening services, which was more pronounced in phases 2 and 3:

“… you would find we have got a huge influx of clients, …looking at the instruments that you have you will find you cannot eh give her a service on that day so she will have to come back another other day to access the service”. (IDI 33- year-old female nurse counsellor).

Health workers attributed the increased demand to the popularity of cervical cancer screening services among women and awareness that the service was offered for free. Health workers revealed doubling and trebling of the number of clients served per day in phases 2 and 3, which they thought further stretched the already limited staff, space, and equipment. As mitigation, health workers reported a shift from a walk-in to a booking system to allow for proper planning and efficient provision of services. The provision of cervical cancer screening outreach services by two of the three New Start Centres was favourably viewed by health workers who felt this increased reach and eased pressure at the sites.

Discussion

In this study including serial multi-methods enquiries, we describe trends in uptake of cervical cancer screening and explore views on cervical cancer screening and its provision in integrated settings among health workers and women attending PSI-Z clinics. Program data showed increased uptake of cervical cancer screening up to the maximum capacity that could be managed by the clinics.

We found that HIV prevalence among urban women who underwent cervical cancer screening in this study was 28%, twice the 14% prevalence among urban women aged 15–49, a finding of the Zimbabwe Population-based HIV Impact Assessment (ZIMPHIA), 2019–2020 [29]. This higher rate likely reflects the fact that screening was conducted in clinics primarily offering HIV services, attracting women at greater HIV risk.

Integration enhanced education and dissemination of information on cervical cancer screening and thus enhancing uptake. The convenience of accessing cervical cancer screening on a single visit under one roof motivated women who would not have otherwise sought screening. Throughout the study, demand for services exceeded supply as evidenced by long appointments and clinic waiting times. Both health workers and clinic attendees reported that availability of services for free increased demand.

Cervical cancer screening services in public health facilities in Zimbabwe are provided free - of - charge or at a nominal fee. However, treatment services remain inadequately funded and financially out of reach for many women in need of services [30–32]. At PSI-Z sites screening services were provided free - of - charge through donor funding which may not be sustainable in view of the declining global donor funding [33]. We highlight the need for a more sustainable approach to the provision of cervical cancer screening services that leverages on educating women and creating demand for screening and treatment services, coupled with the introduction of minimal user fees in the long term, a model that has proven to work elsewhere. There is evidence of willingness to pay for screening services among women in low - and middle- income countries (LMICs) [34, 35], even in the absence of donor support [36], suggesting potential for affordable and sustainable models of cervical cancer service provision in LMICs. Factors such as age, educational level, income, high risk sexual behaviour, awareness of cervical cancer, belief in the risk of cervical cancer and the effectiveness of prevention methods influenced willingness to pay [34].

There was evidence that clinic attendees and communities sometimes shared inaccurate information about cervical cancer screening, including during waiting room discussions, which negatively impacted decisions to take up screening. These myths formed the major barriers to cervical cancer screening.

The influence and importance of knowledge of cervical cancer screening on uptake of screening has been reported in other studies [37–39]. As programs disseminate correct information about services, it is important to be aware of misinformation that happens in communications so that interventions that tackle this are implemented. We found that some discussions that happened in waiting rooms at the clinics undermined the information given out by health workers, which underscores the importance of interventions that prevent this, such as screening informative videos that address common myths, or providing reliable written information. The use of trained volunteer mentors/peer educators to disseminate accurate information on cervical cancer screening to other women in the community could help improve knowledge and this has been successfully implemented in other contexts [40–42]. Such initiatives could address other concerns that were raised in the study, including discomfort about the invasive nature of the cervical cancer screening procedure, fear of pain, fear of a positive diagnosis and uncertainty about possible harms of screening, which have all been commonly cited elsewhere [43–46].

The theme of discomfort with the screening procedure, in particular the use of the speculum, is frequently reported by women as one of the reasons for not screening. However, recent technological advances using methods such as HPV-DNA self-sampling, have been found feasible and acceptable among women in Zimbabwe and elsewhere [47–50] who perceive the procedures as less invasive and less uncomfortable, which will likely increase uptake of screening. HPV-DNA testing is a laboratory test in which cells are scrapped from the cervix to screen for deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) of human papillomaviruses (HPV) [51].

Demand exceeded supply at the New Start Centres. This is likely because of suboptimal provision of cervical cancer screening at public clinics [8, 14, 45]. Building the capacity of public clinics to provide cervical cancer screening could be a solution: in the final phase of this study there was evidence that the private - public - partnership initiative had potential to improve uptake. There is need to optimise implementation of such initiatives. We note that women who needed LEEP services were referred for external services. However, health workers felt that they needed assurance that women accessed the services they were referred for. Having a referral tracking and follow-up system in place could have optimised linkage to post-screening care.

The importance of spousal support for cervical cancer screening is apparent in this and other studies [52–55]. Though male partners were mostly supportive of their spouses undergoing cervical cancer screening, some had poor knowledge of the importance of screening, and this has been reported elsewhere [46, 56, 57]. This underscores the need to target men as well as women with information, education, and communication on cervical cancer screening.

The strengths of this study include its serial nature which allowed the exploration of how views of women and health workers evolved over time. We employed triangulation which confirmed findings across methods and participant groups: women’s views were triangulated with the perspectives of health workers and insights from participant observations provided a nuanced understanding of the processes and experiences of women and health workers at the clinics and how these shaped views and experiences shared through interviews and focus group discussions.

Limitations include the fact that women’s views were self-reported and being clients at the clinics might have censored them in sharing some of their experiences, as they would reflect negatively on service provision and possibly interfere with their relations with staff at the New Start Centres. We endeavoured to minimise this by explaining that study staff were separate from program teams and conducting the interviews and focus group discussions in private.

Although we put in place measures to minimise inconsistences in coding, we acknowledge that employing independent coders could have enhanced inter-coder reliability.

We acknowledge that the data we report was collected 12 years back. However, we observe that our findings from evaluating an integrated screen - and - treat program are still relevant in understanding barriers and facilitators to cervical cancer screening and treatment in LMIC settings, with barriers including poor health seeking behaviour, low perceived personal risk, fear of a positive diagnosis, unbearable costs for accessing services, and suboptimal follow-up systems for referred clients [31, 58]. We note that lessons learnt from integrating cervical cancer screening with other sexual and reproductive health services in this study, namely, enhanced information dissemination, and the convenience of accessing multiple services under one roof remain critical in the context of a drive for improving integration of health services.

Conclusion

Integration of cervical cancer screening, HIV and sexual and reproductive health services is highly feasible and acceptable and was found to increase access to, and coverage of cervical cancer screening among women. The integrated model can be optimised by implementing interventions that promote sharing of accurate information on cervical cancer screening, among both women and men. Implementing referral tracking and follow-up systems involving simple e-health platforms such as Short Message Service (SMS) could strengthen linkage to post-screening care. There is need to increase capacity for cervical cancer screening services – public-private partnerships have potential to drive this.
