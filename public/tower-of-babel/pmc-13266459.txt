To the Editor:

Asthma is a common chronic lung disease in children, and those with severe asthma suffer from poor symptom control despite treatment plan adherence [1]. Severe asthma is a heterogenous disease associated with different inflammatory phenotypes, and the most common phenotype is characterised by increased T-helper 2 (Th-2) cytokines and mediators [1–3]. Release of these biomarkers leads to increased airflow obstruction, airway hyperresponsiveness, mucus production, and remodelling responses; leading to increased symptoms, exacerbations, school absenteeism and decreased quality of life [4, 5]. Although the incidence of severe paediatric asthma is low, the consequences of uncontrolled, severe asthma have significant personal, financial and societal impacts [2, 4]. Therefore, children with uncontrolled severe asthma despite optimised treatments can consider monoclonal antibody (mAb) therapies (“biologics”) [1, 5, 6]. Asthma biologics target biomarkers in the Th-2 inflammatory cascade pathway to suppress asthma pathogenesis [2, 4]. Currently, there are four biologics approved for children (of 6 to 18 years of age) in Canada: omalizumab, mepolizumab, tezepelumab and dupilumab. Each biologic targets a specific inflammatory marker, and health outcomes depend on patients’ underlying level of these different markers [2, 3].

The objective of this study is to compare health outcomes and quantify the patterns of change in healthcare services before and after use of asthma biologics in children with severe asthma.

This is a cohort observational study, utilising data available on the Observational Medical Outcomes Partnership Common Data Model (OMOP-CDM) [7], housed at the Hospital for Sick Children (SickKids), in Toronto, Ontario, Canada (REB#2814). The OMOP-CDM platform carries deidentified, validated data linked to SickKids’ internal electronic medical records. Patient inclusion criteria were: 1) aged 6 to 18 years at biologics treatment; 2) has physician-diagnosed severe asthma; 3) received any of the available four biologics (exposure variable) between 1 June, 2019 and 1 February, 2023; and 4) has at least 1-year of health outcomes before and after biologic initiation. Health outcomes included emergency department (ED) visits, asthma-related hospitalisations, medication use, pulmonary function tests, and serum eosinophil (EOS) levels. Only patients with the exposure and outcome variables at both timepoints were included; therefore, patients with missing exposure and outcome variables were excluded from the analysis. Odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals and interrupted time series (ITS) were conducted using RStudio (RStudio Inc, Boston, MA, USA) to quantify change, where p<0.05 was statistically significant.

A total of 1 928 118 records were available on OMOP-CDM, of which 20 188 were asthma observations and 7178 were diagnosed asthma. Only 44 (0.61%) received biologics for their severe asthma. The mean age was 13.3 (±3.3) years and 23 (52%) were males. Three types of biologics were prescribed in this cohort: 30 (68.1%) used omalizumab; 11 (25.0%) used dupilumab and three used (6.8%) mepolizumab. None used tezepelumab, likely because tezepelumab was only recently approved in Canada (in October 2022, after the study period had begun). After 1-year of biologics, the proportion of study cohort using inhaled and oral corticosteroids decreased by 30.8% (88.6% to 61.4%) and 17.6% (77.3% to 63.6%), respectively. Before biologics, 17 (39%) children used >1 inhaled corticosteroid but after 1-year of biologics, this proportion decreased to 8 (18%). Total and mean healthcare utilisation decreased after 1-year of biologics. Total ED visits decreased from 42 to 3 (OR=14.0, 95% CI 4.47–70.61). Mean ED visits after a year decreased from 3.0 to 0.2, with a significant absolute effect of −5.2 (95% CI −8.0–−2.4). Similarly, total asthma-related hospitalisation decreased from 26 to 3 (OR=8.67, 95% CI 2.66–44.74). Mean hospitalisations decreased from 2.0 to 0.2, with a significant absolute effect of −2.4 (95% CI −4.3– −0.58). Relative reductions in ED visits and hospitalisations were 96.3% and 92.6%, respectively. Mean serum EOS levels also had a significant absolute effect (−329 (95% CI −524–−92)) after 1-year, decreasing from 535 to 399 cells·μL−1. As dupilumab may have limited benefits on serum EOS levels, a sensitivity analysis excluding dupilumab users showed mean serum EOS levels decreased from 540 to 379 cells·μL−1. However, pulmonary function test changes were not statistically significant. ITS showed mean ED visits, hospitalisations and serum EOS levels would have continued to increase if biologics were not administered (table 1).

Our findings align with other studies reporting similar health outcomes post-biologics. These three biologics have shown effectiveness in reducing asthma exacerbations, serum EOS levels and corticosteroid usage, especially in children with high Th-2 biomarkers [2, 4, 8] and serum EOS levels >150 cells·μL−1 [6]. However, each biologic has slightly different indications and may lead to unique benefits. Of the three, omalizumab has been available longer, and is used frequently while demonstrating great effectiveness [6, 9]. Omalizumab targets IgE to downregulate mast cell activation involved in eosinophilia and airway remodelling, reducing corticosteroids use and asthma exacerbations [4, 6, 9].

In this cohort, changes in lung function parameters were not statistically significant. Since most included patients used omalizumab, this may explain the modest and inconsistent lung function improvement observed. This aligns with current evidence where forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1)% improvements and overall lung function were inconsistently observed in children on omalizumab and mepolizumab [2–4, 10]. In contrast, dupilumab can decrease exacerbations in children (aged 6 to 11 years) while improving lung function, especially those with Th-2 phenotype, elevated serum EOS (≥500 cells·μL−1) and exhaled nitric oxide fraction (≥20 to 25 ppb) [3, 4, 11].

Despite biologics’ high costs, our findings provide additional insight into the growing evidence of their effectiveness in minimising asthma burden [8, 10]. We observed a statistically significant decrease in overall corticosteroid use, ED visits, hospitalisations and serum EOS levels after 1-year of biologics. The novel approach of ITS predicted alarming counterfactual effects at an aggregated cohort level, likely resulting in poor health outcomes if biologics were not prescribed. Therefore, in cases of severe asthma, when modifiable factors have already been addressed, timely initiation of biologics should be considered [5, 12]. Since asthma presents as a heterogenous disease in children, it is crucial to consider different biologics based on their targeted mechanisms to offer the best personalised management [2, 4]. Changes in biologic prescriptions over time were observed in past studies, where usage was likely tailored to children's coexisting conditions and evolving needs [13]. Therefore, continuous monitoring and tailoring of biologics may be necessary to ensure personalised treatment [12–14].

This cohort included a small sample size of children with severe asthma using biologics between 2019 to 2023. This sample size may seem unusually small, but may not be as uncommon as it seems, as observed in the review by Chaverri Repáraz et al. [13], conducted at the Pediatric Severe Asthma Unit at the University Hospital of Navarra, Spain. Other factors to explain this small size could be the small proportion of severe asthma being treated by biologics, and patient hesitancy in Canada [15, 16]. These barriers could include accessibility and costs, along with patients and parents’ knowledge of biologics as a potential option, and perceptions towards biologics’ effectiveness and safety [15, 16]. Future studies need to confirm these observations in larger sample sizes and for longer durations, and in individuals from diverse backgrounds [10]. Since each biologic has specific eligibility and mechanisms of action, evaluating their unique health outcomes is necessary [3, 4]. Additionally, identifying required biomarkers, optimal doses, and the best time to initiate, wean and terminate biologics in children is paramount [14].

This study utilised the OMOP-CDM platform, a local electronic medical records data registry and an increasingly popular avenue to access real world data (RWD) [7]. Although results from randomised controlled trials (RCTs) are informative in shaping guidelines and best practices, individuals with severe asthma enrolled in RCTs are usually not reflective of the general population, limiting generalisability and external validity [17, 18]. Therefore, there is greater recognition for complementing RCTs findings with RWD, which standardises data curation for effective utilisation and research [18]. Inception of national and international asthma RWD registries over the years have aimed at improving data collation to create large datasets in order to enhance statistical power for answering research questions [19]. Compilation of RWD across geographical locations (such as the International Severe Asthma Registry, with over 29 countries’ RWD on severe asthma) is necessary, since local sample sizes are usually small and lack statistical power [19]. The limitations noted in our experience with SickKids’ OMOP-CDM platform are consistent with current challenges and gaps of registries having a low proportion of children's RWD, their baseline characteristics and external confounders [17, 19].

A few points from our study warrant attention. This pre–post cohort study included the same patients and health outcomes at baseline and 1-year follow-up, controlling for their baseline differences. However, confounders such as seasonality, life events, race and ethnicity, geographical residence, comorbidities and socioeconomic status could have influenced our findings. Unfortunately, these factors were unavailable on the OMOP-CDM platform, preventing direct adjustments to confounders in this analysis. In addition, asthma medication doses were also unavailable in this dataset, preventing further comparisons of their change at the pre- and post- timepoints.

There are several other limitations. The small sample size limits broad generalisability and applicability. Subgroup analysis was also not possible to assess each biologic's distinct benefits. Additionally, at the time of our study, ITS approach was performed at an aggregated cohort level, but if a larger sample size in the future is available, ITS analysis will be considered on an individual trajectory. Mostly children ≥12 years using omalizumab were included, with limited information on other biomarker levels and corticosteroid and biologic dosages over time. Decreased healthcare utilisation could also be influenced by the pandemic, which coincided with the study period. Lastly, covariate information and patient-reported outcomes such as quality of life, symptoms and asthma control were not available on OMOP-CDM for further comparisons.

In conclusion, biologics can improve asthma control in children with severe asthma, seen in decreased corticosteroids use, healthcare utilisation, and serum EOS, while minimal changes in lung function were observed. Future research needs to assess the impact of biologics on patient-reported outcomes and explore the best timing for initiating and terminating biologics in children.
