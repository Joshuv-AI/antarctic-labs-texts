Evaluation of disinfection efficiency of new high oxygen membrane air disinfecting machine in hospital ward environment
Authors: Zhongna Fu, Qian Liu, Mengjie Shuang, Jiao Li, Qing Wang, Hui Liu, Lijuan Xiong, Xiongjing Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To investigate the air disinfection efficacy of a novel high-oxygen membrane air sterilizer in hospital ward environments. Natural bacteria were used as indicator microorganisms, and air disinfection experiments were conducted under both unoccupied and occupied ward conditions to compare the actual disinfection performance between a high-oxygen membrane air sterilizer and a plasma air sterilizer. The relationship between disinfection efficacy and factors such as ward volume, temperature, humidity, initial bacterial load, and personnel movement was analyzed. Under unoccupied conditions, the natural bacterial extinction rate of the high-oxygen membrane air disinfecting machine was 88.4%, significantly higher than the 82.5% of the plasma air disinfecting machine (P = 0.019). However, under occupied conditions, there was no statistically significant difference in extinction rates between the two devices (80.0% vs. 78.7%, P = 0.165). Additionally, the disinfection efficacy of the high-oxygen membrane air disinfecting machine was not statistically affected by ward volume, temperature, humidity, initial bacterial concentration, or personnel activity (all P > 0.05) within the present experimental settings. Although high-oxygen membrane air disinfection technology demonstrates statistically superior disinfection performance in unoccupied environments, the observed efficacy does not currently support its use as a standalone intervention for reducing the airborne infection transmission. Further research in diverse clinical environments is needed to fully assess its potential.

Introduction

Airborne pathogens in hospital environments are significant vectors for respiratory infections. According to the World Health Organization, over 10 million tuberculosis cases occur globally each year1, and the emergence of various new airborne diseases continues to pose a threat to human health2. The necessity for improving hospital air quality management is becoming increasingly evident. Currently, ventilation is the primary method for enhancing indoor air quality, but hospital buildings often have issues such as high personnel density and poor ventilation efficiency. Natural ventilation fails to keep air pollutant concentrations within safe limits, while mechanical ventilation systems are typically insufficient to meet the 6–12 times per hour air exchange rate required for infection control3. Studies have shown that the proper use of air disinfection units can significantly reduce bacterial colony counts in medical environments, thereby lowering the risk of airborne disease transmission4,5 and reducing the likelihood of patient infection6. Therefore, the use of air disinfection units in densely populated areas and high-risk departments of medical institutions is particularly important.

Existing air disinfection technologies primarily rely on physical methods (such as High-Efficiency Particulate Air (HEPA) filtration; ultraviolet (UV) light); chemical methods (such as ozone; hydrogen peroxide); or other principles (such as plasma; photocatalysis)7,8. However, these technologies generally have significant limitations. Chemical disinfectants can cause secondary pollution. HEPA filters only remove particulate matter through physical interception, with limited removal capacity for gaseous pollutants (e.g., formaldehyde and volatile organic compounds (VOCs)). UV irradiation exhibits non-uniform spatial distribution with localized exposure gaps; and plasma equipment is susceptible to environmental humidity. Moreover, most technologies cannot maintain continuous disinfection in occupied environments9,10. This is especially true for medical facilities that require 24-hour operation, where issues such as high energy consumption and high maintenance costs are more pronounced.

To overcome the limitations of existing air disinfection technologies, this study has developed a new type of air disinfection device based on high-oxygen membrane technology. The core component of the system is a composite filter membrane with efficient oxidation performance, which achieves air purification through the following mechanisms: (1) using strong oxidation to disrupt microbial enzyme systems, achieving highly effective inactivation of pathogenic microorganisms; (2) simultaneously degrading harmful gaseous pollutants such as formaldehyde, ammonia, and hydrogen sulfide. SGS certification results show that the content of this material in 211 Substances of Very High Concern (SVHC) are all lower than 0.1% (w/w) (Test Report No.: SHCPCH210201241E); (3) employing a composite filtration structure that ensures efficient retention of particulate matter (including viruses and other microorganisms) while significantly reducing system wind resistance. Compared to traditional disinfection methods, this system offers significant advantages. It does not produce ozone or other harmful by-products during operation, avoids charge accumulation and does not interfere with medical precision instruments. It features an integrated design requiring no auxiliary equipment; and it achieves coexistence between humans and machines.

This study aims to investigate the disinfection effectiveness of high-oxygen membrane air sterilizers in actual hospital application scenarios. By comparing the extinction rate of natural bacteria in the air and potential influencing factors between high-oxygen membrane air sterilizers and traditional plasma air sterilizers, it verifies the feasibility and effectiveness of their application in air disinfection at medical institutions, promoting the transformation of new air disinfection technologies from theory to practice.

Test method

On-site Air Disinfection Experiment: (1) Before the experiment began, install the air disinfection machine according to the installation instructions provided for the device to be tested at the remote end of the selected test site, connect the power supply, and ensure it operates normally. (2) Pre-disinfection Sampling: After the air in the selected enclosed test site has been static for 5 min, use a six-stage sieve air impact sampler to collect natural airborne bacteria as the pre-disinfection sample (positive control). When sampling, set up one sampling point if the test site is ≤ 10 m²; for sites ≥ 10 m², add one additional sampling point for every 10 m² increase, with a maximum of 5 sampling points. For one sampling point, place the six-stage sieve air impact sampler at a height of 1.0 m above the center of the test site. For multiple sampling points, place the sampler diagonally or in a plum blossom pattern, ensuring it is away from the outlet of the disinfection machine and at least 0.5 m and 1.0 m away from the wall when sampling. The sampling flow rate is 28.3 L/min, and the sampling time is 5 min. (3) Disinfection Treatment: Operate the air disinfection machine according to its user manual. (4) Post-disinfection Sampling: After the air disinfection machine has been running for 2 h, collect natural airborne bacteria using the same method as before at the pre-disinfection sampling point. This serves as the post-disinfection test sample. (5) Cultivation and Result Observation: After sampling, perform sterile operations to remove the plates and incubate them in a 36℃±1℃ incubator for 48 h to count live bacteria. At the same time, use the unused culture medium from the same batch to incubate both sets of samples simultaneously as a negative control. If the negative control group has bacterial growth, it indicates that the culture medium used is contaminated and the test is invalid. Replace it and repeat the test. (6) Data processing:

Statistical analysis methods

SPSS21.0 was used for data analysis, and Graphpad Prism software (version 8, San Diego, CA, USA) was employed for plotting. K-S goodness-of-fit tests were conducted on the total bacterial count, extinction rate, and the volume, temperature, humidity, pre-disinfection bacterial load, and the number of fixed and mobile personnel in both unmanned and manned conditions before and after disinfection, using high-oxygen membrane air disinfecting machines and plasma air disinfectors. It was found that only the extinction rate of high-oxygen membrane disinfectors under manned conditions, and the humidity and the number of fixed and mobile personnel under manned conditions were normally distributed, while the rest were non-normally distributed. Normal distribution data were described using arithmetic mean and standard deviation, while non-normal distribution data were described using median and interquartile range. Wilcoxon rank sum tests for two independent samples were used to compare the extinction rates of the two air disinfection devices under unmanned and manned conditions. Analysis of factors affecting the extinction rate of high-oxygen membrane air disinfectors under unmanned and manned conditions was conducted, using Pearson correlation analysis when both variables were normally distributed, and Spearman’s rank correlation when the variables were non-normally distributed.

Discussion

This study represents the first systematic evaluation of the application effectiveness of high-oxygen membrane air disinfection technology in real hospital environments. Experimental data demonstrate that in unoccupied settings, the high-oxygen membrane disinfection device exhibits significant advantages (natural bacteria extinction rate of 88.4% vs. 82.5%, P = 0.019). This outcome may stem from its unique sterilization mechanism: the high-oxygen membrane continuously releases high concentrations of reactive oxygen species (ROS) through nanoscale pores, enabling more effective penetration of microbial cell walls and disruption of their enzyme systems and DNA structures. Notably, neither disinfection device in this study achieved an extinction rate exceeding 90%, which aligns with the clinical research findings of Wu et al.11, suggesting that air disinfection efficacy in actual medical environments may be constrained by multiple factors. Besides, neither of the two disinfection devices achieved the clinically relevant threshold of at least a 90% reduction (1-log step) in airborne contamination, nor the preferred 99% reduction (2-log steps). Both devices performed substantially below the 99.95% benchmark set by HEPA filtration under identical conditions. Although a single conventional unit failed to reach the clinical performance standard, it is worthy of further discussion whether adopting a larger-capacity model or operating multiple units in parallel could achieve qualified disinfection efficacy. What’s more, the small sample size in this study may lead to insufficient statistical power, and the large fluctuation in initial bacterial counts resulted in certain data deviations, both of which may affect the accuracy and reliability of the results. This further indicates that there is inadequate statistical support for the disinfection efficacy of the two devices, indirectly confirming their limited clinical relevance. Thus, these findings are limited to the present experimental setting and do not definitively negate the potential value of either technology. While the observed efficacy of the high-oxygen membrane technology does not currently support its use as a standalone intervention for reducing airborne infection transmission, further research in diverse clinical environments is needed to fully assess its potential.

Compared with previous studies, the disinfection efficiency of the plasma air sterilizer in this study was lower than the 90% extinction rate reported by Li et al.12, Li Xiaoyan et al.13, and Tan et al.9. This difference may be attributed to the following factors: (1) Environmental variable differences: the airtightness of the ward, seasonal temperature and humidity changes, and frequency of personnel activity can all affect the disinfection efficiency8,14; (2) Methodological differences: field studies cannot fully control air flow velocity, lighting, and other interfering factors9; (3) Evaluation criteria differences: there may be variations in sampling time and colony count methods used by different studies. It is noteworthy that under occupied conditions, the disinfection efficiency of the two devices was similar (80.0% vs. 78.7%, P = 0.165), which may be related to air flow promoting the diffusion of plasma, thereby enhancing the disinfection efficiency of the plasma air sterilizer15.

In addition, this study found that, whether in unoccupied or occupied conditions, the extinction rate of high-oxygen membrane air disinfection units showed no significant statistical correlation with the volume, temperature, humidity, total bacterial count before disinfection, and the number of fixed and mobile personnel. However, there are good data on aerosols and droplets in the air and how their behaviour is influenced by temperature, humidity, air flow and so on16,17. We were not able to measure any influence, which contrasts with the current state of the art in the field. This may be attributed to the following factors: first, limited by the small sample size in this study, the statistical power was insufficient, which may have affected the accuracy and reliability of the results; second, the data contained certain biases, with large fluctuations in the initial bacterial load; third, the novel built-in high-performance micro-nano materials used in high-oxygen membrane air disinfection units have adaptive oxidation capabilities, allowing them to maintain stable performance under different environmental conditions. Furthermore, standardized cleaning and disinfection procedures in hospital wards limit environmental variables to a certain extent, with fluctuations in temperature and humidity, total bacterial count before disinfection, and the number of personnel within a specific range, thereby minimizing the impact of these factors on disinfection effectiveness18,19. A limitation of this study is the lack of direct comparison with HEPA filtration, the current gold standard for air disinfection. Future research should include HEPA filtration as a benchmark to better evaluate the clinical relevance of emerging technologies such as the high-oxygen membrane device.

In recent years, with the continuous development of air disinfection technology, more and more studies20–22 have focused on the application of new disinfection materials and their performance in different environments. Air disinfection technology plays a crucial role in controlling hospital infections, especially in high-risk medical settings, where the use of air disinfection units significantly reduces the risk of airborne pathogens23,24.The study primarily focuses on the disinfection effectiveness of high-oxygen membrane air disinfection units over a specific period, but further research is needed to investigate performance changes and disinfection efficacy degradation during long-term use. Additionally, future research should explore the disinfection effectiveness of high-oxygen membrane air disinfection units in different hospitals and various usage scenarios to verify their broad applicability.

Conclusion

While the high‑oxygen membrane air disinfection technology demonstrates statistically superior disinfection performance compared with plasma technology in unoccupied hospital wards, the observed efficacy (less than 90% microbial reduction) did not meet clinically relevant thresholds and therefore does not currently support its use as a standalone intervention for mitigating airborne infection transmission. Nevertheless, future studies should explore whether scaling up the unit size or operating multiple units in parallel could achieve clinically meaningful performance thresholds. Further studies with larger sample sizes and across diverse clinical settings are warranted to fully evaluate its application potential.
