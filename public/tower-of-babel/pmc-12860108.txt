Economic evaluations of Glecaprevir/Pibrentasvir and Grazoprevir/Elbasvir for the treatment of hepatitis C: a systematic review
Authors: Mojtaba Jafari, Parisa Mehdizadeh, Ehsan Teymourzadeh, Mahmood Salesi, Khosro Keshavarz, Mohammad Ali Abyazi, Mohammad Heiat, Seyed Moayed Alavian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesDirect-acting antivirals (DAAs) have revolutionized hepatitis C (HCV) treatment, yet their high cost necessitates rigorous economic evaluation. Glecaprevir/Pibrentasvir (GLE/PIB) and Grazoprevir/Elbasvir (GZR/EBR) are two key, highly effective DAA regimens, but their comparative cost-effectiveness across different healthcare settings remains unclear. This study aims to systematically review and qualitatively synthesize the economic evidence for GLE/PIB and GZR/EBR, comparing them with each other and other relevant HCV treatments.MethodsA systematic review was conducted by searching PubMed, Scopus, Embase, Science Direct, and the Cochrane Library for full economic evaluation studies published up to September 2025. The quality of included studies was assessed using the CHEERS checklist. Due to significant heterogeneity in study contexts, populations, and methodologies, a qualitative narrative synthesis was performed, and quantitative pooling of economic outcomes was avoided.ResultsA total of 20 economic evaluation studies were included. Both GLE/PIB and GZR/EBR were consistently found to be cost-effective or dominant strategies when compared to older interferon-based therapies. However, the five head-to-head studies comparing GLE/PIB and GZR/EBR yielded conflicting results: GLE/PIB was the economically preferred option in settings like Japan and Brazil, whereas GZR/EBR was favored in analyses from China and Hong Kong. This variability appears to be primarily driven by differences in local drug acquisition prices.ConclusionNeither GLE/PIB nor GZR/EBR can be considered universally more cost-effective than the other. Both regimens represent valuable advancements over previous standards of care, but the optimal economic choice between them is highly context-dependent. These findings underscore the necessity of using local, setting-specific economic evaluations to inform clinical guidelines and reimbursement policies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12962-025-00700-1.

Introduction

The Hepatitis C virus (HCV) represents a significant global public health challenge, causing a spectrum of liver diseases that can progress to cirrhosis and liver cancer [1, 2]. The World Health Organization has previously estimated that 130–150 million people are chronically infected worldwide, placing a substantial clinical and economic burden on healthcare systems [1, 2]. While the prevalence of HCV is decreasing in some developed nations, it remains a growing concern in many developing countries, with varied distribution across different societies [1–5]. For instance, in the United States, an estimated 3.2 million people are affected [6]. HCV exhibits significant genetic diversity, with six major genotypes; genotype 1 is particularly common, accounting for approximately 70% of chronic infections [6]. Although acute HCV infection is often mild, a large proportion of patients (55–85%) develop the chronic form of the disease [7], with higher risks observed in populations with underlying conditions such as thalassemia, diabetes, or hemophilia [8, 9]. Early diagnosis and effective antiviral therapy are critical to halt disease progression, prevent severe liver complications, and reduce associated healthcare costs [10, 11].

Historically, the standard of care for HCV involved a combination of peginterferon-alpha and ribavirin, a regimen that required long treatment durations (24–48 weeks) and was associated with significant side effects and suboptimal cure rates [11–13]. The landscape of HCV treatment was transformed by the development of direct-acting antivirals (DAAs). These novel agents, which target specific viral proteins like NS3-4 A protease or the NS5A viral protein, have revolutionized therapy by offering shorter, interferon-free regimens with markedly higher efficacy and fewer adverse effects [14–18]. This new generation of therapies includes various combinations such as sofosbuvir/ledipasvir, sofosbuvir/velpatasvir, and daclatasvir/asunaprevir [19, 20]. The superior clinical outcomes of DAAs, especially for patients who did not respond to previous interferon-based treatments, have been well-established [21]. However, their high acquisition cost has made economic evaluations a crucial component for healthcare decision-making, leading to numerous studies on their cost-effectiveness [4, 10, 22, 23].

Among the array of available DAAs, Glecaprevir/Pibrentasvir (GLE/PIB) and Grazoprevir/Elbasvir (GZR/EBR) have emerged as two key therapeutic options. Several studies have highlighted their potential economic value; for example, a 2020 study in Japan suggested that GLE/PIB was more cost-effective than other DAAs like sofosbuvir/ledipasvir and GZR/EBR [24], while a 2021 study, also in Japan, found GZR/EBR to have a favorable ICER in certain patient populations [22]. Despite these individual assessments, there is a need for a comprehensive overview of their comparative economic standing. A previous systematic review from 2020 focused solely on the cost-effectiveness of GZR/EBR [25], and to our knowledge, no systematic review has comprehensively synthesized the economic evidence for both GLE/PIB and GZR/EBR and directly compared their economic profiles against each other and the wider DAA landscape. This study aims to fill that gap by systematically reviewing and appraising the evidence from economic evaluations of these two important regimens.

Methods

This systematic review was conducted and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. The review protocol was developed a priori but was not registered with an external registry such as PROSPERO.

Search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted in PubMed, Scopus, Embase, Science Direct, and the Cochrane Library to identify all relevant studies published up to September 2025. The search strategy was developed using a combination of MeSH terms and keywords related to the interventions (“Glecaprevir/Pibrentasvir”, “Grazoprevir/Elbasvir”) and the outcome (“economic evaluation”, “cost-effectiveness”, “cost-utility”). To ensure its comprehensiveness, the search strategy was piloted to confirm it could identify a set of known key articles on the topic. The search was limited to articles published in the English language. Additionally, the reference lists of included studies and relevant review articles were manually screened for any additional eligible publications. The full search strategy for each database is provided in Appendix 1.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies were included if they were full economic evaluations (cost-effectiveness, cost-utility, or cost-benefit analyses) that compared Glecaprevir/Pibrentasvir (GLE/PIB) or Grazoprevir/Elbasvir (GZR/EBR) with each other or any other relevant comparator for the treatment of chronic hepatitis C. Eligible studies had to report primary outcomes such as an incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) or an Incremental Net Monetary Benefit (INMB).

Exclusion criteria were: [1] studies not published in English; [2] non-human (animal) studies; [3] studies that did not evaluate the specified interventions or focused on indications other than HCV; [4] review articles, editorials, letters, and conference abstracts without sufficient data; and [5] duplicate publications.

Study selection and data extraction

All retrieved citations were imported into EndNote X7, and duplicates were removed. Two reviewers (MJ and PM) independently screened the titles and abstracts of all unique records against the eligibility criteria. The full texts of potentially relevant articles were then retrieved and assessed independently by the same two reviewers. Any disagreements at either stage were resolved through discussion and consensus. If a consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer (KK) acted as an arbiter to make the final decision.

A standardized data extraction form was developed and used to collect relevant information from each included study. This included data on authors, year, country, study perspective, patient population characteristics, interventions and comparators, economic model type, time horizon, discount rates, costs, outcomes (e.g., QALYs), reported ICERs, and the study’s main conclusions.

Quality assessment

The methodological quality of the included economic evaluations was assessed using the 24-item Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards (CHEERS) checklist. Two reviewers independently performed the quality assessment. Studies were categorized based on their adherence to the checklist items, but no study was excluded based on its quality score alone. Instead, the quality assessment results were used to inform the narrative synthesis and interpretation of the findings.

Data synthesis and analysis

Due to the significant heterogeneity across studies—in terms of country settings, healthcare system perspectives, patient populations, model assumptions, and methodologies—a quantitative meta-analysis of economic outcomes was deemed inappropriate and potentially misleading. Therefore, a qualitative narrative synthesis was performed to summarize and compare the findings.

To facilitate comparison, all reported costs were converted to 2024 US dollars using the Consumer Price Index (CPI) for medical care for the respective country, using a web-based cost converter tool [26]. We report the ICERs and Incremental Net Monetary Benefits (INMBs) as presented in the original studies. The term INMB is used instead of “incremental net benefit” for greater precision, calculated as INMB = (λ × ΔE) - ΔC, where ΔE is the incremental effectiveness (typically in QALYs), ΔC is the incremental cost, and λ is the willingness-to-pay (WTP) threshold [30–32].

Crucially, rather than applying a uniform WTP threshold, we prioritized the thresholds reported by the authors of each study, as these are context-specific. When a threshold was not explicitly stated, we noted this as a characteristic of the study, and for interpretative purposes only, considered the World Health Organization’s recommendation of using a range of 1 to 3 times the country’s GDP per capita [27–29]. The results are presented descriptively, focusing on identifying patterns and explaining discrepancies in findings based on study characteristics.

Results

Search results and study selection

The literature search yielded 148 records. Following the removal of duplicates and screening of titles, abstracts, and full texts, a final set of 20 economic evaluation studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the qualitative synthesis. To facilitate a standardized comparison, all costs were converted to 2024 US dollars, and the Incremental Cost-Effectiveness Ratios (ICERs) and Incremental Net Monetary Benefits (INMBs) were reported for each comparison. An intervention was generally considered cost-effective if its ICER was below the local willingness-to-pay (WTP) threshold, which corresponds to a positive INMB. This detailed quantitative data, available in the Appendix (Table A8), reveals a significant variability in economic outcomes across different settings. The following narrative synthesis summarizes these findings qualitatively.

A) Head-to-Head Comparisons: GLE/PIB versus GZR/EBR

The results from the five studies that directly compared GLE/PIB and GZR/EBR were conflicting and highly context-dependent. In analyses from Japan [24] and Brazil [36], GLE/PIB was the economically superior option, being either dominant (more effective and less costly) or cost-saving, resulting in a large positive INMB. In stark contrast, analyses from China [33, 35] and Hong Kong [34] concluded that GZR/EBR was the more cost-effective strategy. In these settings, the ICER for GLE/PIB versus GZR/EBR far exceeded local WTP thresholds, resulting in a significant negative INMB and indicating it was not an economically favorable choice. This variability underscores the critical role of local drug pricing in determining cost-effectiveness.

B) GZR/EBR versus Other Comparators

GZR/EBR was consistently reported as a highly cost-effective or dominant strategy when compared to older standards of care like PegIFN/RBV [40, 44, 45]. Its dominance in these comparisons was reflected by substantial health gains paired with cost savings, leading to large positive INMBs. When compared against other modern DAAs, the results were mixed. While it often demonstrated favorable ICERs against regimens like 3DR [42, 46], it was reported as dominated by all other DAA comparators in one comprehensive US-based study [50]. Being dominated is the least favorable economic outcome, as the intervention is both more costly and less effective.

C) GLE/PIB versus Other Comparators

Similar to GZR/EBR, GLE/PIB was demonstrated to be a dominant strategy when compared against older interferon-based regimens, yielding significant cost savings and positive INMBs [37]. The recent analysis from Australia [51] further confirmed its strong economic value, reporting a very low ICER well below accepted WTP thresholds. When compared to other modern DAAs, a US-based study found that while clinically effective, GLE/PIB was not a cost-saving option compared to certain sofosbuvir-based regimens [38], resulting in a negative INMB in that context.

Discussion

Summary of main findings

This systematic review synthesized evidence from 20 economic evaluations of Glecaprevir/Pibrentasvir (GLE/PIB) and Grazoprevir/Elbasvir (GZR/EBR). The findings consistently demonstrate that both regimens are highly cost-effective, and often dominant, when compared to older interferon-based therapies or no treatment. However, the central finding of this review is the significant heterogeneity and conflicting results regarding their comparative cost-effectiveness against each other and other modern direct-acting antivirals (DAAs). No single regimen proved to be universally superior; instead, its economic value is highly dependent on the context of the evaluation.

Interpretation of findings and key drivers of heterogeneity

The variability in cost-effectiveness results is a critical finding that warrants further interpretation. Several key factors appear to drive these divergent conclusions across studies.

First, local drug pricing is likely the most significant factor explaining the conflicting results, particularly in the five head-to-head studies. The observation that GLE/PIB was dominant in Japan [24] and Brazil [36] but not cost-effective in China [33, 35] and Hong Kong [34] strongly suggests that negotiated acquisition costs, rather than minor differences in clinical efficacy, are the primary determinant of cost-effectiveness in these comparisons.

Second, the choice of comparator is fundamental to the outcome of any economic evaluation. Our results show that GZR/EBR was consistently favorable against older regimens [40, 44, 45] but performed poorly when evaluated against a comprehensive slate of alternative modern DAAs in a US-based study [50]. This highlights that a drug’s “value for money” is always relative to the available and relevant alternatives in a specific market.

Third, the characteristics of the target population play a crucial role. The economic value of GZR/EBR, for example, was particularly pronounced in patients with chronic kidney disease [43, 47], a subgroup with high management costs and historically limited treatment options. This underscores the importance of considering specific patient subgroups in reimbursement decisions.

Our findings for GZR/EBR align with a previous systematic review by Liu et al. [25], which also concluded it was a cost-effective option against many comparators. This review, however, expands on the existing literature by including GLE/PIB and more recent evidence, revealing the highly contested economic landscape between these two key regimens.

Strengths and limitations

The primary strength of this review is its comprehensive and up-to-date search across multiple major databases. By standardizing costs and presenting detailed appendix tables, we provide a high degree of transparency. Furthermore, our adherence to a qualitative synthesis allows for a nuanced interpretation of the evidence while avoiding the methodologically inappropriate practice of averaging heterogeneous economic data.

However, this review has limitations that must be acknowledged. First, the search was restricted to English-language studies, which may have introduced a selection bias by excluding relevant evaluations from other regions. Second, the vast majority of included studies adopted a healthcare payer or system perspective, largely ignoring societal costs such as productivity losses. Including a societal perspective could potentially alter the conclusions, likely further in favor of these curative therapies. Finally, our findings are inherently limited by the quality and methodological choices of the primary studies.

Implications for policy and future research

The key implication for policymakers is that a “one-size-fits-all” conclusion about the superior cost-effectiveness of GLE/PIB versus GZR/EBR is not possible. Reimbursement and formulary decisions must be based on local economic evaluations that reflect country-specific drug prices and patient demographics. For future research, there is a clear need for more head-to-head economic evaluations in regions where such evidence is lacking. Studies incorporating a societal perspective are also crucial to capture the full economic value of curing hepatitis C.

Conclusion

In conclusion, both GLE/PIB and GZR/EBR represent valuable and generally cost-effective treatments for chronic HCV compared to older standards of care. The choice between them, however, is not a matter of universal economic superiority but a complex decision that must be tailored to the specific economic and clinical context of each healthcare system.
