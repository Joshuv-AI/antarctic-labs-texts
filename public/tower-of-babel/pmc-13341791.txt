Identification and expression of the Glycyrrhiza uralensis GABA shunt gene family under drought and salt stress with exogenous GABA
Authors: Yuan Jiang, Shuang Liang, Shurui Zhang, Yaping Li, Yifan Yan, Xinying Chen, Yangmei Bao, Zhirong Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlycyrrhiza sp. (licorice) is a high-value medicinal plant, which is primarily distributed in drought and salt environments. However, wild licorice resources are dwindling, and salt and drought stresses reduce biomass, hampering the licorice industry. As a signalling molecule, γ-Aminobutyric acid (GABA) regulates tolerance to various abiotic stresses. Therefore, we studied biochemical indicators and GABA shunt gene expression in G. uralensis under abiotic stress. For the first time, six GABA shunt genes were identified in G. uralensis genome, including three GuGADs, one GuGABP, one GuGABA-T, and one GuSSADH. Their cis-acting elements were related to hormone and environmental responses, revealed that they played a role in G. uralensis growth and development. Under drought and salt stress and stresses combined with exogenous GABA, the genes of GuSSADH, GuGABP, and GuGADs were all significantly upregulated. Moreover, exogenous GABA could promote plant growth, regulate endogenous GABA levels and improve the activity of superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), malondialdehyde (MDA), and the content of soluble sugar and soluble protein. Our findings confirm that exogenous GABA alleviates the damage caused by salt and drought stress by simultaneously boosting the antioxidant system to combat oxidative damage and enhancing osmotic adjustment to maintain cellular water relations. Introduction

Glycyrrhiza sp. is one of the ancient herbal plants of the legume family, which is a high-value medicinal plant and widely used as an ingredient in food, cosmetics, and other products1,2. It has long been known as the “king of medicine” and “national elder” 3. G. uralensis is commonly found in arid and semi-arid areas in Northwest China, which has strong drought and salt tolerance, and it plays an essential ecological role in soil and water conservation, wind prevention4. Unfortunately, due to excessive excavation, wild licorice resources are increasingly scarce and have been listed as a secondary protected plant in China (http://www.iplant.cn/rep/protlist). Meanwhile, salt and drought stresses inhibited plant growth and reduced biomass, which hindered the development of the Glycyrrhiza industry5,6. Due to the seedling stage is extremely sensitive to stress tolerance, it is of great scientific significance to explore the physiological and gene expression of G. uralensis seedlings under adverse conditions in alleviating stress.

Soil salinity and drought became two negative factors in agriculture, limiting plant growth, development7. Plants subjected to salt or drought stresses commonly experience numerous metabolic, physiological, and biochemical changes8. Previous research showed that salt and drought stress cause water deficiency in plants and result in limited nutrient uptake from roots. Thereby reducing nutrient availability and metabolism and leading to reduced crop yields and inducing oxidative stress in seedlings9. The production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) causes membrane lipid peroxidation damage and ultimately leads to a decrease in plant growth10,11. Maintenance of homeostatic levels of ROS is achieved by non-enzymatic antioxidants, including ROS scavenging enzymes, the most important being superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), and proline12. SOD constitutes the only enzymatic mechanism for removing superoxides by dismutating O2− into H2O2 and O2. When plants are subjected to these harmful stresses, they accumulate large amounts of osmotic regulators, such as soluble sugar, soluble protein, and proline, to balance the osmotic potential of intracellular and extracellular ions and resist osmotic stresses13.

γ-Aminobutyric acid (GABA) is a non-protein 4-carbon amino acid that is widely distributed in prokaryotes and eukaryotes. In plants, GABA is a key participant in various metabolic processes and functions as a protective molecule, reducing oxidative damage and enhancing the tolerance of different plant species to various abiotic stresses such as drought, salinity, and temperature14,15. GABA is mainly metabolised through the GABA shunt, which plays a role in maintaining the balance of carbon and nitrogen metabolism and is involved in growth and development16. GABA is synthesised from glutamate by irreversible decarboxylation catalysed by glutamate decarboxylase (GAD) in the cytosol16. Subsequently, GABA is transferred into the mitochondria by GABA transporters (GABP) and subjected to transamination to succinic semialdehyde (SSA) by GABA transaminase (GABA-T/POP2). The SSA is then oxidised to succinate by the NAD-dependent succinate semialdehyde dehydrogenase (SSADH), and subsequently succinate feeds into the TCA cycle17,18. Meanwhile, α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase (α-KGDH) in the TCA cycle is dehydrogenated by glutamate dehydrogenase to glutamate, forming a regulatory loop among glutamate, GABA, and α-ketoglutaric acid18. The previous research showed that when plants recognise the presence of pathogens, GABA accumulation is triggered and is usually associated with increased GAD activity19. Moreover, the GAD1/2 mutant Arabidopsis wilted earlier than the wild type following a prolonged drought stress and ssadh mutants showed growth retardations20. Similarly, the silencing of the GAD and GABA-T genes reduces salt tolerance in tomato21. These studies indicate that the GABA shunt is closely associated with plant tolerance or stress responses.

Above all, the GABA shunt plays a crucial role in plant development and responses to abiotic stresses. However, to date, there have been no reports on the GABA shunt gene family in G. uralensis and its expression analysis under different salt and drought conditions. Therefore, bioinformatics approaches were employed to identify GABA shunt gene family members within the G. uralensis genome. Comprehensive analysis was performed to assess their physicochemical properties, phylogenetic relationships, gene structures, conserved motifs, and cis-regulatory elements, etc. Additionally, Reverse Transcription qPCR (RT-qPCR) was utilised to investigate the expression of GABA shunt genes in G. uralensis, thereby elucidating the role of the GABA shunt gene family in the salt and drought stress response. Furthermore, the role of exogenous GABA in the stress resistance of G. uralensis was also investigated. The findings of this study offer a theoretical foundation for further investigation into the G. uralensis GABA shunt gene family and the development of molecular breeding strategies for improving stress tolerance in G. uralensis.

Result

Characteristics of GABA shunt family members in G. uralensis

To identify all GABA shunt gene family members in G. uralensis, protein sequences of nine GABA shunt proteins in Arabidopsis were downloaded from TAIR (https://www.arabidopsis.org/) as a query to search the G. uralensis genome. After removing identical and incomplete gene sequences, a total of six GABA shunt gene family members were identified, including three GuGADs and one for GuGABP, GuGABA-T and GuSSADH each (Table S1). As shown in Table 1, the amino acid lengths of GuGADs were 465 to 499 aa, were encoded by 1,398 to 1,500 bp CDS, and the molecular weights were 52,849.6 to 56,009.3 kDa. The amino acid lengths of GuGABP, GuGABA-T and GuSSADH were 443, 503 and 536 aa, encoded by 1,332 bp, 1,512 bp and 1,611 bp CDS and the molecular masses were 48,340.66, 55,285.28 and 57,564.323 kDa, respectively.

As shown in Table 1, the isoelectric points of the three GuGADs genes are 5.80, 5.14, and 5.82, respectively, with little difference. However, the isoelectric points of GuGABP, GuGABA-T, and GuSSADH are 8.85, 7.16, and 8.28, respectively, which are all higher than those of the GuGADs members. Overall, all GABA family proteins in G.uralensis are neutral proteins.

We further analysed the stability, hydrophobicity, and heat stability of these proteins. The prediction of the stability of proteins outside the cell showed that, of the three GuGADs, GuGAD1 is unstable with an instability index of 42.86. GuGAD2 and GuGAD3 are stable with instability indices of 32.67 and 36.52, respectively. Additionally, GuGABP and GuSSADH are stable with instability indices of 28.60 and 35.99, respectively, while GuGABA-T is unstable with an instability index of 45.24.

According to the analysis of the aliphatic index, GuGAD1, GuGAD2, and GuGAD3 have aliphatic index of 86.98, 92.99, and 91.02, respectively, and are all high-aliphatic index proteins. Among the GABA shunt family, GuGABP has the highest aliphatic index (105.91), indicating that it has strong hydrophobicity and may be located in the membrane-binding region or form a stable protein core. This result is consistent with the function of GuGABP, which is responsible for the transmembrane transport of GABA and the regulation of the concentration of GABA in the mitochondria and the extracellular space. GuGABA-T and GuSSADH have aliphatic index of 82.66 and 92.85, respectively, and are also high-aliphatic index proteins.

The overall hydrophilicity/hydrophobicity of proteins was further evaluated using GRAVY (grand average of hydropathy): GRAVY values less than 0 indicate hydrophilicity, values greater than 0 indicate hydrophobicity, and values close to 0 indicate neutrality. The results showed that the GRAVY values of GuGAD1, GuGAD2, GuGAD3, and GuGABA-T were − 0.311, -0.163, -0.171, and − 0.193, respectively, indicating hydrophilicity. The GRAVY value of GuGABP was 0.520, indicating hydrophobicity, which is consistent with its high aliphatic content. The GRAVY value of GuSSADH was − 0.021, close to 0, indicating neutrality.

Sequence alignment and phylogenetic analysis of GABA shunt family proteins in G. uralensis

Using DNAman to align the amino acid sequences of GAD homologs from nine species, including G. uralensis and Arabidopsis, it was revealed that all species possess a PLP-binding domain (Fig. S1A). Furthermore, the three GuGADs and certain species (e.g., MdGAD and AtGAD) contain a CaM-binding domain at the carboxy-terminal end (Fig. S1A). Phylogenetic analysis (Fig. 1A) revealed that GuGAD1 is most closely related to MdGAD (bootstrap support value = 100). GuGAD2 clustered with two OsGAD proteins (Os03G5108 and Os03G133000) into a separate branch, while GuGAD3 formed a distinct branch with LjGAD (bootstrap support value = 91).

Homology alignment of GABP proteins revealed high interspecies similarity (Fig. S1B). Among these, GuGABP showed the highest homology with MtGABP (88.31%), while the homologies with GmGABP_Glyma.13G319900 and GmGABP_Glyma.12G084500 were 82.93% and 85.87%, respectively. While the homology with PvGABP_Phvul.011G089500 and PvGABP_Phvul.011G089800 was 81.74% and 83.26%, respectively. The phylogenetic tree (Fig. 1B) revealed that GABPs form three major clades: GuGABP clusters with MtGABP_Medtr4g055610 (bootstrap support value = 98), while GmGABP_Glyma.12G084500 forms a sister clade with PvGABP.

Multiple amino acid sequence alignment of GABA-T revealed that the MuGABA-T protein possesses a conserved Class III aminotransferase domain and eight key sites involved in PLP binding (Fig. S1C). Phylogenetic analysis indicates that GABA-Ts from the same species cluster together (Fig. 1C). The GuGABA-T clustered with GABA-Ts from four Fabaceae species (bootstrap support value = 100). These results indicated higher intraspecies similarity among GABA-T genes than interspecies similarity, with interspecies similarity correlating with evolutionary relatedness.

Homology analysis of SSADH revealed that GuSSADH shares 91.85% and 90.02% homology with GmSSADH_Glyma.08G163700 and GmSSADH_Glyma.15G263500, respectively (Fig. S1D). Homology with MtSSADH, PvSSADH, and LjSSADH at 91.42%, 90.23%, and 90.11%, respectively, collectively indicates high conservation of SSADH across species. The phylogenetic tree showed that OsSSADH_OsKitaake02g058200 occupies the outermost independent branch (Fig. 1D). The SSADH proteins of Fabaceae species were clustered together, including GuSSADH, LjSSADH, PvSSADH, MtSSADH and two GmSSADHs (bootstrap support value = 100).

The results showed that GuGAD1 and GuGAD2 contain seven exons, GuGAD3 contains six exons, and GuGABP, GuGABA-T, and GuSSADH contain 7, 19, and 18 exons, respectively.

G. uralensis GABA shunt family cis-acting element analysis

We used PlantCARE to predict the cis-acting elements in the 2000 bp promoter region upstream of the start codon of the GABA family genes in G. uralensis (Fig. 3 and Table S2). The GuGAD1 promoter contains 61 elements, primarily transcription core elements (22) and light response elements (17), followed by promoter/enhancer region elements (11), and a few plant hormone-related elements (e.g., one auxin response element and four jasmonic acid methyl ester response elements). The GuGAD2 promoter contains 54 elements, with a similar composition to GuGAD1, primarily promoter/enhancer region elements (18), transcription core elements (11), and light response elements (9). The GuGAD3 promoter contains 57 elements, primarily promoter/enhancer region elements, core transcription factor (15), and light response (14) elements. Hormone response elements are scarce (one each for abscisic acid and auxin), but it has five anaerobic induction elements that may play a role in low oxygen stress.

Cis-acting element analysis of members of the gene family of G. uralensis GABA shunt.

A total of 58 cis-acting elements were predicted in the GuGABP promoter, primarily transcription core elements (16), light response elements (13), and promoter/enhancer regions (12). It contained four classes of hormone response elements (four each for abscisic acid and jasmonic acid, one for auxin, and one for salicylic acid), as well as a MYB binding site associated with flavonoid synthesis.

The GuGABA-T promoter contained 59 elements, including 12 transcription core elements, 11 promoter/enhancer region elements, and 10 light response elements. It has a large number of hormone response elements (seven jasmonic acid, four abscisic acid, three auxin, and two salicylic acid), as well as three anaerobic induction, two low temperature response, and one drought induction elements.

The GuSSADH promoter contained 66 elements, primarily transcription core elements (27), followed by light response elements (15) and promoter/enhancer region elements (14). Hormone response elements include four abscisic acid, two salicylic acid, and one auxin response element. Environmental response elements include one anaerobic induction, one drought induction, and one damage response element.

The growth indicators and GABA content of G. uralensis under salt stress and drought stress

In this study, the effects of GABA (G) on plant growth and physiological responses were assessed under drought (D) and salt (S) stress at varying concentrations (Fig. 5). Both drought and salt stress significantly reduced plant height compared to the control group. GABA treatment (CKG, D1G, D2G, D3G, S1G, S2G, S3G) effectively mitigated this reduction (Fig. 5D and J), with the most significant improvement seen under low stress concentrations (S1G, D1G). Stress conditions also caused a reduction in root length. However, GABA application promoted root elongation, especially under moderate drought and salt stress (D2G, S2G), which showed improved root development compared to non-GABA treated stressed plants. Shoot fresh weight followed a similar trend, with reductions under both drought and salt stress. GABA treatment enhanced shoot fresh weight, particularly in the low to moderate stress conditions (S1G, D1G) (Fig. 5E and K). GABA content was higher in both the shoots and roots under stress, with notable increases observed in GABA-treated groups, particularly under moderate stress levels (D2G, S2G). These findings demonstrate that GABA plays a key role in enhancing plant tolerance to both drought and salt stress.

GABA-mediated antioxidant and osmotic responses under drought and salt stress

Drought and salt stress markedly affected the antioxidant system and osmotic adjustment in the tested plants, while exogenous GABA further reshaped these responses (Fig. 7A and L). Under drought conditions, SOD activity showed a concentration - dependent increase from D1 to D3, reaching a maximum at D3 group. However, D3G group resulted in a decline compared with the moderate treatments (Fig. 7A). With increasing drought intensity, CAT activity exhibited a similar upward trend, with the highest value observed in both high-concentration drought treatment groups, D3 and D3G (Fig. 7B). Correspondingly, the MDA content increased progressively under drought stress, indicating enhanced membrane lipid peroxidation (Fig. 7C).

The response to the application of exogenous GABA substrates under drought stress (A–F) and salt stress (G–L). The data represent the mean ± SD, n = 3. Different letters indicate statistically significant differences, P < 0.05.

Osmoregulatory substances were also significantly influenced. The soluble sugar content remained relatively low under drought alone but increased markedly after GABA application, especially at D3G group (Fig. 7D). The content of soluble proteins also significantly increased after the application of GABA, and the highest content was observed in the D3G (Fig. 7E). Proline accumulation was strongly induced by drought, peaking at D3, while the GABA - treated groups reduced the proline content under drought conditions (Fig. 7F).

Under salt stress, the activity of SOD shows a negative correlation with the salt treatment concentration, with it being higher in the low-salt treatment group. Furthermore, after the application of GABA, it was significantly higher than that in the group treated with a single salt (Fig. 7G). CAT activities were significantly enhanced, particularly at moderate to high salinity (S2 and S3), and were further stimulated by GABA treatment (Fig. 7H). Moreover, salt stress also led to substantial MDA accumulation, whereas the S3G treatment (high-concentration salt with GABA) reduced the accumulation of MDA (Fig. 7I). In parallel, the contents of soluble sugar, soluble protein, and proline increased progressively with salinity and were consistently higher in GABA - treated groups (Fig. 7J-L). Similar to the drought groups, the single salt treatment could elevate proline levels, while GABA application would suppress its accumulation (Fig. 7L).

Discussion

GABA is a nitrogen-rich, multifunctional metabolite that is widely distributed in plants, particularly in the leguminous species22,23. As a key molecule in several metabolic processes, GABA plays a protective role in plants by reducing oxidative damage and enhancing tolerance to various abiotic stresses such as drought, salinity, and temperature fluctuations24–26. GABA is synthesised by GAD enzyme and is transported into the mitochondria by the GABP enzyme, where it undergoes catabolism through the GABA shunt27. The regulation of GABA shunt activity affects plant pH, carbon-nitrogen balance, and hormone signalling pathways, playing a crucial role in plant growth, development, and stress responses15,28. In this study, we analysed the GABA shunt gene family in G. uralensis, identifying three GuGADs, one GuGABP, one GuGABA-T, and one GuSSADH. From an evolutionary perspective, members of the GAD, GABA-T, GABP, and SSADH families each form well-defined, gene family specific clades, suggesting that these gene families are evolutionarily conserved across plant species. In particular, the close clustering of the studied genes with homologs from related legumes with high bootstrap values (100% support) implies functional conservation. A few internal nodes display moderate support (52–72% support), which may reflect lineage-specific divergence or limited phylogenetic signal within highly conserved domains.

GAD, as the key enzyme in GABA synthesis, catalyses the decarboxylation of L-glutamate to produce GABA28. CaM/Ca²⁺-dependent GADs in plants typically retain the CaM-binding domain, as observed in Arabidopsis thaliana29, Solanum lycopersicum30,31, and Panax ginseng32. However, some GADs, such as the GAD2 from Oryza sativa, lack this domain33. In this study, we identified three GAD genes in G. uralensis, with GuGAD2 and GuGAD3 containing both the conserved PLP-binding domain and the CaM-binding domain, while GuGAD1 only possesses the PLP-binding domain and lacks the CaM-binding domain. Previous studies indicate that increased Ca²⁺ concentration enhances GAD activity, thereby regulating plant growth and development34. Moreover, we determined the expression of GuGADs related to salt and drought stresses in the plantlet by RT-PCR analysis. GAD is known to be activated by an increase in the cytosolic concentration of Ca2+ or H+ 6. This dual mechanism of regulation relates cellular accumulations of GABA to the nature and severity of environmental stresses. Cytosolic levels of Ca2+ are increased in response to salinity, drought, cold shock, heat shock, and osmotic stress7,17. In drought stress, GuGAD2 mRNA levels significantly increased in D1 and D3 by 1.74 and 1.48-fold. And the expression level of GuGAD1 and GuGAD3 had significantly increased in S2 and S3 with 1.36 and 2.3-fold, respectively.

In the context of the GABA shunt in plants, GABP is a critical transport protein that acts as a bridge between the cytosol and the mitochondria18. In GABP mutants, the carbon input derived from GABA to the TCA cycle is impaired, resulting in a compensatory increase in TCA cycle activity35. This function is crucial for plant growth under conditions of limited carbon supply. There was less research on the role of GABP when plants are subjected to stress. We only retrieved a previous study showed that CsGABP genes facilitate shifting the cyclic flux of the TCA cycle to an alternative non-cyclic pathway via GABA shunt and contribute to the citrus response to the bacterial pathogen ‘Candidatus Liberibacter asiaticus’ 36. In this study, the GuGABP was significantly upregulated in G. uralensis under drought and salt stress. Both the expression levels of downstream genes and endogenous GABA content showed an increase. As a result, we inferred that GuGABP might enhance GABA metabolism, thereby bypassing the impaired TCA cycle and sustaining energy homeostasis in plants. This transformation contributes to G. uralensis’ ability to alleviate drought and salt stress.

After being transported into the mitochondria, GABA is metabolised by GABA-T to produce succinic semialdehyde, which then participates in the TCA cycle35. Multiple sequence alignments and conserved domain analyses indicate that the GuGABA-T gene in G. uralensis shares 67.26%-88.22% amino acid sequence identity with known plant GABA-T genes and belongs to the acetylornithine aminotransferase (AAT_I) superfamily. In this study, when subjected to drought stress (D2), the expression of GuGABA-T significantly increased. Several plant species, such as Arabidopsis, tomatoes, grapevines, and apples, have shown increased GABA levels under drought stress. These results indicated that up-regulated expression of GABA-T in response to drought stress suggests that it may play a role in plant drought stress responses37.

SSADH catalyses the conversion of succinic semialdehyde to succinate in the GABA shunt, participating in plant carbon metabolism38. SSADH belongs to the aldehyde dehydrogenase (ALDH) superfamily and plays an important role in plant growth, development, and stress responses39. Studies have shown that the GABA shunt acts as a supplementary pathway to the TCA cycle, with SSADH playing a crucial role in carbon skeleton composition and energy synthesis39. During non-biological stress responses, SSADH helps balance ROS accumulation, preventing oxidative stress and cell death40. Additionally, enhanced expression of SSADH in sweet sorghum has been shown to improve salt stress tolerance41. Mutations in SSADH in plants have been proven to lead to increased accumulation of reactive oxygen species intermediates and cell death under light and heat stress38. Thus, SSADH plays an important role in plant growth and development.

Gene promoters not only determine gene transcription but also regulate gene expression levels. The presence of various cis-acting elements in promoters may suggest that the gene could be involved in diverse functions in response to stress42. Promoter prediction results revealed that the GABA shunt family members in G. uralensis all contain elements related to light response, hormones, and environmental responses, implying potential roles in the growth and development of G. uralensis. For instance, the presence of light-responsive elements raises the possibility that these GABA shunt genes may be associated with circadian rhythm regulation. Plant hormones are important signaling molecules that regulate plant development, growth, and defense systems43. Recent studies have shown that plant hormones such as abscisic acid (ABA) are also involved in root growth and other structural changes under salt stress44, drought stress45, and nitrogen deficiency46. Auxin (IAA) plays an indispensable role in plant adaptation to salt stress and enhances root and shoot growth under saline-alkali or heavy metal stress47,48. Jasmonic acid (JA) is an important signaling molecule induced by various environmental stresses, including defense responses to drought, salinity, and ultraviolet radiation49. MeJA effectively alleviates salt stress symptoms in soybean seedlings and rice and can counteract the harmful effects of salt stress49–51. Salicylic acid (SA) is involved in plant responses to abiotic stresses such as drought, salinity, cold, and high temperature52. Notably, the GABA shunt gene family in G. uralensis was found to contain cis-acting elements associated with multiple hormones, as well as drought and low-temperature responses, suggesting that these genes may play roles in growth, development, and stress resistance, although experimental validation is required to confirm these functional predictions.

Exogenous GABA alleviated the inhibitory effects of drought and salt stress on plant growth, as reflected by increased plant height, root elongation, and shoot fresh weight, particularly under low to moderate stress conditions. The enhancement of endogenous GABA content in both shoots and roots under stress, especially following GABA application, suggests a feedback regulation that may contribute to stress acclimation. These results indicate that GABA-mediated growth promotion is closely associated with its accumulation in plant tissues, highlighting its protective role against abiotic stress. Moreover, the application of exogenous GABA significantly enhances seedling tolerance to both salt and drought stress, primarily through two interconnected mechanisms53,54. Enhanced Antioxidant Defence: GABA treatment significantly increased the activities of key antioxidant enzymes—SOD and CAT—under both salt and drought stresses. This enhanced enzyme activity allows the plants to more effectively scavenge harmful ROS generated during stress. Additionally, the significant decrease in MDA content provides direct evidence that GABA effectively reduces membrane damage caused by oxidative stress (lipid peroxidation). Enhanced Osmotic Adjustment: Under both salt and drought conditions, GABA treatment promoted the significant accumulation of important osmoregulatory compounds, such as proline, soluble sugars, and soluble proteins. These increases contribute to lowering cellular osmotic potential, helping to maintain water uptake, cell turgor, and overall cellular stability, especially when water availability is limited or osmotic stress is high. In this study, the content of MDA in the drought-treated G. uralensis was generally higher than that in the CK group, indicating that the drought stress caused certain damage to the G. uralensis. The contents of SOD, CAT and proline in the drought-treated G. uralensis also increased compared to the CK group, suggesting that the drought stress triggered some defence mechanisms in the G. uralensis to resist the adverse conditions. After exogenous application of GABA, the activities of SOD and CAT and the content of proline further increased, indicating that GABA enhanced the drought resistance of G. uralensis. At the same time, the contents of soluble proteins and soluble sugars also significantly increased, suggesting that GABA promoted the accumulation of osmotic regulatory substances, thereby alleviating the damage caused by drought. Collectively, these results demonstrate that drought and salt stresses activate antioxidant defenses and osmotic adjustment, while exogenous GABA further enhances protective enzyme activities and osmolyte accumulation, thereby contributing to improved stress tolerance.

Conclusion

In the genome of G. uralensis, for the first time, six GABA shunt gene family were identified, including three GuGADs, one GuGABP, one GuGABA-T, and one GuSSADH. The different genes vary greatly in the structure and conserved motifs of the encoded proteins, but they are highly conserved within the same subfamily. The promoters of the GABA shunt gene family all contain elements related to hormone and environmental responses, which proves their important role in the growth and development of licorice. Using G. uralensis seedlings treated with different degrees of salt stress and drought stress as materials, the results of their GABA gene expression showed that the expression level of the GADs, SSADH, and GABP genes significantly increased. In conclusion, the genes of the GABA shunt family in G. uralensis respond to salt stress and participate in the regulation of expression. Furthermore, the exogenous application of GABA could effectively alleviate the detrimental impacts of both drought and salt stress. This is achieved through the promotion of plant growth and the regulation of endogenous GABA levels. Specifically, exogenous GABA can enhance the activities of superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT), reduce the malondialdehyde (MDA) content, and increase the levels of soluble sugar and soluble protein. Our findings confirm that exogenous GABA alleviates the damage caused by salt and drought stress by simultaneously boosting the antioxidant system to combat oxidative damage and enhancing osmotic adjustment to maintain cellular water relations. The observed stress-intensity specific optimisation points are noteworthy for understanding how GABA modulates these protective responses.

Methods

BLAST identification of four genes of the GABA shunt family in G. uralensis

The whole genome data of G. uralensis used in this study was obtained from http://ngs-data-archive.psc.riken.jp/Gur-genome/download.pl. The sequences of GAD (AT2G02010, AT5G17330, AT2G02000, AT1G65960, AT3G17760, and AT3G1772), GABP (AT2G01170), GABA-T (AT3G22200), and SSADH (AT1G79440) found in Arabidopsis thaliana were used as references from the TAIR database (http://www.arabidopsis.org/index). BLASTP (the protein basic local alignment search tool) was used to identify the genes of the GABA shunt family in the genome of G. uralensis with an E-value threshold of 1 × 10− 5. The coding sequences, DNA sequences, and protein sequences were corrected using Apollo software.

Physicochemical property analysis of the proteins of the GABA shunt family genes in G. uralensis

The amino acid number, molecular weight (MW), theoretical isoelectric point (PI), instability index (II), aliphatic index (AI), and total average hydrophilicity (GRAVY) of the GABA shunt family genes were determined using the ExPaSy website (http://web.expasy.org/). Multiple Em for Motif Elicitation (MEME, http://memesuite.org/) was used to identify motifs with an E-value less than 1 × 10− 30.

Phylogenetic analysis of the GABA shunt family genes in G. uralensis

The protein sequences of the GABA shunt family (GAD, GABP, GABA-T, and SSADH) of nine plants were downloaded from Phytozome (http://www.phytozome.net/). These plants included tomato (Solanum lycopersicum), pasqueflower (Pulsatilla patens), rice (Oryza sativa), alfalfa (Medicago truncatula), apple (Malus domestica), Arabidopsis thaliana, common bean (Phaseolus vulgaris), Lotus japonicus, and soybean (Glycine max). The protein sequences of the GABA shunt family genes in G. uralensis and the above nine plants were analysed for conservation using DNAMAN6.0 software. A Neighbour-Joining Algorithm (NJ) phylogenetic tree was constructed based on the GABA shunt family genes of these nine evolutionary species using MEGA X software. The specific procedures and parameters are as follows: (1) Use the ClustalW tool in MEGA X software to perform multiple sequence alignments for the GAD, GABP, GABA-T, and SSADH genes of G. uralensis and nine other species, with default parameter settings; (2) Use the Phylogenetics tool in MEGA X software to construct neighbor-joining phylogenetic trees using the Kimura 2-parameter (K2-p) model, with 1000 bootstrap replicates; (3) Use Figtree v.1.4.4 software (http://tree.bio.ed.ac.uk/software/figtree/) to visualize the phylogenetic trees.

Analysis of the gene structure, conserved motifs, promoter cis-acting element prediction, and protein structure prediction of the GABA shunt family genes in G. uralensis

The intron-exon arrangement of the coding sequences (CDS) was compared with their corresponding genomic sequences, and the intron-exon arrangement of the CDS was visualised using TBtools v2.310. The conserved motifs of the amino acids in G. uralensis and nine other species were identified using the MEME online software (https://memesuite.org/meme/tools/meme), with the motif number parameter set to 10 55. Visualisation was performed using TBtools v2.119. The 2.0 kb promoter regions of the GABA shunt family genes in G. uralensis were extracted using TBtools v2.310, and potential hormone response or stress-related cis-acting elements were predicted using the online software PlantCARE (http://biinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plancare/html/). Using the online software SWISS MODEL (https://swissmodel.expasy.org/interactive), a 3D model of the amino acid sequence of the GABA shunt family protein of G. uralensis was established by the SWISS-MODEL homology modelling method, and then its tertiary structure was predicted.

Plant material and growth conditions

The G. uralensis seeds were collected from Hangjin Banner, Inner Mongolia, and were identified as Glycyrrhiza uralensis by Professor Zhirong Sun from Beijing University of Chinese Medicine. The seeds were treated with H2SO4 and washed with sterilised distilled water. The germinated seedlings were cultured with hydroponics (Hoagland nutrient solution) in a climatic box under the following conditions: 16 h light at 24 °C; 8 h dark at 18 °C; and relative humidity of 50%. After one week, they were subjected to drought and salt stress treatments. The drought stress treatments include 10%PEG (D1), 15%PEG (D2), 20%PEG (D3), 10%PEG + 1mM/L GABA (D1G), 15%PEG + 1mM/L GABA (D2G), 20%PEG + 1mM/L GABA (D3G). The salt stress treatments include 50mM/L NaCl (S1), 100mM/L NaCl (S2), 150mM/L NaCl (S3), 50mM/L NaCl + 1mM/L GABA (S1G), 100mM/L NaCl + 1mM/L GABA (S2G), 150mM/L NaCl + 1mM/L GABA (S3G).

Determination of biochemistry index

GABA content was determined using a GABA assay kit (Suzhou Greese Biotechnology Co., Ltd.). The contents of soluble protein and soluble sugar were determined by the Coomassie brilliant blue method and anthrone colourimetry method, respectively56,57. The activities of superoxide dismutase (SOD) and malondialdehyde (MDA) were analysed according to the method described by Jiang et al. 58. The content determinations of catalase (CAT) and proline were executed by employing the method outlined by Anderson et al. 59 and Ábrahám et al. 60, respectively.

Confirmation of gene expression of GABA shunt family genes using RT-qPCR

The total RNA from G. uralensis seedling roots (CK, D1, D2, D3, S1, S2, and S3) was extracted by RNeasy Pure Plant Kit (DP432, Tiangen, China), and reverse transcription was carried out using the Prime Script Reagent Kit (Takara, Dalian, China). The primers used to amplify the GABA shunt family genes (GAD1, GAD2, GAD3, GABP, GABA-T, SSADH) by real-time PCR were designed by Primer 5.0. The internal reference gene of G. uralensis, as referenced in Chen et al., was employed for normalization. The primer details are listed in Table S3. All of the primers were tested for their specificity by agarose gel electrophoresis61. RT-qPCR was performed using a SYBR Prime Script RT-qPCR kit (TaKaRa, Dalian, China) with three replicates, and the cycling conditions were set according to the manual. The Bio-Rad CFX96 real-time PCR detection system (Hercules, CA, United States) was used in our experiment.

Data processing

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.3.1. Data were expressed as mean ± SD. Comparisons among multiple groups were conducted using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s HSD post hoc test, while pairwise comparisons were performed using two-tailed Student’s t-test. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.
