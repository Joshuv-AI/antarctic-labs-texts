Deimplementation of inappropriate feeding practices in early care and education: a Hybrid Type 3 cluster-randomized trial
Authors: Taren Massey-Swindle, Julie M Rutledge, Tracie Rushing, Dong Zhang, Geoffry M Curran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe science of deimplementation—reducing harmful or ineffective practices—has focused almost exclusively on clinical prescribing, with no studies conducted in community or educational settings. Early care and education (ECE) settings offer a strategic venue for shaping eating behaviors, with children consuming up to 500 meals annually in these environments. However, ECE educators routinely use feeding practices that undermine self-regulation, including pressuring children to eat, rushing mealtimes, and offering food as reward. These practices contribute to food aversions, diminished self-regulation, and obesity risk.MethodsWe will conduct a Hybrid Type 3 cluster-randomized trial evaluating a co-designed deimplementation strategy package (WISE Words) across 88 ECE sites in Arkansas and Louisiana. Sites will be randomized 1:1 to WISE Words or usual practice, with usual practice sites receiving the intervention after two years (waitlist design). WISE Words includes six strategies: dynamic training using improvisation methods, peer learning collaboratives with goal setting, external facilitation, audit and feedback, environmental reminders, and tailored educational materials. The primary outcome is de-adoption of inappropriate feeding practices measured via direct mealtime observation (Table Talk). Secondary outcomes include adoption of evidence-based practices, acceptability, appropriateness, and sustainability at 12- and 24-months post-intervention. Child outcomes include Body Mass Index, skin carotenoid levels (Veggie Meter) willingness to try new foods (observed) and food neophobia (teacher and caregiver report). An explanatory sequential mixed methods design will test mechanisms of change derived from the Implementation Trust Building Theory of Change examining whether trust mediates strategy effects on outcomes.DiscussionThis trial extends deimplementation science into community settings by targeting culturally embedded behavioral practices rather than clinical prescribing behaviors. Results will inform approaches to shifting entrenched practices in ECE and similar settings while testing trust as a deimplementation mechanism. Sustainability assessments will address a notable gap, as few studies have examined whether deimplementation effects persist.Trial registrationNCT07101321, July 20, 2025.Keywords: Deimplementation, Early care and education, Childhood, Feeding practices, Implementation science, Obesity prevention, Child nutrition

• While most deimplementation trials target clinical prescribing by physicians, this study tests strategies for reducing culturally-embedded behavioral practices among early educators in community settings.

• The mixed methods evaluation tests the Implementation Trust Building Theory of Change, examining trust as a mechanism through which deimplementation strategies may exert their effects.

• Sustainability assessments at 12 and 24 months address a significant gap; fewer than 25% of NIH-funded implementation studies evaluate strategy effects on sustainability, and virtually none examine deimplementation sustainment in community contexts.

Background

An inability to self-regulate dietary intake undermines the development and maintenance of healthy weight across the life course [1, 2]. Dietary self-regulation—the ability to eat in response to internal hunger and satiety cues—is present at birth but begins to weaken in early childhood, with marked individual differences evident by preschool [3–5]. These differences are shaped, in part, by adults’ feeding practices, which influence both children’s self-regulation and willingness to try new foods [6, 7]. Early care and education (ECE) settings represent a critical and efficient context for intervention: children consume up to 500 meals and snacks annually in ECE; more than 70% of children ages 3–5 are in non-parental care each week [1]; and a single educator may influence dietary experiences for up to 20 children per year. Despite this opportunity, ECE educators frequently engage in feeding practices that are misaligned with evidence-based recommendations, including pressuring children to eat, hurrying meals, and using coercive or negative food-related language [8]. Observational studies in ECE settings indicate that educator prompts are substantially more likely to encourage eating than to support children’s attention to hunger and satiety cues [8], a pattern corroborated across multiple studies [3]. Such practices are associated with poorer diet quality, diminished self-regulation, food aversion, neophobia, emotional eating, and excess weight gain [9–14], whereas evidence-based feeding practices promote self-regulation, food acceptance, and healthier dietary patterns [15–19]. Addressing the persistent gap between evidence-based feeding recommendations and routine practice in ECE is therefore a critical implementation challenge with substantial implications for early obesity prevention.

Need for deimplementation research in community settings

The science of deimplementation is just emerging, with over half of NIH-funded grants on this topic funded since 2015 [20]. A 2022 review of over 200 randomized trials for deimplementation summarized that most trials targeted physicians (85%); over half took place in primary care settings (66%); and the majority aimed to reduce use of a particular drug (72%) [21]. No studies were in community or education settings, and few (21%) had a theoretical basis [21]. This review indicated that the most commonly used strategies for deimplementation were education and audit and feedback, with over 80% having some desired effect. Multi-component deimplementation strategies have been more beneficial than single component interventions for reducing low-value medical services [22].

No parallel knowledge base exists to inform deimplementation efforts in community settings like ECE. To advance deimplementation science beyond clinical prescribing, we need to test theoretically based deimplementation approaches in real-world settings targeting different types of professionals. Research evidence on the sustained effects of deimplementation strategies is also scant. A study of audit and feedback to reduce inappropriate antibiotic use found prescribing levels reduced by 50% at initial measurement (12 months) but exceeded baseline levels when measured for sustainment (30 months) [23]. No parallel data exist to inform sustainment efforts for deimplementation in community settings.

This study is well situated to contribute to these knowledge gaps by testing a co-designed, multicomponent deimplementation strategy that is well aligned to known determinants of the target behaviors and grounded in theory. We will measure effects both on initial deimplementation and its sustainment over time for shifting inappropriate feeding practices in ECE.

Prior research supporting this trial

A K01 small-scale implementation trial, an R03 deimplementation pilot, a foundation-funded development project, and a CTSA-funded pilot supported the following preliminary data.

A series of implementation and deimplementation studies conducted in ECE settings has examined educator feeding practices and strategies to modify them. Early implementation research (K01DK110141; 2016–2019) found that although implementation strategies improved fidelity to several evidence-based components of an obesity prevention program, positive feeding practices showed limited improvement despite their association with child dietary intake [24]. These findings suggested that inappropriate feeding practices may interfere with adoption of evidence-based alternatives. Subsequent studies used an Evidence-Based Quality Improvement process to co-design deimplementation strategies with educators, administrators, and parents, identifying skills, social norms, and beliefs as key determinants of feeding practices and mapping these determinants to targeted strategies [25–27]. Pilot testing indicated that the resulting strategy package was delivered as intended and was associated with reductions in inappropriate feeding practices, improvements in positive feeding practices, and increases in co-teacher trust, with educators reporting high acceptability and perceived usefulness alongside practical constraints related to time and workload [28]. The strategies were later adapted for virtual delivery and refined through iterative testing, with feasibility and acceptability supported by usage metrics and qualitative feedback; pilot evaluation demonstrated further reductions in inappropriate feeding practices and increases in evidence-based practices, while also identifying challenges related to sustained engagement with virtual components [29]. Across studies, this work—guided by the Niven process model of deimplementation [30] —has contributed empirical evidence on determinants of feeding practices [8, 31–34] and the feasibility of deimplementation strategies in ECE settings.

Theoretical foundation

This study draws on the Implementation Trust Building (ITB) Theory of Change, which identifies trust as a central determinant of implementation and deimplementation processes [35]. The ITB integrates evidence from systematic reviews, qualitative research, and quantitative models demonstrating associations between trust, team functioning, and behavior change, including in deimplementation contexts [36–41]. In settings where established practices are targeted for discontinuation, perceived threat or criticism may contribute to resistance and persistence of low-value practices, highlighting the relevance of relational processes in deimplementation efforts [39]. The ITB specifies a set of relational and technical trust-building strategies hypothesized to shape implementers’ experiences and perceptions and, in turn, influence capability, opportunity, and motivation for behavior change. This framework informed the specification of our deimplementation strategies tested in Aim 1 and the mechanisms of interest in Aim 2.

Methods

Study design

We will use a Hybrid Type 3 design [42] to determine the effectiveness (Aim 1) and mechanisms (Aim 2) of a deimplementation strategy for reducing inappropriate feeding practices in ECE while also exploring how child health outcomes vary by educators’ engagement in WISE Words. We will complete this study across 3 cohorts; sites will be randomized to treatment (i.e., deimplementation strategies, WISE Words) or control (i.e., usual practice). Sites that are randomized to usual practice will be offered the intervention after 2 years without evaluation. That is, all sites will receive deimplementation support during the grant period, reflective of partner feedback that all sites deserve access. This trial design with a usual practice control group is most appropriate given the limited research evidence to date on deimplementation in community settings. The field needs evidence of the effects and mechanisms of a carefully designed, theoretically grounded deimplementation strategy in the real world before comparing alternative deimplementation approaches.

Aim 1: Determine the effectiveness of a package of deimplementation strategies for reducing the use of inappropriate feeding practices in ECE

We will compare the effect of deimplementation strategies with usual practice. We hypothesize that educators in the WISE Words group will reduce their use of inappropriate feeding practices compared to the group of educators continuing with practice as usual. As a secondary hypothesis, we also expect that educators in the WISE Words group will increase their use of evidence-based feeding practices compared to educators in the usual practice group.

Aim 2: Identify mechanisms of deimplementation using a mixed methods approach

We hypothesize that deimplementation strategies will increase trust with peers and the external facilitators, which will influence educators’ perceived capability, opportunity, and motivation to change. In turn, these will influence deimplementation outcomes. This will be a direct test of the pathway recently advanced by the ITB Theory of Change.

Setting and participants

Sites

Site will be from Arkansas and Louisiana. A site is defined as one ECE location; a site may have multiple classrooms with up to 20 children per classroom. A director provides leadership at each site; educators implement practices in their classrooms. Typically, each classroom has 2 educators responsible for feeding children per classroom.

Usual practice sites

Sites randomized to usual practice will continue with normal activities. These sites will mirror the geography of those in the deimplementation condition. Usual practice in ECE does not require a standard approach to nutrition education, and implementation varies based on the discretion of the site and/or educator. To our knowledge, there are no available training programs in Arkansas and Louisiana that target evidence-based feeding practices that would address similar targets as our program. Usual practice sites will be on a waitlist to receive deimplementation support 2 years following randomization, which reflects community partner input that never receiving support would be unfair and hinder recruitment.

Inclusion criteria

Sites within a 200-mile radius of staff offices serving 15 + children ages 3 to 5 years and agreeing to participate in data collection can be included.

Exclusion criteria

We will exclude sites that have participated in intervention studies with our team in the last 3 years and sites that do not provide lunch for children on site.

Sample

We will recruit 88 sites (44 in Arkansas, 44 in Louisiana). We will recruit sites in 3 cohorts, 44 sites per year in 3 school years (across Y2–Y4). Half will be randomly selected to serve as usual practice sites and be offered the deimplementation strategies at the study’s end. We will pool data across years for analyses and include cohort assignment as a control variable.

Classroom and child inclusion

All classrooms at a site will receive the same deimplementation strategies and participate in data collection. With an estimated average of 3 classrooms per site, we expect 264 total classrooms in 88 sites in our analyses. We will select one classroom at random per site to participate in collection of child outcomes (N = 88 classrooms, 15 children per classroom = [8, 43] 20 children total). Parents will provide permission in accordance with ethics board approval.

Randomization

The study will be cluster randomized at the site level. Sites will be randomized in a 1:1 ratio consistent with procedures of minimization and balancing [44, 45]. First, the balance between groups on potential confounding factors (i.e., number of classrooms, demographics) will be examined. Then, we will randomly select one balanced assignment from the list of random assignments, which will balance sites on key factors while preserving advantages of randomly assigning sites [44, 45].

Community partnership

We will draw on Evidence-Based Quality Improvement (EBQI) methods throughout our study [25, 46, 47]. This process will develop researcher–community partnerships for joint decision making, consistent with Community-Engaged Dissemination and Implementation principles [48]. Our EBQI panel will include educators, directors, and staff from ECE as well as state policy leaders who can inform scale-up. The panel will meet 3 times per year and provide input into study recruitment, roll-out of study protocol, interpretation of findings, and future planning.

WISE words deimplementation strategies

Together, our prior findings demonstrate that we have co-designed, iterated, established preliminary support for, and finalized a fully specified deimplementation strategy package in line with a strong theoretical foundation. We have specified our package of deimplementation strategies consistent with recommendations from Proctor et al. [49] in Table 1, which we have termed “WISE Words.”

Deimplementation strategy specification

The package includes 3 foundational strategies delivered to all participants and 3 tailored strategies selected based on educator goals.

Dynamic training

WISE Words staff provide 6-hour training using improvisation methods [50] to ground educators in concepts. This reduces resistance to change through empathetic and authentic exchange while addressing knowledge gaps. Training is delivered in-person at the beginning of the school year.

Peer learning collaborative with goal setting

Pairs of educators (typically co-teachers) meet to discuss goals, examine and resolve barriers, and share accountability. Educators will set one goal for the fall, and WISE Words staff will set one for the spring. WISE Words staff provide monthly discussion prompts and resources through a virtual platform. This strategy increases motivation to change by shifting social norms and creates co-learning and bi-directional communication to increase skills and shift beliefs.

External facilitation

WISE Words external facilitators provide direct support to educators in alignment with their goals. Beginning 2 weeks after training for 1 year, facilitators conduct monthly (or more upon request) in-person and virtual interactions (alternating). In-person visits include mealtime observation or in-person coaching based on previously recorded mealtime with coaching specific to focal goals; virtual interactions include check-ins about progress, mutual information sharing, celebration of successes, and troubleshooting barriers to change. The facilitator monitors and encourages access to resources offered virtually. This creates and connects networks with a holistic, enabling focus on educators [51].

An additional 3 tailored strategies support the foundational ones. All educators choose the resource type they would like to support them, and those resources are tailored to their individual goals:

Audit and feedback

Upon request, WISE Words staff provide assessment and tailored feedback on educator practices at a recent meal. This provides concrete, responsive information to highlight skills gaps with supportive suggestions for improvement and celebrates wins.

Environmental reminders

Twice per semester, staff provide reminders of targeted practices (e.g., poster). This keeps opportunities to change at front of mind and motivates change.

Educational materials

Educators receive handouts and/or videos as well as practical “how-to” guides and/or expert recommendations tailored to their goals. This provides compelling information to challenge beliefs and increase skills and knowledge, and highlights opportunities for new behavior.

First, all ECE site staff will receive the dynamic, improvisation-based training in an in-person setting. At the training, we will launch the peer learning collaborative wherein educators are paired to discuss their goals for a key feeding practice they want to stop and one they want to start. This process will be accomplished via the virtual platform. The study team will assist educators to access the platform and ensure they can access it on their own devices to prevent login challenges faced in our prior work. After training and throughout the school year, external facilitators will support educators to achieve their goals through monthly in-person visits and on-demand virtual interactions. Self-selected goals will be the focus from August through December. New goals will be suggested for educators in February based on January observations. The suggested goals will target the most frequently used inappropriate practice for stopping and the least frequently used evidence-based practice for starting. Throughout the school year, the virtual platform will prompt educators with their selected resources and peer discussion prompts.

Facilitator training and fidelity

Each study region will have trained facilitators with experience in the ECE setting to serve classrooms in that region. All facilitators will receive standard training and toolkits (e.g., sample scripts, testimonials, motivational interview examples). This is based on the Veterans Health Administration Implementation Facilitation Training [52], which one PI completed in 2019. We have adapted this training for facilitation in ECE settings and expanded to include in-depth training on identifying types of responders we have found common in ECE and strategies for responding to each type [53]. New facilitators will lead visits with support and feedback from an experienced and trained facilitator on the team. Facilitators will take part in monthly reflective supervision calls led by the PIs. All facilitators will log their activities (e.g., visits, calls, emails). The PIs will compare the facilitator logs against the core implementation facilitation activities checklist for monitoring [53] and provide corrective guidance as needed.

Measures and data collection plan

Implementation outcomes

De-adoption (primary outcome)

The primary outcome is de-adoption of inappropriate feeding practices. We will use the Table Talk observational measure [8], which provides an observed count of eight inappropriate and six evidence-based feeding practices within a single mealtime (approximately 30 min). Trained and field-reliable staff blinded to the study condition will collect data consistent with published protocols [29]. Observations occur at baseline (before training), mid-year (January), and end-of-year (April or May). We will repeat observation of the Table Talk primary outcome in the following 2 school years as an indicator of sustainability.

Self-reported feeding practices

We will also collect self-report of feeding practices using the About Feeding Children (AFC) survey [31] that includes 3 primary scales to assess verbal and structural (e.g., classroom rules) strategies that ECEs use when feeding children as well as beliefs about feeding children. We will complete AFC assessments at baseline (i.e., prior to training), midpoint, and at the end of the intervention period.

Acceptability and appropriateness

Secondary implementation outcomes include self-reported acceptability and appropriateness of the deimplementation process (i.e., the degree to which the idea of stopping inappropriate feeding practices is supported) as well as the deimplementation strategies, as assessed with standard pragmatic measures [55, 57]. Assessments occur at baseline, mid-year, and end-of-year.

Child health outcomes

Body mass index

We will assess Body Mass Index (BMI) with a standardized protocol [57] and interpret the data with 2000 CDC growth charts [58]. Assessments occur in August or September (prior to training) and April or May of the school year.

Dietary carotenoid status

To assess impact on dietary intake, we will use Reflection Spectroscopy (RS) assessed by Veggie Meter™ [59]. The Veggie Meter measures skin carotenoid levels as a biomarker for colorful fruit and vegetable intake [62] with an optical finger scan [63, 64]. RS reflects intake over the prior 4 weeks and is sensitive to individual differences and experimental changes [65, 66]. Assessments occur in August or September (prior to training) and April or May.

Willingness to try and food neophobia

We will examine child willingness to try novel foods [60] and fear of new foods (i.e., neophobia). Willingness to try will be assessed through offering novel foods in a classroom-based “Tasting Party,” an approach investigators have used with success in prior work [60, 61]. For assessing neophobia, we will use the brief version of the Child Food Neophobia Scale [61]. Educators for the selected classrooms will be invited to complete a brief form for each child in their class and incentivized based on the portion completed (e.g., 100% = $50 in classroom educational incentives). Parents of children in the selected classrooms will be sent the survey questions and a consent form. Educators will assemble returned packets for research staff collection. Based on our experience with these methods, we expect a 60% response rate. We will attend and recruit at existing parent-attended events to increase response, if needed. Parents who return the baseline packet will be entered into a drawing for each site to win $50. Every parent who completed a baseline packet will be incentivized $10 to complete a follow-up survey.

Mechanism measures (Aim 2)

Educators in the intervention arm will complete assessments of potential mediators during the baseline period (before training), at mid-year, and at the spring assessment (Table 3). We expect 2 educators per classroom to complete the survey, 6 per site on average. This follows the best practice of assessing mediators at a minimum of three points in time [67, 68] and is in line with the temporality criteria to establish causal inference [69]. The research team will collect these data in person with paper surveys or emailed survey links (reflecting technology access and use in ECE). We will capture data with REDCap for secure storage. All classroom staff (lead and assistants) in the deimplementation condition will complete assessments (N = 264 educators).

Mechanism measures (Aim 2); intervention group only

Relational strategy exposure

Our relational strategies will be assessed with an adapted version of the brief Individual Authenticity Measure at Work scale [70]. We will adapt items to reflect educators’ vulnerability and authenticity in interactions with their WISE Words facilitator (e.g., “I don’t feel who I truly am with my WISE Words coach”).

Technical strategy exposure

Our technical strategies will be reflected by a composite variable of the frequency of interactions and number of activities used by the facilitator and recorded in their session logs [71].

Trust

Trust with peers and trust with facilitators will be assessed using an adapted and abbreviated version of the Crosby Relationship Quality measure [70], which includes 6 direct items to assess trust (e.g., “Please rate your trust with your WISE Words coach”).

Capability, opportunity, and motivation

Perceived capability, opportunity, and motivation assessment will use an adapted version of a brief, 6-item established measure [72]. Each construct will be assessed with two pragmatic questions (e.g., “I am motivated to change my feeding practices in the classroom”). This measure has been validated relative to behavior change in multiple domains [72].

Data analysis

We will manage data with REDCap [71], a secure, web-based electronic data capture tool. We will use SPSS Statistics v25 (IBM) for primary analyses. Analysts will examine data for missing values, extreme scores, and variable distributions. We expect missing values on the primary outcome to be minimal because study staff will collect these data. If the missing values percentage exceeds 5% [73], we will use an appropriate method for using all available data such as multiple imputation or full information maximum likelihood (FIML) estimation for analyses.

Aim 1 analyses

For our primary analysis, we will use linear mixed-effects regression models [74] to test for group differences in de-adoption outcomes at the school year end, while accounting for classroom nesting within site. Covariates will include state, site size, cohort, turnover rate, quality rating, and demographics. The statistical significance of the treatment group predictor (two-sided α = 0.05) will be used to determine significant differences in outcomes for the treatment and usual practice groups. Additional analyses will include repeated outcomes from all time points to test for treatment group differences across time and time-by-treatment effects. We will repeat these analyses for secondary implementation outcomes (i.e., adoption, acceptability, appropriateness, sustainability).

We will also examine child-level outcomes using linear mixed-effects regression models, which account for nesting of children within classrooms and sites. Parallel to primary analyses, we will first test treatment group differences at the spring assessment and then examine treatment and time-by-treatment effects using all time points. For all analyses, a significant, positive treatment group effect will support the effectiveness of the deimplementation strategy package on targeted outcomes. We will conduct exploratory analyses to examine the association between implementation outcomes and child outcomes.

Aim 2 analyses

Quantitative mediation

Aim 2 focuses on analyses from the deimplementation group because all variables in the chain of mediation are only plausibly measured in that group. We will test a multilevel, multiple sequential mediator model in a Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) framework [75] to account for classroom nesting within sites using Mplus software. Specifying a multiple mediator model is less biased than testing single mediators one at a time [76]. Using parametric bootstrapping (Monte Carlo) [77], significance tests in SEM are also less biased than sequential hypothesis testing approaches to mediation tests [78]. Using data at three time points, we will be able to model that the independent variable precedes mediators in time, and mediators precede the dependent variable in time; prior levels of mediators and outcomes can be controlled.

Qualitative procedures

We will use qualitative data to provide detailed understanding of response to the deimplementation strategies. Specifically, qualitative interviews will target educators most and least responsive to the strategies. This approach will provide insights into how and why changes were made [79, 80] and highlight potential barriers to activation of trust by our strategies [39]. Common personal traits, beliefs, and contextual characteristics across the groups will contribute to understanding potential moderators of the deimplementation strategies’ effects [81].

We will sample educators for qualitative interviews based on sustainment outcomes to generate the most useful insights for contributing to lasting change in the ECE environment. Specifically, we will sample from the top and bottom 10% on sustained change in inappropriate feeding practices in the school year following the exposure to the strategies. We will interview 4 cases in the top 10% and 4 cases in the bottom 10% per cohort per state for a total of 32 interviews. This number is consistent with guidance that saturation is typically achieved with 5–25 cases per group when studying a discrete phenomenon [82].

Educator interviews will elicit perceived reasons why the strategies worked (or failed) at their site, practical strategies and reasons for change, and relevant factors in the implementation climate influencing deimplementation. Concepts from the ITB Theory of Change will inform interview guides (Table 4). The research team will capture field notes on the site activities, processes, and interactions that may influence response to the strategies using a standardized template to guide analytic comparisons.

Qualitative data analysis

Transcripts will be matched with observed field notes and coded using directed content analysis [82]. The ITB Theory of Change will provide a template of sensitizing concepts to label significant, recurrent ideas [83], particularly ideas that suggest emergent candidate mediators and moderators. We will incorporate inductive codes as we identify additional salient factors [84]. Primary and secondary coders (at least 1 each from Arkansas and Louisiana) will code the same transcripts until inter-rater reliability is established [85, 86]. Coding will be independent after establishing reliability. Coders will hold weekly meetings to discuss iterative expansions to the codebook, to reach consensus about unclear codes, and to document tentative patterns in the data. A third-party team member will resolve disagreements [86]. Participants and EBQI members will review summaries by cohort to inform iterations in interview guide probes. We will conduct analyses of qualitative interviews yearly and use findings to revise the interview guide for the next year (e.g., identify probing needs, generate new questions). We will use NVivo software (QSR International) to code and calculate inter-rater reliability.

Integration

As we interpret Aim 2 findings, we will connect quantitative and qualitative data. This will include: (1) expansion of quantitative findings to provide detail through qualitative data and (2) complementarity to deepen understanding and identify other potential moderators and mediators not focused on in quantitative analyses. Thus, qualitative data will explain and elaborate on quantitative findings.

Statistical power

We used PASS 2020 for the power calculations based on a mixed model test for two means in a 2-level design with randomization at level-2. We assumed an ICC=0.10 (the largest observed ICC from the R03) and a two-sided α = 0.05.

Implementation outcomes

Power for implementation outcomes accounted for classroom nesting within sites and assumed each site would have 3 classrooms. Our estimated sample size is based on power to detect a 2.5-point difference on the Table Talk measure, the primary outcome of de-adoption of inappropriate feeding practices. Assuming SD = 5, the 2.5-point difference corresponds to a standardized mean difference of δ = 0.5 (2.5/5 = 0.5) and would reflect children being exposed to 5 fewer inappropriate practices per meal in one arm versus the other. With 88 sites and 3 classrooms per site (N = 264 classrooms; 132:132), we will have 80% power to detect a standardized mean difference of δ = 0.38 or larger. We do not anticipate site-level attrition, but even with ~ 20% attrition (N = 70 sites; N = 210 classrooms; 105:105), we would have 80% power to detect an effect size of δ = 0.42 or greater.

Child outcomes

Power for child-level outcomes accounted for nesting of children within classrooms and assumed each classroom would have 15 children. With one randomly chosen classroom per site and 15 children per classroom (N = [8, 43] 20 children; 660:660), we will have 80% power to detect an effect size of δ = 0.24 or larger for child-level outcomes, which corresponds to a relatively small effect size [87]. The proposed study, even with site attrition of ~ 20%, is powered to detect practically meaningful small-to-medium effects on the primary outcome and relatively small differences in child-level outcomes.

Mediation analyses

In mediation analyses, the indirect effect is the product of 2 regression coefficients and is not distributed normally, which poses a challenge to power calculations [78]. However, Fritz and MacKinnon [88] recommend a bias-corrected bootstrap method for the indirect effect that, in our sample size of 264 educators, would provide 80% power to detect an indirect effect composed of 2 large-sized constituent effects, or a mix of a large-sized and a medium-sized effect.

Discussion

Arkansas (AR) and Louisiana (LA) are among the states in the US with the highest obesity rates, lowest quality diets, and highest chronic disease rates [87]. Given the limited economic resources of these states, community systems need approaches that optimize resources. This study has the potential to advance the field of implementation science in several ways. First, this study can contribute to a shift from one-size-fits-all implementation strategies to strategically co-designed packages. This is critical given that ECE programs, particularly those in our study, serve children from minority and lower-SES backgrounds, populations at higher child obesity risk [13], and often have limited resources to serve children and families. Our approach may help to optimize the minimum resources needed to achieve desired outcomes.

Second, this study extends deimplementation research by focusing on deimplementing longstanding feeding behaviors supported by the culture (e.g., childcare customs and socioeconomic norms) that were assumed to be helpful but are now deemed harmful. Indeed, because many educators consider these behaviors to be “common sense,” deimplementation could be especially challenging. While the cultural origins of detrimental feeding practices may present a challenge, it also presents an opportunity for implementation science to address a unique target for deimplementation, distinct from targets in clinical settings that are less culturally bound. Specifically, we will contribute to a paradigm expansion from the focus of deimplementation science in clinical prescribing to everyday behaviors of early educators. This approach has the potential to advance implementation science by informing deimplementation approaches in community, rather than clinical, settings.

Third, this study represents a significant contribution to testing theory and examining mechanisms in implementation science. Specifically, our examination of mediators tests the causal linkages proposed by the novel ITB Theory of Change [35]. Consistent with recently identified opportunities in implementation science [39], trust may be a “common element” to successful implementation strategies. Our work will test this theory and, in so doing, contribute to theory refinement [89] and advance knowledge about how and why deimplementation strategies succeed or fail [90]. Our random assignment to different conditions, our use of multiple measurements of mediators at key time points, and our multilevel SEM approach will improve causal inference about the relationship between deimplementation strategies, the proposed mediators, and the targeted implementation outcomes [88].

Finally, this study will provide important data on sustainability. Sustainment assessments at 12 and 24 months will provide rare longitudinal evidence on whether deimplementation effects persist. According to a recent review [91], fewer than 25% of funded R01s in Dissemination and Implementation Science studies have evaluated “the impact of a strategy on sustainability.” Even fewer have examined deimplementation sustainment. Our results are expected to inform realistic expectations and potentially identify predictors of lasting change.

This study has several strengths and limitations relevant to interpretation. Strengths include a pragmatic design that balances rigor with feasibility in early care and education settings, explicit attention to sustainability, and prespecified mediators grounded in theory and prior empirical work. Sustainability is assessed once in the usual practice group and twice in the deimplementation group, reflecting differential exposure to the strategies during the study period; to support equitable conduct and recruitment, usual practice sites will be offered the strategies after study completion, with additional follow-up possible among sites that decline. The study also incorporates procedures to address high staff turnover in ECE settings (approximately 30% annually) [92, 93], including structured onboarding for new educators and analytic adjustment for turnover as a covariate. Limitations include reliance on a priori selection of mediators, which was necessary for parsimony and power but may not capture all relevant mechanisms influencing deimplementation outcomes. The study is not powered to examine moderated mediation or amplification effects; however, qualitative data will be used to contextualize quantitative findings and to identify additional mechanisms and contextual influences. In the event of null mediation findings, analyses will distinguish between lack of strategy impact on proposed mechanisms and lack of association between mechanisms and outcomes, supporting careful interpretation and informing future theory refinement [94].

Conclusions

We expect our study to provide an evidence base for structuring deimplementation support in real-world ECE contexts, ultimately providing a model for deimplementation of inappropriate practices in ECE and similar community settings. Our work will also provide data to guide deimplementation decisions of other interventions through improving targeted application of strategy intensity to optimize resources. The results from this study will position us for future research to test if sequencing removal of inappropriate practices prior to implementation of an evidence-based program improves implementation. This research path will advance us toward our long-term goal of improving ECE feeding environments and, ultimately, weight and dietary outcomes for children.
