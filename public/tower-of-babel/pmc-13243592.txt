Performance improvement of modified light dynamic probing in dense sandy soils with various silt contents
Authors: Hamidreza Salimi, Mahdi Khodaparast, Amir Khoshgoftar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dynamic probes, such as the dynamic probe light (DPL) with a 10 kg hammer weight, are easy to use and enable rapid, in-situ compaction control of embankments. However, the use of these light tools in dense soil is limited because the number of blows can be greatly increased and may even exceed the maximum allowable value. For dense soil types, in order to be able to rely on the test results, heavier tools with hammers of 30–50 kg should be used. This will reduce the speed and ease of testing because of the heavy weight of the tool. In the current study, numerous tests were conducted on soil compacted in a physical model to develop a tool for dense soil. The priority was maintaining speed and ease of testing by modifying the specifications of the DPL tool. The results showed that the efficiency and penetration power of the newly developed tools were higher than those of the DPL tool, even though the hammer weight and energy input remained constant. Statistical analysis of the dynamic probing test results confirmed data repeatability and sufficient confidence for establishing new empirical correlations.

Introduction

Dynamic probing is an important field test for evaluation of soil specifications. It is a practical technique for controlling the density of embankments in the field. This method is cheaper and faster than methods that require drilling of a borehole as well as other local tests. Important attributes of this tool are its low cost, the possibility of identifying layers having low thicknesses, production of continuous and repeatable results, a significant decrease in the number of the borehole and a decreased need for manpower. This has increased the use of dynamic probing test and led to the introduction of experimental relationships between the test results and the characteristics of the soil types which can reduce the cost and time required to obtain the results.

Scala1 introduced the dynamic probing test as a soil identification method. This was followed by the introduction of various standards, including the German2, American3. The different dimensions and specifications for the dynamic penetration testing tool in these standards have produced differences in the amount of energy applied to the soil. Although the weights of the hammers could be light, medium or heavy, the method of use and interpretation of the test results are the same for all tool types. Thus, the suitable tool should be chosen based on the type of soil under study and its hardness and density.

Dynamic probes with light hammers are preferred over other types of probes. Because the allowable range of results for the dynamic penetration test is 3 to 50 blows per penetration stage, the use of a lightweight hammer in dense and hard soil or soil modified with cement, lime, fly ash and nano-additives can cause the test results to fall outside the stated range4,5. An important factor in increasing the penetration ability of the test tool is to decrease diameter of its penetration cone. However, the use of a tool with a very small penetration cone diameter (for example, 20 mm) can rapidly cause damage to the cone that will reduce its efficiency and may require its replacement every time it is damaged. Also, it will not be possible to detect whether or not the penetration cone is being damaged during the test.

The dynamic probe light (DPL) has a hammer of 10 kg with a fall height of 50 cm and is more often preferred over other types of hand probes. The diameter of 35.7 mm of the penetration cone makes it less likely to be damaged in dense and hard soils. However, the lightness of the hammer and the low fall height of the DPL tool, as well as the diameter of its penetration cone will result in test results for soils of medium and high density that will fall outside the permissible penetration range of over than 50 hits at each stage. In this way, the use of the DPL in dense soil will lead to unacceptable results. However, the choice of heavier tools such as the DP medium or DP heavy (DPM or DPH with hammer weights of 30 and 50 kg, respectively) will make the test less easy to perform. The purpose of this research is to make changes in the specifications of the DPL tool so that it can be used in dense and compact soil and the ease and speed of testing maintained.

In this research, numerous dynamic penetration tests with different tools have been conducted on 5 types of sandy soil (each containing a certain percentage of fine silty grains) that uniformly compacted in a laboratory tank with a diameter of one meter by dynamic compaction method. The efficiency of the new tools in dense soils, have been investigated and accordingly, a suitable tool with more efficiency than the DPL tool has been introduced. Also, some new experimental relationships between compaction characteristics of sandy soils such as relative density and dry and wet density and their shear strength are presented. In this project each of the 5 types of soil in 10 cm layers has been compacted in uniform condition with height of 100 cm inside the tank and all the tests have been carried out in uniform and controlled conditions.

Interpretation of dynamic probing test results in past studies

Influencing factors on test results

Among the influencing factors that affect on the results of the dynamic probing test are the quality of the connections of the rods, the perpendicularity of the cone and the tool to the horizontal surface of the ground and possible bending and twisting of the rods. Other factors effecting the results of this test that have been investigated include the length of the penetration rods and the degree of energy loss6 the friction between the connecting rods and the soil7–11 the relative density and the sharpness and unevenness of the surface of the soil particles12 the moisture content13, the particle size, maximum dry weight and porosity ratio14, the soil overburden pressure and confining stress of the soil15,16, the hammer fall height17, the penetration cone diameter18,19 and the angle of the tip of the penetration cone18–22.

Empirical relationships based on dynamic penetration index

The results of dynamic penetration testing can be used in different ways. The initial results of the test include the number of blows for each penetration stage, which reflects the density and hardness of the soil. The higher the number of blows, the higher will be the density and resistance of the soil. The lower the number of blows per penetration stage, the weaker and less resistant will be the soil.

The results of this test can be used to calculate the dynamic penetration index (DPI; mm/blow). This index indicates how much penetration has occurred in the soil from each hammer blow. It has an inverse relationship with the hardness and density of the soil, which means that a lower DPI will indicate harder and denser soil and vice versa. Experimental relations based on dynamic cone resistance

Another method uses the results of the dynamic probing test to calculate the dynamic cone resistance (qd). Calculating and determining the dynamic cone resistance presents the important possibility of comparing the results of different types of dynamic penetrometers according to the effects of their characteristics. In other words, the value of this parameter when used for a specific soil type, should remain constant. The dynamic cone resistance can be calculated from Eq. (2) as DIN En ISO 22476-22.2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${q}_{d}=\left(\frac{M}{M+m}\right){r}_{d}.{r}_{d}=\frac{{E}_{theor}}{A*DPI}$$\end{document}

where M is the hammer mass (kg), m is the mass of the connecting rods (kg), Etheor is the computational potential energy, A is the cross-section of the cone tip (m2) and DPI is the dynamic penetration index (mm/blow).

Calculation of the dynamic cone resistance will reveal the effects of the weight of the tools, geometry of the cone and amount of energy applied to it. The dynamic cone resistance does not depend on the type of penetrometer, although the number of penetration blows is dependent on the type of penetrometer selected. This makes it possible to use the experimental relationships presented with this parameter independent of the type of penetrometer. However, in dynamic penetrometer tests, the presence of vertical stress can affect the results. Lee et al19. introduced the normalized dynamic cone resistance parameter (qdn) in Eq. (3) to remove the effect of the overhead stress and the all-round pressure resulting from it Lee et al19. as:3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$q_{{dn}} = \frac{{\left( {\frac{{q_{d} }}{{p_{a} }}} \right)}}{{\left( {\frac{{\sigma _{m} }}{{p_{a} }}} \right)^{{0.5}} }}$$\end{document}

where qd is the dynamic cone resistance, pa is the base pressure (atmospheric pressure = 100 kN/m2) and σm (the mean principal stress) is calculated by the average of the vertical stress and the horizontal stress.

The dynamic penetration test was performed each time the tank was filled as shown in Fig. 3. The test was performed once at the center of the tank and then at the other four positions in a circle with a diameter of about 60 cm so that the wall of the tank and the location of the tests did not affect the results.

To mitigate boundary effects and test-to-test interference within the tank, Lee et al. (2014) specified a minimum effective radius of 160 mm for the penetration cone. Other researchers have commonly adopted a ratio of approximately 10 between the tank diameter and the effective diameter of the penetration cone, depending on soil type and compaction conditions.

After testing, the soil in the reservoir was removed layer-by-layer and the field density test by sand cone method was performed in each layer. Samples also were taken for the direct shear test. After the complete emptying of the tank, other percentages of fine grains were uniformly mixed with the sandy soil in order to determine the effect of the fine grains on the results of the dynamic probing test at each stage. The soil was then poured back into the tank and compacted using the same dynamic compaction method.

The tools that have more penetrating power have a smaller cone diameter. For example, the penetrating power of DCP with a cone diameter of 20 mm is greater than DPL with a cone diameter of 37.5 mm. For this reason, the selected cone diameter in this research is the average cone diameter of the mentioned tools. Of course, to reduce the effect of friction between the rod and the soil, the diameter of the cone should be considered larger than the diameter of the connecting rod.

A total of 35 field density test by sand cone method were carried out in such a way that the soil of the tank was removed in 10 cm layers and on each layer, the field density test was carried out according to ASTM D155635 on the spot in all depths and different places of the tank. 35 direct shear tests were performed according to the ASTM D 308036 with box dimensions of 100 × 100 mm, vertical stress of 10, 30 and 50 kPa. Also dynamic penetration tests were performed using different tools, each for 80 cm of penetration. Table 5 shows the results of the tests.

The results of dynamic penetration testing have been presented in terms of the dynamic cone resistance parameter, which makes the test results independent of the tool used and its technical specifications.

If the values have a normal distribution on the graph of residuals, it means that no particular order has been observed in their distribution. If about 95% of the values range from + 2 to −2 on the vertical axis, the distribution of the residuals can be considered as normal and the correlation as having a reasonable confidence level.

Conclusion

The current study investigated and expanded on the effectiveness of the light dynamic penetration tool in dense soil. Experimental relationships were established between the results of the dynamic probing test and the soil characteristics of relative density, dry and wet density and soil shear strength. Three new tools have been introduced for use. Sandy soil containing 5%, 10%, 15% and 25% fine silty grains was poured uniformly into a cylindrical tank with a diameter of 100 cm and a height of 120 cm and then compacted.

In addition to the dynamic probe light (DPL) and dynamic probe medium (DPM) tools, penetration tests were performed with ADP90, ADP60 and ADP25 tools. The repeatability of the results was determined by calculation of the COVs and showed that the results of the penetrometry tests had good repeatability and that the data can be trusted to produce new correlation relationships. It also was observed that the average COV of the tools introduced in this research were lower than for the DPL and DPM tools and that the lowest COV was for the ADP90 tool.

The results for the new tools showed that, although the weight of the hammer and the energy required for each blow did not change, the efficiency and penetration power of introduced tools increased compared to the DPL tool. The closeness of the test results for the ADP25 tool compared to the DPM tool indicates that they applied almost the same amount of energy to the soil and that the penetration power of both was similar. It is known that the hammer weight of ADP25 tool is about 33% that of the hammer weight of the DPM tool, which confirms the proper performance of the ADP25 tool.

In order to expand the efficiency of the tools used in this research, new experimental relationships between the dynamic cone resistance and normalized dynamic cone resistance and the geotechnical characteristics of the soil have been presented. Comparison of the distribution diagrams of the residuals and the R2 values of the experimental relationships indicates that the relationships have good validity. In general, it can be concluded that the use of dynamic penetration testing is a practical technique for determining the behavior and controlling the compaction of sand embankments in the field.
