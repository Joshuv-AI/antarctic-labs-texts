Stability and optical quality of liquid crystal films as high repetition rate plasma mirrors in staged laser-plasma accelerators Preprint: AIP/123-QED
A. Vazquez, A. Jewell, M. Cole, A. Abdi, T. K. Le, A. McIlvenny, N. Czapla, A. J. Gonsalves, K. Nakamura, D. W. Schumacher, Z. Eisentraut, C. B. Schroeder, J. van Tilborg, J. Osterhoff, E. Esarey, L. Obst-Huebl

Abstract
Staging multiple laser-plasma accelerators (LPAs), each driven by its own laser pulse, is a promising technique for reaching higher particle energies than achievable in a single LPA. However, introducing subsequent laser pulses for post-acceleration in staged LPA geometries presents a significant challenge for conventional optics. Due to the focused, high-intensity nature of the laser beam interacting with the electron beam, the fluence on the coupling mirror surpasses the damage threshold of any solid-state optic. Replenishable plasma mirrors (PMs) offer a promising solution to this inherent restriction from high-energy pulses; however, they require a high stability and optical quality, and need to be produced at high repetition rates that match the needs to the staging experiment. Here, we investigate the properties of 4-octyl-4’-cyanobiphenyl (8CB) liquid crystal (LC) films, produced with our new "windmill" film formation device, for this application. We conduct this investigation by observing the reliability of film formation at high repetition rates, characterizing the reflected beam profile, and pointing stability as a function of temperature and film formation speed, including film flatness and surface quality, using low-power laser reflection measurements. Our measurements demonstrate that 8CB LC films exhibit unprecedented microscopic surface quality, crucial for preserving laser pulse properties upon reflection. We also show consistent film formation suitable for operation up to 0.5 Hz with great film to film angular stability. The results allow us to conclude that 8CB LC films hold significant potential as reliable and cost-effective PMs that are suitable for coupling LPA stages.

Introduction:

Modern high-power laser facilities

. High power lasers are used in the development of novel laser-plasma accelerators (LPAs)
Tajima and Dawson 1979

Esarey 2009
. To achieve the multi-10 GeV electron energies required for many applications, LPAs can employ
multistage coupling
, where the electron beam from one LPA is injected into a subsequent, independently laser-driven stage
Steinke 2016
. Coupling stages allow for these higher output energies to be achieved, as opposed to the limited output energy that only a single LPA stage can produce. A critical challenge in realizing LPA staging lies in the need to introduce additional, high-intensity laser pulses for post-acceleration into the tight interaction geometry of the subsequent LPA stage. For this crucial inter-stage coupling, the focused, ultra-high intensity of the added laser pulses subjects conventional optical components to laser fluences far beyond their damage threshold.

To overcome this inherent restriction and enable robust inter-stage coupling, plasma mirrors (PMs) have emerged as a promising solution
Thaury 2007
;
Sokollik 2010
. A PM typically consists of a solid state substrate, the surface of which rapidly ionizes upon the arrival of the laser pulse, transforming into a highly reflective plasma
Obst 2018
. This mechanism allows the PM to reflect at least 50% of the beam energy. However, after this interaction of a high intensity pulse with a PM, its respective region of plasma formation used is completely destroyed and needs to be replaced.

In the multi-Gev staging experiment at the BELLA Center’s Petawatt (BELLA PW) laser facility
Nakamura 2017
, a laser pulse from the first beamline drives the first LPA , producing a GeV-class electron bunch
Leemans et al. 2006
. This bunch is transported by an active plasma lens
van Tilborg 2015
and passes through a PM before entering the second LPA stage. An additional laser pulse from a second beamline
Picksley 2022
is coupled via the reflection of the PM onto the electron beam axis to drive the second LPA stage and further accelerate the electron bunch to multi-GeV energies
Stackhouse 2022
;
Sokollik 2010
. The performance of this second acceleration stage will depend strongly on the optical quality of the PM as well as the alignment of the coupled pulse.

In order to enable continuous, uninterrupted experimental PM operation at the pulse repetition rate of the laser (1 Hz for the BELLA PW), systems rely on tape drives (e.g. VHS tapes of 15 µm thickness) that are moved to a fresh, undamaged location after each shot
Barber 2020
;
Shaw 2016
. Previous work at the BELLA Center demonstrated that tape drives are a practical solution for staging requirements, where at an intensity of 4
\text{x}10^{17}
W/cm
2
at the tape surface, the wavefront quality of the reflected pulse was conserved with a peak reflectance of 82%
Shaw 2016
. However, utilizing a tape drive in staging poses risks to the safety of the surrounding equipment due to the high amount of debris that is generated on each shot, and reduces electron bunch quality due to emittance growth as the bunch travels through the tape PM
Zingale 2021
. For these reasons, current PM technology presents a considerable challenge for the sustained operation of high-power, high-repetition-rate LPA systems. While thin film PMs delivered via a tape drive (e.g., 15 µm thick VHS tape) have served as a practical solution for meeting high-fluence redirection and replenishment requirements in staging experiments
Sokollik 2010
;
Shaw 2016
, they present significant limitations for optimal staged LPA performance. A primary concern is the inherent thickness of these films, which, even at 15-20 µm, leads to significant electron beam emittance degradation due to Coulomb scattering, severely impacting overall beam quality and the efficiency of multi-stage coupling of LPAs
Zingale 2021
;
van Tilborg 2015
. This thickness also contributes to a significant amount of debris production that can damage surrounding optics and stages in the long-term.

For the staging experiment at the BELLA PW, the PM formation device used to couple in the second laser pulse needs to fulfill several essential tasks for directing the additional laser pulse and electron bunch to the subsequent LPA stage:

1.

The PM formation device needs to be able to replenish PMs at high repetition rates (1 Hz at the BELLA PW) with low debris production after each interaction.

2.

The produced PM needs to be as thin as possible to minimize emittance growth of an electron bunch as it passes through.

3.

The PMs should minimize additional aberrations to the reflected laser pulses, meaning they should not add significantly to the inherent ~40 nm wavefront root-mean-square (RMS) error of the BELLA PW laser pulse
Picksley 2022
.

4.

The orientation of each new PM surface should be stable from film to film in order to minimize angular offsets of the reflected beam. It should also not exceed 150 µrad, corresponding to a 15 µm lateral offset at the entrance of the second LPA stage (inherent angular pointing jitter is ~1.5 µrad jitter
Nakamura 2017
).

Furthermore, the surface roughness of available tape materials can be a considerable drawback. Even after optimization, the root-mean-square (RMS) wavefront error of the tape was recently measured as 43 ± 9 nm
Stackhouse 2022
. This intrinsic surface roughness contributes to wavefront distortions of the reflected beam
Shaw 2016
. Additionally, achieving a sufficiently flat surface often requires extensive searching and movement around different regions of the tape after each new section is brought into position
Shaw 2016
, highlighting a fundamental lack of reproducible surface quality for continuous, uninterrupted, high-precision operation.

Looking to improve the performance of PMs, previous work at the BELLA Center has achieved promising results using liquid crystal (LC) films composed of the compound 4-octyl-4’-cyanobiphenyl (commonly known as 8CB). These studies demonstrated high-fluence laser beam redirection
Czapla 2025
with improved pulse contrast and negligible electron beam emittance growth
Zingale 2021
. Czapla
et al.
demonstrated reflection of 20 J, 35 fs laser pulses from a novel double plasma mirror (DPM) using free-standing LC films
Czapla 2025
. The DPM, composed of two spinning-film disk inserters, demonstrated a total throughput exceeding 80%, a reflectivity significantly higher than most previously reported DPM systems. The DPM also achieved a maximum contrast enhancement of 2-3 orders of magnitude, a vital result for mitigating detrimental pre-pulses in ultra-intense laser experiments
Czapla 2025
. Additionall, using a single 20 nm thin LC PM, Zingale
et al.
measured ~100 nm of emittance growth on a transmitted 4 µm, 0.84 GeV electron beam, confirming the film’s low disturbance to beam quality
Zingale 2021
.

These results are encouraging for furthering the technological development and use of LC films in multi-staged LPAs. However, crucial questions remain surrounding the stability of LC film formation at high repetition rates, LC film quality, and angular pointing fluctuation per film formed.

We characterized 8CB liquid crystal films produced by our "windmill" formation device, evaluating their optical quality by measuring the wavefront RMS and the stability of the film’s orientation. We also studied its characteristics by analyzing its stability in reliable film formation over long periods of time and various operating conditions in preparation of implementing the assembly into the staging experiment at the BELLA PW.

II
Liquid Crystal Film Formation and Test Setup

We investigate the use of LC films, specifically those formed from 4-octyl-4’-cyanobiphenyl (8CB)
Poole 2016a
;
Poole 2016b
;
Poole 2015
;
Schumacher 2017
for their application as PMs in an LPA staging experiment. 8CB has a smectic phase that allows for the formation of free-standing, ultra-thin (10 nm-50 µm
Schumacher 2017
) films. Consequently, a small volume of LC can generate thousands of films, providing an inexpensive solution that produces minimal debris. We investigate forming these films using a custom-built windmill apparatus, depositing a thin layer of 8CB across a 10 mm diameter aperture using lens tissue wipers. Maintaining the temperature of the LC in the smectic phase (21.5-33.5
∘
C
Poole 2015
) allows the intrinsic surface tension of the PM to produce an optically smooth film surface with nearly uniform film thickness
Poole 2016a
. If the assembly is operated outside of this smectic phase, it could transition into unusable phases such as: crystalline (<21.5
∘
C), nematic (poor film formation; 33.5-40.5
∘
C), or liquid phase (>40.5
∘
C)
Poole 2015
. In the smectic phase, 8CB films behave as dynamically tunable optical elements, offering a unique advantage for minimizing the emittance growth of electrons in PM applications. By precisely controlling parameters such as wiper speed and temperature, we can fine-tune the film thickness to optimize for both minimal emittance growth and high repetition rate film formation
Schumacher 2017
. As the high-intensity laser pulse interacts with the LC film, it rapidly ionizes the primarily carbon and hydrogen based 8CB, creating a dense, highly reflective plasma. The potential 8CB has for relatively consistent film formation, as well as other advantages listed in this section, offers significant benefits in terms of operational efficiency and cost-effectiveness for high-repetition-rate, high-intensity laser systems
Poole 2016a
.

We constructed a test setup to characterize the performance of our windmill film formation device using a low power, 5 mW 532 nm diode beam (Figure 1a). The diagnostic beam was expanded to approximately 6 mm in diameter so that we can use a subsequent iris just before the windmill to reduce the beam size in a flexible range onto the LC film. A photograph of the setup inside the vacuum chamber is displayed in Figure 1b. At the center of our test setup was a windmill assembly (Figure 1c). The assembly featured a stepper motor that controls a 12-arm rotor which drags thin, 11 mm square pieces of Thorlabs lens tissues through a reservoir of 8CB and over an aperture at the top of a polished aluminum plate (Figure 1d). As the LC-coated tissue traversed the aperture, it deposited a meniscus of the material which was then drawn across the opening, forming a stable, freestanding film. It is important to note that in this test setup, we assume the repetition rate of film formation refers to a wiper speed used where films are formed with all 12 wipers attached.

The application in the BELLA staging experiment poses requirements to the assembly’s compactness and vacuum compatibility. The stepper motor generates considerable heat that, due to our compact setup, is conducted directly to the LC reservoir. This thermal load can raise the 8CB’s temperature beyond its smectic phase range, thereby preventing the formation of stable, freestanding films. The smectic phase was maintained by using a ThermoTek T257P-20 Thermoelectric Recirculating Chiller, mounted to the motor but slightly separated from the back surface of the windmill, with a thermocouple monitoring both the surface and motor temperature (Figure 2b). The LC was placed onto the lens tissues manually at an amount that was just enough to saturate the material. The stepper motor was then run for 10-15 minutes at a slow speed to evenly distribute newly applied LC. The film aperture was wiped of excess LC bunching on the edge, and the system was ready for measurements. If this conditioning step was not performed, uneven accumulation of excess LC at the aperture perimeter was found, degrading the reflected wavefront and introducing varying surface orientations from film to film. Due to geometric restrictions in the test chamber, only four to six arms were utilized. The 2 mm diagnostic beam was incident onto the center of the film at a 38-degree angle of incidence with respect to the film surface. In the BELLA staging experiment, all 12 arms will be used for film formation at a 45-degree angle of incidence on the film surface. We found through testing that our 10 mm diameter film had a central flat region diameter of 3-4 mm. The 2 mm diameter beam was chosen for all of our tests as this will be the beam size of the incoming 2BL beam in our staging experiments. The diameter of the LC film is important to note because as the diameter of the film increases, so too does the central flat region
Poole 2015
. A portion of the reflected beam was sent through a second beam expander and collimated downstream to a Thorlabs Shack-Hartmann Wavefront Sensor (WFS20-7AR), and the other beamline was focused to a far-field CCD camera to measure the LC film’s pointing stability.

As mentioned previously, we characterized the impact of LC films formed with our windmill device on reflected wavefront quality and beam pointing stability. We used a two-lens relay system to image a plane 110 mm downstream of the windmill to the wavefront sensor. A reference wavefront was collected using a flat gold mirror in the location of the LC film. Optical aberrations introduced by the LC films were isolated by subtracting this reference wavefront from the wavefront data collected with LC films. To properly assess the film’s optical quality, our analysis focused only on its intrinsic, higher-order aberrations. Therefore, we computationally removed the lowest-order Zernike coefficients (piston, tip, tilt, and focus) from the wavefront measurement.

To quantify the shot-to-shot angular stability of the film for our staging experiment, we measured the centroid location of a reflected diagnostic beam on our far-field camera and used it to calculate the beam angle onto the camera. Using the lens system’s ray transfer (ABCD) matrix, we then derived the beam’s angle after reflection of the film.

III
Achieving High Beam Quality and Stability with High-Repetition-Rate Liquid Crystal Films

We characterized the optical performance of the liquid crystal films by measuring the reflected wavefront, pointing stability, and the success rate of film formation, across a range of operating conditions. We measured these properties at various film wiping speeds and assembly temperatures. An example of a typical laser wavefront profile after LC film reflection and subtraction of the reference wavefront can be seen in Figure 2. All measured wavefronts exhibited a consistent, astigmatism-dominated spatial structure, shown in Figure 2, which features a prominent peak on the left and a valley in the lower-left. Although this characteristic profile was stable, the values of RMS and peak-to-valley (PV) values varied from shot to shot.

This resilience to thermal fluctuations is a critical advantage, as it ensures reliable performance even if the surface temperature varies during an experiment.

IV
Discussion

Our results confirm that LC films formed with our windmill assembly are a promising option for the demanding application of a PM in a staged laser-plasma accelerator. This is built upon foundational work at the BELLA Center which demonstrated that ultra-thin LC films fulfill critical requirements for this application. The need for a medium thin enough to minimize electron beam emittance growth was demonstrated by Zingale
et al.
, who measured a low 100 nm of emittance growth using a 20 nm thick LC film
Zingale 2021
, a dramatic improvement over the significant degradation caused by 15 µm thick tape drives. Similarly, the ability to redirect high-fluence pulses was shown by Czapla
et al.
, demonstrating high reflectivity and contrast enhancement
Czapla 2025
. While this foundational work was promising, three key operational requirements for the staging experiment remained unverified: the need for the added wavefront error to be significantly less than the intrinsic 40 nm RMS of the PW beam, the need for angular pointing stability of the film to be within 150 µrad, and the ability to be replenished at high repetition rates. This work provides the first comprehensive characterization of these critical parameters at a high repetition rate film formation, directly addressing these gaps.

A primary contribution of this study is the characterization of the film’s intrinsic optical quality. Our measurements show the 8CB films contribute an average wavefront error between 11–24 nm RMS. This represents a significant improvement over the 43 ± 9 nm RMS reported for tape drive surfaces and is substantially lower than the BELLA PW laser’s intrinsic wavefront of approximately 40 nm RMS
Stackhouse 2022
;
Nakamura 2017
. This result is critical, as it confirms that the LC film can be a less dominant source of aberration, thereby preserving the quality of the driving laser pulse as it is directed into the second acceleration stage.
Our characterizations were conducted with a low-power diagnostic laser. However, the consistency with the aforementioned high-power experiments indicates that the low-power surface quality measured here is highly representative of the reflection conditions when a plasma is formed, lending strong confidence to our conclusions.

Equally important is the pointing stability required for precise beam coupling, which demands that the beam angle entering the second stage remain within 150 µrad, corresponding to a 15 µm lateral offset. Our measurements revealed film-to-film angular fluctuations ranging from approximately 180 to 400 µrad across the tested operating conditions, where the vertical pointing already fulfilled the <150 µrad requirement in our tests. Based on our scan of operating speeds, we do not expect that the amount of angular fluctuation will significantly grow when further increasing the wiping speed towards 1 Hz. These values are also within the same order of magnitude to the average 290 µrad fluctuations reported for tape drives
Stackhouse 2022
. They are well within the corrective range of a standard motorized tip/tilt gimbal mount. By implementing such an active feedback system, the residual pointing error can be reduced to tens of µrad to meet the experimental tolerance.

To demonstrate this capability, we performed a preliminary pointing correction test using a hexapod mount. After a film was formed, we measured the beam’s pointing offset and manually adjusted the horizontal and vertical angle of the windmill assembly to compensate. This preliminary test showed a factor of ~10 improvement in both the vertical and horizontal axes. This level of improvement is directly comparable to the active stabilization demonstrated on tape drives, where the RMS error in the horizontal direction decreased from 61 µrad to 12 µrad and in the vertical direction decreased from 290 µrad to 12 µrad with pointing correction
Stackhouse 2022
. This confirms that even a basic, shot-to-shot manual correction is sufficient, and a fully automated active stabilization system can be used to improve the reflected high power laser coupling into the second LPA stage.

Interestingly, in part (b) and (d) of Figure 3, an oscillatory behavior can be observed in both the wavefront RMS and the beam pointing stability. As each wiper cut from a single piece of optical cleaning tissue is not identical to the next, each wiping tissue can produce distinctive films, characterized by either higher or lower RMS values relative to the average, as well as predictable beam pointing positions. Although this behavior is not always repeated (due to not every wiper producing a film at times), a dedicated investigation and possible standardized production of wipers is warranted. Also, in some instances where the wipers were not exchanged after several working days to a week (never more than two weeks), the beam was observed to drift horizontally while being wiped (example in Figure 3). We believe this could either be caused by the deterioration of the wipers, or some of the LC drying and affecting the evenness of the material on the windmill surface. It is encouraging to observe that despite this drifting in x, the beam quality was not diminished. To maintain angular pointing within experimental tolerance, we will implement an active stabilization system. This feedback loop will use a CCD camera to measure the beam’s centroid position after film formation, sending a corrective signal to a motorized gimbal mount. Our data indicates the dominant instability is horizontal; the vertical component is already within our 150 µrad pointing limit for the second LPA stage. To further optimize alignment speed and accuracy, we can also train an active stabilization model on a preliminary dataset. This would improve downstream stability and enable higher repetition rates by mitigating the primary source of angular offset.

Finally, we demonstrated reliable, high-quality film formation at repetition rates up to 0.4 Hz, while reliability of film formation decreases to approximately 50% at 0.5 Hz. While these results strongly validate the use of 8CB films for this application, the decrease in formation probability at the highest speeds highlights the need for future optimization to achieve robust 1 Hz operation. A systematic investigation into improving high-speed performance could explore several promising avenues: operating at different temperatures to alter the liquid crystal’s viscosity, testing alternative wiper geometries and materials for more consistent deposition, and applying different surface treatments to the windmill aperture to improve film adhesion. Furthermore, exploring other liquid crystal compounds or mixtures may yield even more favorable properties for high-speed film formation.

Another variable found to assist in the quality of and consistency of film formation was the orientation and size of the wiping tissue. Ensuring each tissue is perpendicular to the direction of travel across the aperture is important for sufficient contact time and uniform coverage. Through trial and error, the wiping tissue was sized at approximately 11 mm in diameter and consisted of two sheets in thickness, chosen specifically to fully cover the aperture and retain a satisfactory amount of 8CB. Future tests will investigate whether varying these parameters can bring significant improvements at higher wiping speeds. Additionally, the precise amount of LC solution applied to each wiping tissue was not well controlled. Our standard practice was to apply enough LC to form an even, uniform layer on the tissue surface contacting the aperture. Standardizing and quantifying this parameter is also planned for future studies at higher operational speeds.

V
Conclusion

This work presents a strong case for LC films formed with our windmill device as an excellent plasma mirror solution for the next generation of high-repetition-rate, staged laser-plasma accelerators. Previous landmark experiments at the BELLA Center have addressed several criteria: a DPM configuration using these films successfully redirected a 20 J pulse while providing significant contrast enhancement
Czapla 2025
, and the passage of a 0.84 GeV electron beam through a single film demonstrated negligible emittance growth
Zingale 2021
. Our results add to this foundation by demonstrating consistent and reliable film formation at repetition rates up to 0.5 Hz, fulfilling the need for a truly replenishable, high-repetition-rate optic. Importantly, we have measured a high reflected wavefront quality of 11–24 nm RMS, a value that is not expected to significantly worsen the wavefront quality of the BELLA PW laser upon reflection off the PM. The shot-to-shot angular orientation of the LC films was found to be stable, requiring only minor corrective adjustments to the assembly to meet the tight pointing tolerances for injection into the second accelerator stage. We plan to use an active feedback system to realize high repetition rates and accurate alignment of the redirected drive pulse into our second LPA stage. By demonstrating high repetition rate viability, exceptional optical quality, and a clear strategy for active stabilization, this work confirms the usability of 8CB liquid crystal films as an enabling PM technology. This represents an important milestone for the successful implementation of multi-GeV staging of laser plasma accelerators.
