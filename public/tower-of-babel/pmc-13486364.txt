Emotion regulation and social information processing as targets of a guided selective prevention app for emerging adults with a history of adverse childhood experiences: A randomised controlled trial
Authors: Lina Stallmann, Sofia Jacinto, Neela Vetsch, Timo Stolz, Johanna Löchner, Simon Marmet, Thomas Berger, Jeannette Brodbeck
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdverse childhood experiences (ACEs) are associated with increased risk for psychopathology and reduced psychosocial functioning across the life course. However, digital selective prevention targeting individuals with a history of ACEs remains scarce. Impairments in emotion regulation and social information processing were confirmed as mechanisms linking ACEs with mental health and potential targets for interventions. This randomised controlled trial evaluated the uptake, efficacy, and differential component effects of the guided FACE app, a theory-based preventive intervention for emerging adults with self-reported ACEs that targets these mechanisms.MethodsEmerging adults (18–24 years) reporting at least one ACE category in a population-based cohort were invited to participate. Of 1514 eligible individuals, 167 (11%) enrolled and were randomised to immediate access to the FACE app or care as-usual with minimal intervention (CAU-MI). The 10-week intervention comprised two transdiagnostic components delivered in a cross-over design: Self- and Emotion Regulation (SER) and Social Skills and Social Information Processing (SSIP). Primary outcomes were resilience and well-being. Secondary outcomes included self-efficacy for managing emotions, social problem-solving, fear of negative evaluation, social avoidance, and self-esteem. Ecological momentary assessments captured real-life affect, burden, resilience, and social distress. Linear mixed models were applied under intention-to-treat and per-protocol principles.ResultsLinear mixed models showed significant improvements over time (intervention and CAU-MI) in resilience, self-esteem, self-efficacy for managing emotions, adaptive problem-solving, momentary positive affect. Group × time interaction effects in favour of the app compared to CAU-MI emerged for some more proximal mechanism-related outcomes: self-efficacy for managing emotions, fear of negative evaluation and negative problem orientation but not for resilience and well-being, which were already at normative levels at baseline. Effects were largely maintained at follow-up. Improvements were stronger and more consistent following the SER compared to the SSIP component. Average time of app use was three hours.ConclusionsThe FACE app did not improve the primary distal outcomes resilience and well-being beyond the CAU-MI condition. Nevertheless, the FACE app demonstrated modest but consistent improvements in proximal mechanisms associated with ACE-related vulnerability. Findings support the relevance of targeting emotion regulation and social-cognitive processes in digital selective prevention. However, limited uptake highlights challenges in active recruitment of unselected high risk populations and underscores the importance of integrating preventive apps into stepped-care or blended care models.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov NCT05824182

Introduction

Adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) include physical or emotional abuse and neglect, sexual abuse, witnessing domestic violence and peer victimisation. ACEs increase the risk for mental health problems across the life course such as depression, anxiety or Posttraumatic Stress Disorder (Halliday et al., 2021; Hashim et al., 2024; Kim and Royle, 2025; Rijlaarsdam et al., 2021; Trompeter et al., 2024). Although many individuals with ACEs do not meet diagnostic criteria, they frequently exhibit modifiable vulnerabilities such as difficulties in emotion regulation and social information processing (Brodbeck et al., 2025; Yeo et al., 2024). These vulnerabilities may contribute to later mental health problems across the life course (McLaughlin et al., 2020). Thus, selective prevention for individuals with a history of ACEs may be critical in mitigating long-term mental health effects. Emerging adulthood (approximately 18–24 years) represents a particularly relevant period for selective prevention. During this developmental phase with increasing autonomy, tasks such as identity formation, establishing intimate relationships, and navigating educational or occupational transitions may exacerbate adversity-related vulnerabilities, while simultaneously provide unique opportunities for adaptive change and resilience-building (Arnett, 2000).

Transdiagnostic approaches to selective prevention

Over the past decade, transdiagnostic interventions have become an important paradigm in clinical psychology. Unlike disorder-specific treatments, transdiagnostic interventions target psychological mechanisms that contribute to a broad range of mental health problems (Dalgleish et al., 2020). They may offer a more efficient response to the often overlapping nature and common aetiology of different mental health problems. Transdiagnostic interventions either apply a common set of therapeutic strategies across diagnoses, as in the Unified Protocol (Barlow et al., 2020) or focus on modifying shared mechanisms underlying multiple disorders (Dalgleish et al., 2020).

One frequently targeted mechanism is emotion regulation. Emotion regulation refers to the processes by which individuals influence their emotional experience and expression and includes strategies such as situation selection, cognitive change, and response modulation (Gross, 1998, Gross, 2015). Emotion regulation difficulties are implicated across multiple mental disorders (Sloan et al., 2017).

Beyond emotion regulation, social cognition and social information processing are increasingly recognised as transdiagnostic processes relevant to mental health and social functioning (Cotter et al., 2018; Crick and Dodge, 1994; Lakhani et al., 2021; McLaughlin et al., 2020; Puzzo et al., 2026; Spithoven et al., 2017). Social cognition refers to understanding oneself and others in social contexts, whereas social information processing involves encoding, interpreting, and responding to social cues, which may become biased toward threat or hostility (Crick and Dodge, 1994; Lakhani et al., 2021).

Emotion regulation and social information processing as mechanisms linking ACEs to psychopathology and intervention targets

Among the transdiagnostic mechanisms implicated in mental health, emotion regulation and social information processing appear particularly relevant in the context of ACEs. The Transdiagnostic Model of Mechanisms Linking Childhood Trauma to Psychopathology (McLaughlin et al., 2020) posits that emotion processing and social information processing are malleable key mechanisms through which ACEs increase vulnerability to psychopathology. Emotion processing includes heightened emotional reactivity and impaired emotion regulation. Social information processing includes for example enhanced threat detection and hostile attribution bias. Recent evidence corroborates that emotion regulation and social information processing mediate the association between ACEs and later psychosocial functioning and mental health (Brodbeck et al., 2025; Minihan et al., 2023; Miu et al., 2022; Vogel et al., 2025), making them promising targets for preventive interventions.

Interventions targeting emotion regulation aim to enhance individuals' capacity to identify, understand, and flexibly modulate emotional responses using adaptive strategies such as cognitive reappraisal, problem solving, and behavioural activation, see for example REMOTION (Bielinski et al., 2020, Bielinski et al., 2023). One important aspect and outcome of emotion regulation interventions is self-efficacy for managing emotions (SEME), an individuals' beliefs in their capacity to regulate emotional responses. A recent meta-analysis showed that low SEME was associated with psychological maladjustment, e.g. behaviours that do not correspond to social norms and are not functional for one's or other's wellbeing as well as negative emotional states. In contrast, high SEME was associated with positive adjustment e.g. prosocial behaviour, well-being and positive emotions (Alessandri et al., 2023). This highlights its relevance as a transdiagnostic intervention target (Southward et al., 2024). Social problem-solving as self-regulation skill integrates cognitive, affective, and behavioural processes to manage internal and external demands. Individuals with ACEs showed impaired problem-solving skills, which mediated the relationship between violence or abuse and poorer mental and physical health outcomes (Alvarez et al., 2022; Dehghan Manshadi et al., 2024; Gerin et al., 2024; Sheffler et al., 2019).

Interventions targeting social skills and social information processing aim to foster social relationships and modify maladaptive interpersonal appraisals—such as hostile attribution biases or exaggerated fear of negative evaluation. Fear of negative evaluation partially mediates the association between childhood maltreatment and social anxiety, while social avoidance has been shown to mediate interpersonal distress following childhood trauma (Dong and Cao1, 2025; Zhang et al., 2025).

By targeting self- and emotion regulation and social information processing as mechanisms linking ACEs with mental health, the FACE app aims to foster indicators of positive mental health, i.e., resilience, well-being, and self-esteem. Resilience, defined as the ability to adapt positively and recover from adversity, trauma, or significant stress (Connor and Davidson, 2003), is a relevant target for selective prevention efforts among this group. Fostering well-being is relevant as not all individuals with a history of ACEs develop clinical diagnoses but many suffer from reduced well-being, which can hinder adequate coping (Loechner et al., 2020). Furthermore, ACEs and child maltreatment were associated with lower levels of self-esteem, which mediated the association between ACEs and depressive symptoms (Kim et al., 2022).

Digital prevention interventions for young people

Digital interventions have established their efficacy for common mental disorders such as depression and anxiety disorders for adults and adolescents (Andersson et al., 2014; Carlbring et al., 2018; Catarino et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2023). However, fewer empirical studies have evaluated selective digital prevention interventions in high-risk populations of young adults. A recent meta-analysis of face-to-face and digital interventions of transdiagnostic interventions among youth with elevated distress established that cognitive behavioural therapy was an effective transdiagnostic approach for treating depression, anxiety and improving life satisfaction (Radunz et al., 2025). The most frequent interventions were cognitive and emotional literacy, psychoeducation, goal setting, and problem-solving. The delivery format moderated the outcomes: Face-to-face interventions showed significant effects for depression and anxiety, but digital interventions did not.

This pattern is reflected in individual trials on digital preventive interventions for adolescents and young adults. In the ECoWeB PREVENT trial for at-risk young adults, only the cognitive-behavioural intervention reduced depressive symptoms at short-term follow-up, with no effects on well-being (Watkins et al., 2024a, Watkins et al., 2024b). Similarly, the ECoWeB PROMOTE trial for healthy young adults found no effects on mental well-being or emotional competence. The JoyPop app aimed to promote resilience through the use of self-regulatory skills such as emotion regulation and executive functioning. It did not improve resilience or executive functioning, but showed dose–response associations with improvements in emotion regulation and depressive symptoms, particularly among participants with higher ACE exposure (MacIsaac et al., 2021). Together, these findings suggest that in less burdened populations, positive mental health outcomes such as improved resilience and well-being may be more difficult to achieve than symptoms of psychopathology.

Although many (transdiagnostic) digital interventions are likely to include participants with ACE histories, they were generally not designed specifically for this population or address childhood adversity explicitly. This distinction is important because emerging adults with a history of ACEs may have intervention needs beyond improving emotion regulation or maladaptive social information processing, such as understanding and contextualising the lasting impact of childhood adversity. One exception is the SELFIE intervention, a blended programme integrating ecological momentary intervention with three sessions delivered by trained mental health professionals (Reininghaus et al., 2024). It was specifically developed for young people with childhood adversity but did not explicitly address ACEs through psychoeducation or ACE-focused intervention content. Compared to care as usual, SELFIE improved self-esteem.

To our knowledge, no digital preventive intervention has combined psychoeducation on ACEs, resilience, and developmental trajectories with mechanism-based components targeting emotion regulation and social information processing in the context of childhood adversity. Such an integrated interventions may help emerging adults contextualise, understand and normalise adversity-related difficulties while fostering resilience and adaptive coping.

The present study

Although digital interventions for young adults have expanded rapidly, evidence for selective digital prevention and among emerging adults with ACEs remains limited. Three important gaps remain. First, it remains unknown whether a digital intervention specifically designed for emerging adults with ACEs can improve positive mental health outcomes, such as resilience and well-being, as well as proximal mechanism-related intervention targets, including emotion regulation and social information processing. Second, it is unknown whether improvements in these proximal targets translate into broader gains in resilience and well-being. Third, no digital prevention study has directly compared intervention components targeting emotion regulation and social information processing.

To address these gaps, we developed the FACE app, a guided digital preventive intervention specifically designed for emerging adults with ACEs. It targets two theoretically grounded transdiagnostic components, Self- and Emotion Regulation (SER) and Social Skills and Social Information Processing (SSIP). In addition, FACE explicitly integrates psychoeducation on ACEs, resilience, and developmental adaptation (see below). Further details are available in the study protocol (Brodbeck et al., 2024) and the ClinicalTrials.gov preregistration.

The first goal of the FACE trial was to evaluate the efficacy of the FACE intervention compared with a minimal intervention care-as-usual (CAU-MI) control group. We hypothesized that at post-measurement participants receiving the FACE intervention would show greater improvements in resilience and well-being (primary outcomes), as well as in SEME, problem-solving, fear of negative evaluation, social avoidance, self-esteem, and aggregated real-life ecological momentary assessment (EMA) outcomes including affective states, social distress, overall burden and resilience. We further expected these improvements to be maintained at follow-up.

The second goal of the FACE trial was to compare the component effects of SER and SSIP. We hypothesized that SER would specifically improve SEME and problem-solving, as well as aggregated momentary affective states assessed with EMA. Similarly, we expected that SSIP would specifically improve fear of negative evaluation and social avoidance as well as aggregated momentary social distress. We additionally compared participant satisfaction with the two components and explored whether the order of component delivery (SER–SSIP vs. SSIP–SER) influenced outcomes.

Method

Study design

This unblinded randomised controlled trial compared an immediate intervention group (IG) with a care-as-usual control group with a minimal intervention (CAU-MI, Goldberg et al., 2023) group. The CAU-MI group continued to have access to routine healthcare services for psychosocial problems available in the community (e.g. counselling, psychotherapy or general practitioner). They received a supportive screening phone call and repeated assessments of outcome variables. In addition, they completed two weeks of ecological momentary assessment (EMA) self-monitoring on affective states, momentary burden, resilience and social distress with feedback on affect and social distress. The CAU-MI group received access to the FACE app after a waiting period of 12 weeks. Using a cross-over design for the intervention, all participants receive both components but in a randomised order (SER-SSIP vs SSIP-SER). This enables the examination of component-specific and sequencing effects (see Fig. 1). Participants were randomised using a computer-generated allocation sequence implemented in the Camunda process orchestration platform integrated in the web platform used to deliver the intervention. Randomisation was conducted in two steps, (1) allocation to the IG or CAU-MI (1:2 ratio) and (2) allocation of intervention participants to the SER–SSIP or SSIP–SER sequence (1:1 ratio)sequence). Group allocation remained concealed until after completion of the baseline assessment. Gender and number of ACEs were balanced across conditions during randomisation.Fig. 1Overview of the study design.Fig. 1

Overview of the study design.

Assessments were conducted at t0 (baseline), at T1 five weeks later (intermediate, after completion of the first component), and at T2 12 weeks after baseline (IG: post-intervention; CAU-MI: post waiting-period/ pre-intervention). After receiving access to the intervention, participants in the CAU-MI group also filled out an intermediate and a post-intervention assessment. A follow-up assessment (fu/T3) was conducted 12 weeks after completion of the intervention. EMA were collected six times per day over seven consecutive days before randomisation, after the first and after the second component and in the CAU-MI group also in the middle of the waiting period. Participants received a small monetary compensation for every survey completed at the end of the trial.

The study was funded by the Swiss National Science Foundation (SNF; grant no. 100010_197731) and approved by the Ethical Committee of Northwestern and Central Switzerland (BASEC number 2022–01143). The trial was prospectively registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05824182, 19. April 2023).

Recruitment and participant characteristics

Participants were recruited from the FACE epidemiological study, a random, list-based general population sample of emerging adults in German-speaking Switzerland. The study assessed multiple forms of ACEs including child maltreatment, household dysfunctions and peer victimisation (Brodbeck et al., 2024). Detailed prevalence rates of ACEs are reported in Marmet et al. (2025). Emerging adults who reported at least one ACE category were invited to participate in the randomised controlled trial (RCT) via email. Email invitations were sent out between May 2023 and May 2024. After providing written informed consent, participants received access to the online questionnaires. Eligibility and suitability were assessed in a brief telephone screening; participants with acute crises were referred to appropriate services. Inclusion criteria were self-reported ACE in the FACE epidemiological study, written informed consent, possession of a smartphone, and sufficient mastery of the German language. Exclusion criteria were acute suicidality and inability to follow study procedures, e.g., due to visual impairment.

Measures

All data were collected via online self-report questionnaires. EMA data were collected within the web app. LimeSurvey was used to assess all other primary and secondary outcomes. ACEs, lifetime professional help-seeking, and background characteristics were assessed as part of the FACE epidemiological study using REDCap and linked to the intervention data.

Primary outcomes related to distal mental health outcomes

Resilience was assessed with the 10-item German version of the widely used Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC (Sarubin et al., 2015); McDonald's ω = 0.82–0.88 across time points). The items were rated on a 5-point scale ranging from 0 = not true at all to 4 = true nearly all the time.

Well-being was assessed using the Warwick-Edinburgh Mental Well-being Scale (WEMWBS; German version: (Lang and Bachinger, 2017; Peitz et al., 2024; English version: Tennant et al., 2007) McDonald's ω = 0.89–0.92 across time points). The scale contained 14 items rated on a 5-point scale ranging from 1 = never to 4 = always.

Secondary outcomes related to SER

Self-efficacy for managing emotions was assessed with the PROMIS Short Form v1.0 – Self-Efficacy for Managing Emotions (PROMIS-SEME, PROMIS, 2015; McDonald's ω = 0.83–0.89 across time points). The item for coping with distress related to a disease was omitted. The remaining 7 items were rated on a 5-point scale ranging from 1 = never to 4 = always.

Problem-solving was assessed with the German version of the short form of the Social Problem-Solving Inventory-Revised (SPSI-R; German version: Graf, 2003; English version D'Zurilla et al., 2002; Sorsdahl et al., 2017). The scale contained 25 items rated on a 5-point scale from 0 = not at all true to 4 = extremely true. Five subscales with 5 items each assess positive problem orientation (PPO, McDonald's ω = 0.74–0.79 across time points), negative problem orientation (NPO, McDonald's ω = 0.79–0.85 across time points), rational problem solving (RPS, McDonald's ω = 0.65–0.79 across time points), impulsivity–carelessness style (ICS, McDonald's ω = 0.61–0.70 across time points, apart from ω = 0.41 for T1 for the CAU-MI group), and avoidant style (AS, McDonald's ω = 0.82–0.88 across time points).

Secondary outcomes related to SSIP

Fear of negative evaluation was assessed with the German short version of the Fear of Negative Evaluation Scale (SANB-5; Kemper et al., 2012; McDonald's ω = 0.88–0.90 across time points). The scale contained 5 items rated on a 4-point Likert scale from 1 = almost never true to 4 = almost always true.

Social avoidance was assessed with the subscale social-behavioural avoidance from the German version of the cognitive-behavioural avoidance scale (CBAS; Röthlin et al., 2010; McDonald's ω = 0.85–0.90 across time points). The subscale contained 8 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale from 1 = not at all applicable to 5 = absolutely applicable.

As further secondary outcome not related to SER and SSIP, self-esteem was assessed with the German version of the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale (RSES; Collani and Herzberg, 2003; McDonald's ω = 0.90–0.93 across time points). The scale contained 10 items rated on a 4-point scale from 1 = not at all true to 4 = extremely true.

Acceptance of the intervention was assessed using the German version of the Patient Satisfaction Questionnaire (CSQ-8; Schmidt et al., 1989; McDonald's ω = 0.88–0.90 across time points), a self-report instrument slightly adapted to evaluate overall satisfaction with digital interventions. The scale comprised eight items rated on a 4-point Likert scale. For four items, response categories were reversed so that all items are coded consistently from 1 = not satisfied at all to 4 = highly satisfied.

Ecological momentary assessments

EMA was used to assess real-life, short-term states. Assessments were conducted at each assessment point, except at follow-up. To capture highly variable daily fluctuations in affect while balancing participant burden, a relatively short assessment period (one week) with frequent daily SMS prompts (six equally time-distributed prompts per day) was implemented. All items were rated on a 7-point scale from 0 – not at all to 6 – extremely. Momentary affect was assessed with an abbreviated 12-item German version of the Positive and Negative Affect Scale (PANAS; Wichers et al., 2012). As state-sensitive measures, momentary burden was assessed with the item”At the moment, I feel burdened”; resilience with the item”At the moment, I feel up to the current situation” and social distress with the item “At the moment, my relationships with other people are a burden to me”.

Study intervention

The guided FACE app is based on the transdiagnostic model of mechanisms linking childhood trauma to psychopathology (McLaughlin et al., 2020). It addresses emotion regulation and social information processing, as well as psychoeducation about ACEs and their impact (Brodbeck et al., 2024). The first module provided information on different forms of ACEs, testimonials, and explained the interplay of risk and protective factors, as well as resilience. Then, the thematic component Self- and Emotion Regulation (SER) targets emotion regulation including problem solving. The Social Skills and Social Information Processing (SSIP) targets interpersonal appraisal and behaviour. Each component consisted of four modules offering psychoeducation materials and exercises in various formats (text, audio, video) and a toolbox with exercises that can easily implemented in daily life (see Table 1). Participants were encouraged to work through one module per week. The app used well-established cognitive-behavioural principles and also adapted content of different existing web-based self-help interventions such as the ECoWeb app for addressing social cognition and emotion regulation (Edge et al., 2021; Newbold et al., 2020), the SOLUS program for addressing loneliness and social activities (Seewer et al., 2022, Seewer et al., 2024), the HERMES program for problem-solving (Bur et al., 2022), the LEAVES program for emotion regulation, social relationships, and activities (Brodbeck et al., 2022) and the REMOTION program for emotion regulation (Bielinski et al., 2020, Bielinski et al., 2023). In addition to the thematic components and their related toolboxes, ecological momentary assessments served as self-monitoring tool for the week before and after each component. Participants received feedback related to their reported positive and negative affect as well as social distress in the form of a line graph summarising their responses over the previous days.

The guidance during the 10-week intervention consisted of weekly written feedback and support from an e-coach, typically a trained and supervised master's student in Clinical Psychology. This weekly individualized message provided feedback on progress and focus and acknowledged and encouraged participants in their engagement with the app.

Statistical analyses

Linear mixed models (LMM) were used to test the general efficacy (IG vs CAU-MI) and the differential component and sequence effects (SER vs SSIP and SER-SSIP vs SSIP-SER) with interactions between time and group. LMM have the advantage that they can account for missing values through maximum likelihood estimation (Graham, 2009).

For the differential component effects analyses, data from both IG and CAU-MI group were pooled to obtain more robust estimates. To ensure an equivalent starting point for the intervention phase, the post-wait assessment in the CAU-MI group was treated as the pre-intervention assessment. The intermediate assessment following the first intervention component served as the post-intervention assessment. Sensitivity analyses including initial group allocation (IG vs CAU-MI) as a factor in the models yielded no substantively different results.

EMA measures were analysed as mean values across the respective week, aggregated within individuals. Fixed effects thus consisted of group/condition, time and the interaction. Random effects were fit with intercepts and slopes on the participant level. The significance level was two-sided at p ≤ .05. To quantify interaction effect sizes, paired t-tests were conducted on the observed pre intervention values and the estimated post values derived from the linear mixed models for each group or condition respectively. Cohen's d and Hedges' g to account for varying group sizes were calculated. As they did not yield different results, only Cohen's d is reported. Model-estimated post-intervention values account for the fitted model structure and therefore typically have smaller standard deviations than the observed data. Therefore, additional effect sizes based on the observed pre and post intervention values were calculated. These are reported where they differed meaningfully from the model-based estimates. In addition, Reliable Change Indices (Jacobson and Truax, 1991) were calculated based on each outcome's standard deviation at the pre-measurement and internal consistency.

Analyses were conducted according to the intention-to-treat (ITT) as well as the per-protocol (PP) paradigm. The criterion for the PP analyses was a minimum of two hours of engagement with the app for the general efficacy analyses, and one hour of engagement in the first component for the differential component effects. No formal correction for multiple testing was applied as analyses were theory-driven with a priori specified hypotheses. Interpretation focused on the consistency and robustness of group × time interaction effects and corresponding effect sizes across analyses.

Power analyses for a linear mixed-effects model conducted in R using the simr package indicate a required sample size of 90 for the comparison of the intervention and CAU-MI group (30 CAU-MI and 60 IG) assuming a medium effect size corresponding to Cohen's d = 0.50 (three repeated measurements nested within participants). For the comparison of SSIP and SER at the intermediate assessment (two repeated measurements), we assume a small effect size corresponding to Cohen's d = 0.30. This results in a required sample size of 210 (fixed effect for time and condition and their interaction; participant-level random intercept, intraclass correlation 0.70, α = 0.05, 1–β = 0.80, simulations with 1000 iterations).

Results

Participants

All 1514 participants who reported at least one ACE category within the FACE epidemiological study were invited to participate in the intervention study. A total of 167 participants (11%) gave informed consent, completed the baseline measurement and were included in the intervention study. Engagement and participant satisfaction

The total time spent on the app (modules, toolbox and chat) was on average 178 min (SD = 184 min), 33 of the 167 participants (19.5%) spent more than 5 h on it. A total of 36 participants (15.2%) started only the first module. On average, participants would send 2.0 chat messages (SD = 3.0) to the e-coach, who regularly sent them feedback on their progress. 70 participants (41.9%) did not write a message of their own during the intervention, while 24 (14.4%) wrote 5 or more. Activity dropped from the first component (M = 106 min, SD = 96 min) to the second (M = 57 min, SD = 87 min). Participants spent significantly more time (M = 209 min, SD = 219 min) in total (modules, toolbox and chat) when presented with the SER component first and the SSIP component second than vice versa (M = 152 min, SD = 142 min), t(164) = 2.00, p = .047, d = 0.31. Time spent on the app correlated with lower pre-app scores of resilience (r = −0.22, p = .005) and SEME (r = −0.20, p = .012) and higher negative problem orientation, r = 0.24, p = .002. There was no significant difference between conditions and no correlation for the number of chat messages.

The average level of participant satisfaction with the app was between largely satisfied and highly satisfied (intermediate measurement: M = 3.06, SD = 0.48; post measurement: M = 3.11, SD = 0.49; 3 = “largely satisfied”, 4 = “highly satisfied”. There were no significant differences after the SER and the SSIP component. No serious adverse events were reported during the study.

Efficacy of the intervention

Intention-to-treat analyses revealed significant overall time effects across the IG and CAU-MI groups, indicating improvements from pre- to post-measurement in resilience (F(1, 142.28) = 4.28, p = .040, d = −0.37), self-esteem (F(1, 138.00) = 4.66, p = .033, d = −0.44), SEME (F(1, 141.91) = 8.08, p = .005, d = −0.58), positive problem orientation (F(1, 139.95) = 4.90, p = .028, d = −0.43), and rational problem solving, F(1, 146.74) = 3.95, p = .049, d = −0.34. Participants also showed an increase in aggregated positive momentary affect, F(1, 1139.65) = 11.36, p < .001, d = −0.65. A trend-level improvement was observed for well-being, F(1, 148.42) = 3.27, p = .073, d = −0.34.

Among the planned group × time contrasts (Table 3), significant interaction effects in favour of the intervention group were found for SEME, negative problem orientation, and fear of negative evaluation. SEME increased only in the intervention group (d = −0.98), whereas negative problem orientation increased in the CAU-MI group (d = −0.61) but slightly decreased in the intervention group (d = 0.26). Similarly, fear of negative evaluation decreased in the intervention group (d = 0.66) but remained largely unchanged in the CAU-MI group, d = −0.08. No significant effects were found for the primary outcomes. Nevertheless, pre–post effect sizes consistently indicated numerically greater improvements in the IG across primary and secondary outcomes. These improvements were largely maintained at follow-up, with an additional small reduction in fear of negative evaluation, t(74) = 2.40, p = .019, d = 0.28. Fig. S1 in the appendix presents patterns over time for both primary outcomes and significant secondary outcomes.

Aggregated RCI results across the three secondary outcomes with significant interaction effects (SEME, negative problem orientation and fear of negative evaluation) favoured the IG. Reliable improvement on at least one outcome was observed in 20.9% of participants in the IG compared with 14.0% in the CAU-MI group. Reliable deterioration was less frequent in the IG (7.3% vs. 17.5%). No reliable change showed 70.9% in the IG and 68.4% in CAU-MI. This difference was marginally significant, t(164) = −1.98, p = .050, d = −0.32.

Per-protocol analyses including participants with a minimum of two hours of app usage (N = 118) were conducted as sensitivity analyses and largely confirmed the ITT findings (Table S1). Additional time effects emerged for well-being (F(1, 112.28) = 4.02, p = .047), fear of negative evaluation (F(1, 112.26) = 4.11, p = .045), social-behavioural avoidance (F(1, 112.10) = 4.59, p = .034) and EMA momentary resilience, F(1, 110.36) = 4.77, p = .031.

Furthermore, a group × time interaction was observed for EMA social distress, indicating a reduction in the IG but not in the CAU-MI group, F(1, 110.79) = 4.55, p = .035; d = 0.66 vs. -0.17. Improvements were generally maintained at follow-up, with an additional small reduction in avoidant problem solving, t(57) = 2.40, p = .020, d = 0.31. Given the reduced sample size and the exploratory nature of the PP analyses, these findings should be interpreted cautiously.

Differential component and sequence effects

Intention-to-treat analyses of the pooled IG and CAU-MI data revealed significant component × time interaction effects favouring the SER component for well-being, SEME, and impulsivity/carelessness problem-solving style (Table 4). Beyond the significant interaction effects, descriptive pre–post effect sizes (d ≥ 0.20) suggested consistent improvements after the SER component for resilience, self-esteem, avoidant problem solving, social-cognitive avoidance, fear of negative evaluation, and momentary social distress. In contrast, the SSIP component was associated with relative deteriorations in several problem-solving scales (rational problem solving, negative problem orientation, avoidant problem solving), and momentary negative affect, although fear of negative evaluation also improved (dSSIP = 0.25), see Table 4.

Aggregated RCI across the three outcomes with significant interaction effects (wellbeing, SEME and impulsivity/carelessness style) further favoured SER. Following SER, 23.8% of participants showed reliable improvement in at least one outcome compared to 4.7% following SSIP. Furthermore, reliable deterioration was less frequent after SER than after SSIP (8.8% vs. 16.3%). No reliable change showed 67.5% in SER and 75.6% in SSIP. The difference between the components was significant, t(161) = 3.42, p < .001, d = 0.54.

Per-protocol analyses including participants with a minimum of one hour of engagement in the first component (n = 103) confirmed only the interaction effect for SEME, F(1, 96.90) = 3.95, p = .050. The overall pattern of pre–post effect sizes in favour of SER (all d ≥ 0.20) was replicated, with additional improvements observed in positive and rational problem solving, avoidant problem solving, social avoidance, and EMA indicators of positive affect, overall burden, and momentary resilience. The SSIP component continued to show relative deteriorations in well-being, negative problem orientation, impulsivity/carelessness problem-solving style, and avoidant problem solving (all d ≥ ±0.20). The deterioration in rational problem solving or momentary affect was no longer evident. Fear of negative evaluation and social avoidance improved following both components, resulting in small between-component differences. Detailed PP results are presented in Table S2 in the appendix.

No statistically significant sequence × time interaction effects (SER–SSIP vs. SSIP–SER) were found in the ITT analyses, except for avoidant problem solving, (F(1, 276.24) = 4.72, p = .031). Avoidant problem solving decreased in the SER–SSIP sequence (dSER–SSIP = 0.50) but slightly increased in the SSIP–SER sequence (dSSIP–SER = −0.21). As this effect did not replicate in the PP analyses (n = 54) it should be interpreted cautiously. Full regression results for the component and sequence analyses are presented in Tables S5 to S8 in the appendix.

Discussion

This randomised controlled trial evaluated a guided digital self-help intervention for selective prevention among emerging adults with a history of adverse childhood experiences. Furthermore, it examined the differential component and sequencing effects of two theoretically grounded components targeting 1) self- and emotion regulation SER and 2) social skills and social information processing SSIP. The FACE app did not improve the primary outcomes resilience and well-being compared with the minimal intervention care-as-usual condition. However, the app improved more proximal mechanism-related outcomes, including self-efficacy in managing emotions (SEME), fear of negative evaluation and negative problem orientation. Improvements were observed in both conventional retrospective self-report instruments and in real-time ecological momentary assessments, which provide higher ecological validity and reduced recall bias. The findings did not support the expected pattern of component-specific effects as most differential effects after the first component were in favour of SER.

Rather than producing immediate improvements in broad indicators of positive mental health, the intervention primarily influenced psychological mechanisms implicated across multiple forms of psychopathology. This pattern suggests that, in young adults with ACEs, changes in transdiagnostic mechanisms may represent earlier and more sensitive indicators of intervention effects than changes in more distal outcomes such as resilience or well-being.

Efficacy for primary and secondary outcomes

Contrary to our hypotheses, the FACE app did not improve resilience, well-being or self-esteem as global indicators of positive mental health. This is consistent with previous digital preventive trials in young adults showing that positive mental health outcomes such as resilience and well-being are more difficult to improve than symptoms of psychopathology or more proximal mechanism-based outcomes (MacIsaac et al., 2021; Watkins et al., 2024a, Watkins et al., 2024b). Furthermore, resilience and well-being were only slightly below normative levels at baseline (Peitz et al., 2024; Watkins et al., 2024b), leaving limited scope for improvement in these distal outcomes.

Interestingly, a small overall time effect was observed across IG and CAU-MI. This may partly reflect the effect of concurrent professional support, which about one third of participants were receiving. Moreover, rather than a passive waitlist, the CAU-MI control group completed repeated assessments, received a screening phone call, and engaged in EMA self-monitoring with feedback. These procedures may have produced non-specific intervention effects and increased response expectancy (Kirsch, 1997), thereby attenuating between-group differences. Thus, the trial evaluated the incremental benefit of the FACE app beyond a care-as usual enhanced with minimal intervention control rather than a passive waitlist.

In line with the theoretical framework underlying the FACE app, significant intervention-specific improvements were observed for SEME, fear of negative evaluation, and negative problem orientation. These variables were directly targeted by the intervention content and reflect mechanisms linking ACEs to later psychosocial functioning (Alvarez et al., 2022; Dehghan Manshadi et al., 2024; Gerin et al., 2024; Sheffler et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2025). From a preventive perspective, improvements in these proximal mechanisms may therefore be clinically meaningful.

Differential component effects of SER and SSIP

The cross-over design allowed for a direct comparison of the two intervention components at the intermediate assessment. The SER component was associated with more favourable changes than SSIP across several outcomes, including well-being, SEME, and impulsivity/carelessness in problem solving. Effect size patterns further indicated broader improvements following SER across emotional, cognitive, and momentary outcomes. Furthermore, participants spent significantly more time engaging with the app when the SER component was presented first. In contrast, the SSIP component was associated with more heterogeneous effects, including mostly small negative changes in problem-solving, well-being, and momentary negative affect. Moreover, 16% of participants compared to 9% in the SER component showed reliable deterioration in at least one of the outcomes SEME, negative problem orientation or fear of negative evaluation. This suggests that digital interventions focused on social information processing and social skills may have adverse effects on some participants and should be evaluated not only for efficacy but also for potential negative effects in vulnerable populations.

Despite these unfavourable intermediate outcomes, no sequence effects were observed for the SER–SSIP and SSIP–SER conditions at post-intervention. This may indicate that these deteriorations were transient. One other possible explanation is that the social-cognitive exercises may have initially increased awareness of maladaptive interpersonal patterns before participants developed more adaptive strategies. Together, these findings suggest that the SER component represents a more suitable entry point for the intervention. Beginning the intervention with SER may facilitate engagement and provide a stronger foundation for subsequent interpersonal work.

Further investigation is required to identify whether the SSIP component as a whole or specific modules, such as assessing current social relationships, identifying and restructuring social expectations, or practising social skills for challenging interpersonal situations may account for the observed detrimental pattern among emerging adults with a history of ACEs. More generally, future research should systematically examine whether interpersonal-focused digital interventions require prior emotional stabilization or more intensive guidance.

Uptake, engagement, and participant satisfaction

As a selective prevention trial, an active recruitment strategy was used in which all participants of a general population epidemiological study who reported ACEs were invited to participate in the intervention. Uptake was modest (13% interest; 11% enrolment), and average app use was limited (≈3 of the recommended 10 h), reflecting common engagement challenges in e-mental health (Torous et al., 2025).

Despite largely normative levels of resilience and well-being at baseline, participants reported levels of psychological distress and fear of negative evaluation comparable to outpatient samples (Kemper et al., 2012; Michel et al., 2024). Approximately 60% of participants reported previous professional support for psychosocial problems and about 30% were receiving ongoing support at study entry. Thus, this recruitment approach also reached a substantial subgroup of 40% without previous professional help despite showing elevated psychological vulnerability. This highlights the potential of selectively offered digital interventions to engage individuals beyond traditional care pathways who may otherwise not seek treatment.

Furthermore, participants reported that they were largely satisfied with the app and with the amount of support they received. They indicated that the app helped them cope with their problems. Despite the less favourable outcomes of the SSIP component, there was no difference after SER and SSIP regarding satisfaction with the app. The perceived benefits may not have been fully captured by the predefined outcome measures. For example, participants may have experienced the psychoeducational content on ACEs, their consequences, as well as associated risk and protective factors, as helpful in understanding and contextualising their own history. Such gains in insight or meaning-making may not have been directly reflected in changes in resilience or well-being scores.

Public health and clinical implications

The present findings have implications for the development and implementation of digital interventions in selective and indicated prevention for emerging adults with ACEs. The low uptake under active recruitment highlights the challenges of reaching unselected high-risk populations. It suggests that scalability may depend more on strategic integration into existing care pathways, educational settings, or stepped-care models rather than on universal outreach. As a selective self-help intervention, the FACE app showed effects on proximal psychological mechanisms and may be more relevant for individuals with elevated emotion regulation or social-cognitive vulnerabilities.

These findings support a shift toward more targeted or indicated approaches. Digital self-help could be offered to individuals with higher symptom burden or lower regulatory capacities and integrated into blended or referral-based care to enhance engagement and efficiency. Clinically, guided prevention apps may serve as early or adjunctive interventions targeting emotion regulation self-efficacy and maladaptive social-cognitive processes. The stronger effects of the SER component further support a phased approach, prioritizing intrapersonal stabilization before addressing more complex interpersonal challenges.

More broadly, the present findings illustrate the challenges of translating digital preventive interventions into meaningful public health impact. The recently proposed TEQUILA framework emphasises that successful implementation depends not only on clinical efficacy but also on evidence quality, usability, engagement, trust, and appropriate accreditation (Löchner et al., 2025). The low uptake and modest distal effects observed in this trial underscore the importance of addressing these implementation dimensions alongside efficacy. Future research should therefore focus not only on optimising intervention content but also on identifying strategies to enhance user engagement, sustain adherence, and strengthen trust in evidence-based digital prevention programmes.

Limitations

Several limitations should be noted. First uptake was lower than expected. Although the sample size allowed the detection of moderate intervention effects between the IG and CAU-MI, power was limited for detecting small between-group differences between SER and SSIP. In addition, per-protocol analyses were based on reduced samples. Thus, findings should be interpreted with caution and replicated with larger and more diverse samples. Second, the exclusive reliance on self-report measures may have introduced shared method variance. Third, participants in the intervention group reported slightly higher rates of previous professional help-seeking at baseline. This may have reduced the potential for additional intervention-related improvements and thereby attenuated between-group differences. Fourth, we did not examine whether intervention effects varied according to baseline distress or level of engagement, apart from the per-protocol analyses. Given the relatively low baseline distress in the present sample, future studies should investigate whether selective digital prevention is particularly effective for individuals with greater initial need and whether higher engagement is associated with stronger treatment effects. Finally, the follow-up period may have been too short to detect delayed effects on distal outcomes such as resilience and well-being. Future research should examine longer-term trajectories, moderators of response, and optimized intervention components and sequencing.

Conclusions

The FACE app did not improve the preregistered primary distal outcomes resilience and well-being beyond the CAU-MI condition. Nevertheless, the intervention showed small but relatively consistent effects on several theoretically relevant proximal mechanisms associated with ACE-related vulnerability. Taken together, the findings support a transdiagnostic approach to digital selective prevention that modifies theoretically central mechanisms that link ACEs with mental health outcomes in emerging adults. The differential efficacy of the self-and emotion regulation component suggests that emotion regulation may be a critical foundation for preventive interventions in this population, informing future optimization and stepped-care approaches. Future iterations of the FACE app may benefit from enhanced engagement strategies, adaptive personalization, or integration into blended care models.
