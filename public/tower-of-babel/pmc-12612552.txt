Impact of an MRI Body Fat Imaging Report on Obesity‐Related Cardiometabolic Risk: Findings From the BODY‐REAL Study, A Randomized Trial
Authors: Shradha M Chhabria, Jaime A Pérez, Ian J Neeland
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveAdvanced imaging can inform risk assessment and preventive/therapeutic decision‐making to enhance care. It is not known how quantification of body fat distribution using advanced imaging and communication of the result through a graphic report may impact cardiometabolic risk factors and attitudes/behaviors among persons living with overweight or obesity. The BODY‐REAL study assessed whether providing patients with (1) visual body fat MRI reports compared with the weight/body mass index (BMI) alone and (2) direct‐to‐patient reports versus through providers would improve cardiometabolic risk among individuals at elevated cardiometabolic risk.MethodsParticipants were randomized in a 2 × 2 factorial design to receive a BMI or MRI body fat distribution report, provided direct‐to‐patient report or a report through their healthcare provider. Clinical and psychosocial outcomes were measured at 2 weeks, 3, and 6 months and compared using analysis of covariance and linear models with both fixed and random (time‐updated) effects.Results29 participants (31% female, mean BMI 33.6 kg/m2) were randomized. After 6 months of follow‐up, MRI report recipients had lower fasting glucose and higher physical activity scores (p < 0.01). Provider‐mediated reporting demonstrated greater medication adherence (p = 0.01) and greater perception of personal control of diabetes (p = 0.02) but lower perception of diabetes‐related risk (p = 0.003). There were no significant differences in BMI, blood pressure, cholesterol, glycosylated hemoglobin, or other risk perception across study arms.ConclusionsThis pilot feasibility study demonstrated potential for direct‐to‐patient visualization of body fat distribution to improve risk markers and preventive behaviors. Further investigation in a sufficiently powered study is warranted.Trial Registration ClinicalTrials.gov NCT04763772Keywords: body fat , MRI, visceral adiposity

Introduction

The marked increase in the prevalence of overweight and obesity has contributed to a doubling in type 2 diabetes incidence over the past 3 decades [1, 2]. This has counterbalanced reductions in other CVD risk factors and is the primary factor contributing to a slowed decline in CVD event rates in the population [3]. In turn, professional societies and stakeholders have renewed efforts targeting obesity in order to reduce the diabetes burden [4, 5]. Clinical trials aimed at lifestyle, pharmacologic, and surgical approaches to diabetes consistently focus on reducing weight or body mass index (BMI). However, emerging data suggest that obesity, simply characterized by increased BMI, is insufficient to promote diabetes and its consequences. BMI does not adequately discriminate diabetes risk among individuals living with obesity and many persons with obesity appear resistant to the development of metabolic disease [6, 7]. Rather, it appears that risk for diabetes varies substantially across different fat depots, and that an excess of visceral adipose tissue (VAT) may be central to the pathogenesis of insulin resistance and diabetes [8, 9, 10, 11, 12]. A recent expert commission proposed using additional anthropometric measurements and/or body fat imaging to diagnose and stratify obesity risk.[3] Anthropometric markers of abdominal adiposity incompletely capture variance in VAT, with body fat imaging emerging as the most precise tool to capture adipose distribution [13].

Body fat distribution is fundamental to obesity‐related diseases. Excess and/or dysfunctional adiposity is the primary driver of cardiovascular‐kidney‐metabolic syndrome pathogenesis, principally reflected by excess visceral and ectopic fat stores [14, 15]. Visceral adipose tissue (VAT), which is frequently accompanied by ectopic fat deposition in normally lean tissues (e.g., liver, heart, skeletal muscle, pancreas, kidney), is associated with the development of insulin resistance, systemic inflammation, and oxidative stress that contribute to the development of metabolic risk factors, CVD, and CKD [16]. Excess visceral and ectopic fat can even occur in those with normal BMI, in part due to genetic and geographical influences [17]. The magnitude of visceral and ectopic fat stores in persons living with obesity can be highly variable and can make up as much as 60% of total body weight [18]. For most of these individuals, greater than 80% of their fat is stored in the subcutaneous adipose tissue (SAT) and the remaining is found in VAT. Non‐modifiable factors such as age, sex, and race/ethnicity impact the distribution of VAT versus SAT [19]. Dysfunctional adiposity also promotes the accumulation of ectopic adipose tissue, a major determinant of systemic and hepatic insulin resistance and the related metabolic alterations which affect both lipid and glucose metabolism. Important examples of ectopic fat include excess hepatic triglycerides, leading to metabolic‐dysfunction associated steatotic liver disease and excess epicardial and/or pericardial fat.

Patients living with obesity are often poorly informed about their risk for future diabetes and cardiovascular disease and most physicians are inadequately equipped to address obesity with their patients [20, 21, 22, 23]. Provision of accurate risk information to patients is likely to be a critical determinant of behavioral change and is well documented to provide much needed motivation for lifestyle changes with downstream improvement in risk factors and clinical outcomes. This concept is most well‐studied for imaging‐based risk stratification tools, such as coronary artery calcium scoring, as patients are more likely to initiate change when faced with direct visual evidence of risk [24, 25]. Direct visualization of excess adiposity and body fat distribution through imaging may positively impact patients' motivation for change; yet, how provision of direct imaging evidence of body fat burden and distribution may impact obesity management and patient‐centered outcomes is unknown and is a major knowledge gap in our approach to obesity care.

Therefore, the aim of the Clinical Effectiveness of Body Fat Distribution Imaging in Real‐World Practice: The BODY‐REAL Study was to determine the real‐world feasibility and effectiveness of body fat magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) versus usual weight/BMI information on patient‐centered outcomes including enhancing risk perception, inducing behavioral change, and improving clinical outcomes among patients living with overweight or obesity.

Methods

Study Population

The BODY‐REAL study was a single center, pilot, randomized, clinical effectiveness trial. Participants were recruited through various cardiometabolic clinics at University Hospitals Medical Center in Cleveland, Ohio. Adults aged 35 years or older were eligible for inclusion if they were living with overweight or obesity (BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2), had a clinical diagnosis of prediabetes or type 2 diabetes, and had at least one additional cardiovascular risk factor defined by ATP III criteria [26], including hypertension, low HDL cholesterol, hypertriglyceridemia, or clinically diagnosed obstructive sleep apnea, coronary artery disease, heart failure, or atrial fibrillation at the time of study enrollment. Participants were excluded if they were unable to complete MRI; were using or planning to use weight‐promoting medications, weight loss pharmacotherapy, or supplements; planned to undergo major surgery,; or to become pregnant or breastfeed during the planned trial period. The planned sample size was 126 participants to detect a 5% control‐corrected relative reduction in body weight. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants at the time of enrollment and the study was approved by the University Hospitals Cleveland Institutional Review Board. The trial was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04763772).

Trial Design and Procedures

The trial utilized a two‐by‐two factorial design, whereby participants were randomized to both report type (anthropometry report or body fat distribution report) and report recipient (direct‐to‐patient or provider‐received), generating four independent trial arms (Figure 1). Randomization was performed using a computer‐generated randomization code, which enrollment staff could not access. The study participants were not blinded due to practical considerations given the use of MRI but all data adjudication was blinded to the intervention assignment.

All participants underwent standard anthropometric measurements, including height and weight on a calibrated scale (model HBF 514C, Omron Healthcare, Lake Forest, IL) and waist and hip circumference, measured using a standardized protocol. Those randomized to a body fat distribution report underwent MRI (1.5 T S Aera MRI scanner, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) using validated protocols with rapid scan time (6‐min dual‐echo Dixon Vibe protocol, multiecho Dixon acquisition) to obtain precise measurements of total and regional body composition, fat distribution, hepatic steatosis and lean muscle quality [27].

All participants across the groups received usual clinical care concomitantly during the trial period.

Participants randomized to an anthropometry report received a simple informational visual report consisting of weight and BMI classification per World Health Organization categorization. Those randomized to a body fat MRI report received a detailed read‐out of body fat and muscle quantitation and distribution including visceral fat, abdominal subcutaneous fat, and thigh muscle volumes (L), thigh muscle fat infiltration (%), and liver fat (%), presented on a visual scale alongside reference data from a normative, metabolic disease‐free population (UK Biobank).

Outcomes

Outcomes included cardiometabolic risk markers and patient risk perception and behaviors. Clinical outcomes were measured at baseline, 3 and 6 months, and included anthropometrics, blood pressure, lipid profile, glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1C) and fasting glucose levels. Participants also completed validated survey instruments at baseline, 2 weeks, 3 and 6 months, including Perception of Risk for Diabetes (RPS‐DD) [28], Perception of Heart Disease (PRHDS) [29], and Global Physical Activity Questionnaire (GPAQ) [30, 31].

Statistical Analysis

The trial was originally powered to detect a 5% control‐corrected relative reduction in body weight, based on clinical significance and the benchmark used for registration trials of weight loss medications. Assuming a mean 2% relative reduction in weight among control (standard weight/BMI) participants and a 7% relative reduction in weight among those provided a detailed report (with a standard deviation of 10%, based on prior data), the expected sample size required was 126 total participants (in a 1:1 randomization scheme) to achieve 80% power to detect a 5% difference between groups at an alpha level of 0.05. Assuming that an estimated 10% of participants might not attend clinical visits within the planned 6 months’ timeframe, the planned enrollment target was a total of 140 participants in order to achieve at least 126 participants with measurable outcomes.

Participant baseline characteristics were compared between trial arms using chi‐square tests for dichotomous variables (frequencies and percentages) and analysis of variance for continuous variables (means and standard deviation). To compare outcomes between the report type and the report recipient, separate linear mixed effects’ models for each outcome were performed, including the two‐way interaction effects of time (baseline, 3‐month follow‐up, and 6‐month follow‐up), report type (BMI and MRI), and time and report recipient (direct‐to‐patient and provider), with the random effect of participant to account for repeated measures within an individual. There was no imputation for missing values. A p‐value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using R version 4.2.1 software (R Core Team, 2022) [32].

Results

The trial was stopped early due to slow recruitment. 29 participants were randomized to four trial arms, with 6–8 participants in each arm. 31% of participants were females, with a mean (standard deviation) age at baseline of 63.0 (10.3) years, BMI of 33.6 (6.0) kg/m2, systolic blood pressure of 133.0 (18.6) mmHg, high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C) of 46.1 (18.4) mg/dL, low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol of 74.9 (29.9) mg/dL and triglycerides of 152.6 (130.9) mg/dL. Mean (SD) HbA1C was 6.3 (0.6)% and fasting glucose was 111.8 (26.6) mg/dL. There were no statistically significant differences in baseline characteristics across randomization arms (Table 1).

No adverse events were seen during the study.

Among participants who received their reports directly, those who received MRI body fat reports had an increase in HDL‐C over time (p = 0.047) compared to BMI report recipients. Those who received reports through their provider showed an increase in preventive behavior scores over time, regardless of report type (p = 0.002). Lastly, regardless of report recipient, those who received BMI reports had lower lifestyle change scores over time (p = 0.026), indicating less reported initiative for healthy lifestyle changes compared to those who received MRI reports.

Discussion

The results of the BODY‐REAL pilot study demonstrate promising potential for use of body fat MRI visual reports to improve fasting glucose and physical activity in adults living with overweight or obesity. Provider‐mediated reporting improved perception of personal control over health but with lower perception of diabetes‐related health risk. Provider‐mediated reporting also improved preventive behaviors over time, and HDL‐C improved over time among those who received direct‐to‐patient MRI reports. The benefits of both provider‐ and patient‐received reporting are notable in a health technology era increasingly moving toward direct‐to‐patient result reporting.

Despite its availability for the last 30 years, advanced body fat imaging has been, to date, relegated to the research domain only [19, 33]. Many experts in the field think that if readily available routine risk indicators, such as blood pressure, blood lipids, blood glucose, waist circumference, and body weight, could be taken from the existing patient record and used, why would quantification and distribution of body fat through graphic imaging be a superior motivational tool for patient behavior modification? The lack of a clear answer to this question was the rationale for conducting this study to evaluate the real‐world utility and feasibility of an advanced body fat imaging approach, especially in light of the rapidly improving technology, increased accessibility, and lower cost of advanced imaging.

The evolution of coronary artery calcium (CAC) scoring is a paradigm example of how advanced imaging can inform risk assessment and preventive/therapeutic decision‐making. Much in the same way that CAC provides important risk information beyond traditional risk factors, it is possible that advanced body fat imaging may add to and improve current obesity‐related risk algorithms, something that BMI and waist circumference have failed to do [34].

Despite the general understanding in the lay public that obesity has long‐term adverse health effects, in more recent years, due to the increase in the average weight/BMI of the population, many patients describe complacency regarding their weight and are often unprepared to utilize provider advice [35]. Behavioral science has long understood the importance of visual cues to modify behavior and motivation. It is well understood that habit learning is facilitated when the new behavior is consistently preceded by specific (e.g., visual) cues [36]. The Multi‐Theory Model of health behavior change is comprised of structures that drive both initiation and sustenance of change, including participatory dialog, behavioral confidence, changes in the physical and social environment, emotional transformation and practice for change [37]. Prior studies have demonstrated that the use of medical imaging for visualization of health risks does have potential to improve preventive health behaviors [38]. It is possible that visualized health risks may increase opportunity for participatory dialog to initiate change and may spur emotional transformation for sustained efforts. This is especially important for behavioral motivation to lose weight, as other health issues are often felt to take precedence, and patients describe inconsistent provision of information and resources to assist them in tackling their weight problems [35]. Therefore, there is strong rationale to implement highly effective visual cues that influence the patient's understanding of the hazard of excess body fat and offer a personalized, visually interpretable representation of that hazard.

Visual representation of adverse body fat distribution is an attractive target to help patients living with overweight or obesity modify behavior and motivation to lose weight and improve their cardiometabolic health. Behavioral motivation programs for weight loss are known to be impactful and relatively inexpensive. For example, in 2020, Spring et al. evaluated cost‐efficiency of remote behavioral weight loss interventions and found that an optimized, 6‐month–long program could be delivered for $427 per participant [39]. However, utilization of behavioral interventions for obesity, including those with high‐risk conditions such as diabetes, remains low [40]. Overall, there is evidence that behavioral motivation programs for treatment of obesity can have a modest but significant positive impact (e.g., significant enough to improve cardiovascular risks) upon body weight and weight‐related behaviors [41, 42]. This is especially true for visceral and ectopic fat depots where exercise/physical activity is highly impactful for reducing VAT and related risk [43].

Physicians lack the time, training, and priority to address obesity with patients and most physicians are inadequately equipped to address obesity with their patients [22, 23]. In a survey of 81 internal medicine residencies, ∼50% of programs reported that obesity management education was covered to a “very little extent” or “not at all” [7]. Furthermore, primary care physicians surveyed about their top priorities in delivery of preventive care did not often include diet, exercise, and weight loss although these were among the 3 preventive services most likely to improve life expectancy [23]. As a consequence, access to obesity treatment remains a significant challenge. Providing visual imaging evidence of body fat burden/distribution directly to the patient, rather than relying on the provider, is an approach that is patient‐centered, highly informative, and transparent and provides a perspective on risk that the patient can simply understand, which could ultimately empower individuals to make lifestyle changes to improve health outcomes.

Overall, these findings are of significant value as body fat imaging emerges as an important tool for risk stratifying patients with excess visceral adipose tissue by cardiometabolic risk potential and provides a more nuanced insight into adipose distribution. While MRI is more costly than other forms of body fat imaging, the rapid sequence protocols followed in this trial help reduce these costs, and may be cost‐effective in comparison to the substantial health system and individual costs of chronic cardiometabolic disease. Unfortunately, the study was stopped early due to slow recruitment and is therefore underpowered to detect meaningful differences in other outcomes between groups.

Conclusions

The BODY‐REAL study demonstrated the potential of body fat MRI with both direct‐to‐patient and provider‐received reports to improve biometric and psychosocial risk factors for cardiometabolic disease among patients with overweight or obesity. A larger, sufficiently powered, study is warranted to help further investigate pragmatic interventions to help patients living with obesity and cardiometabolic disease better understand the severity and risks associated with their disease and to further propel motivation into action toward improved health equity and outcomes.
