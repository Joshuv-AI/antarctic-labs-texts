Isorhamnetin inhibits bladder cancer progression by mediating glycolysis through targeting the SRC/PI3K/Akt axis
Authors: Feng Haiya, Li Qianqian, Lu Yangjun, Wang Hui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate the anti-tumor effects of the natural flavonoid Isorhamnetin (ISO) on BC and elucidate its underlying molecular mechanisms.MethodsWe integrated network pharmacology and bioinformatic analysis to predict the core targets and pathways between BC and ISO. In vitro functional assays were performed using the human BC cell line 5637. Cell viability, proliferation, apoptosis, migration, invasion, and adhesion were assessed via CCK-8, colony formation, flow cytometry, Transwell, and adhesion assays. Glycolytic function was evaluated by measuring extracellular acidification rate (ECAR), oxygen consumption rate (OCR), glucose uptake, and lactate production. Molecular docking and cellular thermal shift assays (CETSA) were used to validate ISO-SRC binding. Western blot and RT-qPCR analyses were conducted to examine protein and mRNA expression of key signaling and glycolytic components. For the rescue experiments, SRC was overexpressed along with treatment using the PI3K inhibitor LY294002.ResultsISO significantly inhibited BC cell proliferation, migration, invasion, adhesion, and glycolysis while inducing G0/G1 phase arrest and apoptosis. Network pharmacology and molecular docking identified SRC as a direct target of ISO, with CETSA confirming their binding. ISO treatment downregulated phosphorylation of SRC and its downstream effectors (PI3K, Akt, mTOR), reduced the expression of glycolytic enzymes (HK2, GLUT1, PFKFB3), and decreased ECAR, OCR, glucose uptake, and lactate production. SRC overexpression reversed the anti-tumor and anti-glycolytic effects of ISO. Furthermore, administration of LY294002 on this basis counteracted the effects induced by SRC overexpression, confirming the critical role of the SRC/PI3K/Akt axis.ConclusionISO inhibits the malignant progression of BC by directly targeting SRC to suppress glycolysis mediated by the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway. These findings provide a mechanistic basis for considering ISO as a potential metabolic-targeting agent for BC, warranting further preclinical evaluation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00432-026-06577-8.Keywords: Bladder cancer, Isorhamnetin, SRC, PI3K/Akt pathway, Glycolysis

Introduction

Bladder cancer (BC) ranks as the tenth most common malignant tumor globally and is a frequently occurring malignancy of the urogenital system, with persistently high incidence and mortality rates (Tan et al. 2024). Epidemiological studies indicate that nearly 600,000 new cases of BC were diagnosed worldwide in 2020. This number is projected to double by 2040 (Dyrskjøt et al. 2023). At present, the core methods for the clinical treatment of bladder cancer include transurethral resection of bladder tumors or en bloc resection, which are often supplemented with immediate postoperative intravesical instillation chemotherapy (e.g., mitomycin C). For high-risk patients, intravesical bacillus Calmette-Guérin immunotherapy is the standard option. For advanced-stage patients, treatment regimens involve radical cystectomy, bladder-sparing trimodal therapy combining chemoradiotherapy, and cisplatin-based systemic chemotherapy(Dyrskjøt et al. 2023). In recent years, novel systemic therapies such as immune checkpoint inhibitors (e.g., pembrolizumab) and antibody–drug conjugates have provided important options for advanced-stage patients. Regrettably, up to 50%-90% of patients still experience recurrence within five years, posing a significant clinical challenge (Dobruch and Oszczudłowski 2021; Peng et al. 2020). Therefore, in-depth exploration of the molecular mechanisms underlying the initiation and progression of BC, and consequently the development of novel and efficient therapeutic strategies, remains a critical scientific problem urgently needing resolution in current clinical BC research.

Glycolytic reprogramming, as one of the most prominent hallmarks of cancer, plays an indispensable role in cancer progression. A key manifestation of this reprogramming is the increased conversion of glucose to lactate, even under oxygen-sufficient conditions, a phenomenon termed "aerobic glycolysis" or the "Warburg effect" (Faubert et al. 2020). This enhanced glycolytic pathway provides cancer cells with various essential biomolecules to sustain nucleotide and triglyceride biosynthesis, thereby supporting their rapid growth and proliferation (Lin et al. 2025). Furthermore, lactate produced by tumor glycolysis has been shown to suppress the function of various immune cells and is associated with resistance to tumor immunotherapy (Yue et al. 2023). Consequently, targeted inhibition of tumor glycolysis holds significant importance and potential for cancer therapy.

Isorhamnetin (ISO) is a naturally occurring flavonoid compound extracted from the leaves, flowers, and fruits of Ginkgo biloba and other plants. Numerous clinical studies have demonstrated that ISO possesses various pharmacological activities, including anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, anti-fibrotic, and anti-cancer effects (Ganbold et al. 2025). Regarding its anti-cancer properties, ISO has been shown to inhibit various human cancers such as colorectal cancer, skin cancer, lung cancer, and breast cancer. Its mechanisms of action involve inducing cell cycle arrest (G2/M and S phases), modulating signaling pathways like PI3K/Akt/mTOR and MEK1/ERKs, and upregulating apoptosis-inducing genes (Casp3, Casp9, and Apaf1) and the mitochondrial-dependent apoptotic pathway (Biswas et al. 2024). In the field of BC, research has revealed the anti-tumor potential of ISO. One study found that ISO can inhibit the tumorigenicity of BC by activating the PPARγ/PTEN/AKT pathway and reducing CA9 expression in BC (Zhang et al. 2023). Another study demonstrated that ISO suppresses BC cell proliferation by inducing ROS-dependent G2/M phase cell cycle arrest and cell death. These findings strongly support the potential of ISO as a candidate drug for BC treatment. However, whether ISO can target and regulate metabolic reprogramming in BC remains unknown.

SRC is a non-receptor tyrosine kinase that plays a pivotal role in integrating and regulating receptor signaling, thereby modulating processes such as cell metabolism, proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, adhesion, migration, invasion, and metastasis (Pelaz and Tabernero 2022). In various solid tumors, SRC is frequently found to be overexpressed or hyperactivated and promotes malignant progression by phosphorylating downstream substrates. Particularly important is the growing body of evidence suggesting a significant role for SRC also in tumor metabolism. It has been reported that SRC can reprogram metabolism to promote malignancy in breast and colorectal cancers (Gong et al. 2025). In hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), SRC can enhance the glycolytic pathway by activating the downstream PI3K/Akt signaling pathway, ultimately promoting HCC growth and metastasis (Luo et al. 2021). The PI3K/Akt signaling pathway is one of the most critical intracellular pro-survival and metabolic regulatory pathways. Its activation can directly enhance glucose uptake and also regulate several glycolytic enzymes through post-translational and transcriptional mechanisms, promoting the Warburg effect, cell proliferation, and tumorigenesis (Zhang et al. 2024). Although some studies suggest that SRC may act as an oncogene in BC, its specific mechanism of action remains unclear at present.

Based on the aforementioned research background, this study integrated network pharmacological analysis, bioinformatic mining, and a series of in vitro experiments to demonstrate that ISO can effectively inhibit the malignant progression of BC by targeting the SRC/PI3K/Akt axis-mediated glycolysis. This may offer a promising therapeutic agent for BC patients.

Materials and Methods

Reagents and chemicals

ISO (C16H12O7, purity ≥ 99.74%), DMSO (purity ≥ 99.9%), and LY294002 (purity ≥ 99.86%) were purchased from MedChemExpress (USA).

Cell culture

5637, T24 (Human BC cells) and SV-HUC-1 (Human normal bladder epithelial cell) were purchased from Wuhan Procell Life Technology Co., Ltd. Cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Procell, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Procell, China) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (P/S, Procell, China). Cells were maintained and passaged in a 37 °C incubator with 5% CO₂.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability of 5637 was detected by CCK-8 assay. Cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 104 cells/well in 96-well plates and cultured overnight at 37 °C. After treatment with ISO (0, 10, 20, 50 and 100 μM) at 37 °C for different times (24, 48, 72 h), 10 μL of Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) reagent (Invigentech) was added to each well according to the manufacturer’s protocol. After 2 h of incubation, absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Colony formation assay

5637 cells (1000 cells/dish) were seeded into culture dishes, treated with 100 μM ISO, and cultured at 37 °C for 2 weeks. Subsequently, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Shanghai Aladdin) for 15 min at room temperature and stained with crystal violet (Shanghai Yeasen) for 30 min. Images were captured, and cell clusters with >50 cells were counted as colonies manually.

Flow cytometric analysis of cell apoptosis and cell cycle

The apoptosis rate was determined by Annexin V-FITC/propidium iodide (PI) double staining followed by flow cytometry. 5637 cells treatment with 100 μM ISO for 48 h, cells were harvested by centrifugation (300 × g, 5 min), washed twice with ice-cold PBS, and resuspended in 100 μL binding buffer (BD Biosciences). Subsequently, cells were incubated with 5 μL Annexin V-FITC and 5 μL PI (50 μg/mL) for 15 min in the dark at room temperature. Prior to analysis, 400 μL binding buffer was added to each sample. Operation was performed, data were analyzed using FlowJo software, where apoptotic cells were defined as follows: Annexin V⁺/PI⁻ (early apoptosis, Q4 quadrant) and Annexin V⁺/PI⁺ (late apoptosis/necrosis, Q2 quadrant).

Cell cycle staining kit (KGA511, Jiangsu KeyGEN Bio TECH) was used to analyze cell cycle distribution of 5637 cells affected by different administration. After digestion by trypsin, cells were collected and fixed in 70% cold ethanol overnight at 4 ℃. After washing 3 time with PBS, mixture solution (volume ratio of Rnase/PI is 1:9) was prepared and added to cells, and then incubated in the dark at room temperature for 45 min. Cell cycle distribution was detected under flow cytometer.

Transwell invasion and migration assay

To assess the invasion and migration ability of 5637 cells, Transwell assays were conducted using 24-well Transwell chambers with 8 μm polycarbonate membranes pre-coated or not pre-coated with Matrigel at 37 °C for 1 h. Cells (1 × 105) suspended in 100 μL DMEM without FBS, with or without 100 μM ISO, were added to the upper chamber, while the lower chamber contained 600 μL DMEM with 10% FBS. After incubation at 37 °C for 24 h, cells on the upper surface of the membrane were removed with a PBS-moistened cotton swab. The membrane was fixed with paraformaldehyde at 37 °C for 30 min, stained with 0.1% crystal violet at room temperature for 30 min, and cells on the lower surface were counted under an optical microscope.

Cell–matrix adhesion assay

The 5637 cells were plated at a density of 5000 cells per well in 96-well plates coated with fibronectin or collagen I (10 μg/ml). The cells were incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. After washing three times with PBS, the attached cells were fixed with 1% glutaraldehyde for 30 min and stained with 0.1% crystal violet in PBS for 30 min. After washing three times with PBS, the stained cells were homogenized with 10% acetic acid for 10 min. The absorbance of each well of the plates was measured at 595 nm.

Network pharmacological analysis

Predicted the relevant action targets of ISO through the SwissTargetPrediction (http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/) online website. Use search words from "bladder cancer" (https://www.genecards.org) and DrugBank database (https://go.drugbank.com) identified in human BC related targets, using UniProt distinguish between coding and non coding protein targets. The intersection target between ISO and BC was determined using Venn plots.The interaction gene search tool (STRING, https://cn.string-db.org) was used to analyze the intersecting targets in the Venn map to study the interactions between these protein-coding genes with a confidence level greater than 0.4. The initial protein–protein interaction (PPI) network was further analyzed using Cytoscape and CytoNCA plugins. Nodes with intermediate values, compactness and degree less than the corresponding intermediate values are filtered out.Through bioinformatics platform (http://www.bioinformatics.com.cn) gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto gene and genome encyclopedia (KEGG) pathway analysis, explore the enrichment of the GO term and KEGG pathways.

Bioinformatics analysis

The online analysis tool UALCAN (https://ualcan.path.uab.edu/analysis.html) based on the TCGA database was used to compare the expression level of SRC in normal samples (n = 19) and BLCA samples (n = 408), as well as its relationship with the clinicopathological stage of patients.

Molecular docking

The molecular structure of ISO (PubChem CID: 5,281,654) was retrieved from PubChem Compound, and the 3D structures of SRC (PDBe: 1a09) were downloaded from PDB (https://www.rcsb.org/). For docking analysis, protein and molecule files were converted to PDBQT format, with all water molecules excluded and polar hydrogen atoms added. The grid box was positioned at the center to cover the protein domains and accommodate the free movement of molecules. Molecular docking was performed using the computational protein–ligand docking software AutoDock 1.5.7, and visualization was conducted with PyMOL software.

Thermal shift assay

Cells treated with ISO were digested with trypsin and collected by centrifugation. The cells were resuspended in PBS and heated at 40, 45, 50, 55, or 60 °C for 4 min. Subsequently, cells were lysed by three cycles of freeze-thawing in liquid nitrogen. The samples were then centrifuged (20 min, 17,000 g), 5× loading buffer was added to the supernatant, and detection was performed via Western blot separation.

RNA extraction and qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from each group using the RNAisoEasy Kit (Takara, Japan). cDNA was synthesized via reverse transcription using the PrimeScript™ RT Master Mix (Perfect Real Time) Kit (Takara, Japan). PCR amplification was performed on a StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, USA) using specific primers and TBGreen® Premix Ex Taq™ reagent (Takara, Japan) to obtain CT values. β-actin served as the internal reference gene, and relative mRNA expression levels of HIF1A and VEGF were calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCT method. Bioenergetics assay

Cell energy metabolism phenotype and glycolytic capacity were measured by the Seahorse XFe 24 Analyzer (Agilent, USA). The night before the experiment, the cells (2 × 104) were planted in a 24-well plate. The sensor cartridge was calibrated with XF Calibrant and placed in a non-CO2 37 °C incubator overnight. On the day of the assay, the growth medium was replaced by assay media. In the energy phenotype experiment, the assay media were seahorse XF Base medium containing 1 mM pyruvate, 2 mM glutamine, and 10 mM glucose. Carbonyl cyanide 4-(trifluoromethoxy) phenylhydrazone (FCCP) and oligomycin (Sigma, USA) were dissolved in the assay medium and stressor mix was created; 55 μL of the stressor mix (the final concentration of both is 1 μM) was loaded into each port A of a hydrated sensor cartridge. The assay media of glycolytic capacity consisted of 2 mM glutamine in seahorse XF Base medium. Fifty-six microliters of glucose (10 mM final concentration), 62 μL of oligomycin (3 μM final concentration), and 69 μL of 2-Dexoy-D-Glucose (2-DG) (50 mM final concentration) were added to the appropriate injection ports. All ECAR and OCR values were normalized to total protein content measured by BCA assay.

Western blot

Cell samples from each group were collected and lysed with RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, China). After centrifugation at 12,000 g for 5 min, the supernatant was collected. Samples were prepared by heating in a metal bath for 5 min with 5× loading buffer. Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk at room temperature for 2 h, washed with TBST, and incubated with the following primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight: SRC (Cat: ab47405; 1:1000), p-SRC (Cat: ab40660; 1:1000), PI3K (Cat: ab140307; 1:1000), p-PI3K (Cat: ab140307; 1:1000), Akt (Cat: ab38449; 1:1000), p-Akt (Cat: ab38449; 1:1000), mTOR (Cat: ab134903; 1:1000), p-mTOR (Cat: ab109268; 1:1000), HK2 (Cat: ab209847; 1:1000), GLUT1 (Cat: ab115730; 1:1000), PFKFB3 (Cat: 181,861; 1:1000) and β-actin (Cat: ab8226; 1:500). After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated Goat anti-Rabbit IgG (H&L) (Cat: ab6721; 1:1000) and Goat anti-Mouse IgG (H&L) (Cat: ab96879; 1:1000) at room temperature for 2 h. All antibodies were from Abcam. After treatment with ECL detection reagent (Yamei, China), images were captured using an imaging system (Bio-Rad, USA), and data were analyzed with Image J 1.54 software.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were repeated three times. Data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation and were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8.3.0. One-way ANOVA was used to analyze differences, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

ISO inhibits the malignant phenotype of BC cells

To investigate the effects of ISO on the malignant phenotype of BC cells, we conducted a series of in vitro functional assays using the human BC cell line 5637 as a model. We first assessed the impact of DMSO and different concentrations of ISO (10, 20, 50, and 100 μM) on the viability of 5637 cells after 24, 48, and 72 h of treatment using the CCK-8 assay. The results demonstrated that ISO significantly inhibited the viability of BC cells in a concentration- and time-dependent manner (Fig. 1A). Therefore, based on the IC50 value, a concentration of 100 μM ISO and a treatment duration of 48 h were selected for subsequent experiments (Zhai et al. 2021). To further validate the generality of this effect and assess selectivity, we extended the CCK-8 assay to another BC cell line (T24) and a normal bladder epithelial cell line (SV-HUC-1). ISO also inhibited T24 cell viability in a concentration- and time-dependent manner (48 h IC50 = 85 μM), while SV-HUC-1 cells were markedly less sensitive (48 h IC50 > 200 μM), indicating a degree of cancer-selective cytotoxicity (Supplementary Fig. 1A, B).

B The long-term proliferative capacity of 5637 cells after ISO treatment was assessed by colony formation assay. C Cell cycle distribution of ISO-treated 5637 cells was analyzed by flow cytometry with PI staining. To further validate the effect of ISO on long-term proliferative capacity, we performed a colony formation assay. The results showed that compared to the DMSO group, the number of colonies was significantly reduced in ISO-treated cells (Fig. 1B), indicating that ISO effectively inhibits the sustained proliferative ability of BC cells.

Next, we explored the effects of ISO on the cell cycle and apoptosis using flow cytometry. Cell cycle analysis revealed that ISO treatment led to a significant increase in the proportion of cells in the G0/G1 phase and a corresponding decrease in the proportion of cells in the S phase (Fig. 1C), suggesting that ISO may inhibit cell cycle progression by inducing G0/G1 phase arrest. Furthermore, results from Annexin V-FITC/PI double staining showed that ISO treatment significantly increased the rates of early and late apoptosis in 5637 cells (Fig. 1D).

To evaluate the effects of ISO on the migration and invasion capabilities of 5637 cells, we performed Transwell migration and invasion assays. Compared to the DMSO group, the number of migrating and invading cells was significantly reduced after ISO treatment (Fig. 1E), indicating that ISO effectively inhibits the migration and invasion abilities of 5637 cells.

Finally, we examined the effect of ISO on cell adhesion capacity. The results demonstrated that ISO treatment weakened the adhesion ability of 5637 cells to key extracellular matrix components, Fibronectin and Collagen I (Fig. 1F). In summary, our experiments indicate that ISO effectively inhibits the proliferation, migration, invasion, and adhesion capabilities of BC cells, and induces cell cycle arrest and apoptosis, exhibiting significant anti-BC activity.

Network pharmacology identifies SRC and the PI3K-Akt pathway as core targets of ISO.

To systematically investigate the potential mechanism of action of ISO against BC, we employed a network pharmacology approach for predictive analysis. By screening multiple public databases, we identified 103 potential targets of ISO and 1385 disease targets related to BC. The intersection of these two sets yielded 35 common targets for both ISO and BC (Fig. 2A).

To further elucidate the interaction relationships among these targets, we performed a Protein–Protein Interaction (PPI) network analysis of the candidate target genes using the STRING database (Fig. 2B). This analysis generated a primary PPI network consisting of 35 nodes and 222 edges, with one unconnected gene being discarded. Using Closeness, Betweenness, and Degree as screening criteria, we established a core network comprising 9 nodes (Fig. 2C), where all parameters for these nodes exceeded the median values. The analysis revealed that targets such as AKT1, EGFR, and SRC occupied central hub positions within the network, suggesting their potential roles as key targets through which ISO exerts its anti-BC effects.

To gain deeper insights into the functions of these protein targets, we conducted Gene Ontology (GO) functional enrichment and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses using the Metascape database. The results indicated that in terms of biological processes (BP), the targets were primarily associated with processes such as the cell surface receptor protein tyrosine kinase signaling pathway, positive regulation of cell migration, and positive regulation of cell motility (Fig. 2D). GO cellular component (CC) analysis highlighted transferase complex, transferring phosphorus-containing groups, chromosomal region, and chromosome, telomeric region as significant aspects (Fig. 2D). Furthermore, GO molecular function (MF) analysis showed that these genes are primarily involved in phosphotransferase activity, alcohol group as acceptor, protein kinase activity, and kinase activity (Fig. 2D). These results strongly suggest that ISO may influence the biological behavior of BC cells by modulating kinase-mediated signal transduction pathways. The KEGG pathway analysis further supported this hypothesis. The results showed that the core targets were significantly enriched in pathways such as EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitor resistance, the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, and Focal adhesion (Fig. 2E). Among these, the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway exhibited a high enrichment score, contained the highest number of targets, and included the core targets SRC and AKT1 at central positions within the pathway. Therefore, based on the integrated network analysis and pathway enrichment results, we hypothesize that ISO may exert its anti-BC effects by targeting SRC, consequently inhibiting the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway.

SRC is confirmed to be highly expressed in BC

To investigate the clinical relevance of SRC in BC, we utilized UALCAN, an online analysis tool based on the TCGA database, to perform an in-depth analysis of the differential expression of SRC between bladder urothelial carcinoma (BLCA) tissues and normal tissues, as well as its relationship with patient clinicopathological stages. We compared the expression levels of SRC in normal samples (n = 19) and BLCA samples (n = 408). Compared to normal bladder tissues, the expression level of SRC was significantly upregulated in BLCA tissues (Fig. 3A), suggesting that SRC may act as an oncogene and play an important role in the pathogenesis and progression of BC. Furthermore, the expression level of SRC also showed significant differences across various pathological stages (Fig. 3B), indicating that high expression of SRC is closely associated with the progression of BC.

C Western blot and D RT-qPCR were used to detect the expression of SRC in SV-HUC-1 and 5637 cells, using β-actin as a control. All data are expressed as mean ± SD, n = 3, *P < 0.05

To validate these bioinformatics findings, we further detected the expression of SRC in BC cell lines. Western blot and RT-qPCR were used to compare the protein and mRNA levels of SRC between the human normal bladder epithelial cell line SV-HUC-1 and 5637 cells. The results showed that compared with normal cells, the protein and mRNA expression levels of SRC in 5637 cells were significantly increased (Figs. 3C, D). These in vitro findings further confirm the high expression of SRC in BC.

ISO directly binds to and inhibits SRC activity

The aforementioned bioinformatic analysis established the clinical value of SRC as a therapeutic target in BC. Next, to validate the predicted interaction between ISO and SRC via network pharmacology, we conducted molecular docking and cellular thermal shift assays (CETSA).

Molecular docking results revealed that ISO stably docks into the active pocket of the SRC protein with a binding energy of − 7.4 kcal/mol. It specifically interacts with residues PHE−101, TYR-152, ARG-158, and GLU-162 within the SRC protein domain (Fig. 4A). The cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA) results demonstrated that in the DMSO control group (without ISO treatment), the abundance of the SRC protein decreased rapidly as the heating temperature increased. In contrast, after ISO treatment, the SRC protein exhibited significantly higher stability at elevated temperatures, with a distinct rightward shift in its melting curve (Fig. 4B). This finding strongly confirms that ISO can directly bind to the SRC protein and enhance its thermal stability, which is consistent with the predictions from the molecular docking analysis.

Molecular docking and CETSA validate the direct binding between ISO and SRC. A Predictive analysis of the molecular docking between ISO and the SRC protein. B CETSA verifying the direct binding interaction between ISO and the SRC protein. C Western blot was used to detect the effect of ISO on SRC, p-SRC (Tyr416), and p-SRC (Tyr527)

We further investigated the effect of ISO on the expression and activation status of SRC in bladder cancer cells. Western blot results showed that ISO treatment significantly reduced the phosphorylation level of SRC at the Tyr416 site in a dose-dependent manner, this site is a key marker of its kinase activity. However, there were no significant changes in the total SRC protein level or the phosphorylation status at the Tyr527 site (Fig. 4C). These results indicate that ISO can specifically inhibit the kinase activity of SRC.

ISO inhibits glycolysis by targeting SRC

To investigate the pathway through which ISO regulates malignant progression in BC by targeting SRC, and given prior evidence linking SRC activation to glycolytic reprogramming, we next examined whether ISO influences glycolysis via SRC. we established an SRC-overexpressing cell model (oe-SRC) in 5637 cells, with a lentiviral empty vector control group (oe-NC).

First, the overexpression efficiency of SRC was confirmed by Western blot and RT-qPCR. Compared to the oe-NC group, both the protein and mRNA levels of SRC were significantly elevated in the oe-SRC group (Fig. 5A, B). Next, we detected key functional indicators of glycolysis, including the extracellular acidification rate (ECAR), oxygen consumption rate (OCR), glucose uptake, and lactate release. Compared to the Control group, ISO treatment significantly reduced the ECAR, OCR, glucose uptake, and lactate release in 5637 cells (Fig. 5C–F), indicating that ISO inhibits both aerobic glycolysis and mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation. However, in SRC-overexpressing cells, the inhibitory effect of ISO on glycolysis was markedly reversed, manifested as increased ECAR and OCR, as well as elevated glucose uptake and lactate release.

E Glucose uptake and F lactate production were measured in 5637 cells under different treatments. To investigate the underlying molecular mechanism, we examined the expression of glycolysis-related enzymes using RT-qPCR and Western blot. The results showed that ISO treatment significantly downregulated the expression of hexokinase 2 (HK2), glucose transporter 1 (GLUT1), and 6-phosphofructo-2-kinase/fructose−2,6-biphosphatase 3 (PFKFB3) at both the mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 5G, H). Similarly, SRC overexpression effectively reversed the inhibitory effects of ISO on these key glycolytic enzymes. Furthermore, Western blot results confirmed that ISO treatment reduced the phosphorylation level of SRC protein (p-SRC), while the total SRC protein level remained largely unchanged (Fig. 5H). In the oe-SRC group, both total SRC and p-SRC levels were significantly increased (Duarte et al. 2024). The inhibitory effect of ISO on the SRC/PI3K/Akt axis and glycolysis was further validated in T24 cells. ISO treatment of T24 cells significantly decreased ECAR, OCR, glucose uptake, and lactate production in T24 cells (Supplementary Fig. 1C–F). Moreover, ISO reduced the phosphorylation of SRC, PI3K, Akt, and mTOR, as well as the expression of HK2, GLUT1, and PFKFB3 (Supplementary Fig. 1G), consistent with the observations in 5637 cells. These results indicate that ISO has a significant inhibitory effect on glycolysis in BC, and this effect is specifically mediated by targeting SRC.

ISO inhibits BC progression by regulating glycolysis via the SRC/PI3K/Akt axis

To ultimately confirm that ISO exerts its effects via the SRC/PI3K/Akt signaling axis, functional rescue assays were performed in the present study with ISO treatment, combined with SRC overexpression and PI3K inhibitor LY294002 administration, respectively.

First, the activation status of core proteins in the SRC/PI3K/Akt signaling axis was analyzed by Western blot. Compared with the SRC overexpression negative control group, the total and phosphorylated protein levels of SRC were significantly increased in the SRC overexpression group, along with a marked upregulation of the phosphorylation levels of downstream PI3K, Akt and mTOR (Fig. 6A). Crucially, in the SRC overexpression plus LY294002 co-treatment group, the phosphorylation levels of downstream PI3K, Akt and mTOR were fully restored. These results clearly demonstrated that LY294002 could successfully bypass the inhibited SRC and directly suppress the downstream SRC/PI3K/Akt signaling pathway.

J Cell migration and invasion capabilities were evaluated by Transwell assay. K Cell adhesion capacity was detected by cell adhesion assay. All data are expressed as mean ± SD, n = 3, *P < 0.05

Subsequently, glycolysis-related functional indices were detected. Compared with the SRC overexpression negative control group, SRC overexpression significantly promoted cellular glycolysis and enhanced glucose uptake and lactate production, whereas LY294002 co-treatment markedly reversed these effects (Fig. 6B–E).

Finally, alterations in the malignant phenotypes of cells were evaluated. CCK-8 and colony formation assays showed that SRC overexpression significantly increased the viability and proliferative capacity of 5637 cells (Fig. 6F, G). Flow cytometry analysis indicated that SRC overexpression alleviated cell cycle arrest and apoptosis (Fig. 6H, I). In addition, Transwell and cell adhesion assays confirmed that SRC overexpression remarkably enhanced the migratory, invasive and adhesive capacities of 5637 cells (Fig. 6J, K). Notably, these changes in malignant phenotypes were completely reversed by LY294002 co-treatment.

In summary, these results indicate that ISO directly targets SRC, inhibits its kinase activity, and consequently downregulates the transduction of PI3K/Akt signaling. The inhibited PI3K/Akt signaling pathway fails to effectively drive glycolytic metabolic reprogramming, ultimately leading to suppressed proliferation, increased apoptosis, and diminished metastatic capacity in BC cells. The anti-tumor effects of ISO could be specifically reversed by upstream SRC overexpression, and such effects of SRC overexpression were further abrogated by subsequent LY294002 co-treatment. These results fully confirm that the SRC/PI3K/Akt signaling axis is the core mechanism underlying the functional effects of ISO.

Discussion

Currently, the treatment of patients with chemotherapy-resistant or advanced BC still poses significant clinical challenges (Pan et al. 2016). Targeting cancer metabolic reprogramming, particularly inhibiting aerobic glycolysis (the Warburg effect), has emerged as a promising anti-tumor strategy in recent years. This study is the first to systematically demonstrate that the natural flavonoid compound ISO can directly target SRC kinase, inhibit the activation of its downstream PI3K/Akt signaling pathway, consequently block glycolysis, and ultimately suppress the proliferation, survival, and metastatic capacity of BC cells. Our findings not only provide novel mechanistic insights into the anti-tumor effects of ISO but also establish a solid theoretical foundation forshi its potential application as a metabolic-modulating therapeutic agent for BC.

ISO represents a potential drug for BC treatment. According to the TCMSP database, ISO exhibits an oral bioavailability (OB) of 49.60% and a drug-likeness (DL) value of 0.31, along with favorable anti-tumor activity. Current studies have reported that ISO induces cell cycle arrest and apoptosis in human BC cells through ROS-mediated activation of the AMP protein kinase signaling pathway (Park et al. 2019). Furthermore, ISO concentration-dependently inhibits cell growth activity in BC cells(Prasain et al. 2016). Consistent with these findings, our study revealed that ISO significantly inhibits the viability, proliferation, migration, invasion, and adhesion capabilities of BC 5637 cells, and induces G0/G1 phase arrest and apoptosis (Zhang et al. 2023). Notably, in addition to its direct anti-cancer effect, ISO also has an important metabolic regulatory function. It has been shown to effectively inhibit glycolysis by suppressing enolase 1 and regulating the PPARα/PGC-1α signaling axis, thereby protecting the heart (Guo et al. 2025). This highlights its potential as a novel metabolic modulator for the treatment of bladder cancer.

Importantly, through the integration of network pharmacology, bioinformatic analysis, and molecular docking experiments, we identified SRC kinase as a direct target of ISO. The SRC protein is a non-receptor tyrosine kinase and the product of the first identified cellular proto-oncogene (Gong et al. 2025). Overexpression or activation of SRC has been shown to directly affect downstream effectors involved in numerous cellular events associated with cell transformation, including metabolism, proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, cell adhesion, migration, invasion, stemness, and metastasis. SRC may also play important roles in the tumor microenvironment by inducing angiogenesis or immune evasion (Pelaz and Tabernero 2022). Our bioinformatic analysis further confirmed that SRC is highly expressed in BC and correlates with pathological stages, underscoring its clinical value as a therapeutic target.

Aerobic glycolysis is a crucial characteristic of energy metabolism in tumor cells, referring to the ability of malignant tumors to obtain ATP through glycolysis under aerobic conditions, also known as the Warburg effect. Furthermore, solid evidence of SRC-mediated glycolysis has been reported early on; it can regulate glycolysis through various mechanisms, including modulating master glycolytic transcription factors (HIF-1α and MYC), insulin secretion, regulating glycolytic enzyme activity (HK, PFKFB3, G6PD) through phosphorylation, and via well-known SRC substrates central to energy metabolism and cancer, such as the PI3K-Akt-mTOR axis and EGFR (Zhang et al. 2023). Studies have found that SRC phosphorylation can activate the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway, inducing mTOR-mediated oncogenic glycolysis and promoting the growth and metastasis of hepatocellular carcinoma (Luo et al. 2021). SRC blockade can inhibit breast cancer development by reducing glucose metabolism through suppressing the levels of glucose transporters (Jain et al. 2015). Moreover, studies on cisplatin resistance in BC revealed that hyperactivation of SRC can upregulate glycolysis, particularly the pentose phosphate pathway, leading to increased nucleotide synthesis and production of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH), thereby neutralizing cisplatin-induced reactive oxygen species and protecting BC cells from cisplatin-induced DNA damage. Inhibiting SRC activity through knockout or SRC inhibitors can effectively reverse this phenomenon (Gong et al. 2025). Our study found that ISO can inhibit aerobic glycolysis and mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation in 5637 cells by targeting and inhibiting SRC, reducing ECAR, OCR, glucose uptake, and lactate release, and downregulating the expression of key glycolytic enzymes (HK2, GLUT1, PFKFB3). These inhibitory effects on glycolysis were significantly reversed upon SRC overexpression.

Subsequently, we further investigated the specific mechanism by which ISO targets SRC to regulate glycolysis and inhibit BC. Results from network pharmacology suggested that the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway plays a crucial role in the anti-BC effects of ISO. Therefore, this pathway was selected for further validation and mechanistic exploration. We performed rescue experiments using SRC overexpression and the PI3K inhibitor LY294002. On one hand, SRC overexpression significantly reversed the ISO-induced inhibition of PI3K/Akt pathway activation, the downregulation of key glycolytic enzymes, the suppression of glucose uptake and lactate production, and the inhibition of malignant phenotypes in BC cells. On the other hand, LY294002 co-treatment was able to bypass the overexpressed SRC, inhibited the signal transduction of PI3K/Akt signaling, and effectively reversed the effects of SRC overexpression on the malignant phenotypes of bladder cancer cells. These results fully confirm that ISO exerts its inhibitory effects on glycolysis and tumor progression by targeting and inhibiting SRC, thereby suppressing its downstream PI3K/Akt signaling pathway. This aligns with previously reported mechanisms of SRC-mediated glycolysis in hepatocellular carcinoma via PI3K/Akt (Luo et al. 2021; Yu et al. 2023), but our study is the first to elucidate this mechanism in BC and specifically for ISO.

A potential concern regarding the novelty of our findings is that ISO has been previously reported to target SRC in colorectal cancer (Saud et al. 2013). However, that study focused on the CSK-mediated Tyr527 phosphorylation and β-catenin signaling, without investigating metabolic regulation. Our study provides several distinct advances: (i) first demonstration of ISO’s anti-cancer effects in bladder cancer; (ii) experimental validation of direct ISO-SRC binding by CETSA; (iii) elucidation of the SRC/PI3K/Akt/glycolysis axis as a key mechanism; (iv) establishment of causality through SRC overexpression and PI3K inhibitor rescue experiments. Thus, while the compound-target pair is not entirely new, the context (bladder cancer), the specific mechanism (glycolysis via PI3K/Akt), and the level of mechanistic validation are novel.

Certainly, this study has some limitations. While we have validated key findings in two bladder cancer cell lines (5637 and T24), all experiments were conducted in vitro. Future studies should establish BC xenograft models to evaluate the efficacy of ISO and its impact on the SRC/PI3K/Akt axis and glycolysis in vivo. Additionally, although we have demonstrated that ISO inhibits glycolysis by targeting the SRC/PI3K/Akt axis, we did not perform ATP assays to further distinguish the relative contributions of glycolytic versus mitochondrial ATP to BC cell survival. Future work using ATP assays could provide deeper insight into the metabolic consequences of ISO treatment.

Conclusions

In summary, this study demonstrates that ISO inhibits the proliferation, survival, and metastatic capacity of BC cells by targeting and inhibiting SRC, thereby suppressing its downstream PI3K/Akt signaling pathway and subsequently blocking glycolysis. Our findings provide a mechanistic basis for considering ISO as a potential metabolic-targeting agent for BC, warranting further preclinical evaluation.
