Ophthalmic artery angioplasty in a cohort of patients with geographic atrophy secondary to non-exudative age-related macular degeneration
Authors: Pedro Lylyk, Ivan Lylyk, Pedro N Lylyk, Carlos Bleise, Esteban Scrivano, Javier Lundquist, Rodolfo Nella-Castro, Nicolas Perez, Jeff Franco, Michael W Calhoun, Luana R Wilbur, Philip J Rosenfeld, Mario J Saravia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThere is increasing evidence that vascular disease and chronic perfusion deficits play a role in the progression of age-related macular degeneration (AMD). This controlled clinical study evaluated the safety and feasibility of treating patients with late-stage AMD and associated geographic atrophy (GA). In addition, exploratory endpoints evaluated the potential to disrupt the disease process and initiate functional changes.MethodsA total of 17 subjects with confirmed late-stage AMD with GA, ophthalmic artery stenosis, and systemic stability were enrolled in this prospective multicenter study. Eleven (64.7%) of these subjects were successfully treated with ophthalmic artery angioplasty using an investigational ophthalmic percutaneous transluminal catheter system designed for this novel anatomical target and indication for use. Primary endpoints included procedure-related complications and procedural feasibility. All exploratory analyses were focused on ophthalmic outcomes and assessed for potential efficacy in choroidal thickness, visual acuity, reading ability, and patient-reported outcomes. Treated subjects were followed for 3 months by the interventional neuroradiology site and for 6 months by the ophthalmology site.ResultsAll systemic procedure-related adverse events (AEs) were potentially expected, effectively treated, resolved without sequelae, and included bronchial abrasion and hemoptysis secondary to intubation, urinary tract infection secondary to urinary catheterization, and vascular access site inflammation and hematoma. Procedure-related ocular AEs (eg, suspected retinal microemboli and potential reperfusion injury) were asymptomatic, graded as mild, and resolved without treatment or sequelae. Mean (SD) primary lesion stenoses decreased 59% from 43.7 (14.25)% to 18.2 (12.09)%. At study exit, mean best-corrected visual acuity improved by 6.7 letters (P=0.003) over baseline, and reading ability results improved 3.4%, 5.1%, and 28.5% (P=0.03) over baseline for reading acuity, critical print size, and reading speed, respectively. Patient-reported outcomes showed improvements in mobility and independence and in reading and accessing information.ConclusionsThe feasibility of ophthalmic artery angioplasty in this population was demonstrated with the investigational devices, while providing an acceptable safety profile. Efficacy and functional improvement were also seen, providing guidance for future study.

Introduction

It is estimated that up to 200 million people worldwide have age-related macular degeneration (AMD), and this number is projected to rise to nearly 300 million by 2040.1 An estimated 80% have the dry form of AMD, and the presence of geographic atrophy (GA) is the most disabling and latest stage.2 There are limited treatment options; those that exist present an undesirable burden to the patient, and the patient’s functional vision continues to decline at a rate of one full line of vision per year.3

AMD is considered a complex genetic disease, and there are epidemiological data linking cardiovascular disease with AMD.4 Patients with AMD are generally at higher risk of being diagnosed with coronary disease, and studies have shown an association between AMD, hypertension, and atherosclerosis.5 Interest in the relationship between chronic vascular disease, hypoperfusion, ocular hemodynamics, and the potential to effect change in ocular health has led to studies showing that revascularization of the internal carotid artery (ICA) proximal to the ophthalmic artery (OA) results in improved ocular microcirculation and visual function,68 which has renewed focus on ocular perfusion in AMD.

With specialized ocular microvasculature imaging it can be seen that choriocapillaris flow deficits accumulate preferentially within the central macula with age,9 10 and the extent of macular choriocapillaris deficits is correlated with the growth of GA in patients with late-stage AMD. This suggests that ocular perfusion is associated with disease progression. Since the blood supply of the photoreceptors and retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) is primarily provided by the choriocapillaris, which is located directly beneath the RPE, and the RPE and photoreceptors undergo atrophy as GA progresses, this suggests that the loss of the blood supply to the photoreceptors would have serious consequences on visual function.5 Furthermore, it has been known for many years that, based on size, the macula receives the highest blood flow of any tissue in the body and anything that reduces the blood supply can cause hypoxia, malfunction, and disease.11

It has been shown that the choroidal blood flow is approximately 10 times that of the cerebral blood flow when considered on the basis of blood flow per unit mass of tissue.12 In addition, there is a fundamental difference between the eye and other end organs since the retinal tissue, particularly the photoreceptors, has the highest metabolic demand. The inner retina has the highest metabolic rate of any central nervous system tissue,13 the outer retina has the most metabolically active tissue,14 and the photoreceptors consume more oxygen per gram than any other cells in the human body.13 The central area of the retina known as the macula is very sensitive to the need for continual nutrient support and debris exchange, which can create a chronically ischemic environment and result in hypoxic imbalance with associated cascading events.

Recent research has shown a statistically significant correlation between AMD disease progression and OA volumetric blood flow (P=0.002) as evaluated by 7T MRI.15 Furthermore, a retrospective study that included a compassionate use case series reported that OA stenoses were confirmed and treated with angioplasty in a population of patients with late-stage AMD. This population of patients was end-stage; however, visual improvements were appreciated.16 Although it is known that the development of AMD includes both genetic and environmental factors, the discovery of OA stenoses is recognized as a treatable environmental stressor in this complex disease process. If left untreated, it continues to provide a relentless source of chronic ischemia, exacerbating the development and progression of late-stage AMD.

For these reasons, this study was designed to evaluate the safety and feasibility of treating OA stenoses with angioplasty in patients with late-stage AMD with GA and represents a novel population for the interventionalist based on indications for treatment alone.

Methods

Study design

This was a prospective multicenter open-label study conducted in Argentina which planned to enroll up to 20 subjects. All subjects were seen at two sites during participation; an ophthalmic site which specializes in the traditional diagnosis and management of the target population and an interventional site which specializes in neurovascular interventional procedures.

Given the novel treatment population, the ophthalmic and interventional sites were each responsible for their individual area of expertise, communication, and subject education for purposes of consent. All subjects completed consenting processes on a two-part Ethics Committee-approved consent form at each of the two sites prior to their respective screening evaluations. Subjects were then required to qualify to participate from both an ophthalmic and interventional neuroradiology standpoint prior to being approved to schedule the study procedure.

The ocular inclusion and exclusion criteria were designed to include the intended population (eg, AMD with GA, vision 20/80 or worse, ≥60 years of age), while excluding the likelihood of confounding variables (eg, differential diagnoses, ocular opacities, active exudation, high myopia), and the worse seeing qualifying eye was enrolled.

The systemic and vascular inclusion and exclusion criteria included the presence of OA stenosis and decreased potential risks to the extent possible (eg, 6-month stability in vascular and cardiac event history) and excluded advanced vascular pathology, added bleeding risks, uncontrolled hypertension, renal insufficiency, and significant new vascular pathology on preoperative MR angiography and CT angiography.

Qualifying subjects were scheduled for the study procedure, treated, and observed to ensure stability before being discharged. All treated subjects participated between May 2023 and July 2024 and were followed for 3 months by the interventional site to assess safety and stability, and for 6 months by the ophthalmic site to assess exploratory endpoints and to guide future study. All subjects completed all visits.

Key evaluations

The National Institute of Health Stroke Scale examination was performed before and after the procedure and at 3 months. Ophthalmic testing included the Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study (ETDRS) best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), as well as native language MN Reading Speed,17 the Impact of Vision Impairment Profile Questionnaire (IVI),18 sub-foveal choroidal thickness (SFChT) via optical coherence tomography (Spectralis, Heidelberg, Germany), and color fundus and autofluorescence imaging. All ophthalmic imaging was processed through an independent reading center (Boston Image Reading Center, Boston, Massachusetts, USA). Adverse events were collected at both sites from the time of consent.

Preoperative screening MRI and CT acquisition

Vascular imaging was separated into a two-sequence strategy. The first sequence used CT (Philips IQon Spectral CT) focused on the chest and neck to use expedited imaging of larger arteries to identify potential risks along the access path. The presence of an OA vascular lesion was confirmed and vascular health evaluated through 3T MRI angiography using a 32-channel head coil (Philips, Eindhoven, Netherlands) from the supraclinoid segment of the ICA to the distal regions of the OA using a 3D time of flight (TOF) imaging sequence. The latter sequence presented a longer acquisition time but provided higher resolution of the target anatomy. The images were then rendered and graded by a masked evaluator to determine preliminary eligibility from a vascular perspective.

Study procedure

Subjects were prepared for the procedure in the AngioSuite and vascular access was initiated in accordance with standard of care. Subjects were administered 5000 units of heparin and an additional 1000 units per hour to achieve and maintain a target activated clotting time of 250–300 s. Fluoroscopic roadmaps were used for selective catheterization, and biplane 2D digital subtraction angiography (DSA) and 3D rotational angiography were performed in addition to VasoCT (cone-beam CT) before the procedure (Azurion, Koninklijke Philips, Eindhoven, Netherlands). The severity of stenosis at the target anatomy was characterized and final eligibility confirmed.

Vascular imaging was reviewed, appropriate devices chosen, and the treatment strategy determined. Selective vascular access was achieved with an introducer sheath and intermediate catheter to reach the ICA. An investigational ophthalmic percutaneous transluminal catheter system (OcuDyne, Brooklyn Park, Minnesota, USA) that included two individual components, an aiming microcatheter and a micro balloon catheter, was then employed.

A neurovascular over-the-wire aiming microcatheter (0.0165 ID) with bidirectional torque and customized tip configurations was used to achieve OA cannulation. A super-selective injection of a calcium channel blocker (nimodipine) into the OA via the aiming microcatheter was used as needed to mitigate vasospasm before and after the intervention. A rapid exchange neurovascular microballoon catheter with a minimized crossing profile was advanced over the 0.014-inch guidewire to the OA and balloon angioplasty was performed on the affected vascular segment(s).

The microballoon catheter was available in an assortment of balloon lengths and diameters to best address individual endovascular targets and included a flexible and kink-resistant balloon with an atraumatic and flexible tip designed to gently traverse small and potentially tortuous anatomy. With a mean native artery diameter of <2.0 mm and pathology that often restricted the lumen size to <1.0 mm, lesion identification, initial balloon selection, and maximum inflation were tightly controlled with a target balloon to artery ratio of approximately 80%.

There were no success or failure criteria assigned to the degree of change effected by the dilatation; however, repeat imaging was obtained post-procedure to ensure no immediate complications and to document post-procedural changes in the OA. All devices were removed, standard closure was performed, patients were admitted for observation per protocol, discharged per standard practice, and returned to their referring ophthalmologist for follow-up examinations.

The use of pre-, peri-, and post-procedural antiplatelet medication was prescribed and adjusted at the discretion of the treating physician based on standard of care, the subject’s concomitant regimen, and clinical judgment.

Intraoperative imaging

Angiographic DSA and the intraoperative cone-beam CT scan were performed with a Philips BiPlane Azurion 7. Images were captured for the precise OA origin and the relationship with an atheroma nearby or in the artery and processed with VasoCT software from a Philips workstation.

A series of images was captured for all subjects pre-, peri-, and post-procedurally, focusing on the ophthalmic segment of the ICA and the OA, from the ostium to the central retinal artery, including balloon inflations (figure 1). In addition, VasoCT was used on a subset of subjects to generate detailed images from the OA origin at the ICA to the terminal branches.

Angioplasty of the ophthalmic artery with 2D DSA using a 1.5 mm x 10.0 mm microballoon catheter. The white arrow indicates the location of the mid-balloon marker band.

Vascular characterization

A modified Warfarin-Aspirin Symptomatic Intracranial Disease (WASID) method19 was adopted to characterize arterial stenosis in the OA due to several limitations in the strict use of WASID. The modified method included use of 3D imaging, considered the potential for asymmetrical lesions, and the baseline criteria were modified to identify an arterial section that determined the native arterial diameter within the confines of the OA itself.

Data analyses

Primary and secondary endpoints were associated with procedural safety and feasibility; all exploratory endpoints were associated with ophthalmic changes that could signal efficacy. Continuous variables were summarized as mean, SD, and range. Normal distribution of datasets was confirmed using the Shapiro–Wilk test (P>0.05), and a single-tailed paired t-test analysis was used to derive statistical significance of changes from baseline and between treated and untreated eyes. Correlation analysis for normal and non-normal distribution datasets used either Pearson’s or Spearman’s rho analysis, respectively, but were not powered for statistical significance.

Results

The safety population includes all subjects in whom a study procedure was attempted (whether completed or not), and the treatment population includes all subjects in whom the study procedure was completed. All treated subjects were seen at all visits; no data were imputed.

Safety population

The study intended to enroll up to 20 subjects; however, a total of 17 were ultimately enrolled and included in the safety population. Extensive delays associated with the global COVID-19 pandemic and resulting investigational device shelf-life limitations precluded the ability to enroll the full number of approved participants.

Of the qualifying subjects, 11 (64.7%) were female, 17 (100%) were Caucasian, and their mean (SD) age was 75.1 (8.45) years. All had confirmed late-stage AMD with GA, and BCVA of ETDRS mean (SD) letters read was 31.9 (18.0) (range 55–1) or a mean of 20/230 (range 20/80–20/957) Snellen equivalent.

With regard to AMD risk factors, 10 (58.8%) had a known familial history of AMD and 11 (64.7%) reported a history of smoking, with a mean (SD) burden of 36.9 (28.27) smoking years. With significant overlap, medical histories included nine (52.9%), seven (41.2%), and three (17.6%) patients with pre-existing hypertension, vascular disease, or diabetes mellitus, respectively. In addition, 13 (76.5%) had a higher than normal body mass index (≥25.0 kg/m2).

Baseline imaging showed diminished choroidal thickness, reticular pseudodrusen, advanced GA, and a confirmed absence of active exudation in the study eye (figure 2).

Blue-light fundus autofluorescence imaging of late-stage geographic atrophy (Spectralis, Heidelberg Engineering, Heidelberg, Germany). The dark central areas of atrophy (white arrows) characterized by loss of the photoreceptor layer and retinal pigment epithelium have expanded and are coalescing. Lighter retained areas between the expanding atrophy (green arrows) and surrounding penumbra (yellow arrows) are common signs of declining retinal function and imminent atrophy growth; however, these are also the areas of potential revitalization prior to complete cellular loss.

Arterial characteristics

A significant number of subjects (58.8%) presented with either a bovine or type III aortic arch, and many had vascular lesions proximal to the OA of varying potential clinical significance. A total of 20 OA lesions were identified in 17 subjects. The mean (SD) percent stenosis in the safety population was 40.0 (13.34)% with a range of approximately 17% for a secondary lesion, up to 67% for a primary lesion.

Primary lesions, defined as the most severe in the presence of multiple lesions, had a mean (SD) 41.6 (13.18)% stenosis (range 25.0–66.7%). Lesions were either focal (<5 mm) or diffuse (≥5 mm) and were located from the OA ostium to just proximal to the central retinal artery bifurcation.

Safety endpoints

The primary endpoint was procedure-related complications from day 0 to week 4 and included four systemic events and five ocular events. Systemically, vascular access wound inflammation and hematoma (one each), hemoptysis (bronchial), and a urinary tract infection were reported; the latter two were secondary to intubation and urinary catheter use during general anesthesia, respectively. All systemic events were potentially expected, effectively treated, and resolved.

Procedure-related ocular events included four events of microemboli or hypoperfusion and one event of suspected reperfusion injury. Transient focal areas of retinal whitening and edema outside the macula (figure 3) are thought to arise from a fine embolus or short period of hypoperfusion. Reperfusion injury was considered the likely cause of a transient area of inner retinal dot hemorrhage. All ocular events were asymptomatic, mild, and resolved without treatment or sequelae.

Treatment population

Due to anatomical challenges associated with the endovascular target, the treatment population included 11 subjects (64.7%) in whom the procedures were completed. Primary lesions had a mean (SD) stenosis of 43.7 (14.25)% (range 29.4–66.7%) with an estimated mean (SD) length of 5.2 (4.25) mm.

Endovascular treatment

Total and study-specific procedure times were evaluated separately. The differentiation is in the time it takes from initial arterial access to the point when a study device enters the surgical field. Total surgical time was a mean (SD) of 89:33 (28:22) min with a range of 56–130 min. However, the study-specific procedure time was a mean (SD) of 37:38 (23:44) min with a range of 13–103 min.

The OA as an endovascular target presented unique challenges associated with the take-off angle from the ICA, the varied vascular tortuosity, and the location of stenoses. The focus of final intraoperative eligibility centered on the confirmed presence of OA stenosis within treatment reach and ensuring the absence of previously undiagnosed vascular risks on the path to that stenosis. There were no anatomical variations or abnormalities in this population that presented a situation where the procedure could not be attempted safely in the judgment of the interventionalist.

The post-treatment mean (SD) stenosis was 18.2 (12.09)%, representing a statistically significant improvement (P<0.001). The individual percent improvement ranged from 40% to 88% in primary lesion stenosis.

Correlation analysis

Although the study was not powered for statistical significance, potential correlations with pretreatment stenosis and treatment effect were explored.

The pretreatment stenosis showed a moderately positive correlation (r2=0.468) with the level of pretreatment GA. This correlation increased (r2=0.613) with the single outlier removed: a subject with GA area >33 mm2 who would not qualify for subsequent study due to the size of the GA nearly double the next largest atrophy. This indicates that there is some correlation between the extent of pretreatment stenosis and the extent of macular atrophy. There was a weaker negative correlation (rs=−0.242) with pretreatment visual acuity: the greater the stenosis, the lower the visual acuity.

The percentage change in primary stenoses showed a positive correlation (r2=0.348) with post-treatment visual acuity gain that increased (r2=0.729) and approached statistical significance (P=0.06) with distance BCVA outliers removed. The few distance BCVA outliers showed improved functional vision at near, but did not demonstrate significantly improved distance visual acuity. A positive correlation with post-treatment percentage change in SFChT was shown (r2=0.371) that increased (r2=0.589) to statistical significance (P=0.04) when a single outlier, the only subject who experienced a post-treatment improvement in SFChT of >22% and more than double the mean increase, was removed from the analysis.

Exploratory analyses

All exploratory analyses were conducted on ophthalmic follow-up data captured for the treatment population to assess potential subjective and objective efficacy. The mean ETDRS BCVA line change from baseline by subject and visit is shown in figure 4. With the exception of one subject (Subject 25-017), who exhibited the loss of one letter at week 1, returned over baseline, and then exited the study with a loss of two letters at month 6, all other subjects were unchanged (one subject) or showed improvement throughout participation and remained above baseline to exit.

Best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) line gain/loss by subject and visit.

Mean BCVA for the study eye cohort showed statistically significant improvement over baseline at all visits. Mean BCVA improved 3.6 ETDRS letters read (P=0.004) at week 1, peaked at week 4 with 7.2 letters (P=0.001) and maintained relative stability through month 6, showing a mean gain of 6.7 letters (P=0.003) at study exit. A statistically significant improvement (P=0.03, P=0.001, P=0.04, P=0.01) was also seen in mean BCVA of treated eyes compared with the fellow eye cohort at week 1, week 4, month 3, and month 6 visits, respectively.

Due to central vision loss and the necessity for a minimum reading acuity of at least 20/632, a subgroup of eyes was able to provide MN Read assessments at every visit. This evaluation assesses the smallest print read at any speed (reading acuity), the most words per minute (reading speed), and the smallest print the subject can read before an appreciable loss of reading speed (critical print size).

This subgroup showed improvement for the study eye in all categories: reading acuity (3.4%), critical print size (5.1%), and reading speed (28.5%) at month 6. These reflect a statistically significant improvement in reading speed (P=0.03) at study exit and demonstrate functional improvement. The evaluable fellow eyes showed improvement in mean critical print size (10.1%), but the reading speed declined (5.3%). This showed that the eyes are collectively reading smaller print more slowly.

Patient-reported outcomes were collected using the IVI questionnaire at each visit. Since questionnaires apply to the whole body function and this study treated the worse seeing eye, the best eye is already relied on and not expected to experience improvement. However, it has been shown that the worse seeing eye has more of an impact on functional quality of life than is generally assumed.20 In this population, significant mean improvement in the areas of mobility and independence and reading and accessing information was demonstrated. Emotional well-being showed initial improvement, followed by a 5% decline at study exit. These data are consistent with the population under study,21 which historically struggles with depression and worry associated with the loss of independence.

A statistically significant increase over baseline in mean SFChT was observed at week 1 (P=0.002) and week 4 (P=0.004), which slowly declined through month 6 but remained above baseline. Similarly, compared with the fellow eye cohort, the mean study eye SFChT showed a statistically significant improvement at week 1 (P=0.01) and week 4 (P=0.03), which remained improved over the fellow eye cohort through to the end of the study, dropping below statistical significance.

Discussion

End organs such as the heart, kidneys, lungs, liver, and brain have various inherent processes that manage variability of perfusion, as does the eye to some degree. However, when ocular perfusion deficits, ischemia, and hypoxic imbalance become chronic, these mechanisms may fail to protect the organ.

When considering chronic hypoxia and the justification for treating it, the discussion must include the difference in procedural risk compared with other intracranial lesions. It is also important to understand why a comparatively small amount of chronic perfusion deficit can provide catastrophic consequences to the eye, given the metabolic demand of retinal tissue.

For over 10 years the Stenting and Aggressive Medical Management for Preventing Recurrent stroke in Intracranial Stenosis (SAMMPRIS) study22 became a benchmark associated with medical treatment as well as intracranial angioplasty and stenting. The result is an intracranial stenotic threshold for the indication of angioplasty at ≥70% or, of note to this discussion, ≤1 mm residual lumen. However, it is important to recognize that stenoses of <70% were found to be hemodynamically relevant in the WASID trial.23 Regardless, the risk versus benefit ratio of patients who have suffered neurovascular events an average of 7 days (range 4–19 days) prior to randomization is substantially different from the study population, which renders this threshold irrelevant.

Extracranially, the requirement of a symptomatic patient (signaling an imminent or acute cardiac event) with a 50% stenosis threshold has long been adopted.24 These changes were supported by evidence that treating 50% stenosis is generally associated with less risk. Additional benchmark studies followed with symptomatic patients with 50% stenosis,25 26 so no significant data are available with a lower threshold. However, this study population does not include acute vascular events or their associated risks, and the clinically relevant stenosis threshold is ultimately unknown until the necessary research adds to this body of knowledge.

To better understand the target anatomy, retinal tissue captures incoming photons and transmits them via neuronal pathways for the brain to perceive a visual picture. The retina includes both inner and outer layers with the outer retina containing the photoreceptors. A fundamental difference between the eye and other end organs is the higher metabolic demand of retinal tissue. The inner retina has the highest metabolic rate of any central nervous system tissue,13 the outer retina has the most metabolically active tissue in the human body,14 and the photoreceptors consume more oxygen per gram than any other cells in the human body.13 This creates a challenging environment for the eye to function when exposed to a chronic perfusion deficit.

The preconceived treatment thresholds in light of the retina as a highly metabolic site with a greater demand for blood flow than any other tissue in the body should therefore be reconsidered. Stenosis of <50% may have a profound effect on the health and function of retinal tissue in this relatively small artery with a mean (SD) diameter of 1.54 (0.04) mm and 1.31 (0.05) mm in male and female patients, respectively.27 For example, following Hagen–Poiseuille’s equation, 50% stenosis results in calculated luminal blood flow declining by 75%. For the metabolically delicate retinal environment with insufficient collateral flow,6 this has additional relevance.

Given the advanced AMD disease of all participants, these data suggest that levels of stenoses lower than those traditionally considered ‘moderate’ (≥50%) are likely to be relevant in the target population (with AMD risk alleles), resulting in a chronic hypoxic state. In the current study, OA stenoses ranged from approximately 25% to 67% in primary lesions. As a result, in some cases the stenosis was below typical thresholds for intracranial angioplasty.28 However, when including the alternative threshold of ≤1 mm residual lumen29 with a mean (SD) of 0.91 (0.32) mm and range of 0.47–1.45 mm, the study population is more closely aligned. In the interest of data-driven decisions, limitations were not placed on the severity of stenoses as long as they were identifiable and within treatment reach. It would appear that the resulting data support this strategy.

The vascular configuration of the inner retina and the metabolic requirements of the outer retina demand an efficient system of oxygen delivery, waste removal, and autophagy. A chronically ischemic environment results in hypoxic imbalance and oxidative damage. The cascading cellular responses such as chronic inflammation, complement activation, and production of vascular endothelial growth factor have been the targets of therapeutic treatments to slow this disease process. However, it must be appreciated that the root cause of chronic ischemia and the underlying retinal environment has not been addressed in the use of these treatment paradigms.

Research has established a relationship between decreased OA blood flow and AMD disease progression compared with controls.15 Additionally, angioplasty has been performed in the OA of patients with end-stage AMD with positive effects on vision.16 In support of the mechanism of action, improved ocular blood flow following endarterectomy or angioplasty has resulted in visual improvement and increased choroidal thickness.30 31

This study adds to the body of knowledge by confirming the safety and feasibility of the procedure in a controlled clinical setting. In addition, insight into the potential to resuscitate declining retinal cellular health has been gained. The hyperfluorescent reflex that produces a penumbra surrounding GA (figure 2) is broadly thought to be a reflection of declining retinal function and cellular health due to the chronic ischemic environment. If these cells are rejuvenated by the study treatment and maintained by perfusion restoration, it makes sense that it would result in the efficacy that was demonstrated. Objective outcomes included an increase in choroidal thickness in all subjects and a mean improvement that was statistically significant. Subjectively, visual and functional improvements were also demonstrated. Moreover, the subjects themselves reported improved mobility as well as reading and accessing information, via a validated questionnaire.

These data show that vascular intervention improved the retinal health of the eye. However, interventional treatment is not without risk. While devices designed specifically for this use will reduce the potential for adverse events, the potential risks—though low in incidence and unlikely in this population—could theoretically carry neurological consequences. This study shows that, although there are known and expected events associated with the study procedure, the observed systemic events are common across all procedures that use either vascular access or general anesthesia. These events are well understood and effectively treated.

More specific to the study procedure, the suspected ocular microemboli (n=4) and reperfusion (n=1) events were collectively observed in four subjects. In the evaluation of the potential root cause of microemboli, it is understood that ocular embolic events are common, are present in routine examinations, and are associated with carotid artery plaque and stenoses, hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, smoking, raised body mass index, and diabetes, all conditions and risk factors prevalent in the current study population.32 The general population aged ≥49 years has presented similar events that were classified as asymptomatic retinal microemboli.33 The emboli are visible during routine ophthalmic examination, consist of cholesterol, fibrin platelet, or calcific material,34 35 and are considered incidental findings.

However, there was a subtle distinction between the common findings described above and the procedure-related microemboli events. The affected arteries were 30–80 µm in diameter, and there was no visible embolic material of any kind present in any of the subjects with procedure-related events. Additionally, it has been known that microbubbles may be introduced into the vasculature during endovascular procedures. Due to an increase in the conduct of these interventional procedures, these events have become more common.36 However, the micron level image resolution of the retinal optical coherence tomography scans provided has not been available for routine use anywhere else in the body to observe potential downstream effects of microbubbles. It has provided a unique level of visibility and analysis. For these reasons, they must be considered as a potential etiology and, depending on size, could have been resorbed within perhaps 30 s or up to a few minutes.37

The suspected reperfusion event presented differently from that of the others. Dot hemorrhages were visible well outside the superior arcade of one subject, far removed from the visual axis, and were observed to gradually resolve over the course of approximately 1 month. Although the exact etiology of any ocular procedure-related event cannot be known with certainty, it should be noted that there were no signs of visible material or emboli present in the retina that could definitively explain them (eg, no visible plaque) and all were located outside the visual axis, thus asymptomatic, graded as mild, and resolved without treatment or sequelae.

Conclusions

While there is experience in accessing the OA ostium for super-selective chemotherapy,38 navigating beyond the ostium in aged arteries can present a challenge. The complexity of the OA is unique, requiring experienced interventionalists and the use of instruments designed to achieve procedural success.

The question of procedural durability must be acknowledged; however, in more contemporary practice of intracranial angioplasty alone,29 events of restenosis are low using like treatment strategies or when accompanied by medical management. Finally, since residual stenosis rates are a major factor affecting restenosis,39 lessons learned and additional focus on morphology-based treatment strategies should improve on the results reported here.

It must also be considered that AMD as a form of vascular disease represents a profound paradigm shift in the world of ophthalmology, even if more easily accepted as a reasonable and logical approach in the interventional setting.
