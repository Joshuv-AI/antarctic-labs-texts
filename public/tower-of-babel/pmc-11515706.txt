The effect of coaching on health information literacy in patients with chronic kidney disease: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Yimin Hu, Bin Zhang, Zhihao Hu, Jingwen Huang, Ling Wang, Yuting Wei, Baojia Zheng, Qing Xue
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSince chronic kidney disease is highly insidious in the early stages, most diagnosed patients have already developed irreversible renal failure. There is a lack of effective implementation and standardization of health education management for patients with chronic kidney disease, and the impact of health education management on health information literacy is not yet known.MethodsThis randomized controlled clinical trial was conducted from March 2022 to March 2023. A total of 130 patients with chronic kidney disease stage 3–4 were selected from a tertiary hospital in Zhuhai via a convenience sampling method and were then randomly assigned to intervention and control groups. The Health Information Literacy Questionnaire for Chronic Kidney Disease was used to assess the health literacy of the patients at month 0 and month 12. In addition, the renal function-related indices of the patients were measured before and after the intervention. The study adhered to SPIRIT guidelines.ResultsThere was no significant difference between the two groups in health information literacy, platelet count, estimated glomerular filtration rate, or albumin level. However, the intervention group showed a significant improvement in health behavioral status such as information acquisition, checkups, and physical activity. Subgroup analysis revealed that hemoglobin was significantly higher in non-employed patients than in the intervention group. There was a significant improvement in hemoglobin level after receiving health guidance in patients with intermediate education, and acquisition capacity and estimated glomerular filtration rate in the intervention group were lower than in the control group for patients with elementary education.ConclusionReceiving health coaching helped to improve self-management behaviors and hemoglobin levels of patients with stage 3–4 chronic kidney disease. Education level is an important factor influencing the effectiveness of health coaching techniques.Trial registrationThis study was prospectively registered in the China Clinical Trial Registry on November 10, 2021, with registration number: ChiCTR2100053103.Keyword: Health coaching, Health information literacy, Chronic kidney disease

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD), which affects between 10 and 13% of the population, is secondary to definitive changes in renal function and/or structure. Progression of CKD is slow but irreversible, and it is usually detected only in the advanced stages of the disease, which are accompanied by renal insufficiency. The disease carries a high risk of complications and death [1, 2]. Interventions for CKD are important as it has significant economic impacts on patients, public health, and medical insurance expenditure. Even in the early stages of CKD that do not require renal replacement therapy, the total health expenditure of CKD is often higher than the costs associated with heart failure or stroke [3]. According to the Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) guidelines, CKD is divided into five stages depending on estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR): ≥ 90 mL/min/1.73 m2 (stage 1), 60–89 mL/min/1.73 m2 (stage 2), 30–59 mL/min/1.73 m2 (stage 3), 15–29 mL/min/1.73 m2 (stage 4), and < 15 mL/min/1.73 m2 (stage 5) [4, 5].

The early stages of CKD are highly latent, with a high number of cases progressing to irreversible renal failure by the time they are diagnosed [6]. Current guidelines emphasize the importance of early detection and multidisciplinary methods for CKD management [7]. In recent years, the concept of a “peridialysis period” has been proposed, which begins when eGFR is < 15 mL/min (the beginning of CKD stage 5) and ends 3 months post-dialysis [8]. Patients in the peridialysis period also have higher complications and mortality rates compared to patients in the stable phase of dialysis who have relevant health literacy and psychological reserves [9]. People who undergo hemodialysis generally undertake 2–3 treatments per week, lasting about 3–4 h each; patients therefore spend many hours per week in contact with nurses, building a therapeutic relationship. The relationship acts as a mediator in achieving health goals, increasing engagement in health education, and supporting dialysis through mutual understanding and respect [10]. In recent years, health management of CKD has been researched in terms of reducing the risk of cardiovascular disease, management of hypertension and diabetes mellitus, medication dosage adjustment, and dietary management. However, there are few prospective clinical studies on whether health management of stage 3–4 CKD patients can improve their health information literacy (HIL), whether it can delay the progression of CKD, and whether it can help smooth the transition of the peridialysis period [11]. HIL is a concept that has become known to the broader public in recent years. Although the definitions of HIL and health literacy seem to overlap, as an independent subconcept HIL still shows a unique influence on improvements in people’s health and health education. Evidence indicates that online health information can effectively enrich people’s knowledge and encourage patients to actively engage in the medical process [12]. With respect to HIL, we consider information to be one of the main commonalities between definitions of health literacy. However, information literacy research, which investigates how people become informed within a specific setting, has been almost completely sidelined from health literacy scholarship. This oversight risks limiting the understanding of how health literacy is practiced as well as narrowing research discourses [13]. In addition to associations with mortality, hospitalization, and clinical events, there are consistent associations between health literacy and favorable self-management behaviors, which could support the development of patient education aimed at improving health literacy [14]. Therefore, this study introduces an assessment of HIL to understand the impact of health management on patients with stage 3–4 CKD.

Health coaching is an important means of health management and has been used for patients with a variety of chronic diseases including diabetes, obesity, and hypertension. Most results indicate that these are effective in controlling weight, blood glucose, or blood pressure, as well as changing health behaviors [15]. Olsen et al. [16] defined the health coaching technique as “a goal-oriented, patient-centered, health-focused, ongoing collaborative process that values patient inspiration and empowerment.” The health coaching technique consists of eight steps: participation, observation, reinforcement, clarification, assistance, encouragement, education, and guidance. The technique is a health-based educational strategy [17] that emphasizes motivating subjects to change their health behaviors and encourages “patient-centered goal setting and solution finding” [18]. HIL is an important concept that has become better known in recent years. HIL is the ability of people to recognize the need for health information, to know how and where to find it, and to know how to evaluate and use it to make good health decisions [12]. However, health education techniques are still little practiced in CKD and there is no literature available to elucidate their impact on disease prognosis. Therefore, this study adopted specific assessment tools [19] to investigate health coaching technology, by assessing changes in HIL and clinical indicators related to renal function in stage 3–4 patients.

Methods

Trial registration

The protocol version number was V6.0, dated June 4, 2024; the recruitment period commenced in November 2021 and concluded in March 2022. A total of 130 patients were enrolled, with 114 cases completing the observation phase and 16 cases withdrawing prematurely. Notably, no serious adverse events were reported. This study was registered with the China Clinical Trial Registry (registration number: ChiCTR2100053103) on November 10, 2021, and followed the SPIRIT guidelines. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of a tertiary hospital (Ethics No. Lun Zi No. K56-2), and all participants provided written informed consent.

Trial design

This study was a single-center prospective randomized parallel-controlled clinical trial aimed at improving the knowledge, attitudes, beliefs, and behaviors of patients with CKD stage 3–4. Health coaching techniques were used to help patients improve their self-management skills. Participants who received the health coaching technique were randomly assigned to the intervention and control groups in a 1:1 allocation ratio. Health behavior assessments were completed at 0 and 12 months during the study. Fig. 1 shows the study design.

The study design

Participants

Participants were recruited from one of the tertiary hospitals in Zhuhai, China, and followed for 12 months from March 2022 to March 2023 (questionnaires were originally planned to be carried out at months 0, 3, 6, and 12, but were changed to months 0 and 12 due to the COVID-19 pandemic. Meanwhile, the actual lost to follow-up rate was 12%, higher than expected due to the COVID-19 pandemic). Inclusion criteria included (1) aged 18 years or older; (2) voluntary participation in this study and signing an informed consent form; (3) being diagnosed by a nephrologist as having stage 3–4 CKD and meeting the criteria of the K/DOQI guidelines developed by the American Kidney Disease Foundation [11]; and (4) being cognitively competent enough to meet the criteria and having a positive need for access to healthcare information, but having a poor ability to use that information. Exclusion criteria included (1) patients with acute respiratory distress syndrome, lung function grade 4 and heart function grade 4, or severe impairment of vital organs such as the heart and lung; (2) patients currently suffering from mental disorders, cognitive disorders, senile dementia, or other diseases affecting cognition, or those unable to cooperate for other reasons, such as communication disorders, activity disorders, inability to take care of themselves, and serious attention or hearing impairment; and (3) patients unable to sign informed consent. Reasons for discontinuation included (1) the participant asked to withdraw from the study; (2) the participant was lost to follow-up; (3) the participant refused to return to the interview; and (4) the researcher classed the participant as not suitable to continue to participate for other reasons, including accidents not related to research, or death.

Interventions

In addition to routine care, participants in the intervention group underwent a 12-month health coaching technology intervention. The program included courses according to the health literacy of the patients identified during the questionnaire conducted at baseline, mini-lectures recorded at the Tencent Meeting (controlled at 5–10 min each time; planned offline lectures were canceled due to the coronavirus pandemic), WeChat sharing salons, exercise check-in, and personalized dietary prescriptions. Subjects in the conventional treatment group received general education based on hospital routines, such as publicity materials and telephone follow-up.

Exercise program

In the health coaching program, participants learned how to practice more favorable health-related knowledge (see Table 1) and apply the “Tencent exercise time” app for daily exercise check-in in WeChat, which allows health coaches and peers to supervise participants. Encouragement was given for better completion.

Personalized dietary prescription

The health coach provided a personalized dietary prescription, including recommendations, precautions, and recommended recipes, every 4–6 weeks. They entered the patient’s age, gender, height, weight, creatinine levels, and other relevant indicators into a CKD diet prescription tool developed by Fresenius Kabi AG (Bad Homburg, Germany). Additionally, the health coach set implementation goals related to risk factors within the WeChat group for participants.

Health coaching

The research team helped patients select articles to read once a week, such as those from the official WeChat account of a tertiary hospital’s health management center and the Health Star App, which was used to disseminate scientific knowledge related to the target information during the initial stage of the intervention. The frequency was changed to once every 2 weeks after 2 months and every 4 weeks after 6 months. Health coaching sessions lasted 5–10 min each time (see Table 1). Patients were reminded to adjust their goals when they experienced states such as negativity and self-denial. In the intervention group, communication and motivational techniques were used to empower patients to implement their health plans during routine clinic reviews.

Randomization and blinding

This study included outpatients and inpatients who met the inclusion criteria at the Fifth Hospital of Sun Yat-Sen University from January 2021 to October 2021, as well as 130 patients from Zhuhai Community Hospital. Block group randomization was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics ver. 21 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA) to generate a table of random numbers. This table, with randomly arranged block sizes of 2, 4, and 6, was stratified by age, literacy, and work status. It was used to allocate participants to intervention and control groups in a 1:1 ratio, resulting in 65 cases per group. Allocation concealment was ensured through automated assignment using the Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) randomization module. An independent researcher set up the REDCap randomization module, and another independent researcher produced the randomized numerical sequence table. Two research staff members, Ling Wang and Yimin Hu, were responsible for recruiting eligible participants and completing group assignments. It was not possible to blind participants and physicians administering the intervention to group allocation due to the nature of the intervention. The trial adhered to established procedures to maintain separation between staff conducting outcome measurements and those delivering the intervention. Staff members responsible for outcome measurements were not informed of the details of health coaching.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes were the ability of patients with CKD stage 3–4 to correctly obtain, understand, and apply health information and services, as well as improvement in patients’ self-management ability (e.g., adherence to medication, improvement in unhealthy lifestyles and dietary habits, and reduction of risk factors). The CKD Health Information Literacy Questionnaire used in this study contains 6 dimensions and 24 items. The overall Cronbach’s A coefficient was 0.922, and the Cronbach’s A coefficient of each dimension varied between 0.722 and 0.939 [20]. Therefore, the questionnaire had good structural validity. A Likert 5-point scoring method was adopted and some entries were scored in reverse. The total score was 120, with 96 indicating excellence and 72 indicating passing. The higher the total score, the higher the level of CKD HIL. An excellent rate of self-management = (excellent + good)/total number of cases × 100%. The questionnaires were evaluated and collected before and after intervention. The secondary outcomes included improvement in hemoglobin, platelet, albumin, and other renal function-related indices. These were collected before and after 12 months of follow-up by drawing blood at the Fifth Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-Sen University and testing it at the hospital’s Laboratory Department.

Sample size

A total of 130 participants were recruited with a superiority cut-off of 10%. The sample ratio of the experimental group to the control group was 1:1, with a test significance level of α = 0.05, a test power of 1 − β = 90%, and a dropout rate of 10% for both groups. The enrollment period was 1–1.5 years, with even enrollment and a follow-up time of 6 months. A minimal clinically important difference of 0.9 was chosen because it is the most accepted value to help improve self-management in patients with CKD [21].

Statistical analysis

Data entry and analysis were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics ver. 21. The primary and secondary outcomes were analyzed with the intention-to-treat approach. Consistency between the results of the two analyses was required to conclude non-inferiority, and the analyses followed a pre-specified statistical analysis plan published prior to the trial. Data were first tested for normal distribution using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed data were reported as mean (standard deviation) and compared between the two groups using the independent t-test. Categorical data were reported as frequency (percentage) and compared using the chi-square test. Statistical superiority was assessed for all primary and secondary outcomes using a bilateral significance threshold of 0.05. For the primary outcome, the total HIL and six dimensions (Acquisition ability, Evaluation ability, Literacy awareness, Application ability, Integration ability, CKD knowledge reserve) were assessed using the two-sample t-test. In post hoc analysis, we compared both primary and secondary outcomes between the intervention and control arms, stratified by work status, literacy, or age groups, using the two-sample t-test. We used a complete set for analysis in case of missing data.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Of the 359 patients screened, 229 were excluded (23 did not meet the inclusion criteria, 100 declined to participate, and 16 were discharged or could not be reached before screening procedures). A further 90 were excluded for other reasons (53 were unwilling to commit, 27 did not own a smartphone, and 10 were not interested). Of the 130 participants randomly assigned (65 in each group), approximately two-thirds were male (75 [57.69%]) and ≤ 59 years of age (83 [63.85%]). In the intervention group, 2 participants died of COVID-19 and 6 were lost to follow-up. In the observation group, 5 participants returned home for treatment and 3 were lost to follow-up. In each group, 57 (88%) entered the analysis, and the flowchart is shown in Fig. 1. Baseline data (e.g., gender, age, education, work status, residence, health insurance status, hemoglobin, platelet count, eGFR, and albumin) were comparable between the two groups (P ≥ 0.05).

Primary outcomes

In the control group, the total HIL score was 93.754 ± 15.299, and the scores for the six dimensions were as follows: Acquisition ability: 33.035 ± 6.024; Evaluation ability: 14.614 ± 2.821; Literacy awareness: 13.070 ± 2.374; Application ability: 12.474 ± 2.105; Integration ability: 10.772 ± 3.520; and CKD knowledge reserve: 9.789 ± 2.024. In the intervention group, the total HIL score was 92.965 ± 16.505, and the scores for the six dimensions were as follows: Acquisition ability: 32.298 ± 7.041; Evaluation ability: 14.649 ± 3.254; Literacy awareness: 13.140 ± 2.364; Application ability: 12.211 ± 2.684; Integration ability: 11.035 ± 3.168; and CKD knowledge reserve: 9.632 ± 2.358. There were no significant differences between the two groups in the scores for the six dimensions of HIL (see Table 3, Acquisition ability [P = 0.549]; Evaluation ability [P = 0.951]; Literacy awareness [P = 0.875]; Application ability [P = 0.561]; Integration ability [P = 0.676]; CKD knowledge reserve [P = 0.702]). There were no significant difference between the two groups in terms of the HIL scores in both older and younger patients (see Table 6, P > 0.05). In addition, the intervention group had better health behaviors than the control group, such as receiving health care promotion (P = 0.038; 35% of the control group [20/57] and 54% of the intervention group [31/57]), receiving annual health checkups (P = 0.014, 60% of the control group [34/57] and 81% of the intervention group [46/57]), and regular exercise (P = 0.041; 61% of the control group [35/57] and 79% of the intervention group [45/57]; see Table 3). Among patients with elementary school education, the acquired competence score was significantly lower in the intervention group than in the control group (23.000 ± 8.287 vs. 30.300 ± 6.516, P = 0.042; Table 5). Patients who were employed had slightly higher Evaluation ability in the intervention group than in the control group (15.741 ± 3.033 vs. 14.409 ± 2.873, P = 0.124; Table 4). Non-employed patients had slightly lower Evaluation ability in the intervention group than in the control group (13.667 ± 3.177 vs. 14.743 ± 2.822, P = 0.153; Table 4).

Secondary outcomes

The hemoglobin level was 120.268 ± 23.739 g/L in the control group and 127.389 ± 20.203 g/L in the intervention group; the platelet count was (214.839 ± 70.721) × 109/L in the control group and (225.944 ± 68.669) × 109/L in the intervention group; the eGFR was 37.908 ± 18.426 mL/min/1.73 m2 in the control group and 36.732 ± 15.963 mL/min/1.73 m2 in the intervention group; the albumin level was 40.152 ± 4.595 g/L in the control group and 39.917 ± 4.851 g/L in the intervention group. There were no significant differences in hemoglobin level, platelet count, eGFR, and albumin level between the two groups (P > 0.05, Table 3). There were no significant differences between the two groups in terms of renal function-related indices in either elderly or young patients (P > 0.05, Table 6). Among non-employed patients, the hemoglobin level of the intervention group was significantly higher than that of the experimental group (124.222 ± 16.944 g/L vs. 111.917 ± 21.250 g/L, P = 0.016; Table 4). Among patients with elementary education level, the eGFR in the intervention group was significantly lower than that in the control group (27.812 ± 12.548 mL/min/1.73 m2 vs. 45.715 ± 6.677 mL/min/1.73 m2, P = 0.001; Table 5). Among the patients with intermediate education level, the hemoglobin level of the intervention group was significantly higher than that of the experimental group (130.000 ± 18.053 g/L vs. 111.625 ± 23.492 g/L, P = 0.001; Table 5).

Discussion

CKD has a profound negative impact on human health, and health coaching is an important tool in the health management of patients with CKD. In this study, we investigated a health coaching technique by assessing the changes in clinical indicators related to HIL and renal function in stage 3–4 patients. It is currently believed that patients with CKD are characterized by lower levels of physical activity and exercise behavior [22], and the degree of lack of exercise is high at all stages of CKD [22]. Dietary habits also have a significant impact on the incidence and progress of CKD [3]. This is associated with increased mortality and adverse clinical events including decreased renal function, increased risk of needing renal replacement therapy, and poorer graft renal survival [23]. Health behavior intervention can directly improve renal function indicators in patients with CKD, and numerous studies have described the benefits of exercise in hemodialysis patients [24]. The effect of exercise on eGFR is better than a placebo of no exercise intervention [22]. In this study, after 1 year of intervention measures for stage 3–4 CKD patients, we found a significant improvement in the intervention group’s health behavioral status, such as access to health information, health checkups, and physical activity (P ≤ 0.05). Therefore, we believe that applying health coaching technology to manage the health of patients with stage 3–4 CKD can effectively improve their HIL. This strengthens patients’ self-management behaviors, which encourages them to acquire more knowledge about the disease, quickly identify health information, and apply the health knowledge and skills they have learned to strengthen their dietary control, physical exercise, and psychological adjustment [25].

We found that hemoglobin in the intervention group increased compared to the baseline level, whereas in the control group it decreased (Tables 2, 3). In subgroup analyses, the hemoglobin level was higher in the intervention group than in the control group among patients with secondary education and non-employed patients (Tables 4, 5) as well as among young and middle-aged patients (Table 6). The prevalence of anemia is approximately 8.4% in the early stages of CKD and rises to 50.3% in CKD stage 4 [26]. Previous large clinical trials have shown that low hemoglobin levels are significantly correlated with adverse events (cardiovascular disease, infections, hospitalizations, and mortality) in patients with CKD [27]. Our early intervention using health coaching techniques improved the anemia status of the patients and therefore improved their health-related quality of life, mainly in the domains of energy/vitality and physical function [28].

Demographic and clinical characteristics of participants at baseline, by group

It has been shown that advanced age, non-employment, and low education level are influential factors for HIL [19]. Therefore, we analyzed these factors in subgroups and found that in patients with elementary school education, the HIL scores were lower in the intervention group than in the control group, especially significant decreases in the ability to acquire knowledge and in eGFR. In contrast, patients with university education had improved HIL scores. The reason might be that low literacy patients had insufficient ability to acquire knowledge from the Internet. Due to the lack of face-to-face communication, they were confused or even misunderstood our interventions and the health information knowledge provided. A previous study found that information consumers require at least a tenth-grade reading level to process online materials [29]. Some studies have shown that patients’ evaluation of the quality of health information on the Internet relies mainly on their own judgment rather than on other factors, because they do not have other channels of health information and seldom consult professionals, and they are more concerned with the richness and comprehensibility of the content of the website than with the authenticity and reliability of the content [30]. As a result, people with low educational attainment have decreased access to correct health information, which leads to a decrease in eGFR, an indicator related to kidney function, after the intervention. The implication is that health education guidance must be individualized for low-education patients, including helping patients screen for high-quality, relevant health information and discern the correctness of the information. This suggests that our “spoon-feeding” health education accustoms patients to passively accepting knowledge, and reminds us that we should play the role of peers and family members in the health education of patients of low education level. This improves their ability to process Internet-based information and apply their knowledge and skills to adjust their own lifestyles, to control and slow down the progression of the disease.

This study used the Internet to help patients access information to build a seamless home-community-outpatient health education and management. This helps them to sustainably change poor lifestyle behaviors, thereby reducing healthcare costs. There are some limitations in this study. First, due to the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic in 2022, patients coming to the hospital for review must go through a pre-screening triage, which may create barriers and affect their motivation, in turn affecting the results. The pandemic also led to cancelation of the health coaching team’s offline interventions, so we relied on online interventions only, which may also have a certain impact on the results. Secondly, following the lifting of the strict control measures for COVID-19 in December 2022, the majority of patients were infected with the COVID-19. This posed an even greater challenge to patients with concomitant CKD and might have affected the impact of the intervention on renal function-related indicators.

Conclusion

Providing a health guidance intervention to patients with stage 3–4 CKD helps to improve their self-management behavior and hemoglobin level. The hemoglobin improvement effect is especially obvious for patients with medium literacy. We also found that literacy is an important factor influencing the effectiveness of health guidance technology, which may negatively affect patients with low literacy. Future clinical work and research should promote individualized health guidance and improve the HIL of different populations, to provide a basis for clinical medical staff to develop new strategies for health management, enhancing work efficiency and quality and thus improving renal function indicators.
