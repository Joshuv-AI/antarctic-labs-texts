Advancements in the treatment of osteochondral lesions of the talus
Authors: Lianjie Cheng, Xuena Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractOsteochondral lesions of the talus (OLT) are common ankle joint pathologies, often caused by traumatic or non-traumatic factors. Due to the anatomical characteristics and limited blood supply of the talus, the spontaneous healing capacity of OLT is poor, posing challenges for clinical treatment. Traditional treatments include conservative therapy and surgical interventions, but their efficacy is limited. In recent years, significant advancements in OLT treatment have been achieved with developments in biomaterials science, cell biology, and tissue engineering. This article summarizes the latest research progress in various treatment methods, including conservative treatment, bone marrow stimulation, chondrocyte transplantation, and osteochondral grafting, and evaluates the role of biological augmentation agents such as platelet-rich plasma (PRP) and concentrated bone marrow aspirate (CBMA) in promoting cartilage repair. Additionally, the application of biological scaffold technology offers new prospects for cartilage regeneration. Although emerging therapies show potential in clinical practice, further research is needed to evaluate their long-term efficacy, indications, and safety. This article aims to provide valuable references for clinicians, researchers, and policymakers, promoting the development and refinement of OLT treatment strategies.Keywords: Osteochondral lesions, Bone marrow stimulation, Chondrocyte transplantation, Osteochondral grafting, Biological augmentation agents

Introduction

Osteochondral lesions of the talus (OLT) refer to localized damage to the articular cartilage of the talus and the underlying subchondral bone, including cartilage fissures, subchondral bone plate fractures, bone marrow edema, and subchondral cysts [1]. This type of injury holds significant importance among ankle joint pathologies and is commonly seen after ankle sprains, dislocations, or fractures [2]. Some non-traumatic factors, including spontaneous necrosis, generalized ligamentous laxity, systemic vascular diseases, metabolic disorders, and embolic diseases, can also lead to osteochondral lesions of the talus [3]. Due to the anatomical characteristics of the talus and its limited blood supply, OLT has a poor capacity for spontaneous healing, posing challenges for clinical treatment [4–6].

The ankle joint is one of the most weight-bearing and frequently active joints in the human body, responsible for functions such as walking, running, and jumping. Among various sports injuries, ankle sprains have the highest incidence, accounting for about 15–20% of all sports-related injuries [7]. Approximately 50% of patients with ankle sprains may have varying degrees of osteochondral lesions of the talus [8]. However, due to early symptoms being inconspicuous or similar to soft tissue injuries, OLT is often overlooked or misdiagnosed, leading to disease progression and poor prognosis.

Traditional treatment methods include conservative therapy and surgical intervention. Conservative treatments such as rest, immobilization, and physical therapy are mainly suitable for patients with mild symptoms or lower degrees of injury. However, studies have shown that the success rate of conservative treatment is relatively low, and many patients eventually require surgical intervention [9]. Traditional surgical methods like arthroscopic debridement and microfracture, osteochondral autograft transplantation, and autologous chondrocyte implantation have improved symptoms to some extent but still present issues such as poor cartilage repair quality, high complication rates, and uncertain long-term outcomes [10].

In recent years, significant advances have been made in the treatment of OLT due to developments in biomaterials science, cell biology, and tissue engineering [11]. New technologies involving novel biomaterials, stem cell therapy, bioactive factors, and gene therapy offer new possibilities for improving cartilage repair quality and functional recovery [12]. Additionally, the application of advanced imaging technologies and minimally invasive surgical tools has further enhanced diagnostic accuracy and surgical precision.

Despite the proposal and clinical application of various new treatment methods, research on their efficacy, safety, and indications remains insufficient. There are discrepancies among different research results, and a unified guideline to direct clinical practice is lacking. The rapid development of new technologies also brings challenges in terms of ethics and regulation. Therefore, it is necessary to systematically summarize and evaluate recent research findings to provide reliable evidence for clinicians.

Given the above context, this review aims to: (1) Summarize and evaluate the latest advancements in the treatment of osteochondral lesions of the talus in recent years, including research in areas such as biomaterials, cell therapy, bioactive factors, gene therapy, and advanced physical therapy methods. (2) Compare and analyze the advantages, disadvantages, indications, and clinical efficacy of different treatment strategies, exploring their feasibility and prospects in practical applications. (3) Identify and discuss existing problems and challenges in current research, such as the lack of standardized efficacy evaluation criteria, insufficient long-term follow-up data, and considerations regarding ethics and safety. (4) Look ahead, proposing possible research directions and innovative ideas to promote development in this field and improve patient prognosis. By systematically reviewing and analyzing existing literature, we hope to provide valuable references for clinicians, researchers, and policymakers, thereby promoting further development and refinement in the treatment of osteochondral lesions of the talus.

Classical conservative treatments

Conservative treatment is a non-surgical approach for managing osteochondral lesions of the talus (OLT), aiming to promote healing of the lesion and alleviate symptoms through non-invasive means. It is primarily suitable for situations where the injury severity is mild—specifically Stage I or II lesions according to the Berndt and Harty classification, where the lesion is small and stable [13, 14]. Patients experiencing only slight pain or discomfort without significant functional impairment are ideal candidates.

The methods of conservative treatment include rest and immobilization, physical therapy, medication, lifestyle modifications [9]. Rest and immobilization involve advising patients to avoid weight-bearing activities such as prolonged standing, walking, running, and jumping. Assistive devices like crutches, walkers, casts, or braces can reduce the load on the ankle joint. The duration of immobilization is typically recommended for 6–12 weeks [15], adjusted based on the patient’s symptoms and recovery progress.

Physical therapy plays a crucial role and includes range of motion exercises to prevent joint stiffness by performing passive and active movements of the ankle within pain tolerance [16]. Muscle strengthening exercises help improve joint stability by targeting the muscles of the lower leg and foot. Balance and proprioception training enhance the patient’s perception of joint position, reducing the risk of re-injury [17]. Cryotherapy and thermotherapy can relieve pain and swelling while promoting circulation [18]. Some studies suggest that modalities like ultrasound and electrical stimulation may aid tissue healing, although evidence is limited [19].

Medication involves the use of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) to reduce pain and inflammation [20]. Analgesics may be used as needed to improve patient comfort.

Lifestyle and activity modifications are also essential. Patients are advised to avoid activities that trigger symptoms, reducing high-impact sports like running, jumping, and competitive athletics. For overweight or obese patients, weight loss can reduce the load on the ankle joint and help alleviate symptoms [21].

Injection therapies have garnered attention as promising treatments for osteochondral lesions of the talus (OLT), as they deliver bioactive substances directly to the site of injury to promote tissue repair and regeneration. Hyaluronic acid has lubricating and anti-inflammatory properties, reducing pain and improving joint function. By enhancing the viscoelasticity of synovial fluid, it decreases joint friction and supports cartilage repair [22]. PRP, a concentrated platelet suspension derived from the patient’s blood, is rich in growth factors such as transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF). These factors stimulate chondrocyte proliferation and extracellular matrix synthesis, enhancing the healing capacity of cartilage lesions [23]. ACS is a serum enriched with anti-inflammatory cytokines, including interleukin-1 receptor antagonist (IL-1Ra), produced through ex vivo culture. It acts by inhibiting inflammation, protecting chondrocytes, and facilitating tissue repair [24].

The efficacy of conservative treatment varies. Success rates generally hover around 45–50%, with some patients achieving pain relief and functional improvement [2]. For Stage I or II lesions, conservative treatment may promote repair of subchondral bone and resolution of bone marrow edema. However, some patients may experience a return of symptoms after resuming daily activities or sports. If symptoms do not improve after 3–6 months, or imaging shows lesion progression, surgical intervention should be considered.

In conclusion, conservative treatment serves as an initial approach for managing osteochondral lesions of the talus, suitable for patients with mild symptoms and lower degrees of injury. Individualized treatment plans should be developed under medical guidance, with close monitoring of the condition. If conservative treatment proves ineffective or symptoms worsen, timely surgical intervention is advisable to avoid delaying optimal treatment and affecting prognosis.

Bone marrow stimulation (BMS)

BMS (Bone Marrow Stimulation) is a surgical intervention widely used to treat cartilage injuries in the ankle and knee joints. This includes techniques such as microfracture and retrograde drilling. Among these, microfracture is the most commonly used, while retrograde drilling is typically employed for subchondral bone lesions where the surface cartilage remains intact [25]. The microfracture technique is usually performed arthroscopically. It begins by thoroughly debriding the damaged cartilage tissue. Then, the microfracture technique is applied to penetrate the subchondral bone plate, stimulating the aggregation of mesenchymal stem cells and growth factors at the site of injury, ultimately inducing the formation of fibrocartilage tissue.

In the 2017 recommendations from the German Society for Orthopedics and Trauma Surgery, BMS is recommended for treating lesions smaller than 1.5 cm² in area and less than 5 mm in depth [26]. In a systematic review by Ramponi et al. [27], it was found that BMS is more suitable for OLTs (Osteochondral Lesions of the Talus) with an area smaller than 107.4 mm² or a diameter of less than 10.2 mm. The consensus of the International Ankle Cartilage Repair Conference suggests that BMS is not suitable for patients with cartilage damage greater than 100 mm² or a diameter greater than 10 mm [28]. While short- to mid-term follow-up reports show good results for BMS in smaller lesions, the long-term degeneration of the repaired fibrocartilage tissue and the recurrence of symptoms remain a concern. In the clinical study by Cheng et al. [29], it was found that for cystic OLTs, the size of the cyst is an important factor influencing the efficacy of microfracture treatment.

The hyaline cartilage of the ankle joint is mainly composed of type II collagen and proteoglycans. Cartilage tissue generated by BMS shows histological similarities to hyaline cartilage in the first 6 weeks postoperatively. However, subsequent biological changes occur, including the differentiation of type II collagen into type I collagen, reduced expression of proteoglycans, and tissue fibrosis, ultimately producing a cartilage-like material. This cartilage-like substance has poor elasticity and endurance, making it more prone to degeneration under shear stress. Although many subjective improvements in symptoms have been reported, numerous studies have found that the fibrocartilage generated after repair gradually degenerates over time. In a systematic review by Jelmed et al. [30], it was found that cartilage scores observed during second-look arthroscopy after BMS were significantly lower than those observed after cartilage transplantation or retrograde drilling, though the correlation between cartilage scores and clinical outcomes requires further study.

Subchondral bone plays a vital role as a supporting structure in the ankle joint, bearing 30% of the load, compared to only 1-3% absorbed by the joint cartilage. Moreover, the specific connection between subchondral bone and articular cartilage facilitates the formation of multiple signaling pathways. Therefore, the incompleteness of the subchondral bone after BMS can lead to some adverse effects. Orth et al. [31] treated sheep with BMS and found a reduction in bone volume and trabecular thickness, as well as an increase in subchondral cysts and osteophytes within the lesion. Additionally, Seow et al. [32] conducted a systematic review and found that BMS resulted in significant histological changes, reducing the structural density of the deep subchondral bone.

In recent years, there have been numerous reports on enhanced BMS techniques. Migliorini et al. [33] treated OLTs patients with BMS using a biological scaffold and found that the efficacy of scaffold-based BMS was superior to simple BMS in a 5-year follow-up comparison. Wen et al. [34] conducted a systematic review comparing simple microfracture with microfracture combined with scaffolds, hyaluronic acid, PRP, bone marrow aspirate concentrate, and bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells, concluding that enhanced microfracture techniques are superior to simple microfracture in the treatment of talar OLTs. Extracellular matrix allografts (ECMA) serve as a scaffold structure that facilitates the infiltration of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells and produces higher-quality cartilage filling to enhance BMS. In an animal experiment by Fortier et al. [35], the combination of PRP and ECMA was used to enhance the effects of microfracture, showing better histological ICRA scores and MRI performance compared to the microfracture-only group. Additionally, Shinozono et al. [36] used ECMA and concentrated bone marrow aspirate (CBMA) to enhance BMS and found, through 20 months of MRI follow-up, that the combined use of bio-cartilage and CBMA was superior to BMS alone. However, longer-term studies are still lacking to demonstrate whether ECMA can provide superior and more durable cartilage repair outcomes.

Chondrocyte transplantation-related techniques

Chondrocyte transplantation techniques include Autologous Chondrocyte Implantation (ACI), Matrix-Induced Autologous Chondrocyte Implantation (MACI), and Autologous Matrix-Induced Chondrogenesis (AMIC), among others. These methods are typically used for larger lesions not suitable for Bone Marrow Stimulation (BMS) or for patients who have failed BMS surgery.

ACI was first reported for treating cartilage defects in the knee joint. It mainly involves two stages. The first stage includes obtaining hyaline cartilage from non-weight-bearing areas of the knee joint or talus, then isolating chondrocytes by enzymatically removing the extracellular matrix. The chondrocytes are cultured and expanded in vitro for 11 to 21 days, returning the cells to a dedifferentiated state. In the second stage, the cells are implanted into the cartilage defect area, the defect is sutured with a periosteal flap, and growth factors are provided to promote cartilage formation. Viglione et al. [37] conducted long-term follow-ups on nine patients with osteochondral lesions of the talus (OLTs) treated with ACI. At least 20 years post-operation, their functional and pain scores significantly improved, suggesting that ACI is an effective method for treating OLTs and can bring lasting clinical symptom improvements.

Recent advancements in ACI have focused on improving cell viability and the quality of regenerated cartilage. Innovations in tissue engineering and biomaterials have led to the development of three-dimensional (3D) scaffolds that provide a conducive environment for chondrocyte growth and extracellular matrix production [38]. Additionally, gene editing techniques like CRISPR/Cas9 are being explored to enhance chondrocyte function and resistance to inflammation [39].

MACI involves embedding cultured autologous chondrocytes into type I/III collagen, hyaluronic acid, or polyglycolic acid scaffolds and fixing them with fibrin glue. Chondrocyte implantation can be performed arthroscopically, avoiding the complications of periosteal harvesting and suturing. Compared to ACI, MACI technology ensures that more viable chondrocytes are implanted into the defect area. Patients receiving MACI treatment have clinical scores comparable to those of ACI; most patients showed significant improvement in AOFAS scores during long-term follow-up [38]. Although the clinical results of MACI are generally good, there are still limitations, such as the need for two surgeries and higher costs [40].

Innovations in MACI have included the use of nanotechnology to create nanofiber scaffolds, which enhance cell adhesion, proliferation, and differentiation [41]. Smart biomaterials that are biodegradable and have controlled release capabilities are being developed to deliver growth factors or anti-inflammatory agents directly to the implantation site, promoting better cartilage regeneration [42]. Furthermore, the combination of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) with chondrocytes has shown promise in enhancing extracellular matrix production and improving the mechanical properties of the regenerated tissue [43].

AMIC is a single-stage cartilage repair technique and is considered a further improvement of autologous chondrocyte transplantation technology [44, 45]. It can also be classified as an enhanced BMS technique [46], showing good application prospects. The process includes using exogenous scaffolds, such as type I/III collagen bilayers, during microfracture surgery. This exogenous scaffold forms a cellular matrix that helps stabilize the blood clot, allowing the mesenchymal stem cells induced by BMS to be covered and stabilized, while also protecting the treated area from the external environment and shear forces [47]. Additionally, biological enhancers like collagen-binding matrix augmentation (CBMA) or platelet-rich plasma (PRP) incorporated into the biological scaffold can further promote hyaline cartilage formation.

AMIC has seen advancements through the incorporation of biological agents like exosomes and microvesicles, which are rich in bioactive molecules that promote chondrocyte proliferation and differentiation [48]. The use of PRP within the scaffold has been shown to enhance hyaline cartilage formation by providing a concentrated source of growth factors [49]. Moreover, the application of three-dimensional printing technology allows for the customization of scaffolds that fit the defect site precisely, improving the integration and effectiveness of the repair [50]. When directly comparing the efficacy of ACI, MACI, and AMIC, the three groups have similar clinical results in short-term follow-ups [51], and a continuous, complete cartilage layer was found to fill the cartilage defect area during the second arthroscopic examination. AMIC is considered a good alternative to ACI and MACI because this technique requires only a single procedure, is more cost-effective, and avoids complications associated with transplantation and chondrocyte harvesting [52, 53]. However, there is still a lack of longer-term follow-up studies and prospective research.

Recent high-quality randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have provided stronger evidence supporting the efficacy and safety of chondrocyte transplantation techniques [54]. Long-term follow-up studies extending beyond five years have demonstrated sustained improvements in symptoms and joint function [55]. Additionally, minimally invasive surgical techniques and precise implantation methods have been developed to reduce tissue damage and enhance patient recovery [56].

Osteochondral grafting techniques

Osteochondral transplantation techniques are suitable for larger lesions (area > 100 mm² or diameter > 10 mm), cystic lesions, damage involving the talar shoulder, and cases of failed initial surgeries [27]. Osteochondral transplantation techniques include.

autologous osteochondral grafting (AOT), allogenic osteochondral grafting (AOG), mosaicplasty.

Autologous osteochondral transplantation (AOT) is a classic surgical approach for treating osteochondral lesions of the talus, particularly suited for small to medium-sized cartilage defects. The AOT technique involves harvesting healthy osteochondral plugs from the patient’s non-weight-bearing area (such as the lateral femoral condyle of the knee) and transplanting them into the damaged area of the talar cartilage to restore cartilage integrity [57]. While it is effective in many cases, it is associated with certain complications, such as development of donor-site pain or degeneration over time, graft mismatch or fracture or damage to the graft. In a cohort study of 72 patients, Kennedy et al. [58] reported a significant improvement in FAOS scores at 28 months following AOT, with MRI at 1-year post-surgery showing that the transplanted cartilage exhibited normal cartilage structure. For athletes with larger lesions, Nguyen et al. [59] reported a significant improvement in Visual Analog Scale (VAS) scores at 45 months post-AOT, with 87% of patients returning to their previous level of sports activity.

Due to the potential postoperative complications, such as pain in the donor areas of femoral condyle cartilage and talar cartilage, and the limited availability of cartilage donors, there has been an increasing number of clinical reports in recent years on autologous bone-periosteum complex transplantation. These include periosteum-covered iliac bone transplantation [60–62] and periosteum-covered tibial bone transplantation. With easy graft harvesting, fewer donor site complications, sufficient donor supply, and the strong differentiation ability of MSCs within the periosteum, this technique is suitable for larger cartilage lesions and large bone cysts [63, 64].

For larger osteochondral lesions or cases involving cystic degeneration, allogenic osteochondral grafting serves as an effective alternative. The primary advantage of allogenic grafting is that it provides larger osteochondral blocks without causing damage to the donor site in the patient. Studies [65] have shown that allograft osteochondral transplantation has a good repair effect for patients with large cartilage defects, particularly demonstrating significant improvements in clinical function and imaging evaluations.

Particulated juvenile allograft cartilage (PJAC), first reported in 2007, is a promising allogeneic cartilage transplantation technique. PJAC is derived from donors younger than 13 years old, and due to the young donor age, the chondrocytes have higher numbers and stronger activity, with cellular activity estimated to be around 100 times that of adult cartilage. PJAC is a ready-to-use graft, and one package can cover approximately 2.5 cm² of cartilage defect [66–68]. Surgeons can choose to implant PJAC either through open surgery or arthroscopy, with the decision depending on factors such as the location and size of the lesion and the surgeon’s proficiency. Currently, most studies on PJAC for treating osteochondral lesions of the talus (OLTs) involve minimally invasive arthroscopic procedures. Although there are few reports, clinical results, particularly long-term follow-up outcomes, are promising and show significant advantages over traditional methods [69, 70]. However, allograft transplantation still carries potential risks of immune rejection and disease transmission. The long-term quality of cartilage regeneration and the maintenance of function after transplantation are also key areas of research focus.

Biological augmentation agents

Platelet-rich plasma (PRP)

Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) is an autologous blood product that contains a high concentration of growth factors, such as platelet-derived growth factor, transforming growth factor-beta, and insulin-like growth factor [71]. In recent years, PRP has been widely applied in the repair of cartilage injuries. Particularly in the treatment of osteochondral lesions of the talus (OLTs), the biological enhancement effects of PRP have attracted significant attention. Recent studies indicate that PRP helps promote, reduce inflammatory responses, and improve postoperative function in patients [72, 73].

Some studies [74] have validated the potential cartilage-protective effects of PRP when used in combination with BMS or AOT. BMS combined with PRP has been shown to create a favorable biological environment that promotes chondrocyte synthesis and increases type II collagen deposition. In a randomized controlled trial conducted by Gormeli et al. [74], patients with osteochondral lesions of the talus treated with BMS combined with PRP had significantly higher postoperative AOFAS scores and lower VAS scores compared to those treated with BMS combined with hyaluronic acid (HA) or saline injection (control group). Additionally, a randomized controlled study by Guney et al. [75] found that BMS combined with PRP yielded better clinical outcomes than BMS alone. However, Seow et al. [76], in a systematic review, concluded that the evidence supporting the role of PRP in enhancing BMS is limited and requires further investigation. Similarly, there is limited research on the role of PRP in enhancing AOT. In an animal study, Boakye et al. [77] found that TGF-β1 levels were higher in rabbits treated with AOT combined with PRP compared to those not treated with PRP. Additionally, Smyth et al. [78] found that, compared to AOT alone, patients treated with AOT combined with PRP had higher postoperative average ICRS scores in an animal experiment. They suggested that PRP could reduce graft bone degradation and improve cartilage integration in animal models, although the addition of HA did not enhance the efficacy of AOT [79].

In conclusion, a large body of both basic and clinical research has shown that PRP can effectively promote cartilage repair and type II collagen deposition, indicating promising potential for its application.

Concentrated bone marrow aspirate

Concentrated Bone Marrow Aspirate (CBMA), an autologous preparation rich in stem cells and growth factors, has garnered significant attention in recent years for the treatment of cartilage injuries, particularly in osteochondral lesions of the talus (OLTs). CBMA is obtained by extracting bone marrow fluid rich in mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), platelets, and other repair factors from the patient’s bone marrow. The aspirate is then concentrated and injected into the injury site to promote the regeneration of cartilage and bone tissue.

CBMA, as an adjunct to BMS, can facilitate the accumulation of MSCs and various cytokines in the cartilage lesion area, promoting the repair of hyaline cartilage and the formation of type II collagen. Fortier et al. [80] compared the outcomes of microfracture alone versus microfracture combined with CBMA in a horse model and found significant improvements in radiological and histological outcomes in the CBMA group. Hannon et al. [81] compared the clinical efficacy of BMS combined with CBMA versus BMS alone in patients with OLTs and found that the combined treatment group had superior postoperative foot and ankle outcome scores (FAOS), matrix-associated autologous chondrocyte transplantation (MOCART) scores, and radiological outcomes compared to the BMS-alone group. Mercer et al. [82] found that in patients receiving AOT for OLTs, the outcomes of the group treated with CBMA alone were comparable to those of the group treated with both CBMA and ECMA, suggesting that CBMA alone provides sufficient biological enhancement for AOT. Despite the promising potential of CBMA in treating osteochondral lesions of the talus, there are still some limitations. First, the preparation process of CBMA is complex and costly, which may limit its widespread clinical application. Second, the therapeutic effects of CBMA may vary due to individual patient differences, particularly in terms of the quantity and quality of stem cells. Additionally, current research on the long-term efficacy of CBMA is limited, especially in cases of large cartilage defects, where more studies are needed to validate its long-term effectiveness.

Biological scaffold technology

The technology of biological scaffolds has gained widespread attention in recent years for the treatment of osteochondral lesions of the talus (OLTs). These scaffolds provide a three-dimensional structural framework for cartilage and subchondral bone, supporting cell proliferation and differentiation while promoting tissue regeneration. With the advancement of biomaterial science, biological scaffold technology has been used to enhance the cartilage repair process, especially when combined with biological enhancers such as platelet-rich plasma (PRP) and concentrated bone marrow aspirate (CBMA) [83]. Currently, biological scaffolds used for cartilage repair mainly fall into two categories: natural materials and synthetic materials [11].

Natural material scaffolds include collagen, hyaluronic acid, and cartilage matrix. These scaffolds have good biocompatibility and degradability, supporting cell growth and eventually degrading into natural tissue. For instance, research has shown that hyaluronic acid scaffolds can effectively promote chondrocyte colonization and tissue reconstruction [84, 85]. Synthetic material scaffolds include polylactic acid (PLA), polyglycolic acid (PGA), and others. These materials have controllable mechanical strength and degradation rates, which can be adjusted based on the specific nature of the injury to achieve optimal repair outcomes [86]. Recent studies have found that the combined application of synthetic scaffolds with stem cells can significantly enhance cartilage regeneration [87].

The combined application of biological scaffolds with platelet-rich plasma (PRP) or concentrated bone marrow aspirate (CBMA) has become a major focus of recent research. PRP and CBMA are rich in growth factors and mesenchymal stem cells, providing additional biological support to promote the regeneration of cartilage and subchondral bone. Rodeo et al. found that the combination of PRP with biological scaffolds significantly improved cartilage tissue regeneration quality and patient functional scores in the treatment of osteochondral lesions of the talus [88]. An animal experimental study demonstrated that the use of CBMA in conjunction with a hyaluronic acid scaffold significantly promoted cartilage tissue repair and improved the regeneration of subchondral bone [89].

Despite the promising application of biological scaffolds in the treatment of osteochondral lesions of the talus, several challenges remain. Different materials used in biological scaffolds show varied performance in cartilage repair. While natural scaffolds offer good biocompatibility, they may lack sufficient mechanical strength. On the other hand, synthetic scaffolds, though stronger, require further optimization in terms of biocompatibility and degradation characteristics. Successful integration of biological scaffolds with the patient’s host tissue is crucial, but this process can be influenced by various factors, such as the scaffold’s material properties, degradation rate, and individual patient differences. Research suggests that the combination of appropriate scaffold design with biological enhancers can effectively address these issues.

Summary

Due to the limited self-regenerative capacity of articular cartilage, the treatment of osteochondral lesions of the talus (OLTs) remains challenging. Currently, surgical strategies are largely determined based on the size of the cartilage lesion, and this approach has not changed significantly. Smaller lesions (with an area < 100 mm² or diameter < 10 mm) are typically managed with minimally invasive procedures, such as arthroscopic bone marrow stimulation, chondrocyte transplantation combined with biological scaffolds, or bio-enhancers. For treating smaller lesions under arthroscopy, microfracture surgery is still the preferred clinical option. In addition, various bio-enhancing agents are being applied to the lesion area, with PRP being the most commonly used. In recent years, there has been significant development in chondrocyte transplantation technology and biological scaffolds, with ongoing innovations in these technologies. Since the cartilage generated through these methods more closely resembles the structure of hyaline cartilage, they represent potential alternatives to bone marrow stimulation. However, at present, they are not the first-line treatment options. For larger lesions (with an area > 100 mm² or diameter > 10 mm), cystic lesions, or those involving the shoulder of the talus or failed previous surgeries, cartilage transplantation techniques are recommended. Currently, osteochondral autograft transplantation is the preferred method. However, due to the advantages in harvesting techniques, bone-periosteum transplantation is gaining increasing clinical use and has become another important alternative. PJAC (Partially-Jointed Articular Cartilage) and other allogeneic cartilage materials are less commonly used in China, but related reports suggest promising application potential, making them a more prominent option in clinical practice.
