A cross-sectional study on the comparison of psychological impact among medical staff before and after COVID-19
Authors: Qingdai Li, Mina Wang, Lin Liang, Jie Sun, Ningan Xiao, Jianhao Liu, Xin Du, Jing Wang, Yutong Ni, Yuanbo Fu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe COVID-19 pandemic has had a profound impact on the mental health of medical workers worldwide. While extensive research has investigated pandemic-related mental health challenges, longitudinal analyses of temporal trends remain scarce. This study employs a cross-sectional design to compare mental health outcomes among medical workers in Beijing’s Mobile Cabin Hospitals during the Early-Pandemic Era (2020) and Post-Pandemic Era (2022), with a focus on occupational disparities. MethodsThis study utilized a cross-sectional design to compare mental health outcomes among medical workers in Beijing’s Mobile Cabin Hospitals during two distinct pandemic phases. Data were collected through anonymized online surveys administered via Wenjuanxing. The questionnaire comprised three domains: demographic characteristics, professional roles within departments and the 12-item General Health Questionnaire (GHQ-12). Emotional distress was operationally defined as a GHQ-12 total score ≥ 4. ResultsComparative analysis revealed a significant deterioration in mental health outcomes among medical workers during the Post-Pandemic Era compared to the Early-Pandemic Era. The prevalence of emotional distress (GHQ-12 ≥ 4) remained elevated across specific subgroups: medical workers aged 30–39 years, married, working as doctors and other professionals. Statistically significant interphase differences emerged in vulnerable populations, including female (P < 0.001), unmarried (P = 0.004), worked with nurses (P = 0.003) and other professionals (P = 0.021), and aged less than 40 years (< 30 years old, P = 0.009; 30–39 years old, P = 0.021). Qualitative symptom profiling indicated that more people reported clinically meaningful manifestations of psychological distress, characterized by depressive symptoms, anxiety, diminished self-worth, and impaired coping capacity during adversity. ConclusionThe cumulative burden of prolonged pandemic has demonstrably exacerbated mental health deterioration among medical workers. These findings underscore the critical need to sustainably safeguard the mental health of medical workers in future public health crises.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s44192-025-00210-9.

Introduction

Coronavirus Disease 2019 (COVID-19), caused by the severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), is characterized as an acute respiratory viral illness with multisystem involvement [1]. Primary clinical manifestations include fever, dry cough, dysphagia, and upper respiratory tract inflammation. While most infections follow a self-limiting course, critical illness disproportionately affects geriatric populations, pre-existing chronic conditions individuals, and people with obesity [2]. Since the outbreak of COVID-19 in December 2019, it has resulted in catastrophic global morbidity, with World Health Organization surveillance data (as of August 2024) documenting 775,867,547 confirmed cases and 7,057,145 fatalities across 241 countries/regions [2, 3].

Following the initial outbreak, China has implemented timely, rigorous and effective pandemic prevention and control measures. This adaptive mitigation strategy targeted pandemic prevention and control region-specific strategies, strengthening the collection of patients' epidemiological history, health monitoring and nucleic acid testing of suspicious patients, and realized early detection, early reporting, early isolation and early treatment [4, 5]. Pandemic prevention and control have been routinized.

The COVID-19 pandemic in Beijing exhibited triphasic progression between 2020 and 2022: Initial Outbreak (early 2020): The virus spread rapidly, placing healthcare workers under significant work pressure and psychological burden. Mid-term Control (mid-2020 to 2021): Through stringent containment measures, the pandemic was effectively controlled, although healthcare workers still had to manage sporadic outbreaks. Late-term Resurgence (late 2021 to 2022): The emergence of the Omicron variant led to a resurgence of cases, subjecting healthcare workers to renewed high-intensity work pressure. In order to fight the pandemic, medical workers always rushed to the front line. According to the survey, most of the medical workers believed that the work obligation during the pandemic prevention period was one to two times that of the usual [6], and they had different degrees of psychological stress response [7, 8]. By the end of 2022, due to the strong transmission, infection, reinfection and immune escape ability of the Omicron variant, the pandemic broke out in Beijing, and the number of infected people continued to rise. Medical workers went to the Mobile Cabin Hospital for treatment of patients again. Mobile Cabin Hospitals are temporary medical facilities composed of movable modular units, equipped with functions such as emergency admission, surgical treatment, and clinical testing. Primarily designed to treat patients with mild symptoms, they integrate medical care with isolation measures, serving as a critical strategy in pandemic control. These hospitals provide monitoring and observation for patients, with the capability to promptly transfer those whose conditions worsen to designated hospitals, thereby establishing a tiered medical response system. By minimizing resource investment and site modifications, Mobile Cabin Hospitals achieve rapid capacity expansion, representing a practical and essential emergency response measure. Research suggested that after people experienced a disaster, a combination of psychological and physical symptoms would occur, especially depression and anxiety disorders [9, 10].

This study aims to investigate the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on the mental health of medical workers in Mobile Cabin Hospitals in Beijing. By comparing mental health conditions during the Early- and Post-pandemic Era, it analyzes trends and underlying causes, revealing distinct phases of psychological stress responses. The research provides new reference data for studies on the mental health of healthcare workers and emphasizes the necessity of psychological support. Furthermore, these findings offer insights for mental health interventions in other high-intensity work environments.

Methods

Participants

The questionnaire-based investigation conducted during the Early-Pandemic Era spanned from March 20 to 29, 2020. A cohort of 287 medical workers participated through self-administered questionnaires, with stratified sampling across three occupational categories: doctors, nurses and other personnel (pharmacists/medical imaging/technicians/medical chemists, etc.). And four distinct hospital departments: the confirmation department (dealing with confirmed cases), the screening department (screening cases), the administrative department and the logistics department.

The Post-Pandemic Era survey was conducted at the samehospital from December 9 to 11, 2022, replicating the methodological framework of the baseline investigation with equivalent questionnaire distribution (N = 287). Valid responses were obtained from 266 medical workers, stratified across three occupational categories: doctors, nurses and others (pharmacists/medical imaging technicians/medical chemists, etc.). And three sectors: confirmation unit (handling confirmed cases), screening unit (screening cases) and logistics unit. Questionnaire dissemination occurred through institutional communication channels to ensure equitable access across professional groups and clinical units. The study adhered to ethical research protocols featuring: Voluntary participation with opt-out rights; Anonymized data collection procedures; Full disclosure of research objectives in the questionnaire preamble; Mandatory completion of all survey items to maintain response integrity.

Instrument

This investigation adopted a cross-sectional study design, employing the software Wenjuanxing (Questionnaire Star) for questionnaire development and digital administration. Survey dissemination occurred via institutional digital communication channels at Mobile Cabin Hospitals, with the Wenjuanxing system incorporating IP address validation and device fingerprinting to preclude duplicate entries. Both questionnaires comprised three standardized sections: basic information, current position in the department and a common scale GHQ-12. Basic information included in the 2020 questionnaire such as gender, age and marital status, and the current position included doctor, nurse or other personnel, confirmation department, screening department, logistics department and administrative department. In 2022, a supplementary survey was assessed the number of times medical staff supported Mobile Cabin Hospitals, the time of this support, and whether they had depression and anxiety in the past. To better understand the medical staff and analyze their mental health status.

Two questionnaires incorporated the 12-Item General Health Questionnaire (GHQ-12) to assess psychological distress levels among medical workers. GHQ-12 has been widely used in epidemiological investigations and screening of community mental health [13]. It has good sensitivity, specificity [14, 15], reliability and validity with Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of each dimension ranging from 0.878 to 0.893 (Table 1), which can accurately reflect the mental health status of medical staff [16]. The GHQ-12 comprises 12 items related to mental health (six positive and six negative wording). The alternatives were: none at all, same as usual, a little more than usual, much more than usual. A dichotomous scoring method (0–0-1–1) was applied, yielding total scores ranging from 0 (optimal mental health) to 12 (severe distress), with higher scores indicating poorer mental health status [17]. Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS26.0 software (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) following rigorous analytical protocols. Descriptive statistical analysis and qualitative indicators are described by frequency table and percentage. Quantitative indicators are described by mean and standard deviation. For comparison of the two groups, the homogeneity test of variance was performed first. The independent sample t-test was used for quantitative data consistent with normal distribution of the Shapiro–Wilk test, and the Wilcoxon rank sum test was used for data inconsistent with normal distribution. Comparative analysis between qualitative data groups, using Chi-square test or Fisher's exact test. In order to test the correlation between demographic characteristics and the severity of emotional distress, the entire demographic characteristics was used as the independent variable, while the total level of emotional distress (GHQ-12 score ≥ 4, as a standard interpretation for the presence of emotional distress, it indicates that the individual may have experienced significant emotional distress recently.) as the dependent variable to conduct logistic regression analysis to predict the possibility of emotional distress. The model was constructed using a binary logistic regression model, with independent variables including gender, age, marital status, position, number of times supporting Mobile Cabin Hospitals, duration of support, and history of depression or anxiety, all of which were categorical variables. Multicollinearity was addressed by detecting it through the variance inflation factor (VIF) and correlation matrix (VIF > 10 or correlation coefficient > 0.7 indicating the presence of multicollinearity). When necessary, variables were removed, or methods such as principal component analysis (PCA) or regularization techniques (e.g., ridge regression, LASSO regression) were applied. Model diagnostics included the Hosmer–Lemeshow test to assess goodness-of-fit (P > 0.05 indicating good fit), ROC curves and AUC values to evaluate predictive ability (AUC > 0.7 indicating good discriminative power), and standardized residuals and Deviance residuals to detect outliers (standardized residuals > 2 or < -2 suggesting potential outliers), P < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

Ethics approval

This study was reviewed and approved as exempt by the Medical Ethics Committee of Beijing Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine as a low-risk with no patient intervention (Ethical Statement File). The objective and purpose of the study were verified briefly by the study participants, and the collected data were anonymous and treated as confidential. Also, online consent was received from all the study participants before conducting the survey. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

In the Post-Pandemic Era, cross-sectional survey data revealed that 30.1% of 266 medical practitioners exhibited clinically significant emotional distress. Demographic analysis identified subpopulations with elevated emotional distress prevalence rates: female respondents (30.9%), medical personnel aged 30–39 years (33.7%), and clinicians with pre-existing anxiety/depression diagnoses (70.0%). Occupational risk stratification demonstrated particularly high emotional distress rates among diagnosis department (32.3%) and doctors (32.1%), with the most pronounced manifestation observed in other professionals (41.7%). Marital and parental status analysis showed maximum emotional distress prevalence in married without children (30.0%). Deployment patterns revealed significant dose–response relationships, with emotional distress rates escalating to 60.0% among staff supporting Mobile Cabin Hospitals for more than three times and 32.1% in the support time of more than one month. Comparative analysis revealed significant age-related disparities in emotional distress prevalence, with participants aged 50–59 years demonstrating substantially lower rates (13.3%) compared to those in the 30–39 cohort (33.7%). Mental health outcomes showed pronounced clinical stratification, as individuals without pre-existing anxiety/depression exhibited a 20.8% distress prevalence, contrasting sharply with the 70.0% rate observed in those with prior psychiatric diagnoses. Gender-specific manifestations emerged in psychosomatic symptoms: female practitioners reported significantly higher rates of worry insomnia disturbances (42.0% vs 27.1% in males). Occupational risk stratification identified confirmation department as high-risk environments (42.2% worry insomnia) compared to logistics department (8.3%). Notably, gendered patterns in psychological sequelae were observed, with male practitioners demonstrating higher rates of self-worth deterioration (16.9% vs 5.3% in females). Interprofessional analysis revealed doctors' heightened vulnerability to identity disruption (17.9% prevalence) compared to nurses (4.8%) and other professionals (8.3%).

Longitudinal analysis of Early- and Post-Pandemic demographic data revealed persistent emotional distress patterns among medical workers in specific subgroups. Married practitioners, doctors, other occupations, and individuals aged 30–39 years exhibited sustained emotional distress prevalence elevations across both timepoints, though these differences did not reach statistical significance (all P > 0.05). Significant temporal shifts emerged in mental health trajectories for vulnerable subpopulations (Table 7). Analysion identified pronounced emotional distress exacerbation among female practitioners (P < 0.001), single workers (P = 0.004), nurses (P = 0.003), and personnel aged < 30 years (P = 0.009). Conversely, other professionals (P = 0.021) and the 30–39 age cohort (P = 0.021) demonstrated significant Post-Pandemic emotional distress attenuation. Discussion

Frontline medical workers have sustained elevated occupational stress levels due to multifactorial stressors, primarily workload intensity and pathogen exposure [18]. Since the outbreak of COVID-19, the virus strains have been mutating constantly, and the pandemic has become increasingly severe and complex. In the Post-Pandemic Era, the Omicron variant spreads rapidly, as a high infection rate, and is difficult to control, which has a great impact on the mental health of front-line medical staff. They have the dual status of survivors and healthcare providers. In the past three years, they not only had to face the anxiety, fear and negative emotions from a large number of confirmed patients, but also needed to overcome the fear and tension of being infected. The huge workload and psychological pressure can easily lead to different degrees of exacerbation of psychological issues of medical staff [19]. Studies have pointed out that medical workers who received or cared for COVID-19 patients are at a higher risk of anxiety and depression disorders than ordinary medical workers [20].

Our study revealed that due to medical staff's professional knowledge and adaptability to public health emergencies [21], some participants' mental health demonstrated better psychological resilience. In the Post-Pandemic Era, an increasing number of healthcare workers feel under significant pressure. They perceive themselves as useless and worthless, struggle to concentrate, lose confidence and the ability to cope with challenges, and frequently experience feelings of sadness and depression. The emotional prevalence rate of female, unmarried, nurses and workers aged less than 30 had increased more markedly. The psychological deterioration female medical staff may be in the face of uncertain factors, the female brain cortex stimulated reaction more strongly, more prone to anxiety symptoms [22, 23]. In addition, female medical workers exhibit greater empathy, when they are confronting patients with critical illness or mortality events, they are more likely to develop anxiety, depression, vicarious trauma and other psychosomatic reactions [24]. In this regard, it is necessary to implement targeted psychological counseling combined with females’ psychological characteristics. Contrary to prior epidemiological reports, this study shows that the deterioration of emotional distress is more pronounced among unmarried medical staff, possibly due to their long-term stress and absence of established social support systems, while dyadic coping mechanisms can effectively help medical workers to implement adaptive coping strategies to face and overcome difficulties [25, 26]. Nurses have operated at the forefront of the outbreak, chronically a high risk of infection, and routine exposure to physical pain and even death, which predisposes this cohort to trauma spectrum disorders [27]. As for the workers aged < 30 years, emerging evidence suggests that they are under the compounded developmental stressors of life and work at this stage. They are navigating critical developmental milestones both in life and work, and are under greater pressure, so the psychopathology prevalence is more obvious [28].

Although there are relatively few articles on the changes in mental health of medical workers before and after the pandemic era, by comparing the existing literature, we found that their demonstrated remarkable consistency. The mental health status of medical workers during the pandemic is generally deteriorated [29–31]. Women, nurses and married medical workers under 40 years old have a significantly elevated risk of emotional distress [32, 33]. According to the previous related studies, contributing factors may include women experience more family care stress with the lack of adequate concerns and safeguards in physiological health care during the outbreak such as sanitary pads, period underwear, and other feminine hygiene products [34], and are neuroendocrine vulnerabilities in stress processing creating more psychological stress responses [35]. During the COVID-19 pandemic, nurses are the highest-risk occupational cohort among front-line medical staff [36], they had heavy tasks, great responsibilities, huge pressure, and low status with longer contact time with patients [37]. Therefore, the psychological pressure of nurses is greater. Married people worried about their family members. According to the study and other healthcare professions, the younger the healthcare workers are more likely to have psychiatric problems. It’s associated with less work experience in this age group [38, 39]. Older age usually with more extensive clinical experience, have more adequate understanding of infectious diseases and life experience, they could have greater emotional control and a good mental attitude, thus with a lower risk of emotional distress [35, 40]. At the same time, other studies believe that the prevention and control pressure in Post-Pandemic Era is greater, and the workload and psychological pressure faced by medical staff also increased [41].

This investigation compared the mental health level of front-line health workers before and after COVID-19, revealing that they were still facing high physical and mental pressure. Most current researches are in the initial stage of the outbreak, with the change of virus mutation, policy adjustment and disease resistance time, the psychological level of medical staff will be different, while the crisis intervention and stress management system for them is still not perfect. Our study continuously focuses on the psychological adaptation status of front-line medical workers, and we use a tracking study designed to reveal the temporal evolution mechanism of the psychological stress response of front-line medical workers.

This study has several methodological limitations that warrant consideration. Firstly, our sampling framework did not encompass healthcare professionals working in Mobile Cabin Hospitals across diverse geographic regions and hospital tiers. Secondly, to minimize response burden and enhance participation rates, the investigation captured only departmental affiliations rather than quantifying specific task parameters or workload intensity through standardized metrics. This lack of granularity in workload characterization may have introduced measurement bias in outcome assessment. Thirdly, temporal heterogeneity in data collection periods introduces potential confounding effects. The baseline survey was conducted during March 20–29, 2020, whereas follow-up data were acquired during December 9–11, 2022. This chronological disparity between data collection intervals may have engendered three distinct categories of confounding variables: (I) Pathogen virulence trajectories altering occupational exposure profiles, particularly concerning evolving viral transmission dynamics and associated biocontainment requirements; (II) The regulatory effect of epidemic control paradigms on psychophysiological stress response in different era (early containment and later mitigation); (III) Acute stress reactivity triggered by episodic public health crises, exemplified by regional outbreak resurgence during November 2022 preceding follow-up data acquisition. While statistical power was optimized through balanced sampling methodology, residual temporal confounders across measurement intervals may nevertheless compromise the validity of causal interpretations. Fourthly, the investigation did not incorporate systematic assessment of psychotherapeutic or pharmacological interventions administered to healthcare workers, thereby precluding analysis of their moderating effects on mental health outcomes. Besides, the 12-item General Health Questionnaire (GHQ-12) employed for psychological evaluation exhibits limited psychometric granularity, constraining its capacity to capture multidimensional mental health constructs with clinical precision. Regarding sampling validity, our cohort predominantly comprised voluntary participants from Xiaotangshan Mobile Cabin Hospital in Beijing-a single-institution convenience sampling approach that introduces potential selection bias. This geographic and institutional homogeneity substantially constrains the generalizability of findings to heterogeneous healthcare systems or populations under distinct sociopandemic conditions. Furthermore, the exclusive focus on pandemic-era data collection inherently limits extrapolation to non-crisis healthcare contexts. In future research, we will implement the following improvements: adopting random sampling and multicenter study designs to enhance sample representativeness, utilizing more advanced statistical models (such as mixed-effects models) and addressing missing data issues, and replicating the study across multiple regions or time points to strengthen the external validity of the results.

In the future, investigations should employ multicenter cohorts and use different assessment tools to further evaluate the psychological status of medical staff at different times. Through the implementation of primary prevention frameworks, we can proactively address mental health challenges among medical workers. By integrating evidence-based intervention strategies—including cognitive restructuring of stress perception and enhancement of social support systems—this approach enables preemptive mitigation of psychological distress in medical personnel. Such systemic improvements not only elevate the psychological well-being of healthcare providers but also optimize their professional functioning, thereby fostering enhanced capacity for clinical service delivery. This preventive paradigm ultimately contributes to sustainable improvements in healthcare quality through workforce mental health promotion.

Conclusion

This study conducted a comparative analysis of mental health outcomes among medical workers in Beijing’s makeshift medical facilities during the Pre- and Post-Pandemic of COVID-19. The results demonstrate that, despite epidemiological stabilization, medical personnel exhibited enduring psychological sequelae, with mental health indices remaining significantly below pre-pandemic baselines. These persistent disparities underscore the necessity for a multidimensional intervention framework encompassing: (1) institutionalized psychological support mechanisms, including routine mental health surveillance, targeted psychotherapeutic interventions, and resilience-building stress mitigation protocols; and (2) systemic policy reforms to strengthen occupational safeguards, such as ergonomic optimization of clinical environments, allocation of dedicated mental health resources, and development of trauma-informed workplace policies for high-stress occupational cohorts. Future research and pandemic response efforts should draw on the findings of this study to provide a scientific basis for mental health interventions in infectious disease control and similar high-intensity work environments. These measures aim to improve the mental health of healthcare workers and offer valuable insights for addressing future public health emergencies.
