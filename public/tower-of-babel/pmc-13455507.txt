Development of a matrix-supported 2.5-dimensional co-culture model: an in vitro platform for studying liver cell interaction and disease mechanisms
Authors: Sun-Young Lee, Dahyun Kim, Hye-Yeon Seok, Yong-Hwan Moon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cell–cell crosstalk and the extracellular matrix are critical for maintenance of tissue-specific structure and functions. Conventional 2-dimensional (2-D) cultures provide analytical simplicity but lack spatial cues, whereas fully embedded 3-dimensional (3-D) systems introduce technical limitations for imaging and downstream molecular analyses. Here, we present a matrix-supported 2.5-dimensional (2.5-D) co-culture platform that combines the analytical advantages of 2-D culture with a more physiologically relevant environment for cells. It is simple and accessible, yet it incorporates the spatial organization of direct cell–cell and cell–matrix interactions, which is typical of 3-D models. As a proof of concept, we used the platform to model hepatic fibrosis by co-culturing hepatic stellate cells (LX-2) with a hepatocyte surrogate (HepG2). The cells cultured in 2.5-D co-culture displayed architectural features similar to those observed in fibrotic tissue and they showed a significant increase in fibrogenic gene expression following TGF-β1 stimulation compared to the monocultures. This system also enabled functional assessment of Δ40p53, a p53 isoform linked to fibrogenesis, revealing a clear association between its expression and collagen deposition during fibrogenesis. This platform provides a biomimetic in vitro platform for investigating cell–cell and cell–ECM signaling. By combining architectural complexity with experimental flexibility, the 2.5-D co-culture platform holds promise for mechanistic studies in fibrosis and tissue remodeling.

Introduction

Liver diseases, including fibrosis, cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma, are among the leading causes of global morbidity and mortality.1 Despite advances in liver disease modeling, current in vitro systems exhibit notable limitations.2,3 Although conventional 2-dimensional (2-D) monocultures are widely used due to their simplicity and cost-effectiveness, they do not accurately represent cellular responses that occur in vivo. These systems lack the spatial and multicellular complexity of tissues and fail to replicate essential cell–cell and cell–extracellular matrix (ECM) interactions. As a result, they have limited physiological relevance and predictive power for drug testing and disease modeling.

To overcome these limitations, co-culture systems that incorporate heterotypic cell interactions have been introduced. Among them, the transwell assay is a widely used for studying paracrine signaling between separated cell types.4 It enables controlled co-culture conditions and independent analysis of each cell population, providing an advantage over direct-contact systems.5 However, it cannot support direct cell–cell interactions, lacks 3-dimensional (3-D) complexity, and potential interference from filtering materials restricts its physiological relevance in modeling liver fibrosis and regeneration. A recent study demonstrated that hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) cultured in compliant matrix environments maintain a more quiescent phenotype than those in rigid plastic culture conditions. Additionally, heterotypic co-culture with hepatic progenitor or epithelial cell populations can activate programs related to fibrosis and promote extracellular matrix remodeling.6 These findings highlight the importance of microenvironment-dependent cell–cell interactions in modeling liver fibrogenesis in vitro.

Recent advances in 3-D culture techniques, including spheroids, organoids, and biomaterial-based scaffolds, aim to overcome these limitations.7–10 These models provide a more physiologically relevant physical, cellular, and biochemical microenvironment that accurately recapitulates the in vivo tissue architecture and microenvironment. Nevertheless, challenges remain in balancing complexity with reproducibility and accessibility, highlighting the need for strategies that bridge the gap between 2-D and 3-D systems.

The liver is structurally and functionally specialized organ and comprises a heterogeneous population of cells. Hepatocytes account for ∼60% of the total liver cells and are responsible for key metabolic, synthetic, and detoxification functions.11,12 Kupffer cells, the resident macrophages, make up ∼15%, whereas HSCs represent approximately 5–10% of the total cell population. HSCs reside in the space of disse between hepatocytes and sinusoidal endothelial cells in a quiescent state.13,14 They store vitamin A and regulate ECM homeostasis. Upon chronic injury, when the liver undergoes wound healing, HSCs are activated and transformed into myofibroblast-like cells that are the primary source of ECM proteins, such as collagen I and III, contributing to fibrotic scarring.13 Such activation is tightly regulated by signaling from adjacent hepatocytes and immune cells, implying that intercellular crosstalk is crucial for homeostasis and fibrosis. However, progress in this area is hindered by the lack of a physiologically relevant experimental model that accurately recapitulates the liver environment for dynamic interactions among hepatocytes, HSCs, and other nonparenchymal cells. Therefore, developing such model is necessary to study disease mechanisms and therapeutic responses. Here, we established a 2.5-dimensional (2.5-D) co-culture model that combines the simplicity of 2-D systems with the physiological relevance of 3-D models. The term “2.5-D” is used here to describe an intermediate culture configuration where cells are cultured on a compliant matrix interface and exposed to matrix-derived biochemical signals without being confined within the matrix. This system preserves many of the experimental advantages of 2-D culture and effectively captures key features of the cellular microenvironment, including a heterogeneous cell population and ECM. As a proof of concept, we applied this model to investigate fibrogenic signaling within the liver microenvironment using co-cultures of human hepatic stellate LX-2 cells with hepatoblastoma-derived HepG2 cells, as they retain many hepatocyte characteristics.15 We evaluated cell morphology and fibrogenic gene expression in mono and co-culture systems following treatment with the profibrogenic cytokine TGF-β1. To further validate the model's utility for functional studies, we examined collagen expression and deposition in LX-2 cells through pharmacological perturbation, knockdown, or overexpression of Δ40p53, a p53 isoform that we previously identified as a key regulator of liver fibrogenesis.16

Our 2.5-D co-culture model enables to partially reproduce the structural and functional aspects of healthy and diseased liver tissue. The model serves as a practical platform for investigation of liver pathophysiology and screening drug potential. Beyond liver research, this system offers a versatile framework for studying heterotypic cellular interactions, cell–matrix interactions, and pharmacological responses in various tissue system and disease contexts.

Materials and methods

Cell lines

Human hepatoblastoma HepG2 cells were purchased from the Korean Cell Line Bank (Cat# 88 065, Seoul, Korea) and were grown in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and antibiotics (50 U mL−1 penicillin and 50 µg mL−1 streptomycin). Human hepatic stellate LX-2 cells were obtained from Merck Millipore (Cat# SCC064, RRID: CVCL_5792, Burlington, MA, USA) and cultured in DMEM supplemented with 2% FBS, 2 mM glutamine (GIBCO), and antibiotics. Terminally differentiated human hepatoma HepaRG cells (Cat# HPR116, Biopredic International, Saint Gregoire, France) were cultured in Williams' E medium supplemented with 2 mM GlutaMax (Cat# 35 050 061, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and Thaw, Plate and General Purpose supplement (Cat# HPRG770, Thermo Fisher Scientific) or Induction additive (Cat# HPRG740, Thermo Fisher Scientific). GFP-labeled HepG2 cells (HepG2–GFP) and mCherry-labeled LX-2 cells (LX-2–mCherry) were used without antibiotic selection after lentiviral transduction to avoid long-term stress responses. Cells were periodically tested for mycoplasma contamination using the BioMycoX mycoplasma PCR detection kit (CellSafe, Yongin, Korea).

2-D, 3-D, and 2.5-D co-cultures

For 2-D, 3-D, and 2.5-D co-culture experiments, HepG2 and LX-2 cells were co-seeded at a density of 1 × 105 cells per cm2 and 1 × 104 cells per cm2, respectively (unless otherwise indicated, hepatocyte surrogates and LX-2 cells were co-seeded at a 10 : 1 ratio), and cultured in DMEM with 2% FBS and antibiotics. For 2.5-D co-culture was performed as previously described with modifications.17,18 In brief, after seeding onto a pre-coated confocal dish with a thin layer of 3% (v/v) reconstituted basement membrane derived from Engelbreth-Holm-Swarm tumor (Matrigel®; Corning, 354 230), we allowed the mixture of HepG2 and LX-2 cells to settle for 30 minutes at 37 °C. We then carefully added culture medium (DMEM with 2% FBS and antibiotics) containing 3% (v/v) matrigel. For 3D co-culture, HepG2 and LX-2 cells were pelleted, resuspended in 300 µL of matrigel, and transferred the mixture to a pre-coated confocal dish with thin layer of 3% (v/v) matrigel. The mixtures were incubated for 20 min at 37 °C to allow matrigel polymerization. The co-cultures were maintained for 3 days (2-D and 2.5-D) or 5–7 days (3-D).

Unless otherwise indicated, 2-D and 2.5-D mono-/co-cultures were established for 48 h prior to treatment, followed by 24 h of vehicle or TGFβ1 treatment, for a total culture duration of 72 h. Modified timelines for specific perturbation experiments are indicated in corresponding figure legends.

Since HepaRG cells differ substantially from HepG2 cells, we modified the HepaRG/LX-2 co-culture protocol. First, HepaRG cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 105 cells per cm2 on surfaces pre-coated with 3% (v/v) matrigel® using Williams' E medium (supplemented with 2 mM GlutaMax and the Thaw, Plate and General Purpose additive). The following day, the medium was replaced with Williams' E medium containing 2 mM GlutaMax and the induction additive. After 18 to 24 h, LX-2 cells were added at 1 × 104 cells per cm2 in culture medium (DMEM with 2% FBS, 2 mM glutamine, and antibiotics) containing 3% (v/v) matrigel®.

After 48 h of initial mono- or co-culture establishment, medium was replaced with fresh culture medium containing vehicle or TGFβ1 (10 ng mL−1) for an additional 24 h unless otherwise specified.

Antibodies and reagents

The following antibodies were used-anti-GFP (Abcam Cat# ab290, RRID:AB_303395), anti-mCherry (Proteintech Cat# 26765-1-AP, RRID:AB_2 876 881, Proteintech Cat# 68088-1-Ig, RRID:AB_2 918 825), anti-collagen I (Proteintech Cat# 14695-1-AP, RRID:AB_2 082 037), anti-collagen III (Proteintech Cat# 22734-1-AP, RRID:AB_2 879 158), anti-α-SMA (Sigma-Aldrich Cat# A5228, RRID:AB_262054), anti-desmin (Proteintech Cat# 16520-1-AP, RRID:AB_2 292 918), anti-human p53 (Pab1801, Santa Cruz Biotechnology Cat# sc-98, RRID:AB_628085), anti-GAPDH (Proteintech Cat# 60004-1-Ig, RRID:AB_2 107 436), anti-α-tubulin (Sigma-Aldrich Cat# CP06, RRID:AB_2 617 116), albumin (Proteintech Cat# 16475-1-AP).

TGF-β1 was purchased from PEPROTECH (Cranbury, NJ, USA). FuGENE 6 (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) and Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Thermo Fisher Scientific) were used for transfection. SB431542 was purchased from MedChemExpress (MCE, NJ, USA).

Plasmids

The pLenti-CMV–GFP–DEST vector (Addgene #736-1) was used to label HepG2 cells, and the pLenti-CMV–mCherry–DEST vector was used to label LX-2 cells. The mCherry gene was cloned into the pENTR1A entry vector (Invitrogen, A10462) and subsequently inserted into the pLenti-CMV–Puro–DEST vector (W118-1) (Addgene # 17 452) using Gateway LR Clonase (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #11791019) recombination to generate the pLenti-CMV-mCherry-DEST vector.

Lentivirus production and transduction

To produce lentiviral particles, packaging plasmids—pLP1, pLP2, and pLP/VSVG—and lentiviral transfer plasmid—pLenti-CMV–GFP or pLenti-CMV–mCherry—were transfected into 293FT cells using FuGene6 (Promega) following the manufacturer's instructions. The transfected cells were cultured in DMEM containing 10% FBS, 50 U mL−1 penicillin, and 50 µg mL−1 streptomycin. After 48 h, the supernatant was harvested and filtered through a 0.45-µm syringe. The viral particles were incubated with HepG2 and LX-2 cells in the presence of 4 µg mL−1 polybrene.

Antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) and transfection

Control and Δ40p53-specific gapmer and steric blocking ASOs were synthesized (Integrated DNA Technologies, Inc., Newark, NJ, USA) as described previously.16 The sequences of the oligonucleotides used were as follows-control ASO: 5′-ACCAGGGCGTATCTCTCCAT-3′ and 5′-ACCAGGGCGUAUCUCUCCAUA-3′; Δ40p53-ASO: 5′-CAGTCCCATGGAATTTTCGC-3′ and 5′-AACGUUGUUUUCAGGAAGUAG-3′. Cells were transfected with ASOs using RNAiMAX (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer's instructions. FAM-labeled oligonucleotides (FAM-luciferase, 5′-ACCAGGGCGTATCTCTCCATA-3′) were transfected and visualized using fluorescence microscopy to assess transfection efficiency.

Immunofluorescence, immunocytochemistry and immunohistochemistry staining

Mono- or co-cultured cells were plated on a confocal dish (SPL, #211 350, Korea) and fixed in either 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) solution for 20 min at room temperature or methanol : acetone (50 : 50, v/v) solution for 10 min at −20 °C. The samples were then blocked with 5% goat serum and 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in IF buffer (137 mM NaCl, 10 mM Na2HPO4, 1.8 mM KH2PO4, 0.1% BSA, 0.2% Triton X-100, and 0.05% Tween 20). Primary antibodies in IF buffer were incubated with 5% goat serum and 1% BSA overnight at 4 °C in a humidified chamber. For 2.5-D cultured cells, Fab Fragment Goat anti-mouse IgG (Jackson ImmunoResearch Labs Cat# 115-007-003, RRID:AB_2 338 476) was added to block mouse immunoglobulins derived from matrigel. After washing three times with IF buffer for 10 min each, Alexa Fluor dye-coupled secondary antibodies (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA) were added and incubated for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with 1 µg mL−1 Hoechst 33 342 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) or DAPI, and the cells were rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and mounted with Fluoremount-G (Electron Microscopy Sciences, Hatfield, PA, USA).

Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) sections of normal (Cat# CS811527) and cirrhotic (Cat# CS811804) liver tissues were purchased from OriGene (Rockville, MD, USA). Collagen staining of both tissue sections and co-cultured cells was performed using a Picrosirius Red staining kit (Rhawn, Shanghai, China). For immunocytochemistry staining of collagen type I, 2.5-D co-cultures were fixed with 4% PFA, blocked, and incubated with an anti-collagen I (Proteintech) overnight at 4 °C according to the manufacturer's instructions. The samples were then incubated with HRP-goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 1 h at room temperature. The samples were incubated with DAB to develop color followed by counterstaining with hematoxylin (Abcam). Stained cells were observed and imaged under a light microscope (Nikon Eclipse 80i).

Flow cytometry

Cells were dispersed in 0.25% trypsin-EDTA (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and pelleted by centrifugation at 500×g for 5 min. The samples were then resuspended with sorting buffer (2.5 mM EDTA and 1% FBS in cold PBS). Flow cytometry was performed on a BD Aria flow cytometer (BD Biosciences) and the data were analyzed with FlowJo software (version 10.10.0).

Hydroxyproline assay

Quantitative evaluation of collagen content was performed using a commercial hydroxyproline assay kit (Cat # MAK569, Sigma-Aldrich) following the manufacturer's instruction. Briefly, cells were harvested, washed, lysed, and subjected to acid hydrolysis in 6 M HCl at 120 °C for 3 h. The hydrolyzed samples were subsequently transferred to a 96-well plate and evaporated. The dried samples were reacted with the oxidation reagent and developer to induce a colorimetric reaction. The absorbance of each samples was measured at 560 nm using a microplate reader. Total collagen content was calculated based on a hydroxyproline standard curve and normalized to the total cell number.

Statistical analyses

GraphPad Prism (version 10.3 RRID:SCR_002798) was used for statistical analyses. All values are presented as the mean ± SD. Statistical significance was determined using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), or two-way ANOVA with Tukey's post hoc tests, as described in the figure legends. P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Current in vitro 2-D and 3-D culture systems and their limitations

In a healthy liver, hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) reside in the space of disse between hepatocytes and sinusoidal endothelial cells in a quiescent state.14 As hepatocytes and HSCs reside in close proximity in vivo, we focused on these two representative hepatic cell types. To model heterotypic co-culture, human hepatic stellate LX-2 cells were cultured with HepG2 cells, which were selected as a functional parenchymal component; they retain key differentiated hepatic features necessary to mediate cross-talk with stellate cells.19–21 As an initial step to establish an in vitro platform capable of assessing heterotypic interactions between hepatic parenchymal and non-parenchymal cells, we first evaluated how cells behaved in the two major culture models widely employed in liver research: conventional 2-D monolayer cultures and matrix-embedded 3-D cultures (Fig. 1b). To directly visualize the heterotypic interactions, we generated GFP-expressing HepG2 (HepG2–GFP) cells and mCherry-expressing LX-2 (LX-2–mCherry) cells. HepG2–GFP hepatocyte surrogates and LX-2–mCherry were co-seeded at a 10 : 1 ratio to consider their in vivo relative abundance in the liver.22 Phase-contrast and fluorescence imaging revealed clear morphological distinctions between the two culture platforms (Fig. 1c). In 2-D conditions, HepG2 and LX-2 cells displayed a typical flattened morphology with limited structural organization. In contrast, in 3-D matrigel-embedded cultures, the cells formed compact spheroid-like structures with increased matrix depth, which led to difficulty in single-plane imaging. Interestingly, fluorescence imaging revealed that the two cell types displayed distinct spatial arrangements in both platforms (Fig. 1c). In 2-D, the cell types remained as segregated patches, whereas in 3-D they formed discernible microstructures instead of being intermixed.

Collectively, these observations revealed the architectural limitations of both conventional 2-D and fully embedded 3-D systems. The 2-D cultures lacked structural complexity, while the 3-D cultures formed dense aggregates that hindered direct cellular imaging. These constraints underscore the need for an alternative culture strategy that balances spatial organization with imaging compatibility.

Development and characterization of 2.5-D co-culture system

Given that the morphological analyses of 2-D and 3-D co-culture revealed compromised cell morphology, matrix dependency, and imaging challenges (Fig. 1c), we sought to establish an intermediate culture system that integrates the individual strengths of both approaches. To this end, we developed a 2.5-D co-culture platform designed to better model in vivo-like cell–cell and cell–matrix interactions while maintaining compatibility with imaging techniques.

To model the hepatic microenvironment, we co-seeded HepG2–GFP and LX-2–mCherry expressing cells at a 10 : 1 ratio on a thin layer of 3% matrigel and cultured them in medium supplemented with 3% matrigel, creating a matrix-supported monolayer referred to as the 2.5-D co-culture (Fig. 2a and b). Such a model offers a biologically relevant matrix context while avoiding the fully embedded constraints typical of 3-D culture.

We next examined the spatial distribution and morphology of the two cell types across 2-D and 2.5-D environments. In 2-D culture on rigid plastic, LX-2 cells predominantly formed clusters (Fig. 2c right upper panel). In contrast, under 2.5-D conditions, LX-2 cells adopted a distinct stratified organization, preferentially occupying intercellular spaces between adjacent HepG2–GFP cells rather than distributing randomly throughout the culture (Fig. 2c right lower panel, Fig. S1). This spatial organization was associated with a more elongated morphology of LX-2 cells compared with 2-D culture. In the 2.5-D co-culture system, TGFβ1-activated LX-2 cells exhibited pronounced morphological changes, extending large lateral edges and forming thick patches that ran through the intercellular spaces of HepG2 cells (Fig. 2c, S1 and S2).

Given the distinct morphological difference observed between 2-D and 2.5-D co-cultures (Fig. 2c and S1), we compared the morphology (Fig. 3a) and cytoskeletal organization (Fig. 3b) of LX-2 cells across 2-D, matrix-supported 2.5-D, and fully embedded 3-D systems. In 2-D monolayers, LX-2 cells exhibited a flattened and extensively spread morphology, and heterogeneous morphology. Conversely, cells in 2.5-D culture displayed elongated and interconnected cellular structures with partial matrix confinement. In contrast, fully embedded 3-D cultures promoted relatively homogeneous population characterized by compact and spatially confined morphologies with minimal cell spreading (Fig. 3c). These data demonstrate that the 2.5-D platform facilitates a morphological state intermediate between planar and fully embedded 3-D culture.

Matrix compliance prevents spontaneous HSC activation in the 2.5-D model

To evaluate whether these distinct spatial and matrix-mediated interactions influence HSC activation, we assessed the expression of α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA), a canonical marker of activated HSCs,23 in 2-D and 2.5-D culture conditions with or without fibrogenic signaling (Fig. 4a). Remarkably, LX-2 cells cultured on rigid 2-D plastic surfaces (both mono- and co-culture) expressed α-SMA even without TGFβ1 stimulation, suggesting that substrate stiffness alone is sufficient to induce spontaneous activation. In contrast, LX-2 cells within the matrix-supported 2.5-D system required TGFβ1 stimulation to induce α-SMA expression. This finding indicates that the compliant 2.5-D microenvironment minimizes substrate-driven spontaneous activation and preserves a lower basal activation state.

Consistent with the α-SMA expression pattern, we observed collagen type I deposition in both 2-D mono- and co-culture conditions without TGFβ1 stimulation (Fig. 4b). Notably, accumulation of fibrillar collagen type I around LX-2 cells was more pronounced in the 2-D co-culture compared to the corresponding monoculture, suggesting that interactions with neighboring HepG2 cells further enhance matrix deposition. Additionally, the total collagen content measured by the hydroxyproline assay revealed that hydroxyproline levels in 2.5-D conditions for both mono- and co-culture were significantly lower than those 2-D culture conditions (Fig. 4c), supporting that matrix-supported microenvironment reduces basal activation of LX-2 cells. Although the overall trends were similar between mono- and co-culture, co-cultured cells consistently exhibited higher hydroxyproline levels than monoculture counterparts in response to TGFβ1 stimulation, indicating that interaction between HepG2 and LX-2 cells augment collagen production, especially when triggered by fibrogenic cytokine while remaining dependent on the surrounding mechanical environment. Collectively, these findings suggest that the combination of a rigid substrate and communication between HepG2 and LX-2 cells promotes excessive fibrotic matrix deposition in conventional 2-D cultures.

To determine whether these observations were specific to HepG2 cells, we established an analogous 2.5-D co-culture using differentiated HepaRG cells. Consistent with observations in HepG2-based co-cultures, HepaRG-based co-cultures on 2.5-D culture system promoted spatial compartmentalization and cell–cell interface formation, accompanied by marked upregulation of α-SMA and collagen type I surrounding activated LX-2 cells under fibrogenic stimulation (Fig. S3).

Together, these findings indicate that the compliant, matrix-supported 2.5-D microenvironment minimizes substrate-driven basal activation of LX-2 cells. Consequently, this lower activation state provides a wider range for measuring fibrosis-related responses compared to rigid 2-D systems.

Biomimetic 2.5-D culture systems more closely capture selected structural and fibrogenic features than conventional 2-D models

To provide a reference for the architectural patterns observed in vitro models, we compared their histological features with those of human liver tissue. 2.5-D co-culture exhibited clear spatial compartmentalization between HepG2 and LX-2 cells, an organizational feature absent in conventional 2-D culture (Fig. 5a and S2). We observed that this compartmentalization became more obvious following TGFβ1 treatment. Notably, in the 2.5-D co-culture, interconnected, densely packed cellular regions and network-like structures were apparent. These structures were particularly prominent after TGFβ1-stimulation, compared with the more dispersed organization observed in 2-D culture. These architectural features resemble the network-like fibrotic remodeling observed in cirrhotic liver tissue (Fig. 5b). In contrast, such structural features were absent or poorly developed in 2-D cultures.

Consistent with these histological observations, substantial production and deposition of collagens in the 2.5-D co-culture following TGFβ1 stimulation (Fig. 5c and S4), which also showed similar pattern with cirrhotic liver tissue (Fig. 5d). This increase in collagen deposition similar to a feature of liver fibrosis, when normal parenchymal architecture is progressively replaced by excessive ECM accumulation.13,24

Taken together, the histological and collagen staining patterns indicate that the 2.5-D co-culture system reproduces several structural characteristics associated with fibrogenic tissue remodeling, including spatial compartmentalization, dense intercellular networking, and collagen accumulation. These features were less noticeable in conventional 2-D culture, supporting the architectural relevance of the matrix-supported co-culture model.

Co-culture of LX-2 HSCs with HepG2 cells enhances fibrogenic gene expression in response to TGFβ1

Because the 2.5-D co-culture system includes a heterogeneous mixture of hepatocyte surrogates and HSCs, direct evaluation of cell-type-specific gene expression presents a technical difficulty. To determine whether fibrogenic transcriptional responses can be reliably quantified within this mixed-culture platform, we evaluated the expression of profibrotic genes in 2.5-D co-culture following fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS). HepG2–GFP and LX-2–mCherry cells were maintained in 2.5-D co-culture with or without TGFβ1 treatment, after the mixed cell population was enzymatically dissociated. The two cell types were subsequently isolated by FACS based on GFP and mCherry expression (Fig. 6a and b). Quantitative RT-PCR (qRT-PCR) was then performed to assess the expression of key fibrogenic markers, including COL1A1, COL3A1, COL4A1, ACTA2, and TGFB1 (Fig. 6c). The analysis revealed that collagens (COL1A1, COL3A1, and COL4A1), and α-SMA (ACTA2) were predominantly expressed in LX-2 cells. In contrast, albumin was expressed specifically in HepG2 cells, confirming distinct cell-specific identities within the co-culture. In addition, TGFB1 expression was elevated in both cell types to a similar extent upon TGFβ1 stimulation, suggesting that LX-2 and HepG2 cells retain comparable sensitivity to TGFβ1 signaling and may contribute to the fibrogenic milieu through autocrine and paracrine mechanism. Importantly, TGFβ1 stimulation induced a significantly greater upregulation of α-SMA and collagen genes in LX-2 cells co-cultured with HepG2 cells than in LX-2 cells cultured alone (Fig. 6c). These findings suggest that heterotypic co-culture with hepatocyte surrogates potentiates HSC activation under fibrogenic conditions.

Co-culture of Δ40p53α overexpressing LX-2 cells with HepG2 cells potentiates TGFβ1-induced fibrogenic responses

To further demonstrate the 2.5-D co-culture platform is compatible with mechanistic perturbation studies, we applied it to investigate the cellular responses to modulation of Δ40p53α, a p53 isoform that we previously reported to influence fibrogenic gene expression in LX-2 cells.16 Since Δ40p53α is involved in extracellular matrix remodeling, it provides a suitable target for evaluating whether the platform can detect microenvironment-dependent changes in fibrogenic responses. We therefore assessed how its overexpression in HSCs impacts the fibrogenic response within a co-culture context. LX-2–mCherry cells overexpressing Δ40p53α were generated and validated by western blot analysis, which confirmed increased expression of collagen type I and collagen type III compared with control LX-2 cells (Fig. 7a). These empty vector (EV) or Δ40p53α-overexpressing LX-2–mCherry cells were then co-cultured with HepG2 cells and compared with monocultures (Fig. 7b). Immunofluorescence staining showed that co-cultures with Δ40p53α-overexpressing LX-2 cells exhibited a marked increase in α-SMA (Fig. S5) and the deposition of fibrillar collagen types I and III, compared to the corresponding monocultures of LX-2 cells (Fig. 7c). These findings indicate that the 2.5-D co-culture platform is capable of detecting enhanced fibrogenic responses following Δ40p53α overexpression within a heterotypic microenvironment.

Together, these findings align with the previous report that Δ40p53α promotes fibrogenic responses in HSCs,16 and they demonstrate that the 2.5-D co-culture platform can detect changes in fibrogenic phenotypes following genetic perturbation within a matrix-supported multicellular microenvironment.

The 2.5-D co-culture platform enables the functional evaluation of gene knockdown and antifibrogenic interventions under fibrogenic conditions

We next examined whether the 2.5-D co-culture platform can be used to effectively investigate inhibitory strategies targeting fibrotic pathways within a physiologically relevant fibrotic microenvironment. To this end, we employed Δ40p53-specific antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs), which we previously demonstrated to suppress fibrogenic gene expression under profibrogenic conditions by downregulating Δ40p53 expression.16 Δ40p53-specific ASOs were administered to a 2.5-D co-culture of LX-2–mCherry and HepG2 cells, either in the presence or absence of TGFβ1 (Fig. 8a). To verify efficient oligonucleotide delivery, transfection efficiency was independently assessed using FAM-labeled control oligonucleotides, which showed effective uptake by the co-cultured cells (Fig. 8b, left). HepG2 cells within the co-culture were identified by albumin immunostaining, which clearly distinguishes between HepG2 and LX-2 cells (Fig. 8b, right). Co-cultures treated with Δ40p53-specific ASOs showed a significant decrease in α-SMA (Fig. 8c) and fibrillar collagen type III (Fig. 8d) deposition compared to control ASO-treated co-cultures under TGFβ1-induced fibrogenic conditions. Consistent with the immunostaining results, western blots confirmed reduced levels of α-SMA and collagen type III in ASO-treated cultures (Fig. 8e), validating the observed phenotypic changes.

To further evaluate the utility of the 2.5-D co-culture system for pharmacological testing, we examined the effects of the TGFβ type I receptor inhibitor SB431542, which blocks canonical profibrotic signaling. Treatment with SB431542 significantly reduced TGFβ1-induced collagen type I expression in the 2.5-D co-culture (Fig. 8f), demonstrating that this platform effectively assesses signaling inhibitors within a multicellular, matrix supported context.

Collectively, these perturbation studies demonstrate the values of the 2.5-D co-culture model as a practical platform for functionally investigating genes of interest, assessing gene knockdown strategies, and evaluating candidate antifibrotic therapeutics in matrix-supported fibrogenic conditions.

Discussion

Cell culture systems that can appropriately recapitulate fibrotic liver environment are critical for basic and translational research. In this study, we present a matrix-supported 2.5-D heterotypic co-culture platform, consisting of hepatocyte surrogates HepG2 and human hepatic stellate LX-2 cells. Our culture model integrates key structural and functional features of the hepatic fibrotic microenvironment while preserving experimental accessibility.

A major limitation of conventional 2-D culture systems is the spontaneous activation of HSCs induced by rigid plastic substrates, which can be misinterpreted as fibrogenic signaling.24–26 In contrast, we demonstrated that our matrix-supported 2.5-D co-culture system minimizes the activation of LX-2 cells to a low basal state, likely by decreasing the substrate-driven mechanical priming. These observations are aligned with a growing body of evidence demonstrating that mechanical cues and ECM context are critical determinants of HSC activation and fibrosis progression in vivo.6,27–30 This compliant microenvironment effectively prevents the constant activation of LX-2 cells typically observed in rigid monolayer cultures, providing an expanded window to induce significant fibrogenic responses upon TGFβ1 stimulation. Consequently, we reasoned that this explains why the differential expression of collagen type I and α-SMA in LX-2 cells was significantly higher in the 2.5-D platform than in 2-D platform following TGFβ1 treatment.

ECM occupies less than 10% of the total liver tissue area within the normal physiological condition.31–33 Although matrigel composition differs from that of the native liver extracellular matrix (Table S1), it provides a compliant, basement membrane-like biochemical microenvironment enriched in laminin, collagen type IV, nidogen, and heparan sulfate proteoglycans.34 These components have been widely reported to support physiologically relevant cell–matrix interactions, cell-adhesion, and epithelial polarity that are not readily achieved on conventional rigid tissue culture plastic.35–37 Therefore, the improved cellular organization observed in our 2.5-D culture system is likely attributed to the combined effects of matrix compliance and the biochemical cues provided by the basement membrane components of matrigel. However, since matrigel has undefined composition and inherent batch-to-batch variability, the present study cannot distinguish contribution of its mechanical properties and biochemical composition to the observed cellular responses. Future studies using chemically defined matrices or tunable hydrogels with independently controllable properties will help dissect the contributions of stiffness and biochemical composition.

Histological analysis revealed that LX-2 cells in the 2.5-D co-culture condition displayed spatial compartmentalization from HepG2 cells, forming a network-like cellular organization that is less apparent in conventional 2-D cultures. Importantly, the 2.5-D co-culture platform enabled cell type-specific molecular analysis such as qRT-PCR within a heterotypic culture. We were able to separate hepatocyte surrogate HepG2 and hepatic stellate LX-2 cells from co-culture and quantify fibrogenic gene expression by employing FACS. This approach revealed that TGFβ1-induced expression of collagen genes (COL1A1, COL3A1, and COL4A1) and ACTA2 was significantly enhanced in LX-2 cells when co-cultured with HepG2 cells compared to monocultures. These findings suggest that the heterotypic co-culture context amplifies HSC activation, which was consistent with the in vivo observation that hepatocyte injury and stress drive stellate cell-mediated fibrogenesis.13,21,38 In addition, these results demonstrated that the matrix-supported 2.5-D co-culture system is compatible with monitoring gene expression changes in a cell type-specific and context-dependent manner. Such detailed observation is often difficult to achieve in monoculture systems, highlighting the feasibility and robustness of this platform.

Our finding demonstrated the functional utility of the 2.5-D co-culture system as a robust platform for investigation the roles of specific genes in liver fibrosis. By modulating Δ40p53α, a p53 isoform that regulates fibrogenesis,16 we validated that its overexpression in LX-2 cells substantially drives collagen production and enhanced collagen fibril formation in the 2.5-D co-culture microenvironment. Notably, the observation that enhanced collagen fibril formation was prominent in the co-cultures of hepatocyte surrogates and HSCs.

A key strength of the 2.5-D platform is its compatibility with genetic and pharmacological perturbations. We demonstrated the functional efficacy of Δ40p53-specific ASOs, reducing α-SMA expression and collagen deposition under fibrogenic conditions. In addition, inhibition of TGFβ signaling using the small-molecule TGFβ1 receptor inhibitor SB431542 (ref. 39) effectively attenuated collagen expression in the TGFβ1-treated co-culture system. This proof-of-concept perturbation study indicates that the 2.5-D model is manageable to evaluating candidate antifibrotic strategies.

In this proof-of-concept study, we utilized LX-2 and HepG2 cells as representative non-parenchymal and parenchymal liver cells, respectively. LX-2 cells are a well-established model for investigating HSC activation and myofibroblast differentiation as they retain key fibrogenic features of primary human HSCs, such as robust induction of α-SMA and collagen expression in response to TGFβ1 stimulation.40,41 HepG2 cells widely used as a surrogate hepatocyte model because they retain many hepatic functions and have been extensively utilized to investigate hepatocyte-stellate cell crosstalk during liver injury and fibrogenesis.42,43 Consequently, the combination of LX-2 and HepG2 cells provides a practical and reproducible multicellular platform for evaluating cell–cell interactions and fibrogenic responses.

Nonetheless, the present 2.5-D model has inherent limitations because it relies on immortalized cell lines and matrigel. Unlike mature primary hepatocytes, HepG2 cells are proliferative and do not fully reproduce the metabolic functions and phenotypic stability of normal differentiated hepatocytes. Similarly, although LX-2 cells exhibit many characteristics of activated HSCs, they do not fully reflect the heterogenous activation states and phenotypic plasticity of primary hepatic stellate cells in vivo. Therefore, the magnitude and dynamics of cellular responses observed in this study may differ from those obtained using primary human liver cells. Furthermore, liver fibrosis is a multicellular process involving complex interactions among hepatocytes, stellate cells, immune cells, endothelial cells, and inflammatory mediators. Because the current model incorporates only hepatocyte and stellate cell surrogates, it does not capture the full cellular complexity of the fibrotic liver microenvironment. Further studies using primary human hepatocytes and primary HSCs, patient-derived cells, and more complex multicellular co-culture systems will be important to further validate the physical relevance of the 2.5-D platform.

3-D culture models represent a revolutionary approach to advancing healthcare research by offering a physiologically more relevant microenvironment than conventional 2-D cultures.27,44 However, they also present drawbacks, including extended culture times to establish cellular architecture and function, and restricted penetration of oxygen and nutrients due to their compact spheroid structure.45–47 Moreover, technical challenges in sample processing for downstream biochemical and molecular analyses persist, which compromise overall reproducibility and feasibility for routine applications.48,49 In the context of biomaterials and regenerative medicine, our matrix-supported 2.5-D co-culture model represents a simple yet functional approach to microenvironment engineering. Moreover, the platform can be readily modified by using defined or synthetic matrices, tuning matrix stiffness and concentration, or introducing additional cell types to enhance disease relevance.

Conclusion

We developed a matrix-supported 2.5-D co-culture platform that reproduces several architectural, molecular, and functional characteristics associated with fibrogenic liver remodeling. This system maintains compatibility with routine imaging, molecular analysis, and experimental perturbation. Moreover, this platform would provide a practical foundation for future refinement of biomimetic liver fibrosis models by enabling assessment of heterotypic cell interactions, extracellular matrix deposition, and antifibrotic modulation within a tractable in vitro setting.
