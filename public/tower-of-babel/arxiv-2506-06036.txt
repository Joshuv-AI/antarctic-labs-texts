Abstract
. We construct a new class of operators that act on symmetric functions with two deformation parameters q q and t t . Our combinatorial construction associates each operator with a specific lattice path, whose steps alternate between moving up and down. We demonstrate that positive linear combinations of these operators are the images of Negut elements via a representation of the shuffle algebra acting on the space of symmetric functions. Additionally, we provide a monomial, elementary, and Schur symmetric function expansion for the symmetric function obtained through repeated applications of the path operators on 1 1 . We apply path operators to investigate a ( q , t ) (q,t) -deformation of the classical hypergeometric tau functions, which generalizes several important series already present in enumerative geometry, gauge theory, and integrability. We prove that this function is uniquely characterized by a family of partial differential equations derived from a positive linear combination of path operators. We also use our operators to offer a new, independent proof of the key result in establishing the extended delta conjecture of Haglund, Remmel, and Wilson.



1.
Introduction

Hurwitz theory is a branch of enumerative geometry that focuses on counting branched coverings of the sphere. Various generating functions in Hurwitz theory can be expressed, using monodromy and the representation theory of symmetric groups, as explicit infinite series involving Schur symmetric functions
[
CM16
]
. This perspective has proven very successful, revealing many beautiful connections between the somewhat forgotten Hurwitz theory and other modern fields such as Gromov–Witten theory, topological recursion, integrable probability, etc.
[
Oko00a
,
OP06
,
KL07
,
EMS11
,
GPH17
]
. A recurring technique that has turned out to be highly effective involves manipulating generating functions by constructing various operators that act on Schur symmetric functions in a controllable manner
[
Oko00b
,
OS00
,
GJ08
,
EMS11
,
BDBKS20
,
BCCGF24
]
.

Until very recently, several questions have remained open in the deformed setting, where Schur symmetric functions are replaced by their one-parameter deformation given by Jack symmetric functions. The operators acting in classical Hurwitz theory are usually constructed through nested adjoint actions, and a similar construction might be used in the deformed setting
[
Las08
]
. However, this approach does not yield an explicit formula for the operators. In
[
CD22
]
, an explicit formula for several such operators was found, yielding a positive formula for the representation of the generators of the algebra
\mathbf{SH}^{>}
introduced by Schiffmann and Vasserot
[
SV13
]
. This was crucial in discovering an enumerative interpretation of Jack-deformed a.k.a.
b
-deformed Hurwitz theory. This positive formula was later reinterpreted in terms of the so-called path operators, an idea that proved to be very fruitful, leading to the proof of Lassalle’s conjecture on the positivity of Jack characters
[
BDD23
]
and providing new insights into
b
-Hurwitz theory
[
Ruz23
,
CDO24a
,
CDO24b
,
BD25
]
. The simplest instance of path operators can be recognized as the trace of a matrix of operators, and such an operator had already appeared in the context of Jack symmetric functions in the work of Nazarov–Sklyanin
[
NS13
]
. It was later incorporated into the framework of path operators to prove global asymptotics of a large class of discrete
\beta
-ensembles
[
Mol23
,
CDM23
]
.

Nazarov and Sklyanin
[
NS15
]
also constructed analogous operators in another deformed case, where Schur symmetric functions are replaced by Macdonald symmetric functions. This deformation consists of two parameters,
q
and
t
, and its classical limit corresponds to the aforementioned Jack deformation. Motivated by their work and the various applications of path operators in the Jack-deformed case, we extend these ideas to the
(q,t)
-deformation. Here is a summary of our main results.

(1)

We construct a new class of operators acting on symmetric functions with two deformation parameters
q
and
t
. Our combinatorial construction associates an operator with a certain lattice path, whose steps alternate between going up and down. For any sequence of integers
\beta\in\mathbb{Z}^{\ell}
, we define an operator
\mathcal{R}_{\beta}
using an explicit formula given by a sum of path operators lying over a special lattice path associated with
\beta
. We demonstrate that this operator is the image of the element
D_{\beta}
from the shuffle algebra, constructed by Negut
[
Neg14
]
, through the representation of the shuffle algebra on symmetric functions.

(2)

We find a monomial, elementary, and Schur expansion of the symmetric function
\mathcal{R}_{\beta^{(n)}}\dotsm\mathcal{R}_{\beta^{(1)}}\cdot 1
, constructed by the repeated action of path operators on
1
.

(3)

We define certain formal series that we call
the
(q,t)
-tau function
, a natural
(q,t)
-deformation of the tau functions for the
G
-weighted
b
-Hurwitz numbers of
[
CD22
]
. Various special cases of this function have appeared as important generating series in enumerative geometry and mathematical physics, such as in the context of mixed Hodge polynomials of character varieties of the Riemann sphere
[
HLRV11
]
, the celebrated AGT conjecture
[
Yan16
]
, and in the recent work of Bourgine and Garbali
[
BG24
]
, as a tau function of a
(q,t)
-extension of the 2D Toda hierarchy. We prove that the
(q,t)
-tau function satisfies a system of partial differential equations (PDEs) of a combinatorial nature built from our path operators. Moreover, we demonstrate these equations uniquely determine the
(q,t)
-tau function.

(4)

We apply our techniques to provide a new, independent proof of the key result
[
BHM+23a
, Theorem 4.4.1]
in proving the extended delta conjecture of Haglund, Remmel and Wilson
[
HRW18
]
. We believe that this approach may be used in the future to tackle related open problems in the field.

1.1.
Path operators

Let
N=(x_{N},y_{N})
be a point of
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{Z}
. We call
a path

\gamma
from the origin
(0,0)
to
N
, a sequence of points
(w_{0},w_{1},\dotsc,w_{x_{N}})
in
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{Z}
such that
w_{j}=(j,y_{j})
, with
w_{0}=(0,0)
and
w_{x_{N}}=N
. Such a path is uniquely encoded by its sequence of
steps

\gamma_{j}\coloneqq y_{j}-y_{j-1}\in\mathbb{Z}
. The
length
of
\gamma
is
x_{N}
and its
degree
is
y_{N}
.

We say that a path
\gamma=(\gamma_{1},\dotsc,\gamma_{2n})
from
(0,0)
to
N
is
alternating
,
if
\gamma_{2j-1}\geq 0
and
\gamma_{2j}\leq 0
for any
0\leq j\leq n
.
In other terms, odd steps are up steps and even steps are down steps,
with the convention that a flat step is considered either an up or a down
step depending on its parity.

We say that a point
V=(x_{V},y_{V})
of
\gamma
is a
valley
of
\gamma
if
x_{V}
is even. This means that
V
is preceded by a down step and followed by an up step, or
V
is the start or the end point of the path. Similarly, we say that
P=(x_{P},y_{P})
is a
peak
if
x_{P}
is odd.

For
\beta=(\beta_{1},\dotsc,\beta_{\ell})\in\mathbb{Z}^{\ell}
, let
\gamma_{\beta}
be the alternating path of length
2\ell
such that for
i=1,\dotsc,\ell
, its steps are

\gamma_{2i-1}=\max(0,\beta_{i}),\qquad\gamma_{2i}=\min(0,\beta_{i}).

Equivalently, for
\beta_{i}\geq 0
(resp.
\beta_{i}\leq 0
) the
i
-th up step is
\beta_{i}
(resp. is horizontal) and the
i
-th down step is horizontal (resp. is
\beta_{i}
).

We denote
\mathbf{R}_{\beta}
the set of alternating paths that start and end at the same points as
\gamma_{\beta}
(i.e. have the same length and degree) and that stay weakly above
\gamma_{\beta}
. Notice that the valleys of
\gamma_{\beta}
have
y
-coordinates
y_{2j}=\sum_{i=1}^{j}\beta_{i}
for
j=0,\dotsc,\ell
.

For
\gamma\in\mathbf{R}_{\beta}
and
j=0,\dotsc,\ell
, let the
\beta
-height
of a valley
V=(2j,y_{2j})
be

(1)

0pt_{\beta}(V)=y_{2j}-\sum_{i=1}^{j}\beta_{i}.

By definition of
\mathbf{R}_{\beta}
, one has
0pt_{\beta}(V)\geq 0
for all valleys
V
of
\gamma
. Moreover, the start and the end point of
\gamma
always have
\beta
-height zero.

\gamma

We denote by
\Lambda
the vector space of symmetric functions over the fraction field
\mathbb{Q}(q,t)
in an alphabet of variables
X:=x_{1}+x_{2}+\dotsm
.

Definition 1.2
.

Let
\gamma\in\mathbf{R}_{\beta}
be an alternating
path. We associate to
\gamma
the
valley weight

(2)

\textup{vw}_{\beta}(\gamma)=\prod_{V\in\mathcal{V}(\gamma)}(qt)^{0pt_{\beta}(V)},

the product being taken over the valleys of
\gamma
. We associate to
\gamma
the linear operator
\mathcal{O}(\gamma)\colon\Lambda\rightarrow\Lambda
as follows: if
\gamma
has steps
(\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{2l})
then

\mathcal{O}_{\beta}(\gamma):=\textup{vw}_{\beta}(\gamma)\mathcal{O}(\gamma_{1})\dots\mathcal{O}(\gamma_{2l}),

where for any integer
m
we define

(3)

\mathcal{O}(m):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(-1)^{m}e_{m}[X]=h_{m}[-X]&\text{ if }m>0\\
h^{\perp}_{-m}[MX]&\text{ if }m<0\\
1&\text{ if }m=0,\end{array}\right.

with
M:=(1-q)(1-t)
.
Finally, we define

\mathcal{R}_{\beta}:=\sum_{\gamma\in\mathbf{R}_{\beta}}\mathcal{O}_{\beta}\left(\gamma\right).

We use a standard notation where square brackets
[\cdot]
denote the plethystic substitution, the operator
e_{m}
is to be understood as the multiplication by the symmetric function
e_{m}
, and similarly
h_{m}^{\perp}
denotes the adjoint of the multiplication by
h_{m}
with respect to the Hall scalar product; see
Section

2.1
for more details.

Remark 1.3
.

Since the set
\mathbf{R}_{\beta}
is infinite, it might not be immediately clear that
\mathcal{R}_{\beta}\colon\Lambda\rightarrow\Lambda
is a well-defined endomorphism of
\Lambda
. It is indeed well-defined since for any
f\in\Lambda
there are only finitely many paths
\gamma\in\mathbf{R}_{\beta}
such that
f\notin\ker(\mathcal{O}_{\beta}(\gamma))
. This is a consequence of the property that for any sequence
(\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{\ell})\in\mathbb{Z}^{\ell}
such that the partial sum
\sum_{i=j}^{\ell}\gamma_{i}
is smaller than
-m
for some
1\leq j\leq\ell
, the operator
\mathcal{O}(\gamma_{1})\cdots\mathcal{O}(\gamma_{\ell})
annihilates the space
\Lambda^{i}
of homogeneous symmetric functions of degree
i\leq m
:

\bigoplus_{i=0}^{m}\Lambda^{i}\subset\ker\left(\mathcal{O}(\gamma_{1})\cdots\mathcal{O}(\gamma_{\ell})\right).

1.2.
Product formula in terms of
D
-operators and connection to the shuffle algebra

We prove that the path operators
\mathcal{R}_{\beta}
have a simple expression using the vertex operator algebra (VOA) formalism involving the series
D(z)
introduced in
[
GHT99
]
. As a corollary we deduce that the path operators are the images of the so-called Negut elements via a representation of the shuffle algebra acting on the space of symmetric functions. Consequently, we find a new explicit combinatorial formula for this representation of the Negut elements.

We say that a formal expression

F(z):=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}a_{n}z^{n}\in\End[\Lambda]\llbracket z,z^{-1}\rrbracket

is a
field
if for any
f\in\Lambda
, there exists
N
such that
f\in\ker(a_{n})
for all
n<N
(see
[
Kac17
]
). It is well-known (one can use a similar reasoning as in
1.3
) that whenever
F_{1},\dots,F_{\ell}
are fields, then the coefficients of the following Laurent series in
z_{1},\dots,z_{\ell}
are well-defined endomorphisms of
\Lambda
:

\frac{F_{1}(z_{1})\dotsm F_{\ell}(z_{\ell})}{\prod_{i=1}^{\ell-1}\bigl(1-z_{i+1}/z_{i}\bigr)}\in\End[\Lambda]\llbracket z_{1},z_{1}^{-1},\dots,z_{\ell},z_{\ell}^{-1}\rrbracket,\quad\bigl(1-z_{i+1}/z_{i}\bigr)^{-1}:=\sum_{n\geq 0}z_{i+1}^{n}\cdot z_{i}^{-n}.

We recall (see
Section

2.1
for more details) that the field
D(z)
is defined as follows:

D(z):=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}D_{n}z^{n}=\sum_{n\geq 0}(-1)^{n}e_{n}[X]z^{n}\cdot\sum_{n\geq 0}h_{n}^{\perp}[(1-q)(1-t)X]z^{-n}=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{R}_{(n)}z^{n},

and it satisfies the following commutation relation:

(4)

\omega(z_{1}/z_{2})D(z_{1})D(z_{2})=\omega(z_{2}/z_{1})D(z_{2})D(z_{1}),\quad\omega(x):=\frac{(1-x)(1-qtx)}{(1-qx)(1-tx)}.

Following this formalism, we prove the following VOA expression for the path operators.

Theorem 1.5
.

Fix
\beta=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{\ell})\in\mathbb{Z}^{\ell}
, then

(5)

\mathcal{R}_{\beta}=[z_{1}^{\beta_{1}}\dotsm z_{\ell}^{\beta_{\ell}}]\frac{D(z_{1})\dotsm D(z_{\ell})}{\prod_{i=1}^{\ell-1}\bigl(1-qtz_{i+1}/z_{i}\bigr)}.

The shuffle algebra
S

[
FT11
,
Neg14
]
is the graded algebra
S=\oplus_{n\geq 0}S_{n}
where
S_{0}=\mathbb{Q}(q,t)
, and for
n\geq 1
an element
F
of
S_{n}
is of the form

F(z_{1},\dotsc,z_{n})=f(z_{1},\dotsc,z_{n})\prod_{1\leq i\neq j\leq n}\frac{z_{i}-z_{j}}{(z_{i}-qz_{j})(z_{i}-tz_{j})},

where
f(z_{1},\dotsc,z_{n})\in\mathbb{Q}(q,t)[z_{1},z_{1}^{-1},\dotsc,z_{n},z_{n}^{-1}]^{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}
is a symmetric Laurent polynomial which satisfies the
wheel condition

f(z_{1},\dotsc,z_{n})=0\qquad\text{whenever}\quad\left\{\frac{z_{1}}{z_{2}},\frac{z_{2}}{z_{3}},\frac{z_{3}}{z_{1}}\right\}=\left\{q,t,\frac{1}{qt}\right\}.

In particular,
S_{1}=\mathbb{Q}(q,t)[z_{1},z_{1}^{-1}]
.

The shuffle algebra
S
is equipped with the following product: for
F\in S_{n},G\in S_{m}
, then
F*G\in S_{n+m}
with

(F*G)(z_{1},\dotsc,z_{n+m})=\frac{1}{n!m!}\operatorname{Sym}\left(F(z_{1},\dotsc,z_{n})G(z_{n+1},\dotsc,z_{n+m})\prod_{i=1}^{n}\prod_{j=n+1}^{n+m}\omega(z_{j}/z_{i})\right),

where
\operatorname{Sym}
denotes the symmetrization with respect to the variables
z_{1},\dotsc,z_{n+m}
.

We will use the following action of the shuffle algebra on the ring of symmetric functions. We refer to
[
FHH+09
,
FT11
]
for more details.

Definition 1.6
.

The shuffle algebra
S
acts on
\Lambda
through the following representation
F\mapsto\hat{F}

which maps
F\in S_{n}
to an operator
\hat{F}\in\End(\Lambda)
and defined on the generators
z^{a}
by

\widehat{z^{a}}=D_{-a}=[z^{0}]z^{a}D(z),\quad\forall a\in\mathbb{Z}.

It follows then from
Eq.

4
that for any
F\in S
,

\hat{F}:=[z_{1}^{0}\dotsm z_{n}^{0}]F(z_{1},\dotsc,z_{n})\frac{D(z_{1})\dotsm D(z_{n})}{\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\omega(z_{j}/z_{i})}.

In
[

]
, Negut introduced specific elements

X_{\beta}(z_{1},\dotsc,z_{\ell}):=\operatorname{Sym}\left(\frac{z_{1}^{-\beta_{1}}\dotsm z_{\ell}^{-\beta_{\ell}}}{\prod_{i=1}^{\ell-1}(1-qtz_{i+1}/z_{i})}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq\ell}\omega(z_{j}/z_{i})\right)\quad\in S_{\ell},

for
\beta=(\beta_{1},\dotsc,\beta_{\ell})\in\mathbb{Z}^{\ell}
. Using the relations of
Eq.

4
of the

coincides with the RHS of

Corollary 1.7
.

Fix
\beta=(\beta_{1},\dotsc,\beta_{\ell})\in\mathbb{Z}^{\ell}
. Then we have

For any partition

1.3.
The

-tau function

Consider two infinite sequences of variables
U=u_{1},u_{2},\dotsc

Let

Let
G(\hbar):=G_{1}(\hbar)\cdot G_{2}^{-1}(\hbar)\in\mathbb{Q}[U,V]\llbracket\hbar\rrbracket

. The notation

•

2.1.
Preliminaries

Let
\Lambda
denote the algebra of symmetric functions in the alphabet
X=x_{1}+x_{2}+\dotsb
with coefficients in
\mathbb{Q}(q,t)
. We denote the classical bases such as monomial, elementary, homogeneous, power-sum, and Schur symmetric functions by
m_{\lambda},e_{\lambda},h_{\lambda},p_{\lambda},s_{\lambda}
, where
\lambda
is an integer partition, which we denote by
\lambda\in\mathbb{Y}
. The
dominance order

\leq
is the partial order on
\mathbb{Y}
defined by

\mu\leq\lambda\iff|\mu|=|\lambda|\text{ and }\hskip 8.5359pt\mu_{1}+...+\mu_{i}\leq\lambda_{1}+...+\lambda_{i}\text{ for }i\geq 1.

For
i\geq 1
, we denote
m_{i}(\lambda)
the number of parts of size
i
in
\lambda
. We then set

z_{\lambda}:=\prod_{i\geq 1}m_{i}(\lambda)!i^{m_{i}(\lambda)}.

The
Hall scalar product

\langle.,.\rangle
on
\Lambda
is defined by

\langle p_{\mu},p_{\nu}\rangle=\delta_{\mu,\nu}z_{\mu},\qquad\text{for any $\mu,\nu\in\mathbb{Y}$,}

where
\delta_{\mu,\nu}
is the Kronecker delta. We also have
\langle m_{\mu},h_{\nu}\rangle=\delta_{\mu,\nu}
, for
\mu,\nu\in\mathbb{Y}
. If
f\in\Lambda
, then we denote
f^{\perp}
the dual of the multiplication by
f
, with respect to this scalar product.

For any symmetric function
f\in\Lambda
and an expression
A
involving indeterminates, we define the plethystic substitution
f[A]
by first defining it on the basis of power-sum symmetric functions
p_{k}[A]
by the substitution
a\mapsto a^{k}
for each indeterminate
a
occurring in
A
, and then extending on the whole algebra of symmetric functions so that
f\mapsto f[A]
is a homomorphism. For instance
p_{k}[X]:=\sum_{i\geq 1}x_{i}^{k}
and

\displaystyle p_{k}[aX]

\displaystyle=a^{k}p_{k}[X]\quad\text{for $a\in\{q,t\}$}

\displaystyle p_{k}[cX]

\displaystyle=cp_{k}[X]\quad\text{for $c\in\mathbb{Q}$}.

An expression we will encounter often is
M:=(1-q)(1-t)\in\mathbb{Q}(q,t)
and hence
p_{k}[MX]=(1-q^{k})(1-t^{k})p_{k}[X]
.

For an expression A, define the associated
plethystic exponential
by

\Omega[A]:=\sum_{n\geq 0}h_{n}[A]=e^{\sum_{i>0}\frac{p_{i}[A]}{i}}.

In particular, the Cauchy and dual Cauchy kernels are respectively given by

\Omega[X]=\sum_{n\geq 0}h_{n}[X]=\prod_{k\geq 1}\frac{1}{1-x_{k}},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Omega[-X]=\sum_{n\geq 0}(-1)^{n}e_{n}[X]=\prod_{k\geq 1}(1-x_{k}).

Following
[
GHT99
]
, we consider the operators that act on
f[X]\in\Lambda
like

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{Y}f[X]

\displaystyle=\Omega[XY]f[X],

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{Y}f[X]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 0}h^{\perp}_{k}[Y]f[X]=f[X+Y].

We have the following commutation relations between operators acting on

\Lambda_{X}
(see
[
GHT99
, Theorem 1.1 and Proposition 1.2]
):

(9)

\mathcal{T}_{Y}\cdot\mathcal{P}_{Z}=\Omega[YZ]\mathcal{P}_{Z}\cdot\mathcal{T}_{Y},

An important role in the theory of Macdonald polynomials is played by the operators
D_{k}
,
k\in\mathbb{Z}
from
[
GHT99
]
. They are defined by the following equality of formal series

D(z)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}D_{k}z^{k}=\mathcal{P}_{-z}\mathcal{T}_{M/z}.

Since
\mathcal{P}_{-z}=\sum_{n\geq 0}(-1)^{n}e_{n}[X]z^{n}
and
\mathcal{T}_{M/z}=e^{\sum_{i>0}\frac{z^{-i}}{i}p_{i}^{\perp}[MX]}=\sum_{n\geq 0}h_{n}^{\perp}[MX]z^{-n}
, it comes that

(10)

D_{k}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m,n\geq 0\\
m-n=k\end{subarray}}(-1)^{m}e_{m}[X]h_{n}^{\perp}[MX].

In particular,

is equal to the path operator
\mathcal{R}_{(k)}
defined in
1.2
:

(11)

\mathcal{R}_{(k)}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m,n\geq 0\\
m-n=k\end{subarray}}\mathcal{O}(m)\mathcal{O}(-n)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m,n\geq 0\\
m-n=k\end{subarray}}(-1)^{m}e_{m}[X]h_{n}^{\perp}[MX]=D_{k}.

Fix a sequence
\beta=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{\ell})\in\mathbb{Z}^{\ell}

has steps

for

\gamma

and the set of corresponding paths is denoted

. The valley weights of those paths, as defined in

, only depend on

, so we define

\textup{vw}_{\beta}(r_{1},\dots,r_{\ell})\coloneqq\textup{vw}_{\beta}(\gamma)=\prod_{1\leq j\leq\ell}(qt)^{\sum_{1\leq i\leq j}(r_{i}-\beta_{i})}.

for any path
\gamma\in\mathbf{R}_{\beta}(r_{1},\dotsc,r_{\ell})
.

.

For any

We start from

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\beta}

\displaystyle=\sum_{(r_{1},\dotsc,r_{\ell})\in\mathcal{VI}(\beta)}\sum_{\gamma\in\mathbf{R}_{\beta}(r_{1},\dotsc,r_{\ell})}\textup{vw}_{\beta}(\gamma)\mathcal{O}(\gamma_{1})\dotsm\mathcal{O}(\gamma_{2\ell})

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma_{2j-1}\geq 0,\gamma_{2j}\leq 0\\
\gamma_{2j-1}+\gamma_{2j}=r_{j}\end{subarray}}\mathcal{O}(\gamma_{2j-1})\mathcal{O}(\gamma_{2j})=D_{r_{j}}=[z^{r_{j}}]D(z).\qed

We will use the following straightforward lemma.

Lemma 2.3

Let

3.1.

Recall (see
[
GHT99

) that a ring of symmetric functions
\Lambda
on the alphabet

possesses a distinguished basis of the so-called modified Macdonald polynomials
\widetilde{H}^{(q,t)}_{\lambda}[X]
. We will need two well-known properties of modified Macdonald polynomials.

Theorem 3.1
.

We have the following:

(i)

Pieri Rule
[
Mac95
]
:
There exist coefficients
d^{\mu,\lambda}\in\mathbb{Q}(q,t)
such that

-e_{1}[X]\widetilde{H}^{(q,t)}_{\mu}=\sum_{\mu\nearrow\lambda}d^{\mu,\lambda}\widetilde{H}^{(q,t)}_{\lambda}

where the sum is taken over partitions
\lambda
obtained from
\mu
by adding exactly one cell.

(ii)

Macdonald operator
D_{0}

[
GHT99
, Theorem 1.2]
:
The modified Macdonald polynomials are eigenfunctions of the operator
D_{0}=[z^{0}]D(z)
defined in (
10
)

D_{0}\cdot\widetilde{H}^{(q,t)}_{\lambda}=\biggl(1-M\sum_{(i,j)\in\lambda}q^{j-1}t^{i-1}\biggr)\widetilde{H}^{(q,t)}_{\lambda},\quad\text{for any $\lambda\in\mathbb{Y}$,}

where the sum is taken over all cells
(i,j)
of the Young diagram
\lambda
.

Recall that the Macdonald Cauchy kernel can be written as follows

\Omega\left[-\frac{XY}{M}\right]=\exp\left(\sum_{i\geq 1}\frac{p_{i}[X]p_{i}[Y]}{ip_{i}[-M]}\right)=\sum_{\mu\in\mathbb{Y}}\frac{p_{\mu}[X]p_{\mu}[Y]}{z_{\mu}p_{\mu}[-M]},

which motivates the definition of the
star scalar product

\langle.,.\rangle_{*}
as the deformation of the Hall scalar product given by

\langle p_{\mu},p_{\nu}\rangle_{*}=z_{\mu}\delta_{\mu,\nu}p_{\mu}[-M]=(-1)^{\ell(\mu)}z_{\mu}\delta_{\mu,\nu}\prod_{1\leq i\leq\ell(\mu)}\left(1-q^{\mu_{i}}\right)\left(1-t^{\mu_{i}}\right),\quad\text{for any $\mu,\nu\in\mathbb{Y}$.}

Modified Macdonald polynomials are orthogonal with respect to the star scalar product:

(18)

\langle\widetilde{H}^{(q,t)}_{\lambda},\widetilde{H}^{(q,t)}_{\rho}\rangle_{*}=\delta_{\lambda,\rho}\left\|\widetilde{H}^{(q,t)}_{\lambda}\right\|^{2}_{*},

we refer to
[
Mac95
, Chapter VI]
for a combinatorial formula of the squared norm
\left\|\widetilde{H}^{(q,t)}_{\lambda}\right\|^{2}_{*}
. The following Cauchy formula then holds:

(19)

\Omega\left[-\frac{XY}{M}\right]=\sum_{\lambda\in\mathbb{Y}}\frac{\widetilde{H}^{(q,t)}_{\lambda}[X]\widetilde{H}^{(q,t)}_{\lambda}[Y]}{\left\|\widetilde{H}^{(q,t)}_{\lambda}\right\|^{2}_{*}}.

Recall that we denote
\Lambda^{\mathbb{K}}_{A}
the algebra of symmetric functions in the alphabet
A=X,Y
with coefficients in
\mathbb{K}:=\mathbb{Q}(q,t)\llbracket U,V\rrbracket
, where
U=u_{1},u_{2},\dotsc
and
V=v_{1},v_{2},\dotsc
are two infinite sequences of variables. Then the
G
-weighted
(q,t)
-tau function is the series in
\Lambda^{\mathbb{K}}_{X}\otimes\Lambda^{\mathbb{K}}_{Y}\llbracket z\rrbracket
defined by:

\tau_{G}(z,X,Y):=\sum_{\lambda\in\mathbb{Y}}z^{|\lambda|}\frac{\widetilde{H}^{(q,t)}_{\lambda}[X]\widetilde{H}^{(q,t)}_{\lambda}[Y]}{\left\|\widetilde{H}^{(q,t)}_{\lambda}\right\|^{2}_{*}}\prod_{(i,j)\in\lambda}G(q^{j-1}t^{i-1}),

where

G_{1}(\hbar):=1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}u_{n}\hbar^{n}\in\mathbb{Q}[U,V]\llbracket\hbar\rrbracket,\quad G_{2}(\hbar):=1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}v_{n}\hbar^{n}\in\mathbb{Q}[U,V]\llbracket\hbar\rrbracket,

and
G(q^{j}t^{i}):=\frac{G_{1}(q^{j}t^{i})}{G_{2}(q^{j}t^{i})}\in\mathbb{K}
.

There is an equivalent way of defining the
G

3.4.

Recall that for any

we denote

the set of alternating paths that start and end at the same points as
\gamma_{\beta}
(i.e. have the same length and degree) and that stay weakly above

From now on, we will be working with sequences

and we will use an alternative description of path operators as

: we think of a path
\gamma\in\mathbf{R}_{\beta}
as an alternating path starting at

ending at
(2\ell,\sum_{1\leq i\leq\ell}\beta_{i})
and such that:

•

•

-coordinate is at least the number of particles sitting on peaks on the left of

\beta
-height of

(as defined in

) is given by the difference between these two integers.

Notice that the condition
\beta\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}\times\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{\ell-1}
implies that there is always at least one particle on the first peak.

We are now ready to give our second parametrization of path operators. If
\beta
is a sequence of size
n
, we define
\alpha:=\psi(\beta)\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n}
as the sequence consisting of the distances between consecutive particles of a path
\gamma\in\mathbf{R}_{\beta}
, where the distance is interpreted as the number of peaks between the particles. By convention,
\alpha_{n}
is the number of peaks after the last particle. We refer to
Fig.

2
for an example.

Equivalently,
\alpha=\psi(\beta)
is the sequence obtained from
\beta
as follows: we start from the empty sequence
\alpha=\emptyset
, and we read the sequence
\beta
from left to right; each time we read an integer

we add a sequence of
i

\alpha
by 1, except at the end of the procedure – in which case we only add a sequence of
i
zeroes. We refer to
Fig.

2
for an example.

Figure 2

A path
\gamma

\mathbf{R}_{(1,2,0,1)}

on the left. On the right we visualize vector

by placing particles over the peaks, and we reparametrize the set
\mathbf{R}_{(1,2,0,1)}
as
\mathbf{Q}_{(1,0,2,0)}

This defines a bijection

between sequences in

For any

For any

Informally,

We start by proving that

is a basis when

•

and

whose

It turns out that commuting one particle operators with

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
\gamma=(\gamma_{1},\dots\gamma_{2n})\in\mathbf{Q}_{(n-1)}=\mathbf{R}_{(1,0^{n-1})}
. Then

\ad_{\frac{D_{0}}{-M}}\left(\mathcal{O}_{(1,0^{n-1})}(\gamma)\right)=\sum_{1\leq i\leq 2n}\sum_{\gamma\nearrow_{i}\gamma^{\prime}}\left([y_{V^{\prime}}]_{(qt)^{-1}}-[y_{V}]_{(qt)^{-1}}\right)\mathcal{O}_{(1,0^{n})}(\gamma^{\prime}),

where the second sum is taken over all paths
\gamma^{\prime}
obtained by extending the
i
-th step of
\gamma
, and where
\mathcal{V}(\gamma\nearrow_{i}\gamma^{\prime})=(V,V^{\prime}).

Proof.

Fix
r>0
. Then by
4.2
, we have

\displaystyle\left[D_{0},\mathcal{O}(r)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 0}\Big[\mathcal{O}(k)\cdot\mathcal{O}(-k),\mathcal{O}(r)\Big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 0}\mathcal{O}(k)\cdot\Big[\mathcal{O}(-k),\mathcal{O}(r)\Big]

\displaystyle=-M\cdot\sum_{k\geq 0}\sum_{1\leq i\leq k\wedge r}\left[i\right]_{qt}\mathcal{O}(k)\cdot\mathcal{O}(-(k-i))\mathcal{O}(r-i).

Similarly if
r<0
, we have

\displaystyle\left[D_{0},\mathcal{O}(r)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 0}\Big[\mathcal{O}(k)\cdot\mathcal{O}(-k),\mathcal{O}(r)\Big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 0}\Big[\mathcal{O}(k),\mathcal{O}(r)\Big]\mathcal{O}(-k)

\displaystyle=-M\cdot\sum_{k\geq 0}\sum_{1\leq i\leq k\wedge-r}[-i]_{qt}\mathcal{O}(r+i)\cdot\mathcal{O}(k-i)\cdot\mathcal{O}(-k).

Note that the summands in these equations correspond precisely to the possible ways of extending a step of degree
r
in an alternating path as in
4.4
. More precisely, we have

\displaystyle\ad_{\frac{D_{0}}{-M}}\left(\mathcal{O}_{(1,0^{n-1})}(\gamma)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{-1}{M}\sum_{1\leq i\leq 2n}\textup{vw}_{(1,0^{n-1})}(\gamma)\mathcal{O}(\gamma_{1})\dots\mathcal{O}(\gamma_{i-1})[D_{0},\mathcal{O}(\gamma_{i})]\mathcal{O}(\gamma_{i+1})\dots\mathcal{O}(\gamma_{2n})

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq i\leq 2n}\sum_{\gamma\nearrow_{i}\gamma^{\prime}}[y_{V^{\prime}}-y_{V}]_{qt}\textup{vw}_{(1,0^{n-1})}(\gamma)\mathcal{O}(\gamma^{\prime}_{1})\dots\mathcal{O}(\gamma^{\prime}_{2n+2}).

Notice that

\textup{vw}_{(1,0^{n})}(\gamma^{\prime})=(qt)^{0pt_{(1,0^{n})}(W)}\textup{vw}_{(1,0^{n-1})}(\gamma),

where
W

is the valley with the larger height among
(V,V^{\prime}):=\mathcal{V}(\gamma\nearrow_{i}\gamma^{\prime})
. As a consequence,

\textup{vw}_{(1,0^{n})}(\gamma^{\prime})=(qt)^{\max(0pt_{(1,0^{n})}(V),0pt_{(1,0^{n})}(V^{\prime}))}\textup{vw}_{(1,0^{n-1})}(\gamma)=(qt)^{\max(y_{V},y_{V^{\prime}})-1}\textup{vw}_{(1,0^{n-1})}(\gamma).

We then have

\displaystyle\ad_{\frac{D_{0}}{-M}}\left(\mathcal{O}_{(1,0^{n-1})}(\gamma)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq i\leq 2n}\sum_{\gamma\nearrow_{i}\gamma^{\prime}}[y_{V^{\prime}}-y_{V}]_{qt}(qt)^{1-\max(y_{V},y_{V^{\prime}})}\textup{vw}_{(1,0^{n})}(\gamma^{\prime})\mathcal{O}(\gamma^{\prime}_{1})\dots\mathcal{O}(\gamma^{\prime}_{2n+2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq i\leq 2n}\sum_{\gamma\nearrow_{i}\gamma^{\prime}}[y_{V^{\prime}}-y_{V}]_{qt}(qt)^{1-\max(y_{V},y_{V^{\prime}})}\mathcal{O}_{(1,0^{n})}(\gamma^{\prime}).

We conclude using the fact that

[y_{V^{\prime}}-y_{V}]_{qt}(qt)^{1-\max(y_{V},y_{V^{\prime}})}=[y_{V^{\prime}}]_{(qt)^{-1}}-[y_{V}]_{(qt)^{-1}}.\qed

4.3.
Second commutation relation

We now prove a second commutation relation allowing us to obtain path operators with
\ell
particles from path operators with
\ell-1
particles.
Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{\ell}
, and define the symmetrized operator:

\mathcal{Q}^{\textup{sym}}_{\alpha}:=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{\ell}}\mathcal{Q}_{\sigma(\alpha)},

where we denote the sequence
\sigma(\alpha):=(\alpha_{\sigma(1)},\dots,\alpha_{\sigma(\ell))})
. Let us introduce two simple operations on vectors
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{\ell}
: for
1\leq i\leq\ell
, we denote

•

\alpha^{\downarrow i}:=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{i-1},\alpha_{i+1},\dots,\alpha_{\ell}),

•

\alpha^{+i}:=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{i-1},\alpha_{i}+1,\alpha_{i+1},\dots,\alpha_{\ell}).

The main result of this section is the following recursive formula for the operators
\mathcal{Q}^{\textup{sym}}_{\alpha}
.

Theorem 4.8

(Second commutation relation)
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{\ell}
be a sequence of non-negative integers of length
\ell\geq 2
. We then have

\mathcal{Q}^{\textup{sym}}_{\alpha}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{1\leq i\leq\ell}\left[\ad^{\alpha_{i}}_{\frac{D_{0}}{-M}}\left(-e_{1}[X]\right),\mathcal{Q}^{\textup{sym}}_{\alpha^{\downarrow i}}\right].

Remark 4.9
.

we know from the first commutation relation (
4.3
), that if
\alpha_{i}>0
then

\ad^{\alpha_{i}}_{\frac{D_{0}}{-M}}\left(-e_{1}[X]\right)=\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha_{i}-1}.

Intuitively, one can then think of the second commutation relation as follows: the symmetrized operator
\mathcal{Q}^{\textup{sym}}_{\alpha}
is obtained by removing (in all possible ways) a part
\alpha_{i}
from
\alpha
, and then commuting the operator obtained with
\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha_{i}-1}/M
.

.

Fix two integers
m\geq 0,\ell\geq 1
, and let
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{\ell}
be a sequence of non-negative integers. Then

\frac{1}{-M}\left[\ad^{m}_{\frac{D_{0}}{-M}}\left(-e_{1}[X]\right),\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}\right]=\sum_{1\leq i\leq\ell-1}\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{i},m,\alpha_{i+1},\dots,\alpha_{\ell}}\\
+\sum_{1\leq i\leq\ell}\textup{sign}(m-\alpha_{i}-1)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r_{1},r_{2}\geq m\wedge(\alpha_{i}+1)\\
{r_{1}+r_{2}=m+\alpha_{i}}\end{subarray}}\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{i-1},r_{1},r_{2},\alpha_{i+1},\dots,\alpha_{\ell}}.

Proof.

We start by proving the case
m=0
and then we use
4.13
to
prove by induction the proposition for any
m
.

Let us consider the following sum

\sum_{1\leq i\leq\ell}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r_{1},r_{2}\geq 0\\
{r_{1}+r_{2}=\alpha_{i}}\end{subarray}}\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{i-1},r_{1},r_{2},\alpha_{i+1},\dots,\alpha_{\ell}}.

We can interpret it combinatorially as follows: for each
1\leq i\leq\ell
we add a new particle between two old particles
separated by
\alpha_{i}
peaks in all possible places, including a
particle over the first old particle, and a
particle below the second old particle, which gives
\alpha_{i}+1
possible
places. This gives in total
\sum_{1\leq i\leq\ell}(\alpha_{i}+1)=|\alpha|+\ell
possible positions.
4.10
implies that
the commutation of
\mathcal{O}_{\psi^{-1}(\alpha)}(\gamma)
with
-e_{1}[X]/M
corresponds to
adding a new particle to all the peaks, which gives
|\alpha|+1

positions. The difference between these two interpretations comes from
the fact that in the first sum we always add a particle below the
second old particle separated by
\alpha_{i}
and over the first particle
separated by
\alpha_{i+1}
, which is the same particle. Therefore the
correct quantity corresponding to the
commutator
\left[\frac{e_{1}[X]}{M},\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}\right]
is equal
to the following correction of the first sum:

\displaystyle\left[\frac{e_{1}[X]}{M},\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}\right]=\sum_{1\leq i\leq\ell-1}\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{i},0,\alpha_{i+1},\dots,\alpha_{\ell}}-\sum_{1\leq i\leq\ell}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r_{1},r_{2}\geq 0\\
{r_{1}+r_{2}=\alpha_{i}}\end{subarray}}\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{i-1},r_{1},r_{2},\alpha_{i+1},\dots,\alpha_{\ell}}.

This finishes the proof of the proposition for
m=0
.

We now fix
m\geq 0
and we assume that the proposition holds for
m
. The Jacobi identity gives:

\displaystyle\left[\frac{\ad^{m+1}_{\frac{D_{0}}{-M}}\left(-e_{1}[X]\right)}{-M},\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}\right]=

\displaystyle\left[\left[\frac{D_{0}}{-M},\frac{\ad^{m}_{\frac{D_{0}}{-M}}\left(-e_{1}[X]\right)}{-M}\right],\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\frac{D_{0}}{-M},\left[\frac{\ad^{m}_{\frac{D_{0}}{-M}}\left(-e_{1}[X]\right)}{-M},\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}\right]\right]-\left[\frac{\ad^{m}_{\frac{D_{0}}{-M}}\left(-e_{1}[X]\right)}{-M},\left[\frac{D_{0}}{-M},\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}\right]\right].

Combining the recurrence assumption and
4.13
, we obtain

\frac{1}{-M}\left[\ad^{m}_{\frac{D_{0}}{-M}}\left(-e_{1}[X]\right),\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}\right]=\sum_{1\leq i\leq\ell-1}\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{i},m+1,\alpha_{j+1},\dots,\alpha_{\ell}}+\sum_{1\leq i\leq\ell}\textup{sign}(m-\alpha_{i}-1)\\
\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r_{1},r_{2}\geq m\wedge(\alpha_{i}+1)\\
{r_{1}+r_{2}=m+\alpha_{i}}\end{subarray}}\left(\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{i-1},r_{1}+1,r_{2},\alpha_{i+1},\dots,\alpha_{\ell}}+\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{i-1},r_{1},r_{2}+1,\alpha_{i+1},\dots,\alpha_{\ell}}\right)\\
-\sum_{1\leq i\leq\ell}\textup{sign}(m-\alpha_{i}-2)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r_{1},r_{2}\geq m\wedge(\alpha_{i}+2)\\
{r_{1}+r_{2}=m+\alpha_{i}+1}\end{subarray}}\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{i-1},r_{1},r_{2},\alpha_{i+1},\dots,\alpha_{\ell}}.

By distinguishing the four cases
\alpha_{i}>m-1
,
\alpha_{i}=m-1
,
\alpha_{i}=m-2
and
\alpha_{i}<m-2
, one check that we obtain the required formula:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{-M}\left[\ad^{m}_{\frac{D_{0}}{-M}}\left(-e_{1}[X]\right),\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}\right]=

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq i\leq\ell-1}\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{i},m+1,\alpha_{i+1},\dots,\alpha_{\ell}}

\displaystyle+\sum_{1\leq i\leq\ell}\textup{sign}(m-\alpha_{i})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r_{1},r_{2}\geq(m+1)\wedge(\alpha_{i}+1)\\
{r_{1}+r_{2}=m+1+\alpha_{i}}\end{subarray}}\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{i-1},r_{1},r_{2},\alpha_{i+1},\dots,\alpha_{\ell}}.

This finishes the proof of the proposition.
∎

Let us now prove
4.8
, which gives the second commutation relation announced at the beginning of the section.

Proof of
4.8
.

Fix
\ell\geq 2
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{\ell}
. We have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{M}\sum_{1\leq i\leq\ell}\left[\ad^{\alpha_{i}}_{\frac{D_{0}}{-M}}\left(-e_{1}[X]\right),\mathcal{Q}^{\textup{sym}}_{\alpha^{\downarrow i}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{1\leq i\leq\ell}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{\ell-1}}\left[\ad^{\alpha_{i}}_{\frac{D_{0}}{-M}}\left(-e_{1}[X]\right),\mathcal{Q}_{\sigma(\alpha^{\downarrow i})}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{\ell}}\left[\ad^{\alpha_{\sigma(\ell)}}_{\frac{D_{0}}{-M}}\left(-e_{1}[X]\right),\mathcal{Q}_{\sigma(\alpha^{\downarrow\ell})}\right].

Applying
4.15
, this is also equal to

-\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{\ell}}\sum_{1\leq j\leq\ell-2}\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha_{\sigma(1)},\dots,\alpha_{\sigma(j)},\alpha_{\sigma(\ell)},\alpha_{\sigma(j+1)},\dots,\alpha_{\sigma(\ell-1)}}\\
-\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{\ell}}\sum_{1\leq j\leq\ell-1}\textup{sign}\left(\alpha_{\sigma(\ell)}-1-\alpha_{\sigma(j)}\right)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r_{1},r_{2}\geq\alpha_{\sigma(\ell)}\wedge\left(\alpha_{\sigma(j)}+1\right)\\
{r_{1}+r_{2}=\alpha_{\sigma(\ell)}+\alpha_{\sigma(j)}}\end{subarray}}\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha_{\sigma(1)},\dots,\alpha_{\sigma(j-1)},r_{1},r_{2},\alpha_{\sigma(j+1)},\dots}.

The first term on the RHS gives

-\sum_{1\leq j\leq\ell-2}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{\ell}}\mathcal{Q}_{\sigma\cdot(\ell,\ell-1,\dots,j+1)(\alpha)}=-(\ell-2)\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{\ell}}\mathcal{Q}_{\sigma(\alpha)}=-(\ell-2)\mathcal{Q}^{\textup{sym}}_{\alpha},

where
(\ell,\ell-1,\dots,j+1)
is the permutation of
\mathfrak{S}_{\ell}
with cyclic notation.
Let us now compute the second term. For a fixed
1\leq j\leq\ell-1
,
define a bijection
\mathfrak{S}_{\ell}\rightarrow\mathfrak{S}_{\ell}
given by
\sigma\mapsto\sigma^{\prime}:=\sigma(j,\ell)
, where

(j,\ell)
is the transposition exchanging
j
and
\ell
. It is easy
to check that a contribution to the second term in the RHS that comes from

\sigma
and the associated
\sigma^{\prime}
is equal to

\mathcal{Q}_{\sigma\cdot(\ell,\ell-1,\dots,j+1)(\alpha)}+\mathcal{Q}_{\sigma\cdot(\ell,\ell-1,\dots,j+1,j)(\alpha)}.

As a consequence, the sum over
all permutations
\sigma
contributing to the second term on the RHS
with a fixed
1\leq j\leq\ell-1
is equal to
\mathcal{Q}^{\textup{sym}}_{\alpha}
, so
that the total contribution is equal to
(\ell-1)\mathcal{Q}^{\textup{sym}}_{\alpha}
. In conclusion,

\frac{1}{M}\sum_{1\leq i\leq\ell}\left[\ad^{\alpha_{i}}_{\frac{D_{0}}{-M}}\left(-e_{1}[X]\right),\mathcal{Q}^{\textup{sym}}_{\alpha^{\downarrow i}}\right]=\big(-(\ell-2)+(\ell-1)\big)\mathcal{Q}^{\textup{sym}}_{\alpha}=\mathcal{Q}^{\textup{sym}}_{\alpha}

as claimed.
∎

4.4.
Combinatorial formula in terms of path operators

We are ready to prove the combinatorial formula for the operators
\mathcal{A}_{F}^{(\ell)}
announced in
3.12
.

Proof of
3.12
.

We prove the theorem by induction. For
\ell=1
, we know from (
21
) and
4.3
, that

\mathcal{A}_{F}^{(1)}=a_{0}\mathcal{Q}_{0}+a_{1}\mathcal{Q}_{1}\dots

giving the theorem for
\ell=1
.

We now assume that (
25
) holds for some
\ell\geq 1
. We then have from (
21
) and the induction assumption

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{F}^{(\ell+1)}=\frac{1}{M}\left[\mathcal{A}_{F},\mathcal{A}_{F}^{(\ell)}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j\geq 0}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{\ell}}a_{j}a_{\alpha}\left[\ad^{j}_{\frac{D_{0}}{-M}}(e_{1}[X]),\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}\right],

where we use
4.3
to obtain the last equality. This can be written as follows

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{F}^{(\ell+1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{0\leq\nu_{1}\leq\dots\leq\nu_{\ell+1}}\frac{1}{|\Aut(\nu)|}a_{\nu}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{\ell+1}}\left[\ad^{\nu_{\sigma(\ell+1)}}_{\frac{D_{0}}{-M}}(e_{1}[X]),\mathcal{Q}_{\sigma(\nu^{\downarrow(\ell+1)})}\right],

where
\Aut(\nu)<\mathfrak{S}_{\ell+1}
is the stabilizer of the partition
\nu
.

Applying
4.8
, we get

\mathcal{A}_{F}^{(\ell+1)}=\sum_{0\leq\nu_{1}\leq\dots\leq\nu_{\ell+1}}\frac{1}{|\Aut(\nu)|}a_{\nu}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{\ell+1}}\mathcal{Q}_{\sigma(\nu)}=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{\ell+1}}a_{\alpha}\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}.

This finishes the proof of the theorem.
∎

5.
Applications: relation to the extended delta conjecture

Define the operators
\Delta_{h_{n}}
and
\Delta_{e_{n}}
by their generating series:

\Pi_{1+uz}=\sum_{n\geq 0}u^{n}\Delta_{e_{n}},\quad\Pi_{(1-vz)^{-1}}=\sum_{n\geq 0}v^{n}\Delta_{h_{n}}.

In particular, they act diagonally on the basis of transformed Macdonald polynomials.
Define also the operator

\Delta^{\prime}_{e_{n}}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}(-1)^{i}\Delta_{e_{n-i}}.

We prove that the symmetric function
\Delta_{h_{l}}\Delta_{e_{k-1}}\cdot e_{n}
, which is the main object of interest in the extended delta conjecture, has an explicit formula in terms of the action of the path operators on
1
. This result together with
2.6
is equivalent to the key result
[
BHM+23a
, Theorem 4.4.1]
in proving the extended delta conjecture. The proof in
[
BHM+23a
]
is based on the intermediate results describing the structure of the elliptic Hall algebra and its representation on
\Lambda
. We give a new proof that relies directly on combinatorial properties of path operators.

Theorem 5.1
.

Fix three integers
l\geq 0
and
0<k\leq n
, and define the function
F\in S_{k+l}

F:=\operatorname{Sym}\left(\frac{z_{1}\dotsm z_{k+l}}{\prod_{i=1}^{l+k-1}(1-qtz_{i+1}/z_{i})}h_{n-k}(z_{1},\dots,z_{k+l})e_{l}(z_{2}^{-1},\dots,z_{l+k}^{-1})\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq\ell}\omega(z_{j}/z_{i})\right).

We have

(34)

\displaystyle(-1)^{n}\Delta_{h_{l}}\Delta^{\prime}_{e_{k-1}}\cdot e_{n}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{k+l-1},\\
|\beta|=n-k\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{k+l-1},\\
|\beta^{\prime}|=l\end{subarray}}\mathcal{R}_{\beta+1^{k+l}-(0,\beta^{\prime})}\cdot 1=\widehat{F}\cdot 1,

where
\widehat{F}
is the operator on
\Lambda
given by the action of the shuffle algebra (see
1.6
).

Proof.

Notice that

(-1)^{n}e_{n}=D_{n}\cdot 1=\mathcal{Q}_{0^{n}}\cdot 1.

Therefore, we can rewrite
\Delta_{h_{l}}\Delta^{\prime}_{e_{k-1}}\cdot e_{n}
as follows:

(-1)^{n}\Delta_{h_{l}}\Delta^{\prime}_{e_{k-1}}\cdot e_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}(-1)^{i-1}[v^{l}u^{k-i}]\Pi_{\frac{1+uz}{1-vz}}\cdot\mathcal{Q}_{0^{n}}\cdot\left(\Pi_{\frac{1+uz}{1-vz}}\right)^{-1}\cdot 1.

Moreover, we know from
3.12
that
\mathcal{Q}_{0^{n}}=\mathcal{A}_{K}^{(n)}
, where
K(z)=1
is the constant polynomial.
3.5
implies that

\Pi_{G}\cdot\mathcal{A}_{K}^{(n)}\cdot\Pi_{G}^{-1}=\mathcal{A}_{KG}^{(n)}=\mathcal{A}_{G}^{(n)}.
We then get

(35)

(-1)^{n}\Delta_{h_{l}}\Delta^{\prime}_{e_{k-1}}\cdot e_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}(-1)^{i-1}[v^{l}u^{k-i}]\mathcal{A}_{\frac{1+uz}{1-vz}}^{(n)}\cdot 1.

By reapplying
3.12
, we have that

[v^{l}u^{k-i}]\mathcal{A}_{\frac{1+uz}{1-vz}}^{(n)}\cdot 1=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n},\\
|\alpha|=l\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{n},\\
|\alpha^{\prime}|=k-i\end{subarray}}\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha+\alpha^{\prime}}\cdot 1=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}\geq 0,\\
|\alpha|=l+k-i\end{subarray}}\binom{\ell(\alpha^{+})}{k-i}\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}}\cdot 1,

where we use the notation
\ell(\alpha^{+})
to denote the number of non-zero parts of
\alpha
. Recall the map
\psi
introduced in
Section

3.4
that relates the operators
\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}=\mathcal{R}_{\beta}
, where
\psi^{-1}(\alpha)=:\beta\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}\times\mathbb{Z}^{l+k-i}_{\geq 0}
for
\alpha
such that
|\alpha|=l+k-i
. Notice that

•

if
\alpha_{n}=0
, then
\ell(\alpha^{+})=\ell(\beta^{+})-1
, and
\beta_{l+k-i+1}>0
,

•

if
\alpha_{n}>0
, then
\ell(\alpha^{+})=\ell(\beta^{+})
, and
\beta_{l+k-i+1}=0
.

Therefore
[v^{l}u^{k-i}]\mathcal{A}_{\frac{1+uz}{1-vz}}^{(n)}\cdot 1
can be rewritten as follows:

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta_{1},\beta_{l+k-i+1}>0,\\
\beta_{2},\dots,\beta_{l+k-i}\geq 0,\\
|\beta|=n\end{subarray}}\binom{\ell(\beta^{+})-1}{k-i}\mathcal{R}_{\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{l+k-i+1}}\cdot 1+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta_{1}>0,\\
\beta_{2},\dots,\beta_{l+k-i}\geq 0,\\
|\beta|=n\end{subarray}}\binom{\ell(\beta^{+})}{k-i}\mathcal{R}_{\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{l+k-i},0}\cdot 1\\
=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta_{1}>0,\\
\beta_{2},\dots,\beta_{l+k-i+1}\geq 0,\\
|\beta|=n\end{subarray}}\binom{\ell(\beta^{+})-1}{k-i}\mathcal{R}_{\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{l+k-i+1}}\cdot 1+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta_{1}>0,\\
\beta_{2},\dots,\beta_{l+k-i}\geq 0,\\
|\beta|=n\end{subarray}}\binom{\ell(\beta^{+})-1}{k-i-1}\mathcal{R}_{\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{l+k-i}}\cdot 1.

The second equality comes from the binomial identity

\binom{\ell(\beta^{+})}{k-i}=\binom{\ell(\beta^{+})-1}{k-i}+\binom{\ell(\beta^{+})-1}{k-i-1}

and from the fact that
\mathcal{R}_{\beta,0}\cdot 1=\mathcal{R}_{\beta}\cdot 1
, which is a consequence of the identity
\mathcal{O}(-k)\cdot 1=\delta_{k,0}
for
k\geq 0
. Applying this in
Eq.

35
, we end up with the following formula:

\displaystyle(-1)^{n}\Delta_{h_{l}}\Delta^{\prime}_{e_{k-1}}\cdot e_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta_{1}>0,\\
\beta_{2},\dots,\beta_{l+k}\geq 0,\\
|\beta|=n\end{subarray}}\binom{\ell(\beta^{+})-1}{k-1}\mathcal{R}_{\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{l+k}}\cdot 1

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{k+l},\\
|\beta|=n-k\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{k+l-1},\\
|\beta^{\prime}|=l\end{subarray}}\mathcal{R}_{\beta+1^{k+l}-(0,\beta^{\prime})}\cdot 1.

To obtain the second part of
Eq.

34
, we use
1.7
to write:

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{k+l},\\
|\beta|=n-k\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{k+l-1},\\
|\beta^{\prime}|=l\end{subarray}}\mathcal{R}_{\beta+1^{k+l}-(0,\beta^{\prime})}=\widehat{F},

with

F=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{k+l},\\
|\beta|=n-k\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{k+l-1},\\
|\beta^{\prime}|=l\end{subarray}}\operatorname{Sym}\left(\frac{z^{\beta+1^{k+l}-(0,\beta^{\prime})}}{\prod_{i=1}^{l+k-1}(1-qtz_{i+1}/z_{i})}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq\ell}\omega(z_{j}/z_{i})\right),

where
z^{\alpha}:=z_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\dots z_{l+k}^{\alpha_{l+k}}.

We conclude using the definitions of homogeneous and elementary symmetric functions.
∎

Notice that there is an extra factor
(-1)^{n}
in
5.1
compared to
[
BHM+23b
]
due to the fact that our conventions for the operators
D_{a}
differ by a factor
(-1)^{a}
.

Remark 5.2
.

In
[
BHM+23a
, Definition 4.3.1]
, the authors use lattice paths (different from the ones we consider here) to define an involutive correspondence between some sequences of nonnegative integers. It is not very hard to see that this correspondence is precisely the map
\psi
introduced in
Section

3.4
. Using the notation from
[
BHM+23a
]
, they introduce the transposed Negut elements
E_{\alpha}
, and they prove in

1.7
