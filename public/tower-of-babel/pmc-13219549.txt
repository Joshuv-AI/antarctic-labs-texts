Infections Caused by Fusobacterium Species: Microbiology, Clinical Syndromes and Management: A Narrative Review
Authors: Michal Paret, Guy Katzenellenbogen, Tal Zilberman, Michal Stein, Ili Margalit, Dafna Yahav
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFusobacterium spp. are Gram-negative, obligate anaerobic bacteria associated with a broad clinical spectrum, including head and neck infections; soft tissue infections; gastrointestinal and genitourinary infections; and bacteremia without an identified source. Clinical manifestations vary by species and site of colonization. F. necrophorum is linked to head and neck infections that may culminate in Lemierre syndrome, a life-threatening septic thrombophlebitis of the internal jugular vein. Although Fusobacterium bacteremia is uncommon, it is associated with substantial mortality. F. necrophorum bacteremia may occur as a complication of head and neck infection in younger, healthy individuals, whereas F. nucleatum bacteremia is reported predominantly in older patients with malignancy, secondary to an abdominal source or without an identified origin. Fusobacterium isolates are usually susceptible to penicillins, cephalosporins, aminopenicillins with β-lactamase inhibitors, carbapenems, and metronidazole, while resistance to clindamycin and moxifloxacin has been increasingly reported. Because susceptibility testing is not routinely performed and susceptibility data remain limited, severe infections are commonly treated with a β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor, a carbapenem, or a β-lactam in combination with metronidazole. This review provides an overview of the microbiology, clinical spectrum, and treatment of Fusobacterium spp.Keywords: Fusobacterium, Anaerobes, Lemierre syndrome, Susceptibility, Treatment, Septic thrombophlebitis

Key Points

Fusobacterium spp. are Gram-negative, obligate anaerobic bacteria that cause head and neck infections and can lead to invasive disease, including Lemierre syndrome, bacteremia, and soft tissue infections.Fusobacterium species differ in clinical presentation, virulence, and antimicrobial susceptibility; F. necrophorum predominantly causes head and neck infections in young, otherwise healthy patients. F. nucleatum is more frequently identified in reported Fusobacterium bacteremia and is often associated with older age and malignancy.The Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) and the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) differ substantially in breakpoints and interpretive criteria for Fusobacterium susceptibility testing.Although susceptibility data are limited, resistance to clindamycin and moxifloxacin appears to be increasing. Empiric therapy for severe infections should include a β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor, a carbapenem, or a metronidazole-containing regimen.Surgical drainage is frequently required, particularly for otogenic and deep-seated infections. Anticoagulation is increasingly used in Lemierre syndrome, but evidence of benefit remains inconclusive.

Introduction

Fusobacterium spp. are Gram-negative, non-spore-forming, obligate anaerobic rods. They are part of the normal microbiota of the oral cavity, gastrointestinal tract, and female genital tract [1], but can become invasive pathogens and cause infections that frequently present with abscess formation [2]. However, unlike many other anaerobes, Fusobacterium spp. may act as primary pathogens in a range of infections [3]. Infections caused by Fusobacterium spp. can be categorized into three main groups: (1) head and neck infections, typically in young, otherwise healthy individuals and most often caused by F. necrophorum, which may progress to severe complications such as Lemierre syndrome; (2) bacteremia without an identified source, most commonly in older patients with malignancy (particularly hematologic cancers), predominantly caused by F. nucleatum; and (3) other soft tissue infections [4, 5]. Additional clinical syndromes have also been described, including hepatobiliary and gastrointestinal infections [6, 7], genital tract infections [8], and less commonly, endocarditis [9] and septic arthritis [10].

Clinical syndromes vary across Fusobacterium species. More virulent species, such as F. necrophorum and F. nucleatum, can act as invasive pathogens, adhering to and invading both epithelial and endothelial cells [8]. In a series of 299 Fusobacterium infection cases, 69% of F. necrophorum infections originated in the oropharynx, followed by the skin and gastrointestinal tract [11]. The most common sources of F. nucleatum infections were the sinuses, gastrointestinal and hepatobiliary tracts, skin, and urogenital tract. Thirty percent of patients with F. nucleatum infection had underlying malignancy. F. gonidiaformans is most often associated with urogenital infections; F. mortiferum with gastrointestinal infections and bacteremia; and F. varium with gastrointestinal and hepatobiliary infections. F. varium has been described as nosocomial and occurring in older patients than infections caused by other Fusobacterium species [11, 12].

Methods

We performed a PubMed search using the term ‘fusobacterium OR fusobacteria’ with the filters ‘humans’ and ‘clinical trial’ up to November 2025, yielding 277 records. None were interventional clinical trials informing treatment of Fusobacterial infections. An additional search was performed using the PubMed search term: “((fusobacterium OR fusobacteria) AND (treatment OR therapy OR antibiotic*)) NOT (microbiota OR probiotic* OR microbiome OR periodontal OR photodynamic)”. This yielded 303 titles when restricted to the last 5 years. No clinical trials were identified to inform the management of Fusobacterial infections. Current data are based on case series and case reports.

This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

Microbiology and Taxonomy

Fusobacterium is a genus in the family Fusobacteriaceae, order Fusobacteriales. Fusobacterium spp. are Gram-negative, non-spore-forming, obligate anaerobes. The species most commonly implicated in human infection are Fusobacterium nucleatum and Fusobacterium necrophorum. Other Fusobacterium taxa such as F. gonidiaformans, F. necrogenes, and F. ulcerans have only occasionally been isolated from human clinical samples.

Of the two F. necrophorum subspecies, subsp. funduliforme is most commonly identified in humans, whereas F. necrophorum subsp. necrophorum is more commonly associated with animal disease. F. nucleatum is a heterogeneous species with five described subspecies: subsp. animalis, subsp. fusiforme, subsp. nucleatum, subsp. polymorphum, and subsp. vincentii.

Important virulence factors related to invasiveness include adhesins, leukotoxin, and plasminogen-binding factors. F. nucleatum is an adherent bacterium and encodes several adhesins, including Fap2, RadD, and Aid1. The FadA adhesin, conserved among F. nucleatum strains, is a key virulence factor and has been proposed as a potential diagnostic marker. FadA has been shown to bind host cells and is among the best-characterized virulence factors in this species to date [13]. Lipopolysaccharides have endotoxic activity and contribute to virulence in Fusobacterium spp. [14].

Epidemiology and Recent Trends in Species Distribution and Antimicrobial Resistance

Recent studies suggest shifts in species distribution and clinical settings of Fusobacterial infections. In Asia, F. varium has been reported as an emerging pathogen. Lee et al. reported F. varium as the cause of approximately one-fifth of 272 Fusobacterial infections at a South Korean center; F. varium cases were mostly hospital-acquired, intra-abdominal, and polymicrobial infections [12]. Kim et al. reported F. varium as the cause of more than half of 86 Fusobacterial infections in another South Korean center [15]. Mori et al. reported F. varium in 8% of 299 Fusobacterial infections in Japan, most commonly with hepatobiliary and gastrointestinal sources [11]. A potential pathogenic role of F. varium in ulcerative colitis has been suggested in Asian cohorts [16].

Antimicrobial resistance patterns are also influenced by geography and species distribution. Resistance to clindamycin and moxifloxacin has been reported to be higher among F. varium isolates from Asia compared with isolates from the United States. Clindamycin resistance among F. varium isolates from Asia ranges from 30 to 48% compared with 10% to 14% in the United States [15]. Moxifloxacin resistance similarly ranges from 24 to 44% in Asia, compared with 10% to 12% in the United States [15].

Clinical Syndromes Caused by Various Fusobacterium spp.

Head and Neck Infections

Head and neck infections caused by Fusobacterium spp. include pharyngotonsillitis, peritonsillar abscess, gingival and periodontal disease, cervical lymphadenitis, otitis media, and mastoiditis (predominantly in children), as well as sinusitis (predominantly in adults). These infections may be complicated by internal jugular vein septic thrombophlebitis (Lemierre syndrome), with septic emboli [1].

Ear Infections: Otitis and Mastoiditis

Fusobacterium necrophorum is an increasingly recognized pathogen in pediatric middle ear infections, with the potential to cause severe complications [17]. In a retrospective study of 25 cases, presentations included uncomplicated acute otitis media (11/25, 44%) and acute mastoiditis (10/25, 40%); some patients developed an otogenic variant of Lemierre syndrome (4/25, 16%) which included sinus vein thrombosis and meningitis. Most complicated cases (such as meningitis and sinus vein thrombosis) occurred in children under 2 years of age [18]. A recent study found that F. necrophorum was identified in 13% of culture-positive pediatric mastoiditis cases, and these cases were associated with a markedly severe clinical course, including a 94.7% complication rate, frequent need for surgical intervention, and significantly prolonged hospitalization [19]. A recent pediatric study also highlighted the potential severity of F. necrophorum in upper respiratory tract infections. In this study, F. necrophorum accounted for 47% of sinogenic intracranial empyema in children, as well as 8% of otogenic intracranial empyema. F. necrophorum infection was associated with cerebral venous thrombosis, as reported in previous studies [20, 21]. A retrospective 2021–2024 cohort study (published in 2025) found that F. necrophorum accounted for 19% of pediatric acute mastoiditis cases and was strongly associated with intracranial complications; together, S. pyogenes and F. necrophorum were identified in over 70% of complicated cases [22].

Oropharyngeal Infections

Although part of the normal microbiota, F. necrophorum and F. nucleatum can become invasive and cause severe infections, including odontogenic infections (F. nucleatum), peritonsillar abscesses and parapharyngeal space infections (F. necrophorum). Clinically, F. necrophorum-associated infections may mimic Streptococcus pyogenes pharyngitis, but are often more severe and may progress to systemic complications, including Lemierre syndrome [23, 24].

The role of F. necrophorum as a causative pathogen of pharyngotonsillitis and peritonsillar abscess remains debated, since this pathogen can be detected in up to 20% of throat samples from asymptomatic adults. Nevertheless, several studies have demonstrated higher rates of detection in symptomatic patients, with the strongest association in the age group of 15–30 years. In addition, several studies have detected F. necrophorum in patients with recurrent or persistent pharyngotonsillitis [23].

A retrospective study of 637 patients with peritonsillar abscess in Sweden found F. necrophorum in 33% of cases, making it the most common pathogen identified, particularly in adolescents and young adults. Importantly, F. necrophorum was the only pathogen significantly associated with both early and late complications, emphasizing its role in severe outcomes and the need for targeted management [25].

None of the current guidelines routinely recommend testing for F. necrophorum while investigating the cause of pharyngotonsillitis [26]. In a retrospective study from Sweden, 3,700 children and adults with pharyngotonsillitis were included if both throat cultures for β-hemolytic streptococci and PCR for F. necrophorum were performed. F. necrophorum was detected in 28% of cases, compared with 10% for group A Streptococcus (GAS), 13% for group C/group G Streptococcus (GCS/GGS), and negative results in 54%. Complications occurred in 26% of patients with F. necrophorum, including pharyngotonsillitis recurrence (13%), hospitalization (11%), peritonsillar abscess (8%), and others (including sepsis/septic complications in 0.5%). Identification of F. necrophorum or Group A streptococcus was significantly associated with risk of complications. Patients treated early with antibiotics had higher rates of complications, likely due to indication bias; therefore, the study could not determine whether antibiotic therapy prevents complications [27].

In a recent study reporting 287 adult cases with brain abscess caused by oral cavity bacteria, Fusobacterium spp. were the third most common cause, isolated in over 50 cases. Neither the causative bacterial species nor the choice of targeted antibiotic regimen was associated with clinical outcome [28].

Necrotizing Ulcerative Gingivitis or Vincent’s Angina

Fusobacterium spp. are among the bacteria commonly associated with Necrotizing ulcerative gingivitis (NUG), also known as Vincent’s angina, and are thought to contribute to its pathogenesis [29]. Other organisms involved in this polymicrobial process include additional anaerobic bacteria and spirochetes. This entity is rare (< 1%) and usually presents in children or young adults. Risk factors include HIV, malnutrition, poor oral hygiene, alcohol, and tobacco use. In addition to the oral signs and symptoms, patients may present with fever, malaise, and local lymphadenopathy. Treatment includes local debridement, antiseptic rinses, and in selected cases, systemic antibiotics with anaerobic coverage. Left untreated, NUG may progress to necrotizing ulcerative periodontitis or noma (cancrum oris). Fusobacterial virulence factors, including proteolytic enzymes, toxins, and production of growth stimulants for other anaerobic bacteria, have been described as facilitating tissue destruction in NUG and its complications [29].

Lemierre Syndrome

Lemierre syndrome is a rare but severe condition characterized by septic thrombophlebitis of the internal jugular vein, most often following an anaerobic oropharyngeal infection and most commonly caused by F. necrophorum. The classic presentation includes fever, sore throat, trismus, and tenderness or induration along the sternocleidomastoid muscle. However, early clinical diagnosis can be challenging; nearly 50% of patients in a cohort of 109 had a normal physical examination on presentation [30]. Diagnosis relies on a high index of suspicion and is supported by four criteria: (1) a primary oropharyngeal anaerobic infection; (2) isolation of Fusobacterium spp.; (3) evidence of metastatic infection at one or more distant sites; and (4) radiologic confirmation of internal jugular vein thrombosis [31, 32].

The most common metastatic infections in Lemierre syndrome are pneumonia, septic pulmonary emboli, and/or empyema. Lung imaging typically reveals necrotic cavitation; other findings may include infiltrates, pleural effusion or empyema, lung abscesses, and necrotizing mediastinitis. In a large contemporary analysis of 712 patients with Lemierre syndrome, distant embolic complications were frequent and clinically significant. Septic emboli were identified in 82% of patients at presentation, most commonly involving the lungs (71%) and, less frequently, the intracranial circulation (11%). Despite appropriate antimicrobial therapy, new or progressive peripheral septic lesions developed in approximately 12% of patients during hospitalization, underscoring the ongoing embolic risk even after initiation of treatment [33].

Other metastatic complications include osteomyelitis, septic arthritis, brain or epidural abscesses, soft tissue abscesses, pyomyositis, splenic and liver abscesses, endocarditis, pericarditis, and renal abscesses. The pathogenesis of these complications involves extension from the internal jugular vein septic thrombus, either antegrade (to the lungs) or retrograde (intracranially) [14, 34].

A large patient-level analysis of Lemierre syndrome showed that Fusobacterium species— predominantly F. necrophorum—remain the leading pathogens, isolated in 58% of cases, whereas F. nucleatum was detected only rarely [35]. Other organisms included Streptococcus species, Staphylococcus aureus, and mixed anaerobes, with 19% of cases remaining culture-negative despite classic clinical features.

The term “necrobacillosis” is commonly used to describe human infections caused by F. necrophorum. While some sources use it broadly [36], others use it specifically to refer to disseminated infections such as bacteremia [37, 38].

Genital Tract Infections

F. nucleatum has been associated with a wide spectrum of adverse pregnancy outcomes, including preterm birth, preterm premature rupture of membranes, chorioamnionitis, and stillbirth. The mechanism underlying these complications has been hypothesized to involve periodontal disease during pregnancy, with translocation of anaerobic oral commensals such as F. nucleatum [8]. Proposed routes include hematogenous spread from the pregnant woman’s oral cavity or transmission from a partner’s subgingival sites. However, the origin of the infection remains debatable, as vaginal Fusobacterium spp. have also been detected, suggesting a possible ascending infection. Studies have demonstrated identical strains between the oral cavity and amniotic fluid or placental tissue, supporting the hypothesis that the oral cavity may serve as a source of pregnancy complications. Additionally, F. nucleatum has been reported as a cause of neonatal sepsis [8]. Rare cases of Fusobacterium spp. causing pelvic inflammatory disease and endometritis have also been reported [39].

Gastrointestinal/Hepatobiliary Infections

In a study of 70 patients who underwent appendectomy for suspected acute appendicitis, Fusobacterium spp. were identified in 62% of appendiceal specimens, and their presence strongly correlated with the severity of inflammation [7]. The most commonly detected species were F. nucleatum (79%), F. necrophorum (12%), and other Fusobacterium spp. (9%). These organisms were associated with invasive disease in suppurative cases and were absent from cecal biopsies of both healthy individuals and disease controls, supporting a pathogenic role in acute appendicitis.

Interestingly, in the context of inflammatory bowel diseases (IBD), F. nucleatum has been detected at significantly higher prevalence in the colonic mucosa of IBD patients compared to healthy controls, and strains from inflamed tissue were reported to demonstrate greater epithelial invasiveness [40].

Fusobacterium spp. are increasingly recognized in hepatobiliary infections, particularly pyogenic liver abscesses. In a literature review by Jayasimhan et al., 48 cases of Fusobacterium-related liver abscesses were analyzed, with F. nucleatum and F. necrophorum identified as the predominant pathogens [6]. Most patients were previously healthy and lacked underlying hepatobiliary disease, raising the possibility of hematogenous spread from oral or gastrointestinal sources.

Fusobacterium Bacteremia

Fusobacterium bacteremia is rare, with a reported annual incidence of approximately 0.5 per 100,000 population. F. nucleatum is the most common species causing bacteremia (0.34/100,000), typically occurring in older patients with comorbidities (e.g., malignancy, dialysis), whereas F. necrophorum is less common (0.14/100,000) and is more often reported in younger, otherwise healthy individuals. F. nucleatum is often associated with bacteremia of unknown source or an intra-abdominal source (e.g., abscess), and is more common in men [5] and older patients with comorbidities, mainly malignancy [41]. Additional reported sources include head and neck infections—most often due to F. necrophorum (with or without Lemierre syndrome)—and genitourinary infections. Often, only genus-level identification is reported without species-level assignment, highlighting the need for more data to classify Fusobacterium bacteremia by clinical source. Previous studies reported no identification at the species level in 20–25% of bacteremia cases [3, 42].

A recent literature review included 205 Fusobacterium spp. bacteremia cases [43]. Overall, the median age was 45.5 years, males predominated (66%), and most cases were community-acquired (67%). The most frequently identified Fusobacterium spp. were F. necrophorum (86/205, 42%) and F. nucleatum (78/205, 38%), followed by a few cases each of F. gonidiaformans, F. varium, and F. mortiferum. The most common clinical presentation was fever (40%), followed by sore throat (14%). The head and neck region was the most frequent source of infection (34%), predominantly associated with F. necrophorum, while intra-abdominal infections (19%) were more commonly linked to F. nucleatum. Twenty-seven percent of F. nucleatum bacteremia cases occurred in patients with cancer. Lemierre syndrome was reported in 10% of bacteremic patients, while 18% had thrombosis (including renal, portal, hepatic, inferior vena cava, dural sinuses), and abscesses were observed in 16% of cases. Similar findings were reported in a series of 75 bacteremia cases from the UK [3].

Thirty-day mortality was 13%, similar to the 10–17% reported in other series [4, 5]. Some studies reported higher mortality (approximately 40–50%) [3, 44] (see Table 1). Underlying comorbid conditions were the only factor associated with mortality in multivariate analysis, and the presence of fever was associated with an increased risk of complications [43]. In a Japanese 10-year hospital series, Fusobacterium bacteremia was more frequent in patients aged ≥ 75 years and in those with cerebrovascular disease or hematologic malignancy (univariate analysis) [11]. Hematological malignancy has also been described as a predisposing condition for Fusobacterium bacteremia in other studies [4]. A recent systematic review compiled 21 adult and pediatric cases of Fusobacterium endocarditis. Most cases were caused by F. necrophorum (48%) or F. nucleatum (43%) and involved native-valve endocarditis associated with poor dental health. Embolic phenomena were reported in 80% of cases, though mortality was low (10%); the most common regimen was a β-lactam plus metronidazole [9].

Although anaerobes rarely cause septic arthritis, Fusobacterium spp. are the most frequently reported anaerobic pathogens. A recent literature review described 43 Fusobacterial septic arthritis cases. Most cases occurred in young men and F. necrophorum was the causative pathogen in two-thirds of cases. Proposed pathogenesis involved a head and neck source with bacteremia and joint seeding or a direct inoculation after trauma. The most commonly affected joints were the knee and hip; the temporomandibular joint, sternoclavicular joint, and shoulder were also involved. Most patients were managed surgically; Antibiotic therapy was varied, most often combining anaerobic coverage with a β-lactam; and approximately 25% of cases resulted in residual joint damage [10].

Fusobacterium Susceptibility to Antibiotics

In general, Fusobacterium spp. exhibit susceptibility to a broad range of antibiotics; however, susceptibility varies by species, with F. nucleatum and F. necrophorum generally more susceptible than other species [15]. High susceptibility rates have been reported to penicillins, ceftriaxone, β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitors, carbapenems, and metronidazole, whereas increasing resistance has been reported to clindamycin and moxifloxacin. F. necrophorum has intrinsic resistance to several antibiotic classes, including macrolides, fluoroquinolones, tetracyclines, and aminoglycosides [14]. Fusobacterium canifelinum is intrinsically resistant to quinolones [45].

There are differences between Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) and the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) breakpoints, as well as methods and interpretation of susceptibility testing of Fusobacterium spp. [46]. EUCAST has published disk diffusion methodology and species-specific breakpoints for F. necrophorum, including both MIC and disk diffusion criteria [47]. Differences between CLSI and EUCAST breakpoints stem from differing strategies regarding susceptibility testing of anaerobes. CLSI provides one set of breakpoints per drug for several anaerobes, except for Bacteroides and Parabacteroides spp. [45]. In contrast, EUCAST provides species or species group-specific breakpoints, including a dedicated table for F. necrophorum. EUCAST breakpoint setting incorporates wild-type MIC distributions (among other inputs); therefore, a larger proportion of isolates may be categorized as resistant compared with CLSI criteria.

In addition, the approach to PK/PD modeling differs, with EUCAST applying stricter assumptions regarding PK/PD target attainment using European-recommended doses of penicillins and carbapenems. Other differences include laboratory testing methods and media used for anaerobes. EUCAST anaerobe breakpoint determinations have also been updated more recently [47].

Therefore, resistance rates differ between species, testing methods, interpretive criteria, and over time [46]. Among 299 Fusobacterium isolates from Japan collected during 2014–2023, clindamycin resistance among F. nucleatum, F. necrophorum, and F. varium isolates ranged from 0 to 36%; and moxifloxacin resistance ranged from 4.6% to 38.5%, according to CLSI breakpoints. Resistance to penicillin G was observed in up to 6.5% of isolates tested, while no resistance was documented to cephalosporins, β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitors, carbapenems, and metronidazole [11]. In recent aggregate susceptibility data provided by CLSI, no resistance was reported among Fusobacterium spp. to meropenem or ampicillin/sulbactam; 4% resistance was reported to piperacillin/tazobactam, 5% to imipenem, 5% to metronidazole, 23% to clindamycin, and 32% to moxifloxacin [45].

In a UK series of F. necrophorum isolates using EUCAST breakpoints, 23% of strains were resistant to penicillin, 4% to meropenem, and 1% to metronidazole [46]. Notably, the 23% (73 isolates) penicillin resistance rate was reported using EUCAST v13.0 breakpoints, whereas EUCAST v11.0 breakpoints classified only three isolates as resistant [46].

A recent European report found > 10% resistance to clindamycin (12%), meropenem (16%), and metronidazole (11%) among F. necrophorum isolates when tested by MIC methods; lower resistance rates were reported using disk diffusion. Resistance to benzylpenicillin was recorded in 11% of F. nucleatum isolates tested by MIC, and > 90% were susceptible to ampicillin/sulbactam, amoxicillin/clavulanate, piperacillin/tazobactam, clindamycin, carbapenems, and metronidazole [48].

Data on antibiotic resistance genes are limited. A recent study by Perry et al. reported genotypic and phenotypic resistance data for F. necrophorum isolates collected in the UK. The presence of tet(M) and tet(32) was associated with increased MICs to older tetracyclines, whereas low MICs for tigecycline were reported. The presence of ermA and ermB was also reported and may confer clindamycin resistance. In publicly available genomes, blaOXA-85 was also identified [46].

Antibiotic Management

Due to the severity of Lemierre syndrome and the possible penicillin resistance reported above, recommended therapy for this syndrome include a carbapenem, ceftriaxone plus metronidazole, or a β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor. Co-infection with other β-lactamase-producing bacteria is also a consideration for broader coverage [14, 49, 50]. Clindamycin, penicillin, and moxifloxacin are alternatives, although emerging resistance has been reported [46].

Role of Surgical Interventions

Surgical interventions play a critical role in the management of Fusobacterium infections. A multicenter study of 51 pediatric cases of invasive Fusobacterium infections highlighted the frequent need for surgical management, particularly for otogenic infections. All children with otogenic infections (55% of cases) underwent surgical procedures, most commonly mastoidectomy and abscess drainage. Ninety-two percent of the cohort required surgery, reflecting the severity of head and neck Fusobacterium infections [51]. Fusobacterium infections frequently lead to abscess formation in organs such as the skin, brain, pleura, and liver, often requiring surgical drainage for optimal management [2]. Some patients with Lemierre syndrome may also require invasive drainage of abscesses [31]. Spinal infections such as vertebral osteomyelitis and discitis have been successfully managed with a combination of drainage and antibiotic therapy [6, 52].

Duration of Therapy

In a literature review of 205 Fusobacterium bacteremia cases, most patients were treated for 2–6 weeks, and only 10% received 14 days or less of therapy. All patients with documented abscesses were treated for more than 2 weeks [43]. In another series of 96 patients that included cases of bacteremia, skin and soft tissue infection, and head and neck infections, 54% received > 2 weeks of therapy [4]. For Lemierre syndrome, treatment durations of 3–6 weeks are commonly recommended, given endovascular involvement [14, 31].

Anticoagulation in Lemierre Syndrome

Anticoagulation in Lemierre syndrome remains controversial. The rationale is to prevent thrombus propagation and septic emboli; however, evidence supporting clinical benefit is limited. In the largest systematic analysis to date (712 patients), Valerio et al. reported that new thromboembolic events occurred in 17% of cases, and anticoagulation was associated with a lower event rate without an increased risk of major bleeding [33]. In a Swedish cohort of 82 patients with Lemierre syndrome, 62% had internal jugular vein thrombosis and 60% received therapeutic or prophylactic anticoagulation [53]. Thrombosis progression, septic emboli, major bleeding, and mortality were rare, with no significant differences between anticoagulated and non-anticoagulated patients. Outcomes were favorable across treatment strategies, suggesting that while anticoagulation is commonly used, its benefit remains unproven.

These findings are consistent with the growing clinical trend toward anticoagulation, although causality remains uncertain given the observational design and treatment heterogeneity. Several case reports also describe venous thrombosis in patients with Fusobacterium spp. infections outside the head and neck, most commonly intra-abdominal thrombosis. The propensity for infection-induced thrombosis in Fusobacterium infections has been attributed to endotoxin production, which may promote platelet aggregation [54].

Fusobacterium and Association with Cancer

F. nucleatum has been associated with colorectal cancer and has been proposed to promote colorectal carcinogenesis. Although F. nucleatum is a common member of the oral microbiota, it is infrequently detected in the healthy colon but is enriched in colorectal cancer tissue. Longitudinal microbiome analyses have shown that the presence of F. nucleatum in the gut correlates with reduced microbial diversity, destabilization of the intestinal microbiota, and enrichment of species linked to inflammatory bowel disease and colorectal cancer [55]. Transient detection of F. nucleatum may reflect an early shift toward dysbiosis, suggesting that it could serve as both a potential contributor to—and biomarker of—early microbial imbalance associated with colorectal carcinogenesis.

Abed et al. demonstrated that colorectal cancer-associated F. nucleatum may originate from the oral cavity [56]. Using genomic analysis of paired oral and tumor isolates, they identified near-identical strains, consistent with hematogenous dissemination rather than local gut overgrowth. In mouse models, intravenous inoculation resulted in preferential tumor colonization, suggesting that transient oral bacteremia could seed colorectal tumors with F. nucleatum.

A recent study by Zepeda-Rivera et al. identified a distinct, highly virulent clade of F. nucleatum subsp. animalis, that appears to predominate in colorectal cancer [57]. In mouse models, this clade promoted tumor growth and oxidative stress, suggesting that F. nucleatum tumor-promoting potential is strain-specific and linked to genetic specialization.

Gaps in Knowledge and Priorities for Further Investigation

Despite growing recognition of Fusobacterium as a clinically significant pathogen, several knowledge gaps persist. A major limitation is reliance on routine culture techniques, which may fail to detect Fusobacterium, particularly when empiric antimicrobial therapy is initiated before specimen collection. While molecular diagnostics have improved detection rates, their integration into routine practice remains inconsistent. Another critical gap is the limited availability of antimicrobial susceptibility data. Most laboratories do not routinely perform susceptibility testing for Fusobacterium spp., resulting in lack of contemporary data on resistance patterns, including β-lactamase production.

Furthermore, the role of anticoagulation in Lemierre syndrome remains a subject of ongoing debate; while many clinicians favor its use, its clinical benefit, optimal regimen, and duration have not been established and warrant prospective evaluation.

Conclusions

This review summarizes the microbiology, epidemiology, clinical manifestations, and treatment of Fusobacterium spp. infections. Given that the susceptibility testing is often unavailable and breakpoints are based on limited data and differ between CLSI and EUCAST, empiric therapy for severe infections should include a β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor or a carbapenem. Metronidazole can be added when additional anaerobic coverage is desired. Further research is needed to better define resistance rates and mechanisms across Fusobacterium spp. Additionally, optimal antimicrobial regimens and treatment durations require prospective evaluation.
