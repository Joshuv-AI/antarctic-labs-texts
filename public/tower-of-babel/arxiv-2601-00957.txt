Algorithmic Applications of Tyshkevich’s Graph Decomposition: A Primer and a Toolkit
Christine T. Cheng, Chelsea Ann Lambert

Abstract
A graph that is completely determined by its degree sequence is called a unigraph . In 2000, Regina Tyshkevich published one of the most important papers on unigraphs [ 17 ] . There are two parts to the paper: a decomposition theorem that describes how every graph can be broken into a sequence of basic graphs and a complete classification of all basic unigraphs. Together, they reveal how every unigraph is constructed. We provide an informal overview of Tyshkevich’s results and show how they enable the computation of various graph parameters of unigraphs in linear time. We also provide a toolkit ( https://chelseal11.github.io/tyshkevich_decomposition_toolkit/ ) that implements the algorithms described in this write-up.

The Decomposition Theorem

Split graphs
play a prominent role in Tyshkevich’s decomposition theorem. A graph
G
is
split
if its vertex set can be partitioned into two sets so that the first set of vertices induce a clique while the second set of vertices induce a stable or independent set. The two sets form what’s called a
KS
-partition
of
G
. The
K
-part is for the clique and the
S
-part is for the stable set.

Consider the tree in Figure
1
. It has a
KS
-partition
(\{b,d\},\{a,c,e\})
, so it is a split graph. In fact, it is the tree’s only
KS
-partition. On the other hand, the complete graph
K_{n}
has two types of
KS
-partitions: the first one has the entire vertex set in the
K
-part and an empty set for the
S
-part; the second one has all but one vertex in the
K
-part and a single vertex in the
S
-part. Hammer and Simeone
[
13
]
describes the

-partitions of a split graph.

Theorem 2.1
.

[

For any

•

•

and

Tyshkevich defined a graph as
decomposable
if it can be obtained as the composition of some split graph and another graph; otherwise, it is an
indecomposable graph
.

How do we know if a graph is decomposable? The answer, it turns out, lies in a graph’s degree sequence! Let us examine the composition operation again. In
(G,A,B)\circ H
, all the vertices in
A
become adjacent to all the vertices in
H
. Thus,

•

when
u\in A
,
deg(u)\geq|V(H)|+|A|-1
since
u
is also adjacent to all the other vertices in
A
;

•

when
v\in V(H)
,
|A|\leq deg(v)\leq|A|+|V(H)|-1
since the number of neighbors of
v
in
H
range from
0
to
|V(H)|-1
;

•

when
w\in B
,
0\leq deg(w)\leq|A|
since the number of neighbors of
w
in
G
range from
0
to
|A|
.

It follows that in the degree sequence of
(G,A,B)\circ H
, the degrees of the vertices in
A
appear first followed by those in
H
and finally by those in
B
. Another way of putting it is if the degree sequence of
(G,A,B)\circ H
is
(d_{1},d_{2},\ldots,d_{N})
, then there are two indices
p
and
q
with
p+q<N
so that

(d_{1},\ldots,d_{p}),(d_{p+1},\ldots,d_{N-q})\mbox{ and }(d_{N-q+1},\ldots,d_{N})

are the degrees of the vertices in
A,H
and
B
respectively.

Following the notation above, we also note that in
(G,A,B)\circ H
, a vertex
u\in A
is adjacent to (i)
p-1
other vertices in
A
, (ii) all the vertices in
H
and (iii) its neighbors in
B
. Hence, if
v_{i}
is the vertex with degree
d_{i}
, and
deg_{B}(v_{i})
is the number of neighbors of
v_{i}
in
B
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{p}d_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{p}\left[(p-1)+(N-p-q)+deg_{B}(v_{i})\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p(p-1)+p(N-p-q)+\sum_{i=1}^{p}deg_{B}(v_{i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p(N-q-1)+\sum_{i=N-q+1}^{N}d_{i}.

Tyshkevich showed that these observations are both necessary and sufficient for proving that a graph is decomposable.

Theorem 2.3
.

[
17
]

A graph
G^{*}
with degree sequence
(d_{1},d_{2},

\ldots
,
d_{N})
is decomposable if and only if there exists indices
p,q
with
0<p+q<N
so that

\sum_{i=1}^{p}d_{i}=p(N-q-1)+\sum_{i=N-q+1}^{N}d_{i}.

If
G^{*}
is decomposable, then
G^{*}=(G,A,B)\circ H
where
(d_{1},\ldots,d_{p})
,
(d_{p+1},\ldots,d_{N-q})
and
(d_{N-q+1},\ldots,d_{N})
are the degrees of the vertices in
A
,
H
and
B
respectively.

*****

When
G^{*}
is the composition of many graphs, there are many ways of expressing
G^{*}
as the composition of two graphs because of the associativity of the composition operation. That is, there are many pairs of indices
(p,q)
that can satisfy the conditions in Theorem
2.3
. But if we choose the pair with the smallest
p
value, then
(G,A,B)
is
guaranteed
to be an indecomposable graph. We can then decompose
H
further. In this way, we obtain a decomposition of

into indecomposable graphs. This leads us to Tyshkevich’s main result.

Theorem 2.4
.

(The Canonical Decomposition Theorem of Tyshkevich
[

]
)

Every graph
G^{*}
can be represented as a composition

G^{*}=(G_{k},A_{k},B_{k})\circ(G_{k-1},A_{k-1},B_{k-1})\circ\ldots\circ(G_{1},A_{1},B_{1})\circ G_{0}

where each
G_{i}
is an indecomposable graph. This decomposition is canonical; that is, let

G^{\prime}=(G^{\prime}_{\ell},A^{\prime}_{\ell},B^{\prime}_{\ell})\circ(G^{\prime}_{\ell-1},A^{\prime}_{\ell-1},B^{\prime}_{k-1})\circ\ldots\circ(G^{\prime}_{1},A^{\prime}_{1},B^{\prime}_{1})\circ G^{\prime}_{0}.

Then
G^{*}\cong G^{\prime}
if and only if
k=\ell
,
G_{0}\cong G^{\prime}_{0}
and
(G_{i},A_{i},B_{i})\cong(G^{\prime}_{i},A^{\prime}_{i},B^{\prime}_{i})
for

]
that outputs a stack that contains the (paired) degree sequence of

on top followed by the paired degree sequences of

Section 3
Indecomposable Unigraphs

In the second part of her paper, Tyshkevich noted the implication of Theorem
2.4
for unigraphs.

Theorem 3.1
.

[
17
]

G
is a unigraph if and only if each indecomposable component in the canonical decomposition of
G
is also a unigraph.

Equally important, Tyshkevich described
all
the indecomposable unigraphs. The proof is substantial; it is twenty pages long with many cases to sort through. Tyshkevich was able to keep the list of indecomposable unigraphs manageable because she made use of the complements and inverses of graphs.

Recall that the
complement
of
G
,
\overline{G}
, has the same vertex set as
G
and two vertices are adjacent if and only if they are not adjacent in
G
. When
G
is a split graph with
KS
-partition
(A,B)
, the complement of
(G,A,B)
is
\overline{(G,A,B)}=(\overline{G},B,A)
. Its
inverse
is
(G,A,B)^{I}=(G^{I},B,A)
where
G^{I}
is obtained from
G
by deleting the edges
\{aa^{\prime}:a,a^{\prime}\in A\}
and adding the edges
\{bb^{\prime}:b,b^{\prime}\in B\}
. The two operations can then be combined to create the
complement of the inverse
of
(G,A,B)
, which is also the
inverse of the complement
of
(G,A,B)
. Figure
3
shows the two graph operations.

The indecomposable non-split unigraphs are described using four types of graphs. The first two are

and

. The third one is

, the graph formed by the disjoint union of

and the star

. The fourth one is

, the graph formed by a center vertex,

and

, the vertices of a graph are assigned colors so that the labeled version of the graph has only one automorphism, the identity map. Formally, a
distinguishing labeling
\phi
of
G

1
]
colors the vertices of

so that for every non-trivial automorphism

of

\phi(v)\neq\phi(\pi(v))
for some vertex

\phi

all the non-trivial automorphisms of

so that the only automorphism of the labeled graph
(G,\phi)
is the identity map. The
distinguishing number
of
G

, is the fewest number of colors needed to create a distinguishing labeling of
G
. For example,
D(K_{n})=n
,
D(P_{n})=2
for

and
D(K_{n,n})=n+1
.

A concept related to distinguishing labelings is

[

,

,

]

is a

of

if the only automorphism of

that fixes every element in

is the identity map. That is, whenever an automorphism of
G
maps each
s\in S
to itself, it also maps each
v\not\in S
to itself. Figure

shows a fixing set of

. The

of

, is the size of a smallest fixing set. For example,

,

for

while

. We note that

if and only if

is a

has no non-trivial automorphisms).

Every fixing set
S
of
G
can be translated into a distinguishing labeling
\phi_{S}
by
individualizing
each vertex in
S

is assigned a distinct color while the vertices not in

are assigned another color (e.g., the “null” color). Thus,

. Sometimes,

but

One of our research questions was whether

can be computed efficiently when

Theorem

A Toolkit

To make our discussion more accessible, we have created a toolkit

•

•

Given the degree sequence of a graph, it outputs the canonical decomposition of the graph. Each indecomposable component is described by its (paired) degree sequence. It determines if the input graph is a unigraph; if so, it also specifies the type of each indecomposable component. It can also determine the clique number, chromatic number, independence number and vertex cover number of the original graph.

•

Given the degree sequence of a graph, it outputs the

Section 7
Conclusion

We have described Tyshkevich’s main results and elaborated on the algorithmic implications of her work, showing that various graph parameters can be computed in linear time when the input graph is a unigraph. It is very likely that more polynomial-time algorithms can be designed for unigraphs. The results here also beg the question whether a similar approach can work for other graph classes. That is, can their indecomposable components be completely classified and then used for algorithmic purposes? We hope that our exposition, along with the toolkit we have developed, will encourage readers to engage with this topic and inspire further research.

This time, let
H
be some split graph and
\mathit{DegSeq}
be its degree sequence. The algorithm
DetermineSplitType
(
\mathit{DegSeq}
) works the same way as
DetermineNonSplitType
by comparing the degree sequence of
H
,
\overline{H}
,
H^{I}
,
\overline{H}^{I}
with those of a single vertex graph,
S(p,q)
,
S_{2}(p_{1},q_{1},\ldots,p_{k},q_{m})
,
S_{3}(p,q_{1},q_{2})
and
S_{4}(p,q)
. The algorithm first checks if the degree sequence has the correct basic format. It then derives the parameters and checks if the parameters are valid. Finally, if certain parts of the degree sequence are based on the parameters but were not used in the derivation of the parameters, the algorithm checks if they have the right values.

Let
S
be the degree sequence of
H
,
\overline{H}
,
H^{I}
or
\overline{H}^{I}
. Let us demonstrate the above steps when the algorithm checks if
S
is isomorphic to

for some

,
i=1,\ldots,m
. From Table
2
, the degree sequence of the latter is
((p_{1}+n-1)^{q_{1}},\cdots,

(p_{m}+n-1)^{q_{m}};

1^{p_{1}q_{1}+\cdots+p_{m}q_{m}})
where

with
m\geq 2
,
p_{1}>p_{2}>\ldots>p_{m}\geq 1
and
q_{i}\geq 1
for
i=1,\ldots,m
.

First, the algorithm checks if in the paired degree sequence
(D_{A},D_{B})
,
D_{A}
has length at least
2
and
D_{B}
has length
1
. If so, its format is
(d_{1}^{r_{1}},d_{2}^{r_{2}},\dots,d_{m}^{r_{m}};d_{B}^{r_{B}})
with

and

. It then sets the parameters

. Next, it verifies that

Finally, it checks that
