Protocol for establishing liver tumors via ultrasound-guided intrahepatic injection prior partial hepatic resection in a mouse model
Authors: Shadin Ghabra, Rajiv Trehan, Francisco Rodriguez-Matos, Chi Ma, Tim F. Greten
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryWe present a protocol to generate a liver tumor murine model utilizing orthotopic ultrasound-guided intrahepatic injections of tumor cells and a partial hepatectomy. We describe cell and animal preparation and ultrasound-guided intrahepatic injections. We then depict how to conduct a partial hepatectomy addressing solutions to potential problems. This approach introduces a rapid and reproducible orthotopic implantation model of liver tumor model in a minimally invasive manner, allowing the surgical procedure to be optimized with minimal intra-abdominal injury.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Brown et al.1

Before you begin

This protocol describes various steps to generate a liver tumor model utilizing a hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) cell line, RIL-175, in wild type C57BL/6 mice. This approach is minimally invasive via ultrasound image guidance. The selected cell line possesses unique luciferase properties; therefore, surveillance of tumor burden can be performed utilizing in vivo bioluminescent imaging (BLI). We subsequently delineate how to perform a partial hepatectomy once the tumor has grown to the desired size in a step-by-step fashion.

Survival surgery on mice can be broken down into five major steps, preparation, anesthesia, the surgical procedure, analgesia, and then post-operative care. The operator must ensure that the surgeon is comfortable with performing both anesthesia and the surgical procedure itself. Surgical proficiency can be enhanced by watching videos, reading published methodology, and practicing on euthanized animals. The operator must understand the delicacy of sustaining sterility throughout the procedure using sterile drapes and gloves. All surgical instruments must be autoclaved to ensure sterility before use and after every 5 animals. The use of analgesia requires an opioid and non- steroidal anti-inflammatory drug (such as meloxicam or carprofen). It is best to follow guidelines dictated by individual’s institutional Animal Care and Use Committee.

Furthermore, this protocol requires early planning as tumor kinetics and tumor growth must be considered. Experiments must be planned ahead of time accordingly. Approval from the appropriate animal research review board should be obtained prior to conducting any experiments involving murine models. For this protocol, female C57BL/6 wild type mice were housed on 12-h light/dark cycles and received ad libitum access to food and water.

Cell culture preparation

Timing: 10–12 days 1.Rapidly thaw tumor cells of interest (RIL-175) from the liquid nitrogen storage system. Note: Maintain sterility in all the following steps and in an appropriate unidirectional cell culture hood. 2.Prepare the culture media that is appropriate for these cells, i.e., RPMI + GlutaMAX with 1% penicillin-streptomycin solution, and 10% Fecal Calf Serum (FCS). Note: penicillin-streptomycin is an antibiotic solution that prevents contamination of the culture medium with the growth of Mycoplasma species. 3.Add 10 mL of culture media to a 15 mL tube, then add the cells.4.Centrifuge tube, discard supernatant and resuspend cells in 1 mL of culture media. Transfer the cells to a flask and add desired amount of prepared cell culture medium.5.To allow the cells to acclimate to culture conditions, incubate at 37°C in 5% CO2 in a cell culture incubator following the thawing process.6.After 24 h post thawing, wash cells with Phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Note: washing cells with PBS will remove any leftover dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). Residual DMSO causes reduction in cell proliferation. 7.Verify cell morphology and growth every 1–2 days under a microscope. CRITICAL: Do not allow cells to reach a high level of confluency or over 90% in the flask. This will drastically affect cell behavior and culture kinetics. 8.Every 2–3 days, wash the cells twice with sterile PBS and renew culture medium enough to cover all the cells in the flask.9.Culture cells after three to four passages prior to injection, no more than eight passages.10.Once 80%–90% cell confluency is achieved, prepare cells for the injection process.

Materials and equipment

To explore basic preclinical studies in murine models, we use the Vevo 3100 Imaging System by FUJIFILM VisualSonics ultrasound for real-time, in vivo physiological information. The FUJIFILM VisualSonics Vevo Imaging Station is the second part of the ultrasound workstation.

Advantages of the imaging system include having an integrated rail base to control the height and further adjust forward and backward movements using control knobs increases fine movements and precision. The link in the key resources table is to a user manual. Additionally, this system is equipped with an ultrasound probe transducer-securing mounting system and a temperature-controlled heated platform. Vitals signs (heart rate, respiratory rate, and core temperature) are measured through sensors and reflected on a monitor.Note: Although this model is no longer manufactured and not available by the manufacturer at present, alternative models are currently in use such as Vevo F2 and Vevo F2 LT.

Expected outcomes

With ultrasound guided tumor cell line injection, tumor growth may somewhat lag when compared to injections in an open surgical manner that our group has previously described.4 We anticipate that this is due to multiple factors. 1) The injection of the cell suspension takes place within the parenchyma; this is opposed to the subcapsular injection of the cell suspension in the open technique under direct vision. 2) The speed for which the injection takes place may lead to accidental spillage of the cell suspension. Nonetheless, once the tumor grows, it should reach the expected size when performed in an open manner when a partial hepatectomy to the respective lobe injected has not been performed (Figure 8) Therefore, tumor growth has taken 2 weeks to be properly appreciated on BLI. We recommend imaging the mice twice a week until the desired tumor size is reached.Figure 8Tumor-bearing liverLiver harvested on day 21 post ultrasound-guided injections of RIL-175 of a mouse that has not been subject to a partial hepatectomy.

Limitations

Ultrasound injections and partial hepatectomies are both procedures that are operator dependent. A certain amount of training time is required to effectively perform both procedures. To master both, practice is key, although time consuming. Lack of tumor growth may be a result of poor injection technique or cell preparation. In regards to our location of the injection, we deliberately inject in the left side of the liver, given the fact that we perform a partial hepatectomy as described in the literature to the left lateral and left medial lobes. This is also in line with our ability to perform our partial hepatectomy on the left side to mimic surgical resection of the tumor bearing organ as performed in a clinical setting.

Although advisable to maintain as near to the capsule as possible, choosing an anatomical landmark such as the portal is used to ensure reproducibility to the same location throughout the experiment. Of note, surgical resection to the right lobe is not advisable, the venous and arterial supply to the liver lies in the posterior aspect and would lead to lethal results if injured. In addition, the gallbladder would be disrupted and would compromise spillage of bile into the abdominal cavity.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1: Seeding from injection to adjacent structures

It is vital to ensure that the needle remains within the liver parenchyma during injection. Should the needle bevel accidently bypass the liver in a ‘through and through’ fashion, the tumor cell suspension may reach the mesentery or small bowel. If so, tumor cell seeding may occur. Moreover, if the needle mistakenly injects the portal vein, this will cause the pancreas to form tumors in a metastatic manner (Figure 9). Seeding may also take place upon needle extraction after the intrahepatic injection.Figure 9Accidental seeding into liver adjacent structuresAccidental injection of tumor cell line into the portal vein leading to a pancreatic lesion (yellow arrow).

Accidental seeding into liver adjacent structures

Accidental injection of tumor cell line into the portal vein leading to a pancreatic lesion (yellow arrow).

Potential solution

Injection into liver-adjacent structures is avoided by obtaining a clear visualization of the needle bevel within the liver parenchyma (Please refer to the corresponding protocol step 26). While the mouse is placed on the heated platform, use the control knobs to move forward/backwards, left or right if liver parenchyma visualization is not in the center of the screen. The depth of the image reflected on the ultrasound screen may also be manipulated to ensure optimal needle visualization. Once the needle is in the optimal spot, inject the cells slowly and over 3–4 s to decrease spillover. Never inject cells in a blind manner. The needle must be retracted slowly after the tumor suspension has been injected.

Problem 2: Hypothermia

Shaving and removal of fur from the abdominal wall may subject mice to hypothermia due to their high surface area to body weight ratio. Additionally, hypothermia is also an adverse event during anesthesia. Nonetheless, hypothermia may lead to bradycardia, delayed recovery, and disturbed circadian rhythm. An obvious indication of hypothermia includes shivering, if detected in mice, indicates inadequate warming methods throughout the procedure and requires immediate attention from the operator. Therefore, mice will require supplementary thermal support by external heating devices during and after anesthesia.

Potential solution

The best option is to avoid hypothermia altogether, this is done through many different methods.

A heated platform is used during ultrasound injections transmitting heat to the mouse throughout the procedure (Please refer to the corresponding protocol step 4).•The gel used during ultrasound-guided injections is kept in a warmer.•Hand warmers and a heated lamp light are both utilized during the recovery period located within the cage or directed at the cage until the mouse returns to normal mobilization and placed back into the original housing cage.

Problem 3: Abdominal wall injury

On entering the abdomen, avoid steering away from the midline and linea alba. The linea alba is an avascular plane and ideal for bloodless entry into the abdomen. Moreover, there are blood vessels present on either side of the abdominal wall and bleeding may be encountered if injured.

Potential solution

Silver nitrate maybe used for hemostasis and should be present on the operating field for easy access when performing the partial hepatectomy. It is best to address the bleeding as soon as it has been detected as blood may potentially obscure visualization of the surgical field if left unattended (Figure 10). Of note, silver nitrate should only be used for minor bleeding. Applying pressure to the bleeding area is another method that leads to bleeding tamponade. Major bleeding may also be tied off; however, this is rarely encountered.Figure 10Intraoperative bleeding controlThe use of silver nitrate to control minor bleeding from the abdominal wall.

Problem 4: Surgical bleeding

Bleeding may occur from hepatic vessels secondary to loosely tied knots or from the surgical liver surface. If bleeding from the hepatic vessels is encountered, this is usually brisk bleeding that is difficult to control, and typically fatal. In addition, technical errors that lead to hemorrhage from hepatic vessels may not be apparent immediately in the early post-operative period, however, can result in mice death within 24 h of procedure. Accidental release of the surgical knot by accidently cutting the knot around the liver used will lead to immediate brisk bleeding and mouse demise. This must be avoided at all times.

Potential solution

•Surgical knots must be secure when performing hepatectomy; however, these knots should not be too tight. If a loose knots or an ‘air knots’ is accidently made, the knot should be cut and made once again in the proper manner.•All tissue must be handled in a gentle manner. Any toothed or sharp instruments should not be used inside the abdominal cavity.•Silver nitrate can be used on the liver bed after resection, but not for major bleeding. In addition, silver nitrate should never be used blindly inside the abdominal cavity. This may cause necrosis to surrounding tissue.

Problem 5: Wound infection or wound dehiscence

Surgical site infection or wound dehiscence may potentially occur should the mouse attempt to remove loosely placed metal surgical clips. Additionally, a wound infection may occur if sterilization is breeched from the operating surgeon contaminating the surgical field.

Potential solution

To avoid dehiscence, the peritoneum is approximated with an interrupted Vicryl suture layer for intensified strength and faster healing. The skin is then closed with metal clips. It is important to ensure that the metal surgical clips are secure and bring the wound edges parallel to each other for adequate healing.

When working on multiple cages of mice, we recommend changing gloves, gowns, and sterile drapes for every cage, i.e., every 5 mice. Maintain sterility throughout surgical procedures by avoiding cross contamination and having sterilized instruments in close proximity to the surgeons reach on a sterile field.
