Cyclicity Analysis of the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck Process
Vivek Kaushik

Abstract
In this thesis, we consider an N N -dimensional Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) process { 𝐱 ⁡ ( t ) } t ≥ 0 \left\{\mathbf{x}(t)\right\}_{t\geq 0} satisfying the linear stochastic differential equation d ​ 𝐱 ​ ( t ) = − 𝐁 ​ 𝐱 ​ ( t ) ​ d ​ t + 𝚺 ​ d ​ 𝐰 ​ ( t ) . d\mathbf{x}(t)=-\mathbf{B}\ \mathbf{x}(t)\ dt+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\ d\mathbf{w}(t). Here, 𝐁 \mathbf{B} is a fixed N × N N\times N circulant friction matrix whose eigenvalues have positive real parts, 𝚺 \boldsymbol{\Sigma} is a fixed N × M N\times M matrix for some M ∈ ℕ , M\in\mathbb{N}, and { 𝐰 ⁡ ( t ) } t ≥ 0 \left\{\mathbf{w}(t)\right\}_{t\geq 0} is the standard M M -dimensional Wiener process. We consider a signal propagation model governed by this OU process. In this model, an underlying signal propagates throughout a network consisting of N N linked sensors located in space. For each t ≥ 0 , t\geq 0, we interpret x n ​ ( t ) , x_{n}(t), the n n -th component of the OU process at time t , t, as the measurement of the propagating effect made by the n n -th sensor. The matrix 𝐁 \mathbf{B} represents the sensor network structure: if 𝐁 \mathbf{B} has first row ( b 1 , … , b N ) , (b_{1}\ ,\ \dots\ ,\ b_{N}), where b 1 > 0 b_{1}>0 and b 2 , … , b N ≤ 0 , b_{2}\ ,\ \dots\ ,\ b_{N}\leq 0, then the magnitude of b p b_{p} quantifies how receptive the n n -th sensor is to activity within the ( n + p − 1 ) (n+p-1) -th sensor, where n + p − 1 n+p-1 is indexed mod N . N. Finally, the ( m , n ) (m,n) -th entry of the matrix 𝐃 = 𝚺 ​ 𝚺 T 2 \mathbf{D}=\frac{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\text{T}}}{2} is the covariance of the component noises injected into the m m -th and n n -th sensors. For different choices of 𝐁 \mathbf{B} and 𝚺 , \boldsymbol{\Sigma}, we investigate whether Cyclicity Analysis enables us to recover the structure of network. Roughly speaking, Cyclicity Analysis studies the lead-lag dynamics pertaining to the components of a multivariate signal. We specifically consider an N × N N\times N skew-symmetric matrix 𝐐 , \mathbf{Q}, known as the lead matrix, in which the sign of its ( m , n ) (m,n) -th entry captures the lead-lag relationship between the m m -th and n n -th component OU processes. 1.1
Leader-Follower Dynamics Questions

In this section, we pose two very general questions pertaining to the leader-follower dynamics amongst the
N
components of
\mathbf{x}
relative to their evolution throughout time. These questions are central to the thesis as a whole.

First, fix two specific indices
1\leq m,n\leq N
and assume that the corresponding component signals
x_{m}
and
x_{n}
evolve very similarly up to a time shift. Does
x_{n}

lead (precede)
or
follow (lag)

x_{m}
throughout time ? To illustrate the meaning of this question, we consider the signals
f,g:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined by
f(t)=\exp(-\pi t^{2})
and
g(t)=f(t-1).
We plot these signals in
Figure
1.1
.
Note that
g
is a horizontal translation of
f
to the right by
1
unit of time, and the number
1
is the value of the time shift. As a result, throughout time, we can describe the evolution of
f
and
g
in relation to one another: when
f
increases,
g
later increases, and when
f
decreases,
g
later decreases. Thus, we say that
f
leads
g,
or equivalently,
g
follows
f.

f(t)=e^{-\pi t^{2}}
and
g(t)=f(t-1).
We interpret
f
to lead
g.

Next, assume all of the
N
component signals of
\mathbf{x}
trace some underlying real-valued signal up to scaling constants and time shifts. What is the order in which the component signals evolve throughout time ? More specifically, what is the order of the time shifts ? To illustrate the meaning of this question, let
T
be the time interval
[0,1],
and consider the sinusoidal signal
\mathbf{x}:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N},
whose
n
-th component signal is defined by

\displaystyle x_{n}(t)

\displaystyle=\sin\left(2\pi\left(t-\frac{n-1}{N}\right)\right)

for each

In

-th component signal. Thus, the

N
component signals evolve throughout time is

1.2
Analysis of Periodic Signals

In this section, we focus on analyzing periodic signals. Recall that a signal
\mathbf{x}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N}
is
periodic
if there exists a minimal constant
P>0
such that
\mathbf{x}(t+P)=\mathbf{x}(t)
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}.
The constant
P
is known as the
period
of
\mathbf{x}.
We specifically say
\mathbf{x}
is
P
-periodic in order to emphasize
\mathbf{x}
has period
P
. Equivalently, we can view a
P
-periodic signal as a well-defined map between
\mathbb{R}/(P\mathbb{Z})
and
\mathbb{R}^{N},
where
\mathbb{R}/(P\mathbb{Z})
is the additive group
[0,P)
equipped with addition modulo
P.

To analyze periodic signals in general, one uses tools from harmonic analysis, which include
Fourier series

[
1
, Chapter 2]
and the
Fourier transform

[
1
, Chapter 5]
. Another common tool that is suitable for analyzing periodic signals is the cross-correlation function
[
2
, Chapter 12]
.
Recall if
f,g:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
are two
P
-periodic signals, then the
(periodic) cross-correlation
of
f
and
g
is the
P
-periodic signal
f\star g:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined by

\displaystyle(f\star g)(\tau)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{P}f(t-\tau)\ g(t)\ dt.

(1.2)

The cross-correlation function measures the similarity between the input signals
f
and
g
as a function of the displacement variable
\tau.
The displacement at which the cross-correlation attains its absolute maximum reveals information about
f
and
g
. Up to an integer multiple of the period, the maximum displacement coincides with the time shift value such that shifting
f
by this value would result in the signals
f
and
g
being most aligned.

For example, let
f,g:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be the periodic signals
f(t)=\sin(2\pi t)
and
g(t)=\cos(2\pi t).
Explicitly computing the cross-correlation of
f
and
g
using (
1.2
), we obtain

\displaystyle(f\star g)(\tau)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\sin(2\pi(t-\tau))\cos(2\pi t)\ dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\left(\sin(2\pi t)\cos(2\pi\tau)-\sin(2\pi\tau)\cos(2\pi t)\right)\cos(2\pi t)\ dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\sin(2\pi t)\cos(2\pi t)\cos(2\pi\tau)-\sin(2\pi\tau)\cos^{2}(2\pi t)\ dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\sin(4\pi t)}{2}\cos(2\pi\tau)-\sin(2\pi\tau)\left(\frac{1-\cos(4\pi t)}{2}\right)\ dt

\displaystyle=-\frac{\sin(2\pi\tau)}{2}.

We plot this cross-correlation function in
Figure
1.3
. On one hand, note that
g
is a time-shifted copy of
f
to the right by
\frac{3}{4}
units of time. On the other hand, we observe the cross-correlation function
f\star g
attains its maximum at
\tau=\frac{3}{4}+k
for any
k\in\mathbb{Z}.
This means the displacement at which the cross-correlation function attains its maximum coincides with the time shift between
f
and
g
up to an integer.

and
g
on the left, and we use a dashed red line to indicate the time shift needed to align

with

coincides with the displacement at which

1.2.1
The Chain of Offsets Model

We answer the leader follower dynamics questions that we posed in
Section
1.1
for periodic signals. We assume that
\mathbf{x}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N}
obeys a
Chain of Offsets model (COOM)
, which was first introduced in
[
3
]
. This model stipulates the existence of a
P
-periodic signal
\phi:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},
scaling constants
c_{1}\ ,\ \dots\ ,\ c_{N}>0,
and
offsets

\alpha_{1}\ ,\ \dots\ ,\ \alpha_{N}\in\mathbb{R}/(P\mathbb{Z})
such that

\displaystyle x_{n}(t)

\displaystyle=c_{n}\phi(t-\alpha_{n})

(1.3)

for each
1\leq n\leq N.
In other words, under COOM, all component signals of
\mathbf{x}
trace an underlying real-valued periodic signal up to scaling constants and offsets.

Answering our posed leader follower dynamics questions amounts to determining the order of the offsets
\alpha_{1}\ ,\ \dots\ ,\ \alpha_{N}
under COOM. However, we emphasize this order of offsets is a
cyclic order
; it corresponds to the order in which the points
e^{2\pi i\alpha_{1}}\ ,\ \dots\ ,\ e^{2\pi i\alpha_{N}}
are traversed on the unit circle in the counterclockwise direction. We represent the cyclic order as a permutation
\sigma\in S_{N}
of the indices
\left\{1\ ,\ \dots\ ,\ N\right\}
satisfying the condition that
e^{2\pi i\alpha_{\sigma(n)}}
comes before
e^{2\pi i\alpha_{\sigma(n+1)}}
in the counterclockwise direction.
In terms of the signals themselves, we interpret
x_{\sigma(n)}
as (cyclically) preceding
x_{\sigma(n+1)}
for each
1\leq n<N.
Furthermore,
\sigma
is unique up to cyclic shifts of indices. This means if
\widetilde{\sigma}\in S_{N}
is another permutation representing the cyclic order of the offsets, then there exists an integer
d
such that

\displaystyle\widetilde{\sigma}(n)=\sigma(d+n-1)

for all
1\leq n\leq N,
in which
d+n-1
is indexed mod
N.

We give an example of how a permutation representing the cyclic order is unique up to cyclic shifts. Reconsider the sinsoidal signal
\mathbf{x}
with
n
-th component signal defined in (
1.1
) for each
1\leq n\leq N.
Note that
\mathbf{x}
satisfies COOM in (
1.3
) if we set
\phi(t)=\sin(2\pi t)
and
c_{n}=1
and
\alpha_{n}=\frac{n-1}{N}
for each
1\leq n\leq N.
On one hand, note that
0=\alpha_{1}<\ \dots\ <\alpha_{N}<1,
which means the points
e^{2\pi i\alpha_{1}}\ ,\ \dots\ ,\ e^{2\pi i\alpha_{N}}
are traversed in that order on the unit circle in the counterclockwise direction beginning from the positive real axis. Hence, the identity permutation
\text{Id}\in S_{N}
is one permutation that represents the cyclic order of the offsets. In fact, any permutation
\widetilde{\sigma}\in S_{N}
defined by
\widetilde{\sigma}(n)=n+d,
where
d\in\mathbb{N}
is a fixed integer and
n+d
is indexed mod
N,
is a valid permutation representing the cyclic order of the offsets. Nevertheless, observe that

\displaystyle\text{Id}(n)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\sigma}(n+N-d),

(1.4)

which means the identity permutation is equal to
\widetilde{\sigma}
up to
N-d
cyclic shifts.

1.2.2
Recovery of the Cyclic Order under COOM

Given the
P
-periodic signal
\mathbf{x},
we outline a procedure to recover the cyclic order of the offsets
\alpha_{1}\ ,\ \dots\ ,\ \alpha_{N}
under COOM. This procedure involves the utilization of the cross-correlation function, which we defined previously in (
1.7
).

First, we write the underlying
P
-periodic signal
\phi
in COOM as a Fourier series:

\displaystyle\phi(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\hat{\phi}_{k}\ e^{\frac{2\pi ikt}{P}},

(1.5)

where
\hat{\phi}_{k}
is the
k
-th Fourier coefficient of
\phi
for each
k\in\mathbb{Z}.
Then, for each
1\leq m,n\leq N,
the cross-correlation of the component signals
x_{m}
and
x_{n}
is explicitly

\displaystyle(x_{m}\star x_{n})(\tau)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{P}x_{m}(t-\tau)\ x_{n}(t)\ dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{P}\sum_{k,\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}c_{m}c_{n}\ \hat{\phi}_{k}\ \hat{\phi_{\ell}}\ e^{\frac{2\pi ik(t-\tau-\alpha_{m})+2\pi i\ell(t-\alpha_{n})}{P}}\ dt

\displaystyle=c_{m}c_{n}\sum_{k,\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}\hat{\phi}_{k}\ \hat{\phi_{\ell}}\ e^{\frac{-2\pi ik(\tau+\alpha_{m})-2\pi i\ell\alpha_{n}}{P}}\int_{0}^{P}e^{\frac{2\pi i(k+\ell)t}{P}}dt

\displaystyle=c_{m}c_{n}\sum_{k,\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}\hat{\phi}_{k}\ \hat{\phi_{\ell}}\ e^{\frac{-2\pi ik(\tau+\alpha_{m})-2\pi i\ell\alpha_{n}}{P}}\ P\ \delta_{k,-\ell}

\displaystyle=Pc_{m}c_{n}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\left|\hat{\phi}_{k}\right|^{2}\ e^{\frac{2\pi ik(\alpha_{n}-\alpha_{m}-\tau)}{P}}

\displaystyle=Pc_{m}c_{n}\left|\hat{\phi}_{0}\right|^{2}+2Pc_{m}c_{n}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\left|\hat{\phi}_{k}\right|^{2}\cos\left(\frac{2\pi k(\alpha_{n}-\alpha_{m}-\tau)}{P}\right),

where
\delta_{\cdot,\cdot}
is the Kronecker delta function. Differentiating the cross-correlation function with respect to
\tau
, we obtain

\displaystyle(x_{m}\star x_{n})^{\prime}(\tau)

\displaystyle=-4\pi c_{m}c_{n}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}k\left|\hat{\phi}_{k}\right|^{2}\sin\left(\frac{2\pi k(\alpha_{n}-\alpha_{m}-\tau)}{P}\right).

Equating the derivative to
0,
we see that any critical point of
x_{m}\star x_{n}
is of the form

\displaystyle\tau

\displaystyle=\alpha_{n}-\alpha_{m}+kP,

where
k\in\mathbb{Z}.
This means that the critical points of the
x_{m}\star x_{n}
are equal to the difference between the offsets
\alpha_{n}
and
\alpha_{m}
up to integer multiples of the period
P.

Now, we are ready to construct a permutation of the indices
\left\{1\ ,\ \dots\ ,\ N\right\}
representing the cyclic order of the offsets under COOM. For each
1\leq m,n\leq N,
let

\displaystyle\tau_{m,n}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{n}-\alpha_{m},

in which
\alpha_{n}-\alpha_{m}
is specifically reduced modulo
P.
More explicitly, we choose an integer
k\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
\alpha_{n}-\alpha_{m}+kP\in[0,P)
and set
\tau_{m,n}
equal to
\alpha_{n}-\alpha_{m}+kP
for this choice of
k.
Define the permutation
\sigma\in S_{N}
be a permutation as follows: let
\sigma(1)
be arbitrary, and for each
1<n\leq N,
let
\sigma(n)
be the smallest index not in
\left\{\sigma(1)\ ,\ \dots\ ,\ \sigma(n-1)\right\}
such that
\tau_{\sigma(n-1)\ ,\ \sigma(n)}
is minimal i.e. we have
\tau_{\sigma(n-1)\ ,\ \sigma(n)}\leq\tau_{\sigma(n-1)\ ,\ m}
for all
m\notin\left\{\sigma(1)\ ,\ \dots\ ,\ \sigma(n-1)\right\}.
Observe
\sigma
ensures that
e^{2\pi i\alpha_{\sigma(n)}}
is the closest point on the unit circle to the point
e^{2\pi i\alpha_{\sigma(n-1)}}
in the counterclockwise direction. Therefore,
\sigma
is a candidate permutation representing the cyclic order.

It remains to show that our constructed permutation
\sigma
from the previous paragraph is unique up to cyclic shifts. To this end, let
\widetilde{\sigma}\in S_{N}
be another permutation constructed according to the procedure in the previous paragraph. Note that there exists a unique index
1\leq d\leq N
such that
\widetilde{\sigma}(1)=\sigma(d),
which follows from the fact that the composition
\widetilde{\sigma}\circ\sigma^{-1}
is itself a permutation in
S_{N}.
We claim that for this chosen index
d,
we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{\sigma}(n)

\displaystyle=\sigma(d+n-1)

(1.6)

for all
1\leq n\leq N,
in which
d+n-1
is indexed mod
N.
We prove this by induction. The statement (
1.6
) already holds for
n=1
by the property of
d.
Now, suppose the statement (
1.6
) holds for
n=k
for some
1<k<N.
We show it must also hold for
n=k+1.
By the construction of
\widetilde{\sigma},
we have that
\widetilde{\sigma}(k+1)
is the smallest index not in
\left\{\widetilde{\sigma}(1)\ ,\ \dots\ ,\ \widetilde{\sigma}(k)\right\}
such that
\tau_{\widetilde{\sigma}(k)\ ,\ \widetilde{\sigma}(k+1)}
is minimal. On the other hand, by the induction hypothesis,
\widetilde{\sigma}(k+1)
is the smallest index not in
\left\{\sigma(1)\ ,\ \dots\ ,\ \sigma(d+k-1)\right\}
such that
\tau_{\sigma(d+k-1)\ ,\ \widetilde{\sigma}(k+1)}
is minimal. By the construction of
\sigma,
we conclude
\widetilde{\sigma}(k+1)=\sigma(d+k).
Therefore, the statement (
1.6
) holds for
n=k+1.
Thus, we have shown our candidate permutation
\sigma
is unique up to cyclic shifts.

1.3
Analysis of Non-Periodic Signals

In the previous section, we utilized the cross-correlation function to answer our posed leader follower dynamics questions in
Section
1.1
for periodic signals. In this section, we investigate whether cross-correlation can be used to answer our posed leader follower dynamics questions for signals that are not necessarily periodic. The
cross-correlation
of two generic signals
f,g:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is defined as the map
f\star g:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},
where

\displaystyle(f\star g)(\tau)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(t-\tau)g(t)\ dt,

(1.7)

provided the integral on the right hand side exists.

1.3.1
Time Shifted Model

Suppose
f:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a signal that is compactly supported in a closed interval i.e. the closure of the set of all points
t\in\mathbb{R}
with
f(t)\neq 0
is a closed interval. Let
g:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be the map defined by

\displaystyle g(t)

\displaystyle=f(t-\tau_{0}).

(1.8)

for some time shift
\tau_{0}\in\mathbb{R}.
We refer to the model (
1.8
) as a
time shifted model
. Determining the leader follower relationship between
f
and
g
under the time shifted model amounts to determining the sign of the time shift
\tau_{0}.
If
\tau_{0}>0,
then we interpret
f
to lead
g.
If
\tau_{0}<0,
then we interpret
f
to follow
g.

Given the signals
f
and
g,
we determine the value of the time shift
\tau_{0}
under the time shifted model (
1.8
). In order to do this, we utilize the cross-correlation of
f
and
g
defined in (
1.7
). For each
\tau\in\mathbb{R},
we have

\displaystyle(f\star g)(\tau)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(t-\tau)\ g(t)\ dt

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{(f(t-\tau))^{2}+(g(t))^{2}-(f(t-\tau)-g(t))^{2}}{2}\ dt

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{(f(t-\tau))^{2}+(f(t-\tau_{0}))^{2}}{2}\ dt-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\left(f(t-\tau)-f(t-\tau_{0})\right)^{2}}{2}\ dt

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{(f(t-\tau))^{2}}{2}\ dt+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{(f(t-\tau_{0}))^{2}}{2}\ dt-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\left(f(t-\tau)-f(t-\tau_{0})\right)^{2}}{2}\ dt

(1.9)

\displaystyle=(f\star f)(0)-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\left(f(t-\tau)-f(t-\tau_{0})\right)^{2}}{2}\ dt,

(1.10)

in which the first term of (
1.10
) follows from observing the first two integral terms in (
1.9
) are both equal to
\frac{(f\star f)(0)}{2}
via the respective changes of variables
t\mapsto t-\tau
and
t\mapsto t-\tau_{0}.

Note that the integral after the minus sign in (
1.9
) is a non-negative quantity because it is the integral of a non-negative function. This implies the cross-correlation function has an upper bound

\displaystyle(f\star g)(\tau)\leq(f\star f)(0).

(1.11)

for all
\tau\in\mathbb{R}.
Equality in (
1.11
) holds if and only if the integral term in (
1.10
) is
0
for some
\tau\in\mathbb{R}.
Because
f
is continuous and compactly supported in a closed interval, equality in (
1.11
) holds if and only if the integrand of the integral term in (
1.10
) is
0
i.e.

\displaystyle f(t-\tau)

\displaystyle=f(t-\tau_{0}).

for all
t\in\mathbb{R}.
But since
f
is not periodic, we have
(f\star g)(\tau)=(f\star f)(0)
if and only if
\tau=\tau_{0}.

Therefore, the unique displacement at which the cross-correlation
f\star g
attains its maximum is precisely the time shift
\tau_{0}
under the model (
1.8
). Having recovered the time shift
\tau_{0},
we can determine the leader follower relationship between the signals
f
and
g
upon inspecting the sign of
\tau_{0}.

1.3.2
Time Reparameterization Invariance

Based on our analysis so far, it seems that the cross-correlation function is a useful signal processing tool that enables us to determine the leader follower relationship between any two input signals, provided these signals have nice enough properties. We wonder whether there are drawbacks to using the cross-correlation.

We focus on one drawback central to the thesis. The cross-correlation function suffers under time reparameterization. A
time reparameterization
of the time interval
T
is a strictly increasing homeomorphism between
T
and itself. This means that if we use the cross-correlation as a tool to analyze signals, then the results given by cross-correlation may not be the same across all time reparameterizations. In other words, the results we obtain via cross-correlation may vary according to how we observe the signals throughout time.

To illustrate with an example, let
T=[-3,2],
and consider the signals
f:[-3,2]\rightarrow\mathbb{R},
where

\displaystyle f(t)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\sin^{2}(2\pi t)&t\in[-1,1]\\
0&\text{else}\end{cases}

(1.12)

and
g(t)=f\left(t-\frac{1}{4}\right).
Consider the map
r:[-3,2]\rightarrow[-3,2]
defined by

\displaystyle r(t)=\frac{t^{3}+6}{7}.

(1.13)

Note that
r
is a valid time reparameterization of
[-3,2].
Indeed, we have
r(-3)=-3
and
r(2)=2,
and since
t\mapsto t^{3}
is strictly increasing on
\mathbb{R}
, the map
t\mapsto r(t)
is also strictly increasing on
\mathbb{R}
and thus strictly increasing in
[-3,2].
Therefore,
r([-3,2])=[-3,2].
Finally, the inverse map
r^{-1}(y)=(7y-6)^{\frac{1}{3}}
is continuous on all of
\mathbb{R}
and is thus continuous in
[-3,2].
Therefore,
r
is a strictly increasing homeomorphism, which means it is a valid time reparameterization. However, in
Figure
1.4
, we plot the correlation functions
f\star g
and
(f\circ r)\star(g\circ r).
We see that the displacement maximum for the original cross-correlation
f\star g
and the displacement maximum for the reparameterized cross-correlation
(f\circ r)\star(g\circ r)
differ in sign. Hence, the cross-correlation function does not allow us to conclude whether
f
leads
g
or vice versa.

Figure 1.4:
We illustrate how time reparameterization can impact the results of cross-correlation. Consider the signal

defined by

for
t\in[-1,1]
and
0
otherwise. Let
g(t)=f(t-0.25).
In the first row, we plot the original signals
f
and
g
on the left, and we plot the reparameterized signals
f\circ r
and
g\circ r
on the right. In the second row, we plot the cross-correlation
f\star g
on the left and the cross-correlation
(f\circ r)\star(g\circ r)
on the right. Observe the displacement at which
(f\star g)
takes on its maximum differs in sign from the displacement at which
(f\circ r)\star(g\circ r)
takes on its maximum.

Thus, in order to perform any meaningful analysis of signals, we need to consider using tools that are time reparameterization invariant.

1.4
Cyclicity Analysis

In this section, we consider cyclic signals. A signal
\mathbf{x}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N}
is
cyclic
if
\mathbf{x}\circ r
is periodic for some time reparameterization
r:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}.
Cyclic signals generalize periodic signals. Roughly speaking, cyclic signals have repeating temporal patterns, but such patterns do not repeat in a predictable fashion, which makes them different from periodic signals. Many real world phenomena are cyclic in nature. For example, the economy undergoes expansion and recession, but not predictably due to various external factors that control the economy. Other examples include cardiac cycles and fMRI signals
[
4
]
.

We illustrate the difference between a periodic and a cyclic signal with a theoretical example. Consider the signals
f(t)=\sin(2\pi t)
and
g(t)=f(t^{3})
on
\mathbb{R}.
In
Figure
1.5
, we plot
f
and
g
and the resulting signals we observe after shifting the time axis by
1
unit. Since
f
is
1
-periodic, shifting the time axis by
1
unit results in us observing the exact same signal that we started with. On the other hand,
g
is not
1
-periodic; shifting the time axis by
1
unit results in us observing a completely different signal than the one we started with. In fact,
g
is not
P
-periodic for any constant
P>0.
However,
g
is cyclic: if
r:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is defined by
r(t)=t^{\frac{1}{3}},
then note that
r
is a valid time reparameterization satisfying
f=g\circ r.

Figure 1.5:
We illustrate the difference between a cyclic and a periodic signal. Let
f(t)=\sin(2\pi t)
and
g(t)=f(t^{3}).
On the first row, we plot
f(t)
on the left and
g(t)
on the right. On the second row, we plot
f(t+1)
on the left and
g(t+1)
on the right. Note
f
is unaltered by the time shift of
1
unit, but
g
is altered.

In this section, we describe the procedure of
Cyclicity Analysis
, first introduced in
[
3
]
, which is the study of the leader-follower dynamics pertaining to cyclic signals. Cyclicity Analysis directly answers both of our posed leader follower questions in
Section
1.1
in a time reparameterization invariant fashion.

1.4.1
Iterated Path Integrals

First, we assume
\mathbf{x}:T\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N}
is a generic signal in which
T=[a,b]
is a closed interval. We recall the first question we posed in
Section
1.1
, which is to determine the pairwise leader follower relationship between the
m
-th component and
n
-th component signals of
\mathbf{x}
for fixed indices
1\leq m,n\leq N,
assuming such component signals evolve similarly throughout time.

In order to answer this question,
Cyclicity Analysis invokes the theory of iterated path integrals
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
]
. For each
K\in\mathbb{N}
, a
K
-th order
iterated path integral
of
\mathbf{x}
is a functional
\text{IP}_{i_{1}\ ,\ \dots\ ,\ i_{K}}
of the form

\displaystyle\text{IP}_{i_{1}\ ,\ \dots\ ,\ i_{K}}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{K!}\int_{a\leq t_{1}\leq\ \dots\ \leq t_{K}\leq b}x^{\prime}_{i_{1}}(t_{1})\ \dots\ x^{\prime}_{i_{K}}(t_{K})\ dt_{K}\ \dots\ dt_{1},

(1.14)

where
1\leq i_{1}\leq\ \dots\ \leq i_{K}\leq N.
For example, a first order iterated path integral of
\mathbf{x}
is explicitly of the form

\displaystyle\text{IP}_{n}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{b}x^{\prime}_{n}(t)\ dt

\displaystyle=x_{n}(b)-x_{n}(a),

which is the
increment
of the trajectory parameterized by
x_{n}:T\rightarrow\mathbb{R}.

Iterated path integrals date back to the times of Riemann and Picard. They were initially used as tools to construct and analyze solutions to ordinary differential equations. Later on, K.T. Chen
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
]
discovered the relevance of iterated path integrals to algebraic topology, while Terry Lyons
[
9
,
10
]
discovered the relevance to the analysis of
rough paths
, which are Holder trajectories.

Iterated path integrals of the signal
\mathbf{x}
obey several invariant properties
[
3
]
, which make them a suitable tool for data analysis. Firstly, they are indeed time reparameterization invariant: for any time reparameterization
r:T\rightarrow T,
we have

\displaystyle\text{IP}_{i_{1}\ ,\ \dots\ ,\ i_{K}}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\text{IP}_{i_{1}\ ,\ \dots\ ,\ i_{K}}(\mathbf{x}\circ r),

which follows directly from performing a change of variables
t\mapsto r(t)
in (
1.14
). Next, the iterated path integrals of
\mathbf{x}
are invariant under parallel translation: if
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is fixed and if
\mathbf{y}:T\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N}
is the signal defined by
\mathbf{y}(t)=\mathbf{x}(t)+\mathbf{a},
then

\displaystyle\text{IP}_{i_{1}\ ,\ \dots\ ,\ i_{K}}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\text{IP}_{i_{1}\ ,\ \dots\ ,\ i_{K}}(\mathbf{y}).

Moreover, the iterated path integrals are invariant under
detours
. If
C_{1}\ ,\ \dots\ ,\ C_{L}
are oriented curves in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
such that the ending point of
C_{\ell}
is the starting point of
C_{\ell+1},
their concatenation possesses a
detour
if for some
1\leq\ell<L,
the curves
C_{\ell+1}
and
C_{\ell}
are the same but with opposite orientations. In other words, if we traverse
C_{1}\ ,\ \dots\ ,\ C_{L}
in that order, then our traversal will involve backtracking. If
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
parameterize the same curve, in which
\mathbf{x}
traverses it with detours but
\mathbf{y}
traverses it without detours, then

\displaystyle\text{IP}_{i_{1}\ ,\ \dots\ ,\ i_{K}}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\text{IP}_{i_{1}\ ,\ \dots\ ,\ i_{K}}(\mathbf{y}).

Finally, K.T. Chen
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
]
proved a stronger statement: two signals
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}:T\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N}
that parameterize their respective curves without detours are the same up to time reparameterization and parallel translation
if and only if
all of their corresponding iterated path integrals agree.

1.4.2
Oriented Areas

Now, assume
\mathbf{x}:T\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N}
parameterizes a closed curve i.e.
\mathbf{x}(a)=\mathbf{x}(b).
We consider the second order iterated path integrals of
\mathbf{x}.
For each
1\leq m,n\leq N,
we substitute
K=2
and
i_{1}=m
and
i_{2}=n
into (
1.14
) to obtain the explicit form of the second order iterated path integral:

\displaystyle\text{IP}_{m,n}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{a\leq s\leq t\leq b}x_{m}^{\prime}(s)\ x_{n}^{\prime}(t)\ ds\ dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{a}^{b}\int_{a}^{t}x_{m}^{\prime}(s)\ x_{n}^{\prime}(t)\ ds\ dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{a}^{b}(x_{m}(t)-x_{m}(a))\ x_{n}^{\prime}(t)\ dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{a}^{b}x_{m}(t)\ x_{n}^{\prime}(t)\ dt-\frac{\left(x_{n}(b)-x_{n}(a)\right)\ x_{m}(a)}{2}

(1.15)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{a}^{b}x_{m}(t)\ x_{n}^{\prime}(t)\ dt,

in which the second term of (
1.15
) vanishes due to the assumption
\mathbf{x}
parameterizes a closed curve.

For all
1\leq m,n\leq N,
we define

\displaystyle A_{m,n}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\text{IP}_{m,n}(\mathbf{x})-\text{IP}_{n,m}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{a}^{b}x_{m}(t)\ x_{n}^{\prime}(t)-x_{n}(t)\ x_{m}^{\prime}(t)\ dt.

(1.16)

By Green’s Theorem, the integral (
1.16
) is a line integral that is equal to the area of the region enclosed by the curve parameterized by the map
(x_{m},x_{n}):T\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}.
We note, however, this area is actually an
oriented (signed) area
. In particular,
A_{m,n}(\mathbf{x})
is positive if
(x_{m},x_{n}):T\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
traverses the aforementioned curve in the counterclockwise direction and is negative otherwise. We heuristically interpret the oriented area as an indicator of the lead-lag relationship between the signals
x_{m}
and
x_{n}
. We say that
x_{m}

leads (precedes)

x_{n}
if
A_{m,n}(\mathbf{x})>0.
We say that
x_{m}

follows (lags)

x_{n}
if
A_{m,n}(\mathbf{x})<0.
Otherwise, we say
x_{m}
and
x_{n}
are
in sync
.

We illustrate the oriented area with an example. Reconsider the signals
f,g:[-\infty,\infty]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with
f(t)=e^{-\pi t^{2}}
and
g(t)=f(t-1)
with
f(\pm\infty)=g(\pm\infty)=0.
The curve
(f,g):[-\infty,\infty]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a closed curve oriented in the counterclockwise direction, which we plot in
Figure
1.6
. The oriented area of the region enclosed by this curve is explicitly

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(t)\ g^{\prime}(t)-f^{\prime}(t)\ g(t)\ dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}-2\pi(t-1)e^{-\pi t^{2}}e^{-\pi(t-1)^{2}}+2\pi te^{-\pi t^{2}}e^{-\pi(t-1)^{2}}\ dt

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\pi e^{-\pi t^{2}-\pi(t-1)^{2}}\ dt=\frac{\pi e^{-\frac{\pi}{2}}}{\sqrt{2}},

which is positive. Therefore, we interpret
f
to lead
g
based on the oriented area.

Figure 1.6:
An illustration of the oriented area. Let
f,g:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be the signals
f(t)=e^{-\pi t^{2}}
and
g(t)=f(t-1).
We plot
f
and
g
on the left and plot the curve parameterized by
(f,g):[-\infty,\infty]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
on the right. We indicate the curve’s orientation via black arrows. Because the curve is oriented counterclockwise, we interpret
f
to lead
g.

Recall our assumption
\mathbf{x}
parameterizes a closed curve. We can modify the definition of the oriented area in the case
\mathbf{x}
does not parameterize a closed curve. In this situation, we may consider any map
\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}:T\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N}
that parameterizes a closed curve formed by concatenating the curve parameterized by the original signal
\mathbf{x}
and any oriented curve in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
with starting point
\mathbf{x}(b)
and ending point
\mathbf{x}(a).
Different choices of
\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}
yield different values of
A_{m,n}(\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}).
For our purposes, however, we will choose a signal
\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}:T\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N}
such that

\displaystyle A_{m,n}(\widetilde{\mathbf{x}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{a}^{b}x_{m}(t)\ x_{n}^{\prime}(t)-x_{n}(t)\ x_{m}^{\prime}(t)\ dt.

(1.17)

i.e. the
(m,n)
-th oriented area of
\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}
is the same as the
(m,n)
-th line integral of
\frac{x_{m}x_{n}^{\prime}-x_{n}x_{m}^{\prime}}{2}
over the curve parameterized by
(x_{m},x_{n}):T\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}.

Consider the signal
\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}:T\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N}
defined by

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}(t)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\mathbf{x}(2t-a)&t\in\left[a,\frac{a+b}{2}\right]\\
\mathbf{x}(b)+\left(\mathbf{x}(a)-\mathbf{x}(b)\right)\left(\frac{a+b-2t}{a-b}\right)&t\in\left[\frac{a+b}{2},b\right].\end{cases}

(1.18)

This signal parameterizes the curve formed by concatenating the curve parameterized by the original signal
\mathbf{x}
and the oriented line segment with starting point
\mathbf{x}(b)
and ending point
\mathbf{x}(a).
We show this signal satisfies our requirement (
1.17
). Explicitly computing
A_{m,n}(\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}),
we have

\displaystyle A_{m,n}(\widetilde{\mathbf{x}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{a}^{b}\widetilde{x}_{m}(t)\ \widetilde{x}_{n}^{\prime}(t)-\widetilde{x}_{n}(t)\ \widetilde{x}_{m}^{\prime}(t)\ dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{a}^{\frac{a+b}{2}}\widetilde{x}_{m}(t)\ \widetilde{x}_{n}^{\prime}(t)-\widetilde{x}_{n}(t)\ \widetilde{x}_{m}^{\prime}(t)\ dt

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\frac{a+b}{2}}^{b}\widetilde{x}_{m}(t)\ \widetilde{x}_{n}^{\prime}(t)-\widetilde{x}_{n}(t)\ \widetilde{x}_{m}^{\prime}(t)\ dt

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{\frac{a+b}{2}}x_{m}(2t-a)\ x_{n}^{\prime}(2t-a)-x_{n}(2t-a)\ x_{m}^{\prime}(2t-a)\ dt

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{x_{n}(a)-x_{n}(b)}{b-a}\int_{\frac{a+b}{2}}^{b}x_{m}(b)+\left(x_{m}(a)-x_{m}(b)\right)\left(\frac{a+b-2t}{a-b}\right)\ dt

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\frac{x_{m}(a)-x_{m}(b)}{b-a}\int_{\frac{a+b}{2}}^{b}x_{n}(b)+\left(x_{n}(a)-x_{n}(b)\right)\left(\frac{a+b-2t}{a-b}\right)\ dt

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{\frac{a+b}{2}}x_{m}(2t-a)\ x_{n}^{\prime}(2t-a)-x_{n}(2t-a)\ x_{m}^{\prime}(2t-a)\ dt

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{x_{m}(b)(x_{n}(a)-x_{n}(b))}{2}-\frac{(x_{m}(a)-x_{m}(b))(x_{n}(a)-x_{n}(b))}{4}

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+\frac{x_{n}(b)(x_{m}(a)-x_{m}(b))}{2}+\frac{(x_{n}(a)-x_{n}(b))(x_{m}(a)-x_{m}(b))}{4}

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{\frac{a+b}{2}}x_{m}(2t-a)\ x_{n}^{\prime}(2t-a)-x_{n}(2t-a)\ x_{m}^{\prime}(2t-a)\ dt

(1.19)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{a}^{b}x_{m}(t)\ x_{n}^{\prime}(t)-x_{n}(t)\ x_{m}^{\prime}(t)\ dt,

(1.20)

in which (
1.20
) follows from performing the change of variables
t\mapsto 2t-a
in (
1.19
). Thus, our choice of
\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}
satisfies our specific requirement in (
1.17
).

So from henceforth, if the signal
\mathbf{x}
does not parameterize a closed curve, then we define
A_{m,n}(\mathbf{x})=A_{m,n}(\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}),
where
\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}
is the signal defined in (
1.18
).

1.4.3
The Lead Matrix

If
\mathbf{x}:T\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N}
is a signal with
T=[a,b],
then we will write
A_{m,n}
as the oriented area in place of the original notation
A_{m,n}(\mathbf{x}).
In isolation, the oriented area is simply a number that heuristically indicates the pairwise leader follower relationship between any two specific components of
\mathbf{x}.
For future purposes, however, we would like to consider all of these
N^{2}
total oriented areas together. So we will arrange all the oriented areas into an
N\times N
matrix. Let
\mathbf{A}
be the
N\times N
matrix whose
(m,n)
-th entry is the oriented area
A_{m,n}
for each
1\leq m,n\leq N.
We refer to
\mathbf{A}
as the
lead(-lag) matrix
corresponding to
\mathbf{x}
. More succinctly, we can rewrite
\mathbf{A}
as an integral of a matrix-valued function:

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{a}^{b}\mathbf{x}(t)\ \dot{\mathbf{x}}^{\text{T}}(t)-\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)\ \mathbf{x}^{\text{T}}(t)\ dt,

(1.21)

where
\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)=\left(x_{1}^{\prime}(t)\ ,\ \dots\ ,\ x_{N}^{\prime}(t)\right)
is the derivative of
\mathbf{x}
with respect to
t.

We state several properties pertaining to the lead matrix. Firstly, the matrix
\mathbf{A}
is
skew-symmetric
i.e.
\mathbf{A}^{\text{T}}=-\mathbf{A}.
Equivalently,
A_{m,n}=-A_{n,m}
for all
1\leq m,n\leq N,
which follows immediately upon swapping the indices
m
and
n
in the equation (
1.16
). Next, each eigenvalue of
\mathbf{A}
is either
0
or purely imaginary
[
11
, Exercise 7B.4]
. These purely imaginary eigenvalues of
\mathbf{A}
come in complex conjugate pairs: if
i\lambda
is an eigenvalue of
\mathbf{A}
for some nonzero
\lambda\in\mathbb{R},
then
-i\lambda
is also an eigenvalue of
\mathbf{A}.
Lastly,
\mathbf{A}
is diagonalizable
[
11
, Exercise 7B.4]
. In particular, there is an orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{C}^{N}
consisting of the eigenvectors of
\mathbf{A}.

For each
1\leq n\leq N,
we let
\lambda_{n}(\mathbf{A})
be the
n
-th largest eigenvalue of
\mathbf{A}
in modulus and consider the corresponding orthonormal basis of eigenvectors
\left\{\mathbf{v}_{n}(\mathbf{A})\right\}_{n=1}^{N},
in which
\mathbf{v}_{n}(\mathbf{A})
is the associated unit eigenvector with
\lambda_{n}(\mathbf{A})
for each
1\leq n\leq N.
We refer to the eigenvector
\mathbf{v}_{1}(\mathbf{A})
as the
leading (dominant) eigenvector
of
\mathbf{A}.
If
\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A})
is nonzero, then our notation for the largest eigenvalue and leading eigenvector is actually ambiguous because
\lambda_{2}(\mathbf{A})
would be the complex conjugate of
\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A})
and thus satisfy
|\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A})|=|\lambda_{2}(\mathbf{A})|.
For our purposes, however, this ambiguity does not matter. By
[
11
, Exercise 7B.4]
, we have
\mathbf{v}_{2}(\mathbf{A})=\overline{\mathbf{v}_{1}(\mathbf{A})},
in which
\overline{\mathbf{v}_{1}(\mathbf{A})}
is the vector whose
n
-th component is equal to the complex conjugate of the
n
-th component of
\mathbf{v}_{1}(\mathbf{A}).
Therefore, regardless of whether
\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A})
is assigned to have a positive or negative imaginary part, the leading eigenvector
\mathbf{v}_{1}(\mathbf{A})
is unique up to the complex conjugation of its components.

Finally, we mention how to construct a lead matrix so that it is suitable for practical applications. In the real world, one does not directly observe a continuous signal. Rather, one observes a signal at finitely many different times. Given a signal
\mathbf{x}:T\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N}
with
T=[a,b],
a
time series
is a finite collection of the form
\left\{\mathbf{x}_{t_{k}}\right\}_{k=1}^{K},
where
K\in\mathbb{N}
is fixed,
\left\{t_{k}\right\}_{k=1}^{K}
is an increasing sequence in
T
with
t_{1}=a
and
t_{K}=b,
and
\mathbf{x}_{t_{k}}=\mathbf{x}(t_{k}).
Typically,
K
is a very large integer. We define the lead matrix for the time series
\left\{\mathbf{x}_{t_{k}}\right\}_{k=1}^{K}
to be the lead matrix
\mathbf{A}
for the signal
\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}:T\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N}
parameterizing the oriented curve formed by concatenating the oriented line segments
L_{1}\ ,\ \dots\ ,\ L_{K},
in which
L_{k}
has starting point
\mathbf{x}_{t_{k}}
and ending point
\mathbf{x}_{t_{k+1}}
for each
1\leq k<K
and
L_{K}
has starting point
\mathbf{x}_{t_{K}}
and ending point
\mathbf{x}_{t_{1}}.
Explicitly,

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{K-1}\mathbf{x}_{t_{k}}\mathbf{x}_{t_{k+1}}^{\text{T}}-\mathbf{x}_{t_{k+1}}\mathbf{x}_{t_{k}}^{\text{T}}.

(1.22)

If we write out
\mathbf{x}_{t_{k}}=(x_{t_{k},1}\ ,\ \dots\ ,\ x_{t_{k},N}),
then the
(m,n)
-th entry of
\mathbf{A}
is the oriented area of the convex polygon with vertices
\left(x_{t_{1},m}\ ,\ x_{t_{1},n}\right)\ ,\ \dots\ ,\ \left(x_{t_{K},m}\ ,\ x_{t_{K},n}\right)
computed via the
Shoelace Formula

[
12
]
.

1.4.4
Chain of Offsets Model

Now, we assume that
\mathbf{x}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N}
is a cyclic signal. We recall our second leader-follower dynamics question that we posed in
Section
1.1
, which is to determine the order in which the
N
component signals of
\mathbf{x}
evolve throughout time assuming all these signals trace some underlying signal up to scaling constants and time shifts. Formally, we assume that the cyclic signal
\mathbf{x}
satisfies the following
Chain of Offsets Model (COOM)

[
3
]
, which stipulates the existence of an underlying real-valued
P
-periodic signal
\phi,
scaling constants
c_{1}\ ,\ \dots\ ,\ c_{N}>0,
offsets
\alpha_{1}\ ,\ \dots\ ,\ \alpha_{N}\in\mathbb{R}/(P\mathbb{Z}),
and a time reparameterization
r:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle x_{n}(t)

\displaystyle=c_{n}\phi(r(t)-\alpha_{n}).

(1.23)

for each
1\leq n\leq N
and each

Given the cyclic signal
\mathbf{x}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N},
we determine the cyclic order of the offsets under COOM. But this time, we will utilize the lead matrix to do so. Without loss of generality, we may assume that
r
is the identity map, which reduces the COOM in (
1.23
) to the COOM defined earlier in (
1.3
). If
r
is not the identity map to begin with, then we can the consider the COOM in (
1.3
) for the
N

\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{x}\circ r^{-1}.

As before, we begin by writing the underlying signal
\phi
as the Fourier series in (
1.5
). Let
\mathbf{A}
be the lead matrix for
\mathbf{x}
over one period under COOM, that is, the lead matrix for the restricted signal
\mathbf{x}|_{[0,P]}.
Then, for each
1\leq m,n\leq N,
the
(m,n)
-th entry of
\mathbf{A}
is explicitly

\displaystyle A_{m,n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{P}x_{m}(t)\ x_{n}^{\prime}(t)-x_{n}(t)\ x_{m}^{\prime}(t)\ dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{P}\sum_{k,\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}c_{n}c_{m}\left(\frac{2\pi i\ell}{P}\right)\hat{\phi}_{k}\ \hat{\phi}_{\ell}\ e^{\frac{2\pi ik(t-\alpha_{m})}{P}}\ e^{\frac{2\pi i\ell(t-\alpha_{n})}{P}}\ dt

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{P}\sum_{k,\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}c_{n}c_{m}\left(\frac{2\pi ik}{P}\right)\hat{\phi}_{k}\ \hat{\phi}_{\ell}\ e^{\frac{2\pi ik(t-\alpha_{m})}{P}}\ e^{\frac{2\pi i\ell(t-\alpha_{n})}{P}}\ dt

\displaystyle=\pi i\ c_{n}c_{m}\ \sum_{k,\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(\ell-k\right)\ \delta_{k,-\ell}\ \hat{\phi}_{k}\ \hat{\phi}_{\ell}\ e^{-\frac{2\pi ik\alpha_{m}+2\pi i\ell\alpha_{n}}{P}}

\displaystyle=-2\pi i\ c_{n}c_{m}\ \sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}k\left|\hat{\phi}_{k}\right|^{2}e^{\frac{2\pi ik\left(\alpha_{n}-\alpha_{m}\right)}{P}}

\displaystyle=2\pi c_{m}c_{n}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}k\left|\hat{\phi}_{k}\right|^{2}\sin\left(\frac{2\pi k(\alpha_{m}-\alpha_{n})}{P}\right).

(1.24)

1.4.5
One Harmonic

Assume that the underlying
P
-periodic signal
\phi
in (
1.23
) has only one harmonic. This means the Fourier Coefficient
\hat{\phi}_{k}
is nonzero for only one index
k\in\mathbb{N}.
We show that we can recover the cyclic order of the offsets via the leading eigenvector of the lead matrix
\mathbf{A}
defined in (
1.24
). Note for each
1\leq m,n\leq N,
the
(m,n)
-th entry of the lead matrix
\mathbf{A}
is explicitly

\displaystyle A_{m,n}

\displaystyle=2\pi c_{m}c_{n}k\left|\hat{\phi}_{k}\right|^{2}\sin\left(\frac{2\pi k(\alpha_{m}-\alpha_{n})}{P}\right)

\displaystyle=2\pi c_{m}c_{n}k\left|\hat{\phi}_{k}\right|^{2}\left(\sin\left(\frac{2\pi k\alpha_{m}}{P}\right)\ \cos\left(\frac{2\pi k\alpha_{n}}{P}\right)-\cos\left(\frac{2\pi k\alpha_{m}}{P}\right)\ \sin\left(\frac{2\pi k\alpha_{n}}{P}\right)\right).

(1.25)

We show that if the lead matrix
\mathbf{A}
is nonzero, then it must have rank two. Recall if
\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{C}^{N},
then the
outer product
of
\mathbf{a}
and
\mathbf{b}
is the complex
N\times N
matrix
\mathbf{a}\otimes\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}^{*},
where
\mathbf{b}^{*}=\overline{\mathbf{b}^{\text{T}}}
is the conjugate transpose of
\mathbf{b}.
A statement made in
[
3
]
is that a complex skew-symmetric matrix has rank two if and only if it is of the form
\mathbf{a}\otimes\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{b}\otimes\mathbf{a}
for some linearly independent
\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}.
So we verify that our lead matrix
\mathbf{A}
indeed satisfies this condition stated in
[
3
]
. Define the vectors

\displaystyle\mathbf{a}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2\pi k}|\hat{\phi}_{k}|\left(c_{1}\cos\left(\frac{2\pi k\alpha_{1}}{P}\right)\ ,\ \dots\ ,\ c_{N}\cos\left(\frac{2\pi k\alpha_{N}}{P}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\mathbf{b}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2\pi k}|\hat{\phi}_{k}|\left(c_{1}\sin\left(\frac{2\pi k\alpha_{1}}{P}\right)\ ,\ \dots\ ,\ c_{N}\sin\left(\frac{2\pi k\alpha_{N}}{P}\right)\right).

Then, by (
1.25
) immediately implies

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{a}\otimes\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{b}\otimes\mathbf{a}.

(1.26)

Now, we need to show that
\mathbf{a}
and
\mathbf{b}
are linearly independent in
\mathbb{C}^{N}.

Consider the
N\times 2
matrix
\mathbf{X}
whose first row is
\mathbf{a}
and second row is
\mathbf{b}.
The condition that
\mathbf{a}
and
\mathbf{b}
are linearly independent is equivalent to the condition
\mathbf{X}
has rank
2.
The latter condition is equivalent to the condition there exists a
2\times 2
submatrix of
\mathbf{X}
with a nonzero determinant. Recall our assumption
\mathbf{A}
is nonzero. This means
A_{m,n}
has a nonzero entry for some two indices
1\leq m<n\leq N.
On the other hand, consider the submatrix
\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}
formed by extracting the
m
-th and

-th rows of
\mathbf{X}
for the same choices of

and

namely the
2\times 2

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{m,n}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\sqrt{2\pi k}|\widehat{\phi}_{k}|c_{m}\cos\left(\frac{2\pi k\alpha_{m}}{P}\right)&\sqrt{2\pi k}|\widehat{\phi}_{k}|c_{m}\sin\left(\frac{2\pi k\alpha_{m}}{P}\right)\\
\sqrt{2\pi k}|\widehat{\phi}_{k}|c_{n}\cos\left(\frac{2\pi k\alpha_{n}}{P}\right)&\sqrt{2\pi k}|\widehat{\phi}_{k}|c_{n}\sin\left(\frac{2\pi k\alpha_{n}}{P}\right)\end{bmatrix}.

By (
1.25
), the determinant of
\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{m,n}
is precisely

which shows

has rank two.

We now determine the leading eigenvector of
\mathbf{A}.
Since
\mathbf{A}
has rank two, it has exactly two nonzero eigenvalues coming in a purely imaginary pair, namely
\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A})
and
\lambda_{2}(\mathbf{A}),
in which we recall
\lambda_{n}(\mathbf{A})
is the
n
-th largest eigenvalue of
\mathbf{A}
in modulus. By
[
3
]
,

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A})

\displaystyle=i\sin(\theta)\|\mathbf{a}\|\|\mathbf{b}\|

is the largest eigenvalue of
\mathbf{A}
with associated eigenvector

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{1}(\mathbf{A})

\displaystyle=-e^{-i\theta}\frac{\mathbf{a}}{\|\mathbf{a}\|}+\frac{\mathbf{b}}{\|\mathbf{b}\|},

where
\theta
is the angle between the vectors
\mathbf{a}
and
\mathbf{b},
and
\|\mathbf{a}\|
is the Euclidean
2
-norm of
\mathbf{a}.
In particular, if
v_{1,n}(\mathbf{A})
is the
n
-th component of
\mathbf{v}_{1}(\mathbf{A}),
then upon extracting real and imaginary parts of
v_{1,n}(\mathbf{A}),
we obtain

\displaystyle\Re(v_{1,n}(\mathbf{A}))

\displaystyle=-\frac{a_{n}\cos(\theta)}{\|\mathbf{a}\|}+\frac{b_{n}}{\|\mathbf{b}\|}

(1.27)

\displaystyle\Im(v_{1,n}(\mathbf{A}))

\displaystyle=\frac{a_{n}\sin(\theta)}{\|\mathbf{a}\|},

(1.28)

where

\displaystyle a_{n}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2\pi k}|\hat{\phi}_{k}|c_{n}\cos\left(\frac{2\pi k\alpha_{n}}{P}\right)

\displaystyle b_{n}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2\pi k}|\hat{\phi}_{k}|c_{n}\sin\left(\frac{2\pi k\alpha_{n}}{P}\right)

are the
n
-th components of
\mathbf{a}
and
\mathbf{b},
respectively.

Through the equations (
1.27
) and (
1.28
), we see
\left(\Re(v_{1,n}(\mathbf{A}))\ ,\ \Im(v_{1,n}(\mathbf{A}))\right)
is the result of a certain linear transformation evaluated at the point
\left(a_{n},b_{n}\right).
Since the points
(a_{1},b_{1})\ ,\ \dots\ ,\ (a_{N},b_{N})
lie on a circle, the components of the leading eigenvector
\mathbf{v}_{1}(\mathbf{A})
lie on an ellipse. The cyclic order in which the points

are traversed on the circle is the cyclic order in which the points
\left\{\left(\Re(v_{1,n}(\mathbf{A}))\ ,\ \Im(v_{1,n}(\mathbf{A}))\right)\right\}_{n=1}^{N}
are traversed on the ellipse. Thus, the cyclic order of
\left\{\text{Arg}\left(v_{1,n}(\mathbf{A})\right)\right\}_{n=1}^{N},
where

is the principal argument of the complex number

matches the cyclic order of the offsets in COOM. We refer to the principal arguments of the components of the leading eigenvector as

.

We show an example of how the cyclic order of the leading eigenvector component phases and the cyclic order of the offsets match when the underlying signal

has only one harmonic. Reconsider the sinusoidal signal (

) for each

1.4.6
Multiple Harmonics

Suppose the underlying
P
-periodic signal
\phi
in (
1.23
) has multiple harmonics, meaning
\phi
has multiple nonzero Fourier coefficients. Assume, however, one of these Fourier coefficients
dominates
: there exists exactly one
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\displaystyle|\hat{\phi}_{k}|^{2}\gg\sum_{\ell\neq k}|\hat{\phi}_{\ell}|^{2}.

With these assumptions, it is still possible to approximately recover the cyclic order of the offsets
\alpha_{1}\ ,\ \dots\ ,\ \alpha_{N}
under COOM via the leading eigenvector of the lead matrix over one period.

Let
\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{k}
be the rank two skew-symmetric
N\times N
matrix, whose
(m,n)
-th entry is equal to (
1.25
) for all
1\leq m,n\leq N
. Then, the
(m,n)
-th entry of
\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{k}
will dominate the series expansion in (
1.24
). This means we well approximate the original lead matrix
\mathbf{A}
with the lower rank matrix
\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{k}.

On the other hand, a rank two approximation of
\mathbf{A}
is the matrix
\mathbf{P}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{P},
in which
\mathbf{P}
is the
N\times N
matrix corresponding to the projection operator onto the subspace of
\mathbb{C}^{N}
spanned by the vectors
\mathbf{v}_{1}(\mathbf{A})
and
\mathbf{v}_{2}(\mathbf{A}).
The smaller the Frobenius norm
\|\mathbf{P}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{P}-\mathbf{A}\|_{F}
is, the better this low rank approximation is of
\mathbf{A}
. Moreover, since
\mathbf{P}
sends
\mathbf{v}_{1}(\mathbf{A})
to itself, we have

\displaystyle(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{P})\ \mathbf{v}_{1}(\mathbf{A})

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{A})\ \mathbf{v}_{1}(\mathbf{A})

\displaystyle=\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A})\ \mathbf{P}\ \mathbf{v}_{1}(\mathbf{A})

\displaystyle=\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A})\ \mathbf{v}_{1}(\mathbf{A}),

which means the leading eigenvector of
\mathbf{P}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{P}
is
\mathbf{v}_{1}(\mathbf{A}).
Therefore, assuming the low rank matrix
\mathbf{P}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{P}
approximates
\mathbf{A}
well enough, we can still approximately recover the cyclic order of the offsets
\alpha_{1}\ ,\ \dots\ ,\ \alpha_{N}
in COOM via the cyclic order of the component phases of
\mathbf{v}_{1}(\mathbf{A}).

For practical purposes, one heuristic indicator
[
3
]
that measures the dominance of the low rank
2
approximation of the lead matrix
\mathbf{A}
is the magnitude of
\left|\frac{\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A})}{\lambda_{3}(\mathbf{A})}\right|,
the ratio of the first to third largest eigenvalue of
\mathbf{A}.
This is because in the rank
2
situation, this ratio would be infinite.

1.5
Ornstein-Uhlenbeck Process

In this section, we fix a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mathbb{P})
and
N\in\mathbb{N}.
We consider an
(
N
-dimensional) Ornstein Uhlenbeck (OU) process
on this probability space, which is an
N
-dimensional stochastic process
\left\{\mathbf{x}(t)\right\}_{t\geq 0}
satisfying the linear stochastic differential equation
[
13
]
:

\displaystyle d\mathbf{x}(t)

\displaystyle=-\mathbf{B}\ \mathbf{x}(t)\ dt+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\ d\mathbf{w}(t).

(1.29)

Here,
\mathbf{B}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N}
is a fixed linear operator such that
-\mathbf{B}
is
Hurwitz (stable)
i.e. all eigenvalues of
-\mathbf{B}
have negative real parts,
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}:\mathbb{R}^{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N}
is a fixed linear operator for some
M\in\mathbb{N},
and
\left\{\mathbf{w}(t)\right\}_{t\geq 0}
is the standard
M
-dimensional Wiener process independent of the initial random vector
\mathbf{x}(0)
. We refer to
\mathbf{B}
as the
friction (drift) operator
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
as the
volatility operator
, and we collectively refer to
\mathbf{B}
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
as the
OU model parameters
. We refer to the matrix

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}

\displaystyle=\frac{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\text{T}}}{2}

(1.30)

as the
diffusion operator
.

The OU process is an ergodic Gaussian diffusion process
[
14
]
that prominently appears in Financial Mathematics and Biology
[
15
,
16
,
17
]
. In engineering disciplines, the OU Process is heuristically characterized as the solution to the
Langevin equation

[
18
]
:

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)

\displaystyle=-\mathbf{B}\ \mathbf{x}(t)+\boldsymbol{\eta}(t),

(1.31)

in which
\left\{\boldsymbol{\eta}(t)\right\}_{t\geq 0}
is the
N
-dimensional Gaussian “white noise” process whose autocovariance function satisfies

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}(s,t)

\displaystyle=2\mathbf{D}\ \delta(s-t),

for all
s,t\geq 0.
Here,
\delta(\cdot)
is the Dirac measure. The OU process has a unique stationary distribution
[
14
,
19
]
, namely the Gaussian distribution with mean
\mathbf{0}
and covariance matrix
\mathbf{S},
in which
\mathbf{S}
satisfies the Lyapunov equation
[
20
]
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}\mathbf{S}+\mathbf{S}\mathbf{B}^{\text{T}}

\displaystyle=2\mathbf{D}.

(1.32)

For this reason, we refer to
\mathbf{S}
as the
stationary covariance operator
. Any realization of the OU process will fluctuate around the origin
\mathbf{0}
in the long term
[
18
]
. These long term fluctuations vary according to the stationary Gaussian distribution. Finally, if
\mathbf{x}(0)
is the Gaussian distribution with mean
\mathbf{0}
and covariance matrix
\mathbf{S},
then the OU process is stationary.

1.6
Problem Statement

In this thesis, we will investigate Cyclicity Analysis for the
stationary OU process
with model parameters
\mathbf{B}
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma},
in which
\mathbf{x}(0)
is the Gaussian distribution with mean
\mathbf{0}
and covariance matrix
\mathbf{S}.
We define the auxiliary
lead process
, which is the
N\times N
matrix-valued stochastic process
\left\{\mathbf{A}(t)\right\}_{t\geq 0},
where

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{x}(s)\ d\mathbf{x}^{\text{T}}(s)-d\mathbf{x}(s)\ \mathbf{x}^{\text{T}}(s)

(1.33)

for each
t\geq 0.
The right hand side is a random matrix whose entries are one-dimensional Ito stochastic integrals
[
21
]
with respect to the component OU processes
\left\{x_{n}(t)\right\}_{t\geq 0}
. In particular, the
(m,n)
-th entry of
\mathbf{A}(t)
is a one-dimensional Ito integral of the form

\displaystyle A_{m,n}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}x_{m}(s)\ dx_{n}(s)-x_{n}(s)\ dx_{m}(s).

By definition,
\mathbf{A}(t)
represents the lead matrix corresponding to a
realization
of the OU process over the interval
[0,t].

We will prove the lead process obeys the strong law of large numbers identity:

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\mathbf{A}(t)}{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbf{B}\mathbf{S}-\mathbf{S}\mathbf{B}^{\text{T}}}{2},

(1.34)

almost surely. The identity (
1.34
) states we can infer the long term average pairwise leader follower dynamics amongst the
N
component OU process through a time-averaged lead matrix corresponding to one single realization of the OU process. For this reason, we refer to the matrix

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbf{B}\mathbf{S}-\mathbf{S}\mathbf{B}^{\text{T}}}{2}

(1.35)

as the
lead matrix
of the OU process.

Next, we consider a
cyclic OU process
, in which the friction matrix
\mathbf{B}
is specifically a
circulant matrix
: if
(b_{1}\ ,\ \dots\ ,\ b_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is the first row of
\mathbf{B},
then the
(m,n)
-th entry of
\mathbf{B}
is
b_{n-m+1},
where
n-m+1
is indexed mod
N.
We assume
b_{2}\ ,\ \dots\ ,\ b_{N}\leq 0
and
b_{1}>0
is large enough so that
\mathbf{B}
is a valid friction matrix. We consider a signal propagation model governed by this cyclic OU process. In our signal propagation model, we assume that there is a network of
N
sensors located in space and an underlying one-dimensional signal propagating throughout the network. Similar signal propagation models are considered in
[
22
,
4
,
23
]
, for example. Letting
\left\{\mathbf{x}(t)\right\}_{t\geq 0}
be the OU process, we interpret the
n
-th component of
\mathbf{x}(t)
as a measurement made by the
n
-th sensor of the propagating effect at time
t\geq 0.
The friction matrix
\mathbf{B}
represents the cyclic sensor network structure. We refer to the constant
b_{p}
as a
propagation coefficient
for each
p>1,
and we interpret its magnitude to represent how receptive the
n
-th sensor is to the activity within the neighboring
(n+p-1)
-th sensor for each
1\leq n\leq N,
in which
n+p-1
is indexed mod
N.
We refer to the constant
b_{1}
as a
suppression coefficient
, and we interpret its magnitude to represent how quickly the signal dissipates as it is propagating throughout the network. Meanwhile, the diffusion matrix
\mathbf{D}=\frac{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\text{T}}}{2}
is the covariance matrix whose
(m,n)
-th entry is the covariance of the
m
-th and
n
-th component noises injected into the
m
-th and
n
-th sensors, respectively.

For large dimension
N
and for different choices of cyclic OU model parameters
\mathbf{B}
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma},
we derive the lead matrix
\mathbf{Q}
of the cyclic OU process. Then, we investigate whether Cyclicity Analysis enables us to recover the network structure given by
\mathbf{B}.
More specifically, we investigate whether the structure of the leading eigenvector
\mathbf{v}_{1}(\mathbf{Q})
recovers the structure of the network.

For example, let
\mathbf{B}
be the
N\times N
circulant friction matrix such that the suppression coefficient
b_{1}>1,
the propagation coefficient
b_{2}=-1,
and all other propagation coefficients are
0.
The cyclic network structure of
\mathbf{B}
is such that the
n
-th sensor is only linked to the
(n+1)
-th sensor, the neighboring sensor to its right. Does the structure of the leading eigenvector
\mathbf{v}_{1}(\mathbf{Q})
reflect this ground truth network structure in any way ? For example, does the (cyclic) order of the leading eigenvector component phases match the expected (cyclic) order of the sensors receiving the signal as it propagates throughout the network ? In addition, do the leading eigenvector component moduli and the lead matrix eigenvalues indicate anything about the network structure ?

We will investigate our problem statement under two specific regimes: the first regime is when the suppression coefficient
b_{1}
is large i.e. the propagating signal quickly dissipates, and the second regime is when
b_{1}
is small i.e. the propagating signal does not quickly dissipate. In the former regime, the circulant friction matrix
\mathbf{B}
is perturbed significantly from an unstable matrix, while in the latter regime,
\mathbf{B}
is perturbed slightly away from an unstable matrix. We investigate these regimes because they yield different types of asymptotic structures pertaining to the lead matrix
\mathbf{Q}
and its leading eigenvector
\mathbf{v}_{1}(\mathbf{Q}).

1.7.1
Lead Lag Dynamics

There are various tools to identify lead lag dynamics between two signals or between two time series. Such tools have been utilized to study financial markets
[
24
]
and fMRI signals
[
25
]
. We already discussed one tool, namely the cross-correlation function. Another algorithmic tool is Dynamic Time Warping (DTW)
[
26
]
. We briefly describe it. Consider two one-dimensional time series of the form
\left\{x_{m}\right\}_{m=1}^{M}
and
\left\{y_{m}\right\}_{n=1}^{N}.
The goal of DTW is to find the optimal alignment of such time-series. To this end, one constructs an
M\times N
matrix
\mathbf{C}
whose
(j,k)
-th entry corresponds to the optimal cost between the restricted time series
\left\{x_{m}\right\}_{m=1}^{j}
and
\left\{y_{m}\right\}_{n=1}^{k}.
The optimal cost is defined via the formula

\displaystyle C_{j,k}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}d(x_{j},y_{k})&j=k=1\\
d(x_{j},y_{k})+\min\left(C_{j-1,k}\ ,\ C_{j-1,k-1}\ ,\ C_{j,k-1}\right)&\text{else},\end{cases}

(1.36)

where
d(\cdot,\cdot)
is a specified distance metric. Then, a
warping path
is any collection of indices
\left\{(j_{1},k_{1})\ ,\ \dots\ ,\ (j_{P},k_{P})\right\}
satisfying
(j_{1},k_{1})=(1,1)
and
(j_{P},k_{P})=(M,N)
and
0\leq j_{p+1}-j_{p}\ ,\ k_{p+1}-k_{p}\leq 1
for all
1\leq p<P,
where
P\geq 2
is a fixed length. Finally, the
optimal alignment
between the original time-series
\left\{x_{m}\right\}_{m=1}^{M}
and
\left\{y_{m}\right\}_{n=1}^{N}
is the warping path
\left\{(j_{1},k_{1})\ ,\ \dots\ ,\ (j_{P},k_{P})\right\}
minimizing the quantity

\displaystyle\sum_{p=1}^{P}C_{j_{p},k_{p}}^{2}.

DTW, however, has drawbacks. Seeking the warping path to guarantee optimal alignment is a computationally expensive problem. Moreover, DTW is prone to noise.

1.7.2
Cyclicity Analysis

We review the literature on Cyclicity Analysis. As mentioned earlier, Cyclicity Analysis was officially introduced in
[
3
]
as a means to study cyclic signals. Later on, a dissertation
[
23
]
was published demonstrating how Cyclicity Analysis of fMRI signals yields new insights into understanding the brain processes of patients with a neurological condition known as tinnitus. Moreover, the authors in
[
4
]
published an experimental article comparing the results given by Cyclicity Analysis on fMRI signals to results given by other standard tools.

We emphasize that in this thesis, we are applying Cyclicity Analysis in a completely different setting. We are using it to investigate signals generated via a stochastic process.

1.7.3
OU Process

As mentioned earlier, the OU process is one of the most common stochastic processes with applications in Financial Mathematics and Biology.

Firstly, many authors have studied the estimation of OU process model parameters. In
[
27
]
, for example, the authors showed using MAP estimation how to recover OU model parameters
\mathbf{B}
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma},
in which
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
is an
N\times N
invertible matrix, given a time series that approximates a realization of an OU process. In
[
14
]
, the authors showed how to approximate the stationary distribution of a generalized OU process with a modified drift coefficient. We emphasize that our problem statement is completely different from model parameter estimation. We are not trying to recover the friction matrix
\mathbf{B}
itself from realizations of the OU process; rather, we interpret
\mathbf{B}
as a weighted adjacency matrix associated with a network of sensors, and we are investigating whether we can recover the network structure induced by
\mathbf{B}
from any realization of the OU process.

Moreover, the lead matrix
\mathbf{Q}
of the OU process defined in (
3.40
) has been studied in the literature as a means to characterize the time-irreversibility of the OU process
[
18
,
28
,
29
]
. A stationary stochastic process
\left\{\mathbf{y}(t)\right\}_{t\geq 0}
is said to be
time-reversible

[
29
]
if for all
K\in\mathbb{N}
for all times
0\leq t_{1}\ ,\ \dots\ ,\ t_{K}\leq\tau,
the probability distributions of
\left(\mathbf{y}(t_{1})\ ,\ \dots\ ,\ \mathbf{y}(t_{K})\right)
and of
\left(\mathbf{y}(\tau-t_{1})\ ,\ \dots\ ,\ \mathbf{y}(\tau-t_{K})\right)
are equal. The main result is the stationary OU process is time reversible if and only if
\mathbf{B}\mathbf{S}
is symmetric, where
\mathbf{S}
is the stationary covariance matrix of the OU process. Equivalently, the OU process is time reversible if and only if the matrix
\mathbf{Q}
vanishes.

To study the degree to which the OU process
\left\{\mathbf{x}(t)\right\}_{t\geq 0}
is time irreversible, we briefly outline the approach described in
[
28
]
. Let
P:\mathbb{R}^{N}\times[0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be the probability density function of
\mathbf{x}(t)
at time
t\geq 0.
Since we are considering the OU stationary process, for each
t\geq 0,
the random vector
\mathbf{x}(t)
has a Gaussian distribution with mean
\mathbf{0}
and covariance matrix
\mathbf{S}.
Explicitly, we have

\displaystyle P(\mathbf{x},t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^{N}|\det(\mathbf{S})|}}\ e^{-\frac{\mathbf{x}^{\text{T}}\mathbf{S}^{-1}\mathbf{x}}{2}}

(1.37)

for each
t\geq 0.
The density function
P
satisfies the
Fokker-Planck (Kolmogorov forward) equation

[
28
]
, which is the parabolic partial differential equation

\displaystyle\frac{\partial P}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\nabla\cdot\left(\mathbf{B}\ \mathbf{x}\ P+\mathbf{D}\ \nabla P\right),

(1.38)

where
\mathbf{D}
is the diffusion matrix,
\nabla P
is the gradient of
P
with respect to variable
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1}\ ,\ \dots\ ,\ x_{N}),
and
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{F}
is the divergence of the vector field
\mathbf{F}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N}.
Define the map
\mathbf{J}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\times[0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N}
by the expression within the divergence operator on the right hand side of (
1.38
), namely

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{x},t)

\displaystyle=-\mathbf{B}\ \mathbf{x}\ P(\mathbf{x},t)-\mathbf{D}\ \nabla P(\mathbf{x},t).

(1.39)

The map
\mathbf{J}
is known as the
probability flux (current)

[
18
,
28
]
. In general, the probability flux describes how probabilities associated with different states in the state space of a stochastic process change over time. Intuitively, when the probability flux is nonzero, the realizations follow paths that lead towards regions with higher probability density i.e. those states with higher probability of occurrence.

For each
t\geq 0,
upon recognizing that
\nabla P(\mathbf{x},t)=-\mathbf{S}^{-1}\ \mathbf{x}\ P(\mathbf{x},t),
we can rewrite the probability flux in the following way:

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{x},t)

\displaystyle=-(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}\ P(\mathbf{x},t)+\mathbf{D}\mathbf{S}^{-1}\mathbf{x}\ P(\mathbf{x},t))

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbf{D}\mathbf{S}^{-1}-\mathbf{B}\right)\mathbf{x}\ P(\mathbf{x},t)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\mathbf{B}\mathbf{S}+\mathbf{S}\mathbf{B}^{\text{T}}}{2}\ \mathbf{S}^{-1}-\mathbf{B}\right)\mathbf{x}\ P(\mathbf{x},t)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\mathbf{S}\mathbf{B}^{\text{T}}\mathbf{S}^{-1}-\mathbf{B}}{2}\right)\mathbf{x}\ P(\mathbf{x},t)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\left(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{S}-\mathbf{2}\mathbf{Q}\right)\mathbf{S}^{-1}-\mathbf{B}}{2}\right)\mathbf{x}\ P(\mathbf{x},t)

\displaystyle=-\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{S}^{-1}\mathbf{x}\ P(\mathbf{x},t).

Thus, the stationary OU process is time-reversible if and only if the probability flux
\mathbf{J}
vanishes. If the flux does not vanish, then observe
\mathbf{Q}
is involved in the measurement of the flux.

Now, let
\mathbf{A}\in\text{Mat}_{N,N}(\mathbb{F}).
We denote
\det(\mathbf{A}),\text{tr}(\mathbf{A}),
and
\mathbf{A}^{-1}
as the determinant, trace, and inverse of
\mathbf{A},
respectively. Recall the matrix exponential of
\mathbf{A}
is the power series

\displaystyle\exp(\mathbf{A})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{\mathbf{A}^{k}}{k!}.

(2.1)

We state the basic properties of the matrix exponential that we will be using later in the thesis. Proofs of these properties can be found in
[
30
]
, for example.

Theorem 2.1

(Matrix Exponential Properties)
.

If
\mathbf{A}\in\text{Mat}_{N,N}(\mathbb{R}),
then the following properties hold.

i)

\exp(\mathbf{A})
commutes with
\mathbf{A}
under matrix multiplication.

ii)

\exp(\mathbf{A})
is invertible with
(\exp(\mathbf{A}))^{-1}=\exp(\mathbf{-}\mathbf{A}).

iii)

We have

\displaystyle\left(\exp{\mathbf{A}}\right)^{\text{T}}=\exp\left(\mathbf{A}^{\text{T}}\right).

(2.2)

iv)

The matrix-valued map
t\mapsto\exp(t\mathbf{A})
is differentiable on
\mathbb{R}
. In particular,

\displaystyle(\exp{t\mathbf{A}})^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{A}\ \exp(t\mathbf{A})=\exp(t\mathbf{A})\ \mathbf{A}.

(2.3)

Let
\mathbf{A}\in\text{Mat}_{N,N}(\mathbb{F}).
The matrix
\mathbf{A}
is
Hurwitz
if all of its eigenvalues have negative real parts. Note if
\mathbf{A}
is Hurwitz, then
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\exp(t\mathbf{A})=\mathbf{0}.
The matrix
\mathbf{A}
is
Hermitian
if
\mathbf{A}^{*}=\mathbf{A}
and
skew-Hermitian (anti-Hermitian)
if
\mathbf{A}^{*}=-\mathbf{A}.
Note that Hermitian real matrices are the same as symmetric real matrices, while skew-Hermitian real matrices are the same as skew-symmetric real matrices. We state the important properties of Hermitian and skew-Hermitian matrices. One may consult
[
30
]
for proofs.

Theorem 2.2

(Hermitian and skew-Hermitian Matrix Properties)
.

If
\mathbf{A}\in\text{Mat}_{N,N}(\mathbb{F})
is either Hermitian or skew-Hermitian, then the following properties hold

i)

\mathbf{A}
is skew-Hermitian if and only if
i\mathbf{A}
is Hermitian.

ii)

If
\mathbf{A}
is Hermitian or skew-Hermitian, then
\mathbf{A}
is diagonalizable; there is an orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{C}^{N}
consisting of the eigenvectors of
\mathbf{A}.

iii)

If
\mathbf{A}
is Hermitian, then all its eigenvalues are real. If
\mathbf{A}
is skew-Hermitian, then all eigenvalues of
\mathbf{A}
are either
0
or purely imaginary.

Finally, let
\mathbf{A}\in\text{Mat}_{N,N}(\mathbb{F}).
The matrix
\mathbf{A}
is
circulant
if there exists a sequence of numbers
\left\{a_{n}\right\}_{1\leq n<N}
such that
A_{m,n}=a_{m-n+1},
where
m-n+1
is indexed mod
N.
Circulant matrices obey several properties
[
31
]
. Circulant matrices form a subspace of
\text{Mat}_{N,N}(\mathbb{F}).
They commute under matrix multiplication. The product of circulant matrices is circulant, and the transpose of a circulant matrix is circulant.

We state and prove the properties pertaining to the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of circulant matrices that are essential for this thesis.

Theorem 2.3

(Circulant Matrix Properties)
.

If
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}\in\text{Mat}_{N,N}(\mathbb{F})
are circulant matrices, then the following properties hold.

i)

\mathbf{A}
is diagonalizable. In particular, if the first row of
\mathbf{A}
is of the form
(a_{1}\ ,\ \dots\ ,\ a_{N}),
then for each
1\leq n\leq N,
the vector

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}_{n}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}}\left(1\ ,\ \omega_{n}\ ,\ \dots\ ,\ \omega_{n}^{N-1}\right),

(2.4)

is an eigenvector of
\mathbf{A},
associated with the eigenvalue

\displaystyle\mu_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=1}^{N}a_{p}\ \omega_{n}^{p-1},

(2.5)

where
\omega_{n}=e^{\frac{2\pi in}{N}}
is an
N
-th root of unity.
Furthermore, the collection of eigenvectors
\left\{\mathbf{w}_{n}\right\}_{n=1}^{N}
forms an orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{C}^{N},
and we have
\mathbf{w}_{n}^{*}=\mathbf{w}_{N-n}^{T}
for each
1\leq n\leq N.

ii)

For each
k\geq 0,
we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}^{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mu_{n}^{k}\ \mathbf{w}_{n}\mathbf{w}_{N-n}^{\text{T}}.

(2.6)

iii)

For each
t\in\mathbb{R},
the matrix exponential of
t\mathbf{A}
is explicitly

\displaystyle\exp(t\mathbf{A})

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{N}e^{\mu_{n}t}\ \mathbf{w}_{n}\mathbf{w}_{N-n}^{\text{T}}.

(2.7)

Moreover,
\exp(t\mathbf{A})
is a circulant matrix.

Proof.

We prove the first statement. For each
1\leq j,n\leq N,
the
j
-th component of
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{w}_{n}
is of the form

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}}\left(\sum_{p=1}^{N}a_{p-j+1}\ \omega_{n}^{p-1}\right)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}}\left(\sum_{p=1}^{N-j+1}a_{p}\ \omega_{n}^{p+j-2}+\sum_{p=N-j+2}^{N}a_{p}\ \omega_{n}^{p-(N-j+2)}\right)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}}\left(\sum_{p=1}^{N-j+1}a_{p}\ \omega_{n}^{p+j-2}+\sum_{p=N-j+2}^{N}a_{p}\ \omega_{n}^{p+j-2}\right)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}}\sum_{p=1}^{N}a_{p}\ \omega_{n}^{p+j-2}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{p=1}^{N}a_{p}\ \omega_{n}^{p-1}\right)\ \frac{\omega_{n}^{j-1}}{\sqrt{N}}

\displaystyle=\mu_{n}\ \frac{\omega_{n}^{j-1}}{\sqrt{N}},

which is the
j
-th component of
\mu_{n}\mathbf{w}_{n}.
This shows
\mu_{n}
is an eigenvalue of
\mathbf{A}
with associated eigenvector
\mathbf{w}_{n}.
Next, note that

\displaystyle\omega_{N-n}

\displaystyle=e^{\frac{2\pi i(N-n)}{N}}

\displaystyle=e^{-\frac{2\pi in}{N}}=\overline{\omega_{n}},

(2.8)

for each
1\leq n\leq N,
which implies
\mathbf{w}_{n}^{*}=\mathbf{w}^{\text{T}}_{N-n}.
Finally, for all
1\leq m,n\leq N
with
m\neq n,
we use the finite geometric series identity

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}r^{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{r-r^{N+1}}{1-r}

for all
r\neq 1,
to see that

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}_{m}^{*}\mathbf{w}_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{p=1}^{N}\omega_{N-m}^{p-1}\omega_{n}^{p-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{p=1}^{N}\omega_{N-m+n}^{p-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\ \left(\frac{1-\omega_{N-m+n}^{N}}{1-\omega_{N-m+n}}\right)

\displaystyle=0.

If
n=m,
then

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}_{n}^{*}\mathbf{w}_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{p=1}^{N}\omega_{N-n}^{p-1}\omega_{n}^{p-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{p=1}^{N}\omega_{N}^{p-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{N}{N}=1.

Since
\left\{\mathbf{w}_{n}\right\}_{n=1}^{N}
is a set of
N
orthonormal vectors in
\mathbb{C}^{N},
we deduce it is a basis for
\mathbb{C}^{N}.
This proves the first statement.

We prove the second statement. Let
k\geq 0
be fixed. Because
\mu_{n}
is an eigenvalue of
\mathbf{A},
note that
\mu_{n}^{k}
is an eigenvalue of
\mathbf{A}^{k}
with associated eigenvector
\mathbf{w}_{n}.
For each
1\leq m\leq N,
the orthonormality of the collection of eigenvectors

yields

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mu_{n}^{k}\ \mathbf{w}_{n}\mathbf{w}_{N-n}^{\text{T}}\mathbf{w}_{m}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mu_{n}^{k}\ \mathbf{w}_{n}\ \delta_{m,n}

\displaystyle=\mu_{m}^{k}\mathbf{w}_{m}=\mathbf{A}^{k}\mathbf{w}_{m}.

This shows that the linear operator
\mathbf{A}^{k}
and the linear operator
\sum_{n=1}^{N}\lambda_{n}^{k}\ \mathbf{w}_{n}\mathbf{w}_{N-n}^{\text{T}}
agree on the basis of eigenvectors for each

. Because every vector in

is a unique linear combination of vectors from this basis, the operators agree on all of

and thus are identical. This proves the second statement.

Now, we prove the last statement. By the definition of the matrix exponential in (

), we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{(t\mathbf{A})^{k}}{k!}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{t^{k}\mathbf{A}^{k}}{k!}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{t^{k}}{k!}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mu_{n}^{k}\ \mathbf{w}_{n}\mathbf{w}_{N-n}^{\text{T}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{(\mu_{n}t)^{k}}{k!}\mathbf{w}_{n}\mathbf{w}_{N-n}^{\text{T}}

(2.9)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{N}e^{\mu_{n}t}\ \mathbf{w}_{n}\mathbf{w}_{N-n}^{\text{T}},

(2.10)

in which we obtained

2.10
) by simplifying the inner sum in (

) using the Taylor series of the ordinary exponential function:

\displaystyle e^{z}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{z^{k}}{k!}.

The product of circulant matrices is circulant and the scalar multiple of a circulant matrix is also circulant. Therefore, for each

the matrix

is a circulant matrix. Since the sum of circulant matrices is circulant, the partial sum

is circulant for each

Thus, upon taking the limit as

we deduce

is a circulant matrix.

2.3
Stochastic Processes

Throughout this section, we let
\left(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mathbb{P}\right)
be probability space,
\left\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\right\}_{t\geq 0}
be a filtration on
\Omega,
and we fix
N\in\mathbb{N}.
Recall an event
A\in\mathcal{A}
occurs (
\mathbb{P}
)-
almost surely (with probability one)
if
\mathbb{P}(A^{c})=0.

Recall an
(
N
-dimensional) stochastic process
on
\Omega
is a map
\mathbf{x}:[0,\infty)\times\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N}
such that for each fixed
t\geq 0,
the map
\mathbf{x}(t,\cdot):\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N}
is an
N
-dimensional random vector on
\Omega.
A
realization (sample path/trajectory)
of
\mathbf{x}
is a map of the form
\mathbf{x}(\cdot,\omega):[0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N},
where
\omega\in\Omega
is fixed. From henceforth, we adopt the notation
\left\{\mathbf{x}(t)\right\}_{t\geq 0},
in which we write
\mathbf{x}(t)=\mathbf{x}(t,\cdot)
as the random vector at the time
t.
The process
\left\{\mathbf{x}(t)\right\}_{t\geq 0}
is
adapted
to the filtration
\left\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\right\}_{t\geq 0}
if
\mathbf{x}(t)
is a random vector on
\Omega
specifically equipped with the sigma algebra
\mathcal{F}_{t}
for each
t\geq 0.
In particular, the
standard (natural) filtration
of the process
\left\{\mathbf{x}(t)\right\}_{t\geq 0}
is the filtration
\left\{\mathcal{S}_{t}\right\}_{t\geq 0},
such that
\mathcal{S}_{t}
is the sigma algebra generated by the collection of random vectors
\left\{\mathbf{x}(s)\right\}_{s=0}^{t}
for each
t\geq 0.
Note that
\left\{\mathbf{x}(t)\right\}_{t\geq 0}
is always adapted to its own standard filtration.

Let
\left\{\mathbf{x}(t)\right\}_{t\geq 0}
be a stochastic process. We recall the following time dependent statistical functions associated with the process. The
mean function
,
autocovariance function
, and the
covariance function
of
\left\{\mathbf{x}(t)\right\}_{t\geq 0}
are defined respectively as the maps
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathbf{x}}:[0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N}\ ,\ \boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathbf{x}}:[0,\infty)^{2}\rightarrow\text{Mat}_{N,N}(\mathbb{R}),
and
\mathbf{V}_{\mathbf{x}}:[0,\infty)\rightarrow\text{Mat}_{N,N}(\mathbb{R}),
where

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathbf{x}}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}(t)]

(2.12)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathbf{x}}(s,t)

\displaystyle=\text{Cov}(\mathbf{x}(s),\mathbf{x}(t))

(2.13)

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}_{\mathbf{x}}(t)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathbf{x}}(t,t).

(2.14)

The process
\left\{\mathbf{x}(t)\right\}_{t\geq 0}
is
(strictly) stationary
if the cumulative distribution functions of
\mathbf{x}(t)
and
\mathbf{x}(t+h)
are identical for all
t,h\geq 0.
The process
\left\{\mathbf{x}(t)\right\}_{t\geq 0}
is
weakly (wide sense) stationary
if its mean function
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathbf{x}}
is identically constant and the map
(t,h)\mapsto\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathbf{x}}(t,t+h)
between
[0,\infty)^{2}
and
\text{Mat}_{N,N}(\mathbb{R})
depends only on the variable

Note that all stationary processes are weakly stationary.

Let
\left\{\mathbf{x}(t)\right\}_{t\geq 0}
be a stochastic process adapted to the filtration
\left\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\right\}_{t\geq 0}.
The process
\left\{\mathbf{x}(t)\right\}_{t\geq 0}
is
Markov
with respect to the filtration
\left\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\right\}_{t\geq 0}
if for all
s,t\geq 0
and any measurable, bounded function
f:\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[f(\mathbf{x}(s+t))\ |\ \mathcal{F}_{s}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[f(\mathbf{x}(s+t))\ |\ \mathbf{x}(s)\right]

almost surely. We simply say
\left\{\mathbf{x}(t)\right\}_{t\geq 0}
is Markov if it is Markov with respect to its standard filtration. Moreover, the process
\left\{\mathbf{x}(t)\right\}_{t\geq 0}
is a
diffusion process
if it is a Markov process having continuous sample paths with probability one. The process
\left\{\mathbf{x}(t)\right\}_{t\geq 0}
is a
martingale
with respect to the filtration
\left\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\right\}_{t\geq 0}
if for all
0\leq s\leq t,
the expectation
\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\mathbf{x}(t)\right|\right]
exists and
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{x}(t)\ |\ \mathcal{F}_{s}\right]=\mathbf{x}(s).
We simply say
\left\{\mathbf{x}(t)\right\}_{t\geq 0}
is a martingale if it is a martingale with respect to its standard filtration.

Let
\left\{\mathbf{x}(t)\right\}_{t\geq 0}
be a stochastic process. The process is said to be
(
N
-dimensional) Gaussian
if for all
K\in\mathbb{N}
and all times
t_{1}\ ,\ \dots\ ,\ t_{K}\geq 0
and all
\alpha_{1}\ ,\ \dots\ ,\ \alpha_{K}\in\mathbb{R},
the linear combination
\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{k}\ \mathbf{x}(t_{k})
is a Gaussian random vector. For Gaussian processes, the notions of stationarity and weak stationarity are equivalent. The process
\left\{\mathbf{x}(t)\right\}_{t\geq 0}
is the
(
N
-dimensional) standard Wiener process (Brownian Motion)
if it is a diffusion process such that
\mathbf{x}(0)=\mathbf{0}
almost surely with independent, Gaussian increments: for all
0\leq s_{1}<t_{1}\leq s_{2}<t_{2},
both the increments
\mathbf{x}(t_{2})-\mathbf{x}(s_{2})
and
\mathbf{x}(t_{1})-\mathbf{x}(s_{1})
are independent and
\mathbf{x}(t_{1})-\mathbf{x}(s_{1})\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},(t_{1}-s_{1})\ \mathbf{I}),
where
\mathbf{I}
is the
N\times N
identity matrix. Note that the Wiener process is a Gaussian process and a martingale. Furthermore, its component processes
\left\{w_{m}(t)\right\}_{t\geq 0}
are independent for each
1\leq m\leq M.

Let
\left\{\mathbf{x}(t)\right\}_{t\geq 0}
be a Markov process adapted to the filtration
\left\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\right\}_{t\geq 0},
and let the map
(t,\omega)\mapsto\mathbf{x}(t)(\omega)
defined between
[0,\infty)\times\Omega
and
\mathbb{R}^{N}
be
\mathcal{B}([0,\infty))\times\mathcal{A}
measurable, where
\mathcal{B}([0,\infty))
is the Borel sigma algebra on
[0,\infty).
Let
\left\{\theta_{t}\right\}_{t\geq 0}
be a collection of shift operators on
\Omega
such that
\mathbf{x}(t)(\theta_{s})=\mathbf{x}(s+t)
for all
s,t\geq 0.
For each
t\geq 0
and
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{N},
let

p_{t}(\mathbf{y},B)=\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{x}(t)\in B\ |\ \mathbf{x}(0)=\mathbf{y}).

A probability measure
\pi
on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
equipped with the Borel sigma algebra is
invariant
with respect to
\left\{\mathbf{x}(t)\right\}_{t\geq 0}
if

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}p_{t}(\mathbf{y},B)\ d\pi(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\pi(B)

for each
t\geq 0
and each
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}.
A set
A\in\mathcal{A}
is
shift invariant
if
\theta_{t}^{-1}(A)=A
for all
t\geq 0.
The Markov process
\left\{\mathbf{x}(t)\right\}_{t\geq 0}
is
ergodic
if it admits an invariant probability measure
\pi
satisfying
\mathbb{P}_{\pi}(A)\in\{0,1\}
for every shift invariant set
A,
in which
\mathbb{P}_{\pi}
is the probability measure defined by

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\pi}(B)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}p_{t}(\mathbf{y},B)\ d\pi(\mathbf{y}).

(2.15)

The most important theorem pertaining to Markov ergodic processes is Birkhoff’s Ergodic Theorem
[
32
]
.

Theorem 2.4

(Birkhoff’s Ergodic Theorem)
.

If
\left\{\mathbf{x}(t)\right\}_{t\geq 0}
is a Markov ergodic process, then for any
p\in\mathbb{N}
and for any measurable
f:\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
satisfying the condition that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(f(\mathbf{y}))^{p}\ d\pi(\mathbf{y})
exists, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}f(\mathbf{x}(s))\ ds

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[f(\mathbf{x}(0))].

(2.16)

\mathbb{P}_{\pi}
-almost surely, where

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[f(\mathbf{y})]

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}f(\mathbf{y})\ d\pi(\mathbf{y}).

(2.17)

2.4
Stochastic Differential Equations

As before, let
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space and fix
M,N\in\mathbb{N}.
Let
\left\{\mathbf{w}(t)\right\}_{t\geq 0}
be the standard
M
-dimensional Wiener process. In this section, we briefly review the theory of stochastic differential equations.

Let
\mathbf{F}:[0,\infty)\times\Omega\rightarrow\text{Mat}_{N,M}(\mathbb{R})
be a map such that the
MN
-dimensional stochastic process
\left\{\text{vec}(\mathbf{F}(t,\cdot))\right\}_{t\geq 0}
is adapted to the standard filtration of the Wiener process, where
vec
is the vectorization operator that maps
N\times M
matrix to the
NM
-dimensional vector formed by stacking the columns of the matrix one after another. The
time integral
of
\mathbf{F}
and
stochastic integral
of
\mathbf{F}
with respect to the Wiener process are respectively defined as

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{F}(s,\omega)\ ds

\displaystyle=\lim_{K\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{K-1}\mathbf{F}(s_{k},\omega)\ \left(s_{k+1}-s_{k}\right)

(2.18)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{F}(s,\omega)\ d\mathbf{w}(s)

\displaystyle=\lim_{K\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{K-1}\mathbf{F}(s_{k},\omega)\ \left(\mathbf{w}(s_{k+1})-\mathbf{w}(s_{k})\right),

(2.19)

where
\left\{s_{k}\right\}_{k=1}^{K}
is an increasing sequence in
[0,t]
satisfying
s_{1}=0
and
s_{K}=t.
Note the limits in (
2.18
) and (
2.19
) are taken in the sense of
L^{2}
i.e. the mean-squared limit.

An
Ito process
is a process
\left\{\mathbf{x}(t)\right\}_{t\geq 0}
adapted to the standard filtration of the Wiener process satisfying

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{x}(0)+\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{b}(s,\mathbf{x}(s))\ ds+\int_{0}^{t}\boldsymbol{\sigma}(s,\mathbf{x}(s))\ d\mathbf{w}(s),

(2.20)

where
\mathbf{b}:[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
\boldsymbol{\sigma}:[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow\text{Mat}_{N,M}(\mathbb{R})
are measurable functions. Informally, we write (
2.20
) as an equation of the form

\displaystyle d\mathbf{x}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{b}(t,\mathbf{x}(t))\ dt+\boldsymbol{\sigma}(t,\mathbf{x}(t))\ d\mathbf{w}(t),

(2.21)

which we refer to as a
stochastic differential equation (SDE)
. We interpret any Ito process in (
2.20
) as a solution to the SDE (
2.21
).

We state the existence and uniqueness criteria
[
21
, Theorem 5.2.1]
pertaining to the solution of the SDE (
2.21
).

Theorem 2.5

(SDE Solution Existence and Uniqueness Conditions)
.

Consider the SDE (
2.21
). Suppose there exist constants
C,D
satisfying

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{b}(t,\mathbf{y})\|+\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}(t,\mathbf{y})\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq C(1+\|\mathbf{y}\|)

(2.22)

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{b}(t,\mathbf{z})-\mathbf{b}(t,\mathbf{y})\|

\displaystyle\leq D(\|\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{y}\|),

(2.23)

for all
\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and all
t\geq 0,
where
\|\cdot\|_{F}
is the Frobenius norm and
\|\cdot\|
is the Euclidean
2
-norm. Suppose further that the initial random vector
\mathbf{x}(0)
satisfies
\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{x}(0)\|^{2}]
being finite and
\mathbf{x}(0)
is independent of the Wiener process. Then, the SDE (
2.21
)
has a unique solution for
\mathbf{x}(t),
which is a diffusion process adapted to the standard filtration of the Wiener process. Moreover, the solution is square integrable i.e.
\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\|\mathbf{x}(s)\|^{2}\ ds\right]
is finite for each
t\geq 0.

Chapter 3
The Ornstein-Uhlenbeck Process

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space. Recall in the introduction, we defined an
N
-dimensional OU Process on this probability space, namely the
N
-dimensional stochastic process
\left\{\mathbf{x}(t)\right\}_{t\geq 0}
satisfying the SDE

\displaystyle d\mathbf{x}(t)=-\mathbf{B}\ \mathbf{x}(t)\ dt+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\ d\mathbf{w}(t).

(3.1)

The linear operator
\mathbf{B}\in\text{Mat}_{N,N}(\mathbb{R})
is called the friction operator which satisfies the condition
-\mathbf{B}
is Hurwitz. The linear operator
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\in\text{Mat}_{M,N}(\mathbb{R})
is called the volatility operator. The process
\left\{\mathbf{w}(t)\right\}_{t\geq 0}
is the
M
-dimensional standard Wiener Process. Moreover, we assume that
\mathbf{x}(0)\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{S})
is independent of the Wiener process, in which we recall
\mathbf{S}\in\text{Mat}_{N,N}(\mathbb{R})
is the stationary covariance operator satisfying the Lyapunov equation

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}\mathbf{S}+\mathbf{S}\mathbf{B}^{\text{T}}

\displaystyle=2\mathbf{D},

(3.2)

and

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}

\displaystyle=\frac{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\text{T}}}{2}

(3.3)

is the diffusion operator. We view the friction operator
\mathbf{B}
and volatility operator
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
as OU model parameters.

In this thesis, we will utilize the matrix-valued function
\mathbf{G}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\text{Mat}_{N,N}(\mathbb{R})
defined by

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}(t)

\displaystyle=\exp(-t\mathbf{B}),

(3.4)

which is commonly known as the
Green’s function (propagator)
in the literature
[
18
]
. We note that
\mathbf{G}
is the fundamental solution operator to the linear ordinary differential equation
\dot{\mathbf{y}}=-\mathbf{B}\mathbf{y}.

The stationary covariance matrix
\mathbf{S}
has an explicit formula as a matrix-valued integral involving
\mathbf{G}(t).
We will be using this explicit formula throughout this thesis.

Theorem 3.1

(OU Process Stationary Covariance Matrix Explicit Formula)
.

The stationary covariance matrix
\mathbf{S}
is explicitly

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbf{G}(t)\ \mathbf{D}\ \mathbf{G}^{\text{T}}(t)\ dt.

(3.5)

Moreover,
\mathbf{S}
is positive semidefinite.

Proof.

First, we verify that (
3.5
) is indeed a solution to the Lyapunov equation (
3.2
). Substituting the right hand side of (
3.5
) for
\mathbf{S}
into the left hand side of the equation (
3.2
) and utilizing the property
\mathbf{G}^{\prime}=-\mathbf{B}\mathbf{G}
from
Theorem
2.1
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}\mathbf{S}+\mathbf{S}\mathbf{B}^{\text{T}}

\displaystyle=2\mathbf{B}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbf{G}(t)\ \mathbf{D}\ \mathbf{G}^{\text{T}}(t)\ dt\right)+2\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbf{G}(t)\ \mathbf{D}\ \mathbf{G}^{\text{T}}(t)\ dt\right)\mathbf{B}^{\text{T}}

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbf{B}\ \mathbf{G}(t)\ \mathbf{D}\ \mathbf{G}^{\text{T}}(t)\ dt+2\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbf{G}(t)\ \mathbf{D}\ \left(\mathbf{B}\ \mathbf{G}(t)\right)^{\text{T}}dt

\displaystyle=-2\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbf{G}^{\prime}(t)\ \mathbf{D}\ \mathbf{G}^{\text{T}}(t)\ dt-2\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbf{G}(t)\ \mathbf{D}\ \left(\mathbf{G}^{\prime}(t)\right)^{\text{T}}dt

\displaystyle=-2\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\mathbf{G}(t)\ \mathbf{D}\ \left(\mathbf{G}^{\text{T}}(t)\right)\right)^{\prime}\ dt

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}-2\ \mathbf{G}(t)\ \mathbf{D}\ \mathbf{G}^{\text{T}}(t)+2\ \mathbf{G}(0)\ \mathbf{D}\ \mathbf{G}^{\text{T}}(0)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0}+2\mathbf{D}=2\mathbf{D}.

Thus, the right hand side of (
3.5
) is a solution of (
3.2
).

Next, we verify our solution to the Lyapunov equation (
3.2
) is unique. To this end, we let
\mathbf{S}_{1}
and
\mathbf{S}_{2}
be two solutions to (
3.2
). Via the product rule, we obtain

\displaystyle\left(\mathbf{G}(t)\ (\mathbf{S}_{1}-\mathbf{S}_{2})\ \mathbf{G}^{\text{T}}(t)\right)^{\prime}

\displaystyle=-\mathbf{G}(t)\ \mathbf{B}\ (\mathbf{S}_{1}-\mathbf{S}_{2})\ \mathbf{G}^{\text{T}}(t)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\mathbf{G}(t)\ (\mathbf{S}_{1}-\mathbf{S}_{2})\ \mathbf{B}^{\text{T}}\ \mathbf{G}^{\text{T}}(t)

\displaystyle=-\mathbf{G}(t)\ \left(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{S}_{1}+\mathbf{S}_{1}\mathbf{B}^{\text{T}}-\mathbf{B}\mathbf{S}_{2}-\mathbf{S}_{2}\mathbf{B}^{\text{T}}\right)\ \mathbf{G}^{\text{T}}(t)

\displaystyle=-\mathbf{G}(t)\ \left(2\mathbf{D}-2\mathbf{D}\right)\ \mathbf{G}^{\text{T}}(t)=\mathbf{0},

which implies
\mathbf{G}(t)\ (\mathbf{S}_{1}-\mathbf{S}_{2})\ \mathbf{G}^{\text{T}}(t)
must be a constant function of
t.
Since
-\mathbf{B}
is Hurwitz, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\mathbf{G}(t)\ (\mathbf{S}_{1}-\mathbf{S}_{2})\ \mathbf{G}^{\text{T}}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0},

which forces

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}(t)\ (\mathbf{S}_{1}-\mathbf{S}_{2})\ \mathbf{G}^{\text{T}}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0}

(3.6)

for all
t\geq 0.
Since
\mathbf{G}(t)
and
\mathbf{G}^{\text{T}}(t)
are invertible, we left multiply both sides of the equation by
\mathbf{G}(-t)
and right multiply both sides of the equation by
\mathbf{G}^{\text{T}}(-t)
to obtain the equality
\mathbf{S}_{1}-\mathbf{S}_{2}=\mathbf{0}.
This shows
\mathbf{S}_{1}=\mathbf{S}_{2},
which means the Lyapunov equation (
3.2
) has only one solution.

Finally, we show
\mathbf{S}
is positive semidefinite. Note that

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}\mathbf{S}^{\text{T}}+\mathbf{S}^{\text{T}}\mathbf{B}^{\text{T}}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{S}+\mathbf{S}\mathbf{B}^{\text{T}}\right)^{\text{T}}

\displaystyle=2\mathbf{D}^{\text{T}}=2\mathbf{D},

(3.7)

which implies
\mathbf{S}^{\text{T}}
solves the Lyapunov equation (
3.2
). But by uniqueness, we have
\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{S}^{\text{T}},
which means
\mathbf{S}
is symmetric. Now, for all nonzero
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{N},
we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}^{\text{T}}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbf{y}^{\text{T}}\ \mathbf{G}(t)\ \mathbf{D}\ \mathbf{G}^{\text{T}}(t)\ \mathbf{y}\ dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbf{y}^{\text{T}}\ \mathbf{G}(t)\ \boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\text{T}}\ \mathbf{G}^{\text{T}}(t)\ \mathbf{y}\ dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\text{T}}\ \mathbf{G}^{\text{T}}(t)\ \mathbf{y}\right\|_{2}^{2}\ dt,

which is non-negative because the integrand
\left\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\text{T}}\ \mathbf{G}^{\text{T}}(t)\ \mathbf{y}\right\|_{2}^{2}
is non-negative for all
t\geq 0.
Hence,
\mathbf{S}
is positive semidefinite.
∎

3.1
Statistical Properties

In this section, we will recall the important statistical properties of the OU Process. We first derive the explicit representation of the OU process i.e. the solution to the governing stochastic differential equation (
3.1
). The solution also reveals the essential statistics of the OU process.

Theorem 3.2

(OU Process Explicit Representation)
.

The unique solution of the governing OU process stochastic differential equation (
3.1
) is explicitly

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{G}(t)\ \mathbf{x}(0)+\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{G}(t-s)\ \boldsymbol{\Sigma}\ d\mathbf{w}(s).

(3.8)

Moreover, the OU process is an Ito diffusion process that is square integrable.

Proof.

First, we show that the right hand side of (
3.8
) solves the stochastic differential equation (
3.1
). Let

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{G}(-t)\ \mathbf{x}(t).

Using Ito’s product rule and the Ito formalisms
[
21
, Theorem 4.2.1]
, namely

\displaystyle(dt)^{2}=0\ ,\ d\mathbf{w}(t)\ dt=\mathbf{0},

we obtain

\displaystyle d\mathbf{y}(t)

\displaystyle=d\left(\mathbf{G}(-t)\ \mathbf{x}(t)\right)

\displaystyle=d\mathbf{G}(-t)\ \mathbf{x}(t)+\mathbf{G}(-t)\ d\mathbf{x}(t)+d\mathbf{G}(-t)\ d\mathbf{x}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{B}\mathbf{G}(-t)\ \mathbf{x}(t)\ dt+\mathbf{G}(-t)\left(-\mathbf{B}\ \mathbf{x}(t)\ dt+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\ d\mathbf{w}(t)\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\left(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{G}(-t)\ dt\right)(-\mathbf{B}\ \mathbf{x}(t)\ dt+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\ d\mathbf{w}(t))

\displaystyle=\mathbf{G}(-t)\ \boldsymbol{\Sigma}\ d\mathbf{w}(t)+\left(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{G}(-t)\ dt\right)(-\mathbf{B}\ \mathbf{x}(t)\ dt+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\ d\mathbf{w}(t))

\displaystyle=\mathbf{G}(-t)\ \boldsymbol{\Sigma}\ d\mathbf{w}(t),

(3.9)

Formally, (
3.9
) is the integral equation

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}(t)-\mathbf{y}(0)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{G}(-s)\ \boldsymbol{\Sigma}\ d\mathbf{w}(s).

(3.10)

Upon recognizing that
\mathbf{y}(0)=\mathbf{x}(0)
and recalling that
\mathbf{G}(-t)
is invertible with inverse
\mathbf{G}(t)
by
Theorem
2.1
,
we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{G}(t)\ \mathbf{y}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{G}(t)\left(\mathbf{y}(0)+\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{G}(-s)\ \boldsymbol{\Sigma}\ d\mathbf{w}(s)\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{G}(t)\left(\mathbf{x}(0)+\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{G}(-s)\ \boldsymbol{\Sigma}\ d\mathbf{w}(s)\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{G}(t)\ \mathbf{x}(0)+\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{G}(t-s)\ \boldsymbol{\Sigma}\ d\mathbf{w}(s).

Thus, the right side of (
3.8
) is a solution to the governing stochastic differential equation (
3.1
).

Next, we show that (
3.1
) has a unique solution that is an Ito diffusion process that is square integrable. We need to verify the existence and uniqueness criteria listed in
Theorem
2.5
for the OU process. Define
\mathbf{b}:[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
\boldsymbol{\sigma}:[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow\text{Mat}_{N,M}(\mathbb{R})
by

\displaystyle\mathbf{b}(t,\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=-\mathbf{B}\mathbf{y}

(3.11)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}(t,\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}.

(3.12)

Since
\mathbf{b}(t,\cdot)
is a linear operator that is independent of the variable
t,
it is continuous and is hence measurable. Since
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
is constant function in both
\mathbf{y}
and
t,
it is automatically measurable. Furthermore,
\mathbf{b}
is bounded; by the definition of boundedness, there exists a constant
D
directly satisfying (
2.23
). Now, let
C=\max(D,\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\|_{F}).
Then

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{y},t)\|+\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}(\mathbf{y},t)\|_{F}

\displaystyle=\|\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{y},t)\|+\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq D\|\mathbf{y}\|+\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq C\|\mathbf{y}\|+C=C(1+\|\mathbf{y}\|).

Hence, our choice of
C
satisfies (
2.22
).
Moreover, recall by assumption that
\mathbf{x}(0)\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{S})
is independent of the Wiener process, and
\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{x}(0)\|^{2}]
exists by virtue of
\mathbf{x}(0)
having a finite covariance matrix. Hence, the SDE (
3.1
) satisfies the assumptions listed in
Theorem
2.5
. This completes the proof.
∎

Using the solution of the OU process, we derive its important statistical properties.

Theorem 3.3

(OU Process is Gaussian)
.

The OU process is a Gaussian process.

Proof.

Consider the stochastic processes
\left\{\mathbf{y}(t)\right\}_{t\geq 0}
and
\left\{\mathbf{z}(t)\right\}_{t\geq 0},
where

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{G}(t)\ \mathbf{x}(0)

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{G}(t)\ \int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{G}(-s)\ \boldsymbol{\Sigma}\ d\mathbf{w}(s)

for each
t\geq 0.
Note that every finite linear combination of random vectors from the OU process is the sum of a finite linear combination of random vectors from
\left\{\mathbf{y}(t)\right\}_{t\geq 0}
and a finite linear combination of random vectors from
\left\{\mathbf{z}(t)\right\}_{t\geq 0}.
Furthermore, any linear combination of random vectors from
\left\{\mathbf{y}(t)\right\}_{t\geq 0}
is independent of any linear combination of random vectors from
\left\{\mathbf{z}(t)\right\}_{t\geq 0}
since
\mathbf{x}(0)
is independent of the Wiener process.

We show that the process
\left\{\mathbf{y}(t)\right\}_{t\geq 0}
is Gaussian. Every finite linear combination of random vectors from
\left\{\mathbf{y}(t)\right\}_{t\geq 0}
is of the form

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{k}\ \mathbf{y}(t_{k})

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{k}\ \mathbf{G}(t_{k})\right)\ \mathbf{x}(0)

(3.13)

for some
K\in\mathbb{N}
and times
t_{1}\ ,\ \dots\ ,\ t_{K}\geq 0
and constants
\alpha_{1}\ ,\ \dots\ ,\ \alpha_{K}\in\mathbb{R}.
Observe that the right hand side of (
3.13
) is a deterministic linear transformation of the Gaussian random vector
\mathbf{x}(0),
which means it is also Gaussian. Hence,
\left\{\mathbf{y}(t)\right\}_{t\geq 0}
is a Gaussian process.

Next, we show
\left\{\mathbf{z}(t)\right\}_{t\geq 0}
is a Gaussian process. Consider the map
(s,\omega)\mapsto\mathbf{G}(t-s)\ \boldsymbol{\Sigma}
defined between
[0,\infty)\times\Omega
and
\text{Mat}_{N,M}(\mathbb{R}).
Since this map does not depend on the variable
\omega,
it is a deterministic matrix-valued function whose
NM
entry functions are deterministic real-valued functions. These entry functions are automatically adapted to the standard filtration of the Wiener process and are continuous in
s,
which means they are square integrable over any closed, finite interval. Hence, by
[
21
, Theorem 3.2.1]
, the process
\left\{\mathbf{z}(t)\right\}_{t\geq 0}
is a Gaussian process.

Finally, every finite linear combination of random vectors from the OU process is the sum of two independent Gaussian random vectors, one of which is a linear combination of random vectors from the process
\left\{\mathbf{y}(t)\right\}_{t\geq 0},
while the other is a linear combination of random vectors from the process
\left\{\mathbf{z}(t)\right\}_{t\geq 0}.
Therefore, every linear combination of random vectors from the OU process is a Gaussian random vector. This proves the claim.

∎

Theorem 3.4

(OU Process Statistics)
.

The OU process is a Gaussian process whose mean, auto-covariance functions are

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathbf{x}}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0}

(3.14)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathbf{x}}(s,t)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{G}(s)\ \mathbf{S}\ \mathbf{G}^{\text{T}}(t)+2\int_{0}^{\min(s,t)}\mathbf{G}(s-u)\ \mathbf{D}\ \mathbf{G}^{\text{T}}(t-u)\ du,

(3.15)

respectively.

Proof.

First, we determine the mean function of the OU process. Let
\left\{\mathbf{y}(t)\right\}_{t\geq 0}
and
\left\{\mathbf{z}(t)\right\}_{t\geq 0}
be the processes as defined in the proof of the previous theorem. Since
\mathbf{x}(t)=\mathbf{y}(t)+\mathbf{z}(t)
for each
t\geq 0,
we use the linearity of the expectation operator to obtain

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathbf{x}}(t)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathbf{y}}(t)+\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathbf{z}}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{G}(t)\ \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}(0)]+\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{G}(t-s)\ \boldsymbol{\Sigma}\ d\mathbf{w}(s)\right]

(3.16)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0}+\mathbf{0}=\mathbf{0},

in which we recognized the expectation of the stochastic integral term in (
3.16
) vanishes due to the process
\left\{\mathbf{z}(t)\right\}_{t\geq 0}
being a martingale.

Next, we calculate the autocovariance function of the OU process. Using the definition (
2.13
) and the bilinearity of the covariance operator, we have

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathbf{x}}(s,t)

\displaystyle=\text{Cov}\left(\mathbf{x}(s),\mathbf{x}(t)\right)

\displaystyle=\text{Cov}\left(\mathbf{y}(s)+\mathbf{z}(s),\mathbf{y}(t)+\mathbf{z}(t)\right)

(3.17)

\displaystyle=\text{Cov}\left(\mathbf{y}(s),\mathbf{y}(t)\right)+\text{Cov}\left(\mathbf{z}(s),\mathbf{z}(t)\right)

(3.18)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathbf{y}}(s,t)+\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathbf{z}}(s,t)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{G}(s)\ \text{Cov}(\mathbf{x}(0),\mathbf{x}(0))\ \mathbf{G}^{\text{T}}(t)+\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathbf{z}}(s,t)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{G}(s)\ \mathbf{S}\ \mathbf{G}^{\text{T}}(t)+\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathbf{z}}(s,t),

in which (
3.18
) is the result of expanding out the covariance expression in (
3.17
) using bilinearity and the fact
\mathbf{y}(s)
and
\mathbf{z}(t)
are independent and hence uncorrelated. We now determine
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathbf{z}}(s,t).
For all
s\leq t,
we use the additivity of the stochastic integral and the bilinearity of the covariance operator to obtain

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathbf{z}}(s,t)

\displaystyle=\text{Cov}\left(\int_{0}^{s}\mathbf{G}(s-u)\ \boldsymbol{\Sigma}\ d\mathbf{w}(u)\ ,\ \int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{G}(t-v)\ \boldsymbol{\Sigma}\ d\mathbf{w}(v)\right)

\displaystyle=\text{Cov}\left(\int_{0}^{s}\mathbf{G}(s-u)\ \boldsymbol{\Sigma}\ d\mathbf{w}(u)\ ,\ \int_{0}^{s}\mathbf{G}(t-v)\ \boldsymbol{\Sigma}\ d\mathbf{w}(v)\right)

(3.19)

\displaystyle\qquad+\text{Cov}\left(\int_{0}^{s}\mathbf{G}(s-u)\ \boldsymbol{\Sigma}\ d\mathbf{w}(u),\int_{s}^{t}\mathbf{G}(t-v)\ \boldsymbol{\Sigma}\ d\mathbf{w}(v)\right)

\displaystyle=\text{Cov}\left(\int_{0}^{s}\mathbf{G}(s-u)\ \boldsymbol{\Sigma}\ d\mathbf{w}(u)\ ,\ \int_{0}^{s}\mathbf{G}(t-v)\ \boldsymbol{\Sigma}\ d\mathbf{w}(v)\right)

(3.20)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{s}\mathbf{G}(s-u)\ \boldsymbol{\Sigma}\ \left(\mathbf{G}(t-u)\ \boldsymbol{\Sigma}\right)^{\text{T}}\ du

(3.21)

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{s}\mathbf{G}(s-u)\ \mathbf{D}\ \mathbf{G}^{\text{T}}(t-u)\ du,

in which the second covariance term in (
3.19
) vanishes due to the Wiener process having independent increments, and (
3.21
) follows from evaluating the covariance term in (
3.20
) with Ito’s Isometry
[
21
, Corollary 3.1.7]
. Similar reasoning gives

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathbf{z}}(s,t)

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{G}(s-u)\ \mathbf{D}\ \mathbf{G}^{\text{T}}(t-u)\ du

for
s\geq t.
Upon combining the results corresponding to the cases
s\leq t
and
s\geq t,
we have

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathbf{z}}(s,t)

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{\min(s,t)}\mathbf{G}(s-u)\ \mathbf{D}\ \mathbf{G}^{\text{T}}(t-u)\ du

for all
s,t\geq 0.
Hence, we obtain the OU process autocovariance function.
∎

Based on the time-dependent statistics, we can deduce the OU process is stationary.

Theorem 3.5

(OU Process Stationary)
.

The OU process is stationary, and its covariance function is

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}_{\mathbf{x}}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{S}

(3.22)

for all
t\geq 0.

Proof.

Since the OU Process is a Gaussian process, it suffices to show the process is weakly stationary. In particular, we show the map
(t,h)\mapsto\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathbf{x}}(t+h,t)
defined between
[0,\infty)^{2}
and
\text{Mat}_{N,N}(\mathbb{R})
depends only on
h.
Differentiating the map
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathbf{x}}(t+h,t)
with respect to
t,
we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\ \boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathbf{x}}(t+h,t)}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\mathbf{G}(t+h)\ \mathbf{S}\ \mathbf{G}^{\text{T}}(t)+2\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{G}(t+h-u)\ \mathbf{D}\ \mathbf{G}^{\text{T}}(t-u)\ du\right)

(3.23)

\displaystyle=-\mathbf{B}\ \mathbf{G}(t+h)\ \mathbf{S}\ \mathbf{G}^{\text{T}}(t)-\mathbf{G}(t+h)\ \mathbf{S}\ \left(\mathbf{B}\ \mathbf{G}(t)\right)^{\text{T}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+2\ \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{G}(u+h)\ \mathbf{D}\ \mathbf{G}^{\text{T}}(u)\ du\right)

(3.24)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{G}(t+h)\ (-\mathbf{B}\mathbf{S}-\mathbf{S}\mathbf{B}^{\text{T}})\ \mathbf{G}^{\text{T}}(t)+\mathbf{G}(t+h)\ (2\mathbf{D})\ \mathbf{G}^{\text{T}}(t)

(3.25)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{G}(t+h)\ (-\mathbf{B}\mathbf{S}+\mathbf{S}\mathbf{B}^{\text{T}}+2\mathbf{D})\ \mathbf{G}^{\text{T}}(t)=\mathbf{0},

(3.26)

where the second term of (
3.24
) follows from applying the change of variables
u\mapsto t-u
on the integral term within the partial derivative expression of (
3.23
), and the first term of (
3.25
) follows from rewriting the first term of (
3.24
) using the the fact that
\mathbf{B}
and
\mathbf{G}(t)
commute according to
Theorem
2.1
. Finally, (
3.26
) follows from the Lyapunov equation (
3.2
).
Since the partial derivative of
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathbf{x}}(t+h,t)
vanishes on the connected set
[0,\infty)^{2},
we conclude that
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathbf{x}}(t+h,t)
depends only on
h.

Next, for each
t\geq 0,
we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}_{\mathbf{x}}(t)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathbf{x}}(t,t)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathbf{x}}(0,0)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{V}_{\mathbf{x}}(0)=\mathbf{S}.

∎

The previous theorem implies
\mathbf{x}(t)\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{S})
for each
t\geq 0,
which trivially implies the stationary distribution of the OU process is the Gaussian distribution with mean
\mathbf{0}
and covariance matrix
\mathbf{S}.

We provide a sufficient condition for the OU process to be ergodic.

Theorem 3.6

(OU Process Ergodicity)
.

If the diffusion matrix
\mathbf{D}
is invertible, then the OU process is ergodic.

Proof.

In order to prove ergodicity, we need to show that the stationary distribution of the OU process, namely the Gaussian distribution with mean
\mathbf{0}
and covariance matrix
\mathbf{S},
is unique
[
32
]
.

Consider a generic (time homogeneous) Ito diffusion process
\left\{\mathbf{y}(t)\right\}_{t\geq 0}
satisfying the stochastic differential equation

\displaystyle d\mathbf{y}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{y}(t))\ dt+\boldsymbol{\sigma}(\mathbf{y}(t))\ d\mathbf{w}(t)

(3.27)

with
\mathbf{b}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
\boldsymbol{\sigma}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow\text{Mat}_{N,M}(\mathbb{R})
being Borel measurable functions. A sufficient condition
[
33
, Theorem 5.3.2]
for this diffusion process to have a unique stationary distribution is that there is an open, bounded domain
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
with smooth boundary
\partial U
such that

i)

For each
\mathbf{x}\in U,
the smallest eigenvalue of the diffusion coefficient
\frac{\boldsymbol{\sigma}(\mathbf{x})\ \boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\text{T}}(\mathbf{x})}{2}
is bounded away from
0.

ii)

The process is recurrent relative to
U
: for each
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus U,
the mean time
\tau
at which a path issuing from
\mathbf{z}
reaches the set
U
is finite and
\sup_{\mathbf{z}\in K}\mathbb{E}[\tau|\mathbf{z}]
exists for each compact set
K
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}.

Moreover, in
[
34
, Theorem 3.9]
, it was shown that the condition
ii)
is equivalent to the
Foster Lyapunov drift condition
, which states there is a non-negative, twice continuously differentiable function
V:\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
for which
\mathcal{L}V
is negative on
\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus U.
Here,
\mathcal{L}
is the infinitesimal generator defined by

\displaystyle(\mathcal{L}f)(\mathbf{z})

\displaystyle=\left(\nabla f\right)^{\text{T}}\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{z})+\text{tr}\left(\nabla^{2}f\ \frac{\boldsymbol{\sigma}(\mathbf{z})\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\text{T}}(\mathbf{z})}{2}\right).

(3.28)

for any twice continuously-differentiable
f:\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},
where
\nabla f
is the gradient of
f
and
\nabla^{2}f
is the Hessian matrix of
f.

First, we verify statement
i)
holds for the OU process. Let

\displaystyle U=\left\{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\ :\ \|\mathbf{z}\|<\sqrt{3\text{tr}(\mathbf{P})\ \text{tr}(\mathbf{D})}\right\},

where
\mathbf{P}\in\text{Mat}_{N,N}(\mathbb{R})
is the unique positive definite solution to the Lyapunov equation

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}^{\text{T}}\mathbf{P}+\mathbf{P}\mathbf{B}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{I},

(3.29)

where
\mathbf{I}
is the
N\times N
identity matrix. Since
\mathbf{P}
and
\mathbf{D}
are positive definite, their traces are positive. Hence,
U
is nonempty. It is an open ball in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
centered around
\mathbf{0}.
Note that the diffusion coefficient of the OU process is the constant matrix
\mathbf{D}.
Since
\mathbf{D}
is positive definite, its eigenvalues are all positive. So automatically, its smallest eigenvalue is bounded away from
0
on
U.
This means the OU process satisfies statement
\text{i}).

Next, we verify the second statement
ii)
by verifying the Foster Lyapunov drift condition holds. For the OU process, the infinitesimal generator is explicitly

\displaystyle(\mathcal{L}f)(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=-(\nabla f)^{\text{T}}\ \mathbf{B}\mathbf{y}+\text{tr}(\nabla^{2}f\ \mathbf{D}),

(3.30)

for each twice-continuously differentiable
f:\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}.
Consider the Lyapunov function
V:\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined by

\displaystyle V(\mathbf{z})=\mathbf{z}^{\text{T}}\mathbf{P}\mathbf{z}.

Then, we compute

\displaystyle\nabla V(\mathbf{z})

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{P}+\mathbf{P}^{\text{T}})\ \mathbf{z}=2\mathbf{P}\mathbf{z}

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}V(\mathbf{z})

\displaystyle=2\mathbf{P}.

For all
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus U,
by the Lyapunov equation (
3.29
), we have

\displaystyle(\mathcal{L}V)(\mathbf{z})

\displaystyle=-\left(2\mathbf{P}\mathbf{z}\right)^{\text{T}}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{z}+\text{tr}(2\mathbf{P}\mathbf{D})

\displaystyle=-2\mathbf{z}^{\text{T}}(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{B})\ \mathbf{z}+2\text{tr}(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{D})

\displaystyle=-\mathbf{z}^{\text{T}}(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{B}+\left(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{B}\right)^{\text{T}})\ \mathbf{z}+2\text{tr}(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{D})

\displaystyle=-\mathbf{z}^{\text{T}}(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{B}+\mathbf{B}^{\text{T}}\mathbf{P})\ \mathbf{z}+2\text{tr}(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{D})

\displaystyle=-\|\mathbf{z}\|^{2}+2\text{tr}(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{D})

\displaystyle\leq-3\text{tr}(\mathbf{P})\ \text{tr}(\mathbf{D})+2\text{tr}(\mathbf{P})\ \text{tr}(\mathbf{D})

(3.31)

\displaystyle=-\text{tr}(\mathbf{P})\ \text{tr}(\mathbf{D})<0,

in which the second term of (
3.31
) follows from the Cauchy Schwarz inequality applied to the trace of the product of two positive semidefinite matrices. Hence, the OU process satisfies statement
\text{ii)},
and we conclude the process is ergodic.
∎

3.2
The Lead Process

In this section, we consider the OU process generated by model parameters
\mathbf{B}
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma},
and we assume the process is ergodic. We recall the auxiliary
lead process
that we defined in the introduction, namely the
N\times N
matrix-valued stochastic process
\left\{\mathbf{A}(t)\right\}_{t\geq 0},
in which

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{x}(s)\ d\mathbf{x}^{\text{T}}(s)-d\mathbf{x}(s)\ \mathbf{x}^{\text{T}}(s).

(3.32)

for each
t\geq 0.
More explicitly, for each
1\leq m,n\leq N,
the
(m,n)
-th entry of
\mathbf{A}(t)
is an Ito stochastic integral of the form

\displaystyle A_{m,n}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}x_{m}(s)\ dx_{n}(s)-x_{n}(s)\ dx_{m}(s),

(3.33)

where
\left\{x_{n}(t)\right\}_{t\geq 0}
is the
n
-th component OU process. By definition, each
\mathbf{A}(t)
is the lead matrix corresponding to a realization of the OU process over the finite time interval
[0,t].

We prove a strong law of large numbers identity pertaining to the lead process.

Theorem 3.7

(Lead Process Strong Law of Large Numbers Identity)
.

The lead process satisfies the following strong law of large numbers identity:

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\mathbf{A}(t)}{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbf{B}\mathbf{S}-\mathbf{S}\mathbf{B}^{\text{T}}}{2}

(3.34)

almost surely.

Proof.

Using the governing stochastic differential equation (
3.1
), we write

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2t}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{x}(s)\ d\mathbf{x}^{\text{T}}(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2t}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{x}(s)\ \left(-\mathbf{B}\ \mathbf{x}(s)\ ds+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\ d\mathbf{w}(s)\right)^{\text{T}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2t}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{x}(s)\ \mathbf{x}^{\text{T}}(s)\ ds\ \mathbf{B}^{\text{T}}+\frac{1}{2t}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{x}(s)\ d\mathbf{w}^{\text{T}}(s)\ \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\text{T}}.

(3.35)

We need to determine the almost sure limits of both integral terms in (
3.35
) as
t\rightarrow\infty.

First, we show that the almost sure limit of the first term of (
3.35
) is
-\frac{\mathbf{S}\mathbf{B}^{\text{T}}}{2}
as
t\rightarrow\infty.
To this end, consider the map
f:\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow\text{Mat}_{N,N}(\mathbb{R})
defined by

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}^{\text{T}}.

(3.36)

This map has
N^{2}
real-valued entry functions of the form
f_{m,n}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined by
f_{m,n}(\mathbf{y})=y_{m}y_{n}
for
1\leq m,n\leq N.
Each
f_{m,n}
is continuous and hence measurable between the spaces
\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
\mathbb{R}
equipped with their respective Borel sigma-algebras. Hence,
f
itself is measurable and by
Theorem
2.4
, we obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{2t}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{x}(s)\ \mathbf{x}^{\text{T}}(s)\ ds\ \mathbf{B}^{\text{T}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{2t}\int_{0}^{t}f(\mathbf{x}(s))\ ds\ \mathbf{B}^{\text{T}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}[f(\mathbf{x}(0))]\ \mathbf{B}^{\text{T}}}{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbf{S}\mathbf{B}^{\text{T}}}{2}

almost surely.

Next, we show that the almost sure limit of the second term of (
3.37
) is
\mathbf{0}.
To this end, consider the
N\times M
matrix-valued stochastic process
\left\{\mathbf{R}(t)\right\}_{t\geq 0}
defined by

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{x}(s)\ d\mathbf{w}^{\text{T}}(s).

for each
t\geq 0.
We need to show that

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\mathbf{R}(t)}{t}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0}

almost surely. Equivalently, we need to show each entry process of
\left\{\mathbf{R}(t)\right\}_{t\geq 0},
namely the process
\left\{R_{n,m}(t)\right\}_{t\geq 0},
in which

\displaystyle R_{n,m}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}x_{n}(s)\ dw_{m}(s)

for each
t\geq 0,
satisfies

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{R_{n,m}(t)}{t}

\displaystyle=0

(3.37)

almost surely. Note that
\left\{R_{n,m}(t)\right\}_{t\geq 0}
is a continuous time square integrable martingale with respect to the standard filtration of the Wiener process because the component OU process
\left\{x_{n}(t)\right\}_{t\geq 0}
is square integrable and is adapted to the component Wiener process
\left\{w_{m}(t)\right\}_{t\geq 0}.
By Ito’s Isometry, for each
t\geq 0,
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[R^{2}_{n,m}(t)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{t}x^{2}_{n}(s)\ ds\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left[x^{2}_{n}(s)\right]\ ds

\displaystyle=tS_{n,n}.

Moreover, the discrete process
\left\{R_{n,m}(K)\right\}_{K\in\mathbb{N}\cup\left\{0\right\}}
is a discrete time square integrable martingale.

We show that the discrete time martingale
\left\{R_{n,m}(K)\right\}_{K\in\mathbb{N}\cup\left\{0\right\}}
satisfies the strong law of large numbers identity

\displaystyle\lim_{K\rightarrow\infty}\frac{R_{n,m}(K)}{K}

\displaystyle=0

(3.38)

almost surely, in which
K
runs over the integers. We consider the martingale difference sequence
\left\{Y_{n,m}(K)\right\}_{K\in\mathbb{N}},
where

\displaystyle Y_{n,m}(K)

\displaystyle=R_{n,m}(K)-R_{n,m}(K-1)

for each
K\in\mathbb{N}.
Because
\left\{R_{n,m}(K)\right\}_{K\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}}
is a martingale, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[R_{n,m}(K)\ R_{n,m}(K-1)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[R_{n,m}(K-1)\ \mathbb{E}\left[R_{n,m}(K)\ |\ \mathcal{S}_{K-1}\right]\ \right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[R^{2}_{n,m}(K)\right],

where
\left\{\mathcal{S}_{t}\right\}_{t\geq 0}
is the standard filtration of the Wiener process. Therefore,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[(Y_{n,m}(k))^{2}\right]}{k^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\left(R_{n,m}(k)-R_{n,m}(k-1)\right)^{2}\right]}{k^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[(R_{n,m}(k))^{2}\right]-2\mathbb{E}\left[R_{n,m}(k)R_{n,m}(k-1)\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[(R_{n,m}(k-1))^{2}\right]}{k^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[(R_{n,m}(k))^{2}\right]-2\mathbb{E}\left[(R_{n,m}(k-1))^{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[(R_{n,m}(k-1))^{2}\right]}{k^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[(R_{n,m}(k))^{2}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[(R_{n,m}(k-1))^{2}\right]}{k^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{kS_{n,n}-(k-1)S_{n,n}}{k^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{S_{n,n}}{k^{2}},

which converges as
K\rightarrow\infty
. By
[
35
, Exercise 4.4.10]
, we deduce
\frac{R_{n,m}(K)}{K}
converges almost surely to some limit as
K\rightarrow\infty.
Because the sequence
\left\{k\right\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is an increasing sequence diverging to infinity, we deduce by Kronecker’s Lemma
[
35
, Theorem 2.5.9]
that

\displaystyle\lim_{K\rightarrow\infty}\frac{R_{n,m}(K)}{K}

\displaystyle=0

almost surely, which proves the identity (
3.38
). Now, by Doob’s submartingale inequality
[
35
, Theorem 4.4.2]
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{K\geq 0}\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{t\in[K,K+1]}\left|R_{n,m}(t)-R_{n,m}(K)\right|>K^{\frac{2}{3}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{K\geq 0}\frac{\mathbb{E}[\left|R_{n,m}(K+1)-R_{n,m}(K)\right|^{2}]}{K^{\frac{4}{3}}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{K\geq 0}\frac{S_{n,n}}{K^{\frac{4}{3}}},

which we note converges as
K\rightarrow\infty
. Therefore, by the Borel Cantelli Lemma, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left\{\sup_{t\in[K,K+1]}\left|R_{n,m}(t)-R_{n,m}(K)\right|>K^{\frac{2}{3}}\right\}\text{ infinitely often}\right)

\displaystyle=0.

This means for large
t\geq 0,
there is a unique integer
K(t)
with
K(t)-1\leq t\leq K(t)
such that

\displaystyle\left|\frac{R_{n,m}(t)}{t}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\frac{R_{n,m}(t)-R_{n,m}(K(t))}{t}\right|+\left|\frac{R_{n,m}(K(t))}{t}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{R_{n,m}(t)-R_{n,m}(K(t))}{t}\right|+\left|\frac{R_{n,m}(K(t))}{K(t)}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{K(t)^{\frac{2}{3}}}{K(t)}+\left|\frac{R_{n,m}(K(t))}{K(t)}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{K(t)^{\frac{1}{3}}}+\left|\frac{R_{n,m}(K(t))}{K(t)}\right|,

(3.39)

almost surely. Therefore, as
t\rightarrow\infty,
we have
K(t)\rightarrow\infty,
which means (
3.39
) converges almost surely to
0.
Hence, we deduce the identity (
3.37
) and conclude the second integral in (
3.35
) almost surely converges to
\mathbf{0}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\text{T}}=\mathbf{0}.

Thus, we conclude

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\mathbf{A}(t)}{t}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mathbf{S}\mathbf{B}^{\text{T}}}{2}-\left(-\frac{\mathbf{S}\mathbf{B}^{\text{T}}}{2}\right)^{\text{T}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbf{B}\mathbf{S}-\mathbf{S}\mathbf{B}^{\text{T}}}{2}.

almost surely.
∎

Because of this strong law of large numbers identity, we use the matrix

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbf{B}\mathbf{S}-\mathbf{S}\mathbf{B}^{\text{T}}}{2}

(3.40)

as our main tool for the Cyclicity Analysis of the OU process. We refer to
\mathbf{Q}
as the
lead matrix
of the OU process. In the next chapter, we will investigate the structure of this matrix in depth.

Chapter 4
Cyclicity Analysis of the Cyclic Ornstein-Uhlenbeck Process

In this chapter, we consider the
N
-dimensional cyclic OU process
\left\{\mathbf{x}(t)\right\}_{t\geq 0}
with model parameters
\mathbf{B}
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma},
in which
\mathbf{B}
is a circulant friction matrix. We write
\mathbf{B}=\text{Circ}(b_{1}\ ,\ \dots\ ,\ b_{N}),
in which
b_{p}\leq 0
for each
p>1
and
b_{1}>0
is large enough so that
\mathbf{B}
is a friction matrix. We let
\mathbf{D}
be the
N\times N
diffusion matrix corresponding to this OU process, as defined in (
3.3
). Recall by
Theorem
2.3
, for each
1\leq n\leq N,

\displaystyle\mu_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=1}^{N}b_{p}\ \omega_{n}^{p-1}

(4.1)

is an eigenvalue of
\mathbf{B}
with associated eigenvector

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\left(1\ ,\ \omega_{n}\ ,\ \dots\ ,\ \omega_{n}^{N-1}\right),

(4.2)

in which
\omega_{n}=e^{\frac{2\pi in}{N}}.

Using the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{B}
, we can determine the explicit lower bound of
b_{1}
to ensure that
\mathbf{B}
is a valid friction matrix.

Theorem 4.1

(Cyclic OU Process Friction Matrix Stability Condition)
.

\mathbf{B}
is a valid friction matrix if and only if
b_{1}>-\sum_{p=2}^{N}b_{p}.

Proof.

In order for
\mathbf{B}
to be a valid friction matrix,
-\mathbf{B}
must be Hurwitz i.e.
\Re(\mu_{n})>0
for all
1\leq n\leq N,
where
\Re(z)
is the real part of the complex number
z.
This is equivalent to the condition that
\min_{1\leq n\leq N}\Re(\mu_{n})>0.

We determine
\min_{1\leq n\leq N}\Re(\mu_{n})
explicitly.
For each
1\leq n\leq N,
we extract the real part of
\mu_{n}
to obtain

\displaystyle\Re(\mu_{n})

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=1}^{N}b_{p}\ \cos\left(\frac{2\pi n(p-1)}{N}\right)

\displaystyle=b_{1}+\sum_{p=2}^{N}b_{p}\ \cos\left(\frac{2\pi n(p-1)}{N}\right)

\displaystyle\geq b_{1}+\sum_{p=2}^{N}b_{p}

(4.3)

\displaystyle=\Re(\mu_{N}),

in which the inequality (
4.3
) follows from the fact that
b_{p}
is non-positive and
b_{p}\cos\left(\frac{2\pi n(p-1)}{N}\right)\geq b_{p}
for each
1<p\leq N.

Hence, we see

\displaystyle\min_{1\leq n\leq N}\Re(\mu_{n})

\displaystyle=\Re(\mu_{N}),

which means
\Re(\lambda_{N})>0
if and only if

\displaystyle b_{1}+\sum_{p=2}^{N}b_{p}>0.

Rearranging the above inequality yields the claim.
∎

as a

4.1
Lead Matrix Explicit Representation

In this section, we derive the explicit formula of the lead matrix

and

defined at the beginning of the chapter.

Theorem 4.2

(Cyclic OU Process Lead Matrix Explicit Formula)
.

The lead matrix
\mathbf{Q}
of the cyclic OU process is explicitly

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m,n=1}^{N}\frac{\sum_{p=2}^{N}b_{p}(\omega_{m}^{p-1}-\omega_{n}^{p-1})}{2b_{1}+\sum_{q=2}^{N}b_{q}(\omega_{m}^{q-1}+\omega_{n}^{q-1})}\ \mathbf{w}_{m}\ \mathbf{w}_{N-m}^{\text{T}}\ \mathbf{D}\ \mathbf{w}_{N-n}\ \mathbf{w}_{n}^{\text{T}}.

(4.4)

Proof.

First, we explicitly compute the stationary covariance matrix
\mathbf{S}
of the OU process in (
3.5
). By
Theorem
2.3
, the Green’s Function
\mathbf{G}(t),
defined in (
3.4
), is explicitly

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{N}e^{-\mu_{n}t}\ \mathbf{w}_{n}\ \mathbf{w}_{N-n}^{\text{T}}.

Substituting this expression for
\mathbf{G}(t)
into the integrand of (
3.5
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{\infty}\sum_{m,n=1}^{N}e^{-\mu_{m}t}\ \mathbf{w}_{m}\ \mathbf{w}_{N-m}^{\text{T}}\ \mathbf{D}\ \left(e^{-\mu_{n}t}\ \mathbf{w}_{n}\ \mathbf{w}_{N-n}^{\text{T}}\right)^{\text{T}}\ dt

\displaystyle=2\sum_{m,n=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-t(\mu_{m}+\mu_{n})}\ \mathbf{w}_{m}\ \mathbf{w}_{N-m}^{\text{T}}\ \mathbf{D}\ \mathbf{w}_{N-n}\ \mathbf{w}_{n}^{\text{T}}\ dt

\displaystyle=2\sum_{m,n=1}^{N}\frac{\mathbf{w}_{m}\ \mathbf{w}_{N-m}^{\text{T}}\ \mathbf{D}\ \mathbf{w}_{N-n}\ \mathbf{w}_{n}^{\text{T}}}{\mu_{m}+\mu_{n}}.

Now, upon substituting this explicit representation of
\mathbf{S}
into the lead matrix definition in (
3.40
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{B}\left(\sum_{m,n=1}^{N}\frac{\mathbf{w}_{m}\ \mathbf{w}_{N-m}^{\text{T}}\ \mathbf{D}\ \mathbf{w}_{N-n}\ \mathbf{w}_{n}^{\text{T}}}{\mu_{m}+\mu_{n}}\right)-\left(\sum_{m,n=1}^{N}\frac{\mathbf{w}_{m}\ \mathbf{w}_{N-m}^{\text{T}}\ \mathbf{D}\ \mathbf{w}_{N-n}\ \mathbf{w}_{n}^{\text{T}}}{\mu_{m}+\mu_{n}}\right)\mathbf{B}^{\text{T}}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{m,n=1}^{N}\frac{\mu_{m}\mathbf{w}_{m}\ \mathbf{w}_{N-m}^{\text{T}}\ \mathbf{D}\ \mathbf{w}_{N-n}\ \mathbf{w}_{n}^{\text{T}}}{\mu_{m}+\mu_{n}}\right)-\left(\sum_{m,n=1}^{N}\frac{\mathbf{w}_{m}\ \mathbf{w}_{N-m}^{\text{T}}\ \mathbf{D}\ \mathbf{w}_{N-n}\ (\mathbf{B}\ \mathbf{w}_{n})^{\text{T}}}{\mu_{m}+\mu_{n}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m,n=1}^{N}\frac{\mu_{m}-\mu_{n}}{\mu_{m}+\mu_{n}}\ \mathbf{w}_{m}\ \mathbf{w}_{N-m}^{\text{T}}\ \mathbf{D}\ \mathbf{w}_{N-n}\ \mathbf{w}_{n}^{\text{T}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m,n=1}^{N}\frac{\sum_{p=1}^{N}b_{p}(\omega_{m}^{p-1}-\omega_{n}^{p-1})}{\sum_{q=1}^{N}b_{q}(\omega_{m}^{q-1}+\omega_{n}^{q-1})}\ \mathbf{w}_{m}\ \mathbf{w}_{N-m}^{\text{T}}\ \mathbf{D}\ \mathbf{w}_{N-n}\ \mathbf{w}_{n}^{\text{T}}.

\displaystyle=\sum_{m,n=1}^{N}\frac{\sum_{p=2}^{N}b_{p}(\omega_{m}^{p-1}-\omega_{n}^{p-1})}{2b_{1}+\sum_{q=2}^{N}b_{q}(\omega_{m}^{q-1}+\omega_{n}^{q-1})}\ \mathbf{w}_{m}\ \mathbf{w}_{N-m}^{\text{T}}\ \mathbf{D}\ \mathbf{w}_{N-n}\ \mathbf{w}_{n}^{\text{T}}.

This completes the proof.

Theorem 4.3

\displaystyle=\frac{d_{s}b_{p}}{N}\sum_{m,n=1}^{N}\frac{(\omega_{m}^{p-1}-\omega_{n}^{p-1})\ \omega_{m+n}^{1-s}}{2\epsilon+b_{p}(\omega_{m}^{p-1}+\omega_{n}^{p-1}-2)}\ \mathbf{w}_{m}\mathbf{w}_{n}^{\text{T}}.

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}(\epsilon)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m,n=1}^{N}\frac{b_{p}(\omega_{m}^{p-1}-\omega_{n}^{p-1})}{2\epsilon+b_{p}(\omega_{m}^{p-1}+\omega_{n}^{p-1}-2)}\ \mathbf{w}_{m}\ \mathbf{w}_{N-m}^{\text{T}}\ \mathbf{D}\ \mathbf{w}_{N-n}\ \mathbf{w}_{n}^{\text{T}}

Theorem 4.4

Upon dividing the numerator and denominator of the summand in (

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}(\epsilon)

0&\text{else}\end{cases}.

\displaystyle Q_{j,k}(\epsilon)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{d_{s}b_{p}}{2\epsilon}&j\equiv s+1-p\mod N\ ,\ k\equiv s\mod N\\

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\begin{cases}\frac{d_{s}b_{p}}{2\epsilon}&k\equiv s+1-p\mod N\ ,\ j\equiv s\mod N\\

\displaystyle j

\displaystyle\equiv s+1-p\mod N\ ,\ k\equiv s\mod N

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{Q}(\epsilon)-\mathbf{A}(\epsilon)\|_{F}

to obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}(\epsilon)

\displaystyle=\frac{d_{s}b_{p}}{N}\sum_{m,n=1}^{N}\frac{\frac{\left(\omega_{m}^{p-1}-\omega_{n}^{p-1}\right)\ \omega_{m+n}^{1-s}}{b_{p}\left(\omega_{m}^{p-1}+\omega_{n}^{p-1}-2\right)}}{1+\frac{2\epsilon}{b_{p}\left(\omega_{m}^{p-1}+\omega_{n}^{p-1}-2\right)}}\ \mathbf{w}_{m}\mathbf{w}_{n}^{\text{T}}

\displaystyle=\frac{d_{s}}{N}\sum_{m,n=1}^{N}\frac{\frac{\left(\omega_{m}^{p-1}-\omega_{n}^{p-1}\right)\ \omega_{m+n}^{1-s}}{\omega_{m}^{p-1}+\omega_{n}^{p-1}-2}}{1+\frac{2\epsilon}{b_{p}\left(\omega_{m}^{p-1}+\omega_{n}^{p-1}-2\right)}}\ \mathbf{w}_{m}\mathbf{w}_{n}^{\text{T}}.

(4.21)

Suppose

\displaystyle\left|\frac{2\epsilon}{b_{p}\left(\omega_{m}^{p-1}+\omega_{n}^{p-1}-2\right)}\right|<1,

for all
1\leq m,n\leq N.
Then, we can rewrite (
4.21
) as a geometric series. We have

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}(\epsilon)

\displaystyle=\frac{d_{s}}{N}\sum_{m,n=1}^{N}\frac{\left(\omega_{m}^{p-1}-\omega_{n}^{p-1}\right)\ \omega_{m+n}^{1-s}}{\omega_{m}^{p-1}+\omega_{n}^{p-1}-2}\ \sum_{r\geq 0}\left(-\frac{2\epsilon}{b_{p}\ (\omega_{m}^{p-1}+\omega_{n}^{p-1}-2)}\right)^{r}\ \mathbf{w}_{m}\mathbf{w}_{n}^{\text{T}}

\displaystyle=\frac{d_{s}}{N}\sum_{r\geq 0}\sum_{m,n=1}^{N}\left(\frac{\left(\omega_{m}^{p-1}-\omega_{n}^{p-1}\right)\ \omega_{m+n}^{1-s}}{\omega_{m}^{p-1}+\omega_{n}^{p-1}-2}\right)\ \left(-\frac{2\epsilon}{b_{p}(\omega_{m}^{p-1}+\omega_{n}^{p-1}-2)}\right)^{r}\ \mathbf{w}_{m}\mathbf{w}_{n}^{\text{T}}

(4.22)

\displaystyle=\frac{d_{s}}{N}\sum_{m,n=1}^{N}\left(\frac{\left(\omega_{m}^{p-1}-\omega_{n}^{p-1}\right)\ \omega_{m+n}^{1-s}}{\omega_{m}^{p-1}+\omega_{n}^{p-1}-2}\right)\ \mathbf{w}_{m}\mathbf{w}_{n}^{\text{T}}+O(\epsilon),

(4.23)

in which we obtained (
4.23
) by extracting the term
r=0
in the outer sum of (
4.22
) and replacing the sum over all
r>0
with
O(\epsilon).

Now, we need to justify defining the summand in the double sum of (
4.20
) to be
0
when
m=n=N.
Suppose
1\leq m,n\leq N
are two indices satisfying

\displaystyle\omega_{m}^{p-1}+\omega_{n}^{p-1}=2.

(4.24)

Upon taking the moduli of both sides and using the triangle inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle 2

\displaystyle=|\omega_{m}^{p-1}+\omega_{n}^{p-1}|

\displaystyle\leq|\omega_{m}^{p-1}|+|\omega_{n}^{p-1}|

\displaystyle=1+1=2.

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle|\omega_{m}^{p-1}+\omega_{n}^{p-1}|=|\omega_{m}^{p-1}|+|\omega_{n}^{p-1}|.

(4.25)

Recall if
z,w\in\mathbb{C}
satisfy
|z+w|=|z|+|w|
i.e.
z,w
are such that equality is obtained in the triangle inequality for complex numbers, then either one of
z,w
must be zero or
\text{Arg}(z)=\text{Arg}(w)
(see
[
36
]
). Since
\omega_{m}^{p-1},\omega_{n}^{p-1}
are both nonzero, (
4.25
) implies
\text{Arg}(\omega_{m}^{p-1})=\text{Arg}(\omega_{n}^{p-1})
i.e.
\frac{2\pi m(p-1)}{N}=\frac{2\pi n(p-1)}{N},
and so
m=n.
Now, substituting
m=n
into (
4.24
), we obtain
\omega_{m}^{p-1}=1,
which implies

m(p-1)\equiv 0\mod N.

Because
\gcd(p-1,N)=1
divides
0,
we see the congruence equation has a solution for
m.
In particular, by
[
37
, Section 2.6]
, we have

\displaystyle m

\displaystyle=N(k+1)

(4.26)

for
k\in\mathbb{Z}.
But since
1\leq m\leq N,
this forces
m=N.
This shows (
4.21
) implies
m=n=N.
Finally, note that

\displaystyle\frac{\omega_{m}^{p-1}-\omega_{m}^{p-1}}{\omega_{m}^{p-1}+\omega_{m}^{p-1}-2}

\displaystyle=0

if
m<N.
But taking the limit of the left hand side as
m\rightarrow N,
we obtain via L’Hospital’s rule:

\displaystyle\lim_{m\rightarrow N}\frac{\omega_{m}^{p-1}-\omega_{m}^{p-1}}{\omega_{m}^{p-1}+\omega_{m}^{p-1}-2}

\displaystyle=\lim_{m\rightarrow N}\frac{\frac{2\pi i(p-1)}{N}\left(\omega_{m}^{p-1}-\omega_{m}^{p-1}\right)}{\frac{2\pi i(p-1)}{N}\left(\omega_{m}^{p-1}+\omega_{m}^{p-1}\right)}=0.

(4.27)

This completes the proof.
∎

At the current time of writing, we are not aware of any closed formulas for the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the matrix defined on the right hand side of (

Theorem 4.7

Upon expanding the expression

4.3.1

We investigate the spectrum of
\mathbf{A},
whose
(j,k)
-th entry is defined in (
4.29

N
. To make things suitable for future calculations, we will consider the Hermitian matrix
i\mathbf{A}
in place of the original
\mathbf{A}.
Note that the eigenvalues of
i\mathbf{A}
are the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{A}
multiplied by
i.
However, the eigenvectors of
i\mathbf{A}
are the same as those of
\mathbf{A}.
Furthermore, we parameterize
i\mathbf{A}
by its size
N.
In the end, we define
\mathbf{A}_{N}
to be the
N\times N
Hermitian matrix whose
(j,k)
-th entry is the quantity (
4.29
) multiplied by
i
for all
1\leq j,k\leq N.
Throughout, we let

be the

-th largest eigenvalue of
\mathbf{A}_{N}
and
\left\{\mathbf{v}_{n}(\mathbf{A}_{N})\right\}_{n=1}^{N}
be the orthonormal basis of eigenvectors such that
\mathbf{v}_{n}(\mathbf{A}_{N})
is the associated unit eigenvector of
\mathbf{A}_{N}
with

Finally, we denote

Recall the

We concentrate on the largest eigenvalue of
\mathbf{A}_{N}
for large

By Gershgorin’s Circle Theorem
[

Conjecture 4.1

(Gershgorin Radii Upper Bound)

The Gershgorin radii of

\displaystyle\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}R_{N,N-j}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{\binom{2j}{j}}{2^{2j-1}}&j>0\\
1&j=0\end{cases}.

To see this conjecture holds for

we evaluate

\displaystyle R_{N,N-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{N}|A_{N,N-1,k}|

(4.45)

(Asymptotic Binomial Matrix Largest Eigenvalue Convergence)
.

The sequence of largest eigenvalues
\left\{\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A}_{N})\right\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
converges.

Proof.

To show convergence, we show that the sequence
\left\{\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A}_{N})\right\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}

First, we show the sequence is increasing. By induction on

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}0&\mathbf{a}_{N}^{*}\\
\mathbf{a}_{N}&\mathbf{A}_{N-1}\end{bmatrix},

where
\mathbf{a}_{N}\in\mathbb{C}^{N-1}
is the first column of
\mathbf{A}_{N}
after excluding its first component
0
. Notice that
\mathbf{A}_{N-1}
is the first principal minor of
\mathbf{A}_{N},
that is, we obtain
\mathbf{A}_{N-1}

\mathbf{A}_{N}.
By the Cauchy Interlacing Theorem
[

]
, the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{A}_{N-1}

\mathbf{A}_{N},
which specifically means

\displaystyle\lambda_{N}(\mathbf{A}_{N})\leq\lambda_{N-1}(\mathbf{A}_{N-1})\leq\lambda_{N-1}(\mathbf{A}_{N})\leq\dots\ \leq\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A}_{N-1})\leq\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A}_{N})

(4.47)

The above inequality directly implies
\left\{\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A}_{N})\right\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}

Next, we show that
\left\{\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A}_{N})\right\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}

the
(N-j)
-th Gershgorin disk radius of

Assuming

4.1
holds, for

\displaystyle\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}R_{N,N-j}

\displaystyle=\frac{\binom{2j}{j}}{2^{2j-1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{2j}{j}\binom{2(j-1)}{j-1}}{2^{2j-1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\binom{2(j-1)}{j-1}}{2^{2j-2}}

\displaystyle<\frac{\binom{2(j-1)}{j-1}}{2^{2(j-1)-1}}=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}R_{N,N-(j-1)}.

Hence, as
N\rightarrow\infty,
we see the Gershgorin radii of
\mathbf{A}_{N}
are bounded above by
1.
This implies the sequence
\left\{\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A}_{N})\right\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded above by
1.

∎

4.3.2
Structure of Leading Eigenvector

For large
N,
we now investigate the structure of
\mathbf{v}_{1}(\mathbf{A}_{N}),
the leading eigenvector of
\mathbf{A}_{N},
assuming all the previous conjectures we stated pertaining to the spectrum of
\mathbf{A}_{N}
hold.

First, we describe the behavior of
|v_{1,n}(\mathbf{A}_{N})|,
the modulus of the
n
-th component
\mathbf{v}_{1}(\mathbf{A}_{N}).

Theorem 4.9

(Asymptotic Binomial Matrix Leading Eigenvector Component Moduli Structure)
.

For each
1\leq n<N,
we have

\displaystyle|v_{1,n}(\mathbf{A}_{N})|<|v_{1,n+1}(\mathbf{A}_{N})|.

(4.50)

Moreover, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}v_{1,1}(\mathbf{A}_{N})

\displaystyle=0.

(4.51)

Proof.

We first prove the first statement. We utilize the
eigenvalue-eigenvector identity

[
40
]
which states

\displaystyle\left|v_{1,j}(\mathbf{A}_{N})\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\prod_{n=1}^{N-1}\left(\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A}_{N})-\lambda_{n}(\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{N,j})\right)}{\prod_{n=2}^{N}\left(\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A}_{N})-\lambda_{n}(\mathbf{A}_{N})\right)}.

(4.52)

Assuming
Conjecture
4.3
holds, we have

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A}_{N})-\lambda_{n}(\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{N,j})

\displaystyle<\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A}_{N})-\lambda_{n}(\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{N,j+1}).

This means we can bound the numerator on the right hand side of (
4.52
) in the following way:

\displaystyle\left|v_{1,j}(\mathbf{A}_{N})\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\prod_{n=1}^{N-1}\left(\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A}_{N})-\lambda_{n}(\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{N,j})\right)}{\prod_{n=2}^{N}\left(\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A}_{N})-\lambda_{n}(\mathbf{A}_{N})\right)}

\displaystyle<\frac{\prod_{n=1}^{N-1}\left(\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A}_{N})-\lambda_{n}(\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{N,j+1})\right)}{\prod_{n=2}^{N}\left(\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A}_{N})-\lambda_{n}(\mathbf{A}_{N})\right)}=\left|v_{1,j+1}(\mathbf{A}_{N})\right|^{2},

which proves the first statement.

Now, we prove the second statement, recall that
\lambda_{n}(\mathbf{A}_{N})\leq\lambda_{n}(\mathbf{A}_{N-1})
by the Cauchy Interlacing inequality. We have

\displaystyle|v_{1,1}(\mathbf{A}_{N})|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\prod_{n=1}^{N-1}\left(\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A}_{N})-\lambda_{n}(\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{N,1})\right)}{\prod_{n=2}^{N}\left(\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A}_{N})-\lambda_{n}(\mathbf{A}_{N})\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\prod_{n=1}^{N-1}\left(\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A}_{N})-\lambda_{n}(\mathbf{A}_{N-1})\right)}{\prod_{n=2}^{N}\left(\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A}_{N})-\lambda_{n}(\mathbf{A}_{N})\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{(\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A}_{N})-\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A}_{N-1}))\ \prod_{n=2}^{N-1}\left(\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A}_{N})-\lambda_{n}(\mathbf{A}_{N-1})\right)}{\prod_{n=2}^{N}\left(\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A}_{N})-\lambda_{n}(\mathbf{A}_{N})\right)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A}_{N})-\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A}_{N-1}))\ \prod_{n=2}^{N-1}\left(\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A}_{N})-\lambda_{n}(\mathbf{A}_{N})\right)}{\prod_{n=2}^{N}\left(\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A}_{N})-\lambda_{n}(\mathbf{A}_{N})\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A}_{N})-\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A}_{N-1})}{\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A}_{N})-\lambda_{N}(\mathbf{A}_{N})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A}_{N})-\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A}_{N-1})}{2\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A}_{N})},

which approaches
0
as

by
Theorem
4.8
. This proves the second statement.
∎

We interpret the previous theorem. The previous theorem states for large
N
, the component moduli
\mathbf{v}_{1}(\mathbf{A}_{N})
decrease from right to left. We interpret this in the context of our signal propagation model. As the signal propagates throughout the network, the left most sensors are less receptive to the signal than the right most sensors.

Proof.

and

for all

into the general Cyclic OU lead matrix formula (
4.4

\mathbb{C}^{N},

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}(\epsilon)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m,n=1}^{N}\frac{b_{p}(\omega_{m}^{p-1}-\omega_{n}^{p-1})}{2\epsilon+b_{p}(\omega_{m}^{p-1}+\omega_{n}^{p-1}-2)}\ \mathbf{w}_{m}\ \mathbf{w}_{N-m}^{\text{T}}\mathbf{w}_{N-n}\mathbf{w}_{n}^{\text{T}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{b_{p}(\omega_{N-n}^{p-1}-\omega_{n}^{p-1})}{2\epsilon+b_{p}(\omega_{N-n}^{p-1}+\omega_{n}^{p-1}-2)}\ \mathbf{w}_{N-n}\mathbf{w}_{n}^{\text{T}}

\displaystyle\sin(\theta)

\displaystyle\cos(\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{i\theta}+e^{-i\theta}}{2}.

Theorem 4.15

(Cyclic OU Process One Nonzero Propagation Coefficient and Independent and Identical Noise in All Sensors Second Regime Leading Eigenvector Explicit Formula)
.

Consider the lead matrix
\mathbf{Q}(\epsilon)
defined in (
4.66
). Then, as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0,
the leading eigenvector of
\mathbf{Q}(\epsilon)
is
\mathbf{w}_{q},
where
1\leq q\leq N
satisfies
q(p-1)\equiv 1\mod N.

Proof.

First, we need to determine the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{Q}(\epsilon).
In order to do this, we need to compute the first row of
\mathbf{Q}(\epsilon).
Since
\mathbf{Q}(\epsilon)
is skew-symmetric, its first row is the opposite of its first column. To obtain its first column, we compute the product of
\mathbf{Q}(\epsilon)
and
\mathbf{e}_{1},
where
\mathbf{e}_{1}
is the first standard basis vector in
\mathbb{C}^{N}.
This means the first row of
\mathbf{Q}(\epsilon)
is

\displaystyle-\mathbf{Q}(\epsilon)\ \mathbf{e}_{1}

\displaystyle=i\ \sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{b_{p}\sin\left(\frac{2\pi n(p-1)}{N}\right)}{\epsilon-2b_{p}\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi n(p-1)}{N}\right)}\ \mathbf{w}_{N-n}\mathbf{w}_{n}^{\text{T}}\ \mathbf{e}_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{b_{p}\sin\left(\frac{2\pi n(p-1)}{N}\right)}{\epsilon-2b_{p}\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi n(p-1)}{N}\right)}\ \mathbf{w}_{N-n}.

(4.68)

As a result, for each
1\leq q\leq N,
each eigenvalue of
\mathbf{Q}(\epsilon)
is of the form

\displaystyle\widetilde{\lambda_{q}}(\epsilon)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{N}\ \mathbf{w}^{\text{T}}_{q}\left(\frac{i}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{b_{p}\sin\left(\frac{2\pi n(p-1)}{N}\right)}{\epsilon-2b_{p}\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi n(p-1)}{N}\right)}\ \mathbf{w}_{N-n}\right)

\displaystyle=i\ \sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{b_{p}\sin\left(\frac{2\pi n(p-1)}{N}\right)}{\epsilon-2b_{p}\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi n(p-1)}{N}\right)}\mathbf{w}_{q}^{\text{T}}\ \mathbf{w}_{N-n}

\displaystyle=i\ \sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{b_{p}\sin\left(\frac{2\pi n(p-1)}{N}\right)}{\epsilon-2b_{p}\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi n(p-1)}{N}\right)}\delta_{N-q,N-n}

\displaystyle=\frac{b_{p}\sin\left(\frac{2\pi q(p-1)}{N}\right)}{\epsilon-2b_{p}\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi q(p-1)}{N}\right)}\ i.

Note that

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\left|\widetilde{\lambda_{q}}(\epsilon)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{b_{p}\sin\left(\frac{2\pi q(p-1)}{N}\right)}{-2b_{p}\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi q(p-1)}{N}\right)}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{\sin\left(\frac{\pi q(p-1)}{N}\right)\cos\left(\frac{\pi q(p-1)}{N}\right)}{\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi q(p-1)}{N}\right)}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\cot\left(\frac{\pi q(p-1)}{N}\right)\right|.

(4.69)

We seek the index
q
maximizing
\left|\cot\left(\frac{\pi q(p-1)}{N}\right)\right|.
Note that the map
\theta\mapsto|\cot(\theta)|
is strictly decreasing on
(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
and strictly increasing on
\left(\frac{\pi}{2},\pi\right).
Since
\cot(\theta)=\cot\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-\theta\right)
for all
\theta\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2}),
the map
\theta\mapsto|\cot(\theta)|
restricted to the interval
(0,\pi)
is symmetric about
\frac{\pi}{2}.
Finally, note that
\theta\mapsto|\cot(\theta)|
is
\pi
-periodic. The integers
u\in\mathbb{Z}
maximizing
\left|\cot(\frac{u\pi}{N})\right|
specifically in the domain
(0,\pi)
are
u=1
and
u=N-1.
By periodicity, the integer
u\in\mathbb{Z}
maximizing
\left|\cot(\frac{u\pi}{N})\right|
on its entire domain satisfies
u\equiv\pm 1\mod N.

This means an index
1\leq q\leq N
that maximizes the eigenvalue limit (
4.66
) must satisfy either the congruence equation
q(p-1)\equiv 1\mod N
or the congruence equation
q(p-1)\equiv-1\mod N.
Both congruence equations have integer solutions because
\gcd(p-1,N),
which is equal to
1
by assumption, divides
\pm 1.

Let
1\leq q\leq N
be such that
q(p-1)\equiv 1\mod N.
Then, the associated eigenvector of the eigenvalue
\widetilde{\lambda}_{q}(\epsilon)
is
\mathbf{w}_{q},
which is the leading eigenvector of
\mathbf{Q}(\epsilon).

∎

Knowing the leading eigenvector of
\mathbf{Q}(\epsilon)
under the second regime, we investigate whether the structure of the leading eigenvector recovers the structure of the cyclic sensor network.

Theorem 4.16

(Cyclic OU Process One Nonzero Propagation Coefficient and Independent and Identical Noise in All Sensors Second Regime Leading Eigenvector Structure)
.

Let
\mathbf{w}_{q}
be the leading eigenvector of
\mathbf{Q}(\epsilon).
Then, the permutation
\sigma\in S_{N}
representing the cyclic order of the component phases
\mathbf{w}_{q}
is defined by
\sigma(n)\equiv(N-(n-1)(p-1))\mod N.

Proof.

First, we note that
\gcd(q,N)=1.
To see this, recall the previous theorem states that
q(p-1)\equiv 1\mod N.
By definition, this means

q(p-1)-Nk=1

for some integer
k.
Hence, we can write
1
as an integer linear combination of the numbers
q
and
N,
which implies
\gcd(q,N)=1.

Let
\sigma\in S_{N}
be the permutation such that

\displaystyle q(\sigma(n)-1)

\displaystyle=n-1\mod N.

(4.70)

for each
1\leq n\leq N.
Firstly, we note
\sigma
is well-defined. For each
1\leq n\leq N,
because
\gcd(q,N)=1
divides
n-1,
there is a unique solution to the congruence equation (
4.70
) for
\sigma(n).

For each
1\leq n<N,
we have

\displaystyle q(\sigma(n+1)-\sigma(n))

\displaystyle\equiv(n+1-n)\mod N

\displaystyle\equiv 1\mod N.

Therefore, we deduce

\displaystyle q(\sigma(n+1)-\sigma(n))\equiv q(p-1)\mod N.

Because
\gcd(q,N)=1,
we may divide both sides of the congruence equation by
q
to deduce that

\displaystyle\sigma(n+1)-\sigma(n)

\displaystyle\equiv p-1\mod N.

(4.71)

Finally, we claim
\sigma
is a cyclic order permutation corresponding to the component principal arguments of the eigenvector
\mathbf{w}_{q}.
Recall the
n
-th component of
\mathbf{w}_{q}
is explicitly

\displaystyle w_{q,n}

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega_{q}^{n-1}}{\sqrt{N}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega_{1}^{q(n-1)}}{\sqrt{N}}.

By (
4.70
), we have

\displaystyle w_{q,\sigma(n)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega_{1}^{q(\sigma(n)-1)}}{\sqrt{N}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega_{1}^{n-1}}{\sqrt{N}},

which has principal argument
\frac{2\pi(n-1)}{N}\in[0,2\pi).
Therefore, our permutation
\sigma
ensures the components of
\mathbf{w}_{q}
are sorted in increasing order by their corresponding principal arguments. Thus,
\sigma
is a valid cyclic order permutation.
∎

We interpret the result of the previous theorem. If we inject independent and identical noise into all sensors, then under the second regime, the structure of the leading eigenvector of
\mathbf{Q}(\epsilon)
enables us to recover the expected network structure induced by the friction matrix
\mathbf{B}(\epsilon).
In particular, the cyclic order of the phases of the leading eigenvector matches the expected cyclic order of the sensors receiving the signal in our propagation model.

Chapter 6
Conclusion

In this thesis, we posed very general questions pertaining to the lead lag dynamics amongst the components of an
N
-dimensional signal. Firstly, if two signals evolve similarly throughout time, which signal leads and which signal follows ? Secondly, if all signals evolve similarly throughout time, what is the order in which such signals evolve ?

We discussed Cyclicity Analysis, an established way of answering the aforementioned two questions for cyclic signals, which are repeating, yet aperiodic, signals. Cyclicity Analysis is different from many standard tools in that it is time reparameterization invariant; the results of the analysis do not depend on how one observes the signals throughout time. It involves two parts. The first part is to construct an
N\times N
lead matrix, in which the sign of the
(m,n)
-th entry heuristically captures the pairwise leader follower relationship between the
m
-th and
n
-th component signals. It is a specific construction based on iterated path integrals, which are the functionals of the signal
\mathbf{x}
that are time reparameterization invariant. The second part of Cyclicity Analysis is to assume a model in which the component signals of
\mathbf{x}
trace some periodic signal up to time reparameterization, scaling constants, and time shifts. Under this model, one determines the order in which the component signals evolve throughout time, which is the cyclic order of those time shifts. Under certain baseline situations, the leading eigenvector of the lead matrix can approximately recover the order in which the component signals of
\mathbf{x}
evolve throughout time. More specifically, the cyclic order of the eigenvector component phases reflects the cyclic order of the offsets.

In this thesis, we investigated Cyclicity Analysis in a novel setting. We considered an
N
-dimensional Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) stochastic process with model parameters
\mathbf{B}
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma},
in which
\mathbf{B}
is a friction matrix and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
is a volatility matrix. We considered a signal propagation model governed by the cyclic OU process, in which the model parameter
\mathbf{B}
is a circulant friction matrix. In this model, there is a signal propagating throughout a network of
N
sensors. The matrix
\mathbf{B}
represents the cyclic network structure of the sensors, while the diffusion matrix
\mathbf{D}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\text{T}}/2
is the covariance matrix whose entries are pairwise covariances of noises injected into any two sensors.

To make Cyclicity Analysis suitable for analyzing the OU process, we defined an auxiliary
N\times N
lead process, a matrix-valued stochastic process in which each random matrix in the collection represents a lead matrix corresponding to a realization of the OU process. The first main result of the thesis is that the lead process obeys a strong law of large numbers identity: its time average converges almost surely to a skew-symmetric matrix
\mathbf{Q}
.

Then, with different choices of cyclic OU model parameters
\mathbf{B}
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma},
we investigated whether Cyclicity Analysis could enable us to recover the network structure induced by
\mathbf{B}
under our signal propagation model. More specifically, we analyzed whether the structure of the leading eigenvector of
\mathbf{Q}
could enable us to recover the network structure induced by
\mathbf{B}.

For our analysis, we assumed the ground truth network structure is such that every sensor receives the propagating signal from exactly one sensor to its right. In addition, we assumed independent and identical noise is injected into multiple sensors, which corresponds to the situation where
\mathbf{D}
is a diagonal diffusion matrix whose nonzero diagonal entries are equal.

With the imposed assumptions on
\mathbf{B}
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma},
our experimental results showed that Cyclicity Analysis was in fact able to partially recover the structure of the network. For example, in the situation where we inject noise only into the last node, the cyclic order of the component phases of the leading eigenvector of
\mathbf{Q}
partially reflected the expected cyclic order of the sensors receiving the signal in our model. As we increase the number of sensors receiving noise, we observed the cyclic order of the component phases approximately match the expected cyclic order of the sensors. In the ultimate case identical noise is injected into all sensors, the cyclic orders perfectly match.

There is future work that can be done. For example, what if we remove the assumption that
\mathbf{D}
has equal positive diagonal entries ? This would correspond to a situation where independent, but not identical, noise is injected into multiple sensors. What if
\mathbf{B}
represents a network structure such that each sensor does not receive the signal from only one other sensor, but multiple sensors ? Does the leading eigenvector of the lead matrix
\mathbf{Q}
capture those phenomena ?
