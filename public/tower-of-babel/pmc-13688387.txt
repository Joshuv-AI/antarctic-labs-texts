Exploring the Acceptability of a Specialist Obesity-Treatment Intervention (Fast Track to Health) for Adolescents: A Mixed-Method Study
Authors: Sarah Lang, Eve T. House, Natalie B. Lister, Hiba Jebeile, Johanna Hoare, Kaitlin Day, Megan L. Gow, Mary-Kate Inkster, Justin Brown, Helen Truby, on behalf of the Fast Track to Health Study team
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFast Track to Health was a 12-month intervention for adolescents with obesity and associated complications. Adolescents followed a very low-energy diet and intermittent energy restriction (IER) or continuous energy restriction (CER). This study explored adolescents’, parents’, and dietitians’ perspectives of the intervention.MethodsA convergent parallel mixed-methods evaluation was conducted. Acceptability questionnaires were collected from adolescents (week 16: n = 113, week 52: n = 88) and parents (week 16: n = 108, week 52: n = 80). Semi-structured interviews were conducted with adolescents (n = 14), parents (n = 11), and dietitians (n = 6) at week 52. Interview transcripts were thematically analysed.ResultsAdolescents rated enjoyability (score 0–100) at week 16 (mean [SD] IER: 63.5 [21.1] vs. CER: 65.9 [20.2], p = 0.550) and week 52 (IER: 67.2 [18.1] vs. CER: 61.0 [23.4], p = 0.198), showing no significant difference between groups. Most parents reported benefits at week 16 (IER: 86% vs. CER: 93%, p = 0.263) and week 52 (IER: 79% vs. CER: 83%, p = 0.717). Adolescents positively rated their experience with dietitians. Qualitative feedback emphasised the impact of clear dietary advice, easy and frequent access to support, continuity of care, and family engagement.ConclusionsThis evaluation offers insights into a specialised dietary intervention for adolescents. Findings can inform future obesity treatments for adolescents in outpatient tertiary healthcare settings.

Introduction

Adolescent obesity remains a global healthcare priority with obesity during childhood and adolescence linked with poorer physical and mental health outcomes across the life course [1, 2]. Clinical practice guidelines for paediatric obesity recommend a multicomponent lifestyle intervention, including diet, physical activity, and behaviour modification, as first-line treatment [3]. Guidelines recommend intensive dietary approaches to promote weight loss for adolescents with severe obesity and/or obesity-related comorbidities [3]. Optimising diet and dietary change is also recommended as supportive care for individuals using obesity treatment medications (including glucagon-like peptide 1 receptor agonists) [4]. Fast Track to Health (Fast Track) was a dietitian-led multidisciplinary obesity treatment intervention (informed by prior research [5–7]) developed to support weight loss in adolescents with obesity and related complications in tertiary care settings in Australia. In brief, Fast Track assessed if intermittent energy restriction (IER) (intervention) resulted in a lower BMI z-score after 52 weeks compared with continuous energy restriction (CER) (standard care) [8]. The long-term objective was to offer adolescents and families seeking obesity treatment a range of effective and acceptable dietary options. A detailed protocol [8], primary outcomes [9], changes in eating disorder risk scores and symptoms of depression [10], and quantitative data on the efficacy, safety, and acceptability of the 4-week very low-energy diet (VLED) [11] phase have been published. The primary outcomes of the Fast Track trial noted that both the IER and CER resulted in reduced BMI z-score with no significant differences in changes in body composition or cardiometabolic outcomes between groups [9].

Translation of research interventions into clinical practice requires consideration of intervention acceptability during development and evaluation, with ongoing review of acceptability over time [12]. Intervention acceptability can influence participants’ engagement with the intervention and, in turn, affect fidelity, feasibility, scalability across healthcare settings, and longer term sustainability [12]. Mixed-methods evaluation of intervention acceptability enables the global collection of quantitative data using low-intensity questionnaires alongside in-depth qualitative exploration of participant experiences and sentiments towards the intervention. The theoretical framework of acceptability is a framework encapsulating seven factors known to influence the acceptability of healthcare interventions, including affective attitude, ethicality, intervention coherence, perceived effectiveness, opportunity costs, burden, and self-efficacy [13]. This mixed-method study uses the theoretical framework of acceptability to explore adolescents’, parents’, and the dietitians’ delivering the intervention perspectives on the acceptability of Fast Track.

Methods

A mixed-method convergent parallel evaluation study design was used (online suppl. Fig. S1). The epistemological foundation is pragmatic, accepting that pragmatic inquiry seeks to better understand a problem to generate practical, real-world solutions. Knowledge is considered unique to each individual based on social experience and context [14, 15].

Refer to Lister et al. [8] for the full study protocol. The 12-month trial included a 4-week VLED followed by randomised interventions, CER or IER, and a transition to a maintenance plan focused on healthy eating.

Quantitative Acceptability Assessment

All enrolled adolescents and their parents/caregivers were invited to complete a study-specific acceptability survey at weeks 4, 16, and 52. Week 4 results (evaluating the VLED) are reported elsewhere [11]. At weeks 16 and 52, adolescents and their parents indicated their agreement with statements regarding perceptions of the dietary plan, impact on their social/family life, and intervention usefulness. Difficulty and enjoyability were rated on a 100-point Likert scale (0 being least and 100 being most enjoyable/easy). Adolescents completed the Visual Consultation and Relational Empathy (CARE) questionnaire at week 36, with scores 0–50, with 50 being the most positive perception of care [16]. Surveys were distributed online via University of Sydney REDCap [17, 18]. Categorical responses were dichotomised for analysis, with frequencies and percentages reported. Refer to online supplementary Material 1 for further details of quantitative data analysis.

Qualitative Acceptability Assessment

Adolescents, their parents/caregivers, and dietitians were invited to participate in a semi-structured interview after 52 weeks of the intervention. Adolescents and parents/caregivers were purposively recruited using heterogeneous sampling to recruit adolescents of different ages, genders, and randomised to the IER/CER plan. Study dietitians were purposively sampled as key informants. Interviews were conducted (from May 2019 to January 2021) by a female research dietitian and doctoral student (SL) who had no prior relationship with adolescents/parents but an established working relationship with dietitians. Interview questions explored adolescents’, parents’/caregivers’, and dietitians’ perceptions of the trial, experiences implementing dietary changes, and recommendations for improvement (online suppl. Materials 4, 5, and 6). Interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and thematically analysed using the framework method [19, 20]. Items from the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research checklist have been reported (online suppl. Material 3) [21]. Participant quotes are presented using pseudonyms to maintain anonymity. Refer to online supplementary Material 2 for further details on participant recruitment, qualitative study sample size, and data collection; refer to online supplementary Table S1 for an example section of the framework matrix and to online supplementary Table S2 for the finalised coding framework.

Convergence of Qualitative and Quantitative Results

A mixed-method convergent parallel design involves the independent collection and analysis of both qualitative and quantitative data, followed by convergence and comparison of the findings. Following data analysis, key findings were mapped to each construct within the theoretical framework of acceptability [22] to explore factors known to influence the perceived acceptability of healthcare interventions. Refer to online supplementary Table S3 for definitions, detailed mapping of the data to these constructs, and a summary of how the qualitative and quantitative findings (online suppl. Fig. S1) were converged.

Results

Of the 141 participants enrolled and randomised, 97 adolescents completed the 52-week Fast Track study protocol. Acceptability data were available for 113 adolescents at week 16 (of 122 adolescents who completed phase 2; 93% completion rate) and 88 adolescents at week 52 (of 97 who completed phase 3; 91% completion rate). Visual CARE data were available from 68 adolescents at week 36 (of 97 who completed phase 3; 70% completion rate) (online suppl. Fig. S2). Parents completed an acceptability survey at week 16 (n = 108), week 52 (n = 80) (quantitative).There were no significant differences in baseline characteristics between adolescents who completed an acceptability survey and those who did not (online suppl. Table S4).

Adolescents (n = 49) and their parents/caregivers were invited to complete an interview at week 52 (qualitative). Twenty-three families expressed interest in participating in an interview; ten could not be contacted or reported scheduling conflicts. Thirteen families (n = 14 adolescents, n = 11 parents) participated and randomised to both the IER (n = 7 adolescents, 50%) and CER (n = 7 adolescents, 50%). Families completed a joint interview (n = 6 families) or adolescents (n = 7)/parents (n = 5) participated individually. Two parents of interviewed adolescents declined to be interviewed themselves due to scheduling conflicts (n = 1) or hesitancy to be interviewed in English (n = 1). No other caregivers choose to participate (all were parents). Two adolescents were siblings. All adolescents had completed the 12-month study protocol. All dietitians who delivered the intervention (n = 6) participated in an interview. Interview length ranged from 13 to 70 min. Refer to Table 1 for adolescents’/parents’ demographic information. Qualitative analysis resulted in eight themes. Key findings were mapped to and interpreted according to the seven constructs of the theoretical framework of acceptability (Table 2).

Affective Attitude, Intervention Coherence, and Ethicality

Affective attitude relates to participants’ feelings towards the intervention design, intervention coherence explores participants’ understanding of the intervention, and ethicality considers the intervention alignment with family’s belief systems [13]. Quantitative data exploring these constructs found differences in parent or adolescent ratings of meal plan enjoyability at either time point based on dietary intervention group (week 16: parent t(df) = −1.95(91.33), p = 0.055, adolescent t(df) = −0.60(100.93), p = 0.550; week 52: parent t(df) = 1.57(60.47), p = 0.121, adolescent t(df) = 1.30(73.69), p = 0.198) or weight outcomes (week 16: parent t(df) = 0.705(62.417), p = 0.483, adolescent t(df) = 0.256(61.319), p = 0.799; week 52: parent t(df) = −0.694(48.070), p = 0.491, adolescent t(df) = −0.771(58.834), p = 0.443) (Fig. 1, online suppl. Table S5, S6). At both time points, approximately 70% of parents believed their child felt good about the meal plan all or most of the time (online suppl. Fig. S3, online suppl. Table S5). At week 36, visual CARE questionnaire responses indicated adolescents rated their experience with the dietitian positively, with no subgroup differences in CARE ratings (median [IQR] IER: 49(8.0) and CER: 50(5.5) out of a possible maximum score of 50) (Fig. 1). Most parents (93%) reported that family members were supportive of the adolescent in the study, and few reported that the adolescent’s friends were not supportive (online suppl. Fig. S3, online suppl. Table S5). At week 16, parents of adolescents with ≥5% reduction in BMI expressed as a percentage of the 95th percentile (BMI95) at study completion were more likely to report (100%) that family members have been supportive of their child in the study compared to parents of adolescents with <5% reduction in BMI95 (90%) (χ2 = 5.221, p = 0.049). At week 52, no parents of adolescents with ≥5% reduction in BMI95 at study completion reported that friends had not been supportive of the meal plan, compared to 18% parents of adolescents with <5% reduction in BMI95 (χ2 = 9.540, p = 0.005). There were no other subgroup differences in parental perceptions of support from family and friends (online suppl. Fig. S3, online suppl. Table S5, S6).

Theme 1: Experiences of Structured Dietary Recommendations

Adolescents and parents reported valuing the clear structure of the 4-week VLED phase and suggested that the minimal scope for decision-making alleviated pressure to make dietary decisions. Adolescents suggested the convenience/transportability of meal replacements and reduced need for food preparation assisted with adherence. Dietitians reflected that the initial short VLED period helped regulate, change, and foster new healthy eating behaviours, such as eating regularly and increasing vegetable consumption. Nonetheless, some adolescents noted increased hunger. Additional low-carbohydrate, low-energy foods and a meal replacement product after school were reported to help adolescents feel satiated. Some adolescents disliked meal replacement products and experienced flavour/texture fatigue. Dietitians noted that strong food preferences could make the VLED more challenging. However, when transitioning from the VLED, dietitians reported some adolescents hesitated to cease the VLED due to the improved confidence to manage eating behaviours. Some adolescents wanted to continue the VLED for more than 4 weeks due to the perceived ease of the plan, whereas other were ready to cease the VLED or chose to cease the VLED early. Adolescents indicated that the IER or CER plan seemed more achievable and was an easier adjustment after the VLED, but the increased scope for food-related decision-making with the IER and CER could be challenging.

“I didn’t have, you know, much worry about at that point … I knew what I was gonna eat” (Participant 10, CER).

“I’m not going to lie … it was quite tough when we first started it, I was definitely very hungry, it did get easier as your body gets used to it, but compared to the things that I was eating beforehand I definitely found it was like a bit hard” (Participant 14, CER).

After the VLED, adolescents suggested the CER plan was clear, simple, and straightforward, with good food variety. Some adolescents found the flexibility of the CER plan challenging, with too much scope for decision-making. Participants reflected on the importance of tailoring the CER plan to adolescents’ preferences and energy requirements.

“I liked the whole fact that they give you like different options, like there is a bit of variety in how much I can eat that is of low calorie” (Participant 1, CER).

When discussing IER, participants valued the flexibility of non-fasting days alongside the structure of energy-restricted days. However, dietitians felt the IER could be challenging to understand and needed to be tailored to suit each adolescent’s preferences. Adolescents and parents could find adhering to energy recommendations challenging and requested adjustments to suit their activity level/lifestyle.

“It wasn’t too strict, I felt there was flexibility with the non-fasting day” (Participant 11, IER)

“I think he was just sort of always trying a different … configuration of days ... He has sport nearly every day and … the commitment to the fasting really, it was too tricky” (Mother of Participant 12, IER).

Regardless of the dietary plan, adolescents appreciated having a social meal, including a takeaway or preferred meal at home, with friends or family once a week to provide flexibility and encourage achievable goal setting.

Theme 2: Knowing How to Implement Change – The Importance of Practical Recommendations

Adolescents and parents discussed the importance of understanding “how” to practically implement dietary recommendations, with resources, written information, recipes, and meal plans helpful to translate dietary recommendations into practical solutions. Parents recommended that more recipes are provided and suggested tailoring resources for adolescents from culturally diverse backgrounds. Dietitians reported using portions or meal plans to enable adolescents to practically follow advice. Adolescents requested additional structured physical activity recommendations. Interestingly, dietitians indicated that adolescents rarely used the provided fitness trackers. One adolescent suggested that dietitians did not give feedback on fitness trackers, and a greater focus on monitoring activity “would be good.”

“[My son] got really bored with the recipes that I was making … because I kept continually making the same ones I kind of feel like it would’ve been really helpful if there were more recipe ideas” (Mother of Participant 11, IER).

Self-Efficacy

Self-efficacy relates to participants’ motivation and confidence to engage with dietary recommendations. Quantitative data exploring self-efficacy found no differences in parent or adolescent ratings of the ease of following the meal plan between IER/CER groups (week 16: parent −1.08 (94.22), p = 0.285, adolescent t(df) = −0.18 (103.40), p = 0.861; week 52: parent t(df) = 0.75(58.56), p = 0.456, adolescent t(df) = −0.71 (60.94), p = 0.478) (Fig. 2, online suppl. Table S5). At week 16, about three-quarters of parents in both groups reported that their child felt more in control of their eating habits while following the plan (online suppl. Fig. S4, online suppl. Table S5) and more than 90% of parents felt their child would be able to continue to follow their allocated meal plan (Fig. 2, online suppl. Table S5). At week 52, this reduced to 63% and 79%, respectively, with no between-group differences at either timepoint (Fig. 2, online suppl. Table S5).

Theme 3: Motivation and Ownership of Change

Adolescents enrolled in Fast Track to address health concerns, improve general well-being, fitness, self-confidence, and/or weight management, and were motivated to access professional support. In some circumstances, parents encouraged adolescents to participate, which was often coupled with limited readiness to change and poor engagement from adolescents. Therefore, adolescent ownership of dietary changes was often critical for successful change.

“I guess cos I haven’t had the best liver and that was like one of the reasons why I was on it and ... it is going to be beneficial to me if I do it properly” (Participant 1, CER).

“At each visit we went to … they asked, ‘what exercise have you done?’ … Initially, she [my daughter] always made excuses … and then I think it sort of clicked, ‘oh, they’re going to ask me again, I better do something’. I think it sort of started from that … she actually started to enjoy doing things … and then hasn’t looked back since” (Mother of Participant 6, IER).

Observing tangible outcomes contributed to the adolescent’s sense of achievement and motivated adolescents to remain engaged and maintain lifestyle changes. Some adolescents suggested that weight loss was motivating, especially if they had previously struggled with weight loss. Discussing other outcome measures, such as changes or improvements to blood test results or body composition, was often motivating and helped adolescents understand how dietary changes improved their health. Developing healthy routines, planning, self-monitoring, staying busy, and positive distractions (i.e., physical activity) helped maintain motivation and changes. Questionnaires and surveys collected without adolescents being given feedback were considered burdensome.

“I liked the fact that you could see the amount of muscle and the fat I had in my body from like the first DEXA scan to the last one … it made me feel like I achieved something” (Participant 14, CER).

Nonetheless, adolescents’ motivation to implement and maintain changes evolved over the 12-month intervention. Adolescents reported intervention fatigue and decreasing enthusiasm over time. Dietitians indicated that if adolescents had unrealistic expectations or experienced a “plateau” with goals, their motivation to maintain changes might decrease. Dietitians highlighted the importance of managing expectations and setting realistic goals. Adolescents indicated that regular dietetic appointments helped sustain motivation.

“Over the year, like, it kind of like dulled down a bit … I guess I was just more like excited for the program [at the start], like I was going to learn something new and I was just, you know, willing to give it my best shot” (Participant 9, IER).

Theme 4: Adolescents Are Reliant on Their Family to Support Change

Adolescents, parents, and dietitians reflected on the benefits of a whole family approach to change. Parents were particularly influential in enabling dietary change. Adolescents relied on parents to assist with shopping, meal planning and preparation, encouraging healthy behaviours, and reducing the availability of discretionary options within the home. A parent following the diet plan alongside their adolescent was often considered supportive, with adolescents reporting feeling less isolated when a parent followed the same dietary recommendations.

“I did the whole program with [my son] … because I said to him, ‘look if you do this, I’ll do this with you.’ … I said ‘I wouldn’t ask you to do something unless I was prepared to do it as well, so we did it together” (Mother of Participant 11, IER).

“My mum was … very helpful, and she was also eating healthy with me, which I think helped a lot” (Participant 11, IER).

Parents cited the importance of being engaged and adequately educated themselves to ensure they had the necessary skills, knowledge, and confidence to support their adolescent. It was challenging for adolescents when parents made few changes to their own eating or activity behaviours and made few changes to the home environment. Adolescents who ate or cooked separate meals found this difficult to sustain.

“I kind of felt like eating with the rest of the family, eating what the family would be eating, … I just don’t really like chips anymore, ice cream, I’m like, you know, not bothered … I’d just be eating it because I didn’t want to feel left out in the family” (Participant 9, IER).

Parents and dietitians indicated that the dietary intervention needed to be a priority for parents. However, parents had competing demands and may have needed to prioritise work, other family members’ needs, or were reluctant to implement changes that impacted the family unit. Family support was shaped by the evolving dynamics and relationships within each family unit, with conflict between adolescents and parents common. Siblings could encourage adolescents to eat healthily or challenge adolescents and cause conflict. Dietitians highlighted the importance of engaging extended family.

“I was going through a very stressful time that made things very difficult, and my family didn’t really have most of my back the whole time” (Participant 10, CER).

“[My son] didn’t want me involved in it” (Mother of Participant 10, CER).

Theme 5: Continuity of Care and the Complexity of the Adolescent-Dietitian Therapeutic Relationship

Adolescents and parents valued the dietitian’s guidance and often described the therapeutic relationship with “their” dietitian as critical to success. Adolescents reported that their dietitian was approachable, credible, and a trusted source of advice. Adolescents liked that their dietitian tailored and personalised recommendations, and described a sense of encouragement, monitoring, and accountability derived from regular appointments. Nonetheless, dietitians reflected on the complexity of this therapeutic relationship and the challenge of tailoring recommendations to align with each adolescent’s interests, health literacy, and age.

“I’m keen on someone to build a relationship with, and I knew that was going to be important for [my son] … he needed to sort of feel understood as an individual, and I think that definitely happened” (Mother of Participant 12, IER).

Dietitians navigated care within each family’s evolving psychosocial dynamics and reported needing to be equipped with the skills to manage family conflict and support adolescents with complex health needs. Dietitians and parents reflected on the importance of a multidisciplinary approach. Dietitians and parents suggested that adolescents may have had misconceptions, long-term struggles, and anxieties regarding their eating behaviours and weight, with stress or emotional eating common. Dietitians and parents reflected on the potential benefits of increasing input from psychologists.

“I think the age that they’re at, there is always going to be some sort of conflict … especially with the older adolescents … so that adds another layer of complexity” (Dietitian 2).

Burden and Opportunity Costs

Burden explores the perceived amount of effort to engage with the intervention [13]. Quantitative data found more than half of adolescents viewed meetings, phone calls/text messages from the dietitian, and written materials as useful/very useful (online suppl. Table S5). Fewer adolescents reported the automated weekly text messages were useful/very useful (42% at week 16 and 37% at week 52) (online suppl. Table S5). At week 16, adolescents in the CER group were more likely to rate meetings with the dietitian as useful/very useful (χ2 = 10.16, p = 0.001) (online suppl. Table S5). At week 52, adolescents in the CER group were more likely to rate text messages with recipe ideas as useful/very useful compared to those in the IER group (χ2 = 9.63, p = 0.002) (online suppl. Table S5). Most parents reported their child had enough food on the meal plan and that shopping had not been more difficult while their child was following the meal plan (online suppl. Fig. S5, online suppl. Table S5). Less than half of parents reported preparing family meals had been easier all/most of the time during the study (online suppl. Fig. S5, online suppl. Table S5). Few parents reported the meal plan was difficult to follow at school or disruptive for their child (Fig. 3, online suppl. Table S5). At week 52, parents of adolescents with ≥5% reduction in BMI95 at study completion were less likely to report that the study had been disruptive for their child (0% vs. 11%) compared to parents of adolescents with <5% reduction in BMI95 (χ2 = 5.571, p = 0.043) (online suppl. Table S6). There were no other subgroup differences in parent perceptions of burden associated with the dietary patterns at either time point (online suppl. Fig. S5, online suppl. Table S5, S6).

a–d Burden, opportunity costs, and perceived effectiveness: adolescent and parent ratings of the dietary patterns separated by intervention group.

Opportunity costs relate to perceptions of what may need to be sacrificed or the challenges of engaging with a specialist dietary intervention [13]. Over half of adolescents agreed it was hard or sometimes hard to go out with friends while following their allocated meal plan (online suppl. Fig. S5, online suppl. Table S5). In contrast, most parents (94%) reported their child experienced challenges going out with family and friends some/a little or none of the time (online suppl. Fig. S5, online suppl. Table S5). Approximately a quarter of adolescents and less than 10% of parents reported family life was more difficult while following the meal plan (online suppl. Fig. S5, online suppl. Table S5). More than half of parents reported the meal plan cost the same or less than their usual food costs (Fig. 3, online suppl. Table S5). There were no between-group differences in adolescent or parent views of opportunity costs (online suppl. Table S5).

Theme 6: Access to Regular and Longer Term Dietitian Support

Families valued regular dietetic input and access to advice between appointments. Some families suggested that increased dietitian appointments in the second half of the trial may be beneficial, with some indicating a preference for dietitian appointments every 6 weeks. Longer term support (>12 months) was suggested to encourage maintenance of change.

“It is harder to manage your goals when it [the dietitian appointments] is not as often” (Participant 2, CER).

Face-to-face appointments were described as helpful, with adolescents appreciating in-person clinical assessment, monitoring, and practical resources. However, attending face-to-face appointments could be disruptive, with families requesting weekends or out-of-hours appointments. Dietitians reflected that telehealth appointments could be more flexible, but engaging families in the home environment could be challenging (noting that the trial had to transition rapidly to telehealth during COVID-19 lockdowns). Adolescents received automated text messages to encourage healthy habits (i.e., increasing physical activity, choosing healthy snacks) between appointments. These messages were often perceived as unhelpful as they were not tailored to their situation. However, adolescents and parents liked the personalised, “check-in” calls/text messages from their dietitians between appointments. Interestingly, adolescents found the “check-in” calls and messages encouraging even if they did not plan to engage or respond. The social media page on Facebook was less helpful, with adolescents not being allowed to use social media or disinterested.

“The messages from the dietitian were like good, because she like kept checking up on me and stuff” (Participant 6, IER).

Theme 7: Change within the Broader Context of a Young Person’s Life

Adolescents reported mixed experiences when implementing changes at school. Fitting in or avoiding drawing attention to modified eating behaviours was often important, with some adolescents reluctant to follow dietary recommendations at school. Others were comfortable having meal replacements or following dietary recommendations at school. Implementing changes and attending appointments could be challenging while navigating homework and exams (especially during their final year of school). Nonetheless, adolescents and parents suggested that the school routine facilitated dietary change, with dietary changes often more challenging during the school holidays. Peers could be supportive, provide encouragement, and accept the adolescent’s changing food choices, or challenge adolescents by encouraging discretionary food choices. Broader social circles, including adult family friends, could encourage or discourage healthy behaviours.

“It was kind of weird, I felt a bit like an outcast [having meal replacements at school] but at the same time it was kind of like, well at least I’m doing this and I’m losing weight and getting rid of the health problems” (Participant 14, CER).

“I found it harder to follow on holidays, because I think school… although I am not the biggest fan of it, it sort of structures it a little bit” (Participant 11, IER).

Alongside school, adolescents and parents reported that illnesses/injuries could take precedence and prevent adherence to dietary recommendations. Navigating special occasions, eating at restaurants, and events could require careful planning. Some families reported that healthier food choices were often more expensive and were concerned about the increased costs when shopping.

Finally, this study was conducted during the COVID-19 global pandemic. Dietitians reported that community lockdowns decreased incidental activity, increased intake of discretionary choices, and home-schooling increased stress. Dietitians adjusted dietary recommendations to be more achievable and promote good mental health during the pandemic.

“[During the Covid-19 pandemic] they were really bored at home.… they tended to do a lot more eating, much more sitting with computer games” (Dietitian 4).

“Often these kids are already emotional eaters so that [Covid-19] … slight increase in anxiety or stress will… increase the snacking. Umm, and then coinciding with that, was less activity… My approach was just to … tell them that they were doing really well, just to take the pressure off actually. So, we did what we needed to do to make it achievable for them and to give them some sense of achievement … and in actual fact they did really well” (Dietitian 6).

Perceived Effectiveness

Perceived effectiveness relates to participants’ perceptions of how successfully the intervention supported dietary change [13]. Parents of adolescents in the IER group (42%) were more likely to report that other family members had lost weight than in the CER group (19%) at week 16 (χ2 = 6.55, p = 0.010). At week 16, parents of adolescents with ≥5% reduction in BMI95 at study completion were also more likely to report (40%) that other family members had lost weight compared to parents of adolescents with <5% reduction in BMI95 (10%) (χ2 = 5.8910, p = 0.003). Less than half of parents reported food habits of some family members had changed during the study (online suppl. Fig. S6, online suppl. Table S5), and almost all reported that the study had been beneficial for their child, with no difference between dietary intervention groups (Fig. 3, online suppl. Table S5). At week 52, parents of adolescents with ≥5% reduction in BMI95 at study completion were more likely to report (90%) that the study had been beneficial for their child compared to parents of adolescents with <5% reduction in BMI95 (68%) (χ2 = 5.976, p = 0.015). There were no other between-group differences in perceived effectiveness (online suppl. Fig. S6, online suppl. Table S5, S6).

Theme 8: The Impacts and Value of Change

Adolescents and parents spoke of developing healthier eating behaviours, including eating more vegetables, fewer discretionary foods, practising healthier cooking techniques, eating balanced meals alongside meal planning, and managing portions. Adolescents reported having a better understanding of their satiety cues. Adolescents reported engaging more regularly in physical activity, enjoying exercise and increasing their incidental activity (outside of COVID-related lockdown periods). When reflecting on their experiences, some adolescents valued increased professional support with dietary change, encouragement, accountability from routine appointments, the opportunity to ask questions and develop meaningful therapeutic relationships with dietitians. After participating in the trial, families reflected on better health, including improvements in adolescents’ blood test results and pre-existing health issues (e.g., irritable bowel syndrome). Parents reflected on the positive changes to their adolescent’s self-confidence, with some noting health benefits for the broader family unit. However, some adolescents did not achieve their goals or have the success they hoped for but suggested that they now have more skills and knowledge to implement dietary changes in the future.

“Seeing like the blood results from, like, at the beginning of the trial to the end, and, like, seeing them all drop, that was like pretty cool” (Participant 1, CER).

“I used to get a bit of reflux and I haven’t had that in ages … I would have just little health issues and they aren’t even there anymore” (Participant 12, IER).

Discussion

This mixed-method study provides insights into adolescents’, parents’, and dietitians’ experiences of participating in a specialist dietary intervention delivered in tertiary care, capturing global insights of acceptability from quantitative data alongside in-depth reflections from qualitative data. The findings suggest that both IER and CER can be acceptable to adolescents with obesity and its related complications. The primary outcomes of the Fast Track trial noted that both the IER and CER resulted in reduced BMI z-score with no significant differences in changes in body composition or cardiometabolic outcomes between groups [9]. Considering that the efficacy and acceptability of each plan are similar, study findings encourage and create an opportunity for clinicians to offer adolescents and families the choice to select the specialist nutrition intervention that best meets their needs. Nonetheless, adolescents’ and parents’ viewpoints reinforce the importance of tailoring recommendations alongside providing regular and sustained multidisciplinary input.

Adolescents’ acceptability ratings of IER and CER were similar. However, fewer adolescents randomised to IER completed the 52-week protocol (n = 43 of 71 randomised; 60%) than those randomised to the CER (n = 54 of 70 randomised; 77%), which may be indicative that the IER is less acceptable than CER. Quantitative data noted no between-group differences in the perceived impacts of CER or IER on family/social life. This finding is inconsistent with hypotheses that the IER would offer adolescents’ increased flexibility and support participation in social activities that may be limited in the CER model of care [8]. When comparing acceptability results between adolescents with ≥5% reduction in BMI95 and those with <5% reduction at week 52, parents of adolescents with ≥5% reduction were more likely to report that the study was disruptive only some or none of the time, suggesting that they likely adapted and integrated recommendations into their routine. Qualitative findings noted that adolescents valued tailoring of the intervention, with dietitians often adapting recommendations to align with adolescents’ needs. Tailoring of advice and the therapeutic relationship, continuity of care, and the consequential trust and rapport between the dietitian and the adolescent were therefore often considered critical for success. A 2018 qualitative systematic literature review exploring adolescents’ viewpoints of obesity-treatment interventions found that adolescents often value personal attention and a deeper, more meaningful relationship with professionals [23]. This review noted that the absence of this more personal relationship could lead to feelings of neglect and frustration, resulting in adolescents not adhering to recommendations, and was crucial to continued participation [23]. The perceived helpfulness and CARE scores did not appear to be related to the degree of weight change, suggesting parents and adolescents valued this dietetic support beyond weight outcomes. Our findings suggest that frequent reviews, alongside continuity of care with the same healthcare provider, may optimise adolescents’ engagement with obesity-treatment interventions.

Fast Track was designed to provide support that tapered down over 12 months, with early intensive support designed to enable early skill development/behaviour change to reduce the need for intensive support later in the intervention. However, due to the significance of the therapeutic relationship, qualitative findings found families described a sense of loss as the frequency of dietitian appointments decreased in the second half of the intervention. The decrease in support may have contributed to the lower acceptability ratings across several aspects of the intervention at week 52 compared to week 16 [24]. Similar findings have been reported in qualitative data from adult dietary interventions, where increased flexibility, alongside a decreasing intervention intensity and clinical supervision, was reported to make sustained behaviour change more challenging [24]. The adolescents in our study suggested that their adherence to dietary recommendations became more challenging over time. Offering regular contact instead of tapered support may help adolescents and parents navigate the psychosocial challenges experienced when implementing dietary change.

Finally, a family-focused approach was central to successful dietary change, with adolescents reliant on their parents to provide practical assistance alongside guidance, encouragement, and monitoring. Qualitative findings suggested that adolescents found it easier to implement changes when a parent concurrently followed the specialised dietary plan. When comparing acceptability results between adolescents with ≥5% reduction in BMI95 and those with <5% reduction, parents of adolescents with ≥5% reduction were more likely to report that family members supported the child and that other family members lost weight in the 16 weeks. We hypothesise that parents who implement changes to address their own health concerns are likely to prioritise changes, role model, and encourage their adolescents to implement similar changes. This is consistent with our systematic review which found family and peer support was crucial for success [25]. Interestingly, discrepancies noted between adolescent and parent ratings of support (including difficulty going out with friends or family life) suggest parents may not always recognise adolescents’ challenges. While the quantitative data did not emphasise the impact of family dynamics on the program acceptability, our in-depth qualitative findings highlighted the complexity of the adolescent-parent relationship. Parents reported navigating competing priorities and complex and evolving family dynamics. Adopting family-based approaches for intensive health behaviour interventions is consistent with clinical practice guidelines for obesity treatment in children and adolescents [26]. Long-term success relies on adolescents and their families developing the skills to manage the individual, social, and environmental challenges that impact weight loss maintenance, further highlighting the need for multidisciplinary support [25]. Furthermore, our findings highlight the importance of equipping clinicians with the relevant skills to anticipate challenges and support families as they navigate conflict and dietary change as an interdependent family unit.

Strengths and Limitations

A mixed-method approach to assessing acceptability was a strength of this research. Qualitative findings capture insights from a representative sample of adolescents of varying ages recruited across the two sites of this multisite trial, randomised to the IER and CER, with varying levels of family support and perceived success with the intervention. Triangulating adolescent, parent, and dietitian feedback strengthened findings. However, qualitative data were not collected for non-completers and participants who had a positive experience of the program may have opted to participate in an interview. Understanding the perspectives of adolescents and parents who withdraw from interventions is important, will offer insight into necessary modifications to improve intervention engagement, and should be considered for future research.

Participants’ quantitative insights on acceptability were captured at three timepoints over the 12-month intervention. Completion rates for the acceptability surveys at week 16 and week 52 were 91% and 93%, respectively. The CARE survey completion rate was 70%. CARE data were collected after a dietitian-led review appointment, and such appointments were sometimes conducted via telehealth, instead of a major outcome visit which were conducted face-to-face. We hypothesise it was more difficult for participants to complete at home. The lower completion rate is a limitation of the quantitative data. A longitudinal approach, particularly for qualitative data collection, may capture more insights as adolescents transition through each phase of the intervention, especially as this trial was disrupted by COVID-19 and families were applying different coping strategies during this time. Using established theory to inform the interview guide would have strengthened the versatility of the questions in capturing a breadth of insights from participants. Similarly, the study may have been strengthened by using a validated questionnaire to examine the acceptability of the intervention and would allow comparison with similar interventions. When collecting qualitative data, adolescents and parents were aware that the interviewer was a research team member and dietitians were colleagues of the interviewer, which may have impacted the participants’ willingness to disclose certain viewpoints. Interpreting services were not available, which prevented participation from families less fluent in English. Finally, member checking was not used to ensure that qualitative findings were representative of the participants’ sentiments and is a study limitation.

Conclusion

This study provides unique insights from adolescents, their parents, and study dietitians into the acceptability of a 12-month structured lifestyle intervention delivered in tertiary care, providing novel perspectives of two specialist dietary interventions. Regular access to and maintenance of dietitian support, continuity of care, meaningful family engagement, strategies to sustain adolescent motivation, and tailoring of care to each adolescent’s personal circumstances contributed to successful dietary change. Clinicians need to be equipped with the relevant skills to support families in overcoming prevalent psychosocial challenges that influence the experiences of implementing dietary changes. Services delivering specialist obesity-treatment interventions must be adequately equipped to provide families with sustained care to establish and maintain the therapeutic relationships that enable behaviour change. The findings of this study provide valuable insights that can be used to inform and tailor acceptable specialist obesity treatments to optimise adolescents’ and their families’ current and future health.
