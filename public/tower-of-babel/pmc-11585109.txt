Glycated albumin levels in the third trimester of women with gestational diabetes mellitus are associated with adverse pregnancy-related outcomes
Authors: Yueshuai Pan, Ruting Gu, Qianqian Li, Jingyuan Wang, Yan Zhang, Lin Zhao, Yue Wu, Lili Wei
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGlycated albumin (GA) levels have been considered as a promising biomarker for estimating glycemic control during pregnancy, but the relationship between GA levels and the incidence of adverse pregnancy outcomes in women with gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) remains unclear. Our study aimed to investigate the relationship between GA levels during the third trimester and 13 different adverse pregnancy-related outcomes among women with GDM in China.MethodsWe retrospectively extracted clinical data from the medical records of 819 pregnant women with GDM who underwent prenatal examinations and child delivery at the Affiliated Hospital of Qingdao University between January 2022 and October 2022. The cohort was divided into GA-high (GA-H) and GA-low (GA-L) groups based on the median GA level of 10.6%. Then, the incidence rates of 13 specific adverse pregnancy outcomes were compared between the two groups. Furthermore, we estimated the mean GA levels in pregnant GDM women with or without specific adverse outcomes. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to assess whether the GA levels (high or low) were independent risk factors for specific adverse outcomes in pregnant women with GDM. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to assess the predictive value of GA levels for the adverse pregnant outcomes in women with GDM. E-value for sensitivity analysis was performed to assess the robustness of the findings to unmeasured confoundings.ResultsWe included 819 pregnant women with GDM, whose average age was 33.09 ± 4.47 years, average pre-pregnancy BMI was 23.51 ± 3.67 kg/m2, and the average gestational week in which GDM diagnosed was 24.80 ± 1.79 weeks. The analysis showed that 80.71% (661/819) pregnant women with GDM were associated with adverse pregnancy-related outcomes. Pregnant women in the GA-L group showed higher incidence of the premature rupture of membranes (PROM), whereas those in the GA-H group showed higher incidence of neonatal hypoglycemia. The GA levels showed acceptable clinical performance for predicting neonatal hypoglycemia with an area under the ROC curve (AUC) value of 0.700 (P = 0.010), sensitivity of 71.4%, and specificity of 70.2%. The optimal cut off value for GA was 11.55%.ConclusionsThis study demonstrated that GA levels were significantly associated with specific adverse pregnancy outcomes, especially PROM and neonatal hypoglycemia. Furthermore, GA levels in the third trimester showed acceptable clinical performance for predicting neonatal hypoglycemia among pregnant women with GDM. In the future, the potential role of GA as a predictor of adverse pregnancy outcomes need to be further confirmed and explored in GDM women.Keywords: Gestational diabetes mellitus, Glycated albumin, Adverse pregnancy outcomes, Premature rupture of membranes, Neonatal hypoglycemia

Background

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is one of the most common pregnancy-related complications and is characterized by a varying degree of glucose intolerance during the second or third trimester of pregnancy [1]. According to the IDF Diabetes Atlas (10th edition), hyperglycemia was reported for approximately 21.1 million (16.7%) pregnant women in 2021 worldwide, and 80.3% of these cases were categorized as GDM [2]. The prevalence of GDM is estimated to increase consistently because of a gradually rise in the living standards and the delayed childbearing age of women. Furthermore, GDM is associated with a higher short-term and long-term risk of metabolic disorders for both the mothers and their offspring [3]. GDM is also associated with significantly higher risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes such as premature birth, fetal distress, fetal growth restriction, macrosomia, postpartum hemorrhage, fetal malformations, and even stillbirths [4, 5]. Therefore, GDM is associated with significant physical and emotional burden for the affected mothers and their offspring, and is a high economic burden on the family and the public healthcare system [6].

Therefore, strict glycemic control throughout the gestational period is critical in preventing or decreasing the incidence of adverse pregnancy outcomes [7]. Currently, glycated hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) is the gold standard biomarker for assessing glycemic control because it gives an estimate of the blood glucose levels for the previous 2–3 months [8]. However, HbA1c levels may not be an appropriate biomarker to monitor the glycemic status during pregnancy because of an increased turnover of the red blood cells (RBCs) and the increased incidence of iron deficiency-related anemia during pregnancy, especially during the second and the third trimesters [9]. Glycated albumin (GA) is recommended as an alternate biomarker for estimating glycemic control during pregnancy because it estimates the average glucose level over the previous 2–3 weeks and is not affected by hemoglobin metabolism or iron deficiency-related anemia [10]. Furthermore, previous studies have shown that GA is more sensitive than HbA1c in estimating the glycemic fluctuations during pregnancy [11, 12]. Thus, GA levels during the third trimester are more likely to be associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes in GDM women.

Multiple studies have demonstrated that GA is a promising biomarker for the diagnosis and management of diabetes mellitus [13–15], but the relationship between GA levels and the incidence of adverse pregnancy outcomes in women with GDM is not clearly established. Moreover, multiple studies have reported contradictory findings regarding the association between GA levels and adverse pregnancy outcomes in women with GDM. Mendes et al. reported that higher levels of GA in the pregnant mothers with GDM were associated with at least one neonatal complication in their newborns [16]. Sugawara et al. reported that infants of mothers with higher GA levels during pregnancy were large for gestational age (LGA) and diagnosed with hypoglycemia and respiratory disorders [17]. Wu et al. reported that GDM patients with GA ≥ 10.33% were associated with increased incidences of cesarean delivery, hypertension, and fetal distress [18]. However, Dong et al. reported that higher GA levels during the 3rd trimester only in GDM women with postpartum hemorrhage, but GA levels were not associated with adverse outcomes such as LGA, hypertension, preterm delivery and preeclampsia (PE) in GDM women [19]. Furthermore, Zhuang et al. showed that GA levels in the third trimester (34–40 weeks) were not associated with any adverse pregnancy outcomes [20]. Therefore, Our study investigated the relationship between GA levels in the third trimester and adverse pregnancy outcomes in Chinese pregnant women with GDM, and estimated the predictive value of GA for the adverse pregnancy-related outcomes in order to guide better clinical management for GDM and reduce the occurrence of adverse pregnancy outcomes.

Methods

Study design

This retrospective study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Qingdao University (No. QYFY WZLL 28129).

Participants

We reviewed medical records of pregnant women with GDM who underwent obstetric examinations and delivered at the Affiliated Hospital of Qingdao University between January 2022 and October 2022. Pregnant women with singleton pregnancies who were older than 18 years and diagnosed with GDM were included in this study. The exclusion criteria included patients with other pregnancy complications (e.g., pregnancy hypertension) or other diseases (e.g., pulmonary hypertension, congenital heart disease, and others). GDM was diagnosed according to the diagnostic criteria recommended by the International Association of Diabetes and Pregnancy Study Groups (IADPSG) in 2010 [21], and was based on a 75 g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) at 24–28 weeks of gestation. GDM was positively diagnosed when the subjects met one of the three following criteria: fasting plasma glucose (FPG) ≥ 5.1 mmol/L; 1-h postprandial blood glucose ≥ 10.0 mmol/L; or 2-h postprandial blood glucose ≥ 8.5 mmol/L. All pregnant women diagnosed with GDM in our study group were given health education on diet, exercise, and blood glucose monitoring, etc. In addition, they were advised to conduct self-monitoring of glycemia daily. The glycemic targets of GDM women during pregnancy are recommended to be as follows: fasting and premeal blood glucose ranges from 3.3 to 5.3 mmol/L, 1-h postprandial blood glucose < 7.8 mmol/L, 2-h postprandial blood glucose < 6.7 mmol/L, and nighttime blood glucose ≥ 3.3 mmol/L.22

Collection of clinical data

We retrieved the following clinical characteristics of the study participants from the medical records: age, pre-pregnancy height and weight, weight at the last prenatal examination, gravidity and parity, family history of diabetes mellitus, regularity of the menstrual cycle, history of adverse pregnancy, and adverse pregnancy outcomes. Pre-pregnancy body mass index (pre-pregnancy BMI) was calculated by dividing the pre-pregnancy weight (kg) by the pre-pregnancy height (m2). Weight gain during pregnancy was calculated by subtracting the pre-pregnancy weight from the weight at the last prenatal examination. Laboratory parameters included the results of the 75 g OGTT at 16–33 weeks with an average of (24.74 ± 1.80) weeks, triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC) and GA levels at 28–41 weeks with an average of (34.02 ± 2.53) weeks. GA levels were estimated by high-performance liquid chromatography in our study.

We assessed 13 specific adverse outcomes in this study, including premature birth, premature rupture of membranes (PROM), placental abruption, caesarean delivery, fetal distress, fetal malformation, fetal growth restriction, shoulder dystocia, stillbirth, macrosomia (birth weights ≥ 4000 g), neonatal asphyxia, neonatal hypoglycemia (plasma glucose<2.6 mmol/L within one hour after birth [23]), and postpartum hemorrhage.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using the SPSS Statistical software version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Probability plot was used to determine whether the data was normally or non-normally distributed. The normally distributed numerical data was represented as mean ± standard deviation. The skewed numerical data was represented as median (interquartile range). The categorical data was expressed as counts and percentages. The median GA value was used as the cutoff value to categorize the study subjects into the GA-high (GA-H) and GA-low (GA-L) groups. The incidences of adverse outcomes between the two groups were analyzed using the chi-square test. Student’s t test was used to analyze whether the differences in the mean GA levels between any two groups with and without specific adverse outcomes were statistically significant or not. Multivariate logistic regression analysis with a backward stepwise approach was used to further determine whether the GA level was an independent risk factor for predicting specific adverse outcomes in pregnant women with GDM. A collinearity test was performed prior to the logistic regression analysis. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to assess the predictive value of GA for estimating specific adverse pregnant outcomes in pregnant women with GDM. The sensitivity analysis to unmeasured confounding factors using the E-value methodology of VanderWeele and Ding [24]. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

In this study, the incidences of one or more adverse outcomes in the cohort of 819 pregnant women with GDM was 80.71%. Caesarean delivery, PROM, and macrosomia were the top 3 pregnancy-related adverse outcomes with the incidences of 58.36%, 18.32%, and 12.33% among women with GDM, respectively. Compared with our results, Tian et al. study showed the lower incidences of cesarean (40.5%), and macrosomia (9.2%) in a cohort of 25,995 pregnant women with GDM from Hebei Province, China [25].

GA is a biomarker of glucose homeostasis and is significantly associated with the incidence rates of specific adverse pregnancy outcomes among women with GDM. We used 10.6% as the cut-off value for GA and compared the incidences of specific adverse outcomes between subjects with GA ≥ 10.6% (GA-H group) versus subjects with GA<10.6% (GA-L group) and did not find any significant differences in the incidence rates of 11 out of 13 adverse outcomes that were assessed. However, GA-H group showed lower incidence of PROM and higher incidence of neonatal hypoglycemia than the GA-L group. After adjusting for the confounding factors, GA<10.6% was associated with a 1.752-fold higher risk of PROM compared to those with GA ≥ 10.6% (Table 5). Furthermore, our data showed that GA ≥ 10.6% was not an independent risk factor for neonatal hypoglycemia.

Premature rupture of membranes (PROM) is defined as the rupture of the amniotic membranes and release of the amniotic fluid before the onset of labor [26]. The etiology of PROM is unknown. Previous studies have shown that maternal hyperglycemia increased the risk of infection at the choriodecidual interface, which weakened the fetal membranes and increased the incidence of PROM [27, 28]. Furthermore, nutrient deficiency causes premature weakening and rupture of the fetal membranes. Pregnant women with GDM are deficient in the energy and nutrient levels because their food intake is restricted to control the blood glucose levels [29]. Therefore, women in the GA-L group are expected to be more deficient in the energy and nutrient levels despite better glycemic control than the GA-H group in our study. This nutritional and energy deficiency may explain the higher risk of PROM in the pregnant women belonging to the GA-L group. Moreover, our data also showed that pre-pregnancy BMI was an independent risk factor that was negatively associated with PROM. This result was in agreement with the findings of previous studies, which reported that women with higher pre-pregnancy BMI showed lower incidence of PROM [27, 30].

We then compared the mean GA levels in women with GDM that were sub-grouped into those with or without each of the 13 pregnancy-related adverse outcomes. The results showed that the GA levels were higher in the third trimester for GDM women in the group with neonatal hypoglycemia compared to the group without neonatal hypoglycemia. Sugawara et al. retrospectively analyzed 77 Japanese pregnant women with GDM and reported higher incidence of neonatal hypoglycemia in those with higher GA levels compared to those with lower GA levels [31]. Another study by Sugawara et al. showed similar results which the higher GA levels in third trimester were associated with higher incidence of neonatal hypoglycemia for pregnant women that were diagnosed with hyperglycemia because of GDM, type 1 diabetes or type 2 diabetes [17]. These data were similar to our findings and showed that higher GA levels during the 3rd trimester indicated poor glucose control in the pregnant women. The fetus receives higher levels of glucose during maternal hyperglycemia. This is counteracted by a compensatory hyperinsulinemia in the fetus to metabolize the excess glucose. This hyperinsulinemia is the main cause of neonatal hypoglycemia after delivery [32].

Our data also showed reliable clinical value for the GA levels in predicting neonatal hypoglycemia. Logistic regression analysis results showed that the risk of neonatal hypoglycemia increased by 1.404-fold for every 1% increase in the maternal GA levels (Table 7). As shown in Fig. 1, the predictive performance of GA was acceptable with an AUC value of 0.70 (P = 0.010). The sensitivity and specificity values were 71.4% and 70.2%, respectively, when the GA cut off value was 11.55%. Therefore, in a population of 1000 women with an incidence rate of 1.71% for neonatal hypoglycemia, our data predicted that 5 patients with neonatal hypoglycemia would receive false negative results and 310 patients without neonatal hypoglycemia would receive false positive results. Our data also suggested that the GA levels in pregnant women with GDM should be below 11.55% to decrease the risk of neonatal hyperglycemia.

To assess the robustness of the findings to unmeasured confoundings, we used the E-value for sensitivity analysis. E-value was calculated to estimate the extent to which an unmeasured confounder would have to be associated with both GA levels and adverse pregnancy outcomes to nullify the observed association between GA levels and outcomes [24]. The results indicated that unmeasured confounders could fully account for the association of GA levels with PROM and neonatal hypoglycemia if it were associated with the exposure and outcomes by ORs of 3.27 and 2.16, which weaker confounding factors could not do so. Therefore, the robustness of the conclusions that GA levels in the third trimester were associated with the occurrence of PROM and neonatal hypoglycemia were validated.

More than 80% of women with GDM were associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes in our study. And our sthdy found that both high and low GA levels were associated with different adverse pregnancy outcomes in women with GDM. Therefore, identifying an appropriate GA range is of great significance to prevent adverse pregnancy outcomes. However, there is no explicitly GA recommended reference interval for GDM. Only a few studies focused on establishing the GA reference ranges in pregnancy and there are differences in the reference values for GA in related studies [18, 19]. Moreover, the results of a meta-analysis showed that the average cut-off values reported in these GDM studies were much lower than those for another type of non-GDM [33]. Therefore, it is reasonable to infer that the GA values that predict adverse pregnancy outcomes in GDM may also differ from those in non-GDM. In the future, a reference range for GA is more valuable to establish for women with GDM.

Limitations

This study has a few limitations. Firstly, the causality and generalizability of the results was limited because this was a single-center retrospective study. In the future, a multi-center prospective cohort study should be designed to confirm our findings and improve external validity. Secondly, due to the retrospective nature of this study, we could not obtain accurate data for their treatment which may have prevented the occurrence of adverse outcomes in GDM patients, such as diet, exercise, medication and parameters of glycaemic control. And our study may be subject to those unmeasured confounders although we used logistic regression analysis that incorporated major clinically relevant factors (e.g., results of the 75 g OGTT, ) to address the risk of confounding factors. Thirdly, we used the median of GA levels as a cut-off value, which is relatively intuitive and stable, but the actual distribution and characteristics of the data are not taken into account. Thus, our results did not definitely prove that GA levels are not associated with other adverse pregnancy outcomes. In the future, it may be necessary to explore more reasonable classification based on the actual distribution and characteristics of the data.

Conclusions

Our study showed that altered GA levels in pregnant women with GDM were associated with increased prevalence of PROM and neonatal hypoglycemia. And low GA levels were associated with increased risk of PROM, whereas high GA levels were associated with increased risk of neonatal hypoglycemia. Moreover, GA levels showed acceptable prediction performance for neonatal hypoglycemia. Due to the limitations of our study, the potential role of GA as a predictor of adverse pregnancy outcomes need to be further confirmed and explored in GDM women.
