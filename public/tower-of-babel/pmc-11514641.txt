Study protocol for a multicenter non-inferiority randomized controlled trial to assess functional outcomes and cost-effectiveness of a primarily non-operative treatment strategy with cast immobilization versus immediate operative treatment followed by cast immobilization for patients with complete ulnar collateral ligament ruptures, including Stener lesions: MUSCAT study
Authors: Louise de Haas, Bastiaan van Hoorn, Veronique van de Lücht, N Schep, M Dijkgraaf, M van Heijl, MUSCAT Trial Collaborator group
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGuidelines recommend operative treatment followed by cast immobilization for acute complete ulnar collateral ligament (UCL) ruptures, including Stener lesions. This recommendation is based on expert opinion, anatomic theories, and low-quality observational case series. High-quality studies comparing non-operative treatment to operative treatment are lacking. We hypothesize that primarily non-operative treatment with cast immobilization (cast immobilization) is non-inferior regarding functional outcome and carries concomitant lower costs compared with immediate operative treatment followed by cast immobilization (operative treatment) for complete UCL ruptures, including Stener lesions.MethodsThis is a multicenter randomized controlled non-inferiority trial (RCT) including patients of 18 years and above, requiring treatment for an acute complete UCL rupture, including Stener lesions. Patients are randomized to cast immobilization or operative treatment followed by cast immobilization. Immobilization consists of 4 weeks of a non-removable cast around the metacarpophalangeal (MCP) and carpometacarpal (CMC) joint of the thumb in a neutral position, followed by a removable cast for 4 weeks for both groups. Patients in the cast immobilization group are re-evaluated 2 to 3 weeks after the start of cast immobilization to examine thumb stability and determine if secondary surgery is required. In case of persistent laxity, secondary surgery is required. The primary outcome is hand function expressed as the Michigan Hand outcome Questionnaire (MHQ) at 6 months (from injury to 6 months after).DiscussionIf cast immobilization is non-inferior to operative treatment, the proposed treatment strategy will reduce patient burden by preventing surgery. It is expected that about one in ten patients who started with cast immobilization will need secondary surgery during re-evaluation. As a result, completion of the treatment will take longer for these patients compared to patients who received immediate operative treatment.Trial registrationCentral Committee on Research Involving Human Subjects (CCMO), NL78886.100.21; registered on 4 October 2021. Introduction

Background and rationale {6a}

Ulnar collateral ligament (UCL) injuries of the metacarpophalangeal joint (MCP) of the thumb are relatively common with an estimated incidence of 50 per 100,000 emergency room visitors [1]. In the Netherlands, approximately 10,500 patients are diagnosed with an acute UCL injury of the thumb yearly. These UCL injuries are classified into three grades based on the laxity of the ligament. Grade 1 injuries present with tenderness along the UCL, without laxity compared with the non-injured thumb. They are classified as sprains and have an intact UCL. Grade 2 injuries demonstrate an increase in laxity but have a firm endpoint which indicates a partial rupture of the UCL. Grade 3 injuries are complete UCL ruptures with increased laxity without a firm endpoint. Around 75% of UCL injuries are complete UCL ruptures. These complete UCL ruptures include Stener lesions, meaning an interposition of a part of a thumb muscle between the torn ligament ends. The prevalence of Stener lesions varies widely between studies, ranging from 11 to 87% [2, 3]. Dutch guidelines drafted by the society of Surgery and Plastic Surgery recommend immobilization of the thumb with a cast for 4 to 6 weeks for grade 1 and 2 injuries. For complete UCL ruptures, they recommend immediate operative treatment followed by cast immobilization. This recommendation is based on expert opinion, anatomic theories, and low-quality observational case series, mainly level IV evidence. High-quality studies comparing non-operative treatment to operative treatment are lacking [4]. Outcomes of surgery for complete UCL ruptures, including Stener lesions, are generally good regardless of the surgical technique. Regarding non-operative treatment, two observational studies that evaluated thumb immobilization with a cast for complete ruptures, including Stener lesions, showed promising results: in 71 patients who underwent cast immobilization, 63 (89%) felt normal or had minimal disability after 1 year. Those who remained symptomatic were successfully treated with surgery. One patient remained symptomatic after surgery (which was similar to patients treated with initial surgery) [5, 6].

Taking those studies into account, a non-operative treatment strategy for complete UCL ruptures, including Stener lesions, after which patients only have a small chance of secondary surgery, could be justified. Non-operative treatment with cast immobilization is minimally invasive and could lead to lower costs compared to operative treatment.

Therefore, we want to conduct a randomized controlled trial (RCT) with non-inferiority design comparing functional outcomes and cost-effectiveness of a primarily non-operative treatment strategy including cast immobilization, only followed by secondary surgery in case of persistent laxity after 2 to 3 weeks of cast immobilization, with immediate operative treatment followed by cast immobilization.

Objectives {7}

The primary objective is to assess if cast immobilization is non-inferior to operative treatment. The primary outcome is hand function expressed as the Michigan Hand Questionnaire (MHQ) at 6 months (from injury to 6 months after).

The secondary objective is to assess up to 12 months after injury the following: hand function reported by patient specific daily life activities using the Patient-Specific Functional Scale (PSFS), pain intensity, patient satisfaction, range of motion of the thumb joints, grip and pinch strength, quality of life, work absence, and cost-effectiveness.

Trial design {8}

This is a multi-center randomized controlled non-inferiority trial. Patients will be randomly assigned to the intervention group, primarily non-operative treatment with cast immobilization (cast immobilization), or the comparison group, immediate operative treatment followed by cast immobilization (operative treatment) with a 1:1 allocation ratio.

Methods: participants, interventions, and outcomes

Study setting {9}

This study takes place in 13 community hospitals in the Netherlands and one academic hospital in Belgium. A list of study sites can be obtained from the coordinating investigator or the study website. Adult patients presenting at the emergency department (ED) with a sustained trauma to the thumb, resulting in acute pain and swelling around the MCP joint, are screened for eligibility. The diagnosis of an acute complete UCL rupture, including a potential Stener lesion, needs to be confirmed by the surgeon through physical examination at either the ED or during the first outpatient department (OPD) visit. When the diagnosis of a complete UCL rupture is confirmed, the patient meets the inclusion criteria, and informed consent is obtained, the patient is randomized.

Interventions

Explanation for the choice of comparators {6b}

Primarily, non-operative treatment with cast immobilization (cast immobilization), the intervention, is compared to immediate operative treatment followed by cast immobilization (operative treatment), usual care.

Intervention description {11a}

Patients presenting at the ED with a sustained trauma to the thumb, resulting in acute pain and swelling around the MCP joint of the thumb, are potentially eligible for inclusion in the study. An appointment is scheduled with a hand surgeon at the OPD within 3 to 5 days from presentation at the ED or the patient is attended by a hand surgeon at the ED.

During the appointment with the hand surgeon, the surgeon (with sufficient experience in treatment of UCL injuries) will examine the injured thumb. Laxity of the MCP joint of the thumb is examined by providing radial force to the proximal phalanx, while stabilizing the metacarpal bone. Stressing the MCP joint in the acute setting may be painful and guarding or muscle spasm may give a false-negative examination. An injection with a local anesthetic may be used for better examination. When the diagnosis of a complete UCL rupture is confirmed (according to the criteria provided under the inclusion criteria) and the patient meets the inclusion criteria and provides informed consent, the patient is randomized to the intervention group or the usual care group.

Intervention group

Patients in the intervention group receive primarily non-operative treatment in a cast to immobilize the MCP and CMC joint of the thumb. The MCP joint is immobilized in 10–30° slight flexion (neutral position), and the CMC joint is in 30–40° palmar abduction. The IP-joint and wrist are free.

Patients will be immobilized in a non-removable cast for a period of 4 weeks in total, starting from the first day of immobilization. Two to three weeks after the start of immobilization, a second appointment is scheduled to re-examine the thumb. The laxity of the MCP joint of the thumb is re-examined, preferably by the same hand surgeon as the initial visit. The hand surgeon examines if “tightening” of the UCL has occurred. “Tightening” is not a subtle finding and might even be present with less opening during radial deviation stress when compared to the contralateral thumb. When the hand surgeon concludes that there is an endpoint and less laxity, cast immobilization is continued until a total immobilization period of 4 weeks is completed. When there is no endpoint and laxity persists, resembling laxity observed at inclusion in the MCP joint, patients are scheduled for surgery within five working days, switching to usual care.

After 4 weeks, the non-removable cast is replaced for a removable splint (similar position of the hand), and patients are instructed to start using the thumb for regular daily activities without resistance and start thumb exercises consisting of a combination of opposition and abduction/adduction movements. Patients are advised to repeat the exercises 5–10 times a day. An immediate referral is made to the hand therapist.

Usual care group

The Dutch hand fracture guideline committee recommends operative treatment for patients with a complete UCL rupture, including Stener lesions, and postoperative management with cast immobilization.

Patients in the usual care group will receive operative treatment of the thumb. Generally, two surgical techniques are employed in daily clinical practice and will be utilized in this study. The first technique comprises of sutures used to reattach the ligament remnants together and is used when the UCL is ruptured in the middle of the ligament. The second technique where the UCL is reattached directly to the bone using suture anchors is used when no viable UCL ligaments are present.

Patients can choose between WALANT-method of anesthesia (wide-awake local anesthesia no tourniquet), regional anesthetics of the upper arm, or complete sedation. The procedure normally takes 30–45 min. Postoperatively, patients are treated with a cast (in a similar way as described for the intervention group) for 4 weeks. After 10 days, the skin sutures are removed at the OPD, and the cast is renewed. After completing 4 weeks of non-removable cast immobilization, the cast is removed and replaced with a removable cast for four more weeks, and patients will receive similar instructions as the intervention group to start using the thumb. An immediate referral is made to the hand therapist.

Criteria for discontinuing or modifying allocated interventions {11b}

For patients allocated to the intervention group, an appointment is scheduled with the hand surgeon at the OPD 2 to 3 weeks after the start of cast immobilization. The hand surgeon re-examines the laxity of the MCP joint by applying radial deviation stress to the thumb; the hand surgeon can assess whether “tightening” of the UCL had occurred. The chance that re-examination at 2 to 3 weeks may induce a re-rupture or a Stener lesion is close to zero, as re-examination at this time is also part of the current clinical practice in case of cast immobilization. “Tightening” is not a subtle finding and might even be present with less opening during radial deviation stress when compared to the contralateral thumb. When the hand surgeon concludes that there is no firm endpoint and laxity persists resembling laxity observed at inclusion of the UCL (meaning there is no adequate healing or “tightening” of the UCL), patients are scheduled for secondary surgery within five working days (switching to the usual care treatment strategy; as described under usual care). This is an intention to treat RCT. All patients randomized to the intervention group (primarily non-operative treatment strategy) will be included in the intervention group for all analyses, even if they present with persistent laxity at the 2 to 3-week re-examination and require secondary surgery. Potential secondary surgery is part of the treatment strategy in the intervention group. Patients randomized to the usual care group, who receive immediate operative treatment followed by cast immobilization, will remain in the usual care group for all analyses. Thus, the analysis populations will consist of all patients as randomized, reflecting their originally assigned treatment strategy. This approach is consistent with the intention to treat principle, ensuring that all patients are analyzed according to their assigned treatment group.

Participants are informed about the study and give consent prior to randomization between the two treatment groups. At all times, participants have the option to discontinue the allocated treatment or withdraw participation from the study, without any reason and without consequences. All withdrawal and dropouts of enrolled subjects from the trial are reported and explained on the CRFs.

We further assume that both treatment strategies are safe. A potential surgical procedure for the intervention group is not associated with negative consequences to the patient, since patients in the usual care group will receive surgery as standard care, as recommended by the Dutch hand fracture guidelines.

Outcomes {12}

All study parameters are selected by using the International Consortium for Health Outcomes Measurement International (ICHOM) standard outcome set for hand and wrist conditions and performed as described below.

Main study parameter/endpoint

The Michigan Hand Questionnaire (MHQ), expressed as the MHQ at 6 months (final value at 6 months, from injury to 6 months after), is the primary endpoint of the study. The MHQ is a validated tool for assessment of functional outcome in patients with pathology of the hand. The MHQ is a questionnaire divided in six subscales: overall hand function, activities of daily living (ADLs), pain, work performance, aesthetics, and patient satisfaction with hand function. Each subscale has a formula to calculate a score. The final score is a summation of the six individual subscale scores divided by six and goes from 0 (severe disability) to 100 (no disability) [7].

Secondary study parameters/endpoints

Patient-Specific Functional Scale (PSFS) is a self-reported, patient-specific outcome measure, designed to assess functional change. Patients are requested to identify a minimum of three important daily life activities (with which they are having difficulty to perform due to the injury) and rate performance from 0 (unable to perform) to 10 (able to perform). The PSFS will be assessed at baseline, at 4 weeks after start of cast immobilization/surgery, and at 3, 6, and 12 months after injury during the follow-up appointment. The PSFS is presented as final values at all assessments

Sample size {14}

The sample size calculation is based on our primary endpoint (hand function at 6 months from injury expressed the MHQ score after 6 months) and calculated using G Power 3.1.9.2. The MHQ score ranges from 0, indicating worst pain and severe impairment, to 100, no complaints of the hand. We hypothesized the MHQ score at 6 months of patients treated with cast immobilization is not substantially worse than the MHQ score of patients in the operative treatment group. Not substantially worse is defined with a non-inferiority margin. The null hypothesis states that cast immobilization is inferior to operative treatment. The results from two earlier studies on the subject were used for the sample size calculation [14, 15]. We used a one-sided significance level of 0.025 and a power of 80% with a standard deviation (SD) of 15 and setting the non-inferiority limit at 8. A total of 112 patients are required to achieve sufficient statistical power to reject the null hypothesis of inferiority of cast immobilization compared to operative treatment. With a 10% loss to follow-up included, this results in an acquired sample size of 126 patients, with a total of 63 patients in each treatment arm.

The non-inferiority limit of 8 is based on the previous estimated minimal clinically important difference (MCID) of 15 points by Shauver et al. The MCID of 15 is based on the average of the MCID’s of 23, 13, and 8 for the pain, function, and work domains, respectively, in patients with carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS). The MCID of the MHQ score for patients with thumb injury is not yet investigated. Therefore, we decided to use the MCID of CTS patients. The MCID of 15 was divided by 2 and rounded up to 8.

The SD of 15 was based on the highest reported SD in a case series by Legerstee et al. in patients who underwent open thumb UCL repair for complete UCL injuries. They reported a SD of 15, 14, and 12 of the MHQ at baseline, 3 months, and 12 months respectively.

Considering the uncertainty of the SD of the MHQ scores for patients with UCL-ruptures in our own study population, an independent statistician will assess if the sample size calculation needs adjustment, based on the MHQ score and SD in the control group, after half of the patients pass their 6-month follow-up (N = 32).

Statistical methods

Statistical methods for primary and secondary outcomes {20a}

Data will be analyzed following intention-to-treat. Categorical variables will be presented as counts and percentages and continuous variables as median and interquartile ranges or mean and standard deviation based on the normality of the data. Distribution will be visually checked by plotting data of continues variables in a histogram and in addition statistically checked using Shapiro–Wilk and Kolmogorov–Smirnov test (p < 0.05 non normal distribution). Differences in characteristics between groups will be compared using Fisher exact and Pearson’s χ2 tests for categorical variables, and Wilcoxon rank sum tests will be used for continuous variables, where appropriate to confirm if randomization was successful.

We will conduct an additional linear regression analysis for longitudinal data (i.e., a mixed model) in which we correct for relevant prognostic factors (time between trauma and definite treatment, duration of hand therapy, hand comorbidities, etc.) and baseline scores of the Michigan Hand Questionnaire. In an additional step, we will correct for surgeons depending on the number of treating surgeons as well as the number of patients per treating surgeon. Results will be presented as differences between treatment and control with two-sided 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). The intervention will be labeled as non-inferior when the non-inferiority margin of 8 lies outside the 95% two-sided CI of this difference, regarding the Michigan Hand Questionnaire measured at 6 months as our primary endpoint. In case of missing data, we will perform an ignorability analysis or correct for this using the appropriate missing data technique depending on the underlying missing value mechanism. The secondary outcomes—PSFS, EQ-5D-5L, ROM, Kapandji score, grip strength, pinch strength, and pain—will be analyzed in a similar manner. There was no non-inferiority margin defined for the secondary outcomes. These will be analyzed following standard superiority inferential statistics. Differences between the two treatment groups in complication rates will be analyzed using the chi-square test or the Fisher’s exact test (in case the expected incidence was less than five).

In theory, surgery in the intervention arm could result in an unexpectedly suboptimal outcome compared to immediate surgery. To determine whether this is the case, an exploratory analysis will be performed by ranking the MHQ scores in the entire group of patients who (eventually) underwent surgery and comparing the mean rankings. If the mean ranking of the subgroup that eventually underwent surgery in the intervention arm indicates a suboptimal outcome (mean ranking more than 2 different in the direction of worsening to the detriment of the intervention arm), a critical event committee will be established to assess, based on a relevant extract from the patient file, whether direct surgery would have resulted in a different outcome. To increase the uncertainty for the committee and to avoid assessment bias, several directly operated patients from the control group will also be added to the set to be assessed. The qualitative reporting of the CEC assessment will be of a descriptive nature.

Methods for additional analyses (e.g., subgroup analyses) {20b}

Cost-effectiveness analysis

This evaluation focuses on the question if the proposed intervention for patients with a complete UCL rupture, including Stener lesions, using a primarily non-operative treatment strategy with cast immobilization, is cost-effective compared to immediate operative treatment followed by cast immobilization. The economic evaluation will be performed as a cost-effectiveness (CEA) as well as a cost-utility analysis (CUA) from the societal as well as health care perspectives [16]. The primary economic outcome for the CEA is the costs per unit difference between the intervention and control group in overall hand function and closely relates to the clinical outcome measure. The CEA is performed to enable identification of diagnostic or interventional strategies to be preferred economically in the field of hand surgery. The primary economic outcome for the CUA is the costs per quality-adjusted life year (QALY). The CUA is performed to enable priority setting during health care policy making across disease areas and health care settings. Data is derived from the questionnaire data and the electronic patient file.

If the null hypothesis in this study of cast immobilization not being clinically non-inferior at 6 months is not rejected, but indeed lower costs are associated with cast immobilization, then incremental cost-effectiveness ratios will be calculated as the extra savings per extra unit in MHQ score lost or the extra savings per additional QALY lost. If the null hypothesis is rejected, a costs analysis and an outcome analysis will be done as distinct partial economic evaluations. The time horizon will be 12 months. It is expected that effects and costs beyond 12 months are similar for both treatment strategies and that a lifetime time horizon is redundant.

Differences between groups will be assessed after correction for bias and using accelerated non-parametric bootstrapping to account for sampling variability, generating 5000 replications. Results will be presented graphically by incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) planes. Cost-effectiveness acceptability curves will be drawn for various levels of willingness-to-pay per QALY up to €50,000, because higher levels cannot be justified by the maximum observable disease burden in this target group of patients. A scenario analysis will be applied by excluding the productivity losses following sick leave. Subgroup analyses will be performed for patients with a paid job.

Missing cost and effect data will be imputed using predictive mean matching or multivariate imputation of chained equations, depending on the structure of the data and missing data counts and patterns which will be assessed after data cleaning; the imputation approach will follow the recommendations of Faria et al. [17].

Measurement and valuation of costs

We will gather data on the use of resources and the related costs regarding health care (e.g., medical specialist, hand-physiotherapist, treatment of complications), productivity loss (absenteeism (sick leave) and presenteeism (lowered efficiency while at work due to sick leave from work and lowered productivity while a work)), and out-of-pocket expenses by patient and family members (e.g., health related travel, extra private help). The Dutch iMTA Medical Consumption Questionnaire and iMTA Productivity Cost Questionnaire will be adapted to and prepared for the current study setting and disseminated to patients for completion after 2, 3, 6, and 12 months of follow-up [8, 16]. Case report forms or electronic patient dossiers will be used to gather in-hospital data. Unit costing will be based on the most recent Dutch guideline on costing in health care research at the time of data analysis. Costing of productivity losses will adhere to the friction costs approach with the current friction period at time of analysis [16]. The base year for costing will be 2023 with unit costs price indexed if originating from other calendar years.

Patient outcome analysis

Patient outcome analysis in addition to the clinical outcome measures relevant to patients (disability, pain, hand function) in the economic evaluation will include the measurement of health status at baseline, 2, 3, 6, and 12 months after injury with the EQ-5D-5L [18]. This questionnaire consists of 5 items, measuring (at 5-point scales) whether patients experience problems and, if so, to what extent with regard to mobility, self-care, daily activities, pain/complaints, and mood. Existing health valuation scoring algorithms (Dutch reference values available through www.euroqol.org) will be used to assign a health utility to each 5-items score pattern. The number of QALYs for each patient will subsequently be calculated by interpolation between successive measurement and determining the area under the curve for the follow-up period of 12 months.

Discussion

This research article describes the protocol of a multicenter non-inferiority randomized controlled trial. The study aims to investigate functional outcomes and cost-effectiveness of cast immobilization, followed by operative treatment only in cases of persistent laxity after 2–3 weeks of cast immobilization, compared to immediate operative treatment followed by cast immobilization for complete UCL ruptures, including Stener lesions. Currently, treatment recommendations are based on expert opinion, anatomic theories, and low-quality observational studies. This study aims to provide high-quality evidence to redefine the best treatment strategy.

The findings from this study could have a substantial impact on clinical practice in the treatment of complete UCL ruptures, should the results demonstrate that cast immobilization is as effective as immediate operative treatment. Presently, the prevailing approach favors immediate operative treatment, which is demanding on resources, healthcare personnel, and operating room availability in times of chronic and increasing shortages. Transitioning to a treatment strategy where surgery is reserved only for cases showing persistent laxity after initial conservative treatment could have several benefits. This approach may reduce the burden on operating room availability and healthcare personnel and lead to shorter wait times for patients. Additionally, conservative treatment could decrease healthcare costs associated with surgery, freeing up resources for other areas of patient care. Moreover, patients could directly benefit from avoiding operative treatments, even though the procedure is minor, as a surgical intervention is inherently more invasive than a conservative approach. Reducing unnecessary surgical interventions, when conservative treatment has shown to be as effective, can enhance healthcare accessibility and sustainability without compromising functional outcomes. By investigating both the clinical efficacy and cost-effectiveness of cast immobilization for UCL ruptures, this study supports the broader goal of delivering efficient, high-quality care with limited present and future resources. The project will take 4 years, from preparation to reporting of the results. Collaboration with the Dutch hand surgery committees (Netherlands Society for Surgery and Netherlands Society for Plastic Surgery), health insurance companies, and medical experts will ensure the successful implementation of these findings, potentially setting a new standard in the treatment of UCL ruptures and contributing to the overall resilience of the healthcare system.

Trial status

The first participant was enrolled in February 2022 (protocol version: V3, dated 21 January 2022). The aim was to recruit the final patient within 30 months. Currently, 83 out of 126 patients have been included. Recruitment is expected to be completed by August 2025. The publication of the protocol was postponed due to new literature becoming available, which allowed for a more accurate sample size calculation. This adjustment required approval from our METC and the funding party, ZonMW.
