Exact new mobility edges
Yongjian Wang, Qi Zhou

Abstract
. Mobility edges (ME), defined as critical energies that separate the extended states from the localized states, are a significant topic in quantum physics. In this paper, we demonstrate the existence of two exact new mobility edges for two physically realistic models: the first, referred to as Type II ME, represents the critical energy that separates the critical states from localized states; the second, referred to as Type III ME, marks the critical energy that separate the critical states from extended states. The proof is based on spectral analysis of singular Jacobi operator on the strip.



1.
Introduction

Anderson localization is a fundamental quantum phenomenon characterized by pure point spectrum, with eigenfunctions that exhibit exponential decay. In 1D and 2D disordered systems, all quantum states are localized, irrespective of the disorder strength. However, in 3D systems, localized and extended states can coexist across different energy levels
[
1
]
. A critical energy, known as the
mobility edge
(ME), as defined by Mott, separates localized states from extended states, illustrating the coexistence of these states within the system. ME is central to the Anderson metal-insulator transition, a phenomenon of great significance in condensed matter physics. Currently, ME remains one of the most prominent research topics in the field, serving as a fundamental aspect for numerous key problems
[
38
]
. Despite its critical importance, a rigorous proof of the ME remains an open question
[
79
]
. This question is so vital that Simon
[
79
]
included it as Problem 1 in his list of Schrödinger operator problems for the twenty-first century.

A breakthrough in this area was achieved through the manipulation of ultra-cold atoms, which provided a novel and well-controlled tool for directly observing the mobility edge
[
16
,
76
]
. Consequently, there is a growing interest in exploring the mobility edge in 1D quasiperiodic models, particularly concerning its exact characterization to better understand the extended-localized transition and to advance the detailed study of fundamental mobility edge physics
[
77
,
87
]
.

Moreover, quasiperiodic systems can host a third type of state known as critical states, which correspond to singular continuous spectrum in spectral theory. These critical states are fundamentally distinct from the localized and extended states observed in spectral statics
[
44
]
, multifractal properties
[
32
]
, and dynamical evolution
[
62
]
. However, the underlying mechanism of critical states remains enigmatic, especially in light of their possible coexistence with localized and extended states. To clarify this, we refer to the traditional mobility edge as Type I ME and introduce the following:

•

Type II ME: the critical energy separating critical states (singular continuous spectrum) from localized states (Anderson localization).

•

Type III ME: the critical energy separating critical states (singular continuous spectrum) from extended states (absolutely continuous spectrum).

Once we introduce this, the question is whether there are some (physical) quasiperiodic models that exhibit exact Type II and Type III ME? Furthermore, is there a universal mechanism to produce these MEs? In this paper, we will investigate this issue.

Main results

1.1.1.
Type I ME

As mentioned earlier, in the random case, the proof of the Type I ME itself remains unresolved. This is despite the significant progress made in establishing the existence of localization near the edges of the spectrum

In contrast, the quasiperiodic case has seen extensive investigation into the coexistence of absolutely continuous spectrum and Anderson localization. Here, the quasiperiodic models are defined as quasiperiodic Jacobi operator on

Indeed, mixed spectra are expected to be general phenomenon of one-frequency operators

1.1.2.
Type II ME

Type II ME can be traced back to Simon’s proposal of the random decaying model
[
64
]

In the quasiperiodic case, the Maryland model, defined by

[

58

59

Here,

We denote

[

To further elucidate the mechanism underlying Type II MEs and motivated by the mosaic model
[

introduced the following mosaic-type model, which also features exact Type II MEs:

,
A_{n}
is defined by
(T,A)^{n}=(T^{n},A_{n})
. Thus
A_{0}(x)=id
,

A_{n}(x)=\prod_{j=n-1}^{0}A(T^{j}x)=A(T^{n-1}x)\cdots A(Tx)A(x),\ for\ n\geq 1,

and
A_{-n}(x)=A_{n}(T^{-n}x)^{-1}
. The Lyapunov exponent is defined as

\quad L(T,A):=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\int_{X}\ln\|A_{n}(x)\|dx.

In this paper, we will consider the following two useful cocycles.

•

X=\mathbb{T}
and
T=R_{\alpha}
, where
R_{\alpha}\theta=\theta+\alpha
, then
(\alpha,A):=(R_{\alpha},A)
is a quasiperiodic cocycle.

•

X=\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}
and
T=T_{\alpha}
, where
T_{\alpha}(\theta,n)=(\theta+\alpha,n+1)
, then
(T_{\alpha},A)
defines an almost-periodic cocycle.

These dynamical system
(X,T)
is uniquely ergodic if
\alpha
is irrational
[
73
]
.

We say an
SL(2,\mathbb{R})
cocycle
(T,A)
is uniformly hyperbolic if, for every
x\in X
, there exists a continuous splitting
\mathbb{R}^{2}=E_{s}(x)\oplus E_{u}(x)
such that for every
n\geq 0
,

\quad\begin{split}|A_{n}(x)v(x)|&\leq Ce^{-cn}|v(x)|,\ v(x)\in E_{s}(x),\\
|A_{-n}(x)v(x)|&\leq Ce^{-cn}|v(x)|,\ v(x)\in E_{u}(x),\end{split}

for some constans
C,c>0
. Clearly, it holds that
A(x)E_{s}(x)=E_{s}(Tx)
and
A(x)E_{u}(x)=E_{u}(Tx)
for every
x\in X
, and if
(T,A)
is uniformly hyperbolic, then
L(T,A)>0
.

2.3.
Dynamical defined Jacobi operators.

Let
X
be a compact metric space,
(X,\nu,T)
be ergodic, and
v,c:X\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
is continuous. Then one can define the Jacobi operator on

\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
:

[\mathcal{H}_{c,v,x}u]_{n}=c(T^{n}x)u_{n+1}+c(T^{n-1}x)u_{n-1}+v(T^{n}x)u_{n},\ \ \forall x\in X.

It is well known that the spectrum of
\mathcal{H}_{c,v,x}
is a compact subset of
\mathbb{R}
, independent of
x
if
(X,T)
is minimal
[
29
]
, we shall denote it by
\sigma^{\mathcal{H}}(c,v)
.
The integrated density of states (IDS)
N_{v,c}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow[0,1]
of
\mathcal{H}_{c,v,x}
is defined as

N_{v,c}(E)=\int_{X}\tilde{\mu}_{v,c,x}(-\infty,E]d\nu,

where
\tilde{\mu}_{v,c,x}
is the canonical spectral measure of
\mathcal{H}_{c,v,x}
. Note any formal solution
u=\{u_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
of
\mathcal{H}_{c,v,x}u=Eu
can be rewritten as

\begin{pmatrix}u_{n+1}\\
u_{n}\end{pmatrix}=S_{E}^{v,c}(T^{n}x)\begin{pmatrix}u_{n}\\
u_{n-1}\end{pmatrix},

where

S_{E}^{v,c}(\theta)=\frac{1}{c(\theta)}\begin{pmatrix}E-v(\theta)&-c(T^{-1}\theta)\\
c(\theta)&0\end{pmatrix}=:\frac{1}{c(\theta)}\widetilde{S}_{E}^{v,c}(\theta),

and we call
(T,S_{E}^{v,c})
the Jacobi cocycle.
The Thouless formula
[
83
]
links
N_{v,c}(E)
and
L(T,S_{E}^{v,c})
:

L(T,S_{E}^{v,c})=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\ln|E-E^{\prime}|dN_{v,c}(E^{\prime})-\mathbb{E}(\ln|c(x)|).

2.4.
Global theory of one frequency quasiperiodic cocycle.

Let us make a short review of Avila’s global theory of one-frequency quasiperiodic cocycles
[
5
]
. Suppose that
D\in C^{\omega}({\mathbb{T}},M(2,{\mathbb{C}}))
admits a holomorphic extension to
\{|\Im\theta|<h\}
. Then for

|\epsilon|<h
, we define
D_{\epsilon}\in C^{\omega}({\mathbb{T}},M(2,{\mathbb{C}}))
by
D_{\epsilon}(\cdot)=A(\cdot+i\epsilon)
, and define the acceleration of
(\alpha,D_{\varepsilon})
as follows

\omega(\alpha,D_{\varepsilon})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\lim_{h\to 0+}\frac{L(\alpha,D_{\varepsilon+h})-L(\alpha,D_{\varepsilon})}{h}.

The acceleration was first introduced by Avila for analytic
SL(2,{\mathbb{C}})
-cocycles
[
5
]
, and extended to analytic
M(2,{\mathbb{C}})
cocycles by Jitomirskaya-Marx
[
55
]
. It follows from the convexity and continuity of the Lyapunov exponent that the acceleration is an upper semicontinuous function in parameter
\varepsilon
. The key property of the acceleration is that it is quantized:

Theorem 2.2

(Quantization of acceleration
[
5
,
55
,
56
]
)
.

Suppose that
(\alpha,D)\in(\mathbb{R}\backslash\mathbb{Q})\times C^{\omega}(\mathbb{T},M_{2}(\mathbb{C}))
with
\det D(\theta)
bound away from 0 on the strip
\{|\Im\theta|<h\}
, then
\omega(\alpha,D_{\varepsilon})\in\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{Z}
in the strip. Moreover, if
D\in C^{\omega}(\mathbb{T},SL(2,{\mathbb{C}}))
, then

\omega(\alpha,D_{\varepsilon})\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Analytic one-frequency
SL(2,{\mathbb{R}})
cocycles that are not uniformly hyperbolic can be classified into several categories:
it is subcritical if
L(\alpha,A)=0
and
\omega(\alpha,A)=0
, it is supercritical if
L(\alpha,A)>0
, and critical if
L(\alpha,A)=0
and
\omega(\alpha,A)>0

[
5
]
.

Theorem 2.3
.

[
2
,
6
]

Let
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}\backslash{\mathbb{Q}}
and
A\in C^{\omega}({\mathbb{T}},SL(2,{\mathbb{R}}))
be such that
(\alpha,A)
is subcritical. Then
(\alpha,A)
is almost reducible: There exist
\delta>0
, a constant
A_{*}\in SL(2,{\mathbb{R}})
and a sequence of analytic maps
B_{n}\in C^{\omega}({\mathbb{T}},PSL(2,{\mathbb{R}}))
such that
\sup_{|\Im x|<\delta}\|B_{n}(x+\alpha)A(x)B_{n}(x)^{-1}-A_{*}\|\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.

3.
New exact mobility edges of critical and localized states

In this section, we aim to prove
\mathcal{H}_{c,v,\theta}
defined by (

) has exact Type II ME.

3.1.
Explicit formulas of LE of
\mathcal{H}

To do this, the first step is to determine its Lyapunov exponent.
Note in (
2
),
v
and
c
are defined on
\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, consequently (
2
) induces an almost-periodic Jacobi cocycle
(T_{\alpha},S_{E}^{v,c})
where
T_{\alpha}(\theta,n)=(\theta+\alpha,n+1)
. Although
(T_{\alpha},S_{E}^{v,c})
is not a quasiperiodic cocycle in the strict sense, its iterate

\begin{split}(2\alpha,\tilde{A}^{E})&:=(2\alpha,S_{E}^{v,c}(\theta,1)S_{E}^{v,c}(\theta,0))\end{split}

indeed defines a quasiperiodic cocycle. Furthermore, through straightforward calculation, we find

(7)

\begin{split}\tilde{A}^{E}(\theta)=\frac{1}{\lambda\cos 2\pi\theta}\begin{pmatrix}E^{2}-2E\cos 2\pi\theta&-\lambda E+\lambda\cos 2\pi\theta\\
\lambda E-\lambda\cos 2\pi\theta&-\lambda^{2}\end{pmatrix}=:\frac{1}{\lambda\cos 2\pi\theta}\tilde{B}^{E}(\theta).\end{split}

Lemma 3.1
.

For any
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}\backslash{\mathbb{Q}}
,
L(2\alpha,\tilde{A}^{E}_{\epsilon})
is a continuous function with respect to the parameter
\epsilon
.

Proof.

Obviously,
(2\alpha,\tilde{B}^{E})
is an analytic cocycle, which implies
L(2\alpha,\tilde{B}^{E}_{\epsilon})
is continuous in
\epsilon

[
5
]
.
Denote
I_{\epsilon}=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\ln|\lambda\cos 2\pi(\theta+i\epsilon)|d\theta
, and we have

L(2\alpha,\tilde{A}^{E}_{\epsilon})=L(2\alpha,\tilde{B}^{E}_{\epsilon})-I_{\epsilon}.

Applying Poisson-Jensen formula yields
I_{\epsilon}=2\pi|\epsilon|+\ln|\lambda/2|\text{ for all }\epsilon,

which implies the continuity of
L(2\alpha,\tilde{A}^{E}_{\epsilon})
in
\epsilon
.
∎

As
\tilde{B}^{E}_{\epsilon}
(
\epsilon\neq 0
) is non-singular, we can calculate the Lyapunov exponent
L(2\alpha,\tilde{A}^{E}_{\epsilon})
for
|\epsilon|>0
, and obtain the Lyapunov exponent for
\epsilon=0
by Lemma
3.1
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Suppose
\lambda\neq 0
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R\backslash Q}
. For
E\in\sigma^{\mathcal{H}}(c,v)
, the Lyapunov exponent can be expressed as

L(T_{\alpha},S_{E}^{v,c})=\frac{1}{2}\ln\left|\frac{|E|+\sqrt{E^{2}-\lambda^{2}}}{\lambda}\right|.

Proof.

Let us begin by considering the behavior of
\tilde{A}^{E}(\theta+i\epsilon)
uniformly in
\theta\in\mathbb{T}
. As
\epsilon\to+\infty
, we have

\tilde{A}^{E}(\theta+i\epsilon)=\tilde{A}^{E}_{\infty}+o(1):=\frac{1}{\lambda}\begin{pmatrix}-2E&\lambda\\
-\lambda&0\end{pmatrix}+o(1).

The continuity of the Lyapunov exponent implies that

L(2\alpha,\tilde{A}^{E}_{\epsilon})=L(2\alpha,\tilde{A}^{E}_{\infty})+o(1),\quad\text{as}\quad\epsilon\to+\infty

By applying the quantization of acceleration
[
5
]
, we deduce that

L(2\alpha,\tilde{A}^{E}_{\epsilon})=L(2\alpha,\tilde{A}^{E}_{\infty})
for all
\epsilon>0
sufficiently large. The convexity, continuity, and symmetry of
L(2\alpha,\tilde{A}^{E}_{\epsilon})
with respect to
\epsilon
further imply that

L(2\alpha,\tilde{A}^{E}_{\epsilon})=L(2\alpha,\tilde{A}^{E}_{\infty})
for all
\epsilon
. Consequently, we find that

L(2\alpha,\tilde{B}^{E}_{\epsilon})=L(2\alpha,\tilde{A}^{E}_{\infty})+2\pi|\epsilon|+\ln|\lambda/2|\text{ for all }\epsilon.

Note that the constant matrix
\tilde{A}^{E}_{\infty}
possesses two eigenvalues
\frac{-E\pm\sqrt{E^{2}-\lambda^{2}}}{\lambda}
, the result follows. ∎

Define

\sigma_{\text{sup}}^{\mathcal{H}}(c,v)=\sigma^{\mathcal{H}}(c,v)\backslash[-\lambda,\lambda],

and

\sigma_{\text{cri}}^{\mathcal{H}}(c,v)=\sigma^{\mathcal{H}}(c,v)\cap(-\lambda,\lambda).

By Lemma
3.2
, the Lyapunov exponent is positive on
\sigma_{\text{sup}}^{\mathcal{H}}(c,v)
and zero on
\sigma_{\text{cri}}^{\mathcal{H}}(c,v)
. Then, we have the following elementary observations:

Lemma 3.3
.

For all irrational
\alpha
,
\sigma_{\text{sup}}^{\mathcal{H}}(c,v)\neq\emptyset
.

Proof.

Otherwise, for any
E\in\sigma^{\mathcal{H}}(c,v)
, we have
|E|\leq\lambda
.
Note that

\begin{split}\mathcal{H}_{c,v,\theta}\delta_{n}=&v(\theta,n)\delta_{n}+c(\theta,n-1)\delta_{n-1}+c(\theta,n)\delta_{n+1},\\
\mathcal{H}_{c,v,\theta}^{2}\delta_{n}=&v(\theta,n)(v(\theta,n)\delta_{n}+c(\theta,n-1)\delta_{n-1}+c(\theta,n)\delta_{n+1})\\
&+c(\theta,n-1)(v(\theta,n-1)\delta_{n-1}+c(\theta,n-2)\delta_{n-2}+c(\theta,n-1)\delta_{n})\\
&+c(\theta,n)(v(\theta,n+1)\delta_{n+1}+c(\theta,n)\delta_{n}+c(\theta,n+1)\delta_{n+2}).\end{split}

For any
\theta,
select
n
such that
v(\theta,n)\neq 0
. By the spectral theorem, one has

\lambda^{2}<v^{2}(\theta,n)+c^{2}(\theta,n-1)+c^{2}(\theta,n)=\langle\delta_{n},\mathcal{H}_{c,v,\theta}^{2}\delta_{n}\rangle=\int E^{2}d\tilde{\mu}_{\theta,\delta_{n}}\leq\lambda^{2},

which leads to a contradiction.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

For all irrational
\alpha
,
\theta\in\mathbb{T}_{0}
,
\pm\lambda
are not eigenvalues of
\mathcal{H}_{c,v,\theta}
.

Proof.

Assume that

is a non-trivial solution of

. We have the following equations

\cos 2\pi(\theta+2n\alpha)\ u_{2n}+\cos 2\pi(\theta+2n\alpha)\ u_{2n+1}+\lambda u_{2n-1}=Eu_{2n},

and

\cos 2\pi(\theta+2n\alpha)\ u_{2n+1}+\lambda u_{2n+2}+\cos 2\pi(\theta+2n\alpha)\ u_{2n}=Eu_{2n+1}.

Let
t:=E/\lambda
, then
t=\pm 1
. We can rewrite the above equations as

(8)

\cos 2\pi(\theta+2n\alpha)(u_{2n}+u_{2n+1})+\lambda u_{2n-1}=Eu_{2n},

and

(9)

t(u_{2n+1}-u_{2n})=u_{2n+2}-u_{2n-1}.

If
t=-1
, summing equation (
9
) over
n
from
m
to
\infty
, we obtain

(10)

u_{2m}+u_{2m-1}=0,\ \forall m\in\mathbb{Z}

since

. Substituting the above result into (
8
), we have

\cos 2\pi(\theta+2n\alpha)(u_{2n}+u_{2n+1})=-\lambda(u_{2n}+u_{2n-1})=0.

Since
\theta\in\mathbb{T}_{0}
, it follows that

(11)

u_{2n}+u_{2n+1}=0,\ \forall n\in\mathbb{Z}.

Combining equations (
10
) and (

), we observe that
|u_{n}|
is constant for all

, which leads to a contradiction.

If
t=1
, summing equation (
9

n
from
m
to
\infty

u_{2m}-u_{2m-1}=-2\sum_{k=m+1}^{\infty}(u_{2k}-u_{2k-1})=:-2S_{m+1},

Taking the limit as

tends to
\pm\infty

. Note

it follows that

for all

. Thus, we conclude
u_{2m}=u_{2m-1},\ \forall m\in\mathbb{Z}.
Substituting the above equation into (

), we obtain

\cos 2\pi(\theta+2n\alpha)(u_{2n}+u_{2n+1})=0.

Therefore, we have
u_{2n}+u_{2n+1}=0,\ \forall n\in\mathbb{Z}.

One has
|u_{n}|
is constant for all

, leading to a contradiction. ∎

Once we have this, we obtain the following consequence:

.

For all irrational
\alpha
,
\pm\lambda\in\sigma^{\mathcal{H}}(c,v)
. Moreover,
\sigma_{\text{cri}}^{\mathcal{H}}(c,v)\neq\emptyset

We just prove

Now assume

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{c,v,\theta}w=\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{2}}(\delta_{n+2}-\delta_{n-1})

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{c,v,\theta}^{2}w=

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{2}}(c(\theta,n+2)\delta_{n+3}+\lambda\delta_{n+1}+v(\theta,n+2)\delta_{n+2})

\displaystyle+\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{2}}(-\lambda\delta_{n}-c(\theta,n-2)\delta_{n-2}-v(\theta,n-1)\delta_{n-1}),

which implies

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}=\langle w,\mathcal{H}_{c,v,\theta}^{2}w\rangle=\int E^{2}d\tilde{\mu}_{\theta,w}\geq\lambda^{2}.

Then, it follows that

\displaystyle E^{2}-\lambda^{2}=0\text{ for }\tilde{\mu}_{\theta,w}\text{-a.e. }E\in\sigma^{\mathcal{H}}(c,v),

and so

∎

3.2.
Singular continuous spectrum of
\mathcal{H}

Having established the coexistence of the supercritical regime (
\sigma^{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{sup}}(c,v)
) and critical regime
(\sigma^{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{cri}}(c,v))

a.e.\ \theta\in{\mathbb{T}}
. To achieve this, we first rule out the possibility of an absolutely continuous spectrum, as detailed in the following proposition:

Proposition 3.6

(Proposition 7.1 in
[
55
]
)
.

Let
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}\backslash{\mathbb{Q}}

[\mathcal{H}_{c,v,\theta}u]_{n}=c(\theta+n\alpha)u_{n+1}+\bar{c}(\theta+(n-1)\alpha)u_{n-1}+v(\theta+n\alpha)u_{n}.\

If

has a real root, then the absolutely continuous spectrum is empty.

Then, we have the following direct consequence:

Corollary 3.7
.

We have
\left|\sigma^{\mathcal{H}}(c,v)\cap[-\lambda,\lambda]\right|=0

Proof.

By Lemma
3.2
, for any

\left|\sigma(\mathcal{H}_{c,v,\theta})\cap[-\lambda,\lambda]\right|=0
. Note that

is independent of

and coincides with
\sigma^{\mathcal{H}}(c,v)
.
∎

Next, we demonstrate the absence of point spectrum in the critical regime

A point

For any irrational

-rational

Proof.

By Proposition
3.6
, it suffices to show that there is no point spectrum component for all non-
2\alpha
-rational
\theta\in{\mathbb{T}}_{0}

(12)

\begin{split}&D(x):=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\lambda(t^{2}-1)}{t+\cos 2\pi(x+2\alpha)}&-\frac{t+\cos 2\pi(x-2\alpha)}{t+\cos 2\pi(x+2\alpha)}\\

e^{-2\pi i\theta}g(x-2\alpha)&e^{2\pi i\theta}g(-x+2\alpha)\end{pmatrix},\\
&d(x):=t+\cos 2\pi x,\quad R_{\theta}=\begin{pmatrix}e^{2\pi i\theta}&0\\
0&e^{-2\pi i\theta}\end{pmatrix}.\end{split}

Thus,
M_{\theta}(x)\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T},M(2,\mathbb{C})),
and the equation can be rewritten as

(16)

D(x)M_{\theta}(x)=M_{\theta}(x+2\alpha)R_{\theta}.

Consider the set
\mathbb{T}_{t}:=\left\{x\in\mathbb{T}:t+\cos 2\pi(n\alpha+x)\neq 0,\forall n\in{\mathbb{Z}}\right\}
. Clearly, we have
|\mathbb{T}_{t}|=1
.
For any
x\in\mathbb{T}_{t}
, it follows from (
16
) that

|\det M_{\theta}(x)|\cdot|d(x-2\alpha)|=|\det M_{\theta}(x+2\alpha)|\cdot|d(x+2\alpha)|.

Using the ergodicity of irrational rotations, we deduce that

|\det M_{\theta}(x)|\cdot|d(x-2\alpha)|\cdot|d(x)|\overset{\text{a.e. x}}{=}b,\text{ for some }b\geq 0.

Since
d(x)\neq 0
on
\mathbb{T}_{t}
, it follows that
b>0
if and only if
\det M_{\theta}(x)\neq 0
for almost every
x
. Moreover, note that the set
\{x\in\mathbb{T}_{t}:\det M_{\theta}(x)=0\}
is invariant under rotations. Consequently, its measure must be either zero or one.

Assume
\det M_{\theta}(x)=0
for almost every
x
. Then, there exists a function
\phi(x)
such that, for almost every
x
,

(17)

\begin{pmatrix}g(x)\\
e^{-2\pi i\theta}g(x-2\alpha)\end{pmatrix}=\phi(x)\begin{pmatrix}g(-x)\\
e^{2\pi i\theta}g(-x+2\alpha)\end{pmatrix}.

In particular,
\phi(x)=\frac{g(x)}{g(-x)}\in\overline{{\mathbb{C}}}
is a non-identically vanishing, measurable function on
\mathbb{T}
. Equation (
17
) further implies

\phi(x+2\alpha)=e^{-4\pi i\theta}\phi(x),\ a.e.\ x.

By ergodicity,
\phi(x)=b^{\prime}
for some
b^{\prime}\neq 0
, and
\phi(x)\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T})
. Writing
\phi(x)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\hat{\phi}_{n}e^{2\pi inx}
, it follows that

\hat{\phi}_{n}(e^{4\pi in\alpha+4\pi i\theta}-1)=0,\ \forall n\in\mathbb{Z}.
By assumption, we have excluded all
\theta
that are
2\alpha
-rational. Consequently,
\phi\equiv 0
, which leads to a contradiction.
Thus, for all non-
2\alpha
-rational
\theta\in{\mathbb{T}}_{0}
and almost every
x\in\mathbb{T}
, it holds that

(18)

|\det M_{\theta}(x)|=\frac{b}{|d(x-2\alpha)d(x)|},

for some constant
b>0
.

Since
|t|<1
, there exists
x_{0}\in\mathbb{T}
such that
d(x_{0})=0
. The presence of singularity implies
\frac{1}{d(x-2\alpha)d(x)}\notin L^{1}({\mathbb{T}})
. Consequently, by (
18
), we have

|\det M_{\theta}(x)|=\frac{b}{|d(x-2\alpha)d(x)|}\notin L^{1}(\mathbb{T}).

This contradicts
M_{\theta}(x)\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T},M(2,\mathbb{C}))
, thereby completing the proof.
∎

3.3.
Anderson Localization of
\mathcal{H}

In this section, we aim to prove that
\mathcal{H}_{c,v,\theta}
exhibits Anderson localization in the supercritical regime for almost every
\theta
:

Theorem 3.10
.

Suppose that
\alpha\in DC(\gamma,\sigma)
. Then
\mathcal{H}_{c,v,\theta}
exhibits Anderson localization in
\sigma^{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{sup}}(c,v)

for every
\theta\in\Theta
, where

\Theta=\cup_{\eta>0}\Theta(\eta):=\cup_{\eta>0}\left\{\theta\in{\mathbb{T}}_{0}:\|2\theta-k\alpha\|\geq\frac{\eta}{|k|^{\sigma}},\forall k\neq 0\right\}.

We follow the basic setup presented in
[
51
]
. Throughout the proof, we denote by

the Green’s function
(\mathcal{H}_{c,v,\theta}-E)^{-1}(n,m)
of the operator
\mathcal{H}_{c,v,\theta}
when restricted to the interval
[n_{1},n_{2}]
, with zero boundary conditions at
n_{1}-1
and
n_{2}+1
. For brevity, we will omit the explicit mention of the dependencies on
v
,

, and
\alpha
whenever possible.

By Lemma 1.8 in
[
83
]
, we have the inclusion

\sigma^{\mathcal{H}}(c,v)\subseteq\left[-\lambda-2,\lambda+2\right].

Let
M_{k}(\theta)
denote the
k
-step transfer matrix for the equation
\mathcal{H}_{c,v,\theta}u=Eu
, and define

P_{k}(\theta):=\det\left[(\mathcal{H}_{c,v,\theta}-E)|_{[0,k-1]}\right],\quad Q_{k}(\theta):=\det\left[(\mathcal{H}_{c,v,\theta}-E)|_{[1,k]}\right].

Then, the
k
-step transfer matrix can be written as

\displaystyle M_{k}(\theta)=\frac{(-1)^{k}}{\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}c(\theta,j)}\begin{pmatrix}P_{k}(\theta)&c(\theta,-1)Q_{k-1}(\theta)\\
-c(\theta,k-1)P_{k-1}(\theta)&-c(\theta,k-1)c(\theta,-1)Q_{k-2}(\theta)\end{pmatrix}=:\frac{(-1)^{k}}{\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}c(\theta,j)}\widetilde{M}_{k}(\theta),

where the second equality defines
\widetilde{M}_{k}(\theta)
. This expression can be derived by expanding the determinant of
(\mathcal{H}_{c,v,\theta}-E)|_{[0,k-1]}
along its final row and then using induction on

By Kingman’s subadditive ergodic theorem, the Lyapunov exponent satisfies

L(E)=\inf_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{n}\int_{\mathbb{T}_{0}}\ln\|M_{n}(\theta)\|d\theta=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\ln\|(M_{n}(\theta)\|,

for almost every
\theta\in\mathbb{T}_{0}
.

Observe that if

\begin{split}0<L(E)&=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{k}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}_{0}}\ln\|\widetilde{M}_{k}(\theta)\|d\theta-\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{k}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}_{0}}\ln|c(\theta,0)\cdots c(\theta,k-1)|d\theta\\
&=:\widetilde{L}(E)-\frac{1}{2}\ln\left|\frac{\lambda}{2}\right|,\end{split}

where both limits exist by the subadditive ergodic theorem. Furthermore, we obtain the relation

(19)

\widetilde{L}(E)=L(E)+\frac{1}{2}\ln\left|\frac{\lambda}{2}\right|=\inf_{k\geq 1}\frac{1}{k}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}_{0}}\ln\|\widetilde{M}_{k}(\theta)\|d\theta=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{k}\ln\|\widetilde{M}_{k}(\theta)\|,

for any fixed
E
and almost every
\theta\in{\mathbb{T}}_{0}
.

Moreover, we recall the following result:

Proposition 3.11

(
[
9
]
)
.

Let

and

Then we can establish the uniform growth of the matrix

Lemma 3.12
.

For every

and

Proof.

Note that

∎

Now, consider the matrix

\displaystyle\widetilde{M}_{2k}(\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}P_{2k}(\theta)&c(\theta,-1)Q_{2k-1}(\theta)\\

(possible with different

Lemma 3.13
.

For any

and

\displaystyle(v(\theta,2k-2)-E)P_{2k-1}(\theta)=P_{2k}(\theta)+c^{2}(\theta,2k-2)P_{2k-2}(\theta),

\displaystyle(v(\theta,0)-E)Q_{2k-1}(\theta)=P_{2k}(\theta)+c^{2}(\theta,0)P_{2k-2}(\theta+2\alpha),

and

\begin{split}&(v(\theta,0)-E)(v(\theta,2k-2)-E)Q_{2k-2}(\theta)\\
&\quad=P_{2k}(\theta)+c^{2}(\theta,0)P_{2k-2}(\theta+2\alpha)+c^{2}(\theta,2k-2)P_{2k-2}(\theta)\\
&\quad\quad+c^{2}(\theta,2k-2)c^{2}(\theta,0)P_{2k-4}(\theta+2\alpha).\end{split}

Proof.

We begin by noting the recurrence relations for

and

v(\theta,2n)=v(\theta,2n+1)=c(\theta,2n),\quad c(\theta,2n+1)=\lambda,\quad v(\theta,n+2)=v(\theta+2\alpha,n).

Expanding the determinant of

\begin{split}&(v(\theta,2k-2)-E)P_{2k-1}(\theta)=P_{2k}(\theta)+c^{2}(\theta,2k-2)P_{2k-2}(\theta),\\
&Q_{2k-1}(\theta)=(v(\theta,2k-2)-E)Q_{2k-2}(\theta)-c^{2}(\theta,2k-2)Q_{2k-3}(\theta).\end{split}

Similarly, expanding the determinant of

From this, we derive:

\displaystyle(v(\theta,0)-E)Q_{2k-1}(\theta)=P_{2k}(\theta)+c^{2}(\theta,0)P_{2k-2}(\theta+2\alpha).

Substituting the expression for

\displaystyle(v

\displaystyle(\theta,0)-E)(v(\theta,2k-2)-E)Q_{2k-2}(\theta)

\displaystyle=P_{2k}(\theta)+c^{2}(\theta,0)P_{2k-2}(\theta+2\alpha)+c^{2}(\theta,2k-2)P_{2k-2}(\theta)

\displaystyle\quad+c^{2}(\theta,2k-2)c^{2}(\theta,0)P_{2k-4}(\theta+2\alpha).

We thus finish the whole proof.
∎

Lemma 3.14
.

For every
k\in 2{\mathbb{N}}
, there exists a polynomial
R_{\frac{k}{2}}
of degree
\frac{k}{2}
such that

P_{k}(\theta)=R_{\frac{k}{2}}\left(\cos 2\pi(\theta+(\frac{k}{2}-1)\alpha)\right).

Proof.

The eigenfunction equation
\mathcal{H}_{c,v,\theta}u=Eu

can be written as:

C\begin{pmatrix}u(2j+2)\\
u(2j+3)\end{pmatrix}+C^{*}\begin{pmatrix}u(2j-2)\\
u(2j-1)\end{pmatrix}+V_{j}(\theta)\begin{pmatrix}u(2j)\\
u(2j+1)\end{pmatrix}=E\begin{pmatrix}u(2j)\\
u(2j+1)\end{pmatrix},

where

\lambda&0\end{pmatrix},\quad V_{j}(\theta)=\cos 2\pi(2j\alpha+\theta)\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
1&1\end{pmatrix}.

For
k=2l
, the restriction of
\mathcal{H}_{c,v,\theta}
to
[0,k-1]
is given by

\mathcal{H}_{c,v,\theta}|_{[0,k-1]}=\begin{pmatrix}V_{0}(\theta)&C&O_{2}&\cdots&O_{2}\\
C^{*}&V_{1}(\theta)&C&\cdots&O_{2}\\
O_{2}&C^{*}&V_{2}(\theta)&\cdots&O_{2}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
O_{2}&O_{2}&O_{2}&\cdots&V_{l-1}(\theta)\end{pmatrix}_{2l\times 2l},

where

is the

Define the matrix
Q
as

Q=\begin{pmatrix}&&1\\
&\begin{sideways}$\ddots$\end{sideways}&\\
1&&\end{pmatrix}_{2l\times 2l}.

Applying the transformation
Q
to
\mathcal{H}_{c,v,\theta}|_{[0,k-1]}
, we find

\begin{split}&QH_{c,v,\theta-(l-1)\alpha}|_{[0,k-1]}Q^{-1}\\
&=\begin{pmatrix}V_{l-1}(\theta-(l-1)\alpha)&C&O_{2}&\cdots&O_{2}\\
C^{*}&V_{l-2}(\theta-(l-1)\alpha)&C&\cdots&O_{2}\\
O_{2}&C^{*}&V_{l-3}(\theta-(l-1)\alpha)&\cdots&O_{2}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\

which implies

(21)

P_{k}(\theta-(l-1)\alpha)=P_{k}(-\theta-(l-1)\alpha).

Thus, the Fourier expansion of
\theta\to P_{k}(\theta-(l-1)\alpha)
reads

since all the

terms are absent due to (

due to

where

. From this representation the lemma follows since one can see inductively that the linear span of

is equal to that of
\{1,\cos 2\pi x,\dots,\cos^{l}2\pi x\}
.
∎

When the Lyapunov exponent is positive, Lemma
3.12
implies that some of the entries must grow exponentially. These entries in turn appear in a description of Green’s function of the operator restricted to a finite interval. Namely, by Cramer’s Rule, if we denote

\Delta_{m,n}(\theta)=\det[(\mathcal{H}_{c,v,\theta}-E)|_{[m,n]}],

then for
n_{1},n_{2}=n_{1}+k-1
, and

(22)

\begin{split}&\left|G_{[n_{1},n_{2}]}(n_{1},n)\right|=\left|\frac{\Delta_{n+1,n_{2}}(\theta)}{\Delta_{n_{1},n_{2}}(\theta)}\prod_{l=n_{1}}^{n-1}c(\theta,l)\right|,\\
&\left|G_{[n_{1},n_{2}]}(n,n_{2})\right|=\left|\frac{\Delta_{n_{1},n-1}(\theta)}{\Delta_{n_{1},n_{2}}(\theta)}\prod_{l=n+1}^{n_{2}}c(\theta,l)\right|.\end{split}

A useful definition related to Green’s function is as follows:

Definition 3.15
.

Fix
E\in\mathbb{R}
and
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
. A point
n\in\mathbb{Z}
is called
(\xi,k)
-regular if there exists an interval
[n_{1},n_{2}]
, where
n_{2}=n_{1}+k-1
, containing
n
, such that

|G_{[n_{1},n_{2}]}(n,n_{i})|<e^{-\xi|n-n_{i}|},\quad\text{and}\quad|n-n_{i}|\geq\frac{1}{7}k,\quad i=1,2.

Otherwise,
n
is called
(\xi,k)
-singular.

It is well known that any formal solution
u
of the equation
\mathcal{H}_{c,v,\theta}=Eu
at a point

can be reconstructed from the boundary values via

(23)

u(n)=-c(\theta,n_{1}-1)u(n_{1}-1)G_{[n_{1},n_{2}]}(n,n_{1})-c(\theta,n_{2})u(n_{2}+1)G_{[n_{1},n_{2}]}(n,n_{2}).

This implies that if
u
is a generalized eigenfunction corresponding to
E
, then every point
n\in\mathbb{Z}
where
u(n)\neq 0
is
(\xi,k)
-singular for sufficiently large
k
. In the following, we assume
u_{0}\neq 0
(if not, we can replace
u_{0}
by

).

Proposition 3.16
.

[
51
]

Suppose
\alpha\in DC(\gamma,\sigma)
. If

is an analytic function,

Denote the set

Then we have the following result:

Lemma 3.17
.

Suppose

, and

We can express the polynomial

.

4.
Aubry duality

In this section, we aim to establish Aubry duality for singular Jacobi operators on the strip. As we mentioned, for the mosaic-type models, one can consider it as
singular Jacobi operators on the strip:

(27)

[\mathcal{S}_{C,V,\omega}\boldsymbol{u}](n)=C\boldsymbol{u}(n+1)+C^{*}\boldsymbol{u}(n-1)+V(\omega+2n\alpha)\boldsymbol{u}(n),

where
C
is a singular matrix. Indeed, direct computations show that

Example 1
.

(Mosaic model (
1
)) In this case, one can take

C_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},V_{1}(\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}2\lambda\cos 2\pi\theta&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}

Example 2
.

(Mosaic-type model (
2
)) In this case, one can take

C_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
\lambda&0\end{pmatrix},V_{2}(\theta)=\cos 2\pi\theta\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
1&1\end{pmatrix}

4.1.
Dynamical Aubry duality

Assume that

[\mathcal{S}_{C,V,\theta}\boldsymbol{u}](n)=E\boldsymbol{u}(n),

has an analytic quasiperiodic Bloch wave,

\boldsymbol{u}(n)=e^{2\pi in\omega}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}(2n\alpha+\theta),

where
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}:{\mathbb{T}}\to{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
being analytic and
\omega\in{\mathbb{T}}
the Floquet exponent. If
\{\boldsymbol{\psi}(n)\}_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
are the Fourier coefficients of
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}
, direct computation shows that they satisfy the following difference equation

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}V^{(k)}\boldsymbol{\psi}(n-k)+(Ce^{2\pi i(2n\alpha+\omega)}+C^{*}e^{-2\pi i(2n\alpha+\omega)})\boldsymbol{\psi}(n)=E\boldsymbol{\psi}(n),\quad n\in{\mathbb{Z}},

where
\{V^{(k)}\}_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
are the Fourier coefficients of
V
, i.e.
V(\theta)=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}V^{(k)}e^{2\pi ik\theta}.
In the following, we call

[\widehat{S}_{C,V,\omega}\boldsymbol{\psi}](n)=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}V^{(k)}\boldsymbol{\psi}(n-k)+(Ce^{2\pi i(2n\alpha+\omega)}+C^{*}e^{-2\pi i(2n\alpha+\omega)})\boldsymbol{\psi}(n)

the dual operator of
\mathcal{S}_{C,V,\theta}
. Direct computations show the following:

Example 3
.

(Dual operator of Mosaic model (
1
))

\displaystyle[\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{C_{1},V_{1},\omega}\boldsymbol{u}](n)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{u}(n+1)+\begin{pmatrix}\lambda&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{u}(n-1)+\begin{pmatrix}0&1+e^{-2\pi i(2n\alpha+\omega)}\\
1+e^{2\pi i(2n\alpha+\omega)}&0\end{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{u}(n),

Example 4
.

(Dual operator of Mosaic-type model (
2
))

\displaystyle[\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{C_{2},V_{2},\omega}\boldsymbol{u}](n)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
1&1\end{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{u}(n+1)+\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
1&1\end{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{u}(n-1)+\lambda\begin{pmatrix}0&e^{-2\pi i(2n\alpha+\omega)}\\
e^{2\pi i(2n\alpha+\omega)}&0\end{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{u}(n).

Moreover, let
U_{2}
be the following operator on
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}},{\mathbb{C}}^{2})

\displaystyle[U_{2}\boldsymbol{u}](n)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
1&-1\end{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{u}(n).

Then,
U_{2}^{*}\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{C_{2},V_{2},\omega}U_{2}
and
\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{C_{2},V_{2},\omega}
are unitarily equivalent,
thus share the same spectral types. Indeed, one can easily check that
U_{2}^{*}\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{C_{2},V_{2},\omega}U_{2}
is just Type III ME model (
3

(28)

\displaystyle[U_{2}^{*}\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{C_{2},V_{2},\omega}U_{2}\boldsymbol{u}](n)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{u}(n+1)+\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{u}(n-1)+\lambda\begin{pmatrix}\cos 2\pi(2n\alpha+\omega)&i\sin 2\pi(2n\alpha+\omega)\\
-i\sin 2\pi(2n\alpha+\omega)&-\cos 2\pi(2n\alpha+\omega)\end{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{u}(n).

Since the base dynamic is minimal, the spectrum of the long-range operators
\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{C,V,\omega}
does not depend on the chosen
\omega
, so that one can write
\sigma^{\widehat{\mathcal{S}}}(C,V)=\sigma(\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{C,V,\omega}).

4.2.
Spectral Aubry duality

To rigorously establish the Aubry duality, let us consider the following Hilbert space,

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}=L^{2}({\mathbb{T}}\times{\mathbb{Z}},{\mathbb{C}}^{2}),

which consists of functions
\boldsymbol{\Psi}=\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\theta,n)
satisfying

\displaystyle\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}\|\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\theta,n)\|^{2}d\theta<\infty.

The extensions of the singular operators
\mathcal{S}
and their long-range duals
\widehat{\mathcal{S}}
to
\mathbb{H}
are given in terms of their
direct integrals
, which we now define. The direct integral of the singular operator
\mathcal{S}_{C,V,\theta}
is the operator
\mathbf{S}_{C,V}
, defined as

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}_{C,V}=\int_{\mathbb{T}}^{\oplus}\mathcal{S}_{C,V,\omega}d\omega:\int_{\mathbb{T}}^{\oplus}\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}},{\mathbb{C}}^{2})d\omega\to\int_{\mathbb{T}}^{\oplus}\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}},{\mathbb{C}}^{2})d\omega,

and

[\mathbf{S}_{C,V}\boldsymbol{u}](\theta,n)=C\boldsymbol{u}(\theta,n+1)+C^{*}\boldsymbol{u}(\theta,n-1)+V(2n\alpha+\theta)\boldsymbol{u}(\theta,n).

Similarly, the direct integral of
\widehat{S}_{C,V,\omega}
denoted as
\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{C,V}
is

\displaystyle[\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{C,V}\boldsymbol{u}](\omega,n)=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}V^{(k)}\boldsymbol{\psi}(\omega,n-k)+(Ce^{2\pi i(2n\alpha+\omega)}+C^{*}e^{-2\pi i(2n\alpha+\omega)})\boldsymbol{\psi}(\omega,n).

These two operators are bounded and self-adjoint in
\mathbb{H}
.
Define the Aubry duality
\mathbf{A}_{2\alpha}
on
\mathbb{H}
as

\displaystyle[\mathbf{A}_{2\alpha}\boldsymbol{u}](\theta,n)=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\int_{\mathbb{T}}e^{-2\pi i(\theta+2n\alpha)k}e^{-2\pi in\eta}\boldsymbol{u}(\eta,k)d\eta.

Then, for any fixed real analytic
V
and non-resonant frequency
2\alpha
, the direct integrals
\mathbf{S}_{C,V}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{C,V}
are unitarily equivalent in the sense that the conjugation
\mathbf{S}_{C,V}\mathbf{A}_{2\alpha}=\mathbf{A}_{2\alpha}\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{C,V}

holds.
Since
\mathbf{A}_{2\alpha}
is an unitary operator, we have

\displaystyle\sigma(\int^{\oplus}_{\mathbb{T}}\mathcal{S}_{C,V,\theta}d\theta)=\sigma(\int^{\oplus}_{\mathbb{T}}\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{C,V,\theta}d\theta).

converges to a continuous, non-decreasing, function

, which is independent of
\omega
, and is called the integrated density of states (IDS) of the operator
\mathcal{S}_{\omega}
. In fact, the IDS is the distribution function of
d\mathrm{n}
. Moreover,
d\mathrm{n}
is also continuous and
\text{supp}(d\mathrm{n})=\sigma^{\mathcal{S}}(J,V)

[
14
]
.

5.3.
Thouless formula

Let
\mathcal{S}_{\omega}
be the operator defined in (
29
), and suppose that
\ln|z-f_{22}(\cdot)|\in L^{1}({\mathbb{T}})
for any
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
. Then,
the following Thouless formula holds for singular Jacobi operators on the strip:

Lemma 5.4

(Thouless formula)
.

For every
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}L(T,A^{z})+\frac{1}{2}{\mathbb{E}}(\ln|z-f_{22}(\cdot)|)=\int\ln|E-z|d\mathrm{n}(E).

Proof.

Denote
B^{z}(\omega):=(z-f_{22}(\omega))A^{z}(\omega)
, then
L(T,B^{z})=L(T,A^{z})+{\mathbb{E}}(\ln|z-f_{22}(\cdot)|)
.

Let us first consider
z\in{\mathbb{C}}\backslash{\mathbb{R}}
. According to (
38
), we have

\displaystyle B^{z}_{n}(\omega)=\begin{pmatrix}P_{n,\omega}(z)&-(z-f_{22}(T\omega))Q_{n-1,\omega}(z)\\
(z-f_{22}(T^{n}\omega))P_{n-1,\omega}(z)&-(z-f_{22}(T\omega))(z-f_{22}(T^{n}\omega))Q_{n-2,\omega}(z)\end{pmatrix}

for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, where
P_{n,\omega}(z)=\det(z-\mathcal{S}_{\omega}|_{[1,n]})
and
Q_{n,\omega}(z)=\det(z-\mathcal{S}_{T\omega}|_{[1,n]})
. Moreover, for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we have

\displaystyle P_{n,\omega}(z)=\prod_{j=1}^{2n}\left(z-E_{\omega,n}^{(j)}\right),\quad Q_{n,\omega}(z)=\prod_{j=1}^{2n}\left(z-E_{T\omega,n}^{(j)}\right),

where
E_{\omega,n}^{(1)},\dots,E_{\omega,n}^{(2n)}
denote the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{S}_{\omega}|_{[1,n]}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2n}\ln\left|P_{n,\omega}(z)\right|=\int\ln|E-z|d\mathrm{n}_{\omega,n}(E),

and

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2n}\ln\left|Q_{n,\omega}(z)\right|=\int\ln|E-z|d\mathrm{n}_{T\omega,n}(E).

Since
z\in{\mathbb{C}}\backslash{\mathbb{R}}
, the function
E\to\ln|E-z|
is bounded and continuous on
\sigma^{\mathcal{S}}(J,V)=\text{supp}(d\mathrm{n})
, so Proposition
5.3
yields

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{2n}\ln|P_{n,\omega}(z)|=\int\ln|E-z|d\mathrm{n}(E)

for almost every
\omega
. Since all norms on
2\times 2
matrices are equivalent, it holds that

(39)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}L(T,B^{z})=\int\ln|E-z|d\mathrm{n}(E)

for
z\in{\mathbb{C}}\backslash{\mathbb{R}}
.

Let us now consider
E\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Denoting the right-hand side of (
39
) by
\gamma(z)
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi r^{2}}\int_{|z-E|\leq r}\frac{1}{2}L(T,B^{z})dz=\frac{1}{\pi r^{2}}\int_{|z-E|\leq r}\gamma(z)dz

for every
r>0
, since the integrands agree on
B_{r}(E)\backslash{\mathbb{R}}
. Since both sides are subharmonic, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}L(T,B^{E})=\lim_{r\to 0}\frac{1}{\pi r^{2}}\int_{|z-E|\leq r}\frac{1}{2}L(T,B^{z})dz=\lim_{r\to 0}\frac{1}{\pi r^{2}}\int_{|z-E|\leq r}\gamma(z)dz=\gamma(E).

Thus, the equation (
39
) holds for every
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
.
∎

5.4.
Estimate of Lyapunov exponent

5.4.1.
Harmonic analysis argument

Deift and Simon
[
33
]
provide a harmonic function-based proof to estimate the lower bound of the Lyapunov exponent for Schrödinger cocycle. While their argument is feasible for single-chain Jacobi cocycles, it is only partially applicable to singular Jacobi cocycles on the strip. The key lies in constructing an analytic function derived from Thouless formula, defined as

\displaystyle F(z)=-2\cosh\Big(-2\int\ln(E-z)d\mathrm{n}(E)+\int\ln(V_{22}(\omega)-z)d\omega\Big),

where the branch of
\ln
is taken with
\ln(-1)=-\pi i
, and
\ln(z)
remains continuous in the region
\Im z\leq 0
. Denoting the argument by

\displaystyle\beta(z)=2\int\operatorname{arg}(z-E)d\mathrm{n}(E)-\int\operatorname{arg}(z-V_{22}(\omega))d\omega,

the Thouless formula implies that

\displaystyle F(z)=-2\cosh(-L(T,A^{z})+i\beta(z))=z-{\mathbb{E}}(V_{11}(\cdot))+O(|z|^{-1}),

uniformly as
|z|\to\infty
in the upper half-plane. Notably,
\Im F(z)=2\sin\beta(z)\sinh L(T,A^{z})
and
\Im z
are harmonic functions. To establish the inequality
\Im F(z)\geq\Im z
. It suffices to verify
\Im F(z)\geq\Im z-\epsilon
on the boundary of
D_{R,\epsilon}=\{z:|z|<R,\Im z>\epsilon\}
for large
R
and
\epsilon\to 0
. However, on the boundary segment where
\Im z=\epsilon
, the inequality requires
\beta(z)\in[0,\pi]
, which is not satisfied in this case. Indeed, if
\Re z<\inf V_{22}
and
\mathrm{n}(\Re z)>\frac{1}{2}
, we find that

\displaystyle\lim_{\Im z\to 0^{+}}\beta(z)=2\pi(1-\mathrm{n}(\Re z))-\pi=2\pi(\frac{1}{2}-\mathrm{n}(\Re z))<0.

So we introduce a hyperbolic geometry argument:

5.4.2.
Hyperbolic geometry argument

In the following, we consider the Schrödinger type cocycle

1&0\end{pmatrix}.

It is well-known result that if

, then
(\alpha,S^{\tilde{v}})
is uniformly hyperbolic
[
4
]
, our aim to estimate its Lyapunov exponent.

For matrices in
SL(2,{\mathbb{C}})
, an action on the Riemann Sphere

through Möbius transformations is defined as:

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\cdot z=\frac{az+b}{cz+d}.

For

\displaystyle Q=\frac{-1}{1+i}\begin{pmatrix}1&-i\\
1&i\end{pmatrix}.

The map

maps bijectively

to

, the real Lie group of matrices
\begin{pmatrix}u&\bar{v}\\
v&\bar{u}\end{pmatrix},u,v\in{\mathbb{C}}
such that

. Denote
\mathbb{D}
the unit disk of

, then we have the following:

Lemma 5.5
.

[
11
]

Suppose

. If
\mathring{A}(x)\cdot{\mathbb{D}}\subset{\mathbb{D}}_{e^{-\epsilon}}
for every

One can easily check that

, which implies that if

,

can’t uniformly contract the upper half-plane. To see the hyberbolicity, one way is to consider the open hemisphere of

centered on the line through

1\end{pmatrix}

[
4
]
, another way is to consider second iterate of the
Schrödinger type cocycle:

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
(\alpha,S^{\tilde{v}})\in{\mathbb{R}}\backslash{\mathbb{Q}}\times C^{0}({\mathbb{T}},SL(2,{\mathbb{C}}))
.
Assume that

,

where

Proof.

For simplicity, denote
\tilde{v}(x)=\xi=p+iq
,
\tilde{v}(x+\alpha)=\eta=s+it
, then the second iterate of the Schrödinger cocycle takes the form

\displaystyle B(x)=(S^{\tilde{v}})^{2}(x)=S^{\tilde{v}}(x+\alpha)S^{\tilde{v}}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}\eta\xi-1&-\eta\\
\xi&-1\end{pmatrix},

by assumptions,

,

, and let

.

We only need to calculate

\displaystyle L=\sup_{\zeta\in{\mathbb{D}}}\|\mathring{B}(x)\cdot\zeta\|_{0}=\sup_{r\in{\mathbb{R}}}\|QB(x)\cdot r\|_{0}.

Direct computation shows that

\displaystyle L^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{|(\eta\xi-1-i\xi)r-\eta+i|^{2}}{|(\eta\xi-1+i\xi)r-\eta-i|^{2}}

\displaystyle=1-\frac{4q(tq+1)r^{2}+4t(pr-1)^{2}}{[(sq+pt+p)^{2}+(-sp+tq+q+1)^{2}]r^{2}-2(ps^{2}+pt^{2}+2pt+p-s)r+[(t+1)^{2}+s^{2}]}

\displaystyle\leq 1-4\epsilon\frac{[\epsilon^{2}+1]r^{2}+(pr-1)^{2}}{[(3l^{2})^{2}+(4l^{2})^{2}]r^{2}+(6l^{3})^{2}+r^{2}+5l^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq 1-4\epsilon\frac{r^{2}+(pr-1)^{2}}{l^{6}(26r^{2}+41)}

\displaystyle\leq 1-\frac{4\epsilon}{41l^{6}}\frac{r^{2}+(pr-1)^{2}}{r^{2}+1}

\displaystyle=:1-\frac{4\epsilon}{41l^{6}}f(r,p).

The first inequality is due to
(sq+pt+p)^{2}+(-sp+tq+q+1)^{2}>0
and

\displaystyle 4(ps^{2}+pt^{2}+2pt+p-s)^{2}-4[(sq+pt+p)^{2}+(-sp+tq+q+1)^{2}][(t+1)^{2}+s^{2}]

\displaystyle=-4(q+t+2qt+qs^{2}+qt^{2}+1)^{2}<0.

Since

uniformly converges to

on

, there exists

such that

for every

is positive and continuous, it attains a minimum value on the compact set

\displaystyle\ln L\leq-\frac{2\epsilon}{41l^{6}}\min_{(r,p)\in[-R,R]\times[-l,l]}\{\frac{1}{2},f(r,p)\})=:-c(l)\epsilon.

This implies that

, by Lemma

∎

Therefore, there exists

Applying Corollary

\displaystyle\det(z-\mathcal{S}_{\omega}|_{[1,N-1]})=p(z)\prod_{\tiny\begin{matrix}\langle\boldsymbol{v}_{j},\boldsymbol{\delta}_{N}\rangle\neq 0,\\

\displaystyle\det(z-\mathcal{S}_{\omega}|_{[1,N]})

\text{for }S_{\omega}|_{[1,N]}\end{matrix}}(z-E_{N}^{(j)})\prod_{\tiny\begin{matrix}\langle\boldsymbol{v}_{j},\boldsymbol{\delta}_{N}\rangle\neq 0,\\

if

. Therefore, the meromorphic function

Recall that the singular Jacobi cocycle on the strip takes the form

\displaystyle A^{E}(\omega)=\begin{pmatrix}E-V_{11}(\omega)-\frac{|V_{12}(\omega)|^{2}}{E-V_{22}(\omega)}&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}.

thus if

, then actually
A^{E}\in C^{0}(\Omega,SL(2,{\mathbb{R}}))
, thus the fibered rotation number of the cocycle
(\alpha,A^{E})
is well-defined, here, with the help of Interlacing Lemma (Lemma

), we will try to establish the relationship between the integrated density of states and the fibered rotation number as follows:

Theorem 5.10
.

Suppose
(\Omega,\nu,T)
is uniquely ergodic. Denote
\rho(E)
the fibered rotation number of
(T,A^{E})
. Then, the following hold:

(1)

For
\sup V_{22}<E
, we have

\mathrm{n}(E)=1-\rho(E).

(2)

For
E<\inf V_{22}
, we have

\mathrm{n}(E)=\frac{1}{2}-\rho(E).

Proof.

Denote
\Delta_{n,\omega}(E)=\frac{\det(E-\mathcal{S}_{\omega}|_{[1,n]})}{\prod_{j=1}^{n}(E-V_{22,\omega}(j))}
, and

\displaystyle Z_{n,\omega}(E)=\begin{pmatrix}\Delta_{n}(E)\\
\Delta_{n-1}(E)\end{pmatrix},\qquad Z_{n,\omega}(E)=A^{E}(T^{n-1}\omega)Z_{n-1,\omega}(E),

It is clear that

\displaystyle Z_{n,\omega}(E)=A^{E}_{n}(\omega)\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix},\qquad Z_{n-1,\omega+\alpha}(E)=A^{E}_{n-1}(\omega+\alpha)\begin{pmatrix}0\\
-1\end{pmatrix}.

Thus we have

\displaystyle(Z_{n,\omega}(E),-Z_{n-1,\omega+\alpha}(E))=A_{n}^{E}(\omega)=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\det(E-\mathcal{S}_{\omega}|_{[1,n]})}{\prod_{j=1}^{n}(E-V_{22,\omega}(j))}&-\frac{\det(E-\mathcal{S}_{\omega}|_{[2,n]})}{\prod_{j=2}^{n}(E-V_{22,\omega}(j))}\\
&\\
\frac{\det(E-\mathcal{S}_{\omega}|_{[1,n-1]})}{\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}(E-V_{22,\omega}(j))}&-\frac{\det(E-\mathcal{S}_{\omega}|_{[2,n-1]})}{\prod_{j=2}^{n-1}(E-V_{22,\omega}(j))}\end{pmatrix}.

Consider a projective cocycle
F_{A^{z}}
on
\Omega\times\mathbb{S}^{1}
:

\displaystyle(\omega,\psi)\mapsto\left(\omega+\alpha,\frac{A^{z}(\omega)\psi}{\|A^{z}(\omega)\psi\|}\right).

It is possible to choose a continuous lift of
F_{A^{z}}
to
{\mathbb{R}}
. Suppose
\xi_{n,\omega}(E)
is a lift of
Z_{n,\omega}(E)
, then we have

•

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\xi_{n,\omega}(E)=\rho(E).

•

If we denote by

the path
t\mapsto\zeta_{n}(E)
(where
\zeta_{n}(E)
represents the complex-plane point corresponding to

), then we have

\displaystyle\xi_{n,\omega}(E_{2})-\xi_{n,\omega}(E_{1})=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma_{E_{1},E_{2}}}\frac{dz}{z}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{E_{1}}^{E_{2}}\frac{\zeta_{n}^{\prime}(E)}{\zeta_{n}(E)}dE.

Since IDS and the fibered rotation number are independent of the phase, it suffices to verify the following inequalities on the full-measure subset
\Omega_{0}

Lemma 5.11
.

(1) For
\sup V_{22}<E_{1}<E_{2}
and
\omega\in\Omega_{0}

\displaystyle\left|2\xi_{n,\omega}(E_{2})-2\xi_{n,\omega}(E_{1})+\#\left\{\sigma(\mathcal{S}_{\omega}|_{[1,n]})\cap[E_{1},E_{2}]\right\}\right|\leq 2,

(2) For

Proof.

(2) Similarly, it suffices to observe that if (for example)

6.
Subordinacy Theory

6.1.
Cyclic vectors and Green’s function

For
2\times 2
non-singular matrix-valued Jacobi operators,
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}},{\mathbb{C}}^{2})
can be divided into the direct sum of four cyclic subspaces. However, for singular matrix-valued Jacobi operators, such cyclic subspaces are unclear and may be even infinite. In this section, we study the spectrum outside
\operatorname{Ran}(V_{22})
. More precisely, if we denote
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}\delta_{n}
and
\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{n}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix}\delta_{n}
, then
we have the following proposition:

Proposition 6.1
.

Denote

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\delta}=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}:\operatorname{dist}(x,\operatorname{Ran}(V_{22}))\geq\delta\}

with
\delta>0
. Then, for any compactly supported
\boldsymbol{\phi}\in\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}},{\mathbb{C}}^{2})
, we have

\displaystyle\chi_{\mathcal{T}_{\delta}}(\mathcal{S})\boldsymbol{\phi}=\Big(\prod_{j=k}^{l}f_{n}(\mathcal{S})\Big)\Big[p(\mathcal{S})\chi_{\mathcal{T}_{\delta}}(\mathcal{S})\boldsymbol{\delta}_{0}+q(\mathcal{S})\chi_{\mathcal{T}_{\delta}}(\mathcal{S})\boldsymbol{\delta}_{1}\Big]

with suitable real polynomials
p,q
and
k,l\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Moreover,
\|f_{n}(\mathcal{S})\|\leq\frac{1}{\delta},\forall n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Proof.

Notice first that

[\mathcal{S}-V_{22}(n)]\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{n}=V_{12}(n)\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n},

and

\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}=\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n-1}+\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n+1}+V_{11}(n)\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}+V_{21}(n)\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{n}.

Since
\operatorname{dist}(\mathcal{T}_{\delta},\operatorname{Ran}(V_{22}))\geq\delta
, we have

\displaystyle\chi_{\mathcal{T}_{\delta}}(x)=\chi_{\mathcal{T}_{\delta}}(x)-\chi_{\mathcal{T}_{\delta}}(V_{22}(n))=(x-V_{22}(n))f_{n}(x),

where

\displaystyle f_{n}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{1}{x-V_{22}(n)}&\forall x\in\mathcal{T}_{\delta},\\
0&\text{else}.\end{array}\right.

Obviously,
|f_{n}(x)|\leq\frac{1}{\delta}
and
f_{n}(x)\chi_{\mathcal{T}_{\delta}}(x)=f_{n}(x)
. Since the function
f_{n}(x)
is bounded and can be approximated by polynomials,
f_{n}(\mathcal{S})
is well-defined, which implies

\displaystyle\chi_{\mathcal{T}_{\delta}}(\mathcal{S})\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{n}=V_{12}(n)f_{n}(\mathcal{S})\chi_{\mathcal{T}_{\delta}}(\mathcal{S})\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n},

and

\displaystyle\chi_{\mathcal{T}_{\delta}}(\mathcal{S})\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=(\mathcal{S}-V_{11}(n)-|V_{12}(n)|^{2}f_{n}(\mathcal{S}))\chi_{\mathcal{T}_{\delta}}(\mathcal{S})\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}-\chi_{\mathcal{T}_{\delta}}(\mathcal{S})\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n-1}

\displaystyle=f_{n}(\mathcal{S})\left\{\left[(\mathcal{S}-V_{11}(n))(\mathcal{S}-V_{22}(n))-|V_{12}(n)|^{2}\right]\chi_{\mathcal{T}_{\delta}}(\mathcal{S})\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}-(\mathcal{S}-V_{22}(n))\chi_{\mathcal{T}_{\delta}}(\mathcal{S})\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n-1}\right\}.

By induction, there are polynomials
p
and
q
with no more than
2n-2
degrees so that
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}=\left(\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}f_{n}(\mathcal{S})\right)[p(\mathcal{S})\boldsymbol{\delta}_{0}+q(\mathcal{S})\boldsymbol{\delta}_{1}]
for all
n>1
. It is similar for
n<0
. Taking finite linear combinations, the proposition follows.
∎

Hence, the sum of the spectral measures associated with
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{0}
and
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{1}
can serve as a canonical spectral measure on
\mathcal{T}_{\delta}
for
\mathcal{S}
, i.e.,
\mu=\mu_{\boldsymbol{\delta}_{0}}+\mu_{\boldsymbol{\delta}_{1}}
. Then,
\mu_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}|_{\mathcal{T}_{\delta}}
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\mu|_{\mathcal{T}_{\delta}}
for any
\boldsymbol{\phi}\in\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}},{\mathbb{C}}^{2})
, where
\mu|_{\mathcal{T}_{\delta}}=\mu(\cdot\cap\mathcal{T}_{\delta})
.
Meanwhile, we have the following elementary observation:

Lemma 6.2
.

Suppose
z\notin\{V_{22}(j):j\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}
, and
\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}
solve
\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{x}=z\boldsymbol{x}
. For any pair of solutions
\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}
, the Wronskian

W_{[\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}]}(n):=\langle\bar{\boldsymbol{v}}(n-1),J\boldsymbol{u}(n)\rangle_{{\mathbb{C}}^{2}}-\langle\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}(n-1),J\boldsymbol{v}(n)\rangle_{{\mathbb{C}}^{2}},

is independent of
n,z
. Moreover,
W_{[\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}]}
vanishes iff
\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}
are linearly dependent.

Proof.

It is a direct result by (
30
) and (
31
).
∎

Once we have this, we can introduce Green’s function.
For
\Im z>0
,
R(\mathcal{S},z)=(\mathcal{S}-zI)^{-1}
exists and is a bounded operator, then it is easy to check that there exists unique
\boldsymbol{u}^{+}_{z}
obeying

\displaystyle J\boldsymbol{u}_{z}^{+}(n+1)+J\boldsymbol{u}_{z}^{+}(n-1)+V(n)\boldsymbol{u}_{z}^{+}(n)

\displaystyle=z\boldsymbol{u}_{z}^{+}(n),

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}_{z}^{+}(0)=\left(a_{z}^{+}(0),b_{z}^{+}(0)\right)^{\mathsf{T}}

\displaystyle=\left(1,\frac{V_{21}(0)}{z-V_{22}(0)}\right)^{\mathsf{T}},

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\|\boldsymbol{u}_{z}^{+}(n)\|^{2}

\displaystyle<\infty.

Similarly, there exists unique
\boldsymbol{u}_{z}^{-}(n)
obeying

\displaystyle J\boldsymbol{u}_{z}^{-}(n+1)+J\boldsymbol{u}_{z}^{-}(n-1)+V(n)\boldsymbol{u}_{z}^{-}(n)

\displaystyle=z\boldsymbol{u}_{z}^{-}(n),

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}_{z}^{-}(0)=\left(a_{z}^{-}(0),b_{z}^{-}(0)\right)^{\mathsf{T}}

\displaystyle=\left(1,\frac{V_{21}(0)}{z-V_{22}(0)}\right)^{\mathsf{T}},

\displaystyle\sum_{n=-\infty}^{0}\|\boldsymbol{u}_{z}^{-}(n)\|^{2}

\displaystyle<\infty.

The Green’s function of
\mathcal{S}
is denoted by

G(n,m;z):=\langle\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n},(\mathcal{S}-z)^{-1}\boldsymbol{\delta}_{m}\rangle,

where
n,m\in\mathbb{Z},z\notin\sigma(\mathcal{S})
. Note that, in general, when considering the Green’s function, one should also consider the matrix elements

\langle\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n},(\mathcal{S}-z)^{-1}\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{m}\rangle
,
\langle\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{n},(\mathcal{S}-z)^{-1}\boldsymbol{\delta}_{m}\rangle
and
\langle\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{n},(\mathcal{S}-z)^{-1}\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{m}\rangle
. However, by Proposition
6.1
,
\chi_{\mathcal{T}_{\delta}}(\mathcal{S})\boldsymbol{\delta}_{0},\chi_{\mathcal{T}_{\delta}}(\mathcal{S})\boldsymbol{\delta}_{1}
form cyclic vectors in the subspace
\chi_{\mathcal{T}_{\delta}}(\mathcal{S})\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}},{\mathbb{C}}^{2})
. Consequently, to study the spectrum of
\mathcal{S}
in
\mathcal{T}_{\delta}
, it suffices to analyze the Green’s function elements
\langle\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n},(\mathcal{S}-z)^{-1}\boldsymbol{\delta}_{m}\rangle
. Indeed, direct computations show the following:

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
\Im z>0
, and
\boldsymbol{u}_{z}^{\pm}
be as above. Then we have

\displaystyle G(n,m;z)=\frac{a_{z}^{-}(n\land m)a_{z}^{+}(n\lor m)}{W_{[\boldsymbol{u}_{z}^{+},\boldsymbol{u}_{z}^{-}]}},

where
n\land m=\min(n,m),n\lor m=\max(n,m)
, and
W_{[\boldsymbol{u}_{z}^{+},\boldsymbol{u}_{z}^{-}]}
denotes the Wronskian.

Proof.

Due to the assumption of
z
,
\boldsymbol{u}_{z}^{-}
and
\boldsymbol{u}_{z}^{+}
must be linearly independent, so we may assume without loss of generality that

W_{[\boldsymbol{u}_{z}^{+},\boldsymbol{u}_{z}^{-}]}(1)=a_{z}^{+}(1)a_{z}^{-}(0)-a_{z}^{-}(1)a_{z}^{+}(0)=1.

Define

\boldsymbol{\phi}_{m}(n):=a_{z}^{-}(n\land m)a_{z}^{+}(n\lor m)\begin{pmatrix}1\\
\frac{V_{21}(n)}{z-V_{22}(n)}\end{pmatrix}.

Since
\boldsymbol{u}_{z}^{\pm}
are square-summable at
\pm\infty
, it follows that
\boldsymbol{\phi}_{m}\in\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}},{\mathbb{C}}^{2})
. If
n>m
, we have

[\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{m}](n)=z\boldsymbol{\phi}_{m}(n).

Similarly, we also have
[\mathcal{S}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{m}](n)=z\boldsymbol{\phi}_{m}(n)
whenever
n<m
. Finally, we observe that

[S\boldsymbol{\phi}_{m}](m)=z\boldsymbol{\phi}_{m}(m)+\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

Thus, we have that
(\mathcal{S}-z)\boldsymbol{\phi}_{m}=\boldsymbol{\delta}_{m}
, so
\boldsymbol{\phi}_{m}=(\mathcal{S}-z)^{-1}\boldsymbol{\delta}_{m}
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle[(\mathcal{S}-z)^{-1}\boldsymbol{\delta}_{m}](n)=\frac{a_{z}^{-}(n\land m)a_{z}^{+}(n\lor m)}{W_{[\boldsymbol{u}_{z}^{+},\boldsymbol{u}_{z}^{-}]}}\begin{pmatrix}1\\

Proof.

\|2\mathrm{n}(E)+\langle\tilde{n}_{j},\alpha\rangle\|_{\mathbb{T}}\leqslant 2\epsilon_{j-1}^{\frac{1}{15}}.

Moreover, we have

\sup_{0\leq s\leq\epsilon_{j-1}^{-\frac{1}{8}}}\|(S_{E}^{V,J})_{s}\|_{0}\leq 4\Gamma^{2}|\ln\epsilon_{j-1}|^{8\sigma}.

To study the regularity of the IDS, we need the following lemma, which is quoted from
[
9
]
:

Lemma 7.7
.

[
9
]

Let
\alpha\in DC
, if
(\alpha,A)
is analytically almost reducible, then for any
continuous map
B:\mathbb{T}\rightarrow SL(2,\mathbb{C}),
we have

|L(\alpha,A)-L(\alpha,B)|\leqslant C_{0}\|B-A\|_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}},

where
C_{0}
is a constant depending on
\alpha
.

Using this lemma, we can derive the following result:

Lemma 7.8
.

Assume that
\alpha\in DC(\gamma,\sigma)
. Then the integrated density of states
\mathrm{n}
is
\frac{1}{2}
-Hölder continuous on
\mathcal{W}(\delta_{0})
.

Proof.

Fix
\delta_{0}>0
, choose
0<\epsilon\ll\delta
. By Lemma
7.7
, we have

\left|L(\alpha,S_{E+i\epsilon}^{V,J})-L(\alpha,S_{E}^{V,J})\right|\leq C_{1}\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}.

On the other hand, Lemma
5.4
states that

\displaystyle L(\alpha,S_{E}^{V,J})=2\int\ln|E-E^{\prime}|d\mathrm{n}(E^{\prime})-{\mathbb{E}}(\ln|E-V_{22}(\cdot)|),

combining (
58
), this implies that for every
\epsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\left|L(\alpha,S_{E+i\epsilon}^{V,J})-L(\alpha,S_{E}^{V,J})\right|\geq

\displaystyle\int\ln\left(1+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{(E-E^{\prime})^{2}}\right)d\mathrm{n}(E^{\prime})+\frac{1}{2}\int\ln\left(1-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{(E-V_{22}(\omega))^{2}}\right)d\omega

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\int_{E-\epsilon}^{E+\epsilon}\ln\left(1+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{(E-E^{\prime})^{2}}\right)d\mathrm{n}(E^{\prime})-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{\delta^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle(\mathrm{n}(E+\epsilon)-\mathrm{n}(E-\epsilon))\ln 2-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{\delta^{2}},

which gives
\mathrm{n}(E+\epsilon)-\mathrm{n}(E-\epsilon)\leq C_{2}(\delta)\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}},

for sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
. Moreover,
\mathrm{n}
is locally constant in the complement of
\sigma^{\mathcal{S}}(J,V)
, which means precisely that
\mathrm{n}
is
\frac{1}{2}
-Hölder continuous on
\mathcal{W}(\delta_{0})
.
∎

As a consequence, we can establish a lower bound estimate for
\mathrm{n}
:

Lemma 7.9
.

For
\delta_{0}>0
small enough, if
E\in\mathcal{W}(\delta_{0})
, then there exists a constant
c(\delta_{0})>0
such that for sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
,
\mathrm{n}(E+\epsilon)-\mathrm{n}(E-\epsilon)\geq c\epsilon^{\frac{3}{2}},

where
c(\delta_{0})
is a small universal constant.

Proof.

The proof was originally developed by Avila
[
7
]
in the quasiperiodic Schrödinger operator case. We provide a sketch of the proof here for completeness. Let
\delta=c\epsilon^{3/2}
. For any
E\in\mathcal{W}(\delta_{0}),
we have
L(\alpha,S_{E}^{V,J})=0.
Then, by Lemma
5.4
, we obtain

\displaystyle L(\alpha,S_{E+i\delta}^{V,J})

\displaystyle=\int\ln\left(1+\frac{\delta^{2}}{|E-E^{\prime}|^{2}}\right)d\mathrm{n}(E^{\prime})-\frac{1}{2}\int\ln\left(1+\frac{\delta^{2}}{|E-V_{22}(\omega)|}\right)d\omega

\displaystyle\leq\int\ln\left(1+\frac{\delta^{2}}{|E-E^{\prime}|^{2}}\right)d\mathrm{n}(E^{\prime})-C_{1}\delta^{2},

where
C_{1}=\sup_{E\in\sigma^{\mathcal{S}}(J,V),\omega\in\Omega}|E-V_{22}(\omega)|/2
.
We now split the integral into four parts:
I_{1}=\int_{\left|E-E^{\prime}\right|\geq\frac{\delta_{0}}{2}}
,
I_{2}=\int_{\epsilon\leq\left|E-E^{\prime}\right|<\frac{\delta_{0}}{2}}
,
I_{3}=\int_{\epsilon^{4}\leq\left|E-E^{\prime}\right|<\epsilon}
and
I_{4}=\int_{\left|E-E^{\prime}\right|<\epsilon^{4}}
.

Clearly, we have
I_{1}<\frac{4c^{2}\epsilon^{3}}{\delta_{0}^{2}}
.
For sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
, by Lemma
7.8
we have

\begin{split}I_{4}=\sum_{k\geq 4}\int_{\epsilon^{k}>\left|E-E^{\prime}\right|\geq\epsilon^{k+1}}\ln\left(1+\frac{\delta^{2}}{\left|E-E^{\prime}\right|^{2}}\right)d\mathrm{n}(E^{\prime})\leq\sum_{k\geq 4}\epsilon^{\frac{k}{2}}\ln(1+c^{2}\epsilon^{1-2k})\leq 2\epsilon^{\frac{7}{4}}.\end{split}

We also have the estimate

\displaystyle I_{2}\leq\sum_{k=0}^{m}\int_{e^{-k-1}\leq|E-E^{\prime}|<e^{-k}}\ln\left(1+\frac{\delta^{2}}{\left|E-E^{\prime}\right|^{2}}\right)d\mathrm{n}(E^{\prime})\leq\sum_{k=0}^{m}e^{-\frac{k}{2}}\delta^{2}e^{2k+2}\leq C_{2}c^{2}\delta,

where
m=[-\ln\epsilon]
.
It follows that

\displaystyle I_{3}\geq L(\alpha,S_{E+i\delta}^{V,J})+C_{1}\delta^{2}-2\delta^{\frac{7}{6}}-C_{2}c^{2}\delta.

Since
\sigma^{\mathcal{S}}(J,V)
is bounded and
\inf_{E\in\mathcal{W}(\delta_{0})}|E-V_{22}(x)|\geq\delta_{0}
, there exist
l>0
such that

\displaystyle|\Re\left(z-V_{11}(x)-\frac{|V_{12}(x)|^{2}}{z-V_{22}(x)}\right)|\leq l

and

\displaystyle l>\Im z(1+\frac{\sup|V_{12}|}{\delta_{0}})>\Im\left(z-V_{11}(x)-\frac{|V_{12}(x)|^{2}}{z-V_{22}(x)}\right)=\Im z+\Im z\frac{|V_{12}(x)|^{2}}{|z-V_{22}(x)|}\geq\delta.

Applying Lemma
5.6
, we conclude that
L(\alpha,S_{E+i\delta}^{V,J})\geq C_{3}\delta
for sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
, where
C_{3}
is an universal constant. Since the constant
c
above is consistent with our choice of
\delta
, we can shrink it such that
I_{3}\geq\frac{C_{3}}{2}\delta
. Since
I_{3}\leq C(\mathrm{n}(E+\epsilon)-\mathrm{n}(E-\epsilon))\ln\epsilon^{-1},

the result follows.
∎

Proof of Theorem
7.2

Let
\mathcal{B}
be the set of
E\in\mathcal{W}(\delta_{0})
such that the cocycle
(\alpha,S_{E}^{V,J})
is bounded.
By the subordinary theory for singular Jacobi operator (Corollary
6.8
), it is enough to prove that

\mu_{\omega}(\mathcal{W}(\delta_{0})\backslash\mathcal{B})=0
for any
\omega\in{\mathbb{T}}
.

Let
\mathcal{R}
be the set of
E\in\mathcal{W}(\delta_{0})
such that
(\alpha,S_{E}^{V,J})
is reducible, then
\mathcal{R}\backslash\mathcal{B}
only contains
E
for which
(\alpha,S_{E}^{V,J})
is analytically reducible to a constant parabolic cocycle. Recall that for any
E\in\mathcal{W}(\delta_{0})
, by well-known result of Eliasson
[
36
]
, if

\rho(\alpha,S_{E}^{V,J})
is rational or Diophantine w.r.t
\alpha
, then
(\alpha,S_{E}^{V,J})
is reducible. It follows that
\mathcal{R}\backslash\mathcal{B}

is countable. Moreover, if
E\in\mathcal{R}
, then any non-zero solution of
\mathcal{S}_{J,V,\omega}\boldsymbol{u}=E\boldsymbol{u}
, satisfies
0<\inf_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}|a(n)|^{2}+|a(n+1)|^{2}\leq\inf_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}|\boldsymbol{u}(n)|^{2}+|\boldsymbol{u}(n+1)|^{2}
, so there are no eigenvalues in
\mathcal{R}
and
\mu_{\omega}(\mathcal{R}\backslash\mathcal{B})=0
. Therefore, it is enough to show that for sufficiently small
\delta_{0}>0
,

\mu_{\omega}(\mathcal{W}(\delta_{0})\backslash\mathcal{R})=0.

Note that
\mathcal{W}(\delta_{0})\backslash\mathcal{R}\subset\limsup K_{m}
, by Borel-Cantelli Lemma, we only need to prove
\sum_{m}\mu_{\omega}(\overline{K}_{m})<\infty
.

Let
J_{m}(E)
be an open
\epsilon_{m-1}^{\frac{2}{45}}
neighborhood of
E\in K_{m}
. By Lemma
6.9
and Corollary
7.6
, we have

\begin{split}\mu_{\omega}(J_{m}(E))&\leq\sup_{0\leq s\leq\epsilon_{m-1}^{-\frac{2}{45}}}\|(S_{E}^{V,J})_{s}\|_{0}^{2}|J_{m}(E)|\\
&\leq\sup_{0\leq s\leq\epsilon_{m-1}^{-\frac{1}{8}}}\|(S_{E}^{V,J})_{s}\|_{0}^{2}|J_{m}(E)|\leq C|\ln\epsilon_{m-1}|^{16\sigma}\epsilon_{m-1}^{\frac{2}{45}}.\end{split}

Let
\cup_{l=0}^{r}J_{m}(E_{l})
be a finite subcover of
\overline{K}_{m}
. By refining this subcover, we can assume that every
E\in\mathbb{R}
is contained in at most two different
J_{m}(E_{l})
.

On the other hand, by Corollary
7.6
, if
E\in K_{m}
, then

\|2\mathrm{n}(E)+\langle n,\alpha\rangle\|_{{\mathbb{T}}}\leq 2\epsilon_{m-1}^{\frac{1}{15}},

for some
|n|<2N_{m-1}
.
This shows that
2\mathrm{n}({K}_{m})
can be covered by
2N_{m-1}
intervals
I_{s}
of length
2\epsilon_{m-1}^{\frac{1}{15}}
.
By Lemma
7.9
,

2\mathrm{n}(J_{m}(E))\geq c|J_{m}(E)|^{\frac{3}{2}},

thus by our selection
|I_{s}|\leq\frac{1}{c}|2\mathrm{n}(J_{m}(E))|
for any
s

and
E\in{K}_{m}
, there are at most
2([\frac{1}{c}]+1)+4
intervals
J_{m}(E_{l})
such that
2\mathrm{n}(J_{m}(E_{l}))
intersects
I_{s}
.
We conclude that there are at most
2(2([\frac{1}{c}]+1)+4)N_{m-1}

intervals
J_{m}(E_{l})
to cover
K_{m}
. Then

\mu_{\omega}(\overline{K}_{m})\leq\sum_{j=0}^{r}\mu_{\omega}(J_{m}(E_{j}))\leq CN_{m-1}|\ln\epsilon_{m-1}|^{16\sigma}\epsilon_{m-1}^{\frac{2}{45}}<\epsilon_{m-1}^{\frac{1}{45}},

which gives
\sum_{m}\mu_{\omega}(\overline{K}_{m})<\infty
.

Lemma 7.10
.

[

Let
\lambda>0,\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}\backslash{\mathbb{Q}},|\tau|<1

\displaystyle A(\omega)=\begin{pmatrix}E-\frac{2\lambda\cos 2\pi\omega}{1-\tau\cos 2\pi\omega}&-1\\

Suppose that
(\alpha,A)

L(\alpha,A)=0\text{ and }\omega(\alpha,A)=0

sgn(\lambda)\tau E<2(1-\lambda).

Once we have this, note

\displaystyle S_{E}^{V,J}(\omega)=\begin{pmatrix}E-V_{11}(\omega)-\frac{|V_{12}(\omega)|^{2}}{E-V_{22}(\omega)}&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{E^{2}-\lambda^{2}}{E+\lambda\cos 2\pi\omega}&-1\\

and rewrite

\displaystyle\frac{E^{2}-\lambda^{2}}{E+\lambda\cos 2\pi\omega}=\frac{E^{2}-\lambda^{2}}{E}-\frac{\lambda(E^{2}-\lambda^{2})\cos 2\pi\omega}{E^{2}(1+\frac{\lambda}{E}\cos 2\pi\omega)},

|\frac{\lambda}{E}|<1,\quad\forall E\in\sigma^{\mathcal{S}}(J,V)\backslash[-\lambda,\lambda].

Since

(2\alpha,S_{E}^{V,J})

5.2
), we can apply Lemma
7.10
to obtain
\mathcal{W}=\sigma^{\mathcal{S}}(J,V)\backslash[-\lambda,\lambda].
Then, according to Theorem
7.2
, the operator
\mathcal{S}_{J,V,\omega}
has purely absolutely continuous spectrum in
\sigma^{\mathcal{S}}(J,V)\backslash[-\lambda,\lambda]
for every
\omega\in{\mathbb{T}}
. Moreover, combining this with Theorem
7.1
, we complete the proof.
