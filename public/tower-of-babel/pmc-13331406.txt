Diagnostic Accuracy of Ischemia and Hemorrhage on Cone-Beam CT Immediately After Endovascular Thrombectomy
Authors: Lovisa Sandell, Henrik Andersson, Mattias Drake, Helena Mellander Oxholm, Emma Hall, Veronica Fransson, Björn M. Hansen, Johan Wassélius
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Detection of ischemia and intracranial hemorrhage (ICH) directly after endovascular thrombectomy (EVT) could enable earlier diagnosis and treatment. We evaluated whether post-EVT Sine Spin cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) on the Siemens ARTIS Icono biplane system at the end of the procedure can predict ischemia and ICH on subsequent follow-up computed tomography (FUCT) within 48 hours.METHODS:Adult patients undergoing EVT in 2024 with available CBCT and FUCT were included. Two interventional neuroradiologists independently evaluated the CBCT examinations for ischemia, ICH (including Heidelberg classification), and image quality. FUCT, annotated by a neuroradiologist, was used as reference standard. Large ischemia was defined as ischemia involving ≥3 predefined vascular territories.RESULTS:Of 331 patients with EVT, 225 had both CBCT and FUCT, of which 197 (88%) were successfully recanalized. Ischemia was observed in 127 (56%) and ICH in 64 (28%) of the FUCTs. For ICH, sensitivity was 75%/77% (reviewer 1/reviewer 2), specificity 85%/84%, and positive predictive value 67% for both reviewers, negative predictive value 92%/91%, and accuracy 84%/83%. Accuracy for large ischemia (n=24, 11%) was 78%/67%. Sensitivity for correctly located ischemia on CBCT was 48%/73%, with specificity of 87%/60%, positive predictive value of 82%/71%, negative predictive value of 56%/63%, and accuracy of 65%/68%. CBCT was rated overall nondiagnostic in 0%/1% of the cases. For the posterior fossa specifically, 6%/16% of examinations were rated nondiagnostic.CONCLUSIONS:Sine Spin CBCT on the Siemens ARTIS Icono biplane system after EVT showed high accuracy for predicting ICH and large ischemia on FUCT. CBCT may help identify patients needing closer monitoring or early neurosurgical intervention.

Endovascular thrombectomy (EVT) is the standard of care for acute ischemic stroke caused by large vessel occlusions.1,2 However, EVT—as well as the stroke itself—is associated with complications such as intracranial hemorrhage (ICH), seen in ≈25% of patients.3 Although most ICHs are asymptomatic, they delay secondary stroke prevention by contraindicating oral antiplatelet and anticoagulation therapy. Another serious adverse event is malignant infarction, occurring in up to 5% of patients with EVT.4 Malignant infarction is associated with poor prognosis; however, timely neurosurgical intervention with hemicraniectomy may be life-saving.5 Early radiological outcome after EVT, including ICH and infarction, is typically assessed by a follow-up computed tomography (FUCT) within 48 hours of the procedure.

Cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) in modern angiography suites has improved considerably during the last decade and may be performed in <1 minute, enabling its utility for immediate post-EVT assessment of adverse events. The presence of parenchymal hyperdensities on post-EVT CBCT has been associated with a higher risk of hemorrhagic transformation and worse clinical outcome.6 However, technical limitations of CBCT—such as image artifacts from the temporal bones and poor gray/white matter discrimination—limit the diagnostic capacity for detecting hemorrhage and early ischemic changes compared with FUCT.7–10 In the Siemens ARTIS Icono biplane (Siemens ARTIS Icono, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany), CBCT acquisition is performed with a sinusoidal pathway (Sine Spin) to reduce image artifacts and improve image quality, especially in the posterior fossa. Recent studies have confirmed that the Sine Spin acquisition pathway results in reduced image artifacts11–13 and increased gray/white matter differentiation.12,14 However, data on the diagnostic accuracy of CBCT for ICH and ischemia immediately after EVT remain limited.14,15 The ability to evaluate common and potentially life-threatening adverse events in the angiography suite could enable earlier diagnosis and treatment, thereby improving patient outcome.

The aim of this study was to assess the diagnostic accuracy of post-EVT CBCT for ICH and ischemia in the correct prespecified brain region/regions with routine FUCT as a reference standard.

Methods

Study Design

We performed a retrospective cross-sectional diagnostic accuracy study of ICH, ischemia, and large ischemia on post-EVT CBCT using routine FUCT as ground truth. The study was reported in accordance with the STARD 2015 guidelines (Standards for Reporting of Diagnostic Accuracy; Supplemental Material).16

The study was approved by the Swedish Ethical Review Authority (no. 2024-03485-01), and individual informed consent was waived by the authority.

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request.

Patient and Procedural Characteristics

All patients undergoing EVT during 2024 at a regional comprehensive stroke center with a coverage of 1.93 million inhabitants were screened for potential inclusion in the study. Eligible patients were identified in the regional picture archiving and communication system (PACS; IDS7, Sectra, Linköping, Sweden). Patients were included in the study if a CBCT was performed during or immediately after the EVT procedure and an FUCT was performed within 48 hours of the procedure.

Patients aged <18 years and patients without access to either the CBCT or FUCT images were excluded. If a patient had >1 EVT during 2024, only the first one was included in the study.

Baseline and procedural data were collected from PACS. Baseline characteristics included age, sex, preprocedural stroke severity according to the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score, use of intravenous thrombolysis, and location of the vessel occlusion.

Procedural characteristics included anesthesia form (general anesthesia or conscious sedation), final recanalization grade according to the modified Treatment in Cerebral Infarction score, and complications, such as perforation, dissection, distal embolization, and malignant infarction. Processing times such as symptom onset time, recanalization time, time of CBCT examination, and time of FUCT examination were also collected. From the above-mentioned times, onset to recanalization, onset to CBCT, and CBCT-to-FUCT times were calculated.

Scanning Protocol

Cone-Beam Computed Tomography

All CBCT examinations were performed using 1 of 2 identical Siemens ARTIS Icono biplane angiography systems at a regional comprehensive stroke center during or immediately after EVT. All patients were scanned with the same 7 seconds DynaCT (7sDCT) Sine Spin protocol according to the manufacturer’s specifications, typically directly after recanalization. The scan parameters are typically: tube voltage=115 kV (109 kV lateral, up to 125 kV ap), tube current=250 mA, up to 4 ms per frame with 546 frames during a rotation time of 6.7 seconds. The sine spin CBCT makes the C-arms rotate in a sinusoidal pathway during image acquisition to reduce image artifacts typically caused by the temporal bone.

The CBCT images were reconstructed into 1 and 4 mm axial, and 3 mm coronal and sagittal images used in the study.

Follow-Up Computed Tomography

FUCT examinations performed within 48 hours of the EVT were allowed in the study. The examinations were performed using 1 of 6 multidetector computed tomography scanners. The scanners included Siemens SOMATOM Definition Flash (n=184, 82%), Siemens NEOTOM Alpha (n=3, 1.3%), Siemens SOMATOM On.site (n=1, 0.4%), Siemens Definition AS+ (n=1, 0.04%; Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany), GE Revolution Apex (n=23, 10%; GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, WI), and Philips iQon (n=13, 5.8%; Philips Healthcare, Best, the Netherlands). The FUCT scanning parameters are shown in Table S1.

When performing routine post-EVT FUCT examinations at our institution, the use of a dual-energy capable computed tomography scanner is recommended and was used, as well as a reconstructed virtual noncontrast examination in addition to the reconstructed 1 and 4 mm axial, and 3 mm coronal and sagittal regular computed tomography. If >1 FUCT was available in a patient, the first examination after the EVT was used.

Imaging Assessment

The CBCT and FUCT examinations were exported from the clinical PACS to a dedicated research PACS with similar specifications by the same manufacturer. All images were pseudonymized, ensuring that all clinical information was removed.

In the research PACS, examinations were reviewed according to a standardized image assessment protocol (Figure S1). The CBCT examinations were independently assessed by 2 experienced neuroradiologists (reviewers 1 and 2). To resemble the typical clinical image review, all images and reconstructions were made available to the reviewers, with the option to adjust windowing and zoom.

The assessments included 3 tasks:

Subjective image quality assessment.

Before the image assessments, a consensus meeting was held where the raters could familiarize themselves with the assessment protocol and align their assessment using patient examples not included in the study cohort.

The reviewers did not have access to any preprocedural imaging. Reviewer 1 reassessed all images in a second read 3 weeks after the first, used only for intrareader agreement assessment.

Ischemia Assessment

The reviewers assessed the examinations for any signs of acute ischemia (yes/no/nondiagnostic). If ischemia was identified, the side (right, left, bilateral, or nonapplicable) and vascular territory (anterior cerebral artery cortex, middle cerebral artery cortex divided into 3 parts [anterior/mid/posterior], posterior cerebral artery cortex, basal ganglia, thalamus, and cerebellum) were reported. A schematic image guide over the vascular territories was provided with the assessment protocol (Figure S1). The reviewers were instructed to register only what they assessed to be acute ischemia/infarct, excluding old infarcts.

Furthermore, the reviewers classified reported ischemic lesions according to their presentation (decreased gray/white matter differentiation or swelling/contrast enhancement/mixed).

Large Ischemia Assessment

Ischemia involving ≥3 of the predefined vascular territories (Figure S1) on FUCT, according to the reference standard, was defined as large ischemia in the analysis. For the accuracy metric calculation of large ischemia, an equal number of cases with ischemia involving <3 areas were included.

Hemorrhage Assessment

The reviewers assessed the examinations for the presence of ICH (yes/no/nondiagnostic). Any ICH identified were further registered according to lateralization and subtype according to the Heidelberg classification.17 A schematic image guide of the Heidelberg classification was also provided within the assessment protocol (Figure S1).

Image Quality

Image quality was rated by both reviewers on a 5-point ordinal scale (5—excellent, 4—good, 3—fair, 2—poor, 1—nondiagnostic) for overall image quality and posterior fossa image quality, respectively.

Reference Standard

All FUCT examinations were annotated by reviewer 1 in the research PACS with identical image review settings and access to all image reconstructions, including virtual noncontrast images if available, and blinding of all image parameters and patient identity, and this annotation was used as a reference standard.

Agreement With the Reference Standard

For ischemia, agreement between the reference standard and CBCT was defined as identifying ischemia ipsilateral and in the same area(s) as the reference standard, or the absence thereof. However, for the middle cerebral artery territories, it was also considered agreement if 2 or 3 ipsilateral ischemic areas were identified and the areas differed by one from the reference standard.

For ICH, agreement was defined as identifying an ipsilateral ICH or the absence thereof.

Statistical Analysis

IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows version 30 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY) and GraphPad Prism version 10.5.0 for iOS (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA) were used for statistical analysis. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies, and continuous variables were presented as median and interquartile range (IQR) or mean with SD. Categorical variables were analyzed using χ2 test, and continuous variables were analyzed using unpaired t test or Mann-Whitney U test, where applicable.

Diagnostic Accuracy

The diagnostic accuracy of identifying ischemia and ICH on CBCT was assessed for sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value (NPV), and accuracy in relation to the reference standard.

A subanalysis was performed on patients with large ischemia on FUCT according to the reference standard, and an equal number of patients with <3 ischemic areas, assessing diagnostic accuracy for predicting large ischemia on the reference standard FUCT with CBCT for both reviewers. Positivity on CBCT was defined as identifying ipsilateral ischemia to the large ischemia seen on FUCT according to the reference standard.

An exploratory subgroup analysis based on time between CBCT and FUCT, assessing sensitivity and specificity for ICH and ischemia, was performed. The groups were defined as CBCT-to-FUCT time <12 hours, 12 to 24 hours, and >24 hours.

Interreader and Intrareader Agreement

Interreader agreement for CBCT was assessed by Cohen κ coefficient between both reviewers using the same above-mentioned agreement criteria.

Intrareader agreement for CBCT for reviewer 1 was analyzed based on 2 separate review sessions 3 weeks apart to minimize recall bias, and also measured by Cohens κ coefficient.

Association Between Procedural Times and Ischemia Detection on CBCT

Univariate logistic regression analyses were performed to assess the relationship between the procedural times, onset to reperfusion, groin to reperfusion, and onset to CBCT, and the outcome ischemia detection on CBCT for each reviewer.

Results

Patient and Procedural Characteristics

During 2024, 331 patients were referred for EVT at our institution and screened for potential inclusion in the study. Of the 331 patients, 231 (70%) patients had a CBCT examination performed during the EVT procedure and a FUCT examination within 48 hours of the procedure. Six (6) patients were excluded, 2 were aged <18 years old, and 4 cases were not assessed due to technical errors when importing the CBCT or FUCT images to the research PACS. This resulted in the final study population of 225 patients (Figure S2).

In the group that had no CBCT, EVT was not performed in 44 of 100 cases. There was no significant difference between the final study population and the patients treated by EVT, but not examined by CBCT at the end of the procedure regarding mean age (74.5 years versus 73.8 years [95% CI, −2.79 to 4.11]; P=0.875), sex (male 48% versus 50%; P=0.830), or median preprocedural National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale scores (14 versus 12.5; P=0.109).

FUCT was done in <12 hours of CBCT in 50%, in 12 to 24 hours in 47%, in 24 to 36 hours in 2.6%, and in 36 to 48 hours in 0.5% of the patients.

The demographic and clinical characteristics of the final and excluded patient population are shown in Table S2. In the final study population, the mean age was 75 years, and 48% were male patients. The most common occlusion site was the M1 segment of the middle cerebral artery (n=91, 40%), and 15 patients had posterior circulation occlusions (6.7%). Tandem occlusions were present in 19% of cases (n=42). The median preprocedural National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score was 14 (IQR, 10–19), and the frequency of intravenous thrombolysis administration was 35% (n=78). Most procedures were performed under general anesthesia (n=179, 80%).

Successful recanalization was achieved in 88% (n=197) of cases. Procedural complication rate was 22% (n=49), with distal embolization being the most prevalent complication. Twelve patients (5.3%) developed malignant infarction, 6 of whom were treated with decompressive craniotomy. Median onset-to-recanalization time was 256 minutes (IQR, 192–344), median groin-to-recanalization time was 46 minutes (IQR, 27–86), median onset-to-CBCT time was 260 minutes (IQR, 202–333), and median CBCT-to-FUCT examination time was 919 minutes (IQR, 527–1246).

Diagnostic Accuracy for Intracranial Hemorrhage

ICH was identified on CBCT in a total of 71 patients (32%) for reviewer 1 and 75 patients (33%) for reviewer 2 (Table 1). These were classified as subarachnoid hemorrhage in 55 of 71 cases (77%) for reviewer 1 and 63 of 75 cases (84%) for reviewer 2. Of the cases diagnosed as subarachnoid hemorrhage on CBCT, 17 of 71 of reviewer 1’s cases (24%), and 21 of 75 of reviewer 2’s cases (28%) had no bleeding on FUCT according to the reference standard.

Of the patients with ICH according to the reference standard FUCT, the same Heidelberg bleeding classification was identified on CBCT in 49 and 50 patients (77% and 78%) for reviewers 1 and 2, respectively.

For Reviewer 1, there was no association between ischemia detection on CBCT and onset to reperfusion time (odds ratio [OR], 1.00 [95% CI, 0.99–1.00]; P=0.441), groin to reperfusion time (OR, 1.00 [95% CI, 0.99–1.01]; P=0.792), or onset to CBCT time (OR, 1.00 [95% CI, 0.99–1.00]; P=0.242). Discussion

In this study, we show that sine spin CBCT performed using the Siemens ARTIS Icono biplane angiography system immediately after EVT has a high accuracy for predicting ICH seen on FUCT in patients treated for acute ischemic stroke. The sensitivity, specificity, and NPV are all high and similar between independent reviewers, especially in cases where FUCT is performed within 24 hours of the CBCT. Our findings suggest that sine spin CBCT at the end of EVT is a useful tool to predict ICH before the patient has even left the angiography suite. This may have important implications for secondary prevention as well as postoperative care and observation. Especially when EVT procedures are also performed in centers without access to neurosurgery, CBCT may be a useful tool to get an early indication for patients who may need closer monitoring or are at high risk of requiring neurosurgical care.

Although CBCT immediately after EVT has a high accuracy for predicting ICH, hemorrhagic complications may occur up to several days post-EVT. The false negative rate for ICH likely reflects this, as most FUCT examinations are performed beyond 12 hours post-EVT. The false positives may be due to difficulty distinguishing hemorrhage from contrast extravasation on CBCT, as reviewers reported virtual noncontrast in FUCT as useful for ruling out hemorrhage. Another factor may include interpreting artifacts on CBCT as hemorrhage, especially in the posterior fossa, where the image quality was poorer compared with supratentorial image quality. Thus, FUCT still has a fundamental role in postoperative stroke management.

We also show that the sensitivity for predicting large ischemia on FUCT in patients treated with EVT for acute ischemic stroke is almost as high as for ICH, and is also similar between independent reviewers. Evidently, not all patients with detectable ischemia on CBCT progress to large ischemia or malignant infarction. However, most patients who later developed malignant infarction already had signs of ischemia on CBCT, and a minority had established large ischemia, suggesting that detecting ischemia on CBCT may indicate a risk of malignant transformation. Nonetheless, these results are still encouraging, since it is important to identify patients who may be at risk of developing malignant infarction as soon as possible to consider additional imaging and decompressive craniotomy at an early stage before the mass effect causes further damage. This is especially important when performing EVT at hospitals without in-house neurosurgery.

Unlike the high diagnostic accuracy of CBCT for ICH and large ischemia, the accuracy for predicting any ischemia at the FUCT is distinctly lower, and the variation between the reviewers is larger. The difference between the reviewers for sensitivity and specificity for any ischemia was balanced, resulting in similar accuracy for both reviewers, suggesting that the difference is largely generated by different individual assessment thresholds between the reviewers. This is understandable given the subtlety of the findings in many cases, as illustrated by case 3 in Figure 2. In comparison, a large ischemia with ischemic imaging findings in ≥ 3 assessment regions would likely be more evident, as illustrated by case 4 in Figure 2, and therefore, explain the higher sensitivity and specificity for both reviewers. Another contributing reason to the modest accuracy for any ischemia may be that the ischemia, in some cases, had not yet developed at the time of the CBCT, but had developed to be visible on the FUCT a couple of hours later. Sensitivity and specificity for ischemia decrease for both reviewers as the time from CBCT to FUCT increases, although it does not entirely explain the difference between the reviewers. There were no associations found between lead times and ischemia detection on CBCT, further indicating challenges in identifying ischemia on CBCT, likely affected by factors such as image quality and artifacts, and possibly different assessor thresholds.

The recent technical development of CBCT in the angiography suite is very encouraging, and yet there is even further development to be expected in the future, for example, using both X-ray sources in complementary sinusoidal pathways to acquire more projections to increase image quality and reduce artifacts further, especially in the posterior fossa. Image artifacts are still a limitation of CBCT in the posterior fossa, as shown by a relatively high frequency of examinations rated as nondiagnostic or poor. Other potential improvements may include adding spectral capability or novel postprocessing tools to improve imaging quality.18

Strengths and Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, this was a single-center study performed at a comprehensive stroke center with regional coverage.

Second, CBCT was performed as part of the clinical routine during EVT at the operator’s discretion, which may have introduced selection bias. However, the rates of complications, including ICH, are comparable to national Swedish rates, as are baseline characteristics.19,20 Furthermore, the 5% rate of malignant infarction in this cohort is similar to international rates, although these patients may potentially be underdiagnosed and undertreated.4

Third, data on clinical complications, such as symptomatic ICH, were unavailable for this study. This information would have provided further insight into the ability to predict important complications using CBCT compared with FUCT.

Fourth, the FUCT was performed on several different computed tomography scanners, and although the general protocol is regional, minor differences in protocol and image characteristics and quality between scanners or over time are reasonable to assume, including a portion of the scans being done on scanners without spectral capability and therefore without virtual noncontrast images.

In addition, one of the readers assessed CBCT as well as FUCT, although all examinations were anonymized, a bias against or for CBCT could impact the result.

Strengths of this study include a relatively large population with high regional coverage and a standardized CBCT protocol used for all patients.

Conclusions

Sine spin CBCT on the Siemens ARTIS Icono biplane system at the end of the procedure has a high accuracy for predicting ICH. Sine Spin CBCT at the end of the EVT procedure also has high accuracy for predicting large ischemia on FUCT, whereas the accuracy for predicting smaller ischemia is lower.
