Indirect traumatic optic neuropathy in a high-risk diabetic adolescent: A rare clinical presentation
Authors: Rajaa El Azzouzi, Oumaima Boukhlouf, Bouchra Dani, Malik Boulaadas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Indirect traumatic optic neuropathy (ITON) is a rare but severe complication of craniofacial trauma. Its management remains controversial, ranging from high-dose corticosteroids and surgical decompression to observation. Comorbidities may significantly impact therapeutic decision-making. We report the case of a 16-year-old male patient with poorly controlled type 1 diabetes (HbA1c = 11%) who sustained a road traffic accident with craniofacial trauma. On day 3, he developed acute visual loss in his right eye. Imaging revealed a right zygomatic tripod fractures without ocular involvement and optic nerve elongation on Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), consistent with ITON. This case highlights a dramatic therapeutic dilemma: attempting to preserve vision at the cost of significant systemic risks. The rarity of ITON in young patients and the absence of specific guidelines for diabetic patients, make decision-making particularly challenging. Multidisciplinary discussion; Ophthalmology, maxillofacial surgery, endocrinology, intensive care was crucial. The management of ITON in patients with uncontrolled diabetes raises critical therapeutic, prognostic, and ethical issues. This case emphasizes the need to tailor treatment strategies and carefully weigh comorbidities in the risk-benefit balance.

Introduction

Indirect traumatic optic neuropathy (ITON) is a rare, neuro-ophthalmological emergency resulting from closed head or facial trauma without visible anatomical disruption of the optic nerve. Because post-traumatic visual loss can be severe and permanent, prompt diagnosis is critical.

The optimal management of ITON remains highly controversial. To date, no consensus exists, as high-dose corticosteroids, decompressive surgery, and conservative management (active therapeutic abstention) have shown equivalent efficacy in high-level evidence studies. Furthermore, the influence of systemic comorbidities, particularly diabetes, on visual prognosis and therapeutic response remains poorly understood.

To our knowledge, no published study in the literature has exclusively evaluated the management of ITON in diabetic patients. Existing data are limited to occasional, isolated diabetic cases reported within broader ITON case series [1,2]. Consequently, this study presents a rare case of ITON in an adolescent patient with poorly controlled type 1 diabetes, aiming to analyze the specific therapeutic approach selected and the subsequent visual prognosis.

Case report

We report the case of a 16-year-old male with a medical history of poorly controlled diabetes. The trauma occurred during a road traffic accident: he was an unhelmeted motorcyclist ejected after a slide, sustaining a head and facial trauma with a “coup contre-coup” mechanism. Given the patient's history of diabetes, an urgent endocrinology consultation was requested. Initial laboratory investigations revealed an admission fasting blood glucose level of 3.69 g/L and an HbA1c of 11%. Regarding management, the high-dose corticosteroid protocol standardly indicated for ITON was considered by the endocrinologist to carry a significant risk of severe metabolic decompensation. Consequently, following close multidisciplinary discussion and immediate adjustment of his insulin therapy, the decision was made to safely initiate an IV methylprednisolone bolus of 120 mg per day on the fourth day post-trauma for 3 days, followed by oral prednisolone at 1 mg/kg in a tapering dose administered for 2 weeks. There was no indication for surgical or orthopedic intervention for the zygomatic fracture due to the absence of displacement with no aesthetic or functional consequences.

The clinical course showed no visual improvement: only positive light perception persisted in the right eye. To assess the extent of the damage, a paraclinical workup including optical coherence tomography (OCT) and visual evoked potentials (VEPs) was performed.

Macular OCT with ganglion cell layer and inner plexiform layer (GCL + IPL) analysis demonstrates a severe structural involvement of the right eye. In the right eye, there is a diffuse and marked thinning of the GCL + IPL, statistically significant on normative comparison maps, with values clearly below normal limits (average thickness approximately 40 µm and minimum approximately 21 µm). Deviation and asymmetry maps show a pronounced right–left asymmetry, consistent with significant neuronal loss in the right eye, compared to the left eye that showed preserved GCL + IPL thickness, with values within normal limits. Discussion

Traumatic optic neuropathy (TON) is a rare condition, that can result in vision impairment due to damage to the nerve. Based on injury, traumatic optic neuropathy can be classified into 2 categories; direct and indirect. Direct traumatic optic neuropathy occurs when there is direct physical trauma to the optic nerve, often due to penetrating injuries or fractures involving the bones around it. On the other hand, indirect TON occurs when blunt force is transmitted to the nerve through tissues or bony structures of the eye socket, causing shearing stretching or ischemic injury to the nerve. Motor vehicle accidents are the leading cause of ITON, with falls, assaults, and sports injuries following suit [3].

The precise pathophysiology remains incompletely understood but likely involves mechanical injury, vascular factors (eg, vasospasm and ischemia), and inflammatory processes that collectively lead to stretching, tearing, or damage to the blood supply of the optic nerve in the indirect TON [2]. In particular, diabetic microangiopathy was associated with capillary basement membrane thickening, increasing optic nerve susceptibility to secondary post-traumatic ischemia and limiting spontaneous axonal regeneration [4].

The clinical presentation of TON is characterized by highly variable features, including acute vision loss and a relative afferent pupillary defect. In our case, the 16-year-old patient presented with a delayed decrease in VA starting on the third day post-injury, illustrating the classic diagnostic challenge where the absence of early clinical signs or reliable prognostic biomarkers can initially mask the severity of ITON.

To navigate these challenges, a multimodal diagnostic approach is essential. Multidetector thin-slice CT scanning serves as the primary screening tool to rule out compressive or surgically actionable lesions. In our patient, the initial maxillofacial CT scan successfully identified a non-displaced zygomatic tripod fracture without mechanical impingement or compressive bone fragments, thereby ruling out direct mechanical or compressive causes of optic nerve injury. When clinical suspicion remains high despite an unremarkable CT, orbital and brain MRI is indicated as a highly complementary modality to evaluate soft-tissue injuries. This was clearly demonstrated in our report, where the MRI revealed optic nerve elongation, establishing the definitive diagnosis of ITON by confirming structural stretch injury without macroscopic transection.

Electrophysiological and structural evaluations further objectively document the functional and anatomical impact of the trauma. While VEPs provide critical functional assessment—as evidenced by the abolished VEP responses on the affected side in our patient OCT serves as an invaluable tool for structural quantification, provided its postinjury timing is carefully considered. In the hyperacute phase, macular OCT and retinal nerve fiber layer thickness typically appear normal. However, during the subacute to chronic phases, retrograde axonal degeneration leads to quantifiable thinning. This pathophysiological process correlates perfectly with our patient’s paraclinical workup, where macular OCT with GCL + IPL analysis revealed a severe, statistically significant right-left asymmetry and marked thinning.

Ultimately, this multimodal framework allowed for a rigorous differential diagnosis, ruling out other post-traumatic causes of vision loss such as direct avulsion, vitreous hemorrhage, retinal detachment, or central retinal artery occlusion. In this case, the combination of early neuroimaging and delayed structural OCT analysis successfully characterized a severe, unilateral right ITON, providing objective evidence of irreversible neuronal loss.

The management of TON remains highly controversial, encompassing conservative observation, medical therapy, and surgical decompression. Conservative treatment is traditionally reserved for patients with stable baseline VA and a VEP amplitude ratio >50\%. While literature reports spontaneous visual recovery in 40%-60% of these cases [2], an outcome comparable to medical or surgical cohorts—these data suffer from inherent selection bias. When observation is chosen, serial clinical and electrophysiological monitoring is mandatory, with emergent repeat neuroimaging (CT/MRI) indicated upon any signs of visual deterioration.

When medical intervention is pursued, systemic corticosteroid therapy is the historical standard, though protocols vary widely regarding dosage and duration. High-dose regimens (eg, megadose intravenous methylprednisolone >5400 mg/d) carry life-threatening risks, including gastrointestinal hemorrhage, severe infections, sepsis, and acute psychosis. Data extrapolated from National Acute Spinal Cord Injury Study (NASCIS III) suggest that initiating steroids within 3-8 hours post-injury and sustaining them for 48 hours yields optimal outcomes. However, a definitive visual benefit in TON remains unestablished globally [5].

The existing literature offers conflicting evidence regarding corticosteroid efficacy. For instance, a retrospective study by Sujithra et al. [6] demonstrated that while untreated patients (Group A) showed no significant visual recovery, patients treated with intravenous methylprednisolone within 72 hours (Group B) or even after 72 hours (Group C) experienced statistically significant visual improvement (P = .002 and P = .043, respectively) [6].

A major limitation of the existing literature on ITON is the insufficient consideration of significant systemic comorbidities, particularly diabetes mellitus. The management of ITON in diabetic patients remains especially challenging because high-dose systemic corticosteroids are relatively contraindicated in the setting of poor glycemic control. Therapeutic decisions therefore require careful consideration of the potential benefits for visual recovery against the substantial risk of severe metabolic complications, including profound hyperglycemia, diabetic ketoacidosis, and hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state.

This issue has been highlighted in several reports that emphasize the need for therapeutic adaptation in diabetic patients. In the study by Sivakumar et al. [3] 2 patients with uncontrolled diabetes were intentionally allocated to conservative management in order to avoid steroid-induced metabolic decompensation [3]. Similarly, Lee et al. [1] in a cohort of 24 patients with ITON secondary to severe craniofacial or orbital trauma, reported that 7 patients were managed conservatively because of severe head injury, uncontrolled diabetes mellitus, or young age [1]. Although visual improvement was observed in 91.7% of corticosteroid-treated non-diabetic patients, the diabetic patients treated conservatively did not experience significant visual recovery in both studies.

Wright et al. [4] further investigated prognostic factors and systemic comorbidities influencing visual recovery in 42 patients with ITON. The authors emphasized that a conservative “watchful waiting” strategy should be considered in patients with significant metabolic compromise, in whom high-dose corticosteroid therapy may carry substantial risk. Their findings demonstrated that baseline systemic health significantly influenced final visual outcomes. In particular, diabetic microangiopathy was associated with capillary basement membrane thickening, increasing optic nerve susceptibility to secondary post-traumatic ischemia while impairing spontaneous axonal regeneration. Importantly, patients with diabetes and other vascular comorbidities exhibited poorer visual recovery compared with metabolically healthy individuals, supporting the role of systemic metabolic dysfunction as an independent negative prognostic factor in ITON [4].

Kumaran et al. [2] in a narrative review of 23 studies, further emphasized the absence of standardized therapeutic guidelines and questioned the overall efficacy of corticosteroid therapy in light of its adverse effect profile. Poorly controlled diabetes mellitus was identified as a major contraindication to high-dose corticosteroid protocols. The authors therefore advocated an individualized therapeutic strategy, favoring either close observation or early surgical decompression, particularly through a transnasal transsphenoidal approach performed within the first week, when conventional medical treatment is contraindicated [2].

Our case clearly illustrates this therapeutic dilemma. The patient, a 16-year-old adolescent with poorly controlled type 1 diabetes mellitus on admission. Although previous reports, including the study by Sujithra et al. [6] support delayed initiation of intravenous methylprednisolone, the use of conventional megadose corticosteroid regimens was considered to carry an unacceptably high metabolic risk in this context [6]. Following multidisciplinary discussion, a tailored therapeutic strategy was adopted, consisting of a cautious 120 mg intravenous methylprednisolone bolus initiated on the fourth day after trauma in association with intensive insulin adjustment and close metabolic monitoring. This case underscores the limitations of universally applying standard ITON treatment protocols to patients with severe systemic metabolic disorders and highlights the importance of individualized management strategies.

Alternative therapeutic options remain limited when corticosteroid administration is restricted. Surgical decompression is generally reserved for cases with radiological evidence of direct mechanical or osseous compression of the optic nerve within the optic canal, a finding that was absent in our patient, whose zygomatic fracture was nondisplaced. More recently, neuroprotective therapies have emerged as potential alternatives aimed at avoiding steroid-related toxicity. Among these, erythropoietin has been the most extensively investigated. Its antiapoptotic and anti-inflammatory properties have shown encouraging results in terms of visual recovery in preliminary clinical studies. Nevertheless, large-scale randomized controlled trials are still needed to clarify its precise therapeutic role and to determine its efficacy relative to conventional treatment modalities, particularly in complex patients with significant systemic comorbidities [7].

Conclusion

ITON in patients with poorly controlled diabetes mellitus represents a critical therapeutic dilemma. Our case underscores the importance of individualized, multidisciplinary decision-making that carefully balances the potential for visual recovery against the risk of severe systemic complications. Our experience further emphasizes that conservative or adapted low-dose corticosteroid strategies may represent a safer alternative in selected high-risk patients, while reinforcing the need for clearer evidence-based guidelines for the management of ITON in the setting of significant metabolic comorbidities.
