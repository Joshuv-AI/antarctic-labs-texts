Kinetic endovascular micro-incision creation (KEMIC) in arteriovenous access: A U.S. and European experience demonstrating economic benefit
Authors: Robert Shahverdyan, Daniel G Clair, Jordan P Knepper
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Purpose:Repeated interventions for arteriovenous (AV) access dysfunction in hemodialysis patients are costly, burdensome, and detrimental to quality of life. The FLEX Vessel Preparation (VP) system utilizes a technique termed kinetic endovascular micro-incision creation (KEMIC) to improve vessel compliance and durability prior to angioplasty. This study compares real-world procedural and specifically the economic outcomes from matched U.S. and European cohorts.Materials and methods:This is a retrospective, quality-improvement (QI)-exempt comparative study from two high-volume centers. Twelve FLEX patients managed by a single U.S. vascular surgeon were compared to 60 matched PTA controls via propensity matching. The European cohort included 74 patients treated with FLEX at a single center and compared to 80 matched PTA controls in Germany. FLEX-VP was performed using three to eight passes with a balloon-free, microblade catheter, typically via a 6 F sheath and followed by low-pressure angioplasty and/or drug-coated balloon.Results:U.S. FLEX patients underwent 1.5 fewer interventions per patient-year than PTA controls (0.7 vs 2.2, p < 0.01). In Germany, FLEX reduced reinterventions by 0.4 per patient-year compared to PTA (0.6 vs 1.0, p < 0.001). Depending on local procedure costs, these reductions translate to estimated per-patient annual savings of $1451–$2902 in the U.S. and €426–€767 in Germany. Kaplan–Meier analyses demonstrated superior reintervention-free survival at 12 months in both cohorts (U.S.: 70.7% vs 46.1%; Germany: 67.4% vs 44.3%).Conclusions:KEMIC is a reproducible, low-risk, stent-sparing approach that improves access durability while potentially delivering substantial savings to payers and systems.

Introduction

Vascular access (VA) dysfunction remains a leading cause of hospitalization in patients on hemodialysis (HD). Despite the clinical preference for autogenous arteriovenous fistulas (AVFs), long-term durability of both AVFs and arteriovenous grafts (AVG) is often limited by recurrent stenosis, particularly in anatomically complex zones like the cephalic arch or graft venous anastomosis (GVA). While percutaneous transluminal balloon-angioplasty (PTA) remains first-line therapy, its shortcomings include barotrauma, elastic recoil, and (rapid) recurrent restenosis, especially when performed at high pressure.

The FLEX Vessel Prep system (FLEX Vascular, previously VentureMed, Minneapolis, MN, USA) introduces kinetic endovascular micro-incision creation (KEMIC) as an adjunct to angioplasty. Through three to eight longitudinal passes of a balloon-free, microbladed catheter, KEMIC reduces wall resistance and balloon inflation pressure requirements. When used prior to standard or drug-coated balloon-angioplasty (DCB), this vessel preparation improves vessel compliance, lowers rates of barotrauma, and may reduce the need for permanent stents/stent grafts. Several studies have demonstrated the advantage of FLEX Vessel Prep (VP) device in the treatment of VA stenoses.1–3

The purpose of this study was to compare outcomes of KEMIC in two geographic regions: a propensity-matched cohort in the United States (U.S.) and a prospectively maintained single-site registry in Germany, analyzed retrospectively, with the primary goal to evaluate annual reintervention rates and hence calculate real-world per-patient cost savings.

Methods

Study aim and overview

The primary aim of this study was to evaluate whether vessel preparation with FLEX reduces the frequency of reinterventions and associated costs compared with PTA in dysfunctional VA. Secondary aims included subgroup evaluation of cephalic arch lesions and high-risk patients with multiple prior interventions, assessment of stent utilization, and replication of results across two health care systems (U.S. and Germany).

Study design

This retrospective, observational analysis included quality-improvement (QI)-exempt data from two regions. In the U.S., 12 consecutive patients treated with KEMIC by a single vascular surgeon were compared to non-FLEX patients treated by the providers at the same institution. The German cohort consisted of 74 patients consecutively treated with KEMIC at a Vascular Access Center by a single vascular surgeon and compared to 80 matched non-FLEX patients from the same surgeon between May 2023 and October 2024. All U.S. procedures were performed by a single operator (JPK) and all German procedures by a single, different operator (RS); each cohort was therefore treated by one consistent surgeon. Matching was performed using available summary demographics and comorbidities. Due to retrospective nature of the study neither an Ethic Committee nor Institutional Review Board approval was required.

Cohort selection

Inclusion criteria were adults ⩾18 years with AVF or AVG, undergoing endovascular intervention for VA dysfunction. Exclusion criteria were thrombosed or non-functional access at presentation, prior stent at the target lesion, or <6 months of potential follow-up. Matching and control definition

From the U.S. dataset,12 FLEX patients were compared with 60 PTA patients (1:5 ratio). Propensity scores were estimated using age, sex, diabetes, access type (AVF vs AVG), lesion site (cephalic arch vs other), and prior interventions (continuous). Matching used nearest neighbor without replacement with a caliper of 0.2 SD of the logit of the propensity score.

From Germany, 7 4 FLEX patients were compared with 80 PTA patients. Matching covariates included age, sex, diabetes, comorbidity index, access type, and lesion site. Balance across groups was assessed using standardized mean differences.

Intervention protocol

KEMIC was performed using a 6 F sheath and standard 0.014″ or 0.018″ guidewire. Three to eight longitudinal passes were made through the target lesion(s). A semi- or non-compliant balloon was used as PTA at the lowest possible pressures to reach full effacement to avoid elastic recoil and barotrauma. DCB angioplasty (DCBA) was not performed routinely yet depending on the surgeon’s preference. When DCBA was performed, the DCB was used either directly after KEMIC, or after PTA, as recommended by the manufacturer.

Data collection and outcomes

Annual reintervention rates, and cost modeling were captured. Total procedural cost estimates were derived using U.S. Premier and EU DRG estimates. Kaplan–Meier curves were generated to evaluate reintervention-free survival (Figure 2).

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using Stata (version 17.0; StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA). Propensity scores were estimated via logistic regression on pre-specified covariates (age, sex, diabetes, access type, lesion site, and number of prior interventions) and used to perform nearest-neighbor matching without replacement, with a caliper of 0.2 standard deviations of the logit of the propensity score. Post-matching covariate balance was assessed using standardized mean differences, with values <0.10 considered indicative of adequate balance. The primary outcome—annual reintervention frequency—is a count variable subject to overdispersion relative to a Poisson distribution. Reintervention rates were therefore modeled using negative binomial regression with a log-link function and the natural logarithm of observed follow-up time included as an offset term, to account for variation in follow-up duration across subjects. Results are reported as incidence rate ratios (IRR) with 95% confidence intervals derived from robust standard errors. Time to first reintervention was analyzed using the Kaplan–Meier estimator; between-group differences were evaluated using the log-rank test, with patients censored at last follow-up or 12 months, whichever occurred first. Economic outcomes were modeled by multiplying the observed reintervention rate differential by a range of assumed per-procedure costs reflecting variation in local and national reimbursement schedules. Sensitivity analyses varied assumed procedure costs by ±20%. A two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant throughout.

Primary clinical outcomes

In the U.S. cohort, FLEX-treated patients required 1.5 fewer interventions per patient-year (0.7 vs 2.2, p < 0.01). Stent use was reduced by 0.9 placements per patient. In Germany, reintervention frequency dropped from 1.0 to 0.6 per patient-year (p < 0.001). Kaplan–Meier analysis of the total cohort showed 59.7% reintervention-free survival at 12 months in the cephalic arch subgroup. Median time to first reintervention was not reached in either FLEX group and was 5.3 months in U.S. PTA group and 4.8 months in German PTA group, respectively.

Secondary clinical outcomes

Reintervention frequency was significantly reduced in FLEX patients across both cohorts. In the U.S. cohort, FLEX patients underwent 0.7 interventions per patient-year versus 2.2 for PTA patients (IRR 0.38, 95% CI 0.22–0.66). In Germany, FLEX patients underwent 0.6 interventions per patient-year versus 1.0 for PTA patients (IRR 0.41, 95% CI 0.29–0.59). In both cohorts, FLEX use was associated with reduced covered stent placement (0.2 vs 1.1 per patient, p < 0.01) and lower rates of access abandonment (5% vs 14%, p = 0.04).

Economic outcomes

The absolute reduction in reintervention frequency translates to estimated per-patient annual savings of $1451–$2902 in the U.S. and €426–€767 in Germany, depending on local procedure cost assumptions (Tables 2 and 3). Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of these estimates across a ±20% range of assumed procedure costs.

Subgroup analyses

The subgroup analysis of cephalic arch lesions demonstrated in the U.S. cohort that FLEX patients had 12-month reintervention-free survival of 65% versus 32% for PTA, with a mean savings of $3000 per patient. In the German cohort, 12-month freedom from reintervention was 30 percentage points higher with FLEX than with PTA (p < 0.01), with €2800 per-patient savings.

For high-risk patients (⩾4 prior interventions), FLEX reduced reinterventions by 52% in the U.S. and 49% in Germany compared to PTA. Cost savings in this group were greater than in the overall cohort, averaging $3200 per patient in the U.S. and €2900 in Germany.

Sensitivity analyses

Adjusting assumed procedure costs ±20% did not alter the overall direction or significance of results. German tariff adjustments yielded stable findings (Table 4).

Discussion

KEMIC offers a mechanistically novel, biologically elegant solution to a long-standing problem in vascular access. By incising the intimal and medial layers in a controlled fashion, the FLEX system reduces radial resistance without permanent implants. This results in vessel remodeling, which allows durable angioplasty at low pressures, particularly in high-resistance zones like the cephalic arch.

Unlike cutting balloons, KEMIC minimizes the risk of barotrauma-induced tissue disruption, thereby reducing the likelihood of explosive trauma to both the area stenosis but specifically the adjacent healthy venous wall. Additionally, in cases where DCBs are being used, the uptake of the drug from DCBs into the treated lesion might be improved through circumferential application of the drug after vessel preparation. 2

Stent avoidance is another key benefit, as FLEX may reduce covered stent utilization. Stents introduce higher costs and long-term risks including fracture, thrombosis, and infection—particularly problematic in immunocompromised dialysis patients. KEMIC enables stent-free patency while preserving options for surgical revision.

Comparative studies have shown that stent (graft) placement in AV access, particularly in cephalic arch lesions, can reduce short-term restenosis but may increase long-term risk of in-stent restenosis and procedural complexity during future interventions. Ratnam et al. reported that stent grafts improve primary patency relative to plain balloon angioplasty, with benefit sustained up to 24 months and fewer reinterventions offsetting their higher initial device cost. 4 KEMIC offers a non-implant strategy that improves durability while preserving the treated segment for future reintervention and later surgical or endovascular access options, without leaving a permanent implant.

Economically, this strategy is aligned with the goals of value-based care, especially in the context of Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services’ (CMS) End Stage Renal Disease (ESRD) Treatment Choices model and global NHS pressures. By reducing procedural volume and reliance on prosthetics, KEMIC delivers both patient-centered and system-wide value. Limitations include the retrospective nature of the U.S. data and lack of full matching granularity. However, converging results from two distinct health systems suggest external validity. A prospective registry and U.S. multicenter trial are currently being designed.

Additionally, DCB use was not included as a matching covariate; its differential distribution between groups (U.S.: 8.3% FLEX vs 40.0% PTA; Germany: 92.2% FLEX vs 78.1% PTA) represents a residual confound that should be addressed in future prospective analyses.

Conclusion

KEMIC is a promising adjunct to AV access intervention. It reduces procedure frequency, stent reliance, and annual maintenance costs. These results support further prospective validation and broader integration into vascular access care pathways.
