Measuring irreversibility in stochastic systems by categorizing single-molecule displacements.
Alvaro Lanza, Inés Martínez-Martín, Rafael Tapia-Rojo, Stefano Bo

Abstract
Quantifying the irreversibility and dissipation of non-equilibrium processes is crucial to understanding their behavior, assessing their possible capabilities, and characterizing their efficiency. We introduce a physical quantity that quantifies the irreversibility of stochastic Langevin systems from the observation of individual molecules’ displacements. Categorizing these displacements into a few groups based on their initial and final position allows us to measure irreversibility precisely without the need to know the forces and magnitude of the fluctuations acting on the system. Our model-free estimate of irreversibility is related to entropy production by a conditional fluctuation theorem and provides a lower bound to the average entropy production. We validate the method on single-molecule force spectroscopy experiments of proteins subject to force ramps. We show that irreversibility is sensitive to detailed features of the energy landscape underlying the protein folding dynamics and suggest how our methods can be employed to unveil key properties of protein folding processes.

II
Stochastic thermodynamics of Langevin systems

We consider systems described by the overdamped Langevin equation

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{t}=\frac{F}{\gamma}+\sqrt{2D}\xi_{t}

(1)

where,
x_{t}
is the degree of freedom we are tracking (for example, the position of a single particle),
F
is the deterministic force acting on it (which can depend on position and time),
\xi_{t}
is a Gaussian white noise satisfying
\langle\xi_{t}\xi_{t^{\prime}}\rangle=\delta(t-t^{\prime})
and the fluctuation-dissipation theorem links the diffusion
D
to friction
\gamma
via the temperature
T
,
D=k_{B}T/\gamma
.
The entropy produced along a trajectory
\mathbf{x}
can be defined in terms of its irreversibility as
the log-ratio

\displaystyle\Delta S_{tot}(\mathbf{x})\equiv k_{B}\ln{\frac{\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x})}{\mathcal{P}^{R}(\widetilde{\mathbf{x}})}}

(2)

where
\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x})
is the probability of observing the trajectory
\mathbf{x}
forward in time and
\mathcal{P}^{R}(\widetilde{\mathbf{x}})
is the probability of observing the time reversed one
84
. Since the space of trajectories grows exponentially with their length, it is practically impossible to sample it efficiently, and non-trivial coarse-graining procedures are required
32
;
78
;
30
;
44
.
Taking the average of Eq. (
2
) shows that the average entropy production can be written as a
Kullback-Leibler Divergence (
D_{KL}
)

\displaystyle\langle\Delta S_{tot}\rangle=k_{B}D_{KL}\left(\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x})||\mathcal{P}^{R}(\widetilde{\mathbf{x}})\right)\,,

(3)

where the
D_{KL}
for two probability distributions
p
and
p^{\prime}
is defined as
D_{KL}(p||p^{\prime})\equiv\int dx\,p\ln(p/p^{\prime})
.
This expression agrees with the second law of thermodynamics since Kullback-Leibler divergences are never negative.
A key result of stochastic thermodynamics is that this definition of entropy coincides with the thermodynamic entropy defined by studying heat exchanges with reservoirs

\displaystyle\Delta S_{tot}(\mathbf{x})=

\displaystyle\underbrace{k_{B}\ln{\frac{P_{0}(x_{0})}{P_{t}(x_{t})}}}_{\Delta S_{sys}}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{T}\int F(x_{t})\circ dx_{t}}_{\Delta S_{env}}\,,

(4)

which has contributions
\Delta S_{sys}
due to changes in the entropy of the system and
\Delta S_{env}
from dissipation into the environment. The average rate of entropy production can be written in terms of the probability fluxes
j
sustained by the system
84

\langle\dot{S}_{tot}\rangle=\int dx\,\dot{\sigma}(x)
, where

\displaystyle\dot{\sigma}(x)\equiv k_{B}\frac{j(x)D^{-1}j(x)}{P(x)}\,.

(5)

For a stationary system, this corresponds to an integral over the average heat over temperature that is locally dissipated.

Measuring irreversibility

III.1
Classes of displacements and their distributions.

We consider a system that is observed at discrete time intervals
\Delta t
, in which it moves from the initial position
x^{\prime}
to

\ell=x-x^{\prime}

In many systems, such as biomolecular condensates

For such heterogeneous diffusion, be it with respect to an inhomogeneous diffusion coefficient and/or complex energy landscapes, it is natural to group displacements on the basis of where they take place, which is a key novelty of our approach. We categorize displacements based on where the initial and final positions are with respect to reference boundaries.
These displacement distributions were studied experimentally in Ref.

In the example depicted in Fig.

\mathcal{C}_{3}=\{k^{\rightarrow},\,k^{\leftarrow},\,B\}

The last class is composed of displacements where the initial and final points are on the same side of the boundary (bulk,
B
), like the gray trajectory in Fig.
1
(a).
The remaining two classes are for
displacements where the initial and final points are on opposite sides of
k
, where
k^{\rightarrow}
and
k^{\leftarrow}
denote displacements that cross
k
from left to right and right to left (orange and blue trajectories in Fig.
1
(a), respectively.)

Let us consider as an example, class

The statistics of such displacements are described by the joint probability density of observing a displacement

and of the displacement belonging to class

\displaystyle q_{\Delta t}(\ell,k^{\rightarrow})\equiv\int\limits_{-\infty}^{k}dx^{\prime}\int\limits_{k}^{\infty}dx\,\delta(\ell-(x-x^{\prime}))P_{\Delta t}(x|x^{\prime})P(x^{\prime})

(6)

where

is the probability of finding the particle at

P_{\Delta t}(x|x^{\prime})
is the propagator,

We also define the marginal probability of observing a displacement of class

\displaystyle p_{\Delta t}(k^{\rightarrow})\equiv\int_{-\infty}^{k}dx^{\prime}\int^{\infty}_{k}dx\,P_{\Delta t}(x|x^{\prime})P(x^{\prime}).

(7)

For a generic class of displacements

, the probability density is defined as

\displaystyle q_{\Delta t}(\ell,C)\equiv\iint\limits_{\Omega_{C}}dx^{\prime}\,dx\,\delta\left(\ell-(x-x^{\prime})\right)P_{\Delta t}(x|x^{\prime})P(x^{\prime})\,,

(8)

where the integral is over the domain

, which defines the class, and

\displaystyle p_{\Delta t}(C)\equiv\iint\limits_{\Omega_{C}}dx^{\prime}\,dx\,P_{\Delta t}(x|x^{\prime})P(x^{\prime})\,.

(9)

By normalisation we have that
\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}}\int d\ell\,q_{\Delta t}(\ell,C)=\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}}\,p_{\Delta t}(C)=1

III.3
Irreversibility and entropy

At equilibrium, the detailed balance condition
P_{\Delta t}(x|x^{\prime})P(x^{\prime})=P_{\Delta t}(x^{\prime}|x)P(x)

In the limit of small

\displaystyle\lim_{\Delta t\to 0}\frac{q_{\Delta t}(-\ell,\tilde{C})}{q_{\Delta t}(\ell,C)}=\lim_{\Delta t\to 0}\langle e^{-\Delta S_{tot}/k_{B}}|\ell,C\rangle\,

(11)

\ell

(see Appendix
B

Equation (

;

A hierarchy of bounds

IV
Case studies

IV.1
Nonequilibrium steady states

IV.1.1
Driven particle on a ring

As a first example, we consider a particle on a ring subject to a constant force
F=v\gamma
.
The system reaches a non-equilibrium steady-state (NESS) with a constant (in time and space) probability distribution and a constant probability flux
j\propto v
. This leads to a constant entropy production rate [see Eq. (
5
)] and in a time
\Delta t

\langle\Delta S_{tot}\rangle=k_{B}v^{2}\Delta t/D

The displacement distributions can be calculated analytically, because the propagators are Gaussian and the steady-state distribution is space-independent.
Since the system is uniform in space, it is not necessary to partition the displacements and
\Sigma_{\Delta t}(\mathcal{I})=k_{B}v^{2}\Delta t/D
coincides with total entropy produced, with
\Sigma_{\Delta t}(\mathcal{I})=\Sigma_{\Delta t}(\mathcal{C}),\,\forall\mathcal{C}
. For this example, a suitably defined TUR bound [see Eq. (
16
)] also captures the full dissipation.

V
Discussion

V.1
Summary of the results

We have introduced an experimentally accessible, model-free, direct measure of irreversibility based on classifying displacements according to their location and comparing them with suitably defined time-reversal displacements.
The method is experimentally feasible because it does not attempt to sample the overwhelmingly large space of trajectories and focuses on the initial and final points of displacements.
The risk of overlooking dissipation in this potentially lossy approach is mitigated by the fact that the method distinguishes displacements that take place in different locations.
We have discussed the relation between the irreversibility estimated by our method and entropy production, to which it can provide a lower bound, for example, for stationary dynamics.
By studying a forced particle in a potential on a ring, we have shown how the method captures more irreversibility than a naive TUR and progressively improves as we increase the number of classes in which we categorize the displacement.
We have also shown how, by monitoring the system at relatively high (but experimentally feasible) frequency, the method can identify the spatial profile of dissipation, as shown in Fig.
2
(c).
We have showcased the method’s potential in time-dependent nonequilibrium systems and demonstrated how it can be applied to study single-molecule force-spectroscopy experiments. This demonstrates that the applicability of the method is not restricted to ideal situations but extends to experiments where measurement noise and a limited number of trajectories impact the study.

V.2
Relation to existing measures of irreversibility

A direct comparison between the measure of irreversibility we are presenting and existing methods in the literature is not straightforward.
The main difficulty lies in the scarcity of model-free methods that can be applied to time-dependent continuous systems (such as driven Langevin systems) without requiring non-trivial processing of the data.
This is easier for stationary systems, where the TUR can be directly applied, as we discuss below.
For time-dependent systems, however, this is challenging. Applications of the TUR to systems under time-dependent force protocols require observing the response of the system to slight changes in the driving at every time point
46
, or knowledge of the spatial derivatives of the full force
96
(and thus an error-free estimation of the energy landscape). The machine-learning method of
66
and the estimator of
53
take after the TUR, and show accurate applicability to an RNA unfolding protocol at the cost of requiring relatively larger datasets and a less interpretable procedure. The recent method presented in
21
is suitable for studying linear force ramps of a protein, but it requires mapping the dynamics to discrete Markovian events like leaving (entering) the folded (unfolded) state. This is not trivial when working with unstable proteins (such as the wild-type R3 domain we are considering here), particularly if there is a strong presence of measurement noise as one detects spurious transitions.

The method of Ref.
87
is accessible and not data-intensive, but requires knowing the diffusion coefficient. This is challenging to estimate for systems evolving in the complex energy landscapes (such as proteins) measured at finite time resolution
74
, and made additionally difficult by measurement noise
57
.
In
SM-XI.1
, we compare our method to that of Ref.
87
for simulations in which
D
is known a priori. We find that, while the trend of higher dissipation at higher driving is also inferred, our method better captures the relative difference in irreversibility between low and high barrier systems and between folding and refolding ramps of the same, as shown in Fig.
S12
.
For the experimental studies, due to the challenges in measuring the diffusion coefficient,
we assume a reference space constant diffusion coefficient in both protein constructs. This prevents us from comparing the magnitude of the estimators but allows us to study their profiles in time and trends across experimental conditions. While the trends are roughly consistent, the method
of Ref.
87
does not capture that unfolding is more irreversible than folding in the R3
IVVI
construct at any pulling rate.

V.2.1
Relation to the thermodynamic uncertainty relation (TUR) for stationary systems.

For stationary systems, it is possible to directly compare our method to estimates made by the TUR using the same type of displacement statistics.
The TUR provides a lower bound to entropy production by considering the mean and fluctuations of a time-integrated current.
The displacements we are considering can be analyzed with a TUR approach since
\ell
is an integrated current
over the time window
\Delta t
,
\ell=\int_{t=0}^{t=\Delta t}dx_{t}
satisfying the
\ell\rightarrow-\ell
odd-parity under time reversal. For such a current, the TUR gives a bound of the entropy production by the mean net displacement
\langle\ell\rangle
and its variance as:

\displaystyle 2k_{B}\frac{\langle\ell\rangle^{2}}{\langle\ell^{2}\rangle-\langle\ell\rangle^{2}}\leq\langle\Delta S_{tot}\rangle\,.

(16)

This type of analysis does not take advantage of categorizing the displacements based on where they take place. In this respect, it can be compared to our method for the partitioning where all displacements belong to the same class
\mathcal{I}
.
Our case studies confirm that the two measures yield similar estimates of irreversibility, as shown in Fig.
2
(d).
For the cases we considered, categorizing displacements based on where they occur returns a better estimate of irreversibility, making them preferable to the TUR, especially for cases where the dynamics vary considerably in space.
When the system is driven far from equilibrium, all estimators capture more of the irreversibility (as noted for the TUR in a sinusoidal potential in
41
), but our method still outperforms the TUR.

It would be an interesting future direction to investigate the relation between the multi-dimensional TUR and our method based on categorizing displacements
53
.

V.3
Applications to biomolecular folding

Stochastic thermodynamics and its application to biomolecular folding have a rich history, dating back to Jarzynski and Crooks’ seminal work
43
;
19
. The application of fluctuation theorems to nonequilibrium single-molecule rupture experiments has enabled the recovery of folding free energies in general with good agreement with bulk biochemical data
17
, and, more recently, of other thermodynamic quantities such as enthalpy or heat capacity changes
75
.
These methodologies require starting from an equilibrium case, and therefore have mostly been employed to study thermodynamic differences between the folded and unfolded states.
Our measurement of irreversibility naturally handles nonequilibrium scenarios, providing detailed profiles of how irreversibility evolves in time. This offers new insight into the molecular mechanisms that drive transitions between the folded and unfolded states.

By precisely quantifying irreversibility, we have demonstrated that it is possible to distinguish thermodynamic differences between the folding mechanisms of the R3 wild-type and the widely studied R3
IVVI
mutant. Strikingly, while the mechanically fragile wild-type variant exhibits nearly symmetric unfolding and refolding transitions, R3
IVVI
shows higher irreversibility for unfolding compared to refolding. We traced back this asymmetry to features of the underlying energy landscape, in which the unfolding barrier lies closer to the folded basin, producing distinct curvatures for the folded and unfolded wells. These results agree with the observations made for DNA hairpins
56
;
2
.
Our method does not involve the difficult exponential average of Jarzynski-like estimators, which are typically biased
33
;
68
and is more robust to the presence of outliers. Additionally, unlike estimators using Crooks’ relation
19
, it does not require performing experiments with both the time-forward and time-backward protocols.
However, since our experimental protocol contains both the forward and backward protocols, we are able to estimate the dissipated work using Crooks’ relation and cross-validate our results. The dissipated work compute via Crooks’ relation is directly linked to entropy production when the protocol starts and ends at equilibrium.
The trend that wild-type talin R3 is more reversible than the IVVI mutant is confirmed, as is the fact that, for the mutant, unfolding is more irreversible than refolding.
We report these measurements in Fig.
S13
.

From the perspective of protein folding, talin R3 shows a rather unique behavior, hallmarked by its exquisite force-sensitivity both for unfolding and refolding that underpins its biological function as a threshold for cellular mechanotransduction
25
. By contrast, most classically studied proteins are mechanically rigid, unfolding at high forces with only weak force-dependence in their unfolding rates, while refolding at very low forces.
This large mechanical hysteresis generates a high dissipation during unfolding/refolding cycles, a property that could be essential to protect cellular structures against mechanical shocks
51
.
In this context, we speculate that such shock-absorbing proteins will be characterized by more pronounced asymmetries in entropy production across cyclic force protocols. Applying our method to these proteins would allow us to disentangle the differences in dissipation between folding and unfolding, identifying which step is responsible for most of the dissipation.

VI
Conclusions

Investigating the out-of-equilibrium behavior of small systems sheds light on their features, properties, and capabilities.
This is especially valuable for living systems, which typically operate away from equilibrium.
To this aim, we have proposed an experimentally accessible model-free method, and related it to entropy production.
We have highlighted the situations where the relation with entropy is tight and scrutinized the reasons behind it.
For stationary systems, the method can be leveraged to provide a spatial profile of dissipation identifying where the system dissipates the most.
Testing the method on nonequilibrium force-spectroscopy measurements of proteins revealed how, by providing a quantitative framework for nonequilibrium thermodynamics in biomolecular transitions, our method is sensitive to subtle mechanical and thermodynamic signatures that govern protein responses to force. This establishes it as a rigorous tool for rationalizing the function of mechanically active proteins.

The method we have proposed relies on the displacements measured at fixed time intervals. In the future, it will also be interesting to explore approaches where time is the random variable being observed to probe equilibrium, as done for example in Refs.
3
;
8
;
29
.

Appendix A
Proof of the entropy bound.

To prove that our measure of irreversibility gives a lower bound to entropy production, Eq. (
12
), we proceed in two steps.
The first step is to show that considering only the initial and final point of a displacement contains less information about irreversibility than the whole trajectory. The second step demonstrates that focusing on displacements
\ell=x-x^{\prime}
and on which class they belong to instead of retaining both
x^{\prime}
and
x
, results in a further loss of information.
We start by introducing the notation
P_{\Delta t}(x^{\prime},x)=P_{\Delta t}(x|x^{\prime})P(x^{\prime})
to denote the joint probability of starting with a particle in position
x^{\prime}
and finding it in position
x
after a time
\Delta t
.
Note that the order of the two variables matters, and
P_{\Delta t}(x,x^{\prime})=P_{\Delta t}(x^{\prime}|x)P(x)
denotes the joint probability of starting in
x
and arriving at
x^{\prime}
.

Starting from Eq. (
3
), we can marginalize the intermediate points in the trajectories
x^{\prime}\rightarrow x
. For a Markov process, this yields the joint probabilities as a function of the propagator and the initial distribution
P_{\Delta t}(x^{\prime},x)=P_{\Delta t}(x|x^{\prime})P(x^{\prime})
. Therefore as shown in
SM-I
,

\displaystyle D_{KL}\left(\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x}\right)||\mathcal{P}(\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}))\geq D_{KL}(P_{\Delta t}(x^{\prime},x)||P_{\Delta t}(x,x^{\prime}))\,,

(17)

where equality holds for one-dimensional systems and for systems where the propagator obeys a detailed-balance-like condition
\log[P(x|x^{\prime})/P(x^{\prime}|x)]=g(x)-g(x^{\prime})
for a generic function
g
.
For the second step, let us re-express the Kullback-Leibler divergence as a sum over the different classes of displacements that we are considering.
For a generic collection of classes
\mathcal{C}_{n}
, we have

\displaystyle D_{KL}(P_{\Delta t}(x^{\prime},x)||P_{\Delta t}(x,x^{\prime}))=\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{n}}\iint\limits_{\Omega_{C}}dx^{\prime}\,dx\,P_{\Delta t}(x^{\prime},x)\ln{\frac{P_{\Delta t}(x^{\prime},x)}{P_{\Delta t}(x,x^{\prime})}}\,.

(18)

Each class
C
, is defined by its support in
x^{\prime}
and
x
,
\Omega_{C}
, meaning from which region the displacement starts in and in which region it arrives at.
The time reversal of a displacement entails swapping the initial and final points. The time reversal of displacements belonging to class
C
, then, belong to class
\tilde{C}
with a support over domain
\Omega_{\tilde{C}}
where the initial and final domains are swapped (
i.e.
,
x^{\prime}
and
x
are exchanged).
We restrict ourselves to the case in which the domains are continuous intervals such that they can be written as
x^{\prime}\in[x^{\prime C}_{min},x^{\prime C}_{max}]
and
x\in[x^{C}_{min},x^{C}_{max}]
, with
x^{\prime C}_{min}<x^{\prime C}_{max}
and
x^{C}_{min}<x^{C}_{max}
. The integral over the domain
{\Omega_{C}}
, can then be written in terms of Heaviside theta functions, yielding

\displaystyle\iint\limits_{\Omega_{C}}dx^{\prime}\,dx=\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx^{\prime}\,dx\,[\Theta(x^{\prime}-x^{\prime C}_{min})-\Theta(x^{\prime}-x^{\prime C}_{max})][\Theta(x-x^{C}_{min})-\Theta(x-x^{C}_{max})]\,.

(19)

We define the short-hand notation

\displaystyle\theta_{x^{\prime},x}^{C}\equiv[\Theta(x^{\prime}-x^{\prime C}_{min})-\Theta(x^{\prime}-x^{\prime C}_{max})][\Theta(x-x^{C}_{min})-\Theta(x-x^{C}_{max})]\,,

(20)

substitute these into Eq. (
18
), and perform the change of variables
x\rightarrow x^{\prime}+\ell
with
d\ell=dx
to get

\displaystyle D_{KL}(P_{\Delta t}(x^{\prime},x)||P_{\Delta t}(x,x^{\prime}))=\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{n}}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx^{\prime}d\ell\;\theta_{x^{\prime},x^{\prime}+\ell}^{C}\,P_{\Delta t}(x^{\prime},x^{\prime}+\ell)\ln{\frac{P_{\Delta t}(x^{\prime},x^{\prime}+\ell)}{P_{\Delta t}(x^{\prime}+\ell,x^{\prime})}}\,.

(21)

Note
x^{C}_{min},x^{C}_{max}
are constants, which remain in

\theta_{x^{\prime},x^{\prime}+\ell}^{C}
, which reads

\displaystyle\theta_{x^{\prime},x^{\prime}+\ell}^{C}=[\Theta(x^{\prime}-x^{\prime C}_{min})-\Theta(x^{\prime}-x^{\prime C}_{max})][\Theta(x^{\prime}+\ell-x^{C}_{min})-\Theta(x^{\prime}+\ell-x^{C}_{max})]\,,

(22)

thus enforcing the same domain after the change of variables.

Focusing on a single
C
term of the class summation above, we can introduce the theta terms in the log-ratio and apply the log-sum inequality for a
dx^{\prime}
integration,

\displaystyle\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx^{\prime}d\ell\;\theta_{x^{\prime},x^{\prime}+\ell}^{C}\,P_{\Delta t}(x^{\prime},x^{\prime}+\ell)\ln{\frac{\theta_{x^{\prime},x^{\prime}+\ell}^{C}\,P_{\Delta t}(x^{\prime},x^{\prime}+\ell)}{\theta_{x^{\prime},x^{\prime}+\ell}^{C}\,P_{\Delta t}(x^{\prime}+\ell,x^{\prime})}}\geq

\displaystyle\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\ell\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx^{\prime}\;\theta_{x^{\prime},x^{\prime}+\ell}^{C}\,P_{\Delta t}(x^{\prime},x^{\prime}+\ell)\ln{\frac{\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx^{\prime}\,\theta_{x^{\prime},x^{\prime}+\ell}^{C}\,P_{\Delta t}(x^{\prime},x^{\prime}+\ell)}{\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx^{\prime}\,\theta_{x^{\prime},x^{\prime}+\ell}^{C}\,P_{\Delta t}(x^{\prime}+\ell,x^{\prime})}}\,

(23)

where the inequality follows from the log-sum inequality, which states that, for non-negative
a_{i}
and
b_{i}
,
\sum a_{i}\log(a_{i}/b_{i})\geq a\log(a/b)
, where
a=\sum_{i}a_{i}
and
b=\sum_{i}b_{i}
.
The integrands in
x^{\prime}
on the RHS of Eq. (
23
)
can be linked to the displacement distributions.
The term appearing in the numerator in the log ratio
coincides with the definition of the joint displacement distributions
q_{\Delta t}(\ell,C)
, as can be shown by carrying out the integral over
x

in Eq. (
8
):

\displaystyle q_{\Delta t}(\ell,C)\equiv\iint\limits_{\Omega_{C}}dx^{\prime}\,dx\,\delta\left(\ell-(x-x^{\prime})\right)P_{\Delta t}(x|x^{\prime})P(x^{\prime})=\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx^{\prime}\;\theta_{x^{\prime},x^{\prime}+\ell}^{C}\,P_{\Delta t}(x^{\prime},x^{\prime}+\ell)\,.

(24)

The denominator of the log-ratio of Eq. (
23
) is the integral of the time reverse joint probability
P_{\Delta t}(x^{\prime}+\ell,x^{\prime})=P_{\Delta t}(x^{\prime}|x^{\prime}+\ell)P(x^{\prime}+\ell)
, but still in the support
\theta_{x^{\prime},x^{\prime}+\ell}^{C}
of the forward class
C
.
To connect to Eq. (
10
), we wish to express this integral in terms of the displacements in the time-reverse class
\tilde{C}
.
Since the domain of the time-reverse displacements
\tilde{C}
corresponds to swapping the initial and final domains of the original class, we have

\theta_{x^{\prime},x^{\prime}+\ell}^{C}=\theta_{x^{\prime}+\ell,x^{\prime}}^{\tilde{C}}
(as one can check by direct substitution).
We then have (renaming the dummy integrating variable
x^{\prime}
as
x
for convenience)

\displaystyle\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\,\theta_{x,x+\ell}^{C}\,P_{\Delta t}(x+\ell,x)=\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\,\theta_{x+\ell,x}^{\tilde{C}}\,P_{\Delta t}(x+\ell,x)

(25)

\displaystyle=\iint\limits_{\Omega_{\tilde{C}}}dx^{\prime}\,dx\,P_{\Delta t}(x|x^{\prime})P(x^{\prime})\delta(\ell-(x^{\prime}-x))=q_{\Delta t}(-\ell,\tilde{C}).

This shows how the RHS of
Eq. (
23
) simplifies as

\displaystyle\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\ell\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx^{\prime}\;\theta_{x^{\prime},x^{\prime}+\ell}^{C}\,P_{\Delta t}(x^{\prime},x^{\prime}+\ell)\ln{\frac{\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx^{\prime}\,\theta_{x^{\prime},x^{\prime}+\ell}^{C}\,P_{\Delta t}(x^{\prime},x^{\prime}+\ell)}{\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx^{\prime}\,\theta_{x^{\prime},x^{\prime}+\ell}^{C}\,P_{\Delta t}(x^{\prime}+\ell,x^{\prime})}}=\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\ell\;q_{\Delta t}(\ell,C)\ln{\frac{q_{\Delta t}(\ell,C)}{q_{\Delta t}(-\ell,\tilde{C})}}\,.

(26)

Recalling the definition of our measure of irreversibility from
Eq. (
10
), the bound expressed in Eq. (
12
) then follows by applying the log-sum inequality Eq. (
23
) to each class in
\mathcal{C}_{n}
.
To summarize the proof and illustrate the bound, the two steps of information processing, starting from the full path probabilities, give

\displaystyle\langle\Delta S_{tot}\rangle=k_{B}D_{KL}\left(\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x}\right)||\mathcal{P}^{R}(\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}))\geq k_{B}D_{KL}(P_{\Delta t}(x^{\prime},x)||P_{\Delta t}(x,x^{\prime}))\geq\Sigma_{\Delta t}(\mathcal{C}_{n}).

(27)

This bound holds for any stationary systems described by an overdamped Langevin process. In the following subsection, we explain how it is generalized to non-stationary cases where the underlying forces are not time-dependent.

A.1
Time-dependent systems

We present here the generalization of our measure of irreversibility to time dependent systems, and discuss its relation to entropy production.
We consider two types of time-dependent systems: systems subject to a time-constant force profile, relaxing towards a steady state, and systems that are driven by time-dependent force protocols.
For time-constant profiles, we show how the measure of irreversibility provides a lower bound to entropy production for arbitrary
\Delta t
, while for time-dependent force protocols this holds in the
\Delta t\rightarrow 0
. We discuss the properties of the measure of irreversibility for finite
\Delta t
.

A.1.1
Relaxation dynamics

Consider the entropy production
\langle\Delta S_{tot}\rangle^{t}_{t-\Delta t}
in a time window
\Delta t
, happening between the times
t-\Delta t
and
t
.
When the forces governing the Langevin dynamics are time-independent, but the system has not relaxed to a steady-state yet, we have a time-dependence in the joint probability due to the starting distribution
P_{\Delta t,t-\Delta t}(x^{\prime},x)=P_{\Delta t}(x|x^{\prime})P_{t-\Delta t}(x^{\prime})
. In analogy to what we showed for time-independent dynamics in Eq. (
17
), we have

\displaystyle\langle\Delta S_{tot}\rangle^{t}_{t-\Delta t}\geq k_{B}D_{KL}(P_{\Delta t,t-\Delta t}(x^{\prime},x)||P_{\Delta t,t}(x,x^{\prime}))=k_{B}\iint dx^{\prime}\,dx\,P_{\Delta t}(x|x^{\prime})P_{t-\Delta t}(x^{\prime})\ln{\frac{P_{\Delta t}(x|x^{\prime})P_{t-\Delta t}(x^{\prime})}{P_{\Delta t}(x^{\prime}|x)P_{t}(x)}}\,.

(28)

The main difference with Eq. (
18
) is that for the time reversal the trajectories start at time
t
, sampled from
P_{t}(x)
.
We take this into account for classifying the displacements where the joint probability
q_{\Delta t,t-\Delta t}(\ell,C)
of displacements
\ell=x_{t}-x^{\prime}_{t-\Delta t}
starting at
t-\Delta t
in class
C
, and
q_{\Delta t,t}(-\ell,\tilde{C})
for the time-reverse displacements
-\ell=x_{t}-x^{\prime}_{t+\Delta t}
in class
\tilde{C}
. With this identification, we generalize our measure of irreversibility to time-dependent systems by defining

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\Delta t,t}(\mathcal{C}_{n})\equiv k_{B}\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{n}}\int d\ell\,q_{\Delta t,t-\Delta t}(\ell,C)\ln{\frac{q_{\Delta t,t-\Delta t}(\ell,C)}{q_{\Delta t,t}(-\ell,\tilde{C})}}\,.

(29)

Following the same logic as Appendix
A
, this quantity provides a lower bound to the average entropy production

\displaystyle\langle\Delta S_{tot}\rangle^{t}_{t-\Delta t}\geq\Sigma_{\Delta t,t}(\mathcal{C}_{n})

(30)

An additional remark is necessary for the calculation of
\Sigma_{\Delta t}^{trans}
under transience, modifying its definition Eq. (
14
).
The marginal probabilities of belonging to class
C
defined in Eq. (
9
), now have a global time
t
component

p_{\Delta t,t}(C)\equiv\iint\limits_{\Omega_{C}}dx^{\prime}\,dx\,P_{\Delta t}(x|x^{\prime})P_{t}(x^{\prime})=\int d\ell\,q_{\Delta t,t}(\ell,C)\,.

(31)

\Sigma_{\Delta t}^{trans}
is then defined as

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\Delta t,t}^{trans}\equiv k_{B}\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{n}}p_{\Delta t,t-\Delta t}(C)\ln{\frac{p_{\Delta t,t-\Delta t}(C)}{p_{\Delta t,t}(\tilde{C})}}

(32)

For the stationary case, displacements that under time reversal belonged to the same class (as the bulk classes in Section
IV.1.2
) canceled out and did not appear in Eq. (
14
).
For time-dependent systems, however, the probability of belonging to a class changes with time so that also classes that do not change under time reversal (as the bulk classes) must be accounted for in Eq. (
32
).
From Eq. (
31
) and applying the log-sum inequality to Eq. (
29
) (integrating over
d\ell
for each
C
) we have the bound

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\Delta t,t}(\mathcal{C}_{n})\geq\Sigma_{\Delta t,t}^{trans}\equiv k_{B}\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{n}}p_{\Delta t,t-\Delta t}(C)\ln{\frac{p_{\Delta t,t-\Delta t}(C)}{p_{\Delta t,t}(\tilde{C})}}\,.

(33)

A.1.2
Time-dependent driving

In cases where the forces
F(x_{\tau},\lambda(\tau))
in the Langevin dynamics have a time-dependent component
\lambda
, the time-reverse protocol [
\tilde{\lambda}(\tau)=\lambda(t-\tau)
, for a total protocol time
t
] must be included in the reverse path
\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}
probabilities to define the correct path-wise entropy production

\displaystyle\Delta S_{tot}(\mathbf{x})=k_{B}\ln{\frac{\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x},\lambda(\tau))}{\mathcal{P}^{R}(\widetilde{\mathbf{x}},\lambda(t-\tau))}}

(34)

As in Appendix
A.1.1
, we consider the entropy production
\langle\Delta S_{tot}\rangle^{t}_{t-\Delta t}
in a time window
\Delta t
, happening between the times
t-\Delta t
and
t
. Here the propagator component of the joint probability will also have an explicit global time dependence,
P_{\Delta t,t-\Delta t}(x^{\prime},x)=P_{\Delta t,t-\Delta t}(x|x^{\prime})P_{t-\Delta t}(x^{\prime})
, with
P_{\Delta t,t-\Delta t}(x^{\prime},x)
marking the evolution for a time
\Delta t
starting at
t-\Delta t
.

Infinitesimal time intervals

For infinitesimally small time intervals
dt
, the explicit time dependence of the propagator plays a secondary role and can be ignored. In order to see this,
consider the entropy produced for an infinitesimal time step
dt
during
[t-dt,dt]
, including protocol reversal from
t
in the next step
[t,t+dt]
,

\displaystyle dS_{tot}(x|x^{\prime})=k_{B}\ln\frac{P_{dt,t-dt}(x|x^{\prime})P_{t-dt}(x^{\prime})}{\widetilde{P}_{dt,t}(x^{\prime}|x)P_{t}(x)}\,,

(35)

where
dS_{tot}(x|x^{\prime})
denotes the entropy produced in a trajectory starting at position
x^{\prime}
and arriving at position
x
.
\widetilde{P}_{dt,t}(x^{\prime}|x)
is the infinitesimal propagator for the subsequent step with protocol reversal such that
\tilde{\lambda}(t+dt)=\lambda(t-dt)
.
To explicitly evaluate this expression, one can make use of the explicit Gaussian expression of the propagator for infinitesimal time steps
50

\displaystyle P_{dt,t-dt}(x|x^{\prime})\propto\exp\left[-\frac{\left(x-x^{\prime}-v_{\bar{t}}(\bar{x})dt\right)^{2}}{4Ddt}-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial v_{\bar{t}}(\bar{x})}{\partial x}\right]

(36)

where we evaluate the deterministic drift
v_{\tau}(x)=F(x,\lambda(\tau))/\gamma
at the midpoint
v_{\bar{t}}(\bar{x})
where
\bar{t}=t-dt/2
,
\bar{x}=(x^{\prime}+x)/2
, and for simplicity, assume a constant
D
.
Denoting
dx=x-x^{\prime}
, the ratio in Eq. (
35
) becomes

\displaystyle\frac{dS_{tot}(x|x^{\prime})}{k_{B}}=\frac{dx}{D}v_{\bar{t}}(\bar{x})+\ln{\frac{P_{t-dt}(x^{\prime})}{P_{t}(x)}}+\mathcal{O}(dt^{3/2})

(37)

where we have used that the reversed protocol,
\tilde{\lambda}(t+dt/2)=\lambda(t-dt/2)
.
Note that evaluating the drift at the start of the protocol,
v_{t-dt}(\bar{x})
, yields the same result to leading order, because, from the Langevin equation,
dx
contains contributions of order
dt
and
dt^{1/2}
.

If one cannot reverse the protocol, in an infinitesimal time step, comparing the displacements starting at
t-dt
with the ones starting at
t
leads to the same result as Eq. (
37
), to leading order

\displaystyle\ln{\frac{P_{\Delta t,t-dt}(x|x^{\prime})P_{t-dt}(x^{\prime})}{P_{\Delta t,t}(x^{\prime}|x)P_{t}(x)}}=\frac{dx}{D}v_{t}(\bar{x})+\ln{\frac{P_{t-dt}(x^{\prime})}{P_{t}(x)}}+\mathcal{O}(dt^{3/2})\,.

(38)

Repeating the same steps, as for the relaxation dynamics, shows that the measure of irreversibility introduced in Eq. (
29
) provides a lower bound to the average entropy production also for time-dependent driving, in the limit of infinitesimally small time intervals:
\Sigma_{\Delta t,t}(\mathcal{C}_{n})\leq\langle\Delta S_{tot}\rangle^{t}_{t-\Delta t}
as
\Delta t\rightarrow 0
.
For larger time intervals, the bound is not valid. This becomes more evident for faster dynamics, such as the fast pulling rates, which feature a strong time-dependence of
v
.
In Fig.
3
(c), we observed a slight overestimation of the true entropy production for the faster pulling rates measured with
\Delta t=5

\mathrm{m}\mathrm{s}
for the low barrier case. Note that, if one can reverse the protocol in the
q_{\Delta t,t}(-\ell,\tilde{C})
displacements of Eq. (
29
), the bound will hold for any
\Delta t
, but this is typically not operationally accessible.

Appendix B
Fluctuation Theorem

As discussed in Appendix
A
, in the limit of vanishing
\Delta t
, Eq. (
17
) is an equality. It then follows that

\displaystyle\frac{P_{\Delta t}(x|x^{\prime})P(x^{\prime})}{P_{\Delta t}(x^{\prime}|x)P(x)}=e^{\Delta S_{tot}(x|x^{\prime})/k_{B}}=e^{-\Delta S_{tot}(x^{\prime}|x)/k_{B}}\,,

(39)

where
\Delta S_{tot}(x|x^{\prime})
denotes the entropy produced in a trajectory starting at position
x^{\prime}
and arriving at position
x
10
10

10

In addition to infinitesimally short trajectories, the entropy produced is a function of the initial and final point only, also for one-dimensional trajectories as shown in
SM-I
.
.
This allows us to rewrite the probability of a displacement in terms of that of its time reversal and the entropy produced along such displacement.
We can rewrite Eq. (
8
) as

\displaystyle q_{\Delta t}(\ell,C)\equiv\iint\limits_{\Omega_{C}}dx^{\prime}\,dx\,\delta\left(\ell-(x-x^{\prime})\right)P_{\Delta t}(x|x^{\prime})P(x^{\prime})=\iint\limits_{\Omega_{C}}dx^{\prime}\,dx\,\delta\left(\ell-(x-x^{\prime})\right)P_{\Delta t}(x^{\prime}|x)P(x)e^{-\Delta S_{tot}(x^{\prime}|x)/k_{B}}\,.

(40)

Exchanging the role of the starting and final position
x\leftrightarrow x^{\prime}
maps the integration domain
\Omega_{C}
on to the one of its corresponding time-reversed class
\Omega_{\tilde{C}}
, so that we have

\displaystyle q_{\Delta t}(\ell,C)=\iint\limits_{\Omega_{\tilde{C}}}dx^{\prime}\,dx\,\delta\left(\ell+(x-x^{\prime})\right)P_{\Delta t}(x|x^{\prime})P(x^{\prime})e^{-\Delta S_{tot}(x|x^{\prime})/k_{B}}\,.

(41)

Taking the ratio of this probability with the probability of a time-reversed displacement, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{q_{\Delta t}(\ell,C)}{q_{\Delta t}(-\ell,\tilde{C})}=\langle e^{-\Delta S_{tot}/k_{B}}|-\ell,\tilde{C}\rangle\,,

(42)

which is the average of minus the entropy that is produced in a displacement, conditioned on the fact that the displacement is
-\ell
and of class
\tilde{C}
.
Similarly, one has

\displaystyle\frac{q_{\Delta t}(-\ell,\tilde{C})}{q_{\Delta t}(\ell,C)}=\langle e^{-\Delta S_{tot}/k_{B}}|\ell,C\rangle\,,

(43)

which is Eq. (
11
) in the main text.
This expression can be used to provide an alternative proof that the measure of irreversibility we have introduced provides a lower bound to entropy production, Eq. (
12
).
Applying Jensen inequality for convex functions, we have that

\displaystyle\langle e^{-\Delta S_{tot}/k_{B}}|\ell,C\rangle\geq\exp\left[\langle-\Delta S_{tot}/k_{B}|\ell,C\rangle\right]

(44)

Combining this with Eq. (
43
), taking the log and changing signs, we have

\displaystyle\langle\Delta S_{tot}/k_{B}|\ell,C\rangle\geq\ln\frac{q_{\Delta t}(\ell,C)}{q_{\Delta t}(-\ell,\tilde{C})}\,,

(45)

from which Eq. (
12
) follows, upon multiplication by
q_{\Delta t}(\ell,C)
and integration.

SM-I
1D Langevin Conservation of Entropy under Marginalization of Propagators

The joint probability distribution
P_{\Delta t}(x^{\prime},x)
is obtained by integrating out the constituent infinitesimal steps of a path
\mathbf{x}=[x_{0}=x^{\prime},x_{1},...,x_{N-1},x_{N}=x]
with
Ndt=\Delta t
, as by the Chapman-Kolmogorov relation

P_{\Delta t}(x^{\prime},x)=P(x^{\prime})\int\prod_{j=1}^{N-1}\,dx_{j}\,\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x}|x^{\prime})=P(x^{\prime})\int\prod_{j=1}^{N-1}\,dx_{j}\prod_{i=0}^{N-1}P(x_{i+1}|x_{i})

(S1)

Equivalently, we have
P_{\Delta t}(x,x^{\prime})
for the time-reverse trajectory
\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}=[x,x_{N-1},...,x_{1},x^{\prime}]
. These marginalizations give Eq. (
17
) via the log-sum inequality:

\displaystyle\langle\Delta S_{tot}\rangle

\displaystyle=k_{B}D_{KL}(\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x})||\mathcal{P}(\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}))

\displaystyle=k_{B}\int\mathcal{D}\mathbf{x}\,\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x}|x^{\prime})P(x^{\prime})\ln{\frac{\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x}|x^{\prime})P(x^{\prime})}{\mathcal{P}(\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}|x)P(x)}}

(S2)

\displaystyle\geq k_{B}D_{KL}(P_{\Delta t}(x^{\prime},x)||P_{\Delta t}(x,x^{\prime}))\equiv k_{B}\iint dx^{\prime}\,dx\,P_{\Delta t}(x|x^{\prime})P(x^{\prime})\ln{\frac{P_{\Delta t}(x|x^{\prime})P(x^{\prime})}{P_{\Delta t}(x^{\prime}|x)P(x)}}\,.

(S3)

From above, it is clear the equality will occur when the log ratio of the propagators
P_{\Delta t}(x|x^{\prime})/P_{\Delta t}(x^{\prime}|x)
is equal to that of trajectory probabilities
\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x}|x^{\prime})/P(\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}|x)
,
i.e.
, if
x^{\prime}
and
x
are the only relevant degrees of freedom to the total dissipation. As we will show below, this is the case for a one-dimensional Langevin system subject to a time-independent drift and diffusion coefficient: with dynamics
\dot{x}_{t}=v(x_{t})+\sqrt{2D}\xi_{t}
but it is no longer true for a time-dependent drift.

Let us start by constructing the ratio of the full path probabilities before marginalizing to the joints. We discretize the path integral in the Stratonovich sense, which makes the discussion of time-reversal more direct. For an additive noise, the infinitesimal propagator
50
reads

\displaystyle P(x_{i+1}|x_{i})\propto\exp\left[{-\frac{1}{4Ddt}(\Delta x_{i}-v_{i}(\bar{x}_{i})dt)^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\nabla v_{i}(\bar{x}_{i})dt}\right]

(S4)

with
\bar{x}_{i}=\frac{1}{2}(x_{i}+x_{i+1})
the midpoint where the forces
F_{i}(\bar{x}_{i})=\gamma v_{i}(\bar{x}_{i})
are evaluated, and
\Delta x_{i}=x_{i+1}-x_{i}
. In one dimension, a non-conservative force
f_{\rm{0}}
and a conservative one stemming form a potential
W
can be written as the derivative of an effective potential
V(x)=W(x)-f_{\rm{0}}x
. Here, we also consider a time-linear force ramp
with rate
r
, so

, and the path probabilities

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x}|x^{\prime})=\prod_{i=0}^{N-1}P(x_{i+1}|x_{i})

\displaystyle\propto\exp\left[\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}{-\frac{1}{4Ddt}(\Delta x_{i}-v_{i}(\bar{x}_{i})dt)^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\nabla v(\bar{x}_{i})dt}\right]

(S5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}|x)=\prod_{i=0}^{N-1}P(\tilde{x}_{i+1}|\tilde{x}_{i})

\displaystyle\propto\exp\left[\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}{-\frac{1}{4Ddt}(\Delta x_{i}+v_{i}(\bar{x}_{i})dt)^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\nabla v(\bar{x}_{i})dt}\right]

(S6)

with
\tilde{x}_{i}=x_{N-i}
leading to a sign flip in Eq. (
S6
) to substitute
\Delta x_{i}
in terms of the time-forward increments. We drop the
i
subscript of
\nabla v_{i}(x_{i})
because it does not depend on time. The two expressions above differ only in the cross term of infinitesimal displacement with drift in the squared brackets (
\Delta x_{i}v_{i}(\bar{x}_{i})
, other terms will cancel inside the log-ratio of Eq. (
S2
)). Substituting for
v(\bar{x}_{i})
, these terms give

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}{\frac{1}{2D\gamma}\Delta x_{i}(-\nabla V(\bar{x}_{i})+r(i+\frac{1}{2})dt)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2k_{B}T}(W(x^{\prime})-W(x)+(x-x^{\prime})f_{0})+\frac{r}{2k_{B}T}\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}{\Delta x_{i}(i+\frac{1}{2})dt}

(S7)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}{\frac{-1}{2D\gamma}\Delta x_{i}(-\nabla V(\bar{x}_{i})+r(i+\frac{1}{2})dt)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2k_{B}T}(W(x)-W(x^{\prime})+(x^{\prime}-x)f_{0})-\frac{r}{2k_{B}T}\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}{\Delta x_{i}(i+\frac{1}{2})dt}

(S8)

for the time-forward and reverse path probabilities, respectively. The first term in each expression contains a contribution from the conservative and non-conservative forces, which only depend on the initial and final states of the paths.
This shows how, if the force is not changed (
r=0
), the path probabilities depend only on the initial and final points. As a consequence, marginalizing over the intermediate steps is trivial, with
\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x}|x^{\prime})=P(x|x^{\prime})
and
\mathcal{P}(\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}|x_{N})=P(x^{\prime}|x)
, there is no information lost going from Eq. (
S2
) to Eq. (
S3
). In this case, we can recover the full entropy production with only information about the
x^{\prime}
and
x
degrees of freedom,

\displaystyle\langle\Delta S_{tot}\rangle=k_{B}D_{KL}(\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x})||\mathcal{P}(\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}))=k_{B}D_{KL}(P_{\Delta t}(x^{\prime},x)||P_{\Delta t}(x,x^{\prime})).

(S9)

For time-dependent forces, however, we cannot simplify the last ramp-dependent terms, so applying the
\prod_{j=1}^{N-1}\,dx_{j}
marginalization to the path propagators will lose information about the dissipation the longer the considered
\Delta t
is.

The log-ratio of the two path probabilities is given by the difference of Eqs. (
S7
) and (
S8
)

\frac{\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x}|x^{\prime})}{\mathcal{P}(\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}|x)}=\frac{1}{k_{B}T}\left[W(x^{\prime})-W(x)+(x-x^{\prime})f_{0}\right]+\frac{r}{k_{B}T}\left[-\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}x_{i}dt+\left(N-\frac{1}{2}\right)x\,dt-\frac{1}{2}x^{\prime}dt\right]

(S10)

where the last sum is more conveniently represented in the continuum limit

\frac{\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x}|x^{\prime})}{\mathcal{P}(\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}|x)}=\frac{1}{k_{B}T}\left[W(x^{\prime})-W(x)+(x-x^{\prime})f_{0}\right]+\frac{1}{k_{B}T}\left(rx\Delta t-r\int_{0}^{\Delta t}x_{t}dt\right).

(S11)

Again, this is equal to

only if there is no change in the applied force,

SM-II
Short time expansion of
\Sigma_{\Delta t}^{trans}

In this section we provide the derivation for the short time approximation Eq. (
15
) of the
\Sigma_{\Delta t}^{trans}
defined in Eq. (
14
).

The small
\Delta t
propagator for a general Langevin process
\dot{x}=v(x_{t})+\sqrt{2D}\xi_{t}
with deterministic drift
v(x)=\frac{1}{\gamma}(F_{0}-\nabla W(x))
, in the Ito discretization is given by
76

P_{\Delta t}(x|x^{\prime})\approx\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi D\Delta t}}e^{-\left(x-x^{\prime}-v(x^{\prime})\Delta t\right)^{2}/4D\Delta t}.

(S12)

Assuming that, for short times, displacements which cross
k
are start/end in its vicinity,
we Taylor expand
P(x^{\prime})
and
v(x^{\prime})
around
k
. We have
P(x^{\prime})\approx P(k)+(x^{\prime}-k)\nabla P(k)
and
v(x^{\prime})\approx v(k)+(x^{\prime}-k)\nabla v(k)
. We keep the 2nd and 1st order terms in each, respectively, because their higher orders of
x^{\prime}
do not affect the lowest order term in
\Delta t
in the final result. Expanding the marginal probabilities [see Eqs. (
7
) and (
9
)] of crossing
k
in
\Delta t
,

\displaystyle p_{\Delta t}(k^{\rightarrow})

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{k}dx^{\prime}\int^{\infty}_{k}dx\,P_{\Delta t}(x|x^{\prime})P(x^{\prime})

\displaystyle\approx\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi D\Delta t}}\int_{-\infty}^{k}dx^{\prime}\int^{\infty}_{k}dx\,e^{-\left(x-x^{\prime}-v(k)\Delta t\right)^{2}/4D\Delta t}(P(k)+(x^{\prime}-k)\nabla P(k))

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{D\Delta t}{\pi}}e^{-v(k)^{2}\Delta t/4D}\left(P(k)-\frac{\Delta t}{2}v(k)\nabla P(k)\right)+\frac{\Delta t}{2}\left(1+\textrm{Erf}\left[v(k)\sqrt{\frac{\Delta t}{4D}}\right]\right)\left(vP(k)-(D+\frac{\Delta t}{2}v(k)^{2})\nabla P(k)\right)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{D\Delta t}{\pi}}P(k)+\frac{1}{2}(v(k)P(k)-D\nabla P(k))\Delta t+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{3/2})

(S13)

\displaystyle p_{\Delta t}(k^{\leftarrow})

\displaystyle=\int^{\infty}_{k}dx^{\prime}\int_{-\infty}^{k}dx\,P_{\Delta t}(x|x^{\prime})P(x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{D\Delta t}{\pi}}P(k)-\frac{1}{2}(v(k)P(k)-D\nabla P(k))\Delta t+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{3/2})

(S14)

Recognizing that
v(k)P(k)-D\nabla P(k)=j(k)
, the probability flux at
k
, we first make the identification
p_{\Delta t}(k^{\rightarrow})-p_{\Delta t}(k^{\leftarrow})=j\Delta t
. This is generally true for all times. Let’s continue with the expansion of the
\Sigma_{\Delta t}^{trans}
bound for lowest order in
\Delta t
;

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\Delta t}^{trans}/\Delta t

\displaystyle=\frac{k_{B}}{\Delta t}[p_{\Delta t}(k^{\rightarrow})-p_{\Delta t}(k^{\leftarrow})]\ln{\frac{p_{\Delta t}(k^{\rightarrow})}{p_{\Delta t}(k^{\leftarrow})}}

(S15)

\displaystyle\approx j(k)k_{B}\ln{\frac{1+\frac{j(k)}{2P(k)}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{D\Delta t}}}{1-\frac{j(k)}{2P(k)}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{D\Delta t}}}}\Delta t=\frac{j(k)^{2}}{P(k)}k_{B}\sqrt{\frac{\pi\Delta t}{D}}+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t)

(S16)

\displaystyle=\dot{\sigma}(k)\sqrt{\pi D\Delta t}+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t)

(S17)

where the density of dissipation rate
\dot{\sigma}(k)=k_{B}\,j(k)^{2}/DP(k)
.

SM-IV
Empirical estimation of Kullback-Leibler Divergences

Calculating KL-divergences naively from empirical histograms incurs systematic biases
36
, difficult to correct for with limited sample sizes, and suffers from a strong dependence on binning size.
Therefore, we choose to calculate the
D_{KL}
’s of displacement distributions using a nearest-neighbors (NN) technique, which avoids binning
98
. This must be adapted to the estimation of the mixed discrete and continuous
\Sigma_{\Delta t}(\mathcal{C}_{n})
, outlined here.

We exemplify the procedure on one integral term for the displacement class
C\in\mathcal{C}_{n}
in the summation of Eq. (
10
), which can be written in terms of the
D_{KL}
of the conditional distributions (with respect to just the continuous variable
\ell
):

\displaystyle\int d\ell\,q_{\Delta t}(\ell,C)\ln{\frac{q_{\Delta t}(\ell,C)}{q_{\Delta t}(-\ell,\tilde{C})}}=p_{\Delta t}(C)\left[D_{KL}\left(q_{\Delta t}(\ell|C)\big|\big|q_{\Delta t}(-\ell|\tilde{C})\right)+\ln{\frac{p_{\Delta t}(C)}{p_{\Delta t}(\tilde{C})}}\right],

(S27)

where the conditional probability density
q_{\Delta t}(\ell|C)\equiv q_{\Delta t}(\ell,C)/p_{\Delta t}(C)
.

For a total number of tracked particles
N
, we consider the empirical probabilities of displacement classes
C
and
\tilde{C}
,
\hat{p}_{\Delta t}(C)\equiv N_{C}/N
and
\hat{p}_{\Delta t}(\tilde{C})\equiv N_{\tilde{C}}/N
, such that
\langle\hat{p}_{\Delta t}(C)\rangle=p_{\Delta t}(C)
,
\langle\hat{p}_{\Delta t}(\tilde{C})\rangle=p_{\Delta t}(\tilde{C})
.
Crucially, this method does not require estimating the empirical densities
\hat{q}_{\Delta t}(\ell|C)
and
\hat{q}_{\Delta t}(\ell|\tilde{C})
, but estimates their
D_{KL}
based on nearest neighbors assignments across the set of displacement measurements belonging to class
C
and
\tilde{C}
.
For each element
\ell_{j}
of the empirical set of displacement measurements belonging to class
C
,
\{\ell_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N_{C}}
, we identify the element
that has the closest value in the set,
\ell_{k}
, (
i.e.
, its nearest neighbor, the element with the shortest distance
\rho(j)=|\ell_{j}-\ell_{k}|
).
We then turn to the
the set of reversed displacements of class
\tilde{C}
,
\{-\tilde{\ell}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N_{\tilde{C}}}
, and identify the nearest neighbor there,
\tilde{\ell}_{k^{\prime}}
, which has the shortest distance from
\ell_{j}
,
\nu(j)=|\ell_{j}-\tilde{\ell}_{k^{\prime}}|
.
The empirical
D_{KL}
is then obtained as

\displaystyle\hat{D}_{KL}\left(q_{\Delta t}(\ell|C)\big|\big|q_{\Delta t}(-\ell|\tilde{C})\right)=\frac{1}{N_{C}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{C}}\left[\log{\frac{\nu(j)}{\rho(j)}}\right]+\ln{\frac{N_{\tilde{C}}}{N_{C}-1}}\,.

(S28)

Substituting the above into Eq. (
S27
), and the marginal probability estimates, we have our estimator

\displaystyle\hat{\Sigma}_{\Delta t}(\mathcal{C}_{n})\equiv\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{n}}^{n}\frac{N_{C}}{N}\left[\frac{1}{N_{C}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{C}}\left[\log{\frac{\nu(j)}{\rho(j)}}\right]+\ln{\frac{N_{C}}{N_{C}-1}}\right].

(S29)

where

SM-XI.2
Dissipated work via Crooks’ Fluctuation Theorem

For a non-equilibrium process starting at equilibrium, Crooks’ Fluctuation Theorem is a reliable method for measuring equilibrium free energy differences
\Delta F
, provided one has access to the reverse protocol, also starting at equilibrium. It is given by
19

\displaystyle\frac{p(W)}{p(-W^{R})}=\exp\left(\frac{W-\Delta F}{k_{B}T}\right),

(S34)

where
p(W)
is the distribution of work done during the forward protocol and
W^{R}
during the reverse. The quantity in the exponential term is bounded by the second law of thermodynamics and is known as the dissipated work
W_{d}=W-\Delta F\geq 0
. When the process in question also
ends
at equilibrium (we have that
\Delta F-\langle\Delta V\rangle=-T\langle\Delta S_{sys}\rangle
for a change in internal energy
\Delta V
), then
W_{d}/T=\langle\Delta S_{tot}\rangle

9
. In our non-equilibrium force ramps of proteins, even at high pulling forces, we can assume the system to be practically equilibrated at the end of each ramp. In fact, we have checked in experiment and simulation there is no measurable dissipation during the relaxation of the protein at constant force at the end of the protocol.

In Fig.
S13
, we report our measurements of
W_{d}
in experiment and simulation, using Crooks’ Fluctuation Theorem for
\Delta F
by solving
p(W)=p(-W^{R})
via the Bennet Acceptance Ratio method
7
. Crucially, with data from a magnetic tweezers setup,
W
can be directly evaluated from
W=\int\partial_{\lambda}Vd\lambda=-\int x\,df
, since the control parameter of the protocol
\lambda
is the pulling force itself
1
. From the simulation analysis of Fig.
S13
(b) we corroborate the equivalence with the total entropy production, given by the stars closest to the respective
W_{d}
measurement. Despite using
5000
trajectories and adding noise to the positions there is good agreement with the theory.

One expects then, that the experimental measurements in Fig.
S13
(a) mark values very close to the true dissipation. We note again the use of two different proteins used for the
|r|=5
and 20
\mathrm{pN}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}
in the R3
IVVI
experiments. Unlike our
\Sigma_{\Delta t}(\mathcal{C}_{4})
measurement, we cannot pool both datasets to find
p(W)=p(-W^{R})
, as distributions of
W
are very different when the extensions are too. This can be seen in the
W_{d}
measurements for
\pm 20~$\mathrm{pN}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}$
in Fig.
S13
(a) stemming from their averages. The trends across pulling rates are in general agreement with those captured by our irreversibility measurement in Fig.
4
(d). Namely, higher dissipation as pulling rate is increased, similar dissipation between folding and refolding ramps in the R3
WT
, more dissipation overall in the R3
IVVI
compared to the R3
WT
, and a consistently higher dissipation for unfolding over refolding in the R3
IVVI
.

The single-molecule experiments were conducted on a home-made magnetic tweezers setup as described in

The image is collected with a complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor camera.

Single-molecule experiments were conducted in custom-made fluid chambers assembled as two cover slides (40x24 mm and 22x22 mm) sandwiched between a laser-cut parafilm pattern. The bottom glasses are silanized for amine functionalization after cleaning and plasma-activation, as described in
