Directions for Establishing a Korean National One Health Joint Plan of Action: Focusing on Zoonotic Disease Preparedness and Response
Authors: Su Ji Heo, Hwa-Young Lee, Jong-Hee Kim, Hyeonsu Lee, Sang Gu Yeo, 수지 허, 화영 이, 종희 김, 현수 이, 상구 여
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study proposes directions for establishing a Korean National One Health Joint Plan of Action to address health threats arising from complex interactions among humans, animals, and the environment, with a particular focus on zoonotic disease preparedness and response.MethodsThis study analyzed the six Action Tracks outlined in the One Health Joint Plan of Action proposed by the Quadripartite organizations, comprising the World Health Organization, Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations, World Organization for Animal Health, and United Nations Environment Programme. Considering the domestic administrative structures and institutional context of the Republic of Korea (ROK), this study focused on Action Tracks 2 and 3, which are directly associated with zoonotic disease preparedness, prevention, and response.ResultsThis study demonstrated that responses to zoonotic and vector-borne diseases should move beyond traditional single-sector approaches and evolve into integrated response models based on multisectoral collaborative governance, interconnected surveillance systems linking human, animal, and environmental sectors, and community-based risk management frameworks. Furthermore, the proposed implementation directions provide an institutional foundation for transforming the current response system from a reactive, post-event framework into a preventive and risk-based management system.ConclusionsThis study contextualized the international One Health Joint Plan of Action within the ROK’s domestic zoonotic disease response framework. The proposed implementation strategies may provide policy evidence for establishing a national One Health governance framework that integrates prevention, early detection, and risk management through coordinated intersectoral roles and institutional collaboration.Keywords: One Health, Zoonotic diseases, Vector-borne disease, Climate change

Key messages

① What is known previously?

Climate change, ecological disruption, and increasing human–animal interactions have heightened zoonotic disease risks, reinforcing the global need for a One Health approach that integrates human, animal, and environmental sectors. The Quadripartite organizations developed the One Health Joint Plan of Action and recommended national implementation strategies to prevent future pandemics and strengthen global health security. However, a comprehensive institutional framework for implementing such an approach has not yet been established in the Republic of Korea.

② What new information is presented?

This study proposed a Korean national One Health implementation framework tailored to the domestic context, focusing on zoonotic disease preparedness and response through integrated governance mechanisms, interoperable surveillance systems, and risk-based management approaches.

③ What are implications?

The proposed framework provides policy-level justification for institutionalizing multisectoral coordination and advancing prevention- and risk-based infectious disease governance.

Introduction

Due to factors such as climate change, ecosystem changes, and increased contact between humans and animals, the risk of occurrence of emerging and re-emerging infectious diseases is increasing globally [1,2]. Infectious diseases emerge and spread within complex interactions among humans, animals, and the environment, and approximately 75% or more of emerging infectious diseases are zoonotic diseases transmitted between humans and animals [1,3]. In addition, climate change and ecosystem disruption are reported as major factors that increase the risk of occurrence of zoonotic diseases and vector-borne diseases by altering the distribution of vectors and hosts and changing the pathogen transmission environment [3].

As an integrated approach to effectively respond to these infectious disease threats, the international community presents One Health as an important public health strategy [3]. One Health is defined as a collaborative system to prevent and manage health threats through cooperation among sectors such as healthcare, veterinary medicine, and the environment, based on the concept that the health of humans, animals, plants, and the environment is interdependent [4]. This has developed into an integrated strategy encompassing not only the response to zoonotic diseases but also various public health issues, including antimicrobial resistance, food safety, environmental pollution, and biodiversity conservation [5,6]. In other words, One Health provides a foundation to comprehensively understand the root causes of the occurrence and spread of infectious diseases to achieve optimal health outcomes. Furthermore, it establishes and implements prevention, surveillance, and response strategies in a multidisciplinary manner [5,7].

In accordance with these international trends, the Quadripartite, which is composed of the World Health Organization, the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations, the World Organisation for Animal Health, and the United Nations Environment Programme, released the “One Health Joint Plan of Action (OH JPA)” in 2022. Centering on six Action Tracks, which include strengthening health systems, responding to emerging and re-emerging zoonotic diseases, controlling vector-borne diseases, food safety, antimicrobial resistance, and the environment, the OH JPA presented national-level One Health action plans and recommended that countries establish action plans suited to their respective policy environments [8]. Furthermore, through subsequent guidelines, it presented a stepwise implementation framework ranging from national situational analysis to implementation, monitoring, and evaluation to ensure that national-level One Health joint action plans can be established and implemented based on multisectoral governance and intersectoral collaboration frameworks [9].

Since then, internationally, various implementation cases have been reported to establish One Health implementation systems at the national and regional levels and to strengthen multisectoral collaboration [10]. Domestically, a One Health approach linking the human, animal, and environmental sectors is also being applied to some infectious disease response policies. For example, the operation of a cooperative response system between the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency (KDCA) and the Ministry of National Defense for malaria eradication, joint responses by related ministries such as joint epidemiological investigations upon the occurrence of secondary transmission cases of severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome, development of joint epidemiological investigation manuals, and One Health-based inter-ministerial simulation exercises have been promoted. In addition, the foundation for multisectoral collaboration is also expanding, including wildlife disease surveillance, establishment of human-animal infectious disease information-sharing systems, and operation of consultative bodies of related ministries. However, domestic One Health-related policies and projects are still often promoted focusing on individual infectious diseases or specific areas, and research on specifying the international OH JPA as a national-level implementation strategy by linking it with the domestic administrative system is limited [5,11]. In particular, because zoonotic diseases occur and spread at the point where complex risk factors of humans, animals, and the environment intersect, the effectiveness of prevention and control can be maximized only when multisectoral information sharing and joint response systems are linked from the early stages of an infectious disease outbreak [12].

Therefore, this study aimed to propose the basic direction of a Korean National OH JPA reflecting the domestic administrative system and policy environment, based on the Quadripartite’s OH JPA. In particular, focusing on the Action Tracks directly linked to zoonotic disease response and vector-borne disease management, the study sought to derive domestically applicable implementation strategies and provide baseline data for establishing national-level One Health governance and preparing inter-ministerial collaboration systems in the future.

Methods

This study was conducted based on literature analysis and policy analysis. It analyzed the OH JPA [8] and national-level implementation guidelines [9], and compared and analyzed domestic One Health-related policies and administrative systems to derive the implementation direction of the Korean National OH JPA suitable for the domestic administrative system and policy environment.

The research procedure was conducted in three steps. First, a literature analysis was conducted on the OH JPA and its implementation guidelines. The analysis items were set as the Goal, Action Track, Action, Activity, national-level implementation framework, and multisectoral collaboration framework of the OH JPA. Through this, the Goal-Action-Activity structure and national-level implementation framework of the OH JPA were summarized. Second, domestic One Health-related policies and administrative systems were analyzed. Legislation, basic plans, and policy materials of relevant ministries—including the KDCA, the Ministry of Agriculture, Food and Rural Affairs (MAFRA), the Animal and Plant Quarantine Agency (APQA), and the Ministry of Climate, Energy and Environment (MCEE)—and materials related to the One Health collaboration framework were collected to analyze the roles and functions of each agency, inter-ministerial collaboration frameworks, infectious disease response systems, and current status of One Health implementation. Furthermore, case studies of domestic One Health-based collaboration were reviewed to derive collaboration frameworks and policy linkage elements to be considered when establishing a national-level OH JPA. Third, the preceding results were synthesized to compare and analyze the linkages between the OH JPA and domestic policies and administrative systems. Based on this, the Goal-Action-Activity framework proposed in the OH JPA was substantiated into implementation directions applicable to the domestic policy environment, and implementation strategies and detailed activities suitable for domestic zoonotic disease responses were derived. In this process, among the six Action Tracks of the OH JPA, the implementation directions were structured focusing on Action Track 2 (Reducing the risks from emerging and re-emerging zoonotic epidemics and pandemics) and Action Track 3 (Controlling and eliminating vector-borne diseases), which are directly relevant to domestic zoonotic disease response.

Results

1. Core Contents of the One Health Joint Plan of Action (2022–2026)

The OH JPA is a framework to systematically implement the One Health approach at the national and international levels. It consists of six Action Tracks comprising Goals, implementation strategies, and detailed activities. Furthermore, based on the interconnections among humans, animals, and the environment, it presents a multisectoral collaboration system and national-level governance as core principles to manage health threats across the whole-of-cycle of prevention, early detection, response, and recovery.

Action Track 1, with the goal of strengthening One Health capacity to strengthen health systems, proposed the establishment of One Health governance, training and education of professional personnel, and strengthening inter-ministerial cooperation systems as major implementation strategies. Action Track 2, with the goal of reducing the risk of emerging and re-emerging zoonotic diseases and pandemics, included risk factor management, early detection, and the establishment of a surveillance, early warning, and response system linking humans, animals, and the environment as major implementation strategies. Action Track 3, with the goal of controlling and eliminating endemic zoonotic diseases, neglected tropical diseases (NTDs), and vector-borne diseases, proposed prevention and management focused on vulnerable areas and high-risk groups, improvement of policies and institutions, and strengthening of international cooperation as major implementation strategies. Action Track 4, with the goal of assessing and managing food safety hazards and strengthening risk communication, included the establishment of a national food safety management system, scientific risk assessment, and strengthening of foodborne infectious disease surveillance systems as major contents. Action Track 5, with the goal of responding to antimicrobial resistance, proposed the appropriate use of antimicrobials in the human, animal, and environmental sectors, the establishment of an integrated surveillance system, and strengthening of international cooperation as major implementation strategies. Action Track 6 proposed implementation directions to integrate environmental factors into the One Health approach through biodiversity conservation, environmental pollution management, and the promotion of ecosystem health.

In other words, it was confirmed that the OH JPA is not limited to responding to individual infectious diseases but presents a national-level One Health implementation framework that includes establishing governance, multisectoral collaboration, integrated surveillance systems, and risk-based management, and provides a basic framework for establishing implementation plans tailored to policy environment of each country.

2. Overview of the Korean National One Health Joint Plan of Action

In the domestic human, animal, and environmental sectors, management systems are operated centering on the KDCA, MAFRA and APQA, and the MCEE, respectively. Although surveillance and response systems for each sector, including human infectious disease surveillance, livestock disease management, and wildlife and environmental management, are established centering on individual ministries, it was analyzed that a regular cooperation system for integrally linking human, animal, and environmental information and a national-level integrated implementation system are limited. As One Health-related policies are also being promoted centering on individual ministries, there is a need to strengthen the national-level cooperation system and the foundation for joint implementation.

As a result of synthesizing the literature analysis and the analysis of domestic policies and administrative systems, this study derived the implementation direction of the Korean National OH JPA applicable to domestic conditions, using the six Action Tracks of the OH JPA as a benchmark. While reflecting the whole-of-cycle approach encompassing prevention, early detection, and response proposed in the OH JPA, the implementation direction was concretized around the response to zoonotic diseases, considering the domestic infectious disease management system and inter-ministerial role structures. Zoonotic diseases are representative One Health issues arising from interactions among humans, livestock, wildlife, and the environment, and are areas where collaboration among the human, animal, and environmental sectors is essential. Accordingly, the Korean National OH JPA structured its implementation direction focusing on Action Track 2 and Action Track 3, which are directly linked to the domestic zoonotic disease response among the six Action Tracks of the OH JPA.

As the core implementation directions of the Korean National OH JPA, the establishment of multisectoral governance; an integrated surveillance system linking human, animal, and environmental information; joint risk assessment; a risk-based management system; and community participation were proposed. In addition, the implementation directions were structured based on a stepwise framework linking Goal-Implementation Strategy-Detailed Activity and were materialized by reflecting the domestic policy environment and inter-ministerial cooperation systems.

3. Application of the One Health Joint Plan of Action Centered on the Response to Zoonotic Diseases

1) Action Track 2. Emerging and re-emerging zoonotic diseases and pandemic risk reduction

As a result of the literature analysis and the analysis of domestic policies and administrative systems, Action Track 2 was identified as an implementation area that can be directly linked to the domestic zoonotic disease response system (Table 1, Figure 1). Accordingly, this study derived the establishment of multisectoral governance, a risk-based management system, an integrated surveillance system, joint risk assessment, and the linkage of wildlife-based surveillance systems as key implementation strategies for domestic application. First, in the area of governance, to establish a permanent cooperative system covering the whole-of-cycle of prevention, detection, and response, the operation of a high-level multi-ministerial coordination system and sector-specific technical consultative bodies was proposed as a key implementation strategy. As the domestic cooperative system among relevant ministries is characterized by being case-specific or temporary, this study proposed a direction for establishing governance centered on a permanent cooperative system. In addition, the study suggested improving the existing quarantine and management systems, which are currently segmented by animal species, and transitioning toward an integrated management direction that considers risk levels and the breeding and distribution environment. In the livestock sector, the study proposed strengthening animal welfare and biosecurity standards and presenting a biosecurity management system applicable to farms and livestock facilities. For this purpose, there is a need to strengthen the cooperative system among livestock quarantine officers, veterinary experts, and local government practitioners while simultaneously operating education and inspection systems that consider on-site applicability. Furthermore, the introduction of a risk-based management system (risk-based approach) could be considered to select high-risk areas for zoonotic diseases by comprehensively considering environmental and ecological factors, such as the history of infectious disease outbreaks, climate and environmental changes, and wildlife habitat characteristics, and to apply differentiated surveillance and quarantine measures to those areas. This has high applicability in that it can strengthen proactive response capabilities focused on high-risk areas while efficiently utilizing limited resources and personnel.

Integrated risk management framework for emerging and re-emerging zoonotic diseases based on a One Health approach

In the companion animal and wildlife management sector, a risk-based management system considering the human-animal interface was proposed as a major implementation strategy. In the companion animal sector, risk-based management of trade and movement processes, improving the effectiveness of the companion animal registration system, and education and public awareness for companion animal owners were included as major detailed activities. In the wildlife sector, along with the management of illegal trade, the establishment of a proactive risk management system utilizing habitat, migration routes, and trade information was proposed, and the establishment of a wildlife-based infectious disease surveillance system through information linkage among the KDCA, APQA, and MCEE was presented as a major implementation direction. Finally, in the integrated surveillance system sector, the establishment of an integrated surveillance system linking surveillance information from the human, animal, and environmental sectors was presented as a core implementation strategy. To this end, the standardization of disease codes and data collection standards, and the establishment of an information-sharing system between sectors were organized as major detailed activities.

2) Action Track 3. Control and elimination of vector-borne diseases

In Action Track 3, with the goal of controlling and eliminating vector-borne diseases, the establishment of an integrated surveillance system, community-based risk management, a citizen-participatory surveillance system, and the strengthening of multisectoral response capacity were derived as key implementation strategies for domestic application (Table 2, Figure 2). In addition, considering that the domestic occurrence of NTDs, which were presented alongside vector-borne diseases in the OH JPA, is limited, the Korean National OH JPA structured its implementation direction around responding to vector-borne zoonotic diseases, for which domestic occurrence and management are highly necessary.

Integrated risk management framework for vector-borne disease based on a One Health approach

First, in the field of integrated surveillance systems, the establishment of a data-driven surveillance system that links and integrates surveillance information collected from the human, animal, and environmental sectors was presented as a major implementation strategy. Therefore, establishing a data management system that links patient occurrence information, animal infection information, vector distribution data, and climate and environmental information, preparing an inter-agency data linkage system, selecting core surveillance indicators for joint utilization, and operating local-level pilot projects were proposed as key detailed activities. In the field of community-based risk management, a risk-based management system reflecting regional environmental conditions and ecological characteristics was derived as a core implementation strategy. The implementation strategy was structured to analyze major vector distributions and regional risk factors centering on local governments to operate customized control strategies and prevention projects and to link intensive surveillance focused on high-risk areas with community-based prevention activities. Key detailed activities included vector habitat environment management, intensive surveillance in high-risk areas, and community-participation prevention activities.

In addition, citizen-participation vector monitoring, risk area reporting, and living environment improvement activities, which strengthen the roles of citizens and local communities in the process of responding to vector-borne diseases, were presented as key implementation strategies. When carried out in parallel with institutional management systems, this participation-based approach can contribute to strengthening community-level infectious disease response capabilities and building a sustainable preventive management system. Furthermore, to strengthen vector-borne disease response capabilities, field-oriented practical training and multisectoral joint response drills were included as key implementation strategies, and regular training and joint mock drills for practitioners in the fields of public health, environment, disease control, and veterinary medicine were proposed as key detailed activities.

Conclusion

This study proposed the implementation direction of the Korean National OH JPA to effectively respond to complex health threats, such as zoonotic diseases, focusing on zoonotic disease response. Synthesizing the results of literature and policy analyses, it was confirmed that zoonotic disease response relying solely on a response system centered on a single ministry has limitations in effectively responding to the interconnected health threats among humans, animals, and the environment and that an integrated approach based on multisectoral collaboration and information linkage is required.

Accordingly, based on the Quadripartite’s OH JPA, this study identified key implementation areas centered on Action Tracks 2 and 3, considering the domestic administrative system and implementation conditions. As a result, the establishment of multisectoral governance, an integrated surveillance system linking human, animal, and environmental information, joint risk assessment, a risk-based management system, and community participation were identified as the key implementation directions of the Korean National OH JPA. This is significant as it provides a policy foundation for expanding domestic zoonotic disease response from a system centered on the management of individual infectious diseases to a whole-of-cycle management system encompassing prevention, early detection, risk assessment, and response.

However, as this study was conducted based on an analysis of literature and policy data on the international OH JPA and domestic policies and administrative systems, it has a limitation in that it did not empirically verify the feasibility or effectiveness in the actual policy implementation process. Furthermore, given that the joint plan of action presented in this study is intended to propose implementation directions rather than a finalized policy plan at the national level, there is a need to concretize detailed implementation strategies and the division of roles through consultations among relevant ministries and related agencies in the future.

In the future, policy discussions for the establishment and smooth implementation of the Korean National OH JPA should continue through the operation of the Integrated Infectious Disease Management Council, a One Health-based collaborative body. Additionally, if empirical research evaluating the priorities, feasibility, and policy effects of the implementation directions proposed in this study is conducted, it could contribute to enhancing the effectiveness of the Korean National OH JPA.
