Artificial Intelligence as a Radiology Gap-Filler in Somalia: A Narrative Review with a Pragmatic Implementation Framework
Authors: Rahmo Mohamed Ali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiagnostic imaging supports emergency care, trauma assessment, tuberculosis detection, maternal and neonatal care, cancer diagnosis, and surgical decision-making, yet timely specialist interpretation remains limited in many low-resource health systems. Somalia is rebuilding a health system affected by workforce shortages, fragmented information systems, uneven service distribution, infrastructure constraints, and limited regulatory capacity. This narrative review integrates peer-reviewed evidence, global guidance, and Somalia-specific policy sources to examine where artificial intelligence could support radiology and to translate that evidence into a pragmatic implementation framework. The Somalia-specific contribution is a context-sensitive mapping of priority use cases, implementation barriers, governance safeguards, and clinical workflow requirements. Priority applications include computer-aided chest X-ray screening for tuberculosis, emergency imaging triage, image quality assessment, structured reporting support, and teleradiology prioritization. The proposed framework links readiness assessment, priority use-case selection, local validation, supervised deployment, workflow integration, continuous monitoring, and cautious scale-up. It also assigns human accountability at each stage and distinguishes pre-deployment readiness from clinical and operational risks during use. Artificial intelligence may act as a human-supervised capacity multiplier, but poor local validation, weak data protection, automation bias, workflow failure, and vendor dependency can undermine safety and equity. Somalia should therefore pursue leadership-led, locally validated, and auditable implementation that strengthens existing clinical services rather than introducing isolated technology.Keywords: artificial intelligence, radiology, medical imaging, Somalia, low-resource settings, teleradiology, diagnostic access, health workforce, patient safety, digital health

Introduction

Global Diagnostic Imaging and Artificial Intelligence Context

Diagnostic imaging is a core component of modern healthcare. It guides decisions in emergency medicine, trauma, tuberculosis, maternal and neonatal complications, non-communicable diseases, cancer care, surgical planning, and referral prioritization. Access remains highly unequal. Low- and middle-income countries face constraints in equipment availability, trained personnel, maintenance, interpretation, and quality assurance.1,2 These constraints delay diagnosis, increase repeat imaging and referral costs, and weaken patient safety.

Artificial intelligence can support abnormality detection, urgent-case prioritization, computer-aided detection, structured reporting, image quality review, and workflow optimization.3 However, reliable artificial intelligence begins before an algorithm analyzes an image. Standardized acquisition, trained staff, quality control, consistent equipment, and predefined processing pipelines are essential. The China Phenobank body MRI/CT protocol provides an operational example: investigators standardized acquisition across organs, optimized image quality, used dedicated equipment, and applied defined pipelines to extract imaging-derived phenotypes.4 Although that protocol was developed for a well-resourced research cohort, its practical lesson is relevant to low-resource implementation: artificial intelligence outputs are only as dependable as the acquisition and data processes that produce them.

Somalia-Specific Health System Context

Somalia’s current radiology gaps reflect a longer history of conflict, state collapse, fragmented service delivery, and underinvestment in public health infrastructure. National and international recovery efforts now emphasize primary health care-led recovery, universal health coverage, stronger stewardship, and more resilient health systems.5 The Somalia Health Sector Strategic Plan 2022–2026 similarly prioritizes affordable, equitable, and quality services, while the Essential Package of Health Services defines a national framework for essential care.6,7 Diagnostic imaging remains less visible in health planning than medicines, primary care, maternal health, or infectious disease programs, despite its role across these services.

A clinical scenario illustrates the implementation problem. A person with chest trauma may reach a rural district hospital after a road traffic injury. A radiographer obtains a digital chest X-ray, but no radiologist is immediately available. A locally validated tool could flag a suspected pneumothorax and move the image to the top of a teleradiology worklist. The responsible clinician would assess the patient, provide urgent stabilization when indicated, communicate with a radiologist, and arrange referral. In this scenario, value comes from a complete workflow, not from an algorithm alone. If image quality is poor, connectivity fails, an alert has no responsible recipient, or referral transport is unavailable, the technology cannot deliver safe care.

Problem Statement and Research Gap

Global ethics, regulation, reporting, and evaluation frameworks provide important principles for artificial intelligence in health, but they do not by themselves specify how a fragile and mixed public-private health system should choose initial radiology use cases, define minimum digital readiness, assign accountability, connect alerts to referral pathways, and monitor equity. Somalia-specific evidence on radiologist numbers, imaging equipment, picture archiving and communication systems, reporting delays, and artificial intelligence performance is limited. The policy gap is therefore not simply whether artificial intelligence can detect abnormalities, but how Somalia can translate global evidence into a safe, feasible, and accountable implementation pathway.

Contribution of This Paper

This narrative review makes four linked contributions. First, it separates global artificial intelligence capabilities from Somalia-specific health system constraints. Second, it identifies priority use cases that match high clinical need and relatively clear pathways. Third, it translates evidence and governance principles into a phased implementation framework with a step-by-step clinical workflow. Fourth, it distinguishes readiness and infrastructure requirements before deployment from clinical and operational risks during use. The resulting framework is intended to guide policy discussion, institutional pilots, implementation research, and procurement decisions in Somalia and comparable low-resource settings.

Narrative Review and Analytical Approach

This narrative review used a focused and iterative search of peer-reviewed literature, global health guidance, artificial intelligence governance documents, reporting standards, and Somalia-specific health policy sources. Searches were conducted in PubMed, Google Scholar, World Health Organization repositories, Somalia Federal Ministry of Health websites, and journal publisher databases, and were updated on June 1, 2026. Search concepts included artificial intelligence, radiology, medical imaging, low-resource settings, low- and middle-income countries, teleradiology, Somalia health workforce, digital health, health information systems, computer-aided detection, tuberculosis chest X-ray screening, implementation, governance, and patient safety.

Priority was given to World Health Organization guidance, Somalia policy documents, peer-reviewed radiology and digital health literature, and established artificial intelligence reporting and evaluation standards. Sources were selected for their relevance to four domains: clinical use cases, health system readiness, governance and safety, and implementation evaluation. The search was not designed as a systematic review, and no formal risk-of-bias assessment or meta-analysis was performed.

The analytical integration followed four steps. First, global evidence was reviewed to identify plausible radiology functions and known failure modes. Second, Somalia policy and health system evidence was used to map infrastructure, workforce, information-system, referral, and governance constraints. Third, the global evidence was translated into Somalia-specific readiness, workflow, accountability, and equity requirements. Fourth, these requirements were organized into a phased conceptual implementation framework, a practical workflow, a pre-deployment readiness checklist, and a clinical and operational risk matrix.

Radiology Gaps as a Health System Problem in Somalia

Radiology gaps in Somalia should be understood within wider health system recovery, workforce shortages, fragile infrastructure, and uneven service distribution. Somalia’s health information system has made progress, including DHIS2-based reporting, but a World Health Organization assessment described fragmentation, limited private-sector reporting, and continuing challenges in reliable and timely health information.8 These issues directly affect artificial intelligence-assisted radiology because safe use depends on digital image availability, standard identifiers, interoperable data flows, audit systems, connectivity, privacy safeguards, and performance monitoring.

Workforce constraints are also central. An analysis of health professions education and the workforce environment in Somalia found a concentration of training institutions in urban areas, particularly Benadir, and emphasized urban-rural disparities and low health professional density.9 Somalia-specific radiologist workforce data remain limited, but the wider pattern is relevant to imaging services, where specialist interpretation may be scarce or concentrated in major cities.

The radiology gap likely includes overlapping barriers: limited specialist availability, urban concentration of services, variable acquisition quality, limited PACS/RIS integration, equipment maintenance constraints, incomplete teleradiology networks, weak critical-result communication, and underdeveloped quality assurance. These barriers can produce delayed reporting, repeated imaging, unnecessary referrals, missed urgent findings, and avoidable patient expenses. Radiology should therefore be treated as a core health system function rather than an isolated technical specialty.

Why Artificial Intelligence is Relevant but Must Be Carefully Framed

Artificial intelligence is relevant where it can strengthen a defined clinical pathway and support an accountable health worker. In low-resource settings, trustworthiness is affected by limited data diversity, opaque models, inadequate infrastructure, and limited participation of resource-poor institutions in model development and validation.3 The World Health Organization global strategy on digital health likewise emphasizes that digital initiatives require coordinated financial, organizational, human, and technological resources.10 The practical objective in Somalia should be supervised capacity multiplication, with clinicians retaining responsibility for interpretation, escalation, and patient management.

Initial use cases should be high-volume, high-need, protocol-driven, and linked to a clear response pathway. Chest X-ray computer-aided detection for tuberculosis is a strong example. The World Health Organization has recommended computer-aided detection for tuberculosis screening and issued an updated policy statement on its use to help bridge case-detection gaps.11,12 Other plausible uses include emergency image prioritization, critical-result flagging, image quality assessment, structured reporting support, and teleradiology worklist prioritization. Priority Artificial Intelligence Use Cases for Somalia

Chest X-Ray Artificial Intelligence for Tuberculosis Screening

Tuberculosis is an appropriate early use case because chest X-ray screening is common, the pathway is relatively defined, and international policy guidance exists.11,12 Somalia could pilot computer-aided detection in high-volume urban facilities, tuberculosis centers, mobile screening programs, and referral hospitals with digital radiography. Evaluation should include sensitivity, specificity, referral completion, microbiological testing, treatment initiation, turnaround time, cost, user acceptability, and equity. The score should support screening and triage, while clinical assessment, confirmatory testing, and national tuberculosis protocols determine care.

Emergency and Trauma Imaging Triage

Emergency departments and trauma services require rapid prioritization. Artificial intelligence could flag suspected pneumothorax, fractures, intracranial hemorrhage, severe chest abnormalities, or other urgent findings so that clinicians and radiologists review them earlier. The principal value is faster escalation. Every alert should identify a responsible recipient, specify the required response time, and remain linked to a documented fallback pathway when the tool or network is unavailable.

Teleradiology Integration

Teleradiology can extend scarce specialist capacity, while artificial intelligence can help sort studies by urgency, identify image quality problems, and organize worklists. Radiologists in Mogadishu or regional centers could support peripheral facilities, provided that the service defines acquisition standards, patient identification, secure transfer, report format, turnaround targets, medico-legal responsibility, and critical-result communication.13 A teleradiology service should remain functional when artificial intelligence is unavailable.

Structured Reporting and Quality Assurance

Artificial intelligence may support structured reporting by promoting consistent terminology and reducing omissions. It may also assist quality assurance by identifying discordance between preliminary and final interpretations, flagging poor image quality, and supporting case review. Generative and multimodal systems require particular caution because fluent text can contain fabricated, omitted, or clinically inconsistent information. World Health Organization guidance on large multimodal models emphasizes governance, transparency, safety, and post-deployment oversight.14 Any generated draft should remain visibly provisional until a qualified clinician verifies and signs it.

Training and Capacity Building

Artificial intelligence tools may support education through annotated cases, structured feedback, image-quality assessment, and teaching datasets. These functions can strengthen hospital continuing professional development, postgraduate education, and radiographer competency programs. Training should include limitations, false positives, false negatives, automation bias, data protection, escalation procedures, and the ability to work safely without the tool.

Phase 1: Readiness Assessment

Facilities should assess imaging equipment, digital radiography, PACS/RIS capacity, patient identifiers, internet reliability, local processing options, electricity and backup power, staffing, reporting turnaround time, referral pathways, incident reporting, and data protection. A facility without minimum digital and clinical readiness should first invest in basic systems rather than deploy artificial intelligence prematurely.

Phase 2: Priority Use-Case Selection

Leaders should select a use case with high clinical value, a measurable problem, a clear response pathway, and available human oversight. Tuberculosis chest X-ray screening, emergency X-ray prioritization, and teleradiology worklist support are stronger starting points than autonomous interpretation of complex CT or MRI. Selection should consider disease burden, volume, urgency, feasibility, cost, interoperability, and equity.

Phase 3: Local Validation

Models trained elsewhere may perform differently in Somalia because disease patterns, equipment, acquisition quality, patient characteristics, and workflows differ. Local validation should compare outputs with qualified interpretation and, where feasible, clinical outcomes. Performance should be examined by facility, modality, age group, sex, image quality, and disease category. Data preparation requires curation, labeling, quality control, and bias assessment.15 Validation thresholds should be agreed before clinical use, and results should guide workflow design rather than serve only as technical documentation.

Phase 4: Supervised Deployment

Artificial intelligence outputs should enter the workflow as decision support. The interface should show the output clearly, avoid overstating certainty, and allow clinicians to disagree. Staff should receive training on failure modes, escalation, documentation, and downtime procedures. Implementation research shows that adoption depends on technical, organizational, clinical, legal, and cultural factors.16,17 The facility should begin with a limited pilot, named clinical owners, and a defined review period.

Phase 5: Monitoring, Audit, and Scale-Up

After deployment, hospitals should monitor diagnostic performance, turnaround time, referral completion, missed findings, false alerts, override patterns, downtime, user experience, equity, costs, and patient safety incidents. Performance may drift after software updates, equipment changes, or shifts in the patient population. Scale-up should occur only after evidence of safety, acceptability, operational feasibility, and sustainable financing.

Operational Workflow Integration in Practice

The following workflow places human accountability and patient safety at each stage:

1. Clinical request and patient identification. A clinician records the indication, relevant history, urgency, and a unique patient identifier.

2. Standardized image acquisition and quality check. A trained radiographer follows the local protocol and confirms positioning, exposure, completeness, and image quality before analysis.

3. Secure artificial intelligence processing. The system receives the image through an approved local or cloud pathway, applies the validated model, and records the software version and time of analysis.

4. Output display and prioritization. The interface presents the flag, score, or quality warning with clear limitations. It does not present the output as a final diagnosis.

5. Human interpretation and clinical decision. A radiologist or appropriately trained clinician reviews the image, the artificial intelligence output, and the clinical context, then accepts, modifies, or rejects the suggestion.

6. Critical-result communication and referral. The responsible clinician receives urgent findings through a documented escalation pathway and initiates stabilization, confirmatory testing, specialist consultation, or transfer as needed.

7. Final documentation and accountability. The report records the responsible human interpreter, the clinical action, and whether artificial intelligence assisted the process. Final responsibility remains with the designated clinician and institution.

8. Feedback, incident review, and audit. Disagreements, missed findings, false alerts, downtime, and referral failures enter a quality-improvement process that can change thresholds, training, procurement, or use-case scope.

The workflow must include a downtime plan. Imaging and clinical care should continue through conventional interpretation, telephone consultation, or teleradiology fallback whenever the algorithm, internet connection, or power supply fails.

Limitations and Real-World Failures of Artificial Intelligence in Radiology

Real-world failure often occurs at the interaction between model performance and the surrounding system. A model may perform well in its development dataset but lose sensitivity or specificity when equipment, acquisition protocols, disease prevalence, or patient characteristics change. Poor image quality can produce unreliable outputs, while incomplete local validation can create false confidence. Data curation and external validation are therefore safety requirements, not optional research activities.3,15,17

Clinical use introduces additional failures. False negatives may delay treatment, false positives may increase unnecessary referrals and costs, and automation bias may lead a clinician to discount clinical evidence. Alert fatigue can cause teams to ignore important flags. An artificial intelligence result may also arrive too late, reach the wrong person, or fail to trigger transport and referral. These are workflow failures even when the algorithm technically functions.

Generative reporting systems can produce plausible but incorrect statements, omit a critical abnormality, or create inconsistency between findings and impression. Software updates can alter performance without obvious warning, and vendor-controlled systems may limit independent auditing. Privacy breaches, weak contracts, vendor lock-in, and unclear liability can further undermine trust. Facilities should therefore maintain human verification, incident reporting, version control, performance monitoring, transparent procurement, and an exit plan.14,16–19

Governance, Ethics, and Patient Safety

Artificial intelligence-assisted radiology should be governed as a clinical safety intervention. World Health Organization guidance on ethics and governance identifies risks and principles for public benefit, while regulatory considerations emphasize safety, effectiveness, transparency, stakeholder dialogue, and lifecycle monitoring.18,19 Somalia can translate these principles into institutional and national safeguards.

Minimum safeguards include approval by an institutional or national health technology committee, documented human oversight, local performance validation, secure data storage and transfer, clear vendor agreements, named accountability for final decisions, incident reporting, and periodic audit. Procurement should require access to performance documentation, software-version history, cybersecurity expectations, interoperability terms, maintenance arrangements, costs, and exit clauses. Patients and communities should receive understandable information about how their data and images are used, consistent with applicable law and institutional policy.

The greatest policy risk is technology-first adoption driven by vendor pressure, competition, or unrealistic expectations. A health-system-first approach begins with the clinical problem, strengthens acquisition and referral systems, validates the selected tool, and measures whether the complete pathway improves care. Equity should be monitored because deployment limited to urban private hospitals could widen access gaps, while carefully governed use in public referral hospitals, tuberculosis programs, mobile services, and teleradiology networks could extend diagnostic support.

Reporting Standards and Evaluation Research

Future studies in Somalia should use established reporting and evaluation frameworks. The Checklist for Artificial Intelligence in Medical Imaging provides best-practice guidance for medical imaging studies.20 DECIDE-AI guides early-stage clinical evaluation of artificial intelligence decision-support systems.21 CONSORT-AI and SPIRIT-AI address clinical trial reports and protocols, and STARD-AI supports diagnostic accuracy studies.22–24 Use of these standards can improve transparency, reproducibility, and comparison across sites.

Priority research questions include: How accurate are selected tools on Somali imaging datasets? How does performance vary by facility, equipment, image quality, age, and sex? Does artificial intelligence reduce reporting or referral delays? Does it improve tuberculosis screening yield or emergency prioritization? What are the effects on workload, patient costs, and equity? How do clinicians, radiographers, patients, and managers perceive the system? Which financing and procurement models are sustainable? How should responsibility be assigned when artificial intelligence contributes to a missed or delayed diagnosis?

Policy and Leadership Implications

Artificial intelligence-assisted radiology should be incorporated into digital health, workforce, referral, and universal health coverage planning rather than purchased as isolated software. National leadership should define minimum standards for procurement, validation, interoperability, data protection, clinical oversight, incident reporting, and audit. Hospital leaders should require a clinical owner, a measurable service problem, staff training, a fallback workflow, and sustainability planning before procurement.

A national or institutional roadmap could begin with three actions. First, map imaging assets, workforce, digital capacity, reporting delays, referral pathways, and quality-assurance practices across public and private facilities. Second, pilot one or two high-priority uses, particularly tuberculosis computer-aided detection or emergency X-ray prioritization, in facilities with digital imaging and accountable clinical oversight. Third, create a learning network that shares validation results, safety events, implementation lessons, and procurement experience.

Leadership should also invest in the foundations that artificial intelligence cannot replace: radiologist and radiographer training, equipment maintenance, PACS/RIS development, reliable electricity, secure connectivity, patient identification, referral transport, and quality improvement. These investments make both conventional and artificial intelligence-assisted radiology safer.

Limitations of This Narrative Review

This paper is a narrative review and does not include a systematic search strategy, formal risk-of-bias assessment, or meta-analysis. Somalia-specific data on radiologist numbers, equipment distribution, acquisition quality, reporting turnaround time, referral completion, and artificial intelligence performance remain limited. Some recommendations therefore draw on broader low-resource radiology, digital health, and governance evidence. The proposed framework is conceptual and should be tested through stakeholder consultation, facility readiness assessments, prospective validation, implementation studies, economic evaluation, and patient-safety monitoring before large-scale adoption.

Conclusion

Artificial intelligence could help address selected radiology gaps in Somalia by supporting tuberculosis chest X-ray screening, emergency prioritization, image quality assessment, structured reporting, quality assurance, and teleradiology. The main contribution of this narrative review is a Somalia-specific translation of global evidence into a phased implementation framework and an accountable clinical workflow. Success will depend on standardized acquisition, infrastructure, local validation, workforce training, referral capacity, data protection, transparent procurement, human responsibility, and continuous monitoring. A cautious, leadership-led approach can use artificial intelligence to strengthen diagnostic services and equity while reducing the risks of error, workflow failure, and unregulated adoption.
