SynthPix: A lightspeed PIV images generator
Antonio Terpin ETH Zürich aterpin@ethz.ch, Alan Bonomi ETH Zürich abonomi@ethz.ch, Francesco Banelli ETH Zürich fbanelli@ethz.ch, Raffaello D’Andrea ETH Zürich rdandrea@ethz.ch

Abstract
We describe SynthPix, a synthetic image generator for Particle Image Velocimetry (PIV) with a focus on performance and parallelism on accelerators, implemented in JAX. SynthPix supports the same configuration parameters as existing tools but achieves a throughput several orders of magnitude higher in image-pair generation per second. SynthPix was developed to enable the training of data-hungry reinforcement learning methods for flow estimation [ 3 ] and for reducing the iteration times during the development of fast flow estimation methods [ 4 ] used in recent active fluids control studies [ 33 ] with real-time PIV feedback. We believe SynthPix to be useful for the fluid dynamics community, and in this paper we describe the main ideas behind this software package. \url https://github.com/antonioterpin/synthpix pip install synthpix

Motivation and significance

\gls

*piv is one of the most important modalities for quantifying flow fields in bluff body wakes
[
37
]
. In
\gls
*piv, laser pulses illuminate neutrally buoyant particles in the fluid at specific intervals, while the camera captures images used to estimate velocity fields; see the plot on the right.
Despite being a problem studied since the early 20th century, when foundational work on wake development around bluff bodies was conducted by Rayleigh and von Kármán
[
26
,
34
]
, flow-field quantification remains so rich that new processing algorithms continue to emerge.
Initially, PIV analysis relied on linear, cross-correlation-based approaches that track particles displacements between two consecutive snapshots
[
38
]
. Over the years, this basic scheme has been substantially refined
[
28
,
36
,
35
,
1
,
29
]
, including learning-based components
[
9
,
17
]
.
Later, nonlinear optical-flow methods such as Horn–Schunck
[
12
]
and Lucas–Kanade
[
2
]
were adapted to
\gls
*piv images
[
8
,
40
]
.

More recently, the community has resumed the exploration of deep-learning approaches
[
9
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
21
,
15
,
25
,
18
,
41
,
27
,
13
]
.
These methods are data-hungry and their effectiveness is limited by the availability of large amounts of high-quality data. Currently, these approaches either rely on general, domain-agnostic data or on (very) limited real experimental data, which often prove insufficient for training robust, generalizable models. Synthetic datasets
[
16
,
31
,
24
,
22
]
bring several benefits:

•

Accurate ground-truth reference:
synthetic images are generated from known velocity fields, yielding quantitative benchmarks for algorithms assessment.

•

Scalable data volume:
virtually unlimited samples can be rendered, supporting deep-learning workflows and statistically rigorous testing.

•

Controlled variability:
flow regimes and imaging settings can be tuned systematically, enabling sensitivity analyses and generalization studies.

However, existing
\gls
*piv generators are not compatible with modern deep-learning frameworks and are substantially slower than the training time itself; see
Figure

1
.
For these reasons, existing solutions represented a blocker for our research on using reinforcement learning to simplify algorithms tuning
[
3
]
and speeding up flow quantification for fluids control studies
[
33
]
.

Contributions

We present SynthPix, an easy-to-install, easy-to-use, fast, and reliable GPU-enabled package for synthetic
\gls
*piv image generation.
SynthPix delivers throughput orders of magnitude higher in
\gls
*piv image-pair production than existing tools, while offering the same level of configuration flexibility.
Because it’s written in Python using JAX, it integrates seamlessly with modern deep-learning frameworks.
To avoid “reinventing the wheel” issues–where every new PIV-deep-learning study builds its own generator–we adopted software engineering best practices and propose SynthPix as the de facto standard for synthetic
\gls
*piv data generation. For an overview of the pipeline and results, see
Figure

1
.

GPU enabled

The user can specify in the configuration file which device to use (e.g., “cpu”, “cuda:0”, or multiple ones).

Configuration file

The configuration file specifies all options SynthPix needs to generate synthetic
\gls
*piv images. SynthPix allows to specify
the image shape (e.g.,
1024\times 1024
), the batch size (e.g.,
256
), how many different flow fields to use for each batch (e.g.,
64
, so that
256/64
image pairs are generated from the same flow field), and for how many consecutive batches should a flow field be used after loading it (e.g.,
1
).
SynthPix also provides several configurable generation and noise parameters, such as the rasterization-related ones, and the seeding density (in particles per pixels).

Data-loaders and pre-fetching

We load the flow fields from the specified files in the configuration file to the device for processing.
SynthPix offers a unified interface to different flow field representations, such as the
.hdf5
from the John Hopkins Turbulence Database
[
20
,
23
]
, the
.flo
from the dataset used in
[
6
]
, the
.mat
of
[
14
]
and
.npy
with array format
(x,y,u(x,y),v(x,y))
.
It also allows loading pre-existing
\gls
*piv images alongside the flow field and, thus, datasets like the one used in
[
6
]
can be integrated fully.
SynthPix is compatible with flow fields specified via a Python function. Thus, one can integrate, e.g., randomly generated fluid flows
[
30
,
21
]
.
More datasets
[
10
,
19
]
can be integrated with either a tailored data-loader or an appropriate converter.
To amortize the disk-to-GPU transfer time, we implement a simple pre-fetching strategy for the flow fields, for which we keep a queue of loaded flow fields and a thread routinely adding new ones as the queue is emptied.

Mathematical model for the contribution of the single particle

We model the contribution of the single particle to the pixel intensity
I(x,y)
in the image as

I_{0}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2(1-\rho^{2})}\left(\frac{(x-x_{0})^{2}}{\sigma_{x}^{2}}-2\rho\frac{(x-x_{0})(y-y_{0})}{\sigma_{x}\sigma_{y}}+\frac{(y-y_{0})^{2}}{\sigma_{y}^{2}}\right)\right),

(1)

where
I_{0}
is the peak intensity, and the parameters
\sigma_{x},\sigma_{y},\rho
determine the shape of the contribution. This model is the classical one used for rendering sythetic
\gls
*piv images, as presented in
[
37
]
, with the difference that by setting
\rho>0
it also allows axis-misaligned particles. All the parameters are sampled uniformly at random from a configurable interval for each particle in the first image. For the particles in the second image, we sample the variation of each parameter for each particle from a zero-mean gaussian with specified variance.

Remark 2.1
.

A different approach for calculating
I_{0}
, which we defer to future releases, is to consider the particles in the three-dimensional space, apply a three-dimensional flow, and then compute

I_{0}(z)=q\exp\left(-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\left|\frac{2Z^{2}}{\Delta Z_{0}^{2}}\right|^{s}\right),

where the particles scattering efficiency
q
, the thickness of the laser sheet
\Delta Z_{0}
, and the shape factor
s
are configurable parameters; see
[
37
]
.

Remark 2.2
.

Including the projection model for the case of non-orthogonal configurations
[
16
,
32
]
is inconsequential in terms of generation speed and we defer it to a future release, together with the option to simulate the limited fill ratio of a
\gls
*ccd
[
16
, Equations 5 and 6]
.

Finally, Synthpix-generated images are optionally adjusted according to a user-specified intensity histogram.

Sampling the particles

For the first set of images, we first sample a number of particles that is equivalent to the maximum numbers of particles per pixel
ppp
from the configured range, and then we sample the positions of
H\times W\times\texttt{ppp}
particles in the image view. We then sample the actual
ppp
uniformly at random from the configured range, and mask the particles in excess by setting their intensity to zero
For the second image, we move the particles according to the mapping
(x,y)\mapsto(x+u(x,y),y+v(x,y))
, where
(x,y)
are the particles coordinates and
(u(\cdot,\cdot),v(\cdot,\cdot))
are the flow field coordinates; see
Figure

1
.

Considerations on the aggregation of the particles contributions

We sum the contribution of all the particles to obtain the resulting image. This superposition is commonly adopted in the literature, and relies on the assumption that the number of particles per pixel
ppp
is low but also on the absence of interference phenomena
[
16
]
. We believe a future extension of SynthPix should introduce summation in the spectral domain to model these effects as well. In the current version, to mitigate this, we allow the user to select a probability of hiding a particle from each image. That is, particles in the first image may not appear in the second, and vice-versa. To exploit parallelism on accelerators with JAX, we keep the number of particles constant by setting the intensity of the hidden particles to zero.

Efficient rasterization

To efficiently sum the contributions of the particles, we first generate in parallel for each particle a batch of Gaussian kernels with patch sizes of
3\max(\{d_{i}\})+1
, where
d_{i}
are the diameters of the particles. These patches are generated according to the selected mathematical model for the single particles. Then, we sum these kernels to obtain the final image.

3.1.2
It is fast, but is it accurate?

To the best of our knowledge, there is not an established way to measure the quality of synthetic PIV images other than visual inspection. We propose to quantify it with respect to accepted datasets by verifying that SynthPix can generate images on which an estimator performs similarly.

Experimental setup

We estimate the flow
on both the original images from the PIV dataset
[
6
]
and on SynthPix-generated images. Then, we compare the resulting
\gls
*epe by computing

\mathcal{L}(u_{1},u_{2})\coloneqq\frac{\absolutevalue{\mathrm{EPE}(u_1, \hat{u}) - \mathrm{EPE}(u_2, \hat{u})}}{\mathcal{L}_{1}(u_{1},\hat{u})},\quad\mathrm{EPE}(u,v)\coloneqq\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}|}\sum_{I\in\mathcal{I}}\frac{1}{|I|}\sqrt{\sum_{(x,y)\in I}\norm{u(x, y) - v(x, y)}_{2}^{2}}.

Intuitively,
\mathcal{L}(u_{1},u_{2})
captures how an evaluation of an estimator on Synthpix predicts the performances on the established benchmark in
[
6
]

[
6
]
have different generation parameters, we manually tune most of the images to visually look alike.

In this section, we assess the performance impact of the generation parameters.

Experimental setup

We perform ablations on the number of GPUs, image size, seeding density, maximum particles diameters, minimum seeding density in the batch with fixed maximum, batch size, flow fields per batch, batches generated with the same flow field.
For each ablation study, we report the throughput in image pairs per second. The parameters that are not under study are fixed. In particular, we use a single GPU and consider an image size of
512\times 512
, a seeding density of
0.06
, particles diameters sampled uniformly in
[0.8,1.2]
, a batch size of
64
, and
1
flow field per batch, kept for all the batches. The statistics are computed over
1000
batches.

Results

We collect the results in
Figure

5
and make the following observations:

•

The throughput scales linearly with increasing number of GPU used, as one would expect from a software exploiting parallelism on accelerators.

•

The throughput decreases with increasing image size, but remains high even for very large images, enabling the generation of high-resolution datasets.

•

The throughput mildly decreases with increasing seeding density, approximately in a linear relationship. The throughput is not affected, instead, by the spread between the minimum and the maximum seeding density.

•

The throughput decreases with increasing maximum particles diameters, but the trend resembles a staircase: the maximum particles diameter affect in discrete steps the Gaussian kernel patch size.

•

The throughput increases substantially with the batch size initially, and then only mildly. This is not unexpected, since the increase in batch size amortize the initial overheads of the accelerators, but these benefits are substantial only when moving from a single batch to a few multiple ones.

•

The throughput is not affected by the number of flow fields used during generation, consistently with the parallelization over the flow fields.

•

The throughput increases only slightly with the number of batches with the same flow field. Future releases will load the data directly to device.

4
Impact

SynthPix empowers researchers with a high-throughput pipeline to generate realistic and high-resolution
\gls
*piv images compatible with modern deep-learning frameworks. SynthPix is written in JAX, enabling efficient large-scale training on hardware accelerators. With SynthPix, researchers can now push the limits of the use of synthetic data in the development of flow quantification methods and deploy more data-hungry algorithms.
SynthPix has already supported multiple research works in adaptive PIV tuning
[
3
]
,
large-scale flow quantification
[
4
]
, assessment of hard-constrained neural networks
[
11
]
, and real-time feedback control
[
33
]
.
Beyond these applications, SynthPix standardizes the generation of synthetic PIV datasets, removing the need for each study to “reinvent the wheel”.
Its design and interoperability deep learning frameworks promote reproducibility and adoption across the machine-learning and fluid-dynamics communities,
laying the foundation for new advances in data-driven flow quantification.
