Prediction models of severe radiation-induced oral mucositis: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Shanshan Zhang, Hongkun Liu, Jingna Wei, Peng Liu, Jin wang, Shuzhe Gao, Yingzi Si, Xuebing Jing
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRadiation-induced oral mucositis (RIOM) is a common complication in cancer survivors following radiotherapy, and numerous studies have established predictive models to evaluate the risk of severe RIOM. However, there are significant differences in the methodological quality, predictive performance, and clinical application value of these models.ObjectivesThis systematic review evaluated risk prediction models for severe RIOM in cancer survivors, analyzed the limitations of the current research, and proposed recommendations for optimization, thereby providing a reference for clinical nursing staff in selecting appropriate assessment tools.MethodsThe search period spanned from the database's inception to February 2025 and included CNKI, VIP, Wanfang, Chinese Biomedical Literature Database, CINAHL, PubMed, Cochrane Library, and Embase. A meta-analysis was conducted to evaluate the incidence of severe RIOM and its predictive factors. The systematic evaluation was registered in the Platform for International Prospective Systematic Evaluation Registry (PROSPERO) database under registration CRD420250655070.ResultsA total of ten studies were included, involving 2,881 cancer survivors, encompassing 14 distinct models. Seven studies conducted internal validation, while only two studies performed external validation. Fourteen models reported the area under the Area Under Curve (AUC), which ranged from 0.657 to 0.942. The quality of the literature was assessed using the Prediction model Risk Of Bias Assessment Tool (PROBAST), revealing that nine out of the ten studies evaluated were at a high risk of bias. The meta-analysis revealed a 36% incidence of severe RIOM (95%CI = 25%-48%). Age ≥ 60 years, diabetes, smoking, and a history of periodontal disease were identified as independent risk factors for severe RIOM (P < 0.05).ConclusionsThe relevant predictive models exhibited excellent performance. However, most existing models lack external validation, limiting their extrapolation and clinical applicability. Moving forward, medical researchers should focus on developing models with exceptional predictive accuracy while minimizing the risk of bias, following standardized development guidelines.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-025-07369-1.Keywords: Cancer survivors, Severe oral mucositis, Radiotherapy, Predictive model, Systematic review

Background

In recent years, cancer has emerged as a leading cause of mortality globally, significantly affecting patient life expectancy and quality of life [1]. According to data from the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) [2, 3], the global incidence of cancer rose from 14.1 million cases in 2012 to 20 million cases in 2022, with approximately 9.7 million cancer-related deaths recorded during this period. The global burden of cancer is anticipated to continue its upward trajectory over the next three decades. In China, there were 4.8247 million new cancer survivors and 2.5741 million cancer deaths in 2022, and the incidence and mortality rates of cancer are higher than the global average [3, 4]. The "Healthy China Initiative (2019–2030)," "Outline of the Healthy China 2030 Plan," and "Medium-and Long-Term Plan for the Prevention and Control of Chronic Diseases in China (2017–2025)" all regard increasing the survival rate of cancer survivors as an important indicator for measuring the overall health level of the population [4].

Radiotherapy is a primary treatment modality for patients with cancer. However, although radiation effectively damages DNA and induces apoptosis in tumor cells, it also inflicts collateral damage to healthy tissues, leading to adverse effects in patients. Although the survival rate of cancer survivors increases after receiving radiotherapy, so does the number of complications, such as RIOM, oropharyngeal candidiasis, radiation osteonecrosis, trismus, radiation caries, periodontitis, mucosal pain, and gustatory dysfunction [5]. Among these, RIOM is the most common adverse reaction and mainly manifests as local symptoms such as oral erosion, swelling, ulcers, pain, and dysphagia [6]. Relevant studies have shown [7] that for patients receiving conventional-dose radiotherapy, the incidence of RIOM is 20%–40%, and approximately 20%–50% of patients may progress to severe RIOM. The European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO) has also reported that the incidence of severe RIOM in patients receiving radiotherapy for head and neck cancer was nearly 100% [8]. Owing to the different antitumor treatment methods, the onset time and manifestations of oral mucositis vary. RIOM usually appears 3–10 days after treatment, reaches its peak at 7–14 days, and, if there is no concurrent infection, usually recovers spontaneously within 2–3 weeks [9]. In addition to being related to the cancer site, irradiated area, radiation dose, and radiotherapy technique, the incidence of RIOM is also affected by concurrent treatment. If not administered in a timely manner, it leads to systemic effects such as fatigue, weight loss, cachexia, and depression in patients, ultimately resulting in the interruption or suspension of radiotherapy, thus affecting its therapeutic effect [9].

Early screening, diagnosis, and treatment are key to preventing severe RIOM. Previous studies [10–12] have shown that advanced age, high body mass index (BMI), smoking, diabetes, leukopenia, combined chemotherapy, a history of periodontal disease, and radiation dose are risk factors for severe RIOM. Early screening and identification of cancer survivors who have not yet developed severe RIOM and the precise management of modifiable risk factors are crucial steps in reducing the incidence of severe RIOM. Yang et al. [13] conducted a study of 311 patients with nasopharyngeal cancer receiving radiotherapy. They constructed a risk prediction model using logistic regression and presented it in the form of a nomogram. The results showed that the predicted risk in the model was highly consistent with the actual incidence. For 2024, Fei et al. constructed a prediction model using logistic regression for 502 patients with head and neck cancer [12]. The model indicated good calibration. This study also increased the clinical benefits of the model by identifying more true positives among positive patients, thereby achieving the maximum net benefit of avoiding unnecessary consumption of medical resources and excessive intervention in false-positive patients. Currently, prediction models that integrate multiple variables to assess individual risks have assisted medical staff in screening high-risk groups for severe RIOM. However, there are significant differences in the methodological quality, predictive efficiency, and clinical application of these models, and external validation is generally lacking [11–13]. This situation has caused numerous difficulties for clinical medical staff in selecting appropriate prediction models to accurately identify high-risk groups for severe RIOM.

In our study, a systematic evidence-based review of risk prediction models for severe RIOM was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses of studies on health Measurement Instruments (PRISMA-COSMIN) [14]. The focus was a comprehensive comparison and analysis of these models in terms of their construction methods, predictive factors, model performance, and bias risk assessment. This study aimed to provide clinicians with accurate and reliable evidence to facilitate the selection of appropriate predictive models of severe RIOM in clinical practice.

Methods

This study was conducted according to the Priority Reporting Entries for Systematic Evaluation and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) [15]. The systematic evaluation was registered in the Platform for International PROSPERO database under registration CRD420250655070.

Data sources and search strategy

The search was conducted across eight databases: CNKI, VIP, Wanfang Data, Chinese Biomedical Literature Database, CINAHL, PubMed, Cochrane Library, and Embase. A combination of subject headings and free-text keywords was employed in this process. The search timeframe extended from the inception of each database to January 2025. The keywords utilized in the search included Medical Subject Headings (MESH) as well as the following terms or their synonyms: severe oral mucositis, oral mucositis, stomatitis, oral inflammation, radiotherapy, radiation therapy, prediction model, prediction, model, nomogram, scoring scale, scoring system, calculator, machine algorithm, and algorithm. The scope of the search was confined to titles and abstracts.

All the original studies were meticulously screened to identify those pertinent to this topic. The specific search strategy is detailed in the supplementary materials and adheres to PRISMA guidelines. Based on the strategy of constructing review questions defined by the Cochrane Collaboration Prognosis Methods Group, that is, PICOTS, the evidence-based question was constructed [16]. This strategy clarified the purpose of the evaluation and formulated a search strategy for relevant studies, as well as the inclusion and exclusion criteria. The following describes the key contents of this systematic review.

P (Population): Patients aged ≥ 18 years who have been diagnosed with cancer and are undergoing radiotherapy.

I (Intervention model): All developed models that can be used to predict the occurrence of severe RIOM.

C (Comparator): None.

O (Outcome): The outcome indicator is defined as severe RIOM (the diagnostic criteria meet ≥ Grade Ⅲ in the Radiation Therapy Oncology Group (RTOG) Acute Radiation Injury Grading Criteria or ≥ Grade 3 in the World Health Organization (WHO) Oral Mucositis Grading Criteria) [17].

T (Timing): From the start of radiotherapy until the diagnosis of severe RIOM.

S (Setting): A risk prediction model was used for individualized prediction of severe RIOM and timely implementation of intervention measures for cancer survivors.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion Criteria were as follows: (a)Target Population: cancer survivors aged 18 years or older who were undergoing radiotherapy; (b) Research Content: Development and/or validation of predictive models and/or factors for severe RIOM in cancer survivors, including at least one performance metric (such as area under the curve, sensitivity, and specificity); (c) Research Design: Prospective cohort study, retrospective cohort study, case–control study or case-cohort study, cross-sectional design, or randomized trial; and (d)The study must be published in English or Chinese.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (a) Studies that solely analyzed the risk factors associated with severe RIOM without developing or validating a predictive model; (b) Inability to obtain complete datasets; (c) Publications in the form of titles, abstracts, letters, conference announcements, or draft interventions; and (d) Failure to acquire the original article despite attempts to contact the author.

Study selection

Two researchers independently screened the literature based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Duplicate entries were eliminated. Irrelevant studies were excluded by reviewing the titles and abstracts. Finally, the full texts were examined to ensure that the studies adhered to the established inclusion and exclusion criteria. In instances of disagreement, the two researchers engaged in discussions to reach a consensus. If disputes persisted, a third party was consulted for arbitration.

Data extraction

The data extraction form was developed based on a key assessment and data extraction checklist for systematic reviews [14]. It includes the following: first author, publication year, research region, research type, research design, research subjects, case collection time, oral mucositis assessment tool, sample size, outcome indicator, modeling method, variable selection, candidate variables, processing method for continuous variables, processing method for missing data, performance and presentation form of the model, predictive factors included in the model, and applicability and limitations of the model.

Assessment of risk of bias

Two researchers used the PROBAST tool to assess the bias and applicability of the studies [14]. PROBAST covers four domains: study population, predictors, outcomes, and statistical analyses. The evaluation results for each domain include "low", "high", or "unclear". The bias risk assessment consists of 20 landmark questions across the four domains, and the evaluation results for these landmark questions include "yes/probably yes," "no/probably no," or "no information." The adaptive evaluation only assessed the first three areas. Any objections during the qualitative evaluation were discussed with a third researcher and finalized.

Data synthesis

The included studies were analyzed descriptively in tabular form, and the key features of the predictive models were analyzed. The predictive performance of the prediction model was measured by AUC and calibration (calibration plot/Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test). Meta-analysis of the incidence and predictors of severe RIOM in cancer survivors in the included studies was performed using Stata 18.0 software, with effect statistics for incidence expressed as rate (95% CI) and predictors expressed as RR values; differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05. The I2 test was used to determine heterogeneity among the included studies. If I2 < 50% and P > 0.1, the heterogeneity among studies was not significant, and a fixed-effects model was used for incorporation. If I2 ≥ 50% or P ≤ 0.1, the heterogeneity among studies was significant, in which case sensitivity analyses would be performed. After excluding the literature article by article, if heterogeneity still existed, a random-effects model was used for merging. If the combined effect value did not change significantly, the results of the meta-analysis were considered stable.

Results

Search results

Figure 1 presents a flowchart of the literature selection process. According to the search strategy, 2,179 articles were initially searched, of which 1,026 duplicates were excluded by the software and manually. A further 975 articles that were not related to the topic were excluded by reading the title and abstract. Finally, after reading the full text of the remaining 178 articles, 168 studies that did not meet the exclusion criteria were excluded, resulting in 10 studies to be included [12, 18–26]; 14 prediction models were identified.

Construction of prediction models

Ten studies reported 14 risk-prediction models for severe RIOM. The sample size for model building in these studies ranged from 49 to 802 cases, and the number of candidate predictive variables ranged from 3 to 20. In terms of modeling methods, all ten studies [12, 18–26] included a logistic regression model. One study [25] used logistic regression, support vector classification, and the random forest algorithm for modeling, and another study [26] used logistic regression, the Gaussian Naive Bayes algorithm (GNB), and Extreme Gradient Boosting (XG Boost) algorithms for modeling. Regarding variable selection, six studies [12, 18–22, 26] were based on logistic regression. In terms of the treatment of continuous variables, none of the ten studies [12, 18–26] processed the variables while maintaining continuity of the continuous variables. For the handling of missing data, eight studies [12, 18–24] did not clearly report whether there was missing data. The construction of these models is presented in detail in Table 2.

Performance of the prediction models

Model differentiation was evaluated using AUC. Seven studies [12, 18–23] reported the AUC at modeling, which ranged from 0.657 to 0.942; three studies [24–26] reported the internal validation AUC of the model, which ranged from 0.720 to 0.810; two studies [12, 23] reported the model with external validation AUCs of 0.805 to 0.902, and the AUCs of the models were > 0.7, except for one study [22] at the time of modeling. The calibration of the models was assessed using the H–L goodness-of-fit test (P > 0.05), calibration curves, and calibration plots. Six studies [12, 18–23] reported the calibration in the form of calibration curves or calibration plots, with three studies [12, 18, 19] reporting H–L goodness-of-fit tests and three studies [21–23] reporting calibration curves. In terms of model validation, seven studies [12, 18, 21, 23–26] performed internal validation and two studies [18, 23] performed external validation. In terms of model presentation, two studies [12, 21] presented the results in the form of column line graphs, two studies [18, 19] presented the results through regression equations, three studies [24–26] presented the results in the form of clustered heat maps, one study [20] presented them in the form of tree plots, and one study [23] presented them in the form of scatter plots, as detailed in Table 3.

Risk of bias and transparent reporting

The risk of bias and applicability of the included studies were evaluated using PROBAST. The results showed that the studies presented a high risk of bias and good applicability. Based on the PROBAST evaluation criteria, among the ten included studies [12, 18–26], eight studies [18–22, 24–26] had a high overall risk of bias, which was mainly reflected in the domains of the study population, the domains of the outcomes, and the domains of the analysis. Regarding the field of research subjects, five studies [12, 18–20, 26] were judged to be at high risk of bias because their data information was derived from retrospective studies, and the reliability and validity of the datasets could be improved. Regarding the domain of outcome, four studies [12, 18, 20, 24] were rated as "unclear" because two studies [12, 20] did not state or mention the reasonableness of the predictor assessment and the time interval for determining the outcome, and two studies [18, 24] did not state whether there was an association between the predictor and the outcome of the study. Two studies [18, 24] did not state whether there was an association between the predictor and the study outcome, so the question "Is the information about the predictor clear when determining the outcome" was answered as "Unclear." In terms of the analytic domain, seven studies [12, 18–21, 23, 24] were rated as high-risk, none of which mentioned and described the handling of missing data, and two studies [22, 26] were rated as unclear; three studies [19, 20, 22] did not report on internal or external validation and lacked research on model performance; six studies [19–21, 23, 25, 26] did not mention the specificity and sensitivity of the predictive model. To address high bias risks, we recommend adopting a prospective cohort study design with predefined statistical strategies for handling missing data. Second, strict adherence to the TRIPOD reporting standards is essential to ensure transparency. Most importantly, external validation must be conducted using independent representative cohorts. We encourage the use of large-scale, multicenter electronic health record (EHR) databases for verification while exploring machine learning techniques to automatically generate synthetic validation cohorts that differ from the original data distribution, thereby efficiently assessing model robustness.

In terms of applicability, the ten included studies, with the exception of the R. Hansen study [23], were all rated as low-risk, showing good overall applicability, as shown in Table 4.

Results of meta-analysis

In this meta-analysis of the incidence of severe RIOM in cancer survivors, heterogeneity was observed among the ten studies [12, 18–26] (I2 = 97.3%, P < 0.001). The main reason for the high heterogeneity in this study is the differences between the included studies. First, the research subjects varied significantly: five studies focused on nasopharyngeal cancer, while the other five targeted head and neck cancer. Second, there are inconsistencies in the assessment tools for OM. Eight studies adopted WHO criteria, while two used RTOG standards, with potential discrepancies in the definition of "severe" cases, which contributed to the overall heterogeneity. The incidence was analyzed with sensitivity, and the results did not change significantly after the exclusion of any of the studies; therefore, a random-effects model was used. The meta-analysis showed that the incidence of severe RIOM in cancer survivors was 36% (95%CI = 25%−48%) (Fig. 2). When synthesizing predictor effect sizes, meta-analysis pooling was performed for the ten included studies [12, 18–26], focusing on the following frequently recurring predictors: age ≥ 60 years, diabetes mellitus, smoking, and prior periodontal disease history (Table 5). However, three studies [23–25] lacked sufficient data for pooling. Sensitivity analysis confirmed stable results with no significant changes in the pooled effect estimates. The findings identified age ≥ 60 years, diabetes mellitus, smoking, and prior periodontal disease as independent risk factors for severe RIOM in patients with cancer (P < 0.05) (Figs. 3, 4, 5, 6).

Discussion

After a comprehensive review of the risk prediction models for severe RIOM in cancer survivors, ten studies [12, 18–26] encompassing 14 distinct models were ultimately included. These models exhibit good overall applicability, and most studies employ logistic regression for their modeling approaches. However, these studies lacked external validation and presented a high risk of bias. Furthermore, none of the models included across the studies shared identical sets of predictors, and significant variations were observed in their evaluation metrics.

The performance of the predictive models included in this study is intricately linked to the choice of predictors, the modeling approach employed, and the overall study design. The differentiation among these models was assessed using the AUC metric; a value closer to 1 indicates superior classification performance. Seven studies [12, 18–23] all reported AUCs at modeling, with AUCs ranging from 0.657 to 0.942; three studies [24–26] reported internal validation AUCs of the models, with AUCs ranging from 0.720 to 0.810; and two studies [12, 23] reported external validation AUCs, with AUCs of 0.805 to 0.902. Except for one study [22] at the time of modeling, the AUCs of the models were > 0.7, which showed good discrimination, and also indicated that these models were able to accurately predict the occurrence of severe RIOM in cancer survivors.

Model calibration is a crucial method for enhancing probability accuracy; it strengthens credibility and optimizes decision-making processes. Therefore, to ensure the clinical applicability of models, it is essential to provide comprehensive performance test reports that enable accurate reproduction of model effectiveness across diverse populations. In this study, the calibration of the models was evaluated using the (H–L) goodness-of-fit test (P > 0.05), calibration curves, and calibration plots. Six studies [12, 18, 19, 21–23] reported calibration methods presented as calibration curves, H–L goodness-of-fit tests, and calibration plots. Specifically, three studies [12, 18, 19] reported H–L goodness-of-fit test results, whereas three studies [21–23] presented calibration curves, which are core components for ensuring that prediction models maintain consistent performance in new datasets or target populations. It is typically divided into internal validation, which evaluates the model performance, and external validation, which verifies the cross-population applicability using independent external data. The combination of both approaches can comprehensively validate the reliability of the models and their clinical translation values.

Regarding model validation, seven studies [12, 18, 21, 23–26] conducted internal validation using bootstrap resampling, random split validation, and tenfold cross-validation (10-FCV). Two studies [18, 23] performed external validation, with both [18, 23] also conducting internal validation. For model presentation formats, two studies [12, 21] used nomograms, two studies [18, 19] presented results via regression equations, three studies [24–26] utilized clustering heatmaps, one study [20] employed a dendrogram, and one study [23] used scatter plots. Six studies [12, 18–22, 26] were from China, and the publication time was concentrated in the last 5 years, indicating that China has been widely concerned with the risk prediction model of severe RIOM in the clinic. However, the studies were generally retrospective, with a low percentage of prospective studies. Moreover, the reliability and validity of the dataset need to be further improved.

Currently, the prediction model about the risk of severe RIOM generally lacks external validation, the studies are mostly single-center studies, and the internal and external validation populations are from the same location as the population for model development, which may have a large bias, and the effect of the model portability cannot be guaranteed, which limits the predictive ability of the model to a certain extent. To ensure the quality of external validation datasets for clinical risk prediction models, prospective study designs should be employed to enhance data reliability and model generalizability. Additionally, multicenter study designs should be adopted to increase population diversity, reduce single-center bias, and improve external validity and generalization ability. Second, given the considerable variability in predictors included in existing models, future research should leverage data mining techniques to conduct an in-depth exploration of high-risk factors for severe RIOM, thereby improving predictive accuracy. Existing risk prediction models for severe RIOM demonstrate significant homogeneity in modeling methods, with most models relying on traditional logistic regression. For example, Dean et al. [25] compared logistic regression, random forest, and support vector machine algorithms for predicting radiation-induced oral mucositis, and found that support vector machines achieved higher prediction accuracy than logistic regression and random forest. Similarly, Dong et al. [26] constructed predictive models using logistic regression, GNB, and XGBoost, with the GNB model performing optimally. These findings suggest that future research should consider incorporating advanced machine learning and deep learning algorithms, such as support vector machines, random forests, neural networks, and decision trees [27–29] to improve models’ predictive performance.

According to the risk of bias and applicability evaluation of the included literature according to PROBAST, the studies showed overall high risk of bias and good applicability, and among the ten included studies [12, 18–26], eight studies [18–22, 24–26] showed overall high risk of bias, which was mainly reflected in the study subject, outcome, and analysis domains. In the participant domain, five studies [12, 18–20, 26] were judged high risk because their data were sourced from retrospective research with need for improved reliability and validity. Regarding the outcome domain, four studies [12, 18, 20, 24] were rated "unclear" because two studies [12, 20] did not explain the time interval between predictor assessment and outcome determination, and two studies [18, 24] failed to clarify the association between predictors and outcomes. In the analysis domain, seven studies [12, 18–21, 23, 24] were rated high risk as none mentioned handling of missing data, two studies [22, 26] were rated unclear, three studies [19, 20, 22] did not report internal and external validation, and six studies [19–21, 23, 25, 26] did not mention specificity and sensitivity. For applicability, all except R. Hansen [23] were rated low risk, indicating good overall applicability in Table 4. Future research should adhere to PROBAST guidelines [14] by ensuring adequate sample size, standardizing oral mucositis diagnostic criteria, implementing blinding, employing imputation methods for missing data, avoiding univariate analysis, reporting discrimination and calibration, considering overfitting and underfitting, and conducting external validation to construct high-quality predictive models that benefit clinical practitioners.

A complete heterogeneity in predictive factors was observed, with no two models sharing identical sets of variables across the included studies. The most commonly shared predictive factors were age ≥ 60 years old, diabetes mellitus, smoking, and a history of periodontal disease. For the remaining indicators, there were significant differences among various studies, or the data could not be combined, which led to the failure to conduct a meta-analysis. The results of the meta-analysis showed that age ≥ 60 years, smoking, diabetes mellitus, and a history of periodontal disease were independent risk factors for the development of severe RIOM. Age ≥ 60 years was associated with severe RIOM in cancer survivors [18]. Whether age is a risk factor for the development of RIOM is currently controversial. Studies have shown [18, 19] that younger patients have a faster rate of cell proliferation, more vigorous mitosis, and are more susceptible to chemical interference, which increases the incidence of RIOM. Moreover, declining renal function and degenerative lesions in periodontal tissues contribute to the susceptibility of older patients to RIOM. Smoking increases the temperature of the oral cavity, and the phenolics in tobacco stimulate the oral mucosa, disrupting its blood circulation and activating inflammatory cells and inflammatory mediators in the body [30]. Relevant studies have shown [31] that the risk of RIOM in smokers is 2.147 times higher than in non-smokers, especially during radiotherapy, and further targeted strategies and interventions are needed to reduce tobacco use in patients. The risk of RIOM in radiotherapy patients with a history of periodontal disease is 3.326 times higher than that in patients without a previous history of periodontal disease [32]. Periodontal disease is a common oral disease, and long-term recurrent inflammation of periodontal tissues not only affects oral function but also deteriorates the defense function of the oral mucosa [12]. Patients with a history of periodontal disease should be actively treated for periodontal disease, oral problems should be repaired, and the occurrence and development of RIOM should be closely monitored.

Management of RIOM is a key factor in improving patient quality of life and reducing unplanned readmissions [33, 34]. Before radiotherapy, it is important to eliminate all possible sources of mucosal damage by repairing sharp tooth surfaces, removing orthodontic appliances, reducing dentures, and abstaining from smoking and alcohol [35, 36]. Future studies should adopt a multicenter prospective cohort design and construct multivariate prediction models for severe RIOM by integrating three dimensions: patient-specific factors (BMI, nutritional status, oral condition, genetic susceptibility), disease-related characteristics (tumor stage, primary tumor location, lymph node metastasis), and treatment-related parameters (radiotherapy parameters, chemoradiotherapy dose fractionation). Furthermore, emphasis should be placed on establishing external validation cohorts to dynamically verify the predictive efficacy of the model using long-term follow-up data. In terms of predictors, priority should be given to oral-specific assessment indicators [37, 38] (e.g., OMAS score and salivary flow rate measurement) and radiodose parameters [39, 40] (e.g., mean parotid gland dose and oral mucosa V50). Machine learning algorithms such as random forest and gradient boosting trees should be utilized to mine dose–response relationships [41–43]. In clinical practice, healthcare providers should establish evidence-based biopsychosocial multidimensional assessment systems, adopt multidisciplinary collaboration models, dynamically monitor high-risk factors for oral mucositis, and strengthen patient education and shared decision-making to improve treatment compliance. Moving forward, medical institutions must enhance continuing education systems, promote the clinical translation of precision radiotherapy technology and artificial intelligence models, optimize early warning and intervention workflows, and provide full-cycle healthcare to achieve a strategic shift from disease treatment to health maintenance [23, 26].

Limitations

This study had certain limitations. First, most of the included studies were conducted in a single medical center. The limitations of the source of the samples may have affected the generalizability of the results. Second, the study only included findings published in Chinese and English, which may have made the research results unable to represent the overall global population. Meanwhile, this study excluded relatively incomplete research, such as conference abstracts; thus, there is still a possibility that key information might be overlooked. Third, among the included studies, key indicators such as specificity and sensitivity were not fully reported, and most studies lacked external validation, resulting in poor generalizability of the relevant models. Finally, during the process of integrating predictors, because of the inability to combine data from various studies, a meta-analysis could only be conducted for four predictors, making it difficult to comprehensively cover all potential influencing factors.

Conclusions

Many researchers have recognized the importance of RIOM. This study provides clinical healthcare workers with relevant information regarding RIOM prediction models. Although these models have demonstrated good predictive ability, there are still certain limitations in terms of the statistical methods. Due to the lack of a standardized construction process and external validation, the accuracy and integrity of these prediction models in clinical practice are not satisfactory, which may interfere with the accurate identification of RIOM in cancer survivors by clinical healthcare workers. In practical work, healthcare workers can pay special attention to patients aged ≥ 60 years or above, those who smoke, those with diabetes mellitus, and those with a history of periodontal disease. It is recommended that future research should strictly follow the guidelines of PROBAST and TRIPOD and conduct prospective studies with a multicenter approach and large sample sizes. In addition to the rapid development of artificial intelligence in the medical field, it is advisable to consider using artificial intelligence technology for big data mining, combining artificial intelligence with algorithms to construct prediction models with good performance and strong stability. Through multicenter external validation and continuous optimization in practical applications, the extrapolation ability of the models can be further enhanced to provide high-quality support for clinical decision-making.
