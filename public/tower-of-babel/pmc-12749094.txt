Experimental and numerical research on the anisotropic compressive mechanical behavior of layered rock
Authors: Qingliang Liu, Caishuang Zhang, Jianqiang Hou, Lielie Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo study the anisotropic mechanical characteristics of layered rock, typical phyllite specimens with bedding plane angles of 0°, 30°, 45°, and 90° were prepared, and triaxial loading and unloading tests under different confining pressures were conducted. Based on the experimental results, the anisotropic mechanical properties and fracture mechanisms of phyllite under different stress paths were analyzed. The results shown that under varying confining pressures, the elastic modulus and compressive strength of phyllite initially decreased and subsequently increased with the increase of bedding plane angle; With the increase in confining pressure, the compressive strength enhanced under both triaxial loading and unloading conditions; The failure modes of phyllite with different bedding plane angles exhibited significant anisotropy; While the residual friction angle exceeded the peak value in triaxial loading tests and showed the opposite trend in unloading tests, the residual cohesive strength remained consistently lower than the peak under different stress paths. Finally, a numerical model for simulating triaxial loading of phyllite was developed using PFC2D. The numerical simulation results shown a high degree of consistency with the experimental findings, and the post-peak crack counts with different bedding plane angles were analyzed. The occurrence of the layered and matrix composite failure mode was closely associated with the strength ratio between the rock matrix and the bedding plane.Keywords: Phyllite, Anisotropic mechanical behavior, Triaxial loading and unloading test, Numerical simulationSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

Natural rock masses, subjected to prolonged complex geological and climatic processes, gradually evolve into discontinuous media characterized by structural planes with varying orientations and spacing. As a result, their mechanical behavior demonstrates pronounced anisotropy under external loading conditions. In geotechnical engineering, layered rock masses represent a highly prevalent type of geological formation.

The mechanical properties of layered rocks exhibit significant anisotropy with different bedding plane angles, and will have a significant impact on the deformation, stress distribution and long-term stability of engineering rock masses such as water conservancy projects, tunnels, and slopes. Even they may pose a threat to the safety and stability of engineering structures. To investigate the stress redistribution, unloading deformation, and long-term stability of layered rock masses following excavation, researchers have conducted extensive and in-depth studies on the mechanical properties of such rock masses.

Rock masses with layered structures often exhibit different failure modes depending on the inclination angle of the bedding planes, which is related to the failure mechanism of the rock mass. Researchers1–4 have conducted uniaxial compression creep tests on typical layered rock types, including green schist, slate, shale, and jointed sandstone, and systematically analyzed the failure modes of specimens under horizontally, vertically, and inclined bedding conditions. Lin5 utilized FLAC3D software to perform numerical simulations on the failure modes of layered rock slopes and applied the strength reduction technique to investigate how the bedding plane angle affects these failure modes. Teng6 conducted experiments integrated with 3DEC numerical simulations to investigate the failure modes of water-saturated shale under varying bedding plane angles, based on the internal microstructural characteristics of the rock mass. The results indicated that, under different water content levels and bedding angle conditions, the failure modes of the specimens can be categorized into four types. Li7 carried out uniaxial compression tests on layered rock mass specimens with horizontal and vertical bedding plane angles. Under horizontal orientation, vertical tensile cracks did not propagate in a self-similar manner but instead deflected along the direction of maximum shear stress, leading to a stepped-like shear-tension composite failure mode. Zuo8 classified the failure modes of layered rock masses into two categories: interlayer failure and rock material failure. Interlayer failure can be further subdivided into weak plane splitting, sliding along the weak plane, and bending-induced failure along the weak plane, whereas rock material failure includes tensile failure and shear failure of the rock matrix. Ghazvinian9 employed PFC2D to perform numerical simulations of the failure mechanisms in transversely isotropic rock masses under varying bedding angles, and categorized the failure patterns into three types: tensile failure along discontinuities, sliding failure along discontinuities, and fracture failure across bedding layers. Basu10 conducted uniaxial compression tests, Brazilian disc tests, and point load tests on schist and sandstone. Their results indicated that sandstone failure evolved with increasing stress levels from central splitting to sliding along bedding planes, and subsequently to fracturing along one or multiple fracture surfaces. In contrast, schist exhibited foliation-plane-dominated failure at low stress levels, whereas different failure modes were observed under higher stress conditions. Roche11 investigated the fracture modes of limestone containing mudstone layers from a mesoscopic perspective. The findings revealed that the failure mode of the rock mass is influenced by the ratio of the elastic modulus between the matrix and the weak plane. A high ratio tends to result in tensile failure, whereas a low ratio leads to shear failure or composite failure. Kundu12 carried out Brazilian disc tests to investigate the failure modes of mylonitic quartz-mica schist and Toothed quartz-mica schist under indirect tensile loading. The experimental results demonstrated that both types of schist specimens exhibited a variety of failure modes. Wang13 conducted numerical simulations to compare the failure mods using linear and fractal stress–strain behavior of layered rock mass models during excavation, with a focus on investigating the influence of joint roughness. Additionally, the study explored the relationship between excavation radius and the corresponding peak strength of the rock mass. Gao14 conducted uniaxial compression tests on layered shale specimens to reveal the energy evolution and failure mechanism of layered rocks. The anisotropic characteristics of layered rocks were studied, and the energy indicators such as elastic energy, dissipated energy and total input energy of the specimens were further explored. The energy damage evolution mechanism of layered rocks was also revealed.

The deformation characteristics of rock masses play a critical role in geotechnical engineering. Pinto15 and Amadei16 performed uniaxial compression tests on layered rock, and systematically investigated the anisotropy of deformation parameters, including the elastic modulus and Poisson’s ratio. Huang7 carried out uniaxial compression tests on representative schist. Based on an analysis of internal deformation mechanisms, and how the angle between the bedding plane and the major principal stress direction affecting the elastic modulus was investigated. Pomeroy17 and Nasseria18 performed triaxial compression tests on various types of layered rock, and the influence of stress levels on the anisotropy of deformation in layered rock masses was systematically examined. Maranini19 carried out triaxial compression creep experiments on limestone to examine the creep mechanisms of rocks from a microstructural perspective, elucidating the relationship between micro-crack evolution and the progressive deformation and failure behavior under creep conditions. Singh20 investigated the triaxial compression creep deformation behavior of different rock masses under varying stress paths.

Researchers have carried out systematic studies on the strength anisotropy of layered rock masses. The single weak plane strength theory proposed by Jaeger21 is a classical anisotropic strength criterion. This criterion posits that layered rock masses are transversely isotropic materials containing a set of structural planes aligned along a dominant orientation, and it identifies two distinct modes of material failure. Duveau22 modified the linear Mohr–Coulomb criterion to a nonlinear one and improved the Jeager’ strength criterion. Ramamurthy23 analyzed and summarized, based on extensive experimental data, three types of curve relationships between the tensile strength of anisotropic rock masses and the bedding planes angle: U-shaped, shoulder-shaped, and fluctuating. Pei24 investigated the mechanical behavior of layered marble through triaxial loading and unloading tests. The results shown that when the bedding planes were vertically oriented, the peak pre-failure cohesive force obtained from unloading tests was lower than that from loading tests, whereas the residual internal frictional angle was higher. Under identical unloading conditions, the cohesive force of specimens with bedding planes parallel to the loading direction was greater than that of specimens with perpendicular bedding planes, while the residual internal frictional angle exhibited the opposite trend. Shin25 performed uniaxial compression creep tests on six layered rock types, to investigate variations in creep parameters—such as elastic modulus—and the time-dependent strength of these rock masses under varying conditions. Liu26 performed Brazilian splitting tests as well as uniaxial and triaxial compression tests on slate to investigate its mechanical behavior. The results revealed that the rock’s compressive strength initially decreased and subsequently increased with increasing bedding plane angle. As the confining pressure rose, the peak compressive strength progressively increased, while the curvature of the strength variation curve with respect to bedding plane inclination diminished gradually. This trend indicated that the anisotropic characteristics of the layered rock became less pronounced under higher confining pressures. Huang27 investigated the strength anisotropy of layered rock masses, the Mohr–Coulomb and Hoek–Brown criteria were incorporated into the analysis. Building upon single weak plane theory and the maximum axial strain theory, two transversely isotropic strength criteria were formulated, along with a corresponding method for parameter determination. Furthermore, the sensitivity of the strength parameters and confining pressure in characterizing anisotropic strength within the non-sliding failure criterion was also examined.

In terms of other mechanical properties, Xia28 proposed a microscale damage criterion specifically applicable to layered rock. By conducting three-point bending experiments on shale beams with varying bedding plane angles and comparing the results with phase field simulations, the reliability and advantages of the theoretical model were systematically validated, the splitting mechanisms and load-bearing capacity of layered rock masses was further studied. For layered rock, Fu29 conducted experimental and numerical research on the influence of mechanical parameters and layer thickness on the mechanic behavior subjected to continuous tension, including the strength and the failure mode. Wang30 considered the tensile and compressive strengths, proposed a calculation method for the critical sliding displacement of layered rock masses, and established corresponding assessment criteria and numerical methods for slope bending instability. A systematic study was conducted on the entire process of bending instability of layered rock slopes, from deformation to failure. Xu31 performed Brazilian splitting tests on layered rock specimens with varying bedding plane angles. Surface crack initiation and propagation were identified through digital image recognition and quantitative characterization techniques. The Brazilian splitting behavior of layered rocks under different bedding plane angle conditions was numerically simulated using the discrete element method-based software PFC3D. The effects of cohesion strength, tensile strength, and joint roughness coefficient (JRC) of smooth joints on the tensile strength were systematically investigated.

Considerable research has been conducted on the mechanical properties of layered rocks, yielding meaningful insights into their anisotropic behavior. However, existing studies still exhibit notable limitations. The anisotropic characteristics of layered rock masses are not only influenced by factors including bedding plane angle, but confining pressure, stress path and other factors. Therefore, a multi-angle analytical approach is necessary to fully understand this anisotropy. So far, few studies have systematically examined layered rock anisotropy from multiple perspectives, particularly regarding the conditions that lead to composite failure involving both bedding plane and matrix. Therefore, this study conducted triaxial loading and unloading tests on phyllite, a typical type of layered rock, to investigate its anisotropic mechanical properties. Additionally, numerical simulations were performed to further support and complement the experimental findings.

Experimental program

Phyllite mass manufacturing

The phyllite specimens used in the experiments were collected from the portal section of Tao-Ping tunnel in China, exhibiting well-developed foliation structures. All phyllite were processed into cylindrical specimens measuring 50 mm in diameter and 100 mm in height, and were prepared using dry drilling techniques. 1–2 specimens were prepared for each combination of bedding angle and confining pressure. All specimens were kept in a naturally air-dried state before testing, and had their end faces polished for surface smoothness. Core extraction was conducted at angles of 0°, 30°, 45°, and 90° relative to the bedding planes as shown in Fig. 1, in which, denotes the bedding plane angle. Test setup

The uniaxial and triaxial loading and unloading tests of phyllite were conducted using the MTS815 rock mechanics testing system, as shown in Fig. 3(a). Two independent servo systems were employed to control axial and confining pressures, respectively, ensuring rapid and precise response. As shown in Fig. 3(b), vertical extensometers are symmetrically installed along the axial direction of the specimen with a gauge length of 50 mm to measure axial strain, and circumferential strain gauges are arranged in a circumferential direction at the middle of the specimen to monitor radial strain. The load cell is installed between the loading head and the specimen. The sampling frequency of all sensors is 10 Hz.

Loading test setup.

Test loading protocol

Uniaxial and triaxial loading tests

Uniaxial and triaxial compression loading tests were conducted on rock specimens with bedding plane angles of 0°, 30°, 45°, and 90°, respectively. During uniaxial compression, an axial load was applied at a displacement rate of 0.02 mm/s until failure occurred in the rock specimen. When the triaxial compression test was conducting, ① stress control was employed to apply confining pressure at a rate of 0.1 MPa/s until target values (5 MPa, 10 MPa, 20 MPa, 30 MPa, and 40 MPa) were reached; ② with the confining pressure maintained constant, an axial load was applied at a displacement rate of 0.02 mm/s until failure occurred in the rock specimen; ③ following rock failure, the confining pressure remained constant while the axial load was continuously applied at the same displacement rate (0.02 mm/s) until the axial stress stabilized and no longer decreased with increasing strain.

Triaxial unloading test

During triaxial unloading test, ① Confining pressure was applied at a rate of 0.1 MPa/s before the target confining pressures (30 MPa, 40 MPa, and 60 MPa) were attained; ② Maintaining constant confining pressure, the axial load was increased at a rate of 0.5 MPa/s until reaching a predefined stress state prior to rock failure (60% of the peak load); ③ simultaneously, the axial load was applied at a rate of 0.15 MPa/s while the confining pressure was reduced at a rate of 0.05 MPa/s; ④ when the rock specimen was failure, the confining pressure at the point of failure was maintained, and the axial load was continued at an axial strain rate of 0.02 mm/s until complete failure of the rock specimen was achieved.

Experimental results and discussion

Anisotropic analysis of triaxial loading test results

To facilitate the analysis of the influence of confining pressure on the mechanical behavior of phyllite under triaxial loading, uniaxial loading is treated as triaxial loading with a confining pressure of 0 MPa. All results are analyzed and discussed collectively.

Failure mode

The failure modes of phyllite specimens demonstrated pronounced anisotropic characteristics in relation to varying bedding plane angles, as shown in Fig. 4. Overall, with the increase in confining pressure, the brittle failure behavior of the phyllite specimen progressively diminished, whereas the tendency toward ductile softening became increasingly pronounced.

Failure mode of phyllite under triaxial compression.

Phyllite specimens with of 0°, 30°, and 45° all developed macroscopic shear surface subjected to varying confining pressures (see Fig. 4(a), (b) and (c)). As can be seen from Fig. 4(d), for phyllite specimen with a = 90°, splitting failure was observed under low or medium confining pressures, whereas conjugate shear failure occurred under high confining pressures. Furthermore, the inclination angle of the fracture surface increased progressively with rising confining pressure.

For = 0°, the phyllite experienced shear failure across the bedding plane. Initially, the bedding plane undergone compressive closure. Under low and medium confining pressures, a distinct compaction stage was observed. As axial stress increased, shear fractures in different layered blocks progressively coalesced into a macroscopic shear surface. During failure, the shear stress component along the bedding plane was insufficient to induce slip failure. However, due to the heterogeneous distribution of mineral particles, localized stress concentrations could trigger failure in weaker grain regions. At this orientation, the rock sample exhibited a saw tooth-shaped shear failure mode, with its overall strength governed by the intrinsic strength of the rock matrix.

For a bedding plane angle of 30°, the phyllite shown a composite failure mode characterized by simultaneous shear deformation along both the weak plane and the matrix. Initially, the bedding plane undergone compressive closure. Under low and medium confining pressures, a distinct compaction stage was also observed, during which the weak plane particles and the matrix skeleton shared the applied load in a proportional manner, leading to the initiation of micro-cracks under shear stress. As axial stress increased, the shear stress rose, promoting crack coalescence and the eventual formation of a macroscopic shear surface. This represented a typical failure mechanism in layered rock masses, distinguished by a highly irregular and rough failure surface.

When is 45°, the phyllite specimen exhibited sliding shear failure along the weak plane. Initially, a distinct compaction stage was observed under low confining pressure. As axial stress increased, the shear stress rose, leading to crack initiation at the weak plane, which then propagated rapidly along the bedding plane and eventually penetrated the entire interface, resulting in shear slip failure along the bedding surface. This constituted an other typical failure mode in layered rock masses. The strength of the phyllite was governed by the weak plane, and the resulting fracture surface appeared relatively smooth.

For 90° bedding plane angle, the rock owned tensile failure along the bedding planes. Under lateral stress, bedding layers generated tensile cracks, which gradually developed and coalesced into macroscopic joint planes. This failure mechanism resembled the instability of pressure bars. Individual pressure bars were aligned parallel to the joint planes, and due to the lateral constraint provided by the confining pressure, the strength was fully mobilized, resulting in the higher peak strength of the rock specimen. As the confining pressure increased, the fracture mode translated gradually from splitting failure to conjugate shear failure, with localized flaking or sheet-like features observed.

Microscopic analysis

Deformation and failure of phyllite specimen under external loading result from progressive internal damage leading to macroscopic failure. Internal cracks evolve through four stages: closure, elastic deformation, stable propagation, and unstable propagation. Based on SEM observations of fracture surfaces, the evolution of deformation and micro-damage in phyllite specimens with different bedding plane angles is analyzed under uniaxial compression.

As shown in Fig. 5(a), when is 0°, the phyllite specimen fails by shearing across the bedding plane angle, forming a distinct stepped “terrace structure” perpendicular to the bedding plane. The failure along the bedding plane direction is irregular, with a serrated and rough fracture surface. During compression, interlayer fractures close first, transferring load to the rigid particle framework. Stress concentration at micro-fractures and voids initiates early shear cracking. At this stage, tensile stress along the bedding plane is too low to trigger overall sliding on the bedding plane. As axial stress increases, matrix shear cracks grow steadily, causing rapid damage accumulation and progressive weakening. Further loading drives micro-cracks into unstable propagation, linking across blocks and forming a through-going macroscopic fracture. SEM images clearly show shear failure in the matrix. Weak plane particles have lower bonding strength than the matrix. Although no bulk shear sliding occurs under parallel tensile stress, local stress concentrations—due.

SEM image of the specimen’s fracture surface.

to uneven mineral distribution—cause tensile failure of weak particles. Meanwhile, non-uniform micro-defects prevent smooth shear rupture, resulting in an irregular, serrated failure surface. Thus, at this orientation, phyllite strength is governed by the matrix, not the bedding planes.

As shown in Fig. 5(b), when is 30°, the phyllite specimen fails through combined shearing of the weak plane and matrix, which is a typical failure mode in layered rock masses, resulting in an irregular and rough fracture surface. Compared to specimen with = 0°, the “terrace structure” of the matrix is less distinct and barely recognizable. The fracture path along the bedding plane is highly irregular, quartz grains lack ordered arrangement, and the overall fracture surface appears fragmented. During initial compression, the bedding planes close first, after which the weak plane particles and matrix skeleton share the load. Shear stress induces micro-cracks in the weak particles, leading to local stress concentration and partial matrix failure. As axial stress increases, shear stress in both the bedding plane and matrix rises rapidly. Due to differing deformability between matrix and bedding plane materials, tensile stress at their interface gradually accumulates with increasing strain. Rising shear and tensile stresses drive the extension of existing cracks. Meanwhile, due to low bonding strength of weak plane particles, new cracks form under combined tensile and shear loading, accelerating phyllite specimen damage. As the weak plane progressively fails, cracks coalesce and eventually form a through-going macroscopic shear surface.

As shown in Fig. 5(c), the fracture surface of the 45° bedding plane specimen is smoother than those at 0° and 30°. The failure mode is shear sliding along the bedding plane, which is a typical failure mode in layered rock masses, producing a macroscopic, relatively smooth shear plane. During compression, interlayer fractures close first. As axial stress increases, the shear stress along the bedding plane strike rises rapidly. Due to the bedding plane’s loose structure and high heterogeneity, particles with low shear strength fail first, initiating shear cracks along the bedding plane. With continued loading, new cracks form and existing ones grow and link up, eventually forming a through-going shear crack, leading to shear sliding failure along the bedding plane.

As shown in Fig. 5(d), the fracture surface of the specimen with = 90°differs from those at other bedding plane angles. It is relatively flat along the axial stress direction but shows a distinct layered structure perpendicular to it. The failure mode is tensile splitting along the bedding plane. Under load, the matrix undergoes compressive deformation, generating tensile stress between layer perpendicular to the bedding plane. Once this stress reaches the tensile strength of the fractures, cracks begin to propagate. When transverse tensile stress exceeds the micro-crack initiation threshold, new tensile cracks form in the interlayer fillings, marking the onset of stable crack growth. Damage accumulates and the phyllite specimen progressively weakens. As stress increases, tensile cracks extend and coalesce into a macroscopic joint plane parallel to the bedding plane, leading to final failure due to loss of load-bearing capacity.

Strength-deformation characteristic

Figure 6 illustrates the variation in elastic modulus of rock specimens with different bedding angles under triaxial loading of various confining pressures.

Elastic modulus of phyllite specimen under triaxial loading.

As shown in Fig. 6(a), under identical confining pressure, as the bedding plane angle increased, the elastic modulus exhibited an initial decrease followed by an increase. The maximum value was observed at = 90°, while the minimum occurred at = 30°. The result indicated that phyllite exhibited a significantly pronounced anisotropy behavior in elastic modulus, which was primarily attributed to the preferential orientation of mineral particles within the weak bedding planes and the presence of micro-cracks. During the formation of phyllite, the weak interlayer planes exhibited relatively low compaction. For the bedding plane angle of 0°, the axial stress was oriented perpendicular to the bedding planes, the progressive compaction of particles within the weak planes induced to the reduced elastic modulus. When is 90°, the axial stress aligned parallel to the bedding plane, the load was predominantly supported by the relatively rigid rock matrix, resulting in a higher elastic modulus. The variation in elastic modulus was intrinsically linked to the failure mode (see Fig. 4). When = 90°, the failure mode of the phyllite aligned along the axial direction resembled compressive buckling of a strut, characterized by minimal deformation, which resulted in a higher elastic modulus. In contrast, when is 0°, shear failure developed along the cutting plane due to the presence of weak bedding planes, leading to significant deformation and consequently a lower elastic modulus. At bedding plane angles of 30° or 45°, the axial stress intersected the bedding plane at an oblique angle. The resulting deformation was governed by the ratio of normal to tangential stiffness of the weak plane, leading to a relatively low elastic modulus.

As shown in Fig. 6(b), the elastic modulus of phyllite with varying bedding angles progressively increased with rising confining pressure, however, the rate of increase diminished gradually. Notably, for phyllite specimens with bedding plane angles of 0°, 30°, and 45°, the elastic modulus remained nearly constant when the confining pressure exceeded 30 MPa. Nevertheless, the elastic modulus of the 90° phyllite specimen exhibited the most pronounced enhancement with increasing confining pressure. This could be attributed to the increase in confining pressure, which exerted a compaction effect on interlayer cracks, thereby suppressing lateral deformation and exerting a more significant influence on axial deformation. However, when the bedding plane angle deviated from perpendicularity, the effectiveness of confining pressure in compacting micro-cracks between layers, as well as its influence on axial deformation, was significantly reduced.

Figure 7 presents the triaxial compression stress–strain curves of phyllite specimen under varying confining pressures.

Triaxial loading stress–strain curves of phyllite under varying confining pressures.

Under 0 confining pressure, corresponding to uniaxial compression conditions, phyllite exhibited brittle failure characteristics. The compressive strength rapidly decreased after reaching peak point, with minimal post-peak softening and extremely low residual strength. In contrast, under triaxial compression, after reaching the peak stress, the phyllite specimen exhibited a gradual stress reduction with increasing axial strain, rather than an abrupt failure, indicating a transition from brittle to ductile deformation behavior, the stress–strain curve demonstrated a clearly defined softening stage. Therefore, the presence of confining pressure exerted a substantial influence on the mechanical behavior of layered rock formations.

Under confining pressures of 5 MPa, 10 MPa, 20 MPa, 30 MPa, and 40 MPa, the stress–strain behavior of phyllite exhibited four distinct stages. Under high confining pressure, the micro-cracks within the phyllite specimen were largely closed at an early loading stage, leading to the absence of a distinct compaction stage. ① Elastic stage, Stress and strain exhibited a linear relationship, and the deformation of the phyllite specimen was recoverable elastic deformation; ② Elastic–plastic stage, the continuous formation of internal micro-cracks led to a transition from elastic to elastic–plastic deformation behavior, and the tangential modulus at each point gradually decreased; ③ Failure stage, upon reaching the peak stress, the phyllite specimen exhibited a substantial reduction in load-bearing capacity. The tangential modulus transitioned to negative value. Micro-cracks within the phyllite progressively propagated and coalesced, leading to a pronounced strain-softening behavior; ④ Residual stage, following the failure stage, cracks within the phyllite specimen propagated and coalesced into a through-going network, resulting in the formation of a blocky structural configuration. Under the influence of confining pressure, inter-block frictional resistance contributed to the residual load-bearing capacity.

Strength-deformation characteristic

The stress–strain curves of triaxial unloading for different bedding plane angles are shown in Fig. 11. The stress–strain curve during the unloading process was basically consistent with that of the conventional triaxial compression test. The curves could be roughly divided into the following stages: ①Elastic stage, characterized by a linear stress–strain relationship. ②Elastic–plastic stage, where the stress–strain response became nonlinear after the vertical stress reached the yield value. As shown in Table 1, the cohesive force values during the residual stage of both triaxial loading and unloading are generally lower than those observed at the peak stage. This reduction is attributed to the phyllite failure, as the phyllite transforms into discrete blocks. For phyllite specimens with bedding plane angles of 0°, 30°, and 45°, the differences in cohesive force between loading and unloading tests are relatively minor. However, for the 90° specimen, the cohesive force during unloading is notably higher than during loading. This can be interpreted as a result of the abundant micro-cracks in phyllite. Under the influence of initial high confining pressure, these micro-cracks tend to close, particularly in the 90° specimens, where this closure effect is most significant, thereby leading to increased unloading cohesive force.

For triaxial loading tests, the residual frictional angles of phyllite specimens with varying bedding plane angles are generally higher than those at the peak stage, with differences ranging from 8 to 15%. In contrast, in triaxial unloading tests, the residual frictional angles are typically lower than those at the peak stage, showing larger variations between 5.8% and 22.9%. Furthermore, both the peak and residual frictional angles under triaxial unloading test are lower than their counterparts under triaxial loading conditions. During triaxial loading, the presence of confining pressure enhances the contact between fractured rock blocks, allowing the inter-block frictional effect to be fully mobilized, which leads to a higher residual frictional angle compared to the peak value. In triaxial unloading, the gradual reduction of confining pressure weakens the constraint on the damaged rock blocks, resulting in a decrease in the frictional effect and thus a lower residual frictional angle than the peak value. Similarly, as the confining pressure continues to decrease during unloading, the interaction between internal grains and blocks diminishes further, leading to overall lower frictional angles in triaxial unloading compared to triaxial loading.

Numerical simulation analysis

As a numerical simulation tool, PFC2D has found successful application in rock research, and some instructive research outcomes with practical guidance value have been obtained. Constructing a well-validated discrete element model and systematically analyzing the mechanical anisotropy of phyllite can not only compensate for experimental data limitations, but also provide deeper insights into its mechanical behavior. Therefore, numerical simulation research is conducted in this paper based on PFC2D.

In the numerical simulation, the phyllite is regarded as a composite material composed of intact rocks and a series of evenly spaced bedding planes. By changing the inclination angle of the bedding planes, its anisotropic mechanical properties can be simulated.

Building process

The numerical model dimensions are the same as those of the indoor test, as shown in Fig. 14. The process of model generation is as follows:

Numerical model.

(1) Specimen boundary generation: Walls are created according to the dimensions of the indoor test specimens. (2) Particle initialization: Particles are randomly generated within the specimen based on initial porosity, with radii ranging from 0.25 mm to 0.4 mm. Stiffness is assigned to both particles and walls, with wall stiffness set approximately 10% higher than particle stiffness. Initial particle motion under linear contact forces is controlled by resetting velocities to zero every 10 calculation steps. Once equilibrium is reached, excess particles outside the boundaries are removed to form the initial model. (3) Isotropic initial stress application: A small isotropic stress (0.01 MPa) is applied by moving the walls, with a tolerance of 3 × 10⁻3. Stress on each wall is monitored until the relative error from the target is below tolerance, at which point wall movement stops. (4) Floating particle removal: Particles with fewer than three contacts are identified as floating and replaced with new particles at the same locations to ensure each particle has at least three contacts. (5) Assignment of parallel bonding model and smooth joint model.

Loading process

The lower platen is fixed in space, while the upper platen is allowed to move vertically. This movement is controlled to apply a prescribed normal stress to the specimen. A vertical loading velocity of 0.002 m/s is applied, and the numerical time step is set to 8.158 × 10⁻⁸ s to ensure quasi-static conditions during compression, minimizing inertial effects. As a result, the upper platen advances by 1.63 × 10⁻1⁰ m per computational step. Normal displacement is directly measured by tracking the vertical displacement of the upper platen throughout the simulation. Concurrently, the pressure on the lateral walls is continuously monitored and regulated to maintain a constant confining pressure. The average normal stress is calculated from the recorded vertical forces acting on both the lower and upper platens, providing a reliable measure of the applied load.

For bedding plane angles of 0°, 30°, 45°, and 90°, the average ratios of numerical simulation ultimate compressive strength to experimental result are 1.058, 0.952, 0.927, and 0.995, respectively. When the axial confining pressure reaches 40 MPa, the experimental results once again become greater than the numerical predictions. This might be due to the anisotropy of the matrix in the experimental specimens, while the matrix in the numerical specimens is isotropic, which led to the differences. Notably, for bedding plane angles of 0°, 30°, and 45°, the difference between experimental and numerical results increases with rising confining pressure. This trend can be attributed to the fact that the frictional coefficient of the bedding in the numerical model is assumed to be constant. In reality, however, the frictional coefficient may vary with increasing confining pressure due to the heterogeneous distribution of internal particles, leading to discrepancies between the experimental and numerical results.

Crack propagation analysis

The evolution of crack numbers during uniaxial and triaxial compression is closely associated with the failure modes of the phyllite specimens. Figure 18 illustrates the variation in crack numbers at 0.9 times the peak strength for numerical specimens with different bedding plane angles post peak point. Under varying confining pressures, the total number of cracks exhibits an inverted U-shaped distribution, with the maximum observed at 0° bedding angle and the minimum at 45°. This can be explained by the fact that when the bedding planes are horizontal or vertical, the primary load is borne by the phyllite matrix, leading to a higher peak strength, greater fragmentation of the layered rock matrix, a larger shear failure zone, and consequently, an increased number of cracks. When the bedding planes are inclined, the strength of the phyllite specimen is primarily governed by the mechanical properties of the bedding interfaces. This results in a relatively low peak load and predominantly sliding failure along the bedding planes. The phyllite matrix experiences minimal damage, leading to a reduced number of cracks. Both the experimental test failure modes and the numerical simulation results confirm this behavior.

Number of cracks in phyllite specimens with different bedding plane angles.

Additionally, under varying confining pressures, the 0° phyllite specimens are predominantly characterized by matrix tensile cracks. In contrast, the 90° phyllite specimens exhibit splitting failure along the weak planes during uniaxial compression, with a slightly higher proportion of weak plane cracking compared to matrix cracking. As the confining pressure increases, the number of weak plane cracks progressively decreases, and matrix cracking becomes increasingly dominant. For the 45° specimens, weak-plane cracking dominates, particularly under low confining pressures. In uniaxial compression, for instance, weak-plane cracks account for nearly 100% of the total crack population, leading to a failure mode characterized by sliding along the bedding plane. The crack characteristics of the 30° specimen fall between those of the 0° and 45° specimens. Under different confining pressures, the cracks in the matrix and the weak plane each account for a certain proportion. From this, it can be concluded that the number of cracks in specimens with different bedding angles shows significant anisotropy.

Conclusions

This study conducted triaxial loading and unloading tests on phyllite, a typical type of layered rock, to investigate its anisotropic mechanical properties. Additionally, numerical simulations were performed to further support and complement the experimental findings. The following conclusions were drawn:

(1) The mechanical properties of phyllite demonstrate pronounced anisotropy. Under varying confining pressures, the elastic modulus and compressive strength of phyllite exhibit non-linear variation trend, initially decreasing and subsequently increasing with the elevation of the bedding plane angle. With the increase in confining pressure, the compressive strength of phyllite progressively enhances under both triaxial loading and triaxial unloading conditions.

(1) The mechanical properties of phyllite demonstrate pronounced anisotropy. Under varying confining pressures, the elastic modulus and compressive strength of phyllite exhibit non-linear variation trend, initially decreasing and subsequently increasing with the elevation of the bedding plane angle. With the increase in confining pressure, the compressive strength of phyllite progressively enhances under both triaxial loading and triaxial unloading conditions.

(2) The anisotropy in the fracture mechanism of phyllite governs the anisotropic characteristics of failure modes and is closely related to the anisotropy observed in its strength and elastic modulus. During triaxial loading and unloading tests, the phyllite exhibited consistent failure patterns: at a bedding plane angle of 0°, shear failure occurred across the bedding planes; at 30°, a composite failure mode was observed involving simultaneous shearing of both the weak planes and the matrix; at 45°, shear failure occurred along the sliding weak planes; and at 90°, the phyllite experienced tensile splitting failure under low confining pressure and conjugate shear failure under high confining pressure.
