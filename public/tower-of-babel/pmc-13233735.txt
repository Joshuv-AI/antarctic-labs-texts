Semilobar holoprosencephaly associated with maternal diabetes in a toddler: A case report
Authors: Cihan Çelik, Fahad Mirre, İsmail Gedi İbrahim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHoloprosencephaly is a rare congenital malformation of the forebrain caused by incomplete division of the prosencephalon during early embryonic development, and semilobar holoprosencephaly represents the intermediate form of the spectrum. We report the case of a 3-year-old child presenting with developmental delay, seizures, feeding difficulties, and midline facial abnormalities. The child was born to a mother with poorly controlled maternal diabetes mellitus. The MRI scan of the brain showed partial fusion of the frontal lobes, incomplete separation of the hemispheres of the cerebrum, presence of a single ventricular cavity, partial fusion of the thalami, and lack of the septum pellucidum. These clinical signs suggested semilobar holoprosencephaly. This case underlines the importance of neuroimaging in evaluating children with developmental delay and neurological symptoms, and early recognition of such malformations may contribute to better clinical follow-up and family guidance.Keywords: Semilobar holoprosencephaly, Developmental delay, Seizures, Craniofacial abnormalities, MRI

Introduction

Holoprosencephaly is a developmental anomaly resulting from failure of the embryonic forebrain to properly divide into two cerebral hemispheres during the third to fourth week of gestation. The condition includes a series of structural abnormalities ranging from the most severe alobar holoprosencephaly to the mildest lobar holoprosencephaly, whereas semilobar holoprosencephaly is the intermediate form [1]. The disorder is frequently associated with craniofacial abnormalities, neurological deficits, and developmental delay. The overall prevalence of holoprosencephaly in a multicenter study was 1 in 13,000 to 18,000 live births with female preponderance [2]. Many causes of the disease have been discovered, including chromosomal defects such as Trisomy 13, mutations in genes that affect brain development (SHH, ZIC2, SIX3), and maternal diseases such as diabetes mellitus [3,4].

Neuroimaging, particularly magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), plays a crucial role in the diagnosis and classification of the condition. We report a case of semilobar holoprosencephaly diagnosed in a 3-year-old child presenting with seizures and developmental delay.

Case presentation

A 3-year-old child was referred to the pediatric neurology clinic due to recurrent seizures and delayed developmental milestones. The child was born at term via spontaneous vaginal delivery. Antenatal history revealed that the mother had poorly controlled diabetes mellitus during pregnancy. The patient initially presented with feeding difficulties and significant developmental delay. On physical examination, midline facial abnormalities were noted, including hypotelorism (reduced interocular distance), a flattened nasal bridge, and cleft lip (Fig. 1). Neurological examination revealed global developmental delay with impaired motor coordination.

Objective maternal glycemic data (eg, HbA1c or glucose records) were not available at the time of this report; the mother reported suboptimal glycemic control throughout the first trimester. No formal antenatal fetal anomaly screening was documented. Chromosomal and genetic testing (karyotype and targeted gene panel for SHH and ZIC2 and SIX3 mutations) were not performed due to resource limitations. This constitutes a limitation in the current case study; however, such an assessment should be performed in other cases considering that there is a recognized link between holoprosencephaly and genetic defects.

Brain MRI was performed for further evaluation on a 1.5 Tesla scanner using axial and coronal T2-weighted images, axial FLAIR, and axial T1-weighted sequences, with a slice thickness of 4 mm. The imaging findings included:

Management involved treatment with antiepileptic drugs (seizures; generalized tonic-clonic seizures; levetiracetam started but with limited success) for seizure management and referral for a multidisciplinary approach with involvement of pediatric neurology, developmental rehab, and genetic counseling. A repeat neurology review was made after three months to assess seizure control and neurodevelopmental milestones achieved. Referrals for speech therapy and physiotherapy were also made. Neurodevelopmental outcome and risk of recurrence were discussed during family counseling.

Discussion

It is a rare congenital brain defect, and it occurs at a prevalence rate of 1 in 10,000 to 20,000 live births [5]. This disease arises due to the failure of brain separation in early brain development between the 18th to 28th day of gestation [5]. Semilobar holoprosencephaly falls between the other subtypes on the spectrum, and it is defined as the incomplete separation of the posterior regions of the cerebral hemispheres while the anterior portions are fused [6]. Common anomalies include fused thalamus, absent septum pellucidum, and one ventricular system. There is also a presence of an interhemispheric fissure posteriorly and either an absence or hypogenesis of the corpus callosum [6]. Our patient's findings of partial fusion of frontal lobes, one ventricle, fused thalami, agenesis of the corpus callosum, and septum pellucidum are similar to the radiological characteristics of semilobar holoprosencephaly.

Holoprosencephaly is classified into three main subtypes based on the degree of forebrain cleavage failure. The first type is the alobar HPE, which is the most serious one, with a lack of separation of hemispheres, the presence of a single ventricle, thalamic fusion, and a non-existent corpus callosum, with little chance of survival beyond the first year of life. Semilobar HPE, as seen in our patient, is the intermediate form with partial posterior hemispheric separation but persistent anterior fusion. Lobar HPE is the mildest form, with near-complete hemispheric separation but residual frontal pole fusion and generally less severe clinical manifestations. This categorization helps in forecasting and counseling and highlights the indispensable function of MRI in determining subtypes [6].

Clinical manifestations

Clinical manifestations vary depending on the severity of the malformation but commonly include seizures, developmental delay, feeding difficulties, and craniofacial anomalies [7]. Facial abnormalities occur frequently because facial and brain development are closely linked during embryogenesis. The classic saying “The face predicts the brain” is correct in approximately 70–80% of cases [8]. In our case, the presence of hypotelorism, a flattened nasal bridge, and cleft lip further supports this association and is consistent with previously described findings.

Etiology and risk factors

Maternal diabetes mellitus is a recognized risk factor for congenital malformations, including holoprosencephaly, although it should be regarded as a likely contributing risk factor rather than a proven sole cause; genetic and chromosomal etiologies must also be excluded. Hyperglycemia during early pregnancy may disrupt normal embryonic signaling pathways involved in forebrain development [9]. A 200-fold increase in the incidence of HPE in babies of diabetic mothers compared with babies of non-diabetic mothers has been reported [10]. Our patient was born to a mother with poorly controlled diabetes during pregnancy, which is consistent with this association. Other environmental factors include alcohol and tobacco use during pregnancy, TORCH infections, and certain medications.

Diagnosis

MRI is the imaging modality of choice for diagnosis, as it clearly demonstrates the degree of hemispheric separation and associated structural abnormalities [11]. In fact, MRI in our patient profoundly highlighted the typical features of semilobar holoprosencephaly and helped us to categorize the brain malformation accurately. Antenatal ultrasonography can also assist in identifying intracranial abnormalities and associated facial malformations of HPE. Making a diagnosis at an early stage can help the patient receive proper treatments, care, and counseling [12].

Prognosis

The prognosis of HPE depends on the severity and associated anomalies. Most newborns with alobar HPE have a poor prognosis. Children with other forms of HPE rarely survive into adulthood. HPE survivors may experience developmental delay, profound intellectual impairment, and seizures [12]. Consistent with these observations, our patient demonstrated global developmental delay and seizure disorder requiring antiepileptic treatment and multidisciplinary follow-up.

Overall, our patient’s clinical and radiological findings strongly correspond to those of semilobar holoprosencephaly described in the literature. This case supports a possible association between diabetes during pregnancy and brain development abnormalities in the forebrain area and highlights the need for good glucose control as a means to avoid such abnormalities.

This case is best understood within the broader context of pediatric neuroimaging reports of complex congenital anomalies. Recent literature has highlighted the value of MRI in clarifying atypical congenital brain presentations; for example, a 2025 case report demonstrated how MRI delineated unexpected features in a 10-month-old infant with Sturge-Weber syndrome, paralleling our experience in which MRI was essential for establishing the HPE subtype [13]. Furthermore, Haile et al. (2025) described a fatal case of congenital syngnathia with holoprosencephaly in a resource-limited setting, reinforcing that craniofacial and forebrain anomalies frequently coexist and that syndromic evaluation is important [14].

Conclusion

Semilobar holoprosencephaly is one of the rare congenital brain defects that can show symptoms such as seizures, developmental delay, and craniofacial abnormalities. Neuroimaging plays an important role in confirming the diagnosis and determining the severity of the disorder. This report emphasizes the need to remember congenital brain malformations as a differential diagnosis when children present with neurological symptoms, especially if there are maternal risk factors like diabetes present. It is better to think of maternal diabetes as a risk factor that contributes to the situation as opposed to a definite cause; genetic and chromosomal testing should be done when the availability of resources is not an issue. Early identification and a team approach to treatment are key to improving the patient's condition and giving correct counseling to the family.
