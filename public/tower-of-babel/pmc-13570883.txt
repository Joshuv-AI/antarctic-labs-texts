The method matters: free water imaging in Parkinson’s disease is not a binary verdict
Authors: A. Enrique Martinez-Nunez, Angelos Barmpoutis, Michael S. Okun, David Vaillancourt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A recent comparison of neuromelanin and free water (FW) imaging in Parkinson’s disease concluded that FW imaging underperforms. We argue this reflects methodological constraints, not biological truth. Three issues compromise FW sensitivity: use of a registration template that degrades substantia nigra localization, an age imbalance between groups that favors neuromelanin detection, and repetition times insufficient to capture the FW compartment. With unmet acquisition requirements, the technique was never correctly applied.

The recent npj Parkinson’s Disease article comparing neuromelanin MRI and free water (FW) imaging in early Parkinson’s disease (PD)1 raises a fundamental issue regarding the application of appropriate imaging methodology. By concluding that FW imaging does not perform well in early PD, a conclusion solidified in the study’s title, the paper risks framing a methodologically constrained outcome as a biological truth. The authors acknowledge the use of single-shell clinical diffusion data, but the issue is that the imaging sequence and normalization procedures are limited, as the issue just being single-shell is ill-posed. This distinction is not trivial. FW imaging is not a single entity; it is a family of approaches whose performance is highly dependent on acquisition parameters, modeling strategy, and signal-to-noise considerations. When these factors are suboptimal, the technique will predictably underperform. In this study, FW values showed no significant differences between early PD and controls, while neuromelanin measures performed well. The absence of a signal should not be conflated with absence of utility.

The methods used in this study illustrate the core issue. There are several issues with the current methods that depart from prior studies of FW in patients with PD: (1) registration template choice, (2) age imbalance between groups, and (3) repetition time (TR) length.

Registration template choice

The authors used a T1 in MNI space to register the DTI data rather than using a fractional anisotropy (FA) template. Prior work has demonstrated that registration performance for subcortical structures is significantly inferior when using non-FA templates as the reference, with optimal overlap between hand-drawn and automated regions achieved only when FA templates are used2. This issue is important because in early PD3 and in RBD4 the posterior part of the substantia nigra is where the changes in FW are typically observed. When registration is suboptimal, signal from adjacent subregions bleed together, diluting the focal posterior effect that FW imaging is designed to detect. In other words, the region where the biological signal is expected to be strongest is precisely the region most vulnerable to the registration approach used in this study.

Age imbalance between groups

There was a difference in age for the early PD cohort that provides an advantage for neuromelanin imaging and a deficit for FW imaging. The cohort here showed a significantly increased age for the control group compared with the early PD group (Table 1 in Roh’s article). In FW imaging, it is established that FW in the substantia nigra increases with age and increases with PD, thus going in the same direction. Thus, when age is increased in a control group compared with a PD group, the ability to detect a FW effect being increased in PD is compromised. However, for neuromelanin imaging, the signal for aging increases up to about 70 years, and in PD the neuromelanin signal decreases5. This indicates that the age effect and PD effect in the age range for a PD diagnosis go in the opposite directions. Thus, since the significant age difference in this study would cause the PD effect and age effect to go in opposite directions, the lack of controlling age in this study can enhance the ability to detect a PD effect in neuromelanin imaging. We note that the external validation cohort was reportedly balanced for age and sex, reducing the contribution of this particular confound to that dataset. This point is not to detract from the usefulness of neuromelanin imaging, but it does make the point that methods matter.

Repetition time (TR) length

The external validation cohort used a TR of 3100 ms, falling lower than what is recommended in the literature for FW imaging6. This matters for a reason specific to the FW compartment: cerebrospinal fluid and extracellular FW have substantially longer T1 relaxation times than surrounding tissue. T1 relaxation time is calculated over 4000 ms at 3 T compared to ~400 to 1200 ms for white matter7,8. A TR of 3100 ms is insufficient to allow full longitudinal recovery of the FW compartment signal between excitations, systematically attenuating the very component that FW imaging aims to isolate and quantify. It is important to note that the Automated Imaging Differentiation for Parkinsonism, which relies on a single-shell diffusion sequence, was granted Class II medical device status by the United States Food and Drug Administration for assistance in diagnosing Parkinsonism, and the TR required in this approval is recommended >6000 ms. Prior work has shown that longitudinal increases in FW for the substantia nigra are observed using a TR > 6000 ms whereas the effect is not observed with the TR set at 2500 ms9. Prior work has demonstrated that shorter TRs, like those used in multiband acquisitions, can result in movement artifacts related to spin-history effects10. These spin-history effects are caused by the reduced time interval between successive excitation of the same spins, limiting the data quality. In addition, voxel size and acquisition parameters play a critical role. Small voxels, such as those approaching 1 × 1 × 2 mm, increase noise and reduce signal stability unless compensated by longer repetition times and optimized acquisition schemes. Taken together, these are not minor technical footnotes; they are central determinants of whether FW differences can be detected, particularly in early disease where effect sizes are modest. The TR-related acquisition limitations apply to all study cohorts regardless of demographic balance and represent a methodological constraint that is independent of group composition.

It is therefore not surprising that FW did not work under these conditions. The study demonstrates that FW imaging, when applied using a clinically convenient but technically constrained protocol, may fail to detect early PD. That is a valuable observation. However, it does not justify a broader conclusion about the technique itself. A more precise interpretation is that the specific implementation of FW imaging in this comparison lacked the sensitivity required for early disease detection. Indeed, the authors themselves note that FW changes may be more subtle in early stages and that methodological differences across studies are likely to explain discrepant findings.

There is also a conceptual issue worth emphasizing. FW in the substantia nigra has never been, in our view, a standalone diagnostic biomarker for PD. It reflects extracellular microstructural changes associated with neuronal loss in the substantia nigra but is not at a level for diagnostic evaluation of detecting PD. Other work using machine learning approaches and FW imaging, which now have de novo FDA classification as a Class II medical device, does clearly establish that this approach has diagnostic implications for differentiating PD from atypical Parkinsonism2,6 and differentiating Dementia with Lewy Bodies from Alzheimer’s Disease11. In contrast, neuromelanin imaging is linked to dopaminergic neuron integrity12, and its performance in this study is biologically plausible.

In summary, this study underscores an essential principle in neuroimaging: implementation is not an accessory, but the practical expression of the physical principles that define a technique’s biological target. When acquisition parameters fail to meet the technique’s fundamental requirements, the technique was never truly applied. The neuromelanin findings are compelling, and the subregional framework is a meaningful methodological contribution. However, FW imaging did not perform in this dataset for reasons that are methodological, not definitive. Titles and interpretations should reflect this nuance. The field will benefit most from careful optimization and standardization of acquisition protocols rather than premature conclusions about the utility of an evolving imaging biomarker.
