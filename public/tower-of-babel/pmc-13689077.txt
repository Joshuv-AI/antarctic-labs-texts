Comparative efficacy and safety of subcutaneous vedolizumab versus other targeted inflammatory bowel therapies in patients with moderate to severe ulcerative colitis: a network meta-analysis update
Authors: Jeanne Jiang, Abigail Wojtowicz, Jennifer Uyei, He Jin, Kristina Lindsley, Marie Sanchirico
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe growing number of targeted inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) therapies available for the treatment of moderate to severe ulcerative colitis has increased the complexity of treatment decisions for clinicians. Clinical decision-making is further hindered by a paucity of head-to-head clinical trials comparing the relative efficacy and safety of available targeted IBD therapies; however, network meta-analyses (NMAs) can indirectly compare treatments.MethodsNetworks of evidence were formed from 29 randomized controlled trials of targeted IBD therapies. To address differences in study design, adjustments were made to the treat-through trials in order to mimic a re-randomization design. A Bayesian NMA was conducted with the primary analysis using fixed-effect models to estimate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% credible intervals (CrIs) for efficacy and safety outcomes.ResultsThere was no significant difference in the maintenance of clinical response or clinical remission of any targeted IBD therapy relative to vedolizumab 108 mg every 2 weeks (Q2W) subcutaneously (SC), except for adalimumab 40 mg every other week (EOW) SC, which had significantly lower odds (clinical response: OR, 0.36; 95% CrI, 0.17–0.76; clinical remission: OR, 0.35; 95% CrI, 0.16–0.75). The odds of experiencing overall adverse events were significantly higher for patients receiving placebo and treatment regimens for six targeted IBD therapies (adalimumab, etrasimod, golimumab, infliximab, ozanimod, and tofacitinib) than those receiving vedolizumab 108 mg Q2W SC. The odds of serious adverse events were not significantly different between any targeted IBD therapy and vedolizumab 108 mg Q2W SC. The odds of overall infections were significantly higher for those receiving treatment regimens for three targeted IBD therapies (golimumab, ozanimod, and tofacitinib) than those receiving vedolizumab 108 mg Q2W SC; the odds for other treatments were not significantly different.ConclusionsThe results of this NMA update indicate that vedolizumab SC demonstrates efficacy that is either superior or not significantly different from other targeted IBD therapies, including established biologics and novel oral therapies, and a safety profile that is superior or not significantly different with regard to overall adverse events and overall infections. The efficacy and safety of intravenous vedolizumab is equivalent to that of vedolizumab SC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12876-026-04804-7.

Background

Ulcerative colitis (UC) is a chronic, progressive, and relapsing disorder characterized by inflammation of the rectum and colon [1]. Because chronic inflammation can result in functional impairments, need for colectomy, and increased risk of colorectal cancer [1–3], treatment of UC aims to induce and maintain clinical and endoscopic remission [4]. The number of treatment options for patients with UC has increased in recent years and several others are under investigation in phase 3 clinical trials, necessitating frequent updates to guidelines and indirect treatment comparisons to assist healthcare providers to navigate an increasingly complex treatment landscape [5]. Targeted inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) therapies currently available in the USA for moderate to severe UC span several drug classes including anti-tumor necrosis factor α (TNFα) treatments, interleukin inhibitors, integrin inhibitors, Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitors, and sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P) receptor modulators [5].

Although the availability of multiple therapies from different drug classes provides more options for patients with UC to attain remission, it increases the complexity of treatment decisions for clinicians who must assess the benefit–risk profile associated with each treatment [6]. Clinical decision-making is further hindered by a paucity of head-to-head clinical trials comparing the relative efficacy and safety of available targeted IBD therapies [6]. Indeed, only one head-to-head trial (VARSITY) of targeted IBD therapies (adalimumab vs. vedolizumab) has been conducted in patients with moderate to severe UC [7]. Evidence to support clinical decision-making may be obtained from network meta-analyses (NMAs), which indirectly compare multiple treatments using data from existing trials. For example, the American Gastroenterological Association (AGA) recently published clinical practice guidelines on the pharmacological management of moderate to severe UC using an NMA to support their recommendations [8, 9]. While NMAs are the best statistical method for comparing multiple data sets in the absence of direct head-to-head clinical trials, these should be interpreted carefully as the results can vary depending on the methodological approach used, as well as the selection of trials and outcomes for inclusion in the analysis [10, 11].

We conducted an NMA in 2020 to compare the efficacy and safety of targeted IBD therapies relative to placebo and vedolizumab intravenously (IV) using data from randomized controlled trials (RCTs) [12]. Since 2020, the results of additional RCTs have been published, including targeted IBD therapies (etrasimod, mirikizumab, ozanimod, and upadacitinib) that were not included in the previous NMA and have since been approved for the treatment of moderate to severe UC in the USA [13–20]. Furthermore, in 2023, a subcutaneous formulation of vedolizumab received approval in the USA for use as a maintenance therapy in patients with moderate to severe UC [21].

Here, we report the results of an update to the previous NMA, incorporating data from recent RCTs of targeted IBD therapies and comparing the efficacy and safety of targeted IBD therapies relative to vedolizumab subcutaneously (SC) and placebo. In this analysis, we included additional efficacy outcome measures not included in the previous analysis and focused on maintenance due to the importance of long-term disease control [22]. While addressing symptoms and disease flares in the short term is imperative for good patient care, current guidelines emphasize the importance of endoscopic healing as a long-term treatment target in IBD [22].

Methods

Evidence base

A systematic literature review (SLR) was previously undertaken with literature searches conducted in March 2018 and March 2020. The search was performed again on April 19, 2023 using methods previously described to identify RCTs of targeted IBD therapies in adults with moderate to severe UC [12]. The SLR was reported in accordance with Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis 2020 statement [23].

Briefly, the SLR employed searches in Embase, MEDLINE, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (Supplementary Table 1), with supplemental manual searches of clinical trial registries (ClinicalTrials.gov and ClinicalTrials.eu), the bibliographies of five recent publications [12, 24–27], and congress publications. Congresses included in the manual searches were the Annual Scientific Meeting of the American College of Gastroenterology 2022, Congress of the European Crohn’s and Colitis Organisation 2023, Digestive Disease Week 2022, and United European Gastroenterology Week 2022. Four targeted IBD therapies (etrasimod, mirikizumab, ozanimod, and upadacitinib) were newly added to the search strategy for this update. A subcutaneous formulation of CT-P13, an infliximab biosimilar, was also included. At the time of the literature search, risankizumab and guselkumab were not approved in the USA for the treatment of moderate to severe UC and, therefore, were not included. Search results underwent title and abstract screening for eligibility by one reviewer with a trained artificial intelligence classifier used as a second reviewer, followed by full-text review by two independent reviewers. Any discrepancies during screening were adjudicated by a third reviewer.

In total, 29 RCTs were identified, including 19 trials from the prior SLR and 10 trials identified in this update (Supplementary Fig. 1). The newly included trials were ELEVATE UC 12 and ELEVATE UC 52 [13], LIBERTY-UC [28], LUCENT-1 and LUCENT-2 [29], RIVETING [30], SERENE UC [31], True North [14], U-ACCOMPLISH [32], and U-ACHIEVE [15]. ELEVATE UC 12, ELEVATE UC 52, and U-ACCOMPLISH were included despite these trials enrolling patients aged 16 years or older; however, the proportion of patients aged younger than 18 years was less than 2% in ELEVATE UC 52 and U-ACCOMPLISH [33, 34]. RIVETING was included in the analysis; however, it should be noted that this is a dose reduction trial that evaluated the reduction of tofacitinib 10 mg twice daily (BID) to 5 mg BID in patients who were in stable remission [30].

Efficacy and safety data were extracted from eligible RCTs by two independent reviewers. Eligibility criteria, study treatment and designs, and patient characteristics were extracted by a single reviewer and checked for accuracy by a second reviewer. Eligibility criteria and study treatments and designs are summarized in Supplementary Tables 2 and 3, respectively. Patient characteristics during induction and maintenance are summarized in Supplementary Tables 4 and 5, respectively. Reported outcomes in the overall population, the anti-TNFα-naïve population, and the anti-TNFα-experienced population are summarized in Supplementary Tables 6, 7, and 8, respectively.

The quality of individual trials was assessed according to the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool version 2 for RCTs [35]. All new trials added to this update were assessed as having a low risk of bias (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Outcomes

Efficacy outcomes of interest included clinical response and clinical remission, which were included in the previous NMA, as well as mucosal healing, corticosteroid-free remission, and sustained response, which were newly added to this NMA. Efficacy outcomes were analyzed separately for the induction phase and the maintenance phase, except for sustained response, which included data from both induction and maintenance phases. Time points for induction and maintenance were as defined by the study investigators for each study.

Analyses were conducted to evaluate the variability in how outcomes were reported and to identify the most common method of reporting across the 29 trials included in the NMA. Clinical response was typically defined as a reduction in complete Mayo score of ≥ 3 points and ≥ 30% from baseline with an accompanying decrease in rectal bleeding subscore of ≥ 1 point or absolute rectal bleeding subscore of ≤ 1 point. Clinical remission was typically defined as complete Mayo score of ≤ 2 points and no individual subscore > 1 point. Mucosal healing was typically defined as a Mayo endoscopic subscore of ≤ 1; one study also required a Geboes score of < 2.0 [14]. Sustained response was defined as response during induction and maintenance with the time points determined by the study investigators. All of these definitions were deemed sufficiently similar for analysis. For corticosteroid-free remission, only definitions during maintenance were deemed sufficiently similar for analysis. This outcome was typically defined as discontinuation of corticosteroids and clinical remission by week 52 in patients taking corticosteroids at baseline, although other time points were used by some trials (i.e., week 30, week 54, and week 60).

Subgroup analyses were conducted for clinical response and clinical remission based on prior anti-TNFα exposure (i.e., anti-TNFα naïve or anti-TNFα experienced). Mucosal healing, corticosteroid-free remission, and sustained response were not reported for anti-TNFα-naïve and anti-TNFα-experienced subgroups in the VISIBLE 1 trial; therefore, analyses with vedolizumab SC as a reference were only conducted for the overall population.

Safety outcomes of interest included overall adverse events, serious adverse events, overall infections, serious infections, and discontinuation due to adverse events.

Adjustments for trial design

The trials included in the NMA were a mixture of re-randomization trials, in which patients were re-randomized at the end of the induction phase, and treat-through trials, in which patients were randomized at the start of treatment and received the same treatment throughout the trial with no re-randomization. Because the majority of trials included in the analysis were re-randomization trials, adjustments were made to the treat-through trials in order to mimic the re-randomization design and were conducted as previously reported [12]. Four of the RCTs included in this NMA were treat-through trials (ACT-1, ELEVATE UC 52, ULTRA 2, and VARSITY); however, ELEVATE UC 52 did not report the proportion of patients who achieved clinical response at both week 12 and week 52; therefore, imputation was not feasible for this study and raw data were used.

Clinical response and clinical remission during maintenance were calculated such that the denominator was based on the number of responders at the end of the induction. For clinical response, the numerator was the number of patients with sustained response (response at both week 6, 8, or 10 and week 52, 54, or 60). For clinical remission, the numerator was the number of patients with remission (remission at both week 6, 8, or 10 and week 52, 54, or 60). This provided estimates of all trials with week 52, 54, or 60 clinical response and/or remission data in a consistent manner that could be assessed in a single network of evidence.

Network meta-analysis

A Bayesian NMA was conducted with posterior densities for the unknown parameters estimated using Markov chain Monte Carlo simulations. For clinical response and clinical remission, which were considered to be ordered categorical data with three mutually exclusive states (no clinical response, clinical response but no clinical remission, and clinical remission), a multinomial probit model was used [36]. For the binary outcomes of mucosal healing, corticosteroid-free remission, sustained response, and safety outcomes, the NMA was performed based on the proportion of patients experiencing the event of interest using a regression model with a binomial likelihood and logit link function. Models were implemented in OpenBUGS v3.2.3 (OpenBUGS Foundation) and R 3.5.0 (The R Foundation) software.

As with the previous NMA, analyses were conducted using placebo as a reference. For this update, new analyses with vedolizumab 108 mg every 2 weeks (Q2W) SC during maintenance as a reference were conducted. The primary analysis used fixed-effect models because they had lower deviance information criteria than observed in random-effects models. Relative treatment effects were expressed as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% credible intervals (CrIs) and treatments were considered to be significantly different to the reference treatment when the 95% CrI did not encompass 1.

Results

Networks

Efficacy

The networks for induction of clinical response and clinical remission included 22 studies for the overall population and 17 studies for the anti-TNFα-naïve population, of which 7 studies (ELEVATE UC 12, ELEVATE UC 52, LUCENT-1, SERENE UC, True North, U-ACCOMPLISH, and U-ACHIEVE) and 2 studies (U-ACCOMPLISH and U-ACHIEVE), respectively, were newly added in this update (Supplementary Fig. 3). There were 7 studies in the anti-TNFα-experienced network for induction of clinical response and clinical remission, with no new studies added.

The networks for maintenance of clinical response and clinical remission included 17 studies for the overall population and 12 studies for the anti-TNFα-naïve population, of which 7 (ELEVATE UC 52, LIBERTY-UC, LUCENT-2, RIVETING, SERENE UC, True North, and U-ACHIEVE) and 2 studies (LIBERTY-UC and U-ACHIEVE), respectively, were new to this update (Fig. 1a and b). There were 8 studies in the anti-TNFα-experienced network for maintenance of clinical response and clinical remission, of which 1 (RIVETING) was new to this update (Fig. 1c).

Safety

The networks for safety outcomes in the overall population included 20 studies (10 new studies) for overall adverse events, 17 studies for serious adverse events (8 new studies), 14 studies (5 new studies) for overall infections, 14 studies (6 new studies) for serious infections, and 15 studies (6 new studies) for discontinuation due to adverse events (Supplementary Fig. 5).

Targeted IBD therapies versus vedolizumab 108 mg Q2W SC

Efficacy

The odds of clinical response and clinical remission during maintenance were significantly lower with placebo than with vedolizumab 108 mg SC in the overall population, and in the anti-TNFα-naïve and anti-TNFα-experienced populations (Fig. 2). In the overall population, the odds of clinical response during maintenance were not significantly different from vedolizumab 108 mg Q2W SC for almost all targeted IBD therapies, including vedolizumab 300 mg Q8W IV; the only exception was that the odds were significantly lower for adalimumab 40 mg every other week (EOW) SC (OR, 0.36; 95% CrI, 0.17–0.76) than for vedolizumab 108 mg SC (Fig. 2a). Similarly, the odds of clinical remission during maintenance were significantly lower for patients receiving adalimumab 40 mg EOW SC (OR, 0.35; 95% CrI, 0.16–0.75) than those receiving vedolizumab 108 mg Q2W SC (Fig. 2b). The odds of clinical remission for other targeted IBD therapies, including vedolizumab 300 mg Q8W IV, were not significantly different to vedolizumab 108 mg Q2W SC.

In the anti-TNFα-naïve population, there was no significant difference between the odds of clinical response or clinical remission during maintenance for targeted IBD therapies and vedolizumab 108 mg Q2W SC (Fig. 2).

Only a limited number of targeted IBD therapies (adalimumab, tofacitinib, ustekinumab, and vedolizumab) and placebo were included in the anti-TNFα-experienced patients network. Anti-TNFα-experienced patients were included in RCTs for other targeted IBD therapies; however, the necessary outcome data were not reported for the anti-TNFα-experienced subgroup specifically. Among anti-TNFα-experienced patients, the odds of clinical response and clinical remission were significantly lower in patients receiving ustekinumab 90 mg every 12 weeks (Q12W) SC (OR, 0.24; 95% CrI, 0.07–0.73 for both response and remission) than in patients receiving vedolizumab 108 mg Q2W SC (Fig. 2). The odds of clinical response and clinical remission for all other targeted IBD therapies was similar to that of vedolizumab 108 mg Q2W SC.

The outcomes of mucosal healing, corticosteroid-free remission, and sustained response were analyzed relative to vedolizumab 108 mg Q2W SC in the overall population (Fig. 3). There was no difference in the odds of mucosal healing during maintenance for most targeted IBD therapies compared with vedolizumab 108 mg Q2W SC, with the exception that the odds were significantly lower for adalimumab 40 mg EOW SC (OR, 0.41; 95% CI, 0.20–0.83) and ustekinumab 90 mg Q12W SC (OR, 0.41; 95% CrI, 0.19–0.87) (Fig. 3a). All targeted IBD therapies had similar odds to vedolizumab 108 mg SC for corticosteroid-free remission during maintenance (Fig. 3b). Upadacitinib 30 mg once daily (OD) orally (PO) had significantly greater odds of sustained response (OR, 3.75; 95% CrI, 1.63–8.65) than vedolizumab 108 mg Q2W SC; all other targeted IBD therapies had similar odds (Fig. 3c).

Safety

The odds of experiencing overall adverse events were significantly higher for patients receiving placebo, adalimumab 40 mg EW SC and 40 mg EOW SC, etrasimod 2 mg OD PO, golimumab 50 mg Q4W SC and 100 mg Q4W SC, infliximab 10 mg/kg Q8W IV and 120 mg EOW SC, ozanimod 0.92 mg OD PO, and tofacitinib 10 mg BID PO than those receiving vedolizumab 108 mg Q2W SC (Fig. 4a). There was no significant difference in the odds of experiencing serious adverse events for any targeted IBD therapy or placebo compared with vedolizumab 108 mg Q2W SC (Fig. 4b). The odds of overall infections were significantly higher for those receiving golimumab 50 mg Q4W SC and 100 mg Q4W SC, ozanimod 0.92 mg OD PO, and tofacitinib 5 mg BID PO and 10 mg BID PO than those receiving vedolizumab 108 mg Q2W SC (Fig. 4c). For serious infections, the results could not be interpreted owing to the limited availability of data and the rare occurrence of serious infections. Only one trial evaluated vedolizumab 108 mg Q2W SC (VISIBLE 1) and no serious infection events occurred in the placebo arm (n = 56). However, in the comparator trials, the sample size of the placebo arms were larger (n ≥ 121) and therefore it was more likely that an event would occur; at least one serious infection was reported in each placebo arm. The odds of discontinuation due to adverse events were significantly higher for patients receiving placebo (OR, 4.03; 95% CrI, 1.40–13.63) and golimumab 100 mg Q4W SC (OR, 6.02; 95% CrI, 1.52–26.32) than for those receiving vedolizumab 108 mg Q2W SC (Fig. 4d).

Targeted IBD therapies versus placebo

Efficacy

For targeted IBD therapies included in the previous NMA, efficacy results for clinical response and clinical remission relative to placebo were consistent with previous findings [12]. Targeted IBD therapies added to this NMA update (etrasimod, mirikizumab, ozanimod, and upadacitinib) had significantly greater odds than placebo for clinical response and clinical remission during induction (Supplementary Fig. 6) and maintenance (Supplementary Fig. 7) in the overall population.

All targeted IBD therapies had significantly greater odds of attaining mucosal healing than placebo, except for adalimumab 80/40 mg during the induction phase (OR, 1.07; 95% CrI, 0.74–1.53) (Supplementary Fig. 8). Similarly, all targeted IBD therapies included in the analysis had significantly greater odds of sustained response than placebo. For corticosteroid-free remission during maintenance, golimumab 50 mg Q4W SC and 100 mg Q4W SC and infliximab 10 mg/kg did not have significantly greater odds than placebo; the other targeted IBD therapies all had significantly greater odds.

Safety

For targeted IBD therapies included in the previous NMA, safety results relative to placebo were consistent with previous findings [12]. For safety outcomes, there were significant differences for the targeted IBD therapies added to this NMA update compared with placebo (Supplementary Fig. 9). Etrasimod 2 mg OD PO and ozanimod 0.92 mg OD PO had significantly greater odds of overall adverse events than placebo (OR, 1.92; 95% CrI, 1.18–3.38 and OR, 1.68; 95% CrI, 1.16–2.44, respectively). Mirikizumab 200 mg Q4W SC and upadacitinib 30 mg OD PO had significantly lower odds of serious adverse events than placebo (OR, 0.41; 95% CrI, 0.19–0.88 and OR, 0.42; 95% CrI, 0.17–0.93, respectively). The odds of overall infections were significantly higher for ozanimod 0.92 mg OD PO than placebo (OR, 2.23; 95% CrI, 1.35–3.75). Etrasimod 2 mg OD PO had significantly higher odds of discontinuation due to adverse events than placebo (OR, 1.92; 95% CrI, 1.18–3.38) whereas mirikizumab 200 mg Q4W SC and upadacitinib 15 mg OD PO had significantly lower odds than placebo (OR, 0.17; 95% CrI, 0.06–0.42 and OR, 0.32; 95% CrI, 0.11–0.80, respectively).

Discussion

The results of this updated NMA indicate that vedolizumab 108 mg Q2W SC during maintenance demonstrates efficacy that is greater than or not significantly different from that of other targeted IBD therapies, including established biologics and novel oral therapies, in patients with moderate to severe UC. Notably, adalimumab 40 mg EOW SC had a significantly lower likelihood of clinical response, clinical remission, and mucosal healing than vedolizumab 108 mg Q2W SC in the overall population. However, it should be noted that these findings pertain to adalimumab 40 mg EOW dosing and should not be generalized to weekly or optimized dosing; in clinical practice and during the SERENE-UC trial, dose escalation may improve clinical outcomes with adalimumab [31]. Ustekinumab 90 mg Q12W SC had a significantly lower likelihood of clinical response and clinical remission than vedolizumab 108 mg Q2W SC in the anti-TNFα-experienced population and a lower likelihood of mucosal healing in the overall population. The efficacies of vedolizumab 300 mg Q8W IV, vedolizumab 300 mg Q4W IV, and vedolizumab 108 mg Q2W SC were similar.

There were significant differences in safety outcomes between other targeted IBD therapies and vedolizumab 108 mg Q2W SC. Treatment regimens for several targeted IBD therapies had a significantly higher risk of overall adverse events than vedolizumab 108 mg Q2W SC, including adalimumab, etrasimod, golimumab, infliximab, ozanimod, and tofacitinib. Treatment regimens for golimumab, ozanimod, and tofacitinib also had a significantly greater risk of overall infections than vedolizumab 108 mg Q2W SC. Other NMAs have identified favorable safety outcomes for vedolizumab compared with other targeted IBD therapies [37, 38]. Burr et al. reported that vedolizumab IV was the least likely drug to lead to infections [37] and Lasa et al. found that vedolizumab was the best performing agent for safety outcomes, with the lowest ranking for adverse events and serious adverse events [38]. Our analysis expands on these findings, suggesting that that vedolizumab SC has equivalent or superior safety outcomes compared with other targeted IBD therapies and a safety profile that is not significantly different from vedolizumab IV.

To our knowledge, this is the first NMA to specifically compare the efficacy and safety of targeted IBD therapies and placebo relative to vedolizumab SC. The results of the present NMA for comparisons with placebo are consistent with the findings of our previously published NMA [12]. Singh et al. conducted an NMA in 2020, which concluded that infliximab had the greatest efficacy for induction of clinical remission in biologic naïve patients with moderate to severe UC whereas ustekinumab and tofacitinib ranked highest in biologic experienced patients; however, this analysis did not include the newer therapies included in the present NMA [39]. An updated NMA conducted in 2024, which informed the AGA clinical guidelines, ranked risankizumab and ozanimod highest for induction of clinical remission in biologic naïve patients, excluding JAK inhibitors as first-line treatment [9]. However, both of these NMAs used a frequentist rather than a Bayesian approach [9, 39]. The use of both methods could help to address potential heterogeneity or inconsistency in the underlying studies and ensure a balanced interpretation of the results [11]. Most other NMAs have concluded that upadacitinib has the greatest efficacy for induction of clinical remission [37, 38, 40, 41]. Consistent with these findings, in the present NMA, upadacitinib 45 mg OD PO had the largest effect size versus placebo for induction of clinical remission in the overall population and the anti-TNFα-naïve population. However, upadacitinib is only approved in the USA for use after anti-TNFα treatment, and has warnings for major adverse cardiovascular events, malignancies, mortality, serious infections, and thrombosis [19]. It should also be noted that while outcome definitions were deemed sufficiently similar for inclusion in the present NMA, definitions of clinical remission in U-ACHIEVE and U-ACCOMPLISH trials of upadacitinib are based on adapted Mayo score (without Physician Global Assessment) rather than total Mayo score. Use of adapted Mayo score is common among newer trials in response to Food and Drug Administration recommendations issued in 2016, which hinders interpretation of comparisons of results from older versus newer trials [42, 43].

The strengths of this NMA are the inclusion of data for vedolizumab SC from VISIBLE 1, the method used to combine results from re-randomization and treat-through trials, and leveraging the nested nature of clinical response and clinical remission. The limitations of the present analysis are consistent with those of the previous NMA [12]. There is a risk of publication bias because the results of some clinical trials may not be published owing to unfavorable results. Searches were restricted to English language publications; therefore, results published in another language will have been missed. Some treatment arms had small sample sizes, which needs to be taken into account for interpretation of results. Moreover, the evidence for CT-P13 included in this NMA is maintenance-centric, as available data are limited to the maintenance phase in patients who responded to induction therapy. There are also limitations inherent to NMAs, such as a requirement for the data set to meet a number of assumptions, including similarity or exchangeability, homogeneity, and transitivity or consistency [10]. For this NMA, a comprehensive assessment was conducted to identify outcomes for which statistical analysis was feasible, as well as to evaluate potential effect modifiers and the comparability of selected treatments, dosages, and time points across trials. Choice of methodology, including Bayesian versus frequentist approach and fixed-effect versus random-effects modeling, is also an important consideration and requires that the model chosen is the best fit for the data [10]. In the present study, a fixed-effect model was chosen over random-effects due to the limited number of studies providing direct evidence for each treatment comparison. As a result, between-study heterogeneity could not be reliably estimated. Choice of Bayesian or frequentist approach may lead to different findings; however, Bayesian methods are considered suitable for health technology assessment [44, 45]. Use of raw data for analysis of the ELEVATE UC trial may be a source of bias in underestimation of efficacy during the maintenance period compared with adjusted trial data; however, the impact of these differences was minimized from the use of relative treatment effects rather than absolute rates. Lastly, although the results of this NMA indicate relative efficacy based on data from RCTs, the effectiveness of therapies in real-world populations may differ owing to the characteristics of patient populations, adherence, and other variables.

Conclusions

Despite the recent proliferation of therapies for moderate to severe UC, the results of this NMA indicate that vedolizumab SC demonstrates efficacy that is superior or not significantly different from other targeted IBD therapies, including established biologics and novel oral therapies, in the overall population and in the anti-TNFα-naïve population. The safety profile of vedolizumab SC is better than or not significantly different from other targeted IBD therapies and is not significantly different from that of vedolizumab IV. These results contribute to the wealth of data on the efficacy and safety of vedolizumab and will help to inform treatment choices for patients with UC.
