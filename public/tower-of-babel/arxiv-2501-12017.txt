Abstract
. The article aims to describe all covers of every finitely generated variety of cBCK-algebras. It is known that subdirectly irreducible cBCK-algebras are rooted trees (with respect to their order). Moreover, all subdirectly irreducible members of a finitely generated variety are isomorphic to subalgebras of the subdirectly irreducible generators of that variety. The first part of the article focuses on subalgebras of finite subdirectly irreducible cBCK-algebras. The second and main part presents a construction that provides all the covers of any finitely generated variety, which constitutes the central contribution of the article.



1.
Introduction

BCK-algebras were first introduced in
[
8
]
as an algebraic semantics for a non-classical logic based solely on implication. Every BCK-algebra admits a natural ordering, and if it satisfies commutativity (which in a BCK-algebra is not the same as standard commutativity of a binary operation), then the underlying poset forms a meet semilattice. For brevity, we will refer to BCK-algebras satisfying this property as cBCK-algebras. In the literature, this commutativity is sometimes called Tanaka’s identity.

Unlike general BCK-algebras, cBCK-algebras form a variety. While our main focus is on cBCK-algebras, it is worth noting that this variety is properly contained within the larger variety of JBCK-algebras, alongside other notable varieties such as the implicative subreducts of hoops or Hilbert algebras. In this context, the “J” in JBCK refers to an identity that generalises Tanaka’s identity; see
[
2
]
for details. For further information on the subvarieties of JBCK-algebras, see
[
1
]
.

The class of commutative BCK-algebras is also closely related to MV-algebras. Every bounded cBCK-algebra can be turned into an MV-algebra, and conversely, by taking the implicative subreducts of an MV-algebra, one obtains a cBCK-algebra. This construction yields a more special class of BCK-algebras, known as Łukasiewicz BCK-algebras (ŁBCK-algebras). ŁBCK-algebras form a subvariety of cBCK-algebras that additionally satisfies prelinearity, and they are equivalently referred to as cBCK-algebras with the relative cancellation property; this terminology is used, for instance, in
[
7
]
. A notable fact is that every linearly ordered cBCK-algebra is automatically a ŁBCK-algebra. For more details on MV-algebras, the reader may consult
[
4
]
.

Focussing on cBCK-algebras, we first recall some of their fundamental structural properties. The variety of all cBCK-algebras is congruence distributive and 3-permutable, while no non-trivial subvariety is 2-permutable. Finitely generated varieties of cBCK-algebras are semisimple, i.e., any subdirectly irreducible member is simple. Moreover, every finite simple cBCK-algebra is hereditarily simple. Finally, an important structural fact is that subdirectly irreducible cBCK-algebras can be viewed, with respect to their order, as rooted trees
[
12
,
6
]
.

Keeping these structural properties in mind, we now proceed to outline the method used to describe the covers of finitely generated varieties of cBCK-algebras.
Let
\mathcal{V}
be a finitely generated variety of cBCK-algebras. Then, there exists an irredundant set of finite subdirectly irreducible cBCK-algebras
\mathbf{A}_{1}
, …
\mathbf{A}_{n}
such that
\mathcal{V}=\mathrm{V}\!\left({\mathbf{A}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{A}_{n}}\right)
. A crucial observation is that the subdirectly irreducible members of
\mathcal{V}
,
\mathrm{Si}(\mathcal{V})
, consist (up to isomorphism) precisely of the subalgebras of
\mathbf{A}_{1}
, …,
\mathbf{A}_{n}
. Thus,

\mathrm{Si}(\mathcal{V})=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{IS}(\mathbf{A}_{i}).

This follows from Jónsson’s Lemma together with the fact that
\mathbf{A}
is hereditarily simple.

Because subdirectly irreducible members of
\mathcal{V}
are essentially subalgebras of generators, we first examine subalgebras of finite subdirectly irreducible cBCK-algebras. Given a finite subdirectly irreducible cBCK-algebra
\mathbf{A}
, subalgebras of
\mathbf{A}
can be divided into two types: downsets and others. The latter can be characterised as the set of elements of
\mathbf{A}
whose heights are divisible by some
k>1
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Under suitable conditions, such a set indeed forms a subalgebra. A detailed characterisation of these subalgebras constitutes the first part of this article.

We then turn to the central part of the article, which focusses on identifying subdirectly irreducible cBCK-algebras that generate a cover of
\mathrm{V}\!\left({\mathbf{A}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{A}_{n}}\right)
.
The construction formalises a straightforward idea: Given a subdirectly irreducible cBCK-algebra, a tree, one can generate a minimal algebra above it by extending the tree.
This begins by adding a new leaf to an existing leaf, providing a simple visual guide to the process.

2.
Basic definitions

A
BCK-algebra
is an algebra
\mathbf{A}=(A,\ominus,0)
of type
(2,0)
such that the following identities and quasi-identity hold:

(2.1)

\displaystyle((x\ominus y)\ominus(x\ominus z))\ominus(z\ominus y)=0\text{,}

(2.2)

\displaystyle x\ominus 0=x\text{,}

(2.3)

\displaystyle 0\ominus x=0\text{,}

(2.4)

\displaystyle x\ominus y=0\text{ and }y\ominus x=0\text{ imply }x=y\text{.}

In every BCK-algebra, we define an order by

x\leq y\text{ if and only if }x\ominus y=0\text{.}

Clearly, the constant
0
is the bottom element. Further, we write
x\prec y
to indicate that
y
covers
x
.

A
commutative BCK-algebra
(
cBCK-algebra
in short) is a BCK-algebra that additionally satisfies the identity

(2.5)

\displaystyle x\ominus(x\ominus y)=y\ominus(y\ominus x)\text{.}

The class of all cBCK-algebras forms a variety and can be axiomatised by the identities
2.2
,
2.3
,
2.5
and the identity

(2.6)

\displaystyle(x\ominus y)\ominus z

\displaystyle=(x\ominus z)\ominus y\text{.}

The identity
2.5
makes the order a semilattice, where meet is

x\wedge y=x\ominus(x\ominus y)\text{.}

It is a straightforward fact, but it may be useful to keep in mind during computations that
x\ominus y=x\ominus(x\wedge y)
.

In any BCK-algebra, we define

\displaystyle x\ominus 1y=x\ominus y\text{,}\quad x\ominus(n+1)y=(x\ominus ny)\ominus y\text{, }\;n\in\mathbb{N}\text{.}

For technical purposes, we also define
x\ominus 0y=x
.

A subclass of particular interest is the class of so-called ŁBCK-algebras.
They form a variety axiomatised, relative to cBCK-algebras, by the identity

\displaystyle(x\ominus y)\ominus(y\ominus x)=x\ominus y,

or equivalently,

(x\ominus y)\wedge(y\ominus x)=0.

This identity is a form of prelinearity and ensures that subdirectly irreducible members are linearly ordered. Moreover, every cBCK-chain is necessarily a ŁBCK-chain.

For the purposes of this article, the most important examples of ŁBCK-chains are the finite subalgebras of the ŁBCK-chain
(\mathbb{Z}^{+},\ominus,0)
.
Note that
(\mathbb{Z}^{+},\ominus,0)
itself is a subalgebra of
(\mathbb{R}^{+},\ominus,0)
,
where the operation is defined by

x\ominus y=\max\{x-y,0\}.

The finite subalgebras of
\mathbb{Z}^{+}
that are intervals
\mathbf{[0,n]}
with
n\in\mathbb{N}
will be denoted by
\mathbf{S}_{n}
, that is,
S_{n}=\{0,1,\dots,n\}
.

The variety of ŁBCK-algebras is closely related to MV-algebras.
More precisely, every ŁBCK-algebra is a subreduct of an MV-algebra, and conversely any bounded ŁBCK-algebra can be expanded to an MV-algebra; this holds in particular for finite cBCK-chains.
Although introducing MV-algebras is not essential for the present paper, mentioning them provides useful context.
Readers seeking further details, especially on subdirectly irreducible members of this class, may consult
[
7
]
and
[
4
]
.

3.
Subdirectly irreducible algebras and their subalgebras

3.1.
Finite trees are cBCK-algebras

In what follows, by a tree, we mean a lower semilattice
(A,\wedge,0)
with a bottom element
0
, in which any interval
[0,a]
is a chain. Thus, two elements in the tree have an upper bound if and only if they are comparable.

As mentioned earlier, there is a strong connection between subdirectly irreducible cBCK-algebras and trees.
We now state this explicitly.
For more details, see
[
12
, Theorem 3.1]

or its variant in
[
11
, Theorem 5]
.

Theorem 1
.

A cBCK-algebra is subdirectly irreducible if and only if the underlying semilattice is a tree with meet irreducible
0
and such that every maximal chain is subdirectly irreducible cBCK-chain.

Let us remark that if we consider a finite cBCK-algebra, then the condition of
0
being meet irreducible may be replaced by the condition that the tree has a single atom.

While in general the BCK-operation of a subdirectly
irreducible cBCK-algebra is not uniquely determined by its underlying tree semilattice, this does hold in the finite case.
In fact,
[
6
]
establishes an even stronger result: Any tree satisfying the descending chain condition determines a unique way of defining the BCK-operation.
Since we are concerned with finitely generated varieties,
we only need the following corollary of
[
6
, Theorem 1.4]
.

Corollary 2
.

For any finite tree
(A,\wedge,0)
with a single atom, there is, up to isomorphism, a unique subdirectly irreducible cBCK-algebra
\mathbf{A^{\prime}}
whose underlying semillatice is isomorphic to
(A,\wedge,0)
.

Remark 3
.

This corollary allows us to present examples of finite subdirectly irreducible cBCK-algebras simply by giving the Hasse diagram of their underlying trees.

The reader should wonder how the BCK-operation can be defined once the underlying tree is known. Although
[
6
]
provides an answer, we do not reproduce it verbatim. Instead, we start with finite chains, where the definable BCK-operation arises very naturally, and based on this we then show how to define the BCK-operation on finite trees.

Let us recall a few facts about subdirectly irreducible cBCK-chains. Every cBCK-chain is an ŁBCK-algebra.
Hence, a subdirectly irreducible cBCK-chain is necessarily a subdirectly irreducible ŁBCK-chain.
Moreover, every finite ŁBCK-chain is simple,
and every finite simple ŁBCK-algebra is a chain.
Furthermore, any finite ŁBCK-chain is isomorphic to
\mathbf{S}_{n}
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
If
n\neq m
, then
\mathbf{S}_{n}
is not isomorphic to
\mathbf{S}_{m}
.
Thus, for each finite cBCK-chain
\mathbf{A}
, there exists a unique
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\mathbf{A}\cong\mathbf{S}_{n}
. All of these results are well established (see
[
5
,
9
]
); for an extensive overview of the research on ŁBCK-algebras, one may also consult
[
7
]
.

With this in mind, we may view finite subdirectly irreducible
cBCK-algebras simply as trees in which each maximal chain
[0,a]

is (isomorphic to) some
\mathbf{S}_{n}
.
On the other hand, given any finite chain
(C,\wedge,0)
,
there is only one way of defining the BCK-operation,
because having two distinct BCK-operations would result in two cBCK-algebras that (according to the previous paragraph) are isomorphic.

Let
(A,\wedge,0)
be a finite tree, and let
(C_{i},\wedge,0)
,
i\in I
, denote all its maximal chains.
1
1

1

Clearly, the maximal chains cover the tree.
For each
i
, there is a unique way of defining the cBCK-chain
\mathbf{C}_{i}
, and there exists an isomorphism
\iota_{i}\colon\mathbf{C}_{i}\to\mathbf{S}_{n_{i}}
. To define the BCK-operation on the entire
(A,\wedge,0)
, we proceed as follows. For any
a,b\in A
, there is an
i
such that
a\in C_{i}
, and we define

a\ominus b:=\iota^{-1}_{i}(\iota_{i}(a)\ominus\iota_{i}(a\wedge b))\text{.}

In summary, the guiding idea is quite natural:
the BCK-operation in a finite tree behaves like truncated subtraction on
\mathbb{Z}^{+}
. The only adjustment is that, when subtracting incomparable elements, we first project the second element (via the meet) onto the chain determined by the first element.

3.2.
Subalgebras of finite subdirectly irreducible cBCK-algebras

Recall that any finite subdirectly irreducible cBCK-algebra is hereditarily simple. Thus, by studying its subalgebras, we are in fact examining the subdirectly irreducible members of the variety generated by that cBCK-algebra.

Before proceeding, we introduce and clarify some notions.
Let
\mathbf{A}
be a subdirectly irreducible cBCK-algebra. Then:

•

We use
\prec
to denote the covering relation, while
<
denotes the strict order.

•

\mathrm{b}(\mathbf{A})
denotes a set of all branching elements of
\mathbf{A}
. It holds that
b\in\mathrm{b}(\mathbf{A})
iff there exist
c
,
d\in A
such that
c
,
d>b
and
c\wedge d=b
.

•

For
a\in A
,
\mathrm{h}_{A}(a)=|[0,a]|-1
denotes the height of the element
a
. If there is no danger of confusion, we write
\mathrm{h}(a)
instead of
\mathrm{h}_{A}(a)
. Furthermore,
\mathrm{h}(\mathbf{A})=\sup\{\mathrm{h}(a)\mid a\in A\}
denotes the height of
\mathbf{A}
.

•

a\parallel b
denotes that
a
is incomparable to
b
, and
a\not\parallel b
indicates that
a
is comparable to
b
.

•

By the width of
\mathbf{A}
we mean the cardinality of a maximal antichain (a maximal set of mutually incomparable elements) of
\mathbf{A}
.

•

\mathrm{m}(\mathbf{A})
denotes a set of all maximal elements of
\mathbf{A}
. For
\mathbf{A}
with finite height,
|\mathrm{m}(\mathbf{A})|
is a width of
\mathbf{A}
.

The following will be used in the proofs without hesitation.

Lemma 4
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a finite subdirectly irreducible cBCK-algebra. Let
a
,
b\in A
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathrm{h}(a\ominus nb)=\max\{\mathrm{h}(a)-n\mathrm{h}(a\wedge b),\,0\}\text{.}

Moreover, if
e
is an atom of
\mathbf{A}
and
a\neq 0
, then
\mathrm{h}(a)
is the least
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
a\ominus ne=0
.

Proof.

The first part is borrowed from
[
6
, Lemma 2.1]
. The moreover part is obtained as follows. Let
e\in A
be an atom of
\mathbf{A}
. For any
a\in A
,
a\neq 0
, the sequence
(a\ominus ne)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is decreasing and eventually reaches
0
. Therefore, there must exist the least
n\in\mathbb{N}
for which
a\ominus ne=0
. Let
n
denote this natural number. If
\mathrm{h}(a)>n
, then
\mathrm{h}(a)-n=\mathrm{h}(a)-n\mathrm{h}(e)>0
. By the first part of the lemma, it follows that
\mathrm{h}(a\ominus ne)>0
, which is impossible. Therefore, we must have
\mathrm{h}(a)\leq n
.

On the other hand, if
h(a)<n
, then
\mathrm{h}(a)-n=\mathrm{h}(a)-n\mathrm{h}(e)<0
, so that
\mathrm{h}(a)-(n-1)\mathrm{h}(e)\leq 0
, whence
\mathrm{h}(a\ominus(n-1)e)=0
, and finally
a\ominus(n-1)e=0
, since
0
is the only element of height
0
. This contradicts
n
being the least and hence
\mathrm{h}(a)=n
.
∎

Although the following fact is evident, we state it explicitly, as it will be used repeatedly in the article.

Lemma 5
.

In the finite cBCK-chain, every element is uniquely determined by its height.

The first simple fact we can state about finite subdirectly irreducible cBCK-algebras concerns their generators.
In fact, to generate the algebra, it is necessary to take the maximal elements since the BCK-operation is decreasing.
Although this is enough in some cases, in general, to ensure that the entire algebra is generated, we also need to include an atom.

Lemma 6
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a finite subdirectly irreducible cBCK-algebra. Then
\mathbf{A}
is generated by
\mathrm{m}(\mathbf{A})\cup\{a\}
, where
a
is an atom of
\mathbf{A}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a finite chain. Let
a
be an atom of
\mathbf{A}
and
c
the maximal element of
\mathbf{A}
, then for any
b\in A

\mathrm{h}(b)=\mathrm{h}(c)-\big(\mathrm{h}(c)-\mathrm{h}(b)\big)\mathrm{h}(a).

By the Lemma
4
, we have

\mathrm{h}(b)=\mathrm{h}(c\ominus\big(\mathrm{h}(c)-\mathrm{h}(b)\big)a)
. Since each element is uniquely determined by its height it follows that
b=c\ominus\big(\mathrm{h}(c)-\mathrm{h}(b)\big)a
.

Now, let
\mathbf{A}
be a finite subdirectly irreducible cBCK-algebra. Let
a
be an atom. For any
b\in\mathbf{A}
, there exists
c\in\mathrm{m}(\mathbf{A})
such that
b\in[0,c]
. From the first paragraph, it follows that
b=c\ominus ka
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
.
∎

The fact that
\mathrm{m}(\mathbf{A})
may not suffice to generate the entire
\mathbf{A}
is closely related to the description of its subalgebras. If
\mathrm{m}(\mathbf{A})
is not enough – that is, if the generating process does not reach an atom of
\mathbf{A}
– then the subalgebra generated by
\mathrm{m}(\mathbf{A})
is not a downset of
\mathbf{A}
. This suggests that besides the downset subalgebras, there exist other kind of subalgebras as well. We now provide a characterisation of these subalgebras.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a finite subdirectly irreducible cBCK-algebra. Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Denote

A_{k}=\{a\in A\mid k\text{ divides }\mathrm{h}(a)\}\text{.}

If
\mathbf{A}
is a chain, then
A_{k}
forms the universe of a subalgebra of
\mathbf{A}
for any
A_{k}
.
However, if
\mathbf{A}
is not a chain,
A_{k}
does not need to be the universe of a subalgebra.
As we shall see, this depends on whether
\mathrm{b}(\mathbf{A})\subseteq A_{k}
.
In fact, we will rather consider
A_{k}\cup\mathrm{m}(\mathbf{A})
instead of
A_{k}
alone,
which allows for slightly better handling later on.

Remark 7
.

If
\mathbf{A}
has height
n
and

, then

is, by definition, just

. On the other hand, if

, then

contains at least two elements. Furthermore, if

.

Lemma 8
.

Let
\mathbf{C}
be a finite cBCK-chain. Let
a
,
b\in C
be such that
\gcd(\mathrm{h}(a),\mathrm{h}(b))=d
. Then there are a BCK-term

and a unique element

such that
t(a,b)=e
and
\mathrm{h}(e)=d
.

Proof.

For
a=0
or
b=0
, the lemma obviously holds. Therefore, assume that
a\neq 0
and
b\neq 0
.

Since in a finite cBCK-chain
\mathbf{C}
each element is uniquely determined by its height, and every
n\in\mathbb{N}
with

is the height of some element, it follows that there exists a unique element
e\in C
such that
\mathrm{h}(e)=d
.

Without loss of generality, assume
\mathrm{h}(a)>\mathrm{h}(b)
. To construct a term
t(x,y)
, consider the Euclidean algorithm applied to
\mathrm{h}(a)
and
\mathrm{h}(b)
. The algorithm corresponds to the following equalities:

\displaystyle\mathrm{h}(a)-q_{0}\mathrm{h}(b)

\displaystyle=r_{0},

\displaystyle\mathrm{h}(b)-q_{1}r_{0}

\displaystyle=r_{1},

\displaystyle r_{n-2}-q_{n}r_{n-1}

\displaystyle=r_{n}=d,

where
q_{0},\dots,q_{n}
and

are all positive integers.

Since

r_{0}=\mathrm{h}(a)-q_{0}\mathrm{h}(b)=\mathrm{h}(a\ominus q_{0}b).

Abbreviate

. Then, the second line of the algorithm gives

r_{1}=\mathrm{h}(b)-q_{1}\mathrm{h}(c_{0}(a,b))=\mathrm{h}(b\ominus q_{1}c_{0}(a,b)),

and we denote

.

Clearly, the same applies to every subsequent line. In the final line, we obtain

d=\mathrm{h}(e)=\mathrm{h}(c_{n-2}(a,b)\ominus q_{n}c_{n-1}(a,b)).

Thus, we can define

t(x,y)=c_{n-2}(x,y)\ominus q_{n}c_{n-1}(x,y).

As explained earlier,

\mathbf{A}

Now, consider the case of

. Then,
a\ominus b=a\ominus(a\wedge b)
, where
a\wedge b\in\mathrm{b}(\mathbf{A})\subset A_{k}
. Here,
a
and
a\wedge b
are comparable and both belong to
A_{k}
. Using the same argument as above,
a\ominus b\in A_{k}
. Consequently,
A_{k}
is closed under
\ominus
.
∎

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a finite subdirectly irreducible cBCK-algebra.
We denote by
\mathrm{S}_{\delta}(\mathbf{A})
the set of subalgebras of the form
A_{k}\cup\mathrm{m}(\mathbf{A})
, where
k\neq 1
.
Furthermore, let
\mathrm{S}_{d}(\mathbf{A})
denote the subalgebras of
\mathbf{A}
that are downsets.

Proposition 10
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a finite subdirectly irreducible cBCK-algebra. Let
\mathbf{B}\in\mathrm{S}(\mathbf{A})
. If there exists
a
,
b\in\mathbf{B}
such that
\gcd(\mathrm{h}_{A}(a),\mathrm{h}_{A}(b))=1
, then
\mathbf{B}\in\mathrm{S}_{d}(\mathbf{A})
.

Proof.

Let
\gcd(\mathrm{h}_{A}(a),\mathrm{h}_{A}(b))=1
. Assume that
a>b
or
a<b
. Then, by Lemma
8
, there exists a term
t(x,y)
such that
t(a,b)
is an atom of
A
.

If, on the other hand,
a\parallel b
, then
a\wedge b<a,b
. Now, if
\gcd(\mathrm{h}(a\wedge b),\mathrm{h}(a))=1
or
\gcd(\mathrm{h}(a\wedge b),\mathrm{h}(b))=1
, we may use the same argument as before to obtain an atom. Thus, assume the opposite, i.e.,
\gcd(\mathrm{h}(a\wedge b),\mathrm{h}(a))=d_{1}
and
\gcd(\mathrm{h}(a\wedge b),\mathrm{h}(b))=d_{2}
, where
d_{1}\neq 1
and
d_{2}\neq 1
. It follows that
\gcd(d_{1},d_{2})=1
, for otherwise
\gcd(\mathrm{h}(a),\mathrm{h}(b))\neq 1
, which contradicts the premise.

Since
a
,
b
,
a\wedge b\in B
, Lemma
8
provides elements
e,f\in B
with
\mathrm{h}(e)=d_{1}
and
\mathrm{h}(f)=d_{2}
.
Moreover, because
e,f\leq a\wedge b
, it holds
e\not\parallel f
.
Applying Lemma
8
once again, we obtain a term
t(x,y)
such that
t(e,f)
is an atom of
A
.

So, in any case,
\gcd(\mathrm{h}_{A}(a),\mathrm{h}_{A}(b))=1
implies that an atom of
\mathbf{A}
is also in
\mathbf{B}
. Let
e
be an atom of
\mathbf{A}
. Given
c\in B
and
d\in A
such that
d\leq c
, we have
d=c\ominus ke
for some
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
. Consequently,
\mathbf{B}
is a downset of
\mathbf{A}
.
∎

Theorem 11
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a finite subdirectly irreducible cBCK-algebra. Then, (up to isomorphism) the following holds

\mathrm{S}(\mathbf{A})=\mathrm{S}_{d}(\mathbf{A})\cup\mathrm{S}_{\delta}(\mathrm{S}_{d}(\mathbf{A}))\text{.}

Moreover, if
\mathrm{S}_{\delta}(\mathrm{S}_{d}(\mathbf{A}))
consists only of chains, then
\mathrm{IS}(\mathbf{A})=\mathrm{IS}_{d}(\mathbf{A})
.

Proof.

The inclusion

S(\mathbf{A})\supseteq\mathrm{S}_{d}(\mathbf{A})\cup\mathrm{S}_{\delta}(\mathrm{S}_{d}(\mathbf{A}))

is evident. Let us prove the opposite inclusion. Let
\mathbf{B}\in\mathrm{S}(\mathbf{A})
. We will show the implication

\mathbf{B}\notin\mathrm{S}_{\delta}(\mathrm{S}_{d}(\mathbf{A}))\Rightarrow\mathbf{B}\in\mathrm{S}_{d}(\mathbf{A}).

This is sufficient, since it is then impossible for
\mathbf{B}
to lie outside both
\mathrm{S}_{\delta}(\mathrm{S}_{d}(\mathbf{A}))
and
\mathrm{S}_{d}(\mathbf{A})
.

Assume
\mathbf{B}\notin\mathrm{S}_{\delta}(\mathrm{S}_{d}(\mathbf{A}))
.
If
\gcd(\mathrm{h}_{A}(a),\mathrm{h}_{A}(b))=1
for some
a,b\in B
, then, by Proposition
10
,
\mathbf{B}\in\mathrm{S}_{d}(\mathbf{A})
. In this case, it also follows that
\gcd(\mathrm{h}_{A}(b)\mid b\in B)=1
. Otherwise, we must have
\gcd(\mathrm{h}_{A}(b)\mid b\in B)=k>1
, which implies
B=D_{k}
for some
\mathbf{D}\in\mathrm{S}_{d}(\mathbf{A})
. This is impossible since we assumed
\mathbf{B}\notin\mathrm{S}_{\delta}(\mathrm{S}_{d}(\mathbf{A}))
.

To prove the moreover part, let
\mathrm{h}(\mathbf{A})=n
and assume that
\mathrm{S}_{\delta}(\mathrm{S}_{d}(\mathbf{A}))
contains only chains. For any
\mathbf{C}\in\mathrm{S}_{\delta}(\mathrm{S}_{d}(\mathbf{A}))
, we have
\mathrm{h}(\mathbf{C})=k\leq n
. Since for every
i\in\{0,\dots,n\}
there exists
a\in A
with
\mathrm{h}(a)=i
, there exists
a\in A
such that
\mathrm{h}(a)=k
. Then
\mathbf{[0,a]_{A}}\in\mathrm{S}_{d}(\mathbf{A})
is a finite chain of height
k
, as is
\mathbf{C}
, so
\mathbf{[0,a]_{A}}\cong\mathbf{C}
.
∎

.

The opposite implication of the moreover part is not true, i.e. there are algebras

such that
\mathrm{IS}(\mathbf{A})=\mathrm{IS}_{d}(\mathbf{A})
and

Using

Proposition 13
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a finite subdirectly irreducible cBCK-algebra. Then, the smallest set generating
\mathbf{A}
is
\mathrm{m}(\mathbf{A})
if and only if
\mathrm{S}_{\delta}(\mathbf{A})=\emptyset
.

Proof.

(
\Rightarrow
) Suppose that
\mathrm{m}(\mathbf{A})
generates
\mathbf{A}
.
Then there exists a BCK-term
t(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
such that
t(m_{1},\dots,m_{n})=a
, where
m_{1},\dots,m_{n}\in\mathrm{m}(\mathbf{A})
and

is an atom of
\mathbf{A}
.
Now, let
\mathbf{B}\in S_{\delta}(\mathbf{A})
. By the definition of
\mathrm{S}_{\delta}(\mathbf{A})
, this means that
B=A_{k}\cup\mathrm{m}(\mathbf{A})
for some

. Since
\mathrm{m}(\mathbf{A})\subseteq B

.
However,
\mathbf{B}
cannot contain

.

(
\Leftarrow

. Then

.
By Theorem

, we have
\mathbf{B}\in\mathrm{S}_{d}(\mathbf{A})
or
\mathbf{B}\in\mathrm{S}_{\delta}(\mathrm{S}_{d}(\mathbf{A}))
.
Since
\mathrm{m}(\mathbf{A})\subseteq\mathbf{B}
, if

, it would follow that

Hence,
\mathbf{B}\in\mathrm{S}_{d}(\mathbf{A})
.
Clearly,
\mathbf{B}

contains an atom of

. Since
\mathbf{B}
contains an atom of
\mathbf{A}
and

.

It is also evident that no element of

is the smallest set generating

∎

4.
Covers of finitely generated varieties

Let us first clarify the following fact: for any subdirectly irreducible cBCK-algebra
\mathbf{A}
of infinite height, the variety
\mathrm{V}\!\left({\mathbf{A}}\right)
contains all finite chains
\mathbf{S}_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Indeed,
\mathbf{A}
contains an infinite ŁBCK-chain as a subalgebra, and any infinite ŁBCK-chain generates the entire variety of ŁBCK-algebras. Hence, all finite cBCK-chains are necessarily contained in
\mathrm{V}\!\left({\mathbf{A}}\right)
.

Proposition 14
.

Any cover of a finitely generated variety of cBCK-algebras is finitely generated.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{V}
be a finitely generated variety of cBCK-algebras, and let
\mathcal{K}
be a variety that is not finitely generated.
We prove the contraposition, i.e., that
\mathcal{K}
cannot be a cover of
\mathcal{V}
.

Since
\mathcal{V}
is finitely generated, there exists some
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\mathrm{h}(\mathbf{A})\leq n
for every
\mathbf{A}\in\mathrm{Si}(\mathcal{V})
.
If
\mathcal{K}
contains a subdirectly irreducible algebra of infinite height, then it also contains
\mathbf{S}_{n+1}
.
It follows that
\mathcal{V}\subsetneq\mathcal{V}\vee\mathrm{V}\!\left({\mathbf{S}_{n+1}}\right)\subseteq\mathcal{K}
, which contradicts the assumption that
\mathcal{K}
is a cover of
\mathcal{V}
.
Therefore,
\mathcal{K}
cannot contain a subdirectly irreducible algebra of infinite height.

Clearly, if
\mathcal{K}
has infinitely many irredundant generators, then it cannot be a cover, since one can select a subset of these generators to form a variety that is strictly smaller than
\mathcal{K}
but strictly larger than
\mathrm{V}\!\left({\mathbf{A}}\right)
.

It remains to investigate the case where
\mathcal{K}
contains an infinite subdirectly irreducible cBCK-algebra of finite height.
Such an algebra must have infinitely many incomparable elements; that is, its width is infinite.
Let us denote this algebra by
\mathbf{B}
.
Similarly to the previous paragraph, there exists
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
|m(\mathbf{A})|\leq k
for every
\mathbf{A}\in\mathrm{Si}(\mathcal{V})
.

Now, choose
k+1
incomparable elements in
\mathbf{B}
to generate a subalgebra
\mathbf{C}
.
The algebra
\mathbf{C}
is finite, yet
\mathbf{C}\notin\mathcal{V}
.
Hence,
\mathcal{V}\subsetneq\mathcal{V}\vee\mathrm{V}\!\left({\mathbf{C}}\right)\subsetneq\mathcal{K}
.
∎

The previous result can be understood through the structural properties of subdirectly irreducible cBCK-algebras.
Alternatively, it can also be seen from the existence of certain identities that bound the height and width of the subdirectly irreducible algebras.
We present these identities here for the reader’s insight, even though they are not strictly necessary for the argument.

The following is a restated version of
[
12
, Theorem 2.1]
and
[
10
, Proposition 3]
.

Theorem 15
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a subdirectly irreducible cBCK-algebra. Then,
\mathbf{A}
satisfies

x\ominus(n+1)y=x\ominus ny\text{,}

iff
\mathrm{h}(\mathbf{A})\leq n
. Further,
\mathbf{A}
satisfies

\bigwedge_{1\leq i\neq j\leq n}(x_{i}\ominus x_{j})=0\text{,}

iff width of
\mathbf{A}
is
\leq n
. If
\mathbf{A}
has a finite height, then it is equivalent to
|\mathrm{m}(\mathbf{A})|\leq n
.

We have seen that the cover cannot contain a subdirectly irreducible algebra of infinite height or infinite width.
Of course, these restrictions can be made tighter.

Proposition 16
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a finite subdirectly irreducible cBCK-algebra, let
\mathcal{K}
be a cover of
\mathrm{V}\!\left({\mathbf{A}}\right)
and let
\mathbf{B}\in\mathrm{Si}(\mathcal{K})
. Then

(i)

|\mathrm{h}(\mathbf{B})|\leq\mathrm{h}(\mathbf{A})+1
,

(ii)

|\mathrm{m}(\mathbf{B})|\leq|\mathrm{m}(\mathbf{A})|+1
.

Proof.

The arguments proceed similarly to the proof of Proposition
14
.
Let
\mathbf{A}
be a non-trivial finite subdirectly irreducible cBCK-algebra.
Furthermore, let
\mathcal{K}
be a variety, and let
\mathbf{B}\in\mathrm{Si}(\mathcal{K})
.

(i) Suppose that
|\mathrm{h}(\mathbf{B})|>\mathrm{h}(\mathbf{A})+1
.
Then
\mathbf{B}
contains a chain
\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{h}(\mathbf{A})+2}
of height
\mathrm{h}(\mathbf{A})+2
.
Since
\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{h}(\mathbf{A})+1}\notin\mathrm{V}\!\left({\mathbf{A}}\right)
and
\mathrm{V}\!\left({\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{h}(\mathbf{A})+1}}\right)\subsetneq\mathrm{V}\!\left({\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{h}(\mathbf{A})+2}}\right)
, it follows that
\mathrm{V}\!\left({\mathbf{A}}\right)\subsetneq\mathrm{V}\!\left({\mathbf{A}}\right)\vee\mathrm{V}\!\left({\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{h}(\mathbf{A})+1}}\right)\subsetneq\mathcal{K}
. Hence,
\mathcal{K}
is not a cover.

(ii) If
\mathbf{B}
does not have a finite height, then by (i) we know that
\mathcal{K}
cannot be a cover, and the argument is complete.
Thus, assume that
\mathbf{B}
has a finite height.
Then its width is exactly the number of elements in
\mathrm{m}(\mathbf{B})
.
Suppose that
|\mathrm{m}(\mathbf{B})|>|\mathrm{m}(\mathbf{A})|+1
.
It follows that
\mathbf{B}
contains a subalgebra, say
\mathbf{C}
, such that
|\mathrm{m}(\mathbf{C})|=|\mathrm{m}(\mathbf{A})|+1
; for instance, we may choose a suitable subset of
\mathrm{m}(\mathbf{B})
to generate
\mathbf{C}
.
Once again,
\mathrm{V}\!\left({\mathbf{A}}\right)
cannot contain
\mathbf{C}
, and moreover
\mathrm{V}\!\left({\mathbf{C}}\right)\subsetneq\mathcal{K}
(since
\mathbf{C}
has a strictly greater width).
Therefore,
\mathrm{V}\!\left({\mathbf{A}}\right)\subsetneq\mathrm{V}\!\left({\mathbf{A}}\right)\vee\mathrm{V}\!\left({\mathbf{C}}\right)\subsetneq\mathcal{K}
.
∎

Definition 17
.

Let
\mathcal{V}
be a variety of cBCK-algebras. We say that
\mathcal{V}
is
n
-generated if and only if there is
n
-element set of mutually non-isomorphic subdirectly irreducible algebras of
\mathcal{V}
that generate
\mathcal{V}
, and if there is no other set of subdirectly irreducible algebras with the same property and smaller cardinality.

Remark 18
.

If
\mathcal{V}
is
n
-generated, there can be more than one set of subdirectly irreducible algebras witnessing this fact.
For example, for the variety of all ŁBCK-algebras
\mathcal{L}
we have

\mathcal{L}=\mathrm{V}\!\left({\mathbb{Z}^{+}}\right)=\mathrm{V}\!\left({\mathbf{[0,1]}_{\mathbb{R}}}\right)
.
Thus,
\mathcal{L}
is
1
-generated, yet there exist at least two different generating subdirectly irreducible algebras.

However, for finitely generated varieties of cBCK-algebras, this cannot happen. If
\mathcal{V}
is a finitely
n
-generated variety, then there exists a unique (up to isomorphism)
n
-element set of mutually non-isomorphic subdirectly irreducible algebras generating
\mathcal{V}
.
Indeed,

\mathrm{Si}(\mathcal{V})=\mathrm{Si}\bigl(\mathrm{V}\!\left({\mathbf{A}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{A}_{n}}\right)\bigr)=\mathrm{Si}(\mathrm{V}\!\left({\mathbf{A}_{1}}\right))\cup\dots\cup\mathrm{Si}(\mathrm{V}\!\left({\mathbf{A}_{n}}\right)),

and this set consists only of (isomorphic copies of) subalgebras of

\mathbf{A}_{1},\mathbf{A}_{2},\dots,\mathbf{A}_{n}
.

Lemma 19
.

Let
\mathcal{V}
be a finitely
n
-generated variety of cBCK-algebras and let
\mathcal{W}
be its covering. Then
\mathcal{W}
is at most
(n+1)
-generated.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{V}
be
n
-generated, i.e.,
\mathcal{V}=\mathrm{V}\!\left({\mathbf{A}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{A}_{n}}\right)
, where
\mathrm{IS}(\mathbf{A}_{i})\not\subseteq\mathrm{IS}(\mathbf{A}_{j})
for any two distinct
i,j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. Similarly, let
\mathcal{W}
be at least
m
-generated, with
m\geq n+2
. Thus,
\mathcal{W}=\mathrm{V}\!\left({\mathbf{B}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{B}_{m}}\right)
, where
\mathrm{IS}(\mathbf{B}_{i})\not\subseteq\mathrm{IS}(\mathbf{B}_{j})
for any two distinct
i,j\in\{1,\dots,m\}
. Furthermore, assume that
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{W}
. We will show that
\mathcal{W}
cannot be a cover.

It follows that

\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{IS}(\mathbf{A}_{i})\subseteq\bigcup_{j=1}^{m}\mathrm{IS}(\mathbf{B}_{j})\text{.}

For each
\mathbf{A}_{i}
, there exists at least one
\mathbf{B}_{j}
such that
\mathbf{A}_{i}\in\mathrm{IS}(\mathbf{B}_{j})
. From the set of
\mathbf{B}_{j}
,
j\in\{1,\dots,m\}
, we can choose a subset of elements
\mathbf{B}_{k}
,
k\in K
, with
|K|=n
, such that for every
\mathbf{A}_{i}
there is at least one
\mathbf{B}_{k}
for which
\mathbf{A}_{i}\in\mathrm{IS}(\mathbf{B}_{k})
.
Indeed, for each
\mathbf{A}_{i}
we pick just one
\mathbf{B}_{j}
from the set of all possible
\mathbf{B}_{j}
satisfying this condition. This gives a mapping from an
n
-element set, so the image contains at most
n
elements.

Furthermore, since
m\geq n+2
, there exist
\mathbf{B}_{j_{1}}
and
\mathbf{B}_{j_{2}}
such that
j_{1}\neq j_{2}
and

\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathrm{V}\!\left({\mathbf{B}_{k}\mid k\in K}\right)\subsetneq\mathrm{V}\!\left({\{\mathbf{B}_{k}\mid k\in K\}\cup\{\mathbf{B}_{j_{1}}\}}\right)\subsetneq\mathrm{V}\!\left({\{\mathbf{B}_{k}\mid k\in K\}\cup\{\mathbf{B}_{j_{1}},\mathbf{B}_{j_{2}}\}}\right)\subseteq\mathcal{W}\text{.}

Hence,
\mathcal{W}
is not a cover.
∎

We have bounds on the number of irredundant generators for covers. Next, we describe more precisely the elements that such a set may contain.

Lemma 20
.

Let
\mathcal{V}=\mathrm{V}\!\left({\mathbf{A}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{A}_{n}}\right)

\mathcal{K}=\mathrm{V}\!\left({\mathbf{B}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{B}_{m}}\right)

Let
\mathbf{B}_{i_{1}}

\mathbf{B}_{i_{2}}\in C

\mathrm{Prop}(\mathbf{B}_{i})
denote a set containing all
\mathbf{A}_{j}

\mathrm{Prop}(\mathbf{B}_{i})=\{\mathbf{A}\in\{\mathbf{A}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{A}_{n}\}\mid\mathbf{A}\in\mathrm{IS}(\mathbf{B}_{i}),\,\mathbf{A}\not\cong\mathbf{B}_{i}\}.

If
\mathrm{Prop}(\mathbf{B}_{i_{1}})=\mathrm{Prop}(\mathbf{B}_{i_{2}})=\emptyset

\mathcal{V}\subsetneq\mathrm{V}\!\left({\mathbf{B}_{i}\mid i\in\{1,\dots,n\},\,i\neq i_{1}}\right)\subsetneq\mathcal{K}.

Thus
\mathcal{K}
is not a cover.

Without loss of generality, assume now that
\mathrm{Prop}(\mathbf{B}_{i_{1}})\neq\emptyset

\mathcal{V}\subsetneq\mathrm{V}\!\left({\{\mathbf{B}_{i}\mid i\in\{1,\dots,n\},\,i\neq i_{1}\}\cup\mathrm{Prop}(\mathbf{B}_{1})}\right)\subsetneq\mathcal{K}.

That yields once again that
\mathcal{K}
is not a cover.
∎

In the following statement, the set
\mathrm{Prop}(\mathbf{B})
has the same meaning as in the proof above.

Proposition 21
.

Let
\mathcal{V}=\mathrm{V}\!\left({\mathbf{A}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{A}_{n}}\right)
be a finitely
n
-generated variety, and let
\mathcal{K}
be a cover of
\mathcal{V}
.
Then there exists
\mathbf{B}\in\mathrm{Si}(\mathcal{K})
such that

\bigl(\{\mathbf{A}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{A}_{n}\}\setminus\mathrm{Prop}(\mathbf{B})\bigr)\cup\{\mathbf{B}\}

is an irredundant set of generators for
\mathcal{K}
.
Thus

\mathcal{K}=\mathrm{V}\!\left({\bigl(\{\mathbf{A}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{A}_{n}\}\setminus\mathrm{Prop}(\mathbf{B})\bigr)\cup\{\mathbf{B}\}}\right)

is

-generated, where
m=n+1-\lvert\mathrm{Prop}(\mathbf{B})\rvert
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{K}
be a cover of
\mathcal{V}
.
By Lemma
20
, there is exactly one algebra
\mathbf{B}
in an irredundant generating set of
\mathcal{K}
that is not isomorphic to any of
\mathbf{A}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{A}_{n}
. If
\mathrm{Prop}(\mathbf{B})=\emptyset
, then the set
\{\mathbf{A}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{A}_{n},\mathbf{B}\}
is an irredundant set of generators for
\mathcal{K}
, because no
\mathbf{A}_{i}
lies in
\mathrm{IS}(\mathbf{B})
. If
\mathrm{Prop}(\mathbf{B})\neq\emptyset
, then the generating set for

is

\bigl(\{\mathbf{A}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{A}_{n}\}\setminus\mathrm{Prop}(\mathbf{B})\bigr)\cup\{\mathbf{B}\}
,
which is clearly irredundant.
∎

We now move on to the first step in the construction of the covers. Let
\mathbf{A}
be a finite subdirectly irreducible cBCK-algebra.
For any
\mathbf{B}\in\mathrm{S}(\mathbf{A})
and any

with

\mathbf{B}_{a}
as follows:

(1)

Let
c\notin B
be a new element and

.

(2)

Put
a\prec c
.

As stated in Corollary

2

2

We use the same symbol for the algebra because, within the isomorphism class, we can choose a representative with the identical underlying set and tree structure.

\mathbf{B}_{a}
whose underlying semilattice is this tree.
What we have just constructed is a new subdirectly irreducible algebra that depends on the choice of

and differs from the original by the single additional element

Although it is not required for the subsequent arguments, we can describe explicitly how the operations of the new algebra

extend those of the original algebra.
For completeness, we provide this description in the next paragraph.

Denote
\mathbf{B}=(B,\ominus,0)

\displaystyle b\ominus_{a}c

\displaystyle c\ominus_{a}b

To see that the above is indeed a well-defined cBCK-algebra, let us state the following lemma.

Lemma 22

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a finite subdirectly irreducible cBCK-algebra with atom
a
.
For any
b
,
c\in A
,

b\ominus c=b\ominus\mathrm{h}(b\wedge c)\,a.

Proof.

Both
b\ominus c
and
b\ominus\mathrm{h}(b\wedge c)a
lie in the finite chain
[0,b]
, where each element is uniquely determined by its height.
It therefore suffices to show that these two elements have the same height.
By repeated use of Lemma
4
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{h}\bigl(b\ominus\mathrm{h}(b\wedge c)\,a\bigr)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{h}(b)-\mathrm{h}(b\wedge c)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{h}\bigl(b\ominus(b\wedge c)\bigr)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{h}(b\ominus c).

Since they have equal height, the two elements are equal.
∎

With the aid of the above lemma, verifying that
\mathbf{B}_{a}
satisfies the identities
2.2
,
2.3
,
2.5
, and
2.6
is straightforward.
Consequently,
\mathbf{B}_{a}
is a well-defined cBCK-algebra, and we omit the details of this routine computation.

We now proceed to the second step in the construction of the covers.
Let
\mathcal{V}=\mathrm{V}\!\left({\mathbf{A}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{A}_{n}}\right)
be a finitely
n
-generated variety of cBCK-algebras. For every
\mathbf{A}_{i}
,
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, consider
\mathbf{B}\in\mathrm{S}(\mathbf{A}_{i})
and constructed
\mathbf{B}_{a}
, where
a\in A_{i}
,
a\neq 0
. Furthermore, we denote

\mathrm{S}(\mathbf{B}_{a})(c)=\{\mathbf{D}\in\mathrm{S}(\mathbf{B}_{a})\mid c\in D\}.

We are interested in the following set:

\{\mathbf{C}\in\mathrm{S}(\mathbf{B}_{a})(c)\mid\mathbf{C}\notin\mathrm{IS}(\mathbf{A}_{i})\text{, for any }i\in\{1,\dots,n\}\}.

This set may be empty for some choice of
\mathbf{B}
and
a\in A_{i}
. However, if it is not empty, it can be regarded as a subposet of the lattice of subalgebras
\mathrm{S}(\mathbf{B}_{a})
. This subposet does not necessarily have the smallest element, but it does have some minimal elements. Let us denote them as

C^{i}_{\mathbf{B}_{a}}=\min\{\mathbf{C}\in\mathrm{S}(\mathbf{B}_{a})(c)\mid\mathbf{C}\notin\mathrm{IS}(\mathbf{A}_{i})\text{, for any }i\in\{1,\dots,n\}\}.

In the following, we will prove that these minimal elements are in the generating sets of covers. To ensure that we account for all possibilities and thus obtain all covers, we consider the set

\mathrm{Cov}(\mathcal{V})=\bigcup\{C^{i}_{\mathbf{B}_{a}}\mid i\in\{1,\dots,n\},\,\mathbf{B}\in\mathrm{S}(\mathbf{A}_{i}),\,a\in A_{i}\}.

Proposition 23
.

Let
\mathcal{V}=\mathrm{V}\!\left({\mathbf{A}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{A}_{n}}\right)
be a finitely
n
-generated variety of cBCK-algebras.
For any
\mathbf{C}\in\mathrm{Cov}(\mathcal{V})
, the variety
\mathcal{V}\vee\mathrm{V}\!\left({\mathbf{C}}\right)
is a cover of
\mathcal{V}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{V}=\mathrm{V}\!\left({\mathbf{A}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{A}_{n}}\right)
be a finitely
n
-generated variety of cBCK-algebras.
Let
\mathcal{K}
be a cover of
\mathcal{V}
such that
\mathcal{V}\subsetneq\mathcal{K}\subseteq\mathcal{V}\vee\mathrm{V}\!\left({\mathbf{C}}\right)
.
It follows that

\displaystyle\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{IS}(\mathbf{A}_{i})\subsetneq\mathrm{Si}(\mathcal{K})\subseteq\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{IS}(\mathbf{A}_{i})\cup\mathrm{IS}(\mathbf{C}).

From this we conclude that for any
\mathbf{K}\in\mathrm{Si}(\mathcal{K})
, either
\mathbf{K}\in\mathrm{IS}(\mathbf{A}_{i})
for some
i
, or
\mathbf{K}\notin\mathrm{IS}(\mathbf{A}_{i})
for all
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
.

Suppose that
\mathbf{K}\notin\mathrm{IS}(\mathbf{A}_{i})
for any
i
.
Then
\mathbf{K}\in\mathrm{IS}(\mathbf{C})
. Without loss of generality, assume that
\mathbf{K}\in\mathrm{S}(\mathbf{C})
.
Since
\mathbf{C}\in\mathrm{Cov}(\mathcal{V})
, we have
\mathbf{C}\in\mathrm{S}(\mathbf{B}_{a})(c)
and
\mathbf{C}\notin\mathrm{IS}(\mathbf{A}_{i})
for any
i
.
There are two possibilities: Either
c\in\mathbf{K}
, or
c\notin\mathbf{K}
.

If
c\in\mathbf{K}
, then necessarily
\mathbf{K}=\mathbf{C}
, because both
\mathbf{K}
and
\mathbf{C}
satisfy the condition of not being in any
\mathrm{IS}(\mathbf{A}_{i})
, and
\mathbf{C}
is defined as minimal with this property.

If
c\notin\mathbf{K}
, then from the construction of
\mathbf{C}
we have
\mathbf{C}=C^{\prime}\cup\{c\}
.
Moreover,
C^{\prime}
is the universe of a subalgebra of
\mathbf{C}
, and
\mathbf{K}\subseteq\mathbf{C}^{\prime}
.
But
\mathbf{C}^{\prime}\in\mathrm{IS}(\mathbf{A}_{i})
for some
i
, so
\mathbf{K}\in\mathrm{IS}(\mathbf{A}_{i})
as well, which contradicts the assumption that
\mathbf{K}\notin\mathrm{IS}(\mathbf{A}_{i})
for any
i
.

Consequently, the only possibility is that
c\in\mathbf{K}
, in which case
\mathbf{K}=\mathbf{C}
.
Hence, every
\mathbf{K}\in\mathrm{Si}(\mathcal{K})
satisfies either
\mathbf{K}\in\mathrm{IS}(\mathbf{A}_{i})
for some
i
or
\mathbf{K}\cong\mathbf{C}
.
Thus,
\mathcal{K}=\mathrm{V}\!\left({\mathbf{A}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{A}_{n},\mathbf{C}}\right)
.
∎

We have shown that the given construction indeed produces a cover.
However, the crucial question is the converse. Does every cover arise from this construction?
The answer is affirmative, and this forms the main theorem of the paper.

Theorem 24

(Main Theorem)
.

Let
\mathcal{V}=\mathrm{V}\!\left({\mathbf{A}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{A}_{n}}\right)
be a finitely
n
-generated variety of cBCK-algebras.
Let
\mathcal{K}
be a cover of
\mathcal{V}
.
Then there exists
\mathbf{C}\in\mathrm{Cov}(\mathcal{V})
such that
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{V}\vee\mathrm{V}\!\left({\mathbf{C}}\right)
.

Proof.

By Proposition
21
,

\mathcal{K}=\mathrm{V}\!\left({\big(\{\mathbf{A}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{A}_{n}\}\setminus\mathrm{Prop}(\mathbf{B})}\right)\big)\cup\{\mathbf{B}\}

for some
\mathbf{B}\in\mathrm{Si}(\mathcal{K})
, and this set of generators is irredundant.

Let
c\in\mathrm{m}(\mathbf{B})
. Then
B\setminus\{c\}
is the universe of a subalgebra of
\mathbf{B}
, since it forms a downset of
\mathbf{B}
.
Assume that
\mathbf{B\setminus\{c\}}\notin\mathrm{IS}(\mathbf{A}_{i})
for any
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
.
Then
\mathbf{B\setminus\{c\}}\subsetneq\mathbf{B}
and

\mathcal{V}\subsetneq\mathrm{V}\!\left({\mathbf{A}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{A}_{n},\mathbf{B\setminus\{c\}}}\right)\subsetneq\mathcal{K},

because
\mathbf{B}
is not contained in
\mathrm{V}\!\left({\mathbf{A}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{A}_{n},\mathbf{B\setminus\{c\}}}\right)
.
This contradicts the assumption that
\mathcal{K}
is a cover.
Hence, the opposite must hold.

Let
B\setminus\{c\}\in\mathrm{IS}(\mathbf{A}_{i})
for some
i
.
Then
\mathbf{B}
can be viewed as
\mathbf{C}_{a}
constructed from
\mathbf{C}\cong\mathbf{B\setminus\{c\}}
,
where
\mathbf{C}\in\mathrm{S}(\mathbf{A}_{i})
.
Thus, we have
\mathbf{B}\cong\mathbf{C}_{a}
with
C_{a}=C\cup\{d\}
,
where
d
is a new element attached to
a
.
Since
\mathbf{B}\notin\mathrm{IS}(\mathbf{A}_{i})
for any
i
, the same applies to
\mathbf{C}_{a}
. We claim that
\mathbf{C}_{a}
is minimal with this property and containing
d
.
Indeed, if
\mathbf{C}_{a}
was not minimal, there would exist
\mathbf{D}\subsetneq\mathbf{C}_{a}
such that

\mathcal{V}\subsetneq\mathrm{V}\!\left({\mathbf{A}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{A}_{n},\mathbf{D}}\right)\subsetneq\mathcal{K},

which contradicts the fact that
\mathcal{K}
is a cover.

Since
\mathbf{C}_{a}
is minimal, we have
\mathbf{C}_{a}\in\mathrm{Cov}(\mathcal{V})
,
which, together with
\mathbf{B}\cong\mathbf{C}_{a}
, gives

\mathcal{K}=\mathrm{V}\!\left({\mathbf{A}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{A}_{n},\mathbf{C}_{a}}\right)=\mathcal{V}\vee\mathrm{V}\!\left({\mathbf{C}_{a}}\right).

∎

Let us summarise the results of Proposition
23
and the main theorem in the following corollary.

Corollary 25
.

Let
\mathcal{V}=\mathrm{V}\!\left({\mathbf{A}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{A}_{n}}\right)
be a finitely
n
-generated variety of cBCK-algebras,
and let
\mathcal{K}
be a variety of cBCK-algebras.
Then
\mathcal{K}
is a cover of
\mathcal{V}
if and only if

\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{V}\vee\mathrm{V}\!\left({\mathbf{C}}\right)
for some
\mathbf{C}\in\mathrm{Cov}(\mathcal{V})
.

From a practical perspective, we should note that if we have, say, two
1
-generated varieties
\mathrm{V}\!\left({\mathbf{A}_{1}}\right)
and
\mathrm{V}\!\left({\mathbf{A}_{2}}\right)
,
then, if we find a cover
\mathcal{K}
of
\mathrm{V}\!\left({\mathbf{A}_{1}}\right)
and this cover does not coincide with
\mathrm{V}\!\left({\mathbf{A}_{2}}\right)

it follows from the congruence distributivity that
\mathrm{V}\!\left({\mathbf{A}_{2}}\right)\vee\mathcal{K}

\mathrm{V}\!\left({\mathbf{A}_{1},\mathbf{A}_{2}}\right)

In other words, many covers of a finitely
n
-generated variety

\mathrm{V}\!\left({\mathbf{A}_{i}}\right)

5.
Conclusion

In this article, we have described a method for constructing any cover of a finitely
n
-generated variety of cBCK-algebras. Consequently, this provides a way to understand the lower part of the lattice of subvarieties of cBCK-algebras, which may be relevant for future research. Hereditary simplicity is the key to making this construction work. Although this property is quite special, it is natural to ask whether a similar construction could be applied in settings beyond cBCK-algebras.

For instance, it is known that some subvarieties of effect algebras also possess this property; see
[
3
]
for more details. In this context, simple algebras are horizontal sums of MV-algebras. Although horizontal sums of MV-chains are not trees, it may still be possible to consider constructions that extend these algebras in an analogous manner.

Several recent works on BCK-algebras address the structure of subvariety lattices;
for example,
[
1
]
investigates splittings in a wide range of algebraic structures related to BCK-algebras or derived from them,
though not specifically in commutative BCK-algebras.
To our knowledge, the only explicit result in this setting is due to Romanowska
[
12
]
,
who proved that any variety generated by a finite chain is splitting in the subvariety lattice of all cBCK-algebras.
Beyond this, the structure of splitting varieties within commutative BCK-algebras remains incomplete.

The aim of this article has been to describe the covers of finitely generated varieties of cBCK-algebras.
These results may provide useful insights for identifying join-irreducible elements near the bottom of the lattice,
which in turn could serve as candidates for splitting algebras.
Further examination of this approach could be considered.

In addition to theoretical considerations, the construction of covers can also be approached from an algorithmic perspective.
In particular, one could envision a program that computes all the covers (i.e., their irredundant generators) for a given finitely
n
-generated variety.
Such a tool would be useful for studying the lattice of subvarieties, and Remark
26
together with Theorem
11
provides natural strategies for optimising such an implementation.
