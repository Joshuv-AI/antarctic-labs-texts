Effect of the ‘digital plus’ approach to upscaling early childhood development services in rural China: study protocol for a cluster randomised controlled trial
Authors: Boya Wang, Yuyin Xiao, Yiwei Qian, Shanshan Li, Hanwen Zhang, Kelly Reiling, Scott Rozelle
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe high prevalence of developmental delays among young children poses significant barriers to long-term social mobility in low-income and middle-income countries. Digital interventions aimed at early childhood development (ECD) have shown promise in improving developmental outcomes, yet scalable and effective strategies remain under-explored. This study aims to evaluate the effectiveness of a digitally delivered parenting intervention to improve early child development in rural China.Method and analysisWe design a parallel cluster-randomised controlled trial to evaluate a 12-month digitally delivered ECD intervention in rural China. Key inclusion criteria are households with children aged 6–24 months and without relocation intentions. Key exclusion criteria are children with a severe disability. Participants are masked to treatment assignment. The project implements village-level interventions in three counties in central China, involving 70 villages (clusters). Implementation involves two phases: the preparatory phase and the online phase, in which ‘Parenting the Future’ curriculum will be delivered through a newly developed mobile application. During the preparatory phase, county-level parenting trainers will conduct weekly home visits to each sampled household. In the online phase, caregivers will primarily access weekly parenting training through prerecorded videos on a mobile application. Trainers will conduct short, fortnightly home visits to manage toys and picture books loaned through the same application in accordance with the training. The planned total treatment involves 48 sessions over 1 year. Assessments were conducted via home visits at baseline and endline. The primary outcomes include cognitive development measured with the Bayley Scales of Infant and Toddler Development, third edition and social-emotional development measured with the age and stage questionnaire: social emotional. Secondary outcomes include caregiver engagement and the home learning environment measured by family care indicators and responsive caregiving rating scale. The trial aims to test the effectiveness and scalability of a hybrid ECD intervention.Ethics and disseminationEthical approval for this study has been obtained from the Institutional Review Board of Southwestern University of Finance and Economics. Informed consent will be obtained from all participants, with appropriate measures in place to ensure participant confidentiality. Results from the study will be shared through academic publications, policy briefs and presentations to government stakeholders and international organisations, contributing to policy discussions on the scalability of digital ECD interventions.Trial registration numberAEA RCT Registry (AEARCTR-0013908); ISRCTN Registry (ISRCTN15854033).Keywords: Child, Digital Technology, Parents, Randomized Controlled Trial, Psychosocial Intervention

Introduction

Early childhood is a period of rapid growth and learning, widely recognised as a crucial stage for identifying and supporting young children with special needs.1 2 Studies estimate that up to 250 million children under the age of five—representing 43% of all young children in low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs)—are at risk of not reaching their developmental potential.3 To address this, high-quality early childhood development (ECD) initiatives emphasise the need for parental involvement and suitable intervention support.4 In response, a growing number of ECD programmes have been implemented in LMICs, providing substantial empirical evidence of their effectiveness in fostering caregiver-child interactions.5 6 These programmes encourage caregivers to engage in parenting practices that impart scientific knowledge and skills, while also enhancing their confidence and capabilities in child-rearing through hands-on activities and interactive demonstrations, ultimately contributing to improved ECD outcomes.7 8

Despite the positive outcomes of in-person parenting training programmes, they often face a dilemma between feasibility and effectiveness. Programmes that require trained staff to conduct frequent, long-term visits often incur large expenditures in the form of staff salaries and travel expenses.9 These programmes may not be affordable in all contexts or for delivery at scale.10 As a result, research shows that the main barriers preventing parents from participating in and continuing with in-person parenting programmes include a lack of accessible services in their area and being placed on a waiting list before receiving services.11,13 Additionally, adverse socioeconomic conditions such as poverty, low education levels and being a single parent have all been found to predict an increased likelihood of withdrawing from ongoing in-person parenting training programmes.14 On the other hand, interventions with lower intensity of visits and lower cost may be insufficient to drive meaningful change.15 Considering these barriers to in-person participation, studies suggest that online parenting programmes may reach a broader population and reduce access barriers caused by resource limitations.14 Additionally, online programmes may be more cost-effective and, as a result, more sustainable and scalable.16 17

The expanding access to the Internet has made online parenting interventions and assessments through computers or mobile devices more feasible.18 Over the past decade, the use of electronic devices in rural China has surged. Although a digital divide still exists for low-income and ethnic minority populations, there is evidence that this gap is narrowing.19 Results from a nationally representative survey—the China family panel studies—show that the percentage of rural residents using mobile phones rose from 68.7% in 2010 to 98.9% in 2018.20 Given the rapid increase in internet penetration in recent years, these figures may underestimate the current level. Online parenting interventions are more low-cost than in-person programmes, as they eliminate travel-related expenses and time constraints, offering significant savings and flexibility compared with in-person visits, particularly in rural and remote areas. They also allow for relatively easy and flexible customisation to meet the needs of different users (eg, customisation of video text displays). In addition, pre-recorded training materials delivered online are more structured and consistent in their presentation, which ensures complete and accurate delivery of programme content. They also reduce implementation fidelity issues that often arise from providers’ non-adherence to traditional interventions.21

A meta-analysis examining the effectiveness of online Parenting Programme found positive effects on both parents and child development, with the most notable outcomes being increased parental encouragement for children, more positive parenting behaviours and reduced negative parent–child interactions.22 For example, a randomised controlled trial (RCT) of the Internet-based Infant Net programme among 40 parents whose infants were at risk of poor social-emotional development found medium-to-large improvements in infants’ social engagement and environmental engagement in the treatment group compared with the control group.21 Similarly, a 3 month study of the KidzGrow Online parenting portal among parents of children aged 0–6 years found that access to the portal significantly increased the time parents spent with their children, their reported knowledge of child development and confidence in their parenting role when compared with the control group.23 Evidence from a larger-scale study in two Finnish maternity hospitals, involving 760 mothers, found that an internet-based parenting, breastfeeding and infant care support programme had no significant effects on parenting satisfaction. Still, it did improve infants’ centrality development among intervention mothers at 6 weeks postpartum.24 25 Online parenting programmes are not only particularly beneficial for parents who have limited access to in-person resources, but are also easier to scale across multiple regions, cities and even nationwide. However, there are currently relatively few online parenting programmes targeting caregivers of 0-to-3-year-old children,22 and almost none of the existing programmes have been implemented in LMICs. Given the highly culture-specific nature of parenting practices, further research is needed to understand the adaptability and effectiveness of online parenting programmes across a broader range of cultural contexts.

One way to combine the scalability of online parenting interventions with the expert supervision offered by in-person programmes is to integrate online parenting programmes with clinical support. Integrating parent training into paediatric care can benefit families by allowing them to access information in a familiar and convenient environment.26 The 2007 Lancet series on ECD also recommended that more evidence-based ECD interventions be integrated into existing service systems in a feasible and affordable manner.27 28 However, some studies have found that there are no significant differences in outcomes between online parenting programmes with clinical support and those without, suggesting that both types of programmes offer benefits to caregivers of children.22 29 One possible reason for this could be that clinical support requires families to pay additional fees (to care providers) to receive training.30 In the absence of funding or when parents are required to cover costs, the cost of these programmes could discourage sustained participation, especially among low-income caregivers. In light of this, the current study aims to explore whether integrating online parenting programmes with clinical support without requiring direct out-of-pocket expenses can maximise its positive effects on parenting practices. In this study, clinical support refers to the involvement of paediatricians and community health workers in guiding the caregivers in the online phase of the intervention during their routine well-baby visits. Specifically, they provide feedback on the caregiver’s progress with the training, offer guidance on the issues caregivers may encounter, raise awareness of the importance of ECD and scientific parenting, and encourage caregivers to continue participating in the online sessions. In China, paediatricians routinely conduct well-baby visits with caregivers at community health centres and provide basic paediatric health services such as growth monitoring, immunisation and health check-ups. Their service is generally free of charge as part of the National Basic Public Health Service Programme. The additional clinical support they offer as part of the parenting programme will also be offered free of charge to participating families, ensuring that caregivers are not required to make direct out-of-pocket payments.

To overcome costly implementation of in-person parenting, we developed ‘Digital Plus’, an online parenting programme that integrates a digitised version of a previously field-tested in-person parenting programme, ‘Parenting the Future’, loosely adapted from the ‘Reach Up and Learn’ curriculum,31 with existing infrastructures of clinical support. Previous parenting projects using this curriculum have demonstrated the effectiveness of both centre-based and home visit-based parenting training.32 Nonetheless, upscaling these trainings to reach larger numbers of caregivers in remote or dispersed areas remains a significant challenge. The Digital Plus project aims to assess whether delivering parenting training in a digital format can sustainably upscale our parenting intervention for caregivers of children aged 0–3 years old without compromising its effectiveness.

To evaluate the effectiveness of ‘Digital Plus’, we partner with the China Foundation for Rural Development (henceforth, CFRD), a government-affiliated NGO, to conduct a cluster RCT of this intervention across three counties in a province in central China. Parenting trainers and community workers (henceforth, facilitators) will be responsible for local implementation. Additionally, we plan to integrate the intervention into local health systems by having the paediatricians remind caregivers of children aged 0–3 years old to use the online training resources, thereby increasing the existing system’s role in implementation and enhancing the potential for future scaling.

Study objectives

The cluster-RCT of ‘Digital Plus’ pursues two objectives. First, we evaluate the effectiveness of ‘Digital Plus’ in promoting child development and interactive parenting practices compared with no parenting intervention. Second, we assess the cost-effectiveness of the ‘Digital Plus’ intervention by determining whether the partial digitisation of parent-child training can reduce costs while maintaining the quality of ECD outcomes. By leveraging technology to deliver parenting training, we seek to determine whether this digital format retains the effectiveness of traditional face-to-face instruction, which has been well-established in prior ECD interventions. These findings will enable a better understanding of the impacts of online parenting interventions on child development outcomes and provide evidence to support its scalability and sustainability in resource-constrained settings. This study will contribute to the international field of ECD research by examining how technology can facilitate ECD interventions in rural areas, while offering insights into cost-effective strategies for broader government implementation.

Methods and analyses

This protocol was developed in accordance with the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials reporting guidelines (see online supplemental appendix 2).

The study was designed as a 12-month, single-blind, stratified cluster-randomised controlled superiority trial. The 70 villages were automatically divided into two strata based on their unique characteristics. Within each stratum, villages were randomly assigned to one of two groups (see figure 1): the first group received a digitally delivered parenting training intervention focusing on children’s psychosocial stimulation, while the second was a pure control group with no intervention. Randomisation at the village (cluster) level was chosen to minimise spillover between study groups. Villages were randomised at a 1:1 allocation ratio. The study recruited children aged 6–24 months and their caregivers at the time of the baseline survey.

The stratified design of the trial.

The parenting intervention consists of weekly one-on-one training sessions based on a scripted curriculum, Parenting the Future, that was loosely adapted from the Reach Up and Learn curriculum and parent-child interaction observation and feedback sessions loosely adapted from the Chinese version of the parent–child interaction rating scale (IRS-C), a common observation-based system for examining the quality of parent–child interactions.23 To help caregivers become familiar with and adapt to the online learning model, the first 8 weeks of the programme were conducted through home visits, with all subsequent sessions delivered online. To enhance the sustainability of the intervention, trainers and facilitators from each county facilitate the intervention. They are supervised and regulated by the county-level branches of the China Foundation for Rural Development involved in this study (henceforth, CFRD agencies). In addition, paediatricians at township healthcare centres address caregivers’ questions arising during their participation in the digitally delivered parenting programme during routine well-baby visits and work to enhance caregivers’ adherence to the programme as much as possible. The research team provided onboarding training to trainers, facilitators, the CFRD agencies and paediatricians in township healthcare centres at the start of the intervention and offered timely technical support throughout the intervention period.

Study setting and sample selection

This study will be conducted in 70 rural villages across three counties in a province in Central China. These counties are in the southeastern, northwestern and southwestern regions of the province, and each had a total of 344, 235 and 171 non-urban communities (or villages), respectively. In our study province, the average years of schooling among the population aged 15 and above is 10.02 years.33 And based on data released in recent years by the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology, the mobile phone penetration rate in this Province is estimated at around 110–120 mobile phone plan subscriptions per 100 people.24 This high coverage suggests that digital delivery of parenting content is technically feasible in this context.

Since the CFRD agencies in these counties previously provided systematic training on child caregiving knowledge and skills to facilitators in parts of the villages, we use prior training participation as a stratification criterion to categorise villages into those with and without previous training experience. Then, villages were eligible for inclusion in the sample if they met all of the following requirements: (1) Villages without prior training experience must have at least 10 resident children aged 6–24 months, while villages with prior training must have at least 6, (2) The village committee offered support for storing loaner toys complementary with the intervention (ie, there is available space in a central location in the community for storing and loaning toys) and (3) At least one resident in the village was willing to participate in the study as a facilitator. We randomly selected 70 eligible villages for the study.

Eligibility criteria and recruitment strategy

Participant recruitment

The trial flowchart (figure 2) illustrates the timeline and process for participant recruitment and assessment. The target population for this study includes children aged 6 to 24 months at baseline and their caregivers, primarily the child’s mother and grandmother. All children and their caregivers who meet the age requirement in each village, regardless of the number of residents, will be invited to participate in the study, provided they do not plan to move out of the selected village during the study period. Smartphone ownership was not an eligibility criterion, given the high penetration of smartphones in rural China. For the small number of caregivers without a smartphone, typically older adults, facilitators used their own devices together with the caregiver’s individual account to access and view the intervention videos during toy delivery. The parenting stimulation intervention will be open to all caregivers of participating children.

Impact evaluation flow diagram.

The intervention was primarily designed for the primary caregiver of the child. In comparable rural areas, approximately 70% of primary caregivers are mothers.32 While the primary caregiver was the main intended audience, other family members were also encouraged to participate by watching the videos.

Parenting trainer recruitment

We refer to the intervention implementers as parenting trainers, some of whom work in CFRD branch agencies at the county level. Based on the number of children and the treatment arms in each township, CFRD agencies at the county level recruit through an open social hiring process and select via interviews. Eligible candidates are required to have at least a high school education and either formal qualifications in early childhood education or previous work experience in preschool settings. In each township, one to two parenting trainers will be selected. Specifically, unless more than 30 families are enrolled in a township’s intervention arm, only one parenting trainer is needed. For townships with more than 30 families in the intervention arm, we will consult with the local CFRD branch at the county level to determine if a second parenting trainer is needed to fully implement the intervention. If necessary, we will select and hire a second parenting trainer for that township. The criteria for selecting parenting trainers are (1) A minimum education level of high school or above and (2) Being a long-term resident of the county with no plans to leave the study village during the research period.

Facilitator recruitment

Facilitators provide services at the village level. We detail the differences between their responsibilities and the parenting trainers in the intervention section below. In our project, the role of facilitator is undertaken by the children’s committee director in each village. The children’s committee director is a position established within village-level administrative units across all rural areas in China. Its primary responsibility is to ensure that children, especially those in vulnerable circumstances, are protected, receive welfare support and grow up in a nurturing environment. Facilitators are required to have at least a junior high school education. Only one village-level facilitator is needed for the caregiver training intervention.

Paediatrician recruitment

Each township has one healthcare centre that provides well-baby visits for all children aged 0–6 in the area. Typically, each centre has one to four paediatricians. Based on local circumstances, we will recruit two to three paediatricians to participate in the clinical support component to ensure that they provide the necessary support throughout the project period.

Training for staff

Parenting trainers, facilitators and paediatricians all participated in centralised training organised by the research team.

Parenting trainers and facilitators received a 1 week training that included (1) An overview of the project, (2) The importance of ECD, (3) Communication and facilitation skills and (4) Logistical procedures. For parenting trainers, the programme additionally emphasised demonstrations and supervised practice of the one-to-one parenting sessions, while facilitators were not required to complete this practicum component.

Paediatricians attended a half-day training, which focused on (1) The project overview, (2) The importance of ECD, (3) The role of paediatric care in supporting the intervention and (4) The use of the APP to document health assessments and coordinate with the project team.

Intervention

Digitally delivered parenting intervention

The intervention lasts 12 months, from January 2025 to January 2026. It is divided into two main phases: a preparatory phase in the first 2 months after the project’s initiation, and an online phase primarily over the subsequent 10 months. The core components of the intervention include parenting training, parent-child interaction observation and feedback.

Parenting training

The Parenting the Future curriculum is adapted from the Reach Up and Learn curriculum and modified by ECD experts in China for adaptation to the rural Chinese context. The weekly, phased, age-appropriate sessions target children aged 6 to 36 months, with a total of 48 sessions delivered over 1 year. Each session focuses on two of the four developmental modules: cognitive, language, motor and socio-emotional skill development. Every session also includes specific toys or books required for the activities. The Parenting the Future curriculum has been field-tested in China and proven effective in improving young children’s cognitive development through multiple RCTs across the country.25 32

In the preparatory phase, parenting trainers deliver the in-person parenting sessions for each village assigned to the intervention group over the first 2 months for a total of eight sessions. Each session lasts for about 30 min. At the 7th and 8th sessions, parenting trainers are also responsible for teaching caregivers how to use an application called Qiantian Parenting for the subsequent sessions.

During the online phase, caregivers use the application called Qiantian Parenting for their learning. The application allows them to access online pre-recorded course videos based on the curriculum content, which are updated every 2 weeks according to the child’s age. The interface of caregivers is shown in online supplemental appendix 1. Caregivers are required to select their relationship to the child (eg, mother, father, grandmother or other caregiver) before accessing each video. Two 5 min videos are assigned each week. Caregivers can also use the app to contact the parenting trainers for timely support if they have any questions during the learning process. The parenting trainers will conduct four home visits every 2 months (at the beginning of the 5th, 7th, 9th and 11th months of the intervention) to address the caregivers’ concerns and provide additional feedback.

Complementary to the digitally delivered parenting training, we store the toys and books needed for the training at a central location in the community, provided by the local village committee. The facilitator will manage the toys and books, distributing them every 2 weeks to participating families throughout both phases of the intervention. During each distribution, the facilitator will provide toys and books complementary to the training materials for the upcoming 2 weeks and collect those from the previous period. Families can either pick up the toys at the storage location or have them delivered to their homes by the facilitators. We encourage parents to use the borrowed toys and books to interact with their children as frequently as possible during the 2 week period. The application provides reminders and records the distribution of toys. The interface used by trainers and facilitators is shown in online supplemental appendix 1.

To use the existing infrastructure to promote compliance to online parenting training, we integrated clinical support into the intervention. Starting in the third month, as caregivers in the intervention arm transition to online learning, trained paediatricians at each township provide targeted guidance during their routine well-baby visits at community health centres. In China, well-baby visits for children aged 6 to 36 months are routinely scheduled at 6, 9, 12, 18, 24, 30 and 36 months old. Paediatricians are provided with the list of children in the intervention group, and stickers are placed on their paper-based health records to facilitate identification of participants.

Paediatricians will first deliver the standard child health services for children in the intervention group. Then, they provide the additional clinical support services specified by the intervention, which includes: (1) Providing feedback on the caregiver’s progress with the online parenting training from the previous month or quarter, (2) Offering guidance on specific issues encountered during online learning, (3) Raising awareness on the importance of ECD and scientific parenting and (4) Encouraging parents to continue participating in upcoming online parenting training sessions. If caregivers do not bring their child to the township health centre for the scheduled in-person well-baby visit, paediatricians are required to conduct the project-mandated clinical support by telephone. Due to the varying ages of children at intervention initiation, it is estimated that each family in the intervention arm may receive between one and three paediatrician visits during the online phase.

Parent-child interaction observation and feedback

To supplement the weekly online learning, we conducted a parent-child interaction quality observation and provided feedback as a component of the intervention. We followed the guidelines in the Parent-Child Interaction Guidance Manual, developed by the Children’s Development Research Team at Fudan University in 2024 to support parent-child interactions within a structured home-based observational framework based on the past research.23 The primary objective of the manual is to improve the quality of language exposure, emotional responsiveness and physical engagement between parents and young children, independent of structured interaction activities. The manual consists of eight subscales, including 33 observation items, each subscale corresponding to a specific assignment. Each assignment prompts caregivers to reflect on various scenarios of interaction with their child, consider the conditions that may trigger certain thoughts or behaviours, observe their responses and identify areas for improvement.

During the preparatory phase, in each of the eight sessions, the trainer begins observation immediately on entering the home, focusing on both direct parent-child interactions and the overall family environment. After each session, the trainer provides feedback to caregivers, highlighting strengths and areas for improvement. Caregivers also receive weekly assignments during the preparatory phase, enabling parents to gradually improve parenting skills through continuous practice and reflection.

During the online phase, caregivers receive one assignment every 2 months (a total of four assignments) tailored to address areas identified as needing improvement in the previous eight observations.

Quality assurance and monitoring

The application system collects detailed data on caregivers’ engagement with the online courses, including the date and time of each session, the proportion of content viewed, the number of clicks and the caregiver’s relationship to the child. For the intervention delivery, the tablet-based application additionally records whether families received toys every 2 weeks, their consent to receive the toys and the frequency of toy use. For the clinical support component, each support session is documented and uploaded to the platform. To enable real-time supervision, a monitoring dashboard was developed using key implementation indicators to ensure effective and timely oversight of the intervention process. Furthermore, project assistants are responsible for tracking implementation progress and maintaining close communication with parenting trainers, facilitators and paediatricians to identify and resolve problems promptly.

Theory of change

The theory of change for the digitally delivered parenting intervention is shown in figure 3. The diagram presents the main process and outcome indicators that will measure the effectiveness of the intervention, along with the key assumptions and steps in the theory of change pathway.

Theory of change of the digital plus programme. ECD, early childhood development.

The diagram illustrates our assumption that participation in home visits and online sessions by trained parenting trainers and facilitators can improve caregivers’ attitudes, self-efficacy and knowledge regarding early child development (intermediate outcomes). These improvements can subsequently lead to behavioural changes (behavioural outcomes) and improve caregiver mental health, ultimately contributing to enhanced ECD outcomes (child development outcomes). In addition, although the intervention does not directly support caregivers’ mental health, we expect improvements in caregiver mental health because the delivery model establishes repeated, structured social contact between caregivers, facilitators and paediatricians, and because caregivers’ self-efficacy may improve due to their new parenting skills.

Additionally, characteristics at the parenting trainer, facilitator, paediatrician, caregiver, child and household levels are anticipated to moderate various stages along the theory of change pathway. First, staff-related factors, such as age, education and prior knowledge and beliefs, may directly impact caregiver engagement in and learning from home visits. Second, as the intervention focuses on individual behavioural change, caregiver-specific factors—such as age, education, employment status, health—may influence each step along the pathway. Third, child-specific factors, including sex, age, birth order and birth outcomes, are expected to shape their responses to changes in caregiver practices. Lastly, household-level factors—such as family structure, economic status and access to community resources—may determine both their adherence to recommended practices and the long-term trajectories of behaviour changes.

Children’s level of development is evaluated using the standardised scores. We also calculate rates of developmental delay by determining the proportion of sample children whose scores fall below the prespecified, test-specific cut-off scores.

Administrative records

The mobile application for caregivers, parenting trainers, facilitators and paediatricians collects comprehensive administrative data. The caregiver-side application enables real-time tracking of weekly video viewership, including metrics such as view count and total viewing duration. The trainer-side application records session details such as date, duration and location to the children. The facilitator-side application manages data on home visit schedules, locations, visit recipients and toy distribution and return records. The paediatrician-side application logs details of well-baby visits, intervention-related communications and their respective dates.

Masking

Households in the control arm are not informed about the intervention programmes and are only invited to participate in the baseline and endline surveys. The survey enumerators are separately recruited before each round of data collection. Because enumerators work with facilitators to survey households and can easily identify caregivers in the treatment arms during interviews, masking of treatment conditions to enumerators at endline data collection is unnecessary. Nevertheless, enumerators are blinded to treatment allocation. It is not necessary to mask treatment allocation to parenting trainers and paediatricians for two reasons: (1) Parenting trainers were hired at the county level and supervised by the county-level Civil Affairs Bureau and (2) Parenting trainers and paediatricians are only instructed on providing sessions and conducting home visits, whereas they are blinded to the trial design and assessment procedures. Data analysts are also blinded to the identification of the study arms.

Power calculation

Power calculations are estimated assuming two-tailed tests and a 5% significance level for both child cognitive development and stimulating parenting practices. Due to the increasing population mobility in rural China, there is a discrepancy between the number of registered residents and actual residents in certain areas, making it challenging to obtain accurate household counts. To address this, we performed two power calculations. If, during preliminary assessments, we find that the number of eligible households in the area is insufficient to achieve the desired statistical power in the initial calculation, we will implement the secondary calculation. This approach involves expanding the sample size by adding a second cohort, ensuring that the study attains adequate power to detect meaningful intervention effects.

Calculation I: based on previous field research, we assume that an average of 13 eligible households will be present in each village. The intra-cluster correlation (ICC) of each outcome is assumed to be 0.01. We randomly assign 35 villages to the treatment arm and 35 to the control arm. Based on a meta-analysis conducted in rural China, we assume the minimal detectable effect size (MDE) of each of the treatment arms on child cognitive development to be 0.2 of a SD and stimulative parenting practices to be 0.33 of a SD.39 Using these parameters, the power calculation reveals that the current study has 81% statistical power to detect a difference between the treatment and control arm in child cognitive development and 99% statistical power to detect a difference in stimulating parenting practices.

Calculation II: according to data from China’s Seventh National Census, the floating population accounts for 26.6% of the total population in China.64 We assume that an average of 10 eligible households will be present in each village. The ICC of each outcome is assumed to be 0.01. We randomly assign 45 villages to the treatment arm and 45 to the control arm. Based on a meta-analysis conducted in rural China, we assume the MDE of each of the treatment arms on child cognitive development to be 0.2 of a SD and stimulative parenting practices to be 0.33 of a SD.39 Using these parameters, the power calculation reveals that the current study has 81% statistical power to detect a difference between the treatment and control arm in child cognitive development and 99% statistical power to detect a difference in stimulating parenting practices.

Statistical analysis

The quantitative analysis will follow the consolidated standards of reporting trials guidelines using STATA (V.18). We will create a flow chart that shows the number of participants at each trial stage, including the number of eligible, enrolled, randomised, attritted and analysed regarding the primary outcomes. The initial analysis consists of unadjusted comparisons between the control and treatment arms on both primary and secondary outcomes. Means and SDs will be reported for continuous outcomes, while numbers and proportions will be reported for categorical outcomes. Statistical significance will be determined if the p value of the hypothesis test is <0.05.

The primary analysis is an intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis, adjusted for the baseline measurements of the outcomes, baseline controls and county stratum fixed effects. Continuous outcomes will be analysed using ordinary least squares regressions, while binary outcomes will be analysed using logistic regression models. Standard errors will be clustered at the village level using the cluster-corrected Huber-White estimator. We will also estimate the per-protocol effects of each arm on the outcomes among participants with relatively high compliance rates (defined as attendance at ≥75% of scheduled centre sessions). We also report sensitivity analyses using 70% and 80% thresholds and a continuous dosage measure (share of sessions attended). Effect sizes will be reported as standardised mean differences for continuous outcomes and adjusted risk ratios for binary outcomes, both at 95% significant levels.

The secondary analysis aims to examine heterogeneity in treatment effects across caregiver and child subgroups. We conduct this analysis by adding each of the subgroup indicators and the interaction term between the treatment assignment indicator and the subgroup indicator to the ITT regression models from the primary analysis specified above. Ethics and dissemination

Research ethics approval

This study received approval from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) at Southwestern University of Finance and Economics in China.

Protocol amendments

Protocol modifications will be fully disclosed in future publications. In case of any changes, the protocol in the clinical registry and IRBs will be updated accordingly.

Informed consent

All caregivers must give informed oral consent for their own and their child’s participation in the study. Consent will be obtained orally by trained enumerators in the caregiver’s home before the start of project activities following the participant consent form (see online supplemental appendix 3). Enumerators will spend 5 min describing the study to caregivers, with an additional 5 min devoted to asking questions. Caregivers will be notified that their participation in the study is voluntary and that they have the right to withdraw their information if they desire. All survey enumerators will be fully trained in the consent process. Additionally, members of local government offices will assist the research team to ensure all participants understand the project activities and their participation before giving consent. As a certain number of rural caregivers in China, particularly grandparents, are not literate, written consent will not be collected.

Confidentiality

Only the field investigators and the project coordinators have access to personal data for intervention implementation before and during the trial. De-identified data are available to the research team or other scientists under a cooperation agreement. All data is password-protected at all stages. No participant’s identity will be shared in any format to protect confidentiality.

Declaration of interests

We declare no financial or other competing interests for the principal investigators for the overall trial and each one of the study sites.

Dissemination policy

The full protocol will be publicly available in an open-access format. The study findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications, conference presentations and bilingual (Chinese and English) policy briefs. We will also share plain language summaries with stakeholders, including relevant county and township committees, and hold debriefings with implementation partners. With a large network of collaborators from other universities, practitioners and the health system, we will facilitate dissemination of the findings and share them with various audiences (eg, the health system, policymakers, researchers) through four mechanisms: practitioner and community networks, education and knowledge dissemination networks, policy engagement and data accessibility. Only researchers and investigators who meet ICMJE criteria for authorship will be included as co-authors. Other contributors, such as enumerators, staff or participants, will be acknowledged in the published manuscripts.

The recruitment began in October 2024 and was completed in January 2025. The intervention has been ongoing since January 2025 and is projected to be completed in January 2026.
