Monitoring pneumonia onset and severe respiratory infectious diseases using quantitative computed tomography, validation amidst the coronavirus disease 2019 outbreak at the end of 2022 in the Chinese mainland
Authors: Ting Cheng, Tiantian Zhang, Jingci Xu, Yong Li, Yaqing Jin, Shengyu Wang, Li Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEffective surveillance of severe respiratory infectious diseases is crucial. This study aimed to explore an automated quantitative analysis method based on chest computed tomography (CT) for distinguishing individuals with pneumonia and to evaluate its effectiveness for monitoring pneumonia incidence.MethodsHospitalized pneumonia patients and healthy controls with normal chest CTs were enrolled. CT images were analyzed using “uAI Discover Pneumonia” software (United Imaging Intelligence) to derive CT-derived quantitative metrics related to pneumonia, including total lung infection volume and proportion of infected lung volume relative to total lung volume. Differences in these quantitative CT (QCT) infection metrics between the two groups were compared. The cutoff value with the highest Youden index was selected and its sensitivity and specificity were evaluated. CT scans requested from pneumonia-related emergency departments and outpatient departments (PR-ED/OPD) between Week 27, 2022, and Week 26, 2023 were extracted. Chest CT request volume was counted. The proportion of pneumonia-positive CT scans and the estimated pneumonia patients volume within these requests were calculated weekly to reflect temporal changes in pneumonia incidence. These indicators were compared with other surveillance metrics using cross-correlation analysis to evaluate temporal relationships. The proportion of pneumonia-positive CT scans and estimated pneumonia patient volume were compared across typical periods following policy changes.ResultsA total of 209 pneumonia patients and 221 control subjects were included. The median [interquartile range (IQR)] total lung infection volume was 545.1 (255.7–966.7) mL in the pneumonia group and 0.0 (0.0–13.1) mL in the non-pneumonia control group (P<0.001). Median infected volume proportion was 19.90% (7.15–33.85%) vs. 0.00% (0.00–0.30%) (P<0.001). Area under the curve (AUC) values were 0.978±0.005 and 0.980±0.005, respectively. Optimal cutoffs were ≥65 mL (sensitivity 92.34%, specificity 91.86%) and ≥1.75% (sensitivity 93.30%, specificity 93.21%). CT scans from PR-ED/OPD comprised 99,723 encounters. Quantitative analysis was performed on 39,366 eligible encounters, of which 8,955 were classified as pneumonia-positive. Cross-correlation analysis showed that the CT-estimated pneumonia patient volume led regional pneumonia-associated mortality by 1 week (r=0.954, P<0.001), lagged the proportion of influenza-like illness (ILI) cases testing positive for coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) in Chinese sentinel hospitals by 1 week (r=0.824, P<0.001), and was highly synchronized with fluctuations in the number of adult pneumonia inpatients during the same period (r=0.968, P<0.001).ConclusionsCT-derived quantitative metrics exhibit excellent diagnostic accuracy for pneumonia, with recommended cutoffs of ≥65 mL total infected volume and ≥1.75% infected proportion. This automated method effectively monitors pneumonia incidence, reflecting the epidemic status of severe respiratory infections.

Highlight box

Key findings

• Automated quantitative chest computed tomography (CT) analysis using “uAI Discover Pneumonia” software accurately distinguished pneumonia patients from controls, achieving area under the curve values of 0.978 (total infected lung volume) and 0.980 (infected volume proportion). Optimal cutoffs were ≥65 mL (sensitivity 92.34%, specificity 91.86%) and ≥1.75% (sensitivity 93.30%, specificity 93.21%). CT-estimated pneumonia volume strongly correlated with regional mortality (r=0.954, 1-week lead), coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19)-positive influenza-like illness cases (r=0.824, 1-week lag), and adult pneumonia inpatients (r=0.968) amidst the COVID-19 outbreak.

What is known and what is new?

• Effective surveillance of severe respiratory infectious diseases is crucial. Chest CT is a sensitive tool for pneumonia diagnosis, yet current approaches depend on subjective radiologist interpretation or manual delineation.

• This manuscript showed that CT-derived quantitative metrics exhibit excellent diagnostic accuracy for pneumonia, with recommended cutoffs of ≥65 mL total infected volume and ≥1.75% infected proportion. Quantitative CT (QCT) can effectively monitor pneumonia incidence, reflecting the epidemic status of severe respiratory infectious diseases.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Automated QCT analysis can serve as an important tool for pneumonia identification and severe respiratory infectious diseases surveillance.

Introduction

Timely detection, isolation, and treatment of patients with acute infectious diseases are crucial for reducing fatality rates and controlling disease spread. Infectious disease outbreaks cause a sharp surge in healthcare demand, necessitating the mobilization of sufficient medical resources to ensure adequate supply. Conversely, mobilizing and amassing substantial medical resources within a short period incurs significant costs. Therefore, the rapid assessment and prediction of healthcare demand arising from infectious disease outbreaks within a region is of great importance. However, existing infectious disease surveillance methods demonstrate limitations in detecting emerging respiratory infectious diseases and assessing disease burden during outbreaks. Syndromic surveillance offers superior timeliness and sensitivity compared to conventional case surveillance approaches. Establishing appropriate case definitions with high signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) is crucial for enhancing early-warning efficacy in respiratory syndromic surveillance systems. Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) is a respiratory infectious disease caused by severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2). Owing to the extremely high transmissibility and pathogenicity of this pathogen, it has escalated into a global public health catastrophe. From 2020 to September 2025, approximately 779 million cases of COVID-19 and around 7 million related deaths have been confirmed worldwide (1). The COVID-19 pandemic has heightened the urgent need for respiratory syndromic surveillance systems and has served as a critical test for their effectiveness.

Pneumonia is a significant disease threatening human life and health (2). It is also the primary reason requiring hospitalization and causing fatalities for severe acute respiratory infectious diseases outbreaks in the 21st century, such as COVID-19 (3), severe acute respiratory syndrome (SARS) (4), influenza A (H1N1) (5), and Middle East respiratory syndrome (MERS) (6). Therefore, trends in pneumonia incidence can serve as an indicator reflecting both the case numbers and severity of respiratory infectious disease outbreaks.

Although pneumonia caused by other pathogens (such as common bacteria, Mycoplasma, other viruses like influenza), or pulmonary infiltrates due to non-infectious causes (e.g., interstitial lung disease, pulmonary involvement in autoimmune diseases, lung injury from physical or chemical factors) can mimic pneumonia associated with infectious diseases (7,8), at the population level, the incidence of pneumonia from non-infectious pathogens and non-infectious pulmonary infiltrates tends to be relatively stable or predictable. This characteristic facilitates estimation, allowing their contribution to be subtracted when estimating the number of pneumonia cases attributable to infectious diseases.

Chest computed tomography (CT) is the main method for diagnosing pneumonia and assessing its severity. CT demonstrates high sensitivity in diagnosing pneumonia (9,10) and has excellent clinical accessibility. The severity of pneumonia often correlates directly with the extent of pathological changes in lung tissue, which can be effectively visualized on CT scans (11-13). For example, alveolar inflammation presents as parenchymal consolidations, bronchiolitis presents as centrilobular nodular opacities, and inflammation involving the pulmonary interstitium and/or alveolar spaces presents as ground-glass opacities (GGO) on CT (14).

Patients with pneumonia can be identified through subjective interpretation of CT scans by radiologists (visually interpreting chest CT scans without the aid of quantitative analysis by computer software). In the previous study, at a major trauma center within the UK’s National Health Service (NHS), patients admitted for significant trauma were enrolled. The proportion exhibiting COVID-19-related CT features assessed by the radiologists was calculated, and by subtracting the baseline pneumonia rate documented in pre-pandemic control CTs, the prevalence and cumulative incidence of COVID-19 in the general population were estimated. Results from subjects enrolled between January 1 to April 30, 2020 indicated that 45% of the population had been infected by late April 2020, significantly higher than London’s seroprevalence rate (17%) (15). This methodology was extended until February 28, 2021. The trajectory of pneumonia rates observed in trauma patients’ CT scans accurately delineated London’s second pandemic wave. When using the presence of major signs of COVID-19 on CT as the standard, the estimated cumulative incidence (30% by late February 2021) aligned with seropositivity rates in London’s population (16). However, this approach relies on subjective CT interpretation, constitutes significant limitations that include high manpower demands and inter-observer variability among radiologists. Its implementation across diverse hospitals or regions remains challenging, particularly during outbreaks when healthcare resources are strained.

Pneumonia patients can also be identified via Natural Language Processing (NLP) of radiology reports. Three NLP algorithms were developed to track positive CT imaging features of respiratory illness typical in SARS-CoV-2 viral infection in the chest CT report in English. The NLP algorithms were applied to 450,114 patient chest CT comprehensive reports gathered from January 1 to October 3, 2020 from 2,100 facilities throughout all 50 US states. The NLP models demonstrated strong correlation to official cases on a weekly and daily official COVID-19 cases basis (r2=0.91, P<0.005 and r2=0.82, P<0.005) and an early rise in cases followed by the increase of official cases (17). Using a regular expression-based text search algorithm, reports in German were labeled positive/negative for pulmonary infection if described. All chest imaging reports performed in the emergency department of University Hospital Basel, Switzerland, between January 2012 and June 2022 were included (64,046 radiographs; 27,705 CTs). The number needed to image (NNI), the positive rate for pulmonary infection detection and the correlation with influenza/COVID-19 data was investigated. Regional COVID-19 epidemic waves correlated moderately with the positive pulmonary infection CT curve for 2020–2022 (weekly new cases: r=0.53; hospitalizations: r=0.65; P<0.001) (18). However, under traditional chest CT interpretation paradigms, quantification remains challenging. Radiology reports exhibit substantial variability in describing identical imaging findings, with no standardized criteria for characterizing the extent and severity of pneumonia. For instance, pneumonia may be documented as exudation, consolidation, high-density opacities, or GGO. The term “inflammatory changes” could indicate severe pneumonia, mild pneumonia, or chronic sequelae such as fibrosis from prior inflammation. Consequently, merely extracting report contents provides limited clinical utility for determining pneumonia presence or severity.

CT detects lesions by identifying tissue density variations (CT values) or abnormal morphological features. Pneumonia manifestations on CT essentially represent pulmonary regions with higher attenuation than normal lung parenchyma but lower than specific pathological alterations. Delineating pneumonia involvement can be approximated by applying defined density thresholds. Furthermore, distinct density ranges correspond to specific pathological stages: Shin et al. (19) classified regions at −950 to −700 HU as functional lung tissue, while areas measuring −700 to −500 HU as interstitial lesions. Yabuuchi et al. (20) proposed −500 HU as the threshold differentiating consolidation from GGO. Advancing computational technologies now enable rapid automated lung segmentation and quantification of pathological tissue volumes within specific density ranges. This technique was initially applied for assessing emphysema and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) severity (21). Then it was extended to quantitative evaluation of acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) and interstitial lung diseases (22-26). Recently, deep learning (DL)-based methods were developed for automatic segmentation and quantification of infection regions as well as the entire lungs from chest CT scans (27-29). How to utilize the quantitative pneumonia analysis derived from chest CT scans to identify affected individuals and monitor epidemiological metrics warrants further investigation.

At the end of 2022, after 3 years of pandemic control, the Chinese Government changed its management of COVID-19, ended the dynamic zero-COVID policy in mainland and reopened of the country (30). The key time nodes were as follows: “the 20-point measures” were announced on November 11, 2022 (31); “the 10-point measures” were released on December 7, 2022 (32). China began to manage COVID-19 with measures against Class B infectious diseases, instead of Class A infectious diseases from January 8, 2023 (33). A dramatic surge in COVID-19 infections across the Chinese mainland, followed by a sharp decline was observed (34,35). This created an invaluable validation cohort for evaluating quantitative CT (QCT)-based methodologies for estimating pneumonia incidence.

The present study aims to explore an automated QCT method for distinguishing individuals with pneumonia and to evaluate its effectiveness for monitoring pneumonia incidence. The present study employs United Imaging’s Artificial Intelligence (AI)-powered pneumonia quantification module to derive pulmonary lesion volumes. By comparing volumetric measurements associated with pneumonia between confirmed pneumonia patients and healthy controls, we established clinically validated thresholds for determining radiologically significant pneumonia. This framework enabled estimation of pneumonia prevalence among CT-examined individuals and projection of pneumonia incidence in the clinical population. It thereby reflects the incidence of pneumonia and severe respiratory infections within the catchment area. During the late-2022 COVID-19 surge, validation was performed against three epidemiological benchmarks: Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention (China CDC)-reported influenza-like illness (ILI) rates and SARS-CoV-2 positivity rates, and pneumonia-attributable mortality in the certain districts. We present this article in accordance with the STARD reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2025-1-2454/rc).

Methods

Study subjects

Patients admitted to Ruijin Hospital (affiliated with Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine) because of pneumonia between December 2022 and February 2023, whose chest CT scans amenable to quantitative analysis, were enrolled as pneumonia group. A total of 209 patients were enrolled, including 69 females and 140 males. Asymptomatic individuals who underwent routine health examinations at the same institution during July 2023 without radiographic evidence of pneumonia and whose chest CT scans were amenable to quantitative analysis were enrolled as the non-pneumonia control group through age- and sex-matched random sampling (n=221).

Given that patients with respiratory infections commonly present at respiratory clinics, fever clinics, and emergency medicine departments, these units were collectively designated as pneumonia-related emergency departments and outpatient departments (PR-ED/OPD). Since nearly all adults with suspected pneumonia undergo immediate chest CT examinations, and considering that the vast majority of pneumonia inpatients had already undergone CT scans during PR-ED/OPD visits, we exclusively included CT scans ordered through PR-ED/OPD channels, excluding inpatient-requested examinations. Patients receiving chest CT scans in Ruijin Hospital’s PR-ED/OPD between week 27, 2022 (July 4, 2022) and week 26, 2023 (July 2, 2023) were enrolled to estimate the volume of pneumonia. In the present study, all chest CT scans were performed using routine protocol. The patients were placed in a supine position and held their breath during the scan after a deep inspiration. Chest CT was performed from lung apex to diaphragm using automatic exposure control, with tube voltage and tube current adjusted according to patient body habitus. CT images were routinely reconstructed using multiple protocols, including standard algorithm with conventional slice thickness (e.g., 5 mm), lung algorithm with conventional slice thickness, and lung algorithm with thin-section reconstruction (e.g., slice thickness of 1 mm). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Ruijin Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine (Ethical approval No. [2023]100) and individual consent for this retrospective analysis was waived.

QCT analysis

The chest CT images were processed through “uAI Discover Pneumonia” software (United Imaging Intelligence, Shanghai, China) (27-29). “uAI Discover Pneumonia” is a specialized AI-powered computer-aided analysis system developed by United Imaging Intelligent for the automated detection and quantitative assessment of pneumonia from chest CT scans. The software automatically selected the CT series which reconstructed with contiguous thin slices (e.g., slice thickness and reconstruction interval both set to 1 mm) and a lung algorithm, and then resampled the CT images to the same pixel size. Pixel size was routinely set as 512×512. The software automatically delineated the pulmonary tissue, performing lobar and segmental partitioning, subsequently identifying regions of pneumonia. It computed total lung volume along with quantitative infection metrics, including: total infected lung volume, proportion of infected lung volume relative to total lung volume, infected volume per lobe/segment, and proportion of infected volume within each lobe/segment (Figure 1).

The schematic diagram of “uAI Discover Pneumonia” software for pneumonia automatic recognition. (A) A 65-year-old male patient with CT showing bilateral lower lobe pneumonia. As indicated by the red arrows, various manifestations of pneumonia on the patient’s chest CT, including consolidation, ground-glass opacity, and small patchy shadows distributed along the bronchi, are accurately rendered by the “uAI Discover Pneumonia” software. (B) The software automatically identified the pneumonia areas and calculated the lung infection volume as 800 mL (21% of total lung volume). (C) A 35-year-old male with no pneumonia detected on CT. (D) The software fails to identify pneumonia regions, with a lung infection volume of 0 mL (0% involvement). CT, computed tomography.

According to the standard operating procedures for CT examinations, the CT operator has access to the subjects’ clinical data. The performance of chest CT is highly standardized, and QCT analysis is conducted automatically following established protocols. Therefore, neither clinical information nor the gold standard results can influence the QCT results.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed as follows: normality of all metrics was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test; for non-normally distributed parameters, between-group differences in CT-derived quantitative infection metrics between the pneumonia and non-pneumonia cohorts were evaluated with the Mann-Whitney U test; receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were constructed to determine the discriminatory power of each CT infection metric. Total infected lung volume and its percentage relative to total lung volume were selected as primary indicators for distinguishing pneumonia. Optimal cutoff thresholds were identified by maximizing the Youden index on ROC curves. The sensitivity and specificity of these thresholds were calculated for the overall cohort and further assessed across gender and age subgroups.

Due to computational capacity constraints, quantitative analysis could not be performed on all scans; thus, CT studies were randomly selected within each time period according to processing resource availability. According to the optimal cutoff threshold for clinically significant pneumonia obtained in the previous part (≥65 mL of pulmonary infiltration on CT), we calculated the proportion of pneumonia-positive CT scans per time period. This proportion was multiplied by the total number of CT examinations to estimate pneumonia patient volume.

This study concurrently employs several other indicators to reflect the incidence of pneumonia: (I) the number of deceased adult patients in Jiading District, Shanghai in each given time period, with pneumonia as the primary cause of death; (II) the average proportion of patients with ILI and COVID-positive results identified within sentinel hospitals reported by the China CDC; (III) the number of registrations in PR-ED/OPD; (IV) the number of adult patients admitted due to pneumonia; (V) the number of adult COVID-19 patients reported by the hospitals.

We calculated the weekly proportion of pneumonia-positive CT scans requested by PR-ED/OPD and the estimated pneumonia patient volume to reflect temporal changes in pneumonia incidence, comparing these with other pneumonia incidence indicators. Temporal correlations and lead/lag times were analyzed using cross-correlation analysis. Based on COVID-19 prevention policy changes and epidemic progression, the study period was divided into representative phases: 2#The month before “the 20-point measures” (October 11, 2022–November 10, 2022), 3#The period under “the 20-point measures” (November 11, 2022–December 6, 2022), 4#The period under “the 10-point measures” (December 7, 2022–January 7, 2023), 5#The first month after Implementing the Category B Management for COVID-19 (January 8, 2023–February 7, 2023), 6#The second month after Implementing the Category B Management for COVID-19 (February 8, 2023–March 7, 2023), 7#The month during the wave of COVID-19 reinfections (May 8, 2023–June 7, 2023), 8#The second month of the COVID-19 reinfection wave (June 8, 2023–July 7, 2023). Phase #1 refers to the periods outside of phase #2 to phase #8. Due to the heterogeneity of phase #1, it was not included in the analysis. For each given time period, we computed daily proportions of pneumonia-positive CT scans, pneumonia patient volumes, and other incidence indicators. Central tendency was expressed as mean ± standard deviation and median [interquartile range (IQR)], with between-period differences assessed using t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (Version 25; IBM Corp.).

Monitoring pneumonia onset using QCT, validation amidst the COVID-19 outbreak

From Week 27, 2022 to Week 26, 2023, 99,723 chest CT scans were performed in PR-ED/OPD settings. Among these, 39,366 scans qualified for quantitative analysis. Pneumonia-positive CT scans were identified in 8,955 cases (22.7%), while 30,411 scans showed no pneumonia findings, yielding an overall pneumonia prevalence of 29% (Figure 2). The proportion of pneumonia-positive CT scans and estimated pneumonia patient volume are presented in Figure 4, Table 4. Both indicators demonstrated parallel trends: a sharp increase from Week 51, 2022 (November 19, 2022), followed by a significant decline starting Week 5, 2023 (January 30, 2023). A transient rebound occurred at Week 13, 2023 (March 27, 2023) before subsequent reduction two weeks later.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that the proportion of QCT-defined pneumonia and the number of pneumonia cases can reflect fluctuations in the proportion and number of pneumonia cases. During the COVID-19 pandemic, the number of pneumonia cases estimated by QCT in PR-ED/OPD at this hospital showed strong correlations with: (I) the number of pneumonia cases admitted to inpatient wards at this hospital; (II) the number of pneumonia-related deaths in the region; and (III) the proportion of patients with ILI and testing positive for COVID-19 reported by sentinel hospitals.

For infectious diseases such as COVID-19, although strict infectious disease reporting systems have been established, the reported number of COVID-19 cases from the hospitals showed no statistically significant changes during outbreak peaks. This suggests that specialized infectious disease reporting systems can become overwhelmed during epidemics, potentially leading to data distortion. Similarly, the nationally reported number of COVID-19 infections was paradoxically decreased after the inception of the “the 10-point measures” at December 7, 2022. Potential reasons include limited testing capacity, reduced accessibility to healthcare services due to healthcare resource strain, decreased completeness of clinical assessments due to fatigue, and reporting delays caused by excessive workload. Similarly, pneumonia-related outpatient and emergency department visits, as well as the number of patients hospitalized for pneumonia, are constrained by healthcare service capacity and may also exhibit data distortion during outbreaks.

Since death registration and cause-of-death certification must be completed shortly after a patient’s demise, the number of pneumonia-related deaths (rather than deaths from a specific type of pneumonia) in a region accurately reflects the burden and severity of pneumonia within that region and period, and is unaffected by healthcare service availability. Indicators such as the proportion of patients with ILI and the proportion testing positive for COVID-19 among patients visiting sentinel hospitals, obtained through sampling surveys, are less susceptible to healthcare resource strain and are relatively accurate.

In this study, the number of pneumonia-related deaths in the region and the proportion of patients with ILI testing positive for COVID-19 at sentinel hospitals were used as primary reference metrics to evaluate the performance of using QCT-defined pneumonia proportions and case counts from a given hospital to estimate pneumonia incidence.

The results demonstrate that fluctuations in the proportion and number of QCT-defined pneumonia cases during COVID-19 outbreaks corresponded with the number of pneumonia-related deaths in the region and the proportion of sentinel hospital patients with ILI who tested positive for COVID-19.

The proportion of ILI among sentinel hospital patients and the COVID-19 positivity rate primarily reflect the case count of COVID-19 as an infectious disease, but not its severity. The number of pneumonia-related deaths reflects the most severe outcomes of pneumonia. In contrast, the proportion and number of QCT-defined pneumonia cases reflect pneumonia incidence and capture the combined effect of both the incidence rate and severity of respiratory infectious diseases like COVID-19.

Unlike mortality from pneumonia, which exhibits a considerable lag behind disease onset, the proportion and volume of pneumonia detected by QCT (QCT-PP/PV) can be obtained on the day of patient presentation, and the data are readily standardized. Consequently, QCT-PP/PV provides a more timely reflection of the pneumonia disease burden.

Current methods for monitoring respiratory infectious diseases include: the Notifiable Diseases Surveillance System (NDSS), the ILI surveillance system, the hospitalized severe acute respiratory infection (SARI) case surveillance system, and the unexplained pneumonia surveillance system.

The NDSS is widely used for monitoring various infectious diseases. Examples include the National NDSS (NNDSS) in the USA and Australia (36), China’s Nationwide Notifiable Infectious Diseases Reporting Information System (NIDRIS), and its rapid response mechanism, the China Infectious Disease Automated-alert and Response System (CIDARS) (37). Notifiable disease reporting and surveillance systems encompass patients with both confirmed and suspected/clinical diagnoses of infectious diseases. However, the reported incidence is closely tied to diagnostic criteria. Newly emerging infectious diseases require a process of scientific understanding, during which diagnostic criteria undergo multiple adjustments and refinements. When diagnostic criteria are imperfect or testing accessibility is suboptimal, the actual incidence may be significantly underestimated. Additionally, notifiable disease reporting systems rely on the active reporting and review by healthcare workers and administrators, representing a considerable workload. Healthcare workers may actively avoid diagnosing and reporting certain notifiable diseases. Underreporting rates can increase significantly even for well-understood diseases with adequate diagnostic tools during outbreaks.

ILI and Hospitalized SARI Surveillance Systems were designed to monitor influenza and unknown respiratory infectious diseases, widely utilized globally, later expanded to include surveillance of COVID-19 (38). Numerous case definitions for ILI and SARI exist, mainly centering on fever, respiratory symptoms, and the need for hospitalization. In 2011, World Health Organization (WHO) revised the ILI definition as: an acute respiratory illness with fever (measured temperature of ≥38 °C) and cough, with onset within the past 10 days (39). The SARI definition is: an acute respiratory illness with a history of fever or measured fever of ≥38 °C and cough, with onset within the past 10 days, requiring hospitalization (39). Within China’s influenza surveillance system, sentinel hospitals report weekly counts and proportions of ILI cases from outpatient and emergency departments. Respiratory specimens are collected from a subset of ILI patients or suspected ILI/SARI patients for etiological testing (40). However, the ILI surveillance system also faces significant limitations: early symptoms of many respiratory infectious diseases, such as COVID-19 (41), closely resemble those of seasonal influenza and common upper respiratory infections. Given the extremely high incidence of the latter (42), changes in the symptom spectrum within the population caused by outbreaks of novel respiratory pathogens are easily masked by these common illnesses, resulting in a low SNR. Consequently, cases of severe respiratory infectious diseases can be obscured within this background “noise”. Before a novel pathogen is adequately identified, ILI is difficult to reflect the true disease burden of emerging respiratory infections. A prerequisite for SARI identification is patient admission to a ward. During outbreaks of respiratory infectious diseases, limited healthcare resources often prevent the timely admission of many patients, thereby excluding them from SARI case counts. Furthermore, the reporting of both ILI and SARI cases is subject to physicians’ clinical judgment and discretionary action, leading to a very high rate of underreporting (43).

The Pneumonia of Unknown Etiology (PUE) surveillance system was established to facilitate early detection of emerging and reemerging infectious diseases (ERID), such as SARS and human avian influenza. Since 2004, the China CDC has implemented nationwide PUE surveillance. The diagnostic criteria for PUE cases comprise: (I) fever (axillary temperature ≥38 °C); (II) radiographic evidence of pneumonia; (III) decreased or normal total leukocyte count, or reduced lymphocyte count in early disease stage; (IV) inability to attribute the pneumonia to alternative clinical or laboratory diagnoses (44). However, PUE diagnosis relies on clinicians’ subjective judgment, resulting in inter-observer variability. Cases meeting PUE criteria require active reporting by clinicians and subsequent expert panel review, leading to substantial underreporting in practice (45).

QCT surveillance for pneumonia offers several advantages over conventional infectious disease surveillance methods. QCT detects pneumonia without relying on etiological diagnoses, enabling direct screening for SARIs—including novel respiratory diseases. Pneumonia identified through QCT reflects clinically significant manifestations of respiratory infections with high SNRs. Moreover, QCT provides objective diagnostic criteria unaffected by physician interpretation or administrative protocol modifications. QCT assessment of pneumonia incidence is technically straightforward to implement and does not require physician involvement in the evaluation process. Once deployed, the system operates efficiently even during infectious disease outbreaks with high stability, eliminating impacts from subjective clinical judgment or operator experience.

By converting radiological findings into epidemiological data, hospitals or radiology departments can function as real-time sensors with geographic resolution for population health (18). Aggregating these data across local, regional, and national levels supplements existing disease surveillance tools and informs health resource allocation.

The number of pneumonia cases identified by QCT, when utilized in one or several hospitals, can serve as sentinel surveillance for pneumonia, effectively tracking trends in pneumonia incidence rates. At full regional hospital coverage, it establishes comprehensive routine monitoring for estimating pneumonia incidence rates (46). Automated flagging of QCT-identified pneumonia cases linked with clinical data can facilitate PUE detection, and linkage with patient residence or registration addresses can identify clustered cases.

On the other hand, in China, especially in Shanghai, chest CT scans are inexpensive and easily accessible. The cost of a chest CT is less than 250 RMB. Almost all patients suspected of having pneumonia undergo a chest CT scan within a short period.

Limitations

Pneumonia or pneumonitis detected radiographically encompasses a broad spectrum of diseases, including pneumonia caused by various pathogens, autoimmune factors, other physical/chemical factors, and idiopathic pneumonitis. While QCT cannot accurately differentiate between these etiologies, the incidence of pneumonia attributable to non-communicable pathogens and pneumonitis caused by non-infectious factors remains relatively stable. Consequently, observing changes in the overall incidence of pneumonia can still reflect fluctuations in the incidence and severity of acute respiratory infections, such as COVID-19. Calculating pneumonia incidence stratified by age groups would better reflect the burden of pneumonia caused by respiratory infectious diseases. Further distinguishing pneumonia types based on QCT features such as density and morphology holds promise for improved effectiveness.

Due to its irradiating nature, CT inevitably involves radiation exposure, which limits its use in vulnerable populations such as pregnant women and children. Some emerging imaging modalities, such as magnetic resonance elastography (MRE), are sensitive in monitoring lung stiffness—increasing with the severity of COVID-19 symptoms and decreasing during recovery—and may play an important role in disease diagnosis and monitoring (47).

In some low-resourced settings, access to CT scans may be limited, and the cost may be relatively high for some impoverished patients.

Conclusions

CT-derived quantitative metrics related to pneumonia demonstrate good diagnostic sensitivity and specificity for pneumonia. The established diagnostic thresholds are: total CT-quantified lung infection volume ≥65 mL and CT-quantified lung infection volume proportion ≥1.75%. QCT enables the tracking of pneumonia incidence, reflecting the severity of severe respiratory infectious disease outbreaks. During the COVID-19 outbreak, the CT-quantified pneumonia incidence exhibited a significant epidemiological correlation with both the COVID-19 case positivity rate in sentinel hospitals and local pneumonia-related mortality.
