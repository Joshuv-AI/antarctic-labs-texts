Efficacy of Prehabilitation in cancer patients: an Rcts systematic review with meta-analysis
Authors: Daniele Gennuso, Angela Baldelli, Loredana Gigli, Ilaria Ruotolo, Giovanni Galeoto, Daniela Gaburri, Giovanni Sellitto
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients with cancer (PwC) who undergo specific treatments reported greater fatigue and reduced functional capacity as predominant outcomes, compromising their QoL during and following the treatment. Prehabilitation intervention, provided after diagnosis and before treatments, is to optimize the physiological reserve and address modifiable risk factors before surgery or chemotherapy to improve post-treatment results. The primary aim of this study is to obtain a common line of efficacy compared with prehabilitation treatment; the secondary endpoint is to assess the methodological quality of the studies eligible in the review.MethodsThe systematic review was conducted from September to February 2024, in accordance with PRISMA guidelines. Databases consulted were MEDLINE, Scopus, Web of science and CINAHL, RCTs related to Prehabilitation intervention on PwC were included. The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed through the RoB2 Cochrane tool and the PEDro scale. Meta-analysis was performed to estimate relative treatment effects among evaluated outcomes.ResultsForty-two studies were included in the systematic review, 13 were included in the quantitative analysis. The results of the studies reporting reduced postoperative hospital stay, improved endurance, muscle strength, respiratory function, quality of life, and urinary incontinence. Critical analysis of the articles using the PEDro scale revealed 28 RCTs with a good rating, 9 with a fair rating, and 5 with a poor rating; in contrast, the Cochrane RoB2 tool revealed that all articles were at high risk of bias. Meta-analysis showed statistically significant values for 6MWT (38.53, 95%CI 33.03 - 44.04); HADS-depression (-0.71, 95%CI -0.93 -0.49) and HADS-anxiety (-0.49, 95%CI -0.76 -0.23).ConclusionsPrehabilitation represents a specific intervention that aims to improve postoperative outcomes in fragile patients undergoing surgery, increasing their preoperative physiological reserve in anticipation of the stress they will face and facilitating the postoperative recovery of functional capacity. Prehabilitation is a good intervention to use, especially in terms of functional capacity and mental health, the latter being very impactful in terms of reduced levels of anxiety and depression. These data make it possible to justify supportive intervention by physical therapists aimed at improving and restoring health-related QoL especially in the short term.Keywords: Prehabilitation, Physiotherapy, People with Cancer, Systematic Review, Rehabilitation Outcome Measures

Background

The European Commission published the Europe’s Beating Cancer Plan [1], based on the knowledge that in 2020, 2.7 million people in the European Union were diagnosed with cancer and 1.3 million people lost their lives to it. Unless decisive action is taken now, cancer cases are set to increase by 24% by 2035, becoming the leading cause of death in the EU.

It is estimated that there are over 12 million cancer survivors in Europe, including around 300,000 childhood cancer survivors, thanks to advances in early diagnosis and supportive care.

In this scenario it is necessary to develop the best care for people with cancer (PwC) and ensure patients receive the supportive care needed to improve their quality of life (QoL) before, during and after cancer treatment.

Indeed, cancer and its treatment often lead to physical deconditioning and, therefore, a decrease in QoL. The immediate and long-term consequences of cancer treatment, including deconditioning, fatigue, impaired bone quality, altered body composition, depression, and anxiety, are well documented [2–4].

In most cases, PwC who undergo specific treatments, such as surgery, chemotherapy or radiotherapy, report greater fatigue and reduced functional capacity as predominant outcomes, compromising their QoL during and following the treatment.

There is growing evidence of the benefits of exercise on cancer-related health outcomes, such as improved survival, less fatigue, better physical functioning, and improved QoL [5–7].

Physiotherapists, as healthcare professionals, may be ideally placed to assess and treat patients in acute hospital settings as well as in the community and provide the specialist assessments and interventions needed by many cancer patients [8].

Rehabilitation intervention can occur at various stages along the continuum of cancer care.

This work specifically aims to focus on Prehabilitation, provided after diagnosis and before treatments; the role of the Prehabilitation intervention is to optimize the physiological reserve and address modifiable risk factors before surgery or chemotherapy in order to improve post-treatment results [9].

Prehabilitation is an intervention that enable PwC to improve their physical and mental health [10, 11]. The main components of a Prehabilitation program are: cardiovascular training, strength, nutritional management, well-being with psychological support and medical optimization [12].

Whereas there are a few studies on the value of Prehabilitation in PwC even if some evidence is emerging, we aimed to perform a systematic review with meta-analysis to determine the role of Prehabilitation exercise as a means to improve physical condition and QoL in PwC.

The main purpose of this study is to integrate the results of empirical studies to provide an aggregate summary of the results, in order to obtain statistically precise and accurate conclusions on the effectiveness of this type of treatment.

The secondary outcome is to assess methodological quality of the studies to support Pre-habilitation in the anti-tumor therapy guidelines, evaluating all the effects that it can produce in PwC.

Methods

Protocol and registration

The study protocol has not yet been accepted by PROSPERO, so the request ID is available at the following link: https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/prospero.

This review was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses) statement, made of 27 items based on the Methodological Expectations of Cochrane Intervention Review (MECIR) standards [13, 14].

Eligibility criteria: types of studies, participants and interventions

All available studies which included the keywords “prehabilitation” OR “prehab” OR “pre-operative rehabilitation” OR “peri-operative rehabilitation” AND “oncology patients” OR “cancer patients” OR “patients with cancer” OR “cancer” AND “randomized controlled trial” OR “randomized controlled trial” OR “rct” have been included; no restrictions have been applied regarding the publication period or the country in which the study was conducted.

No filters were applied to avoid the drop of potentially interesting documents.

The elegibility criteria, as specified by the PICOS framework, were as follows:

Outcomes: The primary outcomes considered in this study were all those referred to patients' functional capacity, such as motor capacity, cardiopulmonary capacity, and prostate capacity; referred to mental health status, such as anxiety and depression control; and referred to quality of care and hospitalization, as well as for pain and QoL. Exclusion criteria included studies in which the prehabilitation intervention was continued after the start of the first cancer-specific treatment and studies in which there were no rehabilitation outcomes.

Search methods

Study selection was conducted from September to December 2023, with the latest literature search conducted on December 30th, 2023.

The databases used to identify studies for inclusion were PubMed (via MEDLINE), Scopus, Web of Science, CINAHL (via EBSCO).

All included studies were identified by two reviewers.

Study selection

After removing duplicate studies from the databases, their selection was conducted through two screenings performed in parallel by two reviewers.

The first screening was carried out through the analysis of the title and abstract, applying the eligibility criteria reported above. Subsequently, a second screening was carried out through full text analysis.

A final list of studies eligible for inclusion was compiled and any disagreements were resolved by a third reviewer or by consensus.

All articles included in the review were found from the start in the databases used and passed all screening stages; however, a process of “reference checking” and “citation tracking” was carried out to search for additional studies that met the review's eligibility criteria.

Risk of bias and data extraction

To assess the risk of bias (RoB), the Cochrane RoB2 tool [15] and the PEDro scale [16] were used.

Critical analysis was conducted in parallel by two reviewers and any disagreement was resolved by a third reviewer or by consensus.

The following data were extracted from the selected studies: author, year of publication, characteristics of the sample, type of treatment in the intervention group and in the control group, frequency of treatment, the outcome investigated and the respective results, both of the main outcomes and of the follow-up.

Meta-analysis

The meta-analysis was conducted on studies that reported comparable follow-ups for comparable outcomes (reported as mean and standard deviation) and at least two studies were combined where possible. Data analysis was carried out with RevMan Web, a software developed by the Cochrane Collaboration, a free statistical software [17]. Results were combined using fixed and random effects models (DerSimonian-Laird method). Heterogeneity between studies was assessed with the I2 statistic, which represents the percentage of variance due to heterogeneity rather than chance and is not sensitive to the number of studies involved. I2 values ranged from 0 to 100% and were interpreted as low-moderate if less than 50% [18]. The results and heterogeneity obtained were summarized in tables using the “forest plot” graph [19], in which the RoB of the included studies is also reported.

Results

Search results

The research on PubMed, CINHAL, Web of Science and Scopus databases, identified 584 articles; after removing duplicate items, 410 articles were found.

A first screening consisted in reading title and abstract; the items which didn’t fullfill the inclusion criteria were dropped out; most of them was not RCTs.

A second screening consisted in reading the full text of the 92 items remained from the first screening; 47 were further excluded according to the same criteria as the first screening; 4 more items were not included due to the unavailability of the full text. At the end of the screening 42 articles were included in the review, one study was included in our review as part of the “reference checking” and “citation tracking” process, ensuring a comprehensive assessment of the relevant literature; 13 of these were included in the meta-analysis (Fig. 1).

Study characteristics: types of design and partecipants

The included studies are RCTs featuring a Prehabilitation as experimental groups and standard habits or pre-filled programs not supervised by rehabilitation professionals as controls.

The studies have quite heterogeneous characteristics, even if it is possible to identify the main tumor sites included and the different types of intervention. Among the selected studies, the most represented tumor site is the lung in 38% of the studies, followed by colorectal cancer in 27% of the studies. Prostate cancer is also represented in a good percentage (18%); while a lower presence is noted for esophageal cancer (5%); bladder, pancreatic, breast, stomach, and liver cancer, all with equal representation (2%). Only one of the studies reported several tumors, such as colorectal, thoracic, hepatobiliary or urological (2%).

The interventions that constitute the Prehabilitation can be represented as follows:

Moderate aerobic and resistance exercises, muscle strengthening or mobility exercises, in a professionally supervised or unsupervised setting at home: 39% (16 articles)

Moderate aerobic and resistance exercises, along with specific breathing exercises: 24% (10 articles)

As regards the pre-habilitation sessions, they lasted up to 2–3 days before the intervention, on average between 2 and 4 weeks, while the frequency of the interventions varied between 2 and 4 times a week.

The most frequently reported outcomes are represented by the measurement of functional capacity through the 6 Minutes Walking Test (6MWT) and other commonly known and used tests such as the Time Up and Go (TUG), Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB); also considered is the self-reported energy expenditure with the Community Healthy Activities Model Program for Seniors (CHAMPS) questionnaire and other specific tests and scales investigating breathing capacity and functionality of the urogenital system, as well as the days of post-operative hospitalization (LoS) and post-operative complications through the Clavien-Dindo Classification (CDC). QoL is investigated with the 36-Item Short Form Health Survey (SF-36), and other peculiar symptoms of tumor pathology such as fatigue. Conditions such as anxiety and depression reported through the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale questionnaire (HADS) are also examined.

The results of the studies were varied, indeed the experimental groups there was a reduction in post-operative hospital stay, an improvement in resistance, muscle strength, respiratory function, QoL and urinary incontinence. Meta-analysis

A quantitative analysis was carried out comparing the outcomes and follow-ups. There were eight comparable outcomes: 6MWT [62], Mental Health Short Form-36 (MH SF-36) [63], Physical functioning Short Form-36 (PF SF-36), LOS, HADS-anxiety and HADS-depression [64], International Prostate Symptom Score (IPSS) [65], CDC [66].

There are thirteen studies considered in the meta-analysis that have comparable follow-ups 4 weeks post-intervention. The results were performed for all outcomes.

Four studies reported 6MWT, the meta-analysis revealed statistically significant results (p < 0.00001) in favour of the experimental group compared to the control group (mean difference = 38.53, 95% confidence interval (CI) = 33.03 – 44.04) (Fig. 3).

“Forest plot” of PF SF-36 outcome at four weeks after surgery

Discussion

Prehabilitation intervention, although described as a rehabilitative phase from diagnosis to first treatment, whether surgical or chemo-radiotherapy, is not described within medical protocols. This is because the chemo-radiotherapy intervention probably starts very close to the diagnosis and does not give, in election, the opportunity to set up any interventions.

Prehabilitation is identified as the phase which aims to improve the functional and physiological capabilities of patients, even before they undergo surgery, in order to preserve peri-operative homeostasis and facilitate subsequent recovery [24]. It is important, therefore, to consider the presence of modifiable factors in the preoperative period, elements which are associated, moreover, with morbidity and complications after surgery. These factors can be linked to clinical aspects, such as cardiorespiratory capacity, or to other psychophysical aspects such as health-related QoL [26]. In most cases, patients with cancer who undergo specific treatments, report greater fatigue and reduced functional capacity as predominant outcomes, compromising their QoL during and following the treatment intervention itself [36, 46].

Most of the studies regarding Prehabilitation were conducted on people with lung cancer, in particular non-small cell lung cancer. This type of tumor represents the most frequent form of neoplasia and with the highest mortality rate in developed countries [57]. The elective treatment is surgery, often with a lung resection [46, 52, 57], which, due to pathophysiological changes as well as anesthesia, immobilization of the patient and abnormal diaphragmatic function, leads to postoperative complications such as prolonged air leak (PAL), pain [52, 57], hypoxia, hyperventilation and dyspnea [27, 46].

Even in the absence of specific respiratory complications, lung cancer surgery is often associated with significant reductions in functional capacity, considered to be a strong predictor of further complications, mortality and long-term survival [21, 37]. The evidence reports results that are not always in agreement with each other. Some studies demonstrate how carrying out a Prehabilitation intervention, with aerobic or resistance exercises, together with specific respiratory therapy, is effective in preventing the post-operative complications that most commonly occur [27, 30, 45, 52, 57] and improving the QoL before and after the intervention [46].

Colorectal cancer surgery has improved thanks to technological and healthcare development, the complications and risks associated with this operation are still high [22, 39], in particular the presence of pain and difficult gastrointestinal recovery [31]. The age and frailty of patients, together with a condition of malnutrition and sarcopenia, as well as low cardiorespiratory capacity, are just some of the factors associated with worse postoperative outcomes. Prehabilitation intervention as it allows for minor post-operative complications, improving peri-operative physical conditions and decreasing the loss of functional capacity in the period immediately after the operation [22, 34, 35, 39]. A very important element that is reported is that a prehabilitation program allows you to improve the QoL immediately after surgery, as well as speed up the recovery of gastrointestinal function [31].

In cases of prostate cancer, the complications reported following surgery do not correspond to significant morbidity, just as the mortality rate detected is relatively low [28]. The adverse effects that subjects with prostate cancer often experience after surgery are urinary incontinence [54], a condition that can continue even 1 year after surgery, sexual dysfunction and reduced functional capacity; all elements that have a significant impact on the QoL of patients and their post-operative social reintegration [28, 49–51]. A Prehabilitation intervention allows the severity of incontinence to be reduced, although no differences were observed in a global assessment 6 months after the operation compared to subjects who did not carry out any intervention before surgery [51]. It is also reported that Prehabilitation intervention does not actually lead to any difference in the postoperative period [50], but in addition to the specific symptom, that is indicated as an effective intervention in improving muscle strength and functional capacity, together with the reduction of the perception of fatigue before surgery, an aspect that helps to mitigate postoperative outcomes [28, 49, 54].

An element analyzed in this review is the frailty of PwC undergoing surgery. The number of operations in elderly patients is reduced due to frailty related to advanced age, characterized by a reduction in energy and muscle strength, as well as weight loss and increased sedentary lifestyle [53], just as it happens in degenerative diseases [67, 68]; factors that lead to an increase in the vulnerability of these subjects whose health is compromised by the presence of a tumor. This fragility is linked to an increase in postoperative complications, the recovery time of which translates into an increase in hospital stay [20]. Prehabilitation represents a specific intervention that aims to improve postoperative outcomes in fragile patients undergoing surgery, increasing their preoperative physiological reserve in anticipation of the stress they will face and facilitating the postoperative recovery of functional capacity [20, 50].

A consideration that is raised by some studies is the duration of the Prehabilitation intervention. In most of the studies that have investigated the effectiveness of this phase, a program of approximately four weeks has been introduced and recommended, but this choice has been called into question by the possibility that the long duration of the intervention reduces patient compliance, decreasing the acceptability and adherence to the proposed program [24], or more simply, a session of only seven days was proposed for ease of application and tolerance by patients [52]. Although this idea is supported by some studies, more frequently the Prehabilitation intervention group followed a program lasting more than three weeks, and it is observed that this element is not always taken into consideration in determining the programming of the intervention.

In studies in which this aspect is addressed, the question remains whether the frequency of intervention sessions is more important than the intensity of the sessions themselves [36]. Considering the results reported overall in the review, Prehabilitation appears to be an effective intervention in determining fewer postoperative complications, improving functional capacity assessed in the perioperative period.

Finally, it is interesting to report the only study in the review that has as patients women with breast cancer awaiting mastectomy; in this case, prehabilitation was characterized by static stretching exercises of the shoulder girdle muscles while the control group received routine post-operative care. The surgery proved to be safe and effective in reducing postoperative pain and functional deficit of the shoulder joint.

The poverty of studies regarding prehabilitation associated with breast cancer encourages further exploration of this area in light of both the encouraging results of this single study and the results of this review which highlighted how prehabilitation can allow PwC to improve their physical and mental health.

Conclusions

The result shows that Prehabilitation is a good intervention to use in PwC, with visible results one month after the intervention, especially in terms of functional capacity and mental health, the latter of which appears to be very important through the reduction of levels of anxiety and depression [69].

These data make it possible to justify a supportive intervention performed by physiotherapists, aiming to improve and restore health-related QoL in the short term, promoting patient-centred care.

These results show that physiotherapists, as healthcare professionals, should be included as members of the cancer patient's care planning team since the first phase of the disease, because it is the very point that one can aim to optimize the physiological reserve and to address modifiable risk factors before surgery or chemotherapy, in order to improve post-treatment results.

Limitations of the study

A first consideration can be made regarding the choice of the PEDro scale as a tool for evaluating the quality of studies, the reliability of which is questioned in the literature. This limit is contained by the fact that greater relevance was given to the RoB2 results in the interpretation of the results.

Another limitation may be dictated by the fact that the quantitative analyzes carried out, as is good practice, were not stratified by methodological quality of the studies as overall they are all of low quality, for this reason we recommended paying attention on the individual domains of RoB2.

Even if it is a limitation attributable to the nature of the studies, a very close follow-up was taken into consideration in the meta-analysis, it would be appropriate to evaluate the outcomes even at more distant follow-ups.

To conclude, we did not take publication bias into account [70] even though recent literature states that the inspection and analysis of funnel plots cannot reliably identify publication and reporting bias. Researchers should instead thoroughly and systematically search available sources of information such as databases, registers and unpublished reports and even then, it is not possible to know whether a systematic review has discovered all available studies [71].
