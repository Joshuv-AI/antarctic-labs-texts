Do white matter hyperintensities account for the relationship between discrimination and memory?
Authors: Robrielle M. Pierce, Kiana A. Scambray, Jordan D. Palms, Monica Walters, Ketlyne Sol, Noah Green, Sanaa Stough Lacking, Mohamad Alshiko, Adam Brickman, Laura B. Zahodne
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONRacial discrimination (RD) relates to poorer memory, but underlying mechanisms are unclear.METHODSParticipants included 321 Black adults age 55+ from the Michigan Cognitive Aging Project. RD was operationalized with the Major Experiences of Lifetime Discrimination scale. Subjective memory was measured with the Memory Functioning Questionnaire. Episodic memory was a composite of list learning, story memory, and visual memory tests. White matter hyperintensities (WMHs) were quantified from T2 fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) images. Mediation models examined whether WMHs mediate the associations of racial discrimination on subjective and objective memory.RESULTSMore RD was associated with higher WMH volume. In turn, higher WMH volume was associated with worse objective, but not subjective, memory. Independent of WMH, more RD was associated with worse subjective, but not objective, memory.DISCUSSIONRD is an important intervention target to improve cognitive health equity and reduce WMH burden. RD may influence objective memory through vascular and/or inflammatory mechanisms.

BACKGROUND

Black older adults are approximately twice as likely to develop Alzheimer's disease and related dementias (ADRD) compared to White older adults. 1 Socioeconomic and health differences only partly explain this disparity, 2 suggesting that additional mechanisms may contribute. There is a critical need to identify specific mechanisms that underlie increased risk among Black older adults.

Racial discrimination (RD) is a distinct form of chronic social stress reported by Black individuals across institutional and interpersonal domains, including education, employment, housing, and the criminal justice system. 3 It can lead to negative mental and physical health outcomes 4 and may contribute to dementia risk. Greater discrimination is associated with worse episodic memory, 5 and diminished episodic memory is an early symptom of ADRD. 6

Beyond objective memory tests, the recent literature highlights the importance of capturing memory changes through subjective memory concerns. Subjective memory concerns are associated with poorer objective memory cross‐sectionally 7 , 8 and longitudinally. 9 , 10 , 11 Subjective memory concerns are a risk factor for the development of future cognitive decline and AD. 12 , 13 Subjective and objective memory changes subsequently influence one another, 14 which makes studying both constructs important for capturing early cognitive changes. Although prior work linked RD to poorer subjective cognition, 15 , 16 less is known about its direct influence on subjective memory once disaggregated from subjective cognition.

Depressive symptoms, vascular conditions, and systemic inflammation are potential mediators of the relationship between RD and cognition. 17 , 18 However, less is known about potential brain mediators. White matter hyperintensities (WMHs) are areas of increased signal on T2‐weighted and fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) sequence. 19 WMHs of presumed vascular origin are a neuroradiological sign of cerebral small vessel disease, which can increase risk of cognitive impairment and dementia. 20 , 21 Given the higher prevalence of vascular risk factors in Black adults, 22 they experience greater total WMH burden compared to similarly aged White individuals. 23 , 24 , 25 , 26

The link between vascular risk factors and WMH burden may be due to RD. Clark and colleagues proposed a biopsychosocial model detailing the effects of perceived racism in African Americans. 27 This model posits that perceived racism leads to psychological and physiological stress responses, which then increases the risk of negative health outcomes. In line with this framework, RD has been associated with faster WMH accumulation, 28 , 29 which may exacerbate existing health disparities. WMHs are also associated with greater subjective memory concerns 30 , 31 and poorer objective episodic memory. 32 , 33 Given this, RD may contribute to the disproportionate rates of vascular and WMH burden, which in turn may explain differential cognitive outcomes among Black populations. 34

Building on the theoretical framework of Clark and colleagues, 27 we propose that WMHs may reflect physiological responses to RD that can undermine memory functioning. Taken together, this cross‐sectional study investigated WMHs as a potential underlying mechanism between RD and both subjective memory concerns and objective episodic memory. We hypothesized that more reports of lifetime RD would be associated with greater WMHs, and that greater WMHs in turn would be associated with greater subjective memory concerns and poorer objective episodic memory in Black individuals transitioning into older adulthood.

METHODS

Participants and procedures

Participants in the present study were enrolled in the Michigan Cognitive Aging Project (MCAP) at the University of Michigan. MCAP is a community‐based, prospective study that evaluates modifiable factors of cognitive aging associated with racial disparities in ADRD risk among a racially balanced sample of non‐Hispanic White and non‐Hispanic Black individuals. Individuals residing in Southeast Michigan, 60–65 years of age, were recruited as an address‐based sample through direct mail using voter registration lists in tandem with sociodemographic data from the 2010 U.S. census. Additional participants who were at least 55 years of age enrolled in the study via word‐of‐mouth. Geographic areas with high proportions of non‐Hispanic Black residents were oversampled to achieve equal‐sized subsamples of non‐Hispanic Black and non‐Hispanic White participants. Participants were included if they were age 55 or older, possessed fluency in English, lived in Wayne or Washtenaw county, and had no diagnosis of ADRD from a doctor at study enrollment. Assessments were completed in‐person at offices in Ann Arbor, MI; Detroit, MI; Dearborn, MI; or in participants’ homes. Visits included questionnaires on demographic information, psychosocial health, and physical health, in addition to objective physical measurements, a comprehensive neuropsychological battery, and blood collection. Individuals from the core study who are eligible and willing to undergo an MRI are recruited to participate, and transportation is provided if necessary. The current study included cross‐sectional data from 2017 to 2024 on discrimination, memory, and WMHs for 321 non‐Hispanic Black MCAP participants. All procedures were approved by the University of Michigan Institutional Review Board corresponding to the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants completed separate written informed consent for core study procedures and MRI procedures.

Measures

RD. Experiences of discrimination were assessed using the Major Experiences of Lifetime Discrimination 35 scale. The Major Experiences of Lifetime Discrimination scale includes nine dichotomous items that assess perceived occurrence of unfair treatment about major life events. The items ask individuals whether they experienced or believe to have experienced major discriminatory events over their lifespan, including questions such as “At any time in your life, have you ever been unfairly fired from a job?” and “Have you ever been unfairly stopped, searched, questioned, physically threatened or abused by the police?” All nine items where either “Race” or “skin color” were endorsed as an attribution were summed so that higher scores represent a higher frequency of major life events of RD.

WMH. The methods have been described elsewhere. 36 WMH volume was quantified using in‐house software. FLAIR images were conformed to a uniform 256 × 256 × 256 volume with 1 mm3 voxels, reoriented to Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI)152 space, skull‐stripped, 37 and bias‐field corrected, followed by histogram normalization to a 0–255 grayscale range. 37 White matter masks were derived using atlas‐based segmentation (FreeSurfer Labels 2 and 41) 38 and refined using morphological operations to fill internal holes. Data‐driven percentile thresholds were extracted from each image's intensity histogram to initialize a two‐component Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) with Expectation‐Maximization, where the two components represent WMH signal and other high‐intensity white matter voxels (vessels, partial volume effects). Posterior probabilities for each voxel determined classification likelihood of belonging to the WMH component. Robert's edge detection 39 was applied to refine segmentation and reduce false positives along cortical boundaries. Final WMH volume was calculated by summing classified voxels and converting to cm3.

Subjective memory concerns. Subjective memory was surveyed using the 10‐item brief version of the Memory Functioning Questionnaire (MFQ). 40 The 10‐item version is composed of the Frequency of Forgetting subscale and Retrospective Functioning subscale. The Frequency of Forgetting subscale includes items that assess perceived memory problems, including questions such as “How often do the following present a problem for you?” and lists names, faces, and so forth. Responses are on a 7‐point Likert Scale ranging from Always (1) to Never (7). Items were reverse coded so that higher scores reflect more negative perception of one's memory functioning. The Retrospective Functioning subscale includes items that inquire how well individuals can remember things in the past at different time scales now compared to a time before. The questions ask, “How well you remember things which occurred between 1 and 5 years ago?” and “How well you remember things which occurred between 6 and 10 years ago?” Responses are on a 7‐point Likert Scale Ranging from Very Bad (1) to Very Good (7). Items were reverse coded so that higher scores reflect negative views of one's ability to remember things from the past (i.e., more subjective memory concerns). The MFQ subscales were evaluated together and were calculated by averaging the 10 items for analyses.

Objective Memory. A comprehensive neuropsychological assessment is used in MCAP. The present study focuses on episodic memory because it is one of the most sensitive domains of ADRD. 6 Based on a previous confirmatory factor analysis with MCAP, 41 episodic memory included a modified version of the Consortium to Establish a Registry for Alzheimer's Disease (CERAD) word list task (immediate, delayed, and recognition trials), Craft Story 21 (immediate and delayed trials), and the Benson Complex Figure task (delayed recognition trial). Scores on each were standardized into z‐scores and averaged to create an episodic memory composite score. Higher scores on the episodic memory composite correspond with better performance.

Covariates

Age, gender, and education were used as covariates in all mediation models because they could potentially confound associations between RD, WMHs, and cognition. Age was a continuous variable. Gender was self‐reported and coded as a dichotomous variable. Education was a continuous variable corresponding to years of educational attainment (0 to 17).

Statistical analyses

Descriptive statistics were computed using RStudio version 2024.9.0.375 and missing data were handled through the missForest package. Two mediation models were used to compute the total, direct, and indirect effects of the lifetime RD exposure on (1) subjective memory concerns and (2) objective episodic memory via WMHs, controlling for covariates (i.e., age, gender, education). These models were conducted using the PROCESS macro for RStudio, which measures estimates through 95% percentile bootstrap confidence intervals and 5000 bootstrap iterations. Total WMH volume was right‐skewed and was log transformed for analyses.

Statistical assumptions for mediation models were assessed. First, we tested the exposure–mediator interaction by including an interaction term between lifetime RD and WMHs. There was no significant exposure–mediator interaction for subjective memory concerns (p = 0.530) or objective episodic memory (p = 0.099), which suggests that associations between WMHs and these outcomes do not depend on lifetime RD. Although sequential ignorability cannot be directly tested, we attempted to minimize potential confounding by adjusting for age, sex, and education.

Figure 2 summarizes the mediation model for objective memory. Lifetime RD was not associated with objective memory (total effect b = –0.01; 95% CI = –0.058 to 0.029). DISCUSSION

Extending the literature on discrimination and subjective cognition, we found that more lifetime RD was associated with greater subjective memory concerns, but this association was not mediated by WMHs. In line with hypotheses, more RD was associated with increased WMHs, and more WMHs were associated with worse objective memory. These findings suggest that WMH influence the relationship between RD and objective memory but not subjective memory concerns.

Lifetime RD and WMH

Our finding that more lifetime RD was associated with greater WMH volume aligns with previous research. 28 , 29 This builds on adjacent work demonstrating that RD is negatively associated with other indicators of white matter health. 42 , 43 Taken together, this suggests that individuals who experience racism may be at elevated risk for developing white matter pathology. This interpretation aligns with the biopsychosocial model of Clark and colleagues 27 by extending the notion that RD serves as a chronic stressor with negative consequences on brain health. Lifetime RD reflects events of discrimination that may not occur frequently but nevertheless may have lasting stressful consequences. For example, being denied a job due to race and/or skin color may lead to financial strain and subsequent stressors that accumulate after the event.

The biological pathway underlying the relationship between RD and WMH may be attributed to accumulating chronic stress. This concept is supported by a previous study that found that discrimination restricted to experiences attributed to race or skin color had adverse effects on brain health rather than overall discrimination. 28 Repeated regular exposure to RD can lead to sustained activation of a stress response. 4 This cumulative burden can manifest as dysregulation of chronic stress, a phenomenon known as allostatic load, which forces the body into persistently elevated, non‐adaptive physiological states that disrupt homeostasis. 44 The disruption from allostatic load is associated with negative biological consequences that are linked to oxidative stress and inflammatory responses, 45 which can induce poor brain outcomes, including increased WMH burden. 46 , 47 RD could also influence cerebrovascular health through environmental pathways. For example, RD limits life course opportunities (e.g., economic mobility) to acquire protective factors against poorer brain outcomes. 48 Future research is needed to characterize different pathways that drive the link between RD and WMH.

WMH and memory

We found that WMH are associated with objective, but not subjective memory, consistent with previous work. 32 , 49 , 50 An explanation is that white matter tracts necessary for successful encoding and retrieval processes may be disrupted. For example, WMHs were associated with poorer memory in older adults among tracts with frontal–temporal connectivity and frontal–subcortical regions. 51

The finding that WMHs were not associated with subjective memory concerns aligns with some prior studies, 52 , 53 but not others. 30 , 31 A potential explanation is that the MFQ used in our study may not fully capture the multidimensional picture of subjective memory experiences in this population. For example, there were no significant differences in subjective cognitive complaint reports despite Black older adults performing worse on an objective memory measure compared to White older adults. 54

Black adults may be under‐reporting subjective memory concerns relative to White adults despite demonstrating greater objective impairment. This may be due to differences in cultural beliefs about aging or bias in subjective memory measurements that were validated in primarily White samples. Future research is needed to examine how sociocultural factors may influence the interpretation and reporting of subjective memory. In addition, variability in measurement tools could influence participant interpretation and accurate assessment of subjective memory. A recent study found that four subjective memory questionnaires were associated with different patterns of WMH accumulation. 55

Lifetime RD and memory

Our study extended previous findings that RD relates to subjective cognitive decline 15 , 16 by showing that greater lifetime RD is associated with worse subjective memory specifically. This further supports that experiences of racism have adverse effects on cognitive health, and that these changes can be noticed by an individual's subjective experience. Individuals who detect these changes are more likely to seek medical care, 56 which is particularly relevant in situations where these changes are not noticed by others, such as scenarios in which older adults live alone.

The finding that WMHs did not mediate the association between lifetime RD and subjective memory suggests the existence of other pathways. One possibility is that these pathways are more psychological in nature, such as prior work that has examined the intersection between RD, depression, and cognition in Black adults. 5 Another potential avenue may be through other brain pathways, such as hippocampal volume, which is susceptible to stress in older adults. 57

We did not observe a total effect of lifetime RD on objective memory. Findings in this area have been mixed. One study found that greater lifetime RD was related to better objective memory. 58 Similarly, another study found that lifetime discrimination was associated positively with global cognitive function. 59 Previous literature showed that Black adults with higher socioeconomic status often report more discrimination, 3 which is explained by these individuals spending more time in predominantly White spaces. In these instances, greater RD may be a proxy for resilience factors that support memory, which may mask the negative effects of RD.

Despite the absence of a total effect of lifetime RD on objective memory, we found a significant indirect effect through WMHs, such that greater reports of lifetime RD was associated with higher WMH burden, and higher WMH burden in turn predicted poorer objective memory. This finding identifies a direct point of intervention to improve health outcomes in Black populations. Specifically, psychoeducation of WMHs could be implemented in clinical settings to increase the awareness of risk and to encourage engagement in preventative health behaviors. Previous research has shown that modifiable risk factors, including vascular (e.g., hypertension), lifestyle (e.g., diet), and psychosocial (e.g., depression, social support) factors are an area of intervention to decrease progression of WMH pathology and improve white matter health. 60 , 61 However, we acknowledge that the absence of a total effect suggests that the indirect effect should be interpreted with caution. One plausible explanation is that competing paths are operating in the opposite direction 62 , 63 (e.g., through occupational attainment, neighborhood affluence, etc.) or unmeasured confounding may be masking the total effect. Future research should further clarify these relationships and test for replicability.

Limitations, strengths, and future directions

The present study examines mediation models cross‐sectionally, which limits causal inferences. Although temporality in mediation models is strengthened by longitudinal measurements, 64 , 65 there is still value cross‐sectionally when considering the likely chronological nature of constructs. 66 We believe our analyses are theoretically supported by prior work that demonstrated that RD leads to negative health outcomes rather than vice versa, 67 and WMHs represent a plausible biological mechanism. Nonetheless, future work should include longitudinal measurements to examine these pathways. A second limitation in the current study is the subjective report in experiences of RD; individuals may be under‐reporting as they are asked to think back on their entire lifetime. Future work should evaluate WMHs as a mechanism to explain the relationship between other indicators of structural racism (e.g., neighborhood socioeconomic resources) and cognitive outcomes.

This study also had several strengths. Its focus on both subjective and objective memory allows for a more nuanced understanding of how RD may influence ADRD‐related outcomes. In addition, by focusing on a single racial group, our findings more precisely capture the construct of RD, which may hold different meanings in more racially heterogeneous samples. Focusing on within‐group variability is essential, as Black populations are uniquely impacted by their broader social context due to structural racism (e.g., socioeconomic inequalities, barriers to health care), and examining modifiable factors can help explain and address the variance observed in this population. Insights from the current study can inform future work by identifying pathways that may be relevant to other populations, which could expand generalizability and conceptual frameworks. In addition, our examination of WMHs as a brain health indicator in the context of RD allows for expansion of previous work that has observed its utility in identifying individuals at greater risk for poorer cognitive outcomes.

Future research should further examine how RD relates to vascular and inflammatory factors, regionally specific WMHs, and differences between subjective and objective cognition in longitudinal efforts. It would be beneficial to examine how these differences may lead to the development of mild cognitive impairment and ADRD‐related risk in racially minoritized populations.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, this study provides evidence that WMHs play a significant role in the relationship between RD and objective memory among Black adults transitioning to older adulthood. This study also highlights differences in the role of RD in subjective versus objective memory. More work implementing and evaluating policies targeting race‐based discrimination is warranted. Ideally, these efforts will contribute to reducing cognitive morbidity and inequalities seen in dementia risk.
