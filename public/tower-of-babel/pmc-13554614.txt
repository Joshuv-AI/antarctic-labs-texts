Salmonella Outbreaks in Low- and Middle-Income Countries over the Last Decade: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Transmission Dynamics, Clinical Outcomes, and Antimicrobial Resistance
Authors: Peter Ofori Appiah, Akuffo Anita, Wisdom Boateng Owusu, Hafizu Muntala, Nyantseng Vivian Ekua, Paul Omoniyi, Kwarteng Randy Ankrah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSalmonella outbreaks persist as a public health burden in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), yet comprehensive synthesis of their epidemiology over the past decade remains limited.MethodsFollowing PRISMA 2020 guidelines, we searched PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar for laboratory-confirmed Salmonella outbreaks in LMICs (1 January 2016–1 January 2026). Random-effects meta-analysis estimated pooled prevalence rates, with statistical heterogeneity quantified using I2 statistic. Given extreme heterogeneity across most outcomes (I2 > 90%), we present individual study ranges alongside pooled estimates.ResultsTwenty-four studies from 14 LMICs met inclusion criteria. Salmonella Typhi was the predominant pathogen (70.8% of outbreaks). Waterborne transmission was most frequent (37.5%), predominating in Africa (83.3%), whereas foodborne and fecal-oral routes were common in Asia. Pooled laboratory confirmation rate was 28.02% (95% CI: 12.2–52.1; observed range: 1.5–100%), varying by region: Southeast Asia 54.2% (95% CI: 4.3–96.9), Western Pacific 27.6% (95% CI: 8.0–56.9), and Africa 6.1% (95% CI: 5.3–6.9). The pooled case fatality rate was 7.0% (95% CI: 3.4–13.7; range: 3.6–10.0%). Fever (93.0%), abdominal pain (53.6%), and headache (52.0%) were most common symptoms. Pooled antimicrobial resistance reached 59.3% (95% CI: 26.6–85.4%), highest for trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole (94.2%) and ciprofloxacin (61.3%). Extreme heterogeneity and wide confidence intervals were observed across some pooled estimates and should be interpreted with caution.ConclusionsSalmonella outbreaks in LMICs are characterized by high S. Typhi prevalence and predominant waterborne transmission. Elevated mortality and widespread first-line antibiotic resistance show the need for improved WASH infrastructure, enhanced real-time outbreak surveillance, and revised empiric treatment protocols.Keywords: Salmonella, outbreaks, antimicrobial resistance, LMICs, typhoid fever, transmission dynamics, systematic review

1. Introduction

Salmonella infections remain a leading cause of enteric disease worldwide. Salmonella infections are relatively common in countries with poor water supply and sanitation, especially South Asia, Southeast Asia, and sub-Saharan Africa, where they are major causes of death and disability, especially among children.1,2 Annually, an estimated 11–21 million cases of typhoid fever and hundreds of thousands of invasive nontyphoidal salmonellosis (NTS) episodes occur, resulting in substantial morbidity and mortality. 3 While endemic transmission persists in many regions, Salmonella outbreaks continue to emerge with increasing frequency and geographic spread, often overwhelming local health systems and exacerbating socioeconomic vulnerability.4-6 The adaptability of this pathogen to diverse environmental reservoirs, rapid urbanization, informal food systems, and climate-driven disruptions to water safety has created ideal conditions for explosive outbreak dynamics in resource-constrained settings.7,8

Outbreak investigations have identified contaminated drinking water, unsafe food handling, travel and person-to-person transmission as primary drivers of Salmonella spread, yet the relative contribution of each route varies significantly across ecological and humanitarian contexts.5,9-12 Concurrently, the rise of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) has severely complicated outbreak management. Multidrug-resistant NTS strains are now widely documented across Asia and Africa, with fluoroquinolone and third-generation cephalosporin resistance increasingly limiting therapeutic options and prolonging the duration of outbreaks.13-15 Despite these threats, surveillance systems in many LMICs remain fragmented, with inconsistent case definitions, limited laboratory capacity, and underreporting of outbreak-related mortality and hospitalization. 16 Consequently, the true epidemiological landscape of Salmonella outbreaks and their transmission sources, clinical severity, and resistance patterns remain poorly characterized at regional or global scales.

To address this gap, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of human Salmonella outbreaks reported in the last decade. We synthesized epidemiological, microbiological, and clinical data from outbreak investigations to quantify pooled case fatality rates, laboratory confirmation proportions, symptom prevalence, and antimicrobial resistance patterns. We further characterized transmission routes, pathogen distributions (S. Typhi, S. Paratyphi A, and NTS), and resistance patterns across WHO regions. Such an analysis will be important for identifying high-risk transmission pathways, quantifying regional heterogeneity in clinical outcomes, and guiding targeted interventions, including water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) infrastructure investment and AMR surveillance strengthening. 17

2. Materials and Methods

2.1. Search Strategy

The systematic review was performed in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis) guidelines (Figure 1). 18 This systematic review was registered with PROSPERO (CRD420261399913). Comprehensive literature searches were executed across PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar to identify peer-reviewed studies published in English between 1 January 2016 and 1 January 2026. The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary and free-text terms related to Salmonella (e.g., “Salmonella,” “typhoid fever,” “nontyphoidal Salmonella,” “Salmonella infection”) with outbreak-specific terminology (“outbreak,” “epidemic,” “cluster”) and the official World Bank classifications for low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). The complete search syntax for each database is provided in Supplementary Table S2. All retrieved records were imported into Rayyan systematic review software, 19 where automated and manual deduplication was performed. Two independent reviewers subsequently screened titles and abstracts for preliminary eligibility, followed by full-text assessment to confirm inclusion. Discrepancies during the selection process were resolved through consensus discussion, with a third reviewer consulted only if necessary.

The PRISMA flow diagram for the study selection process. Identification, screening, eligibility, and inclusion of studies reporting Salmonella outbreaks in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), 2016–2026

2.2. Eligibility Criteria

Studies were considered eligible for inclusion if they were peer-reviewed publications released between 1 January 2016 and 1 January 2026, reported empirical epidemiological data on laboratory-confirmed human Salmonella outbreaks in countries classified as low- or middle-income by the World Bank 20 and provided at least one quantitative outcome of interest. Although the literature search was restricted to this publication window, we retained studies reporting outbreak onset dates prior to 2016 when they contributed unique, non-duplicated epidemiological or microbiological data not available in more recent publications. This approach accounts for the typical 1–6 years lag between outbreak occurrence, investigation, and peer-reviewed publication in LMICs settings. This ensures the comprehensive capture of high-quality evidence generated during the review period. Studies were excluded if they focused exclusively on sporadic or endemic Salmonella infections rather than defined outbreak events or if they were limited to conference abstracts, review articles, editorials, commentaries, or computational modelling studies lacking primary outbreak data. Studies that investigated Salmonella outbreaks in animal populations, environmental reservoirs, or food production systems without concurrent human case data were also excluded to avoid extreme heterogeneity in outcome measures. Additionally, studies conducted in high-income countries, those evaluating vaccine efficacy or therapeutic interventions rather than descriptive outbreak epidemiology, and reports that did not provide microbiological confirmation of Salmonella through culture, polymerase chain reaction (PCR), or whole-genome sequencing (WGS) were omitted from the final synthesis.

2.3. Quality Assessment

The methodological quality and risk of bias of the included studies were independently assessed by two reviewers via the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist for Prevalence Studies. Interreviewer discrepancies in quality ratings were resolved through consensus discussion prior to data synthesis. This tool was adapted to evaluate outbreak investigation reports and surveillance-based studies, focusing on nine key domains: (1) appropriateness of the sampling frame; (2) suitability of the sampling technique; (3) adequacy of the sample size; (4) detailed description of study subjects and outbreak setting; (5) sufficient coverage of the sample during data analysis; (6) use of valid and standardized methods for laboratory confirmation of Salmonella; (7) reliable and objective measurement of clinical outcomes, transmission routes, and antimicrobial resistance patterns; (8) appropriateness of statistical analysis; and (9) adequate response or participation rate. Each domain was rated as “yes” (low risk of bias), “no” (high risk of bias), “unclear,” or “not applicable.” Based on cumulative domain ratings, studies were categorized as having Low (≥7 ‘Yes’ responses), Moderate (4–6 ‘Yes’), or High (≤3 ‘Yes’) risk of bias, consistent with JBI guidance for prevalence studies. Disagreements between reviewers were resolved through consensus discussion, with a third reviewer consulting for arbitration if consensus could not be reached. The detailed quality assessment scores and risk-of-bias categorization for each included study are presented in Supplementary Table S3.

2.4. Data Extraction and Synthesis

Data extraction was performed independently by two reviewers via a standardized Microsoft Excel spreadsheet specifically designed for this review. The extraction protocol captured comprehensive study characteristics, including geographic location, WHO regional classification, study design, outbreak timeframe, and demographic profiles of the affected populations. Epidemiological metrics such as the number of suspected and laboratory-confirmed cases, reported deaths, case fatality ratios (CFRs), and hospitalization rates were systematically recorded. The microbiological data included Salmonella pathogen classification (Typhi, Paratyphi A, or nontyphoidal Salmonella), specific serotypes, and antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) results across multiple antibiotic classes. Furthermore, contextual and transmission-related variables were documented, including suspected exposure routes (waterborne, foodborne, fecal-oral, or environmental), identified risk factors (e.g., contaminated water sources, poor sanitation, food handling practices), and exposure settings (households, healthcare facilities, or community gatherings).

2.5. Statistical Analysis

Pooled prevalence estimates for laboratory confirmation rates, case fatality ratios, hospitalization frequencies, symptom prevalence, and antimicrobial resistance patterns were calculated via a random effects meta-analysis model with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Statistical heterogeneity was rigorously evaluated via Cochran’s Q test and quantified with the I2 statistic. Due to the inherent diversity of outbreak settings and methodologies, pooled estimates should be interpreted with caution as summaries of a highly variable evidence base, rather than precise population parameters. Publication bias was assessed both graphically through funnel plot symmetry and statistically via Egger’s linear and meta-regression tests when a minimum of ten studies were available for a given outcome. All the statistical computations and meta-analytical procedures were conducted via Python version 3.12, with the following packages: numpy and pandas for data handling, scipy for proportion transformations and hypothesis testing, statsmodels for random-effects pooling and meta-regression, and matplotlib for graphical outputs. A two-sided p value of <0.05 considered statistically significant.

3. Results

3.1 Study Selection

The initial database search yielded 2,639 records (PubMed (n = 632), Scopus (n = 866), Web of Science (n = 341), and Google Scholar (n = 800)). After the removal of 1,059 duplicate records, 1,580 unique records were screened by title and abstract. Of these, 664 records were retained for further assessment after initial screening, whereas 598 records were excluded on the basis of predefined criteria: irrelevance to outbreak epidemiology, focus on sporadic or endemic cases rather than defined outbreaks, publication type (e.g., reviews, editorials, conference abstracts without full-text availability), or language restrictions. The remaining 46 full-text reports were retrieved and assessed in detail for eligibility against the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Twenty-two reports were excluded at this stage for the following reasons: studies conducted in high-income countries (n = 11), review articles lacking primary epidemiological data (n = 5), investigations focusing on animal populations rather than human outbreaks (n = 3), inability to access full-text articles despite reasonable efforts (n = 2), and studies that did not provide microbiological confirmation of Salmonella through culture, polymerase chain reaction (PCR), or whole-genome sequencing (n = 1). Ultimately, 24 studies met all the inclusion criteria and were included in the final qualitative synthesis and quantitative meta-analysis. The study selection process is summarized in the PRISMA 2020 flow diagram (Figure 1).

3.2 Study Characteristics and Geographic Distribution of Outbreaks

A total of 24 studies met the inclusion criteria,4,5,9-12,21-38 representing human Salmonella outbreak investigations across 14 LMICs. Although the literature search focused on publications released between 1 January 2016 and 1 January 2026, six studies reported outbreak onset dates between 2010 and 2015.4,5,12,22,33,34 This may reflect typical publication delays in outbreak investigations. Comprehensive characteristics of all included outbreaks, including country, WHO region, onset year, study design, pathogen distribution, transmission routes, and key epidemiological metrics, are detailed in Supplementary Table S4. Geographically, outbreak reports were concentrated in Asia and Africa, with India and China each contributing six investigations (25.0% each), collectively accounting for half of the dataset. Uganda contributed two studies (8.3%), whereas the remaining ten countries (South Africa, Zimbabwe, Malaysia, Viet Nam, Malawi, Tunisia, Nepal, Bangladesh, Cambodia, and Mexico) were each represented by a single outbreak report (4.2% each) (Figure 2). In the WHO subregion, the Western Pacific region accounted for the largest proportion of studies (n=9, 37.5%), followed by Southeast Asia (n=8, 33.3%) and Africa (n=6, 25.0%), with a single investigation originating from the Americas.

3.3 Temporal Trends in Outbreak Onset

Temporally, the number of years of outbreak onset in the included studies spanned 15 years, with a pronounced concentration of investigations in the mid-to-late 2010s. The year 2016 recorded the highest number of outbreak onset events (n=5, 20.8%), followed by 2019 (n=4, 16.7%) and 2015/2017 (n=3 each, 12.5% each) (Figure 3).

Microbiological and epidemiological characterization revealed distinct pathogen and transmission patterns across regions and time. Salmonella enterica serovar Typhi was the predominant pathogen identified in 17 outbreaks (70.8%), whereas nontyphoidal Salmonella (NTS) and S. Paratyphi A accounted for 4 (16.7%) and 3 (12.5%) investigations, respectively (Figure 4).

3.5 Transmission Routes by Region and Pathogen

Waterborne exposure was the most frequently reported transmission route (n=9, 37.5%), followed equally by foodborne and fecal-oral pathways (n=5 each, 20.8% each). Mixed or context-specific exposures, including waterborne/foodborne, community-acquired, travel-associated, and environmental/waterborne routes, accounted for the remaining 16.7% of the total. Stratification by WHO subregion revealed regionally distinct transmission ecologies: waterborne routes predominated in Africa (5/6 studies, 83.3%), whereas Southeast Asia exhibited a more heterogeneous pattern, with substantial contributions from fecal-oral (4/8) and community-acquired or environmental pathways (3/8) (Figure 5).

Pathogen-specific analysis revealed that waterborne transmission was the most common route for S. Typhi outbreaks (9/17, 52.9%), whereas all NTS outbreaks were exclusively linked to foodborne exposure (4/4, 100%) (Figure 6).

3.6 Overall Pooled Confirmation Rate

The overall pooled laboratory confirmation rate across all included studies was 28.02% (95% CI: 12.22–52.12), based on 18 studies encompassing 6,208 suspected cases (Figure 8). Substantial regional heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 97.4%, p < 0.0001), with individual study confirmation rates ranging from 1.50% to 100%. Due to the inherent diversity of outbreak settings and methodologies, pooled estimates should be interpreted with caution as summaries of a highly variable evidence base, rather than precise population parameters.

Pooled laboratory confirmation rates of Salmonella during outbreaks

3.7 Regional Variation in Confirmation Rates

Studies from Southeast Asia presented the highest pooled confirmation rate of 54.19% (95% CI: 4.33–96.86; 5 studies, 504 suspected cases), with extreme heterogeneity (I2 = 92.1%) and individual study estimates ranging from 3.70% to 100%. In contrast, the Western Pacific region presented a substantially lower pooled confirmation rate of 27.64% (95% CI: 9.96–56.88; 9 studies, 2,184 suspected cases), with considerable heterogeneity (I2 = 96.2%, τ2 = 0.44) and a range of 1.50%–100%. The African region presented the lowest confirmation rate at 6.09% (95% CI: 5.33–6.94; 3 studies, 3,410 suspected cases), with no detectable heterogeneity (I2 = 0.0%) and a narrow-observed range of 5.7%–8.9% (Figure 9).

Pooled Laboratory Confirmation Rate by WHO Subregion. Summary forest plot of pooled laboratory confirmation rates among suspected Salmonella outbreak cases stratified by WHO subregion (n = 3 regions, 18 studies)

3.8 Confirmation Rates by Pathogen Type

The Salmonella Typhi subgroup represented the largest portion of the data, including 14 studies with 4,465 suspected cases. The pooled laboratory confirmation rate for S. Typhi was 25.89% (95% CI: 12.44–46.22), with extremely high statistical heterogeneity (I2 = 97.2%, p < 0.0001) and individual study estimates ranging from 3.70% to 100%. The S. Paratyphi A subgroup included 2 studies with 559 suspected cases; the pooled confirmation rate was 82.94% (95% CI: 2.43–99.89), with very high heterogeneity (I2 = 99.9%) and a range of 28.3%–100%. The non-typhoidal Salmonella (NTS) subgroup comprised 4 studies with 1,211 suspected cases; the pooled confirmation rate for NTS was 17.79% (95% CI: 1.33–49.24), also exhibiting maximal heterogeneity (I2 = 97.1%) and a range of 1.50%–100% (Figure 10).

Pooled laboratory confirmation rates by pathogen type. Forest plot of pooled laboratory confirmation rates among suspected Salmonella outbreak cases stratified by pathogen type

3.9 Clinical Outcomes and Symptomatology

The pooled case fatality rate (CFR) across laboratory-confirmed Salmonella outbreaks in LMICs was 7.0% (95% CI: 3.4–13.7) based on three studies encompassing 162 cases and 11 deaths (Figure 11). The three studies contributing to the pooled CFR estimate reported individual rates of 3.6% (Ho et al, 10; Viet Nam), 4.5% (Bano-Zaidi et al, 21 ; Mexico), and 10.0% (Bulage et al, 22 ; Uganda), with an observed range of 3.6–10.0%. Given the limited number of studies (n = 3), this pooled estimate should be interpreted with caution.

Pooled case fatality rate during Salmonella outbreaks. Forest plot of the pooled case fatality rate (CFR) among laboratory-confirmed Salmonella outbreaks (n = 3 studies, 162 cases, 11 deaths)

Symptom incidence varied substantially across clinical manifestations (Figure 12). Fever was reported in all cases across ten studies (pooled prevalence: 92.99%, 95% CI: 79.21–97.88), although substantial heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 89.9%) with individual study estimates ranging from 50.0% to 100%. Gastrointestinal and systemic symptoms had moderate-to-high pooled prevalence: abdominal pain, 53.79% (95% CI: 41.45–65.68; range: 33.61%–100%); diarrhea, 49.20% (95% CI: 31.34–67.27; range: 12.66%–100%); headache, 50.4% (95% CI: 27.3–73.5; range: 24.00%–89.10%); and vomiting, 46.26% (95% CI: 34.28–58.70; range: 33.33%–79.13%). Nausea (16.68%, 95% CI: 6.61–36.16; range: 6.06%–38.46%) and constipation (9.94%, 95% CI: 3.54–24.95; range: 1.97%–19.87%) were less frequently reported. All symptom categories exhibited very high statistical heterogeneity (I2 = 89.5–95.9%). This may suggest considerable variation in case definitions, diagnostic criteria, or reporting practices across outbreak settings.

Pooled symptom prevalence during Salmonella outbreaks. Summary forest plot of pooled symptom prevalence among Salmonella outbreak cases (n = 7 symptoms, 58 symptom-study observations)

3.10 Antimicrobial Resistance Patterns

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing data were available for 1,137 Salmonella isolates (Figure 13). Pooled resistance estimates indicated substantial variability across antibiotic classes. Resistance to ciprofloxacin was 61.3% (95% CI: 6.4–97.3%; observed range: 2.1–100.0%), and resistance to trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX) was 94.2% (95% CI: 51.0–99.6%; observed range: 73.2–100.0%), based on six and three studies, respectively. Moderate resistance was observed for ampicillin (38.5%, 95% CI: −0.9 to 97.7%; range: 0.9–100.0%), chloramphenicol (38.5%, 95% CI: 0.9–97.7%; range: 0.9–100.0%), tetracycline (50.2%, 95% CI: 3.9–96.1%; range: 3.9–100.0%), and ceftriaxone (45.3%, 95% CI: 0.8–98.9%; range: 8.5–100.0%). The extremely wide confidence intervals and high observed ranges reflect between-study heterogeneity (I2 >90% for all antibiotics) and limited sample sizes for certain antibiotic classes.

Pooled antimicrobial resistance rates by antibiotic class. Summary forest plot of pooled antimicrobial resistance prevalence among Salmonella outbreak isolates by antibiotic class (n = 6 antibiotics, 14 studies)

3.11 Publication Bias

The funnel plot displayed moderate visual scatter, with several studies falling outside the pseudo 95% confidence limits (Figure 14). However, the formal statistical assessment revealed no significant evidence of publication bias (Egger’s test: t = 1.27, df = 16, p = 0.223; bias estimate = 3.02, SE = 2.38). The observed asymmetry is therefore more likely attributable to clinical and methodological heterogeneity across outbreak settings rather than the selective publication of positive findings.

Funnel plot for assessment of publication bias

3.12 Meta-Regression Analysis

Given the substantial between-study heterogeneity observed in the primary pooled estimates, we conducted a mixed-effects meta-regression to identify potential moderators (Table 1). The WHO region (with Africa as the reference category) and key clinical symptoms were entered as covariates. Regional stratification did not significantly predict variation in the confirmation estimates; the coefficients for the Americas (β = 3.18, p = 0.271), Southeast Asia (β = 2.72, p = 0.178), and Western Pacific (β = 1.86, p = 0.316) were all nonsignificant, indicating that geographic location alone does not adequately explain heterogeneity in diagnostic confirmation patterns.

In contrast, clinical symptomatology emerged as a significant moderator. Fever demonstrated a strong positive association with confirmation likelihood (β = 2.16, p < 0.001). Conversely, constipation (β = −2.38, p = 0.0003) and nausea (β = −1.80, p = 0.008) were significantly associated with lower confirmation rates. Diarrhea (β = −0.22, p = 0.645), headache (β = −0.09, p = 0.870), and vomiting (β = −0.34, p = 0.507) did not significantly influence the confirmation estimates. The strong positive association with confirmation likelihood may reflect healthcare-seeking behavior, case severity, and differential testing practices rather than true biological predictors of laboratory confirmation. Example patients presenting with fever are more likely to seek care and undergo diagnostic testing in outbreak settings, whereas those with milder or atypical symptoms (e.g., constipation, nausea) may be managed empirically without laboratory confirmation. Based on this, causal inference is not warranted.

4. Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis provide a synthesis of Salmonella outbreak epidemiology in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) over the past decade. Our analysis of 24 studies revealed several important findings with implications for outbreak preparedness, clinical management, and public health policy.

Outbreaks were more concentrated in Asia (India and China, 25% each) and the western Pacific region (37.5%). This may reflect both the true epidemiological burden and disparities in surveillance capacity and research infrastructure. 3 However, the relatively low representation from Africa (25%), despite the region’s known high burden of infectious diseases, may suggest significant gaps in outbreak detection and reporting systems. 39 This contrasts with recent cholera outbreak reviews, which documented 74% of studies from Africa. 40 This finding indicates better surveillance for cholera than for salmonellosis in the region.

The temporal clustering of outbreaks in 2016 (20.8%) and 2019 (16.7%) may correspond to periods of enhanced surveillance, climatic events conducive to transmission, or improved outbreak reporting mechanisms. The absence of reported outbreaks in 2021 coincides with the global COVID-19 pandemic, which likely disrupted routine surveillance systems and diverted public health resources, potentially leading to under ascertainment rather than a true decline in outbreak occurrence. This finding mirrors observations from cholera surveillance during the same period, where pandemic-related disruptions led to significant gaps in disease reporting. 40 This highlights the vulnerability of enteric disease surveillance during concurrent public health emergencies and emphasizes the need for resilient, integrated surveillance systems.

S. Typhi (70.8%) was the predominant Salmonella causing outbreaks in LMICs in the last decade. This observation aligns with the known epidemiology of typhoid fever in LMICs, where inadequate water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) infrastructure perpetuates transmission. 1 This proportion is higher than the 45–55% typically reported in endemic settings, indicating that outbreak investigations may preferentially detect typhoid fever because of its more dramatic clinical presentation and public health impact. 1 The substantial proportion of nontyphoidal Salmonella (NTS) outbreaks (16.7%) is clear, as NTS has traditionally been associated with sporadic rather than outbreak settings in endemic regions. This may reflect improved detection and reporting of NTS outbreaks, changing epidemiological patterns, or the emergence of hypervirulent NTS strains capable of causing explosive outbreaks. 15

Our analysis revealed distinct transmission ecologies by region and pathogen type. The predominance of waterborne transmission in Africa (83.3%) compared with the more heterogeneous pattern in Asia underscores the critical role of contaminated water sources in driving outbreaks in settings with limited access to safe drinking water. The exclusive association of NTS outbreaks with foodborne transmission (100%) contrasts with the mixed transmission routes observed for S. typhi and suggests different intervention priorities for these pathogens. These findings have important implications for outbreak response: waterborne outbreaks require rapid deployment of water treatment and distribution infrastructure, whereas foodborne outbreaks necessitate increased food safety regulations and vendor licensing. The temporal shift in transmission routes, with waterborne outbreaks peaking in 2015–2016 and foodborne/fecal-oral routes becoming more prominent in 2019 and 2024, may reflect changing exposure patterns, urbanization, informal food system expansion, or improved detection of foodborne outbreaks. This evolving epidemiology necessitates adaptive surveillance systems capable of detecting diverse transmission pathways and rapid response mechanisms tailored to specific exposure contexts.

The overall pooled laboratory confirmation rate of 28.02% appears high; however, the extreme regional heterogeneity (I2 = 97.3%) and the wide variation in confirmation rates across WHO regions warrant careful interpretation. The markedly higher confirmation rate in Southeast Asia (54.19%) than in Africa (6.09%) and the Western Pacific (27.64%) likely reflects disparities in diagnostic infrastructure, case definitions, and testing practices rather than true differences in disease burden.

Several factors may explain these regional disparities. First, studies from Southeast Asia, particularly those from India and Bangladesh, frequently employed selective testing strategies in which only severe or hospitalized cases underwent laboratory confirmation, artificially inflating confirmation rates. Second, the African studies included in this analysis were predominantly large community-based outbreak investigations with broad case definitions and limited diagnostic capacity, resulting in lower confirmation rates. Third, the Western Pacific region exhibited substantial heterogeneity, with some studies employing advanced molecular diagnostics (PCR, whole-genome sequencing) and others relying on culture-based methods with variable sensitivity.

The extremely high statistical heterogeneity (I2 >97.4%) across all pathogen subgroups further highlights the methodological diversity and varying diagnostic approaches across studies. This finding highlights the urgent need for standardized case definitions, harmonized diagnostic algorithms, and strengthened laboratory capacity in LMICs to enable accurate outbreak detection, appropriate clinical management, and effective public health response.

The CFR observed in our analysis (CFR) of 7.0% (95% CI: 3.4–13.7%) is comparable to that reported for cholera outbreaks in Africa (1.5%) but substantially higher than that reported in Asia (0.3%), 40 suggesting that Salmonella outbreaks may be associated with more severe outcomes than cholera outbreaks in similar settings. Our pooled CFR is slightly higher than, but broadly consistent with, the recent comprehensive meta-analysis by Kungu et al,, 41 which reported an overall pooled CFR of 5.72% across 104 studies from 35 LMICs. 41 This may likely be as because our analysis was restricted to defined outbreak investigations, whereas Kungu et al, 41 included both outbreak and endemic/sporadic case reports; outbreak settings may experience delayed recognition, resource constraints, and overwhelmed health systems that elevate mortality risk. Also, Kungu et al, 41 reported important pathogen-specific stratification. Non-typhoidal Salmonella (NTS) infections had a substantially higher CFR (13.85%) than typhoidal infections (2.99%). Our analysis included a mixed pathogen distribution (70.8% S. Typhi, 16.7% NTS), and the inclusion of NTS outbreaks which often affect immunocompromised populations and may present with more severe invasive disease may have contributed to our slightly elevated pooled estimate. The low heterogeneity (I2 = 7.4%) suggests consistent mortality patterns across studies, potentially indicating systematic deficiencies in outbreak response rather than random variation. This finding is particularly concerning because typhoid fever is typically associated with CFRs of <1% in settings with adequate healthcare infrastructure. 3

The occurrence of fever (92.99%) aligns with the classic presentation of typhoid fever and invasive salmonellosis. The high prevalence of gastrointestinal symptoms, including abdominal pain (53.79%), diarrhea (49.2%), and vomiting (46.28%), highlights the clinical overlap with other enteric pathogens and the diagnostic challenges in resource-limited settings. 42 The substantial heterogeneity in symptom prevalence (I2 = 93.3–99.5%) likely reflects variation in case definitions, patient populations (pediatric vs. adult), pathogen types, and healthcare-seeking behaviors.

The moderate prevalence of systemic symptoms such as headache (51.98%) and the lower prevalence of constipation (9.94%) and nausea (16.68%) are consistent with the clinical spectrum of enteric fever. Resistance to ciprofloxacin (61.25%) and trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole (94.22%) represents a critical public health threat and fundamentally undermines the effectiveness of first-line oral antibiotic therapy for Salmonella infections. These findings are consistent with the global emergence of extensively drug-resistant (XDR) S. typhi, particularly in South Asia, where plasmid-mediated resistance to fluoroquinolones and third-generation cephalosporins has been widely documented. 14 The moderate resistance rates observed for ampicillin (38.48%), chloramphenicol (38.48%), tetracycline (50.18%), and ceftriaxone (45.30%) are concerning, particularly the emergence of ceftriaxone resistance, which compromises the effectiveness of primary parenteral therapy for severe salmonellosis. Several factors contribute to the alarming resistance trends observed in this analysis. First, the unregulated use of antibiotics in both the human and animal health sectors in many LMICs creates selective pressure for resistance development. 13 Second, the widespread use of subtherapeutic antibiotic doses and incomplete treatment courses facilitates the emergence and dissemination of resistant strains. Third, the horizontal transfer of resistance genes via plasmids and other mobile genetic elements enables the rapid spread of multidrug resistance across bacterial populations. 14 Our findings are further contextualized by contemporary systematic reviews of antimicrobial efficacy and global disease burden. Sivanandy et al, 39 evaluated four commonly used antibiotics for Salmonella infections and concluded that azithromycin is increasingly favored over fluoroquinolones and third-generation cephalosporins due to its higher clinical effectiveness, faster fever resolution, and more favorable safety profile in resource-limited settings. 39 This directly aligns with our observation of near-universal resistance to trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole (94.2%) and substantial ciprofloxacin resistance (61.3%). This shows the need to shift empiric treatment protocols toward azithromycin or targeted parenteral therapy guided by local susceptibility patterns.

These findings have profound implications for clinical management and outbreak response. The loss of efficacy of first-line oral antibiotics necessitates the use of more expensive and less accessible alternatives, such as azithromycin or carbapenems, which may be unavailable in resource-constrained settings. Azithromycin has been identified as the preferred choice for treating Salmonella infection by physicians because of its lower resistance development and comparatively safer profile in terms of side effects and adverse events. 39 The emergence of ceftriaxone resistance further limits treatment options for severe infections and increases the risk of treatment failure and mortality. These challenges show the need for antimicrobial stewardship programs, enhanced surveillance of resistance patterns, and the development of novel therapeutic agents.

Our findings on transmission dynamics align with broader evidence identifying key risk factors for Salmonella infections. The predominance of waterborne transmission in Africa (83.3%) is consistent with documented risk factors such as lack of wastewater treatment, consumption of untreated drinking water, and environmental exposure to contaminated water sources.4,9,21 Similarly, the foodborne and fecal-oral routes observed in Asia align with risk factors including food vehicles (particularly eggs, composite foods, and meat products), poor food handling practices, and person-to-person transmission in crowded settings.5,10,43 Host-specific factors such as travel history, animal contact, and recent antibiotic use have also been consistently associated with sporadic salmonellosis and may contribute to outbreak susceptibility in vulnerable populations.33,44,45

Additionally, neonatal intensive care units (NICUs) represent high-risk settings for Salmonella outbreaks, where prematurity, low birth weight, and healthcare workers’ hands have been identified as primary transmission routes. 44 The use of invasive devices and parenteral nutrition further increases the risk of colonization and infection by multidrug-resistant microorganisms in these vulnerable populations. 45

4.1 Sources of Statistical Heterogeneity

The extreme statistical heterogeneity observed across most pooled estimates (I2 > 95%) reflects the profound diversity inherent in Salmonella outbreak investigations across LMICs. This variation arises from four key factors. First, study design and denominator selection varied substantially. Our analysis synthesized data from diverse investigation types, ranging from retrospective case-control studies to prospective community-based surveillance. For example, laboratory confirmation rates varied dramatically due to denominator differences: community-based investigations (e.g., Zimbabwe, N’cho et al, 29 ) utilizing broad clinical case definitions yielded lower confirmation rates (∼6.0%) because many non-cases presented with similar symptoms. In contrast, hospital-based surveillance or selective testing strategies (e.g., Hooda et al,, 11 Bangladesh; various Chinese cohorts) focused on severe cases requiring laboratory isolation, resulting in confirmation rates approaching 100%. Pooling these fundamentally different ratios introduces substantial statistical dispersion.

Second, biological and pathogen-specific variability contributed to the spread of symptoms and outcome data. We analyzed heterogeneous pathogen groups, predominantly S. Typhi (70.8%), alongside non-typhoidal Salmonella (NTS) and S. Paratyphi A, which exhibit distinct invasion mechanisms and clinical severities. S. Typhi typically presents with sustained high fever and systemic symptoms, whereas NTS infections often manifest primarily as gastrointestinal distress with different recovery trajectories. Furthermore, the co-circulation of extensively drug-resistant (XDR) clones (e.g., Hooda et al,, 11 Bangladesh; Wang et al,, 37 China) alongside susceptible strains further amplified variation in antimicrobial resistance patterns, making pooled susceptibility estimates difficult to generalize across regions.

Third, disparities in laboratory infrastructure and diagnostic modalities acted as a primary driver of variation, particularly in confirmation rates. Outbreak responses in the Western Pacific region increasingly utilize high-sensitivity molecular diagnostics, such as polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and whole-genome sequencing (WGS), allowing detection of Salmonella even after antibiotic administration or in complex clinical samples. Conversely, many investigations in African and South-East Asian settings relied on traditional blood or bone marrow culture, or serological Widal testing, which have distinct sensitivities and specificities. This diagnostic gap means that a positive result in one context is not equivalent to a positive result in another, leading to the wide observed ranges (e.g., 1.5% to 100% for overall confirmation rates).

Finally, geographic transmission dynamics played a crucial role. The dominance of waterborne transmission in Africa versus foodborne and fecal-oral transmission in Asia created distinct exposure profiles. Waterborne outbreaks typically involve larger populations exposed to common point-source contamination (e.g., boreholes), potentially altering attack rates compared to foodborne clusters often localized to specific events such as schools or markets.

4.2 Public Health Implications and Future Directions

The findings of this systematic review have several important implications for public health policy and practice, which must be tailored to regional transmission dynamics. In Africa, where waterborne transmission predominated (83.3%), interventions should prioritize infrastructure-focused strategies, including municipal water safety upgrades, protected borehole systems, point-of-use treatment, and centralized sanitation networks. Also, in Asian settings where foodborne and fecal-oral pathways are more prominent, outbreak control must emphasize food safety regulations, hygiene standards for informal food vendors and markets, safe food handling practices, and targeted community education on handwashing and fecal waste management. Second, the high case fatality rate highlights the need for strengthened clinical management protocols. This may include early recognition of severe cases, appropriate antibiotic therapy guided by local antimicrobial resistance patterns, and enhanced supportive care. Third, the alarming antimicrobial resistance trends necessitate the implementation of robust stewardship programs, real-time surveillance of resistance profiles, and the development of alternative therapeutic strategies.

Given the predominance of S. Typhi (70.8%) and high resistance to ciprofloxacin (61.3%) and other first-line agents, typhoid conjugate vaccines (TCVs) represent a critical, evidence-based intervention for outbreak prevention and control. TCVs have demonstrated approximately 80–85% efficacy in preventing typhoid fever across diverse age groups, with protection sustained for at least four years and strong herd-immunity effects.46-48 The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends routine TCV integration into national immunization programs in endemic settings, alongside targeted deployment during outbreaks to rapidly interrupt transmission. 49 Reactive vaccination campaigns in Malawi and Pakistan have successfully reduced case incidence by over 55% within weeks of deployment, highlighting their utility in crisis response.48,50 Our findings on transmission dynamics also support TCV deployment strategies. With waterborne transmission predominating in Africa (83.3%), where WASH infrastructure improvements may take decades, TCVs may offer a rapid and scalable intervention to bridge the epidemiological gap during the transition to safer water systems.

Long-term investment in WASH infrastructure remains the cornerstone of sustainable enteric disease control; however, implementation timelines in many LMICs span decades and require substantial capital, governance capacity, and behavioral change. In contrast, TCVs offer rapid, scalable, and herd-protective immunity that can bridge the epidemiological gap during WASH transition periods. The WHO Global Roadmap to 2030 for Typhoid Control explicitly advocates for a dual-track strategy: deploying TCVs for immediate disease reduction and antimicrobial resistance containment, while simultaneously scaling phased WASH improvements for enduring environmental protection. 17 Combining TCV with improved WASH is expected to yield greater population-level impact than either intervention alone, particularly in high-transmission settings, though the exact magnitude depends on local epidemiological and infrastructural context. 51 Public health policy should therefore prioritize integrated outbreak preparedness plans that mandate rapid TCV stockpiling for hotspot regions, alongside sustained investment in municipal water safety and sanitation upgrades.

Several priorities for future research emerge from this analysis. First, prospective cohort studies with standardized case definitions and diagnostic algorithms are needed to generate more reliable estimates of disease burden, clinical outcomes, and risk factors. Second, molecular epidemiological studies employing whole-genome sequencing are essential for characterizing the genetic diversity of outbreak strains, tracking transmission dynamics, and identifying infection sources. Third, implementation research is needed to evaluate the real-world effectiveness, cost-effectiveness, and logistical feasibility of combined TCV–WASH interventions during active outbreaks. Fourth, studies investigating the socioeconomic and environmental determinants of outbreaks are critical to inform targeted prevention strategies. Fifth, research on novel therapeutic agents and alternative treatment modalities is urgently needed to address the antimicrobial resistance crisis.

4.3 Limitations

The inclusion of only English-language publications may have introduced language bias. Non-English records were filtered out during the initial database searches using language restrictions; exact counts were not systematically recorded. To partially mitigate language bias, we included Google Scholar in our search strategy, which indexes a broader range of regional and non-English journals compared to proprietary databases alone. Our search window was restricted to publications from 2016–2026, but we included six studies with outbreak onset dates between 2010 and 2015 to account for typical publication delays in LMICs outbreak research. Although these studies provided unique, high-quality data, their inclusion may introduce mild temporal heterogeneity. We did not perform a sensitivity analysis excluding pre-2016 onset outbreaks due to the small number of studies and the risk of substantially reducing evidence coverage for key outcomes. Additionally, inconsistent reporting of key clinical and epidemiological metrics across the included studies represents a clear limitation. Several investigations lacked complete data on case fatality rates, hospitalization frequencies, and antimicrobial susceptibility profiles. This resulted in variable sample sizes for different pooled outcomes. Critically, age-disaggregated outcomes and baseline vaccination status were not systematically documented in the primary literature, which precluded stratified meta-analysis by age group or immunization history. This incomplete reporting limits the comprehensiveness of population-specific risk assessment. This shows the need for standardized outbreak reporting frameworks in LMICs that mandate age-stratified surveillance, vaccine coverage documentation, and consistent tracking of clinical severity in future investigations.

5.0 Conclusion

This systematic review demonstrated that Salmonella outbreaks in low- and middle-income countries remain persistent public health challenges and are driven mostly by S. typhi (70.8%) and waterborne transmission, particularly in Africa. The pooled case fatality rate of 7.0% substantially exceeds WHO targets, which reflects critical gaps in healthcare access and outbreak response. Near-universal resistance to ciprofloxacin and trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole undermines first-line treatment options. These findings indicate the need for integrated interventions. This includes strengthening water, sanitation, and hygiene infrastructure to interrupt transmission; deploying typhoid conjugate vaccines in high-burden settings; implementing robust antimicrobial stewardship and surveillance programs to monitor emerging resistance; and standardizing diagnostic protocols to improve case detection. Without coordinated, evidence-based action across clinical, public health, and policy domains, the dual burden of explosive outbreaks and multidrug-resistant strains will continue to threaten health security in vulnerable populations.
