2.2 Forced-Convection Loop and Sodium Exposure Experiments
A forced-convection sodium loop was used to simulate SFR service conditions. The loop consists
of two sodium exposure vessels with a total sodium inventory of ~20 kg. The vessels can be
operated at different temperatures, and each vessel has two sample loading ports. After about 10
years of operation, the loop experienced a sodium leak during maintenance and normal operation
was suspended. The system has since been refurbished with a new cold trap and modified piping
and returned to normal operation. Figure 3 shows the updated loop configuration.

 Figure 3. Forced-convection loop used for sodium exposure experiment

Four sample holders were assembled, each containing 24 tensile samples and two nickel foil
samples. The nickel foils were exposed at the same time to evaluate the carbon activity in sodium.
Figure 4 shows a sample holder assembly ready for insertion.

To minimize the risk of sodium vapor, sample insertions were performed at a relatively low
temperature (~300°C). Using two sample exchange chambers, the sample loading ports were
purged through several cycles of vacuum and argon backfill. After the gate and ball valves on the
sample ports were opened, the sample holders were lowered into the vessels. The system was then
sealed and heated to operating temperatures of 550°C and 600°C for two vessels, respectively.
Using two electromagnetic (EM) pumps, sodium flow was established through the cold trap and
the two vessels. Figure 5 shows a portion of the temperature history of the vessels and the cold
trap. Note that the cold trap was maintained at <130 °C to ensure low oxygen concentration in the
system.
To separate thermal-aging effects from sodium exposure effects, thermal-aging experiments were
also conducted on A709 samples. Thermal aging was performed under vacuum with the same
sample geometry shown in Figure 1. Each sample was wrapped with tantalum foil and
encapsulated in a quartz tube under vacuum (Figure 6). Encapsulated samples were then placed
in furnaces operated at the target temperatures. Thermally aged samples are being withdrawn at
selected intervals for comparison with sodium-exposed specimens.

2.3 Corrosion and Mechanical Property Evaluation
Before sodium exposure, each sample was weighed using an analytical balance with a precision
of 10 µg. Sample thickness was also measured with a micrometer. Sodium exposure and thermal-
aging experiments are currently ongoing, and samples will be retrieved at planned intervals for
evaluation.
After sodium exposure, the samples are cleaned with alcohol followed by water to remove residual
sodium. Samples are then visually inspected and re-measured for weight and thickness. These
measurements are compared with pre-exposure values to assess corrosion performance.

 7

After corrosion evaluation, tensile tests are performed on both sodium-exposed and thermally aged
specimens. All tests are conducted with an electromechanical testing system equipped with a three-
zone furnace. For each test, the test temperature is set to match the sample sodium-exposure or
thermal-aging temperature. Tests are performed at a nominal strain rate of 10-3 s-1. From each
engineering stress-strain curve, the yield stress (YS), ultimate tensile strength (UTS), uniform
elongation (UE), and total elongation (TE) are determined.

3 Sodium Chemistry
3.1 Carbon content in sodium
Carbon is a key element influencing the mechanical properties of steels. Changes in carbon
concentration in A709 and LPBF 316H SS during sodium exposure can significantly affect their
microstructure and mechanical properties. The extent of carburization or decarburization in these
alloys depends on temperature and the carbon activity of the sodium. Therefore, the carbon content
in sodium needs to be measured.
Accurate determination of carbon concentration in sodium is challenging due to low carbon
activity. Several techniques have been developed in the past, including the foil equilibration
method, electrochemical and diffusion-type carbon meters. Among these, the equilibration method
is relatively simple and cost-effective, with acceptable accuracy. The equilibration material should
have a low corrosion rate in liquid sodium, sufficiently high carbon diffusivity, and a large carbon
distribution coefficient between the material and sodium [12]. In this study, nickel was selected as
the equilibration material. High-purity (99.999%) nickel foils were placed in the 600 °C vessel
along with sodium-exposure samples. The composition of the nickel foil used in this study is
shown in Table 5.

3.2 Oxygen content in sodium
Oxygen content in sodium was also assessed using a foil-equilibration method. Vanadium was
selected as the equilibration material based on early work by Smith [14]. Four vanadium foils
with nominal dimensions of 10 x 65 x 0.125 mm were installed on a sample holder. After insertion
into the vessel, the loop temperature was increased to 600 °C and the vanadium foils were
submerged in sodium until equilibrium was reached.
After the vanadium foils were retrieved from the vessel, sodium adhering to the foils was removed
by immersion in ethanol at room temperature. After the sodium-alcohol reaction was complete,
the foils were rinsed in distilled water and dried. The foils were then electropolished in a solution
of 80 vol.% absolute methanol and 20 vol.% concentration sulfuric acid. Two platinum cathodes
separated by 3 cm and a 7.5-V open-circuit voltage were used for electropolishing. Oxygen
analysis is ongoing with the polished vanadium foils, and the result will be reported in a future
report.

4 Corrosion Results
After removal of residual sodium, A709 samples withdrawn from the exposure vessels were
measured for thickness and weight. The results are inconclusive at this point due to the relatively
short exposure time (~1000 hours). Thickness measurements show little change after exposure.
However, post-exposure weight measurements indicate a slight decline in sample mass (Table 6
and Figure 11). This apparent mass loss after short-duration sodium exposure may be associated
with the reduction of oxide layer and/or preferential leaching of alloying elements. Additional
characterization is needed to confirm this initial observation.

Figure 12 shows the engineering stress-strain curves from the baseline tests. At room temperature
(Figure 12a), all samples, regardless of their heat treatment, display similar yield and strain
hardening behavior. One precipitation-treated sample exhibits somewhat lower elongation than
the others, but still exceeds 40% total strain. At elevated temperatures (Figure 12b, c), serration
tensile curves are observed for all samples, consistent with dynamic strain aging at the test
temperatures and strain rate.
A range of uniform and total elongation is observed at 550 °C and 600 °C. It appears that the
precipitation treatment (775°C for 10 hrs) reduces elongation but does not significantly increase
strength. This effect is more evident at 600°C than at 500°C. At 600 °C, the precipitation-treated
material shows a higher strain hardening rate and a lower uniform elongation (~37%). The
deformation microstructure contributing to the reduced uniform elongation will be examined in
further work.

Thermally aged A709 samples were tested at their respective aged temperatures. Table 8
summarizes the results of 3000-hour aged samples. At 550°C, all samples show similar work

treatment does not appear to affect tensile behavior strongly after aging at 600 °C. One possible
explanation is that the precipitate microstructure evolves during 600°C aging regardless of prior
treatment, leading to similar tensile responses. The solution-annealed sample exhibiting reduced
elongation may be an outlier, and the test will be repeated.

6 Conclusions
This report establishes the experimental basis for long-term sodium exposure testing of
commercial A709 (ATI Heat 529900, solution annealed with/without precipitation treatment) and
LPBF 316H SS with as-printed surfaces in Argonne’s forced-convection sodium loop. The loop
was returned to normal operation with two exposure vessels at 550 °C and 600 °C and a cold trap
maintained at <130 °C.
Sodium carbon chemistry was assessed using the nickel-foil equilibration method. Combined with
established solubility and activity relations for carbon in nickel and sodium, the approach provided
a temperature-dependent carbon activity profile for the loop, establishing the chemistry context
needed to understand the carburization/decarburization behavior of exposed alloys.
Initial post-exposure corrosion measurements of A709 after ~1,000 h showed negligible thickness
change but a small mass decrease across conditions, consistent with early-stage surface oxide
reduction and/or preferential elemental leaching. Longer exposure durations and follow-on
surface/near-surface characterization are required before drawing quantitative conclusions on
corrosion rate.
Baseline tensile properties of the four Alloy 709 conditions were measured at room temperature,
550 °C, and 600 °C. The results showed the expected reduction in strength with increasing
temperature and serration tensile curves at elevated temperatures, consistent with dynamic strain
aging at the test temperatures. After 3,000 h thermal aging, samples aged at 550 °C exhibited
modest strengthening and reduced ductility relative to baseline, whereas results at 600 °C were not
conclusive.
Overall, the loop chemistry has been assessed, and the first set of corrosion and tensile benchmarks
has been generated. Continued testing will focus on longer sodium exposure times and integrated
microstructural characterization on both A709 and LPBF 316H SS.
