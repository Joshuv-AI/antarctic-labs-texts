Development and External Validation of a Clinically Applicable Model for Identifying Diabetic Kidney Disease in Population-Based and Clinical Cohorts
Authors: Nan-Nan Li, Lv Liu, Gao-Hui Cao, Yi Tang, Wei Peng, He-Yu Zhou, Liang-Liang Fan, Ji-Shi Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDiabetic kidney disease (DKD) is a leading cause of chronic kidney disease worldwide. Early identification of DKD remains challenging in routine clinical practice.ObjectiveWe aimed to develop and externally validate a clinically accessible model integrating metabolic and renal biomarkers for identifying DKD among individuals with diabetes.MethodsData from 3494 diabetic participants in the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) were utilized for model development. Independent predictors were identified via multivariable logistic regression. To ensure robust generalizability, external validation was conducted in two independent cohorts: a national survey cohort from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) and a real-world clinical cohort from our hospital. Model performance was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), calibration curves and decision curve analysis (DCA).ResultsThe final model incorporated age, gender, the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index, blood urea nitrogen and Cystatin C. In the CHARLS cohort, the model demonstrated robust discriminative power (AUC = 0.868) and excellent calibration. At the optimal cutoff (8.02%), the negative predictive value (NPV) reached 98.43%. In the NHANES validation, the model maintained high discriminative ability (AUC = 0.82). Crucially, in the hospital-based clinical cohort, the model demonstrated stable performance (AUC = 0.781, 95% CI: 0.648–0.888). DCA confirmed a consistent and significant net clinical benefit across a wide threshold range (0.2 to 0.8) in real world.ConclusionThe proposed model may serve as an adjunctive tool for identifying individuals with diabetes who have a high probability of prevalent DKD and who may therefore benefit from confirmatory kidney assessment.

Introduction

Diabetes has emerged as a major global public health challenge, with its prevalence increasing annually.1,2 Diabetic kidney disease (DKD) is a common complication of diabetes, and about 40% of diabetic patients will develop DKD.3 DKD is also the leading cause of end-stage renal disease (ESKD) in diabetic patients, causing approximately 30–50% of ESKD cases worldwide.3 DKD not only significantly increases the risk of cardiovascular events and all-cause death, but also places a heavy financial burden on the healthcare system.4 Therefore, early identification of high-risk individuals of DKD in diabetic population has important clinical significance.

At present, the clinical diagnosis of DKD mainly relies on urinary albumin excretion rate and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR).5,6 However, these traditional indicators have obvious limitations: on the one hand, studies have shown that a large number of patients with type 2 diabetes have renal function decline but no albuminuria, which indicates that the occurrence and progression of renal function decline may also be independent of the development of albuminuria;7 On the other hand, eGFR decreases tend to occur after renal structure and function have been significantly impaired.8 In addition, although renal biopsy is the gold standard for diagnosis, it is difficult to promote it in grassroots or large-scale screening because of its invasiveness and cost constraints.9 Therefore, there is an urgent need for a simple, non-invasive and widely applicable tool for identifying DKD in diabetic populations.

Insulin resistance plays a key role in the occurrence and progression of DKD.10 Compared with traditional insulin resistance assessment methods, triglyceride-glucose index (TyG) index is based on routine biochemical detection, which has the advantages of low cost and strong repeatability.11 As a simple and stable surrogate index of insulin resistance, TyG index has been confirmed to be closely related to cardiovascular disease, metabolic syndrome and chronic kidney disease in recent years.11–13 Although CysC and BUN are closely related to renal functional impairment, they do not fully capture the metabolic disturbances involved in DKD. TyG is a readily available surrogate marker of insulin resistance and may provide metabolic information complementary to these renal biomarkers. Therefore, integrating TyG with CysC and BUN may enable the simultaneous assessment of metabolic dysfunction and renal functional impairment, potentially improving DKD identification compared with reliance on individual biomarkers alone.

Therefore, this study utilized large-scale population data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) and the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) databases to systematically evaluate the independent association between the TyG index and DKD. Building upon this, we integrated age, gender and key renal biomarkers to construct a prediction model for DKD. Through comprehensive internal and external validation across national survey databases and an additional independent clinical cohort from our hospital, we assessed the model’s discriminative power, calibration performance and clinical decision value. By demonstrating consistent performance without relying on urine-based markers, we aim to provide a practical tool for early DKD identification.

Methods

Study Population and Data Sources

The study population was derived from CHARLS database, a nationally representative longitudinal survey targeting individuals aged 45 and older in China. Specifically, data from the third wave of the survey were utilized following a rigorous data cleaning process. The details of the study design and population characteristics have been reported previously.14 A clean analytical cohort was established by excluding participants with missing information on key predictors, including age, gender, TyG index, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), Cystatin C (CysC) and the primary outcome. A total of 3494 patients with diabetes were included, comprising 192 individuals with DKD and 3302 non-DKD. For external validation, data were obtained from NHANES. After excluding participants with missing data, an independent cohort of 741 individuals was identified, consisting of 178 patients with DKD and 563 non-DKD (Figure S1).

External validation was performed using two independent sources. First, data were obtained from the NHANES database; after excluding participants with missing data, an independent cohort of 741 individuals was identified, consisting of 178 patients with DKD and 563 non-DKD. Second, a large-scale real-world validation was conducted using a clinical dataset from our hospital, initially encompassing 30,185 participants. Following the same inclusion and exclusion criteria, a total of 60 patients with comprehensive laboratory records were specifically analyzed to evaluate the model’s performance in a targeted clinical setting.

This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Hunan Provincial People’s Hospital (Ethics Approval No.: 2024–408) and conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients for the collection of local clinical data, which were de-identified and securely stored. The NHANES data used are from a public and anonymized database, and their utilization complies with the official terms of NHANES. Throughout the study, there was no risk of data fabrication or privacy leakage. Subsequent extended studies will require re-approval from the ethics committee.

Data Collection and Endpoint Definitions

Baseline demographic and clinical data were collected, including age, gender, residence, body mass index (BMI), waist circumference, blood pressure, smoking status and physical exercise habits. Laboratory measurements encompassed fasting blood glucose (FBG), triglycerides (TG), CysC, BUN, serum creatinine (Scr), serum uric acid, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) and C-reactive protein (CRP). The TyG index was calculated using the following formula: TyG index = ln [FPG (mg/dl) × TG (mg/dl)/2]. Additional clinically relevant variables included diabetes duration, urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR), history of microalbumin testing and glucose-lowering treatment. The availability and definitions of these variables differed across data sources. Because these variables had substantial missing data or could not be consistently harmonized across the development and validation cohorts, they were summarized using variable-specific available-case denominators and were not included in the primary model. No data imputation was performed.

The diagnosis of diabetes and DKD followed established clinical guidelines based on self-reported medical history, FBG and renal functional indicators, including the eGFR and UACR. DKD was defined among participants with diabetes who exhibited an eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2. Model Establishment and Nomogram Drawing

To ensure the stability of the multivariate model, collinearity diagnosis was first conducted. The variance inflation factor (VIF) of all variables was calculated. A VIF < 2 indicates the absence of significant multicollinearity. The final model included five variables: Age, Gender, TyG index, BUN and Cystatin C. A participant was classified as having a high probability of prevalent DKD if the estimated probability exceeded the optimal cutoff of 8.02%, as determined using the maximum Youden index.

Model Validation and Evaluation

The discriminative ability of the model was evaluated using the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC). Calibration was assessed through calibration plots, bias-corrected curves generated via bootstrapping (1000 iterations), and the mean absolute error (MAE) between predicted and observed probabilities. Clinical utility was determined via decision curve analysis (DCA), which quantifies the net benefit across a spectrum of risk threshold probabilities. External validation was conducted in an independent cohort to evaluate the generalizability of the model. Furthermore, we compared the discriminative power and calibration of the integrated model against those of individual predictors.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range, IQR), while categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. For continuous variables, between-group comparisons were performed using Student’s t-test or the Mann–Whitney U-test, as appropriate. Categorical variables were compared using the Chi-square test. All analyses were two-sided, and a P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.5.2).

Independent Predictors of DKD and Multivariable Logistic Regression Analysis

Next, we try to identify the factors independently related to DKD, and establish a multivariate prediction model that can accurately identify patients with DKD among diabetic patients. Based on clinical relevance and baseline comparison results, candidate predictors were pre-selected, including multiple variables such as age, sex, waist circumference, smoking, residential status, TyG index, CysC, BUN and CRP. After collinearity analysis and multivariate logistic regression analysis, age, sex, TyG index, CysC and BUN were determined to construct a prediction model of DKD. All variables had VIF values between 1 and 2, indicating that there was no significant multicollinearity between them and a small correlation between pairs (Table S2). In multivariate logistic regression analysis, we assessed the independent effects of the above candidate variables on DKD (Table 2). CysC showed the strongest association with prevalent DKD, while age, BUN and the TyG index also remained independently associated with DKD in the multivariable analysis. Gender showed a borderline association with prevalent DKD (OR = 0.704, 95% CI 0.490–1.005; P = 0.0547). Development of a Nomogram for Estimating the Probability of Prevalent DKD

Based on the final multivariable model, a nomogram was constructed to estimate the individual probability of prevalent DKD among participants with diabetes (Figure 1). Each predictor was assigned a weighted score based on its relative contribution to DKD risk. Among all predictors, CysC contributed the most, followed by age and BUN, while gender and TyG index contributed less to the overall risk assessment. By adding the scores of each variable, an individualized total score is obtained, which corresponds to the predicted probability of DKD, ranging from about 0.2 to 0.8. Discriminative Performance and Internal Validation of the Diagnostic Model

The diagnostic model demonstrated robust performance in identifying DKD among patients with diabetes. At the optimal cutoff probability of 8.02% (corresponding to a linear predictor value of –2.4396), the model achieved a sensitivity of 75.52%, a specificity of 89.34%, and a remarkably high negative predictive value (NPV) of 98.43%, indicating a minimal risk of missed diagnosis. The bias-corrected AUC was 0.868, highlighting the model’s excellent discriminative capacity (Figure 2A). These findings suggest that the model could serve as a preferred screening tool in primary healthcare settings, potentially reducing unnecessary specialist referrals and the burden of expensive secondary examinations. Model Calibration and Clinical Utility Analysis

To further verify the utility of the model, we performed a comprehensive validation analysis. The results showed that the bias-corrected calibration curve closely aligned with the ideal reference line and largely remained within the confidence interval, indicating high consistency between the predicted probability of DKD and actual observations (Figure S2A). The mean absolute error (MAE) from internal validation was 0.011, suggesting that the model is well-calibrated with minimal deviation between predicted and observed values. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the prediction model not only possesses robust discriminative power (AUC = 0.868) but also exhibits high calibration accuracy, providing a reliable reflection of individual DKD risk in patients with diabetes.

Furthermore, DCA demonstrated that the standardized net benefit of the combined diagnostic model surpassed both the “treat-all” and “treat-none” strategies across the majority of high-risk threshold ranges, indicating significant clinical utility (Figure 2B). Notably, the net benefit of the model reached its peak at a risk threshold of approximately 8%, which is consistent with the optimal cutoff probability. This finding suggests that the model can effectively guide primary screening, minimizing the risk of missed diagnoses while reducing the burden of unnecessary further examinations.

External Validation and Generalizability in the NHANES Cohort

In the external validation cohort, a total of 741 patients were included, consisting of 563 non-DKD and 178 DKD cases. As shown in Table S3, no significant difference was observed in gender distribution between the two groups. Patients in the DKD group were significantly older. Renal function indicators, including BUN and CysC, were significantly elevated in the DKD group, while the TyG index showed no significant difference between the two groups. The median duration of diabetes was 15 years in the DKD group and 7 years in the non-DKD group. The corresponding median UACR values were 14.31 mg/g and 11.31 mg/g, respectively. Oral glucose-lowering medications were used by 41.9% of participants in the DKD group and 66.8% in the non-DKD group, whereas insulin use was reported in 32.2% and 14.3%, respectively.

The ROC curve analysis revealed an AUC of 0.82 in the external validation cohort, demonstrating favorable diagnostic performance. Consistent with the findings in the CHARLS cohort, the combined model achieved a higher AUC than any individual predictor (Figure 3A). Model calibration was also assessed in the NHANES cohort. The external calibration curve exhibited a systematic intercept shift (1.051), indicating a higher baseline risk in the NHANES population. This shift is likely attributable to the notably higher prevalence of DKD in the NHANES cohort (24%) compared to the CHARLS cohort (5.5%). Nevertheless, the model’s slope of 0.7458 suggests that the predictive trend remains highly robust across different populations (Figure S2B). These results imply that while local recalibration of absolute probabilities may be necessary for diverse populations, the model remains whereas the low slope indicated that the predicted risks were overly extreme and generalizable for risk-ranking and identifying high-risk individuals. Furthermore, DCA demonstrated that the model maintained a higher standardized net benefit compared to both the “treat-all” and “treat-none” strategies across a wide range of risk thresholds, further confirming its clinical utility (Figure 3B).

Performance and Clinical Utility Analysis in an Independent Clinical Validation Cohort

To further evaluate the model’s performance in a real-world clinical setting, we conducted an additional external validation using an independent cohort (n = 60) collected from our hospital. The clinical characteristics of the hospital validation cohort are summarized in Table S4. Compared to the national survey databases, this cohort represented a specialized hyperglycemic population with a DKD prevalence of 73.3%. Significant differences were observed in renal functional markers (BUN and Cystatin C) between the DKD and non-DKD groups, providing a rigorous basis for testing the model’s discriminative capacity.

In this clinical cohort, the model maintained a robust discriminative ability with an AUC of 0.781 (95% CI: 0.648–0.888). We further compared the model’s performance against individual biomarkers (Figure 4A). The combined model significantly outperformed the metabolic-based TyG index (AUC = 0.587). Although the AUC was numerically higher but statistically comparable to Cystatin C (AUC = 0.780) and BUN (AUC = 0.773), the integrated model demonstrated a more balanced profile for multi-dimensional risk assessment. Discussion

Through the CHARLS cohort, we assessed the association between the TyG index and prevalent DKD and developed a multivariable model for identifying prevalent DKD by integrating metabolic and renal function measures. The model achieved high AUCs in training (0.868), NHANES validation (0.82), and an independent hospital cohort (0.781). With a 98.43% NPV, it effectively excludes DKD and offers significant clinical net benefit. This blood-based tool provides a practical, non-invasive screening alternative for real-world settings where urine-based testing is often unavailable or impractical.

Insulin resistance can lead to glomerular hyperfiltration, oxidative stress, inflammation and tubulointerstitial fibrosis.18–21 The TyG index is considered a marker of insulin resistance (IR), and its relationship with diabetic nephropathy has also been studied in recent years.22,23 Studies have pointed out that TyG index is a potential predictor of DKD in patients with type 2 diabetes, and its AUC is 0.69.24 This is consistent with our findings. Our multivariate analysis confirms that TyG index is an independent risk factor for DKD. However, as a single predictor, its ability to identify DKD from diabetic patients is poor, and its AUC is only 0.521. This is quite different from the results of previous studies, possibly because TyG index, a metabolic index, is more closely related to type 2 diabetes.25 In addition, some studies have found a nonlinear relationship between TyG index and mortality caused by CVD and other factors in DKD patients, indicating its effectiveness in evaluating potential adverse events of DKD.23,26,27 Notably, we showed a small difference in TyG index between DKD and non-DKD groups in univariate analysis but became significant in multivariate models. This may indicate that the predictive value of contemporary metabolic markers may be enhanced when considered together with age and renal function indicators. This was further corroborated by our hospital cohort results, where the integrated model provided a significant diagnostic lift over the TyG index alone, suggesting that the value of metabolic markers is substantially enhanced when combined with age and renal indicators.

In recent years, CysC has been shown to be more sensitive than serum creatinine to reflect early glomerular filtration rate (GFR) decline, because it is less affected by muscle mass or dietary intake.28,29 Consistent with previous literature, CysC was the strongest predictor in our model (AUC = 0.835). However, by combining CysC with BUN and metabolic markers, its diagnostic efficacy is improved to 0.868. It is worth noting that the diagnostic efficacy of CysC in NHANES is only 0.657, while the diagnostic efficacy of the multi-variable diagnostic model has increased to 0.82, which further illustrates the diagnostic ability of the multi-variable model. Although CysC is still a powerful predictor of renal function, our comprehensive diagnostic model combined with TyG index, a marker of insulin resistance, can more comprehensively assess the risk of DKD. The clinical cohort validation highlighted this synergy: while single markers like CysC are powerful, our multivariable model bridged the gap between purely renal assessment and metabolic risk evaluation, providing a more comprehensive risk profile.

Our study found that age and BUN are independent risk factors for DKD, which is also consistent with previous studies. Age is an identified non-interventionable risk factor for DKD, which may be associated with the cumulative effects of long-term hyperglycemia in conjunction with age-related structural alterations of the kidney, such as nephrosclerosis and decreased functional nephrons.30,31 BUN, as a traditional marker, has been included in routine clinical evaluation for the assessment of renal function.32 Its measurement provides important information about the kidney’s ability to excrete urea, a waste product of protein metabolism, which can be used as a supplement to CysC.33 The combined use of BUN and CysC can improve diagnostic accuracy, especially in early detection of nephrotoxic injury and kidney injury.34 Therefore, there are not only CysC but also bun in our diagnostic model. Although gender did not reach an obvious level of statistical significance in multivariate models, there are many studies showing differences associated with gender and DKD incidence and progression, which are influenced by gender hormones, obesity patterns, as well as healthcare differences.16,35 Despite its borderline statistical significance, gender was retained in the model because documented sex-related differences in DKD susceptibility and progression support its clinical and biological relevance.17

The model showed high discrimination (AUC 0.868) and excellent calibration. With an NPV of 98.43%, it is ideal for excluding DKD in low-risk patients. DCA confirmed significant clinical utility, with net benefits exceeding “treat-all” or “treat-none” strategies at an 8.02% threshold. This utility was reaffirmed in the hospital dataset across a 0.2–0.8 threshold range. Validation in the NHANES (AUC 0.82) and hospital (AUC 0.781) cohorts highlights strong generalizability. The substantial differences in DKD prevalence across the three cohorts should also be considered when interpreting model performance. DKD prevalence increased from 5.5% in CHARLS to 24.0% in NHANES and 73.3% in the hospital cohort. Because predictive values and calibration depend on the underlying event rate, estimates obtained from one cohort may not be directly transferable to populations with different baseline risks. Although AUC is less directly affected by prevalence, differences in case mix and disease severity may have contributed to the reduced discrimination observed in the hospital cohort. Moreover, the hospital cohort included only 60 participants, resulting in limited precision and potentially unstable performance estimates. Therefore, these findings should be regarded as preliminary and require confirmation through larger, multicenter prospective validation and local recalibration.

Emerging biomarkers may provide complementary biological information regarding DKD. A cross-sectional study reported that maresin 1 (MaR1) and chitinase-3-like protein 1 (CHI3L1) were oppositely associated with renal dysfunction, suggesting that the early identification of DKD may rely on capturing the interplay between metabolic inflammation and kidney injury.36 Our model prioritizes routinely available blood-based variables, while future studies should determine whether these emerging biomarkers such as MaR1 can provide additional value beyond the TyG, CysC and BUN.

Notably, the model demonstrated strong discriminative performance in both the CHARLS and NHANES cohorts; however, its performance declined in the hospital-based validation cohort, with the AUC decreasing to 0.781 and the calibration slope to 0.327. These findings may reflect differences in clinical characteristics across the study populations. Compared with participants recruited from community-based surveys, patients in real-world hospital settings often have more severe acute comorbidities, more complex medication burdens, and greater heterogeneity in the underlying causes of kidney dysfunction. The low calibration slope indicates that the predicted risk differences may have been overly extreme in this high-risk clinical setting. Therefore, the model may serve as a simple blood-based tool that does not require urine testing for the preliminary identification and risk stratification of DKD at the population level; however, its results should be interpreted cautiously in clinical practice. Local recalibration and prospective validation in larger, multicenter hospital cohorts are essential before routine clinical implementation.

Despite its strengths, this study has limitations. First, its retrospective design precludes causal inference and may involve residual confounding. Therefore, real-world research is needed before routine application. In addition, the cross-sectional design and contemporaneous assessment of the predictors and DKD status preclude interpretation of the model as a tool for predicting future DKD onset. Prospective longitudinal studies are therefore required to determine whether the model can predict incident DKD before renal involvement is clinically established. Second, despite descriptive differences observed between the DKD and non-DKD groups, diabetes duration, quantitative proteinuria, and detailed medication usage were not incorporated into the primary prediction model due to substantial missingness and variable heterogeneity across cohorts.37 Future prospective studies with granular, standardized tracking of longitudinal clinical variables are warranted to validate and refine our findings. Third, relying on routinely accessible variables enhances feasibility but overlooks genetic or imaging markers, potentially missing biological heterogeneity. Furthermore, while the model shows robust discrimination across diverse populations, variations in DKD prevalence caused systematic calibration shifts. This indicates that local recalibration may be necessary when applying the tool to populations with significantly different risk profiles. Prospective studies are needed to confirm these findings before routine clinical implementation.

Conclusion

In summary, we developed and externally validated a blood-based model integrating the TyG, CysC, BUN, age and gender to identify prevalent DKD among individuals with diabetes. Although the model demonstrated favorable discrimination without requiring urine samples, its performance in the hospital setting warrants caution regarding clinical translation. Further prospective validation and local recalibration in larger and more diverse clinical populations are required before routine implementation.
