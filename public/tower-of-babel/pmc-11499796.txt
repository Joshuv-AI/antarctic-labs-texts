Characterisation of myeloid cells in circulation and synovial fluid of patients with psoriatic arthritis
Authors: Noah Fine, Michael Glogauer, Vinod Chandran, Katerina Oikonomopoulou
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivePsoriatic arthritis (PsA) is an inflammatory arthritis associated with psoriasis. Adding to studies focused on the role of T cells and macrophages, we sought to investigate the systemic activation of leukocytes in PsA.MethodsWe assessed the activation state of leukocyte populations, including polymorphonuclear neutrophils (PMNs) and monocyte/macrophages, in blood and synovial fluid (SF) by multicolour flow cytometry. We also evaluated the correlation between leukocyte numbers and expression of activation markers with disease activity parameters.ResultsSF PMNs showed an elevated activation state compared with blood PMNs, but a reduced activation state compared with oral PMNs of non-arthritic controls. In vitro stimulation caused SF PMNs to become further activated, demonstrating that they retain a reserve capacity for activation in response to specific triggers. We found significant variability between patients in the expression of SF PMN CD activation markers, indicating a range of possible activation states across patients. However, PMN CD marker expression remained consistent over two sequential visits in a subset of patients, indicating patient-specific distinct inflammatory states during flares. We further found that markers of disease activity increased with elevated SF macrophage numbers. Expression of several CD markers on blood or SF cells, for example, PMN expression of the high-affinity Fc-receptor CD64, correlated with disease activity markers, including pain score and Disease Activity in Psoriatic Arthritis score.ConclusionThese preliminary findings support a potential role for surface antigens on PMNs and monocytes/macrophages as prognostic or disease activity monitoring tools.Keywords: Psoriatic arthritis, Synovial fluid, Inflammation, Arthritis

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

With this pilot study, we performed an analysis of PMN and monocyte/macrophage activation markers to better understand the inflamed microenvironment in the PsA joint and in the circulation and its correlation with disease activity parameters. We demonstrated that systemic markers of disease activity may be related to increased synovial fluid (SF) macrophage load, and several markers of activation in SF and/or blood cells, including the blood PMN expression of the high-affinity Fc-receptor, CD64.

Introduction

Psoriatic arthritis (PsA) has a characteristic pattern of joint involvement, which includes inflammation, monocyte and polymorphonuclear neutrophil (PMN) infiltration, cartilage degradation and new bone formation.1 Despite the availability of targeted therapies, persistent inflammation remains an issue.2 Identification of novel therapeutic avenues is hampered by the lack of a thorough understanding of the exact contribution of the immune and related cells associated with PsA and their relationship with disease endotypes.

Among the immune cells involved in PsA, monocytes/macrophages play a major role.3 In response to specific triggers, these cells exhibit a wide phenotypic spectrum with reversible dynamic plasticity that allows them to contribute to both the inflammatory and resolution phases of arthritis.3 The presence of activated macrophages in the joint has been shown to correlate with osteoarthritis (OA) pain and severity. Specifically the double-positive CD14+CD16+/total synovial fluid (SF) macrophage ratio is a significant predictor of patient-reported outcomes reflecting pain and stiffness in OA.4 It has been shown that PsA is associated with an increased frequency of CD14+CD16+ monocytes in the circulation.5 Our group has further described different macrophage populations in PsA SF, where both pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory subpopulations were identified, but cells with intermediate phenotype seemed to have a primary role.6

PMNs also seem to have a role in the inflamed joints of patients with PsA, but their systemic and localised role in disease is not well defined. PMNs are recruited to tissues in response to inflammatory stimuli, and once in the three-dimensional tissue matrix, they are exquisitely sensitive to diverse inflammatory cues, which trigger appropriate protective functions, including phagocytosis, degranulation, formation of neutrophil extracellular traps and/or cytokine secretion.7 PMN activation and their responses are highly attuned to the specific threat encountered at the site of inflammation8 9 and can include pro-inflammatory and inflammation-resolution activities. PMNs display significant phenotypic and functional diversity in a range of inflammatory conditions, including some types of arthritis.10 PMN infiltration is associated with disease severity in arthritis, while PMN depletion/inhibition results in decreased inflammation and bone damage in animal models of arthritis.11 12 PMNs have important roles in both the acute and chronic phases of inflammation in arthritis,13 14 and are abundant in synovial tissues of patients with PsA.15 The role of PMNs in PsA is reinforced by the observation that PMN depletion prevents skin inflammation in animal models of psoriatic disease,16 however, their precise role in PsA, both systemically and in the joint, has not been fully elucidated.

As part of our previous work, we have designed a panel of PMN markers of activation, which include CD66a, CD11b, CD18, CD63, CD64, CD16 and CD55.8 9 With the exception of CD16, which was used to gate on PMNs in blood and SF, upregulated surface expression of these markers is associated with a primed or activated PMN state.8 9 In this work, we employed our flow cytometric approach to assess leukocytes in the blood and SF of patients with PsA.

Materials and methods

Blood (n=9; 7 individual patients, 2 of which had a repeat visit) and SF (n=14; 9 individual patients, 4 of which had a repeat visit - 1 patient had both knees affected at repeat visit) samples from patients with PsA attending the Psoriatic Arthritis clinic at Toronto Western hospital, and blood and saliva/oral-rinse samples from healthy volunteers at the University of Toronto Faculty of Dentistry, were obtained after signed informed consent, in accordance with the Helsinki declaration of 1975-revised in 2013. The patients with PsA satisfied the Classification Criteria for Psoriatic Arthritis and had at least one painful and swollen joint that required aspiration for therapeutic purposes. Exclusion criteria included the concomitant presence of infection, cancer or other inflammatory diseases. Blood was collected in vacutainer tubes by a trained phlebotomist. SF was aspirated by a rheumatologist in sterile containers and immediately sent to the clinical laboratory for routine clinical tests to rule out infection and crystal-induced arthritis, as well as to the research lab for processing. Saliva samples (non-arthritic-referred to as healthy volunteer oral-rinse) were collected as previously described8 and used as reference samples that have previously demonstrated high levels of immune cell activation. Briefly, volunteers were instructed to rinse 10 times with 3 mL of cold saline solution, and the collected oral-rinses were pooled. At the time of assessment, PsA patients completed questionnaires and the assessing rheumatologist completed a standard protocol that documented measures of skin and joint disease activity including the Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI), tender and swollen joint counts, dactylitis and enthesitis count, pain score (Visual Analogue Scale, range 0–10) and the Disease Activity Index for Psoriatic Arthritis [with or without C-reactive protein (CRP); DAPSA and cDAPSA]. Blood was also sent for routine laboratory tests including testing for the CRP levels.

Flow cytometry

Fixation, labelling and flow cytometric analysis were performed as previously described.8 9 Briefly, whole blood samples were fixed with methanol-free formaldehyde (1.6% final concentration) for 15 min on ice prior to processing. Red blood cells were lysed by repeated treatment with Pharm Lyse (BD Biosciences) for 5 min on ice. To preserve the selected surface marker expression levels, aspirated SF and oral-rinse samples were fixed with fresh, methanol-free formaldehyde (1.6% final concentration) for 15 min on ice and filtered through an 11 µm mesh filter. Cell counts were acquired on a Coulter Counter (Beckman) with a size range of 8–12 µm. Cells were resuspended in fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) buffer (Hanks−/−, 1% bovine serum albumin, 2 mM EDTA), blocked for 20 min, washed and then labelled with a multi-marker antibody cocktail for 30 min on ice in the dark and washed three times with FACS buffer.

PMNs (SSChi/CD16+ve) and monocytes/macrophages (CD16−ve/CD64+ve) were gated by flow cytometry, and cell percentages, combined with raw counts, were used to determine the absolute counts. Surface expression of CD66a, CD11b, CD18, CD63, CD64, CD16 and CD55 in SF and blood-derived cells were determined as markers of PMN priming/activation. Sample acquisition was performed using an LSR Fortessa or X-20 (BD Biosciences) flow cytometer. Flow cytometer channel voltages were calibrated manually using rainbow beads and compensation was performed with single-stained OneComp eBeads (eBioscience). At least 2×105 gated events were acquired per sample. Data were analysed using FlowJo software (V.10; TreeStar). Human multicolour flow cytometry panels and gating of cells from blood and oral-rinse samples were performed as previously described.8 Gating of SF cells was conducted based on the same surface markers as blood and oral-rinse (online supplemental figure 1). Appropriate isotype control antibodies were used to generate fluorescence minus one samples to establish negative staining for each antibody. For cell stimulation assays, 100 µL of phosphate-buffered saline containing 1×106 SF-derived cells was incubated for 30 min at 37°C, in the presence or absence of formyl-Met-Leu-Phe (fMLP, Sigma-Aldrich) or pHrodo (Invitrogen).

Statistical analysis

The Wilcoxon paired t-test, linear regression analysis and Spearman correlation coefficient analysis were performed using the GraphPad Prism software (V.7.00) as indicated in each figure. P value≤0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

SF PMNs characterisation

Surface marker expression on SF PMNs was compared with expression levels on blood (naïve/healthy or from PsA patients) and non-arthritic reference oral-rinse (pro-inflammatory/activated) PMNs. Based on the surface expression of several markers of activation,8 we found that SF PMNs had an expression profile that was intermediate between blood and oral-rinse PMNs (figure 1A, online supplemental figure 2). Cytospins indicated the presence of mixed leukocyte populations in blood and SF, confirming the flow cytometric results (figure 1B). Oral-rinse cells were primarily PMNs, as anticipated based on our previous findings.8 While blood PMNs were naïve and oral PMNs were activated, the intermediately-activated SF PMNs retained a significant capacity for further activation when stimulated in vitro with fMLP or pHrodo, confirming that PsA SF PMNs are in a partially-activated state (figure 1C). PMN CD marker expression exhibited a natural variability between patients. In order to evaluate further this observed variation over time, we sampled SF aspirates for a group of patients longitudinally. Four patients, who were returning to the clinic due to recurring PsA flares, underwent a repeat aspiration and their SF cells were analysed using our flow cytometry panel (figure 1D). We found that, for two patients (patient 1 and patient 2), the expression of all used CD markers was remarkably consistent, demonstrating a signature level of PMN activation. Patient 3 and patient 4 had a stable expression of CD63, CD64 and CD16, however, they showed reduced expression of CD66 and CD18 on the repeat visit. CD11b expression was increased for patient 3 and decreased for patient 4 on the second visit. The reason behind these differences in PMN activation marker levels is unclear, but suggests some degree of variability in the inflammatory microenvironment of the synovial space over time in these patients, in alignment with the heterogeneous nature of PsA across patients and over the course of the disease.

Systemic markers of PSA disease activity correlate with cell numbers and expression of cell markers

Correlation analysis was performed to determine whether leukocyte activation state in the blood or SF of PsA patients or cell counts in the SF were associated with clinical measures of PsA disease activity, including blood CRP, PASI (index of psoriasis activity), pain score, DAPSA and cDAPSA. We found that blood CRP and DAPSA had a trend towards correlation with macrophage counts per mL of SF (figure 2A; P=0.17 and 0.13, respectively; r=0.39 and 0.42, respectively), indicating a potential association between disease activity and macrophage load in the joint. Many of the CD markers tested were significantly correlated with measures of PsA disease activity on at least one of the two leukocyte subsets from the two biological fluids evaluated (figures2 3 and online supplemental table 1). We found a statistically significant correlation between pain score and DAPSA with blood PMN surface expression of the high-affinity Fc-receptor, CD64 (figure 2C). However, PsA activity measures were not significantly correlated with the expression of CD64 on blood monocytes (figure 2B), SF macrophages (figure 2D) or SF PMNs (figure 2E). Expression of CD55, the decay accelerating factor, on blood monocytes and blood PMNs was inversely correlated with PASI (figure 3A and B). Expression of CD18, the β2-integrin, on blood PMNs (figure 3B) and SF macrophages (figure 3C) was directly correlated with pain score, on SF macrophages (figure 3C) inversely correlated with PASI, and on SF PMNs (figure 3D) was inversely correlated with PASI. CD63 expression on SF macrophages was inversely correlated to CRP (figure 3C). Expression of CD11b on SF PMNs was inversely correlated to PASI, while CD66a expression on SF PMNs was directly correlated with pain score (figure 3D).

Discussion

Using samples derived from mouse models, we have previously identified tissue-specific PMN activation states in healthy and inflamed tissues17 by flow cytometry. Blood PMNs were shown to be in a resting state compared with tissue PMNs, as determined by the expression of cell surface activation markers. Similarly, PMNs in human saliva have been shown to be highly activated compared with circulating, blood PMNs.8 Furthermore, PMNs from saliva/oral-rinse are in an advanced activation state in patients that have periodontal disease,8 compared with healthy controls. These findings indicate that localised inflammatory or microbial (in the case of oral-rinse samples) cues can influence the PMN phenotype. Furthermore, we have shown that local tissue inflammation can alter the priming/activation state of PMNs in circulation.9

To our knowledge, the current study is the first attempt to phenotype PMNs in the SF of patients with PsA. Through comparison of our pilot cohort with healthy volunteers from a previous study,9 which used the same CD marker panel utilised in this work, we found that blood PMN activation marker expression was similar in healthy volunteers and patients with PsA (see: figure 1 and online supplemental figure 2). In the samples obtained from patients with PsA, SF PMNs had higher expression of surface markers of activation compared with blood PMNs. Consistent with our previous tissue-derived cell characterisation work,17 we found that SF PMNs are in an activation state that appears intermediate between blood and saliva, which is consistent with a lower-grade inflammatory microenvironment compared with the mouth cavity, a microbe-rich microenvironment, but in a higher-grade inflammatory activation state comparing to blood.

Significant myeloid cell counts were found in SF aspirates from all the patients with PsA in our study, all of whom presented clinically with swelling. We found that macrophage numbers in the SF were correlated (trend) with systemic markers of disease activity (figure 2A). In addition, blood PMN expression of the high-affinity Fc-receptor, CD64, was correlated to disease activity (figure 2C). Increased CD64 expression on PMNs has previously been identified as a biomarker for inflammatory and autoimmune conditions, including arthritis, inflammatory bowel disease, lupus and Behçet’s disease.18 19 Increased CD64 expression on monocytes has previously been identified as a marker of disease activity in polyarticular PsA20 and rheumatoid arthritis.21 CD64 expression on SF PMNs or macrophages was not correlated with PsA disease activity. Further studies are required to evaluate the potential value of CD64 in monitoring disease activity/prognosis in PsA. In addition to CD64, we identified several other surface markers whose expression was correlated with PsA disease activity on blood or SF cells (figure 3), including CD55, CD18, CD11b, CD63 and CD66a, either directly or inversely (a potential result of marker ‘shedding’ on activation). Our results indicate that PsA severity can impact the phenotype of blood leukocytes, suggesting that cell phenotypic modulation therapies (eg. reference 22) with the goal to reverse the systemic and/or localised recruitment or function of pro-inflammatory cells could potentially decrease or prevent inflammation and lower the risk of associated comorbidities in patients with PsA.

Another important note is that the intermediate state of activation of SF PMNs in patients with PsA may be a key factor on how these first-line inflammatory cells associate with and are cleared by macrophages during efferocytosis, a major process with a key role in the resolution of inflammation.23 Subsequent studies should, therefore, investigate whether modulating the activation state of PMNs can instigate the efferocytic clearance in the inflamed joints, resulting in a potential therapeutic approach for PsA.24

Overall, our results indicate that the activation state of blood and/or SF leukocytes in PsA patients is associated with disease activity. Future studies are required to compare the SF PMN phenotypes in PsA patients with naïve/non-arthritic reference SF PMNs, as well as with blood and oral-rinse samples derived from a larger cohort of patients with PsA. Furthermore, similar to our previous monocyte/macrophage investigations, comparison with other forms of arthritis including osteoarthritis, a less inflammatory control commonly used in PsA investigations, as well as the more inflammatory chronic and acute types of arthritis, rheumatoid arthritis and gout, respectively, should be considered. Such investigations would be valuable to fully characterise the pro-inflammatory functional/phenotypic potential of local and systemic PMNs in patients with PsA and leverage this knowledge towards the therapeutic modulation of neutrophil (and monocyte/macrophage) phenotype/function for the management of PsA. Further longitudinal studies are required to confirm our findings.
