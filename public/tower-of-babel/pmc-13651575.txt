Optimizing Mydriasis in Cataract Surgery: Efficacy and Safety of Intracameral Regimen vs Topical Cyclopentolate in RayOne EMV Implantation
Authors: Adam Kabiesz, Karol Demel, Piotr Gościniewicz, Daria Jorg, Patryk Zemła
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to compare the safety and efficacy of phacoemulsification performed using topical cyclopentolate (Cykloftyal) vs standard intracameral mydriatic and anesthetic combination (Mydrane) in patients undergoing implantation of enhanced monofocal intraocular lenses.Material/MethodsThe medical records of 100 patients (100 eyes, 50 per group) were retrospectively analyzed. Patients received either intracameral Mydrane alone or topical cyclopentolate with intracameral lidocaine. Pupil diameter at the start and end of surgery, surgical duration, visual acuity, intraoperative complications, and postoperative adverse events were compared.ResultsThere was no statistically significant difference in surgical duration between the intracameral Mydrane group (8.80 min) and the topical cyclopentolate plus intracameral lidocaine group (9.24 min; P = 0.54), nor in postoperative visual acuity at any distance. However, the initial pupil diameter was significantly larger in the topical cyclopentolate group (7.39 ± 0.47 mm) than in the intracameral Mydrane group (6.08 ± 1.05 mm; P < 0.005), indicating a very large clinical effect size (Cohen’s d = 1.61). Pupil size at the end of the procedure showed no significant difference between the groups. No major intraoperative complications, such as posterior capsule rupture, were observed in either group.ConclusionsIn this cohort, topical cyclopentolate served as a safe and effective alternative to intracameral mydriasis, offering significantly wider initial pupil dilation while maintaining comparable surgical efficiency in uncomplicated cataract cases. Given its cost-effectiveness and wide accessibility, it remains a valuable strategy for resource-limited settings and complex cases requiring maximal visualization. Its potential utility in complex cases requiring maximal visualization remains a hypothesis to be evaluated in future prospective studies.

Introduction

Cataract remains the leading global cause of reversible blindness [1], with over 20 million phacoemulsification procedures performed annually [2,3]. Therefore, continuous optimization of surgical safety and efficiency remains a primary clinical objective. A critical factor in achieving these goals is obtaining a sufficiently wide pupil, which provides optimal access to the lens and deeper intraocular structures [4]. To obtain adequate mydriasis, a number of preparations are used in the form of ophthalmic drops and intracameral solutions. One of the standard mydriatic drugs widely used is topical cyclopentolate (Cykloftyal 10 mg/mL; 5 mL, Verco, Warsaw, Poland). Alternatively, the concept of intracameral mydriasis, first introduced in 2003, has become an established alternative to traditional topical regimens [4]. Standardized intracameral combinations (Mydrane) have demonstrated proven efficacy, significantly improving both perioperative workflow and patient comfort, as evidenced by recent prospective studies [5,6].

The aim of this retrospective study was to compare the safety and efficacy of phacoemulsification performed following the administration of topical cyclopentolate vs the standard intracameral mydriatic combination Mydrane in patients undergoing implantation of the enhanced monofocal intraocular lens.

Material and Methods

Study Design and Ethics

Medical records of patients who underwent cataract surgery were reviewed. All procedures were performed in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration on Human Medical Research and its subsequent amendments or comparable ethical standards. All methods were conducted in accordance with guidelines and regulations. Prior to the surgery, all patients or their legal guardians gave informed, written consent for cataract phacoemulsification with artificial lens implantation. Our retrospective analysis of patients’ medical records fell into a category exempt from institutional review board (IRB) approval. Retrospective, anonymous, analysis of patients’ data does not require IRB approval according to local law.

Surgical Protocol and Grouping

In this non-randomized retrospective comparison, patients were categorized into 2 groups based on the mydriatic protocol they had received during their procedure. The assignment of the mydriatic method was historically determined by the surgical workflow and the availability of the intracameral preparation on specific operating days. The first group included 50 patients (50 eyes) who underwent cataract surgery with RayOne EMV intraocular lens implantation using intracameral administration of 0.2 mL of Mydrane (0.2 mg tropicamide, 3.1 mg phenylephrine hydrochloride, and 10.0 mg lidocaine hydrochloride; Thea, Clermont-Ferrand, France) without preoperative topical mydriatic agents. The second group included 50 patients (50 eyes) who underwent cataract surgery with RayOne EMV implantation using preoperative topical cyclopentolate and intracameral lidocaine. The protocol involved administration of 2 drops of cyclopentolate into the conjunctival sac approximately 30 minutes before surgery, with a 5-minute interval between applications. To ensure patient comfort and a painless procedure, 0.1 mL of 2% lidocaine solution (lidocainum hydrochloricum WZF 2%, Polfa Warsaw, Warsaw, Poland) was administered intracamerally immediately after the side-port incision (paracentesis), after pupil width measurement and before viscoelastic injection.

In both groups, patients with visual acuity below 0.6 decimal and a confirmed diagnosis of cataract were eligible for surgery.

Immediately before disinfection of the surgical site and application of the eyelid dilator, all patients received 1 drop (0.05 mL) of proxymetacaine hydrochloride (Alcaine, 5 mg/mL, Alcon, Geneva, Switzerland) topically. During phacoemulsification, a cohesive viscoelastic with a density of 1.4% was used in all patients. To ensure postoperative emmetropia, RayOne EMV intraocular implants were calculated as close as possible to zero postoperative refraction in all eyes. All calculations for selecting the appropriate intraocular lenses were performed using the Carl Zeiss IOL Master 500. The formulas used were in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations: Holladay 1 for axial lengths between 22 and 25 mm, Hoffer Q for below 22 mm, and SRK/T for above 25 mm.

Data Collection and Exclusion Criteria

Pupil diameter was measured intraoperatively by the operating surgeon under the high magnification of the operating microscope using a calibrated surgical caliper (Castroviejo caliper). The use of surgical caliper is a documented and clinically accepted method in recent ophthalmic research to evaluate pupillary dynamics [7]. This approach allows for real-time, direct assessment of mydriatic efficacy at critical surgical time points, consistent with methodologies used in recent studies investigating intracameral mydriatics. To ensure accuracy, the diameter was calculated as the mean of 2 perpendicular measurements (horizontal and vertical). All measurements were performed by the same experienced surgeon under standard microscope illumination at 2 time points: strictly before the administration of any ophthalmic viscosurgical device and immediately prior to capsulorhexis and at the completion of surgery (during the administration of intracameral cefuroxime; Aprokam, Thea, Clermont-Ferrand, France). This timing was chosen specifically to ensure that the measured pupil diameter reflected the pharmacological effect of the mydriatic agents alone, without the confounding factor of mechanical dilation caused by ophthalmic viscosurgical device injection. At the end of surgery, normotensive intraocular pressure was established by the surgeon prior to the final pupil measurement. Postoperative intraocular pressure was measured on the day of the procedure using an iCare rebound tonometer. Crucially, the pupil exhibited no reactivity to changes in microscope light intensity, confirming that the obtained mydriasis was stable. Additionally, the duration of the surgery was recorded for each case. The start of surgery was explicitly defined as the moment of the first clear corneal incision, and the end was defined as the completion of stromal hydration.

Safety assessment was comprehensively defined based on intraoperative, subjective, and postoperative parameters. Intraoperative safety was evaluated by recording the incidence of major surgical complications, specifically posterior capsule rupture, vitreous loss, and zonular dialysis. Additionally, the operating surgeon provided a subjective assessment of procedural difficulty for each case. Postoperative safety was assessed at follow-up visits by monitoring for adverse events, including transient corneal edema, intraocular pressure elevation (> 21 mm Hg), and ocular surface symptoms such as photophobia, dry eye exacerbation, pain, or transient conjunctival hyperemia and irritation.

Exclusion criteria included a history of glaucoma, pseudoexfoliative syndrome, diabetes, retinal or optic nerve diseases, and allergy to any ingredient of the substances used during standard procedures. The presence of posterior and anterior synechiae or prior ophthalmic surgery (besides cataract surgery in the other eye) also excluded patients from admittance into the study groups. The absence of mydriasis after the use of topical preparations at previous visits, which could suggest the possibility of intraoperative floppy iris syndrome or lens subluxation, was also considered an exclusion criterion. While the frequency of intraoperative floppy iris syndrome may increase from the population level of about 1% to 2% up to 90% after using Tamsulosin in the treatment of prostatic hyperplasia, none of the pharmacological methods of obtaining stable mydriasis guarantee success. Out of 159 eyes in the studied period, 100 were selected for analysis.

Statistical Analysis

A descriptive analysis was performed to characterize the study population and verify the collected data. A 95% CI was used, and the distribution of quantitative variables was analyzed. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to determine distribution of variables. All results regarding normality testing were not statistically significant (P > 0.05), meaning variables followed a normal distribution. Since the variables followed a normal distribution, they were expressed as means and their standard deviations (SD). Comparative analysis was performed to evaluate differences between the study groups. The following factors were taken into consideration: patient age, preoperative visual acuity for distance, duration of surgery, and pupil diameter at the beginning and end of the procedure.

Quantitative variables were analyzed using the t test for independent samples. To determine the clinical relevance of the observed differences, the effect size was calculated using Cohen’s d index. Effect sizes were interpreted according to standard criteria: 0.2 represents a small effect, 0.5 a medium effect, and 0.8 a large effect. Homogeneity of variance was tested using Levene’s test, and the level of statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. Levene’s test reported lack of statistical significance (P > 0.05). All calculations and statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 26 (Armonk, New York, USA).

Results

The study included 100 patients (100 eyes), including 56 males and 44 females, with a mean age of 54.6 (± 4.7) years. Baseline characteristics were comparable between the groups; preoperative visual acuity was 1.04 ± 0.46 logMAR in the topical group (cyclopentolate) and 0.96 ± 0.42 logMAR in the intracameral group (mean difference: 0.08; P = 0.36), confirming the comparability of the study population despite the retrospective design. Occurrence of hypertension was also studied. In the topical group, 8 patients had hypertension, while in the intracameral group, 11 had hypertension (P = 0.61). Patients in both groups were White race. There were no eye injuries reported in either group. Descriptive statistics for of the 2 groups are presented in Table 1.

Surgical Outcomes

A statistically significant difference was observed in pupil diameter at the onset of capsulorhexis. Patients in the topical cyclopentolate group achieved a significantly larger mean pupil diameter (7.39 ± 0.47 mm) compared with the intracameral group (6.08 ± 1.05 mm). The mean difference was 1.31 mm (P < 0.005, 95% CI: −1.63 to −0.99). Notably, the calculated effect size for this parameter was very large (Cohen’s d = 1.61), indicating a strong clinical benefit of the topical preparation (Figure 1).

No statistically significant difference was found regarding pupil size at the end of the procedure (6.82 ± 0.49 mm vs 6.77 ± 1.18 mm; P = 0.77; Cohen’s d = 0.06) or in the duration of the surgery (9.24 min vs 8.80 min; P = 0.54), where the effect size was negligible (Cohen’s d = 0.12). This suggests that while cyclopentolate provided better initial dilation, both methods allowed for efficient surgery without prolonging operative time. The comparative analysis is summarized in Table 1.

Regarding safety, no major intraoperative complications, such as posterior capsule rupture, vitreous loss, or zonular dialysis, were observed in either group. The operating surgeon also reported no subjective differences in surgical difficulty. The mean postoperative intraocular pressure measured with the iCare tonometer on the day of surgery was stable and physiological (approximately 14 mm Hg) across both groups. Minor postoperative adverse events included transient corneal edema, temporary intraocular pressure elevation (> 21 mm Hg), exacerbation of dry eye symptoms, photophobia, transient reduction in visual acuity, mild eye pain, and transient conjunctival hyperemia and irritation (Table 2). In total, these minor events occurred in 12 cases in the topical group and 10 cases in the intracameral (P = 0.81). Some patients presented more than 1 adverse event. All resolved completely within the first week with standard postoperative care. The level of pupillary dilatation was sufficient to perform an appropriately wide capsulorhexis and further stages of the procedure. In the topical group, additional viscoelastic was used to stabilize the anterior chamber in 5 cases, and in the intracameral group, in 4 cases. It should be noted that both methods of obtaining pharmacological mydriasis proved to be very effective; patients receiving Mydrane and those treated with cyclopentolate achieved appropriate pupil sizes allowing for the surgery. No concerns were reported during the procedures, and pupil dilation was maintained throughout the surgery.

Discussion

Our findings indicate that topical cyclopentolate provides significantly greater pupil dilation at the onset of capsulorhexis compared with the intracameral Mydrane regimen (mean difference: 1.31 mm; Cohen’s d = 1.61, P < 0.005). The exceptionally large effect size (Cohen’s d = 1.61) observed for this initial pupil diameter merits contextualization regarding both the study design and clinical relevance thresholds. This magnitude is an expected outcome influenced by the differing administration protocols; topical mydriatics are administered preoperatively, allowing them to reach maximum absolute dilation, whereas intracameral regimens are administered intraoperatively. Robust pooled data from international clinical trials evaluating standardized intracameral combinations [8] underscore these distinct temporal pharmacokinetics, noting that the conventional topical route relies on an extended preoperative window for corneal penetration. However, despite this variation in initial absolute diameter, intraoperative fixed combinations deliver reliable mydriatic efficacy and excellent pupillary stability throughout the active surgical procedure [8]. Furthermore, contemporary clinical parameters establish that a baseline pupil size of at least 6.0 mm serves as an adequate clinical relevance threshold for performing uncomplicated cataract surgery. Therefore, the effect size observed in our study reflects a discrepancy in temporal pharmacokinetics and measurement baselines inherent to the study design rather than a clinical deficit, as the absolute initial pupil sizes in the intracameral group (6.08 ± 1.05 mm) remained well above the required threshold for safe and efficient surgery. This aligns with the results of the meta-analysis by Iftikhar et al [9], in which topical regimens generally yielded wider pupils than intracameral protocols alone. However, unlike previous studies suggesting that the greater and more sustained mydriasis achieved with topical regimens may improve surgeon comfort and potentially reduce intraoperative complications in cases of limited visualization [10], we did not observe a significant difference in procedure duration (P = 0.54). This suggests that while Mydrane yields a more constricted pupil, it is still sufficient for experienced surgeons to perform phacoemulsification safely and efficiently without prolonging the operation.

It is also worth addressing the use of intracameral lidocaine in the intracameral group. While unpreserved intracameral lidocaine is known to possess a mild independent mydriatic effect, the operating surgeon observed that maximal and stable mydriasis had already been achieved following the preoperative topical application of cyclopentolate. However, because this is a retrospective review and specific measurements delineating the effect of lidocaine alone were not recorded, we must cautiously acknowledge that its independent contribution to the final measured pupil size cannot be fully excluded. While clinical observation suggests this additive mydriatic effect was likely minor due to the preexisting cycloplegia, the presence of intracameral lidocaine remains a potential pharmacologic confounder in this comparative analysis.

From a clinical workflow perspective, the choice between these 2 methods involves a trade-off between logistical efficiency and maximal dilation. Unlike standardized intracameral formulations, which typically entail higher costs and supply chain dependencies, topical cyclopentolate is widely accessible and cost-effective. These attributes make it a particularly advantageous option in environments with budget constraints or in high-volume cataract centers, where minimizing reliance on expensive intracameral drugs can offer significant economic benefits without compromising surgical outcomes. As recently highlighted by Dereń-Szumełda et al, intracameral administration provides rapid and stable mydriasis within seconds, effectively streamlining the surgical workflow [10]. Furthermore, intracameral administration significantly reduces systemic exposure to mydriatic agents through the conjunctiva and nasal mucosa, thereby minimizing the risk of cardiovascular adverse effects such as tachycardia or hypertension, which is particularly beneficial in elderly patients with comorbidities [8].

It is important to emphasize that our study population consisted of uncomplicated cataract cases. While the profound mydriatic effect of topical cyclopentolate observed here raises the hypothesis that it could be particularly advantageous in complex cases requiring maximal visualization, such as dense cataracts, this extrapolation remains unsupported by our present data. Therefore, any suggestions regarding the superior efficacy of topical protocols specifically for complex cases remain strictly hypothetical and require rigorous evaluation in future prospective, targeted studies. Long-term follow-up studies confirm that intracameral protocols are safe for the corneal endothelium [11], supporting their routine use.

Due to the presence of three active substances in Mydrane, patients may theoretically have a higher likelihood of intolerance to 1 or more components of the preparation. In such cases, the use of cyclopentolate may represent an effective alternative, allowing the surgeon to safely perform the procedure. At the same time, we must consider the potential adverse effects of cyclopentolate and the need to exercise special caution when using it in older adults due to their increased susceptibility to systemic anticholinergic toxicity. Potential adverse reactions include central nervous system disturbances such as disorientation, ataxia, hallucinations, and behavioral changes, as well as urinary retention or tachycardia.

In 2021, the results of a meta-analysis were published, covering 14 randomized clinical trials examining the benefits of using different methods and drugs to ensure adequate pupil width for cataract surgery [9]. The comparison of intracameral administration of mydriatic substances with the local application of drops included an analysis of 8 randomized clinical trials. In 4 studies (739 patients, 757 eyes), in which the pupil width was assessed at the time of initiation of capsulorhexis, all authors indicated better results achieved after local than intracameral application; however, due to small mean differences (1.06 mm), it is difficult to draw clear conclusions [9]. In an analysis of 5 studies, in which the pupil width was assessed after the procedure (799 patients, 817 eyes), different results were obtained, which lacked a clear enough answer as to which type of application would be more effective. The authors reached the same conclusions by analyzing the average time of the procedures, without clearly indicating a better method [9]. Currently, there are many studies in the scientific literature comparing the efficacy of different methods of obtaining mydriasis while maintaining patient comfort [4,9,12].

Limitations of the Study

The present study has several limitations inherent to its retrospective design. Ideally, a prospective, comparative study design would be more suitable for thoroughly evaluating pupil size during cataract surgery. The relatively small sample size (n = 100) and non-randomized design introduce a potential risk of selection bias. Specifically, group assignment was determined by surgical workflow and drug availability, which may have resulted in systematic differences between groups. Although baseline characteristics were comparable, advanced statistical adjustments or matching to account for potential confounding were not performed and should be acknowledged. Despite the large effect size observed for pupil diameter, which strengthens the statistical validity of the findings, this allocation method remains a limitation.

The single-center design may also limit the generalizability of the results to other surgical settings with different preoperative protocols. Furthermore, pupil size in older adults is influenced by multiple physiological factors. Future protocols should include a preoperative assessment of baseline pupil dilation to ensure that participants can achieve a pupil diameter of at least 6.0 mm before inclusion, thereby reducing potential confounding.

Another limitation related to the retrospective design was the lack of additional standardized pupil measurement time points (eg, immediately before the initial corneal incision and 30–40 seconds after intracameral administration), as these data were not routinely available in historical medical records. Such standardized intervals should be incorporated into future prospective studies.

Additionally, standardized patient-reported outcome measures, such as the Visual Function Index-14 questionnaire, subjective satisfaction surveys, contrast sensitivity assessments, and photic phenomena evaluations, were not collected. Although baseline comparability between groups was demonstrated, other potentially relevant factors, including cataract density and additional ocular comorbidities, could not be fully accounted for.

Finally, third-generation formulas were used for intraocular lens (IOL) power calculation. Although newer-generation formulas may provide greater predictive accuracy, this limitation is unlikely to affect the primary outcome of the study, which was the comparative efficacy of the mydriatic agents. Future prospective, randomized studies with larger cohorts are warranted to confirm these findings.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this analysis indicates that while both the intracameral Mydrane and topical cyclopentolate regimens allowed for safe and effective phacoemulsification in this retrospective cohort, topical cyclopentolate provided significantly superior initial mydriasis. Importantly, the smaller pupil diameter observed with the intracameral protocol did not translate into prolonged operative times or increased complication rates in the hands of an experienced surgeon. The topical regimen offers a cost-effective and widely accessible alternative, making it particularly advantageous for healthcare systems with budget constraints. Therefore, topical cyclopentolate remains a valuable tool for routine, uncomplicated phacoemulsification, whereas intracameral Mydrane offers a streamlined workflow with comparable safety and surgical efficiency. Whether the superior maximal pupil dilation achieved with the topical regimen translates into a definitive clinical advantage for complex cases remains a hypothesis in need of future prospective investigation. The choice of mydriatic strategy should be tailored to the specific clinical scenario and logistical needs of the surgical center.
