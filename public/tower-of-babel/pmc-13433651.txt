Role of Renal Biomarkers in Sarcopenia or Frailty in Patients With Liver Cirrhosis
Authors: Jung Hee Kim, Jae Yoon Jeong, Ju-Yeon Cho, Soo Jin Park, Heon-Ju Kwon, Kyoung A Ko, Hyunwoo Oh, Yong Kyun Cho, Byung Ik Kim, Won Sohn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSarcopenia and frailty are risk factors for poor prognosis in patients with liver cirrhosis. This study investigated the role of renal biomarkers in sarcopenia or frailty in patients with cirrhosis.MethodsA total of 78 patients with liver cirrhosis and serum creatinine levels < 1.5 mg/dL were analysed for serum renal biomarkers (creatinine-to-cystatin C ratio, interleukin [IL]-18, kidney injury molecule [KIM]-1, and neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin [NGAL]), sarcopenia, and liver frailty index. Sarcopenia was assessed using the skeletal muscle index of the third vertebra. We analysed the risk of sarcopenia or frailty based on various renal biomarkers of cirrhosis.ResultsThe mean age was 59.2 years and 67.9% were men. The Child-Pugh class B score was noted in 17.9%. Sarcopenia or frailty (pre-frail and frail) was observed in 43 patients (55.1%). No significant differences in serum IL-18 (P = 0.285), NGAL (P = 0.537), or KIM-1 (P = 0.482) levels were observed according to sarcopenia or frailty. However, the creatinine-to-cystatin C ratio was significantly lower in patients with sarcopenia or frailty than in those without sarcopenia or frailty (0.73 vs. 0.83, P = 0.002). Multivariable analysis indicated that the risk factors for sarcopenia or frailty were Child-Pugh class B (odds ratio [OR], 9.04; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.06–76.85; P = 0.044) and creatinine-to-cystatin C ratio < 0.8 (OR, 4.03; 95% CI, 1.45–11.25; P = 0.008). Serum IL-18, NGAL, and KIM-1 levels were not associated with sarcopenia or frailty.ConclusionA lower serum creatinine-to-cystatin C ratio was associated with an increased risk of sarcopenia or frailty in patients with cirrhosis and favourable renal function.

INTRODUCTION

Sarcopenia, characterized by progressive skeletal muscle wasting, is highly prevalent among individuals with malnourishment who have cirrhosis and has emerged as a significant prognostic indicator, with reported prevalence rates ranging from 30% to 70%.123 Sarcopenia is associated with adverse clinical outcomes including reduced overall survival, impaired quality of life, increased incidence of cirrhosis-related complications, and poor post-transplant prognosis.23456 Frailty, sarcopenia, and malnutrition are interrelated conditions that share overlapping pathophysiological mechanisms and often progress in parallel with liver disease severity.7 Therefore, early detection and management of these conditions are critical for the comprehensive care of patients with cirrhosis. However, standardised assessment of sarcopenia remains challenging. Although imaging modalities, such as computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging offer accurate evaluations, their clinical applicability is limited by cost, availability, and concerns regarding radiation exposure.89 Clinical tools such as the liver frailty index (LFI) and the 6-minute walk test are currently employed to assess frailty in cirrhosis; however, the role of biochemical markers in this context remains unclear.7

In patients with cirrhosis, serum creatinine levels frequently underestimate renal function due to reduced creatinine production secondary to sarcopenia, a common complication in cirrhosis.10 In order to better estimate the renal function in this population, cystatin C, a glomerular filtration marker that is independent of muscle mass has been proposed as a more reliable alternative.11 In addition, novel biomarkers, such as neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL), kidney injury molecule (KIM)-1, and interleukin (IL)-18, have been explored as early indicators of renal injury in patients with cirrhosis.12 Interestingly, the creatinine-to-cystatin C ratio has been proposed as a surrogate marker of sarcopenia in various populations without cirrhosis, including individuals with diabetes or malignancies and those receiving dialysis.131415

Studies on the association of renal biomarkers with sarcopenia or frailty in patients with cirrhosis and normal serum creatinine levels remain limited. Therefore, we aimed to investigate the relationship between renal biomarkers, sarcopenia and frailty in patients with cirrhosis and evaluate their potential utility as predictive markers for the early identification of individuals at risk.

METHODS

Study design and patients

This study enrolled patients with cirrhosis admitted at Kangbuk Samsung Hospital, Seoul, Republic of Korea. The study participants were recruited between May 2022 and January 2023. The inclusion criteria were 1) diagnosed with liver cirrhosis, 2) age ≥ 20 years, and 3) serum creatinine ≤ 1.5 mg/dL. We excluded participants with the following criteria: 1) ongoing hemodialysis or peritoneal dialysis, 2) history of kidney transplantation, and 3) a recent history of acute kidney injury defined as serum creatinine > 1.5 mg/dL within 6 months. Clinical characteristics, blood tests, urinalysis, and dynamic contrast-enhanced CT were investigated. A questionnaire was used to assess the clinical characteristics including the aetiology of liver cirrhosis, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, dyslipidemia, currently administered drugs, history of esophageal or gastric variceal hemorrhage, ascites, hepatic encephalopathy, and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). The patients were evaluated for platelet count, hemoglobin, prothrombin time-international normalised ratio (PT-INR), serum albumin level, total bilirubin, aspartate transaminase, alanine transaminase, serum sodium, total cholesterol, triglyceride, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, fasting blood glucose, hemoglobin A1c, Child-Pugh score, and model for end-stage liver disease score.

Renal biomarkers

Renal function was assessed with serum creatinine, cystatin C, IL-18, NGAL, KIM-1, spot urine protein, and spot urine creatinine levels. Serum IL-18, NGAL, and KIM-1 levels were measured using Quantikine enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay human immunoassay (R&D Systems, Inc., Minneapolis, MN, USA). The serum IL-18, NGAL, and KIM-1 levels were determined as the mean values of two measurements. In addition to the above-mentioned biomarkers, we assessed renal function using the serum creatinine-based estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR),16 serum cystatin-C-based eGFR,16 and serum creatinine-to-cystatin-C ratio.

Nutrition, sarcopenia, and frailty

The Royal Free Hospital-Nutritional Prioritising Tool (RFH-NPT) was used to screen the nutritional status.17 RFH-NPT was developed to screen for the nutritional status of patients with chronic liver disease. It includes three components: BMI, weight loss, and dietary intake. Malnutrition risk by RFH-NPT was classified as low (score 0), moderate (score 1), or high (scores 2–7).

Body composition was evaluated using abdominal CT with Anyvol V.1.4 software (iAID Inc., Seoul, Korea). An axial CT image at the level of the inferior endplate of the third lumbar vertebra (L3) was selected for analysis. Skeletal muscle area (SMA, cm2) was defined as the sum of the areas of the paraspinal, psoas, transversus abdominis, interior and exterior obliques, and rectus abdominis muscles, using a predefined CT attenuation range of −29 to +150 Hounsfield Units (HU). Subcutaneous adipose tissue (cm2) and visceral adipose tissue (cm2) were also delineated and quantified using a predefined HU range of −190 to −30. SMA was then normalized for height (m2) and expressed as the skeletal muscle index (SMI, cm2/m2). Sarcopenia was defined as an SMI of < 36.54 cm2/m2 for men and < 30.21 cm2/m2 for women, in accordance with previously established criteria.18 The Japanese Society of Hepatology (JSH) criteria for Asian populations (SMI < 42 cm2/m2 in men and < 38 cm2/m2 in women) was also utilized.19

The LFI was used to assess frailty in patients with liver cirrhosis.20 LFI, developed as a tool to measure physical function in patients with liver cirrhosis, includes three performance-based tests (grip strength, chair stand, and balance).20 Frailty was classified based on LFI scores of < 3.2 (robust), 3.2–4.4 (pre-frail), and ≥ 4.5 (frail).

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were analysed using descriptive statistics. The mean value with standard deviation was used for continuous variables and frequencies with percentages were used for categorical variables. Univariable and multivariable logistic regression models were used to assess the risk factors (including renal biomarkers) for sarcopenia or frailty in patients with liver cirrhosis. Multivariable analysis of the logistic regression model was performed using all variables that were associated with the outcome at P < 0.05 with a minimum of ten outcomes for each independent variable. Forward conditional stepwise procedure was used to avoid multicollinearity. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using the SPSS Statistics ver. 24.0 (IBM Co., Armonk, NY, USA).

Ethics statement

All study participants provided written consent for the analyses of their blood samples for research purposes. The Institutional Review Board of Kangbuk Samsung Hospital approved this study (KBSMC 2021-12-003), and participants for the study were approached and given both written and verbal information regarding the study. Written informed consent was obtained from every participant prior to enrolment. The study was performed in accordance to the principles expressed in the Helsinki Declaration of 1996.

Risk of sarcopenia or frailty according to renal biomarker

The differences in renal biomarkers according to sarcopenia or frailty (pre-frail and frail) in patients with liver cirrhosis are shown in Fig. 2. Sarcopenia or frailty was observed in 43 (55.1%) patients. No significant differences in serum IL-18 (205.8 ± 90.0 vs. 236.7 ± 149.0 pg/mL, P = 0.285), NGAL (110.8 ± 42.6 vs. 104.3 ± 49.0 ng/mL, P = 0.537), and KIM-1 (224.6 ± 136.9 vs. 250.5 ± 178.7 pg/mL, P = 0.482) levels were observed according to sarcopenia or frailty. However, the serum creatinine-to-cystatin C ratio was significantly lower in patients with sarcopenia or frailty than in patients without sarcopenia or frailty (0.73 ± 0.12 vs. 0.83 ± 0.16, P = 0.002).

The univariable and multivariable analyses of the risk factors for sarcopenia or frailty in patients with liver cirrhosis are shown in Table 3. The univariable analysis revealed that sarcopenia or frailty was associated with the female sex (odds ratio [OR], 2.88; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.03–8.04; P = 0.043), Child–Pugh class B (OR, 14.73; 95% CI, 1.82–119.4; P = 0.012), cystatin C-based eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 (OR, 6.32; 95% CI, 1.66–24.03; P = 0.007), and serum creatinine-to-cystatin-C ratio < 0.8 (OR, 5.59; 95% CI, 2.09–14.96; P = 0.001). In the multivariable analysis, the significant risk factors for sarcopenia or frailty were Child–Pugh class B (OR, 9.04; 95% CI, 1.06–76.85; P = 0.044) and serum creatinine-to-cystatin-C ratio < 0.8 (OR, 4.03; 95% CI, 1.45–11.25; P = 0.008).

Univariable and multivariable analyses of risk factors for sarcopenia or frailty in patients with liver cirrhosis (Supplementary Table 1) based on the JSH criteria was also conducted. According to the JSH criteria for Asian populations, the prevalence of sarcopenia was 39.7% (31/78) and the prevalence of sarcopenia or frailty was 44.9% (35/78). The results were consistent with our original findings, showing that a serum creatinine-to-cystatin C ratio < 0.8 remained an independent risk factor for sarcopenia or frailty. The OR in the univariable and multivariable analyses was 2.62 (95% CI, 1.02–6.75; P = 0.046) and 4.26 (95% CI, 1.35–13.39; P = 0.013), respectively.

DISCUSSION

This study investigated the association of renal biomarkers with sarcopenia or frailty in patients with liver cirrhosis. Among the renal biomarkers analysed, serum creatinine-to-cystatin C ratio was significantly lower in patients with sarcopenia or frailty than in patients without sarcopenia or frailty, whereas no significant differences were observed in serum IL-18, NGAL, and KIM-1 levels between the two groups. Multivariable analysis demonstrated that a lower creatinine-to-cystatin C ratio (< 0.8) and Child-Pugh class B score were independently associated with an increased risk of sarcopenia or frailty. Thus, creatinine-to-cystatin C ratio may serve as a useful surrogate marker for the early identification of patients with cirrhosis at risk of sarcopenia or frailty, even in patients with normal serum creatinine levels.

Historically, sarcopenia, frailty, and malnutrition in cirrhosis have been perceived as sequelae of progressive hepatic decompensation rather than independent prognostic entities, thereby limiting their recognition compared to established biochemical indicators, such as bilirubin, albumin, and prothrombin time.21 However, growing evidence supports their critical prognostic significance, demonstrating an association with increased morbidity, mortality, waitlist attrition, and inferior post-transplant outcomes.521222324 Nevertheless, the clinical evaluation of these conditions remains challenging owing to a lack of standardised definition and diagnostic criteria specific to the population with cirrhosis. Conventional anthropometric measures, such as BMI or body weight, are often rendered inaccurate in the context of advanced cirrhosis due to the presence of fluid overload conditions such as ascites and peripheral oedema.25 Moreover, the existing literature is limited by selection bias because patients with pronounced frailty or advanced sarcopenia are often excluded from clinical trials, resulting in a paucity of data for this vulnerable subgroup.23

The present study utilised a multidimensional assessment strategy that included LFI, CT-derived L3 SMI, and RFH-NPT to comprehensively evaluate nutritional and functional status. By demonstrating a significant association between the creatinine-to-cystatin C ratio and both frailty and sarcopenia, the study results highlight the potential utility of this ratio as a practical and reproducible biomarker to enhance clinical risk stratification and guide early interventions in patients with cirrhosis.

As creatinine is primarily derived from muscle tissue, loss of muscle mass may lead to an overestimation of renal function when assessed using serum creatinine. By contrast, cystatin C is produced by all nucleated cells and is less affected by muscle mass, sex, or dietary intake, making it a more reliable marker of the glomerular filtration rate.26 In patients with liver cirrhosis, serum cystatin C can better reflect renal function and enable the early detection of renal dysfunction regardless of muscle mass or sex compared to serum creatinine.2728 The key determinant of the discrepancy between these two markers is skeletal muscle mass. In other words, although serum creatinine is an unreliable indicator of renal function in patients with cirrhosis, its strong dependence on skeletal muscle mass persists. Accordingly, the serum creatinine-to-cystatin C ratio can serve as an indirect and practical surrogate marker of sarcopenia in this population. Based on this rationale, Kashani et al.29 have proposed serum creatinine-to-cystatin C ratio as a simplified method for identifying patients with reduced muscle mass. Since then, this ratio has been shown to correlate well with muscle mass and clinical outcomes in various acute and chronic diseases.131430 In general, a higher creatinine-to-cystatin C ratio is associated with a better health status and a lower mortality risk.31 Our multivariable analysis demonstrated that a serum creatinine-to-cystatin C ratio < 0.8 was independently associated with a significantly higher risk of sarcopenia or frailty. This finding highlights the potential clinical utility of creatinine-to-cystatin C ratio as a surrogate marker of muscle mass and function, particularly in patients with liver cirrhosis.

To date, studies investigating the relationship between the creatinine-to-cystatin C ratio, sarcopenia, and frailty in patients with liver cirrhosis remain limited. In a prospective study conducted in China involving 109 patients with cirrhosis, the creatinine-to-cystatin C ratio showed a better diagnostic performance for malnutrition than the Subjective Global Assessment and cholinesterase levels, with an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) of 0.833.32 Similarly, in a retrospective study of 91 patients with liver cirrhosis complicated by primary HCC, the creatinine-to-cystatin C ratio was identified as an independent risk factor for sarcopenia.33 However, the AUROC for predicting sarcopenia in that study was < 0.75, indicating only a modest discriminatory ability. Furthermore, although the creatinine-to-cystatin C ratio is part of the Hospital Italiano de Buenos Aires score, which effectively predicts sarcopenia and mortality in liver transplant candidates, it was not statistically significant in univariate analysis for sarcopenia prediction.34 These findings highlight the need to identify specific subgroups to which the creatinine-to-cystatin C ratio can be applied more effectively as a predictive tool. The strength of our study was its comparative analysis of various renal biomarkers to predict sarcopenia and frailty in patients with liver cirrhosis, highlighting creatinine-to-cystatin C ratio as a potential surrogate marker.

This study has several limitations. The number of patients was relatively small, which may have limited the generalisability of the findings. In addition, the observed prevalence of sarcopenia was lower than that reported in previous studies,123 likely due to the limited inclusion of patients with decompensated cirrhosis, such as those on liver transplantation waiting lists, where sarcopenia is more common. Hence, we were unable to perform a detailed analysis of the relationship between the creatinine-to-cystatin C ratio and the distinct components of muscle health, namely muscle quantity (sarcopenia) and muscle quality (frailty). Furthermore, the prognostic impact of the creatinine-to-cystatin C ratio on long-term outcomes, such as overall survival in patients with cirrhosis, could not be assessed in this study. Despite these limitations, patients with liver cirrhosis were prospectively enrolled, and sarcopenia and frailty were assessed using multiple validated tools, including the LFI, L3 SMI, and RFH-NPT. These multidimensional assessments enabled us to examine the association between creatinine-to-cystatin C ratio and muscle status, along with the potential relevance of other renal biomarkers in this population. In liver cirrhosis, where traditional assessments of nutritional and functional status are often challenging, the creatinine-to-cystatin C ratio may be a simple, inexpensive, and non-invasive tool for identifying patients at a higher risk of adverse outcomes related to muscle loss.

In conclusion, a lower serum creatinine-to-cystatin C ratio was independently associated with an increased risk of sarcopenia or frailty in patients with cirrhosis and preserved renal function. Large-scale prospective studies are warranted to validate its prognostic value and further explore its utility in routine clinical assessments.
