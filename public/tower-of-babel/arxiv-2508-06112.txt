Structural Equation Modeling with Latent Variables and Composites


Abstract
Structural equation modeling (SEM) is a prevalent approach for studying constructs. Traditionally, these constructs are modeled as reflectively measured latent variables – common factors that account for the variance-covariance structure of their associated indicators. Over the past two decades, there has been growing interest in an alternative way of modeling constructs: the composite, i.e., a linear combination of indicators. However, existing approaches to estimating composite models either limit researchers from fully leveraging SEM’s capabilities, such as handling missing data, evaluating overall model fit, and testing group differences, or significantly increase complexity of the model specification by introducing additional variables. Against this background, this paper presents SEM with latent variables and composites. Our presented model specification, along with its model-implied variance-covariance matrix, enables researchers to: (i) utilize well-established SEM estimators, including maximum likelihood and generalized least squares estimators, and (ii) fully exploit SEM’s capabilities in model specification, assessment, and missing data handling. This advancement aims to enhance the flexibility and applicability of SEM in analyzing constructs.

Structural equation modeling (SEM) is a widely used statistical technique to study constructs including their relationships, i.e., relationships among constructs and their relationships with observed variables, so called indicators
(
Bollen 1989a
)
.
Its versatility has made SEM indispensable in various disciplines, including psychology
(
MacCallum & Austin 2000a
)
, criminology
(
Higgins 2002a
)
, and business research
(
Hult et al. 2006a
)
.
By encompassing a variety of statistical techniques such as confirmatory factor analysis
(
Jöreskog 1969a
)
, confirmatory composite analysis
(
Schuberth et al. 2018b
)
, regression analysis
(
Graham 2008a
)
, and latent growth curve analysis
(
McArdle 1988a
)
, SEM provides a comprehensive framework for testing theoretical models.

In research across various disciplines, accurately modeling constructs – whether constructs that are measured like mental health
(e.g.,
Gerino et al. 2017a
)
or constructs that are formed like socio-economic status
(
Chen et al. 2025a
)
– is crucial
(e.g.,
Bollen & Bauldry 2011a
;
Strauss & Smith 2009a
)
.
These constructs, often not directly observed, are inferred from a set of other variables.
Traditionally, SEM relies on latent variables, i.e., common factors explaining the variance-covariance structure of their indicators
(
Spearman 1904a
;
Jöreskog 1969a
)
.
Consequently, latent variables are assumed to be the underlying common cause of their indicators.
An example is illustrated in Figure
1
, where the latent variable
\eta
is the common cause underlying its measures
y_{1},\dots,y_{P}
and therefore explains the variance-covariance structure of the measures.
The variance of a measure
y_{i}
that cannot be explained by the latent variable is captured by the corresponding random measurement error
\varepsilon_{i}

(
Bollen 1989a
)
.
This approach is particularly useful for modeling constructs that are measured such as intelligence, emotions, or attitudes
(
Henseler 2021a
)
.
Hereafter, we refer to structural equation models involving latent variables as
SEM with latent variables
.

Several approaches to SEM with latent variables have been developed.
Traditionally, estimators that minimize the discrepancy between the model-implied and the sample variance-covariance matrix of the observed variables are used to estimate the model parameters.
These include approaches such as normal-theory-based generalized least squares
(
Jöreskog & Goldberger 1972a
;
Browne 1974a
)
and weighted least squares
(
Browne 1974a
)
.
The maximum likelihood (ML) estimator
(
Jöreskog 1969a
;
Jöreskog 1970a
)
is nowadays regarded as the gold standard for estimating structural equation models
(
Hoyle & Gottfredson 2023a
)
.
Moreover, alternative approaches have emerged that rely on composites, i.e., linear combinations of observed variables for latent variables.
This includes methods such as sum score regression
(
Richardson 1936a
)
and partial least squares path modeling
(PLS-PM,
Wold 1975a
)
.
Initially touted for their smaller sample requirements and milder distribution assumptions, these approaches are known to yield biased parameter estimates when used for SEM with latent variables
(e.g.,
Schuberth et al. 2023b
;
Schuberth et al. 2024a
)
.

However, not all constructs are assumed to be the underlying cause of their indicators
(
Rhemtulla et al. 2020a
)
; for example, constructs like customer loyality are more likely formed by variables such as recommending the company, choosing the company in the future again, and regretting to have chosen the company in the past
(
Hair et al. 2022a
)
.
Thus, some constructs are better represented as composites
(
Hwang et al. 2020a
;
Schuberth 2023a
;
Cho et al. 2022a
;
Henseler 2017a
)
.
Specifically, using a latent variable to represent a construct that is not the underlying cause of its indicators can result in poor construct validity, where the modeled construct fails to represent the actual construct of interest
(
Rhemtulla et al. 2020a
)
.
This misalignment can lead to substantial parameter biases, particularly in relationships between constructs, and adversely affect model assessment
(e.g.,
Schuberth et al. 2024a
)
.
In such cases, a researcher risks to draw wrong conclusions from their analysis.

In contrast to assuming that the construct is the underlying common cause of its indicators, as in the case of latent variables, modeling constructs as composites assumes that the construct is composed of its indicators
(
Yu et al. 2021a
;
Henseler & Schuberth 2020c
)
.
Modeling constructs as composites is particularly useful if the construct is formed by humans
(
Henseler 2021a
)
or if the construct is a collection of heterogeneous causes
(
Grace & Bollen 2008a
)
.
For example, extraversion in human development research has been modeled as a composite composed of warmth, gregariousness, assertiveness, activity, excitement seeking, and positive emotions
(
Edwards & Bagozzi 2000a
)
; The firm performance index in information systems research has been modeled as a composite composed of customer relationships, operational excellence, and revenue growth
(
Rai et al. 2006a
)
; Soil conditions in ecology has been modeled as a composite composed of soil texture, soil moisture, and soil ph
(
Verheyen et al. 2003a
)
.
As Figure
2
illustrates, composites do not impose restrictions on the variance-covariance structure of their indicators, fundamentally distinguishing them from latent variables.
Composites are therefore useful if the construct is formed by a set of other variables.
Further applications of composites in SEM include item parceling
(
Little et al. 2002a
)
and modeling fixed-weights composites, i.e., linear combinations of observed variables with given weights, like sum scores
(
Schuberth et al. 2025a
)
.
In line with the definition of SEM with latent variables,
SEM with composites
includes the analysis of any structural equation model with composites.

Traditional SEM is regarded as limited when dealing with structural equation models involving composites
(
MacCallum & Browne 1993a
)
.
While alternative methods such as PLS-PM
(
Wold 1975a
)
and generalized structured component analysis
(GSCA,
Hwang & Takane 2004a
)
can deal with composites, they do not fully leverage the comprehensive capabilities of traditional SEM approaches, such as model assessment and handling missing data.
The problem is exacerbated when both latent variables and composites are included in a model simultaneously.

To overcome these limitations, we introduce
SEM with latent variables and composites
. SEM with latent variables and composites refers to the specification, estimation, identification, and assessment of structural equation models that involve both composites and latent variables.
Our method allows seamless embedding of composites within the SEM framework.
Specifically, we present a model specification that allows composites to be embedded in the SEM framework in such a way that (i) estimators known from SEM with latent variables can be easily applied, (ii) we can deal with models containing both composites and latent variables, and (iii) we allow for different scaling methods for both composites and latent variables.
As a result, model assessment criteria, approaches to dealing with missing values and other approaches developed in the traditional SEM framework can be applied.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows.
We begin by reviewing the current state of SEM regarding composites and latent variables.
We then introduce our approach by explaining specification, identification, estimation, and assessment of these models.
Further, our approach is implemented in the open-source R package
lavaan

(
Rosseel 2012a
;
Rosseel et al. 2025a
)
.
We discuss this implementation followed by a scenario analysis that illustrates how to specify a structural equation model involving composites and latent variables and that demonstrates that our approach can retrieve the population parameters in case of a sample that perfectly represents the population.
Next, we illustrate our approach by an empirical example that shows that the results of our approach are similar to those of other approaches to study structural equation models with latent variables and composites.
We conclude with a discussion on our approach.

1
Extant Approaches to Include Composites (and Latent Variables) in a Structural Equation Model

SEM with latent variables has a long tradition and remains the default in many disciplines.
Its origins lie in the introduction of factor analysis
(
Spearman 1904a
)
and path analysis
(
Wright 1934a
)
.
Its popularity increased due to the work of Karl Gustav
Jöreskog 1969a
;
Jöreskog 1970a
and the introduction of LISREL
(
Jöreskog & Sörbom 1993a
)
.
Detailed overviews of SEM’s history can be found in
Tarka 2017a
and
Bollen et al. 2022a
.

In contrast, SEM with composites originated with Herman Wold’s development of PLS-PM
(
Wold 1975a
)
.
Initially, composites were primarily seen as a means for dimension reduction, and researchers did not ascribe any conceptual unity to the components of a composite
(
Bollen & Bauldry 2011a
)
.
This perspective shifted with the advent of theoretical frameworks that provided a rationale for modeling constructs using composites
(
Diamantopoulos & Winklhofer 2001a
;
Henseler 2017a
;
Henseler & Schuberth 2020b
;
Sarstedt et al. 2016a
;
Fornell & Bookstein 1982a
)
, alongside the formal establishment of the composite model
(
Dijkstra 2013a
;
Dijkstra 2017a
)
.

Despite growing awareness of different types of modelling constructs and the serious consequences of incorrect construct modeling, incorporating composites in a structural equation model remains challenging.
Over the past decades, various approaches to dealing with composites in SEM have been introduced.
These can be divided into (i) estimators specifically designed for composite models and (ii) composite specifications aimed at incorporating composites into traditional structural equation models.
The following two subsections discuss these approaches, including their advantages and disadvantages.

1.1
Estimators for Composite Models

The first approach to handling composites in SEM involves using estimators specifically designed for this purpose.
This category includes methods such as PLS-PM and GSCA, along with their various extensions.

PLS-PM is arguably the most widely employed estimator for composite models.
It is a two-step procedure
(
Lohmöller 1989a
;
Wold 1975a
)
.
In the first step, weights are estimated using the iterative partial least squares algorithm, which are used to construct composites.
In the second step, relationships between the composites are estimated using their variance-covariance matrix.
However, when PLS-PM is applied to models containing latent variables, it likely leads to inconsistent parameter estimates
(
Dijkstra 1983a
)
.
To address this issue, consistent partial least squares (PLSc) was introduced
(
Dijkstra & Henseler 2015b
;
Dijkstra & Henseler 2015c
)
.
PLSc corrects for attenuation bias when latent variables are present, offering consistent parameter estimates for models that include both composites and latent variables
(
Schuberth et al. 2018c
)
.
Despite this advantage, PLS-PM and PLSc are limited in several regards.
There is little flexibility in specifying models
(
Schuberth et al. 2023c
)
.
Specifically, only some error correlations can be freed
(
Rademaker et al. 2019a
)
and PLS-PM and PLSc do not allow researchers to impose constraints on model parameters
(
Schuberth et al. 2023c
)
.
Finally, since the distribution of the parameter estimates is unknown, statistical inference for PLS-PM and PLSc has to rely on bootstrapping.

As an alternative to PLS-PM, GSCA has been proposed
(
Hwang & Takane 2014a
;
Hwang & Takane 2004a
)
.
GSCA originally estimates models with composites by minimizing the sum of squared residuals using an alternating least squares algorithm.
As PLS-PM, GSCA produces inconsistent parameter estimates for models involving latent variables
(
Henseler 2012a
)
.
To address this issue, GSCA with uniqueness terms for accommodating measurement error was introduced
(
Hwang et al. 2017a
)
.
In addition, for models containing both latent variables and composites, Integrated GSCA
(
Hwang et al. 2021a
)
can be used to achieve consistent parameter estimates.
While GSCA offers flexibility in model specification, it remains limited in terms of model assessment.

Despite the advancements of PLS-PM and GSCA, both were developed outside the traditional SEM framework, which restricts their ability to leverage the full capabilities of traditional SEM techniques such as conducting comprehensive model assessments, handling missing data, and model comparisons.

1.2
Composite Specifications in SEM

Within the SEM literature, composites gained more interest and approaches have been developed that allow for integrating composites into structural equation models.
Consequently, these approaches can deal with models that include both composites and latent variables.

In the classical one-step approach
(
Grace & Bollen 2008a
)
, a composite is modeled as a formatively measured latent variable whose error term variance is constrained to zero.
Consequently, the latent variable is entirely composed of its indicators, i.e., it is a composite.
This specification restricts composites to independent positions in the structural model and prohibits the estimation of covariances between the composite and other variables
(
Schuberth 2023a
)
.
To partly address this limitation, the effects of other variables on the composite can be modeled via its indicators, significantly increasing the number of model parameters.

Another approach to study composites in SEM is the pseudo-indicator approach
(
Rose et al. 2019a
)
.
This approach exploits the idea that instead of modeling a composite as a weighted linear combination of its indicators, one indicator can be expressed as the difference between the composite and other indicators.
However, this method currently lacks a mechanism for freely estimating composite weights and does not provide a means to test whether the composite fully transmits effects to its indicators
(
Schuberth et al. 2025a
)
.

The Henseler–Ogasawara (H–O) specification
(
Henseler 2021a
;
Schuberth 2023a
;
Yu et al. 2023a
)
overcomes the limitations found in other composite specifications within SEM.
This specification extracts as many composites as there are indicators and expresses the relationships between composites and indicators using composite loadings.
Although promising, the H–O specification has several limitations.
First, it increases specification complexity by introducing additional variables.
Second, as traditional SEM software was not specifically developed for the H–O specification, it frequently encounters convergence issues using the default starting values.
Lastly, direct statistical inference about composite weights is not feasible, as weights are not directly estimated.

Finally, a method has been introduced to estimate composite models where all composites are scaled to have unit variance
(
Schamberger 2022a
, Chapter 6)
.
This approach derives model parameters by minimizing the fit function known from the ML estimator for SEM with latent variables
(
Jöreskog 1969a
)
, under the constraint that all composites have unit variance.
However, this approach comes with several disadvantages.
First, only standardized estimates can be obtained for the structural parameters.
Second, this approach relies on constrained maximum likelihood estimation, which inherently increases computational time.
Third, this method does not directly accommodate models containing both composites and latent variables.
Finally, it is not implemented in any readily available software.

In summary, current approaches for handling composites face limitations in model specification, identification, and assessment.
To fully leverage the capabilities of SEM, we need to address these limitations.
Therefore, we present in the next section SEM with latent variables and composites.

2
Structural Equation Modeling with Latent Variables and Composites

This section introduces the steps of SEM with latent variables and composites: (i) model specification, (ii) model identification, (iii) model estimation and assessment.

2.1
Model Specification

At the heart of SEM lies the structural equation model, a statistical model expressed by linear structural equations.
In empirical studies, it is usually a formalized representation of researchers’ conceptual understanding of the real world.
We will demonstrate how to specify structural equation models that incorporate both composites and latent variables, ensuring consistency with models that contain only latent variables.
Additionally, we derive the model-implied variance-covariance matrix of the indicators.
For simplicity, we assume that each variable has a mean of zero.
Any structural equation model can be divided into two components: (i) the model that contains relations between constructs and their indicators, and (ii) the model that relates the constructs, i.e., the structural model.
We will introduce both models in the following.

As a starting point to specify a structural equation model involving composites and latent variables, we specify the model that relates the constructs with the indicators as shown in the following equation:

\displaystyle\bm{y}=\bm{\Lambda}\bm{\eta}+\bm{\varepsilon},

(1)

where the (
M\times 1
) random vector
\bm{\eta}
contains the constructs, and the (
P\times 1
) random vector
\bm{y}
contains the indicators.
As we allow the model to contain both latent variables and composites, Equation
1
can be rewritten as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\bm{y}^{l}\\
\bm{y}^{c}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{\Lambda}^{l}&\bm{0}\\
\bm{0}&\bm{\Lambda}^{c}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\bm{\eta}^{l}\\
\bm{\eta}^{c}\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}\bm{\varepsilon}^{l}\\
\bm{\varepsilon}^{c}\end{pmatrix},

(2)

where
\bm{y}^{l}
is a (
P^{l}\times 1
) random vector of the indicators related to the latent variables, which are comprised in the (
M^{l}\times 1
) random vector
\bm{\eta}^{l}
.
Consequently,
\bm{y}^{l}
is referred to as
latent variable indicators
.
Similarly,
\bm{y}^{c}
is a (
P^{c}\times 1
) random vector of the indicators related to the composites, which are comprised in the (
M^{c}\times 1
) random vector
\bm{\eta}^{c}
.
Thus, this type of indicators is referred to as
composite indicators
.
Further, the (
P^{l}\times 1
) random vector
\bm{\varepsilon}^{l}
comprises the random measurement errors of the latent variable indicators, which are assumed to be uncorrelated with
\bm{\eta}
.
The (
P^{c}\times 1
) random vector
\bm{\varepsilon}^{c}
contains potential errors in the composite indicators.
For simplicity and following the literature on composite models
(e.g.,
Grace & Bollen 2008a
)
, we assume
\bm{\varepsilon}^{c}=\bm{0}
in this paper.
The (
P^{l}\times M^{l}
) vector
\bm{\Lambda}^{l}
contains the effects of the latent variables on their indicators, commonly referred to as factor loadings.
Similarly, the (
P^{c}\times M^{c}
) matrix
\bm{\Lambda}^{c}
captures the regression coefficients of the composites on their associated indicators, the so-called composite loadings.

Since a composite is a linear combination of indicators, the composite model is usually defined in terms of weights instead of loadings, i.e.:

\displaystyle\bm{\eta}^{c}=\bm{W}^{\prime}\bm{y}^{c},

(3)

where the (
P^{c}\times M^{c}
) matrix
\bm{W}
contains the weights to form the composites.
Consequently, in this composite model, composite indicators are treated as exogenous variables.
The variances of the composite indicators are unrestricted, as are the covariances between indicators that form a specific composite.
These unrestricted variances and covariances are captured in the (
P^{c}\times P^{c}
) matrix
\bm{T}
which is defined as follows:

\displaystyle T_{ij}=\begin{cases}\text{cov}(y^{c}_{i},y_{j}^{c})\quad&,\text{if the two indicators }y^{c}_{i}\text{ and }y_{j}^{c}\text{ are related to the same composite}\\
0\quad&,\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(4)

Using Equation
3
, the composite loadings
\bm{\Lambda}^{c}
in Equation
2
can be derived as the coefficients from an ordinary least squares regression of the composites on their directly related indicators:

\displaystyle\bm{\Lambda}^{c}=\bm{T}\bm{W}(\bm{W}^{\prime}\bm{T}\bm{W})^{-1},

(5)

where
\bm{T}\bm{W}
captures the covariances between the composites and their related indicators and the matrix
(\bm{W}^{\prime}\bm{T}\bm{W})
is a diagonal matrix containing the composite variances.
We show the derivation of Equation
5
in the Appendix.

In addition to the relations between the constructs and their indicators, constructs are allowed to be related with each other.
These relations are expressed in the structural model:

\displaystyle\bm{\eta}=\bm{B}\bm{\eta}+\bm{\zeta}\qquad\Leftrightarrow\qquad\bm{\eta}=(\bm{I}-\bm{B})^{-1}\bm{\zeta},

(6)

where the (
M\times M
) matrix
\bm{B}
contains the relations between the constructs, and the (
M\times 1
) random vector
\bm{\zeta}
contains the structural error terms
1
1

1

If the construct
\eta_{i}
is not explained by other variables in the structural model, the corresponding structural error term
\zeta_{i}
is equal to the construct
\eta_{i}
. We further assume that
\bm{I}-\bm{B}
has full rank.
which are assumed to be uncorrelated with
\bm{\varepsilon}^{l}
.
Next to modeling relations between constructs, also relations between constructs and observed variables which do not serve as indicators neither for a latent variable nor for a composite, i.e. covariates, can be modeled.
To do so, these observed variables, which are comprised in the random vector
\bm{x}
, can be modeled as single-indicator latent variables where the indicators have no measurement error and the factor loadings are set to 1.
Consequently, they are just special cases of the latent variable model and can also be subsumed in
\bm{y}^{l}
.
Using the relations of the structural model (see Equation
6
), the variance-covariance matrix of
\bm{\eta}
is derived in the following equation:

\displaystyle\text{V}(\bm{\eta})=(\bm{I}-\bm{B})^{-1}\text{V}(\bm{\zeta})(\bm{I}-\bm{B})^{\prime-1}.

(7)

As the composites are dependent variables in the composite model, it needs to be taken into account that the variances of the composites are also determined via their relations with the indicators.
To combine the restrictions of the composite model and the structural model, the variances of the composites given by the last
M^{c}
diagonal elements in Equation
7
must be set equal to the diagonal elements of
\bm{W}^{\prime}\bm{T}\bm{W}
.
Consequently, the variances of the structural error terms related to composites are no free parameters, but functions of the remaining model parameters.

If we combine the constraints of all parts of the model, we can derive the model-implied variance-covariance matrix of the observed variables.
If the model solely contains latent variables, i.e.,
M^{c}=0
, the model-implied variance covariance matrix is given as:

\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{\theta})^{l}=\bm{\Lambda}^{l}\text{V}(\bm{\eta}^{l})\bm{\Lambda}^{l^{\prime}}+\bm{\Theta}^{l},

(8)

where
\bm{\Theta}^{l}
comprises the variances and covariances of the measurement errors
\bm{\varepsilon}^{l}
.
Similarly, the model-implied variance-covariance matrix can be derived for the model soley containing composites.
In this case, we need to take into account that the variances of the composite indicators and some of their covariances are unrestricted and comprised in the matrix
\bm{T}
.
We can derive
\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{\theta})^{c}
as follows:

\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{\theta})^{c}

\displaystyle=\bm{\Lambda}^{c}\text{V}(\bm{\eta}^{c})\bm{\Lambda}^{c^{\prime}}-\bm{\Lambda}^{c}\bm{W}^{\prime}\bm{T}\bm{W}\bm{\Lambda}^{c^{\prime}}+\bm{T}

(9)

\displaystyle=\bm{\Lambda}^{c}\text{V}(\bm{\eta}^{c})\bm{\Lambda}^{c^{\prime}}+\bm{\Theta}^{c}.

(10)

Finally, if we allow for both latent variables and composites in the model, the model-implied variance-covariance matrix of the indicators is given by:

\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{\theta})=\bm{\Lambda}\text{V}(\bm{\eta})\bm{\Lambda}^{\prime}+\bm{\Theta},

(11)

where we define
\bm{\Theta}
as:

\bm{\Theta}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{\Theta}^{l}&\bm{0}\\
\bm{0}&\bm{\Theta}^{c}\end{pmatrix}
with
\bm{\Theta}^{c}=\bm{T}-\bm{\Lambda}^{c}\bm{W}^{\prime}\bm{T}\bm{W}\bm{\Lambda}^{c^{\prime}}
.
To conclude, the model including its model-implied variance-covariance matrix resembles the model known from SEM with latent variables.

2.2
Model Identification

In this section, we present the necessary conditions for identifying a structural equation model with both composites and latent variables.
Ensuring that the model parameters are identified is crucial because otherwise the results cannot be interpreted reliably, as re-estimating the same model with identical data can produce entirely different results
(
Bollen 1989a
)
.

The first necessary condition for identification is adhering to the
t
rule, which requires that the degrees of freedom are greater than or equal to zero
(
Bollen & Davis 2009a
)
.
Just like in traditional SEM, the degrees of freedom are defined as the difference between the number of the non-redundant variances and covariances of the indicators and the number of the model parameters
K
:

\displaystyle\text{df}=(P\cdot(P+1))/2-K

(12)

For a model with both composites and latent variables, the model parameters are given by:

•

The non-redundant elements in
\bm{\Theta}^{L}
, i.e., the free (co)variances of the measurement errors

•

The non-redundant elements in
\bm{T}
, i.e., the free (co)variances of the composite indicators

•

The free factor loadings in
\bm{\Lambda}^{L}

•

The free composite weights in
\bm{W}

•

The free directed relations between constructs in
\bm{B}

•

The free covariances of the structural error terms, i.e., the off-diagonal elements in
V(\bm{\zeta})

•

The free variances of the structural error terms, i.e., the respective
M^{L}
main diagonal elements in
V(\bm{\zeta})

Ensuring model identification further requires that all sub-models – latent variable model, composite model, and structural model – are identified.
For the structural model, traditional SEM rules apply.
We refer to
Bollen & Davis 2009a
and
O’Brien 1994a
for further discussion.

Similarly, we can rely on the rules known from SEM with latent variables for identification of the latent variable model.
Scaling each latent variable is crucial.
For this purpose, several scaling methods have been proposed
(e.g.,
Klopp & Klößner 2021a
)
, e.g., fixing one loading per latent variable, or constraining the variances of the latent variables.
Additional identification rules are discussed in
Bollen 1989a
.

For the composite model, scaling of the composites is necessary.
Our model permits alternative scaling methods.
Specifically, composites can be scaled by fixing one weight, constraining the variances of composites to one, or imposing other restrictions on the weights, such as fixing their sum.
Additionally, a composite needs to be related to at least one other variable not forming the composite
(
Dijkstra 2017a
)
.
This is because the variances and covariances of observed variables making up a composite are free parameters, and thus, an isolated composite would have a negative number of degrees of freedom unless all weights are pre-specified.

To conclude, identifying a structural equation model that includes both composites and latent variables involves applying traditional SEM rules for latent and structural models and ensuring that the composite model is identified through proper scaling and relating the composites to other variables (or fixing all composite weights).

2.3
Model Estimation and Model Assessment

Once a structural equation model is specified and all parameters are identified, we can proceed to estimate the model parameters and assess the model.
Our proposed model specification, along with its model-implied variance-covariance matrix, enables us to directly apply established methods from SEM with latent variables for this purpose.
Consequently, model estimation and assessment for structural equation models involving composites and latent variables are conducted in the same manner as in SEM with only latent variables.

In particular, the model parameters can be estimated using various optimization criteria, such as weighted least squares
(WLS,
Browne 1984a
)
, generalized least squares
(GLS,
Jöreskog & Goldberger 1972a
)
, two-stage least squares
(2SLS,
Bollen 1996a
)
, and ML
(
Jöreskog 1969a
;
Jöreskog 1970a
)
.
ML is the most widely used approach, likely due to its capabilities, such as handling missing values, and the fact that it is the default method in most SEM software packages.

Employing these estimators from SEM with latent variables, including ML, WLS, and GLS, not only provides consistent, and asymptotically unbiased and efficient estimates
(
Davidson & MacKinnon 1993a
)
, but also provides various model assessment options.
Specifically, the analytic standard errors of the estimates, including their robust versions
(
Satorra & Bentler 1994a
)
, can be derived similarly to SEM with latent variables, allowing straightforward inference about the model parameters.
Additionally, this approach enables us to assess the overall model fit, determining whether the model is consistent with the collected data. This assessment can be conducted using the
\chi^{2}
test
(
Wilks 1938a
;
Jöreskog 1969a
)
.
Alternatively, researchers can evaluate overall model fit using fit indices such as the standardized root mean squared residual
(SRMR,
Jöreskog & Sörbom 1988a
)
or the root mean square error of approximation
(RMSEA,
Browne & Cudeck 1992a
)
.
For a detailed overview of these fit indices and their interpretation, we refer to
Hu & Bentler 1999a
and
Schermelleh-Engel et al. 2003a
.
However, some of the criteria and respective cut-off criteria are not well suited to assess models involving composites
(
Henseler et al. 2025a
;
Schuberth 2021a
)
.

3
Implementation in lavaan

In this section, we explore the implementation of our approach to SEM with latent variables and composites in the open-source software R
(
R Core Team 2020a
)
package lavaan
(
Rosseel 2012a
;
Rosseel et al. 2025a
)
.
This package is very popular in the field of SEM with latent variables and frequently used in practice
(
Jorgensen 2017a
)
.
To use the package with support for composites, it is currently necessary to install the development version from GitHub:
https://github.com/yrosseel/lavaan
.
In addition, our approach will be available in the forthcoming package version 0.6-20 on CRAN.
Generally, the analysis for structural equation models involving composites and latent variables follows a similar procedure as the analysis for models involving only latent variables.
Specifically, the same functions are applicable, and there is no need to modify the model syntax for the structural and measurement models.
In the following, we briefly outline the necessary adaptations for integrating composites.

To incorporate composites in the lavaan syntax, the operator ‘
<\sim
’ is used, with the composite specified on the left side and the composite indicators connected via ‘+’ operators.
By default, composites are scaled, setting the weight of the first indicator per composite to one.
This is similar to the default scaling method for latent variables.

The
sem
function can be used to obtain the parameter estimates.
To obtain the standardized parameter estimates, the
standardized
argument can be set to ‘TRUE’, for instance, in the
summary
function.
Standardized parameter estimates are particularly useful for comparisons with PLS-PM results, as PLS-PM estimates are usually standardized.
By default, the composite indicator (co)variances are fixed to their sample statistics. Although there is currently no option to estimate these (co)variances within lavaan, any estimation would yield values that match the sample statistics.

The current implementation has some limitations.
Firstly, while standardized parameter estimates can be obtained, scaling constructs to have unit variance during the estimation procedure is not yet supported when composites are involved.
Secondly, due to the absence of an analytical gradient, the option
optim.gradient
must be set to
"numerical"
during optimization.
Lastly, the current implementation does not yet support mean structures.
Nevertheless, these limitations are planned to be addressed in future updates.

6
Discussion

In this study, we introduce SEM with latent variables and composites.
We address the necessity for our approach due to the limitations of existing approaches for studying structural equation models involving latent variables and composites.
These approaches often fall short in several areas; (i) model specification: current methods do not allow for imposing constraints on parameters (as seen in PLS-PM), they restrict where composites can be positioned within the model (as seen in the one-step approach), or they limit the free estimation of composite weights (as in the pseudo-indicator approach); (ii) model assessment: for many existing approaches no analytic standard errors are available (e.g., PLS-PM and GSCA), or they do not facilitate direct inference on composite weights (as in the H–O specification); (iii) model complexity: some methods introduce additional variables, complicating the model specification (as in the H–O specification).

Our proposed approach overcomes these limitations, providing a more flexible and comprehensive framework for SEM analysis.
Specifically, we present a joint model specification for structural equation models involving composites and latent variables, which allows for the derivation of a model-implied variance-covariance matrix for such models.
Moreover, we present the necessary condition to identify the model parameters.
Subsequently, this specification and identification criteria allow us to rely on traditional SEM approaches for model estimation and assessment.

SEM with latent variables and composites offers several key advantages:
First, it is implemented in the open-source R package lavaan which is widely used for SEM with latent variables.
This ensures broad accessibility and ease of use for the research community.
Second, our method aligns with established SEM techniques, allowing us to leverage existing estimator properties, model assessment methods, and ongoing developments in the field.
Third, our approach is very flexible in terms of model specification, e.g., by allowing to use the same indicator to form different composites, i.e., we allow for cross-block weights.

To demonstrate our approach, we conducted a scenario analysis using a sample accurately representing the population.
This scenario analysis confirmed that SEM with latent variables and composites employing the ML estimator can retrieve the population parameters in such a scenario.
Additionally, we presented an illustrative example commonly used in the literature to further illustrate our method and demonstrate how it can be applied in the open-source R package lavaan.
We further compared the results of our approach with those of other methods for estimating structural equation models involving composites and latent variables.
This comparison demonstrates that the standardized parameter estimates and model fit assessment criteria are similar among the approaches.

We have demonstrated that our approach allows for reliance on traditional SEM techniques in terms of model estimation and model assessment.
Furthermore our model specification allows us to integrate other SEM techniques such as handling missing data, analyzing multi-group scenarios, and examining moderation effects.
Future research should explore the performance of these approaches for SEM with latent variables and composites.
Generally, assessing the overall model fit is well explored for SEM with latent variables.
However, some of the criteria are not well suited to assess models involving composites
(
Henseler et al. 2025a
;
Schuberth 2021a
)
.
Specifically, validity, reliability, or fit indices as the average variance extracted are designed for latent variables, limiting their applicability for composite models.
Currently, research on the use of model fit criteria for composite models is rather limited
(
Hwang et al. 2023a
)
.
Future research should elaborate more on the applicability and interpretability of different model assessment criteria for SEM with latent variables and composites.
Additionally, new criteria should be developed to assess such models.
Moreover, currently indicators are restricted to be either composite or latent variable indicators.
Future research should elaborate on including indicators as measures for latent variables and indicators of composites simultaneously.
Finally, it is crucial to investigate the assumption that composite indicators are free from measurement error.
Given the likelihood of measurement errors in empirical research, strategies to incorporate and address such errors within our framework should be developed.

In conclusion, our approach significantly broadens the applicability of SEM for studying structural equation models involving composites and latent variables, making it more accessible and practical for a wider research audience.
