Hope level and its associated factors among widowed older adults in long-term care facilities
Authors: Li Chen, Wei Liu, Renshan Cui
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo investigate the hope level and identify its associated factors among widowed older adults residing in long-term care facilities.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted using convenience sampling. 228 widowed older adults meeting inclusion criteria were recruited from several long-term care facilities in Liaoning Province for face-to-face questionnaire surveys.ResultsThe hope level average score among widowed older adults in long-term care facilities was (31.73 ± 3.31). Multiple linear regression analysis revealed that nine factors were significantly associated with hope levels: educational level, duration of widowhood, frequency of children’s visits, pension income, number of chronic diseases, frequency of participation in recreational activities, medical payment method, evaluation of the long-term care facility, and total perceived social support score. These factors collectively explained 81.4% of the variance in hope levels (Adjusted R² = 0.814, F = 96.027, P < 0.001).ConclusionHope levels among widowed older adults in long-term care facilities were at a moderate-low level. Nursing staff and facility administrators should pay attention to the hope levels of these residents and implement targeted interventions based on the identified associated factors to enhance hope levels and consequently improve their quality of life.Keywords: Long-term care facilities, Widowed older adults, Hope level, Associated factors

Introduction

Global demographic shifts have made population aging a significant societal and public health challenge, particularly pronounced in China. United Nations reports indicate that the population aged 65 and older is growing faster than younger age groups worldwide, posing ongoing pressures on healthcare and social care systems [1]. In China, this challenge is more pressing, with the widowed elderly population exceeding 50 million, characterized by significant aging and feminization trends [2]. Population projections suggest that by 2050, the proportion of China’s population aged 60 and above will rise to approximately 34.9% [3], creating unprecedented demands on the social care system for older adults.

Within this macro-backdrop, widowed older adults in long-term care facilities (LTCFs) represent a particularly vulnerable subpopulation, facing multiple risks including advanced age, spousal loss, and institutional living. Research indicates that institutional residence itself is a significant factor for social isolation and depression risk [4], while widowhood, as one of the most severe negative life events in later life, can compound these effects, predisposing individuals to persistent loneliness, anxiety, and complex grief reactions [5].

However, previous research has shown notable perspective limitations, predominantly focusing on pathological psychological symptoms such as anxiety and depression in this population [6]. From a positive psychology perspective, cultivating intrinsic positive psychological capital, particularly future-oriented hope, plays a more fundamental role in promoting successful aging. Hope, grounded in Snyder’s hope theory, represents a cognitive motivational construct based on goals, pathways, and agency thinking [7]. Rather than a static trait, hope serves as a crucial internal force that helps individuals reconstruct life meaning and adapt to new roles in adversity, and has been demonstrated to be a strong predictor of psychological resilience and quality of life in older adults [8].

Critically, hope is not developed in a vacuum but is dynamically shaped by an individual’s socio-ecological context. Among these factors, perceived social support, that is, the subjective assessment of the availability and adequacy of social support, has been consistently identified as a crucial antecedent variable across multiple studies [9]. For widowed older adults in institutional settings, the permanent loss of spousal support makes social networks from children, peers, and caregivers particularly crucial. Their ability to effectively perceive and internalize these external supports directly determines their capacity to reconstruct life goals (agency thinking) and identify ways to achieve them (pathways thinking) following loss [10].

Despite existing research progress, significant knowledge gaps remain. First, there is a contextual misalignment in research focus: most studies either examine widowed older adults in community settings [11] or treat institutionalized older adults as a homogeneous group [12], lacking focused investigation on widowed individuals within long-term care facilities specifically. Second, analytical approaches are often siloed, examining factors in isolation rather than using an integrated framework to reveal how individual, interpersonal, institutional, and societal factors collectively shape hope levels.

To systematically address these research gaps, this study employs the social-ecological systems theory as its overarching analytical framework [13]. This theory emphasizes that individual development (or psychological state) is embedded within a series of interconnected environmental systems with dynamic interactions across levels. This perspective provides our study with an “individual- interpersonal -institutional-societal” multi-level analytical lens to effectively dissect the complex formation mechanisms of hope levels, an approach that has demonstrated strong explanatory power in gerontological research [14, 15].

Guided by this theory and closely aligned with the specific context of long-term care facilities, we pre-constructed an operationalized analytical framework comprising four levels: The individual intrinsic system (corresponding to the microsystem) encompasses personal characteristics and resources forming the psychological and physiological foundation of hope, including factors such as educational level, duration of widowhood, and number of chronic diseases. The interpersonal support system (corresponding to the mesosystem) refers to the direct relational networks that nurture hope, with core analytical variables including frequency of children’s visits and perceived social support. The institutional environment system (integrating features of micro- and exosystems) serves as the key microenvironment for hope cultivation, focusing on factors such as recreational activity participation frequency, evaluation of the long-term care facility, and housing type. The socioeconomic system (corresponding to exo- and macrosystems) refers to the broader socioeconomic environment providing foundational security for hope, analyzing factors including pension income and medical payment methods.

Based on this framework and using cross-sectional data collected from five long-term care facilities, this study aims to describe the hope levels of widowed older adults in long-term care facilities, systematically examine and identify key factors associated with hope levels across these four system levels, and provide empirical evidence and theoretical support for future development of precise, multi-level psychosocial intervention strategies.

Methods

Study design and participants

During the COVID-19 pandemic, long-term care facilities universally implemented closed management and restricted external visits, making traditional sampling methods challenging to execute. This study employed a convenience sampling method between June to December 2022, selecting widowed older adults who met the inclusion and exclusion criteria from five long-term care facilities in Liaoning Province.

Inclusion criteria:

① Age ≥ 60 years; ② Duration of widowhood ≥ 6 months [16, 17]; ③ Length of residence in the long-term care facility ≥ 6 months; ④ Ability to communicate normally and cooperate with researchers to complete questionnaires.

Exclusion criteria:

① Diagnosis of severe physical illness (including terminal cancer, cardiac, hepatic, or renal failure), inability to cooperate, or emotional instability; ② History of mental illness with inability to cooperate.

Sample size calculation

According to the rough sample size estimation principle for multiple regression analysis proposed by Duleba and Olive [18], the sample size should be 5–10 times the number of independent variables. Considering potential missing or invalid questionnaires, the final sample size required a 20% increase. This survey collected 228 questionnaires, with 218 valid responses, resulting in a validity rate of 95.6%.

Research instruments

General information questionnaire

Based on the theory of social ecosystem, this study designed 18 indicators aimed at collecting data related to the four system levels defined by the framework, such as gender, age, ethnicity, religious belief, educational level, duration of widowhood, number of children, frequency of children’s visits, frequency of others’ visits, pension income, number of chronic diseases, frequency of participation in recreational activities and long-term care facility costs.

Herth hope index (HHI)

Developed by American scholar Herth in 1992 [19] and translated and revised by Professor Zhao Haiping in 2000 [20]. The scale contains 12 items across three dimensions: positive attitude toward reality and future (Temporality and future, T), taking positive actions (Positive readiness and expectancy, P), and maintaining close relationships with others (Interconnectedness, I). Each item uses a 1–4 point scale, where 1–4 represents strongly disagree, disagree, agree, and strongly agree, respectively. The total score ranges from 12 to 48, with higher scores indicating higher hope levels. Herth proposed clinical boundary recommendations, suggesting total scores ≤ 23 indicate high-risk mental health status, while scores exceeding 36 represent a threshold for good psychological adaptation [21]. Therefore, this study adopted the following classification standards: low level: 12–23 points; medium level: 24–35 points; high level: 36–48 points. The scale demonstrated good reliability and validity, with a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.85.

Perceived Social Support Scale (PSSS)

Reflecting the overall level of social support perceived by individuals, most domestic studies use the Chinese version of the Perceived Social Support Scale (PSSS) introduced and revised by Jiang Qianjin [22]. The scale contains 12 items across three dimensions: family support, friend support, and other social support. The scale uses a Likert 7-point scoring method, from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree,” with each item scoring from 1 to 7 points and a total score range of 12–84 points. Scores of 12–36 indicate low support status, 37–60 medium support status, and 61–84 high support status, with higher scores indicating stronger perceived social support. The scale demonstrated good reliability and validity, with a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.92.

Data collection

This study obtained informed consent from participants and conducted a cross-sectional survey. Personal interviews were conducted with widowed older adults in long-term care facilities who met the inclusion and exclusion criteria, and questionnaires were distributed with on-site item-by-item verification for omissions or errors.

Data analysis

This study used SPSS 26.0 statistical software for data analysis and processing. Measurement data conforming to normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (x̄ ± s); count data were expressed using frequencies and composition ratios (%); univariate analysis used independent samples t-test or analysis of variance; correlation analysis used Pearson correlation analysis; multivariate analysis used multiple linear stepwise regression analysis, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The Affiliated Hospital of Liaoning University of Traditional Chinese Medicine (Ethics approval number: 2022190FS(KT)−013-02). All participants were informed of the study purpose before the survey and signed written informed consent forms. This study strictly followed the principles of voluntariness, fairness, beneficence, and confidentiality.

Females accounted for 56.4% (123 individuals) and males for 43.6% (95 individuals). The 75–89 age group had the highest proportion, reaching 152 individuals (69.7%). Older adults with widowhood duration exceeding three years accounted for a larger proportion, totaling 129 individuals (59.2%). Specific data are detailed in Table 1.

Characteristics and Univariate Analysis of Hope Levels Among Widowed Older Adults in Long-Term Care Facilities (n=218)

Hope level and perceived social support scores among widowed older adults in long-term care facilities

The hope level average score of widowed older adults in long-term care facilities was (31.73 ± 3.31), at a moderate-low level. The average scores of the three dimensions from highest to lowest were: positive action (11.24 ± 1.15), positive attitude toward reality and future (10.41 ± 1.36), and maintaining close relationships with others (10.05 ± 1.52). The average perceived social support score was (52.11 ± 5.94), overall at a medium level.

Univariate analysis of hope levels among widowed older adults in long-term care facilities

Results showed statistically significant differences (P < 0.05) in overall hope level scores across 15 aspects: age, educational level, duration of widowhood, number of children, frequency of children’s visits, frequency of others’ visits, pre-retirement occupation, main income source, pension income, number of chronic diseases, frequency of participation in recreational activities, long-term care facility costs, housing type, medical payment method, and evaluation of the long-term care facility. Details are shown in Table 1.

Correlation analysis between hope level and perceived social support among widowed older adults in long-term care facilities

Pearson correlation analysis showed a close positive correlation between hope level and total perceived social support score among widowed older adults in long-term care facilities (r = 0.628, P < 0.01). Family support had the greatest impact on hope level total score, followed by others’ support, with statistical significance (P < 0.01).

Multivariate analysis of hope levels among widowed older adults in long-term care facilities

With hope level total score as the dependent variable, 15 variables showing statistical significance in univariate analysis results and perceived social support total score were used as independent variables. Unordered multi-categorical variables were converted into dummy variables for multiple linear regression analysis. Independent variables and assignment conditions are shown in Table 2. When constructing the multiple linear regression model, all variables from Table 2 (16 items total), including age (X1), educational level (X2), duration of widowhood (X3), etc., were initially included in the initial model. Stepwise regression (forward selection) was used for variable screening, with inclusion criteria set at P < 0.2 in univariate analysis and exclusion criteria at P ≥ 0.1. The final model retained variables with P < 0.05. Dummy variable coding was used for categorical variables such as educational level and pre-retirement occupation, with reference groups set as “primary school and below” and “workers,” etc. Multicollinearity was tested using variance inflation factor (VIF), with all variables’ VIF values < 5, indicating no serious multicollinearity issues.

Discussion

Hope levels among widowed older adults in long-term care facilities are at a moderate-low level

This study revealed that hope levels among widowed older adults in long-term care facilities scored (31.73 ± 3.31), within the moderate-low range. It is important to note that data collection occurred during the COVID-19 pandemic. In the study locations, long-term care facilities generally implemented strict control policies including prolonged lockdown management and restricted family visits. Therefore, the hope levels observed in this study should be interpreted as representing the specific status of the surveyed widowed older adult population within long-term care facilities under this particular public crisis context. Compared to data from normal circumstances, our findings may reveal more severe psychological challenges faced by this group under extreme stress conditions.

To more accurately contextualize the problem, we compared our findings with previous studies focusing on different vulnerable elderly groups. Results showed that hope levels in our study population were significantly lower than those reported for community-dwelling empty-nest older adults with chronic pain [23] and older adults with chronic diseases [24], and also lower than those of older adult stroke patients facing clear physical functional impairments [25]. Even when compared to rural empty-nest older adults, who are also considered to have relatively weak social support [26], our study population showed a tendency toward lower hope levels. This series of striking comparisons strongly suggests that the negative impacts of widowhood as a key adverse event and institutionalized living environment, particularly under the unique circumstances of the pandemic, may have produced profound cumulative effects, collectively leading to significantly reduced hope levels in this population.

Hope level, as a core indicator of an individual’s future-oriented positive beliefs and motivational drive [7], with its moderate-low status. This clearly indicates that the older adults in our study face widespread difficulties in planning for the future, perceiving life meaning, and proactively confronting challenges. Therefore, this finding underscores the urgency of prioritizing mental health interventions for widowed older adults in long-term care facilities.

Hope levels among widowed older adults in long-term care facilities are shaped by multiple factors

Based on social-ecological systems theory, this study constructed a four-level analytical framework of “individual intrinsic— interpersonal support institutional environment—socioeconomic” to systematically explore the factors associated with hope levels among widowed older adults in long-term care facilities. The research found that key factors were identified across all four system levels, collectively explaining most of the variance in hope levels, strongly confirming that hope, as a positive psychological construct, is shaped by the dynamic interaction between individuals and their complex environments [4].

Individual intrinsic system factors: the psychophysiological foundation of hope

Our study demonstrates that educational level, duration of widowhood, and number of chronic diseases were significantly associated with hope levels, constituting core intrinsic elements.

First, compared to those with primary school education or less, older adults with college education or bachelor’s degrees and above showed significantly higher hope levels. This confirms that education, as a key indicator of cognitive reserve, can enhance information processing capacity and executive function, empowering individuals to engage in more effective meaning reconstruction and problem-solving in widowhood adversity [27]. Highly educated individuals often possess richer psychological resources and coping strategies, which are crucial for maintaining hope as a future-oriented cognitive motivation. Second, the positive correlation between duration of widowhood and hope levels indicates that grief adaptation is a dynamic process. Hope levels are typically at their lowest during early widowhood, closely related to the “future-oriented thinking disruption” common in acute grief periods [28]. Over time, some older adults can gradually integrate loss experiences and rebuild connections with the future, with hope levels consequently showing a recovery trend, reflecting the inherent potential for psychological adjustment. Furthermore, the number of chronic diseases is a significant depleting factor for hope. Multimorbidity not only directly restricts social participation through pain and functional limitations but also continuously erodes the psychological energy individuals have for emotional regulation and goal pursuit [29]. The persistent stress associated with chronic disease management may fundamentally weaken the agency thinking of older adults in setting and pursuing future life goals.

Interpersonal support system factors: the direct nourishment for hope

At the interpersonal support level, frequency of children’s visits and perceived social support were demonstrated to be the direct nourishment for hope. Our study showed that higher frequency of children’s visits correlated with higher hope levels. Regular family interaction represents the most direct manifestation of emotional support, effectively buffering loneliness caused by widowhood and providing sustained psychological motivation for older adults [30]. Particularly important, perceived social support also emerged as a key predictive factor, indicating that objective supportive behaviors must be subjectively perceived and internalized by individuals to transform into psychological capital that supports hope [31]. This suggests that the quality of support (i.e., perceived support) is equally important as the frequency of support, and external support needs internal perception to truly function.

Institutional environment system factors: the key microenvironment for hope cultivation

The institutional environment, as the immediate microenvironment inhabited by older adults, directly shapes daily experiences and hope levels through recreational activity participation frequency and evaluation of the long-term care facility.

Frequent participation in recreational activities serves not only as pastime but also as an important pathway for older adults to establish new social connections, gain belongingness, and positive experiences [32]. Particularly in technology-enabled contexts, group activities based on innovative technologies like virtual reality can more effectively enhance participants’ pleasure and self-efficacy through immersive, novel experiences [33]. Simultaneously, positive evaluation of the long-term care facility (including environment, services, and interpersonal atmosphere) constitutes the psychological soil for hope cultivation. When older adults perceive the environment as safe, respectful, and supportive, their psychological security is greatly enhanced, making them more willing to actively engage in life and set new goals [34].

Socioeconomic system factors: the foundational security for hope

At the outermost socioeconomic system level, pension income and medical payment method provide the foundational security for hope levels.

Our study revealed that compared to older adults with medical insurance, those fully self-paying showed significantly lower hope levels. Basic medical insurance, as an institutional safety net can greatly alleviate older adults’ fears of financial risk from major illnesses, eliminating the foremost future concern [35]. Meanwhile, higher pension levels not only symbolize economic independence but also, at a deeper level, maintain social dignity and sense of life control for older adults. Economic autonomy enables older adults to choose higher-quality services and participate in diverse activities, directly enhancing their pathways thinking for achieving future goals [36].

Limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional survey design prevents causal inferences between variables; future research could adopt longitudinal designs to track the dynamic trajectories of hope levels. Second, constrained by survey conditions during the pandemic, this study employed convenience sampling, with participants coming from only five long-term care facilities in Liaoning Province, limiting the generalizability of findings. Future studies should conduct broader random sampling validation nationwide. Additionally, data collection occurred during the special control phase of the COVID-19 pandemic. While this unique historical context provides valuable perspectives for understanding older adults’ psychology during crisis situations, it also means that the results may be influenced by specific environmental pressures of this period (such as prolonged social isolation). Therefore, caution should be exercised when generalizing the hope levels measured in this study and the effect strengths of correlated factors to normal circumstances. Future research conducting repeated surveys during non-pandemic periods for comparison would be highly valuable. Finally, this study did not include some important potential confounding or mediating variables related to health and functional status, such as activities of daily living (ADLs), cognitive function, or the length of residence in the facility. Future research should incorporate these critical variables to better elucidate the underlying mechanisms and control for their potential confounding effects.

Despite these limitations, this study provides important baseline data and theoretical foundations for understanding hope levels among widowed older adults in long-term care facilities through systematic exploration during a special period, offering certain theoretical and practical reference value.

Conclusion

In summary, grounded in social-ecological systems theory, this study demonstrates that hope levels among widowed older adults in long-term care facilities are shaped by the synergistic interaction of factors across four levels: individual intrinsic resources, interpersonal support networks, institutional environment quality, and socioeconomic security. This finding transcends traditional intervention limitations focused solely on individual psychology, suggesting that future professional practice should shift toward constructing multi-level, integrated support ecosystems. Specifically, intervention measures should simultaneously target: strengthening integrated grief counseling and chronic disease co-management at the individual level; encouraging high-frequency, high-quality family interaction and promoting peer support at the interpersonal level; optimizing activity design and humanistic environment to enhance overall satisfaction at the institutional level; and consolidating economic and medical security systems at the social policy level. Through such cross-level, systematic intervention, hope levels in this population can be tangibly enhanced, ultimately improving their quality of life.
