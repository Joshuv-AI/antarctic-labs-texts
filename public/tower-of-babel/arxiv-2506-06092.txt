LinGuinE: Longitudinal Guidance Estimation for Volumetric Lung Tumour Segmentation
Nadine Garibli, Mayank Patwari, Bence Csiba, Yi Wei, Kostas Sidiropoulos

Abstract
Segmentation of lung gross tumour volumes is an important first step in radiotherapy and surgical intervention, and is starting to play a role in assessing chemotherapy response. Response to a drug is measured by tracking the tumour volumes over a series of CT scans over a time period i.e. a longitudinal study. However, there currently exist few solutions for automated or semi-automated longitudinal tumour segmentation. This paper introduces LinGuinE, an automated method to segment a longitudinal series of lung tumours. A radiologist must provide an initial input, indicating the location of the tumour in a CT scan at an arbitrary time point. LinGuinE samples points inside this tumour and propagates them to another time point using rigid registration. A click validity classifier selects points which still fall within the tumour; these are used to automatically create a segmentation in the new time point. We test LinGuinE on a dataset acquired from a phase 3 clinical trial for lung tumours and the publicly available 4-D lung CBCT dataset. We find that LinGuinE improves the Dice on both test sets by over 20% (p < 0.05) across 63 longitudinal studies. We show that any time point can be used as a starting point, conduct ablation experiments, and find that our LinGuinE setup yields the best results on both test datasets.

[

LinGuinE introduces the following innovations: (i) the use of an interactive guided volumetric segmentation model to segment lung tumours (ii) transfer of guidance points into another scan at a different time point using rigid registration based on extracted skeletal landmarks (iii) filtering of transferred guidance based on a lightweight random forest model. Moreover, LinGuinE allows radiologists to specify the tumours that they wish to use for quantifying a response to chemotherapy. We conduct ablation studies, and find that our LinGuinE setup provides the best results on both test datasets considered.

2.1
Guided Volumetric Segmentation

We use the SwinUNETR
[
7
]
architecture as the backbone of the segmentation model and include a guidance layer which encodes a coordinate
C
indicating the tumour location.
C
is smoothed using a Gaussian function (
\mu=0,\sigma=2
). We fed the SwinUNETR with
96\times 96\times 96
overlapping CT image blocks from the CT volume
I
, with a stride of
48\times 48\times 48
.

\textit{SwinUNETR}(I,C)\rightarrow M(k)

(1)

where
M
is the segmentation mask generated for the tumour
k
.

We fine tuned the SwinUNETR weights on the publicly available NSCLC-Radiomics dataset
[
1
]
, consisting of 418 CT scans collected at the MAASTRO clinic in the Netherlands and the corresponding annotation of the primary lung tumour. 16% of the scans were set aside for validation and an additional 16% for testing, leaving 68% for training. The model was trained for 350 epochs on 4 NVIDIA A10G GPUs using the PyTorch lightning framework (version 1.9.4).

We trained the model to work in both unguided and guided mode. In unguided mode, the model received no guidance signal, and so performed fully automatic segmentation of all tumours in the scan. Unguided examples comprised 6.25% of the cases during training. In guided mode, the model received smoothed positive guidance clicks and only segmented the tumour(s) highlighted by the guidance clicks. On the test split, SwinUNETR achieves a Dice of 0.613 in unguided mode and improves to 0.700 with a single center click.

2.2
Longitudinal Guidance Propogation

Problem definition.
Let
P
denote a set of CT cans from the same patient. Let
I_{i}\in P
be a CT scan and
C_{i}
be a guidance click (in the form of a 3D voxel coordinate) on
I_{i}
, indicating the location of a target tumour. The LinGuinE model produces a segmentation mask for the same target tumour on every scan in
P
, resulting in a set of segmentation masks.

\textit{LinGuinE}(I_{i},C_{i})\rightarrow\{M_{j}(k)\;|\;I_{j}\in P\},

(2)

where we call
I_{i}
the source scan,
I_{j}
the destination scan, and
M_{j}(k)
the segmentation mask of tumour
k
in the destination scan. Any scan in
P
can be selected as the source.

To produce
M_{j}(k)
, LinGuinE propagates
n
guidance clicks, sampled from
M_{i}(k)
, from the coordinate space of
I_{i}
to the coordinate space of
I_{j}
. We denote a propagated click as
C_{i\rightarrow j}
. The LinGuinE model then invokes SwinUNETR with
C_{i\rightarrow j}
to produce
M_{j}(k)
on
I_{j}
. The longitudinal segmentation task can be represented as below.

\begin{split}&\textit{SwinUNETR}(I_{j},C_{i\rightarrow j})\rightarrow M_{j}(k)\\
&\textit{LinGuinE}(I_{i},C_{i})\rightarrow\{\textit{SwinUNETR}(I_{j},C_{i\rightarrow j})\;|\;I_{j}\in P\}\end{split}

(3)

The intuition behind click propagation is the fact that the same tumour doesn’t move arbitrarily from one scan to another scan.
C_{i}
indicates the anatomical location of that tumor in
I_{i}
. The same tumor in
I_{j}
should be near the same anatomical location. The challenge is to find
C_{i}
in
I_{j}
.
When
n>1
, LinGuinE propagates each click as defined above and invokes SwinUNETR with multiple clicks.

Click propagation using rigid registration.
The coordinate represented by
C_{i}
is in the space of
I_{i}
and cannot be directly applied in the space of
I_{j}
, because scans taken at different time-points are likely to differ in size, resolution, field of view, and patient position. LinGuinE uses Arun’s rigid registration algorithm
[
2
]
to find an anatomically aware mapping between
I_{i}
and
I_{j}
that can be used to compute any
C_{i\rightarrow j}
in the space of
I_{j}
. Formally,

C_{i\rightarrow j}=\textbf{R}C_{i}+\textbf{t}

(4)

where
R
is a
3\times 3
rotation matrix and
t
is a
3\times 1
translation vector. To find
R
and
t
, Arun’s method relies on a set of anatomical landmarks whose coordinates are known in both scans. The optimal parameters would transform each landmark in
I_{i}
to a location near the equivalent landmark in
I_{j}
. Hence,
R
and
t
are found by minimizing the Euclidean distance between the landmarks in
I_{j}
and the landmarks that are transformed from
I_{i}
using equation (
4
).

Skeletal landmark generation
We used the publicly available TotalSegmentator model
[
18
]
for segmentation of all thoracic and lumbar vertebrae, both clavicles, all ribs, and the sternum. The generated masks were used to compute 47 landmarks including; (i) the center of mass of each vertebra, (ii) the outermost lateral point of each rib, defined as the center of mass on the outermost sagittal slice, (iii) The innermost and outermost point of each clavicle, defined as the center of mass on the innermost and outermost sagittal slices, and (iv) the center of mass of the sternum on the most inferior axial slice. All scans were standardised to a spacing of 1.5mm
\times
1.5mm
\times
2.0mm, so the landmarks could be defined as voxel coordinates in the image space without compromising the Euclidean distances used in the minimization problem. Due to differing fields of view, not all landmarks are present in all scans. Additionally, any landmark derived from a bone that is not fully visible in the scan is discarded to avoid inaccuracies. We have an average of 41 skeletal landmarks generated per scan.

2.3
Click Validity Classification

During the course of chemotherapy, the location, shape, and size of a tumour can change. Therefore,
C_{i\rightarrow j}
may land outside the tumour in
I_{j}
. The Click Validity Classifier (CVC) is presented as a solution to this problem: the CVC is a random forest trained to predict whether
C_{i\rightarrow j}
lies within a tumour. Information about the Hounsfield units and unguided SwinUNETR predictions at both time points are used to create inputs to the CVC.

From the source scan,
I_{i}
, we extract features of the target tumour,
k
. If
M_{i}(k)
, is available as guidance, it is used directly. Alternatively, if only
C_{i}
is provided, we use equation (
1
) to generate
M_{i}(k)
.
M_{i}(k)
is used to compute the mean, median, variance, standard deviation, and inter-quartile range of the voxels within
M_{i}(k)
. This allows the CVC to determine if the attenuation value at
C_{i\rightarrow j}
is likely to belong to the distribution previously observed for
k
. Similarly,
M_{i}(k)
is used to compute the mean and variance of the unguided SwinUNETR probabilities for each voxel in
k
to allow the CVC to contextualise the tumour class probability at
C_{i\rightarrow j}
.

As seen in Figure
1
, the CVC is used to select the clicks which are passed as input to the SwinUNETR. LinGuinE uniformly samples
m
clicks (in this paper,
m=25
) from the voxels in
M_{i}(k)
. These clicks are propagated to the destination scan and passed through the CVC. The probabilities of the clicks being valid are then used to select the top
n
clicks to use as SwinUNETR guidance. Only the clicks with probability above 0.5 are kept - if there are no such clicks, the LinGuinE segmentation is the same as the unguided SwinUNETR segmentation.

3
Experimental Results

3.1
Datasets

We tested LinGuinE on two datasets unseen by SwinUNETR. The first is a publicly available dataset consisting of 4D-CBCT scans and corresponding primary tumour segmentations from 10 patients with locally advanced non-small cell lung cancer over the course of 9 weeks of chemoradiotherapy
[
3
]
. The dataset contains 10 scans per timepoint, one for each phase of the breathing cycle. We only consider the scan at the 90% phase, corresponding to an inhale, which is the usual standard for CT. This defines a subset of 48 scans used to test LinGuinE.

The second dataset (Phase-3) originated from a confidential phase 3 monoclonal antibody study targeting metastatic non-small cell lung cancer. This dataset encompassed data from 30 countries and included 2,398 patients. We randomly selected and annotated 370 CT scans from 99 patients, spanning over 52 weeks. 211 scans across 53 patients from this dataset comprise our test set. The remaining scans were set aside for CVC development. The scans were segmented by two radiologists with more than 15 years of combined clinical experience.

3.2
Improved Dice with LinGuinE

To show that segmentation using LinGuinE produces higher Dice than unguided segmentation using SwinUNETR, we propagated guidance from screening segmentations to all remaining scans for that patient, as in Figure
2
.

Figure 2:
LinGuinE segmentations of longitudinal studies for two patients in the 4D-CBCT dataset. All guidance was propagated from the screening label to obtain segmentations (red contours) on subsequent time points. The ground truth is denoted by yellow contours. The 2D slice displayed is the central slice containing the tumour in the screening scan, and the anatomically equivalent slices in the subsequent scans.

On 4D-CBCT, LinGuinE achieved a mean Dice of 0.678 across 38 tumors in 38 non-screening scans, outperforming the unguided SwinUNETR, which yielded a Dice of 0.504 on these tumors. This constitutes a 34.52% improvement, which is statistically significant (paired t-test,
p=0.000
). On Phase-3, LinGuinE obtained a mean Dice of 0.593 for 168 tumors across 135 scans. This represents a 21.74% improvement over the unguided SwinUNETR’s score of 0.504 on the same tumours (paired t-test,
p=0.000
). These Dice scores are achieved when the CVC selects 5 clicks with the highest probability of being valid from the screening segmentation. The observed benefits on the unseen 4D-CBCT dataset suggest generalisability of the CVC.

We ensured that the interactive SwinUNETR in unguided mode performs comparably to one trained solely for unguided segmentation by training an equivalent model without any guided examples. The paired t-test conducted to assess performance differences on the LinGuinE test datasets revealed no statistically significant differences in Dice (
p=0.194
on Phase-3 and
p=0.509
on 4D-CBCT, respectively). Therefore, comparisons to the unguided SwinUNETR are equivalent to comparisons to state-of-the-art automatic segmentation algorithms.

3.3
Time Point Agnosticity

To demonstrate that LinGuinE can propagate guidance in any temporal direction, we evaluated the distribution of LinGuinE Dice scores when using three different starting scans for each patient: (i) the earliest, (ii) the middle, and (ii) the latest. The set of segmentations for this three-way comparison is smaller than in other experiments, as we exclude any scan used as a starting point and any tumour not present in all starting scans for a patient. In Phase-3, 47 tumors from 39 scans across 26 patients were available. We found no statistically significant difference (Friedmann chi-squared test,
p=0.112
) in Dice regardless of the source time point. We conducted the same analysis on 4D-CBCT, across 18 scans from 9 patients. Similar results were noted (Friedmann chi-squared test,
p=0.678
).

3.4
Ablation Studies

Impact of CVC

62/168
tumors in the Phase-3 dataset had a click propagated to locations outside the tumour. Nonetheless, a click positioned close to the tumour can be beneficial by directing the model’s attention to the appropriate region, which occurred in
27/62
cases. When using the CVC with
n=1
, the number of invalid clicks reduces to 22, a reduction of 67
\%
, with 13 of these offering segmentation benefit. The number of detrimental clicks reduced by 75
\%
.

In Phase-3, there are 21 pairs of scans between which a tumour disappeared due to treatment. These cases were excluded from the Dice analysis above. However, without the CVC, LinGuinE propagates a click to the locations newly free of the tumour, resulting in a false positive. The CVC prevented
12/21
, that is, 57
\%
of false positives due to invalid clicks propagated from disappearing lesions. 4-D CBCT has no disappearing tumours, so the analysis was not conducted.

Click number
To determine the optimal number of clicks to propagate, we trialed all
n\in[1,10]
for both datasets. We found that Phase-3 achieved the optimal Dice with 4 clicks, and subsequently decreased in Dice scores. 4-D CBCT performed differently, with the optimal Dice being reached with 10 clicks. We conclude that the number of clicks is highly dependent on the dataset. To produce a sufficient Dice among both datasets, we decided to continue with
n=5
for further experimentation.

Radiologist Inputs
Two radiologists semi-automatically re-segmented lung tumors at one random time point for 53 patients in the Phase-3 dataset. Radiologists were instructed to place at least one click on each tumour and were not informed of the experiment’s aim, ensuring authentic interaction. We propogated the radiologist placed clicks to segment all remaining scans for each patient via LinGuinE. The result was 153 tumor segmentations across 133 scans, with a mean Dice of 0.633. The unguided model produced a mean Dice of 0.544 on the same tumours, indicating that LinGuinE improved segmentation accuracy by 16.39%. Standard LinGuinE resulted in a mean Dice of 0.644 (a 18.31% improvement over the unguided SwinUNETR). We found no statistically significant difference in Dice between the two scenarios (paired t-test,
p=0.535
).

Conclusion

In this study, we presented LinGuinE, a technique for longitudinal semi-automatic volumetric segmentation of lung tumours. LinGuinE demonstrates accurate segmentation on 63 longitudinal studies, outperforming unguided segmentation by over 20%, while only requiring radiologist interaction on a single scan. LinGuinE scales to an arbitrary number of time points, is time point agnostic for the starting scan, and (semi-)automatically segments the tumour boundaries. LinGuinE can potentially be applied as a facilitating tool for the introduction of volumetric biomarkers as a response criterion in chemotherapy. Further work is needed to establish LinGuinE as a segmentation tool in other organs and imaging modalities.
