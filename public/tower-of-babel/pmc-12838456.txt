Physiological and turf quality responses of tall fescue to varying irrigation levels and nitrogen doses under Mediterranean climate conditions
Authors: Sinem Zere Taskin, Fikret Yonter, Burak Nazmi Candogan, Asuman Cansev, Ugur Bilgili
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWater scarcity and growing environmental concerns have increased the need for effective irrigation and fertilization practices in turf management. This study investigated the effects of different irrigation levels (IL) and nitrogen doses (ND) on turf quality, color, clipping yield, and selected physiological parameters of tall fescue [Schedonorus arundinaceus (Schreb.) Dumort] under Mediterranean climate conditions. The experimental design was randomized blocks in a split‒split plot design with three replications. The irrigation levels were set at 25% (IL1), 50% (IL2), 75% (IL3), and 100% (IL4) pan evaporation, while nitrogen doses of 0.00, 1.25, 2.50, and 5.00 g m−2 were applied.ResultsThe irrigation level and nitrogen application significantly affected turf color, quality, clipping yield, leaf relative water content, chlorophyll content, turgor loss, and electrolyte leakage. Increased irrigation and nitrogen levels increased the leaf RWC and chlorophyll content while reducing turgor loss and electrolyte leakage, ultimately improving plant tolerance to environmental stress.ConclusionsOur findings suggest that acceptable turf color and quality throughout the year can be achieved with a 50% irrigation level and a nitrogen dose of 5.00 g m−2. A more economical and environmentally friendly alternative may be the combination of 50% irrigation and 2.50 g m−2 nitrogen, which is particularly effective in spring and autumn. In contrast, the combination of 25% or 50% irrigation with 1.25 g m−2 nitrogen did not result in acceptable turf quality in any season. However, acceptable turf color and quality can still be maintained in water-limited regions, especially during spring and autumn, by applying 5 g m−2 nitrogen under 25% irrigation.Keywords: Irrigation application, Nitrogen fertilization, Turf physiology, Turf performance

Background

The turfgrass sector includes irrigated and nonirrigated areas such as lawns, golf courses, parks, and sports fields [1]. These areas provide multiple ecological and social benefits, including erosion control, improved water quality, and recreational space [2].

Tall fescue [Schedonorus arundinaceus (Schreb.) Dumort; syn. F. arundinacea Schreb.] is one of the principal cool-season turf and forage grass species cultivated across temperate regions worldwide [3]. Among cool-season species, tall fescue is considered one of the most drought-tolerant due to its deep and extensive root system [4]. These agronomic features provide significant benefits over other cool-season turfgrass species such as Kentucky bluegrass (Poa pratensis L.) and perennial ryegrass (Lolium perenne L.) [5]. Previous studies have reported greater drought resistance in tall fescue than in other cool-season turfgrasses [6, 7]. Notable inter- and intraspecies variations in drought stress have been found across cool-season turfgrass species and cultivars [8–12]. Braun et al. [1] reported that some general rankings have remained consistent over time, with tall fescue and fine fescues (Festuca L. spp.) consistently ranked among the most drought-tolerant species, while Agrostis spp., rough bluegrass (Poa trivialis L.), and annual bluegrass (Poa annua L.) are typically found among the least drought-tolerant. However, the performance of tall fescue under prolonged summer drought still depends on adequate supplemental irrigation [13–15].

Turfgrasses are typically irrigated, and nitrogen (N) fertilizer is applied to improve turf color and quality [16]. Low-input cultural management strategies should be implemented to reduce the environmental impact of all inputs to turfgrass systems [17]. Moreover, increasing concerns about water scarcity have made efficient irrigation practices and water conservation critical, particularly in regions where irrigation is essential to preserve turf quality.

Nitrogen (N), which is the most important nutrient for turfgrass growth and development, is often the primary limiting factor [18, 19]), and proper N fertilizer is needed [20] to maintain the visual appeal of turfgrass and to repair damage caused by traffic stress [21, 22]. N is essential for the synthesis of amino acids, nucleic acids, pigments, and hormones and is key to achieving healthy, dark green turf through proper fertilization. However, excessive N fertilization leads to nitrogen leaching and contamination of groundwater and surface water bodies [23, 24]. Reducing nitrogen inputs while maintaining acceptable turf quality may help minimize this risk.

Turfgrass plants undergo drought stress when water availability to the roots becomes limited or when the transpiration rate increases excessively. The limitation of the water supply to roots and the increase in the transpiration rate often occur simultaneously in arid and semiarid climates [25], and their severity may be further exacerbated by climate change, which threatens the long-term persistence of perennial turf species [26]. In turfgrass systems, nitrogen fertilization plays a crucial role not only in promoting shoot growth and canopy density but also in influencing water consumption. Increased nitrogen availability stimulates vegetative growth and leaf area expansion, which in turn elevates transpiration demand and evapotranspiration (ET) rates. This relationship has been demonstrated in cool-season grasses such as tall fescue, where higher nitrogen inputs lead to denser turf canopies and greater water use due to increased stomatal conductance and leaf surface area [27]. On the other hand, under soil water deficit conditions, nitrogen uptake efficiency declines due to reduced mass flow, limited root activity, and restricted nitrate mobility in the soil solution [28].

Over the past four decades, extensive research has focused on turfgrass evapotranspiration, irrigation requirements, and strategies for improving water use efficiency. These topics remain relevant due to increasing population pressure and competition for limited water supplies [29]. Guo et al. [30] examined the interaction between irrigation and nitrogen in tall fescue under controlled greenhouse conditions, applying three nitrogen rates (0, 2, and 4 g m−2) with five irrigation levels corresponding to 90–100%, 75–85%, 60–70%, 45–55%, and 30–40% of field water capacity (FWC). They reported that 75–85% FWC × 2 g m−2 nitrogen rate improved both water and nitrogen use efficiency without compromising turf quality. Candogan et al. [31] evaluated 25%−125% Class A pan evaporation levels combined with two nitrogen rates (25 and 50 kg ha−1 month−1) and reported that ET increased with both nitrogen rate and irrigation level, with the highest seasonal turf quality achieved under the 100% irrigation × 50 kg N ha−1 treatment. They also concluded that acceptable quality could be maintained with reduced irrigation × nitrogen combinations depending on the month and rainfall, thereby enabling water conservation.

In this context, this study aimed to investigate the effects of different irrigation levels and nitrogen doses on turf performance and the physiological responses of tall fescue. These findings may contribute to the identification of cost-effective fertilization rates and water-saving irrigation strategies that help maintain acceptable turf quality while supporting more sustainable turf management under Mediterranean climate conditions.

Materials and methods

Plant material

Tall fescue cv. ‘Jaguar 4G’ was used as the plant material. Jaguar 4G is a widely used commercial cultivar [32] that shows high tolerance to heat, drought, salinity, and wear. The experiment was carried out on previously established trial plots.

Experimental design

The present research was conducted using a split‒split plot design and the treatments were randomized with three replications. The irrigation levels and nitrogen doses were assigned to the main plots and subplots, respectively. The plot dimensions were set to 2 × 1. Ammonium nitrate (N:33%) was used for nitrogen fertilization. Four different doses of nitrogen (0.00, 1.25, 2.50, and 5.00 g m−2) were applied by hand once every month from April to October throughout the experimental period (2 consecutive years). Irrigation was conducted every three days from May to October each year, with water applied to match 25%, 50%, 75%, and 100% of the pan evaporation [33].

Experimental area and weather conditions

This study was conducted in turfgrass experimental plots located at the Faculty of Agriculture, Bursa Uludag University, in Bursa, Türkiye (40°13′36″ N, 28°51′35″ E; 112 m above sea level). Tall fescue plots were established in 2013. The research site is situated in a region characterized by a typical Mediterranean climate, with hot, dry summers and mild, wet winters [34]. The study was carried out between 2014 and 2015 (Fig. 1).

View of the turfgrass experimental subplots in the research area

The weather data are presented in Fig. 2. Temperature data revealed that average temperatures, which were calculated as 16.0 °C in the first year of the study and 15.4 °C in the second year, were higher than the long-term average (14.6 °C). Monthly average temperatures ranged between 8.6 °C (February) and 25.7 °C (August) in year I, and 5.4 °C (January) and 26.4 °C (August) in year II. Regarding monthly precipitation, rainfall ranged between 4.6 mm (July) and 143.2 mm (December) in year I. In year II, monthly rainfall ranged between 0.0 mm (July) and—112.0 mm (January). Total annual precipitation was recorded as 846.6 mm in year I, 660.1 mm in year II, and 694.1 mm for the long-term average.

Temperature (°C) and rainfall (mm) data for the experimental plots during the experimental seasons

The soil of the experimental area was analyzed at the Laboratory of Soil Science and Plant Nutrition, Faculty of Agriculture, Bursa Uludağ University. According to the analysis results, the soil was classified as loam, with a texture comprising 46.25% sand, 30.99% silt, and 22.76% clay. The soil pH was measured at 8.48, indicating a moderately alkaline condition. Electrical conductivity (EC) was 468 µS cm⁻1, showing that the soil is non-saline. The organic matter content was 2.091%, while the total nitrogen (%N) level was relatively low at 0.106%. Available phosphorus (P) was measured at 30.95 mg kg⁻1, and the total potassium (K) content was notably high, reaching 5180 mg kg⁻1.

Irrigation treatments

The US Weather Bureau Class A pan evaporation method (Epan) was used to calculate the irrigation levels. To create a 90° wetting pattern, sprinkler heads were positioned at the four corners of each 4 × 6 m plot, and irrigation was applied at a rate of 7.5 mm h−1, which was less than the soil infiltration rate of 8.0 mm h−1.

Prior to irrigation, the soil water content was determined at depths of 0.15 m and 0.45 m via a neutron probe (503 DR Hydroprobe, CPN International, Inc., Martinez, CA, USA) positioned in the center of each plot.

Turfgrass color, quality and clipping yield

Turfgrass color [1 = completely yellow; 6 = acceptable (medium green); 9 = dark green] and quality (1 = poorest; 6 = minimally acceptable; 9 = excellent) were visually rated monthly on a scale from 1 to 9 [35–37]. The subplots were mowed to 4 cm once the turfgrass reached 6–8 cm to determine the clipping yield. After mowing the border areas to eliminate edge effects, a 0.5 × 1.0 m strip from the center of each plot was harvested at each clipping date, placed in paper bags, oven-dried at 70 °C for 48 h, and subsequently weighed. A total of 24 turf color and quality observations (12 per year, once each month) and 8 clipping yield measurements (4 per year) were conducted during the experiment.

Physiological measurements

To understand the impact of the various treatments on physiological measurements of turfgrass species, leaf relative water content (RWC), turgor loss (TL), relative chlorophyll content (CC), and electrolyte leakage (EL) were measured in leaf samples during the hottest summer month, July. For physiological measurements, turf layers with their root zone and attached soil were collected from each subplot and transported to the laboratory.

Three 0.5 cm diameter discs were taken from the plant leaves to measure the leaf RWC and TL. The discs were weighed fresh, soaked in distilled water for 4 h to measure turgor weight, and dried at 70 °C for 24 h to record dry weight. The RWC and TL were calculated on the basis of the obtained data [38].

The CC was assessed via a portable chlorophyll meter (SPAD-502, Konica Minolta Sensing, Inc., Japan) on ten leaves per plot. Each leaf was measured at three different points, and the average of the readings was calculated [39].

EL was determined using the ion leakage method. Leaf discs (0.5 cm in diameter) were excised from the collected samples and placed into test tubes containing 20 mL of distilled water. The tubes were then subjected to vacuum and incubated at room temperature (24 ± 1 °C) for 4 h on a shaker set at 250 rpm. Following incubation, initial conductivity measurements were taken using a conductivity meter (WTW TetraCon 325, InoLab Cond Level 1, Weilheim, Germany). The final conductivity was measured after autoclaving the samples to induce complete cell death.

The total amount of irrigation water and rainfall were recorded during the experiments. In the first year and second years, the total quantity of irrigation water varied between 144.3 and 577.2 mm and between 188.5 and 753.8 mm, respectively. The total rainfall was 226.2 mm in the first year and 88.3 mm in the second year (Fig. 3).

Total amount of irrigation water and rainfall during the experiments. IL1, IL2, IL3, and IL4 represent the application of water to match 25%, 50%, 75%, and 100% of the open-pan evaporation, respectively

Statistical analysis

Data obtained from the study were subjected to analysis of variance (ANOVA) according to a split-split plot design. Probability levels of 0.01 and 0.05 were used in the significance tests. When F values were significant, differences between means were determined via the least significant difference (LSD) test. All statistical analyses were conducted using JMP Pro 13. Homogeneity of variances between the first and second trials was evaluated using Hartley’s F-max test [40]. Hartley’s F-max test [40] showed no significant differences in the variances for color, quality, and clipping yield between the first and second year trials,therefore, the data were combined for analysis. Data for irrigation treatments were analyzed separately for each year, and physiological measurements were evaluated using data collected during the second year of the experiment.

Results

Turfgrass color, quality, and clipping yield

The irrigation level (IL), nitrogen dose (ND), and the IL × ND interaction had statistically significant effects on all the parameters. IL1 × 0.00, IL1 × 1.25, IL2 × 0.00, IL2 × 1.25, and IL3 × 0.00 resulted in unacceptable turf color and quality in all seasons. The IL1 × 2.50 interaction produced acceptable color in spring, and quality in spring and autumn. The IL1 × 5.00 interaction yielded acceptable color in spring and autumn, and acceptable quality during winter, spring, and autumn. The plots under IL2 × 2.50 interaction were within the acceptable range for color in spring and autumn, and regarding quality in the same seasons. The IL2 × 1.25 interaction provided acceptable turf color and quality values in both spring and autumn. The IL2 × 5.00, IL3 × 2.50, IL3 × 5.00, IL4 × 1.25, IL4 × 2.50, and IL4 × 5.00 interactions consistently maintained acceptable turf color and quality across all seasons (Table 1).

The interaction between increasing nitrogen doses under each irrigation level resulted in increased clipping yield values. The lowest yield was observed in the IL1 × 0.00 combination, in summer (57.3 g m−2), whereas the highest yield was recorded under IL4 × 5.00, in autumn (642.3 g m−2) (Table 1).

In the spring season, the highest clipping yield was obtained from the IL4 × 5.00 interaction (638.0 a), followed by the IL4 × 2.50 (495.0 b), IL3 × 5.00 (526.6 b) and IL2 × 5.00 (520.0 b) interactions which were in the same statistical group. In the summer season, the highest clipping yield was obtained from the IL4 × 5.00 (449.6 a) interaction. It was followed by the IL3 × 5.00 (369.6 b) interaction. During the winter season, the highest yield was once again achieved from the IL4 × 5.00 (863.6 a) interaction. Similar to spring, this interaction was followed by the IL4 × 2.50 (642.3 b), IL3 × 5.00 (661.6 b), and IL2 × 5.00 (590.6 b) interactions (Table 1).

Physiological parameters

IL, ND, and their interaction had statistically significant effects on RWC, TL, CC, and EL. Examination of the data presented in Table 2 revealed that increasing irrigation levels and nitrogen doses resulted in higher RWC and CC values, whereas TL and EL values declined. The highest RWC and CC values were observed in the IL4 × 5.00 g N m−2 interaction. This was followed by the 100% irrigation level with 2.50 g N m−2 and the 75% irrigation level with 5.00 g N m−2, which were in the same statistical group. In contrast, the lowest RWC and CC values were obtained under IL1 × 0.00 g N m−2, where the turf color and quality were also the lowest. The highest TL and EL were observed under the IL1 × 0.00 g N m−2 interaction, whereas the lowest values occurred under the IL4 × 5.00 g N m−2 combination (Table 2).

Discussion

The evaluation of IL × N interactions revealed clear patterns in turf color, quality, and clipping yield responses. Low-input combinations such as IL1 × 0.00, IL1 × 1.25, IL2 × 0.00, IL2 × 1.25, and IL3 × 0.00 treatments failed to achieve acceptable turf color and quality in any season. Moderate nitrogen input under low to moderate irrigation, as in IL1 × 2.50 and IL2 × 2.50, was sufficient to produce acceptable color and quality during spring and autumn, while higher nitrogen rates (5.00 g m−2) extended acceptability to additional seasons. Clipping yields followed a similar trend, increasing with nitrogen rate within each irrigation level. These results emphasize that irrigation and nitrogen management should be considered jointly to optimize turf performance. Our study confirmed that acceptable turf color and quality can be achieved in all seasons with at least IL2 irrigation (50% Epan) with 5 g N and IL3 irrigation (75% Epan) and a nitrogen dose of 2.50 g m−2. Similarly, Guo et al. [30] reported that maintaining the soil water-holding capacity at 85–75% and applying nitrogen at 2 g m−2 ensured good turf quality in tall fescue with minimal maintenance input. Despite its deep rooting capacity, the performance of tall fescue in summer, particularly during rainless periods, still depends on adequate supplemental irrigation. Qian and Engelke [41] reported that maintaining acceptable turf quality in ‘Rebel II’ tall fescue required irrigation equivalent to about 67% of evaporation from a Class A pan. On the other hand, some researchers have suggested different irrigation and nitrogen application strategies for tall fescue in different months to maintain turf color and quality. Overall, they reported that 100% of the Epan evaporation combined with 50 kg N ha−1 provided sufficiently dark turf color and quality. However, for May, they recommended 25% Epan × 25 kg N ha−1; for June, July, and August, 50% Epan × 25 kg N ha−1; and for September, either 50% Epan × 25 kg N ha−1 or 50% Epan × 50 kg N ha−1 [31]. Schiavon et al. [42], in their study evaluating the performance of four tall fescue cultivars under two nitrogen rates (7.5 and 15.0 g N m−2 year−1) applied twice annually as a controlled-release fertilizer, reported that the application of 15.0 g N m−2 year−1 slightly improved turf quality during the winter months, whereas 7.5 g N m−2 year−1 was generally sufficient to maintain acceptable turf quality under the humid subtropical climate of northeastern Italy. However, in Mediterranean regions, summer heat and a longer growing season may increase maintenance requirements. Wu et al. [43] reported that, under the Mediterranean climate of southern California, the longer growing period necessitated nitrogen applications higher than the typical recommendations for tall fescue (146–293 kg N ha−1 yr−1; UC [44]). In that study, the researchers applied 195, 293, and 390 kg ha−1 N, and found that the 390 kg ha−1 N rate produced the highest values for color (6.4) and quality (6.1), whereas 293 kg ha−1 N resulted in a turf quality score (5.9) that was below the acceptable level.

Increased nitrogen and irrigation applications increased the clipping yield in tall fescue. However, greater plant height resulting from this growth necessitates more frequent mowing, which is undesirable in turfgrass management [45]. Nitrogen is generally not applied with the aim of maximizing turfgrass clipping yield, as this leads to higher maintenance requirements. Instead, nitrogen fertilization is intended to maintain dense, healthy, and aesthetically pleasing turf. Consequently, most fertilization programs are designed to regulate nitrogen availability, ensuring that shoot growth remains below the maximum potential [46].

The RWC indicates the plant water status, reflecting the physiological water deficit at the cellular level. It symbolizes changes in osmotic adjustment, turgor potential, and water potential [47]. RWC is considered a useful indicator for selecting genotypes with superior drought tolerance [48]. In this study, increased irrigation and nitrogen levels generally led to increased leaf relative water content (RWC) and chlorophyll content (CC). Although the IL4 × 2.50 g N m−2 and IL3 × 5.00 g N m−2 treatments also performed well and fell within the same statistical group, the IL4 × 5.00 g N m−2 treatment had superior effects on both physiological traits and turf visual quality. These findings suggest that combining full irrigation with a high nitrogen dose can have a relatively strong positive effect on the physiological health of turfgrass. Our findings are supported by those of Jiang and Huang [49], who reported that decreases in turf color and quality under drought stress were closely associated with reduced RWC in the early stages of stress. Mahdavi et al. [50] reported that increasing drought stress significantly reduced RWC in tall fescue. Chang et al. [51] reported that turfgrasses subjected to high nitrogen application maintain higher leaf RWC under drought conditions than those subjected to low nitrogen application. On the other hand, it should be noted that the relative water content may vary significantly among genotypes. Kirigwi and Saha [48] reported that RWC values ranged from 33.7% to 97.3%, with an average of 79.7%, in their study involving 1000 tall fescue genotypes. On the basis of this large-scale screening, the researchers concluded that tall fescue genotypes present distinct physiological characteristics and substantial variability in RWC. A relatively high CC provides several advantages to plants under drought stress, as it is closely linked to their photosynthetic capacity. In plants, CC serves as a reliable indicator of leaf nitrogen status and is strongly correlated with both leaf nitrogen and chlorophyll concentrations [52]. Benett et al. [53] reported that increasing nitrogen application leads to a linear increase in chlorophyll levels, highlighting the relationships among chlorophyll content, nitrogen nutrition, and plant performance.

In the present study, we found that increased irrigation and nitrogen availability significantly reduced turgor loss in tall fescue. Although the existence of a deep and extensive root system has been suggested as a mechanism for drought avoidance in tall fescue [54, 55], our results indicated that without adequate irrigation, this potential advantage may not be sufficient to maintain turgor pressure under prolonged water stress.

Plants exposed to adverse environmental conditions often undergo significant physiological changes, and one of the most sensitive structures affected is the cell membrane. Electrolyte leakage (EL) is a key indicator of membrane integrity and typically increases under stress conditions [56]. In our study, EL levels increased under water stress, with the highest values observed at lower irrigation levels. Conversely, the lowest EL was recorded under full irrigation (IL4), indicating that an adequate water supply helped maintain membrane stability and reduced stress-induced damage. In support of our results, Jiang and Huang [57] reported that turf quality and leaf RWC decreased, whereas EL increased under drought stress in tall fescue cultivars.

Conclusion

This study aimed to investigate the responses of tall fescue in terms of turf color, quality, clipping yield, and some physiological parameters at different irrigation levels and nitrogen doses under Mediterranean climate conditions. The irrigation level and nitrogen application significantly affected turf color, quality, clipping yield, leaf relative content, chlorophyll content, turgor loss, and electrolyte leakage. In summary, increased IL and ND increased leaf RWC and CC, reduced TL and EL, and ultimately improved plant tolerance to environmental stress.

Our findings suggest that acceptable turf color and quality throughout the year could be achieved with a 50% irrigation level and 5.00 g N m−2. Providing acceptable turf color and quality with a 50% irrigation level and a 2.50 g m−2 nitrogen dose interaction for more economical and environmentally friendly application in tall fescue seems possible in spring and autumn. In the summer season, acceptable turf color and quality can be achieved with at least the 50% × 5.00 interaction; however, similar results can also be obtained with the 75% × 2.50 interaction. On the other hand, at the 25% and 50% irrigation levels, the interactions with the 1.25 g m−2 nitrogen dose did not result in acceptable turf color and quality in any season. In regions where water is scarce, an acceptable turf color and quality can be obtained, especially in the spring and autumn seasons, when a 5 g m−2 nitrogen dose is applied at a 25% irrigation level.
