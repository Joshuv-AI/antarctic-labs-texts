EfficientXLang: Towards Improving Token Efficiency Through Cross-Lingual Reasoning
Sanchit Ahuja, Praneetha Vaddamanu*, Barun Patra

Abstract
Despite recent advances in Reasoning Language Models (RLMs), most research focuses solely on English, even though many models are pretrained on multilingual data. In this work, we investigate: Is English the most token-efficient language for reasoning? We evaluate three open-source RLMs: DeepSeek R1 , Qwen 2.5 , and Qwen 3 , across four math datasets and seven typologically diverse languages. We find that reasoning in non-English languages not only reduces token usage, but also preserves accuracy. These gains persist even after translating the reasoning traces into English, suggesting genuine shifts in reasoning behavior rather than surface-level linguistic effects. The extent of improvement, however, depends on the model’s multilingual strength. Our findings motivate a broader view of reasoning in language models, highlighting the potential of multilingual reasoning and the importance of strong multilingual foundations. Introduction

Recent advancements have shown that increasing inference-time compute can significantly enhance the downstream performance of large language models. In particular, RLMs benefit from extended test-time computation through long chains-of-thought, resulting in improved reasoning capabilities and task performance
(
DeepSeek-AI 2025
;
OpenAI et al. 2024b
)
. Despite this progress, the majority of prior work has focused on reasoning in English, implicitly assuming it as the default or optimal language for thought
(
Wendler et al. 2024
)
.

A key limitation of current RLMs lies in the number of “thinking tokens” they consume during inference. Since test-time compute scales with the number of tokens generated, longer reasoning traces directly impact latency and cost. This inefficiency is often exacerbated by the phenomenon of overthinking
(
Cuadron et al. 2025b
)
, where models produce unnecessarily verbose intermediate reasoning. To address this, several works have explored length-aware rewards in reinforcement learning setups to encourage brevity in reasoning traces
(
Aggarwal & Welleck 2025
;
Sui et al. 2025b
)
. However, these approaches typically require model retraining, which is computationally expensive and infeasible for many practitioners.

In this work, we take a different perspective on language and reasoning by asking a simple yet underexplored question:
Is English the most efficient language for reasoning?
We explore whether reasoning in other languages can produce shorter but equally effective reasoning traces, potentially reducing inference-time compute. This question is timely for two reasons: (1) many reasoning language models (RLMs) are multilingual by design
(
Team 2025a
)
; and (2) prior work suggests that languages vary in how clearly they express mathematical ideas, affecting reasoning performance
(
Han 2001
)
. Our key contributions are:

-
We show that across a range of mathematical reasoning datasets, reasoning in alternative languages consistently results in fewer tokens, often matching the final English accuracy.

-
We demonstrate that even when translated back to English, reasoning traces originally generated in other languages remain shorter than those natively generated in English. This suggests that the reduction in token count is not merely a function of tokenizer fertility or linguistic compactness, but reflects genuine differences in the characteristics of reasoning traces compared to their English counterparts.

-
We provide quantitative evidence that English is not always the most effective language for reasoning, with traces in other languages consistently preferred for their conciseness, clarity, and structure.

Experiments

To assess whether reasoning in other languages can enhance thinking token efficiency, we experiment with seven typologically diverse languages: Chinese, Russian, Spanish, Hindi, Arabic, Korean, and Turkish, along with English. We prompt the reasoning models in English, instructing them to carry out their reasoning in the target language while providing the final answer in English. This setup allows us to isolate the effect of language on the reasoning process itself. For inferencing, we used the SGLang framework
Zheng et al. 2024
, which facilitates the extraction of intermediate reasoning traces.

Metrics:
Different RLMs vary in their ability to follow prompt instructions for generating reasoning traces in the indicated target language. To better evaluate multilingual reasoning performance, we introduce two complimentary metrics: Target Language Consistency (TLC), and Target Language Pass @k (TLP@k) in conjunction with the commonly used Pass@k metric. We use
k=4
in all our results.

Target Language Consistency (TLC)
: measures how well models adhere to the target language constraint when prompted. Given a dataset of size
m
, with question-answer pairs
(q_{i},y_{i})
, and target language
j
, let
\mathcal{R}^{(k)}(i,j)=\{r_{1},...,r_{k}\}
be the set of generated reasoning traces under the prompt to reason in language
j
. TLC for a sample is the fraction of traces in the correct language, and the overall TLC is the average across all samples. We use a LID model
Joulin et al. 2016
to detect the language of a reasoning trace.

Target Language Pass @k (TLP@k)
: Analogous to the Pass@k metric, we propose TLP@k to measure the fraction of samples where at least one of the top-k reasoning traces both elicits the correct answer and is in the target language. Extending
Chen et al. 2021
, for measuring TLP@k, we use a low variance estimator
\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left[1-\frac{\binom{n-c}{k}}{\binom{n}{k}}\right]
, where
c
is the number of reasoning traces that yield the correct answer and are in the correct target language among the
n
generated traces. The final TLP@k is the average across all samples. Further details about the implementation of this estimator are provided in the Appendix
A.2
.

RQ2: Do multilingual reasoning traces differ meaningfully from their English counterparts?
To answer this, we examine whether token efficiency gains arise from linguistic conciseness or deeper semantic differences in reasoning. We use GPT-4o (temperature = 0.6, top-p = 0.95) to translate non-English traces into English and compare their token counts to the original English versions.

As shown in Table
1
, translated traces remain significantly shorter, indicating that non-English prompts can elicit more concise reasoning paths. This suggests that multilingual prompting can uncover efficient strategies not naturally produced in English.

In conclusion, multilingual reasoning traces are meaningfully different and often more concise. Their brevity persists after translation, suggesting more efficient reasoning strategies—not just linguistic differences.

RQ3: Are Multilingual Reasoning Traces Better Than English?
To further assess whether non-English reasoning traces are
better overall
than their English counterparts, we conduct an automated pairwise comparison using GPT-4o as the judge. For each language, we select cases where both English and non-English reasoning are correct. We then compare the English trace to the GPT-4o-translated non-English trace, leveraging GPT-4o’s high translation quality
OpenAI et al. 2024a
to control for linguistic brevity. Evaluations use GPT-4o with greedy decoding and a prompt (Appendix
A.1
) focused on clarity, logic, and efficiency. To reduce position bias, we assess each pair in both orders, (A, B) and (B, A) and retain only consistent preferences.
We leverage a one-sided binomial test to establish if reasoning in the native language is (statistically) significantly better than reasoning in English. Table
3
reports results for
DeepSeek R1
. Across benchmarks, most languages show significant gains when reasoning in the native language, with Arabic, Korean, and Spanish exceeding 90% win rates.
Qwen 3
shows similar gains across all non-English languages (Table
9
, Appendix
A.4
). We also observe qualitative difference between English outputs and language translated outputs (Ref Appendix
A.5
for a
Qwen 3
example). We omit
Qwen 2.5

These findings suggest that multilingual reasoning can enhance the quality of a model’s thought process. Since all responses are translated to English for evaluation, structural differences are controlled for, indicating that gains likely arise from improved clarity, structure, and conciseness.

Related Work

Efficient Reasoning
: Recent interest in LRMs has spurred investigations into reasoning efficiency as to why some tasks demand more reasoning tokens and whether models can answer correctly without explicit reasoning steps
(
Cuadron et al. 2025a
;
Hashemi et al. 2025
;
Sui et al. 2025a
;
Chen et al. 2025
;
Ma et al. 2025
;
Marjanović et al. 2025
)
. This work explores reasoning efficiency from a multilingual perspective, analyzing whether certain reasoning paths across languages are both accurate and token-efficient.

Multilingual Reasoning
: Prior studies showed that prompting models in the target language often underperforms compared to reasoning in English
Qin et al. 2023
;
Shi et al. 2022
;
Ko et al. 2025
. However, RLMs like Deepseek R1 and Qwen have shown stronger multilingual reasoning without explicit tuning
DeepSeek-AI 2025
;
Team 2025a
;
Yang et al. 2024
. We build on this by introducing tighter evaluation bounds and fine-grained trace-level analysis, uncovering language-specific reasoning paths that are both accurate and token-efficient.

Concurrently, several recent works have also begun to explore multilingual reasoning in depth
Gao et al. 2025
;
Bajpai & Chakraborty 2025
.

5
Conclusion and Future Work

In this work, we explore two central questions: (1) Is English the optimal language for reasoning? and (2) Are multilingual reasoning traces meaningfully different from their English counterparts?

We find that multilingual prompting can reduce token usage by 20–40% without compromising accuracy, presenting a simple yet effective strategy for improving inference efficiency without retraining. These efficiency gains persist even after translating the outputs back to English, suggesting that the improvements stem from genuine differences in reasoning behavior rather than mere linguistic compactness.

Importantly, the effectiveness of this approach is tied to the model’s underlying multilingual strength. Our findings highlight the need to improve base multilingual alignment to better support efficient and high-quality reasoning across languages. Future work should explore training and preference optimization across more diverse languages to unlock these benefits more broadly.

Limitations

In this work, we focus exclusively on reasoning traces generated through English prompts. The behavior of models may vary when prompted in target languages or when guided using techniques like teacher-forcing with a few target-language tokens. Our analysis is limited to seven languages and relies solely on open-source reasoning models. Proprietary models such as OpenAI’s o1
OpenAI et al. 2024b
may exhibit different behavior from those studied here.
