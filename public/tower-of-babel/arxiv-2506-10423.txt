PAL: Probing Audio Encoders via LLMs - A Study of Information Transfer from Audio Encoders to LLMs
Tony Alex, Wish Suharitdamrong, Sara Atito, Armin Mustafa, Philip J. B. Jackson, Imran Razzak, Muhammad Awais

Abstract
The integration of audio perception capabilities into Large Language Models (LLMs) has enabled significant advances in Audio-LLMs. Although application-focused developments, particularly in curating training data for specific capabilities e.g., audio reasoning, have progressed rapidly, the underlying mechanisms that govern efficient transfer of rich semantic representations from audio encoders to LLMs remain under-explored. We conceptualize effective audio-LLM interaction as the LLM’s ability to proficiently probe the audio encoder representations to satisfy textual queries. This paper presents a systematic investigation on how architectural design choices can affect that. Beginning with a standard Pengi/LLaVA-style audio-LLM architecture, we propose and evaluate several modifications guided by hypotheses derived from mechanistic interpretability studies and LLM operational principles. Our experiments demonstrate that: (1) delaying audio integration until the LLM’s initial layers establish textual context that enhances its ability to probe the audio representations for relevant information; (2) the LLM can proficiently probe audio representations exclusively through LLM layer’s attention submodule, without requiring propagation to its Feed-Forward Network (FFN) submodule; (3) an efficiently integrated ensemble of diverse audio encoders provides richer, complementary representations, thereby broadening the LLM’s capacity to probe a wider spectrum of audio information. All hypotheses are evaluated using an identical three-stage training curriculum on a dataset of 5.6 million audio-text pairs, ensuring controlled comparisons. Our final architecture, which incorporates all proposed modifications, achieves relative improvements from 10% to 60% over the baseline, validating our approach to optimizing cross-modal information transfer in audio-LLMs. Project page: https://ta012.github.io/PAL/

Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs)

;

;

have emerged as the foundational technology for natural language interaction with machines, demonstrating remarkable conversational fluency. Despite this success, their perceptual capabilities remain limited primarily to text, restricting their ability to understand the physical world. This limitation has inspired significant research into multi-modal LLMs (MLLMs)
liu2023visual
;
gong2024listen
;

;
jiang2024mixtral
;
maaz2023video
, which expand traditional LLMs by integrating additional sensory modalities such as vision (Vision LLMs)

;

;
wang2024cogvlm
, audio (Large Audio Language Models (LALMs) or simply audio-LLMs)

;

;
ghosh2025audio
, and other inputs
brohan2023rt
;
thawkar2023xraygpt
to foster more natural, intuitive, and effective human-machine interfaces.

Audio-LLMs, aim to integrate audio representations generated by various audio encoders into LLMs. A widely adopted architectural approach is the Pengi/LLaVA
deshmukh2023pengi
;
liu2023visual
style, exemplified by audio-LLMs such as Pengi
deshmukh2023pengi
, LTU
gong2024listen
, SALMONN
tang2024salmonn
and GAMA
ghosh2024gama
.
These architectures project audio embeddings into the LLMs’ input word embedding space.
The resulting audio embeddings are prepended to textual tokens, enabling the LLM to process audio alongside text, effectively treating audio information as additional textual tokens. An alternative architectural approach inspired by Flamingo
alayrac2022flamingo
is the Audio-Flamingo architecture
kong2024audio
. Audio-Flamingo introduces cross-attention and Feed-Forward Network (FFN) blocks inserted between successive LLM layers to pass audio representations via latent token into the LLM. Within the new cross-attention block, textual tokens attend to latent audio tokens, then are passed to cross-attention block’s FFN module, before proceeding to the LLM’s next layer.

Recent advances in audio representation learning have yielded powerful models through self-supervised methods like Audio-MAE
huang2022amae
, BEATs
chen2022beats
, ASiT
Ahmed_2024
, EAT
chen2024eat
, SSLAM
alex2025sslam
and multi-modal supervised approaches such as CLAP
elizalde2023clap
, LAION-CLAP
wu2023large
, ReCLAP
ghosh2025reclap
. While these models learn rich audio representations, they typically reside in abstract embedding spaces, necessitating further engineering for downstream tasks like audio event detection or acoustic scene analysis. A common technique,
linear probing
, involves training a simple linear layer on these representations for specific tasks; yet the outputs from such probing are not inherently human-interpretable. We posit that audio-LLMs provide a more direct interface, allowing
humans to probe these sophisticated audio representations
through natural language interaction.
Consequently, to enable an LLM to effectively probe audio encoder representations and satisfy textual queries, the connection mechanism must effectively transfer crucial audio details, such as event signals and temporal dynamics, from the audio encoder into the LLM’s embedding and concept space. Once the LLM receives this information, it can leverage its reasoning and generative capabilities to respond appropriately to user queries, whether requiring concise answers, detailed explanations, or the generation of more complex and contextually nuanced textual outputs, such as poetry adhering to specific rhyming schemes. Therefore, ensuring this signal effectively reaches the LLM’s conceptual space is fundamental. Our exploration specifically focuses on optimizing this information transfer. Furthermore, recent advances in mechanistic interpretability
bricken2023monosemanticity
;
templeton2024scaling
, particularly techniques like sparse autoencoders
bricken2023monosemanticity
, offer valuable tools for understanding the internal workings of LLMs. We use these insights from interpretability studies to guide architectural design choices for improved information flow.

In this paper, we conduct a principled comparative study focused on the information transfer mechanisms from audio encoders to LLMs. Beginning with a baseline architecture inspired by the Pengi or LLaVA style (e.g., Pengi, LTU, GAMA), we systematically investigate architectural modifications. Each modification is motivated by a specific hypothesis regarding information flow. We evaluate these modifications through detailed empirical analysis, employing a standardized training curriculum and dataset to ensure fair comparisons. Our objective is to identify architectural designs that facilitate efficient information transfer from the audio encoder to the LLM, balancing performance gains with computational efficiency and minimizing parameter overhead.

Our primary contributions in this work are:

1. Novel audio-LLM integration insights:
We propose and systematically investigate various techniques for effective integration of audio modalities with LLMs. Our methodology is intuitive, drawing significant inspiration from mechanistic interpretability research alongside other relevant fields. The key findings of our investigations include:

Delayed fusion benefits:
Delaying the introduction of audio representations to later LLM layers (termed
delayed fusion
) is more effective, as it enables the LLM to first establish and refine textual context before querying audio information.
Attention sufficiency:
Processing audio information exclusively through the LLM’s attention mechanism, and subsequently discarding the audio representations before FFN submodules, is a sufficient strategy for effective and efficient audio-language integration, maintaining or improving performance compared to full propagation.
Audio encoder ensemble advantage:
Employing an ensemble of diverse audio encoders, combining a self-supervised encoder for fine-grained semantics with specialized language-aligned encoders for general audio, music, and speech, enhances the audio-LLM’s ability to handle varied auditory inputs.

2. Fair and rigorous architectural comparisons:
To accurately assess the inherent potential of each audio-LLM architecture, we employ a standardized three-stage training curriculum as in Figure
1
, ensuring consistency across critical factors like pre-training data, batch size, training epochs, and overall curriculum design.

3. Development of a novel audio-LLM:
Based on the insights gained, we have developed a new audio-LLM architecture that incorporates these findings.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: First, we present our architectural hypotheses and their corresponding empirical evaluations. Next, we detail our experimental setup, including the standardized training curriculum and datasets used to ensure fair comparisons. We then discuss our model’s performance relative to prior art. The Appendix contains supplementary qualitative assessments, featuring both human evaluations and AI-based comparisons of output samples between our approach and previous works.

2.1
Baseline Architecture

Our baseline audio-LLM adopts the Pengi/LLaVA style architecture for integrating audio, similar to recent works such as Pengi
deshmukh2023pengi
, LTU
gong2024listen
, GAMA
ghosh2024gama

, to generate audio representations. These representations are then processed through a cross-attention mechanism with learnable latent tokens, followed by a projection layer to map them into the LLM’s token embedding space. Further details regarding this latent cross-attention mechanism-based connector module is provided in Section
2.4
. The model input follows the sequence: system prompt, audio tokens, user prompt, response. This format is used throughout the paper. We trained this baseline architecture using the experimental setup detailed previously. Evaluation results are presented in Table

2.2
Hypothesis 1: Bypassing initial layers for audio integration

The baseline architecture, as described in Section
2.1
, integrates audio representations by inserting them between the system prompt and the user prompt tokens in the LLM’s input embedding space. We challenge this early integration strategy. Recent mechanistic interpretability studies
nanda2023factfinding
suggest that the notion of
single-step detokenization
is inaccurate; instead, tokens are gradually converted into meaningful concepts over several initial LLM layers. We hypothesize that inserting audio representations before the LLM has sufficiently processed the textual prompts (system and user) to form initial concepts might hinder its ability to effectively query the audio information. The presence of audio tokens in the very first layers could potentially disrupt the text-based concept formation required to understand the user’s query itself.

Based on this reasoning, we propose a
delayed fusion
approach. We hypothesize that delaying the introduction of audio representations until after the initial text-only processing layers will allow the LLM to first establish a textual context, enabling it to better utilize the subsequent audio information. For this experiment, we modify the baseline by bypassing the first four layers for audio integration. The audio representations are introduced starting from the fifth layer of the LLM, as illustrated in Figure
2
(A) with
k
= 5, allowing the initial layers to operate solely on the textual tokens.

We evaluated this hypothesis training curriculum, discussed in Section
4.1
. The results, presented in Table
1
, show improved performance across all training stages compared to the baseline.

Finding 1

Introducing audio representations after initial text-only processing layers allows the LLM to establish a textual context first. This leads to more effective querying for relevant audio information from the audio representations by the LLM and results in improved overall performance compared to early integration.

2.3
Hypothesis 2: Attention necessary and sufficient for audio encoder information transfer to LLM

Recent mechanistic interpretability studies of Large Language Models (LLMs) investigate how concepts are represented. LLMs often exhibit superposition, compressing numerous features within hidden activations
elhage2022superposition
. Sparse autoencoders
bricken2023monosemanticity
;
templeton2024scaling
are employed to decompose these, revealing that semantic concepts can correspond to specific, activatable features. For instance, a concept like
"dog"
may be identified with a distinct feature. Activating such a feature, identified via sparse autoencoders, can directly influence model behavior, as demonstrated in the
Golden Gate Bridge Claude

templeton2024scaling
experiment, where this caused the LLM to persistently reference the landmark even when un-related questions were asked.

Building on this understanding of concept representation through feature activation, we hypothesize that effective cross-modal grounding in audio-LLMs, specifically for audio-to-LLM concept association, similarly requires audio tokens to induce the activation of relevant sound-related conceptual features within the LLM’s textual token embeddings. This mechanism, where distinct auditory inputs trigger corresponding linguistic concept activations, is posited as crucial for an audio-LLM to perform tasks such as answering,
Which animal sound is heard in the audio?
For example, if the input audio contains a
dog’s bark
, we propose that features within the textual embeddings corresponding to the concept
dog
must be activated for the model to correctly associate the auditory stimulus with its linguistic counterpart, mirroring the kind of concept-specific activations observed in text-only LLMs
bricken2023monosemanticity
.

A standard LLM layer comprises an attention submodule and an FFN submodule. In typical audio-LLM architectures (e.g., Pengi
deshmukh2023pengi
, LTU
gong2024listen
, GAMA
ghosh2024gama
) and the previous variants discussed above, both audio and text tokens are processed through attention and FFN submodules. It is evident from prior studies that, for audio tokens to influence and activate concepts within text tokens, the LLM’s attention mechanism, which facilitates inter-token interaction, is a
necessary
condition. To investigate whether attention is also a
sufficient
condition for this cross-modal concept activation, we introduce an architectural variant.
In this variant, audio tokens interact with text tokens exclusively within the attention submodule of each LLM layer by passing the information from audio tokens to text tokens via causal attention. Subsequently, these audio representations are discarded and only the text tokens are propagated to the FFN submodules, as illustrated in Figure
2
(B).
Regarding implementation, we project audio tokens separately as input for each LLM layer, pass them to the layer’s attention mechanism, and then discard them, as shown in Figure
2
(B). While specific implementation details and further optimizations are deferred to section
2.4
, Appendix
C
and Table
4
, our primary objective here is to evaluate the hypothesis that processing audio information exclusively through the LLM’s attention mechanism, followed by discarding it, is both a necessary and sufficient condition for effective audio-language integration. Empirical results, presented in Table
1
, indicate that this architectural modification maintains or improves performance across all three stages of our training curriculum (Section
4
) compared to the preceding variant.

Leveraging parametric versus contextual knowledge
: Here we posit how this architectural modification utilizes two types of knowledge inherent in pre-trained LLMs: (1) parametric knowledge, primarily embedded within the FFN layers as a result of extensive language pre-training, and (2) contextual knowledge, which is dynamically incorporated through attention mechanisms. The empirical success of this variant shows that audio input, as contextual information, can effectively induce required concept activations in text token representations via attention-based modulation, without needing direct FFN processing of audio representations. Consequently, audio information indirectly accesses the LLM’s parametric knowledge: the audio context "piggybacks" on text tokens, as attention mechanisms reconfigure these representations, which then engage relevant concept-related pathways during FFN processing. This outcome demonstrates that robust cross-modal concept alignment is attainable while preserving the integrity of the LLM’s pre-trained linguistic knowledge, predominantly encoded in its FFNs. Such a strategy offers gains in architectural efficiency and provides deeper insights into multimodal information integration.

Finding 2

Integrating audio information exclusively via an LLM layer’s attention submodule, without propagating it to the layer’s FFN submodule, is sufficient for the LLM to effectively probe audio representations and engage relevant pathways during FFN processing to satisfy textual queries. This method maintains or improves performance and may preserve the FFN’s pre-trained linguistic knowledge while still facilitating cross-modal information transfer.

2.4
Hypothesis 3: Encoder ensembles enhance audio-LLM performance

Audio representation learning research is notably specialized, encompassing distinct sub-disciplines for speech (e.g., Whisper
radford2023robust
, LAION-CLAP-Speech
wu2023large
), music (e.g., MERT
li2023mert
, LAION-CLAP-Music
wu2023large
), and general audio (e.g., SSLAM
alex2025sslam
, LAION-CLAP-General
wu2023large
). This divergence contrasts with modalities like vision, where general-purpose encoders such as DINOv2
oquab2024dinov2learningrobustvisual
can effectively represent a broad range of RGB images. Consequently, for audio-LLMs designed to handle diverse auditory inputs (spanning music, speech, and general audio), the use of specialized encoders, or ensembles thereof, becomes a critical consideration.

Furthermore, prior work on audio-LLMs (e.g., LTU
gong2024listen
, GAMA
ghosh2024gama
, Audio Flamingo
alayrac2022flamingo
) has demonstrated that employing language-aligned audio encoders significantly improves model performance. This enhancement is attributed to the initial alignment between audio representations and the language modality. Consequently, a common practice involves an additional phase of text-audio pre-training for audio encoders, which were originally trained via self-supervised or supervised methods (e.g., CAV-MAE
gong2022contrastive
in LTU, AST
gong2021ast
in GAMA), prior to their integration into the audio-LLM framework. However, this subsequent language alignment, while enhancing the integration with LLMs, may inadvertently diminish the rich, fine-grained representational capacity inherent in the original self-supervised pre-training. Studies such as Cambrian-1
tong2024cambrian
and LongVU
shen2024longvu
have explored this trade-off, suggesting that while language-aligned encoders excel at capturing dominant concepts, non-contrastive self-supervised learning objectives tend to preserve more detailed, fine-grained information.

To evaluate this hypothesis, we augmented the SSLAM encoder (audio encoder used in variants discussed in Section
2.1
,
2.2
,
2.3
) with additional audio encoders (Figure
2
(D)). Specifically, we incorporated LAION-CLAP-general
wu2023large
, LAION-CLAP-music and speech
wu2023large
. The latent token-based connector module enables us to integrate as many audio encoders as desired without increasing the LLM input tokens. These additional encoders are language-aligned, additionally pre-trained on extensive general audio, music, and speech datasets, respectively. The rationale for this ensemble is that the self-supervised SSLAM encoder provides rich, fine-grained semantic information. Concurrently, the CLAP encoders contribute robust, language-aligned representations specialized for their respective domains: general audio, music, and speech. This combination aims to equip the network with a more comprehensive and versatile understanding of diverse auditory inputs. The empirical results from our three-stage experimental setup (Section
4
) for this multi-encoder configuration are presented in Table
1
.

Finding 3

Multi-audio encoder ensembles broaden an LLM’s audio querying capabilities, enabling it to query a wide range of audio information. Employing a diverse ensemble of audio encoders, which combines self-supervised for fine-grained details, language-aligned for better language alignment, and domain-specific for areas like music and speech, provides richer, complementary representations. Notably, we demonstrate an efficient integration mechanism for such ensembles that avoids increasing the LLM’s input token count, leading to improved overall performance.

Connector module
: Our connector module consists of two main operations: cross-attention between latent tokens and audio encoder outputs, followed by projection of these latent tokens to the LLM’s hidden dimension
tong2024cambrian
;
shen2024longvu
. The connector initializes with a single latent token that is replicated into
n
tokens, where
n
is determined by the resolution of the audio encoder output. These replicated latent tokens perform cross-attention with output representations from multiple audio encoders while preserving time and frequency resolution information. Specifically, each latent token interacts with a designated region across all output encoders to gather information from that region, as illustrated in Figure
2
(C). Following cross-attention, these information-enriched latent tokens are projected to match the LLM’s hidden dimension. For experiments where audio information is integrated exclusively into the attention submodule of specific LLM layers (Sections
2.3
and
2.4
), we employ one connector to prepare audio inputs for each LLM layer where audio-LLM integration occurs. Our experiments demonstrate that this process can be further optimized with similar or improved performance by sharing the cross-attention modules across layers, which we discuss in detail in the supplementary material.

Discussion: Performance relative to prior art

While our primary contribution is a systematic investigation of audio-LLM architectural strategies and their impact on information transfer, we provide performance comparisons with prior systems in Table

This outcome highlights the significance of our architectural innovations, suggesting that optimized cross-modal information transfer can effectively compensate for reduced model scale and training resources.

Experimental setup

In this section, we define the standardized experimental setup used for our experiments. These components are used throughout this comparative study, ensuring fairness in the experiments. Additional training details are provided in Appendix
A
.

4.1
Training curriculum

We employ the three-stage pre-training and fine-tuning curriculum and data proposed by LTU
gong2024listen
.

Stage 1: Connector pre-training.
We begin by pre-training the newly added connector modules (i.e., any new layers introduced to connect the audio encoder with the LLM). In this stage, we utilize approximately 1.2 M audio question-answer data points focused on classification and acoustic descriptions. The LLM parameters are kept frozen to prevent catastrophic forgetting. The use of deterministic question-answer pairs trains the connector module to extract relevant information from the audio encoder outputs required by the LLM, while simultaneously preventing the LLM from generating lengthy or hallucinated responses.

Stage 2: Initial fine-tuning.
In this stage, all components, excluding the audio encoder, undergo fine-tuning. The training data is augmented with additional closed-ended question-answer pairs, bringing the total to 1.9 M such pairs.

Stage 3: Final fine-tuning.
In the final stage, we continue to fine-tune all components except the audio encoder. We augment the dataset with open-ended question-answer pairs, bringing the total dataset size to approximately 5.6 M examples.

4.2
Training data

We utilize OpenAQA-5M, the audio question-answer (QA) pair dataset proposed by LTU
gong2024listen
. This dataset, comprising approximately 5.6 million (audio, question, answer) tuples in our downloaded version, is sourced from publicly-available audio datasets. The QA pairs include closed-ended questions for fundamental audio perception (e.g., classification, acoustic characteristics, temporal information) and open-ended questions, generated using GPT-3.5. Further details regarding the dataset composition and generation are provided in Appendix
B
.

4.3
Evaluation strategy: audio Encoder to LLM information transfer

As previously described, our primary goal is to determine whether critical audio event information successfully transfers from the audio encoder to the LLM’s latent representation space. We posit that the audio-LLM’s performance on downstream classification tasks effectively indicates the quality of this information transfer. Following LTU’s evaluation framework
gong2024listen
, we implement two complementary assessment methods: 1) for classification datasets, we measure semantic similarity by encoding both the model’s text outputs and target audio event labels using gpt-text-embedding-ada; and 2) for captioning capabilities, we employ standard audio captioning datasets and evaluate generated descriptions using established metrics including CIDEr and SPICE.

To ensure reproducibility and determinism during inference, we employ greedy decoding. This deterministic approach selects the most probable token at each step, contrasting with stochastic sampling strategies (e.g., top-
k
or top-
p
) utilized in the evaluation of previous works such as LTU and GAMA.

4.4
LLM

We used Llama-3.2 1B
grattafiori2024llama
for all experiments in the analysis of architectural design hypotheses (Section
2

5
Literature review

Audio-LLM architectures
:
Architectures for audio-LLMs primarily diverge into two main paradigms for integrating audio information with pre-trained LLMs. The first, often referred to as the Pengi
deshmukh2023pengi
or LLaVA
liu2023visual
style, is adopted by models such as Pengi, LTU
gong2024listen
, SALMONN
tang2024salmonn
, and GAMA
ghosh2024gama
. In these systems, audio embeddings, derived from an audio encoder, are projected into the LLM’s token embedding space. These audio tokens are then typically prepended to the text tokens and processed jointly through the LLM’s layers. This approach treats audio information akin to additional text, subjecting it to both the attention and FFN submodules within each LLM layer. The implications and necessity of processing audio representations through FFNs, which are primarily trained for linguistic patterns, warrant further investigation.

An alternative approach is inspired by the Flamingo architecture
alayrac2022flamingo
, as seen in models like Audio-Flamingo
kong2024audio
. This paradigm introduces new, dedicated cross-modal layers, comprising cross-attention and FFN blocks, interleaved between the existing LLM layers. Within these inserted layers, text tokens from a given LLM layer
l
perform cross-attention to the audio tokens before the text representations proceed to the subsequent LLM layer
l+1
. While this allows for explicit and gated interaction with audio features, it necessitates the addition of new FFN and cross-attention modules, leading to a considerable increase in model parameters and computational overhead.

Instruction tuning and reasoning capabilities in audio-LLMs
:
Beyond architectural design, a significant research thrust aims to enhance audio-LLM instruction-following and reasoning. This involves creating specialized datasets and benchmarks for rigorous evaluation. For instance, works like

;

;
deshmukh2025adiff
focus on evaluation metrics and datasets for audio reasoning, while

;
ghosh2025audio
contribute high-quality instruction tuning datasets. Training existing audio-LLM architectures on these datasets scales their capabilities for complex reasoning and nuanced instruction adherence.

Limitations and observations

Our work focuses on enhancing the LLM’s ability to extract relevant information from audio representations to satisfy textual queries. Accordingly, our evaluation methodology primarily included audio event classification tasks to assess whether the LLM could accurately capture audio events and temporal information from audio representations. Additionally, we incorporated audio captioning tasks to evaluate descriptive capabilities.

While our evaluations did not extend to complex reasoning abilities or other advanced tasks that typically require substantial, specialized instruction tuning, we supplemented our quantitative benchmarks with a qualitative analysis comparing our approach to previous works. This analysis provides additional insight, suggesting that our model achieves comparable or superior quality scores on the examined samples. Sample outputs with corresponding qualitative evaluations are presented in Appendix
D
. These encouraging results highlight the potential for targeted instruction tuning to achieve further substantial gains when leveraging our foundational improvements to audio-text information transfer.

7
Conclusion

In this work, we conducted a systematic investigation into the architectural mechanisms governing efficient information transfer from audio encoders to LLMs, conceptualizing effective interaction as the LLM’s ability to proficiently probe audio representations. Guided by hypotheses derived from mechanistic interpretability and LLM operational principles, we systematically evaluated several novel audio-LLM integration strategies.

Our findings validate three key architectural insights: (1) delaying audio integration allows LLMs to first establish crucial textual context, enhancing their ability to query audio information effectively; (2) processing audio representations exclusively through the LLM’s attention submodules, without propagation to FFNs, is a sufficient and efficient mechanism for cross-modal concept activation; and (3) employing a diverse ensemble of audio encoders significantly broadens the LLM’s capacity to probe a wider variety of audio information. These insights were consistently supported by empirical evidence from a rigorous three-stage training curriculum on a publicly-available dataset, ensuring fair and controlled comparisons.

The culmination of these findings is a novel audio-LLM architecture (PAL) that, by incorporating these principled design choices, demonstrates substantial relative performance improvements ranging from 10% to 60% over a standard baseline. This study not only provides actionable architectural recommendations for building more effective audio-LLMs but also contributes to a deeper understanding of how to optimize cross-modal information flow in such systems. The insights gained here lay a strong foundation for future work aimed at developing audio-LLMs with even more advanced capabilities in understanding complex acoustic environments and engaging in sophisticated audio-grounded reasoning.
