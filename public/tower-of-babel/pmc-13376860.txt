LTBP4 deficiency inhibits NLRP3 inflammasome activation in cardiomyocytes and attenuates heart failure in male mice
Authors: Siyu Ma, Nan Jiang, Zheng Zuo, Zhipeng Lian, Jian Wu, Jinghua Ma, Xiangxiang Wei, Yunquan He, Qi Pan, Jiayi Lin, Yongbo Li, Yannan Hou, Xiuling Zhi, Xiaobo Li, Elena Osto, Yuxiang Dai, Jun Li, Jieyu Guo, Dan Meng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLatent transforming growth factor β-binding protein 4 (LTBP4) has been reported to be associated with heart failure (HF), but its role in HF remains unclear. We observe increased LTBP4 expression in plasma and cardiomyocytes of HF patients, and in a male mouse HF model induced by transverse aortic constriction (TAC). Cardiomyocyte-specific Ltbp4 deficiency attenuates NLRP3 inflammasome activation, cardiac dysfunction, and fibrosis post-TAC. Mechanistically, pressure overload upregulates LTBP4 partially via the transcription factor SP1. Angiotensin II promotes the recruitment of intracellular LTBP4 to the microtubule-organizing center (MTOC) via dynein. Subsequently, LTBP4 facilitates the dynein-mediated NLRP3 translocation to the MTOC and promotes NLRP3-NEK7 interaction, thereby driving NLRP3 inflammasome activation. Additionally, LTBP4 upregulates NLRP3 transcription and correlates positively with NLRP3 and interleukin-1β in HF patients. Here we show that LTBP4 is an important regulator of the NLRP3-NEK7 interaction and NLRP3 inflammasome activation in cardiomyocytes, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target for HF.Subject terms: Heart failure, Inflammasome

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) represents the terminal stage of cardiovascular disease, characterized by elevated intracardiac pressures and the loss of cardiomyocytes due to cell death1,2. The activation of the inflammation plays a vital role in HF progresses3. Understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying inflammation in HF is crucial for identifying potential therapeutic targets to improve patient outcomes. During pressure overload, signals originating from stressed but viable cardiomyocytes initiate an inflammatory cascade4. However, the complex regulation of inflammatory signaling in cardiomyocytes during HF remains incompletely understood.

The NACHT, leucine-rich repeat (LRR), and pyrin domain (PYD)-containing protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome is a cytosolic protein complex that assembles in response to exogenous pathogenic invasions and endogenous cellular damage5. Pro-inflammatory cytokines, along with damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), or pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), activate Nuclear Factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB), promoting the transcription of NLRP3 and pro-interleukin-1β (IL-1β)6–8. Subsequently, NLRP3, apoptosis-associated speck-like protein containing CARD (ASC), and pro-caspase-1(CASP1) come together to form the NLRP3 inflammasome. This assembly triggers the cleavage of pro-CASP1 into active CASP1, facilitating the maturation and release of the inflammatory cytokines IL-1β and interleukin-18 (IL-18)9–11. Notably, the binding of NLRP3 to NIMA-related kinase 7 (NEK7) at the microtubule-organizing center (MTOC) is a critical step in the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome12,13. The translocation of NLRP3 to the MTOC is mediated by dynein together with histone deacetylase 6 (HDAC6)14. However, the precise mechanisms by which NLRP3 responds to stimuli and initiates the assembly of the NLRP3 inflammasome remain incompletely understood.

The NLRP3 inflammasome is activated under various pathological conditions and plays a crucial role in its pathogenesis5. NLRP3 deficiency has been shown to alleviate myocardial pyroptosis and maintain cardiac function under pressure overload11. Furthermore, the administration of selective NLRP3 inflammasome inhibitors has been shown to prevent the development of HF caused by hematopoietic or myeloid Tet2 deficiency15. Beyond inflammatory cells, the inflammasome in cardiac cells can initiate local inflammation through intercellular communication. Specifically, pressure overload in the left ventricle activates sympathetic efferent nerves (SEN), promoting extracellular ATP secretion, further stimulating the P2X7 purinergic receptor in cardiomyocytes, and activating the NLRP3 inflammasome16. Nevertheless, the precise mechanisms by which the NLRP3 inflammasome is activated in cardiomyocytes in HF remain not fully clarified17.

Latent transforming growth factor β-binding protein 4 (LTBP4), a member of the LTBP family, is an important component of the extracellular matrix18. LTBP4 consists of two primary isoforms: the short isoform (LTBP4S) and the long isoform (LTBP4L), arising from the utilization of different promoters19. Ltbp4 global (Ltbp4s and Ltbp4l) knock out mice postnatally died, whereas Ltbp4s global knock out mice survived to adulthood20. LTBP4 is essential for the proper folding and secretion of transforming growth factor β1 (TGF-β1)21. Additionally, LTBP4 plays a critical role in regulating the deposition of elastin onto extracellular matrix microfibrils by binding to fibulin-4 and fibulin-522–24. Coronary artery disease (CAD) risk gene ADAM metallopeptidase with thrombospondin type 1 motif 7 (ADAMTS7) can influence elastogenesis in the cardiovascular system by cleaving LTBP425,26. Moreover, LTBP4, in a panel of three genes (including also tenascin XB (TNXB) and transforming growth factor beta receptor 1 (TGFBR1)), has the potential to enhance the prediction of heart failure following myocardial infarction27. Furthermore, the LTBP4 rs10880 T/T genotype significantly delays the onset of dilated cardiomyopathy in patients with Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) undergoing steroid treatment28. However, whether LTBP4 contributes to HF progression remains unclear.

In the present study, we analyzed the expression levels of LTBP4 in left ventricular and plasma samples from Heart Failure with Reduced Ejection Fraction (HFrEF) patients and healthy individuals. Additionally, we generated tamoxifen-induced cardiomyocyte-specific Ltbp4 (Ltbp4s and Ltbp4l) knock-out mice to study the impact of Ltbp4 deficiency on HF using a transverse aortic constriction (TAC) model. We aimed to explore the role of LTBP4 in inflammasome activation, cardiac function, and fibrosis during HF, and to elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms.

Results

LTBP4 expression is increased in cardiomyocytes of HF patients and mice, and in AngⅡ- or PE-stimulated NRCMs

To investigate whether LTBP4 is involved in the progression of HF, we examined the expression of LTBP4 protein in the ventricles of HFrEF patients and healthy controls. Our results showed a significant increase in LTBP4 protein levels in the hearts of HFrEF patients compared to healthy individuals (Fig. 1A). We then collected plasma samples from 22 HFrEF patients and 25 healthy controls (Table S1) and assessed LTBP4 levels using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). The results revealed a significant elevation in plasma LTBP4 levels in HFrEF patients compared to healthy individuals (Fig. 1B). Consistently, quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) analysis (Fig. S1A) and Western blot analysis (Fig. 1C) revealed that mRNA and protein levels of LTBP4 in the left ventricles of TAC-induced HF mice were significantly higher than those in control mice.

We next analyzed a single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) dataset from the hearts of human patients with idiopathic dilated cardiomyopathy and healthy donors (GEO accession no. GSE270788)29. We identified 12 cell types, including cardiomyocytes, endothelial cells, fibroblasts, glias, macrophages, smooth muscle cells, pericytes, mast cells, T cells, and adipocytes, based on their specific markers (Fig. S1B). Notably, we observed that cardiomyocytes 1 cluster was associated with the apoptotic process and inflammatory response (Fig. S1C). Furthermore, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Gene Ontology (GO) biological process analyses indicated that the cardiomyocyte cluster 1 in the HF group was associated with adrenergic and cGMP-PKG signaling pathways, calcium overload, and increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) (Fig. S1D, E). Importantly, we also observed elevated LTBP4 expression in cardiomyocytes 1 cluster (Fig. S1F). In line with these findings, immunofluorescence staining showed that LTBP4 protein was significantly upregulated in cardiomyocytes of HFrEF patients’ hearts and failing hearts in TAC mice (Fig. 1D, E). Immunofluorescence analysis showed a slight, nonsignificant increase in LTBP4 expression in cardiac fibroblasts of TAC mice compared to sham controls (Fig. S1G).

Then, we employed Angiotensin II (AngII) and Phenylephrine (PE) to stimulate neonatal rat cardiomyocytes (NRCMs) and neonatal rat cardiac fibroblasts (NRCFs) to mimic the pathophysiological conditions associated with TAC-induced cardiac stress. NRCMs exhibited a dose-dependent increase in LTBP4 with AngII or PE treatment (Figs. 1F and S1H), whereas NRCFs showed no significant change (Fig. S1I, J). Collectively, these data suggest that LTBP4 expression is elevated in the myocardial cells and plasma of HFrEF patients, in the hearts of TAC-induced HF mice, as well as in AngII or PE-stimulated NRCMs.

SP1 partially mediates pressure overload-induced LTBP4 transcription

To elucidate the mechanisms underlying the increased expression of LTBP4 in TAC-induced HF mice and AngII-stimulated cardiomyocytes, we performed a motif analysis using the JASPAR database. We discovered that the promoter region of LTBP4 contained a Specificity Protein 1 (SP1) binding motif. To investigate the role of SP1 in AngII-induced LTBP4 expression, we employed SP1-specific siRNA knockdown experiments. Our findings demonstrated that AngII stimulation upregulated both LTBP4 and SP1 expression, while knockdown of SP1 significantly attenuated the AngII-induced expression of LTBP4 in NRCMs (Fig. 1G, H). We further found increased protein levels of SP1 in TAC-induced HF mice (Fig. 1C), which exhibited a similar pattern to that of LTBP4. To further determine whether SP1 contributes to TAC-induced elevation of LTBP4 in mouse hearts, we administered the specific SP1 inhibitor Plicamycin30. Notably, we found that the pressure overload-induced elevation of LTBP4 in mouse hearts was suppressed by SP1 inhibition (Fig. 1I). Taken together, our results confirm that the upregulation of LTBP4 induced by cardiac pressure overload in the heart of mice is partially mediated by SP1.

LTBP4 deficiency in cardiomyocytes alleviates heart failure in the TAC mouse model

To explore the impact of LTBP4 in cardiomyocytes on the progression of HF, we generated the cardiomyocyte-specific Ltbp4 inducible knockout mice (Ltbp4cko) by crossing transgenic mice that express Cre recombinase specifically in cardiomyocytes (Myh6-CreERT2) mice with Ltbp4flox/flox (Ltbp4fl/fl) mice. The Ltbp4fl/fl mice were created by flanking exon 2–4 of Ltbp4 with loxP sites (Fig. S2A, B). To confirm the cardiomyocyte-specific deletion of Ltbp4, we isolated ventricular cardiomyocytes from 7-day-old Ltbp4cko and their Ltbp4fl/fl littermate controls. Immunofluorescence staining and Western blot analysis showed that LTBP4 expression was specifically ablated in the cardiomyocytes of Ltbp4cko mice (Figs. 2A and S2C). Additionally, Ltbp4cko mice and Ltbp4fl/fl littermate controls showed no significant differences in cardiac function and fibrosis under baseline conditions (Figs. S2D and 2E–H).

Next, we performed TAC surgery to induce HF in 10-12-week-old male Ltbp4cko and Ltbp4fl/fl littermate mice following tamoxifen administration (Fig. 2B). Four weeks after TAC, Ltbp4fl/fl mice exhibited significant cardiac dysfunction (Fig. 2E), along with increased expression levels of HF markers [atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP) and brain natriuretic peptide (BNP)] (Fig. S3B), confirming the success of the HF model. The cardiomyocyte-specific deletion of Ltbp4 in the hearts of Ltbp4cko mice was next validated by immunofluorescent staining (Fig. 2C) and Western blot analysis (Fig. S2E) using murine hearts collected 4 weeks after TAC. ELISA assays showed that plasma LTBP4 levels were significantly reduced in Ltbp4cko mice compared to Ltbp4fl/fl mice post-TAC (Fig. 2D). Subsequently, Ltbp4cko mice exhibited a significant improvement in cardiac function, as indicated by significantly increased left ventricular ejection fraction (EF %) and fractional shortening (FS %) (Fig. 2E), and significantly reduced left ventricular end-diastolic diameter (LVEDD) and left ventricular end-systolic diameter (LVESD) (Fig. S2F), compared to Ltbp4fl/fl mice after TAC. However, there were no significant differences in the heart-to-body weight ratio (HW/BW) or the heart weight to tibial length ratio (HW/TL) between Ltbp4cko and Ltbp4fl/fl mice post-TAC (Fig. S2G, H).

Next, we evaluated the impact of Ltbp4 deficiency on cardiac fibrosis, a pathological feature commonly associated with HF. Masson’s trichrome staining (Fig. 2F) and Picrosirius red staining (Fig. 2G) demonstrated that fibrosis was significantly reduced in the hearts of Ltbp4cko mice compared to Ltbp4fl/fl littermate mice 4 weeks after TAC. Consistently, Ltbp4cko mice showed the decreased expression of Periostin (POSTN), a well-recognized marker for fibrosis31 and cardiac remodeling32 compared to Ltbp4fl/fl littermate mice 4 weeks post-TAC (Fig. 2H). qRT-PCR analyses further revealed that the expression of fibrosis markers, including Collagen Type I Alpha 1 Chain (Col1a1), and Collagen Type III Alpha 1 Chain (Col3a1), were significantly lower in the left ventricles of Ltbp4cko mice compared to Ltbp4fl/fl littermate mice 4 weeks after TAC (Fig. 2I).

Additionally, we extended the duration of the TAC-induced HF model to 6 weeks (Fig. S3A). The expression levels of ANP and BNP were significantly elevated in Ltbp4fl/fl mice 6 weeks post-TAC (Fig. S3B). Furthermore, Ltbp4cko mice exhibited significant improvements in cardiac contractile function (Fig. S3C), as well as a substantial reduction in myocardial fibrosis compared to their Ltbp4fl/fl littermates 6 weeks post-TAC (Fig. S3D, E). Together, these findings suggest that LTBP4 cardiomyocyte deficiency plays a protective role in cardiac function during the progression of HF.

LTBP4 deficiency in cardiomyocytes inhibits the NLRP3 inflammasome signaling pathway in failing hearts

To elucidate the potential mechanisms through which LTBP4 cardiomyocyte deficiency protects against cardiac dysfunction induced by TAC, we performed RNA sequencing to analyze the transcriptomic profiles of the hearts of Ltbp4cko and Ltbp4fl/fl mice following TAC. Our analysis revealed 376 upregulated genes and 488 downregulated genes in the Ltbp4cko-TAC group compared to the Ltbp4fl/fl-TAC group (fold change >1.5, p < 0.05; Fig. 3A). GO biological process analysis showed that the downregulated genes in Ltbp4cko-TAC group were notably enriched in processes related to immune response, inflammatory response, and interleukin-1 beta production, etc. (Fig. 3B). In particular, the expression of genes associated with the NLRP3 inflammasome signaling pathway, such as Nlrp3, Casp1, Il-1β, and Il-18, were significantly reduced in the failing hearts of Ltbp4cko mice compared to those from the Ltbp4fl/fl mice (Fig. 3C).

To validate this finding, we measured the plasma levels of IL-1β and the mRNA levels of Casp1, Il-1β, Il-18, and Nlrp3 in the left ventricles of Ltbp4cko mice and Ltbp4fl/fl control mice with or without TAC-induced HF. In the absence of TAC surgery, no significant differences were observed between Ltbp4cko mice and Ltbp4fl/fl littermate controls in the plasma levels of IL-1β or the mRNA levels of NLRP3 inflammasome-related genes (Fig. 3D, E). ELISA measurements showed that the plasma levels of IL-1β were significantly reduced in Ltbp4cko mice compared to Ltbp4fl/fl mice following TAC (Fig. 3D). Consistent with this, qRT-PCR analysis revealed decreased expression levels of Casp1, Il-1β, Il-18, and Nlrp3 in the left ventricles of Ltbp4cko mice following TAC (Figs. 3E and S3E). Western blot analysis revealed increased protein levels of NLRP3 and Cleaved-CASP1 (p20) in adult mouse hearts after TAC, exhibiting a pattern similar to that of LTBP4 (Fig. S4A). More importantly, the elevations in NLRP3, Cleaved-CASP1, and the fibrosis markers (COL1A1, α-SMA) were significantly attenuated in Ltbp4cko mice compared to Ltbp4fl/fl mice post-TAC (Fig. 3F). Additionally, immunofluorescent staining for CD45 revealed that cardiomyocyte-specific Ltbp4 deficiency in mice led to a significant reduction in inflammatory cell infiltration following TAC (Fig. 3G). Taken together, the deficiency of LTBP4 in cardiomyocytes attenuated the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome signaling pathway following TAC.

LTBP4 activates the NLRP3 inflammasome signaling pathway and exacerbates cell death in cardiomyocytes

To investigate whether LTBP4 specifically affects the NLRP3 inflammasome signaling pathway in cardiomyocytes rather than cardiac fibroblasts, we utilized AngII or PE stimulation in NRCMs and NRCFs. We found that in NRCMs, the expression of NLRP3, Cleaved-CASP1, and COL1A1 was upregulated in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. S4B, C), similar to the expression pattern of LTBP4. However, NRCFs exhibited no significant alterations in NLRP3 and Cleaved-CASP1 levels with AngII or PE treatment (Fig. S4D, E). Additionally, NRCMs transfected with adenovirus-driven LTBP4-specific shRNAs (Ad-shLtbp4) showed a significant attenuation of AngII- or PE-induced elevations in NLRP3 and Cleaved-CASP1 levels compared to adenovirus-control shRNAs (Ad-shNC) (Figs. 3H and S5A). However, LTBP4 silencing did not affect NLRP3 and Cleaved-CASP1 levels in NRCFs, regardless of basal conditions or AngII stimulation (Fig. S5B). Collectively, these results suggest that LTBP4 silencing selectively influences the NLRP3 inflammasome signaling pathway in cardiomyocytes.

We next employed hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂), a known trigger for NLRP3 inflammasome activation33, to investigate the impact of LTBP4 on the NLRP3 inflammasome signaling pathway. The protein levels of NLRP3 and Cleaved-CASP1 were elevated following H2O2 treatment, but this increase was attenuated by the knockdown of LTBP4 in the rat cardiomyocyte cell line H9C2 (Fig. 4A). Conversely, compared to the adenovirus-mediated negative control (Ad-NC), adenovirus-mediated overexpression of LTBP4 (Ad-LTBP4) led to increased protein levels of NLRP3 and Cleaved-CASP1 under H₂O₂ treatment (Fig. 4B). To investigate whether LTBP4 affects NLRP3 protein stability, we conducted cycloheximide (CHX) assays in NRCMs. The findings showed no significant difference in the half-life of NLRP3 protein between cells overexpressing LTBP4 and the negative control, suggesting LTBP4 does not substantially influence NLRP3 stability (Fig. S6A). Furthermore, qRT-PCR analysis confirmed that LTBP4 overexpression upregulated Nlrp3 mRNA levels (Fig. S6B), indicating that LTBP4 increases NLRP3 levels through transcriptional regulation rather than protein stability.

Given the evidence of NLRP3 inflammasome in promoting cell death34, we investigated the potential involvement of LTBP4 in NLRP3-mediated cardiomyocyte death using Calcein AM staining and LDH release in NRCMs, both with and without H₂O₂ treatment. Our results indicated that H₂O₂-induced cell death was significantly attenuated by knockdown of Ltbp4 (Fig. 4C, D). In contrast, overexpression of LTBP4 exacerbated cell death in H₂O₂-treated NRCMs (Fig. 4E, F). These findings suggest that LTBP4 promotes the NLRP3 inflammasome signaling pathway and exacerbates cell death in cardiomyocytes exposed to H₂O₂ treatment.

To further ascertain whether NLRP3 acts as a downstream effector of LTBP4, we employed the specific NLRP3 inhibitor MCC95035 to suppress its activity. Western blot analysis revealed that MCC950 significantly diminished AngII-induced NLRP3 activation, as evidenced by decreased levels of cleaved-CASP1 (Fig. 4G). MCC950 also reduced the upregulation of both proteins caused by LTBP4 overexpression in NRCMs after AngII stimulation (Fig. 4G). Moreover, MCC950 significantly attenuated LTBP4 overexpression-induced exacerbation of H₂O₂-mediated cell death in NRCMs (Figs. S6C and 4H). Overall, those results suggest that NLRP3 serves as a critical downstream mediator of LTBP4 in the inflammatory pathway and the process of cell death.

Intracellular LTBP4 activates the NLRP3 inflammasome signaling pathway in cardiomyocytes

To determine whether LTBP4 activates the NLRP3 inflammasome pathway through its extracellular or intracellular form in cardiomyocytes, we generated a signal peptide-deficient LTBP4 adenovirus (Ad-LTBP4Δ2-27aa), which prevents targeting to secretory vesicles and confines its functional activity to intracellular compartments (Fig. 5A). Supernatants from HEK293T cells expressing full-length LTBP4 showed LTBP4 presence, while those from Ad-LTBP4Δ2-27aa-infected cells had minimal LTBP4 (Fig. S6D), suggesting LTBP4Δ2-27aa is intracellular. Notably, Western blot analysis indicated that LTBP4Δ2-27aa increased NLRP3 and Cleaved-CASP1 expression similarly to full-length LTBP4 in NRCMs, under both basal and AngII-stimulated conditions (Fig. 5B, C). Furthermore, LTBP4Δ2-27aa significantly increased H₂O₂-induced cell death, like wild-type LTBP4 in NRCMs (Fig. 5D, E). These findings suggest that intracellular LTBP4 primarily drives NLRP3 inflammasome signaling activation and cell death in cardiomyocytes.

LTBP4 interacts with NLRP3 and NEK7

To elucidate the molecular mechanism by which LTBP4 modulates the NLRP3 signaling pathway, we performed bioinformatics analyses utilizing the BioGRID database36, which revealed an interaction between LTBP4 and NLRP3. This interaction was further confirmed through coimmunoprecipitation (co-IP) assays, including those with overexpressed LTBP4-FLAG and NLRP3-MYC in HEK293T cells (Fig. S7A), LTBP4-FLAG and endogenous NLRP3 in H9C2 cells (Fig. 6A), as well as endogenous LTBP4 and endogenous NLRP3 in H9C2 cells (Fig. 6B). We conducted glutathione S-transferase (GST) pull-down assays to verify a direct interaction between LTBP4 and NLRP3, which showed that GST-tagged NLRP3 directly interacted with FLAG-tagged LTBP4 in HEK293T cell lysates (Fig. 6C). Furthermore, LTBP4-FLAG was co-transfected with NLRP3-MYC in U2OS cells, and immunofluorescence staining revealed colocalization of LTBP4-FLAG with NLRP3-MYC (Fig. 6D). Additionally, immunofluorescence staining revealed an increase in the interaction between endogenous NLRP3 and endogenous LTBP4 in H9C2 cells following H₂O₂ treatment, compared to the non-H₂O₂-treated control (Fig. 6E).

To explore the mechanism by which LTBP4 overexpression increases the activation of NLRP3 inflammasome, we conducted immunoprecipitation-mass spectrometry in NRCMs transfected with Ad-LTBP4. The mass spectrometry-based proteomics analysis identified NEK7 as one of the interacting proteins with LTBP4 (Fig. S7B). The interaction between LTBP4 and NEK7 was further confirmed by co-IP assays involving the co-transfection of LTBP4-FLAG and NEK7-HA in HEK293T cells (Fig. 6F), as well as LTBP4-FLAG and endogenous NEK7 in H9C2 cells (Fig. 6G). Furthermore, LTBP4-FLAG was co-transfected with NEK7-HA in U2OS cells, and immunofluorescence staining revealed colocalization of LTBP4-FLAG with NEK7-HA (Fig. S7C). Consistently, the interaction between LTBP4 and NEK7 was also increased in cardiac tissues from mice after TAC, compared to sham-operated controls (Fig. 6H).

We further employed AngII stimulation in cardiomyocytes to mimic in vivo cardiac stress responses following TAC. Our results showed that LTBP4 colocalized to the MTOC and enhanced its interaction with NEK7 at this site upon AngII stimulation in NRCMs (Fig. 6I). To investigate the mechanism by which AngII induces changes in the localization of LTBP4, we conducted bioinformatics analyses using the BioGRID database. These analyses identified an interaction between LTBP4 and dynein light chain Tctex-type 1 (DYNLT1)37, which is a subunit of cytoplasmic dynein38. Dynein is known to facilitate the transport of the NLRP3 inflammasome to the MTOC and is essential for the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome14. The LTBP4 and DYNLT1 interaction was verified through co-IP assays in AngII-stimulated NRCMs (Fig. 6J). To further determine whether AngII stimulation promotes the localization of LTBP4 to the MTOC via dynein in NRCMs, we treated NRCMs with Ciliobrevin A (CilioA), a dynein inhibitor39, to suppress dynein function. Immunofluorescence staining showed that AngII stimulation enhanced LTBP4 localization to the MTOC in NRCMs, whereas CilioA treatment reduced the colocalization of LTBP4 at the MTOC upon AngII stimulation (Fig. 6K). These findings collectively suggest that the translocation of LTBP4 to the MTOC upon AngII stimulation is partially mediated by dynein.

LTBP4 promotes the interaction between NLRP3 and NEK7

It is well-established that NLRP3 binds to NEK7 to form oligomers, which in turn activate downstream inflammatory pathways40, and we have confirmed that LTBP4 can interact with both NLRP3 and NEK7. To further investigate whether LTBP4 influences the interaction between NLRP3 and NEK7, we performed co-IP assays utilizing overexpressed LTBP4-FLAG, NLRP3-MYC, and NEK7-HA in HEK293T cells. The results indicated that the overexpression of LTBP4 significantly increased the interaction between NEK7 and NLRP3 (Fig. 7A, B). Consistent with these findings, immunofluorescence staining revealed that overexpression of LTBP4 enhanced the interaction between NLRP3 and NEK7 in U2OS cells (Fig. S7D). Additionally, co-IP assays showed that cardiomyocyte-specific Ltbp4 deficiency significantly reduced the interaction between NLRP3 and NEK7 in the hearts of Ltbp4cko mice compared to Ltbp4fl/fl mice following TAC (Fig. 7C). Given that dynein mediates the transport of NLRP3 to the MTOC, thereby binding to NEK713, we further investigated whether LTBP4 overexpression facilitates the translocation of NLRP3 to the MTOC through a dynein-dependent mechanism. Immunofluorescence staining revealed that LTBP4 overexpression enhanced the translocation of NLRP3 to the MTOC in NRCMs. However, this increased translocation was attenuated by the application of CilioA (Fig. 7D). Collectively, these findings suggest that LTBP4 overexpression promotes the recruitment of NLRP3 to the MTOC by dynein. To explore LTBP4’s role in NLRP3 inflammasome formation, we infected NRCMs with Ad-NC or Ad-LTBP4. co-IP assays indicated that LTBP4 overexpression significantly enhanced the interaction between NLRP3 and Pro-CASP1 (Fig. 7E), confirming that LTBP4 facilitates the NLRP3 inflammasome assembly.

To further investigate the role of the NEK7-LTBP4 interaction in the NLRP3 inflammatory signaling pathway, we conducted molecular docking analyses, which indicated that the C-terminal region of the LTBP4 protein interacted with both proteins (Fig. S7E). To further delineate the exact regions on LTBP4 that bind NLRP3 and NEK7, we performed detailed truncation experiments. We transfected HEK293T cells with plasmids expressing various fragment lengths of LTBP4 and NEK7-HA. Notably, the truncations LTBP4Δ1326-1624aa (Δ1), LTBP4Δ1403-1624aa (Δ2), and LTBP4Δ1574-1624aa (Δ3) failed to interact with NEK7 (Fig. 7F), suggesting that 1574-1624 amino acids of LTBP4 is critical for providing a binding cavity for NEK7. To examine whether the amino acids 1574-1624 in LTBP4 facilitate the NLRP3 inflammasome signaling pathway, we generated an adenovirus variant of LTBP4 lacking these specific amino acids (Ad-LTBP4Δ3) and subsequently investigated its impact. Western blot analysis revealed that LTBP4Δ3 was unable to upregulate the expression of Cleaved-CASP1 to the same extent as wild-type LTBP4 in H9C2 cells exposed to H₂O₂ treatment (Fig. 7G). Additionally, LTBP4Δ3 did not exacerbate cell death as significantly as wild-type LTBP4 in H₂O₂-treated NRCMs (Fig. 7H). These results suggest that the region encompassing 1574-1624 amino acids is critical for LTBP4’s modulation of the NLRP3 inflammasome signaling pathway. To address why LTBP4 has differential effects among cell types, we isolated cardiomyocytes, cardiac fibroblasts, and endothelial cells from adult mouse hearts. We found that NEK7 was highly expressed in cardiomyocytes, whereas its expression in cardiac fibroblasts was markedly lower than that in cardiomyocytes (Fig. S7F). Therefore, these findings highlight that the binding of LTBP4 to NEK7 is essential for LTBP4 to enhance NLRP3 inflammasome activation in cardiomyocytes.

LTBP4 is positively correlated with NLRP3 and IL-1β in HFrEF patients

To further explore the relationship between NLRP3 and LTBP4, we performed immunofluorescent staining in heart tissue samples from HFrEF patients and in a murine model of HF induced by TAC. Our results showed that both LTBP4 and NLRP3 were highly expressed and colocalized in cardiomyocytes from both HFrEF patients and the murine model of HF (Figs. 8A and S7G). Moreover, a significant positive correlation was observed between plasma levels of LTBP4 and IL-1β in patients with HFrEF (Fig. 8B). In addition, we found a positive correlation between LTBP4 and NLRP3 expression in left ventricles of patients with HF (GEO accession no. GSE84796)41 (Fig. 8C). Together, LTBP4 was colocalized with NLRP3 in both human and murine failing heart tissues, and LTBP4 expression was positively correlated with NLRP3 and IL-1β levels in HF patients.

Discussion

LTBP4 has been reported to be associated with HF following myocardial infarction and to exhibit a negative correlation with EF42. However, the specific role of LTBP4 in HF remains poorly understood. Our study demonstrates that LTBP4 deficiency in cardiomyocytes inhibits NLRP3 inflammasome activation and alleviates pathological heart remodeling in murine models of TAC-induced HF. Mechanistically, AngⅡ stimulation promotes the recruitment of intracellular LTBP4 to the MTOC via dynein. Subsequently, intracellular LTBP4 facilitates the dynein-mediated translocation of NLRP3 to MTOC. This promotes the interaction between NLRP3 and NEK7, thereby driving NLRP3 inflammasome activation in cardiomyocytes. Additionally, LTBP4 upregulates the transcriptional expression of NLRP3 (Fig. 8D). Our findings identify LTBP4 as a novel regulator of the NLRP3-NEK7 interaction and NLRP3 inflammasome activation in HF.

LTBP4 is abundantly expressed in the heart and skeletal muscle, with its expression being induced during myoblast differentiation and muscle regeneration following injury43. Consistent with these observations, we found that the expression of LTBP4 was elevated in cardiomyocytes from both HF patients and mice. Furthermore, stimulation with AngII, PE, or H2O2 increased LTBP4 expression in NRCMs. We hypothesize that this upregulation in cardiomyocytes is likely driven by elevated epinephrine levels and increased ROS production following heart failure. Notably, knockdown of transcription factor SP1 significantly attenuated the pressure overload-induced elevation of LTBP4, indicating that the upregulation of LTBP4 may be partially mediated by SP1.

Additionally, elevated plasma levels of LTBP4 have been associated with vascular dysfunction in the abdominal aortic aneurysm and with an aggressive phenotype in patients with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF)44,45. Our findings indicate that plasma levels of LTBP4 are significantly elevated in individuals with HFrEF and exhibit a positive correlation with IL-1β, suggesting that LTBP4 may serve as a biomarker for HF. Moreover, plasma LTBP4 levels were markedly lower in Ltbp4cko mice compared to Ltbp4fl/fl mice post-TAC. Our data indicate that cardiomyocytes are a major source of elevated plasma LTBP4 following TAC and is associated with inflammatory processes.

Recent studies have shown that NLRP3 inflammasome is activated by pressure overload in murine models of HF16,46,47. However, the precise mechanisms underlying NLRP3 inflammasome activation following TAC remain unknown. Our study demonstrates that LTBP4 deficiency in cardiomyocytes attenuates inflammasome activation and pathological fibrosis in failing hearts in male mice, suggesting that LTBP4 may serve as an important mediator linking pressure overload to NLRP3 inflammasome activation. Notably, LTBP4 lacking a signal peptide activates the NLRP3 pathway similarly to wild-type LTBP4, suggesting that intracellular LTBP4 in cardiomyocytes drives NLRP3 inflammasome activation rather than exogenously secreted LTBP4.

Accumulating evidence suggests that the binding of NLRP3 to NEK7 is essential for NLRP3 oligomerization and inflammasome activation12,48–50. While dynein is known to facilitate the transport of NLRP3 to the MTOC for subsequent NEK7 binding14, it remained unclear whether this dynein-mediated translocation occurs in cardiomyocytes. In this study, we identify LTBP4 as a regulator of the NLRP3–NEK7 interaction in cardiomyocytes. Our results demonstrate that LTBP4 interacts with dynein and promotes dynein-dependent translocation of NLRP3 to the MTOC in cardiomyocytes. Furthermore, MCC950, a well-known NLRP3 inhibitor, significantly attenuated the upregulation of NLRP3 mediated by LTBP4 overexpression in NRCMs, suggesting the upregulation of NLRP3 induced by LTBP4 might be due to the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome.

Previous studies have shown that Nlrp3 mRNA and Casp1 activity were increased in CMs, but not in non-cardiomyocytes, in mice after TAC46, suggesting NLRP3 inflammasome activation occurs mainly in cardiomyocytes post-TAC. Consistent with this, our findings demonstrated that LTBP4 promoted NLRP3 inflammasome activation in cardiomyocytes rather than in fibroblasts. We further identified that the binding of LTBP4 to NEK7 was essential for LTBP4 to enhance NLRP3 inflammasome activation in cardiomyocytes. Given that NEK7 expression is markedly higher in cardiomyocytes than in cardiac fibroblasts, we hypothesize that its low expression in fibroblasts impairs the ability of LTBP4 to promote NLRP3 inflammasome activation in cardiac fibroblasts. However, knockdown of LTBP4 in NRCFs reduced Col1a1 expression. Since LTBP4 is also detected in serum, it might possess fibroblast-specific functions independent of the NLRP3 inflammasome activation. Future work will be required to further elucidate the role of serum LTBP4 in fibroblast function.

Our study reveals the detrimental effect of LTBP4 on cardiomyocytes, consistent with a previous report showing that anti-LTBP4 treatment in dystrophic mice reduced muscle fibrosis and protected muscle against force loss in vivo51. Conversely, another study demonstrated that Ltbp4S−/− mice exhibited heightened inflammation and fibrosis following renal ischemia-reperfusion injury52. The different isoforms of LTBP4 play distinct roles in fibrosis43 and contribute to varying phenotypes of right ventricular hypertrophy20. Specifically, Ltbp4−/− mice exhibited right ventricular hypertrophy, possibly due to pulmonary arterial hypertension, whereas Ltbp4S−/− mice did not. Notably, our study uses cell-specific Ltbp4-knockout mouse lines, which helps eliminate the secondary effect of global Ltbp4 knock out in other tissues. Cardiomyocyte-specific Ltbp4 deficiency in mice led to ameliorate cardiac function and decreased BNP levels following TAC. BNP is widely employed as a marker for heart failure and cardiac dysfunction53,54. We therefore speculate that the decreased BNP levels may be due to improvement of heart function in Ltbp4-deficient mice.

In CANTOS (Canakinumab Anti-inflammatory Thrombosis Outcome Study), canakinumab, an anti-IL-1β antibody, reduced HF-related hospitalizations and mortality55, but treatment with canakinumab did not improve cardiovascular disease mortality, and caused an increase in infections56. Notably, we found that plasma IL-1β levels were significantly reduced in Ltbp4cko mice compared to Ltbp4fl/fl mice post-TAC. Thus, inhibition of LTBP4 could benefit HF patients by reducing inflammation and fibrosis.

In conclusion, our study identifies LTBP4 as a key factor in inflammasome activation and its association with IL-1β levels in HFrEF patients. LTBP4 deficiency in cardiomyocytes inhibits NLRP3 inflammasome activation and attenuates pathological heart remodeling during HF. This study provides crucial insights into the regulatory role of LTBP4 in the NLRP3 inflammasome signaling pathway and the progression of HF, suggesting novel therapeutic strategies for HF.

Methods

Ethics statement

All human specimens used in this study were approved by the human ethics committee of Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University (approval numbers: B2021-418R and B2024-243R) and the Ethics Committee Board at the School of Basic Medical Sciences, Fudan University (Ethics protocol approval number: 2021-C004). Each patient provided written informed consent, and all procedures complied with the ethical guidelines outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Additionally, all animal handling protocols were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee at the School of Basic Medical Sciences, Fudan University (approval number: 20250117-001), and were conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Human samples

The plasma levels of LTBP4 and IL-1β were measured in a cohort comprising 22 patients with HFrEF and 25 healthy controls, all recruited from Zhongshan Hospital of Fudan University, and all participants were given written informed consent. A summary of patient information is provided in Table S1. Cardiac LTBP4 levels were evaluated using six failing left ventricular tissues from HFrEF patients, sourced from Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University. Additionally, six samples of normal heart tissues with noncardiac death were obtained from the Department of Forensic Medicine, School of Basic Medical Sciences, Fudan University.

Animals

Ltbp4 loxP mice (Ltbp4flox/flox) in the C57/BL background were generated by flanking exon 2-4 of the Ltbp4 gene with two loxP sites, sourced from Shanghai Model Organisms Center Inc (Shanghai, China). The MYH6-CreERT2 mice were kindly provided by Prof. Gong (Shanghai Institute of Cardiovascular Diseases, Zhongshan Hospital, China). Ltbp4flox/flox mice were then crossed with the MYH6-CreERT2 mice to generate MYH6-CreERT2-Ltbp4flox/flox (Ltbp4cko) mice for cardiomyocyte-specific deletion of Ltbp4 in the C57BL/6 background. The primers used for genotyping are listed in Table S2. Mice were housed in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) facility under a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle. The ambient temperature was maintained at a room temperature of 22 °C ± 2 °C, humidity of 50% ± 5%, with free access to food and water.

For the mouse transverse aortic constriction (TAC) model, male cardiomyocyte-specific conditional Ltbp4 knockout mice Ltbp4cko and wild-type littermates Ltbp4flox/flox mice were used. 10–12-week-old male mice were intraperitoneally injected with tamoxifen (30 mg/kg) once every other day for a total of four injections to induce cardiac-specific conditional knockout. For heart tissue collection, animals were euthanized with intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital (60 mg/kg) followed by cervical dislocation.

For neonatal cardiac-specific Ltbp4 knockout, as described in previous studies57, Ltbp4cko and Ltbp4flox/flox dams were administered tamoxifen (30 mg/kg, intraperitoneal injection) every other day for a total of four injections, starting on postnatal days 1–2. Neonatal littermates were exposed to tamoxifen via breastfeeding and euthanized at postnatal day 7.

Reagents

The main reagents used in this paper are listed as follows: Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) (Life technologies, Carlsbad, CA, #12800082); Fetal Bovine Serum (Gbico, Carlsbad, CA, #10099-141); Collagenase II (Life technologies, Carlsbad, CA, #17101015); Bovine Serum Albumin (Yeasen, Shanghai, China #36101ES25); Tamoxifen (MCE, Monmouth Junction, NJ, # HY-13757A); PMSF (Beyotime, Shanghai, China, #ST505); Protease inhibitor cocktail (MCE, Monmouth Junction, NJ, #HY-K0010); DAPI (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, #D9542-1MG); Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, #T9284); Hipure Gel Pure DNA Mini Kit (Magen, Guangzhou, China, #D2111-02); Protein A/G PLUS-Agarose (Santacruz, Santa Cruz, CA, #SC-2003); ReverTra Ace qPCR RT kit (Toyobo, Osaka, Japan, #FSQ-101); Lipofectamine™ 3000 Transfection Reagent (Gbico, Carlsbad, CA, #L3000075); Opti-MEM (Gbico, Carlsbad, CA, #31985088); Trizol reagent (Vazyme Biotech, Nanjing, China, #R401-01); ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme Biotech, Nanjing, China, #Q711).

Cell culture

Neonatal rat cardiomyocytes (NRCMs) were isolated and cultured from 1- to 2-day-old Sprague-Dawley rats (Bikai, Shanghai, China), while neonatal mouse cardiomyocytes (NMCMs) were derived from 7-day-old Ltbp4fl/fl and Ltbp4cko mice following previously described protocols with minor modifications58. The neonatal rats and mice were first anesthetized deeply using 5% isoflurane, and the absence of a toe pinch or tail pinch reflex was confirmed before incision. Post-extraction, the hearts were rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to eliminate blood cells. The ventricles were then halved and digested overnight at 4 °C using 0.25% trypsin-EDTA (Gibco, Carlsbad, CA, #25200072). Subsequently, the pre-digested cardiac tissues were incubated with 1 mg/mL collagenase II (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, #17101015) and 5 mg/mL bovine serum albumin (BSA) (Yeasen, Shanghai, China, #36101ES25) for 7 to 10 min at 37 °C. The enzymatic reaction was halted by transferring the supernatant into a 15 mL centrifuge tube containing 2 to 3 mL of 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS)-DMEM. Digestion was considered complete when the tissue fluid was no longer turbid. After several rounds of digestion, the supernatant was centrifuged at room temperature at 250 × g for 5 min. The supernatant was then carefully removed, and the cells were resuspended in 2 to 3 mL of 10% FBS-DMEM. The resulting cell suspension was evenly plated in a 100 mm dish containing 10% FBS and incubated in a 37 °C CO2 incubator for 2 h. Non-adherent (suspension) cells were subsequently harvested and resuspended in a DMEM and M199 mixture (3:1) containing 3% FBS, 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Beyotime, Shanghai, China, #C0222), and bromodeoxyuridine (1 mM, to inhibit fibroblast growth). Finally, approximately 2 × 105 cells were seeded into each well of a six-well plate.

Neonatal rat cardiac fibroblasts (NRCF) were collected from the adherent cells recovered during the final step of NRCM isolation. After one cell passage to remove residual adherent cardiomyocytes, approximately 2 × 10⁵ cells were subsequently seeded into each well of a six-well plate and cultured in a medium containing 10% FBS-DMEM and 1% penicillin/streptomycin.

The rat cardiomyoblast cell line H9C2, human embryonic kidney (HEK) 293 T cells, and U2 Osteosarcoma (U2OS) cells were kindly provided by Prof. Ding (University of Chinese Academy of Sciences, China). These cells were maintained in 10% FBS-DMEM and 1% penicillin/streptomycin.

For cell expansion, the cells were treated with 0.25% trypsin-EDTA and subcultured at a 1:3 ratio. The culture medium was refreshed daily before passaging. All these stimulants were used at concentrations as previously described with some modifications. For inflammatory stimulation, NRCMs or NRCFs were serum-starved for 24 h, followed by treatment with Ang II59 (MCE, Monmouth Junction, NJ, #HY-13948) or PE59 (MCE, Monmouth Junction, NJ, # HY-B0471) for 24 h, Nigericin14 (MCE, Monmouth Junction, NJ, #HY-127019) for 1.5 h, or H2O260 (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, #88597) for 6 h. For inflammatory suppression, NRCMs were treated with MCC95059 (MCE, Monmouth Junction, NJ, # HY-12815) for 24 h. For DYNLT1 suppression, NRCMs were treated with CilioA39 (MCE, Monmouth Junction, NJ, # HY-100790) for 1 h. For protein stability analysis, NRCMS were treated with CHX61 (MCE, Monmouth Junction, NJ, # HY-12320) for 0, 3, and 6 h.

Virus infection

To achieve overexpression of LTBP4, a FLAG-tagged recombinant adenovirus encoding the full-length LTBP4 (Ad-LTBP4; Hanbio Biotech, Shanghai, China) was utilized. For overexpression of the LTBP4Δ2-27aa variant, a FLAG-tagged recombinant adenovirus encoding LTBP4 28-1624aa (Ad-LTBP4Δ2-27aa; Hanbio Biotech, Shanghai, China) was utilized. For the overexpression of LTBP4Δ1574-1624aa variant, a FLAG-tagged recombinant adenovirus encoding LTBP4 1-1573aa (Ad-LTBP4Δ1574-1624aa; PackGene Biotech, Guangzhou, China) was employed. LTBP4 suppression was achieved using a recombinant adenovirus containing Ltbp4-specific short hairpin RNA (shLtbp4; Hanbio Biotech, Shanghai, China), with the corresponding shRNA sequences detailed in Table S3. Control groups included blank adenoviruses (Ad-Con) for the overexpression and a null adenoviral vector (shNC) for the knockdown experiments. H9C2 cells or NRCMs were seeded in six-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells and infected with Ad-LTBP4 (MOI 1:30) to induce LTBP4 overexpression, Ad-LTBP4Δ2-27aa (MOI 1:30) to induce LTBP4Δ2-27aa overexpression, Ad-LTBP4Δ1574-1624aa (MOI 1:50) for LTBP4Δ1574-1624aa overexpression, and shLtbp4 (MOI 1:50) for Ltbp4 suppression. Ad-Con and shNC served as control groups.

Constructions and transfections

The human LTBP4-FLAG, NLRP3-MYC, and NEK7-HA plasmids were purchased from MiaoLing Plasmid Platform (Wuhan, China). The cDNA fragments that encoded LTBP4 mutant versions of the sequence lacking portions of 1326-1624aa, 1403-1624aa, and 1574-1624aa with a FLAG-tag were amplified with appropriate sets of primers and cloned into the P-CMV plasmids. HEK293T cells cultured in 6-well plates were transfected with 2 μg plasmids per well using jetPEI (Polyplus-transfection SA, Strasbourg, France, #24765-1) to determine protein–protein interactions.

Transverse aortic constriction (TAC) model

To generate Ltbp4cko mice, tamoxifen (MCE, Monmouth Junction, NJ, # HY-13757A) was administered intraperitoneally every other day for a total of four injections at a dose of 30 mg/kg body weight, inducing Cre/loxp recombination at 8 weeks of age. To inhibit SP1, plicamycin30 was administered intraperitoneally to adult mice at a dose of 0.4 mg/kg body weight every other day for a total of six injections, with two administered prior to TAC surgery and four administered subsequent to TAC surgery. The TAC model was established as previously described58. Mice aged 10 to 12 weeks were anesthetized through continuous inhalation of 2% isoflurane during the surgical procedure. The animals were placed in a supine position, and a midline cervical incision was performed to expose the trachea for direct intubation. Subsequently, a small incision was created in the second intercostal space to access the aortic arch. The transverse aorta was isolated and constricted using a 27-gauge needle and a 7-0 silk suture, after which the chest wall was closed in layers. Mice that underwent the same surgical procedure without aortic constriction were designated as sham controls. After the surgery, the mice were kept in a warm cage maintained at 37 °C until they were fully awake. Cardiac function was assessed via echocardiography (FUJIFILM VisualSonics Inc., Toronto, Canada, Vevo 3100, 30 MHz, 450 probes). Measurements were obtained from M-mode images of the left ventricular long axis. During the scanning process, heart rate (approximately 500 beats per minute) and body temperature were continuously monitored. Left ventricular ejection fraction and fractional shortening were calculated using VevoLAB analysis software (v5.6.1, FUJIFILM VisualSonics Inc., Toronto, Canada) according to established guidelines. Mice were euthanized with an intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital (60 mg/kg), and cardiac tissues were subsequently collected.

RNA extraction and real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted using Trizol reagent (Vazyme Biotech, Nanjing, China, #R401-01) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Equal amounts of RNA (1 μg) were reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using the Rever Tra Ace qPCR RT Kit (Toyobo, Osaka, Japan, #FSQ-101). Real-time quantitative PCR was performed with the ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme Biotech, Nanjing, China, #Q711), with primer sequences provided in Table S4. The reaction mixture was subjected to an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 40 amplification cycles (15 s at 95 °C, 30 s at 58 °C, and 30 s at 72 °C), concluding with a final extension at 72 °C for 7 min. Melting curves were generated by increasing the temperature from 55 °C to 95 °C in 0.5 °C increments at 10-s intervals, ensuring that each primer produced a single peak. Threshold cycle (Ct) values were determined using the CFX Manager analysis software (Bio-Rad). The mRNA expression levels were normalized to those of β-actin, with the primer sequences provided in Table S4.

Immunoprecipitation and western blotting

Murine cardiac tissues or cells were lysed using RIPA lysis buffer (150 mM NaCl, 100 mM Tris, 5 mM EDTA, and 1% Triton X-100), supplemented with 0.1 mM PMSF (Beyotime, Shanghai, China, #ST505) and 1 mM protease inhibitor cocktail (MCE, Monmouth Junction, NJ, #HY-K0010). Cellular membranes were removed by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. Equal amounts of protein were mixed with 6 × loading buffer and heated at 100 °C for 10 min.

For exogenous coimmunoprecipitation (co-IP) assays, the supernatants (1000 µg) were incubated with 20 µL of anti-DYKDDDDK G1 Affinity Resin (GenScript, Nanjing, China, #L00432), anti-HA Nanoab Agarose Beads (Everylab, Shanghai, China, #IP07), or anti-MYC Nanoab Agarose Beads (Everylab, Shanghai, China, #IP08) at 4 °C for 6 h. For endogenous proteins, supernatants (1000 µg) were incubated overnight with 5 µg of anti-NLRP3 (Cell Signaling, Danvers, MA, #15101T) or anti-NEK7 (Abcam, Cambridge, UK, #ab133514) antibodies, followed by incubation with Protein A/G PLUS-Agarose beads (Santa Cruz, Santa Cruz, CA, #SC-2003) for an additional 6 h. The beads were washed six times with Tris-buffered saline supplemented with 0.1% Tween 20. Elution was performed by incubating the washed beads in 30 µL of 2× SDS sample buffer at 100 °C for 10 min. After centrifugation at 4 °C (12,000 × g) for 5 min, the supernatant was collected for Western blot analysis. The samples were then separated by SDS-PAGE on 8–12% polyacrylamide gels and transferred to a 0.45 µm PVDF membrane using a wet transfer system. The membranes were blocked with a solution containing 5% nonfat powdered milk (Beyotime, Shanghai, China, # P0216) in PBS supplemented with 0.1% Tween-20 (PBST) and subsequently incubated with primary antibodies diluted in 1% Bovine Serum Albumin (Yeasen, Shanghai, China, #36101ES25) in PBST. Following this, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies in 5% nonfat powdered milk in PBST. Chemiluminescence signals were detected using a Tanon-5200 Imaging System (Tanon Science & Technology Ltd, Shanghai, China). The intensity of the bands was quantified through densitometric analysis using ImageJ software and normalized to the control. Antibodies against target proteins were used for immunoblotting, and the relevant antibody information is listed in Table S5. Uncropped and unprocessed scans of the most important blots are supplied in the Source Data file.

Glutathione s-transferase (GST) pull-down assays

The interaction between LTBP4 and NLRP3 was determined using GST precipitation assays. In brief, the FLAG-tagged LTBP4 plasmids (10 μg per dish) were transfected into HEK293T cells using PEI (Polyplus-transfection SA, Strasbourg, France, #24765-1). The LTBP4 proteins were purified 48 h after transfection. The GST-tagged NLRP3 was subcloned into the Escherichia coli expressing pGEX vector and expressed in the Escherichia coli strain BL21. The bacterial cultures were grown at 37 °C, induced with 0.1 mM isopropyl-β-D-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG), and then harvested by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The GST-tagged recombinant proteins NLRP3 and the purified FLAG-tagged LTBP4 proteins were then incubated together for 2 h at 4 °C, followed by three washes. The conjugated proteins were then eluted by SDS loading buffer and examined by Western blotting.

Immunofluorescence staining

Mice hearts were collected and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) overnight, then processed into paraffin sections cut at 3-micron intervals for subsequent staining. Masson’s trichrome staining (Yeasen, Shanghai, China, #60532ES74) and Sirius Red staining (Solarbio, Beijing, China, #G1472) were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The fibrotic area was quantified using ImageJ software and expressed as a percentage of the total fibrotic area relative to the total area of the left ventricle.

Immunofluorescence staining was conducted following deparaffinization and rehydration, with antigen retrieval achieved by immersing tissue sections in Tris-EDTA buffer (10 mM, pH 9.0) and heating in a 100 °C water bath for 30 min. The sections were then permeabilized using a solution containing 1% Triton X-100. Cells were seeded onto coverslips in a 12-well plate and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. After washing the cells three times with PBS, the cells were permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100. Next, both tissues and cells were blocked with 5% donkey serum (Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories, West Grove, PA, #017-000-121) for 2 h. Then, the tissues and cells were blocked with 5% donkey serum for 2 h. Immunostaining was performed using the following antibodies, diluted in 5% donkey serum and incubated overnight at 4 °C: cTNT (Abcam, Cambridge, UK, #ab8295); LTBP4 (Abmart, Shanghai, China, #PHY6049); NLRP3 (Proteintech, Rosemont, IL, #68102-1-Ig); POSTN (Proteintech, Rosemont, IL, #19899-1-AP); FLAG (Proteintech, Rosemont, IL, #66008-4-Ig); HA (huaxingbio, Beijing, China, #HX1820); CMYC (ACE Biotechnology, Changzhou, China, #BP0001-01); FLAG-488 (GenScript, Nanjing, China, #A01809), etc. After washing, sections were incubated with secondary antibodies: Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-mouse (Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories, West Grove, PA, #715-545-150), Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-rabbit (Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories, West Grove, PA, #711-545-152), Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated anti-mouse (Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories, West Grove, PA, # 715-585-150), Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated anti-rabbit (Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories, West Grove, PA, #711-585-152), and Alexa Fluor 647-conjugated anti-rabbit (Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories, West Grove, PA, #711-605-152), all diluted in 5% donkey serum and incubated for 2 h. The sections were then washed with PBST and stained with DAPI (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, #D9542) for 10 min. Images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). The quantitative analysis was performed using NIH ImageJ software, with cell counts conducted in five random fields. Antibodies against target proteins were used for immunofluorescence, and the relevant antibody information is listed in Table S5.

Cell viability

Cell viability was assessed by staining primary neonatal rat cardiac myocytes with the vital dye calcein acetoxymethyl ester (Calcein-AM) (Beyotime, Shanghai, China, #C2012). Following the manufacturer’s instructions, cells were washed with PBS 1 to 2 times after treatment. The Calcein AM staining working solution was then incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 30 min. After this incubation, the medium was replaced with fresh, pre-warmed culture medium (37 °C), and the cells were incubated again in the dark at 37 °C for an additional 30 min. This step ensured the complete hydrolysis of Calcein AM by intracellular esterases, resulting in the formation of green fluorescent Calcein. Subsequently, the cells were washed 2 to 3 times with PBS and stained with DAPI to visualize the nuclei. Images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). The quantitative analysis was performed using NIH ImageJ software, with cell counts conducted in five random fields.

LDH release assay

Cell death was assessed using the LDH release kit (MCE, Monmouth Junction, NJ, #HY-K1090). Briefly, cells were seeded into 96-well plates and subjected to appropriate treatments. From each well, 50 μL of the medium supernatant was aspirated, with the remaining medium and cells serving as the detection targets. Subsequently, 50 μL of Working Solution was added to each well, followed by thorough mixing via shaking. The samples were then incubated at room temperature in the dark for 30 min. Finally, 50 μL of Stop Solution was added to each well, and the absorbance at 490 nm was immediately measured with a microplate reader, followed by data processing.

MitoSOX staining

NRCMs were treated with 2.5 μM MitoSOX for 30 min, followed by washing with PBS. Images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). Quantitative analysis was performed using NIH ImageJ software, with cell counts conducted in five random fields.

ELISA

The plasma levels of LTBP4 were measured using the human LTBP4 ELISA Kit (ABclonal, Hubei, China, RK11421) and the mouse LTBP4 ELISA Kit (Uping Bio, Shenzhen, China, SYP-M2169). Plasma IL-1β levels were determined using the human IL-1β ELISA Kit (Huabio, Hangzhou, China, EH0006) and the mouse IL-1β ELISA Kit (ABclonal, Hubei, China, EM0001) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. First, 100 μL of the standard or sample was added to each well, which were then covered with an adhesive strip and incubated for 2 h at 37 °C. After washing the wells three times with a wash buffer, 100 μL of a working biotin-conjugate antibody was added to each well, followed by incubation for 1 h at 37 °C. After three additional washes, 100 μL of working streptavidin-HRP was added, and the wells were incubated for another hour at 37 °C. The plates were then washed three times, and tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) solution was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Finally, a stopping buffer was added, and the absorbance was measured at wavelengths of 450 nm and 630 nm.

snRNA-seq analysis

We employed software packages like Seurat (version 4.1)62 for data normalization, the removal of low-quality cells, and the filtering of low-expressed genes. Next, clustering analysis was performed based on gene expression features to identify different cell types. Cell populations were visualized using dimensionality reduction techniques, t-SNE, generating plots including Feature Plot.

RNA-seq analysis

The FASTQ files were aligned to the mouse reference genome mm10 using HISAT263. To quantify mRNA abundance, Cufflinks64 was utilized, with results reported as counts per million (CPM). Differential expression analysis was performed to identify genes that exhibited significant expression changes across different conditions, employing statistical tools such as DESeq265. Furthermore, differentially expressed genes underwent gene enrichment analysis and functional annotation to elucidate their biological significance. Finally, the results were visualized through the generation of volcano plots, heatmaps, and other graphical representations.

Statistical analysis

The data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The Shapiro–Wilks normality test was used to evaluate the distribution of the data. For comparisons between two groups with normally distributed data, Student’s t test was employed when variances were similar; Welch’s correction was applied if equal variances were not assumed, as determined by an F-test. For comparisons involving more than two groups, the Brown-Forsythe test was utilized to assess variance similarity, followed by either ordinary ANOVA or Welch’s ANOVA, based on the assumption of equal variances. For data that did not follow a normal distribution, the Mann–Whitney U test was used for two-group comparisons, while the Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn’s multiple comparisons test was applied for comparisons among more than two groups. Pearson’s correlation analysis was performed to evaluate the relationships between variables. The number of biological experimental replicates for each group is provided in the figure legends, and the exact p values are specified in the figures. A p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 9.5 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).
