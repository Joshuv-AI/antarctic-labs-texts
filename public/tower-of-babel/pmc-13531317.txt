Parent-Child Shared Book Reading With Preschoolers During and After the COVID-19 Pandemic
Authors: Kwanghee Jung, W. Steven Barnett
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This survey study evaluates trends in parent-reported shared book reading and variables associated with frequent reading among US preschool-aged children from 2020 through 2024.

Introduction

Parent-child shared book reading supports early language, literacy, cognitive, and socioemotional development.1,2 The COVID-19 pandemic disrupted preschool participation, family routines, and home learning environments.3,4 Although pandemic-related declines in school readiness have been documented,5,6 less is known about changes in home literacy practices. We examined trends in parent-reported shared book reading among US preschool-aged children from 2020 through 2024 and variables associated with frequent reading.

Methods

We analyzed 7 waves of a nationally representative online survey of parents of children aged 3 to 5 years not yet in kindergarten, conducted from April 2020 through September 2025. The Rutgers institutional review board approved the study. Parents provided informed consent electronically. This report follows applicable American Association for Public Opinion Research (AAPOR) reporting guidelines for survey studies. Analyses were conducted from January to April 2025. Each wave included approximately 1000 respondents recruited using stratified sampling, with survey weights applied to reflect national demographic distributions. Child race and ethnicity were parent-reported using survey items and categorized as American Indian or Alaska Native, Asian or Pacific Islander, Black, Hispanic, non-Hispanic White, and other or multiple races. Race and ethnicity were assessed in this study because prior studies found substantive associations of parent-child book reading with race and ethnicity independent of other family background characteristics. We assessed race and ethnicity as these associations may have persisted or changed postpandemic.

The primary outcome was whether a parent or household member read to the child 3 or more times in the past week, matching the 2019 National Household Education Survey (NHES) Early Childhood Program Participation measure.7 Logistic regression models estimated associations between frequent reading and cohort, race and ethnicity, parental education, household income, single-parent status, child characteristics, early education enrollment, indoor and outdoor play, screen time, and access to physical books and eBooks. Missing data were addressed using multiple imputation by chained equations. All analyses were performed using Stata version 18.0 (StataCorp). A 2-sided P < .05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The full analytic sample included cohorts 1 to 7 (6969 respondents); a restricted sample including cohorts 2 to 7 (6019 respondents) was used for models with variables introduced after cohort 1. Among children in the full analytic sample, 2930 (48.8%) were female, 2831 (40.7%) were aged 4 years, and the racial and ethnic groups were Black (749 children [10.8%]), Hispanic (1371 children [19.7%]), and non-Hispanic White (4282 children [61.5%]) (Table 1). In spring 2020, 780 parents (82.5%) reported reading to their child 3 or more times per week, compared with the 2019 NHES national estimate of 84.6% (approximately 6.9 million children). Reading frequency declined after spring 2020, with the largest decrease in spring 2021 (odds ratio [OR], 0.50; 95% CI, 0.40-0.62; P < .001); frequency remained lower through 2024 (Table 2).

In the full sample, higher parental education (OR, 1.37; 95% CI, 1.29-1.46; P < .001) and household income (OR, 1.14; 95% CI, 1.09-1.18; P < .001) were associated with greater odds of frequent reading, whereas single-parent households had lower odds (OR, 0.69; 95% CI, 0.59-0.79; P < .001). Consistent with prior research,8,9 Black and Hispanic children had lower unadjusted odds of frequent reading than non-Hispanic White children. In the restricted sample, after adjustment for socioeconomic characteristics, daily activities, and home literacy resources, the Black-White difference was no longer statistically significant (OR, 0.87; 95% CI, 0.71-1.06; P = .17), whereas the Hispanic-White difference persisted (OR, 0.61; 95% CI, 0.52-0.71; P < .001).

Indoor play and outdoor play were positively associated with frequent reading, whereas screen time was negatively associated, consistent with evidence that screen-based media may displace interactive learning activities.10 Early education enrollment was associated with higher odds of frequent reading (OR, 1.22; 95% CI, 1.06-1.39; P = .004). Among home literacy resources, each additional 10 physical books in the home was associated with 8% higher odds of frequent reading (OR, 1.08; 95% CI, 1.06-1.09; P < .001), whereas eBook access had no statistically significant association with reading frequency (OR, 1.02; 95% CI, 0.99-1.05; P = .28).

Discussion

In this survey study, parent-child shared book reading declined after the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic and had not returned to prepandemic levels by 2024. Several pandemic-related mechanisms may explain this sustained decline. Disruptions to family routines, preschool participation, library access, parent work schedules, and parental stress may have reduced opportunities for shared reading. Increased screen time may also have displaced interactive home learning activities, including reading.

Reading frequency was associated with socioeconomic characteristics, daily routines, early education enrollment, and physical book availability. Adjustment for behavioral and resource factors substantially reduced racial differences, with the Black-White difference becoming nonsignificant. The persistent Hispanic-White difference suggests that unmeasured factors, such as language, cultural relevance of available materials, work schedules, or parent beliefs about literacy acquisition for dual language learners, may shape shared reading practices. Interventions may need to combine access to books with culturally responsive strategies that help families integrate reading into daily routines.

This study has limitations. Measures were parent-reported and may be affected by recall or social desirability bias. The cross-sectional design limits causal inference, and reading frequency does not capture duration or quality. We did not have information on participation in parenting support, home visiting, or clinic-based literacy promotion programs, which might increase reading frequency. Sample sizes also limited analyses for Asian or Pacific Islander, American Indian or Alaska Native, and multiracial groups.
