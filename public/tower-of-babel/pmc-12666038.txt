Clinical and behavioural correlates of early diabetic foot ulcer risk at Jimma Medical Center, Ethiopia: application of Inlow’s 60-Second Screening Tool in a resource-constrained setting
Authors: Tekleab Kifle, Eyob Girma Abera, Kidus Tesfaye, Ermias Habte Gebremichael, Esayas Kebede Gudina
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo assess the early risk of diabetic foot ulcer (DFU) among adult patients with diabetes and to identify clinical and behavioural correlates associated with DFU risk using Inlow’s 60-Second Screening Tool.DesignA cross-sectional analytical study.SettingConducted at a tertiary-level referral hospital in Southwest Ethiopia.ParticipantsThe study included 164 adult patients with diabetes (aged ≥18 years) who attended routine follow-up visits at the diabetic clinic between February and March 2025. Patients who were critically ill, unable to communicate during data collection or pregnant were excluded. The median age of participants was 55 years (IQR 46–60), and 54.9% were male.Primary and secondary outcome measuresThe primary outcome was early DFU risk, categorised as ‘at risk’ or ‘not at risk’ using Inlow’s 60-Second Screening Tool. Secondary outcomes included factors associated with DFU risk, such as glycaemic control, lipid profile and foot care practices.ResultsOf the 164 participants, 32.3% (n=53) were found to be at risk for DFU. Participants who performed foot self-checks infrequently had more than threefold higher odds of DFU risk compared with those who practiced daily foot care (adjusted OR (AOR)=3.35; 95% CI 1.48 to 7.58; p=0.004). Poor glycaemic control (AOR=2.39; 95% CI 1.03 to 5.55; p=0.042) and dyslipidaemia (AOR=2.63; 95% CI 1.18 to 5.85; p=0.018) were also significantly associated with increased DFU risk.ConclusionsNearly one-third of patients with diabetes in this Ethiopian hospital setting were at early risk for DFU. Factors such as inadequate foot care, poor glycaemic control and dyslipidaemia were associated with increased risk. Incorporating rapid foot screening tools like Inlow’s 60-Second assessment into routine diabetes care, together with strengthened patient education and metabolic management, may help reduce the risk of DFU in resource-limited settings.

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus is a chronic metabolic disorder characterised by hyperglycaemia and associated with a wide array of complications, including nephropathy, retinopathy and cardiovascular disease.1 Among its most disabling sequelae is the diabetic foot ulcer (DFU), a complex and preventable condition that significantly contributes to patient morbidity, lower-limb amputations and elevated healthcare costs.2

Globally, individuals with diabetes face a 15–25% lifetime risk of developing a foot ulcer, with recurrence rates exceeding 50% within 5 years.3 Critically, more than 85% of diabetes-related lower-limb amputations are preceded by DFUs.4 The burden of DFU is especially pronounced in low- and middle-income countries, where access to specialised foot care and structured screening tools is limited. In Ethiopia, a recent meta-analysis reported a pooled DFU prevalence of 12.98%,5 with a similar rate (11.6%) documented among patients with diabetes attending follow-up at Jimma Medical Center (JMC).6

Known risk factors include a prolonged duration of diabetes (more than 10 years), peripheral neuropathy, peripheral arterial disease, structural foot abnormalities, a history of ulceration or amputation, poor glycaemic control and nephropathy.7 Although early identification of at-risk patients through systematic foot screening is crucial for preventing DFUs, ensuring timely referrals and reducing amputations,8 standardised risk assessment protocols are still inconsistently applied in routine practice, especially in resource-limited settings.

The Inlow’s 60-Second Diabetic Foot Screening Tool offers a rapid, structured approach to identifying modifiable risk factors including skin changes, deformities, callus formation and neuropathy during routine care.9 Widely validated and used in multiple settings, this tool enables clinicians to categorise patients into low, moderate or high-risk groups, guiding both immediate management and referral decisions.10 However, its use has not yet been evaluated or adopted at JMC. This study seeks to bridge critical gaps in clinical practice and knowledge by integrating the Inlow 60-Second Screening Tool into routine diabetes follow-up care at JMC. It aims to determine the prevalence and distribution of early DFU risk factors within a low-resource setting and to identify high-risk patient subgroups for targeted intervention. By generating context-specific evidence and promoting a standardised risk assessment protocol, the study supports improved DFU prevention, more efficient use of limited healthcare resources and better overall outcomes in diabetic care.

Methods

Study design and setting

A hospital-based cross-sectional analytical study was conducted from February to March 2025 at JMC, located in Jimma town, approximately 356 km southwest of Addis Ababa. JMC is one of the largest tertiary medical institutions in Ethiopia, serving an estimated population of over 20 million in the southwestern region of the country. The hospital offers a wide range of medical services, encompassing nearly all major specialties. The diabetic clinic at JMC operates two times a week; on Mondays and Tuesdays and provides follow-up services to patients living with diabetes. Care is delivered by a multidisciplinary team comprising one endocrinologist, internists, medical residents, general practitioners and medical interns.

Study population and eligibility criteria

The source population for this study comprised all patients attending follow-up at the diabetic clinic of JMC during the study period. The study population included all adult patients with diabetes (aged 18 years and above) who were on follow-up at the JMC diabetic clinic between February and March 2025. Patients with other comorbid illnesses were also eligible for inclusion. However, individuals who were critically ill, unable to communicate throughout the data collection period, or pregnant were excluded from the study.

Sample size determination and sampling procedure

The sample size for this study was determined using a single population proportion formula, assuming a 5% margin of error and a 95% confidence level. An estimated prevalence of DFU risk was taken as 11.6% based on previous literature.6 After applying the standard formula, the initial calculated sample size was 149. However, since the total source population of adult patients with diabetes on follow-up at JMC was less than 10 000, a finite population correction was applied, using the hospital’s 2024 Health Management Information System data, which reported approximately 2500 patients with diabetes in regular follow-up. After this adjustment, the final sample size was determined to be 164.

Participants were selected using a consecutive sampling approach, whereby all eligible patients with diabetes attending the clinic during their routine appointment dates within the study period were invited to participate until the required sample size was achieved. This method was chosen due to its feasibility and efficiency in a busy outpatient setting.

Data collection procedure

Data were collected using a validated, pretested and structured questionnaire developed after an extensive review of relevant literatures.11 12 The tool was designed to comprehensively assess both background characteristics and clinical factors relevant to diabetic foot risk. It included three main components. The first captured socio-demographic information such as age, gender, educational status and occupation. The second focused on clinical details, including the type and duration of diabetes mellitus, treatment modalities, foot care practices, smoking habits, alcohol consumption, comorbid illnesses and recent glycaemic control assessed through haemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) levels. Additionally, anthropometric measurements including body mass index (BMI) and waist circumference were obtained using standard procedures. The final component consisted of a foot risk assessment using the standardised Inlow’s 60-Second Diabetic Foot Screening Tool.13 This screening involved inspection of the skin and nails, assessment of foot deformities, footwear evaluation, temperature changes (cold or hot spots), range of motion, sensory testing using a monofilament, evaluation of pedal pulses and identification of signs such as dependent rubor or erythema.

Study outcome

The primary outcome was early risk of DFU, assessed at a single time point using Inlow’s 60-Second Screening Tool, which assesses a range of structural and clinical foot factors. Participants were classified as ‘at risk’ if any factor was present. This classification reflects the current risk status of patients and was used as the outcome variable in subsequent logistic regression analyses.

Data management and statistical analysis

The collected data were entered into Microsoft Excel and checked for completeness and consistency before being exported to SPSS V.26 (IBM, New York, USA) for analysis. Normality of continuous variables was assessed through visual inspection of histograms and Q-Q plots, as well as the Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Shapiro-Wilk tests. Normally distributed variables were summarised using mean±SD, while non-normally distributed variables were reported as median with IQR. Univariate analysis of categorical variables was performed using χ2 tests when expected cell counts were ≥5 and Fisher’s exact test when expected counts were <5. Continuous variables were compared between outcome groups using a non-parametric test based on the distribution of the data.

A binary logistic regression model was used to identify factors associated with DFU risk. Variables with a p value≤0.25 in the bivariable analysis were included in the multivariable model. Variables with fewer than five occurrences were excluded due to insufficient data for stable estimates. In the multivariable analysis, a p value<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Patient and public involvement

None.

Among 164 patients with diabetes, the overall median age was 55 years (IQR 44–63). Males comprised 60.4% of the at-risk group and 52.3% of the not-at-risk group. The majority of the participants were urban residents (n=110, 67.1%) with relatively good literacy status; 86% have attended elementary education or above (table 1).

Clinical profile

Type 2 diabetes was predominant, particularly among at-risk patients (98.1% vs 73.0%, p<0.001). Patients classified as at risk more frequently received a combination of insulin and oral glucose-lowering agents (66.0% vs 30.6%, p<0.001) and reported occasional foot self-checks more often (39.6% vs 18.0%, p=0.005). Dyslipidaemia was more common among at-risk patients (39.6% vs 18.9%, p=0.004), and median HbA1c was higher in this group (8.1% vs 7.5%, p=0.006). Other clinical variables, including hypertension, BMI, waist circumference and comorbidities, did not differ significantly between the groups (table 2).

Assessment of diabetic foot risk

Among the 164 patients included in the study, 67.7% (n=111) of participants had no risk, 30.5% (n=50) were at moderate risk, and 1.8% (n=3) were at high risk of developing diabetic foot complications based on Inlow’s 60-Second Screening Tool. About 43% (n=71) had dry skin with light callus on both feet, while heavy callus buildup was less common (left foot 13.4%, n=22; right foot 14.6%, n=24). Nail abnormalities were frequent, with unkempt and ragged nails observed in 62.2% (n=102) on the left and 59.1% (n=97) on the right foot. The majority had no deformities (left 83.5%, n=137; right 84.8%, n=139). Nearly half wore inappropriate footwear (48.2%, n=79), and foot temperature was predominantly warm (>85%). Full range of motion was noted in 89.6% (n=147) of both feet. Sensory symptoms were reported in 44.5% (n=73) of left feet and 37.2% (n=61) of right feet. Pedal pulses were present in 98.8% (n=162) of left and 100% (n=164) of right feet (table 3).

Factors associated with DFU risk

In the univariate analysis, several behavioural and clinical variables showed potential associations with DFU risk and were considered for inclusion in the multivariable logistic regression model using a p value threshold of ≤0.25. While nine variables met this criterion, type of diabetes and treatment modality were excluded from the final model due to significant imbalances in their distributions, which could lead to unstable or unreliable statistical estimates. Therefore, age, smoking history, foot care practice, BMI (kg/m2), waist circumference, blood glucose level (HbA1c) and dyslipidaemia were included in the final multivariable model.

In the multivariable model, three factors emerged as independently associated with increased DFU risk. Participants who only occasionally performed foot self-checks had more than three times the odds of being at risk compared with those who practiced daily foot care (adjusted OR (AOR)=3.35; 95% CI 1.48 to 7.58; p=0.004). Similarly, high blood glucose was also significantly associated with higher DFU risk (AOR=2.39; 95% CI 1.03 to 5.55; p=0.042). Additionally, the presence of dyslipidaemia was independently linked to elevated DFU risk, with more than two times the odds compared with those without lipid abnormalities (AOR=2.63; 95% CI 1.18 to 5.85; p=0.018) (table 4).

Discussion

This study found that among 164 patients with diabetes on follow-up at JMC, nearly one-third (32.3%) were classified as at risk for DFU. Factors significantly associated with increased risk included infrequent foot care, particularly occasional self-inspection of the feet, elevated blood glucose levels (HbA1c≥7%) and dyslipidaemia.

Our finding that 32.3% of patients with diabetes were at early risk for developing DFUs, as assessed by Inlow’s 60-Second Screening Tool, is lower than rates reported in several other low- and middle-income settings. For example, studies from Egypt (47.9%)14 and Bangladesh (44.5%)15 reported substantially higher proportions at risk using the same tool, while a study in Guyana using a simplified version of the tool found that 48% of patients either had active DFUs or were at high risk.16 These higher estimates in other settings may reflect differences in patient profiles, such as longer duration of diabetes, poorer glycaemic control, higher prevalence of peripheral neuropathy or limited access to routine foot care services. In contrast, a study from Southern India reported much lower risk levels, 15.3% for the left foot and 17.3% for the right,17 possibly due to differences in screening approaches, study populations and inclusion criteria, as very few patients in that study were classified as high risk.

Our study also highlighted the high prevalence of structural and sensory foot abnormalities among individuals with diabetes, underscoring the ongoing need for routine foot screening. Nearly half of the participants (43.3%) presented with dry skin and callus formation, over 60% had unkempt toenails, and more than one-third (34.8%) exhibited sensory deficits, with monofilament testing detecting fewer than 10 sensation sites. These challenges are consistent with previous observations in real-world primary care settings, where implementing risk models for foot ulcer prediction faces practical limitations.18 These physical signs are well-established precursors to skin breakdown, ulceration and infection.19 The presence of such abnormalities not only signals a higher risk for diabetic foot complications but also reflects gaps in patient education and foot hygiene practices. These findings reinforce the importance of incorporating regular, structured foot assessments into diabetes management, as emphasised by international guidelines such as those of the International Working Group on the Diabetic Foot.20 Early detection and intervention for such abnormalities can significantly reduce the incidence of foot ulcers and lower-extremity amputations,19 particularly in low-resource settings where access to advanced wound care is limited.

Foot care practices emerged as a key modifiable behavioural factor in our study. Patients who only occasionally performed foot self-checks had more than three times the odds of being at risk for DFUs (AOR=3.35; 95% CI 1.48 to 7.58; p=0.004) compared with those who engaged in daily foot care. This finding echoes evidence from previous studies in Indonesia, which found a significant correlation between self-care levels and DFU risk, with higher levels of self-care associated with a lower risk of ulceration (p<0.05).21 Similarly, studies from other low- and middle-income countries (LMICs)2224 and high-income countries (HICs)25 26 have reported that poor foot self-care practices are strongly linked to increased foot complications and ulceration, emphasising the universal importance of promoting daily foot care behaviours to prevent DFUs and their consequences.

We also found a strong association between high blood glucose level (HbA1c≥7%) and DFUs risk (AOR=2.39; 95% CI 1.03 to 5.55; p=0.042). This supports previous studies conducted in both LMICs14 27 and HICs28 29 that identified poor glycaemic control as a central risk factor for neuropathy, impaired wound healing and ulcer development.30 31 These findings underscore the importance of strict glycaemic control as a preventive strategy for DFUs.

The significant association between dyslipidaemia and DFU risk observed in our study reinforces the growing body of evidence linking lipid abnormalities to microvascular and neuropathic complications in diabetes. The presence of dyslipidaemia was independently linked to elevated DFU risk, with more than twice the odds compared with those without lipid abnormalities (AOR=2.63; 95% CI 1.18 to 5.85; p=0.018), suggesting that elevated cholesterol and triglyceride levels may contribute to impaired blood flow, delayed wound healing and peripheral nerve damage.32 This finding is consistent with prior studies33 34 that have identified dyslipidaemia as a contributing factor to the development and progression of DFUs, emphasising the need for comprehensive metabolic control beyond glycaemic management. Integrating lipid monitoring and control into routine diabetes care may therefore play a vital role in reducing the burden of foot complications and improving patient outcomes.

Conclusion

This study highlights a relatively high early risk of DFU among individuals living with diabetes, particularly those with poor glycaemic control, dyslipidaemia and inadequate foot care practices. The high prevalence of structural and sensory foot abnormalities underscores the importance of routine foot examinations and early preventive measures. The findings emphasise the value of integrating simple, rapid screening tools such as Inlow’s 60-Second screen into routine diabetes care to facilitate early identification and prevention in resource-limited settings. Future prospective longitudinal assessment will be crucial to track DFU incidence, validate these baseline risk findings and inform targeted interventions for effective long-term prevention and management of diabetic foot complications.
