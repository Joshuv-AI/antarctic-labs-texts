Extending the Diagnostic Frontier: Slim Endobronchial Ultrasound in Navigation Bronchoscopy: A Case Series
Authors: Johannes M.A. Daniels, Marjolein A. Heuvelmans, Peter I. Bonta, Jouke T. Annema
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionConventional convex endobronchial ultrasound (EBUS) is limited to the central airways, whereas radial EBUS lacks real-time guidance. As navigation bronchoscopy expands into the peripheral lung, a gap persists in the (sub)segmental “mid-zone,” where visualization, tool alignment, and vascular anatomy challenge diagnostic sampling.Case PresentationSeven patients with suspected lung cancer in the mid-zone underwent slim EBUS (s-EBUS) with or without cone-beam CT-guided navigation bronchoscopy. Anatomical access, sampling strategy, diagnostic contribution, and safety were evaluated. s-EBUS allowed precise real-time visualization and needle placement in mid-zone targets, including stations 12L and peribronchial lung nodules beyond (sub)segmental bronchi. In two cases, a pulmonary artery branch separated airway and lesion; under ultrasound control, transvascular needle access was feasible without adverse events. In a single case, s-EBUS-guided cryobiopsy was successfully performed using a 1.1-mm probe and yielded diagnostic tissue for histology and molecular analysis. s-EBUS provided clinically relevant diagnosis or staging information in all cases.Discussions-EBUS extends ultrasound-guided bronchoscopy beyond the central airways and may close an important anatomical diagnostic gap in navigation bronchoscopy. Advantages include mid-zone access, vascular mapping, selective transvascular sampling, and targeted cryobiopsy. Further studies should assess performance, safety, and clinical impact.

Introduction

Navigation bronchoscopy has dramatically expanded bronchoscopic access to peripheral pulmonary lesions, enabling diagnostic sampling in areas that were previously beyond the reach of conventional bronchoscopy [1]. Despite these advances, important diagnostic challenges remain, particularly for lesions and lymph nodes located adjacent to the (sub)segmental or “mid-zone” airways.

In this anatomical region, current bronchoscopic tools have notable limitations. Radial endobronchial ultrasound (EBUS), while widely used to confirm tool-in-lesion during navigation bronchoscopy, is less reliable for extraluminal targets in the mid-zone [2]. In addition, the relatively rigid airway wall and the proximity of larger blood vessels often preclude the use of forceps or cryobiopsy, limiting tissue acquisition options. Convex probe EBUS enables real-time ultrasound-guided sampling but due to its large diameter is restricted to central airways and therefore cannot address this diagnostic gap.

Slim EBUS (s-EBUS) bronchoscopes combine a reduced outer diameter with real-time ultrasound imaging and sampling guidance, potentially allowing ultrasound-guided sampling beyond the central airways. This raises the possibility of extending real-time, ultrasound-guided diagnostics to lesions and lymph nodes adjacent to mid-zone airways during navigation bronchoscopy.

In this report, we present a series of seven cases illustrating the clinical application of s-EBUS in navigation bronchoscopy. These cases highlight the feasibility of s-EBUS for real-time ultrasound-guided sampling in the mid-zone of the lung and address key technical and workflow considerations relevant to its successful use.

Case Presentations

We present seven cases illustrating the use of s-EBUS either as stand-alone technique or as an adjunct to navigation bronchoscopy for sampling pulmonary lesions and lymph nodes adjacent to mid-zone airways. The Olympus BF-UCP190F s-EBUS bronchoscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) is a convex probe EBUS scope with a reduced outer diameter of 5.9 mm, a 1.7-mm working channel, and enhanced upward angulation of up to 170°, allowing advancement into more distal and also upper lobe (sub)segmental airways compared to conventional convex EBUS bronchoscopes. Despite its small caliber, the scope maintains real-time ultrasound imaging and sampling guidance. In contrast to radial EBUS, s-EBUS enables continuous ultrasound visualization during needle placement, facilitating ultrasound-guided sampling beyond the central airways. Case 1

An 81-year-old female was referred for navigation bronchoscopy because of a fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG)-avid 12-mm solid lesion in the right upper lobe (Fig. 2a). As the lesion was located adjacent to the RB3b, s-EBUS (Olympus BF-UCP190F) was selected instead of a curved extended working channel approach, in combination with cone-beam CT (CBCT) navigation.

Case 1. a Axial chest CT demonstrating a 12-mm solid lesion in the right upper lobe, located adjacent to the RB3b. b Augmented fluoroscopy image showing positioning of the s-EBUS scope in the RB3b under CBCT-based navigation guidance. c Real-time ultrasound image obtained with s-EBUS, demonstrating the target lesion separated from the airway lumen by an intervening pulmonary artery branch (white arrow), necessitating a transvascular sampling approach. d CBCT confirmation of tool-in-lesion following 25 G needle placement.

After CBCT segmentation of the target lesion, the s-EBUS scope was advanced into the RB3b under augmented fluoroscopic guidance (Philips Azurion; Fig. 2b). Ultrasound imaging demonstrated a pulmonary artery branch interposed between the airway lumen and the target lesion, precluding direct extraluminal access. Consequently, a transvascular approach was performed using a 25-gauge EBUS needle (Olympus ViziShot; Fig. 2c). Tool-in-lesion was subsequently confirmed with CBCT (Fig. 2d). TBNA samples demonstrated neoplastic cells, cytomorphologically consistent with a neuro-endocrine tumor (NET).

Case 2

A 68-year-old female was referred for a 29 × 18 mm FDG-avid lesion para-aortal in the left upper lobe (LUL; Fig. 3a) and FDG-avid lymph nodes on station 5 and 12L (Fig. 3d). The lesion in the LUL was reached with CBCT navigation bronchoscopy (CBCT-NB) with augmented fluoroscopy and 180° bended catheter (Medtronic) and sampled with a 1.1-mm cryoprobe (ERBE; Fig. 3b, c). The station 12L lymph node was reached by inserting the s-EBUS scope in the left upper lobe bronchus (Fig. 3e). Histological samples of the lesion in the LUL and cytological samples of the 12L lymph node demonstrated lung adenocarcinoma. NGS on the cytological sample showed no targetable mutations and PD-L1 performed on the histological sample was >75%.

Case 2. a Axial PET-CT image demonstrating the tumor in the left upper lobe. b Augmented fluoroscopy image showing positioning of the 1.1-mm cryoprobe (arrow) through the navigation catheter. c Cone-beam CT confirmation of tool-in-lesion of the 1.1-mm cryoprobe (arrow) in the left upper lobe lesion. d Axial PET-CT image demonstrating the FDG avid station 12L lymph node. e s-EBUS sampling of station 12L with the 25 G needle.

Case 3

A 56-year-old male was referred with a 14-mm FDG-avid solid lesion in the LUL and suspected lymph node metastases on station 5 and 12L (Fig. 4). The patient underwent systematic lymph node staging with conventional EBUS and EUS-B under propofol sedation without CBCT. Because no targetable suspicious lymph nodes were identified with the conventional EBUS scope, s-EBUS was added in the same procedure. After inserting the s-EBUS scope in the LUL bronchus, station 12L was identified and sampled with the 25 G needle (Fig. 4c). Cytology was consistent with squamous cell carcinoma, NGS revealed no targetable mutations and PD-L1 expression was 100%.

Case 3. a Axial PET-CT image demonstrating station 12L lymph node. b PET-CT image demonstrating FDG avidity of the 12L lymph node. c s-EBUS sampling of station 12L with the 25 G needle.

Case 4

A 78-year-old female was under surveillance for 6 pulmonary nodules and referred for navigation bronchoscopy because one lesion had shown consistent growth. This lesion was located in segment 5 of the middle lobe with a diameter of 12.5 mm and was FDG-avid (Fig. 5a). CBCT-NB with augmented fluoroscopy and 180° bended catheter (Medtronic) was performed. Radial EBUS showed the nodule with an eccentric view (Fig. 5b). Five TBNAs and 7 forceps biopsies were performed; however, the samples showed no representative material. The patient was planned for a repeat bronchoscopy, this time using s-EBUS. The lesion was visualized and sampled with a 25 G needle (Fig. 5c) and s-EBUS-guided cryobiopsy with a 1.1-mm probe (ERBE) was performed as well. Cytology showed adenocarcinoma. Histology confirmed the diagnosis of adenocarcinoma and immunohistochemistry was consistent with metastatic thyroid cancer. NGS performed on the cryobiopsy showed no targetable mutations and PD-L1 was >50%.

Case 4. a Axial CT image demonstrating 12.5-mm nodule (arrow) in segment 5 of the middle lobe. b Radial EBUS image showing excentric view of the nodule during (nondiagnostic) CBCT-NB. c s-EBUS ultrasound image showing the nodule and the 25 G needle sampling.

Case 5

A 78-year-old male with concomitant prostate cancer was evaluated by the urologist because of a new lesion in the right kidney, on imaging most consistent with renal cell carcinoma. In addition, three pulmonary nodules were found in the right lower lobe (8 mm), the middle lobe (15 mm), and the left lower lobe (LLL, 16 mm). The lesion in the LLL was clearly growing and also most suitable for navigation bronchoscopy (Fig. 6a). A CBCT-NB with s-EBUS was performed. The target lesion was visualized with s-EBUS and 8 TBNA samples were acquired with the 25 G needle (Fig. 6b, c). Cytology revealed malignant cells, but not enough material was available for cyto-immunochemistry and molecular analysis. Therefore, the patient was referred for surgical biopsy.

Case 5. a Axial CT image demonstrating 16-mm nodule (arrow) in segment 8 of the LLL. b Fluoroscopy image demonstrating positioning of the s-EBUS scope in the LB8b bronchus. c s-EBUS ultrasound image showing the nodule and the 25 G needle sampling.

Case 6

A 66-year-old female with a history of thymoma was referred for diagnosis of a growing 13-mm nodule in the RUL, present since 2023, an 8-mm nodule in segment 9 of the RLL and a 16-mm ground glass lesion in the RLL (Fig. 7a). CBCT-NB was performed. The lesion in the RUL was reached with a 180° bended catheter and sampled with TBNA, forceps-, and cryobiopsy. The nodule in the RLL was visualized with s-EBUS under CBCT guidance (Fig. 7b, c) and sampled with a 25 G needle (Fig. 7d). Cytology of this lesion showed malignant cells consistent with lung adenocarcinoma. PD-L1 was 100%, there was insufficient tissue for NGS.

Case 6. a Axial CT image demonstrating the 16-mm ground glass lesion (circle) and the 8-mm nodule adjacent to the RB9b (arrow). b Augmented fluoroscopy demonstrating s-EBUS scope in position in the RB9b. Slight CT-to-body divergence is evident as the actual lesion is visible around the EBUS needle. c s-EBUS ultrasound image showing the nodule adjacent to the RB9b. d s-EBUS ultrasound image showing the sampling of the nodule adjacent to the RB9b with the 25 G needle.

Case 7

A 69-year-old female was referred with an FDG-PET-negative 10 mm non-spiculated lesion in the LLL, segment 10, most consistent with low-grade NET (Fig. 8a). The lesion was an incidental finding on a CT scan performed in the work-up of chronic cough. CBCT-NB was performed and because the lesion was adjacent to the LB10b bronchus, s-EBUS was added (Fig. 8b). The lesion and adjacent pulmonary artery branches were visualized, and the lesion was sampled successfully with a 25 G needle via a transvascular approach (Fig. 8c, d). Cytology showed malignant cells, morphologically consistent with NET.

Case 7. a Axial CT image demonstrating 10-mm nodule (arrow) in segment 10 of the LLL. b Fluoroscopy image demonstrating positioning of the s-EBUS scope in the LB10b bronchus. c Cone-beam CT confirmation of tool-in-lesion following 25 G needle placement (arrow). d s-EBUS ultrasound image showing the nodule, the adjacent pulmonary artery branches (white arrows) and the transvascular 25 G needle sampling.

In none of the cases, immediate or delayed complications were observed. Procedure duration was not systematically recorded. However, in our experience, the integration of s-EBUS into a structured navigation bronchoscopy workflow adds only limited procedural time. No equipment damage was observed in any of the cases.

This case series was conducted and reported in accordance with the CARE guidelines; the completed CARE checklist is provided in the online supplementary material (for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000551967).

Discussion

This case series illustrates how s-EBUS can extend real-time ultrasound-guided bronchoscopy into the (sub)segmental mid-zone of the lung, especially in the upper lobes, a region that remains challenging despite advances in navigation bronchoscopy. This anatomical corridor represents an underrecognized diagnostic blind spot between conventional convex EBUS staging and peripheral navigation bronchoscopy, where neither technique alone provides real-time ultrasound-guided sampling. In addition, percutaneous biopsy in this area is unattractive due to the proximity of large vascular structures. Our initial experience suggests that s-EBUS can fill this diagnostic gap by combining a small outer diameter and enhanced angulation with continuous ultrasound visualization and sampling guidance enabling both TBNA and cryobiopsy beyond the (sub)segmental airways.

A key finding of this series is the practical gain in access to mid-zone lesions and lymph nodes. In several cases, s-EBUS enabled sampling of peribronchial nodules from subsegmental bronchi and station 12L lymph nodes that are beyond the reach of standard convex EBUS. This allowed diagnostic and staging information to be obtained in a single bronchoscopic session, which is particularly relevant in patients with suspected lung cancer, where timely integration of histology, immunohistochemistry, and molecular profiling is crucial for treatment decisions.

The cases further illustrate the complementary roles of s-EBUS and advanced imaging during navigation bronchoscopy. In our experience, CBCT guidance is not required in most cases, as s-EBUS alone may provide sufficient real-time visualization to reach and sample mid-zone targets. However, advancing a convex ultrasound bronchoscope into subsegmental bronchi can compromise bronchoscopic orientation due to the absence of distinct ultrasound landmarks and limited endoscopic visualization in smaller airways. In such situations, CBCT with augmented fluoroscopy can facilitate more efficient and precise localization of the target lesion and confirmation of tool-in-lesion positioning.

In the present series, the majority of procedures were planned in a hybrid suite to allow the use of CBCT-guided navigation bronchoscopy, or to have it available as a back-up in case s-EBUS alone did not allow adequate lesion identification. It is conceivable that, with increasing experience, s-EBUS as a stand-alone technique may be sufficient to identify and sample the majority of mid-zone lesions. Such an approach could reduce radiation exposure and procedural costs in selected patients. This combined and adaptable strategy proved particularly advantageous in mid-zone lesions, where relatively rigid airway anatomy and intervening vascular structures may separate the bronchial tree from the target.

An important technical and conceptual observation is that deliberate transvascular access can be considered in carefully selected cases. In two cases (1 and 7), a pulmonary artery branch clearly separated the airway lumen from the target lesion; under continuous ultrasound visualization, a transvascular trajectory with a 25-gauge needle was chosen when no safer alternative route was available. Although no immediate complications occurred in our limited experience, this approach should be regarded as exceptional and reserved for centers with substantial EBUS expertise, robust imaging support, and appropriate peri-procedural monitoring. Systematic data on the safety of transvascular EBUS-TBNA in the mid-zone are lacking. Although transvascular needle passage during conventional central EBUS staging has been shown to be feasible and safe in selected series [4], larger dedicated studies will be required before this approach can be considered beyond a salvage strategy in mid-zone targets.

Another novel aspect of our series is the demonstration that s-EBUS-guided cryobiopsy is technically feasible in mid-zone lesions. In 1 patient, a prior CBCT navigation bronchoscopy using radial EBUS, forceps biopsies, and TBNA had been nondiagnostic. During the repeat procedure, s-EBUS allowed real-time visualization of the lesion and needle, followed by targeted cryobiopsy using a 1.1-mm probe. In our experience, the 1.1-mm cryoprobe could be advanced through the puncture site created by the 25-gauge needle without difficulty. This may be related to the relatively lower rigidity of the airway wall in the mid-zone compared to the central airways, which may facilitate probe passage following needle puncture. While our case does not allow firm conclusions about incremental diagnostic yield, it illustrates that cryobiopsy can be safely performed under s-EBUS guidance in selected mid-zone targets, potentially improving tissue volume and quality for histology and molecular analysis.

The use of s-EBUS in this context requires specific workflow adaptations. First, careful planning of the access route and sheath management is essential: in our experience, visualization of the target may be lost once the sheath is advanced, unless the scope is positioned firmly against the airway wall at the point of interest before sheath insertion. Second, the working channel diameter, although sufficient for a 25-gauge needle and 1.1-mm cryoprobe, imposes limits on tool choice and may increase resistance during device manipulation in sharply angulated positions. These aspects underline that s-EBUS should be viewed as a complementary tool within a broader EBUS staging and navigation bronchoscopy strategy, rather than a stand-alone solution.

This report has several limitations. It represents a small, single-center, descriptive series without a control group, preventing any robust statements on diagnostic yield, complication rates, or cost-effectiveness compared with established techniques such as radial EBUS-guided navigation or percutaneous biopsy. All procedures were performed in a high-volume center with extensive experience in navigation bronchoscopy, EBUS, and CBCT, which may limit generalizability. Furthermore, long-term outcomes and downstream clinical impact were not systematically assessed.

Despite these limitations, our findings suggest that s-EBUS can meaningfully expand the diagnostic frontier in navigation bronchoscopy by providing real-time ultrasound guidance in the mid-zone of the lung, enabling access to otherwise difficult lesions, including mid-zone lymph nodes, selected transvascular targets, and s-EBUS-guided cryobiopsies. Future prospective studies should evaluate diagnostic yield, safety, and learning curves, as well as define patient and lesion characteristics in which s-EBUS offers the greatest added value over existing bronchoscopic and non-bronchoscopic approaches.
