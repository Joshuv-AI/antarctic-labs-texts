Trimester Specific Anatomical and Histological Characterization of Testicular Descent and Development in Sheep Foetuses
Authors: Barış Can Güzel, Banu Kandil, Muhammet Zahid Atli, İhsan Işbilir, Oleksii O Melnyk, Fatma Işbilir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThis study aimed to investigate the morphological, histological and ultrastructural characteristics of prenatal testicular development in male Hamdani crossbred sheep foetuses.MethodsA total of 17 male foetuses with gestational ages ranging from 50 to 125 days were examined. Gestational age was estimated using crown–Rectum length (CRL) measurements and the foetuses were classified according to trimester. Macroscopic evaluations were performed to determine the position of the testes in relation to the abdominal cavity, inguinal canal and scrotum. Testicular length, width and weight were measured and statistically analysed. Histological examinations were conducted on paraffin sections stained with Crossmon's trichrome, while ultrastructural features were evaluated using scanning electron microscopy (SEM).ResultsTesticular descent was observed to begin around Day 50 of gestation and progressed toward the inguinal canal; however, complete descent into the scrotum had not occurred by Day 125. Morphometric measurements showed a significant increase in testicular size and weight with advancing gestational age. Histological findings revealed the progressive formation and organization of seminiferous tubules, the presence of Sertoli and Leydig cells, and the development of interstitial tissue. SEM observations provided detailed visualization of the surface architecture and cellular organization of the developing foetal testes.ConclusionThis study provides a comprehensive developmental timeline of testicular organogenesis in Hamdani crossbred sheep foetuses. The findings contribute to the understanding of foetal reproductive biology and offer valuable anatomical data for comparative developmental studies and the evaluation of congenital testicular disorders.Keywords: foetal testis, Hamdani crossbred sheep, histology, prenatal testicular development, scanning electron microscopy (SEM), testicular descent

Introduction

It is estimated that sheep were domesticated earlier than other ruminant animals. Initially spreading to specific regions of Central and South Asia and Southern Europe, these animals were later domesticated in advanced civilizations such as Iran, Greece, Rome and Egypt, where they were utilized for various products (Ezzatpoor 2004). Numerous sheep breeds are raised worldwide. The Hamdani sheep breed, originating from Iraq, is commonly raised in the Eastern and Southeastern Anatolia regions of Türkiye, particularly in the provinces of Hakkari, Van, Siirt, Batman and Bitlis. This breed, adapted to mountainous areas, is characterized by its elongated body structure and large, pendulous ears (Demir and Aygün 2021; Örkiz et al. 1984).

The regulation of reproductive system development and external genital organs, including testicular descent into the scrotum, represents the initial and essential step for normal spermatogenesis. Testicular hormones mediate this process, primarily androgens (Mäkelä et al. 2019; Rotgers et al. 2018). It has been reported that testicular development is supported by increasing plasma testosterone concentrations, which rise from approximately 0.6 nmol/L on Day 50 of gestation to 3.7 nmol/L by Day 110 (Paul et al. 2005; Rae et al. 2002). The descent of the testes into the scrotum occurs through the contraction and expansion capacity of the gubernaculum testis, allowing testicular passage through the inguinal canal (El Zoghbi et al. 2007). Testicular descent (descensus testis) is completed at different stages among species: at birth in humans, just before birth in rabbits, pigs and horses, in early gestation in ruminants, and postnatally in carnivores (Hughes and Acerini 2008; Bahadır and Yıldız 2016).

Prenatal testicular developmental anomalies may result in conditions such as cryptorchidism (Bay et al. 2007; Ferlin et al. 2008), gonadal dysgenesis including hypospadias (Domenice et al. 2016; El‐Khairi and Achermann 2012), inadequate masculinization and intersex formation at birth (Larney et al. 2014; Shima et al. 2013), and infertility (O'Bryan et al. 2008; Singh et al. 2013), all of which may arise from environmental or genetic factors. Several studies have reported a significant relationship between reproductive activity and the biometric analysis of testicular development (El‐Wishy and El‐Sawaf 1974). Therefore, a comprehensive understanding of normal testicular organogenesis during embryonic and foetal stages is crucial for the diagnosis and treatment of andrological developmental disorders in both prenatal and postnatal life (Barreto et al. 2018). A thorough understanding of the testis' organization and physiological development is essential to gain insights into functional spermatogenesis and reproductive capacity. For this purpose, the volume of the testis, morphometric characteristics and volumes of its structures, as well as the organization of interstitial tissue and seminiferous tubules must be studied (Sakamoto et al. 2007; Wing and Christensen 1982).

Sheep have been used as a translational model in the study of human testicular development and related disorders (Guan and Martin 2017). In the technique known as xenografting, germ cells are retrieved from functional testicular tissue to preserve the genetic pool of endangered species, with sheep serving as a model organism for this method (Arregui et al. 2008). In the literature, various studies have examined male testicular development using different techniques in species such as buffaloes (Abdel‐Raouf 1960), sheep (Sadeghinezhad et al. 2021), Karadi sheep (Amin and Alwan 2010), horses (Barreto et al. 2018) and humans (Malas et al. 2001). Furthermore, descriptive studies have highlighted the macroscopic, morphometric and histological characteristics of prenatal testicular development in sheep by emphasizing the structures of inter‐ and intra‐tubular cells (Parchami and Fatahian Dehkordi 2008). However, although previous studies have described prenatal testicular development in sheep using macroscopic and histological approaches, no study has comprehensively evaluated foetal testicular development using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). While conventional histology provides valuable information on tissue architecture and cellular organization, SEM enables high‐resolution visualization of the three‐dimensional surface morphology, seminiferous cord organization and cellular interactions that cannot be fully appreciated by light microscopy. Therefore, SEM provides complementary ultrastructural information that contributes to a more comprehensive understanding of prenatal testicular development.

The objective of this study was to comprehensively characterize prenatal testicular development in Hamdani crossbred sheep foetuses during the second and third trimesters. Specifically, the study aimed to (i) determine the topographical changes associated with testicular descent, (ii) evaluate morphometric growth patterns of the foetal testes, (iii) describe the histological organization of the developing testicular tissue, and (iv) characterize the ultrastructural features of foetal testicular development using SEM. By integrating macroscopic, histological, morphometric and SEM findings, this study provides a comprehensive description of prenatal testicular organogenesis in sheep.

Material and Methods

Study Design

This study was designed as a descriptive, cross‐sectional anatomical and histological study to evaluate prenatal testicular development in Hamdani crossbred sheep foetuses during the second and third trimesters using macroscopic, morphometric, histological and scanning electron microscopic examinations.

Samples

A total of 17 male foetuses were used in this study, including 10 from the second trimester and 7 from the third trimester, all obtained from privately operated slaughterhouses located in the Southeastern Anatolia region. The uterine tissues were carefully dissected on‐site, and only male foetuses from single pregnancies were included in the study. Attention was paid to ensure that neither the uterus nor the foetuses exhibited any gross congenital malformations, developmental defects, traumatic lesions, macroscopic pathological changes or signs of tissue autolysis.

The crown‐anus length of the second‐trimester foetuses ranged from 8.2 to 26.80 cm, while that of the third‐trimester foetuses ranged from 33.95 to 42.52 cm. Gestational age was estimated from crown‐anus length (CAL) using the following equation: X = 2.1 (Y + 17), where X represents the gestational age (days) and Y represents the crown‐anus length (cm), as previously described by İşbilir, Kandil, et al. (2024). Based on this equation, the foetuses were estimated to be between 50 and 92 days in the second trimester and 107 and 125 days in the third trimester.

Macroscopic Examination and Morphometric Measurements

In the macroscopic examination, the foetal weights were first measured using a precision scale. The abdominal cavities of the foetuses were carefully opened, and changes in the topographical positions of the testicular tissue were observed, recorded and photographed. The tissues were meticulously dissected and removed from the body cavity. The weights of the testes were measured using a sensitive balance.

Morphometric measurements were performed using a digital calliper (Mitutoyo, CDN‐20C, Japan) and the ImageJ software (Version 1.4). The calculation of the gonadosomatic index (GSI) was conducted based on the method described by Morais et al. (2012).

Light Microscopic Examination

All 17 foetuses were included in the macroscopic and morphometric evaluations. Histological examination was performed on testicular tissues from 12 foetuses (six from the second trimester and six from the third trimester). The remaining five foetuses were used for SEM. Because the foetal testes were very small, the SEM preparation protocol (including fixation, dehydration, drying and gold coating) rendered these specimens unsuitable for subsequent histological processing.

To complete the histological procedures, testicular tissues from six foetuses in the second trimester and six in the third trimester were used. The testes were bisected dorsoventrally to reduce their size. The collected tissues were fixed in 10% formaldehyde solution for 24 h. Following routine histological procedures, the tissues were embedded in paraffin wax to prepare tissue blocks. Sections of 5 µm thickness were stained using Crossmon's trichrome staining method (Crossmon 1937). Images were captured using a light microscope equipped with a camera. After staining, the diameters of 10 well‐defined, round seminiferous cords were measured using ImageJ software at ×40 magnification in five randomly selected, non‐overlapping microscopic fields from each testis. All measurements were performed by the same observer to ensure consistency. The mean values obtained from these measurements were used for statistical analysis.

Scanning Electron Microscopy Examination

The dissected tissue samples were fixed for 24 h in 2.5% glutaraldehyde solution (pH 7.2) for electron microscopic examination. Washing was performed using 0.1 M phosphate buffer. A second fixation step was carried out in osmium tetroxide solution. The tissues were then washed three times with phosphate buffer. Dehydration was performed using a graded ethanol series. The dehydrated tissues were incubated in 96% and 100% ethanol for 30 min each. The samples were then dried in an oven at 60°C. Finally, gold coating was applied to complete the preparation process. The electron microscopy procedures were carried out according to the protocol stated by İşbilir, Özgüden Akkoç, et al. (2024). SEM imaging was conducted using a JEOL JSM‐5600 device at the Central Research Laboratory Application and Research Center of Eskişehir Osmangazi University.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 23.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The normality of the morphometric and histometric data was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed data were analysed using the independent samples t‐test, whereas non‐normally distributed data were analysed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Data are presented as mean ± standard error (SE) throughout the manuscript. Statistical significance was accepted at p < 0.05.

Results

Macroscopic and Morphometric Results

In 50‐ and 53‐day‐old foetuses, it was determined that the testes had begun to separate from the posterior region of the kidneys and had started to descend toward the scrotum. The testes were positioned bilaterally at the roof of the abdominal cavity and ventral to the lumbar vertebrae (Figure 1A). Macroscopic examination revealed poorly defined epididymal structures. In 66‐day‐old foetuses, although the testes continued to descend toward the scrotum accompanied by the gubernaculum testis, they were still located within the abdominal cavity. At Day 75, the testes were still intra‐abdominal but were observed within the inguinal region and the inguinal canal (Figure 1B). At this stage, the epididymis was clearly visible on macroscopic examination. By Day 85, the testes were positioned just anterior to the scrotum (Figure 1C), and by Day 92, they were observed at the external inguinal ring (Figure 1D). In the 107‐day‐old foetus, while the cauda epididymis was inside the scrotum, the testis itself had not yet descended into the scrotum (Figure 2A). In the 114‐day‐old foetus, both the cauda epididymis and the caudal extremity of the testis were located within the scrotum (Figure 2B). At 125 days, the testicular tissue had completely descended into the scrotum (Figure 2C), resembling the position and appearance of an adult testis on macroscopic examination. Although the testes were located within the scrotum, the gubernaculum had not completely regressed, indicating that the process of testicular descent (descensus testis) had not yet been fully completed. Microscopic Examination Results

In both the second and third trimesters, seminiferous cords, which will later differentiate into seminiferous tubules, had formed and constituted the majority of the foetal testicular parenchyma. Although the development of the seminiferous cords progressed during the third trimester, no lumens were observed in the cords in either trimester (Figure 4). In both periods, the seminiferous cords were surrounded by peritubular myoid cells. These cells had elongated shapes and heterochromatic nuclei (Figure 4E,F). In the third trimester, the basement membrane of the seminiferous cords was visible (Figure 4F).

In both trimesters, gonocytes and pre‐Sertoli cells were observed within the seminiferous cords. The cell boundaries of gonocytes and Sertoli cells were indistinct and irregular in shape. Gonocytes, which had round nuclei, were located either centrally or peripherally within the cords. Some of these cells were undergoing mitosis, and gonocytes at different stages of mitosis were detected in the histological sections. The pre‐Sertoli cells, with round euchromatic nuclei, were situated between the gonocytes and around the periphery of the cords (Figure 4E,F).

In both the second and third trimesters, interstitial tissue containing pre‐Leydig cells, blood vessels, mesenchymal cells and fibroblasts was located between the seminiferous cords. In the second trimester, the seminiferous cords and interstitial tissue appeared to form a highly compact structure. Compared with the second trimester, the interstitial tissue increased in the third trimester (Figure 4). In addition, the development of blood vessels was more advanced in the third trimester. Histological sections from this period revealed a greater number and size of blood vessels (Figure 4C,D). Leydig cells, which were observed in clusters within the interstitial tissue, had polygonal shapes with euchromatic nuclei (Figure 4E,F). The presence of eosinophilic granules in the cytoplasm of Leydig cells was notable on gestational Days 62, 68, 73, 85, 92 and 125 (Figure 5). Histometric analysis revealed no statistically significant differences in seminiferous cord diameters between trimesters. Descriptive statistical data and p‐values are presented in Table 2.

Scanning Electron Microscopy Results

On Days 50, 53, 61 and 62 of gestation, star‐shaped mesenchymal cells that would form the testicular framework in the genital ridge were observed. In addition, seminiferous cords formed by the aggregation of mesenchymal cells, and oval‐shaped migrating gonocytes within the mesenchyme were identified. Also noted were the oval‐shaped tubular boundaries formed by the differentiating mesenchymal cells, as well as mesh‐like structures created by the cytoplasmic extensions of the star‐shaped mesenchymal cells (Figure 6).

Magnifications: A ×400; B ×800; C ×800.

In the developing testicular tissue (in the late stages of the second trimester), seminiferous cords entering the maturation process were evident, and clusters of gonocytes beginning to appear within the lumens of the tubules were particularly noteworthy. On Day 85 of gestation, the boundaries of the seminiferous cords were more clearly defined and distinctly visible. In the testicular tissue of a 92‐day‐old foetus, in addition to germ cell clusters lining the lumen, interstitial cells were observed in the differentiating intertubular areas and on the surfaces of the seminiferous cords (Figure 7).

Scanning electron microscopic (SEM) appearance of the testes during the late second trimester. Arrow indicates seminiferous cords; asterisk denotes a cluster of gonocytes; tailed arrow indicates interstitial cells. Magnifications: A ×400; B ×800; C ×800.

With the onset of the third trimester, seminiferous cords were found to be more structurally organized, with lumens filled with gonocytes. Precursor forms of peritubular myoepithelial cells were also identified on the surface of these cords. Alongside the more clearly defined seminiferous cord cells, the intertubular tissue was prominent. By Day 125 of gestation, seminiferous cord cavities showed that germ cell migration into the lumen had been largely completed. A noticeable accumulation of cells in the interstitial tissue was also observed (Figure 8).

Discussion

In our study, a macroscopic, morphometric and microscopic description of the testicular tissue was performed during the foetal period (second and third trimesters) in Hamdani crossbred sheep. In Karadi sheep foetuses with a crown–Rectum length (CRL) of 7.2 and 10 cm, the testes were reported to be located caudal to the kidneys (Amin and Alwan 2010). In goats, by Day 52 of gestation, the testes were found to be positioned at the mid‐ventrolateral region of the corresponding metanephros (Farooqui et al. 2011). In Karadi sheep, when CRL reached 19–20 cm, the testes approached the inguinal ring, and at a CRL of 22 cm, they were observed at the inguinal ring (Amin and Alwan 2010). In goats, on Day 88 of gestation, the testes were located exactly at the margin of the internal inguinal ring, and by Day 89, they had entered the inguinal canal together with the tunica vaginalis (Farooqui et al. 2011). Following these observations, the time of entry into the inguinal canal was estimated as Day 85 in our study.

In sheep, testes were located at the inguinal ring or scrotum within a CRL range of 24–29 cm, and at a CRL of 32 cm, they were identified within the scrotum (Amin and Alwan 2010). Our findings contrast with Gier and Marion's (1970) study, which reported that testes pass the inguinal ring in foetal sheep at approximately 80 days. However, similar to reports in goats (Farooqui et al. 2011) and sheep (Amin and Alwan 2010), we observed the testes at the external inguinal ring on Day 92. Entry into the scrotum was observed on Day 102 in sheep (Amin and Alwan 2010) and Day 101 in goats (Farooqui et al. 2011), consistent with our results. At this stage, within the tunica vaginalis sheath, the testis, epididymis, testicular artery, veins, nerves, lymphatic vessels and vas deferens were present. From a comparative developmental perspective, the general anatomical pathway of testicular descent is largely similar among mammals. However, the timing of entry into the inguinal canal, passage through the external inguinal ring, and scrotal positioning may vary according to species, breed and the foetal age‐staging criteria used. Therefore, the reported differences should be interpreted not as direct contradictions but as species‐ and staging‐related variation (Wakamatsu et al. 2025).

Although Arthur (1964) reported that testicular descent into the scrotum occurs on average between 2.5 and 3 months in sheep, similar to studies in goats (Farooqui et al. 2011) and sheep (Hejazi et al. 2011), we found that on Day 125, the testis had not yet fully reached the distal end of the scrotum. Kumar et al. (1988) reported that scrotal migration began on Day 90 in goats, but in our study, migration was considered to start around Day 100, after Day 92. This finding aligns with the study conducted on goats (Farooqui et al. 2011). While epididymis was identified in sheep at 72.62 days (CRL = 15.5 mm), in our study, macroscopic differentiation of the epididymis was observed on Day 75, consistent with Hejazi et al. (2011). Klonisch et al. (2004) reported the timing of testicular descent into the scrotum as 245 days in humans; at birth in pigs, horses, cattle and sheep; and 21 days in dogs and mice. Therefore, considering the process extending from the external inguinal ring to the scrotum as ‘transinguinal’ and ‘scrotal’ subphases may facilitate interpretation of the timing differences reported in small ruminants (Wakamatsu et al. 2025).

In Karadi sheep, testis weight was reported as 0.015 g at around 50 days of gestation and 1.87 g at 130 days (Amin and Alwan 2010). In our study, testis weight was measured as 0.013 g at 50 days and 0.65 g at 125 days. A statistically significant difference (p < 0.05) was found between the second and third trimesters in the GSI, a parameter used as an indicator of sexual maturity related to the development of ovaries and testes in animals. The higher GSI values observed in third‐trimester foetuses indicate a more advanced stage of gonadal development. In pig foetuses, testis weight during late gestation has also been reported not always to be directly related to foetal size. Therefore, testis weight and GSI should be interpreted together with foetal age, body size and histological maturity (Stenhouse et al. 2022).

Macroscopically, our findings align with those of Hejazi et al. (2011), suggesting that testicular descent occurs at birth in sheep, accompanied by shortening and atrophy of the gubernaculum after the testes have descended into the scrotum. In goat foetuses, collagen fibres and fibroblasts in the gubernaculum were reported to become more organized during testicular migration, while the tissue began to undergo atrophy near birth. This finding supports our macroscopic evaluation indicating regression of the gubernaculum after descent (Hejazi et al. 2024).

Both light microscopy and SEM analyses revealed the formation of seminiferous cords during the second and third trimesters, consistent with studies in humans (Jost et al. 1981). Similar to findings in goat foetuses (Farooqui et al. 2012), the development of the basal membrane in seminiferous cords became more prominent with foetal growth. Our findings are in agreement with previous studies reporting that differentiation of peritubular myoid cells contributes to basal lamina formation in the developing testis (Svingen and Koopman 2013). The basal membrane serves as a connective interface between pre‐Sertoli cells, which are responsible for the formation of the blood‐testis barrier, and peritubular cells (Byskov and Hoyer 1994). Therefore, it is suggested that the pronounced basal membrane observed in the third trimester may be related to the formation of the blood‐testis barrier. In Red Sokoto goat foetuses, seminiferous cords were also reported to be weakly distinguishable in the first trimester but clearly evident in the early second trimester. This finding supports the gradual progression of seminiferous cord development in small ruminants (Udoumoh et al. 2025). In terms of species diversity, Sertoli cells have been reported to be the main structural component of testis cords in mice (Nel‐Themaat et al. 2011), while testicular cords in the human foetal testis have been reported to become distinct between 8 and 14 weeks (Li et al. 2023). In SEM examination, seminiferous cords that were loose and had indistinct borders in the second trimester became more organized in the third trimester. This finding supports the light microscopic results in three dimensions. However, the prominence of the basal membrane alone does not indicate complete formation of the blood‐testis barrier. This is because the blood‐testis barrier is mainly formed through maturation of specialized junctional complexes between adjacent Sertoli cells, and classical studies show that this structure is completed in the postnatal period (Cheng and Mruk 2012). The later completion of Sertoli cell maturation and blood‐testis barrier development in postnatal pigs also supports this interpretation (Zheng et al. 2022).

The SEM examinations enabled detailed visualization of the surface organization of the seminiferous cords, the mesh‐like arrangement of mesenchymal cells, and the three‐dimensional cellular interactions that could not be fully appreciated by conventional light microscopy. These ultrastructural observations complemented the histological findings and provided additional insight into the spatial organization and differentiation of the developing foetal testis. Consequently, SEM contributed to a more comprehensive understanding of prenatal testicular development beyond the information obtained from routine histological examination.

In light microscopic examination, lumen formation was not observed in either developmental period. This finding is consistent with studies reporting lumen formation occurring during the postnatal third week in mice (Nel‐Themaat et al. 2011) and postnatally in humans (Waters and Trainer 1996). Contrary to our findings, Farooqui et al. (2011) reported that sex cords began to form on Day 44 of gestation in goat foetuses, with lumination initiating between Days 78 and 84. These results may suggest a species‐specific developmental pattern. A stereological study conducted in sheep embryos between 9 and 20 weeks reported that seminiferous tubules remained as solid cords without a central lumen and that no marked lumination was observed during the foetal period (Sadeghinezhad et al. 2021). Therefore, the absence of lumen formation in our study suggests that the Hamdani crossbred sheep testis had not yet transitioned into mature seminiferous tubule structure during the examined period.

Similar to studies conducted on foetal bovine testes (Abdel‐Raouf 1960) and sheep testes (Parchami and Fatahian Dehkordi 2008), two cell types were identified in the testicular tissue: primordial cells (gonocytes) with round nuclei located either centrally or peripherally in the seminiferous cords, and pre‐Sertoli cells with round euchromatic nuclei situated between gonocytes and around the seminiferous cords. The presence of gonocytes both in the centre and at the periphery of the cords indicates that migration toward the basal region had not yet been completed and that the testis was still in a pre‐spermatogenic immature stage (Manku and Culty 2015).

Polygonal pre‐Leydig cells with euchromatic nuclei responsible for steroid hormone secretion were observed in the interstitial tissue of the foetal testis, alongside blood vessels, mesenchymal cells and fibroblasts (Sinowatz et al. 1987). Jorgensen and Jiang (2024) reported that pre‐Leydig cells possess well‐developed smooth endoplasmic reticulum and mitochondria to support steroid synthesis. In our study, from Day 62 and continuing through Day 125, the presence of eosinophilic granules within pre‐Leydig cells was notably evident (Abd‐Elmaksoud 2005). These granules are thought to be associated with increased steroidogenic activity of Leydig cells (Ergün et al. 2011). Because steroidogenesis in Leydig cells depends on the coordinated activity of specific enzymes, morphological findings alone do not directly prove active steroid synthesis (Zirkin and Papadopoulos 2018).

A study on male sheep foetuses demonstrated that testosterone levels rise between Days 35 and 70, decline between Days 70 and 90, and remain low until birth (Veiga‐Lopez et al. 2011), suggesting that the peak steroidogenic activity occurs in mid‐gestation. In our study, eosinophilic granules in pre‐Leydig cells were observed from mid‐second trimester and persisted until mid‐third trimester. This observation suggests that these granules may not be directly related to testosterone production. However, further detailed studies are necessary to determine, even indirectly, whether eosinophilic granules have a role in testosterone synthesis.

During early development, endothelial cells originating from the mesonephros migrate into the testis to form a vascular plexus between the cords (Svingen and Koopman 2013). Combes et al. (2009) demonstrated in mice that endothelial migration shapes primary arterial branches extending into the tunica albuginea and interstitium. Similarly, in our study, a prominent interstitial vascular structure and interconnected vascular networks between the cords were observed, increasing in number and size as gestation progressed, thereby confirming these developmental patterns.

This study has several limitations. The number of available foetuses was relatively limited, and the distribution of specimens across gestational ages was uneven because the study relied on naturally available materials collected from slaughterhouses. In addition, first‐trimester foetuses were not available; therefore, the earliest stages of testicular differentiation could not be evaluated. Furthermore, immunohistochemical analyses and hormonal measurements were beyond the scope of the present study. Future studies incorporating these approaches and larger sample sizes would contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of prenatal testicular development in sheep.

Conclusions

This study has comprehensively elucidated the prenatal development of testes in Hamdani crossbred male sheep foetuses at macroscopic, morphometric, histological and SEM levels, detailing the process of testicular organogenesis. As a result, it was determined that testicular descent into the scrotum had not yet been completed by Day 125. Throughout this process, the guiding role of the gubernaculum testis was clearly observed. Macroscopic and morphometric analyses revealed significant increases in testicular size concurrent with positional changes. Histological examinations provided detailed descriptions of seminiferous cord formation and intertubular cellular structures according to trimester stages. In addition, SEM allowed for high‐resolution observation of the testicular tissue's surface characteristics and cellular differentiation, contributing valuable data to the limited body of literature on this subject. These findings facilitate a better understanding of critical stages in foetal testis development and serve as a foundation for translational research in ovine reproductive biology. Furthermore, they provide valuable insights for early identification of testicular anomalies and comparative developmental models.
