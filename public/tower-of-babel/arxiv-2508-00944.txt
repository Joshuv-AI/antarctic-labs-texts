Positivity of Nearly Linearly Recurrent Sequences Thanks: amaury.pouly@cnrs.fr , mahsa@irif.fr Thanks: james.worrell@cs.ox.ac.uk
Amaury Pouly, Mahsa Shirmohammadi, James Worrell

Abstract
In this paper we formulate the Positivity Problem for nearly linear recurrent sequences. This is a generalisation of the Positivity Problem for linear recurrence sequences and is a special case of the non-reachability problem for linear time-invariant systems. Our main contribution is a decision procedure for the Positivity Problem for nearly linear recurrences of order at most 3 whose characteristic roots have absolute value at most one. The decision procedure relies on a new transcendence result for infinite series that is of independent interest.



1
Introduction

A
nearly linearly recurrence sequence (NLRS)
of order
d
is a sequence

\boldsymbol{u}=(u_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
of rational numbers such that
there exist
a_{0},\ldots,a_{d-1}\in\mathbb{Q}
and
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{Q}
satisfying

\displaystyle-\varepsilon\leq u_{n+d}-a_{0}u_{n+d-1}-\cdots-a_{d-1}u_{n}\leq\varepsilon

(1)

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. In the case
\varepsilon=0
we recover the
classical notion of linear recurrence sequence (LRS).
We say that such a recurrence is
bounded
if the characteristic roots of the recurrence have absolute value at most one.

The notion of NLRS was introduced by Akiyama, Evertse, and
Pethő
[
1
]
, motivated by the study of shift radix
numeration systems and discretized rotations in the
plane
[
2
]
. NLRS are considered in
[
1
]

as LRS subject to bounded disturbances, but they can also be seen as
special cases of linear time-invariant systems (a fundamental notion in control
theory
[
10
]
) and linear constraint loops (as studied
in static program analysis
[
21
]
). The preceding notions
all involve discrete-time dynamical systems whose one-step transitions
are specified by systems of linear inequalities relating the before
and after values of the system variables. In the case of linear
time-invariant systems, this allows modelling the possible actions of
an external controller, whereas in static analysis it allows an
abstract treatment of programming constructs, such as conditional
branching, non-linear functions, or size abstractions of data
structures. The non-determinism is such systems makes their analysis particularly
challenging.

We study the
Positivity Problem for NLRS
, where the input is a
recurrence of the form (
1
) and initial values

u_{0},\ldots,u_{d-1}\in\mathbb{Q}
, and the task is to determine
whether every sequence
\boldsymbol{u}
satisfying (
1
)
with the given initial values is positive, meaning that
u_{n}>0
for
all
n
. Evidently the Positivity Problem for NLRS generalises the
Positivity Problem for LRS:
determine whether all terms of a
given LRS are positive
. The decidability of the latter was posed as
an open question some 50 years ago in
[
22
, Section
II.12]
in the context of automata and formal
power series. The Positivity Problem for LRS arises in many different
settings, including program termination
[
19
]
, control
theory
[
10
]
, and stochastic
processes
[
7
,
20
]
. Decidability is known
for LRS of order at most 5
[
18
]
, but remains open in
general. In the references above, one shows hardness of various
different problems by reduction from the Positivity Problem.
In the other direction, we note that
there is a straightforward reduction to the Positivity
Problem of the celebrated
Skolem Problem
determine whether a given LRS has a zero
term

[
12
, Section 1]
.

As shown in Section
2
,
the complement of the Positivity Problem for NLRS of order
d

(“
does there exist some sequence satisfying (
1
) that is not positive?
”)
is a special case of the reachability problem for linear time-invariant systems in dimension
d
.
The decidability of the latter is open already in dimension 2
[
10
, Section 4]
.

The main result of this paper is a decision procedure for the
Positivity Problem for NLRS for bounded recurrences of order at most
3. We reduce the task of determining the positivity of a given NLRS
to that of determining the sign of certain infinite sum. The key
technical contribution is to show that this sum cannot be zero and
therefore that its sign can be determined by numerical
computation. This is achieved via a novel transcendence result. The
proof of the transcendence result combines lower bounds on linear
forms in logarithms together with a technique from
[
16
]
that uses
Schlickewei’s
p
-adic Subspace Theorem in Diophantine approximation
[
23
]
.

1.1
Related Work

The paper
[
1
]
carried out the first fundamental study
of NLRS. Among other things, this work
characterised the possible asymptotic behaviours of NLRS in terms of a Binet-type formula.
It also shows that the analog of the Skolem-Mahler-Lech Theorem on the set of zeros of an LRS fails for NLRS.

A number of recent works consider decision problems for perturbed versions of linear dynamical systems.
The paper
[
3
]
studies the decidability of
robust versions of decision problems on linear recurrence sequences.
That work considers only perturbations in the initial values of the recurrence,
whereas in the present paper we consider perturbations of the recurrence at each time step.
The paper
[
5
]
considers linear dynamical systems with rounding in an attempt to model bounded precision arithmetic.
In that setting, rounding is deterministic and is applied after each transition step.
The main result of
[
5
]
is a decision procedure for a variant of Kannan and Lipton’s orbit problem in the
presence of rounding for hyperbolic linear dynamical systems.

There is a rich body of work on the notion of
chain reachability
in dynamical systems
[
14
, Chapters 6 and 7]
.
This is a notion of reachability that is stable under arbitrarily small
perturbations of the standard dynamics. The paper
[
8
]
shows the decidability of the natural analogue of
the Skolem Problem for LRS with respect to chain reachability.
By contrast, the present work deals with LRS under a fixed perturbation—the quantity
\varepsilon
in Equation (
1
).
The resulting notion of NLRS is a strict generalisation of LRS, and from an algorithmic viewpoint this makes the decision problems
even more challenging than for LRS, whereas decision problems
associated with chain reachability appear to be more tractable and do
not obviously generalise the classical Skolem and Positivity Problems
for LRS.

A significant difference between the present work and those mentioned
in the above paragraph is our use of a transcendence result to prove
decidability. A similar approach was proposed for LTI systems
in
[
10
]
and a first result in this direction
is
[
15
, Theorem 1(ii)]
, which is a version of
Theorem
1
in which the constant
a
is assumed to be
real. Allowing
a
to be an arbitrary algebraic number in
Theorem
1
is necessary for the present application to
positivity of NLRS and involves a different proof strategy.

This paper focusses on nearly linear recurrence sequences. Another
natural generalisation of linear recurrence sequences are holonomic
sequences, which satisfy linear recurrences with polynomial rather
than constant coefficients. These sequences are ubiquitous in
combinatorics, and the developing of algorithms for determining positivity of holonomic
sequences is an active area of research
[
11
,
13
,
17
]
.

2
Decidability of Positivity at Order 3

In this section we give a decision procedure for the Positivity
Problem for NRLS of order at most 3. The procedure depends on a
transcendence result, which is the subject of Sections
3

and
4.2
. We start by recalling some basic facts about
linear recurrence sequences (see
[
9
, Section 1.1]
for details).

2.1
Linear Recurrence Sequences

Let
A\in\mathbb{Q}^{d\times d}
be a
d\times d
matrix of rational numbers and
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{Q}^{d}
.
Then the sequence
(u_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
defined by
u_{n}=\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}A^{n}\boldsymbol{y}
is a linear recurrence sequence
that admits a closed-form representation

u_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}P_{i}(n)\lambda_{i}^{n}
, where

\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k}
are eigenvalues of
A
and

P_{1},\ldots,P_{k}
are non-zero polynomials
with algebraic coefficients. (It turns out that
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k}
are the characteristic roots of the minimum-order linear recurrence satisfied by
(u_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
.)
If the matrix
A
is diagonalisable then the polynomials
P_{i}
are all constant.

A characteristic root
\lambda
of maximum absolute value is called
dominant
.
We recall that
(u_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
is positive only if one of its dominant characteristic roots is positive real
[
22
, Theorem 10.1]
and
that
(\mathrm{sgn}(u_{n}))_{n=0}^{\infty}
is ultimately periodic only if
there exists
M>0
such that
\lambda^{M}
is positive real for every dominant characteristic root
\lambda
.

2.2
Matrix Formulation

As a first step we give a matrix formulation of the nearly linear recurrence (
1
). To this end, for the companion matrix

\displaystyle A:=\begin{bmatrix}a_{0}&a_{1}&a_{2}&\cdots&a_{d-1}\\
1&0&0&\cdots&0\\
0&1&0&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\cdots&0\end{bmatrix}

(2)

we consider the vector recurrence

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}_{n+1}=A\boldsymbol{x}_{n}+r_{n}\boldsymbol{e}_{1}\quad(n\in\mathbb{N},\,r_{n}\in[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon])\,,

(3)

where
\boldsymbol{e}_{1}
denotes the coordinate vector
(1,0,\ldots,0)^{\top}
.
Clearly, every sequence
(u_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
satisfying (
1
) has the form
u_{n}=\boldsymbol{e}_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{n}
for
a solution
(\boldsymbol{x}_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
of (
3
) with initial vector
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}=(u_{d-1},\ldots,u_{0})^{\top}
.
Conversely, for every solution
(\boldsymbol{x}_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
of (
3
), the sequence

(u_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
defined by
u_{n}:=\boldsymbol{e}_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{n}
satisfies (
1
).

We can recast the Positivity Problem for NLRS in terms of vector
recurrences of the form (
3
):
for a given
instance of (
3
) and given initial vector

\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\in\mathbb{Q}^{d}
, is it the case that for all

r_{0},r_{1},r_{2},\ldots\in[\varepsilon,\varepsilon]
the solution
of (
3
) remains in the half-space

\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\boldsymbol{e}_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}>0\}
?
We note that systems of the form (
3
)
are known in control theory as
linear time-invariant (LTI)
systems
. Borrowing terminology from this area we will call the
scalars
r_{n}

controls
. In terms of the
recurrence (
3
), the complement of the Positivity
Problem asks whether there exist
n
and controls

r_{0},\ldots,r_{n-1}\in[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]
such that

\boldsymbol{e}_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{n}\leq 0
. This is an instance of
the reachability problem for LTI
systems
[
4
,
10
]
(in which the target is a
halfspace and the control set is a bounded line segment).

The solution of (
3
) can be written in the form

\boldsymbol{x}_{n}=A^{n}\boldsymbol{x}_{0}+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}r_{n-1-k}A^{k}\boldsymbol{e}_{1}\,.

We thus focus on the positivity of the following sequence over all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and all
choices of
r_{0},r_{1},r_{2},\ldots\in[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]

of controls:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{e}_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{n}=\boldsymbol{e}_{1}^{\top}A^{n}\boldsymbol{x}_{0}+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}r_{n-1-k}\boldsymbol{(}e_{1}^{\top}A^{k}\boldsymbol{e}_{1})

(4)

To further analyse (
4
) we introduce two auxiliary linear recurrence sequences. First, we have the

zero-control LRS

\boldsymbol{u}^{(z)}=\big(u^{(z)}_{n}\big)_{n=0}^{\infty}
, defined by
u^{(z)}_{n}:=\boldsymbol{e}_{1}^{\top}A^{n}\boldsymbol{x}_{0}
. This corresponds to the
evolution of the system in which every control is zero (equivalently, where the recurrence (
1
) holds up to equality).
Secondly, we define the
control LRS

\boldsymbol{u}^{(c)}=(u^{(c)}_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
by
u^{(c)}_{n}:=\boldsymbol{e}_{1}^{\top}A^{n}\boldsymbol{e}_{1}
.
Recall from Section
2.1
that the characteristic roots of
\boldsymbol{u}^{(z)}=\big(u^{(z)}_{n}\big)_{n=0}^{\infty}
and
\boldsymbol{u}^{(c)}=\big(u^{(z)}_{n}\big)_{n=0}^{\infty}

are, in both cases, eigenvalues of
A
.
We can rewrite (
4
) in terms of the zero-control and control LRS as follows:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{e}_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{n}=u_{n}^{(z)}+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}r_{n-1-k}u_{k}^{(c)}\qquad(r_{0},r_{1},r_{2},\ldots\in[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon])\,.

(5)

For a given
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
we can minimise the sum in (
5
) by minimising each summand individually.
The choice of controls
r_{0},\ldots,r_{n-1}\in[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]
that achieves this is
r_{n-1-k}:=\varepsilon
if
u_{k}^{(c)}<0

and
r_{n-1-k}:=-\varepsilon
if
u_{k}^{(c)}\geq 0
.
(The fact that we always choose an endpoint of the interval

[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]
as control is an instance of the
so-called
bang-bang principle
, in which one always chooses an extreme
value of the control set).
It follows that (
4
)
is positive for all
n
and all
r_{0},\ldots,r_{n-1}\in[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]
if and only if the following expression is positive for all
n
:

\displaystyle u^{(min)}_{n}:=u_{n}^{(z)}-\varepsilon\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}|u_{k}^{(c)}|\,.

(6)

2.3
Decision Procedure

We now explain how to determine positivity of the
sequence (
6
) in the case that the matrix
A

in (
2
) has dimension 3 and spectral radius at most one,
which corresponds to bounded NRLS of order 3. There are four main cases.

Case 1: The Zero-Control LRS is not positive.

This is the case that
\boldsymbol{u}^{(z)}
is not positive. (Note
that the positivity of
\boldsymbol{u}^{(z)}
can be determined
effectively, since the Positivity Problem is decidable for LRS of
order at most 5
[
18
]
.) Since

\boldsymbol{u}^{(\mathrm{min})}\leq\boldsymbol{u}^{(z)}
,
inspecting (
6
) we immediately deduce that

\boldsymbol{u}^{(min)}
is not positive.

Case 2: The Control LRS has ultimately periodic sign.

This is the case that there exists a strictly positive integer
M

such that
\lambda^{M}\in\mathbb{R}
for every dominant
characteristic root
\lambda
of
\boldsymbol{u}^{(c)}
. This implies that
\boldsymbol{u}^{(c)}

has ultimately periodic sign. More precisely, there exists
n_{0}
such that for all
n\geq n_{0}

the sign of
u^{(c)}_{n}
is determined by
n\bmod M
.
From (
6
) we see that the sequence
\big(u^{(min)}_{n_{0}+n}\big)_{n=0}^{\infty}
can be expressed as the interleaving of

M
sub-sequences, each an LRS of order at most 3.
Hence, the positivity of
\boldsymbol{u}^{(min)}
can be effectively determined in this case.

Remaining Cases:

Suppose that neither Case 1 nor Case 2 hold.
Since
\boldsymbol{u}^{(z)}
is assumed to be positive, it has
a positive real dominant characteristic root
\rho
.
Since the sign of
\boldsymbol{u}^{(c)}
is assumed to not be ultimately periodic, it has
has pair of complex-conjugate dominant characteristic roots
\lambda,\overline{\lambda}
,
such that
\lambda^{n}\not\in\mathbb{R}
for all
n>0
.
Recalling that
|\lambda|,\rho\leqslant 1
because
the spectral radius of
A
is assumed to be at most one, we divide into two cases.

Case 3:
\rho<1
or
|\lambda|=1
.

We claim that
\boldsymbol{u}^{(min)}
is not positive in this
case. First, suppose that
\rho<1
. Since
\rho
is the dominant root of
\boldsymbol{u}^{(z)}
it follows that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}u_{n}^{(z)}=0
and thus

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}u_{n}^{(min)}=-\varepsilon\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|u_{n}^{(c)}|<0
. Hence
\boldsymbol{u}^{(min)}
is not positive.
Next, suppose that

|\lambda|=1
. By assumption we have
u^{(c)}_{n}=b\rho^{n}+a\lambda^{n}+\overline{a\lambda^{n}}
for
a,b\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
,
with
a
non-zero.
By equidistribution of
\{\lambda^{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
on the unit circle in the complex plane, there exists
\delta>0
such that

|u_{n}^{(c)}|>\delta
for infinitely many
n
. As a consequence,

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|u_{n}^{(c)}|
is infinite while
|u_{n}^{(z)}|
is bounded (having simple characteristic roots of absolute value at most one). We conclude
again that
\boldsymbol{u}^{(min)}
is not positive.

Case 4:
|\lambda|<\rho=1
.

This is the case that
the Zero-Control LRS strictly dominates the Control LRS. We claim that the
limit

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left(u_{n}^{(z)}-\varepsilon\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}|u_{k}^{(c)}|\right)

(7)

exists and is non-zero. On the one hand, we have
u_{n}^{(z)}=c\rho^{n}+d\lambda^{n}+\overline{d\lambda^{n}}
for some
c,d\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
. Hence

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}u_{n}^{(z)}=c
is algebraic. On the other
hand, we have
u_{n}^{(c)}=a\lambda^{n}+\overline{a\lambda^{n}}
for
some non-zero
a\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
and hence

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|u_{n}^{(c)}|
is transcendental by
Theorem
1
, below.
Since
\varepsilon
is rational,
we conclude that the limit (
7
)
is non-zero. This proves the claim. Now we can approximate the limit (
7
) numerically to arbitrary precision
and
\boldsymbol{u}^{(min)}
is positive if and only if the limit is positive.

This completes the description of the decision procedure for the
Positivity Problem of bounded NLRS at order 3. The critical
ingredient in the preceding argument was Theorem
1
:

Theorem 1
.

Let
\lambda,a\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
be algebraic numbers such that
|\lambda|<1
and
\lambda^{n}\not\in\mathbb{R}
for all
n>0
.
Then

\alpha:=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|a\lambda^{n}+\overline{a\lambda^{n}}|

is transcendental.

The proof of Theorem
1
is by contradiction, starting from
the assumption that
\alpha
is algebraic. The details are given in
Sections
3
and
4
. Here we give a brief overview.

The starting point is to observe that the term

a\lambda^{n}+\overline{a\lambda^{n}}
satisfies a three-term linear
recurrence with algebraic coefficients. While the summand

|a\lambda^{m}+\overline{a\lambda^{m}}|
in Theorem
1
is
not itself an LRS, we are able to identify a family of three-term linear
recurrences of successively higher orders such that the sequence

|a\lambda^{m}+\overline{a\lambda^{m}}|
satisfies each recurrence at
“almost all” indices. The construction of the recurrences is based
on Diophantine-approximation properties of the number
\theta\in(0,1)

such that
\lambda=|\lambda|\exp(2\pi i\theta)
.

In the second step of the proof, we find a single linear form

L(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})
involving
coefficients
\alpha
and
\pm 1
and a sequence of tuples
\Lambda_{n}\in\mathcal{O}^{m}
, for some number ring
\mathcal{O}
,
arising from the above-mentioned three-term linear recurrences, such that

L(\Lambda_{n})
converges to zero very quickly as
n
tends to infinity.

In the third step we apply the Subspace Theorem (here is where we use
the assumption that
\alpha
is algebraic) to the linear form
L
. We
thereby obtain another linear form
F
such that
F(\Lambda_{n})=0
for all
n
.
By subtracting a multiple of
F
from
L
we eliminate one of the
variables from
L
to obtain a new simpler linear form

L^{\prime}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})
such that
|L^{\prime}(\Lambda_{n})|=|L(\Lambda_{n})|
for
infinitely many
n
. Crucially, for this new form
L^{\prime}
,

L^{\prime}(\Lambda_{n})
can be expressed as a fixed number of monomials. We
use this fact to derive a lower bound for
|L^{\prime}(\Lambda_{n})|

using Proposition
4
. We conclude by showing that this lower bound
contradicts the previously obtained bounds on the speed of convergence of
L(\Lambda_{n})
to zero.

3
Approximation by Linear Recurrences

In this section we analyse the sequence of numbers
|a\lambda^{n}+\overline{a\lambda^{n}}|
appearing as summand in the series in Theorem
1
.
While this sequence is not an LRS, we exhibit a family of three-term linear recurrences, of successively higher orders,
that the preceding sequence satisfies at almost all indices.

3.1
Continued Fractions

For
\lambda
as in the statement of Theorem
1

let
\theta\in[0,1)
be such that
\lambda=|\lambda|\exp(2\pi i\theta)
, i.e.,
\theta
is the argument of
\lambda
normalised to lie in
[0,1)
.
By the assumption that
\lambda^{n}\not\in\mathbb{R}
for all
n>0
it holds that
\theta
is irrational.
We start with
an analysis of the continued-fraction expansion of
\theta
.

Every
r\in\mathbb{R}

can be written uniquely in the form

r=\lfloor r\rfloor+\{r\}
, where

\lfloor r\rfloor\in\mathbb{Z}
is the
integer part
of
r

and
\{r\}\in[0,1)
is the
fractional part
of
r
. Write also

\|r\|
for the distance of
r
to the nearest integer.
Consider the continued fraction expansion

\theta\,=\,[0;a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},\ldots]\,=\,\cfrac{1}{a_{1}+\cfrac{1}{a_{2}+\cfrac{1}{a_{3}+\cfrac{1}{\ddots}}}}\,,

where each
a_{i}
is a positive integer.
Given
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we write

\frac{p_{n}}{q_{n}}\,=\,[a_{0};a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n}]\,=\,a_{0}+\cfrac{1}{a_{1}+\cfrac{1}{a_{2}+\cfrac{1}{\ddots+\cfrac{1}{a_{n}}}}}

for the
n
-th convergent
of the expansion.
We will need the following three general facts about the convergents of continued fractions:

1.

For all
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have
q_{n}<q_{n+1}
.

2.

For all
n\in\mathbb{N}
the expressions
q_{n}\theta-p_{n}
and
q_{n+1}\theta-p_{n+1}
have opposite sign and

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(a_{n+1}+2)q_{n}}<\left|q_{n}\theta-p_{n}\right|<\frac{1}{a_{n+1}q_{n}}\,.

(8)

3.

We have the
law of best approximation
:

q\in\mathbb{N}
is equal to
q_{n}
for some
n
if and only if

\|q\theta\|<\|q^{\prime}\theta\|
for all
q^{\prime}
with
0<q^{\prime}<q
.

We define the
sequence
(r_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
via the
continued-fraction expansion
[a_{0},a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},\ldots]

of
\theta
as follows. If the expansion is unbounded, then choose

\ell_{1}<\ell_{2}<\cdots
, either all odd or all even, such that

a_{\ell_{n}+1}\geq a_{m}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and all

m\leq\ell_{n}
. This entails that
\frac{a_{{\ell_{n}}+1}}{a_{m+1}+2}\geq\frac{1}{3}

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}

and
m\leq\ell_{n}
. On the other hand,
suppose that the expansion is bounded—say
a_{n}\leq M
for all
n
. Then choose

\ell_{1}<\ell_{2}<\cdots
to be the even natural numbers.
Then
\frac{a_{{\ell_{n}}+1}}{a_{m+1}+2}\geq\frac{1}{M+2}
for all all
n\in\mathbb{N}

and
m\leq\ell_{n}
.
In
either case, there exists a constant
\varepsilon>0
such that
for
all
n\in\mathbb{N}

and
m\leq\ell_{n}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{a_{{\ell_{n}}+1}}{a_{m+1}+2}\geq\varepsilon\,.

(9)

Now define

r_{n}
to be the denominator
q_{\ell_{n}}
of the
\ell_{n}
-th
convergent. Since the
r_{n}
all have the same parity by construction, by Item 2 above we have
that either
\|r_{n}\theta\|=\{r_{n}\theta\}
for all
n
or

\|r_{n}\theta\|=1-\{r_{n}\theta\}
for all
n
. We assume the
former case; the reasoning in the latter case requires minor
modifications. Furthermore, by passing to a tail of the sequence
(r_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
, if necessary,
we may assume also without loss of generality that
\|r_{n}\theta\|<\frac{1}{4}
for all
n
.

Proposition 2
.

For
0\leq q<r_{n}
and
c>0
, if

\|q\theta\|<c\|r_{n}\theta\|
then

q\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{c}r_{n}
.

Proof.

Assume that
\|q\theta\|<c\|r_{n}\theta\|
. Choose the
largest
m
such that
q_{m}\leq q
.
By the law of best approximation we have

5
Conclusion

We have shown decidability of the Positivity Problem for NLRS in the
case of bounded recurrences of order at most 3. The obstacle to removing the assumption of boundedness (all characteristic roots
have absolute value at most one) appears to be the case in which there are three characteristic roots, one real and two
complex, all of the same absolute value, strictly greater than
one. Note that Equation
1
, which defines the notion of
NLRS, is not homogeneous and that one cannot simply scale the
recurrence to make it bounded.

In this paper, we focussed on the problem of determining whether
every
sequence satisfying the recurrence (
