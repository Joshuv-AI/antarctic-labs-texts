ERAS following spine surgery in the elderly: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Seng Pei Khaw, Azlan Marzunisham, Sue Yee Goh, Hassan Jabar, Martin T W Kueh, May Loong Tan, Fahd Adeeb, Zairul AKB, Han Sim Lim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAs the global population ages, Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocols have gained increasing importance for improving recovery, pain control, and reducing surgical morbidity. However, their impact on spinal surgery in the frail and elderly population remains limited.ObjectiveTo compare clinical outcomes of ERAS versus conventional care in elderly patients undergoing spinal instrumentation, with included studies defining elderly as aged ≥ 60 or ≥ 65 years.MethodPubMed, Embase, Scopus, and Cochrane were systematically searched for studies comparing ERAS protocol with conventional care in elderly spinal surgery before June 2025. For dichotomous outcomes, pooled relative risks (RR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported; mean differences (MD) and 95%CIs for continuous outcomes. A random-effects meta-analysis model was used.ResultsTwelve observational studies involving 3184 patients were included. No significant difference in operative time [MD – 7.98 min (95%CI – 18.62, 2.66)] but estimated blood loss was significantly lowered with ERAS [MD – 22.79mL (95%CI – 34.10, – 11.48)]. ERAS was associated with shorter hospital stay [10.47 vs. 12.14 days; MD – 1.67 days (95%CI – 2.51, – 0.82)], earlier ambulation [1.73 vs. 2.50 days; MD – 0.77 days (95%CI – 1.20, – 0.33)], days to first void [1.61 vs. 2.28 days; MD – 0.67 days (95%CI – 1.34, – 0.00)] and first bowel movement [3.41 vs. 3.96 days; MD – 0.55 days (95%CI – 0.96, – 0.14)]. Overall complication rates were reduced [RR 0.67 (95%CI 0.54, 0.83)], particularly for surgical wound-related, gastrointestinal, urological and thromboembolic events. Pain outcomes were largely similar, but more ERAS patients achieved 90-day Minimum Clinically Important Difference (MCID) threshold for leg pain (VAS) and Disability (ODI) scores.ConclusionERAS in elderly spinal surgery appears safe and effective, but findings are limited by retrospective study design and risk of bias. Observed benefits included shorter hospital stays, faster functional recovery, fewer postoperative complications and readmissions. Potential improvements in patient-reported lower limb pain outcomes were also noted.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13018-025-06407-6.Keywords: ERAS, Spine fusion, Spine instrumentation, Aged, Surgical morbidity, Recovery, Meta-analysis

Introduction

In today’s rapidly ageing world, the population aged over 65 was estimated at 700 million in 2019 and is projected to double by 2050 [1]. This demographic shift has made frailty increasingly prevalent, and spine-related conditions have emerged as a significant contributor to the global surgical burden [2, 3].

With advances in technology, degenerative spine surgeries are thought to relieve pain, restore function, and improve quality of life [4]. However, its efficacy in frail patients remains debatable as morbidity risk following surgery is high, undermining these clinical benefits [5, 6]. A systematic review by Baek et al. 2023 reported higher mortality, prolonged hospital stays, and major complications like acute renal failure, myocardial infarction, and reintubation. These findings highlight the need for strategies to mitigate surgical stress and improve outcomes in vulnerable groups, hence the growing focus on the role of Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS).

ERAS is a multimodal perioperative care pathway that combines evidence-based interventions targeting pre-, intra-, and post-operative phases [7]. First introduced in colorectal surgery by Kehlet in 1997, who coined the term ‘fast-track surgery’8, ERAS has since been adopted in numerous specialties and has lately gained attention in spinal surgery [9–11].

Evidence suggest ERAS in spine surgery can shorten hospital stay and time to ambulation while reducing morbidity and pain [12]. Jamshaid et al. 2024 also reported benefits in operative outcomes like blood loss and opioid use [13]. Although less established, similar benefits are evident in elderly subgroups in several observational studies [2, 14, 15].

By streamlining perioperative care, ERAS not only improves outcomes but also reduces healthcare costs, largely by shortening hospital stay. This is especially relevant in the elderly, given the rising healthcare demands in ageing populations with heavier frailty burdens.

At present, evidence on ERAS efficacy in elderly spinal surgery is limited. This systematic review aims to consolidate available data comparing implications of ERAS versus conventional care in spinal surgeries of the elderly.

Methods

Study design

A systematic review was conducted in adherence to AMSTAR [16] and PRISMA guidelines [17]. A literature search was conducted across 4 databases: PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and Cochrane, obtaining all relevant studies comparing ERAS vs. non-ERAS following elderly spinal surgery, up to 28 June 2025. MeSH and Emtree were utilized in the search alongside key terms such as ‘ERAS’, ‘elderly’, and ‘spinal surgery’. A complete set of search terms for each database is provided in supplementary material 1. The protocol of this systematic review was registered prospectively in Prospero (CRD420251083371).

Eligibility and selection criteria

All acquired papers were screened independently by two authors (SPK, HJ) against a set of predetermined eligibility criteria. All included studies, randomized or non-randomized must include ERAS vs. non-ERAS cohorts with extractable data on elderly subsets aged 60 years and above, following spinal surgery. Exclusion criteria include (1) review papers, case reports, and editorial pieces, (2) no comparative data, (3) no age-stratified data on the elderly, and (4) ERAS protocol not clearly defined or not aligned with current acceptable standards of perioperative care. Any discrepancies or disagreements were resolved by consensus or adjudication by a third reviewer. Studies with mean and standard deviation of age ≥ 60 years were considered representative of an elderly cohort and thus included in our analysis. Given the variability in age cut-off, we broadly define elderly patients as individuals aged 60 years and older, in line with the United Nations arbitrary threshold [18]. By adopting flexibility, our study sample ensures a greater degree of representation among elderly surgical patients.

Data extraction and outcome

Data extraction was independently carried out by two review authors (SPK, AA). All data pertaining to the study, patient baseline characteristics, and reported outcomes were extracted for both the ERAS and non-ERAS cohorts for comparative analysis. The primary outcomes of interest for this systematic review were (1) length of hospital stay (2) overall postoperative complication and major complication categories (3) 30- and 90-day readmission rates. The secondary outcomes included (1) operative characteristics (2) functional recovery parameters (3) patient-reported outcomes. Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) and Visual Analog Scale (VAS) and (4) achievement of the minimal clinically important difference (MCID), defined as reduction of 12.8 points for ODI, 1.2 points for VAS back pain, 1.6 points for VAS leg pain. Reports with overlapping surgical terminologies and institutional origin were assessed carefully if they referred to the same study. Where suspected, only one study per outcome was included to avoid duplicate effects.

Statistical analysis

The effect measures were calculated using relative risk (RR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) for dichotomous outcomes. For continuous variables, mean difference (MD) 95% CI was used. Where varying units of measure were used to report an outcome, the standardized mean difference (SMD) was used instead. Statistical analysis was primarily done using Review Manager® software, with a random effects meta-analysis model preferred to account for heterogeneity. Meta-analytic effects were generated by applying the Mantel-Haenszel method for categorical data and the inverse variance method for continuous data. A significance level of p < 0.05 was applied. To consolidate a dichotomous dataset with zero events, a + 0.5-continuity correction will be applied for OR calculations, as per Cochrane guidelines [19]. For studies that report median and interquartile range (IQR), Wan et al. method of estimation was used to generate the approximate mean and standard deviation [20]. Sensitivity analysis was performed to assess the robustness of the findings by excluding studies with potential source of heterogeneity (critical bias, frailty, outlier). Where data allowed, subgroup analyses were performed by surgical stratum and geographic location to examine subgroup-specific effects.

Quality assessment

All studies included in the final analysis were assessed independently by two authors (SP, SYG) for risk of bias using ROB 2 tool for randomized control trials and ROBINS-I for non-randomized studies of interventions, as per the Cochrane guidelines [19]. Subsequently, certainty of evidence for key outcomes was appraised using GRADE assessment, considering the risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. Any disagreements were resolved by consensus.

Results

Study and patient characteristics

Our search identified 906 publications across 4 databases: PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and Cochrane. Subsequent title, abstract and full text screening included 614 and 55 reports, respectively. All records were thoroughly assessed against a set of predefined eligibility criteria. Twelve observational studies were included in the final analysis [14, 15, 21–30]. Figure 1 (PRISMA diagram) outlined the study selection process. The full search strategy is in supplementary material 1. Overall, 3,184 elderly patients underwent spinal surgery, of which 1,831 and 1,353 patients were in the ERAS and non-ERAS cohorts, respectively. Three studies incorporated propensity score matching (PSM) [14, 22, 26]. All studies incorporated well-defined ERAS criteria ranging from 10 to 22 components spanning pre-, intra-, and post-operative domains. Supplementary material 18 summarized the breakdown of ERAS components across included studies.

PRISMA flowchart

The mean age of patients in the ERAS and non-ERAS cohorts is 70.5 (range: 65.0–79.9) years and 69.3 (range: 64.2–79.3) years, respectively. BMI was 26.7 kg/m2 in ERAS (range: 24.2–29.4) and 25.8 kg/m2 in non-ERAS (range: 24.7–30.1). The proportion of males is 44.5% in ERAS and 39.5% in non-ERAS. Baseline comorbidities were primarily assessed using the ASA classification, with proportions of ASA III-IV being similar in both groups. [ERAS 26.0% vs. non-ERAS 18.1%].

Surgical procedures largely include cervical fusion (ERAS 7.8% vs. non-ERAS 8.7%), posterior lumbar fusion (47.2% vs. 63.0%), thoracolumbar procedure (20.3% vs. 17%), and spinal decompression (24.6% vs. 9.0%). Minimally invasive fusion techniques were specifically mentioned in only two studies [27, 30]. Table 1 provides a full summary of the study and patient characteristics.

All included studies were retrospective cohorts, judged at moderate to high risk of bias, reflecting the inherent limitations of retrospective studies. No critical risk of bias, hence all studies were methodologically adequate. Funnel plots for operation time and estimated blood loss showed no obvious asymmetry (supplementary material 19). For other outcomes, publication bias was not formally assessed as fewer than 10 studies were available.

Primary outcomes

Length of hospital stay and readmission

The mean length of hospital stay following elderly spinal surgery was significantly shorter for patients receiving ERAS [6.92 vs. 10.58 days]. Figure 2 showed the pooled estimate MD – 1.67 days (95%CI – 2.51, – 0.82) p = 0.0001]. 30-day readmission rates were comparable between ERAS and non-ERAS [RR 1.21 (95%CI 0.59, 2.48) p = 0.60]. Although our analysis suggested a statistically significant reduction in 90-day readmissions [RR 0.33 (95%CI 0.12, 0.89), p = 0.03] among ERAS patients, this finding should be interpreted with caution given the lack of difference at 30 days, which is the more clinically relevant timeframe for ERAS pathways. The corresponding forest plots are shown in Fig. 3.

Readmission rate

Mortality and postoperative complications

All three studies that reported mortality documented zero deaths in both groups [15, 27, 29].

The overall complication rates in the ERAS cohort were significantly reduced [RR 0.67 (95%CI 0.54, 0.83) p = 0.0003] (Fig. 4). Seven studies reported wound-related complications, which is shown to be statistically significant [RR 0.68 (95%CI 0.52, 0.90) p = 0.007]. Spinal leak and epidural hematoma were not significant between the two groups. Figure 5 shows the forest plots for local complications.

Overall complications

Local complication

In terms of major organ complications, we found that the risk of neurological [RR 0.85 (95%CI 0.41, 1.76) p = 0.66], cardiac [RR 0.90 (95%CI 0.39, 2.08) p = 0.80], and pulmonary [RR 0.59 (95%CI 0.29, 1.24) p = 0.16] complications were not different between the groups (Fig. 6). However, the risk of gastrointestinal (GI) complications was significantly lowered by approximately 52% in ERAS [RR 0.47 (95%CI 0.32, 0.68) p < 0.0001]. The GI complications predominantly comprised nausea, vomiting, ileus, and constipation. Figure 6 shows the forest plots.

Major organ system complications

The risk of urinary tract infection [RR 0.34 (95%CI 0.14, 0.81) p = 0.02] and urinary retention [RR 0.50 (95%CI 0.29, 0.85) p = 0.01]] was also significantly reduced in ERAS. There is also a significant reduction in thromboembolic events [RR 0.28 (95%CI 0.09, 0.81) p = 0.02]. (See supplementary materials 5–6 for forest plots)

In terms of reoperation rates following spinal surgery, no significant difference was noted between the two groups [RR 1.53 (95%CI 0.19, 12.13) p = 0.68] (supplementary material 7).

Additional outcomes

Short-term operative characteristics

A total of eight studies reported the operation time (OT). The pooled estimates showed no significant difference in the duration of surgery between the ERAS and non-ERAS group [MD – 7.98 min (95%CI – 18.62, 2.66) p = 0.14]. In contrast, intraoperative blood loss in the ERAS group, although statistically significant, showed a modest reduction [MD -22.79 mL (95%CI – 34.10, – 11.48) p < 0.0001]. The difference is unlikely to have a major clinical impact. However, both groups had similar levels of intraoperative transfusion [MD – 10.63 ml (95%CI-22.10, 0.84) p = 0.07]. Supplementary material 8 shows the corresponding forest plots. These findings should be interpreted with caution, as many included studies reported mixed cervical and lumbar cohorts, with varying fusion level. Subgroup analyses (supplementary material 20) stratified by procedure type are presented below.

Recovery parameters and disability outcome

Six studies reported the mean time to first ambulation with ERAS group achieving earlier ambulation [1.30 days (≈ 31 h) vs. 2.50 days (≈ 60 h); MD – 0.77 days ≈ 18.5 h; (95%CI – 1.20, -0.33) p = 0.0005]. Furthermore, the time taken to first void [1.61 days (≈ 39 h) vs. 2.28 days (≈ 55 h); MD – 0.67 days ≈ 16 h (95%CI – 1.34, – 0.00) p = 0.05], and first bowel movement [3.41 days (≈ 82 h) vs. 3.96 days (≈ 95 h); MD – 0.55 days ≈ 13 h (95%CI – 0.96, – 0.14) p = 0.009] were also shorter in the ERAS group. When assessing in terms of the change in Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) between postoperative day 0 to 90, our results showed no significant difference [MD + 1.45 points (95%CI – 2.78, 5.68) p = 0.50]. Forest plots detailing the recovery parameters are shown in supplementary material 9. Two studies also reported the proportion of patients meeting the Minimum Clinically Important Difference (MCID) threshold based on ODI assessment at 90 days (supplementary material 9). Findings revealed a significantly higher proportion of MCID achieved in the ERAS group, approximately 27% higher MCID rate in the ERAS group [RR 1.27 (95%CI 1.09, 1.48) p = 0.003].

Postoperative pain outcomes

Pain outcomes were predominantly assessed by three studies, using the visual analogue scale (VAS) for back and leg pain across multiple postoperative periods ranging from POD 0–3, 30 days, 90 days, and 180 days. All timepoints showed non-significant changes in VAS reported pain outcomes. (supplementary material 10–11)

However, when assessing improvements in VAS leg pain scores using the MCID threshold, ERAS achieved 23% increased likelihood [RR 1.23 (95%CI 1.03, 1.48) p = 0.02]. The proportion of patient VAS scores for back pain that met the MCID threshold was not significant (supplementary material 12).

Postoperative opioid usage was not significant. The mean difference in daily opioid use between the 2 groups was − 4.15 mg (95%CI – 20.08, 11.78; p = 0.61) (supplementary material 13–14).

Due to limited reporting and variation in surgical approaches, our conclusion regarding pain, ODI an opioid use should be interpreted with caution.

Postoperative drain removal and discharge outcome

In addition, the mean timing for drain removal, postoperatively, was reported by three studies and found to be no different between the 2 groups. [MD – 0.35 days (95%CI – 1.31, 0.60) p = 0.47] (supplementary material 15).

The discharge outcomes showed no difference in the patients needing nursing or rehabilitation care following discharge [RR 0.52 (95%CI 0.19, 1.45) p = 0.21]. The forest plots are shown in supplementary material 16.

Certainty of evidence

GRADE approach was used to assess certainty of evidence for key outcomes with results summarized in the Summary of Findings table (Table 2). Overall, certainty was predominantly downgraded due to risk of bias in the included observational studies, with additional downgrading for heterogeneity in few outcomes.

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the three studies that focused exclusively on frail patients (supplementary material 17a) [14, 22, 26]. Compared to the pooled analysis, operative time became significantly shorter in ERAS implementation. Recovery outcomes including LOS, time to first ambulation and time to first bowel movement remained significantly improved in ERAS group. Regarding complication rates, gastrointestinal complications remained reduced by ERAS. When limited to studies judged at moderate risk of bias (n = 4), ERAS was still associated with reduced length of stay, blood loss, gastrointestinal and urinary complications, but benefits for wound complications, and DVT were no longer significant (supplementary material 17b). These findings should be interpreted cautiously, given the limited number of studies.

Subgroup analysis of short vs. long level spine procedure

Subgroup analysis of short vs. long level spine procedure showed that ERAS modestly reduced blood loss in short level cases [MD – 12.43 mL (95%CI – 32.79, 7.54) p = 0.22], and more substantially in long-level procedures [MD – 183.35 mL (95%CI – 348.92, – 17.78) p = 0.03] with significant subgroup difference (p = 0.04). There was a similar operation time, length of stay, functional outcomes, and postoperative complications (supplementary material 20).

Subgroup analysis of geographic influence

Subgroup analysis of geographic influence was also performed (supplementary material 21). ERAS protocol in the China group had a significantly shorter hospital stay [MD – 2.34 days (95%CI – 2.98, – 1.70) p < 0.000001] but statistically non-significant in the USA group [MD − 0.14 days (95% CI − 1.36, 1.07) p = 0.82]. Subgroup difference was significant (p = 0.0002). Operation time, blood loss, functional outcomes, readmission, and postoperative complications were similar regardless of geographic location.

Postoperative complications by ERAS status

The distribution of postoperative complications in the ERAS and non-ERAS groups was further analyzed (See Fig. 7). Nine studies were included, with a total of 600 complications and a lower incidence in the ERAS group (ERAS 226 vs. non-ERAS 374). The predominant burden was represented by wound-related complications for both groups [ERAS 34.1%; non-ERAS 29.1%]. Furthermore, gastrointestinal complications (including nausea and vomiting) accounted for a significant proportion of major organ system complications but reported a significantly lower proportion in ERAS (21.1%) compared to non-ERAS (26.8%). Although constituting a modest fraction, urological complications and deep vein thrombosis (DVT) [ERAS 3.7%; non-ERAS 5.8%] were also lower in the ERAS group. Spinal leak, pulmonary, cardiovascular, and neurological deficits were amongst the least reported and revealed comparable proportions in both groups.

Discussion

Postoperative recovery and complications in spinal surgeries remain a significant clinical challenge, particularly among elderly patients. In recent years, ERAS protocols have gained momentum as a preventive strategy to address these challenges. However, their outcomes are still not well established Our study findings suggest that ERAS provides several important benefits, including fewer postoperative complications, improved intraoperative outcomes and functional recovery.

Postoperative complication remains a major contributor to perioperative failure, and in the elderly, this risk is further amplified [31, 32]. Our findings showed that ERAS protocols are effective at reducing post-operative complications, demonstrating their role as a key driver in improving the quality of perioperative care for older patients. These findings are consistently corroborated by a growing body of observational studies. Although not exclusively focused on elderly patients, a meta-analysis by Bansal et al. reported ERAS implementation significantly lowered complication rates, shortened hospital stay, but maintained comparable readmission and mortality rates; findings that aligned with our age-stratified outcomes [33]. In particular, we reported approximately a 37% reduction in overall complications, with most improvements attributed to surgical wound-related, gastrointestinal, UTI, and thromboembolic events. The rationale for this can be directly traced back to various components of ERAS, such as promoting early mobilization and catheter removal, aseptic wound handling, and an opioid-sparing approach to maintain gut motility. Opioids have been unequivocally identified as major contributors to postoperative nausea, vomiting, constipation, and urinary retention, thereby reinforcing the importance of judicious prescribing as emphasized in ERAS care [23, 34, 35].

Despite lower 90-day readmission rates with ERAS, the absence of corresponding benefits at 30 days made this finding less robust. Some studies did, however, report reduced readmissions, likely driven by fewer postoperative complications, especially preventable infections like urinary tract infection and wound-related complications [21, 24, 36, 37].

Our findings demonstrated a statistically significant, though modest, reduction in blood loss with ERAS protocols. Variability in surgical techniques, fusion levels, and ERAS components may explain the heterogeneity seen across studies. Nevertheless, strategies such as the routine use of tranexamic acid (TXA) and intraoperative normothermia likely contributed to this benefit, in line with recent meta-analyses [37] [38, 39]. While operative time was similar in both groups, the true effect is difficult to ascertain due to variation in surgical technique, approach, and level of complexity between studies.

Functional recovery parameters, such as time to first ambulation (– 18 h), voiding (– 16 h), and bowel movement (– 13 h) also showed significant improvement, highlighting the role of ERAS in mitigating physiological stress and restoring function. Although these differences appear modest, they are particularly important in elderly patients who have reduced physiological reserves and increased vulnerability to frailty. Delayed recovery in this subgroup can lead to clinical deterioration and lasting impacts on quality of life [40, 41]. Early ambulation also reduces thromboembolic events and serves as a reliable predictor of QoL, shorter hospitalization, and fewer readmissions [42–44].

While length of stay was consistently reduced with ERAS, we observed geographic differences. For instance, the effect was greater in Chinese hospitals compared to the USA. Similar disparities in effect size across various surgeries are evident in prior meta-analyses, likely reflecting differences in healthcare structure and discharge process [45, 46].

Postoperative multimodal analgesia was implemented in all included ERAS cohorts and is widely perceived to reduce postoperative pain and opioid use [47, 48]. However, our meta-analysis showed similar postoperative pain improvements and opioid use in both groups. But due to heterogeneity in outcomes reported, only three studies were included in our pooled analysis for postoperative pain relief thus, our findings are preliminary and merit further exploration. Furthermore, given the subjectivity of VAS assessment and advances in standard care practices over time, it is likely that the gap in pain outcomes between ERAS and non-ERAS may be diminishing, as supported by the mixed findings reported in the existing literature [12, 49, 50].

Data on pain and disability scores were also limited in our study, thus, assessing using MCID outcomes offered supportive insights, showing a greater proportion of the ERAS cohort achieved MCID for improvements in ODI and VAS leg pain scores.

Several limitations to our study must be acknowledged. All included studies were retrospective cohort studies subject to inherent confounding and selection bias. Three studies attempted to mitigate this effect by employing propensity score matching [14, 22, 26]. Variation in surgical approach and ERAS protocols remains a major source of heterogeneity, as with previously published reviews [50]. Recent narrative reviews, such as Naftalovich et al., have emphasized the need for standardized ERAS pathways in spine surgery to allow more consistent evaluation of outcomes [51]. Evidently, some included protocols omitted nutritional optimisation, smoking/alcohol cessation and use of formal discharge criteria. These components have important implications associated with wound healing, infectious complications, and readmission rates [52–54]. Nevertheless, the standard perioperative ERAS components such as patient education, nutrition assessment, tranexamic acid, CBD management, early ambulation and feeding were consistently implemented across included studies, thus contributing to the overall clinical benefits observed (supplementary material 18). Additionally, several of the included studies involved a mixture of spine procedures (e.g., short- and long-level fusions, cervical and thoracolumbar), which likely contributed further to variability in outcomes. Age cutoff for “elderly” varied slightly across studies (≥ 60 or ≥ 65 years), which may have introduced additional heterogeneity. Due to limited reporting, our analysis did not account for outcomes associated with cost, direct measures of QoL, and other long-term functional parameters. Although patient characteristics were relatively balanced, some included studies had relatively small sample sizes, affecting its representativeness. The findings, although they highlight the value of ERAS in optimizing elderly spinal surgery, the overall certainty of evidence ranged from moderate to low. Only three studies reported frail only cohorts [14, 22, 26].

Our study represents a novel lens at consolidating the literature on elderly spinal surgery, emphasizing the safety and efficacy of ERAS implementation in this susceptible subgroup. There have been several published systematic reviews on ERAS in spinal surgery. However, to date, none have thoroughly explored its clinical utility in the elderly patient population [12, 50, 55]. Our findings support ERAS implementation in elderly patients, showing considerable improvements in complication rates, intraoperative blood loss, and recovery parameters with potential benefits in pain control. We intend for our study to lay the groundwork for future research to explore and broaden its implementation and promote new personalized, age-specific strategies, which may pave way for safer surgeries in the future. Ongoing trials and multicentred prospective studies [56–60] will provide a more comprehensive overview of short and long-term ERAS-mediated outcomes for elderly patients, such that we can ascertain its benefits.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis present comprehensive findings supporting the safety and efficacy of ERAS implementation in elderly patients undergoing spinal surgery. There is a significantly shorter hospital stay and faster recovery of functional parameters. Most notably, there was a substantial reduction in postoperative complications, particularly those related to readmission, surgical wound, gastrointestinal, DVT, and UTI. There may be improvements in lower limb pain control. However, as all included studies were retrospective cohorts, judged at moderate to serious risk of bias, further randomized controlled trials and prospective studies are warranted to confirm the observed benefits.
