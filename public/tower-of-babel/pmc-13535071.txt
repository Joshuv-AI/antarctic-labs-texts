Postoperative Depression, Anxiety, and Stress in Relation to Health-Related Quality of Life Among Patients Undergoing Total Knee Replacement in Jordan
Authors: Wael Al-Tamimi, Majdi Alhadidi, Omar Alqaisi, Mohammed Dibas, Maha Subih, Ahmad Al-Rawashdeh, Patricia Tai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUp to 25% of patients undergoing total knee replacement (TKR) for end-stage knee osteoarthritis report persistent pain, functional limitation, and reduced health-related quality of life (HRQoL). Although depression, anxiety, and stress are recognized determinants of surgical outcomes, their postoperative impact remains poorly studied in the Arab world, with no multicenter data from Jordan.ObjectivesThis study aimed to assess the postoperative depression, anxiety, and stress and their association with HRQoL among TKR patients in Jordan, and to identify independent predictors of HRQoL.MethodsA cross-sectional descriptive correlational design was used. A total of 140 adults were recruited from two government orthopedic hospitals in Jordan. Assessments were conducted at a single postoperative time point, 6–12 weeks following TKR surgery. Postoperative depression, anxiety, and stress were measured using the validated Arabic DASS-21 (α = 0.94), and HRQoL was assessed using the Arabic SF-12 (α = 0.84). Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, Pearson correlation, and stepwise multiple linear regression.ResultsParticipants demonstrated moderate depression (M = 18.66, SD = 7.18) and anxiety (M = 14.13, SD = 7.63), and mild stress (M = 17.17, SD = 7.65) post-TKR. The mean HRQoL score was 39.56 (SD = 10.07). A statistically significant negative correlation was found between psychological distress and HRQoL (r = −0.27, p = 0.001). Multiple regression identified body mass index (BMI) (B = −0.382, p < 0.001) and postoperative psychological distress (DASS-21 total score; B = −0.054, p < 0.001) as independent predictors of HRQoL, explaining 17.7% of total variance. Educational level and comorbidities were significantly associated with postoperative depression, anxiety, and stress.ConclusionPostoperative TKR patients in Jordan demonstrated poor overall HRQoL alongside moderate psychological distress. Routine psychological screening and targeted multidisciplinary interventions addressing modifiable factors such as BMI and psychological distress may help improve patient outcomes following TKR.

Introduction

Total knee replacement (TKR) is a clinically effective and cost-efficient surgical intervention for end-stage knee osteoarthritis. 1 Despite high surgical success rates, up to 25% of patients continue to experience chronic pain, compromised postoperative functioning, and reduced quality of life following the procedure. 2 Predictors of poor postoperative outcomes can be classified into modifiable and non-modifiable categories. 3 Non-modifiable risk factors such as younger age and greater comorbidity burden have been extensively studied, whereas modifiable factors, including preoperative pain intensity and postoperative depression, anxiety, and stress, are increasingly recognized as significant determinants of persistent postoperative pain. 4 These modifiable factors are of particular clinical interest, as targeted interventions such as pain neuroscience education or physical therapy may alter their trajectory. 5

Multiple studies have examined pain, physical function, psychological factors, and comorbidities following TKR.6-8 Health-related quality of life (HRQoL) reflects an individual’s subjective perceptions of physical and mental health, including mood, energy, social functioning, and socioeconomic status, and their collective impact on overall well-being. Evaluating HRQoL following TKR thus provides a comprehensive assessment of surgical outcomes beyond objective physical measures.

A critical step in improving patient outcomes after TKR is identifying modifiable risk factors for poor results. 8 Literature has identified several factors, such as pain catastrophizing; however, targeted interventions addressing these factors have not consistently demonstrated advantages in terms of pain or function compared with standard postoperative care. 9 This suggests that risk factors may exert a more complex influence on postoperative outcomes than previously considered. 8 Evaluating both preoperative and acute postoperative risk factors has been shown to improve the prediction of chronic post-surgical pain, 10 and certain factors, such as postoperative self-efficacy, may be more predictive of outcomes when assessed during the postoperative phase rather than preoperatively. 11

Over the past decade, psychosocial factors including pain catastrophizing, anxiety, and depression have been consistently linked to unfavorable TKR outcomes. 12 Preoperative anxiety and depression are major predictors of poor surgical outcomes and patient dissatisfaction.13,14 Additionally, lower preoperative mental health status has been associated with worse postoperative functioning and diminished HRQoL. 15 However, evidence on the influence of psychological factors assessed postoperatively remains limited, 7 particularly in the Arab world, including Jordan, where no recent multicenter data exist on postoperative psychological outcomes among TKR patients.

Sociodemographic characteristics further shape the relationship between postoperative depression, anxiety, and stress and HRQoL. Factors such as age, gender, educational attainment, body mass index (BMI), and comorbidities, particularly hypertension and diabetes mellitus, have been associated with differential postoperative outcomes.7,16,17 Understanding these interactions is essential for identifying high-risk subgroups and developing targeted, patient-centered postoperative care pathways. Accordingly, this study aimed to investigate postoperative psychological distress and its association with HRQoL among patients undergoing TKR in Jordan. Secondary objectives included identifying predictors of HRQoL and exploring associations between selected sociodemographic variables and both postoperative depression, anxiety, and stress and quality-of-life outcomes.

Methods

Study Design

A quantitative, cross-sectional, descriptive, correlational design was employed. Cross-sectional designs are appropriate for examining the degree and direction of relationships between two or more variables at a single point in time. The study was conducted between January 2023 and December 2023. This study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement for cross-sectional studies.

Study Setting

The study was conducted in the orthopedic departments of two government hospitals in Jordan: Al-Salt Government Hospital in Al-Balqa Governorate and Al-Basheer Hospital in Amman, with a combined capacity of 2,275 beds. Both facilities were selected for their specialization in orthopedic surgery, capacity to manage a high volume of TKR patients, adherence to ethical research standards, and provision of multidisciplinary postoperative care.

Study Sampling

A convenience sampling strategy was adopted. Eligible participants were enrolled consecutively during morning and evening shifts. Inclusion criteria required participants to be: (1) Jordanian nationals who had undergone TKR and were Arabic-speaking; (2) aged 18 years or older; (3) willing to provide written informed consent; and (4) capable of completing the self-administered questionnaires independently or with assistance from a family member or healthcare provider. Patients with cognitive impairment, those with serious or unstable medical conditions, those who had a pre-existing, formally diagnosed psychiatric disorder (including major depressive disorder, generalized anxiety disorder, or bipolar disorder) that was clinically active prior to surgery, and those who declined participation were excluded. It is acknowledged that subclinical psychological symptoms prior to surgery were not systematically assessed using structured clinical interviews, which represents a limitation of the current study design.

Sample Size

Sample size was estimated using G*Power software, specifying a linear multiple regression model with an alpha level of 0.05, statistical power of 0.80, and a medium effect size (f2 = 0.15). 18 These parameters yielded a minimum required sample of 128 participants. Allowing for approximately 10% nonresponse or incomplete data, the final target sample was set at 140 participants. The medium effect size was based on prior literature reporting associations between psychological factors and HRQoL in TKR populations. While a pilot-study-derived SD-based calculation would have been preferable, the achieved sample of 140 participants exceeded the minimum required (n = 128) and provided sufficient power for the planned analyses. The observed SD of 10.07 for the HRQoL score in this study may serve as a benchmark for future sample size calculations in similar populations.

Instruments

Data were collected using a three-part self-administered questionnaire:

Part 1: Sociodemographic and Clinical Data: A researcher-developed form capturing age, sex, marital status, educational attainment, monthly family income, residential location, BMI, and presence of comorbidities (diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and cardiovascular disease).

Part 2: Depression, Anxiety and Stress Scale (DASS-21): A validated 21-item self-report instrument measuring the severity of depressive, anxious, and stress-related symptoms over the preceding week. Each subscale comprises seven items rated on a four-point Likert scale (0 = “did not apply to me at all” to 3 = “applied to me very much”), with higher scores reflecting greater psychological distress. The original English version demonstrated high internal consistency for the depression (α = 0.94), anxiety (α = 0.87), and stress (α = 0.91) subscales. 19 The Arabic version has demonstrated strong reliability and validity in Arabic-speaking populations. 20

Severity levels were interpreted according to the DASS-21 scoring guidelines described by Lovibond (1995). 21 After summing the responses for each subscale, the scores were multiplied by two to obtain the final scores. Depression scores were classified as normal (0–9), mild (10–13), moderate (14–20), severe (21–27), and extremely severe (≥28). Anxiety scores were classified as normal (0–7), mild (8–9), moderate (10–14), severe (15–19), and extremely severe (≥20). Stress scores were classified as normal (0–14), mild (15–18), moderate (19–25), severe (26–33), and extremely severe (≥34).

Part 3: Short Form Health Survey (SF-12): A 12-item self-report instrument assessing HRQoL across eight domains and commonly yielding two summary scores: the Physical Component Summary (PCS) and the Mental Component Summary (MCS). 22 Norm-based PCS and MCS scores are standardized to a population mean of 50 (SD = 10), with higher scores indicating better health-related quality of life; scores below 50 indicate health status below the reference-population average. 22 The Arabic version was used, which has demonstrated adequate reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.84). 23

Ethical Considerations

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Al-Zaytoonah University of Jordan (Approval No. 2024-2023/08/62) and the Jordanian Ministry of Health (Approval No. 3105/2024). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. 24 All participants provided written informed consent and were informed of their right to withdraw without consequence to their care. Participant anonymity was maintained through coded identifiers, and all data were stored securely with access restricted to the principal investigator. Permissions to use the DASS-21 and SF-12 instruments were obtained from the respective developers.

Pilot Study

Prior to the main study, a pilot study was conducted with 30 TKR patients from a hospital not included in the main sample to assess the feasibility, clarity, and time requirements of the data collection instruments. Participants required approximately 20 minutes to complete all questionnaires, and all items were readily understood.

Data Collection

Following receipt of ethical and institutional approvals, facilitation letters were dispatched to the designated hospitals, and meetings were arranged with nursing officers and department heads to coordinate recruitment. Questionnaire packages, including informed consent forms, were administered to eligible patients at a single postoperative assessment time point, specifically during their routine outpatient follow-up visit between 6 and 12 weeks following TKR surgery. This time point was selected as it represents a clinically important period when patients are engaged in active rehabilitation yet continue to experience significant postoperative adjustment. All participants were assessed at this single time point; no preoperative or longitudinal follow-up assessments were conducted in this study.

Data Analysis

Data were entered and analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 26. Descriptive statistics, frequencies, means, and standard deviations were computed for all study variables. Pearson correlation coefficients were used to examine associations between sociodemographic characteristics, postoperative depression, anxiety, and stress, and HRQoL. Multiple linear regression analysis using the stepwise method was employed to identify independent predictors of HRQoL. Multicollinearity was assessed using tolerance and Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) values, and autocorrelation was examined via the Durbin-Watson statistic. For the SF-12, a single composite HRQoL score was used in the present analyses; PCS and MCS were not analyzed separately. Descriptive and correlational analyses are presented for this composite score, alongside item-level means, for transparency. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Participant Characteristics

A total of 140 participants were enrolled in this study (Table 1). The sample comprised slightly more females (50.7%) than males (49.3%), with the majority being married (70.7%). The mean age was 66.65 years (SD = 7.85), and the mean body mass index (BMI) was 28.05 kg/m2 (SD = 3.10), which falls within the overweight range. Regarding educational attainment, 41.4% held a diploma-level qualification, and 38.6% had secondary school education or lower. The most prevalent comorbidity was hypertension (25.7%), followed by diabetes mellitus (22.1%) and concurrent hypertension with diabetes mellitus (22.1%). Level of Health-Related Quality of Life

Overall health-related quality of life (HRQoL), as measured by the SF-12, had a mean score of 39.56 (SD = 10.07; range, 12–75). The highest mean score at the item level was recorded for “Have you felt calm and peaceful?” (M = 3.16, SD = 0.95), while the lowest was for “Were you limited in the kind of work or other activities?” (M = 1.29, SD = 0.46) (Table S2).

Predictors of HRQoL

Stepwise multiple linear regression was performed to identify independent predictors of HRQoL (Tables 5 and 6). Multicollinearity diagnostics confirmed that VIF and tolerance values were within acceptable ranges, and the Durbin-Watson statistic (1.864) indicated no problematic autocorrelation. In Model 1, BMI was the sole statistically significant predictor of HRQoL (B = −0.379, p < 0.001), accounting for 10.3% of the variance (R2 = 0.103, F = 15.66, p < 0.001). In Model 2, the model explained 17.7% of the variance (R2 = 0.177), and the addition of postoperative psychological distress significantly improved model fit (ΔR2 = 0.074, ΔF = 12.28, p < 0.001), with both BMI (B = −0.382, p < 0.001) and postoperative psychological distress (DASS-21 total score; B = −0.054, p < 0.001) remaining significant independent predictors. Discussion

This study investigated the postoperative depression, anxiety, and stress of TKR patients in Jordan and its relationship with HRQoL. The findings revealed moderate levels of depression and anxiety, mild stress, and a poor overall HRQoL. A statistically significant negative association was observed between psychological distress and quality of life, with BMI and postoperative psychological distress (DASS-21 total score) emerging as the only independent predictors of HRQoL.

Level of Postoperative Depression, Anxiety, and Stress Post-TKR

The moderate levels of depression and anxiety observed in the present study are consistent with prior literature. Ayers et al. reported a prevalence of anxiety and depressive symptoms of 37% and 20%, respectively, among postoperative patients and identified female gender, lack of social support, and unemployment as the primary contributing factors. 25 Similarly, Polascik et al. highlighted the high incidence of psychiatric disability in patients recovering from musculoskeletal procedures and emphasized the role of sociodemographic factors in psychological adjustment. 26 The present finding that moderate anxiety and depression coexisted with only mild stress may reflect the acute postoperative nature of the assessment, during which immediate physical concerns such as pain and functional limitation predominate over broader psychosocial stressors. Ai et al. demonstrated that preoperative pain catastrophizing and anxiety were significant predictors of postoperative pain intensity, reinforcing the bidirectional relationship between postoperative depression, anxiety, and stress and physical recovery in arthroplasty patients. 27 Vitaloni et al. urged healthcare providers to remain vigilant in identifying early psychological distress to enable timely intervention before clinically significant conditions develop. 28 An important consideration is whether the postoperative depression and anxiety observed in this study represent transient, recovery-related states that normalize over time, or more enduring features of the patient’s psychological constitution. Evidence from longitudinal studies suggests that psychological distress following TKR typically peaks in the early postoperative weeks and gradually improves with physical recovery over the first three to six months, though a subset of patients, particularly those with preoperative psychological vulnerability, may experience persistent symptoms.13,14 As the present study did not include preoperative or longitudinal follow-up assessments, it cannot be determined whether the documented moderate depression and anxiety represent pre-existing trait-level vulnerabilities or acute state responses to the surgical experience and rehabilitation process. Future research incorporating multiple postoperative assessment time points is needed to characterize the trajectory of psychological recovery following TKR in Jordanian patients.

Level of HRQoL Post-TKR

The overall HRQoL score observed in this study (M = 39.56) is consistent with findings reported in the literature. A cohort study by Rezzadeh et al. reported that TKR patients demonstrated lower physical activity levels, higher pain scores, and reduced overall quality of life in the early postoperative period, indicating that TKR does not uniformly produce immediate quality-of-life gains. 29 In the Jordanian context, Al Thaher et al. similarly noted that female patients reported markedly lower HRQoL scores than male patients post-TKR; however, sex was not significantly associated with HRQoL in the present study. 30 The wide score range (12–75) indicates substantial inter-individual variability in postoperative HRQoL, underscoring the need for personalized post-surgical care pathways. As recommended by González-Sáenz-de-Tejada et al., longitudinal studies tracking HRQoL over time would help elucidate the trajectory of recovery and identify patients at risk of persistent poor quality of life. 31 Furthermore, González-Sáenz-de-Tejada et al. identified age as a key determinant of HRQoL post-TKR, with older patients experiencing lower scores and greater pain, 31 while Khired et al. demonstrated significant associations between BMI, sex, age, and both pain intensity and knee function. 32

Postoperative Psychological Distress and Its Relationship With HRQoL

The significant negative correlation between psychological distress and HRQoL (r = −0.27, p = 0.001) supports the clinical importance of psychological well-being as a determinant of postoperative outcomes in TKR patients. These findings are consistent with those of Al Thaher et al. and Sunadi et al., who reported that addressing psychological distress is essential for optimizing HRQoL and promoting holistic recovery in this population.30,33 Psychological factors, including mood disturbances, emotional distress, and perceived stress, adversely affect multiple HRQoL domains, including physical functioning, emotional well-being, and social interactions.34,35 A 2018 study further confirmed that physical health was significantly impaired in patients with comorbid conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis, obesity, and back pain, which compounds the psychological burden experienced by TKR patients. 36

From a clinical standpoint, these findings highlight the necessity of incorporating routine psychological screening into postoperative assessment protocols. 37 A pilot clinical trial supports the potential role of evidence-based interventions, including cognitive-behavioral therapy, relaxation techniques, and mindfulness-based approaches, to address psychological distress and improve HRQoL outcomes and overall patient satisfaction. 38 Collaborative care models involving orthopedic surgeons, psychologists, and allied health professionals can facilitate integrated management of both the physical and psychological dimensions of recovery. 39

Sociodemographic Variables and Postoperative Depression, Anxiety, and Stress

Among the sociodemographic variables examined, only educational level and the co-occurrence of hypertension and diabetes mellitus were significantly associated with the postoperative depression, anxiety, and stress. This association may reflect differences in health literacy and access to coping resources across educational strata. 33 Previous studies have also shown that poorer preoperative mental well-being is associated with less favorable patient-reported outcomes following total joint replacement, highlighting the importance of psychological health in recovery. 15 The association between concurrent hypertension and diabetes mellitus and elevated psychological distress corroborates the findings of Ayers et al., who demonstrated that patients with multiple chronic conditions experience compounded psychological burden and reduced quality of life. 25

Notably, variables commonly hypothesized to influence psychological outcomes, including age, sex, marital status, and income, did not reach statistical significance in the present study. This may reflect the relatively homogeneous sociodemographic composition of the Jordanian sample, or the inherent limitations of cross-sectional designs in capturing the dynamic nature of psychosocial relationships over time. Parekh et al. suggested that older age may be associated with elevated depression and anxiety due to concerns about mobility and independence. 40 However, this association was not confirmed in the current sample, possibly due to the short postoperative assessment window.

Sociodemographic Variables and HRQoL

BMI emerged as the strongest and most consistent independent predictor of HRQoL (B = −0.382, p < 0.001), explaining 10.3% of total variance. This finding aligns with Abujaber et al., 41 who similarly identified BMI as a significant determinant of postoperative outcomes in TKR patients, and with Khired et al., 32 who demonstrated significant associations between BMI, pain intensity, and knee function post-TKR. The inverse relationship between BMI and HRQoL is mechanistically plausible, as higher BMI is associated with increased mechanical loading on the implanted joint, impaired wound healing, reduced physical mobility, and heightened systemic inflammation, all of which may directly compromise postoperative quality of life. 31 These findings underscore the critical importance of structured weight management programs as an integral component of both preoperative optimization and postoperative rehabilitation.

The addition of postoperative psychological distress to the regression model significantly improved predictive power (ΔR2 = 0.074, p < 0.001), confirming that psychological distress contributes independently to HRQoL beyond the effect of BMI alone. This supports recommendations for multidisciplinary care teams encompassing orthopedic surgeons, physiotherapists, clinical psychologists, and nutritionists to deliver holistic, patient-centered postoperative care. 32 Regarding sex, Al Thaher et al. reported lower HRQoL scores among female patients than male patients after TKR; however, sex was not significantly associated with HRQoL in the present study. 30 Moreover, higher educational attainment has been associated with better health literacy and greater engagement in rehabilitation, potentially improving HRQoL outcomes. 42

Strengths and Limitations

This study has several strengths. To our knowledge, it is among the first studies conducted in Jordan to examine postoperative depression, anxiety, and stress and their association with health-related quality of life among patients undergoing total knee replacement. Validated Arabic versions of the DASS-21 and SF-12 were used in the study, which improved the reliability and cultural suitability of the measurements. Moreover, participants were recruited from two of the main government orthopedic hospitals, increasing the applicability of the findings to patients receiving care within the Jordanian public healthcare system. In addition, the study identified modifiable factors associated with poorer HRQoL, including psychological distress and body mass index, which may help inform future postoperative assessment and supportive care strategies for patients undergoing total knee replacement.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and limits the assessment of HRQoL trajectories over time. Assessments were conducted at a single postoperative time point (6–12 weeks post-TKR); consequently, it is not possible to determine whether the observed depression, anxiety, and stress represent post-surgical psychological states (i.e., state characteristics) or pre-existing dispositional features of the patient (i.e., trait characteristics). The absence of preoperative psychological baseline assessments is a significant limitation. Second, recovery profiles following TKR change rapidly within the first 3 months postoperatively; thus, findings derived from a single time point may not capture the dynamic trajectory of psychological adjustment. Third, the DASS-21, while a validated and widely used screening tool, is primarily a measure of depression, anxiety, and stress symptom severity rather than a comprehensive assessment of postoperative psychological functioning. Other relevant psychological constructs, including pain catastrophizing, psychological flexibility, resilience, and committed action, were not assessed and may independently contribute to HRQoL outcomes. Future studies should employ a broader psychological battery. Fourth, the SF-12 composite HRQoL score was used in the primary analyses; the Physical Component Summary (PCS) and Mental Component Summary (MCS) were not examined as distinct outcomes, which limits comparison with studies reporting these separately and may have obscured differential relationships between physical and mental health components and the DASS subscales. Fifth, correlations with common physical outcome measures, such as the Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (KOOS) and the Oxford Knee Score (OKS), were not examined, limiting clinical interpretation of the observed associations. Sixth, the convenience sampling strategy and single-country context may restrict the generalizability of findings to other settings. Future longitudinal, multicenter, and interventional studies incorporating preoperative assessments, physical outcome measures, and a comprehensive psychological battery are warranted.

Conclusion

Postoperative TKR patients in Jordan experience moderate depression and anxiety, mild stress, and a mean HRQoL score of 39.56. A statistically significant negative association was found between psychological distress and HRQoL (r = −0.27, p = 0.001), indicating that higher psychological distress corresponds to poorer quality of life. Stepwise multiple regression identified BMI (B = −0.382, p < 0.001) and postoperative psychological distress (DASS-21 total score; B = −0.054, p < 0.001) as the only independent predictors of HRQoL, collectively explaining 17.7% of its variance. Educational level and concurrent hypertension with diabetes mellitus were the only sociodemographic variables significantly associated with psychological distress. These findings underscore the need for integrating routine psychological screening using validated tools such as the DASS-21 into standard postoperative TKR care, alongside multidisciplinary interventions targeting BMI reduction and psychological support, with attention to patients across educational strata and those with multiple comorbidities. Future longitudinal, multicenter studies are warranted to establish causal pathways and evaluate the efficacy of targeted interventions in this population.
