Prevalence of pulmonary tuberculosis and associated factors among suspected tuberculosis patients attending Halaba Kulito General Hospital, Ethiopia: cross-sectional study

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPulmonary tuberculosis remains the leading cause of mortality worldwide from a single infectious agent. Background

Pulmonary tuberculosis (PTB) is a chronic infectious disease caused by the rod-shaped bacillus Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB) [1]. It is the leading cause of communicable disease-related mortality worldwide, with approximately 10.8 million new cases and 1.25 million deaths in 2024 [2]. This problem is higher in developing countries compared to developed ones [3]. Sub-Saharan Africa bears a disproportionately high burden of the disease, accounting for ¼ of cases and over 33% of deaths despite representing only 14% of the global population [4]. Ethiopia is the 12th among the 30 high PTB burden countries globally, with an incidence of 132 per 100,000 population [1]. Despite the existence of national tuberculosis guidelines to end by 2030, the incidence and prevalence of tuberculosis have not shown a substantial decline in Ethiopia [5, 6].

Based on previous studies in developing countries, the burden of PTB is exacerbated by different conditions such as malnutrition, HIV co-infection, alcoholism, smoking, khat chewing, and large family size [1, 7, 8].

Although many studies on PTB have been carried out in different parts of Ethiopia, there is no data that indicates the prevalence of PTB and associated risk factors in the current study area. Therefore, this study was designed to assess the prevalence and associated risk factors of PTB among suspected patients at Halaba Kulito General Hospital, Central Ethiopia. As this hospital serves a predominantly rural population, the findings of this study may provide baseline evidence on PTB prevalence and associated factors, supporting tuberculosis control efforts in this and similar rural settings.

Methods

Study design, setting, and period

A cross-sectional study design was conducted from October 27 to December 27, 2025, at Halaba Kulito General Hospital in the Halaba Zone of central Ethiopia. The hospital served as the main referral center for both urban and rural communities, providing a wide range of clinical services, including MTB diagnosis and treatment.

Eligibility criteria

Pulmonary tuberculosis-suspected patients who were able to provide sputum specimens and willing to participate were included in the study, while severely ill patients, those who had hearing or mental problems, and those who could not provide appropriate information were excluded.

Sample size

The required sample size for this study was calculated based on the prevalence rate of PTB of 10.7% as reported in a previous study [1], considering a confidence interval of 95% and a 5% marginal error. The sample size (n) was determined using the statistical formula for a single proportion as follows:

d = the margin of error between the sample and the population.

Z = 95% confidence interval.

P = prevalence of PTB based on the previous study

Considering a 10% nonresponse rate and one additional eligible participant, the final sample size was 163.

Sampling technique

The data were collected using a pretested, structured questionnaire specifically prepared for this study after a thorough literature review. The questionnaires were developed in English and translated into the Amharic language, which is the local language in the study area. Slight modifications were made after the pre-test was performed on 5% of the study population at Wolkite University Comprehensive Specialized Hospital. The questionnaire contains socio-demographic factors (age, sex, residence, educational status, marital status, occupational status, housing condition, and income status), clinical factors (HIV status, diabetes, and contact history with PTB cases), and behavioral factors (smoking, drinking alcohol, and drinking raw milk). Preliminary participant selection was based on clinical suspicion of PTB, defined by the presence of symptoms including fever, cough lasting for 2–3 weeks, night sweats, and weight loss. Consecutive sampling was employed until the required sample size was attained. Only subjects who signed the informed consent or assent participated in the study.

Sputum samples collection and laboratory analyses

Purulent sputum samples (5–10 mL) were collected using a sterile container (Falcon tube) from suspected PTB patients by a trained laboratory professional. Standard operating procedures (SOPs) of GeneXpert MTB/RIF assay processing techniques were used. Each sample is treated with the GeneXpert sample reagent, which contains sodium hydroxide (NaOH) and isopropanol to homogenize the sputum and inactivate MTB. The mixture is then incubated at room temperature for 15 min to allow adequate liquefaction of the specimen. After incubation, 2 mL of the treated sample is transferred into a multi-chambered plastic cartridge preloaded with liquid buffers and lyophilized reagent beads required for automated sample processing and Deoxyribose nucleic acid (DNA) extraction. The cartridge is inserted into the GeneXpert® MTB/RIF system, an automated, real-time Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR)-based diagnostic platform. The system completes all remaining assay steps, including cell lysis, nucleic acid amplification, and detection of MTB DNA. Upon completion, the results are displayed in the system’s results window, allowing rapid interpretation and documentation. All testing was performed with automated internal quality checks using the sample processing control and probe check control. Results were considered valid when assay controls met the manufacturer-defined criteria. Negative results required a valid sample processing control. Invalid results, errors, or sample processing control failures were retested using the original specimen. All results were reviewed by trained laboratory technologists and reported in accordance with the GeneXpert MTB/RIF assay SOP and the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistical analysis

The data were first coded, cleaned, and entered into Epi-Info v 3.1, then transformed and analyzed using Statistical Package for Social Science (SPSS) version 26 statistical software. The results of the analysis were presented in text, figures, or tables where appropriate. Frequency and percentage were used to summarize categorical variables. Normally distributed data were presented as the mean with a standard deviation. The association between variables was assessed using binary logistic regression and multivariable regression analysis. All independent variables with p < 0.25 in bivariate analysis were entered for multivariable analysis [9]. Forward logistic regression analysis was done to identify the best-fit model. In all the cases, a p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Operational definition

Suspected PTB refers to a patient with signs and symptoms of pulmonary tuberculosis, such as night sweats, loss of appetite, loss of weight, fever, and cough that lasts for 2–3 weeks [1].

Results

Study population

A total of 163 participants were included in the study. Of these, 90 (55.2%) were male, and 73 (44.8%) were female, yielding a 1.2:1 male-to-female ratio. Most participants were married 111 (68.1%), while 42 (25.8%) were single, and 10 (6.1%) were divorced. Regarding educational status, 91 (55.8%) participants were able to read and write, 63 (38.7%) were unable to read and write, and only 9 (5.5%) had attained a degree-level education or above. The majority of respondents resided in rural areas 105 (64.4%) (Table 1).

Sociodemographic and economic characteristics of the study participants

The majority, 123 (75.5%), described their living room ventilation as poor. More than half of the households, 88 (54%), had a family size of 3–6 members, while 69 (42.3%) had more than six members. Additionally, nearly half of the respondents, 82 (50.3%), reported that more than two people slept in the same room (Table 1).

Behavioral, clinical, and health-related characteristics

The majority of participants, 158 (96.9%), reported having a cough lasting two weeks or more during the last month. A family history of PTB was reported by 54 (33.1%) participants, and 32 (19.6%) had a previous history of PTB. Regarding behavioral characteristics, 40 (24.5%) of participants reported cigarette smoking, 90 (55.2%) reported chewing khat, and 23 (14.1%) had a history of alcohol consumption. A high proportion, 141 (86.5%), reported drinking raw milk. With respect to comorbid conditions, 46 (28.2%) of participants were on long-term medication, 39 (23.9%) had diabetes mellitus, and 8(4.9%) reported being HIV positive (Table 2).

Prevalence and factors associated with pulmonary tuberculosis

Among the 163 study participants, 13 tested positive for PTB, giving an overall prevalence of 8.0% (13/163; 95% CI 4.3–13.3%) (Fig. 1). In bivariate logistic regression analysis, age, cigarette smoking, alcohol consumption, long-term medication use, and HIV seropositivity showed an association with PTB. However, after multivariable adjustment, only HIV positivity (AOR = 6.683, 95% CI = 1.286–34.727) and drinking alcohol (AOR = 3.773, 95% CI = 1.047–13.593) remained statistically associated with PTB positivity (Table 3).

Discussion

This study aimed to investigate the prevalence of PTB and its associated factors in a resource-limited setting in Ethiopia, and found a non-negligible prevalence of PTB. HIV seropositivity and alcohol consumption were significantly associated with PTB. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study conducted in Halaba Kulito General Hospital to assess the prevalence and associated factors of PTB in Halaba town and the surrounding areas. The findings provide baseline epidemiological evidence for stakeholders and policymakers to design preventive and control strategies for the disease.

Our study showed that the overall prevalence of PTB was 8.0% (13/163; 95% CI 4.3–13.3%). This is consistent with prevalence studies in Durbete Primary Hospital, 9.44% [9], Ataye District Hospital, 8.98% [10], Haramaya district, 7.8% [11], Sawla General Hospital, 10.7% [1], and Addis Ababa, 6.5% [12]. However, it exceeds prevalence rates reported in South Africa, 3.9% [13], Northwest Ethiopia, 2.9% [14], and Dessie and Debre Birhan, 2.6% [15]. In contrast, the rate is lower than that reported from Debre Markos Referral Hospital, 23.2%, and Adare General Hospital, 30.5% [16]. Possible reasons for this discrepancy may be study population differences, the case definition for inclusion, and differences in sensitivity and specificity of diagnostic methodologies.

Although this single-center study cannot assess progress towards End TB targets, the observed prevalence among suspected PTB patients reinforces the importance of sustained case detection, HIV integration, and risk factor screening in high-burden settings like Ethiopia.

In the present study, HIV seropositivity (AOR = 6.683, 95% CI = 1.286–34.727) was significantly associated with PTB among study participants. HIV positive individuals had 6.683 times higher odds of PTB compared with HIV negative individuals. This finding is consistent with studies conducted in Ethiopia and other Sub-Saharan African countries (AOR = 8.8, 95% CI = 2.4–23.8) [17], (AOR = 7.7, 95% CI = 2.24–26.40) [18], and (AOR = 6.4, 95% CI = 1.6–25.7) [19]. This may be due to HIV-induced immunosuppression, which reduces the ability to fight infections, including PTB, and increases the risk of reactivation of latent MTB and progression to active PTB [3].

In the present study, drinking alcohol (AOR = 3.773, 95% CI = 1.047–13.593) was also significantly associated with PTB among study participants. Individuals who drink alcohol had 3.773 times higher odds of PTB compared with those who do not drink alcohol. This finding is consistent with studies conducted in Kazakhstan (AOR = 2.6, 95% CI = 1.2–5.7) [20], India (AOR = 1.5, 95% CI = 1.2-2.0) [21], and Ethiopia (AOR = 2.15, 95% CI = 1.12–4.11) [22]. This association may be explained by alcohol impairing the immune functions of the alveolar macrophage and increased oxidative stress in the alveolar space, which lowers host defenses against MTB and facilitates progression to active PTB [22, 23]. In addition, alcohol-related social behaviors may increase exposure.

In bivariate logistic regression analysis, age and cigarette smoking were associated with PTB, but these associations did not persist in the multivariate model after adjustment. This suggests that the crude associations were confounded by other variables in the model.

Despite providing valuable insights, this study had limitations. The relatively small sample size, single-center design, and short enrolment priod may limit the generalizability of the findings. Moreover, the small number of PTB-positive cases may affect the precision of the adjusted odds ratio estimates. In addition, behavioral factors such as alcohol use, smoking, and khat chewing were assessed through self-report, which may introduce recall and social desirability biases. Hence, the results of this study should be interpreted with due consideration of these limitations.

Conclusion

This study found a non-negligible prevalence of PTB among the study population. HIV seropositivity and alcohol consumption were significantly associated with PTB. These findings support the need for integrated tuberculosis and HIV services and consideration of alcohol-use assessment within tuberculosis screening and prevention activities. Future multicenter studies with larger sample sizes and longer enrollment periods are warranted.
