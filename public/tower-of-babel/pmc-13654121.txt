Atrial and ventricular mechanical dysfunction and myocardial injury predict long-term outcomes in hypertrophic cardiomyopathy: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Sheng-Liang Liu, Xue-Dong Wang, Ye Li, Xuan-Lang Zhang, Yun-Ling Li, Guo-Kun Wang, Mao-Mao Zhang, Yan-Ming Zhao, Xia Gu, Bo Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLeft ventricular (LV) diastolic dysfunction is a hallmark of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) and imposes chronic pressure overload on the left atrium, leading to progressive atrial remodeling and functional impairment. Conventional left atrial (LA) volumetric indices reflect comparatively advanced structural alterations and are incapable of identifying early functional deterioration. The present research sought to investigate the prognostic significance of LA and LV myocardial deformation parameters obtained from cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) in patients with HCM.MethodsThis single-center retrospective study included 126 HCM patients and 40 healthy volunteers who underwent comprehensive CMR examinations. LA longitudinal strain (reservoir, conduit, and booster components) was assessed using the fast long-axis method; LV global strain and torsion were derived using feature-tracking analysis. Late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) was quantified to assess myocardial fibrosis. Patients with HCM were followed longitudinally for the occurrence of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs). Cox regression analyses were performed to identify independent predictors of MACEs.ResultsCompared with healthy volunteers, patients with HCM exhibited significantly impaired LA reservoir, conduit, and booster strain, as well as reduced LV global strain parameters (all P<0.05). Following a median observational period of 1,055 days, 41 patients (32.5%) experienced MACEs. Patients with MACEs demonstrated markedly reduced LA strain and LV torsion compared with those without events. In multivariable Cox regression analysis, LA booster strain [hazard ratio (HR): 0.854, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.799–0.914], LV torsion (HR: 0.517, 95% CI: 0.298–0.895), and serum creatine kinase myocardial band (CKMB) (HR: 1.032, 95% CI: 1.018–1.047) emerged as independent predictors of MACEs. LA booster strain yielded the optimal prognostic efficacy for predicting MACEs [area under the curve (AUC): 0.827].ConclusionsLA booster strain, LV torsion, and CKMB represent complementary markers reflecting atrial function, ventricular mechanics, and myocardial injury, respectively. Their independent associations with adverse outcomes highlight the multifactorial nature of disease progression in HCM and support a more integrative approach to risk stratification beyond single-domain assessment. Incorporating these parameters into routine CMR and clinical evaluation may facilitate earlier identification of patients at increased risk for MACEs and support individualized surveillance strategies.

Introduction

Left ventricular (LV) diastolic dysfunction is a hallmark pathophysiological feature of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) and a major determinant of clinical deterioration and adverse outcomes (1,2). Due to elevated LV filling pressure, impaired ventricular compliance, and myocardial fibrotic remodeling, the left atrium is exposed to chronic pressure overload, leading to progressive structural remodeling and functional impairment (3). Although left atrial (LA) enlargement has long been recognized as a surrogate marker of chronic diastolic burden, volumetric indices primarily reflect relatively late manifestations of disease progression and may fail to detect subtle abnormalities in myocardial mechanics before irreversible structural changes occur (4,5). Consequently, adverse clinical events may still develop in patients without marked structural abnormalities, highlighting the need for more sensitive imaging parameters that can identify early atrial dysfunction. Prior research has demonstrated that LA functional metrics are superior to structural indices in evaluating LV diastolic function (6,7). LA strain has been validated as a sensitive marker of LV diastolic function in healthy individuals (8), yet its specific role and prognostic value in the HCM population remain inadequately characterized.

The left atrium contributes to cardiac performance through three distinct, sequential phases: the reservoir phase, during which it stores pulmonary venous blood return in ventricular systole; the conduit phase, facilitating passive blood transit into the LV during early diastole; and the booster pump phase, providing active contraction in late diastole (9). LA reservoir function relies on LA compliance and LV basal descent; conduit function is largely determined by LV relaxation and early diastolic properties; and booster function is governed by intrinsic LA contractility and LV end-diastolic pressure (10). Thus, a comprehensive assessment integrating both ventricular systolic/diastolic mechanics and LA functional dynamics is likely to provide superior prognostic information and enhance risk stratification in HCM.

Accordingly, the objective of this work was to appraise whether LA and LV myocardial strain parameters acquired via cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) using fast long-axis methodology and feature tracking carry prognostic value in HCM patients. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2026-0483/rc).

Methods

Study population

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This retrospective study was approved by The Second Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University Ethics Committee (No. KY2024-217), and written informed consent was provided by all participants enrolled between September 2021 and December 2023. Two cohorts were enrolled: (I) patients with HCM, and (II) healthy volunteers without any prior medical disorders. HCM was defined as a maximal left ventricular wall thickness (LVWT) at end-diastole >15 mm (or >13 mm among those with familial HCM history) according to established CMR diagnostic criteria (11). The exclusion criteria for the HCM cohort included conditions accounting for increased ventricular thickness, such as phenotypic mimics of HCM encompassing cardiac amyloidosis, Danon disease, and Anderson-Fabry Disease, alongside systemic diseases and hypertensive heart disease, congenital heart disease, and primary valvular disease [mitral regurgitation (MR) grade ≥2, aortic regurgitation grade ≥2, or aortic stenosis with an area <1 cm2] (12). Additional exclusions applied to all participants: a history of myocarditis or significant coronary artery disease (stenosis >50% on angiography), poor CMR image quality due to severe cardiac motion artifacts, prior alcohol septal ablation or myectomy, and conventional contraindications (implanted pacemakers, defibrillators, metallic intracranial implants, claustrophobia, or renal function impairment).

Image acquisition

CMR imaging acquisition was performed via a 3.0-tesla (3 T) scanner (Ingenia CX, Philips Healthcare, the Netherlands) equipped with a 32-channel phased-array torso coil. Breath-hold, electrocardiogram-synchronized steady-state free precession (SSFP) sequences were applied for the acquisition of cardiac cine images. Imaging included two-, three-, and four-chamber long-axis views, and contiguous short-axis planes covering the LV from base to apex. Scanning parameters were as follows: repetition time (TR)/echo time (TE): 2.8/1.4 ms, field of view (FOV): 300×300 mm2, voxel: 1.8×2.0×8.0 mm3, sensitivity encoding (SENSE): 1.5, flip angle: 45°, matrix: 160×150, gap: 3 mm, slice thickness: 8 mm. Late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) images were obtained 10–15 minutes following intravenous gadolinium contrast administration (0.3 mmol/kg; Magnevist, Bayer Healthcare, Berlin, Germany). Three-dimensional phase-sensitive inversion recovery (PSIR) sequencing was conducted with the following scanning parameters: TR/TE: 6.1/3.0 ms, FOV: 300×300 mm2, voxel: 1.8×1.68×8.0 mm3, SENSE: 1.6, flip angle: 25°, matrix: 168×154, gap: 3 mm, slice thickness: 8 mm.

Image analysis

All acquired CMR images were analyzed on CVI42 workstation (Circle Cardiovascular Imaging Inc., Calgary, Alberta, Canada) by two professional radiologists (G.K.W. and Y.L., with 12 and 4 years of CMR experience, respectively), blinded to clinical data, study group assignment, and MACE outcomes. Cardiac morphologic and functional characteristics were assessed based on SSFP cine images. LV endocardial and epicardial borders were semi-automatically traced with manual calibrations to derive ventricular dimensions and volumes, cardiac output (CO), and ejection fraction. LV mass was calculated as the total myocardial volume multiplied by the specific gravity (1.05 g/mL) without papillary muscles. LGE extent was quantified using a threshold of 5 standard deviations (SD) above the mean signal intensity of remote myocardium, and expressed as the LGE/LV mass ratio. Myocardial deformation analysis was performed using feature-tracking strain. LV global radial strain (GRS), global circumferential strain (GCS), and global longitudinal strain (GLS) were derived from two-dimensional short- and long-axis cine images. LV torsion was calculated as the peak apical-to-basal rotational gradient divided by the ventricular longitudinal length. For regional analysis, the LV was divided into basal, mid, and apical segments. LA analysis was performed using the biplane area-length method, excluding LA appendage and pulmonary veins, to obtain left atrial diameter (LAD), atrial volumetric analysis, and LA emptying fraction (EF). LA longitudinal strain was assessed via the fast long-axis method on long-axis views, with manually located reference points at the atrioventricular junctions and the mid-posterior atrial wall (Figure 1). LA longitudinal strain was decomposed into reservoir, conduit, and booster components according to LV end-systole, LV end-diastole, and pre-atrial systole, respectively.

Follow-up study

Extended clinical observational follow-up was performed via standardized telephone interviews administered every six months post-CMR. Follow-up data were adjudicated by two experienced cardiologists (X.G. and Y.L.L., with 10 and 11 years of experience, respectively). The primary endpoint was major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs), a composite of cardiac death, heart transplantation, heart failure hospitalization, and life-threatening arrhythmia (13).

Reproducibility analysis

Intra- and interobserver reproducibility for LA booster strain was analyzed in a random cohort of 30 HCM individuals. For intraobserver variability, the measurements were repeated by the same radiologist after a 4-month interval, blinded to the initial results. Interobserver variability was assessed by two independent radiologists who were mutually blinded to their respective measurements.

Statistical analysis

Continuous data were displayed as mean ± SD or medians with interquartile range (IQR) according to data distribution. Categorical variables were summarized as absolute frequencies with proportional percentages. Group comparisons were performed using independent t-test, Mann-Whitney U test, Fisher’s exact test, or chi-square test, as appropriate. Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were used to identify parameters associated with MACEs. The predictive power of significant parameters was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis; area under the curve (AUC), specificity, sensitivity, and Youden’s index were calculated to determine optimal cut-off values. Survival distributions were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier (KM) method and compared with the log-rank test. The correlations between myocardial strain and functional parameters were assessed using Pearson or Spearman’s correlation coefficients. Intra- and interobserver variabilities were quantified using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) and Bland-Altman analysis. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) or MedCalc version 20 (MedCalc Software, Ostend, Belgium). Statistical significance was set at a threshold of P<0.05.

Results

Participant characteristics

The study consisted of 166 participants, including 126 HCM patients (81 men; age 55.04±12.68 years) and 40 healthy volunteers (19 men; age 55.45±15.16 years). A total of 62 participants were subsequently excluded: 45 due to moderate and severe MR, 11 due to a history of myocarditis, myocardial infarction, or poor image quality, and 6 were lost to follow-up (Figure 2). Baseline clinical characteristics are presented in Table 1. Compared with healthy volunteers, HCM patients presented a greater incidence of hypertension and smoking (both P<0.05). Additionally, HCM patients exhibited significantly higher serum levels of N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP), troponin I (TnI), and creatine kinase myocardial band (CKMB), and more frequently received beta-blockers (all P<0.05). No statistical differences were observed between the two groups regarding age, sex, height, weight, body surface area (BSA), heart rate, CO, MR, or the prevalence of diabetes mellitus (DM) and hyperlipidemia (all P>0.05). Among the 126 HCM patients, 30 (23.8%) had obstructive HCM. The baseline clinical characteristics and CMR parameters of patients with obstructive and non-obstructive HCM are also summarized in the Supplementary data (Tables S1,S2).

The HCM cohort was categorized by outcome into an MACEs group (n=41, 32.54%) and a no-MACEs group (n=85, 67.46%). There were no significant differences between the two subgroups in terms of age, sex, height, weight, BSA, CO, or the prevalence of hypertension, DM, hyperlipidemia, and smoking (all P>0.05). However, patients in the MACEs group showed a higher heart rate (83.68±21.84 vs. 74.44±13.91 beat/min, P=0.02), higher incidence of MR (43.90% vs. 11.76%, P<0.001), and higher levels of NT-proBNP [1,781 (638, 2,703) vs. 526 (212, 1,187) pg/mL, P<0.001], TnI [0.06 (0, 0.79) vs. 0.03 (0, 0.29) µg/L, P=0.03], and CKMB [3.00 (1.23, 13.30) vs. 1.50 (1.10, 4.98) µg/L, P=0.02]. The intake of beta-blockers was similar between the two subgroups (P=0.18) (Table 1).

Cardiac function by CMR

Compared with healthy volunteers, the HCM group demonstrated significantly higher left ventricular mass index (LVMi), LV end-systolic volume index (ESVi), minimum left atrial volume index (LAVimin), maximum left atrial volume index (LAVimax), maximum ventricular wall thickness (MVWT), and LAD. Conversely, left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), LA total EF, LA passive EF, and LA active EF were all significantly lower in the HCM group (all P<0.05). LV end-diastolic volume index (EDVi) and left ventricular end-diastolic dimension (LVEDD) were comparable within the two study groups (both P>0.05) (Table 2). When comparing HCM subgroups, LVEF, LA total EF, LA passive EF, and LA active EF were significantly higher in the no-MACEs group than in the MACEs group (LVEF, 61.54±10.15% vs. 56.19±14.07%; LA total EF, 46.78±11.28% vs. 32.72±13.75%; LA passive EF, 18.75±7.20% vs. 13.36±6.84%; LA active EF, 27.84±8.64% vs. 19.08±9.05%; all P<0.05). Conversely, LAD, LAVimin, and LAVimax were significantly elevated in the MACEs group compared with no-MACEs group (LAD, 43.20±8.31 vs. 36.97±7.42 mm; LAVimin, 42.13±18.12 vs. 27.49±14.51 mL/m2; LAVimax, 60.46±18.53 vs. 49.34±15.96 mL/m2; all P<0.05). No statistically significant differences were found between the two subgroups for LVMi, LV EDVi, LV ESVi, LVEDD, and MVWT (all P>0.05) (Table 2).

Myocardial strain and LGE

The HCM group exhibited significant impairments in LA reservoir, conduit, and booster strain compared with healthy individuals (reservoir, 25.26±14.67% vs. 44.74±11.86%; conduit, 11.51±8.04% vs. 24.98±9.16%; booster, 13.75±8.39% vs. 19.76±6.67%; all P<0.05). Similarly, LV GRS, GCS, and GLS were markedly lower in the HCM group relative to healthy individuals (GRS, 21.70±7.40% vs. 34.21±5.99%; GCS, −13.61±3.55% vs. −19.28±2.01%; GLS, −10.13±3.29% vs. −18.12±2.02%; all P<0.001). This impairment was consistent across all LV segments (basal, mid, and apical) in all three strain directions (all P<0.001). LV torsion did not differ between HCM and healthy individuals (P=0.31). The overall burden of LGE was significantly greater in the HCM group than it was in healthy individuals [2.37% (0.10%, 12.87%) vs. 0 (0%, 0%); P<0.001] (Table 2).

Within the HCM cohort, the MACEs group exhibited more severe impairment in myocardial deformation than did the no-MACEs group. LA reservoir, conduit, and booster strain were significantly lower in the MACEs group (reservoir, 14.22±9.08% vs. 30.59±13.88%; conduit, 6.70±4.61% vs. 13.83±8.32%; booster, 7.50±5.53% vs. 16.76±7.88%; all P<0.001). LV GRS, GCS, and GLS were also significantly reduced in the MACEs group (GRS, 17.92±7.40% vs. 23.52±6.70%; GCS, −11.75±3.71% vs. −14.51±3.11%, GLS, −8.22±3.42% vs. −11.05±2.82%; all P<0.001), a pattern that held true for all regional strain parameters (all P<0.05). Torsion was significantly lower in the MACEs group than the no-MACEs group (1.30±0.59 vs. 1.78±0.63 °/cm, P<0.001). No significant difference was observed in LGE extent between the MACEs and no-MACEs groups [0.4141 (0.10% (0.10%, 8.01%) vs. 3.10% (0.10%, 13.87%); P=0.32] (Table 2).

Clinical outcomes

The median observational follow-up spanned 1,055 days (IQR: 541 to 1,374 days); MACEs occurred in 41 patients (32.54%). The composite endpoint included cardiac death (n=4), heart transplantation (n=0), life-threatening arrhythmia (n=17), and hospitalization for heart failure (n=20) (Figure 2). Univariate Cox regression analysis identified several parameters associated with MACEs, including heart rate, LAD, CKMB, torsion, LV GRS, LV GCS, LV GLS, LA reservoir strain, LA conduit strain, LA booster strain, LAVimin, LAVimax, and MR (all P<0.05). In the stepwise multivariate Cox regression model, CKMB [hazard ratio (HR): 1.032, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.018–1.047, P<0.001], torsion (HR: 0.517, 95% CI: 0.298–0.895, P=0.02), and LA booster strain (HR: 0.854, 95% CI: 0.799–0.914, P<0.001) remained independent predictors of MACEs (Table 3).

The measurement of cardiac strain parameters demonstrated excellent reproducibility. Discussion

In our study, we comprehensively evaluated LA and LV myocardial deformation parameters using the fast long-axis method and feature tracking derived from CMR in HCM patients and investigated their associations with long-term adverse clinical outcomes. The key findings are as follows: (I) LA reservoir, conduit, and booster strain, along with corresponding LA total, passive, and active EF were markedly impaired in the MACEs group compared with the no-MACEs group; (II) compared with patients without MACEs, those who experienced MACEs exhibited significantly reduced global and segmental LV strain, as well as more pronounced impairment in LV torsion; (III) CKMB, LV torsion, and LA booster strain emerged as independent predictors of MACEs, with LA booster strain demonstrating the highest prognostic value. These findings highlight the complex and multifactorial mechanisms underlying adverse outcomes in HCM and underscore the value of integrating atrial function, ventricular mechanics, and biochemical markers of myocardial injury for risk stratification.

LA strain provides a sensitive and dynamic assessment of atrial myocardial function that complements conventional volumetric indices (14). Unlike LA volume, which reflects relatively late structural remodeling due to chronic pressure overload, LA strain captures functional impairment that may precede overt structural remodeling (15). In our cohort, LA reservoir, conduit, and booster strain were all significantly reduced in HCM patients, with the most pronounced impairment observed in those who developed MACEs during follow-up; LA booster strain even provides incremental prognostic value beyond LA structural and functional indices for predicting MACEs. These findings are consistent with prior echocardiographic studies suggesting that LA strain outperforms conventional LA parameters in stratifying risk for a composite of adverse outcomes (16).

LA booster function reflects intrinsic atrial contractility and constitutes the final active contribution to LV filling (17). During compensated stages, enhanced atrial systole helps maintain LV preload and CO. However, sustained pressure overload and chronic atrial wall stress eventually lead to atrial myopathy, fibrosis, and exhaustion of contractile reserve (18). Once LA booster function deteriorates, the atrium can no longer effectively compensate for impaired ventricular filling, resulting in abrupt rises in pulmonary venous pressure and clinical decompensation (19). Therefore, reduced LA booster strain likely marks a critical transition from adaptive atrioventricular coupling to overt hemodynamic failure. Although LA booster strain demonstrated the strongest individual prognostic performance in our cohort, LV torsion and CKMB provided complementary information regarding distinct pathophysiological processes. Together, these parameters capture different aspects of HCM progression and may improve the understanding of the mechanisms underlying adverse outcomes beyond atrial dysfunction alone.

LV torsion represents a complementary marker of ventricular mechanical efficiency (20). LV strain quantifies linear myocardial deformation in a specific direction, whereas torsion reflects three-dimensional myocardial deformation resulting from the counter-directional rotation of basal and apical segments (21,22). In HCM, myocardial hypertrophy, fiber disarray, and interstitial fibrosis disrupt the normal transmural helical architecture, resulting in altered torsional mechanics (23). In our study, reduced LV torsion independently predicted MACEs, highlighting its role as a marker of advanced myocardial mechanical dysfunction. Importantly, impaired torsion compromises not only systolic efficiency but also diastolic recoil and untwisting, which are essential for early diastolic suction (24). Attenuated untwisting delays LV filling and increases reliance on atrial contraction, thereby amplifying the functional burden on the left atrium (25). This ventricular-atrial mechanical interaction provides a plausible explanation for the coexistence of reduced LV torsion and impaired LA booster strain observed in patients with MACEs, suggesting a vicious cycle of ventricular-atrial mechanical uncoupling that accelerates disease progression.

In addition to imaging-derived parameters, CKMB was the only biochemical marker independently associated with MACEs in our multivariable model. The retention of CKMB rather than NT-proBNP in the final model may be explained by their different biological roles. NT-proBNP primarily reflects hemodynamic burden and filling pressures, which are already captured by LA functional parameters, particularly LA booster strain. In contrast, elevated CKMB levels reflect ongoing myocardial injury related to microvascular ischemia, increased wall stress, and supply-demand mismatch (26,27). Importantly, CKMB elevation reflects an active and dynamic pathological process that is not directly visualized by static imaging markers such as LGE, which primarily identifies established replacement fibrosis. The lack of significant differences in LGE extent between the MACEs and no-MACEs groups in our study further supports the notion that biochemical evidence of ongoing myocardial damage may precede or occur independently of overt fibrotic burden. Accordingly, biochemical markers of myocardial injury capture a distinct temporal dimension of disease activity, complementing the structural and mechanical information provided by imaging.

LV myocardial strain and LGE are established CMR markers reflecting ventricular systolic mechanics and focal myocardial fibrosis, respectively, and have been widely used for risk stratification in HCM (28,29). However, both parameters predominantly characterize static or ventricular-centric aspects of the disease and may not fully capture the dynamic and integrative processes that drive clinical deterioration. LV global strain parameters primarily assess myocardial deformation during systole and are sensitive to regional contractile impairment (30). Nevertheless, systolic dysfunction is often modest or heterogeneous in HCM, particularly in patients with preserved LVEF, limiting the discriminatory power of LV strain alone (31). Similarly, LGE identifies replacement fibrosis, but underestimates diffuse interstitial fibrosis and ongoing myocardial injury—two pathological features closely linked to diastolic dysfunction (32). In our study, the absence of significant differences in LGE extent between the MACEs and no-MACEs groups further suggests that focal fibrosis burden alone may be insufficient to discriminate risk in a heterogeneous HCM population. The apparently lower LGE extent observed in the MACEs group should be interpreted cautiously, given the substantial overlap in LGE values between groups, the limited sample size, and the heterogeneous nature of the composite endpoint. In this context, our findings demonstrate that LA booster strain, LV torsion, and CKMB provide incremental prognostic information beyond LV strain and LGE, highlighting their complementary roles. This multidimensional assessment offers a more comprehensive representation of HCM pathophysiology and better identifies patients at heightened risk for adverse outcomes.

Taken together, LA booster strain, LV torsion, and CKMB represent complementary markers spanning atrial function, ventricular mechanics, and myocardial injury. Their independent associations with adverse outcomes suggest that risk stratification in HCM should move beyond single-domain assessment toward a more integrative framework. Specifically, patients exhibiting impaired LV torsion and elevated CKMB may experience progressive diastolic dysfunction and myocardial damage, ultimately overwhelming atrial compensatory mechanisms, as reflected by reduced LA booster strain. Incorporating these parameters into routine CMR and clinical assessment could enhance the early identification of patients at heightened risk for MACEs, thereby informing closer surveillance and individualized therapeutic strategies.

This study has several limitations. First, its single-center, retrospective design and relatively modest sample size may limit generalizability and introduce residual selection bias, despite adequate event numbers for multivariable analysis. Second, different methodologies were used for LA and LV strain assessment (fast long-axis method vs. feature tracking), which may limit direct comparisons between these parameters. Third, LGE assessment focused on replacement fibrosis and may underestimate diffuse interstitial fibrosis, as T1 mapping was not available. In addition, CKMB was measured at a single time point and may not fully reflect temporal variability in myocardial injury.

Conclusions

LA-LV mechanical dysfunction and myocardial injury independently predict adverse outcomes in HCM. LA booster strain, LV torsion, and CKMB offer complementary prognostic value and may improve CMR-based risk stratification.
