Landiolol for preventing postoperative atrial fibrillation: lessons from Japan for all?
Authors: Isabelle Frei, David Santer, Eckhard Mauermann
Published: 2026

Scope of the problem/relevance

Postoperative atrial fibrillation (POAF) is defined as a new-onset atrial fibrillation in the immediate postoperative period (1). Estimates range from 20% to 30% after coronary artery bypass graft (CABG) surgery (2) to over 50% for select populations (1). This makes POAF not only the most common sustained postoperative arrhythmia, but one whose incidence is rising as populations continue to age (3). Furthermore, although most prevalent after cardiac surgery, it is also a common complication in non-cardiac surgery and intensive care at 5% and 30%, respectively (1).

POAF is not only associated with major adverse outcomes such as heart failure, thromboembolism, myocardial infarction, stroke, and mortality, but also with longer hospital and intensive care unit (ICU) length of stay, hemodynamic instability, infection, renal complications, bleeding, and greater healthcare costs (1,4). Although POAF is often self-limiting, it is associated with a 4–5-fold increase in atrial fibrillation within the next 5 years (5), portending future morbidity.

In their recent publication “Low dose of landiolol does not prevent postoperative atrial fibrillation after cardiac surgery in non-Asian patients: a multicenter randomised study” in the British Journal of Anaesthesia, Amour and colleagues published a prospective, multicenter, randomized, placebo-controlled trial from three French centers examining the effect of a 24-hour postoperative administration of low-dose landiolol on POAF in 318 non-Asian patients undergoing elective cardiac surgery (6). Inclusion criteria were patients aged 65 years or older undergoing cardiac surgery with sternotomy and cardiopulmonary bypass and with an ejection fraction (EF) of 40% or higher. Patients were excluded if they met either preoperative exclusion criteria (e.g., POAF, contraindications to -blockers, severe hypotension, cardiac conduction abnormalities, etc.) or postoperative exclusion criteria (sinus bradycardia requiring pacing, low cardiac output, severe hypotension, surgical revision, etc.). Landiolol, dosed at 2 mcg/kg/min, or placebo was initiated within the first 4 hours after arrival in the ICU and continued for at least the first 24 hours, ensuring the mean arterial pressure (MAP) remained ≥65 mmHg and the heart rate >50 beats per minute. Hemodynamics permitting, bisoprolol 1.25 mg twice daily or the patient’s usual -blocker (some 60%) was begun or resumed 12 hours after surgery. No difference in the rates of POAF were found for the landiolol and placebo groups [29.8% vs. 31.0%, respectively, P=0.77; relative risk (RR) =0.93; 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.67–1.30]. The authors are to be commended for this important study. A number of strengths include: multicenter design, large size, real-life and fast-track setting, and controlling for placebo.

The question remains, however, how do we integrate the negative results from this study into previous literature, which has found sizeable reductions in POAF (7-11). After all, two previous pooled meta-analyses of 868 patients and 1,264 patients revealed an impressive risk reduction of 0.40 (95% CI: 0.30–0.54) (12) and an odds ratio of 0.35 (95% CI: 0.23–0.53) (13).

Discussion points

Patient selection/risk factors/risk scores

Clearly, POAF is not simply a nuisance potentially requiring some sort of therapy, but rather a serious complication requiring risk stratification and patient identification, with prevention in part based on patient risk (14). A number of risk factors have been described, including age, history of AF, renal failure, mitral valve surgery/disease, heart failure, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) (14), culminating in the creation of a POAF score for cardiac surgery (15) based on age, COPD, renal function, emergency surgery, the use of an intra-aortic balloon pump, the left ventricular EF, and valve surgery. The “average” patient in the study by Amour et al. [71 years of age, 2% COPD, modification of diet in renal disease (MDRD) 74, 90% EF >50%, elective surgery, 55% CABG, 7% mitral valve, 16% aortic valve, 20%complex aortic] yielded an estimation of 28% probability of POAF (16), similar to the anticipated 25% of the sample size. However, the fact that 1,794 patients were screened and nearly two-thirds were excluded possibly indicates limited transferability. It is unclear why these patients were excluded. Was it age? Was it emergency surgery? Was it poor EF? Were many CABG surgery patients off-pump? It seems possible that a relevant portion of patients with a higher risk (e.g., valve surgery via a minimal invasive approach) may have been excluded. Also, of the 400 consenting patients, 80 were excluded due to postoperative exclusion criteria such as bradycardia requiring pacing, use of inotropes, severe hypotension, surgical revision, etc. In sum, one cannot exclude the possibility that there may have been a selection toward healthier and less at-risk patients, particularly as the assumed attrition rate of 10% was too low and 400 rather than 330 patients had to be consented. Nonetheless, the sample size seems reasonable, and the very optimistic effect size (50% reduction in POAF through landiolol) is supported by the available literature (12).

Recommendations for prevention of POAF

Despite its clinical relevance, the options for preventing POAF are limited. Current guidelines from cardiac surgery, cardiology, anesthesia, and intensive care are limited to perioperative continuation/de novo administration of β-blockers, de novo perioperative administration of amiodarone, and a posterior left pericardiotomy (1). A number of other preventative measures have been examined, but are either nonconclusive or recommended to a lesser degree and include: sotalol, magnesium, postoperative corticosteroids, postoperative colchicine, (bi-)atrial pacing, and botulinum injection into the epicardial fat (1).

Nonetheless, despite the evidence for β-blockers and/or amiodarone, their potential side effects are substantial, and their implementation perioperatively is limited. A worldwide survey of Society of Cardiovascular Anesthesiologists (SCA) and European Association of Cardiothoracic Anaesthesiology and Intensive Care (EACTAIC) cardiac anesthesiologists with 641 respondents showed that in patients at high risk of POAF, only some 20% of respondents always or almost always gave prophylactic beta blockers in the intraoperative and postoperative period, with a third never or almost never administering β-blockers perioperatively (14). The main reasons for not administering β-blockers were (I) the belief that the risk outweighed the benefit (40%); (II) the risk of bradycardia (35%); and (III) either the surgeon was opposed (10%) or the treating physician did not believe in a benefit (10%). Similarly, a more recent survey of 15 Dutch cardiothoracic centers showed that 6 of the 14 responding centers (43%) used local protocols for preoperative POAF prophylaxis in at least high-risk patients and all 14 responding centers (100%) employed postoperative POAF prophylaxis (17). Prophylaxis was mainly based on non-sotalol β-blockers, with amiodarone only used for treating POAF, despite potential benefit from combining β-blockers with a lower cumulative dose of amiodarone (17).

Landiolol may be a promising and safe agent due to its highly selective β1 nature and ultra-short half-life, both of which are highly beneficial during the intraoperative and early postoperative period. The reservations in using perioperative β-blockade in cardiac surgery patients may potentially be alleviated, provided there is a tangible benefit in the population at hand.

Placing this study in the literature: dose, duration, and ethnicity

The first meta-analysis of nine randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and 868 patients clearly showed a substantial benefit of landiolol in reducing POAF (RR =0.4; 95% CI: 0.3–0.54), which was largely confirmed in a recent meta-analysis of 11 studies and 1,264 patients (13), which additionally included this study by Amour et al. (6) and a smaller study of 50 patients undergoing off-pump cardiac surgery (18). A number of reasons might explain why this study did not find a reduction (12).

First, in terms of pathophysiology, POAF generally occurs 2 to 4 days after surgery, with the general assumption being that vulnerable atrial tissue is stimulated by inflammation, dysfunction of the autonomic nervous system, hypovolemia, etc. (19). As such, stopping landiolol after 24 hours and then relying on 1.25 mg bisoprolol twice daily may have resulted in suboptimal -blockade during the most vulnerable window. Eight of the nine studies in the cited meta-analysis administered landiolol for a minimum of 48 up to 96 hours (12). While this decision to stop landiolol after 24 hours reflects a real-world experience and the trend towards enhanced recovery after cardiac surgery, it may simply be too early for the inflammatory response to clear to a relevant extent.

Second, the fixed rate of 2 mcg/kg/min of landiolol may have been insufficient for the population studied. Most studies have used 2–10 mcg/kg/min generally titrated to a specific heart rate (7-9,20,21). Furthermore, the dose of landiolol was slowly reduced upon commencing oral β-blockade, leading to significant overlap in cumulative β-blockade in many of these studies, which appears not to have been the case in the study by Amour et al. Nonetheless, one study used a low dose of 1 mcg/kg/min over 72 hours and was successful in reducing POAF (11).

Third, the elephant in the room is that eight of the nine studies included in the meta-analysis were studies involving Japanese patients. Overall, the almost exclusively Japanese meta-analysis showed very low heterogeneity. Ferraris et al.—the only non-Japanese study—was a small dose-finding study of 59 patients receiving landiolol over 120 minutes in 0.5, 1, 2, 5, and 10 mcg/kg/min in CABG patients, not designed to examine a reduction in POAF. However, landiolol did reduce heart rate significantly, while leaving MAP, stroke volume, and various measures of microcirculation unchanged (22). However, other than showing efficacy and safety at a dose of up to 10 mcg/kg/min in non-Japanese patients, no other conclusions can be drawn. As pointed out by Amour et al. and as evident in the title of their study, genetic polymorphisms may be important for finding suitable concentrations for different patients, although it remains uncertain why the title cites non-Asian population rather than non-Japanese. This may have to do with the fact that while it is known that atrial fibrillation has an inheritable component (23) with a multitude of specifically identified loci for atrial fibrillation (24), studies have shown a more favorable response to β-blocker therapies in β1-adrenergic receptor polymorphisms prevalent in East Asians (25). To the extent that genetic polymorphisms may be relevant, the different examined populations (Amour et al. examined non-Asians in French centers, most landiolol studies are in Japanese patients, and the genetic polymorphism described above was in East Asians and East Asian descendants) may significantly alter dosing and timing. This may be an eye-opener for many, as the converse question is: to what extent are studies based on patients from North America and Europe applicable to the rest of the world? As with sex-related differences in the presentation, identification, therapy, etc. of cardiac disease, genetic composition might have to be considered.

Conclusions

The interesting study by Amour et al. highlights what we do not yet know—despite several publications—about preventing POAF after cardiac surgery with landiolol. Not only are the patients included in this study a small selection of the clinical spectrum, but it remains uncertain what concentration and duration of landiolol are required for a reduction in POAF, particularly when one considers the possible relevance of genetic polymorphisms. For the clinician, however, landiolol appears safe and titratable.
