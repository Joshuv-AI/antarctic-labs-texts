Construction and validation of a prediction system for postherpetic neuralgia: based on meta-analysis
Authors: Zheng Lin, Hongfei Wang, Yueyi Shi, Yi Cao, Zuyong Zhang, Ping Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Postherpetic neuralgia (PHN) is a chronic and intractable pain disorder. Existing PHN prediction systems have limited clinical applicability. A systematic search of the literature was conducted in PubMed, Web of Science, Embase and the Cochrane Library between 1950 and 2025, finally 44 studies were included (PROSPERO: CRD420251011300). Training (n = 417) and validation (n = 228) datasets were obtained from First Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang Chinese Medical University and Third People’s Hospital of Hangzhou. Based on the effect sizes from the meta-analysis, a linear regression model was fitted using the training set to develop the PHN Prediction Scoring Scale (PPSS), which was subsequently validated in the independent validation set. Seven key risk factors identified from the meta-analysis were selected for scoring,, including age (odds ratio(OR) (95% confidence interval): 1.66 (1.47, 1.88)), acute pain (OR: 2.61 (2.24, 3.05)), prodromal pain (OR: 1.79 (1.38, 2.31)), severe rash (OR: 2.64 (1.95, 3.56)), rash on the head(OR: 1.96 (1.8, 2.13)), diabetes (OR: 1.34 (1.24, 1.44)), immunosuppression (OR: 2.23 (1.95, 2.56)). Based on the above factors and weights, the PPSS were constructed. The results showed that on the validation set the area under curve of PPSS was 0.759 (0.693–0.824), accuracy was 0.684 (0.624–0.745), precision was 0.515 (0.417–0.612), recall was 0.693 (0.589–0.798), specificity was 0.680 (0.606–0.754). These findings suggest that the PPSS holds promise for generalization and application in clinical practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-50555-6.

Introduction

Herpes zoster (HZ) is a cutaneous infectious disease caused by the varicella-zoster virus (VZV). It has been reported that approximately 1/3 of the population will develop HZ in their lifetime1. With growing awareness of HZ and its sequelae, the prevention and management of postherpetic neuralgia (PHN) have emerged as important goals in the treatment of HZ. PHN is the most common complication of HZ infection. According to most guidelines, it is defined as persistent pain lasting more than 90 days after the complete healing of the HZ rash2. The prevalence of PHN ranges from 5% to 30% in different regions and populations3,4. PHN imposes a significant burden of disease on the patient in terms of negatively affecting pain perception, sleep quality, and economic status5.

Numerous studies have shown that certain potent pain management methods are effective in preventing PHN, such as spinal cord stimulation, stellate ganglion block, and laser therapy6–8. However, given the current challenges in predicting PHN, these potent pain management methods are often utilized in the treatment of recalcitrant PHN rather than in the prevention of PHN during the clinical management of HZ9. This underscores the urgent need for accurate prediction of PHN as a critical clinical objective.

In recent years, with the intensive application of machine learning techniques in the clinical field, a large number of literatures have reported their machine learning prediction models of PHN10,11. However, due to a number of factors, these published models still face large challenges in terms of large-scale application. First, most of these models were modeled from single dataset and have not been externally validated. Second, these models were constructed using heterogeneous sets of predictors; although some variables may enhance model performance, they are not routinely collected in clinical practice. Finally, many models suffer from limited interpretability, whereas clinicians tend to prioritize key risk factors over complex algorithmic outputs.

Therefore, in this study, we integrated the effect values of the most commonly used risk factors in clinical practice through meta-analysis, constructed the PHN predictive scoring scale (PPSS) based on these effect values and verified it in two independent cohorts. With the aim of establishing a scoring table that is suitable for rapid screening of PHN risks and easy to understand.

Method

Meta-analysis

Our study followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines (PRISMA 2020)12. Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) registration number: CRD420251011300.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria: (1) Type of included studies: prospective and retrospective studies with at least 1 month of follow-up. (2) Study population: eligible studies whose participants were patients diagnosed with HZ based on typical symptoms or VZV-related laboratory tests. However, even if the specific diagnostic criteria for HZ were not clearly listed in the study, the study was equally included as long as the participants were clearly defined as patients with HZ in the methods Sect. (3) Study endpoint: The study endpoint was the onset of PHN. PHN was defined as pain persisting more than 1 to 6 months after HZ infection. Studies were also included in the analysis if they did not use the term PHN to define the outcome but described an outcome consistent with the characteristics of PHN.

Exclusion criteria: (1) Non-English publications, studies for which full text was not available, and studies with missing or non-transformable data were excluded. (2) A study was excluded if it did not use regression modeling to determine adjusted risk factors associated with PHN.

Information sources and search strategies

PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, and Cochrane Library databases were searched for articles published between the establishment of the databases and February 2025. Study search strategies are summarized in the Supplementary Material - search strategies.

PICOS framework

This systematic review clearly defined the research elements based on the PICOS framework, as follows:

P (participants): Patients clinically diagnosed with HZ or those with HZ-related disease codes.

I (intervention): Binary logistic regression model was used to identify potential risk factors for PHN in patients with HZ.

C (comparison): Patients grouped according to the presence or absence of PHN (PHN group vs. non-PHN group).

O (outcome): The primary outcome was to identify clinical and demographic characteristics significantly associated with PHN occurrence to clarify its risk factors.

S (study design): Cohort studies, case-control studies, and cross-sectional studies were included.

Selection process, data collection process and risk of bias assessment

Titles and abstracts of potentially eligible studies were screened by two independent researchers. Subsequently, the full text of these studies was systematically assessed to further confirm whether they met the inclusion criteria. In addition, two independent researchers assessed the potential for bias of the included literature based on the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS). In case of disagreement about the inclusion and evaluation of literature, it was resolved through discussion with another researcher.

For publications included in the analysis, the following information was systematically collected by two independent researchers: (1) basic information about the study, including authors, year of publication, country, study design, time of baseline data collection, sample size, and diagnostic criteria for HZ and PHN. (2) Elements used for assessing the risk of bias. (3) The adjusted odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (95%CI) corresponding to the potential risk factors. In subsequent calculations, these odds ratios will be transformed into logarithmic form (log(OR)) for statistical analysis. The following variables were selected: age, gender, obesity, smoking history, acute pain, prodromal pain, abnormal sensation, severe rash, rash site, duration of initial treatment, immunosuppressive, diabetes, autoimmune disease, anxiety or depressive symptoms, and history of tumor. In addition, we evaluated the consistency of the work of the two data administrators through Kappa test.

Statistical analysis

Meta analyses in this module were done based on the Metafor package in R 4.4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). The primary statistical measure employed in meta-analysis is the standardized regression coefficient (Beta), that is, log odds ratios (log(OR)). And the results of the analyses were presented in forest plots by converting Beta to OR (95%CI). I-square was used to assess the level of heterogeneity of the included studies. When I-square was less than 50%, the fixed-effects model was selected for meta-analysis; when I-square was greater than or equal to 50%, the random-effects model was used for meta-analysis. Further subgroup analyses were conducted for studies using different diagnostic criteria. If inter-study heterogeneity is significant, potential sources of heterogeneity will be explored through sensitivity analysis and meta-regression. Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots and egger’s test. If significant publication bias was found, the analysis was corrected using the Trim-and-fill method.

Study population

In this study, we collected clinical data from patients with HZ hospitalized (from 2022 to 2024) in The First Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang Chinese Medical University (TJHCM, used as the training set) and Hangzhou Third People’s Hospital (HTPH, used as the validation set). The procedures used in this study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All methods were conducted in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational studies in Epidemiology) guidelines for observational studies and local regulations.

The training set subject recruitment protocol was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of TJHCM (Ethical review number: 2024-KL-304-01). The Ethics Committee waived the requirement of patient informed consent given that this part was only a retrospective analysis of the existing database. The validation set subject recruitment protocol, on the other hand, was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of HTPH (Ethical review number: 2025-KC-001), and registration of the study was completed in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (NCT registration number: NCT06261801). All study participants included in the validation set signed an informed consent form before enrollment.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria were: (1) age > 18 years; (2) Meet clinical diagnostic criteria for HZ or have completed virologic testing, with specific diagnostic criteria referenced in the S2k guidelines published in 202013.

Exclusion criteria were: (1) those with > 30% missing case data; (2) Those failed to complete the follow-up assessments; (3) Study participants with neurological disorders or other disease that may interfere with pain perception, such as stroke, Alzheimer’s disease, etc.; (4) Study participants with atypical manifestations of HZ, such as visceral HZ or meningeal HZ; (5) Study participants who did not receive routine antiviral and analgesic treatments.

Primary outcome and covariates

The occurrence of PHN was the primary outcome of the study. Patients were followed up for three months from the date of their initial visit and confirmation of HZ diagnosis, in order to determine the incidence of PHN. PHN was defined as persistent pain lasting more than three months, with a Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) score greater than 3.

Information on the following covariates was collected for this study: (1) basic characteristics: including age, gender, BMI, and smoking history; (2) Rash and pain-related indicators: NRS score, prodromal pain, abnormal sensations, severe rash, and rash distribution site; (3) Comorbidities: including immunosuppressive and history of diabetes. All data were collected during the initial clinical consultation at the two participating medical institutions.

Acute pain is defined as the pain score obtained by the patient on the first day of consultation through the NRS. Prodromal pain was defined as pain that occurs before the appearance of a rash. Abnormal sensations were defined as burning, electric shock, itching and other abnormal sensations in addition to pain in the rash area. The definition of severe rash was that the rash spreads beyond the range of a single ganglion.

Variable filtering

Based on the results of the meta-analysis, to ensure the stability of the results, we used the variables with more than 5 included studies and positive results for the construction of the PPSS.

Statistical analysis

All analyses in this module were done based on R 4.4.2. Descriptive statistics were accomplished through the CBCgrps package. The cutoff value is calculated by the cutoff package, and all visualization work is accomplished through the ggplot package. Data from the training and validation sets were first analyzed for within-group univariate analysis of variance and between the two groups. Normally distributed data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, skewed data were expressed as median (quartiles), and categorical variables were described as n (%). The t-test was used for normally distributed data, Mann-whitney rank sum test was used for skewed data, and the Pearson chi-square test was used for nonparametric data. Differences were considered statistically significant if p < 0.05.

The process of handling missing values was as follows: variables with a missing rate exceeding 20% were excluded and not included in the subsequent modeling analysis; for variables with a missing rate below 20%, the random forest method was used to handle the missing values. The missing values in the training set and the validation set are filled independently to avoid data leakage and ensure the reliability of model evaluation.

The PPSS was constructed as follows: first, the beta coefficients obtained from the meta-analysis were multiplied by 10 and then rounded to assign scores to the variables. These specified scores are then used as the slope of a linear formula. The scores derived from this linear combination can to some extent be interpreted as an estimated probability of developing PHN after having shingles. Second, perform linear fitting of PPSS on the training set, the purpose of fitting PPSS on the training set is to determine the optimal cutoff value to effectively distinguish high-risk individuals from low-risk ones. The Cutoff package in R was used to calculate the optimal cutoff value. Ultimately, the study participant’s score was compared to this cutoff value, and if the score was greater than the cutoff value, it was determined that the participant was likely to have PHN.

The performance of PPSS is evaluated using validation set data. The prediction system is comprehensively evaluated by calculating the area under the curve (AUC), accuracy, precision, recall, specificity and Kappa index, and the results are visualized with the help of receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, calibration curves and decision curves.

Result

Meta-analysis

Study selection and study characterization

The search yielded a total of 24,365 relevant studies. After removing 5,668 duplicates, 18,697 studies remained. Screened by title and abstract, 18,300 of these studies were excluded, and 397 studies were retained. After further screening of the full text, the remaining 44 studies were ultimately included in the meta-analysis. In summary, a total of 44 papers were included in the meta-analysis, and the process by which the meta-analysis was conducted can be seen in Supplementary Fig. 114–57.

The 44 included studies included 21 prospective and 23 retrospective studies. The number of HZ patients involved in studies ranged from 40 to 308,857, and a total of 707,831 HZ patients were covered in meta-analysis. Of these, 36 studies diagnosed HZ by clinical symptoms combined with virologic testing, while 8 studies based their diagnosis on diagnostic codes. The definition of PHN varied across studies, as evidenced by different criteria for pain duration: 10 studies defined it as 1 month, 1 as 2 months, 27 as 3 months, 1 as 3.5 months, 1 as 4 months, 2 as 6 months, and 2 studies did not give a clear definition. Literature quality assessment revealed 39 studies with NOS > = 7 (high quality) and the remaining 5 studies with NOS scores between 4 and 6 (moderate quality). The Kappa test results (Supplementary Table 1), indicated that Lin and Wang had a high degree of consistency in literature inclusion (Kappa = 0.95 (95% confidence interval (CI): 0.90–0.99), data collection (Kappa = 0.91 (95% CI: 0.85–0.98), and literature evaluation (Kappa = 0.88 (95% CI: 0.79–0.96). Basic information about included studies can be found in Supplementary Table 2, and the NOS scores of the included studies can be found in Supplementary Table 3.

Meta-analysis of risk factors (Fig. 2)

Age

20 studies reported age (per decade/per year), by multiplying the beta value of age (per years) by 10 and combining it with the β value of age (per decades), then taking the natural logarithm of the combined beta value, the OR value corresponding to age (per decade) is ultimately calculated. The meta-analysis showed that age (per decade) (OR (95% CI) :1.66 (1.47, 1.88)) was a risk factor for PHN (Supplementary Fig. 2). 16 studies reported age (categorized), and the meta-analysis showed that age (> 50 vs. < 50) (OR (95%CI): 2.72 (2.15, 3.45)) was a risk factor for PHN (Supplementary Fig. 3).Fig. 2Forest plot of all factors-PHN.

Forest plot of all factors-PHN.

Gender

22 studies reported gender, and the meta-analysis showed that gender (female vs. male) (OR (95%CI): 1.02 (0.84, 1.25)) was not a risk factor for PHN (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Obesity

2 studies reported obesity, and the meta-analysis showed that obesity (OR (95%CI): 1.13 (1.05, 1.21)) was a risk factor for PHN (Supplementary Fig. 5).

Smoking

2 studies reported smoking, and the meta-analysis showed that smoking (OR (95% CI): 1.28 (1.19, 1.37)) was a risk factor for PHN (Supplementary Fig. 5).

Acute pain

22 studies reported acute pain, and the meta-analysis showed that acute pain (severe vs. moderate vs. mild) (OR (95% CI): 2.61 (2.24, 3.05)) was a risk factor for PHN (Supplementary Fig. 6).

Prodromal pain

10 studies reported prodromal pain, and the meta-analysis showed that prodromal pain (OR (95% CI): 1.79 (1.38, 2.31)) was a risk factor for PHN (Supplementary Fig. 7).

Abnormal sensation

4 studies reported abnormal sensation in the area of the rash, and meta-analysis showed that abnormal sensation (OR (95%CI): 2.51 (1.29, 4.92)) was a risk factor for PHN (Supplementary Fig. 8).

Severe rash

6 studies reported severe rash, and the meta-analysis showed that severe rash (moderate to severe vs. mild) (OR (95% CI): 2.64 (1.95, 3.56)) was a risk factor for PHN (Supplementary Fig. 9).

Rash site

6 studies reported rash on the head, and the meta-analysis showed that rash on the head (OR (95%CI): 1.96 (1.8, 2.13)) was a risk factor for PHN (Supplementary Fig. 10). 2 studies reported rash on the waist and sacrum, and the meta-analysis showed that rash on the waist and sacrum (OR (95%CI): 0.87 (0.46, 1.66)) was not a risk factor for PHN (Supplementary Fig. 10).

Initial treatment time

In the meta-analysis, the studies defined the initial treatment time as the time interval from the first appearance of rash or pain symptoms of herpes zoster to the initiation of systemic antiviral therapy. 2 studies reported the initial treatment time, and the meta-analysis showed that the initial treatment time (OR (95% CI): 1.3 (0.51, 3.33)) was not a risk factor for PHN (Supplementary Fig. 5).

Immunosuppression

15 studies reported immunosuppression, and the meta-analysis showed that immunosuppression (OR (95%CI): 2.23 (1.95, 2.56)) was a risk factor for PHN (Supplementary Fig. 11).

Diabetes

15 studies reported diabetes, and the meta-analysis showed that diabetes (OR (95%CI): 1.34 (1.24, 1.44)) was a risk factor for PHN (Supplementary Fig. 12).

Autoimmune diseases

Five data points from 2 studies reported autoimmune diseases, and the meta-analysis showed that autoimmune diseases (OR (95%CI): 1.5 (1.17, 1.92)) were risk factors for PHN (Supplementary Fig. 12).

Anxiety and depression

4 studies reported anxiety and depression, and meta-analysis showed that anxiety and depression (OR (95%CI): 1.43 (1.29, 1.6)) were risk factors for PHN (Supplementary Fig. 12).

Tumor

7 studies reported a history of tumor, and the meta-analysis showed that tumor (OR (95% CI): 1.06 (0.72, 1.57)) was not a risk factor for PHN (Supplementary Fig. 12).

Assessment of sources of heterogeneity

Heterogeneous risk factors: heterogeneity test showed high heterogeneity for age (per decade) (I-squared = 79.98%), age (categorization) (I-squared = 94.76%), gender (I-squared = 95.69%), diabetes (I-squared = 75.91%), autoimmune diseases (I-squared = 87.11%), tumor (I-squared = 91.00%). Sensitivity analysis (Supplementary Figs. 13–15) showed that the results remained robust after deleting any one study. Subgroup analyses (Supplementary Fig. 3) showed that different classification criteria for age was a source of heterogeneity. Meta-regression showed that the different definitions of PHN were one of the sources of heterogeneity in age, and the year of publication was one of the sources of heterogeneity in autoimmune diseases and tumor. The meta-regression of the remaining risk factors did not identify the source of heterogeneity.

To seek the robustness of the results, we conducted a sensitivity analysis on the variables included in the PPSS model based on different definitions of PHN (> 30 days and > 90 days). The relevant results are shown in Supplementary Fig. 16. The sensitivity analysis indicated that after uniform scaling of the units, there was no significant difference between the meta-analysis results with PHN > 90 days as the primary endpoint and the original analysis results. Therefore, we believe that the existing evidence supports the robustness of the current model.

Other risk factors: heterogeneity test showed that obesity (I-squared = 0%), smoking (I-squared = 0%), acute pain (I-squared = 21.24%), prodromal pain (I-squared = 0%), severe rash (I-squared = 0%), rash on the head (I-squared = 0%), rash on the waist and sacrum (I-squared = 0%), immunosuppression (I-squared = 7.68%), and anxiety and depression (I-squared = 24.59%) were less heterogeneous. Although there was some heterogeneity in abnormal sensations (I-squared = 64.90%) and initial treatment time (I-squared = 70.74%), sensitivity analyses and subgroup analyses were not performed due to the small number of literatures included.

Publication bias assessment

By Egger’s test (Supplementary Figs. 2–12) and funnel plot test (Supplementary Figs. 17–20), acute pain (Egger’s test: p = 0.001), abnormal sensation (Egger’s test: p = 0.008), diabetes (Egger ‘s test: p = 0.037) had potential publication bias. After filling by Trim and Fill Method (Supplementary Figs. 6, 8, 12), indicating the robustness of the previous results despite potential publication bias.

On the training set, the performance metrics of PPSS were as follows: the AUC was 0.853(95%CI: 0.815–0.89), accuracy was 0.772(95%CI: 0.732–0.812), precision was 0.769(95%CI: 0.703–0.835), recall was 0.67(95%CI: 0.602–0.739), specificity was 0.849(95%: 0.803–0.894), Cohen’s Kappa was 0.528(95CI%: 0.444–0.611), and the cutoff value was 40.

On the validation set, the performance metrics as follows: the AUC was 0.759 (95%CI: 0.693–0.824), accuracy was 0.684 (95%CI: 0.624–0.745), precision was 0.515 (95%CI: 0.417–0.612), recall was 0.693 (95%CI: 0.589–0.798), specificity was 0.680 (95%CI: 0.606–0.754), and Cohen’s Kappa was 0.343 (95%CI: 0.217–0.468).

The calibration curve shows that the PPSS model has a good consistency between the predicted probabilities and the actual observed probabilities on both the training set and the validation set, indicating that the model has a relatively high calibration accuracy. The Brier scores of the model on the training set and the validation set are 0.152 and 0.179 respectively, further verifying its prediction accuracy. The decision curve analysis shows that within different disease onset risk threshold ranges, the net benefit brought by using the PPSS model for predicting postherpetic neuralgia is significantly higher than the strategy of not using the model.

Discussion

PHN prediction system

This is the first study to date that utilizes a meta-analysis to establish a prediction system for PHN. The PPSS offers a balance between acceptable predictive performance and a simple, practical structure. All key elements required for the PPSS assessment can be obtained through direct patient interview, making it highly accessible in clinical settings. A previous meta-analysis of PHN prediction models reported that the area under the curve (AUC) of machine learning-based models reached 0.87 (95% CI: 0.83–0.89)58. Although the predictive performance of the PPSS (AUC: 0.76 (95%CI: 0.69–0.82)) is slightly less than that of the machine learning model, it is more intuitive, easier for physicians to understand, and more applicable to clinical practice. Therefore, the PPSS holds promise for generalization and practical implementation.

In clinical practice, HZ patients with a PPSS score greater than 40 are considered to be at higher risk of developing PHN. For such patients, the use of advanced pain management strategies as described previously may help to reduce the incidence of PHN. Conversely, HZ patients with a PPSS below 40 have a lower probability of developing PHN and may be more suitable for conventional analgesic and antiviral therapy, thereby contributing to the rational allocation of healthcare resources. It should be noted that, in the validation set, the PPSS demonstrated superior performance in terms of accuracy, recall, and specificity, but showed relatively low precision (0.515). This result suggests that PPSS may exhibit a certain rate of false positives in the identification of positive outcomes, while it shows high accuracy in the judgment of negative results.

Summary of meta-analysis evidence

The meta-analysis identified age, smoking, acute phase pain intensity, prodromal pain, severe rash, rash on the head, immunosuppression, diabetes mellitus, anxiety and depression, and autoimmune disorders as significant risk factors for PHN. In contrast, gender and lumbosacral rash were not significantly associated with PHN. These findings are qualitatively consistent with previously published meta-analyses, although some differences in effect sizes were observed59. In addition, the present study draws the following new conclusions: abnormal sensations in the rash area (itching, burning, etc.) and obesity were risk factor for PHN, and history of tumor was not significantly associated with PHN.

The underlying mechanisms by which VZV reactivation triggers PHN remain incompletely understood, largely because VZV is a human-specific pathogen and it is difficult to establish an animal model of pain after reactivation60. Based on the existing literature review, the pathogenesis of PHN can be summarized into two aspects: viral reactivation due to weakened immune surveillance and peripheral and central sensitization due to ganglionic inflammation61.

The main causes of diminished immune surveillance include immune senescence and immunosuppression. When exploring the relationship between age and PHN, immune senescence is an important factor that cannot be ignored. During aging, thymic involution leads to reduced T-cell output, which in turn triggers a decline in immune function and slows down the clearance process of VZV infection62. At the same time, this age-related immune change creates a characteristic chronic inflammatory state, which may result in a synergistic inflammatory effect when VZV is reactivated63,64. Furthermore, evidence suggests that smoking and metabolic disorders accelerate the progression of immune senescence65,66. Immunosuppression, mainly caused by autoimmune diseases and artificially administered immunosuppressive treatments, is also associated with a significant decline in the function of T-cell subsets, which increases the risk of serious infections67.

The severity of rash and acute pain may reflect the extent of neuroinflammatory damage during the acute phase of HZ Peripheral and central sensitization are the most direct mechanisms leading to PHN68. On the one hand, neuroinflammatory necrosis promotes the release of inflammatory mediators, which lowers the thresholds of peripheral injury receptors and enhances nerve impulse firing, leading to peripheral sensitization69. On the other hand, persistent neuroinflammatory injury in later stages induces structural changes in the dorsal horn of the spinal cord, resulting in an increased number of afferent fibers responsive to noxious stimuli and ultimately leading to central sensitization70. Another risk factor for PHN is the appearance of the rash on the head. This may be attributable to the anatomical proximity of cranial nerves to the central nervous system and the fact that their myelin sheaths are formed by oligodendrocytes rather than Schwann cells. Compared to the myelin sheath of the peripheral nervous system, it is less tolerant of mechanical stimuli and has a relatively limited ability to repair itself after inflammatory injury71.

The link between abnormal sensation in the rash area and PHN was not mentioned in previous meta-analyses, and our study strengthens the link. One study has reported that inflammatory mediators secreted by ganglia may be responsible for abnormal sensation and thermal sensory sensitization72. In clinical practice, dermatologists usually focus more on assessing the severity of rashes and acute pain and relatively less on abnormal sensations. We recommend that dermatologists and clinical researchers devote greater attention to abnormal sensory symptoms in patients with HZ during both routine practice and clinical investigations. Such efforts may enable more accurate assessment of PHN risk and improve the quality of evidence available for future meta-analyses.

Strengths and limitations

Compared with previous studies, the present study has several advantages. First, this represents the first study to utilize the meta-analysis results of PHN-related risk factors for quantitative assessment. The proposed PPSS achieves a balance between parsimony and predictive performance, and its structure is readily interpretable for clinicians. Second, all studies included in this meta-analysis were published in English, and the overall quality of these papers was good, so the effect sizes obtained were highly reliable. Finally, the data used for modeling and validation in this study came from two independent cohorts, and the PPSS demonstrated consistent predictive ability across both, suggesting that the scoring system possesses a certain degree of external validity and generalizability.

Several limitations of this study should also be acknowledged. First, the dataset used for modeling and validation has a small sample size and lacks population representativeness, which limits the generalization and application of PPSS to some extent. Moreover, considerable heterogeneity was observed between the two datasets, which may affect the accuracy of the performance evaluation of PPSS. Our group is actively seeking collaborators to address this issue in future work. Second, owing to the heterogeneities in the definition of PHN across the studies included in the meta-analysis, there may be some impact on the robustness of the results. Finally, only predictors that were statistically significant in at least five studies were retained in the scoring system. While this approach prioritizes robust predictors, it may inadvertently exclude clinically relevant factors that did not reach significance in the existing literature. This limitation could lead to the omission of potentially important variables. Future high-quality research is needed to further refine the construction and validation of the PPSS and to mitigate the impact of such biases.

Conclusion

In this study, we quantitatively assigned scores to seven key risk factors, namely, age, acute pain, prodromal pain, severe rash, rash on the head, diabetes, immunosuppression based on the results of the meta-analysis, and constructed the PPSS accordingly. This scoring system demonstrated acceptable predictive performance in the training set and validation set. Therefore, we recommend that clinicians use the PPSS to evaluate patients with HZ, thereby identifying those at high risk of developing PHN and enabling timely administration of active pain interventions to help reduce the incidence of PHN.
