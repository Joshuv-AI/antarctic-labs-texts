Real-World Experience with Janus Kinase Inhibitors in Severe Refractory Hidradenitis Suppurativa: A Retrospective Case Series
Authors: Yingying Dai, Yuxiao Han, Siyu Zhang, Haotian Chen, Qin Yi, Xian Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHidradenitis suppurativa (HS) is a chronic inflammatory disease with limited treatment options for patients with severe, treatment-refractory disease. The JAK–STAT pathway represents a potential therapeutic target, although real-world evidence for JAK inhibitors (JAKi) in HS remains limited. This retrospective case series describes eight male patients with Hurley stage III HS who received tofacitinib (n=3), upadacitinib (n=4), or ivarmacitinib (n=1) after inadequate response to conventional therapies. Clinical outcomes were assessed using the International Hidradenitis Suppurativa Severity Score System (IHS4) and a numerical rating scale (NRS) for pain at the latest available follow-up. Follow-up ranged from 1 week to 12 months. Seven patients showed improvement in inflammatory lesions and/or pain, whereas one patient receiving tofacitinib showed limited clinical benefit. The median reduction in IHS4 score was 9.5 points (range, 2–18), and the median reduction in NRS pain score was 4.0 points (range, 0–5). Temporary interruption of upadacitinib in one patient was followed by disease flare, with improvement again observed after treatment re-initiation. No severe infections, thromboembolic events, HBV reactivation, or treatment-limiting adverse events were identified during the available follow-up. These preliminary observations suggest that JAKi may have a potential role in selected patients with severe, treatment-refractory HS. However, responses and follow-up durations were heterogeneous, and the small sample size precludes comparisons between agents. Prospective controlled studies with standardized assessment timepoints and longer follow-up are needed to clarify efficacy, durability, and long-term safety.

Introduction

Hidradenitis suppurativa (HS) is a chronic, recurrent, and debilitating inflammatory skin disease of the pilosebaceous unit, characterized by painful nodules, abscesses, draining sinus tracts, and scarring, predominantly in intertriginous areas.1 The worldwide prevalence averages approximately 1%, with the highest standardized prevalence occurring in patients aged 18–39 years, significantly affecting quality of life across physical, psychological, social, and occupational domains.2 Although its pathogenesis is multifactorial and incompletely elucidated, follicular occlusion with subsequent rupture and a dysregulated immune response are considered key events that drive persistent inflammation and tissue remodeling.3 Genetic susceptibility, including variants affecting γ-secretase, Notch, and Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathways, may further contribute to disease development in a subset of patients.4

Therapeutic options for moderate-to-severe HS have expanded in recent years, with the approval of biologic agents targeting TNF-α (adalimumab) and the IL-17 axis (secukinumab and bimekizumab). Nevertheless, a clinically important proportion of patients fail to achieve adequate disease control, while others experience secondary loss of response after an initial improvement.5,6 Treatment options remain particularly limited for patients with severe Hurley stage III disease who have persistent inflammatory activity despite systemic antibiotics, biologic therapy, and surgical procedures. These unmet needs support the investigation of treatments with alternative and potentially broader mechanisms of action.5,6

The Janus kinase–signal transducer and activator of transcription (JAK–STAT) pathway represents a biologically plausible therapeutic target in HS, as it integrates signaling from multiple cytokines implicated in HS inflammation, including IL-6, interferons, and IL-12/23-related pathways.7,8 Unlike monoclonal antibodies that neutralize individual extracellular cytokines, JAK inhibitors (JAKi) are small molecules capable of modulating downstream signaling of multiple cytokine pathways, potentially enabling broader immunomodulatory effects.9 JAKi differ in kinase selectivity and established clinical applications. Tofacitinib, which predominantly inhibits JAK1 and JAK3, has been used in rheumatoid arthritis, psoriatic arthritis, and ulcerative colitis; the JAK1-selective inhibitor upadacitinib has been used across rheumatoid arthritis, atopic dermatitis, and inflammatory bowel disease; and JAK1/JAK2 inhibitors such as baricitinib have been used in rheumatoid arthritis, atopic dermatitis, and alopecia areata.10

HS lesions demonstrate dysregulation of multiple inflammatory mediators that signal directly or indirectly through JAK–STAT-dependent pathways, supporting investigation of this pathway in refractory disease.11,12 Emerging clinical trials and real-world studies have reported potential benefit from systemic JAK1 inhibition in moderate-to-severe HS, particularly with upadacitinib and povorcitinib.13–16 However, real-world evidence remains limited, especially in patients with severe, treatment-refractory Hurley stage III disease, and comparatively little is known about clinical responses across different JAK inhibitors. Evidence regarding ivarmacitinib, a selective JAK1 inhibitor, is particularly scarce in HS.

We therefore report a real-world case series of eight male patients with severe, treatment-refractory Hurley stage III HS who received tofacitinib, upadacitinib, or ivarmacitinib. The study aimed to describe patient-level clinical responses, treatment courses, and observed tolerability across these three JAK inhibitors, including an initial clinical experience with ivarmacitinib in HS.

Methods

Study Design and Patients

This retrospective case series included patients with Hurley stage III HS who received JAKi therapy at the Department of Dermatology, West China Hospital, Sichuan University, between January 2022 and January 2026. Clinical data available through March 1, 2026, were retrieved from the dermatology electronic medical record system. All patients who received JAKi therapy for HS during the study period and had evaluable baseline and follow-up clinical data were included. Cases were not selected according to treatment response, safety outcomes, or follow-up results.

Treatment-refractory HS was defined clinically as persistent inflammatory nodules, abscesses, and/or draining tunnels despite appropriate conventional systemic treatment, or inability to continue such treatment because of inadequate response, intolerance, or contraindications. The decision to initiate JAKi therapy was made by dermatologists experienced in HS management after considering disease activity, previous treatments, comorbidities, tolerability, and individual patient circumstances.

Treatment and Assessments

The selection and dose of tofacitinib, upadacitinib, or ivarmacitinib were individualized during routine clinical care rather than assigned according to a study protocol. Treatment decisions were based on previous treatment exposure, disease manifestations, comorbidities, available clinical evidence, medication accessibility, tolerability, and patient preference. Concomitant systemic treatments during the assessment period were also recorded.

Clinical severity was evaluated using Hurley staging and the International Hidradenitis Suppurativa Severity Score System (IHS4). IHS4 was calculated as the number of inflammatory nodules plus twice the number of abscesses plus four times the number of draining tunnels.17 Pain was assessed using an 11-point numerical rating scale (NRS), ranging from 0 to 10. IHS4 assessments were performed and recorded by the treating dermatologists during routine clinical visits, whereas NRS pain scores were self-reported by patients and documented by the treating clinicians. These scores were recorded as part of routine clinical care and were not retrospectively reconstructed from medical photographs.

Because follow-up was conducted according to routine clinical practice rather than a study-mandated schedule, the latest visit with available IHS4 and NRS data was used for each patient. Clinical photographs were used as supplementary visual documentation and were not used to derive clinical scores.

Before JAKi initiation, patients underwent routine clinical and laboratory safety assessments, including blood counts, liver and renal function tests, lipid profiles, infection screening, and review of relevant comorbidities and treatment risks. During treatment, laboratory monitoring and adverse-event assessment were performed according to clinical need. Safety information was obtained from electronic medical records, routine clinical follow-up documentation, and telephone follow-up conducted as part of the retrospective review. For the patient with chronic hepatitis B virus infection, entecavir was initiated before JAKi treatment, and liver function and HBV DNA were monitored during therapy.

Statistical Analysis

Given the descriptive nature and small sample size of this case series, no formal hypothesis testing was performed. Continuous variables were summarized as medians and ranges, and categorical variables as numbers. Changes in IHS4 and NRS scores were calculated from baseline to the latest available follow-up with available score data. These pooled changes were regarded as descriptive summaries and were not used to compare different JAKi or dosing regimens.

Results

Patient Characteristics

Eight male patients with severe, treatment-refractory Hurley stage III HS who received JAKi therapy were included in this retrospective case series. The median age was 42.5 years (range, 17–55 years). Disease involvement included the buttocks, axillae, groin, scalp, face, and lower extremities. Several patients had clinically relevant comorbidities, including psoriasis, Behçet disease, obesity, chronic hepatitis B virus carriage, heart failure, emphysema, and a history of rectal neuroendocrine tumor, and three patients had undergone previous HS-related surgery. Patients received tofacitinib (n = 3), upadacitinib (n = 4), or ivarmacitinib (n = 1). Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Individual Case Presentations

Case 1 was a 52-year-old man with a 40-year smoking history and concomitant psoriasis who presented with buttock HS characterized by erythema, ulceration, and purulent drainage. Following an inadequate response to conventional therapies, tofacitinib 5 mg twice daily was initiated. Improvement in ulceration and purulent drainage was observed after 2 weeks of treatment. At the 3-month assessment, marked reductions in erythema, ulceration, and purulent discharge were documented, corresponding to the post-treatment assessment summarized in Figure 1.

Case 2 was a 41-year-old non-smoking man with Behçet disease, sacroiliitis, chronic enteritis with ulceration, and heart failure who presented with HS involving the buttocks and axillae. He had previously undergone two surgical interventions. Following an inadequate response to prior therapies, tofacitinib 5 mg once daily was initiated while the patient was receiving methotrexate 10 mg weekly and prednisone 40 mg daily for concomitant Behçet disease. At the 2-month assessment, inflammatory lesions persisted without meaningful clinical improvement, and the patient subsequently discontinued tofacitinib.

Case 3 was a 44-year-old non-smoking man without significant comorbidities who presented with recurrent abscesses involving the scalp, face, and axillae. Following an inadequate response to prior medical therapy, tofacitinib 5 mg twice daily was initiated. At the 3-month assessment, substantial reductions in abscess formation and inflammatory activity were documented, corresponding to the post-treatment assessment summarized in Figure 1 and the clinical images presented in Figure S1.

Case 4 was a 17-year-old man with a 1-year smoking history and a previous surgical intervention who presented with bilateral axillary abscesses. Following an inadequate response to prior conventional therapies, upadacitinib 15 mg once daily was initiated. Clinical improvement was observed after 1 week of treatment, with reductions in inflammatory nodules and abscesses. At the 4-month assessment, marked clinical improvement was documented, with resolution of active abscesses and only a few residual inflammatory nodules and post-inflammatory scars, corresponding to the post-treatment assessment summarized in Figure 1 and the clinical images presented in Figure S2.

Case 5 was a 55-year-old man with a 40-year smoking history and obesity (body mass index, 31.25 kg/m2) who presented with multiple abscesses and nodules involving the scalp and groin. Following an inadequate response to conventional therapies, upadacitinib 30 mg once daily was initiated. Clinical improvement was observed during treatment. A temporary 2-week interruption after 9 months of therapy was followed by disease flare; improvement was again observed after treatment re-initiation. At the 12-month assessment, only minimal residual scarring was observed, corresponding to the post-treatment assessment summarized in Figure 1.

Case 6 was a 22-year-old non-smoking man with a history of surgical treatment who presented with inflammatory nodules and draining sinus tracts involving the inguinal region. Following an inadequate response to previous therapies, including biologic treatment, upadacitinib 30 mg once daily was initiated. At the 1-week clinical assessment, reductions in inflammatory lesions and pain were documented, corresponding to the post-treatment IHS4 and NRS scores summarized in Figure 1. A patient-provided photograph obtained after 1 month of treatment showed further visible reduction in inflammatory lesions and active drainage (Figure S3); however, no standardized IHS4 or NRS assessment was available at that timepoint.

Case 7 was a 52-year-old man with a 30-year smoking history, a rectal neuroendocrine tumor, and emphysema who presented with buttock abscesses. Following an inadequate response to conventional therapies, ivarmacitinib 8 mg once daily was initiated. At the 1-month assessment, reductions in abscess formation and pain were documented, with occasional mild recurrence of small papular lesions, corresponding to the post-treatment assessment summarized in Figure 1.

Case 8 was a 38-year-old non-smoking man with chronic hepatitis B virus carriage who presented with disseminated nodules, abscesses, cysts, and draining sinus tracts involving multiple anatomical sites (Figure 2). Following an inadequate response to conventional therapies, upadacitinib 30 mg once daily was initiated. Closure of multiple sinus tracts was observed after 1 week of treatment. Transient isolated pustules appeared during the second week but resolved spontaneously. At the 1-month assessment, marked reductions in inflammatory lesions and pain were documented, corresponding to the post-treatment assessment summarized in Figure 1 and the clinical response presented in Figure 2. Discussion

This retrospective case series describes the real-world use of three JAK inhibitors—tofacitinib, upadacitinib, and ivarmacitinib—in eight male patients with severe, treatment-refractory Hurley stage III HS. At the latest available follow-up, JAKi therapy was associated with a median reduction of 9.5 points in IHS4 and 4.0 points in NRS pain scores. Clinical responses were heterogeneous: seven patients showed clinically apparent improvement in inflammatory lesion burden and/or pain, whereas Case 2 had only limited benefit from tofacitinib. In Case 5, temporary interruption of upadacitinib was followed by disease flare, with improvement after treatment re-initiation. This observation suggests that continued pathway suppression may be required to maintain disease control in some patients, although causality cannot be established from an uncontrolled case series. No severe infections, thromboembolic events, HBV reactivation, or treatment-limiting adverse events were identified during the available follow-up; however, the small sample size and short, variable follow-up preclude conclusions regarding the safety of JAK inhibition in HS.

The therapeutic rationale for JAK inhibition in HS is based on the role of the JAK–STAT pathway as an intracellular signaling hub for multiple cytokines implicated in disease pathogenesis. Follicular occlusion and rupture initiate innate immune activation and sustained production of pro-inflammatory mediators within lesional skin.18,19 Th17-associated cytokines and related inflammatory circuits contribute to amplification and persistence of disease activity.20,21 Several cytokines relevant to HS—including IL-6, interferons, and IL-12/23-related pathways—signal through JAK–STAT components.22,23 STAT3-mediated transcription also contributes to inflammatory T-cell differentiation and tissue remodeling processes relevant to chronic disease.24–26 Unlike biologic agents that target individual extracellular cytokines, JAK inhibitors can modulate signaling from multiple cytokine receptors and may therefore attenuate converging inflammatory pathways.27–30

Clinical evidence for JAK inhibition in HS is increasing but remains preliminary (Table 2 and Table S1). Among systemic JAK inhibitors, upadacitinib currently has the most developed clinical evidence. A Phase 2 randomized study reported improvement in moderate-to-severe HS, and a real-world cohort similarly described favorable outcomes in refractory disease.13,14 The reductions in IHS4 and NRS observed among Cases 4, 5, 6, and 8 are directionally consistent with these reports, although direct comparisons are limited by differences in study design, patient characteristics, treatment duration, and outcome-assessment timepoints. Evidence for tofacitinib in HS is currently limited to small case reports and case series.34,35 In the present cohort, responses were variable: Cases 1 and 3 improved, whereas Case 2 experienced limited benefit. This variability may reflect differences in disease biology, comorbidities, previous treatments, concomitant therapies, dose, and duration of exposure, rather than a clear drug-specific effect. Ivarmacitinib (SHR0302), a selective JAK1 inhibitor, was associated with reductions in inflammatory lesions and pain in Case 7. Ivarmacitinib has demonstrated anti-inflammatory efficacy in other immune-mediated dermatoses, but evidence in HS is currently insufficient.36,37 Accordingly, the response observed in this single patient should be regarded as an initial clinical observation rather than evidence of efficacy or of superiority of selective JAK1 inhibition.

The wide range of follow-up durations is an important consideration when interpreting these findings. The latest available follow-up ranged from 1 week to 12 months, and outcomes obtained at these different timepoints are not directly comparable. A short assessment may capture an early response without establishing durability, whereas a longer assessment may be influenced by treatment interruption, changes in adherence, concomitant therapy, and the natural fluctuation of HS. Therefore, the pooled median changes in IHS4 and NRS should be interpreted as descriptive summaries of this case series rather than standardized estimates of treatment effect. The flare after upadacitinib interruption in Case 5 and improvement after re-initiation are clinically notable, but they cannot distinguish a treatment effect from temporal disease fluctuation in the absence of controlled observation.

Although no severe infections, thromboembolic events, HBV reactivation, or treatment-limiting adverse events were identified during the available follow-up, the cohort included patients with potential risk factors such as long-term smoking, heart failure, chronic hepatitis B virus carriage, pulmonary disease, and a history of malignancy. The available follow-up was insufficient to evaluate uncommon or delayed adverse outcomes, including serious infection, hepatitis reactivation, major adverse cardiovascular events, venous thromboembolism, and malignancy. Safety concerns associated with systemic JAK inhibition include serious infection, malignancy, major adverse cardiovascular events, and thromboembolic events, particularly among older patients and those with cardiovascular or smoking-related risk factors.30,38 Careful patient selection, pretreatment risk assessment, and ongoing laboratory and clinical monitoring are therefore necessary when these agents are considered for off-label treatment of HS.

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective, single-center design and small sample size introduce the potential for selection bias and limit the precision and generalizability of the observations. Second, the absence of a control group prevents attribution of clinical improvement solely to JAK inhibition; spontaneous disease fluctuation, concomitant therapies, and differences in treatment adherence may also have contributed. Third, treatment selection, dosing, prior treatment exposure, comorbidities, and follow-up duration were heterogeneous, and post-treatment assessments were not performed at a standardized timepoint. Fourth, the relatively short and variable observation period limits assessment of long-term safety outcomes associated with systemic JAK inhibitors, including major adverse cardiovascular events, malignancy, and thromboembolic events.30,38 Fifth, all patients were male and had Hurley stage III disease; therefore, the findings may not be generalizable to female patients, patients with milder disease, or broader HS populations. Finally, the use of three different JAK inhibitors in a limited number of patients precludes meaningful comparisons between agents or conclusions regarding the relative effectiveness of selective versus non-selective JAK inhibition. Therefore, careful patient selection and long-term safety monitoring remain essential in clinical practice.

In conclusion, this small retrospective case series provides preliminary, hypothesis-generating observations suggesting that JAK inhibition may have a role as salvage therapy in selected patients with severe, treatment-refractory Hurley stage III HS. Improvement was observed in most patients, but responses were heterogeneous, and the durability and long-term safety of treatment remain uncertain. These findings support further prospective, controlled studies using standardized assessment timepoints to define efficacy, safety, optimal treatment duration, and patient-selection criteria for JAK inhibitors in HS.
