A multi species autophagy-inducing nano-vaccine platform: uncovering immunoenhancement properties of PCV2 cap nanoparticles
Authors: Zhuofan Deng, He Qiu, Xudong Yin, Huiling Xu, Shengkun Zhang, Meiqi Sun, Haotian Yang, Han Gu, Weihuan Fang, Xiaoliang Li, Fang He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cap VLPs (virus-like-particles) are self-assembling nanoparticles (NPs) derived from PCV2 ORF2 with ordered and repetitive antigen display, which have been widely used as a landmark veterinary vaccine in the swine industry. Building upon the pivotal role of virus-activated autophagy in antigen presentation and immune regulation, efforts were made to identify the key molecular elements and mechanisms by which Cap NPs activate autophagy to enhance immunogenicity and expand their application as a vaccine platform. Self-assembled Cap NPs exhibited efficient cell internalization, upregulated various immune regulatory cytokines, particularly TNF-α, and promoted the expression of molecules involved in antigen presentation. Cap NPs induced autophagy by activating the CaMKKβ-AMPK-mTOR pathway and elevating intracellular ROS, which facilitated the processing and degradation of the Cap antigen and led to subsequent activation of TNF-α signaling. Inhibition of either autophagy or TNF-α downregulated antigen presentation. The animal immunization experiment confirmed that Cap NP-induced autophagy and TNF-α signaling promoted humoral and cellular immune responses. To further investigate the key components affecting immunogenicity, truncated Cap peptides were displayed on self-assembled mi3 nanoparticles. Among these, the chimeric mC-9-10 NPs autonomously induced autophagy-TNF-α signaling and stimulated immune responses, suggesting potential adjuvant-like activity. More importantly, Cap NPs were able to enter and activate autophagy-TNF-α signaling in multiple cell lines across different species, making them candidates for a broad-spectrum antigen delivery nano-vaccine platform. Overall, this work not only systematically elucidates the complete molecular mechanisms by which Cap NPs enhance immune responses through cellular internalization, autophagy induction, TNF-α signaling activation, and antigen presentation, but also highlights the great potential of Cap NPs and their functional peptides as multi-species nano-vaccine carriers and novel immune adjuvants. These findings provide a solid theoretical foundation and technical feasibility for the design of next-generation nano-delivery platforms and the development of immune-enhancing tools.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04527-7.

Introduction

In recent years, nanotechnology has garnered considerable attention in vaccine development. As a novel vaccine vehicle, self-assembled protein nanoparticles (SAPNs) can efficiently present antigens to immune cells and activate innate and adaptive immune responses [1, 2]. Among these SAPNs, virus-like particles (VLPs) formed by viral protein self-assembly hold great promise for applications, owing to their adjustable size, high immunogenicity, excellent biocompatibility and low production costs [3]. Some VLP-based nano-vaccines have been commercially available against human papilloma virus (HPV), hepatitis B virus (HBV) and Norwalk virus [3, 4].

Porcine circovirus type 2 (PCV2), a small non-enveloped DNA virus belonging to the genus Circovirus of the Circoviridae family, is considered the main pathogen of porcine circovirus-associated diseases (PCVAD) [5]. PCV2 capsid protein (Cap) encoded by open reading frame 2 (ORF2) consists of 60 protein subunits [5]. As the main immunogenic protein, PCV2 Cap has been applied in the development of subunit vaccines. To date, vaccination remains the major approach for preventing and controlling viral infections. Many studies have found that Cap protein can be successfully expressed in vitro, and spontaneously assemble into nanoparticles (NPs) of approximately 17 nm, also known as VLPs [6, 7]. Cap NPs-based vaccines can trigger strong host immune responses through the ordered and repetitive arrangement of Cap subunits [8]. More importantly, Cap NPs are also found to serve as an excellent delivery platform by displaying multiple copies of exogenous peptides. Antigenic epitopes from classical swine fever virus (CSFV), foot-and-mouth disease virus (FMDV), or porcine reproductive and respiratory syndrome virus (PRRSV) can be integrated into the cap gene to generate various chimeric nano-vaccines [8–10]. Compared to monovalent vaccines, these chimeric vaccines typically induce higher levels of immune responses against exogenous antigens. However, most research focuses on the assessment of vaccine efficacy of Cap NPs, while the mechanisms for enhanced immune responses remain unclear.

Autophagy is a highly conserved cellular process involved in maintaining cellular homeostasis through the degradation and recycling of cellular components, such as organelles, protein aggregates, and intracellular pathogens [11]. This complex process plays a crucial role in various physiological and pathological conditions, including immunity. Studies have demonstrated that autophagy contributes to antigen uptake by engulfing damaged organelles, pathogens, and extracellular proteins such as NPs, and delivering them to lysosomes for degradation [12, 13]. This mechanism of antigen processing allows cells to specifically target and eliminate pathogens, ultimately enhancing immune responses against infection. Simultaneously, this process also triggers the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α), interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and interferons (IFNs), thus promoting the activation of immune cells [12, 14, 15]. Furthermore, autophagy also regulates the differentiation of immune cells, including macrophages, dendritic cells, and T cells, influencing their effector functions and overall immune responses [13, 16]. Given the important role of autophagy in immune regulation, vaccine optimization strategies targeting autophagy have been reported. For example, a recombinant BCG vaccine containing an autophagy-inducing C5 peptide enhances MHC-II-dependent antigen presentation and elicits stronger expression of IL-12, IL-1β, and TNF-α in mice [17]. Nevertheless, there is little research on how vaccine antigens activate autophagy.

Tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) is a pleiotropic cytokine that plays a central role in inflammation, immunity and apoptosis [18, 19]. TNF-α is produced by macrophages, T lymphocytes and natural killer cells [20]. The main biological function of TNF-α is to activate macrophages and granulocytes, and enhance the phagocytic and killing activities [21]. Besides, TNF-α can also stimulate monocytes/macrophages to secrete cytokines and promote gene expression related to anti-infection immune responses [22]. A study has shown that an anti-tuberculous vaccine that inhibits TNF-α-downregulating genes in Mycobacterium tuberculosis can mediate TNF-α production by host cells, thereby enhancing immunogenicity [23]. Recent studies have suggested that there may be a crosstalk between autophagy and TNF-α signaling. Inhibition of autophagy by chloroquine (CQ) attenuates LPS-induced release of TNF-α and IL-6 [24]. The induction of mitophagy by TNF-α enables MHC-I to process and present mitochondrial antigens on mouse macrophage surfaces [25]. The relationship between autophagy and TNF-α signaling, as well as their impacts on vaccine immunity, still need further research.

PCV2 Cap NPs have been commercially available for subunit vaccine development. Our previous studies have revealed that PCV2 infection and Cap protein transfection can trigger autophagy via calcium/calmodulin-dependent kinase kinase beta (CaMKKβ)-AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK)-mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway and promote viral replication [26–28]. Cap NPs-based vaccines are also found to elicit strong TNF-α signaling [29]. Hence, we prepared Cap NPs and systemically studied their interactions with autophagy markers, TNF-α signaling and antigen presentation molecules in vitro and in vivo. The study aims to explore the mechanisms of the superior immune-enhancing efficacy of Cap NPs and their diverse potential applications. Here, we reported that Cap NPs could promote the processing and presentation of self-antigen by activating autophagy and TNF-α signaling nexus in host cells, thereby enhancing Cap-specific immune responses. The chimeric mi3-Cap peptide-9-10 NPs were observed to trigger autophagy-TNF-α signaling and elevate immune response related factors, suggesting that the Cap fragment (9–10) may contribute to the adjuvant-like activity of the mi3-Cap chimeric nanoparticles. Meanwhile, the excellent multi-species cell internalization efficiency and autophagy activation ability of Cap NPs also made them a potential universal vaccine platform.

Results

Cap NPs could internalize efficiently and trigger autophagy in 3D4 cells

To characterize Cap NPs, the sequence of PCV2 ORF2 containing a C-terminal 6×His tag was cloned into the vector pET-30a and expressed in E. coli. A ΔCap mutant lacking critical residues (16-45aa) for self-assembly into NPs was used as a control [7]. The fusion proteins were one-step purified by Ni-NTA agarose. Figure 1A shows that purified Cap and ΔCap proteins are consistent with the predicted sizes, and exhibit reactivity with the Cap monoclonal antibody. In transmission electron microscopy, wild-type Cap was observed to efficiently self-assemble into uniform nanoparticles, while ΔCap mutant failed to form ordered and regularly shaped nanoparticles (Fig. 1B). To investigate the immune regulation of Cap NPs on host, immortalized porcine alveolar macrophages (3D4), as critical target cells of PCV2 infection and important immune cells, were used in this study. CCK-8 test showed that Cap NPs possessed excellent biocompatibility (Figure S1). Confocal microscopy analysis revealed that Cap NPs could efficiently internalize into 3D4 cells and exhibit co-localization with lysosomes compared to ΔCap mutant (Fig. 1C). Over 35% of 3D4 cells contained Cap NPs, as determined by flow cytometry. However, the internalization efficiency of ΔCap mutant was less than 5% (Fig. 1D). The findings indicate that successful self-assembly of Cap subunits was required for efficient internalization.

Our previous studies have found that both PCV2 infection and overexpression of Cap protein can activate autophagy in PK-15 cells [26–28, 30]. However, it remains unknown whether Cap NPs possess the potential to trigger autophagy. Figure 1E and F suggest that higher fluorescence intensity and more LC3 puncta are observed in Cap NPs-internalized 3D4 cells, compared to PBS and ΔCap treatments. Meanwhile, confocal microscope images show co-localization of LC3 vesicles and lysosomes, indicating that Cap NPs induce the fusion of autophagosomes and lysosomes (Fig. 1G). To further confirm that Cap NPs promote the formation of autophagosomes, Cap NPs-internalized 3D4 cells were collected for TEM. As shown in Fig. 1H and I, compared with PBS treatment, Cap NPs elicited the generation of numerous autophagosome-like and autolysosome-like vesicles in 3D4 cells. Western blotting showed that Cap NPs significantly upregulated LC3-II in a dose-dependent manner, while p62 was decreased (Fig. 1J). Moreover, Cap NPs also increased LC3-II at various time points (Fig. 1K). These findings suggest that Cap NPs could trigger complete autophagy by promoting the formation of autophagic vesicles and subsequent fusion with lysosomes.

Cap NPs initiated autophagy via activating CaMKKβ-AMPK-mTOR axis and elevating intracellular ROS

To further explore the mechanisms by which Cap NPs initiated autophagy, Western blotting was performed to examine the hallmark molecules involved in autophagy. Both Cap NPs and rapamycin treatments inhibited mTOR phosphorylation, suggesting that Cap NPs were capable of triggering autophagy by mTOR signaling (Fig. 2A and B). Since our previous studies have discovered that PCV2 can activate autophagy via the CaMKKβ-AMPK-mTOR pathway, which is beneficial for its replication in PK-15 cells [26–28], we speculated that a similar pathway might be employed by Cap NPs. As expected, Cap NPs promoted the expression of CaMKKβ and increased subsequent phosphorylation of AMPK (Fig. 2C and D). Moreover, our early research also has shown that PCV2 infection increases intracellular ROS by inducing endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress and mitochondrial fission [30, 31]. And some studies revealed that ROS can activate AMPK-mTOR signaling to trigger autophagy [32, 33]. Thus, we further detected the intracellular redox status in Cap NPs-internalized 3D4 cells. Figure 2E and F show that Cap NPs significantly increase intracellular ROS, while antioxidant N-acetylcysteine (NAC) treatment attenuates ROS levels, suggesting that Cap NPs induce oxidative stress. To further explore whether ROS stimulated by Cap NPs is involved in autophagy regulation, chemical reagents H2O2 and NAC were used to examine the autophagy flux. Western blotting revealed that both Cap NPs and H2O2 treatments increased LC3-II protein level with reduced p62 protein, while NAC treatment downregulated LC3-II, accompanied by an accumulation of p62 (Fig. 2G and L). These results indicate that Cap NPs induced autophagy by activating CaMKKβ-AMPK-mTOR pathway and promoting intracellular ROS generation.

Autophagy facilitated the processing and degradation of Cap NPs

Numerous studies have reported that autophagy is important for the uptake and degradation of exogenous antigens, and the substrates can be used for MHC class I or MHC class II presentation, thereby promoting immune responses [12, 34]. To explore the effects of Cap NPs-induced autophagy on antigen processing, 3D4 cells were transfected with an LC3-GFP plasmid and then incubated with Cap NPs for internalization. Confocal microscopy revealed an overlap between LC3, Cap NPs and lysosomes, suggesting that autophagic vesicles could deliver Cap NPs to lysosomes for further processing (Fig. 3A). Both autophagy inhibitor CQ and proteasome inhibitor MG132 treatments increased the level of Cap protein to different degrees, indicating that autophagy and proteasome pathways were involved in Cap degradation (Fig. 3B). To further investigate which pathway played a dominant role and whether there was crosstalk, we expressed different Cap NPs mutants, including autophagy-deficient Cap NPs (ΔA-Cap NP), ubiquitin-deficient Cap NPs (ΔU-Cap NP), and autophagy-ubiquitin double deficient Cap NPs (ΔAU-Cap NP) (Fig. 3C and Figure S2) [35, 36]. As shown in Fig. 3D, compared with wild-type Cap NPs, ΔA-Cap NPs or ΔAU-Cap NPs decreased LC3-II, accompanied by an increase in p62. Both ΔA-Cap NPs and ΔAU-Cap NPs increased the stability of Cap protein in 3D4 cells, while ΔU-Cap NPs, like the wild-type Cap NPs, remained degraded (Fig. 3E). After treatment with CQ or MG132 in ΔA-Cap NPs-internalized 3D4 cells for 48 h, there was a slight upregulation of Cap protein (Fig. 3F). However, CQ treatment significantly increased Cap protein in ΔU-Cap NPs-internalized 3D4 cells, indicating that autophagy played a crucial role in Cap processing and degradation (Fig. 3G).

Autophagy further elicited TNF-α signaling in Cap NPs-internalized cells

Cytokines are indispensable molecules for dynamically coordinating immunity, from initiating inflammation and guiding adaptive responses to performing effector functions. To investigate the immune regulation of Cap NPs on the host, mRNA levels of proinflammatory cytokines, chemokines and regulation of cytokines in Cap NPs-internalized cells were further assessed by qRT-PCR. Figure 4A shows that Cap NPs stimulate various cytokine expression. Interestingly, among numerous cytokines, TNF-α displayed a sharp increase. Similar results were also found in bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BM-DCs) (Figure S3). Given that autophagy is widely involved in immunity [13, 37], and a recent study has also reported that autophagy can activate TNF-α and NF-κB signaling [38], we explored the relationship between Cap NP-induced autophagy and TNF-α. The autophagy inhibitors 3-MA and CQ, as well as TNF-α inhibitor R-7050, were used in this study. As shown in Fig. 4B and E, after treatment with 3-MA or CQ in Cap NPs-internalized 3D4 cells, the transcription of TNF-α was decreased. As an important downstream effect factor of TNF-α, the activity of NF-κB (p65) was also evaluated by Western blotting. Figure 4C, D, F and G suggest that both 3-MA and CQ treatments downregulate the phosphorylation of NF-κB (p-p65). However, inhibiting TNF-α with R-7050 did not significantly affect p-mTOR, p62 and LC3-II in Cap NPs-internalized 3D4 cells (Fig. 4H and L). Furthermore, compared with wild-type Cap NPs, ΔA-Cap NPs and ΔAU-Cap NPs also triggered lower levels of TNF-α (Fig. 4M). These findings indicate that autophagy induced by Cap NPs participated in the activation of TNF-α signaling.

Autophagy-TNF-α axis triggered by Cap NPs is beneficial for the expression of antigen presentation-related molecules

As a crucial link between innate and adaptive immunity, antigen presentation plays an important role in enhancing vaccine efficacy. Immortalized porcine alveolar macrophages (3D4) and mouse bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BM-DCs) were used for antigen presenting assessment in this study. Our results showed that Cap NPs promoted the transcription of co-stimulatory molecules CD80 and CD86 in 3D4 cells and BM-DCs, which played important roles in antigen presentation (Fig. 5A and D). Meanwhile, flow cytometry further revealed that Cap NPs significantly increased the proportions of CD80+, CD86+ and CD80+CD86+ BM-DCs compared to ΔCap and PBS groups (Fig. 5E and G). As a molecule mainly responsible for exogenous antigen presentation, the level of MHC-Ⅱ was also up-regulated in Cap NPs-internalized 3D4 cells and BM-DCs (Figure S4A and S4B). Since previous studies have demonstrated that autophagy and TNF-α signaling can enhance antigen presentation [39, 40], we further investigated the influence of autophagy-TNF-α axis triggered by Cap NPs on CD80 and CD86. Figure 5H and K show that CQ treatments downregulate CD80 and CD86, compared with Cap NPs-internalized 3D4 cells or BM-DCs in the absence of chemical treatments. Additionally, inhibition of the autophagy-related gene ATG5 by siRNA also decreased the transcription of CD80 and CD86 in Cap NPs-internalized 3D4 cells (Fig. 5L and M). Similarly, Fig. 5N and Q show that R-7050 treatments downregulate CD80 and CD86 in both Cap NPs-internalized 3D4 cells and BM-DCs. The above results indicate that Cap NPs-induced autophagy and TNF-α signaling promoted antigen presentation-related molecule expression.

Cap NPs enhanced humoral immune responses by activating autophagy-TNF-α pathway

To verify the experimental results in vitro, a mouse model was established by inoculating Cap NPs, with ΔCap as a control. Specific inhibitors CQ and R-7050 were used to evaluate the effects of autophagy and TNF-α on Cap NPs immunogenicity. As shown in Fig. 6A and B, Cap NPs inoculation group increased LC3-II and was accompanied by a decrease in p62 in the spleen of mice. CQ treatment upregulated both LC3-II and p62 protein levels. This is consistent with the results in vitro. Interestingly, an elevated p62 also appeared in R-7050 treatment group. Cap NPs inoculation group stimulated high levels of TNF-α production, while CQ or R-7050 treatment decreased TNF-α concentration (Fig. 6C). This suggested a possible crosstalk between autophagy and TNF-α in mice. The specific antibodies against PCV2 Cap at 14 dpi and 28 dpi in Cap NPs inoculation group were also higher than those of ΔCap and chemical treatment groups (Fig. 6D and E). Importantly, Cap NPs inoculation triggered strong neutralizing antibodies, while CQ or R-7050 treatment suppressed the production of antibodies (Fig. 6F). These data demonstrate that Cap NPs-activated autophagy and TNF-α signaling boosted humoral immunity.

Cap NPs elevated cellular immune responses by activating autophagy-TNF-α pathway

As an important antiviral and immune regulatory factor, IFN-γ plays a crucial role in cellular immune responses. Figure 7A shows that PCV2-specific IFN-γ secreting cells are increased significantly in Cap NPs inoculation group, while CQ or R-7050 treatment inhibits this upregulation. Lymphocyte proliferation assay revealed that Cap NPs inoculation also enhanced stimulation index (SI) compared to ΔCap and chemical treatment groups (Fig. 7B). In addition, the percentages of total T cells in splenocytes or PBMCs were obviously higher in Cap NPs inoculation group than in CQ or R-7050 treatment groups. Moreover, there was no significant difference in the ratio of CD4+ or CD8+ T cells to total T cells, suggesting that Cap NPs might promote a broad mobilization of T cells, with a coordinated activation of both CD4+ and CD8+ T cell subsets (Fig. 7C and D). These data indicate that Cap NPs enhanced cellular immune responses via activation of autophagy-TNF-α pathway.

Mi3-Cap peptide (193-213aa) NPs could induce autophagy-TNF-α signaling and stimulate immune responses

Since ΔA-Cap NPs and ΔAU-Cap NPs mutants attenuated the degradation of Cap antigen and induced lower levels of TNF-α, we inferred that Cap fragments produced by autophagy degradation might further participate in the activation of TNF-α. To verify our hypothesis, 3D4 cells were transfected with various truncated EGFP-Cap recombinant plasmids and then treated with rapamycin to accelerate protein degradation (Fig. 8A). Figure 8B shows that overexpression of full-length EGFP-Cap (E-C) fusion protein in 3D4 cells significantly upregulates TNF-α transcription compared with EGFP control protein. Additionally, almost all truncated EGFP-Cap fusion proteins promoted the expression of TNF-α. Among them, significantly reduced TNF-α transcription was observed in E-C7 (16-153aa) and E-C8 (16-173aa) truncated proteins, while an increase was found in E-C9 (16-193aa) compared with full-length E-C protein. In order to examine the effects of Cap fragments E-C7-C8 (153-173aa), E-C8-C9 (173-193aa) and E-C9-C10 (193-213aa) on autophagy and TNF-α signaling, an artificially designed self-assembling protein nanoparticle mi3 was used to display these fragments (Fig. 8C). SDS-PAGE suggested that mi3 and mi3-Cap (mC) fragments were expressed and purified successfully (Fig. 8D). Moreover, all the mi3-Cap fragment proteins could self-assemble into nanoparticles efficiently (Fig. 8E). Among them, mC-9-10 NPs induced an increase in LC3-II with reduced p62, compared with mi3 NPs (Fig. 8F and H), indicating that Cap fragment 9–10 could activate autophagy. More importantly, as shown in Fig. 8I, qPCR revealed that mC-9-10 NPs significantly promoted the transcription of proinflammatory cytokines (TNF-α and IL8), antigen presenting molecules (CD80, CD86 and MHC-Ⅱ) and chemokines (CXCL10, CCL20 and CSF1). The above results suggest that after chimerization with Cap fragment 193-213aa, the resulting mC-9-10 NPs could induce autophagy-TNF-α signaling independently and stimulate the expression of multiple cytokines.

Broad-spectrum internalization characteristics and autophagy induction ability of Cap NPs

Although Cap NPs have been previously fused with other antigens for the development of chimeric vaccines, these efforts have primarily focused on the prevention of porcine viruses [41, 42]. The potential of Cap NPs as a universal vaccine carrier in the development of human or other animal vaccines deserves further evaluation. Given that the efficiency of antigen uptake by immune cells critically influences subsequent immune responses, we evaluated the internalization capability of Cap NPs in cell lines derived from different species, including monkey (Vero), human (HEK 293T), and dog (MDCK). Strikingly, Cap NPs efficiently entered all tested cell lines after only 2 h of incubation followed by 12 h of culture (Fig. 9A and E, and 9I). Moreover, Cap NPs also activated autophagy in cells from different species (Fig. 9B, F and J). To further assess whether internalization and autophagy induction in these cells were associated with immune activation, we measured the immune markers TNF-α and IL-6. The results showed that internalization of Cap NPs significantly upregulated the transcription of TNF-α and IL-6 in Vero (Fig. 9C and D), HEK 293T (Fig. 9G and H), and MDCK cells (Fig. 9K and L) compared with the ΔCap or PBS group, consistent with our observations in 3D4 cells and BM-DCs. These findings suggest that Cap NPs may serve as a broad-spectrum vaccine platform capable of enhancing vaccine efficacy through autophagy activation.

Discussion

Vaccination is currently the most effective method for controlling infections. Some vaccines have achieved great success, such as smallpox, polio, and tetanus vaccines [43–45]. Nevertheless, there are still many limitations in the development of vaccines. Traditional inactivated vaccines and subunit vaccines generally possess poor immunogenicity, while live or attenuated vaccines carry the risks of virulence reversal. As an emerging strategy, vaccines based on nanoparticles are becoming a promising alternative for traditional vaccines due to their excellent characteristics such as high loading capacity, antigen targeted delivery, antigen presentation, strong cellular immune responses and customized surface chemistry [46, 47]. As one of the nano-vaccines, Cap NPs derived from PCV2 have been commercially available for effective prevention and control of PCVAD [48, 49]. Meanwhile, some chimeric vaccines based on Cap NP platform have also been reported to display excellent immunogenicity [8–10]. However, the mechanism for the significant immune efficacy of Cap NPs is still unknown. In this study, we clearly demonstrated that Cap NPs could induce autophagy via activating CaMKKβ-AMPK-mTOR axis and elevating intracellular ROS. The increased level of autophagy promoted the processing of Cap antigen and activation of TNF-α, followed by upregulation of antigen presenting molecules. Inhibition of autophagy or TNF-α signaling impaired humoral and cellular immune responses induced by Cap NPs. Further, the chimeric mC-9-10 NPs were observed to activate the autophagy-TNF-α pathway and induce various immune activation markers independently, suggesting that mC-9-10 NPs may exhibit adjuvant-like activity. More interestingly, we found that Cap NPs could enter cells of multiple species and activate autophagy-TNF-α pathway, which suggests that it may become a universal nano-vaccine carrier with broad application prospects (Fig. 10).

In vaccine immunization, antigen uptake by antigen-presenting cells (APCs) is the key to initiating immune response. Highly structured nanoparticles are generally beneficial for uptake by APCs. Mo et al. reported that the N terminus of Cap (1-45aa) is involved in the formation of NPs [7]. Thus, a Cap mutant (ΔCap) that could not self-assemble into NPs was constructed by deleting the first 45 residues at the N terminus, and used as a control. As shown in Fig. 1 and Figure S3, Cap protein was able to self-assemble into stable NPs, and efficiently internalize into 3D4 cells and BM-DCs. This is consistent with a previous study showing that Cap NPs expressed in vitro can enter PK-15 cells [6]. However, ΔCap cannot internalize efficiently into cells, indicating that nanoparticulated Cap protein improved antigen uptake efficiency of APCs. Another study also found that although Cap monomer can induce strong humoral immune responses in pigs, most antibodies produced lack virus neutralizing activity, while Cap NPs can elicit high levels of neutralizing antibodies and protect pigs from viremia [50], suggesting that the structural form of Cap protein affects vaccine efficacy.

Autophagy is a conserved process of maintaining cell homeostasis through lysosomal degradation of cellular components [11]. Viral invasion usually triggers autophagy, which can participate in innate and adaptive immune responses and has a double-edged effect on viral infection [51]. Some viruses can activate autophagy, leading to the degradation of their own components, while others, including PCV2, can antagonize, evade, or even hijack autophagy-related processes to facilitate their infection [30, 52]. Our previous research has found that PCV2 infection can induce endoplasmic reticulum stress (ER stress) [31, 53], which disrupt intracellular calcium and ROS homeostasis [54]. Elevated intracellular calcium further activates CaMKKβ-AMPK-mTOR pathway to initiate autophagy and facilitate virus replication [26, 27]. However, whether Cap NPs can elicit autophagy and the effects on immunogenicity has not been reported. In this study, Cap NPs activated complete autophagy via inducing CaMKKβ-AMPK-mTOR axis and intracellular ROS (Figs. 1 and 2). Given that PCV2 infection induces both ER stress and mitochondrial fission, each capable of contributing to ROS production with potential crosstalk between the two processes [30, 31], the present study mainly focused on the integrated oxidative stress response and downstream autophagy effects. For the first time, Cap NPs have been reported to induce autophagy upon cell internalization.

Autophagy is involved in the processing of antigens [55]. Previous studies have found that autophagy can deliver viral antigens to the lysosome and further process and assemble them with MHC-II molecules for presentation to CD4+ T cells, enhancing host antiviral immune responses [34]. In addition, the processing of antigen by autophagy is also conducive to MHC-I molecular cross-presentation [39]. To explore the effects of Cap NPs-triggered autophagy on antigen processing, we overexpressed GFP-LC3 fusion protein in 3D4 cells. Confocal fluorescence microscopy revealed that Cap protein, LC3 and lysosomes were co-localized, indicating that the autophagosomes could deliver Cap antigen to lysosomes for processing (Fig. 3). By chemical treatments and the construction of Cap NPs mutants [35, 36], results further confirmed that autophagy plays a major role in the processing and degradation of Cap antigen.

Autophagy also mediates the regulation of immune responses. Our results showed that Cap NPs upregulated proinflammatory cytokines, chemokines and regulation of cytokines, especially TNF-α (Fig. 4). Similar results were also observed in BM-DCs (Figure S3). A recent study has revealed that autophagy reverses gemcitabine induced immune inhibition by promoting TNF-α, IL-6 and MHC-II expression [56], while another study found that TNF-α can stimulate p62 mediated autophagic activity [57]. Thus, we wondered whether there is a correlation between Cap NPs elicited autophagy and TNF-α signaling. Figure 4 revealed that autophagy promoted TNF-α signaling activation while TNF-α has little effect on autophagy. Another research also suggested that mitochondrial autophagy promotes the expression of NF-κB and TNF-α [38]. However, this seems to contradict several reports showing that autophagy can alleviate inflammatory response stimulated by inflammation-related markers, such as IL-1β, TNF-α and IL-6 [58–60]. This could be due to the fact that autophagy activated by Cap NPs also accelerated the degradation of its own Cap antigen, and the degradation products might further affect TNF-α.

Research shows that autophagy can promote MHC-I and MHC-II presentations [12, 34]. As the biological functions of TNF-α are diverse, it has been shown to enhance antigen presentation and regulate the development and activation of immune cells [22]. Thus, we detected the influences of the autophagy-TNF-α pathway activated by Cap NPs on antigen presentation. Figure 5 and Figure S4 show that Cap NPs increase the expression of co-stimulatory molecules CD80 and CD86, as well as MHC-II, indicating that Cap NPs promote MHC-I/II-mediated antigen presentation, which is consistent with our previous findings [61]. Further study suggested that inhibition of the autophagy-TNF-α axis downregulated CD80 and CD86, indicating that the autophagy-TNF-α axis was beneficial to antigen presentation. Similar study has also shown that TNF-α stimulates the maturation and activation of dendritic cells, leading to their increased antigen presentation and T cell activation [62].

In order to verify the results of the experiment in vitro, we conducted a preliminary exploration by inoculating Cap NPs in mice. Figures 6 and 7 show that Cap NPs enhance autophagy and TNF-α expression in mice, while suppression of autophagy or TNF-α signaling impairs humoral and cellular immunity. Zhao et al. also found that construction of a pJME-LC3 DNA vaccine targeting autophagosomes by fusion of Japanese encephalitis virus prM and E protein with LC3 gene can enhance cellular immunity and promote the production of neutralizing antibody [63]. Moreover, another study reported that blocking TNF-α signaling inhibits the number of specific B cells, T cells and antibody production induced by SARS-CoV-2 vaccine [64]. These results indicate that autophagy-TNF-α signaling induced by Cap NPs participated in immune regulation. It should be noted that we only investigated that inhibition of the autophagy-TNF-α pathway restricts the immune regulation induced by Cap NPs internalization in mice. Whether positive activation of this pathway could further enhance the immune effect of Cap NPs in animals remains to be verified.

Since inhibition of autophagy attenuated the degradation of Cap antigen and downregulated TNF-α, we speculated that Cap peptides generated by autophagy degradation might mediate TNF-α signaling. Thus, we overexpressed a series of truncated EGFP-Cap fusion proteins in 3D4 cells to further explore the key domains of Cap NPs activating TNF-α. The results showed that the deletion of 153-213aa in Cap proteins significantly impacted TNF-α transcription (Fig. 8). Other studies have also shown that fragment 153-213aa may be a potential B-cell epitope and immunodominant epitope of PCV2 Cap protein [65, 66]. By using mi3 NPs for antigen display, we found that chimeric mC-9-10 (193-213aa) NPs significantly activated autophagy-TNF-α pathway and immune markers (Fig. 8). These results indicate that mi3-displayed Cap (193-213aa) NPs show adjuvant-like activity, and the peptide sequence may play a role, but the necessity of nanoparticle presentation requires further investigation. The adjuvant activity and immunogenicity of the free Cap peptide (193-213aa) alone will be addressed in future work. Interestingly, we also found that Cap NPs could internalize and activate autophagy-TNF-α pathway in cells of multiple species, suggesting that Cap NPs possessed the potential to become a universal nano-vaccine platform (Fig. 9).

In conclusion, our study provides a novel insight into the immune mechanisms and future application expansion of Cap NPs vaccine: Cap NPs promoted their own antigen processing and degradation, and enhanced immunoregulatory signal TNF-α by inducing autophagy. The activated autophagy-TNF-α nexus further facilitated the expression of molecules related to antigen presentation, and induced high levels of neutralizing antibodies and strong cellular immune responses in mice. Further, chimeric mi3-Cap peptide (193-213aa) NPs were found to activate autophagy-TNF-α signaling and drive expression of various immune activation markers. More importantly, the multi-species cellular internalization and autophagy-TNF-α induction characteristics of Cap NPs make them a promising candidate for a universal nano-vaccine platform. More research is warranted to explore the immunogenicity and safety of Cap NPs and their peptide (193-213aa) in different species.

Materials and methods

Cells and virus

Immortalized porcine alveolar macrophage (3D4/21), porcine kidney epithelial (PK-15), HEK 293T, MDCK and Vero cell lines were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). 3D4 cells were grown in RPMI-1640 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, C11875500BT) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; ExCell Bio, FSP500) at 37 °C with 5% CO2. PK-15, 293T, MDCK and Vero cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM; Thermo Fisher Scientific, C11995500BT) supplemented with 10% FBS at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

Mouse bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BM-DCs) were isolated from femoral bone marrow of BALB/c male mice with RPMI-1640. Red blood cells (RBCs) Lysis Buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, C3702) was used to lyse RBCs and the remaining cells were collected. Subsequently, cells were resuspended in 6-well plates with RPMI 1640 supplemented with 10% FBS, 10 ng/mL mouse GM-CSF, 10 ng/mL mouse IL-4) at a density of 106 cells/well. The medium was refreshed every two days. After culturing for 7 days, most cells developed typical dendritic morphology and were used for subsequent experiments.

PCV2 strain JH (PCV2d, GenBank accession no. MG245867) was originally isolated from lungs of pigs with naturally occurring PCVAD in Zhejiang Province, China, and propagated in PK-15 cells. The virus titer of PCV2 strain JH was 1 × 105.75 TCID50/ml.

Reagents and antibodies

Chemicals 3-Methyladenine (3-MA; Selleck, S2767), Chloroquine (CQ; Selleck, S6999), Rapamycin (Rapa; Beyotime Biotechnology, S1842), R-7050 (Med Chem Express, HY-110203), and MG132 (Beyotime Biotechnology, S1748) were used at the designated concentrations in appropriate experiments. Primary antibodies used included rabbit anti-LC3B antibody (ABclonal Technology, A19665), rabbit anti-p62 antibody (ABclonal Technology, A21702), rabbit anti-phospho-mTOR antibody (ABclonal Technology, AP0094), rabbit anti-phospho-NF-κB (p-p65) antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, 3033), rabbit anti-total-NF-κB (t-p65) antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, 4764), rabbit anti-total-AMPK (t-AMPK) antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, 2795), rabbit anti-phospho-AMPK (p-AMPK) antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, 2535), rabbit anti-CaMKKβ antibody (ABclonal Technology, A2508), rabbit and mouse anti-LAMP1 antibody (Bioss antibodies, bs-1970R and bsm-51301 M), rabbit anti-β-actin antibody (ABclonal Technology, AC026), and mouse anti-Cap antibody (D11-1, laboratory storage). Horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (AS003) and HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (AS014) for Western blotting were purchased from ABclonal Technology. Alexa Fluor ® 555 conjugated donkey anti-mouse IgG (A31570), Alexa Fluor ® 488 conjugated donkey anti-rabbit IgG (A21206), and Alexa Fluor ® 647 conjugated donkey anti-rabbit IgG (A32795) for confocal imaging were obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific.

Cloning, expression and purification of NPs

DNA sequences coding for PCV2d ORF2 were optimized (amino acids 16 to 233, GenBank MG245867), synthesized with a 6×His tag, and cloned into pET30a to generate pET30a-Cap-His. A ΔCap mutant lacking critical residues (16-45aa) required for self-assembly into NPs was constructed as a control [7]. Meanwhile, a series of Cap NPs mutants that cannot induce autophagy and/or ubiquitination were constructed [35, 36]. The mi3 NPs-encoding sequences (GenBank AXF54357.1) were obtained as described previously [67]. Cap fragments E-C7-C8, E-C8-C9 and E-C9-C10 were connected to platform mi3 with linkers. Correct recombinant plasmids were transformed into E. coli Transetta (DE3) (TransGen Biotech, CD201) for protein expression. An overnight culture of E. coli Transetta (DE3) cells in LB medium containing 50 µg/ml kanamycin was diluted 1:100 in 0.4 L of fresh LB medium and incubated at 37 °C. When the OD600 value reached 0.6 ~ 0.8, the final concentration of 1 mM of isopropyl β-d1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) was added, and the bacteria were incubated at 16 °C for 20 h. After centrifugation, the bacterial pellet was collected for ultrasonic crushing. Target proteins were obtained and purified by ProteinIso Ni-NTA Agarose (Yeasen Biotechnology, 20502ES60) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Eluted Cap NPs and mi3 NPs were quantified using BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, P0010) and detected by SDS-PAGE and Western blotting (mouse anti-Cap antibody and mouse anti-His antibody).

NPs internalization, chemical treatments and transfection

Purified Cap NPs and mi3 NPs were added to a tube centrifugal dialyzer (Merck Millipore, UFC910008) for concentration and buffer replacement into PBS. A CCK-8 (Beyotime Biotechnology, C0037) assay was performed to evaluate the cytotoxicity of Cap NPs. Cells (3D4, BM-DCs, HEK293T, MDCK and Vero) grown to approximately 60% to 70% confluence were washed twice with Hanks’ balanced salt solution (HBSS; MeilunBio, MA0039) and incubated with various Cap NPs (2 µM) at 37 °C for 2 h. After incubation, cell monolayers were rinsed with HBSS to remove unattached Cap NPs, and then cultured in fresh medium at 37 °C and 5% CO2 for the indicated time points. Cells treated with ΔCap protein were used as control. For chemical treatments, 3D4 cells were first incubated with Cap NPs and then cultured at 37 °C and 5% CO2 in fresh medium containing 3-MA (6 mM), CQ (50 µM), rapamycin (2 µM), R-7050 (2.5 µM) or MG132 (5 µM) for 12–48 h. An equivalent volume of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was added to the corresponding control wells. For transfection, 3D4 cells were transfected with LC3-GFP or EGFP-Cap recombinant plasmids using Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 11668019) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. An equivalent volume of the transfection reagent was added to the corresponding control wells. After transfection for 24 h, cells were then internalized with Cap NPs or treated with chemicals for the indicated time points.

RNA interference

Small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) targeting porcine ATG5 (sense sequence: 5’-GCUUCGAGAUGUGUGGUUUtt-3’) and a negative control siRNA (si-NC) were synthesized by GenePharma. 3D4 cells were transfected with the specific siRNAs at a final concentration of 50 nM using siRNA-Mate Plus (GenePharma, G04026) for 24 h.

Quantitative reverse transcription PCR

Total RNA of cells was extracted by RNA Extract Kit (Easy-do, DR0401050), and cDNA was synthesized using the HiScript II Q RT SuperMix for qPCR (+ gDNA wiper) (Vazyme, R223) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Specific primers were used to detect the transcription of TNF-α, IL-6, IL-8, MCP-1, IFN-γ, IL-10, CD80, CD86, and MHC-II (Table S1 and S2). β-actin (mice), gapdh (pig), and egfp genes were used as internal controls. Quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed using the ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Q711) on an Mx3005P real-time qPCR system (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA). Relative mRNA expression levels were calculated using the 2− ΔΔCT method [68].

SDS-PAGE and western blotting

SDS-PAGE and Western blotting were performed as described previously [69]. In brief, purified proteins were separated on 12% SDS-PAGE gels and stained with coomassie brilliant blue. For Western blotting, cells in culture plates were washed twice with HBSS and then treated with RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, Biotechnology, P0013B). Cell lysates were separated on 12% or 8% SDS-PAGE gels and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Merck Millipore, ISEQ00010 and IPVH00010). After blocking for 1 h at 37 °C, membranes were incubated with different primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Membranes were washed with TBST buffer (20 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, and 0.05% Tween-20, pH 7.6) and incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies at 37 °C for 1 h. Immunoreactive bands were visualized using an ECL kit (Fd bio science, FD8030), and images were captured by the Gel 3100 chemiluminescent imaging system (Sagecreation, Beijing, China).

Confocal fluorescence microscopy

To detect antigen internalization and autophagy induced by Cap NPs, confocal fluorescence microscopy was used to visualize the subcellular localization and quantify the fluorescence intensity of Cap, LC3, and LAMP1. 3D4 cells or BM-DCs grown on coverslips were incubated with Cap NPs for 2 h at 37 °C and then cultured in fresh medium for another 12 h. The cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Sangon Biotech, E672002-0500), and Triton X-100 (0.1%) was added for permeabilization. After blocking in PBS containing 5% goat serum, cells were incubated with the corresponding primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by labeling with Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies. Cell nuclei were stained with DAPI (Beyotime Biotechnology, C1002). Cells were observed using a confocal laser scanning microscope (IX81-FV1000; Olympus, Japan). Co-localization of LC3 and LAMP1 in Cap NPs-internalized 3D4 cells was analyzed using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, MD, USA).

Transmission electron microscopy

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was used to assess the self-assembly of NPs and visualize the ultrastructure of autophagy. As described previously [61], Cap NPs or mi3 NPs were placed on a carbon-coated copper grid and allowed to adsorb for 60 s. The grids were negatively stained with 2% PTA for 60 s. Subsequently, the stained samples were observed using a TEM (H-7650, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan).

For examination of autophagic vesicles, 3D4 cells treated with Cap NPs or rapamycin were collected by centrifugation and fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde in phosphate buffer (PB, 0.1 mol/L, pH 7.0) at 4 °C overnight. After washing three times with PB, the samples were post-fixed with 1% OsO4 in PB for 1 h. Dehydration of the samples was conducted using graded ethanol (30%, 50%, 70%, 80%, 90%, 95%, and 100%), and then transferred to pure acetone for 20 min. After treatment with Spurr resin and acetone, samples were placed in pure Spurr resin overnight. Ultrathin sections were cut using a LEICA EM UC7 ultrathin slicer (Vienna, Austria) and stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. Finally, autophagic vesicles were observed by TEM.

Animal experiments

The protocol for animal experiments was approved by the Laboratory Animal Management Committee of Zhejiang University (approval number: 27403). Male BALB/c mice (6–8 weeks old) were obtained from the animal experiment center of Zhejiang Chinese Medical University and randomly divided into seven groups of five animals each. Cap antigen was emulsified with ISA-206 adjuvant (Seppic). Groups A, B and C were subcutaneously inoculated with 50 µg Cap NPs. Groups D, E and F were inoculated with the same dose of ΔCap protein. To explore the effects of autophagy and TNF-α on the immunogenicity of Cap NPs, the inhibitors CQ (30 mg/kg) and R-7050 (6 mg/kg) were administered intraperitoneally to groups B, E, C, and F, respectively. Chemical treatments were performed every other day after the primary immunization. PBS was inoculated as negative control. A booster immunization with the same dosage was administered two weeks later. Serum samples were collected at 14 and 28 days-post immunization (dpi) for further analysis of PCV2-specific antibodies.

Serological assays

Indirect enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) was performed to evaluate PCV2 Cap-specific antibody levels. Briefly, ELISA plates were coated with recombinant Cap protein (5 µg/ml) overnight at 4 °C. After blocking with PBS containing 1% caseinate at 37 °C for 2 h, the plates were washed for three times with PBS containing 0.05% Tween-20 (PBST). Serum samples (diluted in PBST, 1:1000) were added and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. Subsequently, HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG was used for further incubation. After rinsing with PBST, the reaction was examined with TMB (JingQi Biological, JQ-XS01), and stopped by 2 M H2SO4. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured by using a microplate reader (Bio Tek, USA).

To assess neutralizing antibodies against PCV2 in serum samples, a virus neutralization test (VNT) was conducted. Serum samples were heat-inactivated at 56 °C for 30 min and subjected to two-fold serial dilutions. The PCV2 JH strain (100 TCID50) was mixed with an equal volume of diluted serum and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. The mixtures were added to pre-seeded PK-15 cells in 96-well plates for incubation. After 72 h post infection (hpi), cells were fixed in 80% acetone, and an indirect immunofluorescence assay (IFA) was carried out to detect virus infection. VNT titers were calculated and expressed as the reciprocal of the highest serum dilution to block 50% of PCV2 infection.

Flow cytometry

The efficacy of Cap NPs internalization was measured by flow cytometry. Cap NPs-internalized 3D4 cells or BM-DCs were harvested and fixed. Cells were treated with ΔCap protein as control. Mouse anti-Cap antibody was used as the primary antibody, followed by incubation with Alexa Fluor ® 555 conjugated donkey anti-mouse IgG. After washing with PBS, cells were detected by using a BD FACSCalibur flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, NJ, USA). Data analysis was conducted on FlowJo software (Tree Star, USA).

To assess the protein expression levels of CD80 and CD86, BM-DCs internalized with Cap NPs were harvested and stained with a mixture of PE anti-mouse CD11c mAb, Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated rabbit anti-mouse CD80 mAb, and APC-conjugated rabbit anti-mouse CD86 mAb (ABclonal Technology, A27691, A23965 and A25824) at the recommended concentrations for 30 min on ice. After three washes with PBS, the cells were incubated with DAPI for 3 min. Fluorescence signals from the corresponding channels were then detected by flow cytometry.

To investigate cellular immune responses, splenocytes and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were collected to analyze different cell subsets. Mice were euthanized on day 28 post primary immunization. Splenocytes were harvested as described previously [61]. PBMCs from blood samples were isolated by a Mice PBMC Isolate Kit (Solarbio, P6340). Then, cells were stained with fluorescein-conjugated antibodies specific for CD3, CD4, and CD8 molecules (Univ Biotechnology, 553063, 553051 and 553030). Total T cells, CD4+ T cells, and CD8+ T cells were identified as CD3+, CD3+ CD4+, and CD3+ CD8+, respectively, by flow cytometry.

To detect intracellular ROS levels, the DCFH-DA fluorescent probes (final concentration 20 µmol/L) were diluted with DMEM medium and added to the cells. After incubation at 37℃ for 30 min in the dark, cells were washed with PBS, then centrifuged and resuspended. Intracellular DCF fluorescence was detected by flow cytometry.

Immunohistochemistry

Mouse spleens were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C for 24 h and then embedded in paraffin. A microtome (KuoHai, China) was used to prepare the sections, followed by immunohistochemistry (IHC) using p62 and LC3 polyclonal antibodies (diluted 1:100 in PBS).

Lymphocyte proliferation assay

Mouse splenocytes from each immunization group were cultured in RPMI 1640 supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 15240-062) and seeded into 96-well plates. PCV2 Cap protein (10 µg/mL) was added as a stimulus. Concanavalin A (10 µg/mL) and RPMI 1640 were used as positive and negative controls, respectively. Plates were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 48 h, and then cell proliferation was tested by CCK-8. The stimulation index (SI) was calculated according to the formula: SI = (OD value of immunization group-OD value of blank control) / (OD value of negative control group།OD value of blank control).

IFN-γ Enzyme-Linked Immunospot

Specific cellular responses against PCV2 were measured by a Mice IFN-γ ELISpot PLUS kit (Mabtech AB, 3321-4HST-2) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Mixtures of mouse splenocytes and PCV2 Cap protein (10 µg/mL) were added to mouse IFN-γ mAb pre-coated plates. After 48 h of cultivation at 37 °C and 5% CO2, plates were rinsed with PBS and incubated with biotinylated detection antibody (1 µg/ml) for substrate development. Spots were counted using a dissecting microscope.

TNF-α protein analysis

TNF-α in mouse serum was quantified using a mouse TNF-α ELISA kit (Univ Biotechnology, abs520010-96T) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistical analysis

Data were obtained from at least three independent experiments for quantitative analyses and were expressed as means ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 6.0 with one-way ANOVA, two-way ANOVA or two-tailed Student’s t-test according to the data type (as described in the figure legends). Differences were regarded as statistically significant with P values < 0.05 and highly significant with P values < 0.01.

Fig. 10A schematic illustration of PCV2 Cap NPs exploiting the autophagy-TNF-α pathway to enhance immunogenicity and their expanded application in the development of nano-vaccines. (A) The PCV2 Cap protein could self-assemble into icosahedral Cap NPs, enabling internalization into cells, while the mutant ΔCap was unable to complete NP assembly, impeding cell internalization. Subsequently, internalized Cap NPs induced the CaMKKβ-AMPK-mTOR pathway and elevated intracellular ROS levels, leading to autophagy activation. The increased level of autophagy facilitated the main processing and degradation of Cap NPs, while autophagy-deficient Cap NPs (ΔA-Cap NP) and autophagy-ubiquitin double deficient Cap NPs (ΔAU-Cap NP) decelerated their self-degradation. Moreover, activated autophagy elevated the immunoregulatory signal TNF-α and antigen presentation-related molecules CD80, CD86, and MHC-II. The autophagy-TNF-α signaling induced by Cap NPs promoted humoral and cellular immunity in mice. (B) In this study, a functional Cap peptide (193-213aa) was identified based on the immune enhancing mechanisms of Cap NPs. Compared with mi3 NPs, the chimeric mi3-Cap peptide NPs could trigger the autophagy-TNF-α pathway and stimulate the expression of a series of immune activation markers, suggesting that the functional peptide may serve as a novel immune adjuvant. Furthermore, Cap NPs were also found to internalize and activate the autophagy-TNF-α pathway in cells of multiple species, making them candidates for a broad-spectrum antigen delivery nano-vaccine platform. These findings hold promise for developing novel vaccines with higher immunogenicity in the future
