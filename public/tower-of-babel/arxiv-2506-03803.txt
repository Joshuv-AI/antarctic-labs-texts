Levitated macroscopic rotors with 10 hours of free spin at room temperature Preprint: APS/123-QED
Xianfeng Chen, Nirmala Raj, Ruvi Lecamwasam, Mingxi Chen, Christina Yuan Ling Tan, Syed M. Assad, Ping Koy Lam

Abstract
Low-dissipation rotors with large angular momentum are essential for precision sensing and probing macroscopic quantum phenomena. To date, low dissipation can only be achieved for micro-scale rotors. Here, we report a diamagnetically levitated millimeter-scale rotor exhibiting a measured dissipation rate as low as 3.85 µ 3.85\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro} at room temperature, corresponding to a free spinning duration exceeding 10 hours 10\text{\,}\mathrm{h}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{u}\mathrm{r}\mathrm{s} . The rotor is levitated stably over an axisymmetric permanent magnet trap, and can be driven up to 930 RPM 930\text{\,}\mathrm{R}\mathrm{P}\mathrm{M} using contactless electrostatic actuation in high vacuum. Leveraging its low damping rate and large angular momentum, we realize a precision gyroscope with a measured sensitivity of 6.5 × 10 − 3 ° 6.5\text{\times}{10}^{-3}\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolDegree} and an estimated thermal-limited stability of 5.7 × 10 − 7 ° 5.7\text{\times}{10}^{-7}\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolDegree} . These results establish diamagnetic levitation as a promising room-temperature platform for high-performance gyroscopes.



I
Introduction

Levitation offers six degrees of freedom in unconstrained motion for rigid bodies, opening new frontiers in precision sensing and enabling new tests of macroscopic quantum physics
Gonzalez-Ballestero et al. 2021
. For such applications, it is essential to reduce energy dissipation, which adds noise, reduces sensitivity, and decoheres quantum states. Recent advances in levitated optomechanics have demonstrated low dissipation in various levitation platforms
Gonzalez-Ballestero et al. 2021
;
Millen et al. 2020
, including electrically levitated nanoparticles with dissipation rates as low as
69\text{\,}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}

Dania et al. 2024
, and optically levitated nanoparticles whose center-of-mass motion has been cooled down to the quantum ground state
Delić et al. 2020
;
Piotrowski et al. 2023
. Such systems exhibit extraordinary sensitivity in measuring forces
Melo et al. 2024
, pressures
Liu et al. 2024
, accelerations
Timberlake et al. 2019
, spin
Jin et al. 2024
, and magnetic field
Ji et al. 2025
.

The free rotation enabled by levitation has unique potential for probing macroscopic quantum phenomena
Stickler et al. 2018
;
Ma et al. 2020
;
Stickler et al. 2021
, and detecting ultra-weak torques
Ahn et al. 2020
. Over the past decade, ultra-fast rotors with rotation speed from MHz to GHz have been realized
Ahn et al. 2020
;
Ahn et al. 2018
;
Kuhn et al. 2017a
;
Schuck et al. 2018
;
Monteiro et al. 2018
.
Similarly, using electromagnetic forces, sub-millimeter steel spheres have been driven to extreme rotation speeds
>

600\text{\,}\mathrm{k}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}

Schuck et al. 2018
.

Common levitation methods such as optical tweezers or electromagnetic traps require a continuous power supply to sustain levitation, which induces extraneous noise and added system complexity. Moreover, while optical tweezers have made impressive strides towards mechanical quantum states, they are restricted to nano- or microscale particles. Access to the macroscopic regime is crucial for precision inertial sensors, where sensitivity scales with mass, as in gravimeters
Rademacher et al. 2020
;
Abramovici et al. 1992
, accelerometers
Timberlake et al. 2019
,
and gyroscopes
Everitt et al. 2011
;
Poletkin et al. 2017
. Furthermore, low-dissipation macroscopic mechanical systems could offer a pathway toward exploring the quantum-classical boundary and testing models of quantum gravity
Chen 2013
;
Fröwis et al. 2018
.

An alternative means of levitation is provided by diamagnetic materials, which can stably levitate with zero energy consumption
Simon and Geim 2000
. One of the most strongly diamagnetic materials is pyrolytic graphite, allowing for levitodynamics of centimeter-scale systems suspended above permanent magnets at room temperature
Chen et al. 2020
;
Chen et al. 2024
. This offers a unique platform for sensitive macroscopic levitated sensors, and has been used from measurements of the Earth’s tides
Leng et al. 2024
to exotic forces
Yin et al. 2025
. The key limitation of diamagnetic levitation is eddy currents, which are induced in graphite as it moves through the magnetic field, and cause strong dissipation.
These can be suppressed by either engineering slits
Romagnoli et al. 2023
;
Xie et al. 2023
, or using composites of graphite microparticles
Chen et al. 2022
;
Tian et al. 2024
, enabling quality factor of
5\times 10^{5}
in millimeter systems at room temperature.

The rotational dynamics of levitated graphite have been studied, predominantly for applications in bearingless motors and generators
Khanna et al. 2010
;
Su et al. 2015
;
Xu et al. 2017
;
Moser et al. 2001
;
Ozawa et al. 2021
. To date, almost all experiments on rotational dynamics have been conducted in air, and with pyrolytic rather than composite graphite. Thus, low dissipation regimes relevant to precision measurements and quantum sensing schemes remain unexplored. Moreover, although diamagnetic levitation has been proposed as a promising approach for high-performance gyroscopes, there has not yet been systematic experimental validation.

Here, we levitate millimeter-scale diamagnetic rotors above a permanent magnet trap in an ultra-high vacuum environment. The axial symmetry of the magnetic potential permits unconstrained rotation of the rotor about the vertical axis. We use contactless electrostatic actuation to drive the rotors to high angular velocities and employ laser interferometry to measure their rotational dynamics precisely. By comparing the damping rates across different materials, dynamic modes, and pressures, we identify the dominant dissipation mechanisms and achieve a record-low dissipation rate for macroscopic rotors. Leveraging on both high angular momentum and low damping, we demonstrate a levitated gyroscope whose thermal-limited sensitivity is in the navigation grade.

II.2
Rotational dynamics

For a rotor levitated in an axisymmetric magnetic-gravitational potential well, there is no restoring force for rotation around the
z
-axis. The rotational dynamics are governed by:

I_{\mathrm{z}}\dot{\Omega}_{\mathrm{z}}+\mu\Omega_{\mathrm{z}}=\tau_{\mathrm{e}},

(1)

where
I_{\mathrm{z}}
is the moment of inertia of the rotor around
z
-axis,
\Omega_{\mathrm{z}}
is the angular velocity,
\mu
is the damping coefficient, and
\tau_{\mathrm{e}}
is the electrostatic driving torque. According to Eq. (
1
), the steady-state rotational velocity is determined by the balance between driving torque and damping. Once the driving force is removed, the motion decays exponentially:

\Omega_{\mathrm{z}}=\Omega_{0}e^{-\gamma t},

(2)

where
\Omega_{0}
is the initial angular velocity, and
\gamma=\mu/I_{\mathrm{z}}
is the rotational damping rate, expressed in units of
\text{\,}\mathrm{r}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{d}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{s}
.

Using the driving method and measurement setup (see Fig.
1
and Methods), we spin up the rotor in high vacuum to observe its dynamic response. In Fig.
2
e, we show a representative result for a rotor with a
12\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{m}
diameter, continuously driven with the signal in Fig.
2
a. The rotor accelerates gradually, reaching approximately
500\text{\,}\mathrm{R}\mathrm{P}\mathrm{M}
, before abruptly decelerating to below
200\text{\,}\mathrm{R}\mathrm{P}\mathrm{M}
. Under continuous driving, the dynamic cycle repeats periodically.

Differentiating the angular velocity over time yields the rotor’s angular acceleration, as shown in Fig.
2
f. During the spin-up phase, the rotor’s acceleration decreases from approximately
1.0\text{\,}\mathrm{r}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{d}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{s}^{2}
to
0.5\text{\,}\mathrm{r}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{d}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{s}^{2}
, followed by a sharp transition to negative values. In high vacuum, the net torque on the rotor arises from two contributions: the electrostatic driving torque and the damping torque, which will be analyzed further in the following sections.

II.3
Electrostatic driving torque

The electrostatic force arises from capacitive interactions between the rotor’s arms and the underlying electrodes. As this force is always attractive, a torque is generated when there is asymmetry in the overlapping area. In our levitation setup, high voltage signals ( typically above
100\text{\,}\mathrm{V}
) are applied to the electrodes (see Fig.
1
), creating a spatially varying electric field that induces a redistribution of charges on the conducting rotor.

To analyze the driving torque quantitatively, we use finite element method (FEM) to simulate the electrostatic interaction between the rotor and electrodes. The electrostatic torque is computed as a function of the angle (
\alpha
) between a rotor arm and the electrodes when applied with +
500\text{\,}\mathrm{V}
, with the results shown in Fig.
2
b. The simulation reveals that the charges are redistributed on the rotor with negative charges in regions near the electrodes, while positive charges distribute over the remaining areas. The net charge on the rotor remains zero, as expected for an electrically floating conductor in vacuum. Moreover, the charge redistribution leads to different electrostatic torques at various angles. For instance, at
\alpha=-30\degree
, the majority of negative charges reside on the rotor’s main body, producing nearly zero net torque. In contrast at
\alpha=-10\degree
, the negative charges accumulate near the rotor arms’ edges, generating a significant torque.

We can see that the electrostatic torque is periodic with rotor angle. The torque is only positive when
\alpha>-27\degree
and is maximum at
\alpha=-10\degree
. Therefore, efficient rotor driving requires precise control of
the driving signal to ensure that torque is consistently applied in the desired direction (see Methods). The peak simulated torque reaches several
\mathrm{n}\mathrm{N}\cdot\mathrm{m}
, approximately one order of magnitude higher than the experimentally derived net torque
0.464\text{\,}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{N}\cdot\mathrm{m}
to
0.23\text{\,}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{N}\cdot\mathrm{m}
inferred from Fig.
2
f using Eq. (
1
). This difference is expected given that
\tau_{\mathrm{total}}=\tau_{\mathrm{e}}-\mu\Omega_{\mathrm{z}}
, and that the net measured torque depends on both the effective actuation duration and the location of the laser spot used for triggering the actuation (see Methods).

II.4
Rigid body modes

To investigate the origin of the sudden drop in rotational speed, we record the rotor’s behavior during spin-up using a high-speed camera. We find that when the rotor approaches
500\text{\,}\mathrm{R}\mathrm{P}\mathrm{M}
, the rotor starts to wobble dramatically and ultimately stops upon contacting the magnets. This critical speed
500\text{\,}\mathrm{R}\mathrm{P}\mathrm{M}
corresponds to a rotational frequency of
8.33\text{\,}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}
, suggesting a possible resonance with one of the rotor’s rigid body modes.

To verify this, we characterize the rotor’s rigid body dynamics by applying a combined DC and AC voltage to the electrodes while the rotor is levitated at rest (i.e., not spinning). Figure
3
shows the detected amplitude and phase response of the rotor when sweeping the AC frequency from
6\text{\,}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}
to
25\text{\,}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}
. Three dominant resonance peaks are observed. Using a camera, we identify the three modes as: a horizontal mode (X mode), a librational mode (
\theta
mode), and a vertical translational mode (Z mode). In principle, five rigid body modes are expected for a levitated plate
Chen et al. 2020
. The degenerate counterparts of the X and
\theta
modes are not visible in Fig.
3
a, likely because the laser focus point used for detection has low sensitivity to those particular vibrations. By fitting the resonance peaks with Lorentzian functions, we extract their
Q
-factors, also shown in Fig.
3
a. In high vacuum, these
Q
s are dominated by eddy current damping
Chen et al. 2020
;
Chen et al. 2022
. Interestingly, the
Q
s of the levitated rotor with a total diameter of
12\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{m}
(the outer diameter of the rotor main body is
8\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{m}
) are approximately 5 times lower than those measured for the levitated square plates
Chen et al. 2020
. This discrepancy can be attributed to differences in both geometry and magnetic configuration. In our case, the axisymmetric rotor–magnet arrangement enables freer eddy current circulation with almost all the currents flowing towards one direction, resulting in increased energy dissipation (see inset of Fig.
3
a).

II.5
Energy dissipation

We next investigate the energy dissipation associated with the levitated rotor’s rotational mode. In diamagnetically levitated systems, damping in high vacuum is typically dominated by eddy currents
Chen et al. 2020
. Prior studies have shown that such losses can be significantly reduced by tailoring the geometry
Romagnoli et al. 2023
;
Xie et al. 2023
or fabricating diamagnetic composites
Chen et al. 2022
;
Tian et al. 2024
;
Xiong et al. 2025
. However, the damping mechanisms in their rotational modes remain poorly understood, despite prior demonstrations of diamagnetic levitation rotors
Liu et al. 2008
;
Su et al. 2015
;
Xu et al. 2017
.

To characterize rotational damping, we perform ringdown measurements in high vacuum. We first drive the rotor into a high rotation speed above
200\text{\,}\mathrm{R}\mathrm{P}\mathrm{M}
, then remove the drive and record the free decay of the angular velocity. Figure
4
a shows representative decay traces at various pressures for a
12\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{m}
and a
5\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{m}
levitated rotor. The logarithmic decay curves exhibit linear behavior, indicating a damping torque proportional to
\Omega_{\mathrm{z}}
, consistent with the linear damping model
\mu\Omega_{\mathrm{z}}
. By fitting these decays with Eq. (
2
), we extract the damping rates
\gamma/2\pi
, which are shown alongside the traces in Fig.
4
a.

To investigate the pressure-dependent damping, we conduct measurements for the
12\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{m}
rotor over a wide pressure range from
1000\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{b}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{r}
to
1\text{\times}{10}^{-7}\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{b}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{r}
. As shown in Fig.
4

$3\text{\times}{10}^{-1}\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{b}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{r}$<P<$3\text{\times}{10}^{-5}\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{b}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{r}$
, confirming the dominance of residual gas damping. Below
3\text{\times}{10}^{-5}\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{b}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{r}
, the damping rate plateaus, falling three orders of magnitude below its atmospheric value, indicating a transition to pressure-independent, intrinsic dissipation. Notably, the damping rate of the rotational mode (
\Omega_{\mathrm{z}}
) is approximately five orders of magnitude lower than that of the non-rotational rigid body modes (X,
\theta

In an ideal axisymmetric levitation system, rotational eddy-current damping should vanish. This is because, according to Faraday’s law of induction, the electromotive force generated by a magnetic field is:

\mathrm{emf}=\frac{\mathrm{d\Phi_{B}}}{\mathrm{d}t},

(3)

where
\Phi_{B}
is the magnetic flux through the rotor. For a perfectly balanced rotor spinning around its
z
-axis, the magnetic flux stays constant
\mathrm{d}\Phi_{\mathrm{B}}=0
, implying no eddy current generation. However, in practice, we observe residual damping even under high vacuum conditions. This is attributed to wobbling of the rotation axis, which breaks the symmetry and introduces time-varying magnetic flux
\mathrm{d}\Phi_{\mathrm{B}}\neq 0
, thereby generating eddy currents.

To investigate this further, we fabricate two additional rotors and measure their damping rates in both the Z and
\Omega_{\mathrm{z}}
modes. The first rotor is a smaller pyrolytic graphite rotor with a diameter
D=$5\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{m}$
. Due to a smaller size and moment of inertia, the rotor can be easily driven to a higher rotation speed. The rotor reaches a maximum speed of
930\text{\,}\mathrm{R}\mathrm{P}\mathrm{M}
, followed by a sudden drop due to resonance with a rigid body mode, similar to the larger rotor (Fig.
2
e). As shown in Fig.
4
c, ringdown measurements at initial speeds of
700\text{\,}\mathrm{R}\mathrm{P}\mathrm{M}
and
930\text{\,}\mathrm{R}\mathrm{P}\mathrm{M}
show that the damping rate at
930\text{\,}\mathrm{R}\mathrm{P}\mathrm{M}
is roughly one order smaller. At
700\text{\,}\mathrm{R}\mathrm{P}\mathrm{M}
, mild wobbling is observed, possibly due to coupling with a subharmonic of a rigid body mode or arising from the driving process. At
930\text{\,}\mathrm{R}\mathrm{P}\mathrm{M}
, the rotor spins more stably, resulting in reduced damping.

The second rotor is fabricated by dispersing micro graphite particles in an epoxy matrix and cut into the same size as the small graphite rotor. As shown in Fig.
4
c, the Z-mode damping rate is reduced by approximately 4 orders of magnitude compared to the graphite rotor. This substantial reduction is because the epoxy isolates the graphite particles from each other, which significantly suppresses the eddy currents
Chen et al. 2022
. However, the damping rate of the
\Omega_{\mathrm{z}}
mode in the composite rotor does not decrease; instead, it increases slightly. This is due to greater wobble caused by lower intrinsic damping in the rigid modes.

These results support the conclusion that rotational energy dissipation in diamagnetically levitated rotors under high vacuum primarily originates from eddy currents induced by wobbling motion. This wobble arises from imperfections in rotor geometry and dynamic coupling to rigid body modes (see also Fig.
5
a). During spin-up, additional wobble can be introduced by asymmetric electrostatic actuation if the driving torque is not perfectly aligned with the rotation axis.

To assess the stability of the levitated rotors, we use a lock-in amplifier to track the precession amplitude at their rotational frequencies. IV
Discussion and conclusions

Levitated rotors with different levitation techniques have been demonstrated at fast rotation speeds and proposed for developing gyroscopes
Kuhn et al. 2017a
;
Ahn et al. 2018
;
Zielińska et al. 2024
;
Ahn et al. 2020
;
Schuck et al. 2018
. In this work, we demonstrate, for the first time, a diamagnetically levitated millimeter-scale gyroscope operating at room temperature. The performance of the
12\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{m}
graphite rotor, spinning at
510\text{\,}\mathrm{R}\mathrm{P}\mathrm{M}
, surpasses that of a smaller
5\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{m}
rotor at
930\text{\,}\mathrm{R}\mathrm{P}\mathrm{M}
, highlighting the crucial role of rotor size in enhancing inertial sensor performance, i.e., larger moment of inertia yields higher angular momentum and thus improved sensitivity.

The best achieved angular resolution,

0.0065\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolDegree}
in our experiments, is one order of magnitude lower than the first optically levitated gyroscope operating at MHz speeds (
0.08\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolDegree}
)
Xiong et al. 2025
. This enhancement is due to the rotor’s angular momentum, as gyroscope sensitivity is fundamentally limited by thermal noise and scales inversly with
I\Omega

Poletkin et al. 2017
(see also Eq. (
4
)). As summarized in Fig.
6

The gyroscope demonstrated here reaches commercial-grade performance
Passaro et al. 2017

ARW

ARW=\frac{\sqrt{4k_{\mathrm{B}}T\mu_{\mathrm{\theta}}}}{I_{\mathrm{z}}\Omega_{\mathrm{z}}}\frac{180\cdot 60}{\pi}\quad(\degree/\sqrt{h}),

(4)

where

\theta

Beyond gyroscopic applications, the levitated rotor exhibits an exceptionally low energy dissipation rate of
3.85\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro}

While composite materials reduce damping in translational modes by limiting eddy current loops

In summary, we investigate the rotational dynamics and energy dissipation of millimeter-scale rotors levitated via diamagnetic force in high vacuum. Using electrostatic actuation and FPGA-based control, we achieve rotation speeds up to
930\text{\,}\mathrm{R}\mathrm{P}\mathrm{M}

3.85\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro}

6.5\text{\times}{10}^{-3}\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolDegree}

ARW
may reach
5.7\text{\times}{10}^{-7}\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolDegree}
, marking a significant step toward benchmark, high-performance inertial sensors and quantum optomechanical platforms.

This study establishes that diamagnetically levitated rotors can achieve exceptionally low dissipation at macroscopic scales—beyond the reach of existing optical levitation—paving the way for high-performance gyroscopes

V
Methods

Sample preparation:
Two diamagnetic materials were employed in this study. The first is a pyrolytic graphite sheet, mechanically polished to the desired thickness to minimize surface roughness. The second is a diamagnetic composite, fabricated following the procedure described in our previous work
Chen et al. 2022
, by dispersing micro-scale graphite particles (Nanografi Nano Technology) into an epoxy matrix. The graphite particles have a nominal size of 1–
5\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
, and the mass ratio of graphite to epoxy is
35\%
. Both materials were patterned into four-armed rotor geometries using a precision micro laser cutter, as shown in Fig.
1
c.

Driving strategy:

As illustrated in Fig.
1
a, the levitated rotors are actuated via electrostatic forces using four electrodes placed beneath them. These electrodes are patterned on custom-designed printed circuit boards (JLCPCB) and are powered through a
\times
100 high-voltage amplifier (Trek Model 2210).

To initiate rotation, we manually toggle the electrodes until the rotor begins spinning. Once rotation is established, an automated control program developed in LabVIEW and deployed on a National Instruments FPGA is activated.

As shown in Fig.
1
a and Figs.
2
a–d, when a rotor arm intersects the laser beam, a voltage spike is detected by the system. This event triggers the FPGA to activate the electrodes for a short duration (
t_{1}
to
t_{2}
in Fig.
2
a), corresponding to the angular span during which the arm is directly under the laser—approximately
11.5\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolDegree}
for the large rotor and
13.6\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolDegree}
for the small rotor.

The choice of trigger angle
\alpha_{0}
is crucial for efficient rotation, as demonstrated in Figs.
2
a-b. For instance, triggering at
\alpha_{0}=$-40\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolDegree}$
results in nearly zero net torque, while triggering at
\alpha_{0}=$-6\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolDegree}$
yields almost zero net work over the actuation period. Effective synchronization between detection and actuation ensures optimal electrostatic torque generation and sustained rotor acceleration.

Supplementary information

Here we describe the modeling of a levitated 2-axis rate gyroscope, adapted from reference
Poletkin et al. 2017
.
The coordinate systems are defined as shown in Fig.
1
a. XYZ is the fixed coordinate frame. xyz is the coordinate frame of the rotor.
x_{r}y_{r}z_{r}
is the rotating frame with a speed of
\Omega_{Z}
with respect to XYZ, which are not shown in the figure.

The equations of motion expressed by the two generalized rotational coordinates
q_{x},q_{y}
for the gyroscope are given by:

\begin{cases}I_{x}\ddot{q}_{x}+\mu_{x}\dot{q}_{x}+C_{dx}q_{x}-h_{y}\dot{q}_{y}=\tau_{x};\\
h_{x}\dot{q}_{x}+I_{y}\ddot{q}_{y}+\mu_{y}\dot{q}_{y}+C_{dy}q_{y}=\tau_{y},\end{cases}

(5)

where the torques acting on the
x
and
y
axes of the rotor are given by:

\begin{cases}\tau_{x}=\tau^{\prime}_{Y}\sin\Omega_{Z}t-\tau^{\prime}_{X}\cos\Omega_{Z}t;\\
\tau_{y}=\tau^{\prime\prime}_{Y}\cos\Omega_{Z}t+\tau^{\prime\prime}_{X}\sin\Omega_{Z}t,\end{cases}

(6)

where

\displaystyle\tau^{\prime}_{X}

\displaystyle=(I_{z}+I_{x}-I_{y})\Omega_{Z}\cdot\Omega_{Y}+I_{x}\dot{\Omega}_{X};

(7)

\displaystyle\tau^{\prime}_{Y}

\displaystyle=(I_{z}+I_{x}-I_{y})\Omega_{Z}\cdot\Omega_{X}-I_{y}\dot{\Omega}_{Y};

\displaystyle\tau^{\prime\prime}_{X}

\displaystyle=(I_{z}-I_{x}+I_{y})\Omega_{Z}\cdot\Omega_{Y}+I_{x}\dot{\Omega}_{X};

\displaystyle\tau^{\prime\prime}_{Y}

\displaystyle=(I_{z}-I_{x}+I_{y})\Omega_{Z}\cdot\Omega_{X}-I_{y}\dot{\Omega}_{Y}.

\Omega_{X}
and
\Omega_{Y}
are the input angular rates. The parameters
h_{x}
and
h_{y}
are given by:

h_{x}=I_{x}(1-\kappa_{x})\Omega_{Z},\\
h_{y}=I_{y}(1-\kappa_{y})\Omega_{Z},

(8)

where

\kappa_{x}=\frac{I_{z}-I_{y}}{I_{x}},\quad\kappa_{y}=\frac{I_{z}-I_{x}}{I_{y}}.

(9)

These are the respective gyroscope constructive parameters relative to the
x
and
y
axes. The damping terms
C_{dx}
and
C_{dy}
include contributions from dynamic stiffness:

C_{dx}=c_{s}+\kappa_{x}I_{x}\Omega_{Z}^{2},\quad C_{dy}=c_{s}+\kappa_{y}I_{y}\Omega_{Z}^{2},

(10)

where
c_{s}
represents the angular position stiffness produced by a contactless suspension holding and rotating the rotor.

The behaviour of the gyroscope with respect to the fixed coordinate frame (CF) is described by the angular generalized coordinates,
\beta_{X}
and
\beta_{Y}
. These coordinates have the following relationship to
q_{x}
and
q_{y}
:

\begin{cases}\beta_{X}=q_{x}\cos\Omega_{Z}t-q_{y}\sin\Omega_{Z}t;\\
\beta_{Y}=q_{x}\sin\Omega_{Z}t+q_{y}\cos\Omega_{Z}t.\end{cases}

(11)

In our experiments, we can only measure the
Z
-direction displacement of a point on the rotor. Now, we derive the position of a point on the edge of a spinning rotor.

A point at the edge of the rotor, which has a radius
R
, is described in the rotor’s local coordinate system
(x,y,z)
as:

P_{r}=\begin{bmatrix}R\cos(\Omega_{Z}t)\\
R\sin(\Omega_{Z}t)\\
0\end{bmatrix}.

(12)

To express this point in the fixed coordinate frame
XYZ
, we apply rotations about the
X
and
Y
axes. First, a rotation by
\beta_{X}
around the
X
-axis:

R_{X}(\beta_{X})=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&\cos\beta_{X}&-\sin\beta_{X}\\
0&\sin\beta_{X}&\cos\beta_{X}\end{bmatrix}

(13)

Then, a rotation by
\beta_{Y}
around the
Y
-axis:

R_{Y}(\beta_{Y})=\begin{bmatrix}\cos\beta_{Y}&0&\sin\beta_{Y}\\
0&1&0\\
-\sin\beta_{Y}&0&\cos\beta_{Y}\end{bmatrix}

(14)

The total transformation matrix is:

R=R_{Y}(\beta_{Y})R_{X}(\beta_{X})

(15)

Applying the transformation:

P_{X}=RP_{r},

(16)

and expanding this multiplication, the final coordinates of the point in the fixed frame are obtained:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}X&=R\cos\beta_{Y}\cos(\Omega_{Z}t)+R\sin\beta_{Y}\sin\beta_{X}\sin(\Omega_{Z}t)\\
Y&=R\cos\beta_{X}\sin(\Omega_{Z}t)\\
Z&=-R\sin\beta_{Y}\cos(\Omega_{Z}t)+R\cos\beta_{Y}\sin\beta_{X}\sin(\Omega_{Z}t)\end{cases}

(17)

The displacement
Z
is derived by substituting Eq. (
5
) and Eq. (
11
) into Eq. (
17
). Using the measured parameters for the rotors in our experiments, we can obtain the scale factors relating the displacement
Z
to the input angular rates
\Omega_{X}
and
\Omega_{Y}
.
