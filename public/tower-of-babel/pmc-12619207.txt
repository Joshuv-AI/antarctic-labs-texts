Sexual and reproductive empowerment scale for adolescents and young adults: a psychometric analysis among Chinese college students
Authors: Rui Hou, Shao-Lei Fan, Xiu Zhu, Na Wang, Yu Zang, Hong Lu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study aimed to translate the Sexual and Reproductive Empowerment (SRE) Scale for adolescents and young adults (AYAs) into Chinese and adapt it to the Chinese cultural context. The objective was to provide a reliable assessment tool for evaluating sexual and reproductive health empowerment among Chinese AYAs, thereby supporting efforts to promote and improve their sexual and reproductive health.MethodsA validation study of the scale was conducted. Translation and back-translation followed the Brislin translation model, and the Chinese version of SRES for AYAs (C-SRES) was finalized after cultural adaptation through expert consultation. A cross-sectional survey was then administered using the C-SRES. A total of 581 nursing college students from Henan province, China, were recruited through convenience sampling. Psychometric properties were assessed in line with the COnsensus-based Standards for the selection of Health Measurement Instruments (COSMIN) checklist. Reliability was evaluated using Cronbach’s α, split-half reliability. Stability using the test-retest method (Intraclass Correlation Coefficients, ICCs). Validity was assessed through content validity, exploratory factor analysis (EFA), confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), as well as convergent and discriminant validity.ResultsConfirmatory factor analysis indicated that the C-SRES, comprising 6 dimensions and 21 items, demonstrated strong reliability and validity (Cronbach’s α = 0.89, scale content validity index = 0.96, test-retest reliability = 0.89). Model fit indices were acceptable: incremental fit index (IFI) = 0.91, goodness-of-fit index (GFI) = 0.90, comparative fit index (CFI) = 0.91, root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) = 0.07, and root mean square residual (RMR) = 0.07.ConclusionThe C-SRES demonstrated satisfactory reliability and validity, supporting its use as an effective tool for evaluating SRE among Chinese AYAs. In addition to providing a reliable assessment instrument, the scale can assist researchers, educators, and clinicians in identifying factors often overlooked in health studies. It thus offers a valuable basis for developing targeted interventions and further strengthening the sexual and reproductive empowerment of AYAs in China.

Introduction

Sexual and reproductive health and rights (SRHR) are widely recognized as a cornerstone of human health and survival, economic development, and overall well-being [1]. In 2015, all United Nations Member States adopted The 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development, which explicitly incorporates targets related to sexual and reproductive health [2]. According to the Guttmacher Institute, the concept of SRHR has evolved to encompass not only the absence of disease, dysfunction, or infirmity, but also a state of physical, emotional, mental, and social well-being in all aspects of sexuality and reproduction [1]. Similarly, the World Health Organization (WHO) defines sexual and reproductive health as a state of complete physical, mental, and social well-being in all matters relating to the reproductive system. This definition implies that individuals are able to enjoy a safe and satisfying sexual life, possess the capacity to reproduce, and have the freedom to decide if, when, and how often to do so [3].

Adolescents and young adults(AYAs) [4] constitute a significant proportion of the global population and represent a driving force for future sustainable development. This stage of life is both critical and unique, as AYAs not only acquire knowledge and survival skills but also develop their sexual and reproductive capacities. At the same time, they face challenges that pose particular risks to their sexual and reproductive health (SRH), including inequitable gender norms, child marriage, and gender-based violence [5, 6]. The well-being of young people—especially the SRH of those who are unmarried—has therefore become an increasing concern in global public health [7].

According to the World Bank’s latest national income classification standards for the fiscal year 2024 (released in July 2023), 36 low- and middle-income countries are located in Asia. Together, these countries have a combined population of approximately 4.4 billion, representing 64.7% of the global population within this income group. They constitute priority regions for adolescent reproductive health interventions. However, adolescents and young adults in Asia continue to face multiple challenges in sexual and reproductive health (SRH), with core issues including unplanned pregnancies, limited access to reproductive health services, inadequate education, and persistent inequalities among marginalized groups.

Over the past 70 years, China has make remarkable progress in SRH, particularly in reducing maternal and child mortality [7]. As the largest country in East Asia, according to China’s seventh national census, by the end of 2020 the total population had reached 1.41 billion, with individuals aged 15–59 accounting for 63.35% [8]. By the end of 2021, 17.5% of the population was between 10 and 24 years old [9]. AYAs therefore represent an indispensable component of China’s demographic resources. Their SRH especially critical in the context of rapid population ageing and declining fertility ratesis vital for the whole country in the setting of rapid ageing and decreasing fertility. The Healthy China 2030 Plan highlights adolescents as a key population for initiatives in sexual ethics, sexual health, and safety, calling for comprehensive interventions to reduce unintended pregnancies and the transmission of sexually transmitted diseases [10]. Nevertheless, as in many other countries, cultural norms and social practices continue to pose challenges to improving SRH among Chinese AYAs. Issues such as unintended pregnancy, unplanned childbearing, and sexually transmitted infections remain significant public health concerns [11, 12].

An important strategy for promoting adolescent health and development is SRH education [13]. A review of the literature shows that many school educators and researchers have conducted extensive and valuable explorations in this field [14–18], often drawing on behavioral science theories [19]. Some of these studies emphasize school-based interventions [20–26], while others involve re-evaluations of systematic reviews [27]. However, most indicators used to measure improvements in adolescent SRH focus primarily on knowledge acquisition, attitudinal change, and self-efficacy, while giving insufficient attention to individual decision-making—what we refer to as empowerment. According to Kabeer’s discourse, empowerment is defined as “the expansion of people’s ability to make strategic life choices in a context where this ability was previously denied to them [28]”. AYAs grow up in complex and dynamic environments, including their families, communities, and broader societies. To overcome environmental obstacles and achieve developmental SRH goals, they must be able to make strategic decisions independently. Empowerment has been shown to be a key determinant in the development of sexual and reproductive capacity and long-term well-being [5, 29, 30]. To achieve universal coverage of women’s reproductive, maternal, newborn, child, and adolescent health (RMNCAH) by 2030, it is essential to safeguard the health rights of AYAs worldwide, particularly by improving their socioeconomic status and enhancing their empowerment [2].

To address the issue, Dr Ushma et al. [31] developed the Sexual and Reproductive Empowerment Scale for Adolescents and Young Adults (SRES for AYAs) to assess key dimensions of sexual and reproductive health among young people. The scale was administered to a national sample of individuals aged 15–24 years in the United States and demonstrated strong it shows reliable reliability and validity. However, it is important to recognize that this instrument was developed within the context of American culture. In China, the SRH of AYAs reflects distinctive cultural characteristics. Firstly, cultural taboos persist around open discussion of sexuality and reproductive topics. Second, the collectivistic orientation of Chinese society, in which family opinions strongly shape individual decision-making, may influence concepts of sexual and reproductive empowerment in ways that differ from individualistic Western contexts. Moreover, disparities in access to sex education and entrenched gender norms underscore the need to ensure the items are both relevant and appropriate for the target population. Consequently, direct application of the original scale in China is unsuitable, necessitating a systematic process of cultural adaptation. This sturdy therefore aimed to adapt the SRES for AYAs to the Chinese context and to evaluate its psychometric properties, with the goal of providing an effective tool to assess sexual and reproductive empowerment among Chinese AYAs.

Methods

Design and sample

This study employed a cross-sectional design to evaluate the psychometric properties of the translated scale. According to methodological guidelines for reliability and validity testing, as well as factor analysis, the recommended sample size is 5–10 times the number of scale items, with a minimum of more than 300 participants [32, 33]. In addition, a 10%–20% invalid response rate was taken into consideration. Based on these requirements, a total of 581 nursing students were recruited from a university in Xinzheng City, Henan Province, China, between August and September 2022.

The inclusion criteria for participants were as follows: (a) aged 18–24 years; (b) currently enrolled as undergraduate university students; and (c) absence of communication barriers such as deafness, blindness, or severe mental disorders. All participants were informed of the purpose of the study and provided written informed consent prior to participation. They were assured of their right to refuse or withdraw from the study at any time without penalty. No exclusion criteria were applied beyond those specified above.

Instrument development

The C-SRES was developed and validated in two phases: (Ⅰ) Translation and cultural adaptation: The original SRE Scale was translated into Chinese and adapted to the local cultural context;

(Ⅱ) Psychometric evaluation: the reliability and validity of the C-SRES were assessed through exploratory factor analysis (EFA), confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), and tests of internal consistency.

Phase Ⅰ. measure and cultural adaption

Translation and back-translation

Prior authorization was obtained from the developer of the original scale [31]. The translation process strictly followed Brislin’s translation model [34] and involve the following steps. First, two bilingual (English-Chinese) experts independently translated the original scale into Chinese. Next, a panel of two monolingual Chinese experts reviewed the forward-translated versions for linguistic appropriateness, including semantic equivalence and grammatical accuracy, and produced a consensus draft in Chinese. This draft was then independently back-translated into English by two bilingual (Chinese-English, with medical backgrounds) experts with medical backgrounds. The back-translated version was compared with the original scale by the research team to identify and resolve discrepancies. This iterative process continued until semantic consistency was achieved.

Expert reviews

A panel of seven bilingual experts in medical specialties (two obstetrician–gynecologists, one nurse, and four university professors) were invited to.

complete two rounds of expert consultation. Experts were selected based on the following criteria: (a) possession of a master’s degree or higher; (b) professional engagement in clinical nursing, obstetrics and gynecology, or nursing education; (c) willingness to participate in this study. The experts assessed the semantic equivalence and content validity of each item using a 4-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (not equivalent) to 4 (most equivalent).

During the first round, if more than 20% of experts rated an item as 1 (“not equivalent”) or 2 (“somewhat equivalent”), the item was considered an inappropriate translation and revised accordingly [35]. In the second round, experts re-evaluated the revised items to confirm content validity.

Phase Ⅱ. Psychometric property evaluation

Pilot survey

A pilot study was conducted with 10 nursing students from a university in Xinzheng City, Henan Province, to evaluate the cultural appropriateness and comprehensibility of the translated items prior to formal data collection. This step was critical for identifying and resolving potential ambiguities, thereby improving the reliability of the instrument for large-scale administration. Participant feedback was systematically analyzed to assess the face validity of the C-SRES.

Subsequently, comprehensive psychometric testing was conducted, including: (1) item analysis to examine difficulty indices and discrimination power; (2) exploratory factor analysis (EFA) to identify the preliminary factor structure; (3) confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) to verify dimensional consistency with the original scale; and (4) reliability assessment to evaluate internal consistency (Cronbach’s α) and test-retest stability using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs).

Research instruments

General information questionnaire

A demographic questionnaire was developed by the research team to collect participants’ background information. Variables included gender, age, academic year, place of residence (urban or rural), singleton status, parental marital status, and religious affiliation.

C-SRES

The SRE Scale for AYAs was originally developed by Upadhyay et al. to assess sexual and reproductive empowerment among AYAs in the United States. The scale consists of 23 items across seven dimensions: comfort talking with partner; choice of partners, marriage, and children; parental support; sexual safety; self-love; sense of future; and sexual pleasure. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale (0 ="Not at all true” to 4 = “Extremely true”), producing a total score ranging from 0 to 92. The original developers reported good internal consistency for all subscales (Cronbach’s α > 0.70). Following transcultural adaptation, we administered the Chinese version (C-SRES), which retained the original scale’s dimensional structure, item count, and scoring methodology.

Data collection

The questionnaire was converted into an electronic format and administered using Enjoining (Changsha Ranxing Technology, China; https://www.wjx.cn), a secure online survey platform equipped with IP restriction and data encryption protocols. This platform was selected for its robust data protection measures, user-friendly interface, and compatibility across devices, which facilitated secure and anonymous participation—particularly important for research conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic.

The study received ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board of The Third Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University (Approval No. 2022-139−01; 26 July 2022). All participants provided written informed consent after being fully informed about the study. The research was conducted in strict accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant ethical guidelines.

Statistical analysis

Psychometric analyses were conducted in accordance with the Consensus-based Standards for the Selection of Health Measurement Instruments (COSMIN) checklist [36]. All statistical procedures were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, Version 26.0 (Armonk, NY, USA). Participant demographic characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics: continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages.

The reliability of the C-SRES was assessed through three approaches: (1) internal consistency, with Cronbach’s α ≥ 0.70 considered acceptable) [37], (2) item analysis; and (3) test-retest reliability. Item analysis followed these steps: (a) extreme group comparison, in which item scores were divided into upper 27% and lower 27% groups; and (b) item-total correlations, i.e., the correlation between each item score and the total scale score. Items with a critical ratio (CR) >3.0 or item-total correlations between 0.30 and 0.80 were retained [38, 39]. Item-total correlations were evaluated using Pearson’s correlation coefficient, with r >0.40 considered acceptable [37]. Test-retest reliability was examined in a subsample (n = 20) using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) with a two-week interval [40].

The validity of C-SRES was evaluated through content validity and structure validity. Content validity was assessed using content validity indices at both the item-level (I-CVI) and the scale level (S-CVI). Values of I-CVI ≥ 0.78 and S-CVI ≥ 0.80 were considered acceptable [41].

For structural validity, the total sample was randomly divided into two independent subsamples based on the of questionnaire collection: one for exploratory factor analysis (EFA; n = 296) and the other for confirmatory factor analysis (CFA; n = 285). EFA was conducted to explore the underlying factor structure of the C-SRES. Given the anticipated correlations among factors, the oblique promax rotation method was applied [42]. The loading threshold for item attribution was set at >0.40. Items with loadings < 0.40 on all factors, or those with cross-loadings (i.e., loadings >0.40 on two or more factors with a difference < 0.20), were removed [43]. Prior to EFA, the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) test and Bartlett’s test of sphericity were performed to evaluate the suitability of the data. A KMO value ≥ 0.60 and a significant Bartlett’s test (p < 0.05) indicated that the data were appropriate for factor analysis [44]. If discrepancies in item attribution emerged compared with the original scale, expert consensus was sought to determine modifications within the Chinese cultural context. CFA was conducted using Amos 24.0 to confirm the factor structure and further assess structural validity.

Overall, the majority of participants were women (80.7%), and 567 (97.6%) were under the age of 23 years. In terms of academic year, 347 participants (59.7%) were freshmen or sophomores. Validity

Content validity

After the first round of expert consultation to assess semantic equivalence, experts were invited to evaluate the content validity of the first draft of the C- SRES. The item-level content validity index (I-CVI) was calculated as the number of experts rating an item as 3 or 4, divided by the total number of experts. The scale-level content validity index (S-CVI) was computed as the average of all I-CVI values. The results showed that the S-CVI was 0.96 and the I-CVI ranged from 0.571 to 1.000, indicating that the C-SRES demonstrated satisfactory content validity.

Discussion

The application value of the C-SRES

In recent years, the SRH issues of AYAs have received widespread attention. With the rapid development of China’s economy, the sexual and reproductive health literacy of Chinese AYAs has improved significantly. However, they continue to face substantial challenges and threats in the SRH domain, including increasingly open sexual attitudes, rising rates of sexually transmitted infections, premarital sex, unintended pregnancy, and abortion [46]. Consequently, it is essential to investigate the factors influencing sexual and reproductive health literacy and empowerment, and to design effective intervention strategies. Building on the work of Dr. Ushma and colleagues, the present study carried out cross-cultural adaptation and psychometric testing of the SRES among Chinese college students, ultimately producing the validated Chinese version (C-SRES). This instrument has dual application value: first, it can be used to identify factors influencing the sexual and reproductive empowerment of AYAs in China; second, it provides a reliable tool for evaluating the effectiveness of health education and other intervention programs, thereby ensuring the quality of reproductive health initiatives.

The findings of this study demonstrate that the C-SRES possesses strong reliability and validity, supporting its use as a robust instrument for assessing sexual and reproductive health (SRH) empowerment among Chinese AYAs. Beyond measurement, the C-SRES can aid educators in designing targeted SRH education programs and serve as a valuable reference for evaluating the effectiveness of related interventions.

Validity

Content validity

During the C-SRES development process, seven bilingual experts were invited to evaluate the semantic equivalence and content validity of the scale. Item 12 (“While walking down the street, I feel like my body belongs to me alone”) received an I-CVI value of less than 0.78, falling below the acceptable threshold. However, the research team did not remove this item. Considering the high S-CVI (0.96) and the strong factor loadings observed in the subsequent exploratory factor analysis, the decision was made to retain it.

While the Content Validity Ratio (CVR) was not calculated, the scale demonstrated excellent content validity as evidenced by a high Scale-Content Validity Index (S-CVI of 0.96). The necessity of all items was further supported by strong psychometric properties in subsequent item and factor analyses.

Although the original scale was developed within the cultural context of the United States, no semantic discrepancies emerged during the forward- and back-translation process, likely due to the straightforward and accessible wording of the items. In addition, feedback from a pilot survey of 10 Chinese college students indicated that the scale was moderately lengthy but remained readable and clearly structured.

Construct validity

Based on the EFA results for the C-SRES, two items were deleted (Items 11 and 13), and two items were reassigned to different dimensions (Items 12 and 14). Gender equality is one of the key factors influencing adolescent SRH [47], and it is closely linked with sexual safety. In the original scale, Items 11 and 13 were part of the sexual safety dimension. However, data from the Fourth Chinese Women’s Social Status Survey (2021) indicate that public awareness of the rule of law has increased substantially over the past decade, and gender equality has increasingly become a widely accepted social value and behavioral standard [48]. The deletion of these two items may therefore reflect the fact that their content does not present substantial challenges for Chinese adolescents, suggesting that progress has been made in advancing gender equality in recent years. Nevertheless, cultural challenges persist. Gender norms, shaped by sociocultural environments and expressed through socially assigned roles of men and women, exert a strong influence on individual SRH and rights [1]. Importantly, intimate partner sexual violence remains stigmatized in China, often regarded as a private matter that is shameful to disclose [49, 50]. As a result, some college students who participated in the survey may have been reluctant to report experiences of sexual violence, even if they had encountered it.

The contents of items 12 and 14, like the deleted items 11 and 13, were originally part of the sexual safety dimension. However, EFA indicated that these items loaded onto the self-love dimension. Although the EFA results supported their reassignment according to the predefined criteria, a panel of experts was convened to discuss the findings and ensure methodological rigor. From a theoretical perspective, Maslow’s hierarchy of needs highlights that the fulfillment of safety needs provides the foundation for esteem, self-confidence, and self-actualization. Similarly, Bronfenbrenner’s social ecological theory [51] emphasizes that environmental safety extends beyond the family to include community security (e.g., freedom from violence), social protection (e.g., adequacy of welfare systems), and cultural norms (e.g., promotion of tolerance and equality). Collectively, these environmental factors shape an individual’s self-perception and sense of self-worth. Environmental safety and self-love are thus interdependent, forming either a virtuous or a vicious cycle: a secure environment fosters self-love, while self-loving individuals contribute to sustaining a safe and supportive environment. Items 12 and 14 reflect self-awareness and perceived safety within the social context. Given their relevance to self-acceptance and self-perception, their reassignment was considered appropriate. Accordingly, this factor was renamed “Sexual Safety and Self-Acceptance.”

The dimensions and items of the C-SRES were identified through exploratory factor analysis, yielding six dimensions with a total of 21 items. These dimensions were labeled as follows: sexual safety and self-love, sexual pleasure, parental support, sense of future, comfort talking with partner, and choice of partners, marriage, and children.

Reliability

Reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s α coefficient, which reflects the correlation, internal consistency, and stability of items within the scale. In this study, Cronbach’s α, split-half reliability, and test–retest reliability of the C-SRES were all greater than 0.70. Conventionally, values of Cronbach’s α and split-half reliability ≥ 0.70 are considered indicative of good internal consistency, confirming that the C-SRES demonstrated satisfactory reliability.

Limitations

Although this study yielded important findings, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study sample was drawn from a single university in central China, with the majority of participants being women. Moreover, information on participants’ gender orientation was not collected. Given China’s vast territory and regional disparities in development, the representativeness of the sample may therefore be limited. Second, data collection relied on online questionnaires, which may have restricted sample heterogeneity and introduced potential bias. Third, while marital status and parenthood are recognized determinants of sexual and reproductive health, this study did not collect data on partnership status (e.g., single, in a relationship, or cohabiting) or sexual activity. Furthermore, the study focused solely on cultural adaptation and psychometric testing of the scale. It did not examine individual differences in SRH empowerment levels or explore associated influencing factors, particularly among individuals with diverse gender identities and sexual orientations. Given that most participants were unmarried college students, future research should employ stratified sampling and multi-center designs to incorporate these variables, thereby providing deeper insights into the determinants of sexual and reproductive empowerment among Chinese AYAs.

Conclusion

This study developed the C-SRES through a rigorous process of translation, back-translation, and psychometric testing. The final instrument consists of 6 dimensions and 21 items. While the C-SRES diverges slightly from the original scale due to cultural differences between China and Western contexts, its overall reliability and validity met established psychometric standards. The C-SRES is clearly worded and easily understood by AYAs, providing a reliable tool for assessing their SRE across diverse social-ecological contexts. In addition, it offers a valuable instrument for evaluating the effectiveness of interventions designed to enhance the sexual and reproductive empowerment of AYAs.
