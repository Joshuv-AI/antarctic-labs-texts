Randomised, double-blind clinical trial protocol to evaluate whether early and perioperative tranexamic acid administration reduces transfusion rates and blood loss of patients undergoing hip fracture surgery in Spain
Authors: Josu Arrieta, Gaspar De La Herran, Luis Angel Mínguez Cabeza, María Isabel Fernández González, María Teresa Revilla Guarinos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionIn patients with a hip fracture, anaemia has been associated with increased transfusion requirements, poor functional outcomes, prolonged hospital stays and increased mortality. While anaemia in elderly patients with hip fractures has traditionally been attributed to bleeding during or after surgery, many of these patients are anaemic on hospital admission. Thus, detecting and managing anaemia in the perioperative, postoperative and, most significantly, the preoperative period is important to avoid the need for blood transfusions and to improve patient outcomes. The protocol for this clinical trial is designed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of both combined intravenous and topical tranexamic acid (TXA) therapy, or topical administration alone, assessing its effect on blood loss and the need for blood transfusions in elderly patients undergoing hip fracture surgery.Methods and analysisThis is a multicentre, double-blinded, randomised, placebo-controlled trial with a 1:1 allocation ratio. Patients of both sexes, aged ≥65 years, who are admitted to the emergency department and will undergo hip fracture surgery are eligible for enrolment. Eligible patients who provide their consent will be stratified according to the type of fracture (intracapsular and extracapsular) and whether or not they are suitable for intravenous TXA therapy, and they will then be randomly allocated to receive either TXA or a placebo. The primary outcome is the blood transfusion rate from patient admission to the emergency department until discharge, while the secondary outcomes include: the preoperative, perioperative and postoperative haemoglobin and haematocrit levels; the preoperative and postoperative occult and total blood loss; the mean length of hospital stay; and any adverse events assessed for up to 1 year after patient discharge.Ethics and disseminationThe study was approved by the Basque Country Ethics Committee (Ref.: 2021012) and the Spanish Agency for Medicines and Healthcare Products (Agencia Española de Medicamentos y Productos Sanitarios). All participants will provide their written informed consent prior to study inclusion. Introduction

Hip fractures are among the most common orthopaedic injuries in the elderly, associated with considerable morbidity and mortality. Indeed, the worldwide annual incidence of hip fractures was estimated to be about 1.6 million in 20001 and due to world population ageing, it is expected to increase to 2.6 million by 2025 and 4.5 million by 2050.2 Hip fracture surgery is associated with numerous adverse events (AEs)3 and with a 1 year mortality ranging from 21.9% to 33.5%.4,6 Blood loss is often an associated AE7 and it is significant as it may produce complications like anaemia, increased transfusion requirements and prolonged hospital stays. Occult blood loss after hip fracture surgery, in addition to intraoperative blood loss, can reach up to 1500 cc,7 8 and the perioperative transfusion rate for patients with hip fractures ranges from 20% to 60%.5 9 10 Moreover, both perioperative and preoperative anaemia have been associated with postoperative mortality.5 11 12

Elderly patients who suffer hip fractures frequently have comorbidities that make them more susceptible to complications associated with blood loss. While anaemia in elderly patients with hip fractures has traditionally been attributed to surgical or postsurgical bleeding,13 there are reports that more than 42% of hip fracture surgery patients were anaemic on hospital admission,514,16 and that the proportion of patients with anaemia increased between admission and surgery. This underlines the importance of detecting anaemia and managing it in the preoperative period in order to prevent it from deteriorating and thus, to avoid transfusions and improve the patient’s surgical outcomes. A mean reduction of 9 g/L in haemoglobin (Hb) levels between admission and day 1 presurgery, and of 12 g/L on day 2 presurgery has been reported for elderly patients with hip fracture, regardless of gender, fracture type or age.5 Hence, patients often experience persistent bleeding following their fall and specifically, in the ensuing 24–48 hours while they await surgery.5 These reductions in Hb levels are associated with the need for postoperative transfusion of packed red blood cells (PRBCs) and in-hospital mortality.5

Although postoperative anaemia after major orthopaedic surgery can be managed through blood transfusion, it is not without risks. The use of tranexamic acid (TXA), an anti-fibrinolytic agent that inhibits plasminogen from dissolving clots, has been postulated as a potential means to reduce perioperative blood loss and postoperative transfusion requirements in this type of surgery. Increasing evidence supports intravenous TXA use in hip fracture surgery to increase postoperative Hb levels and reduce the length of hospital stays.78 13 17,20 A meta-analysis based on seven studies into the use of TXA in hip fracture surgery, including a total of 770 participants (TXA: 341; control: 429), indicated that intravenous TXA produced a 46% risk reduction in blood transfusion requirement compared with a placebo (RR: 0.54; 95% CI 0.35 to 0.85; I2: 78%; Inconsistency (χ2), p≤0.0001; n=750).8 In an updated meta-analysis of 11 randomised controlled trials (RCTs) assessing the efficacy and safety of intravenous TXA in patients during hip fracture surgery, intravenous TXA was found to efficaciously reduce transfusion requirements by nearly 20%.17 Furthermore, another meta-analysis of five RCTs in patients undergoing intertrochanteric fracture surgery, TXA administration was associated with a 17% reduction in the need for transfusion with no increase in AEs.19

Many patients with hip fracture undergoing surgery are at high risk of deep vein thrombosis (DVT) due to their advanced age, multiple comorbidities, delayed surgery and immobilisation during the initial period of rehabilitation.21 Although some studies found a higher risk of thromboembolic events in patients receiving TXA,22 23 most of the literature on TXA use in hip fracture surgery (including RCTs)17 19 20 24 25 confirms its safety with no increase provoked in the risk of thrombosis but rather, a reduced transfusion rate.5 8 17 18 24 However, the follow-up of patients in these studies was purely perioperative and thus, 1-year mortality rates and the incidence of late-onset complications were not evaluated. Moreover, in many of these studies, the number of patients excluded due to the risk of thromboembolic events based on their medical history reached 42%24 to 51%9 of the eligible patients. Hence, it would be of interest to assess the safety and efficacy of using TXA in patients where there might be a greater risk of thromboembolic events, perhaps modifying the dose and/or administration regime. For this reason, these patients are not excluded in this trial but rather they are included as a group indicated as ‘with contraindications to intravenous TXA’. This group includes patients with explicit contraindications as listed in the TXA product information, as well as other conditions that would lead to their exclusion in other trials, as described. This group of patients consists of those currently receiving anticoagulant and/or antiplatelet therapy and those with a history of thrombotic events (stroke, myocardial infarction, DVT, pulmonary thromboembolism (PTE), etc) and they will receive only topical TXA treatment intraoperatively, at the end of surgery.

While most studies into TXA and hip fracture surgery focus exclusively on intravenous administration, topical TXA use has been assessed for interventions like hemiarthroplasty and total hip arthroplasty, with similar effectiveness to intravenous TXA administration in terms of reducing blood loss and lowering transfusion rates, with no associated increase in the incidence of postoperative complications.26,29 Furthermore, the combined use of these two routes in total hip arthroplasty showed TXA administration to effectively reduce blood loss, Hb decline and the need for transfusion, without increasing the rate of thromboembolic complications.30

Despite the overwhelming evidence on the potential benefits of TXA administration to reduce blood loss and the need for transfusions following hip fracture surgery, the inter-study variation of protocols, poor study design and the small sample size in many cases limit the interpretation and the generalisability of their findings. Thus, there is a consensus that larger, well-designed studies with longer follow-up periods are necessary to establish the optimal dose and timing of TXA administration, to assess the long-term safety outcomes after hip fracture surgery and to identify which patient groups may benefit most from such treatment.

Trial objectives

Primary objective: The primary objective of the study is to assess whether administering TXA to hip fracture patients, both at the time of hospital admission (intravenous administration) and during the perioperative period (topical or intravenous administration) results in a lower rate of blood transfusions.

Secondary objectives: The secondary objectives of the study are to assess whether preventive TXA treatment effectively results in reduced occult blood loss during the preoperative period, a lower total blood loss, shorter hospital stays and altered AE incidence.

Methods and analysis

Study design and setting

This is a prospective, multicentre, double-blind, placebo-controlled, parallel-group RCT, with a 1:1 allocation ratio, designed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of both combined intravenous and topical TXA administration, or topical administration alone, in reducing blood loss of patients with hip fracture. This study will be conducted at three hospitals across the Basque Country (Spain): Zumárraga Hospital, Zumárraga (Guipúzcoa)—the co-ordinating centre; the Donostia University Hospital, San Sebastián (Guipúzcoa); the Araba University Hospital, Vitoria-Gasteiz (Álava). The co-ordinating principal investigator (PI) is JA from the Zumárraga Hospital and the clinical trial is sponsored by the Instituto de Investigación Sanitaria Biogipuzkoa (formerly Biodonostia). Patients will be enrolled over 2 years following the commencement of the trial and enrolled participants will be followed for 1 year after their hip fracture surgery.

Trial status

The current protocol is version No. 3, dated 1 March 2024. The three hospitals were activated to start recruitment in May 2023 and the first participant was enrolled onto the study in July 2023. Recruitment is now closed, with the participants’ follow-up period concluding in April 2026. Completion of final trial report is planned for December 2026.

Sample size calculation for primary outcome

At the Zumárraga Hospital, the transfusion rate for patients with hip fractures in 2019 was 45% for extracapsular fractures and 27% for intracapsular fractures. In five previous studies, a mean reduction in the transfusion rate of around 20% was reported in patients given TXA.10 20 22 25 31 In the case of extracapsular fractures, to detect a reduction in the transfusion rate between the two arms in our settings (from 45% in the placebo group to 25% in the TXA group), with 80% power and using a bilateral χ² test for two independent samples at a 5% significance level, and assuming a 50% transfusion rate in the placebo group relative to the total, 89 participants are required per group. Thus, if an expected dropout rate of 10% is assumed, 99 participants are required per group, which gives a total of 198 participants with extracapsular fractures in the study. Similarly, in the case of intracapsular fractures, a reduction in the transfusion rate is expected from 27% in the placebo group to 7% in the TXA group, and thus the number of participants required to reach 80% power is 55 patients per group. This will require 62 patients per group to be recruited if a 10% dropout rate is again assumed, giving a total of 124 participants with intracapsular fractures required for the study. In addition, since the study design includes another layer, whether or not the patients can receive intravenous TXA, 2 additional groups (placebo and TXA groups) for each fracture type are required. Hence, a total of 644 participants will be required, establishing 4 groups of participants with extracapsular fractures (396 patients) and 4 with intracapsular fractures (248 patients). The calculation of the sample size was performed using G*Power V.3.1 software.

Randomisation and blinding

Prior to commencing the study, the Sponsor will prepare a computer-generated randomisation list including 644 patients divided into 8 groups, 4 study groups and 4 control groups (see table 1). Eligible and consenting participants will be randomised to receive either the TXA or the placebo using the appropriate function in the electronic case report form (eCRF) software into which the randomisation sequence with allocation concealment has previously been introduced (Algorithm used, randomised permuted block randomisation; Block size, 10; Number of randomisation groups, 2). The stratification will be based on the type of fracture and the existence or lack of a contraindication for intravenous TXA. Patients receiving anticoagulant and/or antiplatelet medication and those with a history of thrombotic events (stroke, myocardial infarction, DVT, PTE, etc) will be directly included in the group of patients only administered topical TXA. The remaining patients will be included in the group to receive intravenous TXA and eligible to receive a combination of intravenous and topical TXA (figure 1).

The patients attending the A&E department who are eligible to participate in the study and who provide their informed consent will be registered with a trial participant code in the eCRF. This information is then recorded by the attending physician in e-Osabide, the management software used by the participating hospitals, generating an alert that the patient is participating in the study. The patient is then stratified in the eCRF by fracture type (intracapsular or extracapsular) and contraindication to intravenous TXA (yes or no), and subsequently randomised. The participant’s order number and weight will be communicated to the hospital’s pharmacy by the physician who will prepare the corresponding medication and mask it. The participant will be prescribed treatment (TXA/placebo) by the attending physician according to the trial protocol uploaded in e-Osabide and the pharmacy will dispense it, ensuring the traceability of the medication and that the physicians remain blind to the treatment assigned to the patients. The clinical centre’s pharmacy shall record the following: the patient code (which shall be correlative) and the treatment assigned (TXA or placebo). Only the pharmacy will know the relationship between the patient’s order number and the study group they are assigned to by the randomisation sequence, information that will be maintained in a folder. The investigator will only be informed of the participant’s trial code and in no case of the treatment assigned. In this way, the randomisation sequence generated will remain hidden, ensuring that trial’s participants and the staff responsible for data collection do not know the study group to which the next participant will be assigned in advance (intervention or control). The surgical teams, anaesthetists, research teams and the patients will all be blind to the allocation.

Breaking of the blind

Whenever the attending physician deems it necessary (worsening of an AE, the patient’s life is threatened, etc), they may request the Sponsor or the coordinating investigator to break the blind. The investigator may only ‘unblind’ a patient’s assigned treatment in an emergency, when it is deemed essential to know the medication received by the patient for their adequate clinical management or well-being. Whenever possible, the investigator should first discuss the options with the Sponsor or the coordinating investigator before revealing the treatment assigned. If this is not possible, the investigator should notify the Sponsor as soon as possible but without revealing the assigned treatment unless this information is important for the safety of the other patients included in the study. To break the blind, a duly sealed and marked envelope containing information on the allocated treatment will be held by the hospital’s pharmacy. The details and reason for ‘unblinding,’ as well as the measures taken, must be recorded in the eCRF. If possible, the patient for whom the blind has been broken should continue in the study, with the study procedures carried out by personnel who do not know the treatment assigned at the beginning of the study.

Withdrawal

Participants may withdraw from the study at any time and for any reason at their own discretion. Study participants may also be withdrawn from the study at the discretion of the physician responsible whenever considered appropriate (such as for non-compliance with the protocol, difficulty in performing certain tasks). The reason for the participant’s withdrawal from the study must be documented in the eCRF and communicated to the Sponsor. For patients who signed an informed consent but who were not included in the next step of the clinical trial, their demographic data, inclusion/exclusion criteria and the reason for non-selection shall be documented in their screening visit page in the eCRF.

Intervention and procedure timelines

Investigational product and placebo

Investigational medicinal product (IMP): Amchafibrin 500 mg (or equivalent brand name) as an injectable solution.

Formulation: Ampoule—Each ampoule contains 500 mg TXA.

Contraindications to the IMP are outlined in the exclusion criteria.

Placebo equivalent: 0.9% physiological saline solution (infusion solution).

Formulation: Each 100 mL of solution contains 0.9 g of sodium chloride (INN).

The medicinal products used in this study have been approved by the Spanish Agency for Medicines and Medical Devices. Intravenous administration will be based on the product/prescribing information, while its topical use is not contemplated in the relevant product information and as such the doses used are specific to this clinical trial. The IMP will be obtained through the National Health System’s usual channels as a medication routinely used for this type of patient in clinical practice.

The study products will be distributed from the pharmacy at each participating hospital to the attending physician. The IMP and placebo will be properly labelled, packaged, stored and supplied by the pharmacy at each participant hospital during working hours and when possible, just prior to administration. However, patients may also be operated on at the weekend or outside regular working hours, when the pharmacy may be closed. Hence, it is necessary to have the treatments available in these situations, although there is no information available regarding the stability of this product when stored at room temperature (RT) or refrigerated (2°C–8°C). Consequently, to ensure the product was not affected by its storage and that it could be used on patients operated outside of regular pharmacy working hours, the physicochemical stability of a 3 g formulation of TXA diluted in a 0.9% normal saline solution and maintained in a syringe over 48 hours was assessed. Two samples of TXA (3 g in saline solution) and two placebo samples were prepared, one of each (TXA or placebo) stored at RT or refrigerated (2°C–8°C). A validated high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) assay was used to assess the stability of the samples at 0 (freshly prepared sample), 2, 4, 8, 10, 24, 30 and 48 hours, also measuring the pH and osmolality, and visually inspecting the solutions. When stored at RT or refrigerated (2°C–8°C), both the TXA and placebo solutions remained colourless, clear and free of visible particles throughout the 48 hours. Similarly, the mean pH and osmolarity of both the TXA and placebo solutions remained stable over that period when the samples were stored at RT or refrigerated. The HPLC assay indicated that the TXA concentration remained between 99.3% and 100.69% of the initial concentration when the samples were stored at RT, and between 97.99% and 100.08% for samples stored refrigerated (2°C–8°C). These results confirm that a formulation of 3 g TXA diluted in 0.9% NaCl and maintained in a syringe was stable for up to 48 hours when stored at RT or refrigerated (2°C–8°C).

For patients in the groups that will receive intravenous TXA, a total of 4 lots will be prepared and distributed in two packages: one will be sent to the A&E department containing the doses to be administered to patients in that department within 12 hours of their admission; the other containing the remaining three doses will be sent to the operating room the day of surgery. For patients with contraindications to intravenous TXA, the doses for topical administration will be distributed in one package that will be sent to the operating room the day of surgery.

Access to and administration of the IMP and equivalent placebo preparations will be limited to the investigator and authorised personnel. The quantities of product delivered and used at the participating hospitals and that recovered during the study period will be recorded, collated and verified by (or on behalf of) the Sponsor. Unused product will be returned to the pharmacy and the IMP will not be destroyed without prior written authorisation from the Sponsor, and once any discrepancies have been investigated, properly explained and accepted. An inventory of returned medication and a record of the destruction operations will be kept by the pharmacy services and the Sponsor, permitting backward traceability of batch and patient numbers.

Description of interventions and procedures

A meta-analysis on the use of TXA in patients with hip fractures indicates that most studies report the use of TXA either preoperatively or preoperatively and postoperatively, with a total dose varying from 10 mg/kg to 1–4 g.17 It has been established that a therapeutic plasma concentration of TXA of 10 ng/mL32 is necessary for an 80% reduction in plasminogen activator activity to suppress fibrinolysis in tissues.33 However, a dose of TXA of 10 mg/kg can maintain this plasma concentration for only 3 hours and this may be insufficient to prevent postoperative bleeding, such that it has been suggested that higher doses should be used.34 These limitations highlight the need for new, carefully designed, randomised clinical trials.17 18 Virtually all studies begin with the use of TXA just before surgery, which does not interfere with preoperative blood loss, estimated to be approximately one-third of total blood loss. Previous studies in elective hip and knee replacement surgery have suggested that TXA is more effective when started before the fibrinolysis process begins rather than after.35,37 Starting TXA administration on patient admission rather than on the day of surgery requires further study,20 and existing clinical studies are few and of limited methodological quality, making this study necessary to verify the efficacy of the treatment.

Patients assigned to the treatment arms for intravenous TXA will receive a 15 mg/kg dose of intravenous TXA diluted in 100 cc of NaCl 0.9%, preferably on admission to the A&E department or once transferred to the ward and within a maximum of 12 hours after A&E admission (visit No. 2). In addition, they will receive two more doses during the perioperative period: 15 mg/kg of intravenous TXA during anaesthetic induction, 15–20 min before the incision; and another dose of 10 mg/kg of intravenous TXA administered 3 hours after the first dose administered at the induction of anaesthesia (visit No. 4). These patients will also receive 3 g of TXA diluted in 30 cc of NaCl 0.9% administered topically at the surgical site prior to surgical closure, once the metal implant has been put in place (visit No. 4). The treatment arms in which intravenous TXA is not recommended will only be administered a single 3 g dose of topical TXA diluted in 30 cc of saline at the surgical site prior to closure (visit No. 4). Those patients assigned to the placebo groups will be administered an equivalent volume of saline solution alone following the same guidelines as for the experimental groups (see flow diagram in figure 1, and the study visit schedule and associated outcome assessments summarised in online supplemental table 1).

As indicated above, intravenous treatment will be administered to those patients with no contraindication to intravenous TXA at visit No. 2 (preferably in the A&E room or once transferred to a ward, within a maximum of 12 hours after admission to the A&E department) and visit No. 4 (in the operating theatre at anaesthetic induction, 15–20 min before making the initial incision). Subsequently, a topical dose will be administered to all patients at the end of the surgery irrespective of whether they receive intravenous TXA (see figure 2). Finally, those patients with no contraindication to intravenous TXA will receive an intravenous TXA administration in the resuscitation room 3 hours after inducing anaesthesia. The occurrence of AEs or the absence of clinical benefit may require temporary or permanent discontinuation of the treatment. These measures will be taken based on the product characteristics and under the discretion of the qualified investigator.

Topical TXA or placebo will be administered in the operating room at the end of the surgical procedure. If a hip prosthesis is to be introduced during the surgical procedure, the topical TXA will be administered by intra-articular injection. If prosthetic replacement is not required (eg, with pertrochanteric and subtrochanteric fractures and cephalic screw issues), topical TXA will be administered by injection at the perifracture level.

The hospital’s standard blood transfusion criteria will be followed (Hb <75 g/L in all patients or Hb <90 g/L in the case of patients with symptomatic anaemia or a relevant clinical history), at the physician’s discretion. While hospitalised, patients with Hb <120 g/L will be administered a single intravenous dose of 1000 mg of Fe carboxymaltose and the number of bags transfused will be recorded on patient discharge. Hb and haematocrit (Ht) values will be recorded daily from admission to the A&E department until the third postoperative day (see online supplemental table 1). To calculate blood loss, the patient’s estimated blood volume will be calculated using reference values,38 and the laboratory values will be used to calculate the estimated preoperative and postoperative occult blood loss. Early patient mobilisation will be performed in the immediate postoperative period and weight-bearing on the operated leg will be recommended for patients once sufficient stability has been achieved. DVT prophylaxis will be standardised with 40 mg of enoxaparin every 24 hours beginning on admission until 30 days after the surgery. Diagnostic imaging studies to confirm thromboembolic events (ie, DVT, pulmonary embolism (PE), stroke) will only be performed if the patient’s blood shows clinical signs or symptoms that justify their use.

In order to analyse the factors influencing blood loss and the effect of TXA administration on blood loss, Hb evolution will be monitored at the time of hospital admission, every 24 hours thereafter for 3 days before surgery, just before surgery, and every 24 hours during the first 3 days of the postoperative period. Hb levels will also be recorded at any time during hospitalisation after a blood transfusion. In the perioperative period, the incidence of blood transfusion, total blood loss and AEs (transfusion reactions, cerebrovascular accidents (CVAs)—myocardial infarctions, PEs, symptomatic DVT, surgical wound infection and death) will be assessed at visits No. 2–5. Participants will be also assessed at 1, 3, 6, 9 and 12 months postoperatively to evaluate long-term AEs and to determine the mortality rate (visits No. 6–10)

Data analyses plan

Data collection and management

All the information collected during the study to be transmitted to Health Authorities will be recorded on an eCRF generated in REDCap and provided by the Sponsor, which will be signed and dated by the PI. All corrections will be recorded with an audit trail system identifying the person who made the correction and the date. Patient eCRFs may be submitted to the AEMPS on request of the Health Authorities. The patient’s records, original documents, CRFs, and any correspondence between the Ethics Committee and the Sponsor will be archived.

In addition to the eCRF, the PI will maintain the subjects’ original data, including the dates of any examinations, their clinical history, and the data concerning vital signs, examinations performed and any AEs. All this information constitutes the ‘source documents’ on which data recorded in CRFs is supported. The eCRF should be properly maintained and kept up to date so that they always reflect the latest observations of the patients enrolled in the study. On completion of the clinical trial, the informed consent should be archived with a copy of the completed eCRF in the clinical trial file. The patients’ clinical history and other original data should be retained for the maximum period of time permitted by the hospital. The essential documents relating to the clinical trial must be retained for at least 25 years after its completion.

Statistical analyses

A first descriptive analysis will be performed initially to ensure the quality of the data by checking whether all the data values are within expected ranges and there are no logical inconsistencies or impossible data. Any inconsistencies will be corrected by checking the information against the clinical history to have a clean database before conducting the descriptive statistical analysis. Categorical variables will be expressed as absolute frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables as means and SDs. The χ² test will be used to analyse the effect of TXA on the transfusion rate, and a Student’s t-test to assess its effect on blood loss. To quantify the magnitude of the effect on transfusion frequency, relative risk, absolute risk reduction and the number needed to treat, along with their 95% CI will be presented, while the mean difference and its 95% CI will be used to quantify the magnitude of the effect on blood loss. Logistic regression will be used to study variables associated with transfusion frequency and variables related to blood loss will be analysed by multiple linear regression. A subgroup analysis will be performed to determine whether treatment effects are influenced by the type of fracture or recommendations against intravenous TXA use. The analysis will be performed using the intention-to-treat method, ensuring compliance with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines for reporting RCTs. SPSS V.23 statistical software will be used for all statistical analyses.

It is proposed that an interim analysis will be carried out when half of the participants are enrolled onto the trial to rule out any unethical differences relating to the primary study variable and the risk of thrombosis. This analysis will be carried out by an independent committee appointed for this purpose, which will determine the criteria for deciding on the continuation of the study.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design of the trial, the collection and interpretation of data, or the dissemination plans of this research.

Ethics and dissemination

Ethical considerations

The final study protocol, including the final version of the informed consent, was authorised by the AEMPS and approved by the Basque Country Ethics Committee (Ref.: 2021012). The study’s Sponsor, IIS Biogipuzkoa (formerly Biodonostia), is responsible for informing the Ethics Committee and the regulatory authorities of any modification to the protocol in accordance with current legislation. Approvals, authorisations and notifications, where required, will be fully documented at each centre prior to commencing the study. The study will be conducted in full conformity with the international ethical standards outlined in the Helsinki Declaration for research and clinical studies involving human participants, and according to the standards of Good Clinical Practice (GCP), the current legal regulations (Royal Decree 1090/2015) and the recommendations of the AEMPS regarding clinical studies. The trial commenced only after it was approved by the Basque Country Ethics Committee and it was authorised by the AEMPS, and a contract was signed by all the participating institutions. Written informed consent will be obtained from all the participants prior to their inclusion onto the study.

Data monitoring and safety

The Sponsor, IIS Biogipuzkoa, will be responsible for monitoring the study. They will periodically contact and visit the PIs to inspect the eCRF and other relevant documentation, provided that the patient’s right to privacy is respected in accordance with local regulations. The aim of this monitoring is to verify compliance with the protocol, and the completeness, consistency and accuracy of the data entered. The monitor will also ensure that informed consent is obtained from all participants prior to their inclusion on the trial, and that the trial is conducted in accordance with GCP guidelines, applicable regulatory requirements and the procedures set forth in the trial protocol. The monitor will check that AEs are recorded in the CRF and that severe AEs (SAEs) and AEs of special interest (AESIs) are reported to the Sponsor in a timely manner according to the protocol. The monitor will review and ensure that the essential trial documents in the investigator’s file are up to date, and will monitor the patient enrolment rate relative to expectations.

AEs will be recorded in the patient’s medical records and all AEs for which a causal relationship with the IMP is suspected, any SAEs and AESIs will be documented in the patient’s eCRF, including the following information: start date, description, severity, intensity, treatment, causal relationship, progression and outcome. Hospitalisations planned prior to patient inclusion in the study (ie, elective or planned surgery scheduled before the patient’s inclusion in the study) will not be considered AEs. The causal relationship of AEs to the IMP will also be determined by the investigator as ‘Probably Related’, ‘Possibly Related’ or ‘Unlikely to be Related’. Given the particular conditions of the patients in this study, DVT will be considered a SAE but not a reason to break the masking. AESIs will include: thrombotic AEs and transfusion reactions, CVAs, myocardial infarctions, PTE, symptomatic DVT, surgical wound infection and death. Transfusion reactions will be recorded and classified according to their time of onset relative to the transfusion, either as acute or delayed reactions according to the ‘Guideline Recommendations for the Transfusion of Blood Components and Plasma Derivatives’, fifth edition, published by the Spanish Society of Blood Transfusion and Cell Therapy.

AEs for each participant will be recorded by the research teams from the time of signing the informed consent throughout the intervention and the follow-up period until the final study visit (1 year). AE and SAE occurrence will be assessed at every visit from: information spontaneously provided by the patient/caregiver; open-ended, non-prompted verbal questioning of the patient/caregiver; and through observation by the research teams, other caregivers or family members. During the study period, ongoing AEs will be tracked by the investigator until resolution or stabilisation, unless the investigators consider the AE is unlikely to remit due to the underlying disease. The investigators shall report SAEs and AESIs to the Sponsor without undue delay and within 24 hours of becoming aware of them. The investigator shall, where appropriate, send the Sponsor a follow-up report to enable the Sponsor to assess whether the SAE and the AESI have an impact on the benefit-risk balance of the clinical trial. The Sponsor will notify the AEMPS of all AEs suspected to be related to the IMP that are both serious and unexpected, and of which it has become aware.

Dissemination plan

Once the statistical analysis is completed, the results of the clinical trial will be reviewed and discussed by the research team. The Sponsor will prepare the final report in collaboration with the investigators involved in the study. The final trial report will be submitted to the Basque Country Ethics Committee and the AEMPS in accordance with current legislation. The Sponsor and the coordinating investigator undertake to publish the trial’s results, whether positive or negative. The results of the study will be made publicly available through the European Union (EU) Clinical Trials Register after the end of the clinical trial.

Confidentiality

All records identifying the participants will be confidential and will not be made publicly available. The data collected should be submitted by the investigator in such a way that the patient’s identification is not obvious. The patient will be identified only by a code in the eCRF so that the physician responsible for the treatment is the only person capable of identifying the patient. The physician will maintain a list to enable patients’ records to be identified in case of doubt and the patient will be informed prior to entering the study that the following people could have access to their medical records: those authorised by the study Sponsor for the purpose of verifying the study data and procedures; and the Health Authorities and the Basque Country Ethics Committee, provided that their data is kept confidential at all times. The processing, communication and transfer of all participants’ personal data will comply with the provisions of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 27 April (‘General Data Protection Regulation’), and the Organic Law 3/2018 of 5 December on the Protection of Personal Data and Guarantee of Digital Rights in Spain.

Discussion

The purpose of the study is to assess the benefits of early TXA administration to hip fracture patients on their admission to the A&E department, particularly in relation to blood loss and blood transfusion outcomes. Patients with hip fracture experience significant blood loss and associated AEs, a substantial portion of which is caused by the injury itself rather than the surgical procedure; these patients undergo a presurgical blood loss that is often underestimated.7 Thus, these patients have characteristics that make them well-suited for the proposed RCT on TXA administration at hospital admission and during the perioperative period. Our hypothesis is that TXA administration will reduce blood loss in patients with hip fractures and decrease the allogeneic blood transfusion rate. In line with the conclusions and the recommendations that emerged from the most important meta-analysis,8 our study is designed to assess the management of presurgical anaemia with a combination of iron and TXA administration from the moment the patient is admitted to the A&E department and not only during the intraoperative and postoperative period as studied previously. This will allow us to assess the influence of early TXA administration on blood loss and blood transfusion outcomes. The strategy of using early TXA to reduce preoperative anaemia is already employed in bleeding trauma patients, an approach based on the results of the CRASH‐2 trial (Clinical Randomisation of an Antifibrinolytic in Significant Haemorrhage 2) that concluded early TXA treatment was significantly more effective than late treatment in reducing death due to bleeding in trauma patients.39 40 In addition, the participants will not only be monitored preoperatively and perioperatively but also, they will be followed for up to 1 year after the hip surgery to gain a long-term assessment of AEs and the 1 year mortality rate. This will help clarify the thrombotic risk associated with TXA, which is crucial to determine the clinical utility of TXA treatment in this patient population.

One methodological limitation of the study is the variability in the timing of the first dose due to the emergency setting. Indeed, the treatment was administered intravenously to those patients with no contraindications to intravenous TXA, preferably on admission to the A&E department or once transferred to a ward, and within a maximum of 12 hours after admission. This variability in when the first intravenous dose is administered to patients introduces a potential bias that should be taken into account when analysing the results. Conversely, a novelty of this study is that it is powered to also assess a difference in blood transfusion outcomes between the two study arms independently, intracapsular and extracapsular hip fractures. Patients with intracapsular hip fractures have previously been shown to have significantly less preoperative blood loss and to require significantly fewer preoperative transfusions than their extracapsular counterparts.41 Given the difference in blood loss following injury between these two hip fracture patterns,13 independent demonstration of the efficacy of TXA treatment for these different types of fracture is of great interest. In addition, the study has added value residing in the evaluation of the influence of TXA when administered to patients with hip fractures irrespective of their thrombotic history. In previous studies, hip fracture patients taking anticoagulants or antiplatelet agents and those with a history of thromboembolic events (DVT or PE, heart attack, stroke, etc) were commonly excluded from trials, representing almost half of all patients who suffer a hip fracture. According to the product information sheets that we have access to, acute venous or arterial thrombosis is considered a contraindication for the use of TXA, as is hypersensitivity to this product. In addition, it is recommended to avoid concomitant use of TXA injection and medical products that are pro-thrombotic, as this may enhance the risk of thrombosis. For this reason, patients in these circumstances are commonly excluded from clinical trials assessing the benefits and safety of using this drug. By contrast, the protocol presented here contemplates the inclusion of these patients with a risk of thromboembolism, although they will only receive TXA topically in the perioperative period as opposed to intravenous TXA administration, evaluating the benefits of this regimen in terms of transfusion rates. Accordingly, the efficacy of TXA administration will be assessed independently for patients with and without a history of thrombotic events, providing novel and important information regarding their treatment. This data can influence future perioperative blood management of such cases, potentially shifting standards for the preoperative and perioperative surgical management of hip fractures.

The efficacy of TXA in reducing transfusion rates by decreasing blood loss in patients with hip fractures ultimately results in shorter hospital stays and improved outcomes in these patients. In turn, this will reduce associated healthcare costs and increase patient satisfaction. If the results of the present study demonstrate the efficacy of TXA administration when treating different types of hip fractures in patients with and without a history of thrombotic events and from the moment the patient is admitted to the emergency department, this study could change the standards of care for patients with hip fractures across Spain.
