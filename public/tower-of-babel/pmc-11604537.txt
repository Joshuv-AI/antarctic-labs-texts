EXPANDing in Japan
Authors: Khung Keong Yeo, Ningyan Wong
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

Since the first MitraClip (Abbott Cardiovascular) implantation in 2003 for severe mitral regurgitation (MR), mitral transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (M-TEER) has made significant advancement over the past 2 decades.1 Randomized trials including EVEREST (Endovascular Valve Edge-to-Edge Repair Study) II, COAPT (Cardiovascular Outcomes Assessment of the MitraClip Percutaneous Therapy for Heart Failure Patients with Functional Mitral Regurgitation), and MITRA-FR (Multicentre Study of Percutaneous Mitral Valve Repair MitraClip Device in Patients With Severe Secondary Mitral Regurgitation), along with real-world registries, have provided the robust evidence that has culminated in guideline recommendations for M-TEER in the treatment of patients with degenerative MR who are at high surgical risk, and those with functional MR who remained symptomatic despite guideline-directed medical therapy.2, 3, 4 Several factors contribute to the positive clinical outcomes observed—greater operator experience, better imaging expertise and technology, improved patient selection, and notable progress in the technology itself. The device has undergone several iterations: the original to the NT system in 2016, NTR/XTR in 2018, and the most recent G4 in 2019. The latest iteration offers 4 clip sizes with varying widths (4 and 6 mm) and lengths (9 and 12 mm) to address a variety of leaflet pathologies. Additionally, controlled gripper actuation, now a standard for any transcatheter edge-to-edge repair technology, enables independent leaflet grasping and optimization. These advancements have expanded treatable pathologies beyond the narrow criteria established by EVEREST II to encompass more complex anatomies, including noncentral or commissural lesions, small mitral valve areas (MVAs), wide jets, and excessive prolapse or flail segments. Nevertheless, it remains crucial to gather evidence to support the anticipated improvements in MR reduction and clinical outcomes.

Despite the rapid adoption and success of M-TEER in Western countries, its uptake in Asia has been slow to gain momentum. Although the first M-TEER in Asia was performed in 2011, the technology’s adoption has been gradual.5 Several challenges, including high cost, lack of national reimbursement, and local regulatory hurdles, have contributed to the slow uptake. However, the completion of the premarket AVJ-514 trial, which led to the approval of M-TEER in Japan, significantly accelerated its growth in the region.6 Current available evidence of M-TEER in Asia has been primarily limited to site-adjudicated clinical and echocardiographic data from the MARS (MitraClip Asia-Pacific Registry), Japan Post-Marketing Surveillance study, and the OCEAN-Mitral registry.7, 8, 9

In this issue of JACC: Asia, Morikawa et al10 present timely results from the Japan cohort of the EXPAND G4 study, showcasing contemporary, echocardiographic core laboratory (ECL)-adjudicated real-world outcomes of M-TEER using the fourth-generation system. Of the 1,164 subjects in the overall EXPAND G4 cohort, 95 were from Japan. The key finding was significant and sustained MR reduction, with 98.7% having mild or less MR (≤1+) at 1 year, and over one-half (55.3%) with none or trace MR. These remarkable results surpass previous studies—43.0% in EVEREST II (first-generation), 69% in COAPT (first-generation) and 89.2% in the EXPAND study (third-generation) with MR ≤1+.2,3,11 The outcomes from Japan were even more favorable than the overall EXPAND G4 cohort, where 92.3% had MR ≤1+. This outcome underscores the clinically relevant technological improvements as well as the expertise of the operators and imagers.

As expected in a physically smaller Asian population, MVA and left ventricle chamber size are smaller than in the overall cohort. However, the availability of 4 clip sizes allows for careful device selection to maximize MR reduction without compromising mitral inflow gradients. This study noted a greater use of narrower clips (17% NT and 9% XT) in the Japanese cohort compared with the full cohort (8% NT and 4% XT), particularly among patients with smaller MVAs (<4.5 cm2). Consequently, the mean mitral valve gradient remained reasonable at 2.9 ± 1.8 mm Hg at 1 year. Despite using the same delivery system in a more constrained space among the smaller-sized Asian population, the procedure remained safe, with no procedural or in-hospital mortalities. Only 1 patient experienced single-leaflet device attachment, and 2 required mitral valve replacement surgery within 1 year.

The surgical risk for the Japanese cohort was notably high, evidenced by a Society of Thoracic Surgeons Replacement Score of 11.9% ± 9.1%, compared with 7.6% ± 6.2% for the full cohort. Despite this elevated risk, the 1-year mortality rate was remarkably low at 9.5% (9 of 95 patients). Although not directly proven, one could speculate that improved clinical outcomes may be linked to better MR reduction and positive left ventricle remodeling. Importantly, because of the therapy's success, patients feel better, with 96.2% achieving NYHA functional class I/II and a median improvement of 11 points in the Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire score at 1 year.

There are, however, some important limitations that frame these impressive results. First, apart from 14 subjects whom the ECL was unable to assess for MR etiology, only 16% had what was considered complex mitral valve anatomy. This included 8.6% with a primary jet outside A2/P2, 4.9% with bileaflet flail or prolapse, 3.7% with severe degenerative leaflets/wide flail gaps, 1.2% showing more than 1 significant jet, another 1.2% with short leaflet length, and 1.2% with significant cleft. Notably, no cases had wide jets, calcified landing zones, or small valves. These figures appear lower than the complexity of cases encountered in routine clinical practice, which raises caution in the generalizability of the study’s results to real-world scenarios. Second, there is a considerable gap in echocardiographic data, because only 76 of 95 treated subjects had MR severity grading at 1 year. Therefore, although the study reported 98.7% mild or less MR at 1 year, this has to be interpreted in the context of only 80% of the treated subjects. Third, it is intriguing that at baseline, 5.2% of subjects were classified with a preintervention ECL-adjudicated MR severity of ≤1+, whereas 25.0% had a severity of 2+ and 69.8% had 3+/4+. This discrepancy raises concerns regarding the varying assessments between site and ECL-adjudicated MR severity derived from the same echocardiographic images. Fourth, these results apply only to the MitraClip TEER therapy. PASCAL (Edwards Lifesciences) has only recently begun to enter some Asian markets, and Chinese technologies like DragonFly (Valgen Medtech) remain limited within China.

Nevertheless, the findings presented in the publication by Morikawa et al10 serve as a robust testament to the progression of TEER across multiple dimensions—technological advancements, operator experience, imaging proficiency, and patient selection. The notable reduction in MR with TEER, coupled with the procedure's safety, establishes a benchmark for future studies assessing transcatheter mitral valve interventions. Importantly, this study marks the first ECL-adjudicated TEER investigation conducted in Asia. Given that Asia is the world’s most populous continent, housing over 4 billion people and exhibiting marked diversity in demographics, disease characteristics and socioeconomic status, there is a critical need for a comprehensive TEER database, ideally one that captures data from different technologies that accurately reflect real-world experience for M-TEER across different Asian countries.
