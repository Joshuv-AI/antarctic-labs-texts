Association between premenstrual syndrome severity and musculoskeletal pain among nursing students: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Fatih Okan, Sevil Okan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMusculoskeletal pain is highly prevalent among nursing students and may negatively affect their physical functioning and readiness for clinical practice. Premenstrual syndrome (PMS), characterized by physical and psychological symptoms, has been suggested to be associated with pain sensitivity and musculoskeletal discomfort. However, the relationship between PMS severity and musculoskeletal pain in nursing students has not been well established.MethodsThis study employed a descriptive cross-sectional design and included 257 female nursing students. Data were collected using the Premenstrual Syndrome Scale (PMSS) to evaluate the presence and severity of PMS symptoms and the Extended Nordic Musculoskeletal Questionnaire (NMQ-E) to assess musculoskeletal complaints (pain, discomfort, or ache) across nine distinct body regions. Descriptive statistics, independent samples t-tests, chi-square tests, and logistic regression analysis were performed. All inferential statistical analyses were performed with a significance level set at p < 0.05.ResultsPMS was identified in 82.9% (n = 213) of participants, and 90.7% (n = 233) reported musculoskeletal pain. Students with pain had significantly higher PMSS scores (148.5 ± 28.5) than those without pain (90.5 ± 26.5; p < 0.001). Logistic regression analysis showed that PMS severity was independently associated with musculoskeletal pain (OR = 1.08, 95% CI: 1.05–1.11, p < 0.001). Chi-square analysis revealed that musculoskeletal pain in the neck, shoulder, lower back, and hips was significantly associated with increased healthcare utilization, medication use, and sick leave (p < 0.05). The lower back (75.9%, n = 195), upper back (42.0%, n = 108), and hip regions (39.3%, n = 101) were the most commonly affected regions.ConclusionsPMS severity is significantly associated with musculoskeletal pain in nursing students and may be linked to pain-related functional limitations. Early identification of students with severe PMS symptoms and incorporation of menstrual health considerations into musculoskeletal assessments may support preventive strategies aimed at reducing absenteeism, healthcare utilization, and pain-related disability, while improving the occupational health and clinical functioning of future nurses.Keywords: Premenstrual syndrome, Musculoskeletal pain, Nursing students

Background

Premenstrual syndrome (PMS) is characterized by a group of physical, behavioral, and emotional symptoms that typically appear at least five days before menstruation and subside within four days after its onset, persisting for a minimum of three consecutive menstrual cycles [1]. The symptoms vary in intensity and timing among individuals and may include breast tenderness, abdominal bloating, headache, fatigue, irritability, anxiety, mood swings, depression, difficulty concentrating, and social withdrawal [2]. Although the precise etiology of PMS remains uncertain, various endocrine, genetic, and psychosocial factors have been implicated. Fluctuations in ovarian steroids and altered neurotransmitter activity, particularly involving serotonin, GABA, and β-endorphins, are believed to contribute to symptom development [3]. Hormonal imbalances, metabolic disturbances, and stress-related mechanisms are also thought to play a role in the manifestation and severity of PMS [4]. Nursing is a profession that involves physically demanding and psychologically stressful work environments. Heavy workloads, prolonged standing, patient handling, and emotional strain are known to adversely affect nurses’ physical and mental well-being, leading to decreased job satisfaction and performance [5]. The study conducted by Chayachinda et al. on Thai nurses showed that nurses may have a higher predisposition to PMS due to occupational stress and irregular working hours, with prevalence rates around 25% among practicing nurses [6]. Among Turkish university students, PMS prevalence has been reported to range from 33% to 91.8%, reflecting significant variability across different populations [7–9].

Musculoskeletal disorders (MSDs) are among the leading causes of occupational injury and disability worldwide. Nurses, in particular, represent a high-risk group because of their exposure to biomechanical strain, repetitive movements, and patient-handling tasks [10]. Previous studies have shown that even undergraduate nursing students, before entering professional practice, experience substantial rates of musculoskeletal pain [11, 12]. This is likely due to early exposure to clinical environments where physical demands and ergonomic risks are similar to those faced by professional nurses. Moreover, the development of musculoskeletal symptoms during undergraduate years may predispose students to chronic pain and recurrent problems later in their careers [13, 14].

Given these overlapping physiological, psychological, and occupational stressors, nursing students constitute a particularly vulnerable population for both PMS and MSDs. However, the relationship between PMS symptom severity and musculoskeletal pain has not been adequately explored in this group. Understanding the relationship between these two conditions is critical for developing preventive strategies that enhance students’ well-being, reduce absenteeism, and improve long-term professional health outcomes.

Therefore, this study aimed to determine the association between PMS severity and musculoskeletal pain among undergraduate nursing students and to evaluate the association between PMS severity and the presence of MSD-related symptoms.

Methods

Research design

This study was conducted using a descriptive cross-sectional research design.

Study population

The population of the study consisted of 448 female students enrolled in the Department of Nursing at the Faculty of Health Sciences during the 2025–2026 academic year.

Sample size

The study sample consisted of 257 female nursing students who were included between May and June 2025. Participants were selected using a simple random sampling technique. A list of all female students (n = 448) was obtained from faculty records, and each student was assigned a unique identification number. A computerized random number generator was then used to randomly select participants from this list. Selected students were invited to participate. Based on a previous similar study [15], the required sample size was calculated as 255 participants using G*Power 3.1.9.4 software with 80% power, a 0.05 margin of error, and an effect size of 0.18. Considering a 5% non-response rate, questionnaires were distributed to 269 students.

Inclusion Criteria.

The inclusion criteria for the study were: [1] being a female student enrolled in the Department of Nursing during the 2025–2026 academic year [2], volunteering to participate in the study, and [3] providing informed consent.

Exclusion criteria

Participants were excluded if they did not volunteer to participate, experienced pain due to a chronic illness (e.g., cancer or neuropathic pain), had a diagnosed inflammatory rheumatic disease, received medical treatment for pain within the last three months, had a history of orthopedic surgery, or had a diagnosed psychiatric condition.

Data collection tools

Data were collected using a Personal Information Form, the Premenstrual Syndrome Scale (PMSS), and the Extended Nordic Musculoskeletal Questionnaire (NMQ-E).

Personal information form

Developed by the researchers, this form includes 23 items regarding socio-demographic characteristics and information related to the menstrual cycle.

Premenstrual syndrome scale

The PMSS was developed based on DSM-III and DSM-IV-TR criteria, and its validity and reliability studies were performed by Gençdoğan [16], reporting a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.75. The scale consists of 44 items rated on a five-point Likert scale (1 = Never, 5 = Always), taking approximately 10 min to complete. It evaluates nine domains: depressive affect, anxiety, fatigue, irritability, depressive thoughts, pain, appetite changes, sleep changes, and bloating. Total scores range from 44 to 220, with higher scores indicating more severe PMS symptoms. A total score of ≥ 111 indicates the presence of PMS.

Extended nordic musculoskeletal questionnaire (NMQ-E)

The NMQ-E assesses the presence of musculoskeletal complaints (pain, discomfort, or ache) in nine body regions (neck, shoulders, upper back, elbows, wrists/hands, lower back, hips/thighs, knees, and ankles/feet) across different time periods: lifetime, past 12 months, past four weeks, and current day. The tool, developed by Dawson et al. [17], has demonstrated reliability and validity in Turkish [18].

Data collection procedure

Students selected for the sample were invited to participate in the study. Data were collected through self-report questionnaires in a suitable classroom environment during free hours (between or after classes).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 22.0 (Chicago, IL, USA). Descriptive statistics were reported as mean, standard deviation, minimum–maximum values, frequency, and percentage. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to evaluate the normality of the data. The independent samples t-test was applied to compare continuous variables between two independent groups, and chi-square tests were used for categorical comparisons. Logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify factors associated with the outcomes. Variables considered clinically relevant or previously associated with musculoskeletal pain in the literature were included in the logistic regression model. The model included total PMSS score, BMI, age, age at menarche, academic year, chronic disease status, alcohol consumption, regular physical activity, and regular menstrual cycle status. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Use of artificial intelligence tool

ChatGPT (OpenAI) was used solely to assist with English language editing and translation. The authors reviewed, edited, and approved all AI-assisted content and accept full responsibility for the accuracy, integrity, and originality of the manuscript. No artificial intelligence tools were used in the study design, data collection, analysis, or interpretation.

Results

A total of 257 questionnaires were returned fully completed and met the inclusion criteria, yielding a response rate of 95.5%. The participants had a mean age of 21.10 ± 1.74 years (range: 18–27), and the mean age at menarche was 12.96 ± 1.36 years (range: 10–18). Most participants were fourth-year students (26.1%, n = 67) and reported that their income was equal to their expenses (70.0%, n = 180). The majority lived in a nuclear family (83.3%, n = 214) and were covered by social security (72.4%, n = 186). A large proportion did not have a chronic disease (87.9%, n = 226), did not smoke (86.0%, n = 221), and did not consume alcohol (94.9%, n = 244). Only about one in four participants (26.1%, n = 67) engaged in regular physical activity. PMS was reported by 82.9% (n = 213) of the participants, and musculoskeletal pain was experienced by 90.7% (n = 233). According to BMI categories, 68.1% (n = 175) were within the normal weight range, 15.2% (n = 39) were overweight, and 7.0% (n = 18) were obese. Furthermore, 78.6% (n = 202) reported regular menstruation. Menstrual periods were perceived as sometimes affecting social life (63.8%, n = 164) and school life (63.4%, n = 163) (Table 1).

Participants who reported musculoskeletal pain had significantly higher mean scores across all subscales of the PMSS compared with those without pain (p < 0.001 for all). Specifically, depressive affect, anxiety, fatigue, irritability, depressive thoughts, pain, appetite change, sleep disturbance, and bloating subscale scores were all elevated among participants with pain. The total PMS score was also markedly higher in participants with musculoskeletal pain (148.45 ± 28.50) than in those without pain (90.50 ± 26.50) (t = 9.544, p < 0.001). Overall, the results indicate that the presence of musculoskeletal pain is strongly associated with increased severity of both physical and psychological premenstrual symptoms (Table 2).

Lower back pain was significantly associated with work-related limitations (p = 0.002), medication use (p = 0.031), and sick leave (p < 0.001). Hip pain was significantly related to hospitalization (p = 0.017), work-related limitations (p = 0.032), medication use (p = 0.030), and sick leave (p = 0.005). The results of the logistic regression analysis examining factors associated with musculoskeletal pain are presented in Table 4. Among the variables included in the model, only the total PMS score was found to be significantly associated with musculoskeletal pain (OR = 1.082; 95% CI: 1.052–1.112; p < 0.001). An OR of 1.082 (95% CI: 1.052–1.112) indicates that each one-point increase in PMSS score is associated with an 8.2% increase in the odds of musculoskeletal pain. However, given the wide range of the PMSS (44–220), this unit change represents a relatively small increment and may limit clinical interpretability. When interpreted over a 10-point increase, this corresponds to approximately a 2.2-fold increase in the odds of musculoskeletal pain (1.082¹⁰ ≈ 2.19).

Other variables, including body mass index (BMI) (OR = 1.033; p = 0.628), age (OR = 0.641; p = 0.108), age at menarche (OR = 1.356; p = 0.181), academic year, presence of chronic disease, alcohol consumption, regular physical activity, and regular menstrual cycle, were not significantly associated with musculoskeletal pain (p > 0.05).

The explanatory power of the model was relatively high, with a Nagelkerke R² value of 0.572, indicating that approximately 57.2% of the variance in musculoskeletal pain was explained by the variables included in the model. The Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test indicated acceptable model fit (χ² = 11.237, df = 8, p = 0.189) (Table 4).

Among the 257 participants, 195 (75.9%) reported lower back pain, 108 (42.0%) upper back pain, 101 (39.3%) hip pain, 100 (38.9%) elbow pain, 96 (37.4%) knee pain, 71 (27.6%) shoulder pain, 60 (23.3%) neck pain, 50 (19.5%) ankle pain, and 38 (14.8%) wrist pain.

The analysis revealed significant differences in PMS scale total score means across body regions. The “Yes” group showed consistently higher mean scores compared to the “No” group in all body regions. The largest mean differences were observed in the shoulder and upper back regions, while the smallest differences occurred in the knee and ankle regions. Discussion

This study aimed to examine the relationship between PMS and musculoskeletal pain among nursing students. The findings revealed a high prevalence of both PMS and musculoskeletal symptoms, with significantly higher PMS scores among participants experiencing musculoskeletal pain.

Previous studies conducted among university and nursing students have also reported high PMS prevalence rates, although prevalence estimates vary considerably across populations and study settings [19–23, 24]. The high prevalence and severity of PMS observed in this study may be explained by both biological and psychosocial factors. Nursing students face heavier academic and clinical workloads than students in other disciplines, and exposure to unfamiliar clinical environments can increase stress, anxiety, and tension [25]. Fluctuations in estrogen and progesterone levels throughout the menstrual cycle can cause emotional alterations and increase vulnerability to stress [26]. Collectively, these observations highlight the complex interaction between academic stressors and hormonal factors, suggesting that effective stress management and menstrual health support programs may be associated with lower PMS prevalence and severity among nursing students.

From a mechanistic perspective, hormonal fluctuations may not only influence mood but also modulate central pain processing, potentially lowering the pain threshold and increasing susceptibility to musculoskeletal pain [3]. Musculoskeletal pain was highly prevalent among nursing students. The three most commonly reported pain sites were the lower back, upper back, and hips. Similar findings have been reported in studies conducted across different countries, emphasizing the widespread prevalence of MSDs among nursing students [11 - 14, 27 - 30]. In the present study, musculoskeletal pain in the neck, shoulder, lower back, and hip regions during the past 12 months was found to interfere with participants’ work activities. Moreover, the association between neck, shoulder, upper back, and wrist pain and increased medical consultation may reflect the physical and postural demands encountered during nursing education and clinical training. Similarly, the relationship between pain in the shoulder, lower back, hip, and ankle regions and medication use suggests that musculoskeletal symptoms may be associated with limitations in daily functioning and pain management behaviors. In addition, the association of lower back and hip pain with sick leave may indicate the greater functional burden of pain in weight-bearing body regions.

The high prevalence of musculoskeletal pain among nursing students observed in this study reflects a critical occupational health issue that can emerge even before professional practice begins. The predominance of lower back, upper back, and hip pain suggests that both academic and clinical training environments may expose students to biomechanical strain. These pain-related outcomes, including increased medical consultation, medication use, and sick leave, indicate not only individual discomfort but also potential implications for workforce readiness and future patient care quality. Addressing these issues through early ergonomic interventions, regular physical activity programs, and institutional support strategies may contribute to reducing pain-related limitations and supporting students’ functional capacity, academic performance, and long-term career sustainability. However, the relatively high prevalence of PMS and musculoskeletal pain observed in this study should be interpreted cautiously. Several population-specific, cultural, and methodological factors may have contributed to these findings. Nursing students are exposed to substantial academic stress, clinical workload, prolonged standing, and physically demanding training environments, which may increase both symptom perception and reporting. In addition, cultural attitudes toward menstrual symptoms and pain perception may influence self-reporting behaviors as observed in similar studies conducted among Turkish nursing students [31, 32]. The use of self-reported questionnaires and sensitive screening instruments may also have increased symptom detection and contributed to higher prevalence estimates. Furthermore, the inclusion of participants from a single institution may limit the representativeness of the sample and may have influenced the observed prevalence rates.

In this study, a significant association was observed between the severity of PMS and the occurrence of musculoskeletal pain. Participants reporting pain exhibited higher PMS scores compared with those without such complaints, and the regression analysis identified PMS severity as a factor significantly associated with musculoskeletal discomfort. Although the odds ratio per one-point increase appears modest, the wide range of the PMSS (44–220) suggests that larger increments provide a more meaningful interpretation. For instance, a 10-point increase in PMSS score corresponds to approximately a 2.2-fold increase in the odds of musculoskeletal pain, suggesting that higher PMS severity may be linked to greater pain perception. From a clinical and preventive perspective, the observed association between increasing PMSS scores and musculoskeletal pain suggests that nursing students with more severe premenstrual symptoms may represent a higher-risk group for pain-related functional limitations. The finding that a 10-point increase in PMSS score was associated with approximately a twofold increase in the odds of musculoskeletal pain may support the use of PMS severity assessments as part of early screening strategies in student health services. Early identification of students with severe PMS symptoms could help guide preventive interventions such as ergonomic education, exercise-based rehabilitation programs, stress management strategies, and menstrual health counseling aimed at reducing pain-related disability and improving academic and clinical functioning.

Previous research supports this relationship between hormonal fluctuations and pain sensitivity. Wijnhoven et al. [33] demonstrated that hormonal and reproductive factors are associated with chronic musculoskeletal pain in women, highlighting the systemic influence of reproductive physiology on pain mechanisms. Similarly, Smith et al. [34] reported that nurses experiencing premenstrual tension were more likely to suffer from lower and upper back MSDs, emphasizing the occupational and hormonal interplay contributing to pain. In addition, Abdul-Razzak et al. [35] found that women with musculoskeletal pain experience more pronounced premenstrual physical and psychological symptoms (including fatigue, headache, irritability, mood swings, anxiety, depression, and social withdrawal) than those without musculoskeletal pain. Lee and Park [36] further noted that although menstrual and musculoskeletal pain represent distinct phenomena, women frequently perceive them as overlapping sensations, particularly when the pain intensity is high. Taken together, these observations underscore the complex interplay between hormonal cycles, psychological distress, and musculoskeletal pain perception in women. The cyclical nature of hormonal fluctuations may be associated with increased pain sensitivity and reduced coping capacity, particularly in individuals with heightened premenstrual symptoms. Recognizing these interconnections could inform more comprehensive pain management and rehabilitation strategies that consider both physiological and psychological dimensions of women’s health.

These findings may also be interpreted within the biopsychosocial model of pain, which emphasizes the interaction of biological, psychological, and social factors in pain perception. Hormonal fluctuations during the menstrual cycle may influence nociceptive processing and pain sensitivity through alterations in estrogen and progesterone levels. At the same time, psychological stress, anxiety and emotional distress, may further amplify pain perception and reduce coping capacity [37] among nursing students. Repeated exposure to pain and stress-related symptoms may also contribute to central sensitization mechanisms, characterized by increased responsiveness of the central nervous system to painful stimuli [38]. Therefore, the relationship between PMS severity and musculoskeletal pain is likely multifactorial and may involve complex neuroendocrine and psychosocial interactions rather than a solely physical or hormonal process.

Given this interconnectedness, addressing both hormonal and musculoskeletal factors in management strategies becomes essential. From a rehabilitative perspective, the main goal in musculoskeletal pain management is to restore locomotor function and reduce discomfort. However, pain relief alone may be insufficient without improvements in movement quality. For this reason, exercise-based rehabilitation approaches have been recommended for women suffering from menstrual-related musculoskeletal pain. As menstrual pain can aggravate musculoskeletal symptoms, targeted exercise interventions may help improve both menstrual and musculoskeletal pain outcomes [39]. In conclusion, the present findings indicate that PMS severity shows a significant association with musculoskeletal pain perception among women. The interaction between hormonal, psychological, and functional factors may be associated with greater pain sensitivity and limitations in daily activities. Therefore, integrating hormonal cycle–aware exercise programs and psychosocial support into rehabilitation plans could contribute to better pain management and quality of life in this population.

This study is among the few that simultaneously examined the relationship between PMS and MSDs in nursing students, a population often exposed to both physiological and occupational stressors. Despite its contributions, this study has several limitations. First, its cross-sectional design prevents establishing a causal relationship between PMS and musculoskeletal pain. Second, the reliance on self-reported data introduces the possibility of recall bias, as participants may have under- or overestimated their symptoms. Third, the study was conducted in a single institution with only female nursing students, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other student populations or health disciplines.

An important limitation of this study is that potential confounding variables such as stress levels, sleep quality, ergonomic risks, and psychological distress were not assessed. These factors may independently influence both PMS severity and musculoskeletal pain perception. For example, increased stress is known to elevate cortisol levels, and cortisol dysregulation may contribute to pain perception [40, 41]. In addition, the high academic stress and clinical workload experienced by nursing students may affect cortisol levels, potentially lowering the pain threshold and contributing to greater PMS symptom severity. Similarly, inappropriate postural positions and physical strain during clinical training may directly contribute to musculoskeletal pain as biomechanical stressors [27]. The inability to include these variables in the analysis may have influenced the observed association between PMS and musculoskeletal pain and contributed to residual confounding.

Although the logistic regression model demonstrated acceptable fit and moderate explanatory power, the absence of several potentially relevant confounding variables should be considered when interpreting the findings. Future studies should incorporate these variables to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the observed associations. Therefore, future research using longitudinal and multi-center designs is needed to confirm these results and explore causal mechanisms.

Conclusion

This study revealed a significant association between premenstrual syndrome severity and musculoskeletal pain among nursing students. Participants experiencing musculoskeletal pain had markedly higher PMS scores across all symptom domains, suggesting that greater PMS severity may be linked to higher levels of musculoskeletal discomfort. These findings highlight a critical but often overlooked interaction between reproductive and musculoskeletal health in young women preparing for physically demanding healthcare professions. Future longitudinal and multi-center studies are warranted to clarify causal mechanisms and to develop evidence-based strategies aimed at reducing the co-occurrence of PMS symptoms and musculoskeletal pain. Understanding this interaction may help inform strategies to improve the physical well-being, clinical performance, and long-term occupational health of future nurses.
