Modified Nuss procedure in patients with recurrent pectus excavatum following the Ravitch procedure with a retained strut: report of two cases
Authors: Yu-Hsu Liu, Nay Htut, Chan-Yang Hsu, Yeung-Leung Cheng

Background

Pectus excavatum (PE) is a common congenital chest wall deformity that is often treated using surgical techniques such as the Ravitch or Nuss procedure [1]. The traditional Ravitch procedure involves the detachment of the depressed sternum from its surrounding tissue and its repositioning by wedge resection from the anterior and posterior surfaces of the sternum. The technique involves elevating the sternum by overlapping the cartilage of the second rib on either side using an angled incision. A modified version of the Ravitch procedure incorporates a stainless-steel retrosternal bar to maintain sternal elevation which is typically left in place for approximately 6 months before removal [2, 3].

In contrast, the Nuss procedure involves the placement of a curved stainless-steel bar beneath the sternum to elevate the depressed chest wall without requiring resection of the costal cartilage [4]. The bar usually remains in place for ≥ 2 years or depending on the patient’s progress. Although both techniques are effective, recurrence has been reported in 2–37% of patients. Large revision series implicate mechanical failure or prolonged retention of the supporting strut as a major risk factor for surgical complications [5]. The modified Nuss procedure is a minimally invasive surgery that can be highly effective in treating recurrent PE, especially after prior open surgery such as the Ravitch procedure [6]. This corrective surgery also involves the insertion of one or more curved metal bars under the sternum to push them outward and correct the deformity.

Here, we present two cases of recurrent PE in patients who retained their original stainless-steel bars for > 15 years, an unusually prolonged duration, after a modified Ravitch procedure. Both patients were treated successfully using the Nuss procedure.

Case presentations

Case 1

A 26-year-old woman presented at our thoracic surgery outpatient department with progressive shortness of breath and palpitations. Her medical history included severe PE, for which she had undergone a modified Ravitch procedure approximately 20 years prior. During the initial procedure, a stainless-steel strut was placed under her sternum. Approximately 1 year before this visit, her symptoms had worsened, necessitating further evaluation and intensive treatment. Physical examination revealed a severe anterior chest wall depression with a tall and slender body habitus. She had an old surgical scar on the anterior chest wall (Fig. 1E). Chest radiographs showed severe chest wall deformities and a stainless-steel strut under the lower sternum (Fig. 1A and B). Non-contrast chest computed tomography revealed a Haller index of 11.87 (Fig. 1C) and marked Pectus excavatum with external compression of the right atrium, right ventricle, and displacement of the heart to the left chest. Echocardiography showed preserved biventricular systolic function and prolapse of the mitral and tricuspid valves with moderate mitral valve regurgitation. Spirometry demonstrated a mild restrictive defect with air-trapping. Laboratory values were within normal limits; however, electrocardiography revealed abnormalities, suggesting possible left atrial enlargement, septal infarction, and T-wave abnormalities.

Considering her clinical presentation, she was promptly scheduled to undergo surgical correction using a multidisciplinary approach. A pre-operative consultation was obtained from cardiac surgeon Dr Hsu, and a fully primed extracorporeal circulation was prepared and kept on standby for peri-operative use. The surgical procedure was primarily based on the technique which described in Cheng et al. [7]. In brief, under general anesthesia with double-lumen intubation, bilateral more than 5-cm skin incisions were made at the 5th intercostal space along the midaxillary lines, and bleeding was controlled using electrocautery. Intraoperatively, under the thoracoscopic vision, a stainless-steel strut was identified between the sternum and heart, adhering to the lung and mediastinum (Fig. 1F). The mediastinal tunnel was carefully dissected using an introducer and the chest tube was threaded through the tunnel. A 12-inch stainless steel bar was contoured to a bridge shape, positioned using the chest tube, passed through the mediastinal tunnel, and rotated using flippers. The bars were inserted horizontally across the 4th and 5th intercostal spaces and anchored bilaterally using wire sutures [7]. After the sternum had been elevated, the old surgical scar was opened for the safe removal of the adhered strut (Fig. 1G and H); no chondrotomy or sternotomy was performed. The patient’s postoperative course was uneventful, and she was discharged on day 10. At the 10-month follow-up visit, echocardiography showed a normal cardiac silhouette, a markedly improved left-ventricular ejection fraction of 83.4%, and persistent mitral- and tricuspid-valve prolapse. Clinically, she reported better physical function and was highly satisfied with the anatomic outcome. (Fig. 1D).

Case 2

A 29-year-old man was admitted to our hospital with complaints of discomfort and dyspnea that had persisted for the preceding 2 months and worsened with activities such as exercise and stair climbing. He had PE and had undergone a modified Ravitch procedure 15 years prior to admission. Physical examination revealed a persistent depression extending from the upper to the lower anterior chest wall and a transverse surgical scar on the anterior chest wall (Fig. 2E). Chest radiography showed a depressed anterior chest wall and a stainless-steel strut in the anterior mediastinum (Fig. 2A and B). Chest computed tomography revealed a Haller index of 4.0 (Fig. 2C). Baseline spirometry showed a reduced vital capacity consistent with a restrictive pattern, and echocardiography documented mitral-valve prolapse with mild tricuspid regurgitation and an LVEF of 62%. The modified Nuss procedure using a multidisciplinary approach was planned after thorough counseling. Under general anesthesia using double-lumen intubation, 3–4 cm skin incisions were made at the 5th intercostal space along the mid-axillary line, with bleeding controlled via electrocautery; thoracoscopy was introduced (Fig. 2F). A 10-cm incision was made along the previous surgical scar on the anterior chest wall without chondrotomy or sternotomy. Using video-assisted thoracoscopic monitoring, the original orthopedic strut was located, carefully dissected, and removed. After measurement, two 14-inch bars were placed horizontally across the 5th and 6th intercostal spaces, with a stabilizer positioned at the lower-left edge of the lower bar after removal of the old strut (Fig. 2G and H). The procedure was completed without complications. Postoperatively, the patient received analgesia via epidural patient-controlled analgesia and intravenous pain management. Medications were prescribed to prevent bleeding, and the patient began mobilization on postoperative day three. His hospital stay was 9 days. Early postoperative assessments and the 20-month follow-up visit both confirmed a progressive improvement in his symptoms (Fig. 2D). As in Case 1, definitive postoperative pulmonary function testing will be carried out after bar removal. A follow-up echocardiogram has not yet been performed because the patient has been unable to return for imaging; nevertheless, he reports a marked improvement in exercise tolerance and overall well-being.

Discussion

In contrast to the modified Ravitch procedure, the Nuss procedure is a minimally invasive technique introduced by Dr. Nuss in 1998 as an alternative to the modified Ravitch procedure for the treatment of PE, the most common chest wall malformation [1]. For rigid or mixed excavatum–carinatum deformities, several centers now adopt a “sandwich” strategy in which an external compression bar is combined with one or two internal Nuss bars; the original series of 58 patients reported by Park and Kim achieved significant morphologic correction with a < 5% three-year recurrence rate [8]. A recent comprehensive review likewise endorses the sandwich construct as a full-remodeling option for complex or very rigid chests [9], but because our two patients exhibited pure recurrent depression without anterior protrusion, the conventional multi-bar minimally invasive technique was deemed sufficient. Correction of PE not only enhances the cosmetic appearance of patients but also expands the thoracic cavity, which improves cardiac and pulmonary function by relieving anterior compression of the heart by the sternum [4]. For open repair, the risk of recurrence can stem from factors such as incomplete initial repair, undergoing the procedure at a young age, excessive dissection, insufficient support structures, inadequate chest wall healing and premature removal. Although premature strut removal can precipitate early relapse, indefinite retention is not without risk. Case reports describe late strut fracture with cardiac penetration [10] and intrapericardial migration more than 30 years after the index Ravitch repair [11] while biomechanical analyses recommend elective removal within 6–12 months to avoid hardware fatigue and secondary deformity. For patients who undergo primary Nuss repair, issues related to support bars, such as their placement, number, migration, and premature removal, can contribute to failure. Connective tissue disorders such as Marfan syndrome can also complicate this situation and increase the risk of recurrence for both types of PE repair [6]. The approach to the preoperative evaluation for any patient with recurrent PE is similar to that for a patient with primary, uncorrected PE, but with more emphasis on clinical symptoms, including chest pain, respiratory symptoms, palpitations, or exercise intolerance, as well as the patient’s age and psychosocial purpose [12].

Correction of recurrent PE is significantly more challenging and complex than primary repair. In the initial open procedure, irregular fusions and ossifications of the regenerated costal cartilage often create adhesions to the pericardium and lungs, accompanied by dense intrathoracic adhesions between the sternum and mediastinal structures, as evidenced in these patients. Although the Ravitch approach directly reshapes the chest wall through cartilage resection, the presence of a long-retained bar can contribute to similar complications. There is an ongoing debate regarding the optimal surgical approach for patients who have experienced recurrent PE after initial repair [13]. Hebra et al. [14] reported that the Nuss procedure could achieve complete correction of chest deformities only in adult patients with symmetrical pectus defects. Patients with severe asymmetrical deformities and significant calcifications in the regrown costal cartilage should initially be treated using open surgical correction. Liang et al. [13] describe a modified Nuss procedure involving a subxiphoid incision that enables the surgeon to safely dissect the pericardium from the substernal area and allows the bar to pass safely through the mediastinum with the surgeon’s guidance. Cheng et al. [7] further demonstrated the value of this modified, bilateral-thoracoscopy–assisted approach in 96 adults, including six with recurrence after a Ravitch repair, reporting a median operative time of 80 min, minimal blood loss, no cardiopulmonary injuries, and a 91% patient-satisfaction rate. Collectively, these studies support the modified Nuss procedure, which combines a subxiphoid incision and bilateral thoracoscopy as a promising alternative for recurrent PE after open repair. However, Kevin et al. [15] reported that patients with malunion, floating sternum, or significant ossification and deformity required a hybrid procedure using thoracoscopic support bars and sternal elevation, multiple open osteotomies, and chest wall fixation.

In general, the strut inserted during a Ravitch procedure is intended to be removed approximately 6 months after surgery. In our cases, however, postoperative chest-wall depression persisted, and after consultation with their original surgeon, they elected to leave the strut in place for a longer period. Despite this, only limited improvement was observed over time. In these two cases, we reviewed our institution’s experience with recurrent PE repair and evaluated the effectiveness of the modified Nuss procedure. We decided that the two patients should undergo modified Nuss surgery. Both patients had recurrent PE with worsening clinical symptoms. In patient 1, the prolonged bar retention may have contributed to mitral valve prolapse and moderate mitral regurgitation, underscoring the potential cardiac implications of recurrent or prolonged PE. In addition, both patients had a stainless-steel strut that was retained for >15 years after the modified Ravitch procedure; therefore, we used thoracoscopic guidance to enhance visualization, enabling precise placement of the bars and careful removal of the retained strut. Besides, scoliosis was noted in case 2. Treatment sequencing for combined PE and scoliosis hinges on deformity severity. PE dominance (high Haller index) with a stable Cobb angle < 45° generally warrants chest-wall repair first; severe or rapidly progressing curves (≥ 45–50° or >5–10°/yr) call for spine-first fusion. Reports of post-Nuss curve acceleration and small series of same-day dual repairs highlight the need for tailored planning and close follow-up [16–19]. Our Case 2 (Cobb 30°, Haller 4.0) therefore, underwent modified Nuss procedure after orthopedic consultation, with radiographic follow-up and a contingency for later fusion should the curve exceed 45° or functional limits emerge [20]. Both our patients underwent a successful modified Nuss procedure, emphasizing its potential as a suitable option in the management of recurrent PE. Postoperative management is important in these cases, with patients undergoing comprehensive pain management, early mobilization, and regular follow-up assessments. Both patients’ cardiac and pulmonary functions improved, as did their psychosocial well-being, after the follow-up. These findings suggest that the modified Nuss procedure can effectively address functional and cosmetic issues in patients with recurrent deformities after the Ravitch procedure, even when the retrosternal bar has been retained for an extended period.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our two cases demonstrate that the modified Nuss procedure is a safe and effective salvage option for recurrent pectus excavatum following an index Ravitch repair, even in the presence of long-retained stainless-steel struts. The approach offered a short operative time and favorable functional and cosmetic outcomes. These findings suggest that timely strut removal may help reduce mediastinal adhesions and minimize the complexity of future reoperations. Furthermore, current evidence supports deferring definitive repair until adolescence to balance surgical efficacy with growth-related considerations [21–24]. Further prospective studies are needed to determine optimal timing for strut/bar explantation and to guide management of recurrence.
