Bisphenol exposure in preterm neonates: a cohort study with measurements at admission and discharge in a neonatal intensive care unit in Ankara, Türkiye
Authors: Melda Celik, Irem Iyigun, Siddika Songül Yalcin, Murat Cagan, Anil Yirun, Deniz Arca Cakir, Hulya Tezel Yalcin, Ferid Aliyev, Hasan Tolga Celik, Ozgur Deren, Pinar Erkekoglu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study assessed bisphenol exposure in preterm infants (born before 35 weeks of gestation) hospitalized in a neonatal intensive care unit (NICU), along with associated prenatal exposure. Fifty-eight infants and 48 mothers were included. Bisphenol A (BPA) and bisphenol F (BPF) levels were measured in maternal urine, cord blood, and infant urine samples using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). There was a significant negative association between maternal urine BPF (MuBPF) and the newborn’s birth weight percentile. A significant positive correlation was found between the BPA levels of the infant’s urine taken on the first day (NuBPA1) and the second sample taken on the last day (NuBPA2) in the NICU. There was a significant negative association between tertile levels of cord BPA (cBPA) and stretched penile length (SPL). NuBPF1 levels in the first-born babies were significantly higher compared to the later-borns. Detectable NuBPF1 levels and decreased NuBPF2 compared to NuBPF1 levels were observed more frequently in males. Cord BPA (cBPA) and BPF (cBPF) levels did not significantly correlate with maternal or infant urinary levels. No association was found between NICU procedures and BPA or BPF exposure levels. The absence of significant associations between neonatal urine BPA levels and both maternal urine and cord blood BPA, as well as NICU procedures, suggests that newborns may be exposed to these chemicals through other, unpredictable sources that were not captured in this study. The study highlights the need to minimize BPA and BPF exposure, especially during pregnancy and NICU stays, to protect fetal health and prevent future issues.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-025-06265-5.Keywords: Bisphenol A, Bisphenol F, Cord, Exposure, Newborn, Preterm, Urine

Introduction

Bisphenol A (BPA) is a well-known endocrine disruptor, with over 4 million tons of production annually. The effects of BPA have been extensively documented, including its effects on the endocrine system, developmental cancers, reproductive disorders, neurological issues, cardiovascular diseases, and metabolic disorders such as obesity and diabetes [1, 2]. Fetuses, newborns, and infants are particularly vulnerable to toxic substances like BPA due to their rapidly growing and developing organs, immature detoxification and excretion systems, and higher exposure per kilogram of body weight [1]. Fetuses, newborns, and infants exhibit unique vulnerabilities to environmental toxicants. The growth rate in utero and during the first few months of life following birth is faster than during the rest of life [3]. Tissues with rapidly dividing cells, such as blood, epithelium, and lungs, may be especially vulnerable to carcinogens. The absorption of specific nutrients from the intestines of a developing child is higher than that of adults, enabling them to respond to their nutritional needs. However, this can also lead to increased absorption of some toxic substances (such as increased lead absorption competing with increased calcium absorption) [4]. The fetus may be exposed through the placenta to certain toxic substances that the mother has been exposed to and/or accumulated in her body and which can easily pass through the placenta [5]. During breastfeeding, fat-soluble toxic substances (e.g. dioxins, polychlorinated biphenyls or chlorinated pesticides) that have previously accumulated in the mother’s body may pass into the milk and the breastfed baby may be exposed to them [6]. The newborn’s skin surface area relative to body weight is approximately three times greater than that of adults, increasing dermal absorption of environmental chemicals [7]. Differences in gastrointestinal absorption and higher water intake per kilogram further enhance exposure to water-soluble toxicants. Additionally, infants have higher respiratory rates than adults, resulting in greater inhaled doses of air pollutants per unit of body weight [3, 6].

Studies have shown that prenatal exposure to BPA can impair fetal growth [8], cause low birth weight [9], lead to prematurity [10], and thyroid hormone disorders [11].

Considering the potential health hazards of BPA, an alternative bisphenol analog, bisphenol F (BPF), has been introduced into the plastics industry [12, 13]. BPF is structurally similar to BPA and is used in various products, from food packaging to industrial coatings [14]. BPF exposure has been detected in food, indoor dust, and personal care products. BPF has been shown to accumulate in the fetal compartment, potentially leading to similar adverse effects as BPA, including endocrine and developmental disorders [15–17]. Research indicates that BPF may have estrogenic and antiandrogenic effects comparable to or stronger than BPA [14, 18].

Bisphenols can be found in various consumer products and medical devices, and their migration into food and contact surfaces can result in significant exposure [5]. Leaching bisphenols from medical devices and polymerized plastics by diffusion is considered an essential source of human exposure [19, 20]. Premature infants in neonatal intensive care units (NICUs) are particularly vulnerable to bisphenol exposure due to their developmental and physiological immaturity, which brings the need for the use of more medical equipment that may contain bisphenol [21]. Exposure of hospitalized neonates in NICUs to BPA through medical equipment can occur via parenteral, oral, and inhalation routes [1]. Given their immature detoxification systems, these infants may retain higher levels of bisphenol in their biological fluids, which may increase the risk of adverse effects.

To our knowledge, while there are a few studies on BPA exposure in premature NICU infants, there is a lack of research examining the BPA and bisphenol analogs like BPF simultaneously. In this study, we aimed to investigate BPA and BPF exposure levels in premature infants under 35 weeks of age hospitalized in the NICU of a tertiary university hospital. We also examined maternal exposures and factors associated with these levels. By analyzing BPA and BPF concentrations in three biomatrixes, namely in maternal urine, cord blood, and neonatal urine samples, this study seeks to provide a comprehensive understanding of prenatal bisphenol exposure routes, thereby informing future guidelines and interventions to minimize risks in vulnerable populations.

Materials and methods

Study population

Preterm newborns who were born before 35 weeks of gestation by normal vaginal delivery or cesarean section in our hospital between August 1, 2021, and January 1, 2022, and who were hospitalized in the NICU of Hacettepe University Faculty of Medicine Children’s Hospital after birth and their mothers older than 18 years of age, were included in the study. Newborns with anencephaly and critical congenital heart defects that significantly reduce life expectancy were identified as exclusion criteria.

An informed consent form was obtained from the mothers included in the study before delivery. An investigator (MC) conducted a face-to-face interview with the mother to complete a study file, gathering information on demographic characteristics and certain lifestyle habits that could be related to potential BPA and BPF exposure during pregnancy. The medical history of the mother was obtained from hospital records.

Study protocol

At birth, researchers took 3 ml of cord blood samples from the umbilical cords of babies born in the delivery room. These samples were directly collected in the de-plasticized and heparinized glass test tubes, and the tube openings were covered with clean aluminum foil to avoid contact with plastic materials. Samples were immediately centrifuged at 800 × g for 15 min to obtain plasma.

A 3 ml urine sample was collected from the mother and her infant hospitalized in NICU within the first 24 h after birth. The urine sample from the newborn was collected by squeezing sterile cotton gauze swabs placed in the diaper into glass tubes when wet with urine. The urine sample from the mother was collected in a de-plasticized glass beaker. On the day the newborns were transferred from the NICU to the general care unit, a second 3 mL urine sample was collected from the newborns.

All samples were placed in de-plasticized glass tubes and vials. All glass tubes and vials were kept at 400 °C for four hours to remove plasticizers. All glassware was washed with n-hexane and tetrahydrofuran (1:1, v/v) for four hours and then dried in an incubator after a general cleaning procedure to remove any residues of plastic material. All serum and urine samples were aliquoted and kept at −80 °C until analysis.

The newborn’s medical history was obtained from hospital records. Within 24 h after birth, general physical examinations of all newborns were performed by the same pediatrician (II) to reduce measurement errors. In addition, anthropometric measurements (birth weight, head circumference), anogenital distance (AGD), and stretched penile length (SPL) measurements were performed for all newborns. AGD was measured following the methodology described by Salazar-Martinez et al. [22]. SPL was measured with the aid of a rigid plastic ruler from the tip of the glans to the pubopenile skin junction while applying tension to achieve maximal stretching of the penis [23].

During birth and the monitoring period in the NICU, all medical devices, procedures, materials, treatments [e.g., umbilical catheters, central catheters, total parenteral nutrition (TPN), intravenous fluids, nasogastric (NG)/orogastric (OG) catheters, urinary catheters, intubation tubes, tracheostomy cannulas, nasal Continuous Positive Airway Pressure (nCPAP), hood, blood product transfusions], and feeding methods of the infants [breastfeeding, formula, OG catheter] that could be potential sources of BPA and BPF exposure were recorded in the study file.

*Parenteral Exposure of Mother (PEM): Hospitalization in the last week of pregnancy and/or 48 h, interventional procedure in the previous 48 h.

*Dermal Exposure of Mother (DEM): This included activities such as shopping, handling cash register receipts, using plastic cleaning gloves, handling plastic tablecloths, operating printers, fax machines, photocopiers, applying sunscreen, and using various cleaning products like liquid soap, bar soap, and cleaning gel. It also involved using hair and body care products such as shower gel, shampoo, and conditioner, as well as applying makeup and using other body care and beauty products. Additionally, visiting beauty salons and using household cleaning agents like detergents and bleach within the last 48 hours were also considered.

Each exposure item was expressed as the total number of events in the above groups. BPA and BPF exposure sources in newborns were parenteral, oral, respiratory, and dermal exposure. This grouping was made as follows:

*Parenteral Exposure of Newborn (PEN): TPN, IV treatments such as IV antifungals, fluids, antibiotics, umbilical, central or Peripherally Inserted Central Catheter (PICC)-line, peripheral vascular access, blood transfusion, and surgery.

*Parenteral Exposure of Newborn (PEN): TPN, IV treatments such as IV antifungals, fluids, antibiotics, umbilical, central or Peripherally Inserted Central Catheter (PICC)-line, peripheral vascular access, blood transfusion, and surgery.

*Oral Exposure of Newborn (OEN): breast milk, formula, bottle, pacifier, OG or NG catheter, gastrostomy tube;

*Oral Exposure of Newborn (OEN): breast milk, formula, bottle, pacifier, OG or NG catheter, gastrostomy tube;

*Respiratory Exposure of Newborn (REN): Positive-pressure ventilation (PPV) or surfactant administration at birth, nCPAP, endotracheal intubation;

*Respiratory Exposure of Newborn (REN): Positive-pressure ventilation (PPV) or surfactant administration at birth, nCPAP, endotracheal intubation;

*Dermal Exposure of Newborn (DEN): use of diaper rash cream, baby oil, or moisturizing cream. Each exposure was expressed as the total number of agents in the above groups.

*Dermal Exposure of Newborn (DEN): use of diaper rash cream, baby oil, or moisturizing cream. Each exposure was expressed as the total number of agents in the above groups.

The mother’s weight gain during pregnancy (kg) was calculated and classified into three groups: “inadequate, adequate, and excessive,” according to the NIH (National Institute of Health) guidelines [24].

Analysis of blood and urine samples

In the study, total BPA and BPF levels were measured in maternal urine (MuBPA and MuBPF, ng/ml), umbilical cord blood (cBPA and cBPF, ng/ml), first (NuBPA1 and NuBPF1, ng/ml) and second urine samples (NuBPA2 and NuBPF2, ng/ml) of newborns using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC).

Chemicals and chromatography equipment

Glucuronidase/aryl sulfatase enzyme (Helix pomatia) was obtained from Roche (Mannheim, Germany). BPA, Bisphenol B (BPB), and BPF were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO). HPLC equipment was purchased from Agilent (Santa Clara, CA).

Bisphenol A and bisphenol F measurements in cord blood and urine samples

The modified extraction and HPLC method of Yang et al. [25] was used to detect plasma and urinary BPA and BPF levels. In the extraction step, plasma and urine samples (500 µl) were spiked with 50 µl of 100 ng/ml BPA or 100 ng/ml BPF (10 ng/ml final concentration) for total BPA or BPF analysis. Afterward, ammonium acetate Buffer (30 µl, 2.0 M, pH 5.0) and glucuronidase/aryl sulfatase (10 µl, from Helix pomatia) were added. The mixture was vortexed and incubated in a 37 °C water bath for three hours. Subsequently, the extraction was performed using 5 ml of n-hexane: diethyl ether (70:30), which included 100 ng/ml bisphenol B as an internal standard. Later, the mixture was then centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 5 min. The supernatants (3 ml) were transferred to glass tubes and evaporated under a nitrogen stream. The residues were stored at −20 °C until analysis. On the day of analysis, the residues were dissolved in 300 µl of 60% acetonitrile. Standards and samples were injected into HPLC (Agilent 1200 Series, Vienna, Austria) as 100 µl. A Spherisorb C18 ODS2 column (particle size five µm, 25 cm; 4.6 mm i.d.) was used in the experiments. The column temperature was set at 25 °C. The mobile phase was acetonitrile and tetrahydrofuran (2.5% v/v, in water) with a 0.4 ml/min flow rate. The analysis time was 40 min. Gradient elution was used throughout the analysis period (60:40 to 5:95). Fluorescence was measured at λexcitation = 230 nm and λemission = 315 nm. The limits of detection (LOD) for BPA and BPF were 3.9 ng/mL and 3.5 ng/ml, while the limits of quantification (LOQs) were determined as 11.84 ng/mL and 10.76 ng/mL for BPA and BPF, respectively. For BPA, the retention time for plasma samples was 18.4–18.5 min, while the retention times for urine and milk samples were 18.5–18.6 min and 18.6–18.7 min, respectively. For BPF, the retention time for plasma samples was 10.4–10.5 min while the retention times for urine and milk samples were 10.9–11 min and 11.1–11.2 min, respectively. The standards used for BPA and BPF analysis were 0, 1,25, 2,5, 5, 10, 25, 50, and 100 ng/ml.

We used matrix spiking because some samples had lower BPA and BPF levels than LOD. Matrix Spiking is a technique used to evaluate an analytical procedure’s performance when testing a specific sample (matrix) type. We ensured that the spike did not increase the sample volume by more than 5%. Matrix spiking simplified the calculations and avoided introducing unknown effects from dilution of the matrix. For matrix spiking, we used BPA and BPF at known concentrations as indicated above, and used two proportions of a sample: matrix spike portion and test sample portion. In the “matrix spike” portion, we added a known amount of standard to increase the concentration by a known amount. When we tested the sample and the matrix spike, the matrix spike resulted higher by that known amount added. The spiking level was chosen according to the HPLC method and was validated for at least ten samples for each bisphenol derivative. Although matrix spiking was used, some samples did not contain BPA and/or BPF, and the levels of bisphenol derivatives found after analyses were not higher than the matrix spike levels. These samples were judged as non-detectable (nd). The recovery rate (%R) was calculated by subtracting the level of the matrix spike from the obtained result, dividing it by the known spike added concentration, and multiplying the obtained result by 100. Recovery studies were performed by comparing blank plasma and urine samples with plasma and urine spiked samples with 10 ng/ml of BPA and 10 ng/ml of BPF as a final concentration. Urinary BPA recovery was 87.39 ± 1.42%, and plasma BPA recovery was 90.9 ± 1.57% over ten analyses. Urinary BPF recovery was 85.27 ± 2.18%, while plasma BPF recovery was 91.56 ± 2.27%. While calculating within-day and between-run precision levels, averages of 10 different analyses were taken. Urinary BPA showed a within-day precision of 3.26 ± 1.06% CV and a between-run precision of 6.85 ± 2.35% CV. For plasma BPA, within-day precision was 4.76 ± 1.98% CV, and between-run precision was 9.78 ± 3.17% CV. Urinary BPF had a within-day precision of 4.41 ± 2.01% CV and a between-run precision of 7.27 ± 1.82% CV. Plasma BPF demonstrated a within-day precision of 3.28 ± 0.99% CV, with between-run precision at 9.45 ± 2.28% CV. In order to overcome blank and matrix effects in fluorescence detection, reagent blank was chosen as deionized water; laboratory fortified blank was a blank urine or plasma sample without any analyte and matrix spike analyte was LFB which was spiked with known amount of the analytes (i.e. BPA or BPF).

Urinary creatinine levels

The urinary creatinine levels were also measured to normalize the results for urinary BPA levels. Urinary creatinine levels were determined by modifying a previous method with HPLC [26]. Briefly, creatinine standard solutions were prepared. The urine samples were diluted with deionized water at a 1/10 ratio. Potassium dihydrogen phosphate buffer (15 mM) containing 2.5% methanol (pH 7) was used as the mobile phase. The analyses were made in the C18 column with a UV detector at 235 nm. The creatinine levels of the samples were calculated as ng/mL. The results were multiplied by ten since the samples had been previously diluted. An adjustment to correct for variation in urinary volume using creatinine excretion was not feasible in the study, as all measured creatinine concentrations fell outside the validated range (1–26 mg/dL). This limitation is expected, given the functional immaturity of preterm neonates’ kidneys [27]. In premature infants, creatinine clearance shows considerable variability, and renal function begins immaturely, taking significant time to reach total capacity. Therefore, both creatinine and specific gravity could be considered imperfect measures of urine dilution in neonates [28]. Consequently, we reported bisphenol levels in ng/ml without creatinine adjustment. Similar approaches for preterm infants have been reported in the literature [19, 21, 29].

Statistical analysis

We examined the association between maternal exposures and NuBPA1 and NuBPF1, cBPA and cBPF, and MuBPA and MuBPF levels, the association between newborn exposures and NuBPA2 and NuBPF2, and the change in NuBPA and NuBPF during NICU hospitalization by using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 23.0 (Armonk, NY: IBM Corp).

The typical distribution characteristics, skewness, and kurtosis were analyzed with the Shapiro-Wilk test, and it was observed that the BPA and BPF values were right-skewed. Therefore, BPA and BPF values were divided into tertile groups: cBPA tertile values (1st tertile: <25 ng/ml, 2nd tertile: 25–89 ng/ml, 3rd tertile: ≥90 ng/ml), cBPF tertile values (1st tertile: <2.8 ng/ml, 2nd tertile: 2.8–4.9 ng/ml, 3rd tertile: ≥5.0 ng/ml), MuBPA tertile values (1st tertile: <10 ng/ml, 2nd tertile: 10–40 ng/ml, 3rd tertile: >40 ng/ml), MuBPF tertile values (1st tertile: <1 ng/ml, 2nd tertile: 1.0−1.5 ng/ml, 3rd tertile: >1.5 ng/ml), and NuBPA tertile values (1st tertile: <5 ng/ml, 2nd tertile: 5–50 ng/ml, 3rd tertile: >50 ng/ml).

Due to limited number of cases, differences in mother-infant characteristics for the tertile bisphenol groups were compared with the Kruskal-Wallis test. When p < 0.05 was found in the comparison, pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correction were performed to identify the groups causing the difference.

Since most (about half) of the NuBPF1 and NuBPF2 levels were not detectable, two subgroups were created based on whether the levels were detectable. Differences between groups were analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Changes in infant urine bisphenol levels over time were analyzed with the Wilcoxon test.

To evaluate the relationships between BPA concentrations in different biological matrices, Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients (rho) were calculated due to non-normal distribution of some variables. Maternal urine, cord blood, and infant urine measurements were included. Two-tailed tests were used.

To compare paired BPA concentrations measured at different biological matrices and at different times, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was applied, as the data were not normally distributed.

Normality of birth weight percentiles and penis length was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For birth weight percentiles, the mean, 37.1, exceeded the median, 33.5, indicating slight positive skewness. Skewness (0.43 ± 0.31) and kurtosis (−0.99 ± 0.62) values were within the ± 1.5 range, suggesting approximate normality, although the Shapiro-Wilk test was significant (p = 0.001). Homogeneity of variances was confirmed by Levene’s test (F = 0.516, p = 0.600). Descriptive statistics indicated a mean SPL of 2.37 cm (95% CI: 2.26–2.49), median 2.40 cm, standard deviation 0.31, range 1.7–3.0 cm, slight negative skewness (−0.398) and near-zero kurtosis (−0.029). For SPL, both the Shapiro-Wilk (W = 0.970, p = 0.545) and Kolmogorov-Smirnov (D = 0.110, p = 0.200) tests indicated approximate normality. Skewness (−0.398 ± 0.427) and kurtosis (−0.029 ± 0.833) values were within acceptable limits, and Levene’s test confirmed homogeneity of variances (F = 0.034, p = 0.856). Therefore, analyses of covariance (ANCOVA) were conducted to examine the effects of pollutant tertile groups on [1] birth weight percentiles, while controlling for gestational age, maternal weight gain, maternal pre-pregnancy BMI, and singleton pregnancy, and [2] SPL, while controlling for gestational age and singleton birth. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons were subsequently performed, using Bonferroni adjustment for birth weight and the Least Significant Difference (LSD) method for SPL. Potential confounders were selected based on biological plausibility and evidence from previous studies [30–35]. Estimated marginal means with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported.

Categorical data were analyzed using the Chi-square test. In three-group comparisons, adjusted residuals were calculated when p < 0.05 was found.

Results

Samples obtained from participants

Three maternal urine samples collected within the first 24 h following delivery from 48 mothers who agreed to participate in the study could not be analyzed due to an insufficient amount. Forty-five maternal samples were analyzed for BPA and BPF (Fig. 1).

Flowchart of the maternal and neonatal sample analysis

Urine samples could not be obtained from 7 out of 58 preterm newborns within the first 24 h. Among these, nine were twins, one was a triplet, and 37 were singletons, all immediately admitted to the NICU. Therefore, 51 urine samples were included in the BPF 1 analysis, with one sample excluded due to insufficient amount for BPA 1 analysis. The second urine sample could not be obtained from 12 newborns, and one of the samples from 46 was not included in the analysis due to insufficient amount. Consequently, a total of 45 second neonatal urine samples were analyzed. Eight cord blood samples were not included in the study because they were hemolyzed after collection. While the remaining 50 cord blood samples were included in the BPF analysis, one of them could not be analyzed for BPA due to an insufficient amount of blood obtained (Fig. 1).

General characteristics of newborns and exposures in NICU

Fifty-eight preterm neonates born before 35 weeks and 48 mothers were included in the study. The mean (± SD) gestational duration was 31.9 (± 2.2) weeks, birth weight was 1679.3 (± 548.1) g, and head circumference was 29.7 (± 2.7) cm. Thirty (51.7%) of the newborns were male.

The mean (± SD) anogenital distance measured immediately after birth was 1.75 (± 0.40) cm, and SPLwas 2.37 (± 0.30) cm. Mean (± SD) APGAR values of the neonates at the 0th, 5th, and 10th minutes were 7.5 (± 1.4), 8.3 (± 1.4), and 9.1 (± 1.2) respectively.

Twenty (34.6%) babies were first-born, and 38 (65.5%) were later-born. Of all newborns, 98.3% were fed with breast-milk, 48.0% were exclusively fed with breast-milk, and 52.0% were fed mixed with breast milk and formula. Overall, 56.9% were bottle-fed. None were fed only with formula.

The mean (± SD) duration of NICU hospitalization was 25.0 (± 29.5) days, with a range of 4 to 146 days. Complete blood count and biochemistry tests were also performed during NICU hospitalization. The mean duration of breast milk intake was 11.5 (± 12.3) days, formula intake was 4.1 (± 7.4) days, and bottle use was 4.8 (± 8.0) days (Table 1).

All but one of the infants in the NICU included in the study were monitored in incubators (98.3%); most of them were receiving TPN (67.2%) and were fed by OG catheter (79.3%). General characteristics of mothers and exposures during pregnancy

The mothers included in the study were between 19 and 39 years of age, with a mean age of 30.3 (± 5.2) years. The mean pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI) was 25.6 (± 5.7). The average number of pregnancies was 2.3 (± 1.2), and the average parity was 1.9 (± 0.9). Additionally, 56.3% (n = 27) of the mothers were housewives. All pregnancies were spontaneous, and most (91.7%) ended with cesarean delivery. Among the pregnancies, one had placenta previa, one had polyhydramnios, and four had intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR). Nine of the mothers had twins, and one had triplets. Regarding weight gain during pregnancy, 43.8% of the mothers had inadequate weight gain, 39.6% had adequate weight gain, and 16.7% had excessive weight gain (Table 1).

Association of BPA and BPF levels with mother-newborn characteristics

Neonatal urine BPA and BPF changes

We observed that urinary BPA decreased in 10 (22.2%), remained unchanged in 15 (33.3%), and increased in 20 (44.4%) of the newborns in the second urine sample. Of these, urinary BPF decreased in 11 (24.4%), remained stable in 27 (60.0%), and increased in 7 (15.6%). No association was found between interventions performed for newborns and the changes in bisphenols during the follow-up period (increased, stable, and decreased from the 1 st sample to the 2nd one). Median head circumference percentage was the lowest in cases with increased BPF (BPF2 > BPF1) among others (p = 0.054). Cases with decreased NuBPF are more likely to be male newborns than those with stable NuBPF (p = 0.041); however, in the increased NuBPF group, the male-to-female distribution is similar (Supplementary Table 3).

Multiple-Birth infants

Our study had nine sets of twins, one triplet (21 multiple-birth infants), and 37 singletons. Spearman correlation analysis was performed to evaluate the relationship between BPA levels in multiple-birth infants. No significant correlations were observed between twins for cord blood BPA (rho = −0.326, p = 0.391, n = 9), NuBPA1 (rho = −0,262, p = 0.531, n = 8), and NuBPA2 (rho = 0.679, p = 0.094, n = 7).

Discussion

In this study, we aimed to evaluate maternal prenatal BPA and BPF exposures related to medical interventions during the NICU hospitalization of premature infants. Therefore, we preferred to collect urine samples from the mother as it is not invasive, cord blood at birth to show maternal-induced infant exposure, infant urine on the first day to show maternal-induced infant exposure, and infant urine samples on the last day (second sample) before dischange from NICU to show exposure in the NICU. Our study detected BPA in approximately 91% of maternal urine samples, 94% of first neonatal urine samples, and 98% of second neonatal samples (Table 2 ). Between 2009 and 2010, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) found detectable levels of BPA in 95% of urine samples collected from 2749 Americans over six years of age. Similar levels were obtained in other biomonitoring studies in Asia, Europe, and North America [36, 37].

In our study, the median value of total BPA concentrations was 15.5 ng/ml and 24.3 ng/ml in the second newborn urine, which was lower than the median value reported by Calafat et al. in 2009 [19]. Similar to other studies [19, 21, 38, 39], average BPA concentrations in premature infants in our study were higher than in children aged 5 to 12 [39–44]. We found no association between BPA levels in infants' first and second urine samples and the number of medical applications/devices. Calafat et al. evaluated urinary concentrations of various phenols, including BPA, concerning the use of medical devices in 42 premature infants hospitalized in NICU and found that total BPA concentrations in urine samples from premature infants undergoing therapeutic interventions in the NICU ranged from 1.6 to 946 ng/ml in 2009 [19]. In another study by Calafat et al. in 2008, the researchers showed that the intensity of use of medical devices was proportional to BPA exposure and that BPA levels increased 8-fold when four or more medical devices were used [39].

In our study, BPA was more frequently detectable than BPF. There are few studies in the literature on the exposure of humans, and especially children, to bisphenol analogs such as BPF [36, 37]; according to data from the US National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), BPA, BPF, and BPS were detected in 97.5%, 55.2% and 87.8% of urine samples (1831 children aged 8–19 years) in 2013–2016, respectively [36].

BPA is widespread in the environment and can be detected in many biological fluids [45]. Our study’s median BPA values were 66.9 ng/ml in cord blood, 24.3 ng/ml in second neonatal urine, 23.9 ng/ml in maternal urine, and 15.5 ng/ml in first infant urine in descending order. Lee et al. reported that BPA was detectable in 79.5–100% of the samples of 318 maternal-term neonatal pairs; the median BPA concentrations were 4.75 ng/ml in neonatal urine, 2.86 ng/ml in maternal urine and 1.71 ng/ml cord blood [46]. Maternal serum (1.56 ng/ml), breast milk (0.74 ng/ml), and placenta (0.53 ng/g) levels were lower than urine and cord blood samples. Ikezuki et al. found higher urine BPA concentrations in amniotic fluid in the early stages of pregnancy (15-18th weeks) compared to the later stages of pregnancy (32-38th weeks) [47]. They suggested that BPA might pass through the placenta and accumulate in the fetus in early pregnancy due to the lower metabolic clearance of BPA or it may be conjugated more efficiently in the fetal liver in later pregnancy [47]. Accordingly, higher BPA levels might be expected in preterm infants. In addition, the variable BPA concentrations determined in cord blood in studies suggest that the fetus is probably exposed to different amounts of BPA through transplacental passage [48, 49]. Our results suggest that BPA, found at higher levels in cord blood than in other samples, could be transmitted from the mother to the baby via the placental route. Since metabolic clearance is low in premature infants, we observed that BPA probably accumulated in the infant and was excreted at low levels in infant urine in the early period. In the urine obtained at the end of the infant’s hospitalization in the NICU (mean 24.96 ± 29.50 days), we observed that BPA levels were higher than the first urine samples due to increased metabolic clearance and exposures in the NICU. One study by Duty et al. [21] performed on 50 preterm infants revealed that premature infants had significantly higher urinary BPA concentrations than term infants. UrineBPA concentrations were 16–32 times higher in hospitalized infants than healthy infants and children [21]. Their median infant urine BPA concentrations were changed by gestational age (4.3 ng/ml at >37 weeks, 12.4 ng/ml at 32–36 weeks, 34.2 ng/ml at 32 weeks; p < 0.001) and birth weight [9.2 ng/ml for normal weight (>2500 g), 12.7 ng/ml for low birth weight (LBW, 1500–2499 g) and 36.6 ng/ml for very low birth weight (VLBW < 1500 g); p = 0.001] [21]. In our study, all cases were premature infants born under 35 weeks. Median urine BPA levels (NuBPA1 level 15.5 ng/ml and NuBPA2 level 24.3 ng/ml) were slightly lower than those of 32–36 week-old infants in Duty et al.’s study but higher than those of term infants. In our study, neither birth weight percentile nor gestational age was associated with NuBPA levels.

We also found no association between the number of interventions and devices used in the NICU and NuBPA or NuBPF levels. Duty et al.’s study found three times higher median BPA levels in the urine of newborns who received medical treatment using four or more devices compared to infants treated with three or fewer medical devices (36.6 ng/ml and 13.9 ng/ml, respectively) [21].

MuBPA and MuBPF

In our study, birth weight percentile was significantly associated with MuBPF levels. Birth weight percentile was the highest at the lowest BPF tertile levels and decreased in the middle tertile. However, this relationship did not continue in the same way when MuBPF was in the highest level range. This showed that the BPF effect showed a non-monotonic interaction rather than a linear one. Similarly, some studies have reported an inverse relationship between BPF exposure during pregnancy and birth size [32, 36, 45, 50], while one study found no significant association [51]. Hu et al. observed that in 845 pregnant women in a longitudinal prenatal cohort in Wuhan, China, quartiles of maternal BPF urine concentrations were significantly associated with low birth weight, birth length, and ponderal index [52]. Aker et al. also found that maternal urinary BPF concentrations at 16–20 weeks were associated with an increased risk of smaller size at birth [32]. In a study by Liang et al. performed on 2023 mother-infant pairs, maternal serum BPF concentrations were negatively associated with birth weight, ponderal index, and birth weight Z scores [45]. Additionally, adverse effects of BPF on zebrafish embryos, such as inhibition of hatching and teratogenic effects, decreased birth weight, and birth length, were also shown [53].

Our study found no relationship between MuBPA concentrations and infant birth weight. Results in the literature are mixed, with some studies reporting a positive association between prenatal BPA exposure and birth weight [54], others showing a negative association [8, 9, 45, 55], and some finding no association at all [32, 51]. Liang et al. found thatmaternal serum concentrations of BPA were associated with decreased birth weight [45].

In our study, tertile levels of MuBPA and MuBPF were not significantly associated with cBPA, cBPF, or NuBPA1 and NuBPF1 levels. These findings suggest that the maternal-to-cord transfer of BPA and BPF may not be paralel with maternal urinary excretion [52]. The lack of correlation between cord blood and infant urine levels indicates that neonates may be exposed to these chemicals through sources other than transplacental transfer. Such sources could include environmental exposures during or after delivery, which we were unable to assess in this study. Although we did not detect an association between NICU procedures and neonatal BPA or BPF exposure, a significant positive correlation was observed between BPA levels in the first and last urine samples collected from newborns in the NICU. Examination of medical devices and materials in the NICU showed that all were labeled as BPA-free. Given the high potential for BPA and BPF exposure in the general population, and the fact that NICU devices are BPA-free, hospitalization and medical interventions in the NICU likely did not contribute substantially to neonatal exposure.

cBPA and cBPF

Recent studies have shown that the placenta is not a good barrier for endocrine disrupting chemicals such as BPA and its analogues , and there are still growing concerns about possible fetal effects [30,54]. In the study by Sunman et al., BPA was detected in 99% of cord blood samples, and the median cord blood BPA level was 4.77 ng/ml (min 0.06-max 27.07) [56]. We detected BPA in approximately 92% of cord blood samples, but the median value (66.9 ng/ml) was higher than in previous studies. This suggests that exposure to these EDCs is also abundant in mothers and, thus, in newborns. The high exposure to BPA may also be since our cases were immature and vulnerable preterm newborns.

In our study, the highest tertile group of cBPA (>90ng/ml) had the lowest SPL compared to other tertile groups (p = 0.025). In a study conducted on 100 male infants, it was shown for the first time that cBPA level was associated with decreased penile length in male newborns [56]. Our study results support these findings by demonstrating a negative association between cBPA levels and the SPL. As an endocrine disruptor, BPA may be responsible for impaired penile development as they may interfere with average hormonal balance. BPA may disrupt androgen secretion from Leydig cells by acting through luteinizing hormone (LH) released from the pituitary [29]. Current studies also show that BPA exposure decreases the expression of enzymes responsible for testosterone biosynthesis [57]. Sufficient androgen is required for normal development of the male external genitalia between 8 and 14 weeks of gestation. It is thought that anti-androgenic chemicals such as BPA act during this period of pregnancy and can disrupt in-utero male urogenital system development [58]. In our study, although maternal exposures during pregnancy or in the last 6 months-1 year were questioned in general, the exposure time could not be asked since there were many exposure sources.

We detected no relationship between bisphenol tertile level groups and AGD in both male and female newborns. Similarly, Sunman et al. reported no association between BPA and AGD in male newborns [56]. However, since the AGD may shorten over the years, as Miao et al. [55] reported, the infants in this study should be followed until adulthood to observe the potential adverse effects of BPA exposure. Furthermore, because BPA has a short half-life, the high BPA concentrations we measured may be related to recent exposure. Since we could not directly associate these high values with exposure during the fetal period, we cannot determine the relationship between anogenital distance and BPA. This point is a limitation of our study.

NuBPA and NuBPF

Only infants < 35 weeks were included in our study, and no significant relationship was found between gestational age and NuBPA levels. NuBPA median values obtained in the first 24 h and before discharge were 15.5 ng/ml and 24.3 ng/ml, respectively. Strommen et al., in 38 VLBW preterm infants, found that the median urinary BPA concentrations of VLBW infants in the first and fifth weeks of life (11.3 ng/ml and 7.27 ng/ml, respectively) were much higher than those of term infants and older children. Lower gestational age (< 28 weeks) was associated with higher BPA concentrations [29]. Urinary BPA concentrations were found to be higher in infants born before 28 weeks of gestation compared to infants born after 28 weeks of gestation and in infants with late-onset sepsis and bronchopulmonary dysplasia compared to those without these pathological conditions at the fifth week of life. This result has been attributed to more drug and medical device exposure in LBW infants compared to others [29]. The neonatal urine BPA levels reported by Strommen et al. [29] are lower than ours. This may be due to differences in the populations studied and exposure levels. Furthermore, since we did not include a control group in our study, we were unable to make comparisons with infants aged 35 weeks and over.

In our study, the higher level of NuBPF1 in the first-born baby compared to the later-borns’ may be related to the higher amount of BPF passing through the placenta and excreted in the urine in the first pregnancy. However, the reduction in BPF traversing the placenta and excreted in the urine during subsequent pregnancies may be attributed to the diminished accumulation of stored BPF in the maternal body. This may be because BPF, like free BPA, can be stored in body fat, slowly released into the bloodstream, and then conjugated and excreted in the urine. It is known that free BPA taken into the body by any exposure route can be stored in body fat and slowly released into the bloodstream [5].

In our study, the positive association between maternal dermal exposure and infant first urine BPF level may be related to the fact that the dermal BPF exposure sources of these mothers during pregnancy were higher than those of others and passed to the infant through the placenta. However, since there was no significant association between cord BPF and NuBPF levels, the source of NuBPF1 elevation in the infant may differ from maternal exposure. It is unknown which DEM sources contain more BPF than BPA and lead to exposure. More research on this issue is needed.

Another result of our study was that NuBPA levels were not associated with cBPA and BPF, but NuBPA1 and NuBPA2 levels and NuBPF1 and NuBPF2 levels were positively associated. Despite the short half-life of BPA, the positive association between NuBPA1 and NuBPA2 suggests that they are not entirely independent of each other and that similar exposure ways might be present. In premature infants, BPA, which is not excreted more in the urine due to renal immaturity at the beginning, may be excreted more in the urine collected for the second time with increased renal maturation during hospitalization in the NICU. The fact that infant parenteral exposure in the NICU was not associated with NuBPA2 or NuBPF2 levels may be explained by the fact that the materials used in the NICU do not contain BPA and its derivatives.

NuBPA and NuBPF changes

When changes in NuBPA and NuBPF levels during follow-up were evaluated, the patterns did not match initial expectations. NuBPF levels remained stable over time, indicating that under the conditions and timeframe studied, there were no NICU-related exposure events affecting BPF levels. NuBPA levels also showed no consistent change in relation to the measured exposure factors, suggesting that BPA exposure may originate from unpredictable or unmeasured sources, including NICU environmental factors that were not examined in this study. These findings highlight that the sources and dynamics of BPA and BPF exposure differ, with BPF exposure being limited and unaffected by the NICU stay, whereas BPA exposure may reflect additional, less predictable contact pathways.

Detectable NuBPF1 levels and a decrease in NuBPF in the second sample were seen more frequently in male newborns. No such relationship was found for BPA. BPA may affect androgen, estrogen, and glucocorticoid metabolism, increasing or decreasing circulating levels of these hormones in the fetus [29]. For example, BPA is associated with high androgen levels in male fetuses [58], but there is no similar association shown with BPF. In a cross-sectional study of adolescents, urinary BPA concentrations were reported to be negatively associated with testosterone in boys but positively associated with testosterone in girls [59]. However, no study on the effect of these hormones on BPF metabolism and BPF excretion in the urine of male newborns was found in the literature. Deepika et al. developed a sex-specific pediatric physiologically-based pharmacokinetic model (PBPK) in which the plasma concentration of BPA was simulated to be higher in girls aged 6–12 years compared to boys. It was suggested that this might be due to the slower conversion to BPA glucuronide, which can be excreted in the urine, compared to boys [60]. Although there is no similar study on BPF in the literature, due to its similar chemical structure to BPA, the higher urinary excretion of BPF in male infants compared to female infants may be explained by a similar mechanism, as also observed in our study.

The present study revealed a borderline significant inverse association between the head circumference of newborns and the BPF2-BPF1 difference (decreased, stable, and increased groups) (p = 0.054). In a study by Zhou et al., an elevated MuBPA level was associated with a significant reduction in head circumference in male fetuses and an increase in female fetuses, as observed via ultrasound examination during the 36th to 42nd gestational week [61]. The positive association of prenatal low-level BPA exposure with anthropometric measurements observed in boys suggests sex differences in the response to prenatal bisphenol exposure. BPF, a BPA analog, may be expected to have a similar effect, but no studies were found in the literature.

Strengths and limitations

One of the key strengths of our study is the collection of baseline cord blood samples at birth, alongside infant and maternal urine samples, within the first 24 h postpartum. These non-invasive methods effectively capture maternal BPA exposure at birth. Unlike similar studies, we also assessed prenatal exposure to medical devices among preterm newborns in the NICU using environmental history obtained from the mother. Moreover, we utilized a variety of biological samples to evaluate maternal and infant exposure. To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate both BPA and BPF exposure in preterm neonates hospitalized in NICUs, providing novel data on BPF exposure in preterm infants, which has not been reported in the literature. This may shed light on further studies.

The diapers used for the babies are BPA- and BPF-free, and the labels of the diapers also indicate that they are bisphenol-free, as there is a significant resistance to bisphenol-containing baby products in our country. We have also tested whether the diapers have caused contamination to the urine of the babies by using bisphenol-free urine, and after HPLC analysis, we observed that none of the urines tested contained any amount of BPA or BPF.

However, the small sample size may have limited the statistical power of our findings related to BPA and BPF levels and exposures. Further studies with larger sample sizes are necessary to address this limitation. Additionally, the medical devices used in the NICU were not directly analyzed for BPA and BPF content; instead, evaluations were based on label information. Another limitation of our study is that we could not adjust bisphenol levels for variations in urine volume using creatinine excretion, as all measured creatinine concentrations of the infants were outside the validated range. This is expected due to the functional immaturity of their kidneys and the high variability in creatinine clearance in preterm infants [27, 28].

Another limitation of our study is that we have included a relatively high number of multiple-birth infants. In our study, the multiple pregnancy rate was 36% (21/58), higher than the national rate of multiple pregnancies in Turkey was 3.1% in 2020 [62]. At Hacettepe University Hospital, which is one of the leading tertiary centers in perinatology in Turkey, the rate of multiple pregnancies is approximately 3.5%. This figure reflects all pregnancies, regardless of gestational age, as our hospital receives referrals for high-risk pregnancies, including multiple pregnancies, from across the country. This higher rate in our study is because we included preterm infants younger than 35 weeks rather than those born between 35 and 37 weeks, and preterm birth is more common in multiple pregnancies. According to the literature, multiple pregnancies are the leading cause of preterm birth, accounting for approximately 20% of cases [31]. We have included multiple-birth infants because we aimed to monitor the impact of intensive care conditions and postnatal changes. In addition, upon examining the initial data, we found that the values of cBPA, and NuBPA were not correlated between the first and second newborns (Spearman’s rho: p = 0.391 and p = 0.531, respectively). Therefore, multiple births were included in the study sample. Lastly, maternal exposure was based on self-reported data, which may introduce bias.

Conclusions

In this study, we found that maternal urinary BPF tertile levels were inversely associated with infant birth weight percentile, and cord BPA tertile levels were inversely associated with SPL in male infants. A significant positive association was found between first and second neonatal urine BPA (NuBPA1 and NuBPA2) levels in NICU.

Detectable NuBPF1 levels and decreased NuBPF2 compared to NuBPF1 levels were observed more frequently in males.

Cord blood BPA and BPF levels were not significantly correlated with maternal or infant urinary levels. The lack of association between cord, maternal, and neonatal urine bisphenol levels suggests that the newborn is exposed to these chemicals from different sources than the mother, and the BPA and BPF sources may differ. Although cBPA and cBPF levels were higher than in other samples, they may be transmitted to the infant in lesser amounts than expected, possibly by a mechanism other than simple diffusion. However, these possibilities need further investigation.

These findings highlight that the sources and dynamics of BPA and BPF exposure differ, with BPF exposure being limited and unaffected by the NICU stay, whereas BPA exposure may reflect additional, less predictable contact pathways. Although potential exposure to BPA and BPF in the general population is high, the instruments and devices used in the NICU were not labeled as containing these chemicals, suggesting that NICU hospitalization and associated procedures may not have contributed substantially to infants’ exposure. Nevertheless, the observed association between the first and the second NuBPA levels may reflect exposure from other sources outside the NICU, such as products or devices with unknown BPA content, which could not be assessed in this study.

This study contributes to the limited literature on environmental exposures, particularly in preterm neonates, by investigating the exposure status, exposure routes, and relationships between BPA and BPF in the NICU and prenatal period in three different biomatrices.

Although the study could not demonstrate a significant relationship with exposure sources, some effects were observed in preterm infants, consistent with the literature. Therefore, this study will guide further studies with larger samples to investigate the sources of bisphenol exposure in preterm infants. Furthermore, our study aims to raise awareness of the importance of minimizing bisphenol exposure and related health risks during pregnancy and postnatally in NICUs to protect the vulnerable preterm infant population, thereby informing future guidelines and interventions to minimize these risks.
