Resistive Anomaly near a Ferromagnetic Phase Transition: A Classical Memory Effect
Dmitrii L. Maslov 1, Vladimir I. Yudson 2,3, Cristian D. Batista 4,5

Abstract
We investigate resistive anomalies in metals near ferromagnetic phase transitions, focusing on the role of long-range critical fluctuations. Our analysis reveals that diffusive motion of electrons near the critical temperature ( T c T_{c} ) enhances a singular behavior of the resistivity near T c T_{c} through a classical memory effect, surpassing the prediction by Fisher and Langer Fisher and Langer 1968 . We show that, close enough to T c T_{c} , the resistivity exhibits a cusp or anticusp, whose profile is controlled by the critical exponent of the order parameter. We also parameterize the non-Drude behavior of the optical conductivity due to a classical memory effect in terms of critical exponents. These findings offer a deeper understanding of resistive anomalies and their connection to critical exponents in metallic systems.



Introduction:

Second-order phase transitions are accompanied by fluctuations of the incipient order parameter, which become more pronounced as the critical temperature
T_{c}
is approached. A well-known manifestation of such fluctuations is critical opalescence,
i.e., strong
scattering of light
by a fluid near its critical point.
Similarly, electron waves in
a nearly-critical metal
are scattered
by fluctuations of the order parameter.
An analog of critical opalescence in a metal near a magnetic phase transition
is a knee- or cusp-like anomaly in the temperature dependence of its resistance near
T_{c}
.

Starting with the pioneering experiments by Gerlach
Gerlach 1932
,
resistive anomalies have been widely documented
in elemental metallic ferromagnets (FMs), like Ni, Fe, Co, and Gd (see Ref.
Campbell and Fert 1982
and references therein), rare-earth intermetallic compounds
Kawatra et al. 1970
;
Blanco et al. 1994
,
also in elemental
( Tb and Ho)
Nellis and Legvold 1969
and intermetallic
Fote et al. 1970
antiferromagnets (AFMs).
The study of resistive anomalies offers two key advantages. First, it provides a quick and reliable means of identifying a magnetic transition without requiring direct magnetic measurements. Second, the shapes of these anomalies valuable information about the critical exponents of the phase transition, which is particularly useful since resistance can be measured with significantly higher precision than specific heat.
However, extracting this information requires
an appropriate theoretical model
Källbäck et al. 1981
. In this Letter, we revisit and refine
such a model.

Theoretical analysis of resistive anomalies dates back to
the 1958 paper by de Gennes and Friedel (dGF)
De Gennes and Friedel 1958
, who proposed that the additional–on top of scattering by impurities and/or phonons– scattering mechanism near a FM
transition comes from spin-flip scattering of free electrons by localized magnetic moments. Treating spin-flip scattering as being due to quenched long-range disorder (LRD), dGF calculated the
corresponding transport scattering time (
\tau_{\rm tr}
) via the Fermi’s Golden Rule (FGR).
Generalizing their argument to include both FMs and AFMs, we can write

\displaystyle 1/\tau_{\rm tr}\propto\int d^{d}q\frac{q^{2}}{2k_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}\Delta t(q)W({\bf q}),

(1)

where
k_{\mathrm{F}}
is the Fermi momentum,
\varv_{\mathrm{F}}
is the Fermi velocity, and
W({\bf q})=\langle{\bf S}_{{\bf q}}\cdot{\bf S}_{-{\bf q}}\rangle
is the
two-point correlation function of localized magnetic moments in momentum space. The factor of
q^{2}/2k_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}
is the “transport factor”, which suppresses the contribution of small-angle scattering events, while the factor of
\Delta t(q)=1/\varv_{\mathrm{F}}q
(in the simplest case) arises from averaging the energy-conserving delta function over the directions of
{\bf q}
.
At the mean-field level,

W(q)\propto\left(({\bf q}-{\bf Q})^{2}+\xi^{-2}\right)^{-1}
, where
\xi\propto|\theta|^{-1/2}
is the correlation length with
\theta=(T-T_{c})/T_{c}
and
{\bf Q}
is the ordering wavevector. Given that
{\bf Q}=0
for FMs,
the integral (
1
) is controlled by large, of order
k_{\mathrm{F}}
, momentum transfers. Subtracting off this contribution, dGF kept only the universal part, coming from the region
q\sim\xi^{-1}\ll k_{\mathrm{F}}
, which yields
a cusp in the resistivity:
\delta\rho_{\rm dGF}\propto 1/\tau_{\rm tr}\propto-|\theta|\ln(1/|\theta|)
for
d=3
. For
d=2
, the same argument gives
\delta\rho_{\rm dGF}\propto-|\theta|^{1/2}
.

Ten years later, Fisher and Langer (FL)
Fisher and Langer 1968
challenged two aspects of dGF’s analysis. First, FL (correctly) noted that impurity and/or phonon scattering cannot be treated independently from magnetic scattering,
if the mean free path due to the former (
\ell_{\mathrm{s}}
) is shorter than
\xi
, which inevitably occurs close enough to
T_{c}
. While they conjectured that this effect would weaken the dGF singularity, they did not provide a quantitative description of how this occurs.
Instead, FL focused on the second issue:
the short-range (
q\sim k_{\mathrm{F}})
contribution to the integral (
1
), which dGF discarded, but actually exhibits a critical dependence on
\theta
. They noted that in a typical metallic
magnet

k_{\mathrm{F}}\sim a^{-1}_{\rm M}\sim a^{-1}
, where
a_{\rm M}
is the distance between magnetic moments and
a
is the lattice spacing. In this case, the upper limit of the the integral in Eq. (
1
) probes the short-range part of the correlation function, and thus the corresponding part of
\tau_{\rm tr}^{-1}
scales with
\theta

like the magnetic contribution to the internal energy,
U(T)
.
Beyond the mean-field level,
the specific heat
C(T)=dU(T)/dT
behaves as
|\theta|^{-\alpha}
with
0<\alpha<1
, and thus FL concluded that
d\delta\rho/dT\propto d\tau_{\rm tr}^{-1}/dT\propto C(T)\propto|\theta|^{-\alpha}
or

\displaystyle\delta\rho_{\rm FL}\propto{\rm sgn}\,\theta\,|\theta|^{1-\alpha},

(2)

which became known as “FL scaling”. Unlike the dGF prediction, FL scaling implies that the resistivity is a monotonic and, on general grounds, increasing function of
\theta

Alexander et al. 1976
, while its derivative has a cusp at
T_{c}
, which agrees with observations in
elemental FMs sufficiently far away from the transition

Campbell and Fert 1982
.

While FL’s argument applies both to FMs and AFMs, Suezaki and Mori (SM)
Suezaki and Mori 1969
noted that in a typical metallic AFM with
Q\sim k_{\mathrm{F}}\sim a^{-1}
there is another short-range contribution, from the region
{\bf q}\approx{\bf Q}
. In this case, the transport factor and
\Delta t(q)
in Eq. (
1
) do not affect the critical scaling, while the integral over
{\bf q}^{\prime}={\bf q}-{\bf Q}
is dominated by
q^{\prime}\sim\xi^{-1}
. This yields

\displaystyle\delta\rho_{\rm SM}\propto\int dq^{\prime}q^{\prime d-1}W(q^{\prime})=\langle\delta\eta^{2}\rangle\propto|\theta|^{2\beta},

(3)

where
\delta\eta
is a fluctuation of the magnetic order parameter and
\beta
is the critical exponent, defined by
\langle\eta\rangle\propto(-\theta)^{\beta}
for
\theta<0
.

Here, we show in that for
\xi\gg\ell_{\mathrm{s}}
Eq. (
3
) applies not only to AFMs but also to FMs. In contrast to AFMs, however,
the anomaly
in FMs arises from
long-range critical fluctuations, i.e., from the same mechanism, as considered by dGF.
Our result deviates from dGF’s prediction precisely due to the breakdown of Matthiessen’s rule,
pointed out by FL. However, when treated properly, this breakdown enhances rather than weakens the resistive anomaly.
In Ref.
Timm et al. 2005
, mesoscopic fluctuations were shown to amplify a resistive anomaly. Here, we demonstrate that the even a simple diffusive motion of phase-incoherent electrons in the presence of long-range magnetic fluctuations already provides a competing mechanism to FL scaling. The underlying physical mechanism is a classical memory effect arising from multiple returns of a diffusive trajectory to the same spatial point.
Here, we neglect potential quantum phase-coherence effects on the resistive anomaly, considered
in Ref.
Timm et al. 2005
.

Qualitative arguments.

As in the previous
work, we will treat spin-flip scattering as an effect of quenched LRD.
One can then reframe the problem as follows. Suppose we
have a metal with two types of disorder. The first one is short-range
disorder (SRD), which mimics elastic scattering by impurities or quasielastic scattering by phonons at temperatures higher than the Bloch-Grüneisen temperature.
The second one is LRD,
characterized by its correlation function
W(q)
. Given that LRD is weaker than SRD,
the key question is: what is the resultant
resistivity of the metal?

A partial answer
to this question can be
obtained at a phenomenological level, within
classical electrodynamics
Landau and Lifshitz 1994
. Indeed, in the regime of
primary interest,
\xi\gg\ell_{\mathrm{s}}
,
a region of size
\sim\xi
represents an Ohmic resistor,
such that the conductivity
\sigma({\bf r})
varies from one region to another. Accordingly, the electric field and current density also fluctuate in space, following Ohm’ law
{\bf j}({\bf r})=\sigma({\bf r}){\bf E}({\bf r})

(for simplicity, we assume that the medium is isotropic).
In an experiment, one measures the conductivity by relating the average current to the average electric field (the latter defined as the ratio of the
measured voltage to the sample length):
\langle{\bf j}\rangle=\sigma_{\rm exp}\langle{\bf E}\rangle

Dykhne 1971
. If fluctuations of
\sigma({\bf r})
are small,
\sigma_{\rm exp}
can be written as the sum of two parts
Al’tshuler 1985
:

\displaystyle\sigma_{\rm exp}=\sigma_{\rm K}+\delta\sigma_{\rm fl},

(4)

where
\sigma_{\rm K}
is the conductivity in a
uniform
electric field,
calculated
via the Kubo formula, and
\delta\sigma_{\rm fl}
is a correction due to spatial fluctuations. By drawing an analogy between the Poisson equation and
the continuity equation for a
dc
current one can translate the result of Ref.
Landau and Lifshitz 1994
for the effective dielectric constant of a weakly inhomogeneous medium into
\delta\sigma_{\rm fl}=-\langle\delta\sigma^{2}\rangle/d\,\sigma_{\rm K}

Dykhne 1971
;
Al’tshuler 1985
;
Timm et al. 2005
. Since in our case spatial variations of
\sigma({\bf r})
result from fluctuations of the order parameter,
\delta\sigma_{\rm fl}
must be proportional to
\langle\delta\eta^{2}\rangle
, which explains the proportionality in Eq. (
3
). This argument alone casts doubt on the perceived difference between resistivity anomalies in FMs and AFMs, as it applies equally well to both cases.

In its turn,
\sigma_{\rm K}
consists of two parts:
\sigma_{\rm K}=\sigma_{\rm s}+\delta\sigma_{\ell}
, where
\sigma_{\rm s}
is the Drude conductivity due to SRD and
\delta\sigma_{\ell}
is a correction due to LRD. The form of
\delta\sigma_{\ell}
depends on the relation between
\xi
and
\ell_{\mathrm{s}}
. The crucial quantity is the “interaction time”
\Delta t(q)
in Eq. (
1
), which is the duration of a scattering event with momentum transfer
q\sim\xi^{-1}

Altshuler and Aronov 1985
. By the uncertainty principle, the momentum transfer
q
occurs in a region of size
\sim 1/q
. In the ballistic regime (
\xi\ll\ell_{\mathrm{s}}
),
an electron
travels through this region in time
\Delta t(q)=1/\varv_{\mathrm{F}}q\sim\xi/\varv_{\mathrm{F}}
, which is much shorter than the mean free time due to scattering by SRD,
\tau_{\mathrm{s}}=\ell_{\mathrm{s}}/\varv_{\mathrm{F}}
. Therefore, SRD and LRD scatter an electron independently from each other, and the corresponding
scattering
rates
add up

à la
Matthiessen’s rule, which is the key assumption behind
dGF scaling.

The diffusive regime (
\xi\gg\ell_{\mathrm{s}})
differs from the ballistic one in two aspects. First, scattering by SRD smears out electron states, which
implies that the energy-conserving delta function in FGR needs to be replaced by a Lorentzian of width
1/\tau_{\mathrm{s}}
, as was done in many previous papers
Geldart and Richard 1975
;
Entin-Wohlman et al. 1975
;
Geldart and De’Bell 1985
;
Kataoka 2001
. Accordingly,
\Delta t(q)\sim\tau_{\mathrm{s}}
, and the integrand in of Eq. (
1
) becomes
less
singular at
q\to 0
. This leads to a weakening of the dGF resistive anomaly, as conjectured by
FL.

However, this is not the primary effect of diffusion. The key difference is that a diffusing electron takes a significantly longer time to travel a distance of order
\xi
:

\Delta t(q)\sim 1/D_{\rm s}q^{2}
, where
D_{\rm s}=\varv_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\tau_{\mathrm{s}}/d
is the diffusion coefficient in SRD. As a result, the integrand of Eq. (
1
) becomes
more
singular than in the ballistic regime. In fact, the diffusion singularity in
\Delta t(q)\propto 1/q^{2}
cancels out the
q^{2}
from the transport factor, and the integrand is reduced to
\int dqq^{d-1}W(q)\sim\langle\delta\eta^{2}\rangle
. As a result,
\delta\sigma_{\ell}\sim\delta\sigma_{\rm fl}\propto-\langle\delta\eta^{2}\rangle
, in agreement with Eq. (
3
).

Discussion.

The most adequate interpretation of the enhanced resistivity anomaly in the diffusive regime is a classical memory effect, stemming from multiple returns of electron trajectory to the same position
Wilke et al. 2000
. However, we would also like to point out a similarity between diagrams
d-g
in Fig.
1
and the diagrams describing the Altshuler-Aronov (AA) correction to the conductivity, which arises from quantum interference between electron-electron and electron-impurity interactions
Altshuler and Aronov 1985
. In AA diagrams, the dashed line is replaced by the dynamical potential of electron-electron interaction and, in addition, each vertex of this interaction is dressed by a diffuson ladder. Since our LR potential is static, it cannot change the analyticity of the Green’s functions surrounding the vertex, and vertex corrections vanish to leading order in
1/\mu\tau_{\mathrm{s}}
. However, the common theme of quantum interference and classical memory effects is that diffusing electrons are more susceptible to external perturbations, be it the Coulomb potential of other electrons in the AA case or LRD in our case.

Connection to the experiment.

While the qualitative prediction
for a resistive anomaly near the Curie temperature
T_{c}
is
confirmed
in many metallic
FMs
Gerlach 1932
;
Bittel and Gerlach 1938
;
Craig et al. 1967
;
Campbell and Fert 1982
, the scaling predicted by Fisher and Langer
is not always observed.
In some
FMs, such as Ni, the resistivity anomaly aligns with FL scaling over some temperature range around
T_{c}

Källbäck et al. 1981
, while in others, such as rare-earth
FMs of the type RNi
5

with R=Tb, Dy or Er, it deviates significantly from the FL prediction because
\rho
itself
(rather than
d\rho/dT
) exhibits a
maximum at
T_{c}

Blanco et al. 1994
. Moreover, even for Ni, a careful fit
that accounts only for the FL singular contribution plus regular terms fails to reproduce the resistivity within a
\pm 1
K interval around
T_{c}

Källbäck et al. 1981
.

While the FL anomaly corresponds a peak in
d\rho/dT
, the resistive anomaly in the diffusive regime, discussed in this Letter, leads to a cusp or anticusp structure in
\rho
, which is consistent with a peak in
the resistivity
measured in the rare earth ferromagnets RNi
5

Blanco et al. 1994
. Furthermore,
a cusp in
\rho
, along with the corresponding asymmetric peak-antipeak anomaly in
d\rho/dT
that results from adding the regular and the singular FL contributions explains the resistivity anomaly observed in the GaR
2

FMs with R=Ni, Rh and Pt
Kawatra et al. 1970
.

Distinguishing between the FL contribution and the one predicted in this Letter may require measuring the resistivity too close to
T_{c}
for the conclusions to
be reliable.
In this regard,
a non-Drude behavior of the optical conductivity, depicted in Fig.
2
, may serve as a more robust indicator of the classical memory effect behind the resistive anomaly.
As an illustration, we consider a ferromagnetic transition in Ni, where
\ell_{\mathrm{s}}\approx 25
Å  at
T=T_{c}=625
K

or, taking
\varv_{\mathrm{F}}=10^{8}
cm/s,

1/\tau_{\mathrm{s}}\approx 250
meV.
The nonanalytic downturn starts at
\Omega\sim E_{\rm Th}(|\theta|)
. Taking
\xi=a/|\theta|^{\nu}
,
with
a\sim 1
Å  and
\nu\approx 0.7

O(3)
in
d=3
,
we obtain

.
The system transitions to a diffusive regime when
\xi(\theta_{D})=\ell_{\mathrm{s}}
, i.e., at
|\theta|=\theta_{D}
, where
\theta_{D}=(a/\ell_{\mathrm{s}})^{1/\nu}=0.01

a=1
Å  and
\nu=0.7
. In terms of
\theta_{D}

However, there is a limit on how close one can get to

An alternative approach is to investigate periodic intermetallic materials, in which the spacing between localized magnetic moments is comparable to
\ell_{\mathrm{s}}

thereby extending the relative temperature window,

In principle, these systems allow extracting the

Additionally, ongoing efforts aim to engineer 2D versions of such metallic magnets
