Quantitative EEG biomarkers for STXBP1 ‐related disorders
Authors: Alberto Cossu, Francesca Furia, Jacopo Proietti, Caterina Ancora, Chiara Reale, Francesca Darra, Roberto Previtali, Bernardo Dalla Bernardina, Guido Rubboli, Sandor Beniczky, Rikke S Møller, Gaetano Cantalupo, Elena Gardella
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveEEG patterns and quantitative EEG (qEEG) features have been poorly explored in monogenic epilepsies. Herein, we investigate regional differences in EEG frequency composition in patients with STXBP1 developmental and epileptic encephalopathy (STXBP1‐DEE).MethodsWe conducted a retrospective study collecting electroclinical data of patients with STXBP1‐DEE and two control groups of patients with DEEs of different etiologies and typically developing individuals matched for age and sex. We performed a (1) visual EEG assessment, (b) qEEG analysis, and (c) electrical source imaging (ESI). We quantified the relative power (RP) of four frequency bands (α β, θ, δ), in two electrode groups (anterior/posterior), and compared their averages and dynamics (standard deviation [SD] over time). The ESI was performed by applying the standard Distributed Source Modeling algorithm.ResultsWe analyzed 42 EEG studies in 19 patients with STXBP1‐DEE (10 female), with a median age at recordings of 9.6 years (range 9 months to 29 years). The δRP was higher in recordings of STXBP1‐DEE (p < .001) compared to both control groups, suggesting the pathogenicity and STXBP1‐specificity of these findings. In STXBP1‐DEE, the δRP was significantly higher in the anterior electrode group compared to the posterior one (p = .003). There was no correlation between the anterior δRP and the epilepsy focus, age at recordings, and concomitant medications The ESI modeling of this activity showed a widespread involvement of the dorsomesial frontal cortex, suggesting a large corticosubcortical pathologic network. Finally, we identified two groups of recordings: cluster.1 with higher anterior δRP and low dynamics and cluster.2 with lower δRP and higher dynamics. Patients in cluster.1 had a more severe epilepsy and neurological phenotype compared to patients in cluster 2.SignificanceThe qEEG analysis showed a predominant frontal slow activity as a specific STXBP1 feature that correlates with the severity of the phenotype and may represent a biomarker for prospective longitudinal studies of STXBP1‐DEE.Keywords: biomarker, developmental and epileptic encephalopathy, quantitative EEG, STXBP1, translational neuroscience

INTRODUCTION

The Syntaxin‐Binding Protein 1, encoded by the STXBP1 gene, is a protein widely expressed throughout the brain and involved in vesicle release from the pre‐synaptic terminal. This protein interacts with the products of VAMP2, STX1B, and other proteins forming the SNARE‐complex (soluble N‐ethylmaleimide‐Sensitive Factor attachment protein receptor), necessary to prime the fusion of neurotransmitter vesicles downstream from Ca2+ voltage‐gated channels and action potentials. 1

The first cohort of patients carrying a pathogenic STXBP1 variant presented with Ohtahara syndrome. 2 Variants in STXBP1 are now associated with a spectrum of neurodevelopmental disorders, including developmental and epileptic encephalopathies (DEEs) and a few cases with developmental delay (DD) or intellectual disability (ID) without epilepsy. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7

The epilepsy phenotype of STXBP1‐DEE is heterogeneous and includes multiple seizure types, frequently refractory to anti‐seizure medications (ASMs). 8 A recent study in an STXBP1‐DEE mouse model has reported an impairment in striato‐cortical transmission as a possible mechanism in evoking epileptiform activity. 9 Given the importance of epilepsy in patients STXBP1‐DEE, the EEG features have been previously qualitatively described, 4 although little to no attention has been dedicated to the quantitative EEG (qEEG) analysis 10 and to the source localization of the EEG abnormalities.

In recent years, the identification of biomarkers for neurodevelopmental disorders has been established as an emerging field of research in clinical and translational neuroscience. 10 , 11 , 12 Clinical neurophysiology, particularly electrical source imaging (ESI) and qEEG, is crucial for capturing dynamic features that allow a specific, safe, reproducible correlation to clinical features of DEEs. Given the growing attention to natural history studies in monogenic DEEs, the identification of a biomarker for STXBP1‐DEE could help in monitoring the long‐term outcome of the disease and the efficacy of the interventions.

In this study, we performed a qEEG analysis of STXBP1‐DEE, investigating regional differences in EEG frequency composition and source modeling of these activities, and correlating them with different phenotypes.

METHODS

We collected clinical data and EEG recordings of patients with genetically confirmed STXBP1‐DEE, including seizure type and frequency, presence of developmental regression and a degree of intellectual and motor disability, presence of movement disorders, behavioral symptoms, and autistic features at the time of inclusion.

We also collected electro‐clinical data from two control groups of age‐ and sex‐matched individuals. The first control group consisted of patients with DEEs of different etiologies (10 Dravet syndrome [DS], 8 Angelman syndrome (AS), and 6 magnetic resonance imaging [MRI]–negative, non‐genetic etiology) and the second group of typically developing individuals. We chose DS because of its larger frequency among monogenic DEEs and AS for its recognizable interictal EEG features, apparently similar to STXBP1‐DEE. The typically developing group included 15 individuals with normal developmental milestones and no chronic diseases, recorded for some minor clinical suspicion (syncope, shuddering, headache), and then proved healthy, and four people with self‐limiting epilepsy on remission, normal EEG, and no ASM since 10–22 years, referred for a standard EEG recording for their driving license.

EEG recordings were carried out during wakefulness, with a standard array of 19 or 25 electrodes placed over the scalp according to the international 10–20 system and a sampling rate of 256 Hz. The EEG analysis consisted of (1) a visual assessment of the whole recording, (2) frequency analysis, and (3) and ESI.

Visual analysis and epoch selection

We identified the ictal focus and the localization of the interictal epileptiform discharges (IEDs), and of focal slowing of the background activity, if present, on whole raw EEG data. Then we identified “bad” EEG segments when they contained major artifacts, blinking artifacts, seizures, or post‐ictal periods. Identification of bad EEG segments on controls was undertaken by an investigator (C.A.) who was blinded to the patient's diagnosis and to the study purpose and methods. Epoch selection on EEG studies from patients with STXBP1‐DEE was performed by a different investigator (A.C.), who was blinded to patients’ clinical features. Resting‐state EEG during relaxed wakefulness with eyes open is the gold standard for investigating intrinsic brain activity. Therefore, EEG epochs with eyes closed and during sleep/drowsiness were rejected. The remaining wakefulness intervals were filtered using a band‐pass filter (0.6–30 Hz) and divided into consecutive 10‐s epochs.

Frequency analysis

Fast Fourier transform (FFT)–based analysis was performed on Brain Quick (Micromed S.p.A., Mogliano Veneto, Italy). We defined the four major frequency bands as: delta (δ: 0.5–3.5 Hz), theta (θ: 4–7.5 Hz), alpha (α: 8–12.5 Hz), and beta (β: 13–30 Hz). The analysis was performed on 10‐s epochs in two electrode groups defined as anterior (Fp1‐Fp2‐F3‐F4‐F7‐F8) and posterior (O1‐O2‐P3‐P4‐P7‐P8), based on the visual analysis. Recordings performed with additional low‐row electrodes contained F9 and F10 in the anterior group and P9 and P10 in the posterior group. The analysis was performed using a Laplacian reference with custom matrices.

We quantified the following parameters for every epoch in both electrode groups:

We compared the averages of all the described parameters in the first 120 epochs of each recording. We calculated the standard deviation (SD) between epochs of these averages for each recording. In this case, the SD between epochs is used as a surrogate marker of EEG modulation, reflecting the difference in frequency composition between 10‐s epochs and named “dynamics” (Figure 1).

Analysis workflow. Wakefulness EEG recording underwent a blinded epoch rejection, selecting 10‐s EEG epochs free from movement and blinking artifacts, seizures, and post‐ictal activity. The selected EEG epochs were analyzed to obtain comparable parameters consisting of: Relative power (RP) for every frequency band: Quotient between the absolute power of a frequency band and the sum of the absolute power of all four bands in the spectrum. Spectral edge frequency (SEF): Defined as the frequency encompassing 95% of the power of the whole spectrum. Median dominant frequency (MDF): Weighted average of the power of all frequencies in the spectrum.

Electrical source imaging (ESI)

Source modeling was performed on 25‐electrode array recordings using BESA Research 7.1 (BESA GmbH, Gräfelfing, Germany). We averaged delta waves, time‐locked to the negative peaks, which were detected automatically using template‐matching based on the average waveform of the visually detected transients. Modeling was performed by applying the standard Distributed Source Modeling (DSM) algorithm LORETA (Low‐Resolution brain Electromagnetic Tomography Analysis) 13 using age‐appropriate Finite Element Models (FEM) for each recording.

Statistical analysis

qEEG and clinical data were analyzed using SPSS Version 25.0 (IBM Corp. Released 2017. Armonk, NY). Patients’ ages at recording were analyzed as a continuous variable and also codified as a categorical variable, subdividing patients into four age groups at the time of recording: (1) younger than 3 years of age, (2) between 3 and 8 years of age, (3) between 9 and 16 years old, and (iv) older than 16 years.

Average RP of four delta bands within a patient's group were compared using a paired t test (α = 0.05), comparisons between groups were made using an independent sample t test (α = 0.05), after confirmation of normal distribution using the Shapiro–Wilk test (α = 0.05). The SD of RP averages between epochs was used as a continuous variable. Correlations of averages and correspondent dynamics were made with a bivariate Pearson correlation (α = 0.05).

Using a two‐step cluster analysis, we classified the EEG recordings based on the anterior δRP and the corresponding anterior δ dynamics for each recording. Correlation of other parameters with cluster classification was performed with the Kruskal–Wallis analysis of variance (ANOVA) test, Mann–Whitney U test, or chi‐square analysis.

Standard protocol approvals, registration, and patient consent

The regional ethics committee approved the study protocol (SJ‐722; 24 September 2018), and participants signed an informed consent form in compliance with the Helsinki Declaration of 1975.

RESULTS

We analyzed a total of 85 EEG recordings, including 42 EEG recordings from 19 patients with STXBP1‐DEE (10 female/9 male), with a median age of 9.6 years (range: 9 months to 29 years), each taking one to three ASMs.

In STXBP1‐DEE, the visual qEEG analysis revealed the presence of focal slowing in 57% (24/42) of the recordings. The localization of the slowing was in the frontal region in 79% (19/24) of the cases, synchronous and asynchronous on the two hemispheres. In 47% of recordings (9/19), we identified multifocal slowing, where the frontal slowing coexisted with other localizations, typically in the temporal region of one hemisphere (7/9) or bilaterally (2/9).

IEDs were detected in 64% (27/42) of recordings. In 14% (6/42) of EEG recordings, IEDs were generalized, in 28% (12/42) they were localized in the anterior electrode group, and in 21% (9/42) they were localized in the posterior electrode group. In 12% of recordings (5/42), the focal slowing co‐localized with the IEDs; two of five recordings (one patient) in the right temporal region, and three of five (two patients) in the frontal regions.

Frequency composition

In the STXBP1‐DEE group, the average parameters analyzed in the first 120 epochs selected for each recording were as follows: the MDF was 1.47 Hz anteriorly and 1.53 Hz posteriorly; the SEF was 3.37 Hz anteriorly and 3.42 Hz posteriorly. The average RP and Amp of the four bands in the two electrode groups for the STXBP1‐DEE group are reported in Table 1. The average δRP both anteriorly and posteriorly was significantly higher than the RP of the other three bands in the same electrode group (p < .001 for all pairs). The anterior and posterior δAmp, MDF, and SEF did not differ significantly anteriorly and posteriorly (p > .05).

To compare the EEG frequency composition with control groups, we selected one recording per patient in the STXBP1‐DEE group, age‐matched with recordings from both control groups. We, therefore, compared 19 EEG studies in 19 patients (10 female/9 male) with STXBP1‐DEE, with a median age at recordings of 11.2 years (range: 1 year to 29 years), with 24 EEG recordings from 24 patients with other‐DEEs (11 male/13 female), with a median age of 11.2 years (range: 1 to 27 years), and 19 EEG studies from 19 typically developing individuals (9 male/10 female), with a median age of 12 years (range: 1 to 29 years).

Within the three different populations, the frequency spectrum composition showed a statistically higher δRP than the other frequency bands in the STXBP1‐DEE and the typically developing control group (p < .0001). However, the comparison between STXBP1‐DEE, other‐DEEs, and typically developing controls showed a significantly higher δRP in both STXBP1‐DEE and other‐DEEs groups when compared to typically developing (p < .0001) and a significantly higher δRP in the STXBP1‐DEE group compared to other‐DEEs (p < .0001, Figure 2B). No other EEG parameter (Amp, MED, SEF) reached statistical significance on multiple pairwise comparisons between different populations.

In the STXBP1‐DEE group, the average anterior δRP was significantly higher than the correspondent RP in the posterior regions (p = .003, Table 1). This significance was not found in any of the control groups. Regional differences in spectrum composition between etiology subgroups of genetically determined DEEs documented a significantly higher δRP both anteriorly and posteriorly, in patients with STXBP1‐DEE when compared to patients with both DS (p < .001) and AS (p < .001, Figure 2C).

Frequency composition of STXBP1 ‐DEE in the first 120 epochs

In overall EEG recordings (n = 42) in the STXBP1‐DEE group, a higher anterior δRP (average for each recording) was associated with a lower anterior δ dynamics (p < .001; Pearson's coefficient − 0.58). A similar trend was found for the posterior δRP and correspondent dynamics (p < .005; Pearson's coefficient − 0.46).

However, in the other frequency bands (α, β, θ), a higher average RP was associated with a higher correspondent dynamic (α and β: p < .001 anteriorly and posteriorly; θ: p < .001 anteriorly, p < .005 posteriorly). Similarly, the δAmp was significantly higher than αAmp, βAmp, and θAmp, when compared pairwise (δAmp‐αAmp, δAmp‐βAmp, δAmp‐θAmp), in the same electrode group (p < .001). However, the anterior and posterior δAmp did not differ significantly (p = .4).

When investigating the correlation between age groups and EEG parameters, we found a lower posterior δRP in older patients (p = .001), in particular in adults (older than 16 years of age), and a consequently higher RP in the other three bands. However,, the anterior δRP was not statistically different between the four age groups (Figure 3A).

We replicated comparisons among recordings in different age groups, and we found a significantly higher δRP in the anterior region compared to the posterior δRP in patients between 9 and 16‐year‐olds (p = .05) and in individuals older than 16 years of age (p = .007), but not in younger age groups.

There was no significant correlation between anterior δRP, patients´ ages at the time of recording (overall and among groups), the localization of the IEDs in the anterior region (p = .809), and the number or type (e.g., benzodiazepines, valproate) of concomitant ASMs (Figure 3B).

Frequency composition of STXBP1 ‐DEE in the 60 epochs centered around the maximum anterior δRP

We investigated the same EEG parameters in the 60 epochs centered around the maximum δRP in the anterior electrode group, to further characterize this peculiar activity.

In these epochs, the MDF was 1.4 Hz anteriorly and 1.5 Hz posteriorly. The SEF was 3.3 Hz anteriorly and 3.4 Hz posteriorly. The average values of the RP, dynamics, and Amp in all four bands and all electrode groups are reported in Table 1.

The same differences reported for the first 120 epochs were confirmed in the 60 epochs around the maximum. Of interest, the MDFs in both regions were the same, showing the same dominant spectral frequency in both time‐windows.

The same relationship between RP and dynamics was found in these group of epochs, with a stronger correlation (anterior δ p < .001, Pearson coefficient − 0.69; θ p < .001, Pearson coefficient 0.67; α p < .001, Pearson coefficient 0.76; β p < .001, Pearson coefficient 0.79).

There was no significant correlation between the anterior δRP, the patient's age, epileptic focus, number, or type (benzodiazepines or valproate) of concomitant ASMs.

Cluster analysis

A two‐step cluster analysis was performed with the variables anterior δRP and anterior δ dynamics as input. The analysis identified two clusters: (1) cluster.1 (22 recordings, mean age at recordings 13.9 years) with high δRP and low variance, (2) cluster.2 (20 recordings, mean age at recordings 12.5 years) with δRP and variance comparable to the average of the whole population (66.7%, dynamic 11.6% vs 74.4%, dynamic 10.4%). The most frequent seizure type was generalized tonic–clonic seizures (GTCS) in 60% (9/15) of patients, followed by tonic in 53% (8/15), focal motor in 47% (7/15), focal with impaired awareness seizures (FIAS) in 40% (6/15), and myoclonic seizures in 13% (2/15). Seventy‐four percent (12/15) of patients presented with more than one concurrent seizure type, with 27% (4/15) having two seizure types, 33% (5/15) having three different types of seizures, and two patients having respectively four and five different types. Fifty‐three percent (8/15) of patients had frequent seizures (daily or weekly), whereas 33% (5/15) had rare seizures (monthly or yearly), and only 13% (2/15) were seizure‐free at the last follow‐up. All patients were treated with ASM; 47% of patients (7/15) needed two, 20% (3/15) needed three, 20% (3/15) needed one, and two patients needed four and five, respectively. The psychomotor development was characterized by regression in six patients; 20% (3/15) were diagnosed with Autism Spectrum Disorder, and 40% (6/15) had externalizing behavioral symptoms (inattention, aggression, hyperactivity). The degree of ID was profound in 20% (3/15) of cases, severe in 40% (6/15), and moderate in 40% (6/15); one patient (7%) had an average IQ. Sixty‐seven percent (10/15) of patients were non‐verbal and the rest had productive language, and only 20% (3/15) could pronounce complete sentences; 40% (6/15) were unable to walk independently, and only 20% (3/15) could move independently; 40% (6/15) had movement disorders (ataxia, tremor, bradykinesia).

Patients in different clusters had different seizure types, with patients in cluster.1 presenting more frequently with tonic seizures and patients in cluster.2 presenting more frequently with FIAS (Figure 4C,D). Furthermore, none of the patients in cluster.1 were seizure‐free; they presented with more polymorphic seizures, and were more frequently treated with more than two ASMs simultaneously.

The clinical features at the latest follow‐up were also different in the two EEG clusters (Figure 3), although the differences did not reach statistical significance. Notably, patients in cluster.1 had a more severe neurodevelopmental delay, with a higher degree of ID, language impairment, and mobility issues compared to patients in cluster.2.

Electrical source imaging

We analyzed 23 recordings from 13 different patients. The ESI analysis showed a constant involvement of the frontal cortex in generating the slow activity (Figure 3C). The involvement was variably widespread, involving the whole frontal lobe bilaterally (57%, 13/23), or with a side predominance (43%, 10/23). When focal, the modeled sources were more frequently located in the mesial frontal sub‐lobe, often with a side predominance. Recordings in cluster.1 presented more frequently with a more extensive cortical involvement (79%, 11/14), whereas patients in cluster.2 had more focal cortical sources (78%, 7/9), possibly due to amplitude differences and different neurophysiological properties of the underlying network. 13

DISCUSSION

We performed a detailed visual and quantitative analysis of the interictal EEG features in STXBP1‐DEE, focusing on the non‐epileptiform EEG activity. By qualitative analysis, we observed that a recurrent EEG feature was diffuse background slowing with superimposed focal slowing, predominant in the anterior region (79% of recordings). Although generalized EEG slowing is common in patients with widespread cortical dysfunction, including DEEs, focal EEG slowing suggests a restricted impairment of cortical function. 14 To the best of our knowledge, focal anterior EEG slowing has not been reported before in STXBP1‐DEE. This could be due to the relatively recent increase of STXBP1‐DEE patient cohorts, as well as the lack of detailed EEG descriptions in these patients.

The quantitative EEG analysis documented the higher RP for δ frequencies, in both STXBP1‐DEE and the typically developing controls, as expected by the nature of an oscillating signal. 15 However, compared to typically developing controls, a significantly higher δRP was documented in patients with other‐DEEs, regardless of their etiology, suggesting a diffuse central nervous system impairment. This finding was in line with what was reported in the literature. 16 Surprisingly, STXBP1‐DEEs showed a further statistically significant global increase of the δRP compared to other‐DEEs. A confirmation of the initial visual impression, in the STXBP1‐DEE group the slow activity was quantitatively more represented in the anterior regions, as indicated by the significantly higher δRP in this electrode group, suggesting an additional focal central nervous system impairment. These regional differences were not found in other DEEs, suggesting the specificity of this feature to STXBP1‐DEE, and could represent a distinct neurophysiological characteristic of the condition. Particularly, EEG in AS is known to be characterized by focal slowing in the frontal and occipital regions. However, unlike in STXBP1‐DEE, the focal slowing in patients with AS is often accompanied by focal IEDs. 17 Furthermore, our findings were quantitatively distinguishable from the ones of the DEE control group in general, and from recording in patients with AS in particular (Figure 2).

Compared to control groups, we also observed a peculiar dynamic of the anterior δRP in the STXBP1‐DEE cohort. When a higher activity in the delta frequency band was quantified, it was less modulated over time (lower dynamics) and monomorphic. This finding further supports the pathogenicity of the anterior delta activity, as in the case of persistent focal slowing related to brain lesions. 18 Because no anatomic brain alteration has been observed in our STXBP1‐DEE cohort, we postulate that these findings might reflect an extended functional altered network.

Of interest, neither the δRP nor its dynamics had a significant relationship with the topography of the IEDs, the type and position of the STXBP1 variant, or the concomitant ASM. Notably, we observed a correlation of the patient's age at the time of recordings with the posterior δRP, but not with the anterior δRP. The posterior δRP was lower in age groups above 9 years, possibly due to a developmental modulation of the delta band posteriorly, mirroring a physiological development. 11 The lack of similar correlations anteriorly suggests that the anterior slow activity is the expression of a dysfunctional development in the anterior regions, regardless of epileptic activity or pharmacological effect.

We identified two STXBP1‐DEE groups, one where the anterior delta activity was highly represented and monomorphic, and one with less represented and more modulated slow activity. The group of patients with more abundant and less modulated slow activity had worse motor performance, more severe intellectual disability, and language impairment. This suggests that a continuous slow activity over the anterior regions in patients with STXBP1‐DEE, regardless of the patient's age and ongoing treatment, could represent a biomarker of phenotype severity.

The ESI analysis estimates the cortical generator of the anterior delta activity as widely distributed along the frontal cortex. LORETA is one of the most frequently used ESI approaches, with good accuracy and spatial resolution for modeling EEG generators limited to the cerebral cortex. 13 , 19 However, previous studies showed that the LORETA modeling of a diffused cortical activity in the frontal regions suggests a large underlying cortical–subcortical generator. 20 Thus we hypothesize that the anterior delta activity in STXBP1‐DEE may reflect the dysfunction of an extended cortical–subcortical network. The functional linkage between the frontal cortex and other subcortical nuclei, such as the basal ganglia, and in particular, the striatum, has been investigated extensively, 21 , 22 with evidence of a subcortical pacemaker in the sub‐thalamic nucleus of rats. 23 This role of pacemaker and relay of cortically driven rhythms on scalp EEG has been demonstrated by a recent study in humans, carried out with intracranial EEG and ESI on high‐density scalp EEG. 24 Regarding STXBP1, Miyamoto and collaborators, identified a similar network involving the mesial prefrontal cortex, the striatum, and the somatosensory cortex, as the driver of epileptic activity in a mouse model of STXBP1‐DEE. 9

Our findings interpreted in the context of a cortical–subcortical network, suggest the presence of a predominant, dysfunctional neural system in STXBP1‐DEE, with a specific electrophysiological signature and pathological development. In addition, a higher degree of disability in patients with a higher and more continuous activity shows a possible link between this neurophysiological finding and clinical features, strengthening the hypotheses of a dysfunctional network. Furthermore, the independence of this activity from epileptiform discharges, patient's age, and concomitant medications suggests a primary causative role of the STXBP1 gene dysfunction in the perturbation of this network.

Limitations of this study are the relatively small sample size and the lower spatial resolution of a standard 25‐electrode EEG recording. However, similar recording techniques have been used previously in EEG frequency and coherence analysis studies of developmental and epileptic encephalopathies. 25 , 26 Furthermore, previous studies have suggested that it was feasible to apply to focal slowing the ESI methodology established for epileptiform EEG activity; it is unclear whether this can also be extrapolated to the use of age‐matched template models and the 25‐electrode array. We would argue that the larger spatial extent of the slow waves makes ESI less sensitive to these issues. 26 However, this activity was not related to epileptiform activity in STXBP1‐DEE. The lack of comparable activities identified in other DEEs included in this work did not permit the replication of the ESI study in the control group. Future studies with a larger sample are needed to confirm our findings.

CONCLUSIONS

We describe the presence of prominent focal slow activity over the frontal regions as a hallmark of STXBP1‐DEE that correlates with the severity of the phenotype independently from the epileptic focus, ASMs, and patient's age at the time of recordings. This activity suggests a large cortico‐subcortical dysfunctional network, consistent with previous observations in the STXBP1‐DEE mouse model.

We propose qEEG as a biomarker for STXBP1‐DEE, potentially useful for the definition of sub‐phenotypes and outcome measures in interventional and non‐interventional studies of STXBP1‐DEE. This approach can be extended potentially to clinical and translational studies of other monogenic epilepsies.
