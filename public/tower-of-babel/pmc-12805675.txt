Discrepancy in perceived appropriate and desired resuscitation durations between physicians and laypersons in cardiac arrest patients
Authors: Yuko Tanabe, Takeshi Namba, Shoichi Maeda, Mitsuaki Nishikimi, Eri Ishikawa, Shinichiro Ohshimo, Nobuaki Shime
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study examined a discrepancy that exists between the duration of resuscitative efforts that physicians perceive as appropriate and the duration that laypersons desire. We conducted an online nationwide cross-sectional survey with 323 physicians and 2,667 laypersons. Physicians were significantly more likely than laypersons to consider a duration of ≥ 30 min as appropriate for resuscitation, especially in younger patients (age 0–6:85% vs. 27%; age 7–17:84% vs. 29%; both p < 0.001). Although these responses arise from fundamentally different psychological frames, identifying this discrepancy may provide a conceptual foundation for discussing more appropriate termination-of-resuscitation approaches in real-world clinical settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40560-025-00841-8.

Introduction

The decision to terminate resuscitation in cardiac arrest (CA) patients is both critical and ethically challenging. Currently, no clear standards exist for termination-of-resuscitation (TOR) in a hospital setting [1]. Consequently, decisions regarding in-hospital TOR are often highly subjective. We hypothesized that a discrepancy may exist between the duration of resuscitative efforts that physicians perceive as appropriate and the duration that laypersons desire.

Age is a crucial factor influencing TOR decisions and previous reports have suggested that physicians tend to continue resuscitation longer in younger patients [2]. However, little is known about laypersons’ perceptions of appropriate resuscitation duration across age group. In this study, we aimed to compare the physician-perceived appropriate duration of resuscitation with the duration desired by laypersons across different age groups using a cross-sectional survey. By identifying potential discrepancies between these groups, we sought to illuminate an important but under-recognized issue in TOR practices.

Patients and methods

We conducted a nationwide cross-sectional survey between October and December 2022. Physician participants were recruited via the mailing list of the Japanese Society of Intensive Care Medicine, whereas laypersons were sampled through an online panel managed by GMO Research and AI Inc. The contractor distributed the survey to members of the Japan Cloud Panel, one of Japan’s largest online network panels (> 30 million registered monitors; male: 42%, female: 58%). No financial incentives were provided for participation at this study. Questionnaires were randomly distributed to individuals after stratifying them into 12 groups by age and sex. Physicians were defined as licensed doctors actively engaged in emergency resuscitation.

Participants were asked the maximum duration they considered appropriate in cases where no return of spontaneous circulation was achieved. Six age groups were evaluated: 0–6, 7–17, 18–34, 35–64, 65–74, and ≥ 75 years, with four duration options: “ < 15 min”, “15–29 min”, “30–59 min”, and “ ≥ 60 min”. The questionnaires used in this study are presented in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2. The starting point for resuscitation duration was defined as the time of hospital arrival. Responses of “ ≥ 30 min” were compared between physicians and laypersons using Fisher’s exact test. JMP Pro 18 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) was used for all the analyses.

Results

Surveys were distributed to 7,859 physicians, and 831 (10.6%) responded. After excluding residents who were not actively involved in emergency resuscitation, 773 remained, of whom 493 were currently engaged in resuscitation. Of these, 323 (65.5%) answered all questions. Among laypersons, questionnaires were distributed to 2,728 individuals, and 2,667 responses were obtained (response rate: 97.8%). The median age of the layperson group was 49 years (interquartile range [IQR], 35–64), 50% were male, and 10.3% reported having experienced the death of a family member in the emergency department.

Among 323 physicians, clinical experience was < 5 years in 3%, 5–9 years in 11%, 10–14 years in 20%, 15–19 years in 18%, 20–24 years in 14%, and ≥ 25 years in 34%. Regarding specialties, 78% were emergency physicians, 6% were pediatricians, and 17% were from other fields.

The proportions of physicians who considered ≥ 30 min appropriate were 85% (273/323), 84% (270/323), 77% (248/323), 68% (221/323), 47% (153/323), and 34% (110/323) across increasing age groups. In contrast, corresponding laypersons proportions were 27% (714/2,667), 29% (783/2,667), 34% (896/2,667), 32% (841/2,667), 24% (629/2,667), and 21% (547/2,667). Across all age groups, physicians were significantly more likely than laypersons to support ≥ 30 min of resuscitation effort (Fig. 1, all p < 0.001). Discussion

Physicians were significantly more likely than laypersons to support resuscitation for ≥ 30 min, especially in pediatric patients. This is consistent with evidence that physicians often extend resuscitation for younger patients due to several ethical and emotional factors [2–4]. In contrast, laypersons may perceive resuscitation efforts lasting > 30 min as excessively long. Although we did not investigate the reason behind this preference, this discrepancy suggests that clinicians should be attentive to family perceptions, especially when resuscitation continues beyond 30 min in pediatric cases. Also, prior studies suggest that some families value the perception that “everything possible was done”, even with low survival likelihood [5, 6]. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating and underscore the need for further research.

This study has several limitations. First, the physician response rate was relatively low, raising the possibility of selection bias. Second, because we used a commercial contractor to recruit the layperson group, sampling bias cannot be completely excluded, despite the large national panel. Third, the study primarily surveyed laypersons in hypothetical scenarios rather than actual experiences; although the 10% of laypersons who had experienced resuscitation of a family member, showed similar trends, this limitation should be considered when interpreting the findings.
