Query complexity lower bounds for local list-decoding and hard-core predicates (even for small rate and huge lists) A preliminary version of this manuscript appeared in the proceedings of ITCS 2021 [ RSV21 ] .
Noga Ron-Zewi, Ronen Shaltiel, Nithin Varma

Abstract

We prove lower bounds on q q , that apply even if L L is huge (say L = 2 k 0.9 L=2^{k^{0.9}} ) and the rate of Enc \operatorname{Enc} is small (meaning that n ≥ 2 k n\geq 2^{k} ): • For ϵ ≥ 1 / k ν \epsilon\geq 1/k^{\nu} for some universal constant 0 < ν < 1 0<\nu<1 , we prove a lower bound of q = Ω ⁡ ( log ⁡ ( 1 / δ ) ϵ 2 ) q=\Omega(\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\epsilon^{2}}) , where δ \delta is the error probability of the local list-decoder. This bound is tight as there is a matching upper bound by Goldreich and Levin (STOC 1989) of q = O ⁡ ( log ⁡ ( 1 / δ ) ϵ 2 ) q=O(\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\epsilon^{2}}) for the Hadamard code (which has n = 2 k n=2^{k} ). Introduction

We prove limitations on local list-decoding algorithms and on reductions establishing hard-core predicates.

1.1
Locally list-decodable codes

List-decodable codes are a natural extension of (uniquely decodable) error-correcting codes, as it allows (list) decoding for error regimes where unique decoding is impossible. This is an extensively studied area; see
[
Gur06
]
for a survey. In this paper, we will be interested in list-decoding of binary codes.

Definition 1.1

(List-decodable code)
.

For a function
\operatorname{Enc}:\{0,1\}^{k}\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{n}
, and
w\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, we define

\operatorname{List}^{\operatorname{Enc}}_{\alpha}(w)=\left\{{m\in\{0,1\}^{k}:\mathsf{dist}(\operatorname{Enc}(m),w)\leq\alpha}\right\}.

We say that
\operatorname{Enc}
is
(\alpha,L)
-list-decodable if for every
w\in\{0,1\}^{n}
,
|\operatorname{List}^{\operatorname{Enc}}_{\alpha}(w)|\leq L
.

The task of
algorithmic
list-decoding is to produce the list
\operatorname{List}^{\operatorname{Enc}}_{\alpha}(w)
on input
w\in\{0,1\}^{n}
.

Local
unique decoding algorithms are algorithms that given an index
i\in[k]
, make few oracle queries to
w
, and reproduce the bit
m_{i}
(with high probability over the choice of their random coins), where
m
is such that

\operatorname{Enc}(m)
is the unique codeword close to
w
. This notion of
local decoding
has many connections and applications in computer science and mathematics; see
[
Yek12
]
for a survey.

We will be interested in
local
list-decoding algorithms
that receive oracle access to a received word
w\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, as well as inputs
i\in[k]
and
j\in[L]
. Informally, we will require that for every
m\in\operatorname{List}^{\operatorname{Enc}}_{\alpha}(w)
, with high probability, there exists a
j\in[L]
such that for every
i\in[k]
, when the decoder
\operatorname{Dec}
receives oracle access to
w
and inputs
i,j
, it produces
m_{i}
with high probability over its choice of random coins.
Local list decoding algorithms have many applications in theoretical computer science, for example in cryptography
[
GL89
]
, learning theory
[
KM93
]
, average-to-worst-case reductions
[
Lip90
]
, and hardness amplification
[
BFNW93
,
STV01
]
. More formally, it is defined as follows.

Definition 1.2

(Randomized local computation)
.

We say that a procedure
P(i,R)

locally computes
a string
m\in\{0,1\}^{k}
with error
\delta
, if for every
i\in[k]
,
\Pr[P(i,R)=m_{i}]\geq 1-\delta
(where the probability is over a uniform choice of the “string of random coins”
R
).

The definition of local list-decoders considers an algorithmic scenario that works in two steps:

•

At the first step (which can be thought of as a preprocessing step) the local list-decoder
\operatorname{Dec}
is given oracle access to
w
and an index
j\in[L]
. It tosses random coins (which we denote by
r^{\operatorname{shared}}
).

•

At the second step, the decoder receives the additional index
i\in[k]
, and tosses additional coins
r
.

•

It is required that for every
w\in\{0,1\}^{n}
and
m\in\operatorname{List}^{\operatorname{Enc}}_{\alpha}(w)
, with probability at least
2/3
over the choice of the shared coins
r^{\operatorname{shared}}
, there exists
j\in[L]
such that when the local list-decoder receives
j
, it locally computes
m
(using its “non-shared” coins
r
). The definition uses two types of random coins because the coins
r^{\operatorname{shared}}
are “shared” between different choices of
i\in[k]
and allow different
i
’s to “coordinate”. The coins
r
, are chosen independently for different choices of
i\in[k]
.

This is formally stated in the next definition.

Definition 1.3

(Local list-decoder)
.

Let
\operatorname{Enc}:\{0,1\}^{k}\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{n}
be a function. An
(\alpha,L,q,\delta)
-local list-decoder (LLD)
for
\operatorname{Enc}
is an oracle procedure
\operatorname{Dec}^{(\cdot)}
that receives oracle access to a word
w\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, and makes at most
q
calls to the oracle. The procedure
\operatorname{Dec}
also receives inputs:

•

i\in[k]
: The index of the symbol that it needs to decode.

•

j\in[L]
: An index to the list.

•

Two strings
r^{\operatorname{shared}},r
that are used as random coins.

It is required that for every
w\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, and for every
m\in\operatorname{List}^{\operatorname{Enc}}_{\alpha}(w)
, with probability at least
2/3
over choosing a uniform string
r^{\operatorname{shared}}
, there exists
j\in[L]
such that the procedure

P_{w,j,r^{\operatorname{shared}}}(i,r)=\operatorname{Dec}^{w}(i,j,r^{\operatorname{shared}},r)

locally computes
m
with error
\delta
. If we omit
\delta
, then we mean
\delta=1/3
.

Remark 1.4

(On the generality of Definition
1.3
)
.

The goal of this paper is to prove lower bounds on local list-decoders, and so, making local list-decoders as general as possible, makes our results stronger. We now comment on the generality of Definition
1.3
.

•

In Definition
1.3
we do not require that
L=|\operatorname{List}^{\operatorname{Enc}}_{\alpha}(w)|
, and allow the local list-decoder to use a larger
L
. This means that on a given
w
, there may be many choices of
j\in[L]
such that the procedure
P_{w,j,r^{\operatorname{shared}}}(i,r)=\operatorname{Dec}^{w}(i,j,r^{\operatorname{shared}},r)
locally computes messages
m\not\in\operatorname{List}^{\operatorname{Enc}}_{\alpha}(w)
.

•

In Definition
1.3
we do not place any restriction on the number of random coins used by the local list-decoder, making the task of local list-decoding easier.

•

We allow
\operatorname{Dec}
to make
adaptive
queries to its oracle.

•

We are only interested in the total number of queries made by the combination of the two steps. It should be noted that w.l.o.g., a local list-decoder can defer all its queries to the second step (namely, after it receives the input
i
), and so, this definition captures local list-decoding algorithms which make queries to the oracle at both steps.

•

To the best of our knowledge, all known local list-decoders in the literature are of the form presented in Definition
1.3
.

1.1.1
Lower bounds on the query complexity of local list-decoders

In this paper we prove lower bounds on the number of queries
q
of
(\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon,L,q,\delta)
-local list-decoders. Our goal is to show that the number of queries
q
has to be large, when
\epsilon
is
small
.
Our lower bounds apply even if the size of the list
L
is huge and approaches
2^{k}
(note that a local list-decoder can trivially achieve
L=2^{k}
with a list of all messages). Our lower bounds also apply even if the rate of the code is very small, and
n\geq 2^{k}
.

We remark that this parameter regime is very different than the one studied in lower bounds on the number of queries of local decoders for
uniquely
decodable codes (that is, for
L=1
). By the Plotkin bound, uniquely decodable codes cannot have
\epsilon<\frac{1}{4}
, and so, the main focus in uniquely decodable codes is to show that local decoders for codes with “good rate” and “large”
\epsilon=\Omega(1)
, must make many queries. In contrast, we are interested in the case where
\epsilon
is small, and want to prove lower bounds that apply to huge lists and small rate.

Lower bounds for large
\epsilon
.

Our first result is a tight lower bound of
q=\Omega(\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\epsilon^{2}})
on the number of queries, assuming
\epsilon
is sufficiently large, namely
\epsilon\geq\frac{1}{k^{\nu}}
for some universal constant
0<\nu\leq 1
.

Theorem 1.5

(Tight lower bounds for large
\epsilon
)
.

There exists a universal constant
\nu>0
such that for any
L\leq 2^{k^{0.9}}
,
\epsilon\in(k^{-\nu},\frac{1}{4})
, and
\delta\in(k^{-\nu},\frac{1}{3})
, we have that
every
(\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon,L,q,\delta)
-local list-decoder for
\operatorname{Enc}:\{0,1\}^{k}\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{n}
must have
q=\Omega(\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\epsilon^{2}})
.

Theorem
1.5
is tight in the sense that the Hadamard code (which has length
n=2^{k}
) has
(\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon,L=O(1/\epsilon^{2}),q=O(\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\epsilon^{2}}),\delta)
local list-decoders
[
GL89
]
.
In fact, the Hadamard code was the motivation for this research, and is a running example in this paper.

Our results show that even if we allow list sizes
L
which approach
2^{k}
, it is impossible to reduce the number of queries for the Hadamard code.
Our results also show that even if we are willing to allow very small rate (
n\geq 2^{k}
), and huge list sizes, it is impossible to have codes whose local list-decoders make fewer queries than the local list-decoders for the Hadamard code.

Comparison to previous work.

Theorem
1.5
improves and extends an earlier work by Grinberg, Shaltiel and Viola
[
GSV18
]
that gave the same bound of
q=\Omega(\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\epsilon^{2}})
for a more limited parameter regime: Specifically, in
[
GSV18
]
, for the lower bound to hold, it is also required that
n\leq 2^{k^{\gamma}}
, for some universal constant
\gamma>0
, and that the total number of coins tossed by the local list-decoder is less than
k^{\gamma}-\log L
(which in particular implies that
L\leq 2^{k^{\gamma}}
).
2
2

2

The work of
[
GSV18
]
is concerned with proving lower bounds on the number of queries of “nonuniform reductions for hardness amplification”
[
Vio06
,
SV10
,
AS11
,
GSV18
]
. As explained in
[
Vio06
,
SV10
,
AS11
,
GSV18
]
such lower bounds translate into lower bounds on local list-decoders, by “trading” the random coins of a local list-decoder for “nonuniform advice” for the reduction, and proving a lower bound on the number of queries made by the reduction.
We stress that because of these two limitations, the lower bounds of
[
GSV18
]
do not apply to the Hadamard code and other low rate codes.

Extensions to large alphabet and erasures.

The scenario that we consider in Theorem
1.5
has
binary
alphabet, and decoding from
errors
. We remark that in the case of large alphabets, or decoding from erasures, there are local list-decoders which achieve
q=O(\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\epsilon})
(which is smaller than what is possible for binary alphabet and decoding from errors), as was shown for the case of Hadamard codes and erasures in
[
RRV18
]
, and for large alphabets in
[
IJKW10
]
.
Our results can be extended to give a matching lower bound of
q=\Omega(\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\epsilon})
for decoding from erasures (for any alphabet size), and also the same lower bound on decoding from errors for any alphabet size.

Lower bounds for small
\epsilon
.

The best bound on
q
that Theorem
1.5
(as well as the aforementioned lower bounds of
[
GSV18
]
) can give is
q\geq k^{\nu}
for a universal constant
\nu>0
. The next theorem shows that even for small
\epsilon<1/k
, we can obtain a lower bound on
q
which is polynomial in
1/\epsilon
.

Theorem 1.6

(Tight lower bounds for small
\epsilon
)
.

There exist universal constants
\beta>0
and
a,c>1
such that for any
L\leq\beta\cdot 2^{k}
,
\epsilon\in(\frac{a}{\sqrt{n}},\frac{1}{4})
, and
\delta<\frac{1}{3}
and we have that
every
(\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon,L,q,\delta)
-local list-decoder for
\operatorname{Enc}:\{0,1\}^{k}\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{n}
must have
q\geq\frac{1}{c\sqrt{\epsilon}\log k}-\log L
.

Note that for sufficiently small
\epsilon=1/(\log k)^{\omega(1)}
, we get
q=\Omega(\frac{1}{\epsilon^{1/2-o(1)}})
. It follows that together, Theorems
1.5
and Theorem
1.6
give a lower bound of
q=\Omega(\frac{1}{\epsilon^{1/2-o(1)}})
that applies to every choice of
\epsilon\geq\Omega(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}})
. To the best of our knowledge, Theorem
1.6
is the first lower bound on local list-decoders that is able to prove a lower bound of
q\geq k
(and note that this is what we should expect when
\epsilon<\frac{1}{k})
.
We also remark that the requirement that
\epsilon
is not too small compared to
n
(as is made in Theorem
1.6
) is required (as we cannot prove lower bounds on the number of queries in case
\epsilon<\frac{1}{n}
).

Goldreich and Levin
[
GL89
]
showed that locally list-decodable codes with small
\epsilon<1/k
can be used to give constructions of hard-core predicates. We explain this connection in the next section.

1.2
Hard-core predicates

The celebrated Goldreich-Levin theorem
[
GL89
]
considers the following scenario: There is a computational task where the required output is
non-Boolean
and is hard to compute on average. We would like to obtain a
hard-core predicate
, which is a
Boolean
value that is hard to compute on average.

The Goldreich-Levin theorem gives a solution to this problem, and in retrospect, the theorem can also be viewed as a
(\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon,L^{\operatorname{Had}}=O(\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}),q^{\operatorname{Had}}=O(\frac{k}{\epsilon^{2}}),\delta=\frac{1}{2k})
-local list-decoder for the Hadamard code, defined by:
\operatorname{Enc}^{\operatorname{Had}}:\{0,1\}^{k}\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{n=2^{k}}
, where for every
r\in\{0,1\}^{k}
,

\operatorname{Enc}^{\operatorname{Had}}(x)_{r}=\left(\sum_{i\in[k]}x_{i}\cdot r_{i}\right)\mod 2.

In retrospect, the Goldreich-Levin theorem can also be seen as showing that
any
locally list-decodable code with suitable parameters can be used to produce hard-core predicates.

We consider two scenarios for the Goldreich-Levin theorem depending on whether we want to extract a hard-core bit from a function
g:\{0,1\}^{\ell}\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{\ell}
that is
hard to compute
on a random input, or to extract a hard-core bit from a one-way function
f:\{0,1\}^{\ell}\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{\ell}
that is
hard to invert
on a random output.

1.2.1
Functions that are hard to compute

Here the goal is to transform a
non-Boolean function

g
that is hard to compute on a random input, into a
predicate

g^{\operatorname{pred}}
that is hard to compute on a random input. More precisely:

•

Assumption:
There is a non-Boolean function that is hard to compute with probability
\rho
.

Namely, a function
g:\{0,1\}^{\ell}\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{\ell}
such that for every circuit
C
of size
s
,

\Pr_{x\leftarrow U_{\ell}}[C(x)=g(x)]\leq\rho.

•

Conclusion:
There is a predicate
g^{\operatorname{pred}}:\{0,1\}^{\ell^{\prime}}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
that is hard to compute with probability
\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon
.
Namely, for every circuit
C^{\prime}
of size
s^{\prime}
,

\Pr_{x\leftarrow U_{\ell^{\prime}}}[C^{\prime}(x)=g^{\operatorname{pred}}(x)]\leq\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon.

•

Requirements:
The goal is to show that for every
g
, there exists a function
g^{\operatorname{pred}}
with as small an
\epsilon
as possible, without significant losses in the other parameters (meaning that
s^{\prime}
is not much smaller than
s
, and
\ell^{\prime}
is not much larger than
\ell
).

The Goldreich-Levin theorem for this setting can be expressed as follows.

Theorem 1.7

(Goldreich-Levin for functions that are hard to compute
[
GL89
]
)
.

For a function
g:\{0,1\}^{\ell}\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{\ell}
, define
g^{\operatorname{pred}}:\{0,1\}^{\ell^{\prime}=2\ell}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
by
g^{\operatorname{pred}}(x,r)=\operatorname{Enc}^{\operatorname{Had}}(g(x))_{r}
, and
\rho=\frac{\epsilon}{2\cdot L^{\operatorname{Had}}}={\operatorname{poly}}(\epsilon)
. If
for every circuit
C
of size
s
,

\Pr_{x\leftarrow U_{\ell}}[C(x)=g(x)]\leq\rho,

then for every circuit
C^{\prime}
of size
s^{\prime}=\frac{s}{q^{\operatorname{Had}}\cdot{\operatorname{poly}}(\ell)}=s\cdot{\operatorname{poly}}(\frac{\epsilon}{\ell})
,

\Pr_{x\leftarrow U_{2\ell}}[C^{\prime}(x)=g^{\operatorname{pred}}(x)]\leq\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon.

The Hadamard code can be replaced by any locally list-decodable code with list size
L
for decoding from radius
\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon
, with
q
queries for
\delta=1/(2k)
. For such a code (assuming also that the local list-decoder can be computed efficiently) one gets the same behavior. Specifically, if the initial function is sufficiently hard and
\rho=\frac{\epsilon}{2L}
, then the Boolean target function is hard to compute, up to
\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon
for circuits of size roughly
s^{\prime}=s/q
.

Is it possible to improve the Goldreich-Levin theorem for
\rho\ll 1/s
?

Suppose that we are given a function
g:\{0,1\}^{\ell}\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{\ell}
that is hard to compute for circuits of size
s={\operatorname{poly}}(\ell)
, with success, say,
\rho=1/2^{\sqrt{\ell}}
. When applying Theorem
1.7
, we gain nothing compared to the case that
\rho=1/{\operatorname{poly}}(\ell)
. In both cases, we can obtain
\epsilon=1/{\operatorname{poly}}(\ell)
, but not smaller! (Since otherwise
s^{\prime}=s\cdot{\operatorname{poly}}(\epsilon/\ell)
is smaller than
1
and the result is meaningless).

This is disappointing, as we may have expected to obtain
\epsilon\approx\rho=1/2^{\sqrt{\ell}}
, or at least, to gain over the much weaker assumption that
\rho=1/{\operatorname{poly}}(\ell)
. This leads to the following open problem:

Open problem 1.8

(Improve Goldreich-Levin for functions that are hard to compute)
.

Suppose we are given a function
g:\{0,1\}^{\ell}\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{\ell}
such that circuits
C
of size
s={\operatorname{poly}}(\ell)
cannot compute
g
with success
\rho=1/2^{\sqrt{\ell}}
. Is it possible to convert
g
into a predicate with hardness
\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon
for
\epsilon=1/\ell^{\omega(1)}
?

This is not possible to achieve using the Hadamard code, because the number of queries is
q\geq 1/\epsilon
, and Theorem
1.7
requires
s\geq s^{\prime}\cdot q\geq q\geq 1/\epsilon
, which dictates that
\epsilon\geq 1/s
.

Note that when
\rho
is small, we can afford list-decodable codes with huge list sizes of
L\approx 1/\rho
. Motivated by this observation, we can ask the following question:

Is it possible to solve Open Problem
1.8
by substituting the Hadamard code with a better code? Specifically, is it possible for local list-decoders to have
q=\frac{1}{\epsilon^{o(1)}}
if allowed to use
huge
lists of size say
2^{\sqrt{k}}
, approaching the trivial bound
2^{k}
? (Note that in the Hadamard code, the list size used is
{\operatorname{poly}}(1/\epsilon)={\operatorname{poly}}(k)
which is exponentially smaller).

Theorem
1.6
shows
that it is impossible to solve Open Problem
1.8
by replacing the Hadamard code with a different locally list-decodable code.

The natural next question is whether we can use other techniques (not necessarily local list-decoding) to achieve the goal stated above. In this paper, we show that this cannot be done by
black-box techniques
:

Informal Theorem 1.9

(Black-box impossibility result for functions that are hard to compute)
.

If
\rho\geq 2^{-\ell/5}
and
\epsilon=\frac{1}{s^{\omega(1)}}
,
then there does not exist a map
that converts a function
g
into a function
g^{\operatorname{pred}}
together with a black-box reduction showing that
g^{\operatorname{pred}}
is a hard-core predicate for
g
.

The parameters achieved in Theorem
1.9
rule out black-box proofs in which
\epsilon=\frac{1}{s^{\omega(1)}}
, not only for
s={\operatorname{poly}}(\ell)
and
\rho=2^{-\sqrt{\ell}}
(as in Open Problem
1.8
) but also for
\rho=2^{-\Omega(\ell)}
, and allowing
s
as large as
2^{\ell}
.

The precise statement of Theorem
1.9
is stated in Theorem
5.2
, and the precise model is explained in Section
5.1
.
To the best of our knowledge, this is the first result of this kind, that shows black-box impossibility results for Open Problem
1.8
. Moreover, we believe that the model that we introduce in Section
5.1
is very general and captures all known black-box techniques. In particular, our model allows the reduction to introduce nonuniformity when converting an adversary
C^{\prime}
that breaks
g^{\operatorname{pred}}
into an adversary
C
that breaks
g
. See discussion in Remark
1.14
.

1.2.2
Functions that are hard to invert

Here the goal is to transform a one-way function
f
into a new one-way function
f^{\operatorname{newOWF}}
and a
predicate

f^{\operatorname{pred}}
such that it is hard to compute
f^{\operatorname{pred}}(x)
given
f^{\operatorname{newOWF}}(x)
. More precisely:

•

Assumption:
There is a one-way function that is hard to invert with probability
\rho
.

Namely, a function
f:\{0,1\}^{\ell}\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{\ell}
such that for every circuit
C
of size
s
,

\Pr_{x\leftarrow U_{\ell}}[C(f(x))\in f^{-1}(f(x))]\leq\rho.

•

Conclusion:
There is a one-way function
f^{\operatorname{newOWF}}:\{0,1\}^{\ell^{\prime}}\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{\ell^{\prime}}
, and a predicate
f^{\operatorname{pred}}:\{0,1\}^{\ell^{\prime}}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
, such that it is hard to predict
f^{\operatorname{pred}}(x)
with advantage
\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon
, when given access to
f^{\operatorname{newOWF}}(x)
.
Namely, for every circuit
C^{\prime}
of size
s^{\prime}
,

\Pr_{x\leftarrow U_{\ell^{\prime}}}[C^{\prime}(f^{\operatorname{newOWF}}(x))=f^{\operatorname{pred}}(x)]\leq\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon.

•

Requirements:
The goal is to show that for every
f
, there exist functions
f^{\operatorname{newOWF}},f^{\operatorname{pred}}
with as small an
\epsilon
as possible, without significant losses in the other parameters (meaning that
s^{\prime}
is not much smaller than
s
, and
\ell^{\prime}
is not much larger than
\ell
).

The Goldreich-Levin theorem for this setting can be expressed as follows.

Theorem 1.10

(Goldreich-Levin for functions that are hard to invert
[
GL89
]
)
.

For a function
f:\{0,1\}^{\ell}\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{\ell}
, define
f^{\operatorname{newOWF}}:\{0,1\}^{2\ell}\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{2\ell}
by
f^{\operatorname{newOWF}}(x,r)=(f(x),r)
,
f^{\operatorname{pred}}:\{0,1\}^{2\ell}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
by
f^{\operatorname{pred}}(x,r)=\operatorname{Enc}^{\operatorname{Had}}(x)_{r}
, and
\rho=\frac{\epsilon}{2\cdot L^{\operatorname{Had}}}={\operatorname{poly}}(\epsilon)
. If
for every circuit
C
of size
s
,

\Pr_{x\leftarrow U_{\ell}}[C(f(x))\in f^{-1}(f(x))]\leq\rho,

then for every circuit
C^{\prime}
of size
s^{\prime}=\frac{s}{q^{\operatorname{Had}}\cdot{\operatorname{poly}}(\ell)}=s\cdot{\operatorname{poly}}(\frac{\epsilon}{\ell})
,

\Pr_{x\leftarrow U_{2\ell}}[C^{\prime}(f^{\operatorname{newOWF}}(x))=f^{\operatorname{pred}}(x)]\leq\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon.

Remark 1.11
.

The problem of obtaining a hard-core predicate for one-way functions is interesting only if an unbounded adversary
\phi:\{0,1\}^{\ell^{\prime}}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
can predict
f^{\operatorname{pred}}(x)
when given
f^{\operatorname{newOWF}}(x)
as input. If this is not required, then one can take
\ell^{\prime}=\ell+1
,
f^{\operatorname{pred}}(x)=x_{1}
, and
f^{\operatorname{newOWF}}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+1})=f(x_{2},\ldots,x_{n+1})
. However, this is trivial, and is not useful in applications. Therefore, when considering this problem, we will assume that there exists such a
\phi:\{0,1\}^{\ell^{\prime}}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
.

A natural example is the case where the original one-way function
f
and the constructed function
f^{\operatorname{newOWF}}
are one-way permutations.
In fact, in the case that
f,f^{\operatorname{newOWF}}(x)
are permutations, the setup of “functions that are hard to invert” can be seen as a special case of the setup of “functions that are hard to compute” by taking
g=f^{-1}
, and
g^{\operatorname{pred}}(y)=f^{\operatorname{pred}}((f^{\operatorname{newOWF}})^{-1}(y))
.

We point out that, in this setting, the circuit
C^{\prime}
that is trying to invert
f
(that is, to compute
g
) has an advantage over its counterpart in the setup of “functions that are hard to compute”: It can use the efficient algorithm that computes the “forward direction” of
f
, when trying to invert
f
. In terms of
g
, this means that the circuit
C^{\prime}
can compute
g^{-1}
for free. This distinction is explained in Section
5.2
.

Is it possible to improve the Goldreich-Levin theorem for
\rho\ll 1/s
?

The same problem that we saw with functions that are hard to compute, also shows up in the setup of functions that are hard to invert.
Suppose that we are given a function
f:\{0,1\}^{\ell}\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{\ell}
that is hard to invert for circuits of size
s={\operatorname{poly}}(\ell)
with success, say,
\rho=1/2^{\sqrt{\ell}}
. When applying Theorem
1.10
, we gain nothing compared to the case that
\rho=1/{\operatorname{poly}}(\ell)
. In both cases, we can obtain
\epsilon=1/{\operatorname{poly}}(\ell)
, but not smaller! This is expressed in the next open problem:

Open problem 1.12

(Improve Goldreich-Levin for functions that are hard to invert)
.

Suppose we are given a one-way function
f:\{0,1\}^{\ell}\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{\ell}
such that circuits
C
of size
s={\operatorname{poly}}(\ell)
cannot invert
f
with success
\rho=1/2^{\sqrt{\ell}}
. Is it possible to obtain a hard-core predicate
f^{\operatorname{pred}}
with hardness
\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon
for
\epsilon=1/\ell^{\omega(1)}
for some choice of one-way function
f^{\operatorname{newOWF}}
?

In this paper, we show that this cannot be done by
black-box techniques
. The formulation of Theorem
1.13
below, is very similar to that of Theorem
1.9
with some small modification in the parameters.

Informal Theorem 1.13

(Black-box impossibility result for functions that are hard to invert)
.

If
\rho\geq 2^{-\ell/5}
,
s=2^{o(\ell)}
, and
\epsilon=\frac{1}{s^{\omega(1)}}
, then there does not exist
a map that converts a function
f
into functions
f^{\operatorname{newOWF}},f^{\operatorname{pred}}
together with a black-box reduction showing that
f^{\operatorname{pred}}
is a hard-core predicate for
f^{\operatorname{newOWF}}
.

The precise statement of Theorem
1.13
is stated in Theorem
5.11
, and the precise model is explained in Section
5.2
.
To the best of our knowledge, this is the first result of this kind, that shows black-box impossibility results for Open Problem
1.12
. Moreover,
we believe that the model that we introduce in Section
5.2
is very general, and captures all known black-box techniques. In particular, our model allows the reduction to compute the easy direction of the function
f
, and to introduce nonuniformity when converting an adversary
C^{\prime}
that breaks
f^{\operatorname{pred}}
into an adversary
C
that breaks
f
.

Remark 1.14

(The model we use for black-box proofs)
.

Many different models of “black-box techniques” for cryptographic primitives were studied in the literature and the reader is referred to
[
RTV04
]
for a discussion and a taxonomy.
The model for “black-box techniques” that we use is described in detail in Section
5
. The notion that we use is more general than the notion of “fully-BB reductions” discussed in
[
RTV04
]
, and incomparable to the other notions discussed in
[
RTV04
]
, specifically:

•

We require that there is a “transformation map” which given any function
f
produces functions
f^{\operatorname{newOWF}}
and
f^{\operatorname{pred}}
, however, unlike the notions studied in
[
RTV04
]
, we do not make the requirement that this transformation map can be efficiently computed.

•

We require that there is a reduction
\operatorname{Red}
such that for any
f
, and for every adversary
C^{\prime}
(not necessarily efficient) breaking the security of
f^{\operatorname{pred}}
,
\operatorname{Red}^{f,C^{\prime}}
can be used to invert
f
. So far, this is similar in spirit to the notion of “fully-BB reduction” defined in
[
RTV04
]
. However, we give reductions more power than is given in
[
RTV04
]
in the case of fully-BB reductions. Specifically, we also allow
\operatorname{Red}
to introduce nonuniformity (that could depend on
C^{\prime}
and
f
). More formally, for every adversary
C^{\prime}
that breaks the security of
f^{\operatorname{pred}}
, we require that there exists a short nonuniform advice string
\alpha
such that
\operatorname{Red}^{C^{\prime},f}(\cdot,\alpha)
inverts
f
.

1.3
More related work

Lower bounds on the number of queries of local decoders for
uniquely
decodable codes.

In this paper, we prove lower bounds on the number of queries of local list-decoders.
There is a long line of work that is concerned with proving lower bounds on the number of queries of uniquely decodable codes. As we have explained in Section
1.1.1
, the parameter regime considered in the setting of uniquely decodable codes is very different than the parameter regime we consider here
[
Yek12
]
.

Lower bounds on nonuniform black-box reductions for hardness amplification.

A problem that is closely related to proving lower bounds on the number of queries of local list-decoders is the problem of proving lower bounds on the number of queries of nonuniform black-box reductions for hardness amplification. We have already discussed this line of work
[
Vio06
,
SV10
,
AS11
,
GSV18
,
Sha20
]
in Section
1.1.1
.

Lower bounds on such reductions can be translated to lower bounds on local list-decoders (as long as the number of coins tossed by the local list-decoders is small). We remark that for the purpose of hardness amplification, it does not make sense to take codes with small rate (namely, codes with
n=2^{k^{\Omega(1)}}
). The focus of Theorem
1.5
is to handle such codes.
Additionally, when using codes for hardness amplification, it does not make sense to take
\epsilon<1/k
(or even
\epsilon<1/\sqrt{k}
). In contrast, the parameter regime considered in Theorem
1.6
focuses on small
\epsilon
.

Motivated by hardness amplification, there is also a related line of work studying limitations on the complexity of local list decoders (and specifically, whether these decoders need to compute the majority function)
[
Vio06
,
SV10
,
GR08
,
AS11
,
GSV18
,
Sha20
]
.
Another approach to prove limitations on hardness amplification is to show that assuming certain cryptographic assumptions, hardness amplification that is significantly better than what is currently known is impossible, see e.g.,
[
DJMW12
]
for a discussion.

Other improvements of the Goldreich-Levin theorem.

In this paper, we are interested in whether the Goldreich-Levin theorem can be improved. Specifically, we are interested in improvements where, when the original function has hardness
\rho=2^{-\Omega(\ell)}
for polynomial size circuits, then the hard-core predicate has hardness
\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon
for
\epsilon=\ell^{-\omega(1)}
.
We remark that there are other aspects of the Goldreich-Levin theorem that one may want to improve.

•

When given an initial non-Boolean function on
\ell
bits, the Goldreich-Levin theorem produces a hard-core predicate on
\ell^{\prime}=2\ell
bits. It is possible to make
\ell^{\prime}
smaller (specifically,
\ell^{\prime}=\ell+O(\log(1/\epsilon))
by using other locally list-decodable codes instead of Hadamard. Our limitations apply to
any
construction (even one that is not based on codes) and in particular also for such improvements.

•

It is sometimes desirable to produce many hard-core bits (instead of the single hard-core bit that is obtained by a hard-core predicate). This can be achieved by using “extractor codes” with a suitable local list-decoding algorithm. The reader is referred to
[
TZ04
]
for more details. Once again, our limitations obviously apply also for the case of producing many hard-core bits.

Open problems and subsequent work

We end this section with a couple of interesting open problems for future research.

1.

Unlike Theorem
1.5
(that handles large
\epsilon
), Theorem
1.6
(that handles small
\epsilon
) does not achieve a bound of
q=\Omega(\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\epsilon^{2}})
, and only achieves a bound of
\Omega(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\epsilon}})
. A natural open problem is to improve the bound on
q
for small
\epsilon
to match the bound for large
\epsilon
.

A step in this direction was made in the subsequent work of Shaltiel and Viola
[
SV22
]
. They show that if
L\leq 2^{\frac{k}{20}}
,
\delta<\frac{1}{3}
and
n=\Omega(\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}})
(which are slightly stronger conditions than the one we require in Theorem
1.6
) then the bound on the number of queries can be improved from
q=\Omega(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\epsilon}\cdot\log k})-O(\log L)
to
q=\Omega(\frac{1}{\epsilon\cdot\log k})
.

The improvement of
[
SV22
]
builds on the methodology used in this paper (namely, converting a local list decoder into a depth 3 circuit, and then employing a bound on depth 3 circuits to obtain a lower bound on the number of queries). However, rather than reducing to a depth 3 circuit for the coin problem (as we do in this paper) Shaltiel and Viola
[
SV22
]
note that the reduction used in this paper gives a depth 3 circuit for a more specific problem. They then show a quantitatively stronger lower bound for this specific problem, and this results in the improved bound on the number of queries.

2.

In the case of large
\epsilon
, Theorem
1.5
can be extended to handle local list-decoding from
erasures
, and gives a lower bound of
q=\Omega(\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\epsilon})
on the number of queries of local list-decoders that decode from a
1-\epsilon
fraction of erasures. We do not see how to extend the proof of Theorem
1.6
to erasures.

3.

The model of black-box proofs that we introduce in Section
5
is quite general, and to the best of our knowledge, covers all known proofs in the literature on hard-core predicates for general one-way functions. Is it possible to circumvent the black-box limitations and answer open problems
1.8
and
1.12
for
specific candidates
for one-way functions?
More generally, is it possible to come up with non-black-box techniques that circumvent the limitations?

Techniques

In this section we give a high level overview of our techniques.
Our approach builds on earlier work for proving lower bounds on the number of queries of reductions for hardness amplification

2.1
Local list-decoders on random noisy codewords

Following
[
Vio06
,
SV10
,
GSV18
]
, we will consider a scenario which we refer to as “random noisy codewords” in which a uniformly chosen message
m
is encoded, and the encoding is corrupted by a binary symmetric channel.

Definition 2.1

(Binary symmetric channels)
.

Let
\operatorname{BSC}^{n}_{p}
be the experiment in which a string
Z\in\{0,1\}^{n}
is sampled, where
Z=Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n}
is composed of i.i.d. bits, such that for every
i\in[n]
,
\Pr[Z_{i}=1]=p
.

Definition 2.2

(Random noisy codewords)
.

Given a function
\operatorname{Enc}:\{0,1\}^{k}\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{n}
and
p>0
we consider the following experiment (which we denote by
\operatorname{RNSY}^{\operatorname{Enc}}_{p}
):

•

A message
m\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{k}
is chosen uniformly.

•

A noise string
z\leftarrow\operatorname{BSC}^{n}_{p}
is chosen from a binary symmetric channel.

•

We define
w=\operatorname{Enc}(m)\oplus z
.

We use
(m,z,w)\leftarrow\operatorname{RNSY}^{\operatorname{Enc}}_{p}
to denote
m,z,w
which are sampled by this experiment. We omit
\operatorname{Enc}
if it is clear from the context.

Our goal is to prove lower bounds on the number of queries
q
of a
(\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon,L,q,\delta)
-local list-decoder
\operatorname{Dec}
for a code
\operatorname{Enc}:\{0,1\}^{k}\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{n}
. For this purpose, we will consider the experiment
\operatorname{RNSY}_{p}
for the values
p=\frac{1}{2}-2\epsilon
and
p=\frac{1}{2}
.

For
p=\frac{1}{2}-2\epsilon
, and
(m,z,w)\leftarrow\operatorname{RNSY}_{\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon}
, by a Chernoff bound, the relative Hamming weight of
z
is, with very high probability, less than
\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon
. This implies that
\mathsf{dist}(w,\operatorname{Enc}(m))\leq\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon
, meaning that
m\in\operatorname{List}_{\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon}^{\operatorname{Enc}}(w)
. It follows that there must exist
j\in[L]
such that when given input
j
, and oracle access to
w
, the decoder
\operatorname{Dec}
recovers the message
m
.

For
p=\frac{1}{2}
, and
(m,z,w)\leftarrow\operatorname{RNSY}_{\frac{1}{2}}
, the string
z
is uniformly distributed and independent of
m
. This means that
w=\operatorname{Enc}(m)\oplus z
is uniformly distributed and independent of
m
. Consequently, when
\operatorname{Dec}
is given oracle access to
w
, there is no information in
w
about the message
m
, and so, for every
j\in[L]
, the probability that
\operatorname{Dec}
recovers
m
when given input
j
and oracle access to
w
is exponentially small.

Loosely speaking, this means that
\operatorname{Dec}
can be used to distinguish
\operatorname{BSC}^{n}_{\frac{1}{2}-2\epsilon}
from
\operatorname{BSC}^{n}_{\frac{1}{2}}
. It is known that distinguishing these two distributions requires many queries. We state this informally below, and a formal statement appears in Lemma
3.2
.

Informal Theorem 2.3
.

Any function
T:\{0,1\}^{q}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
that distinguishes
\operatorname{BSC}^{q}_{\frac{1}{2}-2\epsilon}
from
\operatorname{BSC}^{q}_{\frac{1}{2}}
with advantage
4
4

4

We say that a function
T:\{0,1\}^{q}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
distinguishes a pair of distributions
B_{1}
,
B_{2}
over
\{0,1\}^{q}
with
advantage

\delta
if
\Pr_{x_{1}\leftarrow B_{1},x_{2}\leftarrow B_{2}}\left[T(x_{1})=T(x_{2})\right]\leq\frac{1}{2}+\delta
.

\delta
, must have
q=\Omega(\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\epsilon^{2}})
.

Thus, in order to prove a tight lower bound of
q=\Omega(\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\epsilon^{2}})
, it is sufficient to show how to convert a
(\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon,L,q,\delta)
-local list-decoder
\operatorname{Dec}
, into a function
T
that distinguishes
\operatorname{BSC}^{q}_{\frac{1}{2}-2\epsilon}
from
\operatorname{BSC}^{q}_{\frac{1}{2}}
with advantage
\delta
. Note that we can allow
T
to be a “randomized procedure” that tosses coins, as by an averaging argument, such a randomized procedure can be turned into a deterministic procedure.

2.2
Warmup: the case of unique decoding

Let us consider the case that
L=1
(that is unique decoding). We stress that this case is uninteresting, as by the Plotkin bound, it is impossible for nontrivial codes to be uniquely decodable for
\epsilon<\frac{1}{4}
, and so, there are no local decoders for
L=1
and
\epsilon<\frac{1}{4}
, regardless of the number of queries. Nevertheless, this case will serve as a warmup for the approach we use later.

Our goal is to convert
\operatorname{Dec}
into a randomized procedure
T:\{0,1\}^{q}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
that distinguishes
\operatorname{BSC}^{q}_{\frac{1}{2}-2\epsilon}
from
\operatorname{BSC}^{q}_{\frac{1}{2}}
.
The procedure
T
will work as follows: On input
z\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{q}
, we choose
m\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{k}
, and
i\leftarrow[k]
. We then run
\operatorname{Dec}
on input
i
, and when
\operatorname{Dec}
makes its
t
’th query
\ell_{t}\in[n]
to the oracle, we answer it by
\operatorname{Enc}(m)_{\ell_{t}}\oplus z_{t}
. That is, we answer as if
\operatorname{Dec}
is run with input
i
and oracle access to
w=\operatorname{Enc}(m)\oplus z
, for
z
chosen from a binary symmetric channel. The final output of
T
is whether
\operatorname{Dec}
reproduced
m_{i}
. This procedure
T
simulates
\operatorname{Dec}^{w}(i)
, and therefore distinguishes
\operatorname{BSC}^{q}_{\frac{1}{2}-2\epsilon}
from
\operatorname{BSC}^{q}_{\frac{1}{2}}
, implying the desired lower bound.

Both Theorem
1.5
and Theorem
1.6
will follow by modifying the basic approach to handle

. In the remainder of this section, we give a high level overview of the methods that we use. The formal section of this paper does not build on this high level overview, and readers can skip this high level overview and go directly to the formal section if they wish to.

2.3
Reducing to the coin problem for AC
0

We start with explaining the approach of proving Theorem
1.6
.
Consider a randomized procedure
C
that on input
z\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, chooses
m\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{k}
and prepares
w=\operatorname{Enc}(m)\oplus z
. The procedure then computes
\operatorname{Dec}^{w}(i,j)
for all choices of
i\in[k]
and
j\in[L]
and accepts if there exists a
j\in[L]
such that
\operatorname{Dec}^{w}(\cdot,j)
recovers
m
. By the same rationale as in Section
2.2
,
C
distinguishes
\operatorname{BSC}^{n}_{\frac{1}{2}-2\epsilon}
from
\operatorname{BSC}^{n}_{\frac{1}{2}}
. This does not seem helpful, because
C
receives
n
input bits, and we cannot use Theorem
2.3
to get a lower bound on
q
.

Inspired by a lower bound on the size of nondeterministic reductions for hardness amplification due to Applebaum et al.
[
AASY16
]
, we make the following observation:
The procedure
C
can be seen as
k\cdot L
computations (one for each choice of
i\in[k]
and
j\in[L]
) such that:

•

These
k\cdot L
computations can be run
in parallel
.

•

Once these computations are made, the final answer
C(z)
is computed by a constant-depth circuit.

•

Each of the
k\cdot L
computations makes
q
queries into
z
, and therefore can be simulated by a size
O(q\cdot 2^{q})
circuit of depth 2.

Overall, this means that we can implement
C
by a circuit of size
s={\operatorname{poly}}(k,L,2^{q})
and constant depth. (In fact, a careful implementation gives depth 3).

This is useful because there are lower bounds showing that small constant-depth circuits cannot solve the “coin problem”. Specifically, by the results of Cohen, Ganor and Raz
[
CGR14
]
circuits of size
s
and depth
d
cannot distinguish
\operatorname{BSC}^{n}_{\frac{1}{2}-2\epsilon}
from
\operatorname{BSC}^{n}_{\frac{1}{2}}
with constant advantage, unless
s\geq\mbox{exp}(\Omega(\frac{1}{\epsilon^{d-1}}))
.
5
5

5

These results of
[
CGR14
]
improve upon earlier work of Shaltiel and Viola
[
SV10
]
that gave slightly worse bound. These results are tight as shown by Limaye et al.
[
LSS+19
]
(that also extended the lower bound to hold for circuits that are also allowed to use parity gates).
This gives the bound stated in Theorem
1.6
.

We find it surprising that an
information theoretic
lower bound on the number of queries of local list-decoders is proven by considering concepts like constant-depth circuits from
circuit complexity
.

Extending the argument to lower bounds on hard-core predicates.

It turns out that this argument is quite versatile, and this is the approach that we use to prove Theorems
1.9
and
1.13
. Loosely speaking, in these theorems, we want to prove a lower bound on the number of queries made by a reduction that, when receiving oracle access to an adversary that breaks the hard-core predicate, is able to compute (or invert) the original function too well. Such lower bounds imply that such reductions do not produce small circuits when used in black-box proofs for hard-core predicates.

We will prove such lower bounds by showing that a reduction that makes
q
queries can be used to construct a circuit of size
s\approx 2^{q}
and constant depth that solves the coin problem. Interestingly, this argument crucially relies on the fact that constant-depth circuits
can
distinguish
\operatorname{BSC}^{n}_{\epsilon}
from
\operatorname{BSC}^{n}_{2\epsilon}
with size
{\operatorname{poly}}(n/\epsilon)
which follows from the classical results of Ajtai on constant depth circuits for approximate majority
[
Ajt83
]
.
6
6

6

The proof of Theorem
1.13
uses an additional versatility of the argument (which we express in the terminology of codes): The argument works even if the individual procedures that are run in parallel are allowed to have some limited access to the message
m
, as long as this does not enable them to recover
m
. This property is used to handle reductions in a cryptographic setup, where reductions have access to the easy direction of a one-way function.

2.4
Conditioning on a good
j

A disadvantage of the approach based on the coin problem is that at best, it can give lower bounds of
q=\Omega(1/\sqrt{\epsilon})
, and cannot give tight lower bounds of the form

which
does

Our approach builds on the earlier work of Grinberg, Shaltiel and Viola
[

]
that we surveyed in Section

.
When given a
(\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon,L,q,\delta)
-local list-decoder
\operatorname{Dec}
, we say that an index
j\in[L]
is
decoding
for
m,w
, if when
\operatorname{Dec}
is given oracle access to

and input

1-10\delta
over

\operatorname{Dec}^{w}(i,j)

We use a careful averaging argument to show that there exists an index

\operatorname{Dec}

(m,z,w)\leftarrow\operatorname{RNSY}_{\frac{1}{2}-2\epsilon}
,

(m,z,w)\leftarrow\operatorname{RNSY}_{\frac{1}{2}-2\epsilon}

conditioned
on the event
\left\{{\mbox{$j^{\prime}$ is decoding for $m,w$}}\right\}
.

We have made progress, because in the experiment
\operatorname{RNSY}^{\prime}_{\frac{1}{2}-2\epsilon}
there exists a unique
j^{\prime}
that is decoding, and so, when we implement the strategy explained in Section
2.2
we only need to consider this
single

j^{\prime}
, which intuitively means that our scenario is similar to the warmup scenario of unique decoding described in Section
2.2
.

The catch is that when choosing
(m,z,w)\leftarrow\operatorname{RNSY}^{\prime}_{\frac{1}{2}-2\epsilon}
, we no longer have that
z
is distributed like
\operatorname{BSC}^{n}_{\frac{1}{2}-2\epsilon}
(as the distribution of
z
may be skewed by conditioning on the event that
j^{\prime}
is decoding).

Shaltiel and Viola
[
SV10
]
(and later work
[
GSV18
,
Sha20
]
) developed tools to handle this scenario. Loosely speaking, using these tools, it is possible to show that a large number of messages
m
are “useful” in the sense that there exists an event
A_{m}
such that if we consider
(m,z,w)
that are chosen from
\operatorname{RNSY}^{\prime}_{\frac{1}{2}-2\epsilon}

conditioned
on
A_{m}
, then there exists a subset
B(m)\subseteq[n]
of small size
b
, such that
z|_{B(m)}
is fixed, and
z|_{[n]\setminus B(m)}
is distributed like
\operatorname{BSC}^{n-b}_{\frac{1}{2}-2\epsilon}
.

If the number of possible choices for sets
B(m)
is small, then by the pigeon-hole principle, there exists a fixed choice
B
that is good for a large number of useful messages
m
. This can be used to imitate the argument we used in the warmup, and prove a lower bound.
7
7

7

Loosely speaking, this is because for useful messages, in the conditioned experiment,
z
is distributed like
\operatorname{BSC}_{\frac{1}{2}-2\epsilon}
(except that some bits of
z
are fixed as a function of
m
). Furthermore, as there are many useful messages, the local list-decoder does not have enough information to correctly recover the message when given oracle access to
\operatorname{Enc}(m)\oplus\operatorname{BSC}^{n}_{\frac{1}{2}}=\operatorname{BSC}^{n}_{\frac{1}{2}}
.

Extending the argument to the case of small rate.

A difficulty, that prevented
[
GSV18
]
from allowing length as large as
n=2^{k}
,
is that
B(m)
is a subset of
[n]
, and so, even if
b=|B(m)|=1
, the number of possible choices for such sets is at least
n
. For the pigeon-hole principle argument above, we need that the number of messages (that is
2^{k}
) is much larger than the number of possible choices for
B(m)
(which is at least
n
). This means that one can only handle
n
which is sufficiently smaller than
2^{k}
, and this approach cannot apply to codes with small rate (such as the Hadamard code).

We show how to solve this problem, and prove lower bounds for small rate codes. From a high level, our approach can be explained as follows: We consider the distribution of
B(m)=\left\{{Y_{1}(m)<\ldots<Y_{b}(m)}\right\}
for a uniformly chosen useful
m
.
We first show that if all the
Y_{j}
’s have large min-entropy, then it is possible to prove a lower bound on
q
by reducing to Theorem
2.3
(the details of this are explained in the actual proof).

If on the other hand, one of the
Y_{j}
’s has low min-entropy, then we will restrict our attention to a subset of useful messages on which
Y_{j}
is fixed. Loosely speaking, this reduces
b
by one, while not reducing the number of useful messages by too much (because the low min-entropy condition says that the amount of information that
Y_{j}
carries on
m
is small).
In this trench warfare, in every iteration, we lose a fraction of useful messages, for the sake of decreasing
b
by one.
Thus, eventually, we either reach the situation that all the
Y_{j}
’s have large min-entropy, in which case we are done, or
we reach the situation where
B(m)
is fixed for all messages which we can also handle by the above.

We can withstand the losses and eventually win if
\epsilon
is sufficiently larger than
1/\sqrt{k}
.

4
Query complexity lower bounds for local list-decoding

In this section we prove Theorem
1.5
and Theorem
1.6
and provide lower bounds on the query complexity of local list-decoders.
In Section
4.1
, we introduce a relaxed concept that we call “approximate local list-decoders on noisy random codewords” (ARLLD) in which the local list-decoder is only required to recover a random message that was corrupted by a binary symmetric channel (meaning that the original message appears in the list of messages that are locally computed by the local list-decoder).
We then use a careful averaging argument to show that any local list-decoder (LLD) can be converted into an ARLLD with roughly the same parameters, and furthermore, the obtained ARLLD is
deterministic
.
This means that when proving Theorem
1.5
and Theorem
1.6
it is sufficient to consider ARLLDs, and these proofs appear in Sections
4.2
and
4.3
respectively.

4.1
Definition of approximate local list-decoders on noisy random codewords

Our goal is to prove lower bounds on the number of queries
q
of
(\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon,L,q,\delta)
-local list-decoders. We will show that it is sufficient to consider local list-decoders that need to perform an easier task. More specifically, we relax the task of a local list-decoder in the following ways:

•

The local list-decoder does not need to succeed on every
w\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, but only with not too small probability
over a “random noisy codeword” (as defined in Definition
2.2
) which is sampled by encoding a uniformly chosen message
m
, and hitting
\operatorname{Enc}(m)
with the noise generated by a binary symmetric channel, to obtain a word
w
.
It is required that with not too small probability over the choice of the message
m
and the random noise, there exists
j\in[L]
such that the local decoder with oracle access to
w
, and input
j
, recovers
m
.

•

The local decoder is
approximate
and is not required to recover
m_{i}
correctly on every
i\in[k]
. Instead, it is allowed to err on a
\delta
fraction of
i
’s.

This makes the task of the decoder easier.
It turns out that with this relaxation, random coins are not very helpful to the local list-decoder, and so, it is sufficient to consider deterministic local list-decoders (which do not have access to random coins
9
9

9

This is because any local list-decoder with failure probability
\delta
can be converted into a deterministic approximate local list-decoder that errs on at most a
\delta
-fraction of the entries by fixing a random string for which the local list-decoder correctly recovers at least a
(1-\delta)
-fraction of the entries (such a string exists by averaging).
). This is captured in the following definition.

Definition 4.1

(Approximate local list-decoder on noisy random codewords)
.

Let
\operatorname{Enc}:\{0,1\}^{k}\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{n}
be a function, and
\epsilon<\frac{1}{4}
. A
(\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon,L,q,\delta)
-approximate
\operatorname{RNSY}
local list-decoder (ARLLD)
for
\operatorname{Enc}
is a
deterministic
oracle procedure
\operatorname{Dec}^{(\cdot)}
that receives oracle access to a word
w\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, and makes at most
q
calls to the oracle. The procedure
\operatorname{Dec}
also receives inputs:

•

i\in[k]
: The index of the symbol that it needs to decode.

•

j\in[L]
: An index to the list.

It is required that, with probability at least
1/3
over choosing
(m,z,w)\leftarrow\operatorname{RNSY}^{\operatorname{Enc}}_{\frac{1}{2}-2\epsilon}
, there exists
j\in[L]
such that

\Pr_{i\leftarrow[k]}[\operatorname{Dec}^{w}(i,j)=m_{i}]\geq 1-\delta.

We stress again that
\operatorname{Dec}
is deterministic and the probability in Definition
4.1
is taken over the choice of a uniform random coordinate
i\in[k]
.

The following proposition shows that in order to prove lower bounds on local list-decoders (LLDs, Definition
1.3
), it is sufficient to prove lower bounds on approximate
\operatorname{RNSY}
local list-decoders (ARLLDs, Definition
4.1
).

Proposition 4.2

(LLD implies ARLLD)
.

There exists a universal constant
a>1
such that
for every
a\cdot\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}}\leq\epsilon<\frac{1}{4}
,
if there exists an
(\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon,L,q,\delta)
-local list-decoder for a function
\operatorname{Enc}:\{0,1\}^{k}\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{n}
then there also exists an
(\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon,L,q,10\cdot\delta)
-approximate
\operatorname{RNSY}
local list-decoder for
\operatorname{Enc}
.

Proof.

Within this proof, in order to avoid clutter, we use
\operatorname{RNSY}
to denote
\operatorname{RNSY}^{\operatorname{Enc}}_{\frac{1}{2}-2\epsilon}
.
Let
\operatorname{Dec}
denote an LLD for
\operatorname{Enc}
.
For
(m,z,w)\leftarrow\operatorname{RNSY}
, by a Chernoff bound, for
\gamma=2^{-\Omega(\epsilon^{2}\cdot n)}
, with probability
1-\gamma
, we have that
\mathsf{dist}(\operatorname{Enc}(m),w)\leq\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon
, meaning that
m\in\operatorname{List}^{\operatorname{Enc}}_{\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon}(w)
. By the definition of LLD, this gives that whenever this occurs, with probability at least
2/3
over the choice of
r^{\operatorname{shared}}
, there exists
j\in[L]
such that the procedure
P_{w,j,r^{\operatorname{shared}}}(i,r)=\operatorname{Dec}^{w}(i,j,r^{\operatorname{shared}},r)
locally computes
m
with error
\delta
, where
r^{\operatorname{shared}},r
are the randomness strings used by
\operatorname{Dec}
.

Let
E_{1}
be the experiment in which
(m,z,w)\leftarrow\operatorname{RNSY}
and
r^{\operatorname{shared}}
is an independent uniform string. It follows that:

\Pr_{E_{1}}[\exists j\in[L]:\mbox{$P_{w,j,r^{shared}}$ locally computes $m$ with error $\delta$}]\geq\frac{2}{3}-\gamma.

By averaging, there exists a fixed string
\hat{r}^{\operatorname{shared}}
such that:

\Pr_{\operatorname{RNSY}}[\exists j\in[L]:\mbox{$P_{w,j,\hat{r}^{shared}}$ locally computes $m$ with error $\delta$}]\geq\frac{2}{3}-\gamma.

Let
S
denote the set of triplets
(m,z,w)
in the support of
\operatorname{RNSY}
for which the event above occurs. For every such triplet, we have that there exists a
j\in[L]
for which
P_{w,j,\hat{r}^{shared}}
locally computes
m
with error
\delta
. Let
f
be a mapping that given a triplet
(m,z,w)\in S
, produces such a
j\in[L]
. This means that:

\Pr_{\operatorname{RNSY}}[\mbox{$P_{w,f(m,z,w),\hat{r}^{shared}}$ locally computes $m$ with error $\delta$}]\geq\frac{2}{3}-\gamma.

Let
\operatorname{RNSY}^{\prime}
be the experiment in which
(m,z,w)\leftarrow\operatorname{RNSY}|(m,z,w)\in S
. Namely, we choose
(m,z,w)
from the experiment
\operatorname{RNSY}
, conditioned on the event that
(m,z,w)\in S
.

Let
E_{2}
be the experiment in which we choose independently a random string
r
,
i\leftarrow[k]
and
(m,z,w)\leftarrow\operatorname{RNSY}^{\prime}
.
We obtain that:

\Pr_{E_{2}}[\operatorname{Dec}^{w}(i,f(m,z,w),\hat{r}^{shared},r)=m_{i}]\geq 1-\delta,

since
P_{w,f(m,z,w),\hat{r}^{shared}}
computes correctly each coordinate

with probability at least
1-\delta
over the choice of
r
.

By averaging, there exists a fixed string
\hat{r}
such that:

\Pr_{(m,z,w)\leftarrow\operatorname{RNSY}^{\prime},i\leftarrow[k]}[\operatorname{Dec}^{w}(i,f(m,z,w),\hat{r}^{shared},\hat{r})=m_{i}]\geq 1-\delta.

By Markov’s inequality:

\Pr_{(m,z,w)\leftarrow\operatorname{RNSY}^{\prime}}\left[\Pr_{i\leftarrow[k]}[\operatorname{Dec}^{w}(i,f(m,z,w),\hat{r}^{shared},\hat{r})\neq m_{i}]\geq 10\delta\right]\leq\frac{1}{10}.

Let
\overline{\operatorname{Dec}}^{w}(i,j)=\operatorname{Dec}^{w}(i,j,\hat{r}^{shared},\hat{r})
. We obtain that:

\Pr_{(m,z,w)\leftarrow\operatorname{RNSY}^{\prime}}\left[\Pr_{i\leftarrow[k]}[\overline{\operatorname{Dec}}^{w}(i,f(m,z,w))=m_{i}]>1-10\delta\right]>\frac{9}{10}.

Which gives that:

\Pr_{(m,z,w)\leftarrow\operatorname{RNSY}}\left[\Pr_{i\leftarrow[k]}[\overline{\operatorname{Dec}}^{w}(i,f(m,z,w))=m_{i}]>1-10\delta\right]>\left(\frac{2}{3}-\gamma\right)\cdot\frac{9}{10}\geq\frac{1}{3},

where the second inequality follows because by our requirements on

, we can choose

so that

. Thus, the oracle procedure
\overline{\operatorname{Dec}}^{(\cdot)}
is a
(\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon,L,q,10\cdot\delta)
-ARLLD as required.
∎

Definition 4.10
.

For a random variable
W
over
\{0,1\}^{n}
, a set

, such that

Remark 4.11
.

For a random variable
W
over
\{0,1\}^{n}
, it is important to distinguish
\operatorname{Fix}_{B\rightarrow v}(W)
from
\operatorname{Cond}_{B\rightarrow v}(W)
. The former means that we sample
w\leftarrow W
and replace the content of
w
in the indices in
B
by the corresponding values taken from
v
. The latter is only defined if
W
is a random variable for which the event
\left\{{W_{h}=v(h)~~\forall h\in B}\right\}
can occur, and for such a variable,
\operatorname{Cond}_{B\rightarrow v}(W)
is obtained by
conditioning
the random variable
W
on the event
\left\{{W_{h}=v(h)~~\forall h\in B}\right\}
. In particular, this conditioning may mean that when restricting
\operatorname{Cond}_{B\rightarrow v}(W)
and
W
to indices that are not in
B
, we may get different distributions. This is in contrast to
\operatorname{Fix}_{B\rightarrow v}(W)
where by definition, restricting
\operatorname{Fix}_{B\rightarrow v}(W)
and
W
to indices that are not in
B
, gives the same distribution.

A useful observation is that if
W
is a sequence of
n
independent bit variables, then for every
B,v
,
\operatorname{Cond}_{B\rightarrow v}(W)=\operatorname{Fix}_{B\rightarrow v}(W)
.

We shall use the following lemma from
[
Sha20
]
, which improves a similar lemma (with more conditions) that was proven in
[
GSV18
]
.

Lemma 4.12

(
[
Sha20
]
)
.

Let
W
be a probability distribution over
\{0,1\}^{n}
, let
A\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}
be an event such that
\Pr[W\in A]\geq 2^{-a}
, and let
W^{\prime}=(W|W\in A)
. For every
\eta>0
, there exist a set
B\subseteq[n]
of size
b=O(qa/\eta)
, and
v\in\{0,1\}^{B}
such that for every oracle procedure
D^{\cdot}
that makes
q
queries:

|\Pr[D^{\operatorname{Cond}_{B\rightarrow v}(W)}=1]-\Pr[D^{\operatorname{Cond}_{B\rightarrow v}(W^{\prime})}=1]|\leq\eta.

We now explain why this lemma is useful. Note that if we start with some distribution
W
over
\{0,1\}^{n}
, then after conditioning on the event
\left\{{W\in A}\right\}
, the bits in the obtained distribution
W^{\prime}=(W|W\in A)
may become correlated. The Lemma says that there exist a set
B\subseteq[n]
and
v\in\{0,1\}^{B}
such that if we
further condition
both
W
and
W^{\prime}
on the event
\left\{{W_{h}=v(h)~~\forall h\in B}\right\}
, to obtain the distributions
\operatorname{Cond}_{B\rightarrow v}(W)
and
\operatorname{Cond}_{B\rightarrow v}(W^{\prime})
, then these two distributions are “similar” in the sense that a procedure
D
that makes few oracle calls, cannot significantly distinguish between them.

This is useful because if
W=\operatorname{BSC}^{n}_{p}
, then
W
is a sequence of independent bits, and so,
\operatorname{Cond}_{B\rightarrow v}(W)=\operatorname{Fix}_{B\rightarrow v}(W)
. Namely, a distribution in which the bits in
B
are fixed, and the bits outside of
B
are independent and distributed like
\operatorname{BSC}^{n-b}_{p}
. Loosely speaking, this means that as long as we do not mind to condition on the event
\left\{{W_{h}=v(h)~~\forall h\in B}\right\}
, then in order to understand how
D
behaves when given oracle to
W^{\prime}
it is sufficient to understand how it behaves when given oracle access to
W
.

\displaystyle\frac{2^{k}}{6L}\cdot 2^{-(t+1)b}

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{k}}{6L}\cdot\left(2^{12}\cdot b\cdot q\right)^{-b}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{2^{k}}{L}\cdot\left(\frac{\delta}{q^{2}\log L}\right)^{O(q(\log L)/\delta)}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{2^{k}}{L}\cdot\left(\frac{\epsilon\delta}{\log L}\right)^{O(\log(1/\delta)(\log L)/(\delta\epsilon^{2}))}

\displaystyle=2^{k}\cdot\exp\left(-(\log L\log\log L)\cdot{\operatorname{poly}}(1/\delta,1/\epsilon)\right),

where the first equality follows recalling that
t=11+\log b+\log q
by Claim
4.20
, the second inequality follows
recalling that
b=O(q\cdot(\log L)/\delta)
by Claim
4.18
, and the third inequality follows by assumption that

q\leq\log(1/\delta)/\epsilon^{2}
. Finally, note that by choosing a sufficiently small constant
\nu>0
, and recalling our assumption that
L\leq 2^{k^{0.9}}
and

\epsilon,\delta\geq k^{-\nu}
, we can guarantee that the above expression is at least
2^{k-k^{0.99}}
. This concludes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Claim
4.20
will follow from the next two claims:

Claim 4.21
.

Suppose that
\operatorname{MD}
is a uniform distribution over a set
S
of size at least
2^{k-k^{0.99}}
, and that
for every
j\in[b]
,
H_{\infty}(h_{j}(\operatorname{MD}))\geq t
for
t=11+\log b+\log q
. Then

\Pr_{m\leftarrow\operatorname{MD},z\leftarrow\operatorname{BSC}^{n}_{\frac{1}{2}},i\leftarrow[k]}\left[D^{\operatorname{Fix}_{B(m)\rightarrow v(m)}(z)}(i)=m_{i}\right]\leq 0.501.

Proof.

Let
E_{B}
denote the event that
D^{\operatorname{Fix}_{B(m)\rightarrow v(m)}(z)}(i)
makes a query into
B(m)
, and let
\tilde{E}_{B}
denote the event that
D^{z}(i)
makes a query into
B(m)
. Then we have that when choosing
m\leftarrow\operatorname{MD}
,
z\leftarrow\operatorname{BSC}^{n}_{\frac{1}{2}}
, and
i\leftarrow[k]
,

\Pr\left[D^{\operatorname{Fix}_{B(m)\rightarrow v(m)}(z)}(i)=m_{i}\right]\leq\Pr\left[\left(D^{\operatorname{Fix}_{B(m)\rightarrow v(m)}(z)}(i)=m_{i}\right)\cap\neg E_{B}\right]+\Pr[E_{B}].

To bound the right-hand term, we first claim that
\Pr[E_{B}]=\Pr[\tilde{E}_{B}]
. To see this, note that for any fixed
m,i
, the set of strings
z
on which
D^{\operatorname{Fix}_{B(m)\rightarrow v(m)}(z)}(i)
makes a query into
B(m)
is identical to the set of strings
z
on which
D^{z}(i)
makes a query into
B(m)
, since the locations of the queries made before the first query to
B(m)
are the same for the oracles
\operatorname{Fix}_{B(m)\rightarrow v(m)}(z)
and
z
. Thus, to bound the right-hand term, it suffices to bound the probability of the event
\tilde{E}_{B}
.

To bound the probability that
\tilde{E}_{B}
occurs, we this time fix the string
z
and the index
i
, and note that this determines the query pattern of
D^{z}(i)
. Next we recall our assumption that for
m\leftarrow\operatorname{MD}
,
H_{\infty}(h_{j}(m))\geq t
for all
j\in[b]
. Thus for any
j\in[b]
, the probability, over
m\leftarrow\operatorname{MD}
(noting that this choice of

is independent of the fixing of
z,i
), that a specific query of
D^{z}(i)

is to
h_{j}(m)
is at most
2^{-t}
.
Hence, by a union bound, the probability that the event
\tilde{E}_{B}
occurs is at most
q\cdot b\cdot 2^{-t}
.
Finally, by our setting of
t=11+\log b+\log q
, we have that this probability is at most
2^{-11}
. We conclude that the right-hand term satisfies
\Pr[E_{B}]\leq 2^{-11}.

To bound the left-hand term, we once more claim that

\Pr\left[\left(D^{\operatorname{Fix}_{B(m)\rightarrow v(m)}(z)}(i)=m_{i}\right)\cap\neg E_{B}\right]=\Pr\left[\left(D^{z}(i)=m_{i}\right)\cap\neg\tilde{E}_{B}\right].

Once more, this follows since when fixing
m,i
, the set of strings
z
on which
D^{\operatorname{Fix}_{B(m)\rightarrow v(m)}(z)}(i)
does not make a query into
B(m)
is identical to the set of strings
z
on which
D^{z}(i)
does not make a query into
B(m)
, and fixing each such string
z
induces
the same query pattern for
D^{z}(i)
and
D^{\operatorname{Fix}_{B(m)\rightarrow v(m)}(z)}(i)
. Thus we conclude that for fixed
m,i
, the set of strings
z
which lead to the event
\left(D^{\operatorname{Fix}_{B(m)\rightarrow v(m)}(z)}(i)=m_{i}\right)\cap\neg E_{B}
is identical to the set of strings
z
that
lead to the event
\left(D^{z}(i)=m_{i}\right)\cap\neg\tilde{E}_{B}
, and so the probabilities are the same. It thus suffices to bound the probability of the event
\left(D^{z}(i)=m_{i}\right)\cap\neg\tilde{E}_{B}.

To bound the probability that
\left(D^{z}(i)=m_{i}\right)\cap\neg\tilde{E}_{B}
occurs, we note that it is at most the probability that

D^{z}(i)=m_{i}
occurs. Recalling our assumption that
\operatorname{MD}
is uniform over a set of size at least
2^{k-k^{0.99}}
, by
Lemma
3.1
, this latter probability is at most
0.5001
. So the left-hand term satisfies that

\Pr\left[\left(D^{\operatorname{Fix}_{B(m)\rightarrow v(m)}(z)}(i)=m_{i}\right)\cap\neg E_{B}\right]\leq 0.5001.

Summing up the two probabilities, we get that

\Pr_{m\leftarrow\operatorname{MD},z\leftarrow\operatorname{BSC}^{n}_{\frac{1}{2}},i\leftarrow[k]}\left[D^{\operatorname{Fix}_{B(m)\rightarrow v(m)}(z)}(i)=m_{i}\right]\leq 0.5001+2^{-11}\leq 0.501,

which concludes the proof of the claim.
∎

4.3
Proof of Theorem

In this section we prove Theorem
1.6
, restated below.

See
1.6

The Theorem will follow from the next lemma (which argues that an ARLLD with certain parameters, yields a depth 3 circuit that distinguishes between
\operatorname{BSC}_{\frac{1}{2}-2\epsilon}

\operatorname{BSC}_{\frac{1}{2}}

Our overall plan for proving Theorem
1.6
is to argue that a local list-decoder with the parameters specified in Theorem
1.6
yields an ARLLD with the parameters specified in Lemma

, which in turn yields the circuit specified in Lemma
4.23
, and this will be used to prove a lower bound on

Lemma 4.23
.

There exist universal constants
\beta>0
and
c>1
such that the following holds for any

and
\epsilon<\frac{1}{4}
.
Let
\operatorname{Dec}
be a
(\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon,L,q,\frac{1}{2k})
-ARLLD for
\operatorname{Enc}:\{0,1\}^{k}\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{n}

•

•

We first prove that Lemma

implies Theorem
1.6
.

Proof of Theorem
1.6
.

Consider a
(\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon,L,q,\delta)
-LLD
\operatorname{Dec}
for
\operatorname{Enc}:\{0,1\}^{k}\to\{0,1\}^{n}
, where
\delta\leq 1/3
.
It is possible to amplify the error probability
\delta
from
\frac{1}{3}
to
\frac{1}{20k}
as follows: After choosing the random string
r^{\operatorname{shared}}
, we choose
e=O(\log k)
independent uniform strings
r_{1},\ldots,r_{e}
, and apply
\operatorname{Dec}^{(\cdot)}(i,j,r^{\operatorname{shared}},r_{\ell})
for all choices of
\ell\in[e]
. We then output the majority vote of the individual
e
outputs. It is standard that this gives a
(\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon,L,q^{\prime}=O(q\log k),\frac{1}{20k})
-LLD for
\operatorname{Enc}:\{0,1\}^{k}\to\{0,1\}^{n}
.

By our requirements on
\epsilon
, we can use Proposition
4.2
to show that there exists a
(\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon,L,q^{\prime},\frac{1}{2k})
-ARLLD for
\operatorname{Enc}:\{0,1\}^{k}\to\{0,1\}^{n}
.
By Lemma
4.23
, there exists a circuit
C
of size

s=O(L\cdot k\cdot 2^{2\cdot q^{\prime}})=L\cdot k\cdot 2^{c_{1}q\log k}

and depth
3
that distinguishes
\operatorname{BSC}^{n^{\prime}}_{\frac{1}{2}-2\epsilon}
from
\operatorname{BSC}^{n^{\prime}}_{\frac{1}{2}}
. By Corollary
3.5
such a circuit must have size:

s\geq 2^{1/(c_{2}\sqrt{\epsilon})}.

This implies that

q\geq\frac{1}{c_{1}c_{2}\sqrt{\epsilon}\log k}-\frac{\log(Lk)}{c_{1}\log k}\geq\frac{1}{c_{1}c_{2}\sqrt{\epsilon}\log k}-\log L.

This completes the proof of the theorem.
∎

In the remainder of this section we prove Lemma
4.23
.
Let
\operatorname{Dec}
be a
(\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon,L,q,\frac{1}{2k})
-ARLLD for
\operatorname{Enc}:\{0,1\}^{k}\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{n}
.
We will construct the circuit
C
in the following sequence of claims:

Definition 4.24
.

For every
i\in[k]
and
j\in[L]
, let
A_{i,j}:\{0,1\}^{n}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
be defined by:

A_{i,j}(w)=\operatorname{Dec}^{w}(i,j)
.

Claim 4.25
.

For every
i\in[k]
and
j\in[L]
, there exist CNF circuits
A^{T}_{i,j}:\{0,1\}^{n}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
and
A^{F}_{i,j}:\{0,1\}^{n}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
of size
O(q\cdot 2^{q})
such that for every
w\in\{0,1\}^{n}
,
A^{T}_{i,j}(w)=A_{i,j}(w)
, and
A^{F}_{i,j}(w)=1-A_{i,j}(w)
.

Proof.

For fixed
i,j
, we can view the computations
A_{i,j}(w)=\operatorname{Dec}^{w}(i,j)
as a depth
q
decision tree that makes queries to
w
. Such a decision tree can be simulated by a size
O(q\cdot 2^{q})
DNF, which is a disjunction over the at most
2^{q}
accepting paths of the tree, where each path is a conjunction of
q
literals. This also gives a CNF of the same size for
1-A_{i,j}
. The same argument can be repeated for
1-A_{i,j}
giving a CNF of the same size for
A_{i,j}
.
∎

Definition 4.26
.

For every
m\in\{0,1\}^{k}
we define the circuit
C_{m}:\{0,1\}^{n}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
that is hardwired with the message
m\in\{0,1\}^{k}
, and the encoding
\operatorname{Enc}(m)
. Given input
z\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, the circuit
C_{m}
acts as follows:

•

Prepare
w=\operatorname{Enc}(m)\oplus z
.

•

For every
i\in[k]
and
j\in[L]
compute
A_{i,j}(w)
, and compute
b_{i,j}\in\{0,1\}
which answers whether
A_{i,j}(w)=m_{i}
.

•

For every
j
, compute
b_{j}
which is the conjunction of
b_{1,j},\ldots,b_{k,j}
.

•

Compute the disjunction of
b_{1},\ldots,b_{L}
and output it.

Claim 4.27
.

For every
m\in\{0,1\}^{k}
the circuit
C_{m}
can be implemented in size
O(k\cdot L\cdot 2^{2q})
and depth
3
. Furthermore, for every
m\in\{0,1\}^{k}
, and
z\in\{0,1\}^{n}
,
C_{m}(z)=1
if and only if there exists
j\in[L]
such that for every
i\in[k]
,
\operatorname{Dec}^{\operatorname{Enc}(m)\oplus z}(i,j)=m_{i}
.

Proof.

It is immediate that the circuit
C_{m}
performs the task described in the claim. We now explain how to implement the circuit in small size and depth.

The string
m
is of length
k
. We note that when using
\operatorname{Enc}(m)
to prepare
w
, we only need to have
\operatorname{Enc}(m)
at coordinates
\ell
such that there exist
i,j
such that
A_{i,j}(w)
depends on the
\ell
’th input. As each circuit
A^{T}_{i,j}
is a circuit of size
O(q\cdot 2^{q})
it depends on at most
O(q\cdot 2^{q})
input bits. Thus,
C_{m}
only requires
O(k\cdot L\cdot q\cdot 2^{q})
bits of
\operatorname{Enc}(m)
. Overall, the size of the advice of
C_{m}
is
O(k\cdot L\cdot q\cdot 2^{q})
.

Computing every bit of
w
amounts to at most one negation gate, and does not increase the depth.
For every
i\in[k]
and
j\in[L]
, we want to compute the bit
b_{i,j}
which is
1
if and only if
A_{i,j}(w)=m_{i}
. Note that if
m_{i}=1
then
b_{i,j}=A^{T}_{i,j}(w)
and if
m_{i}=0
then
b_{i,j}=A^{F}_{i,j}(w)
. As
m_{i}
is a fixed constant, this gives that for every
i\in[k]
and
j\in[L]
,
b_{i,j}
can be computed by a CNF of size
O(q\cdot 2^{q})
that is applied on the input
z
.
For every
j\in[L]
, computing
b_{j}
is done using a single AND gate, and, since the top gate of the CNF computing
b_{i}
is also an AND gate, this does not increase the depth. Finally, computing the output adds a top OR gate.
overall, the depth is
3
and the size is bounded by:

O(k\cdot L\cdot q\cdot 2^{q})\leq O(k\cdot L\cdot 2^{2q}).\qed

It is important to note that in Lemma
4.23
, we start from the assumption that
\operatorname{Dec}
has
\delta=\frac{1}{2k}
(which is achieved by reducing the initial
\delta
at the cost of making more queries). This value of
\delta
is smaller than
\frac{1}{k}
. By the definition of an ARLLD, this implies the following.

Claim 4.28
.

\Pr_{m\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{k},z\leftarrow\operatorname{BSC}^{n}_{\frac{1}{2}-2\epsilon}}[C_{m}(z)=1]\geq\frac{1}{6}
.

Proof.

By the definition of an ARLLD we have that with probability at least
1/3
over choosing
(m,z,w)\leftarrow\operatorname{RNSY}^{\operatorname{Enc}}_{\frac{1}{2}-2\epsilon}
, there exists
j\in[L]
such that

\Pr_{i\leftarrow[k]}[\operatorname{Dec}^{w}(i,j)=m_{i}]\geq 1-\frac{1}{2k}.

However, as we consider choosing a random
i\leftarrow[k]
, if the latter probability is greater than
1-\frac{1}{k}
then it must be one. We can therefore conclude that with probability at least
1/3
over choosing
(m,z,w)\leftarrow\operatorname{RNSY}^{\operatorname{Enc}}_{\frac{1}{2}-2\epsilon}
, there exists
j\in[L]
such that for every
i\in[k]
,
\operatorname{Dec}^{w}(i,j)=m_{i}
.
By Claim
4.27
this gives that

\Pr_{m\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{k},z\leftarrow\operatorname{BSC}^{n}_{\frac{1}{2}-2\epsilon}}[C_{m}(z)=1]\geq\frac{1}{3}
as required.
∎

On the other hand, we can show that:

Claim 4.29
.

\Pr_{m\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{k},z\leftarrow\operatorname{BSC}^{n}_{\frac{1}{2}}}[C_{m}(z)=1]\leq L\cdot 2^{-k}
.

Proof.

The first step of
C_{m}(z)
is to prepare
w=\operatorname{Enc}(m)\oplus z
.
However, for
p=\frac{1}{2}
, and
z\leftarrow\operatorname{BSC}^{n}_{p}
, we have that
w=\operatorname{Enc}(m)\oplus z
is uniformly chosen, and independent of
m
. This means that the bits
A_{i,j}(w)
for
i\in[k]
and
j\in[L]
are independent of
m
. Consequently, for every
j\in[L]
, we have that:

\displaystyle\Pr_{m\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{k},z\leftarrow\operatorname{BSC}^{n}_{\frac{1}{2}},w=\operatorname{Enc}(m)\oplus z}[m=A_{1,j}(w)\circ\ldots\circ A_{k,j}(w)]

\displaystyle\leq\Pr_{m\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{k},w\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{n}}[m=A_{1,j}(w)\circ\ldots\circ A_{k,j}(w)]\leq 2^{-k}

By a union bound over all choices of
j\in[L]
, we have that:

\Pr_{m\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{k},z\leftarrow\operatorname{BSC}^{n}_{\frac{1}{2}},w=\operatorname{Enc}(m)\oplus z}[\exists j:\mbox{s.t.\ }m=A_{1,j}(w)\circ\ldots\circ A_{k,j}(w)]\leq L\cdot 2^{-k}

It follows that:

\Pr_{m\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{k},z\leftarrow\operatorname{BSC}^{n}_{\frac{1}{2}}}[C_{m}(z)=1]\leq L\cdot 2^{-k},

as required.
∎

We are finally ready to prove Lemma
4.23
.

Proof of Lemma
4.23
.

By our choices, we have that
0<L\cdot 2^{-k}\leq\beta<1
.
By applying Markov’s inequality to Claims
4.28
and
4.29
, we can see that:

\displaystyle\Pr_{m\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{k}}\left[\Pr_{z\leftarrow\operatorname{BSC}^{n}_{\frac{1}{2}-2\epsilon}}[C_{m}(z)\neq 1]>\frac{9}{10}\right]

\displaystyle<\frac{5/6}{9/10}\leq\frac{95}{100}.

\displaystyle\Pr_{m\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{k}}\left[\Pr_{z\leftarrow\operatorname{BSC}^{n}_{\frac{1}{2}}}[C_{m}(z)=1]>\sqrt{\beta}\right]

\displaystyle<\sqrt{\beta}.

Therefore, for a sufficiently small constant
\beta>0
, by a union bound, we get that there exists
m\in\{0,1\}^{k}
satisfying both:

\displaystyle\Pr_{z\leftarrow\operatorname{BSC}^{n}_{\frac{1}{2}-2\epsilon}}[C_{m}(z)=1]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{10},

\displaystyle\Pr_{z\leftarrow\operatorname{BSC}^{n}_{\frac{1}{2}}}[C_{m}(z)=1]

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\beta},

It is possible to amplify these thresholds to
0.99
and
0.01
as follows: Let
c
be a constant that we choose later, and let
n^{\prime}=cn
. Consider a circuit
C:\{0,1\}^{n^{\prime}}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
that when receiving input
x\in\{0,1\}^{n^{\prime}}
, treats it as
c
strings
x_{1},\ldots,x_{c}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
. The circuit
C
will apply
C_{m}
on each of the
c
strings, and the final output is the OR of the results. As the top gate of
C_{m}
is an OR gate, adding an additional OR gate, does not increase the depth of the circuit. The size of the circuit increases by a constant factor.

We can view
z\leftarrow\operatorname{BSC}^{n^{\prime}}_{p}
as obtained by concatenating
c
strings
z_{1},\dots,z_{c}
, where each of them is sampled uniformly and independently at random from
\operatorname{BSC}^{n}_{p}
.
Hence, the event
C(z)=1
is identical to
\bigvee_{\ell\in[c]}C_{m}(z_{\ell})=1
.
Therefore,

\Pr_{z\leftarrow\operatorname{BSC}^{n^{\prime}}_{\frac{1}{2}-2\epsilon}}[C(z)=1]\geq 1-\left(\frac{9}{10}\right)^{c},

and

\Pr_{z\leftarrow\operatorname{BSC}^{n^{\prime}}_{\frac{1}{2}}}[C(z)=1]\leq c\cdot\sqrt{\beta}.

By choosing
c
to be sufficiently large, we can see that
1-(\frac{9}{10})^{c}\geq 0.99
.
By choosing
\beta
to be sufficiently small, we also have that
c\sqrt{\beta}\leq 0.01
.
∎

5
Limitations on black-box proofs for hard-core predicates

In this section, we present our results regarding the limitations on black-box proofs for hard-core predicate theorems. In Section
5.1
, we state our results for functions that are hard to compute, give a formal restatement of Theorem
1.9
, and prove the theorem. In Section
5.2
, we state our results for functions that are hard to invert, give a formal restatement of Theorem
1.13
, and prove the theorem.

5.1
The case of functions that are hard to compute

5.1.1
The model for black-box proofs

In this section, we state and explain our model for black-box proofs for hard core predicates, in the setting of functions that are hard to compute. The formal definition is given in Definition
5.1
. Below, we provide a detailed explanation for the considerations made while coming up with the formal definition. The reader can skip directly to the formal definition if they wish to.

Explanation of the model:

Recall that (as explained in Section
1.2.1
) the Goldreich-Levin theorem (stated precisely in Theorem
1.7
) has the following form:

•

We are given an arbitrary hard function
g:\{0,1\}^{\ell}\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{\ell}
. (Intuitively, it is assumed that it is hard to compute
g
with success probability
\rho
).

•

There is a specified construction that transforms
g
into a predicate
g^{\operatorname{pred}}:\{0,1\}^{\ell^{\prime}}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
for some
\ell^{\prime}
related to
\ell
. (Intuitively, we will want to argue that
g^{\operatorname{pred}}
is a hard-core predicate that is hard to compute with success
\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon
).

We will model this construction as a map
\operatorname{Con}
, which, given
g
produces
g^{\operatorname{pred}}
. We place
no limitations
on the map
\operatorname{Con}
(and, in particular, do not require that
g^{\operatorname{pred}}
can be efficiently computed if
g
is). This only makes our results stronger.

In the case of Theorem
1.7
, we have that:
\operatorname{Con}(g)=g^{\operatorname{pred}}
where
\ell^{\prime}=2\ell
and we think of the
\ell^{\prime}
-bit long input of
g^{\operatorname{pred}}
as two strings
x,r\in\{0,1\}^{\ell}
, setting:

g^{\operatorname{pred}}(x,r)=\operatorname{Enc}^{\operatorname{Had}}(g(x))_{r}=(\sum_{i\in[\ell]}g(x)_{i}\cdot r_{i})\mod 2.

•

We model the proof showing that
g^{\operatorname{pred}}
is a hard-core predicate in the following way: The proof is a pair
(\operatorname{Con},\operatorname{Red})
where
\operatorname{Red}^{(\cdot)}
is an oracle procedure, such that when
\operatorname{Red}^{(\cdot)}
receives oracle access to an “adversary”
h:\{0,1\}^{\ell^{\prime}}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
that breaks the security of
g^{\operatorname{pred}}
, we have that
\operatorname{Red}^{h}
breaks the security of
g
. More precisely, we require that: for every
g:\{0,1\}^{\ell}\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{\ell}
and for every
h:\{0,1\}^{\ell^{\prime}}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
such that:

\Pr_{x\leftarrow U_{\ell^{\prime}}}[h(x)=g^{\operatorname{pred}}(x)]\geq\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon,

it holds that:

\Pr_{x\leftarrow U_{\ell}}[\operatorname{Red}^{h}(x)=g(x)]\geq\rho.

•

In the actual definition, we will allow the reduction to have more power (which only makes our results stronger). As we are aiming to prove a result on circuits (which are allowed to use nonuniform advice) we will allow the reduction to receive an advice string
\alpha
of length
t
, where, this advice string can depend on
g
and
h
. This leads to the following strengthening of the requirement above. Namely, we will require that: for every
g:\{0,1\}^{\ell}\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{\ell}
and for every
h:\{0,1\}^{\ell^{\prime}}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
, such that:

\Pr_{x\leftarrow U_{\ell^{\prime}}}[h(x)=g^{\operatorname{pred}}(x)]\geq\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon,

there exists
\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{t}
such that:

\Pr_{x\leftarrow U_{\ell}}[\operatorname{Red}^{h}(x,\alpha)=g(x)]\geq\rho.

We remark that in many related settings (for example, “hardness amplification”; see
[
SV10
,
GSV18
]
, for a discussion) known proofs by reduction
critically make use
of the ability to introduce nonuniformity, and so, we feel that when ruling out black-box proofs in scenarios involving circuits, it is necessary to consider nonuniform black-box reductions.

•

We make no restrictions on the complexity of the procedure
\operatorname{Red}^{(\cdot)}
, except for requiring that it makes at most
q
queries to its oracle (for some parameter
q
). Our black-box impossibility results will follow from proving lower bounds on
q
.

Formal definition:

We now give a formal definition of our model for black-box proofs for hard-core predicates.

Definition 5.1

(Nonuniform black-box proofs for hard-core predicates for hard-to-compute functions)
.

A pair
(\operatorname{Con},\operatorname{Red})
is a
nonuniform black-box proof
for
hard-core predicates for hard-to-compute functions
with parameters
\ell,\ell^{\prime},\rho,\epsilon
, that uses
q
queries
, and
t
bits of advice
if:

•

\operatorname{Con}
is a
construction map
which given a function
g:\{0,1\}^{\ell}\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{\ell}
, produces a function
\operatorname{Con}(g)=g^{\operatorname{pred}}
, where
g^{\operatorname{pred}}:\{0,1\}^{\ell^{\prime}}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
.

•

\operatorname{Red}^{(\cdot)}
is a
reduction
, that is an oracle procedure that, given oracle access to a function
h:\{0,1\}^{\ell^{\prime}}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
, makes at most
q
queries to its oracle.

Furthermore, for every functions
g:\{0,1\}^{\ell}\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{\ell}
and
h:\{0,1\}^{\ell^{\prime}}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
such that:

\Pr_{x\leftarrow U_{\ell^{\prime}}}[h(x)=g^{\operatorname{pred}}(x)]\geq\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon,

there exists
\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{t}
, such that:

\Pr_{x\leftarrow U_{\ell}}[\operatorname{Red}^{h}(x,\alpha)=g(x)]\geq\rho.

The role of the number of queries, and black-box impossibility results:

We now explain the role of the parameter
q
(that measures the number of queries made by
\operatorname{Red}
) and why lower bounds on
q
translate into black-box impossibility results.

For this purpose, it is illustrative to examine the argument showing that nonuniform black-box proofs yield hard-core predicates: When given a pair
(\operatorname{Con},\operatorname{Red})
that is a nonuniform black-box proof for hard-core predicates for hard-to-compute functions with parameters
\ell,\ell^{\prime},\rho,\epsilon
, that uses
q
queries, and
t
bits of advice, we obtain that for any function
g:\{0,1\}^{\ell}\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{\ell}
, if there exists a circuit
C^{\prime}:\{0,1\}^{\ell^{\prime}}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
of size
s^{\prime}
such that:

\Pr_{x\leftarrow U_{\ell^{\prime}}}[C^{\prime}(x)=g^{\operatorname{pred}}(x)]\geq\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon,

then there exists
\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{t}
, such that:

\Pr_{x\leftarrow U_{\ell}}[\operatorname{Red}^{C^{\prime}}(x,\alpha)=g(x)]\geq\rho.

Note that if the reduction
\operatorname{Red}
can be implemented by a circuit of size
r
, then the circuit
C(x)=\operatorname{Red}^{C^{\prime}}(x,\alpha)
is a circuit of size:

s=r+t+q\cdot s^{\prime}

that computes
g
with success probability
\rho
.

It follows that in a black-box proof, with
q
queries, and
t
bits of advice, we get a hard-core theorem that needs to assume that the original function
g
has hardness against circuits of size
s
, for:

s\geq q+t.

5.1.2
Precise statements of limitations

Our main result on black-box proofs for hard-core predicates in the setting of functions that are hard to compute is the following theorem.

Theorem 5.2
.

There exist universal constants
\beta>0
and
c>1
such that the following holds for any sufficiently large
\ell
and
\ell^{\prime}
,
\epsilon>0
,
t\leq 2^{\ell/5}
, and
\rho\geq 2^{-\ell/3}
.
Let
(\operatorname{Con},\operatorname{Red})
be a nonuniform black-box proof for hard-core predicates for hard-to-compute functions with parameters
\ell,\ell^{\prime},\rho,\epsilon
, that uses
q
queries, and
t
bits of advice. Then

q\geq\frac{1}{\epsilon^{\beta}}-c(t+\ell).

We now explain why Theorem
5.2
implies the informal statement made in Theorem
1.9
. Recall that in Section
5.1.1
we explained that when using a nonuniform black-box proof to obtain a hard-core predicate, we get a hard-core predicate theorem in which
s\geq q+t
.

Theorem
5.2
implies that it is impossible for such a proof to establish
\epsilon=s^{-2/\beta}
(even if
\rho
is very small). This follows as otherwise, using the fact that
s\geq q+t\geq t
and
s\geq\ell
(since the input to the circuit is
x\in\{0,1\}^{\ell}
), we get that:

q\geq\frac{1}{\epsilon^{\beta}}-c(t+\ell)\geq s^{2}-c(t+\ell)>s,

which is a contradiction to
s\geq q+t\geq q
. In particular, the parameter setting considered in Theorem
1.9
, in which
\epsilon=\frac{1}{s^{\omega(1)}}
, is impossible to achieve.

5.1.3
Proof of Theorem
5.2

Theorem
5.2
will follow from the next lemma, showing that a proof with small

can be transformed into a small constant depth circuit for the coin problem.

Lemma 5.3
.

There exists a universal constant
d>1

such that the following holds
for any sufficiently large
\ell
and
\ell^{\prime}
,
\epsilon\geq 2^{-\ell^{\prime}/3}
,

, and
\rho\geq 2^{-\ell/3}
.
Let
(\operatorname{Con},\operatorname{Red})
be a nonuniform black-box proof for hard-core predicates for hard-to-compute functions with parameters
\ell,\ell^{\prime},\rho,\epsilon
, that uses
q

bits of advice. Then there exists a circuit
C
of size
{\operatorname{poly}}(2^{q},2^{\ell},2^{t})
and depth

such that:

•

.

•

\Pr_{z\leftarrow\operatorname{BSC}^{n}_{\frac{1}{2}}}[C(z)=1]\leq 0.01
.

We first show that Theorem
5.2
follows from Lemma

.

Proof of Theorem
5.2
.

First note that as
x
is sampled from the uniform distribution on
\{0,1\}^{\ell^{\prime}}
, we have that
\epsilon\geq 2^{-\ell^{\prime}}
. Moreover, by definition it follows that if
(\operatorname{Con},\operatorname{Red})
is a nonuniform black-box proof for hard-core predicates with parameter
\epsilon
, then it is also a nonuniform black-box proof for hard-core predicates with parameter
\epsilon^{1/3}\geq 2^{-\ell^{\prime}/3}
.
Lemma
5.3
and Corollary

then give that:

{\operatorname{poly}}(2^{q},2^{\ell},2^{t})\geq\exp\left(d\cdot\epsilon^{-\frac{1}{3(d-1)}}\right).

The statement of Theorem

∎

In the remainder of this section we prove Lemma
5.3
.
Let
(\operatorname{Con},\operatorname{Red})
be a nonuniform black-box proof for hard-core predicates for hard-to-compute functions with parameters
\ell,\ell^{\prime},\rho,\epsilon
, that uses
q
queries, and
t
bits of advice. Throughout this section we assume that the requirements made in Lemma
5.3
are met.

We will identify functions
h:\{0,1\}^{\ell}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
with strings
h\in\{0,1\}^{2^{\ell}}
. More precisely, we fix some ordering on strings
x\in\{0,1\}^{\ell}
and then, the value of string
h
at position

is the function
h
applied on
x
.
We will use
h
to denote these two objects (both the function and the string) and this means that a function
h
can be given as an argument to a function that receives strings of length
2^{\ell}
.

High level description of the proof of Lemma
5.3
.

The proof will use the same structure as the proof of Lemma
4.23
, which was the main technical lemma in the proof of Theorem
1.6
. Loosely speaking, the reduction
\operatorname{Red}
plays the role of a local list-decoder, the function
g
plays the role of the message, the construction map
\operatorname{Con}
plays the role of the encoder, and the function
g^{\operatorname{pred}}
(viewed as a
2^{\ell^{\prime}}
bit long string) plays the role of the encoding of the message.

Imitating the approach used in the proof of Lemma
4.23
, we will consider the function (or string)
h\in\{0,1\}^{2^{\ell^{\prime}}}
defined by
h=g^{\operatorname{pred}}\oplus z
where
z\leftarrow\operatorname{BSC}^{2^{\ell^{\prime}}}_{p}
for
p=\frac{1}{2}
and
p=\frac{1}{2}-2\epsilon
. We will try to show that when the function
g
is chosen uniformly, then for
p=\frac{1}{2}-2\epsilon
,
\operatorname{Red}^{h}
has to succeed, and for
p=\frac{1}{2}
,
\operatorname{Red}^{h}
cannot succeed. We will then leverage this difference to produce a constant depth circuit of size roughly
2^{q}
that distinguishes
\operatorname{BSC}^{2^{\ell^{\prime}}}_{\frac{1}{2}-2\epsilon}
from
\operatorname{BSC}^{2^{\ell^{\prime}}}_{\frac{1}{2}}
.

However, there are some complications. When the reduction
\operatorname{Red}^{h}
succeeds for
p=\frac{1}{2}-2\epsilon
, we only have that there exists an
\alpha
with which it computes
g
with success
\rho
which is extremely small, and is not much larger than the success probability of
\operatorname{Red}^{h}
for
p=\frac{1}{2}
(which we will show is less than
\rho/10
).

At first glance, this seems like a problem, as in general, in order to distinguish success probability
a+\rho/10
from
a+\rho
for an arbitrary value of
a\in[0,1]
, constant depth circuits need to have size that is
2^{(1/\rho)^{\Omega(1)}}
which is much too large for our purposes. Fortunately, for
a=0
(that is for the task of distinguishing success probability
\rho/10
from
\rho
) it is possible to distinguish with circuits of size
{\operatorname{poly}}(1/\rho)
. The formal statement of this is given in Lemma
5.4
.

We now return to the formal proof, starting with the following lemma.

Lemma 5.4
.

There exists a universal constant
d>1
, such
that any
n,\rho
there exists a circuit
D^{n}_{\rho}:\{0,1\}^{n}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
of size
{\operatorname{poly}}(n/\rho)
and depth
d
, such that for every
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
:

•

If
\operatorname{weight}(x)\geq\rho
then
D^{n}_{\rho}(x)=1
.

•

If
\operatorname{weight}(x)\leq\rho/10
then
D^{n}_{\rho}(x)=0
.

We note that by the lower bound of Razborov and Smolensky
[
Raz87
,
Smo87
]
small constant-depth circuits cannot compute the majority function. Nevertheless, by the results of Ajtai
[
Ajt83
]
(stated in Theorem
3.3
) small constant-depth circuits can compute
approximate majority
. That is, they can distinguish strings with relative Hamming weight
\geq P
from strings with relative Hamming weight
\leq p
whenever
p<P
are constants. The proof of the lemma uses circuits for approximate majority.

Proof of Lemma
5.4
.

We first construct a distribution over circuits that achieves the goal. Let
a>1
be a constant that we choose later. Let
n^{\prime}=\frac{a}{\rho}
.
Let us consider the experiment
E_{1}
in which a uniform multi-set
S
of
[n]
of size
n^{\prime}
is chosen uniformly. (That is

are chosen independently and uniformly from
[n]
and
S
is the multi-set
\left\{{i_{1},\ldots,i_{n^{\prime}}}\right\}
). Note that for every
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
,

•

If
\operatorname{weight}(x)\geq\rho
then
\mathbb{E}_{S\leftarrow E_{1}}[\sum_{i\in S}x_{i}]\geq a
.

•

If
\operatorname{weight}(x)\leq\rho/10
then
\mathbb{E}_{S\leftarrow E_{1}}[\sum_{i\in S}x_{i}]\leq a/10
.

For every multi-set
S\subseteq[n]
of size
n^{\prime}
we consider the circuit
C_{S}:\{0,1\}^{n}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
that works as follows:

•

For every choice of
a/5

in
S
, compute the conjunction of
x_{j_{1}},\ldots,x_{j_{a/5}}
.

•

Compute the disjunction of the
(n^{\prime})^{a/5}
bits from the previous item.

This gives that there exists a constant
c
, that depends on

such that
C_{S}
is a circuit of size
(1/\rho)^{c}
and depth
2
. Furthermore
C_{S}(x)
answers

if and only if
\sum_{i\in S}x_{i}\geq a/5
.

By a (multiplicative) Chernoff bound,
10
10

10

Specifically, we mean the following version of the Chernoff bound: If
X
is the sum of
n

X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\in[0,1]
, and
\mathbb{E}(X)=\mu

\Pr[|X-\mu|\geq\delta\mu]\leq 2e^{-\frac{\delta^{2}\cdot\mu}{3}}
.

it follows that there exists a universal constant

•

If

•

If
\operatorname{weight}(x)\leq\rho/10

\Pr_{S\leftarrow E_{1}}[C_{S}(x)=1]\leq 1/3
.

By taking
t=O(n)
independent copies of

and computing approximate majority, we can reduce the error probability from

to

, and apply Adleman’s argument to obtain a single circuit of size

and constant depth. Details follow:

For
t=O(n)

of size

which for every

Let

denote the experiment in which the

•

If
\operatorname{weight}(x)\geq\rho

•

Claim 5.5
.

On the other hand, we can show that:

Claim 5.8

x\in\{0,1\}^{\ell}
is at most:

{2^{\ell}\choose\rho^{\prime}\cdot 2^{\ell}}\cdot\frac{1}{2^{\rho^{\prime}\cdot\ell\cdot 2^{\ell}}}\leq\left(\frac{e}{\rho^{\prime}}\right)^{\rho^{\prime}\cdot 2^{\ell}}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{2^{\ell}}\right)^{\rho^{\prime}\cdot 2^{\ell}}\leq\frac{1}{2^{\ell/2}},

where the first inequality follows from
{n\choose k}\leq(\frac{en}{k})^{k}
and the second inequality follows by our requirements on
\rho\geq 2^{-\ell/3}
.

For fixed
w\in\{0,1\}^{2^{\ell^{\prime}}}
, the condition that
v_{\alpha}
agrees with
g
in a
\rho/10
fraction of inputs
x\in\{0,1\}^{\ell}
, can also be phrased as:

\Pr_{x\leftarrow U_{\ell}}[\operatorname{Red}^{w}(x,\alpha)=g(x)]\geq\rho/10.

It follows that for every
\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{t}
, with probability at least
1-2^{-\ell/2}
over the choice of
g\leftarrow F_{\ell},z\leftarrow\operatorname{BSC}^{2^{\ell^{\prime}}}_{\frac{1}{2}}
, we have that:

\Pr_{x\leftarrow U_{\ell}}[\operatorname{Red}^{g^{\operatorname{pred}}\oplus z}(x,\alpha)=g(x)]<\rho/10,

By a union bound over all
2^{t}
choices of
\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{t}
, with probability at least
1-2^{t}\cdot 2^{-\ell/2}
over the choice of
g\leftarrow F_{\ell},z\leftarrow\operatorname{BSC}^{2^{\ell^{\prime}}}_{\frac{1}{2}}
, we have that
for all
\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{t}
,

\Pr_{x\leftarrow U_{\ell}}[\operatorname{Red}^{g^{\operatorname{pred}}\oplus z}(x,\alpha)=g(x)]<\rho/10,

which by Claim
5.6
implies that
C_{g}(z)=0
.
Consequently, in order to complete the proof of the claim, it remains to verify that:

2^{t}\cdot 2^{-\ell/3}\leq 0.001.

This follows by the requirements that
t\leq 2^{\ell/5}
.
∎

We are finally ready to prove Lemma
5.3
.

Proof of Lemma
5.3
.

From Claims
5.7
and
5.8
, it follows that:

\displaystyle\Pr_{g\leftarrow F_{\ell}}\left[\Pr_{z\leftarrow\operatorname{BSC}^{2^{\ell^{\prime}}}_{\frac{1}{2}-2\epsilon}}[C_{g}(z)=0]>0.01\right]

\displaystyle\leq 0.1,

\displaystyle\Pr_{g\leftarrow F_{\ell}}\left[\Pr_{z\leftarrow\operatorname{BSC}^{2^{\ell^{\prime}}}_{\frac{1}{2}}}[C_{g}(z)=1]>0.01\right]

\displaystyle\leq 0.1.

Thus, by a union bound, there exists
g:\{0,1\}^{\ell}\to\{0,1\}^{\ell}
such that

\displaystyle\Pr_{z\leftarrow\operatorname{BSC}^{2^{\ell^{\prime}}}_{\frac{1}{2}-2\epsilon}}[C_{g}(z)=1]

\displaystyle\geq 0.99,

\displaystyle\Pr_{z\leftarrow\operatorname{BSC}^{2^{\ell^{\prime}}}_{\frac{1}{2}}}[C_{g}(z)=1]

\displaystyle\leq 0.01.

This completes the proof of the lemma, as
C_{g}
satisfies all the other requirements as well.
∎

5.2
The case of functions that are hard to invert

5.2.1
The model for black-box proofs

In this section we state and explain our model for black-box proofs for hard core predicates, in the setting of functions that are hard to invert. The precise formal definition is given in concise form in Definition
5.9
. Below, we provide a detailed explanation for the considerations made in the formal definition. The reader can skip directly to the formal definition if they wish to.

This setting is very similar to the case of functions that are hard to compute, but there are several key differences that we explain below.

Explanation of the model:

Recall that (as explained in Section
1.2.2
) the Goldreich-Levin theorem (stated precisely in Theorem
1.10
) has the following form:

•

We are given an arbitrary function
f:\{0,1\}^{\ell}\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{\ell}
. (Intuitively, it is assumed that
f
is a one-way function, meaning that it is hard to invert
f
with success probability
\rho
)

•

There is a specified construction that transforms
f
into two functions: A “new one-way function”
f^{\operatorname{newOWF}}:\{0,1\}^{\ell^{\prime}}\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{\ell^{\prime}}
and a predicate
f^{\operatorname{pred}}:\{0,1\}^{\ell^{\prime}}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
for some
\ell^{\prime}
related to
\ell
. (Intuitively, we will want to argue that
f^{\operatorname{pred}}
is a hard-core predicate such that for
x\leftarrow U_{\ell^{\prime}}
,
f^{\operatorname{pred}}(x)
is hard to compute with success
\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon
when given
f^{\operatorname{newOWF}}(x)
).

We will model this construction as a map
\operatorname{Con}
which given
f
produces a pair of functions
(f^{\operatorname{newOWF}},f^{\operatorname{pred}})
. Once again, we place
essentially no limitations
on the map
\operatorname{Con}
(and in particular do not require that
f^{\operatorname{newOWF}},f^{\operatorname{pred}}
can be efficiently computed if
f
is). This only makes our results stronger. We remark that unlike the case of functions that are hard to compute, in this setting, we do place some limitations on the construction map
\operatorname{Con}
(specifically, that the construction of hard-core predicate is nontrivial, in a precise sense explained right after Definition
5.9
).

In the case of Theorem
1.10
, we have that:
\operatorname{Con}(f)=(f^{\operatorname{newOWF}},f^{\operatorname{pred}})
where
\ell^{\prime}=2\ell
and we think of the
\ell^{\prime}
bit long input of
(f^{\operatorname{newOWF}},f^{\operatorname{pred}})
as two strings
x,r\in\{0,1\}^{\ell}
, setting:

f^{\operatorname{newOWF}}(x,r)=(f(x),r),\mbox{and}

f^{\operatorname{pred}}(x,r)=\operatorname{Enc}^{\operatorname{Had}}(x)_{r}=(\sum_{i\in[\ell]}x_{i}\cdot r_{i})\mod 2.

•

We model the proof showing that
f^{\operatorname{pred}}
is a hard-core predicate, in the following way: The proof is a pair
(\operatorname{Con},\operatorname{Red})
where
\operatorname{Red}^{(\cdot)}
is an oracle procedure, such that when
\operatorname{Red}^{(\cdot)}
receives oracle access to an “adversary”
h:\{0,1\}^{\ell^{\prime}}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
that breaks the security of
f^{\operatorname{pred}}
, we have that
\operatorname{Red}^{h}
breaks the security of
f
.

It is illustrative to consider the case where
f,f^{\operatorname{newOWF}}
are
permutations
, and with this choice, the model we have introduced so far is identical to the one considered in Section
5.1
if we set
g=f^{-1}
.

In the setup of functions that are
hard to invert
, a reduction
\operatorname{Red}
can potentially want to
compute the function
f
(as we are implicitly assuming that
f
is efficiently computable). Many reductions in the cryptographic literature (e.g. from one-way functions to pseudorandom generators) critically rely on this ability, and so, if we want to handle a general case, we should allow the reduction
\operatorname{Red}
to also
receive oracle access to
f
, allowing it to compute
f
on chosen values, if it wants to.

This means that in the actual definition,
\operatorname{Red}^{(\cdot,\cdot)}
is an oracle procedure with
two
oracles: It receives oracle access both to
h
and to
f
.
More precisely, we require that: for every
f:\{0,1\}^{\ell}\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{\ell}
and for every
h:\{0,1\}^{\ell^{\prime}}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
, such that:

\Pr_{x\leftarrow U_{\ell^{\prime}}}[h(f^{\operatorname{newOWF}}(x))=f^{\operatorname{pred}}(x)]\geq\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon,

it holds that:

\Pr_{x\leftarrow U_{\ell}}[\operatorname{Red}^{h,f}(f(x))\in f^{-1}(f(x))]\geq\rho.

Note that this means that even in the case that
f,f^{\operatorname{newOWF}}
are permutations, reductions (in the setting of functions that are hard to invert) are more powerful then reductions (in the setting of functions that are hard to compute) for the function
g=f^{-1}
, and indeed, it is more difficult to prove impossibility results for the case of functions that are hard to invert.

•

As explained in the case of functions that are hard to compute, we are once again aiming to prove a result for circuits (which are allowed to use nonuniform advice) and as in the case of functions that are hard to compute, we will allow the reduction to receive an advice string
\alpha
of length
t
. (Intuitively, this advice string can depend on
f
and
h
). This leads to the following strengthening of the requirement above. Namely, we will require that: for every
f:\{0,1\}^{\ell}\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{\ell}
and for every
h:\{0,1\}^{\ell^{\prime}}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
, such that:

\Pr_{x\leftarrow U_{\ell^{\prime}}}[h(f^{\operatorname{newOWF}}(x))=f^{\operatorname{pred}}(x)]\geq\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon,

there exists
\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{t}
such that:

\Pr_{x\leftarrow U_{\ell}}[\operatorname{Red}^{h,f}(f(x),\alpha)\in f^{-1}(f(x))]\geq\rho.

•

Once again, we make no restrictions on the complexity of the procedure
\operatorname{Red}^{(\cdot,\cdot)}
except for requiring that it makes at most
q
queries to each of its two oracles (for some parameter
q
). Our black-box impossibility results will follow from proving lower bounds on
q
.

Formal definition:

Following this discussion, we now give a formal definition.

Definition 5.9

(Nonuniform black-box proof for hard-core predicates for hard to invert functions)
.

A pair
(\operatorname{Con},\operatorname{Red})
is a
nonuniform black-box proof
for
hard-core predicates for hard to invert functions
with parameters
\ell,\ell^{\prime},\rho,\epsilon
, that uses
q
queries
, and
t
bits of advice
if:

•

\operatorname{Con}
is a
construction map
which given a function
f:\{0,1\}^{\ell}\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{\ell}
, produces two functions
\operatorname{Con}(f)=(f^{\operatorname{newOWF}},f^{\operatorname{pred}})
such that
f^{\operatorname{newOWF}}:\{0,1\}^{\ell^{\prime}}\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{\ell^{\prime}}
and
f^{\operatorname{pred}}:\{0,1\}^{\ell^{\prime}}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
.

•

\operatorname{Red}^{(\cdot,\cdot)}
is a
reduction
, that is an oracle procedure that given oracle access to functions
h:\{0,1\}^{\ell^{\prime}}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
, and
f:\{0,1\}^{\ell}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
, makes at most
q
queries to each of its two oracles.

Furthermore, for every functions
f:\{0,1\}^{\ell}\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{\ell}
and
h:\{0,1\}^{\ell^{\prime}}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
such that:

\Pr_{x\leftarrow U_{\ell^{\prime}}}[h(f^{\operatorname{newOWF}}(x))=f^{\operatorname{pred}}(x)]\geq\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon,

there exists
\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{t}
, such that:

\Pr_{x\leftarrow U_{\ell}}[\operatorname{Red}^{h,f}(f(x),\alpha)\in f^{-1}(f(x))]\geq\rho.

Avoiding trivial constructions:

We now explain that it is possible to have black-box proofs that are
trivial
and provide hard-core predicates that are hard because of trivial reasons. We need to avoid such trivial constructions if we want to prove interesting limitations.

Specifically, it is easy to obtain hard-core predicates that are hard because information on
f^{\operatorname{pred}}(x)
is not present in
f^{\operatorname{newOWF}}(x)
. Indeed, consider the construction
\operatorname{Con}(f)=(f^{\operatorname{newOWF}},f^{\operatorname{pred}})
with:

f^{\operatorname{pred}}(x)=x_{1},\mbox{and}

f^{\operatorname{newOWF}}(x)=x_{2},\ldots,x_{\ell}.

In this case,
f^{\operatorname{pred}}
is a hardcore-predicate, because it is impossible (even for unbounded adversaries) to compute
f^{\operatorname{pred}}(x)
when given
f^{\operatorname{newOWF}}(x)
. This means that for this construction map, there is a reduction that makes
q=0
queries. Consequently, in order to prove lower bounds, we need to avoid such trivial (and uninteresting) construction maps, and require that for every
f
, there exists a function
\phi_{f}:\{0,1\}^{\ell^{\prime}}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
such that for every
x\in\{0,1\}^{\ell^{\prime}}
,
\phi_{f}(f^{\operatorname{newOWF}}(x))=f^{\operatorname{pred}}(x)
, meaning that there is information on
f^{\operatorname{pred}}(x)
in
f^{\operatorname{newOWF}}(x)
.

An additional case of a trivial construction that we want to avoid, is the case in which

H_{\infty}(f^{\operatorname{newOWF}}(U_{\ell^{\prime}}))<\log(1/\rho).

Such a construction is uninteresting, because in such a case, if we define the function
\psi_{f}:\{0,1\}^{\ell^{\prime}}\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{\ell^{\prime}}
to output the constant
x\in\{0,1\}^{\ell}
such that

\Pr[f^{\operatorname{newOWF}}(U_{\ell^{\prime}})=f^{\operatorname{newOWF}}(x)]\geq\rho,

(and note that such an
x
exists if
H_{\infty}(f^{\operatorname{newOWF}}(U_{\ell^{\prime}}))<\log(1/\rho)
) then we get that there is a constant function
\psi_{f}
such that
\psi_{f}
inverts the function
f^{\operatorname{newOWF}}
with probability
\rho
. Such a construction is uninteresting because in that case
f^{\operatorname{newOWF}}
is obviously not a one-way function.

This leads to the following characterization of nontrivial construction maps, in which we require that
\operatorname{Con}
avoids these two trivial examples.

Definition 5.10

(Nontrivial construction map)
.

We say that a construction map
\operatorname{Con}(f)=(f^{\operatorname{newOWF}},f^{\operatorname{pred}})
is
\rho
-
non-trivial
if it satisfies the following two requirements:

•

For every
f
, the functions
(f^{\operatorname{newOWF}},f^{\operatorname{pred}})
produced by
\operatorname{Con}(f)
are such that there exists a function
\phi_{f}:\{0,1\}^{\ell^{\prime}}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
such that for every
x\in\{0,1\}^{\ell^{\prime}}
,
\phi_{f}(f^{\operatorname{newOWF}}(x))=f^{\operatorname{pred}}(x)
.

•

H_{\infty}(f^{\operatorname{newOWF}}(U_{\ell^{\prime}}))\geq\log(1/\rho)
.

We say that a pair
(\operatorname{Con},\operatorname{Red})
is
\rho
-
nontrivial
, if
\operatorname{Con}
is
\rho
-non-trivial.

The role of the number of queries, and black-box impossibility results:

We now explain the role of the parameter
q
(that measures the number of queries made by
\operatorname{Red}
) and why lower bounds on
q
translate into black-box impossibility results. This explanation is similar to the one given in Section
5.1
(with the modifications explained above).

For this purpose, it is illustrative to examine the argument showing that nonuniform black-box proofs yield hard-core predicates: When given a pair
(\operatorname{Con},\operatorname{Red})
that is a nonuniform black-box proof for hard-core predicates for hard to invert functions with parameters
\ell,\ell^{\prime},\rho,\epsilon
, that uses
q
queries, and
t
bits of advice, we obtain that for any function
f:\{0,1\}^{\ell}\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{\ell}
, if there exists a circuit
C^{\prime}:\{0,1\}^{\ell^{\prime}}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
of size
s^{\prime}
such that:

\Pr_{x\leftarrow U_{\ell^{\prime}}}[C^{\prime}(f^{\operatorname{newOWF}}(x))=f^{\operatorname{pred}}(x)]\geq\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon,

then there exists
\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{t}
, such that:

\Pr_{x\leftarrow U_{\ell}}[\operatorname{Red}^{C^{\prime},f}(f(x),\alpha)\in f^{-1}(f(x))]\geq\rho.

Note that if the reduction
\operatorname{Red}
can be implemented by a circuit of size
r
, and the function
f
can be computed by a circuit of size
m
,
then the circuit
C(y)=\operatorname{Red}^{C^{\prime},f}(y,\alpha)
is a circuit of size:

s=r+t+q\cdot m+q\cdot s^{\prime}

that inverts
f
with success probability
\rho
.

It follows that in a black-box proof, with
q
queries, and
t
bits of advice, we get a hard-core theorem that needs to assume that the original function
f
cannot be inverted by circuits of size
s
, for:

s\geq q+t.

5.2.2
Precise statements of limitations

Our main result on black-box proofs for hard-core predicates in the setting of functions that are hard to invert is the following theorem.

Theorem 5.11
.

There exist universal constants
\beta>0
and
c>1
such that the following holds for any sufficiently large
\ell
and
\ell^{\prime}
,

t\leq 2^{\ell/5}
,
\rho\geq 2^{-\ell/5}
, and
\rho\leq\beta\cdot\epsilon^{2}
. Let
(\operatorname{Con},\operatorname{Red})
be a
\rho
-nontrivial nonuniform black-box proof for hard-core predicates for hard to invert functions with parameters
\ell,\ell^{\prime},\rho,\epsilon
, that uses
q
queries, and
t
bits of advice. Then

q\geq\frac{1}{\epsilon^{\beta}}-c(t+\ell).

We now explain why Theorem
5.11
implies the informal statement made in Theorem
1.13
. This explanation is essentially identical to the one following Theorem
5.2
.

Recall that in Section
5.2.1
we explained that when using a nonuniform black-box proof to obtain hard-core predicates, we get a hard-core predicate theorem in which
s\geq q+t
.

Theorem
5.11
implies that it is impossible for such a proof to establish
\epsilon=s^{-2/\beta}
(even if
\rho
is very small). This follows as otherwise, using the fact that
s\geq q+t\geq t
and
s\geq\ell
, we get that:

q\geq\frac{1}{\epsilon^{\beta}}-c(t+\ell)\geq s^{2}-c(t+\ell)>s,

which is a contradiction to
s\geq q+t\geq q
.

Next note that we may assume that
\rho=2^{-\ell/5}
, as by definition, if
(\operatorname{Con},\operatorname{Red})
is a nonuniform black-box proof for hard-core predicates with parameter
\rho^{\prime}>\rho
, then it is also a nonuniform black-box proof for hard-core predicates with parameter
\rho
.
Similarly, we may take
\epsilon=\frac{1}{s^{\omega(1)}}
to be sufficiently large so that
\epsilon\geq 2^{-o(\ell)}
for
s=2^{-o(\ell)}
.
Under these assumptions, we have that

\rho\leq\beta\cdot\epsilon^{2}
, and therefore the parameter setting considered in Theorem
1.13
is impossible to achieve.

5.2.3
Proof of Theorem
5.11

The proof of Theorem
5.11
is similar in structure to the proof of Theorem
5.2
with three main differences:

•

Rather than choosing the initial function uniformly from
F_{\ell}
(the set of all functions from
\ell
bits to
\ell
bits) we will restrict the choice to permutations. This is helpful because for a permutation
f
, the function
f^{-1}
is well defined, and inverting
f
(that is producing an element in
f^{-1}(f(x))
when given
f(x)
) can be thought of as computing
f^{-1}
that is producing
x
on input
f(x)
.

•

A more significant difference, is that as explained in detail in Section
5.2.1
, in the setup of functions that are hard to invert, the reduction
\operatorname{Red}
has oracle access to
f
(in addition to oracle access to
h
). This means that it is no longer the case that the answer of the reduction on inputs
x,\alpha
and oracle
h
is
determined
by
h,x,\alpha
(as the answer depends on
f
). Therefore, it is not the case that there exists circuits
A_{x,\alpha}(h)
that simulate the reduction (as we argued in Claim
5.5
) and we need to be more careful when showing that for every function
f
, there exists a circuit
C_{f}(z)
that is analogous to the circuit guaranteed in Claim
5.6
.

Furthermore, as
\operatorname{Red}
gets oracle access to
f
, we can no longer claim that when
h
is independent of
f
, then
\operatorname{Red}
has no information on
f
. Instead, we use results by Gennaro and Trevisan
[
GT00
]
showing that an oracle circuit that makes a subexponential number of queries to a random permutation
f
, cannot invert
f
with high probability.

•

Unlike the case of Section
5.1
, the distribution that is given as input to
h
is not necessarily uniform. More precisely, the distribution of
f^{\operatorname{newOWF}}(U_{\ell^{\prime}})
(on which
h
needs to predict the hard-core predicate) is not necessarily uniform. However, by the nontriviality condition in Definition
5.10
we have that this distribution has high min-entropy, and we need to adjust the argument to hold with this weaker requirement.

Theorem
5.11
will follow from the next lemma.

Lemma 5.12
.

There exists a universal constant
d>1

such that the following holds
for any sufficiently large
\ell
and
\ell^{\prime}
,
t\leq 2^{\ell/5}
,
\rho\geq 2^{-\ell/5}
and
\rho\leq\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{d}
. Let
(\operatorname{Con},\operatorname{Red})
be a
\rho
-nontrivial nonuniform black-box proof for hard-core predicates for hard to invert functions with parameters
\ell,\ell^{\prime},\rho,\epsilon
, that uses
q
queries, and
t
bits of advice. Then there exists a circuit
C
of size
{\operatorname{poly}}(2^{q},2^{\ell},2^{t})
and depth
d
such that:

•

\Pr_{z\leftarrow\operatorname{BSC}^{n}_{\frac{1}{2}-2\epsilon}}[C(z)=1]\geq 0.99
.

•

\Pr_{z\leftarrow\operatorname{BSC}^{n}_{\frac{1}{2}}}[C(z)=1]\leq 0.01
.

Once again, just like in the previous section, by reduction to the coin problem, Theorem
5.11
follows from Lemma
5.12
.

Proof of Theorem
5.11
.

The theorem follows directly from Lemma
5.12
and Corollary
3.5
, which give that:

{\operatorname{poly}}(2^{q},2^{\ell},2^{t})\geq\exp(d\cdot\epsilon^{-\frac{1}{d-1}}).

The statement of Theorem
5.11
follows by taking the logarithm on both sides and setting
\beta<\frac{1}{d-1}
.
∎

In the remainder of this section we prove Lemma
5.12
.
Let
(\operatorname{Con},\operatorname{Red})
be a nontrivial nonuniform black-box proof for hard-core predicates for hard to invert functions with parameters
\ell,\ell^{\prime},\rho,\epsilon
, that uses
q
queries, and
t
bits of advice. Throughout this section we assume that the requirements made in Lemma
5.12
are met.

We will prove Lemma
5.12
using the following sequence of claims. The proof uses the same structure as the proof of Lemma
5.3
however, the reduction is now more powerful as it has oracle access to the function
f
, and the setting is more general as the distribution
f^{\operatorname{newOWF}}(U_{\ell})
(on which the oracle is judged) is not necessarily uniform.
In this setting, there is no direct analog of Claim
5.5
, and instead we prove an analog of Claim
5.6
directly.

Claim 5.13
.

There exists a universal constant
d
such that
for every permutation
f:\{0,1\}^{\ell}\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{\ell}
, there exists a circuit
C_{f}:\{0,1\}^{2^{\ell^{\prime}}}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
of size
{\operatorname{poly}}(2^{q},2^{\ell},2^{t})
and depth
d
such that the following holds for every
z\in\{0,1\}^{2^{\ell^{\prime}}}
:

•

If there exists
\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{t}
such that
\Pr_{x\leftarrow U_{\ell}}[\operatorname{Red}^{\phi_{f}\oplus z,f}(f(x),\alpha)=x]\geq\rho
then
C_{f}(z)=1
.

•

If for all
\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{t}
,
\Pr_{x\leftarrow U_{\ell}}[\operatorname{Red}^{\phi_{f}\oplus z,f}(f(x),\alpha)=x]\leq\rho/10
then
C_{f}(z)=0
.

Proof.

The circuit
C_{f}
will be hardwired with
f
and
\phi_{f}
(where
\phi_{f}
is the function whose existence is guaranteed for
f
by the nontriviality condition in Definition
5.10
). Upon receiving an input
z\in\{0,1\}^{2^{\ell^{\prime}}}
it will act as follows:

•

Prepare
w=\phi_{f}\oplus z
. (Here we think of
\phi_{f},z,w
as strings in
\{0,1\}^{2^{\ell^{\prime}}}
).

•

For every
x\in\{0,1\}^{\ell}
and
\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{t}
compute
\operatorname{Red}^{w,f}(f(x),\alpha)
, and compute

b_{x,\alpha}\in\{0,1\}
defined by:

b_{x,\alpha}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}0&\operatorname{Red}^{w,f}(f(x),\alpha)\neq x\\
1&\operatorname{Red}^{w,f}(f(x),\alpha)=x\end{array}\right.

•

For every
\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{t}
, let
v_{\alpha}
denote the
2^{\ell}
bit long concatenation of all bits
(b_{x,\alpha})_{x\in\{0,1\}^{\ell}}
(fixing some order on
x\in\{0,1\}^{\ell})
, and compute

b_{\alpha}=D^{2^{\ell}}_{\rho}(v_{\alpha}),

where
D^{2^{\ell}}_{\rho}
is the circuit guaranteed in Lemma
5.4
.

•

Compute the disjunction of the
2^{t}
bits
(b_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{t}}
and output it.

It is immediate that the circuit
C_{f}
performs the task specified in the lemma.
We now explain how to implement the circuit in small size and depth.

The string
f
can be described by
\ell\cdot 2^{\ell}
bits. We note that when using the string
\phi_{f}
to prepare
w
, we only need to have
\phi_{f}
at coordinates
y\in\{0,1\}^{\ell^{\prime}}
such that there exist
x,\alpha
such that
\operatorname{Red}^{w,f}(f(x),\alpha)
depends on
w_{y}
. As on every pair
(f(x),\alpha)
the reduction
\operatorname{Red}^{w,f}(f(x),\alpha)
makes at most
q
queries to its oracle
w
, it can depend on at most
2^{q}
choices of
y\in\{0,1\}^{\ell^{\prime}}
.
Thus, going over all choices of
x\in\{0,1\}^{\ell}
and
\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{t}
,
C_{g}
only requires
O(2^{t}\cdot 2^{\ell}\cdot 2^{q})
bits of
\phi_{f}
. Note that any query that the reduction makes to its oracle
f
, is a constant that
C_{f}
has hardwired (because
C_{f}
is hardwired with
f
).
Overall, the size of the advice of
C_{f}
is
O(2^{t}\cdot 2^{\ell}\cdot 2^{q})
.
The circuit
C_{f}
is constant depth by construction, and its size is indeed:

{\operatorname{poly}}(2^{q},2^{\ell},2^{t},1/\rho)={\operatorname{poly}}(2^{q},2^{\ell},2^{t}),

by the requirement that
\rho\geq 2^{-\ell}
.
∎

We will consider the case where
f:\{0,1\}^{\ell}\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{\ell}
is a uniformly chosen permutation, and will analyze the behavior of
C_{f}
on
z\leftarrow\operatorname{BSC}^{2^{\ell^{\prime}}}_{\frac{1}{2}-2\epsilon}
and on
z\leftarrow\operatorname{BSC}^{2^{\ell^{\prime}}}_{\frac{1}{2}}
.
In what follows, let
\Pi_{\ell}
denote the set of all permutations
f:\{0,1\}^{\ell}\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{\ell}
.

Claim 5.14
.

\Pr_{f\leftarrow\Pi_{\ell},z\leftarrow\operatorname{BSC}^{2^{\ell^{\prime}}}_{\frac{1}{2}-2\epsilon}}[C_{f}(z)=1]\geq 0.999
.

Proof.

Imagine that
f\leftarrow\Pi_{\ell}
is already chosen and fixed. Let
(f^{\operatorname{newOWF}},f^{\operatorname{pred}})=\operatorname{Con}(f)
, and let
\phi_{f}:\{0,1\}^{\ell^{\prime}}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
be the function guaranteed by the fact that
\operatorname{Con}
is nontrivial.
We now consider the additional experiment of choosing
z\leftarrow\operatorname{BSC}^{2^{\ell^{\prime}}}_{\frac{1}{2}-2\epsilon}
.
Let
h:\{0,1\}^{\ell^{\prime}}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
be defined by
h=\phi_{f}\oplus z
. Our goal is to show that with probability at least
0.999
over choosing
z\leftarrow\operatorname{BSC}^{2^{\ell^{\prime}}}_{\frac{1}{2}-2\epsilon}
, we have that:

\Pr_{x\leftarrow U_{\ell^{\prime}}}[h(f^{\operatorname{newOWF}}(x))=f^{\operatorname{pred}}(x)]\geq\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon.

(2)

This is because whenever
f,z
satisfy the condition above, then by the properties of
\operatorname{Red}
, we have that there exists
\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{t}
such that:

\Pr_{x\leftarrow U_{\ell}}[\operatorname{Red}^{h,f}(f(x),\alpha)=x]\geq\rho.

which in turn by Claim
5.13
implies that
C_{f}(z)=1
.

By definition, for every
x\in\{0,1\}^{\ell^{\prime}}
,
\phi_{f}(f^{\operatorname{newOWF}}(x))=f^{\operatorname{pred}}(x)
. Consequently, the event

\left\{{h(f^{\operatorname{newOWF}}(x))=f^{\operatorname{pred}}(x)}\right\}

that appears in (
2
) can be expressed as

\left\{{h(f^{\operatorname{newOWF}}(x))=\phi_{f}(f^{\operatorname{newOWF}}(x))}\right\}.

As
h=\phi_{f}\oplus z
, this event can also be expressed as

\left\{{z(f^{\operatorname{newOWF}}(x))=0}\right\}.

Thus, in order to prove the claim, it is sufficient to prove that with probability at least
0.999
over choosing
z\leftarrow\operatorname{BSC}^{2^{\ell^{\prime}}}_{\frac{1}{2}-2\epsilon}
, we have that:

\Pr_{x\leftarrow U_{\ell^{\prime}}}[z(f^{\operatorname{newOWF}}(x))=0]\geq\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon.

Note that
Y=f^{\operatorname{newOWF}}(U_{\ell^{\prime}})
is not necessarily uniform. For every
y\in\{0,1\}^{\ell^{\prime}}
, we define
p_{y}=\Pr[Y=y]
.
By the nontriviality of
\operatorname{Con}
we have that
H_{\infty}(Y)\geq\log(1/\rho)
, which means that for every
y\in\{0,1\}^{\ell^{\prime}}
,
p_{y}\leq\rho
.
Thus, in order to conclude the proof, it is sufficient to show that:

\Pr_{z\leftarrow\operatorname{BSC}^{2^{\ell^{\prime}}}_{\frac{1}{2}-2\epsilon}}[\sum_{y\in\{0,1\}^{\ell^{\prime}}}p_{y}\cdot z_{y}<\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon]\geq 0.999.

(This can be thought of as a “weighted version” of Hamming weight in which the
p_{y}
are not all the same).
When
z\leftarrow\operatorname{BSC}^{2^{\ell^{\prime}}}_{\frac{1}{2}-2\epsilon}
, the
2^{\ell^{\prime}}
random variables
x_{y}=p_{y}\cdot z_{y}
(one for each choice of
y\in\{0,1\}^{\ell^{\prime}})
are independent, and lie in the interval
[0,\rho]
. We have that:

\mathbb{E}_{z\leftarrow\operatorname{BSC}^{2^{\ell^{\prime}}}_{\frac{1}{2}-2\epsilon}}[\sum_{y\in\{0,1\}^{\ell^{\prime}}}p_{y}\cdot z_{y}]=\frac{1}{2}-2\epsilon.

We can apply a Chernoff bound to bound the probability of deviation from the expectation and obtain that:

\Pr_{z\leftarrow\operatorname{BSC}^{2^{\ell^{\prime}}}_{\frac{1}{2}-2\epsilon}}[\sum_{y\in\{0,1\}^{\ell^{\prime}}}p_{y}\cdot z_{y}<\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon]\leq e^{-\Omega(\epsilon^{2}/\rho)},

By our requirement that
\rho\leq\beta\cdot\epsilon^{2}
for a sufficiently small
\beta>0
, we get that the probability is indeed larger than
0.999
.
∎

On the other hand, we can show that:

Claim 5.15
.

\Pr_{f\leftarrow\Pi_{\ell},z\leftarrow\operatorname{BSC}^{2^{\ell^{\prime}}}_{\frac{1}{2}}}[C_{f}(z)=1]\leq 0.001
.

In order to prove Claim
5.15
we will use the following result by Gennaro and Trevisan
[
GT00
]
:

Theorem 5.16

(
[
GT00
]
)
.

For sufficiently large
\ell
, for every oracle procedure
P^{(\cdot)}
that makes at most
2^{\ell/5}
queries to its oracle, and accepts inputs
x\in\{0,1\}^{\ell}
and
\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{t}
for
t\leq 2^{\ell/5}
, it holds that:

\Pr_{f\leftarrow\Pi_{\ell}}[\exists\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{t}:\ \Pr_{x\leftarrow U_{\ell}}[P^{f}(f(x),\alpha)=x]\geq 2^{-\ell/5}]\leq 2^{-2^{\ell/2}}

We now prove Claim
5.15
.

Proof of Claim
5.15
.

The first step of
C_{f}(z)
is to prepare
w=\phi_{f}\oplus z
.
However, for
f\leftarrow\Pi_{\ell}
and
z\leftarrow\operatorname{BSC}_{\frac{1}{2}}^{2^{\ell^{\prime}}}
, we have that
w=\phi_{f}\oplus z
is uniformly chosen, and independent of
f
.

Therefore, for any choice of advice
\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{t}
oracle access to
w
does not help the reduction
\operatorname{Red}(\cdot,\alpha)
to invert
f
. More precisely, Let
P^{(\cdot)}(x,\alpha)
be an implementation of
\operatorname{Red}^{(\cdot,\cdot)}
where whenever
\operatorname{Red}
makes a query to its first oracle
h
, the query is answered by a fresh uniform random bit.
We have that:

\Pr_{f\leftarrow\Pi_{\ell},z\leftarrow\operatorname{BSC}^{2^{\ell^{\prime}}}_{\frac{1}{2}}}[C_{f}(z)=1]

\ \leq\Pr_{f\leftarrow\Pi_{\ell},z\leftarrow\operatorname{BSC}^{2^{\ell^{\prime}}}_{\frac{1}{2}}}[\exists\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{t}:\ \Pr_{x\leftarrow U_{\ell}}[\operatorname{Red}^{\phi_{f}\oplus z,f}(f(x),\alpha)=x]\geq 2^{-\ell/5}]

\ =\Pr_{f\leftarrow\Pi_{\ell},w\leftarrow\operatorname{BSC}^{2^{\ell^{\prime}}}_{\frac{1}{2}}}[\exists\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{t}:\ \Pr_{x\leftarrow U_{\ell}}[\operatorname{Red}^{w,f}(f(x),\alpha)=x]\geq 2^{-\ell/5}]

\leq\Pr_{f\leftarrow\Pi_{\ell}}[\exists\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{t}:\ \Pr_{x\leftarrow U_{\ell}}[P^{f}(f(x),\alpha)=x]\geq 2^{-\ell/5}]

\leq 2^{-2^{\ell/2}}\leq 0.001,

where the first inequality follows from Claim
5.13
and assumption that
\rho\geq 2^{-\ell/5}
, and
where the penultimate inequality follows from Theorem
5.16
.
∎

The proof of Lemma
5.12
now follows from Claims
5.14

.

From Claims
5.14
and

\displaystyle\Pr_{f\leftarrow\Pi_{\ell}}\left[\Pr_{z\leftarrow\operatorname{BSC}^{2^{\ell^{\prime}}}_{\frac{1}{2}-2\epsilon}}[C_{f}(z)=0]>0.01\right]

Appendix A
Proof of Lemma
3.1

In this section we prove Lemma
3.1
, restated below.

See
3.1

In this proof we use the following notation. For two distributions
X,Y
over
\{0,1\}^{n}
, we say that they are
\epsilon
-close if for every event
A\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}
,
|\Pr[X\in A]-\Pr[Y\in A]|\leq\epsilon
. We will also use Shannon’s entropy given by

H(X)=\sum_{x}\Pr(X=x)\cdot\log\left(\frac{1}{\Pr(X=x)}\right),

and the following statement of Pinsker’s lemma:

Lemma A.1

(Pinsker’s lemma)
.

If
X
is a distribution over
\{0,1\}^{n}
and
H(X)\geq n-\epsilon
, then
X
is
\sqrt{\epsilon}
-close to
U_{n}
.

Proof of lemma
3.1
.

By the requirements on
M
, we have that
H(M)\geq k-k^{0.99}
.
The Shannon entropy function satisfies
H(M_{1})+\ldots+H(M_{k})\geq H(M_{1},\ldots,M_{k})
and therefore:

H(M_{1})+\ldots+H(M_{k})\geq k-k^{0.99}

It follows that:

\mathbb{E}_{i\leftarrow[k]}H(M_{i})=1-k^{-0.01}.

By Markov’s inequality, for every
c
, the fraction of
i\in[k]
such that
H(M_{i})<1-c\cdot k^{-0.01}
is less than
1/c
.
By Pinsker’s lemma, for every
i
such that
H(M_{i})\geq 1-c\cdot k^{-0.01}
, we have that
M_{i}
is
\sqrt{c\cdot k^{-0.01}}
-close to
U_{1}
.
Therefore,

\Pr_{m\leftarrow M,i\leftarrow[k]}[D(i)=m_{i}]\leq\frac{1}{2}+\sqrt{c\cdot k^{-0.01}}+1/c\leq 0.5001,

for a sufficiently large constant
c
, and sufficiently large
k
.
∎

Appendix B
Proof of Corollary
3.5

In this Section we prove Corollary
3.5
, restated below.

See
3.5

The proof is by reduction to Theorem
3.4
.

Proof of Corollary
3.5
.

For
\epsilon^{\prime}=\Theta(\epsilon
), given a circuit
C
that satisfies:

•

\Pr_{z\leftarrow\operatorname{BSC}^{n}_{\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon^{\prime}}}[C(z)=1]\geq 0.99
,

•

\Pr_{z\leftarrow\operatorname{BSC}^{n}_{\frac{1}{2}}}[C(z)=1]\leq 0.01
.

We will show the existence of a circuit
C^{\prime}
that satisfies:

•

\Pr_{z\leftarrow\operatorname{BSC}^{n}_{\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon}}[C^{\prime}(z)=1]\geq 0.9
,

•

\Pr_{z\leftarrow\operatorname{BSC}^{n}_{\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon}}[C^{\prime}(z)=1]\leq 0.1
.

We will start by constructing a randomized circuit
C^{\prime}
which upon receiving input
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, for every
i\in[n]
independently,
C^{\prime}
replaces input bit
x_{i}
by zero with probability
p=\frac{2\epsilon}{1+2\epsilon}
, let
x^{\prime}_{i}
denote the obtained bit, and let
C^{\prime}(x)=C(x^{\prime})
.
The choice of
p
is made so that for every
i
:

•

If
x\leftarrow\operatorname{BSC}^{n}_{\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon}
then
x^{\prime}\leftarrow\operatorname{BSC}^{n}_{\frac{1}{2}}
.

•

if
x\leftarrow\operatorname{BSC}^{n}_{\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon}
then
x^{\prime}\leftarrow\operatorname{BSC}^{n}_{\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon^{\prime}}
for
\epsilon^{\prime}=\epsilon+\epsilon\cdot\frac{1-2\epsilon}{1+2\epsilon}=\Theta(\epsilon)
.

It follows that if
C
distinguishes between
\operatorname{BSC}^{n}_{\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon^{\prime}}
and
\operatorname{BSC}^{n}_{\frac{1}{2}}
then
C^{\prime}
satisfies:

•

\Pr_{z\leftarrow\operatorname{BSC}^{n}_{\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon}}[C^{\prime}(z)=1]\geq 0.99
,

•

\Pr_{z\leftarrow\operatorname{BSC}^{n}_{\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon}}[C^{\prime}(z)=1]\leq 0.01
.

This gives that:

\Pr_{z\leftarrow\operatorname{BSC}^{n}_{\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon}}[C^{\prime}(z)=1]-\Pr_{z\leftarrow\operatorname{BSC}^{n}_{\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon}}[C^{\prime}(z)=1]\geq 0.98,

where the probability in the expressions above is also over the randomness of
C^{\prime}
.
Therefore, there exists a fixing of the random coins of
C^{\prime}
which achieves this gap of
0.98
and, hence, satisfies the requirements on
C^{\prime}
(this follows because for numbers
0\leq p\leq P<1
that satisfy
P-p\geq 0.98
, it holds that:
P\geq 0.9
and
p\leq 0.1
).
We note that the size and depth of
C^{\prime}
are bounded by the size and depth of
C
respectively, and the corollary follows.
∎
