A framework for Conditional Reasoning in Answer Set Programming
Mario Alviano, Laura Giordano, Daniele Theseider Dupré

Abstract
In this paper we introduce a Conditional Answer Set Programming framework (Conditional ASP) for the definition of conditional extensions of Answer Set Programming (ASP). The approach builds on a conditional logic with typicality, and on the combination of a conditional knowledge base with an ASP program, and allows for conditional reasoning over the answer sets of the program. The formalism relies on a multi-preferential semantics (and on the KLM preferential semantics, as a special case) to provide an interpretation of conditionals.

Introduction

Preferential logic

[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
]
have been proposed to provide axiomatic foundations of non-monotonic or defeasible reasoning, and to capture commonsense reasoning.
They have their roots in conditional logics
[
10
,
11
]
,
and they allow for conditional statement (or simply,
conditionals
), of the form
\alpha\mathrel{{\scriptstyle\mid\!\sim}}\beta
, meaning that “normally if
\alpha
holds,
\beta
holds".

Answer set programming
(ASP) is one of the main computational logic paradigms. It is a formalism for declarative problem solving, allowing for the declarative specification of knowledge through a set of rules.
The semantics of an ASP program is given by assigning it a collection of
stable models

[
12
,
13
]
, also called
answer sets
.

ASP has been used for encoding entailment in some preferential logics, specifically, for preferential extensions of low complexity Description Logics (DLs) of the
\mathcal{EL}^{\bot}
family
[
14
]
. In particular, ASP has been used for reasoning in a ranked concept-wise extension of
\mathcal{EL}^{\bot}
with typicality
[
15
]
,
for reasoning with many-valued weighted conditional knowledge bases
[
16
]
,
and also for defeasible reasoning in
\cal{SROEL}
, based on the Rational Closure
[
17
]
.

In this paper, we follow a different path. We aim at combining conditional logic and answer set programming by pairing a conditional knowledge base
K
with an ASP program
\Pi
,
with the aim of using the conditional logic in the verification of conditional properties of the ASP program. In the preferential semantics, a conditional knowledge base induces a preference relation between worlds (propositional interpretations);
when combined with an ASP program
\Pi
, the preference
relation is induced on the set of
answer sets
of
\Pi
,
and a preferential interpretation can be constructed from such a set.
Verifying
conditional properties of an ASP program
, given the associated conditional KB,
is especially of interest when
\Pi
has many answer sets, so that inspecting them is not viable.

In the literature many different semantics have been considered for conditional knowledge bases, and different closure constructions for strengthening preferential and rational entailment have been explored. Among them are the well known Rational Closure
[
6
]
and Lexicographic Closure
[
18
]
.
The Rational closure and the lexicographic closure exploit a single preference relation, while, in the paper, we explore an approach based on
multiple preference relations
, which has been considered in conditional Description Logics with typicality for ranked knowledge bases

[
15
]
, and for weighted (many-valued) knowledge bases
[
19
]
.

As in the Propositional Typicality Logic
[
20
]
(and in some DLs with typicality
[
21
]
), we adopt a language in which conditionals are formalized based on material implication plus the
typicality operator

{\bf T}
.
The typicality operator allows for the definition of
conditional implications

{\bf T}(\alpha)\rightarrow\beta
, which correspond to the KLM conditionals
\alpha\mathrel{{\scriptstyle\mid\!\sim}}\beta

[
4
,
6
]
, meaning that “in the typical situations in which
\alpha
holds,
\beta
also holds”.
For instance, the conditional implication:

{\bf T}(\mathit{granted\_Loan})\rightarrow\mathit{living}\_\mathit{in}\_\mathit{town}\wedge\mathit{young}

means that, normally, if a loan is granted to a person, she lives in town and is young.
The typicality operator allows for more general implications of the form
\alpha\rightarrow\beta
,
where
{\bf T}
may occur in both
\alpha
and
\beta
.
For instance, the converse implication

\mathit{living}\_\mathit{in}\_\mathit{town}\wedge\mathit{young}\rightarrow{\bf T}(\mathit{granted\_Loan})

means that living in town and being young implies that normally the loan is granted.

The preferential semantics of the logic exploits
multiple preference relations

<_{A}
with respect to different distinguished propositions
A
, following the approach developed for ranked and weighted KBs in description logics, based on a
multi-preferential semantics

[
15
,
16
]

and for the propositional calculus
[
22
]
, where preferences are allowed with respect to different aspects.

The formalism builds on a conditional logic with typicality, which encompasses the definition of a conditional knowledge base, and combines it with an ASP program to define a Conditional ASP program.

In the paper, we let a
Conditional ASP (CondASP) program
to be a pair
(\Pi,K)
, where
\Pi
is an ASP program and
K
is a
defeasible knowledge base
. We consider the special case where
K
is a
weighted KB, in which distinguished propositions
A_{i}
are associated with a set of conditionals, each one with a weight, representing the plausibility/implausibility of a defeasible property.

For instance, consider the following example, in which defeasible properties of students and employees are specified through a set of weighted conditionals, as follows:

(d_{1})

\mathit{{\bf T}(employee)\rightarrow young}
, -50
(d_{4})

\mathit{{\bf T}(student)\rightarrow young}
, 90

(d_{2})

\mathit{{\bf T}(employee)\rightarrow has\_boss}
, 100
(d_{5})

\mathit{{\bf T}(student)\rightarrow has\_boss}
, -80

(d_{3})

\mathit{{\bf T}(employee)\rightarrow has\_classes}
, -70
(d_{6})

\mathit{{\bf T}(student)\rightarrow has\_classes}
, 80

Conditionals
(d_{1}\--d_{3})
describe properties of typical employees,
who have a boss, and are not likely to be young nor to have classes;

(d_{4}\--d_{6})
describe properties of typical students.
Absolute values of weights correspond to degrees of (im)plausibility.
Consider a situation
S_{1}
describing a student and employee who is young, has no boss and has classes, and a situation
S_{2}
describing a student and employee who is not young, has a boss and has no classes.
Given the weights above, one would regard

S_{1}
as a more plausible situation for a student than
S_{2}
, i.e.,
S_{1}<_{\mathit{student}}S_{2}
;
and
S_{2}
as a more plausible situation for an employee than
S_{1}
, i.e.,

S_{2}<_{\mathit{employee}}S_{1}
.

In the paper, after defining (Section
2
)
a multi-preferential logic with typicality,
we develop in Section
3
a construction for defining, for a set of distinguished propositions
A_{i}
, preference relations
\leq_{A_{i}}
, preorders on the answer sets of the program.
In general, alternative constructions
may be viable for defining the preference relations
\leq_{A_{i}}
, depending on the definition of the conditional knowledge base (whether it contains conditionals having a rank, or having a weight, or none).
Although the proposed approach provides a framework for conditional reasoning in ASP, under different preferential interpretations of conditionals, the paper focuses on weighted KBs.

The formalism is complemented with an approach for combining preferences,
to define a preference
\leq_{A}
with respect to a boolean combination
A

of distinguished propositions
A_{i}
.
This is discussed in Section
4
, where
it is proved that
entailment of conditional implications
{\bf T}(A)\rightarrow B

from a CondASP program satisfies the KLM properties.
Section
5
provides an ASP approach for verifying
entailment from a CondASP program.

2
A Multi-Preferential Logic with Typicality

In this section we define a two-valued preferential logic with typicality, which generalizes Kraus Lehmann and Magidor preferential semantics
[
4
,
6
]
,
by allowing for multiple preference relations (i.e., preferences with respect to multiple aspects), rather that a single preference relation.
We also develop a notion of weighted conditional knowledge base, which will be instantiated, later, for Conditional ASP.

Let
{\cal L}
be a propositional logic, whose formulae are built from a set
Prop
of propositional variables using the boolean connectives
\wedge
,
\vee
,
\neg
and
\rightarrow
, as usual.
We assume that
\bot
(representing falsity) and
\top
(representing truth) are formulae of
{\cal L}
.

We extend
{\cal L}
by introducing a typicality operator as done in
[
23
]
and
[
20
]
for the propositional calculus.
Intuitively, “a sentence of the form
{\bf T}(\alpha)
is understood to refer to the
typical situations in which
\alpha
holds
"
[
20
]
.
The typicality operator allows for the formulation of
conditional implications
(or
defeasible implications
) of the form

{\bf T}(\alpha)\rightarrow\beta

whose meaning is that “normally, if
\alpha
then
\beta
”,
or “in the typical situations when
\alpha
holds,
\beta
also holds”.
They correspond to conditional implications
\alpha\mathrel{{\scriptstyle\mid\!\sim}}\beta
of KLM preferential logics
[
6
]
, but here we consider a preferential semantics which exploits multiple preferences.

As in PTL
[
20
]
, the typicality operator cannot be nested.
When
\alpha
and
\beta
do not contain occurrences of the typicality operator, an implication
\alpha\rightarrow\beta
is called
strict
.
We call
{\cal L}^{\bf T}
the logic obtained by extending
{\cal L}
with a unary typicality operator
{\bf T}
.
In the logic
{\cal L}^{\bf T}
,
general implications

\alpha\rightarrow\beta
are allowed, where
\alpha
and
\beta
may contain occurrences of the typicality operator.

As mentioned above, the interpretation of a typicality formula
{\bf T}(\alpha)
is defined with respect to a
multi-preferential interpretation
.
The KLM preferential semantics
[
4
,
6
,
3
]
exploits a set of worlds
\mathcal{W}
, with their valuation and a preference relation
<
among worlds (where
w<w^{\prime}
means that world
w
is more normal than world
w^{\prime}
).
A conditional
A\mathrel{{\scriptstyle\mid\!\sim}}B
is satisfied in a KLM preferential interpretation, if
B
holds in all the most normal worlds satisfying
A
, i.e., in all
<
-minimal worlds satisfying
A
.

Here, instead, we consider a multi-preferential semantics, where preference relations are associated to distinguished propositional variables
A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}\in\mathit{Prop}
(called
distinguished propositions
in the following). The idea is that how much a situation (a world) is normal (or less atypical) with respect to another one depends on the aspects considered for comparison.
The semantics introduced below exploits a set of preference relations
<_{A_{i}}
, each associated to a distinguished proposition
A_{i}
, where
w<_{A_{i}}w^{\prime}
means that world
w
is less atypical than world
w^{\prime}
concerning aspect
A_{i}
.
For instance, referring to the example in the introduction, a world
w
can be regarded as capturing a more plausible situation describing a student than world
w^{\prime}
(i.e.,
w<_{\mathit{student}}w^{\prime}
),
but a less plausible situation than
w^{\prime}
describing an employee (i.e.,
w^{\prime}<_{\mathit{employee}}w
).

Multi-preferential semantics have been previously considered for defining refinements of the rational closure construction
[
22
]
,
for defining preferences with respect to different modules of a conditional knowledge base
[
24
]
, for defining a concept-wise semantics in ranked defeasible
\mathcal{EL}^{\bot}
knowledge bases
[
15
]
, and weighted conditionals in many-valued DLs with typicality
[
19
]
.

Given a finite set of
distinguished propositions

A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}
, we let
\leq_{A_{i}}\subseteq W\times W
be the
preorder
associated with
A_{i}
(
w\leq_{A_{i}}w^{\prime}
means that
w
is not less plausible than
w^{\prime}
).
A strict partial order
<_{A_{i}}
and an equivalence relation
\sim_{A_{i}}
can be defined as usual from the preorder
\leq_{A_{i}}
by letting

w<_{A_{i}}^{\prime}
iff
w\leq_{A_{i}}w^{\prime}
and
w^{\prime}\not\leq_{A_{i}}w
, and letting
w\sim_{A_{i}}w^{\prime}
iff
w\leq_{A_{i}}w^{\prime}
and
w^{\prime}\leq_{A_{i}}w
.

2.1
Multi-preferential semantics

In the following, we limit our consideration to finite KBs, and restrict our attention to preferential interpretations with a finite set of preference relations
<_{A_{i}}
, one for each distinguished proposition
A_{i}
.
For the moment, we assume that, in any typicality formula
{\bf T}(A)
,
A
is a distinguished proposition.
In Section
4
we will lift this restriction.

Definition 1
.

A (multi-)preferential interpretation is a triple
\mathcal{M}=\langle\mathcal{W},\{<_{A_{i}}\},v\rangle
where:

\bullet

\mathcal{W}
is a non-empty set of worlds;

\bullet
each
<_{A_{i}}\subseteq\mathcal{W}\times\mathcal{W}
is an irreflexive and transitive relation on
\mathcal{W}
;

\bullet

v:\mathcal{W}\longrightarrow 2^{\mathit{Prop}}
is a valuation function, assigning to each world
w
a set of propositional variables in
\mathit{Prop}
, the variables which are true in
w
.

A
ranked interpretation
is a (multi-)preferential interpretation
\mathcal{M}=\langle\mathcal{W},\{<_{A_{i}}\},v\rangle
for which all preference relations
<_{A_{i}}
are
modular
, that is: for all
x,y,z
, if

x<_{A_{i}}y
then
x<_{A_{i}}z
or
z<_{A_{i}}y
(the preorder
\leq_{A_{i}}
is total).
A relation
<_{A_{i}}
is
well-founded
if it does not allow for infinitely descending chains of worlds
w_{0},w_{1},w_{2},\ldots
. with
w_{1}<_{A_{i}}w_{0}
,
w_{2}<_{A_{i}}w_{1}
,
\ldots
.

The valuation
v
is inductively extended to all formulae of
{\cal L}^{\bf T}
:

\mathcal{M},w\models\top

\mathcal{M},w\not\models\bot

\mathcal{M},w\models p
iff
p\in v(w)
, for all
p\in\mathit{Prop}

\mathcal{M},w\models A\wedge B
iff
\mathcal{M},w\models A
and
\mathcal{M},w\models B

\mathcal{M},w\models A\vee B
iff
\mathcal{M},w\models A
or
\mathcal{M},w\models B

\mathcal{M},w\models\neg A
iff
\mathcal{M},w\not\models A

\mathcal{M},w\models A\rightarrow B
iff
\mathcal{M},w\models A
implies
\mathcal{M},w\models B

\mathcal{M},w\models{\bf T}(A_{i})
iff
\mathcal{M},w\models A_{i}
and
\mathit{\nexists w^{\prime}\in\mathcal{W}}
s.t.
\mathit{w^{\prime}<_{A_{i}}w}\mbox{ and }\mathcal{M},w^{\prime}\models A_{i}.

Whether
{\bf T}(A_{i})
is satisfied at a world
w
also depends on the other worlds of the interpretation
\mathcal{M}
.

Let
[[A]]^{\mathcal{M}}
be the set of all the worlds in
\mathcal{M}
satisfying a formula
A
(i.e.,
[[A]]^{\mathcal{M}}=\{w\in\mathcal{W}:\mathcal{M},w\models A\}
)
and let
Min_{<_{A}}(\mathcal{S})
be the set of
<_{A}
-minimal worlds in
\mathcal{S}
, for any set of worlds
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{W}
, and strict partial order
<_{A}
, that is:

Min_{<_{A}}(\mathcal{S})=\{w\in\mathcal{S}\mid\mbox{ there is no }w^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S},\mbox{ such that }w^{\prime}<_{A}w\}

Note that, when
\mathcal{S}\neq\emptyset
and the preference relation
<_{A}
is well-founded, the set
Min_{<_{A}}(\mathcal{S})
is non-empty. For a well-founded preference relation
<_{A}
,
one can reformulate the semantic condition for the typicality operator as follows:

\mathcal{M},w\models{\bf T}(A_{i})
iff
w\in Min_{<_{A_{i}}}([[A_{i}]]^{\mathcal{M}})
.

We say that a formula
A
is satisfiable
in the multi-preferential semantics if there exist a multi-preferential interpretation
\mathcal{M}=\langle\mathcal{W},\{<_{A_{i}}\},v\rangle
and a world
w\in\mathcal{W}
such that
\mathcal{M},w\models A
.
A formula
A
is valid in an interpretation
\mathcal{M}
(written
\mathcal{M}\models A
) if, for all worlds
w\in\mathcal{W}
,
\mathcal{M},w\models A
.
A formula
A
is
valid
in the multi-preferential semantics (simply,
A is valid
) if
A
is valid in any multi-preferential interpretation
\mathcal{M}
.
Restricting our consideration to ranked interpretations, leads to the notions of satisfiability and validity of a formula in the
ranked (or rational) multi-preferential semantics
.

As mentioned above, when a defeasible implication has the form
{\bf T}(A)\rightarrow B
, with
B
in
{\cal L}
, it stands for a conditional
A\mathrel{{\scriptstyle\mid\!\sim}}B
in KLM logics
[
4
]
.
Note that, if we assume that all preference relations are well-founded, a defeasible implication
{\bf T}(A)\rightarrow B
is valid in a preferential interpretation
\mathcal{M}
(i.e.,
\mathcal{M}\models{\bf T}(A)\rightarrow B
)
iff
for all worlds
w\in\mathcal{W}
,
w\in Min_{<_{A}}([[A]]^{\mathcal{M}})
implies
w\in[[B]]^{\mathcal{M}}
, i.e.,
iff

Min_{<_{A}}([[A]]^{\mathcal{M}})\subseteq[[B]]^{\mathcal{M}}
holds.
In this case, if we assume that all the preference relations
<_{A}
coincide with a single well-founded preference relation
<
, then a multi-preferential interpretation
\mathcal{M}
becomes a preferential interpretation as in KLM semantics:

\mathcal{M}\models{\bf T}(A)\rightarrow B

iff

Min_{<}([[A]]^{\mathcal{M}})\subseteq[[B]]^{\mathcal{M}}
,

i.e.,
\mathcal{M}\models{\bf T}(A)\rightarrow B
holds when the conditional formula
A\mathrel{{\scriptstyle\mid\!\sim}}B
is satisfied in the preferential model
\mathcal{M}
according to the preferential KLM semantics
[
4
]
(resp., to the rational KLM semantics, when
<
is assumed to be modular).
The multi-preferential semantics is indeed a generalization of the KLM preferential semantics.

Let a
knowledge base
K
be a set of (strict or defeasible) implications.
A
preferential model of
K
is a multi-preferential interpretation
\mathcal{M}

such that
\mathcal{M}\models A\rightarrow B
, for all implications
A\rightarrow B
in
K
.
Given a knowledge base
K
, we say that an implication
A\rightarrow B
is preferentially entailed from

K
if
\mathcal{M}\models A\rightarrow B
holds, for all preferential models
\mathcal{M}
of
K
. We say that
A\rightarrow B
is rationally entailed from

K
if
\mathcal{M}\models A\rightarrow B
holds, for all ranked models
\mathcal{M}
of
K
.

It is well known that preferential entailment and rational entailment are weak. As with the rational closure
[
6
]
and the lexicographic closure
[
18
]
for KML conditionals, also in the multi-preferential case one can strengthen entailment by restricting to specific preferential models,
based on some
closure constructions
, which allow to define the preference relations
<_{A_{i}}
from a knowledge base
K
, also exploiting the ranks and weights of conditional implications, when available.
Some examples of closure constructions for the multi-preferential case have been considered,
e.g., for variants of the rational closure
[
22
]
and of the lexicographic closure
[
24
]
, and for ranked or weighted defeasible DLs with typicality
[
15
,
19
]
.

In the next section, we define an approach for combining an ASP program with a conditional knowledge base, and develop a construction based on the weighted conditional KB for reasoning on the answer sets of the ASP programs.

2.2
Weighted conditional knowledge bases

It is well known that preferential entailment and rational entailment are weak. As with the rational closure
[
6
]
and the lexicographic closure
[
18
]
for KML conditionals, also in the multi-preferential case one can strengthen entailment by restricting to specific preferential models,
based on some
closure constructions
, which allow to define the preference relations
<_{A_{i}}
from a knowledge base
K
, also exploiting the ranks or weights of conditional implications, when available.
Some examples of similar constructions, in the multi-preferential case, have been considered in
[
22
,
24
]
, and for ranked and weighted defeasible description logics
[
15
,
19
]
.
In this paper, we consider conditional knowledge bases in which weights are associated to conditionals.

A
weighted conditional knowledge base
for the distinguished propositions
A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}
is a pair
K=(K_{s},K_{d})
where
K_{s}
is a set of
strict implications
and
K_{d}
is a set of
defeasible implications
, each one associated with a weight
h
, an integer number.
More precisely, each distinguished formula
A_{i}
is associated with a set of pairs
({\bf T}(A_{i})\sqsubseteq B_{i,j},h_{i,j})
, consisting of a defeasible implication with a weight
h_{i,j}
.
The weight
h_{i,j}
is a positive or negative number and is intended to represent the
plausibility
(or
implausibility
) of the associated defeasible implication, where negative weights represent penalties.

Example 1
.

As an example of weighted conditional knowledge base, we consider a knowledge base
K
describing the properties of employees and students, where
\mathit{Emp}
and
\mathit{Student}
are distinguished formulae.
We assume
K
contains the set of strict implications

K_{s}=

\{\mathit{Emp\rightarrow Adult}
;
\mathit{Adult\rightarrow has\_SSN}
;
\mathit{PhdStudent\rightarrow Student}\}
.
and the set
K_{d}
of weighted defeasible implications
(d_{1}),\ldots,(d_{6})
from the introduction.

Given a weighted conditional knowledge base
K
, in the following we will construct a multi-preferential interpretation
\mathcal{M}=\langle\mathcal{W},\{<_{A_{i}}\},v\rangle
such that, the set of worlds
\mathcal{W}
is the set of all the propositional interpretations (
I\subseteq\mathit{Prop}
) satisfying all the strict implications in
K
, while we will use the defeasible part of
K
to define the preference relations
<_{A_{i}}
on
\mathcal{W}
with respect to the distinguished formulae
A_{i}
.

Let us consider all the defeasible implications
({\bf T}(A_{i})\rightarrow B_{i,j}\;,h_{i,j})\in K_{d}
, with
{\bf T}(A_{i})
in the l.h.s. of the implication.
The preference relation
<_{A_{i}}
associated with the distinguished formula
A_{i}
is determined by computing, for each propositional interpretation
I\in\mathcal{W}
, the weight
W_{A_{i}}(I)
of
I
with respect to formula
A_{i}
, as follows:

W_{A_{i}}(I)=\sum_{{j:I\models B_{i,j}}}h_{i,j}

(1)

where
\sum_{{j:I\models B_{i,j}}}h_{i,j}=0
, when there is no defeasible implication
({\bf T}(A_{i})\rightarrow B_{i,j}\;,h_{i,j})\in K_{d}
.

Informally, the weight
W_{A_{i}}(I)
of
I
wrt
A_{i}
is the sum of the weights of all defeasible implications for
A_{i}
satisfied by
I
. The more plausible are the satisfied implications, the higher is the weight of
I
with respect to
A_{i}
.
The lowest weight,
-\infty
, is given to the propositional interpretations which do not satisfy
A_{i}
. If there is no defeasible implication
({\bf T}(A_{i})\rightarrow B_{i,j}\;,p_{i,j})
in
K_{d}
for the distinguished proposition
A_{i}
, we have
W_{A_{i}}(I)=0
.

For each distinguished formula
A_{i}
, a total preorder
\leq_{A_{i}}
is defined on the set of the interpretations in
\mathcal{W}
as follows: for all
I_{1},I_{2}\in\mathcal{W}
,

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle\leq_{A_{i}}I_{2}\mbox{ \ \ iff \ \ }W_{A_{i}}(I_{1})\geq W_{A_{i}}(I_{2})

Given a weighted conditional knowledge base
K=(K_{s},K_{d})
, we define a multi-preferential model of
K
.

Definition 2
.

Given a weighted conditional knowledge base
K=(K_{s},K_{d})
, a multi-preferential model of
K
is a preferential interpretation
\mathcal{M}=\langle\mathcal{W},\{<_{A_{i}}\},v\rangle
such that:

- for all the strict implications in
A\rightarrow B\in K_{s}
,
\mathcal{M}\models A\rightarrow B
;

- for all distinguished formulae
A_{i}
, the preference relation
<_{A_{i}}
is defined as follows: for all
w_{1},w_{2}\in\mathcal{W}
,

\displaystyle w_{1}

\displaystyle<_{A_{i}}w_{2}\mbox{ \ \ iff \ \ }W_{A_{i}}(v(w_{1}))>W_{A_{i}}(v(w_{2}))

(2)

A weighted knowledge base
K
is satisfiable if
K
has a model.
As we are considering a finite knowledge base
K
, we can take
\mathit{Prop}
as the finite set of the propositional variables occurring in
K
and limit our consideration to finite interpretations. When
K
is satisfiable, a
canonical model
of
K
can be defined.

Definition 3
.

For a satisfiable weighted knowledge base
K=(K_{s},K_{d})
, the canonical multi-preferential model
\mathcal{M}^{K}=\langle\mathcal{W},\{<_{A_{i}}\},v\rangle
of
K
is defined as follows:

-

Restricting to preferential models over a set of propositional interpretations is a standard approach in KLM logics such as, for instance, in the definition of the semantics of the rational closure and of the lexicographic closure of a conditional knowledge base

Another option to define the preorders

Different approaches may be viable for the definition of preferences, provided a preorders

3
A conditional extension of Answer Set Programming

In this section, we aim at combining a defeasible knowledge base with an ASP Program, to exploit conditionals to define preferences between the Answer Sets of the ASP program. Based on such preferences, conditional properties of the program can be validated over a preferential interpretation of the program, based on the semantics of the conditional logic with typicality introduced in the previous section.

More precisely, a
Conditional ASP (CondASP) program
is a pair
(\Pi,K)
, where
\Pi
is an ASP program and
K
is a defeasible knowledge base. Both
\Pi
and
K
rely on the same set of propositional variables
\mathit{Prop}
.
In this paper we specifically consider weighted knowledge bases, and
assume that
K
is a weighted KB, i.e., a set of conditional implications, with an associated integer weight.

Let
A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}
be a set of distinguished propositional variables.
A
weighted defeasible knowledge base

over the distinguished propositions
A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}
is a set of
defeasible implications

of the form
({\bf T}(A_{i})\sqsubseteq B_{i,j},h_{i,j})

A_{i}
is a distinguished proposition,
B_{i,j}\in\mathcal{L}
and the weight
h_{i,j}
is an integer number.
The weight is intended to represent the
plausibility
(or
implausibility
) of the associated defeasible implication, where negative weights represent penalties.

Let us consider the following example program, which is inspired by the cinema scenario in
[

]

But they cannot both go out if they have children and a babysitter is not available.

\mathit{busy(X)\ \text{:--}\ go\_for\_pizza(X).}

Concerning Ada’s preferences, she likes going to the cinema.
However, as she is Mary’s friend, she is equally happy when Mary can go to the cinema on Saturday.

Which are the typical situations in which Mary is happy, or Mary and Bob are happy on Saturday evening? And what does also hold in such situations?
We aim at using the conditional logic to answer such questions.
For instance, considering the typical situations in which Bob and Mary are happy on Saturday, are these situations where they go for pizza, i.e.,

{\bf T}(happy\_Sat(mary)\wedge happy\_Sat(bob))\rightarrow go\_for\_pizza(mary)\wedge go\_for\_pizza(bob)

Further, are these typical situations in which Mary is happy on Saturday, i.e.,

{\bf T}(happy\_Sat(mary)\wedge happy\_Sat(bob))\rightarrow{\bf T}(happy\_Sat(mary))

Other preference definitions: Preferences based on the ranks of conditionals

When the defeasible knowledge base is a set of conditional implications

W_{A\vee A}(S)=W_{A}(S)

W_{A\wedge A}(S)=W_{A}(S)

W_{(A\vee B)\vee C}(S)=W_{A\vee(B\vee C)}(S)

W_{(A\wedge B)\wedge C}(S)=W_{A\wedge(B\wedge C)}(S)

As a consequence, two equivalent formulae
have the same weight in an answer set
S
:

Lemma 1
.

Let
A
and
B
be boolean combinations of the distinguished propositions
A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}
.
If
A
and
B
are equivalent in propositional logic, then
W_{A}(S)=W_{B}(S)
, for all answer sets
S
.

The preference relation associated with a complex formula
A
can be defined by extending condition (
5
) to any formula
A
which is a boolean combination of the distinguished propositions
A_{i}
.
For all
S_{1},S_{2}\in\mathcal{W}
, we let:

S_{1}\leq_{A}S_{2}
iff
W_{A}(S_{1})\geq W_{A}(S_{2})
.

The preference relation
\leq_{A}
is a total preorder. As a direct consequence of Lemma
1
, the following proposition holds:

Proposition 2
.

Let
A
and
B
be boolean combinations of the distinguished propositions
A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}
.
If
A
and
B
are equivalent in propositional logic, then,
for all answer sets
S_{1},S_{2}\in\mathcal{W}
,
S_{1}\leq_{A}S_{2}
iff
S_{1}\leq_{B}S_{2}
.

Kraus et al.
[
4
]
studied the properties of several families of nonmonotonic
consequence relations
, i.e., binary relations
\mathrel{{\scriptstyle\mid\!\sim}}
on
\cal L
, corresponding to well-behaved sets of conditional assertions.
In particular, they introduced a notion of
preferential consequence relation
as a consequence relation satisfying the following properties (also called KLM postulates) that we reformulate using the typicality operator
2
2

2

In the KLM postulates below,
\models\mathit{A\equiv B}
means that
A
and
B
are equivalent formulae in propositional logic, and
\models\mathit{B\rightarrow C}
means that the implication
B\rightarrow C
is valid in propositional logic
:

\mathit{(Reflexivity)}~\mathit{{\bf T}(A)\rightarrow A}

\mathit{(Right\;Weakening)}~\mbox{ If }\models\mathit{B\rightarrow C}\mbox{ and }\mathit{{\bf T}(A)\rightarrow B}\mbox{ then }\mathit{{\bf T}(A)\rightarrow C}

\mathit{(Left\;Logical\;Equivalence)}~\mbox{ If }\models\mathit{A\equiv B}\mbox{ and }\mathit{{\bf T}(A)\rightarrow C}\mbox{ then }\mathit{{\bf T}(B)\rightarrow C}

\mathit{(And)}~\mbox{ If }\mathit{{\bf T}(A)\rightarrow B}\mbox{ and }\mathit{{\bf T}(A)\rightarrow C}\mbox{ then }\mathit{{\bf T}(A)\rightarrow B\wedge C}

\mathit{(Or)}~\mbox{ If }\mathit{{\bf T}(A)\rightarrow C}\mbox{ and }\mathit{{\bf T}(B)\rightarrow C}\mbox{ then }\mathit{{\bf T}(A\vee B)\rightarrow C}

\mathit{(Cautious\;Monotonicity)}~\mbox{ If }\mathit{{\bf T}(A)\rightarrow B}\mbox{ and }\mathit{{\bf T}(A)\rightarrow C}\mbox{ then }\mathit{{\bf T}(A\wedge B)\rightarrow C}

We prove that the entailment from a CondASP program
(\Pi,K)
is a preferential consequence relation.
Let us remember that
{\bf T}(A)\rightarrow B
is entailed from the program
(\Pi,K)
, if
{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}\models{\bf T}(A)\rightarrow B
holds.

Proposition 3
.

Given a consistent CondASP program
(\Pi,K)
, entailment from
(\Pi,K)
satisfies the KLM postulates above.

For instance, to prove that the property
(Or)
is satisfied, one has to prove that, assuming that
{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}\models\mathit{{\bf T}(A)\rightarrow C}
and
{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}\models\mathit{{\bf T}(B)\rightarrow C}
hold, then
{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}\models\mathit{{\bf T}(A\vee B)\rightarrow C}
also holds.
The detailed proof of the proposition can be found in Appendix.

One observation is that the property of Rational Monotonicity:

\mathit{(RM)}~\mbox{ If }\mathit{{\bf T}(A)\rightarrow C}\mbox{ and }\mathit{{\bf T}(A)\not\rightarrow\neg B}\mbox{ then }\mathit{{\bf T}(A\wedge B)\rightarrow C}

is not satisfied by the entailment from a CondASP program
(\Pi,K)
.
In general, it is not the case that,
if
{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}\models\mathit{{\bf T}(A)\rightarrow C}
and
{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}\not\models\mathit{{\bf T}(A)\rightarrow\neg B}
, it follows that
{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}\models\mathit{{\bf T}(A\wedge B)\rightarrow C}
.

As an example, suppose that from a conditional program
(\Pi,K)
, we can conclude that typical birds fly and that there are some typical birds which are
black\&white
(e.g., the warbler). Should we require that the most typical birds which are
black\&white
do fly? For instance, the most typical
black\&white
birds might be the penguins, and we do not want to conclude that they fly.
In this case, it looks reasonable that (RM) does not hold (the detailed counterexample is in appendix).

Note that our approach for combining preferences by combining the weights of answer sets with respect to boolean formulae does not addresses directly the issue of preference with priority, raised by
[
26
]
. However, one can customize the intended preferences by playing with the weights of the conditionals as well as with the definition of
W_{A_{1}\wedge A_{2}}(S)
,
W_{A_{1}\vee A_{2}}(S)
and
W_{\neg A_{i}}(S)
, depending on the context of application. This is not to say that any choice for the weights of answer sets wrt. boolean formulae would satisfy all KLM postulates. For instance, an alternative definition of
W_{A_{1}\wedge A_{2}}(S)
,
W_{A_{1}\vee A_{2}}(S)
and
W_{\neg A_{i}}(S)
, which is inspired to Łukasiewicz combination functions, is the following one:

W_{A_{1}\wedge A_{2}}(S)=max(W_{A_{1}}(S)+W_{A_{2}}(S)-Max,Min)

W_{A_{1}\vee A_{2}}(S)=min(W_{A_{1}}(S)+W_{A_{2}}(S),Max)

W_{\neg A_{i}}(S)=Max-W_{A_{i}}(S)+Min
.

5
Verifying conditional implications in ASP

We describe how ASP can be used to verify entailment of
an implication
from a program
(\Pi,K)
.

In order to use meta-programming, the program
\Pi
is transformed replacing all atoms
\mathit{A}
with
\mathit{holds(A)}
.
e.g., the first rule in the example in section
3
becomes:

1 { holds(go_to_cinema(X)); holds(go_for_pizza(X));
holds(stay_at_home(X))} 1 :- holds(person(X)).

A weighted defeasible implication
({\bf T}(A_{i})\sqsubseteq B_{i,j},h_{i,j})
in
K
is represented with a fact

\mathit{wdi(A_{i},B_{i,j},h_{i,j})}
.
Then, the weight with respect to an atomic formula
A
can be computed using

\mathit{\#sum\{W1,B:wdi(A,B,W1),holds(B)\}.}

For the case of conditional implications
{\bf T}(A)\rightarrow B
,
where
A,B
are boolean formulae,
a solution can be adapted from
[
16
]

(where an ASP encoding of entailment in a defeasible description logic with typicality was considered in the many-valued case).
The implication
{\bf T}(A)\rightarrow B
to be verified is represented as

\mathit{query(typ(A),B)}
, using, in
\mathit{A}
and
\mathit{B}
, symbols
\mathit{and/2,or/2,neg/1}
for representing the structure of the formulae.
The following constraint (with additional rules to extend
\mathit{holds}
to non-atomic formulae):

\mathit{\text{:--}\ query(typ(A),B),not\ holds(A).}

is used to select answer sets of
\Pi
in which
A
holds.
If there are none, the implication is trivially true.
The following weak constraint is used to prefer (at priority level 2) answer sets of
\Pi
where the weight of
\mathit{A}
is maximum, i.e., the answer sets in

Min_{<_{A}}([[A]]^{{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}})
:

\mathit{:\sim query(typ(A),B),weight(A,W).\ [-W@2]}

where
\mathit{weight}
is defined based on the structure of
\mathit{A}
, and, for atomic formulae, as follows:

\mathit{weight(A,W)\ \text{:--}\ in\_lhs\_atomic(A),holds(A),}

\mathit{\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ W=\#sum\{W1,B:wdi(A,B,W1),holds(B)\}.}

\mathit{weight(A,-bignum)\ \text{:--}\ in\_lhs\_atomic(A),not\ holds(A).}

and
\mathit{in\_lhs\_atomic}
is used for grounding, and is
defined to hold for atomic formulae occurring in
\mathit{A}
,
and
\mathit{bignum}
is a suitably large number.

The following rules and weak constraint:

\mathit{rhs\ \text{:--}\ query(\_,B),holds(B).}

\mathit{counterexample\ \text{:--}\ not\ rhs.}

\mathit{:\sim counterexample.\ [-1@1]}

are used to prefer, at level 1, the presence of

\mathit{counterexample}
: in this way, if there is an answer set of
\Pi
which falsifies the implication
{\bf T}(A)\rightarrow B
, we get

\mathit{counterexample}
in all answer sets that are optimal with respect to the weak constraints; in none, otherwise. Then, a single ASP solver call computing a single optimal answer set is enough to establish entailment of the implication.

An example formula that is entailed
for the conditional ASP program in section

3

is:

{\bf T}(happy\_Sat(mary))\rightarrow go\_to\_cinema(mary)

The case of general implications
A\rightarrow B
with
{\bf T}
occurring in
A
and
B
can be dealt with as follows.
First, a solver call is performed for each occurrence
{\bf T}(C)
of
{\bf T}
in
A,B
, to establish (using a weak constraint) the maximum value of

W_{C}(S)
across the answer sets
S
of
\Pi
.
Then, a further solver call is performed to see if there is an answer set of
\Pi
which is a counterexample for

A\rightarrow B
, evaluating the truth of formulae
{\bf T}(C)
with:

\mathit{holds(typ(C))\ \text{:--}\ holds(C),maxw(C,MW),weight(C,MW).}

where
\mathit{maxw}
is used to represent the maximum weight computed before. This encoding provides a
\sc{P^{NP}}
upper bound to the complexity of entailment
[
30
]
.

Some implications that result to be entailed
by the conditional ASP program in Section

3

are:

{\bf T}(happy\_Sat(mary))\rightarrow\neg{\bf T}(happy\_Sat(bob))

{\bf T}(happy\_Sat(bob))\rightarrow\neg{\bf T}(happy\_Sat(mary))

{\bf T}(happy\_Sat(mary)\wedge happy\_Sat(bob))\rightarrow\neg{\bf T}(happy\_Sat(mary))

{\bf T}(happy\_Sat(mary)\wedge happy\_Sat(bob)))\rightarrow{\bf T}(happy\_Sat(bob))

since Mary’s and Bob’s top preferences are different, and the case that maximizes weight for
happy\_Sat(mary)\wedge happy\_Sat(bob)
is the one where they both go for pizza, which is Bob’s top preference but not Mary’s.

The two algorithms described above are implemented in dedicated reasoners based on
ASP Chef

[
31
]
, a web-based platform for answer set programming.
ASP Chef allows users to compose and execute declarative recipes, combining ASP programs with additional logic-based components, such as conditional knowledge bases.
The first algorithm, for queries of the form
{\bf T}(A)\rightarrow B
, is available online at

https://asp-chef.alviano.net/s/ICLP2025/conditional-asp-simple
.
The second algorithm handles the general case and is accessible at

https://asp-chef.alviano.net/s/ICLP2025/conditional-asp
.
In both implementations, users can specify a
query
, a
logic program
, and a
weighted conditional knowledge base
through an intuitive web interface.
The system elaborates the inputs by translating them into an ASP-based reasoning process, and computes the result directly in the browser.
This makes the tool accessible without requiring any software installation, thus supporting both experimentation and education in conditional reasoning with ASP.

6
Conclusions

In this paper we have studied a framework for providing a loose combination of ASP program and conditional knowledge bases. More precisely, we focused on a weighted knowledge bases to define Conditional ASP programs as pairs
(\Pi,K)
, where
\Pi
is an ASP program and
K
is a set of weighted conditionals over the same set of propositional variables.
The framework allows for conditional reasoning over the set of the answer sets of the program
\Pi
, by verifying implications including the typicality operator over the set of all the answer sets.
We have described ASP encodings of entailment from a CondASP program, and corresponding reasoners in ASP Chef.

The idea of associating weights/ranks to the properties of concepts in weighted KBs, as a measure of their saliency, was inspired by Brewka’s framework for basic preference descriptions
[
25
,
26
]
, and by Lehmann’s lexicographic closure
[
18
]
.
Among the recent work on preference combination in ASP, let us mention the algebraic framework for preference combination in multi-relational contextual hierarchies proposed in
[
32
]
and the work by
[
33
]
on concept combination based on typicality.
Our use of weighted knowledge bases also has some relation to

threshold concepts
in description logics
[
34
]
and to

weighted threshold operators

[
35
,
36
]
,
which lead to monotonic DLs for weighted concept combination.
The notion of typicality we have considered is also reminiscent of
prototype theory

[
37
]
and relates to Freund’s ordered models for concept representation
[
38
]
although, here, we do not have a representation of concepts, but we are considering propositions.

Appendix A
Proof of Proposition 3

Proposition 3

Given a consistent CondASP program
(\Pi,K)
, entailment from
(\Pi,K)
satisfies the KLM postulates above.

Proof.

Let
{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}=\langle\mathcal{W},\{<_{A_{i}}\},v\rangle
.

-
\mathit{(Reflexivity)}
By definition, if
S\in Min_{<_{A}}([[A]]^{{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}})
, then
S\in[[A]]^{{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}}
. Therefore,
{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}\models{\bf T}(A)\rightarrow A
holds.

-
\mathit{(Right\;Weakening)}
Let us assume that
\models\mathit{B\rightarrow C}
and that
{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}\models\mathit{{\bf T}(A)\rightarrow B}
. We have to prove that
{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}\models\mathit{{\bf T}(A)\rightarrow C}
holds.

From
{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}\models\mathit{{\bf T}(A)\rightarrow B}
, we have

Min_{<_{A}}([[A]]^{{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}})\subseteq[[B]]^{{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}}
.

From
\models\mathit{B\rightarrow C}
, it follows that

[[B]]^{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}\subseteq[[C]]^{{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}}
, and the thesis follows as well.

-
\mathit{(Left\;Logical\;Equivalence)}

Let us assume that
\models\mathit{A\equiv B}

and that

{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}\models\mathit{{\bf T}(A)\rightarrow C}
.
We have to prove that
{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}\models\mathit{{\bf T}(B)\rightarrow C}
.

From
\models\mathit{A\equiv B}
, it follows that

[[A]]^{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}=[[B]]^{{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}}
.
From
{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}\models\mathit{{\bf T}(A)\rightarrow C}
, we have

Min_{<_{A}}([[A]]^{{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}})\subseteq[[C]]^{{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}}
.

To prove that
{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}\models\mathit{{\bf T}(B)\rightarrow C}
, assume
S\in Min_{<_{B}}([[B]]^{{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}})
. By the equivalence of
A
and
B
,

S\in Min_{<_{B}}([[A]]^{{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}})
.
By Proposition
2
,
<_{A}
and
<_{B}
are the same preference relation, and therefore
S\in Min_{<_{A}}([[A]]^{{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}})
. Thus, it follows that
S\in[[C]]^{{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}}
. We have proved that

Min_{<_{B}}([[B]]^{{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}})\subseteq[[C]]^{{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}}
,
that is,
{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}\models\mathit{{\bf T}(B)\rightarrow C}
.

-
\mathit{(And)}

Let us assume that
{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}\models\mathit{{\bf T}(A)\rightarrow B}
and that
{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}\models\mathit{{\bf T}(A)\rightarrow C}
. We have to prove that
{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}\models\mathit{{\bf T}(A)\rightarrow B\wedge C}
.

From the hypothesis,

Min_{<_{A}}([[A]]^{{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}})\subseteq[[B]]^{{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}}
and

Min_{<_{A}}([[A]]^{{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}})\subseteq[[C]]^{{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}}
hold.

Assume that
S\in Min_{<_{A}}([[A]]^{{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}})
. Then

S\in[[B]]^{{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}}
and
S\in[[C]]^{{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}}
.
It follows that
S\in[[B\wedge C]]^{{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}}
. Therefore,

{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}\models\mathit{{\bf T}(A)\rightarrow B\wedge C}
.

-
\mathit{(Or)}

Let us assume that
{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}\models\mathit{{\bf T}(A)\rightarrow C}
and that
{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}\models\mathit{{\bf T}(B)\rightarrow C}
. We have to prove that
{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}\models\mathit{{\bf T}(A\vee B)\rightarrow C}
.

From the hypothesis,

Min_{<_{A}}([[A]]^{{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}})\subseteq[[C]]^{{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}}
and

Min_{<_{B}}([[B]]^{{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}})\subseteq[[C]]^{{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}}
hold.
Assume that,
S
is a minimal answer set for
A\vee B
, that is,
S\in Min_{<_{A\vee B}}([[A\vee B]]^{{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}})
. We want to prove that
S\in[[C]]^{{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}}
.

By absurd, suppose
S
is neither a minimal answer set for
A
, nor a minimal answer set for
B
.
That is,
there is an
S^{\prime}\in[[A]]^{{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}}
, such that
S^{\prime}<_{A}S
and there is an
S^{\prime\prime}\in[[B]]^{{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}}
, such that
S^{\prime}<_{B}S
.
It holds that
S^{\prime},S^{\prime\prime}\in[[A\vee B]]^{{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}}
.
However,
S^{\prime}\not<_{A\vee B}S
and
S^{\prime\prime}\not<_{A\vee B}S
, otherwise
S
would not be a minimal answer set for
A\vee B
.

From the fact that
S^{\prime}\not<_{A\vee B}S
, it follows that
S\leq_{A\vee B}S^{\prime}
, and that
W_{A\vee B}(S)\geq W_{A\vee B}(S^{\prime})
. Clearly, from the definition of the weights,
W_{A\vee B}(S^{\prime})\geq W_{A}(S^{\prime})
. From
S^{\prime}<_{A}S
, we have
W_{A}(S^{\prime})>W_{A}(S)
. Therefore,

W_{A\vee B}(S)\geq W_{A\vee B}(S^{\prime})\geq W_{A}(S^{\prime})>W_{A}(S),

so that
W_{A\vee B}(S)>W_{A}(S)
.

Similarly, from the fact that
S^{\prime\prime}\not<_{A\vee B}S
and
S^{\prime\prime}<_{B}S
,
we get

W_{A\vee B}(S)\geq W_{A\vee B}(S^{\prime\prime})\geq W_{B}(S^{\prime\prime})>W_{B}(S),

so that
W_{A\vee B}(S)>W_{B}(S)
.
However, from the definition of the weights
W_{A_{1}\vee A_{2}}(S)=max(W_{A_{1}}(S),W_{A_{2}}(S))
, it cannot be the case that
W_{A_{1}\vee A_{2}}(S)>W_{A}(S)
and
W_{A_{1}\vee A_{2}}(S)>W_{B}(S)
, a contradiction.

Therefore, either
S
ia a minimal answer set for
A
(
S\in Min_{<_{A}}([[A]]^{{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}})
) or
S
ia a minimal answer set for
B
(
S\in Min_{<_{B}}([[B]]^{{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}})
).
In both cases, it follows from the hypothesis that
S\in[[C]]^{{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}}
.
Therefore,

Min_{<_{A\vee B}}([[A\vee B]]^{{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}})\subseteq[[C]]^{{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}}
, and the thesis follows, that is
{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}\models\mathit{{\bf T}(A\vee B)\rightarrow C}
.

-
\mathit{(Cautious\;Monotonicity)}

Let us assume that
{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}\models\mathit{{\bf T}(A)\rightarrow B}
and that
{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}\models\mathit{{\bf T}(A)\rightarrow C}
. We have to prove that
{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}\models\mathit{{\bf T}(A\wedge B)\rightarrow C}
.

From the hypothesis,

Min_{<_{A}}([[A]]^{{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}})\subseteq[[B]]^{{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}}
and

Min_{<_{A}}([[A]]^{{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}})\subseteq[[C]]^{{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}}
hold.

Let us assume that

S\in Min_{<_{A\wedge B}}([[A\wedge B]]^{{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}})
.
As

W_{A\wedge B}(S)=min\{W_{A}(S),W_{B}(S)\}
, it holds that
W_{A\wedge B}(S)\geq W_{A}(S)
.

We want to prove that
S\in Min_{<_{A}}([[A]]^{{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}})
.
Suppose by absurd that it does not hold.
Then, there must be an answer set
S^{\prime}
, such that
S^{\prime}<_{A}S
and
S^{\prime}\in[[A]]^{{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}}
.
Therefore,
W_{A}(S^{\prime})>W_{A}(S)
.
Without loss of generality, we can assume that
S^{\prime}
is
<_{A}
-minimal among the answer sets satisfying
A
. In this case, it also holds that
S\in[[B]]^{{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}}
, so that
S
satisfies
A\wedge B
.

As
S
is a minimal answer set satisfying
A\wedge B
,
W_{A\wedge B}(S)\geq W_{A\wedge B}(S^{\prime})
. Therefore,

W_{A\wedge B}(S)\geq W_{A\wedge B}(S^{\prime})\geq W_{A}(S^{\prime})>W_{A}(S)
. It follows that,

W_{A\wedge B}(S)>W_{A}(S)
, a contradiction.

Therefore,
S\in Min_{<_{A}}([[A]]^{{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}})
must hold, and
and
S\in[[C]]^{{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}}
.
Therefore,

Min_{<_{A\wedge B}}([[A\vee B]]^{{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}})\subseteq[[C]]^{{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}}
, and the thesis follows, that is,
{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}\models\mathit{{\bf T}(A\wedge B)\rightarrow C}
.

∎

, -50

\mathit{S_{1}=\{bird,warbler,flies,has\_wings,black\&white\}}

{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}\models\mathit{{\bf T}(bird)\rightarrow flies}
and that
{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}\not\models\mathit{{\bf T}(bird)\rightarrow\neg black\&white}
.
Note that both
\mathit{S_{1}},\mathit{S_{2}}\in min_{<_{bird}}([[Bird]]^{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}})
, while
\mathit{S_{3},S_{4},S_{5}}\not\in min_{<_{bird}}([[Bird]]^{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}})
(as
W_{bird}(S_{1})=W_{bird}(S_{2})=+90
,
W_{bird}(S_{3})=W_{bird}(S_{4})=+50
and
W_{bird}(S_{5})=-50
).

\mathit{S_{1}}
describes a typical bird, a warbler, who is black and white, while
S_{3}
describes a typical black and white bird, a penguin.

In particular, we have:

\mathit{W_{bird\wedge black\&white}(S_{1})}

=\mathit{min\{W_{bird}(S_{1}),W_{black\&white}(S_{1})\}}
=\mathit{min\{+90,+10\}=+10}
,

\mathit{W_{bird\wedge black\&white}(S_{2})}

=\mathit{min\{W_{bird}(S_{2}),W_{black\&white}(S_{2})\}}
=\mathit{min\{+90,-40\}=-40}
,

\mathit{W_{bird\wedge black\&white}(S_{3})}

=\mathit{min\{W_{bird}(S_{3}),W_{black\&white}(S_{3})\}}
=\mathit{min\{+50,+80\}=+50}
,

\mathit{W_{bird\wedge black\&white}(S_{4})}

=\mathit{min\{W_{bird}(S_{4}),W_{black\&white}(S_{4})\}}
=\mathit{min\{+50,+80\}=+50}
,

\mathit{W_{bird\wedge black\&white}(S_{5})}

=\mathit{min\{W_{bird}(S_{5}),W_{black\&white}(S_{5})\}}
=\mathit{min\{-50,+80\}=-50}
.

It follows that
S_{3}
is the only answer set in
min_{<_{bird\wedge black\&white}}([[bird\wedge black\&white]]^{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}})
(note that
black\&white
is false in
S_{4}
).
As
S_{3}\not\in[[flies]]^{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}
, then

{\mathcal{M}^{\Pi}_{K}}\not\models\mathit{{\bf T}(bird\wedge black\&white)\rightarrow flies}
, which
falsifies Rational Monotonicity.

∎
