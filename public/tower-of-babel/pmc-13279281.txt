Epigenetic programming by H3K23ac defines lineage fate of Meg3+ haematopoietic stem cells and drives immune ageing
Authors: Ni Wei, Huiwen Zhan, Yujun Deng, Min Liu, Yao Xiao, Yanqiu Gong, Xiaodong Wang, Pengbo Guan, Xiaoxian Lou, Yusi Xie, Yuemeng Wang, Zhonghan Li, Lunzhi Dai, Hongbo Hu, Huiyuan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Haematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) produce all blood and immune cells throughout life, but ageing progressively impairs their function, generating excessive myeloid and megakaryocyte cells at the expense of lymphocytes. This lineage imbalance contributes to immune decline, chronic inflammation and increased disease susceptibility in the elderly, yet the underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood. Here we show that a specific Meg3+ HSC subset (CD150hiSca1hiCD24hiCD201+CD9+CD63+ long-term HSCs) expands dramatically during ageing and drives this lineage skewing. Using multi-omics profiling, we found that inflammatory signals increase H3K23ac levels in aged Meg3+ HSCs, enhancing PU.1 activity through recruitment of the reader protein TRIM24. This epigenetic mechanism promotes excessive megakaryocyte and myeloid production. Of note, disrupting H3K23ac–TRIM24 interaction in aged HSCs restored balanced lineage output and reduced inflammatory signals. Our findings reveal a key mechanism linking inflammation to HSC ageing and identify potential therapeutic targets for reversing ageing-related immune decline.

Main

Haematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) sustain lifelong blood and immune cell production, but ageing leads to the accumulation of functionally impaired HSCs with skewed lineage output, characterized by increased myeloid and megakaryocyte (MK)/platelet production and reduced lymphoid potential1–4. Accordingly, these ageing-related changes in HSCs drive declining immune function, contributing to increased pathogen susceptibility, poor vaccine responses, chronic inflammation and elevated risk of cardiovascular disease, neurodegeneration and cancer in the elderly5–8. Nevertheless, despite recognized drivers of ageing such as DNA damage9, epigenetic modifications10, shifts in cell polarity11 and disruptions in metabolic/proteostasis processes12,13, which collectively impact HSC lineage fate decisions, the precise mechanisms governing HSC fate during ageing remain inadequately understood.

HSCs are functionally heterogeneous under steady-state and reconstitution conditions, with distinct lineage-biased subsets such as MK/platelet-biased HSCs14. An alternative model posits that such lineage bias reflects uni- or oligopotent self-renewing progenitors within the phenotypic HSC compartment15. Recent single-cell sequencing of human and murine haematopoietic progenitors has uncovered cellular and molecular diversity underlying haematological diseases4,16–19, yet unravelling the global mechanisms of HSC lineage decisions remains challenging and requires integrating single-cell transcriptomic, epigenomic and functional approaches across steady-state and ageing contexts.

In this study, we used single-cell RNA sequencing to profile two defined subsets within CD150hi myeloid-biased HSCs (SH-HSCs and SL-HSCs) alongside CD150lo lymphoid-biased HSCs1,20,21, capturing a spectrum of lineage specification and commitment states. We mapped haematopoietic differentiation trajectories and identified Meg3+ HSCs as the developmental apex. These cells expanded markedly starting in middle age, accompanied by dynamic transcriptional changes linked to histone modification and MK/myeloid differentiation. Transplantation assays revealed an altered ability to replenish myeloid, lymphocytes and MKs, and declined self-renewal capacity in middle-aged Meg3+ HSCs. Multi-omics profiling of Meg3+ HSCs unveiled increased H3K23ac level in middle-aged Meg3+ HSCs, mediated by a pioneer transcription factor p65/RELA-KAT6A activation in response to inflammatory signals. H3K23ac-associated genes were enriched in MK/platelets and myeloid cell differentiation pathways, highlighting the critical role of H3K23ac in modulating HSC fate decision during ageing. Mechanistically, H3K23ac recruited TRIM24 to enhance the transcriptional activity of PU.1. Interfering the binding of TRIM24 and H3K23ac in aged HSCs rescued MK/platelets and myeloid lineage skewing and reduced senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP). Collectively, our study delineates the dynamic changes associated with ageing in HSCs, and the underlining mechanisms driving lineage skewing and immune ageing.

Results

CD150hi and CD150lo LT-HSCs encompass a spectrum of lineage specification and commitment cell states

A previous study delineated a shift of proportion between CD150hi myeloid-biased and CD150lo lymphoid-biased HSCs during ageing22. We refined the characterization of lineage-biased HSC subsets by further subdividing CD150hi myeloid-biased long-term (LT)-HSCs based on Sca1 and CD24 expression into CD150hiSca1hiCD24hi (SH-HSCs) and CD150hiSca1loCD24lo (SL-HSCs), alongside CD150lo lymphoid-biased HSCs (CL-HSCs) (Extended Data Fig. 1a). In young mice, SH-HSCs and SL-HSCs were present at similar numbers. With ageing both subsets increased in proportion and absolute number in bone marrow, whereas CL-HSCs decreased proportionally but remained stable in absolute number (Fig. 1a,b and Extended Data Fig. 1a–c). Transplantation into lethally irradiated recipients revealed distinct reconstitution capacities. SH-HSCs contributed ~40% donor chimerism at 8 weeks that remained stable over 20 weeks, whereas CL-HSCs and SL-HSCs achieved only ~20% and ~10%, respectively. Source data

To delineate the transcriptional landscape of these subsets, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) of SH-HSCs, SL-HSCs and CL-HSCs from young mice. Integration of SH-HSCs, SL-HSCs and CL-HSCs revealed six distinct clusters (Fig. 1c), highlighting the transcriptional heterogeneity within immunophenotypically defined HSC subsets23. To compare these HSC subsets with previously defined HSCs, we integrated our scRNA-seq data with immunophenotyped HSCs (iHSCs) and Vwf+ HSC data generated using SMARTseq2 (refs. 24,25) (Fig. 1d,e and Extended Data Fig. 1g). The combined HSCs still segregated into six clusters as defined above, with full integration of three datasets (Fig. 1e). SH-HSCs mainly consisted of C0 and C4. C1 mainly arose from SL-HSCs. C2 was primarily derived from SL-HSCs and CL-HSCs, whereas C3 was almost exclusively from the CL-HSC fraction (Fig. 1c,e). In comparison, HSC-LT (CD34−CD135−LSK)26 comprised only about 30% C0, whereas ESLAMSK (CD201+CD150+CD48−CD45+Sca1+cKit+)27 and fraction I (CD150+CD41−CD34−LSK)15 had over 75% C0. And either balanced or biased Vwf+ HSCs were a mixture of HSC subsets defined above (Fig. 1e). In summary, various immunophenotypically defined HSCs exhibited heterogeneity and could be further classified into the sub-clusters defined in this study.

We next annotated the clusters based on the most highly expressed marker genes. For instance, C0 shows high expression of genes such as Meg3, Procr, Hes1 and Hlf. Among these, Meg3, a tumour-suppressive long non-coding RNA, is dispensable for HSC maintenance, but highly and specifically expressed in HSCs28. Notably, Meg3 was expressed in 50% of C0 and at a significantly higher level (81-fold) compared with other clusters. In contrast, Hlf and Hes1 were coexpressed in C1(Fig. 1f and Supplementary Table 1). Accordingly, Meg3 was selected as the representative marker for C0, and the remaining clusters were annotated using the same approach (Fig. 1f). Meg3+ HSCs, Pf4+ HSCs and Clec4d+ HSCs predominantly remained in G0/G1 phase, whereas the rest were in a relatively activated phase (Fig. 1g). Consistently, most SL-HSCs were in quiescent phase, whereas CL-HSCs exhibited the highest proportion of proliferative HSCs and SH-HSCs were in between (Extended Data Fig. 1h).

Meg3+ HSCs and Pf4+ HSCs exhibited high expression of genes essential for maintaining HSC stemness, such as Hlf 29,30, Meis1 (ref. 31) and Hes1 (ref. 32). Surface marker genes Procr (encoding CD201) and Cd74 were highly expressed in Meg3+ HSCs, while Ifitm1 and Cd63 were enriched in both Meg3+ and Pf4+ HSCs (Fig. 1f). Single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) of transcriptome and regulons of key transcriptional factors both revealed enrichment of Gene Ontology (GO) terms related to ‘cell fate determination’, ‘T cell differentiation’ and ‘B cell differentiation’ in Meg3+ HSCs (Fig. 1h, Extended Data Fig. 2a,b and Supplementary Table 1), indicating features of stemness and multipotential differentiation. Notably, Pf4+ HSCs showed marked upregulation genes driving MK and platelet development, including Pbx1 (ref. 33), Pf4 (refs. 34,35), Plek36, Fli1 (ref. 37), Stat4 (ref. 38) and Gata2 (ref. 39) (Fig. 1f and Extended Data Fig. 2a). Correspondingly, GO terms such as ‘megakaryocyte differentiation’ were enriched in Pf4+ HSCs (Fig. 1h, Extended Data Fig. 2b and Supplementary Table 1).

Clec4d+ HSCs exhibited pathways related to ‘T cell differentiation’, and displayed high activity of transcriptional factors predicted to promote early T cell differentiation, including Egr1 (ref. 40), Sox4 (ref. 41), Fos42, Zmiz1 (ref. 43) and Myb44,45 (Fig. 1f,h, Extended Data Fig. 2a,b and Supplementary Table 1). Integrative analysis of three HSC subsets with downstream lymphoid progenitors that recently been reported to give rise to T lineages46–48 revealed a direct developmental trajectory from Clec4d+ HSCs to thymus-seeding progenitors (TSPs)46,48. Consistently, genes involved in thymus seeding (Ccr7 and Ccr9)49 and early T cell development (Sox4 (ref. 50), Fos51 and Lyl1 (ref. 52)) were upregulated along the putative path (Extended Data Fig. 2c–e), supporting a potential differentiation trajectory from Clec4d+ HSCs to T lineages.

Rhd+Mt2lo and Rhd+Mt2h HSCs shared common transcriptional factor and surface marker features, including Gata1, a critical erythroid development transcriptional factor53 and Rhd, a gene encoding Rh blood type D antigen54 (Fig. 1f and Extended Data Fig. 2a). Pebp1hi HSCs shared common features with Clec4d+ HSCs, such as ‘regulatory T cell differentiation’ and ‘CD8+ αβT cell differentiation’ (Fig. 1h and Supplementary Table 1). Despite enrichment in GO terms regarding erythrocyte differentiation, genes regulated erythroid development, such as Gata1 (ref. 53), Rhd55 and E2f4 (ref. 56), were reduced in Pebp1hi HSCs, reflecting decreased potential for erythroid differentiation (Fig. 1f).

Meg3+ HSC sits at the apex of the HSC developmental trajectory

To assess lineage bias among these HSC subsets, we employed the STEMNET algorithm57 (Extended Data Fig. 2f,g). STEMNET positioned the Meg3+ and Pf4+ HSCs subsets along the B lineage, T lineage and MK/platelet branches, indicating their propensity to give rise to lymphocytes and MK/platelets. Pf4+ HSCs exhibited higher MK/platelet lineage potential (Extended Data Fig. 2f,g), consistent with the upregulation of Plek, Pf4 and Pbx1(Fig. 1f). Conversely, Clec4d+ HSCs showed the highest potential towards CD8+ T lymphocytes, whereas their potential to generate MK/platelets lineage decreased compared with Pf4+ HSCs (Extended Data Fig. 2f,g). Rhd+Mt2lo, Rhd+Mt2hi and Pebp1hi HSCs were predicted to primarily contribute to the erythroid lineage. Notably, HSC sub-clusters exhibited a lower myeloid potential than other cell types (Extended Data Fig. 2f,g), aligning with previous findings that myeloid-biased MPPs serve as a potent source of myeloid amplification58. Overall, quiescent LT-HSC subsets, including Meg3+, Pf4+ and Clec4d+ HSC, maintained a higher differentiation potential of lymphocytes and MK/platelets, whereas the potential to develop the erythroid lineage increased in Rhd-expressing HSCs.

To infer developmental trajectories of HSC subsets, we employed various computational approaches, including Monocle3 (Fig. 1i), force-directed graph (FDG), diffusion map and partition-based approximate graph abstraction (PAGA) (Extended Data Fig. 3a,b). All three models collectively indicated Meg3+ HSC as the trajectory origin (Fig. 1i and Extended Data Fig. 3a,b). Meg3+ HSCs connected directly to Pf4+ HSCs, followed by Clec4d+ and Rhd+Mt2lo HSCs (Fig. 1i and Extended Data Fig. 3a,b), with concurrent downregulation of genes related to stemness, including Hes1 (ref. 32), Hlf 29,30, Meis1 (ref. 59), Erg 60 and Gata2 (ref. 61) (Extended Data Fig. 3c). Monocle3 and FDG visualization also identified two branches from Rhd+Mt2lo HSCs, leading to erythroid-bias differentiation (Rhd+Mt2hi HSC, fate 1) or increased T-lineage potential (Pebp1hi HSCs, fate 2; Fig. 1i and Extended Data Fig. 3a). Genes regulating erythrocyte differentiation, erythroid components and mitochondria function, such as Gata1 (ref. 53), E2f4 (ref. 56), Hmbs62, Rhd63 and Mcp1 (ref. 64), upregulated along the fate 1, whereas genes promoting T cell development65–68, inhibiting erythrocyte differentiation69 or regulating mitochondria function70,71, such as Timm13, Tcf12, Mrpl12, Tcf3 and Pebp1, increased along the fate 2 (Extended Data Fig. 3c). Collectively, these data provide a comprehensive view of putative haematopoietic differentiation trajectories.

To investigate HSC subset microenvironments, we classified bone-marrow niche cells and analysed interactions between niche clusters and HSC subsets (Extended Data Fig. 3d–g)72,73. This revealed distinct interactions between each HSC subset with niches, including endosteal (osteoprogenitors, osteolineage cells and chondrocytes), arteriolar (arterial/arteriolar epithelial cells (ECs) and pericytes) and sinusoidal (sinusoidal ECs and pericytes)74 niches. Arteriolar/endosteal niches support lymphoid differentiation, whereas the sinusoidal niche favours myeloid output75–78. Meg3+ HSCs and Pf4+ HSCs interact with arterial/arteriolar ECs via Esam-Esam and Jam2-Jam3 signalling to support self-renewal79,80, whereas signals from mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), sinusoidal ECs and pericytes to Meg3+ HSCs through Angpt1-Tek and Jag1-Notch1 may regulate quiescence and repopulation81,82. In comparison, Clec4d+ HSCs interact with sinusoidal ECs, pericytes and periosteal cells via Icam1-Itgal signalling (Extended Data Fig. 3e–g and Supplementary Table 1), suggesting a distinct niche preference.

Dynamic change of transcriptional activities driving lineages skewing in middle-aged Meg3+ HSCs

Previous studies have revealed an expansion of both murine and human HSCs at middle age, with a shift between CD150hi and CD150lo HSC compartments83,84. However, the dynamic changes happened within HSC compartment during ageing remain incompletely understood. Therefore, we performed scRNA-seq on SH-HSCs, SL-HSCs and CL-HSCs from middle-aged (8-month-old, 6,654 cells) and aged mice (28-month-old, 13,317 cells), and integrated data from 2-month-old HSC subsets (2,790 cells). Integrated data segregated into six clusters (Fig. 2a,b). Meg3+ HSCs and Pf4+ HSCs showed increased proliferative activity during ageing, consistent with their expansion during ageing (Fig. 2c–e). However, Clec4d+, Rhd+Mt2lo, Rhd+Mt2hi and Pebp1hi HSCs dramatically decreased during ageing (Fig. 2d). As Meg3+ HSCs expanded with age, gene sets related to inositol phosphate metabolism, telomere maintenance, DNA repair and quiescence (early G0/G1) were markedly decreased in middle-aged cells (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Table 2). Concurrently, genes associated with MK/platelet and myeloid differentiation were upregulated, consistent with an ageing-induced shortcut in platelet differentiation85. Transcriptional activity, histone acetylation and epigenetic regulation also increased in middle-aged Meg3+ HSCs. Compared with young cells, aged Meg3+ HSCs (28 months) upregulated genes involved in fatty acid oxidation, TNF superfamily cytokine production and response to reactive oxygen species (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Table 2). Reflecting this increased transcriptional activity, SCENIC analysis86 revealed distinct regulon landscapes. Young Meg3+ HSCs were enriched for erythropoiesis-related TFs and regulons, including Klf1 (ref. 87), Gata1 (ref. 53) and Ezh2 (ref. 88). Middle-aged Meg3+ HSCs exhibited a coordinated hub of upregulated TFs, including Cebpb, Bhlhe40, Spi1 and Maff. These TFs regulated each other and showed highly correlated regulon modules related to megakaryocyte and myeloid differentiation, cellular response to stress and post-translational protein modification. In aged Meg3+ HSCs, Cebpd89–91, Jund92 and Egr3 (ref. 93) were markedly upregulated, along with pathways that associated with chromosome organization, myeloid cell differentiation and response to stress (Fig. 2g,h, Extended Data Fig. 3i and Supplementary Table 3).

Validation of the surface markers of annotated HSC subsets

To characterize the lineage differentiation potential through functional assays, we analysed scRNA-seq data for cluster-specific marker genes to guide fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) isolation. Both Meg3+ and Pf4+ HSCs exhibited elevated expression of Cd9 and Cd63, with Meg3+ and a minority of Pf4+ HSCs also highly expressed Procr (CD201). In contrast, Rhd+Mt2lo, Pebp1hi and Rhd+Mt2hi HSCs displayed little Cd9 expression but showed high to intermediate expression of Rhd (CD240D) instead (Fig. 1f).

To annotate the three G0/G1 phase subsets, namely Meg3+, Pf4+ and Clec4d+ HSCs, we performed CITE-seq to assess surface expression of CD201, CD9 and CD63. The protein expression of these markers was consistent with their RNA level (Fig. 3a). Characterization of the self-renewal capacity and lineage potential of HSC subpopulation by transplantation assay

To evaluate lineage differentiation potential, we transplanted sorted Meg3+, Pf4+ and Clec4d+ HSCs (from dTomato+CD45.2+ mice) into lethally irradiated recipients (dTomato⁻CD45.2+), with equal numbers of LT-HSCs as controls (Extended Data Fig. 5a). Due to partial loss of dTomato fluorescence in donor-derived T and myeloid cells (Supplementary Fig. 2), we used CD45.2 to identify T cells, B cells and myeloid cells.

Over 40 weeks, Meg3+ HSCs showed higher reconstitution than LT-HSCs, indicating superior self-renewal and stemness, whereas Pf4+ HSCs showed lower reconstitution and Clec4d+ HSCs the lowest (Extended Data Fig. 5b). At 20 weeks, all subsets generated platelets, erythrocytes, myeloid cells, B/T lymphocytes and dendritic cells (Extended Data Fig. 5c). Pf4+ and Meg3+ HSCs exhibited high platelet reconstitution (12.7% versus 10.8%), while Clec4d+ HSCs showed minimal MK/platelet but increased erythroid output. Meg3+ and Pf4+ HSCs produced balanced lineages (~30% T, ~50% B and 10–20% myeloid), whereas Clec4d+ HSCs predominantly gave rise to T cells with reduced B cells. By 40 weeks, Meg3+ and Pf4+ HSCs were skewed towards myeloid cells with fewer lymphocytes and dendritic cells, whereas Clec4d+ HSCs increased platelet output (Extended Data Fig. 5c).

End-point analysis confirmed that Meg3+ HSCs reconstituted most robustly across bone marrow, spleen and lymph nodes. Clec4d+ HSCs preferentially reconstituted T cells in spleen and nodes. In primary recipients, Meg3+ HSCs regenerated all HSC subsets including themselves, Pf4+ HSCs reconstituted all except Meg3+ HSCs, and Clec4d+ HSCs mainly gave rise to Rhd+Mt2hi, Rhd+Mt2lo and Pebp1hi HSCs, supporting the predicted trajectories (Extended Data Fig. 5d–g).

To further assess self-renewal capacity, we performed secondary transplantation 20 weeks after primary transplantation (Extended Data Fig. 5h–m). Meg3+ HSCs maintained the highest reconstitution and multilineage potential, albeit with less pronounced myeloid/MK skewing, and self-renewed while generating the other five subsets. In contrast, Pf4+ and Clec4d+ HSCs had drastically reduced reconstitution, indicating limited self-renewal. Notably, Meg3+ HSCs achieved higher chimerism and LT-HSC proportions in secondary recipients, possibly due to the young recipient niche (2-month-old)94 (Extended Data Fig. 5d,g,j,m and Supplementary Fig. 3).

Middle-aged Meg3+ HSCs exhibit altered lineage potential and reduced self-renewal capacity

As Meg3+ HSCs expanded during ageing and exhibited marked transcriptional changes, we transplanted either young or middle-aged Meg3+ HSCs (CD45.1+CD45.2+dTomato+) into age-matched recipients (CD45.2+) to assess their stemness and lineage potential (Fig. 3e–n). Flow cytometric analysis demonstrated reduced reconstitution capacity of middle-aged cells in both peripheral blood and lymphoid organs, suggesting compromised self-renewal capacity. Furthermore, compared with young Meg3+ HSCs, middle-aged Meg3+ HSCs exhibited increased differentiation into CD11b+ myeloid cells along with decreased proportions of CD3+ and CD19+ lymphoid cells in peripheral blood and lymphoid organs. Notably, in the thymus, T cells derived from middle-aged Meg3+ HSCs were more frequently arrested at the CD4−CD8− stage (Fig. 3f–j).

Additionally, a higher proportion of CD41+MKs were found in bone marrow reconstituted with middle-aged Meg3+ HSCs. MKs of different ploidy levels perform distinct functions: low-ploidy (2N) immune-modulating MKs, intermediate-ploidy (4N-8N) niche-supporting MKs, and high-ploidy (16N-32N) platelet-producing MKs95. Middle-aged Meg3+ HSCs preferentially gave rise to 2N immune-modulating and 16N-32N platelet-producing MKs, whereas young Meg3+ HSCs predominantly generated 4N-8N niche-supporting MKs (Fig. 3k). Reconciled with that, middle-aged Meg3+ HSCs showed significantly enhanced potential to generate MK-biased Pf4+ HSCs during differentiation, which aligns well with the MK analysis results (Fig. 3l–n). These findings collectively demonstrate that Meg3+ HSCs exhibit increased myeloid/megakaryocytic differentiation potential during ageing, consistent with our previous bioinformatics analysis (Fig. 2f,h).

We applied the fuzzy c-means algorithm97 to cluster expression profiles across ages at both the protein and RNA levels, and identified four temporal clusters with different expression kinetics. Cluster 1 represented the highest proteins and RNA expression in middle-aged Meg3+ HSCs, whereas cluster 2 showed the opposite trend (Extended Data Fig. 6e). Over-representation analysis of cluster 1 and 2 demonstrated concordant upregulation of platelet activation, glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation at the RNA and protein levels, together with coordinated downregulation of processes and terms such as eukaryotic translation initiation and nonsense-mediated decay (Extended Data Fig. 6f).

To identify whether acetylated proteins were also involved in the ageing of Meg3+ HSCs, we adapted a published algorithm to quantify overall protein acetylation changes (∆As), and classified acetylated proteins into hyperacetylated (∆As > 1.5-fold) or hypoacetylated (∆As < −1.5-fold) group96,98. Ageing-related hyperacetylated proteins were mainly enriched in myeloid cell differentiation, cellular responses to lipid and DNA repair, in both middle-aged and aged Meg3+ HSCs, whereas platelet activation-associated proteins showed increased acetylation in middle-aged Meg3+ HSCs (Extended Data Fig. 6g–l and Supplementary Table 4), suggesting that altered acetylation contributes to lineage skewing.

Enrichment of H3K23ac on genes associated with MK- and myeloid skewing in middle-aged Meg3+ HSCs

ssGSEA indicated altered histone acetylation in middle-aged Meg3+ HSCs (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Table 2). Acetylome profiling further revealed increase H3K18/K23ac in both middle-aged and aged Meg3+ HSCs (Fig. 4c), in contrast to our previous finding that H3K23ac, but not H3K18ac, increased in aged HSCs96. Corroborating this, intracellular flow cytometry confirmed elevated H3K23ac without a concurrent increase in H3K18ac in aged Meg3+ HSCs (Extended Data Fig. 7a).

We next employed confocal microscopy, confirming a significant increase in H3K23ac during HSC ageing (Fig. 4d,e and Extended Data Fig. 7b,c). In contrast, H4K16ac, a histone mark linked to HSC quiescence and activation99, exhibited an opposite trend (Fig. 4d,f). Notably, H3K23ac increased more prominently in aged Meg3+ and Pf4+ HSCs than in Clec4d+ HSCs, possibly owing to varying expression of Kat6a and Hdac6 across these subsets (Extended Data Fig. 7d–f). Elevated H3K23ac was also detected in middle-aged MPP3 cells and various progenitor populations, including common myeloid progenitors (CMPs), granulocyte–monocyte progenitors (GMPs), MK progenitors (MKPs) and common lymphoid progenitors(CLPs) (Extended Data Fig. 7g,h).

To illustrate genes directly regulated by H3K23ac, we performed CUT&Tag in young, middle-aged and aged Meg3+ HSCs (Fig. 4g and Supplementary Table 5). H3K23ac peak enrichment significantly increased in middle-aged and aged Meg3+ HSCs (Fig. 4h). Overlapping genes with H3K23ac-enriched transcription start site (TSS) regions (±3 kb) with transcriptome data from Meg3+ HSCs showed that H3K23ac-associated genes in middle-aged and aged Meg3+ HSCs were mainly involved in MK/platelet and myeloid differentiation. Examples of such genes include Gp5 (ref. 100), Hoxa7 (ref. 101), Trib1 (ref. 102), Hif1a103, Myc104 and Fes105. In contrast, H3K23ac-enriched genes in young HSCs were mainly linked to the chromatin three-dimensional (3D) structure and interferon (IFN) signalling (Fig. 4i,j, Extended Data Fig. 7i and Supplementary Table 5). H3K23ac in middle-aged Meg3+ and Pf4+ HSCs also enriched in TFs including Msi2 (ref. 106), Gata2 (ref. 107), Runx1 (ref. 108), Runx2 (ref. 109), Hoxa7 (ref. 110) and Tcf3 (ref. 111), which formed a transcriptional regulatory hub to activate pathways related to cell cycle progression and proliferation (Extended Data Fig. 7j,k and Supplementary Table 5). To further investigate the overlap between H3K23ac peaks and open chromatin regions (OCRs), we conducted a joint analysis of published ATAC-seq data of young and middle-aged HSCs84,112 with H3K23ac CUT&Tag data. Colocalization of H3K23ac and OCRs revealed notable differences in chromatin accessibility between the two age groups (Fig. 4k), and genes with H3K23ac-enriched OCRs in middle-aged Meg3+ HSCs were strongly associated with platelet activation and myeloid cell differentiation pathways (Fig. 4l and Supplementary Table 5).

To confirm the role of H3K23ac in HSC lineage decision, we overexpressed the gain-of-function mutant H3K23Q (mimicking constitutive acetylation), the loss-of-function mutant H3K23R or wild-type (WT) H3K23 in young HSCs (Extended Data Fig. 8a). Compared with H3K23R, H3K23Q significantly promoted CD41+ MK/platelet and myeloid differentiation at the expense of B220+ lymphocytes, a finding further supported by co-transfer experiments, which also revealed reduced stemness in H3K23Q-overexpressing HSCs (Extended Data Fig. 8b–g). CUT&Tag and ATAC-seq analysis in WT or H3K23Q-overexpressing cells revealed increased overlap between H3K23Q peaks and OCRs at MK- and myeloid-related genes and pathways, including CEBPA113, STAT3 (refs. 114,115), CLEC5A116, VAV1 (ref. 117) and TSPAN32 (ref. 118), with corresponding enrichment of PU.1, ELF4 and SPIB motifs in these overlapping promoter regions (Extended Data Fig. 8h–l and Supplementary Table 6). Collectively, our results indicate an essential role for H3K23ac in the lineage decision of aged Meg3+ HSCs.

To investigate the mechanism underlying MK/myeloid skewing, we performed motif analysis on loci co-occupied by TRIM24 and H3K23ac in young versus middle-aged Meg3+ HSCs. Binding motifs for PU.1 (Spi1) and STAT2 (Stat2) were enriched at TSS regions specifically in middle-aged cells (Fig. 6c and Supplementary Table 8). Confocal analysis validated a significantly increased of PU.1, a key transcriptional factor that regulates myeloid and MK differentiation123,124, colocalized with H3K23ac in middle-aged HSCs (Fig. 6d). Similarly, analysis of public datasets revealed elevated PU.1 expression in HSCs from middle-aged humans125–134(Fig. 6e and Extended Data Fig. 9e,f).

PU.1 enrichment was dramatically increased in middle-aged Meg3+ HSCs as confirmed by CUT&Tag assay, and colocalized with TRIM24 and H3K23ac (Fig. 6f and Supplementary Table 9). Out of 1,705 genes directly bound by PU.1 and upregulated in middle-aged Meg3+ HSCs, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed their close association with pathways including platelets activation and innate immune system (Extended Data Fig. 9g and Supplementary Table 9). In addition, PU.1 also orchestrated downstream transcriptional activity of Egr1, Klf10 and Bhlhe40, to positively regulate platelet activation and innate immune response. In comparison, PU.1-regulated genes in young Meg3+ HSCs were associated with antigen processing and T cell differentiation (Fig. 6g, Extended Data Fig. 9j,i and Supplementary Table 9). Further analysis of genes simultaneously coregulated by PU.1, TRIM24 and H3K23ac in middle-aged Meg3+ HSCs also revealed the enrichment in pathways associated with myeloid and MK/platelet differentiation (Fig. 6h and Supplementary Table 9). Together, these data demonstrated that increased H3K23ac in middle-aged Meg3+ HSCs regulates fate decision through TRIM24 and PU.1.

Disrupting TRIM24-H3K23ac binding decreased MK and myeloid skewing in aged HSCs by modulating PU.1 transcriptional activity

Next, we tested whether modulating PU.1 could reverse MK and myeloid lineage skewing. Knocking down PU.1 in middle-aged HSCs significantly reduced MK/platelet frequency and absolute numbers. It also decreased the absolute numbers of B and myeloid cells without altering their relative proportions, due to markedly decreased total differentiated cells (Extended Data Fig. 10a–h), indicating a broader role for PU.1 in haematopoietic differentiation. Consistent with this, PU.1 knockdown profoundly altered histone acetylation profiles (Extended Data Fig. 10i). Thus, directly targeting PU.1 failed to correct lineage skewing.

Disrupting the H3K23ac–TRIM24 interaction suppresses lineage skewing and attenuates age-associated inflammation

We next sought to test whether disrupting H3K23ac–TRIM24 interaction in vivo produced similar effects. Due to the short half-life of IACS-9571, we turned to a genetic approach. Because TRIM24 engages multiple signalling partners (for example, p53 (ref. 141), WNT142 and TRAF3 (ref. 143)), complete TRIM24 depletion could not isolate the specific effect of its interaction with H3K23ac. Indeed, mice reconstituted with Trim24-deficient bone marrow showed no suppression of megakaryocytic or myeloid differentiation but exhibited skewed B cell versus T cell output (Supplementary Fig. 4).

We therefore introduced either WT TRIM24 (Trim24-WT) or a bromodomain mutant (F979A/N980A; Trim24-Mut) that disrupts H3K23ac binding122,144 into Trim24 KO HSCs (Fig. 8a–f and Extended Data Fig. 10q,r). Compared with Trim24-WT, Trim24-Mut HSCs produced fewer MK and myeloid cells in vitro (Fig. 8a–f) and displayed lower PU.1 reporter activity (Fig. 8g). In vivo, disrupting the H3K23ac–TRIM24 interaction significantly suppressed MK and myeloid differentiation (Fig. 8h–j and Extended Data Fig. 10s). Trim24-Mut HSCs generated fewer CD11b+ and CD41+ cells both in frequency and absolute number (Fig. 8k–m and Extended Data Fig. 10t). In summary, our findings establish a critical role for H3K23ac in middle-aged Meg3+ HSCs. Disrupting the crosstalk between TRIM24 and H3K23ac suppresses PU.1 activity, thereby rescuing MK/platelet and myeloid skewing and mitigating inflammaging in aged HSCs.

Discussion

In this study, we delineated the transcriptional landscape of age-associated lineage specification and commitment within the HSC compartment, uncovering a spectrum of cell states among LT-HSCs. We identified Meg3+ HSCs as the apex of the HSC hierarchy, capable of replenishing all haematopoietic lineages with the highest reconstitution capacity. During ageing, Meg3+ HSCs expanded markedly and exhibited altered transcription factors, metabolism and histone modifications, giving rise to MK- and myeloid-biased HSCs. H3K23ac increased significantly during early ageing, and its upregulation was closely associated with MK/platelet and myeloid skewing in Meg3+ HSCs, driven by inflammatory signals via the p65/RELA–KAT6A axis. Mechanistically, H3K23ac recruits TRIM24 to enhance PU.1 transcriptional activity, and disrupting this interaction suppressed PU.1 function and rescued MK/myeloid skewing while reducing SASP in aged HSCs. Together, our findings provide insights into the mechanisms underlying HSC development, ageing and lineage fate decisions.

Single-cell studies have revealed extensive heterogeneity across haematopoietic stem and progenitor cells, including a continuum of core progenitor states where pre-myeloid and pre-lymphoid fates coexist, whereas erythroid and megakaryocytic programmes remain more distinct145,146. However, whether early fate decision within LT-HSCs follows similar rule has remained unclear. Here, we identified incipient MK/platelet potential in the Pf4+ subset, consistent with recent reports of multipotent but MK/platelet-biased HSCs in humans and mice4,14,147. Accordingly, Pf4+ and Meg3+ HSCs exhibited MK/platelet bias while maintaining multipotency, and Meg3+ HSCs remained heterogeneous in single-cell assays, yielding restricted, dual and multipotent outputs147 (Extended Data Fig. 10u). Both subsets generated MK/platelets, potentially with different kinetics or functional outputs25,148, or provide a shortcut path to generate platelets upon ageing. Consistent with this, middle-aged Meg3+ HSCs generated significantly more Pf4+ HSCs and mature MKs. Notably, although CD48− MKPs, which directly generate MK/platelets from HSCs85,148,149, increased with age in steady-state mice, middle-aged Meg3+ HSCs generated fewer CD48− MKPs after transplantation than young counterparts (Fig. 3k and Extended Data Fig. 10v,w), possibly reflecting accelerated downstream differentiation into MKs.

Previous studies have attributed age-related myeloid and MK/platelet skewing primarily to the expansion of lineage-biased HSC subsets1,21,150,151. Here, we show that altered lineage fate decisions originate at the haematopoietic apex, with significant expansion and epigenetic remodelling in Meg3+ HSCs. Although epigenetic changes, such as H3K56ac and H4K16ac, critically influence HSC ageing10,152–154, the histone acetylation landscape in aged HSCs has remained incompletely understood. We characterized this landscape in Meg3+ HSCs and identified increased H3K23ac, a mark elevated in aged human haematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs)155 and associated with several cancers122,156, but whose function in HSCs was unknown. Notably, we established a direct link between proinflammatory signals (IL-1α, LPS, poly(I:C)) and H3K23ac induction via NF-κB signalling, providing evidence that inflammatory cues during ageing imprint the HSC epigenome and reshape lineage output.

p65/RELA functions as a pioneer transcription factor (TF) in determining H3K23ac specificity by recruiting KAT6A. In contrast, PU.1 expression does not determine H3K23ac. Although PU.1 has been reported to drive myeloid skewing in aged HSPCs157–159, the mechanism of its target selection remains unclear. Our findings support a model in which PU.1 is recruited to the open chromatin sites marked with elevated H3K23ac, contributing to lineage fate decisions in HSCs. These findings reconciled with previous evidence that chromatin context constrains PU.1 binding160. In addition, unlike the transit activation of p65/RELA, PU.1 exhibited consistent upregulation and nucleus entre in middle-aged HSCs. Therefore, we collectively unveiled a critical mechanism by which proinflammatory sustained H3K23ac to regulate HSC fate decision by navigating PU.1. Notably, KAT6A also mediates H3K23 propionylation (H3K23pr) via the BRPF1–KAT6 complex161. Given that distinct histone acylations may cooperate in transcriptional regulation162, it is possible that H3K23 acetylation and propionylation act together during HSC ageing to regulate lineage-associated genes, warrants further investigation.

Aged HSCs directly contribute to senescent immune remodelling and inflammaging through skewed lineage differentiation163–168. Recent studies suggest that depleting myeloid-biased HSCs may rejuvenate aged immunity and alleviate inflammaging22. Our findings extend this concept by showing that interfering with H3K23ac/TRIM24/PU.1 activity in aged HSCs can rebalance lineage differentiation and reduce SASP. We deliberately avoided directly targeting KAT6A, given its additional activity on H3K9, H3K14 and H3K27 (ref. 169), and instead used IACS-9571 or a TRIM24 bromodomain mutant to specifically disrupt the H3K23ac–TRIM24 interaction. This approach more precisely interrogates the H3K23ac-associated pathway and may hold therapeutic potential for alleviating senescent immune remodelling and inflammaging.

HSC transplantation assay

For HSC population transplantation assay, age-matched female C57BL/6J mice (CD45.2+) were used as recipients. Mice were fed with gentamycin for 7 days before the onset of experiments, and were exposed to a split dose of 8 Gy X-ray irradiation, at 4-h intervals. To assess the self-renewal potential and lineages biases of Meg3+, Pf4+ or Clec4d+ HSCs, 50 HSCs of interest were sorted from age-matched female donor mice (dTomato+CD45.2+ or dTomato+CD45.2+CD45.1+) and were transplanted into each lethally irradiated recipient (CD45.2+) together with 2–5 × 105 whole bone-marrow cells from competitor mice (CD45.1+). For the secondary transplantations, age-matched female C57BL/6J mice (CD45.2+) were treated as mentioned above and used as recipients. At 20 weeks after the primary transplantation, 4 × 106 bone-marrow cells were randomly collected from a part of the chimeric mice, and intravenously injected into new recipients. Peripheral blood samples were obtained from 8 to 40 weeks following the primary transplantation, or 4 to 20 weeks following the secondary transplantation, as indicated; Bone marrow and lymphoid organs were collected at 40 weeks post-primary, or at 20 weeks post-secondary transplantation, cells were stained with antibodies and analysed by flow cytometry to assess reconstitution efficiency and the output of lineages.

To assess the functional impact of H3K23 mutations, a competitive transplantation assay was performed. Approximately 100 HSCs infected with H3K23Q retrovirus (CD45.1+CD45.2+dTomato+) and 100 HSCs infected with H3K23R retrovirus (CD45.1+dTomato−) were co-transplanted into lethally irradiated CD45.2+ recipient mice, together with 2 × 105 radioprotective CD45.2+ bone-marrow cells. Peripheral blood and bone marrow were collected 8–20 weeks post-transplantation and analysed by flow cytometry to evaluate haematopoietic reconstitution and lineage output.

To evaluate whether interfering the recruiting of TRIM24 to H3K23ac would affect lineage output of HSCs, Trim24-deficient HSCs (CD45.2+) were infected with WT control (Trim24-WT) or TRIM24 F979A/N980A mutant (Trim24-Mut) retrovirus and transplanted into lethally irradiated recipient mice (CD45.1+CD45.2+dTomato+) through i.v. injection. Trim24-deficient HSCs were sorted from CreER Trim24f/f mice (8-week-old) treated with tamoxifen. Tamoxifen was intraperitoneally injected at a dose of 2 mg per mouse, every other day, over a 1-week interval. Recipients were treated with gentamycin as described above. Of each recipient, 200 retroviral infected HSCs were transplanted, along with a 5 × 105 radiation-protective dose of bone-marrow cells (CD45.1+CD45.2+dTomato+). Peripheral blood was collected from 8 to 16 weeks for flow cytometry analysis. At 20 weeks post-transplant, donor-derived CD45.2+GFP+ HSCs (Trim24-WT or Trim24-Mut) were isolated from primary chimeras, combined with 5 × 105 GFP+ whole bone-marrow cells, and transplanted into secondary lethally irradiated recipients. Secondary recipients were analysed by flow cytometry from week 12 to 20 after transfer.

LC–MS/MS analysis

Meg3+ HSCs were sorted as described above, then were lysed in 100 mM TEAB (containing 0.5% sodium deoxycholate) by freeze–thaw in liquid nitrogen, and digested by trypsin. scRNA-seq, scCITE-seq, epigenomic and proteomic data processing

Upstream analysis of scRNA-seq data from 10x Genomics

scRNA-seq data from 10x Genomics were mapped against the mm10 genome and quantified using Cell Ranger software (v.2.02) with default settings to generate raw gene expression matrix. Cells with fewer than 200 genes detected and more than 20% mitochondrial reads were considered as low-quality cells and were filtered out. Doublets identified by Doubletdetection were also removed. Genes expressed in fewer than 3 cells were removed as well.

Cell heterogeneity detection and annotation

To reveal the heterogeneity of HSC populations from 2-month-old mice, we used a routine pipeline of Seurat package174 to process the raw count data, reduce dimensionality, cluster and perform differential expression with accustomed protocol and parameters unless mentioned specially. We manually annotated the cell identity based on DEGs computed with the FindAllMarkers function (two-tailed Wilcoxon rank-sum test, P value adjusted for multiple testing using the Bonferroni correction). To reveal the heterogeneity of HSC populations from mice in different age groups, we performed pipeline described in https://satijalab.org/seurat/v3.1/integration.html to anchored and generated batch corrected matrices, following data scaling, dimension reduction, clustering and identity annotation as described above.

CITE-seq data analysis

CITE-seq RNA reads were mapped to the genome (mm10) and transcripts were quantified using Cell Ranger (v.3.1.0). The antibody-derived tag libraries were generated using the CITE-seq count program, and normalized using a centred log ratio (CLR) transformation across cells, implemented in Seurat with the NormalizeData function and the normalization. Method parameter set to “CLR”. The RNA counts obtained from CITE-seq of each cell were further integrated with scRNA-seq data, and processed as described above (Cell heterogeneity detection and annotation). We next extracted count matrix of HSC subsets and visualized the three surface proteins and corresponding RNA (CD201, CD63 and CD9) by the Seurat FeaturePlot function.

SMART-seq2 data analysis

Gene count matrices were sourced from two public SMART-seq2 datasets24,25. For matrix of Vwf+ HSCs from GSE188118, we aligned raw reads to mm10 using STAR (v2.7.6a) and performed quantification with featureCounts (v.2.1.1). The matrix of iHSCs from GSE116530 was downloaded directly from Gene Expression Omnibus. The gene count matrices were normalized for gene length175 and then log-normalized using the Seurat NormalizeData function. We utilized the Seurat pipeline described in the ‘Cell heterogeneity detection and annotation’ section to integrated HSC subsets defined in this study, Vwf+ HSCs and iHSCs.

Cell cycle analysis via single-cell transcriptome

To annotate the cell cycle status of each cell, a previously reported core gene set176,177 including 43 G1/S and 54 G2/M genes obtained from Seurat package were converted to homologous genes of mice and used to perform cell cycle analysis via the CellCycleScoring function in Seurat, which returned the cell cycle phases and scores information. However, to effectively reduce effects of cell cycle in the data, we obtained three cell cycle-related gene sets from GO (GO:0051301, GO:0006261 and GO:0007059). The mean expression of each gene set in the data was calculated and genes in each gene sets that exhibited low correlation with the corresponding mean expression were filtered out (r < 0.3). Then we calculated the score of each filtered gene set via AddModuleScore function in Seurat, which was later passed to ‘vars.to.regress’ parameter in ScaleData function and were regressed out.

Pathway analysis by ssGSEA

The ssGSEA algorithm178 was applied to identify gene sets with statistically significant differences between different cell subsets or the same cell subsets in different age groups. Gene sets of the Reactome, KEGG and GO databases collected in the Molecular Signatures Database were tested. The ssGSEA algorithm accepted a gene expression matrix as input and returned a pathways × cells matrix, which was then applied to Wilcoxon rank-sum test function in R to compute the significance, with P values adjusted for multiple testing using the Bonferroni correction.

STEMNET analysis

To infer the developmental potential of HSC populations, STEMNET analysis57 was performed. First, we combined HSCs in our data with mature cells in published data (GSE116530)24. All the data were from 2-month-old mice. Then we used STEMNET algorithm to calculate posterior probabilities of all the HSC populations based on the top 2,000 highly variable genes. The STEMNET visualization was obtained using the plot function, while the posterior probabilities used for boxplots were extracted from the STEMNET object.

Developmental trajectory analysis

We inferred the developmental trajectory of HSCs by using the FDG and PAGA methods in Scanpy179 and Monocle3 (ref. 180).

The pipeline of inferring developmental trajectory in Scanpy was similar to clustering analysis pipeline in Seurat. After obtaining the neighbourhood graph via the Scanpy pipeline, we computed diffusion map (scanpy.tl.diffmap with 20 components), pseudotime (scanpy.tl.dpt setting the Meg3+ HSC subsets as root based on current knowledge), PAGA graph (scnapy.tl.paga function with model equal to v.1.2) and the ForceAtlas2 (FA2) using PAGA-initialization (scanpy.tl.draw_graph function, which exploits the FA2 class from fa2 (v.0.3.5) Python, using the Meg3+ HSC as root and maxiter equal to 100).

As for the Monocle3 pipeline, the raw count matrix containing HSC populations was first imported to generate a cell dataset object. Normalization and principal-component analysis (PCA) were conducted with the preprocess_cds command from Monocle3 using the first 30 dimensions. UMAP dimensional reduction was performed using the reduce_dimension command with umap.min_dist set to 1. Cells were clustered with cluster_cells using ‘Leiden’ algorithm with the nearest neighbours set to 20. The trajectory graph was learnt on the Monocle-derived clusters by calling learn_graph. Cells on the UMAP plot were coloured by manual annotation of Seurat-derived clusters. Pseudotime was determined using Meg3+ HSC as the starting point based on current knowledge. Dynamic genes along with the trajectory were inferred via the generalized additive model in the gam package.

To investigate the developmental relationship between Clec4d+ HSCs and T-lineage progenitors, we performed Seurat integrative analysis of phenotypic LMPP181,182, MPP4 (refs. 47,131,183) and CLP181,182 in public datasets, which have recently been reported to give rise to T lineages46–48. The lymphoid-primed progenitor was defined as the cell cluster highly expressing Satb1, Il12a, Lmo2, Flt3 and Wfdc17, as indicated in a previous study47. Moreover, TSP was defined as cell cluster highly expressed Ccr7, Ccr9 and Hoxa9 as reported46,48. We then integrated Meg3+, Pf4+ and Clec4d+ HSC subsets with downstream lymphoid-primed progenitor and TSP via Seurat pipeline, withthe k.filter parameter in the FindIntegrationAnchors function set to be 50. PCA was carried out with the Seurat RunPCA command using the scaled count matrix. UMAP dimensional reduction was performed using the Seurat RunUMAP command with the top 20 principal components and random seed set to be 88. The UMAP embeddings and cell cluster information were then passed to the Monocle3 workflow. The trajectory graph was constructed on the Seurat-derived clusters. Pseudotime was determined using Meg3+ HSC as the starting point. Dynamic genes along with the trajectory were inferred via generalized additive model in the gam package.

Gene regulatory network analysis

To further assess the transcriptional and regulatory characteristics of different cell subsets or the same cell subsets with different age groups, gene regulatory network analysis was performed using SCENIC86. Regulatory modules (regulons; TFs and their target genes) were inferred from coexpression and DNA motif analysis. The AUC score of each regulon was then evaluated in each cell to infer the TF activity. Function of target genes regulated by specific TFs (fold change > 1.3 and adjusted P values < 0.05) of each HSC populations from 2-month-old mice were revealed by GSEA (v.4.1.0)186. To understand the collaborative biological function of transcription factors upregulated in Meg3+ HSCs from 8-month-old (versus 2-month-old) and 28-month-old (versus 2-month-old) mice, we selected corresponding TFs whose AUC scores shared high Spearman correlation. Then we applied target genes regulated by TFs mentioned above to GSEA using Reactome, KEGG and GO databases.

Proteome and transcriptome correlation analysis

We obtained genes overlapping between the proteome and transcriptome, computed their average expression values within respective age groups and subsequently employed the R (v.3.6.3) Spearman method based on these average expression values to calculate the correlation of these genes with ageing changes in both the proteome and transcriptome. Pathway enrichment analysis was conducted on the proteomic data using GSEA (v.4.1.0 (ref. 186)) software. The distribution of correlation coefficients (ρ) for genes enriched in pathways was then utilized to characterize the relationship between the proteome and transcriptome.

Clustering analysis of gene ageing expression patterns based on the average expression values in the transcriptome and proteome across different age groups was used MFUZZ97 package in R (v.4.1.0). Pathway enrichment via over-representative analysis (ORA) was performed using clusterProfiler (v.4.6.2 (ref. 187)).

CUT&Tag upstream analysis

Raw sequencing data (.fastq) were executed quality control using Fastp188 and were aligned to the mouse genome (UCSC, mm10) using bowtie2 (v.2.4.1)189 with routine parameters. The resulting sam files were converted to bam format, and reads were sorted. Duplicate reads were then removed using SAMtools (v.1.10)190 or Picard (v.2.6.0; https://github.com/broadinstitute/picard). Replicate samples within each group were merged using SAMtools (v.1.10)190, followed by sorting and deduplication. Subsequently, the bam files were converted to bedgraph format using BEDtools (v.2.29.2)191. SEACR192 (v.1.3, FDR < 0.05) was employed for peak calling. Peaks were filtered against genomic ‘blacklist’ regions (http://mitra.stanford.edu/kundaje/akundaje/release/blacklists/mm10-mouse). Colocated peaks was obtained using BEDtools intersect command (v.2.29.2)191.

CUT&Tag downstream analysis

The identification of differential peaks was performed using MACS2 bdgdiff with the ‘-diff’ parameter. Heatmap of peaks was generated by deeptools (v.3.5.0)193. Peaks were annotated using the CHIPseeker194. Pathway analysis was performed with GSEA (v.4.1.0)186 or the ORA187 command based on GO KEGG and Reactome databases. PiePlot ViolinPlot and RidgePlot were generated by open source R (v.3.6.3). Motif analysis was performed using MEME-ChIP (https://meme-suite.org/meme/tools/meme-chip), FIMO (https://meme-suite.org/meme/tools/fimo) and HOMER (v.4.11.1; https://github.com/javrodriguez/HOMER). TF regulatory network analysis was performed using Python SCENIC195 and visualized using R (v.3.6.3) ggraph and pheatmap.

ATAC-seq data upstream analysis

Raw sequencing data (.fastq) were executed quality control using Fastp188 and were aligned to the mouse genome (UCSC, mm10) using bowtie2 (ref. 189) with routine parameters. The resulting sam files were converted to bam format, and reads were sorted. Duplicate reads were then removed using SAMtools (v.1.10)190 or Picard (v.2.6.0; https://github.com/broadinstitute/picard). Replicate samples within each group were merged using SAMtools (v.1.10)190, followed by sorting and deduplication. MACS196 (default parameters) was employed for peak calling.

Integrated analysis of CUT&Tag and ATAC-seq data

For integration of H3K23ac CUT&Tag data and ATAC-seq data from young and middle-aged HSCs, the identification of differential peaks was performed using MACS bdgdiff196 with the ‘-diff’ parameter. Colocated peaks were obtained performed using BEDtools intersect command (v.2.29.2)191 (-intersect). Peak signals were calculated using deeptools (v.3.5.0)193 and visualized with R ggplot2. Pathway analysis was performed using GSEA (v.4.1.0)186 command based on GO KEGG and Reactome database with scRNA-seq expression data in 2 M and 8 M Meg3+ HSCs.

For integration of H3K23Q CUT&Tag data and ATAC-seq data in H3K23Q-infected cells, the identification of differential peaks was performed with the DiffBind package (v.2.14.0). Colocated peaks were obtained using BEDtools window command (v.2.29.2)191 with a window size of 1,000 bp. Peak signals were calculated using deeptools (v.3.5.0)193 and visualized with R ggplot2. Pathway analysis was performed using ORA based on GO, KEGG and Reactome databases. Motif analysis was performed using HOMER (v.4.11.1; https://github.com/javrodriguez/HOMER).

Statistics and reproducibility

No statistical methods were used to predetermine sample sizes but our sample sizes are similar to those reported in previous publications197–199. For all experiments, no data were excluded from the analyses. The experiments were not randomized and animals were assigned to experimental groups by age. The investigators were not blinded to group allocation during experiments or outcome assessment, as outcome assessment relied on objective quantitative measurements, making blinding not applicable. Data distribution was assumed to be normal but this was not formally tested. Detailed sample information is provided in the figure legends. The statistical methods used for each analysis are described in the figure legends. In brief, statistical significance between two groups was calculated using a two-tailed Student’s t-test or two-tailed Wilcoxon rank-sum test with Bonferroni correction. For multiple groups, significance was evaluated by one-way or two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey or Šídák’s multiple comparisons tests, or Kruskal–Wallis test. Statistical significance for cell–cell interaction analysis and GSEA was assessed using one-tailed permutation test. For ORA and motif enrichment, significance was assessed using a hypergeometric test. All experimental findings were reproducibly and independently validated. The number of independent replicates is indicated in the corresponding figure legends. Reproducibility was assessed by calculating the coefficient of variation for the measured ratios of overlapping peptides between replicates. All statistical analyses were performed in R (v.3.6.3) or GraphPad Prism (v.8.0).
