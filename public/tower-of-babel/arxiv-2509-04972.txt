The Classification of Rotationally symmetric hypersurfaces in the Heisenberg groups H n H_{n}
Hung-Lin Chiu, Sin-Hua Lai and Hsiao-Fan Liu

Abstract
. In this paper, we show the fundamental theorems for rotationally symmetric hypersurfaces, and thus, together with the earlier results in [ 3 ] and [ 4 ] , provide a complete classification of umbilic hypersurfaces in the Heisenberg groups H n H_{n} . In addition, we give a complete description of generating curves for rotationally symmetric hypersurfaces with constant p p -mean curvature H = c H=c (including H = 0 H=0 ) in the Heisenberg group H n H_{n} . We also establish the validity of Alexandrov’s theorem for rotationally symmetric hypersurfaces in H n H_{n} .

Introduction

Pseudohermitian geometry (
[
12
,
13
,
19
,
20
]
) came from several complex variables. It is a branch of Cartan geometry (
[
17
,
18
]
). The corresponding Klein geometry is the Heisenberg group
H_{n}
(
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
8
]
). That is, any pseudohermitian manifold with vanishing torsion and curvature is locally part of the Heisenberg group. In this sense, the Heisenberg group in Pseudohermitian geometry plays the same role as the Euclidean space plays in Riemannian geometry.

The classical topic to study in the geometry of submanifolds is the umbilic hypersurfaces of Euclidean space. In the Heisenberg group, there is also a similar umbilicity concept, which was first introduced in
[
3
]
by J.-H. Cheng, H.-L. Chiu, J.-F. Hwang, and P. Yang, to study Alexandrov’s theorem, which states that, in the Heisenberg group
H_{n}
, the only connected, closed,
C^{2}
constant
p
-mean curvature hypersurfaces are the Pansu spheres. For the definition of umbilicity and related concepts, we refer the readers to
[
3
]
and
[
4
]
, or Section
2
. There are two kinds of eigenvalues,
k
and
l
, of the shape operator. The eigenvalue
l
is just the
p
-curvature of the characteristic curves. For the Pansu sphere, we have that
l=2k
.

Let
PSH(n)
be the symmetric group of the Heisenberg rigid motions on
H_{n}
with CR-dimension
n
, and
\Sigma
a hypersurface in
H_{n}
. If
\Sigma
is invariant under the action of the subgroup
U(n)\subset PSH(n)
, then we say that the surface
\Sigma
is rotationally symmetric. If the function
\alpha
of
\Sigma
vanishes, i.e.,
\alpha=0
at each point of the rotationally symmetric hypersurface
\Sigma
, then
\Sigma
is a cylinder. On the other hand, on the region where
\alpha\neq 0
, it can be defined by the graph of a function depending only on the radius
r
. For
n\geq 2
, in Proposition 3.1 in
[
3
]
, we showed that each rotationally symmetric hypersurface is an umbilic hypersurface. Actually we have

Proposition
(
Proposition 3.1
. in
[
3
]
) If
\Sigma
is rotationally symmetric, then it is umbilic with
\alpha^{2}+k^{2}=\frac{1}{x^{2}}>0
. If, in addition, it is closed
and satisfies the condition
l=2k
, then
\Sigma
must be the Pansu sphere
S_{\lambda}
with
\lambda=k
.

Conversely, we have

Theorem
(
Theorem B
in
[
4
]
) Let
\Sigma
be an immersed, connected, umbilic hypersurface in
H_{n},n\geq 2
.
Let
S_{\Sigma}
denote the set of all singular points in
\Sigma
. Then

(a) either
\alpha^{2}+k^{2}\equiv 0
on
\Sigma
or
\alpha^{2}+k^{2}>0
at all points in
\Sigma\backslash S_{\Sigma}
.

(b) Suppose
\alpha^{2}+k^{2}\equiv 0
on
\Sigma
. Then
\Sigma
is congruent with part of a hypersurface
C^{n-1}\times\gamma\times\R
, where
\gamma
is a curve in the Euclidean plane

with signed curvature
l(=H)
.

(c) Suppose
\alpha^{2}+k^{2}>0
at all points in
\Sigma\backslash S_{\Sigma}
. Then
\Sigma
is congruent with part of a rotationally invariant (symmetric) hypersurface. Moreover, the radius of leaves in the associated foliation is
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+k^{2}}}
.

Interestingly, there is another geometric meaning for the eigenvalue
k
. The meaning is also true for
n=1
. Actually, the value
k
can be realized as the derivative of a kind of angle function. This angle function makes the generating curve a horizontal generating curve. For the details of this geometric meaning of
k
, we refer the reader to Proposition
3.3
in Section
3
. Let
\Sigma
be a rotationally symmetric hypersurface in
H_{n}
with generating curve

(0,\cdots,0,x(s),0,\cdots,0,0,t(s))

on the
x_{n}t
-plane, then using the horizontal arc-length
\tilde{s}
(for the definition, see
[
5
,
8
]
), we have (see (

\begin{split}x(\tilde{s})&=\int\alpha x(\tilde{s})d\tilde{s}\ \ \left(\Leftrightarrow x(\tilde{s})=e^{\int\alpha d\tilde{s}}\right)\\
t(\tilde{s})&=-\int kx^{2}(\tilde{s})d\tilde{s},\end{split}

with

x(\tilde{s})^{2}=\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}+k^{2}}(\tilde{s}),

where
\tilde{s}
is the horizontal arc-length of the corresponding horizontal generating curve.
Conversely, for rotationally symmetric hypersurfaces, we have the following fundamental theorem, which shows that the two functions
\alpha
and

constitute a complete set of invariants. This is the first main theorem in this paper.

Theorem I.

Given two functions
\bar{\alpha}(s),\bar{k}(s)
which satisfy the integrability condition (
3.21
).
Then there exists a rotationally symmetric hypersurface in
H_{n}
such that
\alpha(s)=\bar{\alpha}(s)
and
k(s)=\bar{k}(s)
for all
s
, and
s
is the horizontal arc-length. In addition, such a hypersurface is unique, up to a Heisenberg rigid motion, with the generating curve on the
x_{n}t
-plane as follows:

\begin{split}x(s)&=e^{\int\bar{\alpha}ds}\\
t(s)&=-\int\bar{k}x^{2}ds.\end{split}

Notice that the generating curve is uniquely defined up to a translation along the

Therefore, together with
Theorem I
and

in

From

, there is an immediate corollary, please see Theorem

In addition, if the
p
-mean curvature
H
of a rotationally symmetric hypersurface is constant, there is an associated invariant for
\Sigma
, which is called the energy
E
. This energy
E
is a constant and was first introduced by M. Ritoré and C. Rosales in their paper
[
15
]
to study the rotationally symmetric hypersurfaces in the Heisenberg groups. Actually, M. Ritoré and C. Rosales showed that the generating curve
\gamma=(x,t)
of a rotationally symmetric hypersurface with constant
p
-mean curvature
H
satisfies a system of ordinary differential equations whenever
x>0
. Moreover, the system they studied has a first integral that is constant along any solution. M. Ritoré and C. Rosales called the constant
E
the energy of the solution and showed that the first integral allows them to give a complete description of the solution. It turned out that they used the sign of the energy
E
, the
p
-mean curvature
H
, together with the sign of the product
EH
, to divide the generated constant
p
-mean hypersurfaces into six classes (for the details, see Theorem 5.4 in
[
15
]
). In this paper, in some sense, we showed that such hypersurfaces are in one-to-one correspondence with the energy
E
. We are also able to describe its generating curve as specified by the following
Theorem II
and
Theorem III
, which are our next two main theorems in this paper. Actually, in subsection
3.2
, we introduce the concept of the reflective rotationally symmetric hypersurfaces. Basically, each rotationally symmetric hypersurface locally is part of a reflective one. Theorems
II
and
III
point out that they are eventually in one-to-one correspondence with the energy
E
. In subsection
4.1
, we will give another realization of the energy
E
. This realization will help us to write out the generating curve of a reflective one.

Theorem II.

For rotationally symmetric hypersurfaces with constant
p
-mean curvature
H=c>0
, the energy
E
has a lower bound as follows:

(1.1)

E\geq\frac{-1}{2n}\left(\frac{2n-1}{c}\right)^{2n-1}.

This lower bound is optimal. For any such value
E
, there exists a unique reflective rotationally symmetric hypersurface with constant
p
-mean curvature
H=c
with
E
as the energy. The corresponding generating curve is

(1.2)

\begin{split}x(\tilde{s})&=F_{E}(\tilde{s})\\
t(\tilde{s})&=-\int\left(\frac{c}{2n}-\frac{E}{x^{2n}}\right)x^{2}d\tilde{s}.\end{split}

Here, the horizontal arc-length
\tilde{s}=G_{E}(x)
is defined by the anti-derivative of the function with respect to
x
,

\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}\left(\frac{E}{x^{2n}}-\frac{c}{2n}\right)^{2}}},

for all
x\in(a,b)
, an interval depending on
E
. And
F_{E}(\tilde{s})
is the inverse of
G_{E}(x)
.

When the constant
c<0
,
Theorem II
has a duality description in which we just replace the optimal lower bound of
E
by the optimal upper bound

E\leq\frac{1}{2n}\left(\frac{2n-1}{|c|}\right)^{2n-1}.

The proof of the duality is the same as the one for
Theorem II
.

Theorem III.

Given any
E\in\R
, there exists a unique reflective rotationally symmetric hypersurface with
p
-mean curvature
H=0
with
E
as the energy. The corresponding generating curve is

(1.3)

\begin{split}x(\tilde{s})&=F_{E}(\tilde{s})\\
t(\tilde{s})&=\int\left(\frac{E}{x^{2n-2}}\right)d\tilde{s}.\end{split}

Here, the horizontal arc-length
\tilde{s}=G_{E}(x)
is defined by the anti-derivative of the function with respect to
x
,

\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\left(\frac{E}{x^{2n-1}}\right)^{2}}},

for all
x>|E|^{\frac{1}{2n-1}}
. And
F_{E}(\tilde{s})
be the inverse of
G_{E}(x)
.

As applications, we can obtain some partial results in Alexandrov’s Theorem and Bernstein’s Problem (

2.
Umbilic hypersurfaces

In this section, we make a brief introduction to the basic materials of umbilic hypersurfaces in the Heisenberg group
H_{n}
for
n\geq 2
. For the details, we refer the reader to
[
3
,
4
]
.
Throughout this paper, we always assume
\Sigma
is immersed
(say,
C^{2}
smooth and
C^{\infty}
smooth in the region of regular
points, see the definition of regular point below), and let
\xi

(
J,
resp.) denote the standard contact (
CR
, resp.) structure on
H_{n},

defined by the kernel of the contact form

\Theta=dt+\sum_{j=1}^{n}(x_{j}dy_{j}-y_{j}dx_{j})

where

A point

\xi

=

T\Sigma
at

Otherwise,

is called regular or nonsingular. For a regular point, we define
\xi^{\prime}

\subset

\xi\cap T\Sigma

\xi^{\prime}=(\xi\cap T\Sigma)\cap J(\xi\cap T\Sigma).

Let (

\xi

\xi^{\prime}
with respect to the Levi metric
G:=\frac{1}{2}d\Theta(\cdot,J\cdot)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}[(dx_{j})^{2}+(dy_{j})^{2}].
It is not hard to see that
\dim(\xi

\cap

T\Sigma)

\cap
(
\xi^{\prime}
)
⊥

=

1.
Take

\in
(
\xi

\cap

T\Sigma)

\cap
(
\xi^{\prime}
)
⊥
of unit length. Define the horizontal normal
e_{2n}:=Je_{n}
. Let

\nabla
denote the pseudohermitian connection associated to
(J,\Theta).

Let
\alpha
be the function on
\Sigma
, such that
\alpha e_{2n}+T

\in

T\Sigma
where

:
=

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}.
Define

J^{\prime}=J\text{ on }\xi^{\prime}\text{ and }J^{\prime}e_{n}=0.

We now have a symmetric shape operator

\xi\cap T\Sigma\rightarrow\xi\cap T\Sigma,
defined by

(2.1)

\mathfrak{S(}v\mathfrak{)}=-\nabla_{v}e_{2n}+\alpha J^{\prime}v.

(see Proposition 2.2 in
[

is
called an umbilic point if
\mathfrak{S}(\xi^{\prime})

\subset

\xi^{\prime}
at

and all the eigenvalues of

acting on

On the other hand, by Proposition 2.3

[

\mathfrak{S}(e_{n})=le_{n}

for some real number

In terms of

we have

In

3

, J.-H. Cheng, H.-L. Chiu, J.-F. Hwang, and P. Yang showed that a rotationally symmetric hypersurface is umbilic. They also study umbilic hypersurfaces with positive constant

-mean curvature.
Among others, we show that any umbilic sphere of

is a Pansu sphere up to a

is the union of the graphs of the functions

(2.2)

g(z)=\frac{1}{2\lambda^{2}}\left(\lambda|z|\sqrt{1-\lambda^{2}|z|^{2}}+\cos^{-1}{\lambda|z|}\right),\ \ \ |z|\leq\frac{1}{\lambda}.

It is known that

has

-mean

(see Section 2 in
[

]
for instance).

Example 2.1
.

In
[

, there are two umbilic hypersurfaces with

, that is,

We have

e_{2n}=\frac{f_{n}\mathring{e}_{n}+f_{2n}\mathring{e}_{2n}}{\sqrt{(f_{n})^{2}+(f_{2n})^{2}}},\ \ \textrm{where}\ \ f_{n}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{n}},f_{2n}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial y_{n}}.

Since

\begin{split}-\nabla_{e_{n}}e_{2n}&=-e_{n}\left(\frac{f_{n}}{\sqrt{(f_{n})^{2}+(f_{2n})^{2}}}\right)\mathring{e}_{n}-e_{n}\left(\frac{f_{2n}}{\sqrt{(f_{n})^{2}+(f_{2{}n})^{2}}}\right)\mathring{e}_{2n}\\

Thus,

3.
Rotationally symmetric hypersurfaces

In this section, we will focus on the study of the rotationally symmetric hypersurfaces in the Heisenberg groups
H_{n}
for
n\geq 1
. The proof of
Theorem I
will be given at the end of this section.

3.1.
The formulae for
\alpha
,
k
, and
l
.

Recall that if
\alpha=0
for a rotationally symmetric hypersurface, then it is the cylinder. If
\alpha\neq 0
, then it can be presented as the graph of a function that only depends on the radius variable
|z|
. We inherit the notation in
[
3
]
and thus suppose that
t^{2}=f=f(r)
is a function of
r,(r=|z|^{2})
, then
u=f(r)-t^{2}
is a defining function of the hypersurface
\Sigma
.
With respect to
the horizontal normal

(3.1)

e_{2n}=\frac{\nabla_{b}u}{|\nabla_{b}u|}

so that
e_{n}=-Je_{2n}
, we have in
[
3
]
that

(3.2)

\begin{split}\alpha&=\frac{t}{|z|\sqrt{(f^{\prime})^{2}+f}},\\
k&=\frac{-f^{\prime}}{|z|\sqrt{(f^{\prime})^{2}+f}},\\
l&=\frac{|z|^{2}-f^{\prime}}{|z|\sqrt{(f^{\prime})^{2}+f}}-\frac{(1+2f^{\prime\prime})|z|f}{((f^{\prime})^{2}+f)^{3/2}},\end{split}

where in this subsection
f^{\prime}(r)=\frac{df}{dr}
and
f^{\prime\prime}(r)=\frac{d^{2}f}{dr^{2}}
. Note that we have chosen this horizontal normal
e_{2n}
to make sure that the Pansu sphere has positive
p
-mean curvature. Actually, formulae (
3.2
) also hold in the dimension

, while, instead of being an eigenvalue of the shape operator, the function
k
has an interesting geometric meaning (see Proposition

below).
After a straightforward computation, we see that

(3.3)

\begin{split}e_{2n}&=\sum_{\beta=1}^{n}\frac{(f^{\prime}x_{\beta}-ty_{\beta})\mathring{e}_{\beta}+(f^{\prime}y_{\beta}+tx_{\beta})\mathring{e}_{n+\beta}}{|z|\sqrt{(f^{\prime})^{2}+f}},\\
e_{n}&=\sum_{\beta=1}^{n}\frac{(f^{\prime}y_{\beta}+tx_{\beta})\mathring{e}_{\beta}-(f^{\prime}x_{\beta}-ty_{\beta})\mathring{e}_{n+\beta}}{|z|\sqrt{(f^{\prime})^{2}+f}}.\\
\end{split}

We can also write out
e_{n}
as

(3.4)

e_{n}=\frac{\sum_{\beta=1}^{n}[(f^{\prime}y_{\beta}+tx_{\beta})\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{\beta}}-(f^{\prime}x_{\beta}-ty_{\beta})\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{\beta}}]+(f^{\prime}r^{2})\frac{\partial}{\partial t}}{|z|\sqrt{(f^{\prime})^{2}+f}}.

3.2.
The reflective symmetric hypersurface

The hypersurface
\Sigma
is divided into the union of two parts, where

\Sigma=\Sigma^{+}\cup\Sigma^{-}
,

(3.5)

\begin{split}\Sigma^{+}&=\{(z,t)\in\Sigma\ |\ t=\sqrt{f}\},\\
\Sigma^{-}&=\{(z,t)\in\Sigma\ |\ t=-\sqrt{f}\}.\end{split}

These two parts might be disjoint from each other. If we use
\alpha^{\pm},\ k^{\pm}
, and
\l^{\pm}
to denote the corresponding geometric invariants for
\Sigma^{\pm}
, then from (

), for all

in the domain of

, we have that both
\alpha
and

have the same sign, and both

and

have the opposite sign. And

(3.6)

\alpha^{+}(r)\geq 0,\ \ \ \alpha^{+}(r)=-\alpha^{-}(r),\ \ \ k^{+}(r)=k^{-}(r),\ \ \ l^{+}(r)=l^{-}(r).

These two parts can be transformed into each other by the reflection with respect to the hyperplane

. Thus,
\Sigma
is also a reflective symmetric hypersurface. For the properties of a rotationally symmetric hypersurface, it suffices to study such reflective symmetric hypersurfaces. The generating curve
\gamma
for a reflective hypersurface

will consist of two parts,

, one for
\Sigma^{+}
, the other for
\Sigma^{-}
.

The formulae for

′

Proposition 3.1

With respect to the orientation defined by

\begin{split}\alpha&=\frac{x^{\prime}}{\sqrt{(xx^{\prime})^{2}+(t^{\prime})^{2}}}(s)\\

Notice that

Proof.

Proposition 3.4

For any

(3.17)

\frac{\partial\tilde{\gamma}}{\partial\theta}(\tilde{s},\theta)=x^{2}(ke_{n}+\alpha e_{2n}+T).

Proof.

Taking the derivative of

3.4.
Fundamental theorems for rotationally symmetric hypersurfaces

In this subsection, we will give a proof of
Theorem I
.
Let
\Sigma
be a rotationally symmetric hypersurface in
H_{n}
with generating curve

(0,\cdots,0,x(s),0,\cdots,0,0,t(s))

on the
x_{n}t
-plane, then using the horizontal arc-length
\tilde{s}
, by (
3.13
), we have

(3.19)

\begin{split}x(\tilde{s})&=\int\alpha x(\tilde{s})d\tilde{s}\ \ \left(\Leftrightarrow x(\tilde{s})=e^{\int\alpha d\tilde{s}}\right)\\
t(\tilde{s})&=-\int kx^{2}(\tilde{s})d\tilde{s},\end{split}

with

x(\tilde{s})^{2}=\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}+k^{2}}(\tilde{s}),

where
\tilde{s}
is the horizontal arc-length of the corresponding horizontal generating curve.
Conversely, given two functions
\bar{\alpha}(s),\bar{k}(s)
, we define a curve on the
x_{n}t
-plane by

(3.20)

\begin{split}x(s)&=e^{\int\bar{\alpha}ds},\\
t(s)&=-\int\bar{k}x^{2}ds,\end{split}

here
x(s)
is determined up to a constant multiple, and thus the curve is determined up to a Heisenberg dilation. Note that parameter
s
here need not be the arc-length. We normalize
(x(s),t(s))
by means of the condition

(3.21)

\frac{(xx^{\prime})^{2}+(t^{\prime})^{2}}{x^{2}}=1.

This curve is therefore uniquely defined up to a translation along the
t
-axis, and generates a rotationally symmetric hypersurface in
H_{n}
. The following theorem (Theorem
3.7
) shows that this generated hypersurface is just the rotationally symmetric one with
\bar{\alpha}(s)
and
\bar{k}(s)
as its
\alpha
-function and
k
-function.

Theorem 3.7
.

Given two functions
\bar{\alpha}(s),\bar{k}(s)
which satisfy the integrability condition (
3.21
).
Then there exists a rotationally symmetric hypersurface such that
\alpha(s)=\bar{\alpha}(s)
and
k(s)=\bar{k}(s)
for all
s
. And
s
is the horizontal arc-length. In addition, such a hypersurface is unique, up to a Heisenberg rigid motion.

Proof.

We consider the horizontal generating curve

\tilde{\gamma}(s)=(0,\cdots,0,x(s)\cos{\theta(s)},0,\cdots,0,x(s)\sin{\theta(s)},t(s)).

for some angle function
\theta=\theta(s)
. After a straightforward computation, we have

\begin{split}\frac{d\tilde{\gamma}}{ds}(s)&=(x^{\prime}\cos{\theta}-x\sin{\theta}\cdot\theta^{\prime})\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{n}}+(x^{\prime}\sin{\theta}+x\cos{\theta}\cdot\theta^{\prime})\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{n}}+(t^{\prime})\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\\
&=(x^{\prime}\cos{\theta}-x\sin{\theta}\cdot\theta^{\prime})\mathring{e}_{n}+(x^{\prime}\sin{\theta}+x\cos{\theta}\cdot\theta^{\prime})\mathring{e}_{2n}+(x^{2}\theta^{\prime}+t^{\prime})\frac{\partial}{\partial t},\end{split}

which implies that
\tilde{\gamma}(s)
is horizontal if and only if

\theta^{\prime}=-\frac{t^{\prime}}{x^{2}}.

Thus, we have

\begin{split}\left\|\frac{d\tilde{\gamma}}{ds}\right\|^{2}&=(x^{\prime}\cos{\theta}-x\sin{\theta}\cdot\theta^{\prime})^{2}+(x^{\prime}\sin{\theta}+x\cos{\theta}\cdot\theta^{\prime})^{2}\\
&=(x^{\prime})^{2}+x^{2}(\theta^{\prime})^{2}=\frac{(xx^{\prime})^{2}+(t^{\prime})^{2}}{x^{2}},\end{split}

which shows that the normalization (
3.21
) just means that
s
is the horizontal arc-length
\tilde{s}
of the corresponding horizontal generating curve. Next, from the definition of the generating curve (
3.20
), we immediately have

(3.22)

\bar{\alpha}=x^{-1}x^{\prime}\ \ \textrm{and}\ \ \bar{k}=-x^{-2}t^{\prime}.

Thus,

\begin{split}\bar{\alpha}^{2}+\bar{k}^{2}&=x^{-2}(x^{\prime})^{2}+x^{-4}(t^{\prime})^{2}\\
&=\frac{1}{x^{2}}\left(\frac{(xx^{\prime})^{2}+(t^{\prime})^{2}}{x^{2}}\right)=\frac{1}{x^{2}}.\end{split}

Let
\alpha
and
k
be the
\alpha
-function and
k
-function of the generated surface. Then (
3.19
) and (
3.20
) yield that

(\alpha^{2}+k^{2})(\tilde{s})=(\bar{\alpha}^{2}+\bar{k}^{2})(s)

and

(3.23)

\begin{split}t(\tilde{s})&=-\int kx^{2}(\tilde{s})d\tilde{s}=-\int\bar{k}x^{2}(s)ds\\
&=-\int\bar{k}x^{2}(s)\frac{ds}{d\tilde{s}}d\tilde{s},\end{split}

for all
\tilde{s}
.
Therefore, we have

(3.24)

\begin{split}k(\tilde{s})&=\bar{k}(s)\frac{ds}{d\tilde{s}},\\
\alpha^{2}(\tilde{s})&=\bar{\alpha}^{2}(s)+\bar{k}^{2}\left(1-(\frac{ds}{d\tilde{s}})^{2}\right).\end{split}

In particular, we have
k(\tilde{s})=\bar{k}(s)
and
\alpha^{2}(\tilde{s})=\bar{\alpha}^{2}(s)
as
\frac{ds}{d\tilde{s}}=1
, i.e,
s
is the horizontal arc-length of the corresponding horizontal generating curve. Since

x(s)=e^{\int\alpha ds}=e^{\int\bar{\alpha}ds},

we have that
\alpha(\tilde{s})=\bar{\alpha}(s)
. This completes the proof of Theorem
3.7
and thus provides a proof for
Theorem I
.
∎

We have the following immediate consequence.

Theorem 3.8
.

Suppose
\bar{\alpha}(s)
is a function such that

(3.25)

\frac{1}{(e^{\int\bar{\alpha}ds})^{2}}-\bar{\alpha}^{2}\geq 0,

for some anti-derivative
\int\bar{\alpha}ds

\bar{k}^{2}=\frac{1}{(e^{\int\bar{\alpha}ds})^{2}}-\bar{\alpha}^{2}.

and

for all

. And
s
is the horizontal arc-length. In addition, such a hypersurface is determined up to a Heisenberg rigid motion.

Proof.

We define the generating curve
(x(s),t(s))
by

x(s)=e^{\int\bar{\alpha}ds},\ \ t(s)=-\int\bar{k}x^{2}ds.

We then claim that this curve satisfies the condition (

) as the following:

\frac{dx}{ds}=\bar{\alpha}e^{\int\bar{\alpha}ds}=\bar{\alpha}x\ \ \textrm{and}\ \ \ \frac{dt}{ds}=-\bar{k}x^{2}.

Thus,

\begin{split}(xx^{\prime})^{2}+(t^{\prime})^{2}&=\bar{\alpha}^{2}x^{4}+\bar{k}^{2}x^{4}\\
&=\bar{\alpha}^{2}x^{4}+\left(-\bar{\alpha}^{2}+\frac{1}{(e^{\int\bar{\alpha}ds})^{2}}\right)x^{4}\\

∎

If

Example 3.10
.

We compute the example

Consider the generating curve in the

The velocity

Suppose that

4.1.
The energy
E

In this subsection, we will give another realization of the energy
E

Theorem II

We write the
p

We observe both the first equations of (

(l-2k)\frac{dx}{d\tilde{s}}-x\frac{dk}{d\tilde{s}}=0.

Suppose that

Multiplying

\begin{split}0&=2n(\lambda-k)x^{2n-1}\frac{dx}{d\tilde{s}}+x^{2n}\frac{d(\lambda-k)}{d\tilde{s}}\\
&=\frac{d}{d\tilde{s}}\left[(\lambda-k)x^{2n}\right].\end{split}

That is,

\begin{split}(\lambda-k)x^{2n}&=x^{2n-2}\frac{dt}{d\tilde{s}}+\lambda x^{2n}\\
&=\frac{x^{2n-1}t^{\prime}}{\sqrt{(xx^{\prime})^{2}+(t^{\prime})^{2}}}+\lambda x^{2n},\end{split}

which is just the first integral
E

[
15
]
. Here
′
means
\frac{d}{ds}
.
Conversely, suppose that the energy
E
is constant. If we follow the above arguments backwards, then it is easy to see that
\lambda
is constant.
In summary, we have the following propositions for rotationally symmetric hypersurfaces.

Proposition 4.1
.

The

is constant if and only if the energy
E
is constant.

Recall that in Section

\Sigma

Constant

Suppose that

(4.1)

(4.2)

Thus, by (

(4.3)

\begin{split}1&=x^{2}(\alpha^{2}+k^{2})=x^{2}\alpha^{2}+x^{2}k^{2}\\

that is,

(4.4)

(x^{\prime})^{2}=1-x^{2}\left(\frac{E}{x^{2n}}-\frac{c}{2n}\right)^{2},

Notice that we have chosen the orientation

We solve the above equation to get

for some constant

in (

Theorem II

4.3.
The proof of
Theorem II

We first prove the lower bound of
E
for the cases
c>0
. From (
4.2
), and notice that
(\alpha^{2}+k^{2})x^{2}=1
, we have that

\left(\frac{E}{x^{2n}}-\frac{c}{2n}\right)^{2}\leq\frac{1}{x^{2}},

that is,

(4.7)

\frac{c}{2n}x^{2n}-x^{2n-1}\leq E\leq\frac{c}{2n}x^{2n}+x^{2n-1},\ \ \textrm{for all}\ \tilde{s}.

Let
f:\R\rightarrow\R
be a function defined by the left polynomial in (
4.7
),

f(x)=\frac{c}{2n}x^{2n}-x^{2n-1}.

It is a straightforward computation that

\frac{df}{dx}=(cx-(2n-1))x^{2n-2},\ \ \ \frac{d^{2}f}{dx^{2}}=(cx-(2n-2))(2n-1)x^{2n-3}.

Thus, the point
x=\frac{2n-1}{c}
is the unique critical point at which the second derivative
\frac{d^{2}f}{dx^{2}}(\frac{2n-1}{c})
is positive. We conclude that the function
f(x)
has the absolute minimum at
x=\frac{2n-1}{c}
, that is,

f(x)=\frac{c}{2n}x^{2n}-x^{2n-1}\geq\frac{-1}{2n}\left(\frac{2n-1}{c}\right)^{2n-1}.

Example
4.3
later shows that this bound is optimal. Next, we prove the existence and uniqueness. Given any value
E
, which is strictly greater than the optimal lower bound. From the first inequality in (
4.7
), there exists an interval
(a,b)
such that

-1<\frac{2nE-cx^{2n}}{2nx^{2n-1}}<1,\ \ \ \textrm{for}\ x\in(a,b),

which is equivalent to the inequality

1-x^{2}\left(\frac{E}{x^{2n}}-\frac{c}{2n}\right)^{2}>0,\ \ \ \textrm{for}\ x\in(a,b).

Define a function
\tilde{s}
on
(a,b)
by
\tilde{s}=G_{E}(x)
, where
G_{E}(x)
is the anti-derivative of

\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}\left(\frac{E}{x^{2n}}-\frac{c}{2n}\right)^{2}}},

where the sign (
+
or
-
) allows us to define two generating curves later. We have
\frac{dG_{E}}{dx}\neq 0
, which implies that
G_{E}
has the inverse function. Let
F_{E}(\tilde{s})
be the inverse of
G_{E}(x)
. We define
x(\tilde{s})=F_{E}(\tilde{s})
and then define

\alpha(\tilde{s})=\frac{x^{\prime}}{x}(\tilde{s}),\ \ \ k(\tilde{s})=\frac{c}{2n}-\frac{E}{x^{2n}}.

We have

\alpha(\tilde{s})=\frac{x^{\prime}}{x}(\tilde{s})=\frac{F_{E}^{\prime}}{x}=\frac{1}{x\frac{dG_{E}}{dx}}=\frac{\sqrt{1-x^{2}\left(\frac{E}{x^{2n}}-\frac{c}{2n}\right)^{2}}}{x},

and hence

\alpha^{2}+k^{2}=\frac{1}{x^{2}},

which is equivalent to the integrability condition (
3.21
) in Theorem
3.7
. By Theorem
3.7
, these two functions,
\alpha
and
k
, determine a unique rotationally symmetric hypersurface that is also reflective (due to the sign
\pm
, which, respectively, defines the generating curves
\gamma^{+}
and
\gamma^{-}
for
\Sigma^{+}
and
\Sigma^{-}
). In addition, from the definition of
k
, we have that
(\lambda-k)x^{2n}
is just the constant
E
. Therefore, from the proof of Proposition
4.1
, we conclude that the mean curvature

is constant. This completes the proof of
Theorem II

4.5.
The case for

.

In this subsection, we will show that
Theorem II
and
Theorem III
are just Theorem A and Theorem B in
[

]
in the case

n
.
If
c>0
, then

(4.9)

\begin{split}\pm\tilde{s}+c_{1}&=\int\frac{xdx}{\sqrt{x^{2}-\left(E-\frac{c}{2}x^{2}\right)^{2}}}=\frac{1}{2}\int\frac{\frac{1}{\lambda}dy}{\sqrt{\frac{y+E}{\lambda}-y^{2}}}\ \ \ (y=\lambda x^{2}-E)\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\int\frac{\frac{1}{\lambda}dy}{\sqrt{\left(\frac{E}{\lambda}+\frac{1}{4\lambda^{2}}\right)-(y-\frac{1}{2\lambda})^{2}}}\\
&=\frac{1}{c}\int\frac{dy}{\sqrt{\left(\frac{2E}{c}+\frac{1}{c^{2}}\right)-(y-\frac{1}{c})^{2}}}\end{split}

or

\pm c\tilde{s}+cc_{1}=\int\frac{d\left(y-\frac{1}{c}\right)}{\sqrt{\left(\frac{2E}{c}+\frac{1}{c^{2}}\right)-(y-\frac{1}{c})^{2}}},

which implies that

\pm c\tilde{s}+cc_{1}=\sin^{-1}{\frac{\lambda x^{2}-(E+\frac{1}{c})}{a}},\ \ \ \mbox{where }a=\frac{\sqrt{2cE+1}}{c}.

Thus,

x^{2}=\frac{2\sqrt{2cE+1}}{c^{2}}\sin{(\pm c\tilde{s}+cc_{1})}+\frac{2cE+2}{c^{2}}.

If
c=0
, then

\begin{split}\pm\tilde{s}+c_{1}&=\int\frac{xdx}{\sqrt{x^{2}-E^{2}}}\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\int\frac{dy}{\sqrt{y}},\ \ \ y=x^{2}-E^{2},\end{split}

and thus

x^{2}=(\pm\tilde{s}+c_{1})^{2}+E^{2}.

For general CR dimension
