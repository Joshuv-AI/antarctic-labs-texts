Effect of psychological care on the prenatal mental state and vaginal delivery in pregnant women during the early stages of the COVID-19 epidemic
Authors: Yanping Liu, Xinai Xu
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
The COVID-19 pandemic has had a profound impact on the lives of people globally, including pregnant women. We aimed to explore the influence of a psychological care intervention based on behavioral change theory on the prenatal mental state and mode of delivery in pregnant women from June 2020 to June 2022 during the early stage of the COVID-19 epidemic in China. A retrospective analysis was conducted of the records of pregnant women who delivered in our hospital from June 2020 to June 2022. They were assigned to an observation and a control group according to the care that they received. The Symptom Checklist-90 (SCL-90) was used to evaluate their negative emotions. The duration of each labor stage and the mode of delivery (cesarean section, spontaneous vaginal delivery, or assisted vaginal delivery) were recorded. A total of 216 women were included in the analysis. Before care, there were no significant differences in the SCL-90 scores between the 2 groups. After delivery, the SCL-90 scores in the observation group were significantly lower than those in the control group. The vaginal delivery rate in the observation group was 92.59%, which was significantly higher than that in the control group (70.39%). The duration of the first stage of labor and the total duration of labor in the observation group were 447.95 ± 53.45 minutes and 498.15 ± 35.14 minutes, respectively, which were significantly lower than those in the control group. The implementation of psychological care based on behavioral change theory in pregnant women during the early stage of the COVID-19 epidemic significantly improved their prenatal psychological state, promoted vaginal delivery, and help shorten the labor process, and reduce the risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes. This intervention is thus worth promoting.

1. Introduction

Coronavirus disease (COVID-19) remains a focus of global clinical and epidemic control efforts.[1] By the end of December 2022, nearly 500 million cases had been confirmed in China, including 483,780 cases in Hong Kong, Macao, and Taiwan, and 13,552 deaths.[2,3] On January 31, 2020, COVID-19 was recognized by the World Health Organization as a Public Health Emergency of International Concern. The Chinese government attaches great importance to the prevention and control of COVID-19 and classifies it as a Class B infectious disease under the Prevention and Control of Class A Infectious Diseases. As such, it requires regular prevention and control over a long period. Currently, the COVID-19 epidemic in China has frequent surges, and the Delta variant, Omicron variant, and Omicron subvariants have emerged in succession, and have higher transmissibility and infectiousness, and wider drug resistance than the original strain.[4] On November 26, 2021, World Health Organization assessed the overall risk of Omicron variant as “very high” worldwide, with high potential for spread in the general population.

Pregnant women are a distinct group, characterized by a unique state of immune tolerance in their bodies. This affects disease manifestations and treatment.[5] During pregnancy the mucous membrane of the upper respiratory tract becomes thickened, mildly congested, and edematous, influenced by elevated levels of estrogen and progesterone, predisposing pregnant women to upper respiratory tract infections.[6] The inflammatory response to viral respiratory infections in pregnant women is notably heightened. Therefore, if infected with SARS-CoV-2, pregnant women are prone to rapid disease progression, often developing severe disease. This necessitates heightened vigilance, and intensified preventive measures. During the early stage of the COVID-19 epidemic, new forms of care were needed for pregnant women. Due to a lack of childbirth experience and the sensitive and special period, women are prone to anxiety, depression, fear and other negative emotions during late pregnancy. This can have an effect on their productivity, the birth canal, and other conditions, and can seriously affect the quality of delivery.[7,8] Therefore, effective intervention measures should be provided to pregnant women before delivery. Psychological care based on behavioral change theory was introduced into the care of pregnant women during the early stage of the COVID-19 epidemic to improve their self-care ability.[9] The intervention emphasized the participation of women’s families in the intervention process, working with nursing staff to relieve negative emotions in pregnant women to improve the quality of delivery.[10–14]

The purpose of this study was to explore the effect of psychological care based on behavioral change theory on the prenatal mental state of pregnant women and vaginal delivery during the early stage of the COVID-19 epidemic.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study design and setting

This study is a retrospective study. A total of 216 pregnant women who delivered in our hospital from June 2020 to June 2022 were considered as the study participants.

2.2. Eligibility criteria

The inclusion criteria were: (1) pregnant women with a singleton pregnancy; (2) abdominal B-ultrasound examination showed cephalic presentation, and relevant examinations before delivery confirmed that the woman met the indications for vaginal delivery; and (3) no perinatal complications. The exclusion criteria were: (1) gestational diabetes or preeclampsia; (2) mental health, cognitive or psychiatric problems; and (3) multiple pregnancy.

2.3. Ethics approval and informed consent

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanxi Fenyang Hospital.

2.4. Routine care

Women in the control group were provided with routine care: (1) environmental interventions to provide a quiet, comfortable and clean environment for pregnant women; (2) health guidance to inform pregnant women and their families of predelivery preparations, advantages of vaginal delivery, delivery procedures and postpartum maternal and child health knowledge; (3) vital signs monitoring: monitoring blood pressure, heart rate, and fetal heart rate; (4) psychological guidance according to the woman’s education level: personalized psychological intervention was carried out to answer the questions raised by pregnant women, and encourage them to undergo vaginal delivery; (5) protection and helping pregnant women to get out of bed during the labor and delivery period; providing pain relief and abdominal and lumbosacral massage for women with severe contractions; providing eggs, milk, chocolate, and other foods in intervals between contractions to replenish women’s strength.

2.5. Psychological care intervention

In addition to routine care, women in the observation group were provided with psychological care based on behavioral change theory. The psychological care intervention included the following measures:

2.5.1. Identifying a responsible family member.

A family member with strong sense of responsibility, learning ability, and communication ability was selected as the responsible family member to facilitate care of the pregnant woman. Generally, the mother or husband of the pregnant woman was selected as the responsible family member. The responsible family member was required to accompany the pregnant woman on health service visits throughout the pregnancy.

2.5.2. Intervention measures.

(1) Training and guidance: (i) one-to-one guidance was provided to the responsible family members, including information on psychological characteristics of pregnant women, common types of negative emotions, ways of emotional counseling, nursing measures for family members, communication skills with pregnant women and information about childbirth; (ii) a unified training was provided for pregnant women and responsible family members nightly, which included information about childbirth and parenting, and guided them to discuss childbirth and parenting care, so as to improve the trust of pregnant women in their family members’ ability to support them during childbirth. (iii) Delivery room visit: Pregnant women and their responsible family members were provided with a guided tour of the delivery room in advance, before the onset of labor, to explain the layout of the delivery room and the placement of corresponding equipment, to reduce the strangeness of the delivery room and facilitate their adaptation to the delivery room environment.

2.6. Indicators of the effect of the intervention

2.6.1. Psychological state.

The Symptom Checklist-90 (SCL-90) was used to evaluate the negative emotions of pregnant women before the start of the intervention and before delivery.[15] The scale consisted of 9 dimensions. The score of each dimension was measured on a scale of 1 to 5, and the score was positively correlated with the severity of negative emotions.

2.6.2. Vaginal delivery rate.

The mode of delivery (cesarean section, spontaneous vaginal delivery, or assisted vaginal delivery) was recorded.

2.6.3. Labor.

The duration of each labor stage was recorded.

2.7. Statistical analysis

SPSS 23.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY) was used for data analysis. Continuous data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and independent samples t-tests were used for comparisons between groups, and categorical data were expressed as percentage (%), and χ2 tests were used for comparisons between groups. P values < .05 were considered statistically significant.

4. Discussion

Some pregnant women are prone to fear, irritability, tension and other adverse emotions due to the pain caused by childbirth, which can have an adverse effect on the delivery progress and affect the health of the women and their newborns.[16,17] However, conventional obstetric care pays more attention to the physical health of mothers and newborns, and often ignores the psychological needs of pregnant women, leading to severe mood swings before delivery, which can affect the delivery outcome.[18,19] The SCL-90 questionnaire has a long history of use in the assessment of mental health problems and has good reliability.[15] In this study, we used SCL-90 scores to evaluate the negative emotions of pregnant women. The results show that the intervention significantly reduced negative emotions in pregnant women. In this study, the mothers or husbands of pregnant women in the observation group were designated as responsible family members and accompanied the pregnant women throughout the whole process, which improved the degree of cooperation of pregnant women with nursing measures, and the responsible family members provided pregnant women with emotional support and encouragement. Moreover, by improving the intensity of family support, pregnant women felt cared for by their families which relieved their mental stress. Most pregnant women show signs of severe tension, anxiety, and other emotions after entering the maternity room.[20–22] In this study, pregnant women improved their familiarity with the childbirth environment by visiting the maternity room and decorating the maternity room before delivery. This may have helped them maintain their emotional stability.

In this study the vaginal delivery rate of women in the observation group was significantly higher, and the duration of labor was significantly shorter than that in the control group, which suggests that the intervention, including family support, helped to improve the vaginal delivery rate.[23] During the early stage of the COVID-19 epidemic some pregnant women chose to undergo an cesarean section due to fear, tension, and other factors, although no indication for cesarean section was present.[24,25] In this study, through the guidance of pregnant women and their responsible family members, their understanding of the benefits of vaginal delivery was significantly improved, thus reducing the probability of cesarean section. The interventions increased the confidence of pregnant women in vaginal delivery. Improving the responsible family members understanding of the mental state of pregnant women by recording psychological diaries laid a foundation for effective intervention measures. In addition, the psychological state of the mother in late pregnancy can affect labor and delivery through the endocrine system. Therefore, the collaborative intervention helped women to have uncomplicated deliveries, and avoided the need for cesarean section delivery by shortening the labor time.

This study has several limitations. First, the small sample size is a major limitation. Second, the study was a single-center study; therefore, the generalizability may be limited. Third, the study was conducted in a Chinese population; therefore, the findings may not be generalizable to women in other countries.

5. Conclusion

This study demonstrated that during the early stage of the COVID-19 epidemic, the implementation of psychological care based on behavioral change theory among pregnant women significantly improved their prenatal psychological state, increased the likelihood of a vaginal delivery, and shortened the duration of labor, helping to avoid adverse pregnancy outcomes. Therefore, this intervention is worth promoting. Gynecologists and nurses should master the latest epidemiological characteristics and clinical manifestations of COVID-19, and take appropriate measures for prevention and control. Establishing and improving the triage process of emergency trauma in obstetrics and gynecology, optimizing the management of isolation wards, and the system of medical protection in emergency operating rooms can effectively reduce SARS-CoV-2 transmission in hospitals, reduce the risk of infection, and provide safe, efficient, and high-quality medical services for pregnant emergency patients during the epidemic period. This study provides a source of reference for obstetrics and gynecology care on the clinical front line.
