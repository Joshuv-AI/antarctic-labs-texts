Injectable alginate chitosan hydrogel as a promising bioengineered therapy for acute spinal cord injury
Authors: Fatemeh Saadinam, Mahmoud Azami, Mir Sepehr Pedram, Javad Sadeghinezhad, Massoumeh Jabbari Fakhr, Atena Salimi, Hossein Aminianfar, Mohammad Molazem, Mohammad Reza Mokhber Dezfouli, Mohammad Mehdi Dehghan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractDealing with spinal cord injuries presents problematic due to multiple secondary mechanisms. Beyond primary concerns like paralysis and disability, complications including urinary, gastrointestinal, cardiac, and respiratory disorders, along with substantial economic burdens may occur. Limited research focuses on modeling and treating contusion and compression injuries. Tissue engineering emerges as an innovative treatment, targeting lesion pathophysiology. This study was evaluated implanting injectable biomaterials into injury-induced cavity before glial scar formation, avoiding tissue incisions and minimizing further damage. The efficacy of injectable alginate/thiolated chitosan hydrogel was investigated for acute spinal cord injury induced by Vanický method in Wistar rats. Three days post-injury, hydrogel was administrated through microinjection after laminectomy. After 60 days, the hydrogel group demonstrated notable motor function enhancement compared to the control by the BBB locomotor test (P < 0.05). However, no statistically significant differences were observed in MRI assessment concerning lesion severity. Stereological and histopathological evaluations revealed a reduction in vacuole volume and the presence of axon profiles within the scaffold (P < 0.05), alongside reduced infiltration of inflammatory and Gitter cells in the hydrogel group, although the latter was not statistically significant compared to the control. Thiolated chitosan/ alginate hydrogel implantation may be regarded as a promising treatment to enhance motor function by restraining destructive processes post-acute spinal cord injury.Keywords: Chitosan, Alginates, Contusions, Spinal cord injuries, RatsSubject terms: Regeneration and repair in the nervous system, Spinal cord injury, Preclinical research

Introduction

Spinal cord injuries (SCI) are recognized as one of the major contributors to motor disability in human medicine and resulting in both temporary or permanent paralysis1–3. Most human SCIs caused by slow blows like motor vehicle accident, leading to a permanent and devastating loss of nerve function below the injury site4. Unfortunately, the adult mammalian spinal cord has limited regenerative capacity due to an unfavorable tissue environment for axon repair5. Beyond their physical consequences, SCIs pose notable socio-economic burdens, including the substantial costs of treatment, lifestyle adjustments, and decreased productivity6,7.

SCI has evolved into a complex condition with a poor prognosis, impacting multiple organs2,8. These secondary mechanisms initiated within minutes of the primary injury and persisting for weeks or months, encompass a combination of cellular, molecular, and biochemical phenomena2. These factors include nerve fiber damage, neuronal and oligodendrocyte death, demyelination, glial scar formation, inflammation, apoptosis and necrosis around the lesion9–13. Following SCI, astrocytes are activated within 1–2 days and undergo rapid proliferation (3–5 days post-injury) to form a dense limiting border, known as the glial scar (fibrotic scar core)13–16. These scar-forming astrocytes differ from reactive astrocytes in adjacent neural tissue14. Proliferation gradually subsides about 2 weeks after injury and chronic phase begins14. The glial scar plays both protective and inhibitory roles in SCI pathological process13,14. Various components, including astrocytes, microglia, oligodendrocyte progenitor cells, macrophages, fibroblasts, leptomeningeal cells, and Schwann cells, contribute to a dense cellular response by expressing inhibitory molecules such as keratan sulfate proteoglycans, chondroitin sulfate proteoglycans, semaphorins, ephrins and tenascins, filling the extracellular matrix (ECM) around the lesion site14–17. Due to the prevalence of cystic cavities forming a significant part of the lesion, physical support required for axonal growth17,18. Nevertheless, glial scar acts as a relative barrier to axonal growth13,17,18. If adequate regenerative factors are present, Axonal regeneration is possible in the inhibitory environment following acute SCI14,17.

Minimally invasive methods are preferred in treating SCI to reduce complications and avoid further damage19. Injectable hydrogels, as biomaterials, provide a unique advantage compared to prefabricated scaffolds by forming viscoelastic networks at the injury site without requiring cellular interference, tissue cutting, or molding9,18,20. They directly integrate with host tissue in situ, enhancing astrocyte/axon interactions and promoting improvement9,20.

Hydrogels offer a promising option due to their three-dimensional structure, porosity, biodegradability, biocompatibility, elasticity, ease of use, proper permeability, minimal invasiveness for deep access, complete lesion site filling, and promotion of angiogenesis21,22. This makes them suitable for reducing pain, scarring, and possibility of infection21,23. In a 2021 study, the structural properties of an injectable chitosan/thiolated alginate hydrogel were examined24. Based on the efficacy of these polymers in stimulating neural regeneration, the in vivo application of the injectable thiolated chitosan/alginate hydrogel for treating a contusion model was investigated relying on the laboratory results obtained in the prior study.

Results

Hydrogel preparation

In the prior 2021 study, the hydrogel demonstrated a porous microstructure conducive to cell culture, with a mean pore diameter of 21.89 ± 0.32 μm observed in SEM images (Fig. 1)24. Maximum swelling ratio occurred at 4 h, and the highest biodegradability rate was noted on the 21st day24. Importantly, the MTT test confirmed the hydrogel’s non-toxicity to L929 cells ensuring cell viability24.

Microbial evaluation and pH test

Following a 72-hour microbial culture, no microorganisms were observed to grow in any of the mediums. Also, the pH of the synthesized hydrogel remained within the range of 6.5-7.

During the frequency swing test under constant pressure, a regional linear viscoelastic behavior is evident in the initial sections of both the storage modulus (G’) and loss modulus (G”) diagrams, particularly following a decrease. Throughout the test, the storage modulus (G’) consistently exceeds the loss modulus (G”), and both moduli show slight increases. Viscosity decreases with a certain slope within the shear rate range of 0.04–0.4 (Fig. 2A). This viscosity remains constant in the subsequent range of 0.4–0.8, aligning with typical polymer characteristics. Nevertheless, beyond the 0.8 shear rate threshold, there is a noticeable increase in viscosity, indicating that higher applied pressure induces gelation within this range. Alamar Blue staining assay

The cell population demonstrates an increase over time in scaffold group (P < 0.05), suggesting the absence of hydrogel toxicity in cell growth (Fig. 2B).

FTIR analysis

The FTIR spectra revealed a relatively similar structural composition among chitosan, alginate and the hydrogel. The characteristic absorption bands of alginate are illustrated (Fig. 2C). In the first curve, distinctive peaks of alginate are evident: 3395 cm−1 for the O-H active group, 2924 cm−1 to (C-H), 1642 cm−1 related to the symmetric tension of COO−; and 1380 cm−1 for the asymmetric tension COO−. 1149 cm−1 peak corresponds to the C-O group, 1076 cm−1 and 1029 cm−1 are associated with the traction of the C-O-C group. In contrast, peaks at 873 cm−1, 811 cm−1 and 672 cm−1 arise from C-H vibrations of the pyrenoid group (alginate ring).

In the chitosan spectrum, the absorption peaks within the range of 3414 cm−1 are associated with (N-H) and (O-H), while those at 2933 cm−1 are attributed to (C-H). Additionally, peaks at 1085 cm−1 correspond to (N-C), 1380 cm−1 to (C-H) and 1152 cm−1 to (C-O-C). Notably, peaks at 1631 cm−1 and 1521 cm−1 peaks, as well as 1317 cm−1, indicate the amide I band, amide II band and thiol group, respectively.

Within the spectrum of the thiolated chitosan/alginate hydrogel, peaks were identified at 3417 cm−1, 1634 cm−1, 1505 cm−1, 1382 cm−1, 1066 cm−1, 1003 cm−1, 542 cm−1 and 405 cm−1. A comprehensive analysis of the spectra reveals that all the peaks corresponding to each component are present in the thiolated chitosan/alginate hydrogel. Notably, no new peaks have emerged in the hydrogel sample, indicating the preservation of the original substance characteristics.

Behavioral assessment

Three days post-SCI operation, all rats exhibited complete paralysis without signs of recovery. Hematuria was observed in most rats during the initial days after SCI, emphasizing urinary complications as a primary concern. Urinary retention emerged as a significant complication following SCI. After three days, both groups showed an identical BBB test locomotor score of 0–2, indicating consistent motor function impairment.

Throughout the 60-day study, the control group demonstrated no substantial improvement, maintaining a BBB score within the 0–2 range. In contrast, the scaffold group showed notable progress during the same period, achieving a score of 8–9. Statistical analyses were conducted using the Repeated measures ANOVA test. A significant difference existed in the BBB test scores between the control and scaffold groups (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3). Although the increase in BBB scores was significantly better in the hydrogel group, it does not reach a suggestive level of regeneration of supraspinal control of hind limb locomotion.

The Basso–Beattie–Bresnahan (BBB) locomotor rating scale was evaluated post-operation weekly. Repeated measures ANOVA test demonstrated a significant difference between the control group (n = 5) and the scaffold group (n = 10) (P < 0.05). Data shows in Median ± SD.

Discussion

In recent decades, the quest for an effective SCI treatment strategy has become crucial due to the subsequent challenges. The absence of spontaneous CNS repair, various degrees of paralysis, muscles atrophy, bedsores, infection, urinary/fecal incontinence, skin burns post-SCI, complicate medical interventions1,7,25. Axonal communication is critical for providing natural synaptic guidance in neural repair post-injury22,26.

The study aimed to employ an injectable scaffold as a regenerative stimulus, focusing on restricting cystic cavity and glial scar formation in acute SCI models to enhance repair. In the acute stage of SCI, the choice of hydrogel is due to its potential to seal the dura mater and aid anti-inflammatory drug release19. Conversely, fibers and channels may be more effective in later stages for neural regeneration19. In the 2021 study, thiolated chitosan/alginate hydrogel properties like swelling, porosity, cytotoxicity, and SEM structure, were examined24. It presents a convenient, rapid, and cost-effective preparation method, with superior injectability compared to other hydrogels24.

Recent studies emphasize combination therapies, incorporating growth factors or stem cells alongside scaffolds27. In a 2012 study, Matrigel and adipose tissue-derived mesenchymal cells were employed in a dog contusion-compression model using the Fogarty catheter method. The results revealed reduced SCI severity, improved motor recovery and BBB scores, credited to decreased inflammation and enhanced neuronal regeneration, leading to better outcomes than the control28. In 2013, Xue et al. employed acellular muscle with amniotic cells in a hemisected spinal cord, leading to increased axonal growth and improved locomotor status29. In 2014, acellular spinal cord with bone marrow stem cells improved BBB scores, cell differentiation, and reduced secondary tissue damage in a hemisected rat spinal cord30. In 2018, Yin et al. employed decellularized spinal cord with adipose tissue-derived stem cells in a rat hemisection model, reporting enhanced axonal regeneration and reduced glial scar31. In another study in 2018, injectable acellular nerve with Schwann cells treated spinal cord contusion model in rats, improving motor status and axonal growth32. Stem cell transplantation, a potential treatment altering traditional clinical approaches, faces challenges like stimulating tissue around the transplant site, leading to irregular stem cell growth. This hinders their capacity to make a proper axonal connection with the transected tissue or extend the regenerated axon. Furthermore, the adhesion of implanted cells to the substrate, influenced by cerebrospinal fluid circulation, presents another complexity10. Cell culture is time-consuming and costly. Researchers seek effective, commercially available treatments with easy storage and mass production. However, implanted cells have unique storage requirements. This study aimed to reduce secondary damage severity and accelerate regeneration in acute spinal cord injuries. The short golden time for treatment prevents obtaining autograft cells and their differentiation. Additionally, using an allograft source raises immune response stimulation challenges, eliminating cell culture.

Histopathological findings in similar studies showed reduced spinal cord injury severity, diminished glial scar formation, and enhanced axonal regeneration9,28–32. In this study, thiolated chitosan/alginate hydrogel partially filled injury-induced cavity, reducing vacuole volume in the scaffold group. It also prevented inflammatory and gitter cell infiltration into the spinal cord. Normal axonal sections in the scaffold group, in contrast to Wallerian degeneration observed in the control, demonstrate the efficacy of the hydrogel in promoting axonal regeneration. These findings align with improved locomotor scores in the scaffold group versus the control.

The quantitative histological evaluation is susceptible to potential bias, affecting the interpretation of spinal cord morphology33. Stereology, a morphometric method employing point or line measurements within a defined range, supplements qualitative histopathological data with quantitative assessment, though it remains underutilized33. Only the study by Khodabandeh et al. in 2021 employed stereological evaluation in rats treated with bone marrow-derived stem cells following spinal cord lesions, noting a significant reduction in gray matter volume in the treatment group than the control34. The findings closely paralleled the present study on gray matter volume. Stereology, assessing spinal cord volumes grossly without considering the histological structure and cell types, reported that the control group exhibited better volumes of white and gray matter. However, the scaffold group demonstrated improved recovery marked by normal axonal sections, milder inflammatory cell infiltration, and reduced glial scar volume, as confirmed by histopathology results. Consequently, these findings correlate with improved locomotor scores in the treatment group.

Unlike others, this study employs diagnostic imaging assessments like CT-scan and MRI. Various studies have established a definite relationship between the pattern of acute SCIs, as revealed in MRI images and the improvement of patients’ neurological conditions35. The optimal time for spinal cord diagnostic imaging is within 24–72 h post-injury35. MRI is the gold standard in neural tissue imaging, highly effective in evaluating acute spinal cord contusion and distinguishing between reversible and irreversible lesions8,35–37. Unlike most comparable studies, this research was employed CT scan images to determine the optimal filling location of the Fogarty balloon within the spinal canal. Furthermore, MRI images taken at two intervals showed increased lesion severity in the scaffold group based on two parameters, may be attributed to the filling role of the hydrogel within the contusion-induced cavity. It is expected that these parameters will diminish over a longer study period, aligning with histopathological results, the regenerative effect of the hydrogel, and the presence of normal axonal sections.

Thiolated chitosan/alginate hydrogel injection post-SCI moderately improved motor function and reduced secondary injury cavity. While the hydrogel did not completely prevent glial scar formation, the presence of normal axons and reduced Wallerian degeneration compared to the control group, along with less inflammatory cell infiltration into the cavity, indicates that the hydrogel supports axonal regeneration. In contrast, the additional injury caused by needle injection in the scaffold group, compared to the control, could be considered a significant factor contributing to the observed lack of expected clinical improvement from this hydrogel. Additionally, despite injection being one of the least invasive methods for scaffold implantation compared to other available methods, it is crucial to consider the potential damage induced by the needle in the sensitive spinal cord tissue.

Materials and methods

All methods in this study were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

Hydrogel preparation

The injectable thiolated chitosan/alginate hydrogel was produced following the 2021 study’s procedures24. Initially, chitosan was thiolated with Thioglycolic acid (TGA) and 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl) carbodiimide (EDC). Thiolated chitosan was dissolved in sterile distilled water at 2% (w/v). Simultaneously, alginate was dissolved in sterile distilled water at 1% (w/v). The two polymer solutions were then mixed in a 1:1 volume ratio. A solution of 30% beta-glycerol phosphate (βGP) and 30% calcium chloride (CaCl2) was added to the mixed solution to facilitate the crosslinking of chitosan and alginate. The analysis including SEM imaging, swelling/shrinkage ratio, biodegradability rate, and viability, was conducted following previously detailed methods24.

Microbial evaluation and pH test

Microbial evaluation and pH test: The hydrogel was prepared under sterile conditions. The hydrogel samples were applied to Blood Agar, MacConkey Agar, and Sabouraud Dextrose Agar plates using a microbial culture loop and incubated at 37 °C for 72 h.

Additionally, 10 cc of the hydrogel was prepared, and the pH was measured using a JENWAY 3320 pH meter.

Rheometric evaluation

The elastic and viscosity properties of hydrogel are evaluated through storage modulus (G’) and loss modulus (G”), respectively. Polymers typically exhibit decreasing viscosity as shear rate increases, which is attributed to the alignment of polymer chains and the subsequent reduction of entanglement. The rheological properties of thiolated chitosan/alginate hydrogel, functioning as a viscoelastic polymer, were determined using a programmable rheometer (Paar Anton 301 MCR Physica) under constant pressure conditions. Following hydrogel preparation, rheological measurements were immediately conducted at 37°C, spanning a frequency range from 0.1 and 10 Hz, with subsequent calculations of G’ and G”. The damping factor, reflecting elasticity, was determined by evaluating the ratio of loss modulus to storage modulus.

Alamar Blue staining assay

104 murine fibroblast L929 cell lines (Pasteur Institute, Tehran-Iran) were seeded into per well and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 24 h. Subsequently, 150 µl of hydrogel was added to each well. Cell counts were assessed on days 1, 3, 5 and 7. Four hours after adding Alamar Blue staining to each well, viable cells exhibited a color change from blue to purple and pink. Optical absorption was measured using a microplate reader (USA, BioTek) at wavelengths of 570 nm and 600 nm.

FTIR analysis

Infrared spectra of freeze-dried hydrogel samples were recorded from 400 to 4000 cm−1 with a resolution of 4 cm−1, using the Nicolet Thermo 670 Nexus FTIR spectrometer. The measurements were conducted at room temperature, and analyzed using Omnic software.

In vivo study

In compliance with the ARRIVE guidelines, this study involved 15 adult male Wistar rats (300 ± 30 g), which were commercially purchased from the Pasteur Institute of Iran.The research was conducted following the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals from the National Institutes of Health and was approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of Tehran. Additionally, the ARRIVE guidelines were considered to perform all experiments in this study. Anesthesia was induced using Medetomidine Hydrochloride (0.05 mg/kg) and ketamine (5 mg/kg). Acute SCI was induced via Vanický method38. Under aseptic conditions, an incision was made along the dorsal and lumbar regions, and laminotomy was performed between the 10th and 13th thoracic vertebrae. Guided by CT scan, a 2-French Fogarty embolectomy catheter was inserted into the dorsal epidural space. A 20 µl contrast-filled balloon was held for 5 min to cause SCI, followed by balloon deflation and catheter removal (Fig. 7). Muscles and skin were sutured conventionally.

(A) The distance between the laminotomy site (blue arrow) and the location of the Fogarty catheter balloon was determined through a CT scan, involving a laminectomy performed above the spinal cord lesion site (black arrow). (B) Subsequently, scaffold injection was carried out using a microinjection device.

Three days post-SCI, rats underwent anesthesia for laminectomy following the described method. The incision was made on the spinous processes, exposing the laminotomy site from the prior surgery. Utilizing CT scan data, the spinous process of the vertebra corresponding to the damaged spinal cord area was removed, and a laminectomy was performed. Rats were then divided into two groups: control (no treatment) and scaffold.

In scaffold group, 6 µl of injectable scaffold was microinjected into the affected area using a 27 Gauge needle over 1 min. The injury site was marked with nylon suture material, and standard suturing of muscles and skin was performed. Broad-spectrum antibiotics (Enrofloxacin 10 mg/kg) and analgesics (Tramadol 10 mg/kg) were administered subcutaneously once daily for 5 days. The bladders of paralyzed rats were manually emptied at least twice daily until they regained urinary control.

Behavioral assessment

Motor function improvement was evaluated using the Basso–Beattie–Bresnahan (BBB) locomotor rating scale, initially on the 3rd day post-operation and then weekly thereafter39. The BBB assessments were conducted by two evaluators in a blinded manner.

Imaging assessments

CT scan images were obtained immediately after inserting the Fogarty embolectomy catheter into the spinal canal to precisely assess the location and extent of the complication. The imaging was performed using the German-made Somatoma Siemens device.

A Siemens 3.0 T Prisma MRI scanner was employed for imaging three days post-SCI and the 60th day after surgery. Evaluation of edema, bleeding and cyst formation used T1w coronal, T2w (coronal, sagittal, axial), sagittal T2 STIR sequences. Cerebrospinal fluid attenuation and intramedullary hyperintensity were observed in T2w Heavily and T2w Sagittal sequences, respectively40,41. Their ratio to L2 vertebral length was measured. Statistical analysis was conducted using the independent samples t-test. These procedures were conducted at the Iranian National Brain Mapping Laboratory (NBLM).

Histopathological and stereological assessments

Sixty days post-SCI, rats were anesthetized, and nerve tissue fixation was performed using the Vital perfusion method, involving euthanasia followed by perfusion fixation through the heart with 4% phosphate-buffered formalin.

The spinal cord segments were isolated, and the dura mater and arachnoid membrane were opened dorsally. The spinal cord was then immersed in the same fixative for further fixation. Each sample was weighed, and its volume was calculated based on its weight42. The samples were processed routinely for histology and embedded in paraffin. For stereological estimation, 8–10 sections with a thickness of 5 μm were taken from each block using the systematic random sampling (SRS) procedure. The selected sections were stained with Hematoxylin and Eosin. Axonal counting within 5 high-power fields (HPF) and the percentage of cavity area were assessed for each slide. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 9 via the One- way ANOVA test.
