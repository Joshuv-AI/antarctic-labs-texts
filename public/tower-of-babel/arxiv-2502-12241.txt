Certification of quantum correlations and DIQKD at arbitrary distances through routed Bell tests
Pavel Sekatski, Jef Pauwels, Edwin Peter Lobo, Stefano Pironio, Nicolas Brunner

Abstract
Transmission loss represents a major obstacle to the device-independent certification of quantum correlations over long distances, limiting applications such as device-independent quantum key distribution (DIQKD). In this work, we investigate the recently proposed concept of routed Bell experiments, in which a particle sent to one side can be measured either near or far from the source. We prove that routed Bell tests involving only entangled qubits can certify quantum correlations even in the presence of arbitrary loss on the channel to the distant device. This is achieved by adapting concepts from self-testing and quantum steering to the routed Bell test framework. Finally, as a natural extension of our approach, we outline a DIQKD protocol that, in principle, is secure over arbitrary distances.



Device-independent (DI) protocols aim to certify quantum properties—such as entanglement or security—without making assumptions about the internal functioning of the measurement devices
[
1
]
. At the core of any DI claim lies a fundamental classical-quantum gap that can be established solely from the observed correlations. In addition, such claims rely on a specific causal structure—typically that of a Bell experiment—in which spatially separated parties perform local measurements on a shared quantum state. Within this framework, the classical-quantum gap manifests as a violation of a Bell inequality and is synonymous with the notion of quantum nonlocality
[
2
]
.

In practice, harnessing the power of quantum nonlocality comes with significant experimental challenges.
In particular, local detection efficiencies must exceed a specific threshold; otherwise, a local model might exploit the so-called “detection loophole” to explain the observed correlations
[
3
,
4
]
.

The detection efficiency required to violate a Bell inequality varies considerably with the experimental setup. While Bell tests involving many measurement settings and high-dimensional entangled states can, in principle, tolerate very low efficiencies
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
]
, practical implementations typically rely on low-dimensional systems such as qubits. In these scenarios, the efficiency thresholds remain relatively high—typically above 60%—even when loss is the only imperfection
[
2
]
. For applications such as device-independent quantum key distribution (DIQKD), the requirements are even more stringent, with standard protocols becoming insecure below 50% efficiency
[
12
,
13
]
.

Although recent experiments have achieved loophole-free Bell violations
[
14
,
15
]
and proof-of-principle demonstrations of DIQKD
[
16
,
17
]
, distributing nonlocal correlations over long distances remains a major challenge. Transmission losses—which effectively reduce the overall detection efficiency—increase exponentially with distance, imposing severe limitations even when local detectors operate at unit efficiency. This has motivated the exploration of heralded entanglement generation
[
18
,
19
,
20
,
21
]
, though such approaches remain out of reach with current technology.

Recently, an alternative approach to mitigating transmission loss in Bell experiments– coined “routed Bell tests”–was proposed
[
22
,
23
]
. In this scenario, illustrated in Fig.
1
, a standard bipartite Bell experiment is modified so that one of the particles (e.g., Bob’s) can be measured at two different locations: either near the source or at a distant site. Interestingly, these tests can tolerate more loss than standard Bell tests
[
23
]
, and can increase robustness to loss for DIQKD
[
24
,
25
]
. The setup is closely related to DI protocols with a “local Bell test”
[
26
]
.

In the present paper, we prove that routed Bell tests using entangled qubits can tolerate arbitrarily high transmission loss, enabling the distribution of genuine quantum correlations over an arbitrarily large distance. Our approach is analytical, drawing concepts from self-testing
[
27
,
28
]
and quantum steering
[
29
,
30
,
31
]
. In particular, we derive a tight family of steering inequalities for qubits, which may be of independent interest. As an extension of our analysis, we outline a DIQKD protocol for the routed Bell scenario, which can in principle achieve a non-zero key rate at arbitrary distances.

The routed Bell scenario.—

In a routed Bell test
[
22
,
23
]
, illustrated in Fig.
1
, a source prepares a bipartite quantum state
\rho_{AB}
, sending subsystem
A
to Alice –who is positioned close to the source – and subsystem
B
toward Bob. Upon receiving an input
x
, Alice performs a measurement described by a set of positive operator-valued measures (POVMs)
\{\text{A}_{a|x}\}_{x}
and obtains an outcome
a
. Bob’s particle is routed by a switch to one of two measurement stations, depending on an input
r\in\{S,L\}
. If
r={\rm S}
(for “short path” or SP), the system is measured at a nearby device
{\rm B}^{\rm S}
; if
r={\rm L}
(for “long path” or LP), it is measured at a distant device
{\rm B}^{\rm L}
. The measurements at SP and LP are described by POVMs
\{{\rm B}^{\rm S}_{b|y}\}_{y}
and
\{{\rm B}^{\rm L}_{b|y}\}_{y}
, respectively, where
y
is the input and
b
the output. These measurements may differ between the two locations. The correlations observed in the experiment are given by

p(a,b|x,y,r)=\tr(\rho_{AB}\A_{a|x} \otimes\B^r_{b|y})\,.

(1)

In the DI spirit, no assumptions are made about the internal workings of the source, the measurements, or the switch—all are treated as untrusted. The only physical assumptions are causality constraints enforcing the tensor product structure in Eq. (
1
), and the independence of the devices. For example, Alice’s measurement process is assumed to be independent of the inputs and outputs on Bob’s side (which includes the switch and both measurement stations), and vice versa.

In the routed Bell scenario, the fact that the measurement devices
{\rm A}
and
{\rm B}^{\rm S}
are located close to the source reduces losses and noise, making it easier to observe a Bell violation in the SP. Such a violation allows for the partial certification of key properties of the source and Alice’s measurement device. The central goal is then to certify genuine quantum correlations in the LP between
{\rm A}
and
{\rm B}^{\rm L}
, possibly leveraging the information provided by the SP violation.

Unlike standard Bell tests and traditional device-independent (DI) protocols, our aim is not to exclude a classical explanation for the entire experiment—this is already achieved via the Bell violation in the SP. Instead, we seek to rule out a broader class of hybrid quantum-classical models, in which the devices near the source may operate quantumly, while only classical information reaches
{\rm B}^{\rm L}
. Correlations of this kind are referred to as short-range quantum (SRQ), following Ref.
[
23
]
.

SRQ correlations can thus be reproduced by strategies where the quantum system traveling along the LP is measured before reaching
{\rm B}^{\rm L}
. More precisely, the process is modeled by a POVM
\{{{\rm E}_{\lambda}\}}_{\lambda}
acting on Bob’s quantum system, producing a classical variable
\lambda
. The distant device
{\rm B}^{\rm L}
then outputs
b
based on the input
y
and
\lambda
, via a purely classical post-processing function
p(b|y,\lambda)
. SRQ correlations therefore admit the following decomposition:

p(a,b|x,y,r)\!\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm SRQ}}{{=}}\!\begin{cases}\tr(\rho_{AB} \A_{a|x} \otimes\B^{\rm S}_{b|y} )&\!\!r\!=\!{\rm S},\\
\sum_{\lambda}p(b|y,\lambda)\tr(\rho_{AB} \A_{a|x} \otimes{\rm E}_\lambda)&\!\!r\!=\!{\rm L}.\end{cases}

(2)

Correlations that cannot be expressed in the above form are termed long-range quantum (LRQ). These require the distribution of entanglement along the LP and are essential for any application that relies on certifying quantum properties between distant parties
[
23
,
24
]
. Similar to nonlocal correlations in the standard Bell scenario, LRQ correlations can be detected by the violation of a “routed” Bell inequality
[
23
]
. We now introduce a family of inequalities that can be violated by a quantum model even in the presence of arbitrary losses along the LP.

A family of routed Bell inequalities.—

We consider a routed Bell scenario where A and
{\rm B}^{\rm S}
have two binary-outcome measurements:
a,x,b,y\in\{0,1\}
. The device
{\rm B}^{\rm L}
has ternary outcomes

b\in\{0,1,\varnothing\}
, where we will later identify the outcome
\varnothing
with a no-click. The input of
{\rm B}^{\rm L}
takes
n
values
y\in\{0,\ldots,n-1\}
, that we also label for convenience as the ‘angles’
\theta_{y}=y\frac{\pi}{n}
.

Our routed Bell inequality is based on the following three interconnected observable quantities: the SP CHSH expectation
\mathcal{S}
,

\mathcal{S}=\sum_{x,y=0,1}(-1)^{x\cdot y}\,\langle A_{x}B^{\rm S}_{y}\rangle\,,

(3)

the LP asymmetric Bell expectation

\mathcal{W}_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{y=0}^{n-1}\Big(\cos{\theta_y}\,\langle A_{0}B_{y}^{\rm L}\rangle+\sin\theta_{y}\,\langle A_{1}B_{y}^{\rm L}\rangle\Big),

(4)

and the average click probability of
{\rm B}^{\rm L}
’s detector

\mathcal{T}_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{y=0}^{n-1}\sum_{b=0,1}p(b|y,r={\rm L})\,.

(5)

In the above expressions, we introduced the observables
A_{x}:={\rm A}_{0|x}-{\rm A}_{1|x}
and
B_{y}^{r}:={\rm B}^{r}_{0|y}-{\rm B}^{r}_{1|y}
, defining the SP and LP correlators
\langle A_{x}B^{r}_{y}\rangle=\sum_{a,b=0,1}(-1)^{a+b}\,p(a,b|x,y,r)
.

As we will show, local correlations (in a standard Bell scenario where the SP test is ignored) satisfy the following non-linear Bell inequality
\mathcal{W}_{n}\leq\sqrt{2}\sin(\frac{\pi}{2}\T_n )/n\sin(\frac{\pi}{2 n})
.

In a routed Bell scenario, the SRQ bound on the LP quantity
\mathcal{W}_{n}
is a function of the SP CHSH violation
\mathcal{S}
, and is reduced relative to the local bound by a factor that depends on
\mathcal{S}
. This is formalized in the following result.

Result

(Routed Bell inequality)
.

For
\mathcal{S}\geq 2
and
\frac{\pi}{2}\mathcal{T}_{n}/\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\mathcal{T}_{n}\right)\geq\sqrt{2}\,\gamma(\mathcal{S})
,
SRQ correlations satisfy the routed (non-linear) Bell inequality

\mathcal{W}_{n}\leq\frac{\sqrt{2}\,\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\mathcal{T}_{n}\right)}{n\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2n}\right)}\gamma(\mathcal{S})\,,

(6)

where
\gamma(\mathcal{S})=\left(\mathcal{S}+\sqrt{8-\mathcal{S}^{2}}\right)/4
.

The SP factor
\gamma(\mathcal{S})
is equal to
1
when
\mathcal{S}
saturates the local bound
\mathcal{S}=2
and decreases to
\sqrt{2}/2\simeq 0.71
when
\mathcal{S}=2\sqrt{2}
.

We prove the above result in detail in Appendix
D
and sketch it below. In the Appendix, we also generalize Eq. (
6
) to the whole range of values
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{T}_{n}
. We also consider a second scenario where
{\rm B}^{\rm L}
has a continuous input
y\equiv\theta\in[0,\pi)
, which can be thought of as the limit
n\to\infty
of the discrete input case. We show that the above bound is tight in that limit.

Alternatively to Eq. (
6
), we also prove that SRQ correlations satisfy a family of linear inequalities of the form

\mathcal{W}_{n}+\mu\mathcal{T}_{n}+\nu\mathcal{S}\leq\xi
, useful for experimental tests. Such linear inequalities can be readily combined with standard tools for finite-statistics analysis of the experimental data, enabling the computation of p-values and confidence intervals on the average violation.

Sketch of the proof.—
The key idea underlying the proof is that the SP CHSH violation provides information about the measurements performed by Alice. For instance, when
\mathcal{S}=2\sqrt{2}
, Alice’s measurements can be self-tested as two-qubit anti-commuting Pauli measurements. Since A is characterized, the LP test can then be interpreted as a steering test in which
{\rm B}^{\rm L}
is untrusted. Our proof therefore combines the partial self-testing of Alice’s measurements from the SP with a steering inequality applied in the LP, treating
{\rm B}^{\rm L}
as the untrusted party.

We begin by discussing the steering part of the argument. For this, we assume that Alice performs two qubit measurements parameterized by an angle
\alpha
in the
X-Z
plane. That is, we assume that Alice’s observables
are of the specific form

A_{x}\equiv A_{x}^{\alpha}=\cos\alpha\frac{Z+X}{\sqrt{2}}+(-1)^{x}\sin\alpha\frac{Z-X}{\sqrt{2}}\,.

(7)

The correlations in the LP can then be written as
p(ab|xy,L)=\tr_{B}(\sigma_{b|y}A_{x}^{\alpha})
, where
\sigma_{b|y}
is the assemblage created by the distant Bob through his measurement. If the correlations are SRQ, then the assemblage can be written as
\sigma_{b|y}=\sum_{\lambda}p(b|y,\lambda)\rho_{\lambda}
, where
\rho_{\lambda}=\tr_{B}(\rho_{AB}\mathds{1}\otimes{\rm E}_{\lambda})
, i.e., the assemblage admits, in the language of steering, a LHS model.
Thus, any steering inequality involving the trusted observables
A_{x}^{\alpha}
is satisfied by the LP correlations for SRQ models.

We derive the following steering inequality in App.
B

\mathcal{W}_{n}^{\alpha}\leq\frac{\sqrt{2}\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\mathcal{T}_{n}\right)}{n\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2n}\right)}\gamma\left(\mathrm{s}_{\alpha}\right)\,,

(8)

where we introduced
\mathrm{s}_{\alpha}=2(\cos\alpha+\sin\alpha)
. That is, we will show that if the measurements of Alice are of the form
A_{x}^{\alpha}
, then the LP quantities
\mathcal{W}_{n}^{\alpha}
and
\mathcal{T}_{n}
satisfy Eq. (
8
) whenever
\sigma_{b|y}=\tr_{B}(\rho_{AB}\mathds{1}\otimes{\rm B}^{\rm L}_{b|y})
admits an LHS model.
For complementary measurements
(\alpha=\nicefrac{{\pi}}{{4}})
we derive a tight characterization of the LHS correlation set in terms of the quantities
(\mathcal{W}_{n}^{\nicefrac{{\pi}}{{4}}},\mathcal{T}_{n})
in App.
B
. These results are of independent interest and complements the steering inequalities of Ref.
[
31
]
.

We now relax the assumption on
A_{x}
and characterize the measurements of Alice based only on the SP CHSH violation. Since Alice performs two binary-outcome measurements, Jordan’s lemma
[
32
,
33
]
implies that her observables
A_{x}
and the shared state
\rho_{AB}
can be decomposed into qubit blocks as

A_{x}=\bigoplus_{i}A_{x}^{\alpha_{i}}\,,\quad\rho_{AB}=\bigoplus_{i}\mu_{i}\outerproduct{\psi_{i}}{\psi_{i}}\,,

(9)

where
\mu_{i}=\tr[\rho_{AB}\,\Pi^{i}_{A}\otimes\mathds{1}_{B}]
is the probability to find the state in the corresponding qubit block (see App.
C
).
Substituting Eq. (
9
) into Eq. (
3
), we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}=2\sum_{i}\mu_{i}\Big(

\displaystyle\cos\alpha_{i}\bra{\psi_i}\frac{Z+X}{\sqrt{2}}\otimes B^{\rm S}_{0}\ket{\psi_i}+

(10)

\displaystyle\sin\alpha_{i}\bra{\psi_i}\frac{Z-X}{\sqrt{2}}\otimes B^{\rm S}_{1}\ket{\psi_i}\Big)\leq\sum_{i}\mu_{i}\mathrm{s}_{\alpha_{i}}

where we used the fact that
\|A\otimes B\|\leq 1
for any pair of dichotomic observables
A
and
B
. Therefore, the distribution of the “measurement angle”
\alpha_{i}
is constrained by the observed CHSH score

\sum_{i}\mu_{i}\mathrm{s}_{\alpha_{i}}\geq\mathcal{S}\,.

(11)

Further, from the steering inequality Eq. (
8
), we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{n}=\sum_{i}\mu_{i}\mathcal{W}_{n}^{\alpha_{i}}\leq\frac{\sqrt{2}}{n\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2n}\right)}\,\sum_{i}\mu_{i}\,\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\mathcal{T}_{n}^{i}\right)\,\gamma(\mathrm{s}_{\alpha_{i}})\,.

(12)

As we show in the App.
D.3
, the function
F(\mathrm{s}_{\alpha},\mathcal{T}_{n}):=\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\mathcal{T}_{n}\right)\gamma(\mathrm{s}_{\alpha})
is equal to its concave envelope whenever
\frac{\pi}{2}\mathcal{T}_{n}/\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\mathcal{T}_{n}\right)\geq\sqrt{2}\gamma(\mathrm{s}_{\alpha})
. Therefore,

\displaystyle n\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2n}\right)\mathcal{W}_{n}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2}\,\sum_{i}\mu_{i}\,F(\mathrm{s}_{\alpha_{i}},\mathcal{T}_{n}^{i})

(13)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2}\,F\left(\sum_{i}\mu_{i}\,\mathrm{s}_{\alpha_{i}},\sum_{i}\mu_{i}\,\mathcal{T}_{n}^{i}\right)\,

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2}\,\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\mathcal{T}_{n}\right)\gamma\quantity(\sum_i \mu_i \sb_{\alpha_i})\,.

Since the function
\gamma(x)
is monotonically decreasing, we can bound
\gamma\left(\sum_{i}\mu_{i}\mathrm{s}_{\alpha_{i}}\right)\leq\gamma(\mathcal{S})
from Eq. (
11
), which yields Eq. (
6
).

We emphasize that the logic just presented provides a general method for promoting any family of steering inequalities—valid for Alice’s trusted measurements
A_{x}^{\alpha}
across all angles
\alpha
(such as Eq. (
8
))—into a routed Bell test. The formal mapping is provided in Lemma
3
in the appendix.

We now show that a simple two-qubit strategy can yield a violation of our routed Bell inequality for an arbitrary transmission
\eta
in the LP.
We consider a quantum implementation of the routed Bell experiment where the source prepares the two-qubit maximally entangled state
\ket{\phi^+}_{AB}=(\ket{00}+\ket{11})/\sqrt{2}
, and the devices
{\rm A}
and
{\rm B}^{\rm S}
perform the Pauli measurements
\{Z,X\}
and
\{\frac{Z\pm X}{\sqrt{2}}\}
, respectively, while the device
{\rm B}^{\rm L}
performs
n
measurements along the bases
\{Z\cos\theta_{y}+X\sin\theta_{y}\}_{y\in\{0,\ldots,n-1\}}
.
Non-detection events
\varnothing
, corresponding to the situation when a detector fails to click, are treated as a separate outcome by
{\rm B}^{\rm L}
and binned with one of the other two outcomes by
{\rm A}
and
{\rm B}^{\rm S}
.

The correlations quantities can always be expressed as

\mathcal{S}=2\sqrt{2}(1-\epsilon),\quad\mathcal{T}_{n}={\eta},\quad\mathcal{W}_{n}=\eta(1-\delta)

(14)

where
\eta
represents the total detection efficiency of Bob’s distant device
{\rm B}^{\rm L}
and
\epsilon
,
\delta
capture any other imperfections. Substituting these values into the routed Bell inequality (
6
) gives us the following condition satisfied by all SRQ correlations

\begin{multlined}\frac{\eta}{\sin(\frac{\pi}{2}\eta)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm SRQ}}{{\leq}}\frac{1-\epsilon+\sqrt{\epsilon(2-\epsilon)}}{1-\delta}\frac{1}{n\sin(\frac{\pi}{2 n})}.\end{multlined}

(15)

For small imperfections and large number of settings, i.e., for
\epsilon
,
\delta
, and
1/n
small, we can expand the right-hand side of the above inequality to obtain the following condition for demonstrating LRQ correlations with the qubit model:

\eta>\frac{1}{n}\sqrt{1+\frac{24}{\pi^{2}}n^{2}\left(\delta+\sqrt{2\epsilon}\right)}\,.

(16)

For ideal devices, only limited by the finite transmission
\eta
in the LP, i.e., for
\epsilon=\delta=0
, LRQ correlations can be witnessed as long as
\eta>\frac{1}{n}
, that is, for arbitrary low detection efficiencies provided that
{\rm B}^{\rm L}
has enough measurement inputs.

This shows that the routed Bell scenario offers a dramatic advantage in terms of robustness to loss compared to standard Bell tests. For comparison, we note that in a standard Bell test, the correlations
p(a,b|x,y,{\rm L})
observed by Alice and
{\rm B}^{\rm L}
in our example are local for
\eta\leq\frac{2}{\pi}\simeq 0.64
(see App.
A
for a detailed discussion). Additionally, it is worth noting that the required number of measurement settings
n>1/\eta
is known to be minimal
[
6
,
23
]
.

Inequality (
16
) remains violated even when the local devices are sufficiently good, though not perfect, i.e.,
\epsilon,\delta\ll 1
. To be concrete, consider a specific noise model, where all local detectors have limited efficiency
\eta_{d}
, the entanglement source has visibility
\nu
(white noise), the transmission efficiency in the SP is
\eta_{SP}
, and the transmission efficiency in the LP is
\eta_{LP}
. For this model, straightforward algebra gives the values

\begin{split}\eta&=\eta_{LP}\eta_{d},\quad\delta=1-\eta_{d}\nu\,\quad\\
\epsilon&=1-\eta_{d}^{2}\eta_{SP}\,\nu-\frac{(1-\eta_{d})^{2}(1-\eta_{SP})}{\sqrt{2}}\,.\end{split}

Plugging these values into Eq. (
16
), it is easy to see that for any transmission efficiency
\eta_{LP}
in the LP, LRQ correlations can be witnessed provided the imperfections of the local devices are sufficiently small. For instance, taking
\eta_{d}=\nu=\eta_{SP}=1-\zeta
, we find that the condition (
16
) is equivalent to

\eta_{LP}>\frac{1}{n}(1+\frac{24}{\pi^{2}}n^{2}\sqrt{2\zeta})\,.

(17)

to smallest order in
\zeta
. The LP transmission efficiency
\eta_{LP}
can thus be arbitrarily small provided
n
is large enough and the local imperfections are of order
\zeta<1/n^{4}
.

Possibility of DIQKD with arbitrary loss.—

LRQ correlations are necessary for the security of DIQKD in routed setups
[
24
]
. Having demonstrated that LRQ correlations can be certified over arbitrary distances, a natural question is whether such correlations also enable DIQKD. This is far from obvious since the requirements on the detection efficiency for DIQKD are typically more stringent than for certifying the distribution of entanglement
[
12
]
. Furthemore, the correlation scenario we introduced involves two measurement bases on Alice’s side. To maximize correlations for key generation, it would be natural to pair these two measurements with two corresponding measurements among Bob’s
n
possible settings. However, it is known that no DIQKD protocol based solely on two measurement settings for key generation can achieve security when the detection efficiency falls below
1/3
.

Nevertheless, our correlation scenario can be generalized by introducing multiple measurement settings for Alice and the SP device
{\rm B}^{\rm S}
. Specifically, each party can implement
n
measurement settings, employing the chained Bell inequality
[
34
]
rather than the CHSH inequality for self-testing. The underlying logic is analogous to the two-measurement case we have studied: the SP test using the chained Bell inequality certifies the state and Alice’s measurements, which can then be leveraged for certifying LRQ correlations involving Bob’s distant device.

Specifically, consider the setup of Fig.
1
, but now each party (Alice,
{\rm B}^{\rm S}
and
{\rm B}^{\rm L}
) has
n
possible binary measurements, described by the observables

A_{x}=\cos(\frac{ x \pi}{n})Z+\sin(\frac{ x \pi}{n})X

(18)

with
x=0,\dots,n-1
for Alice, and similarly for
{\rm B}^{\rm S}
and
{\rm B}^{\rm L}
.
As before, the source produces a maximally entangled two-qubit state
\ket{\Phi^+}
. Under these conditions, the SP correlations achieve the maximal violation of the
n
-input chained Bell inequality
[
34
]
. Using known self-testing results
[
35
]
, we certify that the state is
\ket{\Phi^+}
and that Alice’s observables are of the form (
18
). Consequently, conditioned on Alice’s measurement outcome
a
for setting
x
, the state transmitted to
{\rm B}^{\rm L}
is the pure qubit state

\rho_{a|x}=\frac{1}{2}\Bigg(\mathds{1}+(-1)^{a}\Big(\cos\left(\frac{x\pi}{n}\right)Z+\sin\left(\frac{x\pi}{n}\right)X\Big)\Bigg).

(19)

Hence, due to the test performed in the SP, the combination of the source and Alice’s measurement device can be viewed as preparing states of the form (
19
), which are then sent along the LP to
{\rm B}^{\rm L}
. The LP correlations now correspond to a prepare-and-measure scenario in which
{\rm B}^{\rm L}
performs qubit measurements of the form
B_{y}^{\rm L}=\cos(y \pi/n)Z+\sin(y \pi/n)X
. This recovers precisely the set-up of the receiver-device-independent (RDI) QKD protocol introduced in Ref.
[
36
,
37
]
. The parties can then securely extract a secret key based on Bob’s input, and this is possible for any
\eta>1/n
. We have thus introduced a proof-of-principle routed DIQKD protocol capable of producing a non-zero key rate at arbitrarily low transmission, provided that the number of inputs
n
is chosen sufficiently large.

Security is established here only in the ideal case, assuming perfect violation of the chained Bell inequality and exact self-testing. The first step towards studying the robustness of such a DIQKD protocol under realistic conditions and imperfections would be to analyze the robustness of the underlying chained Bell correlations, following the approach we presented here for the CHSH-based SP test.

Conclusion.—
We have shown that quantum correlations can be certified at arbitrary transmission efficiencies in a routed Bell scenario. This can be achieved with a two-qubit source and without complicated heralding setups . A key insight underlying this result is that the short-path test functions as a (partial) self-test of Alice’s device, allowing us to derive an explicit routed Bell inequality which imposes strong constraints on any potential classical simulation of the long-path correlations. Additionally, we presented a proof-of-principle DIQKD protocol tailored to the routed Bell test scenario which can tolerate arbitrary loss. These results demonstrate the clear potential of the routed Bell scenario for long-distance quantum correlations and its applications.

In the future, it would be interesting to characterize the admissible trade-off between noise and loss in routed Bell tests, and develop efficient protocols for demonstrating nonlocal quantum correlations and their applications. In particular, we believe that considering the full statistics of the experiments on the long path could improve the noise versus loss threshold.

Appendix A
Local hidden variable models for lossy singlet correlations

Here, we review some results on the critical detection efficiency for standard Bell nonlocality for the specific scenarios considered in this work, i.e., focusing on cases with asymmetric losses, where Alice’s device operates at unit efficiency and Bob’s has finite efficiency,
\eta
. This corresponds to a situation where transmission is the only source of loss, and Alice is close to the source while Bob is far away.

As the local dimension
d
of the entangled state increases, nonlocality can be observed at arbitrarily low transmission rates, specifically
\eta\geq 1/d

using a Bell inequality with
d
settings
[
7
]
. However, this result is largely impractical due to the increase in settings and Hilbert space dimension as
\eta
decreases. Limiting the dimension or number of measurements thus provides more realistic insights.

General bounds on the detection efficiency can also be established based solely on the number of measurement settings. Specifically, when Bob’s settings
n_{B}
satisfy
n_{B}\leq 1/\eta
, the correlations become local and can be reproduced by a local hidden-variable (LHV) model via data rejection
[
3
,
4
]
. In the scenarios considered in the main text—where Alice has two settings with binary outcomes and Bob has any number of settings with ternary outcomes (the third outcome corresponding to a no-click event)—all facets of the local polytope correspond to relabelings of the CHSH inequality
[
38
]
. Therefore, demonstrating nonlocality in such cases reduces to violating a CHSH inequality. Consequently, all correlations in these scenarios become local when
\eta\leq\eta_{CHSH}=1/2

[
39
,
40
]
.

Another interesting case is when the number of settings is unrestricted but we fix the state to be a maximally entangled two-qubit state. For projective measurements, an LHV model can simulate correlations when
\eta\leq\eta_{PVM}=1/2

[
41
]
. Below, we make two small extensions to this result: (1) When Alice’s measurements are restricted to a plane, an LHV model can simulate correlations up to
\eta_{RPVM}=2/\pi
; (2) for arbitrary POVMs by Bob, critical detection efficiency further decreases to
\eta_{POVM}=1/4
and
\eta_{RPOVM}=1/\pi
, respectively. These results are summarized in the table below.

Bob measures all PVMs

Bob measures all POVMs

Alice measures all PVMs

\eta_{*}^{PVM}=\frac{1}{2}

\eta_{*}^{PVM}=\frac{1}{4}

Alice measures all PVMs in a plane

\eta_{*}^{RPVM}=\frac{2}{\pi}

\eta_{*}^{RPOVM}=\frac{1}{\pi}

Table 1:
The critical detection efficiencies (for Bob) below which the correlations obtained with a maximally entangled two-qubit state and the measurements specified in the table can be reproduced by an LHV model.

Beyond the case of maximally entangled two-qubit states with projective measurements on Alice’s side, we are unaware of any results demonstrating the locality of such lossy quantum correlations. It is worth noting that these correlations remain extremal in the set of probabilities, making it particularly challenging to construct LHV models. Specifically, convex decomposition methods typically used for white noise
[
42
]
cannot be applied here.

A.1
LHV models for the singlet

We start by summarizing the LHV model constructed in Ref.
[
41
]
,before extending it to include (i) POVMs on Bob’s side and (ii) a restriction to measurements in the plane.

The LHV model of Gisin-Gisin –

First, we recall the model of Ref.
[
41
]
, which considers the correlations obtained from the single state
\ket{\Psi^-}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{01}-\ket{10})
, with Alice and Bob performing measurements with inputs given by vectors
\bm{x},\bm{y}
on the Bloch sphere. Alice’s measurements are ideal PVMs, with output
a=\pm 1
, while Bob’s have a finite efficiency
\eta
, with output
b=+1,-1,\varnothing
. The correlations observed in this scenario are given by

\displaystyle p(a,b|\bm{x},\bm{y})=\bra{\Psi^-}\text{A}_{a|\bm{x}}\otimes\text{B}_{b|\bm{y}}\ket{\Psi^-}

(20)

where
{\rm A}_{a|\bm{x}}=\frac{1}{2}(\mathds{1}+aA_{\bm{x}})
,
{\rm B}_{b|\bm{y}}=\begin{cases}\frac{\eta}{2}(\mathds{1}+bB_{\bm{y}})&b=\pm 1\\
(1-\eta)\mathds{1}&b=\varnothing\end{cases}
and we introduced
A_{\bm{x}}=\bm{x}\cdot\bm{\sigma}
and similarly for Bob.

Note that the choice of the maximally entangled states plays no role, as they are all related by a local unitary transformation, which can be absorbed as a relabeling of the measurement settings (since we consider an invariant set of possible measurements). In the model of
[
41
]
Alice and Bob share a random anti-parallel pair of vectors on the sphere
(\bm{\lambda},\bm{\lambda}^{\prime}=-\bm{\lambda})
. Alice response function is deterministic

a(\bm{\lambda},\bm{x})={\rm sign}\,\bm{x}\cdot\bm{\lambda}.

(22)

Bob’s response function
p(b|\bm{\lambda},\bm{y})
is slightly more complicated, he outputs no-click with probability

p(\varnothing|\bm{\lambda}^{\prime},\bm{y})=1-|\bm{y}\cdot\bm{\lambda}^{\prime}|

(23)

and otherwise outputs the sign
b={\rm sign}\,\bm{y}\cdot\bm{\lambda}^{\prime}
.
Straightforward algebra allows one to verify
[
41
]
that this response function reproduces the singlet correlations
p(a,b|\bm{x},\bm{y})
for
\eta=\eta_{*}^{PVM}=\frac{1}{2}
. It is straightforward to adjust the model for any lower value of transmission.

Modification of the Gisin-Gisin model to include POVMs on Bob’s side–

We now show how the above model can be modified to also simulate lossy POVM measurements on Bob’s side. As the first step, note that by relabeling
b=1\to b=0
and merging
b=-1,\varnothing\to b=\varnothing
the above model can be used to simulate any two-outcome POVM with
\eta=\frac{1}{2}

{\rm B}_{b|\bm{y}}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}\outerproduct{\bm y}{\bm y}&b=0\\
\mathds{1}-\frac{1}{2}\outerproduct{\bm y}{\bm y}&b=\varnothing,\end{cases}

(24)

where
\outerproduct{\bm y}{\bm y}=\frac{1}{2}(\mathds{1}+\bm{y}\cdot\bm{\sigma})=\frac{1}{2}(\mathds{1}+B_{\bm{y}}).

Now let us consider any extremal POVMs on Bob’s side, which in the ideal case reads
\{B^{\prime}_{b}=\alpha_{b}\outerproduct{\bm y_b}{\bm y_b}\}
with
\sum_{b=0}^{n-1}\alpha_{b}=2
and
\sum_{b}\alpha_{b}\,{\bm{y}}_{b}=0
, and has
2\leq n\leq 4
outcomes
[
43
]
. After adding losses of transmission
\eta^{\prime}
before this measurement (or lowering its efficiency to
\eta^{\prime}
) the POVM becomes

{\rm B}_{b}=\begin{cases}\eta^{\prime}\,\alpha_{b}\outerproduct{\bm y_b}{\bm y_b}&0\leq b\leq n-1\\
(1-\eta^{\prime})\mathds{1}&b=\varnothing\end{cases}.

(25)

For
\eta^{\prime}=\frac{1}{4}
this POVM can be decomposed as a convex mixture of
n
two-outcome POVMs, labeled by
b^{\prime}\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}
,

{\rm B}_{b|b^{\prime}}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}\outerproduct{\bm y_b}{\bm y_b}&b=b^{\prime}\\
\mathds{1}-\frac{1}{2}\outerproduct{\bm y_b}{\bm y_b}&b=\varnothing\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(26)

Indeed for
p_{b}^{\prime}=\frac{\alpha_{b^{\prime}}}{2}
(with
\sum p_{b^{\prime}}=1
) one verifies that

{\rm B}_{b}=\sum_{b^{\prime}}p_{b^{\prime}}{\rm B}_{b|b^{\prime}}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{4}\,\alpha_{b}\outerproduct{\bm y_b}{\bm y_b}&0\leq b\leq n-1\\
(1-\frac{1}{4})\mathds{1}&b=\varnothing.\end{cases}

(27)

Hence, for
\eta\leq\eta_{*}^{POVM}=\frac{1}{4}
the correlations
p(a,b|\bm{x},\{{\rm B}_{b}\})
obtained with a maximally entangled states projective measurements of Alice and any lossy measurement of Bob admit a LHV representation.

A LHV model for correlations in a plane. –

Now let us restrict the set of possible measurements of Alice to real projective measurements, that is we now have
{\rm A}_{a|\varphi}=\frac{1}{2}(\mathds{1}+aA_{\varphi})
with

A_{\varphi}=\cos\varphi\,Z+\sin\varphi\,X.

(28)

We will also change the state to
\ket{\Phi^+}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{00}+\ket{11})
.
Since
A_{\varphi}
and the shared
\Phi^{+}
are real (in the computational basis) we find

p(a,b|\bm{x},\bm{y})=\bra{\Phi^+}\text{A}_{a|\bm{x}}\otimes\text{B}_{b|\bm{y}}\ket{\Phi^+}=\bra{\Phi^+}\text{A}_{a|\bm{x}}\otimes\text{B}_{b|\bm{y}}^{*}\ket{\Phi^+}=\bra{\Phi^+}\text{A}_{a|\bm{x}}\otimes\frac{\text{B}_{b|\bm{y}}+\text{B}_{b|\bm{y}}^{*}}{2}\ket{\Phi^+}.

i.e. the only contribution to the correlations comes from the real part
{\rm Re}[\text{B}_{b|\bm{y}}]=\frac{\text{B}_{b|\bm{y}}+\text{B}_{b|\bm{y}}^{*}}{2}
of Bob’s measurement operators. We can thus limit his measurements to be real without loss of generality.

First, let us assume that they are also projective
{\rm B}_{b|\bm{y}}=\frac{1}{2}(\mathds{1}+b\bm{y}\cdot\bm{\sigma})
. The real part of such PVM

{\rm Re}[\text{B}_{b|\bm{y}}]
is a convex combination of projective measurements in the plane

{\rm B}_{b|y}=\frac{1}{2}(\mathds{1}+bB_{y})
with
B_{y}=\cos\theta\,Z+\sin\theta\,X
. Hence, it is sufficient to consider Bob measuring
{\rm B}_{b|\theta}
. The correlations are then given by

\displaystyle p(a,b|\varphi,\theta)

\displaystyle=\eta\bra{\Phi^+}\frac{1}{4}\left(\mathds{1}+aA_{\varphi}+bB_{\theta}+abA_{\varphi}\otimes B_{\theta}\right)\ket{\Phi^+}

(29)

\displaystyle=\frac{\eta}{4}\left(1+a\,b\cos(\varphi-\theta)\right).

(30)

We now consider the following LHV model. Let Alice and Bob share a random vector
\bm{\lambda}=\binom{\cos\zeta}{\sin\zeta}
on the circle
\zeta\in[0,2\pi]
. As before Alice’s response function is deterministic

a(\bm{\lambda},\bm{x})=a(\zeta,\varphi)={\rm sign}\,\bm{x}\cdot\bm{\lambda}=\text{sign}\cos(\zeta-\varphi),

(31)

which gives the right marginals
p(a|\varphi)=\frac{1}{2}
.
Bob’s response function is similar as before but with a slightly different
dependence, he outputs no-click with the probability

p(\varnothing|\zeta,\theta)=1-|\cos(\zeta-\theta)|,

(32)

and otherwise outputs
b={\rm sign}\,\cos(\zeta-\theta)
. For the overall no-click probability we find

\eta_{*}^{RPVM}=p(b\neq\varnothing|\theta)=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\differential\zeta}{2\pi}|\cos(\zeta-\theta)|=\frac{2}{\pi}.

(33)

To see that the full correlations are correct, we introduce the indicator function

\chi(\alpha)=\begin{cases}1&\cos(\alpha)\geq 0\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases},

(34)

and
compute

\displaystyle p(a=1,b=1|\varphi,\theta)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{\differential\zeta}{2\pi}|\cos(\zeta-\theta)|\chi(\zeta-\varphi)\chi(\zeta-\theta)

(35)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{\differential\zeta}{2\pi}|\cos(\zeta)|\chi(\zeta+\theta-\varphi)\chi(\zeta)

(36)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\frac{\differential\zeta}{2\pi}\cos(\zeta)\chi(\zeta+\theta-\varphi)

(37)

\displaystyle=\frac{1+\cos(\theta-\varphi)}{2\pi}=\eta_{*}^{RPVM}\frac{1+\cos(\theta-\varphi)}{4}.

(38)

This is indeed the same value as in Eq. (
29
). The computation for the remaining probabilities can be done similarly, leading to the conclusion that the LHV model simulates the lossy correlations obtained with a maximally entangled state, Alice performing projective measurements restricted to a plane, and Bob performing any projective measurements with efficiency
\eta\leq\eta_{*}^{RPVM}=\frac{2}{\pi}.

Finally, by replicating the discussion of Section.
A.1
it is straightforward to show that the model can be modified to work when Bob performs any POVM with efficiency
\eta\leq\eta_{*}^{RPOVM}=\frac{1}{\pi}
.

Proof of the inequality (
8

Lemma 1

(A family of steering inequalities)
.

All correlations
p(a,b|x,y)
unsteerable for Alice’s trusted measurement opertors
A_{x}^{\alpha}=\cos\alpha\frac{Z+X}{\sqrt{2}}+(-1)^{x}\sin\alpha\frac{Z-X}{\sqrt{2}}
, i.e., admitting a LHS model

\displaystyle p(a,b|x,y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm LHS}}{{=}}\sum_{\lambda}\tr\big( \A_{a|x}^\alpha\, \rho_\lambda\big)p(b|y,\lambda)\quad\text{with}\quad\rho_{\lambda}\succeq 0,\,\tr\sum_{\lambda}\rho_{\lambda}=1

(39)

and
{\rm A}_{a|x}^{\alpha}=\frac{1}{2}(\mathds{1}+(-1)^{a}A_{x}^{\alpha})
, satisfy the steering inequality

n\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2n}\right)\mathcal{W}_{n}^{\alpha}\leq\sqrt{2}\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\mathcal{T}_{n}\right)\gamma\left(\mathrm{s}_{\alpha}\right)

(40)

where
{\rm s_{\alpha}}=2(\cos(\alpha)+\sin(\alpha))
,
\gamma({\rm s}_{\alpha})=\left(\mathcal{S}+\sqrt{8-\mathcal{S}^{2}}\right)/4
, and

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{n}^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{y=0}^{n-1}\sum_{a,b=0}^{1}(-1)^{a+b}\Big(\cos{\theta_y}\,p(a,b|0,y)+\sin\theta_{y}\,p(a,b|1,y)\Big),

(41)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{y=0}^{n-1}\sum_{b=0,1}p(b|y)\,.

(42)

Proof.

We begin by rewriting the correlators for the LHS model as

\displaystyle\sum_{a,b=0}^{1}(-1)^{a+b}p(a,b|x,y)=\tr\big(A^\alpha_x \sum_\lambda\rho_\lambda(p(0|y,\lambda) -p(0|y,\lambda) )\big)=\tr\big(A_x^\alpha(\rho_{0|y}- \rho_{1|y})\big),

(43)

where
\rho_{b|y}=\sum_{\lambda}p(b|y,\lambda)\rho_{\lambda}
.
This lets us write
\mathcal{W}_{n}^{\alpha}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{n}^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{y=0}^{n-1}\tr\Big( (\cos\theta_y A_0^\alpha+ \sin\theta_y A_1^\alpha) (\rho_{0|y}- \rho_{1|y})\Big)

(44)

\displaystyle=\frac{\cos\alpha+\sin\alpha}{\sqrt{2}}\,\mathsf{W}_{n}+\frac{\cos\alpha-\sin\alpha}{\sqrt{2}}\,\overline{\mathsf{W}}_{n}\,

(45)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{s}_{\alpha}}{2\sqrt{2}}\,\mathsf{W}_{n}+\frac{\sqrt{8-\mathrm{s}_{\alpha}^{2}}}{2\sqrt{2}}\,\overline{\mathsf{W}}_{n}\,,

(46)

where

\displaystyle\mathsf{W}_{n}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{y=0}^{n-1}\tr[(Z\cos\theta_{y}+X\sin\theta_{y})(\rho_{0|y}-\rho_{1|y})]\,,

(47)

\displaystyle\overline{\mathsf{W}}_{n}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{y=0}^{n-1}\tr[(Z\sin\theta_{y}+X\cos\theta_{y})(\rho_{0|y}-\rho_{1|y})]\,.

(48)

Similarly,
\mathcal{T}_{n}
can be written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{n}=\mathsf{T}_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{y=0}^{n-1}\tr[(\rho_{0|y}+\rho_{1|y})]\,.

(49)

We will show in Appendix
B.2
that for any assemblage
\{\rho_{b|y}\}_{b,y}
that admits a LHS model (
\rho_{b|y}=\sum_{\lambda}p(b|y,\lambda)\rho_{\lambda}
), the following inequalities hold:

\mathsf{W}_{n}\leq\frac{\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\mathsf{T}_{n}\right)}{n\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2n}\right)}\quad\text{and}\quad\overline{\mathsf{W}}_{n}\leq\frac{\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\mathsf{T}_{n}\right)}{n\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2n}\right)}\,.

(50)

Substituting the above inequalities into (
46
) gives us the desired result in Eq. (
8
).

∎

B.2
Steering inequality with discrete input
\theta_{y}

In this section we consider the characterization of the set of unsteerable correlations (LHS) in terms of the following discrete steering observables

\displaystyle\mathsf{W}_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{y=0}^{n-1}\mathsf{w}(\theta_{y})

\displaystyle\qquad\text{with}\qquad\mathsf{w}(\theta)=\tr[(\cos(\theta)\,Z+\sin(\theta)\,X)(\rho_{0|\theta}-\rho_{1|\theta})]

(51)

\displaystyle\mathsf{T}_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{y=0}^{n-1}\mathsf{t}(\theta_{y})

\displaystyle\qquad\text{with}\qquad\mathsf{t}(\theta)=\tr(\rho_{0|\theta}+\rho_{1|\theta}),

(52)

where Bob’s input is discrete and labeled by the angles
\theta_{y}=y\frac{\pi}{n}
for
y\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}
.
We now prove the following result.

Lemma 2

(LHS set for discrete setting)
.

The set of values of
(\mathsf{T}_{n},\mathsf{W}_{n})
in Eq. (
52
,
51
) attainable by LHS models is the polytope with vertices (extremal points)
\left(\widehat{\mathsf{T}}_{k|n},\pm\widehat{\mathsf{W}}_{k|n}\right)
for
k=0,\dots,n
and

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathsf{T}}_{k|n}

\displaystyle=\frac{k}{n}

(53)

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathsf{W}}_{k|n}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\frac{k}{n}\right)}{n\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2n}\right)}.

(54)

Proof.

We want to find all pairs of values
(\mathsf{T}_{n},\mathsf{W}_{n})
compatible with a LHS model. Recall that, since
\mathsf{t}(\theta)
and
\mathsf{w}(\theta)
only depend on the real part of the stats
\rho_{b|\theta}
, one can assume without loss of generality that the hidden states are sampled from
\{\mu_{\zeta}\Psi_{\zeta}\}
where all
\Psi_{\zeta}
in Eq. (
80
) are pure and real (see App.
B
for the full argument).

Let us fix a specific hidden state
\Psi_{\zeta}=\frac{1}{2}(\mathds{1}+\cos(\zeta)Z+\sin(\zeta)X)
and consider the values
(\mathsf{T}_{n}^{(\zeta)},\mathsf{W}_{n}^{(\zeta)})
that can be attained by varying the response function
p(b|\theta_{y},\zeta)
. As before we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{t}^{(\zeta)}(\theta_{y})

\displaystyle=p(0|\theta_{y},\zeta)+p(1|\theta_{y},\zeta)

(55)

\displaystyle\mathsf{w}^{(\zeta)}(\theta_{y})

\displaystyle=\big(p(0|\theta_{y},\zeta)-p(1|\theta_{y},\zeta)\big)\cos(\theta_y-\zeta),

(56)

leading to the bounds

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\mathsf{t}^{(\zeta)}(\theta_{y})\leq 1

(57)

\displaystyle|\mathsf{w}^{(\zeta)}(\theta_{y})|

\displaystyle\leq|\cos(\theta_y-\zeta)|\,\mathsf{t}^{(\zeta)}(\theta_{y}),

(58)

that can be saturated by the right choice of the response funciton
p(b|\theta_{y},\zeta)
. For the average quantity
\mathsf{W}_{n}^{(\zeta)}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{y=0}^{n-1}\mathsf{w}^{(\zeta)}(\theta_{y})
this gives the bound

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{y=0}^{n-1}|\cos(\theta_y-\zeta)|\,\mathsf{t}^{(\zeta)}(\theta_{y})\leq\mathsf{W}_{n}^{(\zeta)}\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{y=0}^{n-1}|\cos(\theta_y-\zeta)|\,\mathsf{t}^{(\zeta)}(\theta_{y})

(59)

which is also saturable. The set containing the attainable values
(\mathsf{T}_{n}^{(\zeta)},\mathsf{W}_{n}^{(\zeta)})
is thus described by the linear constraints

\displaystyle\mathsf{T}_{n}^{(\zeta)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{y=0}^{n-1}\mathsf{t}^{(\zeta)}(\theta_{y})

(60)

\displaystyle|\mathsf{W}_{n}^{(\zeta)}|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{y=0}^{n-1}|\cos(\theta_y-\zeta)|\,\mathsf{t}^{(\zeta)}(\theta_{y})

(61)

\displaystyle 0\leq\,

\displaystyle\mathsf{t}^{(\zeta)}(\theta_{y})\,\leq 1\qquad\forall y,

(62)

and is a polytope, whose vertices are deterministic strategies (each
\mathsf{t}^{(\zeta)}(\theta_{y})
is 0 or 1). However, not all deterministic strategies are vertices (some are inside the polytope), and we want to find those which are.

For all vertices the value of
\mathsf{T}_{n}^{(\zeta)}
is of the form
\widehat{\mathsf{T}}_{k|n}^{(\zeta)}=\frac{k}{n}
for
k=0,\dots n
(where
k
is the number of
\mathsf{t}^{(\zeta)}(\theta_{y})=1
), and it remains to determine the corresponding extremal values of
\widehat{\mathsf{W}}_{k|n}^{(\zeta)}
. We find

|\widehat{\mathsf{W}}_{k|n}^{(\zeta)}|=\max_{\sum_{y}\mathsf{t}^{(\zeta)}(\theta_{y})=k}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{y=0}^{n-1}|\cos(\theta_y-\zeta)|\,\mathsf{t}^{(\zeta)}(\theta_{y})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{y\in\mathds{I}_{k}^{(\zeta)}}|\cos(\theta_y-\zeta)|

(63)

with
\mathds{I}_{k}^{(\zeta)}\subset\{0,\dots,n-1\}
denoting set of
k
indices with the highest values of
|\cos(\theta_y-\varphi)|
. Therefore, we conclude that for a fixed local hidden state
\Psi_{\zeta}
, the set of attainable values is contained in the polytope with vertices

\left(\widehat{\mathsf{T}}_{k|n}^{(\zeta)}=\frac{k}{n},\,\pm\,\widehat{\mathsf{W}}_{k|n}^{(\zeta)}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{y\in\mathds{I}_{k}^{(\zeta)}}|\cos(\theta_y-\zeta)|\right)\qquad\text{for }k=0,\dots,n

(64)

Next, we consider the values
(\mathsf{T}_{n},\mathsf{W}_{n})
attainable by general LHS models, involving mixture of different
\zeta
. By convexity the extremal points of this set are in the union of the points
\left(\widehat{\mathsf{T}}_{k|n}^{(\zeta)},\pm\,\widehat{\mathsf{W}}_{k|n}^{(\zeta)}\right)

for all
\zeta
and
k
. Nevertheless, since
\widehat{\mathsf{T}}_{k|n}^{(\zeta)}=\frac{k}{n}
is independent of
\zeta
, it is sufficient to determine

\widehat{\mathsf{W}}_{k|n}:=\max_{\zeta}\,\widehat{\mathsf{W}}_{k|n}^{(\zeta)}

(65)

in order to obtain the vertices of the whole LHS set.

To solve this maximization, note that without loss of generality we can restrict
\zeta\in[0,\frac{1}{2}\frac{\pi}{n}]
by the symmetry of the problem. In this case, noting that
|\cos((n-y)\frac{\pi}{n}-\zeta)|=|\cos(y \frac{\pi}{n}+\zeta)|
we see that the largest values of
|\cos(\theta_y-\zeta)|
are attained in the decreasing order by
\theta_{0},\theta_{1},\theta_{n-1},\theta_{2},\theta_{n-2},\dots
so that

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathsf{W}}_{k|n}^{(\zeta)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\left(\cos(\zeta)+\cos(\frac{\pi}{n} - \zeta)+\cos(\frac{\pi}{n} + \zeta)+\cos(2 \frac{\pi}{n} - \zeta)+\cos(2 \frac{\pi}{n} + \zeta)+\dots\right)

(66)

with exactly
k
terms. Here by construction all the angles are mapped to the interval
[0,\frac{\pi}{2}]
such that the cosines are all positive. It is then convenient to split the analysis in two branches, with even
k=2j
and odd
k=2j+1
. For the two cases we obtain

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathsf{W}}_{2j|n}^{(\zeta)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{j}\left(\cos((\ell-1) \frac{\pi}{n}+\zeta)+\cos(\ell\frac{\pi}{n}-\zeta)\right)

(67)

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathsf{W}}_{2j+1|n}^{(\zeta)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\cos(\zeta)+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{j}\left(\cos(\ell\frac{\pi}{n}-\zeta)+\cos(\ell\frac{\pi}{n}+\zeta)\right).

(68)

Recall that in all of the above expressions the argument of the cosines are in the interval
[0,\frac{\pi}{2}]
, where
\cos(x)
is a concave function satisfying
\cos(x_1)+\cos(x_2)\leq 2\cos(\frac{x_1+x_2}{2})
. Therefore, we have the following inequalities

\displaystyle\cos(\ell\frac{\pi}{n}- \frac{\pi}{n}+\zeta)+\cos(\ell\frac{\pi}{n}-\zeta)\leq 2\cos(\ell\frac{\pi}{n}-\frac{\pi}{2 n})

(69)

\displaystyle\cos(\ell\frac{\pi}{n}-\zeta)+\cos(\ell\frac{\pi}{n}+\zeta)\leq 2\cos(\ell\frac{\pi}{n})

(70)

and also
\cos(\zeta)\leq 1
. By using these inequalities for all term in the sums of Eqs. (
67
,
68
) we obtain

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathsf{W}}_{2j|n}^{(\zeta)}

\displaystyle\leq\widehat{\mathsf{W}}_{2j|n}^{(\frac{\pi}{2n})}=\frac{2}{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{j}\cos((\ell-\frac{1}{2}) \frac{\pi}{n})=\frac{1}{n\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2n}\right)}\sin\left(\pi\frac{2j}{2n}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\widehat{\mathsf{W}}_{2j+1|n}^{(0)}=\frac{1}{n}+\frac{2}{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{j}\cos(\ell\frac{\pi}{n})=\frac{1}{n\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2n}\right)}\sin\left(\pi\frac{2j+1}{2n}\right),

telling us that that one of the two values

depending on the parity of

.
Combining the two expressions gives the maximal value for all

, which takes care of the optimization over
\zeta

\widehat{\mathsf{W}}_{k|n}=\frac{\sin\left(\pi\frac{k}{2n}\right)}{n\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2n}\right)}

We have thus established that the pairs of values

Let us also verify that all of these points are extremal, i.e. none of them are inside the polytope. This can be done by showing that the slope of the line connecting each subsequent pair of points is strictly decreasing

(116)

is a routed Bell inequality, proving the assertion
(i)
.

D.4
(iii) A family of routed Bell tests linear in the LP quantities

In the following, we will derive a family of linear routed Bell inequalities for SRQ correlations. Let
{\rm E}_{n,\beta}(\mathcal{S})
denote the maximum value of the expression below that can be achieved by SRQ correlations when a CHSH value of at least
\mathcal{S}
is observed in the SP, i.e.,

\mathcal{W}_{n}-{\lambda_{n}}\,{\beta}\,\frac{\pi}{2}\,\mathcal{T}_{n}\leq{\rm E}_{n,\beta}(\mathcal{S}),\,

(117)

where
\beta\in\,]0,1]
is a constant,
\lambda_{n}:={1}/({n\sin(\frac{\pi}{2n})})
. Using the Jordan decomposition, and the steering inequality (Eq. (
8
))
\mathcal{W}_{n}^{\alpha}\leq\lambda_{n}\sqrt{2}\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\mathcal{T}_{n}\right)\gamma(\mathrm{s}_{\alpha})
, we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{n}-{\lambda_{n}}\,{\beta}\,\frac{\pi}{2}\,\mathcal{T}_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\mu_{i}\left[\mathcal{W}_{n}^{\alpha_{i}}-{\lambda_{n}}\,{\beta}\,\frac{\pi}{2}\,\mathcal{T}_{n}^{i}\right]

(118)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i}\mu_{i}\left[\lambda_{n}\sqrt{2}\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\mathcal{T}_{n}^{i}\right)\gamma(\mathrm{s}_{\alpha_{i}})-{\lambda_{n}}\,{\beta}\,\frac{\pi}{2}\,\mathcal{T}_{n}^{i}\right]\,.

(119)

To simplify the notation, let us define

\displaystyle C_{\alpha_{i}}:=\frac{\mathrm{s}_{\alpha_{i}}}{2\sqrt{2}}\,,\quad f_{\beta}(x):=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\beta}\gamma(2\sqrt{2}\,x)=\frac{x+\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}{\beta},\quad\tilde{\mathcal{T}_{n}}^{i}:=\frac{\pi}{2}\mathcal{T}_{n}^{i}\,,

(120)

which gives us the following bound on
C_{\alpha_{i}}
, from Eq. (
11
), due to the observed value of
\mathcal{S}
in the SP

\sum_{i}\mu_{i}C_{\alpha_{i}}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}\sum_{i}\mu_{i}\mathrm{s}_{\alpha_{i}}\geq\frac{\mathcal{S}}{2\sqrt{2}}\,.

(121)

This lets us rewrite Eq. (
119
) as

\mathcal{W}_{n}-{\lambda_{n}}\,{\beta}\,\frac{\pi}{2}\,\mathcal{T}_{n}\leq\lambda_{n}\,\beta\,\sum_{i}\mu_{i}\left[f_{\beta}(C_{\alpha_{i}})\sin(\tilde\TTn^i)-\tilde{\mathcal{T}}_{n}^{i}\right]\,.

(122)

Note that since
C_{\alpha_{i}}\in[1/\sqrt{2},1]
and
\beta\in\,]0,1]
, we have
f_{\beta}(C_{\alpha_{i}})\in[1,\infty[\,
. Furthermore, the family of functions

g_{\alpha,\beta}(\tilde{\mathcal{T}}_{n}):=f_{\beta}(C_{\alpha})\sin(\tilde\TTn)-\tilde{\mathcal{T}}_{n}\,,

(123)

defined on the domain
\tilde{\mathcal{T}}_{n}\in[0,\pi/2]
, are differentiable and admit a unique maximum at the point
\tilde{\mathcal{T}}_{n}=\arccos\left({1}/{f_{\beta}(C_{\alpha})}\right)
. Substituting this value of
\tilde{\mathcal{T}}_{n}
in Eq. (
122
), and using the identity
\sin(\arccos(x))=\sqrt{1-x^{2}}
, we obtain

\mathcal{W}_{n}-{\lambda_{n}}\,{\beta}\,\frac{\pi}{2}\,\mathcal{T}_{n}\leq\lambda_{n}\,\beta\,\sum_{i}\mu_{i}\left[\sqrt{f_{\beta}(C_{\alpha_{i}})^{2}-1}\,\,-\arccos({1}/{ f_{\beta}(C_{\alpha_i})})\right]\,.

(124)

To proceed further, we note that the family of functions

h_{\beta}(x):=\sqrt{f_{\beta}(x)^{2}-1}\,\,-\arccos({1}/{f_{\beta}(x)})\,

(125)

are nonincreasing and concave for
x\in[1/\sqrt{2},1]
, i.e.,
h_{\beta}^{\prime}(x)\leq 0
and
h_{\beta}^{\prime\prime}(x)\leq 0
whenever
\beta\in\,]0,1]
(see App.
E
). Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{n}-\lambda_{n}\,\beta\,\frac{\pi}{2}\,\mathcal{T}_{n}

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{n}\,\beta\,\sum_{i}\mu_{i}\,h_{\beta}(C_{\alpha_{i}})

(126)

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{n}\,\beta\,h_{\beta}\left(\sum_{i}\mu_{i}C_{\alpha_{i}}\right)\,

(127)

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{n}\,\beta\,h_{\beta}\left(\frac{\mathcal{S}}{2\sqrt{2}}\right):={\rm E}_{n,\beta}(\mathcal{S}),

(128)

where we have used the fact that
h_{\beta}(C_{\alpha})
is a concave nonincreasing function, and is hence maximized for the minimum value of its argument, which is given by Eq. (
121
).

To obtain a linear routed Bell inequality from above, we only need to note that since
h_{\beta}(x)
is a concave function, it is upper bounded by the family of tangents at every point of it argument. Explicitly,

\forall\xi\in[1/\sqrt{2},1]\,,\quad h_{\beta}\left(\frac{\mathcal{S}}{2\sqrt{2}}\right)\leq h_{\beta}(\xi)+h^{\prime}_{\beta}(\xi)\left(\frac{\mathcal{S}}{2\sqrt{2}}-\xi\right)\,,

(129)

which, when combined with Eq. (
128
), leads to the linear routed Bell inequality

\forall\beta\in\,]0,1]\,,\xi\in[1/\sqrt{2},1]\,,\quad\frac{n\sin(\frac{\pi}{2n})}{\beta}\mathcal{W}_{n}-\frac{\pi}{2}\mathcal{T}_{n}\leq h_{\beta}(\xi)+\left(\frac{\mathcal{S}}{2\sqrt{2}}-\xi\right)\,h^{\prime}_{\beta}(\xi)\,.

(130)

We can use these linear inequalities to arrive at the main result in Eq. (
6
). We discuss this below.

D.4.1
Using the linear inequalities to characterize the SRQ set

To arrive at the main result in the form of Eq. (
6

\forall\beta\in\,]0,1],\quad\frac{n\sin(\frac{\pi}{2n})}{\beta}\mathcal{W}_{n}-\frac{\pi}{2}\mathcal{T}_{n}\leq\sqrt{\left({\sqrt{2}\,\gamma({\mathcal{S}})}/{\beta}\right)^{2}-1}\,-\arccos\left({\beta}/{(\sqrt{2}\,\gamma(\mathcal{S})})\right).

We can now use this family of inequalities to bound the set of allowed values of
\mathcal{W}_{n}

\mathcal{T}_{n}

(133)

Although the upper bound on

{n\sin(\frac{\pi}{2n})}\mathcal{W}_{n}\leq\sqrt{2}\,\gamma(\mathcal{S})\,\sin(\frac{\pi}{2}\TTn)\,,

(134)

valid whenever

(\ast\ast\ast\ast)\quad\quad\sqrt{2}\,\gamma(\mathcal{S})\leq 1/\cos(\frac{\pi}{2}\TTn)\,.

(135)

This region is denoted by
(\ast\ast\ast\ast)
in Fig.

\rm R

D.5.1
Local concavity

To start, we compute the Hessian of the function

{\bf H}_{F}(\epsilon,t)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\partial_{\epsilon}^{2}F(\epsilon,t)&\partial_{\epsilon}\partial_{t}F(\epsilon,t)\\
\partial_{t}\partial_{\epsilon}F(\epsilon,t)&\partial_{t}^{2}F(\epsilon,t)\\
\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}f^{\prime\prime}(\epsilon)g(t)&f^{\prime}(\epsilon)g^{\prime}(t)\\
f^{\prime}(\epsilon)g^{\prime}(t)&f(\epsilon)g^{\prime\prime}(t)\\
\end{array}\right),

(140)

with

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(\epsilon)

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\epsilon}{\sqrt{(2-\epsilon)\epsilon}}-1\qquad f^{\prime\prime}(\epsilon)=\frac{1}{((2-\epsilon)\epsilon)^{3/2}}

(141)

\displaystyle g^{\prime}(\epsilon)

\displaystyle=\cos\left(\frac{\pi t}{2}\right)\qquad g^{\prime\prime}(\epsilon)=-\frac{\pi}{2}\sin\left(\frac{\pi t}{2}\right).

(142)

Here the trace of the Hessian is non-positive
f^{\prime\prime}(\epsilon)g(t)+f(\epsilon)g^{\prime\prime}(t)\leq 0
, therefore it must have at least one negative eigenvalue, or both are zero. The sign of the other eigenvalue can be determined from its determinant, and we find

(\ast)\quad{\bf H}_{F}(\epsilon,t)\leq 0\Longleftrightarrow\det\,{\bf H}_{F}(\epsilon,t)\geq 0.

(143)

We know that the function
F(\epsilon,t)
is locally concave iff its Hessian is negative semidefinite. Hence
(\ast)
gives the first necessary condition for
F(\epsilon,t)=\widehat{F}(\epsilon,t)
.

D.5.2
Tangent hyperplane

Next, we consider the hyperplanes
H_{\epsilon_{0},t_{0}}(\epsilon,t)
tangent to the function
F(\epsilon,t)=f(t)g(t)
at
(\epsilon_{0},t_{0})
. The hyperplane is given by the equation

\displaystyle{\rm H}_{\epsilon_{0},t_{0}}(\epsilon,t)

\displaystyle:=F(\epsilon_{0},t_{0})+\partial_{\epsilon}F(\epsilon_{0},t_{0})(\epsilon-\epsilon_{0})+\partial_{t}F(\epsilon_{0},t_{0})(t-t_{0})

(144)

\displaystyle=f(\epsilon_{0})g(t_{0})+f^{\prime}(\epsilon_{0})g(t_{0})(\epsilon-\epsilon_{0})+f(\epsilon_{0})g^{\prime}(t_{0})(t-t_{0})

(145)

We know that if the hyperplane at
(\epsilon_{0},t_{0})
upper-bounds the function
H_{\epsilon_{0},t_{0}}(\epsilon,t)\geq F(\epsilon,t)
on the whole domain
[0,\epsilon_{max}]\times[0,1]
, then the function is equal to its concave envelope when
\widehat{F}(\epsilon_{0},t_{0})=F(\epsilon_{0},t_{0})
. Therefore, we want to identify all values
(\epsilon_{0},t_{0})
, such that the function

\Delta_{\epsilon_{0},t_{0}}(\epsilon,t)=H_{\epsilon_{0},t_{0}}(\epsilon,t)-F(\epsilon,t).

(146)

is nonnegative. In other words, we want to determine all values
(\epsilon_{0},t_{0})
such that the global minimum of
\Delta_{\epsilon_{0},t_{0}}(\epsilon,t)
on
[0,\epsilon_{max}]
is nonnegative. To answer this question it will useful to manipulate the derivatives of the function

\displaystyle\partial_{\epsilon}\Delta_{\epsilon_{0},t_{0}}(\epsilon,t)

\displaystyle=f^{\prime}(\epsilon_{0})g(t_{0})-f^{\prime}(\epsilon)g(t)

(147)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\Delta_{\epsilon_{0},t_{0}}(\epsilon,t)

\displaystyle=f(\epsilon_{0})g^{\prime}(t_{0})-f(\epsilon)g^{\prime}(t).

(148)

We also remark that the Hessian of the function is given by
{\bf H}_{\Delta}(\epsilon,t)=-{\bf H}_{F}(\epsilon,t)
and is independent of
(\epsilon_{0},t_{0})
.

D.5.3
The values of
\Delta_{\epsilon_{0},t_{0}}
on the boundary of the domain.

We first look for the global minimum of
\Delta_{\epsilon_{0},t_{0}}
on the boundary of the domain. Noting that
g(0)=0
,
g^{\prime}(1)=0
,
f^{\prime}(\epsilon_{max})=0
and
\lim_{\epsilon\to 0_{+},t>0}f^{\prime}(\epsilon)=-\infty
, we conclude the following properties

\displaystyle\partial_{\epsilon}\Delta_{\epsilon_{0},t_{0}}(0,t)\to-\infty\qquad\text{if}\qquad t>0

(149)

\displaystyle\partial_{\epsilon}\Delta_{\epsilon_{0},t_{0}}(\epsilon_{max},t)=f^{\prime}(\epsilon_{0})g(t_{0})\geq 0

(150)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\Delta_{\epsilon_{0},t_{0}}(\epsilon,1)=f(\epsilon_{0})g^{\prime}(t_{0})\geq 0.

(151)

These inequalities imply that the the function can not have its global minimum at
\epsilon=\epsilon_{max}
,
t=1
and
\epsilon=0
if
t>0
, because it decreases in the direction of the interior. In turn, for
t=0
we find that

\displaystyle\partial_{\epsilon}\Delta_{\epsilon_{0},t_{0}}(\epsilon,0)=f^{\prime}(\epsilon_{0})g(t_{0})\geq 0,

(152)

i.e.
\Delta_{\epsilon_{0},t_{0}}(\epsilon,0)
is non-decreasing with
\epsilon
and attains its mininum on this interval at
\epsilon=0
.

Hence the on the boundary of the domain the function
\Delta_{\epsilon_{0},t_{0}}
can only admit a global minimum at
(\epsilon,t)=(0,0)
. For our task we must thus verify that the function is nonnegative here. This adds the following constraint

\displaystyle(\ast\ast)\qquad\Delta_{\epsilon_{0},t_{0}}(0,0)\geq 0.

(153)

D.5.4
Local minima of
\Delta_{\epsilon_{0},t_{0}}

Now that we settled the question of the boundary it remains to verify that the local minima of the function are also positive. Its local extremas are given by the equations
\partial_{\epsilon}\Delta_{\epsilon_{0},t_{0}}(\epsilon,t)=\partial_{t}\Delta_{\epsilon_{0},t_{0}}(\epsilon,t)=0
, whose explicit form is

\displaystyle\sin(\frac{\pi}{2}t)f^{\prime}(\epsilon)

\displaystyle=\sin(\frac{\pi}{2}t_0)f^{\prime}(\epsilon_{0})\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\sin(\frac{\pi}{2}t)=\sin(\frac{\pi}{2}t_0)\frac{f^{\prime}(\epsilon_{0})}{f^{\prime}(\epsilon)}

(154)

\displaystyle\cos(\frac{\pi}{2}t)f(\epsilon)

\displaystyle=\cos(\frac{\pi}{2}t_0)f(\epsilon_{0})\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\cos(\frac{\pi}{2}t)=\cos(\frac{\pi}{2}t_0)\frac{f(\epsilon_{0})}{f(\epsilon)}

(155)

where one notes that the functions of the rhs are always nonnegative on the domain of interest, as
f(\epsilon),f^{\prime}(\epsilon)
, sine and cosine are nonnegative. In fact, we already know one local minimum of the function, which is given by
(\epsilon,t)=(\epsilon_{0},t_{0})
where
\Delta_{\epsilon_{0},t_{0}}(\epsilon_{0},t_{0})=0
by construction.

Let us now show that the function can at most admit two local extrema. To see this we eliminate the variable
t
by using
\cos^{2}(\frac{\pi}{2}t)+\sin^{2}(\frac{\pi}{2}t)=1
. This leaves us with the following equation

\displaystyle\frac{\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{2}t_{0}\right)(f^{\prime}(\epsilon_{0}))^{2}}{\big(f^{\prime}(\epsilon)\big)^{2}}+\frac{\cos^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{2}t_{0}\right)\big(f(\epsilon_{0})\big)^{2}}{\big(f(\epsilon)\big)^{2}}=1

(156)

Here, one can see that the function on the left hand side is convex, since both functions
1/\big(f^{\prime}(\epsilon)\big)^{2}
and
1/\big(f(\epsilon)\big)^{2}
are convex, which can be directly seen from their second derivatives

\displaystyle\frac{\differential^{2}}{\differential\epsilon^{2}}\frac{1}{\big(f^{\prime}(\epsilon)\big)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{6\left(1-\epsilon+\sqrt{(2-\epsilon)\epsilon}\right)}{\sqrt{(2-\epsilon)\epsilon}\left(1+\sqrt{(2-\epsilon)\epsilon}-\epsilon\right)^{4}}=\frac{6\,f(\epsilon)}{\sqrt{(2-\epsilon)\epsilon}\left(\epsilon+\sqrt{(2-\epsilon)\epsilon}-1\right)^{4}}\geq 0

(157)

\displaystyle\frac{\differential^{2}}{\differential\epsilon^{2}}\frac{1}{\big(f(\epsilon)\big)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{8\sqrt{(2-\epsilon)\epsilon}-(12\epsilon^{3}-36\epsilon^{2}+26\epsilon-2)}{((2-\epsilon)\epsilon)^{3/2}\left(\epsilon+\sqrt{(2-\epsilon)\epsilon}-1\right)^{4}}\geq 0.

(158)

In both cases the denominator is nonnegative, and the sign is determined by the numerator. In the first case we know that
f(\epsilon)\geq 0
. In the second case we see numerically that the numerator

8\sqrt{(2-\epsilon)\epsilon}-(12\epsilon^{3}-36\epsilon^{2}+26\epsilon-2)
is lower bounded by
2
on
[0,\epsilon_{max}]
, from there its positivity can be guaranteed by e.g. noting that
-(12\epsilon^{3}-36\epsilon^{2}+26\epsilon-2)^{2}\geq 0
for
\epsilon\leq 1-\sqrt{5/6}
while the whole function is Lipschitz continuous for
\epsilon\geq 1-\sqrt{5/6}
. Since the function in the left hand side of Eq. (
156
) is convex in
\epsilon
and non-constant, it follows that the equation can have at most two solutions for
\epsilon
. In other words, the local extrema of
\Delta_{\epsilon_{0},t_{0}}(\epsilon,t)
might occur for at most two distinct values of
\epsilon
.

Let us now come back to the Eqs. (
154
,
155
). By inverting the trigonometric functions we find the curves on which the derivatives of
\Delta_{\epsilon_{0},t_{0}}
vanish

\displaystyle\partial_{\epsilon}\Delta_{\epsilon_{0},t_{0}}(\epsilon,t)=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad t

\displaystyle=X_{\epsilon_{0},t_{0}}(\epsilon):=\frac{2}{\pi}\arcsin\left[\frac{\sin(\frac{\pi}{2}t_0)f^{\prime}(\epsilon_{0})}{f^{\prime}(\epsilon)}\right]

(159)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\Delta_{\epsilon_{0},t_{0}}(\epsilon,t)=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad t

\displaystyle=Y_{\epsilon_{0},t_{0}}(\epsilon):=\frac{2}{\pi}\arccos\left[\frac{\cos(\frac{\pi}{2}t_0)f(\epsilon_{0})}{f(\epsilon)}\right].

(160)

Since the arguments of arcsin and arccos are positive, both functions
0\leq X_{\epsilon_{0},t_{0}},Y_{\epsilon_{0},t_{0}}\leq 1
produce valuess in the valid range if the arguments do not exceed one, otherwise the corresponding derivative does not vanish for the chosen value of
\epsilon
. The local minima are obtained at the the intersection of these two functions in the domain
\epsilon\in[0,\epsilon_{max}]
. It is easy to see that both functions are monotonically increasing in
\epsilon
, since we know that
\arcsin(x)
with
1/f^{\prime}(\epsilon)
are both monotonically increasing, and
\arccos(x)
with
1/f(\epsilon)
are both monotonically decreasing. Local extrema are thus given by the equation
X_{\epsilon_{0},t_{0}}(\epsilon)=Y_{\epsilon_{0},t_{0}}(\epsilon)
, which can have at most two solution as we have shown.

By construction we know that
(\epsilon,t)=(\epsilon_{0},t_{0})
is a local minimum of the function, since at this point it has a positive semi-definite Hessian
{\bf H}_{\Delta}(\epsilon,t)=-{\bf H}_{F}(\epsilon,t)\geq 0
as implied by the condition
(\ast)
. If there is another local extremum, it can be obtained by decreasing or increasing
\epsilon
while following the curves
\bm{x}_{\epsilon}=\binom{\epsilon}{X_{\epsilon_{0},t_{0}}(\epsilon)}
and
\bm{y}_{\epsilon}=\binom{\epsilon}{Y_{\epsilon_{0},t_{0}}(\epsilon)}
until they intersect again.
Where we have used the fact that the curves are connected, which is guaranteed by the fact that
X_{\epsilon_{0},t_{0}}(\epsilon)
and
Y_{\epsilon_{0},t_{0}}(\epsilon)
are monotonically increasing. Now, consider following the curve
\bm{y}_{\epsilon}
, on which the gradient of the function with respect to
t
vanishes
\partial_{t}\Delta_{\epsilon_{0},t_{0}}(\epsilon,t)=0
.
Because of this, the derivative of the function
\Delta_{\epsilon_{0},t_{0}}(\bm{y}_{\epsilon})
along the curve is simply given by its gradient with respect to
\epsilon

\displaystyle\differential\Delta_{\epsilon_{0},t_{0}}(\bm{y}_{\epsilon})=\binom{\partial_{\epsilon}\Delta_{\epsilon_{0},t_{0}}(\bm{y}_{\epsilon})}{\partial_{t}\Delta_{\epsilon_{0},t_{0}}(\bm{y}_{\epsilon})}\cdot\differential\bm{x}_{\epsilon}=\binom{\partial_{\epsilon}\Delta_{\epsilon_{0},t_{0}}(\bm{y}_{\epsilon})}{0}\cdot\binom{\differential\epsilon}{\differential Y_{\epsilon_{0},t_{0}}(\epsilon)}=\partial_{\epsilon}\Delta_{\epsilon_{0},t_{0}}(\bm{y}_{\epsilon})\differential\epsilon.

(161)

At
\epsilon=\epsilon_{0}
we know that
\partial_{\epsilon}\Delta_{\epsilon_{0},t_{0}}(\bm{y}_{\epsilon})<0
for
\epsilon=\epsilon_{0}-\differential\epsilon
and
\partial_{\epsilon}\Delta_{\epsilon_{0},t_{0}}(\bm{y}_{\epsilon})>0
for
\epsilon=\epsilon_{0}+\differential\epsilon
since this is an isolated local minimum. By following the curve to the other local extremum
(\epsilon_{*},t_{*})
, where
\partial_{\epsilon}\Delta_{\epsilon_{0},t_{0}}(\bm{y}_{\epsilon_{*}})=0
, by the intermediate value theorem we find that

\displaystyle\partial_{\epsilon}\Delta_{\epsilon_{0},t_{0}}(\bm{y}_{\epsilon_{*}+\differential\epsilon})<0\quad\text{if}\quad\epsilon_{*}<\epsilon_{0}

(162)

\displaystyle\partial_{\epsilon}\Delta_{\epsilon_{0},t_{0}}(\bm{y}_{\epsilon_{*}-\differential\epsilon})>0\quad\text{if}\quad\epsilon_{*}>\epsilon_{0}

(163)

In both cases the bounds guarantee that
(\epsilon_{*},t_{*})
can not be a local minimum of the function, since the function increases when approaching it from
(\epsilon_{0},t_{0})
along
\bf y_{\epsilon}.
We conclude that on the domain of interest the function
\Delta_{\epsilon_{0},t_{0}}
has a unique local minimum at
(\epsilon_{0},t_{0})
.

D.5.5
The condition
(\ast\ast)
implies
(\ast)

Under the condition
(\ast)
, we have thus shown that the global minimum of
\Delta_{\epsilon_{0},t_{0}}(\epsilon,t)
on the domain
[0,\epsilon_{max}]\times[0,1]
is to be found either at
(\epsilon_{0},t_{0})
, where
\Delta_{\epsilon_{0},t_{0}}(\epsilon_{0},t_{0})=0
by construction, or at the origin
(0,0)
where
\Delta_{\epsilon_{0},t_{0}}(0,0)\geq 0
gave rise to the condition
(\ast\ast)
. Therefore, the hyperplane tangent to
F(\epsilon,t)
at
(\epsilon_{0},t_{0})
is an upper bound on the function on the whole domain if and only if both conditions
(\ast)
and
(\ast\ast)
are fulfilled. In turn, this is equivalent to the function being equal to its concave envelop at the tangent point
F(\epsilon_{0},t_{0})=\widehat{F}(\epsilon_{0},t_{0})
. To summarize, we have shown that

\displaystyle F(\epsilon_{0},t_{0})=\widehat{F}(\epsilon_{0},t_{0})\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\begin{cases}\det\,{\bf H}_{F}(\epsilon_{0},t_{0})\geq 0&(\ast)\\
\Delta_{\epsilon_{0},t_{0}}(0,0)\geq 0&(\ast\ast)\end{cases}.

(164)

We now would like to put these constraints in a more usable form. We have

\displaystyle(\ast)\quad\det\,{\bf H}_{F}(\epsilon,t)\geq 0\quad

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\quad f^{\prime\prime}(\epsilon)f(\epsilon)g(t)g^{\prime\prime}(t)-(f^{\prime}(\epsilon)g^{\prime}(t))^{2}\geq 0

D.6
(iv) Tightness of the bounds in the large

We now construct an SRQ model saturating the bound of Eq. (
108
) in the
n\to\infty
limit. To do se let the source distribute the maximally entangled two qubit state
\ket{\Phi^+}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{00}+\ket{11})
and Alice perform the two measurements corresponding to the observables

A_{x}^{\alpha}=\left(\cos(\alpha)H+(-1)^{x}\sin(\alpha)M\right)

(184)

with
H=\frac{Z+H}{\sqrt{2}}
,
M=\frac{Z-H}{\sqrt{2}}

and some angle
\alpha\in[0,\frac{\pi}{4}]
. With this, let
{\rm B}^{\rm S}
perform the the measurement maximizing the CHSH score in the SP, we have seen (e.g. in the derivation of Lemma
2
) that this results in the following score

\mathcal{S}=2\sqrt{2}\left(\frac{\cos(\alpha)+\sin(\alpha)}{\sqrt{2}}\right)=2\sqrt{2}\,C_{\alpha}.

(185)

To complete the SRQ model, in the LP after the router let Bob’s system be measured by the two-outcomes PVM measurement
\{{\rm E}_{\lambda}\}
in the eigenbasis of
H
. Doing so, and observing an output
\lambda=\pm 1
with equal probability, collapses Alice’s reduced state to

\varrho_{\pm}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathds{1}\pm H\right).

(186)

A copy of the value
\lambda
is sent to the measurement device of
{\rm B}^{\rm L}
(manifestly there is no entanglement shared between Alice and
{\rm B}^{\rm L}
) which is instructed to implement the deterministic response function
p(b|\lambda,\theta)\in\{0,1\}
. This is an LHS model, which conditional one
{\rm B}^{\rm L}
’s measurement output
b
leaves Alice’s qubit in the state

\rho_{b|\theta}=\frac{1}{2}\Big(p(b|+,\theta)\,\varrho_{+}+p(b|-,\theta)\,\varrho_{-}\Big)=\frac{p(b|+,\theta)+p(b|-,\theta)}{4}\mathds{1}+\frac{p(b|+,\theta)-p(b|-,\theta)}{4}H.

(187)

We now assume that the response function has the symmetry
p(0|\pm,\theta)=p(1|\mp,\theta)
, which allows us to write

\displaystyle\rho_{0|\theta}

\displaystyle=\frac{p(0|+,\theta)+p(1|+,\theta)}{4}\mathds{1}+\frac{p(0|+,\theta)-p(1|+,\theta)}{4}H

(188)

\displaystyle\rho_{1|\theta}

\displaystyle=\frac{p(0|+,\theta)+p(1|+,\theta)}{4}\mathds{1}-\frac{p(0|+,\theta)-p(1|+,\theta)}{4}H.

(189)

Accordingly, in the continuous setting limit
n\to\infty
for our SRQ model the LP quantities are given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{\differential\theta}{\pi}\tr\left[\rho_{0|\theta}+\rho_{1|\theta}\right]

(190)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{\differential\theta}{\pi}\big(p(0|+,\theta)+p(1|+,\theta)\big)

(191)

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{\differential\theta}{\pi}\tr\left[(\cos(\theta)A_{0}+\sin(\theta)A_{1})(\rho_{0|\theta}-\rho_{1|\theta})\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{\differential\theta}{\pi}\cos(\alpha)(\cos(\theta)+\sin(\theta))\tr\left[H(\rho_{0|\theta}-\rho_{1|\theta})\right]

(193)

(194)

where we used
\cos(\theta)+\sin(\theta)=\sqrt{2}\cos\left(\theta-\frac{\pi}{4}\right)

\Big(p(0|+,\theta),p(1|+,\theta),p(\varnothing|+,\theta)\Big)=\begin{cases}(1,0,0)&\left|\theta-\frac{\pi}{4}\right|\leq\Omega\\
(0,1,0)&\left|\theta-\frac{\pi}{4}+\pi\right|\leq\Omega\\

(195)

(196)

We have therefore shown that by varying the parameters
\alpha

\Omega

(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{T},\mathcal{W})=\left(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{T},\frac{\mathcal{S}+\sqrt{8-\mathcal{S}^{2}}}{2\sqrt{2}}\frac{2}{\pi}\sin(\T\frac{\pi}{2})\right)

(197)

for all
\mathcal{T}\in[0,1]
and
\mathcal{S}\in[2,2\sqrt{2}]

\mathcal{T}=\mathcal{T}_{\infty}
and
\mathcal{S}
we have constructed an SRQ model satisfying

\frac{\pi}{2}\mathcal{W}_{\infty}=\frac{\pi}{2}\mathcal{W}=\frac{\mathcal{S}+\sqrt{8-\mathcal{S}^{2}}}{2\sqrt{2}}\sin(\T\frac{\pi}{2})={\rm R}(\mathcal{T}_{\infty},\mathcal{S})

(198)

(199)

Proof that the functions

Here we show that the family of functions

h_{\beta}^{\prime}(x)\leq 0
and
h_{\beta}^{\prime\prime}(x)\leq 0
whenever
\beta\in\,]0,1]
. The function
f_{\beta}(x)
is given by

f_{\beta}(x):=\frac{x+\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}{\beta}\,.

(202)

Computing the first and second derivatives of
h_{\beta}(x)
, we find

\displaystyle h_{\beta}^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=f_{\beta}^{\prime}(x)\frac{\sqrt{{f}^{2}_{\beta}(x)-1}}{{f}_{\beta}(x)}\,,\qquad h_{\beta}^{\prime\prime}(x)={f}_{\beta}^{\prime\prime}(x)\frac{\sqrt{{f}^{2}_{\beta}(x)-1}}{{f}_{\beta}(x)}+({f}_{\beta}^{\prime}(x))^{2}\frac{1}{{f}^{2}_{\beta}(x)\sqrt{{f}^{2}_{\beta}(x)-1}}\,,

(203)

where,

{f}_{\beta}^{\prime}(x)=\frac{1}{\beta}\left(1-\frac{x}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}\right)\leq 0\,,\quad{f}_{\beta}^{\prime\prime}(x)=\frac{1}{\beta}\left(\frac{-1}{(1-x^{2})^{3/2}}\right)\leq 0\,.

(204)

The fact that
h_{\beta}(x)
is nonincreasing follows from
{f}_{\beta}^{\prime}(x)\leq 0
. In turn,

h_{\beta}(x)
is concave if

h_{\beta}^{\prime\prime}(x)\leq 0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad{f}_{\beta}^{\prime\prime}(x)\,{f}_{\beta}(x)\,({f}^{2}_{\beta}(x)-1)+({f}_{\beta}^{\prime}(x))^{2}\leq 0

(205)

Straightforward algebra gives

\displaystyle{f}_{\beta}^{\prime\prime}(x)\,{f}_{\beta}(x)\,({f}^{2}_{\beta}(x)-1)+({f}_{\beta}^{\prime}(x))^{2}=-\frac{\beta^{2}(x-x^{3})+3\left(x-x^{3}\left(\frac{2+\beta^{2}}{3}\right)\right)+\left(2(x^{2}-\beta^{2})+1\right)\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}{\beta^{4}\left(1-x^{2}\right)^{3/2}},

(206)

which is indeed nonpositive since all terms in the numerator and denominator are nonnegative, implying
