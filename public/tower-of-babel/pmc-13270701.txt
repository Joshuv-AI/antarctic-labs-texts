Ethical perspectives of Brazilian community pharmacists: a pilot study
Authors: Francielly Lima da Fonseca, Cristy Evelin Melo de Souza, Diogo Souza de Araujo, Lucas Tenorio Carmo do Nascimento Bezerra, Aline Santana Dosea, Alfredo Dias de Oliveira Filho, Alessandra Rezende Mesquita, Divaldo Pereira de Lyra Jr
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn recent years, community pharmacies have played a prominent role both in the context of primary health care, especially in the rational use of medicines, and in the global economic landscape. In this context, pharmacists are increasingly subject to commercial, institutional, and social pressures that can compromise their ethical decisions. However, this issue has been addressed in a limited way in the scientific literature. Thus, the objective of this pilot study was to identify ethical dilemmas experienced by pharmacists working in Brazilian community pharmacies to improve the methodological procedures of the future multicenter study.MethodThus, based on COREQ, a qualitative study with six semi-structured interviews conducted with pharmacists from large-chain community pharmacies in the state of Sergipe was carried out. This study was approved by Research Ethics Committee.ResultsContent analysis identified five preliminary categories related to ethical dilemmas in community pharmacy practice, including conflicts with patients, interference from other professionals, commercial pressures and regulatory constraints, Insufficient ethical training, and limitations of professional autonomy.ConclusionAs a pilot study, the findings provide preliminary insights into ethical dilemmas experienced in large-chain community pharmacies and support the feasibility and methodological refinement of a future multicenter qualitative investigation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12910-026-01425-3.Keywords: Pharmacy ethics, Community pharmacy, Ethical dilemmas, Pharmacists, Medicine retail

Introduction

The global pharmaceutical market reached approximately US$1.1 trillion in 2023, with projections to achieve US$1.9 trillion by 2027 driven by increased medicine consumption, technological innovation, and the growth of emerging economies [1]. Within this global landscape, Brazil is considered the fifth highest consumer market for medicines in the world and dominates 42% of the market in Latin America [2]. In parallel, Brazil is one of the countries with the largest absolute number of pharmacists (400 thousand), positioning community pharmacies as a major employer and a critical point of contact between the population and the health system, but is one of the few countries where regulations that require the presence of pharmacists in community pharmacies are not strictly applied [1, 3].

In community pharmacies, as the first point of access to primary care, pharmacists can provide, in addition to medications, care to patients [3, 4]. However, commercial pressures, ambiguous legislation, and structural challenges lead to ethical dilemmas for pharmacists who find themselves at the interface between patient’s needs and their professional duties [5, 6]. These conditions make the Brazilian context particularly informative for understanding how ethical dilemmas emerge, are negotiated, and impact professional autonomy in real-world practice. Although the principles of beneficence, autonomy, non-maleficence, and justice guide professional conduct, the dilemmas experienced by pharmacists are not always documented or understood in depth [7–9].

Studies often fail to capture subjectivity, reflexivity, and the meanings attributed to ethical challenges by pharmacists, managers, and other stakeholders [10]. Moreover, international studies remain unevenly distributed around the world, with limited representation from large middle-income countries where market expansion, regulatory ambiguity, and workforce scale may intensify ethical vulnerability [11–15]. Ethical dilemmas in community pharmacy practice emerge of professional autonomy, commercial determinants intersection, regulatory ambiguity, and the moral responsibilities with patient care [5, 16]. In retail-oriented pharmaceutical systems, pharmacists navigate tensions between ethical obligations to patients and organizational expectations based on productivity, sales targets, and corporate governance structures (18). These tensions may generate moral distress in everyday professional practice.

In Brazil and across Latin America, these dynamics may be intensified by the rapid expansion of pharmaceutical markets, variability in regulatory enforcement, and differences in professional training. Despite of these structural factors, empirical qualitative evidence examining how pharmacists interpret and experience ethical dilemmas in everyday community pharmacy practice remains limited. This gap limits understanding of how ethical decision-making is shaped in practice and how pharmacists negotiate tensions between commercial expectations, regulatory requirements, and professional responsibilities. Then, a pilot qualitative study was relevant to refine and to strengthen the interview guide, test recruitment strategies, and develop preliminary analytic categories prior to a future multicenter research.

In this context, the present study aimed to explore how ethical dilemmas are experienced and articulated by pharmacists working in Brazilian large-chain community pharmacies while generating preliminary methodological and analytical insights to inform future research.

Method

Study design

This is a qualitative pilot study conducted in November 2024, based on the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research [COREQ] (17). Semi-structured interviews were conducted with pharmacists working in community pharmacies belonging to large-chain community pharmacies in the state of Sergipe. The study included: [1] assessing the clarity and relevance of the interview guide topic; [2] evaluating participants’ receptivity to the study topic; [3] identify methodological and ethical challenges experienced by pharmacists that may arise in the main study; and [4] verify the consistency of the proposed protocol. Based on the data from this pilot study, a multicenter study will be conducted.

Research team

The interviews were conducted by a female lead moderator with previous experience in conducting interviews (18, 19). The moderator is a pharmacist with master’s degree in pharmaceutical sciences and is completing her doctorate in the graduate program in pharmaceutical sciences at the Federal University of Sergipe. Prior contact was established with the participants, who were informed about the study’s objectives, the researcher’s background, and her role in conducting the interviews, ensuring transparency and trust throughout the process. A pharmacy student also accompanied the interviews as an observer to record relevant verbal and nonverbal aspects. No prior professional or personal relationships existed between the interviewer and participants before recruitment.

Participant selection

Of the 11 pharmacists invited to participate in the study, six pharmacists (n = 6) they agreend to do the interviews. Recruitment was conducted via WhatsApp using convenience sampling. The participants were also forwarded the Informed Consent Form and a sociodemographic questionnaire. Inclusion criteria were: a pharmacy degree recognized by the Brazilian Ministry of Education, at least five years of professional experience, and current employment in large-chain community pharmacies in the state of Sergipe. Only pharmacists who met the criteria and was interested in participate this research were included.

Five pharmacists were unable to participate due to scheduling constraints. No participants refused participation after initial contact for reasons other than scheduling constraints. Prior professional or personal relationships between the interviewer and participants did not exist. Exclusion criteria included pharmacists not currently working in community pharmacy settings or with less than five years of experience. No financial or material incentives were offered for participation.

The community pharmacies choice was justified because it is the main setting for pharmacists in Brazil [20], in addition to being an environment with a high volume of patients and exposure to ethical dilemmas [6]. The choice of pharmacists from large-chain community pharmacies allowed us to explore the specific dynamics of these settings, where commercial pressures and company policies can influence professional practice.

Data collection

Semi-structured individual interviews were chosen because they allow deeper insights into perceptions and experiences, while ensuring the confidentiality of the reports. This approach is recommended for sensitive topics, such as ethical issues in the workplace, as it promotes a safe and reflective environment [21, 22]. The interviews were conducted virtually via Google Meet. The topic guide, developed for this study, included seven questions that explore aspects related to the understanding of ethics in pharmaceutical practice, the principles that guide the work of professionals, and everyday situations that represent ethical dilemmas or challenges.

Reflections on observed unethical behaviors, the influence of drug retail policies, external pressures on patient care, and barriers that impact the ethical conduct of pharmaceutical retail were also included. It is worth highlighting that this topic guide may suffer changes after the completion of this study. The topic guide is presented in supplementary file I. Based on previous studies and brainstorming meetings with experts on ethical practice in community pharmacies, the topic guide was constructed and used flexibly, adapting to the dynamics of the conversation [23–25]. In addition, the topic guide was pretested, focusing on assessing clarity, relevance, and comprehensiveness, in accordance with COSMIN methodological recommendations for qualitative studies [10].

The analysis of this stage included feedback from participants and iterative adjustments to maximize the understanding and relevance of the questions. All interviews were, recorded, transcribed, and analyzed using ATLAS.ti software. Data analysis occurred alongside with data collection to support iterative refinement of the interview guide and to identify preliminary patterns to the pilot. The aims were not to achieve data saturation, but rather to assess adequacy and feasibility for the future multicenter protocol. No interviews were repeated. An observer accompanied the interviews and recorded field notes focusing on contextual and nonverbal aspects of participants’ responses. These notes were incorporated into the analytical process to support interpretation of the narratives. Participants provided consent for audio recording and the use of anonymized quotations.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using inductive content analysis, following Bardin’s framework [2016]. The analytical process comprised three stages: pre-analysis, material exploration, and treatment/interpretation. During pre-analysis, transcripts were read repeatedly in a floating reading to achieve immersion in the material and to identify initial analytical directions [26, 27]. Units of meaning were identified at the level of sentences and text segments that expressed relevant experiences related to ethical dilemmas [28]. Coding was conducted inductively, without predefined categories. An open, line-by-line coding approach was applied to the transcripts, generating initial labels grounded in participants’ narratives.

A progressive codebook was developed and refined throughout the analytical process. Codes were continuously compared, merged, or reorganized according to semantic similarity, recurrence, and relevance to the research objective. Codes were subsequently grouped into categories through an iterative process of abstraction and comparison. In this study, a “category” was defined as a set of semantically related codes representing recurrent patterns of meaning across participants’ accounts, with internal coherence and conceptual distinction from other categories. The formation of categories followed principles of similarity, representativeness within the corpus, and relevance to the research question [10].

Two researchers independently coded all transcripts and developed preliminary coding structures. Disagreements were addressed through regular consensus meetings, in which coding decisions were discussed, compared, and documented. A senior researcher mediated discussions when necessary. All analytical decisions, code revisions, and category development processes were recorded in an audit trail to ensure transparency and traceability. Reflexive memoing was conducted throughout the analysis to document researchers’ assumptions, interpretive insights, and methodological decisions.

These memos informed the refinement of codes, category construction, and interpretation. ATLAS.ti software was used to support data organization, codebook management, version control, and storage of analytical materials, including memos and coding revisions. Field notes recorded during the interviews, including observations of nonverbal aspects and interactional context, were incorporated into the analytical process to support contextual interpretation of participants’ narratives. No formal member checking was conducted due to the workplace-sensitive nature of the topic and the potential risk of professional exposure or discomfort for participants.

Ethics approval

All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Research Ethics Committee of [HU/UFS number: 7.372.441]. All participants received information about the study objectives, procedures, and voluntary nature of participation and provided written informed consent prior to the interviews. Consent included permission for audio recording and for the use of anonymized quotations for research and publication purposes. Confidentiality and anonymity were ensured throughout the research process. Identifiable information was removed from transcripts, and participants were identified using alphanumeric codes.

Results

General aspects of the study

The recordings comprised a total of 145 min, with interviews lasting an average of 24 min. Participants described situations involving ethical uncertainty, gaps in ethics training, and challenges in exercising their professional roles within community pharmacy settings.

Participant characteristics

Six pharmacists accepted and were interviewed. All participants were female, with a mean age of 34 years old, and worked in community pharmacies linked to large-chain community pharmacies in the state of Sergipe. The average length of professional experience was seven years, and all of them had postgraduate degrees in areas related to Pharmaceutical Care and Clinical Pharmacy. As a pilot study with a small and context-specific sample, the findings are exploratory and intended to inform methodological refinement and preliminary mapping rather than to provide definitive conclusions.

Categories identified

Content analysis of the interview corpus identified five analytical categories: (1) conflicts with patients; (2) interference from other professionals; (3) commercial pressure and regulatory constraints; (4) insufficient ethical training; and (5) limitations of professional autonomy. These categories represent recurrent patterns of meaning across participants’ narratives and are presented in Fig. 1 and described below with illustrative quotations.

Categories identified through inductive content analysis of participant interviews

Conflicts with patients

Participants reported conflicts with patients, particularly related situations in which patients pressured pharmacists to dispense medicines in disagreement with current regulations, including requests for controlled medicines and antibiotics without a prescription.

F3: “The patient, who was a police officer, threatened to arrest me and said that I would leave the pharmacy in handcuffs because if the doctor prescribed it, he would take the medicine.”

F4: “The patient pressures the pharmacist when he wants us to do something that is not in the legislation.”

F4: “The patient went to another pharmacy, bought the medicine in an irregular way, came back with the receipt, and rubbed it in my face because I hadn’t sold it to him.”

Interference from other professionals

Participants described situations involving the participation of other professionals, including pharmacy staff and prescribers, in pharmacists’ technical decisions.

F5: “Other professionals want to interfere in the pharmacists’ work, such as physicians and dentists who prescribe medications outside the law and leave pharmacists in awkward situations with patients.”

F2: “Sometimes, the pharmacists have to shout in the pharmacy [at managers] to impose themselves in the place.”

Commercial pressure and regulatory constraints

Participants described commercial and regulatory pressures that influenced daily practice, including sales targets, performance monitoring, legislative ambiguity, and expectations from employers. Participants reported situations in which commercial expectations were present alongside professional responsibilities.

F5: “Pharmacists are often pushed to recommend unnecessary drugs just to reach sales goals.”

F1: “If I didn’t reach the pharmacy’s sales goal, I’d get fired. So, what could I do about it?”

F3: “It’s concerning to see professionals selling medicines that violate the law, even though they’re fully aware that it’s wrong.”

F5: “Although the legislation is the same, its interpretation is broad and subjective, which means that each professional behaves differently.”

Insufficient ethical training

Participants reported gaps in undergraduate ethics education and limited preparation to deal with ethical dilemmas encountered in real-world pharmacy practice.

F1: “I realize that I and some pharmacists have not yet learned enough about the legislation that guides our practice. So, how am I supposed to act if I don’t know what to follow?”

Limitations of professional autonomy

Participants reported perceived constraints on professional autonomy within the organizational structure of community pharmacies.

F2: “Insufficient academic training means that many pharmacists enter the market without ethical maturity and autonomy, making them vulnerable to the norms and pressures of the pharmaceutical retail.”

F5: “There is a constant feeling of lack of autonomy, as if the pharmacist’s role were restricted to a structure predefined by the rules of the pharmaceutical retail.”

Discussion

This pilot study provides preliminary insights into how ethical dilemmas are experienced by pharmacists working in large-chain community pharmacies, revealing tensions between professional responsibilities, commercial expectations, and regulatory uncertainties. Rather than merely confirming the presence of ethical dilemmas, the findings suggest that these challenges are embedded in everyday professional practice and associated with organizational, structural, and educational conditions.

The predominance of women observed in the sample reflects the current demographic profile of the pharmaceutical profession in Brazil, where women represent approximately 70% of registered pharmacists [29]. Beyond a descriptive characteristic, this finding may intersect with the ethical tensions identified in this study, particularly in retail environments marked by hierarchical structures, productivity pressures, and limited professional autonomy. Literature suggests that women in the medicine retail sector may face structural challenges such as reduced access to leadership positions, wage disparities, and double workloads, factors that can intensify professional vulnerability and deserve further investigation in relation to ethical decision-making. In addition, studies show that the availability of answering surveys in women is greater than in men [30, 31].

Participants’ narratives indicate that ethical tensions frequently emerge in direct interactions with patients, particularly when pharmacists are pressured to dispense medicines outside legal requirements. These situations position pharmacists at the intersection of patient expectations and regulatory responsibilities, generating interpersonal conflict and emotional burden. In this study, such experiences were often described as involving threats, confrontation, and lack of institutional backing, suggesting that ethical dilemmas are not only clinical but relational and organizational in nature [32]. Previous studies have associated similar contexts with moral distress and increased emotional strain among healthcare professionals working in retail environments [33].

Although not directly reported by participants, broader literature suggests that misinformation circulating through social and digital media may influence public perceptions of medicines and contribute to increased demand for inappropriate dispensing [34, 35]. This should be interpreted as a contextual hypothesis rather than an empirical finding of this study and warrants investigation in future research. Despite the challenges faced, maintaining the role of pharmacists in promoting the rational use of medicines is essential. These findings reinforce the central role of pharmacists in promoting the rational use of medicines, even in contexts marked by tension and uncertainty [36, 37]. Institutional strategies that strengthen professional autonomy, clarify regulatory expectations, and support public health education may help mitigate conflicts and enhance ethical practice in community pharmacies [38, 39].

The findings reveal that commercial pressures in large-chain community pharmacies operate not only as background conditions but as structuring elements of ethical decision-making. Participants described sales targets, performance monitoring, and implicit threats related to job security as factors that directly influenced professional conduct. This configuration suggests that ethical dilemmas are embedded in organizational arrangements and experienced as tensions between professional responsibility and economic survival [40, 41]. While the literature has long recognized the influence of market dynamics on pharmacy practice, the present findings illustrate how these pressures are perceived and negotiated in everyday work, particularly within large retail chains.

In this context, participants’ accounts resonate with the concept of moral distress, understood as the experience of knowing the ethically appropriate action but being constrained from performing it due to external pressures [42, 43]. Rather than an abstract phenomenon, moral distress in this study appears linked to organizational demands, commercial expectations, and regulatory ambiguity, indicating that it may be structurally produced within retail pharmacy environments.

Interference from other professionals, including pharmacy staff and prescribers, further illustrates the fragility of professional boundaries in high-volume retail settings. Participants reported situations in which technical decisions were challenged or overridden, generating interpersonal tensions and reinforcing perceptions of limited authority within the organizational hierarchy [18, 44, 45]. These findings align with literature indicating that collaborative environments may become ethically challenging when professional roles are not clearly defined or respected [7, 46]. The literature also suggests that greater attention to these conflicts may contribute to improving patient safety.

The violations of health legislation, especially in the irregular sale prescription-only medicines, revealed the coexistence of strict rules and permissive practices in the daily routine of community pharmacies. A systematic review published in 2023 in The Lancet shows that the banalization of dispensing without a prescription, as described by several interviewees, indicates not only individual failures but also a structural fragility that naturalizes unethical conduct [47]. This discrepancy between the law and the practice exposes pharmacists to constant moral conflicts, aggravated by commercial pressure and low enforcement, which creates an environment where regulatory violations become silently accepted and, in some contexts, even encouraged.

An additional recurring issue is the fragmentation and differing interpretations of pharmaceutical legislation, magnified by inconsistencies between national, regional, and local regulations. This scenario, similar to that observed in countries such as Mexico, India, the United States, and Canada, creates barriers to standardization of practice, since differences in professional scope and prescription systems hinder the uniform adoption of pharmaceutical services. The literature also points to the absence of accessible and up-to-date channels of regulatory communication in countries such as Brazil and Saudi Arabia, which increases the risk of regulatory violations and unethical decisions, especially in contexts with low levels of continuing education [24, 48]. Thus, alignment between regulatory bodies and the promotion of applied ethical education are essential to ensure legal certainty and strengthen ethical professional practice.

The promotion of unnecessary medicine purchases and the establishment of commercial goals, based on the mercantilist paradigm, expose the dilemmas of pharmacists this pilot study who work on the narrow line between subsistence and conscience [25, 49]. According to the literature, corporate forces [goals, rankings, and implicit threats of firing] constitute institutionalized moral coercion [50, 51]. Such dilemmas confront professional survival and moral duty, creating tension in community pharmacies and undermining the clinical role of professionals [32, 52].

International discussions on ethics in community pharmacy emphasize the need to balance commercial sustainability with patient-centered care and professional integrity [53, 54]. However, some authors point to the lack of a global consensus, suggesting that ethical construction is dynamic, permeated by regulatory disputes and the interests of the pharmaceutical industry [55, 56]. In the face of regulatory fragilities and market pressure, there is a need to develop institutional mechanisms that protect pharmaceutical autonomy, strengthening environments for ethical reflection and educational innovation.

Academic training in pharmacy ethics supports the consolidation of an autonomous and critical professional identity, which is essential for dealing with the dilemmas encountered in daily practice [57, 58]. The interviews revealed that ethical training is a weak point, showing that teaching still follows content-based and decontextualized models that are detached from real-life pharmaceutical care scenarios. This approach limits the development of adaptive ethical skills, which are essential for responsible practice in environments marked by commercial pressures and moral conflicts.

Although undergraduate pharmacy programs in countries such as Brazil and Nigeria often include ethics-related subjects, these components are frequently fragmented and limited in workload, which may hinder the preparation of professionals to deal with real-world dilemmas, interpersonal conflicts, and workplace pressures [19, 25, 59]. In this study, participants’ narratives reinforced this gap, particularly when describing difficulties in interpreting legislation, negotiating commercial demands, and exercising professional autonomy in practice. The result is a professional who graduates technically competent but ethically vulnerable, highlighting the need for integrated and continuous educational approaches aligned with the complexity of contemporary pharmacy practice. This perspective is consistent with literature advocating for curricula oriented toward soft skills, professionalism, and problem-solving capacities [60–63].

This pilot study also contributes methodologically by refining the interview guide, identifying feasible recruitment pathways, and supporting the development of preliminary analytic categories relevant to community pharmacy practice. Beyond operational aspects, the findings provide an initial empirical mapping of how ethical dilemmas are experienced within large-chain pharmacy settings, particularly in relation to commercial pressures, regulatory ambiguity, and constraints on professional autonomy. These elements were inform the design of a future multicenter qualitative study, which underscores the need for comparative studies across different regions and organizational models to assess the consistency of the findings.

Limitations

This study has limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the use of convenience sampling limit the diversity of perspectives captured. As a pilot study conducted in a single Brazilian state (Sergipe), the results reflect a specific organizational, cultural, and regulatory context and may not represent other regional realities. All participants worked in large-chain community pharmacies, which may not reflect the diversity of professional experiences found in independent pharmacies, public-sector services, or among pharmacists with different demographic and professional trajectories. The exclusive focus on large retail chains may have amplified experiences related to commercial pressures, organizational hierarchies, and productivity demands.

Interviews were conducted virtually, which may have influenced rapport, depth of disclosure, and the observation of nonverbal cues. In addition, given the workplace-sensitive nature of the topic, participants may have moderated their responses due to concerns about employer repercussions, contributing to potential self-censorship and social desirability bias. The transferability of the results is restricted to contexts similar to the one studied, considering the situated and contextual character of the participants’ perceptions. Despite these limitations, the study fulfills its purpose as a pilot by providing preliminary analytical categories, identifying methodological challenges, and informing the design of a future multicenter qualitative investigation.

Conclusion

This pilot study provides an initial mapping of ethical dilemmas experienced by pharmacists working in large-chain community pharmacies, highlighting tensions between healthcare and market demands related to regulatory constraints, commercial pressures, and professional autonomy. This contributes to the Latin American literature on pharmaceutical ethics by demonstrating how market-driven pressures reconfigure professional autonomy in community-based settings. The findings should be interpreted as exploratory and context-bound, contributing primarily to methodological refinement and the preparation of a future multicenter study. By identifying preliminary categories and testing qualitative procedures, this pilot study lays the groundwork for multicenter research aimed at consolidating a robust analytical framework on professional pharmaceutical ethics in Brazil.

Future studies

During the development of curriculum, ethics should be considered a cross-cutting competency, included throughout the entire training process and reinforced in the workplace through ethical supervision, clinical meetings, and institutional support. Only the integration of qualified academic education and ethical organizational culture can prepare professionals capable of resisting irregular pressure, defending moral their decisions, and ensuring pharmaceutical care committed to patients and public health. Furthermore, the results indicate the need for multicenter research that considers greater diversity in gender, socioeconomic profile, and region, broadening the understanding of the ethical and structural dimensions that permeate pharmaceutical practice in the national context.
