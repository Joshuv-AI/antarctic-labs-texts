Maximum likelihood estimation of log-affine models using detailed-balanced reaction networks
Oskar Henriksson, Carlos Améndola, Jose Israel Rodriguez, Polly Y. Yu

Abstract
A fundamental question in the field of molecular computation is what computational tasks a biochemical system can carry out. In this work, we focus on the problem of finding the maximum likelihood estimate (MLE) for log-affine models. We revisit a construction due to Gopalkrishnan of a mass-action system with the MLE as its unique positive steady state, which is based on choosing a basis for the kernel of the design matrix of the model. We extend this construction to allow for any finite spanning set of the kernel, and explore how the choice of spanning set influences the dynamics of the resulting network, including the existence of boundary steady states, the deficiency of the network, and the rate of convergence. In particular, we prove that using a Markov basis as the spanning set guarantees global stability of the MLE steady state.

A recent line of research in synthetic and systems biology is

In this work, we focus on the statistical problem of maximum likelihood estimation, for models that are

[

) and hierarchical models in contingency tables

. A log-affine model with

\mathbf{A}=({\boldsymbol{a}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{a}}_{m})\in\mathbb{Z}^{d\times m}
and a scaling vector
{\boldsymbol{c}}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{m}

where we use the notation

The problem of maximum likelihood estimation by a reaction network was first approached by Gopalkrishnan

\ker_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbf{A})

In this paper, we extend the construction to allow for any finite (and possibly linearly dependent) set

\ker_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathbf{A})
as a vector space, and show how such

One of the main questions we address concerns the global stability of the MLE steady state. We prove that the mass-action systems of our construction are always

Theorem

5.2
that the MLE is guaranteed to be a global attractor if the spanning set
\Lambda
in the construction is sufficiently large; more precisely, if
\Lambda

[
2

A key geometric feature of log-affine models that is used in the construction and our global stability proof is that

Algebraic statistics: log-affine models and Birch’s Theorem

Algebraic statistics uses tools from combinatorics, commutative algebra, invariant theory, and algebraic geometry to study problems arising from probability and statistics. In this section we review important concepts from algebraic statistics like log-affine models, Markov bases, and Birch’s Theorem. For a textbook reference, see
[
76
, Chapters 6–7]
.

A probability distribution on a finite state space of size
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
is determined by probabilities
p_{j}\geq 0
for each
j=1,\dots,m
, and can hence be seen as an element of the
(m-1)
-dimensional
probability simplex

\displaystyle\Delta_{m-1}\coloneqq\left\{(p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{m})\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{m}:\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}=1\right\}.

A subset of the probability simplex is called an
algebraic statistical model
when it is parameterized by a polynomial map or when an implicit description is given by polynomials.
The focus of this paper is a particularly well-studied class of algebraic statistical models known as
log-affine models
; see, e.g.,
[
48
,
37
,
38
]
, and
[
76
, Section 6.2]
.

Definition 2.1
.

Given a
design matrix

\mathbf{A}=\begin{pmatrix}{\boldsymbol{a}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{a}}_{2},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{a}}_{m}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d\times m}
that is assumed to contain the vector
\mathbbm{1}\coloneqq(1,1,\dots,1)
in its rowspan, and
{\boldsymbol{c}}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{m}
, the associated
log-affine model
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{A},{\boldsymbol{c}}}\coloneqq\{{\boldsymbol{p}}\in\Int(\Delta_{m-1}):\log{\boldsymbol{p}}\in\log{\boldsymbol{c}}+\row({\mathbf{A}})\}.

In the case when
{\boldsymbol{c}}=\mathbbm{1}
, the model
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{A},\mathbbm{1}}
, denoted
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{A}}
, is said to be
log-linear
.

To see that a log-affine model is an algebraic statistical model, consider the following description of
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{A},{\boldsymbol{c}}}
via the monomial map

\varphi_{\mathbf{A},{\boldsymbol{c}}}\colon\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{m},\quad{\boldsymbol{t}}\mapsto{\boldsymbol{c}}\circ{\boldsymbol{t}}^{\mathbf{A}}=(c_{1}{\boldsymbol{t}}^{{\boldsymbol{a}}_{1}},\dots,c_{m}{\boldsymbol{t}}^{{\boldsymbol{a}}_{m}})\,.

(2.1)

Its image is a scaled positive toric variety, which we denote as
\mathcal{X}_{\mathbf{A},{\boldsymbol{c}}}
(or
\mathcal{X}_{\mathbf{A}}
when
{\boldsymbol{c}}=\mathbbm{1}
), i.e.,

\mathcal{X}_{\mathbf{A},{\boldsymbol{c}}}=\left\{(c_{1}{\boldsymbol{t}}^{{\boldsymbol{a}}_{1}},\dots,c_{m}{\boldsymbol{t}}^{{\boldsymbol{a}}_{m}}):{\boldsymbol{t}}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{d}\right\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{m}.

(2.2)

The model
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{A},{\boldsymbol{c}}}
is obtained by restricting
\mathcal{X}_{\mathbf{A},{\boldsymbol{c}}}
to the probability simplex:

\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{A},{\boldsymbol{c}}}=\mathcal{X}_{\mathbf{A},{\boldsymbol{c}}}\cap\Delta_{m-1}=\left\{(c_{1}{\boldsymbol{t}}^{{\boldsymbol{a}}_{1}},\dots,c_{m}{\boldsymbol{t}}^{{\boldsymbol{a}}_{m}}):{\boldsymbol{t}}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{d},\,c_{1}{\boldsymbol{t}}^{{\boldsymbol{a}}_{1}}+\dots+c_{m}{\boldsymbol{t}}^{{\boldsymbol{a}}_{m}}=1\right\}.

After logarithmizing,
\mathcal{X}_{\mathbf{A},{\boldsymbol{c}}}
is an affine set. From this, we easily obtain an implicit description in terms of binomials; for any set
\Lambda\subseteq\ker_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbf{A})
that spans
\ker(\mathbf{A})
as a vector space, it holds that

\mathcal{X}_{\mathbf{A},{\boldsymbol{c}}}=\left\{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{m}:{\boldsymbol{c}}^{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{+}}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{-}}={\boldsymbol{c}}^{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{-}}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{+}}\text{for all}\>\>{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\Lambda\right\}.

(2.3)

Since we want to treat distributions where some entries are zero, we also consider the Euclidean closure of
\mathcal{X}_{\mathbf{A},{\boldsymbol{c}}}
in
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{m}
:

\overline{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathbf{A},{\boldsymbol{c}}}\coloneqq\overline{\left\{(c_{1}{\boldsymbol{t}}^{{\boldsymbol{a}}_{1}},\dots,c_{m}{\boldsymbol{t}}^{{\boldsymbol{a}}_{m}}):{\boldsymbol{t}}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{d}\right\}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{m}.

Obtaining an implicit description of
\overline{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathbf{A},{\boldsymbol{c}}}
is a more subtle task than for
\mathcal{X}_{\mathbf{A},{\boldsymbol{c}}}
, since we cannot logarithmize vectors with zeroes. In particular, the binomials given by a spanning set
\Lambda
as in (
2.3
) might cut out too large a subset of
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{m}
(see
Example

2.4
below).
Nevertheless, the Zariski closure of
\mathcal{X}_{\mathbf{A},{\boldsymbol{c}}}
in
\mathbb{C}^{m}
is a scaled toric variety, so it follows from the theory of toric ideals that there is an implicit description in terms of binomials, given by what is called a Markov basis.

Definition 2.2
.

A
Markov basis
for a design matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d\times m}

is a finite set

\Lambda\subseteq\ker_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbf{A})
such that the binomials

{\boldsymbol{x}}^{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{+}}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{-}}

for
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\Lambda
generate the ideal

I(\mathcal{X}_{\mathbf{A}})\subseteq\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}]

of all polynomials that vanish on
\mathcal{X}_{\mathbf{A}}
.

That such a finite generating set exists for any design matrix follows from Hilbert’s Basis Theorem and
[
22
, Proposition 1.1.9]
. The concept of Markov bases has a long history in algebraic statistics
[
2
]
, and there are many software packages available for computing a Markov basis for a given design matrix, for instance
4ti2

[
77
]
. In what follows, we will need the following well-known lemma, which follows from the definition of a Markov basis and elementary facts about lattice ideals (see, e.g.,
[
63
, Proposition 7.5]
).

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\Lambda\subseteq\ker_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbf{A})
be a Markov basis for
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d\times m}
. Then
\Lambda
is a spanning set of
\ker(\mathbf{A})
, and for any
{\boldsymbol{c}}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{m}
it holds that

\overline{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathbf{A},{\boldsymbol{c}}}=\left\{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{m}:{\boldsymbol{c}}^{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{+}}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{-}}={\boldsymbol{c}}^{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{-}}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{+}}\text{for all}\>\>{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\Lambda\right\}.

Example 2.4
.

The following will appear throughout the paper as a running example.
Here, we use it to illustrate the importance of picking a sufficiently large spanning set
\Lambda
when considering the Euclidean closure
\overline{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathbf{A},{\boldsymbol{c}}}
.
Let
d=2
and
m=4
, and consider the log-linear model given by

\mathbf{A}=\begin{pmatrix}4&2&3&1\\
0&2&1&3\end{pmatrix}.

This defines the set

\mathcal{X}_{\mathbf{A}}=\left\{(t_{1}^{4},t_{1}^{2}t_{2}^{2},t_{1}^{3}t_{2},t_{1}t_{2}^{3}):(t_{1},t_{2})\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{2}\right\}.

(2.4)

An example of a Markov basis for
\mathbf{A}
is

\Lambda_{\mathrm{mb}}=\{(1,1,-2,0),\>(0,2,-1,-1),\>(2,0,-3,1),\>(1,-1,-1,1)\},

and it follows from
Lemma

2.3
that

\overline{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathbf{A}}=\{(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{4}:x_{1}x_{2}=x_{3}^{2},\>x_{2}^{2}=x_{3}x_{4},\>x_{1}^{2}x_{4}=x_{3}^{3},\>x_{1}x_{4}=x_{2}x_{3}\}.

On the other hand, the vector space basis

\Lambda_{1}=\{(1,1,-2,0),(0,2,-1,-1)\}

is not a Markov basis, and the resulting binomials cut out a strictly larger set in
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{4}
:

\{(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{4}:x_{1}x_{2}=x_{3}^{2},\>x_{2}^{2}=x_{3}x_{4}\}=\overline{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathbf{A}}\cup\{(0,x_{2},x_{3},0):x_{2},x_{3}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\}.

In contrast, it follows from (
2.3
) that the restriction to the
positive
orthant
\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{4}
can be described by either spanning set:

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{\mathbf{A}}

\displaystyle=\{(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{4}:x_{1}x_{2}=x_{3}^{2},\>x_{2}^{2}=x_{3}x_{4},\>x_{1}^{2}x_{4}=x_{3}^{3},\>x_{1}x_{4}=x_{2}x_{3}\}

\displaystyle=\{(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{4}:x_{1}x_{2}=x_{3}^{2},\>x_{2}^{2}=x_{3}x_{4}\}.

Thus far, we have focused on parametric and implicit descriptions of log-affine models. Now we turn to
maximum likelihood estimation
.
Suppose we observe independently and identically distributed data
{\boldsymbol{u}}=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{m})\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{m}\setminus\{{\boldsymbol{0}}\}
where
u_{j}
is the number of observations of state
j
. We denote the total number of observations by
u_{+}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}u_{j}
, and the corresponding observed distribution by
\bar{{\boldsymbol{u}}}={\boldsymbol{u}}/u_{+}\in\Delta_{m-1}
. A

maximum likelihood estimate (MLE)
of
{\boldsymbol{u}}
for a model
\mathcal{M}\subseteq\Delta_{m-1}
is a distribution
\widehat{{\boldsymbol{p}}}\in\mathcal{M}
that “best explains” the data, in the sense that it maximizes the likelihood function

L_{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\colon\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{R},\quad{\boldsymbol{p}}\mapsto p_{1}^{u_{1}}\dots p_{m}^{u_{m}}.

For log-linear models, there is a well-known characterization of the (unique) MLE as the point in the model that satisfies some linear constraints. This will later be the foundation for the construction in
Section

4
. In the algebraic statistics literature, this and similar results are often referred to as Birch’s Theorem
[
65
, Section 1.2]
. For proofs of the version we give here, see
[
32
, Proposition 2.1.5]
,
[
76
, Theorem 8.2.1]
, and
[
58
, Theorem 4.8]
.

Theorem 2.5

(Birch’s Theorem)
.

Let
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d\times m}
with
\mathbbm{1}\in\row(\mathbf{A})
, let
{\boldsymbol{c}}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{m}
, and let
{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{m}
be a vector such that
\bar{{\boldsymbol{u}}}+\ker(\mathbf{A})
intersects
\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{m}
. Then there is a unique solution to the system

\widehat{{\boldsymbol{p}}}\in\overline{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathbf{A},{\boldsymbol{c}}}\quad\text{and}\quad{\mathbf{A}}\widehat{{\boldsymbol{p}}}={\mathbf{A}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{u}}}.

This solution
\widehat{{\boldsymbol{p}}}
has positive entries, and constitutes the MLE of
{\boldsymbol{u}}
for the model
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{A},{\boldsymbol{c}}}
.

From the discussion above, it is clear that the MLE is an algebraic function of the observed data. Interestingly, it is sometimes a rational function of
{\boldsymbol{u}}
, which the following example illustrates. Determining which log-affine models have this property and how the rational MLE depends on
\mathbf{A}
and
{\boldsymbol{c}}
is a central topic in algebraic statistics, and has been studied in, e.g.,
[
56
,
33
,
28
]
.

Example 2.6
.

An additional example, which we will use for figures and explicit calculations throughout the paper, is the
Hardy–Weinberg model
, given by

\mathbf{A}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&2\\
2&1&0\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad{\boldsymbol{c}}=(1,2,1).

The name alludes to the fact that this model appears in population genetics: each point
(t_{1}^{2},2t_{1}t_{2},t_{2}^{2})\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{A},{\boldsymbol{c}}}
encodes the distribution of the three possible diploid genotypes for a gene with two alleles with frequencies
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
. The MLE is a rational function of the observed data
{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}^{m}
(see, e.g.,
[
55
, Example 1.3]
):

\widehat{{\boldsymbol{p}}}=\left(\frac{(2u_{1}+u_{2})^{2}}{4(u_{1}+u_{2}+u_{3})^{2}},\frac{(u_{2}+2u_{3})(2u_{1}+u_{2})}{2(u_{1}+u_{2}+u_{3})^{2}},\frac{(u_{2}+2u_{3})^{2}}{4(u_{1}+u_{2}+u_{3})^{2}}\right).

(2.5)

The model, the positive toric variety, and the MLE are illustrated in
Figure

1
.

2.6
). The model
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{A}}
(orange) is the intersection of
\mathcal{X}_{\mathbf{A},{\boldsymbol{c}}}
(blue) and the probability simplex
\Delta_{2}
(gray).
Birch’s Theorem guarantees that for any observed distribution
\bar{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\in\Delta_{2}

Section

5.3
.
For the first model, the MLE depends rationally on the observed data
{\boldsymbol{u}}
, whereas this is not the case for the other model.

Example 2.7
.

Given two random variables with
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
states, respectively, their joint distribution is a point
{\boldsymbol{p}}=(p_{ij})_{(i,j)\in[r_{1}]\times[r_{2}]}\in\Delta_{r_{1}r_{2}-1}
, where
p_{ij}
is the probability that the first random variable is in state
i
and the second is in state
j
. If the two random variables are independent, the joint distribution belongs to the
independence model
, which is the image of the parametrization

\Delta_{r_{1}-1}\times\Delta_{r_{2}-1}\to\Delta_{r_{1}r_{2}-1},\quad((a_{1},\dots,a_{r_{1}}),(b_{1},\dots,b_{r_{2}}))\mapsto(a_{i}b_{j})_{(i,j)\in[r_{1}]\times[r_{2}]}.

This is a log-linear model, corresponding to the design matrix

\mathbf{A}_{\mathrm{ind}}(r_{1},r_{2})\coloneqq\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc ccc c ccc}1&\cdots&1\\
&&&1&\cdots&1\\
&&&&&&\ddots\\
&&&&&&&1&\cdots&1\\[5.0pt]
\hline\cr\\[-5.0pt]
1&&&1&&&&1&&\\
&\ddots&&&\ddots&&\cdots&&\ddots&\\
&&1&&&1&&&&1\\
\end{array}\right)\in\mathbb{Z}^{(r_{1}+r_{2})\times r_{1}r_{2}}.

This model has a unique minimal Markov basis
[
32
, Proposition 1.2.2]
given by

\pm({\boldsymbol{e}}_{ij}+{\boldsymbol{e}}_{k\ell}-{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i\ell}-{\boldsymbol{e}}_{kj}),\quad\text{ for }\>1\leq i<k\leq r_{1},\>\>1\leq j<\ell\leq r_{2}

(2.6)

where
{\boldsymbol{e}}_{ij}
is the standard basis vector of
\mathbb{R}^{r_{1}r_{2}}
corresponding to the
p_{ij}
coordinate.
It can be verified that the following rational expression in
{\boldsymbol{u}}
satisfies the conditions of Birch’s Theorem, and therefore is the MLE:

\widehat{p}_{ij}=\frac{\big(\sum_{\ell=1}^{r_{2}}u_{i\ell}\big)\big(\sum_{k=1}^{r_{1}}u_{kj}\big)}{\big(\sum_{k=1}^{r_{1}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{r_{2}}u_{k\ell}\big)^{2}}.

(2.7)

.

[

, Example 4]
, which has a

Reaction network theory: detailed-balanced systems

Reaction network theory is the study of certain families of parametrized systems of ordinary differential equations (ODEs) encoded by directed graphs. In this section, we review some basic notions in reaction network theory, including the notion of
detailed-balanced
steady states. For a more comprehensive introduction to the field, see the review articles
[
47
,
31
,
85
,
36
]
, and the textbook
[
34
]
.

Definition 3.1
.

A
reaction network
with species
\{\textsf{X}_{1},\ldots,\textsf{X}_{m}\}
is a simple directed graph
G=(V,E)
with no isolated vertices, where the vertices are points in
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{m}
(interpreted as formal linear combinations of the species), referred to as
complexes
, and the edges, denoted
{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}
for
{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}\in V
, are referred to as
reactions
of the network.
A reaction network
G
is said to be
reversible
if every reaction is part of a reversible pair, i.e.,
{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}\in E
if and only if
{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\in E
. We write
{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\rightleftharpoons{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}
for such a reaction pair.

The goal of reaction network theory is to model the time evolution of the concentrations
{\boldsymbol{x}}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})
of the species
\textsf{X}_{1},\ldots,\textsf{X}_{m}
of the network under some kinetic assumptions. One of the most common,
mass-action kinetics
, is based on the first principle that the reaction rate is proportional to the collision probability of the reacting species.

Definition 3.2
.

A
mass-action system
is a weighted directed graph
(G,{\boldsymbol{{k}}})
, where
G
is a reaction network, and
{\boldsymbol{{k}}}=({k}_{ij})_{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}\in E}
is a tuple of positive real numbers called
rate constants
, indexed by the reactions. The induced dynamics of a mass-action system
(G,{\boldsymbol{k}})
is given by the ODEs

\displaystyle\frac{d{\boldsymbol{x}}}{dt}=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}\in E}{k}_{ij}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}}({\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}-{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}),

(3.1)

where the right-hand side is a vector of polynomials that we will denote
{\boldsymbol{f}}_{G,{\boldsymbol{k}}}({\boldsymbol{x}})
.
For an initial value
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{m}
, we denote the corresponding trajectory of (
3.1
) by
{\boldsymbol{x}}(t;{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0})
or simply
{\boldsymbol{x}}(t)
.

The nonnegative and positive orthants are
forward-invariant
with respect to (
3.1
)
[
81
,
74
]
. In other words, if
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{m}
, then
{\boldsymbol{x}}(t;{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0})\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{m}
for all
t\geq 0
for which the trajectory is defined, and, analogously, if
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{m}
, then
{\boldsymbol{x}}(t;{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0})\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{m}
for all
t\geq 0
for which the trajectory is defined.

The
stoichiometric subspace
of a reaction network
G
is the linear subspace

S\coloneqq\spn_{\mathbb{R}}\{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}-{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}:{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}\in E\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}.

Each initial value
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}
defines a
stoichiometric compatibility class

({\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}+S)_{\geq 0}\coloneqq\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}+{\boldsymbol{s}}:{\boldsymbol{s}}\in S\}\cap\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{m},

where the part intersecting the positive orthant will be denoted
({\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}+S)_{>0}
.
Since the right-hand side of (
3.1
) lies in
S
for any
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{m}
, the subset
({\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}+S)_{>0}
is also forward-invariant.
If
\dim(S)<m
, the nontrivial stoichiometric compatibility classes are given by
conservation laws
, which are linear first integrals, represented by a linear system
\mathbf{W}{\boldsymbol{x}}=\mathbf{W}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}
, where the rows of
\mathbf{W}
span
S^{\perp}
, and
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}
is the initial state. The linear forms
\mathbf{W}{\boldsymbol{x}}
are referred to as
conserved quantities
, and
\mathbf{W}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}
as
total amounts
.

Example 3.3
.

Consider a reaction network consisting of two reversible reactions:

2\,\textsf{X}_{3}
\textsf{X}_{1}+\textsf{X}_{2}
,
{k}_{12}
{k}_{21}
\textsf{X}_{3}+\textsf{X}_{4}
2\,\textsf{X}_{2}
.
{k}_{34}
{k}_{43}

(3.2)

Its associated ODE system is

\displaystyle\frac{d{\boldsymbol{x}}}{dt}=\begin{pmatrix}[l]k_{12}x_{3}^{2}-k_{21}x_{1}x_{2}\\
k_{12}x_{3}^{2}-k_{21}x_{1}x_{2}+2k_{34}x_{3}x_{4}-2k_{43}x_{2}^{2}\\
-2k_{12}x_{3}^{2}+2k_{21}x_{1}x_{2}-k_{34}x_{3}x_{4}+k_{43}x_{2}^{2}\\
-k_{34}x_{3}x_{4}+k_{43}x_{2}^{2}\end{pmatrix}.

Its stoichiometric subspace
S
is the two-dimensional subspace spanned by
(1,1,-2,0)
and
(0,2,-1,-1)
.
Two conservation laws are expected since
\codim(S)=2
; for example,

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}4&2&3&1\\
0&2&1&3\end{pmatrix}({\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0})={\boldsymbol{0}},

as the rows of the matrix span
S^{\perp}
.
One can also confirm the conservation laws by checking that
4\dot{x}_{1}+2\dot{x}_{2}+3\dot{x}_{3}+\dot{x}_{4}=0
and
2\dot{x}_{2}+\dot{x}_{3}+3\dot{x}_{4}=0

Mass-action systems are capable of very diverse dynamics; they can display oscillation, have multiple stable steady states in the same stoichiometric compatibility class, and even exhibit chaotic dynamics (e.g.,
[
39
, Figure 21]
). That said, some classes of mass-action systems are known to be particularly stable. The most well-known are
detailed-balanced systems
, which originated from Boltzmann
[
10
,
11
]
.

Definition 3.4
.

Let
(G,{\boldsymbol{{k}}})
be a mass-action system in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
with associated ODEs
\dot{{\boldsymbol{x}}}={\boldsymbol{f}}_{G,{\boldsymbol{{k}}}}({\boldsymbol{x}})
.

(a)

A point
\widehat{{\boldsymbol{x}}}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{m}
is a
steady state
if
{\boldsymbol{f}}_{G,{\boldsymbol{{k}}}}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{x}}})={\boldsymbol{0}}
. It is a
positive steady state
if
\widehat{{\boldsymbol{x}}}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{m}
; otherwise it is a
boundary steady state
. The set of all positive steady states is denoted
\mathcal{E}_{G,{\boldsymbol{{k}}}}
.

(b)

If the network
G
is reversible, then a positive steady state
\widehat{{\boldsymbol{x}}}
is
detailed-balanced
if

{k}_{ij}\,\widehat{{\boldsymbol{x}}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}}={k}_{ji}\,\widehat{{\boldsymbol{x}}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}}\text{ for all }{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\rightleftharpoons{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}.

(3.3)

The set of positive detailed-balanced steady states is denoted
\mathcal{D}_{G,{\boldsymbol{{k}}}}
.

If a mass-action system
(G,{\boldsymbol{k}})
has a positive steady state that is detailed-balanced, then
all positive steady states are detailed-balanced; therefore, we say
(G,{\boldsymbol{{k}}})
is a
detailed-balanced mass-action system

if
\mathcal{D}_{G,{\boldsymbol{{k}}}}
is nonempty.
This, and other key properties of detailed-balanced systems, are collected in the following theorem.
We note that these results were originally proved for the more general class of
complex-balanced
mass-action systems (see
[
52
]
for a definition and precise statements).

Theorem 3.5

(
[
52
]
)
.

Let
(G,{\boldsymbol{{k}}})
be a reversible mass-action system with stoichiometric subspace
S
, and suppose there exists a detailed-balanced steady state
\widehat{{\boldsymbol{x}}}\in\mathcal{D}_{G,{\boldsymbol{{k}}}}
. Then the following holds:

(a)

\mathcal{D}_{G,{\boldsymbol{{k}}}}=\mathcal{E}_{G,{\boldsymbol{{k}}}}
.

(b)

The set of positive steady states is given by

\mathcal{E}_{G,{\boldsymbol{{k}}}}=\{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{m}:\log{\boldsymbol{x}}-\log\widehat{{\boldsymbol{x}}}\in S^{\perp}\}=\{\widehat{{\boldsymbol{x}}}\circ{\boldsymbol{t}}^{\mathbf{W}}:{\boldsymbol{t}}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{d}\}

for any matrix
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times m}
whose rows span
S^{\perp}
.

(c)

There is exactly one steady state in every positive stoichiometric compatibility class
({\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}+S)_{>0}
.

(d)

The unique positive steady state in
({\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}+S)_{>0}
is asymptotically stable within
({\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}+S)_{>0}
.

In the language of
Section

2
, part
(b)
says that
\mathcal{E}_{G,{\boldsymbol{k}}}=\mathcal{X}_{\mathbf{W},\widehat{{\boldsymbol{x}}}}
, and part

corresponds to Birch’s Theorem (
Theorem

2.5
), with the observed distribution playing the role of
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}
and the MLE playing the role of the detailed-balanced steady state
\widehat{{\boldsymbol{x}}}
.

Remark 3.6
.

The strictly convex function

for the system (

Kullback–Leibler divergence

\operatorname{KL}(\bar{{\boldsymbol{u}}}||{\boldsymbol{p}})
to the empirical distribution

.
See
[

, Section 2]
and
[

, Proposition 11]
.

An important property that we consider in the next section is
global convergence
, and we therefore devote the remainder of this section to discussing global convergence properties of detailed-balanced systems.

A long-standing conjecture in reaction network theory is that the unique positive steady state in each stoichiometric compatibility class is not only locally stable, but
globally stable

[
54
]
. This conjecture is referred to as the
Global Attractor Conjecture
, and has a rich history (see
[
26
,
27
]
). It has been proven for several classes of networks, including networks where the underlying graph is strongly connected
[
8
,
12
]
; other proven cases involve graph-theoretic and geometric assumptions on the network, see, e.g.,
[
66
,
26
,
44
]
.

It has been proven that if a positive steady state of a complex-balanced system fails to be a global attractor, then there exist trajectories that approach the boundary
[
72
,
9
,
70
]
. Below, we state this result in the special case of detailed-balanced systems. Recall that the
\omega
-limit set
of a trajectory
{\boldsymbol{x}}(t;{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0})
is the set of subsequential limit points, i.e.,

\omega({\boldsymbol{x}}_{0})=\left\{{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\text{${\boldsymbol{y}}=\lim_{n\to\infty}{\boldsymbol{x}}(t_{n};{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0})$ for some increasing sequence $(t_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}$}\right\}.

Proposition 3.8

(
[
72
, Theorem 3.2]
)
.

Let
(G,{\boldsymbol{{k}}})
be a detailed-balanced mass-action system, and let
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{m}
.
Then the
\omega
-limit set
\omega({\boldsymbol{x}}_{0})
either consists of boundary steady states or the unique positive steady state in
({\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}+S)_{>0}
.

A network is said to
lack relevant boundary steady states
if no stoichiometric compatibility class that intersects the positive orthant contains a steady state on the boundary of the nonnegative orthant. A well-known consequence of
Proposition

3.8
is that this property implies global convergence.

Proposition 3.9

(
[
72
]
)
.

Let
(G,{\boldsymbol{k}})
be a detailed-balanced mass-action system that lacks relevant boundary steady states. Then for any
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{m}
, the trajectory
{\boldsymbol{x}}(t;{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0})
converges to the unique positive steady state
\widehat{{\boldsymbol{x}}}
in
\mathcal{E}_{G,{\boldsymbol{k}}}\cap({\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}+S)_{>0}
.

A useful condition to rule out relevant boundary steady states comes from the theory of siphons, developed in
[
9
]
and expanded in several subsequent works, e.g.,
[
70
,
29
,
1
,
60
]
.

Definition 3.10
.

A
siphon
in a reaction network
G=(V,E)
with species
\{\textsf{X}_{1},\ldots,\textsf{X}_{m}\}
is a set of indices
Z\subseteq[m]
such that for every
i\in Z
and every reaction
{\boldsymbol{y}}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\prime}\in E
with
y^{\prime}_{i}>0
, there exists
j\in Z
such that
y_{j}>0
.

Intuitively, a siphon keeps track of which species can simultaneously have zero concentrations at equilibrium.
It turns out that there cannot exist relevant boundary steady states if “mass is conserved” within each minimal siphon, in the following sense.

Proposition 3.11

(
[
9
]
,
[
60
, Proposition 2(v)]
)
.

Consider a network with stoichiometric subspace
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Suppose that for every inclusion-minimal siphon
Z\subseteq[m]
, there exists
{\boldsymbol{w}}\in S^{\perp}\cap\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{m}
with
\supp({\boldsymbol{w}})=Z
. Then the network lacks relevant boundary steady states.

A network that satisfies the condition in
Proposition

3.11
is said to be
structurally persistent
.

We end this section by using the notion of siphons to extend
Theorem

3.5
(a)
and
Proposition

3.9
to also account for the dynamics on the boundary
\partial\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{m}
, which we will need in
Section

5.1
.

Consider the set of nonnegative steady states

\mathcal{E}_{G,{\boldsymbol{{k}}}}^{\geq 0}\coloneqq\left\{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{m}:{\boldsymbol{f}}_{G,{\boldsymbol{{k}}}}({\boldsymbol{x}})={\boldsymbol{0}}\right\}.

We say
\widehat{{\boldsymbol{x}}}\in\mathcal{E}_{G,{\boldsymbol{{k}}}}^{\geq 0}
is a
nonnegative detailed-balanced steady state
if it satisfies (
3.3
); we denote the set of such points by
\mathcal{D}_{G,{\boldsymbol{{k}}}}^{\geq 0}
.
The following is the detailed-balanced analog of
[
16
, Theorem 3.1]
.

Proposition 3.12
.

Let
(G,{\boldsymbol{k}})
be a detailed balanced mass-action system. Then the set of nonnegative steady states and the set of nonnegative detailed-balanced states coincide, i.e.,
\mathcal{E}_{G,{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\geq 0}=\mathcal{D}_{G,{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\geq 0}
.

Proof.

The inclusion
\mathcal{D}_{G,{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\geq 0}\subseteq\mathcal{E}_{G,{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\geq 0}
is immediate. To prove the inclusion
\mathcal{E}_{G,{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\geq 0}\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{G,{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\geq 0}
, we begin by introducing some notation. For a set
Z\subseteq[m]
, let
G^{Z}
denote the subnetwork of
G
obtained by removing all reactions where a species with index in
Z
appears as a reactant, and let
{\boldsymbol{k}}^{Z}
be the restriction of
{\boldsymbol{k}}
to the reactions of
G^{Z}
. For any vector
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{m}
and
Z\subseteq[m]
, we denote by
{\boldsymbol{x}}^{Z}
the vector in
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{m}
where
x^{Z}_{i}=x_{i}
for all
i\not\in Z
, and
x^{Z}_{i}=0
for all
i\in Z
. Finally, by a slight abuse of notation, we view the sets of positive steady states
\mathcal{E}_{G^{Z},{\boldsymbol{k}}^{Z}}
and positive detailed-balanced steady state
\mathcal{D}_{G^{Z},{\boldsymbol{k}}^{Z}}
of the subnetwork as being embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, by supplementing with
\widehat{x}_{i}=0
for any
i\in Z
. Then it holds that

\mathcal{E}_{G,{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\geq 0}=\bigcup_{Z\subseteq[m]}\mathcal{E}_{G^{Z},{\boldsymbol{k}}^{Z}}.

We claim that for every
Z\subseteq[m]
such that
\mathcal{E}_{G^{Z},{\boldsymbol{k}}^{Z}}
is nonempty,
Z
must be a siphon. Indeed, if
Z
is not a siphon, then
G^{Z}
has a reaction where some species
\textsf{X}_{i}
with
i\in Z
is formed, but no species
\textsf{X}_{j}
with
j\in Z
are reactants, which means that this reaction appears in
G^{Z}
. Since
G^{Z}
by construction does not have any reactions where
\textsf{X}_{i}
is consumed, this implies that
\mathcal{E}_{G^{Z},{\boldsymbol{k}}^{Z}}
is empty.

Suppose
Z\subseteq[m]
is a siphon. Then for every reversible reaction pair
{\boldsymbol{y}}\rightleftharpoons{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\prime}
that appears in
G^{Z}
, it holds that neither
{\boldsymbol{y}}
nor
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\prime}
involves any species with index in
Z
. Hence,
\widehat{{\boldsymbol{x}}}^{Z}\in\mathcal{D}_{G^{Z},{\boldsymbol{k}}^{Z}}
for every
\widehat{{\boldsymbol{x}}}\in\mathcal{D}_{G,{\boldsymbol{k}}}
. This implies that
\mathcal{D}_{G^{Z},{\boldsymbol{k}}^{Z}}
is nonempty, and therefore equal to
\mathcal{E}_{G^{Z},{\boldsymbol{k}}^{Z}}
by
Theorem

3.5
(a)
.
Thus we obtain

\mathcal{E}^{\geq 0}_{G,{\boldsymbol{{k}}}}=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}Z\text{ is a}\\
\text{siphon}\end{subarray}}\mathcal{E}_{G^{Z},{\boldsymbol{{k}}}^{Z}}=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}Z\text{ is a}\\
\text{siphon}\end{subarray}}\mathcal{D}_{G^{Z},{\boldsymbol{{k}}}^{Z}}\subseteq\mathcal{D}^{\geq 0}_{G,{\boldsymbol{{k}}}}.\qed

The following extends
Proposition

3.9
to the case when the system is initialized at
\partial\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{m}
.

Proposition 3.13
.

Let
(G,{\boldsymbol{k}})
be a detailed-balanced mass-action system that lacks relevant boundary steady states, and let
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{m}
be such that
({\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}+S)_{>0}
is nonempty. Then the trajectory
{\boldsymbol{x}}(t;{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0})
converges to the unique positive steady state
\widehat{{\boldsymbol{x}}}
in
\mathcal{E}_{G,{\boldsymbol{k}}}\cap({\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}+S)_{>0}

Proof.

The desired claim follows from

3.9
if the trajectory
{\boldsymbol{x}}(t;{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0})
enters the positive orthant at some

. Suppose for a contradiction that the trajectory is fully contained in
\partial\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{m}
, and consider the (then nonempty) set

It follows from
[

that

is a siphon. Then, using the notation in the proof of

,
(G^{Z},{\boldsymbol{k}}^{Z})
is a detailed-balanced subnetwork, with

4
Reaction network representation of log-affine models

The goal of this section is to connect the following two scenarios:

•

Algebraic statistics: We saw in
Theorem

2.5
that for a log-affine model
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{A},{\boldsymbol{c}}}
, the unique MLE for a vector of counts
{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{m}
is given by
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{A},{\boldsymbol{c}}}\cap(\bar{{\boldsymbol{u}}}+\ker(\mathbf{A}))
.

•

Reaction network theory: We saw in
Theorem

3.5

that for a detailed-balanced system with positive steady state set
\mathcal{E}_{G,{\boldsymbol{{k}}}}
and an initial value
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{m}

\mathcal{E}_{G,{\boldsymbol{{k}}}}\cap({\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}+S)
.

More specifically, we will construct a mass-action system for which the set of positive steady states is
\mathcal{X}_{\mathbf{A},{\boldsymbol{c}}}
and the stoichiometric subspace is

. Hence, the MLE of the statistical model
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{A},{\boldsymbol{c}}}
for an observed distribution

will be the unique steady state in
(\bar{{\boldsymbol{u}}}+S)_{>0}
.

A key step in the construction will be to choose a finite set
\Lambda\subseteq\ker_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbf{A})
that spans
\ker(\mathbf{A})
in the vector space sense. This generalizes the construction in
[

\Lambda
is a
\mathbb{Z}
-basis for
\ker(\mathbf{A})
and
{\boldsymbol{c}}=\mathbbm{1}
.
Recall that
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{+}
and
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{-}
are respectively the nonnegative and nonpositive parts of a vector
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
.

Construction 4.1
.

For a finite set of integer vectors
\Lambda\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{m}
that span
\ker(\mathbf{A})
, and a vector

, we define the

to be the collection

\gamma^{-}_{1}\textsf{X}_{1}+\cdots+\gamma^{-}_{m}\textsf{X}_{m}
\gamma^{+}_{1}\textsf{X}_{1}+\cdots+\gamma^{+}_{m}\textsf{X}_{m}

{\boldsymbol{c}}^{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{+}}
{\boldsymbol{c}}^{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{-}}
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\Lambda
,

and denote by
G_{\Lambda}
the underlying reaction network.
The associated system of ODEs for
G_{\Lambda,{\boldsymbol{c}}}

\frac{d{\boldsymbol{x}}}{dt}=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\Lambda}\left({\boldsymbol{c}}^{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{+}}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{-}}-{\boldsymbol{c}}^{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{-}}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{+}}\right){\boldsymbol{\gamma}}.

We begin by showing that the set of positive steady states of

is
\mathcal{X}_{\mathbf{A},{\boldsymbol{c}}}

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d\times m}
with
\mathbbm{1}\in\row(A)
, and
{\boldsymbol{c}}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{m}
, and let
\Lambda\subseteq\ker_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbf{A})
be a finite spanning set for

in
Construction

4.1
is detailed-balanced, and the set of positive steady states is

Proof.

The network
G_{\Lambda}
is reversible by construction, and the defining condition (

{\boldsymbol{c}}^{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{+}}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{-}}={\boldsymbol{c}}^{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{-}}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{+}}
for all
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\Lambda

\mathcal{X}_{\mathbf{A},{\boldsymbol{c}}}
by (

\mathcal{X}_{\mathbf{A},{\boldsymbol{c}}}
is nonempty, it follows that

\mathcal{X}_{\mathbf{A},{\boldsymbol{c}}}

(a)
.
∎

Remark 4.3
.

By construction, the rows of
\mathbf{A}
form a spanning set for

\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{A}
in the notation from

In statistics, these total amounts are commonly referred to as the

5
Impact of the spanning set on the dynamics

A key step in
Construction

4.1
is choosing a finite spanning set
\Lambda\subseteq\ker_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbf{A})
for
\ker(\mathbf{A})
. In this section, we discuss how this choice influences the dynamical properties of the mass-action system
G_{\Lambda,{\boldsymbol{c}}}
. We will focus on three properties: global stability (
Section

5.1
), deficiency (
Section

5.2
), and the rate of convergence (
Section

5.3
). The two main options we will consider are the following:

•

If
\Lambda

•

If

5.1
Global stability and boundary steady states

An important question about our maximum-likelihood-estimating networks from the point of view of computational reliability is whether we have
global stability
, in the sense that the trajectory of the initial value problem in
Theorem

4.6
converges to the MLE
\widehat{{\boldsymbol{p}}}
for any vector of counts
{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{m}

As pointed out in
Section

3

, but is only proven for special classes of networks, and does not directly address the case when
{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\partial\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{m}

In this section, we will focus on the property of

(discussed at the end of
Section

3
) as a sufficient condition for global convergence. For

, this is equivalent to the system

\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\Lambda}\big({\boldsymbol{c}}^{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{+}}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{-}}-{\boldsymbol{c}}^{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{-}}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{+}})\cdot{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}={\boldsymbol{0}},\qquad\mathbf{A}{\boldsymbol{x}}=\mathbf{A}{\boldsymbol{u}}

having no solution in
\partial\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{m}
for any
{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{m}
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d\times m}
with
\mathbbm{1}\in\row(\mathbf{A})
, and
{\boldsymbol{c}}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{m}

in the model

Proof.

This is a direct consequence of
Proposition

3.13

4.6
.
∎

It is natural to ask whether the spanning set

5.2
Deficiency

A challenge in the chemical implementation of
G_{\Lambda,{\boldsymbol{c}}}
is fine-tuning the rate constants to the values prescribed by
Construction

4.1
. Because of this, an important question is to what extent the dynamical properties of
G_{\Lambda,{\boldsymbol{c}}}
are preserved under perturbations to the rate constants.

In this section, we will focus on the particularly well-behaved scenario of deficiency zero. The
deficiency
of a (weakly) reversible reaction network
G=(V,E)
is
\delta:=|V|-\ell-\dim(S)
, where
\ell
is the number of connected components and
S
is the stoichiometric subspace. By
[
23
]
,
\delta
is the codimension of the subset of the rate constants for which
G
is complex-balanced (and hence satisfies analogous properties as those given in
3.5
and
3.13
). In particular, if
\delta=0
, the Deficiency Zero Theorem
[
53
]
states that the network is complex-balanced for
all
values of the rate constants. For a more in-depth introduction to deficiency theory, we refer to
[
34
]
.

Theorem 5.5
.

Let
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d\times m}
and let
\Lambda\subseteq\ker_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbf{A})
be a finite spanning set of
\ker(\mathbf{A})
.

(a)

The deficiency of
G_{\Lambda}
is
\delta=|\Lambda|-r-\dim(\ker(\mathbf{A}))
, where
|\Lambda|
is the cardinality of
\Lambda
and
r
is the cycle rank (the number of independent cycles) in the underlying undirected graph of
G_{\Lambda}
.

(b)

If
\Lambda
is a vector space basis for
\ker(\mathbf{A})
, then the undirected graph of
G_{\Lambda}
is acyclic and
\delta=0
.

Proof.

Part (a) follows from the general graph-theoretic fact that the number of edges minus the cycle rank equals the number of vertices minus the number of connected components. Part (b) follows from part (a), combined with the fact that
\delta
cannot be negative.
∎

Note that
G_{\Lambda}
has deficiency zero precisely when the cycle rank equals the number of vectors in
|\Lambda|
that are redundant for spanning
\ker(\mathbf{A})
.
When this property of having deficiency zero coincides with the property of lacking relevant boundary steady states discussed in
Section

5.1
(e.g., when
\Lambda
is both a vector space basis and Markov basis), then the network displays very robust dynamics.

Corollary 5.6
.

Suppose that
\Lambda\subseteq\ker_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbf{A})
is such that
\delta=0
and
G_{\Lambda}
lacks relevant boundary steady states for all rate constants. Then
G_{\Lambda}
has a unique and globally stable positive steady state in each stoichiometric compatibility class for every choice of rate constants.

Proof.

This follows by the complex-balanced analog of
Proposition

3.13
, combined with the Deficiency Zero Theorem
[
53
, Theorem 4A]
.
∎

5.3
Rate of convergence

In the neighborhood of a hyperbolic equilibrium, the rate of convergence is dictated by the eigenvalue
\lambda_{\max}
with the least negative real part for the Jacobian matrix
[
19
]
, assuming all its eigenvalues have negative real parts. Convergence along the eigendirection for
\lambda_{\max}
is the slowest; thus the more negative
\mathrm{Re}(\lambda_{\max})
is, the faster the system converges to equilibrium, with a rate of convergence
-\mathrm{Re}(\lambda_{\max})
. For detailed-balanced systems, we only consider its nonzero eigenvalues, since the detailed-balanced steady state is hyperbolic with respect to its stoichiometric compatibility class
[
71
]
.

In this section, we explore how the rate of convergence depends on the choice of the spanning set, using as two examples: the binary 4-cycle (
Example

2.8
), and the independence model with design matrix
\mathbf{A}_{\mathrm{ind}}(10,10)
(
Example

2.7
).
(Similar observations were made for
\mathbf{A}_{\mathrm{ind}}(r_{1},r_{2})
with
3\leq r_{1}\leq 10
and
2\leq r_{2}\leq 10
, and for scaling factors other than
\mathbbm{1}
.) Each of these models has the property that there is a unique Markov basis
\Lambda_{\mathrm{mb}}\subseteq\ker_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbf{A})
of minimal cardinality; see the database
Markov-Bases.de

[
77
]
and
[
32
, Proposition 1.2.2]
.

In our experiments, we first constructed a strictly increasing sequence of subsets

\Lambda_{1}\subsetneq\Lambda_{2}\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq\Lambda_{9}\subsetneq\Lambda_{10}

where
\Lambda_{1}
is a vector space basis for
\ker(\mathbf{A})
, and
\Lambda_{10}
has the same cardinality as
\Lambda_{\mathrm{mb}}
. Then for each
\Lambda_{i}
and for a random choice of initial conditions

We conclude this section by proving that the numerical observation concerning the independence model holds more generally: the detailed-balanced system constructed using the unique minimal Markov basis always has global rate of convergence
1
. In fact, we give explicit analytical solutions for the independence model, which shows that the rate of convergence is
globally
exponential with rate
1
, not just near the MLE. This is a highly desirable feature from a molecular computation point of view, since it means that the system will converge to the MLE with a rate that is independent of the input (see, e.g., the discussion in
[
7
]

4.7
for another example of when a Markov basis gives rise to a global rate of convergence. An interesting problem for future work is to investigate whether we can always find closed form formulas for models where the MLE depends rationally on the observed distribution.

Proposition 5.8
.

Consider the initial value problem constructed by
Construction

4.1
using the unique minimal Markov basis (
2.6
) for the independence model with
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
states:

\dot{p}_{ij}=\sum_{k\in[r_{1}]\setminus\{i\}}\sum_{\ell\in[r_{2}]\setminus\{j\}}(p_{i\ell}p_{kj}-p_{ij}p_{k\ell}),\quad p_{ij}(0)=\bar{u}_{ij},\quad(i,j)\in[r_{1}]\times[r_{2}].

(5.1)

The unique solution to (
5.1
) is given explicitly by

p_{ij}(t)=\frac{1}{u_{++}^{2}}\Big(u_{i+}u_{+j}+(u_{ij}u_{++}-u_{i+}u_{+j})e^{-t}\Big),

(5.2)

where

u_{+j}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{r_{1}}u_{ij},\quad u_{i+}\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{r_{2}}u_{ij},\quad\text{and}\quad u_{++}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{r_{1}}\sum_{j=1}^{r_{2}}u_{ij}.

Proof.

Observe that

\lim_{t\to\infty}p_{ij}(t)=\frac{u_{i+}u_{+j}}{u_{++}^{2}},

which is consistent with the known MLE (
2.7
) for independence models. Furthermore, we have
p_{ij}(0)=\frac{u_{ij}}{u_{++}}=\bar{u}_{ij}
, so it suffices to show that substituting (
5.2
) into the right-hand side of the ODEs (
5.1
), recovers the derivatives
\dot{p}_{ij}
, which are given by

\dot{p}_{ij}=\frac{(u_{i+}u_{+j}-u_{ij}u_{++})}{u_{++}^{2}}e^{-t}.

The proof proceeds by direct computation, involving
the identities

\sum_{k\in[r_{1}]\setminus\{i\}}\sum_{\ell\in[r_{2}]\setminus\{j\}}u_{kl}=u_{++}-u_{i+}-u_{+j}+u_{ij}

and

u_{i+}u_{+j}-u_{ij}u_{++}=\sum_{k\in[r_{1}]\setminus\{i\}}\sum_{\ell\in[r_{2}]\setminus\{j\}}(u_{i\ell}u_{kj}-u_{ij}u_{k\ell}).\qed

6
Conclusion and outlook

In this work, we describe a method that, for a given statistical log-affine model
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{A},{\boldsymbol{c}}}
, returns a mass-action system
G_{\Lambda,{\boldsymbol{c}}}
with the property that the MLE
\widehat{{\boldsymbol{p}}}
of
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{A},{\boldsymbol{c}}}
with respect to an observed distribution
\bar{{\boldsymbol{u}}}
is the unique positive steady state of
G_{\Lambda,{\boldsymbol{c}}}
in the stoichiometric compatibility class of
\bar{{\boldsymbol{u}}}
.

Throughout the paper, we see examples of how
G_{\Lambda,{\boldsymbol{c}}}
is dynamically more well-behaved if
\Lambda
is chosen as a Markov basis for the design matrix
\mathbf{A}
.
In particular, for a Markov basis, there are no relevant boundary steady states, which guarantees global convergence to the MLE.
Taking
\Lambda
to be a
\mathbb{Z}
-basis is not enough to rule out the existence of relevant boundary steady states, as shown in
Example

5.4
. Furthermore, the numerical experiments presented in
Section

5.3
suggest that convergence to the MLE is faster when using a Markov basis than a vector space basis. For example, for independence models, the minimal Markov bases always give global rates of convergence
1
, while with a vector space basis, the system has components whose time-scales are as high as
10^{3}
, which could lead to unreliable computational performance.

From a synthetic biology point of view, these observations suggest an interesting trade-off between desirable dynamics and feasibility of chemically implementing a given network, since a Markov basis is typically much larger than a vector space basis for
\ker(\mathbf{A})
.
For instance, in the independence model case, the smallest Markov basis for
\mathbf{A}_{\mathrm{ind}}(r_{1},r_{2})
has
\binom{r_{1}}{2}\binom{r_{2}}{2}
elements, while the dimension of the kernel is
(r_{1}-1)(r_{2}-1)
. An interesting direction for future research is therefore to find systematic ways of choosing spanning sets
\Lambda
that are not Markov bases, but still give rise to well-behaved dynamics, similarly to what was achieved in
Example

5.4
(c)
.
