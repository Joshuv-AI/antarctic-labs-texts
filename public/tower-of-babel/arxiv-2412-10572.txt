Revisiting The Rédei-Berge Symmetric Functions via Matrix Algebra
John Irving, Mohamed Omar

Abstract
. We revisit the Rédei-Berge symmetric function 𝒰 D \mathcal{U}_{D} for digraphs D D , a specialization of Chow’s path-cycle symmetric function. Through the lens of matrix algebra, we consolidate and expand on the work of Chow, Grinberg and Stanley, and Lass concerning the resolution of 𝒰 D \mathcal{U}_{D} in the power sum and Schur bases. Along the way we also revisit various results on Hamiltonian paths in digraphs.



1.
Introduction

Let
D
be a digraph on vertex set
[n]:=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
and let
\pi=\pi_{1}\cdots\pi_{n}\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, where here
\pi_{i}
is the image
\pi(i)
of
i
under
\pi
. An index
i\in[n-1]
is said to be a
D
-descent
of
\pi
if
(\pi_{i},\pi_{i+1})
is a directed edge of
D
. Let
\mathrm{Des}_{D}(\pi)
denote the set of all
D
-descents of
\pi
. The
Rédei-Berge symmetric function
of
D
is defined by

\mathcal{U}_{D}:=\sum_{\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}F_{\mathrm{Des}_{D}(\pi)},

(1)

where the expansion is in terms of the fundamental quasisymmetric functions

F_{I}:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i_{1}\leq\cdots\leq i_{n}\\
\text{$i_{j}<i_{j+1}$ for $j\in I$}\end{subarray}}z_{i_{1}}z_{i_{2}}\cdots z_{i_{n}},\qquad I\subseteq[n-1].

The series
\mathcal{U}_{D}
first appears in the work of Chow
[
3
]
, where it manifests as a specialization of his
path-cycle symmetric function
; this connection will be further detailed below.

More recently,
\mathcal{U}_{D}
was studied (and named) by Grinberg and Stanley
[
12
]
, who demonstrated its connection to well-known results of Rédei and Berge concerning the number of Hamiltonian paths in digraphs. For instance, they rederive the classic theorem of Rédei
[
18
]
that every tournament
D
has an odd number of directed Hamiltonian paths, and extend this to new relations on the number of such paths modulo
4
.

In this article, we re-prove results of Chow
[
3
]
, and Grinberg and Stanley
[
12
]
, on expansions of
\mathcal{U}_{D}
in terms of classic symmetric functions. Along the way we rederive formulas for Hamiltonian paths in digraphs that coalesce results of Goulden and Jackson
[
7
]
, Grinberg and Stanley
[
12
]
, Wiseman
[
25
]
, and others. This is done by unifying the theory from these articles into an overarching matrix algebra framework. The majority of these results hinge on Theorem
10
, which explicitly writes
\mathcal{U}_{D}
in terms of symmetric functions that depend on the entries of the adjacency matrix of
D
and its complement. Theorem
10
is ultimately a re-framing of work due to Lass
[
15
]
using the algebra of set functions (
fonctions d’ensembles
). Indeed, our use of the linear coefficient operator
\mathfrak{L}_{n}
, which is critical to our main theory (see Section
3
), is equivalent to Lass’ formalism.

The article is structured as follows: We begin in Section
2
by viewing walks and paths in digraphs through the lens of matrix algebra. Therein we provide proofs of classic results on Hamiltonian paths and cycles in digraphs via their directed adjacency matrices. In turn, these results support Section
3
which is dedicated to symmetric function expansions of
\mathcal{U}_{D}
. We recover the power sum expansions of Grinberg and Stanley
[
12
]
, determine general expansions for
\mathcal{U}_{D}
in the Schur function basis, and recover positivity results in both these bases. We then show how to lift this theory to the full Chow path-cycle symmetric function.

2.
The Algebra of Walks and Paths

2.1.
Preliminaries

We begin with algebraic preliminaries. For
f\in\mathbb{Q}[[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]]
and
I=\{i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}\}\subseteq[n]
, we use the notation
\mathfrak{L}_{I}\,f
to denote the coefficient of
x_{i_{1}}x_{i_{2}}\cdots x_{i_{k}}
in
f
. In the particular case that
I=[n]
, we abbreviate
\mathfrak{L}_{I}
by
\mathfrak{L}_{n}
. For
I\subseteq[n]
, we denote the complement
[n]\backslash I
by
I^{c}
, with
n
understood from context. The group of permutations on
I
is denoted
\mathfrak{S}_{I}
, or simply
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
when
I=[n]
.

Let
A
be an
n\times n
matrix whose
(i,j)
-entry is denoted
A_{i,j}
. Recall that the permanent
\per(A)
and determinant
\det(A)
of
A
are defined by

\per(A)=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}A_{i,\sigma(i)},\qquad\det(A)=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\sgn(\sigma)\prod_{i=1}^{n}A_{i,\sigma(i)},

where
\sgn(\sigma)
is the sign of the permutation
\sigma
.
We denote by
A[S]
the principal submatrix of
A
whose rows and columns are indexed by
S\subseteq[n]
, and let
X=\text{diag}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
.
The following well-known relations between the permanent and determinant are used throughout:

\mathfrak{L}_{S}\det(I+XA)=\det A[S]\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathfrak{L}_{S}\det(I-XA)^{-1}=\per A[S],

(2)

Note that the latter of these is a special case of MacMahon’s Master Theorem (see
[
8
, Section 1.2.11]
and also Lemma
8
, below).

Now recall that if
A
is
n\times m
and
B
is
m\times n
, then Sylvester’s determinant identity states that

\det(I_{n}+AB)=\det(I_{m}+BA).

(3)

We will make particular use of the special case
m=1
, which gives

\det(I_{n}+\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^{T})=1+\mathbf{v}^{T}\mathbf{u}

(4)

for column vectors
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}
. We will also make central use of Jacobi’s Identity, which states that

\det\exp(A)=\exp\tr(A)

(5)

where
\exp(A)=\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{A^{k}}{k!}
. These formulae hold for matrices over the appropriate commutative rings; see, e.g.,
[
8
, Section 1.1.10]
.

2.2.
Walks and Paths in Digraphs

A
digraph

D
consists of a finite set of vertices
V
together with a set
E\subseteq V\times V
of directed edges
(u,v)
. Note this definition forbids parallel edges. The
complement
of
D
, denoted
\overline{D}
, is the digraph on
V
that has a directed edge
(u,v)
precisely when
(u,v)
is not a directed edge in
D
. The
opposite
of
D
, denoted
D^{\text{op}}
, is the digraph that has a directed edge
(u,v)
precisely when
(v,u)
is a directed edge in
D
. If
V^{\prime}\subseteq V
, then the subgraph of
D

induced by
V^{\prime}
, denoted
D[V^{\prime}]
, has vertices
V^{\prime}
and edges
E^{\prime}=E\cap(V^{\prime}\times V^{\prime})
.

We will work exclusively with digraphs on vertex set
[n]
, for some
n
, or induced subgraphs thereof. The reader should assume from hereon that every digraph under consideration is of this type. In particular, the parameter
n
will universally denote the number of vertices in an ambient digraph.

The
adjacency matrix
of a digraph
D
on
[n]
is the matrix
A(D)\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n}
whose
(i,j)
entry is
1
if and only if
(v_{i},v_{j})
is a directed edge in
D
. For brevity we will write
A,\overline{A}
and
A^{\text{op}}
for
A(D),A(\overline{D})
and
A(D^{\text{op}})
, respectively, whenever
D
is implicit. Evidently we have
\overline{A}=\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{T}-A
, where
\mathbf{1}=[1,1,\ldots,1]^{T}
is the
n
-dimensional all-ones column vector. Moreover it is immediate that
A^{\text{op}}=A^{T}
.

A
walk
of
length
k\geq 0
in
D
is a sequence
w=({i_{0}},\ldots,{i_{k}})
of vertices such that
({i_{j}},{i_{j+1}})
is a directed edge in
D
for all
0\leq j<k
. We say
w
is a
path
in
D
if all its vertices
{i_{0}},{i_{1}},\ldots,{i_{k}}
are distinct. A
cycle
in
D
is an equivalence class of walks of the form
({i_{0}},{i_{1}},\ldots,{i_{k}},{i_{0}})
, where
{i_{0}},\ldots,{i_{k}}
are distinct, and two such walks are equivalent if one is a cyclic shift of the other. A
cycle cover
of
D
is a set of vertex-disjoint cycles where each vertex appears in exactly one cycle.

We often will need to refer to cycles in digraphs that are compatible with cycles in permutations. Let
D
be a digraph on
V\subseteq[n]
and let
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{V}
be a permutation on
V
. Then a cycle
(i_{0}\ i_{1}\cdots i_{k})
in the disjoint cycle decomposition of
\sigma
is said to be a
D
-cycle
if
(i_{0},i_{1},\ldots,i_{k},i_{0})
is a cycle in
D
.

A convenient way to keep track of walks in
D
is through its
walk generating function
. Let
\gamma_{0}(D)=1
and, for any
k\geq 0
, let
\gamma_{k+1}(D)
be the polynomial in indeterminates
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}
given by

\gamma_{k+1}(D):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(i_{0},i_{1},\ldots,i_{k})\\
\text{a walk in }D\end{subarray}}x_{i_{0}}x_{i_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{k}}.

That is,
\gamma_{k+1}(D)
accounts for walks of length
k
, which are those containing
k+1
vertices (counted with multiplicity).
The walk generating function of
D
is then the series
W_{D}(z)
in
\mathbb{Q}[x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}][[z]]
defined by

W_{D}(z)=\sum_{k\geq 0}\gamma_{k}(D)z^{k}.

The role of the indeterminates
x_{i}
in
W_{D}(z)
is left implicit for brevity. Evidently,
W_{D}(z)
involves only those
x_{i}
for which
i
is a vertex of
D
. In particular, if
D
is a digraph on
[n]
and
V\subseteq[n]
, then
W_{D[V]}(z)
is therefore obtained from
W_{D}(z)
by setting
x_{j}=0
for all
j\notin V
.

If
D
is a digraph on
[n]
then we can express
W_{D}(z)
in a compact form by using its adjacency matrix
A
and the diagonal matrix
X=\text{diag}(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})
. An easy induction reveals that the
(i,j)
entry of
(XA)^{k}X
is the generating series for walks of length
k
in
D
from vertex
i
to vertex
j
. Thus
\gamma_{k+1}(D)
is the sum of all entries of this matrix, i.e.
\gamma_{k+1}(D)=\mathbf{1}^{T}(XA)^{k}X\mathbf{1}
. Letting
\mathbf{x}=[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]^{T}
, we consequently have

W_{D}(z)=1+\mathbf{1}^{T}\left(z\sum_{k\geq 0}(XA)^{k}z^{k}\right)\mathbf{x}=1+z\mathbf{1}^{T}(I-zXA)^{-1}\mathbf{x}.

(6)

The following reformulation of this identity provides a compact expression for
W_{D}(z)
that is fundamental to our developments. An equivalent observation was made in
[
20
]
.

Lemma 1
.

Let
D
be a digraph on
[n]
with adjacency matrix
A
. Then

W_{{D}}(z)=\dfrac{\det(I+zX\overline{A})}{\det(I-zX{A})}.

Consequently we have
W_{\overline{D}}(z)=(W_{D}(-z))^{-1}
, and when
D
is acyclic
W_{D}(z)=\det(I+zX\overline{A})
.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\det(I+zX\overline{A})

\displaystyle=\det(I+zX(\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{T}-{A}))

\displaystyle=\det(I-zX{A}+z\mathbf{x}\mathbf{1}^{T})

\displaystyle=\det(I-zXA)\det(I+z(I-zXA)^{-1}\mathbf{x}\mathbf{1}^{T})

\displaystyle=\det(I-zXA)(1+z\mathbf{1}^{T}(I-zX{A})^{-1}\mathbf{x}),

where the final equality comes from Sylvester’s identity (
4
) applied to the pair
\mathbf{u}=z(I-zXA)^{-1}\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{1}
.
From Equation (
6
) we then get

W_{D}(z)=1+z\mathbf{1}^{T}(I-zX{A})^{-1}\mathbf{x}=\frac{\det(I+zX\overline{A})}{\det(I-zXA)}.

It immediately follows that
W_{\overline{D}}(z)=(W_{D}(-z))^{-1}
. Finally, if
D
is acyclic then we can relabel its vertices so as to make
A
strictly upper triangular (i.e. topologically ordered), which gives
\det(I-zXA)=1
and thus
W_{D}(z)=\det(I+zX\overline{A})
.
∎

Recall a path of length
n-1
in a digraph
D
on
[n]
is said to be
Hamiltonian
, meaning it encounters every vertex in
D
. For convenience we also consider the empty digraph on zero vertices to have a single Hamiltonian path. The number of Hamiltonian paths in
D
is denoted
\mathrm{ham}(D)
. We now observe that Lemma
1
yields a permanent-determinant formula for this statistic.

Proposition 2
.

The number of Hamiltonian paths in a digraph
D
on
[n]
is given by

\mathrm{ham}(D)=\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}\det\overline{A}[S]\cdot\per{A}[S^{c}].

Proof.

From the definition of
W_{D}(z)
we have
\mathrm{ham}(D)=\mathfrak{L}_{n}\,W_{{D}}(1)
. By Lemma
1
this is

\displaystyle\mathfrak{L}_{n}\,\det(I+X\overline{A})\det(I-X{A})^{-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}\mathfrak{L}_{S}\det(I+X\overline{A})\cdot\mathfrak{L}_{S^{c}}\det(I-X{A})^{-1},

and the result follows from Equation (
2
).
∎

It is clear from definition that for
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
the product
\prod_{i=1}^{n}A_{i,\sigma(i)}
equals
1
if every cycle in the disjoint cycle decomposition of
\sigma
is a
D
-cycle, and equals
0
otherwise. Consequently,
\per(A)
is the number of cycle covers of
D
. We can therefore use Proposition
2
to recover the following result of Wiseman
[
25
]
.

Corollary 3
.

[
25
, Theorem 2.1]

Suppose
D
is an acyclic digraph. Then the number of Hamiltonian paths in
\overline{D}
is equal to the number of cycle covers of
\overline{D}
.

Proof.

Since
D
is acyclic,
\det A[S]=0
for
S\neq\emptyset
, so by Proposition
2
,
\mathrm{ham}(\overline{D})=\per(\overline{A})
. The result follows.
∎

The following result of Berge is immediate from Proposition
2
upon observing that
\det(M)\equiv\per(M)\pmod{2}
for any integer matrix
M
.

Corollary 4
.

[
1
, Section 10.1]

For any digraph
D
we have
\mathrm{ham}(D)\equiv\mathrm{ham}(\overline{D})\pmod{2}
.

Not all digraphs have Hamiltonian paths, but certain classes are known to have at least one. For instance, a classic result of Rédei
[
18
]
establishes that any tournament has an odd number of Hamiltonian paths. Recall that a digraph
D
is a
tournament
if for every pair of distinct vertices
i,j
, exactly one of
(i,j)
and
(j,i)
is a directed edge (and
(i,i)
is not an edge for any
i
). Here we offer a different proof of Rédei’s result using Lemma
1
.

Theorem 5
.

[
18
]

If
D
is a tournament on
[n]
then
\mathrm{ham}(D)
is odd.

Proof.

As before we write
\mathrm{ham}(D)=\mathfrak{L}_{n}\,W_{D}(1)
. Since
D
is a tournament we have
\overline{A}=A^{T}+I
and thus

\det(I+X\overline{A})=\det(I+X(A^{T}+I))=\det(I+(A^{T}+I)X)=\det(I+XA+X),

the middle equality by Sylvester’s Identity (
3
). We are concerned only with the parity of
\mathfrak{L}_{n}\,W_{D}(1)
, so we now work over the quotient ring
\mathbb{F}_{2}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]/\langle{x_{1}^{2},\cdots,x_{n}^{2}\rangle}
in which
X^{2}=0
and
XA=-XA
. From this, Lemma
1
gives

\displaystyle W_{D}(1)=\det(I+XA+X)\det(I-XA)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\det(I-XA+X)\det(I-XA)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\det(I+X(I-XA)^{-1})

\displaystyle=\det(I+X(I+XA+(XA)^{2}+\cdots))

\displaystyle=\det(I+X),

the last equality since
X^{2}=0
. This yields
\mathfrak{L}_{n}\,W_{D}(1)=1
, completing the proof.
∎

Proposition
2
is similar to known expressions due to Goulden and Jackson
[
7
]
and Liu
[
16
]
for the number of Hamiltonian cycles in a digraph
D
. (Recall that a cycle in
D
is said to be
Hamiltonian
if it encounters every vertex; the empty digraph having no Hamiltonian cycles, and a digraph on one vertex having a Hamiltonian cycle if and only if it contains a loop.) Although peripheral to our study, we pause here to show how these results can be derived from similar matrix analyses.
Part (a) of following proposition appears as
[
7
, Theorem 4.1]
, and is proven there using Lagrangian methods. It also appears in
[
23
]
. Part (b) is trivially equivalent to Theorem 2 of
[
16
]
, where it is derived via the Matrix-Tree Theorem.

Proposition 6
.

Let
D
be a digraph on
[n]
. For any
i\in[n]
, the number of Hamiltonian cycles in
D
is given by the following equivalent expressions:

(a)

\displaystyle\sum_{S\subseteq[n]\setminus\{i\}}(-1)^{|S|}\det A[S]\cdot\per A[S^{c}].

(b)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}(-1)^{|S|}|S^{c}|\,\det A[S]\cdot\per A[S^{c}].

Proof.

Let
\mathrm{ham}^{\circ}(D)
denote the number of Hamiltonian cycles in
D
. The
(i,i)
-entry of
(XA)^{k}
is the generating function for walks of length
k
in
D
that start and end with
i
.
So let us set

H:=I+XA+(XA)^{2}+(XA)^{3}+\cdots=(I-XA)^{-1}=\frac{\mathrm{adj}(I-XA)}{\det(I-XA)},

where
\mathrm{adj}(\cdot)
denotes the adjugate, i.e. classical adjoint. Then for any
i\in[n]
we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{ham}^{\circ}(D)=\mathfrak{L}_{n}\,H_{ii}

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{L}_{n}\,\mathrm{cof}_{ii}(I-XA)\det(I-XA)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}\mathfrak{L}_{S}\,\mathrm{cof}_{ii}(I-XA)\ \mathfrak{L}_{S^{c}}\det(I-XA)^{-1},

where
\mathrm{cof}_{ii}
is the
(i,i)
-cofactor. Expression (a) immediately follows using Equation (
2
). Averaging (a) over all
i\in[n]
then yields (b) as follows:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{S\subseteq[n]\setminus\{i\}}(-1)^{|S|}\det A[S]\cdot\per A[S^{c}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}\sum_{i\notin S}(-1)^{|S|}\det A[S]\cdot\per A[S^{c}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}(-1)^{|S|}|S^{c}|\det A[S]\cdot\per A[S^{c}].

∎

We note that Proposition
2
can be derived from Proposition
6
(a) by applying the latter to the digraph
D^{\prime}
that is obtained from
D
by adding a new vertex
0
and a directed edge to and from every vertex in
D
. The number of Hamiltonian cycles in
D^{\prime}
is then the number of Hamiltonian paths in
{D}
. The reduction from
A(D^{\prime})
to
A(D)
can be accomplished via Schur complementation.

3.
Symmetric Function Expansions

3.1.
Preliminaries

We use standard notation for partitions of integers, symmetric functions, and representation theory of the symmetric group, as introduced in Macdonald
[
17
]
. We remind the reader of a few key constructs and auxiliary definitions.

Throughout, we work in the ring of symmetric functions
\Lambda_{\mathbf{z}}=\mathbb{Q}[[z_{1},z_{2},\ldots]]^{\mathfrak{S}}
in the indeterminates
\mathbf{z}=(z_{1},z_{2},\ldots)
. For a partition
\lambda
, we let
r_{\lambda}!:=r_{1}!r_{2}!\cdots
, where
r_{i}
is the number of parts of
\lambda
equal to
i
. In addition to the usual symmetric functions
m_{\lambda},e_{\lambda},h_{\lambda},p_{\lambda},s_{\lambda}
, we employ the
augmented monomial symmetric functions
defined by
\tilde{m}_{\lambda}:=r_{\lambda}!m_{\lambda}
. For
f
in one of the families
\{m_{\lambda},e_{\lambda},h_{\lambda},p_{\lambda},s_{\lambda},\tilde{m}_{\lambda}\}
, we denote by
f_{\mathrm{cyc}(\sigma)}
the symmetric function
f_{\lambda}
where
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{\ell})
is the cycle type of
\sigma
. For example, if
\sigma=(1\ 6\ 5)(2)(3\ 4)(7\ 9)(8)\in\mathfrak{S}_{9}
then
p_{\mathrm{cyc}(\sigma)}=p_{3}p_{2}^{2}p_{1}^{2}
.

Let
H_{\mathbf{z}}(t),E_{\mathbf{z}}(t),P_{\mathbf{z}}(t)\in\Lambda_{\mathbf{z}}[[t]]
be the ordinary generating series in
t
for
\{h_{i}\}_{i\geq 0},\{e_{i}\}_{i\geq 0}
and
\{p_{i+1}\}_{i\geq 0}
respectively. One can readily see that

H_{\mathbf{z}}(t)=\sum_{j\geq 0}h_{j}t^{j}=\prod_{i\geq 1}\frac{1}{1-z_{i}t},\hskip 14.45377ptE_{\mathbf{z}}(t)=\sum_{j\geq 0}e_{j}t^{j}=\prod_{i\geq 1}(1+z_{i}t).

(7)

Furthermore, one can check that
P_{\mathbf{z}}(t)=\frac{d}{dt}\log H_{\mathbf{z}}(t)
and by Equation (
7
),
P_{\mathbf{z}}(-t)=\frac{d}{dt}\log E_{\mathbf{z}}(t)
. By integrating then exponentiating we get

H_{\mathbf{z}}(t)=\exp\left(\sum_{i\geq 1}p_{i}\frac{t^{i}}{i}\right),\ E_{\mathbf{z}}(t)=\exp\left(\sum_{i\geq 1}(-1)^{i-1}p_{i}\frac{t^{i}}{i}\right).

(8)

The
fundamental involution

\omega:\Lambda_{\mathbf{z}}\to\Lambda_{\mathbf{z}}
is the algebra homomorphism defined on the generating set
\{e_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
by
\omega(e_{n})=h_{n}
for each
n
. From Equation (
7
) we see
\sum_{i=0}^{n}(-1)^{i}e_{i}h_{n-i}=0
for
n>0
and from this
\omega(h_{n})=e_{n}
for all
n
.

We see that (
7
) and (
8
) can be used to express one class of symmetric functions in terms of another. We present a few additional such relations pertinent to our discussion. Recall that given a partition
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{\ell})
, its
conjugate
is the partition
\lambda^{T}=(\lambda^{T}_{1},\lambda^{T}_{2},\ldots)
where
\lambda^{T}_{i}
is the number of indices
j
for which
\lambda_{j}\geq i
. For instance, if
\lambda=(4,3,2,2,1)
then
\lambda^{T}=(5,4,2,1)
. The classical Jacobi-Trudi identities express the Schur function
s_{\lambda}
in terms of homogeneous and elementary symmetric functions as follows:

\displaystyle s_{\lambda}=\det[h_{\lambda_{i}-i+j}]=\det[e_{\lambda^{T}_{i}-i+j}].

(9)

Here,
h_{k}=0
(similarly
e_{k}=0
) if
k<0
. The Cauchy identity asserts that in
\mathbb{Q}[[\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z}]]
we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}h_{\lambda}(\mathbf{y})m_{\lambda}(\mathbf{z})=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}s_{\lambda}(\mathbf{y})s_{\lambda}(\mathbf{z})

(10)

Furthermore it is well-known that
h_{\lambda}=\sum_{\mu}K_{\mu,\lambda}s_{\mu}
where the coefficients
K_{\mu,\lambda}
are nonnegative integers (the so-called Kostka numbers). Yet another classic result shows that if
\mu\vdash n
then

p_{\mu}=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}\chi^{\lambda}(\mu)\cdot s_{\lambda},

(11)

where
\chi^{\lambda}(\mu)
is the evaluation of the irreducible character
\chi^{\lambda}
of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
indexed by
\lambda
evaluated at any permutation whose cycle type is given by
\mu
.

The characters of representations of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
show up in another useful context for us. Given an
n\times n
matrix
A
and a partition
\lambda\vdash n
, the

of
A
indexed by
\lambda
is the multilinear expression
\text{Imm}_{\lambda}(A)=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)\prod_{i=1}^{n}A_{i,\sigma(i)}.
When
\lambda=(n)\vdash n
then
\chi^{\lambda}
is the trivial character given by
\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)=1
for all
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
so
\text{Imm}_{\lambda}(A)=\text{per}(A)
. Similarly when
\lambda=(1,1,\ldots,1)
then
\chi^{\lambda}
is the sign character given by
\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)=\sgn(\sigma)
for all
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
so
\text{Imm}_{\lambda}(A)=\text{det}(A)
.

In our study we will be concerned with when symmetric functions expand with nonnegative coefficients with respect to a given basis. We say
f\in\Lambda_{\mathbf{z}}
is
p
-positive
if it can be written as a nonnegative linear combination of power sum symmetric functions. We say
f
is
Schur-positive
if it can be written as a nonnegative linear combination of Schur functions.

Lemma 8

For an

(a)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{L}_{I}\det H_{\mathbf{z}}(XA)=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{I}}p_{\mathrm{cyc}(\sigma)}\prod_{i\in I}{A}_{i,\sigma(i)}

(b)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{L}_{I}\det E_{\mathbf{z}}(XA)=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{I}}p_{\mathrm{cyc}(\sigma)}\sgn(\sigma)\prod_{i\in I}A_{i,\sigma(i)}

Proof.

By Jacobi’s identity (

\displaystyle\det H_{\mathbf{z}}(XA)=\det\exp\left(\sum_{k}\frac{1}{k}p_{k}(XA)^{k}\right)=\exp\left(\sum_{k}\frac{1}{k}p_{k}\tr(XA)^{k}\right).

The computation of

Under these reductions we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k}\tr(XA)^{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{distinct}\\

\displaystyle\det H_{\mathbf{z}}(XA)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{L}_{I}\det H_{\mathbf{z}}(XA)

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{L}_{I}\prod_{\emptyset\subsetneq J\subseteq I}\left(1+p_{|J|}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{C}_{J}}\prod_{j\in J}x_{j}A_{j,\sigma(j)}\right)

Let

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{D}=\mathfrak{L}_{n}\ \det H_{\mathbf{z}}(X\overline{A})\cdot\det E_{\mathbf{z}}(XA)

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{D}=\mathfrak{L}_{n}\,\exp\Big(\sum_{k}\frac{1}{k}p_{k}\big(\tr(X\overline{A})^{k}+(-1)^{k-1}\tr(XA)^{k}\big)\Big)

(c)

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{D}=\sum_{I\subseteq[n]}\left(\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{I^{c}}}p_{\mathrm{cyc}(\sigma)}\prod_{i\in I^{c}}\overline{A}_{i,\sigma(i)}\right)\left(\sum_{\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{I}}p_{\mathrm{cyc}(\tau)}\sgn(\tau)\prod_{i\in I}A_{i,\tau(i)}\right).

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{L}_{n}\,\prod_{i}\frac{\det(I+z_{i}XA)}{\det(I-z_{i}X\overline{A})}

3.3.
Power Sum Expansions

We now apply Theorem
10
to recover several theorems from the literature on the expansion of
\mathcal{U}_{D}
in the power sum basis. We particularly investigate digraphs
D
for which
\mathcal{U}_{D}
is
p
-positive, and interpret their coefficients combinatorially where possible. For convenience we borrow the following nomenclature from
[
12
]
.

Definition 13
.

[
12
]

We let
\mathfrak{S}_{I}(D)
(respectively
\mathfrak{S}_{I}(D,\overline{D})
) denote the set of permutations in
\mathfrak{S}_{I}
whose nontrivial cycles are all
D
-cycles (respectively all
D
-cycles or
\overline{D}
-cycles). We simply write
\mathfrak{S}(D)
and
\mathfrak{S}(D,\overline{D})
for
\mathfrak{S}_{[n]}(D)
and
\mathfrak{S}_{[n]}(D,\overline{D})
respectively.

The following theorem of Grinberg and Stanley gives an explicit expansion of
\mathcal{U}_{D}
in terms of power sum symmetric functions.

Corollary 14
.

[
12
, Theorem 1.3]

Let
D
be a digraph on
[n]
. For
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, define

\phi(\sigma):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma\in\mathrm{Cycs}(\sigma)\\
\text{$\gamma$ a $D$-cycle}\end{subarray}}(\ell(\gamma)-1),

where
\mathrm{Cycs}(\sigma)
denotes the set of cycles in the disjoint cycle decomposition of
\sigma
and
\ell(\gamma)
is the length of
\gamma\in\mathrm{Cycs}(\sigma)
. Then

\mathcal{U}_{D}=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}(D,\overline{D})}(-1)^{\phi(\sigma)}p_{\mathrm{cyc}(\sigma)}.

Proof.

By Theorem
10
(c),

\mathcal{U}_{D}=\sum_{I\subseteq[n]}\sum_{(\sigma,\tau)\in\mathfrak{S}_{I^{c}}\times\mathfrak{S}_{I}}p_{\mathrm{cyc}(\sigma)}p_{\mathrm{cyc}(\tau)}\sgn(\tau)\prod_{i\in I^{c}}\overline{A}_{i,\sigma(i)}\prod_{i\in I}A_{i,\tau(i)}.

The non-zero summands in this expression come from pairs
(\sigma,\tau)\in\mathfrak{S}_{I^{c}}\times\mathfrak{S}_{I}
for which every cycle in
\sigma
is a
\overline{D}
-cycle and every cycle in
\tau
is a
D
-cycle. The contribution to the sum from such a pair is
p_{\mathrm{cyc}(\sigma)}p_{\mathrm{cyc}(\tau)}\sgn(\tau)=p_{\mathrm{cyc}(\rho)}\sgn(\tau)
where
\rho
is the permutation in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
obtained by concatenating
\sigma
and
\tau
. Finally,
\sgn(\tau)
is precisely
(-1)^{\phi(\rho)}
and the result follows.
∎

As an example, consider the digraph
D
in Figure
1
. There are exactly three permutations in
\mathfrak{S}(D,\overline{D})
, namely

\sigma=(1)(2)(3)\hskip 14.45377pt\sigma^{\prime}=(12)(3)\hskip 14.45377pt\sigma^{\prime\prime}=(123).

Note that
\sigma
has one
D
-cycle (of length

), whereas
\sigma^{\prime}
and
\sigma^{\prime\prime}
have no
D
-cycles. Therefore,
\phi(\sigma)=\phi(\sigma^{\prime})=\phi(\sigma^{\prime\prime})=0
. We deduce then that
\mathcal{U}_{D}=p_{1^{3}}+p_{21}+p_{3}
, in agreement with Equation (
13

When
D
is acyclic,
\mathcal{U}_{D}
has the following compact form. This is the so-called
symmetric function determinant
of
\overline{A}
as coined by Stanley in
[

Corollary 15
.

Let
D
be an acyclic digraph on
[n]
. Then

\mathcal{U}_{D}=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}p_{\mathrm{cyc}(\sigma)}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\overline{A}_{i,\sigma(i)}.

In particular,
\mathcal{U}_{D}
is

-positive.

Proof.

If
D
is acyclic then in Theorem

p_{\mathrm{cyc}(\tau)}\sgn(\tau)\prod_{i\in I}A_{i,\tau(i)}=0
for

. This leaves the desired sum.
∎

Example 16
.

Let
\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}V_{i}
be a partition of
[n]
and consider the digraph
D
on
[n]
with edge set
\bigcup_{i>j}V_{i}\times V_{j}
. Then
D
is acyclic, so by Corollary
15
we have

\mathcal{U}_{D}=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}p_{\mathrm{cyc}(\sigma)}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\overline{A}_{i,\sigma(i)}=\sum_{(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{k})\in\mathfrak{S}_{V_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathfrak{S}_{V_{k}}}p_{\mathrm{cyc}(\sigma_{1})}\cdots p_{\mathrm{cyc}(\sigma_{k})}

because for any
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
the product
\prod_{i=1}^{n}\overline{A}_{i,\sigma(i)}
is nonzero if and only if each cycle in
\sigma
is individually contained completely in one of the sets

.

Observe in this case we can write
\mathcal{U}_{D}
very simply in terms of complete symmetric functions. Using the well-known identity
m!h_{m}=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{m}}p_{\mathrm{cyc}(\sigma)}
, we find that

\mathcal{U}_{D}=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{V_{i}}}p_{\mathrm{cyc}(\sigma)}=(\lambda_{1}!\lambda_{2}!\cdots)h_{\lambda},

where
\lambda
is the partition of
n
obtained from rearranging
|V_{1}|,\ldots,|V_{k}|
in nonincreasing order.

If the vertices of an acyclic digraph are labeled in topological order (meaning they are listed so that if
(u,v)
is a directed edge then
u
is listed before

) then we recover, from Corollary
15
,
the following expansion of

that was conjectured by Stanley and proved by Gessel; see
[
21

Recall that a

of a permutation
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
is an index

such that
\sigma(r)>\sigma(i)
for all

.
If

are the records of
\sigma
, then the
record partition
of
\sigma
is the partition of

whose parts are the differences
\{r_{2}-r_{1},r_{3}-r_{2},\ldots,(n+1)-r_{j}\}
. For example,
\sigma=325641\in\mathfrak{S}_{6}
has records
\{1,3,4\}
and record partition
(3,2,1)
.

Corollary 17
.

Suppose
D
is a digraph on
[n]
such that

for every directed edge
(i,j)
. Then

\mathcal{U}_{D}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}\\
\mathrm{Des}_{D}(\sigma)=\emptyset\end{subarray}}p_{\mathrm{rp}(\sigma)}

where
\mathrm{rp}(\sigma)
is the record partition of
\sigma
.

Proof.

Evidently
D
is acyclic, so Corollary

gives
\mathcal{U}_{D}=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}p_{\mathrm{cyc}(\sigma)}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\overline{A}_{i,\sigma(i)}
. The only nonvanishing summands are
p_{\mathrm{cyc}(\sigma)}
for permutations
\sigma
whose cycles are all
\overline{D}
-cycles.
Now transform any such
\sigma
into the following linear form
\sigma^{\prime}
: Write each cycle of
\sigma
with largest element first, and then concatenate the cycles in ascending order of greatest element. For example,
\sigma=(1\,4\,6)(2\,3)(5)
becomes
\sigma^{\prime}=325641
. (The mapping
\sigma\to\sigma^{\prime}
is a restriction of Foata’s fundamental bijection.) Then
\mathrm{cyc}(\sigma)=\mathrm{rp}(\sigma^{\prime})
, and each consecutive pair

for some

\sigma^{\prime}_{j}<\sigma^{\prime}_{j+1}
. In particular, each
(\sigma^{\prime}_{j},\sigma^{\prime}_{j+1})
is a directed edge of
\overline{D}
, so
\mathrm{Des}_{D}(\sigma^{\prime})=\emptyset
.∎

Finally, we have the following result of Grinberg and Stanley which ensures

-positivity of
\mathcal{U}_{D}
when

has no

-cycles.

Corollary 18
.

[
12
, Theorem 1.41]

Suppose
D
is a digraph on
[n]
and has no
2
-cycles. Then
\mathcal{U}_{D}
is

-positive.

Proof.

Recalling that, working in the ring where
X^{2}=0
(which we can do by Theorem
10
), the quantities
\tr(X\overline{A})^{k}
and
\tr(XA)^{k}
enumerate the
k

\overline{D}
and

D
has no 2-cycles. If arc
(u,v)
appears in

then the reverse arc
(v,u)
appears in
\overline{D}
. Thus reversing any

\overline{D}

\tr(X\overline{A})^{k}

3.4.
Tournaments

Grinberg and Stanley
[
12
]
expand
\mathcal{U}_{D}
when
D
is a tournament, showing not only that
\mathcal{U}_{D}
is
p
-positive in this case, but that
\mathcal{U}_{D}\in\mathbb{Z}[p_{1},2p_{3},2p_{5},2p_{7},\dots]
. We can recover this theorem directly from Theorem
10
.

Corollary 19
.

[
12
, Theorem 1.39]

Let
D
be a tournament on
[n]
and for
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
let
\psi(\sigma)
be the number of nontrivial cycles of
\sigma
. Then

\mathcal{U}_{D}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}(D)\\
\text{all cycles of }\sigma\text{ have odd length }\end{subarray}}2^{\psi(\sigma)}p_{\mathrm{cyc}(\sigma)}

Proof.

Recall that Theorem
10
(b) gives

\mathcal{U}_{D}=\mathfrak{L}_{n}\,\exp\Big(\sum_{k}\frac{1}{k}p_{k}\big(\tr(X\overline{A})^{k}+(-1)^{k-1}\tr(XA)^{k}\big)\Big).

We again proceed in the quotient ring
\Lambda_{\mathbf{z}}[[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]]/\langle x_{1}^{2},\ldots,x_{n}^{2}\rangle
,
in which we claim

\tr(X\overline{A})^{k}=\begin{cases}\tr X&\text{if $k=1$}\\
\tr(XA)^{k}&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Indeed,
\overline{A}
has all ones on the diagonal because
D
has no loops, so
\tr(X\overline{A})=\tr X
. Since
D
is a tournament,
D^{op}
is simply
\overline{D}
with its loops removed. Thus any
D
-cycle is the reversal of a non-loop
\overline{D}
-cycle, and vice-versa. When
k>1
, loops contribute to neither
\tr(X\overline{A})^{k}
nor
\tr(XA)^{k}
(since
X^{2}=0
), and so in this case we have
\tr(X\overline{A})^{k}=\tr(XA)^{k}
.

We are left with

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{D}

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{L}_{n}\exp\Big(p_{1}\tr X\Big)\exp\left(\sum_{\text{odd $k>1$}}\frac{1}{k}(2p_{k})\tr(XA)^{k}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{L}_{n}\prod_{i}(1+p_{1}x_{i})\cdot\det H_{\mathbf{z}}(XA)\Big|_{p_{1}\rightarrow 0,\,p_{2i}\rightarrow 0,\,p_{2i+1}\rightarrow 2p_{2i+1}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{I\subseteq[n]}p_{1}^{n-|I|}\cdot\mathfrak{L}_{I}\det H_{\mathbf{z}}(XA)\Big|_{p_{1}\rightarrow 0,\,p_{2i}\rightarrow 0,\,p_{2i+1}\rightarrow 2p_{2i+1}}

But by Lemma
8
, for
I\subseteq[n]
we have

\mathfrak{L}_{I}\,\det H_{\mathbf{z}}(XA)=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{I}}p_{\mathrm{cyc}(\sigma)}\prod_{i\in I}A_{i,\sigma(i)}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{I}\\
\text{ every cycle in $\sigma$ is a $D$-cycle}\end{subarray}}p_{\mathrm{cyc}(\sigma)}

and the result follows.
∎

It was observed in
[
12
]
that Corollary
19
specifies to the following expression for the number of Hamiltonian paths in the complement of a tournament:

Corollary 20
.

[
12
, Theorem 1.39 & Lemma 6.5]
For a tournament
D
on
[n]
,

\mathrm{ham}(\overline{D})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}(D)\\
\text{all cycles of }\sigma\text{ have odd length }\end{subarray}}2^{\psi(\sigma)}.

Proof.

From (
15
) we see that setting
z_{1}=1
and
z_{i}=0
for
i>1
transforms
\mathcal{U}_{D}
into
\mathfrak{L}_{n}W_{\overline{D}}(1)
, which is precisely
\mathrm{ham}(\overline{D})
. Evidently these transformations are equivalent to mapping
p_{i}\rightarrow 1
for all
i
, and the result follows from Corollary
19
.
∎

3.5.
Schur Function Expansions

We now explore the expansion of
\mathcal{U}_{D}
in the Schur function basis. A motivating question is determining conditions on
D
that ensure
\mathcal{U}_{D}
is Schur positive. For instance, we see that the digraphs in Example
16
are all Schur positive. Indeed for such a digraph
D
, if the partition of the vertex set is
V_{1},V_{2},\ldots,V_{k}
with
|V_{i}|=\lambda_{i}
, and we set
\lambda
to be the partition of
n
with the
\lambda_{i}
’s listed in weakly decreasing order, then
\mathcal{U}_{D}=\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}!\right)\sum_{\mu}K_{\mu,\lambda}s_{\mu}
where
K_{\mu,\lambda}
are the Kostka numbers which are known to be nonnegative.
However,
\mathcal{U}_{D}
may fail to be Schur positive even if
D
is acyclic. For example, consider the digraph
D
on vertices
\{1,2,3,4\}
with directed edges
\{(4,3),(3,2),(3,1)\}
. Then
D
is acyclic (in fact, a directed tree), and one can calculate that

\mathcal{U}_{D}=10s_{(4)}+4s_{(3,1)}-2s_{(2,2)}+2s_{(2,1,1)}.

We begin our investigation by developing two general expressions concerning the resolution of
\mathcal{U}_{D}
in the Schur basis. The first of these identifies the coefficient of
s_{\lambda}
in
\mathcal{U}_{D}
as a Jacobi-Trudi determinant.

For any digraph
D
on
[n]
, and for any
k\geq 0
, let

\xi_{k+1}(D):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(i_{0},i_{1},\ldots,i_{k})\\
\text{a path in }D\end{subarray}}x_{i_{0}}x_{i_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{k}}\in\mathbb{Q}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]

be the generating polynomial for paths of length
k
in
D
. Further set
\xi_{0}(D)=1
and take
\xi_{k}(D)=0
for
k<0
. Note that
\xi_{k+1}(D)
is obtained from
\gamma_{k+1}(D)
by setting
x_{i}^{2}=0
for all
i
. Finally, for
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots)\vdash n
, define
P_{\lambda}(D)
to be the
n\times n
Jacobi-Trudi matrix whose
(i,j)
-th entry is
\xi_{\lambda_{i}-i+j}({D})
.

Proposition 21
.

Let
D
be any digraph on
[n]
. Then

[s_{\lambda}]\,\mathcal{U}_{D}=\mathfrak{L}_{n}\det P_{\lambda}(\overline{D})=\mathfrak{L}_{n}\det P_{\lambda^{T}}(D).

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{D}=\mathfrak{L}_{n}\prod_{i\geq 1}W_{\overline{D}}(z_{i})=\mathfrak{L}_{n}\prod_{i\geq 1}\Big(\sum_{k\geq 0}\xi_{k}(\overline{D})z_{i}^{k}\Big)=\mathfrak{L}_{n}\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}h_{\lambda}(\mathbf{y})m_{\lambda}(\mathbf{z})\Bigg|_{h_{i}(\mathbf{y})\rightarrow\xi_{i}(\overline{D})},

with the the last equality holding since
\prod_{i\geq 1}\sum_{k\geq 0}h_{k}(\mathbf{y})z_{i}^{k}=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}h_{\lambda}(\mathbf{y})m_{\lambda}(\mathbf{z})
. The Cauchy identity (
10
) then gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{D}=\mathfrak{L}_{n}\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}{s}_{\lambda}(\mathbf{y})s_{\lambda}(\mathbf{z})\Big|_{h_{i}(\mathbf{y})\rightarrow\xi_{i}({\overline{D}})},

and the first expression for
[s_{\lambda}]\ \mathcal{U}_{D}
follows by the Jacobi-Trudi formula (
9
). Since
W_{\overline{D}}(z)=(W_{D}(-z))^{-1}
,
the identification
h_{i}(\mathbf{y})\rightarrow\xi_{i}(\overline{D})
is equivalent to

e_{i}(\mathbf{y})\rightarrow\xi_{i}(D)
, so the second expression follows similarly.
∎

For example, returning to the digraph
D
in Figure
1
we have

\displaystyle\xi_{1}(D)=x_{1}+x_{2}+x_{3},\qquad\xi_{2}(D)=x_{1}x_{3}+x_{3}x_{2},\qquad\xi_{3}(D)=x_{1}x_{3}x_{2},

with
\xi_{0}(D)=1
and
\xi_{k}(D)=0
for
k>3
. Proposition
21
then gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{D}

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{L}_{3}\overbrace{\left|\begin{matrix}\xi_{3}&0&0\\
0&1&\xi_{1}\\
0&0&1\end{matrix}\right|}^{\det P_{(3)}}\cdot s_{(1^{3})}+\mathfrak{L}_{3}\overbrace{\left|\begin{matrix}\xi_{2}&\xi_{3}&0\\
1&\xi_{1}&\xi_{2}\\
0&0&1\end{matrix}\right|}^{\det P_{(2,1)}}\cdot s_{(2,1)}+\mathfrak{L}_{3}\overbrace{\left|\begin{matrix}\xi_{1}&\xi_{2}&\xi_{3}\\
1&\xi_{1}&\xi_{2}\\
0&1&\xi_{1}\end{matrix}\right|}^{\det P_{(1^{3})}}\cdot s_{(3)}

\displaystyle=s_{(1^{3})}+s_{(2,1)}+3s_{(3)},

in agreement with (
12
) and (
13
).

We can also see using Theorem
10
that
\mathcal{U}_{D}
can be written in the Schur basis with coefficients expressed in terms of immanants of submatrices of
A
and
\overline{A}
and Littlewood-Richardson coefficients

c^{\nu}_{\lambda\mu}
(see
[
21
]
for details).

Proposition 22
.

For any digraph
D
on
[n]
we have

\mathcal{U}_{D}=\sum_{\nu\vdash n}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subseteq[n]\\
\lambda\vdash|I^{c}|,\ \mu\vdash|I|\end{subarray}}\ \mathrm{Imm}_{\lambda}\overline{A}[I^{c}]\cdot\mathrm{Imm}_{\mu^{T}}A[I]\cdot c^{\nu}_{\lambda\mu}\right)s_{\nu}

where
\{c^{\nu}_{\lambda\mu}\}
are Littlewood-Richardson coefficients defined by

s_{\lambda}s_{\mu}=\sum_{\nu\vdash|\lambda|+|\mu|}c^{\nu}_{\lambda\mu}s_{\nu}
.

Proof.

If
I\subseteq[n]
,
\mu
is a partition of
|I|
and
\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{I}
then
\chi^{\mu}(\tau)\sgn(\tau)=\chi^{\mu^{T}}(\tau)
. Using Theorem
10
(c) and Equation (
11
) we therefore get

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{D}

\displaystyle=\sum_{I\subseteq[n]}\left(\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{I^{c}}}p_{\mathrm{cyc}(\sigma)}\prod_{i\in I^{c}}\overline{A}_{i,\sigma(i)}\right)\left(\sum_{\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{I}}p_{\mathrm{cyc}(\tau)}\sgn(\tau)\prod_{i\in I}A_{i,\tau(i)}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{I\subseteq[n]}\left(\sum_{\lambda\vdash|I^{c}|}\left(\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{I^{c}}}\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)\prod_{i\in I^{c}}\overline{A}_{i,\sigma(i)}\right)s_{\lambda}\right)\cdot\left(\sum_{\mu\vdash|I|}\left(\sum_{\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{I}}\underbrace{\chi^{\mu}(\tau)\sgn(\tau)}_{\chi^{\mu^{T}}(\tau)}\prod_{i\in I}A_{i,\tau(i)}\right)s_{\mu}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{I\subseteq[n]}\left(\sum_{\lambda\vdash|I^{c}|}\text{Imm}_{\lambda}\overline{A}[I^{c}]\cdot s_{\lambda}\right)\left(\sum_{\mu\vdash|I|}\text{Imm}_{\mu^{T}}A[I]\cdot s_{\mu}\right).

The result follows.
∎

The expression for
\mathcal{U}_{D}
in Proposition
22
simplifies when
D
is acyclic.

Proposition 23
.

Let
D
be an acyclic digraph on
[n]
. Then

\mathcal{U}_{D}=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}\mathrm{Imm}_{\lambda}(\overline{A})\cdot s_{\lambda}.

Proof.

From Corollary
15
and Equation (
11
), we have

\mathcal{U}_{D}=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}p_{\mathrm{cyc}(\sigma)}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\overline{A}_{i,\sigma(i)}=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)s_{\lambda}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\overline{A}_{i,\sigma(i)}=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}\left(\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)\prod_{i=1}^{n}\overline{A}_{i,\sigma(i)}\right)s_{\lambda}

and the equation follows.
∎

Proposition
23
shows that for an acyclic digraph
D
on
[n]
,
\mathcal{U}_{D}
is Schur positive if and only if
\mathrm{Imm}_{\lambda}(\overline{A})
is nonnegative for every
\lambda\vdash n
. By a well-known result of Stembridge
[
22
]
, all of these immanants are known to be nonnegative in the special case when
\overline{A}
is totally nonnegative (i.e. all minors of
\overline{A}
are nonnegative). However, using
[
2
, Theorem 2.1]
one can see that the tandem conditions of
D
being acyclic and
\overline{A}
being totally nonnegative are very restrictive. For instance, these conditions are satisfied in Example
16
. The next example describes more general instances when this happens, and relates to a celebrated theorem on chromatic symmetric functions.

Example 24
.

Let
P
be a partial order on
[n]
and
Y_{P}=\{(i,j):i>_{P}j\}
. Then it is known that
\mathcal{U}_{Y_{P}}=X_{\text{inc}(P)}
, the chromatic symmetric function of the incomparability graph of
P
. One can use this to piece together the celebrated theorem of Gasharov
[
6
]
that
X_{\text{inc}(P)}
is Schur positive if
P
is
(\bf{3}+\bf{1})
-free. Observe that
Y_{P}
is acyclic, so by Proposition
23
,
X_{\text{inc}(P)}=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}\text{Imm}_{\lambda}(\overline{A(Y_{P})})s_{\lambda}.
Guay-Paquet
[
14
]
establishes that to prove Schur positivity for
(\bf{3}+\bf{1})
-free posets it is sufficient to prove it for
unit interval orders
, that is posets that are
(\bf{3}+\bf{1})
- and
(\bf{2}+\bf{2})
-free. Now by Skandera and Reed
[
19
, Proposition 5]
together with Brualdi
[
2
, Theorem 2.2]
,
\overline{A(Y_{P})}^{T}
is totally nonnegative and hence
A(Y_{P})
is. We deduce again by Stembridge
[
22
]
that
X_{\mathrm{inc}(P)}=\mathcal{U}_{Y_{P}}
is Schur positive.

Remark 25
.

Comparing Propositions
21
and
23
we see that for an acyclic digraph
D
we have

\mathrm{Imm}_{\lambda}(\overline{A})=\mathfrak{L}_{n}\det[\overline{\gamma}_{\lambda_{i}-i+j}]

where
\overline{\gamma}_{k}:=\gamma_{k}(\overline{D})
is given by

\overline{\gamma}_{k}=[z^{k}]W_{\overline{D}}(z)=[z^{k}]\det(I-zX\overline{A})^{-1}.

Similar computations show that the immanants of an arbitrary matrix
M
can be expressed in the Jacobi-Trudi form
\mathrm{Imm}_{\lambda}(M)=\mathfrak{L}_{n}\det[\Delta_{\lambda_{i}-i+j}]
, where
\Delta_{k}=[z^{k}]\det(I-zXM)^{-1}
. See
[
9
, Theorem 2.1]
.

We now investigate combinatorial features of the coefficients in the expansion of
\mathcal{U}_{D}
in the Schur basis. We start by observing that there is a direct combinatorial interpretation for the coefficient of
s_{\lambda}
when
\lambda
is a
hook shape
, i.e.
\lambda=(i,1^{n-i})
for some
1\leq i\leq n
.

Proposition 26
.

Let
D
be a digraph on
[n]
,
1\leq i\leq n
, and
\lambda
be the hook partition
\lambda=(i,1^{n-i})
. Then
[s_{\lambda}]\ \mathcal{U}_{D}
is the number of permutations
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
such that
\mathrm{Des}_{D}(\sigma)=\{i,i+1,\ldots,n-1\}
. In particular,
[s_{(1^{n})}]\ \mathcal{U}_{D}
is the number of Hamiltonian paths in
D
and
[s_{(n)}]\ \mathcal{U}_{D}
is the number of Hamiltonian paths in
\overline{D}
.

Proof.

For a composition
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k})\models n
, define
S(\alpha)=\{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{k-1}\}\subseteq[n-1]
. Then

\mathcal{U}_{D}=\sum_{\alpha\models n}c_{\alpha}F_{S(\alpha)}

where
c_{\alpha}
is the number of permutations
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
with
\mathrm{Des}_{D}(\sigma)=S(\alpha)
.
Since we know
\mathcal{U}_{D}
is symmetric,
[
4
, Theorem 15]
implies that
[s_{\lambda}]\,\mathcal{U}_{D}=c_{\lambda}
for any hook partition
\lambda\vdash n
. The result follows since
\lambda=(i,1^{n-i})
has
S(\lambda)=\{i,i+1,\ldots,n-1\}
.
∎

The following Schur expansion of
\mathcal{U}_{D}
when
D=\{(2,1),(3,2),\ldots,(n,n-1)\}
is given in
[
21
, Exercise 120]
and was also observed by Chow for general directed Hamiltonian paths
D
(see
[
3
, Section 6]
):

\mathcal{U}_{D}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}\cdot s_{(i,1^{n-i})}.

Here,
f_{i}
is the number of permutations in
\mathfrak{S}_{i}
with no reverse successions: that is permutations
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{i}
with no index
j
such that
\pi_{j+1}=\pi_{j}-1
. Note that the coefficients agree with Proposition
26
. Indeed, according to that proposition,
[s_{(i,1^{n-i})}]\,\mathcal{U}_{D}
is the number of permutations in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
with reverse successions precisely at positions
\{i,i+1,\ldots,n-1\}
. Now there is a bijection between such permutations
\sigma
and permutations in
\mathfrak{S}_{i}
with no reverse succession by sending
\sigma
to the standardization of
\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\cdots\sigma_{i}
(and one can readily see this is reversible).

Remark 27
.

Our proof of Proposition
26
relied on
[
4
, Theorem 15]
, which states (in part) that
\left[s_{\lambda}\right]f=\left[F_{S\left(\lambda\right)}\right]f
for any symmetric function
f\in\Lambda_{\mathbf{z}}
and any hook partition
\lambda=(i,1^{n-i})
. Grinberg
[
10
]
has pointed out the following alternative explanation for this identity.

The Hall inner product on

\Lambda

(=\Lambda_{\mathbf{z}})
is a restriction of a bilinear form
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle
between the Hopf algebras
\operatorname*{NSym}
and
\operatorname*{QSym}
of noncommutative and quasisymmetric functions. The ribbon basis
\{R_{\alpha}\}
of
\mathrm{NSym}
and fundamental basis
\{F_{\alpha}\}
of
\mathrm{QSym}
are dual with respect to this pairing, and the canonical injection
\iota:\Lambda\rightarrow\operatorname*{QSym}
and projection
\pi:\operatorname*{NSym}\rightarrow\Lambda
are mutually adjoint.
Since
s_{\lambda}=\pi(R_{\lambda})
for ribbon-shaped
\lambda
, we have

\displaystyle\left[s_{\lambda}\right]f=\left\langle s_{\lambda},f\right\rangle=\left\langle\pi\left(R_{\lambda}\right),f\right\rangle=\left\langle R_{\lambda},\iota\left(f\right)\right\rangle=\left[F_{S\left(\lambda\right)}\right]f.

See
[
11
, Section 5.4]
for details concerning the above assertions.

3.6.
Extensions to the Chow Path-Cycle Symmetric Function

Several of our results regarding
\mathcal{U}_{D}
can be extended to Chow’s symmetric function
\Xi_{D}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y})
. We start by noting the action of the fundamental involution, which we now denote
\omega_{\mathbf{z}}
to emphasize that it is acting only on the
\mathbf{z}
variables. The following result appeared as
[
3
, Theorem 1]
and was reproved by Lass
[
15
]
using an approach equivalent to ours.

Theorem 28
.

[
3
, Theorem 1]
For a digraph
D
on
[n]
we have

[\omega_{\mathbf{z}}\ \Xi_{\overline{D}}(\mathbf{z},-\mathbf{y})]_{\mathbf{z}\to(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y})}=\Xi_{D}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y}),

where the operation
\mathbf{z}\to(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y})
replaces
\mathbf{z}
with the union of variables
\mathbf{z}
and
\mathbf{y}
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, assume
D
has vertex set
[n]
. Recall that

\Xi_{D}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y})=\sum_{S}\tilde{m}_{\text{path}(S)}(\mathbf{z})p_{\text{cycle}(S)}(\mathbf{y})

where the sum is over all path-cycle covers of
D
. Notice that for a given summand
S
, the vertices appearing in the parts of
\text{path}(S)
are complementary to those appearing in the parts of
\text{cycle}(S)
. From this we see that

\displaystyle\Xi_{D}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\sum_{S}\tilde{m}_{\text{path}(S)}(\mathbf{z})p_{\text{cycle}(S)}(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\sum_{I\subseteq[n]}\left(\sum_{S^{\prime}\text{ a path cover of }D[I]}\tilde{m}_{\text{path}(S^{\prime})}(\mathbf{z})\right)\left(\sum_{S^{\prime\prime}\text{ a cycle cover of }D[I^{c}]}p_{\text{cycle}(S^{\prime\prime})}(\mathbf{y})\right)

where
D[I]
and
D[I^{c}]
denote the subgraphs of
D
induced by
I
and
I^{c}
, respectively.

From Corollary
9
, for any
I\subseteq[n]
we have

\sum_{S^{\prime\prime}\text{ a cycle cover of }D[I^{c}]}p_{\text{cycle}(S^{\prime\prime})}(\mathbf{y})=\mathfrak{L}_{I^{c}}\det H_{\mathbf{y}}(XA).

On the other hand, we have

\sum_{S^{\prime}\text{ a path cover of }D[I]}\tilde{m}_{\text{path}(S^{\prime})}(\mathbf{z})=\mathfrak{L}_{I}W_{D[I]}(z_{1})W_{D[I]}(z_{2})\cdots

As noted in Section
2.2
,
W_{D[I]}(z)
is obtained from
W_{D}(z)
by setting
x_{j}=0
for
j\not\in I
. Thus we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{S^{\prime}\text{ a path cover of }D[I]}\tilde{m}_{\text{path}(S^{\prime})}(\mathbf{z})

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{L}_{I}\Bigg(W_{D}(z_{1})W_{D}(z_{2})\cdots\Big|_{x_{j}=0,\,j\not\in I}\Bigg)

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{L}_{I}W_{D}(z_{1})W_{D}(z_{2})\cdots

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{L}_{I}\det H_{\bf{z}}(XA)\det E_{\bf{z}}(X\overline{A}),

(16)

where the last line follows from Lemma 1 just as in the proof of Theorem 10(a).
Altogether this gives

\displaystyle\Xi_{D}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\sum_{I\subseteq[n]}\mathfrak{L}_{I}\det H_{\mathbf{z}}(XA)\det E_{\mathbf{z}}(X\overline{A})\cdot\mathfrak{L}_{I^{c}}\det H_{\mathbf{y}}(XA)

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{L}_{n}\det H_{\mathbf{z}}(XA)E_{\mathbf{z}}(X\overline{A})H_{\mathbf{y}}(XA)

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{L}_{n}\det H_{(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y})}(XA)E_{\mathbf{z}}(X\overline{A}),

from which we obtain

\displaystyle[\omega_{\mathbf{z}}\ \Xi_{\overline{D}}(\mathbf{z},-\mathbf{y})]_{\mathbf{z}\to(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y})}

\displaystyle=[\omega_{\mathbf{z}}\ \mathfrak{L}_{n}\det\left(H_{\mathbf{z}}(X\overline{A})H_{-\mathbf{y}}(X\overline{A})E_{\mathbf{z}}(XA)\right)]_{\mathbf{z}\to(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y})}

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{L}_{n}\det E_{(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y})}(X\overline{A})H_{-\mathbf{y}}(X\overline{A})H_{(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y})}(XA)

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{L}_{n}\det H_{(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y})}(XA)E_{\mathbf{z}}(X\overline{A})=\Xi_{D}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y}).

∎

In
[
3
, Section 6]
, Chow also defines

\hat{\Xi}_{D}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y})=\sum_{S}(-2)^{\ell(\mathrm{cycle}(S))}\tilde{m}_{\mathrm{path}(S)}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y})\,p_{\mathrm{cycle}(S)}(\mathbf{y})

where
\ell(\mathrm{cycle}(S))
is the number of cycles in
S
and the sum is over all path-cycle covers
S
of
D
. The series
\hat{\Xi}
was introduced because of the following involutive property:

Theorem 29
.

[
3
, Section 6]
For a digraph
D
on
[n]
we have,

\omega_{\mathbf{z}}\ \hat{\Xi}_{D}(\mathbf{z},-\mathbf{y})=\hat{\Xi}_{\overline{D}}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y}).

Proof.

Let
I\subseteq[n]
.
Substituting
\mathbf{z}\rightarrow(-\mathbf{y},-\mathbf{y})
in
Corollary
9

yields

\displaystyle\sum_{S\text{ a cycle cover of }D[I]}(-2)^{\ell(\mathrm{cycle}(S))}p_{\mathrm{cycle}(S)}(\mathbf{y})=\mathfrak{L}_{I}\det H_{\mathbf{y}}(XA)^{-2},

whereas substituting
\mathbf{z}\rightarrow(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y})
in equation (
16
) gives

\sum_{S^{\prime}\text{ a path cover of }D[I]}\tilde{m}_{\text{path}(S^{\prime})}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y})=\mathfrak{L}_{I}\det H_{\bf{z}}(XA)H_{\bf{y}}(XA)\det E_{\bf{z}}(X\overline{A})E_{\bf{y}}(X\overline{A}).

(17)

There follows

\hat{\Xi}_{D}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y})=\mathfrak{L}_{n}\ \dfrac{\det H_{\mathbf{z}}(XA)E_{\mathbf{z}}(X\overline{A})E_{\mathbf{y}}(X\overline{A})}{\det H_{\mathbf{y}}(XA)},

and consequently

\displaystyle\omega_{\mathbf{z}}\ \hat{\Xi}_{D}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{-y})

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{L}_{n}\dfrac{\det E_{\mathbf{z}}(XA)H_{\mathbf{z}}(X\overline{A})E_{-\mathbf{y}}(X\overline{A})}{\det H_{-\mathbf{y}}(XA)}

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{L}_{n}\dfrac{\det E_{\mathbf{z}}(XA)H_{\mathbf{z}}(X\overline{A})E_{\mathbf{y}}(XA)}{\det H_{\mathbf{y}}(X\overline{A})}

\displaystyle=\hat{\Xi}_{\overline{D}}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y}),

since
H_{-\mathbf{y}}(t)=1/E_{\mathbf{y}}(t)
and
E_{-\mathbf{y}}(t)=1/H_{\mathbf{y}}(t)
.
∎

Finally, we partially address a question of Grinberg and Stanley
[
12
, Question 1.18]
by generalizing Corollary
14
to expand the Chow path-cycle symmetric function in terms of power sums.

Theorem 30
.

Let
D
be a digraph on
[n]
. Then

\Xi_{D}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y})=\sum_{I\subseteq[n]}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{I}(\overline{D})\\
\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{I^{c}}(D)\end{subarray}}\sgn(\sigma)\cdot p_{\mathrm{cyc}(\sigma)}(\mathbf{z})\cdot p_{\mathrm{cyc}(\tau)}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y}).

Proof.

As seen in the proof of Theorem
28
,

\displaystyle\Xi_{D}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{L}_{n}\det H_{\mathbf{z}}(XA)E_{\mathbf{z}}(X\overline{A})H_{\mathbf{y}}(XA)

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{L}_{n}\det H_{(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y})}(XA)E_{\mathbf{z}}(X\overline{A})

\displaystyle=\sum_{I\subseteq[n]}\mathfrak{L}_{I}\det E_{\mathbf{z}}(X\overline{A})\cdot\mathfrak{L}_{I^{c}}\det H_{(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y})}(XA)

Now applying Lemma

we get

\Xi_{D}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y})=\sum_{I\subseteq[n]}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{I}\\
\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{I^{c}}\end{subarray}}\sgn(\sigma)\cdot p_{\mathrm{cyc}(\sigma)}(\mathbf{z})\cdot p_{\mathrm{cyc}(\tau)}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y})\prod_{i\in I^{c}}A_{i,\tau(i)}\prod_{j\in I}\overline{A}_{j,\sigma(j)}.

The result follows since the product over
I^{c}
and the product over
I
are non-zero in a given summand if and only if
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{I}(\overline{D})
