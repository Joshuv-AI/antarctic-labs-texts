Case presentation

A woman in her 90s with tachy-brady syndrome underwent implantation of a screw-in type atrial leadless pacemaker (Aveir AR, Abbott, Abbott Park, IL). The device was positioned at the base of the right atrial appendage. At implantation, the atrial pacing threshold was 2.75 V at 0.4 ms and the sensing amplitude was 0.6 mV. As the parameters showed improvement during the procedure, implantation was completed. By the following day, the pacing threshold improved to 0.75 V at 0.4 ms. The P-wave amplitude during sinus rhythm was within the normal range, and immediately after pacemaker implantation, atrial capture after pacing spikes was consistently observed (Figure 1A and 1B). At discharge, the device was programmed in AAI mode at 60 ppm, with an output of 1.5 V at 0.4 ms and a sensing sensitivity of 0.25 mV. 7 months later, she presented to her primary care physician with malaise and hypotension and was referred to our hospital. On arrival, electrocardiography demonstrated rapid atrial fibrillation with a ventricular rate of 150 beats/min. Verapamil (40 mg) was administered intravenously for rate control. Subsequently, sinus rhythm was restored, and the heart rate decreased to 55 beats/min despite the pacemaker being programmed in AAI mode with a backup pacing rate of 60 ppm.

Electrocardiography showed sinus rhythm with markedly attenuated P-wave amplitude, accompanied by atrial undersensing and loss of atrial capture (Figure 1C). Device interrogation revealed a marked reduction in atrial sensing amplitude and an increase in atrial pacing threshold (>5.0 V at 0.4 ms), consistent with loss of atrial capture.

As the patient remained hemodynamically stable, conservative management was chosen. By the following day, the atrial sensing amplitude had recovered to 0.5 mV and the pacing threshold improved to 0.75 V at 0.4 ms, consistent with a return toward baseline.

No further threshold fluctuations were observed during follow-up.

Discussion

Verapamil is widely used for rate control in patients with atrial fibrillation, particularly in emergency settings. However, its electrophysiological effects may occasionally extend beyond atrioventricular nodal suppression.

In the present case, intravenous verapamil was followed by marked attenuation of the intrinsic P-wave amplitude together with a substantial increase in atrial pacing threshold, resulting in atrial undersensing and loss of atrial capture.

Calcium channel blockade can reduce action potential amplitude and upstroke velocity in calcium-dependent myocardial fibers, thereby suppressing myocardial excitability. Furthermore, verapamil has been shown to exert minimal effects on normal atrial action potentials but to decrease depolarization amplitude and conduction in depressed atrial fibers.1,2 In elderly patients with atrial remodeling or reduced atrial myocardial voltage, these effects may therefore be exaggerated.3

A prior report described an increase in atrial pacing threshold and pacing failure after verapamil administration in a patient with a transvenous atrial pacemaker,4 for which a literal English translation is provided in Supplemental Document 1. Notably, this effect was observed after oral administration of a relatively high dose (240 mg), suggesting that similar effects may occur with oral therapy. In the present case, although the administered dose was modest, the patient’s advanced age and the intravenous route may have contributed to a more pronounced pharmacological effect. To our knowledge, no such cases have been reported in patients with atrial leadless pacemakers.

In addition, the electrophysiological effects of verapamil are more pronounced in calcium-dependent tissues, such as nodal and diseased atrial myocardium, than in fast-response ventricular myocardium. Therefore, this phenomenon may be particularly relevant in AAI pacing systems. In DDD pacemakers, ventricular pacing may compensate for atrial capture failure, potentially masking this phenomenon and leading to underrecognition in clinical practice.

Atrial leadless pacemakers with active fixation mechanisms are known to exhibit fluctuations in pacing thresholds after implantation. However, in the present case, pacing parameters had remained stable for 7 months before verapamil administration and rapidly returned to baseline after drug washout, supporting a pharmacological rather than mechanical cause.

Clinically, when pacing failure occurs immediately after verapamil administration in a hemodynamically stable patient, drug-induced suppression of atrial excitability should be suspected.
