A robust fully-mixed finite element method with skew-symmetry penalization for low-frequency poroelasticity
Stefano Bonetti, Michele Botti, and Patrick Vega

Abstract
In this work, we present and analyze a fully-mixed finite element scheme for the dynamic poroelasticity problem in the low-frequency regime. We write the problem as a four-field, first-order, hyperbolic system of equations where the symmetry constraint on the stress field is imposed via penalization. This strategy is equivalent to adding a perturbation to the saddle point system arising when the stress symmetry is weakly-imposed. The coupling of solid and fluid phases is discretized by means of stable mixed elements in space and implicit time advancing schemes. The presented stability analysis is fully robust with respect to meaningful cases of degenerate model parameters. Numerical tests validate the convergence and robustness and assess the performances of the method for the simulation of wave propagation phenomena in porous materials.



1
Introduction

The numerical modeling of coupled diffusion-deformation mechanisms in poroelastic media is relevant in several applications in geosciences, including subsidence due to fluid withdrawal, earthquake triggering due to pressure-induced faults slip, injection-production cycles in geothermal fields, and carbon dioxide storage in saline aquifers.
The poroelastic model has been consistently studied in its quasi-static formulations, where the acceleration effects are negligible. Some example of robust discretization schemes for the Biot’s consolidation problems in its quasi-static form can be found in
[
11
,
24
,
28
,
32
,
40
]
. However, for some practical applications, the quasi-static model is not sufficient for giving a comprehensive understanding of the phenomena and acceleration terms in both the solid and fluid phases need to be considered. This is the case of earthquake studies and the the studies of induced seismicity phenomena arising from soil exploitation activities. This model has been introduced by Biot in
[
10
]
and it has been show in
[
22
]
that the dynamic effects are crucial for understanding the wave propagation phenomena in poroelastic media.

In the literature the Biot’s poroelasticity model has been studied in different formulations, e.g. with the acceleration term introduced for the displacement field only
[
15
]
, or coupled with different physics, e.g. the fully-dynamic thermo-poroelastic model
[
14
,
21
]
.
Different method and approaches have been developed for studying this problem. First, these methods include finite difference, finite element,
boundary element, finite volume, and spectral methods
[
23
,
31
,
35
,
39
,
41
]
. More recent approaches
have considered high-order space-time continuous and discontinuous Galerkin methods
[
4
,
5
]
(also on polygonal grids), ADER scheme
[
30
]
, mixed finite element methods
[
34
]
, and hybridizable discontinuous Galerkin (HDG) method
[
38
]
.

In this work, we introduce and analyze a fully-mixed formulation of the dynamic poroelasticity problem having the stress tensor, the fluid flux, the displacement, and the pore pressure as unknowns. The symmetry constraint on the stress field is imposed by adding to the four-field formulation a penalization term depending on the skew-symmetric part of the stress and a penalization parameter that has to be properly selected. The main novel contributions include a robust stability analysis at both continuous and discrete level which encompasses all the meaningful cases of degenerate model coefficients.
The presented stability estimates does not depend on the dilatation and storage coefficients, showing the robustness with respect to volumetric locking and quasi-incompressible porous materials. Moreover, the a priori analysis also supports the case of vanishing densities parameters which make the model degenerate towards the quasi-static case. This is in contrast with respect to
[
34
]
and
[
38
]
were both the storage and densities coefficients are assumed to be strictly positive. Finally, we also mention that the results are obtained under weak data regularity and general boundary conditions. The case of mixed and non-homogeneous boundary conditions is often left out in the analysis of numerical schemes for poroelasticity problems, but is relevant for the practical applications and place several additional difficulties in the a priori analysis.

The rest of the paper is organised as follows.
In Section
2
, we present the dynamic poroelasticity problem written as a first-order hyperbolic system.
In Section
3
, we formulate the problem in variational form and present the main stability result.
Section
4
describes the discrete setting and contains the statement of the discrete problem.
Finally, Section
5
contains a thorough numerical validation of the convergence and robustness properties of the method. A physically-consistent test on wave propagation in poroelastic media is also considered.

2
Dynamic poroelasticity

In this section we present the low-frequency poroelasticity equations. We define the model data and parameters characterizing the initial-boundary value problem and then reformulate it as a first-order fully-coupled hyperbolic system.

2.1
Model problem

We consider wave propagation through fluid-saturated porous media in the low-frequency regime
[
10
]
. Letting
\phi\in(0,1)
denote the reference material porosity, we assume a continuous superposition of solid and fluid phases occupying
(1-\phi)
and
\phi
of the total volume
|\Omega|
, respectively. We refer the reader to
[
20
,
29
]
for the precise derivation of the dynamic problem presented hereafter.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\in\{2,3\}
, denote a bounded connected polyhedral domain with boundary
\partial\Omega
and outward normal
\boldsymbol{n}
. For a given time interval
(0,t_{\rm F}]
, with
t_{\rm F}>0
,
the problem data consist of the forcing term
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}:\Omega\times(0,t_{\rm F})\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
,
volumetric loads
\boldsymbol{f},\,\boldsymbol{h}:\Omega\times(0,t_{\rm F})\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
fluid source
g:\Omega\times(0,t_{\rm F})\to\mathbb{R}
,
initial solid displacement
\boldsymbol{d}_{0}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
initial solid and filtration velocities
\boldsymbol{u}_{0},\boldsymbol{w}_{0}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
and initial pore pressure
p_{0}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
.
Given the previous data, the poroelasticity initial-boundary value problem consists in finding a
displacement field
\boldsymbol{d}:\Omega\times[0,t_{\rm F})\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
velocity field
\boldsymbol{w}:\Omega\times[0,t_{\rm F})\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and a
pore pressure field
p:\Omega\times[0,t_{\rm F})\to\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle\rho_{u}\partial_{tt}\boldsymbol{d}+\rho_{f}\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{\nabla}{\cdot}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{f}

\displaystyle\text{in $\Omega\times(0,t_{\rm F})$},

(1a)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\mathcal{C}\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\rm s}\boldsymbol{d}+\alpha p\boldsymbol{I}_{d}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}

\displaystyle\text{in $\Omega\times(0,t_{\rm F})$},

(1b)

\displaystyle\rho_{f}\partial_{tt}\boldsymbol{d}+\rho_{w}\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{w}+\boldsymbol{K}^{-1}\boldsymbol{w}+\boldsymbol{\nabla}p

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{h}

\displaystyle\text{in $\Omega\times(0,t_{\rm F})$},

(1c)

\displaystyle s_{0}\partial_{t}p+\alpha\partial_{t}(\boldsymbol{\nabla}{\cdot}\boldsymbol{d})+\boldsymbol{\nabla}{\cdot}\boldsymbol{w}

\displaystyle=g

\displaystyle\text{in $\Omega\times(0,t_{\rm F})$},

(1d)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{d}(\cdot,0)=\boldsymbol{d}_{0},\quad p(\cdot,0)

\displaystyle=p_{0}

\displaystyle\text{in $\Omega$},

(1e)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{d}(\cdot,0)=\boldsymbol{u}_{0},\quad\boldsymbol{w}(\cdot,0)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{w}_{0}

\displaystyle\text{in $\Omega$}.

(1f)

In the momentum equilibrium equation (
1a
),
\partial_{t}
and
\partial_{tt}
denotes the first and second time derivative, while the parameter
\rho_{u}

is related to the solid and fluid density
\rho_{s},\rho_{f}>0
according to
\rho_{u}\coloneq\phi\rho_{f}+(1-\phi)\rho_{s}>0
.

In the constitutive relation for the stress (
1b
),
\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\rm s}
denotes the symmetric part of the gradient operator acting on vector-valued fields,

\mathcal{C}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d^{4}}
is the uniformly elliptic fourth-order tensor expressing the linear stress-strain law, and
\alpha\in(\phi,1]
is the Biot–Willis coefficient.
For isotropic materials, the tensor
\mathcal{C}
can be expressed in terms of the Lamé parameters
\mu\in[\underline{\mu},\overline{\mu}]
, with
0<\underline{\mu}<\overline{\mu}
,
and
\lambda\geq 0
as

\mathcal{C}\boldsymbol{\tau}\coloneq 2\mu\ \boldsymbol{\tau}+\lambda\ {\rm tr}(\boldsymbol{\tau})\boldsymbol{I}_{d}\quad\text{ for all $\tau\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$},

where
\boldsymbol{I}_{d}
denotes the identity matrix of
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and

{\rm tr}(\boldsymbol{\tau})\coloneq\sum_{i}^{d}\tau_{ii}
. Introducing the bulk modulus
\kappa\coloneq 2d^{-1}\mu+\lambda

and the deviatoric operator
\boldsymbol{\rm dev}(\boldsymbol{\tau})\coloneq\tau-d^{-1}{\rm tr}(\boldsymbol{\tau})\boldsymbol{I}_{d}
,
an alternative expression for
\mathcal{C}
is given by

\mathcal{C}\boldsymbol{\tau}=2\mu\ \boldsymbol{\rm dev}(\boldsymbol{\tau})+\kappa\ {\rm tr}(\boldsymbol{\tau})\boldsymbol{I}_{d}\quad\text{ for all $\tau\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$}.

(2)

In the dynamic Darcy equation (
1c
), the parameter
\rho_{w}
is such that
\rho_{w}\coloneq\rho_{f}\nu\phi^{-1}
, where
\nu>1
denotes the pores tortuosity,
while
\boldsymbol{K}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\rm s}
is the hydraulic conductivity tensor which, for strictly positive real numbers
\underline{K}\leq\overline{K}
, satisfies

\underline{K}|\boldsymbol{\xi}|^{2}\leq\boldsymbol{K}(\boldsymbol{x})\boldsymbol{\xi}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi}\leq\overline{K}|\boldsymbol{\xi}|^{2}
for every
\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega
and all
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

(3)

Clearly, as a result of (
3
), the uniquely defined inverse tensor
\boldsymbol{K}^{-1}
is also uniformly elliptic in the sense that

{\overline{K}\,}^{-1}|\boldsymbol{\xi}|^{2}\leq\boldsymbol{K}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{x})\boldsymbol{\xi}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi}\leq\underline{K}^{-1}|\boldsymbol{\xi}|^{2}
for every
\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega
and all
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

To be valid, the equation (
1c
) requires that the spectrum of the waves involve frequencies lower than a threshold depending on
\boldsymbol{K}
,
\rho_{f}
, and
\phi
(cf.
[
25
]
).
Otherwise, more complex models such as the one described in
[
36
]
are required.

Finally, in the mass conservation equation (
1d
),
s_{0}\geq 0
denotes the
constrained storage coefficient measuring the amount of fluid that can be forced into the medium
by pressure increments due to the structure compressibility. The relevant case of incompressible grains
corresponds to the limit value
s_{0}=0
.

We close the problem by prescribing the boundary conditions. We introduce two partitions
\partial\Omega=\Gamma_{d}\cup\Gamma_{\sigma}=\Gamma_{p}\cup\Gamma_{w}
such that
\Gamma_{d}\cap\Gamma_{\sigma}=\Gamma_{p}\cap\Gamma_{w}=\emptyset
, assuming that the measures of
\Gamma_{d}
and
\Gamma_{p}
are strictly positive. We impose a given traction on
\Gamma_{\sigma}
, a fixed displacement on
\Gamma_{d}
, a given flux on
\Gamma_{w}
, and a pressure on
\Gamma_{p}
, namely

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}\ \boldsymbol{n}=\boldsymbol{t}\quad\text{on }\Gamma_{\sigma}\times(0,t_{\rm F}]\qquad

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{w}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=w_{n}\quad\text{on }\Gamma_{w}\times(0,t_{\rm F}]

(4a)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{d}=\boldsymbol{d}_{d}\quad\text{on }\Gamma_{d}\times(0,t_{\rm F}]\qquad

\displaystyle p=p_{d}\quad\text{on }\Gamma_{p}\times(0,t_{\rm F}].

(4b)

2.2
First-order hyperbolic system

Different formulations of problem (
1
) have been studied in the literature, including the frequency-domain displacement-pressure formulation of
[
23
]
;
the two-displacement formulation
[
37
,
5
]
, that is obtained by inserting the expression of the total stress
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
and the pore pressure
p
from (
1b
) and (
1d
) in the other equations; and the three-field formulations of
[
2
]
considering the solid displacement, solid velocity, and pressure as unknowns.
In what follows, we focus on the first-order fully-mixed formulation considered, e.g., in
[
34
,
35
]
.
This choice leads to several advantages:
(i)
it produces a better approximation of the Darcy velocity and stress fields;
(ii)
mass and momentum conservation are locally satisfied;
(iii)
additional assumptions on the poroelastic coeffcient are not required; and
(iv)
it is easier to analyze the problem in the framework of strongly continuous semigroups (cf.
[
38
]
).

We introduce the solid velocity
\boldsymbol{u}=\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{d}
and assume that the initial displacement field
\boldsymbol{d}_{0}
is sufficiently regular to associate the initial stress tensor, defined according to (
1b
) as
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{0}=\mathcal{C}\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\rm s}\boldsymbol{d}_{0}-\alpha p_{0}\boldsymbol{I}_{d}
.
The uniformly elliptic fourth-order compliance tensor
\mathcal{A}
is defined such that
\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{C}\boldsymbol{\tau})=\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{A}\boldsymbol{\tau})=\boldsymbol{\tau}
for all
\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. The explicit expression of the action of
\mathcal{A}
derived from (
2
) is given by

\mathcal{A}\boldsymbol{\tau}\coloneq\frac{\boldsymbol{\rm dev}(\boldsymbol{\tau})}{2\mu}+\frac{{\rm tr}(\boldsymbol{\tau})}{d^{2}\kappa}\boldsymbol{I}_{d}.

(5)

We also observe that, taking the trace of the tensor equation (
1b
), allows to express the divergence of the solid displacement as

\kappa\boldsymbol{\nabla}{\cdot}\boldsymbol{d}=\alpha p+d^{-1}{\rm tr}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}).

Thus, taking the time derivative in the previous identity and in (
1b
), plugging the resulting expression into (
1d
), and owing to the definition of the compliance tensor (
5
), we rewrite the dynamic poroelasticity problem as a first-order system:
find the velocities
\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{w}
, the stress tensor
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
, and pressure
p
such that

\displaystyle\rho_{u}\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}+\rho_{f}\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{\nabla}{\cdot}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{f}

\displaystyle\text{in $\Omega\times(0,t_{\rm F})$},

(6a)

\displaystyle\rho_{f}\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}+\rho_{w}\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{w}+\boldsymbol{K}^{-1}\boldsymbol{w}+\boldsymbol{\nabla}p

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{h}

\displaystyle\text{in $\Omega\times(0,t_{\rm F})$},

(6b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\sigma})+\alpha(d\kappa)^{-1}\partial_{t}p\boldsymbol{I}_{d}-\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\rm s}\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\eta}

\displaystyle\text{in $\Omega\times(0,t_{\rm F})$},

(6c)

\displaystyle(s_{0}+\alpha^{2}\kappa^{-1})\partial_{t}p+\alpha(d\kappa)^{-1}{\rm tr}(\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\sigma})+\boldsymbol{\nabla}{\cdot}\boldsymbol{w}

\displaystyle=g

\displaystyle\text{in $\Omega\times(0,t_{\rm F})$},

(6d)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}(\cdot,0)=\boldsymbol{u}_{0},\;\ \boldsymbol{w}(\cdot,0)=\boldsymbol{w}_{0}\;\ \boldsymbol{\sigma}(\cdot,0)=\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{0},\;\ p(\cdot,0)

\displaystyle=p_{0}

\displaystyle\text{in $\Omega$},

(6e)

where the right-hand side term in (
6c
) is defined by
\boldsymbol{\eta}=\partial_{t}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
.

The well-posedness of problem (
6
) has been established in
[
38
, Section 3]
by applying the Hille–Yosida theory. One of the main ingredient needed to establish a stability result for the previous system consists in pointing out the the coupling between the solid and filtration velocities as well as the coupling between the pressure and the stress trace are symmetric and positive, in the sense that the interaction matrices

R_{1}\coloneq\begin{pmatrix}\rho_{u}&\rho_{f}\\
\rho_{f}&\rho_{w}\end{pmatrix}\qquad\text{and }\quad R_{2}\coloneq\frac{1}{d\kappa}\begin{pmatrix}d^{-1}\;&\alpha\\
\alpha\;&s_{0}d\kappa+d\alpha^{2}\end{pmatrix}

(7)

are elliptic. Indeed, due to the definition of the model parameters given in Section
2.1
, we have
{\rm det}(R_{1})=(\phi^{-1}-1)\rho_{s}\rho_{f}\nu+(\nu-1)\rho_{f}^{2}\geq 0
and
{\rm det}(R_{2})=s_{0}d^{-1}\geq 0
.

3
Variational formulation

In this section we introduce some notations that will be used in the sequel, and derive the mixed variational formulation. First, we present a five-field formulation where the stress symmetry is enforce weakly as in
[
33
,
34
]
. Then, we introduce a stabilization term that allows to rewrite the previous problem as a four-field system with a penalization of the skew-symmetric part of the stress.

3.1
Notation for functional spaces

Let

.
Spaces of functions, vector fields, and tensor fields defined over
X
are respectively denoted by italic capital, boldface Roman capital, and special Roman capital letters.
The subscripts “s” and “sk” appended to a special Roman capital letter denotes a space of symmetric and skew-symmetric tensor fields, respectively.
Thus, for example,
L^{2}(X),\textbf{L}^{2}(X)
, and
\mathbb{L}^{2}_{{\rm sk}}(X)
respectively denote the spaces of square integrable functions, vector fields, and skew-symmetric tensor fields over
X
.

For any measured set

, we denote by

the usual Sobolev space of functions that have weak partial derivatives of order up to
m

L^{2}(X)
.
We denote by

and

the usual scalar products in
L^{2}(X)
and

respectively, and by
\|{\cdot}\|_{X}
and

The subscript
X
is omitted whenever

.
The notation

is used for the duality product between two functions

and

.
Additionally, let

be the subspace of

spanned by vector-valued functions admitting a weak divergence in

with natural norm

\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{{\rm div},X}\coloneq\left(\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{X}^{2}+h_{X}^{2}\|\boldsymbol{\nabla}{\cdot}\boldsymbol{v}\|_{X}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

with

denoting the diameter of

.
We define

to be the space of tensor-valued functions in

with weak divergence in

For a vector space
V

(\cdot,\cdot)_{V}
, the space

is spanned by

is spanned by

, respectively; and define the Hilbert spaces

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{U}:=\textbf{L}^{2}(\Omega),

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{W}

\displaystyle:=\{\boldsymbol{z}\in\textbf{H}({\rm div},\Omega)\,:\,\langle\boldsymbol{w}\cdot\boldsymbol{n},\xi\rangle_{\partial\Omega}=0\ \ \forall\xi\in H^{1}_{0,\Gamma_{p}}(\Omega)\},\;

, and introduce the short-handed notation

,
\boldsymbol{\rm skw}
, and

operators, we observe that for all
\boldsymbol{\tau},\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\tau}:{\rm tr}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\boldsymbol{I}_{d}

\displaystyle={\rm tr}(\boldsymbol{\xi}){\rm tr}(\boldsymbol{\tau}),

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\tau}:\boldsymbol{\rm skw}(\boldsymbol{\xi})

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\rm skw}(\boldsymbol{\tau}):\boldsymbol{\rm skw}(\boldsymbol{\xi}),

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\tau}:\boldsymbol{\rm dev}(\boldsymbol{\xi})

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\rm dev}(\boldsymbol{\tau}):\boldsymbol{\rm dev}(\boldsymbol{\xi}),

with the symbol “

3.2
Weak-symmetry and skew-symmetry penalization

In the mixed formulation of elasticity and poroelasticity problems, the symmetry of the stress tensor
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
has to be explicitly enforced, either strongly in the functional space (see, e.g.,
[
8
,
42
,
19
]
), or weakly by the use of a Lagrange multiplier as in
[
7
,
33
,
34
]
.
In what follows, we opt for this second strategy since it leads to a formulation that can be discretized with low order standard finite element spaces.

We introduce the skew-symmetric part of
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{u}
, i.e.
\boldsymbol{\rm skw}(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{u})
, as additional unknown, named
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
, we add to the hyperbolic system (
6
) the equation
\boldsymbol{\rm skw}(\dot{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})=\boldsymbol{0}
, and we replace equation (
6c
) by

\mathcal{A}\dot{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}+\alpha(d\kappa)^{-1}\dot{p}\boldsymbol{I}_{d}-\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{u}+\boldsymbol{\zeta}=\boldsymbol{\eta}\qquad\text{in $\Omega\times(0,t_{\rm F})$}.

The weak formulation of the dynamic poroelasticity problem (
6
) is derived by testing with suitable functions and integrating by parts in (
6b
) and (
6a
).
Let
\boldsymbol{\eta}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega))
,
\boldsymbol{f}\in L^{2}(\textbf{L}^{2}(\Omega))
,
\boldsymbol{h}\in L^{2}(\textbf{L}^{2}(\Omega))
,

,
\boldsymbol{u}_{d}=\dot{\boldsymbol{d}}_{d}\in L^{2}(\boldsymbol{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\partial\Omega))
, and

. For simplicity, we assume
that
\boldsymbol{t}=\boldsymbol{0}
and
w_{n}=0
in (
4a
). The general case of non-homogeneous essential conditions can be obtained by the
\mathbb{H}({\rm div},\Omega)
-regular lifting of boundary data as in
[
18
, Appendix A]
.
The weak formulation with weakly enforced stress symmetry reads:
for almost every

such that

\displaystyle(\rho_{u}\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}+\rho_{f}\dot{\boldsymbol{w}},\boldsymbol{v})-(\boldsymbol{\nabla}{\cdot}\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle=(\boldsymbol{f},\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{U},

(8a)

\displaystyle(\rho_{f}\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}+\rho_{w}\dot{\boldsymbol{w}},\boldsymbol{z})\hskip-1.42262pt+\hskip-1.42262pt(\boldsymbol{K}^{-1}\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{z})-(p,\boldsymbol{\nabla}{\cdot}\boldsymbol{z})

\displaystyle=(\boldsymbol{h},\boldsymbol{z})\hskip-1.42262pt+\hskip-1.42262pt\langle p_{d},\boldsymbol{z}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}\rangle

\displaystyle\forall\boldsymbol{z}\in\boldsymbol{W},

(8b)

\displaystyle(\mathcal{A}\dot{\boldsymbol{\sigma}},\boldsymbol{\tau})\hskip-1.42262pt+\hskip-1.42262pt\Big(\alpha\dot{p},\frac{{\rm tr}(\boldsymbol{\tau})}{d\kappa}\Big)\hskip-1.42262pt+\hskip-1.42262pt(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{\nabla}{\cdot}\boldsymbol{\tau})\hskip-1.42262pt+\hskip-1.42262pt(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\boldsymbol{\tau})

\displaystyle=(\boldsymbol{\eta},\boldsymbol{\tau})+\langle\boldsymbol{u}_{d},\boldsymbol{\tau}\boldsymbol{n}\rangle

\displaystyle\forall\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\boldsymbol{\Sigma},

(8c)

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{s_{0}\kappa+\alpha^{2}}{\kappa}\dot{p},q\Big)\hskip-1.42262pt+\hskip-1.42262pt\Big(\frac{{\rm tr}(\dot{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})}{d\kappa},\alpha q\Big)\hskip-1.42262pt+\hskip-1.42262pt(\boldsymbol{\nabla}{\cdot}\boldsymbol{w},q)

\displaystyle\forall q\in P,

(8d)

\displaystyle-(\boldsymbol{\rm skw}(\dot{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}),\boldsymbol{\xi})

\displaystyle=0

3.3
Well-posedness

This section investigates the well-posedness of the mixed variational problem
(

[

[

In what follows, we focus on proving a stability result under weaker assumptions, which in turn also implies the uniqueness of solutions.

First, we present some preliminary results.
We recall the following inf-sup conditions establishing the stability of the divergence operator.

Lemma 2

There exist positive constants

(12)

Lemma 3

Theorem 5

(Stability)

\lambda

s_{0}

\rho_{u},\rho_{f}

\rho_{w}

Proof.

The proof is divided into several steps:
(i)

(ii)
we infer the control of

and

Using the discrete inf-sup condition (

\displaystyle\beta_{m}\|\boldsymbol{d}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathcal{L}_{3}\|_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{*}}+\|(2\mu)^{-1}\boldsymbol{\rm dev}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})\|+\|(d\kappa)^{-1}({\rm tr}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})+\alpha dp)\|,

\displaystyle\leq C_{4}C_{\rm pb}^{2}+\frac{C_{1}}{2}\|\boldsymbol{d}(t)\|^{2}\hskip-2.84526pt+\frac{C_{2}}{2}\|\tilde{p}(t)\|^{2}\hskip-2.84526pt+C_{5}\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(t)\|_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{2}\hskip-2.84526pt+C_{5}\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{w}}(t)\|_{\rm div}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{4}C_{\rm pb}^{2}+\frac{C_{1}}{2}\|\boldsymbol{d}(t)\|^{2}\hskip-2.84526pt+\frac{C_{2}}{2}\|\tilde{p}(t)\|^{2}\hskip-2.84526pt+\frac{C_{3}}{2}\|\boldsymbol{\nabla}{\cdot}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(t)\|^{2}\hskip-2.84526pt+\frac{C_{3}}{2}\|\boldsymbol{\nabla}{\cdot}\tilde{\boldsymbol{w}}(t)\|^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{4}\|(2\mu)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{\rm dev}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\hskip-2.84526pt\|\boldsymbol{K}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{w}(s)\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s,

with positive constants
C_{4}
and

independent of

, and

. Plugging the two previous bounds into (

) and using the second inequality in (

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{d}(t)\|^{2}\hskip-1.42262pt+\|\tilde{p}(t)\|^{2}\hskip-1.42262pt+\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(t)\|_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{2}\hskip-1.42262pt+\|(2\mu)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{\rm dev}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}(t))\|^{2}+\|\varepsilon^{-\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{\rm skw}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}(t))\|^{2}+

\displaystyle\|R_{1}^{\frac{1}{2}}[\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{w}]^{\rm T}(t)\|^{2}+\|R_{2}^{\frac{1}{2}}[{\rm tr}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}),p]^{\rm T}(t)\|^{2}+\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{w}}(t)\|_{\rm div}^{2}+\hskip-2.84526pt\int_{0}^{t}\hskip-2.84526pt\|\boldsymbol{K}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{w}(s)\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\lesssim C_{\rm pb}^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}C_{\rm pb}(\|\boldsymbol{d}(s)\|\hskip-1.42262pt+\|\tilde{p}(s)\|\hskip-1.42262pt+\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(s)\|_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\hskip-1.42262pt)+\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{w}}(s)\|_{\rm div}\big)\,\mathrm{d}s.

The conclusion follows from the Gronwall-type inequality in

.

4
Discrete setting

In this section we present the fully discrete formulation of the weak problem (
11
). The spatial discretization is based on coupling two mixed finite element methods, one for the Hellinger–Reissner formulation of linear elasticity and the other for mixed Darcy problems.
Weakening the symmetry constraint as in (
11
) allows the use of simpler elements for the stress unknown usually requiring less computational cost. For the time discretization, we employ implicit time-advancing schemes such as
\rm{BDF}
or
\theta
-method.
For the sake of simplifying the presentation, in this section we focus on the backward Euler scheme.

4.1
Space and time meshes and discrete spaces

The spatial discretization is based on a conforming triangulation
\mathcal{T}_{h}
of
\Omega
, i.e. a set of closed triangles or tetrahedra with union
\overline{\Omega}
and such that, for any distinct
T_{1},T_{2}\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
, the set
T_{1}\cap T_{2}
is either a common edge, a vertex, the empty set or, if
d=3
, a common face. We denote by
h_{T}
the diameter of
T
and we set

h\coloneq\max_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}h_{T}>0
.
Having in mind a
h
-convergence analysis, we consider a sequence
(\mathcal{T}_{h})_{h\in\mathcal{H}}
of refined meshes that is shape-regular in the usual sense of Ciarlet
[
27
]
.

The time mesh is obtained subdividing
[0,t_{\rm F}]
into
N\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
uniform subintervals.
We introduce the time step
\tau\coloneq t_{\rm F}/N
and the discrete times
t^{n}\coloneq n\tau
,
n\in\llbracket 0,N\rrbracket
.
For any vector space
V
, all
\varphi\in C^{0}(V)
, and all
n\in\llbracket 0,N\rrbracket
, we let, for the sake of brevity,
\varphi^{n}\coloneq\varphi(t^{n})
.
We also let, for all
(\varphi^{i})_{0\leq i\leq N}\in V^{N+1}
and all
1\leq n\leq N
,

\delta_{t}^{n}\varphi\coloneq\frac{\varphi^{n}-\varphi^{n-1}}{\tau}\in V

denote the backward approximation of the first derivative of
\varphi
at time
t^{n}
.

On the mesh
\mathcal{T}_{h}
, with
h\in\mathcal{H}
, we introduce appropriate finite dimensional spaces

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{h}

\displaystyle\subset\boldsymbol{\Sigma},\qquad\boldsymbol{W}_{h}\subset\boldsymbol{W},\qquad\boldsymbol{U}_{h}\subset\boldsymbol{U},\qquad P_{h}\subset P.

(25)

The discrete space
P_{h}
(resp.
\boldsymbol{U}_{h}
) approximating
L^{2}(\Omega)
(resp.
\textbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)
) is usually composed of scalar-valued (resp. vector-valued) piecewise discontinuous polynomial functions on
\mathcal{T}_{h}
.
In Section
5
, the notation
DG_{l}
is used the space spanned by fully discontinuous polynomials of degree at most
l
.
The
L^{2}
-projector
\Pi_{h}:L^{2}(X)\to P_{h}
is defined such that

(\Pi_{h}q-q,q_{h})=0\quad\text{ for all }q_{h}\in P_{h}.

When dealing with the space
\boldsymbol{U}_{h}
, we use the boldface notation
\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{h}
for the corresponding
L^{2}
-projector acting component-wise.

In what follows, we assume that the pair
(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{h},\boldsymbol{U}_{h})
admits a stable element for the weak-symmetry Hellinger–Reissner formulation of linear elasticity in the sense that, there exist a finite dimensional space
\Lambda_{h}
such that the triplet
(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{h},\boldsymbol{U}_{h},\Lambda_{h})
satisfies the assumptions in
[
34
, Section 3]
(see, e.g., the Arnold–Falk–Winther element of
[
7
]
and the elements presented in
[
13
]
).
Moreover, the finite element pair
(\boldsymbol{W}_{h},P_{h})
is a stable element for the Darcy problem. In Section
5
, we will consider the standard Raviart–Thomas and Brezzi-Douglas-Marini families.

4.2
Fully-discrete problem

The crucial step in the design of the spatial discretization of problem (
11
) is the selection of the skew-symmetry penalization parameter
\varepsilon
. It has to be small enough so that the solution to the perturbed problem is close to the one of (
8
), meaning that the magnitude of the perturbation matches the accuracy of the discretization error.
At the same time, taking
\varepsilon
too small may lead to a restrictive constraint to the space
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{h}
which would compromise the inf-sup stability. In what follows, we choose
\varepsilon
to be piecewise constant on
\mathcal{T}_{h}
such that

\varepsilon_{|T}=\gamma h_{T}^{r}
for all
T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
, where
\gamma>0
is a user-dependent penalty parameter and
r
denotes the convergence rate of the
L^{2}
-approximation in
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{h}
of a sufficiently regular function.

The discretization in space and time of problem (
11
) consists in finding, for all
1\leq n\leq N
, the discrete solutions
(\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{n},\boldsymbol{w}_{h}^{n},\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}^{n},p_{h}^{n})\in\boldsymbol{U}_{h}\times\boldsymbol{W}_{h}\times\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{h}\times P_{h}
such that

\displaystyle\left(R_{1}\begin{bmatrix}\delta_{t}^{n}{\boldsymbol{u}_{h}}\\
\delta_{t}^{n}{\boldsymbol{w}_{h}}\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\\
\boldsymbol{z}_{h}\end{bmatrix}\right)+(\boldsymbol{K}^{-1}\boldsymbol{w}_{h}^{n},\boldsymbol{z}_{h})

\displaystyle-\left(\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\nabla}{\cdot}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}^{n}\\

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{\boldsymbol{\rm dev}(\delta_{t}^{n}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}})}{2\mu}+\frac{\boldsymbol{\rm skw}(\delta_{t}^{n}{\boldsymbol{\sigma_{h}}})}{\gamma h^{r}},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}\Big)+

\displaystyle\left(R_{2}\hskip-1.42262pt\begin{bmatrix}{\rm tr}(\delta_{t}^{n}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}})\\
\delta_{t}^{n}{p_{h}}\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}{\rm tr}(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h})\\
q_{h}\end{bmatrix}\right)

\displaystyle+\left(\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{n}\\

Then, for all

The uniqueness of solutions can be inferred from (

A stability estimate as (
