Quantum Reinforcement Learning with Dynamic-Circuit Qubit Reuse and Grover-Based Trajectory Optimization
Thet Htar Su, Shaswot Shresthamali, Masaaki Kondo

Abstract
A fully quantum reinforcement learning framework is developed that integrates a quantum Markov decision process, dynamic circuit-based qubit reuse, and Grover’s algorithm for trajectory optimization. The framework encodes states, actions, rewards, and transitions entirely within the quantum domain, enabling parallel exploration of state–action sequences through superposition and eliminating classical subroutines. Dynamic circuit operations, including mid-circuit measurement and reset, allow reuse of the same physical qubits across multiple agent–environment interactions, reducing qubit requirements from 7 × T \times T to 7 for T T time steps while preserving logical continuity. Quantum arithmetic computes trajectory returns, and Grover’s search is applied to the superposition of these evaluated trajectories to amplify the probability of measuring those with the highest return, thereby accelerating the identification of the optimal policy. Simulations demonstrate that the dynamic-circuit-based implementation preserves trajectory fidelity while reducing qubit usage by 66 percent relative to the static design. Experimental deployment on IBM Heron-class quantum hardware confirms that the framework operates within the constraints of current quantum processors and validates the feasibility of fully quantum multi-step reinforcement learning under noisy intermediate-scale quantum conditions. This framework advances the scalability and practical application of quantum reinforcement learning for large-scale sequential decision-making tasks. Keywords: quantum reinforcement learning, dynamic circuits, quantum Markov decision process, qubit reuse, Grover’s algorithm, policy optimization, NISQ devices



I
Introduction

Classical reinforcement learning (RL) has achieved remarkable success in sequential decision-making tasks; however, it often suffers from slow convergence and substantial computational demands. Inefficient exploration and high sample complexity remain persistent challenges, particularly in large or complex environments where extensive interactions are required to identify optimal policies
Dulac-Arnold et al. 2021
. To address these limitations, quantum reinforcement learning (QRL) has emerged as a promising paradigm that leverages quantum computing to overcome the computational bottlenecks of classical RL
Jerbi et al. 2021a
;
Chen
. By exploiting superposition, entanglement, and amplitude amplification, QRL offers the potential to accelerate exploration, reduce sample complexity, and enhance policy optimization beyond classical capabilities.

Early research has explored hybrid quantum–classical approaches, where quantum circuits are integrated into selected components of the RL pipeline or quantum-inspired policy optimization algorithms are developed
Meyer et al.
. While such hybrid methods provide valuable insights, they also introduce drawbacks. One critical issue is the reliance on classical–quantum data conversion, which creates input–output bottlenecks that slow computation, introduce noise, and reduce the benefits of quantum acceleration. These issues are particularly severe in the noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) regime, where devices are constrained by short coherence times, limited qubit connectivity, and error-prone operations
Preskill 2018
.

To fully realize the advantages of quantum acceleration, researchers have proposed fully quantum RL approaches in which the agent, environment, and learning processes are implemented natively in the quantum domain
Wiedemann et al. 2023
;
Su et al. 2025
. While the previous work
Wiedemann et al. 2023
established a proof of concept, its applicability to real-world problems remains limited. The study in
Su et al. 2025
, in particular, revealed scalability challenges, as it requires a large number of qubits to model multiple interaction steps, causing resource demands to grow rapidly with the complexity of the environment dynamics.
For instance, solving an environment with four states and two actions required seven qubits for a single interaction, and extending this to
T
interactions would demand 7
\times T
qubits. Such qubit overhead is prohibitive on small quantum processors, where effective qubit counts are already constrained by hardware imperfections
Pawar et al. 2025
. Consequently, while prior work demonstrates feasibility, it remains impractical for handling larger or more complex environments on near-term hardware.

These challenges highlight a fundamental scalability barrier: richer interaction modeling in fully quantum RL has so far been achievable only at the cost of dramatically increased qubit requirements, exceeding the capacity of both NISQ devices and classical simulators.
To overcome this barrier, QRL framework must be redesigned to preserve the benefits of quantum parallelism while drastically reducing physical qubit usage. A promising direction is the use of dynamic circuits, which enables mid-circuit measurement and qubit reuse during execution, allowing the same physical qubits to perform multiple roles within a computation. This capability directly addresses the qubit-scaling problem by recycling qubits for different purposes over the course of a computation, enabling more complex RL dynamics to be executed with fewer qubits.
Dynamic circuits are already supported in superconducting
Nation
and trapped-ion
Pino et al. 2021
architectures, with recent demonstrations on IBM processors
Johnson
confirming their practical feasibility.

Building on these advances, this work proposes a scalable fully quantum RL framework based on dynamic circuit to recycle qubits across interaction steps through mid-circuit measurement and reset. This design substantially reduces qubit requirements, improves scalability, and enables the realization of a fully quantum RL system on near-term hardware. Within this framework, all computations, from state encoding to trajectory search, are performed entirely within the quantum domain, ensuring efficient resource utilization. The primary contribution lies in demonstrating that multi-step agent–environment interactions can be implemented in a quantum circuit without increasing the qubit count, thereby enabling large-scale Markov decision process (MDP) to be solved fully within the quantum domain while remaining compatible with the constraints of near-term quantum devices. The key contributions of this work are as follows:

(1)

Quantum agent–environment representation: A fully quantum encoding of states, actions, rewards, and state transitions using superposition, enabling parallel exploration of state–action pairs.

(2)

Qubit-efficient multiple interactions via dynamic circuits: A quantum MDP (QMDP) design that reuses qubits through mid-circuit measurement and reset, reducing qubit requirements and aligning with the constraints of quantum hardware.

(3)

Quantum trajectory search with Grover’s algorithm: An efficient method to identify the maximum-reward trajectory, enabling faster exploration of trajectories and accelerating policy optimization.

II
Related Work

Quantum reinforcement learning (QRL) has developed along two main approaches: hybrid quantum–classical methods, which combine classical RL algorithms with quantum principles, and fully quantum methods, which aim to implement all RL components entirely within the quantum domain. The following review summarizes key developments in both areas, leading to the specific gap our work addresses.

II.1
Hybrid Quantum-Classical Reinforcement Learning

Hybrid quantum–classical methods include quantum-inspired RL (QiRL), variational quantum circuit (VQC)-based RL, and RL methods leveraging quantum subroutines.

QiRL represented the initial integration of quantum computing (QC) and RL, aiming to overcome classical limitations such as slow learning, the exploration–exploitation trade-off, and high computational costs
Meyer et al.
. By leveraging quantum principles such as superposition and amplitude amplification, researchers advanced the field from representing actions and policies within the quantum domain
Dong et al. 2008
;
Dao-Yi et al. 2006
;
Chen and Dong 2008
;
CHEN et al. 2006
;
Dong et al. 2012
;
Ganger and Hu 2019
;
Cho et al. 2024
to embedding experiences in deep RL
Wei et al. 2022
. These theoretical developments were validated in diverse domains, including robotics
Dao-Yi et al. 2006
;
Dong et al. 2012
, unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs)
Li et al. 2021
, and clinical decision-making
Li et al. 2020
;
Niraula et al. 2021
, demonstrating the potential of QC to accelerate learning and reduce computational overhead in real-world applications
Meyer et al.
.

VQC-based RL employed variational quantum circuits (VQCs) as replacements for classical neural networks in deep RL to address the limitations of QiRL approach in high-dimensional problems
Sequeira et al. 2023
. VQCs were initially used as function approximators in double deep Q-learning for simple environments like Frozen Lake
Chen et al. 2020
, and later extended to more complex tasks such as Cart Pole
Lockwood and Si 2020
, Atari games
Lockwood and Si 2021
, and continuous action spaces
Wu et al. 2025
. Some research
Skolik et al. 2022
showed that performance depended on architecture and hyperparameter choices, enabling significant model simplification compared to classical methods. Beyond value-based methods, VQCs were also applied in policy-based algorithms such as REINFORCE
Sequeira et al. 2023
;
Jerbi et al. 2021b
and Actor–Critic
Kwak et al. 2021
;
Lan

RL with quantum subroutines integrated specific quantum algorithms into classical RL workflows. Notable examples included quantum value iteration, which combined quantum mean estimation and maximum finding into a classical value iteration algorithm to reduce sample complexity
Wang et al. 2021
, and quantum policy iteration, which alternated between quantum policy evaluation via quantum linear system solvers and classical policy improvement based on quantum measurements
Cherrat et al. 2023

II.2
Full Quantum Reinforcement Learning

Earlier work in fully quantum RL
Wiedemann et al. 2023
introduced a quantum policy iteration framework that leveraged quantum superposition for trajectory generation, amplitude estimation for policy evaluation, and Grover’s algorithm for policy improvement. This approach substantially reduced sample complexity relative to classical RL.
A notable recent contribution
Su et al. 2025
advanced this direction by implementing all components of a finite-state Markov decision process (MDP), including state representation, action selection, state transitions, and the reward function, into a unified quantum circuit. Their method employed quantum arithmetic circuits to compute return values and Grover’s algorithm for trajectory optimization, thereby accelerating the RL process and demonstrating superior performance over classical methods in multi-state, multi-step environments.

However, this prior work
Su et al. 2025
faced significant scalability limitations due to its substantial quantum resource overhead, particularly the large number of qubits required for simultaneous computation across multiple interaction stages. Specifically, their quantum circuit required 7 qubits for a single quantum agent–environment interaction to encode the MDP components as quantum states. For an environment that must execute
T
sequential interactions, the quantum circuit expanded linearly to 7×
T
qubits, which quickly used up the available qubit resources. This extensive qubit usage posed a significant challenge to the practical deployment of the framework for larger-scale RL problems, which require many sequential interactions for the learning process to converge to an optimal policy. Furthermore, this growth in qubit demand is especially inefficient for small quantum processors, where calibration losses, limited connectivity, and error-prone qubits already constrain usable qubit counts. As a result, deploying this approach on near-term devices for larger-scale RL problems was impractical.

To address this challenge, our work incorporates dynamic circuit-based qubit reuse, leveraging recent hardware capabilities such as mid-circuit measurement and reset to recycle qubits across interaction steps. These operations allow qubits to be measured, reset, and reused within the same computation, thereby reducing the total number of physical qubits required and improving hardware utilization. This approach significantly lowers qubit demands, enhances scalability, and enables the practical implementation of a fully quantum RL system on quantum hardware.

This dynamic circuit approach has been explored in a number of notable studies aimed at improving qubit resource efficiency. CaQR
Hua et al. 2023
employed dynamic circuits to automatically identify reuse opportunities within a given circuit and compiled a qubit-efficient version that preserved correctness while lowering hardware demands. DeCross et al.
DeCross et al. 2023
introduced qubit-reuse compilation, compressing an 80-qubit QAOA circuit into a 20-qubit implementation, and QRCC
Pawar et al. 2025
integrated qubit reuse with circuit cutting to reduce both the number of required physical qubits and the number of circuit partitions, leading to lower classical post-processing costs and higher fidelity.

Collectively, these studies demonstrate that dynamic-circuit-based qubit reuse is a practical and scalable method for reducing resource requirements, forming the foundation for our proposed extension of a fully quantum RL system.
Our work incorporates dynamic circuits into a fully quantum RL framework in which both the agent and the environment are represented within the quantum domain, enabling direct quantum agent–environment interaction. This direct interaction allows for the simultaneous evaluation of multiple states and actions, thereby enhancing computational parallelism and accelerating the exploration of the state–action space. Through qubit reuse, our framework also supports sequential interactions over multiple time steps with a substantially reduced qubit requirement compared to prior work
Su et al. 2025
. The combination of qubit reuse and direct communication enables the exploration of a large number of possible interaction sequences in parallel without the excessive qubit growth that an earlier approach demanded. Finally, by applying Grover’s algorithm to optimize trajectories directly over the quantum states generated from these interactions, our approach eliminates dependence on classical subroutines and achieves significant computational speedups in multi-state, multi-step MDPs. Together, these innovations establish a scalable, fully quantum RL framework that improves both qubit efficiency and practical applicability, paving the way for deploying fully quantum RL systems in larger and more complex decision-making scenarios on near-term quantum hardware.

III
Background

III.1
Reinforcement learning

Reinforcement learning (RL) is a framework for sequential decision-making, in which an agent interacts with an environment to learn policies that maximize cumulative rewards over time
Sutton and Barto 2018
;
Graesser and Keng 2020
. The fundamental distinction between RL and other machine learning paradigms lies on its trial-and-error learning, whereby the agent refines its strategy based on feedback from both successes and failures
Goodfellow et al. 2016
. This adaptability enables RL to address complex decision-making problems in uncertain environments and has driven progress in areas such as autonomous driving
Shalev-Shwartz et al.
, robotics
Kober et al. 2013
, and game playing
Silver et al. 2017
;
Brown and Sandholm 2019
.

Formally, RL is modeled as a Markov Decision Process (MDP), defined by the state space
S
, action space
A
, reward function
\textit{R(s,a)}:=\mathbb{E}[r_{t}|s_{t}=s,a_{t}=a]
, and transition function
P(s_{t+1}|s_{t},a_{t})

Meyer et al.
. At each discrete time step
t
, the agent observes a state
s_{t}\in S
, selects an action
a_{t}\in A
, receives a reward
r_{t}
, and transitions to a new state
s_{t+1}
. This generates trajectories of the form
s_{0},a_{0},r_{0},s_{1},a_{1},r_{1},s_{2},a_{2},r_{2},...
, which terminate at a terminal state or after
T
steps
Sutton and Barto 2018
. The agent’s goal is to learn a policy
\pi
that maximizes the expected cumulative reward over such trajectories.
Various approaches have been developed to achieve this objective. Value-based methods estimate the value of states or actions and derive policies by maximizing these values. Policy-based methods directly optimize the policy, while model-based methods learn environment dynamics to simulate outcomes. More recently, deep reinforcement learning (DRL) has combined these approaches with deep neural networks, enabling RL to scale to high-dimensional state and action spaces
Plaat 2022
.

A central challenge in RL is balancing exploration (acquiring new knowledge by testing alternative strategies) and exploitation (leveraging existing knowledge to maximize immediate rewards). Excessive exploration can increase costs or slow convergence, while premature exploitation risks convergence to suboptimal policies. Managing this trade-off is therefore essential for effective policy improvement and underpins the ability of RL agents to generalize from past experiences and adapt to complex environments.

III.2
Quantum computing

Quantum computing (QC) leverages principles of quantum mechanics to perform computations that are intractable for classical computers within reasonable time frames
Rieffel and Polak 2011
. Unlike classical bits, which can take only the value 0 or 1, quantum bits (qubits) can exist in a superposition of both states. This property allows qubits to represent and process a vast range of possibilities simultaneously. Quantum computations generally proceed through three fundamental phases. The first phase is initialization, in which qubits are prepared in a well-defined starting state, often a uniform superposition across all computational basis states. The second phase is quantum operations, which apply sequences of unitary gates to transform the state of the qubits according to the desired algorithm. These gates are reversible and preserve the total probability of the quantum system
Nielsen and Chuang 2011
. The final phase is measurement, where qubits collapse from their quantum superposition into classical values, producing probabilistic outcomes that depend on the preceding computation.

This computational framework supports algorithms with potential advantages over classical approaches in certain domains. For example, Shor’s algorithm can factor large integers exponentially faster than the best-known classical algorithms
Shor 1994
;
Shor 1997
;
Ekert and Jozsa 1996
, and Grover’s algorithm provides a quadratic speed-up for unstructured search
Grover 1996
.
In this work, Grover’s algorithm is employed as a quantum subroutine to efficiently explore solution spaces and identify optimal candidates, which forms a core component of our quantum RL framework.

III.2.1
Dynamic Circuit

Recent advances in quantum hardware have introduced new paradigms that extend the flexibility of circuit execution. One such advancement is dynamic circuits, which enable real-time, measurement-based decision-making within a single quantum execution
Johnson
. Unlike static quantum circuits, where all operations are precompiled and executed in a fixed sequence, dynamic circuits incorporate classical processing within the coherence time of the qubits. This allows the results of mid-circuit measurements to determine subsequent quantum operations during runtime, enabling adaptive execution without the need to restart the computation.

Mid-circuit measurements collapse selected qubits into classical states while preserving the coherence of the remaining qubits, which can continue the computation. Combined with mid-circuit reset, this capability allows qubits to be measured, reinitialized, and reused within the same execution. Such functionality offers several performance benefits, including reduced qubit usage, minimized insertion of swap gates, and improved overall fidelity
Hua et al. 2023
. It also provides a practical mechanism for optimizing quantum resource allocation and scaling quantum workloads efficiently.

IBM has recently introduced hardware-level support for mid-circuit measurement and reset operations, marking an important step toward practical dynamic circuits on real quantum devices
Hua et al. 2023
. These capabilities are particularly valuable in applications such as quantum error correction
Das et al. 2022
;
Jokar et al. 2022
;
Wu et al. 2022
, runtime program verification
Huang and Martonosi 2019
;
Liu et al. 2020
, and adaptive quantum algorithms, where in-execution decisions can enhance both accuracy and resource efficiency.

IV.1
Quantum Markov decision process

In reinforcement learning, the agent interacts with the environment across discrete time steps. At each step, the agent observes a state, selects an action, and the environment responds with a new state and an associated reward. The probability distribution over the next state is determined by the state transition function, while the reward function assigns numerical values to transitions, reflecting the benefit of a given state–action pair.
In the classical setting, these components are represented using conventional data structures and encoded with classical bits. States and actions are defined as finite sets, the transition function is represented by a stochastic matrix, and rewards are specified as real numbers.

In the quantum formulation, this structure is preserved but encoded entirely in the quantum domain. Here, quantum superposition and unitary transformations represent the dynamics of the MDP and agent–environment interactions, enabling all possible trajectories to be coherently encoded and explored in parallel within a single quantum system. In this work, we illustrate the idea with a simple stochastic environment comprising four states
(s_{0},s_{1},s_{2},s_{3})
and two actions
(a_{0},a_{1})
. For example, if the agent is in
s_{0}
and selects
a_{1}
, it may transition to
s_{1}
, remain in
s_{0}
, or move elsewhere, depending on the transition probabilities, with the reward function assigning values to each outcome.

Quantum state and quantum action.

In the quantum formulation of MDP, states and actions are embedded within Hilbert spaces, denoted
S
for the state space and
A
for the action space. For the environment considered in this work, we define the state space as
S=\{s_{0},s_{1},s_{2},s_{3}\}
and the action space as
A=\{a_{0},a_{1}\}
. Each state
s\in S
is encoded as an orthonormal basis vector and expressed as a quantum state
\ket{s}
. The number of qubits required to encode a Hilbert space scales logarithmically with its size; specifically,
n=\log_{2}(N)
qubits are sufficient to represent
N
distinct quantum states within the computational basis.
For the four-state environment considered here, two qubits span the entire state space
S
, with the assignments

s_{0}=\ket{00},\quad s_{1}=\ket{01},\quad s_{2}=\ket{10},\quad s_{3}=\ket{11}.

The action space is treated analogously, with each action represented as a quantum basis state
\ket{a}
within its own Hilbert space
A
. Since there are only two actions in this MDP, a single qubit is sufficient to represent the action states, with the encoding

a_{0}=\ket{0},\quad a_{1}=\ket{1}.

The initialization of the registers into quantum superposition marks a key distinction from the classical setting. Beginning from the ground state
\ket{0}
, the application of Hadamard transformations places each quantum register into a uniform superposition of all computational basic states in its respective space. For the state qubits, the Hadamard transformation yields

H(\ket{0_{s}}\otimes\ket{0_{s}})=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}c_{n}\ket{s_{n}},\quad\text{with }c_{n}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}},

(1)

which ensures proper normalization,
\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}|c_{n}|^{2}=1
. In the case of four-state environment where
N=4
, the superposition reduces to

H(\ket{0_{s}}\otimes\ket{0_{s}})=\frac{1}{2}\ket{00}+\frac{1}{2}\ket{01}+\frac{1}{2}\ket{10}+\frac{1}{2}\ket{11}.

(2)

Similarly, the action qubit is placed in superposition through a Hadamard operation, producing

H\ket{0_{a}}=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}c_{n}\ket{a_{n}},\quad c_{n}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}},

(3)

which explicitly becomes

H\ket{0_{a}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\ket{0}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\ket{1}.

(4)

The resulting composite system encodes all possible state–action pairs in coherent superposition. This initialization not only provides a uniform distribution over the joint state-action space but also establishes the basis for the simultaneous exploration of multiple trajectories within the quantum Markov decision process (QMDP).

Quantum state transition function.

Once the state and action registers have been initialized into uniform superpositions, the next step is to model the state transition function of the MDP in the quantum domain. Classically, the transition function
P(s^{\prime}|s,a)
specifies the probability of moving from a current state
s
to a next state
{s^{\prime}}
given an action
a
. In the quantum formulation, these transition probabilities are encoded within the amplitudes of quantum states, where the squared magnitudes of the amplitudes directly correspond to the classical probabilities.

This encoding is achieved through parameterized single-qubit rotations. An ancillary qubit, initialized in the ground state
\ket{0_{s^{\prime}}}
, is subjected to a rotation about the y-axis,
R_{y}(\theta)
. The rotation generates a quantum superposition in which the probability of observing
\ket{0}
is
\cos^{2}\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)
, while the probability of observing
\ket{1}
is
\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)
. To reproduce a desired transition probability
P(s^{\prime}|s,a)
, the rotation angle is selected as
\theta=2\arcsin(\sqrt{P(s^{\prime}|s,a)})
.

In our MDP, these rotations must be applied conditionally, depending on the specific state–action pair that triggers the next-state transition. This is accomplished using multi-controlled
R_{y}(\theta)
gates, where the control qubits correspond to the quantum registers encoding the current state
\ket{s}
and action
\ket{a}
. The conditional operation ensures that the rotation is performed only when the register matches a particular state–action pair
(\ket{s^{*}},\ket{a^{*}})
, where
\ket{s^{*}}\in\textit{S}
,
\ket{a^{*}}\in\textit{A}
, thereby associating the correct transition with that interaction. The result is a quantum process in which the ancillary qubit evolves under a controlled rotation, establishing the next-state within the state space
S
.
Mathematically, this controlled operation is expressed as:

CR_{y}(\theta)(\ket{s}\ket{a}\ket{0_{s^{\prime}}})=\begin{cases}\ket{s}\ket{a}R_{y}(\theta)\ket{0_{s^{\prime}}}&\text{if}\ket{s}=\ket{s^{*}},\\
&\hskip 7.0pt\ket{a}=\ket{a^{*}},\\
\ket{s}\ket{a}\ket{0_{s^{\prime}}}&\text{else}.\end{cases}

(5)

Quantum reward function.

In a classical MDP, the reward function
R(s,a)
assigns a numerical value to each state–action pair, reflecting the benefit associated with a particular transition. When transposed into the quantum domain, this mapping must be embedded within the quantum circuit. In our formulation, the reward is represented by a dedicated reward qubit, initially prepared in the ground state
\ket{0_{r}}
. The evaluation of the reward is performed through conditional operations that depend on the transition from a given state–action pair to its subsequent next-state.

The mechanism employs the Controlled-NOT (CNOT) operation, where the control lines are defined by the qubits representing the next state
\ket{s^{\prime}}
.
This choice effectively incorporates the information of both the current state and the chosen action, since the next-state is determined by the state–action pair under the state transition function. If the transition leads to a next state associated with a reward, the CNOT flips the reward qubit from
\ket{0}
to
\ket{1}
, thereby encoding the reward value into the reward register.
Formally, this process is represented by the transformation in Eq. (
6
).

CNOT(\ket{s^{\prime}}\ket{0_{r}})=\ket{s^{\prime}}\ket{s^{\prime}\oplus 0_{r}},

(6)

where the action of the CNOT gate links the next-state register to the reward register. If the condition encoded in the control qubits is satisfied, the reward qubit undergoes a bit flip, ensuring that the quantum representation of the reward function accurately reflects the underlying MDP structure.

Quantum agent and environment single interaction.

The interaction between the agent and the environment within a quantum Markov decision process (QMDP) can be represented as a sequence of quantum operations. Each interaction begins with the agent receiving the current state
\ket{s}
of the environment.
The agent then applies a unitary operation associated with its policy, represented in the action register
\ket{a}
. This choice of action directly influences the evolution of the system, guiding the transition of the environment into the corresponding next state
\ket{s^{\prime}}
. The resulting transition also determines the reward, encoded in a reward register
\ket{r}
.

This agent–environment interaction can be described by a unitary operator
U(S\otimes A\otimes S\otimes R)
acting on the state, action, next state, and reward registers. This prepares a superposition of all possible trajectories, with the amplitude of each trajectory encoding the probability of observing that sequence. Formally, the resulting quantum state after a single interaction can be expressed as in Eq. (
7
).

\ket{\phi}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\bigg[c_{s^{\prime},r|s,a}\ket{s}\ket{a}\ket{s^{\prime}}\ket{r}\bigg]^{n},

(7)

where
c_{s^{\prime},r|s,a}
denotes the amplitude associated with the transition to next state
\ket{s^{\prime}}
with reward
\ket{r}
, conditioned on the current state
\ket{s}
and action
\ket{a}
. The probability of this outcome is given by the squared magnitude of
|c_{s^{\prime},r|s,a}|^{2}
, while
N
represents the total number of distinct trajectories in that quantum interaction.

IV.2
Quantum agent–environment multiple interactions with dynamic circuit capability

Extending the QMDP framework to model sequential interactions over
T
time steps requires not only consistent quantum evolution of the state, action, next state, and reward at each time step, but also an execution model that can scale within hardware limits. In this work, a key contribution lies in enabling agent–environment interactions over multiple time steps using the same quantum registers for each MDP component. This is achieved through dynamic circuit capabilities, specifically mid-circuit measurement and mid-circuit reset, which allow qubits to be reused across time steps while preserving the logical progression of the interaction. Unlike the previous approach in
Su et al. 2025
, which allocates new qubits for every time step, our method supports scalable execution of long-horizon MDPs in a quantum setting.

Within this reusable dynamic QMDP circuit, the design must address three core requirements across
T
time steps: preserving the data from each interaction, resetting qubits to ground state
\ket{0}
for reuse, and ensuring proper state propagation through the quantum state transition function. At each step
t
, the agent receives a state
\ket{s_{t}}
, selects an action
\ket{a_{t}}
, and the environment transitions to a new state
\ket{s^{\prime}_{t}}
while producing a reward
\ket{r_{t}}
. This sequence repeats for all
T
time steps using the same state, action, next state, and reward registers, with
\ket{s^{\prime}_{t}}
becoming
\ket{s_{t+1}}
for the next interaction.
In the quantum circuit, this propagation is achieved by transferring the value stored in the next state qubits
\ket{s^{\prime}_{t}}
to the state qubits
\ket{s_{t+1}}
. This is implemented using CNOT gates, which conditionally flip the target qubits based on the state of the control qubits. If a control qubit
\ket{s^{\prime}_{t}}
is in the
\ket{0}
state, the corresponding target qubit
\ket{s_{t+1}}
remains unchanged; if it is in the
\ket{1}
state, the target qubit is flipped. This operation effectively maps
\ket{s^{\prime}_{t}}
to
\ket{s_{t+1}}
, ensuring that the result from time step
t
becomes the starting state for time step
{t+1}
. In our quantum circuit, where states, actions, and next states exist in superposition, the CNOT operation preserves quantum coherence, enabling all possible state transitions to evolve in parallel.

The evolution of the agent–environment interaction over
T
time steps is governed by a sequence of quantum operations that incorporate mid-circuit operations directly into the interaction loop. At the initial time step
{t=0}
, the system begins with the state
\ket{s_{0}}
and action
\ket{a_{0}}
prepared in a uniform superposition, enabling equal exploration of all possible state–action pairs. The quantum state transition function is then applied, mapping
\ket{s_{0}}
and
\ket{a_{0}}
to the corresponding next state register
\ket{s^{\prime}_{0}}
according to the prescribed transition probabilities. Subsequently, the quantum reward function is evaluated, producing the reward
\ket{r_{0}}
conditioned on the current state–action pair. Following this interaction, a mid-circuit measurement is performed on all qubits involved in the time step to extract the outcome tuple
(s_{0},a_{0},s^{\prime}_{0},r_{0})
without terminating the computation. The measured results are stored in classical registers, ensuring that the complete probability distribution over trajectories can be reconstructed post-execution while preserving the ability to continue quantum evolution in subsequent time steps.

Immediately after measurement, a mid-circuit reset is applied to prepare the qubits for the next interaction step. First, the state qubits are reset to ground state
\ket{0}
, then updated by propagating the values from the next state qubits of the previous interaction using CNOT gates. This ensures that

The action qubit is also reset and reinitialized into a uniform superposition via a Hadamard gate, ensuring that all actions in the action space remain equally probable. Similarly, the reward qubit is reset to ground state for the next interaction.

Once the registers are updated, the quantum state transition function and the reward function are applied to generate the sequences for the subsequent time step

{t=1}
. At time step
{t=1}

\ket{s_{1}}

{t=0}

\ket{a_{1}}

\ket{r_{1}}

{t=1}
, after which the updated
\ket{s^{\prime}_{1}}

{t=T-1}

This approach not only improves fidelity but also broadens the range of QMDP problems that can be feasibly implemented on current quantum hardware.

IV.3
Quantum trajectories search

In RL, the decision-making process is fundamentally driven by two interconnected computational goals: quantifying the expected return associated with possible trajectories and determining the optimal policy that maximizes this return. The return serves as a scalar measure of a trajectory’s performance, representing the accumulated sum of rewards obtained from each agent–environment interaction, while the policy search identifies the sequence of actions that yields the highest return. Conventionally, these components are executed sequentially, with the agent first evaluating the discounted sum of rewards for each trajectory and then selecting the trajectory that supports the optimal policy. In the quantum domain, however, the QMDP framework enables a seamless integration of these tasks, encoding both the evaluation of returns and the search for optimal policies into a unified quantum process. This approach explores and prioritizes trajectories more efficiently than classical methods, thereby offering the potential for a significant computational advantage.

IV.3.1
Quantum Return Calculation

To evaluate trajectory performance in the quantum domain, the classical concept of return, defined as the discounted sum of rewards, is encoded into a dedicated quantum register
\ket{g}
. The return register
\ket{g}
occupies a Hilbert space
G
that contains a sufficient number of qubits to represent all possible return values.

Initially, the register
\ket{g}
is prepared in the ground state
\ket{0_{g}}
, after which the return is computed through a quantum operation
U_{G}
that acts sequentially on the reward registers
\ket{r_{t}}
generated at each interaction step
t
of the agent–environment interaction. This operation performs quantum addition, implemented through sequences of CNOT and Toffoli gates, to accumulate rewards into the return register
\ket{g}
.
The abstract representation of this transformation, expressed in Eq. (
10

U_{G}:=\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\gamma\ket{g}\bra{r_{t}},

(10)

where
\gamma\in[0,1]
is a fixed discount factor,
T
is the total number of time steps and
\ket{r_{t}}
represents the reward at time step
t

\ket{g}
.

When the return operation
U_{G}
is applied to the QMDP over
T
interaction steps,
the purpose is to compute the return for each possible trajectory by accumulating the rewards, each weighted by the appropriate discount factor, across those time steps. The final quantum state over these interactions, expressed in Eq. (

T

\ket{\phi^{(T)}}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\bigg\{\bigg[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}c_{s^{\prime}_{t},r_{t}|s_{t},a_{t}}\prod_{t=0}^{T-1}\ket{s_{t}}\ket{a_{t}}\ket{s^{\prime}_{t}}\ket{r_{t}}\bigg]\otimes\ket{g}\bigg\}^{n}.

(11)

Therefore, the complete quantum state

This unified representation preserves the full interaction history together with its computed return, enabling the QMDP to efficiently evaluate the performance of all possible sequences of interactions.

IV.3.2
Optimal Policy Search via Grover’s Algorithm

In classical RL, the objective is to learn an optimal policy, a strategy that prescribes the best action for each state to maximize return.
In quantum reinforcement learning (QRL), this objective can be reformulated as a search problem, in which the ensemble of length-
T
quantum trajectories, generated by the quantum interaction over
T
time steps as described in Eq. (
11
), is processed using Grover’s algorithm
Grover 1996
to efficiently locate the trajectories whose return register
\ket{g}
attains the maximum value, thereby enabling the QRL agent to identify the best action for each state.

Each quantum trajectory, denoted as
\ket{T}
, is a sequence of states, actions, and the environment’s responses, expressed as
\ket{T}=\ket{s_{0},a_{0},s^{\prime}_{0},r_{0},s_{1},a_{1},s^{\prime}_{1},r_{1},\ldots,s_{T-1},a_{T-1},s^{\prime}_{T-1},r_{T-1},g}
, where
s
is the current state at each time step from
t=0
to
t=T-1
,
a
is the chosen action by the agent,
s^{\prime}
is the resulting next state,
r
is the reward received, and
g
is the total return accumulated along the trajectory.
To efficiently identify the optimal trajectory, we first define an oracle that selectively marks trajectories likely to achieve the maximum return by applying a phase inversion. Let
\ket{\psi}
be the target quantum state. The oracle
U_{w}
flips the phase of the desired trajectories (those matching
\ket{\psi}
) while leaving all others unchanged. Mathematically, this operation can be described as in Eq. (
12
):

U_{w}\ket{T}=\begin{cases}-\ket{T}&\text{if $\ket{T}$ = $\ket{\psi}$},\\
\ket{T}&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(12)

The next step is to amplify the amplitudes of the marked states using quantum amplitude amplification
Kaye et al. 2007
, which increases the probability of measuring the desired solutions by reflecting the states about their mean amplitude. Let
\ket{\psi_{s}}
denote the superposition of all quantum trajectories before marking. The amplification operator
U_{s}
performs a reflection about
\ket{\psi_{s}}
and is defined in Eq. (
13
)
Guo 2023
as:

U_{s}=2\ket{\psi_{s}}\bra{\psi_{s}}-I,

(13)

where
I
is the identity operator.
By alternating
U_{s}
with the oracle
U_{w}
for the optimal number of iterations, the amplitudes of the highest-return trajectories are increased, thereby maximizing their measurement probability. Consequently, a measurement yields, with high probability, the state–action sequence corresponding to the optimal policy.

By integrating quantum return computation with amplitude amplification, the QMDP framework unifies trajectory evaluation and policy optimization into a single coherent quantum process. This stands in contrast to classical reinforcement learning methods, such as Q-learning and SARSA
Sutton and Barto 2018
, which must evaluate trajectories sequentially. The resulting quantum speedup in identifying the optimal policy offers particular advantages for large-scale decision-making problems where classical exhaustive search becomes computationally prohibitive.

V
Demonstrations and Results

In this section, we evaluate the proposed QRL framework, employing a quantum MDP realized through dynamic circuits to achieve efficient qubit utilization.
The experiments aim to validate the capability of this design to execute multi-step agent–environment interactions entirely within the quantum domain while minimizing the number of physical qubits required. We first assess the functional correctness of the dynamic-circuit-based QMDP by simulating its behavior across representative state–action spaces, confirming the preservation of quantum coherence and trajectory fidelity throughout sequential interactions. We then analyze the scalability of the approach by comparing qubit utilization against prior non-reusable QMDP implementations, demonstrating substantial reductions in physical qubit requirements. The proposed method is evaluated on both ideal quantum simulation and real quantum hardware: the simulation results confirm consistency with the previous method while exhibiting significant qubit resource efficiency, whereas the hardware execution demonstrates the feasibility of the approach and its compatibility with current hardware capabilities. Finally, we integrate the Grover-based trajectory search for policy optimization, highlighting the potential for substantial runtime speedups in identifying optimal trajectories.

Quantum encoding of the classical MDP

The classical MDP described in Fig.

The implementation uses four quantum registers. The state register (qState) consists of two qubits that represent the four states
(s_{0},s_{1},s_{2},s_{3})

\ket{00}
and placed into uniform superposition with Hadamard gates, enabling all states to be represented simultaneously. The action register (qAction) uses single qubit to encode the two actions

\ket{0}

The next-state register (qNextState), composed of two qubits initialized in
\ket{00}

\theta=2\arcsin(\sqrt{P(s^{\prime}|s,a)})

\ket{00}

This coherent representation of state, action, transition, and reward allows the QMDP to exploit quantum superposition for simultaneous exploration of the decision space. By capturing the environment dynamics without repeated sampling, it improves sample efficiency and reduces the interactions required to identify optimal policies. Moreover, implementing the MDP in the quantum domain enables direct integration of a quantum search algorithm into a unified quantum operation. Together, these features enable efficient encoding, evaluation, and discovery of optimal sequences within the quantum domain, offering computational advantages beyond the reach of classical methods. This QMDP circuit also serves as the building block for multi-step interactions when extended via dynamic-circuit operations for qubit reuse, enabling subsequent stages of the framework to perform quantum return calculation and Grover-based trajectory search without reverting to the classical domain.

V.2.1
Implementation of multiple interactions on quantum hardware

To evaluate the practicality of the proposed dynamic-circuit-based reusable QMDP for multiple interactions, we deployed the full three-timestep circuit on the 133-qubit IBM Heron-class quantum processor (ibm_torino). This device represents IBM quantum’s latest generation of superconducting hardware and offers significant performance improvements over the previous flagship 127-qubit Eagle processors, including a reported three- to five-fold increase in overall device performance
AbuGhanem 2025
. This hardware-based evaluation demonstrates both the feasibility of the approach and its compatibility with current quantum devices.

When executing on real hardware, in contrast to the noise-free Aer simulator, additional considerations arise. Due to the use of qubit reuse, mid-circuit measurement and reset, and conditional logic for state propagation across time steps, the circuit is subject to hardware timing constraints. In particular, the backend requires a short stabilization period between a mid-circuit measurement, a subsequent reset, and the reuse of the same qubit. Omitting this delay can result in timing conflicts, backend execution errors, and incorrect state transitions, all of which do not occur in simulation.
To ensure correct execution, we introduced a delay between the mid-circuit measurement and reset operations. This delay allows for qubit readout stabilization and hardware cool-down before the same qubits are reinitialized for the next timestep. Based on the ibm_torino backend’s timing characteristics, the optimal delay was selected to be 2000 ns, which is approximately twice the readout duration while remaining well below the qubits’
T1
coherence times, thereby minimizing decoherence risk.

For hardware validation of the dynamic QMDP circuit, we executed the three-timestep implementation repeatedly to account for temporal fluctuations in device performance and noise. Each execution consisted of ten iterations with 32K shots, and the experiment was repeated five times. This ensured sufficient sampling of quantum trajectories, such that by the fifth execution all possible trajectories were observed. Repeated executions were necessary for several reasons. First, quantum measurements are inherently probabilistic, and multiple runs improve sampling statistics to ensure that even low-probability but valid trajectories are captured. Second, hardware performance can vary over time due to fluctuations in qubit error rates, gate fidelity, and readout accuracy, and repeated runs help average out these transient effects. Third, because of its depth, multi-controlled gates, qubit reuse, and conditional state propagation, the QMDP circuit is particularly sensitive to noise, making iterative execution essential to obtain a trajectory distribution that reflects consistent circuit behavior rather than the artifact of a single noisy run.

These results confirm that the dynamic-circuit-based QMDP can be successfully executed on state-of-the-art quantum hardware, maintaining fidelity to the intended multi-step interaction model while simultaneously achieving qubit efficiency. The ability to reproduce the same trajectory set as in noise-free simulation establishes not only the conceptual validity of the dynamic-circuit approach but also its practical feasibility and compatibility with current hardware capabilities, underscoring its readiness for quantum RL experiments on NISQ devices.

To better understand the practical implications of adopting a dynamic-circuit approach, we outline the trade-offs between static and dynamic implementations of QMDP. These differences clarify the respective strengths and limitations of each method when deployed on near-term quantum devices. Table
1
summarizes the comparison across key dimensions.

Error Accumulation: Static implementations allocate all required qubits upfront and therefore avoid the need for mid-circuit measurement and reset, reducing exposure to reset and readout errors. However, the larger qubit footprint increases susceptibility to decoherence. Dynamic circuits invert this trade-off: by minimizing the qubit count, they mitigate decoherence but introduce repeated measurement and reset operations. Each reset step adds readout and initialization errors that accumulate across multiple time steps
Rudinger et al. 2022
;
Govia et al. 2023
. This contrast highlights a fundamental balance between decoherence and reset-induced error sources.

Latency Sensitivity: Static circuits incur minimal timing overhead since all qubits are available from the outset. Dynamic circuits, by contrast, rely on mid-circuit measurement and reset cycles, which introduce additional readout and re-initialization delays
Rudinger et al. 2022
. Although calibrated delays can be inserted to maintain correctness, these operations increase execution time and make dynamic circuits more sensitive to latency, particularly on hardware with slower readout processes.

Scalability Potential: Dynamic circuits offer superior scalability in principle, as the required qubit count remains fixed regardless of the number of time steps. This property makes them particularly attractive for near-term devices with limited qubit availability. In practice, however, scalability is constrained by the accumulation of reset and measurement errors and the latency overhead of repeated reset cycles
Hashim et al. 2025
;
Govia et al. 2023
. These effects impose a practical limit on the planning horizon that can be executed reliably. Static implementations, in contrast, are fundamentally restricted by the linear scaling of qubit resources, which quickly becomes prohibitive on NISQ devices.

Together, these trade-offs suggest that dynamic circuits are best suited for the NISQ era, where qubit scarcity is the primary limitation, whereas static circuits may regain relevance on future fault-tolerant machines with abundant, high-fidelity qubits.

By amplifying the probability of high-return trajectories, Grover’s search identifies optimal paths with a substantial reduction in search complexity compared to classical methods, enabling the optimal policy to be derived with significantly fewer quantum operations.

V.3.1
Implementation and validation of optimal trajectory search on quantum hardware

To further evaluate the proposed QRL framework, Grover’s search–based optimal trajectory identification was executed entirely on a quantum device (IBM’s ibm_torino processor), without reliance on quantum simulators or classical subroutines. Each run was configured for 32K shots, and the circuit was executed 30 times to obtain a statistically meaningful distribution of outcomes. The primary objective was to assess the likelihood of successfully sampling the optimal trajectories on quantum hardware. The target trajectories were T-151 and T-143, both corresponding to the maximum-return solutions identified in the prior simulation studies.

The measurement outcomes are presented in Fig.
6
, which compares the sampling distribution from simulation (green bars) with results obtained on quantum hardware (orange bars). While the simulator strongly amplifies T-151 and T-143, the hardware runs exhibit a noisier distribution. Specifically, T-151 was sampled four times and T-143 twice across all executions. On hardware, noise, gate errors, and qubit decoherence further reduced the likelihood of consistently sampling the optimal trajectories. In some runs, only a single instance of one target was observed, while in others neither target appeared. Non-optimal trajectories also emerged with appreciable frequency, reflecting the impact of device imperfections and underscoring the challenges of implementing Grover’s algorithm on current NISQ-era processors.

Despite these limitations, the hardware outcomes are consistent with the simulation in that both optimal trajectories were sampled. This provides experimental validation that the dynamic-circuit-based QMDP architecture, when combined with Grover’s search, can amplify and recover maximum-return trajectories on real quantum hardware. These results highlight the promise of the proposed QRL framework for large-scale reinforcement learning implementations in the quantum domain, extending beyond simulation to practical hardware execution.

VI
Discussion

The proposed dynamic-circuit-based QRL framework addresses one of the most persistent challenges in fully quantum RL architectures: the excessive qubit demand in multi-step agent–environment interactions. By integrating mid-circuit measurement, reset, and qubit reuse into the quantum Markov decision process (QMDP), this work demonstrates that long-horizon decision-making tasks can be executed entirely within the quantum domain while maintaining a fixed, minimal qubit footprint. Compared to prior static QMDP implementations that scale linearly with the number of time steps, the present approach achieves a 66 percent qubit reduction for three-step interactions without loss of functional fidelity.

Simulation and hardware experiments confirm that the dynamic QMDP preserves coherence and trajectory integrity across sequential interactions. Ideal simulations reproduce static results exactly, validating that the qubit reuse mechanism preserves the essential MDP dynamics. Execution on IBM’s Heron-class superconducting hardware further establishes feasibility, requiring only minor adjustments such as calibrated delay insertions to accommodate readout–reset cycles. This demonstrates that the approach is compatible with current NISQ devices, where usable qubits are often constrained by calibration losses, noise, and limited connectivity.

Beyond qubit efficiency, embedding Grover’s trajectory search within the QMDP unifies trajectory evaluation and policy identification into a single quantum process. This removes the need for classical post-processing and exploits quantum parallelism and amplitude amplification to accelerate policy discovery. We further demonstrate Grover’s algorithm on quantum hardware as part of the complete QRL framework, with measured output distributions closely matching theoretical predictions. Residual discrepancies are attributable to hardware noise sources such as gate infidelities and readout errors, rather than to limitations of the algorithm. These findings provide strong evidence that Grover-based trajectory search can be effectively deployed in a dynamic-circuit setting on present-day devices, underscoring its readiness for practical quantum-native decision-making systems.

VII
Conclusion

This work presents a fully quantum reinforcement learning (QRL) framework that integrates dynamic-circuit-based qubit reuse into a quantum Markov decision process (QMDP) with Grover’s trajectory search, enabling multi-step agent–environment interactions within the strict qubit limitations of near-term devices. By eliminating the linear growth in qubit count while preserving trajectory fidelity in both simulation and hardware experiments, the framework establishes a scalable foundation for quantum-native decision-making. Embedding Grover’s search further unifies policy identification into a single coherent quantum process, demonstrating reliable amplitude amplification and oracle-based trajectory marking in a qubit-reuse setting.

While the proposed design advances the feasibility of fully QRL on near-term devices, several considerations remain. First, performance on hardware is inevitably constrained by noise sources such as readout errors, gate infidelities, and limited coherence times. Although qubit reuse reduces the total number of physical qubits, deep multi-step circuits remain vulnerable to error accumulation due to the extended sequence of quantum operations. Incorporating error-mitigation strategies tailored to dynamic-circuit architectures, such as measurement error mitigation, dynamical decoupling, and optimized qubit mapping, could further enhance fidelity in future implementations.

Scaling beyond small MDPs to high-dimensional state–action spaces also presents challenges. Although the methodology is generalizable, larger environments increase the complexity of multi-controlled gate operations and transition functions, which must be managed carefully to control circuit depth and noise. More efficient gate decompositions, supported by advanced compilation techniques, will be key to extending scalability in practice.

Finally, the current Grover-based search relies on a perfect oracle to identify optimal-return trajectories. In realistic reinforcement learning settings with stochastic or partially observable dynamics, return estimation will be noisy or incomplete, potentially degrading search performance. Addressing this limitation will require approximate or adaptive oracle constructions and hybrid quantum–classical methods for policy refinement, thereby enhancing the robustness and applicability of the framework to real-world decision-making tasks.

As quantum hardware continues to advance in qubit count, connectivity, and error performance, the techniques developed in this work are well positioned to enable increasingly sophisticated quantum reinforcement learning systems capable of addressing large-scale, complex environments beyond the reach of classical approaches.
