Molecular dynamic simulation study on admissibility of green additives in hydraulic fracturing fluid
Authors: Aminah Qayyimah Mohd Aji, Dzeti Farhah Mohshim, Alissa Azlan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ContextLow interfacial tension (IFT) between fracturing fluids and reservoir fluids is essential for enhancing gas recovery and mitigating formation damage in shale gas systems. However, conventional surfactants used in hydraulic fracturing pose environmental and sustainability concerns, necessitating the development of greener alternatives. This study evaluates the potential of the bio-derived surfactant D-limonene in reducing methane–water interfacial tension under both ambient and high-pressure high-temperature (HPHT) conditions representative of shale reservoirs. A systematic relationship between IFT and solvation free energy (ΔGₛₒₗᵥ) was observed across all investigated conditions. Increasing temperature significantly reduced IFT, with reductions of up to ~ 80% under ambient conditions (18–90 °C), whereas the reduction was less pronounced under HPHT conditions (~ 7 mN/m). Pressure exhibited a comparatively minor influence, with IFT increasing by approximately 8% between 70 and 120 MPa. Increasing D-limonene concentration consistently lowered IFT, although higher concentrations were required under HPHT conditions to achieve comparable reductions.MethodsAll calculations were performed using classical molecular dynamics simulations. Molecular interactions among methane, water, and D-limonene were described using the Optimized Potentials for Liquid Simulations (OPLS) force field. Molecular topologies were generated using the Automated Topology Builder, and simulations were conducted using GROMACS®. Systems were equilibrated under constant number of particles, volume, and temperature (NVT) and constant number of particles, pressure, and temperature (NPT) ensembles. The solvation free energy (ΔGₛₒₗᵥ) was computed using thermodynamic integration (TI), where the ensemble-averaged energy derivatives ⟨∂V/∂λ⟩ were evaluated across 21 λ-coupling states and numerically integrated using the trapezoidal rule. Interfacial tension was subsequently determined via its thermodynamic correlation with ΔGₛₒₗᵥ.

Introduction

Energy is vital for sustaining life as it acts as a lifeline for various sectors, including communication, transportation, lighting, heating, and electric power generation. Despite rapid progress in renewable energy technologies, oil and natural gas continue to play a dominant role in meeting global energy demand [1, 2].

In recent years, oil and natural gas production has been diverted to unconventional reservoirs due to huge resources and the depletion of conventional hydrocarbon resources. In these reservoirs, hydrocarbon extractions required special equipment and techniques, such as hydraulic fracturing.

Extraction of oil and gas from unconventional reservoirs is typically achieved through hydraulic fracturing, which involves injecting water, chemical additives, and proppants into the formation at high pressure [4]. This method induces fractures and enhances fracture connectivity in low-permeability formations such as shale, coal-bed methane, and tight sandstone reservoirs, thereby improving hydrocarbon flow from the reservoir to the wellbore.

Hydraulic fracturing

Hydraulic fracturing relies on the high-pressure injection of fluids to create fracture networks and improve connectivity within reservoir rocks [5, 6]. Water is the primary and most significant component in hydraulic fracturing fluid, typically comprising between 90% and 99.5% of the total volume injected into a well [7]. Nevertheless, the low viscosity of water-based fracturing fluids promotes fluid retention in low-permeability shale formations, leading to water blocking, reduced effective permeability, and impaired hydrocarbon production [8].

Therefore, it is an industrial practice to tackle the issues by having water-based hydraulic fracturing fluid with chemical additives [5]. The chemical additives were added in hydraulic fracturing to overcome various problems faced during stimulation, such as increasing the viscosity of the hydraulic fracturing fluid, as a friction reducer, inhibiting the growth of microorganisms and decreasing the biofouling of fractures [6, 9–11].

In unconventional shale reservoirs, the nano- to micro-scale pore structure and ultra-low permeability amplify capillary pressure effects and promote strong fluid–rock interactions [12]. These conditions favour the retention of injected fracturing fluids within the shale matrix, leading to phase trapping, reduced effective permeability, and sustained formation damage [11, 13–16]. Alteration of shale wettability and reduction of gas–liquid interfacial tension (IFT) is therefore critical for lowering capillary forces, enhancing fluid mobility, and improving hydrocarbon recovery from tight pore networks.

Injecting surfactants as part of the fracturing fluid offers an effective approach to overcoming capillary-dominated flow limitations in shale reservoirs. Surfactants can alter shale wettability and reduce gas–liquid interfacial tension (IFT), thereby lowering capillary pressure within confined pore spaces [11, 16]. This reduction enhances fluid displacement efficiency, promotes the release of trapped fracturing fluids, and improves hydrocarbon mobility toward the fracture network. Consequently, surfactant-assisted fracturing fluids can mitigate water blocking, increase effective permeability, and contribute to improved hydrocarbon recovery from ultra-low-permeability formations.

Green surfactant D-limonene

The environmental and health impact of chemical additives in large-scale hydraulic fracturing has become one of the significant issues being discussed in the industry. The ecological risk includes water consumption and water contamination, which are among a few concerns that have been raised [17]. Thus, recent studies focus on developing green alternatives for chemical additives.

Green surfactants represent a significant shift toward sustainability within the energy sector, offering effective yet more environmentally conscious alternatives to traditional chemical agents. Driven by growing regulatory pressure and corporate environmental responsibility initiatives, these compounds are now widely developed and utilised across various oil and gas operations [18–20]. Specific applications where green surfactants are deployed include hydraulic fracturing, where they help reduce interfacial tension with minimal environmental impact; drilling fluids, improving lubricity and waste management; and in Enhanced Oil Recovery (EOR) techniques, enhancing oil displacement efficiency in a greener manner. Their adoption helps mitigate some of the environmental footprint associated with conventional operations, making them a key area of research and implementation in modern petroleum engineering.

Among the various green surfactants investigated, D-limonene has emerged as a particularly versatile and effective bio-based solvent for oil and gas applications [21–25]. D-limonene has been widely studied as a sustainable alternative to conventional aromatic solvents such as xylene and toluene, driven by its favourable physicochemical properties and environmental profile [25].

Studies show that D-limonene can act as a surfactant without any chemical additives, which may significantly reduce the interfacial tension (IFT). Laboratory studies have demonstrated that D-limonene can function effectively as a standalone surfactant without the need for additional chemical additives. Experimental measurements have shown that D-limonene significantly reduces gas–liquid interfacial tension (IFT) and alters rock wettability through strong molecular affinity with hydrocarbon and mineral surfaces [19]. Field applications have reported the stimulation of oil and gas wells in the Granite Wash, Barnett Shale, Signal Peak Field, Fayetteville Shale, and the Appalachian Basin using small quantities of bio-based D-limonene nanofluids at concentrations as low as 0.2 vol.%. These treatments resulted in approximately 25% higher oil or gas production within a 30-day period compared to untreated wells. In a subsequent field study, Yue et al. demonstrated the successful deployment of a formulated green surfactant as a flowback aid in the Permian Basin, achieving effective performance at a low treatment dosage of 1 gal per 1000 gal of water [26].

However, most D-limonene experiments are lied to at ambient pressure and temperature conditions. A greater spectrum of operating environments should be evaluated to truly understand the behaviour of D-limonene surfactants in shale reservoirs with extreme reservoir conditions. Therefore, conducting a simulation on the effects of high pressure and elevated temperature through molecular dynamics simulation on D-limonene surfactant efficiency is the purpose of this study. Green hydraulic fracturing fluid is expected to replace conventional surfactants in hydraulic fracturing. Still, some thermodynamic limitations need to be considered in this study: the temperature of the reservoir and the reservoir pressure.

At equilibrium, methane and water are immiscible, forming distinct gas–liquid interfaces at the macroscopic scale. Molecular dynamics investigations that explicitly address such phase immiscibility and interfacial thermodynamic properties typically construct biphasic or multiphase simulation boxes with clearly defined boundaries [27, 28]. This immiscibility resulted from the weak intermolecular interactions between non-polar methane molecules and the hydrogen-bonded water network, which leads to phase segregation and the formation of a distinct gas–liquid boundary. Molecular dynamics investigations that explicitly address such phase immiscibility and interfacial thermodynamic properties typically construct biphasic or multiphase simulation boxes with clearly defined interfaces. In these approaches, spatially separated phases are maintained within the simulation domain, enabling direct evaluation of interfacial structure, density profiles, and interfacial tension.

However, molecular dynamics simulations of methane–water systems are also commonly performed using finite-size periodic systems in which methane is treated in a dispersed configuration within the aqueous phase under constant NPT conditions. In such models, methane is typically represented at infinite dilution, allowing direct evaluation of hydrophobic hydration free energy and local solvent structure [29]. Previous studies have shown that this approach enables accurate characterisation of hydration structure, radial distribution functions, and solvent reorganisation around the solute, without the complexity introduced by an explicit gas–liquid interface [30–32].

This framework isolates molecular-scale interactions and allows the thermodynamic contributions of the hydration shell to be quantified, including cavity formation and solute–solvent interaction energies within the hydrogen-bonded water network [30]. Consequently, dispersed configurations are widely adopted to investigate solvation thermodynamics of nonpolar molecules in aqueous environments, even though methane and water remain immiscible at macroscopic equilibrium.

In this study, a dispersed methane–water system is adopted to compute ΔGₛₒₗᵥ using thermodynamic integration (TI). Rather than explicitly modelling a macroscopic interface, interfacial behaviour is inferred through an established thermodynamic correlation between ΔGₛₒₗᵥ and IFT. This approach enables systematic evaluation of surfactant effects under varying temperature, pressure, and concentration conditions while maintaining computational efficiency and molecular-level insight.

Accordingly, this work investigates the effect of D-limonene on methane–water systems using molecular dynamics simulations across ambient and HPHT conditions. The influence of temperature, pressure, and surfactant concentration on solvation thermodynamics and effective interfacial properties is analysed, providing fundamental insights into the performance of green surfactants for shale gas applications.

Simulation modelling

The molecular simulation system was constructed by first preparing the molecular structures of methane, water, and D-limonene. The corresponding molecular topologies were generated based on bond lengths, bond angles, torsional angles, and dihedral parameters, following established protocols (Qi et al., 2018). The number of molecules for each system was determined based on target concentration and density relationships, ensuring consistency with the specified thermodynamic conditions, as summarised in Table 3.

The simulation box was defined as a cubic domain with dimensions of 50 Å × 50 Å × 50 Å. Molecular packing was performed using PyMOL, where D-limonene molecules were initially positioned near the centre of the simulation box, while methane and water molecules were distributed throughout the domain to represent a homogeneous mixture. The packing procedure avoided atomic overlap by enforcing van der Waals exclusion radii consistent with Bondi (1964), thereby ensuring physically realistic initial configurations. A schematic representation of the molecular arrangement is shown in Fig. 3. The generated configuration was subsequently exported into GROMACS® format for molecular dynamics (MD) simulations.Fig. 3Schematic representation of the packing molecules with water, methane, and D-limonene

The initial molecular configuration generated using PyMOL was employed as the starting structure for the molecular dynamics simulations. Prior to the production runs, the system was subjected to energy minimisation using the steepest descent algorithm to remove steric clashes and ensure a physically stable configuration. Following energy minimisation, the system underwent a two-stage equilibration process. First, equilibration was performed under the NVT ensemble (constant number of particles, volume, and temperature) to stabilise the system temperature. This was followed by equilibration under the NPT ensemble (constant number of particles, pressure, and temperature), allowing the system density and simulation box dimensions to adjust to the specified thermodynamic conditions.

Subsequently, the ΔGₛₒₗᵥ of methane in the water–D-limonene system was calculated using an alchemical free-energy approach based on a coupling parameter, λ. The parameter λ defines a continuous transformation between fully interacting (λ = 1) and fully decoupled (λ = 0) states of the methane molecule. Within this framework, methane is treated as the solute, while water and D-limonene constitute the solvent environment. During the alchemical transformation, the non-bonded interactions (Lennard–Jones and electrostatic interactions) between methane and the surrounding solvent molecules were gradually scaled as a function of λ, while intramolecular interactions remained unchanged. The D-limonene molecules were not subjected to alchemical decoupling and remained fully interacting throughout the simulation, thereby preserving the physicochemical characteristics of the solvent environment.

Each λ state was equilibrated using a 1 ns NVT simulation followed by a 1 ns NPT simulation, after which a 5 ns production run was conducted. System configurations were sampled using a 2-fs integration timestep.

The solvation free energy was calculated using thermodynamic integration (TI), where the ensemble-averaged derivative ⟨dV/dλ⟩ was evaluated at each λ state. This approach ensures a continuous and thermodynamically consistent transformation between the fully interacting and fully decoupled states of the methane molecule.

To ensure reproducibility of the alchemical free-energy calculations, the decoupling process was carried out using consistent simulation time parameters, as summarised in Table 2. For each λ window, a total of 2 ns equilibration (1 ns NVT followed by 1 ns NPT) was performed prior to a 5 ns production simulation. In total, this corresponds to 105 ns of cumulative production simulation time across all λ windows, providing sufficient sampling for statistically converged free-energy estimates.

Conclusions

This study evaluated the potential of the bio-derived surfactant D-limonene for reducing methane–water IFT in hydraulic fracturing fluids under both ambient and HPHT conditions using molecular dynamics simulations. The work addresses the limitation of many previous D-limonene studies, which are largely conducted under ambient conditions, by extending the assessment to reservoir-relevant HPHT environments.

Under ambient conditions, increasing temperature from 18 to 90 °C reduced the calculated IFT significantly, from approximately 40 to 2.15 mN/m, corresponding to an approximate 80% reduction. This reduction is associated with more thermodynamically favourable methane–solvent interactions, as reflected by increasingly negative \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta {G}_{\mathrm{s}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{l}\mathrm{v}}$$\end{document}ΔGsolv values. Under HPHT conditions, the same D-limonene concentration also reduced IFT with increasing temperature; however, the reduction was less pronounced, with IFT values approaching approximately 7 mN/m. This indicates that although D-limonene remains effective under HPHT conditions, elevated pressure and temperature modify solvent structure, molecular packing, and methane–solvent interactions, thereby reducing the magnitude of IFT reduction compared with ambient conditions.

At a constant temperature of 160 °C and D-limonene concentration of 0.4 wt%, increasing pressure from 70 to 120 MPa increased IFT from 6.72 to 7.29 mN/m, equivalent to an approximate 8% increase. The corresponding reduction in the magnitude of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta {G}_{\mathrm{s}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{l}\mathrm{v}}$$\end{document}ΔGsolv suggests that increasing pressure slightly weakens the thermodynamic favourability of methane solvation in the water–D-limonene system. This behaviour is attributed to molecular confinement and restricted solvent reorganisation under compressed HPHT conditions. Compared with temperature, however, pressure plays a secondary role in controlling IFT behaviour within the investigated range.

Under ambient conditions, the addition of only 0.05 wt% D-limonene reduced IFT substantially from approximately 42 to 18.2 mN/m, while increasing the concentration to 0.5 wt% further reduced IFT to 4.7 mN/m. This trend indicates that D-limonene promotes more favourable methane–solvent interactions and reduces the energetic penalty associated with methane solvation. Under HPHT conditions, the same trend was observed, but higher D-limonene concentrations were required to achieve comparable IFT reduction. This suggests that surfactant performance is strongly condition-dependent and that HPHT reservoir environments may require higher additive loading to maintain effective IFT reduction.

The final \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta {G}_{\mathrm{s}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{l}\mathrm{v}}$$\end{document}ΔGsolv value obtained by trapezoidal integration was approximately − 30.09 kJ/mol for the analysed system, supporting the numerical stability of the TI procedure. These results strengthen the use of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta {G}_{\mathrm{s}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{l}\mathrm{v}}$$\end{document}ΔGsolv as a thermodynamic descriptor for evaluating D-limonene performance.

Overall, this study demonstrates that D-limonene is a promising green surfactant for reducing methane–water IFT, particularly under ambient and moderately elevated conditions. Temperature is the most influential parameter controlling IFT reduction, followed by surfactant concentration, while pressure has a comparatively minor but non-negligible effect. The findings indicate that D-limonene can enhance methane–solvent interactions and improve interfacial behaviour, supporting its potential application as a greener hydraulic fracturing additive.

The IFT values reported in this study are thermodynamic estimates derived from solvation free energy rather than direct mechanical measurements from an explicitly phase-separated methane–water interface. Therefore, the results are most appropriately interpreted in terms of relative trends with temperature, pressure, and surfactant concentration. Future work should incorporate explicit biphasic simulations, pressure tensor-based IFT calculations, and experimental validation under HPHT conditions to further refine the predictive accuracy of the model and strengthen the applicability of D-limonene-based formulations for shale gas reservoirs.
