Mifepristone versus placebo to increase the rate of spontaneous labour in people with a prior caesarean: a double-blind randomised controlled trial (Mi-labourTrial)
Authors: Meghan G. Hill, Charlotte Oyston, Michelle R. Wise, Robin Cronin, Moerangi Tamati, Gergely Toldi, Lynn Sadler
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFor people with a prior caesarean, avoiding a repeat caesarean and undergoing vaginal birth after caesarean (VBAC) may decrease both short- and long-term complications. Current options include awaiting labour or undergoing induction of labour (IOL) with mechanical cervical dilation followed by amniotomy and oxytocin infusion. Spontaneous labour is preferred as the use of exogenous oxytocin increases the rate of uterine rupture, a feared complication of VBAC. Additional options are vital to improve the chance of labour in people with a prior caesarean scar. Mifepristone, a progesterone receptor antagonist, may have utility in people with a prior caesarean. The medication occupies progesterone receptors on the myometrium, thereby making the uterus more receptive to endogenous contractile agents.MethodsThe MiLabour trial is a double-blinded randomised controlled trial being carried out at two sites in New Zealand. People with a history of lower uterine segment caesarean birth are eligible. Participants are randomised in a 1:1 ratio to either a single dose of 200mg of mifepristone or to a single dose of placebo. The primary outcome of the MiLabour trial is onset of labour within 48 h of ingestion of study medication. To identify an increase in the rate of spontaneous labour within 48 h from 10 to 30% in the group receiving mifepristone, a minimum of 168 participants are required with a confidence interval of 95% and power at 90%.DiscussionSafely preventing repeat caesarean births is a health priority. If mifepristone is an appropriate option, this would greatly impact pregnancy care. Research in this area has been hampered by the well-founded fear of uterine rupture, a surgical emergency with the potential for morbidity and mortality. If mifepristone achieves a higher rate of labour without the need for oxytocin, this could become a standard treatment approach for people with a lower uterine segment scar as they approach their due date.Trial registrationThe trial is registered on the Australian and New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry, anzctr.org.au. Full registry title is “Mifepristone versus placebo to increase the rate of spontaneous labour in people with a prior caesarean: A double blind randomised controlled trial (Mi-labourTrial)”. Trial ID: ACTRN12624000103561. Date registered 06/02/2024.

Introduction

Background and rationale {6a}

Having a caesarean birth confers a higher chance of repeat caesareans throughout a person’s reproductive years [1]. Multiple caesarean births carry both short- and long-term risks including abnormal placentation, acute surgical complications and adhesive disease for the maternal patient, and have implications for birth timing, stillbirth risk and physiologic adaptation to birth for the baby [2].

People with a prior caesarean birth who aim to avoid a repeat caesarean ideally enter labour without the need for contractile agents. Induction of labour (IOL) with oxytocin increases both maternal and fetal risk and decreases the chance of a vaginal birth in this population. In spite of this, there are few options available to decrease the need for oxytocin induction.

In the late 1990 s and early 2000 s, misoprostol, a prostaglandin E1 analogue, was shown to markedly increase the uterine rupture rate in people with uterine scars [3–7]. A uterine rupture is a surgical emergency that can lead to permanent fetal damage or death, as well as severe morbidity or mortality for the maternal patient. Since the evidence of harm with misoprostol in people with a prior caesarean has been recognised, the use of prostaglandins (both E1 and E2 analogues) has appropriately become uncommon. However, there has not been introduction of alternatives to increase the rate of labour for people with a prior caesarean. If a person with a uterine scar needs an induction, this is usually accomplished with an intracervical balloon followed by amniotomy and oxytocin infusion. This process does in fact increase the rate of uterine rupture, with further increases with higher oxytocin infusion rates [8], but the risk remains sufficiently low as to enable its use as an option.

In most high-resource countries, patients with a lower uterine segment scar are offered a repeat caesarean at 39 weeks or an IOL at 41 weeks. This leaves people with a choice of a sure ‘birth date’ versus an uncertain wait that may result in the induction of labour they have been advised to avoid, either because they reach 41 weeks gestation, or because a pregnancy complication develops during late gestation necessitating planned earlier birth. Identifying an alternative, safe intervention to increase the likelihood of establishing labour is an important goal for improving the health of pregnant people and their babies. Mifepristone, a progesterone receptor antagonist, may offer an alternative.

Mifepristone may offer a physiologic pathway for people to enter labour without oxytocin. By occupying progesterone receptors on the myometrium, mifepristone causes cervical changes due to decreasing collagen organisation [9, 10]. Studies from Asia, Europe and the USA indicate that when given at term, mifepristone results in greater readiness of the cervix for labour [11–13]. This is especially important as there is ample evidence that people are more likely to give birth vaginally with a favourable cervical examination, especially if they have a history of caesarean birth [14, 15]. Studies indicate that when given at term gestational ages, mifepristone will cause labour in some people [16–20], a candidate mechanism being that the drug makes the uterus more sensitive to contractile agents such as endogenous prostaglandin and endogenous oxytocin [21, 22]. There is also evidence that people who receive mifepristone prior to a conventional IOL require less exogenous oxytocin if it is required [23, 24].

Mifepristone is already a part of the established care pathway for people undergoing second and third trimester abortion and stillbirth care in Aotearoa New Zealand, including those with a prior lower uterine segment caesarean birth. A study from Western Australia reported on 199 people with one or more caesareans who received mifepristone as a part of second trimester abortion care with no uterine ruptures [25], a lower need for contractile medication and a shorter interval to completion of their abortion.

Prior studies utilising mifepristone to cause labour at term gestational ages include few people with prior caesareans. During the 1990 s, one pilot study including 32 participants with a history of caesarean suggested effectiveness [18]. A larger trial, with a subset of people attempting a VBAC (n = 52), reported three uterine ruptures, which is highly concerning. However, closer inspection revealed that these clinical complications were not clearly identified as ruptures or dehiscences (where the myometrium is no longer approximated, but the overlying serosa is intact, a non-emergency finding quite commonly noted at repeat caesarean birth). In addition, participants in this trial also received other contractile agents to effect birth. Considering the trial was published in 2005, it is likely that the subgroup of participants with prior caesareans were receiving contractile agents (including now contraindicated prostaglandins) and supra-therapeutic dosages of mifepristone [19]. Two small recent trials from India have included 52 and 36 participants with prior caesarean scars who were randomised to IOL with or without mifepristone [26, 27] with encouraging results relating to oxytocin use and a reduced repeat caesarean rate. So, while there is not a large amount of data regarding the use of mifepristone to effect labour at term gestational ages in people with a caesarean scar, the information available does suggest that the use of mifepristone in these circumstances may confer benefit, without substantially increasing risk of uterine rupture. However, definitive evidence of benefit is required via performance of a large clinical trial.

We are performing a double-blinded, randomised controlled trial to assess whether mifepristone increases the rate of spontaneous labour in people with a prior caesarean birth. We are recruiting participants from two catchments within Auckland, Te Toka Tumai Auckland and Te Whatu Ora Counties Manukau. If our study identifies that people attempting VBAC have a higher chance of initiating labour when provided with mifepristone, this may provide important benefits for mothers and babies and would represent the first widespread advance in therapy for this patient population in 20 years.

Objectives {7}

To assess the rate of spontaneous labour in people with a prior caesarean birth receiving 200 mg oral misoprostol versus oral placebo.

Trial design {8}

The MiLabour trial is a double-blinded randomised controlled trial. Participants are people with either one or two prior lower uterine segment caesarean births who prefer and are suitable for a vaginal birth after caesarean. Participants are randomised to either 200 mg mifepristone or a look-alike placebo in a 1:1 ratio, stratified by gestational age (36 + 6/7–38 + 5/7 and 38 + 6–41 + 6) and hospital site (Te Toka Tumai Auckland or Te Whatu Ora Counties Manukau). The primary hypothesis is that people receiving 200 mg oral mifepristone will have a higher chance of entering spontaneous labour within 48 h than those receiving oral placebo.

Methods: participants, interventions and outcomes

The MiLabour trial is a double-blinded randomised controlled trial being carried out at two sites in New Zealand. People with a history of caesarean birth are eligible to participate.

The primary outcome of the MiLabour trial is onset of labour within 48 h of ingestion of study medication. The SPIRIT reporting guideline is utilized for this trial protocol [28].

Study setting {9}

The MiLabour trial is a double-blinded RCT, being carried out at two sites: Te Toka Tumai Auckland and Te Whatu Ora Counties Manukau. Te Toka Tumai Auckland serves central Auckland and Te Whatu Ora Counties Manukau serves South Auckland. Both hospitals include assessment and birthing units, affiliated clinics and access to level 3 neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) facilities. The hospitals, between them, provide care for approximately 14,000 births per year. Te Toka Tumai Auckland provides care to approximately 1000 people with a prior caesarean each year. The VBAC rate approximates 12–15% each year. Equivalent data is not available for Te Whatu Ora Counties Manukau. Though data from 2023 and 2024 indicated a VBAC rate of 26.5% in this hospital. This is likely due to several factors, including the absence of private obstetric care at Counties Manukau, with private obstetricians having a high rate of elective caesarean birth in the New Zealand context. In comparison to global rates of VBAC, the low rates at both centres can be attributed to a range of factors including the low birth rate in New Zealand and the fact that most people eligible to do so do not attempt a VBAC, opting for a 39-week repeat caesarean birth.

Who will take informed consent? {26a}

Potential participants are referred to the study team via self-referral (posters/pamphlets available at hospital clinics); health professional referral (via phone call or email); and identification from hospital schedules, particularly the IOL schedule. The health professional providing antenatal care is contacted regarding the study. With their assent, the potential participant is contacted via phone by a member of the research team and the purpose and inclusion and exclusion criteria of the MiLabour trial are reviewed. If they are interested, a time for a follow-up phone call is made and they are provided with an electronic copy of the study Participant Information Sheet and Consent From (PIS/CF). The follow-up phone call confirms intent to participate and a plan for the trial visits is made. Trial visits can be carried out at the participant’s home, a hospital visit or coinciding with an antenatal care appointment. At the first trial visit, the PIS/CF is provided in hard copy for signature prior to the start of trial procedures. Written informed consent is required for participation in the trial.

Additional consent provisions for collection and use of participant data and biological specimens {26b}

N/A. Participant data is not to be utilised for ancillary studies. This research does not presently include collection of biological specimens.

Interventions

Explanation for the choice of comparators {6b}

Intervention groups are the active drug arm, in which participants receive 200 mg oral mifepristone, and the placebo drug arm, in which participants receive a look-alike placebo. The placebo is made of similar filler compound to the mifepristone formulation information provided by ISTAR, the supplier of mifepristone in New Zealand. Standard care in both hospitals is no treatment (equivalent to the placebo arm). The primary hypothesis is that people-administered mifepristone will have a higher rate of labour within 48 h than those administered placebo.

Intervention description {11a}

Once a participant signs the consent form, a cardiotocograph (CTG) is performed to confirm the fetus has normal oxygenation prior to trial start. The participant is offered a cervical examination, though not required to have one. If the participant elects to have a cervical examination, a gloved hand is used to perform a vaginal examination to locate the cervix. The 5 components of a Bishop score are assessed (dilation, effacement, station, consistency, position) and a score (derived from the components) is noted.

An online randomisation system is accessed via a computer or mobile device and participants are administered the study medication in the allocated bottle number.

Active/mifepristone arm

A total of 200 mg oral mifepristone is administered (this is blinded as described above).

Inactive/placebo arm

Oral placebo composed of filler compound is administered (this is blinded as described above).

The participant is seen again approximately 24 h later. A repeat CTG is performed, and a repeat cervical examination is offered.

From 12 weeks post-birth, all participants are contacted via phone to complete a survey regarding their trial and birth experience. At this time, all those who were approached for participation but declined the trial are contacted via phone to complete a survey regarding their experience of being approached to participate and about their birth experience. The people who declined to participate provide verbal consent to complete the survey at the time.

Criteria for discontinuing or modifying allocated interventions {11b}

There are no criteria for discontinuing or modifying allocated interventions. The pre-screening with CTG occurs prior to randomisation. Drug ingestion occurs immediately following randomisation. In the unlikely event that a person would change their mind about participation in the interval between computerised randomisation and drug ingestion, they would be kept in intention-to-treat analysis.

Participants may withdraw from study procedures as they wish. However, as detailed, ingestion of study drug is likely in all participants. Should a participant have emesis within an hour of drug ingestions, they may not have complete drug absorption. However, they will be analysed as per their allocated trial arm. Participants likewise may withdraw prior to the second study visit, which is largely a labour screening visit, in which their antenatal care provider would be contacted to make other arrangements for care. Participants may elect not to complete the survey after they have given birth.

The study does provide for withdrawal of data, for which verbal request need only be made.

We have requested approval to recruit up to 200 participants in the MiLabour trial (the power calculation for the trial indicates a minimum requirement of 168 participants).

Strategies to improve adherence to interventions {11c}

The MiLabour study is largely run by employed research midwives at each hospital site. These midwives have training regarding use of the online randomisation website, communicate directly with the Pharmacy about provision of study drug kits and co-ordinate study visits. This familiarity ensures appropriate adherence to study procedures. All participants are directly observed ingesting the study medication during their first study visit. Considering that the study intervention is a single dose of medication, no adherence strategy is required. Staff on the birthing units do not have to modify their usual clinical care for trial participants.

Relevant concomitant care permitted or prohibited during the trial {11d}

The antenatal care provided to trial participants is separate from the study and per the usual standard of care for all healthcare professionals. Labour and birthing care is unchanged from usual hospital clinical guidelines.

Provisions for post-trial care {30}

There are no additional provisions for clinical care post-trial. All participants can receive care through the New Zealand public healthcare system. Treatment injuries (this encompasses injuries sustained during either vaginal or caesarean birth) are submitted for assessment by the Accident Compensation Corporation (ACC) in New Zealand.

Sample size {14}

OpenEpi (https://openepi.com) was utilised to perform the trial power calculation. From existing literature, a 30% rate of labour within 48 h was considered a reasonable estimate for the participants receiving mifepristone [16–20]. We chose a rate of 10% for the comparator (placebo) group as participants are at term and labour does occur spontaneously at this time. With power set at 90% and a 95% confidence interval, 168 participants are required if randomised in a 1:1 ratio (84 per group). We have approval to recruit up to 200 participants in the trial. The additional 32 participants ensure that sufficient data is available and account for withdrawal of study data by participants and the potential for loss to follow-up. To date, we have had no instance where either has occurred, though it is possible for a participant to choose to deliver outside of the hospital with healthcare professional notes being unavailable to the study team.

Recruitment {15}

Recruitment occurs via three main pathways. Participants attending hospital clinics may self-refer after viewing promotional materials displayed in the waiting room and clinic rooms. Antenatal healthcare professionals may discuss the study with potential participants and refer with their permission. Lastly, potential participants may be identified from hospital schedules, namely the IOL schedule ahead of time. In this instance, the health professional providing antenatal care is contacted prior to the potential participant being approached.

Research midwives discuss the study, usually via phone with potential participants, provide further information electronically and make a time to re-contact the potential participant. If the person remains invested in participation, a visit appointment is made with a member of the study team. This visit may occur in the participant’s home, at a hospital site or alongside another appointment with the clinician overseeing the participant’s clinical care. At the first study visit, participants are given a hard copy of the PIS/CF and provide written informed consent prior to commencement of study procedures.

Assignment of interventions: allocation

Sequence generation {16a}

The Liggins Institute Clinical Data Research Hub [29] is utilised for the randomisation scheme. An online randomisation site allows access to randomisation via password input which is unique to hospital site (first stratification). The second stratification is gestational age (36 + 6/7–38 + 5/7 and 38 + 6/7–41 + 6/7). Relevant data is entered into the randomisation site which is a screening and recording tool. Provided the screening information is acceptable, the patient treatment allocation (medication bottle number) is displayed.

Concealment mechanism {16b}

Apart from the numbered labels on the study medication bottles, they are identical. The bottles are sealed with a ‘sticker seal’ which breaks to indicate if a bottle has been opened. The medication is placed inside identical white/opaque capsules. The member of the research team watches the participant ingest the study medication. All used bottles are returned to the research pharmacy.

The Liggins Institute Clinical Data Research Hub [29] and the Te Toka Tumai Auckland clinical trials pharmacy both have access to the numbered allocations. The trial team do not.

Implementation {16c}

After providing written informed consent, a CTG is performed and a cervical examination offered to each participant. The computerised randomisation is performed by a member of the study team.

Drug ingestion is observed.

A follow-up visit is carried out 24 h after the first visit. At this appointment, the CTG is repeated and participants are offered a repeat cervical examination.

After the study visits are completed, the participant resumes their usual antenatal care. While many participants receive study medication several days prior to a planned IOL, people who do not have a planned IOL may participate from 38 + 6/7 weeks gestational age. Should participants not labour within the 48 h post-drug ingestion, there is no requirement for an IOL or repeat caesarean birth. These procedures are carried out for the usual clinical indications.

Screen failures

Screen failures occur should the screening tool on the randomisation website indicate that the potential participant is not eligible for the trial (for example, outside of gestational age range for randomisation). Additionally, if a participant consented to inclusion but had a pre-randomisation CTG which was classified as abnormal, they would not be randomised and would be treated as a screen fail.

Assignment of interventions: blinding

Who will be blinded {17a}

Study participants, research staff and the clinical team caring for the participant are all blinded. The statistician will be blinded to the study intervention during the analysis and unblinding will only occur after the analysis is performed. Contingencies for early unblinding are described below.

Procedure for unblinding if needed {17b}

The Data Safety Monitoring Committee (DSMC) receives reports of any SAEs within 48 h. They may request to be unblinded as to study allocation of a participant, and at the direction of the principal investigator (PI), the research pharmacy would release study allocation to the DSMC chair. The DSMC chair will be provided with unblinded reports every 25% of recruitment. The PI could be unblinded to the allocations of the groups if the DSMC thought it critical that this should happen. This would likely be in a circumstance where the DSMC was recommending the trial be stopped early due to evidence of harm.

There are no contingencies in which unblinding would modify treatment. Surgical complications stemming from labour and uterine rupture are a major concern in patients attempting a vaginal birth after caesarean birth. However, the surgeon being made aware of whether or not the patient had been administered mifepristone would not alter the management.

Data collection and management

Plans for assessment and collection of outcomes {18a}

Each participant has data entered into a Redcap database stored on a secure University of Auckland server by a member of the research team. Baseline demographic data occurs from chart extraction. Times/dates of study drug administration are imported from the online randomisation system. Clinical outcome data is entered after electronic medical record (EMR) review. Primary outcome data collection is straightforward as a presentation to hospital in labour is recorded as a Women’s Assessment Unit visit and cervical examination findings and CTG monitoring are both collected in the EMR. Secondary outcomes are ascertained from EMR review. Health professionals who have access agreements at the study sites utilise the Badgernet (an antenatal, intrapartum and postpartum EMR system) to notate both antenatal and intrapartum care. The majority of the outcomes being collected are not open to interpretation (dates/times for presentation in labour, induction dates/times, blood loss entered into the medical record by non-study personnel), and for this reason, the data to be collected is likely to be accurate. The study team will discuss any data that appears to require clinical interpretation and make a decision regarding the particular outcome. In addition, a random 10% sample (approximately 20 records) will be re-extracted and the data checked against the original data entry. The accuracy of the original data entry will then be estimated and further data checking undertaken for any data items where the accuracy is less than 95% (if there are substantive errors in at least 1 record).

Post-birth surveys are carried out via phone a minimum of 12 weeks after the participant (or person who has declined trial participation) has given birth. Responses are entered directly into Redcap.

Plans to promote participant retention and complete follow-up {18b}

To date, there have been no issues with participants completing both study visits (a 100% follow-up rate). This is likely due to the relationship that the participants develop with the research midwives during discussions via phone prior to the first study visit, as well as the availability of research visits in the home. In New Zealand, VBAC is encouraged in the hospital setting and is considered a contraindication to home birth. To date, no participants have given birth outside of the hospital setting. This has promoted participant retention and complete follow-up information for all participants to this point.

Data management {19}

All study-related data is kept in a secure REDCap dataset on the University of Auckland server. The dataset is password protected and able to be accessed only by research staff. Participants are identified by study number within this dataset. The participant identifiers (name, date of birth, National Health Index {NHI} number) are accessible via the randomisation log stored through the Liggins Institute [29]. For some data points, validations are built into the REDCap programme (for example, ‘out of range’ values for cord gas data, alerts for severe adverse events).

Confidentiality {27}

Participant identifiers are entered into the randomisation system. The REDCap system restricts access to the study number and reidentification to the members of the research team with access to links between the randomisation scheme and the REDCap Data. Consent forms are stored in a locked safe at each participating hospital. Consent forms will be transferred to secure storage at completion of the trial.

Publications resulting from this research will detail only aggregated data; hence, individual trial participants will not be able to be identified.

Plans for collection, laboratory evaluation and storage of biological specimens for genetic or molecular analysis in this trial/future use {33}

Presently, no biological specimens are collected or stored as a part of this research. We have applied for funding to perform research on maternal serum samples. In the event we receive this funding, the ANZCTR website will be updated accordingly.

Statistical methods

Statistical methods for primary and secondary outcomes {20a}

Study groups will be presented with descriptive data.

The principle of intention-to-treat will be employed for each person per study group. Any missing data will be reported as a number/percentage. These data will not be imputed.

No per-protocol analysis is planned.

Any protocol deviations (for example, a participant is randomised and then decides not to swallow the study medication) will be reported in the results. However, all patients randomised will remain in the intention-to-treat analysis.

Adjustment will be made for the two stratifications (gestational age and hospital site) for both the primary and secondary analyses utilising regression techniques.

Continuous outcomes will be presented as mean differences or relative risks as appropriate, with 95% confidence intervals.

Statistical significance will be considered to be a p value of < 0.05. Analyses will be reported without adjustment for multiplicity, and secondary analyses interpreted with caution.

Economic analysis

Cost-effectiveness for participant and baby dyad will be performed based on cost to the public health system using routinely collected hospital data on participant length of stay during birth admission and any readmission within 7 days of birth, neonatal length of stay during birth admission, use of the operating theatre and neonatal intensive care unit.

Interim analyses {21b}

There are no interim analyses planned that will be available to the study team. The Data Safety Monitoring Committee will be provided with a report at 25%, 50% and 75% of recruitment. These reports will include primary outcome data, limited data relating to secondary outcomes and morbidity data. These reports will be provided by an investigator not involved in the clinical care of trial participants (LS, epidemiologist). The DSMC will be provided with severe adverse event (SAE) reports within 48 h of notification.

Methods for additional analyses (e.g. subgroup analyses) {20b}

Currently, there are no unspecified analyses planned. Potential future analyses will be added to the statistical analysis plan before data lock. Should post hoc analyses be added, these will be specified as such in any future publications.

Methods in analysis to handle protocol non-adherence and any statistical methods to handle missing data {20c}

Missing data will be presented as number/percentage. Data will not be imputed.

Plans to give access to the full protocol, participant level-data and statistical code {31c}

The trial is registered, with protocol available on the Australian and New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry. At the time of study consent, participants may elect to be provided with a copy of the results of the study. The study dataset will not be available to the public.

Oversight and monitoring

Composition of the coordinating centre and trial steering committee {5d}

The MiLabour trial investigators as well as the research midwives employed at each site comprise the Trial Steering Committee (TSC). The TSC will consider recommendations posed by the DSMC.

Composition of the data monitoring committee, its role and reporting structure (21a}

The DSMC is composed of three members. There is a statistician (Chair), a Maternal–Fetal Medicine specialist and a neonatologist serving on the committee. The group will receive a report after each 25% of the study recruitment. Reports will contain recruitment data, primary outcome and relevant secondary outcome data, information on study withdrawals and a summary of SAEs. All SAEs are additionally reported to the DSMC chair within 48 h.

Recommendations from the DSMC are made to the principal investigator (PI) and these are then relayed as necessary to the Trial Steering Committee. Recommendations may relate to trial promotion, maintenance and protocol deviations or participant withdrawals. The DSMC may also request additional data to that initially reported. The statistical analyses reported to the DSMC will be provided by a co-investigator not involved in recruitment or clinical procedures. The contents of this report will not be shared with the rest of the trial team.

Adverse event reporting and harms {22}

Each participant will have adverse events (AEs) collected. The AEs include chorioamnionitis in labour (defined as maternal fever > 37.5 °C on 2 occasions, or ≥ 38 °C on one occasion, in labour and one other clinical symptom or sign, or maternal fever and decision to treat empirically), isolated maternal fever > 37.5 °C or ≥ 38 °C in labour without the diagnosis of chorioamnionitis, postpartum haemorrhage (defined as > 500 mL blood loss postpartum), postpartum endometritis (defined as maternal fever ≥ 38 °C and hospitalisation for intravenous antibiotics, within 7 days of giving birth), maternal birth injury (severe perineal laceration, complicated repeat caesarean birth defined as uterine extensions) and neonatal birth injury (neonatal birth trauma from caesarean or vaginal birth, neonatal seizures not due to hypoxic ischaemic encephalopathy).

These AEs do not require immediate reporting to the DSMC and rather are reported every 25% of recruitment.

The AEs will be included in the trial results.

Any unexpected events which occur during the research will be recorded alongside AE data. The PI will review each unexpected event and make a determination as to whether this should be considered an AE.

The severe adverse events (SAEs) for the MiLabour trial are antepartum haemorrhage (defined as > 500mL blood loss intrapartum), maternal adverse events that require unexpected hospitalisation (this excludes normal presentation for labour care), maternal birth injury (surgical—need for hysterectomy), blood transfusion of 4 or more units of PRBCs, maternal admission to intensive care unit or equivalent, maternal death, stillbirth, neonatal death (early), neonatal encephalopathy (moderate or severe, as determined by the treating team), umbilical cord prolapse, neonatal birth trauma (neonatal fracture, intracranial haemorrhage, nerve palsy, spinal injury) and uterine rupture.

Each trial participant has SAEs recorded during initial birth data collection and again on follow-up chart review. All SAEs are to be reported to the DSMC within 48 h of notification. Summary of SAEs are also provided with each DSMC full report (per 25% recruitment). The trial results will include a summary of the SAEs.

Frequency and plans for auditing trial conduct {23}

There are no pre-specified audits. Both the Health and Disability Ethics Committee (HDEC) and the Clinical sites may initiate an audit of trial conduct or documentation.

Plans for communicating important protocol amendments to relevant parties (e.g. trial participants, ethical committees) {25}

Both the HDEC and the clinical sites require that major changes to the protocol be submitted and approved as an amendment. The trial protocol document and PIS/CF need to be updated according to the changes made.

Dissemination plans {31a}

As is standard for New Zealand clinical trials, all participants are asked to indicate their preference about receiving trial results on their trial consent form. Results will be sent after the trial is complete.

Results from the MiLabour trial are intended to be shared with local groups via professional networks at both clinical sites and via reporting at the annual clinical report day (held annually). The intention is to both present results at an international conference and to publish the trial in a peer-reviewed journal.

Discussion

The MiLabour trial enrolled the first participant at Te Toka Tumai Auckland in July of 2024. After some initial hesitancy regarding the use of outpatient mifepristone in patients with a prior caesarean, recruitment increased substantially. MiLabour opened at Te Whatu Ora Counties Manukau in December of 2024 with the first participant recruited at this site in January of 2025. To date, the population that this trial is most appealing to is people with a caesarean scar who are scheduled for an induction of labour. In Auckland, people are counselled extensively through obstetric clinics about the risk of VBAC, including uterine rupture, and are made aware of the likely doubling of the rate of this complication with IOL. Hence, people accepting the risk of an IOL are motivated to pursue a vaginal birth and appear to see the MiLabour trial as a way to possibly avoid the risks associated with oxytocin use.

While the trial is relatively popular amongst those scheduled for IOL, this does not result in a large number of people who are eligible. Each hospital performs approximately 2 IOLs per week in people with caesarean scars. With the current recruitment rate, we anticipate an average 6–8 recruitments per month with the sites combined. The MiLabour trial should therefore recruit for 2–3 years to enrol all intended 200 participants.

While the study has been well received by potential participants who seem motivated for more options than caesarean or IOL, this has not been true of all healthcare providers. There is caution on the part of some midwives and obstetricians providing care for potential participants. The finding that misoprostol use in people with a prior caesarean vastly increases the risk of uterine rupture is a relatively recent development. As a result, many providers are concerned about safety of medications for new indications in the population of people with a prior caesarean. In New Zealand, it is relatively commonplace to advise patients with a scarred uterus not to undergo IOL, let alone proceed with an experimental treatment prior to an IOL. Views have been expressed to the research team that people should not VBAC at all due to the associated risks. Considering the birth rate in New Zealand is 1.66 per fertile woman, this opinion, while paternalistic, is reasonable. The view is also a prevalent one, reflected in our institutional rates of VBAC, which are low. Approximately 1000 people with a prior caesarean receive care at Te Toka Tumai Auckland each year. Only 10–15% of those people have a VBAC, indicating that the most common choice is an elective repeat caesarean. There is ample evidence that repeat caesarean rates are largely provider-driven [30, 31]. Hence, the largest barrier has in fact been healthcare providers rather than potential participants, who have an apparent willingness to participate if approached.

The study is now approved at both clinical sites. Factors that have encouraged healthcare professionals at both sites include the desire to avoid or minimise the use of oxytocin and awareness that we are already using outpatient mifepristone in people with a prior caesarean at both hospital sites for second and third trimester abortion and stillbirth care.

Encouragingly, to date, we have had appropriate follow-up with all trial participants for their second visits and no issues with adherence to the trial protocol. Should mifepristone increase the rate of labour in people with a prior caesarean, this may represent the first new therapeutic option for this population in decades.
