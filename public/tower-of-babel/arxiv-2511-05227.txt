On the smooth Lorentzian optimal transport problem
Alec Metsch, [2ex] Universität zu Köln, Institut für Mathematik, Weyertal 86-90D - 50931 Köln, Germanyemail: ametsch@math.uni-koeln.de[1ex] Key words: Optimal transport, regularity of Kantorovich potentials,[1ex] weak KAM methods[1ex] MSC Classification: 49J30, 49Q22, 49Q20, 53C50

Abstract
In this paper, we want to establish some general results in the Lorentzian optimal transport theory – that have well-known Riemannian counterparts. As a first result, filling the missing gap in [ 17 ] , we will provide non-trivial assumptions on the measures to ensure strong (Kantorovich) duality, i.e. the existence of a maximizing pair in the dual optimal transport formulation. We will also show that, under suitable assumptions, optimality of a causal coupling is characterized by c c -cyclical monotonicity. The second result deals with the regularity of c c -convex functions and (weak) Kantorovich potentials. We show that, in general, regularity results known in the Riemannian context do not extend to the Lorentzian setting. Under suitable assumptions on the measures, we will prove nevertheless that these (weak) potentials are locally semconvex on an open set of full measure. As our last result, we will prove that, under quite general assumptions, for any two intermediates measures along a displacement interpolation, there exists a C l ​ o ​ c 1 , 1 C^{1,1}_{loc} -regular maximizing pair in the dual formulation.

such that
\varphi_{s}\in C^{1,1}_{loc}(\Omega_{s})
and

.

Moreover, if

is optimal in the dual Kantorovich formulation, so is

.

The structure of this paper is as follows:
The cost function

possesses an underlying Lagrangian structure. In Section

and Corollary

-convex functions (here,

The Lagrangian

The cost function
c
from the introduction naturally arises as the minimal time-
1
-action of a Lagrangian
L:TM\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
. In this short section, we define this Lagrangian and recall the most important definitions/notations and results that will be used many times in this paper. The rest of this section is deferred to the appendix as it is well-known and similar to
[
20
]
or
[
22
]
.

For the rest of this paper
, let
p\in(0,1)
and let
(M,g)
be an
(n+1)
-dimensional globally hyperbolic spacetime, where the metric
g
is taken to have signature
(-,+,...,+)
. In particular,
M
is time-oriented. We refer to future-directed causal (resp. timelike) vectors simply as causal (resp. timelike), and explicitly specify past-directed causal when needed. We understand
0
to be a causal vector. A curve is always assumed to be piecewise smooth if not otherwise said. In particular, a curve is referred to as causal (timelike) if it is piecewise smooth and future-directed causal (timelike). We denote by
I^{+}
resp.
J^{+}
the set of pairs
(x,y)
which can be connected by a timelike resp. causal curve. The Lorentzian distance function is denoted by
d
:

d(x,y):=\begin{cases}\sup_{\gamma}{\ell}_{g}(\gamma),&\text{ if }(x,y)\in J^{+},\\
0,&\text{ else,}\end{cases}

where the supremum is taken over all causal curves
\gamma:[a,b]\to M
connecting
x
to
y
and the (Lorentzian) length
{\ell}_{g}
of a causal curve
\gamma:[a,b]\to M
is defined as

\int_{a}^{b}\sqrt{|g(\dot{\gamma}(t),\dot{\gamma}(t))|}\,dt.

For
x\in M
, we denote by
{\cal C}_{x}\subseteq T_{x}M
the cone of causal vectors. Note that
{\cal C}_{x}
is closed. We also set
{\cal C}:=\{(x,v)\in TM\mid v\in{\cal C}_{x}\}
. We can equip
M
with a complete Riemannian metric, which will be fixed and denoted by
h
. All balls
B_{r}(x)
,
x\in M
,
r>0
, are understood to be taken w.r.t. the metric
h
. The
h
- and
g
-norm of a tangent vector
v\in T_{x}M
are denoted by
|v|_{h}
and
|v|_{g}:=\sqrt{|g(v,v)|}
, respectively.

Definition 2.1
.

We define the Lagrangian
L:TM\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
as

\displaystyle L(x,v):=\begin{cases}-|v|^{p}_{g},&\text{ if }v\in{\cal C}_{x},\\[10.0pt]
+\infty,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

See also
[
25
]
, Section 2, and
[
20
]
, Section 3.

Definition 2.2
.

(a)

The
action
of a curve
\gamma:[a,b]\to M
is defined by

\displaystyle\mathbb{L}(\gamma):=\int_{a}^{b}L(\gamma(t),\dot{\gamma}(t))\,dt\in(-\infty,\infty].

Note that
\mathbb{L}(\gamma)
is finite if and only if
\gamma
is causal.

(b)

A curve
\gamma:[a,b]\to M
is called an
L
-
minimizer
if for any other curve
\tilde{\gamma}:[a,b]\to M
with the same endpoints, we have
\mathbb{L}(\gamma)\leq\mathbb{L}(\tilde{\gamma})
.

Notation 2.3
.

We reserve the term
maximize
to refer specifically to the Lorentzian length functional. That is, a maximizing curve
\gamma:[a,b]\to M
is a causal curve that satisfies
\ell_{g}(\gamma)=d(\gamma(a),\gamma(b))
. The term maximal, on the other hand, is reserved for geodesics and refers to a geodesic defined on its maximal existence interval. In particular, a maximal causal geodesic is not necessarily maximizing. The following result is well-known.

Theorem 2.4
.

For any two points
(x,y)\in J^{+}
, there exists a maximizing geodesic connecting
x
to
y
. Moreover, any maximizing curve must be a pregeodesic.

Proof.

See
[
26
]
, Chapter 14, Proposition 19 and
[
24
]
, Theorem 2.9.
∎

Definition 3.1
.

Let
U,V\subseteq M
. A function
\varphi:U\to\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbb{R}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
is said to be

-convex relatively to
(U,V)
if there exists a function
\psi:V\to\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbb{R}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
such that

\displaystyle\varphi(x)=\sup_{y\in V}\left(\psi(y)-c(x,y)\right)\text{ for all }x\in U.

(3.1)

Here, we use the convention
+\infty-\infty:=-\infty
.
The
c
-transform of
\varphi
is the function

\displaystyle\varphi^{c}:V\to\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbb{R}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu,\ \varphi^{c}(y):=\inf_{x\in U}(\varphi(x)+c(x,y)).

Here, we use the convention
-\infty+\infty:=+\infty
.

The
c
-subdifferential of
\varphi
is defined as

\partial_{c}\varphi:=\{(x,y)\in U\times V\mid\varphi(x)\in\mathbb{R},\ \varphi^{c}(y)\in\mathbb{R},\ \varphi^{c}(y)-\varphi(x)=c(x,y)\}.

Note that
c(x,y)<\infty
whenever
(x,y)\in\partial_{c}\varphi
.

Remark 3.2
.

If
\varphi
is
c
-convex relatively to
(U,V)
, then
\varphi
can be extended to a
c
-convex function relatively to
(M,M)
. Indeed, let
\psi:V\to\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbb{R}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
be as in the definition above, and extend
\psi
to
M\backslash V
by
-\infty
. If we denote this extension by
\psi^{\prime}
, we can define the
c
-convex function

\varphi^{\prime}(x):=\sup_{y\in M}(\psi^{\prime}(y)-c(x,y))=\sup_{y\in V}(\psi^{\prime}(y)-c(x,y)),\ x\in M.

Conversely, if
\varphi:M\to\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbb{R}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
is
c
-convex relatively to
(M,V)
, then
\varphi_{|U}
is
c
-convex relatively to
(U,V)
for any

.

Therefore, we will only consider
c
-convex functions relatively to
(M,V)
. Sometimes we will just speak informally of a
c
-convex function and mean a
c
-convex function relatively to
(M,V)
for some
V\subseteq M
.

Definition 3.3
.

(i)

We say that two Borel probability measures are

if there exists a coupling
\pi
that is concentrated on

. In this case, the coupling

(ii)

We say that a pair of Borel probability measures is

Recall the definition of a
\pi

Definition 3.4
.

Let
\mu,\nu\in{\cal P}
and
\pi\in\Gamma_{\leq}(\mu,\nu)

\pi
-solution is a

-convex function
\varphi:M\to\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbb{R}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu

\displaystyle\pi(\partial_{c}\varphi)=1.

.

A set

is called

-cyclically monotone
if for any finite chain of pairs

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

(i)

The fact that
\varphi
is a
\pi
-solution can be proved similarly to the proof of Theorem 2.8 in
[
17
]
. Due to different definitions of strict timelikeness, we provide a proof in the appendix.

For the second part of (i), given any
\pi
-solution
\varphi
, let us first show that
\varphi
is locally bounded from below on
\operatorname{supp}(\mu)
. Let us denote by
p_{1},p_{2}:M\times M\to M
the projection onto the respective components.

Fix
x_{0}\in\operatorname{supp}(\mu)
. If
\bar{\pi}
is a strictly timelike coupling, then by the causal compactness of
\operatorname{supp}(\nu)
, there exists
y_{0}\in\operatorname{supp}(\nu)
with
(x_{0},y_{0})\in\operatorname{supp}(\bar{\pi})
. In particular,
x_{0}\in I^{-}(y_{0})
. Since
p_{2}(\partial_{c}\varphi)
is of full
\nu
-measure, there exists

arbitrarily close to

. In particular, there exists such

with
(x_{0},y)\in I^{+}
. Then
(x,y)\in I^{+}
for any

close to
x_{0}
, and hence

\varphi(x)\geq\varphi^{c}(y)-c(x,y)

is uniformly bounded from below for

close to
x_{0}
due to the continuity of
c
on

and the fact that

is finite by definition of the

\varphi
is indeed locally bounded from below on
\operatorname{supp}(\mu)

It remains to prove that
\varphi^{c}
is locally bounded from above, but this follows completely analogous.

(ii)

(1)

First suppose that
\pi(I^{+})=1
, and assume that
\varphi
is a
\pi
-solution. By assumption,
\partial_{c}\varphi\cap I^{+}
has full
\pi
-measure, hence

has full
\mu

\varphi(x)=\varphi^{c}(y)-c(x,y),\text{ where }(x,y)\in\partial_{c}\varphi\cap I^{+}.

Hence, if

converges to

(x_{k},y)\in I^{+}
for large

\liminf_{k\to\infty}\varphi(x_{k})\geq\varphi^{c}(y)-\lim_{k\to\infty}c(x_{k},y)=\varphi^{c}(y)-c(x,y)=\varphi(x).

This shows that
\varphi
is lower semicontinuous as a function defined on the
\mu
-measurable set
p_{1}(\partial_{c}\varphi\cap I^{+})
. Since
\mu(p_{1}(\partial_{c}\varphi\cap I^{+}))=1
, this shows that
\varphi
is
\mu
-measurable.

(2)

Now suppose that
\mu
does not give mass to countably
n
-rectifiable sets. It suffices to show that the sets

\varphi^{-1}((a,+\infty])\text{ are $\mu$-measurable for all $a\in\mathbb{R}$.}

Let
a\in\mathbb{R}
be given, and
A:=\varphi^{-1}((a,\infty])

[
30

that the set

B:=\{x\in A\mid I^{+}(x)\cap A=\emptyset\}

is countably
n
-rectifiable, hence
\mu

For
x\in A\backslash B
, there exists
y\in I^{+}(x)
with

. In particular, if
x^{\prime}\in I^{-}(y)

\varphi(x^{\prime})=\sup_{y^{\prime}\in J^{+}(x^{\prime})}(\psi(y^{\prime})-c(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}))\geq\sup_{y^{\prime}\in J^{+}(y)}(\psi(y^{\prime})-c(y,y^{\prime}))=\varphi(y)>a.

Hence, there exists

with

. Thus, since

is second-countable, there exists

To prove Corollary
1.5
, we have to formalize the paragrpah below Theorem
1.4
in the introduction. This is the context of the following lemma.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
\mu,\nu\in{\cal P}
, and suppose that
C(\mu,\nu)\in\mathbb{R}
. Suppose that
\pi\in\Gamma_{\leq}(\mu,\nu)
admits a
\pi
-solution
\varphi
. If
\varphi
is
\mu
-measurable, then
\varphi^{c}
is
\nu
-measurable. Moreover, if
\varphi^{-}\in L^{1}(\mu)
and
(\varphi^{c})^{+}\in L^{1}(\nu)

\pi
is optimal and
(\varphi,\varphi^{c})
is optimal for the dual Kantorovich problem.

Proof.

The proof follows the well-known method in
[
1
]
, proof of Theorem 6.1.4 and
[
30
]
, proof of Theorem 5.10(ii), (b)
\Rightarrow
(c).

Let
\tilde{\varphi}
be Borel with
\tilde{\varphi}=\varphi

\mu
-a.e. Let
\Gamma\subseteq\partial_{c}\varphi
be a Borel set with
\pi(\Gamma)=1
. Shrinking
\Gamma
if necessary, we may assume that
\tilde{\varphi}=\varphi
on
p_{1}(\Gamma)
. Disintegrate
\pi
w.r.t.
\nu
, that is,

\pi=\int_{M}d\pi_{y}(x)\,d\nu(y)

where

is a Borel family of probability measures on
M
. Since
\pi
is concentrated on
\Gamma
, for
\nu
-a.e.

,

is concentrated on
\{x\in M\mid(x,y)\in\Gamma\}
. Thus, for
\nu
-a.e.
y
, we have
\tilde{\varphi}(x)+c(x,y)=\varphi^{c}(y)
for

-a.e.
x\in M
. Since
(x,y)\mapsto\tilde{\varphi}(x)+c(x,y)
is Borel, the function

y\mapsto\int_{M}\tilde{\varphi}(x)+c(x,y)\,d\pi_{y}(x)

(which is defined to be

\varphi^{c}(y)

\nu
-a.e. Hence,
\varphi^{c}
is
\nu
-measurable.

If
\varphi^{-}\in L^{1}(\mu)
and

. Since

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}\ni\int_{\operatorname{supp}(\nu)}\varphi^{c}\,d\nu-\int_{M}\varphi\,d\mu

4
Riemann vs Lorentz – Counterexamples

Suppose that
N
is an
n
-dimensional complete and connected Riemannian manifold, and let
\tilde{c}
be some cost function. If
\tilde{c}
is well-behaved (as in Theorem 10.24 in
[
30
]
), it is known that
\tilde{c}
-convex functions
\varphi:M\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
are locally bounded on the interior of their domain
\operatorname{dom}(\varphi):=\varphi^{-1}(\mathbb{R})
, that
\operatorname{dom}(\varphi)\backslash\operatorname{int}(\operatorname{dom}(\varphi))
is countably
(n-1)
-rectifiable , and that the
\tilde{c}
-subdifferential
\partial_{\tilde{c}}\varphi(x)
, as
x
varies over a compact subset of
\operatorname{int}(\operatorname{dom}(\varphi))
, is compact itself. In particular,
\varphi
is continuous on
\operatorname{int}(\operatorname{dom}(\varphi))
.

Under the additional assumption that
\tilde{c}
is locally semiconcave (for the definition, see Appendix
C
),
\varphi
is even locally semiconvex on
\operatorname{int}(\operatorname{dom}(\varphi))
. This result was extended by
[
13
]
to the case where
\tilde{c}
equals the squared Riemannian distance.

We will show that the result does not extend in this generality to the Lorentzian setting. Even for very simple
c
-convex function, continuity may fail on the interior of the domain. Moreover, it is not difficult to construct a
c
-convex function whose
c
-subdifferential is unbounded when
x
varies in a compact set.

Before providing counterexamples, let us prove the following lemma, which at least shows that the first part of the results in
[
30
]
or
[
13
]
continues to hold. Thanks to the specific cost function (in particular, Inequality (
4.1
)), this lemma represents one of the rare cases where the Lorentzian framework turns out to be simpler than the Riemannian one.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\varphi:M\to\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbb{R}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
be a
c
-convex function relatively to
(M,V)
,
V\subseteq M
. Set
\operatorname{dom}(\varphi)=\{x\in M\mid\varphi(x)\in\mathbb{R}\}
. Then
\varphi
is locally bounded on
\operatorname{int}(\operatorname{dom}(\varphi))
and
\partial\operatorname{dom}(\varphi)
is countably
n
-rectifiable.

Proof.

Set

D^{\pm}:=\{x\in M\mid\varphi(x)\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{\pm\infty\}\}.

Note that
\partial\operatorname{dom}(\varphi)=\partial D^{+}\cup\partial D^{-}
.

Let
x,x^{\prime}\in M
with
x^{\prime}\in J^{+}(x)
. For any
y\in M
, the following implication holds:

y\in J^{+}(x^{\prime})\Rightarrow y\in J^{+}(x)\text{ and }c(x,y)\leq c(x^{\prime},y).

Hence, denoting
\psi:=\varphi^{c}
, we have

\displaystyle\varphi(x)=\sup_{y\in J^{+}(x)}\Big(\psi(y)-c(x,y)\Big)\geq\sup_{y\in J^{+}(x^{\prime})}\Big(\psi(y)-c(x^{\prime},y)\Big)=\varphi(x^{\prime}).

(4.1)

Now, if
x\in\operatorname{int}(\operatorname{dom}(\varphi))
, choose points
x^{\pm}\in I^{\pm}(x)
such that
\varphi(x^{\pm})\in\mathbb{R}
. For
x^{\prime}
close to
x

Theorem 7.26
.

Let
s_{2}
be as in the previous lemma. There exists
s_{0}\in(0,s_{2}]
such that, for any
s\in[0,s_{0}]
,

\displaystyle\hat{T}_{s}^{loc}f_{i,t}(\psi_{i,t,s}(y))=f_{i,t}(y)-c_{s}(\psi_{i,t,s}(y),y),

In proving the theorem, it suffices to consider the cases

(with

, i.e. we will prove that the family

\{\hat{T}_{s}^{loc}f_{i,t}\circ\phi^{-1}\mid s\in[0,s_{0}],t\in J,i\in I_{t}\}

is uniformly locally semiconcave.
Then, by Proposition
7.19
(i), the uniform local semiconcavity also holds when adding
s=0
(note that
C^{2}
-boundedness implies uniform local semiconcavity
[
11
]
).

We will work both in local coordinates w.r.t. the chart
\phi:V_{3}\to W_{3}
, and intrinsically on the manifold. To distinguish between these settings, we will often use a tilde to indicate the local coordinate version of an object.

Define the index set
\Lambda:=\{(i,t)\mid t\in J,i\in I_{t}\}
. By Proposition
7.19
(ii), the set

C:={\{(y,d_{y}f_{\lambda})\mid y\in V_{1},\lambda\in\Lambda\}}

is relatively compact in
\operatorname{int}({\cal C}^{*})
. Therefore, there exists a precompact open neighbourhood

of
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muC\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
, and a time
T>0
such that the Hamiltonian flow
\psi
w.r.t.
H=L^{*}
(see Remark
A.11
) is well-defined on
(-2T,2T)\times O\subseteq\mathbb{R}\times T^{*}M
and takes values in
T^{*}V_{3}
. Let
\tilde{O}:=T^{*}\phi(O)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2n+2}
, where
T^{*}\phi
denotes the contangent bundle chart associated with
\phi
.
Then we can consider the map

\displaystyle F:(-2T,2T)\times\tilde{O}\to\mathbb{R}^{n+1},\ (s,\tilde{y},\tilde{p})\mapsto(\phi\circ\pi^{*}\circ\psi_{-s})((T^{*}\phi)^{-1}(\tilde{y},\tilde{p}))-\tilde{y}.

This map

is smooth and satisfies

. Consequently, there exists a modulus of continuity
\omega_{F}:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
such that, for any
s\in[-T,T]
, the map
F(s,\cdot,\cdot)
has Lipschitz constant
\omega_{F}(s)
on the compact set
\tilde{C}:=T^{*}\phi(C)\subseteq\tilde{O}
.

We now define
0<s_{0}\leq\min\{s_{2},T\}
such that

\displaystyle 1-(K+1)\omega_{F}(s_{0})>0\text{ and }C_{1}s_{0}^{1-p}\leq d_{h}(V,\partial V_{1})/2,

where
K
bounds the family
(f_{\lambda}\circ\phi^{-1})_{\lambda}
in
C^{2}(W_{3})
(Proposition
7.19
(i)), and
C_{1}
is the constant from the previous lemma.

Now, fix
s\in(0,s_{0}]
and
\lambda\in\Lambda
. Note that
\psi_{\lambda,s}
is well-defined on
V_{1}
by definition of
s_{0},T
and
C
. Since
s_{0}\leq s_{2}\leq s_{1}
, Lemma
7.24
(c) guarantees that
\hat{T}^{loc}_{s}f_{\lambda}
is locally semiconvex on
V
, and thus differentiable almost everywhere on
V
.
Furthermore, Lemma
7.24
(b) states that for every differentiability point
x\in V
of
\hat{T}^{loc}_{s}f_{\lambda}
, there exists a unique
y\in V_{3}
such that

\displaystyle\hat{T}^{loc}_{s}f_{\lambda}(x)=f_{\lambda}(y)-c_{s}(x,y),

(7.29)

and necessarily
y\in I^{+}(x)
. Moreover, by Lemma
7.25
and the choice of
s_{0}
, we have
y\in V_{1}
and
d_{h}(y,\partial V_{1})\geq d_{h}(V,\partial V_{1})/2
.
We claim that
x=\psi_{\lambda,s}(y)
.

Indeed, Lemma
7.24
(b) gives

\displaystyle d_{x}(\hat{T}^{loc}_{s}f_{\lambda})=\frac{\partial L}{\partial v}(x,\dot{\gamma}(0))\text{ and }d_{y}f_{\lambda}=\frac{\partial L}{\partial v}(y,\dot{\gamma}(s)),

where
\gamma:[0,s]\to M
is the unique maximizing geodesic connecting
x
to
y
. In particular, since the Hamiltonian flow is conjugate to the geodesic flow (Remark
7.20
),

\displaystyle(x,d_{x}(\hat{T}^{loc}_{s}f_{\lambda}))=\bigg(\gamma(0),\frac{\partial L}{\partial v}(\gamma(0),\dot{\gamma}(0))\bigg)

\displaystyle={\cal L}(\gamma(0),\dot{\gamma}(0))

\displaystyle={\cal L}(\phi_{-s}(\gamma(s),\dot{\gamma}(s)))

\displaystyle=\psi_{-s}\bigg(y,\frac{\partial L}{\partial v}(y,\dot{\gamma}(s))\bigg)

\displaystyle=\psi_{-s}(y,d_{y}f_{\lambda}).

(7.30)

It follows that
x=\psi_{\lambda,s}(y)
, as claimed.

Now let
\tilde{\psi}_{\lambda,s}:=\phi\circ\psi_{\lambda,s}\circ(\phi_{|V_{1}})^{-1}:W_{1}:=\phi(V_{1})\to W_{3}

\psi_{\lambda,s}

Since the map

and the uniform

, the mapping

\displaystyle W_{1}\to\mathbb{R}^{n+1},\ \tilde{y}\mapsto\tilde{\psi}_{\lambda,s}(\tilde{y})-\tilde{y}=F(s,\tilde{y},D(f_{\lambda}\circ\phi^{-1})(\tilde{y})),

. Thus, for any

, we have

\displaystyle|\tilde{\psi}_{\lambda,s}(\tilde{y})-\tilde{\psi}_{\lambda,s}(\tilde{y}^{\prime})|\geq|\tilde{y}-\tilde{y}^{\prime}|-(K+1)\omega_{F}(s)|\tilde{y}-\tilde{y}^{\prime}|.

(7.31)

By definition of

, this shows that

, and hence

, are homeomorphisms onto its image with locally Lipschitz inverse.

Now, if
\hat{T}^{loc}_{s}f_{\lambda}
is differentiable at

and

is such that (

) holds, we already saw that

,

and

. In particular, since locally semiconvex functions are differentiable a.e. (
[

]

, namely all the differentiability points of

. It follows that

:

, let

be such that (

) holds (with

). Then, thanks to the local Lipschitz continuity of

and

,

converges to some

, and the continuity of

. Thus, we indeed have

Moreover, denoting by

) shows for any differentiability point

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\hat{T}^{loc}_{s}f_{\lambda}}(x)=\psi_{-s}\circ\Gamma_{f_{\lambda}}\circ\psi_{\lambda,s}^{-1}(x).

Hence, if

denotes the Hamiltonian flow in local coordinates, we have for

, the differentiability point in local coordinates,

\displaystyle\Gamma_{(\hat{T}^{loc}_{s}f_{\lambda})\circ\phi^{-1}}(\tilde{x})=\tilde{\psi}_{-s}\circ\Gamma_{f_{\lambda}\circ\phi^{-1}}\circ\tilde{\psi}_{\lambda,s}^{-1}(\tilde{x}).

and Lipschitz continuous with Lipschitz constant

\displaystyle\operatorname{Lip}(\tilde{\psi}_{|[-T,T]\times\tilde{C}})(K+1)(1-(K+1)\omega_{F}(s_{0}))^{-1}

which is independent of

and

. In particular,

must be

on

In particular, the family

[

]

Finally, if

,

, then

is a differentiability point of

, and our first claim in the proof shows that

\displaystyle\hat{T}^{loc}_{s}f(\psi_{\lambda,s}(y))=f(y)-c_{s}(\psi_{\lambda,s}(y),y).

This concludes the proof.

, and let

(see Remark

\displaystyle\hat{T}_{s}T_{t}u(x)=T_{t}u(y)-c_{s}(x,y),

If

\displaystyle p:=\frac{\partial L}{\partial v}(y,\dot{\gamma}(s))\in\partial^{+}(T_{t}u)(y).

Therefore, Proposition

\psi_{i,t,s}(z)=\gamma(0)=x\in V

Hence, by the previous theorem, we obtain

Lemma A.1

Let

Proof.

This follows immediately from Hölder’s inequality with exponents

\displaystyle\bar{\pi}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}(J^{-}(A)\times A)\in\Gamma(\mu\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}J^{-}(A),\nu\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}A).

\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}

\displaystyle(p_{1})_{\#}\tilde{\pi}(B)=\bar{\pi}((B\cap J^{-}(A))\times A)\leq\bar{\pi}((B\cap J^{-}(A))\times M)=(\mu\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}J^{-}(A))(B).

On the other hand, the causality of

A similar argument shows that
(p_{2})_{\#}\tilde{\pi}=\nu\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}A
.

To prove the claim, by the connectedness of
\operatorname{supp}(\mu)
, it suffices to prove that
I^{-}(A)\cap\operatorname{supp}(\mu)
is non-empty, closed and also open in
\operatorname{supp}(\mu)
. We pick a causal coupling
\bar{\pi}\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)
with
\operatorname{supp}(\bar{\pi})\subseteq I^{+}
.

(1)

It is immediate that
I^{-}(A)\cap\operatorname{supp}(\mu)
is open in
\operatorname{supp}(\mu)
(since
I^{-}(A)
is open).

(2)

To prove that the set is non-empty, we choose
\bar{x}_{0}\in\operatorname{supp}(\mu)
with
(\bar{x}_{0},y_{0})\in\operatorname{supp}(\bar{\pi})\subseteq I^{+}

.

(3)

To prove that

\operatorname{supp}(\mu)

(x_{k})_{k}\subseteq\operatorname{supp}(\mu)\cap I^{-}(A)
with

x_{k}\in\operatorname{supp}(\mu\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}J^{-}(A))

\operatorname{supp}(\nu\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}A)

, some

(x_{k},y_{k})\in\operatorname{supp}(\bar{\pi}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}(J^{-}(A)\times A))
(here we use the fact that
\bar{\pi}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}(J^{-}(A)\times A)\in\Gamma(\mu\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}J^{-}(A),\nu\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}A)
). We can assume that
(x_{k},y_{k})\in J^{-}(A)\times A
. Again by the causal compactness of
\operatorname{supp}(\nu)
we have that, along a subsequence,
(x_{k},y_{k})\to(x,y)\in\operatorname{supp}(\bar{\pi}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}(J^{-}(A)\times A))\subseteq I^{+}
for some

and, therefore,

.

This concludes the proof of the claim.
✓

(2) We have shown that, for any
(x_{0},y_{0})\in\Gamma
and

, there exists

such that

.

Suppose this was not the case. Then we can find, for every

, some
(x_{0,k},y_{0,k})\in\Gamma\cap(K\times M)
and

and

\operatorname{supp}(\nu)
, also

\operatorname{supp}(\mu)
, there exists
\bar{x}_{0}\in\operatorname{supp}(\mu)

(\bar{x}_{0},y_{0})\in\operatorname{supp}(\bar{\pi})\subseteq I^{+}

\mu

\tilde{x}_{0}\approx\bar{x}_{0}\in p_{1}(\Gamma)\text{ with }\tilde{x}_{0}\in I^{-}(y_{0}).

Proof of the first part of Theorem
1.4
(i).

Let
\Gamma\subseteq\operatorname{supp}(\pi)\subseteq J^{+}
be a
c
-cyclically monotone set on which
\pi
is concentrated. Since subsets of
c
-cyclically monotone sets are again
c
-cyclically monotone and
\pi
is inner-regular (
[
14
]
, Lemma 2.1.9), we may assume that
\Gamma
is
\sigma
-compact.

Fix
(x_{0},y_{0})\in\Gamma
, and let us define the function

\displaystyle\varphi:M\to\overline{\mathbb{R}},\ \varphi(x):=\sup\bigg\{\sum_{i=0}^{k}c(x_{i},y_{i})-c(x_{i+1},y_{i})\bigg\},

where the supremum is taken over all
k\in\mathbb{N}
and all chains
\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{1\leq i\leq k}\subseteq\Gamma
. Here,
x_{k+1}:=x
.
We claim that this function is a
\pi
-solution.

Note that
\varphi
is well-defined with values in
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbb{R}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
since
c(x_{i},y_{i})\in\mathbb{R}
. By construction,
\varphi
is
c
-convex relatively to
(M,\operatorname{supp}(\nu))
with

\psi(y):=\sup\bigg\{\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\Big(c(x_{i},y_{i})-c(x_{i+1},y_{i})\Big)+c(x_{k},y)\bigg\}\text{ in }\eqref{eqbs},

where the supremum is taken over all
k\in\mathbb{N}
and all chains
\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{1\leq i\leq k}\subseteq\Gamma
with
y_{k}:=y
and the convention that
\sup(\emptyset):=-\infty
(cf.
[
30
]
, Step 3 of Theorem 5.10(i)).

Since
\Gamma
is
c
-monotone, we have
\varphi(x_{0})=0
. Moreover, we have

\displaystyle\varphi(x^{\prime})\geq\varphi(x)+c(x,y)-c(x^{\prime},y)\text{ for all }(x,y)\in\Gamma\text{ and }x^{\prime}\in M.

In particular,
\varphi^{c}(y)=\varphi(x)+c(x,y)
for
(x,y)\in\Gamma
. If we can show that
\varphi(x)\in\mathbb{R}
for all
x\in p_{1}(\Gamma)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\varphi^{c}(y)-\varphi(x)=c(x,y)\text{ on }\Gamma,\text{ hence }\pi\text{-a.e.},

which would complete the proof of the first part of (i). Thus, in the rest of the proof we will show that
\varphi
is finite on
p_{1}(\Gamma)
.

Let
x\in\operatorname{supp}(\mu)
. If
\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{0\leq i\leq k}
is a sequence as in the above proposition with
x\in I^{-}(y_{k})
, then

\varphi(x)\geq\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\Big(c(x_{i},y_{i})-c(x_{i+1},y_{i})\Big)+c(x_{k},y_{k})-c(x,y_{k})>-\infty.

On the other hand, if
x\in p_{1}(\Gamma)
, pick
y\in M
such that
(x,y)\in\Gamma
, and consider a chain
\{(\tilde{x}_{i},\tilde{y}_{i})\}_{0\leq i\leq k}
as in the above proposition with
(\tilde{x}_{0},\tilde{y}_{0})=(x,y)
and
x_{0}:=\tilde{x}_{k+1}\in I^{-}(\tilde{y}_{k})
. Then

0=\varphi(x_{0})\geq\varphi(x)+\sum_{i=0}^{k}\Big(c(\tilde{x}_{i},y_{i})-c(\tilde{x}_{i+1},y_{i})\Big).

Hence, also
\varphi(x)<\infty
, and this shows that
\varphi
is finite on
p_{1}(\Gamma)
. As noted above, this concludes the proof of the first part.
∎

Appendix C
Local semiconcavity

Definition C.1
.

(a)

A function
f:U\to\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbb{R}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
defined on an open set
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is said to be
super-differentiable
(resp.
sub-differentiable
) at
x\in U
with
super-differential
(resp.
sub-differential
)
p\in(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{*}
if
f(x)\in\mathbb{R}
and

\displaystyle f(y)\leq f(x)+\langle p,y-x\rangle+o(|y-x|)

\displaystyle(\text{resp. }

\displaystyle f(y)\geq f(x)+\langle p,y-x\rangle+o(|y-x|)).

(b)

A function
f:U\to\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbb{R}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
defined on an open subset
U\subseteq N
of a smooth manifold
N
is said to be
super-differentiable
at
x\in U
with
super-differential

p\in T_{x}^{*}N
if
f(x)\in\mathbb{R}
and if, for one (or any) chart
(\phi,V)
of
U
around
x
, the function
f\circ\phi^{-1}
is super-differentiable at
\phi(x)
with super-differential
p\circ d_{\phi(x)}\phi^{-1}
.
We denote by
\partial^{+}f(x)
the set of all super-differentials at
x
.

A similar definition holds for sub-differentiable functions, whose sub-differentials at
x
are denoted by
\partial^{-}f(x)
.

Definition C.2
.

(a)

A function
f:U\to\mathbb{R}
defined on an open set
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is said to be
K
-semiconcave
if, for all
x\in U
, there exists
p\in(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{*}
such that

\displaystyle f(y)\leq f(x)+\langle p,y-x\rangle+K|y-x|^{2}\ \forall y\in U.

(C.1)

(b)

A family of functions
f_{i}:U\to\mathbb{R}
,
i\in I
, defined on an open set
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is said to be
uniformly semiconcave
if the constant
K
in definition (a) can be chosen uniformly for all functions
f_{i}
.

(c)

A function
f:U\to\mathbb{R}
defined on an open set
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is said to be
locally semiconcave
if, for every
x\in U
, there exists an open neighborhood
V\subseteq U
around
x
such that
f
is
K
-semiconcave on
V
for some
K>0
.

(d)

A function
f:U\to\mathbb{R}
defined on an open subset
U\subseteq N
of a smooth manifold
N
is said to be
locally semiconcave
if, for each chart
(\phi,V)
of
U
, the function
f\circ\phi^{-1}
is locally semiconcave.

(e)

A family of functions
f_{i}:U\to\mathbb{R}
,
i\in I
, defined on an open subset
U\subseteq N
of a smooth manifold
N
is said to be
uniformly locally semiconcave
if, for each
x\in U
, there exists a chart
(\phi,V)
of
U
around
x
such that the family of functions
f_{i}\circ\phi^{-1}
,
i\in I
, is uniformly semiconcave.

(f)

All of the above definitions also apply to semiconvexity by replacing
f
with
-f
.
