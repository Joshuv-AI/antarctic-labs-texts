Association between serum uric acid and benign prostatic hyperplasia among Chinese men aged 45 years or older
Authors: Jiangtao Li, Peng Cao, Yanfang Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) is a leading cause of lower urinary tract symptoms in older men and imposes a growing health burden on aging populations. However, the association between serum uric acid levels and BPH remains inconclusive, and evidence from Chinese populations is scarce. This study investigates the prospective association between baseline serum uric acid (UA) levels and benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) among Chinese men aged 45 years or older, with particular attention to potential nonlinear and subgroup-specific associations. Baseline and follow-up data were obtained from the 2011 and 2015 waves of the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study, which used a multistage, stratified probability sampling design. Multivariable logistic regression was conducted to evaluate the association between baseline serum UA levels and BPH using three progressively adjusted models that included 11 covariates: age, education, residence, marital status, smoking status, alcohol consumption, body mass index, hypertension, history of cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and dyslipidemia. Restricted cubic spline regression was applied to examine potential nonlinear and dose-response associations. All analyses were performed using R version 4.3.2. Of the 4755 participants, 250 (5.26%) had BPH at follow-up. After adjustment for covariates, participants in the highest UA quartile (Q4) had higher odds of BPH (odds ratio [OR], 1.025; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.006–1.045). Restricted cubic spline analysis indicated a U-shaped nonlinear association between serum UA levels and BPH (P for nonlinearity = 0.029). Serum UA levels above 5.92 mg/dL were associated with higher odds of BPH, while each interquartile range increase in UA corresponded to a 19.8% increase in the odds of BPH (OR, 1.198; 95% CI, 1.024–1.402). Receiver operating characteristic analysis yielded an area under the curve of 0.779 and identified 5.26 mg/dL as the optimal cutoff value. Subgroup analyses further showed a significant interaction between serum UA levels and residence. Baseline serum UA levels were nonlinearly associated with BPH at follow-up among Chinese men aged 45 years or older. However, the observational design precludes conclusions regarding causality or clinical utility. These findings should therefore be considered hypothesis-generating and require confirmation in studies using clinically validated BPH outcomes.

1. Introduction

Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) is a prevalent condition in older men, marked by uncontrolled overgrowth of epithelial and fibromuscular tissue in the urethra, resulting in lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS), including increased frequency, urgency, intermittent incontinence, weak flow, difficulty urinating, and incomplete bladder emptying.[1] From 1990 to 2019, the incident counts of BPH increased by 105.70%, with the highest rates observed among individuals aged 65–69 years.[2] In addition to age, several modifiable lifestyle and cardiometabolic factors, such as sedentary behavior, obesity, hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia, have been associated with BPH/LUTS.[3–6] Recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses further indicate that metabolic syndrome (MetS) is associated with increased prostate volume and greater symptom burden.[7,8]

Uric acid (UA), the end product of purine metabolism in humans, exhibits context-dependent antioxidant, pro-oxidant, and pro-inflammatory properties.[9–12] Both elevated and abnormally low serum UA levels have been associated with adverse health outcomes, suggesting a potentially nonlinear association between serum UA and health status.[13,14] Serum UA is also closely linked to metabolic syndrome (MetS) and related cardiometabolic abnormalities.[15,16] Hyperuricemia plays a central role in the pathogenesis of gout, and longitudinal evidence suggests that patients with gout have a higher risk of BPH than those without gout.[17,18] Additionally, UA is positively associated with the International Prostate Symptom Score (IPSS).[19] Conversely, some studies suggest that elevated UA levels may protect against BPH, with individuals who have higher UA levels showing a lower risk of LUTS than those with lower levels.[20,21] The variability in these findings may reflect the distinct roles of UA in different chemical environments and genetic polymorphisms in its molecular targets.[22,23] While interest in the association between UA and BPH is growing, the nature of this relationship remains incompletely understood. The dual pro-inflammatory/pro-oxidant and antioxidant properties of UA provide a biologically plausible basis for a nonlinear association with BPH. Clarifying this relationship is clinically relevant because serum UA is routinely measured, while BPH is highly prevalent and substantially impairs quality of life in older men. Nevertheless, longitudinal evidence in Chinese men remains limited, and few studies have concurrently evaluated dose-response nonlinearity and potential effect modification.

Therefore, using data from the 2011 China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) baseline survey and the 2015 follow-up, we aimed to assess the association between baseline serum UA levels and BPH at follow-up, characterize the dose-response pattern, and determine whether this association differed across clinically relevant subgroups.

2. Methods

2.1. Participants

The China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), jointly conducted by Peking University and Wuhan University, aims to collect high-quality microdata on Chinese adults aged 45 years and older at both the individual and household levels. The national baseline survey was conducted in 2011 and covered 150 counties and 450 villages across 28 provinces.[24] CHARLS employed a four-stage, stratified probability sampling design. After stratification by geographic region, urban–rural status, and level of economic development, county-level units were selected using probability proportional to size. Villages or urban communities, households, and eligible individuals were then sampled sequentially. This approach was designed to yield a nationally representative sample of middle-aged and older adults in China.

The CHARLS protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Peking University (IRB00001052-11015), and all participants provided written informed consent before enrollment. As the present study involved a secondary analysis of de-identified, publicly available CHARLS data and no direct participant contact, additional informed consent was not required.

The present analysis used data from the 2011 baseline and 2015 follow-up waves of CHARLS and included adults aged 45 years or older at baseline (n = 17,314). At baseline, participants were excluded because of missing serum UA data (n = 5880), missing data on other covariates (n = 5758), or prevalent BPH (n = 306), leaving 5370 eligible participants. An additional 615 participants were excluded because of missing BPH status at follow-up, resulting in a final analytical sample of 4755 participants (Fig. 1). This study was conducted as a single prospective observational cohort rather than using separately recruited intervention and control groups. All participants in the analytical cohort were free of BPH at baseline and were prospectively followed from 2011 to 2015. For exposure-based comparisons, participants were categorized according to baseline serum UA quartiles, with the lowest quartile (Q1) serving as the reference category in categorical analyses.

3. Measurement

3.1. Assessment of uric acid

Following an overnight fast, serum samples were collected during the 2011 CHARLS wave, transported to Peking University, stored at − 80°C, and analyzed within 2 weeks. Serum UA concentrations were measured centrally using the UA Plus enzymatic colorimetric assay and reported in milligrams per deciliter (mg/dL). According to the CHARLS laboratory documentation, the intra-assay and inter-assay coefficients of variation were 1.10% and 1.90%, respectively, with quality-control samples included in each analytical batch. Further details of the quality-control procedures have been reported elsewhere.[25]

3.2. Assessment of benign prostatic hyperplasia

BPH status was assessed using the standardized CHARLS question, “Have you ever been diagnosed with a prostate illness, such as prostate hyperplasia (excluding prostatic cancer)?” Participants who responded “yes” were classified as having BPH, whereas those who responded “no” were classified as not having BPH. Because BPH status was based on a self-reported physician diagnosis, it could not be independently validated against medical records, prostate imaging, prostate volume measurements, or the International Prostate Symptom Score in this dataset. This limitation was considered when interpreting the findings.

3.3. Assessment of covariates

Covariates included age, educational attainment, marital status, residence, body mass index (BMI), smoking status, alcohol consumption, dyslipidemia, hypertension, diabetes, and a history of cardiovascular disease. Educational attainment was categorized as illiterate, below primary school, or middle school or higher. Residence was classified as rural village or urban community, and marital status as married or other, including never married, separated, divorced, or widowed. BMI was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared and categorized as < 18.5, 18.5–<24, 24–<28, or ≥ 28 kg/m2. Smoking status was classified as never, former, or current, whereas alcohol consumption was categorized as never, less than once per month, or at least once per month. Self-reported hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and history of cardiovascular disease were each classified as present or absent.

3.4. Validity, reliability, and quality control

No new questionnaire or measurement instrument was developed for this secondary analysis. CHARLS employed standardized questionnaires, trained interviewers, computer-assisted personal interviewing, centralized laboratory testing, and prespecified quality-control procedures to ensure measurement consistency. As described above, the repeatability of the UA assay was assessed using intra-assay and inter-assay coefficients of variation. However, the self-reported BPH outcome was not independently validated against clinical measures in the present analysis.

3.5. Statistical analysis

Normally distributed continuous variables were summarized as means with standard deviations, whereas non-normally distributed variables were presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs). Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages.

Serum UA was initially analyzed as a continuous variable. To examine dose–response patterns, UA levels were subsequently categorized into quartiles, and the median value of each quartile was entered into the model as a continuous term to test for linear trend. Baseline characteristics and BPH status were compared across UA quartiles using one-way analysis of variance, the Kruskal–Wallis H test, or the chi-square test, as appropriate.

Three logistic regression models were fitted to estimate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for BPH, with UA modeled either continuously per IQR increase or categorically by quartile; for the categorical analyses, the lowest UA quartile (Q1) was used as the reference group. Model 1 was unadjusted. Model 2 was adjusted for age, educational attainment, residence, and marital status. Model 3 was further adjusted for BMI, smoking status, alcohol consumption, hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and a history of cardiovascular disease.

An exploratory receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was conducted to evaluate discrimination, with the optimal cutoff value determined using the Youden index. Effect modification was assessed by including interaction terms between UA and each prespecified subgroup variable. Restricted cubic spline regression was applied to examine and visualize potential nonlinear dose–response associations between UA and BPH.

All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A two-sided P value < .05 was considered statistically significant.

4. Result

4.1. Baseline characteristics

The study included 4755 participants with a median age of 59.6 years, of whom 250 (5.26%) reported BPH at follow-up. The median baseline serum UA concentration was 4.30 mg/dL (IQR, 3.56–5.16). Compared with participants without BPH, those with BPH were older, more likely to reside in urban communities, and had higher educational attainment and serum UA levels. They were also more likely to smoke, consume alcohol, and have dyslipidemia (Table 1).

4.2. Dose–response relationship between uric acid and benign prostatic hyperplasia

Table 2 presents the associations between serum UA levels and BPH, with UA modeled both continuously and by quartile. The odds of BPH increased across UA quartiles (P for trend < .05). After adjustment for age, educational attainment, residence, marital status, BMI, smoking status, alcohol consumption, hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and a history of cardiovascular disease, participants in the highest UA quartile (Q4) had higher odds of BPH than those in the lowest quartile (Q1) (OR, 1.025; 95% CI, 1.006–1.045). When modeled continuously, serum UA was positively associated with BPH (OR, 1.010; 95% CI, 1.001–1.019). Exploratory receiver operating characteristic analysis yielded an area under the curve of 0.779, with 5.26 mg/dL identified as the cutoff that maximized the Youden index.

4.3. Stratified analysis

The association between serum UA and BPH was generally consistent across the examined subgroups. However, a significant interaction was observed between serum UA and residence (P for interaction < .001). Among participants residing in urban communities, each IQR increase in serum UA was associated with a 111.0% increase in the odds of BPH (OR, 2.110; 95% CI, 1.420–3.159) (Fig. 3).

Forest plot of stratified analysis of the association of UA with the risk of BPH. The plot indicates significant interaction between UA and residence.

5. Discussion

In this prospective cohort analysis of data from a nationally representative survey, higher baseline serum UA levels were associated with greater odds of BPH at follow-up. Participants who reported BPH were older, more likely to reside in urban areas, and had higher educational attainment and serum UA levels, together with higher prevalences of smoking, alcohol consumption, and dyslipidemia. After adjustment for potential confounders, each IQR increase in serum UA corresponded to a 19.8% increase in the odds of BPH. Dose–response analysis further revealed a nonlinear association between serum UA and BPH.

BPH is a major cause of LUTS and poses a growing health burden in aging men.[26] Its pathophysiology is multifactorial, with age-related hormonal alterations and chronic inflammation considered key contributors.[27,28] Recent findings further highlight the complexity of the UA–BPH relationship. A comprehensive review published in 2025 identified serum UA as a controversial factor in BPH, whereas a large cross-sectional study of 48,653 men found a lower prevalence of BPH among individuals with higher serum UA levels.[29,30] Experimental evidence further supports the biological plausibility of this relationship. In a testosterone-induced BPH model, Abdali et al demonstrated that probenecid and eugenol attenuated prostate enlargement, accompanied by reductions in UA-associated inflammation, oxidative stress, and pro-survival signaling.[31] Han et al identified an inverse association between serum UA and testosterone levels in adult men,[32] whereas Wang et al reported positive associations between UA and free, bioavailable, and total estradiol in another male cohort.[33] Recent mechanistic reviews further suggest that age-related alterations in sex hormone signaling, particularly imbalances in sex hormone levels, may contribute to BPH pathogenesis.[34] Collectively, these findings indicate a complex interplay between UA and sex hormones in relation to BPH.

Inflammation may represent another potential link between UA and BPH. Elevated serum UA levels and uric acid–based inflammatory indices have been associated with systemic inflammatory and metabolic states.[35] Recent evidence also suggests that chronic prostatic inflammation, immune cell infiltration, and cytokine- and growth factor–mediated signaling may contribute to the development and progression of BPH.[36–38] In particular, interleukin-6 signaling may activate the Janus kinase/signal transducer and activator of transcription pathway, promote phosphorylation of signal transducer and activator of transcription 3, and induce the transcription of genes involved in cell proliferation.[39] Taken together, these mechanisms may partly explain the observed association between elevated serum UA levels and BPH.

Nevertheless, the role of serum UA in prostate health remains controversial. A cross-sectional study reported an inverse association between UA and BPH,[20] whereas a cohort study of 101,091 middle-aged Korean men found that higher UA levels were associated with a lower probability of LUTS.[21] These findings suggest that the antioxidant properties of UA may exert protective effects under certain conditions. Serum UA levels are generally inversely associated with testosterone, while testosterone itself may influence prostate volume. In middle-aged eugonadal men, exogenous testosterone increased prostate volume by approximately 12%,[40] and testosterone concentrations in the transition zone have been associated with prostate volume.[41] Thus, the interplay among hormonal status, chronic hyperuricemia, and low-grade inflammation may be relevant to prostate enlargement.

The association between serum UA and BPH varied by residence, with higher serum UA levels associated with greater odds of BPH among urban residents. This pattern may partly reflect urban–rural differences in health-care utilization and diagnostic opportunities,[42] together with variations in dietary patterns and physical activity.[43,44] However, these potential explanations remain speculative and warrant further investigation.

The observed U-shaped association should not be interpreted as evidence that serum UA levels should be deliberately increased or decreased to prevent BPH. Given the observational design, residual confounding, outcome misclassification, and reverse causation cannot be excluded. Instead, these findings support further prospective studies incorporating clinically validated BPH outcomes, prostate volume measurements, symptom scores, and repeated assessments of serum UA before preventive or therapeutic recommendations can be considered.

This study has several limitations. First, residual confounding may persist despite adjustment for multiple covariates. Second, BPH was ascertained through self-reported physician diagnosis and may, therefore, be subject to recall bias and outcome misclassification. Third, serum UA was measured only at baseline, and longitudinal changes were not assessed. Fourth, the complete-case analysis may have introduced selection bias. Finally, the study population was restricted to adults aged 45 years or older, limiting the generalizability of the findings to younger populations.

6. Conclusions

Among Chinese men aged 45 years or older, baseline serum UA levels showed a nonlinear association with BPH at follow-up. Serum UA concentrations above the estimated inflection point were associated with greater odds of BPH, and the strength of this association varied by residence. Given the observational design, these findings do not establish causality or support intentional modification of serum UA levels for BPH prevention. Further validation using objectively ascertained clinical outcomes is warranted.
