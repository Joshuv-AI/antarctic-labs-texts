Symmetric matrices defined by plane vector sequences
Mikiya Masuda

Abstract
. Motivated by a work of Fu-So-Song, we associate a symmetric matrix A A to a plane vector sequence v v and give a formula to find the signature of A A in terms of the sequence v v . When A A is nonsingular, we interpret the relation between A A and A − 1 A^{-1} from a topological viewpoint. Finally, we associate an omnioriented quasitoric orbifold X X of real dimension four to the sequence v v and show that A − 1 A^{-1} is the intersection matrix of the characteristic suborbifolds of X X .

1.
Introduction

Let
v=(v_{0},v_{1},\dots,v_{n},v_{n+1})

(n\geq 1)
be a sequence of plane vectors where

(1.1)

\text{$v_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix}$, $v_{i}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{i}\\
b_{i}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}$ for $1\leq i\leq n$, and $v_{n+1}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}$}.

Motivated by Fu-So-Song
[
2
]
, we form a symmetric matrix
A
of order
n
with
a_{i}b_{j}
as
(i,j)
entry for
i\leq j
from the sequence
v
, that is

(1.2)

A=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}b_{1}&a_{1}b_{2}&\dots&a_{1}b_{n-1}&a_{1}b_{n}\\
a_{1}b_{2}&a_{2}b_{2}&\dots&a_{2}b_{n-1}&a_{2}b_{n}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
a_{1}b_{n-1}&a_{2}b_{n-1}&\dots&a_{n-1}b_{n-1}&a_{n-1}b_{n}\\
a_{1}b_{n}&a_{2}b_{n}&\dots&a_{n-1}b_{n}&a_{n}b_{n}\end{pmatrix}

One can easily find that

(1.3)

\det A=(-1)^{n+1}\prod_{i=0}^{n}\det(v_{i},v_{i+1})=a_{1}b_{n}\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}(a_{i+1}b_{i}-a_{i}b_{i+1}).

Since
A
is symmetric, its eigenvalues are all real numbers and the signature of
A
, denoted by
\Sign(A)
, is defined to be the number of positive eigenvalues of
A
minus the number of negative eigenvalues of
A
.
If
\det(v_{i},v_{i+1})=0
for some
i
, then one can see that the symmetric matrix
A^{\prime}
defined by a vector sequence
v^{\prime}
with
v_{i}
or
v_{i+1}
removed from
v
has the same signature as
A
. Therefore, we assume

(1.4)

\text{$\det(v_{i},v_{i+1})\not=0$ for every $i=0,\dots,n$, i.e. $\det A\not=0$}.

We understand that
v_{i}
rotates toward
v_{i+1}
with angle less than
\pi
for each
i
. The vectors
v_{0},v_{1},\dots,v_{n},v_{n+1},v_{0}
rotate around the origin of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
possibly many times and may go back and forth. We introduce two invariants of the sequence
v=(v_{0},v_{1},\dots,v_{n},v_{n+1})
:

(1.5)

\begin{split}R(v)&=\text{ the rotation number of $v$ around the origin of $\mathbb{R}^{2}$},\\
S(v)&=1+\sum_{i=0}^{n}{\rm sgn}(\det(v_{i},v_{i+1}))\end{split}

where
{\rm sgn}(a)
denotes
1
if
a>0
and
-1
if
a<0
, and
1
at the right hand side above comes from
{\rm sgn}(\det(v_{n+1},v_{0}))
. Our first main result is the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

\Sign(A)=4R(v)-S(v)
.

Remark
.

For an almost complex compact manifold
X
of complex dimension two, it is well-known that
T(X)=\frac{1}{4}(\Sign(X)+\chi(X))
where
T(X)
is the Todd genus of
X
,
\Sign(X)
is the signature of
X
, and
\chi(X)
is the Euler characteristic of
X
. Hence we have
\Sign(X)=4T(X)-\chi(X)
. Theorem
1.1
is a combinatorial analogue of this identity.

Example 1.2
.

(1) We take
n=2
and
v_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}-1\\
-1\end{pmatrix}
,
v_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
-1\end{pmatrix}
. Then
A=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}
(so
\Sign(A)=0
) while
R(v)=1
and
S(v)=4
.

(2) We take
n=2
and
v_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}-1\\
-1\end{pmatrix}
,
v_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}-2\\
-1\end{pmatrix}
. Then
A=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
1&2\end{pmatrix}
(so
\Sign(A)=2
) while
R(v)=1
and
S(v)=2
.

(3) We take
n=4
and

v_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}-1\\
-1\end{pmatrix},\ v_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix},\ v_{3}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix},\ v_{4}=\begin{pmatrix}-1\\
-1\end{pmatrix}

Then

A=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&-1&1\\
0&0&1&-1\\
-1&1&0&0\\
1&-1&0&1\end{pmatrix}

One can find
\Sign(A)=2
while
R(v)=2
and
S(v)=6
.

It follows from the definition of the signature,
\Sign(A)
must be
n-2k
for some
0\leq k\leq n
. The following corollary shows that any such value can be realized as
\Sign(A)
.

Corollary 1.3
.

Suppose that all
v_{i}
’s lie on the upper half plane of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. If the sequence
v=(v_{0},v_{1},\dots,v_{n+1})
rotates counterclockwise up to
v_{k}
for some
0\leq k\leq n
and clockwise from
v_{k}
around the origin, then
\Sign(A)=n-2k
.
In particular,
A
is positive definite
(
i.e.
\Sign(A)=n
)
if
v=(v_{0},v_{1},\dots,v_{n},v_{n+1})
rotates around the origin clockwise.

Proof.

In this case
R(v)=0
and
S(v)=-n+2k
since
\det(v_{i},v_{i+1})<0
if and only if
k\leq i\leq n
.
∎

A simple calculation shows that the inverse
A^{-1}
of our matrix
A
is a nonsingular tridiagonal symmetric matrix with entries in the superdiagonal (i.e. the diagonal just above the main diagonal) of
A^{-1}
being all nonzero (Proposition
3.1
). Conversely, any such nonsingular tridiagonal symmetric matrix can be obtained as
A^{-1}
of our matrix
A
(Proposition
3.3
).
We observe the relation between
A
and
A^{-1}
from a topological viewpoint in Section
4
. This observation is made in
[
3
]
from a different perspective when each
v_{i}
is a primitive integer vector and the sequence
v
rotates around the origin once counterclockwise.

To any integer vector sequence
v
satisfying the condition (
1.4
), one can associate an omnioriented quasitoric orbifold
X
of real dimension four. When
\{v_{i},v_{i+1}\}
forms a basis of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
for any
0\leq i\leq n
,
X
is smooth. For instance, the
X
associated to the integer vector sequence
v
in Example
1.2
is respectively
\mathbb{C}P^{2}\#\overline{\mathbb{C}P^{2}}
,
\mathbb{C}P^{2}\#{\mathbb{C}P^{2}}
,
3\mathbb{C}P^{2}\#\overline{\mathbb{C}P^{2}}
, where
\overline{\mathbb{C}P^{2}}
denotes
\mathbb{C}P^{2}
with the opposite orientation. Note that
\Sign(X)=\Sign(A)
. In general, the characteristic suborbifolds
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
of
X
corresponding to
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}
define a basis
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}
of
H_{2}(X;\mathbb{R})
. We prove that the intersection matrix of
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
is the inverse of

(Theorem
5.1
). This result together with the observation made in Sections
3
and
4
implies the following.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
X
be an omnioriented quasitoric orbifold associated to an integer vector sequence
v
satisfying (
1.4
) and let
\alpha_{1}^{*},\dots,\alpha_{n}^{*}
be the Kronecker dual to
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}
, i.e.
\alpha_{i}^{*}\in H^{2}(X;\mathbb{R})
and
\langle\alpha_{i}^{*},\alpha_{j}\rangle
is the Kronecker delta
\delta_{ij}
. Then
a_{i}b_{j}=\langle\alpha_{i}^{*}\cup\alpha_{j}^{*},[X]\rangle
for
i\leq j
, i.e.
A=(\langle\alpha_{i}^{*}\cup\alpha_{j}^{*},[X]\rangle)
, where
[X]
denotes the fundamental class of
X
.

This result is proved in
[
2
]
(by a different method) also when each
v_{i}
is a primitive integer vector and the sequence
v
rotates around the origin once counterclockwise (in this case
X
is a genuine toric orbifold).

2.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

For a while, we allow
v_{i}
’s
(1\leq i\leq n)
to be arbitrary plane (even zero) vectors.
The following is (
1.3
) in the introduction.

Lemma 2.1
.

\det A=a_{1}b_{n}\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}(a_{i+1}b_{i}-a_{i}b_{i+1})
.

Proof.

We regard
a_{i}
’s and
b_{i}
’s as indeterminates and prove the lemma by induction on
n
. When
n=1
, the lemma is obvious. Suppose that
n\geq 2
and the lemma holds for
n-1
. Adding the
(n-1)
st column multiplied by
-b_{n}/b_{n-1}
to the
n
-th column in (
1.2
), we obtain

(2.1)

\det A=\begin{vmatrix}a_{1}b_{1}&a_{1}b_{2}&\dots&a_{1}b_{n-1}&0\\
a_{1}b_{2}&a_{2}b_{2}&\dots&a_{2}b_{n-1}&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
a_{1}b_{n-1}&a_{2}b_{n-1}&\dots&a_{n-1}b_{n-1}&0\\
a_{1}b_{n}&a_{2}b_{n}&\dots&a_{n-1}b_{n}&a_{n}b_{n}-a_{n-1}b_{n}\cdot\frac{b_{n}}{b_{n-1}}\end{vmatrix}

Since the
(n,n)
entry above is
(a_{n}b_{n-1}-a_{n-1}b_{n})b_{n}/b_{n-1}
, it follows from the induction assumption that (
2.1
) turns into

\begin{split}\det A&=a_{1}b_{n-1}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n-2}(a_{i+1}b_{i}-a_{i}b_{i+1})\right)(a_{n}b_{n-1}-a_{n-1}b_{n})\frac{b_{n}}{b_{n-1}}\\
&=a_{1}b_{n}\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}(a_{i+1}b_{i}-a_{i}b_{i+1}),\end{split}

proving the lemma.
∎

If
v_{i}
is zero for some
1\leq i\leq n
, then entries in the
i
-th row and
i
-th column of
A
are all zero. Therefore, if
A^{\prime}
is
A
with
i
-th row and
i
-th column removed, then the set of eigenvalues of
A^{\prime}
agrees with the set of eigenvalues of
A
with one
0
removed. In particular,
\Sign(A^{\prime})=\Sign(A)
. The
A^{\prime}
is associated to the sequence
v
with
v_{i}
removed, so we may remove
v_{i}
from
v
in order to find
\Sign(A)
whenever
v_{i}
is zero. Thus, we may assume that all
v_{i}
’s are nonzero.

Suppose that
v_{i}
and
v_{i+1}
are linearly dependent for some
0\leq i\leq n
. If
i=0
, then
a_{1}=0
since
v_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix}
; so entries in the
1
st row and
1
st column of
A
are all zero. Therefore, we may remove
v_{1}
from the sequence
v
by the same reason as above. A similar argument works when
i=n
.

In the sequel, we assume
v_{i+1}=kv_{i}
for some
k\in\mathbb{R}
and
1\leq i\leq n-1
. Then, if
P
denotes a nonsingular matrix of order
n
with
1
’s on the diagonal entries,
-k
on the
(i+1,i)
entry, and
0
on the other entries, then
A^{\prime}=PAP^{T}
is
A
with all entries in the
(i+1)
st row and
(i+1)
st column replaced by
0
’s. Since
A^{\prime}=PAP^{T}
and
P
is nonsingular, Sylvester’s law of inertia says that
\Sign(A)=\Sign(A^{\prime})
. Therefore, we may remove
v_{i+1}
from the sequence
v
in this case by the same reason as above.

Thus, in order to find
\Sign(A)
, we may assume that
v_{i}
and
v_{i+1}
are linearly independent for any
0\leq i\leq n
and hence
\det A\not=0
by Lemma
2.1
.

We recall the following fact which will be used to prove Theorem
1.1
.

Theorem 2.2

(Sylvester)
.

Let
A
be a symmetric matrix of order
n
with real entries such that every principal upper left
k\times k
minor
\Delta_{k}
is nonzero. Then the number of negative eigenvalues of
A
is equal to the number of sign changes in the sequence

\Delta_{0}=1,\ \Delta_{1},\dots,\ \Delta_{n}=\det A.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

If we replace
v_{i}
by
-v_{i}
for some
1\leq i\leq n
, then the
i
-th column and
i
-th row in
A
are both multiplied by
-1
; so
\Sign(A)
remain unchanged by this replacement. On the other hand, the right hand side of the identity in the theorem also remain unchanged by this replacement. Indeed, one can observe that if
v_{i-1},v_{i},v_{i+1}
rotate counterclockwise (resp. clockwise), then
R(v)
decreases (resp. increases) by
1
and
S(v)
decreases (resp. increases) by
4
, and otherwise
R(v)
and
S(v)
remain unchanged; so
4R(v)-S(v)
remain unchanged in any case.
Therefore, we may assume that every
v_{i}
lies on the upper half plane of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Since
R(v)=0
in this case, what we have to prove is
\Sign(A)=-S(v)
when all
v_{i}
’s lie on the upper half plane of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

We may assume that
b_{k}>0
for any
1\leq k\leq n
by rotating
v_{k}
slightly when
b_{k}=0
. The matrix
A
slightly changes by this slight rotation and the eigenvalues of
A
slightly change. However, since
\det A\not=0
, the signs of the eigenvalues do not change and hence
\Sign(A)
remain unchanged.

It follows from Lemma
2.1
that

\Delta_{k}=a_{1}b_{k}\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}(a_{i+1}b_{i}-a_{i}b_{i+1})\not=0\quad\text{for $1\leq k\leq n$},

where we understand
\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}(a_{i+1}b_{i}-a_{i}b_{i+1})=1
when
k=1
. Then, for
0\leq k\leq n-1
we have

\frac{\Delta_{k+1}}{\Delta_{k}}=\frac{b_{k+1}}{b_{k}}(a_{k+1}b_{k}-a_{k}b_{k+1})=-\frac{b_{k+1}}{b_{k}}\det(v_{k},v_{k+1}).

Since
b_{k+1}/b_{k}>0
, the number of sign changes in the sequence

\Delta_{0}=1,\ \Delta_{1},\dots,\ \Delta_{n}=\det A

is the number of
k
’s for
0\leq k\leq n-1
with
\det(v_{k},v_{k+1})>0
. Therefore, if we denote the number of negative (resp. positive) eigenvalues of
A
by
n_{-}
(resp.
n_{+}
), then

n_{-}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq k\leq n-1\\
\det(v_{k},v_{k+1})>0\end{subarray}}{\rm sgn}(\det(v_{k},v_{k+1}))

by Theorem
2.2
, and

n_{+}=-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq k\leq n-1\\
\det(v_{k},v_{k+1})<0\end{subarray}}{\rm sgn}(\det(v_{k},v_{k+1})).

Hence

(2.2)

\Sign(A)=n_{+}-n_{-}=-\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}{\rm sgn}(\det(v_{k},v_{k+1})).

Since
b_{n}>0
and
v_{n+1}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}
, we have
\det(v_{n},v_{n+1})<0
and hence
{\rm sgn}(\det(v_{n},v_{n+1}))=-1
. This together with (
2.2
) shows
\Sign(A)=-S(v)
, proving the theorem.
∎

3.
The inverse of the matrix
A

We assume that our matrix
A
in (
1.2
) is nonsingular.

Proposition 3.1
.

We set

\begin{split}m_{i}&=\det(v_{i},v_{i+1})\quad\text{for\ $0\leq i\leq n$},\\
s_{i}&=-\det(v_{i-1},v_{i+1})/m_{i-1}m_{i}\quad\text{for\ $1\leq i\leq n$}.\end{split}

Then the inverse of
A
is a tridiagonal symmetric matrix given by

(3.1)

{\small\begin{pmatrix}s_{1}&m_{1}^{-1}&0&0&\dots&0\\
m_{1}^{-1}&s_{2}&m_{2}^{-1}&0&\dots&0\\
0&m_{2}^{-1}&s_{3}&m_{3}^{-1}&\dots&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\dots&0&m_{n-2}^{-1}&s_{n-1}&m_{n-1}^{-1}\\
0&\dots&0&0&m_{n-1}^{-1}&s_{n}\end{pmatrix}}

Proof.

By definition

(3.2)

\begin{split}m_{i}&=a_{i}b_{i+1}-a_{i+1}b_{i},\\
s_{i}&=-(a_{i-1}b_{i+1}-a_{i+1}b_{i-1})/(a_{i-1}b_{i}-a_{i}b_{i-1})(a_{i}b_{i+1}-a_{i+1}b_{i}).\end{split}

Let
B
be the matrix in (
3.1
). We shall prove that the product
AB
is the identity matrix by direct computation. Since
AB
is a symmetric matrix, it suffices to check the
(i,j)
entry
(AB)_{i,j}
of
AB
for
i\leq j
.

Case 1. The case where
i=j
. In this case

(3.3)

(AB)_{i,i}=a_{i-1}b_{i}m_{i-1}^{-1}+a_{i}b_{i}s_{i}+a_{i}b_{i+1}m_{i}^{-1}

where we note that
a_{0}=0
when
i=1
and
b_{n+1}=0
when
i=n
.
Using (
3.2
), the right hand side of (
3.3
) is equal to

\frac{a_{i-1}b_{i}}{a_{i-1}b_{i}-a_{i}b_{i-1}}-\frac{a_{i}b_{i}(a_{i-1}b_{i+1}-a_{i+1}b_{i-1})}{(a_{i-1}b_{i}-a_{i}b_{i-1})(a_{i}b_{i+1}-a_{i+1}b_{i})}+\frac{a_{i}b_{i+1}}{a_{i}b_{i+1}-a_{i+1}b_{i}}

and a simple calculation shows that this is equal to
1
.

Case 2. The case where
i<j
. In this case

(3.4)

(AB)_{i,j}=a_{i}b_{j-1}m_{j-1}^{-1}+a_{i}b_{j}s_{j}+a_{i}b_{j+1}m_{j}^{-1}

where we note that
b_{n+1}=0
when
j=n
.
Using (
3.2
), the right hand side of (
3.4
) is equal to

\frac{a_{i}b_{j-1}}{a_{j-1}b_{j}-a_{j}b_{j-1}}-\frac{a_{i}b_{j}(a_{j-1}b_{j+1}-a_{j+1}b_{j-1})}{(a_{j-1}b_{j}-a_{j}b_{j-1})(a_{j}b_{j+1}-a_{j+1}b_{j})}+\frac{a_{i}b_{j+1}}{a_{j}b_{j+1}-a_{j+1}b_{j}}

and a simple calculation shows that this vanishes.
∎

Example 3.2
.

We take
n=4
and

v_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
4\end{pmatrix},\ v_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}2\\
3\end{pmatrix},\ v_{3}=\begin{pmatrix}3\\
2\end{pmatrix},\ v_{4}=\begin{pmatrix}4\\
1\end{pmatrix}

Then the matrix
A
in (
1.2
) associated to this sequence of vectors and its inverse

are as follows:

A=\begin{pmatrix}4&3&2&1\\
3&6&4&2\\
2&4&6&3\\
1&2&3&4\end{pmatrix}\qquad A^{-1}=\frac{1}{5}\begin{pmatrix}2&-1&0&0\\
-1&2&-1&0\\
0&-1&2&-1\\
0&0&-1&2\end{pmatrix}

In general, if we take
v_{i}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n+1}}\begin{pmatrix}i\\
n+1-i\end{pmatrix}
for

, then the sequence

in (
1.2
) associated to this sequence

is positive definite by Corollary

and

turns out to be the Cartan matrix of type

. (To the contrary, a formula to find the inverse of a Cartan matrix is given in
[

]
for any Lie type.)

We consider the configuration space

(3.5)

V=\left\{\left(v_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}\\
b_{1}\end{pmatrix},\dots,v_{n}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{n}\\
b_{n}\end{pmatrix}\right)\in(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{n}\ \middle|\ a_{1}b_{n}\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}(a_{i}b_{i+1}-a_{i+1}b_{i})\not=0\right\}

and the space
W
of nonsingular tridiagonal symmetric matrices of order
n
with nonzero entries in the superdiagonal, i.e.

(3.6)

W=\left\{B=\begin{pmatrix}p_{1}&q_{1}&0&0&\dots&0\\
q_{1}&p_{2}&q_{2}&0&\dots&0\\
0&q_{2}&p_{3}&q_{3}&\dots&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\dots&0&q_{n-2}&p_{n-1}&q_{n-1}\\
0&\dots&0&0&q_{n-1}&p_{n}\end{pmatrix}\ \middle|\ \begin{split}&\det B\not=0,\\
&q_{i}\not=0\ \text{$(1\leq\forall i\leq n-1)$}\end{split}\right\}

The map sending
(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})\in V
to
A^{-1}
defines a map

\Psi\colon V\to W

by Proposition
3.1
. The space
V
admits an action of
\mathbb{R}^{*}
sending
\begin{pmatrix}a_{i}\\
b_{i}\end{pmatrix}
to
\begin{pmatrix}ra_{i}\\
r^{-1}b_{i}\end{pmatrix}

(r\in\mathbb{R}^{*})
simultaneously for every
i
. The matrix
A
in (
1.2
) associated to an element of
V
remains changed under this action, so the map
\Psi
induces a map

\widehat{\Psi}\colon V/\mathbb{R}^{*}\to W.

The following proposition gives a characterization of nonsingular symmetric matrices of the form (
1.2
) or a characterization of nonsingular tridiagonal symmetric matrices with nonzero entries in the superdiagonal.

Proposition 3.3
.

The map
\widehat{\Psi}
is bijective.

Proof.

(Injectivity). We may assume
