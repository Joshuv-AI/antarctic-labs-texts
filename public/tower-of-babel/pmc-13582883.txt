A deep learning system for bacterial identification and resistance prediction from MALDI-TOF data
Authors: Chih-Hung Wang, Shu-Yu Tsao, Yueh-Chen Hsieh, Po-Chun Liao, Yu-Chun Pan, Matthew Huei-Ming Ma, Wan-Huei Sheng, Lian-Yu Chen, Chien-Chang Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight (MALDI-TOF) mass spectrometry (MS) data contain underutilized information beyond species identification. We developed a deep learning system—termed ANTIBIOTIC—that identifies bacterial species, predicts antimicrobial resistance (AMR), and recommends antibiotics directly from raw MALDI-TOF MS data. Using a public dataset (DRIAMS, 2015–2018) and 89,026 MS records from a local hospital dataset (NTUHYL, 2017–2023), we built 26 LightGBM models for identifying the most prevalent bacterial species and 248 Temporal Convolutional Network models for predicting AMR across bacteria–antibiotic combinations. For bacterial identification, models achieved a median area under the curve (AUC) of 0.99 internally and 0.96 on temporally split external data. For AMR prediction, median AUC was 0.94 internally but declined to 0.55 on temporal external data, improving to 0.61 after fine-tuning with recent data. Integrating these models with a large language model enabled an antibiotic recommendation chatbot. These findings demonstrate that deep learning can leverage MALDI-TOF MS data for large-scale bacterial identification and AMR prediction, though AMR models require periodic updating to maintain performance.

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) has emerged as a critical global health challenge1,2. The rising prevalence of AMR has significantly compromised the effectiveness of empirical treatment strategies. Conventional methods for determining AMR typically require several hours to several days to complete3. This delay can hinder the timely initiation of appropriate antibiotic therapy, potentially leading to suboptimal clinical outcomes.

Matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight (MALDI-TOF) mass spectrometry (MS) has revolutionized clinical microbiology by enabling rapid and accurate identification of microorganisms based on their unique spectral profiles4–6. Traditionally, MALDI-TOF MS data analysis has relied on a limited set of features, such as peak height and area under the curve, to support species-level identification. However, there is growing recognition that these spectra also contain underutilized information that may be valuable for AMR profiling7.

Given the high-dimensional and information-rich nature of MALDI-TOF MS data, machine learning (ML) algorithms have increasingly been applied to improve both microbial identification and AMR prediction7. Early approaches primarily employed classical ML techniques, often requiring manual feature selection or the use of targeted protein markers associated with known resistance genes. In contrast, recent advances in deep learning (DL) have enabled the use of raw MS data with minimal preprocessing, allowing models to capture more complex spectral patterns and achieve superior classification performance8.

While these developments are promising, most prior studies have focused on predicting AMR for individual bacteria–antibiotic pairs, with only a limited number extending their scope to include multiple species and antibiotics9. In the present study, we aimed to develop a comprehensive DL–based system capable of identifying bacterial species, predicting AMR profiles, and recommending appropriate antibiotic therapies using MALDI-TOF MS data from a broad range of commonly encountered bacterial species.

Results

Data collected at NTUHYL

A total of 109,803 MALDI-TOF MS records were collected at NTUHYL between 2017 and 2023, encompassing 70 distinct microbial species. For the purposes of this study, the 26 most prevalent bacterial species—accounting for individual prevalences ranging from 0.4 to 15.5%—were selected for model development. These species collectively accounted for 89,026 MS records, representing approximately 80% of the entire dataset. Performance of the AMR-prediction models

As shown in Supplementary Data 5 and 6, a total of 248 AMR prediction models were developed. In the NTUHYL-1722 internal testing dataset, these models demonstrated excellent discriminative performance, with a median AUC of 0.94 (IQR: 0.87–0.99).

To evaluate external generalizability, we assessed 112 bacteria–antibiotic pairs that were also available in the NTUHYL-23 external testing dataset. In contrast to the internal testing results, model performance declined markedly, with a median AUC of 0.55 (IQR: 0.51–0.62).

As shown in Fig. 4, most AUC differences between the internal and external testing datasets exceeded 0.05 and were statistically significant. Sensitivity analysis

The results of the benchmarking sensitivity analysis are presented in Supplementary Fig. 2. Temporal Convolutional Network (TCN)-based models consistently achieved equal or higher AUC values compared to the respective baselines across all three species, with the most pronounced improvements observed for the Escherichia coli–Piperacillin-Tazobactam pair (AUC: 0.55 vs. 0.74), the Staphylococcus aureus–Oxacillin pair (AUC: 0.83 vs. 0.94), and the Klebsiella pneumoniae–Ciprofloxacin pair (AUC: 0.68 vs. 0.88).

The results of the sequential fine-tuning analysis are presented in Supplementary Figs. 3 and 4. For Escherichia coli, the learning curves exhibited heterogeneous patterns: some pairs showed increasing AUC with more fine-tuning data, while others demonstrated performance degradation at larger sample sizes. In contrast, Klebsiella pneumoniae, which had substantially larger sample availability, exhibited a predominantly upward trend across the majority of pairs. These findings suggest that the optimal fine-tuning strategy varies by bacteria–antibiotic pair and that sufficient data volume is a key determinant of successful model updating.

The results of the geographic external testing using the MARISMa dataset are presented in Supplementary Data 8. When our pre-trained models were applied directly to the MARISMa dataset without local fine-tuning, the 93 evaluated bacteria–antibiotic pairs yielded a median AUC of 0.51 (IQR: 0.45–0.58). This performance is comparable to the median AUC of 0.55 observed in the NTUHYL-23 external testing dataset, confirming that AMR prediction models do not generalize well when applied directly to data from a geographically distinct institution without local adaptation.

Antibiotics-Recommending ANTIBIOTIC (AI-powered Network for bacTerial Identification, antimicroBial resIstance prediction, and Optimal antibiotic Therapeutics In Care) System: Integration of the DL Models and the Chatbot.

As demonstrated in Supplementary Movie 1, the bacteria-identification and AMR-prediction models were integrated with a chatbot interface to form the ANTIBIOTIC system. This system enables users to upload raw MALDI-TOF MS data in FID (free induction decay) file format, which is processed by the DL models to predict both the bacterial species and its AMR profile. Based on these predictions, the chatbot recommends optimal antibiotic therapy and further provides three clinical functions: (1) a bacterial summary with resistance pattern analysis, (2) an escalation/de-escalation strategy based on antimicrobial stewardship principles, and (3) renal function-based dosage adjustment guidance.

Discussion

The ANTIBIOTIC system integrates 26 LightGBM-based models for bacterial species identification and 248 TCN-based models for AMR prediction. The species identification models demonstrated consistently excellent performance in both internal and external testing datasets, indicating strong generalizability. In contrast, while the AMR prediction models also showed high performance in the internal testing dataset, their predictive accuracy declined substantially in the temporally split external dataset, highlighting the challenge of maintaining model performance over time.

For bacterial identification, in a systematic review conducted up to January 2020, Weis et al.7 identified 27 studies that employed ML techniques to classify microbial species based on MALDI-TOF MS data. Among these, Wang et al.10 reported the largest dataset, comprising 787 Group B Streptococcus isolates. Using classical ML algorithms, their models achieved an accuracy range of 54.9–87.1% for bacterial identification. Similar to Wang et al.10, most previous studies relied on classical ML methods7, largely due to the use of manufacturer-provided software that supported only a limited range of preprogrammed algorithms. This restricted algorithmic flexibility, coupled with limited accessibility to the developed models, likely hindered external validation and broader adoption in clinical settings.

In contrast to the relatively small datasets used in these earlier studies, we compiled all available MALDI-TOF MS records during the study period, resulting in a substantially larger dataset. This larger sample size reduces the risk of overfitting—a common limitation in prior research—and improves model generalizability. Moreover, with the goal of building a publicly accessible system, we had the flexibility to implement more advanced algorithms tailored to the complexity of our dataset.

We selected LightGBM, a gradient-boosting framework based on tree-based learning, due to its strong performance in classification tasks, high computational efficiency, and scalability in handling large, high-dimensional datasets. The consistent performance of our LightGBM-based models in both the internal and external testing datasets was anticipated, given the inherent differences in spectral patterns across microbial genera and species—differences that form the basis for existing commercial MS fingerprinting platforms. The small performance drop from median AUC of 0.99 to 0.96 may be caused by the fundamental difference between random splitting (interpolation) used in internal testing and the strict temporal splitting (extrapolation) of the external dataset. This temporal gap introduces inevitable technical drifts—such as instrument aging, laser attenuation, or minor variations in reagent batches—that generate spectral noise not present during training. Despite these factors, the sustained high accuracy demonstrates the robustness of the LightGBM algorithm in capturing invariant species-specific spectral features rather than overfitting to transient technical noise.

However, it is crucial to interpret these high performance metrics with caution regarding our problem formulation. Unlike realistic open-set diagnostic scenarios where hundreds of rare or closely related taxa must be distinguished simultaneously, our study was designed as a “proof-of-concept” formulated as a series of 26 binary one-vs-rest classification tasks. This design deliberately prioritized the “vital few”—the 26 most prevalent species accounting for approximately 80% of the clinical isolates in our hospital—rather than attempting comprehensive taxonomic coverage. While this formulation simplifies the identification task and contributes to the high AUC values by distinguishing common species from a general background, it represents a strategic trade-off to maximize performance on the pathogens driving the majority of empirical antibiotic decisions.

Consequently, unlike commercial solutions that strive for exhaustive taxonomic breadth, our approach is designed to deliver a focused, open-end-to-end service for rapid clinical triage. By concentrating on epidemiologically dominant species, we provide a streamlined system that enables the accurate identification of the most clinically relevant pathogens directly from raw MS data, independent of proprietary fingerprint libraries. This targeted strategy prioritizes immediate clinical relevance over taxonomic completeness, thereby enhancing the system’s practical utility and feasibility for real-world deployment.

Furthermore, to rigorously validate this utility, it is important to clarify the ground truth used in our study. Our training labels were derived from the final Laboratory Information System reports, which represent the verified standard of care incorporating validation by certified medical technologists and supplementary testing (e.g., biochemical tests) where necessary, rather than relying solely on the raw output of commercial software. Thus, our high predictive performance represents a strong concordance with the human-validated clinical diagnosis. Functionally, this bacteria-identification module serves a critical role as the “router” for our fully autonomous pipeline. Traditionally, MALDI-TOF identification relies on Peptide Mass Fingerprinting (PMF) database matching. Our study demonstrates that machine learning approaches serve as a viable, rapid alternative, particularly in settings where comprehensive reference databases may not be readily available yet. By integrating these open-source bacteria-identification models, the ANTIBIOTIC system can process raw spectral data directly to trigger species-specific AMR predictions, creating a seamless end-to-end workflow that operates independently of proprietary commercial software interfaces. Beyond its primary role as a pipeline router, this identification module may also serve additional functions in future implementations, including quality control for flagging low-confidence or atypical spectra, and research-oriented benchmarking of identification algorithms across institutions. However, we acknowledge that discordant cases between our AI predictions and the clinical standard were not systematically investigated using molecular methods, which limits the assessment of added diagnostic value and warrants future investigation.

For AMR prediction, in a systematic review conducted up to May 2024, López-Cortés et al.9 identified 40 studies applying ML techniques to predict AMR using MALDI-TOF MS data. Among these studies, the DRIAMS dataset8 stands out as the most extensive resource. Using a subset of this dataset (DRIAMS-A, 145,351 records), Weis et al.8 developed and evaluated three AMR prediction models on a randomly split testing dataset. Their LightGBM-based model achieved an AUC of 0.80 for S. aureus against oxacillin (n = 3790) and 0.74 for E. coli against ceftriaxone (n = 4961). For K. pneumoniae against ceftriaxone (n = 2860), the best-performing model—based on a multi-layer perceptron (MLP)—also achieved an AUC of 0.74.

In contrast to the approach taken by Weis et al.8, which involved multiple algorithms, we implemented a unified framework based solely on TCNs across all AMR prediction tasks. In our internal randomly split testing dataset, the TCN-based models achieved favorable predictive performance: AUCs of 0.972 for S. aureus against oxacillin (n = 9842), 0.840 for E. coli against cefotaxime (n = 9403), and 0.880 for K. pneumoniae against cefotaxime (n = 16,911). These results highlight the favorable performance of TCN for AMR prediction, particularly for S. aureus, and represent a meaningful improvement over previously reported models8,9.

However, the key factor likely influencing model performance is the higher prevalence of AMR in our dataset. In the DRIAMS dataset, the AMR rates were relatively low: 10.0% for S. aureus against oxacillin, 10.0% for E. coli against ceftriaxone, and 8.2% for K. pneumoniae against ceftriaxone. By contrast, our dataset exhibited markedly higher AMR prevalence for the same species–antibiotic pairs: 42.97%, 43.18%, and 40.83%, respectively (Supplementary Data 1). A dataset is typically considered imbalanced when the ratio between majority and minority classes exceeds 1.511. In highly imbalanced datasets, such as DRIAMS, ML algorithms often struggle to learn from the underrepresented class, which in this case corresponds to AMR-positive isolates. This imbalance may have contributed to the lower predictive performance observed in previous models12.

To isolate the impact of model architecture from these dataset-specific confounding factors, we conducted a benchmarking sensitivity analysis (Supplementary Fig. 2). When evaluated head-to-head on the identical DRIAMS dataset8, the TCN-based models consistently outperformed the LightGBM and MLP baselines. This direct comparison suggests that the favorable performance of the ANTIBIOTIC system is not solely attributable to higher AMR prevalence in the NTUHYL dataset, but also stems from the advantageous characteristics of the TCN architecture in capturing sequential dependencies within mass spectra.

The pronounced decline in performance of AMR prediction models observed in the external testing dataset suggests underlying temporal variability in MALDI-TOF MS profiles. This phenomenon is likely driven by a complex interplay of factors: biological evolution, technical instrument variations, and temporal shifts in ground truth labels. While biological evolution and shifts in local microbial populations contribute to phenotypic changes, and updates in clinical breakpoints (such as Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute [CLSI] standards) introduce label inconsistencies for specific pathogen-drug combinations, these factors alone are insufficient to fully account for the observed systemic performance loss. Crucially, non-biological technical artifacts associated with the physical instrument and laboratory workflow likely play an equally, if not more, substantial role. MALDI-TOF instruments are sensitive to physical drifts over time, including laser source attenuation, variations in detector sensitivity, fluctuations in instrument calibration, and lot-to-lot variability in matrix reagents or culture media. These factors collectively introduce “spectral drift” or “batch effects” that create a domain gap between the historical training distribution (2017–2022) and the external testing data (2023).

Weis et al.8 reported a similar phenomenon, observing a remarkable decline in AMR prediction performance on temporally split datasets compared to randomly split datasets. Specifically, the AUC for Klebsiella pneumoniae against ceftriaxone decreased from 0.74 to 0.60. Notably, the AUC for the Klebsiella pneumoniae–ceftriaxone model increased from 0.45 to 0.60 when the training data included MALDI-TOF MS records from the eight months immediately preceding the test period, indicating this pair’s heightened vulnerability to temporal shifts. This recovery suggests that model updating addresses not only the biological emergence of new resistance clones but also performs necessary “domain adaptation” to correct for accumulated technical drifts in the laboratory workflow.

Similarly, as shown in Fig. 4, most models in our study demonstrated superior performance in the NTUHYL-1722 internal testing dataset (random split) compared to the NTUHYL-23 external testing dataset (temporal split). The performance discrepancies, which varied across bacteria–antibiotic combinations, suggest that the temporal generalizability of AMR prediction models is inconsistent. This inconsistency likely reflects a complex interaction between the biological stability of the pathogen (e.g., how fast its resistance mechanism evolves) and the model’s sensitivity to technical spectral noise. These observations underscore the importance of periodic model updating to maintain clinical utility in real-world settings.

To address this issue, we utilized models trained on MALDI-TOF MS data collected between 2017 and 2022 (i.e., the NTUHYL-1722 development dataset) as source models, which captured generalizable patterns for predicting AMR profiles across a large dataset. These pretrained models formed the basis for transfer learning, enabling efficient adaptation to newer data through fine-tuning. This approach is particularly advantageous when only a limited amount of recent data is available. As shown in Fig. 5, fine-tuning the source models with MS data from the first eight months of 2023 led to improved predictive performance in most AMR models. However, the extent of improvement varied, and not all models exhibited performance gains, highlighting variability across different bacteria–antibiotic combinations.

This variability may be explained by two key factors. First, the volume of data available for fine-tuning differed substantially across bacteria–antibiotic pairs (Supplementary Data 7), as clinical policies restrict MALDI-TOF analysis to clinically significant isolates. For more common combinations, eight months of data may be sufficient, while rarer pairs may require longer time frames to accumulate enough representative cases for effective fine-tuning. The eight-month window, as recommended by Weis et al.8, was applied empirically in our study, but our findings suggest that updating intervals should be tailored to data availability for each pair.

Second, the pace at which resistance evolves varies among bacterial species and specific antibiotic exposures. Some bacteria–antibiotic combinations may experience more rapid shifts in resistance mechanisms, necessitating more frequent model updates. Therefore, a one-size-fits-all approach to model updating may not be feasible. Instead, update schedules should be individualized—not only by bacterial species, but also by the specific bacteria–antibiotic pair—to ensure sustained model accuracy over time. To further investigate this, we conducted a sequential fine-tuning analysis on Escherichia coli (Supplementary Fig 3) and Klebsiella pneumoniae (Supplementary Fig. 4). The learning curves confirmed that the optimal fine-tuning data volume varies by bacteria–antibiotic pair. Notably, Klebsiella pneumoniae, which had substantially larger sample availability, exhibited predominantly upward performance trends with increasing data, whereas Escherichia coli showed more heterogeneous patterns. These findings suggest that sufficient data volume is a key determinant of successful model updating, and that multi-center federated learning may be necessary to provide adequate data for optimizing fine-tuning strategies across diverse bacteria–antibiotic pairs.

Beyond prediction, the ANTIBIOTIC system also provides antibiotic recommendations. The Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) techniques13 adopted by the ANTIBIOTIC system’s chatbot offer a key advantage: it enables real-time integration of evolving scientific and clinical guidelines, such as those issued by the Infectious Diseases Society of America. Its modular architecture supports the continuous updating of external knowledge sources, thereby improving the system’s adaptability to emerging evidence and evolving clinical standards14. To maximize the clinical relevance of antibiotic recommendations, the ANTIBIOTIC system integrates two key design features. First, the chatbot is directly coupled with the system’s predictive outputs: the specific AMR phenotype predicted by the TCN models is dynamically injected into the LLM’s context window, enabling the RAG system to retrieve treatment guidelines tailored to the predicted resistance profile of each isolate. Through this integration, the chatbot provides three clinical functions: a bacterial summary with resistance pattern analysis, an escalation/de-escalation strategy based on antimicrobial stewardship principles, and renal function-based dosage adjustment guidance. Second, in addition to Medscape®, the system incorporates the Sanford Guide to Antimicrobial Therapy (accessed through our hospital’s institutional license) as a complementary knowledge source. This addition is critical because the Sanford Guide provides specific antibiotic recommendations in the context of known resistance patterns, thereby enabling the chatbot to generate resistance-informed treatment suggestions rather than relying solely on species-level guidance. The integrated system is demonstrated in Supplementary Movie 1. Importantly, the system architecture is designed to be license-agnostic: the vector database module is interchangeable, allowing other institutions to integrate their own authorized knowledge sources (e.g., UpToDate or localized antibiotic stewardship guidelines). As this implementation was developed for academic research purposes to explore the feasibility of an integrated end-to-end workflow, clinical deployment will require prior clarification of copyright and licensing obligations associated with the use of proprietary guideline databases within automated systems.

Several future directions merit consideration. First, we envision the integration of our MALDI-TOF MS-based AI system with genomics-based surveillance tools (e.g., ResFinder) to create a complementary, multi-tiered diagnostic workflow. While Whole Genome Sequencing (WGS) provides the gold standard for mechanistic resolution, its implementation in established clinical laboratories is often hindered by high costs, complex regulatory accreditation requirements, and turnaround times typically exceeding 24–72 h15. In contrast, our ANTIBIOTIC system leverages widely adopted, accredited MALDI-TOF infrastructure to provide actionable AMR predictions within minutes of culture positivity. Therefore, the future of precision microbiology lies not in choosing one over the other, but in a dual-track approach: utilizing MALDI-TOF AI as a “frontline” rapid screening tool to guide immediate empirical therapy, while reserving WGS for “second-line” confirmation and epidemiological surveillance. This workflow maximizes the value of existing clinical data while bridging the critical time gap in sepsis management.

Second, regarding clinical positioning, we emphasize that the ANTIBIOTIC system is designed to augment, not replace, traditional phenotypic antimicrobial susceptibility testing. Its primary utility lies in the critical 24–72 h diagnostic gap before phenotypic results are finalized—a period where clinicians currently rely on broad-spectrum empirical therapy or static, often outdated, hospital-wide antibiograms. We propose utilizing this system as a dynamic, “Personalized Antibiogram”. Unlike a static antibiogram that applies a population-level average to every patient, our system generates a probability based on the specific spectral fingerprint of the individual isolate. Even with imperfect predictive accuracy, this approach aims to provide a more precise risk assessment than the current standard of empirical guessing, enabling clinicians to make more informed decisions during the acute phase of infection.

Third, the landscape of open-access resources for MALDI-TOF MS research has expanded significantly in recent years. In addition to the pioneering DRIAMS dataset8, the community has benefited from the recent release of the Robert Koch Institute (RKI) database16 and the MARISMa dataset17, which have greatly advanced the field of microbial identification. Our study contributes to this growing ecosystem by introducing the NTUHYL dataset, which enriches the current data landscape with its unique focus on raw FID spectra paired with comprehensive, CLSI-standardized antimicrobial susceptibility profiles across a broad spectrum of clinical pathogens. By providing high-prevalence AMR data that complements the identification-focused or single-species datasets currently available, we aim to create a more diverse and robust foundation for the global research community. The collective availability of these distinct yet complementary repositories will be pivotal in facilitating transfer learning and accelerating the development of generalizable AI systems for clinical deployment.

Fourth, regarding the scalability and regulatory oversight of deploying 248 distinct AMR models, we propose a “Funnel Strategy” for staged implementation. We acknowledge that maintaining such a vast number of models presents a fundamental challenge for continuous monitoring and regulation. Therefore, rather than simultaneous deployment, we envision a phased screening pipeline. Models will first undergo technical screening (Phase 1), followed by multi-center validation via Federated Learning to ensure external generalizability (Phase 2). Subsequently, a retrospective “Shadow Mode” simulation will filter for clinical utility—identifying models that actually possess the potential to alter prescribing decisions (Phase 3). Ultimately, only the high-impact “Core Models” will be prioritized for prospective clinical trials and regulatory submission. This staged approach aligns with emerging regulatory frameworks, prioritizing patient safety while establishing a feasible pathway for progressively realizing the full “Personalized Antibiogram.”

Our study has several limitations. First, regarding geographical generalizability (spatial limitation): While our AMR prediction models leveraged large datasets from Switzerland (DRIAMS) and Taiwan (NTUHYL), the resulting models are inherently “site-specific.” As demonstrated in previous research18, the AMR-prediction performance of artificial intelligence models based on MALDI-TOF MS is heavily influenced by local molecular epidemiology and genotypic diversity. Uncommon sequence types or strains absent from the training distribution—such as those found in highly cosmopolitan regions—may lead to reduced predictive accuracy. Therefore, the pre-trained weights provided in this study may not be directly transferable (“zero-shot”) to other healthcare systems without local fine-tuning. This was empirically confirmed by our geographic external testing using the MARISMa dataset (Supplementary Data 8), in which direct application of our models to data from Spain yielded a median AUC of 0.51 (IQR: 0.45–0.58)—comparable to the temporal degradation observed in the NTUHYL-23 dataset—despite overlapping collection periods. This result underscores that cross-site performance loss is driven primarily by geographic and methodological factors (e.g., differences in local epidemiology, instrument variability, and AST standards) rather than temporal drift alone. We emphasize that the generalizability of this work lies in its methodological framework (the open-source pipeline), which allows other institutions to adapt the system to their specific bacterial populations, rather than in a universal, static model.

Second, regarding temporal stability (temporal limitation), bacterial resistance mechanisms evolve over time. Although we demonstrated that fine-tuning with temporally recent data can restore performance, our sequential fine-tuning analysis (Supplementary Figs. 3, 4) revealed that the optimal data volume and updating strategy vary by bacteria–antibiotic pair and are heavily dependent on sample availability. Our current single-center dataset imposes a fundamental trade-off: as more data are allocated to fine-tuning, less remains for reliable evaluation. While a periodic update strategy aligns with current regulatory frameworks (e.g., U.S. Food and Drug Administration [FDA] Predetermined Change Control Plan [PCCP]), achieving clinically meaningful and sustained performance improvements will likely require multi-center federated learning to provide sufficient data volume for both model updating and rigorous evaluation.

Third, regarding clinical utility and performance metrics: The overall median AUC of 0.61 for AMR prediction indicates that the system cannot replace traditional phenotypic antimicrobial susceptibility testing. The current tool is positioned strictly as a decision support mechanism to bridge the 24–72 h diagnostic gap between species identification and final antimicrobial susceptibility testing results. Clinical application requires careful “human-in-the-loop” integration, potentially utilizing confidence scores to filter out low-certainty predictions to ensure patient safety. Future validation should include retrospective simulation studies comparing the appropriateness of empirical prescriptions made with and without the AI’s assistance, to determine whether the system’s probabilistic output translates into tangible clinical benefit.

Fourth, regarding algorithmic design, to achieve a scalable and deployable system, we employed a unified TCN architecture across all 248 bacteria-antibiotic tasks. We did not perform extensive architecture searching or hyperparameter optimization for each individual species-antibiotic pair. Consequently, the performance for specific rare or complex resistance mechanisms might be suboptimal compared to specialized, purpose-built models.

In conclusion, deep learning can effectively utilize MALDI-TOF MS data for large-scale bacterial identification and AMR prediction. While the bacterial identification models demonstrated favorable external generalizability, the AMR prediction models require periodic updates to maintain predictive performance over time. Making such datasets publicly available is essential for empowering local laboratories to develop and validate their own predictive models for these critical tasks.

Methods

This study was conducted in compliance with the amended Declaration of Helsinki. Approval was obtained from the Research Ethics Committee of National Taiwan University Hospital (202210018 W), which granted a waiver of informed consent due to the use of de-identified data. The results are presented in accordance with the TRIPOD + AI guidelines19.

Study design and setting

This retrospective study utilized MALDI-TOF MS data from two sources: the publicly available Database of Resistance against Antimicrobials with MALDI-TOF Mass Spectrometry (DRIAMS)8, and data from the National Taiwan University Hospital–Yulin Branch (NTUHYL).

These datasets were first used to develop DL models for two primary tasks: bacterial species identification and AMR prediction. Following model development, a chatbot was implemented to recommend appropriate antibiotics based on the predicted bacterial species.

The resulting system—termed ANTIBIOTIC—integrates both the DL models and the chatbot interface. It enables users to upload raw MALDI-TOF MS data, identify bacterial species, predict AMR profiles, and receive tailored antibiotic recommendations in a streamlined and automated manner.

Data downloaded from DRIAMS dataset and data collected at NTUHYL

The DRIAMS dataset comprised bacterial and fungal MALDI-TOF MS records from 303,195 microbial isolates analyzed by four clinical microbiology laboratories in Switzerland using the Microflex Biotyper System (Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany)8 between 2015 and 2018. Preprocessed MALDI-TOF MS records for each isolate were made available for download in text file format, accompanied by labels for bacterial species and AMR profiles.

The NTUHYL is a 943-bed regional referral hospital located in central Taiwan. The NTUHYL dataset comprises bacterial and fungal isolates systematically collected and analyzed using the MALDI Biotyper® sirius one System (Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany) between January 2017 and December 2023. Clinical specimens were obtained from a variety of sources.

MALDI-TOF MS data preprocessing

For the DRIAMS dataset, the preprocessing pipeline included multiple steps to standardize the data: variance stabilization of the mass-to-charge (m/z) ratios, smoothing of intensity values, baseline correction, and intensity calibration. The MS range was restricted to 2000–20,000 Da and subsequently binned into intervals of 3 Da. This preprocessing approach resulted in feature vectors with a fixed dimensionality of 6000 features, corresponding to the number of bins along the partitioned m/z axis.

For the NTUHYL dataset, raw MALDI-TOF MS data were exported in FID file format from the instrument. The m/z ratios and corresponding intensity values were extracted using the Python nmrglue package. To ensure consistency with the DRIAMS dataset, the extracted spectra were preprocessed by binning the data into 3 Da intervals, producing standardized feature vectors with 6000 attributes corresponding to the m/z range of 2000 to 20,000 Da. Within each bin, the maximum peak intensity was used as the representative value, thereby consolidating the spectral information into a fixed-length vector suitable for downstream analysis. Throughout the study period, bacterial identification was performed using the MBT Compass Library. The MBT 6903 MSP Library was used from January 2017 through October 2022, and was subsequently updated to the MBT 10833 MSP Library from November 2022 through December 2023.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

In the DRIAMS dataset, antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) was performed using microdilution assays, minimum inhibitory concentration stripe tests, or disc diffusion tests. The AST results were interpreted in accordance with the guidelines of the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) and the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI). At NTUHYL, AMR was determined using the Bauer-Kirby disk diffusion test and reported according to CLSI standards.

Notably, regarding temporal consistency, our laboratory adopted the CLSI M100 32nd Edition performance standards on July 1, 2022. This update introduced stricter susceptibility breakpoints for specific pathogen-drug combinations compared to previous versions. Specifically, for Enterobacterales, the susceptibility zone diameter breakpoints were increased (tightened) for ciprofloxacin (from ≥21 mm to ≥26 mm) and piperacillin-tazobactam (from ≥21 mm to ≥25 mm). Similarly, for Pseudomonas aeruginosa, the susceptibility thresholds were raised for ciprofloxacin (from ≥21 mm to ≥25 mm) and levofloxacin (from ≥17 mm to ≥22 mm). Consequently, certain isolates that would have been classified as “susceptible” under prior standards were reclassified as “intermediate” or “resistant” in the dataset collected after July 2022.

Dataset utilization and labeling for model development

For the bacteria-identification task, only the NTUHYL dataset was employed. The analysis focused on the 26 most prevalent bacterial species in the dataset. The ground truth labels for each MALDI-TOF MS record were retrieved from the hospital’s Laboratory Information System. These labels represent the final clinically reported identification, validated by certified medical technologists according to standard operating procedures, rather than relying solely on the raw output of the commercial MALDI-TOF software.

For the AMR-prediction task, both the DRIAMS and NTUHYL datasets were utilized. Isolates classified as resistant or intermediate to a specific antibiotic were labeled as AMR-positive, while those classified as susceptible were labeled as AMR-negative. This labeling process resulted in a total of 248 bacteria–antibiotic combinations for model development.

Training bacteria-identification models

As shown in Fig. 1, for training models to identify target bacterial species, only the MALDI-TOF MS data from the NTUHYL dataset were utilized. The MS data obtained between 2017 and 2022 were assigned to NTUHYL-1722 dataset for model development and internal testing while MS data obtained during 2023 were assigned to NTUHYL-23 dataset for external testing.

The NTUHYL-1722 dataset was randomly divided into development and internal testing datasets in a 7:3 ratio. The development dataset was further randomly split into five folds in an 80%:20% ratio. In this five-fold cross-validation process, one fold was reserved as the validation set, while the remaining four were used for training. This process was repeated across all five folds, and the best-performing model from these iterations was selected as the optimal model. During the dataset splitting process, similar proportions of target bacterial species in all subsets were maintained.

The LightGBM framework was adopted for the bacteria-identification models. LightGBM is a gradient-boosting framework that employs tree-based learning algorithms, specifically designed for efficiency and scalability when processing large-scale data. This framework was chosen due to its exceptional performance in classification tasks and its capacity to handle high-dimensional data effectively. The hyperparameters were configured based on standard LightGBM practices. Model training was performed using the gradient boosting decision tree (GBDT) framework, with the objective function set to binary classification (objective = ‘binary’) and binary_logloss specified as the evaluation metric. The model was trained for 500 boosting iterations (n_estimators = 500) with a learning rate of 0.05. Each decision tree was limited to a maximum of 31 leaves (num_leaves = 31), and a feature fraction of 0.9 was applied to randomly select a subset of features at each iteration, helping to mitigate overfitting. The trained model output the probability that a given MS record belonged to a specific bacterial species. A decision threshold of 0.5 was applied to these probabilities to assign final binary classification labels.

Training AMR-prediction models

As shown in Fig. 1, for training AMR-prediction models, both the DRIAMS and NTUHYL datasets were utilized. For NTUHYL dataset, the bacteria-antibiotic pairs obtained between 2017 and 2022 were assigned to NTUHYL-1722 dataset for model development and internal testing while the bacteria-antibiotic pairs obtained during 2023 were assigned to NTUHYL-23 dataset for external testing.

Furthermore, an auto-updating procedure was examined. The NTUHYL-23 dataset was assigned chronologically into the fine-tuning dataset and the temporal testing dataset. The fine-tuning dataset included the bacteria-antibiotic pairs obtained between January and August, 2023, while the temporal testing dataset included the bacteria-antibiotic pairs obtained between September and December, 2023.

The TCN framework was adopted for the AMR-prediction models. To ensure reproducibility and scalability, we implemented a consistent TCN architectural topology across all 248 bacteria–antibiotic prediction tasks. While the architecture remained fixed, the model weights were independently optimized for each specific resistance task through transfer learning.

The model processes the input mass spectra as a one-dimensional sequential signal with an input dimension of 6000 × 1, corresponding to the binned m/z intensity sequence. The backbone architecture comprises a TCN module serving as the primary feature extraction component, designed to capture local and multiscale dependencies in the input sequence. The TCN module was configured with the following hyperparameters: 64 convolutional filters per layer; a kernel size of 2, enabling fine-grained modeling of local spectral patterns while maintaining computational efficiency; dilation factors of (1, 2), allowing the receptive field to expand across multiple scales and facilitating the extraction of both short-range and longer-range dependencies within the signal; causal padding to preserve sequential ordering and prevent information leakage from future positions; layer normalization within the TCN block to stabilize training and improve convergence; spatial dropout with a rate of 0.2 after each dilated convolution to prevent overfitting; and Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) activation functions throughout the hidden layers.

Following the TCN feature extraction module, a multi-head self-attention mechanism was applied to the sequence-level TCN output to model long-range spectral interactions. The self-attention module was configured with 2 parallel attention heads, each with a key dimension of 128, enabling the model to learn rich and diverse spectral representations. The final output of the attention module is passed through a fully connected layer followed by a sigmoid activation function to produce a probability score for binary classification (Resistant vs. Susceptible).

To leverage broader spectral patterns, the AMR-prediction models were initialized with weights from pre-trained models developed on the public DRIAMS dataset and subsequently fine-tuned on the local NTUHYL-1722 dataset. In the NTUHYL-1722 dataset, bacteria–antibiotic pairs were randomly divided into development and internal testing datasets in a 7:3 ratio. The development dataset was further stratified into training and validation subsets in a 7:3 ratio. Stratified sampling was employed to maintain consistent proportions of resistant (positive) and susceptible (negative) cases across all partitions. The training process was supervised using Binary Cross-Entropy (BCE) loss with the Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) optimizer, configured with a batch size of 8 and a learning rate of 0.032. Training was conducted for a maximum of 50 epochs. To mitigate overfitting, an early stopping mechanism was implemented: training was halted if the validation loss did not improve for 10 consecutive epochs (patience), and the model weights yielding the best validation performance were restored for final evaluation.

Managing class imbalance

To address class imbalance during the training of bacteria-identification and AMR-prediction models, the Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE)20 was employed when the proportion of the minority class fell below 30%. Crucially, to prevent data leakage, SMOTE was applied exclusively to the training subset after the dataset splitting process. The validation and testing datasets were maintained in their original, unmodified distributions to accurately reflect real-world clinical prevalence.

We selected SMOTE over random undersampling to preserve valuable spectral information within the minority class, and over simple random oversampling to minimize the risk of the model memorizing specific noise patterns. As an additional safeguard against overfitting to synthetic samples, we implemented an early stopping mechanism that monitored model performance on the non-oversampled validation set, halting training if performance on the pristine data ceased to improve.

Programming the chatbot to recommend the appropriate antibiotics

To develop a chatbot specialized in antibiotic recommendation, Meta’s LLaMA 3 was adopted as the underlying framework for the large language model (LLM). A RAG technique14 was implemented to improve the accuracy and contextual relevance of responses by integrating external knowledge sources from Medscape® and the Sanford Guide to Antimicrobial Therapy (accessed through the hospital’s institutional license), thereby mitigating the risk of hallucinated content. The Sanford Guide was specifically incorporated because it provides antibiotic recommendations in the context of known resistance patterns, enabling the system to generate treatment suggestions that account for predicted AMR phenotypes. In addition to the predicted bacterial species, the specific AMR phenotype predicted by the TCN models was dynamically integrated into the chatbot’s context window, enabling antibiotic recommendations tailored to the predicted resistance profile of each isolate.

The chatbot development process began with offline data preprocessing. Source documents from both knowledge sources were segmented into smaller textual units using a character-level text splitter, configured with a chunk size of 1,000 characters and slight overlap between chunks to preserve contextual continuity. Each chunk was then transformed into dense semantic vectors through a HuggingFace embedding model. These embeddings were indexed and stored in a FAISS vector database to enable fast and scalable semantic search during inference.

In the retrieval phase, the system directly queries the prebuilt FAISS index. The chatbot receives two types of input from the upstream predictive models: the predicted bacterial species (from the LightGBM-based identification models) and the predicted AMR phenotype (from the TCN-based AMR prediction models). These predictions, together with relevant patient clinical parameters selected by the user via a dropdown menu, are used to automatically construct a structured prompt, which is then converted into an embedding vector. This query vector is used to retrieve the most semantically relevant chunks from the FAISS database. The retrieved content, together with the structured prompt, is then passed to the LLaMA 3 model, enabling the generation of context-aware, resistance-informed, and medically grounded antibiotic recommendations.

The chatbot provides three distinct clinical functions, each driven by a structured prompt template that integrates the predicted bacterial species, the predicted AMR phenotype (presented as an antimicrobial susceptibility table), patient clinical information, and the retrieved context from the RAG knowledge base. The prompt templates are as follows:

Function 1 (Bacterial Summary): This function generates a concise overview of the identified bacterium, including its general characteristics, clinical significance, and typical infections. The prompt additionally instructs the LLM to compare the predicted antimicrobial susceptibility results against guideline-recommended patterns and to flag any unusual resistance profiles. The prompt template is: “You are a clinical microbiology and infectious disease expert. Patient information: {patient_status}. Predicted antimicrobial susceptibility results: {amr_table}. Context information: {context}. Question: What are the general characteristics and clinical significance of this bacterium, and the typical infections it causes? Also, analyze whether the predicted antimicrobial susceptibility results match the recommended antibiotics and resistance patterns. Instructions: 1. Summarize general characteristics. 2. Describe clinical significance. 3. Describe infections caused. 4. Compare susceptibility with guidelines. 5. Identify unusual resistance. 6. Recommend optimal antibiotics based on the predicted bacteria and antimicrobial resistance. Limit under 300 words.”

Function 2 (Escalation/De-escalation Strategy): This function provides guidance on antimicrobial stewardship, advising when escalation or de-escalation of antibiotic therapy should be considered based on the predicted resistance profile and patient status. The prompt template is: “You are an infectious disease specialist. Patient information: {patient_status}. AMR table: {amr_table}. Context: {context}. When should escalation or de-escalation be considered? Discuss antimicrobial stewardship strategies. Limit 250 words.”

Function 3 (Renal Function and Dosage Adjustment): This function evaluates whether dosage adjustment is warranted based on the patient’s renal function and pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) principles. The prompt template is: “You are an infectious disease specialist. Patient: {patient_status}. AMR table: {amr_table}. Context: {context}. Should dosage adjustment be considered based on renal function and PK/PD principles? Limit 250 words.”

In all three functions, {patient_status} represents the clinical parameters entered by the user, {amr_table} contains the predicted AMR phenotype from the TCN models, and {context} denotes the retrieved text chunks from the FAISS vector database. As this implementation was developed for academic research purposes to explore the feasibility of an integrated end-to-end workflow, clinical deployment will require prior clarification of copyright and licensing obligations associated with the use of proprietary guideline databases within automated systems.

Sensitivity analysis

To validate that the observed predictive performance stems from the TCN architecture rather than dataset-specific characteristics, we conducted a benchmarking sensitivity analysis using the public DRIAMS dataset. We performed a head-to-head comparison between the TCN-based models and the baseline algorithms established by Weis et al.8: LightGBM for Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus aureus, and MLP for Klebsiella pneumoniae. By training and evaluating these distinct architectures using similar pipelines on an identical dataset, we aimed to isolate the TCN’s specific contribution to capturing sequential spectral dependencies, thereby verifying the model’s robustness independent of the local NTUHYL data distribution.

Furthermore, to investigate whether sequential fine-tuning with increasing volumes of temporally recent data could optimize model updating beyond the fixed eight-month window employed in the primary analysis, we conducted a learning curve analysis on two representative high-impact pathogens: Escherichia coli and Klebsiella pneumoniae. These species were selected based on their high sample volumes within the NTUHYL dataset and their clinical significance. For each bacteria–antibiotic pair, the fine-tuning data from the NTUHYL-23 dataset were incrementally expanded in steps of 50 samples in chronological order, and the model was re-evaluated on the remaining held-out temporal testing data at each step. This design allowed us to assess the relationship between fine-tuning data volume and predictive performance, and to examine whether the optimal updating strategy varies across bacteria–antibiotic pairs.

Additionally, to assess the geographic generalizability of our AMR prediction models, we performed a direct external validation using the publicly available MARISMa dataset,17 which comprises MALDI-TOF MS records collected between 2018 and 2024 in Spain. Although this dataset overlaps temporally with our training period (2017–2022), it originates from a geographically distinct institution, country, and patient population. A total of 93 bacteria–antibiotic pairs across 19 species with available AMR labels were evaluated using our pre-trained models without any local fine-tuning (“zero-shot”).

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages, whereas continuous variables were reported as medians with IQRs. To evaluate model performance, the following metrics were calculated: AUC, accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and F1-score.

Bacteria-identification models utilized a 0.5 probability threshold. For AMR prediction, metrics were calculated at a specificity of 0.75. This high-sensitivity threshold was selected to simulate a “safety-first” strategy for sepsis, prioritizing the minimization of missed resistance. However, the ANTIBIOTIC system allows users to adjust this threshold. This flexibility enables clinicians to balance the risk of overtreatment against antimicrobial stewardship principles based on patient severity (e.g., critical sepsis versus non-life-threatening infection).

The 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated using bootstrap resampling with 1000 iterations. AUC comparisons were performed using the DeLong test, with the choice of method based on whether the ROC curves were correlated or independent, as appropriate21.

All statistical analyses were conducted using MedCalc® Statistical Software version 20.218 (MedCalc Software Ltd., Ostend, Belgium). A two-tailed p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Computing resources

All implementations were carried out using Python 3.10.13. Experiments were conducted on a workstation equipped with an Intel Xeon w5-2455X processor (3.19 GHz), 64 GB of DDR5 RAM (4800 MHz), and an AMD Radeon RX 7600 GPU with 8 GB of VRAM. The operating system was Ubuntu 22.04, with CUDA version 12.3 installed for GPU acceleration. Model development and training were performed using PyTorch version 2.1.1.
