Two decades of biologic and JAK inhibitor prescription patterns in the western region of Melbourne
Authors: Alexander Kwan, Elvina Ingrid, Sara Vogrin, Ken Luong, Fei Jin, Emma Brown, Dongzhe Li, Matthew Jiang, Keith K T Lim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and AimSafety concerns and medication shortages may influence prescribing practices in rheumatology. This study aimed to explore the impact of Janus kinase inhibitor (JAKi) safety warnings and the COVID‐19 pandemic on biologic and targeted‐synthetic disease‐modifying antirheumatic drug (b/tsDMARD) prescription patterns from 2002 to 2024 in the western region of Melbourne.MethodsA retrospective audit was conducted on data from rheumatoid arthritis (RA), psoriatic arthritis (PsA) and ankylosing spondylitis (AS) patients who attended Western Health and Western Rheumatology clinics between 2002 and January 2024 inclusive. b/tsDMARD switching was analysed based on the first date of b/tsDMARD prescription using interrupted time series analysis, with ethics approval granted by the Western Health Office for Research (HREC/23/WH/101783).ResultsThere were 3124 new b/tsDMARD prescriptions. Following the 2021 United States JAKi boxed warning, the JAKi prescription rate decreased to −0.04/month (−0.20, 0.11) from 0.30/month (0.18, 0.42), P = 0.002. Supply shortages during the COVID‐19 pandemic led to 44 b/tsDMARD switches. The most common cause of switching was inefficacy (32.6% of new prescriptions for AS, 42.6% for RA and 46.1% for PsA (P < 0.001)). Less than 5.4% of new prescriptions were from adverse events, underscoring b/tsDMARD safety.Conclusionsb/tsDMARD switches were primarily driven by inefficacy. Black box warning issuance led to decreased JAKi prescriptions. The COVID‐19 pandemic led to minimal b/tsDMARD switches. Switching due to adverse events was infrequent, highlighting the overall safety of b/tsDMARDs in clinical practice.Keywords: biologic, jak inhibitor, COVID‐19, prescribing

Introduction

Rheumatic diseases are a heterogeneous group of disorders characterised by inflammation with frequent involvement of the musculoskeletal system. For many rheumatic diseases, initial therapy involves the use of conventional synthetic disease‐modifying anti‐rheumatic drugs (csDMARDs). In refractory patients, rheumatologists escalate to biologic or targeted‐synthetic anti‐rheumatic drugs (b/tsDMARD).

Many factors influence rheumatologist prescription patterns worldwide. These include the cost and availability of medications, medication efficacy, patient characteristics (such as cardiovascular risk profile and pregnancy) or intolerable side effects. Physician prescribing preferences may also play a role.

Regarding medication availability, b/tsDMARDs in Australia can be accessed through subsidies under the Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme (PBS). b/tsDMARDs are subsidised for various indications including rheumatoid arthritis (RA), psoriatic arthritis (PsA) and ankylosing spondylitis (AS). Depending on the indication, patients require a trial of csDMARD or non‐steroidal anti‐inflammatory drug (NSAID) for a certain duration before being eligible for b/tsDMARD subsidies. After PBS qualification, patients have access to several b/tsDMARDs, including tumour necrosis factor inhibitors (TNFi), interleukin 6 inhibitors (IL‐6i), interleukin 17 inhibitors (IL‐17i), interleukin 12/23 inhibitors (IL‐12/23i), abatacept and Janus kinase inhibitors (JAKi). Although rituximab used to require PBS authority for RA, it is now unrestricted on the PBS. Additionally, the type of bDMARD available under the PBS depends on the indication; for example, RA patients cannot switch to IL‐17i. After commencing a b/tsDMARD, patients are reviewed at 12 to 16 weeks, and then every 24 weeks thereafter. Switching patterns within each disease cohort for PBS‐subsidised b/tsDMARDs are therefore not based on reimbursement.

In recent years, several new factors influenced prescribing patterns of b/tsDMARDs. Following the ORAL surveillance study, the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) issued a boxed warning for JAKi for infections, mortality, malignancy, major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) and thrombosis. 1 In 2023, the Therapeutic Goods Administration of Australia (TGA) released a boxed warning for JAKi highlighting cardiovascular and malignancy risk. 2 Additionally, the recent COVID‐19 pandemic altered the availability of several b/tsDMARDs in Australia. The impact of these factors on Australian b/tsDMARD prescribing patterns requires further enquiry.

This study aimed to characterise b/tsDMARD prescribing patterns across the past two decades in Melbourne's western suburbs – an ethnically, culturally and socioeconomically diverse region – and to explore how factors such as JAKi boxed warnings and the COVID‐19 pandemic impacted these prescribing patterns.

Method

A retrospective audit was conducted on medical records of rheumatology outpatients at Western Health (public patients) and Western Rheumatology (private patients) who were prescribed biologics between January 2002 and January 2024. The prescribers were the same individuals working across both Western Health and Western Rheumatology.

Patients were de‐identified, and data from these records were manually entered into the research electronic data capture (REDCap) tool hosted at Western Health. 3 , 4 PBS application records were used to determine the indication and type of b/tsDMARDs prescribed for each patient.

Demographic and clinical data were obtained based on the b/tsDMARD prescription made. For each de‐identified patient, we recorded age, gender, ethnicity, language, residential area (metropolitan or rural), smoking status, body mass index (BMI) category, co‐morbidities and rheumatic diagnosis as listed in Supplementary Material 1. To focus the analysis, only patients who received PBS applications for the indications of RA, PsA or AS were included.

Reasons for switching were recorded in a single‐answer question box, with options listed as inefficacy, adverse effects, infection, malignancy and other (which included first biologic prescribed, shortage‐related switch, patient profile or preference, streamlined authorisation, boxed warning, cardiovascular event, pregnancy and diagnosis change). Medication inefficacy was identified as the reason for switching when documentation demonstrated inadequate response to b/tsDMARD such as worsening affected joint counts, raised inflammatory markers or higher disease‐specific activity scores. Adverse events included phenomena such as injection site reactions, rash, headaches, nausea and diarrhoea. We note that there was no study‐specific pro forma used to document the reason for switching therapies; reasons for switching were identified from available medical records for patients when there was clear documentation of medication inefficacy, adverse event and so forth.

Statistical analysis

Patient characteristics were presented using median (interquartile range) for continuous variables and frequency (percentage) for categorical variables. The number of patients who switched or swapped to other b/tsDMARDs was graphically represented using Sankey diagrams. Sankey diagrams are read from left to right, with the leftmost bar representing the initial b/tsDMARD being prescribed and rightward bars representing second‐ and third‐line b/tsDMARD prescriptions. The colours of each bar represent the type of b/tsDMARD prescribed. Calendar year of b/tsDMARD commencement, reason for switching and duration of b/tsDMARD use are not represented. Bar thickness, alongside a number and percentage in brackets, indicates the number of individuals commencing, switching or stopping a b/tsDMARD.

Interrupted time series analysis using ordinary least‐squares regression with Newey‐West standard errors was used to examine the effect of FDA and TGA warnings, as well as the COVID pandemic, on prescription of JAK inhibitors. A P‐value less than or equal to 0.05 was determined as statistically significant. All analyses were performed using Stata (StataCorp LLC).

Ethics approval

Patient records were accessed under Western Health Office for Research Ethics Committee approval (HREC/23/WH/101783).

Of these 1452 patients, 421 were from public practice and 1031 were from private practice. The median age (interquartile range) of these patients was 61 (51, 70), 55 (47, 63), and 47 (38, 58) years old for RA, PsA, and AS patients, respectively. The majority of RA patients were female (682 (75%)) whilst the majority of AS patients were male (134 (60.9%)). Most patients in this study were Caucasian (ranging from 155 (70.5%) of AS patients to 703 (77.3%) of RA patients), however other ethnicities included South‐East Asian, First Nations Australian, Polynesian, Montenegrin, and North African, alongside several others.

Regarding co‐morbidities, the most frequent co‐morbidity was hypertension, with 224 (24.6%), 58 (18%), and 43 (19.5%) RA, PsA, and AS patients having co‐morbid hypertension, respectively. Across the range of RA, PsA, and AS patients, between 5% to 11.8% of patients had diabetes mellitus, and 10% to 13% of patients had co‐morbid depression or anxiety. The proportion of patients with a history of cardiovascular disease varied; 9.7%, 7.4%, and 3.2% of RA, PsA, or AS patients had cardiovascular disease, respectively.

Switching pattern

For the 1452 patients in this study, there were 3124 new b/tsDMARDs prescribed throughout the study period. The first, second and third b/tsDMARD prescribed for each condition are demonstrated in Sankey diagrams for RA (Fig. 1), PsA (Fig. 2) and AS (Fig. 3). The number of prescriptions each year ranged from 1 in 2002 to 531 in 2023 (Fig. 4).

Number of b/tsDMARD prescriptions per year.

Of the 3124 new b/tsDMARD prescriptions, the number of first‐prescribed b/tsDMARDs for RA, PsA and AS patients was 899 (44.6%), 319 (44.6%) and 219 (55.3%) respectively. The next most common reason for a new b/tsDMARD prescription (i.e. the most common reason for switching therapy) was inefficacy. The number of new prescriptions due to inefficacy was 857 (42.6%), 329 (46.1%) and 129 (32.6%) for RA, PsA and AS patients respectively. In contrast, switching b/tsDMARD due to adverse events was comparatively rare. Across two decades, the number of new prescriptions due to adverse events was 106 (5.3%), 35 (4.9%) and 20 (5.1%) of b/tsDMARDs for RA, PsA and AS. The number of switches due to other reasons was proportionally much smaller relative to inefficacy and adverse events. For example, only one (0.1%) patient with PsA switched therapy due to malignancy, and only four (1.0%) patients with AS switched therapy due to infection. Together, all other reasons for switching (such as patient preference, pregnancy, medication shortages, malignancy and infection) accounted for the remaining approximate 5%–10% of b/tsDMARD switches for RA, PsA and AS patients during the study period.

Impact of JAKi safety warnings

Out of 3124 total b/tsDMARD prescriptions, 723 prescriptions were for a JAKi. There were 555 (27.6%), 138 (19.3%) and 30 (7.6%) total JAKi prescriptions for the indications of RA, PsA and AS. Relative to initial prescriptions for TNFi (n = 637, 256 and 205 for RA, PsA and AS respectively), far fewer patients received a JAKi as an initial b/tsDMARD (n = 185, 23 and 7 for RA, PsA and AS respectively).

Most patients who commenced JAKi did not switch to other therapy (Figs. 1, 2, 3). Among those who were prescribed a JAKi at any time, the number of switches directly attributable to concerns about the boxed warning for JAKi was low (11 (0.5%), one (0.1%) and one (0.3%) switch/es for RA, PsA and AS patients).

After the US FDA boxed warning for JAKi in 2021, there was an increase in the number of monthly TNFi prescriptions in 2022 (Fig. 5). After the warning, the monthly rate of JAKi prescriptions decreased to −0.04 (95% confidence interval: −0.20, 0.11) relative to the rate of JAKi prescriptions preceding the warning of 0.30 (0.18, 0.42), P = 0.002.

Impact of COVID‐19

With the onset of the COVID‐19 pandemic, Australia experienced supply shortages of tocilizumab and abatacept, necessitating prescription transitions to TNFi or JAKi during this period. Out of all the recorded switches, 44 (2.2%) of new b/tsDMARD prescriptions for RA were due to supply shortages. There were no switches due to supply shortages for patients with PsA or AS. The 44 switches in RA patients occurred in 35 people, with some patients switching more than once due to shortages. Of these 35 people, 17 (49%) switched back to their original therapy: 12 of the 22 patients (55%) originally receiving tocilizumab eventually switched back to tocilizumab, and five of 13 patients (38%) originally receiving abatacept returned to abatacept.

Discussion

Two decades of b/tsDMARD prescription patterns have offered insight into the impact of safety warnings and the COVID‐19 pandemic on prescription practices in the western region of Melbourne. While the JAKi FDA warning led to a reduction in JAKi prescriptions, most patients receiving a JAKi never switched to alternative therapy, suggesting high efficacy and tolerability of JAKi. This may also suggest clinician and patient general acceptance of reported risks. The COVID‐19 pandemic led to prescription switches away from tocilizumab and abatacept, with 49% of patients eventually returning to tocilizumab or abatacept.

Most of the new b/tsDMARD prescriptions that occurred during the study period were the initial b/tsDMARD prescribed (899 (44.6%), 319 (44.6%) and 219 (55.3%) of new b/tsDMARD prescriptions for RA, PsA and AS respectively). The second most common cause of a new b/tsDMARD prescription – and, therefore, the most common reason for switching therapy – was inefficacy (857 (42.6%), 329 (46.1%) and 129 (32.6%) of new prescriptions for RA, PsA and AS patients respectively). Adverse events were the next most common reason for switching therapy, but they occurred at a far lower rate than that of inefficacy (106 (5.3%), 35 (4.9%) and 20 (5.1%) of new prescriptions for RA, PsA and AS respectively). This underscores the safety and tolerability of b/tsDMARDs in general.

In recent years, the safety profile of JAKi has been the subject of much discussion. Despite the safety warning regarding venous thromboembolism (VTE) risk in tofacitinib in 2019, the gross trend of monthly JAKi prescriptions continued to rise until the FDA safety warning in 2021 (Fig. 5). Following the FDA boxed warning for JAKi, the monthly prescription rate of JAKi decreased to −0.04 (95% confidence interval: −0.20, 0.11) relative to the monthly rate of JAKi prescriptions preceding the warning (0.30 (0.18, 0.42), P = 0.002). However, the number of actual medication switches due to the JAKi boxed warning was low (11 (0.5%), one (0.1%) and one (0.3%) of new prescriptions for RA, PsA and AS respectively). This may suggest that for those already receiving JAKi with adequate disease control, patients and clinicians were willing to tolerate cardiovascular risk instead of switching to alternative therapy. However, for patients initiating b/tsDMARD therapy, JAKi may have been less often prescribed, particularly if there was concern for older patients with existing cardiovascular risk factors. 5 This may reflect prescriber responsiveness to national warnings regarding JAKi safety.

An important consideration to mention is that the decreased monthly rate of new JAKi prescriptions may have been influenced by the COVID‐19 pandemic, which occurred contemporaneously with the various JAKi safety warnings. Since JAKi may increase susceptibility to viral infections, 6 it is possible that fewer JAKi were prescribed in the setting of the COVID‐19 pandemic, rather than due to concerns surrounding the risk of VTE, malignancy or cardiovascular risk. While there was no explicit documentation of clinicians deciding against JAKi prescriptions due to COVID‐19, nuances in clinical decision making may not have been documented in the medical records accessed in this study.

Some of the prescription trends in this study are reflected in similar registry‐based or overseas studies. Regarding treatment patterns in RA, a study based on the Australian Rheumatology Association Database (ARAD) identified TNFi as the most common first‐prescribed class of b/tsDMARDs between 2003 and 2018, with inefficacy (~50%) as the most common reason for ceasing b/tsDMARD and adverse events (~20%) as the next most common reason. 7 Similarly, a study based on 16 350 RA patients at the Mass General Brigham Healthcare system found the three most common reasons for discontinuing a b/tsDMARD to be inadequate response (43.4%), lost efficacy (25.8%) and non‐allergic adverse events (13.7%). 8 Relative to other global trends, we also note that the high frequency of TNFi prescriptions in our study is consistent with TNFi being the most prescribed first‐line bDMARD in Japan and Europe for rheumatoid arthritis 9 and in the United States, 10 but we note this may be due to the overall longer period of availability of TNFi relative to other b/tsDMARDs.

The switches away from tocilizumab and abatacept during the COVID‐19 pandemic may partially explain the increase in TNFi prescriptions from 2020 into 2022 in Figure 6 and may have further contributed to the rise in TNFi prescriptions soon after the FDA JAKi safety warning (Fig. 5). This is consistent with a reduction in IL‐6i prescriptions during the COVID‐19 pandemic, as seen in Croatia, 11 and reductions in COVID‐19 era abatacept prescriptions, as seen in France. 12 However, the total number of switches directly related to medication supply shortages is small, demonstrating that COVID‐19‐related supply shortages had minimal impact on b/tsDMARD prescribing practices overall.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. As a retrospective data analysis, this study cannot derive direct causative relationships between safety warnings, socioeconomic factors and their impact on prescription practices at the local level. Furthermore, missing information regarding co‐morbidities and past medical history may not be documented. As a result, we cannot determine precise reasons for prescription trends. For example, we are unable to determine whether the decline in new JAKi prescriptions after the release of safety warnings is fully attributable to safety concerns or concerns regarding viral illnesses in light of the COVID‐19 pandemic.

Additionally, time‐dependent confounders limit the interpretation of Sankey diagrams. As others have explored, these may include the timing of b/tsDMARDs being listed on the PBS, patient death and loss to follow‐up. 7 For instance, the fact that TNFi is the most prescribed b/tsDMARD may be most reflective of longstanding availability rather than other factors.

Lastly, since data were obtained from PBS authority applications and medical records without a study‐specific pro forma, the nuances surrounding decisions to prescribe one b/tsDMARD over another were not necessarily captured in clinic notes. This limits the ability to investigate the rationale behind first‐line b/tsDMARD choices or to what extent cardiovascular risk factors and patient age influenced decisions for or against JAKi therapy.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides insight into the influence of safety warnings and pandemic supply shortages on b/tsDMARD prescription practices on the local level. Future analyses incorporating larger patient populations over a similar period may offer insight into prescription patterns on a national and international scale, demonstrating the impact of these factors on b/tsDMARD prescriptions worldwide.
