Unraveling the formation mechanism of pyrazines and their derivatives in the glucose-glycine Maillard reaction using carbon module labeling coupled with carbonyl analysis
Authors: Shuqi Zhao, Jingyu Li, Huan Liu, Guofeng Jin, Weiting Wang, Li Dong, Pengfei Du, Bo Xiao, Fengxue Zhang, Junke Li, Fang Wang, Chengyu Zeng, Cheng Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Previous and present studies confirm that glucose and glycine serve as key precursors for pyrazines contributing to the roasty aroma, but their formation pathways remain unclear. The formation pathways of pyrazines and their derivatives were elucidated by combining CAMOLA with carbonyls profiling. Continuous accumulation of early-stage Maillard products and melanoidins occurred during the glucose-glycine reaction at 160 °C, with 5-methyl-2-furanmethanol, hexanoic acid, and hexanoic acid pentyl ester identified as reliable reaction markers. Eight pyrazines were identified as temperature- and time-dependent products, which were dominated by unlabeled isotopologues from the recombination of glucose fragments. Products of aldol reaction and keto-enol tautomerism of 1-hydroxy-2-butanone and 1,3-dihydroxypropanone, together with glyoxal and methylglyoxal, reacted with glycine to produce aminocarbonyl intermediates. Differences in the structure and binding sites of aminocarbonyl intermediates were responsible for the formation of diverse pyrazines and their derivatives. This work provides a theoretical foundation for the targeted regulation of roasty aroma.

Introduction

The roasty aroma serves as the most critical sensory attribute of meat products, determining the overall quality and consumer acceptability of roasted meat products. It is generally accepted that multi-branched pyrazine derivatives are the most prominent aroma contributors in roasted meat. Gąsior et al. (2021) identify 2-ethyl-3,5-dimethylpyrazine as a key roasty compound in roasted goose meat with OAVs exceeding 1. The branched-chain pyrazines containing 2,5-dimethyl-3-ethylpyrazine and 2-ethyl-3,5-dimethylpyrazine are the key roasty compounds in roasted mutton skewers, with an increase in their concentrations during roasting inducing an elevation in the response intensity of E-nose sensor signals and the signal values of aroma fingerprints (Hua et al., 2025). Pyrazines and their derivatives containing 2-methylpyrazine and 2,3-dimethyl-5-ethylpyrazine are common roasty compounds in roasted meats, such as beef, pork, lamb, chicken, duck, and goose (Shi et al., 2024). These results consistently indicate that pyrazines serve as common key roasty compounds in roasted meats, while differences in pyrazine profiles are observed among different meat products. No significant change is observed in the roasty intensity of Beijing roasted ducks when 15% maltose is replaced with a water-in-oil nanoemulsion embedded with 3% xylose in its aqueous phase, primarily due to the unchanged content of 2,5-dimethyl-3-ethylpyrazine (Hu et al., 2025). The significant increase in roasty intensity of chicken skin primarily results from the retention of compounds such as 2-methyl-3,5-diethylpyrazine and 2,5-dimethyl-3-ethylpyrazine by triglycerides during roasting, particularly TG (16:1_18:1_18:2) (Nie et al., 2024). Both thermal conditions and water activity can affect the formation of pyrazines between proteins/oligopeptides/free amino acids and glucose, with low water activity and high temperatures significantly enhancing pyrazine production (Scalone et al., 2015). These phenomena indicate that differences in the types and contents of pyrazines mainly depend on variations in reaction substrates and thermal conditions. Our latest preliminary study on roasted pork has revealed negative correlations between glucose/glycine and pyrazines during thermal processing, indicating they are crucial substrates for pyrazine formation (Liu et al., 2025). Variations in the content of tetramethylpyrazine primarily stem from the reaction between different types of amino acids and acetoin (sugar degradation product), with a yield of 11.40% from the reaction of acetoin and glycine (Shu, 1998). High-intensity ultrasound facilitates the formation of abundant dicarbonyls during the Maillard reaction between glucose and glycine, which further accelerates the production of alkylpyrazines including 2,5-dimethylpyrazine and 2,5-dimethyl-3-ethylpyrazine (Yu et al., 2018). However, the formation pathways of pyrazines and their derivatives from the glucose and glycine reaction have not been reported.

Carbon module labeling (CAMOLA), a novel stable isotope tracing technique, has emerged as a powerful tool for deciphering the molecular formation mechanisms of aroma compounds in complex food systems. This technique involves labeling specific carbon modules of reaction substrates with stable isotopes, followed by tracking the isotope distribution in reaction products via high-resolution mass spectrometry, enabling precise identification of carbon flow and intermediate transformation pathways. Specifically, Poisson et al. (2018, 2019) employ CAMOLA technology to elucidate how cleavage and recombination of the sucrose carbon skeleton affect the formation of 2,3-butanedione, 2,3-pentanedione, and 4-hydroxy-2,5-dimethyl-3(2H)-furanone during distinct heating phases, thereby confirming the technology's feasibility. Our team employs CAMOLA technology to delineate the molecular pathways of 2-furfurylthiol, furans, and thiophene derivatives (Liu et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2021). Zhou et al. (2024) clarify the formation mechanism of alkylpyrazines from the heat treatment of N-(1-Deoxy-D-xylulos-1-yl)-alanine and exogenous alanine using CAMOLA technology, with α-dicarbonyls including glyoxal and methylglyoxal as critical intermediates determining their formation rate. The feasibility of CAMOLA technology for unraveling volatile formation along with methods for characterizing the molecular structures of carbonyls provides technical support for simulating the formation pathways of pyrazines and their derivatives via the glucose-glycine Maillard reaction.

The present work aimed to (i) analyze the macroscopic variation patterns during the glucose-glycine Maillard reaction, (ii) clarify the changes of pyrazines, their derivatives, and the isotopic carbon skeletons of these compounds, and (iii) propose the formation pathways of pyrazines and their derivatives by integrating the structural characteristics of monocarbonyls and dicarbonyls. These findings will provide data support for the targeted regulation of roasty aroma generated from the Maillard reaction between glucose and glycine.

Materials and methods

Materials

13C6-glucose (99%), 12C6-glucose (99%), and glycine (99%) were purchased from CIL, Alfa Aesar, and Sigma-Aldrich (Shanghai, China), respectively. 2-Methyl-3-heptanone (99%), n-alkanes (C7-C40, 97%), and tripotassium phosphate (99.5%) were supplied by Dr. Ehrenstorfer, o2si Smart Solutions, and Macklin (Shanghai, China), respectively.

Model reaction

Reactants containing unlabeled and isotopically labeled glucose, as well as glycine, were dissolved in 0.5 mol/L potassium phosphate buffer supplemented with 0.6 M NaCl, which was placed in 40 mL vials. The reaction system was formulated with unlabeled and isotopically labeled glucose and glycine at an equimolar ratio of 1:1. The pH (5.56) was consistent with that of raw pork. A C-MAG HS 7 magnetic stirrer was employed to heat the mixture at 160 °C for 5-40 min. Notably, 160 °C corresponds to the endpoint temperature during pork roasting (Liu et al., 2025).

Absorbance analysis

The absorbance values of reaction products from reactants consisting of 1:1 13C-labeled glucose and glycine were determined using an ultraviolet-visible spectrophotometer. Absorbance measured at 294 nm was employed to monitor colorless and non-fluorescent intermediates formed during the Maillard reaction, whereas absorbance at 420 nm was applied to evaluate the degree of browning, representing the overall progression of the Maillard reaction (Liu et al., 2021).

Pyrazines and derivatives analysis

Volatile compounds were analyzed using a Shimadzu GC×GC-MS system (QP2020NX). The glucose (72.06 mg), glycine (30.02 mg), and 2-methyl-3-heptanone (1.5 μL, 2 μg/μL) were added into a vial. The extraction conditions were set as follows: preheating temperature of 55 °C for 10 min, extraction temperature of 55 °C for 45 min, and desorption temperature of 250 °C for 3 min. The initial column temperature underwent a 3-min hold at 40 °C, followed by ramping to 230 °C at 3 °C/min and a final 5-min hold at the same temperature. Mass spectra were recorded in full scan mode at unit mass resolution, with a mass range of 40-350 m/z and 70 eV ionization. This detection procedure was identical to the method established in our previous work (Liu et al., 2025). The identification analysis of the volatile compounds in the reaction system was conducted using mass spectrometry (MS) library matching combined with linear retention indices (LRI) calculation. 2-Methyl-3-heptanone was used for the quantitation.

CAMOLA analysis

Isotopic profiles of pyrazines and derivatives were determined using mass-to-charge ratios, following the methodology established by Cerny et al. (2003). The isotopologue distribution was obtained through normalization of peak areas corresponding to characteristic ions ranging from [M]+ to [M + n]+, where [M]+ and n denoted the molecular ion and the number of labeled carbon atoms, respectively.

Carbonyls analysis

The carbonyls in the glucose model and glucose-glycine Maillard reaction model heated for 40 min were analyzed according to previous studies (Moreira et al., 2013; Piergiovanni et al., 2023). The derivatization was achieved using 50 μL of O-(2,3,4,5,6-pentafluorobenzyl)hydroxylamine hydrochloride solution. Helium was used as the carrier gas at 1.2 mL/min, with electron ionization providing a mass scanning range of m/z 45-500. The GC oven was programmed with an initial holding at 50 °C for 1 min, a temperature elevation to 220 °C at 4 °C/min, a further increase to 250 °C at 20 °C/min, and a final holding for 10 min.

Statistical analysis

Statistical evaluation of all data derived from the model reaction was conducted using SPSS 19.0 software (IBM Corporation, USA). Independent-samples t-tests were applied for statistical comparison of carbonyls. Differences in all other indicators were evaluated via Fisher's protected least significant difference test, with significance set at p < 0.05.

Results and discussion

Progression of the glucose-glycine Maillard reaction during heating

The absorbance of Maillard reaction products between glucose and glycine at 420 nm reached its lowest value (0.16) at 5 min, followed by a significant increase (p < 0.05) during heating and a maximum value (0.63) at the end of the thermal treatment (Table 1). The formation rate of melanoidins was maintained at a low level in the initial stage (0-10 min), with a sharp increase during the middle stage (20-30 min) followed by a rapid decrease in the final stage (30-40 min). Such a kinetic profile was mainly attributed to the combined effects of substrate concentration and reaction temperature. The formation amounts and reaction rates of Maillard reaction products including furans, pyridines, pyrroles, and pyrazines all increase with rising heating temperature in the Maillard reaction of the dipeptides Asn-Pro and Pro-Asn as well as the mixture of asparagine, proline, and glucose (Xiao et al., 2025). Similar changes in absorbance were detected at 294 nm, supporting the continuous generation of early Maillard products. In detail, these early-stage products were slowly formed at 0-10 min, with a rapid accumulation at 20-30 min. In the initial stage of the Maillard reaction, the formation of early intermediates containing Schiff bases and Amadori rearrangement products is predominant. With the progression of the reaction, these intermediates undergo intensive degradation, condensation and cyclization reactions, thereby facilitating the rapid accumulation of colored browning products (Deng et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2021). PLS-DA was employed for further investigation of the glucose-glycine Maillard reaction and identification of volatile markers in relation to reaction degree discrimination (Fig. 1). The glucose-glycine reaction samples at 5-40 min exhibited a counterclockwise evolutionary trend, accompanied by satisfactory model fitness (R2X = 0.99, R2Y = 0.99, Q2 = 0.99). In detail, samples obtained at 5-20 min showed positive values along the t1 axis, which were clearly distinguished from those at 30-40 min. Samples at 30 min and 40 min displayed positive and negative values along the t2 axis, respectively. Notably, only 2-nonanone was closely associated with 5-10 min samples, while merely 2,5-dimethylpyrazine was correlated with 20-min samples. Most compounds were distributed near 30-40 min samples. A total of 28 compounds including 2-acetyl-3-ethylpyrazine, 2,5-dimethyl-3-ethylpyrazine, and 2,5-diethylpyrazine were clustered near 30-min samples, whereas 26 compounds including 2-ethyl-5-methylpyrazine, 2-methyl-3,5-diethylpyrazine, and 2-ethenyl-5-methylpyrazine were located adjacent to 40-min samples. These findings preliminarily revealed that the pyrazines, especially highly branched structures, tended to form under prolonged high-temperature conditions. This finding is consistent with previous studies, in which pyrazines with fewer branches are not only more easily formed but also mainly generated in the middle and late reaction stages when the system temperature is higher (Koutsidis et al., 2008). Twelve volatile compounds showed VIP higher than 1, among which 2-nonanone (2.59), 2,5-dimethylpyrazine (2.20) and acetoin (1.63) exhibited the highest contributions. The thresholds of VIP > 1 and p < 0.05 are applied to screen potential volatile markers (Liu et al., 2024). Only three components, namely 5-methyl-2-furanmethanol, hexanoic acid, and hexanoic acid pentyl ester, were identified as potential volatile markers for discriminating heating degrees. All three compounds showed the lowest concentrations at 5 min, among which their values reached the maximum at 40 min after a significant increase. Changes of pyrazines and derivatives from the glucose-glycine Maillard reaction during heating

As presented in Table 2, 56 volatile compounds in the glucose-glycine Maillard reaction model were detected, encompassing 14 aldehydes, 12 furans, 8 pyrazines, 7 ketones, 6 acids, 5 alcohols, 3 esters, and 1 phenol. This finding initially suggested aldehydes and furans as potential precursors for the formation of pyrazines and derivatives. Prior studies have validated this phenomenon, in which acidic conditions favor the formation of furans and derivatives while alkaline environments promote the generation of pyrazines and derivatives (Lotfy et al., 2021; Ren et al., 2024). The initial phase of the glucose-glycine Maillard reaction favors the formation of large amounts of furans and their derivatives, while the subsequent stage proceeds via Strecker degradation and other concurrent reactions that produce aldehydes, ketones, dicarbonyls, and heterocyclic compounds (Cui et al., 2021). Hexanal exhibited the highest content (490.00-13637.33 ng), followed by heptanal (214.00-12452.00 ng) and 5-methyl-2-furanmethanol (0-9432.67 ng). Additionally, 5-methyl-2-furancarboxaldehyde, 2-ethyl-3,5-dimethylpyrazine, and 2-ethyl-5-methylpyrazine all showed contents exceeding 6000 ng. The number of volatile compounds varied with heating duration, among which 20, 24, 32, 41, and 54 compounds were identified in models heated for 5, 10, 20, 30, and 40 min, respectively. The thermal reactions of xylose with the alanine-xylose Amadori rearrangement product demonstrate that furans are predominantly derived from the carbon skeleton of the sugar. Among the 8 pyrazines and derivatives, 2-ethyl-3,5-dimethylpyrazine presented the highest content (0-8358.67 ng), followed by 2-ethyl-5-methylpyrazine (331.33-6036.00 ng) and 2-ethenyl-5-methylpyrazine (20.00-2040.67 ng). Conversely, pyrazines with multiple branched chains or complex substituents showed the lowest contents, such as 2,5-dimethyl-3-ethylpyrazine (0-962.00 ng), 2-methyl-3,5-diethylpyrazine (0-420.67 ng), and 2-acetyl-3-ethylpyrazine (0-196.67 ng). All pyrazine compounds except 2,5-dimethylpyrazine demonstrated a gradual increase in content with the progression of the thermal reaction, reaching their maximum values at 40 min of heating. An interesting observation is that the degradation of Amadori rearrangement products is more sensitive than its formation to rising temperature. Heating above 100 °C for 30 min enhances the generation of nitrogen-containing heterocyclic compounds such as pyrazines (Liu et al., 2015; Tang et al., 2019). Three short-chain pyrazines were detected in the reaction system heated for 5-10 min, namely 2,5-dimethylpyrazine (256.00-1018.00 ng), 2-ethyl-5-methylpyrazine (331.33-551.00 ng), and 2-ethenyl-5-methylpyrazine (20.00-52.00 ng). All pyrazines except 2-methyl-3,5-diethylpyrazine were identified in the system heated for 20 min, while all pyrazines except 2,5-dimethylpyrazine were detected in models heated for 30-40 min. The thermal degradation of the Nε-di-Xul-Lys ARP/xylose model demonstrates that elevated temperature favors the generation of pyrazines during heating for 30-60 min, with the formation rate of highly alkyl-substituted pyrazines lower than that of less substituted analogs (Zhang et al., 2025). Interestingly, the concentration of 2-ethyl-3,5-dimethylpyrazine was 19.87 times higher than that of 2-methyl-3,5-diethylpyrazine. While 1-hydroxy-2-butanone acts as a shared carbonyl precursor for both of them, their second carbonyl reactants distinctly differ. Methylglyoxal may participate in the generation of 2-ethyl-3,5-dimethylpyrazine, whereas ethylglyoxal, an intermediate derived from subsequent conversion of 1-hydroxy-2-butanone, may serve as the additional substrate for synthesizing 2-methyl-3,5-diethylpyrazine. In short, the formation of 2-ethyl-3,5-dimethylpyrazine demands far fewer reaction steps and lower energy input compared with 2-methyl-3,5-diethylpyrazine, which explains its substantially higher abundance. This observation well aligns with a previous study, in which the yield of 2-ethyl-3,5-dimethylpyrazine reached 19.94-fold that of 2-methyl-3,5-diethylpyrazine in a glucose-glycine Maillard system incubated at 95 °C for 90 min (Zhang et al., 2022).

Carbon skeleton patterns of pyrazines and derivatives formed in the glucose-glycine Maillard reaction during heating

The CAMOLA technology was applied to trace the carbon skeleton evolution of pyrazines and their derivatives from the Maillard reaction of glucose with glycine (Cerny and Davidek, 2003). 2-Ethenyl-5-methylpyrazine was selected as a representative disubstituted pyrazine. The fraction of its unlabeled carbon skeleton maintained a high level at the early heating stage of 5-10 min, which gradually stabilized in the subsequent reaction period (Table 3). The unlabeled isotopologue ([M]+; m/z 120, 21-62%) displayed the highest relative abundance, followed by tri-labeled ([M+3]+; m/z 123, 8-23%), tetra-labeled ([M+4]+; m/z 124, 1-20%), and fully labeled ([M+7]+; m/z 127, 1-17%) isotopologues. The proportion of molecules with a fully glucose-derived carbon skeleton reached 84% at 40 min. 2,5-Diethylpyrazine showed an isotopologue distribution pattern similar to that of 2-ethenyl-5-methylpyrazine. The unlabeled form ([M]+; m/z 136, 15-34%) remained dominant, accompanied by tri-labeled ([M+3]+; m/z 139, 21-27%) and tetra-labeled ([M+4]+; m/z 140, 16-22%) isotopologues. Its glucose-sourced carbon skeleton accounted for 63% after 40 min of heating. Additionally, trisubstituted pyrazines including 2-ethyl-3,5-dimethylpyrazine were generated through complicated routes. The unlabeled isotopologue ([M]+; m/z 136, 28-30%) presented the highest intensity. Tri-labeled ([M+3]+; m/z 139, 18-21%), tetra-labeled ([M+4]+; m/z 140, 10-15%), hexa-labeled ([M+6]+; m/z 142, 10-15%), and di-labeled ([M+2]+; m/z 138, 3-14%) isotopologues appeared in a relatively high proportion. For 2-acetyl-3-ethylpyrazine, the unlabeled isotopologue ([M]+; m/z 150, 13-55%) was the most abundant. Tri-labeled ([M+3]+; m/z 153, 9-16%), penta-labeled ([M+5]+; m/z 155, 2-14%), and hexa-labeled ([M+6]+; m/z 156, 3-14%) isotopologues constituted the next major fractions. Hepta-labeled and fully labeled isotopologues also provided noticeable contributions. The overall fraction of molecules with a complete glucose-derived skeleton reached 84%. Other pyrazines and their derivatives containing 2-methyl-3,5-diethylpyrazine also exhibited a roughly identical trend in the evolution of their carbon skeletons. Taken together, pyrazines and their derivatives mainly stemmed from rearrangement of glucose carbon skeletons, with glycine also serving as a vital participant. Consistent with previous findings, the sugar serves as the primary precursor for the carbon skeletons of all pyrazines in the Maillard reaction between free amino acids and sucrose (Spreng et al., 2021). Although only four pyrazines including pyrazine, methylpyrazine, 2,5-dimethylpyrazine, and 2,3,5-trimethylpyrazine were detected in the glucose-glycine model heated at 125 °C for 30 min, their formation was primarily driven by the recombination of C2 and C3 fragments from glucose (Totlani and Peterson, 2005). These findings also suggested that carbonyls containing C2 or C3 fragments served as critical intermediates in the formation of pyrazines and their derivatives.

Formation pathways of pyrazines and derivatives from key dicarbonyls produced by the glucose-glycine Maillard reaction

Twenty-two carbonyls were identified in both glucose pyrolysis and the glucose-glycine model, consisting of 19 monocarbonyls and 3 dicarbonyls (Table 4). 2-Butanone was the most abundant of these compounds, with concentrations ranging from 1452.31 to 3104.38 ng. Acetaldehyde and trimethylacetaldehyde also occurred in high quantities, with respective concentrations of 618.69-1320.28 ng and 570.69-1268.88 ng. Formaldehyde, propionaldehyde, and 2-pentanone similarly maintained relatively high levels. Conversely, all dicarbonyls were present at low concentrations, spanning 1.86 to 241.42 ng. This finding implied a substantially lower energy requirement for monocarbonyl production compared to dicarbonyl formation in both reaction systems. Regarding both monocarbonyls and dicarbonyls, the glucose pyrolysis exhibited higher production yields and conversion efficiencies compared to the glucose-glycine reaction. This phenomenon aligns with a previous study, in which the carbohydrate cleavage yields a spectrum of highly reactive carbonyls, including glyoxal and methylglyoxal (Smuda and Glomb, 2013). As illustrated in Fig. 2, a hypothetical formation mechanism for pyrazines and their derivatives was proposed based on previous findings and the changes of carbonyls and pyrazines (Yaylayan et al., 2000; Zhou et al., 2023). Monocarbonyls may act as crucial intermediates in this process, but their conversion into pyrazine derivatives requires the prior formation of dicarbonyls, which are essential participants in the generation of all pyrazines and their derivatives. In detail, glyoxal and glycine underwent a dehydration reaction to form a Schiff base intermediate, which then underwent decarboxylation before further reacting with water molecules to produce an aminocarbonyl compound. The condensation of two molecules of aminocarbonyl compound resulted in the formation of 2,5-dimethylpyrazine. Additionally, the products derived from the aldol condensation and keto-enol tautomerism reactions between 1,3-dihydroxypropanone and acetaldehyde reacted with glycine to form another aminocarbonyl compound. This compound then interacted with the aminocarbonyl compound derived from glyoxal, causing the generation of 2-acetyl-3-ethylpyrazine. Similarly, the products from the aldol condensation and keto-enol tautomerism between 1-hydroxy-2-butanone and formaldehyde reacted with glycine to produce an aminocarbonyl compound. This aminocarbonyl compound further reacted with those derived from methylglyoxal and 1-hydroxy-2-butanone. The formation of 2,5-dimethyl-3-ethylpyrazine, 2-ethyl-3,5-dimethylpyrazine, and 2-methyl-3,5-diethylpyrazine depended on the specific binding sites during the condensation process. The primary formation pathway of pyrazines and their derivatives involves Strecker degradation between α-dicarbonyls and amino acids. The dimerization of the resulting α-aminoketones constitutes a crucial step (Yu et al., 2021). An interesting phenomenon is that the caramelization of glucose preferentially proceeds through 1,2-enediol to form fructose and 3-deoxyglucosone rather than generating dicarbonyls via glucosone. However, the addition of amino acids participates in the Maillard reaction through the enaminol pathway, with its effect significantly overriding the caramelization of glucose (Lee et al., 2022).Fig. 2Possible formation mechanism of pyrazines and derivatives in the glucose-glycine reactionAR: aldol reaction, KE: keto-enol tautomerism.

Isotopic and carbonyl analyses confirm that dicarbonyls serve as key intermediates in the formation of pyrazines and derivatives. Monocarbonyls must first transform into dicarbonyls before engaging in pyrazine formation. This work proposes a putative formation pathway for pyrazines and derivatives in the glucose-glycine Maillard reaction, which requires thorough verification through systematic experimentation. Future work will employ exogenously supplemented 13C-labeled carbonyls. Isotope-labeled carbonyl precursors including glyoxal, methylglyoxal, and 1-hydroxy-2-butanone will be separately added to the glucose-glycine model to quantify their respective carbon contributions to pyrazines and derivatives.

Conclusion

The work first clarified the continuous formation of eight pyrazines from the glucose-glycine Maillard reaction at 160 °C for 5-40 min. Their carbon skeletons mainly arose from the recombination of C2, C3, C4, and C5 fragments derived from glucose. Monocarbonyls including 1,3-dihydroxypropanone and 1-hydroxy-2-butanone may undergo aldol reaction and keto-enol tautomerism to produce dicarbonyls, which further react with glycine to yield pyrazines and their derivatives. However, the key roles of these carbonyls have not been validated by exogenous addition experiments. Particularly, various amino acids and peptides can also participate in the Maillard reaction to yield pyrazines and their derivatives. Therefore, more realistic roasted meat matrix models should be constructed to verify the reaction pathways.
