Abstract
. Although algebraic structures are frequently analyzed using unary and binary operations, they can also be effectively defined and unified through ternary operations. In this context, we introduce structures that contain two constants and a ternary operation. We demonstrate that these structures are isomorphic to various significant algebraic systems, including Boolean algebras, de Morgan algebras, MV-algebras, and (near) rings of characteristic two. Our work highlights the versatility of ternary operations in describing and connecting diverse algebraic structures.

1.
Introduction

Ternary Boolean algebras
[
18
]
were introduced by Grau in 1947 to axiomatize Boolean algebras by means of the ternary operation
xy\circ yz\circ zx
(see also
[
25
]
). In the same year this operation was used independently by Birkhoff and Kiss
[
4
]
to characterize distributive lattices. Both approaches are particular cases of median algebras
[
1
,
20
,
26
]
.
Although the set of axioms is distinct in each case, complete commutativity is a common feature. In 1948, Church
[
7
]
shows that it is possible to axiomatize Boolean algebras in terms of the conditioned disjunction
\bar{y}x\circ yz
which is not completely commutative (see also
[
19
]
).

In this article, we explore the axiomatization of Boolean algebras, de Morgan algebras, MV-algebras, and rings or near rings of characteristic two using ternary structures. By a ternary structure we mean an algebraic system consisting of a set
A
with two constants,
0
and
1
, and a ternary operation
p(x,y,z)\in A
. This ternary operation gives rise to derived unary and binary operations, and each formula specifying
p
in terms of these operations corresponds to a new axiom, leading to subvarieties of the original structure.
Interestingly, each classical structure considered here has a characteristic expression that determines the ternary operation
p
using its derived binary operations. The purpose of this paper is to examine these expressions and their implications for the unification of classical algebras within the framework of ternary structures.

For example, within the set of axioms
(T1)
–
(T4)
, introduced in Section
2
, Boolean algebras form a subvariety if and only if
p(x,y,z)
is interpreted as Church’s conditioned disjunction, as proved in Section
3
.
Section
4
gives the formula for
p
that turns the
(T1)
–
(T4)
structure into a de Morgan algebra, while Section
5
discusses the cases of rings and near rings of characteristic 2. In Section
6
, it is shown how to modify the axioms

(T1)
–
(T4)
so that MV-algebras can be characterized with a ternary structure and the corresponding ternary operation is given. Finally, conclusions are draw in Section
7
where a table is presented with a comparison between the cases considered here.

Further observations and some more details are presented in the preprints
[
13
]
and
[
14
]
.

4.

A de Morgan algebra is a structure

x\circ(y\circ z)=(x\circ y)\circ z

(M2)

0\circ x=x

(M3)

\overline{\overline{x}}=x

(M4)

\overline{0}\circ x=\overline{0}

(M5)

x\circ\overline{x\circ\overline{y}}=y\circ\overline{y\circ\overline{x}}

Usually, the commutativity of the binary operation
\circ
is included as an axiom of MV-algebras. Nevertheless, Kolařík
[
23
]
proved that commutativity of
\circ
is a consequence of the other axioms. The canonical example of a MV-algebra consist of the set
X=[0,1]
and the operations
\bar{x}=1-x
and
x\circ y=min(x+y,1)
.

Next, some well-known properties of MV-algebras are presented.

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
(X,\circ,\overline{()},0)
be a MV-algebra, and consider the following notations:

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=\bar{0}

\displaystyle x\cdot y

\displaystyle=\overline{\bar{y}\circ\bar{x}}

\displaystyle x\vee y

\displaystyle=x\circ(y\cdot\bar{x})

\displaystyle x\wedge y

\displaystyle=(\bar{y}\circ x)\cdot y.

Then, the following properties hold:

•

x\circ y=y\circ x
and
x\cdot y=y\cdot x

•

x\circ\bar{x}=1
and
x\cdot\bar{x}=0

•

x\vee y=\overline{\bar{y}\wedge\bar{x}}

•

(X,\vee,\wedge,0,1)
is a distributive lattice

•

(X,\circ,\cdot,\overline{()},0,1)
is a Boolean algebra
\Leftrightarrow x\circ y=x\vee y

\Leftrightarrow x\circ x=x

•

x\circ(y\wedge z)=(x\circ y)\wedge(x\circ z)
and
x\cdot(y\vee z)=(x\cdot y)\vee(x\cdot z)
.

Proof.

See for instance
[
8
]
.
∎

Considering this properties and Lemma
2.5
, if a MV-algebra comes from a
(T1)
–
(T4)
ternary structure, then it is a Boolean algebra. This means that a ternary structure isomorphic to a general MV-algebra cannot contain the full
(T1)
–
(T4)
structure. We propose here to replace Axiom
(T4)
by some of its consequences, namely particular cases of properties (
11
) and (
14
). This implies that properties (
9
) and (
12
) will not apply in general and, consequently, the operations
\circ
and
\vee
will be different. We call the resulting structure a Ternary MV-algebra.

Definition 6.2
.

A Ternary MV-algebra is a system
(A,p,0,1)
consisting of a set
A
, together with a ternary operation
p:A\times A\times A\to A
and two constants
0,1\in A
satisfying:

(T1)

p(0,a,1)=a

(T2)

p(a,0,b)=a=p(b,1,a)

(T3)

p(a,b,a)=a

(T4-1)

p(a,p(b,c,1),1)=p(p(a,b,1),c,1)

(T4-2)

p(0,b,c)=\overline{p(\bar{c},\bar{b},1)}
and
p(1,b,c)=\overline{p(\bar{c},\bar{b},0)}

(TMV)

p(a,b,c)=p(p(\bar{b},\bar{a},0),p(0,c,b),1)

In the next propositions, it is shown that a Ternary MV-algebra is isomorphic to a MV-algebra.

Proposition 6.2
.

Let
(A,p,0,1)
be a Ternary MV-algebra.
Then, for
\bar{a}=p(1,a,0)
and
a\circ b=p(a,b,1)
, the structure
(A,\circ,\overline{()},0)
is a MV-algebra.

Proof.

It is clear that
0
and
1
are still complements of each other:

\bar{0}=p(1,0,0)\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{{\tiny\ref{C4}}}}}}{{=}}\ 1,\ \bar{1}=p(1,1,0)\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{{\tiny\ref{C4}}}}}}{{=}}\ 0
.

Now, we can prove the 5 axioms of Definition
6.1
:

(M1)
x\circ(y\circ z)=p(x,p(y,z,1),1)\ \,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{{\tiny{\bf(T4-1)}}}}}}{{=}}\ \ p(p(x,y,1),z,1)=(x\circ y)\circ z

(M2)
0\circ x=p(0,x,1)\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{{\tiny\ref{C1}}}}}}{{=}}\ x

(M3)
x\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{{\tiny\ref{C1}}}}}}{{=}}p(0,x,1)\ \,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{{\tiny{\bf(T4-2)}}}}}}{{=}}\ \ \overline{p(\bar{1},\bar{x},\bar{0})}\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{{\tiny\ref{C4}}}}}}{{=}}\ \overline{p(0,\bar{x},1)}\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{{\tiny\ref{C1}}}}}}{{=}}\ \overline{\overline{x}}

(M4)
1\circ x=p(1,x,1)\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{{\tiny\ref{C2}}}}}}{{=}}\,1

\displaystyle(\textrm{M}5)\,x\circ\overline{(x\circ\bar{y})}

\displaystyle=p(x,\overline{p(x,\bar{y},1)},1)\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{{\tiny{\bf(T4-2)}}}}}}{{=}}\ \,p(x,p(0,y,\bar{x}),1)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{{\tiny\ref{C4}}}}}}{{=}}\ p(p(x,\bar{1},0),p(0,y,\bar{x}),1)\ \ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{{\tiny{\bf(TMV)}}}}}}{{=}}\ \ p(1,\bar{x},y)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{{\tiny{\bf(T4-2)}}}}}}{{=}}\ \ \overline{p(\bar{y},x,0)}\ \ \,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{{\tiny{\bf(TMV)}}}}}}{{=}}\ \ \,\overline{p(p(\bar{x},y,0),p(0,0,x),1)}\qquad\quad

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{{\tiny\ref{C4}}}}}}{{=}}\ \overline{p(\bar{x},y,0)}\ \ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{{\tiny{\bf(T4-2)}}}}}}{{=}}\ \,p(1,\bar{y},x)

\displaystyle=y\circ\overline{(y\circ\bar{x})}.

∎

Proposition 6.3
.

Let
(A,\circ,\overline{()},0)
be a MV-algebra, and consider the usual dual operation
a\cdot b=\overline{\bar{b}\circ\bar{a}}
. Then, for

(41)

p(a,b,c)=((\bar{a}\circ\bar{b})\cdot a)\circ(b\cdot c),

the structure
(A,p,0,1)
is a Ternary MV-algebra.

Proof.

First, from (
41
) and using Proposition
6.1
, we observe the following correspondences.

p(1,a,0)=\bar{a}

p(0,a,b)=0\circ(a\cdot b)=a\cdot b

p(b,\bar{a},0)=(\bar{b}\circ a)\cdot b=a\wedge b

p(1,\bar{b},a)=b\circ(\bar{b}\cdot a)=b\circ(a\cdot\bar{b})=b\vee a=a\vee b

p(a,b,1)=((\bar{a}\circ\bar{b})\cdot a)\circ b=(\bar{b}\wedge a)\circ b=(\bar{b}\circ b)\wedge(a\circ b)=a\circ b.

Note that these results are compatible with definitions (
1
) to (
5
).
Now, we can prove the properties of
p
included in Definition
6.2
:

\displaystyle(\textrm{T}1)\,

\displaystyle p(0,a,1)=0\circ(a\cdot 1)=a

\displaystyle(\textrm{T}2)\,

\displaystyle p(a,0,b)=(1\cdot a)\circ 0=a\ \textrm{and}\ p(a,1,b)=(\bar{a}\cdot a)\circ b=0\circ b=b

\displaystyle(\textrm{T}3)\,p(a,b,a)

\displaystyle=((\bar{a}\circ\bar{b})\cdot a)\circ(b\cdot a)=(\overline{b\cdot a}\cdot a)\circ(b\cdot a)=(b\cdot a)\vee a

\displaystyle=(b\cdot a)\vee(1\cdot a)=(b\vee 1)\cdot a=1\cdot a=a

\displaystyle(\textrm{T4-1})\,p(a,p(b,c,1),1)

\displaystyle=a\circ(b\circ c)=(a\circ b)\circ c=p(p(a,b,1),c,1)\quad

(T4-2)

p(0,b,c)=b\cdot c=\overline{\bar{c}\circ\bar{b}}=\overline{p(\bar{c},\bar{b},1)}

p(1,b,c)=c\vee\bar{b}=\overline{b\wedge\bar{c}}=\overline{p(\bar{c},\bar{b},0)}

(TMV)

p(a,b,c)=((\bar{a}\circ\bar{b})\cdot a)\circ(b\cdot c)=(\bar{b}\wedge a)\circ(c\cdot b)=(a\wedge\bar{b})\circ(c\cdot b)

=p(p(\bar{b},\bar{a},0),p(0,c,b),1)
.

∎

Theorem 6.4
.

Ternary MV-algebras and MV-algebras are isomorphic.

Proof.

Let
(A,p,0,1)
be a Ternary MV-algebra and consider the operations
\bar{a}=p(1,a,0)
and
a\circ b=p(a,b,1)
. Then, by Proposition
6.2
, the structure
(A,\circ,\overline{()},0)
is a MV-algebra. By Proposition
6.3
, a new Ternary MV-algebra
(A,p^{\prime},0,1)
is recovered. We prove now that the new Ternary MV-algebra is equal to the original one i.e. that
p^{\prime}=p
:

\displaystyle p^{\prime}(a,b,c)

\displaystyle=((\bar{a}\circ\bar{b})\cdot a)\circ(b\cdot c)=(\bar{b}\wedge a)\circ(b\cdot c)

\displaystyle=(a\wedge\bar{b})\circ(c\cdot b)=p(p(\bar{b},\bar{a},0),p(0,c,b),1)=p(a,b,c).

Conversely, let us begin with a MV-algebra
(A,\circ,\overline{()},0)
. Then, using Proposition
6.3
, a Ternary MV-algebra
(A,p,0,1)
is obtain with

p(a,b,c)=((\bar{a}\circ\bar{b})\cdot a)\circ(b\cdot c)
which, by Proposition
6.2
, gives back a MV-algebra
(A,\circ^{\prime},\overline{()}^{\prime},0)
. We prove now that
\circ^{\prime}=\circ
and
\overline{()}^{\prime}=\overline{()}
:

\displaystyle a\circ^{\prime}b

\displaystyle=p(a,b,1)

\displaystyle=((\bar{a}\circ\bar{b})\cdot a)\circ b=(\bar{b}\wedge a)\circ b=(\bar{b}\circ b)\wedge(a\circ b)=a\circ b.

\displaystyle\bar{a}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=p(1,a,0)=((0\circ\bar{a})\cdot 1)\circ(a\cdot 0)=\bar{a}.

∎

To compare with equalities between binary operations observed in Boolean and de Morgan algebras, let us notice that, in a ternary MV-algebra, the following relations hold:

a\wedge b=p(a,\bar{b},b)\quad\textrm{and}\quad b\vee a=p(a,\bar{a},b).

Note also that in a Ternary MV-algebra, the notion of a sum is still well defined through
a+b=p(a,b,\bar{a})
, with
a+0=a=0+a
and
a+1=\bar{a}=1+a
.

7.
conclusion

We have presented examples of ternary structures that provide a common background for several classical algebras. It has long been recognized that De Morgan algebras with Boolean complements or MV-algebras with idempotency are Boolean algebras. However, other characteristics of Boolean algebras, like the existence of a sum leading to Boolean rings are not so easily generalized (see
[
6
]
for an example). Here, the sum defined as
x+y=p(x,y,\bar{x})
is well defined in all
structures derived from a ternary system and allows for a generalization of the notion of a ring. With respect to the algebras considered here, we observe the following table:

\begin{array}[]{c|c|c|c|c|c|}&T1-T3&T4&BC^{\dagger}&a\cdot a=a&LD^{*}\\
\hline\cr Boolean\ algebra&\checkmark&\checkmark&\checkmark&\checkmark&\checkmark\\
\hline\cr De\ Morgan\ algebra&\checkmark&\checkmark&\times&\checkmark&\times\\
\hline\cr MV-algebra&\checkmark&\times&\checkmark&\times&\times\\
\hline\cr Ring,char=2&\checkmark&\checkmark&\times&\times&\checkmark\\
\hline\cr Near\ ring,char=2&\checkmark&\checkmark&\times&\times&\times\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

†
BC means Boolean complement:
(1+a)\cdot a=0
.

∗
LD means left distributivity of
\cdot
over
+
:
a\cdot(b+c)=(a\cdot b)+(a\cdot c).

Much more can be done in the study of structures involving the operation
+
.

In Section
6
, we explained how the initial ternary structure
(T1)
–
(T4)
is generalized to allow for MV-algebras. Of course, the structure can be modified in other ways given other type of structures. With only
(T1)
–
(T3)
, the unary operation
\bar{x}=p(1,x,0)
is not an involution which could be a starting point to study a ternary version of Heyting algebras, for example.
