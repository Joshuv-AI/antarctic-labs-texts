Uniqueness for Some Mixed Problems of Nonlinear Elastostatics
Phoebus Rosakis

Abstract
We show that certain mixed displacement/traction problems (including live pressure tractions) of nonlinear elastostatics that are solved by a homogeneous deformation, admit no other classical equilibrium solution under suitable constitutive inequalities and domain boundary restrictions. This extends a well known theorem of Knops and Stuart on the pure displacement problem.



1
Introduction

Knops and Stuart 1984
have established global uniqueness of classical equilibrium solutions of the
homogeneous
displacement problem of nonlinear elastostatics (where boundary displacements are prescribed consistent with a given
homogeneous
deformation). They require the reference region
{\Omega}\subset{\mathbb{R}^{N}}
to be star shaped, the stored energy function
W
to be (globally) rank-one convex, and strictly quasiconvex at the prescribed homogeneous deformation (see Definition
2.1
). This seems to be the first global uniqueness result for nonlinear elastostatics under physically acceptable constitutive restrictions, stated here in Theorem
3.1
. For various uniqueness theorems for nonlinear elastostatics, see the works cited by
Knops and Stuart 1984
and
Sivaloganathan and Spector 2018
.

Here we provide uniqueness results analogous to that of
Knops and Stuart 1984
for the homogeneous mixed problem, where displacements and tractions (consistent with a given homogeneous deformation) are prescribed on two complementary subsets
{\mathcal{D}}
and
{\mathcal{L}}
of the boundary
{\partial\Omega}
, respectively.
After some preliminaries in Section
2
, we study in Section
3
the mixed displacement / dead-load traction problem.

Problem A
.

Given
F_{0}\in{\mathrm{Lin}_{+}}
, find a classical equilibrium
y:{\Omega}\to{\mathbb{R}^{N}}
subject to the boundary conditions of displacement

y(x)=F_{0}x\quad\forall x\in{\mathcal{D}}

and dead load traction

S({\nabla y})n=S(F_{0})n\quad\hbox{on }{\mathcal{L}}

where
n
is the unit outward normal on
{\partial\Omega}
.

Here
{\mathrm{Lin}_{+}}
contains all tensors with positive determinant,
S(F)=DW(F)
is the Piola stress, a classical equilibrium is a classically smooth deformation
y
such that
S({\nabla y})
is divergence free; see definition
2.1
. Uniqueness is addressed in Propositions
3.5
,
3.6
,
4.3
.

The presence of additional traction boundary conditions is dealt with using similar tools and methods as in
Knops and Stuart 1984
. In particular, Green’s Identity (Lemma
2.2
) plays a central role, but we require different restrictions on boundary geometry and more stringent constitutive inequalities.

While
{\Omega}
need not be star shaped, the boundary
{\partial\Omega}
is required to satisfy a
partition condition
(Definition
3.2
) that restricts how
{\partial\Omega}
splits into complementary subsets
{\mathcal{D}}
and
{\mathcal{L}}
, where displacements and tractions are prescribed. The Partition Condition is the requirement that
x\cdot n\geq 0
for
x\in{\mathcal{D}}
and
x\cdot n\leq 0
on
{\mathcal{L}}
, with
n
the outward unit normal at
x\in{\partial\Omega}
. Various examples are shown in Fig. 1 and discussed in Section
6.2
.

The stored energy
W
is required to be (sometimes strictly) rank-one convex, but quasiconvexity at the gradient
F_{0}
of the homogeneous deformation is apparently insufficient for uniqueness, due to the presence of traction conditions on part of
{\partial\Omega}
.

Instead, in most of our results we demand that
F_{0}
be a point of convexity of
W
. Although global convexity of
W:{\mathrm{Lin}_{+}}\to\mathbb{R}
is inappropriate for nonlinear elasticity (
Ball 1976
), nonconvex functions can have many points of convexity. In Section
6.3
we show a stored energy function of compressible Neo-Hookean type, in two dimensions, where all deformation gradients
F
with
\det F>1
are points of strict convexity of
W
, while if
\det F<1
,
F
is not even a point of convexity of
W
. In particular, states of uniaxial tension of a slightly tapered slender column as in Fig. 1g are covered by our uniqueness result, Proposition
4.3
, but uniaxial compression is not, in accordance with the expectation of nonuniqueness due to buckling.

We specialize
W
to be isotropic in Section
4
. This allows somewhat weaker convexity hypotheses at the homogeneous state, but these require a much more delicate proof. See Proposition
4.3
. As a result, natural states that are not points of strict convexity due to rotational invariance, uniaxial tension in three dimensions, also certain stored energies with multiple wells that are nonetheless polyconvex (with wells that are not rank-one connected) satisfy our hypotheses.

In Section
5
, we investigate a mixed displacement/Cauchy pressure problem, an example of live loads. The homogenenous deformation is now restricted to be a dilatation:
F_{0}=J_{0}^{1/N}I
with prescribed
J_{0}=\det F_{0}
.

Problem B
.

Given
J_{0}>0
, find a classical equilibrium
y
subject to the boundary conditions of displacement

y(x)=J_{0}^{1/N}x\quad\forall x\in{\mathcal{D}}

and traction

S({\nabla y})n=\varphi^{\prime}(J_{0})\cof({\nabla y})n\quad\hbox{on }{\mathcal{L}}

where
n
is the unit outward normal on
{\partial\Omega}
.

Here
\varphi(J):=W(J^{1/N}I)
for

is the restriction of the energy to dilatations.
The traction boundary condition on
{\mathcal{L}}
is equivalent to prescribing the Cauchy traction, in this case a hydrostatic pressure or tension corresponding to the given dilatation, on the deformed boundary
y({\mathcal{L}})
. This allows considerably weaker constitutive restrictions;
J_{0}
must be a point of convexity of
\varphi
. This is actually weaker than requiring
F_{0}
to be a point of convexity of
W
(Lemma
4.1
due to
Silhavy 2013
), in case the Cauchy stress is a pressure (negative principal Cauchy stress). See Proposition
5.3
for uniqueness of solutions to Problem B.

Section 6 contains a discussion of the various hypotheses we require for uniqueness and examples of regions
{\Omega}
, stored energies
W
and homogeneous states
F_{0}
that satisfy or violate them, in comparison with reasonable expectations for uniqueness. Specifically, we present an explicit example where uniqueness fails when the Partition Condition is omitted from our hypotheses.

We note that
Sivaloganathan and Spector 2018
have shown uniqueness of equilibria for the mixed problem with dead loads, with virtually no restrictions on
{\mathcal{D}}
and
{\mathcal{L}}
, while assuming uniform polyconvexity of
W
. On the other hand, their equilibria are restricted to satisfy additional conditions in the form of certain pointwise bounds. They also have various references on uniqueness. Under our (different) hypotheses, we show by example that our uniqueness results are false when the Partition Condition is omitted from our hypotheses; see Section
6.4
.

2
Preliminaries

Let
{\Omega}\subset{\mathbb{R}^{N}}
be a compact region with nonempty interior
\inter{\Omega}
and
{\partial\Omega}={\mathcal{D}}\cap{\mathcal{L}}
, where
{\mathcal{D}}
and
{\mathcal{L}}
are disjoint, piecewise smooth (hyper)surfaces and

is nonempty. The stored energy function is
W\in C^{2}({\mathrm{Lin}_{+}},\mathbb{R})
. Its derivative is the Piola stress tensor (more pedantically called the
first Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor
)

S(F)=DW(F)\quad\forall F\in{\mathrm{Lin}_{+}}.

Definition 2.1
.

(i) The stored energy function
W\colon{\mathrm{Lin}_{+}}\to\mathbb{R}
is rank-one convex provided

W(A)-W(F)\geq S(F)\cdot(A-F)

(2.1)

holds
\forall\;A
,
F\in{\mathrm{Lin}_{+}}
with
A-F=a\otimes b
,
a
,
b\in{\mathbb{R}^{N}}
. (ii)
F_{0}\in{\mathrm{Lin}_{+}}
is a point of convexity of
W
if (
2.1
) holds with
F=F_{0}
and
\forall A\in{\mathrm{Lin}_{+}}
.
(iii)
W
is quasiconvex at
F_{0}\in{\mathrm{Lin}_{+}}
provided

\int_{\Omega}W(F_{0}+\nabla v(x))dx\geq|{\Omega}|W(F_{0})\;\;\forall v\in C^{1}_{0}({\Omega},{\mathbb{R}^{N}})

(iv)A classical equilibrium is a mapping
y\in C^{2}(\inter{\Omega},{\mathbb{R}^{N}})\cap C^{1}(\bar{\Omega},{\mathbb{R}^{N}})
with
\nabla y\in{\mathrm{Lin}_{+}}
on
{\Omega}
, satisfying

\diver S(\nabla y)=0\hbox{ on }\inter{\Omega}.

(2.2)

Lemma 2.2
.

(Green’s Identity) If
y
is a classical equilibrium, then

N\int_{\Omega}W(\nabla y)dx=\int_{\partial\Omega}\Bigl\{(x\cdot n)W(\nabla y)+S(\nabla y)n\cdot[y-(\nabla y)x]\Bigr\}dS.

(2.3)

Proof.

Assume
y
is a classical equilibrium, so that the Eshelby tensor

P:=W(\nabla y)I-(\nabla y)^{T}S(\nabla y)

(2.4)

is divergence free, which implies that

\diver(P^{T}x)=\rm{tr}P=NW-S\cdot{\nabla y}.

Integrate this over
{\Omega}
, use the Divergence Theorem
and note that

\int_{\Omega}S\cdot\nabla ydx=\int_{\partial\Omega}Sn\cdot ydS

in view of (
2.2
), to obtain

N\int_{\Omega}W(\nabla y)dx=\int_{\partial\Omega}(Pn\cdot x+Sn\cdot y)dS

from which (
2.3
) follows after using (
2.4
).∎

5
The Mixed Displacement/Cauchy-Pressure Problem

Here we assume isotropy; we choose
F_{0}
to be a uniform dilatation and replace the dead-load conditions on
{\mathcal{L}}
by a Cauchy pressure condition. Define

\varphi(J):=W(J^{1/N}I)\quad\forall J>0

(5.1)

and let

{\mathcal{C}}=\{F|F\in{\mathrm{Lin}_{+}},\;F=\delta R,\;\delta>0,\;R\in{\mathrm{Orth}_{+}}\}

be the set of conformal deformation gradients,
so that
F\in{\mathcal{C}}\implies W(F)=\varphi({\rm det}F)
. The following result, due to
Mizel 1998
, shows the importance of the function
\varphi
in (
5.1
), essentially the restriction of the stored energy to dilatations.

Lemma 5.1
.

[
Mizel 1998
]. Given
J>0
let
{\mathcal{A}}_{J}=\{F|F\in{\mathrm{Lin}_{+}},\det F=J\}
.
If
W
is isotropic, strictly rank-one convex, and
W(F)\to\infty
as
|F|\to\infty
, then

W(F)>\varphi(J)\quad\forall F\in{\mathcal{A}}_{J}\backslash{\mathcal{C}}.

Remark 5.2
.

Actually
Mizel 1998
also requires
W(F)\to\infty
as
\det F\to 0
, however this is only needed in case of non-strict rank-one convexity and non-smooth
W
. In fact all that is necessary in the above lemma is that for each
J>0
,
W(F)\to\infty
as
|F|\to\infty
with
F\in{\mathcal{A}}_{J}
. In particular, it in not necessary that
W(\delta I)\to\infty
as
\delta\to\infty
(dilatations)

The Cauchy stress
T
is such that
S(F)=T(F)\cof F
, with
\cof F=\det(F)F^{-T}
for
F\in{\mathrm{Lin}_{+}}
. It is not difficult to show that for a dilatation with
F_{0}=J_{0}^{1/N}I
, so that
\det F_{0}=J_{0}>0
, the corresponding Cauchy stress is

T_{0}=T(F_{0})=\varphi^{\prime}(J_{0})I,

a hydrostatic pressure or tension. Moreover, for a smooth deformation
y
subject to Cauchy traction
T({\nabla y})n_{*}=T_{0}n_{*}
on
{\mathcal{L}}_{*}=y({\mathcal{L}})
(with
n_{*}
the unit outward normal to
{\mathcal{L}}_{*}
), the corresponding Piola traction on
{\mathcal{L}}
is

S({\nabla y})n=T_{0}\cof({\nabla y})n.

We now pose the mixed
displacement/Cauchy Pressure problem
:

Problem B
.

Given
J_{0}>0
, find a classical equilibrium
y
subject to the boundary conditions of displacement

y(x)=J_{0}^{1/N}x\quad\forall x\in{\mathcal{D}}

(5.2)

and traction

S({\nabla y})n=\varphi^{\prime}(J_{0})\cof({\nabla y})n\quad\hbox{on }{\mathcal{L}}

(5.3)

where
n
is the unit outward normal on
{\partial\Omega}
.

Uniqueness for this problem is addressed in

Proposition 5.3
.

Suppose that
W
is isotropic, and (i)
{\mathcal{D}}
and
{\mathcal{L}}
satisfy the Partition Condition (Definition
3.2
), (ii)
W
is strictly rank-one convex, (iii)
J_{0}
is a point of convexity of
\varphi
from (
5.1
), that is,

\varphi(J)\geq\varphi(J_{0})+\varphi^{\prime}(J_{0})(J-J_{0})\quad\forall J>0.

If
y
is an equilibrium solution of Problem B, then

y(x)=J_{0}^{1/N}x\quad\forall x\in{\Omega}.

Proof.

Define

{\widehat{W}}(F):=W(F)-\varphi^{\prime}(J_{0})(\hbox{det}F-J_{0})\quad\forall F\in{\mathrm{Lin}_{+}},

and

\widehat{\varphi}(J):={\widehat{W}}(J^{1/N}I)=\varphi(J)-\varphi(J_{0})-\varphi^{\prime}(J_{0})(J-J_{0})\quad\forall J>0.

Then
{\widehat{W}}
is also isotropic and strictly rank-one convex and satisfies

{\widehat{W}}(F)\geq{\widehat{W}}({\rm det}FI)=\widehat{\varphi}({\rm det}F)\geq\widehat{\varphi}(J_{0})={\widehat{W}}(J_{0}^{1/N}I)=0\quad\forall F\in{\mathrm{Lin}_{+}},

where we have used Lemma
5.1
and Hypotheses (ii) and (iii). Also in terms of the derivative of
{\widehat{W}}
,

\widehat{S}(F):=D{\widehat{W}}(F)=S(F)-\varphi^{\prime}(J_{0})\cof F,

(
5.3
) takes the form
\widehat{S}({\nabla y})n=0
on
{\mathcal{L}}
. Consequently Lemma
3.4
with
W
replaced by
{\widehat{W}}
and
F_{0}=J_{0}^{1/N}I
applies and ensures that for a classical equilibrium solution
y
of Problem 2,
{\widehat{W}}({\nabla y})={\widehat{W}}(F_{0})=\widehat{\varphi}(J_{0})=0
on
{\Omega}
.
But Lemma
5.1
demands that
{\widehat{W}}({\nabla y})>\widehat{\varphi}({\rm det}{\nabla y})
unless
{\nabla y}\in{\mathcal{C}}
.
As a result
y
is conformal. In addition, by (
3.6
)

{\nabla y}=J_{0}^{1/N}I
on
{\mathcal{D}}
.
Choose
z\in{\mathcal{D}}
with dist
(z,{\mathcal{L}})>\varepsilon>0
, let
B=B_{\varepsilon}(z)
(open ball) and extend
y
onto
B\cup{\Omega}

by letting
y(x)=J_{0}^{1/N}x
for
x\in B\backslash{\Omega}
.
Then
y\in C^{1}(B\cup{\Omega})
and conformal, hence the result follows from the Identity Theorem. ∎

6
Remarks and Examples

6.1
Implications

In continuation or bifurcation studies,
Healey and Simpson 1998
for example, existence of solution branches to a parameter-dependent problem is investigated. The initial state is usually chosen to be the reference state (or another homogeneous deformation). Its unqualified uniqueness can eliminate one of the Rabinowitz alternatives regarding boundedness of the solution branch; see
(
Knops and Stuart 1984
, Theorem 4.2)
. Our uniqueness results do precisely that for the mixed problem, in case
{\Omega}
is diffeomorphic to a spherical shell with the Partition Condition in force, so that the outside surface is
{\mathcal{D}}
and the inside surface is
{\mathcal{L}}
(e.g., Fig.1 b).

In some examples where the Partition Condition holds,
{\mathcal{D}}
comprizes two connected components (Fig. 1h,i). Here our results would seem to be violated by rotating one of the components of
{\mathcal{D}}
by an angle of
2\pi
about the vertical axis, while holding the other fixed (we suppose that
{\Omega}
is a solid of revolution about the vertical axis, Fig. 1h,i.) Actually, our results imply nonexistence of “twisted” classical solutions. If such equilibria exist, they must suffer a loss of classical smoothness, possibly with singularities developing at reentrant corners where
{\mathcal{D}}
and
{\mathcal{L}}
touch.

6.2
Partition Condition Examples

Our results apply to regions
{\Omega}
whose boundary satisfies the Partition Condition (Definition
3.2
). An obvious example is spherical shell, with
{\mathcal{D}}
the outside sphere (surface) and
{\mathcal{L}}
the inside one. More generally the difference of two regions,
{\Omega}={\Omega}_{2}\backslash{\Omega}_{1}
with
{\Omega}_{1}\subset{\Omega}_{2}\subset{\mathbb{R}^{N}}
and both star shaped with respect to the origin (Fig. 1a,b). Here
{\mathcal{D}}={\partial\Omega}_{2}
and
{\mathcal{L}}={\partial\Omega}_{1}
. The restriction
{\Omega}_{1}\subset{\Omega}_{2}
can be dropped however, see Fig. 1c.

Given any convex
{\Omega}
with the origin
O
outside it, each ray from
O
intersecting
\inter{\Omega}
will intersect
{\partial\Omega}
at two points. Let the point closest to
O
belong to
{\mathcal{L}}
and the farthest belong to
{\mathcal{D}}
(Fig. 1d,e). Otherwise if a ray touches
{\partial\Omega}
without intersecting the interior, the contact point can belong to either
{\mathcal{L}}
or
{\mathcal{D}}
.

More generally, given
any
(not necessarily star shaped)
{\Omega}
with piecewise smooth
{\partial\Omega}
and any choice of origin
\not\in{\Omega}
, construct
{\mathcal{D}}
and
{\mathcal{L}}
as follows: if a ray from the origin enters

{\Omega}
at
x_{0}\in{\partial\Omega}
(except corners), let
x_{0}\in{\mathcal{L}}
; if it leaves
{\Omega}
at
x_{0}\in{\partial\Omega}
, let
x_{0}\in{\mathcal{D}}
. If the ray is tangent to
{\partial\Omega}
at
x_{0}
, then the latter can belong to either subset (Fig. 1f) As a result the Partition Condition does not restrict the region
{\Omega}
(which need not be star shaped), but rather the way in which
{\partial\Omega}
is partitioned into
{\mathcal{D}}
and
{\mathcal{L}}
.

Another possibility is that
{\Omega}
is the union of two regions star shaped with respect to the origin (Fig. 1h,i).

The Partition Condition does exclude some important configurations, such as a cylindrical region with
{\mathcal{D}}
consisting of the two opposite ends and
{\mathcal{L}}
the lateral boundary, of relevance in modeling the uniaxial test. On the other hand, even the slightest tapering of the cylinder into a truncated cone, whose base is
{\mathcal{D}}
and the rest of
{\partial\Omega}
comprises
{\mathcal{L}}
, ensures that the Partition Condition holds (Fig. 1g).

6.3
Points of Convexity of the Stored Energy

While global convexity of
W
is incompatible with the presence of a natural state and frame indifference (
Ball 1976
), we merely require in Propositions
3.6
and
4.3
that the homogeneous deformation gradient
F_{0}
be a point of (sometimes strict) convexity of
W
which need not be globally convex. In Lemma
3.4
and Proposition
3.5
,
F_{0}
is assumed to be a natural state, which is a point of convexity. A point
F_{0}
of strict convexity of
W
as in Proposition
3.6
requires
{\lambda}^{0}
to be a point of strict convexity of
\Phi
and the strict version of (
4.2
). For
N=2
the latter allows states of uniaxial tension but excludes states of uniaxial compression. In particular, consider the compressible Neo-Hookean stored energy

W(F)=(1/2)|F|^{2}+(\det F)^{-\gamma}/\gamma\quad\forall F\in{\mathrm{Lin}_{+}}

(
N=2
,
\gamma\geq 1
). This is strongly elliptic, hence strictly rank-one convex. One shows that every state
F_{0}
with
\det F_{0}>1
is a point of strict convexity of
W
, whereas states with
\det F_{0}<1
are not even points of convexity. This is because the Hessian matrix of
\Phi({\lambda}_{1},{\lambda}_{2})=({\lambda}_{1}^{2}+{\lambda}_{2}^{2})/2+({\lambda}_{1}{\lambda}_{2})^{-\gamma}/\gamma
is positive-definite on
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}
, whereas
\partial\Phi/\partial{\lambda}_{1}+\partial\Phi/\partial{\lambda}_{2}>0
if and only if
{\lambda}_{1}{\lambda}_{2}>1
, as easily verified. This includes all states of uniaxial tension with
\partial\Phi/\partial{\lambda}_{1}=0
and
\partial\Phi/\partial{\lambda}_{2}>0
, whereupon Proposition
3.6
applies. In contrast, uniaxial compression states (
\partial\Phi/\partial{\lambda}_{1}=0
,
\partial\Phi/\partial{\lambda}_{2}<0
) are not covered by Proposition
3.6
, as they violate (
4.2
), hence they are not points of convexity of
W
. It should be emphasized that this is consistent with our expectations of non-uniqueness, due to the possibility of
buckling
of the tapered column in Fig. 1g under uniaxial compression loads in the vertical direction.

For isotropic materials, strict convexity of
W
at
F_{0}
is slightly relaxed in Proposition
4.3
. Instead, we require that the singular value list
{\lambda}^{0}
of
F_{0}
be an
isolated point of convexity
of
\Phi
in (
4.1
) and Definition
4.2
, and also that (
4.2
) hold. This can include uniaxial tension in three dimensions, but will always exclude uniaxial compression. The natural state of an isotropic
W
is now allowed, although it is not a point of strict convexity of
W
because of frame indifference.

Proposition
5.3
shows uniqueness for Problem B, where traction boundary conditions equivalent to Cauchy hydrostatic pressure or tension are imposed on
{\mathcal{L}}
(Eq. (
5.3
)). Hypothesis (iii) here is weaker than that of Proposition
4.3
. Suppose
J_{0}
is a point of convexity of
\varphi
(see (
5.1
)) in the case of pressure, namely
\varphi^{\prime}(J_{0})<0
. Then
F_{0}=J_{0}^{1/N}I
is
not
a point of convexity of
W
, as it violates (
4.2
), all principal stresses being equal and negative. However, uniqueness still holds for Problem B though not necessarily for Problem A which prescribes the Piola traction due to the specified dilatation. Here (
3.2
) becomes

S({\nabla y})n=J_{0}^{(N-1)/N}\varphi^{\prime}(J_{0})n\hbox{ on }{\mathcal{L}},

which is different from (
5.3
).

Convexity of
\varphi
does not follow from strict rank-one convexity of
W
, or even strong ellipticity. An example with
\varphi
having the form of a two-well potential despite
W
being strictly polyconvex, is given by
Rosakis and Simpson 1994
. This stored energy has two distinct dilatations that are natural states. They are the global minima, hence points of convexity of
\varphi
of (
5.1
). As a result, Proposition
5.3
still applies to
F_{0}
chosen to be one of them, with null traction specified on
{\mathcal{L}}
.

6.4
Partition Condition Omission and Nonuniqueness

We provide an example of nonuniqueness in case the Partition Condition is omitted from our hypotheses. It is well known that the pure dead-load traction problem suffers from lack of uniqueness, so we consider a genuine mixed problem, where both
{\mathcal{D}}
and
{\mathcal{L}}
have nonempty relative interior.

Let
N=2
and
{\Omega}
be the annulus
\{x|x\in{\mathbb{R}^{2}},\;\;1\leq x\leq 5\}
. Suppose
{\mathcal{D}}
is the inside circle
|x|=1
and
{\mathcal{L}}
the exterior one
|x|=5
, whereby both inequalities of the Partition Condition are reversed (violated). Choose

W(F)=\sqrt{|F|^{2}+2\det F}+\frac{k}{2}\Bigl(\det F-\frac{1+k}{k}\Bigr)^{2}\quad\forall F\in{\mathrm{Lin}_{+}},

(6.1)

with
k>0
a constant.
This energy is isotropic; it is strongly elliptic as it satisfies

(
Knowles and Sternberg 1978
, Eq. (1.35))
,
hence strictly rank-one convex, and polyconvex by

(
Rosakis and Simpson 1994
, Proposition 3.1)
.
Moreover, for
k\geq 2
it is globally minimized on
{\mathrm{Lin}_{+}}
at
{\mathrm{Orth}_{+}}
, while
S(I)=0
for any
k>0
. We impose
y(x)=x
for
|x|=1
and
S({\nabla y})x/5=0
for
|x|=5
. This is consistent with null Piola traction conditions (
3.2
) for Problem A and also null Cauchy pressure conditions (
5.3
) for Problem B. The natural state
F_{0}=I
minimizes
W
together with all rotations provided
k\geq 2
. Also
(1,1)
is an isolated point of convexity of

\Phi({\lambda}_{1},{\lambda}_{2})=W(\rm{diag}({\lambda}_{1},{\lambda}_{2}))={\lambda}_{1}+{\lambda}_{2}+\frac{k}{2}\Bigl({\lambda}_{1}{\lambda}_{2}-\frac{1+k}{k}\Bigr)^{2},\quad{\lambda}_{i}>0,\;i\in\{1,2\}

(Definition
4.2
). In addition, the dilatation energy function (
5.1
) now becomes

\varphi(J)=2\sqrt{J}+\frac{k}{2}\Bigl(J-\frac{1+k}{k}\Bigr)^{2}.

For
k\geq 2
,
\varphi(J)
has a global minimum at
J=1
, which is therefore a point of its convexity (although
\varphi
is not globally convex). As a result,
F_{0}=I
with

satisfies Hypothesis (iii) of Propositions
4.1
,
4.3
and

y(x)=x
for all

We construct another radial solution of the form

r=|x|\in[1,5]
, the homogeneous one corresponding to

{\nabla y}

(6.2)

\Phi_{i}(r)=\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial{\lambda}_{i}}\bigl(R^{\prime}(r),R(r)/r\bigr).

As is well known,

(6.4)

to be stress free. Here

where we have used (
6.2

(6.6)

and

(6.7)

Note that

We require

R(r)=\sqrt{r^{2}/20+19/20}\;

We remark that when we interchange
