Temporal trends in sepsis hospitalisations and mortality in Aotearoa New Zealand, 2000–2019: a population-based study
Authors: Sharla McTavish, Jane Zhang, June Atkinson, Amanda Kvalsivg, Alice Hyun Min Kim, Colin McArthur, Paul Huggan, Michael G. Baker
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundGlobally, sepsis remains a substantial public health concern. To inform policy development and interventions for sepsis prevention, treatment, and surveillance in Aotearoa New Zealand (Aotearoa), we describe population-level epidemiological trends in hospitalised sepsis and mortality over two decades by major sociodemographic characteristics.MethodsWe undertook a retrospective, population-based descriptive study of all sepsis-associated hospitalisation (SAH) in Aotearoa between 2000 and 2019 using previously published case identification strategies and International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) codes to identify SAHs in the National Minimum Dataset. Incidence and mortality rates, and case fatality risks were calculated along with analyses of trends over the 20-year observation period. Rate estimates were standardised to the age structure of the total population identified in the 2018 New Zealand Census.FindingsWe identified 266,105 unique SAH cases and 27,402 associated deaths, with an average age-standardised incidence rate (ASIR) of 320.4 admissions per 100,000 population and an age-standardised mortality rate (ASMR) of 34.2 deaths per 100,000 population. An average annual ASIR increase of 4.5% was observed, from 217.3 admissions in 2000 to 386.9 admissions per 100,000 population in 2019. ASMR decreased annually from 37.1 deaths in 2000 to 31.3 deaths per 100,000 population in 2019. Case fatality risk decreased from 15.9 deaths in 2000 to 8.1 deaths per 100 admissions in 2019, primarily driven by improvements in patient survival in ≥40-year age groups. Significant SAH incidence and mortality disparities were observed between patient populations, particularly by age, ethnicity and socioeconomic deprivation. Māori and Pacific Peoples had SAH incidence rates that were 1.7 and 2.3 times, respectively, higher than those of NMPA ethnicity, and had mortality rates that were 1.6 and 1.7 times higher. Similarly, those living in areas of high socioeconomic deprivation were twice as likely to develop SAH, and their SAH mortality rate was twice as high as that of those living in areas of the least socioeconomic deprivation.InterpretationSepsis is common in Aotearoa and makes a substantial contribution to population mortality. Significant variation in incidence and mortality rate estimates was observed between patient populations, with higher rates in those <1 year and ≥70 years, of Māori or Pacific ethnicity, and those living in areas of high socioeconomic deprivation. Our findings highlight the burden of SAH and the need for targeted interventions to address sepsis prevention, surveillance and support for sepsis survivors in Aotearoa.FundingHealth Research Council of New Zealand.

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We searched OVID and PubMed to identify previous longitudinal trend studies of sepsis in Aotearoa, without language or patient population restrictions, between 1990 and 2024. We used the following search terms: (“sepsis” OR “septicaemia” OR “septic shock” OR “severe sepsis”) AND (“trend” OR “trends” OR “longitudinal trends” OR “temporal”) AND (“New Zealand” OR “Aotearoa New Zealand”) AND/OR (“mortality” OR “incidence” OR “mortality rates” OR “incidence rates” OR “case-fatality” OR “case fatality” OR “survival”). Additionally, we searched Google Scholar between 1990 and 2024, using the same search terms. Existing literature reported epidemiological rate estimates for sepsis-associated hospitalisations (SAH) in Aotearoa, primarily restricted by age groups, pre-existing comorbidities, geographical region, hospital site or treatment setting within a hospital. Only one study reported estimates for all SAH events over five years, but was restricted to a single geographical region. No studies reported longitudinal incidence and mortality rate or case fatality risk estimates for all SAH events in Aotearoa.

Added value of this study

Our study is the first to report population-level temporal epidemiological trends for all publicly funded SAHs in Aotearoa, providing a baseline for future research and policy development. By using a national, mandatory reporting administrative database to identify a study population of all publicly funded SAHs and associated deaths spanning two decades, we were able to report a significant and consistent increase in ASIR, minimally decreasing ASMR, and a significantly decreased CFR between 2000 and 2019. We reported substantial variation in ASIR, ASMR and CFR between patient populations, particularly by age, ethnicity and socioeconomic deprivation. In addition, we identified consistent and substantial long-term ASIR and ASMR trends between patient populations, particularly by ethnicity and socioeconomic deprivation.

Implications of all the available evidence

Our findings indicate SAH is increasingly common in Aotearoa and is strongly influenced by the social determinants of health, particularly ethnicity and socioeconomic deprivation, representing an ongoing public health concern. Additionally, we observed that the majority of septic patients survived to hospital discharge, with a significant decrease in CFR over the study period, particularly for patient populations ≥70 years. Combined with the stark disparities between patient populations, increasing survival to hospital discharge over the last two decades highlights the urgent need for long-term policy planning and intervention implementation for sepsis prevention, sepsis survivor support and ongoing public health surveillance of sepsis in Aotearoa.

Introduction

Sepsis, defined as organ failure due to immune dysregulation in response to infection,1 is a common cause of morbidity and mortality, particularly amongst populations living with socioeconomic deprivation and ethnic marginalisation.2 A life-threatening but potentially treatable syndrome, sepsis is globally estimated to be responsible for one in five deaths, with approximately 48.5 million sepsis-associated cases reported in 2017.3 In countries with developed healthcare systems, the incidence and mortality associated with ICU-treated sepsis across patient populations are well reported.4 However, the incidence and outcome of total sepsis-associated hospitalisation (SAH) is likely to provide a better estimate of the burden of sepsis in these countries, yet few studies report such estimates, and none in Aotearoa New Zealand (Aotearoa).

Emerging evidence, focused primarily on hospitalised sepsis patient populations stratified by age at a regional level or individual hospital sites in Aotearoa, has indicated an increase in SAH rates in Māori, the Indigenous peoples of Aotearoa, and Pacific ethnicities, and those living in areas of high socioeconomic deprivation.5,6 It has been postulated that modifiable and non-modifiable exposures,7 and chronic morbidity8 differences between patient populations may account for the observed incidence disparities between these populations.9 With an ageing population, increasing prevalence of predisposing factors such as diabetes mellitus,10 substantial multimorbidity11 and persistent health disparities between patient populations in Aotearoa12 SAH may also be increasing. Thus, we describe the incidence and impact of sepsis in Aotearoa by estimating population-level SAH incidence, mortality rates, and case fatality risk over two decades, by major sociodemographic characteristics.

Methods

Study approval

This study was approved by the University of Otago Ethics Committee (Study ID: HD20/075, 25/01/2023). The Ngāi Tahu Research Consultation Committee was also consulted to ensure appropriate data use and cultural safety. We followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.13 As the retrieved administrative data was de-identified and the current study had no direct patient involvement, patient consent was not required.

Data sources

All de-identified hospitalisation data used in the current analysis were derived from the National Minimum Dataset (NMDS) collated by the Health New Zealand (Te Whatu Ora). From 1999 onwards, the NMDS has collected relevant hospitalisation data (demographic, clinical and mortality) related to all publicly funded hospital discharges. Hospitalisation data retrieved consisted of principal and additional diagnoses coded with the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, 10th revision, Australian Modification (ICD-10-AM). The population denominator data used in the current study were retrieved from StatsNZ, as identified in the 2018 New Zealand Census.

Definitions and filtering

Sepsis-associated hospitalisation (SAH) cases were identified in the NMDS using previously published identification strategies and ICD-10 codes, and characterised as explicit or implicit based on ICD-10-AM codes comparable to those used by Rudd et al. (2020) in a global analysis of sepsis epidemiology.3,14 Explicit SAH cases were hospitalisations that were identified if a specific sepsis-associated ICD-10-AM code was reported either as a principal or additional diagnosis code. Implicit SAH cases were identified if the hospitalisation had an infectious ICD-10-AM code as the primary diagnosis code, plus at least one organ dysfunction code as an additional diagnosis code. ICD-10-AM codes used to identify explicit and implicit SAHs in the current study can be found in Supplementary Table S1.

We assessed all identified SAHs for the 20-year period from 01 January 2000 to 31 December 2019. The data retrieved from the NMDS were filtered to identify unique SAHs by removing duplicate admissions, site transfers, readmissions for the same incident hospitalisation, and overseas visitors. Filtering steps and rationale are identified in Supplementary Table S4. Death was defined as deceased at hospital discharge from the SAH from any cause. A full description of major sociodemographic characteristics used to categorise patient populations, age, gender, ethnicity and socioeconomic status, can be found in the Supplementary Material. Briefly, we used previously published methods to describe patient populations by ethnicity, gender and socioeconomic status.15,16 As per Ministry of Health (Manatū Hauora) ethnicity and sex/gender data protocols, we categorised ethnicities into prioritised ethnicity groups: Māori > Pacific > Asian > Non-Māori/Pacific/Asian (NMPA), and gender as male, or female.15,17 Socioeconomic status was identified using the 2018 New Zealand Deprivation Index (NZDep) and categorised by quintile of exposure to socioeconomic deprivation (NZDep Q1, lowest to NZDep Q5, highest).16

Statistical analyses

Data were analysed using SAS v 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc. USA). Descriptive statistics are reported as frequencies (n) or per cent (%). We generated age-standardised incidence rates (ASIR) and mortality rates (ASMR) for sepsis hospitalisations, based on the age structure of the total population usually resident in Aotearoa in the 2018 New Zealand Census. We reported incidence and mortality rate estimates as admissions or deaths per 100,000 population per year (per 100,000 population). Rate differences between patient populations by age, gender, ethnicity and socioeconomic status are reported as incidence (IRR) or mortality (MRR) rate ratios. Case fatality risk (CFR) was not age-standardised and is reported as deaths per 100 admissions.

We used three measurements to describe incidence and mortality rate, and case fatality risk temporal trends between 2000 and 2019: (1) absolute (n or rate differences) and relative (%) changes in incidence and mortality rates, case fatality risk, and event frequencies by ten-year period (2000–2009 and 2010–2019); (2) the average annual percentage change in ASIR and ASMR between 2000 and 2019; (3) incidence and mortality rate estimate differences between patient population groups stratified by prioritised ethnicity and NZDep quintile by five-year periods between 2000 and 2019.

Role of the funding source

The funders of the current study had no role in the study design, data collection, data analysis, interpretation or writing of the manuscript.

Incidence

The ASIR for SAHs was 320.4 admissions per 100,000 population, which increased on average by 4.5% annually from 217.3 admissions in 2000 to 386.9 admissions per 100,000 population in 2019 (Table 1, Figs. 1 and 2). Significant ASIR variation was observed between patient populations by age, prioritised ethnicity, and socioeconomic variation (Table 2, Fig. 3, Supplementary Figs. S4, S5, S7 & S11). Limited variation was observed in ASIR by gender (Supplementary Fig. S9). The highest ASIRs were observed for patient populations aged <1 year and ≥70 years, with 1340.9 and 1492.7 admissions per 100,000 population (Table 2, Supplementary Figs. S4 & S5), respectively. Māori and Pacific Peoples were 1.66 (95% CI: 1.65, 1.67) and 2.31 (95% CI: 2.29, 2.33) times more likely to experience a SAH than NMPA ethnicities. Similarly, those patients usually resident in NZDep Q5 areas were twice as likely to experience a SAH than those usually resident in NZDep Q1 areas (IRR: 1.97, 95% CI: 1.96, 1.98).

Mortality

We identified 27,402 all-cause deaths (deaths) during SAHs (Fig. 1, Table 1), representing 4.6% of the 593,787 deaths occurring in Aotearoa between 2000 and 2019 (27,402/593,787). SAHs identified by explicit sepsis ICD-10-AM codes represented 73.3% (20,096/27,402) of all deaths in the current study (Supplementary Table S10). Case fatality risk decreased significantly from 15.9 deaths in 2000 to 8.1 deaths per 100 admissions in 2019, particularly for patient populations aged ≥70 years (Fig. 2, Supplementary Fig. S5). Average annual ASMR also decreased from 37.2 deaths in 2000 to 31.3 deaths per 100,000 population in 2019 (Fig. 2).

Significant variation in ASMRs between patient populations by all major demographic characteristics was observed (Table 2, Fig. 3, Supplementary Figs. S4, S5, S7, S9 & S11). The highest mortality rates were in patient populations aged <1 year, ≥70 years, those of Māori or Pacific ethnicities, and those usually resident in NZDep Q5 areas (Table 2). Compared to patient populations of NMPA ethnicities, Māori and Pacific Peoples were 1.59 (95% CI: 1.55, 1.62) and 1.72 (95% CI: 1.67, 1.77) times more likely to die during SAH, respectively (Fig. 3B). In addition, Asian patient populations were 0.62 (95% CI: 0.60, 0.64) times less likely to die from SAH than those of NMPA ethnicities. Patient populations usually resident in NZDep Q5 areas were 1.95 (95% CI: 1.91, 1.99) times more likely to die compared to those who usually resided in NZDep Q1 areas (Fig. 3D). Males were 1.28 (95% CI: 1.27, 1.30) times more likely to die than females.

Discussion

Principal findings

This study confirmed the large health impact of sepsis in Aotearoa. It identified over 260,000 unique sepsis hospitalisations over two decades, which resulted in 27,402 deaths, almost five per cent of total mortality over that period. While annual incidence in Aotearoa has increased significantly, associated mortality remained comparatively stable (Table 1, Fig. 2). Consequently, we saw case fatality risk decreased significantly over the study period, particularly for patient populations ≥70 years. By all demographic descriptors, we identified substantial variation in incidence and mortality rate estimates between patient populations, with significant and sustained disparities observed by ethnicity and socioeconomic deprivation (Table 2, Fig. 3). These findings shed new light on the epidemiology of sepsis-associated hospitalisations in Aotearoa, providing a comprehensive baseline for future research and policy development.

Comparison with other studies

Our study is the first comprehensive population-level study to describe long-term temporal trends in the total burden of hospitalised sepsis and associated health inequity in Australasia. Previous epidemiological studies characterising SAHs in Aotearoa have reported estimates for patient populations restricted by age group, comorbidity, regions or treatment site.5,18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24 Additionally, previous epidemiological estimates for sepsis in Aotearoa have been reported as binational estimates in conjunction with Australia, predominantly based on clinical data retrieved from the Australia New Zealand Intensive Care Society adult or paediatric intensive care patient registries, making it difficult to estimate incidence or mortality rates for Aotearoa. One study reported incidence and case-fatality risk for all SAHs at a regional site in Aotearoa over a five-year period, extrapolating population-level estimates from the study data.5 Similar to our observation of increasing annual incidence, Huggan et al. (2017) reported a significant increase in SAH rates over 5 years at a tertiary hospital in the Waikato region, with similar patterns of incidence disparities between patient populations by age, ethnicity and socioeconomic deprivation.5

Internationally reported population-level temporal trends for the incidence, mortality, and case fatality risk estimates for hospitalised sepsis are limited. This is largely due to differences in study design, including consensus definition changes, differences in ICD editions, duration of study periods, and the choice of ICD codes used to identify SAHs, making direct comparisons between studies difficult. Although the reasons for the trend changes observed in our study are likely to be multifactorial, including demographic shifts, advancements in medical care and changes to ICD coding ie the retiring of codes and aligning of sepsis specific codes to the Sepsis-3 consensus definition, the overall incidence and case fatality risk trends are consistent with previously reported international temporal trends.25, 26, 27, 28, 29, 30, 31

We observed a 4.5% average annual increase in incidence rate over the study period, comparable to that observed in studies from Germany,32 Spain,28 Taiwan and Australia,25 but less than that reported for the United States (USA)30,31,33 and China.26 In addition, our overall ASIR estimate is most similar to the European range (87–286 cases per 100,000 population), but substantially lower than reported incidence in Australia (1163 cases per 100,000 population).25,28,29,32 Over the study period, CFR decreased from 15.9% in 2000 to 8.1% in 2019, consistent with trends observed in other population-level studies. Our overall CFR estimate of 10.3 deaths per 100 admissions (10.3%) was similar to that reported in Australia at 12.0%, but substantially less than the 19–50% range reported by other population-level studies.25,28,29,32,33 This variation in observed case fatality risk may reflect differences in study design, coding practices, geographic access to healthcare and healthcare system capacity.

Our analysis of temporal epidemiological trends for SAHs in Aotearoa by age and gender is also consistent with previous studies. We observed higher incidence and mortality rates, and case fatality risk in patient populations at both ends of the age spectrum, with the most significant annual change occurring in those ≥70 years. The substantial changes observed in incidence and mortality rates, and case fatality risk amongst patients ≥70 years are likely due to multiple reasons, which are outside the scope of the current study and require further investigation. Similar to previous longitudinal studies, we also observed consistently higher mortality rates among male patients compared to female patient populations, potentially due to the immunomodulatory effects of sex hormones.34

Strengths & limitations

The current study has several strengths, including the use of previously published SAH case identification strategies and ICD-10 codes, the retrieval of SAH data from a national administrative database and the length of the study period. Due to the inherent difficulties of identifying sepsis cases in administrative databases without a specific, gold-standard test for sepsis, our method followed that of Angus et al. (2001), which identified SAHs using explicit and implicit sepsis-associated ICD-10 codes in administrative health databases consistent with clinical sepsis diagnoses.14 In addition, we used ICD-10 codes identified by the largest global epidemiological study to date to report sepsis incidence and mortality estimates, as a framework for identifying appropriate ICD-10-AM codes.3

A major strength of our analysis is the use of the NMDS to identify SAHs. The NMDS is a population-level administrative database that collates discharge data from all public hospitalisation events and those private hospitalisation events funded by the public healthcare system in Aotearoa since 1999, which has previously been validated for epidemiological research of this type.35 Thus, our estimates are likely to represent the majority of unique, publicly funded SAHs in Aotearoa occurring between 2000 and 2019. Further, our analysis is strengthened by the length of our study, which was able to report population-level estimates of SAH incidence and mortality rates, and case-fatality risk trends over 20 years by major sociodemographic characteristics, enabling the identification of significant and sustained disparities between patient populations. In addition, our study presents the increase in SAHs in the form of numbers and population rates, which effectively adjusts for natural population increases; thus the 4.5% average annual increase in ASIR SAHs is likely a true estimate increase.

Few international studies have reported on temporal epidemiological trends in SAH by ethnicity or socioeconomic deprivation. Similar to a study conducted in Australia,25 we observed that Indigenous and other ethnically or socioeconomically marginalised patient populations consistently experienced higher incidence and mortality rates than non-marginalised patient populations. Our findings were also consistent with a longitudinal population-based study conducted in the USA characterising sepsis-associated mortality, including deaths occurring outside of the hospital setting, which found significant disparities between Indigenous and ethnically marginalised, and non-Indigenous, non-ethnically marginalised patient populations and those living in areas of high socioeconomic deprivation compared to the least socioeconomic deprivation.36

Our analysis has several limitations. Similar to previous studies, we limited implicit SAHs to those where infection-specific ICD-10-AM codes were the primary reason for hospitalisation, and where secondary codes were also present to indicate sepsis-related organ dysfunction. As a result, our estimates may have undercounted SAHs if the primary reason for hospitalisation was not infection-related and if explicit sepsis ICD-10-AM codes were not used. Further, our study is confined to unique SAHs, and we did not collect data on non-hospitalised, “untreated” sepsis cases in Aotearoa. Thus, our findings can not be extrapolated to all sepsis cases in Aotearoa over the study period. This limitation is particularly important for mortality estimates, as some people who develop sepsis will die before hospitalisation. For example, a US study found that around 13% of sepsis-related deaths occur outside of the hospital setting, in nursing homes and private residences.37

Additionally, NMDS data does not provide detailed clinical information about individual SAHs, such as treatment strategy, the severity of organ dysfunction and clinical sepsis scores. Such information may have further informed the analysis of sepsis burden and outcome disparities between patient populations. Nevertheless, this extensive interrogation over 20 years of a national hospital discharge database provides useful insights into the total burden of hospital-treated sepsis in Aotearoa, with the strengths and limitations of using this large national data collection. Future research could address the surveillance limitations described above. These existing data would benefit from further detailed analysis, including examining spatial variations in the distribution of SAH. Probably the highest priority would be to investigate opportunities to modify this disease burden, including primary prevention and improved early recognition and treatment.

Conclusions

From 2000 to 2019, sepsis-associated hospitalisations increased significantly, presenting a substantial ongoing public health concern in Aotearoa. Sustained significant incidence and mortality disparities were observed between patient populations by age, ethnicity and socioeconomic deprivation. Additionally, we observed a substantial decrease in case fatality risk across the study period, particularly for patient populations ≥70 years. Our findings highlight the burden of hospital-treated sepsis in Aotearoa and the need for targeted interventions to address sepsis prevention and public health surveillance.
