Design switching on graphs
Ferdinand Ihringer, Robin Simoens

Abstract
We show that each ( r , λ ) (r,\lambda) -design yields a class of switching methods that can be used produce cospectral graphs. We use this to explain several specific switching methods such as Godsil-McKay (GM) switching and Wang-Qiu-Hu (WQH) switching.



1
Introduction

One of the central problems in spectral graph theory is to decide whether a graph is determined by its spectrum.
Haemers’ conjecture
[
12
,
17
]
says that almost all graphs are determined
by their spectrum. This claim is backed up by several computer experiments
[
8
,
33
]
,
but a proof seems out of reach. In contrast to Haemers’ conjecture for the class of all graphs, we know that, for particular types of graphs, almost all of them (within that class) are not determined by their spectrum. Examples are trees
[
16
,
28
]
, strongly regular graphs
[
14
]
and cographs
[
31
]
.

A very successful technique in this context has been the method of
switching
.
Informally speaking, a switching for graph is a local modification of the graph from which we obtain a new graph with some properties left invariant. We are concerned
with switchings which produce
cospectral graphs
: graphs with the same spectrum. Two of the most successful
techniques in this context are
Godsil-McKay (GM) switching

[
16
]
and
Wang-Qiu-Hu (WQH) switching

[
32
]
. Recently, more
switchings in this vibe have been defined, for instance
Abiad-Haemers (AH) switching

[
3
]
and
Fano switching

[
3
]
. The first goal of this work is to present a unified framework for all these switching techniques.

There are plenty of constructions of strongly regular graphs from known strongly regular graphs by local modifications.
Often, “linear algebra” explanations in terms of switching make constructions more elegant and the underlying general principles clearer than combinatorial proofs. For instance, compare
[
19
]
and
[
20
]
or
[
13
]
and
[
25
]
.
We believe that our result has the right generality to do so conveniently. In particular, we give a linear algebra explanation for why the switching method due to Kantor
[
6
,
23
]
gives cospectral graphs. There is also a clear connection with the concept of a
trade
in a design, which we discuss later.

A key concept in this work is that of
(r,\lambda)
-designs.
An
(r,\lambda)
-design
is an incidence structure
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{B})
such that

•

every point is contained in exactly
r
blocks, and

•

every pair of points is included in exactly
\lambda
blocks.

Let
J_{v}
denote the
v\times v
all-one-matrix.
Our main result is the following.

Theorem 1

(Design switching)
.

Consider two
(r,\lambda)
-designs
\mathcal{D}_{1}
and
\mathcal{D}_{2}
on
v
points and
b
blocks, whose respective incidence matrices
N_{1}
and
N_{2}
satisfy
N_{1}^{T}N_{1}=N_{2}^{T}N_{2}
.
Define
R=\frac{1}{r-\lambda}\left(N_{1}N_{2}^{T}-\lambda J_{v}\right)
.
Let
\Gamma
be a graph with a subgraph
C
whose
v
vertices are identified both with the points of
\mathcal{D}_{1}
and those of
\mathcal{D}_{2}
.
Suppose that:

(i)

The adjacency matrix
A_{C}
of
C
has the property that
R^{T}A_{C}R
is an adjacency matrix.

(ii)

For every vertex
x\notin C
, its neighbours in
C
form a block of
\mathcal{D}_{1}
.

Make every vertex
x\notin C
that is adjacent to the points of the
i
th block of
\mathcal{D}_{1}
, adjacent to the points of the
i
th block of
\mathcal{D}_{2}
. Replace
C
by the subgraph with adjacency matrix
R^{T}A_{C}R
. The resulting graph is
\mathbb{R}
-cospectral with
\Gamma
.

Let us comment on this result.

Remark 2
.

(1)

We allow designs with repeated blocks (non-simple designs) and blocks may have different sizes. In particular, one can always add the empty block and the block containing all points to both designs, without changing the matrix
R
. Hence a vertex
x\notin C
may also be adjacent to all vertices of
C
or no vertices of
C
. Adding the complement of every block to the design, preserves
R
, so condition (ii) can be relaxed to:

(ii’)

Every vertex
x\notin C
is adjacent to

(a)

all vertices of
C
, or

(b)

no vertices of
C
, or

(c)

the vertices of
C
contained in a block of
\mathcal{D}_{1}
, or

(d)

the vertices of
C
not contained in a block of
\mathcal{D}_{1}
.

(2)

The condition
N_{1}^{T}N_{1}=N_{2}^{T}N_{2}
on the incidence matrices can be stated combinatorially as follows: the blocks are ordered in such a way that
|B_{1i}\cap B_{1j}|=|B_{2i}\cap B_{2j}|
for any two (possibly the same) blocks
B_{1i}
and
B_{1j}
of
\mathcal{D}_{1}
and their corresponding blocks
B_{2i}
and
B_{2j}
of
\mathcal{D}_{2}
. In particular,
|B_{1i}|=|B_{2i}|
for any two corresponding blocks
B_{1i}
of
\mathcal{D}_{1}
and
B_{2i}
of
\mathcal{D}_{2}
.

Note that this is also a necessary condition: in general, for a switching method to work, any two vertices that are not contained in
C
must have the same number of common neighbours in
C
before and after switching. Algebraically, this can be explained by:
x^{T}y=(R^{T}x)^{T}(R^{T}y)
if
R
is orthogonal.

(3)

We can always choose
C
such that the induced subgraph on
C
is edgeless or complete. If we do so, then after switching it is edgeless or complete again.

A special case of Theorem
1
occurs when
\mathcal{D}_{1}=\mathcal{D}_{2}
and the blocks are permuted.

Corollary 3
.

Let
\Gamma
be a graph with a subgraph
C
whose vertices are identified with points of an
(r,\lambda)
-design with incidence matrix
N
. Let
\pi
be a permutation of the blocks such that for all blocks
B_{i}
and
B_{j}
,

|B_{i}\cap B_{j}|=|\pi(B_{i})\cap\pi(B_{j})|.

Define
R=\frac{1}{r-\lambda}\left(N(N^{\pi})^{T}-\lambda J_{v}\right)
, where
N^{\pi}
is obtained from
N
by replacing every
i
th column by the
\pi(i)
th column. Suppose that:

(i)

The adjacency matrix
A_{C}
of
C
has the property that
R^{T}A_{C}R
is an adjacency matrix.

(ii)

For every vertex
x\notin C
, its neighbours in
C
form a block.

Make every vertex
x\notin C
that is adjacent to the points of
B
, adjacent to the points of
\pi(B)
. Replace
C
by the subgraph with adjacency matrix
R^{T}A_{C}R
. The resulting graph is
\mathbb{R}
-cospectral with
\Gamma
.

This paper is structured as follows. In Section
2
we give preliminaries
before proving Theorem
1
in Section
3
.
In Section
4
we analyse design switching in detail for various particular designs.
In particular, we recover GM-, WQH-, and AH-switching.
Then in Section
5
we show that not all cospectrality results for graphs
can be explained using design switching.
Lastly, in Section
6
, we give a few applications for design switching.
In particular, we give the announced spectral explanation for Kantor’s switching method in
[
6
]
.

2
Preliminaries

Let
O_{n}
,
I_{n}
and
J_{n}
denote the
n\times n
null matrix, identity matrix and all-one matrix respectively. If the size is clear, indices are omitted. A matrix
Q
is
orthogonal
if
QQ^{T}=I
. An orthogonal matrix
Q
is
regular
if
QJ=J
. A matrix is
decomposable
if, after a permutation of its rows and columns, it can be written as a block diagonal matrix with at least two non-identity blocks. The
level
of a matrix
Q
is the smallest integer
\ell
such that
\ell Q
is integral, or
\infty
if
Q
is irrational.

The
spectrum
of a graph is the multiset of eigenvalues of its adjacency matrix. Two graphs are
cospectral
if they have the same spectrum. Two graphs with adjacency matrices
A
and
A^{\prime}
are
\mathbb{R}
-cospectral
if
A+rJ
and
A^{\prime}+rJ
have the same spectrum for all
r\in\mathbb{R}
. Two graphs are
\mathbb{R}
-cospectral if and only if they are cospectral and their complements are cospectral, if and only if their adjacency matrices are conjugated by a regular orthogonal matrix
[
22
]
.

A
strongly regular graph with parameters
(v,k,\lambda,\mu)
is a
k
-regular graph (not complete and not edgeless) with
v
vertices such that each pair of adjacent
vertices has precisely
\lambda
common neighbours and each pair of non-adjacent vertices has precisely
\mu
common neighbours. If two strongly regular graphs have the same parameters, then they are cospectral
[
9
]
.

In this work, a
switching method
is a local graph transformation, resulting in a cospectral graph. It needs a
switching set
, a subset of the vertex set of the graph, together with some conditions. The size of the switching set should be constant in order to keep the operation local. Recall that two matrices are cospectral if and only if they are conjugated by an orthogonal matrix. Thus in the case of a switching method, we want the transformation to correspond to a conjugation with an orthogonal matrix

Q=Q(R)=\begin{pmatrix}R&O\\
O&I\end{pmatrix},

where the order of
R
is a constant, while the order of the graph (and thus the matrix
Q
) can be made arbitrarily large.

A switching method is
reducible
to certain other methods if it can be obtained by a sequence of them.
Algebraically, a switching method that corresponds to a conjugation of the adjacency matrix with
Q
is reducible to the switching methods corresponding to conjugations with
Q_{1},\dots,Q_{s}
if
Q=Q_{1}\cdots Q_{s}
and, for every adjacency matrix
A
such that
Q^{T}AQ
is again an adjacency matrix and for every
i<s
, the matrix
(Q_{1}\cdots Q_{i})^{T}A(Q_{1}\cdots Q_{i})
is an adjacency matrix. The notion of reducibility was introduced in
[
3
, Definition 10]
for the special case when
Q
has level 2.

An
incidence structure
is a tuple
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{B})
where
\mathcal{P}
is a set and
\mathcal{B}
is a multiset of subsets of
\mathcal{P}
. A
point
is an element of
\mathcal{P}
and a
block
is an element of
\mathcal{B}
. If
\mathcal{B}
is a set, then the incidence structure is
simple
. The
incidence matrix
of an incidence structure is a matrix
N
indexed by its points and blocks as rows and columns respectively, such that
(N)_{pB}=1
if
p\in B
and
(N)_{pB}=0
if
p\notin B
.

An
(r,\lambda)
-design
is an incidence structure
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{B})
such that

•

every point is contained in exactly
r
blocks, and

•

every pair of points is included in exactly
\lambda
blocks.

For more information on
(r,\lambda)
-designs, see
[
10
, Section VI.49]
.
A
balanced incomplete block design
or
2
-
(v,k,\lambda)
-design
is an
(r,\lambda)
-design with
v
points where each block has size
k
.
If
k=r
, the design is
symmetric
, in which case any two blocks intersect in precisely
\lambda
points.
If any two blocks intersect in either precisely
\mu_{1}
or precisely
\mu_{2}
elements, the design is
quasi-symmetric
.

3
Proof of the main theorem

We need the following observations for the proof of Theorem
1
.

Lemma 4
.

Consider an
(r,\lambda)
-design
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{B})
with
v
points and incidence matrix
N
.

(i)

NN^{T}=\lambda J_{v}+(r-\lambda)I_{v}
.

(ii)

For every point
p\in\mathcal{P}
, we have
\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}B\in\mathcal{B}\\
p\in B\end{subarray}}|B|=r+(v-1)\lambda
.

Proof.

(i)

By definition.

(ii)

By double counting of
\{(q,B)\in\mathcal{P}\times\mathcal{B}\colon\,p,q\in B\}
. ∎

We are now ready to prove the main theorem.

Proof of Theorem
1
.

Let
\mathcal{P}=\{p_{1},\dots,p_{v}\}
denote the vertex set of
C
, so
\mathcal{P}
is equal to the point set of both designs. Denote their block multiset by
\mathcal{B}_{1}=\{B_{11},\dots,B_{1b}\}
and
\mathcal{B}_{2}=\{B_{21},\dots,B_{2b}\}
such that the columns of
N_{1}
and
N_{2}
are labelled in the same order. From
N_{1}^{T}N_{1}=N_{2}^{T}N_{2}
and Lemma
4
, it follows
that
R^{T}R=I_{v}
and
RJ_{v}=J_{v}
, so
R
is a regular orthogonal matrix.
Label the vertices of
\Gamma
in such a way that its adjacency matrix
A_{1}
and the adjacency matrix of the new graph
A_{2}
have block form

A_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}A_{1C}&V_{1}\\
V_{1}^{T}&W\end{pmatrix}\text{ and }A_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}A_{2C}&V_{2}\\
V_{2}^{T}&W\end{pmatrix}

where
A_{1C}

and
A_{2C}
are the adjacency matrices of the induced subgraph on
\mathcal{P}
. We check that
A_{1}=Q^{T}A_{1}Q
, where
Q=Q(R)
.
By construction,
A_{2C}=R^{T}A_{1C}R
. In order to see that
V_{2}=R^{T}V_{1}
is satisfied, choose
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
and let
\chi
be the characteristic vector of
B_{1i}
. It suffices to prove that
R^{T}\chi
is the characteristic vector of
B_{2i}
. Let
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. Since
N_{1}^{T}N_{1}=N_{2}^{T}N_{2}
, we have

\left(N_{1}^{T}\chi\right)_{j}=|B_{1i}\cap B_{1j}|=|B_{2i}\cap B_{2j}|,

see also Remark
2
(2).
A double counting of

\{(p,B)\in\mathcal{P}\times\mathcal{B}_{2}\colon\,p\neq p_{j}\in B\neq B_{2i}\text{ and }p\in B_{2i}\cap B\}

implies

\left(N_{2}N_{1}^{T}\chi\right)_{j}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}B\in\mathcal{B}_{2}\\
p_{j}\in B\end{subarray}}|B_{2i}\cap B|=\begin{cases}(|B_{2i}|-1)\lambda+r&\text{ if }p_{j}\in B_{2i},\\
\lambda|B_{2i}|&\text{ if }p_{j}\notin B_{2i},\end{cases}

and thus

\left(R^{T}\chi\right)_{j}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{r-\lambda}\left((|B_{2i}|-1)\lambda+r-\lambda|B_{2i}|\right)=1&\text{ if }p_{j}\in B_{2i},\\
\frac{1}{r-\lambda}\left(\lambda|B_{2i}|-\lambda|B_{2,i}|\right)=0&\text{ if }p_{j}\notin B_{2i}.\end{cases}\qed

4
Analysis for particular designs

In this section, we apply Theorem
1
to several specific designs. We first show that most of the currently known switching methods follow from this theorem. Next, we look at projective designs as well as examples for small designs.

4.1
Known switching methods

In this section, we point out that many of the currently known switching methods can be obtained by Corollary
3
.

4.1.1
Godsil-McKay switching

The following result is one of the most productive methods for finding cospectral graphs.

Theorem 5

(GM-switching
[
16
]
)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a graph and let
C_{1},\dots,C_{t}
be disjoint subsets of its vertex set such that the following hold for all
i,j\in\{1,\dots,t\}
.

(i)

Every vertex in
C_{i}
has the same number of neighbours in
C_{j}
.

(ii)

Every vertex
x\notin C_{1}\cup\dots\cup C_{t}
has
0
,
\frac{1}{2}|C_{i}|
or
|C_{i}|
neighbours in
C_{i}
.

For all
i\in\{1,\dots,t\}
and every vertex
x\notin C_{1}\cup\dots\cup C_{t}
that has exactly
\frac{1}{2}|C_{i}|
neighbours in
C_{i}
, swap the adjacencies between
x
and
C_{i}
. The resulting graph is
\mathbb{R}
-cospectral with
\Gamma
.

Define
c_{i}=|C_{i}|
for all
i\in\{1,\dots,t\}
. If we want to specify the size of the switching set, we speak of “GM
{}_{c_{1}+\dots+c_{t}}
switching”.
Algebraically, this method corresponds to a conjugation of the adjacency matrix with
the block diagonal matrix
\mathrm{diag}(R_{1},\dots,R_{t},I)
, where

R_{i}=\frac{2}{c_{i}}J_{c_{i}}-I_{c_{i}}

for all
i\in\{1,\dots,t\}
.
Let
\mathcal{P}=C_{1}\cup\dots\cup C_{t}
and let
\mathcal{B}
be the set of all subsets of
\mathcal{P}
that intersect
C_{i}
in
0
,
\frac{1}{2}c_{i}
or
c_{i}
elements, for all
i\in\{1,\dots,t\}
. Together, they form an incidence structure with
r=\frac{1}{2}|\mathcal{B}|=\frac{1}{2}\prod_{i=1}^{t}\left(\binom{c_{i}}{\frac{1}{2}c_{i}}+2\right)
blocks through each point.
If
t=1
, then the number of blocks through any two points is equal to
\lambda=\binom{c_{1}-2}{\frac{1}{2}c_{1}-2}+1
.
If
t\geq 2
and
|C_{i}|=4
for all
i\in\{1,\dots,t\}
, then the number of blocks through any two points is equal to
\lambda=2^{3t-2}
.
In both cases, GM-switching is a particular instance of Corollary
3
when we let
\pi
be the permutation that corresponds to the operation in the statement. But in general, we do not know if a corresponding
(r,\lambda)
-design exists.

Example 6
.

For GM
6+4
switching, the number of blocks through two vertices of
C_{1}
is
40
, while there are 44 blocks through a pair of vertices of
C_{2}
. Yet still, this problem can be fixed by adding multiplicities to certain blocks. For example, if we count all blocks containing exactly half of the vertices of
C_{1}
with multiplicity
4
and the other blocks with multiplicity
11
, then the number of blocks through every pair of vertices is always
\lambda=204
and the number of blocks through a fixed point is always
r=408
. Therefore, also if
t=2
,
|C_{1}|=6
and
|C_{2}|=4
, we can reformulate GM-switching as a particular case of Corollary
3
.

4.1.2
Wang-Qiu-Hu switching

Another powerful switching technique is the following.

Theorem 7

(WQH-switching
[
32
]
)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a graph and let
C_{1}^{1},C_{1}^{2},\dots,C_{t}^{1},C_{t}^{2}
be disjoint subsets of its vertex set such that the following hold for all
i,j\in\{1,\dots,t\}
.

(i)

|C_{i}^{1}|=|C_{i}^{2}|
.

(ii)

The difference
\deg(x,C^{k}_{j})-\deg(x,C_{j}^{3-k})
is constant for each
k\in\{1,2\}
and every vertex
x\in C^{k}_{i}
.

(iii)

Every vertex
x\notin C_{1}^{1}\cup C_{1}^{2}\cup\dots\cup C_{t}^{1}\cup C_{t}^{2}
is adjacent to

(a)

all vertices of
C_{i}^{1}
and no vertices of
C_{i}^{2}
, or

(b)

no vertices of
C_{i}^{1}
and all vertices of
C_{i}^{2}
, or

(c)

the same number of vertices of
C_{i}^{1}
as of
C_{i}^{2}
.

For all
i\in\{1,\dots,t\}
and every
x\notin C_{1}^{1}\cup C_{1}^{2}\cup\dots\cup C_{t}^{1}\cup C_{t}^{2}
for which 1. or 2. holds, swap the adjacencies between
x
and
C_{i}^{1}\cup C_{i}^{2}
. The resulting graph is
\mathbb{R}
-cospectral with
\Gamma
.

Define
c_{i}=|C^{1}_{i}|=|C^{2}_{i}|
for all
i\in\{1,\dots,t\}
. If we want to specify the size of the switching set, we speak of “WQH
{}_{2c_{1}+\dots+2c_{t}}
switching”.
Algebraically, this method corresponds to a conjugation of the adjacency matrix with the matrix
\mathrm{diag}(R_{1},\dots,R_{t},I)
, where

R_{i}=\frac{1}{c_{i}}\left(\begin{array}[]{rr}-J_{c_{i}}&J_{c_{i}}\\
J_{c_{i}}&-J_{c_{i}}\end{array}\right)+I_{2c_{i}}

for all
i\in\{1,\dots,t\}
.

Let
\mathcal{P}=C_{1}^{1}\cup C_{1}^{2}\cup\dots\cup C_{t}^{2}
and let
\mathcal{B}
be the set of all subsets that intersect
C_{i}^{1}\cup C_{i}^{2}
either in
C_{i}^{1}
, in
C_{i}^{2}
or in equally many elements of
C_{i}^{1}
as of
C_{i}^{2}
, for all
i\in\{1,\dots,t\}
. These sets form an incidence structure with

r=\frac{1}{2}|\mathcal{B}|=\frac{1}{2}\prod_{i=1}^{t}\left(2+\sum_{j=0}^{c_{i}}\binom{c_{i}}{j}^{2}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\prod_{i=1}^{t}\left(2+\binom{2c_{i}}{c_{i}}\right)

blocks through each point, where the last equality is obtained by counting the number of north-east lattice paths between
(0,0)
and
(c_{i},c_{i})
.
If
c_{i}=4
for all
i\in\{1,\dots,t\}
, then WQH-switching is equivalent to GM-switching on sets of four vertices, and hence a particular case of Corollary
3
, see Section
4.1.1
.
If
t=1
, then the number of blocks through two points is in general not constant. However, we can modify the design by adding multiplicities to the blocks as follows. Let
c=c_{1}
. The number of blocks through two points that are both in
C_{1}^{1}
or both in
C_{1}^{2}
is equal to

1+\sum_{i=2}^{c}\binom{c-2}{i-2}\binom{c}{i}=\binom{2c-2}{c}+1,

where the equality can be obtained by counting north-east lattice paths between
(0,0)
and
(c,c-2)
. The number of blocks through two points, one from
C_{1}^{1}
and one from
C_{1}^{2}
, is equal to

\sum_{i=1}^{c}\binom{c-1}{i-1}^{2}=\binom{2c-2}{c-1},

the number of north-east lattice paths between
(0,0)
and
(c-1,c-1)
.
If we let the multiplicity of the blocks
C_{i}^{1}
and
C_{i}^{2}
be equal to
\binom{2c-2}{c-1}-\binom{2c-2}{c}\geq 1
, then the number of blocks through every pair of points becomes
\binom{2c-2}{c-1}
. Note that the number of blocks through each point is also increased, but remains a constant. We have found an
(r,\lambda)
-design that, together with the permutation of the blocks that corresponds to the operation in the statement, leads to WQH-switching if
t=1
.
Note that, in contrast with the case
t=1
for GM-switching, the design is not totally obvious here. We need repeated blocks.

4.1.3
Abiad-Haemers switching

Abiad and Haemers
[
4
]
gave an algebraic description of switching methods that correspond with a conjugation of the adjacency matrix with a regular orthogonal matrix
Q
of level 2. There are essentially three different switching methods and they can be described easily by the orthogonal matrix
R
such that
Q=Q(R)
. We refer to
[
3
]
for a combinatorial description of these methods.

Infinite family

The
2m\times 2m
matrix

R_{2m}=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{circulant}(J,O,\dots,O,Y)

where
J
,
O
and
Y=2I-J
are square matrices of order
2
,
yields an infinite family of switching methods which we call AH
2m
switching.
The 01-vectors
v
such that
R_{2m}^{T}v
is another 01-vector, can be obtained from the following lemma.

Lemma 8

(
[
1
, Lemma 15]
)
.

A 01-vector
v
has the property that
R_{2m}^{T}v
is again a 01-vector if and only if the numbers of ones in each of the tuples
(v_{1},v_{2}),\dots,(v_{2m-1},v_{2m})
, is the same modulo 2.
If the number of ones is always even (0 or 2), then
R_{2m}^{T}v=v
. If it is always equal to 1, then
R_{2m}^{T}v=(v_{3},\dots,v_{2m},v_{1},v_{2})^{T}
.

We can rephrase the switching method that corresponds to
R_{2m}
as a particular case of Corollary
3
for the
(r=2^{m},\lambda=2^{m-1})
-design with point set
\mathcal{P}=\{1,\dots,2m\}
and blocks
B\subseteq\mathcal{P}
such that
|B\cap\{2i-1,2i\}|\pmod{2}
is the same for all
i\in\{1,\dots,m\}
. The permutation
\pi
in Corollary
3
is a cyclic shift in those cases where the number is 1 modulo 2, and the identity otherwise.

Fano switching

The
7\times 7
matrix

R_{\text{Fano}}=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{circulant}(-1,1,1,0,1,0,0)

yields a switching method that can be obtained by permuting the lines of the Fano plane, see also Example
13
below.

Cube switching

The
8\times 8
matrix

R_{\text{cube}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{rrrr}-I&I&I&I\\
I&-Z&I&Z\\
I&Z&-Z&I\\
I&I&Z&-Z\end{array}\right)

where

,

and

, are square matrices of order

, corresponds to a switching method that can be obtained from the design that is the affine space
\mathrm{AG}(3,2)
. See also Example
19
below.

4.2
Projective planes and Singer cycles

Taking the points of a finite affine or projective geometry together with subspaces of a fixed dimension for blocks
is a rich source for
(r,\lambda)
-designs. In general, when applying Theorem
1
,
it is unclear when
R^{T}A_{C}R
is again an adjacency matrix. The only choices for
A_{C}
that always work, are
A_{C}=O
or
A_{C}=J-I
.
In some projective planes, there is another choice: a Singer cycle.
A
Singer cycle
of a projective plane is a collineation that permutes the points cyclically. Such a cycle exists if and only if the plane has a cyclic point-line incidence matrix. With some abuse of nomenclature, we could also use the name
Singer cycle
for a subgraph
C
when it is a cycle graph with the same ordering of a Singer cycle in the projective designs.

Theorem 9
.

Consider two projective planes
\Pi_{1}
and
\Pi_{2}
of order
q
that admit a Singer cycle. Let
\Gamma
be a graph with a cyclic subgraph
C
whose vertices are identified both with points of
\Pi_{1}
and
\Pi_{2}
in the same cyclic order.
Suppose that every vertex
x\notin C
is adjacent to the vertices of
C
contained in a line, its complement or all or no vertices of
C
.
Make every vertex
x\notin C
that is (non)adjacent to the points of the
i
th line of
\Pi_{1}
, (non)adjacent to the points of the
i
th line of
\Pi_{2}
. The resulting graph is
\mathbb{R}
-cospectral with
\Gamma
.

Proof.

Let
A=\mathrm{circulant}(0,1,0,\dots,0,1)
be the adjacency matrix of the cycle graph of order
v=q^{2}+q+1
. If
N
is a cyclic incidence matrix of a projective plane of order
q
with point set
\mathcal{P}=\{p_{1},\dots,p_{v}\}
and line set
\mathcal{L}=\{L_{1},\dots,L_{v}\}
, ordered cyclically with indices modulo
v
, then

\displaystyle\left(N^{T}AN\right)_{ij}

\displaystyle=|k\in\mathbb{Z}/v\mathbb{Z}\colon\,p_{k}\in L_{i}\text{ and }p_{k-1}\in L_{j}|

\displaystyle\quad+|k\in\mathbb{Z}/v\mathbb{Z}\colon\,p_{k}\in L_{i}\text{ and }p_{k+1}\in L_{j}|

\displaystyle=|L_{i}\cap L_{j+1}|+|L_{i}\cap L_{j-1}|

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}q+2&\text{ if }i=j\pm 1,\\
2&\text{ else,}\end{cases}

\displaystyle=\left(qA+2J_{v}\right)_{ij}

so
N^{T}AN=qA+2J_{v}
and, by duality,
NAN^{T}=qA+2J_{v}
. If we define
R=\frac{1}{q}\left(N_{1}N_{2}^{T}-J_{v}\right)
as in Theorem
1
, where
N_{1}
and
N_{2}
are the incidence matrices of
\Pi_{1}
and
\Pi_{2}
respectively, then from the previous equalities, together with
JA=AJ=2J
and Lemma
4
, it follows that
R^{T}AR=A
. Thus the statement is a corollary of Theorem
1
.
∎

Example 10
.

Given a projective plane
\Pi=(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{L})
of order
q
with a Singer cycle
\pi=(p_{1},\dots,p_{q^{2}+q+1})
, choose a line
L\in\mathcal{L}
and let
\Omega=\{p_{i}\in\mathcal{P}\colon\,p_{-i}\in L\}
. This is an
oval
: a set of
q+1
points no three of which are collinear
[
18
, Theorem 16]
. Together with its images under
\pi
, it forms an isomorphic projective plane with the same point set but different lines that agree with the cyclic structure, that is,
\pi
induces a collineation of these new lines too. Defining
L_{i}=\pi^{i}(L)
and
\Omega_{i}=\pi^{i}(\Omega)
, the switching operation comes down to replacing (non)adjacencies with
L_{i}
by (non)adjacencies with
\Omega_{i}
.

Remark 11
.

Switching methods coming Corollary
3
for projective planes may be reducible to WQH-switching (Theorem
7
) if the adjacency matrix of the switching set allows it. Indeed, every permutation of lines can be written as a product of transpositions of two lines. Applying Corollary
3
where
\pi
is a transposition, is the same operation as applying WQH-switching on the symmetric difference of those lines, since every other line intersects each of the lines (without the intersection) in equally many points. In particular, if the switching set induces an edgeless or complete subgraph, the switching method from Corollary
3
when applied to a projective plane of order
q
, is always reducible to WQH
2q
switching.

The same can be said about permutations that preserve parallel lines of affine planes: if the adjacency matrix allows it, then the switching method from Corollary
3
when applied to an affine plane of order
q
and a permutation that preserves parallelism, may be reducible to WQH
2q
switching.

4.3
Classification of switching methods from small designs

In this section, we analyse some examples of switching methods that can be obtained from Corollary
3
when applied to certain small
(r,\lambda)
-designs. We make the following restrictions.

•

We only classify methods coming from one design (Corollary
3
) instead of two designs (Theorem
1
).

•

We only consider
simple
designs. The non-simple case (where blocks can be repeated) is explained in Remark
14
below.

Our general strategy is as follows. First assume that we are given a simple
(r,\lambda)
-design
\mathcal{D}
on
v
points and
b
blocks.
Let
G\leq\mathrm{Sym}(b)
be the group of permutations of blocks of
\mathcal{D}
that preserve their intersection sizes. Let
H=\mathrm{Aut}(\mathcal{D})\leq\mathrm{Sym}(v)
be the automorphism group of the design
\mathcal{D}
, acting on its blocks. Observe that
H\leq G
. Denote
\Gamma_{\pi}
for the graph obtained by Corollary
3
.

Lemma 12
.

If
\pi
and
\tau
are in the same double coset of
H\backslash G/H
, then
\Gamma_{\pi}\cong\Gamma_{\tau}
.

Proof.

If
\pi
and
\tau
are in the same double coset of
H\backslash G/H
, then
\tau=h_{1}\pi h_{2}
for certain automorphisms
h_{1},h_{2}
of
\mathcal{D}
. An automorphism induces a permutation of the vertices of the switching set and thus an automorphism of the graph. In other words,
h_{1}
is an automorphism of the graph before switching and
h_{1}
is an automorphism of the graph after switching, so
\Gamma_{\pi}\cong\Gamma_{\tau}
.
∎

So in order to classify the different switching methods that can be obtained by the design
\mathcal{D}
, we choose a representative
\pi
for each double coset in
H\backslash G/H
. Given the permutation
\pi
of the blocks of
\mathcal{D}
, we calculate the matrix
R
as defined in Corollary
3
. Note that every double coset gives a different matrix
R
. For each such matrix
R
, we can then calculate the possible matrices
A_{C}
.

For most examples, we use GAP
[
15
]
to calculate the double coset representatives and the possible matrices
R
and
A_{C}
. Since the group
G
is often difficult to calculate, we calculate instead a group that is somewhere between
G
and
\mathrm{Sym}(b)
, namely the group of permutations of blocks of
\mathcal{D}
that preserve a certain fixed intersection size, as this group can be calculated more easily as the automorphism group of the following graph. Its vertices are the blocks of the design. Two blocks are adjacent if they intersect in a given, fixed size. For this automorphism group, we calculate the double cosets and a representative for each of them. Only after that step, we filter down the representatives to those that preserve the intersection size of blocks. To go over some small designs, we use the
design
package
[
29
]
.

Example 13
.

We illustrate the above approach for the Fano plane PG

Remark 14
.

The above strategy also works for

of a design in GAP
[

,

]
is only defined for simple designs. Therefore, we could consider repeated blocks as the same block with a certain multiplicity that must be preserved under every automorphism. When calculating the matrix

from Theorem

, we could modify the incidence matrix such that on position
(p,B)
, it has a

if the point

is the multiplicity of

, and

otherwise. In this way, the expression for

yields the same result as if we would have

.

Despite the possibility, we restrict our classification to simple designs since there seems to be no useful upper bound on the number of blocks or the replication number

that a non-simple

-design on a fixed number of points can have. For example, the design that corresponds to GM
6+4
switching in Example

, has
816
blocks in total and
408
blocks through each point. Therefore, a computer enumeration of non-simple designs seems infeasible.

5
Incidence structures from switching methods

We can also go the other way around and construct incidence structures from a given switching method. Let
\mathcal{P}
be the vertices of the switching set. Let
\mathcal{B}_{1}
be the set of possible neighbourhoods within
\mathcal{P}
for a vertex outside the switching set, before switching. Let
\mathcal{B}_{2}
be the set of possible neighbourhoods within
\mathcal{P}
for such a vertex after switching.

Algebraically, if the switching method corresponds to a conjugation of the adjacency matrix with the orthogonal matrix
Q(R)
, then the characteristic vectors of the blocks of
\mathcal{B}_{1}
are exactly those 01-vectors
\chi
such that
R^{T}\chi
is again a 01-vector.
The characteristic vectors of the blocks of
\mathcal{B}_{2}
are all their images
R^{T}\chi
.
The designs
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{B}_{1})
and
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{B}_{2})
are not necessarily
(r,\lambda)
-designs. But if they are, then the switching method is exactly the method obtained through Theorem
1
.

If the matrix
R
is regular, then every point lies in
r=\frac{1}{2}|\mathcal{B}_{1}|
blocks (consider the map
\chi\mapsto\mathbb{1}-\chi
). Still, the property that every two distinct points lie on the same number of blocks, is in general not true. Sometimes adding multiplicities to certain blocks can fix this. In general it can not. We give an example of a switching method for which
R
is regular and indecomposable, but the design is no
(r,\lambda)
-design and cannot be made such a design by adding multiplicities.

Proposition 26
.

Let
\Gamma
be a graph with a subgraph
C
with vertex set
V(C)=\{1,\dots,6\}
such that:

(i)

The adjacency matrix of
C
is either edgeless, or the union of two triangles on
\{1,4,5\}
and
\{2,3,6\}
, or the union of the edge
\{1,2\}
and a complete graph on
\{3,4,5,6\}
.

(ii)

For every vertex
x\notin C
,

\deg(x,\{4,5,6\})\equiv 2\cdot\left(\deg(x,\{1\})-\deg(x,\{2,3\})\right)\pmod{5}.

Make every vertex
x\notin C
that has exactly three neighbours in
C
, adjacent to the other three instead. The resulting graph is
\mathbb{R}
-cospectral with
\Gamma
.

Proof.

This is the combinatorial interpretation of conjugating the adjacency matrix with the regular orthogonal matrix

R=\frac{1}{5}\left(\begin{array}[]{r|rr|rrr}2&3&3&-1&-1&-1\\
\hline\cr 3&2&-3&1&1&1\\
3&-3&2&1&1&1\\
\hline\cr-1&1&1&-2&3&3\\
-1&1&1&3&-2&3\\
-1&1&1&3&3&-2\end{array}\right).

One checks (by computer) that:

1.

The possible matrices
A_{C}
for which
R^{T}A_{C}R
is an adjacency matrix again, are the adjacency matrices of the graphs of condition (i), up to symmetry (complementation and conjugation). For all three of them,
R^{T}A_{C}R=A_{C}
.

2.

The 01-vectors
v
for which
R^{T}v
is a 01-vector again, give rise to exactly the neighbourhoods of condition (ii).
The 01-vectors
v
such that
R^{T}v\neq v
, are exactly those with three ones. ∎

Note that the above method is not reducible to any of the known methods from Table
1
since those four methods do not have level 5.
We claim that the above switching method cannot be obtained by design switching.

Proposition 27
.

The switching method from Proposition
26
is not a special case of Theorem
1
.

Proof.

Suppose, by contradiction, that the method is coming from an
(r,\lambda)
-design. It has twelve blocks with certain multiplicities. They correspond to the possible neighbourhoods of a vertex
x\notin C
, see condition (ii) of Proposition
26
. If we denote
m_{B}
for the multiplicity of block
B
, then we have, by looking at the number of blocks through the points
2
and
3
, that

6.1
Trades in designs

Theorem
1
is closely linked to the concept of trades in designs, see
[
5
,
24
,
26
]
. If the design used in Theorem
1
is symmetric and our graph is the intersection graph of a partial linear space, then we arrive at the following result.
1
1

1

Trades in designs are clearly known. Our point here is that they yield cospectral graphs.

Proposition 28
.

Consider a partial linear space
\mathcal{D}=(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{L})
with a projective plane
\Pi_{1}=(\mathcal{P}_{1},\mathcal{L}_{1})
as a subgeometry, that is,
\mathcal{P}_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{L}_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{L}
. If
\Pi_{2}=(\mathcal{P}_{1},\mathcal{L}_{2})
is a projective plane on the same point set, then the block graphs of
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{L})
and
(\mathcal{P},(\mathcal{L}\setminus\mathcal{L}_{1})\cup\mathcal{L}_{2})
are
\mathbb{R}
-cospectral.

Proof.

Let
\Pi_{1}^{*}
and
\Pi_{2}^{*}
denote the dual projective planes of
\Pi_{1}
and
\Pi_{2}
respectively. Since
\Pi_{1}
and
\Pi_{2}
have the same number of points, they have the same order. The induced subgraph on
\mathcal{L}_{1}
is complete. Choose
L\in\mathcal{L}\setminus\mathcal{L}_{1}
. If
L\cap\mathcal{P}_{1}=\emptyset
, then
L
has no neighbours in
\mathcal{L}_{1}
. If
L\cap\mathcal{P}_{1}=\{p\}
for some point
p
, then the neighbourhood of
L
in
\mathcal{L}_{1}
is the set of lines through
p
, which corresponds to a block of
\Pi_{1}^{*}
, and similarly for the neighbourhood of
L
in
\mathcal{L}_{2}
. Thus we can apply Theorem
1
with the designs
\Pi_{1}^{*}
and
\Pi_{2}^{*}
to obtain the result.
∎

6.2
Cospectral mates for
q
-triangular graphs

Let us discuss a specific case of Proposition
28
.
Let
q
be a prime power and
n\geq 4
. The
q-triangular graph

J_{q}(n,2)
has as vertices the lines of PG
(n-1,q)
where two vertices are adjacent if they intersect.

Let
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{B}
be the point set and line set of PG
(n-1,q)
respectively. The graph
J_{q}(n,2)
is exactly the block graph of
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{B})
. Every projective plane PG
(2,q)\subseteq\mathrm{PG}(n-1,q)
forms a symmetric
2
-
(q^{2}+q+1,q+1,1)
subdesign. Proposition
28
tells us that we can replace it by another
2
-
(q^{2}+q+1,q+1,1)
-design, that is, a projective plane of order
q
,
and get an
\mathbb{R}
-cospectral graph. We can also apply a permutation that is not an automorphism. In fact, every permutation
\pi
of
\mathcal{B}
that is not an automorphism, creates maximal cliques of size
q+2
. This new graph cannot be isomorphic to the original one, (re)proving that

Theorem 29

(
[
20
]
)
.

J_{q}(n,2)
,
n\geq 4
, is not determined by its spectrum.

We use this example to give a general sketch
for how one can use Corollary
3
to show that there are many
non-isomorphic graphs with a given spectrum whenever design switching is applicable.
2
2

2

The first author believes that the particular result here is well-known,
but we could not find a suitable reference.
This result is stated in Theorem
33
.

We do this in a similar way as in
[
6
, Section 7.2]
, but the design is different.
Fix a plane
\alpha
and consider the design
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{B})
where
\mathcal{P}
is the set of lines of
\alpha
and
\mathcal{B}
is the set of point-pencils of
\alpha
(all lines through a given point with
\alpha
). This design allows us to apply Theorem
1
with any other
(r=q+1,\lambda=1)
-design. Indeed, each vertex of the graph that is not in
\mathcal{P}
(a line not in
\alpha
), is adjacent to either a point-pencil of
\alpha
or no line of
\alpha
. But in order to prove the theorem, it suffices to use Corollary
3
where we use the same design with the blocks permuted. Given such a permutation
\pi
, denote the new graph by
\Gamma_{\pi}
. Since
\pi
permutes the point-pencils, we can (and will) look at it as a permutation of the points of
\alpha
.

Lemma 30
.

If
\pi\notin P\Gamma L(\alpha)
, then there are at least three non-collinear lines
\ell
in
\alpha
such that
\ell^{\pi}
is not a line.

Proof.

The is at least one such line
\ell
, since otherwise
\pi
would be a collineation. Pick a point
p\in\ell
. If all other lines through
p
would be mapped to lines, then
\ell^{\pi}
would be contained in
\alpha\setminus\bigcup_{\ell^{\prime}\supseteq p}\ell^{\prime\pi}
, which is contained in a line, a contradiction. So there is at least one other such line. Repeating that argument once more with another point on
\ell
gives us a third such line.
∎

Lemma 31
.

If
\pi\notin P\Gamma L(\alpha)
, then
\mathcal{P}
is recognisable in
\Gamma_{\pi}
.

Proof.

We prove that

maximal cliques of size at most
q+2
contain exactly one vertex of
\mathcal{P}

by contraposition.

If a maximal clique is disjoint from
P
, it is necessarily contained in a maximal clique of
\Gamma
with its vertices in
P
removed. Such a clique is either a point-pencil or its dual (all lines in a given plane). A point-pencil through a point
p\in\alpha
without its lines in
\alpha
is not maximal as we can add the point-pencil through
p^{\pi}
in
\alpha
(call this a shifted point-pencil). A point-pencil through a point
p\notin\alpha
has
\frac{q^{n-1}-1}{q-1}
lines, a plane-pencil has size
q^{2}+q+1
and has an intersection of at most one line with
P
.

If a maximal clique contains at least two vertices of
P
, it is either
P
itself or a shifted point-pencil as before.

Now we claim that
\mathcal{P}
is characterised by the above property. Suppose that
C
is a maximal clique with the property. If
\ell
is a line in
\alpha
such that
\ell^{\pi^{-1}}
is not a line, and if
p
is a point not in
\alpha
, then

\{\ell\}\cup\{pq\colon\,q^{\pi}\in\ell\}

is a maximal clique of size
q+2
. Now, use the above lemma to find three non-collinear such lines. If
C
contains all three of them, then it is equal to
\mathcal{P}
. If not, we can use the property on the maximal cliques of size
q+2
and conclude that there is a line in
C
that goes through
p
. But since
p\notin\alpha
can be chosen arbitrarily, the union of the lines of
C
must cover all points of PG
(n-1,q)\setminus\alpha
. So it must be a point-pencil, which this contradicts the “exactly one” part of the property.
∎

Lemma 32
.

We have
\Gamma_{\pi}\cong\Gamma_{\tau}
if and only if
\pi
and
\tau
are in the same double coset of
P\Gamma L(\alpha)
(acting on the lines) in Sym
(\mathcal{P})
.

Proof.

Note that
\pi\in P\Gamma L(\alpha)\Longleftrightarrow\Gamma_{\pi}\cong\Gamma
because if
\ell
is a line such that
\ell^{\pi^{-1}}
is not a line, and if
p
is a point not in
\alpha
, then

\{\ell\}\cup\{pq\colon\,q^{\pi}\in\ell\}

is a maximal clique of size
q+2
. But maximal cliques in
\Gamma
have size at least
q^{2}+2+1
.

So suppose that
\pi,\tau\notin P\Gamma L(\alpha)
and let
f:\Gamma_{\pi}\to\Gamma_{\tau}
be an isomorphism. By the above lemma,
\mathcal{P}
is recognisable in both graphs, so
f(\mathcal{P})=\mathcal{P}
.

Now,
f
maps point-pencils in
\alpha
to point-pencils in
\alpha
, since those are exactly the cliques of size
q+1
in
\mathcal{P}
that can be extended to larger cliques in the full graph. So
f|_{\mathcal{P}}\in P\Gamma L(\alpha)
(acting on the lines).

The point-pencils in PG
(n-1,q)
through a point
p\notin\alpha
are also mapped to such point-pencils again, since they are the only cliques of size
\frac{q^{n-1}-1}{q-1}
disjoint from
\mathcal{P}
. So
f|_{V(\Gamma)\setminus\mathcal{P}}
preserves collinear lines and can be uniquely extended to a collinearity
g\in P\Gamma L(n,q)
.

We show that
\pi f=g\tau
as maps on the lines of
\alpha
, thereby proving the claim. Pick a line
\ell\in\mathcal{P}
. Let
p
be an arbitrary point on
\ell
and let
m
be a line through
p
. Then
m\sim\ell^{\pi}
, so
f(m)\sim f(\ell^{\pi})
. In other words,
f(\ell^{\pi})
goes through
(\alpha\cap f(m))^{\tau}=g(p)^{\tau}
. So
f(\ell^{\pi})=g(\ell)^{\tau}
.
∎

Theorem 33
.

There are at least
q!
non-isomorphic graphs with the same spectrum as
J_{q}(n,2)
,
n\geq 4
.

Proof.

There are at least
q!
double cosets of
P\Gamma L(2,q)
in Sym
(q^{2}+q+1)
because
|P\Gamma L(2,q)|<q^{9}
and
|\mathrm{Sym}(q^{2}+q+1)|=(q^{2}+q+1)!
. Applying the previous lemma gives the result.
∎

6.3
Polar spaces

The goal of this section is to fit several constructions from finite classical polar spaces into our framework.
In particular, we explain the construction in
[
6
, Section 7.1]
algebraically.
It also provides a new perspective on other publications such as
[
11
,
19
,
20
]
.

Let
q
be a prime power.
There are five types of finite classical polar spaces:
O^{+}(2d,q)
,
O(2d+1,q)
,
O^{-}(2d+2)
,

U(n,\sqrt{q})
,
Sp(2d,q)
, where
d
and
n
are positive integers. They are related to various strongly regular graphs, see
[
9
, Chapter 2 and Chapter 3]
. Each of the finite classical polar spaces can be defined by a suitable form over GF
(q)
:

•

For
O^{+}(2d,q)
, the quadratic form
Q(x)=x_{1}x_{2}+\cdots+x_{2d-1}x_{2d}
.

•

For
O(2d+1,q)
, the quadratic form
Q(x)=x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}x_{3}+\cdots+x_{2d}x_{2d+1}
.

•

For
O^{-}(2d+2,q)
, the quadratic form
Q(x)=x_{1}^{2}+\alpha x_{1}x_{2}+\beta x_{2}^{2}+\cdots+x_{2d+1}x_{2d+2}
,
where
x^{2}+\alpha x+\beta
is irreducible over GF
(q)
.

•

For
U(n,\sqrt{q})
, the Hermitian form
\sigma(x,y)=x_{1}y_{1}^{\sqrt{q}}+\cdots+x_{n}y_{n}^{\sqrt{q}}
.

•

For
Sp(2d,q)
, the symplectic form
\sigma(x,y)=x_{1}y_{2}-x_{2}y_{1}+\cdots+x_{2d-1}y_{2d}-x_{2d}y_{2d-1}
.

We say that a vector
x
is
singular
with respect to
Q
if
Q(x)=0

and
isotropic
if with respect to
\sigma
if
\sigma(x,x)=0
.
More generally, a subspace is
totally singular
if all its vector are singular
and a subspace
S
is
totally isotropic
if
\sigma(x,y)=0
for all
x,y\in S
.
For simplicity, we use
isotropic
as synonym for singular, totally singular,
and totally isotropic.
Unless
q
is even, we can associate the finite classical polar space with
a polarity
\perp
of
\mathrm{PG}(n-1,q)
, that is,
\perp
is an incidence preserving
bijectionof order two on the subspaces of
\mathrm{PG}(n-1,q)

with
\dim(U^{\perp})=\mathrm{codim}(U)
for any subspace.
For a subspace
S
, the
radical
of
S
is the
set of vectors
x\in S
such that
\langle x,y\rangle
is isotropic
for all
y\in S
. If
\perp
exists, then
S\cap S^{\perp}
is the radical of
S
.

We write
[k]_{q}=\frac{q^{k}-1}{q-1}
for the number of points of PG
(k-1,q)
.

6.3.1
Adjacent when orthogonal

Let
\Gamma
be a graph whose vertex set
V(\Gamma)
is a subset of the points of PG
(n-1,q)
, where two distinct points
x
and
y
are adjacent if
x\in y^{\perp}
.

Lemma 34
.

Let
U
be a subspace of PG
(n-1,q)
of dimension
k-1
and let
\perp
be a polarity of PG
(n-1,q)
. The restriction of the point-hyperplane design of
U
to
V(\Gamma)\cap U
or
V(\Gamma)\cap U\setminus U^{\perp}
is an
(r=[k-1]_{q},\lambda=[k-2]_{q})
-design that satisfies the (relaxed, see Remark
2
(1)) condition (ii’) of Theorem
1
.

Proof.

Within
U
, every point is contained in
[k-1]_{q}
hyperplanes and every line is contained in
[k-2]_{q}
hyperplanes. Moreover, deleting points of the design does not change
r
or
\lambda
.

For a point
x\notin U
, the hyperplane
x^{\perp}
intersects
U
in either a hyperplane of
U
or
U
itself. Thus
x
is adjacent to a block of the point-hyperplane design of
U
or all points of
U
. If
x\in U^{\perp}
, then it is adjacent to all points of
U
.
∎

There are the following natural choices for
V(\Gamma)
:

•

All isotropic points. Note that we can include
q
even and quadratic forms if we require that
\langle x,y\rangle
is singular.

•

All anisotropic points.

•

For quadratic forms and
q
odd, one isometry type of anisotropic points.

For applying Theorem
1
, we need to find a subspace
U
such that for
C=V(\Gamma)\cap U
or
C=V(\Gamma)\cap U\setminus U^{\perp}
(as in Lemma
34
) the matrix
R^{T}A_{C}R
is an adjacency matrix. The easiest case is when
C
is a clique or a coclique.

Example 35
.

If
U
is a totally isotropic subspace and
C=V(\Gamma)\cap U
, then
C
is a clique. Thus we obtain cospectral graphs. If
U
is a plane or bigger, then we can hope for non-isomorphy.
There are two families of strongly regular graphs related to this case. If
V(\Gamma)
consists of all isotropic points, then this is the example in
[
6
, Section 7.1]
. If the polar space is a hyperbolic quadric and
V(\Gamma)
consists of all isotropic points minus one maximal isotropic subspace, then it is the construction in
[
7
]
.

Example 36
.

Suppose that
V(\Gamma)
consists of anisotropic points only. Take a subspace
U
such that
U\cap U^{\perp}
is a hyperplane of
U
. If we put
C=V(\Gamma)\cap U\setminus U^{\perp}
, then
C
is a coclique. Thus we obtain cospectral graphs. Again, if
U
is a plane or bigger, then we can hope for non-isomorphy.

If
V(\Gamma)
consists of all anisotropic points and the polarity is Hermitian, then
\Gamma
is the strongly regular graph
NU_{n}^{\perp}(q)
in the notation of
[
9
]
.
If
\perp
is quadratic and
V(\Gamma)
consists of one isometry type, then
\Gamma
is a strongly regular graph in some cases for
q\in\{3,5\}
.
This generalizes several constructions in
[
20
]
.

Example 37
.

Suppose that
\perp
is symplectic or Hermitian and that
V(\Gamma)
consists of all isotropic points. Take a subspace
U
such that
U\cap U^{\perp}
has codimension 2 in
U
. If
C=V(\Gamma)\cap U\setminus U^{\perp}
, then the induced subgraph on
C
is a disjoint union of cliques of size
q^{\dim(U)}
. We have
q+1
cliques if the polar space is symplectic and
\sqrt{q}+1
cliques if it is Hermitian.
For
n=5
and
\dim(U)=2
, this is the construction in
[
11
]
.

Note that this construction is also feasible for quadrics, but here we only obtain
2
cliques. The collineation group contains an element exchanging these two, so here no non-isomorphic graphs can be found.

6.3.2
Adjacent when tangential

Let
\Gamma
be a graph whose vertex set
V(\Gamma)
is a subset of points of PG
(n-1,q)
, where two distinct points
x
and
y
are adjacent if the line
\langle x,y\rangle
spanned by them, is a tangent (that is,
\langle x,y\rangle
contains precisely one isotropic point). As before, if
G
is the subgroup of
\mathrm{P\Gamma{}L}(n,q)
that stabilises
V(\Gamma)
set-wise, then
G
induces a subgroup of
\mathrm{Aut}(\Gamma)
.

In the notation of
[
9
]
, the strongly regular graph
NU_{n}(q)
equals
\Gamma
if
V(\Gamma)
is the set of all anisotropic points and
\perp
is Hermitian.
For
n\geq 5
, one can use WQH-switching to show that
NU_{n}(q)
is not determined by its spectrum
[
21
]
. While Theorem
1
can be used to generalize this case, it feels unnatural to do so.

Below, we give a generalization of the method, in a similar fashion as in the previous section.

Example 38
.

Suppose that
V(\Gamma)
consists of all anisotropic points. Take a subspace
U
such that
R=U\cap U^{\perp}
is a hyperplane in
U
. If we define
C=V(\Gamma)\cap U\setminus U^{\perp}
, then the induced subgraph on
C
is complete, because for any
x,y\in C
, we have that
\langle x,y\rangle
is a tangent with
\langle x,y\rangle\cap U^{\perp}
as its isotropic point. As
G
acts transitively on pairs of vertices of
C
, the parameters
r
and
\lambda

necessary for Theorem
1
exist and we can apply it. More specifically, the point set of the design is
C
,
while the blocks are

B_{x}=\{y\in C\colon\,x\text{ and }y\text{ are adjacent}\},

where
x\in V(\Gamma)\setminus C
.

Let us discuss the case of
U
being a projective plane, the smallest interesting case, in more detail. For an anisotropic point
x\notin U
,
W=\langle U,x\rangle
is a solid with the radical
N
being a point, a line, or a plane.

If
N
is a plane, then
x
is on
q^{2}+q+1
tangents in
W
and
hence adjacent to all of
C
. Similarly, if
N
is a line,
then
x
is on
0
tangents in
W
.

If
N
is a point (necessarily,
N\subseteq R
), then
x
lies on
q\sqrt{q}+q+1
tangents,
one of them meeting
U
in
N
, the other ones corresponding to

\sqrt{q}+1
lines through
N
. These form a
2
-
(q^{2},q(\sqrt{q}+1),\sqrt{q})
-design
(which is a blown-up
2
-
(q,\sqrt{q}+1,\sqrt{q})
-design).
