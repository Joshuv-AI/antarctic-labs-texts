Unseen barriers to home healthcare: a qualitative study of providers’ perspectives from Saudi Arabia
Authors: Khulud K Alharbi, Walid A Alkeridy, Nawal A Sindi, Rana A Alhelali, Samah H Hajjar, Tassnym H Sinky
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study investigated the challenges of providing care in Home Health Care (HHC) settings in Saudi Arabia.DesignWe employed a qualitative design and used thematic analysis to identify patterns and themes in the data supplemented with selected techniques commonly associated with grounded theory such as constant comparison, memo writing, and open coding to support rigor and depth without aiming to generate a formal theory.Setting and participantsSixty home health care providers self-selected into the study following an open invitation distributed to home health care facilities in Saudi Arabia. Participants were healthcare professionals, primarily younger than 40 years. Men predominated in roles such as general practice, physiotherapy, and family medicine, especially in the Western region, whereas nurses showed a more balanced sex distribution and broader regional representation.MethodsData were collected through semi-structured interviews conducted between September 2023 and May 2024. Interviews lasted between 30 and 45 min and explored the complexities of delivering care to older adults in home settings. Thematic analysis was applied to identify key themes from the data.ResultsThematic analysis of 60 semi-structured interviews with home healthcare providers across four major regions of Saudi Arabia (Western, Southern, Northern, and Central) – including general practitioners, family physicians, nurses, physiotherapists, social care workers, and psychologists – identified four overarching challenges in delivering care to older adults: disrupted caregiver–provider communication, system fragmentation and resource constraints, environmental and cultural barriers, and the emotional burden of care. Collectively, these challenges undermined continuity of care and weakened patient-provider relationships.Conclusions and ImplicationsThe findings highlight the need for comprehensive strategies to strengthen Saudi Arabia’s home health care sector. Priorities include caregiver training, equitable resource allocation, and standardized referral processes. In addition, raising public and provider awareness will be essential to dispel misconceptions and support the delivery of high-quality, sustainable home health care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12877-026-07973-y.Keywords: Home health care, Care provider, Challenge, Saudi Arabia, Older adults

Introduction

Epidemiological patterns among older adults have undergone changes, characterized by a rising prevalence of chronic diseases. Changes in lifestyle and socioeconomic factors accelerate aging and related diseases [1]. To adequately address the healthcare requirements of older adults and adapt to evolving disease patterns, a comprehensive and integrated approach is essential.

The Kingdom of Saudi Arabia has developed national transformation programs, including home health care (HHC), a holistic intervention service that integrates various aspects of the care model [2]. HHC is a rapidly growing service, with an expected growth rate of 25% from 2021 to 2031 [3]. Providing HHC services is a cost-effective way to reduce hospital occupancy rates [4]. HHC is a comprehensive, regulated program operated by a multidisciplinary team of healthcare providers in patients’ homes [5]. The introduction of HHC in Saudi Arabia has shown a positive relationship with palliative care for the aging population [5]. However, the HHC sector is underdeveloped, with staff requiring additional training to manage conditions ranging from acute to chronic and critical [6]. HHC encompasses a range of medical activities and services (i.e., curative, preventive, educational, rehabilitative, and social) that are tailored for specific patients in their homes, in accordance with specific criteria set by qualified medical teams [7]. HHC is critical for homebound and semi-homebound patients, who are generally older, frail, and managing multiple chronic illnesses. Patients who receive HHC find it convenient, as it allows them to be comfortable at home, avoiding the time, cost, and physical strain of travelling to a clinic or hospital [8].

HHC is cost-effective compared to frequent hospitalizations, which could be critical for lower-income households. Additionally, HHC can prevent hospitalization in many cases by providing regular checkups and monitoring patients’ health [9]. Patients with chronic illnesses are the primary focus of HCC services. In a previous study of 555 patients in Saudi Arabia, 72% had a history of hypertension and 66% had diabetes [10]. Further, patients in need of respiratory care who received treatment at home experienced reduced hospital utilization, increased overall satisfaction with care, and cost savings.

Studies highlight persistent challenges in maintaining safe and effective home health care, including workforce shortages and safety concerns across diverse settings [11–13]. However, limited evidence exists on how these challenges manifest in Saudi Arabia where rapid healthcare transformation, demographic aging, and expanding home health care programs create unique systemic pressures. This study therefore focuses on identifying barriers within the Saudi home health care system from the perspective of care providers themselves.

International studies have underscored recurring challenges in home-healthcare delivery including staff shortages, variable safety standards, and inconsistent support for providers’ well-being across diverse systems [4–13]. To guide this study, we utilized an ecological lens, recognizing that home-care challenges operate at multiple interacting levels: individual (caregiver capacity and provider strain), organizational (referral and staffing structures), and environmental (infrastructure and cultural norms) [14]. This framework informed our exploration of barriers and helped situate findings within the broader healthcare transformation in Saudi Arabia.

With the rising prevalence of chronic diseases and an aging population, Saudi Arabia faces increasing demand for home health care. While national transformation programs have promoted HHC as a cost-effective alternative to hospitalization, the sector remains underdeveloped. Limited evidence exists on the challenges faced by providers, particularly from their own perspectives. This study aims to fill this gap through qualitative interviews with home health care professionals. Despite the extensive international evidence base, little qualitative evidence exists that systematically explores the lived experiences and perspectives of home health care providers in Saudi Arabia. This study, therefore, provides qualitative insight into the contextual, organizational, and caregiver–provider communication dynamics shaping home healthcare delivery within the Kingdom.

Methods

Study design

This study examined the difficulties encountered by home healthcare providers. As our research question focused on the factors that facilitate or hinder healthcare providers’ home health care work, we used thematic analysis to identify themes related to these difficulties. We also drew on selected techniques commonly associated with grounded theory, including memo writing, in vivo coding, and constant comparison to enhance analytic rigor without the aim to generate a formal theory.

Data collection

Data were collected through individual semi-structured interviews conducted from September 2023 to May 2024. The interviews were conducted either remotely or face to face in the offices of home healthcare centers. Interviews were conducted by one member of the research team who trained in qualitative research methods and has experience in conducting semi-structured interviews. The interviewer had no prior personal or professional relationship with the participants before the study commenced. Additionally, coercion risk was minimized through voluntary participation, and managers were not informed subsequently of any information pertaining to the participants. There was also a private consent process that took place with each participant.

The decision to conduct interviews remotely was made to increase accessibility for providers working across different regions and to reduce the logistical and time burdens associated with in-person interviews sometimes. Rapport with participants was established at the beginning of each interview through a clear explanation of the study purpose, assurances of confidentiality, and the opportunity for participants to ask questions before proceeding. Feedback from participants was incorporated iteratively into the interview process, with the guide refined as needed to ensure that emerging issues and perspectives were adequately captured. The semi-structured interview guide was piloted with a small group of providers prior to formal data collection to refine question clarity and flow. While full transcript member-checking was not undertaken, participants were given the opportunity to clarify or expand on their responses during and immediately after interviews to ensure accuracy. In addition, credibility was enhanced through ongoing team-based discussions and peer debriefing, where emerging codes and themes were reviewed and refined collaboratively. The research team also engaged in iterative analysis and consensus-building, ensuring that interpretations remained grounded in the data.

Several strategies were employed to ensure the rigor and trustworthiness of the study. Credibility was enhanced through prolonged engagement with the data, iterative refinement of the interview guide, and triangulation within the research team, where multiple researchers independently reviewed codes and themes [15, 16]. Transferability was addressed by providing detailed descriptions of the study setting, participant characteristics, and contextual factors to allow readers to assess applicability to other contexts. Dependability was supported by maintaining an audit trail that documented coding decisions, memos, and theme development, enabling transparency in the analytic process. Confirmability was ensured through reflexivity, where researchers engaged in regular discussions to challenge assumptions, minimize bias, and reach consensus on discrepancies [15, 16].

To allow respondents to share in-depth insights, we used a qualitative interview guide that included open-ended questions, enabling comprehensive data collection on the issues studied. Based on the interview results, we modified the questions as needed (Table 1. Interview questions). Interviews continued until data saturation was achieved, with no new codes, themes, or concepts emerging after the 40th interview. With participants’ consent, interviews (lasting 30–45 min) were recorded using a mini-recording device and analyzed using MAXQDA software [17] (Germany, 2022).

Reflexivity

The primary interviewer was a public health researcher with clinical experience in community health settings, which provided familiarity with healthcare delivery dynamics but also a potential bias toward systemic interpretations. To mitigate this, reflexive journaling and memo writing were used throughout the study to document assumptions, emotional responses, and analytic decisions. Regular debriefing sessions with co-researchers from nursing and social science backgrounds helped challenge interpretations and ensure that themes reflected participants’ perspectives rather than researcher preconceptions.

Participants and sampling techniques

A purposive sample strategy was used to recruit employees working in home care facilities. An invitation email was sent to all facilities in the Kingdom, and those that agreed to participate facilitated recruitment. Employees who were interested in participating self-selected and contact the research team. The final sample consisted of 60 participants from four regions in Saudi Arabia. This strategy ensured logistical feasibility while maintaining diversity across regions and provider types. Participants varied in age, sex, professional role, and region of practice. Participants’ characteristics

The senior manager provided permission to recruit home health care team members. One exclusion criterion was the refusal to continue participating in this study. When offered the opportunity to participate in the research, some participants declined saying that they did not have the time, did not want to, or did not have anything to share. The study was conducted across many of home health care facilities, these facilities deliver a broad range of services such as nursing, physiotherapy, and social support. The patient populations they serve are primarily older adults and individuals with chronic diseases, disabilities, or post-acute care needs requiring regular monitoring and long-term support in their homes.

Language, translation, and cultural validation

Interviews were conducted in Arabic as it is the first language of the healthcare providers working in Saudi Arabia. However, due to the clinical nature of their work, participants frequently incorporated English medical terminology within their responses. All interviews were transcribed verbatim in Arabic and then translated into English by bilingual members of the research team for analysis. Coding was performed in English in MAXQDA with the Arabic transcript displayed side-by-side to preserve linguistic nuance and cultural meaning [17]. To evaluate translation accuracy, an independent bilingual researcher back translated a quarter (25%) of the translated excerpts; discrepancies were discussed in consensus meetings and the codebook updated accordingly. Culturally specific terms were retained (italicized) with brief bracketed explanations where needed. All quotations presented in the Results are translated from the original Arabic; each translated quote was cross-checked against the source transcript by a second bilingual researcher to ensure semantic equivalence. Reflexive memos documented translation decisions and rationale, forming part of the audit trail and supporting confirmability.

Data analysis

Team-based, codebook thematic analysis following Braun and Clarke’s six-step framework (2006) was used to analyse the data. These steps included which familiarization with the data, generating initial codes, searching for themes, reviewing themes, defining and naming themes, and producing the report [18]. Although our overarching analytic approach was thematic analysis, we incorporated grounded theory techniques such as constant comparison, memo writing, and open coding not as a full grounded theory design, or analytical approach, but to strengthen analytic rigor during data collection and coding.

This integration enabled iterative refinement of the codebook and deeper reflexivity while keeping the analysis focused on identifying patterns and meanings rather than developing a substantive theory. This involved an initial phase of data familiarization through repeated listening. For six interviews, full transcripts were prepared to establish an initial codebook (Supplemental Table 1. Codebook). For the remaining interviews, coding was performed directly from the audio using the established codebook, with constant comparison to ensure consistency and allow new codes to emerge. The codebook developed from the first six transcripts were treated as preliminary and flexible rather than fixed. As analysis progressed, researchers remained attentive to new and emerging codes, and the codebook was iteratively refined and expanded to reflect the full breadth of the data. Regular discussions were held within the research team to review, revise, and agree upon code definitions and structure as new insights emerged. The iterative process ensured that the analysis remained inductive and responsive to the data, rather than constrained by the initial coding framework. Codes were then collated into potential themes, which were reviewed, refined, and clearly defined through iterative discussions weekly. This process drew on both inductive insights emerging from the data and deductive attention to concepts highlighted in the literature. Therefore, the analysis followed a hybrid approach, incorporating both inductive and deductive elements. Initial coding was conducted inductively, allowing themes to emerge directly from the data without being constrained by a pre-existing framework. Subsequently, the identified codes and themes were organized and interpreted using the literature as a guiding structure. This approach enabled the analysis to remain grounded in participants’ perspectives while also situating findings within established frameworks. Coding and theme development were managed in MAXQDA software [17], and an audit trail of coding decisions, memos, and reflections was maintained to ensure transparency. This pragmatic approach ensured feasibility while maintaining analytic rigor, as later interviews were continuously compared against the transcript-based codes for consistency. Encoding and theme construction were the responsibilities of the KA while WA and NS discussed themes and subthemes. Any disagreements in coding or interpretation were resolved through discussion and consensus among the research team, with iterative refinement of codes and themes where needed.

To enhance rigor, discrepancies in coding or theme development were discussed regularly among the research team until consensus was reached. Regular discussions among the researchers improved reliability and minimized individual bias [19]. Saturation was operationalized as the point at which no new codes or concepts appeared in successive interviews; this occurred after the 40th interview, with the remaining interviews used to confirm thematic stability.

Results

A thematic analysis of 60 interviews with home health care providers in Saudi Arabia revealed several key challenges in delivering care to older adults. These challenges were categorized into four overarching themes: disrupted caregiver–provider communication, system fragmentation and resource constraints, environmental and cultural constraints, and emotional burden of provider.

Disrupted caregiver–provider communication

Most participants reported that they sometimes had to call multiple caregivers to obtain information, which was time consuming and increased the risk of miscommunication. Providers described challenges with caregiver competence, consistency, and engagement. These included difficulties contacting caregivers, non-adherence to prescribed care plans, last-minute cancellations, and negligence in patient care. Some caregivers were older adults with their own health issues, limiting their ability to adhere to care plans or recognize medical warning signs, which contributed to care gaps and poorer outcomes.

Many participants expressed concerns about insufficient collaboration between patients’ providers and caregivers. The lack of communication and coordination between caregivers and home health care providers certainly results in unfavorable consequences for patients. However, as one participant stated, caregivers’ responsibilities can extend beyond the household and include employment, making it difficult for them to control the situation. Providers emphasized the need for strategies to improve caregiver consistency, communication, and collaboration. They reported that this would improve the patient experience and home health care outcomes.

Another challenge is that caregivers sometimes do not maintain scheduled visits. This makes it difficult for providers to deliver continuous care and monitor patients’ conditions.

“We visited a patient and found him very ill; [he] needed to be transferred to the emergency room…But the patient’s family refused to do so.” (Social care worker, Southern region).

An issue that emerged repeatedly in the responses was the perceived negligent of certain caregivers. Instead of delivering the attentive and empathetic care that patients needed, they showed a troubling lack of concern about their welfare. Some caregivers neglected to report changes to the medical teams. Caregivers’ negligent actions constituted a breach of their responsibilities, which could have severe implications for patients’ well-being.

“Sometimes, you cannot enter the room because of poor cleanliness.” (Nurse, Western region).

An important concern was the caregivers’ credentials and psychological well-being. Occasionally, caregivers were older adults facing their own health difficulties, which hindered their capacity to effectively adhere to the recommended care plan and remain attentive to patients’ state. Elderly caregivers or caregivers with cognitive impairment may encounter difficulties comprehending medical instructions, accurately administering medications, or identifying signs that necessitate timely communication with healthcare staff. Caregivers’ insufficient capacity might result in care gaps, miscommunication, and inferior patient outcomes.

System fragmentation and resource constraints

Incomplete or inappropriate referrals, misconceptions about home health care, and shortages of staff and supplies emerged as critical challenges. Providers emphasized that fragmented referral pathways, insufficient staffing (especially nurses and physiotherapists), and limited medical resources disrupted continuity of care and hindered service delivery. Providers reported challenges in referrals for home health care in secondary care settings. They reported that these referrals were often incomplete or lacked important information, such as patient diagnosis, treatment, and follow-up plans. This makes it difficult for providers to develop care plans and ensure continuity of care.

“The challenge I am facing is that in safety cases, specialists do not consistently complete the referral form when referring patients.” (Nurse, Southern region).

According to participants, healthcare providers in secondary care occasionally suggested home-based treatment for patients, even if they did not meet the specific requirements for such care. Home health care is designed for patients who are sufficiently medically stable to receive treatment at home. However, participants suggested that healthcare providers often referred patients who were too severely ill to be adequately supported by home caregivers.

Participants emphasized that a key challenge they faced was the complicated nature of the various referral pathways for home health care services. Some participants indicated that individuals seeking medical care might obtain referrals from primary care practitioners or specialized clinics, or by self-reference. However, each route requires distinct procedures and documentation. Owing to the fragmented nature of the system, numerous patients were uncertain about the precise procedure for acquiring the required home health care services. Moreover, the extensive dependence on paper-based referrals poses challenges for patients in monitoring the status and advancement of their requests. Incomplete or inappropriate referrals, misconceptions about HHC, and shortages of staff and supplies emerged as critical challenges. Staff shortages particularly among nurses, physiotherapists, and social care providers constrained visit frequency and service quality. Also, fragmented referral pathways and inconsistent documentation disrupted continuity of care.

“The physician, who was from either a primary care clinic or hospital, has not provided a well-defined care plan for the transition to home health care. The regulation mandates that the patient’s physician must provide a care plan, which includes a specified duration for monitoring the case. The healthcare provider is then required to report the outcome of the care plan back to the physician…But this is not the case…Referral only includes the services required.” (Physician, Southern region).

The scarcity of home health care specialists often impedes the provision of services and collaboration. Physicians providing home health care do not have autonomy and must obtain a care plan from a referral specialist.

“Most physicians in home health care are not specialists, so they must send the patients to specialists in the hospital or outpatient department.” (Nurse, Southern region).

Several participants noted a deficiency in understanding the complete scope and nature of home health care services. Insufficient awareness impedes home health care providers from effectively coordinating patient-centered care and expressing the importance of these essential services to the wider healthcare system.

“The primary obstacles faced by the organization are the lack of awareness or illiteracy among the individuals referring patients regarding the concept of home health care. However, I am confident that they have received instruction through lectures. Nevertheless, there appears to be a lack of practical experience and a limited comprehension of the significance of home health care, as well as the specific situations that necessitate it.” (Nurse, Southern region).

Many patients have an incomplete understanding of the importance and benefits of the wide range of support that high-quality home health care can provide. For instance, individuals may utilize these services exclusively to obtain prescribed drugs or necessary equipment without actively participating in nursing care, therapy, or other comprehensive services that are essential components of home health care programs. Insufficient comprehension can result in less-than-ideal health results, squandered resources, and overlooked chances for patients to attain an improved quality of life and self-sufficiency through comprehensive home-based care.

Human resources

Resource scarcity is also a common problem. Providers said that inadequate resources and community demands adversely affected the disparity between needs and system capacity. The scarcity of human resources poses a key challenge for providers in achieving their visit targets. They frequently reported being unable to meet planned patient loads because of insufficient staffing. This hinders their ability to meet the demand for care and ensure the provision of necessary services to patients.

“We are trying our best to cover all of the patients in a month, but we cannot…We can only visit half of them…We see the patients every two months.” (Nurse, Western region).

Providers stated that the shortage of human resources made it difficult for them to provide continuous care. This makes it difficult for them to get to know patients and provide personalized care.

“Despite the fact that home health care services rely heavily on nursing services, there is currently a lack of nurses.” (GP, Western region).

However, there is a shortage of studies on physiotherapy, social care, and nutrition. Participants indicated a scarcity of these providers, resulting in challenges in obtaining prompt consultations and services for patients. According to their assessment, there were frequent delays of several weeks or even months in the provision of physical therapy to patients, resulting in postponed rehabilitation and treatment.

“The primary obstacles lie in the scarcity of human resources, particularly in the field of physiotherapy. Currently, I only have three male physiotherapists, and we are experiencing a decrease in physical therapy visits.” (Nurse, Western region).

“Nutritionist services are not provided in home health care centers…It is important to consider that the patient’s home condition and the healing process of their bedsores are dynamic factors that can influence their nutritional needs. Therefore, the patient’s nutrition plan should be regularly updated based on their individual circumstances.” (Nurse, Southern region).

The providers stressed the importance of home health care institutions, prioritizing the recruitment and retention of providers to address the scarcity of human resources. They emphasized that resolving human resource shortfalls is crucial for facilitating the delivery of exceptional patient-focused care.

An important issue highlighted by participants was the shortages or gaps in the provision of essential medical equipment, medicines, or nutritional supplies required by patients in their home environment. Patients and their families experience considerable disruptions and frustration owing to the lack of vital supplies. This compromises the efficacy and consistency of the home-based services.

“We are currently experiencing significant shortages, and the patient express[ed] dissatisfaction. A limited number of boxes were distributed among a few individuals, while the remaining people expressed their desire for them.” (Nurse, Northern region).

Environmental and cultural constraints

Providers described the impact of geography, infrastructure, household conditions, and cultural expectations on care delivery. Remote locations, poor road access, inadequate home safety, and cultural preferences regarding provider gender all limited the effectiveness of home health care services. Home health care providers emphasized the considerable logistical challenges that arose from the distinctive environmental and geographical elements of certain regions, such as Makkah and Jeddah. Many patients lived in remote or hilly areas, posing challenges for home health care teams in reaching their residences, especially in adverse weather conditions. The inadequate road infrastructure and congested streets in these neighborhoods exacerbated the challenges of delivering in-home services.

Home health care providers frequently encounter challenging terrain and must traverse long distances on foot to visit patients residing in remote or isolated regions. This imposes substantial time and resource limitations, restricting the capacity to effectively cater to several patients. The providers also indicated that inadequate connectivity, including unstable internet and phone access, posed challenges. Geographical distance, poor road access, and limited connectivity hindered providers’ ability to reach patients.

Patients’ home environments and living conditions significantly affect the quality and efficacy of HHC services. Factors like housing, cleanliness, safety, and accessibility play a role in determining the success of home-based care interventions.

There were some negative comments about sub-optimal home environments characterized by issues like structural deficiencies, lack of cleanliness, fall hazards, and poor accessibility, which can undermine their ability to deliver comprehensive and effective care. Patients living in such environments are at an increased risk of adverse health outcomes, complications, and failed transitions to independent living. Home conditions often posed safety risks, such as poor cleanliness or lack of assistive devices.

Home health care providers emphasized that cultural variables severely limited the pool of available healthcare providers. Preference for a male or female provider is a constraint, thereby diminishing the total capacity of the home health care system. Cultural preferences also restricted provider assignments, particularly gender-based requests that limited scheduling flexibility.

“The caregiver declines to share his sister’s contact information with the providers, despite the fact that she is the one residing at home.” (Nurse, Northern region).

Emotional burden of care

Providers reported that repeated exposure to patient suffering, poor living conditions, and emotionally demanding care environments affected their mental health. Stress, compassion fatigue, and vicarious trauma were commonly described. Home health care jobs frequently expose healthcare providers to emotionally demanding conditions. Home health care providers gain access to their patients’ personal homes, allowing them to witness the intimate aspects of their patients’ lives and health difficulties. They often encounter a consistent stream of pain and loss, ranging from observing the physical and cognitive decline of older adults to assisting families in dealing with terminal illnesses.

Continuous exposure to challenging life conditions and susceptible individuals can substantially affect home health care providers’ mental well-being. As one participant recalled, healthcare providers may encounter elevated stress, compassion fatigue, and vicarious trauma when they internalize the emotional burden of their patients’ experiences. Feelings of powerlessness when confronted with deep-seated human needs can have a significant psychological impact. Providers reported that repeated exposure to suffering and poor living conditions affected their emotional well-being, leading to stress and compassion fatigue.

“I am unable to erase the memory of returning to my residence. I recall each visit during which I experienced feelings of depression.” (Nurse, Northern region).

Discussion

This study provides an in-depth qualitative examinations of home health care in Saudi Arabia from the perspective of frontline providers. Our findings underscore the toll these systemic barriers take on providers’ mental health an issue rarely documented in the regional literature. The results highlight that while infrastructure and policy frameworks exist, the sustainability of home health care will depend on strengthening the workforce, integrating referral pathways, and addressing the psychosocial needs of both patients and providers.

Caregiver-related barriers identified in this study appear to reflect broader structural and cultural factors shaping Saudi Arabia’s home health care system. At the individual level, limited caregiver competency and inconsistent adherence to care plans may stem from insufficient training, unclear role delineation, and the absence of formal accreditation mechanisms for family and paid caregivers [20]. Similar workforce preparation challenges have been reported internationally, where informal caregivers often assume responsibilities without adequate professional support [21, 22]. This highlights the need for standardized caregiver training and supervision frameworks.

At the environmental level, cultural expectations of family caregiving, while deeply rooted, may inadvertently overburden untrained relatives an issue similarly observed in collectivist societies such as China [23]. Policy reforms that balance familial responsibility with institutional support could enhance both care quality and caregiver well-being. Integrating volunteer and community-based support models may also mitigate workforce shortages. Additionally, gender-related preferences expressed by homecare recipients and their family members should not be viewed in isolation, but rather in interaction with organizational factors such as staffing availability and scheduling constraints, which may shape how such preferences are accommodated in practice and how this may influence provider perspectives.

At the organizational level, systemic barriers, particularly fragmented referral pathways, reflect gaps in intersectoral coordination rather than resource scarcity. The lack of standardized referral procedures between hospitals, primary care, and home health services mirrors global patterns reported in Europe and the United States, where poor communication and unclear accountability hinder continuity of care [24, 25]. Strengthening digital health integration and adopting unified referral protocols could improve transition efficiency and patient safety. Participants’ accounts indicated that fragmented referral pathways and communication gaps were major barriers to continuity of care. These findings suggest that improved referral management systems such as digital or telehealth-enabled tools for remote consultations could potentially streamline coordination, enhance efficiency, improve patient safety, and reduce malpractice risks. While participants did not explicitly reference digital solutions, this implication was derived from the broader analysis and supported by international literature highlighting the role of technology in referral integration [26].

In Europe, effective coordination between HHC and hospital services involves liaison nurses, social workers, and computerized patient records. For example, Finland maintains consistent records and Germany has legislation to support hospital-to-home transitions [27]. Across diverse healthcare systems, inadequate support, workforce shortages, and workload strain consistently impact provider well-being and continuity of patient care [28]. Beyond structural challenges, the human impact of these systemic issues manifests as provider fatigue and emotional burden.

The mental and physical toll on home health care providers is another global concern. One study found that over 25% of HHC workers rated their health as fair or poor, with 20% reporting mental health struggles and 14% facing physical health issues [29]. In addition to documenting systemic and logistical barriers, our study highlights the psychological toll on home health care providers a novel contribution to the regional literature. These findings align with occupational health frameworks that recognize psychosocial risks such as burnout, compassion fatigue, and vicarious trauma as critical workplace hazards. These concepts were deductively inferred from participants’ narratives describing emotional exhaustion, empathy fatigue, and stress, rather than being directly measured. Integrating mental health support into HHC systems including stress-management training, confidential counseling access, and institutional recognition of emotional strain could help sustain provider well-being and service quality. Positioning Emotional burden of care within established occupational health models underscores the importance of protecting the workforce not only as a moral imperative but also as a prerequisite for sustainable and high-quality care delivery [30–32].

International evidence from Greece, Taiwan, and the United States [33–36] similarly underscores how workforce limitations and lack of institutional support contribute to provider burnout and fragmented care, confirming that the challenges observed in Saudi Arabia align with global patterns in home healthcare delivery. At the policy and systems level, unlike Western systems, Saudi HHC providers operate within a rapidly transforming, centrally governed healthcare model, where system restructuring and rapid expansion interact with cultural and logistical constraints. This combination creates a unique set of implementation challenges that differ from those in decentralized or privately driven Western systems. The broader relevance of these findings extends to other Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) and Middle Eastern countries undergoing similar healthcare transformations. Strengthening provider training, formalizing referral structures, and integrating mental health support into home healthcare delivery could inform region-wide policy reforms aimed at improving continuity, equity, and workforce sustainability.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, although coding was conducted directly from audio recordings rather than full verbatim transcripts for most interviews, efforts were made to preserve analytic rigor through repeated audio review and team-based consensus during coding. Second, participants were recruited through home care facilities, which may introduce potential selection and social desirability biases, particularly if participants felt influenced by organizational dynamics. To mitigate this, participation was voluntary, and confidentiality was emphasized throughout the study. Also, there is the potential for self-selection bias as the respondents volunteered their participation. Because certain regions and home health care systems were not included, the study’s transferability to all Saudi healthcare contexts may be limited. Participants’ professional or personal perspectives could have shaped their responses, potentially influencing the depth of data interpretation. Also, interviews were conducted using a combination of remote and face-to-face formats, which may have influenced the depth and nature of participant responses. Although no clear differences were identified during analysis, the potential influence of interview mode on data richness cannot be excluded. Although the sample size was sufficient for thematic saturation, the findings reflect contextual insights rather than statistical generalizability. Additionally, the absence of perspectives from policymakers, family caregivers, and patients limits a comprehensive understanding of systemic interdependencies in HHC delivery. Future qualitative research incorporating broader stakeholder groups and diverse healthcare settings could enhance transferability and inform policy development.

Conclusions and implications

The findings indicate that current challenges in Saudi Arabia’s home health care system stem primarily from workforce shortages, fragmented referral systems, and insufficient caregiver preparation.

Implications for practice

Strengthening workforce capacity should include targeted training in geriatrics, palliative care, and chronic disease management; the expansion of continuing professional development programs for home health care providers; and financial or career incentives to improve recruitment and retention. Structured caregiver training and community engagement programs are essential to improve adherence, communication, and patient outcomes.

Implications for policy

Our study results also underscore some suggested priorities in terms of policy. To address fragmented referral systems, digital referral platforms integrated with electronic health records should be established, referral pathways standardized across care levels, and hospital discharge planning aligned with home health care services. Collectively, these strategies align with Saudi Vision 2030’s Health Transformation Program, which emphasizes integrated, community-based, and patient-centered geriatric care. By embedding these measures within national policies, Saudi Arabia can build a sustainable, high-performing home health care system.

Implications for future research

Future qualitative and mixed-methods studies should incorporate perspectives from policymakers, patients, and family caregivers to capture systemic interdependencies and inform policy adaptation. Longitudinal and intervention-based research could further evaluate how workforce development and digital integration influence service continuity and provider well-being.

Collectively, these targeted strategies align with Saudi Vision 2030’s Health Transformation Program. This alignment underscores the translational relevance of our findings for policymakers seeking to operationalize Vision 2030 goals within the home health care sector. In sum, qualitative interviews with 60 home health care providers revealed valuable insights into the daily challenges and stressors that affect frontline service delivery. As an exploratory study, the open-ended design facilitated the identification of previously unexamined issues and new areas for future research, laying a foundation for further investigations and informing future policy discussions.
