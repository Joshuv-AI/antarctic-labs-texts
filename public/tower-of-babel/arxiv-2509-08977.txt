Symmetries in stochastic homogenization and acclimatizations for the RVE method
Binh Huy Nguyen, Matti Schneider

Abstract
We investigate the implications of a given symmetry of a random microstructure on the obtained effective tensor and its fluctuation in the context of thermal conductivity, and study strategies for enforcing these symmetries in postprocessing via orthogonal projectors. Within the framework of the representative volume element (RVE) method, we establish the invariance conditions for the effective tensor and its fluctuation under different symmetry groups of the microstructure. Interestingly, the symmetry of the considered cell type in the RVE method may break the ensemble symmetry and compromise the approximation of the effective properties. To rectify this issue, we introduce dedicated techniques which permit to enforce the expected symmetries in postprocessing and study the implications on the bounds for the effective properties as well as the total, the random and the systematic errors. We provide theoretical arguments that suitable projections lead to unbiased variance-reduction strategies which furthermore enforce the expected symmetries exactly. Through large-scale FFT-based homogenization simulations, we study the symmetry structure of the estimated effective conductivities and their fluctuations. Moreover, we demonstrate the power of the symmetry-projection techniques for fiber-reinforced composite microstructures of industrial scale. Keywords: Random microstructure; Stochastic homogenization; Symmetry groups; Variance reduction; Thermal conductivity

Introduction

1.1
State of the art

In the modern age of digital material design, computational homogenization has became an indispensable tool for various engineering applications where the material behavior at the macroscopic scale can be linked to their microscopic features. Irrespective of the intricacies of the microstructure involved, i.e., multiple phases or sophisticated inclusion geometries, the effective material behavior turns out to be quite simple, in general, provided the microstructure scale is sufficiently small compared to the considered component scale. Identifying such emergent behavior is the goal of homogenization method, which resolves a microscale boundary value problem and employs the resulting solutions to extract the macroscopic behavior
[
1
]
. Homogenization methods can be helpful in various ways. For architected materials, periodic homogenization methods employing periodic unit cells turn out to be appropriate. For materials with random microstructure such as metal matrix composite or reinforced fibers composite, stochastic homogenization is more favorable.

While the effective properties of architected material are obtained by considering the periodic unit cell, material with random microstructure are more challenging. For a start, the aleatoric uncertainty of the microstructure necessitates a stochastic quantification. These approaches often rely on numerical simulations to study the influence of microstructure descriptors such as geometric shapes, grain size distributions, spatial arrangements and aspect ratio on the overall macroscopic material properties. For instance, in Hiriyur et al.
[
2
]
, the extended finite element method (XFEM) in combination with the Monte Carlo method was employed to quantify the uncertainty of the effective elastic Young’s modulus of heterogeneous microstructures consisting of circular and elliptic inclusions in a two dimensional (2D) unit cell of fixed size. Stefanou et al.
[
3
]
performed a similar study to extract statistical information, including the probability distribution and the correlation function of the so-called mesoscale tensor random field in 2D elasticity. While it is important to study the influence of microstructure in a unit cell of fixed size, these numerical studies are not sufficient to incorporate the central object in stochastic homogenization, namely the representative volume element (RVE). Introduced by Hill
[
4
]
, the RVE should be statistically typical for the mixture and sufficiently large such that the apparent property is independent of the boundary conditions. This definition, however, is too strict for practical considerations, often leading to large volume elements, and rather serves as a guiding principle than a law to be rigidly enforced. Relaxing on Hill’s RVE definition, Drugan and Willis
[
5
]
proposed RVE to be a volume element whose apparent properties represent the effective constitutive response with sufficient accuracy. During the last three decades, several alternative definitions of RVEs were proposed. For instance, Evesque
[
6
]
postulated that the determination of the minimum volume of the RVE should take into account the local fluctuations from one RVE to the other. In a series of works on the statistical representative volume element (SVE), Ostoja-Starzewski and co-workers
[
7
,
8
,
9
]
determined the RVE size based on the statistical homogeneity and ergodicity, Hill’s homogeneity condition
[
4
]
and the underlying variational principle. In the case of a carbon-reinforced fiber composite, Trias et al.
[
10
]
estimated the minimum characteristic length of an SVE to be 50 times the fiber radius. Other alternative RVE definitions were surveyed by Gitman et al.
[
11
]
and El Moumen et al.
[
12
]
. To assert Drugan and Willis’s RVE criteria
[
5
]
, Kanit et al.
[
13
]
investigated the RVE size via a statistical approach by combining FEM and Monte-Carlo method, where the apparent properties of several random realizations of microstructures with different sizes were monitored against its empirical mean for a fixed volume size as well as against the effective property of a very large volume element size. These errors are coined as dispersion (or random) and bias (or systematic) errors, respectively. Additionally, the authors studied the impact of three different boundary conditions, namely Dirichlet, Neumann and periodic boundary conditions. Their work finds that periodic boundary conditions yields the closest approximation of the effective properties.

Parallel to the findings from the engineering community, the mathematical community was also active in this area. The mathematical theory of stochastic homogenization of a stationary and ergodic coefficient field defines the (deterministic) effective properties abstractly, but their evaluation requires computations on the whole space. To put it differently, a single infinitely large volume element almost surely provides the effective material property. The mathematical approach permits to study the RVE method
[
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
]
more closely, which approximates the effective properties via an ensemble of possibly periodized finite-size realization of the microstructure. Mathematical results do not only show convergence of the apparent properties obtained by the RVE method to the deterministic effective property as the volume element size approaches infinity but may also be used to quantify the rate of convergence. Specifically, in the works of Gloria and Otto
[
18
]
and Gloria et al.
[
19
]
, the authors quantified the optimal convergence rates of the random and systematic errors, demonstrating a guideline to identify RVE based on desired accuracy of the effective property approximation, at least for simple type of microstructure. To numerically verify the convergence rate for 2D conductivity coefficients as a function of the volume element size, Khoromskaia et al.
[
20
]
employed an efficient FEM discretization in combination with a Monte Carlo method. In a comprehensive work with elaborated microstructure generators and advanced FFT-based computational homogenization methods, Schneider et al.
[
21
]
not only attested the optimal convergence rate of the RVE method but also shed light on the superior asymptotic convergence of the periodized ensemble over the snap-shot ensemble which naturally arises in digital imaging. Furthermore, in addition to the microscopic oscillations, the RVE method in stochastic homogenization also characterizes the macroscopic fluctuation. By recognizing the H-convergence criterion that relates the microscopic oscillatory flux to the product of homogenized tensor and oscillatory field, Duerinckx et al.
[
22
,
23
]
proposed the homogenization commutator and its associated two-scale expansion, namely the standard homogenization commutator, to capture and quantify the macroscopic fluctuations. The leading-order term of such fluctuations is determined by the variance of the homogenization commutator, which may also be represented as the scaled covariance of the apparent tensors in the RVE method. For the scalar-valued elliptic problem, this fluctuation is represented by a fourth-order
\displaystyle\mu
Q-tensor, which may be obtained at no extra cost within the RVE method as shown in
[
20
]
for the 2D cubic setting. A similar concept of the spatial correlation function of random field apparent tensor was studied in the SVE approach
[
24
,
25
,
9
]
. In this method, by virtue of a moving window on a realized microstructure and Hill’s macrohomogeneity condition, the apparent material tensor arises as a random field and is therefore scale-dependent. Numerical studies of the correlation structure of elastic random field in 2D were investigated in different works
[
26
,
27
,
3
,
28
]
.

In homogenization theory, the random heterogeneous microstructures determine the macroscopic properties and thus encode the inherent material symmetries via the statistics of their representatives. Leveraging symmetry in homogenization may be advantageous, especially for periodic homogenization. For instance, as studied in Barbarosie et al.
[
29
]
, a smaller unit cell with proper symmetry-constrained periodic boundary conditions, determined from the group representation of the domain symmetry, was employed in a finite element program to reduce the computational expense from a larger domain for determining effective material tensor of two-phase planar composite. The concepts of plane and space group symmetry of the unit cell were also utilized to construct a large database of periodic micro-architectures in combination with topology optimization for the inverse homogenization by Podestá et al.
[
30
]
and Méndez et al.
[
31
]
. By establishing the relation between lattice and material symmetries, Giusteri and Penta
[
32
]
successfully identified a unit cell symmetry of a locally isotropic two-phase composite with spherical inclusions such that the homogenized elastic tensor exhibited isotropy. Prior to these works, in a more general setting, Ptashnyk and Seguin
[
33
]
formulated the invariance condition of the effective elastic tensor in periodic homogenization under a volume-preserving affine transformation. On the other hand, in stochastic homogenization, based on the probability space setting from Papanicolaou
[
34
]
, Alexanderian
[
35
]
showed how ensemble symmetries lead to invariance properties of the effective second-order diffusion tensor, also performing numerical experiments to verify isotropy invariance of tile-based 2D random media. A similar invariance condition was also described in Zhikov et al.
[
36
]
and demonstrated explicitly for some simple 2D problems. It should be noted that the invariance conditions in these works are prescribed on the deterministic effective tensor. In practice, the apparent material tensor, often obtained from a cubic volume element, is not only tensor-value random variable but also non-invariant with respect to its associated ensemble symmetry. In elasticity, it is well known that the symmetry class of a fourth-order elastic tensor is approximated by the its distance to a known symmetry class
[
37
,
38
,
39
]
. However, the ramification of such approximation in the context of stochastic homogenization for the apparent material tensor is not discussed at length, although it is recognized that the shape of the computational volume element has an effect on the convergence and symmetry of the apparent tensors
[
40
,
41
]
. As the cubic volume element unintentionally breaks the ensemble symmetry, the use of spherical volume element may remedy such undesired consequences by preserving the ensemble symmetry. This feature was numerically demonstrated in references
[
40
,
41
]
within the SVE framework. On the related works, the invariance of the one-point expectation and two-point covariance function of wide-sense stationary tensor random field such as conductivity and linear elasticity under different symmetry class was provided by Malyarenko and Ostoja-Starzewski
[
42
,
43
,
44
]
based on group representation theory. In case the microheterogeneity information is unavailable, whether from digital generation or experimental acquisition, the material tensor random field is assumed to follow a specific probabilistic model. Soize
[
45
]
proposed to generate non-Gaussian, positive definite elastic tensor random fields depending on four parameters, i.e., three correlation lengths and a level of random fluctuation, and employed the results as an a priori mesoscale material tensor in stochastic homogenization
[
46
]
. Meanwhile, Guilleminot and Soize
[
47
]
constructed a probabilistic model of a tensor random field with underlying symmetry from the maximum entropy principle. Thanks to the small number of parameters, these probabilistic tensor random field modeling approaches were also incorporated with stochastic finite elements to identify multi-scale elastic material parameters
[
48
]
. More recently, Shivanand et al.
[
49
]
employed a combination of spectral decomposition and the exponential map to model different class of invariant stochastic positive definite second- and fourth-order elastic tensors.

Despite of different paths to translate the uncertainties from the micro-heterogeneity into the macroscopic-scale properties, the common goal of stochastic homogenization is to provide an uncertainty-quantified apparent material tensor, i.e., the associated statistical means and covariances, for the macroscopic analysis whose solutions and the deduced physical quantities are inherently stochastic. As mentioned earlier, the RVE method provides a theoretical probe on the fluctuation of macroscopic observables, namely the
\displaystyle\mu
Q-tensor
[
22
,
23
]
.
It appears clear that the symmetries of the ensemble are not only inherited by the homogenized tensor but also its fluctuation. Such interplay between symmetry and randomness also has practical implication. For instance, in case the variance of one component of the homogenized material tensor is characterized, the variance of other components may be deduced without the need for additional experiments. Thus, the objective of this paper is to investigate the ramifications of symmetries in stochastic homogenization using RVE method, particularly the apparent material tensor and its corresponding
\displaystyle\mu
Q-tensor.

1.2
Contributions

In this work, we aim to investigate the implications of symmetries in stochastic homogenization, specifically on the apparent material tensor and its associated
\displaystyle\mu
Q-tensor. From a practical point of view, the
\displaystyle\mu
Q-tensor not only quantifies the fluctuation of the macroscopic tensor but also characterizes the interrelations between the random errors of the apparent coefficients. Put differently, the structure of the
\displaystyle\mu
Q-tensor allows the uncertainty quantification of one material coefficient from that of the other. For the demonstrative purpose, we choose to work with the prototypical problem in stochastic homogenization theory, which is the scalar-valued elliptic equation for the static heat conduction, and leads to a quartic
\displaystyle\mu
Q-tensor. Concretely, we make the following contributions:

•

We establish the symmetries of the effective tensor and its associated fluctuation
\displaystyle\mu
Q-tensor as consequences of invariances of the ensemble.

•

We clarify the symmetry breaking induced by the shape of the volume element.

•

We introduce and study symmetry-enforcing projectors to improve the RVE method.

For ease of construction, we recall the analytic setting of the stochastic homogenization from a stationary and ergodic ensemble in section
2.1
with an emphasis on the emergence of the effective conductivity tensor. Section
2.2
lays out the practical RVE method to approximate the effective conductivity tensor by an apparent quantity via a proxy problem, defined on a general computational volume element. The apparent conductivity properties are quantified in terms of the random and the systematic error, decaying at different rates, in addition to their complimentary fluctuation tensor. As long as symmetry is concerned, the first driving question of the paper at hand is:

What is the impact of a given symmetry of the underlying ensemble on the apparent conductivity tensor and its associated
\displaystyle\mu
Q-tensor?

In section
2.3
, we rely on the probability measure and propose the invariance conditions of the effective and apparent conductivity tensors and their corresponding
\displaystyle\mu
Q-tensors, which is stricter than the condition proposed by Zhikov et al.
[
36
]
and Alexandrian
[
35
]
. More importantly, the focal point is placed on the breaking of the ensemble symmetry by the geometry of the computational volume element in the RVE method. To put it in a different perspective, the discrepancy between the apparent and the effective conductivity tensors are caused by the lack of symmetry of the unit cell. This leads us to the second mission:

How to remedy the lack of symmetry of the apparent conductivity tensor and its associated
\displaystyle\mu
Q-tensor induced by cubic cells?

We utilize the equivalent interpretation of an orthogonal projector as an averaging operator to extract the invariant part of the apparent conductivity tensor and its associated
\displaystyle\mu
Q-tensor in section
3.1
. Consequently, an a posteriori symmetry-informed strategy is proposed to improve the approximation errors in section
3.2
. In addition to the explicit demonstrations of invariance condition and the projection, we further demonstrate our claims through computational homogenization of various ensemble symmetry of short fiber-reinforced composite in section
4
. Discussions on the results are given in section
5
.

2
Stochastic homogenization with symmetries

2.1
Stochastic homogenization of thermal conductivity

In this section, we recall some parts of the theory of stochastic homogenization
[
34
,
36
]
, and refer to Neukamm
[
50
, §2.2]
for an accessible introduction.

We consider the phase space of conductivity tensors defined on the whole space,

\mathcal{A}=\left\{\bm{A}\in L^{\infty}(\mathds{R}^{d};\textrm{Sym}(d))\,\middle|\,\alpha_{-}\,\|\bm{\xi}\|^{2}\leq\bm{\xi}\cdot\bm{A}(\bm{x})\bm{\xi}\leq\alpha_{+}\,\|\bm{\xi}\|^{2},\quad\bm{\xi}\in\mathds{R}^{d},\quad\text{a.e. }\bm{x}\in\mathds{R}^{d}\right\},

(2.1)

in
\displaystyle d
spatial dimensions, where
\displaystyle\textrm{Sym}(d)
denotes the vector space of symmetric
\displaystyle d\times d
tensors and
\displaystyle\alpha_{\pm}
are given positive constants. Stochastic homogenization is encoded by a probability measure
\displaystyle\mu
on the space (
2.1
) which we assume to be stationary and ergodic. To express these notions mathematically, we denote by

\left\langle{F}\right\rangle\equiv\int_{\mathcal{A}}F(\bm{A})\,d\mu(\bm{A})

(2.2)

the ensemble average of an integrable function
\displaystyle F
on
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
, i.e.,
\displaystyle F\in L^{1}(\mathcal{A},\mu)
. We assume that the shift operator

\tau:\mathds{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathcal{A},\quad(\bm{z},\bm{A})\mapsto\bm{A}(\cdot+\bm{z})\equiv\tau_{\bm{z}}(\bm{A}),

(2.3)

is measurable. Then,
stationarity
means that the identity

\left\langle{F}\right\rangle=\left\langle{F\circ\tau_{\bm{z}}}\right\rangle

(2.4)

holds for all shift vectors
\displaystyle\bm{z}\in\mathds{R}^{d}
and any integrable function
\displaystyle F\in L^{1}(\mathcal{A},\mu)
. The condition (
2.4
) encodes the fact that the shifting operation (
2.3
) does not influence the outcome of reasonable observables.

In contrast,
ergodicity
ensures the validity of the equation

\left\langle{F}\right\rangle=\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{Y^{\textrm{cub}}_{L}}F(\tau_{\bm{x}}(\bm{A}))\,d\bm{x}

(2.5)

for
\displaystyle\mu
-almost every element
\displaystyle\bm{A}\in\mathcal{A}
, every integrable function
\displaystyle F\in L^{1}(\mathcal{A},\mu)
and where we write

Y^{\textrm{cub}}_{L}=\left\{\bm{x}\in\mathds{R}^{d}\,\middle|\,-\frac{L}{2}\leq x_{i}\leq\frac{L}{2}\text{ for }i=1,2,\ldots,d\right\}

(2.6)

for the cube with edge length
\displaystyle L
centered at the origin. The ergodicity property (
2.5
) ensures that an ensemble average (
2.2
) may be approximated by a spatial average on
a single realization
(almost surely). Loosely speaking, a single realization contains the variations encoded by all other realizations, at least almost surely.

The assumed properties permit to define the effective conductivity tensor

\bm{A}^{\textrm{eff}}\in\textrm{Sym}(d),

(2.7)

which also obeys the bounds

\alpha_{-}\,\|\bm{\xi}\|^{2}\leq\bm{\xi}\cdot\bm{A}^{\textrm{eff}}\bm{\xi}\leq\alpha_{+}\,\|\bm{\xi}\|^{2}\quad\text{for all}\quad\bm{\xi}\in\mathds{R}^{d}.

(2.8)

These effective properties (
2.7
) are useful for approximating, for almost every coefficient field
\displaystyle\bm{A}\in\mathcal{A}
, the solution
\displaystyle\phi_{\epsilon}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
to the problem

-\textrm{div }\left(\bm{A}_{\epsilon}\nabla\phi_{\epsilon}\right)=f-\textrm{div }g\quad\text{with}\quad\bm{A}_{\epsilon}(\bm{x})=\bm{A}\left(\frac{\bm{x}}{\epsilon}\right),

(2.9)

where
\displaystyle\Omega\subseteq\mathds{R}^{d}
is an arbitrary bounded domain,
\displaystyle\epsilon>0
is a small positive parameter and
\displaystyle f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
as well as
\displaystyle g\in L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}
are deterministic. More precisely, the unique solution
\displaystyle\phi_{0}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
to the problem

-\textrm{div }\left(\bm{A}^{\textrm{eff}}\nabla\phi_{0}\right)=f-\textrm{div }g

(2.10)

with homogeneous coefficients arises as the limit of the problems (
2.9
) as
\displaystyle\epsilon\rightarrow 0
in the sense:

1.

The solutions
\displaystyle\phi_{\epsilon}
converge to
\displaystyle\phi_{0}
weakly in
\displaystyle H^{1}(\Omega)
, i.e.,

\|\phi_{\epsilon}-\phi_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\rightarrow 0\quad\text{and}\quad\int_{\Omega}\psi\,\partial_{i}\phi_{\epsilon}\,d\bm{x}\rightarrow\int_{\Omega}\psi\,\partial_{i}\phi_{0}\,d\bm{x}

(2.11)

as
\displaystyle\epsilon\rightarrow 0
for all smooth fields
\displaystyle\psi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
and all indices
\displaystyle i=1,2,\ldots,d
.

2.

The associated fluxes
\displaystyle\bm{A}_{\epsilon}\nabla\phi_{\epsilon}
converge to the flux
\displaystyle\bm{A}^{\textrm{eff}}\nabla\phi_{0}
weakly in
\displaystyle L^{2}(\Omega)
, i.e.,

\int_{\Omega}\bm{\psi}\cdot\bm{A}_{\epsilon}\nabla\phi_{\epsilon}\,d\bm{x}\rightarrow\int_{\Omega}\bm{\psi}\cdot\bm{A}^{\textrm{eff}}\nabla\phi_{0}\,d\bm{x}

(2.12)

as
\displaystyle\epsilon\rightarrow 0
for all smooth fields
\displaystyle\bm{\psi}\in C^{\infty}(\Omega)^{d}
.

Loosely speaking, the effective properties (
2.7
) permit to approximate solutions to highly heterogeneous and stochastic problems (
2.9
) by solutions to the homogeneous and deterministic problem (
2.12
)
on average
, at least in case the parameter
\displaystyle\epsilon
is small.

2.2
Apparent tensors on unit cells

To compute the effective conductivity (
2.7
) in practice, the conventional strategy proceeds via suitable proxy problems on cells of finite size. In principle,
any
bounded domain

Y\subseteq\mathds{R}^{d}

(2.13)

can be used. In computational practice, rectangular cells are typically preferred, i.e.,

Y^{\textrm{rect}}_{L_{1},\ldots,L_{d}}=\left\{\bm{x}\in\mathds{R}^{d}\,\middle|\,-\frac{L_{i}}{2}\leq x_{i}\leq\frac{L_{i}}{2}\text{ for }i=1,2,\ldots,d\right\}

(2.14)

for positive real numbers
\displaystyle L_{i}
(
\displaystyle i=1,2,\ldots,d
), and the most popular unit cell is the cube (
2.6
)

Y^{\textrm{cub}}_{L}=\left\{\bm{x}\in\mathds{R}^{d}\,\middle|\,-\frac{L}{2}\leq x_{i}\leq\frac{L}{2}\text{ for }i=1,2,\ldots,d\right\}

(2.15)

for
\displaystyle L>0
. It is also possible to consider
spherical
unit cells
[
40
,
41
]
, i.e., those which have the form

Y^{\textrm{sph}}_{R}\equiv B_{R}\equiv\left\{\bm{x}\in\mathds{R}^{d}\,\middle|\,\|\bm{x}\|\leq R\right\}

(2.16)

with a positive radius
\displaystyle R
.

Let us return to a
general cell
(
2.13
) and discuss the approach to compute approximations of the effective properties (
2.7
). For a prescribed vector
\displaystyle\bar{\bm{\xi}}
and a given realization
\displaystyle\bm{A}\in\mathcal{A}
, the unit-cell corrector
\displaystyle\phi_{Y,\bm{A},\bar{\bm{\xi}}}
is defined as the unique element
\displaystyle\phi\in H^{1}_{0}(Y)
which solves the cell problem

\textrm{div }\bm{A}(\bar{\bm{\xi}}+\nabla\phi)=0.

(2.17)

Thus, we consider vanishing Dirichlet boundary conditions on the cell
\displaystyle Y
. The results which we discuss also hold for other boundary conditions like Neumann or periodic boundary conditions, which are only sensible for rectangular cells (
2.14
).

As the solutions
\displaystyle\phi_{Y,\bm{A},\bar{\bm{\xi}}}
are linear in the vector
\displaystyle\bar{\bm{\xi}}\in\mathds{R}^{d}
, the associated apparent conductivity is defined implicitly by flux averaging

\bm{A}^{\textrm{app}}_{Y}\bar{\bm{\xi}}=\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{Y}\bm{A}(\bar{\bm{\xi}}+\nabla\phi_{Y,\bm{A},\bar{\bm{\xi}}})\,d\bm{x}.

(2.18)

The apparent conductivity (
2.18
) is symmetric, i.e.,
\displaystyle\bm{A}^{\textrm{app}}_{Y}\in\textrm{Sym}(d)
, and satisfies the bounds

\alpha_{-}\,\|\bm{\xi}\|^{2}\leq\bm{\xi}\cdot\bm{A}^{\textrm{app}}_{Y}\bm{\xi}\leq\alpha_{+}\,\|\bm{\xi}\|^{2}\quad\text{for all}\quad\bm{\xi}\in\mathds{R}^{d}

(2.19)

for almost every realization
\displaystyle\bm{A}\in\mathcal{A}
, i.e., identical estimates as the effective properties (
2.8
). Due to the finitude of the cell
\displaystyle Y
, the apparent conductivity is a random variable, in contrast to the effective conductivity (
2.7

The apparent properties (
2.18
) may be used to approximate the effective conductivity (
2.7
). More precisely, it holds that

\bm{A}^{\textrm{app}}_{LY}\rightarrow\bm{A}^{\textrm{eff}}\quad\text{as}\quad L\rightarrow\infty,

(2.20)

almost surely, where
\displaystyle LY
refers to the unit cell (
2.13
), rescaled by a positive factor
\displaystyle L>0
. We refer to Bourgeat-Piatnitskii
[
14
]
for proofs. Their arguments are restricted to the rectangular cell (
2.14
), but carry over seamlessly to the general case (
2.13
), at least for pure Dirichlet and pure Neumann boundary conditions. In a nutshell, the key argument is to recognize the unit-cell problem (
2.17
) for the corrector
\displaystyle\phi_{LY,\bm{A},\bar{\bm{\xi}}}
as a problem of the form (
2.9
) with parameter
\displaystyle\epsilon=1/L
and pass to the limit (
2.10
). Due to the homogeneity of the limit problem

\textrm{div }\bm{A}^{\textrm{eff}}(\bar{\bm{\xi}}+\nabla\phi_{0})=0,

(2.21)

the fluctuation
\displaystyle\phi_{0}\in H^{1}_{0}(Y)
needs to vanish, and we obtain the convergence statement (
2.20

2.12
) of the fluxes.

In case the error

\texttt{err}^{\textrm{tot}}_{Y}:=\|\bm{A}^{\textrm{app}}_{Y}-\bm{A}^{\textrm{eff}}\|

(2.22)

2.13

[
13
]

\texttt{err}^{\textrm{ran}}_{Y}:=\sqrt{\left\langle{\left\|\bm{A}^{\textrm{app}}_{Y}-\left\langle{\bm{A}^{\textrm{app}}_{Y}}\right\rangle\right\|^{2}}\right\rangle},

(2.24)

also called dispersion, and the systematic error

\texttt{err}^{\textrm{sys}}_{Y}:=\left\|\left\langle{\bm{A}^{\textrm{app}}_{Y}}\right\rangle-\bm{A}^{\textrm{eff}}\right\|,

(2.25)

2.25

2.24

51

52

[
53

54

55

\texttt{err}^{\textrm{sys}}_{LY}\sim\frac{1}{L}

[
56

21
]
lead to the strongly improved decay

\texttt{err}^{\textrm{sys}}_{LY}\sim\frac{1}{L^{d}}

(2.27)

\displaystyle d

[

. In contrast, the random error (
2.24
) typically decays with the rate

\texttt{err}^{\textrm{ran}}_{LY}\sim\frac{1}{L^{\frac{d}{2}}}

(2.28)

expected by the central limit theorem, although exceptions are known
[

]
.

To quantify the fluctuations of the apparent conductivity (
2.18
), we follow Duerinckx et al.
[
22
]
, and define the cell-associated
\displaystyle\mu
Q-tensor

\mathds{Q}_{Y}=\text{vol}(Y)\left\langle{\left(\bm{A}^{\textrm{app}}_{Y}-\left\langle{\bm{A}^{\textrm{app}}_{Y}}\right\rangle\right)\otimes\left(\bm{A}^{\textrm{app}}_{Y}-\left\langle{\bm{A}^{\textrm{app}}_{Y}}\right\rangle\right)}\right\rangle

(2.29)

for the scenario (
2.28
) as the rescaled covariance tensor of the apparent properties (
2.18
). For almost every realization
\displaystyle\bm{A}
, the fourth-order tensor
\displaystyle\mathds{Q}_{Y}
defines an element of the space
\displaystyle\textrm{Sym}(\textrm{Sym}(d))
, i.e., it possesses the major symmetry

\bm{B}:\mathds{Q}_{Y}:\bm{C}=\bm{C}:\mathds{Q}_{Y}:\bm{B},\quad\bm{B},\bm{C}\in\textrm{Sym}(d),

(2.30)

due to its definition (
2.29
) as a covariance, and the minor symmetries inherited by the apparent properties
\displaystyle\bm{A}^{\textrm{app}}_{Y}
. Moreover, the apparent
\displaystyle\mu
Q-tensor (
2.29
) is positive semidefinite

\bm{B}:\mathds{Q}_{Y}:\bm{B}\geq 0,\quad\bm{B}\in\textrm{Sym}(d),

(2.31)

due to its definition (
2.29
), more precisely

\bm{B}:\mathds{Q}_{Y}:\bm{B}=\text{vol}(Y)\left\langle{\left[\left(\bm{A}^{\textrm{app}}_{Y}-\left\langle{\bm{A}^{\textrm{app}}_{Y}}\right\rangle\right):\bm{B}\right]^{2}}\right\rangle\geq 0,\quad\bm{B}\in\textrm{Sym}(d).

(2.32)

Sometimes it is convenient to re-write the formula (
2.29
) in the form

\mathds{Q}_{Y}=\text{vol}(Y)\left(\left\langle{\bm{A}^{\textrm{app}}_{Y}\otimes\bm{A}^{\textrm{app}}_{Y}}\right\rangle-\left\langle{\bm{A}^{\textrm{app}}_{Y}}\right\rangle\otimes\left\langle{\bm{A}^{\textrm{app}}_{Y}}\right\rangle\right).

(2.33)

For a specific random conductance ensemble, Duerinckx et al.
[
22
]
showed that the apparent
\displaystyle\mu
Q-tensor converges to a deterministic limit
\displaystyle\mathds{Q}\in\textrm{Sym}(\textrm{Sym}(d))

\mathds{Q}_{LY}\rightarrow\mathds{Q}\quad\text{as}\quad L\rightarrow\infty,

(2.34)

the ensemble’s
\displaystyle\mu
Q-tensor, which inherits the symmetry property (
2.30
)

\bm{B}:\mathds{Q}:\bm{C}=\bm{C}:\mathds{Q}:\bm{B},\quad\bm{B},\bm{C}\in\textrm{Sym}(d),

(2.35)

and the positive semidefiniteness property (
2.31
)

\bm{B}:\mathds{Q}:\bm{B}\geq 0,\quad\bm{B}\in\textrm{Sym}(d).

(2.36)

from the apparent tensor (
2.29
) via the limit procedure (
2.34
). Moreover, Duerinckx et al.
[
22
]
established the quantitative decay estimate

\left\|\mathds{Q}_{LY}-\mathds{Q}\right\|\sim\left(\frac{\log L}{L}\right)^{\frac{d}{2}}\quad\text{as}\quad L\rightarrow\infty

(2.37)

for periodized ensembles on cubic cells.

2.3
Symmetry properties of effective and apparent tensors

To encode the symmetry of our ensemble (
2.2
), we consider a given closed subgroup
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}
of the orthogonal group

O(d)=\left\{\bm{Q}\in\mathds{R}^{d\times d}\,\middle|\,\bm{Q}^{T}\bm{Q}=\bm{I}\right\}

(2.38)

of matrices which preserve the Euclidean norm. Then, we study the group action

\mathcal{G}\times\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathcal{A},\quad(\bm{Q},\bm{A})\mapsto\bm{A}^{\bm{Q}},

(2.39)

defined point-wise via the formula

\bm{A}^{\bm{Q}}(\bm{x})=\bm{Q}\bm{A}(\bm{Q}^{T}\bm{x})\bm{Q}^{T},\quad\bm{x}\in\mathds{R}^{d},

(2.40)

for
\displaystyle\bm{A}\in\mathcal{A}
and
\displaystyle\bm{Q}\in\mathcal{G}
. It is not difficult to see that this prescription is well-defined, i.e., preserves the eigenvalue bounds (
2.1
), and does indeed lead to a group action, i.e., the identity

\left(\bm{A}^{\bm{Q}_{1}}\right)^{\bm{Q}_{2}}(\bm{x})=\bm{A}^{\bm{Q}_{2}\bm{Q}_{1}}(\bm{x}),\quad\bm{x}\in\mathds{R}^{d},

(2.41)

holds for all elements
\displaystyle\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{Q}_{2}\in\mathcal{G}
. We assume that the action (
2.39
) is
\displaystyle\mu
-measurable for an ensemble (
2.2
) of conductivity tensors.

We say that the ensemble
\displaystyle\mu
is
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}
-invariant if, for any
\displaystyle\mu
-measurable set
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
and every group element
\displaystyle\bm{Q}\in\mathcal{G}
, the transformed set

\mathcal{S}^{\bm{Q}}=\left\{\bm{A}^{\bm{Q}}\in\mathcal{A}\,\middle|\,\bm{A}\in\mathcal{S}\right\}

(2.42)

is also
\displaystyle\mu
-measurable and has identical measure

\mu(\mathcal{S}^{\bm{Q}})=\mu(\mathcal{S}).

(2.43)

As a consequence, any
\displaystyle\mu
-integrable function
\displaystyle F:\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathds{R}
satisfies the equation

\int_{\mathcal{A}}F(\bm{A})\,d\mu(\bm{A})=\int_{\mathcal{A}}F\left(\bm{A}^{\bm{Q}}\right)\,d\mu(\bm{A})

(2.44)

for all group elements
\displaystyle\bm{Q}\in\mathcal{G}
.

The following properties hold:

1.

For a
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}
-invariant ensemble, the associated effective conductivity (
2.7
) is
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}
-invariant, i.e., the identity

\bm{Q}^{T}\bm{A}^{\textrm{eff}}\bm{Q}=\bm{A}^{\textrm{eff}}

(2.45)

holds for all
\displaystyle\bm{Q}\in\mathcal{G}
.

2.

For a
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}
-invariant ensemble, the associated
\displaystyle\mu
Q-tensor (
2.34
) is
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}
-invariant, i.e., the identity

\mathds{Q}^{\bm{Q}}=\mathds{Q}

(2.46)

holds for all
\displaystyle\bm{Q}\in\mathcal{G}
and the implicitly defined action

\bm{\xi}\otimes_{s}\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\eta$}:\mathds{Q}^{\bm{Q}}:\bm{\xi}\otimes_{s}\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\eta$}=\left[(\bm{Q}\bm{\xi})\otimes_{s}(\bm{Q}\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\eta$})\right]:\mathds{Q}:\left[(\bm{Q}\bm{\xi})\otimes_{s}(\bm{Q}\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\eta$})\right],\quad\bm{\xi},\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\eta$}\in\mathds{R}^{d}.

(2.47)

Item 1 is known in the literature, see Alexanderian
[
35
]
and Zhikov-Kozlov
[
36
]
who use a less stringent invariance condition than described by eq. (
2.43
). We proceed differently – the results (
2.45
) and (
2.46
) are a byproduct of their unit-cell based counterparts (
2.54
) and (
2.55
) discussed below. For a spherical unit cell (
2.16
), the limiting procedures (
2.20
) and the
\displaystyle\mu
Q-tensor (
2.34
) imply the statements (
2.45
) and (
2.46
) directly.

The effective properties (
2.7
) satisfy the invariance property (
2.45
), together with the associated
\displaystyle\mu
Q-tensor (
2.46
). However, as these quantities cannot be inferred directly, we are more interested in the ramifications for the RVE method described in section
2.2
. To this end, we define the symmetry group of a given unit cell
\displaystyle Y
via

O_{Y}(d)=\left\{\bm{Q}\in O(d)\,\middle|\,\bm{Q}Y=Y\right\},

(2.48)

comprising those orthogonal transformations which preserve the unit cell (
2.13
). Apparently, the spherical unit cell (
2.16
) is the most symmetric, i.e., it holds

O_{Y^{\textrm{sph}}_{R}}(d)=O(d).

(2.49)

Other unit cells may reduce the symmetry, e.g., we have

O_{Y^{\textrm{rect}}_{L_{1},\ldots,L_{d}}}(d)=\left\{\textrm{diag}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{d})\,\middle|\,\alpha_{i}\in\{-1,+1\},\quad i=1,2,\ldots,d\right\},

(2.50)

for a rectangular unit cell (
2.14
) with
distinct
edge-lengths, i.e.,
\displaystyle L_{i}\neq L_{j}

\displaystyle(i\neq j)
.

The symmetry group (
2.48
) of the cube (
2.15
) in three spatial dimensions,
\displaystyle d=3
, equals the full octahedral group
[

which has
\displaystyle 48

0&1&0\\
0&0&-1\end{array}\right],\quad\left[\begin{array}[]{rrr}1&0&0\\

As the action of any element
\displaystyle\bm{Q}
of the group
\displaystyle O_{Y}(d)

\displaystyle Y

\phi_{Y,\bm{A}^{\bm{Q}},\bm{Q}\bar{\bm{\xi}}}(\bm{Q}\bm{x})=\phi_{Y,\bm{A},\bar{\bm{\xi}}}(\bm{x})

(2.53)

holds for any realization
\displaystyle\bm{A}\in\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle\bar{\bm{\xi}}\in\mathds{R}^{d}
and almost every point
\displaystyle\bm{x}\in Y

A.1

2.53

\bm{Q}\left\langle{\bm{A}^{\textrm{app}}_{Y}}\right\rangle\bm{Q}^{T}=\left\langle{\bm{A}^{\textrm{app}}_{Y}}\right\rangle

(2.54)

and

\left(\mathds{Q}_{Y}\right)^{\bm{Q}}=\mathds{Q}_{Y}

(2.55)

for all elements
\displaystyle\bm{Q}
of the cell-symmetry group

\mathcal{G}_{Y}=\mathcal{G}\cap O_{Y}(d).

(2.56)

The derivation of these results is discussed in the Appendices

A.3

2.16
) coincides with the full symmetry of the ensemble, i.e., the ensemble symmetry is not broken by the cell. In particular, the mean apparent properties
\displaystyle\left\langle{\bm{A}^{\textrm{app}}_{Y^{\textrm{sph}}_{R}}}\right\rangle
share the same symmetries as the effective properties
\displaystyle\bm{A}^{\textrm{eff}}
. Also, the
\displaystyle\mu
Q-tensor of the cell (
2.16
) has the full symmetries expected from the ensemble’s
\displaystyle\mu
Q-tensor.

2.4.5
Cubic cell symmetry

The predominant cell shape in computational homogenization is given by cubic cells (
2.15
). The associated cell-symmetry group (
2.48
) is the octahedral group (
2.51
), generated by the three matrices (
2.52
) in three spatial dimensions. For isotropic ensemble symmetry (
2.58
), the associated mean apparent properties have the form

\left\langle{\bm{A}^{\textrm{app}}_{Y^{\textrm{cub}}_{L}}}\right\rangle=\alpha\bm{I}\equiv\matrixquantity[\lx@physics@matrix\alpha& 0 & 0 \\ 0 & \alpha& 0 \\ 0 & 0 & \alpha\endlx@physics@matrix]

(2.68)

with a single independent constant
\displaystyle\alpha
. In particular, the expected apparent conductivity has the same form as the effective conductivity (
2.59
) in the isotropic case. However, this is merely a coincidence, as a quick glance at the properties of the
\displaystyle\mu
Q-tensor reveal: For an isotropic ensemble, the mean
\displaystyle\mu
Q-tensor has a cubic symmetry, i.e., admits the form

\left\langle{\mathds{Q}_{Y^{\textrm{cub}}_{L}}}\right\rangle\mathrel{\hbox{\hskip 104.778pt\hskip-3.8889pt\hbox{=}\hskip-3.8889pt\hskip-104.778pt\raisebox{7.11319pt}{\hbox{\raisebox{-0.64954pt}{\resizebox{8526975}{141084}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.94444pt}{$\displaystyle\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{0}{0}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\displaystyle\wedge$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{0}{0}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\textstyle\wedge$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{0}{0}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\scriptstyle\wedge$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{0}{0}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\scriptscriptstyle\wedge$}}}}}$}}}}}}\hskip-104.778pt\hskip 104.778pt}}\matrixquantity[\lx@physics@matrix Q_{1111} & Q_{1122} & Q_{1122} & 0 & 0 & 0 \\

0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & Q_{1212} & 0 \\

(2.69)

in Voigt’s notation with three independent constants
\displaystyle Q_{1111}
,
\displaystyle Q_{1122}
and
\displaystyle Q_{1212}

Using cubic cells does not impose restrictions on transversely isotropic (
2.61
) and orthotropic ensembles (
2.65
), as long as the axes of transverse isotropy/orthotropy are aligned with the edges of the cube.

2.4.6
Rectangular cell symmetry

The symmetry group of rectangular cells (
2.14
) with distinct edge lengths coincides with the orthotropic group (
2.65
). Thus, using such non-equiaxed rectangular cells breaks the symmetry to orthotropic. In order to preserve the transversely isotropic symmetry (
2.62
), it is sufficient to use a rectangular cell which are cylindrical in the third direction, i.e., the condition
\displaystyle L_{1}=L_{2}
should hold.

3
Symmetry-informed strategies for improving the RVE method

3.1
Symmetry-enforcing projectors and their properties

Let
\displaystyle V
be a (finite-dimensional) Hilbert space with inner product
\displaystyle\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{V}
. A projector
\displaystyle\bm{P}\in L(V)
is a bounded linear operator on the space
\displaystyle V
which is idempotent:

\bm{P}^{2}=\bm{P}.

(3.1)

Any projector
\displaystyle\bm{P}
gives rise to a decomposition of the Hilbert space
\displaystyle V
into closed complementary subspaces,

V=\textrm{im}(\bm{P})\oplus\textrm{ker}(\bm{P}),

(3.2)

represented by the image
\displaystyle\textrm{im}(\bm{P})
and the kernel
\displaystyle\textrm{ker}(\bm{P})
of the operator
\displaystyle\bm{P}
. In terms of the complementary projector
\displaystyle\bm{P}^{c}=\bm{I}-\bm{P}\in L(V)
, we may also write

\textrm{im}(\bm{P})=\textrm{ker}(\bm{P}^{c})\quad\text{and}\quad\textrm{ker}(\bm{P})=\textrm{im}(\bm{P}^{c}).

(3.3)

We call a projector (
3.1
) orthogonal if it is self-adjoint, i.e., the equation

\langle\bm{P}\bm{u},\bm{v}\rangle_{V}=\langle\bm{u},\bm{P}\bm{v}\rangle_{V}

(3.4)

holds for all
\displaystyle\bm{u},\bm{v}\in\bm{V}
. Equivalent to the condition (
3.4
) is the validity of the Pythagorean Theorem

\|\bm{v}\|^{2}_{V}=\|\bm{P}\bm{v}\|_{V}^{2}+\|\bm{P}^{c}\bm{v}\|_{V}^{2},\quad\bm{v}\in V.

(3.5)

A trivial, yet quite significant consequence of the identity (
3.5
) is the non-expansiveness

\|\bm{P}\bm{v}\|_{V}\leq\|\bm{v}\|_{V},\quad\bm{v}\in V,

(3.6)

of an orthogonal projector
\displaystyle\bm{P}
.

Let us consider a compact group
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}
, and suppose that an orthogonal representation

\bm{L}:\mathcal{G}\rightarrow L(V),\quad\bm{Q}\mapsto\bm{L}_{\bm{Q}},

(3.7)

is given, i.e., a group homomorphism into the invertible linear operators on the space
\displaystyle V
, s.t. every element is length-preserving

\|\bm{L}_{\bm{Q}}\bm{v}\|_{V}=\|\bm{v}\|_{V}\quad\text{for all}\quad\bm{v}\in V,\quad\bm{Q}\in\mathcal{G}.

(3.8)

Denote by

V^{\mathcal{G}}=\left\{\bm{v}\in V\,\middle|\,\bm{L}_{\bm{Q}}\bm{v}=\bm{v}\quad\text{for all}\quad\bm{Q}\in\mathcal{G}\right\}

(3.9)

the set of vectors which are invariant under the group action (
3.7
). The set (
3.9
) defines a closed subspace of the Hilbert space
\displaystyle V
.

Before coming to the main statement of relevance in this section, we need to discuss the concept of Haar measure first. A (Borel) measure
\displaystyle d\mu
on a Lie group
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}
is called left invariant if the condition

\int_{\mathcal{G}}f(\bm{R}\bm{Q})\,d\mu(\bm{Q})=\int_{\mathcal{G}}f(\bm{Q})\,d\mu(\bm{Q})

(3.10)

holds for any integrable function
\displaystyle f\in L^{1}(\mathcal{G};d\mu)
and every group element
\displaystyle\bm{R}\in\mathcal{G}
. It is called right invariant provided the condition

\int_{\mathcal{G}}f(\bm{Q}\bm{R})\,d\mu(\bm{Q})=\int_{\mathcal{G}}f(\bm{Q})\,d\mu(\bm{Q})

(3.11)

is satisfied for any integrable function
\displaystyle f\in L^{1}(\mathcal{G};d\mu)
and every group element
\displaystyle\bm{R}\in\mathcal{G}
. The group-inversion mapping

\iota:\mathcal{G}\rightarrow\mathcal{G},\quad\bm{Q}\mapsto\bm{Q}^{-1},

(3.12)

permits to turn a left-invariant measure
\displaystyle d\mu
(
3.10
) into a right-invariant one (
3.11
) via pull-back
\displaystyle\iota^{*}d\mu
, i.e.,

\int_{\mathcal{G}}f(\bm{Q})\,d\left(\iota^{*}d\mu\right)(\bm{Q}):=\int_{\mathcal{G}}f(\bm{Q}^{-1})\,d\mu(\bm{Q}).

(3.13)

On a compact Lie group, there is precisely one left-invariant measure (
3.10
) which is moreover normalized

\int_{\mathcal{G}}d\mu_{\mathcal{G}}(\bm{Q})=1

(3.14)

to be a probability measure. It is not difficult to see that the measure (
3.14
), called
Haar measure

[
62
]
, is also right-invariant (
3.11
) and also inversion-invariant

\int_{\mathcal{G}}f(\bm{Q})\,d\mu_{\mathcal{G}}(\bm{Q}).:=\int_{\mathcal{G}}f(\bm{Q}^{-1})\,d\mu_{\mathcal{G}}(\bm{Q}),\quad f\in L^{1}(\mathcal{G};d\mu_{\mathcal{G}}).

(3.15)

With this concept at hand, we may represent the orthogonal projector onto the closed subspace (
3.9
) via averaging the orthogonal representation (
3.7
) over the group w.r.t. the normalized Haar measure (
3.14
) on the group
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}
, i.e., the identity

\bm{P}_{V^{\mathcal{G}}}\bm{v}=\int_{\mathcal{G}}\bm{L}_{\bm{Q}}\bm{v}\,d\mu_{\mathcal{G}}(\bm{Q})

(3.16)

holds for all
\displaystyle\bm{v}\in V
. The validity of this statement is ensured by showing that the right-hand side of the expression (
3.16
) defines an orthogonal projector onto the closed linear subspace (
3.9
), see Apx.
B
.

After these abstract considerations let us take a closer look at why the identity (
3.16
) is actually useful. The two hands of the identity (
3.16
) have a different spirit: For a finite-dimensional space, the left-hand side (
3.16
) may be expressed in the form

\bm{P}_{V^{\mathcal{G}}}\bm{v}=\sum_{i=1}^{\dim V^{\mathcal{G}}}\langle\bm{v},\bm{e}_{i}\rangle_{V}\,\bm{e}_{i},

(3.17)

where
\displaystyle(\bm{e}_{1},\bm{e}_{2},\ldots,\bm{e}_{\dim V})
refers to an orthogonal basis of the space
\displaystyle V
of a special form – the first
\displaystyle\dim V^{\mathcal{G}}
vectors span the subspace
\displaystyle V^{\mathcal{G}}
, and the remaining
\displaystyle\dim V-\dim V^{\mathcal{G}}
vectors span its orthogonal complement. In particular, the expression (
3.17
) is
readily computable
. In contrast, the right-hand side (
3.16
) is less computable – except for the case of finite groups – but helps in establishing formal properties, as will become clear in the following.

For the article at hand, the first Hilbert space
\displaystyle V
of interest is the space

V=\textrm{Sym}(d),

(3.18)

equipped with the Frobenius inner product

\langle\bm{A},\bm{B}\rangle=\textrm{tr}(\bm{A}\bm{B})\equiv\bm{A}:\bm{B},\quad\bm{A},\bm{B}\in\textrm{Sym}(d).

(3.19)

The natural action (
2.40
)

\mathds{L}_{\bm{Q}}:\bm{A}\equiv\bm{A}^{\bm{Q}}=\bm{Q}\bm{A}\bm{Q}^{T},\quad\bm{A}\in\textrm{Sym}(d),\quad\bm{Q}\in O(d),

(3.20)

is orthogonal, as

\langle\bm{A}^{\bm{Q}},\bm{B}^{\bm{Q}}\rangle=\textrm{tr}(\bm{A}^{\bm{Q}}\bm{B}^{\bm{Q}})=\textrm{tr}(\bm{Q}\bm{A}\bm{Q}^{T}\bm{Q}\bm{B}\bm{Q}^{T})=\textrm{tr}(\bm{A}\bm{B})=\langle\bm{A},\bm{B}\rangle,\quad\bm{A},\bm{B}\in\textrm{Sym}(d),

(3.21)

where we used the orthogonality of the matrix
\displaystyle\bm{Q}
and the fact that the trace vanishes on commutators, i.e., the identity

\tr(\fM\fN)=\tr(\fN\fM),\quad\bm{M},\bm{N}\in\mathds{R}^{n\times n},

(3.22)

is used with
\displaystyle\bm{M}=\bm{Q}\bm{A}\bm{Q}^{T}\bm{Q}\bm{B}
and
\displaystyle\bm{N}=\bm{Q}^{T}
.

For the work at hand, the advantage of the averaging formulation (
3.16
) is its compatibility with eigenvalue bounds. More precisely, suppose that there are real constants
\displaystyle\alpha_{\pm}
, s.t. a tensor
\displaystyle\bm{A}\in\textrm{Sym}(d)
satisfies the inequalities

\alpha_{-}\,\|\bm{\xi}\|^{2}\leq\bm{\xi}\cdot\bm{A}\bm{\xi}\leq\alpha_{+}\,\|\bm{\xi}\|^{2}

(3.23)

for all vectors
\displaystyle\bm{\xi}\in\mathds{R}^{d}
. Then, for any closed subgroup
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}
of the orthogonal group
\displaystyle O(d)
, the projected tensor satisfies the same bounds

\alpha_{-}\,\|\bm{\xi}\|^{2}\leq\bm{\xi}\cdot\left(\mathds{P}^{\mathcal{G}}:\bm{A}\right)\bm{\xi}\leq\alpha_{+}\,\|\bm{\xi}\|^{2},

(3.24)

where we made use of the simplifying notation

\mathds{P}^{\mathcal{G}}\equiv\mathds{P}_{\textrm{Sym}(d)^{\mathcal{G}}}.

(3.25)

For the argument to establish the bounds (
3.24
), we use the representation (
3.16
)

\mathds{P}^{\mathcal{G}}:\bm{A}=\int_{\mathcal{G}}\mathds{L}_{\bm{Q}}:\bm{A}\,d\mu_{\mathcal{G}}(\bm{Q})\equiv\int_{\mathcal{G}}\bm{Q}\bm{A}\bm{Q}^{T}\,d\mu_{\mathcal{G}}(\bm{Q}).

(3.26)

To derive the inequalities (
3.24
), we notice

\bm{\xi}\cdot\left(\mathds{L}_{\bm{Q}}:\bm{A}\right)\bm{\xi}=\bm{\xi}\cdot\left(\bm{Q}\bm{A}\bm{Q}^{T}\right)\bm{\xi}=\bm{Q}^{T}\bm{\xi}\cdot\bm{A}\bm{Q}^{T}\bm{\xi},

(3.27)

valid for
\displaystyle\bm{\xi}\in\mathds{R}^{d}
and
\displaystyle\bm{Q}\in O(3)
. Invoking the bounds (
3.23
), the two-sided inequality

\alpha_{-}\,\|\bm{Q}^{T}\bm{\xi}\|^{2}\leq\bm{\xi}\cdot\left(\mathds{L}_{\bm{Q}}:\bm{A}\right)\bm{\xi}\leq\alpha_{+}\,\|\bm{Q}^{T}\bm{\xi}\|^{2}

(3.28)

follows. As the element
\displaystyle\bm{Q}\in O(3)
is length-preserving – together with its inverse
\displaystyle\bm{Q}^{T}
– we obtain the estimate

\alpha_{-}\,\|\bm{\xi}\|^{2}\leq\bm{\xi}\cdot\left(\mathds{L}_{\bm{Q}}:\bm{A}\right)\bm{\xi}\leq\alpha_{+}\,\|\bm{\xi}\|^{2}.

(3.29)

Averaging the expression over the group
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}
yields the claimed bounds (
3.24
).

The same form of arguments apply to tensors

\mathds{Q}\in\textrm{Sym}(\textrm{Sym}(d))

(3.30)

which satisfy bounds

\alpha_{-}\,\|\bm{B}\|^{2}\leq\bm{B}:\mathds{Q}:\bm{B}\leq\alpha_{+}\,\|\bm{B}\|^{2},\quad\bm{B}\in\textrm{Sym}(d),

(3.31)

for real numbers
\displaystyle\alpha_{\pm}
, i.e., the estimates

(3.32)

follow for the orthogonal projector

In section

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}

\displaystyle O(d)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}

5
Conclusion

This paper explored the ramifications of the ensemble symmetry in stochastic homogenization of conductivity problems and provided a post-processing strategy to improve the RVE method. Upon investigating isotropic, transversely isotropic and orthotropic ensembles of short fiber-reinforced composite, we make the following remarks:

1.

For a cubic cell, the symmetry-informed projection technique is most effective for an isotropic ensemble, moderately effective for a transversely isotropic ensemble and least effective for an orthotropic ensemble, at least in cases the axes of orthotropy align with the edges of the cell. The latter is not surprising, as no symmetry breaking occurs in this case.

2.

The projection strategy helps to retain the convenience of the cubic cell and avoid the spherical volume elements
[
40
,
41
]
in the case isotropic ensemble or cylindrical volume element in the case of transversely isotropic ensemble. In particular, we the devised projection method leads to a highly effective, low-cost alternative to the spherical unit-cell approach.

3.

The random errors are reduced by factors three to five, while the systematic errors are not affected byo the projection. Thus, we recommend to apply the post-processing projection strategy on the apparent conductivity tensors for better approximation without additional computational cost.

4.

The symmetry-informed projection technique may be complementary to other variance reduction techniques such as the antithetic variables or control variates
[
67
,
68
,
69
]
.

5.

The projected errors attain the same optimal decay rate as their original errors. Please note that we only employ the periodized ensemble for the work at hand, the performance of post-processing projection in snap-shot ensembles
[
21
]
could be interesting to investigate.

6.

The projection technique also works on the
\displaystyle\mu
Q-tensor and helps to recover its optimal convergence rate for the isotropic and transversely isotropic ensemble.

A.1
Covariance of correctors on symmetric cells

The goal of this section is to show the validity of the statement (
2.53
)

\phi_{Y,\bm{A}^{\bm{Q}},\bm{Q}\bar{\bm{\xi}}}(\bm{Q}\bm{x})=\phi_{Y,\bm{A},\bar{\bm{\xi}}}(\bm{x})

(A.1)

for the corrector (
2.17
) on the unit cell
\displaystyle Y
, where
\displaystyle\bm{Q}\in O_{Y}(d)
stands for a cell-preserving orthogonal transformation (
2.48
),
\displaystyle\bm{A}\in\mathcal{A}
denotes a fixed conductivity tensor,
\displaystyle\bar{\bm{\xi}}\in\mathds{R}^{d}
refers to a given vector and the equation (
A.1
) should be valid for almost every
\displaystyle\bm{x}\in Y
.

For this purpose, we first write down the weak form of the equation (
2.17
) which characterizes the unique solution
\displaystyle\phi_{Y,\bm{A},\bar{\bm{\xi}}}(\bm{x})
: Find the scalar field
\displaystyle\phi\in H^{1}_{0}(Y)

\displaystyle v\in H^{1}_{0}(Y)
. Similarly, the function
\displaystyle\psi=\phi_{Y,\bm{A}^{\bm{Q}},\bm{Q}\bar{\bm{\xi}}}
solves the problem

for all fields
\displaystyle w\in H^{1}_{0}(Y)
, which we may also rewrite in the form

0=\int_{Y}\nabla w(\bm{y})\cdot\bm{Q}\bm{A}(\bm{Q}^{T}\bm{y})\bm{Q}^{T}\left[\bm{Q}\bar{\bm{\xi}}+\nabla\psi(\bm{y})\right]\,d\bm{y}=\int_{Y}\bm{Q}^{T}\nabla w(\bm{y})\cdot\bm{A}(\bm{Q}^{T}\bm{y})\left[\bar{\bm{\xi}}+\bm{Q}^{T}\nabla\psi(\bm{y})\right]\,d\bm{y}

in view of eq. (
2.40

to observe

0=\int_{Y}\nabla w(\bm{y})\cdot\bm{Q}\bm{A}(\bm{Q}^{T}\bm{y})\bm{Q}^{T}\left[\bm{Q}\bar{\bm{\xi}}+\nabla\psi(\bm{y})\right]\,d\bm{y}=\int_{Y}\bm{Q}^{T}\nabla w(\bm{Q}\bm{x})\cdot\bm{A}(\bm{x})\left[\bar{\bm{\xi}}+\bm{Q}^{T}\nabla\psi(\bm{Q}\bm{x})\right]\,d\bm{x},

(A.5)

where we used that the transformation
\displaystyle\bm{Q}

(A.6)

defines an element of the Sobolev space
\displaystyle H^{1}_{0}(Y)

\nabla w^{\bm{Q}}(\bm{x})=\bm{Q}^{T}\nabla w(\bm{Q}\bm{x}).

(A.7)

In fact, for any fixed element
\displaystyle\bm{Q}\in O(d)

A.6
) defines an isometric automorphism of the Hilbert space
\displaystyle H^{1}_{0}(Y)
.
Taking into account eq. (
A.7

A.5
) in the form

0=\int_{Y}\nabla w^{\bm{Q}}(\bm{x})\cdot\bm{A}(\bm{x})\left[\bar{\bm{\xi}}+\nabla\psi^{\bm{Q}}\right]\,d\bm{x}.

(A.8)

Setting
\displaystyle w=v^{\bm{Q}^{T}}
, i.e., a "change of coordinates" in the test space, we thus observe that the function
\displaystyle\left(\phi_{Y,\bm{A}^{\bm{Q}},\bm{Q}\bar{\bm{\xi}}}\right)^{\bm{Q}}
solves the equation (
A.2
). Due to the uniqueness of the solution, the desired identity (
A.1

Averaging the action over the group provides an orthogonal projector

The purpose of this section is to show that the bounded linear operator

P\in L(V),\quad\bm{v}\mapsto\int_{\mathcal{G}}\bm{L}_{\bm{Q}}\bm{v}\,d\mu_{\mathcal{G}}(\bm{Q}),

(B.1)

defines an orthogonal projector onto the closed subspace of invariant vectors (
3.9
)

V^{\mathcal{G}}=\left\{\bm{v}\in V\,\middle|\,\bm{L}_{\bm{Q}}\bm{v}=\bm{v}\quad\text{for all}\quad\bm{Q}\in\mathcal{G}\right\},

(B.2)

i.e., the identity (
3.16
) holds. To show this orthogonal projector property, we follow Fulton-Harris’s proof
[
70
]
for the case of a finite group and consider three separate properties:

1.

For every element
\displaystyle\bm{w}\in V
, the action of the operator
\displaystyle P
defined in eq. (
B.1
) produces an element of the invariant set (
B.2
), i.e.,

P\bm{w}\in V^{\mathcal{G}}\quad\text{for all}\quad\bm{w}\in V.

(B.3)

2.

Every invariant element
\displaystyle\bm{v}\in V^{\mathcal{G}}
is preserved by the operator
\displaystyle P
, i.e.,

P\bm{v}=\bm{v}\quad\text{for all}\quad\bm{v}\in V^{\mathcal{G}}.

(B.4)

3.

The operator (
B.1
) is self-adjoint (
3.4
), i.e., the condition

\langle P\bm{v},\bm{w}\rangle_{V}=\langle\bm{v},P\bm{w}\rangle_{V}

(B.5)

holds for all
\displaystyle\bm{v},\bm{w}\in V
.

Once these three properties are shown, the claimed projection property (
3.16
) follows. Indeed, by the first two properties (
B.3
) and (
B.4
), the operator (
B.1
) is a projector with image (
B.2
). By the third property (
B.4
), the operator (
B.1
) is orthogonal (
3.4
).

So, let us consider each of the properties individually.

Suppose an element
\displaystyle\bm{w}\in V
is given. We wish to show the identity

\bm{L}_{\tilde{\bm{Q}}}\bm{v}=\bm{v}\quad\text{for}\quad\bm{v}:=\int_{\mathcal{G}}\bm{L}_{\bm{Q}}\bm{w}\,d\mu_{\mathcal{G}}(\bm{Q})\quad\text{and all}\quad\tilde{\bm{Q}}\in\mathcal{G}.

(B.6)

We notice

\bm{L}_{\tilde{\bm{Q}}}\bm{v}=\bm{L}_{\tilde{\bm{Q}}}\int_{\mathcal{G}}\bm{L}_{\bm{Q}}\bm{w}\,d\mu_{\mathcal{G}}(\bm{Q})=\int_{\mathcal{G}}\bm{L}_{\tilde{\bm{Q}}}\bm{L}_{\bm{Q}}\bm{w}\,d\mu_{\mathcal{G}}(\bm{Q})=\int_{\mathcal{G}}\bm{L}_{\tilde{\bm{Q}}\bm{Q}}\bm{w}\,d\mu_{\mathcal{G}}(\bm{Q}).

(B.7)

By the invariance of the Haar measure under left multiplication (
3.10
) by group elements, a change of variables
\displaystyle\bm{Q}^{\prime}=\tilde{\bm{Q}}\bm{Q}
leads to the transformations

\int_{\mathcal{G}}\bm{L}_{\tilde{\bm{Q}}\bm{Q}}\bm{w}\,d\mu_{\mathcal{G}}(\bm{Q})=\int_{\mathcal{G}}\bm{L}_{\bm{Q}^{\prime}}\bm{w}\,d\mu_{\mathcal{G}}(\tilde{\bm{Q}}^{-1}\bm{Q}^{\prime})=\int_{\mathcal{G}}\bm{L}_{\bm{Q}^{\prime}}\bm{w}\,d\mu_{\mathcal{G}}(\bm{Q}^{\prime})=\bm{v},

(B.8)

showing the claimed statement (
B.3
).

Concerning the next item (
B.4
), we notice that any element
\displaystyle\bm{v}
of the invariant set (
B.2
) satisfies the equation

\bm{L}_{\bm{Q}}\bm{v}=\bm{v}

(B.9)

by definition. Thus, averaging over the group (
B.1
) yields

P\bm{v}\equiv\int_{\mathcal{G}}\bm{L}_{\bm{Q}}\bm{v}\,d\mu_{\mathcal{G}}(\bm{Q})=\int_{\mathcal{G}}\bm{v}\,d\mu_{\mathcal{G}}(\bm{Q})=\bm{v},

(B.10)

where we used the normalization of the Haar measure
\displaystyle d\mu
. Last but not least, we consider the self-adjointness property (
B.5
). We notice

\langle P\bm{v},\bm{w}\rangle_{V}=\left\langle\int_{\mathcal{G}}\bm{L}_{\bm{Q}}\bm{v}\,d\mu_{\mathcal{G}}(\bm{Q}),\bm{w}\right\rangle_{V}=\int_{\mathcal{G}}\langle\bm{L}_{\bm{Q}}\bm{v},\bm{w}\rangle_{V}\,d\mu_{\mathcal{G}}(\bm{Q}).

(B.11)

Using the group homomorphism property of the representation (
3.7
) and the orthogonality (
3.8
), we observe

\begin{split}\langle P\bm{v},\bm{w}\rangle_{V}&=\int_{\mathcal{G}}\langle\bm{L}_{\bm{Q}}\bm{v},\bm{w}\rangle_{V}\,d\mu_{\mathcal{G}}(\bm{Q})\\
&=\int_{\mathcal{G}}\langle\bm{L}_{\bm{Q}}\bm{v},\bm{L}_{\bm{Q}}\bm{L}_{\bm{Q}^{-1}}\bm{w}\rangle_{V}\,d\mu_{\mathcal{G}}(\bm{Q})\\
&=\int_{\mathcal{G}}\langle\bm{v},\bm{L}_{\bm{Q}^{-1}}\bm{w}\rangle_{V}\,d\mu_{\mathcal{G}}(\bm{Q})\\
&=\left\langle\bm{v},\int_{\mathcal{G}}\bm{L}_{\bm{Q}^{-1}}\bm{w}\,d\mu_{\mathcal{G}}(\bm{Q})\right\rangle_{V}\\
&=\left\langle\bm{v},\int_{\mathcal{G}}\bm{L}_{\bm{Q}}\bm{w}\,d\mu_{\mathcal{G}}(\bm{Q})\right\rangle_{V}\\
&=\langle\bm{v},P\bm{w}\rangle_{V},\end{split}

(B.12)

where we used the inversion invariance (
3.15
) of the Haar measure. Thus, the property (
B.5
) is established.
